Optical isolation and circulation using quantum frequency conversion on a chip
Authors: Jierui Hu, Hao Yuan, Joshua Akin, Shanhui Fan, Kejie Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Breaking electromagnetic reciprocity enables directional control of light and is essential for both classical and quantum photonic systems. However, realizing integrated optical nonreciprocity that simultaneously combines low loss, broad bandwidth, and compatibility with quantum states of light remains a major challenge. Here, we demonstrate optical nonreciprocity based on quantum frequency conversion in an integrated χ(2) nonlinear waveguide. Operating from classical power levels down to the single-photon level, the device preserves quantum coherence and entanglement of the input photons. We achieve an isolation of 34 decibels with a low on-chip insertion loss of 0.8 decibels and a broad isolation bandwidth, together with a four-port circulator exhibiting an operational fidelity of up to 0.97. Our results establish parametric frequency conversion as a scalable and quantum-compatible route toward integrated nonreciprocal photonic devices for applications in quantum communication and noise-resilient photonic networks.

INTRODUCTION

Reciprocity is a fundamental property of Maxwell’s equations that enforces symmetric transmission of light in conventional optical systems (1). Breaking this symmetry enables nonreciprocal devices such as optical isolators and circulators, which are essential for controlling the flow of light in both classical and quantum photonic systems. In classical applications, optical isolators are widely used to suppress back-reflections and stabilize lasers and amplifiers (2). In emerging quantum photonic platforms, nonreciprocal components play an even more critical role, as they can protect sensitive quantum emitters and detectors from unwanted noise and enable directional signal routing in quantum networks (3–11).

Despite their importance, realizing integrated optical nonreciprocity that simultaneously achieves low loss, broad bandwidth, and compatibility with quantum light remains a substantial challenge. Conventional magneto-optic isolators, while widely used in bulk optics, are difficult to integrate on chip due to material incompatibilities and excess optical loss (11, 12). Alternative approaches based on atomic systems have demonstrated nonreciprocal operation at the single-photon level, but they are constrained by narrow bandwidths, fixed operating wavelengths, and limited scalability (4–10). More recently, spatiotemporal modulation using electro-optic or acousto-optic effects has enabled nonmagnetic nonreciprocity in integrated platforms (13–25); however, these approaches typically require strong modulation fields and can introduce excess noise, posing challenges for operation in the quantum regime.

Here, we demonstrate optical nonreciprocity based on quantum frequency conversion in an integrated χ(2) nonlinear waveguide. Our device operates from the single-photon level to classical power levels, achieving an isolation of 34 dB, which is competitive among integrated nonmagnetic isolators, together with a low on-chip insertion loss of 0.8 dB and a broad isolation bandwidth. By leveraging efficient and near-noiseless frequency conversion, the demonstrated isolator preserves quantum coherence and entanglement of the input photons. In addition to optical isolation, the same mechanism enables multiport circulation by using the full mode space of the nonlinear interaction. We experimentally demonstrate a four-port optical circulator with high operational fidelity, confirming that the device can perform nonreciprocal routing of quantum signals. These results establish parametric frequency conversion as a versatile approach for realizing integrated, quantum-compatible nonreciprocal photonic devices, with potential applications in quantum communication, information processing, and noise-resilient photonic networks (3, 26–29).

RESULTS

Our optical isolator and circulator are realized on the basis of noiseless parametric frequency conversion (30) in a waveguide with χ(2) nonlinearity (Fig. 1, A and B), although the principle can also be extended to systems with χ(3) nonlinearity. Consider a waveguide supporting three optical modes, a, b, and c, where the χ(2) nonlinearity facilitates a three-wave mixing process described by the Hamiltonian Hˆ=ℏχ(aˆ†bˆ†cˆ+aˆbˆcˆ†) with aˆ (aˆ†) denoting the annihilation (creation) operator for mode a, and similarly for modes b and c. In the forward direction, when the three modes satisfy the frequency- and phase-matching conditions ωa+ωb=ωc and ka+kb=kc, where ω and k denote the angular frequency and wave vector, respectively, and mode a is driven by a classical pump field with amplitude α, the interaction between modes b and c becomes linearized, i.e., Hˆ=ℏχα(bˆ†cˆ+bˆcˆ†), which enables noiseless frequency conversion between the two modes, i.e., sum-frequency generation (SFG) (b→c) and difference-frequency generation (c→b).

When the frequency conversion is efficient, the input photons in mode b(c) can be completely transferred to mode c(b). Thus, filtering out the pump and light in mode c(b) effectively blocks the forward transmission of photons in mode b(c). In the backward direction, the same pump configuration no longer satisfies the phase-matching condition, and frequency conversion between modes b and c is suppressed. Consequently, the light input in mode b(c) is transmitted, resulting in nonreciprocal transmission. It should be noted that in this context, the definitions of forward and backward directions are opposite to those typically used for an isolator. This isolator operates simultaneously in widely separated optical frequency bands and inherently integrates quantum frequency conversion, making it particularly attractive for hybrid quantum networks involving photons of disparate wavelengths. When the full mode space is considered, the scattering matrix of the device, defined by (bL,out,bR,out,cL,out,cR,out)T=S(bL,in,bR,in,cL,in,cR,in)T, becomes asymmetric—a key feature of optical nonreciprocity (1)S=[0100001000011000](1)

This scattering matrix indicates that the χ(2) waveguide can also function as a four-port optical circulator when all modes are used (Fig. 1C), with (bL,bR,cL,cR) corresponding to ports 1 to 4, respectively. Furthermore, the directionality of both isolation and circulation can be reversed by switching the propagation direction of the pump.

This form of optical nonreciprocity, which is based on χ(2)- or χ(3)-mediated parametric frequency conversion (31), is distinct from passive nonlinear optical approaches (32, 33). Because the interaction is linearized by a classical pump, it circumvents the dynamic reciprocity constraint that limits passive nonlinear isolators (34). Moreover, unlike nonreciprocity via parametric down-conversion (35)—which relies on pumping the highest-frequency mode and thus suffers from spontaneous emission noise—our approach leverages noiseless conversion while preserving the original signal frequency in the transmission direction when operating as an isolator. These are critical for the realization of quantum-compatible optical isolation, as they avoid the need for single-photon nonlinearities and allow seamless downstream information processing.

We demonstrate the optical nonreciprocity using the III to V InGaP integrated photonics platform (Fig. 1, D and E) with a substantial χ(2) nonlinearity [χ(2)=220 pm/V] and low optical losses (36–38) (see fig. S1 for device fabrication). The InGaP nanophotonic waveguide is designed to be modal phase matched between the 1550-nm-band fundamental transverse electric mode (for a and b) and the 780-nm-band fundamental transverse magnetic mode (for c). For a phase-matched InGaP nanophotonic waveguide along the (110) direction, the simulated SFG efficiency is ηSFG=520,000%/W/cm2. We also implemented in situ phase-matching tuning nanoheater arrays (Fig. 1, F and G) to control the temperature of each segment of the waveguide and counteract the effect of thickness variations on the phase-matching condition (fig. S2). By minimizing the phase mismatch along the entire waveguide, a high isolation ratio can be achieved.

We use the phase-matching optimized 6-mm-long waveguide to demonstrate the optical isolator. The pump and signal are supplied by two wavelength-tunable telecom lasers, respectively. The pump and the converted light in the 780-nm band are filtered for the transmission measurement of the signal. Figure 2A shows the backward and forward transmissions when the pump is switched on and off, with the pump power of 21.3 mW. The backward transmission remains unchanged with an average on-chip insertion loss of 0.8 dB due to the waveguide propagation loss in the 1550-nm band (total insertion loss, 2.7 dB). The fiber coupling efficiency is 80 and 50% for the 1550- and 780-nm light, respectively. Figure 2B shows the measured isolation, I[λ]=∣tf[λ]/tb[λ]∣2, where tf(b)[λ] is the forward (backward) transmission coefficient as a function of wavelength, and the modeled isolation at approximately 1565 nm for various pump powers and pump wavelength at 1543 nm (see fig. S4 for the forward and backward transmission spectra). Figure 2C shows the corresponding peak isolation ratio. The isolation ratio increases with pump power and reaches a maximum of 33.6 dB at a pump power of 27 mW, corresponding to the first half-oscillation between the 1550- and 780-nm-band modes. The maximum isolation is limited by the residual phase mismatch in the waveguide at length scales shorter than that of individual nanoheaters and can be improved with more discrete nanoheaters. The 3- and 10-dB isolation bandwidths corresponding to the maximum isolation are 44.1 and 14.7 GHz, respectively. The signal power used in this measurement is approximately 17 μW, which is much weaker than that of the pump. We also measured isolation for various signal powers in the range of 1 to 200 μW and the maximum isolation is similar (fig. S5).

The isolation wavelengths, λb and λc, of the dual-band isolator can be tuned by adjusting the pump wavelength, λp, because the isolation is determined by the frequency- and phase-matching conditions of the frequency conversion: 1λp+1λb=1λc and npλp+nbλb=ncλc, where nk is the effective index of the respective mode and wavelength. Numerical simulations indicate that the fabricated waveguide supports wide isolation wavelength ranges, with mode b tunable over more than 1000 nm and mode c tunable over more than 30 nm. Because of the available laser wavelengths in our experiment, we demonstrate the tuning of the isolation wavelength by setting the pump at 1562 nm and reoptimizing the phase-matching condition of the waveguide using the nanoheater array. Under these conditions, a maximum isolation of 30.3 dB is achieved at 1546.7 nm with a pump power of 29 mW, as shown in Fig. 2D.

Quantum frequency conversion mediated by the beamsplitter-like Hamiltonian is fundamentally noiseless (30). However, parasitic noise due to pump-matter interactions in the waveguide can still arise. We measure the on-chip noise to be linear in pump power, reaching approximately N=2×10−4 counts per second per hertz (cps/Hz) for both backward and forward directions at the signal wavelength, about 20 nm away from the pump, and at the pump power corresponding to maximum isolation (Fig. 3A, see section S7 for measurement details). This noise level is well below the single-photon threshold of approximately 1 cps/Hz (one photon per time-frequency mode), confirming that our optical isolator can operate in the quantum regime.

To demonstrate the quantum compatibility of our device, we first verify that signal isolation works for single photons. We use a pair of correlated nondegenerate photons in the telecom band with a bandwidth of 2.3 GHz generated from spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) and send the signal photon in the forward direction of the isolator. Figure 3B shows the coincidence counts of the signal and idler photons when the pump is switched on and off. When the pump is off, the measured coincidence peak verifies the strong correlation between signal and idler; when the pump is on, the signal photons are blocked (by conversion to the 780-nm band and subsequent filtering), and consequently, the coincidence vanishes. The contrast of peak coincidence counts is 16 dB, consistent with the classical isolation ratio of the device during this measurement. We note that the isolation ratio of the device in the single-photon regime will be fundamentally limited by the signal flux–to–noise flux (N) ratio, as the noise flux in our device is bidirectional.

We then demonstrate that the time-energy entanglement of the SPDC photons is preserved after transmission through the optical isolator. As illustrated in Fig. 3C, we send a pair of degenerate SPDC photons with a bandwidth of 0.95 nm in the backward direction of the optical isolator and measure the time-resolved two-photon interference (39) via an unbalanced glass Mach-Zehnder interferometer (MZI). The MZI has a path delay of 1 ns, much longer than the coherence time of the SPDC photons, and its phase difference is controlled through temperature. The measured visibility of the two-photon interference fringe is 91.7%, limited by the imperfect interferometer visibility, which exceeds the Clauser-Horne limit of 1/2≈70.7% (40), thereby confirming the time-energy entanglement of the transmitted photons (see fig. S7 for measurement details).

We then use both modes b and c of the χ(2) waveguide and the quantum frequency conversion between them to achieve optical circulation (see Fig. 1C). In this experiment, we use a 3.2-mm-long phase matching–optimized waveguide. To characterize the optical circulation, we measure the transmission matrix Tij≡〈Fˆi,out〉/〈Fˆj,in〉, where 〈Fˆi,in(out)〉 represents the input (output) photon flux of port i. Figure 3D displays the measured transmission matrix for the four-port circulator with a 31-mW pump power (eq. S7). To quantify the performance of the circulator, we calculate the operational fidelity (5)F=Tr[T˜⋅TidT]Tr[Tid⋅TidT](4)where T˜ is the renormalized transmission matrix, T˜ij=Tij/∑kTkj, and Tid is the transmission matrix of an ideal circulator (see Eq. 1). Here, the fidelity quantifies the similarity between the measured transmission matrix and that of an ideal circulator after normalization, with a value of 1 corresponding to perfect circulation. It can be shown that 0≤F≤1, and for reciprocal transmission (T˜=T˜T), the fidelity is bounded by F≤0.5 (section S9). The fidelity of the measured circulator is F=0.962(4), while the average on-chip insertion loss is η=−10log(14∑ijTij)=2.8dB, which is limited by the 780-nm-band waveguide loss. The circulation direction can be reversed by changing the pump direction, and the measured transmission matrix for this case is shown in Fig. 3E with a fidelity of F=0.970(3). The added noise is 7.3×10−5cps/Hz in the 1550-nm band and 2.5×10−5cps/Hz in the 780-nm band via up-conversion, substantially lower than the single-photon level, indicating that the circulator preserves quantum coherence and entanglement of the input photons. To explicitly demonstrate this, we use a pair of correlated nondegenerate photons in the telecom band with a bandwidth of 2.3 GHz and only send the signal photon in port 1. We measure the correlation between the up-converted signal in port 4 and the idler (see section S10 for measurement details). The noise-subtracted coincidence counts (C0) and total coincidence counts (Ct) in the 2.3-GHz bandwidth are displayed in Fig. 3F. On the basis of this, the fidelity of the entangled photons after the circulator is found to be F=12(1+C0Ct)=0.968(6) (eq. S14).

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, we demonstrated optical nonreciprocity based on quantum frequency conversion that operates across a wide range of light levels—from individual photons to milliwatt powers—and spans a broad spectral range. Figure 4 compares key performance parameters of our isolator with other integrated nonmagnetic isolators demonstrated recently: Our isolator exhibits an isolation of 34 dB, competitive among integrated nonmagnetic approaches, together with low on-chip insertion loss and a broad isolation bandwidth. Because III to V semiconductors have weak electro-optic and piezoelectric effects, our approach based on optical nonlinearity provides a solution for integrated isolators on III to V platforms. Moreover, the same strategy can be extended to other nonlinear photonic materials, such as periodically poled LiNbO3, where extremely wide bandwidth of optical frequency conversion (>1 THz)—and thus isolation—can be achieved (41).

In terms of performance at the single-photon level, our integrated quantum-compatible optical isolator and circulator also outperform previous demonstrations based on atomic systems and magneto-optic effects in key aspects (tables S1 and S2). Our approach, implemented directly on a scalable platform, enables the construction of robust classical and quantum optical circuits. For example, arranging multiple four-port circulators in one- or two-dimensional arrays can realize programmable networks that guide light in specific directions. Integration with quantum emitters, such as quantum dot–embedded III to V semiconductors, would allow the construction of on-chip quantum light-matter interfaces with broken time-reversal symmetry (42, 43). With its inherent reconfigurability and quantum frequency conversion capabilities, this platform offers previously unidentified opportunities for directional quantum communication and noise-resilient quantum networks (3, 29, 44).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Device fabrication

The devices are fabricated from the 112 nm thick disordered In0.5Ga0.5P thin film grown on GaAs substrate by metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (T = 545 C, V/III = 48, precursors: trimethylindium, trimethylgallium and PH3). The device pattern is defined using electron beam lithography and 150 nm thick negative tone resist hydrogen silsesquioxane (HSQ). A 20 nm thick layer of silicon dioxide is deposited on InGaP via plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) to promote the adhesion of HSQ. The device pattern is transferred to InGaP layer via inductively coupled plasma reactive-ion etch (ICP-RIE) using a mixture of Cl2/CH4/Ar gas. After a short buffered oxide etch to remove the residual oxide (both HSQ and PECVD oxide), a layer of 35 nm thick aluminum oxide is deposited on the chip via atomic layer deposition. A second electron beam lithography and subsequent ICP-RIE using CHF3 gas are applied to pattern etch-through holes in the aluminum oxide layer for the undercut of the InGaP device. Next, a third electron beam lithography followed by electron-beam evaporation of 5 nm thick chromium and 20 nm thick gold is performed to define the electrodes. Finally, the InGaP device is released from the GaAs substrate using citric acid-based selective etching.

Phase-matching optimization

We use the nanoheater array to tune the phase-matching condition of the waveguide. Each nanoheater is 0.4-mm long, 400-nm wide, and 25-nm thick, which are individually controlled by a channel of a DC voltage source (NI 9264, 16-channel analog output module). We monitor the SFG signal in the 780-nm-band while fixing the pump in the 1550-nm-band and scanning the wavelength of the 1550-nm-band signal. We begin by heating the first waveguide segment closest to the input, which separates the SFG signal generated in the first segment from the rest of the spectrum. Next, we heat the second segment and align its SFG spectrum with that of the first segment and maximize the combined SFG peak intensity. This procedure is then repeated sequentially for the remaining segments. In practice, a second round of fine tuning is usually performed to further optimize the aligned SFG signal from the first round. We then optimize the extinction of the input 1550-nm-band signal. We switch the detector to monitor the transmission in the 1550-nm-band and perform another round of phase-matching fine tuning. In this round, we focus on the segments close to the waveguide input to minimize the signal transmission, since they are less effective in tuning the SFG signal because of the large loss of 780-nm-band light but can tune the extinction of the 1550-nm-band signal effectively.

Phase-matching characterization

To quantify the phase mismatch of a waveguide, we calculate the self-calibrated efficiency ratio of frequency conversion processes, e.g., SFG and difference-frequency generation (DFG), defined as (45, 46), R≡ηη0=ηαAL, η and η0 denote the peak efficiency of the measured and ideal frequency conversion spectrum, respectively, A=∫η(λ)dλ, and α=2π(dΔk/dλ)−1 is the simulated dispersion factor with Δk the wavevector mismatch between the modes for the respective conversion process. It should be noted that λ in the definition of A and α corresponds to the same mode. Because R is related to the ratio of η and A, it is free of calibration of the fiber coupling efficiency as well as the waveguide loss (46). R thus can be used to calibrate the quality of phase-matching tuning. We find R = 0.45 for the 6-mm-long waveguide after optimization. The discrepancy from the perfect phase matching condition is primarily due to the phase mismatch at length scales shorter than individual nanoheaters.
