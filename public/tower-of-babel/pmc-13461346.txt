Pericyte KATP channel hyperactivity redistributes cortical blood flow in a CADASIL mouse model
Authors: Danielle A. Jeffrey, Eric W. Prince, Niloufar Khakpour, Hannah R. Ferris, Colin H. Peters, Gregory Seedorf, Phinea Z. Romero, Mayra Bueno Guerrero, Abigail N. Russell, Katherine Glodoski, Gregory L. Futia, Stephanie K. Bonney, Michael A. Thornton, Emily A. Gibson, Catherine Proenza, Anastacia M. Garcia, Nikolaos M. Tsoukias, Fabrice Dabertrand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cerebral hemodynamic dysfunction is a key driver of unhealthy brain aging. Impaired microcirculatory reactivity leads to uneven perfusion, rendering deeper brain regions more vulnerable and thereby contributing to cognitive decline. Yet how capillaries contribute to these deficits remains poorly defined. Here we combined spatial transcriptomics with in vivo two-photon and three-photon imaging to measure layer-specific cerebral blood flow in control and small vessel disease model mice (CADASIL TgNotch3R169C). We found downregulation of ATP-synthesizing genes, indicating microvascular metabolic impairment that paralleled impaired pericyte bioenergetics. This energy deficit coincided with diminished tone in the arteriole–capillary transitional zone and reduced deep-layer perfusion. Complementary electrophysiology, ex vivo and in silico approaches, revealed that hyperactive KATP channels in pericytes drive a redistribution of cerebral blood flow toward superficial cortical layers. This loss of spatial perfusion equalization, despite preserved global flow, contributed to deep-layer hypoperfusion, establishing a previously underrecognized but tractable vascular function disrupted in aging pathology.

Main

The brain requires uninterrupted cerebral blood flow (CBF), but healthy cognition also hinges on well-established vascular functions: neurovascular coupling (NVC), blood–brain barrier integrity, autoregulation, waste clearance, immune homeostasis and trophic support1. With nearly 1 m of vessels per cubic mm (ref. 2), the neocortex maintains an average neuron-to-capillary distance of only ∼15 µm across its six layers (I–VI)3. However, capillary density, and thus vascular resistance, is uneven between cortical layers, paralleling differences in neuronal density and metabolic demand2,4,5. Furthermore, the architecture of penetrating arterioles perfusing this heterogeneous capillary network should bias flow toward superficial tissue as the path of least resistance. Yet red blood cell (RBC) flux remains largely uniform across layers6,7, even favoring deeper ones8,9. How this network ensures spatial uniformity of perfusion and global homeostasis, despite such marked anatomical and functional heterogeneity, remains poorly understood.

Disruptions in cerebral hemodynamics are now recognized as central drivers of unhealthy brain aging1,10. Small vessel disease (SVD), the leading vascular contributor to stroke and dementia and a frequent exacerbating factor in neurodegenerative pathologies, exemplifies this principle11,12. Cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) is a hereditary SVD caused by mutations in the NOTCH3 gene, leading to progressive degeneration of vascular smooth muscle cells (SMCs) and impaired CBF12. Experimental and clinical studies associate multiple SVD pathological manifestations with lower microcirculatory reactivity, defined as the capacity of brain arterioles and capillaries to dynamically adjust their diameter in response to vasoactive cues, metabolic needs and blood pressure changes11,12. Neuroimaging studies have revealed that deficits in microcirculatory reactivity, rather than reduced resting CBF, drive spatial heterogeneity in perfusion that disadvantages deeper brain regions13–17. This uneven perfusion appears to underpin the development of white matter hyperintensities14,16, enlarged perivascular spaces14,18 and heterogeneous transit times19,20, and is closely associated with lacunae, microbleeds, strokes and greater overall SVD burden18,21,22. These radiological hallmarks are strongly linked to accelerated cognitive decline in SVD and mixed dementia10,11, suggesting that a failure to maintain spatial perfusion homogeneity, even with preserved global CBF, represents a key mechanism connecting microvascular dysfunction to vascular cognitive impairment.

Cellular metabolism governs organ function and the brain’s vasculature is no exception. Among its molecular gatekeepers, the ATP-sensitive potassium (KATP) channel stands out for its ability to couple cellular excitability to metabolic state23. Recent studies have highlighted its prominent expression in capillary pericytes and its role in activity-dependent increases in local CBF24–26. A subset of pericytes, residing within the first four orders of capillary branches, define the arteriole–capillary transition (ACT) zone and exhibit fast, dynamic, contractile responses to blood pressure changes27,28. At capillary junctions, they actively determine the direction of blood flow, positioning them as key orchestrators of microcirculatory reactivity29. The ACT zone, proposed to preserve blood supply along arteriolar bifurcations30, has also recently emerged as a critical locus in stroke pathogenesis12,31. Altogether, these findings suggest that ACT pericytes are not only ideally positioned but also molecularly equipped to mediate spatial equalization of blood flow across cortical layers, which is essential for maintaining metabolic homeostasis in the face of anatomical heterogeneity. However, whether ACT pericytes fulfill this role, and how they are affected by the decline in metabolic capacity seen in aging, SVD and neurodegeneration, remain largely unexamined1,32,33.

To address this gap, we combined spatial transcriptomics with in vivo two-photon and three-photon imaging to measure layer-specific CBF in transgenic control and SVD model mice34. These studies were complemented by pericyte bioenergetic analysis, advanced ex vivo myography, electrophysiology and in silico modeling to provide a comprehensive view of how changes in microvascular reactivity affect cerebral perfusion. Our findings reveal a selective hyperactivity of KATP channels in ACT pericytes in SVD, driving a redistribution of CBF that favors superficial cortical layers at the expense of deeper ones. This loss of spatial perfusion equalization despite preserved global CBF is expected to contribute to the vulnerability of deep brain structures and white matter in SVD and mixed dementia. Our work identifies spatial perfusion equalization as an underrecognized vascular function, revealed by its disruption in SVD, and offers new therapeutic targets to restore flow homogeneity and mitigate age-related cognitive decline.

Results

SVD ACT zone exhibits larger diameters with reduced pericyte depolarization and pressure-induced constriction

We previously reported that brain pericytes in the ACT zone exist in a partially constricted state in response to pressure28, an intrinsic phenomenon reminiscent of the arterial myogenic tone described by William Bayliss in 1902 (ref. 35). As SVD is characterized by hemodynamic changes, we first sought to precisely compare pericyte responses to luminal pressure between wild-type (WT) and SVD mice within the ACT zone, both in vivo and ex vivo. To measure the level of pericyte capillary tone in vivo, we utilized acute cranial window implantation and acquired cortical z-stacks with two-photon laser scanning microscopy (TPLSM) in anesthetized mice. We visualized the microcirculation with tetramethylrhodamine (TRITC)-labeled dextran (Fig. 1a) and recreated the vascular network by using the network-creation tool of the Imaris software (Extended Data Fig. 1). Parenchymal arterioles (PAs) were identified as previously described28,36 (Extended Data Fig. 1) and first-order to fourth-order capillaries connecting to those PAs were selected for diameter measurements. A heatmap of lumen diameter was created where blue indicated a smaller diameter (such as capillaries) whereas magenta corresponded to a larger diameter, such as PAs (Fig. 1b). Unbiased computational analysis with blinded images revealed that basal diameters of WT ACT capillaries were significantly smaller compared to SVD with an average of 5.56 ± 0.29 μm versus 6.64 ± 0.14 μm (Fig. 1c). We then calculated the amount of tonic constriction under basal conditions by applying artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) depleted of Ca2+ to induce maximal dilation. We found that SVD capillaries had significantly less tone compared to WT controls: WT 31.72 ± 1.95% versus SVD 20.17 ± 0.89% (Fig. 1d). Microvascular tortuosity (filtered diameter <7.5 μm) was slightly increased in SVD (1.41 ± 0.22 versus 1.51 ± 0.23; Extended Data Fig. 2a), although the <1% change falls within physiological variability4 and likely impacts blood flow minimally (Extended Data Fig. 2b). In addition, we interrogated microvascular area fraction as a percentage (Extended Data Fig. 2c) in WT and SVD and found no statistical differences in the upper regions of the cortex (up to 250 μm deep) with 4.75 ± 0.64% in WT versus 4.91 ± 0.32% (Extended Data Fig. 2d). Branch points or offshoots of PA comparison at 250 μm deep within the cortex did not significantly differ between WT and SVD (Extended Data Fig. 2e–g; 1.17 ± 0.17 for WT and 1.21 ± 0.18 for SVD). Pressure-induced constriction in ACT pericytes involves a shift of the plasma membrane potential toward more depolarized values28. To test whether this response is altered in SVD pericytes within the ACT zone, we used our CaPA pressurized system (holding pressure: 40 mm Hg) together with sharp electrode recordings (Fig. 2d). Inclusion of Alexa Fluor 633 hydrazide in the recording micropipette (Fig. 2d) confirmed that membrane potentials were recorded from pericytes and not from underlying endothelial cells. Representative traces from WT and SVD pericytes are displayed in Fig. 2d. We found that ACT pericytes were significantly more hyperpolarized at −51.76 ± 2.72 mV compared to WT resting membrane potentials at −29.04 ± 1.60 mV (Fig. 2f). In SMCs, we previously attributed SVD-induced impairment of membrane potential and arteriolar myogenic tone to upregulation of the voltage-gated K+ (KV1.5) channel, as evidenced by increased sensitivity to the KV1 inhibitor 4-aminopyridine38. Consistent with this voltage dependency, the reduction in arteriolar tone emerges at pressures >40 mm Hg as SMC membranes depolarize. In contrast, tone in ACT pericytes was impaired across all pressure levels, suggesting a distinct underlying mechanism. Supporting this, 1 mM 4-aminopyridine had no differential effect between WT and SVD mice ex vivo (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b), indicating that KV1.5 channels are not upregulated in these cells. Furthermore, depolarization with 60 mM K+ to cause membrane depolarization induced comparable constriction in both groups (Extended Data Fig. 4c), demonstrating preserved voltage-gated Ca2+ channel activity and contractile capacity in ACT pericytes. Collectively, these results support heightened pericyte hyperpolarization in SVD impairing pressure-induced constriction within the ACT zone, leading to increased capillary diameter. To investigate the mechanism underlying this defect, we next adopted a broad-spectrum approach.

Reduced gene expression involving ATP synthesis in SVD brain samples

We investigated gene expression changes in WT and SVD mice to identify potential mechanisms contributing to pericyte dysfunction by performing spatial transcriptomics on coronal brain slices using 10x Genomics Visium and Visium HD platforms (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 5e). A comparable number of spots and genes detected between samples is shown in Extended Data Fig. 5e. In addition, we introduced a new use of the Visium platform to spatially profile isolated microvasculature, which was essential for capturing key microvascular populations, such as ACT pericytes, by preserving vascular branch order (Extended Data Fig. 5c). A subset of canonical CADASIL-associated genes is also shown in the isolated vasculature (Extended Data Fig. 6a). In the Visium approach on isolated vasculature and slices, we first identified several differentially expressed genes consistently present in both brain slices and isolated vascular samples (Extended Data Fig. 6b), with 16.2% of dysregulated genes shared across the two input types. Notable genes included Sparc, Itm2b and Atp1b2, which are involved in extracellular matrix regulation, neurodegeneration and extracellular K+ homeostasis, respectively. Protein–protein interaction enrichment analysis further highlighted dysregulation of multiple ATP synthase genes and alterations in Notch3 signaling (Extended Data Fig. 6c,d). Metascape39 pathway analysis of significantly downregulated genes in SVD-isolated microvasculature revealed disruptions, including electron transport chain activity (Fig. 3b). Visium brain slice data revealed that ATP production via chemiosmotic coupling was the most significantly downregulated pathway (−log10(P) = 24.97) (Fig. 3b).

To refine our interrogation to almost single-cell level, we used high-resolution spatial transcriptomic profiling with Visium HD. Data analysis revealed different cell types across the brain using a Human Biomolecular Atlas Project annotation database combined with Azimuth reference-based mapping40 (Extended Data Fig. 6e). Cell markers and expression levels are displayed in Extended Data Fig. 7. Spatial transcriptional enrichment highlighted mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (MitoCarta 2.041) machinery being decreased in the SVD brain slice, particularly in the cortical region (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, differential expression analysis uncovered global downregulation of genes involved in ATP synthesis in both inputs, echoing our results from our previous Visium brain slice analysis (Fig. 3d). Endothelium-specific pathway analysis revealed decreased elastin fiber development and regulation of SMC contraction (Fig. 3e). For pericytes, differential expression analysis identified downregulation of several metabolic pathways, including ATP synthase complex assembly and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 3f), a finding consistent with previous reports from a pericyte-specific CADASIL model42. SVD pericytes present reduced bioenergetics

To functionally assess mitochondrial bioenergetics, we isolated intracerebral pericytes from both WT and SVD mice. Cells were cultured for 72 h before performing a Seahorse Mitochondrial Stress Test assay. Confirmation of cell-type isolation was validated by using positive α-GFP fluorescence staining in our SMMHC-GCaMP6f mice and positive α-platelet-derived growth factor receptor β (α-PDGFRβ) and α-ATP13a5 to further delineate between SMCs and pericytes (Fig. 3g). Consistent with the transcriptomics analysis, oxygen consumption rates (OCRs) appeared reduced in SVD versus WT pericytes (Fig. 3g), with significant reductions in maximal OCR (Fig. 3h; 104.2 ± 39.02 pmol min−1 per 20,000 cells in WT and 70.33 ± 28.73 pmol min−1 per 20,000 cells in SVD) and nonmitochondrial oxygen consumption (Fig. 3i; 54.04 ± 6.13 versus 35.19 ± 2.51 pmol min−1 per 20,000 cells). In addition, the significant decrease in the bioenergetic balance (OCR to extracellular acidification rate (ECAR)) observed in SVD pericytes (Fig. 3j; 6.05 ± 0.64 in WT and 4.12 ± 0.67 in SVD mice) suggests a metabolic shift toward glycolysis. Full Seahorse parameter results are reported in Supplementary Table 1. Together, these findings suggest that the microvascular unit undergoes both localized and global bioenergetic stress.

KATP channel basal activity is more pronounced in SVD ACT pericytes

Considering spatial transcriptomic and functional evidence pointing to disrupted energy homeostasis in ACT pericytes, we next focused on the hetero-octameric complex Kir6.1 (Kcnj8) and SUR2B (Abcc9) channel, a potassium KATP channel recently identified as highly expressed in pericytes43, implicated in membrane potential and CBF regulation24,25. As KATP channel gating is governed by the intracellular ATP-to-ADP ratio, reduced ATP levels directly promote channel opening, leading to membrane hyperpolarization24. These properties make Kir6.1 a compelling candidate for mediating the observed dysfunction in ACT pericytes under metabolically compromised conditions. We utilized whole-cell, perforated, patch-clamp electrophysiology on isolated, intact, unpressurized microvasculature. This technically challenging approach was deliberately chosen over isolated cell recordings to ensure selective targeting of pericytes within the ACT zone, thereby avoiding contamination from other pericyte subtypes with slower contractile kinetics (workflow shown in Fig. 4a). On a 2-s voltage ramp from −100 mV to +40 mV (Fig. 4b), we recorded both basal currents and currents in the presence of the KATP antagonist glibenclamide. At −70 mV, SVD ACT pericytes displayed a significantly larger basal inward current, −70.50 ± 17.19 pA, compared to WT controls, −20.95 ± 6.85 pA (Fig. 4c,d). To determine whether this elevated basal current was attributable to KATP channel activation, we perfused 10 μM glibenclamide over the microvasculature. To further investigate deviations in KATP channel function, we leveraged our ex vivo CaPA methodology and tested functional responses to both KATP channel agonist pinacidil (1 and 10 µM) and antagonist glibenclamide (10 µM) (Fig. 4e,f). In preparations from SVD mice, vasodilatory responses to pinacidil of the ACT zone were notably blunted compared to WT controls, which exhibited robust dilations (Fig. 4g). This difference was evident at both 1 μM and 10 μM concentrations. Specifically, WT capillaries showed an 18.75 ± 2.4% increase in diameter at 1 μM pinacidil, whereas SVD capillaries only dilated by 6.79 ± 1.16% (Fig. 4h). Increasing the concentration to 10 μM pinacidil enhanced the response in both groups, but the difference remained pronounced: WT capillaries dilated by 43.63 ± 4.53%, whereas SVD capillaries showed a more modest 16.73 ± 3.84% increase (Fig. 4d). This blunted response could reflect KATP channel dysfunction, downregulation or, paradoxically, increased basal activity. To distinguish between these possibilities, we applied the KATP channel antagonist glibenclamide under resting conditions. Previous studies have shown that glibenclamide typically elicits minimal constriction in capillaries at rest due to low basal KATP channel activity25. Consistent with this, WT capillaries exhibited only a modest −10.2 ± 1.65% decrease in diameter in response to 10 μM glibenclamide.

In contrast, SVD capillaries responded with a robust vasoconstriction of −28.94 ± 3.56%, indicating elevated basal activity in the ACT zone (Fig. 4g,i). Supplementary Video 1 shows a representative glibenclamide-induced constriction in a pressurized (40 mmHg) SVD CaPA preparation illustrating the pronounced response. Finally, we tested the impact of metabolism on KATP channel activity by manipulating extracellular glucose concentration. At low glucose levels (0.75 mM), and consistent with an increased KATP channel activity in low metabolic states, glibenclamide-induced constriction in the WT ACT zone was increased (Fig. 4k,l; −26.22 ± 2.5% versus −10.57 ± 1.3% in 4 mM glucose). Unexpectedly, the SVD ACT zone displayed a reduced response to glibenclamide at low glucose (Fig. 4k,l; 0.75 mM, −7.24 ± 2.6%), which may reveal a complete loss of contractile ability. Importantly, high glucose conditions (8 mM) reduced glibenclamide-induced constriction in both the WT and SVD ACT zones compared to physiological glucose levels of 4 mM (Fig. 4k,l; −12.10 ± 1.9% versus −25.57 ± 3.1 for SVD and −4.16 ± 1.38% versus −10.57 ± 1.35%). Overall, these results support the conclusion that KATP channels are aberrantly active under basal conditions in SVD ACT pericytes compared to the quiescent state observed in WT controls.

KATP channel hyperactivity in the SVD ACT zone, in vivo

To extend our KATP channel interrogation in vivo and investigate larger network consequences, we employed acute cranial window implantation combined with TPLSM (Fig. 5a–c and Extended Data Fig. 1). Imaris was again utilized to quantify mean segment diameter in the ACT zone (Fig. 5a–c). Perfusion of 10 μM pinacidil over the cranial window elicited a vasodilatory response at the ACT zone that was significantly larger in WT compared to SVD capillaries (29.52 ± 1.88% versus 17.15 ± 1.28%). Pinacidil, 100 μM, also exhibited differences, with WT capillaries dilating by 39.79 ± 3.69%, compared to SVD capillaries, 25.50 ± 4.06% (Fig. 5d). In addition, the KATP antagonist, glibenclamide, also induced a robust vasoconstriction at 10 μM in the SVD ACT zone, with an average of −14.31 ± 0.58% compared to −0.06 ± 0.97% for the WT ACT zone. To assess the impact of these effects on CBF, simulations were performed using reconstructed cerebral networks (Fig. 6d) under control conditions (WT) and after capillary diameter relaxation by the increased KATP channel currents. Pressure distributions (Fig. 6f) and tone–pressure relationships (Fig. 6c) were used to determine ACT zone diameters in each simulation scenario. Predicted diameters in SVD were typically 25–45% larger than in WT (Fig. 6g). We next examined how blood supply from the PAs was distributed to the capillary network along different cortical layers: approximately 120 capillaries stem from PAs in layers I–III, 78 in layer IV, 85 in layer V and 110 in layer VI. Under control conditions the flow distribution was 38% in layers I–III, 25% in layer IV, 20% in layer V and 17% in layer VI (Fig. 6h). In SVD simulations, this distribution shifted, with a notable increase in flow to layers I–III and a corresponding decrease in flow to layers V and VI (Fig. 6i). Under conditions of constant blood supply this could lead to hypoperfusion of the deeper regions of the brain. We previously provided evidence of attenuation of PA tone in SVD38. Simulations were thus performed examining the impact of larger PA diameter values in SVD. To establish a range of different scenarios, network simulations were further performed in two vascular reconstructions with varying capillary contributions to overall microvascular hydrodynamic resistance (34% and 55%)44,45. Predicted hypoperfusion of deep layers was more pronounced when overall capillary resistance was high: layer VI flow fraction reduction, relative to WT, increased from 11% in the low-capillary resistance network to 41% in the high-resistance ones (Extended Data Fig. 9). It is interesting that simulations predicted that PA dilation can partially mitigate the reduction in flow to deep regions. Thus, blunted myogenic tone in both the PA and the ACT zones appears to exert opposing effects: whereas KATP channel hyperactivity in ACT pericytes drives redistribution of blood flow away from deep territories, compensatory PA dilation may help lessen this imbalance. Nevertheless, the net outcome of a 5% dilation, as measured in SVD38, has reduced perfusion of deep cortical layers across both high-resistance and low-resistance scenarios, highlighting the dominant role of ACT zone dysfunction in SVD pathophysiology.

Discussion

How blood flow remains spatially balanced across cortical depth has remained unknown. We identify a previously underrecognized function within the ACT zone that equalizes perfusion through pressure-sensitive pericyte regulation of local resistance. This homeostatic function becomes evident when disrupted in SVD, leading to a decoupling of total flow from its spatial distribution. Using multiscale approaches combining realistic network modeling with two-photon and three-photon imaging, we observed dilation of arteriole-proximal capillaries and a diversion of blood flow toward superficial cortical layers. Spatial transcriptomics revealed reduced expression of ATP-synthesizing genes in ACT pericytes, suggesting that metabolic dysfunction underlies their impaired flow regulation. Electrophysiology, ex vivo myography and in vivo two-photon imaging further demonstrated that hyperactivation of KATP channels in ACT pericytes compromises their pressure-sensitive contractility. Unable to adjust resistance in response to local pressure changes, these pericytes allow blood flow to be diverted toward the path of least resistance, that is, the superficial cortical layers. Deeper regions thus become underperfused despite preserved total perfusion, establishing a direct link between microvascular dysfunction and the selective vulnerability of deep brain tissue.

Our previous work established that ACT pericytes exhibit pressure-induced depolarization and constriction to maintain basal tone, a hallmark of myogenic behavior traditionally attributed to arteriolar SMCs28. This intrinsic contractility allows rapid dilation or further constriction and can enable ACT pericytes to dynamically regulate vascular resistance in real time. Unlike cerebrovascular SMCs, ACT pericytes display functional KATP channels25 and reside on post-arteriolar capillary segments, where they can actively regulate flow directions at bifurcations and perfusion at the local network scale29. Similarly, pre-capillary sphincters (a ring of contractile smooth muscle-like cells) are present in 28% of junctions between arterioles and capillaries, predominantly in the upper cortical layers30. These focal changes in vessel diameter can create a high-resistance bottleneck for local CBF control and may be disrupted with aging46. However, to what extent small-diameter changes in proximal capillaries alter hydrodynamic resistance, CBF or RBC fluxes needs to be further investigated. Simulations in this study show that tone in the ACT zone can regulate resistance to flow and thus control perfusion of the capillary network. This prediction further implies that loss of pericyte tone in SVD will limit the network’s ability for on-demand increase of local blood supply. Although α-chloralose and urethane preserve cerebrovascular reactivity better than isoflurane, they still exert mild vasodilatory effects, which may influence baseline vessel diameter. In addition, to minimize potential confounding effects of postsurgical inflammation, in vivo imaging was performed shortly after surgery. Importantly, simulations and supporting experimental data also indicate that ACT zone tone can modulate the distribution of CBF along cortical layers. Loss of pericyte tone in SVD appears to lead to perfusion imbalance favoring top cortical layers at the expense of deeper, more vulnerable regions. On the other hand, increased contractility of ACT zone pericytes may also lead to hypoperfusion in deep brain regions8. Therefore, the unique combination of pericytes’ intrinsic tone and their crucial presence at key vascular junctions supports the brain’s angioarchitecture relying on fine-tuned resistance adjustments by ACT pericytes to maintain appropriate perfusion across cortical layers with anatomical and metabolic heterogeneity.

In the present study, we used the TgNotch3R169C mouse model of CADASIL, the most common heritable cause of stroke and vascular dementia, as a lens to investigate pathomechanisms driving more common, yet clinically indistinct, sporadic forms of SVDs12,47. Although this model has been extensively characterized in SMCs and endothelial cells (ECs), our findings reveal a previously underappreciated vulnerability of pericytes in the early stages of the disease. In SMCs and capillary ECs (cECs), extracellular matrix dysregulation drives upregulation of KV1.5 channels and partial inhibition of Kir2.1 channels, impairing arteriolar tone and NVC, respectively34,38. Notably, reduced arteriolar tone in silico tended to favor perfusion of deeper cortical layers, further underscoring the central role of increased ACT zone diameter in driving deep hypoperfusion. It is plausible that extracellular matrix disruption also contributes to metabolic dysfunction in ACT pericytes, although this remains to be directly tested. Leveraging spatial transcriptomics, we identified a downregulation of ATP-synthesizing genes at both tissue and vascular levels, consistent with prior reports on CADASIL33,42,48, and more broadly in neurovascular aging and age-related diseases1,32. In line with this, our functional data demonstrate reduced energetic capacity in SVD pericytes, suggesting increased vulnerability of the microvasculature to additional stressors. It has been proposed that neuronal activity, on glucose depletion, triggers KATP-dependent pericyte relaxation to increase blood flow during neurovascular coupling24. Disruption of this pathway in SVD ACT pericytes would be expected to blunt this response. Lower nonmitochondrial oxygen consumption with a decrease in bioenergetic balance may indicate even broader disruptions in signaling or vascular regulation and a shift toward glycolysis. Although briefly helpful, this shift would become maladaptive over time by promoting lactate accumulation and limiting sustained cellular support. Whether the decrease in ATP that we reported, or the ATP-to-ADP ratio, is directly responsible for KATP channel hyperactivity remains uncertain. Lowering glucose to <1 mM has been shown to cause a KATP channel-dependent profound hyperpolarization of pericytes; however, in vascular myocytes dialyzed with low ATP (100 µM), whole-cell currents remained below half-maximal activation24,49. In our experiments, 0.75 mM glucose increased KATP activity in the WT ACT zone, whereas high glucose (8 mM) significantly decreased KATP activity in the SVD ACT zone. Surprisingly, SVD ACT pericytes lost their hypersensitivity to glibenclamide in 0.75 mM glucose. In parallel, pericytes also failed to elevate oxygen consumption in our bioenergetics assay, suggesting that a minimum baseline level of glucose may be required to sustain constriction in the SVD ACT zone. Other intracellular pathways such as protein kinase A have also been shown to activate KATP channels49. Therefore, the direct mechanism of KATP channel activation in SVD warrants further investigation, whereas our observations and those of others point toward impaired cellular metabolism as a key driver.

Endothelial dysfunction plays a key role in the context of cerebral SVD and vascular dementia, including the formation of white matter lesions1. In the TgNotch3R169C model, cECs exhibit reduced ATP concentrations34 and express KATP channels25, suggesting a potential contribution to the diminished ACT tone. However, the KATP channel current density in cECs is markedly lower, approximately one-third that of pericytes25, insufficient to drive upstream hyperpolarization during NVC24. In contrast, selective pericyte ablation abolishes KATP channel-dependent contributions to neurovascular coupling (NVC)26, underscoring the critical role of the pericyte KATP channel in regulating CBF. Experimental data corroborated by computational analysis in this study demonstrate that increasing KATP current in ACT pericytes is sufficient to induce relevant levels of hyperpolarization, increase ACT capillary diameter and redistribute CBF, although additional mechanisms are likely involved. This supports impaired pericyte contractility disrupting spatial perfusion equalization and reduces the vasodilatory reserve, thereby also limiting NVC responses. Aggravation of white matter lesions may then be further compounded by compromised blood–brain barrier integrity due to endothelial fragility. Consistent with this proposed sequence, TgNotch3R169C mice display early signs of myelin disruption by 6 months of age50, progressing to substantial white matter burden by 20 months47, despite the absence of overt blood–brain barrier leakage. Notably, patients with Cantú’s syndrome, caused by a gain-of-function mutation in the KATP channel, present dilated cerebral blood vessels and white matter lesions51. This is further supported by recent evidence showing that chronic hemodynamic alterations, driven by age-dependent vascular remodeling, deprive deeper brain layers of adequate perfusion, contributing to gliosis and demyelination in mouse white matter8. As SVD progresses toward vascular infarcts, anatomopathological changes in the ACT zone become increasingly evident. The Joutel group demonstrated that ACT zone hyperplasia directly drives hemorrhagic stroke through maladaptive vascular remodeling and vessel rupture31. In contrast, ACT hypoplasia is observed in ischemic SVD models12, indicating that the structural state of the ACT zone critically shapes the disease trajectory. Dysregulated resistance control by ACT pericytes, whether excessive or insufficient, may thus determine whether SVD evolves toward ischemic or hemorrhagic phenotypes.

Taken together, our findings reveal a previously underrecognized mechanism of CBF dysregulation, driven by basal KATP channel hyperactivation in the ACT zone pericytes. This highlights the distinct role of microvascular compartments in unhealthy brain aging. Although the KATP antagonist, glibenclamide, is approved by the US Food and Drug Administration, its systemic effects and poor brain penetration limit its use in SVD. The broad expression of KATP channels, particularly in pancreatic β-cells and cardiac tissue, raises concerns about the potential off-target consequences of direct pharmacological inhibition. Furthermore, radiological hallmarks such as white matter hyperintensities, microbleeds and enlarged perivascular spaces are better interpreted as downstream consequences of vascular dysfunction rather than primary therapeutic targets. Therefore, restoring metabolic integrity may offer a more precise and effective strategy to reduce basal ACT pericyte KATP channel hyperactivity. Future investigations should focus on identifying upstream drivers of ATP depletion, which likely include mitochondrial dysfunction. Addressing these deficits could restore ACT pericyte function, preserve cortical perfusion homogeneity and provide a promising avenue for intervention in vascular cognitive impairment.

Methods

Animals

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus (protocol no. 00817). Male TgNotch3WT (WT) and TgNotch3R169C (SVD) mice (aged 6–7 months) on an FVB/N background were used. These transgenic lines, which model CADASIL-associated SVD, have been described previously52. To visualize mural cells, both lines were crossed with SMMHC-GCaMP6f mice37 and GCaMP6f expression was induced by tamoxifen-containing chow at 16 weeks of age. Genotypes were confirmed by TransnetYX. Mice were housed under standard conditions with free access to food and water. Animals were euthanized by a sodium pentobarbital overdose (100 mg kg−1, intraperitoneal) followed by decapitation.

Acute cranial window implantation in vivo

Windows were implanted as previously described36. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (5% induction, 2% maintenance) and transitioned to α-chloralose (50 mg kg−1) and urethane (750 mg kg−1). TRITC-dextran (10 mg ml−1) was administered by retro-orbital injection to visualize the vasculature. An ~3-mm cranial window was created over the somatosensory cortex, the dura was removed and a customized head plate was secured to the skull. Body temperature was maintained at 37 °C throughout surgery and imaging.

Assessment by pressure myography

Cortex PAs flanking the middle cerebral artery were obtained as previously described37. Definition and characterization of ACT zone

ACT zone pericytes were identified as previously described28, using branch order (first-order to fourth-order capillaries), mural-cell morphology, Myh11 expression, loss of elastin staining with Alexa Fluor 633 hydrazide and vessel diameter criterion (<7.5 μm) (Extended Data Fig. 3).

Membrane potential measurements

These were performed as previously dsecribed28.

Tissue processing and sequencing acquisition for Visium spatial RNA data generation

Brains were rapidly collected, flash-frozen in isopentane, cryosectioned (10 μm) and mounted on to Visium slides. Coronal sections were obtained according to the Franklin and Paxinos mouse brain atlas53. Spatial transcriptomic analyses were performed using Visium HD on brain sections (n = 2 for both WT and SVD) and standard Visium on brain sections (n = 1 for both WT and SVD) and isolated microvasculature (n = 3 for both WT and SVD). Isolated vasculature was mounted on SYLGARD-coated slides and methanol fixed before processing. After hematoxylin and eosin imaging, tissue sections were permeabilized and libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocols (10x Genomics). Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) using paired-end 150-bp reads at a depth of approximately 70,000 read-pairs per spot. Sequencing data were processed using Space Ranger (10x Genomics).

Gene expression clustering and analysis

Reads were aligned to the murine reference genome (mm10) and spatial gene expression matrices were generated. Histological images were aligned with spatial barcode maps to enable spot-level transcript quantification. Visium HD samples were analyzed at 8-µm resolution. All downstream analyses were performed primarily using Seurat (v5), including quality control, normalization, integration, clustering and visualization. To achieve single-cell-like resolution, cell-type deconvolution was performed using the runAzimuth function from the Azimuth package40, with the mouse cortex reference dataset for annotation. Microvascular unit components, including SMCs, ECs and pericytes, were identified based on canonical marker expression (Extended Data Fig. 7). Differential gene expression analysis between WT and SVD conditions was conducted using Seurat’s FindMarkers function with Wilcoxon’s rank-sum test with default parameters (log(fold-change) threshold (logfc.threshold) = 0.25, min.pct = 0.1), with Bonferroni’s correction for multiple testing and a Padj threshold of <0.05 for significance. Genes involved in ATP synthesis and oxidative phosphorylation (for example, Atp5a1, Ndufa4 and Cox4i1, from MitoCarta 2.041) were specifically interrogated. Pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed using Metascape and Enrichr web applications. For spatial transcriptomic data (Visium and Visium HD), enrichment scores were calculated using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function and gene set variation analysis (GSVA) via the Bioconductor escape package (v1.12.0) with the following parameters: method = ‘GSVA’, groups = 1000, min.size = 5. For Visium HD data, gene sets related to ATP and metabolism were extracted from the MsigDB Hallmark and Gene Ontology Biological Process collections (n = 549). GSVA-derived enrichment scores for these gene sets were then compared between SVD and WT samples using Seurat’s FindMarkers function (Wilcoxon’s rank-sum test, Bonferroni’s correction, Padj < 0.05) to identify significantly dysregulated pathways. Visualization was performed using Seurat’s built-in functions alongside customized R scripts for specialized analyses, including spatial gene expression maps, cluster heatmaps and pathway enrichment plots. Colocalization of transcriptomic data with histological features was additionally visualized using Loupe Browser.

Pericyte culture methods

Pericytes were isolated as described in ref. 54. Briefly, brain tissue was enzymatically dissociated with Liberase TL and DNase, followed by myelin removal, filtration and magnetic depletion of nonmural cells. The remaining cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and an antibiotic–antimycotic and maintained on Seahorse XFp PDL miniplates at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 72 h. Pericyte purity was assessed by immunofluorescence using Tg129xSMMHC-GCaMP6f mice. All isolated cells expressed GFP (Invitrogen, cat. no. A10262, 1:1,000; α-chicken-488 secondary, Invitrogen, cat. no. A32931TR, 1:5,000), confirming mural-cell identity. Cells were further characterized using PDGFRβ (Invitrogen, cat. no. 14-1402-82, 1:1,000, mural-cell marker; α-rat secondary, Invitrogen, cat. no. A11006, 1:5,000) and ATP13a5 (Abcam, cat. no. ab181269 1:1,000; α-rabbit secondary, Abcam, cat. no. ab150077, 1:5,000 pericyte marker) immunostaining to distinguish SMCs from pericytes (Fig. 3g). Most isolated cells expressed ATP13a5, whereas <15% expressed PDGFRβ alone, indicating minimal SMC contamination.

Mitochondrial bioenergetic analysis in isolated primary pericytes

Pericytes were cultured on PDL-coated Seahorse XFp miniplates at a density of 20,000 cells per well. WT and SVD cells were plated in triplicate across four independent experiments, with paired WT and SVD samples analyzed on the same plate under identical conditions. Mitochondrial respiration was assessed using the Cell Mito Stress Test on an Agilent Seahorse XFp Bioanalyzer. Before analysis, cells were incubated in Seahorse assay medium (Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine and 10 mM glucose, pH 7.4). Oligomycin (1.0 µM), FCCP (1.0 µM) and combined rotenone and antimycin A (0.5 µM each) were sequentially applied according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After the assay, live-cell numbers were determined using the CyQUANT Direct Cell Proliferation Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and OCR values were normalized to cell numbers and expressed as pmol min−1 per live cell. To account for variability across cell isolation preparations55, data were further normalized. Seahorse XF metabolic parameters (OCR and ECAR) were normalized by expressing disease (SVD) values relative to paired WT controls run on the same plate. For each parameter, values were calculated as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\left({{\mathrm{SVD}}}-{{\mathrm{WT}}}\right)}{{{\mathrm{WT}}}}.$$\end{document}(SVD−WT)WT.

This transformation represents the relative change compared to WT, providing a proportional measure of increase or decrease in SVD samples. This approach is mathematically equivalent to commonly used normalization strategies in Seahorse analyses and facilitates direct comparison between experimental conditions by anchoring measurements to an internal WT reference. Data are reported as the relative change to WT where WT was set to 0.

Electrophysiology

Isolated microvasculature was collected as described above and adhered to SYLGARD-coated coverslips. Preparations were transferred to a recording chamber and superfused (~1 ml min−1) with extracellular solution containing (mM): 80 NaCl, 60 KCl, 1 MgCl2, 0.1 CaCl2, 10 glucose and 10 Hepes, pH 7.4 at 36 °C.

Pericytes were recorded using the amphotericin-perforated, patch-clamp configuration. Perforation solution was prepared by adding 20 mg ml−1 of amphotericin B (Apexbio Technology Llc) to 50 mg ml−1 of Pluronic F-127 (Anatrace) solubilized in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Patch pipettes (5–7 MΩ) were filled with intracellular solution containing (mM): 102 KCl, 38 KOH, 10 NaCl, 1 MgCl2, 1 CaCl2, 10 (ethylenebis(oxonitrilo))tetra-acetate, 10 glucose and 10 Hepes, pH 7.2. Recordings were performed using an Axopatch 200B amplifier, Digidata 144A and ClampEx software (Molecular Devices). Currents were acquired at 10 kHz and measured using a 100-ms step to −100 mV followed by a 2-s voltage ramp to +40 mV from a holding potential of −50 mV.

Basal inward current was measured at −100 mV after perforation. Glibenclamide-sensitive IKATP was determined by subtracting the current measured in the presence of 10 µM glibenclamide from the basal current. Current amplitudes were calculated from the average of three consecutive traces.

Live-cell ATP

This was performed as previously described34. Our CaPAs were then cannulated and pressurized as previously described. Vasculature was then perfused with aCSF to wash off unbound live-cell ATP for 10 min before imaging.

Reagents

Drug stocks were prepared in DMSO and diluted in aCSF immediately before use. Vehicle controls were matched for DMSO concentration. Pinacidil (KATP agonist; MedChemExpress, cat. no. 50-187-3018) and glibenclamide (KATP antagonist; MedChemExpress, cat. no. NC1779907) were continuously applied during ex vivo and in vivo experiments. All other reagents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.

Two-photon and confocal fluorescent image acquisition, processing and analysis

In vivo acute cranial window TPLSM was performed using a Bruker Ultima Investigator multiphoton microscope with Prairie View software and Spectra-Physics Mai Tai eHP DeepSee ultrafast laser at 810 nm as previously described37. Green and red emissions were collected using 525/70 nm and 595/50 nm bandpass filters, respectively, and detected with GaASP photomultiplier tubes. For imaging the vasculature, a Nikon ×16 (0.80 numerical aperture (NA), cat. no. CFI75 LWD) water-immersion objective was employed. To capture the vascular network and quantify changes in blood vessel diameter, z-stacks were acquired at 1.0-μm intervals with a resolution of 1,024 × 1,024 pixels2 and a dwell time of 3.6 μs pixel−1. PAs and corresponding capillaries were identified by the direction of RBC flow into the brain as previously described36. ATP live-cell quantification and pericyte sharp electrode membrane potential validation were accomplished using confocal microscopy z-stack acquisition on a Nikon A1R Ti2 inverted laser scanning confocal microscope with a CFI60 Apochromat Lambda S LWD ×20 water-immersion objective lens (NA 0.95, working distance 0.95 mm, field of view 22 mm, differential interference contrast) or ×40 oil-immersion objective lens (NA 1.3, working distance 0.2 mm, field of view 25 mm, differential interference contrast) with 0.22-μm step sizes to capture and appreciate the microvascular architectural integrity and branch order. All z-stacks were captured using the same acquisition conditions for all samples with the same x, y and z coordinates.

All images were then blinded before uploading into the Imaris software arena for analysis. Quantitative analysis of in vivo microvascular diameter changes and ATP live-cell z-stack images was performed using Imaris microscopy image analysis software (v10.1.). A Gaussian filter was applied to all images consistently to reduce noise. For in vivo analysis, surfaces were then created using Imaris’s internal machine learning feature to create the skeletonization of the microvasculature lumina using TRITC-dextran to indicate vascular lumina as previously described28. Briefly, the same trained algorithm (classifier) was used for pre-z-stack or post-z-stack images to avoid bias. Once lumen surfaces were created then the filament feature was used to quantify microvascular segment diameters pre-treatment and post-treatment. Filament structures in Imaris are built by stacking frustums (cone shapes) end to end. The mean value of all segment diameters measured from the center to the segment surfaces was calculated using the lower threshold value. Imaris filament-rendered vessel diameters were randomly checked with three microvascular segments with full-width half-maximum and VasoMetrics56 in FIJI (Extended Data Fig. 1), as previously described28; only those with only identical (same location and branch order) vessel segments were used to measure pre-treatment or post-treatment. We found there to be no statistical differences between conventional full-width half-maximum and Imaris-rendered filaments (Extended Data Fig. 1). For ATP live-cells the same plugin was used to identify puncta and create surfaces. The volumes of each pericyte soma were then averaged per mouse to give the volume of puncta per respective input. Extended Data Figs. 1 and 9 show our Imaris quantitative workflow for both in vivo vascular diameter analysis and live-cell ATP surface creation. For in vivo studies post-network creation in Imaris, the average vessel diameter was calculated for three to five different capillaries in first-order to fourth-order branched locations. Figure 1a shows the network creation filament application using Imaris and the detailed workflow is described in Extended Data Fig. 1.

Computational modeling

A multiscale modeling approach was utilized to predict how changes in pericyte electrophysiology in SVD can affect brain perfusion. A previously developed mathematical model of vascular smooth muscle electrophysiology that accounts for key membrane and intracellular store components was adapted for cerebral capillary pericytes57,58. The pericyte model incorporates cell-specific current densities for main transmembrane currents previously characterized (calcium-activated K+, NSC, KATP, Kir and VOCC). Membrane potential dynamics are predicted using a Hodgkin–Huxley formalism and mass balance equations account for the concentration of free Ca2+ in the cytosol and in the stores (Fig. 6a). A minimal biomechanics model57 translates changes in pericyte Ca2+ levels to changes in vessel diameter. It incorporates the passive elastic properties of a vessel wall and active mural-cell contraction. Myogenic tone is simulated by accounting for stress-induced activation of NSC channels that triggers depolarization, transmembrane Ca2+ influx and constrictions when pressure increases. Biomechanics parameters were optimized to recapitulate the diameter responses to pressure steps under control passive (0 Ca2+) and active conditions (WT) in Fig. 2a (‘working’ SVD model accounts for increased KATP activity in ACT zone pericytes). Pericyte membrane potential and diameter changes were predicted on increasing KATP activity to the levels observed in experiments (Fig. 4) (that is, KATP conductance, gKATP, from 0.03 nS to 0.4 nS). Model parameters were optimized to match capillary tone data in WT and SVD capillaries and predicted diameter–pressure relationships (Fig. 6c) were fitted using a four-term Gaussian expansion to inform the network hemodynamics model:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D\left(P\right)=\sum _{k=1}^{4}{a}_{{\mathrm{k}}}\exp \left[-{\left(\frac{P-b_{\mathrm{k}}}{{c}_{k}}\right)}^{2}\right]$$\end{document}DP=∑k=14akexp−P−bkck2where P is intraluminal pressure (mm Hg) and D(P) is the normalized vessel diameter. The coefficients (ak, bk and ck) were determined by nonlinear regression of model responses in WT (low KATP) and SVD (high KATP) conditions. Regression coefficients (ak, bk and ck) are obtained from nonlinear fitting of the Gaussian expansion to active pressure–diameter curves under WT and SVD conditions (Supplementary Table 3).

This approach allowed us to account for pressure-dependent diameters in ACT zone capillaries and perform large-scale flow simulations in dynamically changing microvascular networks under the low (WT) and high (SVD) KATP activity scenarios.

Blood flow distribution was predicted in two reconstructed vascular networks of the mouse cortex (2.7 mm3 and 0.55 mm3) from ref. 2. Each network contains ~104 vessels per mm3 of tissue volume and was segmented to the approximate length of an EC (~30 mm) as previously described59. ACT pericytes are assumed to cover the first three to four capillary generations proximal to the arteriole. The diameter of these capillary segments is thus allowed to change dynamically with transmural pressure. Pressurized WT ACT zone capillaries are set to 4-μm diameter and distal capillaries to 3.6 μm. The capillary contribution to hydrodynamic resistance differs between the two networks, with values (34% and 55%) spanning the range of previously reported estimates44,45. Hemodynamic simulations were performed in the three-dimensional vascular network as previously described60–62. Flow calculations were based on the Hagen–Poiseuille equation, incorporating the Pries and Secomb empirical correlations for the Fahraeus–Lindqvist equation (that is, blood viscosity as a function of vessel diameter and local hematocrit (Hct)) and plasma skimming (that is, uneven Hct distribution at bifurcations) effects63,64. The model predicts transmural pressure (P), flow rate (Q) and Hct distributions across the network. As P changes, diameters in proximal capillaries adjust and this feeds back to affect P, Q and Hct distributions. At equilibrium, diameters and flow rates are recorded for the WT and SVD scenarios. The SVD scenario accounts for reduced tone in the ACT zone as captured by the pressure–diameter relationship (Fig. 6c). In some simulations, attenuated PA tone in SVD is considered by increasing PA diameters. We calculated the total flow entering the capillary network in different cortical layers within our simulation volume to assess the distribution of blood supply along the depth of the cortex (Fig. 6d). Simulations allowed us to investigate the physiological relevance of the experimental findings for the regulation of CBF and look at larger-scale consequences in the cerebral microvascular network.

Three-photon imaging with line-scan and z-stack data acquisition

Three-photon imaging was performed using a customized VIVO Multiphoton Open (3i) microscope mounted on a Sutter Moveable Objective Microscope stand as previously described65. Excitation was provided by a 1,300-nm laser source operating at 1 MHz and focused through a ×25 and 1.05 NA Olympus objective. Emission signals were collected in green (525/50 nm indicating wavelength spectrum outside of 500–550 nm was filtered out), red (620/60 nm; indicating wavelength spectrum outside of 415–445 nm was filtered out) and third-harmonic generation (430/25 nm) channels and acquired using SlideBook 2024 (Intelligent Imaging Innovations). Imaging fields were selected from the superficial cortex to depths of approximately 750–1,000 μm. Line scans were collected from capillary segments in cortical layers I–III and V using 2× digital zoom and bidirectional scanning (256 × 256 pixels2). Individual vessel segments were sampled with a line-scan duration of ~1.1 s and sampling frequency of ~4.2 kHz. After line-scan acquisition, z-stacks were collected using a 2-μs pixel dwell time, the frame averaging of 2-μm and 3-μm z-steps.

Statistical analysis

Sample sizes were predetermined based on previous publications from our lab28,36. Representative traces and images show phenomena seen in multiple mice and thus are an average representation. All findings were independently and successfully replicated in separate biological preparations and/or animals, as indicated in the figure legends. All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10 and R. All data were included that met functional baseline limits. For line-scan analysis, cortical depth was blinded for each mouse and genotype was blinded for all two-photon vessel diameter analyses. For Seahorse assays we normalized SVD to its paired WT per plate and performed one-sample Student’s t-tests and Wilcoxon’s tests on these datasets. All raw summary data are listed in Supplementary Table 1. For two-group comparisons involving independent samples, we first assessed whether the data met the assumptions of normality. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, supplemented by visual inspection of histogram distributions to confirm whether the underlying data approximated a Gaussian distribution. If normally distributed, mean group differences were tested using a two-sided, two-sample Welch’s t-test. When comparing measurements across multiple lumen-increased pressure–flow conditions or drug–dose responses, two-way repeated-measures ANOVAs were utilized. Although summary data show multiple measures from the same mouse, all statistical analyses were averaged and compared per mouse and biological and technical replicates are clearly stated. All in vivo summary data are listed in Supplementary Table 2. If any significance was found, then group means were further compared using two-sided Šidák’s or Bonferroni’s multiple-comparison post-hoc test. Data in figures and text are presented as means ± s.e.m. Significance for all tests was assessed at α = 0.05. All statistical methods are indicated in the figure legends with corresponding independent numbers.
