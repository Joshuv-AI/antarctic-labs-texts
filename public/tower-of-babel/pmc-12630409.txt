Synergistic effect of A novel antimicrobial peptide and silver nanoparticles against drug-resistant P. aeruginosa
Authors: Abolfazl Eyni, Mehdi Goudarzi, Fatemeh Peyravii Ghadikolaii, Abbasali Dehpori, Saeed Soltani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa poses a significant clinical challenge due to its intrinsic antibiotic resistance and the formation of metabolically dormant persister cells. Here, we evaluated the antibacterial and anti-persister activities of a novel 20-amino-acid cationic antimicrobial peptide (RRFFKKAAHVGKHVGKAARR) alone and in combination with silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) against P. aeruginosa PAO1. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined by broth microdilution (128 µg/mL for the peptide; 8 µg/mL for AgNPs), and checkerboard assays revealed a strong synergistic interaction (fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) = 0.25). Treatment of colistin-induced persister populations with the peptide–AgNP combination reduced viable cells by 94.3 ± 2.1% (p < 0.01), markedly outperforming either agent alone. MTT assays in Caco-2 cells demonstrated > 85% viability at concentrations up to the MIC, indicating low host cytotoxicity. Mechanistic insights suggest that membrane disruption by the peptide, coupled with reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and intracellular interference by AgNPs, underlies the enhanced eradication of both active and dormant bacterial cells. These findings support further in vivo investigation of AMP–AgNP co-therapy as a promising strategy to overcome antibiotic tolerance in P. aeruginosa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13568-025-01979-2.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) is a prominent opportunistic Gram-negative pathogen implicated in chronic and life-threatening infections, particularly among immunocompromised individuals and patients suffering from cystic fibrosis, burns, and catheter-associated infections (Chegini et al. 2024). The persistence of P. aeruginosa infections is primarily ascribed to its capacity to evade antibiotic treatment through two principal mechanisms: intrinsic antibiotic resistance and the presence of persister cells. These persister cells constitute a phenotypic subpopulation that enters a metabolically dormant state, enabling them to tolerate high concentrations of antibiotics without incurring genetic mutations (Fisher et al. 2017; Olsen 2015). In contrast to resistant mutants, persister cells can resume normal growth once the antibiotic stress is alleviated, resulting in recurrent infections and treatment failures (Kunnath et al. 2024).

In addition to persister cells, P. aeruginosa displays significant intrinsic resistance attributable to low outer membrane permeability, efflux pump systems, and antibiotic-modifying enzymes (Pang et al. 2019; Cohen et al. 2013). P. aeruginosa causes infections that are particularly difficult to eliminate with conventional antibiotics due to its intrinsic resistance and the presence of persisters. thereby necessitating the development of novel antimicrobial agents capable of effectively targeting both actively growing bacteria and dormant persister cells.

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have garnered substantial attention as promising candidates to surmount bacterial persistence and resistance. AMPs are small, cationic molecules that exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and generally function by disrupting bacterial membranes or interacting with intracellular targets (Mahlapuu et al. 2016; Mookherjee et al. 2020). Notably, AMPs have demonstrated efficacy against both metabolically active and dormant bacterial cells, including persisters, rendering them attractive for treating chronic infections (Kang et al. 2017). The unique mechanism of action of AMPs diminishes the likelihood of resistance development, further augmenting their therapeutic potential (Pletzer et al. 2016).

Another innovative strategy to combat antibiotic tolerance involves the utilization of nanoparticles, mainly silver nanoparticles (AgNPs). AgNPs exhibit potent antibacterial activity through multiple mechanisms, including membrane disruption, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and interference with intracellular processes (Dakal et al. 2016). Recent studies have illustrated that AgNPs can effectively target persister cells and diminish bacterial survival under antibiotic stress (de Lacerda Coriolano et al. 2021; Hemeg 2022). Their capacity to enhance the efficacy of antimicrobial agents further underscores their potential in addressing persistent infections.

Recent studies have shown that the use of AgNPs in combination with antibiotics represents a promising strategy to combat infections caused by multidrug-resistant microorganisms. Specifically, Casals et al. (2025) demonstrated that AgNPs can enhance the efficacy of various antibiotics and even restore lost susceptibility in resistant bacterial strains (Casals et al. 2025). This synergistic effect is primarily achieved through bacterial membrane disruption and increased antibiotic penetration, with the strongest reductions in minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) observed when combined with aminoglycosides.

On the other hand Chegini et al. (2025) investigated AgNPs stabilized with C-phycocyanin (Ag-Pc) synthesized via a green method (Chegini et al. 2024). The study demonstrated strong antimicrobial activity against carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa and methicillin-resistant S. aureus, with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 7.4 µg/mL. Ag-Pc effectively suppressed biofilm formation and disrupted mature biofilms more efficiently than AgNPs alone. Moreover, cytotoxicity assays indicated high biocompatibility, with nearly 80% of mammalian cells remaining viable at therapeutic concentrations, highlighting its potential for clinical applications.

While both AMPs and AgNPs have demonstrated individual antimicrobial efficacy, their combined application may yield synergistic effects, enhancing their capacity to eliminate both active bacteria and persister cells. Synergistic strategies can reduce the effective concentrations of each agent, thereby minimizing toxicity and improving treatment outcomes (Li et al. 2010).

This study aimed to evaluate the antibacterial and anti-persister activities of a novel 20-amino acid AMP against P. aeruginosa. Additionally, the study assessed the effects of AgNPs and their potential synergy with the AMP. This research aimed to develop potentially effective therapeutic strategies targeting antibiotic-tolerant bacterial populations, including persisters, to overcome the shortcomings of traditional treatments.

Materials and methods

Peptide design and synthesis

In this study, the standard strain of P. aeruginosa (PAO1) obtained from Department of Microbiology, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Iran was used to create stable cells and biofilms. Also, a linear peptide consisting of 20 amino acids (RRFFKKAAHVGKHVGKAARR) was identified using reputable peptide design and evaluation platforms, such as AntiBP2 (available at https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/antibp2/) (Lata et al. 2010) and DBAASP (Database of Antimicrobial Activity and Structure of Peptides; available at https://dbaasp.org/).

The 20-amino acid peptide sequence (RRFFKKAAHVGKHVGKAARR) was selected based on in silico analysis using reputable platforms such as AntiBP2 and DBAASP. Parameters considered included net positive charge, hydrophobicity, amphipathicity, and predicted antimicrobial activity against P. aeruginosa. The presence of lysine and arginine residues was specifically favored to enhance binding to negatively charged bacterial membranes. The designed peptide sequence was then synthesized by GeneScript (USA) with standard solid-phase peptide synthesis protocols and purified to > 95% purity, ensuring suitability for subsequent antimicrobial and cytotoxicity assays. This sequence was selected because it combines a net positive charge (+ 8), moderate hydrophobicity (~ 45%), and an amphipathic helical structure predicted by in silico tools, features that are considered optimal for membrane-active antimicrobial peptides. Database predictions (AntiBP2 and DBAASP) consistently indicated high activity against P. aeruginosa and low hemolytic potential, supporting its novelty compared with previously reported AMPs. These criteria justified the choice of this specific sequence for synthesis and experimental validation.

It is noteworthy that the inclusion of lysine and arginine residues enhanced the antimicrobial properties of the peptide (Mookherjee et al. 2020). These amino acids have a greater affinity for binding to negatively charged groups, such as carboxyl, phosphate, and sulfate groups, present in bacterial membranes due to the guanidinium group in their structure. Ultimately, the antimicrobial activity of the peptide was confirmed against bacteria using the DBAASP and AI4AMP (Artificial Intelligence for Antimicrobial Peptides; available at https://ai4amp.ncpsb.org.cn/) servers, and the designed sequence was sent to GeneScript Company (USA) for synthesis.

Preparation and evaluation of AgNPs

The synthesis of AgNPs was conducted using the manufacturer’s protocol (US Research Nanomaterial, Inc, Houston, TX, USA) with additional steps to ensure dispersion and stability. Initially, a surfactant/dispersant was added to the solution to promote complete solubilization. Nanopowder was then introduced and subjected to ultrasonication for uniform particle dispersion. Following sonication, the solution was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 30 min to remove any undispersed agglomerates, enhancing long-term stability. The AgNP suspension was prepared at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL in sterile deionized water as the dispersion medium. A non-ionic surfactant (0.1% Tween-80) was employed as a stabilizer to prevent aggregation. The physicochemical properties of the nanoparticles were independently confirmed: the hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS, Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS), while surface charge was assessed by zeta potential analysis, yielding an average potential of approximately − 28 mV, which indicates colloidal stability. TEM (Transmission Electron Microscopy) imaging further validated the particle size distribution observed with DLS. These additional characterizations ensured reproducibility and alignment with standard nanomaterial reporting practices.

The synthesized AgNPs were subsequently characterized using X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Field-Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) and, Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) to confirm their size, morphology, structural properties, and alignment with ISO/TS 80,004 guidelines for nanomaterials (ISO 2015). Initially, a surfactant/dispersant was introduced into the solution to ensure it was fully dissolved. Subsequently, nano powder was incorporated into the mixture and subjected to ultrasonication. The resultant solution was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 30 min after ultrasonication. This centrifugation step aimed to remove any undispersed agglomerates, thereby enhancing the stability of the dispersion over time.

The synthesized AgNPs were analyzed for size, structural properties, optical characteristics, morphology, and topology using XRD, field-emission FESEM, and TEM (Salomoni et al. 2017). XRD was used to identify crystalline phases and measure crystal constants, while the crystallite size was calculated from the diffraction patterns obtained with a powder X-ray diffractometer. TEM, operating at 120 kV, determined the size and distribution of the nanoparticles. Morphological examination was conducted with FESEM. To prepare the imaging samples, the nanoparticle powder was coated with a thin layer of gold for enhanced conductivity, and the powder was dispersed onto a conductive aluminum substrate.

Bacterial strain and culture conditions

Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 was used as the model organism. Bacteria were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C with constant shaking (180 rpm) until the mid-logarithmic phase.

Persister cell isolation

To investigate the formation of persister cells in P. aeruginosa PAO1, a concentration of colistin (Sigma–Aldrich) corresponding to 50 times the MIC was utilized. Colistin was used because it is a standard antibiotic known to induce metabolic dormancy in P. aeruginosa, thereby selecting for persister cells. The process began with inoculating 5 mL of LB broth (Merck) using a single bacterium colony, followed by a 24-hour incubation period. After subculturing, a suspension was created by combining 50 µL of the cultured bacteria with 50 mL of LB broth, which was then incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 120 rpm. This incubation continued until the OD at 600 nm reached 0.4, which required roughly 3 h. Once the bacteria were in the exponential growth phase, they were subjected to a 24-h treatment with the previously mentioned 50-fold MIC of colistin. This concentration (50× MIC) is a well-established standard for inducing metabolic dormancy in P. aeruginosa persister cells (Fisher et al. 2017; Keren et al. 2004). The 3-log reduction in Colony-forming units (CFUs) after treatment serves as a validated indicator of persister-specific killing. still at 37 °C with shaking at 120 rpm for 24 h. A control culture was also maintained without colistin exposure for comparison.

After treatment with high concentrations of colistin or AgNPs, bacterial suspensions were subjected to serial ten-fold dilutions in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to effectively dilute the antibacterial compounds before plating on LB agar. This step ensures that residual antimicrobial activity does not interfere with colony formation, allowing accurate enumeration of viable CFU (Grassi et al. 2017). The anti-persister activity of the peptide, AgNPs, and their combination was evaluated by incubating the persister cell suspension with the respective treatments. CFUs were enumerated after 24 h to assess bacterial viability (Grassi et al. 2017).

Antibacterial activity assay (MIC Determination)

A commercially available antimicrobial peptide was not used as a positive control because the focus of this study was to evaluate the specifically designed peptide sequence and its interactions with AgNPs. Preliminary experiments confirmed the activity of the designed peptide, and the MIC and anti-persister assays provided internal validation for its effectiveness. Future studies may include standard AMPs as positive controls to benchmark the performance of the designed sequence.The MIC of the peptide and AgNPs was determined using the broth microdilution method according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines ( CLSI Performance Standards for Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing. 33rd ed. CLSI supplement M100. Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute; 2023. iacld.com). Serial two-fold dilutions of the peptide and AgNPs were prepared in a 96-well plate, and bacterial suspensions (5 × 105 CFU/mL) were added. After incubation at 37 °C for 18 h, bacterial growth was assessed by measuring absorbance at 600 nm using a BioTek (ELx800, Synergy H1, Synergy HTX). To minimize potential bias from nanoparticle light scattering at OD600, bacterial viability was additionally confirmed by plating serial dilutions on LB agar and counting CFUs. The minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) was also determined by subculturing from wells without visible growth onto fresh agar plates, with MBC defined as the lowest concentration resulting in ≥ 99.9% reduction of the initial inoculum. These complementary methods ensured reliable assessments of antibacterial activity.

Checkerboard synergy assay and FICI calculation

The checkerboard microdilution method was used to assess the interaction between colistin and AgNPs. Overnight cultures of P. aeruginosa PAO1 were adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard and diluted 1:20 in Mueller–Hinton broth. Ten microliters of the bacterial suspension were added to each well of a 96-well plate containing 100 µL of serial two-fold dilutions of colistin (horizontal axis) and AgNPs (vertical axis). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18 h, and MICs were determined as described above. The fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) was calculated as follows:

Persister cell assay

Persister cells were induced from logarithmic-phase cultures. A single colony of P. aeruginosa PAO1 was inoculated into 5 mL LB broth and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Fifty microliters of this culture were transferred into 50 mL of fresh LB broth and incubated with shaking (100 rpm) at 37 °C for 4 h until mid-logarithmic phase (OD600 ≈ 0.4). To enrich for persister cells, cultures were exposed to colistin at 5× MIC for 3.5 h at 37 °C with shaking (220 rpm). Surviving cells were harvested by centrifugation (14,000 rpm, 5 min), washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in fresh LB broth. Bacterial survival was monitored by plating serial dilutions at regular intervals (every 20 min for 80 min) and after 24 h of incubation at 37 °C. Untreated cultures without antibiotic exposure served as controls.

Assessment of cytotoxicity using the MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) assay

The cytotoxicity of peptides and nanoparticles on the Caco-2 cell line was evaluated using the MTT assay. Caco-2 cells were selected due to their well-established intestinal epithelial characteristics, making them a suitable in vitro model to study the cytotoxic effects of peptides and nanoparticles on intestinal tissue, whereas Vero cells, being kidney-derived, may not accurately reflect intestinal responses (Shivaee et al. 2023).

Caco-2 cells, known for their intestinal epithelial characteristics, were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum under controlled conditions (37 °C, CO2 5%f). Various concentrations of the peptides and nanoparticles, including their MIC and Fractional Inhibitory Concentration (FIC) were prepared for the study. FIC was used to evaluate the interactions between peptides and nanoparticles. FIC was determined using a checkerboard assay, in which different concentrations of the peptides and nanoparticles were combined in microplate wells to identify the MIC of each component alone and in combination. The resulting FIC index allowed classification of the interactions as synergistic, additive, or antagonistic. This method has been widely applied in studies of antimicrobial combinations (Meletiadis et al. 2010).

Following a 24-hour (h) exposure period to the test substances, the MTT solution was added to each well, allowing the cells to metabolize the yellow MTT to blue formazan crystals. After incubation, Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to dissolve the formazan, and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a spectrophotometer (Ghasemi et al. 2023). The percentage of cell viability was calculated using the formula:

Anti-persister cell killing assay

The concentrations of AMP (128 µg/mL) and AgNPs (8 µg/mL) were selected based on preliminary MIC experiments, which determined the effective doses required to inhibit P. aeruginosa PAO1 without causing excessive cytotoxicity, ensuring relevance for evaluating anti-persister activity. Persister cells were treated with the AMP (128 µg/mL), AgNPs (8 µg/mL), and their combination for 24 h. Untreated persister cells served as the control. After treatment, bacterial viability was assessed by serial dilution and plating on LB agar, followed by CFU counting.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test, and differences were considered significant at P < 0.05 (Nobakht et al. 2024).

Structural modeling

The 3D structure of the designed peptide was modeled using the SWISS-MODEL server, which predicts tertiary structures based on homology modeling. The peptide sequence was submitted to the server, and the best structural model was selected based on GMQE and QMEAN scores. This model provided insights into potential amphipathic regions and conformational stability relevant to antimicrobial activity.

Structural characterization of silver nanoparticles

The measurement of absorption for both the extract and the nanoparticles was conducted using UV-Vis spectroscopy. The observed absorption peak at a wavelength of 480 nm corresponds to the AgNPs (Fig. 2). Furthermore, the band gap of the nanoparticles was calculated from the data using OriginPro software, yielding a value of 5.90 eV for the silver nanoparticles. This band gap value indicates that the majority of the synthesized particles are on the nanoscale. A negative zeta potential promotes repulsion between particles, minimizing aggregation and enhancing dispersion. Moreover, surface charge can alter interactions with bacterial membranes, cellular absorption, and cytotoxicity, consequently changing the overall biological activity of AgNPs. XRD studies supported the crystalline structure, with peaks corresponding to the Miller indices of 111, 200, 220, and 311. TEM and Field-Emission FESEM indicated a homogeneous shape with an average size of ~ 20 nm. Energy-dispersive EDX examination verified the elemental composition, including silver, oxygen, and carbon, verifying the synthesis’s correctness (Figs. 3 and 4, and 5).

Antibacterial activity and MIC

The designed peptide and AgNPs demonstrated significant antibacterial activity against P. aeruginosa PAO1. The MIC values were determined to be 128 µg/mL for the peptide and 8 µg/mL for AgNPs, as shown in Table 2. When combined, the peptide and AgNPs exhibited a synergistic effect with a FICI of 0.25, indicating enhanced efficacy compared to individual treatments. Statistical analysis revealed significant differences in MIC values between treatments (p < 0.0001).

Although the MIC of the AMP + AgNPs combination (32 µg/mL) appears higher than that of AgNPs alone (8 µg/mL), the combination demonstrates a synergistic effect in persister cell killing and cytotoxicity profiles. Checkerboard assays revealed a FICI of 0.25, indicating strong synergy. This suggests that while higher concentrations were required for MIC determination in planktonic cells, the combination is more effective in reducing persister populations and maintaining low cytotoxicity, highlighting the therapeutic benefit of the combined treatment.

Persister cell elimination

To evaluate the effect of the designed peptide and AgNPs on P. aeruginosa persister cells, persister populations were induced by exposure to 10× MIC of colistin, and viable counts were determined over time by CFU plating. The untreated persister control population remained stable after 7 h, confirming successful enrichment of persister cells. Treatment with the peptide (128 µg/mL) significantly reduced persister viability, resulting in 1.0, 1.84, and 2.35 log reductions at 7, 10, and 24 h, respectively, compared to the untreated control (p < 0.0001). In practical terms, the persister population decreased from ~ 3.0 × 103 CFU/mL in the control to ~ 6.5 × 10² CFU/mL after 24 h, corresponding to a 78% reduction. AgNPs alone (8 µg/mL) also exhibited anti-persister activity, achieving approximately a 1.0 log reduction (~ 1.0 × 103 CFU/mL) at 24 h relative to the control (p < 0.0001). The combination treatment (peptide at 32 µg/mL + AgNPs at 8 µg/mL; based on synergistic MIC determination) demonstrated the strongest effect, producing 1.24, 2.38, and 2.83 log reductions at 7, 10, and 24 h, respectively (p < 0.0001). At 24 h, the persister population was reduced to ~ 1.7 × 10² CFU/mL, corresponding to a 94.3% reduction compared to the control. These results clearly indicate that both the peptide and AgNPs exhibit significant anti-persister activity, and that their combination has a synergistic effect in eradicating persister cells.

Synergy testing

Checkerboard assays confirmed the synergistic interaction between the peptide and AgNPs. While AgNPs alone displayed significant activity, their combination with the peptide reduced the MIC of each component, enhancing the overall antimicrobial potency. This synergy likely arises from complementary mechanisms of action: membrane disruption by AgNPs and intracellular target inhibition by the peptide. The synergistic interaction between the peptide and AgNPs was supported by multiple findings. Checkerboard assays showed a FICI of 0.25, confirming strong synergy. Persister cell killing assays demonstrated that the combination reduced CFU counts by 94.3%, significantly more than either treatment alone (peptide 78%, AgNPs 66.7%, p < 0.0001). These results suggest that the peptide and AgNPs act through complementary mechanisms: AgNPs primarily disrupt bacterial membranes, facilitating increased intracellular access of the peptide, which then targets intracellular components, enhancing overall antimicrobial efficacy.

Cytotoxicity assessment

Cytotoxicity testing on Caco-2 cells revealed minimal toxicity. Given the clinical relevance of gastrointestinal infections caused by P. aeruginosa, Caco-2 cells (human intestinal epithelium) were prioritized for initial toxicity screening per ISO 10993-5 standards (Iso 2009). Further systemic toxicity profiling is recommended for translational studies. The tested concentrations of AgNPs (8 µg/mL) and peptide (128 µg/mL) were selected based on their respective MIC values against P. aeruginosa, ensuring that the concentrations are effective yet minimally cytotoxic to Caco-2 cells. for both individual treatments and their combination. AgNPs at 8 µg/mL exhibited 5% cytotoxicity, the peptide at 128 µg/mL showed 10% cytotoxicity, and their combined formulation at 32 µg/mL demonstrated 9% cytotoxicity. These results suggest that the combined treatment is safe for therapeutic applications at effective concentrations. Discussion

The growing threat posed by P. aeruginosa, a highly adaptable and opportunistic pathogen, underscores the urgent need for innovative therapies to combat its remarkable resistance mechanisms and biofilm-mediated tolerance to conventional antimicrobial agents. Known for causing severe, chronic, and often hard-to-treat infections, P. aeruginosa deploys various survival strategies, including biofilm formation, the generation of persister cells, and the expression of toxin-antitoxin systems (Tuon et al. 2022; Zadeh et al. 2022). These adaptations enable the bacterium to evade the effects of most antibiotics while persisting in hostile environments such as immune system defenses or during prolonged treatment phases. Adding to this challenge is the worrisome emergence of resistance to colistin, a last-resort antibiotic, which serves as an alarming wake-up call for alternative therapeutic interventions (Behzadi et al. 2021).

The findings from this study highlight the therapeutic potential of a novel synthetic AMP and AgNPs against MDR P.aeruginosa. The designed peptide exhibited significant antimicrobial activity with a MIC of 128 µg/mL. The AgNPs demonstrated even greater antibacterial potency in parallel, with a MIC of only 8 µg/mL. In accordance with our results, Papareddy et al. (2016) showed that the peptide NLF20 exhibited promising activity against P. aeruginosa, with a MIC of 128 µg/mL (Papareddy et al. 2016). The peptide was effective against both planktonic cells and biofilm-embedded bacteria. The study highlighted the therapeutic potential of AMPs against invasive P. aeruginosa infections (Papareddy et al. 2016).

The physicochemical properties of the designed peptide, such as its cationic nature, stability, and favorable hydrophobic ratio, indicate its potential effectiveness in targeting bacterial cells. The high net charge (+ 8.5) and theoretical isoelectric point (pI 12.48) suggest that the peptide can interact efficiently with the negatively charged bacterial membranes, facilitating membrane disruption and leading to bacterial death (do Dias et al. 2020; Zasloff 2002). A key rationale behind the design of this synthetic AMP was to optimize structural motifs that enhance electrostatic interactions and amphipathicity, both of which are critical for membrane disruption and antimicrobial efficacy. The high cationic charge and favorable hydrophobic ratio were incorporated to strengthen selective binding to negatively charged bacterial membranes, while minimizing cytotoxicity toward mammalian cells. This structure–activity relationship (SAR) underpins the observed antimicrobial activity and explains the synergistic interactions with AgNPs. Although AgNPs alone demonstrated a lower MIC compared to the peptide, the combination offers unique advantages by targeting bacteria through complementary mechanisms: the peptide disrupts the bacterial membrane, whereas AgNPs induce ROS generation and intracellular damage. Such dual-action strategies reduce the likelihood of resistance development and are especially valuable against persister cells and biofilm-associated infections, where monotherapies often fail. Therefore, the structural design of the peptide not only contributes to its inherent activity but also provides a mechanistic basis for its synergy with AgNPs.

This aligns with the findings of Zasloff et al. who emphasized the importance of electrostatic interactions in AMPs for selectively targeting bacterial cells (Nobakht et al. 2024; Zasloff 2002). Bioinformatics analyses utilizing tools like AntiBP2 and DBAASP confirmed the peptide’s high antimicrobial activity and lack of hemolytic properties. These findings are critical, as the potential toxicity of antimicrobial agents to human cells can limit their clinical applicability. The results indicate that the peptide can selectively target P. aeruginosa without harming human red blood cells, making it a safer alternative than traditional antibiotics (do Dias et al. 2020). This selectivity is essential for creating therapeutic agents that reduce side effects. Structural modeling revealed insights into the peptide’s conformation, indicating that its three-dimensional structure supports antimicrobial activity. Studies have shown that the amphipathic nature of AMPs is essential for their function, allowing these peptides to interact with lipid bilayers effectively (Lu et al. 2021). The predicted structure can enhance our understanding of the mechanism of action and guide future modifications to optimize effectiveness.

In a related study, Nobakhat et al. identified the active region of Listeriolysin S (LLS) and assessed its antibacterial properties following structural modifications. The modified peptide, which incorporated additional arginine residues, demonstrated improved stability, non-toxicity to human erythrocytes, and potent antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Lactobacillus plantarum. Their research aligned with our objectives and aimed to enhance antimicrobial efficacy through peptide modification. In our investigation, the engineered peptide exhibited notable activity against multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa, with a MIC of 128 µg/mL. The modified LLS peptide achieved a high antimicrobial score and effectively diminished bacterial counts. Both peptides exhibited significant membrane selectivity and stability. Our peptide disrupted bacterial membranes efficiently, mirroring the mechanism of the modified LLS peptide, which involves interactions with cell membranes that lead to nucleic acid and protein leakage. These findings underscore the effectiveness of modifying AMPs as a strategy to address antibiotic-resistant bacteria, highlighting their potential as alternative therapeutic options (Nobakht et al. 2024).

In a separate investigation, the antimicrobial efficacy of Polybia MP-1 was assessed against antibiotic-resistant P. aeruginosa strains isolated from mastitic cow milk samples. The peptide demonstrated a MIC of 75 µM (124 µg/mL) against P. aeruginosa (ATCC 27853). It effectively inhibited the growth of 71% of multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolates at a concentration of 6 times the MIC (450 µM). In our study, the engineered AMP exhibited a MIC of 128 µg/mL against MDR P. aeruginosa, which is comparable to the activity reported for Polybia MP-1. However, the slightly lower MIC of Polybia MP-1 suggests a higher potency at reduced concentrations. Both studies emphasized the high membrane selectivity of the peptides, with Polybia MP-1 exhibiting low to moderate hemolytic activity against various animal red blood cells, akin to the selective toxicity observed with our designed peptide. This comparative analysis highlights the potential of synthetic AMPs, such as Polybia MP-1 and our engineered peptide, in effectively targeting antibiotic-resistant P. aeruginosa, thereby underscoring their promising role as alternatives to conventional antibiotic therapies (Shah et al. 2021).

The current research demonstrated that peptides possess significant antimicrobial properties against pathogenic microorganisms. Moreover, the synergistic combination of peptides with other antimicrobial agents can lead to the development of highly effective antimicrobial candidates. One particularly promising aspect in this regard is the use of nanoparticles. In this study, the characterization of silver AgNPs confirmed their successful synthesis and provided essential parameters indicative of their potential for antimicrobial applications. The absorption peak observed at 420 nm and XRD and TEM analyses revealed a uniform morphology and nanoscale dimensions. These characteristics are critical, as they contribute to the enhanced antibacterial properties of the nanoparticles. The large surface area-to-volume ratio of nanoparticles facilitates increased interaction with bacterial membranes, which is crucial for their antibacterial efficacy. Consistent with our findings, a study by Salomoni et al. (2017) reported similar characteristics, emphasizing that nanoscale dimensions significantly enhance the surface area-to-volume ratio, improving interactions with bacterial membranes (Salomoni et al. 2017). Their research confirmed the antimicrobial activity of AgNPs, demonstrating effectiveness in inhibiting the growth of P. aeruginosa at concentrations as low as 8 µg/mL. Furthermore, AgNPs exhibited bactericidal effects against hospital isolates, underscoring their potential as a viable antimicrobial agent in clinical settings (Salomoni et al. 2017).

The crucial aspect of this research lies in the observed synergistic interaction between the peptide and AgNPs, allowing for a combined approach that could effectively combat established biofilms and persister cells often associated with P. aeruginosa infections. The mechanism likely involves complementary actions: membrane disruption by AgNPs and intracellular target inhibition by the AMP. While direct visualization (e.g., TEM of membrane damage) was not performed, the potent synergy (FICI = 0.25) and 94.3% persister eradication support a dual-mechanism model: (i) electrostatic membrane permeabilization by the cationic peptide (Zasloff 2002), and (ii) AgNP-induced ROS/DNA damage (Dakal et al. 2016). This aligns with recent reports on AMP-nanoparticle synergism (Singh et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2021). This concept is supported by findings from Singh et al., who demonstrated enhanced antimicrobial efficacy through combinations of AMPs and nanoparticles (Singh et al. 2023). Furthermore, checkerboard assays in this study confirmed synergy, echoing similar results by Zharkova et al. (2021) (Zharkova et al. 2021).

Regarding antibacterial effectiveness, both the designed peptide and the synthesized AgNPs exhibited significant activity against P. aeruginosa, with MIC values demonstrating that AgNPs were more potent than the peptide alone. The observed synergistic effect when combining the peptide with AgNPs, as indicated by a FICI of 0.25, highlights the advantages of combination therapies. This synergy is particularly promising in overcoming bacterial resistance mechanisms, as it may reduce the likelihood of selecting resistant strains due to the multiplicity of action (Bueno 2020).

A study explored the synergistic effects of tryptophan-containing AMPs with traditional antibiotics against multidrug-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis (MRSE). Penicillin, ampicillin, and erythromycin significantly reduced MIC values when combined with these AMPs, also inhibiting biofilm formation. Similarly, our designed peptide demonstrated potent activity against multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa, achieving enhanced antibacterial efficacy when combined with AgNPs, consistent with the 32- to 64-fold MIC reductions reported in the prior study (Shang et al. 2019).

In another study, 30 short AMPs were evaluated for their synergistic effects with various antibiotics against multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa (PA910). The results revealed multiple synergistic combinations, particularly with polymyxin B, erythromycin, and tetracycline, with FICs ranging from 0.25 to 2, indicating strong synergistic interactions (Ruden et al. 2019); our findings, along with this study, demonstrate the potent antimicrobial activity of AMPs against multidrug-resistant bacteria. In our study, the designed peptide exhibited a MIC of 128 µg/mL against MDR P. aeruginosa, aligning with the vigorous activity observed in synergistic combinations.

Xu et al. developed an AMP@PDA@AgNPs nanocomposite by modifying AgNPs with AMPs to combat bacterial biofilms and drug-resistant strains (Xu et al. 2021).

Their results showed a well-dispersed nanocomposite with intense antimicrobial activity against both Gram-negative (E. coli, P. aeruginosa) and Gram-positive (S. aureus) bacteria.

Similarly, our study found that the designed peptide combined with AgNPs exhibited potent activity against multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa, comparable to the AMP@PDA@AgNPs nanocomposite. Both studies confirmed biocompatibility, emphasizing selective toxicity towards bacteria while minimizing harm to human cells. This highlights the potential of combining AMPs with nanoparticles as innovative therapeutic strategies against drug-resistant bacteria.

In our study, a novel synthetic AMP and AgNPs were tested against MDR P. aeruginosa, paralleling Cruz et al.‘s work with cecropin-melittin (CM) and LL37 peptides on gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) (Cruz 2022); their study found that both individual (40 µg/mL CM-AuNPs and 60 µg/mL LL37-AuNPs) and combined formulations (20 µg/mL CM-AuNPs + 40 µg/mL LL37-AuNPs) effectively permeabilized bacterial membranes.

Our findings also demonstrated significant antimicrobial efficacy, with the designed peptide and AgNPs showing high activity against MDR P. aeruginosa. At the same time, CM-AuNPs and LL37-AuNPs were potent against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. Both studies highlighted the benefits of combination therapies, with our study indicating a synergistic effect (FICI of 0.25) and confirming bacterial membrane disruption as a key mechanism. This underscores the potential of combining AMPs with nanoparticles as innovative strategies against multidrug-resistant bacteria.

Our findings support that employing a dual strategy—utilizing AgNPs alongside AMPs —can effectively reduce bacterial load and address issues related to persister cells, which can survive antibiotic treatment. This concept is echoed in the work of Keren et al. (2004), who identified persister cells as a significant factor in chronic infections and treatment failures. Thus, integrating AgNPs with AMPs represents a promising approach for developing more effective therapies that allow persister cells to survive, offering a promising therapeutic strategy (Keren et al. 2004).

Eliminating persister cells, which are notoriously difficult to eradicate, adds another dimension to the potential therapeutic use of this peptide and AgNP combination. The substantial reduction in persister cell populations highlights the effectiveness of utilizing these agents together. This suggests they may disrupt the protective mechanisms that allow persister cells to survive antibiotic treatment (Muenraya et al. 2022; Yassin et al. 2022). The mechanism of action appears to involve the combined disruption of cell membranes by AgNPs alongside the intracellular actions of the peptide.

Synergy testing through checkerboard assays further corroborated these findings, indicating that the combination treatment reduces the MIC of both components. The observed complementary mechanisms of action between the cell membrane disruption by AgNPs and intracellular target inhibition by the peptide- underscore the importance of combination therapies in enhancing antimicrobial efficacy. This aligns with current trends in antimicrobial research that advocate for using multimodal strategies to combat resistant pathogens (Goel et al. 2023).

The cytotoxicity assays conducted on Caco-2 cells revealed minimal toxicity for both the individual agents and their combination. The treatments maintained cytotoxicity levels below critical thresholds at effective concentrations, supporting their potential for clinical application. This is consistent with Aboyewa et al. (2022), who emphasized the importance of balancing efficacy with safety in antimicrobial nanoparticle development (Aboyewa et al. 2022).

The present study also highlighted the potential for rational peptide design to enhance antimicrobial potency and selectivity. By incorporating specific structural motifs and amino acid sequences, the researchers could engineer AMPs with improved antibacterial activity and reduced cytotoxicity compared to naturally occurring peptides (Klubthawee et al. 2020; Tan et al. 2021).

This represents an important advance, as natural AMPs often suffer from drawbacks such as susceptibility to proteolytic degradation, high manufacturing costs, and suboptimal pharmacokinetic properties. The ability to synthetically optimize AMPs addresses these limitations and brings this class of antimicrobials closer to clinical translation. In a similar study, Ruden S et al. noted that single amino acid substitutions in peptides can significantly enhance synergistic interactions. This insight aligns with our approach to designing peptides to optimize their antimicrobial properties and synergistic potential (Ruden et al. 2019).

Despite these promising findings, the study does have limitations that should be addressed in future research. Firstly, the in vitro nature of the experiments means that the findings may not entirely reflect the complexities of a living organism’s response to these treatments. Additional in vivo studies are necessary to evaluate the pharmacokinetics, distribution, and overall efficacy of the peptide and AgNPs within biological systems. Furthermore, long-term studies are needed to assess the potential for resistance development in P. aeruginosa against the peptide and AgNPs combination. Understanding how these treatments interact with the host’s immune response is critical for predicting clinical outcomes.

Further optimization and preclinical evaluation will be necessary to fully assess these synthetic AMPs’ therapeutic potential. Key areas for future investigation include pharmacokinetics, in vivo toxicology, and evaluating combination therapies with conventional antibiotics. Exploring the ability of the AMPs to prevent or reverse antibiotic resistance development will also be a critical area of focus.

In conclusion, this study reports the design and characterization of a synthetic AMP together with AgNPs, both showing significant antimicrobial activity against MDR P. aeruginosa. Although AgNPs alone exhibited stronger antibacterial potency compared to the peptide, the combination therapy provided a synergistic effect that could reduce bacterial survival through complementary mechanisms. While the results are promising, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as additional studies are needed to fully elucidate the design rationale and the structural basis for the peptide’s activity. Future research should focus on in vivo applications, long-term safety, and the optimization of peptide design to further enhance antimicrobial potency and the synergistic potential with nanoparticles.

Limitations

Despite the promising in-vitro results, our study has several limitations. We adhered to ISO/TS 80,004 for nanoparticle characterization (ISO 2015), but future work should focus on optimizing surface coatings to improve biocompatibility. While our initial toxicity screening used intestinal epithelial cells (Caco-2), systemic safety in a living organism needs to be assessed with in-vivo models. Furthermore, to better understand the mechanism of action, future studies should use TEM to visualize membrane damage and quantify ROS production. These limitations will be addressed in future preclinical studies.
