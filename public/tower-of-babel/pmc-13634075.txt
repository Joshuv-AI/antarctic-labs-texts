Cerebrospinal Fluid Neuromarker Levels in Patients With Schizophrenia and Healthy Control Participants: A Proximity Extension Assay Study
Authors: Shinsuke Hidese, Kotaro Hattori, Takako Enokida, Megumi Tatsumi, Ryo Matsumura, Hiroshi Kunugi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo further evaluate the association between cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) neuromarkers and schizophrenia, we measured CSF neuromarker levels using proximity extension assay (PEA) in patients with schizophrenia and healthy control (HC) participants.MethodsParticipants included 97 patients with schizophrenia (mean age [SD] = 38.8 [10.3] years; 60 men and 37 women) and 71 HC participants (mean age [SD] = 46.9 [15.4] years; 42 men and 29 women). Symptoms in patients with schizophrenia were assessed using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). CSF protein levels were measured using the Olink PEA Neurology Panel.ResultsCSF levels of cadherin 3, FcRL2, granzyme A, NAAA, plexin-B1, and sFRP-3 were lower in patients with schizophrenia than in HC participants (p < .05). Levels of BCAN, beta-NGF, carboxypeptidases A2 and M, CD200, DDR1, GDF-8, MANF, MDGA1, NAAA, neprilysin, neuropilin-2, neutral ceramidase, Nr-CAM, NTRK2, NTRK3, PDGF-R-alpha, plexin-B3, RGMA, ROBO2, testican-1, THY1, and WFIKKN1 positively correlated with PANSS positive scores, whereas protogenin levels negatively correlated with PANSS negative and general psychopathology scores in patients with schizophrenia (p < .05).ConclusionsThis PEA study identified candidate CSF trait and state markers in schizophrenia; however, these findings require confirmation in further studies, as this was a primarily exploratory and hypothesis-generating study.Keywords: Biomarkers, Cerebrospinal fluid, Proteomics, Proximity extension assay, Schizophrenia, Symptom severity

Evidence from clinical biomarkers across multiple domains has been accumulated to discover molecular signatures and develop therapeutic targets in schizophrenia (1). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker studies have been conducted to distinguish major psychiatric disorders from neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, frontotemporal dementia, and young-onset dementia (2, 3, 4). However, CSF biomarker research in schizophrenia remains limited, including in first-episode psychosis (5,6).

A meta-review suggested that CSF evidence of microglial activation and neuroplastic changes is involved in the pathogenesis of acute psychosis and schizophrenia (7). CSF studies have also suggested blood-brain barrier pathology in schizophrenia (8). An individual patient data meta-analysis showed that the albumin CSF-to-serum ratio and other CSF alterations, as markers of blood-CSF barrier disruption, were associated with positive symptom severity in male patients with first-episode psychosis (9). Furthermore, inflammatory processes and folate metabolism were associated with the antipsychotic effect on monoamine metabolism in the CSF of patients with schizophrenia (10).

A systematic review and meta-analysis found no significant differences in CSF neurofibrillary tangles or tau but lower CSF amyloid-β levels in patients with schizophrenia (11). However, in our previous multiplex immunoassay study with a large sample, we identified several candidate trait (including decreased CSF total tau) and state neuromarkers and found no difference in CSF amyloid-β1–42 levels between patients with schizophrenia and healthy control (HC) participants (12). Although amyloid-β and tau are representative CSF biomarkers in Alzheimer’s disease, this study focused on other neuromarkers due to the relatively young age of the patient cohort (mean approximately 39 years).

CSF proteomics has been a useful tool to identify potential biomarker targets for psychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders such as schizophrenia, Parkinson’s disease, and Alzheimer’s disease, providing their unique and common molecular pathways (13). Multiplex immunoassays have been used to investigate CSF trait and state markers in major psychiatric disorders, and proximity extension assay (PEA) was developed to overcome the cross-reactivity and signal crosstalk limitations of multiplex immunoassays (14). Using the Olink Inflammation Panel and the PEA platform, one study found higher CSF glial cell line–derived neurotrophic factor levels in patients with first-episode schizophrenia (n = 29) than in HC participants (n = 44) (15), whereas another found lower CSF cluster of differentiation 5 (CD5) levels in patients with first-episode schizophrenia (n = 77) than in HC participants (n = 56) (16). However, PEA-based findings in schizophrenia remain scarce, underscoring the need for further studies using additional Olink panels. In such exploratory PEA studies, including ours, useful findings of additional diagnosis-related trait markers beyond glial cell line–derived neurotrophic factor (15) and CD5 (16), as well as symptom severity–related state markers, could be identified in the CSF of patients with schizophrenia.

To further examine the association of CSF biomarkers related to neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation, and neurotrophic signaling (i.e., neuromarkers) with schizophrenia (12), we measured CSF neuromarker levels using the Olink PEA Neurology Panel in patients with schizophrenia and HC participants. We largely aimed to generate new data given that CSF analysis in schizophrenia with PEA assays is in its infancy. Therefore, in this study, we used a largely agnostic, hypothesis-free approach. Namely, the main value of this study is not hypothesis testing but rather opening a window into more CSF deep proteomic analysis in biological psychiatry.

Methods and Materials

Participants

This study included 97 patients with schizophrenia (mean age [SD] = 38.8 [10.3] years; 60 men and 37 women) and 71 HC participants (mean age [SD] = 46.9 [15.4] years; 42 men and 29 women) matched for sex and ethnicity (Japanese). The sample size was not based on a priori power analysis because no prior effect size estimates were available. This was an exploratory sample largely to generate hypotheses given a paucity of prior work in this space. All participants were recruited at the National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry (NCNP) through advertisements at NCNP Hospital, on the institutional website, and in free local magazines.

Participants were screened for psychiatric disorders by trained psychiatrists using the Japanese version of the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) (17,18). Consensus diagnoses were established according to DSM-5 (19), based on the MINI, additional unstructured interviews, and medical records, when available. HC participants had no history of contact with psychiatric services. Participants were excluded if they had a history of central nervous system (CNS) disease, severe head injury, substance abuse, or intellectual disability.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after they received a description of the study protocol. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the NCNP and the Teikyo University Ethical Review Board for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (20).

Clinical Assessments

Symptoms in patients with schizophrenia were assessed using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) (21). Board-certified psychiatrists performed the assessments. Daily antipsychotic doses were converted to chlorpromazine-equivalent doses according to a published guideline (22).

Lumbar Puncture

CSF was collected as described in our previous studies (23, 24, 25) and was provided by the NCNP biobank, which is accredited under ISO 20387. After a single thaw on ice for sample set preparation, the samples were refrozen at −80 °C until Olink PEA was performed; thus, each sample underwent 2 freeze-thaw cycles.

Olink PEA

CSF samples were analyzed using the commercially available Olink Target 96 Neurology Panel, which was designed for protein biomarker discovery (Olink Proteomics). The Olink platform is based on the PEA technique, in which paired oligonucleotide-labeled antibodies bind to target proteins and generate a quantitative polymerase chain reaction readout (26). The Neurology Panel includes 92 proteins related to neurobiological processes, such as neural development, axon guidance, and synaptic function, as well as broader processes including cellular regulation, immunology, development, and metabolism.

Samples from the patient and HC groups were randomly distributed across assay plates. Laboratory technicians were blinded to the clinical information. Initial preprocessing and quality control were performed by Olink using the NPX Manager, and values were exported on a log2 scale (26). Reference intra-assay and interassay coefficients of variation were set at <15% and <25%, respectively. Normalized protein expression (NPX) values were median centered for each protein, and batch-to-batch variation was corrected by bridging normalization between the 2 plates using 8 common CSF samples (7 from patients and 1 from an HC participant). Olink Insights Stat Analyzer statistical tests indicated that there was a statistically significant difference between the means of the 2 assay groups for 25 of 92 assays (Figure S1). Proteins for which >25% of values were below the limit of detection in both groups were excluded from further analyses. Of the 92 proteins, 83 were successfully measured, whereas the remaining 9 proteins—C-type lectin domain family 10 member A, C-type lectin domain family 1 member B, glial cell line–derived neurotrophic factor, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, interleukin-5 receptor subunit alpha, kynureninase, latexin, linker for activation of T cells family member 1, and nicotinamide/nicotinic acid mononucleotide adenylyltransferase 1—did not meet the criteria for successful PEA measurement.

Statistical Analyses

Categorical variables (sex) and continuous variables (age and body mass index [BMI]) were compared in patients with schizophrenia and HC participants using χ2 tests and Student’s or Welch’s t tests, respectively. CSF neuromarker levels were compared between patients with schizophrenia and HC participants using multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA), adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and psychotropic medication use. Correlations between CSF neuromarker levels and symptom scores were assessed using Pearson's partial correlation coefficients, adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and psychotropic medication use in patients with schizophrenia. Correlations between CSF neuromarker levels and continuous clinical variables were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. CSF neuromarker levels were also compared by sex and by medication status (drug free vs. medicated) using unpaired Student’s or Welch’s t tests. Correlation matrices for CSF neuromarker levels were also generated using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Effect sizes were reported as ϕ for χ2 tests, Cohen’s d for unpaired t tests, and ηp2 for MANCOVAs.

Normality and homoscedasticity of continuous variables were tested using Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Levene’s tests, respectively, and the results were used to justify parametric analyses. Bonferroni corrections for multiple testing were applied for group comparisons in t tests, MANCOVAs, and correlational analyses on CSF neuromarker levels successfully measured (p < .05/83 = .0006). All statistical tests were 2-tailed, and p < .05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 30.0 (IBM Japan, Ltd.).

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. Patients with schizophrenia were significantly younger and had a higher BMI than HC participants (p < .05). Discussion

Lower CSF levels of cadherin 3, FcRL2, granzyme A, NAAA, plexin-B1, and sFRP-3 may represent candidate trait markers in schizophrenia.

Cadherins are cell-adhesion molecules (27) that have been implicated in the synaptic dysfunction associated with schizophrenia (28), which is consistent with the current findings. FcRL2 is a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily (29). Granzyme A is a cytotoxic protease that promotes the release of proinflammatory cytokines from target cells (30). NAAA mediates anti-inflammatory biochemical reactions by regulating the signaling activity of palmitoylethanolamide (31). Taken together, these findings suggest possible alterations in both proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory pathways in schizophrenia. Plexin-B1 induces growth cone collapse and neurite retraction through interaction with semaphorin 4D (32,33); therefore, the current findings may be associated with disruption of these processes in schizophrenia. sFRP-3 acts as an antagonist in the Wnt signaling pathway (34), raising the possibility of altered Wnt signaling in schizophrenia.

Several CSF neuromarkers, including BCAN, beta-NGF, carboxypeptidases A2 and M, CD200, DDR1, GDF-8, MANF, MDGA1, NAAA, neprilysin, neuropilin-2, neutral ceramidase, Nr-CAM, NTRK2, NTRK3, PDGF-R-alpha, plexin-B3, RGMA, ROBO2, testican-1, THY1, and WFIKKN1, may represent candidate state markers associated with positive symptoms in schizophrenia.

Beta-NGF (35) is one of the growth factors implicated in the pathogenesis of schizophrenia (36). Nr-CAM has been linked to altered synaptic plasticity in schizophrenia (37). Pathological alterations in NTRK2 and NTRK3 signaling have been reported in neurologic and psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia (38). PDGF-R-alpha is expressed in the hippocampus and is important for brain development and function (39). WFIKKN1 functions as a companion protein for various growth factors in the brain (40). Together, these findings suggest that CNS growth factor-related pathways may be associated with positive symptom severity in schizophrenia.

BCAN is a major proteoglycan that plays an important role in cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions in the adult brain (41). DDR1 is involved in cellular adhesion, migration, matrix remodeling, and proliferation (42). MDGA1 regulates the formation of trans-synaptic bridges (43). Disturbed synaptic adhesion may have an association with positive symptoms in schizophrenia. Neuropilin-2 is a single-pass transmembrane receptor involved in axon guidance (44). Plexin-B3 is a functional receptor for semaphorin 5A, which induces growth cone collapse in retinal ganglion cells (32). RGMA contributes to impaired axonal regeneration, neuroinflammation, and neuronal degeneration (45). ROBO2 plays a crucial role in axon guidance (46). THY1 is attached to the outer surface of the plasma membrane and alters actin dynamics and neurite retraction (47). Taken together, these findings raise the possibility that abnormalities in axonal and neuritic processes are associated with positive symptom severity in schizophrenia.

Carboxypeptidase A2 is a digestive enzyme, whereas carboxypeptidase M functions in the processing of peptide hormones (48). The CD200-CD200R axis has been implicated in the regulation of neuron-microglia communication in the pathogenesis of schizophrenia (49). GDF-8, also known as myostatin, is a master regulator of skeletal muscle mass (50). MANF protects neurons from endoplasmic reticulum stress–associated injury, amyloid-β–induced toxicity (51), and age-related dysfunction (52). Neprilysin is an amyloid-β–degrading enzyme (53). Neutral ceramidase hydrolyzes the N-acyl linkage between the sphingoid base and fatty acid of ceramide (54). Testican-1, a multidomain proteoglycan, carries chondroitin sulfate and other oligosaccharides (55). Although their interactions remain unclear, these functions may be associated with positive symptom severity in schizophrenia.

CSF protogenin may represent a candidate negative state marker associated with negative and general psychopathology symptoms in schizophrenia. Protogenin is a transmembrane protein that mediates signaling involved in cell adhesion, neural differentiation, and neurogenesis in the developing nervous system (56,57). Although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, CSF protogenin may be inversely associated with negative and general psychopathology symptoms in schizophrenia.

This study has several limitations. First, this proteomic study could present only exploratory associations, while biologically meaningful conclusions remain to be drawn about the overall candidate neuromarkers presented here. Second, one might consider the fact that the study recruited through advertisements as a source of bias; however, unless CSF is routinely obtained through care, this is probably unavoidable. Third, HC participants were not age matched, although age was included as a covariate in the trait marker analyses. However, given the effects of aging on brain physiology and generally positive correlations between age and CSF neuromarker levels in our sample, higher age in the HC group might have distorted some data from this cohort. Fourth, psychotropic medication use was associated with 14 CSF proteins; however, this factor was included as a covariate in the trait and state marker analyses. Fifth, additional CSF neuromarkers may exist beyond the 92 proteins targeted in this study. Sixth, the identified trait and state markers were only nominally significant, which increases the possibility of type I error. Finally, because of the cross-sectional design, the causal and temporal relationships between CSF neuromarkers and the pathogenesis of schizophrenia could not be determined. Given that schizophrenia usually implies a chronic disorder and long-term treatment with medication, the relevance of a single cross-sectional sample at one point toward a longitudinal neurodevelopmental disorder needs to be carefully interpreted. To reach real trait and state markers of schizophrenia, scope for longitudinal studies including medication-naïve first-episode psychosis integrated into care is warranted in the future, referring to our cross-sectional data.

Conclusions

In this primarily exploratory and hypothesis-generating study, we identified several candidate CSF trait and state markers associated with schizophrenia and symptom severity. Further preclinical and longitudinal clinical studies are needed to validate these candidate neuromarkers and clarify their potential clinical relevance in schizophrenia.
