Equilibrium configurations of a 3D fluid-beam interaction problem
Vincenzo Bianca, Edoardo Bocchi and Filippo Gazzola

Abstract
. We study a fluid-structure interaction problem between a viscous incompressible fluid and an elastic beam with fixed endpoints in a static setting. The 3D fluid domain is bounded, nonsmooth and non simply connected, the fluid is modeled by the stationary Navier-Stokes equations subject to inflow/outflow conditions. The structure is modeled by a stationary 1D beam equation with a load density involving the force exerted by the fluid and, thereby, may vary its position. In a smallness regime, we prove the existence and uniqueness of the solution to the PDE-ODE coupled system. AMS Mathematics Subject Classification: 35Q35, 76D05, 74F10. Keywords and phrases: Fluid-structure interaction, Navier-Stokes equations, beam equation, PDE-ODE system, existence and uniqueness of equilibria.



1.
Introduction

We consider a fluid-structure interaction (FSI) problem in the rectangular parallelepiped

P=(-R,R)\times(-1,1)^{2},\hskip 21.68121ptR\gg 1

representing a wind tunnel in which artificial airflows are set at the inlet and outlet. The wind tunnel is crossed by a beam occupying a
compact connected set

\mathcal{B}
such that
\Omega=P\setminus\mathcal{B}
is not simply connected. The fluid is viscous, homogeneous, incompressible and governed by the stationary Navier-Stokes equations

(1.1)

-\eta\Delta u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p=0,\quad\nabla\cdot u=0\quad\mbox{in}\quad\Omega,

where
u=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the velocity vector field,
p
is the scalar pressure and
\eta>0
is the kinematic viscosity. The fluid is moving due to inflows/outflows located at the faces
\{x=\pm R\}
.
The beam
\mathcal{B}
is either
hinged or clamped
to
\partial P
at
y=\pm 1
and its cross-sections
\mathcal{B}_{y}
(for
-1\leq y\leq 1
)
are usually irregular hexagons with
rounded corners, see Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
The fluid domain
\Omega=P\setminus\mathcal{B}
and a cross-section
\mathcal{B}_{y}
.

This model has natural applications in the stability of suspension bridges under the action of the wind
[
11
,
12
,
16
]
.
The bridge is divided in three adjacent spans, separated by two fixed piers and we focus our attention on the
most vulnerable center span, see the left picture in Figure
1
.

The wind hits the deck horizontally which modifies the flow that goes around the deck: in the (downwind) leeward, the flow creates
asymmetric vortices that
generate an orthogonal force
L=L(u,p,y)
named
lift

[
1
,
31
]
, which triggers the vertical displacements of the deck;
see the experiment in
[
33
]
. Therefore,
\mathcal{B}
moves vertically and reaches a new position
\mathcal{B}^{h}
, where

h=h(y)
is a smooth function describing its vertical displacement. This also changes the fluid domain and (
1.1
) is satisfied in

\Omega_{h}=P\setminus\mathcal{B}^{h}
. Further forces (e.g., the drag and torque) may also appear, see
again
[
1
,
31
]
, but here we do not consider them. The resulting movements of the deck create more perturbations of the flow
and generate a full FSI.

In absence of a flow, the deck
\mathcal{B}
is maintained in its equilibrium position (
h\equiv 0
) by gravity combined with some
elastic forces due to the cables, hangers, and bending of the deck so that the overall forces
f=f(h)
have the same sign as
h
.
In the static setting inside a wind tunnel, the vertical position
z=h(y)
of the barycenter of the cross-section
\mathcal{B}_{y}
obeys to the beam-type equation

(1.2)

h^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(y)+f\big(h(y)\big)=L(y,u,p)=L\big(y,h(y)\big)

complemented with some boundary conditions, see Section
2
. In (
1.2
) the lift
L

also depends on
h
since
(u,p)
solves (
1.1
) in
\Omega_{h}
.

The equilibrium configurations of the FSI problem are obtained by solving
the
system
(
1.1
)-(
1.2
).
It is our purpose to analyse existence, uniqueness, stability of the equilibrium configurations of
(
1.1
)-(
1.2
) as the inflows/outflows vary. In
[
5
,
7
,
8
,
20
]
the 2D version of this problem was investigated, where the structure represents the cross-section of the deck. The associated dynamical setting was studied in
[
13
,
18
,
32
]
.
In 3D, the only known result
[
19
]
is that, when
\mathcal{B}
is a rectangular plate and under
symmetry
assumptions on both

\Omega
and the inflow/outflow (Poiseuille type), for small flows the unique equilibrium position remains
\Omega
; see also
[
10
,
14
]
for related problems. These contributions
are connected to one of the classical Leray’s problems
[
2
,
3
]
.
Other interactions between fluids and elastic structures were extensively studied in the last decade, both mathematically
[
4
,
9
,
21
,
30
]
and from an engineering point of view
[
26
]
, see also references therein.

In Section
2
we describe in full detail the geometric and physical frameworks, also giving
the precise assumptions used in the sequel.

In Section
3
we discuss existence, uniqueness, and a priori bounds for
the solutions to (
1.1
),
assuming that
h
is given
, see Theorem
3.2
. The difficult task here is the choice of the functional spaces for dealing with
strong solutions
, see Definition

3.1
. We need enough regularity to have embedding into boundary spaces where the (1D) lift
L=L(y,u,p)
is well-defined, see (
2.14
) below. Since all the
\Omega_{h}
are merely Lipschitz (and nonconvex),
to gain regularity we first perform a reflection with respect to some faces of
P
. Moreover, at the other angular points of
\partial\Omega_{h}
, since
we cannot increase regularity
in Hilbertian Sobolev spaces as in the classical works by Grisvard
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
, we
increase
integrability
by switching to non-Hilbertian spaces as in the monograph by Maz’ya & Rossmann
[
28
]
, without falling in the more delicate world of weighted spaces.

In Section
4
we take advantage of this functional framework and we tackle the
FSI problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) in
\Omega_{h}
; we prove that in the uniqueness regime for (
1.1
) (small flows) and in a neighbourhood
of
h=0
there exists a unique equilibrium configuration that varies with Lipschitz continuity with respect to the flows, see Theorem
4.1
.
The proof is obtained with a fixed-point procedure that requires the introduction of suitable functional spaces also for the beam displacement
h
.
By exploiting Theorem
3.2
, we first show that for a given
\overline{h}
there exists a unique solution
(u(\overline{h}),p(\overline{h}))
to (
3.2
) satisfying some bounds. Then we prove that, for such
(u(\overline{h}),p(\overline{h}))
, there exists a unique (strong) solution
h
to (
1.2
) with
L(y,\overline{h})
under the “physical” boundary conditions described in Section
2
.
Finally, through a contraction principle we find a unique fixed point
(h,u,p)
, solving (
1.1
)-(
1.2
). We conclude the paper with two results in a symmetric framework, see Theorems
4.8
and
4.9
, and give an explanation of the consequent physical phenomenon.

2.
Geometric and physical frameworks

In the sequel, we denote by

\mathbb{H}\mbox{ either the space }H_{0}^{2}(-1,1)\mbox{ or }H^{2}\cap H_{0}^{1}(-1,1).

It is well-known
[
17
, Theorem 2.31]
(valid for
any
bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for
any

n\geq 1
), that

\mathbb{H}
is a Hilbert space endowed with the scalar product and norm

(2.1)

(h_{1},h_{2})_{\mathbb{H}}=\int_{-1}^{1}h_{1}^{\prime\prime}h_{2}^{\prime\prime},\qquad\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}=\|h^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\sim\|h\|_{H^{2}(-1,1)}.

Moreover, we have the embedding inequality

(2.2)

\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(-1,1)}\leq S_{\mathbb{H}}\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}\quad\text{where}\quad\frac{1}{S_{\mathbb{H}}}=\min_{h\in\mathbb{H}}\,\frac{\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}}{\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(-1,1)}},

see Appendix for an upper bound of
S_{\mathbb{H}}
.
Analogously, we denote by

\mathbb{H}^{4}\mbox{ either the space $H^{4}\cap H^{2}_{0}(-1,1)$ or $\{h\in H^{4}(-1,1)$ s.t. $h(\pm 1)=h^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)=0\}$}

endowed with the scalar product and norm

(2.3)

(h_{1},h_{2})_{\mathbb{H}^{4}}=\int_{-1}^{1}h_{1}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}h_{2}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime},\qquad\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}^{4}}=\|h^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\sim\|h\|_{H^{4}(-1,1)},

see again Appendix. But (
2.3
)
is not
a norm in
H^{4}\cap H^{1}_{0}(-1,1)
: to see this, note that
h(y)=1-y^{2}\in H^{4}\cap H^{1}_{0}(-1,1)
has null
\mathbb{H}^{4}
-norm.

Consider a
C^{1,1}
compact, connected and simply connected set
\mathcal{B}^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that

(2.4)

\begin{array}[]{c}X^{+}=\max_{(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{B}^{*}}x<R,\quad X^{-}=-\min_{(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{B}^{*}}x<R,\\[5.0pt]
Z^{+}=\max_{(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{B}^{*}}z<1,\quad Z^{-}=-\min_{(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{B}^{*}}z<1\\[5.0pt]
\max_{(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{B}^{*}}y>1,\hskip 21.68121pt\min_{(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{B}^{*}}y<-1,\hskip 21.68121pt\mathcal{H}^{2}(\partial\mathcal{B}_{y}^{*})>0\end{array}

where
\mathcal{H}^{2}
denotes the
2
-dimensional Hausdorff measure and

\mathcal{B}_{y}^{*}=\mathcal{B}^{*}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\{y\}\times\mathbb{R})

is the cross-section of
\mathcal{B}^{*}
, namely the intersection with a plane parallel to both the
x
and
z
axes. Let

\mathcal{B}=\overline{P}\cap\mathcal{B}^{*}

be the portion of
\mathcal{B}^{*}
contained in
P
,
so that its boundary
\partial\mathcal{B}
consists of three parts

(2.5)

\partial\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}^{*}_{-1}\cup\mathcal{B}^{*}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}

where
\mathcal{S}
is a
C^{1,1}
-surface that does not intersect the faces
\{y=\pm 1\}
. For any
y\in[-1,1]
we simply denote

\mathcal{B}_{y}=\mathcal{B}_{y}^{*}
. For any
h\in\mathbb{H}
, the non-simply connected domain

(2.6)

\Omega_{h}=P\setminus\mathcal{B}^{h}={P}\setminus\{(x,y,z+h(y))\mbox{ with }(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{B}\}

is the region occupied by the fluid; in particular,
\mathcal{B}^{0}=\mathcal{B}
and
\Omega_{0}=\Omega
. We assume that

\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{B}_{y}\subset(-R,R)\times(-1,1)\mbox{ is a connected planar compact set}\\
\mbox{with barycenter at $(x,z)=(0,0)$ for all $|y|\leq 1$,}\end{array}

see again Figure
1
(right). Then, in view of (
2.6
), the barycenter of the cross-section
\mathcal{B}_{y}^{h}
is located at

(x,z)=(0,h(y))
. In Figure
2
, to simplify the pictures we sketch the domains
\Omega
and
\Omega_{h}
for
nonsmooth

(rectangular) cross-sections.

Figure 2.
Examples of fluid domains
\Omega
(left) and
\Omega_{h}
(right).

We denote the faces of the parallelepiped
P
by

\Gamma_{x}^{-}=\{-R\}\times(-1,1)\times(-1,1),\qquad\Gamma_{x}^{+}=\{R\}\times(-1,1)\times(-1,1),

\Gamma_{y}^{-}=(-R,R)\times\{-1\}\times(-1,1),\qquad\Gamma_{y}^{+}=(-R,R)\times\{1\}\times(-1,1),

\Gamma_{z}^{-}=(-R,R)\times(-1,1)\times\{-1\},\qquad\Gamma_{z}^{+}=(-R,R)\times(-1,1)\times\{1\}.

Hence,
\partial\Omega_{h}
consists of the two closed squares
\overline{\Gamma_{x}^{\pm}}
, the two closed rectangles
\overline{\Gamma_{z}^{\pm}}

and the two closed non-simply connected planar regions
\overline{\Gamma_{y}^{\pm}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{\pm 1}}
.
After putting
\varepsilon^{\pm}(h)=\mathrm{dist}(\mathcal{B}^{h},\Gamma_{z}^{\pm})
, it follows from (
2.4
) that

\ \mathcal{B}^{h}\subset[-X^{-},X^{+}]\times[-1,1]\times[-1+{\varepsilon}^{-}(h),1-{\varepsilon}^{+}(h)].

Although the boundary of
\mathcal{B}^{*}
is assumed to be smooth (
C^{1,1}
), the domain
\Omega_{h}=P\setminus\mathcal{B}^{h}
is
merely Lipschitz

not only because of the vertices and edges of the parallelepiped
P
but also because of the intersection between
\mathcal{B}^{h}
and the faces

\Gamma_{y}^{\pm}
of
P
. In fact, a (nonsmooth) vertical displacement
h=h(y)
could also affect the regularity of
\mathcal{S}
, see (
2.5
),
but, as we shall see, a posteriori
h
will be sufficiently smooth.

Since
\mathbb{H}
compactly embeds into
C([-1,1])
, we have the lower bounds

(2.7)

\begin{aligned} &\varepsilon^{+}(h)\geq 1-\max_{[-1,1]}h-Z^{+}\geq 1-\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(-1,1)}-Z^{+}\\
&\varepsilon^{-}(h)\geq 1+\min_{[-1,1]}h-Z^{-}\geq 1-\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(-1,1)}-Z^{-}\end{aligned}\qquad\forall h\in\mathbb{H}.

A sufficient condition to prevent collisions (intersections) between
\mathcal{B}^{h}
and the top/bottom boundaries of
P
, namely
{\varepsilon}^{\pm}(h)=0
, is then that there exists

1<\omega<\frac{1}{\max\{Z^{+},Z^{-}\}}.

such that

(2.8)

\left\|h\right\|_{L^{\infty}(-1,1)}<1-\omega\max\{Z^{+},Z^{-}\}.\\

We
do not assume
that all the cross-sections
\mathcal{B}_{y}
have the same shape, such as for the suspension bridge over the Grand Canyon in
Zhangjiajie (China), see Figure
3
.

Figure 3.
The Zhangjiajie Bridge.

For the sake of stability, the deck of “asymmetric” bridges should have wider cross-sections at the extremals
y=\pm 1
and smaller
cross-sections in proximity of the center
y=0
of the span. This is why the angles of attachment between
\mathcal{B}
and
\Gamma_{y}^{\pm}

may be larger than the right angle. To make this precise, consider
A=(x,y,z)\in\partial\mathcal{B}_{\pm 1}
, namely a point where

\mathcal{B}^{h}
is attached to the faces
\Gamma_{y}^{\pm}
of
P
. For any
A\in\partial\mathcal{B}_{1}

(same construction for
A\in\partial\mathcal{B}_{-1}
) and
h\in\mathbb{H}

there exists
\alpha\in[0,2\pi)
such that
A
belongs to the curve

(2.9)

\mathbb{C}^{h,\alpha}=\partial\mathcal{B}^{h}\cap\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\quad\mbox{where}\quad\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3};\,(\cos\alpha)x+(\sin\alpha)z=0\}.

Within the plane
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
, the tangent line to
\mathbb{C}^{h,\alpha}
forms an angle of measure
\theta=\theta(A)
with the line

\{y=1\}\cap\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
, see Figure
4
.

Figure 4.
The curve
\mathbb{C}^{h,\alpha}
in the plane
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
.

The angle
\theta(A)
may depend on both
\alpha\in[0,2\pi)
and
h\in\mathbb{H}
. By continuity of
A\mapsto\theta(A)
and compactness of
\partial\mathcal{B}_{\pm 1}\cap\Gamma_{y}^{\pm}
, we infer that

(2.10)

\theta_{*}=\sup_{\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}<C_{\mathbb{H}}}\ \max_{A\in\partial\mathcal{B}_{\pm 1}\cap\Gamma_{y}^{\pm}}\theta(A)<\pi

where
C_{\mathbb{H}}
is sufficiently small to ensure (
2.8
) in view of (
2.2
). Mathematically, any
\theta^{*}\in(0,\pi)
is allowed but the case
\theta^{*}\in(0,\pi/2)
does not physically describe a real bridge. The case
\theta(A)=\pi/2
may occur, for instance if
A
belongs to the upper part of the deck (where cars or pedestrians move).

From now on, with the convention
\tfrac{1}{0}=\infty
, we fix
\sigma
such that

(2.11)

\text{if}\ 0<\theta_{*}<\frac{\pi}{2},\quad 0<\sigma<1,\qquad\text{if}\ \frac{\pi}{2}\leq\theta_{*}<\pi,\quad 0<\sigma<\min\left\{\frac{2\pi-2\theta_{*}}{2\theta_{*}-\pi},1\right\}.

The choice of
\sigma
is fundamental for the regularity of the solution to the Navier-Stokes equations, see Definition
3.1
, and for the definition of the lift that requires one-dimensional traces in the fluid domain, see (
2.14
). We complement (
1.1
) with inflow/outflow conditions at the inlet/outlet
\Gamma_{x}^{\pm}
.
Given

\begin{array}[]{c}V\in W^{2-\tfrac{1}{2+\sigma},2+\sigma}(-1,1)^{2}\subset C([-1,1]^{2})\\[5.0pt]
\text{such that}\quad V(y,-1)=0\ \text{for}\ |y|\leq 1,\quad V(\pm 1,z)=0\ \text{for}\ |z|\leq 1,\end{array}

we take

V_{i},V_{o}\in W^{2-\tfrac{1}{2+\sigma},2+\sigma}(\Gamma_{x}^{\pm})

verifying

(2.12)

\displaystyle\quad\quad V_{i}=V_{o}=V\quad\mbox{on }\quad\overline{\Gamma_{y}^{\pm}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{\pm 1}}\cup\Gamma_{z}^{\pm},\qquad\int_{\Gamma_{x}^{-}}V_{i}=\int_{\Gamma_{x}^{+}}V_{o}=1.

For
\gamma>0
we consider the boundary-value problem

(2.13)

\begin{array}[]{cc}-\eta\Delta u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p=0,\quad\nabla\cdot u=0\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega_{h},\\[5.0pt]
u=0\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\mathcal{B}^{h},\quad u=\gamma Ve_{1}\quad\text{on}\quad\overline{\Gamma_{y}^{\pm}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{\pm 1}}\cup\Gamma_{z}^{\pm},\\[5.0pt]
u=\gamma V_{i}e_{1}\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{x}^{-},\quad u=\gamma V_{o}e_{1}\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{x}^{+}.\end{array}

Since the fluid is incompressible, the flux
\gamma>0
is constant through any cross-section
\{x=x_{0}\}
(for
|x_{0}|\leq R
) of
\Omega_{h}
:
this is why (
2.12
) is a
necessary condition
for the existence of solutions to (
2.13
).

For a solution
(u,p)
to (
2.13
), the lift force acting vertically (in the
z
-direction) on a cross-section
\mathcal{B}_{y}^{h}
for a given

y\in[-1,1]
is computed through the formula

(2.14)

L(y,u,p)=-e_{3}\cdot\int_{\partial\mathcal{B}_{y}^{h}}\mathbb{T}(u,p)n=-e_{3}\cdot\int_{\partial\mathcal{B}_{y}^{h}}[\eta(\nabla u+\nabla^{T}u)-p\mathbb{I}]n,

where
\mathbb{I}
is the
3\times 3
identity matrix and
n
is the outward unit normal vector to
\partial\mathcal{B}^{h}
, pointing inside
\mathcal{B}^{h}
.
Since
\mathcal{B}^{*}
is of class
C^{1,1}
, the normal vector
n
is well-defined and, as we shall see, the solution

(u,p)
is smooth enough to ensure that the integrand in (
2.14
) is bounded.

To model the behaviour of
\mathcal{B}
, we set up a one-dimensional horizontal thin beam covering the interval
(-1,1)
.
If
h(y)
denotes the vertical displacement of the beam (in the
z
-direction)
from the equilibrium position (
\equiv 0
) at the point
y\in(-1,1)
, from
[
17
, Section 1.1.1]
we know that the energy
density
E_{b}
stored by
bending
the beam is proportional to the square of the curvature:

(2.15)

E_{b}(h)=\frac{EI}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{h^{\prime\prime}(y)^{2}}{\left(1+h^{\prime}(y)^{2}\right)^{3}}\sqrt{1+h^{\prime}(y)^{2}}\,dy=\frac{EI}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{h^{\prime\prime}(y)^{2}}{\left(1+h^{\prime}(y)^{2}\right)^{5/2}}\,dy,

where
EI>0
is the flexural rigidity. Formula (
2.15
) highlights the curvature and the arclength.
The functional
E_{b}

does not include the stretching
energy, a term measuring the length variation of the beam
since we consider beams free to move in horizontal directions at their endpoints, as at the piers in suspension bridges.
For small displacements of the beam, in particular for small
h^{\prime}
, the approximated bending energy reads

(2.16)

E_{b}(h)\approx\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{h^{\prime\prime}(y)^{2}}{2}\,dy,

in which we normalised the flexural rigidity.

The beam is subject to restoring forces
f=f(h)
that satisfy

(2.17)

f\in{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{R})\,,\quad f(0)=0\,,\quad h\mapsto f(h)\mbox{ is non-decreasing.}

As mentioned in the introduction, these forces are due to the cables, hangers, and gravity. From a purely mathematical point of view,
(
2.17
) does not exclude
f(h)\equiv 0
that, instead, has to be excluded in the 2D case
[
5
,
7
,
8
,
10
]
:
the reason is that the beam is fixed at its endpoints while in 2D the structure may be “floating”.
Due to the fluid flow, the beam is also subject to the lift force
L=L\big(y,h(y)\big)
; in fact,
L=L(y,u,p)
, see (
2.14
),
where
(u,p)
solves (
2.13
) in
\Omega_{h}
and, hence, depends on
h
. As we shall see in Lemma
4.2
and Proposition
4.7
,
L
is bounded with respect to
y
and Lipschitz continuous with respect to
h
. Hence, after introducing the
potentials

F:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
G:(-1,1)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

F^{\prime}(h)=f(h),\qquad G_{h}(y,h)=L(y,h),

the overall (approximated) energy of the beam is

(2.18)

\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{h^{\prime\prime}(y)^{2}}{2}\,dy+\int_{-1}^{1}F\big(h(y)\big)\,dy-\int_{-1}^{1}G\big(y,h(y)\big)\,dy\qquad\forall h\in\mathbb{H}\,.

Under suitable assumptions on
G
(satisfied in our context), this functional is well-defined over
\mathbb{H}
and its
critical points satisfy the Euler-Lagrange equation

\int_{-1}^{1}\Big[h^{\prime\prime}(y)\phi^{\prime\prime}(y)+f\big(h(y)\big)\phi(y)-L\big(y,h(y)\big)\phi(y)\Big]\,dy=0\qquad\forall\phi\in\mathbb{H}

and, in strong form,

(2.19)

h^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(y)+f\big(h(y)\big)=L\big(y,h(y)\big)\qquad\text{for a.e. }y\in(-1,1)\,,

that coincides with (
1.2
).
In Section
4
, we will prove that
h\in\mathbb{H}^{4}
and that (
2.19
) is satisfied in strong form, see Proposition
4.4
. However, for the regularity of solutions to (
2.13
), it is sufficient to consider the larger space

C_{0}^{1,1}([-1,1])=\left\{h\in C^{1,1}([-1,1])\mbox{ s.t. }h(\pm 1)=0\right\}.

Finally, the differential equation (
2.19
) has to be complemented with some boundary conditions,
depending on how the beam is fixed at the endpoints. For a beam representing a bridge, there are two possibilities:

\bullet
Clamped: mathematically, this means that
h(\pm 1)=h^{\prime}(\pm 1)=0
(homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions), which leads to the request that
h\in H^{2}_{0}(-1,1)
. In such case, the supremum in (
2.10
) disappears since
\theta(A)
does not depend on
h
.

\bullet
Hinged: mathematically, this means that
h(\pm 1)=h^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)=0
(homogeneous Navier boundary conditions), which leads to the request that
h\in H^{2}\cap H^{1}_{0}(-1,1)
;
in fact, in this space
h^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)
may not be defined and this condition should be seen as a natural boundary condition. As just mentioned, in Section
4
, we will see that
h\in\mathbb{H}^{4}
, which implies that
h^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)=0
.

These two boundary conditions are physically represented in Figure
5
.

3.
Fluid boundary-value problem

To simplify notations, we group the boundary conditions in (
2.13
) by defining
u_{*}:\partial\Omega_{h}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
as

(3.1)

Since

4.
Fluid-structure interaction problem

Aiming to study the equilibrium configurations for the FSI problem (
2.13
)-(

), we let the domain
\mathcal{B}^{h}
vary.
For any

, the cross-section

has a barycenter with vertical displacement

The FSI problem we are led to study consists in the coupled PDE-ODE system

\begin{array}[]{c}-\eta\Delta u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p=0,\quad\nabla\cdot u=0\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega_{h}=P\setminus\mathcal{B}^{h},\\[5.0pt]

(4.2)

\begin{array}[]{c}h^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}+f(h)=L(\cdot,h)\qquad\text{in}\quad(-1,1),\\[5.0pt]
h(\pm 1)=0,\qquad h^{\prime}(\pm 1)=0\ \text{ or }\ h^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)=0,\end{array}

where

with

as in (

Let us state the main result on the equilibrium configurations of the fluid-beam interaction problem (

)-(
4.2
). To this end, we introduce the open ball

(4.4)

\mathbb{B}_{\delta}=\{h\in\mathbb{H}^{4}\ \text{s.t.}\ \|h\|_{\mathbb{H}^{4}}<\delta\}\\

and we prove the following:

Theorem 4.1
.

There exists
\beta_{0}>0
and
\gamma_{0}\in(0,\overline{\gamma}]
such that for any

, the FSI problem (

)-(

) admits a unique solution

In addition, there exists
\gamma^{*}\in(0,\gamma_{0}]
such that the map

is Lipschitz continuous on the interval

. More precisely, for any
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in[0,\gamma^{*})
,

\displaystyle\|h(\gamma_{1})-h(\gamma_{2})\|

\displaystyle{}_{\mathbb{H}^{4}}+\|u(\gamma_{1})-u(\gamma_{2})\circ\varphi_{h(\gamma_{2})-h(\gamma_{1})}\|_{W^{2,2+\sigma}(\Omega_{h(\gamma_{1})})}

\displaystyle+\|p(\gamma_{1})-p(\gamma_{2})\circ\varphi_{h(\gamma_{2})-h(\gamma_{1})}\|_{W^{1,2+\sigma}(\Omega_{h(\gamma_{1})})}\leq C(\beta_{0})|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|,

is defined in Lemma

The proof of Theorem

we derive technical results about the lift

;

•

3.4
), we then obtain

\displaystyle\|U_{h}(\tilde{h})\|_{W^{2,2+\sigma}(\Omega_{0})}+\|P_{h}(\tilde{h})\|_{W^{1,2+\sigma}(\Omega_{0})}\leq C(\beta_{0})\gamma\|\tilde{h}\|_{H^{4}(-1,1)},

which, in turn, implies the uniform bound for the norms of the differential operators

and

(4.34)

\sup_{h\in\mathbb{B}_{\beta_{0}}}\|D_{h}U[h]\|+\sup_{h\in\mathbb{B}_{\beta_{0}}}\|D_{h}P[h]\|\leq C(\beta_{0})\gamma.

Finally, let us estimate the variation of the lift, defined in (
4.22
), with respect to the variation of

. Using the same argument and the Trace Theorem as in the proof of Lemma

, we have that, for any

(4.35)

\displaystyle|L(y,h_{1})-L(y,h_{2})|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{\partial\mathcal{B}_{y}}\left(\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}_{h_{1}}(U(h_{1}),P(h_{1}))M_{h_{1}}n_{0}-\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}_{h_{2}}(U(h_{2}),P(h_{2}))M_{h_{2}}n_{0}\right)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{\partial\mathcal{B}_{y}}\bigg[\left(\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}_{h_{1}}(U(h_{1}),P(h_{1}))-\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}_{h_{2}}(U(h_{2}),P(h_{2}))\right)M_{h_{1}}n_{0}

\displaystyle+\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}_{h_{2}}(U(h_{2}),P(h_{2}))(M_{h_{1}}-M_{h_{2}})n_{0}\bigg]\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta_{0})\bigg(\|U(h_{1})-U(h_{2})\|_{W^{2,2+\sigma}(\Omega_{0})}+\|P(h_{1})-P(h_{2})\|_{W^{1,2+\sigma}(\Omega_{0})}

\displaystyle+\left(\|U(h_{2})\|_{W^{2,2+\sigma}(\Omega_{0})}+\|P(h_{2})\|_{W^{1,2+\sigma}(\Omega_{0})}\right)\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{4}}\bigg).

Combining the Mean Value Inequality with (
4.34
) and using (
4.30

), we obtain that

\displaystyle\|L(\cdot,h_{1})-L(\cdot,h_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}(-1,1)}

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta_{0})\left(\gamma+\|u(h_{2})\|_{W^{2,2+\sigma}(\Omega_{h_{2}})}+\|p(h_{2})\|_{W^{1,2+\sigma}(\Omega_{h_{2}})}\right)\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma C(\beta_{0})\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{4}},

which proves the Lipschitz continuity of
L(\cdot,h)
with respect to

.

\Box

\|\cdot\|_{H^{4}(-1,1)}
in the closed subspace
\mathbb{H}^{4}\subset H^{4}(-1,1)
.

Proof.

Let
h\in\mathbb{H}^{4}
. The definition (
2.1
) shows that
\|h^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}
bounds both
\|h^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}
and
\|h\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}
. Hence, it suffices to prove that

\|h^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}
bounds
\|h^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}
and
\|h^{\prime\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}
.

The first bound follows from two integration by parts and the Hölder inequality:

\displaystyle\|h^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{-1}^{1}h^{\prime\prime\prime}h^{\prime}+[h^{\prime\prime}h^{\prime}]_{-1}^{1}=\int_{-1}^{1}h^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}h-[h^{\prime\prime\prime}h]_{-1}^{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|h^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\|h\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\leq C\|h^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\|h^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}

where the boundary terms vanish in both cases and the last inequality follows from the above mentioned estimate
\|h\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\leq C\|h^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}
.

For the second bound, we need to distinguish the two cases. If
h^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)=0
, then an integration by parts shows that

\|h^{\prime\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}=-\int_{-1}^{1}h^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}h^{\prime\prime}+[h^{\prime\prime\prime}h^{\prime\prime}]_{-1}^{1}\leq\|h^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\|h^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\leq C\|h^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}

where the boundary term vanishes because
h^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)=0
and the last inequality follows from the previously proved estimate.

If
h^{\prime}(\pm 1)=0
, we give a simple one-dimensional proof by repeatedly using the Rolle Theorem. From
h(\pm 1)=0
we infer that there exists
y_{0}\in(-1,1)
such that
h^{\prime}(y_{0})=0
.
Subsequently, there exist
y_{1}\in(-1,y_{0})
and
y_{2}\in(y_{0},1)
such that
h^{\prime\prime}(y_{1})=h^{\prime\prime}(y_{2})=0
. Finally, there exists
y_{3}\in(y_{1},y_{2})
such that
h^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{3})=0
.
Then, by the Fundamental Theorem we get

h^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)=\int_{y_{3}}^{y}h^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(t)dt

and we conclude using the Hölder inequality.
\Box
