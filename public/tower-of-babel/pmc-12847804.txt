Pilose antler extract promotes angiogenesis and vascular maturation to accelerate wound healing
Authors: Lishuang Li, Yi Wang, Yuman Ma, Junxiao Gong, Xing Ren, Yanan Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAngiogenesis plays a crucial role in early wound healing by providing essential nutrients and oxygen to tissue cells, supporting the growth of epithelial cells and other tissue cells. The essence of angiogenesis lies in the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells. Pilose antler extract (PAE) accelerates early-stage wound healing. Proteomic analysis shows its role in vascular tension regulation, cell migration, and angiogenesis. This study aims to investigate the effects and pharmacological mechanisms of PAE on angiogenesis and vascular maturation during the early stages of wound healing. We used a full-thickness skin excision model in rats, utilizing endothelial cell analysis to examine PAE’s regulatory mechanisms on angiogenesis. In situ hybridization combined with CD31 immunofluorescence was performed to localize Vegfa mRNA within vascular regions, verifying endothelial-specific upregulation in vivo. In vitro, PI3K inhibitors (LY294002 and Wortmannin) and an AKT inhibitor (MK-2206) were employed to confirm the pathway specificity of PAE-induced angiogenic effects. PAE accelerates the formation of vascular networks and the restoration of blood flow perfusion in early wound healing. In addition, it promotes the proliferation, migration and formation of endothelial cell tubular structures by activating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, upregulating the level of VEGFR2 and p-AKT1 in endothelial cells. The co-localization of Vegfa mRNA and CD31⁺ vessels confirmed that PAE specifically enhances VEGFA expression within vascular endothelium in vivo. Furthermore, PAE dynamically regulates the expression of Ang-1, Ang-2, and Tek, demonstrating its time-dependent regulatory effects on angiogenesis and remodeling during wound healing. The positive effects of PAE on wound healing and recovery of blood flow are counteracted when LY294002 inhibits PI3K activity. Pharmacological inhibition with LY294002, Wortmannin, or MK-2206 significantly reversed PAE-induced endothelial proliferation, migration, and tube formation, confirming that the PI3K–AKT axis is essential for PAE’s pro-angiogenic activity. Local treatment with PAE can increase the expression level of VEGFR2 and activate the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway to promote angiogenesis and accelerate wound healing, while dynamically regulating the expression of Ang-1, Ang-2, and Tek, accelerating vascular maturation, and promoting the formation of functional vessels during wound healing. These findings, supported by endothelial co-localization and inhibitor validation experiments, provide direct mechanistic evidence that PAE promotes angiogenesis through PI3K–AKT pathway activation. Our study first revealed that PAE promotes angiogenesis by activating the PI3K-AKT pathway. Introduction

“Angiogenesis, the process of new blood vessel formation from existing vessels,” is crucial for wound healing and tissue reconstruction1,2. It promotes blood flow to the damaged area, providing essential nutrients and oxygen to cells, while assisting in the removal of metabolic waste and supporting the growth and regeneration of epithelial and other tissue cells. Newly formed blood vessels play a decisive role in regulating inflammation and wound closure speed during the inflammatory phase, providing a pathway for important repair molecules, such as growth factors and immune cells, to quickly reach the wound site. In addition, newly formed trimmed blood vessels play a key role in the remodeling phase of wound healing, not only promoting the recovery of damaged tissue, but also helping to form structurally sound and functional scar tissue3–6.

However, despite the crucial role of angiogenesis in wound healing, it faces several limitations and challenges as a therapeutic strategy. The balance between newly formed blood vessels and mature blood vessels is particularly critical in the angiogenesis process. Excessive formation of new blood vessels can lead to overgrowth, creating abnormal vascular networks, which may not only affect the quality of wound healing but also trigger pathological conditions such as tumor angiogenesis, thereby promoting cancer growth and metastasis. Conversely, insufficient formation of new blood vessels can result in incomplete healing and delay the wound repair process. Achieving a balance between the generation of new blood vessels and the stability of mature vessels is essential for ensuring high-quality tissue healing7,8.

Wound healing is a complex multi-stage physiological process that involves significant changes in blood vessels and blood flow. During the early inflammatory stage, blood perfusion in the damaged area typically increases to support acute inflammatory responses. Blood vessels at this time exhibit irregular and tortuous shapes, reflecting a rapid response to the needs of damaged tissue. In the proliferation stage, the activity of new blood vessel formation remains active to support granulation tissue growth and epithelial cell migration. These newly formed blood vessels gradually evolve into more ordered and straight structures, marking maturity and functional recovery. In the remodeling stage, blood vessels mature and adapt to the needs of new tissue, and blood perfusion stabilizes. Blood vessels at this stage have matured, effectively supporting long-term tissue maintenance and functional recovery. Dynamic regulation of blood perfusion during angiogenesis and the evolution of blood vessel morphology at different healing stages have a decisive impact on the speed of wound healing and the ultimate quality of repaired tissue3,9–12.

Multiple factors collectively regulate this complex physiological process. During the inflammatory stage of wound healing, activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway plays a crucial role. Binding of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) to its receptor, vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR2), can trigger the activation of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) and protein kinase B (AKT), promoting rapid proliferation and survival of endothelial cells to address acute wounds and inflammatory responses. In addition, angiopoietin-2 (ANG-2) is more active at this stage, helping to alleviate the stabilizing effect of angiopoietin-1 (ANG-1) on blood vessels and promoting the formation and reconstruction of new blood vessels to meet the high demand for immune cells and repair factors in damaged tissue. Furthermore, tunica interna endothelial cell kinase 2 (TEK, Tie2), activated by ANG-1, plays a pivotal role in mediating blood vessel maturation and stability, enhancing reparative processes.

In the proliferation stage, the role of AKT in the reorganization of the cellular cytoskeleton becomes particularly important. It regulates endothelial cell migration, supports vessel extension, and the formation of new vascular networks. At this stage, angiogenesis continues and the balance between ANG-1 and ANG-2, as well as the interaction between ANG-1 and its receptor TEK, plays a crucial role in the functionality and maturity of newly formed blood vessels. The role of ANG-1 gradually strengthens, promoting interaction between blood vessels and the surrounding matrix, which helps to improve the stability and functionality of newly formed blood vessels. Activation of the TEK receptor by ANG-1 contributes significantly to the structural integrity and functionality of the vascular network during this phase.

In the healing remodeling stage, the PI3K-AKT pathway continues to influence vascular permeability and barrier function, further affecting blood perfusion and nutrient exchange in the wound area. The role of ANG-1 becomes more significant, helping stabilize and mature blood vessels in the future, while the activity of ANG-2 decreases considerably. During this stage, the morphology of the blood vessels changes from irregular and tortuous to straight and organized, indicating the maturity and functional recovery of newly formed blood vessels and surrounding tissues13–15. Engagement of the TEK receptor by ANG-1 ensures the continuation of vascular stabilization and maturation processes. In effective wound healing, an increase in ANG-1 levels and a relative decrease in ANG-2 levels are generally observed during the proliferation and remodeling phases, reflecting the maturity and enhanced functionality of the vascular network. In contrast, in poorly healed processes, elevated levels of ANG-2 may lead to abnormal proliferation of the vascular network and functional impairment16,17.

The pilose antler is the only known mammalian structure capable of regeneration on an annual basis. Studies have shown that bioactive substances in pilose antler can significantly increase VEGF expression, thus promoting angiogenesis and improving local blood circulation, with positive effects on bone tissue repair and regeneration18,19. Further studies have revealed the characteristics of pilose antler stem cells (ASCs), which are mesenchymal stem cells with embryonic stem cell properties. They show potential application value in promoting angiogenesis and improving blood flow perfusion. ASCs can secrete growth factors such as VEGF, directly promoting endothelial cell proliferation and migration, thus promoting the formation of new blood vessels20,21. Existing studies indicate the potential of bioactive substances, drugs, small molecule therapies, and cell therapy in promoting angiogenesis and wound healing. However, there is a lack of focus on vascular maturation and remodeling, making it difficult to ensure the stability and functionality of newly formed vascular networks22,23. This emphasizes the importance of further research into these processes to develop more effective treatment methods. The study of vascular proliferation, angiogenesis, as well as maturation and remodeling, is of significant importance for understanding and improving the quality of wound healing and the treatment of other vascular-related diseases24. Our previous studies have shown that PAE can promote wound healing in rats25. This study will further explore the role of PAE in the regulation of endothelial cell function, angiogenesis, and blood flow perfusion during wound healing, revealing the molecular mechanisms by which PAE promotes wound healing and regulates angiogenesis.

Materials and methods

Preparation of Pilose antler extract (PAE) and mass spectrometry analysis

PAE was prepared according to methods detailed in the article25. In this study, the pilose antler samples were provided by Changchun Songxiang Animal Husbandry Technology Co., Ltd., sourced from the freshly harvested second antlers of the current season. The process involved the preparation of the sample proteins and their subsequent analysis using mass spectrometry techniques. The sample protein concentration was adjusted to 0.5 µg/µl, followed by a reaction at 37 ° C with 10 mM TCEP and 25 mM CAA for 30 min. The magnetic beads were then introduced and incubated at room temperature for 18 min. After the supernatant was removed, the beads were washed multiple times. Subsequently, trypsin was added to the beads in a 10: 1 protein to trypsin ratio and incubated for more than 4 h at 37 ° C. The digestion was terminated with 5% TFA and the sample was freeze-dried. This procedure was performed as previously described by Hughes CS et al.26.

For TMT labeling, 100 µg of digested samples were treated with TMT reagents for 1 h at room temperature and the reaction was stopped with ammonia. Liquid chromatography was performed under specific elution conditions. Mass spectrometry analysis was conducted using an ORBITRAP ECLIPSE mass spectrometer with pre-set parameters.

The identified proteins were annotated and quantified using Proteome Discoverer (PD) 2.4, searched against the Rattus norvegicus + Muntiacus reevesi protein database. Differentially expressed proteins (fold change ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67, p < 0.05) were subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses using the clusterProfiler R package. The background reference was set to the same Rattus norvegicus + Muntiacus reevesi database. Statistical significance was evaluated using the hypergeometric test, and p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Enriched terms with FDR < 0.05 were considered significant. The enrichment factor (observed-to-expected ratio) was calculated to estimate effect size, and results were visualized as bubble plots, in which bubble size indicates the number of proteins and the x-axis represents enrichment factor.

To ensure batch-to-batch consistency and quality control, all PAE samples were independently extracted six times following the same standardized protocol. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay (P0010, Beyotime, China), and molecular weight distribution was confirmed by SDS–PAGE, showing consistent banding patterns primarily at 35, 45, 75, and 140 kDa (Fig. 1 A). Each batch was further verified by LC–MS/MS proteomic fingerprinting to ensure compositional reproducibility. After extraction, all PAE samples were vacuum-dried at room temperature and stored under sealed, desiccated conditions until use, maintaining their stability and bioactivity.

Animal and wound preparation

One hundred eight-week-old male Wistar rats (180–200 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), a licensed supplier of laboratory animals affiliated with Charles River Laboratories. The animals were maintained under controlled environmental conditions with a 12:12 h light–dark cycle, a standard diet, and free access to water. All experimental procedures complied with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org) and were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (approval No. ERCCACMS-2106-13, approved on June 30, 2021).

For wound preparation, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane and the back hair was removed with depilatory cream, followed by skin cleaning. Two circular skin Sect. (1.4 cm in diameter and 1.54 cm² in area) were excised from the back, 1 cm from the spine on both sides. The wounds were then marked. Subsequently, the rats were randomly divided into three groups: Recombinant human epidermal growth factor gel, rh-EGF (Guilin Huano Wei Gene Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., S20020112) group, PAE group and model group. The model group received saline on the wounds, while PAE was dissolved in saline (8 mg/ml) and applied to the wound in the PAE group. Rh-EGF was dissolved in saline (7 IU/µl) and applied to the wounds in the rh-EGF group, each rat receiving 100 µl of the respective solution per wound. After treatment, 3 M Cavilon was applied to the wounds to ensure longer contact, and the rats were placed in clean cages with daily treatments. This procedure was performed as previously described by Li et al.25.

Wound healing assessment

Following anesthesia with isoflurane, rats were photographed on days 1, 5, 10, and 15 post-wounding using a digital camera held at a fixed distance. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ 8.0 software to calculate the wound healing rate, which is expressed as a percentage: (initial wound area - remaining wound area)/initial wound area × 100%. This procedure was performed as previously described by Li et al.25.

Laser doppler anemometer (LDA)

The LDA device (CAM1CVF, KK Research Technology Ltd., England) was used to examine the characteristics of the blood vessel. Mineral oil was applied to the rats’ back to improve the imaging conditions. The laser was precisely focused on the wound area, capturing three images at each site, each measuring 16 μm (horizontal) × 8 μm (vertical). ImageJ software was used for the analysis. We manually outlined and exported blood vessel images and used the Angiogenesis analysis plug-in for automated analysis, focusing on HUVECs phase contrast. This facilitated automatic generation of network and node diagrams, together with data on junctions, nodes, segments, mesh, and branches. This procedure was performed as previously described by Li et al.9.

To minimize subjective bias, all images were anonymized and randomly coded before analysis. Image quantification was performed using a pre-registered ImageJ macro with fixed thresholds and parameters for batch processing. Analyses were conducted by a single blinded operator, as the workflow was fully automated and parameter-dependent, making inter-observer variation minimal. Although no second independent observer was included, the standardized acquisition protocol and blinded automated analysis ensured reproducibility.

Laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI)

LSCI (SIM BFI HR Pro, Wuhan XunWei Optoelectronic Technology Co., Ltd., China) was used to observe blood flow and vessel diameter. After anesthetizing the rat with isoflurane, it was placed 30 cm away from the stereo microscope. Image analysis was performed with the SIM BFI-WF laser speckle blood flow imaging system (SIM Opto-Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China).

Blood perfusion on the back of the rat was measured in perfusion units (PU), along with the diameter of the blood vessel. The color scale on the perfusion image was from 0 to 366, divided into different ranges (0–90, 90–182, 182–274 and 274–366). The lowest range (0–90) was omitted because the low signal was not related to the wound area. ImageJ’s split channel function was used to analyze the red, green and blue channels separately, providing percentages for different regions of blood perfusion. This procedure was performed as previously described by Li et al.9.

HE staining of wound cross-sections

Skin tissue samples from wounds were collected at 5 days, 10 days, and 15 days post-injury. The collected tissue had the same area as the original wound. These tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by dehydration and embedding in paraffin to create 4 μm paraffin sections suitable for HE staining. The entire wound area was scanned using a panoramic scanner (VS120, Olympus Corporation, Japan), and the wound area measurements were analyzed using ImageJ 8.0 software.

Cell culture

The rat dermal microvascular endothelial cells (REVCs) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were sourced respectively from Qingqi Shanghai Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd. of China and Sciencecell of USA. Cell culture was carried out using DMEM medium (SH30022.01, HYCLONE, USA) and ECM medium (1001, Sciencecell, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (10099141, Cytiva, USA).

To assess the involvement of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, endothelial cells were pre-treated with pathway inhibitors before PAE exposure. LY294002 (10 µM, S1105, Selleck, China) was added 24 h prior to measurement. For cross-validation, Wortmannin (100 nM, S2758, Selleck, China) and MK-2206 (0.1 µM, S1078, Selleck, China) were added for 12 h before PAE treatment.

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CK04, Dojindo Chemical Technology Co., Ltd., Japan).

In vitro migration analysis

Cells were initially cultured in 24-well plates until a confluence of 70% to 80% was reached. Subsequently, PAE was diluted in serum-free medium to yield three distinct concentrations (250 µg/ml, 500 µg/ml, 1000 µg/ml). Cell monolayers were cultured with the respective PAE-containing media for 24 h.

To induce wound healing, sterile gun heads were used to create scratches in the center of the plates. The process was documented immediately and after a 6-hour interval using an SLR camera (DS126431, CANON INC., Japan) beneath an inverted phase-contrast microscope (CKX41, Olympus Corporation, Japan).

In vitro tube formation assay using HUVECs

Because of the inability of REVCs to form tubes, experiments were performed using HUVECs. Cells were initially seeded in 6-well plates and allowed to reach a confluence of 70%–80%. To evaluate pathway dependency, HUVECs were pre-treated with PI3K or AKT inhibitors before PAE exposure. Pre-treatment with 10 µM LY294002 for 24 h, 100 nM Wortmannin (S2758, Selleck, China) for 12 h, or 0.1 µM MK-2206 (S1078, Selleck, China) for 12 h was performed prior to PAE stimulation.

Given the high serum dependence of HUVECs, both inhibitors and PAE were prepared in ECM medium containing 1% serum. PAE was diluted to three concentrations (250 µg/mL, 500 µg/mL, and 1000 µg/mL) with or without inhibitors, and the cells were co-cultured for 24 h before harvesting with trypsin.

Tube formation experiments were conducted as follows: Matrigel was prepared a day in advance and stored overnight at 4 ℃ because of its temperature-sensitive nature. Matrigel was mixed with medium containing serum in a 2:1 ratio, and 50 µl of this mixture was added to each well in a 96-well plate, taking care not to cause bubble formation. After 1 h, the HUVECs were seeded on the Matrigel at a density of 10,000 cells per well. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 4 h before tube formation was observed and documented using digital photography.

Immunofluorescence staining

The skin tissue sections were subjected to dewaxing and treated with antigen retrieval buffer (AR0026, Boster Biological Technology Co. Ltd., China) for 30 min. Simultaneously, cell slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, followed by three washes with PBS to remove excess paraformaldehyde.

After blocking nonspecific binding, both skin tissue sections and cell slides were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with specific antibodies. The antibodies used included anti-CD31 (28083-1-AP, Proteintech, America, 1: 200), anti-p-AKT (AP0637, Abclonal, China, 1: 200), anti-Ki67 (ab15580, Abcam, USA, 1: 500), and anti-VEGFR2 (A5609, Abclonal, China, 1: 200). Subsequently, skin tissue sections and cell slides were washed with PBS three times, followed by incubation with CoraLite 594/488 conjugated secondary antibodies (SA00013-6/7/8, Proteintech Group Inc., USA, 1: 1000) at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the samples were mounted on Mounting Medium with DAPI (S2110, Solarbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China).

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from skin tissues or cell samples and quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0012, Beyotime, China). Subsequently, 30–40 µg of total protein lysates were separated by SDS gel electrophoresis.

Immunoblotting was performed using primary antibodies including anti-VEGFA (A0280, Abclonal, China, 1: 1000), anti-VEGFR2 (A5609, Abclonal, China, 1: 1000), anti-p-PI3K (ab278545, Abcam, America, 1: 2000), anti-PI3KCA (A12484, Abclonal, China, 1: 1000), anti-p-AKT1 (AP0637, Abclonal, China, 1: 1000), anti-AKT1 (A20799, Abclonal, China, 1: 1000), anti-β-ACTIN (66009-1-Ig, Proteintech, America, 1: 1000) and GAPDH (A19056, Abclonal, China, 1: 1000) to target specific proteins.

To visualize proteins of interest, horseradish peroxidase-coupled secondary antibodies (A0208/A0216, Beyotime, China) and Clarity Western ECL substrate (170–5061, BIO-RAD Laboratories, Inc., America) were used. Image capture was carried out using a chemiluminescence imaging system (MP ChemiDoc, BIO-RAD, America).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from skin tissue and cell samples using Trizol reagent (15596018, Invitrogen, America). Complementary DNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the ReverTra Aceq PCR RT Master Mix Kit (FSQ-201, TOYOBO, Japan). Subsequently, qRT-PCR analysis was performed using the SYBR Green Realtime PCR Master Mix Kit (QPK-201, TOYOBO, Japan) and a PCR instrument (1861096, BIO-RAD, America).

For the determination of relative levels of mRNA expression, the 2−△△CT method was used with GAPDH serving as the reference gene. The PCR primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Material 1 (Shanghai Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., China).

ELISA quantification of ANG-1 and ANG-2 in skin tissues

Skin tissues from wound areas were homogenized in ice-cold PBS containing protease inhibitors and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4℃. The supernatant was collected, and total protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0012, Beyotime, China). The levels of ANG-1 and ANG-2 were measured using rat ELISA kits (WOK-01801R1 and WOK-01618R1, Beijing Woke Biotech, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

In situ hybridization of VEGFA mRNA and CD31 co-staining in rat skin tissue

To visualize the spatial distribution of VEGFA mRNA within vascular regions of wound skin, a fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) combined with immunofluorescence co-staining approach was employed. Wound center tissues were fixed, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Hybridization was performed using a custom-synthesized VEGFA mRNA probe (5′-CY3-UUCCUGCAGCCUGGCUCACCGCCUUGGCUUGUCACAUCU-3′, Sangon Biotech, China) at 8 ng/µl, incubated overnight at 37 °C. After washing, sections were co-stained with a rabbit anti-CD31 antibody (1:200, ab222783, Abcam, America) and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody, followed by DAPI nuclear counterstaining. Fluorescence signals were visualized under a fluorescence microscope, where VEGFA, CD31, and nuclei appeared as red, green, and blue signals, respectively. Image analysis was performed using FIJI/ImageJ, applying Otsu’s automatic thresholding to segment CD31⁺ vascular regions. The mean fluorescence intensity and integrated density of VEGFA within these regions were quantified, background-subtracted, and expressed as VEGFA_in-vessel density, representing the relative expression level of VEGFA in vascular areas.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data represents the average result obtained from a minimum of 3 parallel experiments. Quantitative analysis of the image data was carried out using ImageJ 8.0 software, and statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 6.0. Quantitative proteomics data was analyzed using a t-test, while time-varying data was subjected to two-way analysis of variance. Post-hoc analyses of inter-group differences were conducted using either Bonferroni post-tests or Tukey’s post hoc test. The results are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A significance level of P < 0.05 or lower was considered statistically significant.

Result

Proteomic insights into pae’s composition and its role in angiogenesis and wound healing

To ensure the consistency and quality of PAE, six independent protein extractions were performed following the same standardized protocol. The SDS–PAGE profiles demonstrated highly consistent banding patterns across batches, confirming reproducibility of extraction and purity stability (Fig. 1 A). The protein bands were broadly distributed between 35 kDa and above 140 kDa, with the strongest intensity observed around 140 kDa, indicating that PAE is a reproducible protein mixture rather than a single purified compound. Corresponding LC–MS/MS proteomic characterizations and peptide coverage details are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

The proteomic analysis of PAE revealed its molecular characteristics, with relevant mass spectrometry data submitted to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository (PXD037328), ensuring the transparency and reproducibility of the research. Gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyzes revealed the significant impact of PAE on crucial biological processes such as platelet activation, cell adhesion, coagulation, ATP synthesis, and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, all crucial in angiogenesis (Fig. 1 B). Further analysis identified associations with key signaling pathways in angiogenesis, such as complement and coagulation cascades, the HIF-1 signaling pathway and the PI3K-Akt pathway (Fig. 1 C), providing information on the role of PAE in promoting angiogenesis.

Proteomic characterization and compositional consistency of PAE.

To verify the functional consistency between the compositional data and biological activity, proteomic comparisons were conducted between PAE and locally treated wound tissue. The results demonstrated elevated expression of proteins such as Angiotensin 1–10, Neuropilin-1, Vitronectin, Tenascin N, Cell division control protein 42 homolog, and transforming protein RhoA in PAE-treated wound tissues (Fig. 1 D). These proteins play diverse but complementary roles in angiogenesis: Neuropilin-1 acts as a co-receptor of VEGF, vital for angiogenesis27,28. Vitronectin is involved in cell adhesion and migration, key steps in angiogenesis29. Tenascin N is a component of the extracellular matrix, influencing cell-matrix interactions and angiogenesis30. Cell division control protein 42 homolog and transforming protein RhoA regulate cytoskeletal reorganization, crucial for vascular formation and repair31.

The protein network interaction and biological process enrichment analysis further elucidated the roles of Angiotensin 1–10, Neuropilin-1, and other proteins in the regulation of vascular tension, cell migration, and the vascular generation response (Fig. 1 E-F). These findings not only highlight the potential of PAE as an effective therapeutic agent for promoting wound healing and angiogenesis, but also provide a robust molecular basis for further investigation of its therapeutic mechanisms.

PAE treatment facilitates wound closure and angiogenesis in a rat Full-Thickness excision model

To investigate the promoting effects of PAE on wound healing and angiogenesis in rats’ dorsal wounds, HE staining was performed on wound tissues, revealing that on days 5, 10, and 15, the wound closure rate in the PAE-treated group increased significantly compared to the model group and the rh-EGF positive control group (Fig. 2 A༆C). It is particularly noteworthy that the observation was on day 15 of treatment in the PAE-treated group, where the morphological characteristics of the newly formed hair follicles appeared in the wound center (Fig. 2 B), signifying the restoration of skin structure and function. In addition, orderly and straight vessels were observed around the wound center (Fig. 2 I). Compared to the other two groups, PAE promoted angiogenesis and maturation more effectively.

The evolution of vascular morphology reflects the process of vascular maturation and functionalization during wound healing. From initially curved vessels to relatively straight vessels on day 15, this change not only reveals the dynamic process of vascular growth but also straight vessels are more conducive to the efficiency of blood circulation, which is crucial to provide essential oxygen and nutrients to healing tissues32,33. PAE significantly increased the visibility of blood vessels in the center of the wound and promoted the orderly arrangement of surrounding blood vessels, possibly because of its activating effect on VEGF and its promotion of the migration and differentiation of vascular progenitor cells34.

The analysis of LDA and the detailed quantification of the vascular network further confirmed the effect of PAE in promoting angiogenesis, with the increased number of vascular connection points indicating the increased complexity and functionality of the newly formed vascular network (Fig. 2 F-J). The results of the immunofluorescence staining of CD31 were consistent with the LDA findings, showing that PAE treatment increased the number of blood vessels in the wound center (Fig. 2 K-L), highlighting the potential role of PAE in promoting effective angiogenesis and improving wound healing.

In addition, the decrustation time of the wound is also a direct indicator reflecting the speed of epidermal reconstruction. In this study, PAE treatment significantly reduced decrustation time (Fig. 2 D-E), suggesting that PAE may promote wound healing by accelerating epithelialization and promoting the formation of new tissue. Early decrustation is crucial for the protection and maturation of underlying new tissues, helping to reduce the risk of infection and improve the quality of healing35,36.

PAE enhanced wound closure and angiogenesis.

These results indicate that PAE can accelerate wound closure, promote orderly vessel arrangement, and increase the number of newly formed blood vessels, thus effectively expediting wound healing and angiogenesis processes.

Importantly, no systemic toxicity was observed at the administered doses. Histological examination of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) from PAE-treated rats revealed normal tissue morphology without any signs of inflammation, necrosis, or pathological alterations, comparable to those in the control group. These findings confirm that PAE, at therapeutically effective concentrations, promotes angiogenesis and wound repair without inducing adverse systemic effects. Representative H&E images of major organs are provided in Supplementary Material 4.

Analysis of pae’s modulatory effects on angiogenic signaling pathways

In further exploring the mechanism of PAE regulation in wound healing and angiogenesis, we observed a significant elevation in the expression of Vegfa, Vegfr2, Pi3kca, and Akt1 mRNA following PAE treatment (Fig. 3 A), particularly notable on day 5 after wounding. These genes play an essential role in angiogenesis, and activation of Vegfa and its receptor Vegfr2 are crucial initiators of angiogenesis37. VEGFA, by binding to VEGFR2, initiates downstream PI3K-AKT signaling pathways, promoting endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and new vessel formation. Up-regulation of Akt1 further enhances cell survival signals, promoting endothelial cell survival and new vessel stability. In contrast, Pik3cb expression changes were insignificant, indicating a potentially more crucial role for Pik3ca in PAE-mediated angiogenesis regulation, while Pik3cb’s role in this process may be less direct, or its regulatory effect may depend more on protein level alterations than only mRNA expression. This highlights the possibility that different PI3K isoforms may exhibit various functional specificities within the intricate regulatory landscape of angiogenesis38,39.

Regulation of angiogenic factor gene and protein expression by PAE during wound healing.

Western blot further confirmed that PAE treatment increased the AKT1 phosphorylation level and the VEGFR2 protein level, particularly notable on day 5 post-treatment (Fig. 3 B-C). This suggests that PAE accelerates angiogenesis during the early stages of wound healing by activating crucial signaling pathways, with protein levels of these markers gradually returning to near-normal levels on days 10 and 15, reflecting the natural decrease in angiogenic activity as wound healing progresses, while also implying maturation and stabilization of the vascular network during the healing process. To pinpoint the cellular source of VEGFA in vivo, we additionally performed fluorescence in situ hybridization combined with CD31 immunofluorescence. The results showed a significant increase of Vegfa mRNA signal specifically within CD31+ vascular regions in the PAE group (yellow arrows), confirming intravascular up-regulation of VEGFA by PAE (Fig. 3 F–G). At the same time, we do not exclude a contribution from peri-vascular stromal cells (e.g., fibroblasts), which may provide paracrine VEGFA in vivo.

However, excessive angiogenesis can lead to poor wound healing. Therefore, the balance between angiogenesis and maturation is particularly crucial in wound healing, with Ang-1 and Ang-2 mRNA expression playing a significant role, jointly regulating the balance of angiogenesis and maturation40. Ang-1 is associated with vascular stabilization and maturation, typically binding to the Tek receptor (Tie2) to promote vascular stability and maturation, while Ang-2 is associated with angiogenesis and instability, capable of binding to Tek to regulate angiogenesis, but its role lies in promoting vascular breakdown and reconstruction, forming a dynamic equilibrium with Ang-1.

To further verify the secretion levels of these factors, ELISA was performed on wound tissue homogenates (Fig. 3 D-E). Consistent with the transcriptional trends, PAE markedly increased ANG-1 and decreased ANG-2 at day 10 compared with the model group, confirming that PAE promotes the transition from active angiogenesis toward vascular maturation. Moreover, in the PAE-withdrawn (D0-D5) group, ANG-1 elevation was significantly reduced at day 10, suggesting that sustained PAE exposure is required to maintain endothelial stabilization.

In our study, Tek expression increased significantly in the PAE treatment group on day 5, higher than in the other two groups, indicating early activation of angiogenesis under PAE treatment. However, Tek expression decreased on days 10 and 15, possibly reflecting the decrease in angiogenic activity as a result of vascular maturation and stabilization transition. Furthermore, the expression of Ang-2 increased significantly in the model group on day 10, reflecting the instability of angiogenesis, while in the PAE treatment group, the significant increase in Ang-1 and the relative decrease in Ang-2 indicated a transition towards vascular stabilization and maturation.

This, on the one hand, reveals the significant role of Tek and its interaction with Ang-1 and Ang-2 in the processes of angiogenesis, maturation, and remodeling, while, on the other hand, suggests that PAE can contribute to the maturation and stabilization of the vascular network, laying the foundation for tissue recovery in later stage. Together with the spatial evidence from VEGFA/CD31 co-localization (Fig. 3 F–G), the mRNA, protein, and ELISA data demonstrate that PAE not only activates early angiogenic signaling but also promotes subsequent vascular maturation through sustained regulation of the Ang-1/Ang-2/Tie2 axis.

At the same time, this also reveals the phenomenon of reduced expression of Ang-1 and Ang-2 in the rh-EGF treatment group, reflecting the limited effect of rh-EGF on these three key factors in angiogenesis, indicating that its mechanism to promote wound healing can directly activate EGF-related receptors, with a more significant effect on promoting epidermal cell migration (Fig. 3 A). These differences underscore the unique roles of different treatment methods in angiogenesis and wound healing processes and their potential biological significance.

PAE modulates key angiogenic factors in gene expressions and protein levels, promoting angiogenesis and maturation during wound healing. This balance is particularly crucial in the early stages of treatment, and the regulatory effect of PAE can be achieved through a unique mechanism different from traditional growth factors, providing new molecular targets and treatment strategies for wound therapy.

The PI3K inhibitor counteracts pae’s positive effects on wound healing and angiogenesis

To further validate whether PAE’s promotion of angiogenesis depends on PI3K, the animals were intervened with LY294002, an inhibitor of PI3K activation, at this stage. PI3K plays a central regulatory role in various biological processes, particularly in angiogenesis. Activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway promotes cell survival, proliferation, and migration, processes crucial for new vessel formation41.

The experimental results in Fig. 4 A-B demonstrate that, without the use of the LY294002 inhibitor, the wound healing rate in the PAE treatment group is significantly faster than that in the control group and the rh-EGF group (Fig. 4 A-B). However, with the application of LY294002, the difference in healing rates between the three groups (control, model and rh-EGF) was not significant and congestion appeared in the wound center, indicating that inhibition of PI3K weakened the pro-healing effect of PAE, suggesting that PAE’s promotion of wound healing can be achieved through activation of PI3K activity.

Using laser speck contrast imaging (LSCI), it was observed that, under conditions in which LY294002 was not used, the PAE treatment group showed a significant increase in wound blood flow on day 5, which is associated with earlier decrustation time and good blood perfusion at the wound site in the PAE group (Fig. 4 C༆E-H), providing the necessary oxygen and nutrients for the well-perfused wound42. On day 10, PAE treatment significantly increased the proportion of high blood flow perfusion areas (Fig. 4 D), indicating that PAE may promote vascular dilation and increased blood flow, providing more blood supply to the wound. This increase in areas of high blood flow perfusion may have a positive impact on angiogenesis and tissue repair processes. On day 15, the difference in blood flow perfusion between the three groups that did not use LY294002 was not significant, which may reflect the transition of wound healing to a new equilibrium state over time.

PAE activated PI3K to promote wound healing and blood flow restoration.

However, after inhibition of LY294002, no scabbing was observed in any group on day 5 (Fig. 4 C༆E-I), indicating that delayed scabbing may suggest that blocking the PI3K signaling pathway interfered with early wound healing, especially in wound contraction and scab formation43. Therefore, inhibiting PI3K may directly affect the migration ability of epithelial cells, thereby reducing the rate of healing. Typically, in the late stages of angiogenesis, newly formed vessels undergo pruning and maturation, leading to a decrease in blood perfusion and promoting tissue stabilization (Fig. 4 C,E-I). However, inhibition of the PI3K signaling pathway can disrupt this maturation process, resulting in the continued presence of numerous immature vessels, which can be highly permeable and unstable, leading to sustained high blood flow. In addition, the PI3K signaling pathway is closely associated with the coagulation mechanism, and its inhibition can interfere with platelet activation and aggregation, which can affect clot formation, thus affecting the normal progression of wound healing44.

In conclusion, PAE promotes wound healing and angiogenesis by activating the PI3K signaling pathway, while inhibition of PI3K can significantly disrupt this process.

PAE activates PI3K to promote Proliferation, Migration, and tubule formation in REVCs

Further elucidation of the modulation of endothelial cell function by PAE was demonstrated through in vitro experiments on REVCs and HUVECs. Initially, the optimal concentration range of PAE affecting endothelial cell activity was determined using CCK-8 assays. PAE significantly enhanced the viability of both RVECs and HUVECs in a concentration-dependent manner, with optimal effects observed at 250, 500, and 1000 µg/mL (Fig. 5A–B). Even at the highest tested concentrations, PAE did not show cytotoxicity, indicating good cellular tolerance.

PAE activates the PI3K–AKT pathway to promote endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation.

The proliferative effect of PAE was further examined using Ki67 immunofluorescence staining in RVECs. The proportion of Ki67-positive cells increased significantly in the PAE-treated group compared with the untreated control, indicating that PAE effectively promotes endothelial cell proliferation (Fig. 5 C and F).

Scratch assays were used to assess the effect of PAE on RVEC migration. PAE significantly accelerated scratch closure, demonstrating its ability to promote cell migration, whereas LY294002 slowed wound closure, confirming the involvement of the PI3K pathway (Fig. 5D and E).

Finally, tube formation assays were used to evaluate the angiogenic effect of PAE on HUVECs. The number and total length of tubular structures significantly increased after PAE treatment compared with the control group, whereas LY294002 abolished this effect, supporting the critical role of PI3K in the tubulogenesis process (Fig. 5G–I).

To further confirm the dependency of PAE’s pro-angiogenic effects on the PI3K–AKT pathway and to exclude possible off-target effects of LY294002, we conducted cross-validation using a structurally distinct PI3K inhibitor (Wortmannin) and a selective AKT inhibitor (MK-2206). Both inhibitors markedly suppressed PAE-induced migration of RVECs (Fig. 5L–M) and tube formation of HUVECs (Fig. 5N–P).

These results confirm that PAE promotes angiogenesis primarily through the PI3K–AKT axis, where inhibition of either PI3K or AKT effectively blocks its ability to enhance endothelial cell migration and tubulogenesis.

PAE regulates PI3K-AKT pathway to promote migration, and tube formation in REVCs

To explore the specific mechanisms and targets through which PAE affects the biological functions of endothelial cells (REVCs), mRNA expression and protein level assays were conducted. The results revealed significant up-regulation in the mRNA expressions of key genes associated with angiogenesis, including Vegfr2, Vegfa and the two subunits of Pi3k, Pi3kca and Pi3kcb, as well as Akt1, after PAE treatment. This finding suggests that PAE may activate angiogenesis by enhancing VEGFR2 signaling (Fig. 6 A). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that PAE treatment resulted in higher protein levels of VEGFR2, phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT1, and total protein level of PIK3CA and AKT1 (Fig. 6 B-C). The phosphorylated PI3K and AKT1 proteins are key indicators of the activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, and their increase suggests that PAE may enhance endothelial cell proliferation and migration by promoting the activation of this pathway45. Proteins Phosphorylation is crucial for the biological functions of endothelial cells. Phosphorylated AKT1 can promote cell survival and proliferation by influencing the level of cell cycle-related proteins and inhibiting apoptosis-related proteins. Similarly, phosphorylated PI3K improves endothelial cell response to VEGFA, further activating downstream signaling to promote cell migration and the formation of new blood vessels, which are essential for angiogenesis and tissue repair processes.

PAE modulates key factor expression in the PI3K-AKT pathway in REVCs.

However, after the addition of the PI3K inhibitor LY294002, a significant decrease in phosphorylated levels of AKT1 and PI3K, as well as the total level of AKT1 and PI3KCA protein, was observed, indicating that the positive biological effects of PAE in REVCs are mediated through the PI3K-AKT pathway, and inhibition of this pathway can counteract the positive effects of PAE (Fig. 6 B-C).

To further validate pathway specificity and exclude potential off-target effects, two additional inhibitors were employed: Wortmannin, a structurally distinct PI3K inhibitor, and MK-2206, a selective AKT inhibitor. Western blot results demonstrated that both inhibitors markedly suppressed the PAE-induced phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT (Fig. 6D–E), confirming that PAE’ s enhancement of endothelial cell activity and angiogenic signaling is dependent on PI3K and AKT activation.

In angiogenesis, VEGFR2 plays a central role by primarily responding to the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway to promote endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and the formation of new blood vessels. Phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT) is a key molecule in cell survival and proliferation signal transduction, and its level of phosphorylation usually increases when active in endothelial cells, marking activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway46,47.

These findings collectively demonstrate that PAE activates the PI3K–AKT signaling cascade in endothelial cells, and that inhibition of either PI3K or AKT abrogates this activation, supporting that PAE’ s pro-angiogenic effects are specifically mediated through the PI3K–AKT pathway.

We further investigated the effects of PAE on the expression levels of the key factors VEGFR2 and p-AKT1 in REVCs using immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 7). When comparing untreated REVCs with those treated with PAE, VEGFR2 expression increased significantly, mainly localized in the cytoplasm and cell membrane, with a more prominent expression in the cytoplasm (Fig. 7 A-B). This suggests that PAE can up-regulate the expression of VEGFR2 and may facilitate its localization to the cell membrane, which is essential for VEGFR2 to exert its receptor function and further signal transduction48.

PAE modulates the expression of VEGFR2 and p-AKT in REVCs.

Furthermore, phosphorylated AKT1 appeared as red spots in the cytoplasm after PAE treatment, indicating that PAE may enhance AKT1 phosphorylation and potentially affect its intracellular location (Fig. 7 C-D). Typically, phosphorylation of AKT1 activates downstream effector molecules, transmitting signals that regulate angiogenesis49.

Following the addition of the PI3K inhibitor LY294002, a decrease in the fluorescence intensity of VEGFR2 and p-AKT1 was observed. This not only indicates that PAE promotes the expression and phosphorylation of AKT1 through the PI3K-AKT pathway, but also that the PI3K inhibitor can reverse this effect. Interestingly, after treatment with LY294002, there was a notable change in cell morphology from round or irregular to spindle-shaped, which may indicate endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT). EndoMT is a cellular biological process in which endothelial cells acquire the characteristics of mesenchymal cells, often associated with tissue fibrosis and the development of vascular diseases. If PAE can prevent or reduce EndoMT, this can provide a new perspective on how PAE promotes vascular repair and regeneration by altering cell phenotype and function50,51.

Discussion

In wound healing, angiogenesis is crucial for maintaining wound cleanliness and promoting cell proliferation and migration. However, imbalances in the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, VEGFR2 and ANG1-2 can cause abnormal vascular morphology and proliferation, disrupting normal wound healing, and leading to slow healing, excessive scarring, or chronic wounds. Thus, precise regulation of these signaling pathways is vital for effective wound healing promotion and is essential in the development of new wound healing drugs. This study is the first to reveal the specific mechanism by which PAE promotes angiogenesis through the activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. This finding fills a gap in previous research, clarifying the role of pilose antler in promoting wound healing.

The pilose antler, the only mammalian organ with complete regeneration ability, possesses distinctive structures and physiological traits. It mainly comprises the base of the pilose antler, the main beam, the brow tine, and other branches or tips. The pilose antler base connects to the skull of the deer, serving as a vital region for antler growth and development, housing cells responsible for antler growth. The pilose antler is covered in a rich vascular and neural ‘velvet’, which supplies nutrients and protection for antler growth52,53. These structural features suggest the potential of pilose antler in promoting angiogenesis, particularly because of its abundant VEGF and other pro-angiogenic substances53,54. Recent studies have shown that pilose antler cells exhibit properties similar to those of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), with the capacity for self-renewal and multi-lineage differentiation, similar to embryonic stem cells. For example, studies have found that exosomes derived from pilose antler stem cells can accelerate wound healing, suppress the fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition, thus improving regenerative wound healing55. In addition, pilose antler stem cell-conditioned medium (ASC-CM) applied in a rat wound model demonstrated notable efficacy in promoting wound healing21. These attributes underscore the potential utility of pilose antler cells in promoting angiogenesis and wound healing. Most current wound healing studies rely on endpoint methods, overlooking the dynamic equilibrium of factors related to angiogenesis. This study examines dynamic alterations in wound healing and vascular reconstruction for the first time. We discovered that PAE significantly enhances wound healing rate and quality. In vitro experiments indicate that PAE can enhance endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and vessel formation by activating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 5). In vivo investigations validate that PAE can activate PI3K targets, modulate wound healing-associated vascular network formation, improve vascular morphology, maintain blood flow balance, offering a novel approach for treating vascular-related diseases and tissue repair (Figs. 2, 3 and 4).

In this study, we delved into the proteomic analysis to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the promotion of wound healing and angiogenesis by PAE (Fig. 1). Specifically, our investigation focused on the interaction between PAE and proteins such as Angiotensin 1–10 and Neuropilin-1, examining their potential involvement in modulating vascular tension, cell migration, and angiogenesis. Neuropilin-1, acting as a co-receptor of VEGF, plays a crucial role in angiogenesis (Fig. 1). The interaction between neuropilin-1, VEGF, and its receptor VEGFR triggers activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, a critical mechanism in angiogenesis (Fig. 1). PAE could potentially improve endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and neovascularization by activating this pathway (Fig. 1).

To validate our hypothesis, we evaluated the effect of PAE on wound healing using a full-thickness rat skin excision model. Traditional “endpoint” observations such as histological analysis and static imaging techniques in wound healing and angiogenesis studies do not capture the dynamic nature of the processes. These static observation methods overlook the essential characteristic of wound healing as a continuous and dynamic process. To overcome these limitations, we used advanced in vivo imaging techniques such as laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI) and laser doppler anemometry (LDA) to dynamically monitor wound healing and angiogenesis processes. LSCI technology, based on the principle of light scattering, enables real-time monitoring of blood flow dynamics, offering quantitative insights into blood perfusion and microcirculation status. Similarly, LDA, as an imaging technique based on the principle of laser Doppler, facilitates precise observation of vessel shape and orientation, analyzing blood flow characteristics by measuring changes in scattered light frequency. In addition, we performed image extraction analysis using an ImageJ-based image analysis plugin for vessel network analysis. This plugin automatically extracted and quantified vessel network features such as vessel length, branching number, and junctions. Furthermore, we used RGB color channel splitting techniques to differentiate the degree of blood perfusion and calculated the area proportion of each region. This technique, which our research team has previously published related articles, improves our understanding of the dynamics of wound healing and angiogenesis processes9.By combining these two optical imaging methods, we observed that PAE treatment significantly accelerated wound healing, promoted orderly vessel arrangement, facilitated the transition from tortuous to straight vessel morphology, and regulated vessel sprouting and maturation. Previous research has indicated that ‘twisting microvessels promote wound healing through sprouting angiogenesis56 ' and PAE treatment increased the number of newly formed vessels and improved blood perfusion in the wound area (Fig. 2). In addition, we conducted in vitro cell experiments to further validate the functional effects of PAE on endothelial cells, including promoting their proliferation, migration, and tube formation (Fig. 5).

In exploring the regulatory mechanism of PAE on vascular formation during wound healing, we observed a significant elevation in mRNA expression of genes closely associated with angiogenesis, such as Vegfa, Vegfr2, Pik3ca and Akt1, especially noteworthy on day 5 post-wound formation (Fig. 3). The activation and up-regulation of these genes are crucial drivers of angiogenesis, with increasing of VEGFA and its receptor VEGFR2, activating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, promoting endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and new vessel formation. Up-regulation of AKT1 further enhances cell survival signals, helping to stabilize and mature new vessels. According to existing research, the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway plays a central role in angiogenesis, as it can activate increased VEGF secretion and regulate other angiogenic factors, such as nitric oxide and angiopoietin57. This is consistent with the observed trends, indicating that PAE may influence angiogenesis through similar mechanisms. Different PI3K isoforms can exhibit different functional specificities in this process, as evidenced in our study, demonstrating the role of PAE in this complex regulatory network. In addition, we found that PAE treatment increased AKT1 phosphorylation levels and VEGFR2 protein level, particularly on day 5 post-treatment, indicating that PAE accelerates angiogenesis in the early stages of wound healing by activating critical angiogenic signaling pathways. As wound healing progresses, these protein levels gradually return to near-normal levels, reflecting the natural decrease in angiogenic activity and vascular network maturation and stabilization.

Furthermore, PAE significantly affected the process of angiogenesis and maturation by regulating the expression of Ang-1, Ang-2, and their receptor, Tek (Tie2). In the PAE-treated group, Ang-1 increased significantly on day 10, while Ang-2 expression was lower, indicating a trend toward vascular maturation and stabilization. Meanwhile, Tek initially increased after treatment but decreased on days 10 and 15, consistent with the trend of vascular maturation, suggesting possible mechanisms of PAE in promoting vascular maturation. In addition, ELISA results (Fig. 3D-E, newly added and highlighted in the revised manuscript) confirmed that PAE upregulated ANG-2 on day 5 and ANG-1 on day 10, consistent with mRNA expression trends, indicating a time-dependent regulation of the Ang-1/Ang-2/Tie2 axis.

This regulation of Ang-1, Ang-2, and Tek expression reveals that the role of PAE in wound healing is not limited to promoting angiogenesis, but also includes a positive effect on vascular maturation and stabilization, providing an important biological basis for further therapeutic research.

To verify the role of PAE in the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, we used the PI3K inhibitor LY294002. Experimental results indicated that PAE enhances endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tubule formation by activating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 5). Specifically, PAE activates phosphorylated PI3K. PI3K then phosphorylates PIP2 (phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate) to PIP3 (phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate), recruiting and activating PDK1 (phosphoinositide-dependent kinase-1). PDK1 further phosphorylates and activates AKT (protein kinase B). Activated AKT triggers a series of downstream signaling events that promote endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tubule formation, ultimately facilitating angiogenesis. The PI3K inhibitor LY294002 significantly disrupted this process, confirming that PAE promotes angiogenesis through the activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 4).

Importantly, PAE is a protein-rich crude extract composed of multiple bioactive proteins rather than a single purified compound. The consistent proteomic profile across batches indicates that its biological activity most likely arises from synergistic interactions among these protein components rather than an isolated molecule. Although potential antagonistic effects between components cannot be entirely excluded, the reproducible pro-angiogenic outcomes across independent batches suggest that any internal interactions within PAE are cooperative and stable under physiological conditions.

Given the complex nature of this protein mixture, direct experimental evaluation of component–component interactions was not feasible in the current study. Instead, the uniform biological effects observed in both in vivo and in vitro assays indirectly support a balanced synergistic mechanism rather than competitive interference. Future studies will focus on stepwise fractionation and bioactivity-guided assays to identify specific protein fractions responsible for angiogenic regulation, and to systematically assess their individual and combined effects on endothelial signaling pathways.

In summary, this study focused on exploring the regulatory effects of PAE on angiogenesis and maturation during wound healing. We found that PAE significantly promoted endothelial cell behaviors such as proliferation, migration, and tubulogenesis by activating the PI3K-AKT and ANG1-2 signaling pathways, thus accelerating wound healing. The effects of PAE extend beyond the promotion of vascular formation to include the regulation of vascular maturation and stabilization, as evidenced by changes in Ang-1 and Ang-2 expression. The live imaging techniques we used, such as LSCI and LDA, provided previously unknown methods for dynamic observation of angiogenesis and wound healing, The application of this method reveals the time-dependent regulatory effects of PAE on angiogenesis and remodeling during different stages of wound healing. Additionally, our research not only focuses on angiogenesis but also delves into the regulatory role of PAE in the processes of vascular maturation and stabilization. Observations of vascular morphological changes through LDA indicate that, from angiogenesis to maturation, the newly formed vessels in the PAE group transition from a tortuous network to long, straight, and stable mature vessels. This provides a basis for developing new wound treatment strategies. These findings suggest that PAE can serve as a potential therapeutic agent to promote wound healing and vascular regeneration and provide a theoretical basis and direction for the development of novel wound healing-promoting drugs.

Conclusion

The complex process of wound healing requires us to seek effective treatment strategies. In our study, we investigated the effects of PAE on angiogenesis and maturation, finding that PAE can significantly enhance endothelial cell behavior, such as proliferation, migration, and tubule formation, by activating the PI3K-AKT and ANG1-2 signaling pathways, thus accelerating wound healing. However, our study also has certain limitations. In the proteomics analysis, we revealed the interaction between PAE and proteins such as Angiotensin 1–10, Neuropilin-1, and their potential roles in the regulation of vascular tension, cell migration, and angiogenic response. Notably, Neuropilin-1—an essential VEGF co-receptor—also plays a key role in neural development and neurovascular regulation, suggesting that PAE may exert coordinated effects on both angiogenic and neural repair pathways.

Consistent with this, previous studies have shown that extracts from pilose antler tissue can promote neurite outgrowth from neural explants, which is associated with nerve growth factor (NGF) expression in regenerating antler tissues58,59. These findings suggest that PAE might possess neurotrophic properties that couple vascular and neural regeneration through NGF-mediated signaling. Future research will therefore explore the neuroregulatory effects of PAE by immunofluorescent co-staining of βⅢ-tubulin, NF200, and NGF to evaluate neuronal regeneration and neurovascular coupling within wound tissues.

In addition, previous studies by our research group have shown that PAE restores type I and III collagen deposition and promotes fibroblast proliferation by activating the TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling pathway, thereby accelerating regenerative wound healing25. Given that collagen remodeling and angiogenesis are tightly coupled during tissue regeneration, subsequent studies will employ Masson’ s trichrome staining and second harmonic generation (SHG) imaging to quantify collagen I/III deposition and fiber orientation, aiming to clarify how PAE regulates extracellular matrix remodeling in concert with vascular repair.

Future research should focus on the molecular mechanisms, long-term effects, and safety of PAE, comparing its efficacy with other angiogenesis promoters, and validating its application in different wound types. Additionally, exploring the use of PAE in bioengineered scaffolds and dressings, its impact on genetic and epigenetic changes, and its regulation of inflammation and immune responses will be essential. Developing personalized wound care plans will further optimize treatment outcomes and deepen the understanding of PAE’s role in wound healing.
