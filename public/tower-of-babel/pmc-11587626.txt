Time-dependent personalized prognostic analysis by machine learning in biochemical recurrence after radical prostatectomy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Kodai Sato, Shinichi Sakamoto, Shinpei Saito, Hiroki Shibata, Yasutaka Yamada, Nobuyoshi Takeuchi, Yusuke Goto, Sazuka Tomokazu, Yusuke Imamura, Tomohiko Ichikawa, Eiryo Kawakami
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFor biochemical recurrence following radical prostatectomy for prostate cancer, treatments such as radiation therapy and androgen deprivation therapy are administered. To diagnose postoperative recurrence as early as possible and to intervene with treatment at the appropriate time, it is essential to accurately predict recurrence after radical prostatectomy. However, postoperative recurrence involves numerous patient-related factors, making its prediction challenging. The purpose of this study is to accurately predict the timing of biochemical recurrence after radical prostatectomy and to analyze the risk factors for follow-up of high-risk patients and early detection of recurrence.MethodsWe utilized the machine learning survival analysis model called the Random Survival Forest utilizing the 58 clinical factors from 548 patients who underwent radical prostatectomy at Chiba University Hospital. Background

Prostate cancer is one of the common cancers in men, with 270,000 diagnoses in the United States by 2022, and an estimated 34,500 deaths annually, making it the second leading cause of death in men [1]. There are various treatment options depending on the stage of prostate cancer progression and the patient’s background. Radical prostatectomy (RP) is one of the most common options for curative treatment [2, 3]. Several studies with > 5 years of follow-up after RP showed that the Biochemical recurrence (BCR) rate was approximately 20% and that early prediction and intervention of BCR cases may improve prognosis [4–6]

In patients at high risk of recurrence, postoperative treatment includes radiation therapy (RT) for suspected local recurrence cases. RT offers both adjuvant therapy for patients predicted to experience postoperative recurrence and salvage therapy for those who experience recurrence. In prostate cancer, both approaches have shown comparable treatment outcomes, with salvage therapy demonstrating advantages in terms of side effects [7–9]. On the other hand, for cases suspected of having distant micrometastases or cases unsuitable for RT, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) is selected. To ensure timely administration of RT and ADT treatments, close follow-up based on accurate prognostic predictions and analysis of prognostic factors is necessary to detect BCR early. Adverse prognostic factors include aggressive malignant pathological findings in the resected prostate, which have been reported in approximately 30% of cases [10, 11]. However, it has also been reported that 35% of cases with aggressive malignant pathological findings do not experience recurrence, highlighting the complexity of predicting BCR after RP [12, 13].

Prognostic prediction algorithms, such as the D’Amico and Cancer of the Prostate Risk Assessment (CAPRA) score have been used to estimate BCR risk. In addition, machine learning analysis has been performed with the aim of further improving accuracy [14–16]. Machine learning has been used in cancer research to make highly accurate predictions based on multiple factors in a variety of settings, including diagnosis, treatment selection, and prognosis prediction [17–20]. However, existing models struggle with estimating the timing of recurrence and analyzing risk factors. Analyzing the timing of recurrence, such as early and late recurrence, is crucial for predicting BCR [21]. We believe that conducting prognosis analysis considering the time to recurrence and performing time-dependent analysis to understand which factors are associated with early and late recurrence is essential. However, such research has not been conducted thus far.

In this study, we created a prognostic model that analyzed the time-dependent relationship between the timing of recurrence and prognostic factors. Our study demonstrated personalized prognosis analysis and its rationale after RP by employing a machine learning prognostic model. This prognostic model contributes to the early detection of recurrence by enabling clinicians to conduct appropriate follow-ups for high-risk patients.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study initially included all 553 cases diagnosed with. prostate cancer and treated with RP (by open, laparoscopic, or robotic surgery) at Chiba University Hospital between 2006 and 2020. Inclusion criteria consisted of patients who underwent a prostate biopsy and were pathologically diagnosed with prostate cancer, and subsequently underwent RP during this period. Cases were excluded if medical records were incomplete, which precluded verification of recurrence status or observation period. After excluding five cases due to such missing data, a final cohort of 548 cases was analyzed.

Patients underwent prostate biopsy using transrectal ultrasonography and were diagnosed with prostate cancer after a pathological review according to the Gleason scoring system. Computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and bone scans were performed for staging according to the International Union for Cancer Central (UICC) classification [22]. The risk classification for BCR after RP was performed according to the D’Amico risk classification [14]. Clinical information, pathological review of surgical specimens, 39 biomarkers of blood and urine collected preoperatively, and event occurrence and time to event of BCR were used to create the model (Supplementary Table S1). Postoperative BCR was defined as two consecutive postoperative serum prostate specific antigen(PSA) levels greater than 0.2 ng/ml according to the AUA guidelines [23].

Data splitting and missing value imputation

We randomly divided the dataset into training and testing cohorts. To assess any bias in the factors between the two cohorts, we used the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. We considered p value < 0.05 to be statistically significant.

There were missing values in the dataset, the proportion of which was 5.46%. We performed missing value imputation using the missforest (MF) algorithm [24]. MF is a nonparametric missing-value imputation for mixed-type data based on Random Forest (RF), a machine-learning algorithm consisting of many decision trees that learns data with no missing values as supervised data, predicts and assigns missing values, and can complete missing values even when continuous and categorical variables are mixed.

Machine learning models

We used the Random Survival Forest (RSF), a machine-learning survival analysis tool, to predict recurrence after RP. The RSF is an ensemble tree method that uses machine learning to analyze right-censored survival data [25]. The RSF calculates the cumulative hazard function (CHF) using explanatory variables, occurrence of the event, and time to the event as supervised data. The RSF were developed with scikit-learn version 1.2.0 and Python version 3.8.5.

Validation of model accuracy

We employed the time-dependent Area Under Curve (AUC) to validate the model accuracy [26]. In survival analysis, global discrimination indices, such as Harrell’s c-index may not be sufficient, and a time-dependent evaluation of discrimination ability is required [27]. We considered that the time-dependent AUC can evaluate the time-dependent dynamic discriminative power, and then used scikit-survival version 0.19.0, a survival analysis module of scikit-learn, to calculate the time-dependent AUC. A calibration plot was used to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities of the RSF and observed frequencies.

Stratification of BCR risk computed with RSF

To facilitate the interpretation of predicted probabilities computed by RSF, appropriate thresholds were set. Cases with recurrence probabilities lower than this threshold were categorized as low risk for BCR, while those above were classified as high-risk for BCR. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted using predicted recurrence probabilities of testing cohort at postoperative 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year intervals, alongside actual recurrence outcomes, to determine suitable thresholds based on the Youden Index. This approach enabled the creation of an easily interpretable and clinically applicable classification system for risk assessment.

Evaluation and visualization of time-dependent changes in risk factors

SurvSHAP(t) was used to assess the risk factors for BCR after RP and their time-dependent changes [28]. SurvSHAP(t) enables humans to understand the black-box of survival analysis by detecting time-dependent variable effects and calculating their local variable importance by aggregating them. It is based on Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), which are widely used in the domain of machine learning and are applicable to survival analysis models [29]. We obtained SurvSHAP(t) from the model created using RSF. Calculating SurvSHAP(t) by inputting all variables incurs a large calculation cost, and is difficult to realize. Therefore, we calculated SurvSHAP(t) with top 10 important variables, which is a reasonable computational complexity. We obtained the SurvSHAP(t) of RSF using the survshap module version 0.1.1.

Variable selection for each model

To create the RSF model, originally 58 variables were used. These variables included factors previously reported in the literature to correlate with prostate cancer prognosis, blood test data with less than 50% missing values, and as many perioperative factors as possible. For the risk factor analysis using SurvSHAP(t) and accuracy validation with the Cox proportional hazards model, the large number of variables posed significant computational challenges. Therefore, we selected the top 10 variables based on Permutation Importance from the 58 variables used in the final RSF model. Using these top 10 factors, we reconstructed the RSF model to analyze the temporal changes in factor importance through SurvSHAP(t) analysis.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

A total of 548 patients with RP were divided into training data (n = 383, 70%) and testing data (n = 165, 30%). Table 1 presents the main patient characteristics for all cases, training data, and test data. No statistically significant differences were observed for any major variables in the training and testing data. The BCR rate in all cases was 13.0%, while recurrence rate in the training and test data was 12.0% and 15.2%, respectively. No statistically significant differences were observed between the recurrence rates of the training and test data (p = 0.333). The median observation period for recurrence cases was 60.4 months, while the observation period for non-recurrence cases was 53.2 months, with no significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.455).

AUC and predicted probabilities for RSF

We developed an RSF model for predicting recurrence after RP using patient data, and then tested the accuracy of the prediction model using time-dependent AUC. The mean AUC of RSF was 0.785, and for comparison with prognostic scores by statistical analysis, we obtained the mean AUC of the Cox proportional hazards model, CAPRA and D’Amico, which was 0.704, 0.710 and 0.658 (Fig. 1A). Additionally, the time-dependent AUC values for predicting BCR at the surgery date, postoperative year 1, and postoperative year 2 with RSF were 0.770, 0.798, and 0.814, respectively (Table 2). Notably, RSF exhibited a higher predictive accuracy for early postoperative recurrence compared to the Cox proportional hazards model, CAPRA and D’Amico. In addition, the RSF was used to predict the BCR probability of individual patients. It was visually observed that the recurrence prediction probability tended to be higher in the patients who relapsed than those without relapse, and the RSF discriminated recurrence with relatively high accuracy (Fig. 1B). The results of the calibration plot conducted to validate the predictive accuracy of the RSF are shown in Supplementary Figure S1. It was qualitatively demonstrated that there is no substantial difference between the actual recurrence probabilities and the predicted recurrence probabilities.

Stratification of BCR Risk using RSF and Kaplan-Meier curves

Based on the recurrence probabilities calculated by the RSF model, the thresholds for classifying prognosis at postoperative 1, 3, and 5 years were found to be 6%, 13%, and 16%, respectively. These values were determined using the Youden Index. Cases with predicted recurrence probabilities higher than these thresholds were classified as high-risk for BCR. Using these thresholds, cases were stratified, and Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted (Fig. 2). Significant differences were observed between low-risk and high-risk groups in all analyses at 1-year (p < 0.005, Fig. 2A), 3-year (p < 0.005, Fig. 2.B), and 5-year (p < 0.005, Fig. 2C) postoperative periods based on log-rank tests.

Time-dependent risk factors with SurvSHAP(t)

In the analysis using RSF, seminal vesicle invasion (SV), gleason primary score (GS), prostate-specific antigen (PSA), gamma-glutamyl Transpeptidase(GTP), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), resection margin positive (RM), lactate dehydrogenase(LDH), PSA density(PSAD), creatinine(CRE), operative time(op_time), were the 10 most important factors (Supplementary Table S2). And we demonstrated that the importance of these factors dynamically changes for each recurrence time(Fig. 3A). In this study, factors, such as GS, PSA, and SV, had a large effect on early BCR within one year; thereafter, the effect of PSA relatively decreased, and the effects of SV and GS increased. Additionally, we obtained an aggregated SurvSHAP(t), which is the sum of the absolute values of SurvSHAP(t) at each time point, to indicate the contribution of each feature to the prediction (Fig. 3B). Therefore, it is easy to intuitively understand the SurvSHAP(t) of each feature value. GS, SV, and PSA contributed significantly to the prediction of BCR. In this way, demonstrating the varying impact of risk factors with each recurrence period contributes to a better understanding of patient profiles for early and late recurrence.

Personalized BCR prediction

We created an individualized model using RSF and SurvSHAP(t) to output the time of recurrence and the probability for each patient. This predictive model can explain the reasons for predictions on a patient-specific basis (Supplementary Figure S2). By inputting the ten factors shown in Fig. 3B, this model produces the outcomes. Figure S2A shows the time-dependent changes in the predictive probability of recurrence in BCR cases and an individual patient’s risk of recurrence. Factors with a negative SurvSHAP(t) indicated a tendency towards BCR. In this case, SV contributed the most to the prediction of the BCR, followed by GS. Figure S2B shows the time-dependent changes in the predictive probability of recurrence in non-BCR cases and an individual patient’s risk of recurrence. Factors with positive SurvSHAP(t) tended to be non-BCR. Specifically, in this case, a low GS contributed the most to the prediction of non-BCR, followed by the absence of SV and low PSA levels. In this way, the model we have created has the potential to establish a novel application for personalized BCR prediction.

SurvSHAP(t) for three groups classified by CAPRA score

The prognostic prediction model we have developed enables a time-dependent analysis of risk factors for patients stratified by existing scoring systems. We applied this method to the CAPRA score. We divided the test data into three groups according to the CAPRA score (0–2 low risk, 3–5 intermediate risk, 6–10 high risk) [30], and summarized SurvSHAP(t) for each of the three groups (Fig. 4). In the high-risk group, SV had a greatest effect on prognostic prediction, whereas in the intermediate-risk group, GS had a greatest effect on prediction. This suggests that the high- and intermediate-risk groups may have different risk factors for recurrence. In the low-risk group, no characteristic trends in prostate cancer-specific factors were observed, which may be owing to the small number of BCR cases.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a prognostic model to analyze the time-dependent relationship between recurrence timing and prognostic factors after RP. By employing a machine learning prognostic model, we achieved individualized prognostic analysis and its rationale after RP. This prognostic prediction model provides clinicians with information on the timing of postoperative recurrence, enabling appropriate follow-up of high-risk patients and contributing to the early detection of recurrence.

BCR prediction after RP has been performed using statistical analysis and risk classification, such as D’Amico and CAPRA score [14, 15]. However, such scoring system only stratifies patients by risk based on their scores and cannot make personalized predictions for patients taking various factors into account. BCR of prostate cancer is influenced by a complex interplay of various factors, and the values of these factors vary for each patient. Therefore, to predict the timing of recurrence more accurately, personalized predictions for each patient are needed instead of stratification. In this study, we were able to predict individual recurrence timing and probability using machine learning. By considering the time to recurrence, we can distinguish between patients experiencing early and late recurrence after RP. This is expected to lead to appropriate treatment selection according to the time of recurrence. Specifically, this information will support proposing options such as adjuvant RT or ADT in the early postoperative period for patients predicted to experience early recurrence. Conversely, for patients anticipated to have late recurrence, the option of careful PSA monitoring until recurrence becomes feasible.

Machine learning survival analysis is computationally complex and the basis for the predictions is difficult to understand. In order to enhance the interpretability of the machine learning model, we analyzed the time-dependent changes in the predictive factor importance for BCR after RP using survSHAP(t). As a result, survSHAP(t) demonstrated a notably substantial influence of preoperative Gleason score and seminal vesicle invasion on prognostic prediction. We elucidated that these factors consistently maintain a high impact across the chronological spectrum ranging from early to late recurrence. Previous studies have demonstrated that Gleason score and seminal vesicle invasion significantly influence recurrence, and our findings are consistent with this evidence [31, 32]. In this study, we consistently observed a high impact of these factors on BCR; however, we did not elucidate their relationship with the timing or patterns of recurrence. Nonetheless, there are reports indicating that a high Gleason score and positive surgical margins increase the risk of BCR, as well as findings suggesting that the presence of seminal vesicle invasion combined with extraprostatic extension is associated with positive lymph node metastasis. Therefore, it is possible that a combination of multiple factors could enable more accurate predictions of recurrence timing and patterns [33, 34]. PSA ranked prominently as a subsequent prognostic factor following these variables. However, while PSA retained its significance as a prognostic factor for early recurrence, its importance as a factor predicting late recurrence was comparatively less pronounced. This observation suggests the potential for PSA to be a prognostic predictor with distinct characteristics from seminal vesicle invasion and Gleason score.The intriguing observation is the substantial contribution of PSA to early recurrence prediction while its contribution to late recurrence prediction is relatively limited. The majority of adverse prostate cancer outcomes can be attributed to preoperative micrometastasis, with previous research demonstrating a correlation between the presence of micrometastasis and PSA levels [35]. Considering the influence of PSA values obtained in this study on early recurrence with lesser impact on late recurrence, it can be inferred that cases with high PSA levels preoperatively are more likely to harbor micrometastasis, suggesting a mechanism where they are prone to early recurrence. Estimating the mechanisms of recurrence holds clinical significance, as differences in recurrence mechanisms greatly affect the choice of additional treatment. Post-RP recurrence patterns mainly involve local recurrence or distant metastasis. Local RT is effective for local recurrence, but its efficacy is limited for distant metastasis, thus systemic treatment is considered. Hence, in cases strongly suspected of recurrence due to micrometastasis, systemic treatments like ADT, rather than localized RT, become viable options. In this study, we demonstrated the potential to predict recurrence mechanisms and guide treatment selection based on perioperative information.

This study has some limitations. First, it was a retrospective cohort study with a limited number of patients and relapses. Although there was no statistically significant difference between the baseline characteristics of the training and test data when the number of recurrences was small, the bias of the features of the recurrent cases may cause overfitting, resulting in a decrease in prediction accuracy and an error in the risk factors importance. The second limitation is the single-institution cohort. The advantage of machine learning is that many factors can be input into the analysis; however, to realize this, it is necessary to collect accurate data. However, it is practically difficult to accurately collect, integrate, and analyze several data from multiple facilities because of differences in electronic medical record systems and information collection schemes between facilities. Because this was a single-institution cohort, there may be biases owing to the selection of specimen items and follow-up methods, as well as biases in facility-specific operators and surgical techniques. The third limitation is the lack of external validation. In this study, the sample size is relatively small for a machine learning model, and we did not create a separate validation set within the patient cohort. Further splitting the dataset would reduce the model’s predictive accuracy and validity. Instead, we used the entire cohort to maximize the robustness of the findings. For model accuracy verification, we utilized testing data that was not used in the model creation as the validation dataset. However, we acknowledge the importance of external validation and plan to validate our results using an independent cohort in future research.

Future research could focus on multi-institutional studies involving larger and more diverse patient populations, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, developing standardized protocols for data collection and analysis across institutions could help mitigate biases associated with electronic medical records and information gathering. Considering the integration of more advanced machine learning techniques and larger datasets may also improve prediction accuracy and provide deeper insights into the importance of risk factors. By emphasizing these directions, we aim to encourage other researchers to build upon our findings and contribute to a more robust body of evidence in this field.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the capability of utilizing machine learning survival analysis to tailor personalized predictions of post-prostatectomy BCR timing and probability for patients. Furthermore, this model allows stratification of recurrence risk by setting thresholds for recurrence probabilities and illustrates the impact of prognostic factors over time, thereby providing a clear explanation of the predicted outcomes. We expect that such machine learning models will lead to appropriate follow-up of high-risk patients for BCR after RP and contribute to the early detection of recurrence.
