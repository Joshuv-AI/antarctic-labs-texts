Methods used in adult dental extraction patients to improve psychoemotional status: systematic review
Authors: B. Mažutė, I. Astramskaitė-Januševičienė, G. Juodžbalys, D. Razukevičius
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dental anxiety is a common problem that may negatively affect patients undergoing tooth extraction procedures. Non-pharmacological interventions have been proposed as supportive strategies to reduce anxiety and improve patient comfort.The aim of this systematic review was to analyze and summarize non-pharmacological methods used to improve psychoemotional status in adult patients undergoing tooth extraction procedures. Methods A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed (MEDLINE) for studies published between 18 September 2014 and 18 September 2024. A supplementary search was also conducted in ScienceDirect on 18 September 2024, using an adapted keyword strategy and the publication year filter (2014–2024), to reduce the risk of retrieval bias. Randomized controlled trials involving adult patients undergoing dental extraction procedures and evaluating non-pharmacological interventions for anxiety reduction were included. Studies involving pharmacological sedation, non-extraction procedures, or lacking psychoemotional outcomes were excluded. Results The PubMed search identified 3692 records. After screening and eligibility assessment according to predefined criteria, 25 randomized controlled trials were included in the qualitative synthesis. The most commonly investigated interventions included music therapy, virtual reality, aromatherapy, guided relaxation techniques, informational interventions, and acupuncture-based methods. Anxiety outcomes were measured using validated scales such as the Modified Dental Anxiety Scale (MDAS), State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), and Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety and Information Scale (APAIS), as well as physiological parameters including heart rate, blood pressure, and salivary cortisol. Most studies reported reductions in anxiety indicators following non-pharmacological interventions. Conclusion Non-pharmacological interventions may contribute to reducing anxiety in patients undergoing tooth extraction procedures. However, heterogeneity among interventions and outcome measures limits direct comparison across studies. Further well-designed randomized controlled trials are needed to strengthen the evidence base.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08209-6.

Introduction

Dental anxiety is a common issue among adults undergoing tooth extraction procedures, often leading to avoidance of dental care, increased stress, and deteriorated oral health. It is considered a significant barrier to accessing dental treatment, negatively affecting both patients’ psychological well-being and clinical outcomes. According to Logan and Marek, “anxiety is both a physical and emotional response to an anticipated experience that the individual perceives as threatening in some way. […] In its most extreme form, anxiety may significantly limit the individual’s ability to function in everyday life.” [1].

Traditionally, pharmacological approaches such as sedation or the use of anxiolytics have been applied to manage dental anxiety. Finn et al. [2] highlight that oral benzodiazepines can be an effective option for managing dental anxiety in patients who experience significant fear before dental procedures.”

However, the widespread use of such interventions is not always recommended, as they are associated with potential side effects, contraindications, and risk of misuse [2]. As a result, growing attention has been directed toward non-pharmacological methods such as music therapy, guided imagery, virtual reality, audiovisual distraction, and patient education, which aim to reduce anxiety without medical sedation.

While numerous studies have investigated these techniques in broader dental settings, a focused synthesis of their effectiveness in the specific context of tooth extraction remains limited as noted by Wong et al., [3] “non-pharmacological interventions appear to reduce fear and anxiety levels in patients undergoing third molar extraction under local anesthesia,” [3] yet the available evidence is scattered and inconsistent. Moreover, Hao et al. emphasize that “no direct comparative RCTs or network meta-analyses (NMAs) exist to contrast the therapeutic effects of these interventions and determine which one is most effective.” [4].

This gap in the literature highlights the need for a systematic review that consolidates current findings and evaluates the effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions aimed at reducing psychoemotional distress in adult dental extraction patients. Such evidence could help guide clinical decision-making and promote safer, more personalized anxiety management strategies in oral surgery.

The aim of this systematic review is to identify and assess non-pharmacological methods used to improve the psychoemotional status of adult patients undergoing dental extractions, with the goal of providing evidence-based guidance for clinical application and future research.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

The methods and inclusion and exclusion criteria were determined in advance and documented in the protocol. This review was registered in PROSPERO, an international prospective register of systematic reviews. The protocol ID CRD420251030729 and it can be accesed by https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251030729.

This systematic review adhered to the PRISMA statement (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) [5].

Focused question

The following research question was formulated using the PICO framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome):

What non-pharmacological interventions are effective in improving psychoemotional outcomes—such as anxiety, stress, or fear—measured using validated psychological or physiological tools, in adult patients undergoing dental extraction?

Types of studies

This review included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing dental extraction, in which non-pharmacological interventions were applied to reduce anxiety, fear, or psychological distress. Studies were required to report psychoemotional outcomes using validated psychological or physiological measurement tools, such as the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), Modified Dental Anxiety Scale (MDAS), or comparable instruments. Only full-text, peer-reviewed randomized controlled trials were included. Observational studies, case reports, qualitative studies, review articles, conference abstracts, and studies in which pharmacological or sedation-based methods were the primary intervention were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed (MEDLINE) on 18 September 2024. The publication date filter (“last 10 years”) was applied in the PubMed interface to limit the results to studies published between 18 September 2014 and 18 September 2024. No additional study design, age, or clinical filters were applied at the database level. Eligibility criteria were applied during the title/abstract and full-text screening stages.

The complete search strategy as entered in PubMed, including the applied filters, is provided in Supplementary file 1.

The following search string was used in PubMed:

((((((dental phobia) OR (dental anxiety)) OR (dental fear)) OR (teeth extraction fear)) OR (teeth extraction anxiety)) OR (teeth extraction phobia)) AND (((methods) OR (tools)) OR (strategies)).

In addition to PubMed (MEDLINE), a supplementary search was conducted in ScienceDirect on 18 September 2024 to reduce the risk of retrieval bias. The search strategy was adapted to the ScienceDirect interface using the query: (“dental anxiety” OR “dental fear” OR “dental phobia”) AND (“tooth extraction” OR “teeth extraction”) AND (methods OR tools OR strategies). The publication year filter (2014–2024) was applied. A total of 165 records were identified and screened by title and abstract. A detailed list of screened records and reasons for exclusion from the additional ScienceDirect search is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

To ensure completeness of the search, the reference lists of all included studies and relevant review articles were manually screened to identify additional eligible trials.

Selection of studies

All records were screened independently and in duplicate by two reviewers according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion; if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted.

The selection process consisted of three stages. First, titles were screened to exclude clearly irrelevant studies, reviews, conference abstracts, and animal research. Second, abstracts were assessed for relevance to the review question. Full-text articles were evaluated for eligibility based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers. In cases of disagreement, consensus was reached through discussion; if necessary, a third reviewer was consulted to make the final decision.

For each included study, the following information was extracted: authors and year of publication, type of non-pharmacological intervention (e.g., music therapy, virtual reality, aromatherapy, auriculotherapy), study design characteristics, sample size, anxiety or stress assessment tools (e.g., MDAS, STAI, VAS, heart rate, blood pressure, salivary cortisol), main outcomes in both control and intervention groups, and reported statistical significance (p-values, where available).

When a study evaluated more than one intervention, each intervention was extracted and reported separately. All extracted data were organized into structured summary tables to enable comparison across studies and facilitate thematic synthesis.

Assessment of methological quality

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool. This tool evaluates five domains: (D1) bias arising from the randomization process; (D2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions; (D3) bias due to missing outcome data; (D4) bias in measurement of the outcome; and (D5) bias in selection of the reported result.

Each domain was judged as “low risk of bias,” “some concerns,” or “high risk of bias,” and an overall risk-of-bias judgment was assigned to each study accordingly.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach. Because the review used a narrative synthesis and the included studies were heterogeneous in terms of intervention protocols, comparators, and outcome measures, certainty was evaluated by intervention category rather than by pooled effect estimate. Evidence from randomized controlled trials started as high certainty and was downgraded, where appropriate, for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Publication bias was considered qualitatively, as quantitative assessment was not appropriate in the absence of meta-analysis and because fewer than 10 studies were available in most intervention categories.

Synthesis of the results and statistical analysis

Data extracted from the included randomized controlled trials were summarized in tables according to intervention type, outcome measures, and principal findings.

Due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity among the included studies — including differences in intervention protocols, duration and frequency of application, anxiety assessment instruments (e.g., MDAS, STAI, VAS, physiological parameters), timing of outcome measurement, and reporting of effect sizes — a quantitative meta-analysis was not considered appropriate.

Therefore, a narrative synthesis approach was applied. Results were grouped thematically by intervention category (music therapy, virtual reality, aromatherapy, acupuncture/acupressure, guided relaxation techniques, hypnosis, and informational interventions). Findings were analyzed descriptively, with emphasis on statistical significance, direction of effect, magnitude of change (where reported), and consistency across studies.

Results

Study selection

The PubMed (MEDLINE) search with the publication date filter “last 10 years” identified 3692 records. After title and abstract screening, 32 reports were assessed in full text for eligibility. Of these, 7 reports were excluded after full-text evaluation (2 not available in English, 2 inaccessible due to paywall restrictions, and 3 not meeting the randomized controlled trial design criterion). Ultimately, 25 randomized controlled trials were included in the qualitative synthesis. The supplementary search conducted in ScienceDirect identified 165 records, but no additional randomized controlled trials met the inclusion criteria. The selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Exclusion of studies

The reasons for exclusion after full-text assessment were as follows: articles not available in English (n = 2), full-text articles inaccessible due to paywall restrictions (n = 2), and studies that did not meet the randomized controlled trial design criterion (n = 3).

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the 25 included randomized controlled trials was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool. This tool assesses five domains: (D1) bias arising from the randomization process; (D2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions; (D3) bias due to missing outcome data; (D4) bias in measurement of the outcome; and (D5) bias in selection of the reported result.

Each domain was rated as “low risk of bias,” “some concerns,” or “high risk of bias,” in accordance with RoB 2.0 guidance. An overall risk-of-bias judgment was assigned to each study based on domain-level assessments.

Overall, 21 studies were judged to have a low risk of bias across all assessed domains. Four studies were classified as having “some concerns,” primarily due to insufficient reporting of the randomization process or limited detail regarding allocation concealment and outcome measurement procedures. No study was assessed as having a high overall risk of bias.

In general, most trials demonstrated adequate randomization procedures, comparable baseline characteristics between groups, and consistent outcome reporting. Missing outcome data were minimal in the majority of studies, and outcome measures were typically based on validated psychological instruments (e.g., MDAS, STAI, VAS) or objective physiological parameters (e.g., heart rate, blood pressure, salivary cortisol).

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for the included interventions was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach. Because the review used a narrative synthesis and the included studies were heterogeneous in terms of intervention protocols, outcome measures, and statistical reporting, the certainty of evidence was evaluated by intervention category rather than by pooled effect estimates.

Overall, the certainty of evidence ranged from low to moderate across intervention categories. Music-based interventions showed moderate certainty of evidence, whereas virtual reality, informational interventions, aromatherapy, relaxation techniques, and acupuncture-based approaches were supported by low certainty evidence, mainly due to heterogeneity of protocols, variability in outcome measures, and relatively small sample sizes. Types of studies

Among the 25 studies (6–30) included in this review, all were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in which participants were assigned to intervention and control groups to evaluate the effectiveness of various non-pharmacological anxiety-reducing methods prior to tooth extraction. Some studies employed parallel-group designs, while others used crossover randomized designs, particularly in trials investigating music therapy and virtual reality interventions.

Measurement scales used

A variety of standardized instruments were used to assess dental anxiety and physiological stress across the included studies. The Modified Dental Anxiety Scale (MDAS), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), and State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) were among the most frequently applied psychological assessment tools. Some studies also employed additional validated instruments, such as the Corah Dental Anxiety Scale (CDAS), the Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety and Information Scale (APAIS), and the Dental Anxiety Questionnaire (DAQ).

In addition to subjective psychological assessments, several trials incorporated physiological parameters to provide a more objective evaluation of anxiety. These included salivary cortisol levels, heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP), heart rate variability (HRV), oxygen saturation (SpO₂), and, in selected cases, biochemical markers such as noradrenaline levels.

The majority of studies relied on a single primary anxiety assessment instrument, while a subset combined psychological questionnaires with physiological measurements to obtain a more comprehensive evaluation of perioperative anxiety responses.

Positive effect reached

Several non-pharmacological interventions demonstrated beneficial effects in reducing dental anxiety and stress among patients undergoing tooth extraction. The most consistently reported positive outcomes were observed in studies investigating music therapy, virtual reality interventions, aromatherapy, acupuncture or acupressure techniques, guided breathing and relaxation methods, hypnosis-based approaches, and structured psychoeducational strategies.

Music therapy was frequently associated with significant reductions in anxiety scores measured using validated instruments such as MDAS, STAI, and VAS, as well as decreases in physiological stress markers including heart rate and salivary cortisol. Virtual reality interventions were reported to improve patient comfort and reduce intraoperative anxiety levels.

Aromatherapy, particularly the use of essential oils such as lavender, demonstrated calming effects in several trials. Acupuncture and related techniques were associated with improvements in both psychological and physiological parameters. Guided relaxation and breathing techniques showed reductions in subjective anxiety indicators, while psychoeducational and cognitive-behavioral strategies contributed to improved coping and decreased perioperative stress.

In general, improvements were observed in both psychological (MDAS, STAI, VAS, DAQ, APAIS) and physiological (heart rate, blood pressure, salivary cortisol, HRV, SpO₂) outcomes, depending on the intervention type and study design.

Negative effect reached

Although most non-pharmacological interventions demonstrated beneficial effects, some approaches were associated with increased or unchanged anxiety levels. In particular, interventions involving detailed surgical video demonstrations or extensive preoperative informational materials were reported to increase anticipatory anxiety immediately after exposure. These findings suggest that excessive or highly detailed procedural information may heighten stress in certain individuals, especially those with higher baseline anxiety levels.

The timing, format, and personalization of informational interventions appear to be critical factors influencing patient responses.

No effect reached

Some interventions did not demonstrate statistically significant reductions in anxiety when compared with control groups. In several trials, music-based or aromatherapy approaches showed reductions in anxiety scores; however, these changes were not consistently statistically significant across all outcome measures. Differences in intervention duration, delivery method, frequency exposure, and patient characteristics may explain these inconsistencies.

Additionally, certain interventions produced improvements in physiological markers without corresponding statistically significant changes in subjective anxiety scales, highlighting the complexity of measuring psychoemotional outcomes in surgical settings.

Discussion

This systematic review evaluated the effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions in reducing dental anxiety and stress among adult patients undergoing tooth extraction. Overall, the majority of the 25 included randomized controlled trials reported beneficial effects of various interventions on psychoemotional outcomes, supporting the clinical relevance of non-pharmacological anxiety management strategies in dental practice.

Music therapy was among the most frequently investigated interventions and demonstrated consistent reductions in subjective anxiety scores and, in several cases, physiological stress markers such as heart rate and salivary cortisol. However, the magnitude of effect varied across studies, potentially due to differences in music genre, delivery method (headphones vs. ambient sound), duration of exposure, and patient preference. These findings suggest that personalization of music-based interventions may enhance their effectiveness.

Virtual reality interventions were also associated with reductions in intraoperative anxiety and improved patient comfort. By providing immersive audiovisual distraction, virtual reality appears to reduce patients’ focus on surgical stimuli. Nevertheless, variations in hardware type, content design, and timing of application may influence outcomes. Importantly, the format and amount of preoperative information delivered through audiovisual media require careful consideration, as highly detailed procedural demonstrations may increase anticipatory anxiety in some individuals.

Aromatherapy, most commonly involving lavender essential oil, showed calming effects in several trials, although results were not uniformly statistically significant across all studies. Differences in concentration, delivery mode, and patient sensitivity may account for inconsistent findings. Similarly, acupuncture and acupressure techniques demonstrated measurable physiological and psychological benefits in some trials, though effect sizes varied.

Guided breathing and structured relaxation techniques provided low-cost and easily implementable approaches that reduced anxiety indicators in several studies. Cognitive-behavioral strategies and psychoeducational interventions were particularly beneficial for patients with elevated baseline anxiety, highlighting the importance of psychological preparation and coping enhancement.

Despite the generally positive findings, not all interventions produced statistically significant results. Variability in study design, sample size, intervention protocols, and outcome measurement tools likely contributed to heterogeneity across trials. Some studies reported descriptive reductions in anxiety without statistically significant differences between groups, emphasizing the need for adequately powered trials and standardized outcome reporting.

This review also highlights the importance of individualized care. Patient-specific factors—including baseline anxiety level, prior dental experience, personality traits, and preferred coping style—may influence responsiveness to different non-pharmacological strategies. A multimodal and patient-centered approach may therefore provide the most comprehensive benefit.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, only one electronic database (PubMed) was searched, which may limit comprehensiveness. Second, heterogeneity in intervention protocols and outcome measures precluded quantitative meta-analysis. Third, although most studies demonstrated low risk of bias, variations in reporting quality and methodological rigor may influence interpretation of results.

Future research should focus on well-designed, adequately powered randomized controlled trials using standardized anxiety assessment tools and transparent reporting of effect sizes. Comparative studies evaluating combinations of interventions may further clarify optimal strategies for specific patient subgroups.

Overall, the evidence suggests that non-pharmacological interventions can serve as valuable adjuncts in the management of dental anxiety during tooth extraction procedures, particularly when tailored to individual patient characteristics and implemented within structured clinical protocols.

Limitations

Several limitations of this review should be acknowledged. First, the search strategy was limited to a single electronic database (PubMed), which may have reduced the comprehensiveness of the evidence base. Second, substantial heterogeneity in intervention protocols, duration and timing of application, anxiety assessment tools, and outcome reporting limited direct comparison across studies and precluded quantitative meta-analysis.

Many included trials involved relatively small sample sizes, potentially limiting statistical power. Blinding was frequently challenging due to the nature of non-pharmacological interventions, which may introduce performance or detection bias. In addition, long-term follow-up data evaluating the durability of anxiety reduction were rarely reported.

Although most studies were assessed as having low overall risk of bias, variability in reporting transparency and methodological rigor may influence interpretation of the findings. Future well-designed randomized controlled trials using standardized intervention protocols, clearly defined outcome measures, and adequate sample sizes are necessary to strengthen the evidence base.

Although an additional database search (ScienceDirect) was performed to reduce retrieval bias, the primary search was conducted in PubMed, which may still limit the completeness of the identified literature.

Clinical implications

The findings of this review suggest that non-pharmacological interventions can serve as effective and low-risk adjuncts to conventional dental care for managing anxiety during tooth extraction procedures. Approaches such as music therapy, virtual reality–based distraction, aromatherapy, guided relaxation techniques, and structured psychoeducational strategies may enhance patient comfort and improve overall treatment experience.

Importantly, intervention selection should be individualized according to patient characteristics, baseline anxiety levels, prior dental experiences, and personal preferences. While informational and audiovisual tools can be beneficial, excessive or overly detailed procedural information may increase anticipatory anxiety in certain individuals.

Integrating patient-centered, multimodal strategies into routine clinical practice may improve emotional outcomes without relying solely on pharmacological sedation. However, further research is required to determine optimal intervention protocols, identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit, and evaluate long-term clinical effectiveness.

However, the effectiveness of these methods appears to depend on individual patient characteristics, baseline anxiety levels, and the manner in which interventions are implemented.

Certain informational approaches, particularly highly detailed surgical video presentations or excessively technical preoperative explanations, may increase anticipatory anxiety in some patients. Therefore, the amount and format of information delivered should be tailored to the patient’s preferences and coping style.

In clinical practice, a patient-centered and stepwise approach may be appropriate. Simpler interventions such as background music or guided breathing exercises may be suitable for many patients, while more immersive or structured strategies (e.g., virtual reality or cognitive-behavioral techniques) may be considered for individuals with higher anxiety levels.

These methods are generally low-risk and relatively easy to implement in routine dental settings. Nevertheless, clinicians should consider individual variability and avoid assuming universal effectiveness.

Further research is needed to clarify optimal intervention combinations, identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit, and establish standardized implementation protocols.
