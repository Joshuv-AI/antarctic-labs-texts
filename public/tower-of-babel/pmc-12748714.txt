Impact of a single swimming session on inflammatory response and iron homeostasis in adolescent female athletes during the luteal phase
Authors: Marta Mydłowska, Anna Kasperska, Justyna Cichoń-Woźniak, Hanna Dziewiecka, Joanna Ostapiuk-Karolczuk, Anna Skarpańska-Stejnborn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe intensity and duration of physical activity significantly influence hepcidin levels, a vital hormone that manages iron balance. Its increase is associated with exercise-induced inflammation, particularly involving interleukin-6. This study explored the effect of a single swimming session on specific markers of inflammation and iron metabolism in young female athletes during the luteal phase of their menstrual cycle. Eighteen competitive swimmers (13.5 ± 1.2 years old) completed three distances (800 m, 200 m, and 50 m) in the shortest possible time with active recovery periods between segments. Venous blood samples were taken before exercise, immediately afterward, and after 3 h of recovery. Swimming has caused a significant increase in lactate and hypoxanthine, indicating a high level of exercise intensity. While no significant changes in hepcidin or IL-6 were observed at 3 h post-exercise, later time points were not assessed. Immediately following exercise, transferrin levels rose, while total iron-binding capacity declined. Despite the absence of an inflammatory response, mild adaptive changes were observed in selected indicators of iron metabolism. Alterations in concentrations of iron, erythroferrone, ferritin, transferrin, and red blood cell parameters suggest that responses within the iron regulatory system may occur independently of hepcidin activity, with notable contributions from ferritin, transferrin, and mechanisms related to erythropoietic activity.Keywords: Physical effort, Female swimmers, Inflammation, Iron metabolism, Three-stage menstrual cycle controlSubject terms: Biochemistry, Physiology, Zoology

Introduction

Intense and/or prolonged physical exercise triggers a temporary inflammatory response, a physiological reaction of the body to tissue microdamage, which plays a crucial role in the regeneration process1. As explained by Chazaud2, this exercise-induced inflammatory response occurs in distinct stages: first, immune cells are recruited, and there is an increase in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, followed by the resolution of inflammation and the activation of tissue repair mechanisms aimed at restoring homeostasis.

Inflammation can occur even after a single session of intense or prolonged physical activity and is characterized by, among other features, increased macrophage infiltration and higher secretion of both pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8)) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin-10 (IL-10))3,4. Additionally, elevated levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), another important inflammatory marker, are commonly seen, usually peaking around 24 h after exercise4. The extent and nature of this response mainly depend on the type, intensity, and duration of the exercise, as well as the athlete’s training level.

Chronic activation of inflammatory processes, especially when recovery time is insufficient, can cause disruptions in the erythropoietic system, thereby promoting the development of functional iron (Fe) deficiency5,6. A key factor in this process is hepcidin, a hormone produced in the liver whose production increases in response to inflammation, particularly under the influence of IL-67,8. Hepcidin inhibits the activity of ferroportin, a transmembrane protein responsible for exporting Fe from enterocytes, macrophages, and hepatocytes, resulting in decreased Fe availability9 and reduced intestinal Fe absorption10. These alterations contribute to the development of iron deficiency, especially among endurance athletes11.

The onset of iron deficiency in athletes is driven by several mechanisms related to physical exercise. The most prominent include mechanically induced hemolysis, commonly seen in runners12; hemolysis caused by compression of blood vessels during intense muscle activity13,14; increased urinary iron loss (exercise-induced hematuria); and microdamage to the gastrointestinal mucosa leading to occult bleeding15.

All of these mechanisms may contribute to a reduced availability of Fe, which is necessary for proper erythropoiesis, resulting in unfavorable alterations in hematological parameters, including decreased hemoglobin concentration and red blood cell count15.

In the context of iron metabolism regulation, particular attention has been given to erythroferrone (ERFE) - a hormone produced by erythroblasts in response to increased systemic iron demand. ERFE plays a crucial role in mediating the crosstalk between erythropoiesis and iron metabolism by suppressing hepatic hepcidin expression, which, in turn, facilitates the mobilization of Fe from body stores and its availability to the bone marrow16. Although the role of ERFE in adaptation to physical exercise has not yet been fully elucidated, it may play a significant role in maintaining iron homeostasis during periods of training and recovery processes.

Chronic inflammation, elevated hepcidin levels, and reduced iron bioavailability may impair erythropoiesis, leading to decreased physical performance, increased muscle soreness, and heightened susceptibility to infections17–20. Endurance female athletes represent a particularly vulnerable group, as they often exhibit insufficient intake of heme Fe and experience additional Fe losses due to menstrual bleeding21. According to the literature, iron deficiency may affect 30–50% of endurance athletes and even more than half of young female athletes10,22.

Despite growing awareness of the importance of the menstrual cycle in studies involving women, female athletes remain underrepresented in sports science literature, accounting for only 35–39% of study participants23,24. One of the major challenges in conducting research with female subjects is the necessity to control for the phase of the menstrual cycle, which may influence iron metabolism and levels of inflammatory markers25–27. As emphasized by Janse De Jonge et al. (2019), accounting for the menstrual cycle phase is crucial for reliable interpretation of results and minimizing biological variability28.

A systematic review by Badenhorst et al.29 indicates that iron homeostasis in women is subject to complex, cyclical regulation influenced by physiological fluctuations in sex hormone levels. A significant role in this process may be played by hepcidin, whose concentration undergoes dynamic changes across the phases of the menstrual cycle. During menstruation, in response to blood loss, a decrease in hepcidin levels is observed, which physiologically enhances the body’s capacity to absorb dietary Fe. Subsequently, in the late follicular phase, rising estrogen levels may further facilitate hepcidin suppression, thereby optimizing Fe recovery. In contrast, during the luteal phase, the rise in progesterone levels is associated with increased hepcidin expression, contributing to more stable iron metabolism markers, e.g., ferritin (FERR), serum Fe, and transferrin saturation, as reported in numerous studies. Therefore, the luteal phase, characterized by the highest stability in iron-related parameters, represents an optimal time window for investigating post-exercise changes in iron metabolism while minimizing the influence of cyclical hormonal variability on the results.

The present study aimed to evaluate the effect of a single session of intense swimming exercise on selected inflammatory markers and iron metabolism parameters in young female athletes. The exercise test was conducted during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle to reduce biological variability and enhance comparability of the results.

It was hypothesized that intense physical exertion would induce an inflammatory response and transient disturbances in iron homeostasis, including increased hepcidin concentration and reduced Fe bioavailability.

Materials and methods

Participants

A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power software version 3.1.9.730 to estimate the required sample size. Assuming a statistical power of 0.80 and an effect size (ES) of 0.6, the minimum number of participants was calculated to be 11. The assumed effect size was based on previously obtained results from our research group31.

To account for potential exclusions, 18 young female athletes were enrolled in the study (age: 13.5 ± 1.2 years). All participants were swimmers representing the “Olimp” Student Sports Club in Gorzów Wielkopolski and attended the Sports Championship School in the same city. Inclusion criteria were valid medical clearance, absence of health contraindications for physical activity, a regular menstrual cycle, and informed consent obtained from the participant or their legal guardian. Exclusion criteria included: use of hormonal contraceptives, medications, or dietary supplements, current illness, injury, or other medical contraindications. Study design

This observational study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies32. The study protocol included a swimming performance test consisting of three consecutive distances, separated by active recovery periods.

Before the test, a three-stage verification of the menstrual cycle phase was performed, which included: (1) an interview combined with cycle tracking using a mobile application; (2) home ovulation tests; and (3) a single fasting blood draw to assess the hormonal profile. To evaluate the physiological response to exercise, venous blood samples were collected at three time points: before the exercise (B), immediately after its completion (Ex), and following a 3-hour recovery period (R-3 h). The study was conducted during the preparatory phase of the training season, when overall training volume is at its peak, which may have influenced the body’s response to the additional exercise stimulus. The study was conducted by the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Bioethical Committee at the Poznan University of Medical Science, Poland (Approval No. 538/22), and was retrospectively registered in the ClinicalTrials.gov database (Identifier: NCT06486623, date of registration: 2024-07-03). All procedures and potential risks were discussed with the participants before the study. All participants, or their legal guardians in the case of minors, provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Stage 1—Menstrual cycle monitoring.

At this stage, participants completed a preliminary questionnaire regarding their general health status, age at menarche, and the date of their most recent menstrual bleeding. The swimmers were then instructed to monitor their menstrual cycle daily using the free mobile application Period Tracker Period Calendar (Abishkking Limited, Hong Kong, China). The following data were recorded: number of bleeding days, predicted ovulation date, any adverse symptoms related to the cycle, illnesses, and occasional medication use. After five months of monitoring, the athletes completed a follow-up questionnaire and submitted their first app report to the researcher.

Stage 2—Home ovulation tests.

In the second stage, ultra-sensitive midstream ovulation tests (Diather, Gdańsk, Poland) were used. Each participant performed the test five times, starting four days before the estimated ovulation date and continuing through the expected day of ovulation. Testing continued until a positive result was obtained or until all five tests were completed with negative results. Based on the data from the mobile application, ovulation test results, and the individual competition calendar, the date of the exercise test was scheduled for each participant.

Stage 3—Confirmation of the luteal phase.

In the third stage, concentrations of selected sex hormones, progesterone, estradiol, luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), were determined from a fasting venous blood sample collected from the antecubital vein on the day of the exercise test. Hormonal analysis allowed for the confirmation or exclusion of the luteal phase in each athlete26.

The three-stage menstrual cycle control protocol was implemented to ensure the reliability of the study results28,33,34.

Swimming performance test

The exercise protocol used in this study consisted of three swimming segments of varying distances: 800 m, 200 m, and 50 m, as presented in Table 3 (total duration of the whole session: 60:38 ± 2:31 [min: s). The participants were instructed to complete each distance in the shortest time possible. A 15-min active recovery period was scheduled between each segment, during which the swimmers continued swimming at a moderate pace using a stroke of their choice. The selection of three distances with varying lengths and intensities enabled assessment of the physiological response to the whole mixed session, including endurance (800 m), mixed (200 m), and sprint (50 m) efforts. This test structure was chosen based on the observed increase in markers in male swimmers, which are connected with an inflammatory response35. This suggests that the applied protocol may have been sufficiently intense to induce an inflammatory and iron response in female swimmers.

Blood sampling and analysis

Venous blood samples were collected from participants at three time points: before exercise (B), immediately after the test (Ex), and after 3 h of recovery (R-3 h).

Venous blood was drawn from the antecubital vein into polyethylene tubes containing dipotassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTAK2) as an anticoagulant (Improve Medical, Guangzhou, China). Complete blood count was performed using the MYTHIC 18 analyzer (Orphee Medical, Geneva, Switzerland), including leukocyte parameters: White Blood Cells (WBC), Lymphocytes (LYM), Monocytes (MON), Granulocytes (GRA); and erythrocyte parameters: Hemoglobin (HGB), Red Blood Cells (RBC), and Hematocrit (HCT).

Additionally, blood was collected into clot activator tubes (BD Vacutainer, Becton Dickinson and Company, UK) for serum preparation. The samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, transferred into multiple Eppendorf tubes, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Capillary blood was also collected from the earlobe before and immediately after exercise to determine blood lactate concentration (La), using dedicated reagents and the Vario Photometer II portable biochemical analyzer (Diaglobal, Berlin, Germany).

Serum concentrations of biochemical markers, including progesterone, estradiol, LH, FSH, CRP, hepcidin, FERR, hypoxanthine (Hx), interleukins IL-6, IL-10, and interleukin-2 (IL-2), myoglobin (MYO), ERFE, and transferrin (Tf) were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Kits were obtained from DRG Instruments GmbH (Germany) for progesterone, estradiol, LH, FSH, CRP, hepcidin, and FERR; and from SunRed Biotechnology Company (China) for Hx, IL-6, IL-10, IL-2, MYO, ERFE, and Tf. Measurement ranges for each analyte were as follows: Progesterone: 2.9–27.1 ng/ml, Estradiol: 33.6–250.9 pg/ml, LH: ≤ 20.0 mIU/ml, FSH: 2.0–10.0 mIU/ml, CRP: upper limit of normal range 5–8 µg/ml, hepcidin: 0.153–81 ng/ml, FERR: 2.84–800 ng/ml, Hx: 0.3–70 ng/ml, IL-6: 3–600 ng/l, IL-10: 10–3000 pg/ml, IL-2: 0.8–200 ng/l, MYO: 1–300 ng/ml, ERFE: 0.156–10 ng/ml, Tf: 0.05–15 mg/ml. Serum Fe, unsaturated iron-binding capacity (UIBC), and total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) were measured using a colorimetric method (Pointe Scientific, Poland), with the following reference ranges: Fe: 60–150 µg/dl, TIBC: 250–400 µg/dl. TIBC was calculated according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the formula: TIBC (µg/dl) = Fe + UIBC.

All analyses were performed using a SPECTROstar Nano microplate reader with a cuvette port (BMG Labtech, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Boston, USA). Descriptive statistics, including mean and standard deviation, facilitated the visualization of trends and patterns across three time points. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data, while the Brown-Forsythe test evaluated variance equality. A one-way analysis of variance with repeated measures (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, was used to identify differences across the three time points (pre-exercise, post-exercise, and 3-hour recovery). For data that did not follow a normal distribution, the Friedman test was applied. For data with unequal variances, the Geisser-Greenhouse’s correction was applied. Cohen’s d was used to calculate the effect size, interpreted according to Cohen’s criteria as small (d = 0.2), moderate (d = 0.5), or large (d = 0.8). For correlation assessment, Pearson’s coefficient of linear correlation was calculated. A significance level of p ≤ 0.05 was maintained for all tests, and there were no missing data.

Swimming performance test

The swimming test consisted of completing three distances: 800 m, 200 m, and 50 m. The obtained results (Table 3) showed that the shortest distance (50 m) was associated with the lowest heart rate values compared to the other efforts. A statistically significant increase in blood lactate concentration (La) was observed after completion of the exercise protocol (p < 0.0001).

Markers of inflammation and iron homeostasis

The concentrations of Hx, IL-6, IL-2, CRP, MYO, and hepcidin are presented in Fig. 3. A statistically significant increase in Hx concentration was observed immediately post-exercise and after 3 h of recovery (p = 0.0007, Cohen’s d = 0.94, B vs. Ex; p = 0.0022, Cohen’s d = 0.83, B vs. R-3 h). No statistically significant changes in IL-6 levels were found at any time point. However, a moderate effect size of the exercise test on IL-6 levels was noted when comparing baseline to post-exercise and recovery values (Cohen’s d = 0.79, B vs. Ex; Cohen’s d = 0.5, B vs. R-3 h). Similarly, IL-10 levels did not change significantly in response to exercise. IL-2 levels did not differ post-exercise compared to baseline but increased substantially after three hours of recovery (p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 1.17, B vs. R-3 h). No significant differences in CRP levels were observed immediately after the exercise compared to baseline. However, a statistically significant decrease in CRP was found after 3 h of recovery compared to both pre-exercise and immediate post-exercise values (p = 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.24, B vs. R-3 h; p = 0.0019, Cohen’s d = 1.25, Ex vs. R-3 h). MYO levels showed no difference between pre- and post-exercise values. However, a statistically significant increase in MYO concentration was observed after 3 h of recovery (p = 0.0026, Cohen’s d = 0.63, Ex vs. R-3 h). Similarly, hepcidin levels did not show significant changes in response to exercise.

Discussion

This study investigated possible changes in selected iron metabolism markers in the context of the inflammatory response induced by a single bout of physical exercise. It is well established that serum iron availability in women may vary depending on the phase of the menstrual cycle. Therefore, to avoid misinterpretation of results and potential diagnostic errors, standardizing the timing of biological sample collection in relation to the menstrual cycle phase is recommended25,36. In the present study, menstrual cycle control was implemented to ensure that all participants performed the exercise test during the luteal phase, thereby increasing sample homogeneity34.

The results indicate that although the exercise was presumably not maximal, the applied protocol imposed sufficient metabolic stress, evidenced by a significant increase in blood lactate concentration immediately after the test (p < 0.0001). Surprisingly, the lactate threshold (4 mmol/L), considered a reliable indicator of exercise intensity in endurance sports37, suggests involvement of anaerobic energy pathways. What is also important is that the lactate increase at the end of the session could have resulted from the 50-m bout, even though the entire protocol was designed as a continuous effort. Additionally, the sustained elevation of Hx 3 h post-exercise indicates intensified purine degradation and activation of anaerobic activity, at least after a 50-m bout, which is consistent with previous reports38]– [39. As a byproduct of purine metabolism, Hx is considered a sensitive marker of exercise intensity and physiological adaptation, with increases reported following both submaximal efforts and those exceeding the anaerobic threshold38,40. The physiological load experienced by the athletes was further supported by their subjective ratings of perceived exertion using the Borg scale41 (Table 3).

Under such exercise conditions, a more pronounced activation of the classical inflammatory response, particularly involving pro-inflammatory cytokines, was expected. Both intense and prolonged exercise can trigger an immune response characterized by increased levels of cytokines (IL-6, IL-10, IL-2) and acute-phase proteins such as CRP6. According to Pedersen and Febbraio (2008), IL-6 is typically the first to increase in response to exercise9, followed by IL-10 and IL-26. It is therefore particularly noteworthy that, in the present study, no significant changes were observed in IL-6 or IL-10, while an isolated increase in IL-2 was recorded only after 3 h of recovery (Fig. 3). The concurrent decrease in CRP relative to baseline further suggests that the applied swimming protocol did not elicit a strong inflammatory response (Fig. 3).

It should be noted that Emami (2020), using the same test in a group of older swimmers (17 ± 1 years, males), observed an increase in IL-6, IL-10, and CRP immediately after exercise35. This suggests that the applied protocol may be sufficiently intense to induce an inflammatory response.

Sanderson et al. (2020) compared the inflammatory response to high-intensity interval training in water among elite swimmers from two different age groups42. Their findings showed that younger, trained swimmers aged 11–15 years exhibited a limited cytokine response (with an increase observed only in IL-10), whereas the adult athletes showed elevated levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). These results suggest that age may play a significant role in modulating the immune response, which may help explain our observations.

Available studies report increased lactate concentrations after swimming43–45, but data on post-exercise inflammatory responses remain limited. We can assume that the magnitude of this response likely depends on various individual factors. The inflammatory response can be influenced by age, sex, training status, the training period, and the phase of the menstrual cycle.

It is also worth noting that the applied exercise protocol (800 m + 200 m + 50 m), including two 15-minute active recovery periods at moderate intensity, lasted over 60 min in total and represented a continuous load. Gleeson19 emphasized that this type of prolonged exercise, performed at moderate to high intensity, is most associated with impairments in immune function. Similarly, studies by Morgado et al.46,47 suggest that prolonged, intense training may impair immune cell function and reduce their ability to produce cytokines in response to exercise stimuli.

Moreover, research by Aragón-Vela et al. (2021) demonstrated that resistance exercise induces a stronger IL-6 release in men compared to women48. Similarly, Stupka et al. (2000) reported in an animal model that, despite a comparable degree of muscle damage following eccentric exercise, females exhibited a weaker inflammatory response49. In contrast, Edwards et al. (2006) observed a greater post-exercise increase in IL-6 levels in women than in men after maximal exertion50. Nevertheless, as no studies involving swimming exercise or adolescent participants were included in the present analysis, the evidence supporting a sex-related effect on the immune response is limited. While differences in immune function between sexes may partly explain the observed outcomes35, this interpretation should be made with caution, and further research should include both male and female adolescent swimmers to substantiate this hypothesis.

As a result, the actual metabolic cost of the exercise may have been lower than anticipated, which could have contributed to a weaker activation of pro-inflammatory pathways, including limited stimulation of cytokine release such as IL-6 and TNF-α. The absence of a pronounced inflammatory response may thus be attributed not only to physiological adaptation to regular training, but also to an insufficient intensity of the exercise stimulus relative to the athletes’ current fitness level. It is also important to emphasize that all athletes in the present study were in the luteal phase of their menstrual cycle, which, as noted by Rosińska-Lewandoska et al. (2025), may support fatigue resistance, but is not optimal for performing high-intensity efforts51.

Notably, despite a statistically significant increase in WBC count following exercise, the values remained within the reference range, suggesting the absence of an acute inflammatory response in the participants52 (Table 5). The observed increase in IL-2 concentration after three hours of recovery may indicate mild T-cell activation (Fig. 3). At the same time, a decrease in lymphocyte and monocyte counts was observed, which likely reflects their migration from the bloodstream to peripheral tissues (e.g., muscle fibers) (Table 5). These changes are consistent with the findings of Pedersen et al. (2000), who demonstrated that physical exercise mobilizes all lymphocyte subpopulations, leading to a transient reduction in their blood concentrations and temporary suppression of T and NK cell activity53. A post-exercise lymphocyte decrease below baseline values typically occurs within 30 min after exercise, with recovery to baseline observed within 4–6 h53.

Notably, markers indicative of muscle fiber damage do not always respond immediately after exercise. Moreover, as reported in the literature, such damage is not always accompanied by parallel changes in inflammatory cytokine levels, which are typically modulated in response to high-intensity physical activity1. Leite et al. (2023) emphasize that MYO levels are most commonly measured either immediately post-exercise or after 24 h of recovery. Its elevation may occur either directly after exercise or later during the restitution period, depending on the degree of muscle damage54. In the present study, MYO concentrations were significantly elevated at 3 h recovery compared with the immediate post-exercise time point (Fig. 3), consistent with leakage of this muscle-specific protein into the circulation. Concurrently, leukocyte subsets (LYM, MON, GRA) increased vs. baseline (Table 5). This leukocytosis is interpreted not as a direct marker of muscle injury but as evidence of an acute inflammatory response, with immune cell trafficking toward the exercised tissue. Together, the rise in MYO and the exercise-induced leukocyte mobilization reflect the presence of at least mild muscle damage and its associated inflammatory response to the applied protocol1.

Iron homeostasis in women is strongly influenced by both the phase of the menstrual cycle36 and the presence of systemic inflammation55. In the present study, all athletes performed the exercise test during the luteal phase (Table 4), which minimized the confounding effect of hormonal fluctuations—particularly those of estrogen and progesterone - on iron-related parameters.

No statistically significant increase in hepcidin concentration was observed in response to the swimming exercise (Fig. 3). This finding is consistent with the absence of changes in IL-6 levels, one of the key pro-inflammatory cytokines responsible for inducing hepcidin expression7. Hepcidin, recognized as a central regulator of iron metabolism, limits intestinal Fe absorption and its release from body stores, primarily from the liver and macrophages56,57.

In the current study, ERFE levels remained unchanged immediately after exercise but significantly decreased after 3 h of recovery (Fig. 4). This pattern aligns with findings by Knap et al.16, suggesting that ERFE responds more to chronic rather than acute stimuli and may reflect a mild, cumulative inflammatory state associated with training load. The post-recovery decline may indicate a reduced need for enhanced erythropoiesis or a response to low-grade, chronic inflammation. Assessing additional inflammatory markers (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, the soluble IL-6 receptor (sIL-6R)) would help clarify this mechanism. Moreover, available literature indicates that ERFE synthesis depends on erythropoietin activity, iron status, and erythropoietic intensity, acting as a hepcidin antagonist to increase iron availability16,55. The observed post-exercise rise in serum iron, together with the decline in ERFE, suggests activation of homeostatic mechanisms restoring iron balance rather than upregulating erythropoiesis. This adaptive response may serve to limit excess iron and its pro-oxidative effects during recovery.

Iron homeostasis is also regulated by proteins such as Tf, responsible for transporting Fe in plasma, and FERR, which serves a storage function. Under conditions of iron excess, FERR levels increase to allow for safe storage of the element, while during deficiency, FERR concentrations decrease to promote the mobilization of stored Fe58 (Fig. 4). It should also be noted that both Tf and FERR are acute-phase proteins involved in inflammatory processes. However, in the present study, a decrease in FERR levels and an increase in Tf concentration were observed, suggesting that these changes were not the result of an acute-phase reaction. This interpretation is further supported by the absence of significant increases in IL-6 and CRP levels (Fig. 3).

In a study by Govus et al. (2014), Tf levels also increased following exercise but decreased significantly after 3 h of recovery59. In contrast, the increase in Tf observed in the present study persisted after 3 h of restitution, which may indicate an elevated demand for Fe transport to tissues as well as the activation of adaptive mechanisms following physical exertion60.

Govus et al. (2014) observed an increase in FERR levels immediately after exercise, which, similar to present findings, declined after 3 h of recovery58. In contrast, Nikolaidis et al. (2003) reported no significant changes in FERR levels following prolonged moderate-intensity cycling on an ergometer61. Significant decrease in FERR observed in the current study, both after the 3-hour recovery period compared to baseline and relative to post-exercise values62 (Fig. 4) may be attributable either to a weak inflammatory response triggered by the swimming test or to intensified catabolic processes. It is also noteworthy that the baseline FERR levels in the investigated athletes were already exceptionally low, which, despite normal total Fe and Tf concentrations, may indicate Fe store depletion and an elevated risk of iron-deficiency anemia22.

Context for the present study is provided by previous observations by Sporadyk (2002) regarding iron metabolism in adolescent male swimmers. This group was found to be particularly susceptible to iron deficiency, attributed to two factors: high demand for this element during the growth spurt and the physiological strain on the body caused by intensive physical training63. In this study, in addition to rapid growth, the onset of menstruation is an additional risk factor, leading to systemic iron loss in girls64. Importantly, Sporadyk (2002) also showed that iron homeostasis varies significantly in different phases of the endurance training cycle63. Furthermore, our own previous research has shown that baseline ferritin concentration significantly impacts post-exercise iron metabolism in young, elite rowers31. Therefore, it must be considered that the observed changes in iron status parameters may be multifactorial. They appear to be dependent not only on the applied exercise stimulus but also on the broader training context, such as the specific training stage (e.g., the preparatory period). Crucially, these responses may also be influenced by participants’ overall baseline iron status, for which resting ferritin concentration serves as a key indicator.

The observed changes may reflect physiological responses related to the regulation of iron homeostasis after a single bout of swimming exercise. The absence of a strong inflammatory response resulted in only mild disturbances to iron regulation that did not necessitate activation of the IL-6- hepcidin axis. Similar conclusions were drawn in our previous study involving young female basketball players, in which no significant disturbances in iron metabolism or activation of hepcidin in response to exercise were noted31.

Following exercise and during recovery, a significant decline was observed in erythrocyte indices (HGB, RBC, HCT), which are key markers of iron metabolism and oxygen transport capacity (Table 5). These changes may result from several factors, including exercise intensity and type, training phase, sex, and baseline hematological status65,66. Both increases and decreases in hematological parameters have been observed in the literature. Increases in these parameters during exercise may result in temporary hemoconcentration and typically normalize after rehydration67. Conversely, among female endurance athletes, such as swimmers or rowers, subjected to continuous, high-intensity training, low erythrocyte parameters are frequently observed and may reflect so-called “sports anemia,” primarily due to plasma volume expansion68. In our study, the decrease in red blood cell parameters may indicate intravascular hemolysis69. In accordance with the existing literature, this phenomenon may result from exercise-induced oxidative stress, which leads to biochemical alterations in the erythrocyte membrane, thereby increasing its susceptibility to damage and enhancing intravascular hemolysis70. This mechanism is supported by the work of Sporady et al. (2002), who observed heightened intravascular hemolysis as a result of the swimming training applied63. Moreover, Paraiso et al.69 demonstrated that both acute and chronic training in competitive swimmers influences the osmotic stability of erythrocytes. In the present study, hemolysis could have been triggered not only by the single swimming test but also by the cumulative training load preceding the test. As a result, erythropoietic activity may have suppressed an adaptive response frequently observed in athletes exposed to chronic or intense physical effort. Furthermore, hemolysis may explain the observed increase in serum iron concentration post-exercise71. Therefore, considering that hematological shifts may also stem from red blood cell microtrauma and fluid redistribution, warranting careful interpretation, particularly in elite female athletes.

However, certain limitations of the study should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small number of participants limits the generalizability of the findings, typical in studies conducted on highly trained athletes. Moreover, the findings suggest that future studies should include a greater number of blood sampling points (6, 12, and 24 h after exercise) to allow for a more precise assessment of parameters related to iron metabolism and inflammatory status. Lastly, future studies could include a resting control group to isolate the effects of the exercise intervention.

Conclusions

A single bout of swimming exercise performed by the female athletes during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle did not trigger a significant inflammatory response or major disturbances in iron. These findings indicate moderate disturbances in iron homeostasis that were not accompanied by a pronounced inflammatory response. The observed changes may involve ferroportin-dependent pathways facilitating iron export from enterocytes, macrophages, and hepatocytes, as well as Tf-mediated Fe delivery to tissues. The absence of IL-6–hepcidin pathway activation suggests that the typical inflammatory mechanisms leading to Fe retention were not triggered. This response pattern may be consistent with adaptive processes observed in endurance-trained individuals, in whom Fe homeostasis is subjected to dynamic regulation in response to physical exertion. The maintained distribution of iron, without evidence of sequestration, could reflect an efficient regulatory response aimed at preserving iron availability for metabolic processes and erythropoiesis.
