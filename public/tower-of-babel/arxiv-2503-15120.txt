Abstract
. Communication access real-time translation (CART) is an essential accessibility service for d/Deaf and hard of hearing (DHH) individuals, but the cost and scarcity of trained personnel limit its availability. While Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) offers a cheap and scalable alternative, transcription errors can lead to serious accessibility issues. Real-time correction of ASR by non-professionals presents an under-explored CART workflow that addresses these limitations. We conducted a user study with 75 participants to evaluate the feasibility and efficiency of this workflow. Complementary, we held focus groups with 25 DHH individuals to identify acceptable accuracy levels and factors affecting the accessibility of real-time captioning. Results suggest that collaborative editing can improve transcription accuracy to the extent that DHH users rate it positively regarding understandability. Focus groups also showed that human effort to improve captioning is highly valued, supporting a semi-automated approach as an alternative to stand-alone ASR and traditional CART services.



1.
Introduction

Transcription plays a key role in providing
DHH
(
DHH
) individuals with access to spoken language. Real-time captioning presents significant challenges as text must be produced almost synchronously with speech. Professional services, known as
CART
(
CART
), typically rely on stenography or re-speaking (voice-writing). However, the scarcity and high cost of trained transcribers limit the availability of such services and, thus, the accessibility for many events. Advances in
ASR
(
ASR
) have greatly improved its accuracy, offering a scalable and cost-effective alternative
(
Chorowski et al. 2015
;
Chan et al. 2015
;
Gulati et al. 2020
;
Peng et al. 2022
;
Radford et al. 2023
)
. As a result, automated captioning is now integrated into widely used applications and is increasingly relied upon as an accessibility service
(
NDC 2020
)
.

At the same time,
DHH
individuals have consistently reported serious accessibility issues for automatically generated captions
(
Deshpande et al. 2014
;
Kawas et al. 2016
;
Butler 2019
;
Reid 2019
)
and have criticised the lack of a declarative ruling regarding the quality of
ASR

(
Reid 2019
;
Campbell et al. 2024
)
. There seems to be a disparity between measured
ASR
accuracy and
DHH
individuals’ experience. One reason for this discrepancy is that benchmark scores can be misleading
(
Geirhos et al. 2020
)
. The performance of an
ASR
model in practice heavily depends on how well its training data cover the actual conditions
(
Koenecke et al. 2020
)
. While high accuracy is achieved for General American English, noisy recording conditions
(
Kinoshita et al. 2020
)
, specialised vocabulary
(
Radford et al. 2023
)
, or a speaker’s accent
(
Tadimeti et al. 2022
)
can significantly decrease the performance, particularly in lower-resourced languages
(
Barrault et al. 2023
)
. Furthermore, commonly used evaluation metrics offer a limited representation of accessibility, leading to a mismatch between measured accuracy and the perceived transcription quality
(
Wang et al. 2003
;
Mishra et al. 2011
;
Favre et al. 2013
)
. Until
ASR
systems reliably produce accessible captions,
CART
services involving humans are essential, and alternatives that address their limitations must be explored.

One promising yet under-explored approach to improving transcription accuracy is the manual correction of
ASR
output
(
Wald 2006a
;
McDonnell et al. 2021
;
Seita et al. 2022
;
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
. As
ASR
becomes more robust to noise and speaker variability, re-speaking benefits may diminish, allowing users to focus on corrections. This approach reduces the cognitive load on transcribers and could be performed by less trained personnel. This paper uses a mixed methods approach to evaluate a
CART
workflow in which a group of non-professionals correct
ASR
output in real-time to increase transcription accessibility. We developed a prototype that combines a live video stream,
ASR
output, and collaborative text editing. A user study evaluates the feasibility and transcription accuracy of the approach. Complementary,
DHH
focus groups were conducted to correlate their accuracy ratings with the user study results and to align their preferences with the capabilities of the workflow in general. Our results show:

•

Corrections can improve
ASR
accuracy to the extent that
DHH
readers rate it positively regarding understandability.

•

Common evaluation metrics ignore quality aspects that focus groups consider equally important to word accuracy.

•

The editing task is demanding and limits knowledge transfer.

2.
Background

2.1.
Automatic Speech Recognition

ASR
accuracy has improved significantly in recent years, with
SOTA
(
SOTA
) models achieving high accuracy in many languages
(
Radford et al. 2023
;
Barrault et al. 2023
)
. This progress has largely been driven by adopting
E2E
(
E2E
) models, particularly transformer architectures
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
;
Chorowski et al. 2015
;
Chan et al. 2015
;
Gulati et al. 2020
;
Peng et al. 2022
)
, and machine learning techniques. Unsupervised
(
Yu et al. 2010
)
, semi-supervised
(
Rasmus et al. 2015
)
, and self-supervised
(
Schneider et al. 2019
)
learning approaches use web-scale training data to improve the robustness and overall quality of
ASR
systems. Regarding accuracy rates, it has been suggested that
ASR
has reached parity with manual transcription
(
Xiong et al. 2018
)
. However, these results can be misleading, as optimising for specific datasets comes with the risk of shortcut learning
(
Geirhos et al. 2020
)
. Despite improvements, the performance of
ASR
can be limited by poor audio quality
(
Kinoshita et al. 2020
)
, low-resource languages
(
Radford et al. 2023
;
Barrault et al. 2023
)
and biased training data
(
Koenecke et al. 2020
;
Tadimeti et al. 2022
)
.

Studies indicate that the accuracy of automated captioning is insufficient to provide
DHH
readers with full access to spoken content
(
Deshpande et al. 2014
;
Kawas et al. 2016
;
Butler 2019
)
. The
NAD
(
NAD
) has reported significant issues with captioning accuracy, timing, and completeness, and is seeking a declaratory ruling to address these concerns
(
Reid 2019
)
. Furthermore, the readability of ASR-generated captions can be challenging
(
Butler 2019
;
Liao et al. 2023
)
, especially when captions are not syntactically segmented
(
Gerber-Morón et al. 2018
;
Kushalnagar and Kushalnagar 2018
)
. Streaming ASR further complicates the experience by producing intermediate results that cause distracting text changes in live captions
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
.

2.2.
Manual Correction of ASR

Correcting
ASR
effectively reduces error rates
(
Che et al. 2017
)
and has also been suggested by
DHH
individuals
(
McDonnell et al. 2021
;
Seita et al. 2022
)
. This method is already used by re-speakers, who use
ASR
and manually correct the remaining errors. Correction is more efficient than creating a full manual transcription when the error rate is below 30%
(
Gaur et al. 2016
)
, a threshold that
ASR
models can reliably achieve
(
Addlesee et al. 2020
;
Radford et al. 2023
)
. While errors can be reduced by 30% to 50%
(
Munteanu et al. 2008
;
Che et al. 2017
;
Soe et al. 2021
)
, transcription quality can be higher as users tend to correct errors that have a greater impact on comprehension
(
Kushalnagar et al. 2012
;
Bhavya et al. 2022
)
.
DHH
readers also rated manually corrected captions highly accurate, sometimes preferring them over professional transcription services
(
Deshpande et al. 2014
)
.

Real-time transcription is particularly challenging, as speech is up to ten times faster than typing
(
Wald 2006b
)
, and corrections must be made quickly
(
Rander and Looms 2010
)
. When working with non-professionals to overcome the shortage of professional transcribers
(
Lasecki et al. 2012
)
, distributing the task among multiple individuals can reduce the demand and increase quality
(
Harrington and Vanderheiden 2013
)
. Studies indicate that at least three non-expert typists are required to correct
ASR
transcriptions
(
Takagi et al. 2015
)
, reducing errors by around 30%
(
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
. While real-time correction of
ASR
has been proposed to increase accessibility
(
Wald 2006a
;
McDonnell et al. 2021
;
Seita et al. 2022
)
, further research is needed to assess its feasibility and effectiveness
(
Kushalnagar et al. 2012
;
Deshpande et al. 2014
;
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
.

2.3.
Measuring Transcription Quality

The accuracy of
ASR
transcriptions is commonly measured by the
WER
(
WER
), which uses the Levenshtein distance on a word basis to determine the minimum edits between two texts
(
Levenshtein 1966
)
. The
WER
is sensitive to non-semantic differences, treating them equally to missing or incorrect words. To address this, texts are often normalised by removing punctuation and converting to lowercase. Further steps, like unifying abbreviations, contractions, and written numbers, can reduce non-semantic errors
(
Koenecke et al. 2020
;
Radford et al. 2023
)
. However, these modifications may remove aspects that affect comprehension, such as punctuation or semantic formatting.

The
WER
has been criticised as a measure of accessibility, as it does not necessarily reflect human perception of accuracy
(
Wang et al. 2003
;
Mishra et al. 2011
;
Favre et al. 2013
)
. Alternative metrics have been proposed but have not been widely adopted yet
(
Morris et al. 2004
;
Apone et al. 2010
;
Kafle and Huenerfauth 2019
;
Roux et al. 2022
)
. A key problem is that
WER
does not consider the contribution of individual words to the overall understandability of a text
(
Berke et al. 2018
)
.
ML
(
ML
)-based metrics address this issue by classifying the importance of words to determine the severity of errors
(
Kafle and Huenerfauth 2016
;
Amin et al. 2023
)
. While some studies report a higher correlation of such metrics with human perceptions of quality
(
Kafle and Huenerfauth 2017
;
Kafle and Huenerfauth 2019
)
, adoption to other domains may be limited
(
Wells et al. 2022
)
, and metrics may not necessarily perform better than the
WER

(
Arroyo Chavez et al. 2024
)
. Alternatives to automated metrics rely on manual classification of transcription errors
(
Apone et al. 2010
;
Romero-Fresco and Pérez 2015
)
. The usefulness of a transcript can also be assessed through subjective ratings, e.g. by ranking the understandability of a text. While qualitative measures are helpful for specific evaluations, they are difficult to obtain for large datasets and lack objectivity and replicability.

In this study, we use the
WER
as it is the standard metric in speech recognition research and to report accuracy with an objective measure. As it has received criticism as an accessibility metric, we additionally include accuracy ratings from a diverse group of
DHH
individuals.

3.
Methodology

We used a mixed methods approach to evaluate the proposed
CART
workflow: Firstly, we conducted a user study in which non-professionals corrected
ASR
-output in real-time. We measured the accuracy and tested different collaboration scenarios to gather information on efficacy, usability, and user experience. Secondly, we conducted focus groups with
DHH
individuals to align their accuracy ratings with the results of the user study and their captioning requirements with the capabilities of the
ASR
correction workflow.

3.1.
Recording

The recording is an animated video which presents a scientific topic entertainingly.

WPM

The speaker is not visible, and no lip reading is possible. The speech is grammatically correct, with clear pronunciation.

The video was transcribed with ten different
ASR
models (see Appendix
A
). To increase the robustness of the
WER
, we used a custom normaliser for German (see Appendix
B
), inspired by a related study
(
Radford et al. 2023
)
. To start from a reasonable level of accuracy for the editing task, we selected an average transcript with a
WER
of 9.3%. The transcript was automatically formatted (see Appendix
C
). The video was split into four evenly long parts for the
DHH
groups’ caption rating. The parts contained captions with a
WER
of 2%, 5%, 10%, and 15% (see Appendix

). The transcripts were then automatically formatted into captions (see Appendix
D
).

3.2.
User Study

3.2.1.
Participants

Overall, 75 people participated in the user study. Most were undergraduate computer science students, and the other participants were postgraduates and researchers at the same university. They gave informed consent to participate in the study and received a small thank-you gift. Participants were native German speakers and did not have a hearing disability. Participants worked in groups of three to edit the captions. 60 participants were randomly assigned to the different collaboration scenarios and editing roles by signing up for a specific time slot. An additional 15 participants were assigned to a control group.

3.2.2.
Collaboration Scenario

Based on the results of previous research
(
Piskorek et al. 2022
;
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
, we chose four collaboration scenarios to evaluate the impact of temporal and spatial task distribution on correction efficiency and editing conflicts. Temporal task distribution offsets each user’s position in the transcript by delaying audio playback. Although latency must be kept to a minimum in real-time transcription, we wanted to evaluate the extent to which delayed corrections could improve accuracy. In some streaming scenarios, for example where users don’t interact with the presenters, near-live may be tolerable
(
Gerber-Morón et al. 2018
)
. Spatial task distribution mainly reduces user conflicts by assigning them different text segments. We set a group size of three, as suggested by related research
(
Takagi et al. 2015
)
. For each scenario, we had five groups (i.e. 15 participants):

A

Parallel: No task distribution mechanism has been applied.

B

Delayed: Each user received the audio with a different delay (0, 10, 20 seconds).

C

Chunked: Users were assigned chunks of the transcript and took turns correcting them.

D

Mixed: Two users took turns correcting while the third user proofread with a 10-second audio delay.

The scenarios were designed to help users distribute the work, but they did not prevent users from breaking out of their intended text sections. Editors could still help to correct other users’ sections.

3.2.3.
Prototype

The interface has an interactive text document on the left and a video player on the right (see Fig.

(
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
. The design is functional and follows common caption editors and text editing solutions. An Etherpad is used to enable collaborative editing with real-time updates. The video player has only volume controls, and the server controls playback. The
ASR

3.2.4.
Procedure

Participants scheduled a time for the user study in a computer lab or remotely via video conference. They used provided hardware or their own devices. Participants were informed about the study’s goals, provided consent, and received a unique user ID for the editing software and questionnaire. All participants accessed the web application and completed a technical check to verify functionality. They were briefed on the collaboration scenario and instructed to focus on correcting word errors, ignoring capitalisation and punctuation. The video played at normal speed without interruption, and users edited the transcript without communicating with each other. After the video, participants completed a questionnaire including the
RTLX
(
RTLX
) for cognitive load
(
Hart 1986
;
Grier 2015
)
, questions on knowledge transfer from the video, and usability and user experience evaluations. They were then debriefed and given a small thank-you gift.

3.2.5.
Control Group

To classify editing users’ cognitive load and knowledge transfer, a control group of 15 hearing participants watched the video without captions. After watching the video (without the option to rewind it), they answered a questionnaire with the
RTLX
and the same questions regarding the videos’ content as the editing participants.

3.3.
DHH Focus Groups

25
DHH
individuals using assistive technologies like hearing aids or cochlear implants participated in three focus groups. Two groups were local
DHH
interest groups, one using spoken language and the other sign language, with diverse ages and occupations. The third group included university students from a
DHH
initiative. Participants provided informed consent and received financial compensation. Despite diverse impairments, participants could be divided into two groups: those who relied solely on captioning and those who used captioning in addition to hearing. Focus groups were held in the groups’ usual meeting places, with sign language interpreters assisting the sign language group.

Sessions began with a modified experience interview
(
Zeiner et al. 2018
)
, where participants shared positive live captioning experiences using a handout for guidance. These experiences led to group discussions, which were transcribed and analysed for recurring themes and experiences
(
Mayring 2019
)
. Participants watched the same video used for the editing’ study in four parts, each with captions at different accuracy levels. The video was shown on a projector with sound available in the room and via an induction loop. After each part, participants rated caption understandability on a seven-point Likert scale and gave written feedback. Final ratings and comments addressed accuracy, punctuation, formatting, latency, and verbatim transcription. Sessions concluded with debriefing.

4.2.
Editing Users’ Comments

Editing users’ comments on the questionnaire were categorised as positive or negative, and grouped into three topics: related to the general task, the usability of the prototype, and collaboration within the group. In total, 115 comments were negative, and 101 were positive. The number of comments grouped by topic was: task (negative = 56, positive = 28), usability (negative = 31, positive = 13), collaboration (negative = 28, positive = 60). 31 participants perceived the task as stressful or demanding, while 13 mentioned terms like “quite relaxing” or “rather easy”. 11 participants perceived correcting mistakes as rewarding. 37 participants commented positively on teamwork, 6 negatively, and 5 perceived teamwork as competitive. 16 participants mentioned editing conflicts with other team members, and 15 in following the continued video after correction.

5.
Discussion

We tested an alternative
CART
workflow in which non-professionals collaboratively correct
ASR
. We also conducted focus groups with
DHH
individuals to compare their preferences for live captioning with the semi-automated transcription process. We measured caption quality using the
WER
and with ratings from
DHH
caption readers, as there is currently no objective measure for the accessibility of captions.

All editing groups could reduce the
WER
of the
ASR
transcript. The initial
WER
was 9.3%, a level that reached medium acceptance by the
DHH
focus groups. The corrected transcripts achieved an average
WER
of 6.2%, similar to the accuracy range rated positively regarding understandability. The relative error reduction of 33% is consistent with findings from related studies
(
Munteanu et al. 2008
;
Che et al. 2017
;
Soe et al. 2021
;
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
. Although users were instructed to focus on word errors, punctuation and capitalisation errors also decreased, suggesting that people tend to correct more meaningful errors, which are not measured by
WER
but enhance comprehension (cf.
(
Kushalnagar et al. 2012
;
Bhavya et al. 2022
)
). The comprehensibility of the transcript may therefore be higher than the
WER
suggests.

The collaboration scenario had no significant effect on the resulting accuracy of the transcript or the perceived cognitive load of the users. While teamwork was perceived as stress-reducing, a mechanism to guide collaboration seems necessary, as most users within the parallel scenario reported editing conflicts. Editors perceived working in chunks as more positive, as it reduced such conflicts and allowed them more time to review their segment of the text. Since working with delays did not improve performance, and the
DHH
focus groups emphasised their preference for minimal latency (cf.
(
McDonnell et al. 2021
)
), temporal task distribution seems to be neither an effective nor a viable solution. Correcting mistakes was perceived as rewarding, but participants also mentioned a sense of competition. A revised interface could emphasise the task’s collective goal, for example, by displaying the total number of corrections made.

The editor’s average
RTLX
score was 44, which is not overly demanding but does require some effort
(
Grier 2015
)
. The score was significantly higher than that of the control group, who only watched the video. In the quiz following the video, editors gave significantly fewer correct answers than participants in the control group. Most editors reported that they could not or only partially follow the content, especially whilst or shortly after correcting errors. This suggests that following the content of the video and correcting errors in the script is too demanding to be done simultaneously
(
Kushalnagar et al. 2014
)
. Therefore, the task of editing should be performed by dedicated editors so as not to disadvantage other listeners
(
McDonnell et al. 2021
)
.

Reading error-free captions is already a demanding task because the speed of speech often exceeds that of reading. Any mistakes, such as word errors, missing punctuation, incorrect capitalisation, or poor formatting, disrupt the reading flow and require mental compensation that can only be managed to a limited extent
(
Nam et al. 2020
)
. Acceptance ratings among
DHH
users varied significantly depending on the
WER
; captions with 2-5%
WER
were rated significantly higher than those with 10-15%
WER
(cf.
(
Butler 2019
)
). Positive comments were more frequent for captions with fewer errors, while higher error rates led to more negative feedback. The impact of errors on reading speed is also reflected in the
DHH
participants’ negative comments on talking speed in video segments with higher
WER
, despite similar
WPM
rates across segments.

DHH
individuals rated accuracy, capitalisation, punctuation, and semantic formatting as equally important for understandability (cf.
(
Butler 2019
;
Kushalnagar and Kushalnagar 2018
)
), supporting the criticism of
WER
as a limited measure of accessibility
(
Wang et al. 2003
;
Mishra et al. 2011
;
Favre et al. 2013
;
Arroyo Chavez et al. 2024
)
. The importance of different quality aspects is strengthened by the fact that captions with lower
WER
also had fewer punctuation and capitalisation errors. Minimal latency was strongly preferred, as caption reading is often combined with lip-reading or hearing (cf.
(
McDonnell et al. 2021
)
). Focus groups also expressed a clear preference for verbatim transcription. While
ASR
systems can provide low-latency verbatim output, corrections remain essential to address errors that hinder comprehension. Combining the speed of
ASR
with human editing is a promising alternative to traditional
CART
workflows to improve accessibility until fully automated systems can reliably meet user needs.

Participants expressed predominantly negative experiences with
ASR
-generated captions and their preference for professional caption services:
"I can clearly see whether it is people who make an effort to write everything down or whether only ASR is used"
.
ASR
was seen as frustrating and unreliable, described as
"very irritating"
and producing "wondrous linguistic creations". In contrast, human involvement was highly valued. Participants stated,
"A human being should still stand between AI and us"
, and found their transcription errors more tolerable:
"Mistakes made by written interpreters are forgivable"
. The discussions showed that automated captioning lacks accessibility, while human effort is highly valued. An
ASR
correction workflow could bridge this gap and provide a more acceptable solution for
DHH
readers.

6.
Conclusion

ASR
offers a great opportunity to improve the accessibility of events through real-time captioning, but its lack of accuracy limits the participation of people who rely on it. Collaboratively correcting
ASR
could improve accessibility, especially when professional transcribers are unavailable. We evaluated this alternative
CART
workflow in a user study and conducted focus groups to align its capabilities with the preferences of
DHH
individuals.

The user study showed that the initial
WER
of
ASR
can be reduced to the range of high acceptability, even though corrections must be made in real-time. As humans tend to correct errors that influence the meaning of a text, the accessibility may be even higher than the
WER
indicates. As real-time correction of
ASR
is demanding, it requires dedicated personnel apart from the audience to ensure that knowledge transfer is not hindered. Correcting as a group can make the task more manageable, and spatial task distribution can reduce stress and editing conflicts. While relying solely on
ASR
may feel like a second-rate accommodation for
DHH
individuals, human efforts to improve their participation were highly valued, potentially increasing the acceptance of
ASR
in a semi-automated captioning workflow.

7.
Limitations & Future Work

Our findings are based on a single video presentation with a professional speaker, limiting generalisability to other contexts. Less favourable conditions impact
ASR
accuracy, complicate manual correction, and reduce intelligibility for caption readers.
DHH
participants did not rate the edited captions directly to avoid bias from individual editing groups. At this stage of the research, we believe that comparing the
WER
after editing with the
WER
ratings of
DHH
individuals is a more objective and transferable approach to other studies. Future research should evaluate the workflow end-to-end, with editors correcting streaming
ASR
and
DHH
users reading and rating the corrected transcript. It could also be explored how AI models can assist humans by providing autocompletions, suggestions or autonomous corrections.

Appendix B
Text Standardisation

The transcripts were automatically normalised before calculating the WER according to the following rules:

(1)

Replace all white space characters with a space and remove repeated spaces.

(2)

Replace German diacritics (e.g. ä -> ae, ß -> ss).

(3)

Convert currencies and symbols to their written forms (e.g. € -> Euro, % -> Prozent).

(4)

Replace common German abbreviations (e.g. z.B. -> zum Beispiel).

(5)

Convert common German contractions to its long form (e.g. gibt’s -> gibt es).

(6)

Convert all Arabic numbers to a numeric expression (e.g. 200 -> zweihundert).

(7)

Transform text to lowercase and remove all punctuation.

(8)

Remove repeated, leading, and trailing spaces.

Appendix C
Transcript Formatting

The transcript was automatically formatted according to the following rules:

•

Start a new paragraph after a silence of 5 seconds.

•

800 to 1000 characters per paragraph (100 to 200 for chunked scenarios).

•

End paragraph on sentence ending characters (.!?;).

Appendix D
Caption Formatting

The captions were automatically formatted according to the following rules:

•

Length of 60 to 80 characters.

•

End caption if a sentence ending character appears (.!?;).

•

Format the caption into two lines:

–

Break in between the character ranges of 30 to 60.

–

Try to break at a punctuation character (.,!?;:).

–

Otherwise, break at any non-word character (e.g. white space).
