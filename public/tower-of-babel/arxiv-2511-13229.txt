Laplace Learning on Wasserstein Space
Mary Chriselda Antony Oliver, Michael Roberts, Carola-Bibiane Schönlieb, Matthew Thorpe

Abstract
The manifold hypothesis posits that high-dimensional data typically resides on low-dimensional subspaces. In this paper, we assume manifold hypothesis to investigate graph-based semi-supervised learning methods. In particular, we examine Laplace Learning in the Wasserstein space, extending the classical notion of graph-based semi-supervised learning algorithms from finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces to an infinite-dimensional setting. To achieve this, we prove variational convergence of a discrete graph p p -Dirichlet energy to its continuum counterpart. In addition, we characterize the Laplace-Beltrami operator on a submanifold of the Wasserstein space. Finally, we validate the proposed theoretical framework through numerical experiments conducted on benchmark datasets, demonstrating the consistency of our classification performance in high-dimensional settings.

-Laplacian, semi-supervised learning, Wasserstein space, graph-based algorithms, asymptotic consistency, Gamma-convergence

Introduction

The curation of large-scale, fully annotated training datasets remains a major bottleneck due to the high cost and expertise required for manual labelling. For example, in biomedical imaging applications such as flow cytometry
[
67
,
12
]
, gene expression microarrays
[
23
,
24
]
, and proteomic assays
[
18
]
, modern technologies generate high-dimensional data far faster than it can be annotated. As a result, only a small fraction of samples receive reliable labels, despite their routine use in classification tasks. This motivates graph-based semi-supervised methods, which exploits the geometric structure of the data to improve predictions with limited supervision. In this paper, we focus on a special class of graph-based semi-supervised methods, namely Laplace Learning
[
68
]
, to study classification in high-dimensional settings. This method exploits the geometric structure inherent in large quantities of unlabelled data to improve label predictions. However, leveraging the underlying geometry in high-dimensional datasets presents substantial challenges, including the well-known curse of dimensionality
[
44
,
22
]
and poor generalization capacity
[
18
]
.

In theory, a well-established trend in statistics suggests that high-dimensional data often possess an intrinsic low-dimensional structure, a concept formalized by the
manifold hypothesis

[
25
]
. This hypothesis asserts that data are supported (or nearly supported) on a low-dimensional manifold with a small intrinsic dimension. Formally, the general setup of this hypothesis motivates us to study Laplace Learning in more complex spaces, such as the Wasserstein space which we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{a.c.}}{(\Omega)}
for
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Here,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{a.c.}}{(\Omega)}
denotes the set of probability measures on
\Omega
that are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Its infinite-dimensional Riemannian geometry was introduced in
[
50
]
. In particular, we work ‘close’ to a submanifold
\Lambda\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{a.c.}}{(\Omega)}
embedded in the Wasserstein space with a finite-dimensional parameterisation, and denote samples by
\mu_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
. For our specific setting, we are interested in the finite approximation,
\Lambda_{n}=\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, of the submanifold
\Lambda\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{a.c.}}{(\Omega)}
. In practice, we have access to
m
data points for each of the
n
probability measures
\mu_{i}
, and we denote the feature vectors by
\Lambda^{(m)}_{n}=\{\mu^{(m)}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with

\mu^{(m)}_{i}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{x^{(i)}_{j}}
where
\Omega\ni x^{(i)}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mu_{i}
for all
j=1,2,\dots,m
and
i=1,2,\dots,n
. Note that
\Lambda_{n}^{(m)}\not\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{a.c.}}(\Omega)
; it does not lie in
\Lambda
, but serves as an empirical (perturbed) approximation of the submanifold.

Our submanifold assumption implies that we consider a subset
\Lambda\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{a.c.}}{(\Omega)}
of the Wasserstein space which is parameterised by a bi-Lipschitz embedding
E:\mathcal{S}\to\Lambda
where
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a smooth, compact and finite-dimensional manifold. We consider probability measures
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
and
\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
that are related by
\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda}=E_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
. This allows us to assume
\mathcal{S}\ni\theta_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
and define
\mu_{i}=E(\theta_{i})
which implies
\mu_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda}
. We let
\mathcal{S}_{n}=\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be the finite sample approximation of the parameter space
\mathcal{S}
. The empirical distributions using
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and
\{\mu_{i}^{(m)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\theta_{i}}
,
\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda_{n}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\mu_{i}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda_{n}^{(m)}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\mu_{i}^{(m)}}
, respectively.

In the semi-supervised learning setting, we are given the feature vectors
\Lambda_{n}^{(m)}=\{\mu^{(m)}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{P}{(\Omega)}
and
N<n
labels
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with the objective of finding missing labels for the unlabeled feature vectors
\{\mu_{i}^{(m)}\}_{i=N+1}^{n}
.
Formally, we seek a function
f_{n}:\Lambda^{(m)}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies
f_{n}(\mu^{(m)}_{i})=y_{i}
for all
i\leq N
and is smooth with respect to
the geometry induced by the graph

(\Lambda^{(m)}_{n},\mathbf{W}^{(m)}_{n})
, whose nodes are
\Lambda^{(m)}_{n}=\{\mu^{(m)}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and edge weights are
\mathbf{W}^{(m)}_{n}=({W}^{(m)}_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}
.
The edge weights are defined using a decreasing function
\eta:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
(satisfying Assumptions

⁢
E
.1

-

⁢
E
.4

). For fixed scale
\varepsilon>0
we set the weights to be,

\displaystyle W^{(m)}_{ij}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\eta\bigg(\frac{d_{\mathrm{W}^{2}}(\mu^{(m)}_{i},\mu^{(m)}_{j})}{\varepsilon}\bigg)

(1)

where
d_{\mathrm{W}^{2}}(\mu^{(m)}_{i},\mu^{(m)}_{j})
is the
2
-Wasserstein metric between the empirical probability measures
\mu^{(m)}_{i},\mu^{(m)}_{j}\in\Lambda^{(m)}_{n}
. We only retain those edges with weights
W^{(m)}_{ij}>0
. This is known as the random geometric graph model
[
51
]
.
Approximately, the parameter
\varepsilon
determines the range within which two nodes are connected. Here, the nodes represent empirical probability measures. It is chosen to satisfy a lower bound for connectivity.
We will take
\varepsilon\to 0
as
n\to\infty
motivated by: (a) the graph’s geometry becomes increasingly localised making it easier to detect non-linear class boundaries, and
(b) the sparsity of the weight matrix
W^{(m)}_{ij}
affects the algorithm’s scalability, with typically smaller computation time required for fewer edges (equivalently, sparser
\mathbf{W}^{(m)}_{n}
).

In this context, we consider Laplace Learning
[
68
]
, a variational machine learning method, defined by minimising the discrete energy
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon,m,n}:\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda_{n}^{(m)}})\to[0,\infty)
with label constraints where,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon,m,n}(f_{n})=\frac{1}{n^{2}\varepsilon^{p}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}{W}^{(m)}_{ij}\left|{f}_{n}(\mu^{(m)}_{i})-{f}_{n}(\mu^{(m)}_{j})\right|^{p}.

(2)

This energy functional depends on the parameter (connectivity radius)
\varepsilon
, the number of data points per sample
m
, and the number of samples
n
. The minimization is over
f_{n}:\Lambda_{n}^{(m)}\to\mathbb{R}
and subject to the constraints
f_{n}(\mu_{i}^{(m)})=y_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,N
(agreement with the known labels). That is, our motivation is the variational problem,

\mbox{minimise }\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon,m,n}(f_{n})\mbox{ over }f_{n}:\Lambda^{(m)}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}\mbox{ subject to $f_{n}(\mu^{(m)}_{i})=y_{i}$ for all $i\leq N$}.

The limiting problem is described by a continuum functional
which acts on real valued functions:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{E}_{\infty}(f)&:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}|\nabla(f\circ E)(\theta)\cdot h|^{p}\eta(\|B_{\theta}(h)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(E(\theta))})\rho^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}h&\quad\text{if }f\in\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(\Lambda,d_{\mathrm{W}^{2}},\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda})\\
+\infty&\quad\text{else.}\end{array}\right.\\
\end{aligned}

(3)

Here,
B_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathrm{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{k};\mathbb{R}^{k})
is a linear map such that in a formal sense it maps tangent vectors on
\mathcal{S}
to tangent vectors in the Wasserstein space (more precisely, see Assumption

⁢
B
.2

), and

\rho_{\mathcal{S}}
is the density of the probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
on
\mathcal{S}
. The space
\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(\Lambda,d_{\mathrm{W}^{2}},\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda})
, is the Sobolev space defined over
\Lambda
which is equivalent to
\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(\mathcal{S})
, the Sobolev space defined over
\mathcal{S}
(see Subsection
3.3
for the definition and Proposition
3.8
for a proof that the two Sobolev spaces are equivalent). The continuum functional can equivalently be written as

\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(f)=\frac{1}{p}\langle f,\Delta_{\Lambda}f\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda})}
, where
\Delta_{\Lambda}
denotes the Laplace-Beltrami operator, with the explicit formulation provided in Subsection
4.3
.

Our objective is to investigate the asymptotic consistency of Laplace Learning (in the variational sense) from discrete to continuum settings as the limits
n\to\infty
,
m\to\infty
, and
\varepsilon\to 0
, thereby extending the existing methodology from Euclidean spaces to infinite-dimensional settings (see Figure
1
). The strategy of the proof of our main result, variational convergence between
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon,m,n}
defined in (
2
) to
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
defined in (
3
), requires two intermediary graph
p
-Dirichlet energies. The first is defined using the ‘exact’ feature vectors
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\in\Lambda_{n}\subset\Lambda
. In particular,

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon,n}:\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda_{n}})\to[0,\infty)
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon,n}(f_{n})=\frac{1}{n^{2}\varepsilon^{p}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}W_{ij}\left|f_{n}(\mu_{i})-f_{n}(\mu_{j})\right|^{p}

(4)

where
W_{ij}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\eta\bigg(\frac{d_{\mathrm{W}^{2}}(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})}{\varepsilon}\bigg)
in the same spirit as (
1
).
And the second is defined on the parameter space using
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\subset\mathcal{S}
. In particular,
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon_{,}n}:\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}})\to[0,\infty)
is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon,n}(\hat{f}_{n})=\frac{1}{n^{2}\varepsilon^{p}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\hat{W}_{ij}\left|\hat{f}_{n}(\theta_{i})-\hat{f}_{n}(\theta_{j})\right|^{p}

(5)

where
\hat{W}_{ij}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\hat{\eta}_{\theta_{i}}\bigg(\frac{\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)
, for a particular choice of kernel
\hat{\eta}_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
for
\theta\in\mathcal{S}
.

The latter graph
p
-Dirichlet energy has a continuum limit
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\infty}:\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}})\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\infty}(\hat{f})&:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}|\nabla(\hat{f})(\theta)\cdot h|^{p}\eta(\|B_{\theta}(h)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(E(\theta))})\rho^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}h&\quad\text{if }\hat{f}\in\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(\mathcal{S})\\
+\infty&\quad\text{else.}\end{array}\right.\end{aligned}

(6)

We observe that
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(f)=\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\infty}(f\circ E)
.

1.1
Contributions

To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first to present an analysis of Laplace Learning on the Wasserstein submanifold; the results are discussed in greater detail in Section
2
. The key contributions are as follows:

1.

Variational convergence from discrete to continuum.
We rigorously prove the asymptotic consistency of the graph
p
-Dirichlet energy for Laplace Learning, showing convergence from (
2
) to (
3
) in Theorem
4.7
, and establish compactness results in Theorem
4.4
. Together, these results provide a theoretical guarantee (via
\Gamma
-convergence) of the existence of a well-defined continuum limit of Laplace Learning in the finite dimensional Wasserstein submanifold.

2.

Characterization of the Laplace–Beltrami operator.
In Proposition
4.10
, we present an explicit form of the Laplace–Beltrami operator on Wasserstein submanifolds. This establishes a precise correspondence between discrete graph operators and continuum differential operators, enabling their characterisation in finite dimensional Wasserstein submanifold.

3.

Numerical validation.
In Section
5
, we provide numerical experiments demonstrating the robust performance of Laplace Learning in high-dimensional settings on benchmark datasets. These experiments illustrate the practical relevance and effectiveness of our theoretical results.

Related Works

Consistency Results:

A key step in establishing asymptotic consistency is to characterize the suitable notion of convergence from (
2
) to (
3
) in this setting.
One relevant framework is that of pointwise consistency of energy functionals (see
[
7
,
19
,
34
,
42
,
41
,
56
,
59
]
for more details), where a fixed (smooth) function
f:\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}
is considered, and the comparison is made between
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon,n}(f\lfloor_{\Lambda_{n}})
and
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(f)
. We note that in our setting it is unclear how to define
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon,m,n}(f\lfloor_{\Lambda_{n}^{(m)}})
since
f
is not defined at
\mu_{i}^{(m)}
(as
\Lambda_{n}^{(m)}\not\subseteq\Lambda
). Spectral convergence of the graph-Laplacian is sufficient (with a compactness result) to imply the convergence of minimisers when
p=2
, but this does not generalise to
p\neq 2

[
7
,
31
,
15
]
. Bayesian consistency results such as in
[
60
]
suggest that if the graph parameters are suitably scaled the graph-posteriors converge to a continuum limit as the size of the unlabeled data set increases. Variational consistency, implying the convergence of minimisers, has been established in the finite dimensional setting
[
32
]
, for discrete-to-continuum convergence of Laplace Learning in Euclidean spaces
[
57
]
. Furthermore, using PDE-based techniques, the authors in
[
26
]
explored large data limits for two related problems: the first being Lipschitz learning
[
45
]
, corresponding to the case
p=\infty
, and the second being the game-theoretic
p
-Dirichlet energy
[
16
]
.

Since
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon,m,n}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
do not have a common domain, we adapt the
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
topology, introduced in
[
32
]
from the Euclidean settings to general metric spaces. The underlying intuition is to use an optimal transport map
T_{n}:\Lambda\to\Lambda_{n}^{(m)}
such that
{T_{n}}_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda}=\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda^{(m)}_{n}}
, maps regions of
\Lambda
to points in
\Lambda_{n}^{(m)}
(effectively creating a partition of
\Lambda
). For a discrete function
f_{n}
on
\Lambda_{n}^{(m)}
we can define an extended function
\tilde{f}_{n}=f_{n}\circ T_{n}
on
\Lambda
.
To assess the difference between
\tilde{f}_{n}
and
f
, we can apply a
\mathrm{L}^{p}
norm to
\tilde{f}_{n}-f
, leading to the
\mathrm{TL}^{p}_{d_{\mathrm{W}^{2}}}
distance. This adapted metric and its topology are reviewed in detail in Section
3.2
.

Manifold Learning Results:

Many advanced manifold learning techniques leverage graphs constructed from data to facilitate low-dimensional embeddings. For instance, Isomap
[
58
]
uses shortest paths on graphs to approximate geodesics on the manifold, while Diffusion Maps
[
19
]
employ the graph Laplacian and diffusion coordinates for embedding. As demonstrated in
[
8
]
, increasing the sampling density in Isomap causes the graph shortest paths in an
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood graph to converge to the manifold geodesics, thereby approximating the ideal embedding. Coifman and Lafon
[
19
]
showed the convergence of the graph Laplacian to the Laplace–Beltrami operator with increased sampling. The Wasserstein space analogue, Wassmap, was explored by
[
38
]
, though consistency results akin to those for Isomap or Diffusion Maps were not previously established. It is beyond the scope of the paper to prove convergence of the Laplace-Beltrami operator corresponding to (
3
), but given we have established convergence at the level of the energy, it is reasonable to conjecture (with a possibly more restrictive lower bound on
\varepsilon
) convergence of the graph Laplacian to a continuum Laplace-Beltrami operator in this setting.

The structure of the paper is outlined as follows.
In Section
2
, we state the relevant assumptions required and list the main results. In Section
3
, we provide a brief overview of the related concepts relevant for our analysis. In Section
4
we present the proofs in two steps: first establishing compactness, and then proving the
\Gamma
-convergence of the discrete-to-continuum graph
p
-Dirichlet functional of Laplace Learning. We also provide the derivation with the explicit form of the graph Laplacian in the discrete setting and the Laplace-Beltrami operator in the continuum setting. Finally, in Section
5
we provide numerical experiments to illustrate the performance of classification using the Laplace Learning algorithm for different datasets.

3
Background

In this section, we review the preliminary concepts used in the main results. We first present optimal transport theory, a framework for efficiently transporting mass between distributions, and standard results on convergence rates for empirical measures in the
p
-Wasserstein distance. Next, we introduce the
\mathrm{TL}^{p}_{d}
space, which defines the convergence of functions from discrete to continuum spaces in our setting, followed by a characterization of Wasserstein submanifolds in Sobolev spaces. Finally, we briefly review

3.1
Optimal Transport

In this section, we recall the notion of optimal transportation between measures and its associated metric. For general background on this topic, see
[
63
,
62
,
53
,
33
,
54
]
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
be an open, connected and bounded domain with smooth boundary. We denote the space of probability measures on
\Omega
with bounded
p^{\textrm{{th}}}
moment, as follows

\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega):=\Bigl\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}{(\Omega)}:\int_{\Omega}\lvert{x}\lvert^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu({x})<+\infty\Bigl\}.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
and
T:\Omega\to\Omega
be a measurable map, the pushforward of
\mu
by
T
, denoted as
T_{\#}\mu
is the measure

T_{\#}\mu(A)=\mu(T^{-1}(A)).

for all measurable set
A\subseteq\Omega
.

We briefly introduce some special cases of the optimal transport formulations where the cost function
c:\Omega\times\Omega\to(0,\infty]
is the
p
-th power of the Euclidean distance i.e.,
c(x,y)=|x-y|^{p}
.

Given the probability measures
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
the
Monge formulation
of optimal transport is the variational problem

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{T:T_{\#}\mu=\nu}{\int_{\Omega}|x-T(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu({x})}.

(8)

A map
T
satisfying
T_{\#}\mu=\nu
is called a transport map, and the minimizer of the optimization problem in (
8
) is referred to as the optimal transport map. The problem in (
8
) is often difficult to solve due to its non-convexity and the nonlinearity in
T
.

We define the set
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
of couplings between measures
\mu
and
\nu
to be the set of probability measures on the product space
\mathcal{P}(\Omega\times\Omega)
whose first marginal is
\mu
and the second marginal is
\nu
. We call
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
a transport plan.
For any transport map
T
:
\Omega

\rightarrow

\Omega
with
T_{\#}\mu=\nu
the probability measure,

\pi=(\mathrm{Id}\times T)_{\#}\mu,

where
\mathrm{Id}
denotes the identity map satisfies
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
.

The
Kantorovich formulation
of optimal transport is the variational problem

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}{\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}|x-y|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\pi({x},{y})}.

(9)

The minimiser of (
9
) is the optimal transport plan. When the source measure
\mu
is absolutely continuous, the
Kantorovich
and
Monge
formulations are equivalent, as the optimal plan is concentrated on the graph of a measurable transport map. In contrast, for discrete measures, mass splitting may occur, so the Monge problem may fail to admit a solution, whereas the Kantorovich problem remains well-posed.

When
\Omega
is bounded, we have
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega)=\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
. This allows us to define the
p
-Wasserstein distance
[
63
]

p^{\textrm{th}}
power of the Euclidean distance) between
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
for any

. It is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \quad d_{\mathrm{W}^{p}}(\mu,\nu)&=\begin{cases}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\bigg(\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\lvert{x}-{y}\lvert^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\pi({x},{y})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}&\text{for $1\leq p<\infty$,}\\
\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\pi$-$\esssup_{(x,y)}|x-y|&\text{for $p=\infty$.}\end{cases}\\
\end{aligned}

(10)

Recall that
\mu^{(m)}
and
\nu^{(m)}
are the empirical distributions supported on
m
discrete points, i.e.,
\mu^{(m)}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
\nu^{(m)}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{y_{j}}
where
x_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mu
and
y_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\nu
. In the sequel we let

C,c>0
denotes a constant (large, small) that can arbitrarily vary from line to line.

Space (

We introduce the notion of

spaces, which have been adapted from
[

to our context, as a metric to define the convergence from the discrete to the continuum space for point clouds. Recent work motivated by the
\mathrm{TL}^{p}(A)
space, includes
[

]

]
which introduced the space of equivalence classes

to define the space of piecewise smooth segmentations from the Ambrosio-Tortorelli approximation of the Mumford-Shah segmentation model and introduce a new family of metrics for comparing generic signals, benefiting from the robustness of partial transport distances and sliced-partial transport distances
[
5
]
.

In this subsection, we consider an arbitrary metric space
(A,d)
in order to define the
\mathrm{TL}^{p}_{d}(A)
distance. Let
A
be a non-empty set with
p\geq
1, we use
\mathcal{P}(A)
to denote the set of Borel probability measures defined on
(A,d)
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
(A,d)
be a metric space.
We define
\mathrm{TL}^{p}(A)
to be the set of functions
f
and probability measures
\mathbb{P}
on
A
such that
f
is
\mathbb{P}
measurable, i.e.,

\mathrm{TL}^{p}(A):=\big\{(f,\mathbb{P}):f:A\to\mathbb{R}\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{P})\mbox{ where }\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}(A)\big\}.

We define the transportation distance
d_{\mathrm{TL}^{p}_{d}(A)}:\mathrm{TL}^{p}\times\mathrm{TL}^{p}\to[0,\infty)
between the pairs
(f,\mathbb{P})\in\mathrm{TL}^{p}(A)
and
(g,\mathbb{Q})\in\mathrm{TL}^{p}(A)
as follows,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TL}^{p}_{d}}((f,\mathbb{P}),(g,\mathbb{Q}))=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}\bigg(\int_{A\times A}d(x,y)^{p}+|f(x)-g(y)|^{p}\,\textnormal{d}\pi(x,y)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

(16)

where
\pi\in\Pi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})
is the set of transport plans between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
.

In short, the
\mathrm{TL}^{p}_{d}
optimal transport plans, which minimise the variational problem defined in (
16
), strike a middle ground between two objectives: aligning spatial locations closely, as indicated by minimising the distance, i.e., mimizing
\int_{A\times A}d(x,y)^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)
and matching signal features, as represented by minimising
\int_{A\times A}|f(x)-g(y)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)
.

The original
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
distance proposed in
[
32
]

is a special case of the above definition when
A
is a Euclidean space and
d(x,y):=|x-y|
. We deduce the following proposition below which will help us later in understanding the limiting behaviour of Laplace Learning. With an abuse of notation, we denote
\|f(x)-g(y)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\pi(x,y))}=\bigg(\int_{A\times A}|f(x)-g(y)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}
for ease of convenience.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
p\geq 1
and
(A,d)
be a metric space. The following statements hold.

1.

d_{\mathrm{TL}^{p}_{d}(A)}
is a metric.

2.

3.4
\Gamma
- Convergence

The notion of
\Gamma
-convergence is particularly suited for handling sequences of minimization problems, where it can be interpreted as the ‘limiting lower semi-continuous envelope’. This concept is especially valuable when dealing with functionals that exhibit significant oscillations. In such cases, there may not be a strong limit, and any weak limit that does exist may not capture convergence of minima/minimisers.
For a comprehensive introduction, see
[
20
,
11
]
.

Definition 3.10

(
\Gamma
-Convergence)
.

Let
(A,d)
be a metric space,
\mathrm{L}^{0}(A;\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\})
be the set of measurable functions from
A
to
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
, and
(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. The function
\mathcal{X}\ni\omega\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{(\omega)}_{n}\in\mathrm{L}^{0}(A;\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\})
is a random variable. We say that
\mathcal{E}^{(\omega)}_{n}:A\to[0,\infty]

\Gamma
-converges almost surely on
A
to
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}:A\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
with respect to the metric
d
, and write
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}=\Glim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}^{(\omega)}_{n}
, if there exists a set
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{X}
with
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})=1
, such that for all
\omega\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
and all
f\in A
:

1.

(
lim inf-inequality
) for every sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
A
converging to
f

\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(f)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}^{(\omega)}_{n}(f_{n}),\quad\mbox{ and}

2.

(
lim sup-inequality
) there exists a sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
A
converging to
f
such that

\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(f)\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}^{(\omega)}_{n}(f_{n}).

For simplicity we suppress the dependence of
\omega

\{\mu_{i}^{(m_{n})}\}_{i=1}^{n}=\Lambda_{n}^{(m_{n})}

Remark 4.11
.

Both the graph Laplacian
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon_{n},m_{n},n}
and the Laplace–Beltrami operator
\Delta_{\Lambda}
can be characterized as the first variations of their respective Dirichlet energies.
In particular, Proposition
4.10
shows that the directional derivative of
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
at
f
in the direction

satisfies

\partial\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(f;g)=\langle\Delta_{\Lambda}f,g\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda})}.

Consequently, by setting

, we obtain the following identity (see also (
52
)),

\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(f)=\frac{1}{p}\,\langle\Delta_{\Lambda}f,f\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda})}.

An analogous relation holds in the discrete setting,

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},m_{n},n}(f_{n})=\frac{1}{p}\,\langle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon_{n},m_{n},n}f_{n},f_{n}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\Lambda^{(m)}_{n}})}.

This provides a variational interpretation of the discrete operator. It also suggests that, formally,

is the continuum limit of

Numerical Experiments

In this section, we evaluate the performance of Laplace Learning based on the fraction of training labels. These numerical experiments were conducted on two datasets: (a) a 2D dataset of synthetic Gaussian samples (See Figure
2
) and (b) a 3D dataset (images of different furnitures) from ModelNet10
[
66
]
(See Figure

).

1.

Data Representation:

Each sample in the dataset is represented as an empirical probability measure. Specifically, for a fixed number of points
m
, the
i
-th sample is represented as,

\mu_{i}^{(m)}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{x_{j}^{(i)}}.

2.

Construction of the Weight Matrix:

We define the weights based on the distance between the nodes
\{\mu_{i}^{(m)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
For computationally efficiency we use the linear Wasserstein distance (see
[
64
]
,
[
3
, Section 3.4]
for more details) rather than the 2-Wasserstein distance. This makes the following approximation

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{W}^{2}}(\mu_{i}^{(m)},\mu^{(m)}_{j})\approx d_{\mathrm{LW}^{2},\mu_{1}^{(m)}}(\mu_{i}^{(m)},\mu_{j}^{(m)})=\|T_{i}^{(m)}-T_{j}^{(m)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu_{1}^{(m)})}

(53)

where
T_{i}^{(m)}
is the optimal transport map from
\mu_{1}^{(m)}
to
\mu_{i}^{(m)}
.

In the first experiment, we employ an
\varepsilon
-connected graph, while in the second experiment, we make use of a
k
-nearest neighbor (kNN) graph. Further details are provided in the corresponding sections.

3.

Solve the Variational Problem:
For a classification problem with
c

y_{i}

is represented as a one-hot vector in
\mathbb{R}^{c}
, where the
l
-th element is
1
if the samples belong to the class
l
, and the other elements are all zeros.
Given the weights
(W_{ij}^{(m)})_{i,j=1}^{n}
we minimise the discrete graph
p
-Dirichlet energy problem, that is we find the minimiser
f_{n}
subject to the known labels
by solving:

\displaystyle\argmin_{f_{n}:\Lambda^{(m)}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{c}}\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},m,n}(f_{n})\mbox{ subject to $f_{n}(\mu^{(m)}_{i})=y_{i}$ for $i\leq N$.}

(54)

Here, we denote

to represent each component of

associated with class

.

4.

Estimate the Predicted Labels:

We threshold the solution
f_{n}:\Lambda^{(m)}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{c}
in (

) to give a predicted class.
More precisely, we predict the missing labels by

\hat{y}_{i}=\argmax_{l\in{1,2,\dots,c}}\{f_{n,l}(\mu^{(m)}_{i})\}\mbox{ for all $i=\{N+1,\cdots,n\}$.}

Based on the predicted missing labels on the unlabelled vertices, we find the accuracy for different fractions of training labels.

For two classes, Steps 3 and 4 simplify by treating

and therefore

By uniformly sampling points from the surface of each mesh, the images are converted into point clouds, where each point corresponds to a location on the object’s surface. A fixed number of points,
m=2048
, are available for each point cloud, so that

\mu_{i}^{(m)}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{x_{j}^{(i)}}\quad\text{for each }i=1,\dots,n,

up to relabelling of
x_{j}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Again, for computational efficiency we use the linear Wasserstein distance to compute the edge weights as in (
53
). Here, we choose the reference
\mu^{(m)}_{1}
as the first point cloud, belonging to the class ‘table’. We also use a second approximation to reduce the number of edges by using a
k
nearest neighbour (
k
-NN) graph.
We define the weights
by

W^{(m)}_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\mu_{i}^{(m)}\text{ is a $k$NN of }\mu_{j}^{(m)}\text{ or vice versa}\text{ based on }d_{\mathrm{LW}^{2},\mu_{1}^{(m)}}(\mu_{i}^{(m)},\mu_{j}^{(m)})\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We choose
k=15,20,25
in our experiment.

Figure
5
reports the mean classification accuracy over 100 iterations for varying proportions of labelled data (20% to 80%). As expected, increasing the fraction of labelled samples improves the model’s ability to capture the underlying data structure and propagate labels to unlabelled nodes. Smaller
k
values were also associated with higher accuracy. For reference, we evaluated the fully supervised PointNet model
[
52
]
under the same training ratios, observing a comparable trend, with accuracy increasing from 85% (20:80 train:test ratio) to 89% (80:20 train:test ratio). Notably, PointNet is a fully supervised method, whereas Laplace Learning operates in a semi-supervised setting, exploiting both labelled and unlabelled samples via the geometric structure of the data. Although direct quantitative comparisons are not strictly equivalent due to these differing paradigms, Laplace Learning achieves better performance while requiring fewer labelled instances, demonstrating its effectiveness in leveraging geometric relationships for label propagation under limited supervision.

A limitation of our framework is its restriction to discrete probability measures with equal total mass, each supported on the same number of points. While this simplifies the methodology, it limits the generality of the results and precludes application to broader scenarios. Extending the framework to unbalanced variants — such as the Hellinger–Kantorovich (Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao) distance
[
46
,
13
]
to measure spaces, the Unbalanced Gromov–Wasserstein distance
[
55
]
to measure spaces, or the Conic Gromov–Wasserstein and Conic Co-Optimal Transport distances
[
4
]
to measure networks and hyper-networks offers greater flexibility and constitutes a natural direction for future research.
