Work-related stress and consumption of psychoactive substances and medications among early childhood professionals (TraPsyCOL): study protocol for a cross-sectional study
Authors: Walaa Khazaal, Stéphane Onnée, Roomila Naeck, Séverine Morisset-Lopez, Patrick Baril, Olivier Vernay, Raphaël Serreau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWork-related stress is a major public health concern affecting workers across various sectors. It may be associated with increased psychoactive substance use, primarily alcohol, tobacco and cannabis as well as psychoactive medications, which may represent coping mechanisms in response to occupational stress. Work-related stress is also associated with adverse outcomes such as burnout, depression, anxiety and sleep-related problems (excessive daytime sleepiness).This study focuses on three groups of early childhood professionals in France: ‘ATSEMs’ (preschool classroom assistants who support teachers, help maintain the classroom environment and work exclusively in nursery schools), ‘animateurs’ (staff supervising educational and extracurricular activities outside school hours) and ‘agents d’entretien’ (cleaning and maintenance staff) working in nursery and elementary schools, the latter two work in both nursery and elementary schools. These professionals play a crucial role in the education, care and well-being of children but are exposed to high levels of occupational stress due to the emotionally demanding nature of their work, which may increase the risk of substance use, burnout, depression, anxiety and excessive daytime sleepiness. We hypothesise that professionals exposed to job strain (stressed work) will have higher odds of psychoactive substance and medication use, higher levels of substance-related risk or dependence and higher odds of burnout, symptoms of anxiety and depression and sleep-related problems compared with those not exposed to job strain.Methods and analysisThis cross-sectional epidemiological study is being conducted among early childhood professionals working in nursery and elementary schools in participating territories across the Axe ligérien in France. The study aims to investigate the association between work-related stress and the use of psychoactive substances and medications and to evaluate the associated levels of substance-related risk or dependence among ‘ATSEMs’, ‘animateurs’ and ‘agents d’entretien’, as well as the association between work-related stress and burnout, depression, anxiety and excessive daytime sleepiness. Data are being collected anonymously using a self-administered online questionnaire, accessible via a QR code displayed on study posters placed in staff rooms and other shared areas in participating schools as well as on flyers distributed among staff containing the same study information and QR code. The QR code also provides access to an information sheet explaining that the study complies with ethical guidelines and that proceeding to the questionnaire indicates non-objection to participation. A sample size of 234 participants was estimated using BiostaTGV. Descriptive analyses will be conducted. Associations between work-related stress, operationalised as job strain and the study outcomes will be examined using Pearson’s χ2chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, followed by multivariable analyses according to the nature of the outcome variable. Results will be reported using appropriate measures of association with 95% CIs. Studying these associations is essential for informing the development of targeted interventions and preventive strategies.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Centre Hospitalier Universitaire d’Orléans (assigned reference number: CERO 2511-02). Participation is voluntary and anonymous. The findings of the study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed, open-access publications and scientific conferences.

Introduction

Work-related stress, also known as occupational stress or job stress, is a major public health concern that has been increasing steadily over the past few decades among workers in all sectors. It is defined as ‘a pattern of reactions that occurs when workers are presented with demands or pressures (stressors) that are not matched to their knowledge, abilities and skills and which challenge their ability to cope’.1 An estimated 19%–30% of employees among the general working population are affected by workplace stress and burnout.2 3 Work-related stress contributes to 60% of workplace accidents, 40% of turnover and 19% of absenteeism. Moreover, 10%–13% of suicide deaths are due to working conditions. When job demands exceed a person’s resources, needs or abilities, work stress occurs, resulting in negative psychological and cognitive effects. The most important components of these demands are the workload, physical work environment, difficulties balancing work and life, and emotional strain. The scientific community is giving more attention to job stress because it has an impact on job performance, physical and mental health.4–7

In our era, a common problem that is experienced by workers in all professions is psychological distress at work, which results from several adverse health conditions related to workers’ health, safety and well-being. Higher levels of psychological distress have been observed among employees in human service occupations like education, social work and healthcare.2 According to 2012 research, psychological distress was experienced by 1.4% of men and 3.1% of women in France.8 A study showed that among a large French company, the prevalence of psychological distress among employees was 32.8% (95% CI 31.8% to 33.9%), higher among women (48.9%, 95 % CI 46.5% to 51.7%) than men (30.1%, 95 % CI 29.0% to 31.2%).9 Job strain, as defined by Karasek,10 is the combination of high psychological demands, including task quantity, complexity and time pressure, and low decision latitude, including both decision authority and skill discretion. It also includes a dimension of social support at work.11 This encompasses both instrumental and socioeconomic support from interactions with managers and colleagues. Insufficient social support in the workplace is considered a potential risk factor for health. In France, a recent study conducted between November 2023 and January 2024 across 104 assisted reproductive technology (ART) centres, reported that among 464 participants including biologists (n=124), gynaecologists (n=129), technicians (n=107), secretaries (n=51), midwives (n=31) and other professions (n=22), 35.7% of professionals in French ART centres experienced job strain.12 Another French study conducted in 2016 found that 23% of employees were exposed to job strain.13 Moreover, data from the SUMER study that was conducted in France revealed that women (28.2%) were more frequently exposed to job strain, based on the Karasek model, than men (19.6%).11 14

Work-related stress is associated with multiple adverse health consequences and comorbidities, including mental disorders, depression, anxiety,15 sleep disorders6 16 and burnout.2 Burnout is ‘a syndrome conceptualized as resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed’ and characterised by emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation and reduced personal accomplishment in work. The literature has widely documented the association between job stress and negative mental health outcomes. Workers are more likely to experience mental health problems such as depression and anxiety due to stressful work environments. Based on a study done in 2007, people with high psychological demands at work, including both heavy workloads and intense time pressure, were twice as likely to suffer from depression or generalised anxiety disorder as people who had lower job demands. Both women (1.90, 95% CI 1.22 to 2.98) and men (2.00, 95% CI 1.13 to 3.56) showed this association.17 Another study showed that high job strain was associated with 3.8 (95% CI 2.8 to 5.1) times higher odds of depressive symptoms, 1.7 (95% CI 1.1 to 2.6) times higher odds of depressive disorders and 7.4 (95% CI 5.6 to 9.7) times higher odds of burnout than low job strain among employees.18

A study conducted among 1265 French pharmacists revealed that work-related stress was significantly associated with anxiety (29.16±22.93 vs 52.56±21.55 vs 71.39±16.0, normal vs suggestive vs indicative scores of anxiety, respectively; p<0.001) and depression (42.77±26.01 vs 68.56±17.82 vs 76.44±14.68, normal vs suggestive vs indicative scores of depression, respectively; p<0.001). The percentage of anxiety or depression indicative scores was higher for participants with a high level of work-related stress. Moreover, this study also revealed that the proportion of sleep disorders was significantly higher among participants suffering from high levels of work-related stress compared with those with low levels of work-related stress (37.6% vs 12.3%, p<0.001).19 A study done in America showed that the nature, quantity, mental demand, working hours and associated stress of work played a fundamental role in insomnia, where approximately 60% of participants identified that either the development or prolongation of their insomnia was primarily caused by work.20 Furthermore, a Chinese study done among 2116 oilfield workers showed that their sleep quality was poor, and as occupational stress increased, the prevalence of sleep disturbances increased as well.21

Similarly, the issue of stress at work can be associated with the consumption of psychoactive substances and medications. Psychosocial job strain is associated with a higher risk of substance use disorders, mainly alcohol, tobacco and cannabis.22 Several studies have shown that job stress may be linked to increased psychoactive substance use as a coping mechanism to deal with work pressure and difficulties and to minimise the adverse effects of different sources of suffering, and this helps individuals to relax from job stress.7 23 For example, a study conducted using data from the French CONSTANCES cohort shows that men experiencing work-related stress were more likely to consume tobacco (OR=1.17, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.29, p=0.002 for people who previously smoked), use cannabis (OR=1.17, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.29, p=0.001 for use more than a year ago) and have episodes of excessive alcohol consumption (OR=1.29, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.51, p=0.002) compared with those who were not stressed. Exposure to work-related stress was associated with a higher level of tobacco use (OR=1.75, 95% CI 1.34 to 2.30, p<0.001), alcohol consumption (chronic use: OR=1.59, 95% CI 1.12 to 2.25, p=0.009; dependence: OR=2.30, 95% CI 1.54 to 3.44, p<0.001) and cannabis use (OR=1.38, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.75, p=0.006 for use more than once a month) compared with those not exposed among women.22

A recent Brazilian study conducted among university professors revealed a significant association between occupational stress and consumption of psychoactive substances, where among professors experiencing stress at work, 85.7%, 28.6% and 20% consume alcohol, tobacco and cannabis, respectively.24 According to a meta-analysis of European studies, people who smoke are more likely than people who do not smoke to report work-related stress (OR=1.11, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.18), and among people who smoke, those who reported work-related stress smoked on average more cigarettes per week than non-stressed smokers (an average of three extra cigarettes, ie, 103.3 for stressed smokers vs 99.9 for non-stressed smokers).25 Moreover, a Canadian study showed that alcohol consumption tends to be higher when the perception of mental health and work-related or life stress is low. This study highlights the role that the work environment could play in preventing stressful factors and reducing alcohol consumption.26 In Spain, researchers have reported a doubling of the risk of increased alcohol consumption based on exposure to a harmful work environment (OR=2.15, 95% CI 1.51 to 3.06 for moderate exposure level, OR=2.23, 95% CI 1.49 to 3.36 for high exposure level).27 Additionally, the study of Balayssac et al demonstrated that among 421 French male pharmacists, 16.7% of those with a high level of stress at work had alcohol consumption above the WHO limit, compared with 4% of men whose consumption was below the WHO limit (p<0.001).19

Furthermore, the study of CONSTANCES data highlights the effect of public contact on substance consumption: the consumption of tobacco and cannabis among professionals in contact with the public was higher than among professionals without public contact, for both men (OR=1.47, 95% CI 1.22 to 1.78, p<0.001 for high cigarette consumption; OR=1.38, 95% CI 1.17 to 1.63, p<0.001 for cannabis consumption, less than once a month) and women (OR=1.63, 95% CI 1.18 to 2.25, p=0.003 for high cigarette consumption; OR=1.31, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.63, p=0.017 for cannabis consumption, less than once a month). Alcohol consumption (chronic, at risk or significant occasional) was higher among men in contact with the public compared with those who were not (OR=1.71, 95% CI 1.43 to 2.05, p<0.001 for moderate chronic consumption; OR=1.22, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.33, p<0.001 for at-risk consumption; OR=1.33, 95% CI 1.18 to 1.51, p<0.001 for significant occasional alcohol use more than once a month).22

Regarding the association between work-related stress and the consumption of psychoactive medications, a meta-analysis of 18 unique studies showed that high job demands were associated with a statistically significant increased risk of psychotropic medication use (RR 1.16, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.31). In studies reporting OR, high job demands were associated with an OR of 1.39 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.71).28 Another study conducted among French community pharmacies showed that anxiolytic and hypnotic drug use was significantly associated with work-related stress scores, where anxiolytic and hypnotic drug users were more stressed (score ≥70) than non-users (anxiolytics: 65.1% vs 34.9%, p<0.001; hypnotics: 66.7% vs 33.3%, p<0.001) and had higher stress scores (anxiolytics: 71.97±17.34 vs 48.75±27.19, p<0.001; hypnotics: 73.35±19.74 vs 51.15±27.12, p<0.001).19

In France, early childhood professionals, including ‘Agents Territoriaux Spécialisés des Écoles Maternelles (ATSEMs)’, ‘animateurs’ and ‘agents d’entretien’, play an important role in the well-being of children. ‘ATSEMs’ are workers who help teachers in preschool classes; they are responsible for welcoming children, ensuring their hygiene, providing educational and technical support in the classroom, helping teachers with teaching staff during school hours, preparing materials, maintaining the class environment, as well as managing and assisting children throughout extracurricular time, such as breaks for lunch and care after school.29 Early childhood ‘animateurs’ are workers who play an important role in supporting the education, well-being and development of children during extracurricular periods only outside regular school hours, such as the time before classes start, during children’s meal and nap periods and after school hours. They are involved in organising the children’s daily routine within the school, in the leisure centres and during vacation. They also play with the children, supervise them in the playground and organise and manage safe educational, artistic and recreational (such as games and sports) workshops based on their needs, which aids in promoting the development, learning and socialisation of young children.30 ‘Agents d’entretien’ in charge of early childhood professionals are workers who maintain the safety and hygiene of children; they are responsible for cleaning and sanitising areas such as floors, bathrooms and play areas efficiently and effectively to ensure the health and safety of the children.31

The working conditions of early childhood professionals expose them to high levels of psychosocial stress and burnout. Indeed, this profession faces challenging working conditions, including material and human constraints, lack of recognition, paradoxical demands, exhaustion caused by children’s crying and screaming and more. They often work long hours, manage large groups of young children, deal with the emotional needs of children, and interact with parents. Internationally, research in occupational stress among professionals working in early childhood settings has primarily focused on early childhood educators (ECEs). Although the professionals’ roles and terminology vary across countries, ECEs share several workplace characteristics with French early childhood professionals (‘ATSEM’, ‘animateurs’ and ‘agents d’entretien’) working in nursery and elementary schools. ECEs face many stressors through their work, such as high workload, stressful environment, limited chances of progress and low pay, which cause low levels of job satisfaction and higher rates of turnover, as well as negative impacts on themselves, the field, and the children they work with.32 A recent study conducted among 137 ECEs showed that 62% suffer from workload and 44% from job strain, compared with 35% and 36%, respectively, within the national sample.33

In France, a recent study conducted in 340 nursery schools by the CNRACL National Prevention Fund among ATSEMs, which had a 67% response rate from 1347 questionnaires distributed, showed that 62% think that their work negatively impacts their physical or psychological health, 82% stated that their daily activities induce more anxiety, and half of the respondents feel a deterioration in their sleep quality due to their work. Moreover, this study showed that this population suffers from a lack of support from the community and feelings of isolation.34 Similarly, according to an in-depth study on childcare assistants, the average rate of long-term sick leave (90 days in the last 12 months) due to psychosocial risks in this population exceeds that of other territorial professions (30% compared with 25% for other professions).35 This could reflect dissatisfaction within this profession, despite its key role in preventing potential risky behaviours (addictions, dangerous behaviours, etc) that children may develop later on. In fact, according to a study by the Rhône-Alpes Regional Health Observatory, early childhood professionals are ‘hidden actors’ in this prevention. Therefore, their health status, especially mental health, at work is a major public health issue.36

Early childhood professionals may also be affected by depressive disorders, with possible consequences on the behaviour of children who are sensitive to the emotions of those around them.37 The study of Farewell et al demonstrated that among 136 ECEs, the rates of depression were 2.3 times higher than nationally available normative data,33 which were consistent with the study of Linnan et al showing that the rates of depression among this population are three times the national average (36% vs 12% clinically depressed).38

Investigating the relationship between work-related stress and addictions among professionals outside the healthcare sector would be enriching, especially regarding early childhood professionals, whose role is crucial and whose working conditions are challenging. This unique combination of stressors makes them an important population for studying the association between workplace stress and substance use. Despite their essential role, no studies have yet examined this association among early childhood professionals in France, particularly across the territories in the Axe ligérien (territories located along the Loire River in the Centre-Val de Loire and Pays de la Loire regions) in France, making this the first investigation of its kind in these territories.

By focusing on early childhood professionals, this study has the potential to inform the development of targeted interventions and workplace policies aimed at reducing stress and preventing substance use in this vulnerable group, and to better target professionals at risk. This could have significant implications for their well-being and overall job satisfaction, as well as for the well-being of the children under their care.

Objectives

Primary objective

To assess the association between work-related stress and the use of psychoactive substances (alcohol, tobacco and cannabis) and medications among early childhood professionals working in nursery and elementary schools in the participating territories of the Axe ligérien (territories located along the Loire River in the Centre-Val de Loire and Pays de la Loire regions) in France, and to evaluate the associated levels of substance-related risk or dependence.

Secondary objectives

To evaluate the association between work-related stress and burnout.

To assess the relationship between work-related stress and anxiety and depression symptoms.

To assess the relationship between work-related stress and anxiety and depression symptoms.

To study the association between work-related stress and sleep-related problems, particularly excessive daytime sleepiness.

To study the association between work-related stress and sleep-related problems, particularly excessive daytime sleepiness.

Hypothesis

We hypothesise that early childhood professionals exposed to job strain (ie, stressed work characterised by high psychological demands and low decision latitude according to the Job Content Questionnaire (JCQ)) will have higher odds of psychoactive substance or medication use, including higher levels of substance-related risk or dependence compared with those not exposed to job strain.

We also hypothesise that early childhood professionals exposed to job strain (ie, stressed work characterised by high psychological demands and low decision latitude according to the JCQ) will have higher odds of burnout, symptoms of anxiety and depression, and sleep-related problems (excessive daytime sleepiness) compared with those not exposed to job strain.

Methods

Study design

This study is a multicentre, observational, cross-sectional epidemiological study conducted across the Axe ligérien in France. Data collection started in Orléans Métropole, Fleury-les-Aubrais and the CCTVL and is ongoing in these territories. To increase participant recruitment, the investigators are currently seeking the necessary authorisations to extend recruitment to additional territories within the Axe ligérien. It is a non-interventional study with minimal risk and constraints, involving human participants.

The study population consists exclusively of ‘ATSEMs’, ‘animateurs’ and ‘agents d’entretien’, working in nursery and elementary schools in the participating territories of the Axe ligérien. ‘ATSEMs’ work in nursery schools only, whereas ‘animateurs’ and ‘agents d’entretien’ work in both nursery and elementary schools. Teachers are not included in the study.

Sample size

The required sample size was calculated using the BiostaTGV, an online biostatistics platform. As no published data were available regarding the prevalence of psychoactive substances or psychoactive medication use among early childhood professionals in France, the prevalence assumption used for the calculation was informed by occupational health observations provided by RS, an addiction specialist and occupational physician in Orléans Métropole, Fleury-les-Aubrais, and CCTVL between 2013 and 2025. Based on his professional observations, it was estimated that approximately 30% of early childhood professionals used at least one type of psychoactive substance or medication.

As no published study was identified that directly assessed the association between work-related stress and psychoactive substance or psychoactive medication use among early childhood professionals in France, the anticipated OR was informed by the closest relevant available evidence. Airagnes et al, using data from the French CONSTANCES cohort, showed that exposure to occupational stress was associated with an increased risk of alcohol dependence among women (OR=2.3; 95% CI 1.54 to 3.44, p<0.001).22 This study was considered relevant because it was conducted in France and included a large number of employed women, while the target population of the present study is predominantly female. Therefore, an OR of 2.3 was used as the anticipated OR for the sample size calculation.

Assuming a prevalence of psychoactive substance or psychoactive medication use of 30%, an OR of 2.3, a statistical power of 80% and an alpha of 0.05 (5%), the required sample size was estimated at 234 participants.

Participant characteristics and sampling procedure

The study population consists of early childhood professionals, comprising ‘ATSEMs’, ‘animateurs’ and ‘agents d’entretien’ employed in nursery and elementary schools in the participating territories of the Axe ligérien.

Information about the study is communicated to school directors by the research team (WK, RS and SO), who present the objectives and procedures of the study. School directors then relay this information to their staff members (‘ATSEMs’, ‘animateurs’ and ‘agents d’entretien’). In participating schools, posters presenting the study objectives and containing a QR code linking to the online questionnaire (online supplemental file 2) are displayed in staff rooms and other shared areas to allow staff members to access the survey. Flyers containing the same information and QR code are also distributed among staff. The posters and flyers include the contact details of WK so that staff members can request additional information if needed.

Inclusion is based on voluntary participation following the presentation of the study in detail and the sharing of study information through posters and flyers. To maximise participation, minimise selection bias and protect anonymity, all staff members, regardless of their intention to participate, are approached equally, and posters and flyers presenting the study are made available to the entire staff. The posters and flyers include a QR code that, once scanned, links to an information sheet (online supplemental file 1) explaining that the study complies with ethical guidelines, informs participants of their rights and states that by proceeding to the questionnaire, they express their non-opposition to participate. The same QR code also provides direct access to the online self-administered questionnaire following the information sheet. The study is completely anonymous, and no personal identifying information (eg, names, phone numbers, or email addresses) is collected from participants.

Study procedure

Data collection started in Orléans Métropole, Fleury-les-Aubrais and the CCTVL, and is ongoing in these territories. To increase participant recruitment, the investigators are currently seeking the necessary authorisations to extend recruitment to additional territories across the Axe ligérien.

Official approval was obtained from the Directors General of Services (DGS) of Orléans Métropole, Fleury-les-Aubrais and the CCTVL following meetings held in 2025 during which the research team presented the study objectives, methodology, feasibility and implementation procedures. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Centre Hospitalier Universitaire (CHU) d’Orléans on 5 December 2025 (assigned reference number: CERO 2511-02).

Self-administered online questionnaires accessed via QR codes are used for data collection and are completed by participants. The online questionnaire takes approximately 30–40 min to complete. Posters and flyers including the study title, objectives and significance are created to inform the targeted population about the study and invite them to participate. Each poster and flyer includes a QR code linking directly to the online questionnaire accessible through participants’ phones. In addition to the questionnaire, the same QR code is linked to an information sheet presented to participants immediately before the questionnaire. This sheet explains that the study complies with ethical guidelines, informs participants of their rights and states that by continuing, they do not object to participating. It also provides the investigator’s (WK) email address for any questions.

Prior to the start of data collection, meetings were held separately with stakeholders and educational directors of nursery and elementary schools in Orléans Métropole, the CCTVL and Fleury-les-Aubrais. The purpose of these meetings was to present the study in detail, including its objectives, methodology, procedures and overall relevance to the targeted professions. As educational directors are familiar with the working environment and are in direct contact with school staff, discussions during these meetings aimed to identify any practical constraints related to the implementation of the study and to ensure that the questionnaires and study procedures were understandable and feasible in the field. This step is intended to allow for some adjustments to the questionnaire or study procedures, if necessary, based on their experience before the launch of the data collection.

These discussions also aimed to obtain the educational directors’ agreement to facilitate the implementation of the study and to jointly define a clear and feasible plan for how the data collection would be carried out. As previously discussed and agreed with DGSs in Orléans Métropole, the CCTVL and Fleury-les-Aubrais, once the directors have a clear understanding of the study, they help the research team by informing staff members (‘ATSEMs’, ‘animateurs’, ‘agents d’entretien’) about the study and the importance of their participation. They also assist the research team by ensuring that posters containing the QR code linking to the online questionnaire are displayed in staff rooms and other shared areas within the participating schools and that flyers are distributed to staff members. The posters and flyers also include the investigator’s email address, allowing participants to contact her privately and confidentially if they have questions or require further clarification before they complete the questionnaires or decide whether to participate.

In addition, during the data collection phase, dedicated information sessions were organised with some of the staff members in coordination with educational directors. These sessions provided an opportunity for the research team to meet some of the staff members directly, present the study and its importance, explain its objectives and procedures, answer participants’ questions and provide additional information regarding participation in the study.

Throughout the data collection period, several follow-up meetings were conducted with educational directors to monitor the progress of the study, identify any practical difficulties encountered during recruitment or questionnaire administration and discuss appropriate solutions to facilitate study implementation.

To increase participant recruitment, the research team is currently seeking the necessary authorisations from the DGSs and educational directors of additional territories across the Axe ligérien to extend recruitment to these territories. The same collaborative approach will be followed, drawing on the experience gained, practical insights obtained and adaptations made during the implementation of the study in Orléans Métropole, Fleury-les-Aubrais and the CCTVL in order to facilitate and optimise recruitment and data collection in these additional territories.

Data collection process

After the meetings with the directors, staff members (‘ATSEMs’, ‘animateurs’, ‘agents d’entretien’) are informed about the study and its details by their directors. Posters containing QR codes are displayed in staff rooms and other shared areas within the participating schools, and flyers are distributed to staff members. Participants can scan the QR code using their phones, which links directly to the online questionnaire. They can complete the questionnaire at a time that suits them, either during working hours or outside of work, during the data collection period. During the data collection period, follow-up meetings are conducted with educational directors to monitor study progress, discuss any issues related to study implementation and encourage educational directors to continue reminding staff members about the study and inviting them to participate throughout the data collection period. The duration of the data collection period is planned to continue until February 2027. This timeframe allows for the potential inclusion of additional territories across the Axe ligérien and the completion of data collection following the necessary administrative approvals.

No identifiable personal information such as names, email addresses or phone numbers is collected in order to preserve full anonymity of participants, and to help them feel more comfortable when answering sensitive questions regarding work-related stress, the use of psychoactive substances and medications, and their mental health without fear of identification. Participants are also assured that the information collected remains confidential and that participation is entirely voluntary. Participants complete the online questionnaire either during or outside of working hours, during the data collection period, taking approximately 30–40 min. All variables, including work-related stress and study outcomes, are assessed at a single time point through one self-administered online questionnaire during the data collection period.

The questionnaire (online supplemental file 2) used for data collection was composed of several parts:

Sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics: age, gender, marital status, having children, educational level and household composition.

Sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics: age, gender, marital status, having children, educational level and household composition.

Work-related variables: professional role, working hours, job income, duration of employment, job satisfaction, perceived value of their work, relationship with children’s parents and relationship with teachers.

Work-related variables: professional role, working hours, job income, duration of employment, job satisfaction, perceived value of their work, relationship with children’s parents and relationship with teachers.

Assessment of work-related stress: work-related stress will be assessed using Karasek’s JCQ and operationalised as job strain (stressed work), defined by high psychological demands and low decision latitude. Participants will be classified into an exposed group (participants with job strain, also referred to as stressed work) and a non-exposed group (participants without job strain), defined by other combinations of psychological demands and decision latitude, comprising the active, passive and relaxed work categories based on the JCQ.

Participants will be classified into an exposed group (participants with job strain, also referred to as stressed work) and a non-exposed group (participants without job strain), defined by other combinations of psychological demands and decision latitude, comprising the active, passive and relaxed work categories based on the JCQ.

Data measurements

Karasek’s JCQ

The JCQ, developed by Karasek, is an internationally used questionnaire designed to assess self-reported exposure to psychosocial stressors in the workplace. It is one of the most widely used instruments for evaluating such exposures in occupational health research. The JCQ evaluates three dimensions of the work environment: the psychological demands (nine items), the decision latitude (nine items) and the social support (eight items). The questionnaire includes 26 items with a 4-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (4).

The three dimensions are calculated through the following three formulas10 39:

Psychological demands (Q10+Q11+Q12+(5−Q13)+Q14+Q15+Q16+Q17+Q18).

Psychological demands (Q10+Q11+Q12+(5−Q13)+Q14+Q15+Q16+Q17+Q18).

Decision latitude (4×Q4+ 4×(5−Q6)+4×Q8+ 2×(5−Q2)+2×Q5+2×Q7+2×Q1+2×Q3+2×Q9).

Decision latitude (4×Q4+ 4×(5−Q6)+4×Q8+ 2×(5−Q2)+2×Q5+2×Q7+2×Q1+2×Q3+2×Q9).

Social support (Q19+Q20+Q21+Q22+Q23+Q24+Q25+Q26).

Social support (Q19+Q20+Q21+Q22+Q23+Q24+Q25+Q26).

The combination of psychological demand and decision latitude can give four situations at work:

Relaxed work (low demand and high decision latitude),

Relaxed work (low demand and high decision latitude),

Passive work (low demand and low decision latitude),

Passive work (low demand and low decision latitude),

Active work (high demand and high decision latitude),

Active work (high demand and high decision latitude),

Stressed, tense work (high demand and low decision latitude).

Stressed, tense work (high demand and low decision latitude).

High psychological demands were defined as a score >21, low decision latitude as a score <70 and low social support as a score <24. Job strain (stressed, tense work) was defined as the combination of high psychological demands levels (score >21) and low decision latitude levels (score <70). Iso-strain, a more severe form of job strain, was defined by the co-occurrence of job strain and low social support level (score<24).4 12 40 In this study, we will use the validated French version of the JCQ.41

Exposed group: participants classified as experiencing job strain (stressed/tense work), defined as the combination of high psychological demands (score >21) and low decision latitude (score <70).

Non-exposed group: participants not classified as experiencing job strain (stressed/tense work) and classified in the other Karasek categories (relaxed, passive and active work).

Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence

This test is used to assess the level of nicotine dependence among people who smoke.42 It consists of six items, which are summed to give a total score ranging from 0 to 10. Nicotine dependence is categorised into five levels: no dependence (0–2), low dependence (3–4), medium dependence (5–6), high dependence (7–8) and very high dependence (9–10).43 The French version of the FTND will be used in this study.44

Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test

This test is a 10-item screening tool developed by the WHO to assess alcohol consumption, alcohol-related behaviours and alcohol-related problems.45 When the items are summed, they give a score ranging from 0 to 40, which provides an indication of alcohol use disorder severity. Scores between 0 and 7 indicate abstinence or low-risk alcohol use, scores between 8 and 15 suggest hazardous alcohol use, between 16 and 19 harmful alcohol use and ≥20 probable dependence.46 We will use the French validated version of AUDIT in this study.47

Cannabis Abuse Screening Test

This test is a 6-item screening tool used to assess cannabis-related problems.48 The response options are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (very often). The total score obtained after summing the items can range from 0 to 24 and indicates whether or not the respondents are at risk. A score less than 3 indicates no risk of problematic cannabis use, a score between 3 and 6 indicates a low risk of problematic cannabis use and a score of 7 or higher indicates a high risk of dependence. The validated French version of CAST will be used in this study.49 50

Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test

This reliable instrument, developed by the WHO, is designed to identify problematic or risky substance use, including substances used without medical supervision as well as the non-medical use or misuse of prescribed medications.

It includes eight core questions applied across multiple substance categories (tobacco, alcohol, cannabis, cocaine, amphetamine-type stimulants, inhalants, sedatives, hallucinogens, opiates and other drugs). In the present study, the ASSIST will be administered only for substance categories other than tobacco, alcohol and cannabis, as validated instruments (AUDIT, FTND and CAST) are already used to assess these substances. The instrument will focus on substances used for reasons other than medical prescription, or more frequently or at higher doses than those recommended.

Question 1 assesses lifetime use of substances, while question 2 addresses the frequency of substance use during the past 3 months. Responses are rated on a 5-point frequency scale ranging from ‘never (in the past 3 months)’ to ‘daily or almost daily’. If none of the substances have been used in the past 3 months, the interviewer skips directly to the last three questions concerning lifetime problems and patterns of former use. If any substance has been used during the past 3 months, questions 3–5 are asked before concluding with questions 6–8.

Question 3 explores the compulsion to use substances in the previous 3 months (a measure of psychological dependence). Question 4 screens for health, social, financial or legal problems related to substance use within the past 3 months. Question 5 investigates whether participants have failed to meet role obligations. Questions 6–8 screen for lifetime and recent problems, including concerns from relatives, previous attempts to control substance use, and current or lifetime drug injection. For each substance, a score between 0 and 3 indicates non-problematic use (low risk), a score between 4 and 26 indicates abuse (moderate risk) and a score of 27 or higher indicates dependence (high risk). We will use the validated French version of the ASSIST questionnaire.51 52

Maslach Burnout Inventory

The prevalence of burnout is the main outcome of this scale. We will use the validated French version of the MBI-Human Services Survey in our study.53 54 It consists of 22 items, each of which is scored from 0 (never) to 6 (every day). Nine of the items evaluate emotional exhaustion, five evaluate depersonalisation and eight evaluate personal accomplishment.53 The cut-off score for emotional exhaustion is ≥ 30 and that of depersonalisation domain ≥12. The cut-off for reduced personal accomplishment is ≤33.55 56 Burnout is experienced by participants when the level of emotional exhaustion or depersonalisation exceeds the cut-off, regardless of the presence or absence of reduced personal accomplishment.57 58

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale

This 14-item (seven items for each subscale: depression and anxiety) self-report scale is used to assess anxiety and depression and their severity.59 We will use the validated French version of the HADS questionnaire, which has demonstrated a reliable and valid clinical assessment of depression and anxiety.60 61 Each item is scored on a 4-point Likert scale. The total score of each subscale is obtained by summing the respective seven items, ranging from 0 to 21. Three categories based on cut-off scores are commonly used: 0–7 (non-cases), 8–10 (borderline or possible cases) and 11–21 (probable cases).59 62

Epworth Sleepiness Scale

It is an 8-item questionnaire used to assess the level of daytime sleepiness. The validated French version of the ESS scale will be used in this study. The items of the scale have a 4-point scale, where ‘0’ indicates ‘would never doze’, while ‘3’ indicates a ‘high chance of dozing’. The scale questions refer to eight different situations encountered in daily life. The ESS total score is generated by adding all the individual item scores, ranging between 0 and 24. A score of 10 or less indicates no excessive daytime sleepiness, while a score greater than 10 indicates excessive daytime sleepiness.63 64

Data management

Data management will include secure collection, storage and analysis of data, with measures to ensure confidentiality and participant anonymity. The data will be securely stored and accessible only to the principal investigators. The results will be analysed and reported in aggregate form, preventing the identification of individual participants. Data cleaning procedures will be conducted prior to analysis as part of the data management process to ensure the quality and consistency of the data.

Bias

Several measures will be implemented to minimise potential sources of bias. Participation is anonymous in order to reduce social desirability bias and under-reporting of sensitive behaviours, particularly psychoactive substance and medication use as well as burnout, symptoms of anxiety and depression, and sleep-related problems. All staff members are informed through the same communication channels and have equal access to the questionnaire, thereby reducing selection bias. Validated instruments are used to limit measurement bias. Multivariable analyses will be conducted to adjust for potential confounding factors.

Safety considerations

This study is a non-interventional observational study involving minimal risk and constraints. Participation is entirely voluntary and anonymous. Participants may stop completing the questionnaire at any time without providing justification and without any consequences.

In accordance with the provisions of the French Public Health Code (CSP) governing research involving human participants, as well as the requirements of the General Data Protection Regulation, and the recommendations of the French Data Protection Authority (Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés, ‘CNIL’), all necessary precautions are taken to ensure the confidentiality of information relating to the research, the participants, as well as the results obtained. Only anonymised data are collected and processed. Access to the anonymised dataset is restricted to the principal investigators for quality control and statistical analysis.

To ensure anonymity, the online data collection platform used does not collect any identifiable personal information such as names, email addresses or phone numbers. Data are being collected electronically using the LimeSurvey digital platform, a secure system designed for confidential data collection and storage. The data will then be exported directly to IBM SPSS Statistics software for data cleaning and statistical analysis. Should recruitment be extended to additional territories following the necessary authorisations and agreements, data will also be collected using the same platform.

Digitally collected data are securely stored and protected, with access restricted to the investigators. Research-related documents and anonymised data will be retained after 5 years, in accordance with applicable provisions of the CSP and institutional archiving requirements.

Information regarding participants’ rights, data confidentiality and ethical compliance is clearly explained in the information sheet, which participants access via QR codes prior to completing the questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

At the end of data collection, adequate data management will be performed to obtain a clean database. Participants who did not provide sufficient information to assess eligibility according to the inclusion criteria will be excluded from the relevant analysis. After data cleaning and validation, the final database will be established before statistical analyses. Data collected through LimeSurveyu will be exported directly to IBM SPSS Statistics software for data cleaning and statistical analysis.

A descriptive analysis of the study population will be conducted. Results will be presented as percentages and frequencies for categorical variables. Graphical representations will be used for better visualisation of the data. Prevalence rates of psychoactive substances and medication use, including tobacco, alcohol, cannabis and psychoactive substances will be calculated based on participants’ responses to the standardised instruments. The prevalence of the corresponding levels of substance-related risk or dependence will also be estimated according to the classification criteria of each validated instrument. Work-related stress, operationalised as job strain and assessed using the JCQ, will be considered the main exposure variable. Participants with high psychological demands and low decision latitude will be classified as exposed to job strain. The primary outcome variables will include psychoactive substance and medication use, as well as associated levels of substance-related risk or dependence. These outcomes will be assessed for tobacco, alcohol, cannabis and psychoactive medications using validated instruments. The secondary outcome variables will include burnout, symptoms of anxiety and depression and sleep-related problems (excessive daytime sleepiness). Associations between work-related stress and each primary and secondary outcome will be examined separately.

Bivariate statistical analysis will be performed using the Pearson χ2 test (or Fisher’s exact test, when appropriate) for categorical variables to study associations between each of the relevant independent variables and outcome variables. Then, multivariable analyses will be conducted according to the nature of the outcome variable to identify factors associated with the outcomes of interest. Variables showing an association in the bivariate analysis (p value <0.2) will be included in multivariable analysis models. The type I error rate will be set at 5% and a p value <0.05 will be considered statistically significant. Results will be presented using appropriate measures of association with 95% CIs. Participants who do not provide sufficient information to assess eligibility according to the inclusion criteria will be excluded from all analyses. Questionnaire data will be used whenever sufficient responses are available to calculate the relevant score or classification according to the scoring rules of the corresponding instrument. Participants will only be excluded from a specific analysis when incomplete questionnaire data prevent the determination of the outcome or exposure measure required for that analysis. Consequently, incomplete data for one questionnaire or instrument will not necessarily lead to exclusion from all analyses. Analyses will be conducted using all available data, and participants may contribute to specific analysis even if they do not complete all subsequent questionnaires.

To ensure transparency and adherence to ethical guidelines, the QR code first links to an information sheet presented to participants directly before the questionnaire. This sheet explains that our study adheres to ethical guidelines, informs participants about their rights and states that by continuing, they do not object to participating in the study and proceeding to the questionnaire is considered as implied consent (non-opposition). It also includes the investigator’s email address in case participants have questions.

Each participant receives detailed information about the purpose, methods, benefits, risks and confidentiality of the study through information provided by the educational directors and the study materials made available in participating schools.

Participation is entirely voluntary and based on a free decision. Participants are free to complete the questionnaire in full or to stop at any stage. Questionnaires are completed anonymously, without collection of participants’ names. The anonymity and confidentiality of collected information are also explained to participants, a step that remains primary and essential for their motivation and comfort to participate. Data are collected via a secure online platform (LimeSurvey), stored on secure servers in accordance with applicable data protection regulations, and accessible only to the principal investigators. All data will be used exclusively for scientific purposes. Anonymised data will be retained in accordance with applicable French regulations and institutional archiving requirements and will be securely destroyed at the end of the applicable retention period.

The overall findings of the study will be communicated after the completion of data collection, analysis and reporting. The results will be published in open-access scientific journals, ensuring that all interested parties, including participating centres and individuals, can access them freely. In addition, the study and its results will be presented at national and international scientific conferences in the fields of addiction and mental health, building on the dissemination already initiated through presentations at three conferences to date: BIOTECHNOSCIENCES 2024 Congress (poster presentation) in Nouan-le-Fuzelier, France; the ALBATROS 2025 Congress (oral presentation) in Paris, France; and the C³PO—Clinical Research, Projects and Precision in Psychiatry 2025 conference (oral presentation) in Orléans, France.

Expected results

Participants exposed to job strain (stressed work characterised by high psychological demands and low decision latitude) are expected to report higher levels of substance use such as alcohol, tobacco, cannabis and psychoactive medications, as well as higher levels of substance-related risk or dependence, compared with participants not exposed to job strain. In addition, participants exposed to job strain are expected to report higher levels of burnout, symptoms of depression and anxiety, as well as sleep-related problems (excessive daytime sleepiness).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this cross-sectional study is the first conducted in France among early childhood professionals. It sheds light on a population that has not been specifically targeted in previous studies, thereby addressing an important gap in the literature. This study aims to elucidate the relationship between work-related stress (operationalised by job strain and assessed using JCQ) and the use of psychoactive substances and medications, as well as the associated levels of substance-related risks or dependence among early childhood professionals, as well as the association of work-related stress and burnout, depression, anxiety and sleep-related problems (excessive daytime sleepiness).

Strengths and limitations

From a scientific perspective, this study will provide new epidemiological data on the associations between work-related stress, burnout, anxiety, depression, excessive daytime sleepiness and psychoactive substance and medication use among early childhood professionals, a population that remains underrepresented in the French literature. These findings will contribute to a better understanding of psychosocial risks in the early childhood education sector and may serve as a basis for future interventional or preventive studies.

From a societal and occupational health perspective, the study may provide participants with information regarding the association between work-related stress and psychoactive substance and medication use, as well as burnout, depression, anxiety and sleep-related problems, thereby helping to improve understanding of these relationships. It may also increase awareness of mental health and well-being among participants and inform the development of targeted interventions and workplace policies aimed at improving mental health and well-being among early childhood professionals. The study may also contribute to the design of support systems and practices designed to improve quality of life at work.

The study is completely anonymous and confidential; no personal information will be obtained, and data will be collected digitally using QR codes, which may enhance participation.

Despite the strengths of this study, there are a few limitations. First, the moderate sample size may limit the statistical power of the analyses. Second, the use of a self-administered online questionnaire may introduce information bias, including recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly given the sensitive nature of substance use and psychological distress. However, the fully anonymous and confidential design of the study, with no collection of identifying data and digital data collection through QR codes, is expected to encourage honest responses and help reduce these biases; this represents a key strength of this study. Third, the study is conducted in a specific geographical area and targets a specific occupational population, early childhood professionals, which may limit the generalisability of the results to other territories or populations.

Amendments

In the case of any amendments to the study protocol, the revised version will be submitted to the ethics committee of the CHU of Orléans for approval prior to implementation. Any changes to the protocol will be reported in the publications resulting from this study.

In the unlikely event of study termination, the reasons for termination will be documented, and all data collected up to that point will be handled in accordance with applicable ethics guidelines and data protection regulations.
