Cluster size distributions of discrete random fields
Dan Cheng, John Ginos

Abstract
We study discrete random fields { X t : t ∈ ℤ d } \{X_{t}:t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\} parameterized on the d d -dimensional integer lattice ℤ d {\mathbb{Z}}^{d} . For a fixed threshold u u , the excursion set { t ∈ ℤ d : X t > u } \{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}:X_{t}>u\} decomposes into connected components or clusters, whose size, defined as the number of lattice points they contain, are random. This paper investigates the probability distribution of these cluster sizes. For stationary random fields, we derive exact expressions for the cluster size distribution. To address nonstationary settings, we introduce a peak-based cluster size distribution, which characterizes the distribution of cluster sizes conditional on the presence of a local maximum above u u . This formulation provides a tractable alternative when exact cluster size distributions are analytically inaccessible. The proposed framework applies broadly to Gaussian and non-Gaussian random fields, relying only on their joint dependence structure. Our results provide a theoretical foundation for quantifying spatial extent in discretely sampled data, with applications to medical imaging, geoscience, environmental monitoring, and other scientific areas where thresholded random fields naturally arise.



Keywords
: random fields; discrete lattice; spatial extent; connected components; cluster size; peak-based; stationary; nonstationary; threshold.

Understanding the statistical distribution of cluster sizes in random fields is fundamental for both theory and applications. In contrast, for discrete random fields on lattices, it is possible to formulate and compute cluster size distributions explicitly under suitable assumptions. In this paper, we investigate the cluster size distribution of discrete stationary random fields
\{X_{t}:t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\}

{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(|C_{u}|=k\ |\ \text{a cluster $C_{u}$ above $u$ exists}),\quad k=1,2,\ldots,\end{split}

Motivated by recent advances in peak inference for random fields

It is particularly valuable in nonstationary settings, where local variations in the covariance structure influence both the shape and spatial extent of clusters. In contrast, obtaining exact cluster size distributions in the nonstationary case appears analytically intractable.

In many applications, data are observed on discrete grids rather than as continuous fields. The framework developed in this paper thus provides a unified theoretical foundation for cluster modeling and inference in discrete random fields. Beyond its intrinsic theoretical interest, the results have broad practical implications for signal detection and spatial extent inference in correlated lattice data. The exact cluster size distribution

We begin with stationary discrete random fields and derive exact expressions for their cluster size distributions. In general, the analysis of random fields indexed by
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
is conceptually rich and technically demanding. To present the main ideas in a transparent manner, we first focus on the one-dimensional case, where the structure of clusters and the required joint probabilities admit explicit characterization.

2.1
Stationary processes on
{\mathbb{Z}}

Let
\{X_{t}:t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
be a real-valued centered stationary process on the one-dimensional integer lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Fix a threshold
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
and consider the excursion set

\{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}:X_{t}>u\}.

Its connected components (intervals of consecutive integers) are called
clusters
. The size of a cluster is defined as the number of lattice points it contains. We denote the size of a cluster by
S_{u}
and study the distribution

{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}=k),\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,

which is interpreted as the probability that a cluster above level
u
has size
k
, conditional on the event that such a cluster exists. Formally,

{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}=k)={\mathbb{P}}(|C_{u}|=k\ |\ \text{a cluster $C_{u}$ above $u$ exists}),

(2.1)

where
|C_{u}|
denotes the size of a cluster
C_{u}
. Figure
1
provides a visual illustration. The following theorem gives an exact closed-form expression for this cluster size distribution.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\{X_{t}:t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
be a centered stationary process and let
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
be a fixed threshold. Then, for
k=1,2,\ldots
,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}=k)=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{-1}\leq u,X_{0}>u,X_{1}>u,\ldots,X_{k-1}>u,X_{k}\leq u)}{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{-1}\leq u,X_{0}>u)}.\end{split}

(2.2)

Proof.

By stationarity, the size distribution of clusters is invariant under translations of the index set. Hence, it suffices to study clusters whose leftmost point is at the origin. The event that a cluster above
u
starts at
0
is exactly
\{X_{-1}\leq u,\;X_{0}>u\}
.

Thus, the cluster size distribution can be expressed as the conditional probability

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}=k)&={\mathbb{P}}(|C_{u}|=k\ |\ \text{there exists a cluster $C_{u}$ above $u$ starting at 0})\\
&={\mathbb{P}}(|C_{u}|=k\ |\ X_{-1}\leq u,X_{0}>u),\quad k=1,2,\ldots.\end{split}

Under this conditioning, the size of the cluster is the number of consecutive exceedances starting at 0 before the process falls back below
u
. Hence the cluster
C_{u}
has size
k
if and only if

X_{1}>u,\ \ldots,\ X_{k-1}>u,\ X_{k}\leq u.

Formula (
2.2
) follows directly from the conditional probability rule.
∎

Example 2.2

Let
X_{t}
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, be a white noise process with common cumulative distribution function (CDF)
F
. Let
p=F(u)
and
q=1-p
. Then, by (
2.2
),

{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}=k)=\frac{p^{2}q^{k}}{pq}=pq^{k-1},\quad k=1,2,\ldots.

(2.3)

Thus, in the one-dimensional i.i.d. case, the cluster size has a geometric distribution.
∎

2.2
An alternative perspective via the empirical distribution

We next demonstrate that Theorem
2.1
is consistent with the empirical cluster size distribution computed in simulations. Before doing so, we introduce an equivalent interpretation of the cluster size distribution that is particularly convenient for connecting theory with empirical estimation.

In one dimension, each cluster above
u
is an interval of consecutive indices. By stationarity, all such clusters are statistically equivalent up to translation. We therefore designate the
root
of a cluster as its leftmost point and translate so that this root is at the origin. A size-
k
cluster then has the canonical index set
\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
.

Let
w_{k}
denote the probability that there exists a size-
k
cluster above
u
with root at 0, i.e.,

\begin{split}w_{k}&={\mathbb{P}}(\text{there exists a size-$k$ cluster above $u$ starting at 0})\\
&={\mathbb{P}}(X_{-1}\leq u,X_{0}>u,X_{1}>u,\ldots,X_{k-1}>u,X_{k}\leq u).\end{split}

(2.4)

The quantity
w_{k}
represents the
per-lattice density
of size-
k
clusters. Consequently, in a domain of size
N
, the expected number of size-
k
clusters above
u
is
Nw_{k}
.

Summing
w_{k}
over all over all possible sizes
k\geq 1
yields the probability that there exists a cluster above
u
with root at 0:

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}={\mathbb{P}}(\text{there exists a cluster above $u$ starting at 0})={\mathbb{P}}(X_{-1}\leq u,X_{0}>u).

(2.5)

Thus,
\sum_{k\geq 1}w_{k}
represents the per-lattice density of all clusters above
u
, and the expected number of clusters above
u
in a domain of size
N
is
N\sum_{k\geq 1}w_{k}
.

Substituting (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) into the definition of cluster size distribution (
2.1
), and using stationarity, we recover the expression in (
2.2
):

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}=k)&=\frac{w_{k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{j}}.\end{split}

We now connect this to the empirical distribution used in simulations. Suppose the observation window consists of
N
lattice sites. For
k\geq 1
, the empirical probability mass function of the cluster size is given by

\begin{split}&\quad\frac{\#\{\text{size-$k$ clusters above $u$}\}}{\#\{\text{all clusters above $u$}\}}=\frac{\#\{\text{size-$k$ clusters above $u$}\}/N}{\#\{\text{all clusters above $u$}\}/N}\\
&\overset{a.s.}{\to}\frac{\text{average number of size-$k$ clusters per lattice site}}{\text{average number of clusters per lattice site}}\\
&=\frac{w_{k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{j}},\qquad\text{as }N\to\infty,\end{split}

(2.6)

where the convergence follows from the strong law of large numbers under stationarity and ergodicity. Intuitively, as the observation window grows, the empirical relative frequencies of cluster sizes converge to the theoretical cluster size distribution.

2.3
Stationary random fields on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

We now extend the analysis to a stationary random field on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. In higher dimensions, issues that were trivial in one dimension, such as the possible shapes of clusters and the ordering of points, require additional care.

2.3.1
Basic notation

Let
\{X_{t}:t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\}
be a centered stationary random field indexed on the
d
-dimensional integer lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. Clusters are defined as connected components of the excursion set
\{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}:X_{t}>u\}
. To define connectivity we introduce two neighbourhood systems on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

Denote the origin by
o=(0,\ldots,0)
, and let
e_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
, …,
e_{d}=(0,0,\ldots,1)
be the standard basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For
t=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, we define the
nearest neighbor
set of
t
by

\{t^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}:\|t^{\prime}-t\|=1\}=\{t\pm e_{i}:i=1,\ldots,d\},

(2.7)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm. Under nearest neighbor connectivity each site has exactly
2d
neighbors.

For completeness, we also introduce the
Moore neighbor
set of
t
:

\{t^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}:\|t^{\prime}-t\|_{\infty}=1\},

(2.8)

where
\|(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})\|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}|t_{i}|
denotes the maximum norm. Thus, the Moore neighbor includes also diagonals and contains
3^{d}-1
points. Figure
2
illustrates these two neighborhood systems for
d=2
.

We generalize the one-dimensional approach by defining a canonical root for each cluster. In
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, each cluster above
u
is a finite connected set of indices (connected under either the nearest or the Moore neighborhood). As in one dimension, stationarity suggests choosing the “lower left” point of the cluster as its root.

To make this precise, we introduce the
lexicographic order
on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. For two distinct points
t=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{d})
and
t^{\prime}=(t_{1}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,t_{d}^{\prime})
in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, we write

\text{$t<_{\rm lex}t^{\prime}$ if and only if there exists $j$ such that $t_{j}<t_{j}^{\prime}$ and $t_{i}=t_{i}^{\prime}$ for all $i<j$}.

Thus the first coordinate where the two vectors differ determines the ordering. For example, in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
,

(0,0)<_{\rm lex}(0,1)<_{\rm lex}(1,-1)<_{\rm lex}(1,0)<_{\rm lex}(1,1).

This is a total order, so every finite nonempty subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
has a unique smallest element. We define the
root
of a cluster to be this lexicographically smallest element. By stationarity, we may translate a cluster so that its root is at the origin
o
; in particular,
o<_{\rm lex}t
for all nonzero
t
in the cluster.

and

), as the increased number of connections similarly promotes the formation of larger connected components.

2.3.2
Cluster size distribution in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

For
k=1,2,\ldots
, denote the collection of all rooted clusters of size
k
by

\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm root}=\{\text{size-$k$ clusters above $u$ with the root at $o$}\}.

For example, under nearest neighbors in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\rm root}&=\{o\},\quad\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\rm root}=\{\{o,e_{1}\},\{o,e_{2}\}\},\\
\mathcal{C}_{3}^{\rm root}&=\{\{o,e_{1},2e_{1}\},\{o,e_{1},(1,1)\},\{o,e_{1},(1,-1)\},\{o,e_{1},e_{2}\},\{o,e_{2},2e_{2}\},\{o,e_{2},(1,1)\}\}.\end{split}

(2.9)

If one uses Moore neighbors, then
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\rm root}
contains two additional sets
\{o,(1,1)\}
and
\{o,(1,-1)\}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{3}^{\rm root}
includes further shapes such as
\{o,(1,1),(2,2)\}
,
\{o,e_{1},(2,1)\}
, and others.

For a finite set
D\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, we write
X_{D}>u
if
X_{t}>u
for all
t\in D
, and
X_{D}\leq u
if
X_{t}\leq u
for all
t\in D
. Let
\mathcal{N}(D)
denote the
exterior neighbor
set of
D
:

\mathcal{N}(D)=\begin{cases}\{t^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\setminus D:\|t^{\prime}-t\|=1\text{ for some }t\in D\},&\text{nearest neighbors,}\\
\{t^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\setminus D:\|t^{\prime}-t\|_{\infty}=1\text{ for some }t\in D\},&\text{Moore neighbors}.\end{cases}

(2.10)

We can now state the exact cluster size distribution in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\{X_{t}:t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\}
be a centered stationary random field and let
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
be a fixed threshold. Then, for
k=1,2,\ldots
,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}=k)=\frac{w_{k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{j}},\end{split}

(2.11)

where
w_{k}
is the probability that a cluster with root at the origin has size
k
, i.e.,

\begin{split}w_{k}=\sum_{D\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm root}}{\mathbb{P}}(X_{D}>u,X_{\mathcal{N}(D)}\leq u).\end{split}

(2.12)

Proof.

By stationarity we may assume that the cluster under consideration, denoted
C_{u}
, has root at the origin
o
. Equivalently, we condition on the event that
o
is the lexicographically smallest point in its cluster. Then

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}=k)&={\mathbb{P}}\bigl(|C_{u}|=k\,\big|\,\text{there exists a cluster $C_{u}$ above $u$ with root at $o$}\bigr)\\
&=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(\text{$C_{u}$ with root at $o$ has size $k$})}{{\mathbb{P}}(\text{there exists a cluster above $u$ with root at $o$})}.\end{split}

The numerator is obtained by summing over all rooted clusters of size
k
:

{\mathbb{P}}(\text{$C_{u}$ with root at $o$ has size $k$})=\sum_{D\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm root}}{\mathbb{P}}(X_{D}>u,X_{\mathcal{N}(D)}\leq u)=w_{k};

and similarly, the denominator becomes

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{C}_{j}^{\rm root}}{\mathbb{P}}(X_{D}>u,X_{\mathcal{N}(D)}\leq u)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{j}.

Substituting these into the conditional probability formula yields (
2.11
).
∎

Example 2.4

Let
X_{t}
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, be a two-dimensional white noise field with common CDF
F
. Let
p=F(u)
and
q=1-p
. Inspecting the sets in
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm root}
(e.g., (
2.9
)) and the corresponding boundary sizes
|\mathcal{N}(D)|
yields that, under nearest neighbors,

w_{1}=p^{4}q,\quad w_{2}=2p^{6}q^{2},\quad w_{3}=(2p^{8}+4p^{7})q^{3}.

Similarly, under Moore neighbors,

w_{1}=p^{8}q,\quad w_{2}=(2p^{10}+2p^{12})q^{2},\quad w_{3}=(6p^{12}+8p^{14}+4p^{15}+2p^{16})q^{3}.

In general, the combinatorial complexity of
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm root}
grows rapidly with
k
, so closed-form expressions for
w_{k}
are rarely available. Nevertheless,
w_{k}
can be evaluated numerically by enumerating shapes or approximated via Monte-Carlo simulation.
∎

Remark 2.5 [Interpretation of
w_{k}
and empirical relations.]
Each
w_{k}
is the per-lattice density of size-
k
clusters. If the field is observed on a finite domain of
N^{d}
lattice points, then the expected numbers of size-
k
clusters and of all clusters above
u
are
N^{d}w_{k}
and
N^{d}\sum_{j\geq 1}w_{j}
, respectively. Consequently, the empirical proportion of size-
k
clusters among all observed clusters converges to
w_{k}/\sum_{j\geq 1}w_{j}
under stationarity and ergodicity, in direct analogy with (
2.6
) in the one-dimensional case. Moreover,

\begin{split}\widehat{w}_{k}=\frac{\#\{\text{size-$k$ clusters above $u$}\}}{N^{d}}\quad{\rm and}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\widehat{w}_{j}=\frac{\#\{\text{all clusters above $u$}\}}{N^{d}},\end{split}

(2.13)

are natural empirical estimators of
w_{k}
and
\sum_{j\geq 1}w_{j}
, respectively.
∎

Remark 2.6 [Computation of
w_{k}
.]

For large
k
, the probabilities
w_{k}
in (
2.12
) are difficult to compute analytically, and the denominator
\sum_{j\geq 1}w_{j}
in (
2.11
) is rarely available in closed form. Section
2.3.4
below describes Monte–Carlo estimators for
w_{k}
.

If the random field is isotropic (invariant under rotations), then isotropy can substantially reduce the computational burden. Indeed, the probability
{\mathbb{P}}(X_{D}>u,X_{\mathcal{N}(D)}\leq u)
depends only on the shape of
D
up to rigid motions; all rotated or reflected copies of a given shape contribute the same probability and differ only by their multiplicity. For example, under nearest neighbors in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, the set
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\rm root}
consists of two translates of the same shape, while
\mathcal{C}_{3}^{\rm root}
in (
2.9
) reduces to two geometric types: straight-line shapes and
L
-shapes.
∎

Remark 2.7 [Boundary representation of
\sum_{k\geq 1}w_{k}
.]

Motivated by the one-dimensional formula, we present an alternative representation of
\sum_{k\geq 1}w_{k}
. Let

\begin{split}B=\{t\in\mathcal{N}(o):t<_{\rm lex}o\}=\{-e_{i}:i=1,\ldots,d\},\quad H=\{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{N}(o):t<_{\rm lex}o\}.\end{split}

Because the root
o
is the lexicographically smallest point in its cluster, we can write

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}&={\mathbb{P}}(\text{there exists a cluster above $u$ with root at $o$})\\
&={\mathbb{P}}(X_{o}>u,X_{B}\leq u,\text{and there is no exceedance path from $o$ to $H$})\\
&={\mathbb{P}}(X_{o}>u,X_{B}\leq u)\\
&\quad-{\mathbb{P}}(X_{o}>u,X_{B}\leq u,\text{and $\exists$ exceedance path from $o$ to some $t\in H$})\end{split}

(2.14)

When
d=1
, the last probability in (
2.14
) vanishes (there is no exceedance path from
o
to
-2
), so
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}={\mathbb{P}}(X_{0}>u,X_{-1}\leq u)
. For
d\geq 2
, this correction term involves all possible long-range exceedance paths and is generally difficult to evaluate explicitly.
∎

2.3.3
Clusters containing the origin versus rooted at the origin

For
k=1,2,\ldots
, define

\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm inside}=\{\text{all size-$k$ clusters above $u$ that contain the origin $o$}\}.

That is, the origin lies somewhere in the cluster, but is not necessarily the root. For each
D\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm root}
there are
k
placements of the origin in
D
, and therefore

|\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm inside}|=k|\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm root}|.

(2.15)

Let
w_{k}^{\rm inside}
denote the probability that a size-
k
cluster above
u
contains the origin:

\begin{split}w_{k}^{\rm inside}=\sum_{D\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm inside}}{\mathbb{P}}(X_{D}>u,X_{\mathcal{N}(D)}\leq u).\end{split}

(2.16)

By stationarity and (
2.15
), we have

w_{k}^{\rm inside}=kw_{k}.

(2.17)

Combining (
2.17
) with Theorem
2.3
, we obtain immediately the following formula for the cluster size distribution based on
w_{k}^{\rm inside}
.

Corollary 2.8
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}=k)=\frac{w_{k}^{\rm inside}/k}{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(w_{j}^{\rm inside}/j)},\quad k=1,2,\ldots,\end{split}

where
w_{k}^{\rm inside}
is defined in (
2.16
).

2.3.4
Monte–Carlo estimation of
w_{k}
for large
k

For large cluster sizes the exact probabilities
w_{k}
are both analytically intractable and extremely small, which makes direct estimation difficult. In contrast, the quantities
w_{k}^{\rm inside}=kw_{k}
are larger and thus easier to estimate. Heuristically, by (
2.16
),

w_{k}^{\rm inside}\approx\text{average number of size-$k$ clusters above $u$ that contain the origin $o$}.

This observation leads to a simple Monte-Carlo estimator. Simulate
M
independent realizations of the random field on a finite window containing the origin. For the
m
-th realization, let
I_{m}
be the indicator that there exists a size-
k
cluster containing the origin and not touching the boundary of the simulation window (to avoid edge effects). Then

\widetilde{w}_{k}^{\rm inside}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}I_{m}

is an unbiased estimator of
w_{k}^{\rm inside}
. The corresponding
Monte–Carlo estimator
of
w_{k}
is

\widetilde{w}_{k}=\frac{\widetilde{w}_{k}^{\rm inside}}{k}=\frac{1}{Mk}\sum_{m=1}^{M}I_{m}.

(2.18)

A refined estimator.
Building on this idea, we now introduce a refined Monte-Carlo procedure that extracts more information from each simulated realization. Simulate
M
independent realizations of the field on a finite domain of size
N^{d}
containing the origin (for example,
[-50,50]^{2}
when
d=2
). Within this domain, select a smaller subdomain of size
n^{d}
that also contains the origin (e.g.,
[-25,25]^{2}
when
d=2
).

For a given realization, consider all size-
k
clusters that intersect the
n^{d}
subdomain but do not touch the boundary of the full
N^{d}
domain. If a size-
k
cluster lies entirely inside the subdomain, it is counted as one. If a size-
k
cluster intersects the subdomain in exactly
x
lattice points, it is counted with weight
x/k
. We refer to the quantity
x/k
as the
intersection proportion
. This definition naturally includes the fully contained case, for which the intersection proportion equals one.

Let
J_{m}
denote the total weighted count of size-
k
clusters in the
m
-th realization, where each cluster is weighted by its intersection proportion as described above. The
refined Monte-Carlo estimator
of
w_{k}
is then

\widetilde{w}_{k}=\frac{1}{n^{d}M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}J_{m}.

(2.19)

The estimator in (
2.18
) based on
w_{k}^{\rm inside}
corresponds to the special case in which the
n^{d}
subdomain is reduced to the singleton
\{o\}
, so that
n=1
. In this setting, a size-
k
cluster is observed if and only if it contains the origin, and it is counted with weight
1/k
, which coincides with the intersection proportion. Consequently,
J_{m}=I_{m}/k
, and the estimator in (
2.19
) reduces to that in (
2.18
).

The refined estimator therefore generalizes the origin-based approach by incorporating information from multiple lattice sites within a subdomain of size
n^{d}
. As a result, each simulated realization contributes more effectively to the estimation of
w_{k}
, leading to improved statistical efficiency, particularly when
k
is large and
w_{k}
is small.

3
The peak-based cluster size distribution

When the random field
X_{t}
is nonstationary, the cluster size distribution in (
2.1
) becomes challenging to analyze. This difficulty arises because, in the nonstationary setting, the conditional probability in (
2.1
) depends on the spatial location of the observed cluster
C_{u}
, as the covariance structure of the field varies across the domain. Consequently, a single global cluster size distribution is generally not well defined. A more appropriate formulation is a local, spatially varying cluster size distribution that reflects the heterogeneity in the covariance structure of the field.

A second motivation comes from the fact that every cluster contains at least one local maximum (typically only a few), which can serve as a natural representative of the cluster. Moreover, for high thresholds
u
, clusters tend to be dominated by a single prominent peak. Since peak analysis is itself a mature and active area
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
15
,
17
]
, it is natural and valuable to study the peak-based cluster size distribution from both theoretical and applied perspectives. Specifically, given that a local maximum exceeding
u
is observed at
t
, we consider the distribution of the size of the cluster containing that peak.

Formally, let
C_{u}(t)
denote the cluster above
u
that contains
t
, and let

S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)
be its size, conditional on
t
being a local maximum above
u
. The
peak-based cluster size distribution
is defined by

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=k)={\mathbb{P}}(|C_{u}(t)|=k\ |\ t\ \text{\rm is a local maximum and }X_{t}>u),\quad k=1,2,\ldots\end{split}

(3.1)

In signal detection problems, one is often interested in peaks that are not only high in amplitude but also substantial in spatial extent. This is particularly relevant in neuroimaging applications, where extended activation patterns are more scientifically interpretable than isolated spikes
[
10
,
20
,
23
,
25
]
. A peak-based formulation of the cluster size distribution provides a natural and efficient framework for quantifying such effects and assessing their significance, through
p
-values, when detecting spatially extended signals.

As in the study of exact cluster size distributions, we first present the one-dimensional case, where the structure of clusters and the corresponding computations admit explicit characterization, and then extend the results to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

3.1
Discrete processes on
{\mathbb{Z}}

Let
\{X_{t}:t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
be a discrete process on
{\mathbb{Z}}
, and fix a threshold
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The excursion set
\{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}:X_{t}>u\}
decomposes into clusters (connected components). Conditional on a local maximum
t
with
X_{t}>u
, the associated cluster
C_{u}(t)
consists of all connected exceedance sites connected to
t
.

For
k=1
, the cluster
C_{u}(t)
is the singleton
\{t\}
, which occurs precisely when both neighbors of
t
fall below
u
. Hence

{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=1)={\mathbb{P}}(X_{t-1}\leq u,X_{t+1}\leq u\ |\ X_{t}>u,X_{t}>\max\{X_{t-1},X_{t+1}\}).

(3.2)

For
k=2
, the cluster
C_{u}(t)
must be either
\{t,t+1\}
or
\{t-1,t\}
, so

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=2)&={\mathbb{P}}(X_{t-1}\leq u,X_{t+1}>u,X_{t+2}\leq u\ |\ X_{t}>u,X_{t}>\max\{X_{t-1},X_{t+1}\})\\
&+{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t-2}\leq u,X_{t-1}>u,X_{t+1}\leq u\ |\ X_{t}>u,X_{t}>\max\{X_{t-1},X_{t+1}\}).\end{split}

(3.3)

If
X_{t}
is stationary, then by symmetry the two probabilities on the right side of (
3.3
) coincide.

More generally, for a size-
k
cluster containing
t
, the peak site
t
partitions the cluster into a left part and a right part. Since
t
itself exceeds
u
, the number of exceedance sites to the right of
t
, denoted
|C_{u}^{\rm right}(t)|
, can range from
0
to
k-1
; the number to the left is then
|C_{u}^{\rm left}(t)|=k-1-|C_{u}^{\rm right}(t)|
. By (
3.1
), for
k\geq 1
,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=k)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{\mathbb{P}}(|C_{u}^{\rm right}(t)|=j,|C_{u}^{\rm left}(t)|=k-1-j\ |\ t\ \text{\rm is local max and }X_{t}>u).\end{split}

The event
\{|C_{u}^{\rm right}(t)|=j,\ |C_{u}^{\rm left}(t)|=k-1-j\}
is equivalent to

X_{t-k+j}\leq u,\ X_{t-k+j+1}>u,\ \ldots,\ X_{t+j}>u,\ X_{t+j+1}\leq u.

Therefore,

\begin{split}&\quad{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=k)\\
&=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t-k+j}\leq u,X_{t-k+j+1}>u,\ldots,X_{t+j}>u,X_{t+j+1}\leq u,X_{t}>\max\{X_{t-1},X_{t+1}\})}{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}>u,X_{t}>\max\{X_{t-1},X_{t+1}\})}.\end{split}

(3.4)

In the stationary case, the contributions corresponding to
j
and
k-1-j
are equal by symmetry.

Example 3.1

Let
X_{t}
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, be a white noise process with common continuous CDF
F
. Let
p=F(u)
and
q=1-p
. Note that, if
Y_{1},Y_{2}
and
Y_{3}
are i.i.d. with CDF
F
and density
f
, then

{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{1}>Y_{2},Y_{1}>Y_{3}\,|\,Y_{1}=x)={\mathbb{P}}(Y_{2}<x)^{2}=F(x)^{2},

so

{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{1}>u,Y_{1}>Y_{2},Y_{1}>Y_{3})=\int_{u}^{\infty}f(x)F(x)^{2}dx=\frac{1-F(u)^{3}}{3}=\frac{1-p^{3}}{3}.

Similarly,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{1}>Y_{2}>u)&=\int_{u}^{\infty}f(x)(1-F(x))dx=\frac{(1-F(u))^{2}}{2}=\frac{q^{2}}{2},\\
{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{1}>Y_{2}>u,Y_{1}>Y_{3}>u)&=\int_{u}^{\infty}f(x)(F(x)-F(u))^{2}dx=\frac{(1-F(u))^{3}}{3}=\frac{q^{3}}{3}.\end{split}

It follows from (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) that

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=1)&=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t-1}\leq u,X_{t}>u,X_{t+1}\leq u)}{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}>u,X_{t}>\max\{X_{t-1},X_{t+1}\})}=\frac{3p^{2}q}{1-p^{3}},\\
{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=2)&=\frac{2{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t-1}\leq u,X_{t}>X_{t+1}>u,X_{t+2}\leq u)}{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}>u,X_{t}>\max\{X_{t-1},X_{t+1}\})}=\frac{3p^{2}q^{2}}{1-p^{3}}.\end{split}

Similarly,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=3)&=\frac{2{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t-1}\leq u,X_{t}>X_{t+1}>u,X_{t+2}>u,X_{t+3}\leq u))}{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}>u,X_{t}>\max\{X_{t-1},X_{t+1}\})}\\
&\quad+\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t-2}\leq u,X_{t}>X_{t-1}>u,X_{t}>X_{t+1}>u,X_{t+2}\leq u)}{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}>u,X_{t}>\max\{X_{t-1},X_{t+1}\})}\\
&=\frac{p^{2}q^{3}+p^{2}q^{3}/3}{(1-p^{3})/3}=\frac{4p^{2}q^{3}}{1-p^{3}}.\end{split}

In general, for all
k\geq 1
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=k)=\begin{cases}\frac{3p^{2}q}{1-p^{3}},\quad&k=1,\\
\frac{(k+1)p^{2}q^{k}}{1-p^{3}},\quad&k\geq 2.\end{cases}

(3.5)

This gives the exact peak-based cluster size distribution for the one-dimensional i.i.d. case.
∎

3.2
Discrete random fields on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

Now we consider a random field
\{X_{t}:t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\}
on the
d
-dimensional lattice. For
k\geq 1
and a fixed site
t
, define

\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)=\{\text{size-$k$ clusters above $u$ that contain $t$ as a local maximum}\}.

(3.6)

This is a natural analogue to
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm inside}
, with the origin replaced by
t
. For example, in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
under nearest neighbors,
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\rm peak}(t)=\{t\}
,
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\rm peak}(t)
consists of four sets

\{t,t+e_{1}\},\ \{t,t-e_{1}\},\ \{t,t+e_{2}\},\ \{t,t-e_{2}\},

and
\mathcal{C}_{3}^{\rm peak}(t)
consists of 18 sets in total. In general,

|\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)|=|\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm inside}|=k\,|\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm root}|,

since each rooted cluster of size
k
has
k
possible locations for
t
. Under Moore neighbors,
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)
contains additional shapes; for example, when
k=2
, it also includes four diagonal pairs that connect
t
to diagonal neighbors.

Recall that
\mathcal{N}(D)
in (
2.10
) denotes the exterior neighbor set of
D
. We now state the peak-based cluster size distribution on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\{X_{t}:t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\}
be a random field on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and let
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
be a fixed threshold. Then, for
k=1,2,\ldots
,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=k)=\frac{w_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)}{{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{t}>u,X_{t}>\max_{s\in\mathcal{N}(t)}X_{s}\right)},\end{split}

(3.7)

where
w_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)
denotes the probability that
t
is a local maximum above
u
whose associated cluster has size
k
, i.e.,

\begin{split}w_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)=\sum_{D\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)}{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{D}>u,X_{\mathcal{N}(D)}\leq u,X_{t}>\max_{s\in\mathcal{N}(t)}X_{s}\right).\end{split}

(3.8)

Proof.

The conditional event in (
3.1
) can be written as

{\mathbb{P}}(t\ \text{\rm is a local maximum and }X_{t}>u)={\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{t}>u,X_{t}>\max_{s\in\mathcal{N}(t)}X_{s}\right).

Given that
t
is a local maximum above
u
, the cluster
C_{u}(t)
has size
k
if and only if the set of exceedance sites containing
t
coincides with one of the shapes in
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)
. Thus, by (
3.1
), for
k=1,2,\ldots
,

\begin{split}&\quad{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=k)\\
&=\sum_{D\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)}{\mathbb{P}}(X_{D}>u,X_{\mathcal{N}(D)}\leq u\ |\ t\ \text{\rm is a local maximum and }X_{t}>u)\\
&=\frac{\sum_{D\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)}{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{D}>u,X_{\mathcal{N}(D)}\leq u,X_{t}>\max_{s\in\mathcal{N}(t)}X_{s}\right)}{{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{t}>u,X_{t}>\max_{s\in\mathcal{N}(t)}X_{s}\right)},\end{split}

where we have used the fact that
X_{D}>u
implies
X_{t}>u
since
t
belongs to all
D\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)
. Identifying the numerator with
w_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)
in (
3.8
) yields (
3.7
).
∎

Theorem
3.2
holds for general random fields on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
; no stationarity assumption is required. Moreover, the denominator in (
3.7
) involves only the joint distribution of
\{X_{s}:s\in\{t\}\cup\mathcal{N}(t)\}
and is easy to compute explicitly, making it feasible to obtain closed-form expressions for
{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=k)
for moderate
k
. In contrast, for the exact cluster size distribution (
2.11
), the normalizing constant
\sum_{j\geq 1}w_{j}
generally involves global connectivity effects and often requires numerical approximation.

Example 3.3

Let
X_{t}
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, be a two-dimensional white noise field with common continuous CDF
F
. Let
p=F(u)
and
q=1-p
. We first consider nearest neighbors. By analyzing the shapes in
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)
and applying arguments similar to Example
3.1
, one finds that the denominator in (
3.7
) equals
(1-p^{5})/5
, and

\begin{split}w_{1}^{\rm peak}(t)=p^{4}q,\quad w_{2}^{\rm peak}(t)=2p^{6}q^{2},\quad w_{3}^{\rm peak}(t)=\frac{8}{3}(2p^{7}+p^{8})q^{3}.\end{split}

Taking the ratio yields the following probabilities:

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=1)&=\frac{5p^{4}q}{1-p^{5}},\quad{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=2)=\frac{10p^{6}q^{2}}{1-p^{5}},\\
{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=3)&=\frac{40(2p^{7}+p^{8})q^{3}}{3(1-p^{5})}.\end{split}

Under Moore neighbors, the denominator in (
3.7
) is
(1-p^{9})/9
, and

\begin{split}w_{1}^{\rm peak}(t)=p^{8}q,\quad w_{2}^{\rm peak}(t)=2(p^{10}+p^{12})q^{2},\quad w_{3}^{\rm peak}(t)=\frac{4}{3}(5p^{12}+8p^{14}+4p^{15}+2p^{16})q^{3}.\end{split}

Therefore,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=1)&=\frac{9p^{8}q}{1-p^{9}},\quad{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=2)=\frac{18(p^{10}+p^{12})q^{2}}{1-p^{9}},\\
{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=3)&=\frac{12(5p^{12}+8p^{14}+4p^{15}+2p^{16})q^{3}}{1-p^{9}}.\end{split}

As
k
increases, the combinatorial complexity of
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)
grows quickly, so it is difficult to obtain closed-form expressions for
w_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)
. Nevertheless,
w_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)
can be evaluated numerically by enumerating cluster shapes or approximated via Monte-Carlo methods.
∎

Remark 3.4 [An alternative representation.]

By definition (
3.8
),
\sum_{j\geq 1}w_{j}^{\rm peak}(t)
is the probability that
t
is a local maximum above
u
whose associated cluster has some finite size:

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{j}^{\rm peak}(t)={\mathbb{P}}(t\ \text{\rm is a local maximum and }X_{t}>u).

Thus (
3.7
) can equivalently be written as

{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)=k)=\frac{w_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)}{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{j}^{\rm peak}(t)}.

(3.9)

∎

Remark 3.5 [Empirical peak-based distribution for nonstationary fields.]

We now verify that (
3.7
) is consistent with the empirical peak-based cluster size distribution obtained from simulations. Suppose we simulate
M
independent realizations of the nonstationary random field. For a fixed site
t
, the empirical probability mass function is

\begin{split}&\quad\frac{\#\{\text{realizations where $t$ is a peak above $u$ with associated cluster of size $k$}\}}{\#\{\text{realizations where $t$ is a peak above $u$}\}}\\
&=\frac{\#\{\text{realizations where $t$ is a peak above $u$ with associated cluster of size $k$}\}/M}{\#\{\text{realizations where $t$ is a peak above $u$}\}/M}\\
&\overset{a.s.}{\to}\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(\text{$t$ is a peak above $u$ with associated cluster of size $k$})}{{\mathbb{P}}(\text{$t$ is a peak above $u$})}\\
&=\frac{w_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)}{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{j}^{\rm peak}(t)},\qquad\text{as }M\to\infty,\end{split}

where the convergence follows from the strong law of large numbers. Thus, as the number of simulations increases, empirical relative frequencies converge to the theoretical peak-based cluster size distribution.
∎

Remark 3.6 [Empirical peak-based distribution for stationary fields.]

If the random field
X_{t}
is stationary, then
w_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)
and
S_{u}^{\rm peak}(t)
do not depend on
t
, so we simply write
w_{k}^{\rm peak}
and
S_{u}^{\rm peak}
. In this case,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}=k)=\frac{w_{k}^{\rm peak}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{j}^{\rm peak}},\quad k=1,2,\ldots.\end{split}

If the observation domain contains
N^{d}
lattice points, then
N^{d}w_{k}^{\rm peak}
is the expected number of local maxima above
u
whose associated clusters have size
k
. A natural empirical estimator of
w_{k}^{\rm peak}
is

\widehat{w}_{k}^{\rm peak}=\frac{\#\{\text{local maxima above $u$ with associated cluster of size $k$}\}}{N^{d}}.

Similarly,
N^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{j}^{\rm peak}
is the expected total number of local maxima above
u
. Therefore, we have the following empirical relation:

\begin{split}&\quad\frac{\#\{\text{local maxima above $u$ with associated cluster of size $k$}\}}{\#\{\text{local maxima above $u$}\}}\\
&=\frac{\#\{\text{local maxima above $u$ with associated cluster of size $k$}\}/N^{d}}{\#\{\text{local maxima above $u$}\}/N^{d}}\\
&=\frac{\widehat{w}_{k}^{\rm peak}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\widehat{w}_{j}^{\rm peak}}\overset{a.s.}{\to}\frac{w_{k}^{\rm peak}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{j}^{\rm peak}},\qquad\text{as }N\to\infty,\end{split}

where the convergence follows from the strong law of large numbers under stationarity and ergodicity.
∎

Remark 3.7 [Monte-Carlo estimation of
w_{k}^{\rm peak}
for stationary fields.]

For large
k
, the quantities
w_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)
are typically extremely small and difficult to compute exactly. Direct estimation is usually inefficient, as it would require simulating realizations where the site
t
is a local maximum above the threshold
u
, an event that occurs with very low probability. We restrict attention here to stationary random fields, where
w_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)
does not depend on the location
t
and can be written simply as
w_{k}^{\rm peak}
.

We propose a Monte-Carlo estimator analogous to the refined procedure in Section
2.3.4
. Simulate
M
independent realizations of the field on a domain of size
N^{d}
, and select an interior subdomain of size
n^{d}
. For the
m
-th realization, let
L_{m}
denote the number of local maxima above
u
that fall within the
n^{d}
subdomain, whose associated clusters have size
k
and do not touch the boundary of the full
N^{d}
domain (to avoid boundary effects). Then the
Monte-Carlo estimator
of
w_{k}^{\rm peak}
is

\widetilde{w}_{k}^{\rm peak}=\frac{1}{n^{d}M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}L_{m}.

(3.10)

By aggregating peak events across the
n^{d}
interior sites, each realization contributes substantially more information than a single-site estimator, yielding improved statistical efficiency, especially when
k
is large and
w_{k}^{\rm peak}
is small.
∎

Remark 3.8 [High thresholds.]

When the threshold
u
is high, then each cluster typically contains a unique local maximum with probability close to one. For a stationary random field and large
u
, the peak-based and exact cluster size distributions therefore nearly coincide:

{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\rm peak}=k)\approx{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}=k),\quad k=1,2,\ldots.

Furthermore, at high thresholds, large clusters are rarely observed, so both
w_{k}
and
w_{k}^{\rm peak}
are negligible for large
k
. As a result, the computation of both the exact and peak-based cluster size distributions is substantially simplified.
∎

Numerical Results and Simulations

We present numerical and simulation results that illustrate the practical performance of the proposed methods for computing the cluster size distribution and the associated quantities
w_{k}
. For each example (except the nonstationary case where only peak-based distributions are available), we report both theoretical cluster size distributions, including exact and peak-based formulations, and empirical counterparts obtained from simulated random fields.

As discussed earlier, for general random fields, particularly in dimensions
d\geq 2
, explicit expressions for
w_{k}
,
w_{k}^{\rm peak}
,
{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}=k)
, and
{\mathbb{P}}(S_{u}^{\text{peak}}=k)
are typically unavailable. Consequently, theoretical values reported below were evaluated numerically or by Monte–Carlo methods except where explicit formulas exist and are indicated.

In the tables below, we report up to the first ten values of
w_{k}
and
w_{k}^{\text{peak}}
, together with their empirical estimates
\widehat{w}_{k}
and
\widehat{w}_{k}^{\text{peak}}
, all rounded to five decimal places. For completeness, we also report the corresponding partial sums
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}
,
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}^{\text{peak}}
,
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widehat{w}_{k}
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widehat{w}_{k}^{\text{peak}}
over the computed range. Normalizing by these sums yields the cluster size probability mass functions. Specifically, the ratios

4.1
Gaussian processes on
\mathbb{Z}
.

In one dimension we consider three types of Gaussian processes: (i) Gaussian white noise, (ii) stationary correlated Gaussian processes, and (iii) a nonstationary Gaussian process. Results are summarized in Tables
1
–
3
. In all experiments we simulated
M_{1}=10{,}000
independent realizations of the process on an integer lattice of length
N_{1}=1{,}500
.

4.1.1
Stationary Gaussian processes on
\mathbb{Z}

Let
\Phi
denote the standard normal CDF, and let
p=\Phi(u)
and
q=1-p
. For Gaussian white noise (Example
2.1
), the theoretical per-site probabilities are
w_{k}=p^{2}q^{k}
for
k\geq 1
, hence
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}=pq
and the cluster size distribution is geometric as in (
2.3
). The peak-based analytic results of Example
3.1
give

w_{1}^{\rm peak}=p^{2}q,\quad w_{k}^{\rm peak}=(k+1)p^{2}q^{k}/3\text{ for }k\geq 2,

so
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}^{\rm peak}=(1-p^{3})/3
and the peak-based cluster size distribution in (
3.5
) follows.

For a stationary correlated Gaussian process with covariance
C(t,s)=e^{-(t-s)^{2}}
, theoretical
w_{k}
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}
were computed from (
2.4
) and (
2.5
); theoretical
w_{k}^{\text{peak}}
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}^{\text{peak}}
were obtained from the numerator and denominator of (
3.4
), respectively. The empirical estimators used in simulations are

\begin{split}\widehat{w}_{k}&=\frac{\#\{\text{size-$k$ clusters above $u$}\}}{M_{1}N_{1}},\quad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widehat{w}_{k}=\frac{\#\{\text{all clusters above $u$}\}}{M_{1}N_{1}},\end{split}

(4.1)

and for peak-based quantities

\begin{split}\widehat{w}_{k}^{\rm peak}&=\frac{\#\{\text{local maxima above $u$ with associated cluster of size $k$}\}}{M_{1}N_{1}},\\
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widehat{w}_{k}^{\rm peak}&=\frac{\#\{\text{local maxima above $u$}\}}{M_{1}N_{1}}.\end{split}

(4.2)

Tables
1
and
2
report theoretical and empirical values (with normalized probabilities in parentheses) and display histogram comparisons. In both examples the empirical distributions closely match theory. Moreover, as the threshold increases from
u=0.5
to
u=1.5
, the peak-based cluster size distribution approach the exact cluster size distribution, confirming the high-threshold approximation discussed in Remark
3.2
.

4.1.2
Nonstationary Gaussian processes on
\mathbb{Z}

In the nonstationary setting, only the peak-based cluster size distributions are available, and the peak-based quantities
w_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)
generally depend on the spatial location
t
. For simplicity, we focus at a fixed reference point
t_{0}=0
, and write
w_{k}^{\rm peak}(t_{0})=w_{k}^{\rm peak}
.

To obtain robust empirical estimates with repeated structure, we increase the number of local maxima that share the same local behavior at 0 by introducing a periodic mean function. Specifically, we consider

Y_{t}=X_{t}+\cos(\pi t),\quad t\in{\mathbb{Z}},

where
X_{t}
is a centered stationary Gaussian process with covariance
C(t,s)=e^{-(t-s)^{2}}
. Due to stationarity of
X_{t}
and periodicity of
\cos(\pi t)
, the local behaviors of
Y_{t}
at sites
t\in D_{0}=\{0,\pm 2,\pm 4,\ldots\}
(within the simulation domain) are identical. Consequently, the peak-based cluster size distributions coincide at these locations.

Theoretical values of
w_{k}^{\text{peak}}
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}^{\text{peak}}
are obtained from the numerator and denominator of (
3.4
), respectively. Empirical estimators exploit the repeated sites in
D_{0}
:

\begin{split}\widehat{w}_{k}^{\rm peak}(t)&=\frac{\#\{\text{local maxima at $t\in D_{0}$ above $u$ with associated cluster of size $k$}\}}{M_{1}|D_{0}|},\\
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widehat{w}_{k}^{\rm peak}&=\frac{\#\{\text{local maxima at $t\in D_{0}$ above $u$}\}}{M_{1}|D_{0}|},\end{split}

where, for our experiments,
|D_{0}|\approx N_{1}/2
.

Table
3
shows theoretical and empirical values of
w_{k}^{\rm peak}
and
\widehat{w}_{k}^{\rm peak}
, together with normalized probability masses and histogram comparisons. For both threshold levels
u=0.5
and
u=1.5
, the empirical exact peak-based cluster size distributions agree well with theoretical values.

4.2
Gaussian and chi-squared fields on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

On
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
we consider four model classes: (i) stationary correlated Gaussian fields under nearest neighbors, (ii) the same field under Moore neighbors, (iii) Gaussian white noise under Moore neighbors, and (iv) standardized chi-squared fields under Moore neighbors. Results are illustrated in Tables
4
–
7
.

In general, theoretical
w_{k}
are computed from (
2.12
), while theoretical
w_{k}^{\text{peak}}
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}^{\text{peak}}
follow from (
3.8
) and the denominator of (
3.7
), respectively. For Gaussian white noise (Table
6
), these quantities admit closed-form expressions, as derived in Examples
2.3.2
and
3.2
.

Because exact numerical evaluation becomes costly for large cluster sizes, we compute theoretical values exactly for
k\leq 6
and estimate larger
k
by Monte–Carlo. Concretely, for
k\geq 7
we use the refined estimator (
2.19
) to estimate
w_{k}
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}
, and the Monte–Carlo procedure of Remark
3.2
to estimate
w_{k}^{\text{peak}}
. These Monte-Carlo values were produced from
15{,}000
independent realizations of the field on a
100\times 100
integer lattice, with inference restricted to the interior
50\times 50
subdomain to mitigate boundary bias.

Since simulating correlated random fields on large lattices with a prescribed covariance structure can be computationally demanding, we adopt an alternative strategy to facilitate empirical estimation using multiple moderately sized independent realizations. Specifically, we simulate
M_{2}=2{,}000
independent realizations of the field on an integer lattice of size
N_{2}^{2}=300\times 300
. The empirical estimators of
w_{k}
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}
are provided in (
4.1
), with
M_{1}
and
N_{1}
replaced by
M_{2}
and
N_{2}^{2}
, respectively. Similarly, the empirical estimators for the peak-based quantities are given by (
4.2
), with the same substitutions.

Tables
4
and
5
summarize numerical and simulation results for correlated Gaussian fields on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
with covariance
C(t,s)=e^{-\|t-s\|^{2}}
, under nearest and Moore neighbors. The results show that, when moving from nearest to Moore neighbors, as well as from one-dimensional to two-dimensional settings, small clusters occur less frequently while larger clusters become more prevalent. This behavior is a direct consequence of the increased degree of connectivity, which promotes the formation of larger connected components.

In Table
7
, the standardized chi-squared field under consideration is
Z_{t}=(X_{t}^{2}+Y_{t}^{2})/2-1
, where
X
and
Y
are independent Gaussian fields with covariance
C(t,s)=e^{-\|t-s\|^{2}}
. Alternatively, one may take
X
and
Y
to be independent Gaussian white noise; in this case, the analytic formulas derived in Examples
2.3.2
and
3.2
apply directly.

Across all four examples presented in Tables
4
–
7
, the empirical exact and peak-based distributions closely match their theoretical counterparts. As the threshold increases from
u=0.5
to
u=1.5
, the peak-based cluster size distribution converges toward the exact distribution, consistent with the high-threshold approximation described in Remark
3.2
.
