On the shape of the connected components of the complement of two-dimensional Brownian random interlacements
Orphée Collin and Serguei Popov

Abstract
We study the limiting shape of the connected components of the vacant set of two-dimensional Brownian random interlacements: we prove that the connected component around x x is close in distribution to a rescaled Brownian amoeba in the regime when the distance from x ∈ ℂ x\in{\mathbb{C}} to the closest trajectory is small (which, in particular, includes the cases x → ∞ x\to\infty with fixed intensity parameter α \alpha , and α → ∞ \alpha\to\infty with fixed x x ). We also obtain a new family of martingales built on the conditioned Brownian motion, which may be of independent interest.

Introduction

In this paper, we study the model of Brownian random interlacements
in two dimensions. The discrete random interlacements
in higher dimensions are “canonical
Poissonian soups” of doubly infinite
simple random walk’s trajectories
(or “loops passing through infinity”);
they were introduced
by Sznitman in
[
15
]
and then studied quite extensively (see, e.g.
[
5
]
). That original construction
cannot be used in low dimensions where a simple random walk’s trajectory
is recurrent so that even a single trajectory
will a.s. fill the whole space;
one can, however, construct a similar object in a natural
way using the
conditioned
(on avoiding the origin) trajectories.
This was done in
[
4
]
in two dimensions and in
[
1
]

in one dimension. In all dimensions, random interlacements are related to (simple) random walks
on tori: the traces left by the random walk up to a
certain time on a box (with a size much smaller than that of the torus) can be well approximated by the random
interlacements restricted to that box (in dimensions
1
and
2
one also needs to condition the center of the
box to be not visited by the walk).

It is also natural to consider a continuous space/time counterpart
of the above-mentioned models:
namely, the “interlacement soup”
made of Brownian trajectories
(which, in the case of lower dimensions,
also needs some conditioning). This was done in
[
16
]

for the original random interlacement model and in
[
3
]

for the two-dimensional case.

One of the main objects of interest in the context of random interlacements
is
the vacant set
, which is the complement
of the union of the trajectories that make up
the interlacements.
This article is part of a program focused on studying the geometric properties of the vacant set
of the two-dimensional random interlacements
(discrete and continuous), as well as the vacant
set of a single-conditioned trajectory.
Note that in the two-dimensional case,
the vacant set of Brownian interlacements
only has bounded connected
components; the paper
[
3
]
contains some
results mainly related to the (linear) size
of these components. In particular, it was shown there
(see also
[
2
]
) that the vacant set
contains an infinite number of nonoverlapping disks
of constant radius whenever
\alpha\leq 1
,
where
\alpha
is the intensity parameter of the model.
In addition, the radius of the largest
disk around
x
, which is fully contained in the vacant
set is
\exp(-Z\alpha\ln^{2}|x|)
, where
Z
is
a random variable with some (explicit)
distribution.
In Theorem
2.4
below, we extend
that last result by studying not only the distance
to the closest trajectory but also
to the second closest one, and so on,
but the main focus of this paper is to
study the geometric shapes of the connected
components of the vacant set. We mention
that the connected components of the complement
of
one
The Brownian trajectory was studied
in a number of papers, notably (in chronological order)

[
12
,
8
,
17
,
6
]
; in particular,
the limiting shape was the main subject of
[
17
]
.
It is interesting to note that, despite the fact that we are dealing
with many trajectories here, in some regimes
it is still typically only one
trajectory that defines the shape
of a given connected component,
cf. Theorem
2.6
below.
Then, in Theorem
2.7
we also consider
a situation when the shape of the connected component
is typically determined by many trajectories:
in the limit
\alpha\to\infty
,
we prove some geometric properties of the “central
cell” (that is, the connected component which contains
the unit disk centered at the origin, which the trajectories are conditioned not to touch).

Another contribution of this paper we have to mention
is the following: in Section
3.1
,
we present a family of functions
which, when applied to the conditioned Brownian
motion, result in (local) martingales
(see Proposition
3.4
below).
That can be seen as a continuous counterpart
of Proposition 2.4 of
[
13
]

or Proposition 4.10 of
[
14
]
,
where the corresponding families for the simple conditioned two-dimensional random walk
were discussed. It is generally very useful
to be able to construct such martingales since it allows, e.g., estimating hitting probabilities
of various sets via the optional stopping theorem;
therefore, we hope that Proposition
3.4

will find its further applications.

Some auxiliary facts

First, we recall a basic fact about hitting
circles centered at the origin by the conditioned
Brownian motion.
Since
\tfrac{1}{\ln|\widehat{W}_{t}|}

is a local martingale, the optional stopping theorem implies
that for any
1<a<|x|<b<\infty

{\mathbb{P}}_{x}[\widehat{\tau}(b)<\widehat{\tau}(a)]=\frac{(\ln a)^{-1}-(\ln|x|)^{-1}}{(\ln a)^{-1}-(\ln b)^{-1}}=\frac{\ln(|x|/a)\times\ln b}{\ln(b/a)\times\ln|x|}.

(18)

Sending
b
to infinity in (
18
)
we also obtain that for
1\leq a\leq|x|

{\mathbb{P}}_{x}[\widehat{\tau}(a)=\infty]=1-\frac{\ln a}{\ln|x|}.

(19)

Also, in the following,
we will need to refer to the fact that

\ln|x|=\ln|y|+\ln\big(1+\tfrac{|x|-|y|}{|y|}\big)=\ln|y|+O\big(\tfrac{|x-y|}{|y|}\big).

(20)

We denote by
\widehat{W}^{x,r}
the Brownian motion
conditioned on not hitting
{\mathsf{B}}(x,r)
;
it is straightforward to check that it can be obtained
from the Brownian motion conditioned by “canonical” conditioned Brownian motion
\widehat{W}
via the linear space-time transformation,

\widehat{W}^{x,r}_{t}=x+\widehat{W}_{r^{2}t}.

Then, we need a fact about
the conditional entrance measure of a Brownian motion
(we need to state and prove it here because
the second statement of Lemma 3.3 of
[
3
]

is incorrect,
a logarithmic factor is missing there):

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
0<2r<|x|<R
, and let
\nu_{x}^{r,R}
be
the conditional
entrance measure of the Brownian motion started at
x
to
{\mathsf{B}}(r)
,
given that
\tau(r)<\tau(R)
.
Then, we have (abbreviating
s:=|x|
)

\Big|\frac{d\nu_{x}^{r,R}}{d\mathop{\mathrm{hm}}\nolimits_{{\mathsf{B}}(r)}}-1\Big|=O\Big(\frac{r\ln\frac{R}{r}}{s\ln(1+\frac{R}{s})}\Big)

(21)

(here,
\mathop{\mathrm{hm}}\nolimits_{{\mathsf{B}}(r)}
is the harmonic measure on
{\mathsf{B}}(r)
,
which is also uniform on
\partial{\mathsf{B}}(r)
).

Proof.

Without restricting generality, one can assume that

R-s\geq s/2
(otherwise, one can just condition the
first entry point to
\partial{\mathsf{B}}\big(\tfrac{2}{3}s\big)
).
Denote also by
\hat{\nu}
the conditional
entrance measure of the Brownian motion
started at
x
to
\partial{\mathsf{B}}(R)
,
given that
\tau(R)<\tau(r)
.
Consider
M\subset\partial{\mathsf{B}}(r)
and note first
that, for
y
with
|y|\geq 2r

{\mathbb{P}}_{y}\big[W_{\tau(r)}\in M\big]=\mathop{\mathrm{hm}}\nolimits_{{\mathsf{B}}(r)}(M)\big(1+O\big(\tfrac{r}{|y|}\big)\big);

(22)

this is a well-known fact that can be obtained
from the explicit formula for the (unconditional)
entrance measure to a disk from outside; see, e.g. Theorem 3.44 of
[
11
]
or (
24
)
below.
Then, we write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[W_{\tau(r)}\in M,\tau(r)<\tau(R)\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[W_{\tau(r)}\in M\big]-{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[W_{\tau(r)}\in M,\tau(R)<\tau(r)\big]

(by (
22
) and conditioning on the
entrance point to
\partial{\mathsf{B}}(R)
)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{hm}}\nolimits_{{\mathsf{B}}(r)}(M)\big(1+O\big(\tfrac{r}{s}\big)\big)-{\mathbb{P}}_{x}[\tau(R)<\tau(r)]\int_{\partial{\mathsf{B}}(R)}{\mathbb{P}}_{z}\big[W_{\tau(r)}\in M\big]\,d{\hat{\nu}}(z)

(again by (
22
))

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{hm}}\nolimits_{{\mathsf{B}}(r)}(M)\big(1+O\big(\tfrac{r}{s}\big)\big)-{\mathbb{P}}_{x}[\tau(R)<\tau(r)]\mathop{\mathrm{hm}}\nolimits_{{\mathsf{B}}(r)}(M)\big(1+O\big(\tfrac{r}{R}\big)\big)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{hm}}\nolimits_{{\mathsf{B}}(r)}(M)\big({\mathbb{P}}_{x}[\tau(r)<\tau(R)]+O\big(\tfrac{r}{s}\big)\big).

We then divide the above by

{\mathbb{P}}_{x}[\tau(r)<\tau(R)]=\frac{\ln\frac{R}{s}}{\ln\frac{R}{r}}

to arrive to (
21
)
(recall the remark at the beginning of the proof).
∎

3.1
Conditioned Brownian motion: martingales
and hitting probabilities

Now, we will need some facts about hitting probabilities
for conditioned Brownian motions; however, to be able to
estimate these probabilities,
we first develop a method which is “cleaner” than
the one used, e.g. in Lemma 3.7 of
[
3
]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that, for some open set
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

such that
\Lambda\cap{\mathsf{B}}(1)=\emptyset
,
a function
g:\Lambda\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
is harmonic.
Then
g(\widehat{W})/\ln|\widehat{W}|
is a local martingale.

Proof.

Let
x\in\Lambda
and consider any bounded closed subset
G

of
\Lambda
that contains
x
in its interior.
Let
\tau
be the hitting time of
\Lambda\setminus G

and let
t>0
.
Now, recall (
1
) and write
(note that none of the trajectories up to time
t\wedge\tau

can touch the unit disk and therefore be killed)

{\mathbb{E}}_{x}\frac{g(\widehat{W}_{t\wedge\tau})}{\ln|\widehat{W}_{t\wedge\tau}|}=\frac{1}{\ln|x|}{\mathbb{E}}_{x}g(W_{t\wedge\tau})=\frac{g(x)}{\ln|x|}

since
g(W)
is a local martingale, so (since
\widehat{W}
is also Markovian) we obtain that
\frac{g(\widehat{W})}{\ln|\widehat{W}|}
is a local martingale.
∎

Next, we know
(see e.g. Theorem 3.44 of
[
11
]
)
that for
x\notin{\mathsf{B}}(1)
and
z\in\partial{\mathsf{B}}(1)

H(x,z)=\frac{|x|^{2}-1}{2\pi|z-x|^{2}}

(24)

is the Poisson kernel on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus{\mathsf{B}}(1)
,
i.e., the density of the entrance measure
to
{\mathsf{B}}(1)
when starting at
x
.
Define for
x,y\notin{\mathsf{B}}(1)
,
x\neq y
,

\displaystyle L(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ln|x|}\bigg(\ln|x|-\ln|x-y|+\int_{\partial{\mathsf{B}}(1)}\ln|z-y|H(x,z)\,dz\bigg)

(25)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{\ln|x|}\bigg(-\ln|x-y|+\frac{|x|^{2}-1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial{\mathsf{B}}(1)}\frac{\ln|z-y|}{|z-x|^{2}}\,dz\bigg),

(26)

Writing
x=x_{0}(1+\delta)
with
x_{0}\in\partial{\mathsf{B}}(1)
and
\delta>0
, this definition can be rewritten as

\displaystyle L(x,y)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{\ln|x|}\bigg(-\ln\frac{|x-y|}{|x_{0}-y|}+\frac{|x|^{2}-1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial{\mathsf{B}}(1)}\frac{\ln\frac{|z-y|}{|x_{0}-y|}}{|z-x|^{2}}\,dz\bigg)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{\ln|x|}\Bigg(-\ln\frac{|x-y|}{|x_{0}-y|}+\frac{|x|^{2}-1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\ln\frac{|z^{(\varphi)}-y||z^{(-\varphi)}-y|}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}}{|z^{(\varphi)}-x|^{2}}\,d\varphi\Bigg),

(27)

where

z^{(\varphi)}=x_{0}e^{i\varphi}
.
To cover the case
\delta=0
,
define for
x_{0}\in\partial{\mathsf{B}}(1),y\notin{\mathsf{B}}(1)
(denoting by
(\cdot,\cdot)
the scalar product in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
),

\displaystyle L(x_{0},y)

\displaystyle=\frac{(y-x_{0},y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\ln\frac{|z^{(\varphi)}-y||z^{(-\varphi)}-y|}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}}{|z^{(\varphi)}-x_{0}|^{2}}\,d\varphi.

(28)

Then, partly as a consequence of Lemma
3.3
,
we have

Proposition 3.4
.

For any fixed
y\notin{\mathsf{B}}(1)
, it holds that

(i)

the function

\big({\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus({\mathsf{B}}(1)\cup\{y\})\big)\cup\partial{\mathsf{B}}(1)\to{\mathbb{R}},x\mapsto L(x,y)
is continuous;

(ii)

the process
L(\widehat{W}_{t},y)
is a local martingale;

(iii)

L(x,y)\to 0
as
x\to\infty
;

(iv)

for any fixed
r>0
,
L(x,y)

is uniformly bounded in
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus({\mathsf{B}}(1)\cup{\mathsf{B}}(y,r))
;

(v)

L(x,y)>0
for all

x\in({\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus{\mathsf{B}}(1))\cup\partial{\mathsf{B}}(1)
,
x\neq y
.

Proof.

We fix
y\notin{\mathsf{B}}(1)
once and for all.
We begin with the proof of (i).
The continuity outside of
{\mathsf{B}}(1)
is immediate. Let us prove that for all
x_{0}\in\partial{\mathsf{B}}(1)
,

\lim_{x\to x_{0}}L(x,y)=\frac{(y-x_{0},y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\ln\frac{|z^{(\varphi)}-y||z^{(-\varphi)}-y|}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}}{|z^{(\varphi)}-x_{0}|^{2}}\,d\varphi.

(29)

To prove that, we assume that
x=(1+\delta)x_{0}

and then take the limit
\delta\downarrow 0

with the help of (
27
);
we will prove that convergence uniformly in
x_{0}
, together with the continuity in
x_{0}
of
L(x_{0},y)
, and (
29
) will follow by elementary arguments. We proceed term by term.
First,
we compute that for fixed
x_{0}\in\partial{\mathsf{B}}(1)
, we have

\ln|(1+\delta)x_{0}|=\ln(1+\delta)=\delta+O(\delta^{2})

(30)

and

\displaystyle\ln\frac{|(1+\delta)x_{0}-y|}{|x_{0}-y|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{|x_{0}-y+\delta x_{0}|^{2}}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln\Big(1+2\delta\cdot\frac{(x_{0}-y,x_{0})}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}+\delta^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}\Big)

(31)

\displaystyle=\delta\cdot\frac{(x_{0}-y,x_{0})}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}+O(\delta^{2}).

(32)

We derive that, for any
x_{0}\in\partial{\mathsf{B}}(1)
, when
x=x_{0}(1+\delta)
, we have

1-\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{1}{\ln|x|}\ln\frac{|x-y|}{|x_{0}-y|}=1-\frac{(x_{0}-y,x_{0})}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}=\frac{(y-x_{0},y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}.

(33)

We argue that this convergence is in fact
uniform in
x_{0}
. We observe that the coefficients

\frac{(x_{0}-y,x_{0})}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}
and
\frac{1}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}
appearing in (
31
) are bounded,
respectively by
\frac{|y|+1}{(|y|-1)^{2}}

and
\frac{1}{(|y|-1)^{2}}
. Using the fact that

|\ln(1+u)-u|\leq 2u^{2}
for
u\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
,
we see that the
O(\delta^{2})
in (
32
) is uniform in
x_{0}
,
and thus, so is the convergence in (
33
).

We also compute
\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{|x|^{2}-1}{\ln|x|}=2
and see that this limit is uniform
in
x_{0}
(in fact,
\frac{|x|^{2}-1}{\ln|x|}
is independent of
x_{0}
).

Finally, let us argue using the dominated convergence
theorem that, still with
x=(1+\delta)x_{0}
,

\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\ln\frac{|z^{(\varphi)}-y||z^{(-\varphi)}-y|}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}}{|z^{(\varphi)}-x|^{2}}\,d\varphi\underset{\delta\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\ln\frac{|z^{(\varphi)}-y||z^{(-\varphi)}-y|}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}}{|z^{(\varphi)}-x_{0}|^{2}}\,d\varphi,

(34)

uniformly in
x_{0}
.
For the moment, let us reason with fixed
x_{0}
.
It is clear that the integrand in the left-hand side
of (
34
) converges pointwise towards the integrand in the right-hand side of (
34
). Observing that
|x_{0}-z^{(\varphi)}|\leq|x-z^{(\varphi)}|
, we see that the integrand in the right-hand side
of (
34
) dominates the integrand in the left-hand side of (
34
). Let us check that the integrand in the right-hand side
of (
34
) is integrable;
the only difficulty is around
0
.
On the one hand, as
\varphi\to 0
:

\displaystyle\frac{|z^{(\varphi)}-y|^{2}}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|x_{0}-y+x_{0}(e^{i\varphi}-1)|^{2}}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}

\displaystyle=1+2\frac{(x_{0}-y,x_{0}(e^{i\varphi}-1))}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}+\frac{|e^{i\varphi}-1|^{2}}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}

\displaystyle=1+2\varphi\frac{(x_{0}-y,x_{0}i)}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}+2\frac{(x_{0}-y,x_{0}(e^{i\varphi}-1-i\varphi))}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}+\frac{|e^{i\varphi}-1|^{2}}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}

(35)

\displaystyle=1+2\varphi\frac{(x_{0}-y,x_{0}i)}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}+O(\varphi^{2}),

(36)

so

\displaystyle\ln\frac{|z^{(\varphi)}-y||z^{(-\varphi)}-y|}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln\bigg(\frac{|z^{(\varphi)}-y|^{2}}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}\times\frac{|z^{(-\varphi)}-y|^{2}}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln(1+O(\varphi^{2}))=O(\varphi^{2}).

(37)

On the other hand,

|z^{(\varphi)}-x_{0}|^{2}=|e^{i\varphi}-1|^{2}=\varphi^{2}+O(\varphi^{3})
.
We obtain that the integrand on the right-hand side
of (
34
) is bounded around
0
,
thus, it is integrable.
The dominated convergence theorem yields the convergence in (
34
), for fixed
x_{0}
.

To conclude, we need to show that this convergence is uniform in
x_{0}
. We are simply going to refine the above arguments by showing that our estimates are uniform.
Let us denote

\displaystyle f(\varphi,\delta)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{x_{0}\in\partial{\mathsf{B}}(1)}\Bigg|\frac{\ln\frac{|z^{(\varphi)}-y||z^{(-\varphi)}-y|}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}}{|z^{(\varphi)}-x_{0}|^{2}}-\frac{\ln\frac{|z^{(\varphi)}-y||z^{(-\varphi)}-y|}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}}{|z^{(\varphi)}-x|^{2}}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\ =\Big(1-\frac{|e^{i\varphi}-1|^{2}}{|e^{i\varphi}-(1+\delta)|^{2}}\Big)\times g(\varphi),

(38)

with

g(\varphi):=\frac{\sup_{x_{0}\in\partial{\mathsf{B}}(1)}\big|{\ln\frac{|z^{(\varphi)}-y||z^{(-\varphi)}-y|}{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}}\big|}{|e^{i\varphi}-1|^{2}}.

(39)

Let us show that
g(\varphi)
is bounded: the numerator on the right-hand side of (

does not diverge at

. This comes from (

), (
36
) and (
37
): observe that the coefficients in (

) are bounded
in
x_{0}
. Using the fact that

for

, it is
straightforward to establish that the

in (

,
hence

is bounded around

.

In view of (

), we see
that

is dominated by

and converges pointwise towards

\int_{0}^{\pi}f(\varphi,\delta)d\varphi\underset{\delta\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0
.
The uniform convergence in (

From the boundedness of

, as desired.
This concludes the proof of item (i).

Next, item (ii)
follows from Lemma

(recall that
the Poisson kernel is harmonic with respect to

. Then,

In the next result, we
are interested in the regime when the radius of the disk (to be hit)
is small, and the starting point is also close to the disk.

Lemma 3.5
.

Consider some fixed
\delta_{0}>0
. For every
x,y\notin B(1)
such that

|x-y|<\frac{1}{2}

and

|x-y|\leq(|y|-1)^{1+\delta_{0}}
, we have, uniformly for all

:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[\widehat{\tau}(y,r)<\infty\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln|x-y|^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|}{\ln r^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|}\Big(1+O\big(\tfrac{|x-y|{\tilde{\ell}}_{y}}{(|y|-1)(\ln|x-y|^{-1}+\ln|y|)}\big)\Big),

(40)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[\widehat{\tau}(y,r)=\infty\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln\tfrac{|x-y|}{r}}{\ln r^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|}+O\big(\tfrac{|x-y|{\tilde{\ell}}_{y}}{(|y|-1)(\ln|x-y|^{-1}+\ln|y|)}\big).

(41)

Proof.

Instead of relying on Lemma 3.7 (iii) of
[
3
]
,
we will use the optional stopping theorem with the martingale
provided by Proposition
3.4
and the stopping

\widehat{\tau}(y,r)
.

We first establish some estimates.
Let us show that according to our assumptions,
\frac{|x-y|}{|y|-1}
is bounded by 1 and
\frac{|y|-1}{|x|-1}
is bounded by a constant depending on
\delta_{0}
. For the first quantity, observe that when
|y|\leq 2
, we have
|x-y|\leq(|y|-1)^{1+\delta_{0}}\leq|y|-1
, while
|y|>2
we simply have
\frac{|x-y|}{|y|-1}<\frac{1}{2}
.
For the second quantity, write when
|y|\leq 1.7
,

|x|-1\geq|y|-1-|x-y|\geq|y|-1-(|y|-1)^{1+\delta_{0}}\geq(|y|-1)(1-0.7^{\delta_{0}}),

and note that
|y|>1.7

|x|-1\geq|y|-|x-y|-1>|y|-3/2
which yields
\frac{|y|-1}{|x|-1}\leq\frac{|y|-1}{|y|-\frac{3}{2}}\leq\frac{7}{2}
.

From these facts, we now deduce that, under the
conditions of the lemma,

H(x,z)=H(y,z)\big(1+O\big(\tfrac{|x-y|}{|y|-1}\big)\big)

(42)

uniformly in

.
Indeed, using (
24
), we

\displaystyle\frac{H(x,z)}{H(y,z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{|x|^{2}-1}{|y|^{2}-1}\cdot\frac{|y-z|^{2}}{|x-z|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Big(1+\frac{|x-y|^{2}+2(y,x-y)}{|y|^{2}-1}\Big)\Big(1+\frac{|x-y|^{2}+2(x-y,x-z)}{|x-z|^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle=:(1+T_{1})(1+T_{2}).

Note that, since
|y|\geq 1
,
we have
|y|^{2}-1\geq|y|(|y|-1)
,
and recall that
\frac{|x-y|}{|y|-1}
is bounded;
this shows that
T_{1}

is
O\big(\tfrac{|x-y|}{|y|-1}\big)
.
Then, writing
\frac{|x-y|}{|x-z|}\leq\frac{|x-y|}{|x|-1}=\frac{|x-y|}{|y|-1}\frac{|y|-1}{|x|-1}
, recalling that both fractions are bounded, we also find that
T_{2}
is
O\big(\tfrac{|x-y|}{|y|-1}\big)
.
By the boundedness of
\tfrac{|x-y|}{|y|-1}
, this implies (

).

We now consider
r\in(0,|x-y|)
.
Notice that our assumptions imply
r<|y|-1
,
so that
{\mathsf{B}}(y,r)\cap{\mathsf{B}}(1)=\emptyset
. Indeed, if
|y|\leq 2
, then

, while if

.

Recalling (
10
) and using (
20
)
(to substitute
\ln|x|
or
\ln|u|

by
\ln|y|
),
we notice that (
42
)
permits us to obtain

\displaystyle L(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln|x-y|^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|+O\big(\tfrac{|x-y|{\tilde{\ell}}_{y}}{|y|-1}\big)}{\ln|y|+O\big(\tfrac{|x-y|}{|y|}\big)}

and, for any
u\in\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r)

\displaystyle L(u,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln r^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|+O\big(\tfrac{r{\tilde{\ell}}_{y}}{|y|-1}\big)}{\ln|y|+O\big(\tfrac{r}{|y|}\big)}.

Now, by the assumption that
|x-y|\leq(|y|-1)^{1+\delta_{0}}
, and lower bounding
\ell_{y}
by
\ln(|y|-1)
, we have

\frac{1}{1+\delta_{0}}\ln|x-y|^{-1}+\ell_{y}\geq 0

hence
\ln|x-y|^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|\geq\frac{\delta_{0}}{1+\delta_{0}}(\ln|x-y|^{-1}+\ln|y|)
.

We are now ready to conclude. We obtain from the optional stopping theorem
(recall
Proposition
3.4
(iii) and (iv))

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[\widehat{\tau}(y,r)<\infty\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\big(\ln|x-y|^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|+O\big(\tfrac{|x-y|{\tilde{\ell}}_{y}}{|y|-1}\big)\big)\big(\ln|y|+O\big(\tfrac{r}{|y|}\big)\big)}{\big(\ln r^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|+O\big(\tfrac{r{\tilde{\ell}}_{y}}{|y|-1}\big)\big)\big(\ln|y|+O\big(\tfrac{|x-y|}{|y|}\big)\big)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln|x-y|^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|}{\ln r^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|}\Big(1+O\big(\tfrac{|x-y|{\tilde{\ell}}_{y}}{(|y|-1)(\ln|x-y|^{-1}+\ln|y|)}\big)\Big),

thus proving (
40
).
Then, (
41
)
is a direct consequence of (
40
).
∎

3.2
Traces of different conditioned
Brownian motions

Next, the goal is to be able to compare traces left by the Brownian
motion conditioned on not hitting a specific (typically small) disk
and the usual conditioned (on not hitting
{\mathsf{B}}(1)
) Brownian motion
additionally conditioned on not hitting that disk. Recall that the Brownian motion conditioned on not hitting
B(y,r)
is denoted by
\widehat{W}^{y,r}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Assume that
0<r<r^{\prime}
and let
y
be such that

{\mathsf{B}}(y,r^{\prime})\cap{\mathsf{B}}(1)=\emptyset

(meaning that
s:=|y|-r^{\prime}-1
is strictly positive).
Let us denote also by
\tau^{*}(z,r^{\prime})
the hitting time
of
\partial{\mathsf{B}}(z,r^{\prime})
by
\widehat{W}^{y,r}
. Then,
for any
x\in\big({\mathsf{B}}(y,r^{\prime})\setminus{\mathsf{B}}(y,r)\big)\cup\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r)

\Big|\frac{d{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[\widehat{W}_{[0,\widehat{\tau}(y,r^{\prime})]}\in\cdot\,\mid\widehat{\tau}(y,r^{\prime})<\widehat{\tau}(y,r)\big]}{d{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[\widehat{W}^{y,r}_{[0,\tau^{*}(y,r^{\prime})]}\in\cdot\,\big]}-1\Big|=O\big(\tfrac{r^{\prime}}{s\ln s}\big).

(43)

Proof.

First, we note that,
analogously to Section 2.1 of
[
3
]
,
it is possible to define the diffusion
\widehat{W}

conditioned on not touching
{\mathsf{B}}(y,r)
anymore
even for a starting point on
\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r)
.
Since the estimates we obtain below
(in the case
x\in{\mathsf{B}}(y,r^{\prime})\setminus{\mathsf{B}}(y,r)
)
will be uniform in
x
, it is enough to
prove the result for
x\in{\mathsf{B}}(y,r^{\prime})\setminus{\mathsf{B}}(y,r)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a set of (finite) trajectories,
having the following property:
a trajectory belonging to this set has to start at
x
,
it cannot touch
{\mathsf{B}}(y,r)
, and it ends on its first visit
to
\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r^{\prime})
.
Then, we have (by an obvious adaptation of Lemma 2.1
of
[
3
]
)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[\widehat{W}^{y,r}_{[0,\tau^{*}(y,r^{\prime})]}\in\Gamma\,\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[W_{[0,\tau(y,r^{\prime})]}\in\Gamma\,\mid\tau(y,r^{\prime})<\tau(y,r)\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[W_{[0,\tau(y,r^{\prime})]}\in\Gamma\,\big]}{{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[\tau(y,r^{\prime})<\tau(y,r)\big]},

and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[\widehat{W}_{[0,\widehat{\tau}(y,r^{\prime})]}\in\Gamma\,\mid\widehat{\tau}(y,r^{\prime})<\widehat{\tau}(y,r)\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[\widehat{W}_{[0,\widehat{\tau}(y,r^{\prime})]}\in\Gamma\,\big]}{{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[\widehat{\tau}(y,r^{\prime})<\widehat{\tau}(y,r)\big]}

(by Lemma 3.6 of
[
3
]
)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[W_{[0,\tau(y,r^{\prime})]}\in\Gamma\,\big]}{{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[\tau(y,r^{\prime})<\tau(y,r)\big]}\big(1+O\big(\tfrac{r^{\prime}}{s\ln s}\big)\big),

which implies (
43
).
∎

The above result compares the traces left in
{\mathsf{B}}(y,r^{\prime})
by
\widehat{W}^{y,r}

and (conditioned)
\widehat{W}
before going out of
{\mathsf{B}}(y,r^{\prime})

for the first time;
it is important to observe that
it allows us to couple these traces with probability
close to
1
(when the right-hand side
of (
43
) is small).
With
r^{\prime}:=r\ln^{\theta}r^{-1}
,
we also need to compare
the full traces left in
{\mathsf{B}}(y,r^{\prime})

by these two processes:

Lemma 3.7
.

Fix
\theta\geq 2
and
\delta_{0}>0
.
Let
r<1
and
y\notin{\mathsf{B}}(1)
be such that

r\ln^{2\theta}r^{-1}<\min\big(\tfrac{1}{2},|y|-1,(|y|-1)^{1+\delta_{0}}\big).

(44)

Then, for any

x\in\big({\mathsf{B}}(y,\ln^{\theta}r^{-1})\setminus{\mathsf{B}}(y,r)\big)\cup\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r)
,
we have

\Big|\frac{d{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[\widehat{W}_{[0,\infty]}\cap{\mathsf{B}}(y,r\ln^{\theta}r^{-1})\in\cdot\,\mid\widehat{\tau}(y,r)=\infty\big]}{d{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\big[\widehat{W}^{y,r}_{[0,\infty]}\cap{\mathsf{B}}(y,r\ln^{\theta}r^{-1})\in\cdot\,\big]}-1\Big|=O\big(\tfrac{\ln\ln r^{-1}}{\ln r^{-1}+\ln|y|}+\tfrac{r{\tilde{\ell}}_{y}\ln^{2\theta}r^{-1}}{|y|-1}\big).

(45)

Proof.

Once again, as in the proof
of Lemma
3.6
, it is enough
to obtain the proof for the case

x\in{\mathsf{B}}(y,\ln^{\theta}r^{-1})\setminus{\mathsf{B}}(y,r)
.
Abbreviate
r_{1}:=r\ln^{\theta}r^{-1}
,

r_{2}:=r\ln^{2\theta}r^{-1}
.
The idea of the proof is the following: first, by Lemma
3.6
,
the traces left by the two processes on
{\mathsf{B}}(y,r_{2})

(and therefore on
{\mathsf{B}}(y,r_{1})
) up to their first hitting time
of
\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r_{2})

Then, both processes may return a few times from
\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r_{2})

to
\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r_{1})

has almost the same law (in fact, it is precisely
\text{Geom}_{0}(1/2)

for
\widehat{W}^{y,r}

returns have almost the same law. Then, each pair of additional

\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r_{1})
to
\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r_{2})

Now, we fill in the details. First, note that
(e.g., by (2.17) of
[
3
]

\text{ for any }x\in\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r_{2}),\qquad{\mathbb{P}}_{x}[\tau^{*}(y,r_{1})<\infty]=\frac{\ln({r_{1}}/{r})}{\ln({r_{2}}/{r})}=\frac{1}{2}.

(46)

For the (additionally) conditioned process
\widehat{W}
,
we have, for
x\in\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r_{2})

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{x}[\widehat{\tau}(y,r_{1})<\infty\mid\widehat{\tau}(y,r)=\infty]

(

being the conditional entrance measure
to
\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r_{1})
from

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}_{x}[\widehat{\tau}(y,r_{1})<\infty]{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}[\widehat{\tau}(y,r)=\infty]}{{\mathbb{P}}_{x}[\widehat{\tau}(y,r)=\infty]}

(by Lemma

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\ln r_{2}^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|}{\ln r_{1}^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|}\big(1+O\big(\tfrac{r_{2}{\tilde{\ell}}_{y}}{(|y|-1)(\ln r_{2}^{-1}+\ln|y|)}\big)\big)\big(\frac{\ln\frac{r_{1}}{r}}{\ln r^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|}+O\big(\tfrac{r_{1}{\tilde{\ell}}_{y}}{(|y|-1)(\ln r_{1}^{-1}+\ln|y|)}\big)\big)}{\frac{\ln\frac{r_{2}}{r}}{\ln r^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|}+O\big(\tfrac{r_{2}{\tilde{\ell}}_{y}}{(|y|-1)(\ln r_{2}^{-1}+\ln|y|)}\big)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\ln r_{2}^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|}{\ln r_{1}^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|}\cdot\frac{\ln\frac{r_{1}}{r}}{\ln r^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|}}{\frac{\ln\frac{r_{2}}{r}}{\ln r^{-1}+\ell_{y}+\ln|y|}}\Big(1+O\big(\tfrac{r{\tilde{\ell}}_{y}\ln^{2\theta}r^{-1}}{|y|-1}\big)\Big)

\frac{\ln({r_{1}}/{r})}{\ln({r_{2}}/{r})}=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+O\big(\tfrac{\ln\ln r^{-1}}{\ln r^{-1}+\ln|y|}+\tfrac{r{\tilde{\ell}}_{y}\ln^{2\theta}r^{-1}}{|y|-1}\big)\Big).

So, with (

)
and (
47

the excursion counts of the two processes with
probability close to
1
.

Next, we need to be able to couple the entrance points to
\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r_{1})

with high probability. Let
M
be a (measurable) subset
of
\partial{\mathsf{B}}(y,r_{1})

\widehat{W}
