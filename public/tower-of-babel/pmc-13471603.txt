Maternal knowledge of neonatal danger signs and associated factors among postpartum mothers attending public health facilities in Adama town, Ethiopia
Authors: Gemechu Dereje Feyissa, Addis Begashaw, Tilaye Workneh, Ebrahim Mohammed, Tamrat Endebu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe neonatal period, defined as the first 28 days of life, remains a critical phase for child survival. According to the 2019 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey, the neonatal mortality rate was 33 deaths per 1,000 live births. Despite various interventions, neonatal mortality and morbidities related to neonatal danger signs persist. Previous studies on maternal knowledge of neonatal danger signs have reported inconsistent findings.ObjectiveThis study replicates established associations between maternal characteristics and neonatal danger sign knowledge in a previously unstudied urban site within Oromia, Ethiopia, and also examines how prompted recognition affects the measured prevalence of knowledge compared with spontaneous-recall approaches.MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted from April 1–30, 2022. A total of 426 postpartum women were selected using systematic random sampling. Data were collected through face-to-face exit interviews using a structured questionnaire. Data entry was done with EPI Info, and analysis was performed using SPSS version 26. Knowledge assessed via 9 World Health Organization (WHO) neonatal danger signs (NDS) items (prompted yes/no, mean cutoff ≥ 5 = good). Multivariable logistic regression identified factors (p < 0.05).Results75.5% (95% CI 71.6–79.7%) had good knowledge. Factors significantly associated with good maternal knowledge include educational level of college or above [AOR: 4.83; 95% CI: 1.49, 15.64], secondary educational level [AOR: 3.10; 95% CI: 1.09, 8.78], delivery by caesarean section [AOR: 2.47; 95% CI: 1.28, 4.80], multigravida [AOR: 2.11; 95% CI: 1.26, 3.52], and grand multigravida [AOR: 3.68; 95% CI: 1.08, 12.62].ConclusionMaternal knowledge of neonatal danger signs was relatively high in this urban postnatal sample, but the estimate is influenced by the recognition-based measurement approach. The findings reinforce previously reported associations and provide context-specific evidence from Adama town to inform newborn health education.

Introduction

The neonatal period, encompassing the first 28 days of life, is critical for infant survival and lays the foundation for healthy childhood development. Specialized interventions targeting newborn survival are essential to reducing under-five mortality rates globally [1]. Neonatal danger signs (NDS) are nonspecific clinical indicators signaling severe neonatal illness within the first 28 days of life [2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), nine key NDS include breastfeeding difficulties, fever, severe chest retractions, hypothermia, convulsions, jaundice, breathing difficulties, umbilical infection, and eye discharge [3]. Maternal awareness on these signs is crucial to prompt timely health care seeking and reduce neonatal morbidity and mortality [4].

The WHO recommends comprehensive postnatal care within the first 24 h after birth, with at least three additional visits during the neonatal period to reduce preventable newborn deaths [5]. Ethiopia aims to reduce neonatal mortality to 12 per 1,000 live births by 2030 in alignment with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) [6]. Strategies implemented include the National Newborn and Child Survival Strategy [2], Integrated Management of Neonatal and Childhood Illness (IMNCI) [7], and community-based newborn care [8].

Globally, approximately 2.4 million newborn deaths occurred in 2019, with 99% occurring in low- and middle-income countries; 75% of neonatal deaths occur within the first week of life [1, 9, 10]. Sub-Saharan Africa bears the highest burden, with neonatal mortality rates of 27 per 1,000 live births [6, 11]. In Ethiopia, the 2019 Demographic and Health Survey reported neonatal mortality at 33 per 1,000 live births [12]. Despite efforts, neonatal mortality remains high, partly due to inadequate maternal knowledge of NDS [8, 13].

Factors influencing knowledge commonly include maternal education, antenatal and postnatal care attendance, parity, and general health service utilization [13–17]. High neonatal mortality rates persist, partly due to limited maternal recognition of NDS and delayed care seeking [18, 19]. Despite national efforts through strategies such as the IMNCI and community-based newborn care, comprehensive education about NDS remains fragmented in many areas.

Previous Ethiopian studies have reported maternal knowledge of neonatal danger signs ranging from 20.3% to 67.1% [13, 20–23], with an overall pooled prevalence of 40.7% in an earlier systematic review and meta-analysis [24]. More recent evidence has further shown that mothers’ health-seeking practices toward neonatal danger signs remain suboptimal in Ethiopia, with a pooled prevalence of 52.15% in the 2024 systematic review by Ferede et al. [25]. Against this background, the present study provides replication evidence from Adama town, a previously under-reported urban setting in Oromia, where no prior neonatal danger sign knowledge study was identified. This addresses a post-2019 EDHS evidence gap, examining knowledge levels and associated factors among postpartum mothers attending public health facilities to inform targeted SDG 3.2 interventions [6]. Rather than claiming a broad scientific gap, this study aims to replicate established associations in a new geographic context and to quantify the difference between prompted recognition and spontaneous recall measurement approaches. This framing is relevant for comparing findings across studies and for interpreting maternal knowledge estimates in facility-based postnatal care settings.

Methods

Study area and period

This study was conducted at government health facilities in Adama town, located approximately 100 km from Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Adama town consists of six subcities and 18 kebeles, with an estimated population of 407,375 in 2021. There are 18 health facilities providing maternal health services, including nine government facilities, six private facilities, and three organizational health facilities. These include Adama Hospital Medical College, Adama Health Center, Geda Health Center, Denbela Health Center, Hawas Health Center, Anole Health Center, Biftu Health Center, Hangatu Health Center, and Boku Shanan Health Center. The study was conducted from April 1 to 30, 2022.

Study design

A facility-based cross-sectional study design was employed.

Source population

All mothers receiving postpartum services at public health facilities in Adama town constituted the source population.

Study population

All mothers attending postnatal care services at the selected health facilities during the data collection period were eligible for inclusion.

Inclusion criteria

Mothers who attended postnatal services and had resided in Adama town for at least six months were included in the study.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula based on a previous proportion of good maternal knowledge of neonatal danger signs (48.1%) reported in North Wollo, Ethiopia [26]. accounting for an 11% nonresponse rate, the total sample size was adjusted to 426.

Sampling procedure

Adama town has nine government health facilities providing maternal delivery and postnatal care services. Eight of these facilities were randomly included in the study. The total sample size of 426 postpartum mothers was allocated proportionally to each health facility based on the average number of postpartum clients (client flow) attending postnatal care services during the study period. To construct the sampling frame at each health facility, the average number of postpartum mothers expected to attend postnatal care per day was obtained from facility records or estimated from recent client flow data. This number was multiplied by the number of data collection days to estimate the total number of postpartum mothers expected during the study period at each facility, constituting the sampling frame at that facility. Systematic random sampling was then employed at each facility to select participants from the postpartum mothers attending postnatal care. The sampling interval (k) was calculated by dividing the estimated total number of postpartum mothers during the data collection period by the allocated sample size for that facility. On the first day, the first participant was selected randomly using a lottery method from the first k mothers, and thereafter every kth mother was included until the allocated sample size was reached. This method ensured a representative sample of postpartum mothers from each facility proportionate to client flow (Fig. 1).

Variables

Dependent variable

Maternal knowledge of neonatal danger signs.

Independent variables

Sociodemographic variables: age, marital status, educational level, income, residence, occupation.

Health service-related variables: number of antenatal care (ANC) visits, number of postnatal care visits, place of delivery, sources of information about neonatal danger signs.

Obstetric-related variables: parity, number of pregnancies, history of stillbirth or abortion, mode of delivery.

Operational definitions

Postnatal Period

Begins immediately after birth and continues up to six weeks (42 days) postpartum [27]. WHO recommends postnatal visits within 24 h of birth, on day 3 (48–72 h), between days 7–14, and at six weeks after birth [3].

Neonatal period

The first 28 days of life.

Early neonatal period

The first 7 days of life.

Late neonatal period

Days 8 to 28 of life.

Neonatal danger signs (NDSs)

According to WHO [3], these include breastfeeding difficulties, fever, severe chest retractions, hypothermia, convulsions, jaundice, breathing difficulties, umbilical infection (redness or pus discharge), and eye discharge. Maternal knowledge was assessed using ‘yes/no’ questions on these nine danger signs, each weighted equally.

Knowledge status

Mothers scoring equal to or above the mean knowledge score (≥ 5) were classified as having good knowledge, while those below the mean were considered to have poor knowledge.

Data collection tools and procedures

A structured interviewer-administered questionnaire was used, covering sociodemographic characteristics, obstetric history, health service utilization, and maternal knowledge of neonatal danger signs. The questionnaire was developed in English, translated into Afan Oromo and Amharic, and back-translated into English by experts to ensure consistency. Data collectors and supervisors received one day of training on confidentiality, data quality, and interviewing techniques. Data were collected by six diploma nurses and two diploma midwives who were external to the selected health facilities. Data were collected at the health facility level, specifically from mothers attending postnatal care services at eight selected government health facilities in Adama town, including hospitals and health centers. Data collection was conducted through face-to-face exit interviews with postpartum mothers immediately after they received postpartum services at these health facilities. This approach ensures that participants had recent exposure to postnatal care services, supporting the relevance and reliability of the information regarding their knowledge of neonatal danger signs. Data collection was closely supervised by the principal investigator and a designated supervisor, with daily checks for consistency and completeness.

Data quality assurance

A pretest was conducted on 5% of the sample size at similar setting but other than the study area to evaluate clarity and reliability. The questionnaire was revised accordingly after back-translation and pretesting. Data collectors and supervisors received a one-day refresher training on questionnaire administration to standardize procedures and minimize interviewer bias. Data were double entered into EPI Info version 7.2 and cleaned before analysis in SPSS version 26.

Data processing and analysis

Data were entered into EPI Info version 7.2 and exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. Descriptive statistics such as frequencies and percentages summarized categorical variables. Maternal knowledge of NDS was assessed using WHO’s 9 standardized danger signs (breastfeeding difficulties, fever, severe chest retractions, hypothermia, convulsions, jaundice, breathing difficulties, umbilical infection, eye discharge). A structured interviewer-administered questionnaire used prompted yes/no recognition format—participants were read each sign and asked if they recognized it as dangerous. This prompted recognition approach inherently inflates prevalence compared to spontaneous recall-based study [21], where mothers must name ≥ 3 signs unprompted. Total score ranged 0–9; mean score was 5.0 ± 1.9 (normally distributed per Shapiro-Wilk test, p > 0.05). Mothers scoring ≥mean (≥ 5) were classified as having ‘good knowledge’; <5 as ‘poor knowledge’. The cut-off of ≥ 5 was informed by the pretest findings (n = 21; mean score 4.8) and adopted to provide a practical balance between sensitivity and specificity in this setting. Sensitivity analysis was based on spontaneous recall. Mothers who did not volunteer any sign were classified as having poor spontaneous recall, and the distribution of recalled signs was summarized among all participants.

Bivariate logistic regression was performed to identify independent variables associated with maternal knowledge status. Variables with p-values less than 0.25 were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to adjust for potential confounders. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed by calculating variance inflation factors (VIF); all variables included in the multivariable model had VIF values below the commonly accepted threshold of 10, indicating no significant multicollinearity. The goodness-of-fit of the final logistic regression model was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, where a non-significant p-value (p > 0.05) indicated an adequate model fit. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for variables that retained statistical significance (p < 0.05) in the multivariable analysis.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 408 postpartum mothers participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 95.8%. The median age of participants was 28 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 24–30). Regarding educational status, 155 (38%) had completed secondary education, while 107 (26.2%) had primary education. The majority of mothers (245, 60%) were housewives. The average monthly income for 120 (29.4%) participants ranged between 60 and 99 United States dollar (USD) (Table 1).

Maternal knowledge of NDS

Among the participants, 308 (75.5%; 95% CI: 71.6, 79.7%) demonstrated good knowledge of NDS (Fig. 2). We included the 96 mothers with no spontaneous response in the poor-knowledge category for the sensitivity analysis. Among the 312 mothers who volunteered at least one sign, the distribution was 0 signs 1.2%, 1–2 signs 4.7%, 3–4 signs 18.6%, 5–6 signs 42.9%, and 7–9 signs 32.6%. The most commonly recognized danger signs included neonatal convulsions (88.2%) and breastfeeding difficulties (87.3%) (Fig. 3).

Factors associated with maternal knowledge of NDS

In bivariate analysis, maternal education, paternal education, mode of delivery, and number of pregnancies were associated with maternal knowledge at p < 0.25 and included in the multivariable model. Multivariable logistic regression identified maternal education, mode of delivery, and number of pregnancies as significantly associated factors.

Mothers with college-level education or higher had 4.83 times higher odds of good knowledge compared to mothers unable to read and write [AOR: 4.83; 95% CI: 1.49, 15.64]. Those with secondary education had 3.10 times increased odds [AOR: 3.10; 95% CI: 1.09, 8.78]. Mothers who delivered by caesarean section were 2.47 times more likely to have good knowledge compared to vaginal delivery [AOR: 2.47; 95% CI: 1.28, 4.80]. Multigravida mothers had 2.11 times higher odds than primigravida mothers [AOR: 2.11; 95% CI: 1.26, 3.52]. Similarly, grand multigravida mothers had 3.68 times higher odds than primigravida mothers [AOR: 3.68; 95% CI: 1.08, 12.62] (Table 3).

Discussion

This study found that 75.5% of postpartum mothers attending public health facilities in Adama town had good knowledge of neonatal danger signs, which is broadly consistent with the 72% reported from Uganda [28]. Rather than indicating a fundamentally new pattern, these findings provide replication evidence from an urban Oromia setting.

The level of knowledge observed in this study is higher than that reported in many previous studies from China (42%) [29], India (21.2%) [30], Kenya (15.5%) [31] and several regions of Ethiopia, including Central Ethiopia (61.70%) [21], Wolaita zone (67.1%) [20], Arba Minch (40.9%) [13], Gurage (48.2%) [14], Mekedella woreda, Northeast Ethiopia (28.2%) [23], Ambo town (20.3%) [16], Harar town (32.9%) [22], and Dire-Dawa (40.8%) [32]. These differences may reflect variation in socio-demographic characteristics, study setting, timing of the studies, and the availability of health services. Adama town’s relatively better health infrastructure and central location may also have improved access to maternal health information.

The 75.5% prevalence of good knowledge observed in this study is higher than the pooled Ethiopian estimate reported by Demis et al. (40.7%) [24]. Because interviews were conducted immediately after postnatal care, some of the observed knowledge may reflect recent recall of counselling rather than baseline maternal knowledge. This higher prevalence should be interpreted cautiously, because our study used prompted yes/no recognition of nine WHO neonatal danger signs, whereas several prior studies, including Bayih et al. [21] used spontaneous recall thresholds. In addition, Bayih et al. recruited mothers at NICU discharge, a population that likely received more intensive counseling and was not directly comparable with our mixed postnatal facility sample. Therefore, the difference between 75.5% and 61.7% likely reflects both measurement approach and study population rather than instrument format alone. Recent Ethiopian evidence also supports this interpretation. Ferede et al. [25] reported that mothers’ practices toward neonatal danger signs remain variable across settings, reinforcing the need to interpret local estimates within their specific population and assessment context. Taken together, our findings suggest that maternal education, gravidity, and mode of delivery remain important correlates of neonatal danger sign knowledge, while the absolute prevalence estimate is influenced by both setting and assessment method.

Maternal education was identified as a significant predictor of NDS knowledge. Mothers with college-level education or higher were nearly five times more likely to have good knowledge compared to mothers who were unable to read and write, consistent with findings from Ambo [16], Woldia [17] and North Wollo [26]. Likewise, mothers with secondary education showed a threefold increase in knowledge, aligning with studies from Mekedella woreda [23], Arba Minch [13], north central Ethiopia [21], Gurage zone [33], and Harar [22]. This indicates that higher maternal education levels are associated with better knowledge of NDS compared to no formal education. The probable explanation is that educated mothers acquire essential health knowledge through their academic experience and are more likely to engage in antenatal and postnatal care services. Additionally, educated mothers tend to seek timely health care for their sick neonates, during which they receive further information from health care providers, reinforcing their understanding.

Mothers who delivered by caesarean section had higher odds of good neonatal danger sign knowledge than those who delivered vaginally. This may partly reflect greater contact with health workers through longer postnatal stays and more counseling opportunities, as also suggested by findings from Harar [22]. However, caesarean delivery is not randomly assigned, and women who undergo caesarean section may differ from those with vaginal delivery in ways that are not fully captured in the analysis, including pregnancy complications, pre-delivery health service exposure, and other socioeconomic or obstetric factors. Although the model adjusted for maternal education and income, residual confounding remains possible. Because information on the indication for caesarean section was not collected, this association should be interpreted cautiously.

Gravidity was also found to be a significant factor influencing maternal knowledge, with multigravida mothers being twice as likely to have good knowledge compared to primigravida mothers. This association likely reflects increased maternal experience and repeated exposure to health education during antenatal and postnatal care visits. Furthermore, grand multigravida mothers demonstrated more than a threefold increase in knowledge. These findings are consistent with studies conducted in Southern Ethiopia, Harar, and North Wollo [14, 22, 26]. A plausible explanation is that multigravida mothers have had multiple interactions with healthcare providers, which enhances their awareness and understanding of NDS compared to primigravida mothers.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference. Second, the prompted yes/no assessment of neonatal danger sign knowledge may have overestimated knowledge compared with spontaneous recall instruments, which limits direct comparability with studies using different methods. Third, because interviews were conducted immediately after postnatal counselling, some responses may reflect recent recall of information provided during the visit rather than baseline knowledge. Fourth, the facility-based sample may not be representative of mothers who do not attend postnatal care, which limits generalizability. Fifth, the observed association between caesarean delivery and maternal knowledge should be interpreted cautiously, as residual confounding may remain. We did not collect information on the indication for caesarean section; therefore, we could not assess whether the association differed by elective versus emergency surgery or by maternal versus fetal indication. Finally, the estimate for grand multigravida mothers should be interpreted cautiously because of the small subgroup size.

Conclusion

This study replicates established associations between maternal education, mode of delivery, and gravidity with neonatal danger sign knowledge in a previously under-studied urban site within Oromia. The relatively high prevalence observed in Adama town should be interpreted in light of the prompted-recognition measurement approach, which may inflate estimates compared with spontaneous recall instruments. These findings provide context-specific evidence rather than evidence of a new epidemiologic pattern, and they support continued strengthening of neonatal health education during antenatal and postnatal care, particularly for primigravida mothers. Further longitudinal or interventional studies are needed to clarify causal pathways and to assess whether improved knowledge translates into better care-seeking and neonatal outcomes.
