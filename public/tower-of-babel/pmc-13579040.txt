When biochemistry fails: extreme lipemia masking hypertriglyceridemia-induced acute pancreatitis treated with insulin
Authors: Elif Unal Kaya, Mert Ali Kaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 30-year-old previously healthy male presented to the emergency department with malaise, nausea, and abdominal pain. He was alert and oriented but in significant discomfort. Examination revealed abdominal guarding without peritoneal signs, and other systemic examinations were unremarkable. Initial laboratory evaluation was severely limited by extreme lipemia, preventing biochemical analysis and resulting in diagnostic uncertainty. Complete blood count showed leukocytosis, thrombocytosis, and hemoconcentration (hemoglobin 24.6 g/dL [SI: 246 g/L]; reference range 12.0-17.0 g/dL [SI: 120-170 g/L]). Acute pancreatitis was suspected, but abdominal computed tomography showed no definitive pancreatic abnormalities. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit due to diagnostic uncertainty and concern for severe metabolic derangement. In the absence of confirmatory laboratory data, empiric insulin infusion with dextrose supplementation and hourly glucose monitoring was initiated to treat presumed hypertriglyceridemia via lipoprotein lipase activation. On day 3, biochemical testing confirmed hypertriglyceridemia with triglycerides of 6300 mg/dL (SI: 71.2 mmol/L) (reference range <150 mg/dL, [SI: <1.7 mmol/L]). Triglycerides decreased to 243 mg/dL (SI: 2.74 mmol/L) by day 8. Follow-up imaging demonstrated a 5 cm pancreatic pseudocyst managed conservatively. The patient was discharged on lipid-lowering therapy and dietary modification. At 6-week follow-up, the pseudocyst had regressed and he remained asymptomatic.Keywords: hypertriglyceridemia, pancreatitis, intensive care unit

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis is a common gastrointestinal emergency, most frequently caused by gallstones and alcohol use. Hypertriglyceridemia represents a less common but well-established etiology, accounting for approximately 1-10% of cases, with incidence varying across populations [1, 2]. The risk of pancreatitis increases substantially when serum triglyceride levels exceed 1000 mg/dL (SI: 11.3 mmol/L) and rises further at levels above 2000 mg/dL (SI: 22.6 mmol/L) [2, 3]. Hypertriglyceridemia should be considered as a potential etiology of acute pancreatitis, particularly in younger patients without typical risk factors such as gallstones or alcohol use.

The pathophysiology is thought to involve hydrolysis of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins by pancreatic lipase, leading to the release of free fatty acids, which induce lipotoxicity, endothelial dysfunction, and pancreatic inflammation [2, 4]. In severe cases, accumulation of chylomicrons increases plasma viscosity, contributing to pancreatic ischemia and further injury [4].

A major diagnostic challenge arises in cases of extreme hypertriglyceridemia, where marked lipemia interferes with routine laboratory assays, resulting in delayed or unobtainable biochemical results [5]. This interference may obscure key diagnostic markers, including serum amylase, lipase, and electrolytes, and can produce laboratory artifacts such as pseudohyponatremia [5, 6]. In such situations, clinicians must rely on clinical assessment and imaging findings, often necessitating early empiric management despite incomplete data.

Management of hypertriglyceridemia-induced pancreatitis (HTGP) focuses on rapid triglyceride reduction. While therapeutic plasma exchange can achieve rapid clearance, it is not universally available [7]. Insulin infusion, by enhancing lipoprotein lipase activity, represents a widely accessible and effective alternative, particularly in the ICU setting where close metabolic monitoring is feasible [2, 4].

Here, we present a case of severe hypertriglyceridemia with extreme lipemia that precluded initial laboratory evaluation, requiring ICU-based empiric insulin therapy prior to biochemical confirmation, and complicated by subsequent pancreatic pseudocyst formation.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old previously healthy male with no known medical history or regular medication use presented to the emergency department with malaise, nausea, and abdominal pain. On initial evaluation, he was alert and oriented (Glasgow Coma Scale 15) but appeared uncomfortable due to pain. Cardiopulmonary and neurological examinations were unremarkable (heart rate 88 bpm, blood pressure 118/89 mmHg), and his serial electrocardiograms were normal. Abdominal examination revealed guarding without distension or rebound tenderness. His body mass index was 24.2 kg/m2. Further history revealed chronic excessive dietary fat intake, including frequent consumption of large amounts of butter and olive oil. The patient reported no prior hospitalizations, no history of chronic abdominal pain, and no previous episodes suggestive of pancreatitis. He denied any known family history of dyslipidemia, premature cardiovascular disease, or pancreatitis. Physical examination did not reveal eruptive xanthomas, xanthelasma, lipodystrophy, or other stigmata of chronic hyperlipidemia.

Diagnostic assessment

Initial laboratory samples were reported by the laboratory as markedly lipemic, preventing analysis of routine biochemical tests, including serum amylase, lipase, liver function tests, and metabolic panel due to severe lipemic interference. Complete blood count revealed increased white blood cells 12.4 × 109/L (reference range 4.0-10.0 × 109/L), increased platelets with 780 × 109/L (reference range 188-358 × 109/L), and significant hemoconcentration with hemoglobin 24.6 g/dL (SI: 246 g/L) (reference range 11.9-14.6 g/dL [SI: 119-146 g/L]). Fingerstick blood glucose was 122 mg/dL (SI: 6.8 mmol/L) (reference range 70-99 mg/dL [SI: 3.9-5.5 mmol/L]). Abdominal computed tomography (CT) performed in the emergency department demonstrated intestinal gas distension, colonic dilatation, and minimal free fluid within the mesenteric fat, without definitive pancreatic abnormalities. Based on clinical presentation, HTGP was suspected.

Treatment

Despite repeated venous sampling, biochemical analysis remained unobtainable due to severe lipemia. Due to the concern for metabolic derangement, the patient was admitted to the ICU. Initial management included aggressive intravenous hydration, strict nil per os bowel rest, and close hemodynamic and metabolic monitoring.

Repeat laboratory evaluations remained limited by severe lipemia, with only complete blood count available. Given the high suspicion for hypertriglyceridemia, empiric insulin infusion (5 units per hour) was initiated at hour 2 of ICU admission, with concurrent dextrose infusion and hourly glucose monitoring.

On day 3, biochemical analysis became available, revealing triglycerides of 6300 mg/dL (SI:71.2 mmol/L) (reference range, <150 mg/dL [SI: <1.7 mmol/L]), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol 360 mg/dL (SI: 9.31 mmol/L) (reference range, <100 mg/dL [SI: <3.0 mmol/L]) (direct measurement), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol 12 mg/dL (SI: 0.31 mmol/L) (reference range, ≥40 mg/dL in men [SI: ≥1.0 mmol/L in men]). Amylase and lipase were elevated at 360 U/L (SI: 6.0 µkat/L) (reference range, 30-110 U/L [SI: 0.5-1.8 µkat/L]) and 420 U/L (SI: 7.0 µkat/L) (reference range, 13-60 U/L [SI: 0.2-1.0 µkat/L]), respectively, consistent with acute pancreatitis. Additional findings included creatinine of 0.93 mg/dL (SI: 82.2 µmol/L) (reference range 0.5-1.1 mg/dL [SI: 44-97 µmol/L]) and estimated glomerular filtration rate 98 mL/min/1.73 m2. Insulin infusion was continued, resulting in a progressive decline in triglyceride levels, reaching 765 mg/dL (SI: 8.63 mmol/L) by day 5 and 243 mg/dL (SI: 2.74 mmol/L) by day 8 (Fig. 1). Insulin infusion was continued until hospital day 6, when a sustained reduction in triglyceride levels was observed. Blood glucose levels were monitored hourly during therapy and remained within the target range with concurrent dextrose supplementation. After transition to oral intake, no episodes of significant hypo- or hyperglycemia were recorded. During this period, the patient's abdominal pain initially improved but worsened following reintroduction of oral intake. Repeat contrast-enhanced abdominal CT on day 6 demonstrated a pancreatic cystic lesion measuring approximately 5 cm, consistent with a developing pseudocyst. General surgery was consulted, and a conservative “watch-and-wait” approach was recommended.

By day 8, inflammatory markers and organ function tests had normalized, while triglyceride levels showed marked improvement but remained elevated above the normal range. Serial complete blood counts returned to normal ranges, supporting a reactive process related to hypertriglyceridemia. Further history obtained during hospitalization revealed chronic excessive dietary fat intake, including frequent consumption of large amounts of butter and olive oil. He had no history of alcohol use.

The patient's symptoms gradually improved, with complete resolution of abdominal pain by day 12. A follow-up CT scan on day 15 demonstrated persistence of the pancreatic cystic lesion without progression. Continued conservative management was recommended.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient was discharged on day 20 in stable condition. He was prescribed omega-3 fatty acids and fenofibrate for long-term triglyceride control, with endocrinologist and dietitian consultation and initiation of a strict low-fat diet. Following discharge, the patient was referred to an endocrinology clinic at an external institution for further evaluation, including consideration of genetic testing for underlying lipid metabolism disorders. The results of this evaluation were not available to the authors at the time of manuscript preparation. At 6-week outpatient follow-up, repeat imaging demonstrated regression of the pseudocyst to approximately 3 cm, and the patient remained asymptomatic.

Discussion

HTGP is an uncommon but potentially severe form of acute pancreatitis that typically occurs when triglyceride levels exceed 1000 mg/dL (SI: 11.3 mmol/L) [1, 2]. Although the exact mechanism remains incompletely understood, pancreatic hydrolysis of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins leads to release of toxic free fatty acids, resulting in endothelial injury, pancreatic ischemia, and inflammation [2, 4]. In severe hypertriglyceridemia, increased plasma viscosity caused by chylomicron accumulation may further impair pancreatic microcirculation [4].

This case highlights a key diagnostic challenge in HTGP, namely severe lipemia leading to interference with routine biochemical assays and delayed laboratory confirmation. Severe lipemia is known to interfere with spectrophotometric laboratory assays and may lead to delayed or unobtainable results, including serum amylase, lipase, electrolytes, and liver function tests [5, 6]. In such situations, dilution methods or specialized techniques such as ultracentrifugation may be required to enable accurate measurement. In this case, the combination of markedly lipemic serum and the patient's clinical presentation substantially increased suspicion for HTGP, prompting early empiric insulin therapy before biochemical confirmation was available. Importantly, the differential diagnosis of grossly lipemic serum is essentially restricted to severe hypertriglyceridemia, making this a highly informative clinical finding. Recognition of this characteristic appearance can substantially narrow the differential diagnosis and support timely initiation of appropriate therapy when routine biochemical analysis is delayed or compromised by lipemia.

The severity of hypertriglyceridemia in this case raises the possibility of an underlying genetic or multifactorial disorder of lipid metabolism. Although secondary factors such as dietary excess were present, triglyceride levels of this magnitude are uncommon without underlying susceptibility affecting lipoprotein metabolism. Current evidence suggests that many patients with extreme hypertriglyceridemia represent a multifactorial chylomicronemia phenotype rather than a purely monogenic disorder [8]. Genetic testing was not performed during hospitalization, and outpatient evaluation was recommended; therefore, an underlying inherited component cannot be excluded.

Although hypertriglyceridemia is a well-recognized cause of acute pancreatitis, the degree of triglyceride elevation does not consistently correlate with the initial clinical severity. In this case, despite triglyceride levels reaching 6300 mg/dL, the patient presented with relatively mild abdominal symptoms and stable hemodynamics. This dissociation between biochemical severity and clinical presentation has been previously described in HTGP and reflects the heterogeneous clinical expression of the disease [1, 4, 6]. This variability underscores the importance of maintaining clinical suspicion for hypertriglyceridemia even in patients with nonspecific or mild initial presentations, particularly when other common etiologies of acute pancreatitis are not evident.

The patient's initial laboratory findings demonstrated marked hemoconcentration, leukocytosis, and thrombocytosis. Hemoconcentration has been associated with increased severity in acute pancreatitis and may reflect significant intravascular volume depletion secondary to third-space fluid losses [9]. Additionally, the observed hyponatremia was most consistent with pseudohyponatremia caused by severe lipemia, a recognized laboratory artifact resulting from indirect ion-selective electrode measurement techniques [6].

Management of HTGP remains controversial, particularly regarding the optimal triglyceride-lowering strategy. Therapeutic plasma exchange has been proposed for rapid triglyceride removal; however, its availability remains limited, and evidence regarding mortality benefit is inconclusive [7, 10]. In contrast, insulin infusion is widely available, cost-effective, and physiologically rational through activation of lipoprotein lipase, thereby accelerating chylomicron clearance [2, 4, 11]. In our case, empiric insulin therapy was initiated prior to definitive biochemical confirmation because of persistent laboratory limitations and high clinical suspicion. This approach resulted in rapid triglyceride reduction without complications.

An additional noteworthy aspect of this case was the development of a pancreatic pseudocyst despite early treatment and an initially mild clinical course. Pancreatic pseudocysts are recognized local complications of acute pancreatitis and may occur even in clinically improving patients [12]. Conservative management was favored in this case because the patient remained clinically stable and serial imaging demonstrated spontaneous regression during follow-up.

Finally, detailed dietary history revealed chronic excessive fat intake, which may have acted as a precipitating factor in the setting of probable underlying multifactorial chylomicronemia with genetic susceptibility.

Overall, this case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for HTGP in patients with markedly lipemic samples, particularly when laboratory evaluation is delayed or unavailable due to assay interference. In such settings, early ICU-based empiric insulin therapy may be initiated safely and effectively even prior to biochemical confirmation.
