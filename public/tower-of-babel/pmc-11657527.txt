Characterization of Guest DNA Transport and Adsorption within Host Porous Protein Crystals
Authors: Szuhsuan Chen, Julius D. Stuart, Brian Munsky, Christopher D. Snow
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Nucleic acid transport through protein-based pores is a well-characterized phenomenon due in part to advancements in nanopore sequencing. A less studied area is nucleic acid transport through extended protein-based channels, where the additional surface area and increased contact time allow for the study of prolonged binding interactions. Porous protein crystals composed of “CJ”, a putative polyisoprenoid-binding protein from Campylobacter jejuni, represent a favorable, highly ordered material for studying DNA transport and binding/unbinding along protein-based channels. These crystals adopt a hexagonal prism habit and contain a densely packed hexagonal array of 13 nm diameter axial nanopores that run from the top to the bottom of the crystal. After cross-linking, the crystals are easily manipulated for experimentation. An adsorption isotherm between host crystals and guest double-stranded 8 base pair DNA (8mer) revealed a high equilibrium adsorption constant of 206 ± 30 L/g. Fluorescence confocal microscopy tracked the loading of guest DNA into host crystals predominately along the major axial crystal nanopores. Four different computational models based on the finite volume (FV) method were assessed to model the transport process for guest 8mer and 15mer dsDNA loading into empty host crystals in terms of fundamental parameters, such as the intrapore diffusion constant. Fitting the models to the data revealed that the most basic FV model sufficed to describe the observed loading behavior, characterized by a single effective diffusion coefficient. Leveraging Fick’s first law, we more directly fit a numerical range for the observed intrapore diffusion coefficient as a function of time, position within the crystal, and relative guest concentration. This new transport analysis strategy was applied to both out-of-equilibrium loading and fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) experiments. The intrapore diffusion constants are comparable between 8mer and 15mer dsDNA and were found to be 2 orders of magnitude faster for DNA loading into empty crystals than that observed in FRAP experiments, which averaged (10 ± 4) × 10–11 cm2/s.

Introduction

Tough cross-linked protein matrices, such as CJ crystals, that can uptake and potentially protect otherwise vulnerable nucleic acid cargo may find diverse applications, including edible barcodes1 or the delivery of therapeutic macromolecules.2 However, to enable the rational design of new materials in this family, it is essential to understand and quantitatively model transport processes, such as guest nucleic acid uptake and release. Nucleic acid pore-mediated transport is a phenomenon in both biological and synthetic settings.3,4 For example, mRNA enters the cytosolic space via the nuclear pore complex preceding eukaryotic translation.5 Also, single-stranded DNA traverses a protein-delimited nanopore during nanopore sequencing.3 Nucleic acid transport through porous media is also commonly encountered in both bench and applied research settings, including but not limited to separating DNA based on size and characterizing ground aquifers, respectively.6,7

Conventional porous media possess several limitations that frustrate the study of nucleic acid transport. Specifically, researchers often have little control, if any, of the pore length and shape traversed by the nucleic acid and physicochemical composition of the porous environment. In contrast, the solvent channels of a protein crystal are well-defined and highly repetitive. Atomistic knowledge of the pore environment may ultimately promote an elevated understanding of the biophysics influencing the transport of guest nucleic acids through host porous media.

Porous protein crystals provide a novel, precisely known pore environment for studying guest DNA transport.8 The “CJ” porous protein crystals employed herein are derived from cj0420, a putative polyisoprenoid-binding protein from Campylobacter jejuni (Figures S1 and S2 of the Supporting Information). The modified protein, which lacks the native Sec/SPI signal peptide and is augmented with a C-terminal hexa-His tag forms transparent hexagonal prism crystals comprised of an array of axial nanopores that are 13 nm in diameter (Figure 1).9

Due to the crystalline nature, X-ray diffraction (XRD) can be used to determine the ordered structure components of the CJ nanopore environment with atomic resolution [see Protein Data Bank (PDB) entry 5W17]. Moreover, given that the nanopores are lined with amino acid side chains, recombinant protein variants can be crystallized that alter the physicochemical composition of the nanopore walls, thereby altering guest DNA loading.8 Together, these characteristics make porous protein crystals a privileged porous medium for studying nucleic acid pore-mediated transport and adsorption.

Previous work analyzing guest molecular transport through host crystals includes using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) for capturing anisotropic guest fluorescein transport into host lysozyme crystals.2,10,11 Additionally, species ranging from amino acids12 and entire proteins13 to enzymatic substrates14,15 have been soaked in porous protein crystals. Ligand soaking into crystals was previously described as a scaffold-assisted structure determination tool in the context of drug discovery and optimization.16 Fluorescence confocal microscopy has been used for visualizing guest loading into host crystals,2,17 in addition to quantifying the intracrystal guest concentration and pH.18 Guest diffusion within porous microparticles has also been described using fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP),19 and guest loading into host crystals exhibits hindered diffusion due to adsorption, as previously described for proteins within porous agarose gel.20,21 A computational model for simulating guest loading and binding into protein crystals was generated and found highly reflective of experimental loading data sets.22

In 2017, Hartje et al. described the transport and binding of gold nanoparticles into host CJ protein crystals.23 Guest nanoparticle diffusion within crystal nanopores was found to be time-dependent and attenuated relative to bulk diffusion. While previous work has described guest molecular diffusion through protein crystals, to the best of our knowledge, no work has quantitatively examined guest nucleic acid transport within porous protein crystals.

This work also communicates the first quantification of the strikingly strong adsorption of the guest nucleic acid transport into host CJ crystals. An adsorption isotherm was measured and fit with the Langmuir model for determining an equilibrium adsorption constant. Competing models, such as the Freundlich model, provided a slightly inferior fit (Figure S3 of the Supporting Information). Fluorescence confocal microscopy was used to image the uptake of guest nucleic acids into empty host crystals. Utilizing Fick’s first law, we computed the effective diffusion coefficient by integrating confocal imaging data across spatial and temporal dimensions. This coefficient was subsequently corroborated using a simple finite volume (FV) model. Specifically, we wrote a new FV model code in Python (GitHub: https://github.com/Ashlyn303/Biophysics.git) and validated the new code via a comparison to FiPy (Table S1 and Figure S4 of the Supporting Information).24 Our custom code allowed us to test four FV models of increasing sophistication, but the more complex FV models provided negligible additional accuracy (Table S2 and Figure S5 of the Supporting Information). To assess the FV models, we quantified the variance from the experimental ground truth by summing the squared differences between the observed and predicted fluorescence intensities of the guest DNA within the crystal matrix over time and position. Although model 3, which involves three fit parameters, could potentially provide a superior numerical fit compared to the single-parameter model 1, we found no statistically significant improvement in fit quality. Therefore, despite the increased complexity, we favor the more parsimonious model 1 as the preferred model (refer to Figure S5 of the Supporting Information for more details). To assess the steady-state transport dynamics of DNA in equilibrated crystals with pre-existing DNA, we also applied Fick’s first law and a multi-species FV model to fit the transport of both bleached and unbleached DNA species in FRAP experiments.

Methods

Protein Expression and Porous Crystal Growth

The target gene CJ was derived from the CJ0 gene vector (GenBank ID Cj0420 and PDB ID 2FGS) obtained from the Protein Structure Initiative: Biology-Materials Repository. To facilitate uniform expression and purification, the CJ0 gene underwent codon optimization, and its periplasmic signaling peptide was removed, resulting in the CJ gene. This modified gene was then encoded in the pSB3 expression vector, incorporating a C-terminal 6× His tag. Expression of the CJ protein was carried out in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) pLysS cells using a glucose/lactose induction system. The cells were cultured at 17 °C for 36 h. Following expression, the cells were lysed via sonication and the CJ protein was purified using nickel affinity capture on a HisTrap HPTM column. A single chromatography step proved to be sufficient to achieve the purity required for crystallization. The purified CJ protein underwent buffer exchange into a solution containing 0.15 M NaCl, 10 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazine-1-ethane-sulfonic acid (HEPES), and 10% glycerol at pH 7.5. It was then concentrated to 15 mg/mL and stored at −20 °C. CJ crystals were grown overnight using the sitting drop vapor diffusion method. The crystallization was performed at 20 °C in conditions of 3.3–3.6 M ammonium sulfate (AmSO4) and 100 mM bis-tris at pH 7.0.8,25

Cross-Linking

The cross-linking procedure began with a washing step, where crystals were transferred to a solution of 4.2 M trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) and 175 mM H2SO4 at pH 7.5 for 1 h to remove any residual CJ monomers. The first cross-linking step involved transferring the washed crystals into a fresh mixture of the same composition, with the addition of 100 mM 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) and 50 mM imidazole, for 1 h. The second cross-linking step used a different solution comprising 5.0 M NaCl and 200 mM 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (MES) at pH 5.5, supplemented with 200 mM EDC, 100 mM imidazole, and 50 mM sodium malonate. This step lasted for 2 h. To quench the cross-linking reaction, the CJ crystals were finally transferred to a solution of 5.0 M NaCl and 100 mM borate buffer at pH 10.0 for 30 min.8,25

Adsorption Isotherm

For each replicate,1−3 at least six EDC cross-linked hexagonal prism crystals were imaged using Motic software for measuring individual crystal size that allowed for calculation of the total crystal volume and mass (Figure 2A). Crystals were transferred to a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tube containing 200 μL of incrementally increasing FAM-labeled 8mer concentrations: 0.625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, and 10 μM. The solution was covered with 75 μL of hexane to reduce solvent evaporation. The guest 8mer concentration in solution was recorded using a Qubit 2 fluorometer. Following incubation and shaking for 3–4 days to achieve equilibrium, the final guest solution concentration was recorded. Approximately half of the guest solution (100 μL) was removed and replaced with a more concentrated guest solution followed by reinitiation of incubation and shaking. Using the decline in solution guest DNA amount during incubation, the crystal adsorbed guest DNA mass was calculated, normalized to the total crystal mass, and plotted as a function of the equilibrium guest concentration. The adsorbed mass (mg/g) was further converted into an estimated number of 8mer dsDNA duplexes per unit cell nanopore (UCN), which is the volume of a 13 nm diameter cylinder with the height of 5 nm, inset within the CJ unit cell (Figure S6 of the Supporting Information).

(B) Crystal dimensions were used, along with the unit cell volume provided by the 2FGS PDB file, to estimate the total crystal mass. (C) Adsorption isotherm results plotted (blue discs) were fit well by a Langmuir curve (line). The mass loading implies a high number of 8mer dsDNA duplexes per UCN.

Confocal Microscopy

Individual EDC cross-linked CJ crystals (three replicates; Figure S7 of the Supporting Information) were transferred to a microwell containing 0.4% agarose for immobilization during imaging while also ensuring that the crystal remained in the intended orientation, with the axial nanopores parallel to the confocal image plane. Following immobilization, 4 μL of 500 nM 8mer or 15mer in TE buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl and 1 mM EDTA·Na2) at pH 7.5 was added to the microwell and then sealed with transparent tape. Image acquisition was initiated following the addition of guest solution. Images were taken at various planes within the crystals every 2 min for 30 min. Loading experiments were performed on a Nikon Eclipse Ti confocal microscope equipped with an Andor iXon Ultra 897U EMCCD camera and observed with a Plan Apo λ 20× objective and 488 nm excitation laser set to 10% power. Images were analyzed using Fiji and custom Python scripts for modeling that are available at GitHub (https://github.com/Ashlyn303/Biophysics.git).

Fluorescence Recovery after Photobleaching (FRAP)

FRAP was conducted on a Nikon Eclipse Ti confocal microscope equipped with a 100× Plan Apo λ oil immersion objective. FRAP was performed with two crystals previously incubated with 10 μM 8mer in TE buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl and 1 mM EDTA·Na2) at pH 7.5 to achieve equilibration for approximately 24 h. Following incubation, the loaded crystal was transferred to fresh TE buffer to remove unbound, excess DNA for approximately 24 h. For each replicate, a ∼10 μm diameter region was exposed to 100% 405 nm light for 30 s to achieve photobleaching, followed by observation of recovery at 1% 488 nm light for 1 h (Figure 3). Images were analyzed using Fiji and custom Python scripts for modeling (Python script on GitHub: https://github.com/Ashlyn303/Biophysics.git)

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching timelapse images. Loaded crystals were manually oriented to ensure that the nanopores were horizontal in the image plane and perpendicular to the path of the photobleaching laser. Phase 1 presents a crystal replete with green fluorescent double-stranded DNA (dsDNA). During phase 2, the laser selectively bleaches the green fluorescence of the dsDNA, manifesting a darkened zone. In phase 3, there is a positional exchange between fluorescent dsDNA and non-fluorescent dsDNA due to diffusion, which gradually restores fluorescence. Progressing to phase 4, slow restoration of fluorescence continues with less frequent imaging.

Results

Adsorption Isotherm

An equilibrium adsorption isotherm experiment was conducted using a collection of porous protein crystals. The dimensions of individual hexagonal prism crystals were first measured (side length and height; Figure 2A) using a stereozoom microscope to calculate the crystal volume (Figure 2B) and implied crystal mass. These crystals were incubated with guest DNA at varying initial concentrations, and the free DNA concentration in the supernatant was recorded on a Qubit 2 fluorometer before and after reaching equilibrium. The ratio of adsorbed DNA mass to estimated scaffold mass (q) was plotted as a function of the final equilibrium external guest DNA concentration (C), as shown in Figure 2C. The adsorption isotherm results demonstrate highly favorable binding between the guest DNA molecules and the host crystal environment. Specifically, the experimental data were well-described by a Langmuir adsorption model (R2 = 0.97 ± 0.02, with error estimation via bootstrapping), with the competing Freundlich isotherm exhibiting slightly inferior fits (R2 = 0.95 ± 0.02; Figure S3 of the Supporting Information). The Langmuir fit (and bootstrapped error estimation) yielded an equilibrium adsorption constant of 206 ± 30 L/g (=610 ± 86 mM–1) and a maximum binding capacity of 535 ± 21 mg/g (=52 ± 2 mM) for the host crystal scaffold under these conditions. Notably, the scaffold protein in CJ crystals is at 14 mM concentration within the crystal, implying an upper limit of ∼3.6 8mer DNA per CJ monomer. Per our geometric analysis (Figure S6 of the Supporting Information), this corresponds to approximately 43 ± 2 dsDNA copies per unit cell nanopore. A theoretical analysis based on geometrical considerations (Figure S2 of the Supporting Information) suggests an absolute maximum loading capacity of 105 8mer dsDNA molecules per unit cell, assuming perfect packing. The discrepancy between this theoretical maximum and the experimentally determined value of 43 ± 2 is consistent with a more disordered arrangement of 8mer dsDNA within the crystal nanopores under the actual conditions.

The high binding affinity and substantial loading capacity of DNA within the crystal structure necessitate an examination of the stability of the adsorbed nucleic acids. As discussed by Stuart et al.,1 adsorbed DNA is quite resistant to being washed out, with the notable exception of high concentrations of other nucleic acids and/or mononucleotides, such as ATP. This observation underscores the stability of the DNA–crystal binding under typical conditions while also highlighting potential methods for controlled release or displacement of the adsorbed DNA if desired.

Confocal Microscopy

The fluorescent tag (FAM) on the double-stranded 8mer and 15mer dsDNA guest (Figure S2D of the Supporting Information) enabled fluorescence confocal microscopy monitoring of the loading process into the host protein crystals. Figure 4 displays a time-lapse series of a representative crystal during guest infusion. Loading data sets for additional crystal replicates are shown in Figure S7 of the Supporting Information. The crystals were initially non-fluorescent but then exhibited an increase in fluorescence intensity that was first detected at the crystal edges. As loading progressed over time, the fluorescence front moved toward the interior regions of the crystal. Consistent with the large asymmetry between the 13 nm axial pores and <3 nm lateral pores comprising the host scaffold architecture, the confocal loading data clearly demonstrated that guest DNA transport occurred predominantly along the direction of the major cylindrical nanopores. Furthermore, the crystals appear to approach saturation within about 30 min of incubation under these conditions. Presumably, this relatively rapid approach to equilibrium is facilitated by the uncommonly large pore dimensions of the host protein crystal facilitated by the large ratio of the nanopore size relative to the guest size, in contrast to earlier work with fluorescent dyes diffusing into the smaller pores of hen egg white lysozyme crystals.1

Fluorescence microscopy. Unloaded crystals were manually oriented such that the parallel nanopores ran left to right in the image plane. Initially exhibiting low background fluorescence, fluorophore-labeled DNA was added to the crystal solution. Over time, DNA accumulated within the crystals in a symmetrical fashion that started at the crystal edges containing nanopore openings and gradually increased toward the crystal center.

Processing Confocal Images

Confocal microscopy images of the DNA loading experiment (crystals 1, 2, 3, 6, and 7) were captured at 20× magnification, with the normal of the focal plane perpendicular to the z direction of the nanopores (Figure 5A; see Figure S8 of the Supporting Information for crystals 2, 3, 6, and 7). Over a period of 30 min, digital snapshots of each DNA-filled crystal were recorded at 2 min intervals; a representative subset of these images is shown in Figure 5B. Notably, there was observed variation in the apparent diffusion front across the y axis of diffusion (panels A and B of Figure 5). To investigate, we segmented the crystal images into 20 equally spaced stripes along the y axis. Given crystal symmetry, each stripe terminates along z at the crystal midline. The crystal boundaries (z = near 0 or L) were trimmed to minimize optical artifacts from internal reflection at the crystal edges. Given the optical artifacts associated with the edges of the crystal, having crystals with the total nanopore height above 10 μm is quite important, so that the diffusion pattern in the crystal interior can be accurately observed. To improve the signal-to-noise ratio, the pixel intensity values were averaged columnwise (along the y axis) within each stripe, generating a one-dimensional fluorescence intensity vector at every time point (selected z planes shown in Figure 5C) as well as every z position (selected time points shown in Figure 5E).

While precise conversion of the fluorescence intensity to the fluorophore concentration is exceedingly difficult due to optical effects, converting to the estimated local DNA concentration within the crystals (panels D and F of Figure 5) is nonetheless helpful to provide a rough approximation. To accomplish this, average background intensity outside the crystal was subtracted and the intensity values were converted to the estimated concentration using a fluorescence standard curve (Figure 6) followed by smoothing via a three-parameter spline fit for further analysis. The concentration of 15mer double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) was estimated using the same standard curve (Figure 6) based on the assumption that an equivalent number of fluorophores yields identical light intensity under consistent image acquisition parameters. This approach allows for quantification of the 15mer dsDNA by relating its fluorescence intensity to that of known standards. The fluorescence standard images were taken under the same 20× magnification for a known concentration of FAM-labeled 8mer dsDNA in buffer using identical magnification (20×) and laser power (10.0) as the loading experiment. It is important to note that most results in this report are independent of this standard curve. This independence stems from the application of Fick’s first law, wherein the proportionality constant α in the relationship C = αI (eq S7 of the Supporting Information, where C represents the concentration and I denotes the intensity) cancels out on both sides of the equation. Consequently, the calculated diffusion rate remains unaffected by the absolute concentration values (eqs S7–S12 of the Supporting Information).

Figure 8 suggests that the apparent diffusion constants fall within a range rather than the single line expected for simple diffusion (as shown for synthetic transport data in Table S3 and Figure S12 of the Supporting Information). We propose that this analysis strategy reveals the true complexity present in the experimental data. While an idealized crystal would exhibit uniform properties throughout, our experimental crystals demonstrate anisotropic characteristics due to various factors, such as growth surface interactions, differential cross-linking, and non-uniform guest DNA concentration profiles, resulting in potential transport disparities between the “left” and “right” faces of the crystal (panels E and H of Figure 8). This analysis also makes it very clear which portions of the crystal and which time points have apparent behavior that does not match simple diffusion for crystals 1, 2, 3, 6, and 7 (more details in Figures S16–S19 of the Supporting Information). To best estimate intrapore diffusion rates, we omitted these anomalous data points. Apparent non-Fickian diffusion behavior tended to occur at very early or late time periods. It is difficult to speculate as to what physical processes might be preventing purely diffusive behavior at these times, but we note that another source of apparent non-simple diffusion occurs in proximity to the photobleached region’s boundaries. In these areas, the fluorescence intensity transitions from increasing to decreasing over time, inducing stochastic fluctuations in the data and potentially leading to numerical artifacts in analysis. These outliers were excluded when calculating the observed range of diffusion constant values.

Figure 9 displays the RA versus DF analysis for both FRAP experiments and shows that the observed fluorescence recovery could be reasonably explained within a relatively narrow range of diffusion constants. Some data subsets arose from pixels near the edge of the bleached area (Figure S20 of the Supporting Information). These regions were excluded (Figure 9) for the subsequent FV model data fitting.

In a case of perfect simple one-dimensional (1D) diffusion (e.g., Figure S15 of the Supporting Information), all RA versus DF points would fall on a line. Here, there is instead a visible variation in the slope, with data points from the same time interval (Δt) often appearing as distinct lines. Therefore, to capture the observed range of slope values, we calculate the slope (diffusion rate constants) implied for all adjacent pairs of data points found for the same time interval. The resulting range of diffusion rate constants obtained from the data in Figure 8, Figures S16 and S17 of the Supporting Information, and Figure 9 is summarized as whisker plots in Figure 10.

The median diffusion rate constants were determined to be (3.0 ± 1.1) × 10–9, (3.2 ± 0.8) × 10–9, and (1.2 ± 0.2) × 10–8 cm2/s for the 8mer loading experiments (crystals 1, 2, and 3, respectively) and (6.1 ± 1.4) × 10–9 and (3.6 ± 0.4) × 10–9 cm2/s for the 15mer loading experiments (crystals 6 and 7, respectively). The apparent diffusion rate of 15mer dsDNA was comparable to 8mer dsDNA. Within the long-time equilibrated and saturated crystal used for FRAP, the apparent diffusion constants were much lower: (1.2 ± 0.4) × 10–10 and (8.7 ± 1.5) × 10–11 cm2/s for the FRAP experiments (crystals 4 and 5, respectively).

Validate the Median Diffusion Rate Constant to Experimental Data via the FV Model

To further validate the diffusion rate constants derived from the Fick’s law approach (eq S13 of the Supporting Information), we post facto predict the known experimental confocal data from each crystal stripe with a simple diffusion FV model using the median diffusion rate constants (Figure 10) for pixels within that stripe. The Dirichlet boundary condition was modeled by fitting a three-parameter spline curve to the boundary concentration as a function of time (panels A, B, G, and H of Figure 11 and panels C and D of Figure 12). FV model quality was assessed for each stripe by quantifying the sum of squared deviations (SSDs) between predicted and actual intensity at each pixel and time point. The stripe models with the best and worst fit are shown in Figure 11 for the 8mer loading experiments (Figures S23 and S24 of the Supporting Information for more FV fitting results), and the results for the FRAP experiments are shown in Figure 12. Both comparisons exhibited reasonable to good agreement with the experimental data. This provides confirmation on the reliability of the diffusion rate constants calculated by the RA versus DF slope method.

Fluorescence Recovery after Photobleaching (FRAP)

To experimentally determine the guest pore diffusion coefficient under equilibrium conditions where the adsorbed DNA concentration is high, FRAP experiments were performed on protein crystals loaded with the fluorescent guest 8mer dsDNA. Immediately following the photobleaching step, a darker circular region was created that gradually recovered fluorescence intensity over time, presumably due to diffusion of unbleached guest molecules into the bleached area and counter diffusion of bleached species outward (Figure 13A). Analysis of the FRAP data using the EasyFRAP online tool yielded an average fluorescence recovery halftime (τ1/2) of (1.9 ± 0.5) × 103 s (∼31 ± 9 min) with an estimated mobile fraction of 0.57 ± 0.07. The corresponding immobile fraction of 0.43 ± 0.07 (∼14 ± 2 dsDNA/UCN) suggests that a fraction of the guest DNA may be bound much more tightly than the population responsible for the observed FRAP recovery (Figure 13), which occupied the first inner layer of the pore and decreases the pore size by 23% that leads to smaller channels for diffusion. This tight binding could reflect strong adsorptive interactions between the DNA and the protein crystal host under saturated loading conditions.

Conclusion

This study communicates the characterization of DNA transport and diffusion within nanoporous protein crystal scaffolds. Equilibrium adsorption isotherm data quantified our earlier empirical observation22 of strong binding interactions between the guest DNA molecules and the host crystal environment. Implementation of a modified Fick’s law equation coupled with finite volume model validation with the experimental data reveals that the complex diffusion behavior could be reasonably summarized by a single effective diffusion rate constant. This new analysis strategy clearly reveals subtle quantitative transport differences within and between crystals. However, the experimental data set was bifurcated into two distinct regimes (loading and FRAP); a substantial portion of the guest concentration range remains unprobed (Figure 14). Further investigations will be necessary to fully elucidate the quantitative relationship between the intrapore diffusion coefficient, D0, and the guest DNA concentration across the intermediate loading regime. The transport modeling approaches developed here allow key molecular details underlying the diffusive transport process (i.e., the apparent intrapore diffusion constant) to be extracted from the observable fluorescence data, benefiting future efforts to rationally engineer nanoporous protein materials for diverse applications.
