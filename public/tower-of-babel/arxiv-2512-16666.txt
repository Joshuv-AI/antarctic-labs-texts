Explicit finite-time illustration of improper unitary evolution for the Klein–Gordon field in de Sitter space
William T. Emond, Christian Käding, Peter Millington

Abstract
It is known that quantum field theories in curved spacetime suffer from a number of pathologies, including the inability to relate states on different spatial slices by proper unitary time-evolution operators. In this article, we illustrate this issue by describing the canonical quantisation of a free scalar field in de Sitter space and explicitly demonstrating that the vacuum at a given time slice is unitarily inequivalent to that at any other time. In particular, we find that, if both background and Hamiltonian dynamics are taken into account, this inequivalence holds even for infinitesimally small time steps and not only in the asymptotic time limits.



I
Introduction

The prevailing understanding of our universe and its large-scale evolution is expressed in terms of the
\Lambda
CDM model
Carroll:2000fy
;
Peebles:1984ge
;
Peebles:2002gy
. This, the standard model of cosmology, see Ref.
Bull:2015stt
for a review, describes a spatially flat universe whose energy budget is composed of baryonic matter, radiation, cold dark matter (CDM) — modelled as a pressureless perfect fluid — and a cosmological constant (
\Lambda
). Although there are some tensions (see, e.g., Ref.
Perivolaropoulos:2021jda
), with the Hubble tension
Planck:2018vyg
;
Riess:2019cxk
being a notable example, the predictions of the
\Lambda
CDM model are in excellent agreement with observational data. Given the assumptions of spatial homogeneity and isotropy, the coarse-grained geometry of our universe (on scales
\gtrsim\hskip-2.5pt100\,{\rm Mpc}
) is described by the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) metric
2012MNRAS.425..116S
;
Laurent:2016eqo
;
Ntelis:2017nrj
. Thus, when taken alongside a period of inflation
Guth:1980zm
;
Sato:1980yn
;
Starobinsky:1980te
;
Linde:1981mu
;
Albrecht:1982wi
;
Linde:1983gd
, which precedes the hot big bang phase, both the early- and late-time spacetime geometries of our universe are (quasi-)de Sitter. In the case of the early-time accelerated expansion, the necessity that inflation ends means that the geometry cannot be pure de Sitter, while, in the case of the late-time accelerated expansion, we might anticipate that the evolution will lead to a geometry that is asymptotically pure de Sitter, at least if dark energy is attributable to a cosmological constant term in the Einstein–Hilbert action of general relativity (GR), as in
\Lambda
CDM.

At low energies or, equivalently, at large distance scales (relative to the Planck scale), gravity can be well described by classical field theory, and GR continues to stand up to experimental and observational scrutiny as the prevailing candidate. Within GR, the cosmological evolution is dictated by the constituents of the Universe’s energy budget and their respective equations of state, be they matter, radiation, curvature, or something more exotic. Whereas the quantum description of gravity remains an open question, quantum field theory (QFT) provides us with an elementary description of many of the known and potential constituents of the Universe. Since the scales at which quantum effects become relevant for these constituents can be significantly lower than the Planck scale, we are, from the perspective of effective field theory, justified in considering their quantum dynamics in classical but no-less trivial gravitational backgrounds — QFT in curved spacetime.

However, there are known issues with the Schrödinger-picture description in general curved spacetimes
Helfer:1996my
;
Torre:1997zs
;
Torre:1998eq
;
Cortez:2011sr
;
Cortez:2012cf
;
Agullo:2015qqa
;
Kozhikkal:2023ang
. In particular, it is understood that states on different spatial slices are not related by a unitary transformation, and Ref.
Giddings:2025abo
extends this issue even to general quantum dynamical geometries. Due to its relevance during the periods of early- and late-time accelerated expansion, QFT in de Sitter space has been a pertinent example. Notably, Ref.
Anderson:2017hts
has considered a scalar field in a Bunch–Davies vacuum in an expanding spatial section of de Sitter and concluded that there is a constant decay of the vacuum. In the infinite cosmological time limit, this decay leads to a vanishing overlap of two vacuum in- and out-states and consequently to a unitary inequivalence of the vacua. It should be noted that similar problems can also appear in QFTs in flat spacetime. For example, Refs.
Blasone:1995zc
;
Blasone:2025atj
demonstrated this in the case of neutrino mixing, where the flavour and mass representations are unitarily inequivalent in the infinite-volume limit.

In the present article, we follow a similar line of investigation. More precisely, we attempt to canonically quantise the Klein–Gordon field in de Sitter space, to introduce a Fock space and to calculate its transition matrix elements. As expected from the above discussions, this will not go to plan, and the aim of this article is to illustrate through an explicit calculation that the unitary time-evolution operator obtained in this way is not proper in the sense that it maps between unitarily inequivalent representations of the canonical algebra, such that the overlap of vacua at two infinitesimally close time slices is zero in the infinite-volume limit. The immediate consequence is that correlation functions calculated on the so-evolved vacuum states will necessarily violate the Hadamard condition, except at the initial condition
Kay:1988mu
. We interpret our result as a manifestation of Haag’s theorem
Haag:1955ev
in de Sitter space QFT.

The remainder of this article is organised as follows. In Sec.
II
, we review the classical Klein–Gordon scalar field theory in de Sitter space. Section
III
builds upon this to present a canonical quantisation of a scalar field on a classical de Sitter background spacetime. Next, in Sec.
IV
, we construct the Fock basis in the interaction picture and study its time evolution. From computing the vacuum persistence amplitude, we are able to illustrate explicitly that there is zero overlap between vacuum states and infinitesimally close time slices, and that we therefore lack a proper unitary evolution.

II
Scalar field theory in de Sitter

We focus on the following classical action:

S[\phi,g_{\mu\nu}]\ =\ \frac{M^{2}_{\text{Pl}}}{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\;\Big(R\>-\>2\Lambda\Big)\>+\>S_{\phi}[\phi,g_{\mu\nu}]\;,

(1)

with

S_{\phi}[\phi,g_{\mu\nu}]\ =\ \int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\mathscr{L}_{\phi}\ =\ \int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\mathcal{L}_{\phi}\;,

(2)

and

\mathcal{L}_{\phi}\ \coloneqq\ \sqrt{-g}\,\mathscr{L}_{\phi}\ =\ -\sqrt{-g}\,\Big[\frac{1}{2}\,g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\phi\nabla_{\nu}\phi\>+\>\frac{1}{2}\xi R\phi^{2}\>+\>V(\phi)\Big]\;,

(3)

which describes a real classical scalar field
\phi=\phi(x)
coupled to Einstein gravity through the metric
g_{\mu\nu}(x)
and non-minimally to the Ricci scalar
R
, via the dimensionless coupling
\xi
. Herein,
\Lambda>0
is the cosmological constant and
V(\phi)
is the potential of the scalar field. We use the ‘mostly plus’ signature convention for the metric
g_{\mu\nu}
, i.e.,
(-,+,+,+)
, and hereafter work in units where the reduced Planck mass
M_{\text{Pl}}=(16\pi G)^{-1/2}
is set to unity (such that
\phi
is measured in units of
M_{\text{Pl}}
).

With a view to quantising the scalar theory, we assume the spacetime
(\mathcal{M},\,g_{\mu\nu})
is globally hyperbolic, such that
\mathcal{M}
can be foliated into spacelike Cauchy surfaces. This enables us to introduce the classical momentum field

\pi_{\phi}\ =\ \frac{\delta S_{\phi}}{\delta(\nabla_{0}\phi)}\ =\ \sqrt{|h|}\,n^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}\phi\;,

(4)

conjugate to the scalar field
\phi
, wherein
h_{ij}
is the induced metric on a spacelike hypersurface
\Sigma
, and
n^{\mu}=-\sqrt{-g_{00}}\,g^{\mu 0}
the (future-directed) unit normal vector to this surface.

From Eqs. (
1
) and (
3
), it follows that the metric and scalar-field equations of motion are

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}\ =\ T^{(\phi)}_{\mu\nu}\>-\>\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}\;,

(5a)

\displaystyle\left(\Box\>-\>\xi R\right)\phi\>-\>V^{\prime}(\phi)\ =\ 0\>,

(5b)

where
G_{\mu\nu}\coloneqq R_{\mu\nu}-g_{\mu\nu}R/2
is the Einstein tensor (in which
R_{\mu\nu}
is the Ricci tensor),
\Box\coloneqq g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\mu}\left(\sqrt{-g}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\phi\right)
is the d’Alembertian operator acting on
\phi
(written in terms of the covariant derivative
\nabla_{\mu}
),
V^{\prime}(\phi)\coloneqq\partial V(\phi)/\partial\phi
, and

\displaystyle T^{(\phi)}_{\mu\nu}\ =\ \nabla_{\mu}\phi\nabla_{\nu}\phi-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}\big[g^{\alpha\beta}\nabla_{\alpha}\phi\nabla_{\beta}\phi\>+\>2V(\phi)\big]+\xi\big[G_{\mu\nu}\>-\>\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\>+\>g_{\mu\nu}\Box\big]\phi^{2}

(6)

is the (metric) energy-momentum tensor of the scalar field.
The Klein–Gordon inner product
\braket{\ ,\ }
between any two solutions
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
to Eq. (
5b
) is defined on a spacelike hypersurface, as

\langle\phi_{1},\;\phi_{2}\rangle\ =\ \int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}^{3}x\sqrt{|h|}\,n^{\mu}\left(\phi_{1}\nabla_{\mu}\phi_{2}-\phi_{2}\nabla_{\mu}\phi_{1}\right)\ =\ \int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}^{3}x\left(\phi_{1}\pi_{\phi_{2}}-\phi_{2}\pi_{\phi_{1}}\right)\;,

(7)

which is independent of the choice of hypersurface
\Sigma
.

In the analysis presented in this paper, we will assume that
\braket{T^{(\phi)}_{\mu\nu}}=0
and neglect the backreaction of the scalar field on the metric, allowing us to fix the classical background geometry to that of de Sitter space, such that
R=12H^{2}
, where
H^{-1}
is the characteristic length scale of de Sitter, whose inverse we identify with the Hubble parameter.

With cosmological contexts in mind, we choose a flat slicing, foliating de Sitter space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{dS}}
into a continuous set of spatially flat hypersurfaces
\Sigma_{\tau}
, parametrised by the time coordinate
\tau
, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{dS}}=\underset{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}{\bigcup}\Sigma_{\tau}
.
Furthermore, we shall work in a comoving coordinate system
x^{\mu}=(\tau,\mathbf{x})
, with the time coordinate given by conformal time
\tau
, which is related to the physical or cosmic time
t
as

\tau(t)\ =\ -\int_{t}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}}{a(t^{\prime})}\ =\ -\frac{1}{a_{0}H}\,e^{-Ht}\ \in(-\infty,0)\;,

(8)

where
a(t)=a_{0}e^{Ht}
is the cosmic scale factor. The scale factor is then given (with a slight abuse of notation) by

a\ =\ a(\tau)\ =\ -\>\frac{1}{H\tau}\;.

(9)

A particularly desirable aspect of choosing comoving coordinates and conformal time is that the conformal flatness of the background solution to Eq. (
5a
) is manifest, i.e.,

g_{\mu\nu}\ =\ a^{2}\,\eta_{\mu\nu}\;,

(10)

where
\eta_{\mu\nu}
is the Minkowski metric. Accordingly, the Klein–Gordon equation [Eq. (
5b
)] takes the form

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}^{2}\phi\>-\>\nabla^{2}\phi\>+\>2aH\partial_{\tau}\phi\>+\>a^{2}\left(12\xi H^{2}\phi\>+\>V^{\prime}(\phi)\right)\ =\ 0\;,

(11)

where
\nabla^{2}
is the Laplacian, and
\partial_{\tau}\coloneqq\partial/\partial\tau
. By rescaling the field
\phi=a^{-1}\tilde{\phi}
, we can remove the linear time derivative to arrive at a simplified equation in terms of
\tilde{\phi}
:

\partial_{\tau}^{2}\tilde{\phi}\>-\>\nabla^{2}\tilde{\phi}\>+\>2a^{2}H(-1+6\xi)\tilde{\phi}\>+\>a^{4}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\phi}}\left(V(a^{-1}\tilde{\phi})\right)\ =\ 0\;.

(12)

Given this choice of foliation, the conjugate scalar field momentum
\pi_{\phi}
becomes

\pi_{\phi}\ =\ a^{2}\,\partial_{\tau}\phi\;.

(13)

The scalar-field Hamiltonian
H^{0}_{\phi}
can then be constructed as the Legendre transform of
\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
(cf. Eq. (
3
)), giving

\displaystyle H^{0}_{\phi}(\tau)\

\displaystyle=\ \int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,\left(\pi_{\phi}\,\partial_{\tau}\phi-\mathcal{L}_{\phi}\right)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,\Big[\frac{1}{a^{2}}\pi^{2}_{\phi}+a^{2}(\nabla\phi)^{2}+12a^{4}\xi H^{2}\phi^{2}+2a^{4}V(\phi)\Big]\;.

(14)

From this we can readily confirm that
H^{0}_{\phi}(\tau)
satisfies the expected Hamilton equations, as required:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta H^{0}_{\phi}}{\delta\pi_{\phi}}\ =

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{a^{2}}\pi_{\phi}\ =\ \partial_{\tau}\phi\;,

(15a)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta H^{0}_{\phi}}{\delta\phi}\ =

\displaystyle\ -a^{2}\Big[\nabla^{2}\phi-a^{2}\left(12\xi H^{2}\phi+V^{\prime}(\phi)\right)\Big]\ =\ -a^{2}\Big[\partial_{\tau}^{2}\phi+2aH\partial_{\tau}\phi\Big]\ =\ -\partial_{\tau}\pi_{\phi}\;.

(15b)

In this Hamiltonian formulation, we define the Poisson bracket between two functions
A=A[\phi(x),\pi_{\phi}(x)]
and
B=B[\phi(x),\pi_{\phi}(x)]
of the canonical variables
\phi
and
\pi_{\phi}
at equal times as

\displaystyle\{A,\,B\}\ \coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \{A[\phi(x),\pi_{\phi}(x)],\,B[\phi(y),\pi_{\phi}(y)]\}\Big|_{x^{0}=y^{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{z}\left(\frac{\delta A}{\delta\phi(z)}\frac{\delta B}{\delta\pi_{\phi}(z)}\>-\>\frac{\delta A}{\delta\pi_{\phi}(z)}\frac{\delta B}{\delta\phi(z)}\right)\Bigg|_{x^{0}=y^{0}=z^{0}}\;.

(16)

It follows that the canonical variables satisfy

\displaystyle\{\phi(\tau,\mathbf{x}),\,

\displaystyle\phi(\tau,\mathbf{y})\}\ =\ \{\pi_{\phi}(\tau,\mathbf{x}),\,\pi_{\phi}(\tau,\mathbf{y})\}\ =\ 0\;,

\displaystyle\{\phi(\tau,\mathbf{x}),\,\pi_{\phi}(\tau,\mathbf{y})\}\ =\ \delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\;,

(17)

such that Hamilton’s equations can be expressed as

\pi_{\phi}\ =\ -a^{2}\{H_{\phi}^{0},\,\phi\}\;,\qquad\partial_{\tau}\pi_{\phi}\ =\ \{H_{\phi}^{0},\,\pi_{\phi}\}\;.

(18)

For a free field with potential

V(\phi)\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\,m^{2}\phi^{2}\;,

(19)

the solution of Eq. (
5b
) can be written in terms of mode functions
v_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)
that satisfy

\partial^{2}_{\tau}v_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)+2a(\tau)H\partial_{\tau}v_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)+(\mathbf{k}^{2}+a^{2}(\tau)(m^{2}+12\xi H^{2}))v_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)=0\;,

(20)

where
\mathbf{k}
is the
co-moving
momentum. To extract the instantaneous mode frequency, we redefine the mode functions
v_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)=a^{-1}\tilde{v}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)
, leading to a wave equation of the form

\partial^{2}_{\tau}\tilde{v}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)+\omega^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\tilde{v}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\ =\ 0\;,

(21)

such that

\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)=\sqrt{\mathbf{k}^{2}+a^{2}(\tau)m^{2}_{\textrm{eff}}}\;,

(22)

with effective mass
m^{2}_{\textrm{eff}}=m^{2}+12\xi H^{2}-\frac{\ddot{a}(\tau)}{a^{3}(\tau)}=m^{2}+2H^{2}(6\xi-1)
, where we have used that
\partial_{\tau}H=0=\frac{\ddot{a}(\tau)}{a^{2}(\tau)}-2a(\tau)H^{2}
.

The solutions for the redefined mode functions
\tilde{v}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)
are then

\tilde{v}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\ =\ \sqrt{\frac{H\pi}{4}}\,a(\tau)^{-1/2}\,H^{(2)}_{\nu}(-|\mathbf{k}|\tau)\;,

(23)

where
H^{(2)}_{\nu}(z)
is a Hankel function of the second kind,
\nu=\sqrt{\frac{9}{4}-\frac{m^{2}+12H^{2}\xi}{H^{2}}}
, and the normalisation is fixed by the requirement that the vacuum state in the asymptotic past (
\tau\rightarrow-\infty
) is Bunch-Davies. Consequently,

v_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\ =\ \sqrt{\frac{H\pi}{4}}\,a(\tau)^{-3/2}\,H^{(2)}_{\nu}(-|\mathbf{k}|\tau)\;.

(24)

In the next section, we move to an equivalent description in terms of canonical operator algebra, wherein we will recover the same mode functions.

III
Canonical quantisation in de Sitter

Our aim is to construct a canonical operator description of the Klein–Gordon field in de Sitter space. The key difference compared to the familiar canonical quantisation in Minkowski spacetime is the explicit time dependence of the Hamiltonian, which results from the background evolution in our flat slicing of de Sitter space. This will allow us to illustrate the unitary inequivalence of representations of the canonical algebra at different times explicitly.

III.1
A Schrödinger-like picture

We begin by constructing a modified Schrödinger picture in which the states carry the time dependence due to the Hamiltonian evolution, while the canonical operators, viz. the field and conjugate-momentum operators, are time independent. Basis states are usually time independent in the Schrödinger picture. However, since the Hamiltonian carries explicit time dependence, the basis states of this modified Schrödinger picture are necessarily time dependent. In this way, we are able to isolate the effect of this explicit time dependence.

We quantise the classical scalar field theory discussed in the previous section in terms of the canonical field operators
\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})
and
\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})
. These Schrödinger-picture operators are time independent, satisfying

\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\partial_{\tau}\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})=0\;.

(25)

Following the canonical approach, we map Poisson brackets of classical fields to commutators of their associated operator fields, i.e.,
\{A,\;B\}\mapsto\frac{1}{i}[A,\;B]
(in natural units), such that Eq. (
II
) maps to the canonical operator algebra

\displaystyle\big[\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x}),\,\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{y})\big]\ =\ 0\ =\ \big[\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x}),\,\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{y})\big]\;,

(26a)

\displaystyle\big[\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x}),\,\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{y})\big]\ =\ i\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{x}\>-\>\mathbf{y})\,\hat{\mathds{1}}\;.

(26b)

We shall assume that the expectation value of the field operator

\phi_{\psi}(\tau,\mathbf{x})=\braket{\psi(\tau)|\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})|\psi(\tau)}

(27)

is a solution of the classical Klein–Gordon equation in de Sitter, with initial conditions determined by
\ket{\psi(\tau_{0})}
.

From Eq. (
13
), and imposing that
\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})=0
, we find

\braket{\psi(\tau)|\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})|\psi(\tau)}=a^{2}(\tau)\partial_{\tau}\phi_{\psi}(\tau,\mathbf{x})=\braket{\psi(\tau)|ia^{2}(\tau)[\hat{H}_{S}^{0}(\tau),\,\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})]|\psi(\tau)}\;,

(28)

from which we can infer that

\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})=ia^{2}(\tau)[\hat{H}_{S}^{0}(\tau),\,\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})]\;.

(29)

This agrees with the expected result upon quantising the associated classical relation (
18
). Furthermore, combining Eq. (
25
) with the above equation, enforces

\frac{2H}{a(\tau)}\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})+i[\partial_{\tau}\hat{H}_{S}^{0}(\tau),\,\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})]\ =\ 0\;.

(30)

Returning to the Klein–Gordon equation, and substituting the scalar field expectation value, using the condition (
25
), we can infer the operator equation

\displaystyle\frac{i}{a^{2}(\tau)}\left[\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau),\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\right]+i\left[\partial_{\tau}\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau),\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\right]+\frac{2H}{a(\tau)}\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle-\big[\nabla^{2}-a^{2}(\tau)(m^{2}+12\xi H^{2})\big]\,\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})=0~.

(31)

Employing Eq. (
30
), we then have

\frac{i}{a^{2}(\tau)}\left[\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau),\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\right]-\big[\nabla^{2}-a^{2}(\tau)(m^{2}+12\xi H^{2})\big]\,\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})=0~.

(32)

We can construct the Hamiltonian operator by canonically quantising its classical counterpart (
II
), giving

\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau)\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\Big[\frac{1}{a^{2}(\tau)}\hat{\pi}^{2}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})+a^{2}(\tau)\left((\nabla\hat{\phi}_{S})^{2}(\tau,\mathbf{x})+a^{2}(\tau)(m^{2}+12\xi H^{2})\,\hat{\phi}_{S}^{2}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\right)\Big]\;,

(33)

from which we find

\left[\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau),\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\right]\ =\ -ia^{2}(\tau)\big[\nabla^{2}-a^{2}(\tau)(m^{2}+12\xi H^{2})\big]\,\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\;,

(34)

such that Eq. (
32
) is automatically satisfied. Note that Eqs. (
29
) and (
34
) agree precisely with the quantised Hamilton equations (cf. Eqs. (
15
) and (
18
)).

Now, we take the field operators to have the following plane-wave decompositions:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\ =

\displaystyle\ \int_{\mathbf{k}}\,\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\Big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\>+\>\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\Big]\;,

(35a)

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\ =

\displaystyle\ \int_{\mathbf{k}}\,\varpi_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,\Big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\>-\>\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\Big]\;,

(35b)

wherein we have used the shorthand notation

\int_{\mathbf{k}}\coloneqq\int\!\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}~.

(36)

We require that the creation and annihilation operators
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}^{{\dagger}}(\tau)
and
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)

satisfy the equal-time commutation relations

\displaystyle\big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau),\,\hat{b}_{\mathbf{p},S}(\tau)\big]\ =\ 0\ =\ \big[\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau),\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{p},S}(\tau)\big]\;,

(37a)

\displaystyle\big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau),\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{p},S}(\tau)\big]\ =\ (2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p})\,\hat{\mathds{1}}\;.

(37b)

Using these and the field operator canonical commutation relation (
26
), enforces

\varpi_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\ =\ \frac{-i}{2\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}\;.

(38)

The Hamiltonian operator
\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau)
can then be expressed in terms of the creation and annihilation operators
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}^{{\dagger}}(\tau)
and
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)
, as

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\bigg[\mathds{h}^{+}(\tau)\left(\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)+\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathds{h}^{-}(\tau)\Big(\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\hat{b}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)+\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\Big)\bigg]\;,

(39)

where

\mathds{h}^{\pm}(\tau)\ =\ a^{2}(\tau)\varphi^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\left(\mathbf{k}^{2}+a^{2}(\tau)(m^{2}+12\xi H^{2})\right)\pm\frac{1}{4a^{2}(\tau)\varphi^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}\;.

(40)

Requiring the instantaneous Hamiltonian to be diagonalised (i.e.,
\mathds{h}^{-}(\tau)\overset{!}{=}0
) fixes
\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)
to be

\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)=\frac{1}{a(\tau)\sqrt{2\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}}\;.

(41)

This results in our canonical field variables having plane-wave decompositions

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\ =

\displaystyle\ \int_{\mathbf{k}}\,\frac{1}{a(\tau)\sqrt{2\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}}\Big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\>+\>\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\Big]\;,

(42a)

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\ =

\displaystyle\ \frac{-i}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\,a(\tau)\sqrt{\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}\Big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\>-\>\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\Big]\;,

(42b)

and reduces Eq. (
III.1
) to

\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau)\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,\left(\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)+\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\right)\;.

(43)

As is standard, we would like to normal order the Hamiltonian operator to remove the vacuum energy contribution. However, in principle, there is an issue that arises due to the explicit time dependence of the background, which, as we describe in detail below, generates a mixing of creation and annihilation operators as they evolve in time.

By requiring that
\ket{0,\tau}
corresponds to the instantaneous vacuum state at time
\tau
, we enforce the simultaneous conditions

\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\ket{0,\tau}\ =\ 0\;\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\braket{0,\tau|\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau)|0,\tau}\ =\ 0\;,

(44)

such that our normal-ordering prescription reorders all creation operators
\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)
to the
left
of all annihilation operators
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)
with respect to the instantaneous vacuum at time
\tau
. Note that this is a
time-independent
definition, since
\tau
was chosen arbitrarily, and all operators normal-ordered in this manner remain form-invariant for all
\tau
. The ordering is only undone if we wish to express a given operator in a basis of creation and annihilation operators defined with respect to the vacuum at a different time
\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau
. The normal-ordered Hamiltonian is then (suppressing the normal-ordered notation for convenience)

\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau)\ =\ \int_{\mathbf{k}}\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\;.

(45)

III.2
Background evolution via Bogoliubov transformations

All that remains is to determine the evolution of the creation and annihilation operators
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}^{{\dagger}}(\tau)
and
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)
. This can be inferred by means of Eqs. (
25
) to (
42
), which enforce that

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ -\left(\partial_{\tau}\mathrm{ln}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau))\right)\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\ =\ (\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau))\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\;,

(46a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ -\left(\partial_{\tau}\mathrm{ln}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau))\right)\hat{b}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\ =\ (\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau))\,\hat{b}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\;,

(46b)

where

\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\ =\ -\left(\partial_{\tau}\mathrm{ln}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau))\right)\ =\ \frac{\partial_{\tau}\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}{2\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}+a(\tau)H\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\partial_{\tau}\mathrm{ln}\left(a^{2}(\tau)\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\right)

(47)

and we have used Eq. (
41
). Integrating Eq. (
47
), gives

\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{a^{2}(\tau)}{a^{2}(\tau_{0})}\frac{\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}{\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau_{0})}\right)\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{\tau_{0}^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\frac{\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}{\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau_{0})}\right)\;.

(48)

The solutions to Eq. (
46
) are then

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ c_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})+s_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\;,

(49a)

\displaystyle\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ c_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})+s_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,\hat{b}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\;,

(49b)

where we have defined

c_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\ \coloneqq\ \cosh\big(\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\big)\;,\qquad s_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\ \coloneqq\ \sinh\big(\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\big)\;.

(50)

Next, the hyperbolic trigonometric identities lead immediately to

\displaystyle c^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\>-\>s^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ 1\;,

(51a)

\displaystyle c_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\>+\>s_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ \frac{a(\tau)}{a(\tau_{0})}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}{\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau_{0})}}\;.

(51b)

Given these relations, the canonical variables
\hat{\phi}_{S}
and
\hat{\pi}_{S}
, can be recast into explicitly time-independent form:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\ =

\displaystyle\ \int_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{1}{a(\tau_{0})\sqrt{2\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau_{0})}}\left[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}+\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\,e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\right]\ =\ \hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau_{0},\mathbf{x})\;,

(52a)

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\ =

\displaystyle\ -i\int_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{a(\tau_{0})\sqrt{\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau_{0})}}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}-\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\,e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\right]\ =\ \hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau_{0},\mathbf{x})\;,

(52b)

consistent with the requirement that
\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}_{S}=0=\partial_{\tau}\hat{\pi}_{S}
.

In fact, it can be shown (as we shall demonstrate below) that the time evolution of the creation and annihilation operators can be written in terms of a unitary operator

\hat{S}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ =\ \mathrm{T}\,e^{-i\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}{\rm d}\tau^{\prime}\;\hat{F}_{S}(\tau^{\prime},\tau_{0})}\;,

(53)

with

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ \hat{S}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})\,\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\,\hat{S}^{\dagger}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})\;,

(54a)

\displaystyle\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ \hat{S}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\,\hat{S}^{\dagger}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})\;,

(54b)

and where the Hermitian operator
\hat{F}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})
is determined by

\displaystyle-i\Big[\hat{F}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0}),\,\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\Big]\ =

\displaystyle\ \left[\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\right]\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\;,

(55a)

\displaystyle-i\Big[\hat{F}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0}),\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\Big]\ =

\displaystyle\ \left[\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\right]\,\hat{b}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\;.

(55b)

Its explicit form is

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ =

\displaystyle\ \frac{i}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\left[\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\right]\Big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,\hat{b}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\>-\>\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{i}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\left[\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\right]\Big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\,\hat{b}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\>-\>\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\Big]\;.

(56)

To obtain the second line, we have made use of Eq. (
49
) to rewrite
\hat{F}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})
in terms of the creation and annihilation operators at the initial time
\tau_{0}
and from which we see that
\hat{F}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})
carries only the explicit time dependence encoded in
\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)
. We can therefore compute the time integral in the exponent and hence the time-ordering in Eq. (
53
) is trivial, i.e.,

\hat{S}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ =\ e^{-i\,\hat{G}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})}\;,

(57)

where
\hat{G}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})=\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}{\rm d}\tau^{\prime}\;\hat{F}_{S}(\tau^{\prime},\tau_{0})
.

At this point, we pause to consider the form of the operator
\hat{S}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})
in Eq. (
57
). Since the work of Haag
Haag:1955ev
, it has been known that operators of the form
\hat{G}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})
are not proper. This is in the sense that, while preserving the the canonical algebra, the operator
\hat{S}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})
transforms any given state in the Hilbert space into another that has vanishing overlap with any other state in the Hilbert space. Indeed, we will see later that this results in the vacuum state at time
\tau
having vanishing overlap with the vacuum state at time
\tau+\delta\tau
in the limit of infinite spatial volume, such that our representations of the scalar quantum field theory on de Sitter space are
unitarily inequivalent
on each time slice. We emphasise that this is a consequence of having a time-dependent Hamiltonian (see, e.g., Ref.
Giddings:2025abo
), and not unique to scalar quantum field theory on de Sitter space.

Returning to the operator algebra, we can now write the
unequal-time
commutation relations of the creation and annihilation operators (with
\tau>\tau^{\prime}
), which take the forms

\displaystyle\big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{p},S}(\tau),\,\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau^{\prime})\big]\ =

\displaystyle\ s_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{k})\ =\ \big[\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{p},S}(\tau^{\prime}),\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\big]\;,

(58a)

\displaystyle\big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{p},S}(\tau),\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau^{\prime})\big]\ =

\displaystyle\ c_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})\ =\ \big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{p},S}(\tau^{\prime}),\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\big]\;,

(58b)

wherein the non-commutativity of pairs of annihilation operators at different times is indicative of the unitary inequivalence anticipated from the explicit time dependence of the Hamiltonian. Note that these reduce to Eq. (
37
) for equal times.

We now make use of Ref.
Mitter:1984gt
to decompose
\hat{S}_{S}
[cf. Eq. (
57
)] into a product of three exponential operators:

\hat{S}_{S}\ =\ e^{-\frac{\textrm{Vol}}{4}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\text{ln}(c_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(\tau))}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}}t_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,\hat{K}^{+}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\text{ln}(c_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(\tau))\,\hat{K}^{0}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}}t_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,\hat{K}^{-}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})}\;,

(59)

where we have defined
t_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\coloneqq\tanh(\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau))
and introduced

\displaystyle\hat{K}^{+}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\ \coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\;,

(60a)

\displaystyle\hat{K}^{-}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\ \coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,\hat{b}_{-\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\;,

(60b)

\displaystyle\hat{K}^{0}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\ \coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\;.

(60c)

These operators satisfy the closed algebra

\displaystyle\big[\hat{K}^{-}_{\mathbf{p},S}(\tau),\,\hat{K}^{+}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\big]\ =

\displaystyle\ +4\,(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})\,\hat{K}^{0}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\>+\>2\textrm{Vol}\,(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})\;,

(61a)

\displaystyle\big[\hat{K}^{0}_{\mathbf{p},S}(\tau),\,\hat{K}^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\big]\ =

\displaystyle\ \pm 2\,(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})\,\hat{K}^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\;.

(61b)

Note that
\textrm{Vol}\coloneqq\int_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})}\big|_{\mathbf{x}\rightarrow\mathbf{y}}
corresponds to the spatial volume within the patch we are considering. As this patch becomes infinitely large, the corresponding volume
\textrm{Vol}\rightarrow\infty
. It is this term which indicates the unitary inequivalence of the representations of the canonical algebra at different times, and we return to this point in Sec.
IV
.

III.3
Time evolution of operators and states

At this point, it is helpful to clarify what we mean by implicit and explicit time dependence. The time dependence of the background induces an explicit time dependence in the mode frequency
\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)
, causing the field normalisation (
41
) to become explicitly time dependent. However, in this construction, the requirement that the canonical variables are time independent further gives rise to an implicit time dependence (that is, generated by some operator
\hat{G}_{S}
) in the basis operators, viz., the creation and annihilation operators. A generic operator in the theory will therefore be a function of the spacetime coordinates
x^{\mu}=(\tau,\mathbf{x})
, and the canonical variables
\hat{\phi}_{S}
and
\hat{\pi}_{S}
, thus containing both implicit and explicit time dependence:

\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{S}(x)\ \coloneqq\ \hat{S}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{S}(x,\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau_{0},\mathbf{x}),\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau_{0},\mathbf{x}))\hat{S}_{S}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})\;,

(62)

where
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{S}(x,\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau_{0},\mathbf{x}),\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau_{0},\mathbf{x}))
contains the explicit time dependence of the operator. This will generically satisfy an evolution equation of the form

\partial_{\tau}\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{S}(x)\ =\ -i\Big[\hat{G}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0}),\,\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{S}(x)\Big]\>+\>\hat{S}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})\,(\partial_{\tau}\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{S}(x,\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau_{0},\mathbf{x}),\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau_{0},\mathbf{x}))\,\hat{S}_{S}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})\;.

(63)

Since state vectors undergo unitary evolution with respect to the Hamiltonian in this construction, the density operator
\hat{\rho}_{S}(\tau)
will evolve likewise. Specifically, it will satisfy the Liouville–von Neumann equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\hat{\rho}_{S}(\tau)\ =\ -i\big[\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau),\,\hat{\rho}_{S}(\tau)\big]\;,

(64)

with solution

\hat{\rho}_{S}(\tau)\ =\ \hat{U}(\tau,\tau_{0})\hat{\rho}_{S}(\tau_{0})\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})\;.

(65)

The unitary time-evolution operator has the form

\hat{U}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ =\ \mathrm{T}\exp\Big[-i\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau^{\prime})\Big]\;,

(66)

where the time ordering is necessitated by the explicit time dependence of the Hamiltonian. The latter means that the Hamiltonian does not commute with itself at different times, i.e., [cf. Eq. (
45
)]

\displaystyle\big[\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau),\,\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau^{\prime})\big]\ =

\displaystyle\ \int_{\mathbf{k}}\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau^{\prime})\Big[\hat{K}^{0}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau),\,\hat{K}^{0}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau^{\prime})\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \int_{\mathbf{k}}\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau^{\prime})\sinh\left(2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\right)\Big[\hat{K}^{-}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\>-\>\hat{K}^{+}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\Big]\ \neq\ 0~.

(67)

However, given Eqs. (
49
) and (
60
), we can recast the Hamiltonian in terms of the initial time annihilation and creation operators as

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ \int_{\mathbf{k}}\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\hat{K}^{0}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \int_{\mathbf{k}}\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\Big[\mathrm{Vol}\,s_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(\tau)+\left(c_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(\tau)+s_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(\tau)\right)\hat{K}^{0}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})+s_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)c_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\big(\hat{K}^{-}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})+\hat{K}^{+}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\big)\Big]\;.

(68)

Note that the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{S}^{0}(\tau)
is no longer diagonalised or normal ordered since it is no longer expressed in an instantaneous basis of creation and annihilation operators at time
\tau
.

We can now carry out the integration over time in the exponent of the evolution operator (
66
), since it is simply over the
c
-number (time-dependent) coefficients. We can then remove the time ordering, such that

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ =

\displaystyle\ \exp\Big[-i\int_{\mathbf{k}}\Big(\mathrm{Vol}\,f_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})\>+\>g_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})\,\hat{K}^{0}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})+\>h_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})\,\big(\hat{K}^{-}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\>+\>\hat{K}^{+}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})\big)\Big)\Big]\;,

(69)

where

\displaystyle f_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ =

\displaystyle\ \int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau^{\prime})s_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(\tau^{\prime})\ =\ \frac{1}{2}g_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2}g_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})-\frac{1}{2}\left(\tau\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)+\tau_{0}\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau_{0})\right)+\frac{m}{4H}\,\textrm{ln}\left(\frac{\frac{m}{H\tau}+\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}{\frac{m}{H\tau}-\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}\right)

\displaystyle\ -\frac{m}{4H}\,\textrm{ln}\left(\frac{\frac{m}{H\tau_{0}}+\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau_{0})}{\frac{m}{H\tau_{0}}-\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau_{0})}\right)\;,

(70a)

\displaystyle g_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ =

\displaystyle\ \int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau^{\prime})\left(c_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(\tau^{\prime})+s_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(\tau^{\prime})\right)\ =\ \int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau^{\prime})\cosh(2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{\left(\tau^{4}+2\tau\tau_{0}^{3}-3\tau_{0}^{4}\right)\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau_{0})}{6\tau\tau_{0}^{2}}-\frac{m^{2}(\tau-\tau_{0})^{2}(2\tau+\tau_{0})}{6H^{2}\tau^{3}\tau_{0}\,\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau_{0})}\;,

(70b)

\displaystyle h_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ =

\displaystyle\ \int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau^{\prime})s_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau^{\prime})c_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau^{\prime})\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau^{\prime})\sinh(2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2}g_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})-\frac{(\tau^{3}-\tau_{0}^{3})\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau_{0})}{6\tau_{0}^{2}}\;.

(70c)

Note the violation of time-translational invariance, which is expected on general grounds (see Ref.
Giddings:2025abo
).

III.4
The interaction picture

In this section, we define an interaction picture in which the field operators evolve with respect to the free part of the Hamiltonian and the states instead evolve with respect to its interaction part. Note that the interaction and Heisenberg pictures coincide for the free theory that we treat in this work.

The interaction-picture field operators
\hat{\phi}_{I}(x)
and
\hat{\pi}_{I}(x)
are related to their Schrödinger-picture counterparts via the unitary map

\hat{\phi}_{I}(x)\ =\ \hat{U}_{0}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})\,\hat{\phi}_{S}(x)\,\hat{U}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})\;,\qquad\hat{\pi}_{I}(x)\ =\ \hat{U}_{0}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})\,\hat{\pi}_{S}(x)\,\hat{U}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})\;,

(71)

in which
\hat{U}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})
is the evolution operator given in Eq. (
66
), with
\hat{H}^{0}_{S}(\tau)
given by Eq. (
III.3
). These operators can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{I}(x)\ \coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \hat{\phi}_{I}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\ =\ \int_{\mathbf{k}}\,\frac{1}{a(\tau)\sqrt{2\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}}\Big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\>+\>\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\Big]\;,

(72a)

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{I}(x)\ \coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \hat{\pi}_{I}(\tau,\mathbf{x})\ =\ \int_{\mathbf{k}}\,(-i)a(\tau)\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}{2}}\,\Big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)e^{+i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\>-\>\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\Big]\;,

(72b)

where the interaction-picture creation and annihilation operators are obtained via

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ \hat{U}_{0}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})\,\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,\hat{U}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})\;,

(73a)

\displaystyle\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ \hat{U}_{0}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau)\,\hat{U}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})\;.

(73b)

This map preserves the canonical algebra, and thus
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)
and
\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)
satisfy

\displaystyle\big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau),\,\hat{b}_{\mathbf{p},I}(\tau)\big]\ =\ 0\ =\ \big[\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau),\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{p},I}(\tau)\big]\;,

(74a)

\displaystyle\big[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau),\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{p},I}(\tau)\big]\ =\ (2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p})\;.

(74b)

In addition, we have

\hat{S}_{I}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ =\ \hat{U}^{\dagger}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})\hat{S}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})\hat{U}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ =\ \mathrm{T}\,e^{-i\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\hat{F}_{I}(\tau^{\prime},\tau_{0})}\;,

(75)

with

\hat{F}_{I}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ =\ \frac{i}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}}(\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau))\big[\hat{K}^{-}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)\>-\>\hat{K}^{+}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)\big]\;.

(76)

A given interaction-picture operator
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{I}(x)
evolves according to

\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{\hat{O}}_{I}(x)\ =\ i\big[\hat{H}^{0}_{I}(\tau),\,\mathcal{\hat{O}}_{I}(x)\big]\>+\>\hat{U}_{0}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{\hat{O}}_{S}(x)\hat{U}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})\;,

(77)

where
\hat{H}^{0}_{I}(\tau)
is the free theory Hamiltonian in the interaction picture, given by

\hat{H}^{0}_{I}(\tau)\ =\ \hat{U}_{0}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})\hat{H}_{S}(\tau)\hat{U}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ =\ \int_{\mathbf{k}}\,\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\,\hat{K}^{0}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)\;.

(78)

It is important to note here that
\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{\hat{O}}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})
is the time derivative of the Schrödinger picture operator, as governed by Eq. (
63
).
By definition,
\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})=0
and
\partial_{\tau}\hat{\pi}_{S}(\tau,\mathbf{x})=0
, and the interaction-picture field operators therefore evolve subject to

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}_{I}(x)\ =

\displaystyle\ i\big[\hat{H}^{0}_{I}(\tau),\,\hat{\phi}_{I}(x)\big]\;,

(79a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\hat{\pi}_{I}(x)\ =

\displaystyle\ i\big[\hat{H}^{0}_{I}(\tau),\,\hat{\pi}_{I}(x)\big]\;.

(79b)

Equation (
81
) reproduces the field equations

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}_{I}(x)\ =

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{a^{2}(\tau)}\,\hat{\pi}_{I}(x)\;,

(80a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\hat{\pi}_{I}(x)\ =

\displaystyle\ a^{2}(\tau)\big[\nabla^{2}\>-\>a^{2}(\tau)m_{\textrm{eff}}^{2}\big]\hat{\phi}_{I}(x)\;,

(80b)

in agreement with Eqs. (
11
) and (
79
). Finally, the interaction-picture creation and annihilation operators evolve according to

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ -i\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)\>+\>\left[\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\right]\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)\;,

(81a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ +i\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)\>+\>\left[\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\right]\,\hat{b}_{-\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)\;,

(81b)

with the plane-wave decomposition of the field operators taking the form given in Eq. (
72
).

III.5
Time evolution of the interaction picture operators

Returning to the interaction-picture creation and annihilation operators, we would like to solve their evolution equations (
81
) in a closed form. We start by writing the interaction-picture operators
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)
and
\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)
in terms of the evolution operator
\hat{U}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})
and the background evolution operator
\hat{S}_{S}(\tau,\tau_{0})
, acting on their initial time counterparts
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau_{0})\equiv\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})
and
\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau_{0})\equiv\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},S}(\tau_{0})
, as

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)\ =

\displaystyle\ \hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})\,\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau_{0})\,\hat{\mathcal{W}}(\tau,\tau_{0})\;,

(82a)

\displaystyle\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau)\ =

IV
Constructing a Fock basis in the interaction picture

Now we are in a position to construct the Fock basis and consider its time evolutions, focussing in particular on the instantaneous vacuum states.

We start with the vacuum state on the initial time slice
\tau=\tau_{0}
, defined such that

\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau_{0})\ket{0,\tau_{0}}\ =\ 0\;,\qquad\braket{0,\tau_{0}|0,\tau_{0}}\ =\ 1\;,

(101)

where we have normalised the vacuum to unity. Note that the initial-time vacuum state coincides in all three of the standard pictures (Schrödinger, Heisenberg, and interaction). Since we are constructing the Fock basis directly in the interaction picture, we shall omit any subscripts on the states themselves, so as not to over-clutter notation.

Single-particle momentum states are then created by acting on the vacuum with the creation operator, i.e.,

\ket{\mathbf{k},\tau_{0}}\ =\ \hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},I}(\tau_{0})\ket{0,\tau_{0}}\;.

(102)

By virtue of the canonical algebra (
74
), the single-particle momentum states are normalised, so that

\braket{\mathbf{p},\tau_{0}|\mathbf{k},\tau_{0}}\ =\ (2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})\;.

(103)

Continuing in this fashion, we find that an
n
-particle state is given by

\ket{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{k}_{n},\tau_{0}}\ =\ \hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{1},I}(\tau_{0})\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{2},I}(\tau_{0})\cdots\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{n},I}(\tau_{0})\ket{0,\tau_{0}}\ =\ \prod_{i=1}^{n}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{i},I}(\tau_{0})\ket{0,\tau_{0}}\;.

(104)

Together, all
n
-particle states span the Fock space, with completeness relation

\displaystyle\hat{\mathds{1}}\ =

\displaystyle\ |0,\tau_{0}\rangle\langle 0,\tau_{0}|\>+\>\int_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}|\mathbf{k}_{1},\tau_{0}\rangle\langle\mathbf{k}_{1},\tau_{0}|\>+\>\frac{1}{2!}\int_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}}|\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\tau_{0}\rangle\langle\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\tau_{0}|\>+\>\cdots

\displaystyle\ =

\displaystyle\ |0,\tau_{0}\rangle\langle 0,\tau_{0}|\>+\>\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{k}_{n}}|\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{k}_{n},\tau_{0}\rangle\langle\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{k}_{n},\tau_{0}|\;,

(105)

where
\hat{\mathds{1}}
is the identity operator.

Given that we have a mapping from the Schrödinger to the interaction picture [cf. Eq. (
73
)], the vacuum state in the interaction picture at some arbitrary later time
\tau>\tau_{0}
is related to the initial vacuum as

\displaystyle\ket{0,\tau}\ =\ \hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ket{0,\tau_{0}}\;.

(106)

Accordingly, an
n
-particle momentum state evolves as

\ket{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{k}_{n},\tau}\ =\ \hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ket{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{k}_{n},\tau_{0}}\;.

(107)

By acting from the left and the right of Eq. (
IV
) with
\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})
and
\hat{\mathcal{W}}(\tau,\tau_{0})
, respectively, it immediately follows that the completeness relation holds at some arbitrary later time
\tau>\tau_{0}
, and therefore constitutes a basis for the Fock space at time
\tau
. They need not, however, be unitarily equivalent.

IV.1
Coherent-state basis

With the aim of providing an explicit illustration of the unitary inequivalence of the Fock basis on different time slices, we introduce an additional basis, which will prove to be particular convenient for determining the form of the vacuum-to-vacuum persistence amplitude. This basis is composed of coherent states.

A coherent state
\ket{\zeta(\mathbf{k},\tau)}\coloneqq\ket{\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}
is, by definition, the unique eigenstate of the annihilation operator
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)
associated with the eigenvalue
\zeta(\mathbf{k},\tau)\coloneqq\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)
:

\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\ket{\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}\ =\ \zeta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\ket{\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}\;.

(108)

Coherent states can be related to the Fock basis (constructed in the previous section) as follows. Using the completeness relation (
IV
) at some time
\tau>\tau_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\ket{\zeta(\tau)}\ =

\displaystyle\ \braket{0,\tau|\zeta(\tau)}\ket{0,\tau}\>+\>\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{k}_{n}}\braket{\mathbf{k}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{k}_{n},\tau|\zeta(\tau)}\ket{\mathbf{k}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{k}_{n},\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \braket{0,\tau|\zeta(\tau)}\left[\hat{\mathds{1}}\>+\>\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{k}_{n}}\zeta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{\mathbf{k}_{n}}(\tau)\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}(\tau)\cdots\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{n}}(\tau)\right]\ket{0,\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \braket{0,\tau|\zeta(\tau)}\,e^{\int_{\mathbf{k}}\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}\ket{0,\tau}\;.

(109)

All that remains is to determine
\braket{0,\tau|\zeta(\tau)}
. This can be done, as before, by using the normalisation condition for the coherent state,

\displaystyle 1\ =

\displaystyle\ \braket{\zeta(\tau)|\zeta(\tau)}\ =\ |\braket{0,\tau|\zeta(\tau)}|^{2}\bra{0,\tau}e^{\int_{\mathbf{k}}\zeta^{\ast}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}\,e^{\int_{\mathbf{p}}\zeta_{\mathbf{p}}(\tau)\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}(\tau)}\ket{0,\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ |\braket{0,\tau|\zeta(\tau)}|^{2}\,e^{\int_{\mathbf{k}}|\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)|^{2}}\;,

(110)

and therefore

\braket{0,\tau|\zeta(\tau)}\ =\ e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}}|\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)|^{2}}\;,

(111)

up to an arbitrary phase. As such, a given coherent state can be generated from the vacuum state of the full Fock space as follows:

\ket{\zeta(\tau)}\ =\ \ e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}}|\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)|^{2}}\,e^{\int_{\mathbf{k}}\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}(\tau)}\ket{0,\tau}\ =\ e^{\int_{\mathbf{k}}(\zeta(\mathbf{k},\tau)\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}(\tau)-\zeta^{\ast}(\mathbf{k},\tau)\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau))}\ket{0,\tau}\;.

(112)

Now, as advertised, we can use coherent states to construct a basis for the Fock space. However, we must note that the coherent states are not orthogonal, since

|\braket{\zeta|\zeta^{\prime}}|^{2}\ =\ e^{-\int_{\mathbf{k}}|\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}-\zeta^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}}\;.

(113)

Instead, they form an overcomplete basis, and any coherent state can be expanded in terms of all other coherent states (using Eq. (
115
)) as

\ket{\zeta^{\prime}}\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{D}\zeta^{\ast}\mathcal{D}\zeta\,\ket{\zeta}\braket{\zeta|\zeta^{\prime}}\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{D}\zeta^{\ast}\mathcal{D}\zeta\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\left(|\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}-\zeta^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}+2i\text{Im}(\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\ast})\right)}\ket{\zeta}\;.

(114)

Despite their overcompleteness, the states nevertheless satisfy a completeness relation

\hat{\mathds{1}}\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{D}\zeta^{\ast}\mathcal{D}\zeta\,\ket{\zeta}\bra{\zeta}\;,

(115)

where we have introduced the functional integration measure

\mathcal{D}\zeta^{\ast}\mathcal{D}\zeta\ \coloneqq\ \prod_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta^{\ast}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi i}\;.

(116)

The coherent-state basis will provide us with a convenient way of determining expectation values of operators
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
. Indeed, given that it is always possible to cast a product of creation and annihilation operators into the form
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbf{k},I}=\sum_{n,m}c_{nm}\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}^{n}(\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}^{\dagger})^{m}
, the expectation value of this operator with respect to some state
\ket{\psi}
can be written in terms of coherent states as

\displaystyle\braket{\psi|\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbf{k},I}|\psi}\ =\ \sum_{nm}c_{nm}\braket{\psi|\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}^{n}(\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}^{\dagger})^{m}|\psi}\ =

\displaystyle\ \sum_{nm}c_{nm}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{D}\zeta^{\ast}\mathcal{D}\zeta\,\braket{\psi|\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}^{n}|\zeta}\braket{\zeta|(\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k},I}^{\dagger})^{m}|\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{nm}c_{nm}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{D}\zeta^{\ast}\mathcal{D}\zeta\,\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}^{n}(\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}^{\ast})^{m}|\braket{\zeta|\psi}|^{2}\;.

(117)

We now return to the main goal of this section: the calculation of the vacuum-to-vacuum persistence amplitude for the free scalar field theory.

V
Conclusion

QFT in curved spacetimes suffers from a variety of issues, including the unitary inequivalence of vacua at different times
Giddings:2025abo
. Due to its relevance for applications in cosmology, QFT in de Sitter space constitutes an important testing ground for such problems.

In this article, we have performed an explicit finite-time calculation to illustrate how the unitary time-evolution operator in de Sitter space QFT maps between unitarily inequivalent representations of the canonical algebra. For this purpose, we have considered a free Klein-Gordon scalar field in de Sitter space, performed a canonical quantization, introduced a Fock space, and calculated the vacuum persistence amplitude (VPA). We have shown that the overlap of two vacuum states at different conformal times (the VPA) is zero in the infinite-volume limit, even if there is only an infinitesimal time step between them. Consequently, vacua at different conformal times in de Sitter QFT are unitarily inequivalent. This result constitutes an intensification of the situation in Ref.
Anderson:2017hts
, where a unitary inequivalence was only predicted to appear in the asymptotic time limit. Where this qualitative difference in both results stems from can easily be seen when realising that Ref.
Anderson:2017hts
only considers the background dynamics, corresponding to our operator
\hat{S}_{S}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})
, while we also include the Hamiltonian dynamics represented by
\hat{U}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})
via
\hat{S}_{S}^{\dagger}(\tau,\tau_{0})\hat{U}_{0}(\tau,\tau_{0})
in our computation. If we ignored the Hamiltonian dynamics or considered asymptotic states such that we could hide the resulting infinite phase factor in the wave function renormalisation, then we would recover the result of Ref.
Anderson:2017hts
. In addition, our result is confirmed by the general statements made in Ref.
Giddings:2025abo
and is of similar nature as the one in Refs.
Blasone:1995zc
;
Blasone:2025atj
, where a unitary inequivalence in the infinite volume limit has been found in the case of neutrino mixing in flat space.

We leave considerations of the practical consequences of the lack of a proper unitary evolution for future works. In addition, while we have found compelling indications for the positive definiteness of the real part of the integral multiplying the volume factor in the VPA, an analytical proof for finite times remains elusive, and this may be the focus of future work.

\displaystyle(\lambda^{-}_{\mathbf{k}})^{\prime}-2i\lambda^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}(\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}})^{\prime}-4(\lambda^{-}_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}\,e^{-2i\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}}(\lambda^{+}_{\mathbf{k}})^{\prime}\ =\ e^{-2i\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}}(\lambda^{-}_{\mathbf{k}})^{\prime}\ =

\displaystyle\ f^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}\;,

(147b)

\displaystyle(\lambda^{+}_{\mathbf{k}})^{\prime}-2i\lambda^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}(\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}})^{\prime}-4(\lambda^{+}_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}\,e^{-2i\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}}(\lambda^{-}_{\mathbf{k}})^{\prime}\ =\ e^{-2i\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}}(\lambda^{+}_{\mathbf{k}})^{\prime}\ =

\displaystyle\ f^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}\;,

(147c)

\displaystyle(\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}})^{\prime}-4i\lambda^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}\,e^{-2i\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}}(\lambda^{+}_{\mathbf{k}})^{\prime}\ =\ (\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}})^{\prime}-4i\lambda^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}\,e^{-2i\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}}(\lambda^{-}_{\mathbf{k}})^{\prime}\ =

\displaystyle\ f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}\;.

(147d)

For the background evolution operator
\hat{S}_{S}
, the situation is very simple, corresponding to
f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}=0
and
f^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}=\mp i\theta_{\mathbf{k}}
. The resulting differential equations can readily solved using the boundary conditions (
142
):

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0};z)\ =

\displaystyle\ \frac{i}{2}\,\textrm{ln}\left(\cosh(z\,\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau))\right)\;,

(148a)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0};z)\ =

\displaystyle\ \pm\frac{i}{2}\,\tanh(z\,\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau))\;,

(148b)

\displaystyle\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0};z)\ =

\displaystyle\ i\,\textrm{ln}\left(\cosh(z\,\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau))\right)\;,

(148c)

leading precisely to the form given in Eq. (
59
), upon setting
z=1
.

In the case of the free-field evolution operator
\hat{U}_{0}
, we have
f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}=g_{\mathbf{k}}
and
f^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}=h_{\mathbf{k}}
[cf. Eq. (
70
) for their exact forms]. By making the canonical choice

\lambda^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0};z)\ =\ h_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})\,X_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0};z)\;,

(149)

the system reduces to a set of three differential equations

\displaystyle e^{-2i\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}}X^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}\ =

\displaystyle\ 1\;,

(150a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}-2ih^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}X_{\mathbf{k}}\ =

\displaystyle\ 0\;,

(150b)

\displaystyle(\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}})^{\prime}-4ih^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}X_{\mathbf{k}}\ =

\displaystyle\ g_{\mathbf{k}}\;.

(150c)

The solutions depend upon whether the discriminant function
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\coloneqq\sqrt{g_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-4h_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}
is real or imaginary. From Eq. (
70
) (recalling that
\tau_{0},\tau\in(-\infty,0)
, with
\tau>\tau_{0}
, and
\mathbf{k}\in(-\infty,+\infty)
), we have

\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})\ =\ \frac{|\tau-\tau_{0}|}{3H(\tau_{0}\tau)^{3/2}}\sqrt{\tau^{2}+\tau_{0}\tau+\tau_{0}^{2}}\sqrt{3H^{2}\mathbf{k}^{2}\tau_{0}^{2}\tau^{2}+m^{2}(\tau^{2}+\tau_{0}\tau+\tau_{0}^{2})}\;.

(151)

It is clear that
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{R}
and furthermore
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\geq 0
. As such, the solutions lie on the real branch. Upon imposing the boundary conditions (
142
), the system (
150
) can be solved to give

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0};z)\ =

\displaystyle\ -\frac{z}{2}\,g_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})+\frac{i}{2}\,\textrm{ln}\left(\cos(z\,\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0}))-\frac{ig_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}{\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}\sin(z\,\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0}))\right)\;,

(152a)

\displaystyle X_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0};z)\ =

\displaystyle\ \frac{\tan(z\,\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0}))}{\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})-ig_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})\tan(z\,\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0}))}\;,

(152b)

\displaystyle\lambda^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0};z)\ =

\displaystyle\ i\,\textrm{ln}\left(\cos(z\,\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0}))-\frac{ig_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}{\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}\sin(z\,\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0}))\right)\;.

(152c)

At
z=1
, these lead to the solutions given in Eq.
123
. The full operator factorisation specified in Eq. (
IV.2
) is then recovered by factoring back in the volume term
e^{-i\,\textrm{Vol}\int_{\mathbf{k}}f_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}
with
f_{\mathbf{k}}
given by Eq. (
70
).
