Serologies in Lyme disease can be deceiving: a rare case of mycoplasma-induced figurate erythema with persistent IgM Lyme antibodies
Authors: Gustavo Almeida-Silva, Tiago Marques, Joana Antunes, Paulo Filipe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present the case of a 65-year-old woman who came to our clinic with a pruriginous annular skin eruption suggestive of erythema migrans. She reported a previous mild respiratory syndrome. Given the suspicion, the patient was empirically given doxycycline 100 mg q12h for two weeks, which led to a resolution of the dermatosis. Initially, laboratory tests revealed positive IgM for Lyme disease, however these IgM persisted for several months of longitudinal follow-up and IgG was never positive. Seroconversion occurred, however, for Mycoplasma pneumoniae over the course of three weeks. M. pneumoniae is known to cause mucositis, but to our knowledge this is the first reported case of Mycoplasma-induced figurate erythema. She maintained longitudinal follow-up at our clinic and showed no signs of recurrence during this period.Keywords: mycoplasma, figurate erythema, Lyme, erythema migrans

Introduction

Erythema migrans, the hallmark cutaneous manifestation of early Lyme disease, is a prototypical example of a figurate erythema characterized by annular, expanding erythematous lesions. However, the differential diagnosis of figurate erythemas is broad and often challenging, encompassing a diverse range of infectious, autoimmune, neoplastic, and drug-induced etiologies. Clinically similar patterns—such as those seen in erythema annulare centrifugum, tinea corporis, subacute cutaneous lupus, or erythema gyratum repens—can overlap significantly, necessitating a thorough clinical history, targeted laboratory investigations, and histopathological evaluation to guide diagnosis [1].

Despite classically associated with respiratory infections (being responsible for up to 10% of community-acquired pneumonia), Mycoplasma pneumoniae affects other systems in up to one quarter of patients. Mucocutaneous manifestations are heterogeneous, and range from severe vesico-bullous eruptions with marked mucosal involvement (Mycoplasma-induced rash and mucositis) to erythema multiforme, characterized by target-like lesions; which is also a differential diagnosis for figurate eruptions [2].

To our knowledge, and despite the many reports of cutaneous eruptions induced by Mycoplasma infections, there has not been any reported case of figurate eruption induced by this pathogen, which makes this case report an important addition to the existing body of literature.

Case report

A 65-year-old healthy woman came to our Dermatology emergency clinic with a 2-month history of mildly pruriginous annular eruption over the thighs and hips that had progressively grown centrifugally. She reported, however, that she had had a self-limited respiratory infection one week before the onset of the skin eruption but denied any systemic symptoms at this point. There was no history of recent travels, outdoor activities or tick-bite. Upon extensive epidemiological questioning, the patient reported a history of residence in the Pyrenees approximately 30 years prior. Her general practitioner had prescribed topical econazole which didn’t improve the rash.

Physical examination showed polycyclic erythematous and scaly plaques, with a violaceus border, over 10 cm wide over the thighs and hips bilaterally (Fig. 1).

Polycyclic erythematous plaques on the anterior surface of the right thigh (initial presentation).

Complementary diagnostic workup was requested, including skin scrapings for mycological analysis, a skin biopsy, and blood tests encompassing infectious diseases serologies. Laboratory workup in the emergency room was unremarkable for blood count, liver panel and kidney function. Inflammatory markers were within the reference range and hepatitis and HIV serologies were negative. A presumptive diagnosis of erythema migrans was made, she was discharged with empirical doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for one month and a follow-up appointment was scheduled within two weeks.

Upon reevaluation, the lesions had cleared almost completely (Fig. 2), and complementary workup revealed positive IgM antibodies against Borrelia spp. (using ELISA methods) and positive IgM antibodies against M. pneumoniae. Mycological examination of skin scrapings was negative. Lyme disease serology was confirmed with western blot methods, with the same results. Interestingly, three weeks later, Mycoplasma serologies were repeated and showed seroconversion, displaying only positive IgG antibodies. Given the diagnostic doubt, Lyme serologies were also repeated two months later, but still showed only positive IgM antibodies. Biopsy results however were inconclusive, displaying unspecific findings consistent with eczema.

Postinflammatory hyperpigmentation (follow-up appointment).

Given the seroconversion of M. pneumoniae serology following a respiratory infection and the lack of diagnostic criteria for active Lyme disease, the final diagnosis of Mycoplasma-induced figurated erythema was established and the patient maintained longitudinal follow-up in our clinic for several months. No recurrence of the lesions was observed and the positive IgM antibodies for Lyme disease persisted over time, with no accompanying systemic symptoms.

Discussion

At first, several conditions were considered, namely erythema migrans, erythema annulare centrifugum, tinea cruris and granuloma annulare. The latter two were ruled out with the negative skin scraping and biopsy, respectively.

The patient fulfilled the CDC case definition criteria for confirmed case of Lyme disease in low-incidence areas (less than 10 cases per 10.000 inhabitants). This requires suggestive clinical findings as well as positive ELISA serology, that should be confirmed with western blot methods [3].

It might seem odd that our patient maintained positive IgM antibodies and didn’t develop IgG antibodies. But in fact, there has been a great controversy regarding the diagnosis of Borrelia infections using serology for many decades, given its low specificity and sensitivity. Previous studies have found a high prevalence of persistent IgM antibodies and speculated it to stem from a previous Borrelia spp. infection but continuously stimulated by cross-reactive antigens, such as the ones found in wheat, for instance [4].

Scientists found in the 1980s that Borrelia spp. lysates had the ability to induce lymphocytic differentiation and replication [5]. A recent study by Hastey et al. has shown that Borrelia burgdorferi infection induces persistent IgM secretion by B-cells in a fashion that is independent from T-cell activity. This is thought to be the cause of tissue dissemination of the infection, as IgG is far more tissue penetrable [6]. Some authors have even suggested that IgM testing doesn’t add diagnostic value when compared to testing IgG alone [7].

Although chronic infections typically show increased levels of IgG antibodies, the lymphocytic differentiation induced by Borrelia spp. could be the reason behind our patient’s persistent positive IgM antibodies. These had been most likely been positive since she had lived in the Pyrenees, where Lyme disease is far more incident (in some regions, with over 100 cases per 100.000 inhabitants) [8].

Although Lyme disease is rare in Portugal, with only approximately 20 new cases being reported each year, it is thought to be underdiagnosed. Furthermore, despite being an important clinical clue, tick-bite is only reported in 30 to 40% of all cases [9]. It has also been reported that the Portuguese population displays a weaker seroreactivity to the spirochete, which could result in underdiagnosing the condition. Although more unlikely, this could have been a case of two simultaneous infections, as this frequently occurs in Lyme disease. M. pneumoniae is one of the most frequent co-infections of Lyme disease [10]. Moreover, both infections would be appropriately treated with doxycycline.

In conclusion, we believe this case highlights a rare instance of a figurate eruption after a Mycoplasma pneumonia with a confounding persistent IgM Lyme serology, which was probably acquired after contact with Borrelia spp. in a patient who had resided in an endemic area several years prior. This case highlights the necessity of a thorough clinical history and the need of longitudinal follow-up of patients to achieve adequate and correct diagnosis. It also briefly reviews the limitations of serological diagnosis for Lyme disease.

Although cutaneous eruptions are frequent following M. pneumoniae infection, there has been, to our knowledge, no reported case describing a figurate eruption after the infection.
