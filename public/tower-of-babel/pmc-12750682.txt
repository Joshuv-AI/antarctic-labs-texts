Design and rationale for a pilot trial of D-alanine supplementation to reduce the triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio in patients with chronic kidney disease
Authors: Masaru Matsui, Masatoshi Nishimoto, Takayuki Uemura, Shunsuke Kitamura, Wasako Tato, Hideo Tsushima, Takaaki Kosugi, Masahiro Eriguchi, Ken-ichi Samejima, Masashi Mita, Masato Kasahara, Kazuhiko Tsuruya

Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is becoming increasingly prevalent; CKD significantly impacts patient lifespan, quality of life, and medical expenses [1–4]. Recently identified risk factors for CKD progression are closely associated with chronic lifestyle-related diseases rather than primary glomerular diseases [5–9]. Indeed, early intervention for comorbidities, including hypertension and diabetes, can suppress the development of end-stage kidney disease and coronary artery disease in patients with CKD [10, 11]. However, the relationship between CKD and dyslipidemia remains unclear.

A high triglyceride (TG) to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ratio (TG/HDL-C ratio) has recently been recognized as a useful surrogate marker for estimating low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) particle size [12]. The TG/HDL-C ratio is significantly associated with metabolic syndrome [13], coronary artery calcification [14], and the development of intracranial atherosclerosis [15]. We previously showed that CKD preceded elevations in the TG/HDL-C ratio but not in LDL-C levels [16]. In addition, we found that a high TG/HDL-C ratio was significantly associated with CKD progression and cardiovascular disease (CVD) in the general population and patients with diabetic kidney disease [17–19]. Therefore, lowering the TG/HDL-C ratio may help prevent the development and progression of atherosclerotic disease [20, 21].

Amino acids exist as two stereoisomeric forms, L- and D-enantiomers, which are non-superimposable mirror images of each other. Although it has long been believed that protein synthesis exclusively employs L-amino acids, free D-amino acids and D-amino acid-containing peptides have been detected in living organisms, many of which exhibit significant biological activities compared with their L-amino acid-only counterparts. Moreover, D-amino acids are ubiquitous: they exist in bacteria, plants, and mammals [22]. In microbes, D-amino acids synthesized endogenously by D-amino acid aminotransferases, particularly D-alanine and D-glutamate, are essential for cell wall remodeling and constitute critical components of peptidoglycan [23, 24]. In addition, D-serine, D-alanine, and D-aspartate are the most prevalent D-amino acids in mammalian neuroendocrine and endocrine tissues, where they help regulate neurotransmission and hormone secretion [25–27].

In terms of kidney health, D-serine exerts protective effects in acute kidney injury (AKI) via the gut microbiota and is involved in compensatory hypertrophy of the contralateral kidney via mTOR signaling [28, 29]. Furthermore, D-serine is a potential novel biomarker for renal function [30]. D-alanine also demonstrates protective effects against AKI by inhibiting hypoxia-induced mitochondrial damage, as well as a regulatory role in renal gluconeogenesis and circadian rhythms [31, 32]. These results suggest that D-amino acids may have renoprotective effects via the attenuation of chronic inflammation and oxidative stress and the enhancement of gluconeogenesis, both of which favorably influence lipid metabolism. These effects may be reflected by lipid parameters such as the TG/HDL-C ratio, with high levels indicative of small, dense LDL-C and a less favorable lipid profile.

Recently, a clinical study using D-alanine supplementation in healthy individuals reported that D-alanine improves renal function, blood pressure, and fasting glucose [33]. These results suggest that D-alanine may improve metabolic parameters, including the TG/HDL-C ratio and systemic blood pressure, which may help to decrease proteinuria and prevent CKD progression. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate how D-alanine supplementation affects the TG/HDL-C ratio among CKD patients.

Methods

Study design

This open-label, multicenter, single-arm, exploratory trial is designed to assess changes in laboratory tests after the administration of D-alanine in 20 patients with CKD. The total trial duration for each patient will be 10 weeks, comprising a 2-week run-in, 4-week treatment, and 4-week follow-up period. Eligible participants will be recruited at Nara Medical University and Nara Prefectural General Medical Center. Although the lack of a comparator group and blinding may introduce bias and limit the generalizability of the findings, this design was deemed suitable for the exploratory nature of the study and to allow for an initial evaluation of feasibility and potential efficacy prior to a larger randomized controlled trial. Our hospital ethics board approved the study (CRB5200002). The design is in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials and with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials extensions [34, 35].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) patients aged 18 years old and above; (2) patients meeting CKD criteria, defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and/or a urinary protein to urinary creatinine ratio (UPCR) >0.15 g/gCr within 60 days of enrollment; (3) patients with a fasting TG/HDL-C ratio >2.967 for men and >2.237 for women within 28 days of enrollment [20]; and (4) patients with stable management of diabetes, hypertension, or dyslipidemia treatment (via medication, diet, and/or exercise therapy) within 90 days of consent.

Exclusion criteria are as follows: 1) patients taking fibrates, selective peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-α modulators, or n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids within 90 days of consent; 2) patients with TG levels ≥ 500 mg/dL; 3) pregnant or breastfeeding patients or those planning to become pregnant during the study period; 4) patients with poorly controlled diabetes (glycated hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c] > 10%); 5) patients with hypertension not controlled with medication (systolic blood pressure [SBP] ≥ 180 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure [DBP] ≥ 110 mmHg); 6) patients with severe liver disease (aspartate aminotransferase [AST] or alanine aminotransferase [ALT] ≥ 3 × the upper limit of normal, according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) Grade 2; 7) patients with severe kidney disease (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²); 8) patients with malignant tumors; 9) patients participating in another clinical trial within 90 days of drug administration; and 10) patients deemed inappropriate as research subjects by the principal investigator or sub-investigator.

The criteria for discontinuation of study participation are as follows: (1) when the participant withdraws consent or requests to discontinue participation in the study; (2) when it is determined after enrollment that the participant does not meet the eligibility criteria; (3) when continuation of the investigational product is deemed undesirable because of worsening of the underlying disease, such as a serum creatinine ≥ 1.5–3.0 × the baseline level (CTCAE Grade 2); (4) when continuation of the study is considered difficult because of aggravation of comorbidities, such as hepatic dysfunction (AST or ALT ≥ 3 × the upper limit of normal [CTCAE Grade 2]), hyperazotemia (BUN ≥ 80 mg/dL), hyperglycemia (which requires the addition of antihyperglycemic agents or insulin), or hyperphosphatemia (serum phosphate > 6.0 mg/dL [CTCAE Grade 2]); (5) when prohibited concomitant medications are used; (6) when pregnancy is confirmed; (7) when the participant is unable to visit the study site because of relocation or other reasons; (8) when the entire study is terminated; and (9) when the investigator determines that discontinuation of the study is appropriate for any other reason. The criteria for termination of the clinical study are as follows: (1) when critical information regarding the quality, safety, or efficacy of the investigational product becomes available; (2) when recruitment of study participants is deemed difficult, making it unlikely that the planned sample size will be achieved; and (3) when the Certified Review Board instructs modifications to the study protocol or related documents that are unacceptable.

Intervention

Patients will be administered 1 g of D-alanine three times daily after meals during the 28-day treatment period. D-alanine will be provided as a pure alanine powder formulation (Direct Alanine; Kagami Inc., Osaka, Japan) mixed with 1 g of L-alanine in a single packet.

No concomitant or supportive therapies will be administered during the observation period. Pharmaceutical treatments for dyslipidemia (including statins and fibrates), diabetes, and hypertension, as well as lifestyle interventions such as dietary and exercise therapy, will be maintained without modification from those in place at the time of enrollment until the end of the study.

Outcomes and laboratory evaluations

The primary endpoint of this study is the change in fasting TG/HDL-C ratios between baseline and Day 28. The secondary endpoints are changes in fasting TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, AST, ALT, HbA1c, SBP, DBP, UPCR, eGFR, and body weight between baseline and Day 28. Exploratory endpoints are urinary amino acid levels and their correlation with the TG/HDL-C ratio, as well as changes in sleep quality as measured by the Japanese version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index.

The participant timeline is shown in Table 1. A physical examination, comorbidity assessment, and medication review will be performed at baseline (visit 1), Day 28 (visit 2), and Day 56 (visit 3). At each visit, the following laboratory evaluations will be conducted: total protein, albumin, total cholesterol, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, fasting blood sugar, AST, ALT, lactate dehydrogenase, alkaline phosphatase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, and total bilirubin; in addition, HbA1c evaluations will be conducted in patients with diabetes. Plasma and urine amino acid levels (D-alanine, L-alanine, D-serine, L-serine, D-asparagine, L-asparagine, D-proline, and L-proline) will be individually quantified using a two-dimensional high-performance liquid chromatography system (DASH 27B3 × 00322000001; Kagami Inc.), as previously described [33].

Medication adherence

Medication adherence will be assessed using two complementary methods. First, the number of remaining D-alanine sachets will be checked at Day 28. Second, participants will maintain a daily medication diary, recording the date and time of each dose. Adherence will be evaluated by comparing the recorded intake with the prescribed regimen and the residual sachet count at Day 28.

Sample size and recruitment

This study is exploratory research aimed at collecting data for subsequent confirmatory research. Assuming a sample size of 20 and a standard deviation of 2.4 for the measured values or changes, the 95% confidence interval would be approximately the mean ± 1.05. Thus, enrolling 20 participants in the study would allow for the collection of TG/HDL-C ratio data with a reasonable level of precision.

We plan to primarily enroll CKD patients attending the Department of Nephrology at Nara Medical University Hospital and Nara Prefectural General Medical Center. The annual number of outpatients meeting the enrollment criteria is approximately 60. Based on the consent acquisition rate and screening pass rate, we expect to enroll 10 patients per year for 2 years.

Safety

As safety evaluation endpoints, the occurrence and frequency of adverse events (AEs) will be assessed. Among these, events meeting any of the following criteria will be defined as serious AEs: (1) death; (2) diseases or related life-threatening events; (3) diseases or related events that require hospitalization or prolongation of existing hospitalization for treatment; (4) disability; (5) diseases or related events that may lead to disability; (6) diseases or related events that are judged to be serious, comparable to items 3–5, as well as death or life-threatening events; (7) congenital diseases or abnormalities in subsequent generations.

Participants will be instructed to call our hospital if a disease or related event occurs. The site physician will first ensure that appropriate treatment is administered and that the subject is safe. Subsequently, the event will be reported to the principal investigator, who will assess its seriousness, severity, expectedness, and outcome. The participant will continue to be observed until recovery or for as long as monitoring is deemed unnecessary. All serious AEs will also be recorded and reported to the institutional review board and the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare as soon as possible.

Statistical analyses

The analysis population for this study will consist of all enrolled participants, excluding those who did not receive the protocol treatment and those for whom efficacy or safety data were not observed. This pilot trial is not designed for formal hypothesis testing, including repeated-measures analyses. Missing values at the follow-up period will not be imputed.

For the primary endpoint, absolute measurements of TG/HDL-C ratios at baseline, Day 28, and Day 56, along with changes in TG/HDL-C ratios between these time points, will be summarized using descriptive statistics. Confidence intervals for the mean values of the measurements and changes at each time point will be calculated. The time-course estimation will be shown with a line graph indicating the mean values ± confidence intervals. For the secondary endpoint, changes in TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, AST, ALT, HbA1c, SBP, DBP, UPCR, eGFR, body weight, and plasma and urine amino acid levels will be analyzed in the same manner. The Pittsburgh Sleep Questionnaire will also be scored to obtain a total score, and the same analyses will be performed.

Discussion

The prevalence of CKD, particularly in association with lifestyle-related diseases, is increasing worldwide [36, 37]. This has contributed to elevated risks of developing life-threatening conditions such as CVD among CKD patients [38, 39], as well as to increased personal healthcare costs. However, current treatments for CKD are largely limited to certain evidence-based medications such as renin–angiotensin system inhibitors and sodium–glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors [40–42]. This leaves many CKD patients without satisfactory therapeutic options, especially as lowering blood pressure and/or blood glucose levels does not necessarily translate to better kidney or cardiovascular outcomes [43, 44]. Therefore, new surrogate clinical markers that change in association with organ prognosis measured by hard endpoints are needed for CKD patients.

Treatment of dyslipidemia with statins reduces the risk of cardiovascular events in patients without renal impairment [45], but the beneficial effect of lipid management on cardiovascular health decreases as kidney function deteriorates [46]. In addition, the relationship between lipid management and CKD progression risk is unclear. For example, lowering LDL-C with simvastatin plus ezetimibe did not slow kidney disease progression in a wide range of patients with CKD [47], while atorvastatin failed to exhibit renoprotective effects compared with conventional therapy in Japanese patients with dyslipidemia and CKD [48]. Furthermore, pemafibrate did not reduce CVD among patients with type 2 diabetes and was associated with a higher incidence of adverse renal events and venous thromboembolism than placebo [49]. These results suggest that LDL-C and TG levels may not serve as effective surrogate markers of CKD progression among CKD patients undergoing lipid treatment.

By contrast, we demonstrated that the TG/HDL-C ratio and CKD have a bidirectional relationship and that this ratio is also associated with the incidence of renal events and CVD, suggesting that CKD-specific lipid abnormalities may explain the residual risk of CKD-related CVD in patients undergoing lipid management therapies. In the European Society of Cardiology Council for Cardiology Practice, TG/HDL-C ratio thresholds of >2.75 for men and >1.65 for women were identified as strong predictors of metabolic syndrome and the occurrence of a first coronary event, irrespective of BMI [50]. Moreover, the results of this study showed that the optimal cutoff point of the TG/HDL-C ratio for the prediction of hyperinsulinemia was >3.0 for non-Hispanic whites and Mexican Americans and >2.0 for non-Hispanic blacks [51]. A recent report claimed that TG/HDL-C ratios of >2.967 for men and >2.237 for women in Japanese populations serve as useful cutoffs for determining the presence of multiple atherosclerosis risk factors [20]. Given that the TG/HDL-C ratio varies not only across specific disease populations but also by ethnicity, we plan to conduct the present study using the cutoff values from the Japanese cohort. Based on prior evidence, we propose that a reduction of ≥ 20% in the TG/HDL-C ratio may represent a threshold for a clinically meaningful change. Indeed, in the LEADER trial, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist, which reduced major cardiovascular events by approximately 13% in patients with type 2 diabetes [52], lowered the TG/HDL-C ratio by about 20% after six months of treatment [21]. Considering that the biological variation of the TG/HDL-C ratio in healthy individuals is around 20%, such a magnitude of reduction likely exceeds physiological variability and may therefore reflect a genuine metabolic effect [53].

Figure 1 illustrates the mechanisms by which we hypothesize D-alanine supplementation may lead to a reduction in the TG/HDL-C ratio. First, D-alanine affects the circadian clock to induce gluconeogenesis in the kidney [32]. As gluconeogenesis promotes lipid catabolism and oxidation, enhanced gluconeogenesis may contribute to a reduction in TG levels. In addition, D-alanine localizes to pituitary adrenocorticotropic hormone-producing cells and pancreatic β-cells, which supports its potential role in glucose regulation [54]. Second, D-alanine exhibits renoprotective effects in a renal ischemic kidney model via mitochondrial protection in tubular epithelial cells, while low levels of D-alanine are associated with AKI severity [31]. In CKD, decreased hepatic lipase activity leads to TG accumulation, while the selective removal of HDL-C components and alterations in HDL-C particle composition result in reduced HDL-C levels and impaired anti-inflammatory and antioxidant functions [55]. Therefore, the improvement of renal function by D-alanine supplementation may ameliorate these lipid abnormalities and potentially contribute to a decrease in TG levels and an increase in HDL-C levels. A previous study showed that administration of D-alanine for 7 days in humans resulted in increased eGFR levels and significantly decreased uric acid levels from baseline; no serious adverse events were recorded [33]. Based on these results, we believe that administration of D-alanine may ameliorate adverse metabolic states such as CKD-specific dyslipidemia represented by the TG/HDL-C ratio.

The dosage of D-alanine in this study is set at 3 g/day, because compared with 6 g/day, this level previously demonstrated a comparable enhancement in eGFR while yielding greater improvements in blood pressure, blood glucose, and proteinuria [33]. In humans, D-alanine is readily absorbed upon oral administration, extensively distributed throughout the tissues with a volume exceeding that of plasma, and swiftly cleared through renal excretion [56]. Therefore, given that the study population comprises patients with CKD, potential safety concerns associated with elevated plasma concentrations have also been carefully considered. In a previous study, administration of 3 g of D-alanine for 7 days was associated with hyperphosphatemia, hyperglycemia, and hepatic dysfunction in 17%, 8%, and 8% of participants, respectively, although no serious adverse events were reported [33]. Given that the present study involves 28-day administration, the potential risks of renal impairment because of high amino acid load, as well as hepatic dysfunction associated with elevated plasma D-alanine levels, require consideration.

Several limitations of the study are noted. First, given that this study has been designed as an exploratory investigation with a limited sample size and without a comparative control, formal hypothesis testing is not intended, and the findings should be interpreted with due consideration of these inherent limitations. Second, the supplement being used in this study consists of D-alanine mixed with L-alanine. L-alanine is a nonessential amino acid in the human body and is involved in protein synthesis, and during fasting, gluconeogenesis, via the stimulation of glucagon secretion [57]. Therefore, it is highly likely that any observed reductions in the TG/HDL-C ratio cannot be attributed exclusively to the effects of D-alanine. The study is designed to assess the plasma concentrations of D-alanine and L-alanine separately, but future randomized controlled trials should be conducted to confirm the effects of D-alanine supplementation alone. Third, typically, lipid profiles require 8–12 weeks following treatment initiation to achieve stabilization; however, in the present study, a 4-week intervention period will be used to focus on assessing short-term metabolic responses, safety, and treatment adherence, rather than long-term lipid stabilization. Future intervention studies of D-alanine supplementation with a duration of 8–12 weeks or longer will likely be required. Fourth, we required that there be no changes in the management of lifestyle-related diseases during the 90 days prior to enrollment; however, we acknowledge that not all clinical background factors potentially influencing the TG/HDL-C ratio can be fully verified or controlled, which may result in the presence of confounding factors.

In summary, this exploratory investigation will evaluate the efficacy and safety of D-alanine as a dyslipidemia treatment in CKD patients and may provide novel insights for reducing the risk of cardiorenal events in this population. Based on these findings, we plan to conduct a randomized controlled trial to evaluate whether prolonged administration of D-alanine can favorably modulate cardiovascular risk markers with reductions in the TG/HDL-C ratio among CKD patients.
