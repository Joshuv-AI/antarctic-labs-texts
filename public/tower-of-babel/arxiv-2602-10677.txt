Analytic Nonlinear Theory of Shear Banding in Amorphous Solids
Avanish Kumar, Itamar Procaccia

Abstract
The aim of this paper is to offer an analytic theory of the shear banding instability in amorphous solids that are subjected to athermal quasi-static shear. To this aim we derive nonlinear equations for the displacement field, including the consequences of plastic deformation on the mechanical response of amorphous solids. The plastic events collectively induce distributed dipoles that are responsible for screening effects and the creation of typical length-scales that are absent in classical elasticity theory. The nonlinear theory exposes an instability that results in the creation of shear bands. By solving the weakly nonlinear amplitude equation we present analytic expressions for the displacement fields that is associated with shear bands, explaining the role of the elastic moduli that determine the width of a shear band from ductile to brittle characteristics. We derive an energy functional whose Hessian possesses an eigenvalue that goes to zero at the shear-banding instability, providing a prediction for the critical value of the accumulated stress that results in an instability.



I
Introduction

Understanding the mechanical response of amorphous solids, like glasses and granular media, require considerations that go beyond standard approaches to elasticity theory and solid mechanics. To start, contrary to perfect crystalline solids that can exhibit purely elastic responses to strain, amorphous solids suffer from plastic responses for any amount of strain, and these typically appear as quadrupolar “Eshelby inclusions”
54Esh
;
99ML
;
06ML
. Physically, this means that constituent particles can move around in so called “non-affine” processes, not respecting the picture of affine changes of coordinates that are at the basis continuum mechanics which employs curvilinear coordinate systems, co- and contravariant tensors, Christoffel symbols, Mohr’s circles, etc.
82Gur
;
00HH
. Amorphous solids lack long-range order, and they do not possess a unique “ground state”. They can be cooled down to zero temperature, where they can reside in one of many available local equilibria, which under mechanical strains can easily exchange relative stability. Research in the last decade or two indicated that classical elasticity theory needs to be reconsidered for the treatment of amorphous solids.

In our approach we are interested in the response of an amorphous solid that is initially in mechanical equilibrium at zero temperature (i.e having zero resultant force on each particle), which is then subjected to strain, and then relaxed back to mechanical equilibrium at zero temperature by energy minimization. Being aware that this is far from covering the most general mechanical response which can be time-dependent or effected by temperature fluctuations, we discover that this modest protocol exposes a myriad interesting new phenomena that are worthy of detailed study before opening up to effects of strain rate and temperature. We also realize that the naturally measured field for our purposes, whenever possible, is the displacement field (both in numerics and in experiments). We extract the displacement field from the positions of the particles before and after the strain and energy minimization.
We define the system strain and stress tensors from the displacement field, and thus never run into issues of compatibility that can come up in other formulations of the problem.

A major aim of this paper is to explain and provide a theory for the phenomenon of shear banding. Shear banding is a generic and common instability prevalent in amorphous solids from metallic glasses to computer simulations of granular matter
82SPH
;
07SKLF
;
26FJP
. The phenomenon was studied extensively in numerical simulations using quasi-static protocols, in which the external load, like shear strain, is added in small steps after each of which the system is brought back to mechanical equilibrium by energy gradient minimization or damped dynamics
20BLLVP
;
18OBBRT
. Brittle materials exhibit a sharp instability at a critical value of the accumulated stress. Here the displacement field shows a sharp jump across a line in 2-dimensions and across a plane in 3-dimension, concentrating the shear along this separating line or plane. In more ductile materials the shear is less localized, showing a wider and smoother shear band, but nevertheless still clearly noticeable. Our aim here is to offer a theory to predict the critical accumulated stress that results in instability, provide an analytic solution of the displacement field profile during the shear band formation, and understand the material properties that distinguish brittle from ductile materials.

II
The Linear Theory

In this section we provide a summary of the theory of the mechanical response of amorphous solids to external loads that was developed until now. It is impossible to provide full details, but the material is extensively published and the novice reader is advised to consult the provided references. We will develop here the theory for two spatial dimensions, but the extension to 3-dimensions is available, cf. Ref.

\displaystyle U_{\text{Q-el}}=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}x\Gamma^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma\delta}u_{\alpha\beta}Q^{\gamma\delta}\ ,

V.3

d_{\mu,\gamma}d_{\mu,\delta}={f^{\prime}}^{2}(\xi)~m_{\gamma}m_{\delta}\ ,

(41)

d_{\mu,\gamma\alpha}d_{\mu,\delta}=f^{\prime\prime}(\xi)^{\prime}(\xi)~m_{\gamma}m_{\alpha}m_{\delta}.

(42)

These equations will become handy in analyzing the LHS of Eq. (
V.1
).

V.3.1
Analysis of the LHS

Let us label the first four terms on the LHS of Eq. (
V.1
) as
\mathbb{Z}^{(0)}_{k},\mathbb{Z}^{(1)}_{k},\mathbb{Z}^{(2)}_{k},\mathbb{Z}^{(3)}_{k},
and the final long cubic group as
\mathbb{Z}^{(4)}_{k}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}_{k}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}d_{k,\alpha\beta}}_{\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{(0)}}+\underbrace{\widetilde{A}_{\alpha k\gamma\delta}d_{\gamma,\delta\alpha}}_{\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{(1)}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{A}_{\alpha k\gamma\delta}\big(d_{\mu,\gamma\alpha}d_{\mu,\delta}+d_{\mu,\gamma}d_{\mu,\delta\alpha}\big)}_{\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{(2)}}

(43)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\widetilde{A}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\big(d_{\gamma,\delta\alpha}d_{k,\beta}+d_{\gamma,\delta}d_{k,\beta\alpha}\big)}_{\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{(3)}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{A}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\big(d_{\mu,\gamma\alpha}d_{\mu,\delta}d_{k,\beta}+d_{\mu,\gamma}d_{\mu,\delta\alpha}d_{k,\beta}+d_{\mu,\gamma}d_{\mu,\delta}d_{k,\beta\alpha}\big)}_{\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{(4)}}\ .

At this point we will compute the scalar projection
\Pi
defined as

\Pi\equiv e_{k}\,\mathrm{LHS}_{k}=e_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{(0)}_{k}+e_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{(1)}_{k}+e_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{(2)}_{k}+e_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{(3)}_{k}+e_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{(4)}_{k}.

(44)

We consider each term separately.

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{(0)}=e_{k}\Sigma(m)f^{\prime\prime}\ ,\quad e_{k}\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{(0)}=\Sigma(m)f^{\prime\prime}

(45)

where

\displaystyle\Sigma(m)\equiv\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\ .

(46)

The second term in Eq. (
43
) reads

\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{(1)}=\widetilde{A}_{\alpha k\gamma\delta}~e_{\gamma}f^{\prime\prime}~m_{\delta}m_{\alpha}\ ,\quad e_{k}\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{(1)}=\mu^{(1)}(m,e)~f^{\prime\prime}

(47)

where the notation
\mu^{(1)}(\hat{n},e)
indicates that it is a projected element of the elastic tensor
{\bm{\widetilde{A}}}
,

\mu^{(1)}(m,e)\equiv e_{k}~\widetilde{A}_{\alpha k\gamma\delta}~e_{\gamma}~m_{\alpha}m_{\delta}.

(48)

Below we will make this projection explicit using the form of the elastic tensor in homogeneous and isotropic systems.

The third term in Eq. (
43
) reads

\mathbb{Z}^{(2)}_{k}=f’f^{\prime\prime}\;\widetilde{A}_{\alpha k\gamma\delta}~m_{\gamma}n_{\delta}m_{\alpha}\ ,\quad e_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{(2)}_{k}=\mu^{(2)}(m,e)f’f^{\prime\prime}\ ,

(49)

where the projection on the elastic tensor here is

\mu^{(2)}(m,e)\equiv e_{k}\widetilde{A}_{\alpha k\gamma\delta}~m_{\gamma}m_{\delta}m_{\alpha}\ .

(50)

Also this projection will be made explicit below.

The fourth term in Eq. (
43
) reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{(3)}_{k}=2\,f’f^{\prime\prime}\;e_{k}e_{\gamma}\;\widetilde{A}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\;m_{\delta}m_{\beta}m_{\alpha}\ ,

\displaystyle e_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{(3)}_{k}=\,f’f^{\prime\prime}\mu^{(3)}(m,e)\ ,

(51)

where in this case the projection on the elastic tensor is

\mu^{(3)}(m,e)\equiv e_{\gamma}\;\widetilde{A}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\;m_{\delta}m_{\beta}m_{\alpha}\ .

(52)

The last term in Eq. (
43
) reads

\displaystyle Z_{k}^{(4)}=\frac{3}{2}e_{k}~f^{\prime 2}f^{\prime\prime}\widetilde{A}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}~m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}m_{\gamma}n_{\delta}\ ,

\displaystyle e_{k}Z_{k}^{(4)}=\frac{3}{2}~\mu^{(4)}(m)~f^{\prime 2}f^{\prime\prime}\ ,

(53)

where

\displaystyle\mu^{(4)}(m)\equiv\widetilde{A}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}~m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}m_{\gamma}m_{\delta}\ .

(54)

V.3.2
Simplifying for homogeneous isotropic systems

Presently we are ready to simplify the result of the previous subsection, taking into account the explicit form of the elastic tensor in homogeneous isotropic systems:

\widetilde{A}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=\lambda~\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\gamma\delta}+\mu(\delta_{\alpha\gamma}\delta_{\beta\delta}+\delta_{\alpha\delta}\delta_{\beta\gamma}).

(55)

Using this in the definitions (
48
), (
50
), (
52
) and (
54
)
we find

\mu^{(1)}=\mu,~\mu^{(2)}=\mu^{(3)}=0,~\mu^{(4)}=\lambda+2\mu\ .

(56)

V.3.3
Simplifying for simple shear

Next we will specialize our equations for the case of simple shear. To this aim we choose the background stress in the form

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\Sigma\\
\Sigma&0\end{pmatrix}\ .

(57)

At present this tensor is off diagonal in an unspecified coordinate frame that we must make explicit. To this aim we introduce a pair
{\bm{n}},{\bm{t}}
of orthonormal unit vectors associated with the existent background stress. Fixing the lab frame as the
(x,y)
coordinates,
{\bm{n}}\equiv\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta)
and
{\bm{t}}\equiv-\sin(\theta),\cos(\theta)
. For any specified plane with a unit normal
{\bm{n}}
, the traction vector
{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{n}}
decomposes uniquely into a normal components
\Sigma(nn)={\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{n}}
and a shear component
\Sigma(tn)={\bm{t}}\cdot{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{n}}
. These two scalars exhaust the possible traction channels in 2-dimensions. The entire orientation dependence of the traction in simple shear is a point on the Mohr circle defined by

\left(\Sigma(nn),\Sigma(tn)\right)=\left(\Sigma\sin(2\theta),\Sigma\cos(2\theta)\right)\ .

(58)

Note that this pair of unit vectors differ from the previously defined pair
{\bm{e}},{\bm{m}}
that characterize the soft mode orientation. We can express these unit vectors in the basis
({\bm{n}},{\bm{t}})
employing the angle
\phi
with respect to
{\bm{n}}
:

{\bm{m}}={\bm{n}}\cos(\phi)+{\bm{t}}\sin(\phi)\ ,

(59)

with
{\bm{e}}
being orthogonal. With this notation we can select any orientation of the soft mode compatible with the background stress.

Then Eq. (
46
) reads

\displaystyle\Sigma(m)=m_{\alpha}\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}m_{\beta}=\Sigma(nn)\cos(2\phi)+\Sigma(tn)\sin(2\phi)\ .

(60)

This is the only place where the background enters in our effective equation. It will be shown to play an important role in driving the instability of the system leading to shear band formation. Note that if
\phi=0

\Sigma(m)=\Sigma(nn)
while if
\phi=45^{0}
then
\Sigma(m)=\Sigma(tn)
.

VI
The nonlinear effective equation

Having done with the LHS of Eq. (
33
) we need to perform a similar analysis of its RHS. This is done in detail in Appendix
C
. Collecting the results of this appendix
with those of the previous section, we can write now the equation for
f(\xi)
, the function that represents the shear band solution:

\displaystyle\big(\mu+\Sigma(m))\big)f^{\prime\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)f^{\prime}(\xi)^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(\xi)=

(61)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}f(\xi)+{\mathcal{B}}f^{3}(\xi)\ .

The parameters
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are computed in Appendix
C
.
To analyze this equation further we will first find the energy functional whose Euler-Lagrange equation agrees with the present equation (
61
).

VI.1
Energy functional

At this point we can seek an energy functions

F[f]=\int\mathcal{L}(f,f^{\prime},\theta)d\xi

(62)

where the Lagrangian density
\mathcal{L}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(f,f^{\prime};\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mu+\Sigma(m)\right)(f^{\prime})^{2}+\tfrac{1}{8}(\lambda+2\mu)(f^{\prime})^{4}

(63)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{A}}{2}f^{2}+\frac{\mathcal{B}}{4}f^{4}

The function
f(\xi)
is supposed to extremize the energy functional, satisfying the Euler-Lagrange equation:

\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial f^{\prime}}\right)-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial f}=0

(64)

We compute the required derivatives from the Lagrangian (
63
):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial f^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(\mu+\Sigma(m))f^{\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)(f^{\prime})^{3}

(65)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial f^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=(\mu+\Sigma(m)))f^{\prime\prime}+\tfrac{3}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)(f^{\prime})^{2}f^{\prime\prime}

(66)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial f}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{A}f+\mathcal{B}f^{3}

(67)

Using these results in Eq. (
64
) we recapture Eq. (
61
), meaning that our energy functional is the right one.

VI.2
The Hessian operator

With the energy functional at hand, we can proceed to compute the Hessian.
The Hessian operator
\mathcal{H}
is defined as the second functional derivative of the energy. For a small variation
\delta f(x)
around a solution
f(x)
of (
61
), we have:

\delta^{2}F=\frac{1}{2}\int\delta f(\xi)\,\mathcal{H}\,\delta f(\xi)~d\xi

(68)

To find
\mathcal{H}
, we compute the second variation
\delta^{2}F
directly from the energy functional (
63
).
Consider a variation
f(\xi)\to f(\xi)+\delta f(\xi)
. The first variation
\delta F
is:

\delta F=\int\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial f}\delta f+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial f^{\prime}}\delta f^{\prime}\right]d\xi

(69)

Substituting the expressions for the partial derivatives:

\displaystyle\delta F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\Big[(-\mathcal{A}f+\mathcal{B}f^{3})\delta f+\Big\{(\mu+\Sigma(m))f^{\prime}

(70)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)(f^{\prime})^{3}\Big\}\delta f^{\prime}\Big]d\xi

The second variation
\delta^{2}F
is obtained by varying (
70
) again, keeping terms up to second order in
\delta f
.

\displaystyle\delta[(-\mathcal{A}f+\mathcal{B}f^{3})\delta f]=(-\mathcal{A}+3\mathcal{B}f^{2})(\delta f)^{2}

(71)

\displaystyle\delta[\Big\{(\mu+\Sigma(m))f^{\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)(f^{\prime})^{3}\Big\}\delta f^{\prime}]=

\displaystyle[\Big\{(\mu+\Sigma(m))+\tfrac{3}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)(f^{\prime})^{2}\Big\}](\delta f^{\prime})^{2}

(72)

Note that the variation of
\delta f^{\prime}
is
\delta(\delta f^{\prime})
, but since
\delta f
and
\delta f^{\prime}
are independent variations, the cross-term
\delta f\,\delta f^{\prime}
does not appear in the
second
variation; we only get
(\delta f^{\prime})^{2}
.
Thus, the second variation of the energy functional is:

\displaystyle\delta^{2}F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\Big\{\Big[(\mu+\Sigma(m))+\tfrac{3}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)(f^{\prime})^{2}\Big](\delta f^{\prime})^{2}

(73)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Big[-\mathcal{A}+3\mathcal{B}f^{2}\Big](\delta f)^{2}\Big\}d\xi

To express
\delta^{2}F
in the form of (
68
), we integrate the gradient terms by parts, assuming that the boundary terms vanish.
For a general function
g(f^{\prime})
, we have:

\int g(f^{\prime})(\delta f^{\prime})^{2}d\xi=-\int\delta f\,\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(g(f^{\prime})\frac{d}{d\xi}\delta f\right)d\xi

(74)

Applying this to our case:

\displaystyle\int(\mu+\Sigma(m))(\delta f^{\prime})^{2}d\xi=-\int\delta f\,(\mu+\Sigma(m))\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}~\delta f(\xi)~d\xi

(75)

\displaystyle\int\tfrac{3}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)(f^{\prime})^{2}(\delta f^{\prime})^{2}d\xi=-\int\delta f\,\tfrac{3}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)\frac{d}{d\xi}\left((f^{\prime})^{2}\frac{d}{d\xi}\delta f\right)d\xi

(76)

Substituting these results back into (
73
):

\displaystyle\delta^{2}F

\displaystyle=\int\delta f\left\{-(\mu+\Sigma(m))\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}-\tfrac{3}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)\frac{d}{d\xi}\left((f^{\prime})^{2}\frac{d}{d\xi}\right)\right\}\delta f(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle\quad+\int\delta f\left\{-\mathcal{A}+3\mathcal{B}f^{2}\right\}\delta f(\xi)d\xi

(77)

Combining both terms, we identify the Hessian operator
\mathcal{H}
acting on the variation
\delta f
.

\boxed{\mathcal{H}=-\left\{\mu+\Sigma(m)\right\}\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}-\tfrac{3}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)\frac{d}{d\xi}\left((f^{\prime})^{2}\frac{d}{d\xi}\right)-\mathcal{A}+3\mathcal{B}f^{2}}

(78)

In order to guarantee that this Hessian has real eigenvalues and a complete set of orthogonal eigenfunctions we need to show that it is self-adjoint. This is done in Appendix
D
.

VII
The onset of instability

Having the Hessian Eq. (
78
) at hand, we can ask where an instability sets in. To this aim we remind the reader that a displacement field is always computed with respect to a reference state, and in our quasi-static protocol the last step was the equilibration after the previous strain step. So we need to ask about the stability of a
f=0
solution. In other words, the point of instability is determined by the Hessian
\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}
of Eq. (
78
) in which we substitute
f=f^{\prime}=0
, i.e.

\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}=-\left\{\mu+\Sigma(m)\right\}\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}-\mathcal{A}\ .

(79)

With periodic boundary conditions on a domain of length
L
the eigenvector of
\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}
are Fourier modes,

\psi_{m}(\xi)=e^{ik_{m}\xi},\qquad k_{m}=\frac{2\pi m}{L},\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}\ .

(80)

and the linear operator (
79
) gives

\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}\psi_{m}=\lambda_{m}\psi_{m}

(81)

with

\lambda_{m}(\theta)=\Big(\mu+\Sigma(m)\Big)k_{m}^{2}-{\mathcal{A}}\ .

(82)

The lowest nontrivial mode (typically
m=1
) satisfies

\lambda_{1}(\theta)=\Big(\mu+\Sigma(m)\Big)\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}-{\mathcal{A}}\ .

(83)

Instability sets in when

\Big(\mu+\Sigma(m)\Big)\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}={\mathcal{A}}\ .

(84)

At this point we should select our boundary conditions, which can be shear traction,
\phi=45^{\circ}
or shear normal with
\phi=0
. In the case of simple shear at the boundaries the choice is shear traction.
Then the effect of the background stress is maximal when
\cos(2\theta)=1
giving
\theta=0
or
\theta=90^{\circ}
. So the well known appearance of the instability in
45^{\circ}
with respect to the principal shear axis is selected by the physics, not imposed by any geometry or coordinates. So far the formulation is thus internally consistent, coordinate-free, and fully aligned with our physical intuition.

It is important to realize that the linearized Hessian
\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}
can be only used to identify the point of instability, but it does not provide the solution of the displacement field, a solution that is determined by the full nonlinear equation Eq. (
61
). At this point we are ready to find this solution.

VIII
Shear-band solution of Eq. (
61
)

This section seeks a shear-band solution for the function
f(\xi)
that satisfies Eq. (
61
). Since we expect such a solution to exhibit all the variation as a function of
\xi
in the vicinity of the shear band (localized around, say,
\xi_{0}
), we demand the following boundary conditions

\displaystyle f(\xi\to-\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-f_{0}\ ,\quad f(\xi\to+\infty)=+f_{0}

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\pm\infty)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\ .

(85)

To find the value of
f_{0}
we substitute
f^{\prime}=0
in Eq. (
61
) to find

f_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{A}}}{{\mathcal{B}}}}\ .

(86)

At this point it is important to realize that the boundary condition (
86
) refers to the solution of the projected equation (
61
) and not to the full displacement field that is solved by equation (
32
). The latter can include affine displacement fields that are not represented by the projected equation that is purely non-affine.

Armed with the boundary condition we return to Eq. (
61
) and seek a first integral.

VIII.1
First Integral

We choose shorthand notation

\epsilon_{1}=\mu+\Sigma\sin 2\theta,\qquad\epsilon_{2}=\frac{3}{2}(\lambda+2\mu).

(87)

Then Eq. (
61
) becomes

(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}f^{\prime 2})\,f^{\prime\prime}=-{\mathcal{A}}f+{\mathcal{B}}f^{3}.

(88)

Multiplying by
f^{\prime}(\xi)
:

(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}f^{\prime 2})\,f^{\prime\prime}f^{\prime}=(-{\mathcal{A}}u+{\mathcal{B}}f^{3})f^{\prime}.

(89)

Now we use three identities:

f^{\prime\prime}f^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{d\xi}(f^{\prime 2})

(90)

f^{\prime 2}f^{\prime\prime}f^{\prime}=f^{\prime 3}f^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{4}\frac{d}{d\xi}(f^{\prime 4})

(91)

(-Af+Bf^{3})f^{\prime}=\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(-\frac{{\mathcal{A}}}{2}f^{2}+\frac{{\mathcal{B}}}{4}f^{4}\right)

(92)

So (
89
) becomes a total derivative:

\frac{d}{d\xi}\left[\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}f^{\prime 2}+\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{4}f^{\prime 4}+\frac{{\mathcal{A}}}{2}f^{2}-\frac{{\mathcal{B}}}{4}f^{4}\right]=0.

(93)

Therefore the first integral is

\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}f^{\prime 2}+\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{4}f^{\prime 4}+\frac{{\mathcal{A}}}{2}f^{2}-\frac{{\mathcal{B}}}{4}f^{4}=C\ .

(94)

Using our boundary conditions Eqs. (
85
) and (
86
) we find

C=\frac{{\mathcal{A}}}{2}f_{0}^{2}-\frac{{\mathcal{B}}}{4}f_{0}^{4}=\frac{{\mathcal{A}}^{2}}{4{\mathcal{B}}}.

(95)

Substituting back into Eq.(
94
) we end up with

\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}f^{\prime 2}+\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{4}f^{\prime 4}=\frac{{\mathcal{B}}}{4}(f^{2}-f_{0}^{2})^{2}.

(96)

VIII.2
The ductile limit

In the case of ductile systems we expect the shear-band solution to be relatively smooth, with the slope
f^{\prime}
relatively small, allowing us to neglect the quartic term
f^{\prime 4}
in Eq. (
96
).
In other words, we assume that
\epsilon_{1}\gg\epsilon_{2}f^{\prime 2}/2
in equation (
88
); then

\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}f^{\prime 2}=\frac{{\mathcal{B}}}{4}(f^{2}-f_{0}^{2})^{2}.

(97)

This equation can be integrated by separation of variables

\frac{df}{f_{0}^{2}-f^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{B}}}{2\epsilon_{1}}}\,d\xi\ .

(98)

Making use of the standard integral

\int\frac{df}{f_{0}^{2}-f^{2}}=\frac{1}{f_{0}}\,\mathrm{arctanh}\!\left(\frac{f}{f_{0}}\right).

(99)

one obtains

\frac{1}{f_{0}}\,\mathrm{arctanh}\!\left(\frac{f}{f_{0}}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{B}}}{2\epsilon_{1}}}\,(\xi-\xi_{0}),

(100)

where
\xi_{0}
is the integration constant, identified before as the center of the shear band. Inverting in favor of
f(\xi)
we find

\frac{f(\xi)}{f_{0}}=\tanh\!\left(f_{0}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{B}}}{2\epsilon_{1}}}\,(\xi-\xi_{0})\right).

(101)

Since
f_{0}\sqrt{{\mathcal{B}}}=\sqrt{{\mathcal{A}}}
, the width is controlled by
\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}/A}
. We define,

\ell\equiv\sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_{1}}{{\mathcal{A}}}}=\sqrt{\frac{2(\mu+\Sigma\sin 2\theta)}{{\mathcal{A}}}}.

(102)

Then the ductile solution in the final form is:

\boxed{f(\xi)=f_{0}\,\tanh\!\left(\frac{\xi-\xi_{0}}{\ell}\right)}\ ,

(103)

with
\ell
and
f_{0}
given by Eqs. (
102
) and (
86
) respectively.
This is the profile obtained directly from the Eq. (
61
) in the limit of small slope.
At this point we note that in the case that Eq. (
16
) survives the nonlinear extension, then

{\mathcal{A}}=\kappa_{e}^{2}
and the shear band width is directly determined by the screening parameter.

103
) , we plot several cases in Fig.

VIII.3
The Brittle Limit

Starting again from Eq. (
96
), we seek a solution in the brittle limit separating the analysis into an inner solution in the region of the shear band, an outer solution for from the shear band, and then matching these two solution. We start with the inner solution.

VIII.3.1
Inner solution

In the core of the shear band we assume that the quartic term in
f^{\prime}
is dominant over the quadratic term, i.e

\epsilon_{2}(f^{\prime})^{2}/2\gg\epsilon_{1}
. In that case we can estimate

\frac{\alpha}{4}(f^{\prime})^{4}\approx\frac{{\mathcal{B}}}{4}(f^{2}-f_{0}^{2})^{2}.

(104)

Since shear band solution
f(\xi)
satisfied
|f(\xi)|\leq f_{0}
, we can take twice a square root of Eq. (
104
) to find

f^{\prime}\approx\left(\frac{{\mathcal{B}}}{\epsilon_{2}}\right)^{1/4}\sqrt{f_{0}^{2}-f^{2}}.

(105)

We integrate this equation by separating variables,

\frac{df}{\sqrt{f_{0}^{2}-f^{2}}}=\left(\frac{B}{\epsilon_{2}}\right)^{1/4}d\xi\ ,

(106)

and after integrating,

\arcsin\left(\frac{f}{f_{0}}\right)=\frac{\xi-\xi_{0}}{L_{c}},\qquad L_{c}=\left(\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{{\mathcal{B}}}\right)^{1/4}\ .

(107)

Thus the solution at the core, which we will denote as
f_{\rm in}(\xi)
reads

f_{\text{in}}(\xi)=f_{0}\sin\left(\frac{\xi-\xi_{0}}{L_{c}}\right)\ ,

(108)

and the core strain is

f^{\prime}_{\text{in}}(\xi)=\frac{f_{0}}{L_{c}}\cos\left(\frac{\xi-\xi_{0}}{L_{c}}\right)\ .

(109)

Next we need to find the outer solution.

VIII.3.2
The outer solution

Finding the outer solution is easy, since
f^{\prime}\to 0
far from the core, and therefore we face again the condition
\epsilon_{1}\gg\epsilon_{2}f^{\prime 2}
, which is identical to the limit considered in the ductile limit Subsect.
VIII.2
. Therefore we can write without further ado an outer solution

f_{\text{out}}(\xi)=f_{0}\tanh\left(\frac{\xi-\xi_{0}}{\ell_{0}}\right)\ .

(110)

VIII.3.3
The matched solution

Using the results of the last two subsections we can now offer a matched solution,

\displaystyle f(\xi)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle f_{0}\sin\left(\frac{\xi-\xi_{0}}{L_{c}}\right)\,W(\xi)

(111)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle f_{0}\tanh\left(\frac{\xi-\xi_{0}}{\ell_{0}}\right)\,[1-W(\xi)]\ ,

where
W(\xi)
is a smooth function that is
\approx 1
in the core and
\approx 0
in the tails.

It is interesting to note that in the brittle limit the width
L_{c}
of the shear band is determined by the coefficients appearing in the tensors of the cubic dipole term, rather than the screening parameter
\kappa_{e}
. Further simulational work will be needed to furnish better understanding of the nature of this typical scale.

In Appendix
E
we show that an alternative form for the solution in the brittle case can be offered in the form
f(\xi)=f_{0}\tanh[\phi(\xi)]
with
\phi(\xi)
being a nonlinear function of
\xi
.

W
, the smooth interpolation function. We employ the sigmoidal function,

W(\xi)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left(\frac{|\xi-\xi_{0}|-\xi_{c}}{s}\right)}

(112)

IX
Shear localization - zero mode of the nonlinear Hessian

The Fourier mode which is the eigenfunction of the linearized Hessian served to locate the shear banding instability, but it does not provide any connection to the shear-band solution which results from the nonlinear equation (
61
). In this section we show that the zero mode of the nonlinear Hessian is the shear
f^{\prime}(\xi)
which is directly exhibiting the shear localization at the shear band. In other words, we show that

\mathcal{H}\,\psi_{0}=0\quad\text{with}\quad\psi_{0}(\xi)=f^{\prime}(\xi),

(113)

where
f(\xi)
is the kink solution discussed above. This in fact follows very generally from the translations invariance of the kink solution
f(\xi)
. To see this, we note that
the energy functional
\mathcal{F}[f]=\int\mathcal{L}(f,f^{\prime},\theta)\,d\xi
depends on
\xi
only through
f(\xi)
, not explicitly on
\xi
; therefore it is invariant under translations. Hence if
f(\xi)
solves Eq. (
61
), then for any constant shift
\xi_{0}
,
f_{\xi_{0}}\equiv f(\xi-\xi_{0})

also solves Eq. (
61
). Since
f
is a solution, it satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation:

\frac{\delta F}{\delta f}[f]=0.

(114)

But then every translated profile
f_{\xi_{0}}(\xi)
is also a solution:

\frac{\delta F}{\delta f}[f_{\xi_{0}}]=0\quad\text{for all }\xi_{0}.

(115)

We first note that,

\frac{\partial f_{\xi_{0}}(\xi)}{\partial\xi_{0}}=-\frac{\partial f(\xi-\xi_{0})}{\partial\xi}=-f^{\prime}(\xi-\xi_{0}).

(116)

At
\xi_{0}=0
, we have

\left.\frac{\partial f_{\xi_{0}}}{\partial\xi_{0}}\right|_{\xi_{0}=0}=-f^{\prime}(\xi).

(117)

Differentiating (
115
) with respect to
\xi_{0}
and then setting
\xi_{0}=0
(with the chain rule for functionals):

\left.\frac{d}{d{\xi_{0}}}\frac{\delta F}{\delta f}[f_{\xi_{0}}]\right|_{{\xi_{0}}=0}=0

(118)

\left(\frac{\delta^{2}F}{\delta f^{2}}[f]\right)\left(\left.\frac{df_{\xi_{0}}}{d{\xi_{0}}}\right|_{{\xi_{0}}=0}\right)=0.

(119)

But

\left.\frac{df_{\xi_{0}}(\xi)}{d{\xi_{0}}}\right|_{{\xi_{0}}=0}=\left.\frac{d}{d{\xi_{0}}}\right|_{{\xi_{0}}=0}f(\xi-{\xi_{0}})=-f^{\prime}(\xi).

(120)

And
\delta^{2}F/\delta f^{2}
is exactly the Hessian operator
H
. So (
119
) becomes

\mathcal{H}\,(-f^{\prime})=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{H}\,f^{\prime}=0.

(121)

X
Summary and Discussion

It is important to note that the theory presented above contains two types of nonlinearity. The first is the cubic term
{\mathcal{B}}u^{3}(\xi)
on the RHS of Eq. (
61
). This term acts to tame the linear instability that is analyzed in Sect.
VII
. Without this nonlinear term the instability will result in a run-away solution that is not saturated. This is the nature for example of the theory of Rudnicki and Rice
75RR
;
80RR
which was therefore unable to provide a solution of the shear band. The solution that we found above is stabilized by this nonlinearity.
Moreover, together with the linear term on the RHS of Eq. (
61
), this term determines how much shear is contained in the shear-banded system, since
f_{0}=\sqrt{{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{B}}}
.

The second nonlinearity is on the LHS of Eq. (
61
), in the form
3(\lambda+2\mu)\,f^{\prime 2}\,f^{\prime\prime}/2
. This nonlinearity controls the
shape
of the band core, not just its existence. This nonlinearity determines
how
shear localizes (ductile vs brittle). When slopes become large, the equation behaves differently: the band core “feels” a different stiffness than the far field. This is exactly why we can get a brittle-like modified profile, and not just the weak-slope (ductile) tanh-like profile. This is explained in details in Subsect.
VIII.3
. So our theory separates amplitude selection from core-shape selection, and this is quite important. In classical standard
\phi^{4}
theories, there is only one intrinsic lengthscale. In our theory, the instability can develop two intrinsic lengthscales in the strong-slope regime (core vs tail), and this is what brittle localization is expected to show.

Other attempts to understand shear banding were based on the so called elastoplastic models (including lattice models and mesoscale yielding models)
11VR
;
16BSMBST
;
18NFMB
;
22RBMTZ
;
22RT
. These models were shown to capture the avalanche statistics and stress redistribution. However, in their standard form they are local in space, they lack an intrinsic continuum length scale, the localization width is controlled by block sizes or numerical regularization. As a result, shear bands do appear but their thickness is
not
predictive, and their internal strain profile is not uniquely determined. Many Elastoplastic models reproduce the phenomenon of shear localization, but not
how
it localizes spatially in a continuum sense. Our model differs in two decisive ways: it is variational, so equilibrium and stability are well-defined; it contains intrinsic length scales generated by coupling tensors, leading to screened, finite-width shear bands.
In elastoplastic models the stress redistribution is long-ranged (elastic Green’s function), yielding is local and threshold-based, no energetic penalty exists for sharp strain gradients, no intrinsic screening length appears in the equations. Therefore, localization occurs, but it is not a true screened continuum object. It is a geometrical or numerical localization, not a physically selected one. This is the precise sense in which elastoplastic models are incomplete for predicting a shear-band profile. There are extensions of elastoplastic models that introduce a length scale, such as gradient plasticity, non-local elastoplastic models, the intrinsic length scale is not native to standard elastoplastic models; it must be added explicitly. In our model, it is already built into the Lagrangian.

It should be stressed that without screening, there is no shear band. Plastic rearrangements generate long-range elastic fields (Eshelby-type). If these interactions are unscreened, deformation spreads over the entire system. To obtain localized deformation, these interactions must be screened over a finite length. In our theory, screening arises naturally from coupling parameters in the Lagrangian, the competition between destabilizing and regularizing terms generates a screening length
\ell\sim\sqrt{\frac{\text{gradient stiffness}}{\text{softening strength}}}
. This length directly controls shear-band width.
A finite shear-band thickness requires an intrinsic material length scale that screens elastic interactions and penalizes sharp strain gradients. Standard elastoplastic models are local and do not possess such an intrinsic length scale; as a result, while they may exhibit localization, the shear-band width is not selected by the physics and depends on discretization or ad-hoc regularization. In contrast, our model contains an intrinsic length scale arising from coupling parameters in the Lagrangian, leading to screened interactions and enabling the emergence of a finite-width, stationary shear-band solution with an explicit core profile. The intimate connection between the instability and screening is underlined by the explicit appearance of the screening parameter
\kappa_{e}
in the instability criterion Eq. (
84
). If the linear theory prediction for the tensor
{\bm{K}}
continues to be given by Eq. (
16
), then
{\mathcal{A}}=\kappa_{e}^{2}
. Note that only the even component appears in the instability criterion, and that the screening is a stabilizing effect that needs to be overcome by the background stress.

To proceed, we will need to perform some numerical simulations. First issue for immediate research is the matching of the boundary conditions for the displacement field. We should stress that this should be done with the solution of Eq. (
32
) rather than with the projected equation (
61
). For example, if one has a background affine field that is simple shear, the second derivatives on the LHS of Eq. (
61
) are going to annul it. The shear band solution that we derived is purely non-affine, and needs to be added with correct matching to the affine field to obtain a proper solution of Eq. (
32
). This is beyond the scope of the present paper, but will be the subject of follow-up research.

We should also admit that at this point some parameters in our theory are not determined. Although we have expressions for, say, the parameter
{\mathcal{B}}
that determines both the amount of shear carried by the shear band and the brittle inner lengthscale
L_{c}
, it has never been measured. Thus in the near future we plan to conduct careful numerical simulations to determined the theoretical parameters in classical glass forming models, to test the theory in detail. In light of the considerable progress that has been demonstrated in this paper, we find this future effort quite worthwhile.

Appendix B
Energy-Functional for Equilibrium Equation, Its Hessian, Instability and Soft-Mode

The aim of this appendix is to justify from first principles the projection procedure employed in Sect.
V
and to establish its connection to the instability analysis performed in Sect.
VII
.

We note that the equilibrium equation, which is a vector equation, is written for the displacement field
d
. In this section, we will demonstrate that the vector Equilibrium equation can be derived from an energy functional that is explicitly written in terms of the displacement field
d
.
From this energy functional, we compute the Hessian, which is the second variation of the energy functional, and show that it is a self-adjoint operator. To study the instability, we linearize the Hessian around the homogeneous state
d=0
.

Since the Hessian is a self-adjoint operator near the instability, we write the displacement field as an expansion in the orthonormal eigenmodes. We then demonstrate that the zero mode, corresponding to the instability, is the dominant mode. Therefore, at the instability leading to shear banding, it is appropriate to approximate the displacement field by the zero mode of the Hessian.

Then, we write the eigenvalue equation from this Hessian operator and compute its eigenvalues and eigenvectors, which represent its polarization. From these polarizations, we show that transverse polarization facilitates the shear localization. Finally, we write down the structure of the soft mode for the displacement field, which is our projection ansatz leading to post-instability shear band.

B.1
The unprojected energy functional
U[d]

We note that the governing equilibrium equation is an Euler-Lagrange equation so it should be derived form the variation of anenergy functional
U[d]
, that is,

\delta U[d]=0
for all admissible variations
\eta

\eta|_{\partial\Omega}=0
,

Guided by Eq.

(iii) Local Landau-type potential

We will compute this term by term.

(134)

This already matches the RHS of equilibrium equation.

(135)

Therefore,

(136)

Thus,

(137)

In summary we write

-\frac{\delta U}{\delta d_{k}}=\big(\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}d_{k,\beta}\big)_{,\alpha}+\sigma_{\alpha k,\alpha}+\big(\sigma_{\alpha\beta}d_{k,\beta}\big)_{,\alpha}\\
-\,L^{k\zeta}d_{\zeta}+\,T^{kpqr}d_{p}d_{q}d_{r}.

(138)

Setting
(-\delta U/\delta d_{k}=0)
gives us exactly the equilibrium equation.

B.7

We use the

B.8
Linearization and Eigenvalue Problem

In the linear stability analysis, our primary aim is to outline: “Which deformation pattern wants to appear first?” Therefore, it is a selection problem. Selection is always governed by linear stability. Nonlinear terms compete after instability starts, but which mode appears first is determined by linear terms.

B.9
Linearization at

At

d_{k,\beta}=0
,
\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(0)=0
, and
M^{k\zeta}(0)=L^{k\zeta}

\delta u_{\gamma\delta}[0;\zeta]=\tfrac{1}{2}(\zeta_{\gamma,\delta}+\zeta_{\delta,\gamma}).

Thus

(\mathcal{H}_{U}[0]\zeta)_{k}=-(\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}\zeta_{k,\beta})_{,\alpha}-(\delta\sigma_{\alpha k}[0;\zeta])_{,\alpha}+L^{k\zeta}\zeta_{\zeta},

where

\delta\sigma_{\alpha k}[0;\zeta]=\tilde{A}_{\alpha k\gamma\delta}\tfrac{1}{2}(\zeta_{\gamma,\delta}+\zeta_{\delta,\gamma}).

(179)

B.10
The instability operator

The equilibrium condition is,

(180)

Therefore its linearization is

(181)

This is the operator used to study linear instability of the equilibrium equation (the “soft mode” of the PDE). So define the instability (Jacobian) operator

Applying minus to (

B.11
Fourier modes and the eigenvalue problem

We note that in the linearized stability analysis, the equilibrium operator (the equilibrium PDE) and the Hessian have constant coefficients, so its eigenfunctions are plane waves (
e^{iq\cdot{\bf x}}
) labeled by a continuous wavevector
q
. Plane waves are used because they diagonalize the linear operator as shown below. They identify the direction of the instability in the function space.

\zeta_{k}({\bf x})=\hat{\zeta}_{k}\,e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bf x}},\qquad q_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(184)

Then
\partial_{\alpha}\mapsto iq_{\alpha}
. Compute each term carefully:

(a) Prestress-gradient term

\big(\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}\zeta_{k,\beta}\big)_{,\alpha}\mapsto(iq_{\alpha})\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}(iq_{\beta})\hat{\zeta}_{k}=-(\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}q_{\alpha}q_{\beta})\hat{\zeta}_{k}.

(185)

(b) Elastic term

First

\varepsilon_{\gamma\delta}(\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{2}(iq_{\delta}\hat{\zeta}_{\gamma}+iq_{\gamma}\hat{\zeta}_{\delta})e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bf x}}.

(186)

Then

\delta\sigma_{\alpha k}=\tilde{A}_{\alpha k\gamma\delta}\varepsilon_{\gamma\delta}(\zeta)=\tilde{A}_{\alpha k\gamma\delta}\tfrac{1}{2}(iq_{\delta}\hat{\zeta}_{\gamma}+iq_{\gamma}\hat{\zeta}_{\delta})e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bf x}}.

(187)

Now divergence:

(\delta\sigma_{\alpha k})_{,\alpha}\mapsto(iq_{\alpha})\delta\sigma_{\alpha k}=-\tilde{A}_{\alpha k\gamma\delta}~q_{\alpha}q_{\delta}~\hat{\zeta}_{\gamma}

(188)

(using the minor symmetry
\tilde{A}_{\alpha k\gamma\delta}=\tilde{A}_{\alpha k\delta\gamma}
to combine the two halves).

(c) Local term

-L^{k\zeta}\zeta_{\zeta}\mapsto-L^{k\gamma}\hat{\zeta}_{\gamma}.

(189)

B.11.1
The unprojected Fourier eigenvalue matrix

So
\mathbb{J}[0]\zeta=\lambda\zeta
becomes

\Big[(\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}q_{\alpha}q_{\beta})\,\delta_{k\gamma}+\tilde{A}_{\alpha k\gamma\delta}\,q_{\alpha}q_{\delta}-L^{k\gamma}\Big]\hat{\zeta}_{\gamma}=\lambda(q)\,\hat{\zeta}_{k}.

(190)

This is the exact, unprojected Jacobian eigenvalue problem. Instability occurs when
\lambda(q)
first reaches zero at some critical wavevector
q_{0}
.

Let us define

{\bm{q}}=|q|{\bm{m}},\qquad{\bm{m}}=(\cos\phi{\bm{n}},\sin\phi{\bm{t}})\equiv(m_{\alpha},m_{\beta})\ ,

(191)

with
{\bm{e}}\cdot{\bm{m}}=0
.

B.11.2
The prestress projection carrying the
(\theta)
-dependence

We defined the scalar,

\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}(m):=\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}

(192)

so that,

\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}=\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\,|q|^{2}=\Sigma(m)\,|q|^{2}.

(193)

The elastic part in (

\tilde{A}_{\alpha k\zeta\delta}q_{\alpha}q_{\delta}
. Insert this into (
55

q_{\alpha}=|q|n_{\alpha}
:

\tilde{A}_{\alpha k\zeta\delta}q_{\alpha}q_{\delta}=\lambda\,(\delta_{\alpha k}q_{\alpha})(\delta_{\zeta\delta}q_{\delta})\\
+\mu\left(\delta_{\alpha\zeta}\delta_{k\delta}q_{\alpha}q_{\delta}+\delta_{\alpha\delta}\delta_{k\zeta}q_{\alpha}q_{\delta}\right).

(194)

Now simplify term by term:

•

\delta_{\alpha k}q_{\alpha}=q_{k}

•

\delta_{\zeta\delta}q_{\delta}=q_{\zeta}

•

So the
\lambda
-term gives
\lambda\,q_{k}q_{\zeta}
.

For the
\mu
-terms:

\displaystyle\delta_{\alpha\zeta}\delta_{k\delta}q_{\alpha}q_{\delta}

\displaystyle=q_{\zeta}q_{k}

\displaystyle\delta_{\alpha\delta}\delta_{k\zeta}q_{\alpha}q_{\delta}

\displaystyle=(q_{\alpha}q_{\alpha})\delta_{k\zeta}=({\bm{q}}\cdot{\bm{q}})\delta_{k\zeta}=|q|^{2}\delta_{k\zeta}

(195)

Therefore

\tilde{A}_{\alpha k\zeta\delta}q_{\alpha}q_{\delta}=\mu\,|q|^{2}\,\delta_{k\zeta}+(\lambda+\mu)\,q_{k}q_{\zeta}.

(196)

Since

q_{k}q_{\zeta}=\kappa^{2}m_{k}m_{\zeta}

\tilde{A}_{\alpha k\zeta\delta}q_{\alpha}q_{\delta}=|q|^{2}\Big[\mu\,\delta_{k\zeta}+(\lambda+\mu)\,m_{k}m_{\zeta}\Big].

(197)

B.12
Computing the full
2\times 2
eigenvalue matrix

We consider the isotropic systems such that the “local” tensor is isotropic:

L^{k\zeta}={\mathcal{A}}\,\delta^{k\zeta}.

(198)

Now combine (
193
), (
197
), (
198
) into (
190

\mathsf{M}(\kappa,m)=\kappa^{2}\Big[\Sigma(m)\,I+\mu\,I+(\lambda+\mu){\bm{m}}\otimes{\bm{m}}\Big]-{\mathcal{A}}\,I.

(199)

Here,
{\bm{m}}\otimes{\bm{m}}
is the dyadic product. It is a second-rank tensor with components,
({\bm{m}}\otimes{\bm{m}})_{ij}=m_{i}m_{j}
. It acts as a projection operator onto the direction
{\bm{m}}
. When it is applied to a vector
{\bm{v}}
, it projects the component of
{\bm{v}}
along
{\bm{m}}
, i.e.,
({\bm{m}}\otimes{\bm{m}}){{\bm{v}}}=({\bm{m}}\cdot{\bm{v}}){\bm{m}}
. So the eigen-problem is

\mathsf{M}(\kappa,m)\,\hat{\zeta}=\lambda(\kappa,m)\,\hat{\zeta}.

(200)

Note that the matrix is diagonal in the basis
of the longitudinal direction
{\bm{m}}
and the transverse direction
{\bm{e}}
.
Use
({\bm{m}}\otimes{\bm{m}}){\bm{e}}={\bm{m}}({\bm{m}}\cdot{\bm{e}})=0
. So a general
\hat{\zeta}
can be decomposed in these two directions, leading to

\mathsf{M}{\bm{e}}=\Big[|q|^{2}(\Sigma(m)+\mu)-{\mathcal{A}}\Big]{\bm{e}}\ ,

(201)

and

\lambda_{T}(|q|,m)=\big(\Sigma(m)+\mu\big)|q|^{2}-{\mathcal{A}},\qquad\hat{\zeta}={\bm{e}}.

(202)

Similarly

\mathsf{M}{\bm{m}}=\Big[|q|^{2}(\Sigma(m)+\mu+(\lambda+\mu))-{\mathcal{A}}\Big]{\bm{m}}.

(203)

Therefore

\lambda_{L}|q|,m)=\big(\Sigma(m)+\lambda+2\mu\big)|q|^{2}-{\mathcal{A}},\qquad\hat{\zeta}={\bm{m}}.

(204)

Thus, we find that, in the unprojected linearized Hessian, each wave vector direction has two eigenmodes corresponding to the longitudinal mode (
\lambda_{L}
), and the transverse mode with eigenvalue (
\lambda_{T}
). These eigenvalues correspond to the rigidity of the material against deformation with respect to longitudinal and transverse displacements, respectively.
The longitudinal component contains the bulk contribution (
\lambda+2\mu
), whereas the transverse component contains only the shear modulus (
\mu
). Since (
\lambda+2\mu>\mu
) for any elastic solid, we always have (
\lambda_{T}<\lambda_{L}
). Now, as the load increases, the transverse component
\lambda_{T}
goes to zero first and becomes the soft-mode. Since
\lambda_{T}
only represents shear resistance, physically it corresponds to the shear deformation. Therefore, the
\lambda_{T}
represents the shear polarization.

B.12.1
“Shear polarization” as the transverse direction

Here we can understand how “polarization” decides the nature of eigenvalue problem. In the unprojected linearized Fourier eigenproblem, we assume a plane-wave perturbation

\zeta_{k}({\bf x})=\hat{\zeta}_{k}e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bf x}}.

(205)

q
sets the direction along which the field varies (the wavevector).

\hat{\zeta}
is a constant vector that sets the direction of displacement of that mode.
That direction
\hat{\zeta}
is what we call the
polarization
.

B.12.2
Strain produced by a plane wave:

For simplicity let us consider small strain of the perturbation which is

u_{\alpha\beta}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}(\zeta_{\alpha,\beta}+\zeta_{\beta,\alpha}).

(206)

For
\zeta_{\alpha}=\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}e^{iq\cdot{\bf x}}
,

\zeta_{\alpha,\beta}=iq_{\beta}\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bf x}},

so

u_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{i}{2}\Big(q_{\beta}\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}+q_{\alpha}\hat{\zeta}_{\beta}\Big)e^{iq\cdot{\bf x}}.

(207)

Now decompose
\hat{\zeta}
into components parallel and perpendicular to
{\bm{q}}
,

\hat{\zeta}=a\,{\bm{m}}+b\,{\bm{e}}
.

B.12.3
Transverse choice gives pure shear:

Let us take transverse polarization:
\hat{\zeta}=b{\bm{e}}
and compute the normal strain in the propagation direction
m
, denoted as
u^{(mm)}
:

u^{(mm)}=m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}u_{\alpha\beta}.

(208)

Using Eq. (
207
):

u^{(mm)}=\frac{i}{2}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}(q_{\beta}\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}+q_{\alpha}\hat{\zeta}_{\beta})e^{iq\cdot{\bf x}}.

(209)

Insert
q_{\beta}=|q|m_{\beta}
:

\displaystyle u^{(mm)}

\displaystyle=\frac{i|q|}{2}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}(m_{\beta}\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}+m_{\alpha}\hat{\zeta}_{\beta})e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot x}

\displaystyle=\frac{i|q|}{2}\Big({\bm{m}}\cdot\hat{\zeta})+({\bm{m}}\cdot\hat{\zeta})\Big]e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bf x}}.

(210)

So

u^{(mm)}=i|q|({\bm{m}}\cdot\hat{\zeta})e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bf x}}.

(211)

But transverse means
m\cdot\hat{\zeta}=0
. Therefore

u^{(mm)}=0\quad\text{for transverse polarization.}

(212)

Similarly, the normal strain along
{\bm{e}}
,

u^{(ee)}:=e_{\alpha}e_{\beta}u_{\alpha\beta}=0,

(213)

also vanishes for the same reason (because
{\bm{e}}\cdot q=0
).

But the shear component

u^{(me)}=m_{\alpha}e_{\beta}u_{\alpha\beta}

(214)

does not vanish; using (
207
) and
q=|q|m
,
\hat{\zeta}=b{\bm{e}}
, we get

u^{(me)}=u^{(em)}=\frac{i|q|b}{2}e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bf x}}\neq 0.

(215)

Therefore transverse polarization produces strain with only off-diagonal components in the
({\bm{m}},{\bm{e}})
basis. This is pure shear.

B.12.4
The longitudinal choice is not shear

If
\hat{\zeta}=a{\bm{m}}
(longitudinal), then
{\bm{m}}\cdot\hat{\zeta}=a\neq 0
and from the same calculation:

u^{(mm)}=i|q|a\,e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bf x}}\neq 0,

(216)

i.e. it produces compression or extension along
{\bm{m}}
. That is not shear. So, kinematically
transverse polarization(
\hat{\zeta}\perp q)
means shear strain.

A plane-wave mode has two independent directions; the wavevector
{\bm{q}}
, tells us the direction along which the field varies, and the displacement amplitude
\hat{\zeta}
, which tells the direction the material points move. The strain is the symmetric gradient of displacement. If
\hat{\zeta}\perp{\bm{q}}
(transverse), all normal components vanish in the
({\bm{m}},{\bm{e}})
basis and only the mixed component
u^{(me)}
remains; that is exactly pure shear.

B.13
Applied Macroscopic Shear and the Shear Polarization Selected by Hessian

To avoid any confusion between the “applied shear direction” and
the “shear polarization selected by the Hessian” here we outline the basic difference between
these two quantities.

The applied shear is a macroscopic quantity. When we apply a pure shear stress

\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}=\Sigma^{12}=\Sigma
.
This fixes the principal stress directions, loading axes, etc. This is a boundary/loading condition. Therefore, this shear is given.
On the other hand, the shear polarization of a Hessian mode is an outcome of the eigenproblem of the unprojected Hessian. Therefore, this is not an assumed or imposed shear. This shear emerges as an eigenvector.

From the previous discussion we have the unprojected linearized eigenproblem:

\mathsf{M}(\kappa,m)~\hat{d}=\lambda(\kappa,m)~\hat{d}

(217)

Here,
\hat{d}
is a vector displacement amplitude, whereas
(\lambda)
is a stiffness/rigidity representing the eigenvalue, and
m
fixes the wavevector direction
{\bm{m}}
. Once we have this, the eigenvalue problem of the Hessian corresponding to the deformation is: “If the system tries to deform locally with wavevector
{\bm{m}}
, in which direction does it want to move?” That direction is the polarization. In 2D, for a given wavevector direction
{\bm{m}}
, there are exactly two independent displacement polarizations:

•

Longitudinal polarization
\hat{d}\parallel{\bm{m}}

\rightarrow
compression/dilation,

•

Transverse polarization
\hat{d}\perp{\bm{m}}\rightarrow
shear deformation.

These are eigenvectors of the Hessian.

Thus, the shear polarization is selected by the Hessian implies that among all possible infinitesimal displacement fluctuations, the one whose displacement vector is perpendicular to the wavevector is the softest eigenmode of the Hessian.

Therefore, we do not assume shear localization beforehand. We apply a macroscopic shear stress. The Hessian then shows us that the softest fluctuation is transversely polarized, implying that displacement is perpendicular to the band normal. Strain is a shear-like localization that produces a shear band.

B.14
Reduction of Displacement Field Along the Soft-Mode

Note that, as shown earlier, the linearized Hessian
\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}
is a self-adjoint operator acting on vector fields. It has an eigenbasis

\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}{\bm{\psi}}^{(n)}=\lambda_{n}{\bm{\psi}}^{(n)},\qquad\langle{\bm{\psi}}^{(m)},{\bm{\psi}}^{(n)}\rangle=\delta_{mn},

(218)

where

\langle{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}a_{k}({\bf x})b_{k}({\bf x})\,{d^{2}x}.
Then any admissible displacement can be expanded as:

d_{k}({\bf x})=\sum_{n}a_{n}\,\psi^{(n)}_{k}({\bf x}),\qquad a_{n}=\langle{\bm{\psi}}^{(n)},{\bm{d}}\rangle.

(219)

This is the mathematical meaning of “displacement has a component along a Hessian mode.” Now we outline the meaning of each symbol.

B.14.1
Computing the quadratic energy in eigenmode coordinates

We want to compute:

\langle{\bm{d}},\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}{\bm{d}}\rangle.

(220)

To compute this we use the expansion for
{\bm{d}}
,

{\bm{d}}=\sum_{n}a_{n}{\bm{\psi}}^{(n)},\qquad\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}{\bm{d}}=\sum_{n}a_{n}\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}{\bm{\psi}}^{n}=\sum_{n}a_{n}\lambda_{n}{\bm{\psi}}^{n}.

(221)

Taking inner product

\langle{\bm{d}},\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}{\bm{d}}\rangle=\left\langle\sum_{m}a_{m}\psi^{m},\ \sum_{n}a_{n}\lambda_{n}\psi_{n}\right\rangle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda_{n}a_{n}^{2}\ .

(222)

Note that in above equation each term
\lambda_{n}a_{n}^{2}
is the quadratic energy contribution of mode
n
. Large
\lambda_{n}

\rightarrow
costly to excite, small
\lambda_{n}

\rightarrow
cheap to excite, and
\lambda_{n}=0

\rightarrow
costs no quadratic energy.

Thus an instability is signalled by

\lambda_{1}\approx 0\quad\text{at the threshold}\ .

(223)

We will assume below that the next mode stays positive:

\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{D}>0.

(224)

This is the spectral gap assumption.

B.14.2
Inequality for the soft-mode dominance

We start from the exact identity:

\langle{\bm{d}},\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}{\bm{d}}\rangle=\lambda_{1}a_{1}^{2}+\sum_{n\geq 2}\lambda_{n}a_{n}^{2}.

(225)

Now use the spectral difference: for every
n\geq 2
,
\lambda_{n}\geq\lambda_{\rm D}
. Therefore:

\sum_{n\geq 2}\lambda_{n}a_{n}^{2}\geq\sum_{n\geq 2}\lambda_{\rm D}\,a_{n}^{2}=\lambda_{\rm D}\sum_{n\geq 2}a_{n}^{2}.

(226)

Insert (
226
) into (
225
):

\langle{\bm{d}},\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}{\bm{d}}\rangle\geq\lambda_{1}a_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{\rm D}\sum_{n\geq 2}a_{n}^{2}.

(227)

Now isolate the higher-mode contribution. Subtract
\lambda_{1}a_{1}^{2}
:

\langle{\bm{d}},\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}{\bm{d}}\rangle-\lambda_{1}a_{1}^{2}\geq\lambda_{\rm D}\sum_{n\geq 2}a_{n}^{2}.

(228)

Divide by
\lambda_{\rm D}
:

\sum_{n\geq 2}a_{n}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\rm D}}\big(\langle{\bm{d}},\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}{\bm{d}}\rangle-\lambda_{1}a_{1}^{2}\big).

(229)

This is the inequality representing the constraint on the modes other than the soft one. We note that the term
\sum_{n\geq 2}a_{n}^{2}
is the squared norm of the “stiff” modes. We can represent it by
{\bm{d}}^{\rm s}
, where,

{\bm{d}}^{\rm s}:=\sum_{n\geq 2}a_{n}{\bm{\psi}}_{n},\qquad(d^{\rm s})^{2}=\sum_{n\geq 2}a_{n}^{2}.

(230)

The quantity
(d^{\rm s})^{2}
measures how much of the displacement lives outside the soft eigen-space. On the other hand,
\langle{\bm{d}},\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}{\bm{d}}\rangle
is the quadratic energy. It measures the amount of quadratic energy the displacement costs.
Thus, the inequality tells us that if the quadratic energy is small, then the amplitude outside the soft mode must be small. This is the precise mathematical statement of “soft-mode dominance”. In other words, this means that

d_{k}({\bf x})\approx a_{1}\psi_{k}^{(1)}({\bf x}).

(231)

We note that the spectral difference
\lambda_{\rm D}
ensures that only one displacement pattern is soft at the instability; all others remain costly. Since all stiff modes cost finite quadratic energy, any low-energy displacement must suppress them, leaving only the soft mode.

B.15
Structure of the soft eigenmode
{\bm{\psi}}^{(1)}_{k}({\bf x})

The linear Hessian
\mathcal{H}_{\rm lin}
has constant coefficients since we still have
d=0
.
Therefore the eigenmodes can be chosen as plane waves

\psi_{k}^{(1)}({\bf x})=\hat{\psi}_{k}~e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bf x}}

(232)

Since
{\bm{q}}=|q|{\bm{n}}
,
{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bf x}=|q|({\bm{m}}\cdot{\bf x})

We define a new scalar coordinate

Appendix D
Hessian is self adjoint

Here we show that the Hessian operator
\mathcal{H}
derived from the second variation of the energy functional is self-adjoint. This ensures real eigenvalues, which correspond to normal mode frequencies or stability exponents. Self-adjointness guarantees that the system’s energy is real and that time evolution (if considered) preserves norm. Self-adjoint operators have a complete set of orthogonal eigenfunctions, which allows us to expand arbitrary perturbations in eigenmodes, identify instabilities through the sign of the smallest eigenvalue, and use variational methods to estimate critical stresses.

Start from the second variation in the paper

From the explicit Taylor expansion in Equation (71) in the draft the second variation about an equilibrium solution
f(\xi)
is

\delta^{2}F[f;\eta]=\int\left[a(\xi)(\eta^{\prime})^{2}+V(\xi)\eta^{2}\right]d\xi,

(264)

where,
\eta=\delta f
, and
\eta^{\prime}=\delta f^{\prime}
, and

\displaystyle a(\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu+\Sigma(m)+\frac{3}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)(f^{\prime}(\xi))^{2}\ ,

\displaystyle V(\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\mathcal{A}}+3{\mathcal{B}}f^{2}(\xi)\ .

(265)

Here
a(\xi)
is the strain-dependent stiffness (since it depends on
f^{\prime}(\xi)
).

D.0.1
Convert the quadratic form into an operator
H

The general rule is: an operator
H
is associated to
\delta^{2}F
if

\delta^{2}F[u;\eta]=\int\eta(\xi)(H\eta)(\xi)d\xi

(266)

(up to a conventional factor
1/2
). Start with the first term:

\int a(\xi)(\eta^{\prime})^{2}d\xi.

(267)

Integrating by parts once:

\int a(\eta^{\prime})^{2}d\xi=\int a\eta^{\prime}\eta^{\prime}d\xi=\Big[a\eta\eta^{\prime}\Big]_{\partial}-\int\eta\frac{d}{d\xi}\big(a\eta^{\prime}\big)d\xi.

(268)

Under periodic BCs or variations vanishing at boundaries, the boundary term is zero, so:

\int a(\eta^{\prime})^{2}d\xi=-\int\eta\frac{d}{d\xi}\big(a\eta^{\prime}\big)d\xi.

(269)

Therefore (
266
) becomes

\delta^{2}F[f;\eta]=\int\eta\left[-\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(a(\xi)\frac{d\eta}{d\xi}\right)+V(\xi)\eta\right]d\xi.

(270)

So the Hessian operator is

\mathcal{H}=-\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(a(\xi)\frac{d}{d\xi}\right)+V(\xi),

(271)

with
a(\xi),V(\xi)
given by (
D
). This is exactly the self-adjoint Sturm–Liouville form under Dirichlet boundary conditions.”

Self-adjointness with
a(\xi)
dependence on strain

Self-adjointness is a statement about the operator acting on test functions
\eta
, not about whether coefficients depend on the background solution
f(\xi)
. Here
a(\xi)
is a given real function of
\xi
once we fix the equilibrium profile
f(\xi)
. It doesn’t matter that
a
came from
u^{\prime}
; for the linearized problem it is just a real coefficient function.

Explicit self-adjointness check:
Let us take two smooth test functions
\phi(\xi)
and
\psi(\xi)
satisfying the same boundary conditions (vanishing at boundaries), and consider the inner product,

\langle\phi,\psi\rangle=\int\phi(\xi)\psi(\xi)d\xi.

(272)

Compute
\langle\phi,H\psi\rangle
using (
271
):

\langle\phi,\mathcal{H}\psi\rangle=\int\phi\left[-\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(a\psi^{\prime}\right)+V\psi\right]d\xi.

(273)

This can be split into two parts:

Gradient term:

\int\phi\left[-(a\psi^{\prime})^{\prime}\right]d\xi=-\Big[\phi,a\psi^{\prime}\Big]_{\partial}+\int\phi^{\prime}a\psi^{\prime}d\xi.

(274)

Boundary term vanishes under the admissible BCs, so the gradient term equals

\int a\phi^{\prime}\psi^{\prime}d\xi.

(275)

Potential term:

\int\phi V\psi d\xi

(276)

is symmetric because
V(\xi)
is real. Therefore

\langle\phi,\mathcal{H}\psi\rangle=\int a\phi^{\prime}\psi^{\prime}d\xi+\int V\phi\psi d\xi.

(277)

Now compute
\langle H\phi,\psi\rangle
. By the same steps we get

\langle\mathcal{H}\phi,\psi\rangle=\int a\phi^{\prime}\psi^{\prime}d\xi+\int V\phi\psi d\xi.

(278)

Comparing (
277
) and (
278
),

\langle\phi,H\psi\rangle=\langle H\phi,\psi\rangle.

(279)

That is exactly self-adjointness. Thus, Hessian is self-adjoint in the standard
L^{2}
inner product provided (i)
a(\xi)
and
V(\xi)
are real and sufficiently smooth, and (ii) boundary terms vanish.

Connection with the Full Hessian

If we expand (
271
) we recover the paper’s expression:

-\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(a(\xi)\frac{d}{d\xi}\right)=-a(\xi)\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}-a^{\prime}(\xi)\frac{d}{d\xi}.

(280)

With

a(\xi)=\mu+\Sigma(m)+\frac{3}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)(f^{\prime})^{2},

(281)

this becomes precisely the “derivative of
((f^{\prime})^{2})
times derivative operator” term:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\mu+\Sigma(m)\right)\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}-\frac{3}{2}(\lambda+2\mu)\frac{d}{d\xi}\left((f^{\prime})^{2}\frac{d}{d\xi}\right)

(282)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}+3{\mathcal{B}}f^{2},

which is the full expression for Hessian derived in the main text.

Appendix E
Writing a uniform “modified tanh” equation in the whole range

We saw that we cannot have a single exact
\tanh(\xi)
in the brittle case. This is because the core scale is
L_{c}
where as the tail scale is
\ell_{0}
. However, we can write a tanh-form solution in a mathematically correct way if we consider,

f(\xi)=f_{0}\,\tanh\big(\phi(\xi)\big),

(283)

where
\phi(\xi)
is not assumed linear. We determine
\phi(\xi)
from the exact first integral.

Differentiating the above equation, we have:

f^{\prime}=f_{0}\,\sech^{2}(\phi)\,\phi^{\prime}.

(284)

Now insert it into the exact first integral (
96
) written in
U=\tanh\phi
, and noting that

1-U^{2}=1-\tanh^{2}\phi=\sech^{2}\phi,

(285)

then (
96
) becomes an equation for
\phi^{\prime}
:

\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}\Big(f_{0}^{2}\sech^{4}\phi\,\phi^{\prime 2}\Big)+\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{4}\Big(f_{0}^{4}\sech^{8}\phi\,\phi^{\prime 4}\Big)=\frac{{\mathcal{B}}}{4}\Big(f_{0}^{4}\sech^{4}\phi\Big).

(286)

Divide by
\frac{f_{0}^{4}}{4}\sech^{4}\phi
:

2\epsilon_{1}\frac{1}{f_{0}^{2}}\phi^{\prime 2}+\epsilon_{2}\sech^{4}\phi\,\phi^{\prime 4}={\mathcal{B}}.

(287)

Now we use
f_{0}^{2}={\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{B}}
to simplify
2\epsilon_{1}/f_{0}^{2}=2\sigma{\mathcal{B}}/{\mathcal{A}}
. Let

c=\frac{2\epsilon_{1}{\mathcal{B}}}{{\mathcal{A}}}.

(288)

Then the exact
\phi
equation is

c\,\phi^{\prime 2}+\epsilon_{2}\,\sech^{4}\phi\,\phi^{\prime 4}={\mathcal{B}}.

(289)

This is a quadratic equation in
\phi^{\prime 2}
, so we can solve it explicitly:
Let
z=\phi^{\prime 2}
. Then

\epsilon_{2}\sech^{4}\phi\,z^{2}+cz-{\mathcal{B}}=0,

(290)

so

\phi^{\prime 2}(\phi)=\frac{-c+\sqrt{c^{2}+4\epsilon_{2}{\mathcal{B}}\,\sech^{4}\phi}}{2\epsilon_{2}\,\sech^{4}\phi}.

(291)

This gives us the general strong-slope kink in a tanh form:

f(\xi)=f_{0}\tanh\phi(\xi),\qquad\frac{d\phi}{d\xi}=\sqrt{\frac{-c+\sqrt{c^{2}+4\epsilon_{2}{\mathcal{B}}\,\sech^{4}\phi}}{2\epsilon_{2}\,\sech^{4}\phi}.}

(292)

And then
\xi

\xi-\xi_{0}=\int_{0}^{\phi(\xi)}\frac{d\psi}{\sqrt{\frac{-c+\sqrt{c^{2}+4\epsilon_{2}{\mathcal{B}}\,\sech^{4}\psi}}{2\epsilon_{2}\,\sech^{4}\psi}}}.

(293)

Note that in the tails,

\phi^{\prime}\to 1/\ell_{0}

\ell_{0}

\sech^{4}\phi\sim 1
, and
\phi^{\prime}

, recovering the strong-slope core scaling. So this automatically takes care of “modified tanh”.

Thus, when
\alpha

) gives

\phi^{\prime 2}\sim\frac{\sqrt{4\alpha{\mathcal{B}}}-c}{2\epsilon_{2}}\sim\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{B}}}{\epsilon_{2}}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\phi^{\prime}\sim\frac{1}{L_{c}},

(294)

while in the tail

(295)

Therefore:

\phi^{\prime}(\xi)\sim\begin{cases}1/L_{c},&\text{core},\\[4.0pt]
1/\ell_{0},&\text{tails}.\end{cases}

(296)

So the solution is tanh-like with a coordinate that behaves differently in the core and in the tails.
