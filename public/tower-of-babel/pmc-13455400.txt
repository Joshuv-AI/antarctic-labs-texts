Health risk assessment of wildfire smoke exposure: a protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chala Daba Yadate, Daniel Odo, Md Rezanur Rahaman, Hilary Bambrick, Michael Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWildfire, driven by climate change, continues to pose significant health, social, economic and environmental challenges in the twenty-first century. Although the association between wildfire smoke exposure and health outcomes has been studied, comprehensive systematic reviews and meta-analyses on the global health risks of wildfire smoke exposure are lacking, and the quality and strength of evidence remain uncertain. Therefore, we aim to undertake a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the health risks associated with wildfire smoke exposure at global, regional and national levels in the context of climate change, evaluate the quality and strength of available evidence and identify evidence gaps for future research.MethodsThis review will adhere to the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-2020) guidelines. PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science will be searched to identify relevant articles. A group of researchers will independently screen the retrieved articles using Covidence, based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. A narrative synthesis will be performed for the included studies. A random-effects meta-analysis will be performed using R software (version 4.3) to determine the pooled association between wildfire smoke air pollutants and health outcomes. Higgins’ I2 and Egger’s test will be employed to assess heterogeneity and publication bias, respectively. Sensitivity and sub-group analysis will also be performed. Risk of bias will be assessed using the Office of Health Assessment and Translation Risk of Bias Rating Tool for Human Studies. The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation guidelines will be used to evaluate the quality of evidence. The strength of evidence will be rated as either sufficient, limited, inadequate, or lacking according to the Navigation Guide Framework.DiscussionsThis systematic review and meta-analysis will explore the health risk associated with wildfire smoke exposure across global, regional and national levels encompassing a range of climate zones. The findings from this meta-analysis will provide updated evidence to guide policy makers and communities in efforts to reduce mortality and morbidity associated with wildfire-related air pollutants. The results will also help healthcare providers and emergency responders in managing the growing health risk of wildfire-related air pollutant smoke exposure.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD420250654944.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-026-03226-6.Keywords: Wildfire, Bushfire, Health risk assessment, Systematic review, Exposure, Air pollutant, Particulate matter, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Background and rationale

Climate change has heightened the global risk of wildfire (bushfire) by promoting conditions conducive to fire ignition and spread, such as higher temperatures, stronger winds and anomalous rainfall patterns [1]. Growing attention is being paid to wildfire smoke exposure due to its substantial environmental, economic and health impacts worldwide [2–5]. The 2019–2020 Australian Black Summer bushfires, for example, are estimated to have released 806 megatons of carbon dioxide, incurred economic losses of A$100 billion and resulted in 450 deaths [6–8]. The frequency and severity of wildfires are predicted to increase in the future, leading to burning for longer durations in many parts of the world [9–12]. For example, the reports from the United Nation Environment Programme and the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change predict that climate change will lead to 50% increase wildfire events in Western US, Northern Siberia, Central India and Eastern Australia [13], and 10–30% in North America and a 74–118% increase in the area burned in Canada by 2100 [12].

Epidemiological evidence has shown that wildfire smoke is associated with a broad range of adverse health outcomes, including mortality and morbidity related to respiratory diseases [14–18], cardiovascular diseases [14, 16–18], cancers [19, 20], mental health illnesses [21–23], diabetes [24, 25] and adverse birth outcomes [26–30]. These effects of wildfire on health will exert substantial pressure on the health system, such as the need to expand emergency services to address the immediate acute health effects of wildfire exposure and ongoing healthcare services for the treatment of chronic conditions exacerbated by wildfire, particularly in the context of climate change [31, 32]. Globally, wildfires contribute to approximately 1.53 million all-cause deaths annually, primarily from cardiovascular and respiratory mortality [33]. A study conducted in 749 cities from 43 countries also showed that exposure to fine particulate matter (≤ 2.5 µm or less in diameter) from wildfire was associated with 33,510 all-cause deaths (95% CI: 26,204–40,763), 6993 cardiovascular deaths (95% CI: 5466–8510) and 3503 respiratory deaths (95% CI: 2739–4259) [3]. A recent study conducted in Australia, analysing 416,057 records of cardiorespiratory emergency department visits during the 2019–2020 wildfire season, showed that a 6.0% (95% CI: 1.9–10.3) increase in respiratory-related emergency department visits and 10.0% (95% CI: 5.0–15.2) increase in cardiovascular-related emergency department visits due to wildfire smoke [34]. Likewise, Korsiak et al. [19] analysed a cohort of 20 million participants and found that exposure to wildfire smoke within 50 km of a wildfire source increased the risk of lung cancer by 4.9% (adjusted hazard ratio [HD] = 1.049, 95% CI 1.028–1.071).

However, not all findings are consistent. Some studies reported mixed or non-significant associations between certain wildfire-related air pollutants and health outcomes [35–37]. For instance, a study conducted in California indicated that exposure to particulate matter (PM) from wildfire was significantly associated with hospital admissions for asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, while exposure to ozone (O3) from wildfire showed a non-significant association with hospital admissions for respiratory diseases but a significant association with asthma-related emergency department visits [36]. These findings highlight the need for further research in this field.

Projected increase in wildfire will further exacerbate population health in different regions. For instance, wildfire-related premature mortality are projected to increase in Western US by 60% by the end of 2090, resulting in an estimated total cost of USD 36 billion to USD 43 billion annually [38]. Similarly, PM2.5 exposure from wildfires in China is projected to increase mortality by 37.38% by 2100 under socioeconomic pathway (SSP) scenario SSP2–4.5 [39]. As wildfire become more frequent and intense globally due to worsening climate change [10, 11, 40–43], and as the total number of population exposed to wildfires continues to rise [44], research on the health risk assessment of wildfire smoke exposure will play an increasingly critical role in the future climate change risk assessment and adaptation. This understanding is essential for further developing robust climate change-related health risk assessment guidelines and providing evidence-based guidance to protect vulnerable population groups [45].

Despite several reviews conducted on health risks associated with wildfire smoke exposure previously, they are often limited to a single wildfire smoke air pollutant, focusing primarily on wildfire PM2.5, while other pollutants such as PM10 and O3 have not been investigated [46, 47]. The quality and strength of the evidence were not assessed [46, 48–50] and limit the identification of robust risk estimates across vulnerable population groups [49]. Furthermore, although local climatic conditions are recognised as important for contextualising wildfire-related health risk, evidence assessing all-cause and cause-specific mortality and morbidity across climate zones remains limited. These underscore the need for further research on the health risk assessment and adaptation associated with wildfire smoke exposure, encompassing a broader range of wildfire related air pollutants and health outcomes to comprehensively estimate and ultimately reduce the burden on healthcare systems during wildfire events. To address these gaps, we systematically review and quantitatively synthesise existing evidence on the associations between wildfire smoke exposure (PM2.5, PM10 and O3) and morbidity and mortality outcomes, and assess the quality and strength of the available evidence. Further, we examine populations susceptible to morbidity and mortality outcomes associated with wildfire smoke exposure and conduct meta-analyses that incorporate geographical variability based on the Köppen-Geiger climate zones and regions.

Objective

This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to examine the health risks associated with wildfire smoke exposure in the context of climate change and update the assessment of quality and strength of synthesized evidence presented in previous reviews. This review is structured according to the Population-Exposure-Comparisons-Outcomes (PECO) framework [51] to answer the question: “In the general population (P), what are the health outcomes (O) associated with wildfire smoke exposure (E), as compared to populations with minimal or no exposure to wildfire smoke (C), as observed in human epidemiological studies?”.

Methodology

Protocol development and registration

This protocol adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines to ensure transparency and rigor in its reporting [52] (Supplementary Material File 1). This systematic review and meta-analysis protocol has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO): CRD420250654944. This review will be reported in accordance with the PRISMA-2020 guidelines [53] (Fig. 1).

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search strategy was developed by reviewing previously published articles [19, 46, 48, 50, 54], with the assistance of an Australian National University librarian. The PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science databases will be used to search the relevant articles. The databases will be searched with the following keywords for the wildfire exposure terms: (Wildfire* OR Bushfire* OR “Bushfire smoke exposure” OR “Bush fire” OR brushfire* OR “vegetation fire” OR “vegetation fire smoke” OR “landscape fire” OR “forest fire”); with wildfire air pollutant term: (“particulate matter” OR PM OR “fine particulate matter” OR PM2.5 OR PM10 OR ozone OR O3 OR “carbon monoxide” OR CO OR “sulfur dioxide” OR SO2 OR “nitrogen oxides” OR NOx) and combined with health outcomes term: (cardiovascular* OR “coronary heart” OR “coronary artery” OR “cerebrovascular” OR “peripheral arterial” OR “rheumatic heart” OR “congenital heart” OR “deep vein thrombosis” OR “pulmonary embolism” OR “heart attack” OR “cardiac arrest” OR “heart disease” OR “heart failure “OR “myocardial infarction” OR respirator* OR “respiratory disease” OR “bronchial disease” OR “respiratory tract disease” OR “lung disease” OR “lung function” OR asthma OR wheez* OR “mental health” OR “mental disorder” OR dementia OR “birth outcomes” OR “adverse birth outcomes” OR “low birth weight” OR “preterm birth” OR stillbirth OR disease* OR “health outcomes” OR “health risk” OR “pregnancy outcomes” OR cancer OR “lung cancer” OR diabetes* OR “neonatal mortality” OR mortality OR “all-cause mortality” OR “all-cause morbidity”). The detailed search strategy of each database is documented in Supplementary Material File 2 Table S1.

Study selection, inclusion and exclusion criteria

Study selection

The articles retrieved from all databases will be imported into EndNote version 21 [55], and then they will be exported to the online tool Covidence for screening [56]. After removing duplicate articles using Covidence, two reviewers will independently evaluate articles by titles, abstracts, and full texts. Disagreements during screening will be resolved through group discussion and by consultation with a third reviewer. Studies excluded during full-text screening, along with the rationale for their exclusion, will be documented. A final list of included studies will then be compiled for data extraction. To maximize study retrieval, both forward and background citation tracking methods will be used to update the included studies to the latest and manually search the reference lists of the included studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The following peer-reviewed original research articles on health risk assessment of wildfire smoke exposure will be included in the systematic review and meta-analysis: (1) the exposures of interest are wildfire smoke related air pollutants; (2) the health outcomes are potential health conditions associated with wildfire smoke-related air pollutants, including both morbidity and mortality of these health conditions; (3) studies reported the health effect estimates in relative risk (RR), odds ratio (OR), percent change (PC), excess risk (ER), incidence rate ratio (IRR) or hazard ratio (HR); (4) quantitative studies with a cross-sectional, case–control, case-crossover, cohort or time series designs; and (5) studies were published in English language.

The following types of studies will be excluded: (1) the exposures of interest are not wildfire smoke-related air pollutants; (2) the health outcomes are not associated with wildfire smoke-related air pollutants; (3) studies that do not report health effect estimates, either in tables, figures or text; (4) studies published in languages other than English; (5) studies lacking a full report, and (6) studies classified as conference papers, abstracts, editorials, commentaries, perspectives, opinion papers, case reports or short communications.

Population, exposure, comparator and outcomes framework

Participants/population

Human populations were study participants/population.

Exposure(s)

Wildfire smoke exposure, such as PM2.5, PM10, O3, sulfur dioxide (SO2), oxides of nitrogen (NOx), black carbon and other wildfire-related air pollutants.

Comparator(s)

Populations with minimal or no exposure to wildfire smoke.

Outcome of interest

Health outcomes related to wildfire smoke exposure, such as all-cause mortality and morbidity, cardiovascular diseases, respiratory diseases, mental health disorders, cancer, diabetes and adverse birth outcomes.

Setting

Studies conducted in any countries are included.

Year of publication

Year of publication is not restricted.

Data extraction

Data will be extracted by two independent researchers using customized data extraction template, consisting of author names, year of publication, age of the study participants, sex, year of studies carried out, country/states where studies carried out, wildfire air pollutants (PM2.5, PM10 and O3, and other wildfire related air pollutant), lag, effect size (OR, RR, PC, ER, IRR or HR), climate zones, study design, statistical model, exposure assessment methods and main findings. The details data extraction template is documented in Supplementary Excel. The extracted data will be exported to R software (version 4.3) for data analysis.

Data synthesis and meta-analysis

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis will be conducted for the included studies. Specifically, the characteristics of the included studies will be synthesized based on health outcomes, wildfire air pollutants, countries where studies were conducted, study population and study design.

Meta-analysis

The random-effects model meta-analysis will be employed for studies where the exposures are wildfires, the human health risks induced by wildfires and studies that report the effect size as RR, OR, HR, PC, ER and IRR. Der Simonian and Laird random effect models will be used to estimate the overall pooled effect size of wildfire smoke related air pollutants [57]. If the effect estimate of the study (health risk) is reported as OR, it will be converted to RR using standard methods [58–61]: RR=OR1-P0+(P0∗OR) where Po is baseline risk. When OR and HR involve low-incidence outcomes, they will be assumed to approximate RRs [62, 63]. PC and ER will be transformed into RR using the following equations: RR=1+PC100 and 1 + ER [64], respectively. For studies that present effect estimates in graphs, the effect size will be extracted using the WebPlot Digitizer tool [67]. For studies that report only the effect size and p value, a 95% confidence interval (CI) will be calculated using the method of Altman and Bland [68]. Subgroup analysis will also be performed to identify potentially vulnerable populations based on the study locations, age, sex, lags, seasons and health outcomes for each wildfire-related air pollutant.

Heterogeneity and sensitivity analysis

Higgins I2 will be used to determine the heterogeneity of the included studies. Values of < 25%, 25–75% and ˃ 75% will represent low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively [69]. Sensitivity analysis will also be performed using leave-one-out analysis, and outlier detection will be conducted to identify the influence of individual estimates [70]. Egger’s test will be used to determine the publication bias of the included studies [71]. A p value less than 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Risk of bias, quality and strength of evidence assessment

The modified version of Navigation Guide Framework for Systematic Review in Environmental Health (NGFSREH) will be used to assess the quality and strength of the evidence of the included studies [72]. Firstly, the risk of bias (ROB) will be assessed using the Office Health Assessment and Translation (OHAT) Risk of Bias Rating Tool for Human Studies [73]. The key criteria for ROB evaluation methods are exposure assessment, outcome assessment, confounding bias, selection/recruitment bias, and selective reporting. Details of the ROB assessment criteria and their corresponding rating scales are presented in Supplementary Material File 2 Table S2. Secondly, the quality of evidence will be assessed using the upgrading and downgrading factors from the GRADE approach [74, 75]. Initially, the quality of the evidence will be rated as “moderate”. Based on GRADE guidelines, the moderate rating will then be either upgraded, downgraded or remain unchanged, resulting in a final overall quality rating of “high”, “moderate” or “low”. The detailed criteria are presented in Supplementary Material File 2 Table S3. Thirdly, the strength of synthesised evidence will be assessed based on the quality of the evidence, the direction of the effect and confidence in the estimate, and then it will be rated as “sufficient”, “limited”, “inadequate” or “lacking” [72].

Discussion

Although previous systematic reviews have examined the association between wildfire smoke exposure and health risks, they are often limited to one wildfire smoke air pollutant, primarily focusing on exposure to PM2.5 from wildfires while other pollutants such as PM10 and O3 have not been explored [46, 47]. To the best of our knowledge, this review is among the first global studies to explore the health risks associated with wildfire smoke exposure including PM2.5, PM10 and O3 across a wide spectrum of mortality and morbidity health outcomes, such as respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, mental health problems, diabetes and adverse birth outcomes across climate zones and regions. These health impacts place substantial pressure on the healthcare system, requiring emergency services to address the immediate health effects and enhance healthcare services for the treatment of chronic conditions caused by wildfire smoke exposure [31, 32]. In this context, our systematic review and meta-analysis will generate comprehensive, and policy-relevant evidence on wildfire health risk assessment, to inform health decision-makers about wildfire-related mortality and morbidity. This evidence will also be crucial for guiding healthcare and emergency services in making informed decisions and implementing targeted public health interventions and strategies to protect vulnerable groups during wildfire events. It will also support immediate responses and promote community resilience to wildfires.

The findings from this review will be compared with and discussed in relation to previous studies and guidelines from different countries [1, 46–48, 50]. We also believe that our findings will contribute to Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3–Good Health and Wellbeing, particularly by supporting efforts to reduce the global maternal mortality (SDG 3.1), neonatal and child mortality (SDG 3.2), non-communicable diseases (SDG 3.4) including cardiorespiratory diseases and mental health disorders, and improve environmental health (SDG 3.9) by 2030 [76].

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we will assess the risk of bias using a modified version of the OHAT Risk of Bias Rating Tool for Human Studies [73] and evaluate the quality and strength of synthesised evidence using a modified GRADE approach [74, 75]. These methods will enhance the robustness of the study findings, significantly contribute to improved knowledge and inform future studies on the health risks of wildfire smoke exposure in the context of climate change. The resulting protocol will provide a clear and transparent framework for identifying, extracting and synthesizing relevant information.
