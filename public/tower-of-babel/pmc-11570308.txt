A production-inventory model to optimize the operation of distributed energy resource networks in a rolling horizon
Authors: Pablo Cortés, Alejandro Escudero-Santana, Elena Barbadilla-Martin, José Guadix
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract.-The recent advancements in energy production, storage, and distribution are creating unprecedented opportunities in the field. Major consumers can benefit from the implementation of distributed energy resource networks capable of generating electricity or heating from sources, often renewable ones, in close proximity to the point of use, rather than relying on centralized generation sources from power plants. In this paper, we introduce a pioneering model designed to determine the optimal set of energy commands in a distributed energy resource network, minimizing operational costs in a time horizon. Indeed, we propose an innovative mixed-integer linear programming formulation rooted in the production-inventory models commonly employed in aggregate production planning. The system integrates diverse energy generation sources, storage facilities, and demand points, encompassing both electric and heating commodities. The optimum of the model is achieved for all analyzed instances of the test library (2 scenarios-20 instances) in an exceptionally short time, outperforming other approaches previously presented in the literature. We employed the Gurobi optimizer to solve the model, obtaining rapid responses that ensure real-time decision-making and facilitate effective control of the distributed energy resource network within a three-days’ rolling horizon, as discussed in a simulated real-life application case study. Indeed, the proposed model solves in less than 1 s, enabling near-instantaneous decision-making. This swift solution time surpasses any known references in the field, effectively shifting the bottleneck in DER network operation from the decision-making process to the forecasting of demand and weather conditions. While forecasting typically requires a minimum of 15 min, our approach suggests that a reduction in this forecasting time could further enhance the control system's response time, given the model's ability to deliver optimal solutions almost immediately. The real-time availability of optimal solutions allows for the seamless incorporation of stochastic elements into the control loop via a rolling horizon process.

Introduction

The complexities surrounding energy production, storage, and distribution represent a significant challenge on the horizon [[1], [2], [3]]. The emergence of intelligent networks, capable of identifying the most effective methods for energy generation, coupled with the potential for efficient energy storage, has become a key aspect at both urban and building scales [4,5]. These advancements are set to play a crucial role in molding the energy scenario of the near future, where progress in clean energy technologies and achieving carbon neutrality under resource constraints are essential goals [6,7]. In this context, the energy cost is a growing and significant factor for large energy consumers. Meanwhile energy costs are increasing, distributed generation costs are progressively being reduced. In this line, the term distributed generation arises as an energy cost saving opportunity [8,9]. It relates to the possibility of generating electricity or heating from sources, often-renewable energy sources, near the point of use instead of centralized generation sources from power plants. Distributed Energy Resources (DER) refers to often-smaller generation units that are located on the consumer's side of the meter and can include energy generation and energy storage capabilities. In fact, self-consumption emerges as an actual possibility to reduce the energy costs of large consumers using alternative distributed energy generation sources such as cogeneration and other renewable energy systems (RES) such as solar (thermal or photovoltaic) or wind (see Ref. [10] for an analysis about payback of the required investments in DER scenarios or [11] for a discussion on efficiency in wind energy generation optimization).

For all these reasons, Distributed Energy Resource networks emerge as a key opportunity in the new paradigms based on sustainability and energy self-sufficiency. However, the combination of complex energy resources, their different modes of operation, and how they must supply the required energy from the demand side faces new and emerging challenges and requires the design of models capable of performing this task efficiently and optimally, allowing for economically viable operation. The objective of our paper is to develop a control scheme for a Distributed Energy Resource network using a mathematical programming model that optimizes the cost-effective operation of DER networks. This model provides an optimal combination of energy resources for the control system of the DER network, enabling real-time control in response to changes in energy demand and weather conditions through quick model-solving response times via a rolling horizon approach.

The remainder of the paper proceeds with a literature review on modeling DER network operation, followed by a discussion in Section 2 on how our research advances beyond the current state of the art. Then the specific characterization of the problem is presented in Section 3, providing a comprehensive overview. Following that, Section 4 presents the mixed-integer linear programming model, employing the previously introduced production-inventory paradigm, providing insights into the formulation. Section 5 is dedicated to the computational experimentation, contrasting it with other previous approaches. This section also features a case study that illustrates the performance of the control system, paying special attention to the energy systems’ commands along the rolling horizon and how the control systems is able to respond to variations in the expected forecasts. Finally, Section 6 brings the main conclusions from the research, summarizing the key findings and remarking the contributions.

Literature review and progress beyond the state of the art

Distributed Energy Resource networks can involve a wide variety of energy systems. Considering different combinations of such equipment can increase the complexity of modeling and complicate the process of achieving near real-time optimal operation of the various energy resources. In this line, cogeneration and storage energy systems are of a pivotal relevance when modelling such networks. So, the use cogeneration can couple different energy networks (such as electric and heating), while storage systems enable the use of energy generated in earlier periods to meet demands in subsequent periods.

In scenarios where electric and heating networks coexist, cogeneration emerges as a significant element by effectively coupling both systems. Cogeneration is mainly produced by Combined Heat and Power (CHP) systems, which the capability of simultaneous generation of thermal and electric energy. CHP systems are distinguished by their acknowledged energy efficiency, low environmental emissions, and a certain level of autonomy from centralized power grids. These systems demonstrate the capability to generate electric energy while simultaneously harnessing thermal energy from typically discarded sources, thereby contributing to efficient heating processes [12].

Additionally, energy storage systems such as electric batteries or thermal storage can be adequately used to contribute to reduce the purchase of energy grid during those peak periods with higher prices of the energy. Indeed, the efficiency of modern electric batteries currently allows for the effective decoupling of production and demand concepts. This enables the production of energy during periods when the costs of electricity production or acquisition are lower, or when weather conditions favor higher production yields from RES. Subsequently, this stored energy can be consumed during periods of higher demand, which often coincide with more expensive pricing intervals or when energy production from RES is no longer feasible, see Ref. [13] for an economic analysis on the viability of the electric batteries in an office building. On the other hand, technological advancements in thermal storage have also been enabling a more efficient utilization of this storage capacity at the level of heating networks [14] constituting and adequate approach to decoupling production and demand in heating networks.

Therefore, a large consumer implementing a comprehensive Distributed Energy Resource network to meet both electric and heating demands would operate an electric network capable of serving the building's electricity needs through various sources, which could include the electric grid, a Combined Heat and Power (CHP) system, renewable sources (such as photovoltaic and wind power), and electric batteries. Additionally, the system would include a heating network designed to meet the building's heating needs using boilers, CHP, renewable sources (like solar thermal), and thermal storage. The coupling of the electric and heating networks occurs through the CHP system, which concurrently supplies electric energy and heating. The primary objective is to strategically choose the optimal configuration of systems to meet both energy demands while minimizing costs for each considered instant of time. To do so we implement a mathematical programming approach by designing a novel mixed-integer linear programming model able to provide a quick and optimal solution each instant of time it is required.

The application of mathematical programming models to address energy generation and storage problems has garnered considerable attention in the scientific literature. First, there is a set of research papers employing mixed-integer linear programming models to assess the optimality of investment decisions in energy systems, as demonstrated by several authors [15,16]. It corresponds to an analysis from the operator's perspective (supply side).

There is a second set of research papers that focus on using mathematical programming models to optimize operational decisions within the energy network, specifically on how a large consumer can meet its energy demands while minimizing the operating costs of its DER network. It corresponds to an analysis from the customer's perspective (demand side). Our paper falls in this set of the scientific literature.

Many studies have focused on designing models to optimize total heat and electricity production in a CHP system [17], and even on expanding the cogeneration concept to trigeneration by including cold production [18]. Along similar lines, large cogeneration systems for district heating have also been studied using mathematical programming models [19]. A significant contribution in this area is the use of mathematical models based on flow formulations by Refs. [20,21]. Their research developed flow formulations to model coupled electric and heating energy networks, incorporating a CHP system, boilers, and the electric grid. Moreover, the consideration of various energy generation systems, particularly renewable energy sources, has been addressed by optimizing their contribution to energy generation through a multi-objective mathematical programming model, although their network did not include cogeneration systems [22]. In other line, the consideration of energy storage systems together with RES was addressed by Ref. [23], although their work did not considered cogeneration systems.

However, all these prior research works shared a common focus on networks with a limited number of energy production systems and did not consider cogeneration and energy storage equipment together, which significantly complicates network operation. In fact, most of the studies tend to focus predominantly on the generation of optimal set-points for the case of the CHP system without considering the holistic view provided by an extended distributed energy resources network including generation and storage capabilities. However, they often overlook the holistic perspective provided by a full distributed energy resources network including both generation and storage capabilities. Actually, there are not many papers dealing with a full comprehensive DER network.

One of the first studies that considered a more comprehensive network that encompasses a broader spectrum of energy systems, including CHP, RES and energy storage systems was presented in the works [24,25]. Their proposal incorporated initialization costs by using binary variables, converting the problem into a mixed-integer non-linear programming model while still employing a flow formulation. Both papers proposed a deterministic approach capable of providing an efficient solution every 15 min, which was novel at the time. The adoption of metaheuristics, such as genetic algorithms, proved effective in generating promising solutions, although without the guarantee of optimality. However, they did not account for possible variations in the demand curve or RES forecasting. This limitation can be addressed by considering stochastic programming, as seen in Refs. [26,27], although the stochastic nature of these models results in an operating response time of about an hour, quadrupling the response time observed in Refs. [24,25].

In our research, we address a comprehensive Distributed Energy Resource network, as illustrated in Fig. 1, which includes Combined Heat and Power systems, boilers, Renewable Energy Source systems (photovoltaic, solar thermal and wind energy), and energy storage systems such as batteries and thermal storage. Additionally, our approach aims to overcome the limitations identified in previous studies by introducing a novel and simplified mixed-integer linear programming formulation adapted from the production-inventory models commonly used in aggregate production planning (see Ref. [28] for a review for the case of multi-factory production networks and [[29], [30], [31]] for an in depth review on production-inventory formulations). The formulation considers two different commodities in the network: the electric and heating energy. In this context, the multi-period energy demand for each commodity can be fulfilled either from a set of energy generation nodes (analogous to the multi-factory concept in production planning) or from the existing energy storage nodes (equivalent to the inventory concept in production planning).Fig. 1Conceptual architecture of the global system.Fig. 1

The problem

The problem revolves around identifying the most cost-effective approach to meet the electricity and heating demands of a large energy consumer over a day discretized into periods (t = 1, 2, …|T|). This consumer has various options for fulfilling their demand. Specifically, electricity can be procured directly from the grid, sourced from renewable energy, or generated through a combined heat and power system. Similarly, heating can be obtained from a thermal solar renewable system, a boiler, or the CHP system. Additionally, the consumer is equipped with batteries to store excess electricity and thermal storage systems to retain surplus heat. In the event of producing excess electricity that cannot be stored in the batteries, the consumer has the option to sell it to the market at a specified market price. Fig. 1 represents the described architecture and the ways that a large consumer can proceed to meet the electric and heating demand it generates from the sources described above. The figure uses different colors to represent various energy sources and components. Blue color is used for electric energy sources, including the electric grid (top left), Renewable Energy Sources (RES) such as photovoltaic and wind energy (under the grid), and batteries (center top). Magenta color represents the CHP system, located in the center left of the figure. Orange color is used for heating energy sources, including the thermal solar system and boiler (bottom left) and thermal storage (bottom center). Electric and heating energy flows are indicated by arrows following the same color scheme. On the right side of the figure, the large consumer box is shown along with the market (in blue), where excess electric energy can be sold, and a final box indicating excess heating that is not recovered and is therefore wasted (in orange).

This architecture can be represented as a pair of networks coupled by the presence of the CHP (represented in magenta color at Fig. 1), which participates in both blue colored electric and orange colored heating networks.

To meet the energy demand of the large consumer, the entire system relies on a control system operating within a rolling horizon over discrete periods (t = {1, 2, …, |T|}). Fig. 2 outlines the concept of a Distributed Energy Resource Control System (DERCS). At each time period, updated weather and energy demand forecasts, and the status of storage facilities (batteries and thermal storage) are provided. These data, combined with the cost repository, are input into the optimizer engine to determine energy commands for each energy generation source and storage facility. One of the aims of the control system is to be able to provide new commands in a time period as short as possible, focusing its performance to a real-time control system. To attain such objective the optimizer needs to provide the solution as quick as possible.Fig. 2Distributed energy resource control system (DERCS).Fig. 2

Distributed energy resource control system (DERCS).

The system operates within a rolling horizon framework. In each period, t, the optimizer is tasked with solving the problem for the subsequent t to t+|T| periods. However, the commands generated are just applied only to the immediate period, t. This is because, in the following period, t+1, a new calculation, incorporating updated weather and demand forecasts, is conducted for the subsequent t+1 to t+|T|+1 periods. The commands for this newly calculated horizon are then implemented in the next period, and this process iterates accordingly.

The production inventory model

In this section, we present a mixed integer linear programming optimization model based on a production-inventory optimization model. This model constitutes the core of the control system described in Fig. 2. To define the model, a graph structure is conceptualized by nodes set as production sources and demand, as well as inventory processes as described before.

Firstly, it is important to highlight that Fig. 1 illustrates a pair of energy networks: an electric energy network and a heating network interconnected through the presence of the Combined Heat and Power (CHP) system. This intricate configuration can be mathematically formulated as a complex problem involving multiple production sources, demand points, inventories, and capacitated arcs spanning a temporal horizon. Fig. 3 visually represents such a model. The graphs on the left (a) depict two distinct graphs, each representing a single commodity. We have used the same color convention as in Fig. 1: the blue network corresponds to the electric network, while the orange network represents the thermal network. The CHP (magenta color) serves as a coupling element between both networks by producing electric and thermal flows supplied from the gas grid.Fig. 3Production-inventory graph with multiple production sources and capacitated arcs.Fig. 3

Production-inventory graph with multiple production sources and capacitated arcs.

In Fig. 3, side (a), on the left-hand side, the energy sources and the arcs associated with the generated energy flows are depicted. The variables xjt on the arcs represent the flows supplied by source j in period t, where Kjt signifies the capacity of the arc, indicating the maximum energy level that the source can supply (e.g., in the case of RES, it depends on wind or insolation forecasting). On the right-hand side, the representation includes the required demands (Dt) and the excess energy being produced (et), along with inventory processes from one period t to the next (t+1). The variables It represent the level of stored energy at the end of period t, dependent on each energy network (batteries or thermal storage), and IM represents the maximum storage capacity.

Nevertheless, these two networks can be jointly addressed by considering a multicommodity flow graph, specified by the subindex k, where k = 1, 2 (with k = 1 denoting electric flows and k = 2 indicating thermal flows). Fig. 3, side (b), illustrates the multicommodity network in black. Each energy system contributes one single commodity flow, except for the CHP, which supplies both commodity flows. In this scenario, the variables xjkt on the arcs represent the flow supplied by source j of commodity k in period t, with Kjkt denoting the corresponding capacity. Similarly, Ikt signifies the level of stored energy of commodity k at the end of period t, and IMk represents the maximum storage capacity for such commodity in the corresponding storage system.

The outlined problem can be conceptualized as an inventory-production problem involving multicommodity flows, incorporating multiple production sources, inventories, and capacitated arcs to fulfill a predetermined demand within a given temporal horizon.

One relevant issue that needs some discussion concerns the inventory nodes, namely the electric batteries and the thermal storage systems. In a long-term analysis, such systems should account for round-trip efficiency losses, as discussed by Ref. [32]. However, over a short-term period (e.g., within a few weeks horizon), these efficiency losses can be disregarded as they are relatively minor. Therefore, given the horizon analyzed in our paper and our primary focus on a simple and easy-to-implement model, we chose not to incorporate round-trip efficiency losses into our model.

A final important aspect of the model concerns the performance of each energy system. This can be seen graphically in Fig. 4, where the raw energy entering the system results in some energy losses, yielding a net energy flow. This phenomenon necessitates a specific formulation, which is captured through additional constraints in the model.Fig. 4Performance at the energy systems.Fig. 4

Equation (1) corresponds to the objective function, aiming to minimize the total operational costs. These costs encompass both the fixed costs associated with initiating the energy generation sources and storage facilities, as well as the variable operational costs. The objective function also takes into account income generated from electricity sales to the grid provider.

Equation (2) corresponds to the energy balance equation. It states that the total amount of type k energy generated by the type k energy generation sources in period t, along with the amount of type k energy stored at the beginning of the period, must be equal to the demand for type k energy, plus the excess of generated type k energy, and the type k energy stored at the end of the period. Equations (2), (3) corresponds to the graphical description of Fig. 3.

The set of constraints ((3), (8)) regulate the operation of the storage facilities. Equation (3) states that the difference between the type k energy stored at the end and the beginning of period t equals the energy charged minus the energy discharged at the storage facility during the period. It is important to note that if ukt>0 then vkt=0 and vice versa, as both terms are penalized in the objective function (1).

Constraints (4), (5) determine the minimum value for ukt and vkt, respectively. On the one hand, the type k energy storage facility is charged (ukt) during the period t if the amount of type k energy generated exceeds the type k energy demanded plus the excess of type k energy generated. This amount is adjusted based on the performance of the storage facility during the charging process. On the other hand, the type k energy storage facility is discharged (vkt) during period t if the amount of type k energy generated is less than the type k energy demanded plus the excess of type k energy generated. These equations (4), (5) are graphically described in Fig. 4.

Constraint (6) activates the binary variable zkt if the storage facility is engaged in a charging or discharging process during period t. The equation also states that the maximum flow to be charged/discharged is given by UBk. Additionally, constraint (7) determines if the storage facility is initialized in period t. Note that it takes the minimum possible valuable since it is penalized in the objective function (i.e. a value equal to 1 if zkt=1 and zk,t−1=0, and otherwise 0. Moreover, constraint (8) determines that the amount of type k energy in the storage facility cannot exceed the capacity of the battery (k = 1) or the thermal storage (k = 2).

Constraints (9), (10), (11) regulate the supplies from the energy sources and are restricted by the arc capacities. Constraint (9) activates the binary variable yjkt if the type k energy generation source is active in period t, limiting both the maximum and minimum levels of energy flow provided. The value of Kjkt depends on the weather forecasting and the corresponding performance in case of the RES system. For the CHP and boiler, the capacity depends on the maximum flow that the equipment can produce (both electric and thermal energy in the case of the CHP during its operation), as well as it depends on the amount of energy acquired to the grid provider. There is no binary variable associated to the electric grid activation since it is always available and active.

Constraint (10) determines if the type k energy generation source is initialized in period t. Note that it takes the minimum possible value, as it is penalized in the objective function (i.e. a value equal to 1 if yjkt=1 and yjk,t−1=0, and otherwise 0.

Equation (11) relates to the gas acquisition from the grid provider. In the case of generating type k energy at the CHP or boiler, the amount that can be generated depends on the amount of gas acquired from the gas grid provider, adjusted by the performance of the equipment. Note that the CHP generates both electric and thermal energy. This equation appears graphically described in Fig. 4.

Finally, specifications from (12), (13), (14), (15), (16), (17) state the feasible values for continuous variables (non-negative), declare binary variables, and provide the initial conditions for the activation of energy sources and storage facilities, as well as the level of charge of the storage facilities.

Experimentation: the optimizer and the distributed energy resource control system

This section is devoted to the analysis of the operation of the Distributed Energy Resource Control System and the optimizer that enables the appropriate performance of the whole system. Indeed, the optimizer represents the core component of the DERCS control loop illustrated in Fig. 2 and it provides the optimal solution for the Production-Inventory model (PIM) outlined in Section 3. We selected Gurobi optimization software to solve the PIM model. This solver has been developed by Gurobi Optimization, LLC, and it stands as a prescriptive analytics platform and decision-making technology that integrates exact mathematical methods for optimization, and that has been identified as a suitable tool for addressing diverse energy system problems, as extensively explored in Ref. [33]. Additionally, the Institute for Operations Research and the Management Sciences (INFORMS) that is an international society for practitioners in the fields of operations research, management science, and analytics makes a yearly optimization software survey (whose 2023 edition can be found at the website https://pubsonline.informs.org/magazine/orms-today/2023-optimization-software-survey). The survey identifies Gurobi as a suitable optimization software for mixed-integer linear programming models, and it is among the most recommended optimization tools. Beyond its traditional and widely-used APIs (compatible with C, C++, .NET, Python and Java), the Gurobi solver can be integrated with various third-party modeling frameworks, including GAMS, AMPL, Pyomo, and Julia/JuMP. Additionally, Gurobi is capable of solving a wide range of problems to optimality, from linear programming and mixed-integer programming to mixed-integer non-convex quadratically constrained quadratic problems. The choice of this software is also influenced by its availability for free use in university research development.

To assess the effectiveness of the proposed approach, we conduct a thorough evaluation by comparing the outcomes obtained through the optimization of the Production-Inventory model against the metaheuristic approaches presented at [24]. We selected this work for the following reasons: (i) from the literature review, we needed to choose a benchmark that deals with a complete DER network, narrowing the selection to Refs. [[24], [25], [26], [27]]; (ii) to compare the results and align with the control loop proposed in our DERCS, we required approaches that provide solutions in a short period of time. This criterion eliminated references [26,27], as their stochastic processes do not support near-real-time control; and (iii) we selected [24] over [25] due to the solid analysis of the genetic algorithm presented in Ref. [24]. Although [25] would have led to similar conclusions, as they both develop the same nonlinear model solved with different metaheuristics.

The paper presented in Ref. [24] included two genetic algorithms: a basis genetic algorithm (BGA) and a segmented genetic algorithm (SGA). The SGA demonstrated a better behavior outperforming the results provided by the basis genetic algorithm. In the paper, the model was solved using the Gurobi optimizer as well. However, it did not converge in the case of seven instances. These instances will receive special attention in our comparison. This comparative analysis aims to validate the performance and robustness of our approach and is presented in the first subsection. Subsequently, the performance of the DERCS is analyzed in a rolling horizon in the second subsection.

The work presented at [24] introduced a non-linear optimization model featuring non-linear constraints. The major non-linear equation related to the consideration of the equivalent electric performance at the Combined Heat and Power (CHP) system. This parameter assesses the efficiency of a cogeneration plant relative to the electric performance of a conventional plant solely generating electric energy. It becomes relevant in certain European countries, such as Spain, where it must surpass a specific threshold to avoid penalties. The calculation of the equivalent electric performance involves the ratio of the combined electric and heat energy to primary energy consumption. Given that this calculation encompasses three variables in a quotient, the constraint is inherently non-linear. However, it is important to note that this condition is applicable only in specific regulatory contexts within certain countries, and even in the case of mandatory attention it could be adequately encapsulated in the performance equation (see eq. (11)).

Given the [24] non-linear formulation, the exact mathematical methods incorporated into the Gurobi optimizer were unable to provide the optimal solutions for various instances of the test library (concretely 7 out of 20). In contrast, the proposed genetic algorithms demonstrated effectiveness in yielding satisfactory solutions for all the instances, but without guarantee of optimality.

The upcoming subsection pays particular attention on comparing the results obtained from our production-inventory model with the genetic algorithms introduced in Ref. [24], with a focus on instances where the optimal solution was not achieved.

Rolling horizon case study: analysis of the DERCS performance

This section illustrates the behavior of the DERCS using the presented optimizer within a real rolling horizon, providing appropriate insights into the control process. As in previous section, the experimentation was run on a personal computer with an Intel Core i7 processor and 16 GB of RAM.

Indeed, the outstanding response time provided by Gurobi software for the PIM model guarantees real-time optimal operation of a distributed energy resource network. It is important to note that Gurobi delivers the optimal solution based on forecasted weather conditions and predicted demand. However, real-world scenarios often involve variations in these predictions, such as changes in weather forecasts. The optimizer swift response enables timely adjustments to the commands, effectively adapting them to the new actual situation (refer to Fig. 2 for a description of the Distributed Energy Resource Control System, DERCS).

To provide a real practical application case, we have constructed a case study that incorporates variations in both weather and demand forecasts throughout the rolling horizon. We considered data from a three-days’ rolling horizon, which allowed to analyze such variations in the input data and the corresponding analysis of the required adjustments. Given that the DERCS requires data from period t to t + 96 periods (one day) in advance to produce results for period t, having three days of data enables results for an effective two-days’ horizon. Therefore, the solutions presented are depicted for two days (192 periods).

The sensitivity analysis is summarized as follows and involves the following perturbations. First, we consider a weather perturbation. We examine a scenario where solar irradiation varies while wind forecasts remain constant. Specifically, for period 24 (6:00 a.m. on day 1), a new forecast indicates cloudy weather from that period through period 139 (10:45 a.m. on day 2). As a result, solar thermal and photovoltaic production is reduced from 15 kWh to 10 kWh to zero, respectively. This analysis allows us to examine how the DER control system (DERCS) responds to variations in weather forecasts.

Second, we analyze changes in the energy demand pattern of the large consumer. For this simulation, we assume a 20 % increase in both electric and heating demands. This new demand forecast is applied from period 96 (0:00 a.m. on day 2) through the end of the rolling horizon. This analysis helps us understand how the DERCS adjusts to changes in the large consumer's demand.

After completing the comprehensive analysis in this experimentation section, it is important to note the managerial insights derived from the full methodological approach proposed in our paper. The PIM model represents a significant methodological advancement in the control of Distributed Energy Resource (DER) networks, offering a robust tool for managers of large energy consumers to optimize DER operations and achieve cost savings. The model's mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) framework is compatible with various mathematical programming optimization software, as highlighted in the 2023 INFOMRS optimization software survey, which lists up to 32 different solvers, many of which are available for free as part of university projects.

By integrating a straightforward spreadsheet containing basic data on energy system costs with another updated spreadsheet for demand and weather forecasts, the PIM model can be seamlessly optimized using any suitable solver. This setup allows for automatic recalibration every 15 min or in response to forecast changes, ensuring the DER network operates at optimal efficiency concerning cost management (see Fig. 2 that explained the DER network control process).

Implementing these optimal practices not only enhances operational efficiency but also aligns with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Specifically, it contributes to SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy), SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), and SDG 13 (Climate Action), by fostering more sustainable and efficient energy management practices.

Conclusions

This paper has introduced a novel mixed-integer linear programming model building upon the foundations of production-inventory models, drawing inspiration from aggregate production planning principles, to optimize a distributed energy resources' network. The multi-period both electric and heating energy demands can be met either through a set of energy generation nodes (equivalent to the multi-factory concept in production planning) or from existing energy storage nodes (corresponding to the inventory concept in production planning).

This innovative formulation, integrating production and inventory concepts for optimizing the operation of a Distributed Energy Resource network, is a pioneering contribution presented for the first time in the scientific literature within this paper. The elegance of this formulation lies in its capacity to efficiently address the complexities inherent in DER network optimization. Indeed, the model has demonstrated its effectiveness by providing optimal solutions across all evaluated instances. This constitutes a significant advantage over alternative approaches, including non-linear mathematical programming models that require the use of metaheuristics (which are unable to guarantee optimality and require longer computation times), and stochastic approaches that also need larger time slots to compute solutions, leading to a control of the DER network operation that is far from real-time. In fact, metaheuristics require substantial computational resources owing to their iterative processes, resulting in considerable time consumption for execution. This reality suggests that Distributed Energy Resource Control Systems relying on such approaches may face extended time requirements, potentially hindering their ability to achieve effective real-time control. On the other hand, it is remarkable that our approach solving time for each instance was achieved within 1 s. This quick computational performance significantly reduces the required time by the control loop within the rolling horizon of the control system. Consequently, this enables real-time control, constituting a noteworthy contribution to the real-time operation of distributed energy resource networks.

The different rolling horizon scenarios presented in the case study vividly illustrate how the DERCS adeptly adjusts commands to accommodate changes and unforeseen events occurring in real-life scenarios. Indeed, the DERCS consistently demonstrated its ability to adapt and optimize energy commands promptly in response to changing forecasted input data. This dynamic responsiveness, coupled with the efficient performance of the optimizer, underscores the robustness and reliability of the proposed system in addressing real life experiences within a distributed energy resource network.

To summarize, the major contributions of our research are as follows. First, the novel use of a MILP model for DER Networks integrating production and inventory concepts to optimize the operation of a Distributed Energy Resource (DER) network. This approach, to the best of our knowledge, is unprecedented in the scientific literature. The model's elegance lies in its ability to efficiently address the complexities of DER network optimization, consistently providing optimal solutions within a very short time frame.

Second, its rapid problem-solving capability brought by the proposed model that solves in less than 1 s, enabling near-instantaneous decision-making. This swift solution time surpasses any known references in the field, effectively shifting the bottleneck in DER network operation from the decision-making process to the forecasting of demand and weather conditions. While forecasting typically requires a minimum of 15 min, our approach suggests that a reduction in this forecasting time could further enhance the control system's response time, given the model's ability to deliver optimal solutions almost immediately.

Third, the authentic capability for real-time optimization and stochastic Integration. In fact, the real-time availability of optimal solutions (within less than 1 s) allows for the seamless incorporation of stochastic elements into the control loop via a rolling horizon process. This continuous updating of forecasts related to demand and weather conditions affecting RES ensures more precise and timely decision-making. The use of a MILP model guarantees optimal results every second, outperforming stochastic models that typically rely on approximate solutions.

Despite the advancements presented in our study, several open issues remain where additional research and novel contributions are necessary. The most significant challenge lies in addressing the non-linearities affecting battery performance and the calculation of equivalent electric performance in Combined Heat and Power systems. The first-order simplifications discussed in Section 4 enable the quick solution of our MILP (PIM problem). Without these simplifications, Gurobi would struggle to find the optimum for many real instances of a mixed-integer non-linear programming model. Additionally, current metaheuristics often fail to provide optimal solutions for non-linear models and require substantial computation time, making real-time control of DER network operations challenging when considering non-linearities.

To address these challenges, several new research avenues could be explored. One potential approach is the use of special ordered sets of type 2 to linearly approximate the non-linear model, though this may result in a loss of optimality guarantee. Comparing this approach with our current model would be necessary to assess its effectiveness. Another promising direction is the development of faster and more accurate metaheuristics that can find solutions closer to the optimum for non-linear problems. Further exploration of these methods could significantly enhance the real-time control capabilities of DER networks.
