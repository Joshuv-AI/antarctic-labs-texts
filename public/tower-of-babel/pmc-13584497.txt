Effectiveness of a BOPPPS-based peer instruction approach in undergraduate physiology: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Zhifang Zhao, Jibin Yao, Zhong Guo, Lei Song, Yihang Song, Jin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe introduced a BOPPPS-based peer instruction approach supported by the Chaoxing Xuexitong platform and examined its association with examination performance and students’ learning experiences.MethodsThis study employed a total of 180 s-year undergraduate clinical medicine students from six pre-existing administrative classes at Northwest Minzu University. Three classes received lecture-based teaching (control group, n = 85), while the other three received the BOPPPS-based peer-assisted learning intervention (intervention group, n = 95). Outcomes included final examination scores and responses to end-of-course questionnaires.ResultsThe BOPPPS-based peer instruction approach was associated with higher final examination scores than lecture-based teaching (75.29 ± 8.40 vs. 71.29 ± 9.48, P = 0.003). Students in the intervention group reported higher scores than controls across all four shared domains, including learning engagement and classroom participation, self-directed learning and learning regulation, understanding/basic-to-clinical integration, and overall learning experience (all P < 0.001). In the intervention-specific questionnaire, mean scores across the four domains were above 4.2 on a five-point scale.ConclusionsIn this single-institution quasi-experimental study, a BOPPPS based peer instruction model was associated with better examination performance and more positive student-reported learning experiences than lecture-based teaching. The model appears feasible for undergraduate physiology teaching, although multicentre studies with stronger control of confounding and longer follow-up are still needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-10092-2.

Introduction

Physiology is a core subject in the preclinical years of medical training because it gives students the language and logic needed to understand disease, treatment, and clinical decision-making [1]. In lecture-based teaching, instruction is primarily teacher-centered with students playing a secondary role, resulting in a lack of effective interaction and difficulties in stimulating students’ initiative and enthusiasm for learning [2, 3]. The yield in education is rather disappointing, thus the almost instant need to employ newer methods of instruction which foster the need for greater learning.

Medical education has increasingly moved towards student-centred learning. In this approach, students take a more active role in discussion, application, and reflection, while teachers guide and support the learning process [4–6]. To isolate high-yield pedagogical strategies, North American institutions have implemented the BOPPPS framework through Instructional Skills Workshops (ISW). This teaching model is student-centered, with a key emphasis on the closed-loop management of student participation and teaching feedback. It organizes the instructional process into six cyclical stages: Bridge-in, Objective, Pre-assessment, Participatory learning, Post-assessment, and Summary. The BOPPPS model is more than a sequence of classroom activities. Its six stages create an explicit link between learning objectives, assessment, participation, and feedback. The Bridge-in stage establishes relevance and activates prior knowledge, whereas the Objective stage clarifies the expected learning outcomes. Pre-assessment provides information about students’ initial understanding and can help instructors adjust the emphasis of the lesson. Participatory learning then engages students in explanation, discussion, application, and problem solving. Post-assessment provides immediate information about learning progress, and the Summary stage consolidates key concepts and identifies areas requiring further study [7, 8]. Applying the BOPPPS teaching model that combines online and offline approaches can improve students’ final exam scores and enhance the effectiveness of physiology teaching [9].

In this study, lecture-based teaching refers to sessions in which the instructor delivers most of the core content, directs questioning, controls the pace of the lesson, and summarises the main points. Furthermore, peer instruction is becoming a more important part of medical education. Students explaining a mechanism to peers, defending an answer, or addressing a classmate’s misconception requires students to understand the internal reasoning of a concept instead of memorizing the teacher’s slide [10, 11]. Evidence suggests that peer instruction and peer learning achieve knowledge outcomes comparable to teacher-led instruction, and are particularly effective for developing participative and procedural skills, as well as enhancing the learning climate [12, 13].

Information technology is poised to play a central role in mainstream education, driving educational transformation in China. Blended teaching combines digital technologies with lecture-based teaching, allowing learning to extend beyond the limits of time and space [14]. This approach seeks to encourage student engagement and initiative, while offering a more flexible and meaningful learning experience [15]. The Chaoxing Learning Platform facilitates the integration of online self-study outside the classroom with in-class teaching. Basic knowledge that is relatively easy to grasp can be delivered through the platform as online learning resources before class, while challenging content, Q&A sessions, and discussions are arranged during classroom activities [16]. This approach may help students make use of flexible study periods. It may also support classroom engagement, teacher–student interaction, and self-directed learning [7, 17, 18]. However, the effectiveness of an integrated teaching model that combines the BOPPPS framework, peer-assisted learning, and online–offline blended instruction through the Chaoxing Xuexitong platform has not been fully established. In particular, it remains unclear whether this approach provides additional benefits over lecture-based teaching in terms of academic performance, learning engagement, self-directed learning, and students’ perceived integration of basic physiological knowledge with clinical problems.

In this study, we integrated the elements to develop a BOPPPS-based peer instruction model on the Chaoxing Xuexitong platform. The model incorporated a clinical vignette to introduce each topic, required preparatory work to be done prior to the class, scheduled class time for peer-to-peer explanation and discussion, and included brief consolidation tasks to be completed after class. We predicted this instructional model would result in improved final exam scores, as well as more positive student learning feedback than lecture-based teaching.

Methods

This quasi-experimental study was reported with reference to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. The intervention was described with reference to the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist to improve transparency and reproducibility [19, 20].

Study design and participants

The study was conducted at Northwest Minzu University, China, among second-year undergraduate students majoring in clinical medicine. Six naturally constituted classes from the 2024 cohort were included. Because teaching was organised by intact classes rather than by individual randomisation, this study was a class-based quasi-experimental comparison.The six participating classes were pre-existing administrative class groups established by the university when the students first enrolled. Students were assigned to these classes by the university and could not self-select their class or teaching format.

For the present study, Classes 1–3 constituted the lecture-based control group, whereas Classes 4–6 constituted the BOPPPS-based peer instruction group. Because the teaching condition was allocated at the class level, no individual-level randomisation was performed.This resulted in a total of 180 participants, with 85 and 95 students in the control and intervention groups, respectively. No formal a priori sample size or power calculation was performed. All eligible students in the six participating administrative classes were included. The control group included 39 male and 46 female students, while the intervention group included 40 male and 55 female students. Both groups used the same core textbook, Physiology (10th edition, People’s Medical Publishing House), and received the same number of scheduled theoretical teaching hours with identical coverage of course content. All six classes were taught by members of the same physiology teaching team. To maintain consistency across the two teaching conditions, the team conducted joint lesson planning and standardised the syllabus, learning objectives, teaching schedule, core content, and assessment requirements. Thus, the principal difference between the groups was the instructional approach rather than the textbook, contact time, course content, or assessment framework. Instructor identity was not included as an analytical factor, and the study was not designed to estimate teacher-specific effects. The physiology theory course comprised 64 contact hours. Of these, 48 contact hours were delivered using the BOPPPS-based peer instruction intervention. Each session lasted 2 contact hours, giving 24 class meetings that formed the intervention. The intervention was used throughout the modules on Blood Circulation, Respiratory Physiology, Digestive Physiology, Renal Physiology, and Neurophysiology.

Control group

Teachers used PowerPoint presentations and the standard textbook to explain core physiological concepts and mechanisms. Teaching also included teacher-directed questioning, clarification of students’ questions, summaries of key points, and practice assignments. Students could preview the textbook and review course materials independently, but they did not participate in the structured BOPPPS sequence, compulsory platform-based pre-class quizzes, organised peer explanation, cross-group discussion, or structured post-class reflection used in the intervention group.

Intervention group

Students in the intervention group participated in a BOPPPS-based peer instruction approach supported by the Chaoxing Xuexitong platform. Rather than eliminating teacher input, the intervention restructured the use of class time, placing more emphasis on student engagement and the need to explain.

Before class, learning objectives, selected materials, and short video resources were made available on the platform. Students were organised into small groups (4–6 members) and encouraged to review these materials in advance, providing a basic understanding of the topic before entering the classroom.

During class, teaching followed the BOPPPS sequence, beginning with a brief introduction and a short pre-assessment. The main component of the session consisted of peer-based activities, where students discussed key questions in small groups, explained their reasoning to one another, and responded to alternative viewpoints. Interaction between groups was also encouraged, allowing students to compare answers and refine their understanding. The instructor acted mainly as a facilitator, offering clarification when needed and guiding students to connect key concepts.

After class, students completed follow-up tasks or brief reflections on the platform to consolidate their learning. In this way, initial exposure to content took place before class, active processing was emphasised during class through peer interaction, and consolidation was supported after class(Fig. 1).

Illustrative example of one module

The blood circulation module was designed around a trauma case which involved a patient experiencing acute blood loss leading to tachycardia, pallor, and hypotension. The case was used to stimulate student’s responses on the short-term compensatory response to trauma and explain the possible outcomes of increased heart rate, increased cardiac output and blood pressure, increased peripheral resistance, and the baroreflex. The same content was covered in the control group using a PowerPoint lecture and a standard review (Table 1).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the final examination score in physiology. The final examination was marked out of 100 and consisted of: 20 A1-type multiple-choice questions (20 marks; single best answer, testing factual knowledge), 20 A2-type multiple-choice questions (40 marks; clinical vignette-based, testing application), 5 definition/terminology items (15 marks), 2 short-answer questions (15 marks), and 1 case analysis question (10 marks). Questions that directly mapped to the five intervention chapters accounted for 80 marks (80% of the total score). The same examination paper and marking criteria were used for both groups. Examiners were not formally blinded to group allocation. Marking was divided by question type to improve consistency across the six classes. One examiner marked all multiple-choice questions, one marked all definition items, and one marked all short-answer and case-analysis questions. Each examiner therefore assessed the same type of question for students in both groups.Baseline variables included age, sex distribution and previous-semester weighted mean score. To uphold the rigor and equity of the evaluation process, the final examination items were constructed by a consortium of seasoned faculty from the Department of Teaching and Research, strictly adhering to the standardized physiology curriculum. These assessments were specifically engineered to gauge the fundamental pedagogical pillars and essential knowledge base comprising the unified syllabus administered to both the experimental and control cohorts.

To better understand students’ perceptions, we administered a questionnaire to both groups at the end of the course. Both questionnaires were administered anonymously at the end of the course. No names, student identification numbers, or other direct identifiers were collected. Participation was voluntary, and students were informed that their decision to participate would not affect their course grades or academic standing. However, students were aware of the teaching condition they had experienced, and the questionnaire was not blinded to teaching allocation. Questionnaires were considered invalid if responses were incomplete or if the questionnaire was not completed within the specified collection period. Invalid questionnaires were excluded from the analysis, and missing responses were not imputed. The survey included 12 items using a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). A representative item was: “The teaching approach in this course encouraged my active participation in learning.” This questionnaire was used for the comparison between the control and intervention groups and is provided in Supplementary File (1) Second, students in the intervention group were also invited to complete a supplementary questionnaire designed to reflect their experience with the teaching approach. A representative item was: “Explanations and questioning among peers helped me understand physiological mechanisms.” Because these items referred to activities that were not included in the control condition, the questionnaire was administered only to the intervention group and was analysed descriptively. The complete questionnaire is provided in Supplementary File (2) The two questionnaires were developed specifically for this study and were not adapted from previously validated instruments. Their items were written to reflect the objectives of the study and the learning experiences being examined. The instruments were not pilot tested in an independent sample, and no exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis was conducted. The questionnaire items were assigned a priori to content-based domains according to their conceptual meaning and their alignment with the objectives of the study. The 12-item comparative questionnaire comprised four domains with three items in each domain: learning engagement and classroom participation, self-directed learning and learning regulation, understanding and integration of knowledge, and overall learning experience and satisfaction. The 20-item intervention-specific questionnaire comprised four domains with five items in each domain: learning engagement, self-directed learning, peer interaction and classroom participation, and basic-to-clinical integration and perceived learning gain. These groupings were not derived from post hoc factor analysis.

For the 12-item comparative questionnaire, responses to the three items within each domain were summed. Each domain score therefore ranged from 3 to 15, and the overall scale score ranged from 12 to 60. The values reported in Table 3 are the mean and standard deviation of these summed scores. For the 20-item intervention-specific questionnaire, the mean of the five items within each domain was calculated for each respondent. The resulting domain scores ranged from 1 to 5, and the values reported in Table 4 are the mean and standard deviation of these participant-level domain scores. Higher scores on both questionnaires indicated a more favourable response.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5) and SPSS (version 22.0). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. Group differences were examined using either the independent-samples t test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided P value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 180 s-year clinical medicine students were included, with 85 in the control group and 95 in the intervention group. Age and sex were recorded for descriptive purposes only and were not included as covariates or examined in subgroup analyses. The previous-semester weighted mean score was 81.64 ± 5.86 in the control group and 81.46 ± 5.39 in the intervention group. The difference was not statistically significant (95% CI, − 1.83 to 1.47, t = 0.215, P = 0.830, Cohen’s d = -0.03) (Table 2). This measure did not directly assess baseline physiology knowledge.

Academic performance

Students in the intervention group achieved higher final examination scores than students in the control group. The mean score was 75.29 ± 8.40 in the intervention group and 71.29 ± 9.48 in the control group. The between-group difference was statistically significant (95% CI, 1.37–6.65, t = 2.999, P = 0.003, Cohen’s d = 0.45), suggesting that the intervention was associated with better short-term academic performance (Fig. 2).

Student evaluation of the teaching approach

A total of 180 questionnaires were distributed and 177 valid questionnaires were returned, giving a response rate of 98.3%. The effective response rate was 100.0% (85/85) in the control group and 96.8% (92/95) in the intervention group. The intervention group returned 92 valid responses to the 12-item comparative questionnaire but 91 valid responses to the 20-item intervention-specific questionnaire. One student completed the comparative questionnaire but did not submit the longer intervention-specific questionnaire. The 12-item comparative questionnaire yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.959. However, given the marked between-group differences in item responses, the coefficient was interpreted cautiously, and the four domains were treated primarily as prespecified, content-based descriptive composites. Compared with the control group, students in the intervention group reported higher scores in learning engagement and classroom participation, self-directed learning and learning regulation, understanding/basic-to-clinical integration, and overall learning experience (Table 3).

Discussion

In this quasi-experimental study, students exposed to the BOPPPS-based peer instruction approach achieved higher examination scores and reported a more positive learning experience than those in the lecture-based group. The differences were observed not only in academic performance, but also in areas such as classroom engagement, self-directed learning, and perceived integration of concepts.

The observed differences in examination performance and student-reported engagement may be related to how learning activities were organised across the course. Students reviewed selected materials before class and used class time to discuss, explain, and apply key concepts rather than relying mainly on teacher explanation [21]. This arrangement may have created more opportunities for active participation and timely feedback. Previous studies have also suggested that changes in the structure of learning activities can influence student participation and academic performance [22].

The BOPPPS framework provided a clear sequence for organising learning activities, which may have helped students follow the learning process more easily. Allowing students to see the materials before the lesson provided an opportunity for students to begin their learning, although not everyone seemed to be completely prepared. When the instructor gave clear directions, brief checks of understanding helped the lesson move forward and encouraged wider participation in the discussion. The follow-up tasks helped students revisit the main points after class and may have supported consolidation. Continuity in learning, likely an important contributor to the differences we observed, is probably best explained by the overall combined arrangement, not by a single element. Placing more balance and visibleness on the various stages of the lesson tends to provide a logical order to follow [9, 23]. By making different stages of the lesson more visible, it provides a clearer sequence for students to follow. In practice, students are often uncertain about what is expected before, during, and after class, particularly in large courses. A more explicit structure may help reduce this uncertainty, although its effect on deeper understanding likely varies between individuals. Findings from previous work in medical education have similarly suggested that BOPPPS-based approaches can support both knowledge acquisition and student satisfaction [24, 25].

Peer instruction has also likely influenced the results. It is believed that talking things through with peers helps students gain a working understanding of the concepts. The development of problem-based tasks seems to assist the development of clinical reasoning, and more likely supports the positive effect of peer instruction. A more collaborative learning environment may encourage students to take a more active role in the learning process. When students are prompted to explain the rationale of their thinking and respond to the thinking of their peers and to be more thoughtful. Not all students are equally engaged, and some students show preference for passivity [26]. However, even limited interaction may contribute to a classroom environment where students feel more able to ask questions or express uncertainty. This is consistent with earlier studies suggesting that peer-assisted approaches can achieve comparable learning outcomes while supporting engagement [27].

The results of the survey should be noted as well. Students in the intervention group reported higher levels of engagement, stronger self-regulation, and better perceived understanding. These aspects are often less visible than examination scores, but they may be important for longer-term learning. Improvement of academic performance is indeed related to these factors over a longer course and indirectly, greater understanding of teaching and for greater fulfillment of students regarding their learning. However, the questionnaire findings require particular caution. Students in the intervention group reported more favourable experiences across all domains, but the between-group differences were very large. Students knew which teaching approach they had received and completed the questionnaires at the end of the course. Their responses may therefore have been influenced by social desirability, expectations about the intervention, demand characteristics, or a wish to respond favourably to the teaching team. Novelty and Hawthorne effects cannot be excluded. The questionnaire results should therefore be viewed as students’ perceptions of their learning experience rather than as independent evidence of intervention effectiveness. The study mainly examined students’ immediate responses and short-term examination performance. It was not planned as a formal evaluation based on the Kirkpatrick model. Changes in behaviour and longer-term educational outcomes were not measured. Implementation of the intervention also changed the role and expectations of the teaching team. In addition to delivering the prescribed course content, teachers prepared online learning resources, brief quizzes, clinical cases, and peer-discussion tasks. During class, their role shifted from continuous content delivery to facilitating discussion, monitoring group activities, identifying misconceptions, and providing targeted clarification. This approach required additional preparation, particularly during the initial implementation stage, although some materials could be reused or adapted in subsequent teaching cycles.

These findings also have some practical implications for educators. The study combined peer instruction with the BOPPPS framework and used the Chaoxing Xuexitong platform to support blended learning. The aim was to provide educators with a practical model for course design. Teachers can adapt the approach to their learning objectives and course content. The teaching strategy does not need to be identical in every lesson. During the Bridge-in stage, teachers may use a clinically relevant case. The case should attract students’ interest. It should also be understandable to students with different levels of prior knowledge. Peer learning should be introduced at suitable points during the lesson. Teachers should continue to guide the discussion [17]. They should also identify common difficulties and provide timely feedback [28].

This research has several limitations. First, no common pre-course physiology test was administered. Although the groups had similar previous-semester weighted mean scores, this did not directly measure baseline physiology knowledge. The intervention pre-assessments were topic-specific, completed only by the intervention group, and conducted after access to pre-class materials. They could not therefore be used to calculate a comparable baseline or normalised learning gain. Unmeasured differences in prior knowledge may have influenced the examination results, so the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Second, this was a single-institution study involving one cohort of clinical medicine students; the findings may not transfer directly to other settings, disciplines, or class sizes. Third, the main academic outcome was short-term examination performance. We cannot conclude from these data alone that the intervention improved long-term retention, clinical reasoning, or later academic performance. Fourth, instructor effects were not formally assessed. Differences in teaching style, enthusiasm, or skill in facilitating group discussion may have influenced the outcomes. Students and teachers were also aware that a new teaching approach was being used, so novelty and Hawthorne effects cannot be excluded. These factors may have had a particularly strong influence on the questionnaire responses. The very large between-group differences in survey scores may also have been inflated by social desirability, expectations about the intervention, or demand characteristics. Finally, assessment of the change was based on the self-reported perceptions of students’ learning, which should be interpreted as an experience lived by the students and must not warrant the absence of objective assessment of higher-order competencies.

Despite these limitations, the intervention provides a practical way to reorganise physiology teaching within an existing curriculum. The BOPPPS sequence, peer explanation, and platform-supported activities can be incorporated without changing established learning outcomes or removing teacher guidance.

Conclusions

A BOPPPS-based peer instruction model was associated with higher physiology examination scores and more favourable student-reported learning experiences than lecture-based teaching in this cohort of undergraduate medical students. By adjusting the teaching plan in real time based on student feedback, instructors can address several persistent problems seen in lecture-based teaching. For educators, gradual implementation, focused pre-class preparation, clearly structured peer tasks, and active facilitation may be important. Further studies should examine implementation fidelity, teacher workload, long-term retention, and the transfer of learning to clinical reasoning across different institutions and student groups.
