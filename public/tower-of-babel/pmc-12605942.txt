Plasma proteins and osteosarcoma risk: causal evidence from Mendelian randomization
Authors: Hanjun Ma, Ju Liao, Zonglong He, Mingxuan Liu, Fengxu Wei, Qi Wei, Qunqiang Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteosarcoma (OS) is an exceptionally aggressive bone cancer, and the identification of blood-based biomarkers for early detection and prognosis remains a significant challenge. This study aimed to investigate the causal relationships between circulating plasma proteins and OS risk using Mendelian randomization (MR). We utilized genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics, which included 3,282 plasma protein traits from the IEU Open GWAS Project and the FinnGen consortium. Our MR analysis identified 59 proteins positively associated with OS risk, while 66 proteins were inversely associated. Notably, Lactoylglutathione lyase, also known as Glyoxalase 1 (GLO1), showed a significant protective effect on OS risk (IVW OR = 0.2871, 95% CI: 0.1602–0.5145, P = 2.7580 × 10− 5). After false discovery rate (FDR) correction, this association remained significant (FDR_pavl < 0.1). These findings emphasize the potential of circulating proteins, particularly GLO1, as biomarkers for OS, reflecting its role in oxidative stress and inflammation regulation. The study underscores the importance of proteomic analysis in OS pathogenesis and suggests the need for further investigation to validate these associations and explore potential therapeutic targets, thereby providing new insights into the biological mechanisms of OS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03932-y.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is one of the most prevalent primary malignant bone tumors, primarily affecting children and adolescents [1]. It originates from mesenchymal bone-forming cells and is characterized by aggressive local growth and a high propensity for early metastasis, which significantly deteriorates the overall prognosis [2, 3]. Despite advancements in surgical and chemotherapy techniques, survival outcomes for metastatic cases remain suboptimal [4]. Current diagnostic and prognostic methodologies for OS predominantly depend on imaging and histopathology, which frequently fail to identify micrometastases or predict disease progression [5]. Furthermore, the absence of validated blood-based biomarkers constrains the capacity to non-invasively monitor tumor dynamics. These deficiencies underscore the necessity for systematic investigations into circulating proteins.

Emerging evidence indicates that circulating proteins may serve as promising non-invasive biomarkers for OS. For example, elevated expression of Wnt family member 6 (WNT6) has been documented in both serum and tumor tissues of OS patients, showing potential associations with tumor grade, metastasis, and overall survival [6]. Similarly, placenta-specific 1 (PLAC1), a cancer/testis antigen, appears to be upregulated in plasma and may affect tumor cell proliferation and apoptosis [7]. Transcriptomic analyses have also identified manic fringe (MFNG), a glycosyltransferase involved in the Notch signaling pathway, as a potential prognostic marker, with higher expression levels observed in both tumor tissue and peripheral blood, possibly correlating with recurrence and adverse outcomes [8]. Furthermore, increased plasma concentrations of chemokines such as C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 4 (CXCL4) and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 6 (CXCL6) have been observed in pediatric OS patients, and preliminary functional studies suggest their role in promoting OS cell proliferation [9]. While these findings underscore the biological relevance of circulating proteins, proteomic research in OS remains in its nascent stages [10].

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a widely employed method that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to examine the causal effects of modifiable exposures on disease outcomes. Since genetic variants are randomly inherited from parents to offspring and remain fixed at conception, the MR design is less vulnerable to bias from unmeasured confounding and reverse causation [11, 12]. For this reason, MR is often considered a natural form of randomized controlled trial [13]. In this study, we aim to leverage large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) data on plasma protein levels alongside OS GWAS data to explore the causal relationship between circulating proteins and OS risk. This approach could aid in identifying plasma proteins with causal relevance to OS, thereby offering insights into disease biology and highlighting potential targets for further investigation.

Methods

Study design

A large-scale MR analysis was conducted to assess the potential causal effects of circulating plasma proteins on OS risk. To ensure the robustness of causal inference, the study design adhered to the three fundamental assumptions of MR. First, the relevance assumption was met by selecting genetic variants strongly associated with plasma protein levels, ensuring adequate instrument strength. Second, the independence assumption was addressed by excluding variants associated with known confounders, thereby minimizing the risk of bias from environmental or clinical factors. Third, the exclusion restriction assumption was considered valid, under the premise that the instrumental variables influence OS risk solely through their effect on protein levels, rather than via alternative pathways [14, 15]. This approach enables a systematic, hypothesis-free evaluation of proteins with potential causal roles in OS etiology, providing an analytical framework that mitigates key limitations of conventional observational studies. Data source

Genetic instruments for circulating plasma proteins were obtained from the INTERVAL study, a large and extensively characterized genomic cohort comprising approximately 50,000 participants recruited from 25 blood donation centers across England. In this cohort, plasma protein concentrations were measured in a subset of 3301 healthy individuals using the high-throughput SOMAscan proteomic platform [16]. Genome-wide association summary statistics for plasma protein levels, encompassing a total of 3282 protein traits, are publicly accessible through the IEU Open GWAS Project (Dataset_1). Genetic association data for OS were sourced from the R12 release of the FinnGen consortium, a large-scale Finnish biobank that integrates genotype data with nationwide health registry information from over 500,000 participants [17]. The dataset included 132 OS cases and 378,749 controls. All data utilized in this study were derived from individuals of European ancestry.

Selection of instrumental variables

The MR analysis was performed within the framework of its three fundamental assumptions. Genetic instruments for plasma protein levels were selected from GWAS summary statistics using a significance threshold of P < 1 × 10⁻⁶ to ensure robust associations between variants and protein expression levels. To account for linkage disequilibrium, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were pruned using a stringent r² threshold of 0.001 within a 10,000 kb window, based on linkage patterns from the European population of the 1000 Genomes Project [18]. Subsequently, the effect alleles of the selected instrumental variables were aligned between the exposure and outcome datasets to ensure consistent interpretation of effect directions. During this harmonization process, palindromic SNPs with minor allele frequencies exceeding 0.42 were excluded to avoid ambiguity in strand alignment [19]. To assess instrument strength, F-statistics were calculated for each exposure using the following formula: F = (n – 1 - k) / k × R2total / (1 - R2total). The R² value for each individual SNP was calculated using the following formula: R2 = β2/ (β2 + n × se2). In the formula above, R² represents the proportion of variance in the exposure explained by the instrumental variables, n denotes the sample size, k is the number of SNPs, β is the effect size of the SNP on the exposure, and se is the standard error of the SNP–exposure association. The total variance explained by all SNPs used as instruments for each exposure was calculated as R2total. Only plasma proteins with F-statistics greater than 10 were retained in the main analysis to satisfy the relevance assumption and minimize bias arising from weak instruments [20].

Statistical analyses

Causal effects of plasma protein levels on OS risk were primarily estimated using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, which accounts for potential heterogeneity among instruments [21]. Given that IVW is sensitive to horizontal pleiotropy, two complementary MR methods were employed to validate the robustness of the findings: the weighted median estimator and the MR-Egger method [22]. The weighted median approach yields consistent estimates if at least 50% of the instruments are valid, whereas MR-Egger can provide unbiased causal estimates under the assumption that instrument strength is independent of pleiotropic effects [23]. To correct for multiple testing across a large number of protein exposures, a false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment was applied to the IVW-derived P-values, with the threshold for FDR correction set at 0.1.

Cochran’s Q statistic was applied to evaluate heterogeneity among the selected instrumental variables, with evidence of heterogeneity suggesting inconsistency in causal estimates across instruments [24]. To assess the presence of directional pleiotropy, the MR-Egger intercept was tested; a significant intercept was considered indicative of horizontal pleiotropic effects [25]. For proteins that remained significant after FDR correction, Leave-one-out analysis, MR-PRESSO, and the Steiger directionality test were subsequently conducted to evaluate the robustness and causal directionality of the associations. All MR analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR package in R version 4.4.2.

Results

For each plasma protein, the selected instrumental variables yielded F-statistics greater than 10, indicating a low risk of weak instrument bias (Dataset_2). The MR results assessing the associations between plasma protein levels and OS risk are summarized in Dataset_3. Based on a nominal significance threshold of P < 0.05, 59 proteins were positively associated with OS risk, while 66 proteins exhibited negative associations (Fig. 2). No significant heterogeneity was detected across most MR estimates based on Cochran’s Q test (P > 0.05; Dataset_4). Moreover, the MR-Egger intercept analysis indicated that the observed associations were unlikely to be substantially influenced by horizontal pleiotropy (P > 0.05; Dataset_4).

Figures 3 and 4 summarize the top 10 plasma proteins most strongly positively and negatively associated with OS risk, respectively. Notably, Lactoylglutathione lyase was significantly associated with a reduced risk of OS (IVW OR = 0.2871, 95% CI: 0.1602–0.5145, P = 2.7580 × 10⁻⁵), and this association remained robust after FDR correction (FDR_pavl < 0.1). Furthermore, the scatter plot of three MR methods illustrated consistent effect directions across IVW, MR-Egger, and weighted median estimates (Fig. 5A). Cochran’s Q statistic and the MR-Egger intercept test did not detect evidence of heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy among the instrumental variables, as shown in Fig. 4. The MR-PRESSO global test also yielded a non-significant result (P = 0.665), further supporting the absence of horizontal pleiotropy. The Steiger directionality test confirmed the causal direction from exposure to outcome, with the variance explained in the outcome (6.8404 × 10⁻⁵) being smaller than that in the exposure (0.0738). In addition, the Leave-one-out analysis showed that no single SNP disproportionately influenced the overall causal estimate, indicating the stability of the association (Fig. 5B).

Additionally, we also observed a suggestive association of transferrin receptor protein 1 (TFR1, OR = 1.7992; 95% CI, 1.1551–2.8025), which was positively associated with OS risk, as well as growth arrest–specific protein 1 (GAS1, OR = 0.3910; 95% CI, 0.2082–0.7342) and apolipoprotein M (APOM, OR = 0.6088; 95% CI, 0.4267–0.8687), both of which were negatively associated.

Discussion

This MR study systematically assessed the potential causal relationships between plasma protein levels and OS risk. Among the evaluated proteins, 59 exhibited positive associations with OS risk, while 66 were inversely associated. Notably, Lactoylglutathione lyase demonstrated a significant protective association that remained consistent after FDR correction. These findings provide novel insights into the biological mechanisms underlying OS and highlight potential protein targets for future research.

Lactoylglutathione lyase, also known as Glyoxalase 1 (GLO1), is a key enzyme in the glyoxalase system responsible for detoxifying methylglyoxal (MG), a highly reactive byproduct of glycolysis. By catalyzing the conversion of MG into less harmful compounds, GLO1 plays a critical role in maintaining cellular redox balance, particularly under conditions of metabolic and oxidative stress. The accumulation of MG has been shown to promote the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which, in turn, activate pro-inflammatory signaling pathways [26]. By limiting MG accumulation, GLO1 may modulate inflammatory responses and mitigate downstream cellular damage. In GLO1-deficient settings, macrophage resilience to oxidative stress is diminished, and radiation-induced inflammatory responses are exacerbated [27]. Importantly, MG is converted into lactoylglutathione, which can non-enzymatically donate lactoyl groups to histones, promoting histone lactoylation and amplifying the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes in macrophages [28]. Moreover, GLO1 appears to counteract this pro-inflammatory cascade not only through MG clearance but also via regulatory modifications; both phosphorylation and S-nitrosylation of GLO1 have been shown to enhance its ability to inhibit tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-induced nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) transcriptional activity [29]. Given the established role of chronic inflammation in promoting tumor initiation, immune evasion, and metastasis in OS [30–32], GLO1 may influence disease progression, in part, through its ability to restrain inflammation-mediated tumor-promoting pathways.

Beyond its role in modulating inflammation, GLO1 also appears to be essential for osteoclast biology, providing a mechanistic link between metabolic regulation and skeletal cell function that may be relevant to OS. Under conditions of elevated MG, a glycolytic byproduct that accumulates during metabolic stress, osteoclast maturation is impaired due to disrupted mitochondrial membrane potential, increased mitochondrial superoxide production, and attenuated calcium signaling and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) activity [33]. Experimental studies have shown that inhibition or knockdown of GLO1 significantly reduces receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL)-induced osteoclastogenesis, whereas restoration of GLO1 expression reverses these impairments, promoting osteoclast differentiation and preserving mitochondrial integrity [34, 35]. These findings suggest that GLO1 serves as a key metabolic regulator of bone resorption, particularly under stress-related or inflammatory conditions. Such findings gain additional significance in OS, given the evolving perspective of osteoclasts as functionally versatile components of the tumor microenvironment.

Osteoclasts, traditionally associated with tumor-driven bone degradation, may also play a role in tumor suppression under physiological conditions. Maintaining appropriate osteoclast activity has been proposed to help preserve bone architecture, thereby limiting tumor cell dissemination and metastatic potential [36]. Clinically, osteoclast-rich immune microenvironments characterized by the presence of CD163⁺ macrophages and bipotent CD68⁺/CD163⁺ myeloid cells have been linked to more favorable outcomes in OS patients [37]. Additionally, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) have been shown to differentiate into osteoclast-like cells with antitumor properties under specific microenvironmental cues [38]. Taken together, these data suggest that GLO1 may exert a protective effect in OS, not only through direct detoxification of MG or suppression of inflammation, but also by sustaining osteoclast functionality. This integrative role of GLO1 at the intersection of metabolic detoxification, osteoimmune regulation, and microenvironmental homeostasis underscores its potential as a modifiable determinant of OS pathophysiology, warranting further investigation in both translational and therapeutic contexts. Moreover, emerging studies indicate that balanced GLO1 activity may contribute to metabolic stability and cellular resilience. For instance, moderate activation of GLO1 has been associated with delayed tissue aging and extended lifespan in model organisms, whereas overexpression of GLO1 appears to alleviate renal aging phenotypes in vivo [39, 40]. These results imply that GLO1 may function as a broader disease-modifying factor, linking local microenvironmental regulation with systemic homeostasis. Currently, there are no widely accepted blood-based biomarkers for the early detection or risk stratification of OS. The plasma protein GLO1 identified in this study may serve as a promising candidate for a therapeutic target or biomarker for early detection, prognostic assessment, and treatment monitoring, with potential translational and therapeutic value in OS pathophysiology.

In addition to GLO1, investigations of several other proteins may offer valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms of OS development, as supported by findings from previous studies. TFR1, positively associated with risk in our analysis, has been shown to promote tumor proliferation and survival by mediating iron uptake and cooperating with oncogenic signaling pathways such as Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2) [41, 42]. Its overexpression is also linked to epigenetic alterations and immunosuppressive features in the tumor microenvironment, suggesting potential relevance in OS progression [43]. In contrast, GAS1 and APOM were inversely associated with OS risk. GAS1 has been reported to induce cell cycle arrest and inhibit oncogenic transformation, and its downregulation has been associated with more aggressive behavior in other solid tumors [44–46]. APOM, meanwhile, has demonstrated inhibitory effects on cancer cell proliferation and migration, potentially through signaling involving sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) and anti-inflammatory pathways, and may further contribute to tumor immune regulation [47–49]. While these mechanistic insights have primarily been established in other cancer types, they may help explain the observed associations in OS and deserve further investigation.

This proteome-wide MR study identified 125 plasma proteins that may have a causal role in OS risk. While the development of OS is driven by complex interactions between genetic predisposition and environmental factors, the proteins identified in this analysis are involved in key biological pathways associated with tumor initiation and progression, such as oxidative stress regulation, immune modulation, apoptosis, and bone remodeling. Although many of the other proteins were not discussed in detail, they may still contribute to OS biology through mechanisms that remain to be elucidated.

A key strength of this study is its use of large-scale proteomic data, which allows for a systematic and comprehensive evaluation of thousands of circulating plasma proteins in relation to OS risk. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of our findings, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies incorporating more detailed data on environmental exposures and lifestyle factors may help to further clarify the observed associations. Second, the analysis was restricted to individuals of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Future studies should incorporate multi-ancestry datasets to assess the robustness and applicability of these findings across diverse populations. Third, due to the reliance on summary-level GWAS data, stratified analyses based on clinical features such as tumor subtype, stage, or patient age were not feasible. Finally, the entire analysis relies on a single FinnGen cohort, lacking external replication and tissue-level expression validation. Although the FinnGen dataset offers sufficient power for MR analysis, the number of OS cases remains limited due to the rarity of the disease, which may reduce the statistical power to detect smaller or more subtle effects. Importantly, GLO1 remained significantly associated with a reduced OS risk even after FDR correction among 3,282 proteins, suggesting a robust and potentially clinically meaningful protective effect. Nevertheless, further research is needed to validate this finding in independent cohorts and assess its utility in clinical risk stratification. Future studies should incorporate external cohorts and transcriptomic data, along with multi-ancestry datasets and experimental and functional validation, to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights GLO1 as a potential protective protein against OS risk. These findings provide new insight into the biological mechanisms underlying OS and suggest that GLO1 may serve as a promising target for future research. Given the current absence of widely accepted blood-based biomarkers for OS, GLO1 may offer value as a circulating biomarker for early detection, prognostic assessment, and treatment monitoring. Further studies are needed to validate its function through in vitro and in vivo experiments, explore its clinical relevance in large patient cohorts, and assess its potential utility in early screening, prognostic evaluation, and therapeutic development for OS.
