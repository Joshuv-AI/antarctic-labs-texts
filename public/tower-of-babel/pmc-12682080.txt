Neuroimaging signatures of mesial temporal lobe epilepsy: A coordinate-based meta-analysis of structural and resting-state functional imaging literature

Published: 2025

Introduction

Mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE) is the most prevalent seizure disorder, afflicting approximately 50 million people worldwide (World Health Organization, 2019). Though traditionally classified as a focal epilepsy with seizures originating in the hippocampus, MTLE is increasingly recognized as a network-based disease that exhibits structural and functional changes in interconnected brain regions beyond the epileptogenic zone (Cataldi et al., 2013, Garcia-Ramos et al., 2022, González Otárula and Schuele, 2020). Mapping the spatial distribution of these changes in vivo is of utmost importance because it enables clinical researchers to associate regional pathology with the pleomorphic presentations of MTLE, infer underlying pathophysiologic mechanisms of the disease, and develop targeted diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for these patients (Garcia-Ramos et al., 2022, Jiang et al., 2024, Reddy et al., 2019). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), and other neuroradiological tools have been invaluable for this endeavor to detect clinically relevant differences between MTLE patients and healthy subjects. Neuroimaging is now an integral component of epilepsy assessment and serves as the mainstay for guiding invasive monitoring and surgical treatment (Brinkmann et al., 2021, Skidmore and Christopher, 2025). As a result of the widespread use of these tools, a substantial corpus of clinical neuroimaging research has emerged – documenting signatures of MTLE pathology – and continues to grow as novel analytics are developed for targeting subtle manifestations of the disease process (Goodman and Szaflarski, 2021). However, because the regional effects in individual patients vary by MTLE subtype and disease progression, group-level findings reported for MTLE often differ across studies and imaging modalities; consequently individual reports must be interpreted with caution (Lu et al., 2022). For clinical practice to be optimally informed by a burgeoning body of literature, rigorous syntheses of published neuroimaging data are required for navigating diverse results, interpreting evidence offered by novel methodologies, and recognizing the most consistent effects of MTLE on the brain.

International initiatives have made substantial progress toward establishing consensus, at regional and network levels, on structural and functional alterations in TLE using advanced surface-based modeling of cortical and subcortical abnormalities (Larivière et al., 2020, Whelan et al., 2018). By pooling large quantities of primary imaging data, these multi-site studies provided high-confidence assessments of case-control differences and clarified pathologic effects on normal network architecture − substantially enhancing the current understanding of epilepsy pathophysiology. Yet, by design, these mega-analytic frameworks emphasize surface-based morphometry and tractography-informed analyses. As a result, they are not optimized to deliver volumetric, stereotaxic coordinates that can be acted upon directly in pre-operative workflows (e.g., stereotactic EEG (sEEG) implantation, deep brain stimulation (DBS) targeting, and coordinate-defined biomarker development). Moreover, there remains a gap in the literature: no volumetric multimodal synthesis has comprehensively aggregated the trove of well-standardized, coordinate-based neuroimaging literature, including structural atrophy (VBM) and the myriad functional measures (VBP; e.g. FDG-PET, BOLD-based connectivity, resting-state BOLD-derived metrics) commonly used to study MTLE. Addressing this gap is crucial, with direct translational relevance, because coordinate-resolved resources align naturally with stereotaxic planning, allow cross-modality comparisons in a common space, and can be disseminated as standardized targets for use across centers.

Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) is a widely used neuroimaging technique that allows for objective quantification of structural changes in the brain (Ashburner and Friston, 2000, Wright et al., 1995). In MTLE, VBM has revealed consistent patterns of atrophy in the mesial temporal structures (Barron et al., 2013, Zhen get al., 2018), but variability across individual studies remains a challenge for identifying reliable biomarkers of the disease (Fox et al., 2014, Reddy et al., 2019). To address these inconsistencies, coordinate-based meta-analysis (CBMA) methods have been developed to synthesize the findings from multiple VBM studies (Eickhoff et al., 2012, Fox et al., 2014, Lu et al., 2022, Turkeltaub et al., 2002). By aggregating peak coordinates of reported brain abnormalities, CBMA can identify regions that consistently show structural changes across studies, thus providing a more robust understanding of MTLE-related pathology. The first CBMA of VBM studies in MTLE, conducted by Daniel Barron, identified the medial dorsal nucleus (MDN) of the thalamus as a key site of structural alteration, highlighting the importance of thalamic involvement in the disease (Barron et al., 2013). CBMA-derived neuroanatomical targets were then used to define a model of the MTLE disease network that successfully predicted seizure-onset laterality from resting-state fMRI data (Barron et al., 2015b). With an updated meta-analysis that includes the past decade of literature, this MTLE network model could be expanded, incorporating additional data-driven targets for the development of new neuroimaging biomarkers.

Resting-state neuroimaging has also contributed significantly to our understanding of epilepsy, providing insights into the functional alterations that occur in neural networks (Cataldi et al., 2013, Gonen et al., 2020, Sathe et al., 2023). Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) (Biswal et al., 1995, Fox and Raichle, 2007) measures spontaneous brain activity and functional connectivity, revealing disruptions in network dynamics that are characteristic of temporal lobe epilepsy (Gao et al., 2022, Hao et al., 2022, Qin et al., 2020) and associated with and predictive of post-surgical outcomes (Akbarian et al., 2024, DeSalvo et al., 2020, Hinds et al., 2023, Liao et al., 2016, Osipowicz et al., 2024, Syed et al., 2023). Additionally, PET imaging, particularly fluorodeoxyglucose PET (FDG-PET), has demonstrated patterns of asymmetric cerebral metabolism during the interictal period, suggesting distributed network effects of the disease beyond the mesial temporal lobe (Chassoux et al., 2016). Despite these advances, functional neuroimaging biomarkers derived from resting-state data have not yet been approved for use in routine clinical practice, highlighting the need for further validation and evidence of their clinical utility.

The application of whole-brain voxel-wise analytics to functional neuroimaging, also known as voxel-based physiology (VBP) (Gray et al., 2020), has expanded the scope of resting-state methods in MTLE research (Song et al., 2024a, Wang et al., 2020, Zeng et al., 2013, Zhang et al., 2010). Techniques derived from blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals, such as amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF; Yang et al., 2007, Zang et al., 2007), regional homogeneity (ReHo; Zang et al., 2004), and fractional ALFF (fALFF; Zou et al., 2008), have been developed to assess intrinsic brain activity and regional coherence. A recent PET-validation study suggested that these metrics could serve as proxies for quantitative metrics of cerebral metabolic rates of glucose and oxygen (Deng et al., 2022). Although these methods have shown promise in identifying functional abnormalities in MTLE, they have not yet gained widespread adoption in research or clinical settings. Furthermore, no coordinate-based meta-analysis of BOLD-derived studies in MTLE has been conducted, and only a limited number of such analyses exist for other neurological disorders, indicating a gap in the literature that needs to be addressed.

A coordinate-based meta-analysis of VBM and VBP studies in MTLE would provide a unique opportunity to integrate neuroimaging findings of structural and functional (e.g. metabolic, hemodynamic) alterations within a common standardized space. By assembling all CBMA-eligible studies across three decades and across modalities, a comprehensive VBM/VBP synthesis represents a larger aggregate evidence base than any individual multi-site MTLE cohort and, critically, unifies modalities that surface-based frameworks are not designed to jointly analyze at the voxel level / for spatial convergence. Further, the volumetric perspective is not merely technical: it is practically aligned with how epilepsy surgery is planned, i.e. via stereotactic coordinates. To that end, quantifying multimodal convergence (shared foci across VBM-atrophy, FDG-PET, and BOLD-based metrics) and divergence (modality-specific foci) could reaffirm prior consensus on affected regions/networks and enlarge the evidentiary base by identifying specific, translationally relevant foci.

Importantly, focusing on convergence does not deny well-documented inter-patient heterogeneity in hippocampal, mesiotemporal, and neocortical alterations (Bernhardt et al., 2012, Blümcke et al., 2013, Lee et al., 2022, Lee et al., 2024). Rather, convergence maps provide population-level priors (a set of reproducible, stereotaxic anchors) on top of which individualized assessments can be layered, by quantifying individual deviations, to reconcile shared disease architecture with clinically relevant heterogeneity. Concretely, these maps could (1) supply data-driven regions of interest for prospective biomarker studies (e.g., standardized FDG-PET asymmetry indices or rs-fMRI seed-based measures anchored to ALE peaks), (2) prioritize sEEG coverage when lesion status is equivocal or discordant across modalities (e.g. adding contacts to specific thalamic nuclei or extratemporal foci), (3) inform DBS target selection and hypothesis generation, and (4) enable cross-site endpoint harmonization by providing common, open-access coordinate sets and statistical maps. In this way, the volumetric approach of CBMA extends prior mega-analyses by delivering actionable standardized coordinates and ALE maps as an open resource for the clinical research community, bridging group-level discovery with precision intervention planning.

This approach could help resolve inconsistencies across studies, update our understanding of the pleomorphic effects of MTLE, and reveal the brain regions that consistently show structural and functional changes at rest. Additionally, it would highlight emerging imaging techniques with potential clinical utility, thereby advancing the translation of neuroimaging biomarkers for diagnostic and therapeutic applications in MTLE. As such, a comprehensive synthesis of existing neuroimaging data could bridge gaps in research and clinical practice, ultimately improving the management of patients with MTLE.

The primary objective of this study was to identify brain regions that reliably exhibit structural or functional changes in mesial temporal lobe epilepsy. The secondary objective was to compare the regional distributions of MTLE pathology detected by different functional (metabolic and hemodynamic) imaging modalities/metrics. Specifically, we performed a volume-based, coordinate-driven meta-analysis that integrates voxel-based morphometry (VBM) with voxel-based physiology (VBP, including FDG-PET and BOLD-derived measures), to map where structural atrophy and functional/metabolic alterations converge and diverge. These assessments of convergence vs divergence provide a more complete picture of MTLE network pathology and supply stereotactic coordinates to seed data-driven biomarker and network-model development. To maximize reuse and reproducibility, we publicly release (1) complete peak-coordinate tables in MNI and Talairach space (with thalamic sub-nucleus annotations), and (2) unthresholded and thresholded ALE statistical maps in a standard file format (NIfTI), as an open resource for ROI definition, invasive-monitoring hypothesis generation (sEEG/DBS), and cross-site endpoint harmonization. To accomplish these objectives, a total of 36 coordinate-based meta-analyses were performed using Alteration Likelihood Estimation (ALE), a technique designed to quantify spatial convergence across coordinate-based results reported in neuroimaging literature, in order to quantify and compare different neuroimaging signatures of MTLE.

Materials and methods

In order to identify the distributions of brain atrophy and functional alterations in mesial temporal lobe epilepsy, we performed a comprehensive review of published neuroimaging studies of MTLE pathology and quantified the most consistent regional effects using coordinate-based meta-analysis (CBMA), a form of spatial meta-analysis. This study was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (Page et al., 2021). No IRB approval was required.

Selection of studies

A literature search was conducted (last updated 7.Apr.2025), to identify all voxel-based morphometry (VBM) and voxel-based physiology (VBP) studies of mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE). Selection procedures, congruent with standard ALE-CBMA practices (Fox et al., 2005), are summarized by PRISMA flow diagrams in Fig. 1 and detailed below.

PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) flow diagrams summarizing the identification of A) voxel-based morphometry studies reporting structural pathology (atrophy) and B) voxel-based physiology studies reporting resting-state functional pathology, in adult mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE) patients. These comprehensive literature searches are current through April 7th, 2025.

Literature sources included PubMed (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), the BrainMap Database (brainmap.org) (Fox and Lancaster, 2002, Fox and Lancaster, 1994, Vanasse et al., 2018), and the Neurosynth Database (neurosynth.org) (Yarkoni et al., 2011), as well as references listed in prior reviews and meta-analyses. Three PubMed searches were performed, each including the search terms: “Temporal Lobe Epilepsy” OR (“focal” AND “temporal” AND (“epilepsy” OR “seizure”)). To identify VBM studies, the first query also included the terms: “voxel-wise” OR “voxel-based morphometry” OR “VBM”. To identify VBP studies that used BOLD-based imaging, a second query included the terms: “regional homogeneity” OR “ReHo” OR “Amplitude of low frequency fluctuations” OR “ALFF” OR “resting-state” OR “resting state” OR “rs-fMRI” OR “rsfMRI”. To identify non-BOLD-based VBP studies, a third query used the terms: (“PET” OR “positron emission tomography” OR “SPECT” OR “single photon emission computed tomography” OR “ASL” OR “arterial spin labeling”) AND (“SPM” OR “statistical parametric” OR “MNI” OR “Montreal” OR “Talairach” OR “voxel”). BrainMap Database (VBM and VBP sectors) search terms specified the subject diagnosis as: “temporal lobe epilepsy” OR “mesial temporal lobe epilepsy” OR “hippocampal sclerosis”. The Neurosynth Database search term was “temporal lobe epilepsy”. Quantities of records initially identified from each literature source are included in Fig. 1. In total, VBM queries identified 263 records and VBP queries identified a total of 663 records.

Prior to screening, duplicate records were removed; non-English publications (without an available translation) were also removed, as they are not readily accessible by the scientific community for critique. Unpublished works (e.g. graduate theses) were not considered, as they are not peer-reviewed and would substantially limit the rigor and reliability of a meta-analysis.

In screening, records that did not report primary group-level neuroimaging results in humans were excluded (e.g. meta-analyses, case reports, animal studies). In order to comply with the statistical assumptions of coordinate-based meta-analysis (CBMA), studies were required to perform whole-brain, voxel-wise analysis of a case-control contrast (MTLE patients versus healthy controls) and report results as XYZ coordinates in standard space (Talairach or MNI). Inclusion criteria for subjects were: 1) diagnosis of mesial temporal lobe epilepsy with unilateral seizure-onset, 2) adult age (≥18). Case-control contrasts were excluded if MTLE laterality was not reported, if left- and right- sided MTLE patients were grouped together without rectification, or if additional seizure foci were identified. Studies of bilateral TLE were excluded because coordinate-based meta-analysis requires clear seizure laterality for rectification and pooling (also, bilateral studies comprised fewer than 5 % of published cohorts, limiting statistical power for a separate analysis). Inclusion of bilateral cases could also conflate distinct pathophysiologies and compromise spatial specificity. Although bilateral MTLE patients often show more severe pathology, this population warrants a separate dedicated analysis in future work.

Redundancy correction methods were adopted/adapted from prior CBMAs (Vanasse et al., 2018), since incorporating redundant or overlapping cohorts will produce biased meta-analytic results. Briefly, cohort redundancy between the remaining studies was assessed by cross-referencing author lists, institutions, imaging methods, and scan dates; studies with overlapping cohorts reporting redundant experimental contrasts were removed. To illustrate, three VBP papers (Ji et al., 2013, Wei et al., 2016, Zhang et al., 2015) with common authors and affiliations reported ALFF results for MTLE; inspection of methods revealed that the cohorts were scanned at the same institution within overlapping time periods, without an explicit statement that the cohorts were distinct from other work. Although all three papers were compliant with CBMA criteria, only the paper with the most representative cohort (largest sample) was included in the VBP meta-analysis (Zhang et al., 2015).

All records and reports retrieved underwent manual screening by one reviewer; reports passing this initial screening underwent independent assessment for compliance with all criteria by a second reviewer. A total of 61 publications were included (two with both VBM and VBP results: Bouilleret et al., 2008, Chassoux et al., 2016), reporting 75 VBM and 121 VBP experimental contrasts, collectively representing 2,591 MTLE patients and 2,771 healthy controls. Data Collection

Coordinate data and meta-data were manually extracted from all included studies and encoded using Scribe v3.6.2b (brainmap.org/scribe). For each experimental contrast reported in a study, tabular results were recorded (e.g. significant peak coordinates, statistical values, cluster extents) along with the standardized coordinate space (MNI or Talairach), sample size, and sign of observed effect (e.g. MTLE < Controls). Coordinates reported in MNI space were converted to Talairach space using the Lancaster transform (icbm2tal; Laird et al., 2010, Lancaster et al., 2007). Bibliographic details, clinical information (e.g. MTLE laterality, presence of hippocampal sclerosis, medication status, disease onset/duration) and demographic data (e.g. age, gender, handedness) for the patient and control groups, as well as the imaging and analytic methods employed in each study (Fox et al., 2005) were also documented. Detailed documentation procedures are available online (brainmap.org/scribe/manual.pdf). Encoded studies were submitted to the head of the coding team at the BrainMap Project for independent review (quality control) (Fox and Lancaster, 2002, Fox and Lancaster, 1994, Vanasse et al., 2018). The head of the coding team (trained expert) confirmed that all entries met eligibility criteria (peer-reviewed publication, whole-brain voxel-wise analysis, reporting standardized coordinates) and verified that reported coordinates, statistics, and metadata (e.g., cohort demographics, imaging methods, analytics) were coded accurately and completely, in accordance with BrainMap taxonomy. Any necessary revisions were made prior to acceptance into/inclusion in the BrainMap Database. This additional review minimized risk of transcription error while ensuring compliance with CBMA standards. CBMA-compliant studies were then compiled using Sleuth v3.0.4 (brainmap.org/sleuth) and exported, saving the Talairach coordinates and number of subjects for selected experiments to a text file – the substrate for performing spatial meta-analysis.

Alteration likelihood Estimation

Coordinate-based meta-analysis (CBMA) was conducted using Alteration Likelihood Estimation (ALE), a mass-univariate statistical algorithm designed to compute the spatial convergence of coordinate data reported in standard space (Eickhoff et al., 2016, Eickhoff et al., 2012, Eickhoff et al., 2009, Turkeltaub et al., 2012, Turkeltaub et al., 2002). ALE operates by modeling the spatial uncertainty associated with each published coordinate of significant structural or functional change and then pooling this information across studies to assess the likelihood of consistent effects. The ALE algorithm was implemented using the GingerALE v3.0.2 software (brainmap.org/ale) application on the BrainMap Community Portal (portal.brainmap.org; Fox et al., 2023), a high-performance computing platform for neuroimaging research. In this study, ALE was applied to VBM and VBP studies to identify areas of brain atrophy and functional alterations in mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE).

The first step in ALE analysis involved modeling each coordinate from the included studies as a three-dimensional Gaussian probability distribution (Eickhoff et al., 2016, Eickhoff et al., 2012, Eickhoff et al., 2009). The width of each Gaussian distribution was determined by the sample size of the original study, with larger studies resulting in narrower distributions and smaller studies producing wider distributions, to reflect the relative precision of each reported peak. This probabilistic approach helps to account for the variability in coordinates reported across studies due to differences in sample size, smoothing kernels, and other methodological factors. The effects of such factors, beyond those directly accounted for by ALE-CBMA (coordinate location & sample size), are negligible. For example, smoothing kernels (the degree of spatial smoothing applied in preprocessing) may inflate the spatial extent of significant clusters or potentially merge neighboring/adjacent effects (Luders et al., 2022). While this may shift peak coordinates slightly, CBMA approaches such as ALE incorporate spatial uncertainty by modeling each coordinate as a 3D Gaussian kernel whose width (FWHM) is inversely proportional to the sample size, thereby partially compensating for between-study variability in smoothing. Importantly, ALE uses only peak coordinates rather than full statistical clusters, so differences in the spatial extent of clusters reported in individual studies do not affect the results. Likewise, ALE is unaffected by heterogeneity in statistical thresholds and multiple-comparison correction strategies among individual studies. Unlike alternative CBMA techniques such as multi-level kernel density analysis (MKDA) (Kober and Wager, 2010, Nee et al., 2007, Wager et al., 2009) or seed-based D mapping (SDM) (Palaniyappan et al., 2012, Radua and Mataix-Cols, 2012, Radua et al., 2010) which account for conservativeness of statistical thresholds corresponding to different foci, ALE relies solely on experiment sample size to model for spatial uncertainty in coordinate data. Variability in primary study thresholds are expected to have minimal effect on spatial convergence of peak coordinates, given that ALE, MKDA, and SDM generally produce comparable results (Salimi-Khorshidi et al., 2009, Eickhoff et al., 2016).

Probability distributions for all coordinates from a single experiment were then pooled in a modeled alteration (MA) map, generating a whole-brain image for each reported contrast. An ALE score was then computed for each voxel in the brain volume by aggregating the MA maps from all experiments included for meta-analysis. ALE scores represent the likelihood of observing a true effect (corresponding to the contrasts reported in the meta-analyzed experiments) at particular voxel locations in the brain. Higher ALE scores indicate greater convergence of findings across studies, suggesting a consistent pattern of brain involvement in MTLE. Statistical significance of the ALE scores was evaluated against a null distribution, computing a p-value and standardized z-score for each voxel.

Meta-analytic thresholding procedures involved setting a conservative voxel-level threshold of p < 0.01 and applying cluster-level family-wise error (FWE) correction (p < 0.05), to control for multiple comparisons across the brain (Eickhoff et al., 2016). FWE correction ensures that the likelihood of falsely identifying a significant cluster (i.e., Type I error) is minimized. Clusters exceeding this corrected threshold were considered significant, indicating regions where consistent structural or functional changes were observed across the included studies. The minimum recommended number of experiments in an ALE dataset to achieve sufficient statistical power – recently raised from 17 (Eickhoff et al., 2016) to 23 (Frahm et al., 2023) experiments, based on simulations with VBM data – was also considered, though prior analyses and subgroup analyses of 22 VBM experiments have achieved significant (Barron et al., 2013), subsequently validated results (Barron et al., 2015b) in MTLE. Detailed explanations of CBMA statistics and the ALE algorithm have been provided in prior work (Eickhoff et al., 2016, Eickhoff et al., 2012, Eickhoff et al., 2009, Turkeltaub et al., 2012, Turkeltaub et al., 2002). For the current work, the combination of ALE with FWE provided a robust and reproducible approach for detecting spatially convergent patterns of brain involvement in MTLE and making optimally reliable insights into the neuroanatomical and functional substrates underlying this condition.

Analyses and subgroup analyses

A total of 36 ALE meta-analyses and subgroup analyses were performed, as outlined in Fig. 2. Analyses were divided into three main categories based on the type(s) of experiments they included: 1) VBM-only, for meta-analyses of structural atrophy, 2) VBP-only, for meta-analyses of resting-state functional changes, and 3) Pooled VBM and VBP, for meta-analyses of aggregate MTLE pathology. Within each category, separate datasets were created for experiments examining either Left-MTLE or Right-MTLE patients (to account for the effects of seizure-onset laterality). A third “Rectified” dataset was prepared where coordinates from Left-MTLE and Right-MTLE studies were flipped to a common side (right-to-left) and combined to address potential asymmetry and enhance statistical power; additional experiments reporting results for Rectified MTLE cohorts were also included. The content of each MTLE dataset is summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Although the preponderance of experiments represented (either exclusively or predominantly) MTLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis (MTLE-HS), these meta-analyses (nine combinations of experiment-type and laterality; cf. Fig. 2) were repeated for experiments reporting results specifically for MTLE-HS, excluding those with diagnostically heterogenous cohorts; input datasets for MTLE-HS analyses are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Summary of all ALE analyses performed for mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE). Three main groups of meta-analyses were performed, on VBM experiments to identify structural atrophy (datasets 1–6), VBP experiments to identify functional pathology (datasets 7–12), and combined datasets including VBM and VBP experiments, to identify convergent MTLE pathology across these modalities (datasets 13–18). Within each of these meta-analytic groups, six ALE analyses were performed, categorized by patient laterality, on datasets including Rectified (left-lateralized and right-to-left flipped), Left-lateralized only, and Right-lateralized only experiments; as well, these analyses were repeated for experiments specifically examining MTLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis − denoted “HS only”. VBP subgroup analyses (datasets 19–36) were also performed using the Rectified VBP dataset (n = 108 experiments), to quantify the spatial distribution of functional abnormalities detected by individual VBP metrics (increases or decreases, MTLE ≠ HC), as well as the sign of these pathologic changes (increases only, MTLE > HC; or decreases only, MTLE < HC).

Subgroup analyses were performed for individual VBP metrics, to distinguish functional changes detected by different resting-state imaging modalities in MTLE (Rectified). The six main subgroups were: 1) FDG-PET (cerebral metabolism), 2) fMRI-BOLD, (functional connectivity), 3) ALFF (amplitude of low frequency fluctuations), 4) fALFF (fractional ALFF), 5) ReHo (Regional Homogeneity), and 6) Other, (sparsely reported metrics that did not achieve sufficient statistical power for a separate CBMA). Subgroups were further subdivided based on the sign of the contrast reported (e.g. Patients > Controls), to delineate increases and decreases in VBP metrics. VBP subgroup and signed subgroup datasets are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. Subgroup datasets were not required to contain the minimum recommended number of experiments for statistical power. Underpowered subgroup analyses (with experiment counts below recommended thresholds) were pre-specified as exploratory; results are presented to inform future investigation and were interpreted accordingly.

Significant clusters identified in each ALE analysis were anatomically labeled using the Talairach Daemon (Lancaster et al., 2000). Cluster locations and sizes were reported, and peak coordinates were provided in Talairach space.

In order to compare the spatial distributions of convergent structural and functional MTLE pathology, results were plotted on the Colin27 brain template (Kochunov et al., 2002) using Mango software (mangoviewer.com/mango.html). Separate VBM and VBP meta-analyses were visualized together (to assess regional overlap) alongside pooled VBM/VBP meta-analytic results. Spatial distributions of functional pathology identified in VBP subgroup analyses were also assessed.

Post-hoc assessments

Several quantitative comparisons were performed using FSL (FMRIB Software Library; fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl; Woolrich et al., 2009, Smith et al., 2004, Jenkinson et al., 2012). To characterize correspondence (without implying causality), spatial cross correlations were computed between 1) structural versus functional pathology in MTLE (i.e. VBM Versus VBP Comparison), 2) Left versus Right MTLE Pathology, and 3) MTLE pathology versus the canonical networks (Smith et al., 2009).

Cross-sectional proxies for disease progression were estimated by analyzing coordinate co-occurrence patterns for pairs of convergent nodes. Conditional probabilities were computed for each significant region – i.e. the probability that a focus/peak coordinate is reported in Region B, given that a focus is also reported in Region A (P(B|A)) within the same experiment. N.B.: While asymmetries are consistent with early hippocampal involvement and mildly suggestive of disease progression, these computations are necessarily exploratory and cannot substitute for true longitudinal or intra-subject network modeling.

Results

Significant clusters of MTLE pathology were identified in 34 of the 36 ALE meta-analyses. Foci locations of peak ALE values within each significant cluster are reported as standardized XYZ coordinates in Talairach space. All 61 papers (VBM and VBP) included in these meta-analyses are listed in Table 1 alongside information pertaining to the cohorts, experimental design, and data reported in each study. Quantities of experiments, foci, and subjects included in individual meta-analyses are summarized in Supplementary Tables S1–S3 (for MTLE, MTLE-HS, and VBP subgroups, respectively). Key results from these meta-analyses are specified below.

In the meta-analysis of Rectified MTLE experiments, 11 peak coordinates of atrophy were identified. Regions included the hippocampus, bilateral MDN thalamus, pulvinar, caudate body, putamen, superior and inferior temporal gyri, and cerebellar culmen. For L-MTLE experiments, 16 peaks were detected ipsilaterally in the hippocampus, uncus, MDN thalamus, pulvinar, putamen, claustrum, superior temporal gyrus, and medial frontal gyrus, as well as contralaterally in the MDN thalamus, anterior nucleus of the thalamus, caudate body, and cingulate gyrus. For R-MTLE experiments, four clusters were identified with ten peaks in the hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, bilateral MDN thalamus, pulvinar, VLN thalamus, caudate body, and superior temporal gyrus. (cf. Supplementary Table S4).

For VBM experiments specifically reporting results for MTLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis (Rectified), two significant clusters were identified containing 11 peak coordinates of atrophy. Regions included the hippocampus, bilateral MDN thalamus, pulvinar, caudate body, putamen, superior temporal gyrus, and cerebellar culmen. For L-MTLE-HS experiments, two clusters were detected with 19 peaks including the same regions (except for the cerebellar peak) as well as the following contralateral areas: midline nucleus, anterior nucleus of the thalamus, caudate body, and cingulate gyrus. For R-MTLE-HS experiments, two clusters were identified with seven peaks in the hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, MDN thalamus, pulvinar, and contralateral LPN thalamus. (cf. Table 2).

VBP changes

In the VBP meta-analysis (Rectified), four clusters were identified containing 23 peaks of functional abnormalities. Regions included the ipsilateral hippocampus, amygdala, uncus, superior, middle, and inferior temporal gyri, angular gyrus, and claustrum; contralateral peaks were also identified in the parahippocampal gyrus, uncus, amygdala, ventral anterior and ventral lateral thalamic nuclei, and subthalamic nucleus. For L-MTLE experiments, two clusters were identified with eight peaks, including the ipsilateral parahippocampal gyrus and caudate tail as well as the contralateral anterior, lateral dorsal, and ventral lateral thalamic nuclei and subthalamic nucleus. For R-MTLE experiments, two clusters were identified with nine peaks in the ipsilateral amygdala as well as the superior, middle, and inferior temporal gyri. (cf. Supplementary Table S5).

For VBP experiments specifically reporting results for MTLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis (Rectified), five clusters were identified containing 18 peaks of functional abnormalities. Regions included the ipsilateral hippocampus, amygdala, uncus, MDN thalamus, pulvinar, superior and middle temporal gyri, fusiform gyrus, claustrum, and angular gyrus; contralateral peaks were also identified in the parahippocampal gyrus, uncus, MDN thalamus, and subthalamic nucleus. For L-MTLE-HS experiments, two clusters were identified with six peaks, including the ipsilateral parahippocampal gyrus and contralateral MDN thalamus, pulvinar, VLN thalamus, and subthalamic nucleus. For R-MTLE-HS experiments, two clusters were identified with seven peaks in the ipsilateral parahippocampal gyrus, MDN thalamus, and pulvinar, as well as in the contralateral parahippocampal gyrus, putamen, and subthalamic nucleus. (cf. Supplementary Table S7).

FDG-PET

Hypometabolism was identified ipsilaterally (relative to the epileptogenic focus) in mesial temporal regions as well as the middle temporal, inferior temporal, and inferior frontal gyri, and bilaterally in the thalamus (MDN, VLN, pulvinar, subthalamic nucleus). Hypermetabolism was identified contralaterally in mesial temporal regions as well as the cingulate gyrus, parietal lobe, and inferior occipital gyrus. Decreased cerebral metabolism was observed in regions ipsilateral to the epileptogenic hippocampus, including mesial temporal structures as well as the middle temporal, inferior temporal, and inferior frontal gyri; hypometabolism was also identified bilaterally in the thalamus. Increased cerebral metabolism was observed in contralateral regions, including mesial temporal structures as well as the cingulate gyrus, parietal lobe, and inferior occipital gyrus. One peak was also identified in the superior temporal gyrus (unsigned analysis).

fMRI-BOLD

Increases were identified in the (ipsilateral) superior temporal gyrus, insula, and claustrum. Decreases were observed in the (ipsilateral) superior and middle temporal gyri, as well as portions of the contralateral parietal lobe (angular gyrus, inferior parietal lobule, sub-gyral) and middle temporal gyrus. One peak was also identified in the contralateral superior parietal gyrus (unsigned analysis).

BOLD-derived metrics

Two clusters of increased ALFF were identified in the amygdala (ipsilateral) and brainstem (ipsilateral medulla, bilateral pons). No significant clusters of decreased ALFF were identified. No significant clusters were identified in the analysis of experiments reporting coordinates of increased fractional ALFF (fALFF). Decreased fALFF clusters were identified in the middle temporal gyrus and parietal lobe (inferior parietal lobule & angular gyrus). One peak was also identified in the superior temporal gyrus from the unsigned fALFF analysis. Increased ReHo clusters were identified in the amygdala and pre/post-central gyri. Decreased ReHo was also identified in the middle temporal gyrus. One peak was also identified in the subcallosal frontal gyrus from the unsigned ReHo analysis.

Other

In the analyses of other VBP modalities, increases were identified ipsilaterally in the parahippocampal gyrus, caudate head, caudate tail, claustrum, and insula, and bilaterally in the anterior cingulate. Decreases were also observed in the ipsilateral hippocampus. One peak was also identified in the postcentral gyrus (unsigned analysis).

Spatial Cross-Correlations

Structural vs. Functional pathology

To quantify the similarity between convergent patterns of structural and functional pathology in MTLE, spatial cross-correlations were computed between ALE maps for VBM-only and VBP-only datasets. Overall, VBM/VBP correspondence was modest (r = 0.09–0.31 across datasets) – MTLE, Rectified: 0.204 (HS: 0.306); L-MTLE: 0.222 (HS: 0.171); R-MTLE: 0.090 (HS: 0.242) – supporting partial concordance rather than complete co-localization.

Left vs. Right MTLE

To quantify hemispheric asymmetry, spatial cross-correlations were computed between ALE maps for left- and right-lateralized MTLE. Correlations were modest across all analysis types – combined VBM/VBP MTLE: 0.174 (HS: 0.168); VBM-only MTLE: 0.212 (HS: 0.122); VBP-only MTLE: 0.002 (HS: 0.134) – suggesting partially distinct patterns of involvement by hemisphere.

Canonical network loadings

To quantify the overlap between MTLE CBMAs and the standard (canonical) network architecture of the brain, spatial cross-correlations were computed for each of the MTLE ALE map results with the set of canonical networks identified in prior work (Smith et al., 2009) (cf. Supplementary Fig. S2).

MTLE pathology (combined VBM/VBP meta-analyses) exhibited the most substantial overlap with the basal ganglia (thalamus / fronto-striatal) and temporolimbic networks, with weak spatial similarity to the medial visual network. VBM-only meta-analyses were congruent with these results. VBP-only meta-analyses loaded primarily on the temporolimbic network and basal ganglia (not medial visual), except for the Right MTLE meta-analysis which correlated most strongly with the temporolimbic and auditory networks.

Temporolimbic network loadings were notable for VBP subgroup analyses including ALFF (overall & increased), ReHo (overall & increased), and FDG (overall, increased, & decreased), as well as other metrics. Auditory network loadings were notable for fALFF (overall & decreased). Sensorimotor network loading was moderate for ReHo (increased). Salience network loadings were moderate for FDG (overall & decreased). The thalamic / frontostriatal network loading was most notable for FDG (decreased).

Conditional probabilities

Conditional probabilities were computed as proxies for disease progression, by quantifying the likelihood of an experiment reporting a peak coordinate in a convergent region of MTLE pathology, given that a peak coordinate was reported in another such region (cf. Supplementary Fig. S3).

Among VBM experiments reporting results for MTLE-HS (Rectified), atrophy in the epileptogenic hippocampus was found to co-occur in all experiments reporting the cerebellar culmen, P(hippocampus | culmen) = 100 %; the posterior probability was P(culmen | hippocampus) = 11.1 %. Aside from a pair of nodes in the superior temporal gyrus (BA38), all other conditional probabilities were less than 50 %.

Among VBM experiments reporting results for Left MTLE-HS, atrophy in the left hippocampus was found to co-occur in all experiments reporting the left caudate, right caudate, or left claustrum, with posterior probabilities (given atrophy in the hippocampus) of 8.3 % (each). Atrophy in the left hippocampus also often occurred given atrophy in the left pulvinar (66.7 %), left superior temporal gyrus (66.7 %), right midline nucleus (66.7 %), right cingulate BA23 (66.7 %), right MDN (66.7 %), or right anterior nucleus (50 %). All experiments reporting atrophy in the left claustrum also reported the left pulvinar, caudate body, superior temporal gyrus, right midline nucleus, and MDN of the thalamus. All experiments reporting atrophy in the left caudate body also reported the left pulvinar, right midline nucleus, and right caudate body. Atrophy was always reported in the left superior temporal gyrus, given a focus in the left putamen; the posterior probability was P(putamen | superior temporal gyrus) = 50 %. Atrophy was frequently found to co-occur in the left pulvinar and right MDN thalamus, P(R MDN | L Pulvinar) = P(L Pulvinar | R MDN) = 66.7 %.

Among VBM experiments reporting results for Right MTLE-HS, atrophy in the right parahippocampal region (BA27) was found to co-occur in all experiments reporting the right pulvinar, P(parahippocampal | pulvinar) = 100 %; the posterior probability was P(pulvinar | parahippocampal) = 20 %. The (right) hippocampus was reported in 75 % of experiments reporting atrophy in the Right MDN thalamus; the posterior probability was only P(MDN | hippocampus) = 50 %. Atrophy was always found to co-occur in the Left LPN (lateral posterior nucleus) and Right MDN (medial dorsal nucleus) of the thalamus; P(LPN | MDN) = P(MDN | LPN) = 100 %.

For the pooled set of VBM and VBP experiments reporting results for MTLE-HS (Rectified), atrophy in the epileptogenic hippocampus was found to co-occur in all experiments reporting the ipsilateral cerebellar culmen, P(hippocampus | culmen) = 100 %; the posterior probability was P(culmen | hippocampus) = 10 %. Atrophy in the epileptogenic hippocampus was also identified, given reporting of the ipsilateral superior temporal gyrus (66.7 %) or claustrum (66.7 %). Most other conditional probabilities were 50 % or less.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to identify consistent regional effects of MTLE pathology reported in the neuroimaging literature and assess concordance between the structural and resting-state functional changes observed in the disease. By performing a comprehensive review of VBM/VBP literature and computing spatial convergence across published foci, we identified reliable locations of atrophy and functional disturbance in MTLE. Significant findings obtained in the VBM-only and VBP-only meta-analyses were partially colocalized and congruent with the pooled meta-analysis. Concordant effects (e.g., hippocampus, thalamus) were found to coexist with dissociations including atrophy-predominant nodes (e.g., caudate/putamen) and function-predominant regions (e.g., contralateral parietal/occipital/cingulate in signed FDG; selected BOLD-derived clusters). Subgroup analyses indicated that VBM/VBP similarity was primarily driven by reports of cerebral hypometabolism (FDG-PET). In contrast, cerebral hypermetabolism and fMRI-BOLD-derived VBP metrics had largely distinct neuroimaging signatures, revealing additional regions of spatial convergence.

To our knowledge, this study is the first multimodal meta-analysis of VBM and VBP literature performed in MTLE. In our analyses, MTLE pathology was consistently identified in the epileptogenic hippocampus, bilateral thalamus, and striatum. These areas of the brain are known to share physiologic network connections, exhibit co-alteration in disease states, and undergo pathologic changes in MTLE (Barron et al., 2013, Kotkowski et al., 2018). Our observations are thus congruent with the Network Degeneration Hypothesis (i.e. structural pathology follows functional connections) (Seeley et al., 2009, Vanasse et al., 2021), strengthening our understanding of MTLE as a network-based disorder (González Otárula and Schuele, 2020).

As expected, the epileptogenic hippocampus was identified as the most significant region of MTLE pathology. Atrophy in the hippocampus and other mesial temporal structures has been extensively documented (Bonilha et al., 2004, Keller et al., 2002, Larivière et al., 2024, Zubal et al., 2025) in clinical studies as well as animal models of MTLE and is commonly attributed to neuronal cell death (Blümcke et al., 2013). The hippocampus is essential for cognitive functions including memory formation and emotional regulation, and its dysfunction is integral to the pathophysiology of MTLE. The hippocampus is particularly vulnerable to neuropathologic changes that contribute to primary and secondary epileptogenesis (Ben-Ari and Dudek, 2010). Precipitating factors include head trauma, infection, tumor, or status epilepticus (Brinkmann et al., 2021, Reddy et al., 2019). Approximately 70 % of patients referred for surgical evaluation of drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy exhibit overt signs of hippocampal atrophy on MRI (Blümcke et al., 2013). Hippocampal sclerosis (HS), which involves neuronal loss and gliosis, is the most prevalent pathological finding in patients with MTLE, contributing to the seizures and cognitive impairments observed in this condition. Although a variety of mechanisms can be associated with the initial acquisition of MTLE, the atrophy observed on neuroimaging is most directly a result of excitotoxicity. Seizures in the hippocampus – characterized by excessive electrical activity and sustained glutamate release – cause excitotoxic damage (often producing secondary epileptogenic changes perpetuating this damage) and are associated with loss of cells from multiple hippocampal subregions (CA1, CA3, subiculum, dentate gyrus) (Ben-Ari and Dudek, 2010, Cavazos and Sutula, 1990, Villamizar-Torres et al., 2024), contributing to the morphometric reductions observed on imaging. Detailed descriptions of molecular and cellular mechanisms governing MTLE pathophysiology and epileptogenesis are available elsewhere (Ben-Ari and Dudek, 2010, Goldberg and Coulter, 2013, Villamizar-Torres et al., 2024).

Our analyses also revealed significant spatial convergence of MTLE pathology within the thalamus, including peaks in the medial dorsal nucleus (MDN) and pulvinar, congruent with prior work (Barron et al., 2013, Keller et al., 2015, Keller et al., 2014, Metzger et al., 2013, Zhen get al., 2018). The MDN serves as a regulator for cortical networks (Guillery, 1995, Li et al., 2022, Sherman, 2007), contributing to higher cognitive functions known to be affected in MTLE such as attention, emotion, and working memory (Haber and McFarland, 2001, Li et al., 2022, Oyoshi et al., 1996). Animal studies have also demonstrated that the MDN thalamus exerts an excitatory effect on the hippocampus (Bertram and Zhang, 1999, Dolleman-Van der Weel et al., 1997), plays roles in the modulation and spread of seizures, and exhibits neuronal loss in models of limbic epilepsy (Bertram et al., 2008, Bertram et al., 2001, Bertram and Scott, 2000, Cassidy and Gale, 1998). In a subsequent study of progressive morphometric alterations in MTLE patients, structural covariance modeling suggested a causal role for the MDN thalamus, in addition to the hippocampus (Zhang et al., 2017). The pulvinar integrates auditory, visual, and somatosensory information, sharing widespread connections with temporal, occipital, parietal, and prefrontal as well as subcortical regions, many of which have been similarly implicated in MTLE (Benarroch, 2015, Bernhardt et al., 2012, Kalamatianos et al., 2023, McGinn et al., 2024, Zhen get al., 2018). Notably, the anterior nucleus of the thalamus (AN) was also identified in left- but not right-lateralized MTLE; this finding is congruent with a high-resolution study of mesial temporal sclerosis patients that reported a stronger association of anterior thalamic atrophy with left-lateralized seizure-onset (Lucas et al., 2024). Prior meta-analyses of VBM studies have separately implicated the MDN (Barron et al., 2013) and pulvinar (Zheng et al., 2018) as significant regions of extra-hippocampal atrophy, attributing these changes to mechanisms such as excitotoxic damage from seizures or deafferentation from hippocampal cell loss (Barron et al., 2014, Bonilha et al., 2006). While atrophy in the MDN, pulvinar, and AN have been identified together in individual imaging studies and associated with seizure burden (Bernhardt et al., 2012), to our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to replicate these findings and further demonstrate concordant functional abnormalities.

Delineation of these specific thalamic disturbances strengthens our understanding of MTLE as a network-based pathology and supports neuromodulatory therapeutic development. The temporal lobe has been shown to preferentially connect with specific thalamic nuclei including the MDN, pulvinar, and anterior nucleus of the thalamus in healthy subjects (Behrens et al., 2003); this finding was later replicated in an imaging study of temporal lobe epilepsy patients, reporting pathologic changes in the connectivity as well as the structure and integrity of these specific thalamic nuclei (Keller et al., 2014). Invasive electrophysiologic studies of temporal lobe epilepsy patients further demonstrated epileptiform propagation to these regions over the course of a seizure (McGinn et al., 2024). Regarding the therapeutic relevance of these pathways, the anterior nucleus of the thalamus has been shown to be (and approved as) an efficacious target for deep brain stimulation in refractory epilepsy (Fisher et al., 2010, Salanova et al., 2015, Venkatesh et al., 2023). More recently, similar studies leveraging these hippocampo-thalamic connections have demonstrated that the pulvinar is also a potentially viable therapeutic target (Kalamatianos et al., 2023, Vakilna et al., 2023). In an sEEG study investigating ictal involvement of the thalamus, the majority of patients with mesial temporal seizure onset exhibited early propagation to the pulvinar, with 100 % showing pulvinar involvement at some point during a seizure (McGinn et al., 2024). A literature review of medial pulvinar stimulation in temporal lobe epilepsy further highlighted how the utility of pulvinar stimulation likely relies, at least in part, on the temporopulvinar bundle of Arnold which connects this nucleus to the temporal lobe (Kalamatianos et al., 2023). This white matter pathway, described by Klingler and Gloor in their 1960 cadaveric study and others, subsequently extends to connect with anterior portions of the temporal lobe/temporal pole (Klingler and Gloor, 1960, Serra et al., 2017), also identified by our study; additional dissection/tractographic research revealed further connections with the lateral amygdala and medial temporal cortex (Weiss et al., 2021). These findings in conjunction with our own strongly support the continued pursuit of pulvinar targeting with neuromodulation in refractory temporal lobe epilepsy. Additionally, we suggest further investigation of the MDN thalamus as an additional potential avenue for therapeutic development (Fisher, 2023).

Other extra-hippocampal locations of MTLE pathology were identified in the striatum, including atrophy – but not hypometabolism – in the caudate and putamen. Alterations in these regions are not unexpected, as these regions share extensive connections with the temporal lobe as well as the prefrontal cortex and play crucial roles in cognitive functions affected in MTLE (Cataldi et al., 2013, Hermann et al., 2009). However, atrophy without hypometabolism was unexpected, given that MTLE patients exhibit abnormal performance of certain cognitive functions that rely on the caudate and putamen. In temporal lobe epilepsy, damage to these subcortical nuclei and their network projections have been associated with deficits in emotion, working memory and executive function, attributable to disturbances in limbic and frontostriatal signaling (Riley et al., 2012, Riley et al., 2011). For example, reduced caudate volume has been linked to increased perseverative errors in TLE patients (Martin et al., 2000, Riley et al., 2011). Atrophy in the basal ganglia has also been linked to negative psychiatric symptoms experienced by patients with TLE (Geary et al., 2009). Some studies have reported reduced glucose uptake within the basal ganglia (Arnold et al., 1996, Savic et al., 1997, Theodore et al., 1997). However, a subsequent multimodal imaging study of left-lateralized refractory TLE patients identified atrophy with normal cerebral metabolism in the caudate and putamen (Bouilleret et al., 2008). Additionally, they reported reductions in 18F-DOPA uptake in these atrophic, normo-metabolic regions, likely related to dopaminergic signaling from the striatum to the temporal lobe (Bouilleret et al., 2008). These dopaminergic projections are known to inhibit seizure activity (Rektor et al., 2012), evidenced by pharmacologic studies in animal models (Deransart et al., 2000, Deransart and Depaulis, 2002) as well as in clinical cases of generalized epilepsy (Biraben et al., 2004, Bouilleret et al., 2005). In a recent study where deep brain stimulation of the anterior nuclei of the thalamus was used to reduce seizure frequency (in a primate model of TLE), the caudate and putamen were found to have increased levels of dopamine receptors (D1 and D2), emphasizing the antiepileptic effect exerted by the basal ganglia (Du et al., 2021). These observations suggest that the pathophysiologic role of the striatum is characterized by seizure disinhibition (not specific to MTLE) via reduced dopaminergic signaling, possibly attributable to dopamine depletion rather than hypometabolic function (Bouilleret et al., 2008). Although the precise mechanism by which the basal ganglia modulate epileptogenesis is still being investigated (Vuong and Devergnas, 2018), our findings of subtle atrophy without hypometabolism in the bilateral caudate and putamen are congruent with this interpretation.

Recent connectomic research in MTLE has demonstrated: hippocampal decoupling from default-mode hubs with modality-specific links to tissue markers (Bernhardt et al., 2016, Lee et al., 2018); mesiotemporal network topological parameters that predict outcome (Bernhardt et al., 2015, Doucet et al., 2015, Ji et al., 2015, Liao et al., 2016, Roger et al., 2021); cortex-wide contractions of functional connectivity distance mediated by white-matter microstructure (Larivière et al., 2020); and hierarchical disruptions between sensory, language, and paralimbic systems that relate to cognition (Caciagli et al., 2023, Fadaie et al., 2021, Roger et al., 2020). Our ALE meta-analyses converge on hippocampus and extend to thalamic (MDN, pulvinar) and striatal hubs, providing reproducible node-level anchors for patient-specific network models. These findings complement prior connectomic work by localizing consistent subcortical and paralimbic targets and by highlighting regions where structure and metabolism co-localize, while also acknowledging that topological abnormalities may arise without overt voxel-wise atrophy. Together, these results motivate connectomic analyses seeded from our ALE-defined nodes to refine biomarkers of cognition and surgical prognosis; these analyses are currently in progress.

The collective patterns of structural and functional pathology reaffirm our understanding of MTLE as a network-based pathology exhibiting progressive neurodegeneration in the hippocampus and other connected regions (Caciagli et al., 2017, Galovic et al., 2019, Roger et al., 2020, Seeley et al., 2009). Our findings of hippocampal, thalamic, and striatal pathology strongly corroborate hippocampal network architecture (Kotkowski et al., 2018). Kotkowski’s hippocampal network model, defined via large-scale trans-paradigm and trans-diagnostic meta-analyses of healthy functional connectivity and pathologic structural co-alteration, likewise identified hippocampal connections with the MDN thalamus, caudate head and body, as well as the insula and claustrum. Considering this meta-analytic concordance as well as the importance of accurate node selection when developing network models (Smith et al., 2011), selecting coordinates reported herein as nodes could optimize the translational potential of new MTLE network biomarkers. Although other regions of the brain were identified by our meta-analyses as potential targets for future biomarker development in MTLE (and we would encourage investigating these targets’ efficacy as such), the mesial temporal and subcortical regions likely represent the most robust targets for developing data-driven neuroimaging diagnostic tools.

To situate the results within established neural architectures, ALE maps were compared to the canonical resting-state networks defined in prior work (Smith et al., 2009). VBM atrophy showed the highest spatial correlations with hippocampal/temporolimbic and thalamic/frontostriatal networks, FDG-PET hypometabolism aligned similarly while exhibiting additional overlap with the salience network, and BOLD-derived metrics displayed modality-specific loadings (e.g., increased ReHo in sensorimotor regions and decreased fALFF in auditory regions). Qualitative comparisons with specific anatomical circuits revealed both overlap with and divergence from classic seizure pathways. In the Papez circuit, for example, no consistent involvement of mammillary bodies or fornical projections was observed, while robust alterations were identified in the medial dorsal nucleus and pulvinar (associative thalamic nuclei interconnected with the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex) highlighting a broader propagation route than the traditional Papez loop (Kalamatianos et al., 2023, Keller et al., 2014, McGinn et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2017). Similarly, striatal atrophy (caudate, putamen) and cerebellar/brainstem functional changes suggest engagement of cortico-thalamo-cerebellar loops, consistent with emerging evidence that cerebellar stimulation can modulate seizure activity (Stieve et al., 2023, Streng et al., 2023).

These findings support a view of MTLE as a distinct network disorder, with seizure spread along hippocampo-MDN-prefrontal and temporo-pulvinar-cerebellar axes (Barron et al., 2015a, Ishizaki et al., 2023, Kalamatianos et al., 2023). The lack of a one-to-one mapping between our clusters and any canonical atlas underscores the need for MTLE-specific network models (Vanasse et al., 2021). Using our ALE-derived nodes as seeds in graph-theoretic or Bayesian frameworks could clarify directed edges and temporal flow of pathology, informing targeted interventions such as DBS of MDN or pulvinar.

Our coordinate-based meta-analyses compute spatial convergence across foci of MTLE pathology reported in the literature; from these analyses, coordinates of peak statistical significance are identified and interpreted as high-confidence hubs of the MTLE network (N.B.: results do not represent an exhaustive atlas of pathology). In line with established inter-patient heterogeneity in hippocampal/mesiotemporal, subcortical, and other neocortical alterations (Bernhardt et al., 2012, Blümcke et al., 2013, Lee et al., 2022, Lee et al., 2024), it should be explicitly acknowledged that variability across studies and patients likely reflects clinically meaningful differences and not solely methodological factors. Practically, convergence maps serve as a translational anchor, for developing quantitative diagnostics and guiding patient management. Future investigations should test if the maps may be used to: (1) define ROI-based burden scores (atrophy, hypometabolism, BOLD-derived indices) to assist lateralization; (2) seed subject-level structural/functional network models (e.g., connectivity from hippocampus and MDN/pulvinar thalamus); and (3) generate targeted hypotheses for invasive monitoring and neuromodulation. Leveraging meta-analytically derived maps (maximally data-driven targets) should optimize the generalizability and thus translational potential of potential clinical applications (Text Box 1). Complementarily, deviations from this map can be quantified (e.g., distance-from-convergence, network-deviation indices) and tested for prognostic value (cognition, seizure freedom), integrating shared hubs with individual variation.

Potential Clinical Applications.

1. Pre-surgical lateralization support when MRI is negative/ambiguous.

Compute ROI-based burden scores within ALE masks (hippocampus, ipsilateral thalamus) from routine MRI/FDG-PET/rs-fMRI; use concordant asymmetry as confirmatory evidence alongside EEG (e.g., Barron et al., 2015b, Von Oertzen, 2018).

2. sEEG coverage refinement.

When noninvasive data are equivocal, include MD/pulvinar/AN contacts in hypothesis-driven montages because these nuclei show recurrent involvement across modalities (e.g., Keller et al., 2014, McGinn et al., 2024).

3. DBS target consideration.

Convergence in AN (approved) and pulvinar/MD (emerging) supports evaluating these nuclei during invasive monitoring to personalize neuromodulation trials (e.g., Venkatesh et al., 2023, Kalamatianos et al., 2023).

4. Prognostic enrichment.

Use extratemporal hypometabolic patterns that overlap convergent hubs (temporal-parietal/cingulate) to stratify risk for poorer outcomes or cognitive deficits (e.g., Wong et al., 2010, Doucet et al., 2013).

5. Trial endpoints / biomarker seeds.

Register pre-specified ALE-defined ROIs as imaging endpoints (e.g., change in FDG uptake or rs-fMRI burden within hippocampus/MD/pulvinar masks) to improve reproducibility across centers.

Hypometabolism as measured by FDG-PET identified functional abnormalities in a spatial distribution most similar to that of atrophy in MTLE. Concordant findings of atrophy and cerebral hypometabolism in MTLE are significant for understanding the structural and functional changes associated with this condition (Garcia-Ramos et al., 2022). Although spatial convergence does not directly provide evidence of causal structure/function coupling, these findings do help to characterize the pathophysiology and clinical manifestations of MTLE and can inform diagnostic and therapeutic strategies (Reddy et al., 2019). Studies using FDG-PET have demonstrated that areas of hypometabolism often align with regions of structural damage in the temporal lobe and hippocampus (Carne et al., 2007, Diehl et al., 2003, Doucet et al., 2013). Specifically, temporal lobe volumes and hippocampal volumes are significantly smaller on the epileptogenic side, and the PET asymmetries correlate with temporal lobe volume asymmetries in certain subgroups. This suggests a relationship in certain regions between structural damage (atrophy) and functional impairment (glucose hypometabolism) in MTLE. The clinical implications of concordant atrophy and hypometabolism are notable in the context of surgical outcomes and cognitive deficits. The presence and extent of hypometabolism in FDG-PET is a good predictor of postsurgical outcome in TLE patients with mesial temporal sclerosis or negative MRI (Von Oertzen, 2018). Furthermore, prefrontal hypometabolism in MTLE has been associated with deficits in higher-order cognitive abilities, such as executive dysfunction (Doucet et al., 2013). The topography of extratemporal hypometabolism can also provide valuable information and has been associated with secondary generalization as well as worse surgical outcomes (Wong et al., 2010). However, the pathological mechanisms linking atrophy and hypometabolism are complex and not fully understood. While FDG-PET demonstrates a high correlation with MRI-identified hippocampal sclerosis for lateralizing the epileptogenic zone, some studies have found that the magnitude of hypometabolism correlates weakly or not at all with direct or indirect measures of hippocampal neuronal loss (Carne et al., 2007). This suggests that hypometabolism may reflect a broader dysfunction beyond structural damage, possibly related to ictal discharge generation and spread pathways (Chassoux et al., 2004). Other research indicates that temporal neocortical microscopic cortical dysplasia with concurrent hippocampal sclerosis is associated with more prominent lateral temporal metabolic dysfunction compared with isolated hippocampal sclerosis in temporal lobe atrophy (Diehl et al., 2003). This implies that different pathological substrates can influence the patterns of atrophy and hypometabolism in MTLE.

Hypermetabolism was identified contralateral to the seizure focus, in temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes, as well as the cingulate gyrus. Cerebral hypermetabolism in the hemisphere contralateral to seizure onset in mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE) is a notable phenomenon that contributes to the complex understanding of this condition. It has been suggested that hypermetabolism in the non-epileptic temporal lobe and extratemporal areas represents compensatory mechanisms (Feng et al., 2024). These mechanisms appear to be more efficient in left hippocampal sclerosis and in female patients (Chassoux et al., 2016). Factors such as long epilepsy duration, late onset of epilepsy, and high seizure frequency may reduce these adaptive changes. The significance of contralateral hypermetabolism lies in its potential impact on brain function and its relationship with various clinical characteristics. A study involving 114 MTLE patients correlated metabolism with the side of hippocampal sclerosis, structural atrophy, electroclinical features, gender, age at onset, epilepsy duration, and seizure frequency (Chassoux et al., 2016). Gender-related effects were significant mainly in the contralateral frontal lobe, where metabolism was higher in female patients. Epilepsy duration correlated positively with contralateral temporal metabolism in left hippocampal sclerosis but negatively in right hippocampal sclerosis. Additionally, high seizure frequency correlated negatively with contralateral metabolism in left hippocampal sclerosis (Chassoux et al., 2016). These findings suggest that contralateral hypermetabolism may reflect adaptive or maladaptive responses to the primary epileptogenic focus and can be modulated by various clinical factors.

Subgroup meta-analyses of BOLD-derived metrics revealed additional regions of functional disturbance in the absence of atrophy or altered glucose metabolism. While our meta-analytic findings for BOLD-derived metrics are provisional and should be interpreted with caution due to the paucity of published experiments (Frahm et al., 2023), we wish to emphasize that these measures of local function (ALFF, fALFF, ReHo) may capture novel aspects of MTLE pathophysiology and could hold potential for guiding epilepsy treatment. Pre-operative assessments of MTLE often involve the acquisition of resting-state fMRI, yet ReHo (Zang et al., 2004), ALFF (Yang et al., 2007, Zang et al., 2007), and fALFF (Zou et al., 2008) results are rarely obtained or published. These metrics are derived from the same resting-state imaging sequences utilized for functional connectivity analyses and rely on open-source pipelines currently available to research and clinical communities. Early adoption of these techniques has been sparse, likely due to the fact that the first instruction manuals were not available in English. However, analytic pipelines for performing these calculations have been incorporated in common toolboxes such as REST (nitrc.org/projects/rest; Song et al., 2011), CONN (web.conn-toolbox.org/fmri-methods/connectivity-measures/other; Whitfield-Gabrieli and Nieto-Castanon, 2012, Whitfield-Gabrieli and Nieto-Castanon, 2012), DPARSF (Chao-Gan and Yu-Feng, 2010), and DPABI (Yan et al., 2016). Convergent findings in this meta-analysis suggest that ALFF, fALFF, and ReHo may be sensitive to diverse hemodynamic manifestations of MTLE not detected by other methods; however, at present, there is insufficient evidence to support clinical use of these metrics in MTLE diagnosis or treatment planning. Additionally, it is worth reiterating that surgical planning of interventions and stereotactic surgery are done in volumetric (3D) space, rather than 2D space; thus, the preference for 2D space in recent imaging neuroscience publications reduces their clinical value. Further investigations leveraging standardized volumetric BOLD-derived approaches will be required to corroborate and extend our understanding of MTLE pathophysiology and, ultimately, contribute to the diagnostic toolkit available to clinicians. We therefore urge authors of prior resting-state fMRI studies to probe previously collected datasets using these tools, incorporate ALFF/fALFF/ReHo analytics in ongoing research, and report the resulting standard space coordinates in future publications to facilitate future, adequately powered syntheses.

Several limitations of this study are acknowledged. First, ALE meta-analyses rely on published coordinates and require validation using primary imaging data (Barron et al., 2015b, Bassett et al., 2018, Bassett and Sporns, 2017, Fox et al., 2014). The absence of formal connectivity modeling also prevents a comprehensive assessment of how different brain regions interact and contribute to the pathophysiology of MTLE (Barron et al., 2013). Specifically, given the cross-sectional nature of coordinate data, the present findings cannot establish a causal link between the structural and functional changes observed in MTLE, nor can they provide robust evidence for a temporal sequence of pathology. An exploratory analysis of proxies for disease progression did reveal probabilistic paths consistent with early hippocampal involvement and subsequent extratemporal co-alterations, but such associations cannot substitute for true longitudinal studies with subject-level network analysis (Supplementary Fig. S3). In biomarker development studies, CBMA-derived nodes could be leveraged in formal network progression models, drawing on ergodic principles to link cross-sectional and longitudinal dynamics (Adolf and Fried, 2019, Fisher et al., 2018, Fornito et al., 2015); this requires individual-level data and remains beyond the scope of the present work. Future investigations of MTLE should target the coordinates published in this study to quantify connectivity and probe network disturbances as a potential clinical biomarker. Additionally, the exclusion of hypertrophy studies limits the scope of the meta-analysis, as morphometric increases could provide additional insights into mechanisms of structural and functional network plasticity in MTLE (Zubal et al., 2025). Methodological constraints may also limit the interpretability of these meta-analyses. For instance, the current literature offers insufficient data to perform separate, well-powered (Eickhoff et al., 2016, Frahm et al., 2023) meta-analyses of MTLE patients without hippocampal sclerosis (HS), even though this is a distinct clinical population with potentially different structural and functional changes (Garcia-Ramos et al., 2022, Jiang et al., 2024). In the same vein, sample size constraints in resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) subgroup analyses lead to reduced statistical power and increased risk of false-negative findings (Eickhoff et al., 2016). Although stringent study selection criteria did limit the number of studies that could be included, the rigorous approach implemented in the present study minimized bias and ensured the validity of the results, outweighing any sample size benefit from including redundant or potentially spurious (i.e. non-peer reviewed) findings. Heterogeneity in clinical factors, such as disease duration, seizure frequency, and medication regimens, among the included studies also introduces variability that may obscure consistent patterns of structural and functional changes. Finally, publication bias, where studies with statistically significant results are more likely to be published, can lead to an overestimation of the true effect size (Acar et al., 2018). These limitations highlight the need for prospective clinical neuroimaging studies to validate the proposed targets identified in this study, to gain a more complete understanding of this complex disorder.

Conclusions

This study highlights the impact of mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE) on specific extra-hippocampal brain regions that comprise a disease network, including concordant pathological changes in both structure (atrophy) and metabolic function (hypometabolism). We suggest that brain regions exhibiting convergent VBM and VBP pathology represent the most promising data-driven neuroimaging targets for probing disease-related alterations. We recommend incorporating these regions into a functional network model of MTLE to facilitate biomarker development for diagnostic and treatment planning purposes (e.g. pre-operative mapping, neuromodulatory targeting). Accordingly, convergent foci reported here should be used as priors to anchor individualized assessments, whereas patient-specific deviations from these hubs may delineate clinically meaningful subtypes and refine prognostic modeling. Our findings suggest that BOLD-derived metrics (ReHo, ALFF, fALFF) may detect subtle hemodynamic or metabolic abnormalities not captured by conventional imaging, but their clinical utility remains uncertain due to limited published research. Nevertheless, convergent results from our VBP subgroup meta-analyses indicate these metrics warrant further exploration. To advance the field, we strongly encourage the epilepsy imaging research community to compute and report these measures in standard space coordinates for all MTLE case-control studies acquiring resting-state fMRI. Such collective efforts will enable robust meta-analyses to assess the consistency of BOLD-derived neuroimaging signatures across patients, identify potential clinical subtypes, and explore associations with disease progression and post-operative outcomes. Through these initiatives, we can refine neuroimaging approaches for improved diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment strategies in MTLE.

Glossary

ALE is mass-univariate statistical algorithm used for coordinate-based meta-analysis to synthesize findings from multiple neuroimaging studies. ALE computes spatial convergence across standardized coordinate data reported for structural and functional imaging experiments, quantifying the likelihood of consistent effects being observed at each voxel in the brain. When this algorithm is applied exclusively to structural or functional imaging studies, ALE can be specified as “Anatomic” Likelihood Estimation and “Activation” Likelihood Estimation, respectively, reflecting the nature of data substrate; “Alteration” Likelihood Estimation refers to the generalized case (Eickhoff et al., 2016, Eickhoff et al., 2012, Turkeltaub et al., 2012, Turkeltaub et al., 2002).

ALFF is neuroimaging analytic metric derived from the resting-state fMRI-BOLD signal by computing the square root of the power spectrum between 0.01–0.08 Hz, per-voxel. ALFF quantifies the intensity of signal fluctuations as an indirect measure of spontaneous brain activity (Yang et al., 2007, Zang et al., 2007, Zou et al., 2008). ALFF may be a viable proxy for cerebral blood volume, based on a recent PET validation study (C15O tracer) (Deng et al., 2022).

Similar to ALFF, fractional ALFF (fALFF) is a neuroimaging analytic metric derived from the resting-state fMRI-BOLD signal. However, due to the sensitivity of ALFF to physiologic noise, fALFF instead computes a ratio between ALFF and the power spectrum for the full range frequencies, quantifying the relative contribution of low-frequency fluctuations (Zou et al., 2008). fALFF may also be a viable proxy for the cerebral metabolic rate of glucose, based on a recent PET validation study (18FDG tracer); fALFF was also shown to be associated (significantly, but less so) with the cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen, blood flow, and blood volume (Deng et al., 2022).

BOLD refers to the hemodynamic contrast targeted by functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). The BOLD signal measures the relative quantities of oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin by detecting their differential effects on T2* dephasing. Deoxyhemoglobin’s paramagnetic effects reduce signal intensity; however, the hemodynamic response to brain activation increases blood flow to the region, boosting the concentration of oxyhemoglobin and corresponding BOLD signal. Hence, the BOLD signal serves as an indirect measure of neuronal activity (Bandettini and Wong, 1997).

A 27-item checklist (and flow diagram) defining the minimum set of items that should be included in published meta-analyses (and systematic reviews), aimed at ensuring rigor & reproducibility and enhancing transparency in the reporting of these studies (Page et al., 2021).

ReHo is a neuroimaging analytic metric derived from the resting-state fMRI-BOLD signal by comparing the timeseries of each voxel to those of neighboring voxels. ReHo quantifies the similarity or synchronization of activity in a given region of the brain. (Zang et al., 2004). ReHo may also be a viable proxy for the cerebral metabolic rates of glucose and oxygen, based on a recent PET validation study (18FDG and 15OO tracers, respectively); ReHo was also shown to be associated (significantly, but less so) with blood flow and blood volume (Deng et al., 2022).

VBM is a standardized neuroimaging technique used to measure subtle neuroanatomical changes in brain structure (cerebral gray and/or white matter), quantifying tissue volume or concentration/density in a voxel-wise manner (Ashburner and Friston, 2000, Wright et al., 1995).

VBP – the resting-state functional analog of VBM – refers to a class of standardized neuroimaging techniques used to measure pathologic disturbances in brain function. VBP metrics target a variety of neurophysiologic properties including cerebral hemodynamics (e.g. blood flow, blood volume), metabolic processes (e.g. cerebral metabolic rate of glucose, cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen), and synaptic function (e.g. neurotransmission, receptor availability). Thus, the specific metric quantified in a VBP study depends on the selected imaging modality (e.g. fMRI, PET, SPECT, MEG), contrast or radiotracer (e.g. BOLD, [18F]FDG, [99mTc]ECD), and post-hoc analytics (ALFF, fALFF, ReHo) (Deng et al., 2022, Fox et al., 2023).
