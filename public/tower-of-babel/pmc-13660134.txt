Bactericidal effects of diluted povidone-iodine on Staphylococcus epidermidis at different stages of biofilm formation on titanium surfaces
Authors: Iku Tomonaga, Shiro Kajiyama, Masashi Umeki, Itaru Furuichi, Hironobu Koseki, Makoto Osaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImplant-associated infections, particularly those caused by Staphylococcus epidermidis, pose a significant challenge in orthopaedic surgery due to the ability of this organism to form antibiotic-resistant biofilms on titanium surfaces. Although povidone-iodine (PVP-I) is widely used as an intraoperative antiseptic, its effectiveness against biofilms at different stages of development remains unclear. This in vitro study aimed to evaluate the bactericidal effects of 0.35% diluted PVP-I on S. epidermidis biofilms on mirror-polished titanium alloy (ASTM F136) discs. Irrigation with PVP-I or saline (control) was applied at three critical stages: (1) irrigation at the pre-exposure stage; (2) irrigation after the bacterial adhesion stage; and (3) irrigation at the early biofilm stage (6 h after adhesion). Viable bacterial counts were quantified by colony-forming unit (CFU) assays, and biofilm structure was assessed using biofilm coverage rate (BCR) measured by crystal violet staining and image analysis.ResultsIrrigation at the pre-exposure stage did not significantly reduce the number of adherent viable bacteria compared to controls (P = 0.55, d = 0.21). However, irrigation after the bacterial adhesion stage resulted in no viable bacteria being detected in the PVP-I group under the assay conditions (0 CFU/mL; P < 0.0001, d = 2.56). Irrigation at the early biofilm stage with PVP-I achieved a 98% reduction in viable bacteria compared to controls (P < 0.0001, d = 45.3). The relative BCR remained at 102.3% (P = 0.30, d = 0.24), likely due to matrix protein fixation by iodine rather than increased formation.ConclusionsThe timing of antiseptic irrigation is critical for effective reduction of viable bacteria from titanium surfaces. Irrigation after the bacterial adhesion stage with diluted PVP-I appears most effective in preventing biofilm establishment, while irrigation at the pre-exposure stage is insufficient, and chemical irrigation alone cannot fully remove established biofilm matrix structures. These findings highlight the importance of integrating mechanical debridement with antiseptic irrigation for optimal management of implant-associated infections.Keywords: Antiseptic irrigation, Biofilm, Implant-associated infection, Povidone-iodine, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Titanium surfaces

Introduction

The increasing use of orthopaedic implants has been accompanied by a rise in device-associated infections, many of which are caused by Staphylococcus epidermidis, a skin commensal capable of forming biofilms on artificial surfaces such as titanium alloy implants [1–3]. Biofilm-associated infections are notoriously difficult to treat, as bacteria within the biofilm exhibit phenotypic changes that confer enhanced resistance to antibiotics and protection from host immune defenses [4–6]. Once established, these infections often necessitate implant removal and long-term antibiotic therapy, imposing substantial clinical and economic burdens [7, 8].

Orthopaedic implants are highly susceptible to biofilm formation by S. epidermidis, linking basic biofilm biology to device-associated clinical infections. Biofilm formation begins with initial adhesion of planktonic bacteria to a biomaterial surface, followed by secretion of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), leading to maturation into a structured biofilm community [9, 10]. S. epidermidis expresses polysaccharide intercellular adhesin (PIA), encoded by the icaADBC locus, which promotes intercellular aggregation and biofilm stability [11–13]. Numerous in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that biofilm resistance increases with maturation, highlighting the importance of early intervention [4, 14].

One of the widely used intraoperative antiseptics is povidone-iodine (PVP-I), a complex of polyvinylpyrrolidone and iodine, which exhibits broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity by oxidizing microbial proteins and lipids [15–17]. While PVP-I is routinely used for preoperative skin disinfection and intraoperative irrigation, its role in disrupting biofilm structures on implant surfaces remains controversial [14, 15, 18]. In clinical practice, diluted PVP-I irrigation has been reported as a cost-effective strategy for infection prophylaxis in joint arthroplasty [19], and systematic reviews have suggested potential efficacy in preventing surgical site infections [20]. Recent studies have shown that diluted PVP-I solutions (0.35–1.0%) exhibit good bactericidal activity while minimizing cytotoxicity [21–24]. However, most studies have focused on concentration-dependent effects, and the efficacy of irrigation across distinct developmental stages—particularly on standardized ASTM F136 titanium surfaces—remains to be systematically elucidated [25, 26].

While clinical variables such as host immunity and shear stress are complex, simplified in vitro models provide a necessary starting point to isolate the timing-dependent effects of antiseptics. Previous research indicates that PVP-I efficacy is limited once the EPS matrix protects the biofilm [27, 28], however, few studies have systematically tested this on metallic surfaces.

The aim of this study was to investigate the stage-dependent bactericidal effects of 0.35% diluted PVP-I on S. epidermidis biofilms formed on titanium surfaces, and to clarify how the timing of antiseptic irrigation affects bacterial viability and biofilm persistence.

Methods

Bacterial strain and culture conditions

S. epidermidis strain RP62A (American Type Culture Collection [ATCC] 35984, American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA) was used in this study as a well-characterized biofilm-forming strain. Bacteria were cultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB) (Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD, USA) at 37 °C for 24 h under aerobic conditions. A subculture was then prepared by inoculating the overnight culture into fresh TSB and incubating for an additional 4 h to achieve logarithmic-phase bacteria. The bacterial suspension was adjusted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.2, equivalent to approximately 2.3 × 108 CFU/mL.

Titanium substrates

Mirror-polished titanium alloy (ASTM F136) discs (diameter, 12 mm; height, 6 mm) were used to simulate implant surfaces. All discs were sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min prior to use.

Irrigation solutions

Two irrigation groups were compared in this study. The control group was treated with sterile 0.9% normal saline. The PVP-I group was treated with 0.35% diluted PVP-I solution, which was prepared immediately before use by diluting a 10% stock solution (Kaneichi®; Kaneichi Yakuhin Kogyo Co., Osaka, Japan) with sterile 0.9% normal saline. In consideration of clinical practice, normal saline was used for PVP-I dilution and irrigation, while phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was used during bacterial rinsing and culturing procedures to maintain physiological pH conditions. The 0.35% concentration and 3 min exposure duration were selected based on previous studies indicating that this protocol provides potent bactericidal activity while minimizing potential damage to host cells [21, 24].

Experimental design and biofilm models

To test this timing-dependent hypothesis, we designed an in vitro model incorporating three predefined experimental stages: (1) Bacterial Viability after Irrigation in the Pre-exposure Stage, (2) Bacterial Viability after Irrigation in the Bacterial Adhesion Stage, and (3) Bacterial Viability after Irrigation in the Early Biofilm Stage.

These stages were constructed to evaluate the bactericidal effects of 0.35% PVP-I at clinically relevant time points that represent pre-exposure conditioning, immediate post-adhesion irrigation, and irrigation after early biofilm establishment.

Viable bacterial counts were assessed at each stage to quantify bactericidal efficacy. In addition, BCR was evaluated after the early biofilm stage to examine structural persistence following irrigation.

All adhesion and early-biofilm incubations were conducted under static conditions at 37 °C without shaking or fluid flow. This simplified in vitro static model was intentionally utilized to isolate and evaluate the chemical bactericidal effects of PVP-I at specific developmental time points, acknowledging that it does not fully simulate dynamic clinical variables such as fluid shear stress or host immune factors.

After each incubation step, non-adherent cells were removed by gentle PBS rinsing as follows: 1.0 mL of sterile PBS was added to the well, the titanium disc was fully immersed and then lifted out; this immerse–withdraw cycle was repeated three times. All steps were performed without agitation (no vortexing/sonication and no directed jet/stream onto the disc surface).

Irrigation at the pre-exposure stage

Titanium discs were first treated by immersion in either sterile 0.9% normal saline or 0.35% PVP-I for 3 min to condition the surface. After this pretreatment, discs were immediately inoculated with 200 µL of S. epidermidis suspension and incubated for 60 min at 37° C to allow initial bacterial adhesion. Following incubation, specimens were rinsed with sterile PBS (immersed in 1.0 mL PBS and withdrawn, × 3; no agitation) to remove non-adherent and loosely adherent cells, leaving only cells attached to the disc surface.

Irrigation after the bacterial adhesion stage

Titanium discs were inoculated with 200 µL of S. epidermidis suspension and incubated for 60 min at 37 °C to allow bacterial adhesion. After incubation, specimens were rinsed with sterile PBS (immersed in 1.0 mL PBS and withdrawn, × 3; no agitation) to remove non-adherent and loosely adherent cells, leaving only adherent bacteria. Discs were then irrigated by immersion in either sterile 0.9% normal saline or 0.35% PVP-I for 3 min.

Irrigation at the early biofilm stage

Titanium discs were inoculated and incubated for 60 min to allow bacterial adhesion, followed by rinsing with sterile PBS (immersed in 1.0 mL PBS and withdrawn, × 3; no agitation) to remove non-adherent and loosely adherent cells. Discs were then transferred to fresh TSB and incubated for an additional 6 h at 37 °C to promote early biofilm formation. This 6-hour time point was chosen based on the findings of Kajiyama et al. [29], who demonstrated that the two-dimensional expansion of S. epidermidis biofilm on metallic surfaces is substantially completed within 6–8 h of incubation. After incubation, specimens were rinsed with sterile PBS (immersed in 1.0 mL PBS and withdrawn, × 3; no agitation) to remove planktonic cells and subsequently irrigated by immersion in either sterile 0.9% normal saline or 0.35% PVP-I for 3 min.

Quantification of viable bacterial counts

After irrigation, each disc was transferred to a 15 mL sterile tube containing 5 mL of PBS. Bacteria were detached by vortexing for 3 min, sonication at 42 kHz for 10 min, and an additional 3 min of vortexing. The resulting suspension was serially diluted and plated on Compact Dry TC plates (Nissui Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h, then colonies were counted to determine the CFU per milliliter.

Assessment of BCR

BCR was assessed for those discs subjected to irrigation at the early biofilm stage, following a modification of the method described by Kajiyama et al. [29]. While the original method used stainless steel washers and eight measurement areas, we employed solid ASTM F136 titanium discs to better simulate actual orthopaedic implant surfaces and to facilitate more consistent biofilm formation. This change allowed us to increase the evaluation to nine random fields per disc by adding a central measurement point, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the disc surface. This microscopic approach was selected over the conventional method of measuring bulk optical density (OD) after decolorization. The rationale for prioritizing direct visualization and area quantification is that it provides more definitive evidence regarding the physical persistence and spatial distribution of the biofilm matrix on the titanium surface than bulk OD measurements, which was a primary objective of our structural analysis. After treatment, titanium discs were fixed with 95% ethanol for 1 min, air-dried, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 10 min. The discs were then washed with distilled water and completely air-dried. Biofilm-covered areas were observed using an optical microscope (VHX-100; Keyence, Osaka, Japan). For each disc, nine random fields were selected and imaged at 450 × magnification. The biofilm coverage area was quantified from the acquired images using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), and BCR was calculated as the percentage of biofilm-covered area relative to the total observation area.

Statistical analysis

Experiments for viable bacterial counts were performed with 16 replicates per group, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. BCR measurements were conducted using 4 discs per group and expressed as a percentage of surface coverage. Data were assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance before parametric testing. CFU counts were log-transformed prior to analysis to satisfy the assumptions of ANOVA. Cohen’s d effect sizes were calculated to provide a standardized measure of the magnitude of the observed differences, independent of sample size. Group comparisons were made using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. In addition to P-values, Cohen’s d effect sizes (d) were calculated for all group comparisons to evaluate the magnitude of the experimental effects. All analyses were conducted using JMP version 17 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Ethics statement

Ethical approval was not required for this in vitro study as it did not involve human participants or animal subjects.

Results

Bacterial viability after irrigation in the pre-exposure stage

When titanium discs were irrigated with either saline or 0.35% PVP-I prior to bacterial exposure, no significant difference was seen in the number of adherent viable bacteria (Fig. 1). Mean bacterial count was 3.6 ± 1.5 × 107 CFU/mL in the control group and 3.3 ± 1.3 × 107 CFU/mL in the PVP-I group (P = 0.55, d = 0.21), suggesting that pre-exposure irrigation does not affect subsequent bacterial attachment.

Viable bacterial counts after irrigation in the pre-exposure stage. The control group showed 3.6 ± 1.5 × 107 CFU/mL, and the povidone-iodine group showed 3.3 ± 1.3 × 107 CFU/mL. No significant difference was observed between groups (P = 0.55, d = 0.21). n.s.: not significant. Error bars represent standard deviation. CFU: colony-forming units

Bacterial viability after irrigation in the bacterial adhesion stage

In contrast, irrigation immediately after bacterial adhesion resulted in a dramatic reduction in viable bacterial count (Fig. 2). The control group showed 2.9 ± 1.6 × 105 CFU/mL, while no viable bacteria were detected in the PVP-I group (0 ± 0 CFU/mL, P < 0.0001, d = 2.56). This indicates a potent bactericidal effect of PVP-I when applied shortly after bacterial attachment.

Viable bacterial counts after irrigation in the bacterial adhesion stage. The control group showed 2.9 ± 1.6 × 105 CFU/mL, while no viable bacteria were detected in the povidone-iodine group (0 ± 0 CFU/mL; P < 0.0001, d = 2.56). *P < 0.05. Error bars represent standard deviation. CFU: colony-forming units

Bacterial viability after irrigation in the early biofilm stage

After 6 h of biofilm maturation, PVP-I irrigation remained bactericidal (Fig. 3). The mean CFU in the control group was 4.9 ± 0.1 × 106 CFU/mL, compared to 0.8 ± 0.9 × 105 CFU/mL in the PVP-I group (P < 0.0001, d = 45.3), representing an approximately 98% reduction.

Viable bacterial counts after irrigation in the early biofilm stage. The control group showed 4.9 ± 0.1 × 106 CFU/mL, while the povidone-iodine group showed 0.8 ± 0.9 × 105 CFU/mL (P < 0.0001, d = 45.3). *P < 0.05. Error bars represent standard deviation. CFU: colony-forming units

BCR

Despite significant bacterial reduction, biofilm matrix structures remained on the titanium surfaces following PVP-I irrigation. The BCR remained high in both groups: 93.8 ± 10.5% in the control group and 96.0 ± 7.1% in the PVP-I group (P = 0.30, d = 0.24; Fig. 4). This minor numerical increase may reflect, at least in part, alterations in crystal violet staining, potentially related to mild fixation of biofilm matrix components by PVP-I, rather than a true increase in biofilm formation. Representative microscopic images of crystal violet-stained discs demonstrated biofilm-covered areas on the titanium surfaces in both groups (Fig. 5A and B).

BCR after irrigation in the early biofilm stage. The control group showed a BCR of 93.8 ± 10.5%, compared to 96.0 ± 7.1% for the povidone-iodine group (P = 0.30, d = 0.24). n.s.: not significant. Error bars represent standard deviation. BCR: biofilm coverage rate

Summary of quantitative outcomes

The relative effects of PVP-I irrigation compared to the control group are summarized as follows (Fig. 6). At the pre-exposure stage, the viable bacterial count in the PVP-I group was 91.7% (P = 0.55, d = 0.21) relative to the control group. After the bacterial adhesion stage, viable bacteria were completely eradicated, resulting in 0% (P < 0.0001, d = 2.56) relative to the control group. At the early biofilm stage, viable bacteria were reduced to 1.6% (P < 0.0001, d = 45.3) relative to the control group. Regarding biofilm structure, the BCR after irrigation at the early biofilm stage was 102.3% (P = 0.30, d = 0.24) relative to the control group, indicating that the biofilm matrix persisted despite substantial bacterial reduction.

Relative CFU (bars) and BCR (marker) are presented for Irrigation at pre-exposure stage (91.7%; P = 0.55, d = 0.21), Irrigation after bacterial adhesion stage (0%; P < 0.0001, d = 2.56), and Irrigation at early biofilm stage (CFU: 1.6%; P < 0.0001, d = 45.3; BCR: 102.3%; P = 0.30, d = 0.24), indicating persistent matrix despite substantial bacterial killing. Discussion

Implant-associated infections, particularly those involving S. epidermidis biofilms, remain a considerable clinical challenge due to their intrinsic resistance mechanisms against standard antimicrobial treatments and immune clearance [1, 3–5]. Biofilms formed by S. epidermidis on titanium alloy implants significantly complicate clinical outcomes, often necessitating prolonged antibiotic therapy and sometimes requiring implant removal [1, 3]. This study systematically evaluated the bactericidal efficacy of 0.35% diluted PVP-I at distinct stages of biofilm formation on titanium surfaces, emphasizing the critical importance of irrigation timing and the limitations of chemical-only treatment approaches.

Our results indicated that irrigation at the pre-exposure stage with diluted PVP-I prior to bacterial exposure did not effectively inhibit subsequent bacterial attachment. This ineffectiveness may be due to the transient nature of the antimicrobial activity exerted by iodine under biological conditions, highlighting that PVP-I does not maintain a sustained antibacterial surface effect [23, 30]. Clinically, this suggests that pre-exposure pretreatment of titanium surfaces with PVP-I is likely to be ineffective, as it provides only limited prophylactic benefit if bacterial contamination occurs after the antiseptic application. PVP-I exerts a rapid bactericidal effect but does not confer long-lasting antimicrobial activity once it has been removed or diluted by body fluids during surgery. Therefore, even if the implant surface is initially treated with PVP-I before bacterial exposure, any subsequent introduction of bacteria during the surgical procedure would not be effectively impaired by the prior antiseptic treatment, as the active iodine may no longer be present or effective at the time of contamination. This highlights the importance of addressing intraoperative contamination dynamically, rather than relying solely on preoperative surface disinfection.

In contrast, irrigation after the bacterial adhesion stage resulted in no viable bacteria being detected. This finding underscores the critical timing for intervention immediately following bacterial attachment, where intervention can effectively prevent biofilm establishment. The effectiveness observed in this study aligns with previous research emphasizing the importance of early intervention for preventing biofilm maturation, as bacteria have yet to form protective EPS that significantly enhance microbial resistance [21, 27, 31]. Thus, immediately post-adhesion represents an optimal timing to leverage the full bactericidal potential of irrigation with antiseptics such as diluted PVP-I.

While BCR and CFU are useful metrics, they do not fully characterize the three-dimensional architecture, biomass distribution, or thickness of the biofilm. However, irrigation at the early biofilm stage continued to significantly reduce viable bacterial counts, albeit without achieving total bacterial elimination. In our study, irrigation at the early biofilm stage resulted in an approximately 98% reduction of viable bacteria compared to controls, despite the presence of crystal violet-stained material on the titanium surfaces as assessed by BCR analysis. This quantitatively demonstrates that even in the presence of initial EPS development, substantial killing of bacteria can still be achieved with diluted PVP-I. The presence of EPS is known to confer substantial resistance by providing a physical and biochemical barrier to antiseptic penetration [7, 9, 11, 13, 28]. Our findings align with earlier studies by Fluckiger et al. and Patel et al., which showed progressively increasing resistance associated with biofilm maturation [11, 12]. Notably, our microscopic evaluations and BCR analysis demonstrated that crystal violet-stained material remained on the titanium.

surfaces despite substantial reduction of viable bacteria [21, 27, 32, 33]. The slight numerical increase in BCR following PVP-I irrigation (102.3%) may be attributed to the mild fixation of biofilm matrix proteins by iodine, which potentially enhances crystal violet staining without indicating an increase in biofilm formation.

These findings have important clinical implications. Persistent biofilm matrix can facilitate bacterial recolonization, posing a significant risk of infections recurring postoperatively [6, 34–36]. Therefore, although diluted PVP-I is an effective bactericidal agent, surgical procedures should not rely exclusively on chemical antiseptics. Instead, the incorporation of mechanical disruption techniques, such as physical debridement, brushing, or ultrasonic cleaning, is necessary to effectively remove biofilm matrix. An integrated approach is particularly crucial in revision surgeries or hardware-retention procedures, where biofilm removal is paramount to achieving successful outcomes [3, 22, 25, 37]. In joint arthroplasty and bone fixation using metallic implants, our findings provide hypothesis-generating observations suggesting that the timing of irrigation significantly influences its bactericidal efficacy within this model, and that its effect may be substantially diminished once an early biofilm has developed. Although previous reviews have suggested the efficacy of PVP-I irrigation for preventing surgical site infection [19], our study clarified the bactericidal effects at different biofilm stages. Notably, while our 6-hour model does not replicate the complex variables of actual surgery, the sustained efficacy of diluted PVP-I observed at this time point should be interpreted within the constraints of this in vitro system, and direct extrapolation to intraoperative conditions remains limited.

Limitations

This study showed several limitations. First, the in vitro nature of our experiments limits direct clinical extrapolation; therefore, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating observations rather than practice-defining conclusions, as our simplified in vitro model does not fully replicate the dynamic clinical environment.

Specifically, use of a single bacterial strain (S. epidermidis ATCC 35984) may not fully represent the complex diversity and polymicrobial nature of clinical biofilms, which frequently include various bacterial species and host-derived extracellular components. Future work should incorporate polymicrobial models and multidrug-resistant clinical isolates to improve translational relevance. Second, biofilm structure analysis was restricted to early-stage biofilms without exploring the more complex, three-dimensional architectures of mature biofilms, which exhibit significantly higher resistance to antimicrobial treatments. Finally, the study focused solely on chemical antiseptic treatment, excluding the adjunctive mechanical disruption methods commonly employed in clinical settings. Future studies incorporating polymicrobial biofilms, advanced microscopy to evaluate mature biofilm structures, and combined chemical-mechanical intervention approaches are necessary to enhance the clinical relevance and comprehensiveness of these findings. Specifically, future studies employing advanced imaging techniques, such as confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) or scanning electron microscopy (SEM), will be essential to further elucidate the 3D structural changes and biomass distribution within the biofilm matrix.

Our static model isolates chemical effects but differs from the dynamic clinical environment, where factors such as synovial fluid flow, shear stress, host immune cells, and the rapid dilution of PVP-I by tissue fluids in vivo may significantly alter antiseptic efficacy. Therefore, clinical translation should pair chemical with mechanical approaches, and additional validation across pathogens, materials, and flow conditions is warranted.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the timing of 0.35% PVP-I irrigation critically influences its bactericidal efficacy on titanium surfaces. Irrigation at the bacterial adhesion stage was most effective in reducing viable bacteria, whereas at the pre-exposure stage showed no preventive effect and crystal violet-stained material persisted despite substantial bacterial killing at the early biofilm stage. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and suggest that combining timely antiseptic irrigation with mechanical debridement may be important for optimizing infection control strategies.
