I. Background

    Earlier this year, the United States Copyright Office (``Office'') 
received a letter from Representative Scott Fitzgerald (the 
``Congressional Request'') expressing concerns over streaming fraud and 
its impact on royalties for sound recordings and musical works.\1\ The 
Congressional Request asked the Office to ``examine the prevalence of 
streaming fraud across digital platforms and how it may be affecting 
the music industry as a whole.'' \2\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \1\ Letter from Rep. Scott Fitzgerald, Comm. on the Judiciary, 
to Shira Perlmutter, Register of Copyrights and Dir., U.S. Copyright 
Office at 1-4 (May 21, 2026) (``Congressional Request''), https://www.copyright.gov/policy/music-streaming-fraud/usco-letter-on-music-streaming-fraud.pdf.
    \2\ Id. at 1-4.

    In response to the Congressional Request, this notice provides 
background on streaming fraud and the music industry. The notice 
solicits public comments that, together with the Office's own research, 
may contribute to Congress's understanding of streaming fraud, current 
efforts to curb this problem, and potential solutions.

A. Economic Importance of Music Streaming

    Current consumption of recorded music is driven largely by digital 
streaming.\3\ The Digital Media Association estimates that ``streaming 
[is] now driving around 70% of global sales,'' making it a significant 
contributor to the music economy's

[[Page 64189]]

overall health.\4\ For sound recording copyright owners (e.g., record 
labels), the latest figures place streaming-specific revenues at 
approximately $9.5 billion domestically \5\ and $22 billion 
globally.\6\ While public data about streaming-specific revenues for 
musical works is not available, total revenue for their copyright 
owners (e.g., music publishers) is reported to be approximately $7.3 
billion domestically \7\ and $10 billion globally.\8\ Publicly 
available sources predict that music streaming revenue will grow 
significantly over the next decade.\9\ Unfortunately, these benefits 
can be undercut by streaming fraud.

B. How Music Streaming Fraud Operates

    Music streaming fraud \10\ can be defined as ``the illegitimate 
practice of using deceptive, inappropriate, or unpermitted methods to 
artificially increase the number of streams a song or album receives in 
order to skew public perception of notoriety, to gain financial 
rewards, or any other benefit(s) not otherwise obtained by legitimate 
means.'' \11\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \10\ Streaming fraud may also be referred to as ``stream 
manipulation'' or ``artificial streaming'' and be used as a blanket 
statement to describe various criminal offenses. See Sealed 
Indictment at 2-17, U.S. v. Smith, No. 24-cr-504 (S.D.N.Y. Sep. 4, 
2024) (``Smith Indictment''), Doc. No. 3, https://perma.cc/RQL5-6T6Q 
(discussing a ``streaming fraud'' indictment alleging conspiracy to 
commit wire fraud under 18 U.S.C. 1349, wire fraud under 18 U.S.C. 2 
and 18 U.S.C. 1343, and money laundering conspiracy under 18 U.S.C. 
1956(h)).
    \11\ Streaming Fraud 101, Music Fights Fraud All., https://perma.cc/M2GA-AERD (defining ``streaming fraud'') (last visited Sep. 
25, 2026).
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Streaming services commonly pay for their use of copyrighted works 
by dedicating a percentage of their revenue to royalty pools, which are 
then used to pay various rights owners.\12\ Those royalties may be paid 
directly to those rightsholders, or may first flow through different 
intermediaries, including distributors \13\ and statutory collectives, 
such as SoundExchange and the Mechanical Licensing Collective 
(``MLC''), and other collective management organizations (``CMOs'') or 
performance rights organizations (``PROs'').\14\ Generally, under this 
``pro-rata'' system, the more a song is played on a streaming service, 
the more revenue that song's rightsholders will receive.\15\

    Unfortunately, this system can incentivize bad actors to 
fraudulently or deceptively inflate ``plays'' of their songs, diverting 
money from rights owners of legitimately streamed works. Streaming 
fraudsters use bot farms,\16\ click farms,\17\ playlist stuffing,\18\ 
account hijacking,\19\ or other methods of user manipulation \20\ to 
inflate their songs' plays. These activities are frequently 
accomplished by unethical marketing companies.\21\ Streaming fraudsters 
may also engage in ``copyright fraud,'' a term that, as used

[[Page 64190]]

here, would include claiming others' original works as their own or 
uploading and claiming manipulated versions of others' works, sometimes 
called ``carbon copying'' or ``ghost tracking.'' \22\

    Streaming fraud is already harming copyright owners, performers, 
songwriters, digital music providers \23\ (``DMPs''), and others. While 
the primary harm is the dilution of the royalty revenue pool,\24\ it 
can also cause secondary harms, such as affecting where songs are 
placed on a DMP's playlist or in an algorithm's queue; \25\ 
``distorting the [streaming] data artists and venues use to make key 
business decisions,'' including ``when and where to tour''; \26\ 
``reduc[ing] the likelihood of music fans discovering talented, lesser-
known artists''; \27\ or causing other reputational or financial 
harms.\28\ As the fraud detection platform Beatdapp has explained, 
streaming fraud ``does more than just drain revenue,'' ``[i]t poisons 
your analytics, wastes marketing spend, compromises user trust in your 
charts and leaderboards, and leads to inaccurate payouts that damage 
partner relationships.'' \29\

C. Combatting Music Streaming Fraud

    Leaders from across the music industry have condemned streaming 
fraud.\30\ As one music executive stated, ``[t]here aren't a lot of 
advocates for fraud.'' \31\ Different industry sectors--including DMPs, 
distributors, vendors, copyright owners, PROs and CMOs, and law 
enforcement--have approached the problem in different ways. Below, the 
Office has identified some publicly reported strategies used to combat 
streaming fraud. These are intended to be illustrative, not exhaustive, 
especially as not all such efforts have been made public.\32\

partners to combat streaming fraud. Internal approaches can include 
daily evaluations of suspected artificial streams,\33\ including 
analyzing metrics like listening time, users' geographic locations, 
users' streams of specific artists, and the relationship of saves and 
follows to total streams.\34\ DMPs have also imposed fines on uploaders 
of artificially streamed songs.\35\

    Music distributors have taken various approaches to combatting 
streaming fraud.\38\ They have bolstered their identity verification 
departments, created ``Trust and Safety Departments,'' and implemented 
``know your customer'' (KYC) protocols.\39\ Some require their clients 
(songwriters or performing artists) to agree to standard contract terms 
prohibiting streaming fraud \40\ and subjecting violators to 
contractual penalties that range from

[[Page 64191]]

issuing warnings to closing accounts \41\ or imposing monetary 
penalties.\42\ Despite these efforts, some ``music-streaming scammers'' 
are able to continue their ``lucrative schemes'' by ``bouncing from 
distributor to distributor'' once they are detected--a problem that has 
led at least one distributor to propose the creation of ``a worldwide 
database of individuals found to be abusing the streaming system.'' 
\43\ The Music Fights Fraud Alliance, launched in 2023, was created to 
enable distributors and DMPs to better coordinate their efforts to 
combat streaming fraud.\44\

to prevent both streaming and copyright fraud, including through data-
accuracy and related initiatives.\45\ Some copyright owners have 
partnered with DMPs and distributors in their efforts \46\ and recently 
IFPI announced the launch of its ``Streaming Integrity Initiative,'' 
joining copyright owners and distributors who have ``commit[ed] to 
baseline practices to prevent, detect and respond to streaming fraud.'' 
\47\ Others have proposed creative solutions, such as replacing the 
pro-rata royalty system with an artist- or user-centric model, to 
prevent the dilution of royalties owed for legitimate plays.\48\

    Law enforcement agencies also play an important role.\49\ In one 
recent high-profile case, the United States Department of Justice 
indicted a defendant in North Carolina on charges of wire fraud, 
conspiracy to commit wire fraud, and conspiracy to commit money 
laundering related to streaming fraud.\50\ The defendant, Michael 
Smith, had created thousands of ``bot'' accounts on streaming 
platforms, used those accounts to stream his uploaded songs,\51\ and 
fraudulently collected some $10 million in royalties.\52\
