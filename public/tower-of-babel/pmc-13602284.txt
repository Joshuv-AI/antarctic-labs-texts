Deep Brain Stimulation of the Posterior Subthalamic Area and the Subthalamic Nucleus in Tremor‐Dominant Parkinson's Disease: A Randomized, Crossover Trial
Authors: Zhengyu Lin, Zhitong Zeng, Yixin Pan, Peng Huang, Yuyan Tan, Chencheng Zhang, Bomin Sun, Dianyou Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe posterior subthalamic area (PSA) is a familiarized target for Parkinson's disease (PD) in the lesioning era and has recently been reconsidered for deep brain stimulation (DBS).ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to compare the therapeutic efficacy of PSA versus subthalamic nucleus (STN) DBS in tremor‐dominant Parkinson's disease (TD‐PD).MethodsThis single‐center, randomized, crossover trial was conducted at Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai, China, from June 2022 to August 2025. Patients with TD‐PD received bilateral DBS implants via a single trajectory targeting both PSA and STN. Participants received 2 months of PSA stimulation followed by 2 months of STN stimulation or vice versa, with blinded programming and evaluation. The primary outcome was the difference in off medication improvement from baseline to the end of each stimulation period in the tremor subscore of the Movement Disorder Society–sponsored revision of the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale Part III (MDS UPDRS‐III; ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05382858).ResultsOf the 40 patients assessed for eligibility, 27 were randomly assigned and 19 completed the randomized crossover phase. Both PSA‐DBS and STN‐DBS significantly reduced the MDS UPDRS‐III tremor subscore (baseline, 17.5 [3.3]; PSA, 4.5 [2.8]; STN, 8.0 [5.3]; P < 0.001 for both). PSA‐DBS achieved greater tremor suppression than STN‐DBS (adjusted mean difference −3.3; 95% confidence interval [CI]: −5.4 to −1.3; P = 0.004). STN‐DBS led to greater balance improvement (adjusted mean difference −1.7; 95% CI: −2.6 to −0.7; P = 0.002). Three cases of abnormal impedance occurred. Other adverse effects were mostly stimulation related and could be resolved with reprogramming.ConclusionsGiven the limited sample size and study attrition, these findings should be considered hypothesis generating. Nonetheless, PSA‐DBS appears to be a safe and effective alternative or complementary target to STN in treating TD‐PD and warrants further investigation in larger, definitive trials. © 2026 The Author(s). The subthalamic nucleus (STN) and globus pallidus interna (GPi) are two well‐established deep brain stimulation (DBS) targets for the treatment of advanced Parkinson's disease (PD). 1 Tremor is one of the symptoms most responsive to DBS; however, not all patients obtain the expected reduction, with failure rates ranging from 10% to 60% depending on circumstances. 2 , 3 Further, for on medication tremor, existing literature suggests that GPi‐DBS yields more modest improvement around 50%. 4 STN‐DBS may achieve higher rates of tremor control than GPi‐DBS, 4 but this may come at the cost of high‐frequency and high‐amplitude stimulation, which is associated with potential negative impacts on speech, gait, and cognition. The ventral intermediate nucleus of thalamus (VIM) has been further used as an adjunctive target, via thalamotomy or VIM‐DBS, for PD cases with severe tremor, particularly when tremor remains disabling despite adequate STN‐DBS 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 or GPi‐DBS. 4 However, thalamotomy is less feasible for severe bilateral tremor. Moreover, VIM cannot be directly targeted on conventional magnetic resonance (MR) sequences. In addition, planning a frontal approach to target both STN and VIM using a single DBS lead is technically challenging.

The posterior subthalamic area (PSA), mainly consisting of the caudal zona incerta (cZI) and the prelemniscal radiation (Raprl), is a familiarized target for PD in the lesioning era and has been reappraised as a DBS target in recent years. 9 Blomstedt and colleagues compared the efficacy of PSA‐DBS with best medical therapy in PD and demonstrated both the short‐ and long‐term benefits of PSA‐DBS on tremor, bradykinesia, and rigidity. 10 , 11 , 12 Moreover, PSA can be directly localized on T2‐weighted MR sequences using anatomical landmarks, 13 and a single‐trajectory frontal approach traversing both PSA and STN is technically feasible. 14 This raises the possibility for crossover comparisons of PSA‐DBS versus STN‐DBS in PD to determine the role of PSA in the therapeutic armamentarium for PD, especially in relation to STN.

In this randomized and crossover trial, we aimed at directly comparing the efficacy of PSA‐DBS versus STN‐DBS in the treatment of tremor‐dominant PD (TD‐PD). The primary outcome was the difference in improvement from baseline to the end of the PSA versus STN stimulation period in the tremor subscore of the Movement Disorder Society–sponsored revision of the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale Part III (MDS UPDRS‐III) 15 in the randomized phase.

Subjects and Methods

Protocol Approval, Registration, and Patient Consent

This single‐center randomized crossover trial was approved by the Ethical Committee of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai, China (approval number: 2023‐212), was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and was registered with the ClinicalTrials Registry (https://clinicaltrials.gov, NCT05382858). A clerical correction was made to the registry wording to align with the original study protocol, and a detailed disclosure regarding this correction is provided in the Supporting Information. All participants gave written informed consent before enrollment.

Participant Screening and Recruitment

The main inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a clinical diagnosis of idiopathic PD according to the MDS Clinical Diagnostic Criteria for Parkinson's Disease 16 ; (2) age > 18 and ≤75 years; (3) tremor‐dominant phenotype in the on medication condition, defined by the clinical judgment of senior movement disorder specialists based on the predominance of tremor as the most disabling motor symptom; (4) receiving regular antiparkinsonian drugs for more than 6 weeks; and (5) good compliance with written informed consent provided. The main exclusion criteria were atypical parkinsonism; presence of severe cognitive, psychiatric, or other comorbidities that might interfere with the patient's ability to complete evaluations; and presence of significant brain atrophy, infarcts, or anatomical abnormalities in the target regions.

Surgery Procedures

The surgical planning was described in detail in our previous work. 14 In brief, both PSA and STN were directly targeted on 3.0‐T high‐resolution T2‐weighted MR images. The trajectory was planned using the PSA as the primary target. 17 The coronal and sagittal angles were adjusted to obtain a trajectory by traversing the dorsolateral STN target and to avoid sulci, blood vessels, and ventricles. All participants underwent frame‐based DBS implantation under general anesthesia without intraoperative mapping, along with concurrent implantable pulse generator (IPG) implantation. The final position of the contacts was verified by fusion of preoperative MR and postoperative CT. Only patients with at least one contact in the PSA and one in the STN would be entered into the subsequent clinical trial.

Outcome Measures

The primary end point was the difference in off medication improvement from baseline to the end of the PSA versus STN stimulation period in the tremor subscore of the MDS UPDRS‐III 15 in the randomized phase. The tremor subscore was defined as the sum of the MDS UPDRS‐III items 3.15 to 3.18, capturing the severity of postural, kinetic, and rest tremor, as well as rest tremor constancy. 18 Secondary end points included total score and other subscores of the MDS UPDRS‐III, 18 subscales of the MDS UPDRS, total score and subscores 17 of the Fahn‐Tolosa‐Marin tremor rating scale (FTM‐TRS), 19 balance (Berg Balance Scale [BBS]), 20 mood (Beck Depression Inventory‐II [BDI‐II] 21 and Beck Anxiety Inventory [BAI]), 22 nonmotor symptoms (nonmotor symptoms scale for Parkinson's disease [NMSS]), 23 cognition (Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) 24 ], health‐related quality of life (the 39‐item Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire [PDQ‐39]), 25 levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD), total electrical energy delivered (TEED), and adverse events.

Randomization and Blinding

Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to one of two treatment sequences using the Sealed Envelope online simple randomization service (https://www.sealedenvelope.com/). To maintain blinding of both patients and the assessing physician with respect to stimulation site during the crossover phase, each subject was handled by two investigators: one blinded assessor and one unblinded investigator. The blinded investigator carried out all clinical evaluations and outcome assessments, without access to programming settings. The unblinded investigator was responsible for programming and may adjust stimulation parameters as needed, based solely on the clinical judgments.

Sample Size and Power Calculation

The sample size calculation was based on the expected difference in tremor improvement between PSA and STN stimulation under the off medication condition. Methodological details are described in the Supporting Information Methods.

Lead Reconstruction and Visualization

The default pipeline of the Lead‐DBS toolbox 26 (version 3.2; MATLAB R2023a) was used for cohort‐level visualization of location of active contacts. Detailed descriptions are available in the Supporting Information Methods.

Statistical Analysis

All patients who underwent surgery were included in the safety dataset, and those who completed the randomized phase were included in the Full Analysis Set. Participants who experienced profound clinical deterioration deemed inconsistent with the natural history of PD (as determined by investigator assessment) were excluded before the efficacy analysis to ensure data integrity. Outcome measures were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as median with range and interquartile range, as appropriate. Changes in each end point from baseline between PSA and STN were analyzed using a linear mixed‐effects model with repeated measures, accounting for the crossover design and appropriately handling missing data under the assumption of missing at random. To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses using the per‐protocol set and the complete‐case set. The latter was defined as the subset of participants with nonmissing observations for a specific study end point. Comparisons between the postoperative ON stimulation condition and the preoperative baseline were performed using the paired‐sample Wilcoxon signed rank test. These analyses were based on complete cases. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1). Two‐sided P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Consistent with previous studies that had similar crossover design and limited sample sizes, 17 , 27 all secondary end points analyses were considered exploratory, and P values are reported without adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Results

Patients, Randomization, and Protocol Deviations

From June 2022 to August 2024, 40 patients were screened for enrollment. Among them, five patients did not meet the inclusion criteria, and four were excluded because of anticipated noncompliance. Thus, 31 patients underwent surgery. Four patients were subsequently excluded because of the absence of contact within the PSA. Therefore, 27 patients entered the crossover phase (12 STN first, 15 PSA first). Another eight patients, four for each group, were further excluded from the randomization phase because of the following reasons: (1) lost to follow‐up (n = 4); (2) worsening of symptoms as a result of COVID‐19 (n = 2); (3) unexplained worsening of symptoms, probably because of cerebral infarcts (n = 1); and (4) hardware‐related complication (n = 1). Specifically, three individuals exhibited a marked worsening of motor symptoms at the postoperative baseline compared with their preoperative status. This deterioration, occurring within a 2‐ to 6‐month window, was deemed by the investigators to be inconsistent with the typical natural history of PD and was probably attributed to extraneous factors, including the sequelae of COVID‐19 infection or cerebral infarcts. Including these outliers would have introduced significant bias. Therefore, 19 patients (13 males) completed the crossover phase (8 STN first, 11 PSA first), and their data were included for final statistical analysis. Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. The mean age at enrollment was 61.5 ± 7.4 years. The mean disease duration was 9.6 ± 5.0 years. The mean baseline LEDD was 565.6 ± 261.4 mg/day. Eight patients (six STN‐DBS and two PSA‐DBS) did not tolerate respective chronic stimulation condition because of unsatisfactory symptom control. Acute motor evaluation was then completed. During the subsequent open‐label phase, 3 patients were lost to follow‐up due to personal reasons (inconvenience to return for assessments), leaving 16 patients who completed the 12‐month evaluation (Fig. 1B). The follow‐up period for outcome assessment, including efficacy and safety measures, ended in August 2025.

In the STN, the mean coordinates were x = 12.5 ± 1.5 mm, y = −2.5 ± 1.3 mm, and z = −1.0 ± 1.6 mm. The detailed individual coordinates for active contacts are shown in Table 2 and Figure 2. Primary End Point

Compared with baseline, both PSA‐DBS and STN‐DBS significantly reduced the tremor subscore of the MDS UPDRS‐III (baseline: 17.5 ± 3.3; PSA: 4.5 ± 2.8, improvement rate 74.0% ± 17.8%; STN: 8.0 ± 5.3, improvement rate 54.1% ± 31.3%; both P < 0.001). PSA‐DBS further decreased the MDS UPDRS‐III tremor subscore compared with STN‐DBS (adjusted mean difference: −3.3, 95% confidence interval [CI]: −5.4 to −1.3, P = 0.004) (Table 3). The primary end point was not influenced by significant sequence effects or period effects.

Secondary End Point: Other Tremor Subcores

Compared with baseline, both PSA‐DBS and STN‐DBS significantly reduced part A and B scores (baseline: 36.4 ± 19.1; PSA: 9.2 ± 6.4; STN: 15.5 ± 15.0; both P < 0.001), part C score (baseline: 10.5 ± 5.6; PSA: 4.4 ± 3.9, P < 0.001; STN: 6.4 ± 3.4, P = 0.005), hand function subscore (baseline: 26.4 ± 14.0; PSA: 7.2 ± 5.3; STN: 12.0 ± 12.6; both P < 0.001), and axial tremor subscore (baseline: 6.4 ± 4.8; PSA: 1.3 ± 1.2; STN: 2.4 ± 2.4; both P < 0.001) of the FTM‐TRS (Table 3). Compared with STN‐DBS, PSA‐DBS provided significantly greater reduction in part A and B scores (adjusted mean difference: −5.7, 95% CI: −10.9 to −0.5, P = 0.032), part C score (adjusted mean difference: −2.5, 95% CI: −3.8 to −1.2, P = 0.002), and axial tremor subscore (adjusted mean difference: −1.3, 95% CI: −2.2 to −0.4, P = 0.007) of the FTM‐TRS (Table 3). No significant sequence effects or period effects were observed.

Secondary End Point: Other Motor Functions

Compared with baseline, both PSA‐DBS and STN‐DBS significantly improved the total score (baseline: 56.0 ± 10.5; PSA: 29.2 ± 13.0; STN: 31.1 ± 14.3; both P < 0.001), rigidity (baseline: 10.4 ± 3.0; PSA: 7.5 ± 3.1, P = 0.003; STN: 6.6 ± 2.9, P < 0.001), and bradykinesia subscore of the MDS UPDRS‐III (baseline: 22.4 ± 6.6; PSA: 12.8 ± 6.2; STN: 12.5 ± 4.8; both P < 0.001), with insignificant difference between the two targets. These magnitudes of improvement in overall motor symptoms (48.1% ± 20.5% and 44.1% ± 24.1% for PSA‐DBS and STN‐DBS, respectively) are consistent with the efficacy rates reported in the current literature for both targets. 10 , 11 , 28 , 29 , 30 STN‐DBS yielded significant improvements in the axial and gait subscores (P = 0.025 and 0.020 vs. baseline, respectively), whereas PSA‐DBS showed nonsignificant trends toward improvement (P = 0.057 and 0.068 vs. baseline). Regarding balance function, compared with baseline, STN‐DBS produced significant improvements in total score of the BBS, whereas PSA‐DBS did not (baseline: 49.0 ± 10.7; PSA: 50.1 ± 5.5, P = 0.793; STN: 52.0 ± 5.7, P = 0.037). STN‐DBS outperformed PSA‐DBS in improving balance function (adjusted mean difference: −1.6, 95% CI: −2.6 to −0.7, P = 0.002). The TUG time remained comparable with baseline following PSA‐DBS or STN‐DBS (Table 3). No significant sequence effects or period effects were observed.

Secondary End Point: Nonmotor Functions, Quality of Life, and Others

Compared with baseline, both PSA‐DBS and STN‐DBS significantly improved the health‐related quality of life (PDQ‐39 summary index, P = 0.004, 0.013), nonmotor symptoms (NMSS, P = 0.024, 0.015), BAI (P = 0.001, 0.017), and BDI (P = 0.002, 0.028), and reduced the LEDD (P = 0.001, 0.005). Two targets were statistically comparable in these outcome measures. PSA‐DBS yielded significant improvement in MDS UPDRS‐I, II, and IV scale (P = 0.028, 0.043, 0.004), whereas STN‐DBS provided significant improvement only in MDS UPDRS‐I (P = 0.013, 0.192, 0.122). PSA‐DBS outperformed STN‐DBS in improving MDS UPDRS‐II (adjusted mean difference: −3.6, 95% CI: −6.8 to −0.5, P = 0.031). In addition, no cognitive change was documented. No significant sequence effects or period effects were observed.

Sensitivity Analysis

To assess the robustness of the primary findings, we performed sensitivity analyses on the per‐protocol set (n = 11) and the complete‐case set (Supporting Information Table S2). Further details are described in the Supporting Information Results.

Outcomes in the Open‐Label Phase

In the open‐label phase, 5 (26.3%) of the 19 patients favored PSA‐DBS and 4 (21.1%) favored STN‐DBS. The other 10 patients (52.6%) had active contacts in both PSA and STN (Supporting Information Table S3). At the 12‐month follow‐up, marked reductions of the MDS UPDRS‐III total score and subscores, FTM‐TRS, and LEDD were observed for the entire cohort (Supporting Information Table S4).

Adverse Events and Stimulation‐Related Side Effects

No intracranial hemorrhage or infection was documented. After the completion of the randomized crossover phase and in the open‐label phase, three participants were found to have abnormally elevated impedance on at least one electrode contact. Of these, two underwent replacement of their IPG. Further details are described in the Supporting Information Results. Most of the remaining adverse effects could be classified as stimulation‐ or surgical‐related side effects. All patients reported pain at the surgical incision site and mild dizziness perioperatively. During the crossover phase, nine events of paresthesia, six events of dysarthria, five events of forward‐leaning gait disturbance, and three events of dyskinesia were documented under PSA‐DBS, and six events of dyskinesia, four events of forward‐leaning gait disturbance, and four events of dysarthria were documented under STN‐DBS. Most of these adverse effects (20 after PSA‐DBS and 12 after STN‐DBS) could be resolved with reprogramming.

Discussion

In this prospective trial, we intraindividually compared the efficacy of PSA‐DBS versus STN‐DBS in patients with TD‐PD. PSA‐DBS demonstrated comparable efficacy to STN‐DBS in improving overall motor symptoms, nonmotor symptoms, and health‐related quality of life, while showing a potential superiority in reducing tremor severity. In contrast, STN‐DBS appeared to outperform PSA‐DBS in alleviating axial symptoms and improving balance function. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first and only prospective, randomized, crossover trial to date that directly compares the clinical outcomes of PSA‐DBS versus STN‐DBS in TD‐PD.

As noted in the Results section, the percentage of off medication improvement in overall motor symptoms from PSA‐DBS (48.1 ± 20.5%) or STN‐DBS (44.1 ± 24.1%) observed during the crossover phase is comparable with the current literature that targeted PSA 10 , 11 , 28 or STN 29 , 30 alone. The location and variability of the stereotactic coordinates for active contacts in both PSA and STN stimulation in this trial also align with the current literature. For PSA, the reported stereotactic coordinates are diverse, with the most significant variation being observed in the z coordinate. 9 , 31 In comparison with Blomstedt and colleagues, 10 one of the recent and dedicated clinical trials investigating the efficacy of PSA‐DBS versus best medical therapy in PD, the stereotactic coordinates of our PSA target (midcommissural point (MCP)‐based, both hemispheres averaged) did not differ significantly in terms of laterality and verticality, but showed a statistically significant anterior–posterior difference: Δx = −0.61 (95% CI: −1.77 to 0.54, P = 0.290), Δy = 2.65 (95% CI: 1.97–3.33, P < 0.001), Δz = −0.79 (95% CI: −1.63 to 0.06, P = 0.067). This discrepancy is consistent with differences in targeting strategy that we primarily targeted the area between Raprl and cZI, 13 whereas Blomstedt and colleagues 10 focused more closely on the medial part of the posterior tail of the STN, that is, cZI. Regarding STN, the reported stereotactic coordinates of active contacts in STN were close to the reported hot spot located within the dorsal STN. 32 , 33

PSA resides within the subthalamic region and is at the crossroad of the cortico‐basal ganglia network and cerebello‐thalamo‐cortical network 34 ; these two networks are implicated in the genesis and maintenance of parkinsonian tremor according to the “dimmer‐switch” model. 35 , 36 In addition, the cortico‐basal ganglia network may be more involved in dopamine‐responsive tremor, whereas the cerebello‐thalamo‐cortical network might be more implicated in dopamine‐resistant tremor. 37 , 38 Anatomically, stimulation within the PSA would engage the cerebello‐thalamo‐cortical network more extensively than STN‐DBS. Dynamic causal modeling (DCM) analysis also showed that PSA stimulation provided a greater reduction in cortico‐cerebellar coupling compared with STN stimulation. 39 In this cohort, we enrolled patients with TD‐PD who exhibited on medication tremor, indicating the presence of dopamine‐resistant tremor. This characteristic may account for the superior efficacy of PSA‐DBS in reducing tremor severity compared with STN‐DBS. Beyond the statistical significance observed, interpreting the clinical significance of our findings requires caution because a dedicated minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the MDS UPDRS‐III tremor subscore remains undefined. Nonetheless, it is informative to consider that the adjusted estimated difference of −3.3 points for the primary end point is comparable in magnitude with the established MCID for the (MDS) UPDRS‐III total score (3.25–5 points). 40 , 41 Although this comparison is indirect, the fact that tremor improvement alone reached this threshold may suggest a robust clinical effect for tremor‐dominant patients. This preliminary indication of clinical benefit is reinforced by the corresponding improvements in functional disability as measured by the FTM‐TRS part C.

PSA‐DBS provided statistically comparable efficacy in reducing bradykinesia and rigidity relative to STN‐DBS. One plausible mechanism is that current from PSA stimulation may spread to modulate both STN and adjacent cortico‐basal ganglia fiber bundles, possibly engaging the hyperdirect and pallido‐fugal pathways. Consistently, DCM analysis showed that PSA‐DBS could reduce cortico‐subthalamic (ie, hyperdirect) coupling while simultaneously enhancing STN self‐inhibition. 39 However, our results showed that PSA‐DBS was less effective than STN‐DBS in ameliorating axial symptoms, including balance function. Indeed, gait disturbance and ataxia are common side effects associated with PSA/VIM DBS in essential tremor treatment. 17 , 27 , 42 , 43 One explanation is that PSA‐DBS may excessively affect cerebellar‐related circuits and medial lemniscus, which in turn could contribute to gait and balance impairments. 44 , 45

Furthermore, results from the open‐label phase suggest that the potential synergy between PSA and STN stimulation may represent a promising therapeutic avenue. More than half of our participants (52.6%) ultimately transitioned to dual‐target stimulation during the long‐term follow‐up. Although our randomized phase did not demonstrate significant differences between PSA and STN in improving bradykinesia or rigidity, a notable trend favored STN for rigidity control. This finding, although likely constrained by statistical power, aligns with our clinical experience. We empirically use PSA for robust tremor suppression, while leveraging STN stimulation to further modulate bradykinesia and rigidity. This dual‐target strategy may allow for a more comprehensive and individualized motor optimization. In summary, the inclusion of a combined PSA‐STN stimulation arm warrants further investigation in future prospective trials to formally evaluate its long‐term synergistic potential.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, the significant attrition from randomization to study completion reflects the inherent challenges of conducting a rigorous, multimonth crossover trial in this complex patient population. This attrition, combined with the small final sample size, may introduce selection bias because the analyzed cohort likely represents patients who better tolerated both stimulation targets. In addition, 8 of 19 patients experienced protocol deviations because of unsatisfactory tremor control, leading them to evaluate only the acute effects of either PSA or STN stimulation. This resulted in missing data on certain nonmotor scales.

The technical constraints of our study also warrant consideration. Due to the unavailability of directional electrodes at our institution during the trial's implementation, we employed a single‐trajectory dual‐target technique. Consequently, the actual stimulation site within the STN was more dorsal than the conventional target, which may not represent its optimal location. Future studies using directional electrodes are needed to further investigate the comparative efficacy of PSA‐DBS and STN‐DBS. Lastly, the long‐term efficacy and complications of PSA‐DBS require monitoring over a longer time frame.

Conclusions

Considering these constraints, we emphasize that our findings, notably the observed benefits of PSA over STN for tremor suppression in TD‐PD, should be interpreted as hypothesis generating rather than definitive. Nonetheless, this intraindividual comparison provides a unique dataset that offers a robust foundation for future adequately powered multicenter trials. We speculate that PSA may serve as a reliable DBS target or as a complementary target alongside the STN for the TD‐PD population. The latter approach could potentially be implemented through directional DBS technology with multiple independent current controls.
