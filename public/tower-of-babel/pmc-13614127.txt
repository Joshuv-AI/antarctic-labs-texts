Intraday and Interday Repeatability of Laser Speckle Contrast Imaging for Ocular Blood Flow Measurement in Healthy Subjects
Authors: Xiaogang Wang, Giovana Rosa Gameiro, Andrew Hoover, Mariam Moarefi, Shuya Wang, Lian Duan, Xiaoming Wang, Hong Jiang, Jianhua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the intra- and interday repeatability of ocular blood flow velocity index (BFVi) measurements using a laser speckle contrast imaging device.MethodsBFVi measurements were obtained using XyCAM CRE (Vasoptic Medical Inc., Columbia, MD). Two imaging sessions were performed on the same day (26 eyes) and on different days (20 eyes). For optic nerve head–centered datasets, the analyzed regions included the overall field of view, the optic disc, and a standard temporal peripapillary region. For macula-centered datasets, the regions analyzed were the overall field of view, the foveal avascular zone, and the perifoveal region. For each region, heart rate–adjusted measurements of the peak, dip, and mean BFVi were assessed.ResultsFor intraday measurements of all the optic nerve head–centered parameters, the coefficients of variation (CVs) ranged from 4.8% to 8.5%, and the intraclass correlation coefficient ranged from 0.74 to 0.98. For all macula-centered parameters, the CVs ranged from 4.7% to 6.2%, and the ICCs ranged from 0.93 to 0.98. For the interday measurements of all the optic nerve head–centered parameters, the CVs ranged from 7.9% to 12.6%, and the ICCs ranged from 0.56 to 0.93. For all the macula-centered parameters, the CVs ranged from 8.6% to 15.0%, and the intraclass correlation coefficient ranged from 0.79 to 0.94.ConclusionsLaser speckle contrast imaging demonstrated strong intraday repeatability and reliability for BFVi measurement in healthy subjects, with good interday reliability for key parameters despite some variability.Translational RelevanceLaser speckle contrast imaging shows promise for clinical use in ophthalmology due to its robust intraday and good interday reliability for ocular blood flow velocity measurements.

Introduction

The precise and reliable measurement of ocular blood flow (OBF) holds significant promise for understanding ocular disease, offering insights beyond traditional anatomical biomarkers. The OBF plays an important role in the evaluation of retinal dysfunction, including abnormal systemic conditions and ocular diseases, such as stroke,1 cardiovascular complications,2 multiple sclerosis,3 hypertensive retinopathy,4 diabetic retinopathy,5 and high intraocular pressure conditions.6 Compared with other retinal vascular morphology parameters, such as retinal vessel tortuosity7 and vascular density,8 the OBF could potentially provide more functional information reflecting retinal tissue metabolism status for the early detection of the above-mentioned related diseases. Consequently, the development and validation of noninvasive, repeatable, and reproducible methods for quantifying retinal blood flow are paramount for both clinical research and diagnostic applications.

Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) has become a prominent noninvasive method for imaging the microvasculature of the retina and choroid. However, standard OCTA primarily visualizes vascular structures and is limited in its ability to quantify actual blood flow. Its quantitative accuracy can be compromised by factors such as image quality, motion artifacts, and segmentation errors. A significant advancement in this area is the second-generation variable interscan time analysis OCTA, which uses spatially compiled OCTA and a temporal autocorrelation model to quantify capillary-level blood flow.9 This technique, with its high A-scan rates, offers insights into choriocapillaris flow impairment and microvascular lesions. Despite these capabilities, variable interscan time analysis OCTA has several limitations, including a restricted field of view that impedes comprehensive peripheral retinal imaging, an inability to detect extremely slow blood flow, and a lack of dynamic visualization of vascular leakage.9

Other methods for OBF measurement exist, such as Fourier domain Doppler OCT,10 laser Doppler velocimetry,11 laser speckle flowgraphy,12 adaptive optics scanning laser ophthalmoscopy,13 adaptive optics near-confocal scanning ophthalmoscope,14 and retinal function imaging. However, these techniques face challenges like motion artifacts, angle dependence, and limited resolution. Laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI) has inherent advantages: it is quick, noncontact, uses mild near-infrared illumination, provides full-field imaging with fast (milliseconds) image acquisition, and can yield quantitative blood velocity values and characterize blood flow dynamics over one or more cardiac cycles, thus providing a more nuanced assessment of the vascular flow status.15

Given its potential as an important tool in oculomics research and clinical practice, a comprehensive evaluation of its intra- and interday repeatability for blood flow velocity index (BFVi) measurements (a relative unit that maps the movement of red blood cells based on speckle pattern blurring) in healthy subjects, particularly across different retinal regions and over varying timeframes, is essential. Although prior studies have investigated the intracycle and intraday repeatability of LSCI-based BFVi measurements acquired in quick succession, no research has examined the repeatability and reliability of LSCI measurements across multiple days (“interday”).15–18 Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the intra- and interday repeatability and reliability of BFVi measurements obtained using LSCI in normal subjects.

Methods

Participants

This study (UM IRB 20070492) was approved by the University of Miami Ethics Committee. It adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring participants’ rights, safety, and confidentiality throughout the study. All participants provided written informed consent after being fully apprised of the nature and potential consequences of the study.

Subjects were recruited via telephone outreach and direct in-person approach in community settings. They primarily consisted of healthy volunteers, including hospital staff, patients’ family members, and individuals undergoing annual eye examinations at our institution. All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmologic evaluation, including best-corrected visual acuity, intraocular pressure, and a retinal examination, to confirm eligibility. Subject inclusion criteria were equal to or older than 18 years old and able and willing to give informed consent to join this study. The exclusion criteria included a history of ocular trauma; a history of acute vision loss; a history of stroke; uncontrolled hypertension or diabetes mellitus; a history of diabetic retinopathy or macular degeneration; a history of glaucoma or any other ocular disease (other than cataract); any systemic inflammatory or autoimmune conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, or other serious concomitant medical illness; a history of ocular surgeries in the study eye; severe cataracts; and a refractive error of ±6.0 diopters or more. Because the current XyCAM CRE (Vasoptic Medical, Inc., Columbia, MD) version lacks an automated quality scoring module, image quality was evaluated by an experienced operator using a standardized visual inspection protocol immediately post acquisition. Rejection-level low-quality images were defined by three qualitative criteria: (1) inhomogeneous laser illumination or severe reflection glares, (2) blurred macrovascular boundaries preventing reliable region-of-interest localization, or (3) continuous motion or blink artifacts disrupting frame-stack alignment. Data failing any of these criteria were excluded from subsequent computing.

Clinical Procedure and Image Capture

The best-corrected visual acuity, intraocular pressure, body weight, blood pressure, and height were recorded only on the first visit. Peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) and heart rate (beats per minute) were recorded at each visit. After that, subjects underwent LSCI using the XyCAM CRE (Vasoptic Medical, Inc.). The suffix CRE denotes that the instrument used was an investigational version of the XyCAM RI system cleared by the US Food and Drug Administration because its software was customized for research applications. XyCAM CRE comprises the same core imaging hardware unit as the XyCAM RI, but its software is enhanced to output additional region-specific dynamic blood flow metrics in a guided, semiautomated manner for standardized and reliable analysis. XyCAM CRE imaging was conducted in accordance with the manufacturer's guidelines and performed by a single trained operator (GRG) to minimize interoperator variability. Imaging was performed in a dark room to ensure that the pupil diameter was greater than 4 mm. The subjects rested their heads on the chin-rest accessory, and the operator positioned the device so that the optic nerve head (ONH)/macula was centered in the overall field of view (OFV) and well focused in the live-view window.

Intra- and Interday Repeatability and Reliability

Repeatability and reliability were assessed across two longitudinal paradigms: for intraday assessments, imaging sessions were conducted within a 1-hour interval on the same day to minimize physiological fluctuations and external confounding factors. For interday assessments, subjects were imaged on two separate days within one week. To control for diurnal variation, care was taken that imaging was conducted on the second day at a time not more than 2 hours apart from the time when imaging was conducted on the first day.

Repeatability Assessment Protocol

Data Processing Procedure

Data acquired by XyCAM CRE are stored as arrays of BFVi values at every pixel within the OFV in each of 492 image frames acquired over a 6-second duration. For macula-centered sessions, the analyzed regions included the OFV, the foveal avascular zone (FAZ), and the perifoveal region (PFR), a circular area 500 pixels in diameter excluding the FAZ. Region margins were confirmed or refined by overlaying en face OCT/OCTA images in the software. Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 21.0; SPSS, Inc., Armonk, NY). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the continuous variables before conducting significance testing. For data that follow a normal distribution, a paired samples t-test was used to compare potential differences. Conversely, for data that did not meet the criteria for normality, the Mann–Whitney test was used. To quantify the intra- and interday repeatability and reliability across two imaging sessions while accounting for intereye correlation, linear mixed-effects models were used. In this hierarchical framework, the measurement parameter was defined as the dependent variable, the subject ID was designated as a random effect (intercept) to handle the nested structure of bilateral eyes, and the eye (left vs. right) was controlled as a fixed factor. Variance components were estimated using the restricted maximum-likelihood method. Both the single-measurement and average-measure intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were derived from these variance components, with 95% confidence intervals calculated using the Wald method. General interpretation guidelines classify ICC values as follows: less than 0.5 indicates poor reliability, 0.50 to 0.75 suggests moderate reliability, 0.75 to 0.90 signifies good reliability, and greater than 0.9 denotes excellent reliability. The coefficient of variation (CV) and its 95% confidence intervals were calculated as the square root of the within-subject residual variance divided by the grand mean, providing a standardized and rigorous measure of agreement that enables the direct comparison of repeatability across different parameters without artificially inflating the effective sample size. The relative minimum detectable change (MDC%) was calculated for each specific subregion and blood flow parameter using the formula: MDC%=1.96×2×CV×1-ICC, where the interday CV and its corresponding average measurement ICC were directly coupled. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Following initial screening based on the eligibility criteria, 18 healthy subjects (36 eyes) aged 24 to 59 years were enrolled in the study. However, some exclusions were necessary during the analysis phases. For the intraday scanning analysis, six eyes were excluded due to optic media opacity and tear film instability, which resulted in poor image quality. Additionally, two eyes were excluded due to unacceptable cardiac cycles, and another two eyes because their visiting times fell outside the required intervals. For the interday scanning analysis, a total of 16 eyes were excluded. Specifically, six eyes were excluded due to optic media opacity and tear film instability, resulting in poor image quality. Furthermore, four eyes were excluded due to unacceptable cardiac cycles, and six eyes were excluded because their visiting times were outside the specified intervals. Ultimately, 26 eyes were included in the intraday analysis, and 20 eyes were included in the interday analysis. The baseline demographic information for these participants was summarized in Table 1. No significant differences were found in beats per minute and SpO2 values between the first and second visits for either the macula or the ONH (all P > 0.05).

Table 2 demonstrates the intra- and interday repeatability of the macula and ONH measurements. For the intraday measurements of ONH-centered parameters, the CV for the HRAP BFVi, HRAD BFVi, and HRAM BFVi ranged from 4.8% to 8.5%. The OFV and PFR exhibited comparatively lower CVs, from 8.6% to 9.7%, indicating greater consistency between sessions. The FAZ, however, showed higher fluctuations, with CVs ranging from 11.6% to 15.0%.

The reliability of both the intra- and interday macula and ONH measurements was assessed using single-measure and average-measure ICCs (Table 3). The single-measure ICC reflects the reliability of the ratings from a single rater, while the average-measure ICC indicates the reliability of ratings from multiple raters when averaged, typically yielding higher values. For intraday ONH-centered parameters, the ICCs for the HRAP BFVi, HRAD BFVi, and HRAM BFVi ranged from 0.74 to 0.98. The OFV and TPPR demonstrated higher ICCs, ranging from 0.90 to 0.98, signifying excellent reliability across two sessions. The optic disc region showed moderate reliability, with ICCs ranging from 0.74 to 0.90. For the intraday macula-centered parameters, the ICC for the HRAP BFVi, HRAD BFVi, and HRAM BFVi ranged from 0.93 to 0.99. The OFV, FAZ, and PFR also showed similar ICC values, indicating excellent reliability across sessions. For the interday ONH-centered parameters, the ICCs for the HRAP BFVi, HRAD BFVi, and HRAM BFVi ranged from 0.56 to 0.93. The OFV exhibited higher ICCs (0.87–0.93), suggesting superior reliability. Conversely, the optic disc and TPPR showed moderate reliability with ICCs ranging from 0.56 to 0.81. For the interday macula-centered parameters, the ICCs for the HRAP BFVi, HRAD BFVi, and HRAM BFVi ranged from 0.80 to 0.94. The OFV, FAZ, and PFR demonstrated similar ICCs, indicating good reliability.

For the ONH-centered measurements, the interday MDC% thresholds ranged from 5.8% to 12.3% across all the evaluated subregions. Specifically, the OFV exhibited the tightest tracking bounds (5.8% for the HRAD), while the TPPR presented the upper limit (12.3% for the HRAD). For the macula-centered measurements, the derived interday MDC% ranged between 5.98% and 14.0%. The PFR demonstrated the lowest threshold (5.98% for the HRAP), whereas the FAZ established the highest threshold (14.0% for the HRAD).

Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive analysis of LSCI intra- and interday repeatability and reliability, making a significant contribution to the field. The primary findings demonstrate generally favorable intra- and interday repeatability and reliability for various macula and ONH-centered parameters, which are crucial for evaluating OBF. For the intraday ONH-centered measurements, parameters such as the OFV and TPPR showed strong consistency and excellent reliability across sessions. Similarly, the intraday macula-centered parameters also showed high consistency and excellent reliability. Compared with the intraday parameters, interday parameters generally showed moderate to good repeatability and reliability, highlighting their stability and robust performance across different days.

The observed trend of superior intraday repeatability compared with interday repeatability in this study is consistent with findings across various retinal imaging studies using LSCI and other modalities (Table 5). Several factors may contribute to this observation; although heart rate and SpO2 were not significantly different in this study, exogenous factors such as caffeine intake or strenuous exercise, which can influence systemic blood flow and OBF, were not explicitly monitored or controlled.19,20 This physiological variability is over and above the measurement variability that is characterized by intraday CV. Further, any minor changes in the imaging workflow between days may contribute additional variability. The prior literature where the interday variation was found to be higher than the intraday variation also attributes this finding to biological variability or external influences that are difficult to standardize across different days.21–24

Some acquired data were excluded from the repeatability analysis. In some cases, this was because the timing of the data acquisition did not meet the inclusion criteria for analysis. Other exclusions in the interday analysis (10 eyes) and intraday analysis (8 eyes) were attributed to visibly poor image quality and noisy blood flow pulsations that might render the data unusable. These cases highlight the significant impact of optical media opacity and tear film instability, as is typical for most optical imaging modalities, as well as fixation stability during imaging, emphasizing the need for objective metrics to assess imaging quality during real-time data acquisition.

Many techniques have been used for OBF imaging (Table 5). Compared with LSCI, OCTA has limitations that were mentioned in the introduction.9 Moreover, for retinal blood flow measurement, motion artifacts and angle dependence limit the use of Fourier-domain Doppler OCT, and peripapillary sampling may miss macular flow.10 Laser Doppler velocimetry requires a relatively long measurement time, necessitating precise eye fixation and alignment.11 Laser speckle flowgraphy images, as obtained by another speckle-based OBF imager called laser speckle flowgraphy-NAVI (Softcare Co. Ltd., Fukuoka, Japan), have a smaller field of view with a lower spatial resolution. The slower frame rate (30 frames per second) also makes these images more susceptible to noise artifacts from rapid eye motion.12 Adaptive optics scanning laser ophthalmoscopy has limitations, including restricted penetration depth, the need for pupil dilation and stable fixation, and sensitivity to eye motion and blink artifacts.13 Adaptive optics near-confocal scanning ophthalmoscope is limited to vessels that are less than approximately 4° from the scan axis, is sensitive to saccadic eye motion, and requires stable fixation.14,25 Retinal function imaging is sensitive to motion, pigmentation, and various artifacts, and the acquisition process is slower and can be uncomfortable for patients due to high-intensity flashes.26

The findings of this study suggest that repeatable and reliable measures can indeed be obtained using LSCI, with CVs and ICCs serving as key indicators of measurement performance. A lower CV and a higher ICC indicate less variability in measurements, meaning that the results are more consistent when repeated under the same conditions. For both intra- and interday measurements, the CVs and ICCs for ONH-centered parameters and macula-centered parameters align well with and, in some cases, better than previously reported values in literature (Table 5).10,15–18,23,24,27–30 This result suggests that LSCI can offer a competitive and often superior method for quantitative OBF assessment due to its enhanced robustness against various confounding factors. Moreover, the intraday ICCs for ONH- and macula-centered parameters demonstrate good to excellent reliability. This observation suggests low measurement error, therefore, making it possible to implement appropriate controls (e.g., caffeine intake, medication washout) in longitudinal imaging protocols and achieve reliable comparisons. Bland–Altman analysis visually reinforced this high consistency; across both the intra- and interday intervals, the near-zero mean biases confirmed the absence of systematic drift, while the highly constrained 95% limits of agreement visually indexed a minimal random error floor. Furthermore, the derived interday MDC% thresholds for both the ONH and macular subregions remain well below the scale of the documented changes associated with pathology, implying that the XyCAM CRE is capable of discerning disease-related blood flow changes over day-to-day fluctuations. In patients with insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus without signs of diabetic retinopathy, OBF has been observed to decrease significantly—by approximately 32.5%—as measured by video fluorescein angiography.31 Furthermore, the average choroidal blood flow in the group without diabetic retinopathy was 30.2% lower than in the control group, as measured by laser Doppler flowmetry.32 Patients with glaucoma exhibit a mean optic nerve blood flow that is 24% lower than that of control subjects. More specifically, certain areas, such as the inferior temporal neuroretinal rim, showed an even greater reduction of 28% in blood flow when assessed with laser Doppler flowmetry.33 Given these findings, the established repeatability and reliability of LSCI in this study highlights its potential to deliver trustworthy blood flow assessments in clinical environments for longitudinal studies.

Our analysis reveals interesting variations in repeatability and reliability across different retinal regions. Notably, the FAZ, optic disc, and TPPR exhibit greater interday variability. The BFVi measurements from the FAZ capture estimates of choroidal blood flow since this region is devoid of retinal vasculature. Unlike the autoregulated blood flow in the retina, the choroidal blood flow has greater variability and is more dependent on systemic physiology. Conversely, the optic disc and TPPR areas contain a greater number of larger retinal vessels with a highly pulsatile flow.34 The inclusion of these responsive vessels within the measurement area inherently introduces greater variability in blood flow metrics, as their diameter and flow rates can fluctuate with systemic physiological shifts.35 These observed regional differences in repeatability and reliability underscore the critical importance of considering the specific region when interpreting BFVi measurements, especially in longitudinal studies. Therefore, a nuanced understanding of each region's potential for physiological fluctuation should inform any plan for longitudinal comparisons or biomarker development.

Table 5 displays results of previous studies concerning the repeatability of LSCI. We found that previous research reports define “intrasession” and “intersession” inconsistently, leading to variations in how results are presented. Many studies define intrasession as the difference between multiple cardiac cycles within one imaging session, while intersession refers to the difference between two or more imaging sessions at different times on the same day.15–17 This discrepancy explains why many reported intrasession (should be intracycle) repeatability data for LSCI are around 3%; these results do not represent true intrasession comparisons across different imaging sessions on the same day, but rather are comparisons of data points (cycles) within a single imaging session. Such an approach significantly reduces the impact of heart rate and oxygen saturation data, resulting in greater reported repeatability. Only one study has reported the intra- and interday repeatability of an LSCI imaging platform (customized device).23 However, that study included only eight cases, and the researchers could not perform data analysis for specific clinically relevant areas, such as the optic disc and macula, nor did they adjust for heart rate.23 To avoid reader confusion and standardize the description of intra- and inter- terms in this paper, our study expands the sample size, defining comparisons within the same day as “intraday” and between sessions on different days as "interday," thereby comparing their repeatability and reliability.

Despite its valuable contributions, this study is subject to several limitations that warrant consideration. Certain experimental controls on subjects’ physiology such as restricting caffeine and alcohol intake or comprehensively tracking all potential vasoactive medications before imaging were not implemented. The lack of control over natural flow variability or exogenous factors such as caffeine, alcohol, blood sugar, routine medications, and strenuous exercise limits our ability to isolate the precise causes of interday fluctuations.19,20 Blood pressure and intraocular pressure were not measured during the second visit, nor were patients dilated. These factors may have also contributed to the interday variability.23 Furthermore, our restricted final sample size and notable data attrition reflect the hyperconservative protocol gating required for instrumentation benchmarking. Although exclusions for media opacity and tear film instability represent baseline constraints universal to most optical modalities, this strict logistical filtering inevitably limited the cohort size, potentially limiting the external validity of our findings across broader clinical populations. These limitations suggest that, although the LSCI system shows promise, further improvements in environmental controls and patient management protocols are necessary to enhance confidence in its repeatability and broader clinical applicability.

Conclusions

This observational study reports findings from a thorough examination of the intraday and interday repeatability and reliability of a commercial LSCI-based instrument for blood flow measurement in healthy individuals. Although some data had to be justifiably excluded from the analysis, our results demonstrate a commendable level of consistency across most measured parameters, particularly on the same day. Although the interday measurements exhibited a relatively greater variability, a finding consistent with the broader literature across various imaging modalities, several key parameters maintained good reliability, suggesting potential clinical utility for observational and longitudinal studies.
