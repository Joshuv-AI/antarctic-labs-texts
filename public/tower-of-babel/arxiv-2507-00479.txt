On Mitigating Data Sparsity in Conversational Recommender Systems
Sixiao Zhang, Mingrui Liu, Cheng Long, Wei Yuan, Hongxu Chen, Xiangyu Zhao, Hongzhi Yin

Abstract
Conversational recommender systems (CRSs) capture user preference through textual information in dialogues. However, they suffer from data sparsity on two fronts: the dialogue space is vast and linguistically diverse, while the item space exhibits long-tail and sparse distributions. Existing methods struggle with (1) generalizing to varied dialogue expressions due to underutilization of rich textual cues, and (2) learning informative item representations under severe sparsity. To address these problems, we propose a CRS model named DACRS. It consists of three modules, namely Dialogue Augmentation, Knowledge-Guided Entity Modeling, and Dialogue-Entity Matching. In the Dialogue Augmentation module, we apply a two-stage augmentation pipeline to augment the dialogue context to enrich the data and improve generalizability. In the Knowledge-Guided Entity Modeling, we propose a knowledge graph (KG) based entity substitution and an entity similarity constraint to enhance the expressiveness of entity embeddings. In the Dialogue-Entity Matching module, we fuse the dialogue embedding with the mentioned entity embeddings through a dialogue-guided attention aggregation to acquire user embeddings that contain both the explicit and implicit user preferences. Extensive experiments on two public datasets demonstrate the state-of-the-art performance of DACRS.



1
Introduction

Conversational recommender systems (CRSs) provide an effective and real-time mechanism for capturing user preferences and delivering personalized recommendations. Unlike traditional recommender systems, CRSs allow users to interact through free-form natural language conversations, enabling them to express their needs more flexibly and intuitively. This interaction style offers a rich source of semantic information that, if effectively utilized, can significantly enhance the accuracy and relevance of recommendations. Consequently, a core challenge in CRS development lies in how to best leverage the information embedded within these textual dialogues.

Current research on CRSs broadly follows two main directions. The first is the
LM-as-a-recommender
approach, where language models (LMs) process user dialogues directly to generate recommendations, either through reranking
Gao et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
or next-token generation
Fang et al. 2024
;
Xi et al. 2024
;
He et al. 2024
. However, LMs often lack domain-specific knowledge, making it difficult for them to accurately model user preferences and retrieve relevant items from a large candidate pool. As a result, these systems frequently underperform compared to expert recommender models
Wang et al. 2023
. The second direction focuses on
expert CRS models
, which represent users and items as embeddings and make recommendations based on similarity computations
Zhou et al. 2020a
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Yang et al. 2021
. These models consistently achieve state-of-the-art recommendation performance. The recommendations they generate can then be used to guide LLMs in producing coherent and contextually appropriate dialogue responses, making expert CRS models both more practical and effective in real-world applications.

Despite their advantages, existing approaches to CRSs often overlook the critical issue of data sparsity, which manifests in two forms: dialogue sparsity and item sparsity. On the one hand, dialogue modeling in CRSs faces two key challenges due to dialogue sparsity. First, language is inherently flexible, since identical preferences can be expressed in countless ways through varying phrasing, vocabulary, and structure. With limited training data, models often struggle to generalize to previously unseen dialogue variations
Belinkov and Glass 2019
;
Li et al. 2020
. Second, natural language is largely redundant
Bian et al. 2021
, and many tokens contribute noise rather than useful signal, introducing bias into the learned user embeddings. Most works underutilize the textual richness of user dialogues. They either extract only entities from conversations and discard the remaining text, or compress the entire dialogue into a single user embedding for item retrieval. Such strategies fail to capture the flexibility and nuance of natural language, leading to less expressive user representations.

On the other hand, item sparsity, which includes the long-tail distribution and low user-item interaction density, poses a major obstacle for effective recommendation. Most items receive very few interactions, which can lead to popularity bias, where models disproportionately favor popular items in their recommendations. For example, the user-item interaction density is just 0.0956% in the ReDial dataset and 0.2637% in the Inspired dataset. To mitigate this, many systems
Chen et al. 2019
;
Zhou et al. 2020a
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2024
incorporate a knowledge graph (KG) composed of items and related entities to establish stronger correlations among items. However, these graphs are often underutilized due to limited overlap with the training corpus. For instance, ReDial’s KG includes 31,162 entities, yet only 6,675 of them appear in the dialogue corpus. This disconnect exacerbates the data sparsity issue for items, as most of their KG neighbors are rarely updated during training. Consequently, neighborhood aggregation fails to effectively capture implicit item similarities, limiting the recommender system’s generalization ability.

To this end, we aim to address these problems in this paper. We propose a model named

We adopt a two-stage augmentation pipeline to generate augmented texts by harnessing the superior language understanding and generation ability of LLMs. In the first stage, we rewrite the dialogue through LLM rephrasing and LLM summarization to increase the diversity of the texts and increase the generalizability of the model to unseen dialogues. In the second stage, we apply word-level and utterance-level augmentations to mitigate the noise of redundant language.

•

We address the data sparsity problem in CRSs, which includes dialogue sparsity and item sparsity.

•

We propose DACRS that consists of three modules: Dialogue Augmentation, Knowledge-Guided Entity Modeling, and Dialogue-Entity Matching.

•

Extensive experiments on two datasets show the state-of-the-art performance of our method.

2
Related Works

Existing works on conversational recommender systems could be broadly classified into LM-as-a-recommender models and expert CRS models. We review the two types of works in this section.

2.1
LM-as-a-recommender

Some works adopt LMs as the recommender system by reranking or next-token generation, e.g., GRU
Li et al. 2018
, transformer
Chen et al. 2019
, GPT
Zhou et al. 2020b
.
Gao et al. 2023
proposed Chat-rec. They use all information (dialogue and user profile) to build a prompt and query ChatGPT. If ChatGPT determines that it needs to conduct recommendation, it will retrieve and rerank recommendations from a model and generate the response.
Huang et al. 2023
proposed InteRecAgent. They ask LLMs to generate a tool-calling plan according to the dialogue, and then execute the plan to retrieve and rerank candidate items.
Feng et al. 2023
proposed LLMCRS. Their system includes sub-task detection, model matching, sub-task execution, and response generation, where all of them are executed by prompting LLMs.
Li et al. 2024
proposed ChatCRS. They prompt an LLM to predict the conversation goal and meanwhile retrieve KG neighbors of mentioned entities. Then, the LLM is prompted with both the KG neighbors and the predicted goals to generate the response. If the goal indicates a recommendation goal, the CRS will follow the prompt and make a recommendation based on the KG relations, otherwise it will continue the conversation. These works seamlessly combine LLMs with recommendation to produce fluent responses. However, most of these works struggle to outperform the expert models because LLMs could not effectively capture the task-specific implicit user preference
Wang et al. 2023
.

2.2
Expert CRS Models

Expert CRS models focus on modeling users and items in latent embedding spaces and retrieve the recommendations based on the similarity scores. Several works
Zhou et al. 2020a
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Lin et al. 2023
embed users and items separately and align the two embedding spaces into a whole to compute user-item similarities.
Yang et al. 2021
proposed MESE. They encode item metadata directly as item embeddings. They add special tokens to the GPT model and fine-tune it to learn the conversation paradigm. The recommendation is made by retrieving items similar to the embedding of the REC token.
Yang and Chen 2024
proposed ReFICR. They use a fine-tuned LLM to generate embeddings of items and dialogues by encoding the item metadata and conversation texts. They retrieve similar items and users according to the generated embeddings. Then the LLM is prompted to rerank the items given all the information. These works yield state-of-the-art recommendation performance as they could better capture the implicit user preference. However, they overlook the language diversity and item sparsity and are thus less generalizable under limited data.

3.1
Problem Definition

Each dialogue is conducted by a unique user interacting with the recommender. Let the set of all dialogues be denoted as
\mathcal{U}=\{\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{U}_{N}\}
, where
N
is the number of users. A dialogue
\mathcal{U}_{n}
consists of a sequence of utterances
\mathcal{U}_{n}=\{u_{n}^{1},u_{n}^{2},\ldots,u_{n}^{|\mathcal{U}_{n}|}\}
, where each utterance is produced either by the user or the recommender. Given a set of candidate items
\mathcal{I}=\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{I}\}
, the goal of a CRS is to recommend items that align with the user’s preferences in the recommender’s next utterance
u_{n}^{|\mathcal{U}_{n}|+1}
.

Relying solely on dialogue context is insufficient for accurate recommendations, particularly without incorporating knowledge of candidate items. To address this, we leverage an item-entity knowledge graph (KG) to capture item correlations. For each dialogue
\mathcal{U}_{n}
, we extract the set of mentioned entities
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{e_{n}^{1},e_{n}^{2},\ldots,e_{n}^{|\mathcal{E}_{n}|}\}
, following prior works
Zhou et al. 2020a
;
Wang et al. 2022
. The KG, denoted as
\mathcal{G}
, is constructed using DBpedia
Bizer et al. 2009
, where each node represents an entity (including items), and edges represent typed relations between them. For instance, the triplet {
\textit{Chris Evans}-\textit{starring}-\textit{Captain America}
} indicates a relation in the KG, with nodes connected by the edge
starring
.

With this setup, the CRS task is formalized as:

\mathcal{I}_{n}=f(\mathcal{U}_{n},\mathcal{E}_{n},\mathcal{G})

(1)

where
f
denotes the CRS model, and
\mathcal{I}_{n}=\{i_{n}^{1},i_{n}^{2},\ldots,i_{n}^{k}\}
represents the top-k recommended items for dialogue
\mathcal{U}_{n}
.

3.2
Dialogue Augmentation

This module aims to capture the explicit semantic preference of the user from the dialogue. We propose a two-stage dialogue augmentation pipeline designed to generate semantically consistent variations of dialogues, thereby enriching the training data and enhancing model generalizability. In Stage 1, we obtain an altered text
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\prime}
by employing a large language model (LLM) to rephrase or summarize the dialogue, generating alternate expressions that preserve user intent. In Stage 2, we apply a set of text augmentation techniques on
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\prime}
to further introduce diversity and reduce the impact of redundant tokens to obtain
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\prime\prime}
. Each raw dialogue is sequentially processed through both stages during training, with one augmentation method applied per stage. Note that we also set a probability for each stage to output the original text without augmentation.

3.2.1
Stage 1: LLM-Based Rephrasing & Summarization

Rephrasing.

Given a dialogue, we prompt an LLM (e.g., Llama, ChatGPT) to rephrase the conversation. The prompt used is:

"You are given a dialogue between a user and a recommender system. Here is the dialogue:
{
dialogue
}
. Rephrase the dialogue using as different words and styles as possible. Output ONLY the rephrased content."

The rephrased dialogue differs from the original in vocabulary and expression style, while preserving the user’s underlying preferences and maintaining the interactive format of a conversation. This augmentation leverages the strong generative capabilities of LLMs to create diverse linguistic variations of the same semantic content. As a result, the model is exposed to varied surface forms expressing the same user intent, improving its generalization to unseen dialogue expressions.

Summarization.

Alternatively, we prompt the LLM to summarize the user’s preferences expressed in the dialogue. This produces a concise representation that distills the user’s intent into an informative summary. The summarization prompt is:

"You are given a dialogue between a user and a recommender system. Here is the dialogue:
{
dialogue
}
. Summarize the user’s preference for movie recommendations in a compact and informative manner."

Unlike the rephrased dialogue, the summary does not retain the conversational format. Instead, it generates a direct and structured statement of the user’s preferences using explicit concepts. For instance, the summary “the user prefers sci-fi movies with giant robots” could represent the preferences expressed in the dialogue shown in
Fig. 1
. This variant provides the model with clear and focused signals, helping it attend to core preference information while ignoring irrelevant or noisy dialogue content.

3.2.2
Stage 2: Text Augmentation

In the second stage, we apply both word-level and utterance-level augmentation techniques to the outputs from Stage 1. These augmentations mitigate redundancy in natural language and expose the model to a wider distribution of text patterns. The augmentation methods include:

Word-Level Augmentations

•

Deletion
: Randomly remove a proportion of words.

•

Swapping
: Swap adjacent words to simulate alternate phrasing.

•

Cropping
: Remove a contiguous segment of words.

Utterance-Level Augmentations

•

Deletion
: Remove one or more utterances.

•

Swapping
: Swap the positions of two utterances.

If summarization is used in Stage 1, only word-level augmentations are applied in Stage 2, as the dialogue no longer contains distinct utterances.

These augmentations create diverse inputs that reflect similar user semantic preferences, enabling the model to learn robust, semantically grounded dialogue representations and generalize better to real-world dialogue variability.

3.3
Knowledge-Guided Entity Modeling

This module aims to capture the implicit user preference from the mentioned entities. Similar to conventional recommendation tasks, conversational recommender systems also suffer from long-tail distributions and data sparsity. Most entities and items are mentioned only a few times (or not at all) in the training data. As a result, their embeddings receive minimal or no updates during training, leading to poor generalization and inference accuracy. Knowledge graphs offer a promising solution by introducing additional structural dependencies between items and entities. However, the KGs used in CRS are often highly sparse and redundant. For example, in the ReDial dataset, only 6,675 out of 31,162 entities appear in the training corpus. Consequently, rarely mentioned items tend to be distant from frequently mentioned ones in the KG, which weakens the utility of these connections for learning informative item representations. To address this issue, we propose two complementary components to enhance item representation learning: KG-based entity substitution and an entity similarity constraint.

3.3.1
KG-Based Entity Substitution

We observe that most neighboring entities in the KG are not mentioned in the dialogue data. For instance, in the ReDial KG, the average node degree is 4.84, but only 2.47 of those neighbors are mentioned. Similarly, for the Inspired dataset, the average node degree is 3.33, while only 1.32 are mentioned. To strengthen inter-entity connections during training, we propose a substitution strategy that replaces mentioned entities with their 1-hop neighbors in the KG.

Specifically, we extract the set of mentioned entities for each dialogue
\mathcal{U}_{n}
as
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{e_{n}^{1},e_{n}^{2},...,e_{n}^{|\mathcal{E}_{n}|}\}
. We randomly substitute a subset of these entities with their 1-hop neighbors in the KG. This approach introduces a richer variety of entities into the training process and encourages connected entities to share similar preferences.

3.3.2
Entity Similarity Constraint

While KG-based substitution enriches training signals, it does not guarantee that all entities, especially those multiple hops away, are effectively trained. To further address this, we introduce an entity similarity constraint that encourages neighboring entities in the KG to have similar embeddings. We define it as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{entity},m}=-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{m}}\log\frac{e^{\mathbf{h}_{m}\cdot\mathbf{h}_{j}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{E}|}e^{\mathbf{h}_{m}\cdot\mathbf{h}_{k}}}

(2)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{entity}}=\sum_{m=1}^{|\mathcal{E}|}\mathcal{L}_{\text{entity},m}

(3)

where
\mathcal{N}_{m}
denotes the set of neighbors of entity
m
in the KG,
|\mathcal{E}|
is the total number of entities, and
\mathbf{h}_{m}
is the embedding of entity
m
. This constraint encourages connected entities to be closer in the embedding space than unconnected ones. As a result, even entities that do not appear in the training data can still learn meaningful embeddings through their structural connections in the KG.

3.4
Dialogue-Entity Matching

In this module, we compute personalized recommendations by matching dialogue and entity embeddings. Specifically, we first obtain entity embeddings using a Relational Graph Convolutional Network (RGCN)
Schlichtkrull et al. 2018
and derive dialogue embeddings via a fixed LLM encoder. To represent user preferences, we perform a dialogue-guided attention aggregation on the embeddings of mentioned entities. Recommendations are then generated based on the similarity between user and item embeddings.

3.4.1
Entity Encoding

Following prior works
Zhou et al. 2020a
;
Wang et al. 2022
, we use RGCN
Schlichtkrull et al. 2018
to encode entity and item embeddings from the knowledge graph. The RGCN layer is defined as:

\mathbf{h}_{m}^{(l+1)}=\sigma\left(\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{m}^{r}}\frac{1}{c_{m,r}}\mathbf{W}_{r}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}_{j}^{(l)}+\mathbf{W}_{0}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}_{m}^{(l)}\right)

(4)

where
\sigma
is a non-linear activation function,
\mathcal{R}
is the set of relations in the KG,
\mathcal{N}_{m}^{r}
is the set of neighbors of entity
m
under relation
r
, and
c_{m,r}
is a degree-based normalization constant.
\mathbf{W}_{r}^{(l)}
and
\mathbf{W}_{0}^{(l)}
are learnable weight matrices, and
\mathbf{h}_{m}^{(l)}
is the representation of entity
m
at layer
l
. The initial embeddings
\mathbf{h}^{(0)}
are randomly initialized and optimized during training. For brevity, we omit the layer index in subsequent discussion and denote the final embedding of entity
m
as
\mathbf{h}_{m}
.

3.4.2
User Encoding via Dialogue-Guided Attention Aggregation

After augmenting a user dialogue
\mathcal{U}_{n}
using the dialogue augmentation module, we obtain the modified input
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\prime\prime}
. We then pass
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\prime\prime}
through a fixed LLM to produce a dialogue embedding:

\mathbf{s}_{n}=\text{LLM}(\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\prime\prime})

(5)

The resulting embedding
\mathbf{s}_{n}
captures the user’s explicit semantic preferences. However, general-purpose LLMs lack task-specific knowledge and are unaware of candidate items, limiting their ability to model implicit preferences. To mitigate this, we extract the set of mentioned entities from the dialogue, denoted by
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{e_{n}^{1},e_{n}^{2},\ldots,e_{n}^{|\mathcal{E}_{n}|}\}
, and obtain their embeddings from the RGCN:
\mathbf{H}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}_{n}|\times d}
.

We then apply a dialogue-guided attention to aggregate the entity embeddings under the guidance of the dialogue embedding:

Q_{n}=\textbf{s}_{n}W_{Q};\ K_{n}=\textbf{H}_{n}W_{K};\ V_{n}=\textbf{H}_{n}W_{V}

(6)

\mathbf{h}_{n}=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q_{n}K_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)V_{n}

(7)

Here,
W_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\text{llm}}}
projects the LLM embedding into the same dimension as the entity embeddings, and
W_{K},W_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are learnable key and value projection matrices. We omit the multi-head design for simplicity. The aggregated vector
\mathbf{h}_{n}
captures the user’s implicit preferences by leveraging collaborative signals from semantically related entities.

To form the final user embedding, we combine both explicit and implicit representations:

\mathbf{u}_{n}=\lambda\mathbf{s}_{n}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{h}_{n}

(8)

where
\lambda\in[0,1]
is a learnable weight. This fusion allows the user embedding
\mathbf{u}_{n}
to encode both semantic information from dialogue context and collaborative signals from the mentioned entities, facilitating more informed recommendation decisions.

3.4.3
Inference

Given the user embedding
\mathbf{u}_{n}
and item embedding
\mathbf{h}_{m}
, we compute a similarity score using the dot product:

\text{score}_{n,m}=\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{h}_{m}^{\top}

(9)

For each user, we compute scores for all item entities and rank them accordingly. Only item entities are considered in the recommendation step. The top-k items with the highest scores are recommended to the user.

3.5
Training Objective

To train the model, we minimize the cross-entropy loss between users and their mentioned entities. For user
n
, the loss is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{n}=-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}\text{log}\frac{e^{\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{j}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{E}|}e^{\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{k}}}\\

(10)

where
\mathcal{E}_{n}
is the set of mentioned entities in the dialogue,
|\mathcal{E}|
is the total number of entities, and
\mathbf{e}_{j}
is the embedding of entity
j
. The overall recommendation loss is:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{n}

(11)

where
N
is the total number of users. The final training objective combines the recommendation loss with the entity similarity constraint defined in
Eq. 3
:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\text{entity}}

(12)

where
\alpha
is a hyperparameter adjusting the weight of the entity similarity constraint.

Baselines

We compare DACRS with five CRS baselines:

•

KBRD

Chen et al. 2019
. It builds an entity KG and adds the user mentioned entity embeddings into the attention layer of the dialogue transformer network to generate the response and recommendations through next-token generation.

•

KGSF

Zhou et al. 2020a
. It builds a word KG and an entity KG and align the two latent embedding spaces through mutual information maximization,

•

MESE

Yang et al. 2021
. It obtains item embeddings by encoding item textual metadata. It uses prompt learning for item retrieval.

•

UniCRS

Wang et al. 2022
. It adopts prompt learning to generate embedding of a special prompt token to retrieve items.

•

ReFICR

Yang and Chen 2024
. It encodes user dialogues and item metadata using an LLM and directly retrieve recommendation by computing the embedding similarities between user and item.

RQ2: Ablation Studies

We test how each module contributes to our method in
Fig. 2
. We test four variants:

•

w/o DA: remove the Dialogue Augmentation module and use the vanilla dialogue as input to get dialogue embeddings.

•

w/o KGEM: remove the Knowledge-Guided Entity Modeling module such that the entity embeddings are directly acquired from the RGCN without any augmentations.

•

w/o DGAA: replace the Dialogue-Guided Attention Aggregation module with a dot product between the dialogue embedding and the candidate item embeddings.

•

w/o all: remove all above three components.

•

w/o DE: remove DA and DGAA. Recommendation is made by the dot product between the average embedding of the mentioned entities and the candidate item embeddings.

From
Fig. 2
, we have three key observations. First, all variants underperform DACRS, demonstrating the effectiveness of each component. Second, w/o DE is the only variant that removes the dialogue context, and results in the worst performance over all variants. This suggests that the dialogue context is crucial in accurately modeling user preference. Third, w/o KGEM is the worst among the first three variants that incorporate dialogue context and only remove one of the components. This suggests that the knowledge-guided entity modeling module contributes significantly, indicating the importance of strong item correlations.

5
Conclusion

We address the data sparsity problem including the dialogue sparsity and item sparsity in conversational recommender systems by proposing a model named DACRS. It consists of three components. The Dialogue Augmentation module improves the model’s generalization ability to varying texts, the Knowledge-Guided Entity Modeling module alleviates the item sparsity problem to learn more informative entity embeddings, and the Dialogue-Entity Matching module adopts a dialogue-guided attention network to fuse the dialogue embedding with the entity embeddings. Experiments on two datasets show the state-of-the-art performance of DACRS.

Limitations

One important function of CRSs is to interact with the user via conversation. This includes response generation and question asking. Currently we are not considering the response generation because LLMs are powerful enough to generate a good response by prompting it with the context and the generated recommendations. However, question asking is an interesting direction which is worth exploring, as one of the goals of traditional CRSs is to make recommendations in as few turns as possible. This is a non-trivial task and we leave this for future work.
