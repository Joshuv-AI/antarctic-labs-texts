Abstract
Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is shifting from conversational assistants toward agentic systems—autonomous decision-making systems that sense, decide, and act within operational workflows. This shift creates an autonomy paradox: as GenAI systems are granted greater operational autonomy, they should, by design, embody more formal structure, more explicit constraints, and stronger tail-risk discipline. We argue stochastic generative models can be fragile in operational domains unless paired with mechanisms that provide verifiable feasibility, robustness to distribution shift, and stress testing under high-consequence scenarios. To address this challenge, we develop a conceptual framework for assured autonomy grounded in operations research (OR), built on two complementary approaches. First, flow-based generative models frame generation as deterministic transport characterized by an ordinary differential equation, enabling auditability, constraint-aware generation, and connections to optimal transport, robust optimization, and sequential decision control. Second, operational safety is formulated through an adversarial robustness lens: decision rules are evaluated against worst-case perturbations within uncertainty or ambiguity sets, making unmodeled risks part of the design. This framework clarifies how increasing autonomy shifts OR’s role from solver to guardrail to system architect, with responsibility for control logic, incentive protocols, monitoring regimes, and safety boundaries. These elements define a research agenda for assured autonomy in safety-critical, reliability-sensitive operational domains.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is crossing a practical threshold: it is moving from advice to action. The question for many organizations is no longer whether generative AI (GenAI) can draft text or write code, but whether an agent can operate—place orders, route vehicles, allocate clinical resources, balance power grids, coordinate multi-echelon logistics—under real constraints and real uncertainty. This shift from “chatbot” to “operator” exposes a paradox that should guide the next decade of Operations Research (OR): greater autonomy demands more structure. We call this the autonomy paradox, and it follows from a simple mechanism.

Autonomy delivers speed and scale, yet it also amplifies the cost of small errors, hidden constraint violations, and rare failures. In high-stakes settings, expected performance is a weak guide: low-probability regimes can dominate social cost, and “almost always safe” is not safe enough.

1.1
Operational Risk and the Case for OR

In the field, even highly engineered autonomous systems still exhibit edge cases that trigger recalls, investigations, and public concern. In December 2025, for example, a U.S. regulator disclosed a Waymo recall tied to a software issue that could cause vehicles to pass stopped school buses—a rare, high-consequence scenario
(
37
)
. The lesson generalizes. When AI is embedded in operations, safety depends on engineered discipline: systems that are auditable and monitorable, respect hard constraints, and are stress-tested against tail events
(
69
)
.

At the same time, autonomy is advancing quickly under controlled conditions. A late-2025 lab study shows that multi-agent systems built from frontier generative models can manage the MIT Beer Distribution Game and reduce total costs relative to human teams
(
43
)
. For the same setting, the GenAI Beer Game provides an interactive testbed that pairs a natural-language interface with an OR decision engine
(
42
)
. Taken together, field failures and lab gains point to a single bottleneck: autonomy scales under decision regimes that remain feasible and stable when conditions drift and constraints bind in unanticipated ways.

These requirements define a design problem: build autonomous operators whose behavior stays feasible, monitorable, and stable under distribution shift and interaction. OR has spent decades developing the corresponding structure—explicit constraints and feasible-set reasoning, flow conservation and queueing stability, sequential decision control, and robust planning under uncertainty.

Forged during World War II to orchestrate complex military operations and emerging technologies, OR was born in high-stakes settings and then carried into infrastructure, logistics, and service systems
(
27
,
30
)
. Rising autonomy renews that role. In low-autonomy settings, OR acts mainly as a solver. In medium-autonomy settings, it supplies guardrails—constraints, audits, and risk measures. In high-autonomy settings, OR becomes the architect of the operating regime: control logic, incentive protocols, monitoring rules, and safety boundaries within which fleets of agents act. Scaling autonomy responsibly requires this shift.

1.2
Assured Autonomy as an OR Problem

A defining feature of assured operational autonomy (‘‘assured autonomy’’ for short) is that decisions are sequential, stateful, and coupled over time. Actions taken now reshape future feasibility, risk exposure, and information through delayed and nonlinear dynamics. This distinguishes operational autonomy from one-shot generative tasks and places OR’s tradition of sequential decision-making, control, and stability analysis at the center of the problem.

In such settings, the value of a generative model is not its static realism, but whether the closed-loop system it induces remains feasible, stable, and safe over long horizons.

The autonomy paradox implies two design commitments. First, the generative mechanism that drives actions must be constrainable and auditable—closer to an engineered dynamical system than a black-box sampler. This motivates deterministic, flow-based generators—continuous normalizing flows, flow matching, and probability-flow ordinary differential equation (ODE) formulations—that confine randomness to a source distribution while keeping the transformation dynamics deterministic and governed by an ODE. In these architectures, the flow (the ODE) is the engine of generation, while transformers provide representations, interfaces, and orchestration.

Second, operational safety should be built through adversarial design rather than post hoc filtering. Catastrophic failures in aviation, supply chains, power grids, or hospitals arise in tail regimes and under interaction; a natural abstraction is therefore game-theoretic. A controller chooses a policy to minimize cost while an adversary—representing nature, attackers, or distribution shift—chooses scenarios to maximize loss, linking assured performance to distributionally robust optimization and to one of OR’s historical roots: postwar game theory
(
30
,
57
)
.

Together, these commitments locate OR’s leverage. In modern GenAI, ideas matter when they remove binding bottlenecks. Operational settings expose bottlenecks that current generators often sidestep: feasibility by construction, explicit tail regimes that dominate social cost, and generation coupled to optimization so that “plausible” is defined by decision impact rather than surface similarity.

Assured autonomy is autonomy with guarantees: explicit invariants, tail-risk and shift stress tests, and auditable rules for monitoring, escalation, and deferral. It separates systems that look competent in routine settings from systems that remain safe and effective when conditions become novel, extreme, or adversarial. Our claim is that assured autonomy aligns with core OR principles—explicit constraints, worst-case reasoning, and accountable decision rules. Generative models do not produce operational autonomy on their own. Autonomy must be engineered with structure, constraints, and governance, and that redesign is an OR project.

This article is conceptual and design-oriented. Our aim is to specify what assured autonomy requires, diagnose why current GenAI paradigms fail under hard constraints and tail risk, and show how OR fills the gap. We synthesize recent work into an OR-powered integration stack—deterministic, transport-style generation; minimax stress testing; optimization and control; and continuous monitoring with fallback—that serves as both blueprint and research agenda. After diagnosing the limitations of current GenAI (

2
Why Current GenAI Is Insufficient for Assured Autonomy

GenAI has made rapid gains in producing fluent text and realistic media, and it can look compelling in controlled demonstrations. Operational autonomy, however, is judged by a different metric than plausibility. Most GenAI models are trained to generate high-probability continuations of observed patterns, whereas operations require actions that satisfy hard constraints, remain stable under feedback and delay, and perform acceptably under distribution shift—especially in rare, correlated regimes that dominate social cost. The failures are
structural
: imitation-based training does not, on its own, enforce feasibility, stability, or tail robustness. This mismatch becomes sharp along two dimensions—the degree of non-determinism in the decision engine and the safety-criticality of the domain. When both are high, residual stochastic error is no longer a tolerable nuisance; it becomes a systematic source of tail risk. Writing about weapon control, Cummings argues “GenAI is simply too dangerous to include in safety-critical systems”
(
19
)
. The broader point is not limited to weapons: when rare failure modes cannot be modeled, bounded, and monitored, delegating control to a stochastic generator increases exposure to catastrophic outcomes.

Why do these limitations persist? We begin by explaining how stochastic generation complicates certification in safety-critical settings, using LLMs and diffusion models as exemplars. We then distinguish semantic constraints from the structural constraints that define operational feasibility over time. Finally, we discuss tail risk, distribution shift, and accountability—the regimes in which average-case performance is least informative and post hoc diagnosis is essential.

2.1
Stochastic Generators and Safety-Critical Control

LLMs make the gap concrete. An LLM produces the most plausible continuation of a prompt given its training distribution and context. For drafting, summarizing, and brainstorming, this works well. For allocating scarce resources, scheduling tightly coupled activities, or enforcing safety rules that admit no exceptions, it becomes a liability. Correctness here means satisfying feasibility and safety conditions, not producing a reasonable narrative. Reflecting this concern, experts have argued GenAI should be prohibited “to control, direct, guide or govern any weapon” until hallucinations can be modeled and predicted, precisely because the technology
as commonly deployed remains difficult to certify and bound
in environments where failures can cost lives
(
19
)
. Where the acceptable failure rate is effectively zero, plausibility does not substitute for assurance.

Diffusion models
(
15
,
63
,
61
)
illustrate a related structural misalignment. In their standard form, diffusion deliberately destroys structure by adding noise and then reconstructs it via a stochastic reverse-time process
(
7
)
. This paradigm fits images and text, where intermediate states need not be meaningful. Operational systems are different: feasibility, conservation laws, stability conditions, and safety envelopes are invariants that should hold along an entire trajectory, not just at the endpoint. A generator that wanders stochastically and is “corrected” after the fact is hard to certify
without additional architectural constraints
, and it tends to underrepresent tails unless training and evaluation are explicitly redesigned to emphasize the rare regimes that dominate social cost.

2.2
Semantic Versus Structural Constraints

It is tempting to impose constraints on top of these models through prompting, penalty terms, or post hoc filtering. But this strategy is brittle: prompts are not enforceable constraints, and soft penalties are not hard feasibility. Even when an external checker is available, the generator becomes a proposal mechanism whose outputs must be governed by a separate decision regime. Recent work on constrained learning for diffusion models is therefore best read as both progress and diagnosis: Lagrangian-style training can yield meaningful satisfaction guarantees for certain constraint classes
(
36
)
, yet the constraints emphasized in much of that literature are often semantic or preference-based rather than the conservation, capacity, integrality, and stability constraints that define OR problems.

A useful way to clarify the gap is to distinguish
semantic
from
structural
constraints. Semantic constraints regulate
what
is generated (attributes, labels, preferences, and fairness criteria). Structural constraints govern system feasibility as a dynamical object (e.g., flow conservation, capacity limits, integrality, stability, and feasibility over time) and should hold globally throughout execution. When structural constraints are violated, the result is not a lower-quality output but an infeasible or difficult-to-certify action. This is why assured autonomy cannot rest on semantic alignment alone when generative models are embedded in decision loops.

2.3
Tail Risk, Distribution Shift, and Accountability

The deeper problem is tail risk and distribution shift. Operational safety is rarely about being right on average. It is about not failing when the system is stressed: when demand surges, disruptions are correlated, sensors degrade, or strategic interaction produces congestion and cascades. Standard GenAI objectives do not target these regimes; they reward typical-case fidelity and tend to smooth away precisely the rare structures that robust planning should confront. OR frameworks, by contrast, have long emphasized tail events
(
11
)
, asking which deviations matter and then encoding them through chance constraints, ambiguity sets, or worst-case objectives.

Operations also require accountability and diagnosis. High-reliability practice depends on learning from near misses and tracing failures back to their mechanisms
(
69
)
. Black-box generators make this difficult: when performance degrades, it is often unclear whether the cause is data drift, prompt drift, hidden constraint violations, or a failure mode absent from the training distribution. Without an auditable structure, governance becomes reactive and fragile.

Off-the-shelf GenAI is not built to enforce trajectory-level feasibility, to represent and control tail regimes that dominate social cost, or to support the accountability required in high-reliability operations. These gaps explain why current GenAI remains unreliable as an autonomous operator in safety-critical, constraint-driven settings. Flow-Based Generative Models: Structured Evolution in Probability Space

We introduce flow-based generative models as a design pattern for auditable, constrainable generation—not as a claim that any particular method is empirically superior.
Flow-based generative models construct complex distributions by transporting probability mass through a sequence of maps, from discrete normalizing flows to continuous-time formulations based on neural ODEs and related transport-based variants
(
22
,
53
,
1
,
16
,
41
,
31
,
62
,
73
)
. The transport formulation is implementation-agnostic: in low-dimensional or structured settings, maps (or velocity fields) can be affine or piecewise-affine, triangular (Knothe–Rosenblatt), kernel-based, or computed via optimal transport solvers when geometry is explicit
(
48
)
; neural parameterizations matter most when high-dimensional heterogeneity makes expressivity the bottleneck. Viewed through an OR lens, flow-based generation is an iterative algorithm in probability space
(
72
)
, which makes constraints and risk functionals natural to impose at the level of distributional evolution. Accordingly, we proceed in three steps: we first formalize continuous-time distributional dynamics; we then show how deterministic transport yields structure-by-construction and auditability (in contrast to stochastic sampling); and we close by explaining how this interface supports constraint- and tail-aware scenario generation for decision-making.

3.1
Continuous-Time Dynamics and Distributional Evolution

In the continuous-time setting, let
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the state of a system at time
t\in[0,T]
, with initial condition

X_{0}\sim\rho_{0}=P,

where
P
denotes the data or scenario distribution of interest. A flow-based model specifies a time-dependent velocity field

v_{\phi}(x,t):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},

parameterized by
\phi
, and the system evolves according to the ordinary differential equation

\frac{dX_{t}}{dt}=v_{\phi}(X_{t},t),\quad t\in[0,T].

(1)

As particles evolve under this deterministic dynamics, the induced probability distribution evolves continuously over time. Let
\rho_{t}(x)
denote the probability density of
X_{t}
. Its evolution is governed by the continuity equation

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(x)+\nabla\cdot\big(\rho_{t}(x)\,v_{\phi}(x,t)\big)=0,

(2)

which expresses conservation of probability mass along the flow.

Under mild regularity conditions, the dynamics in
eq.

1
are deterministic and invertible, yielding an explicit transport between distributions. In practice, the velocity field
v_{\phi}
is represented by a neural network—often using residual or time-incremental architectures—and learned via variational objectives in probability space or flow-matching formulations. While Gaussian reference distributions are common, the framework is not restricted to this choice: the terminal distribution
Q
may also represent structured or adversarial targets, such as least-favorable distributions arising in distributionally robust optimization
(
74
)
.

Depending on context, the same transport admits two complementary interpretations. In generative use, one draws samples from a simple reference distribution
Q
and pushes them forward through the flow to obtain samples from a complex target distribution
P
. In analytical or decision-oriented settings, the flow may instead be used to push data or scenarios from reference measure
P
(e.g., observed from data) toward a worst-case distribution
Q
. Both viewpoints rely on the same underlying transport structure and differ only in how the learned dynamics are traversed. This perspective connects to Wasserstein gradient-flow structure and JKO-type constructions
(
4
,
17
,
34
)
, providing a variational view of training and stability closely aligned with OR’s optimization foundations.

From an OR perspective,
eqs.

1
and
2
make a central duality explicit. Constraints can be imposed at the level of probability measures—for example, by requiring
\rho_{t}
to concentrate mass on a feasible set or to control tail risk—or at the level of individual sample paths, by designing the velocity field
v_{\phi}(\cdot,t)
so that trajectories preserve invariants or remain within a feasible region. Once the velocity field is fixed, both particle paths and the induced distributional evolution are determined, providing a direct route to feasibility by construction.

Diffusion-based generators can also incorporate constraints; in practice, they are typically enforced through stochastic sampling with guidance, projection, or correction steps applied during generation. These mechanisms work well in many applications, but they make hard sample-wise certification and traceability less straightforward.
fig.

2
illustrates this contrast: Deterministic-by-design transport makes auditability, replayability, and enforcement interfaces intrinsic; these features matter disproportionately in safety-critical operational loops.

Red curves denote a constrained feasible region. Left: flow-based models transport samples deterministically from a reference distribution

, allowing constraints to be incorporated directly into the transformation. Right: diffusion-based models generate samples through iterative stochastic refinement, with randomness injected at each step and constraints typically enforced through guidance or correction mechanisms.

3.2
Deterministic Transport and Structure-by-Construction

Modern flow-based generative modeling can be understood as learning transport maps between probability distributions. Rather than treating uncertainty as a static input, this perspective models system evolution as a structured transformation of distributions governed by invariants, feasibility conditions, and stability requirements. In OR, many constraints are not preferences but identities—such as flow conservation, capacity limits, nonnegativity, balance conditions, integrality, or temporal ordering—that should hold globally and often throughout execution. A transport-based view naturally accommodates these requirements: probability distributions evolve under dynamics that can, in principle, be designed to preserve structure by construction, rather than enforced post hoc through rejection, penalties, or repair. Generation thus becomes a controlled process governed by structure, rather than a black-box sampling procedure aimed primarily at reproducing observed data.

Diffusion and score-based models can also be interpreted within this transport framework, but they typically realize it through stochastic dynamics. In practice, diffusion models generate samples by simulating noisy reverse-time processes
(
32
,
63
)
, and large language models similarly introduce randomness at each token generation step
(
14
)
. Stochasticity enables diversity in creative domains. In operations, it becomes a liability: inconsistent outputs, intermittent feasibility violations, and failure modes that resist diagnosis. While diffusion models admit a deterministic sampling formulation via the probability-flow ordinary differential equation under idealized conditions
(
63
,
61
)
, their predominant formulations and practical usage emphasize stochastic sampling, with determinism playing a secondary role rather than a primary design objective.

Flow-based generative models adopt a different architectural stance that aligns naturally with the transport-map view. Randomness is confined to the initial draw; conditional on this draw, generation proceeds through deterministic dynamics governed by an ordinary differential equation. This design enables exact traceability and replayability: an output can be traced back to a specific initial state, its atypicality under the known distribution quantified, and the trajectory replayed exactly for auditing and diagnosis. From an operational perspective, the deterministic transport map itself becomes the central object of assured autonomy, exposing the control surfaces required for feasibility enforcement, monitoring, and governance. Recent developments such as flow matching, consistency models, and mean-flow formulations further decouple transport from density estimation, alleviating traditional computational concerns while preserving the determinism and controllability that make this paradigm well-suited for safety-critical decision-making
(
31
,
41
,
62
)
.

Operational deployments rarely require training from scratch. A practical alternative is to start from a pretrained generative model (or a pretrained representation model) and adapt it to the operational domain via fine-tuning or lightweight updates—such as conditioning layers, adapters, or low-rank parameterizations
(
33
)
—using limited in-domain data and operational objectives. In the transport-map view, this corresponds to warm-starting the velocity field (or transport map) and then refining it to encode domain constraints, tail-sensitive regimes, and decision-coupled loss signals, reducing data and compute requirements while improving reliability. This adaptation stage is also where OR enters: constraint penalties, risk functionals, and decision-layer feedback can be imposed during fine-tuning rather than deferred to inference-time correction.

4
Game-Theoretic Safety and Robust Autonomy

Constrainability is not safety. A generator that faithfully reproduces the training distribution still fails when demand spikes, sensors degrade, or an adversary probes for weaknesses. Assured autonomy requires not just structure, but stress testing—systematic confrontation with futures that have not yet occurred. OR formalizes this as a game: the decision-maker chooses a policy; an adversary chooses the scenario that breaks it. Operational autonomy is, in the end, a confrontation with surprise. Models improve, sensors proliferate, and data pipelines thicken, yet the world continues to produce combinations of events that sit outside yesterday’s training distribution. Operationally, if an autonomous system is judged by what happens when things go wrong, then safety should be designed for those regimes, not appended as an afterthought. OR has a name for this stance. It is robustness, formalized as a game between a decision-maker and an adversary representing nature, strategic opponents, or a distribution shift.
fig.

3
previews this logic: the designer chooses a policy, an adversary selects a worst-case shift within a credible ambiguity set, and safety is the resulting equilibrium rather than a post-hoc patch.

A baseline coupling between generative models and decisions is given by the stochastic optimization problem

\min_{\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\big[C(\theta,x)\big],

(3)

where
\theta
denotes a decision or controller,
x
represents realized disturbances and inputs,
C(\theta,x)
is the induced operational loss, and
P
is a nominal distribution learned or estimated from data. This formulation captures the classical “average-case” objective but leaves systems exposed to rare, correlated, or unmodeled regimes that dominate operational risk.

A canonical robust representation is the minimax formulation in the form of the distributionally robust optimization (DRO)
(
12
,
28
,
38
,
54
,
55
,
68
, see, e.g.,)
:

\min_{\theta}\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\!\left[C(\theta,x)\right],

(4)

where
\mathcal{P}
is an ambiguity set of distributions regarded as plausible. The designer chooses
\theta
; the adversary chooses
P
; the resulting equilibrium trades off performance against vulnerability. Put differently,
eq.

4
makes “what could go wrong?” operational: specify how the environment may shift, then optimize against the least favorable case.

In the GenAI setting, related minimax and worst-case formulations have emerged in recent work on high-dimensional problems
(
18
,
74
)
. These developments reflect a growing recognition that flow-based generative models can represent and learn complex high-dimensional worst-case distributions, enabling direct sample (scenario) generation from such adversarial models.

4.1
Distribution-Level Constraints and Sample-Level Enforcement

An abstraction for
operational
constraint control is to distinguish between two classes of constraints.

Semantic constraints
regulate output attributes or preferences, while
structural constraints
govern the feasibility of the system as a dynamical object, such as flow conservation, capacity limits, integrality, or stability, and should hold globally throughout execution.
In OR, such structural constraints are not preferences but non-negotiable identities.

For generative models used in decision-making, this distinction motivates imposing constraints directly on the
generated scenarios
, and hence on the induced scenario distribution.
Let
x\in\mathcal{X}
denote an operational scenario (e.g., demand trajectories, lead times, outages), drawn from a scenario distribution
P
, and let
h_{k}(x)\leq 0
encode structural feasibility requirements.
When generation is coupled to downstream decisions, a natural objective is

\mathcal{J}(P)=\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}[C(\theta,x)],

which links constrained scenario generation directly to the minimax formulation in
eq.

4
.

As an illustration, a canonical formulation is to select (or learn) a distribution
P
within an admissible model class
\mathcal{P}
such that feasibility holds with high probability:

\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\ \mathcal{J}(P)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{P}_{x\sim P}\!\big(h_{k}(x)\leq 0\big)\geq 1-\varepsilon_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,K.

(5)

More generally, feasibility can be enforced through risk-sensitive violation functionals,

\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\ \mathcal{J}(P)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\!\big[\psi_{k}(h_{k}(x))\big]\leq\delta_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,K,

(6)

where
\psi_{k}(\cdot)
encodes tail-weighted or risk-sensitive penalties, such as hinge losses or CVaR-type functions.
Introducing Lagrange multipliers yields a relaxation over
distribution-level
constraint violations,

\min_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\ \max_{\lambda_{k}\geq 0}~~-\mathcal{J}(P)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k}\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\!\big[\psi_{k}(h_{k}(x))\big]-\delta_{k}\right),

(7)

which highlights an OR-specific distinction: the constraints
h_{k}
represent
global structural feasibility
, not semantic attributes, and feasibility is enforced at the level of generated operational scenarios rather than individual model parameters.

Flow-based generators have a practical advantage here: distribution-level requirements can be realized at the level of individual samples. Because flow models implement generation as an explicit, deterministic transport map, constraints expressed pointwise in scenario space—such as upper bounds, conservation laws, or state-dependent safety conditions—can be enforced, monitored, or penalized directly along generated trajectories. In this sense, constraints on the induced distribution can often be implemented by shaping the transport dynamics itself. This sample–distribution duality is particularly natural in OR settings, where feasibility is defined pointwise rather than abstractly over model parameters, and it aligns closely with the foundations of stochastic programming.

Comparable constraint control is also possible in diffusion-based generators, but it is more commonly implemented through stochastic dynamics with guidance, projection, or correction mechanisms applied during sampling. While effective in many settings, such approaches can make hard sample-wise guarantees and traceability more delicate. By contrast, the explicit transport structure of flow-based models makes constraint handling more transparent and directly compatible with OR formulations of feasibility and risk.

4.2
Minimax Stress Testing and Least-Favorable Distributions

Minimax stress testing treats reliability as performance against an adversary. The idea traces to von Neumann’s zero-sum games
(
67
)
and enters OR through robust optimization and robust control, which convert worst-case logic into tractable prescriptions under structured uncertainty
(
10
)
. Distributionally robust optimization (DRO) sharpens the same stance by placing ambiguity directly on the data-generating process, often through moment sets,
\phi
-divergences, or Wasserstein neighborhoods
(
52
)
. This tradition is not a slogan. It provides a calibration problem: which stress classes are credible, and how much probability mass should represent misspecification?

Two implications are central for assured autonomy. First, robustness targets more than overt attacks. Operational disasters concentrate in rare, correlated, and cascading regimes that dominate social cost. High-reliability organizations institutionalize this stance through a sustained “preoccupation with failure”
(
69
)
. Grid operators formalize it with contingency standards; aviation relies on certification culture; hospitals operationalize it through escalation protocols. Across domains, expected performance is a weak standard. The operational standard is avoidance of catastrophic modes within defined stress classes.

Second, minimax reasoning becomes engineering only when the adversary is computable. As
fig.

3
suggests, worst-case design requires a representation that can be searched, not merely invoked. Deterministic generators supply such a representation. When uncertainty is modeled as a transport map or flow that pushes forward a reference measure into operational scenarios, the adversary in
eq.

4
can be parameterized by that map. The problem becomes worst-case
generation
: the adversary explores a continuous family of distributions in
\mathcal{P}
rather than a small menu of hand-built stress tests. Recent work characterizing least-favorable distributions in Wasserstein space as pushforwards of transport maps provides this bridge
(
18
)
. In data-scarce regimes, the generator need only represent the ambiguity set with structure and control; fidelity to the true process is secondary. This yields decision-coupled stress testing: the generator searches for failures that are damaging to the deployed policy
\theta
, rather than generic extremes.

4.3
Interaction, Governance, and Operational Escalation

Robust autonomy also has an interaction dimension. Many operational settings are not single-controller problems but systems of agents—vehicles negotiating right-of-way, inventory nodes coordinating replenishment, or software agents bidding in markets. In these environments, safety depends not only on worst-case uncertainty but also on incentives, equilibrium selection, and protocol design. OR’s game-theoretic toolkit is central: design communication and commitment rules that prevent deadlock, align local objectives with system goals, and restrict admissible behaviors to rule out pathological equilibria. The aim is to reduce the strategic degrees of freedom through which interaction can produce systemic harm.

Robustness without governance is incomplete. Deployment is not solving
eq.

4
once; drift, nonstationarity, and updates make control ongoing. Assured autonomy therefore needs an explicit monitoring-and-deference policy: what is measured, what counts as “out of control,” and what action follows. Here OR’s sequential decision and monitoring traditions apply directly: escalation is itself a control policy, and fallback is a designed operating regime rather than an improvised human response.

5
From Solver to System Architect: The Evolving Role of OR in Assured Autonomy

OR began by solving well-posed decision problems. Humans supplied judgment and carried out the plan. With partial automation, OR shifted toward assurance: it checked feasibility, bounded actions, and audited recommendations from heuristics or learning systems. As operational autonomy rises, that separation no longer holds. OR must design the decision regime itself—the rules, constraints, monitoring triggers, escalation policies, and coordination protocols that govern how agents act and interact.

5.1
Designing Decision Regimes for Autonomous Operations

In fully autonomous settings—a dark factory, an autonomous supply chain, or a smart city where signals and vehicles negotiate flows in real time—the central OR task is constitutional. OR specifies the regime: admissible actions, objectives, inviolable constraints, information rights, and procedures for resolving conflicts. The object of design is no longer an instance. It is the system that produces instances and acts on them.

We already see this shift in power systems. Operators once used optimal power flow to recommend adjustments that humans executed. As renewables and fast-acting grid-edge devices proliferate, control becomes automated and the OR emphasis moves to rules and limits: reserve requirements, frequency-response obligations, and operating envelopes that maintain reliability. OR practitioners write the “grid code” autonomous controllers must satisfy.

A parallel shift appears in highly automated fulfillment. In robotized warehouses, managers do not route individual robots. OR defines the operating regime: zoning and staging policies, priority rules, congestion management, and conflict-resolution logic that prevents deadlock while preserving throughput. Optimization remains, but at a higher level: it shapes protocols rather than selecting each move.

OR fits this role because architecture is choice under constraints. Autonomous systems need rules that are efficient and robust, and that align local actions with system objectives. Mechanism design offers a useful analogy: choose the rules so that decentralized behavior yields acceptable outcomes. In autonomy, the “players” are software modules. The designer decides what each module controls, what it observes, how conflicts are resolved, and what coupling prevents local goals from working at cross purposes.

5.2
Monitoring, Escalation, and Fallback

A second design task is fallback. Every autonomous system meets regimes outside its validated envelope. Assured autonomy requires explicit fallback rules: when monitoring signals indicate out-of-control behavior, the system contracts its action space, reverts to conservative policies, or defers to humans when available. Aviation provides the template: automation disengages when sensor inconsistencies exceed what the system can safely reconcile. The OR contribution is to define the triggers and to optimize fallback behavior so safety is preserved while residual performance is retained.

The legislator’s role also includes compliance. External requirements—regulation, safety standards, organizational policy—must be translated into executable constraints. In domains such as hiring or credit, fairness requirements are increasingly expressed as mathematical constraints that discipline learning and optimization. The same logic applies to high-stakes autonomy: compliance is achieved by design when constraints and auditability are built into the regime.

This elevation in OR’s role leads to meta-level optimization. The upper-level problem is to choose objectives, loss functions, information structures, and coordination protocols. The lower-level outcome is the behavior induced by learning and interaction. In effect, it is a bi-level problem: choose the rules so the resulting equilibrium is stable and aligned.

5.3
OR Inside the GenAI Stack: Inference and Resource Scheduling

The interaction between OR and GenAI is bidirectional. OR supplies structure and assurance when generative models enter operational loops, and OR also improves the GenAI stack itself. Inference scheduling, memory and compute allocation, and latency–throughput tradeoffs in LLM serving are queueing and scheduling problems. Supply Chains

Supply chains are a clean laboratory for the autonomy paradox: locally sensible actions, taken under delay and uncertainty, can generate globally unstable dynamics. 6.2
Mobility and Aviation

Transportation autonomy is often framed as a perception breakthrough. In practice, the binding constraints are coordination and regulation. The standard is compliance with bright-line safety rules in environments where other users behave strategically, unpredictably, or simply incorrectly. When an autonomous vehicle mishandles a stopped school bus, regulators treat it as a rule violation, not a marginal performance miss—hence recalls and scrutiny
(
37
)
. When robotaxi programs stumble, enforcement turns on incident response, reporting, and verifiable risk controls
(
46
)
.

These features sharpen the autonomy paradox. Mobility agents act in shared space. If the generative core is stochastic—for example, an iterative denoising procedure—it is hard to certify that separation, stopping, and right-of-way constraints hold along the entire trajectory, rather than only on average or after repair. Flow-based generation shifts the burden from cleanup to design. When trajectories arise from deterministic dynamics with a constraint-aware vector field, feasibility can be made an invariant of the motion. This is OR at its comparative advantage: specify admissible dynamics instead of projecting infeasible samples back into the feasible set.

Aviation shows what assured autonomy looks like inside a mature safety regime. The U.S. National Airspace System already relies on decision support for time-based traffic management that schedules and meters flows through constrained airspace
(
25
)
. The underlying logic is OR by construction: demand–capacity balancing, network constraints, and scheduling with separation requirements. In such a regime, generative or agentic components must remain subordinate to certified invariants. Transformers can help with intent inference, coordination messages, and explanations. The trajectory engine and the supervisory layer that guarantees separation must be auditable, stress-testable, and compatible with certification practice.

The minimax layer is equally concrete. The adversary is the coupled process that creates conflict: weather that restricts airspace, surveillance or communication degradation, and interaction patterns that produce dense encounters. An ambiguity set can bound plausible joint shifts in weather, demand, and sensing error. Transport-based generators offer a tractable representation of such shifts, and minimax optimization supplies the forcing function: a controller that remains safe against least-favorable distributions inside the ambiguity set supports a stronger safety case.

The key distinction from supply chains is timescale and certification. Mobility and aviation run at machine speed (milliseconds to minutes) under tight regulatory norms. Monitoring must therefore be continuous, fast, and action-guiding. In mobility, leading indicators include near-miss rates, envelope violations, and repeated rule conflicts; in aviation, they include loss-of-separation precursors and sustained overload in constrained sectors. Escalation should be explicit: tighten the admissible envelope, revert to conservative policies, or hand off when signals indicate loss of control.

6.3
Healthcare Operations

Healthcare is where the autonomy debate most often blurs categories. “Workflow autonomy”—drafting notes, coding visits, summarizing encounters—differs from “clinical autonomy,” where errors can harm patients. The early gains from GenAI reflect this: ambient scribes can reduce documentation burden and burnout
(
20
)
, while even documentation automation raises governance questions about consent and trust
(
39
)
. The lesson is already visible at low stakes: deployment succeeds only with design, monitoring, and accountability.

Assurance becomes decisive when systems touch operations: emergency-department triage, inpatient bed assignment, and operating-room scheduling. Here the invariants are clinical and operational at once: avoid catastrophic misses, prevent unsafe delays, and preserve stability during surges. Tail risk dominates because capacity is finite. Small shifts in arrivals or acuity can push the system past a threshold where queues grow rapidly and errors propagate across units. In this setting, “AI checks AI” is a weak safeguard. A second model often shares the same data and blind spots, and it does not encode hospital constraints. Assurance belongs outside the language model: statistical monitoring for drift in calibration and case mix, coupled with OR decision logic that tightens admissible actions, triggers review, or reverts to conservative policies when signals indicate loss of control.

Flow-based generative models fit this assurance layer because they generate the right object: structured stress tests rather than text. Hospitals need plausible surge paths, correlated resource shortfalls, and patient-flow trajectories that respect capacity, conservation, and time ordering. Transport-based generators can be trained to produce such trajectories while preserving feasibility by construction. A minimax formulation then turns stress testing into a design principle: an adversary searches within an ambiguity set for arrival, acuity, and service-time distributions that strain the system most, and the decision maker chooses staffing, bed allocation, and triage thresholds that remain safe under least-favorable conditions. “Plan for rare disasters” becomes an operational requirement.

The domain implication is selective autonomy. Administrative work and low-risk allocations can be automated; high-risk decisions require hard boundaries. Those boundaries should take the form of formal deferral rules, triggered by monitoring statistics and calibrated to the cost of false reassurance. Assured autonomy in healthcare protects clinical attention for the cases where autonomy is least appropriate.

6.4
Power Grids

If any sector already lives under the logic of assured autonomy, it is the power grid. Operators do not optimize “on average.” They operate under explicit invariants: supply equals demand, flows respect thermal and voltage limits, and the system remains stable under credible contingencies. These requirements are codified in reliability standards that compel performance under defined classes of adverse events, including sequential contingencies and stability constraints
(
47
)
. Regional planning turns this mandate into routine practice through contingency analysis and reinforcement rules; for example, N-1-1 studies apply a first contingency, allow prescribed corrective actions, and then impose a second contingency, with violations triggering upgrades
(
49
)
. This is minimax reasoning embedded in institutions: plan for adverse regimes rather than typical days.

Agentic AI changes the surface of grid operations but not the governing principle. Forecasting, anomaly detection, and remedial-action recommendation can become more autonomous, increasing speed and complexity in a coupled cyber-physical system. The autonomy paradox follows: faster control demands a decision regime that is deterministic, certifiable, and constraint-preserving by construction. This is why flow-based generative modeling fits the grid. Power systems are organized around flows and dynamics; uncertain exogenous inputs—renewable ramps, weather-driven demand, correlated outages—can be represented as a deterministic transport map from a latent source to physically plausible scenario distributions that respect domain structure.

The minimax layer then becomes concrete. The adversary is nature and correlation: extreme weather, common-mode failures, and sequences of contingencies that push the system toward cascading collapse. An ambiguity set can encode plausible distribution shift, for instance via Wasserstein neighborhoods around empirical distributions of weather and renewable generation. The planner chooses reserves, dispatch, and corrective actions that perform well against least-favorable distributions, consistent with the reliability paradigm. Recent work on worst-case generation in Wasserstein space is relevant because it constructs least-favorable distributions as pushforwards of a transport map, rather than relying on a finite catalog of hand-crafted scenarios
(
18
)
.

The grid also clarifies a governance lesson: monitoring and fallback are part of the design. Power systems already have telemetry, alarms, and protection logic. Assured autonomy therefore means aligning AI components with that discipline. Recommendations should be filtered through security constraints; deviations detected using established stability and adequacy metrics; and fallback made explicit, from conservative redispatch to automated protection and, in extremis, controlled load shedding. The point is not to replace the reliability-first regime, but to make agentic AI compatible with it: transformers can improve interface and explanation, while deterministic flows and explicit optimization preserve the invariants the grid cannot compromise.

Across the four domains, the lesson is the same. Autonomy fails when operations demand invariants, tail-risk discipline, and coordination that stochastic generation and after-the-fact checks do not supply. Assured autonomy rests on three choices: a deterministic, auditable generator; minimax stress tests that search for catastrophic regimes; and monitoring with clear escalation and fallback rules. The binding constraint differs by domain—delays and feedback in supply chains, certified interaction in mobility and aviation, workflow propagation in healthcare, and codified reliability under correlated contingencies in power grids. The agenda is to move assurance upstream: invariants in the model, worst cases in the loop, escalation in the system.

7
Research Agenda

Assured autonomy requires treating autonomous systems as engineered objects: feasible by construction, robust to distribution shift and tail events, and governable over their lifecycle. Four priorities follow: (i) feasibility by construction at the learning–optimization interface; (ii) minimax safety, worst-case generation, and verification; (iii) monitoring, handoffs, and lifecycle governance; and (iv) public goods that make progress cumulative.

7.1
Feasibility by Construction: Learning Meets Optimization

Autonomy collapses the traditional separation between learning and optimization: in many operational settings, the model is not merely an input to a solver; the solver is part of the model. The priority is making feasibility and invariants native to learning, not imposed afterward. One route embeds optimization primitives within learning pipelines via differentiable optimization layers, enabling end-to-end training with explicit constraints

Progress is testable with operational metrics—constraint-violation rates, long-horizon stability, and stress-regime performance—rather than prediction error or episodic reward alone.

7.3
Monitoring, Handoffs, and Lifecycle Governance

Even well-designed autonomous systems drift: data distributions change, incentives evolve, and correlated failures emerge only at scale. Assured autonomy therefore depends as much on governance as on algorithms. Monitoring should be treated as a control function that detects when the system leaves its specification and triggers predefined responses. A useful template is Statistical Process Control (SPC): continuous measurement of performance and violation metrics, calibrated alarms, and explicit escalation rules
(
45
,
56
)
. At its core is a sequential detection problem—identifying change-points or distributional shifts quickly after they occur

—with an emphasis on interpretability and operational relevance.

In operational settings, monitoring is inherently sequential because escalation and fallback decisions trade off detection delay against false alarms. Those choices determine when to tighten admissible actions, require human review, or revert to conservative policies, and they shape the system’s safety–performance profile over its lifecycle. This matches the high-reliability view that treats “preoccupation with failure” as a design principle, not a slogan
(
69
)
. The same lens sharpens human–AI collaboration. The key object is a handoff protocol: what triggers deferral, what information is transferred, and how the system learns from overrides without creating new failure modes. In OR terms, the handoff rule is a policy to be optimized subject to safety, workload, and delay constraints, especially when multiple autonomous agents and human actors share a workflow.

7.4
Benchmarks and Data Readiness

A final constraint is infrastructural. Progress needs benchmarks that are small enough to iterate on yet structured enough to capture operational reality. Vision and language advanced when shared datasets and protocols made progress legible
(
21
)
; RL benefited from shared environments that standardized comparison
(
13
)
. Assured autonomy needs analogous testbeds for constraint-preserving generation, tail-aware stress testing, and decision quality under shift. The metrics should be operational—regret, stability, violation rates, and worst-case performance—not likelihood alone.

Digital twins for autonomous operations present a related opportunity. A useful twin is not a passive simulator; it is a structured stress-testing environment that generates rare events, distribution shifts, and worst-case scenarios under feasibility and performance constraints. Without that structure, twins reinforce average-case behavior rather than surfacing the failure modes that govern safety. Relatedly, the field faces a data-readiness gap. Operational datasets are abundant, but they are rarely curated to expose the objects that matter for assured autonomy: constraint sets, invariants, regime shifts, and rare-event structure. Building reusable datasets and testbeds is slow work, yet it is the basis for cumulative science and credible external validity. Institutional barriers are real as well: OR, statistics, ML, control, and human factors differ in training and norms of evidence. A field-level response requires venues and incentives that reward careful integration and reproducible evaluation, not only new architectures.

What ties these priorities together is a specific vision of autonomy: feasible by construction, hardened by minimax stress testing, governed by explicit monitoring and handoff rules, and accelerated by public benchmarks. Meeting that standard is the price of scaling autonomy responsibly, and it is also OR’s opportunity to shape what autonomy becomes.

8
Conclusion

Autonomous AI is leaving the lab and entering operations—warehouses, supply chains, clinics, mobility, and finance. The gains are real; the failures are fast and sticky. As autonomy rises, structure matters more. OR will shift from optimizing within a given workflow to designing the workflow itself.

This pattern echoes earlier waves of automation. Societies did not make high-energy machines acceptable by trusting average-case performance; they embedded control, standards, and contingency procedures in the surrounding system. Modern financial markets did not become resilient because trading algorithms became “smarter”; they became resilient because trading operated within risk limits, monitoring, and circuit breakers. Autonomy faces the same test. The relevant test is not whether a model can generate plausible outputs, but whether the operational system preserves nonnegotiable invariants, remains stable in tail regimes, and switches to a safe mode when conditions leave the validated envelope. Methodologically, this reframes autonomy as controlled evolution in probability space, with constraints and tail risk treated as explicit design requirements.

The practical implication is investing in models alone will disappoint. Value comes from redesigning the decision regime around the model: defining admissible actions, specifying escalation and deference rules, and institutionalizing stress testing and monitoring as operational processes. The managerial task is to decide
where

when

The research agenda is similarly concrete. Many central questions are not solved by scale: how to enforce feasibility throughout generation; how to search systematically for consequential failure regimes; how to verify safety properties without relying on model self-assessment; and how to coordinate multiple agents with aligned incentives and stable dynamics. These problems sit naturally in the OR toolkit, and they echo Herbert Simon’s argument that AI and OR are stronger together than apart
(
60
)
.

The autonomy paradox organizes this paper. Autonomy increases the value of rules that can be audited and enforced. “Assured autonomy” treats constraints, tail regimes, and monitoring as design inputs, not afterthoughts. With that discipline, autonomy can scale; without it, autonomy imports unpriced risk. It is not a natural extension of current GenAI pipelines. It must be engineered—and OR provides the language and tools to do so.
