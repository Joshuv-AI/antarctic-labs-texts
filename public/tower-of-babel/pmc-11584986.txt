Practitioners’ Needs for Addressing the Challenges of Sea‐Level Rise—A Qualitative Assessment
Authors: D Hirschfeld, K M Archie, E Mateo, J C Arnott, J A Vano
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPractitioners at the local and regional scale are under increased pressure to reduce risks to people and property posed by the threats of sea‐level rise (SLR) and associated impacts. To achieve this, a dialog between practitioners and scientists is imperative. Current research documents impacts of SLR, evaluates local SLR adaptation activities, identifies barriers to action, and works to assess local adaptive capacity. Despite this work, there has been little qualitative assessment of practitioners' needs when it comes to translating SLR science into local changes. To fill this gap, we used a combination of semi‐structured interviews and surveys. The interviews revealed practitioners’ needs, the tools they use, the challenges they face, and the contexts in which they make decisions. The survey allowed practitioners to rank potential interventions according to the level of impact they believed it would have on coastal adaptation planning. In total our study includes the perspectives of 142 practitioners from 24 states, Puerto Rico, the Mariana Islands, and Barbados. Corroborating earlier work, we find that resources broadly and funding specifically is the largest barrier faced by practitioners. We find that practitioners need more localized information and models supported by on the ground monitoring, decision support resources that allow for comparison of different scenarios, and communication tools that will enable them to engage with key audiences. These needs suggest a critical shift toward building trusted relationship between scientists and local practitioners and the need to bolster organizations that can support a bridge between these two contexts.Keywords: sea‐level rise, climate change adaptation, usable science, decision support, resilience, science‐policy interface

Introduction

The accelerating rate of sea‐level rise (SLR) (Nerem et al., 2018) places hundreds of millions of people currently living in coastal zones at significant risk, driving the need for proactive adaptation implementation (Kulp & Strauss, 2019; Nicholls & Cazenave, 2010). Increased flooding from the confluence of higher seas and higher rates of precipitation is one specific risk faced by coastal communities (Wahl et al., 2015). Other places are threatened by saltwater intrusion into aquifers (Griggs, 2017), and others face the threat of the mobilization of contaminants, such as heavy metals, volatile organic compounds, and persistent organic pollutants, that are found in the saturated zone and impacted by changes in aqueous geochemical cycles (Hill et al., 2023; Sheng et al., 2021; Warner et al., 2023). SLR also threatens the biodiversity and functionality of coastal ecosystems, which support people through food and livelihoods (Bindoff et al., 2019).

These SLR threats require significant adaptation action, which is often considered a local responsibility to reduce risks to people and property (Füssel, 2007; Measham et al., 2011). Significant work has been done to define and explain the risks posed by SLR (Evans et al., 2004a, 2004b; Nicholls, 2011) and to explain local vulnerability (Cutter et al., 2003; Fu et al., 2019; Stults & Woodruff, 2017). In response to these risk and vulnerability assessments some cities and towns have written and adopted climate adaptation plans (Bierbaum et al., 2013; Carmin et al., 2012; Carter, 2011). In the United States, researchers identified 156 cities that are creating adaptation plans (Shi et al., 2015) and at least 75 communities that have SLR policies or guidance in place (Hirschfeld et al., 2023). In the past 5–10 years, coastal adaptation efforts in some places have moved beyond planning to implementation in the form of both regulatory changes (Adams‐Schoen, 2018) and physical projects (Hirschfeld et al., 2021; Sutton‐Grier et al., 2018). However, despite this progress, some researchers note that climate adaptation plans in general (Woodruff & Stults, 2016) and SLR vulnerability assessments in particular (Fu et al., 2019) vary greatly in terms of quality. Additionally, progress on implementing adaptation efforts remains challenging. Specifically, research suggests limited adoption of nature‐based solutions (Sutton‐Grier et al., 2018), slow progress in underserved communities (Hill et al., 2023; Shi & Moser, 2021), and a lack of comprehensive action (Bierbaum et al., 2013).

Our approach draws on broader efforts to understand how to make and how to inform decisions in a changing climate (National Research Council (NRC), 2009; Orlove et al., 2020), which is an interdisciplinary domain engaging among many fields, the behavioral sciences, policy sciences, and the social study of science itself (Arnott et al., 2020). Scholars of climate information use underscore the critical interplay between scientific and experiential knowledge and the importance of bridging the gap between researchers and stakeholders to ensure that scientific findings effectively inform policy and action (Kirchhoff et al., 2013). To describe those who use scientific information to inform decisions, we use the term practitioner to include the many different professions (e.g., coastal managers, planners, emergency managers, and other decision‐makers, etc.) and levels of decision‐making (e.g., cities, regions, states, etc.) (Hirschfeld et al., 2023; Van De Wal et al., 2022). This research domain also highlights the importance of understanding the decisions the users are facing and the context they are situated in as starting points because decisions and context differ from case to case and hence require different decision‐making frameworks, which in turn require different kinds of SLR information (Hinkel et al., 2019; Hirschfeld, Boyle, et al., 2024; Jones et al., 2014; Kleindorfer et al., 1993; Simpson et al., 2016). This work also includes an understanding of the decision‐making process that accommodate the uncertain and dynamic nature of climate risk (Lempert & Collins, 2007; Marchau et al., 2019; National Research Council (NRC), 2009).

Others using similar theoretical foundations have reflected on the challenges of creating useable science within the context of SLR (Kopp et al., 2019; Le Cozannet et al., 2017; Ruckelshaus et al., 2020). For example, Kopp et al. (2019) in their theoretical work, advance our collective understanding of the need for a multidisciplinary approach that integrates data‐driven research with local knowledge and community engagement to develop robust adaptation plans. Similarly, others looked at existing climate service systems to understand the science that can be used to inform local decision‐making and the barriers to adaptation (Le Cozannet et al., 2017). While important, much of this theoretical work lacks empirical evidence from those directly engaged with managing coastal communities.

There is a growing body of empirical research that focuses on documenting and evaluating local SLR adaptation activities, moving the conversation beyond simply documenting the impacts of SLR (Esteban et al., 2020; Fu, 2020; Hamin et al., 2014; Hirschfeld et al., 2021; Marino, 2018). For example, in 2014, Hamin et al. conducted interviews of 15 small coastal towns in Massachusetts. They found that communities were either focused on current hazards or had intentions to plan for SLR, but all struggled to translate concerns about SLR into on‐the‐ground actions. In a more recent study, Hirschfeld et al. (2021) carefully examined physical projects built in the coastal zone to confer local resilience. Their evaluation finds a shift toward more habitat‐focused strategies rather than sea walls, thus indicating a more integrated landscape scale approach. While these studies provide insights into actions being taken, they are based on a limited number of places; thus, they cannot provide transferable lessons for all coastal communities threatened by SLR.

Another approach to understanding adaptation actions, is focusing on barriers and adaptive capacity (Amundsen et al., 2010; Eisenack et al., 2014; Ford et al., 2011; Hirschfeld et al., 2020; Measham et al., 2011). Within the barriers literature, there is a subset that uses surveys of local government planning practitioners (Archie et al., 2018; Carmin et al., 2012; Ekstrom & Moser, 2014; Measham et al., 2011; Nordgren et al., 2016). Through this work, barriers identified include insufficient funding, a lack of local staff abilities, a lack of sufficient local data, struggles to gain political support from local citizens, issues with leadership at higher levels of government, and internal governance structures. Focusing on adaptive capacity, a recent study by Fu (2020) gathered 86 surveys from cities and towns across the United States. They found that those with higher capacities were implementing more adaptation actions, thus indicating that bolstering capacity could significantly progress adaptation efforts. While insightful, none of this research explicitly asks practitioners what they need in order to adapt to rising sea levels.

Recently, researchers have started to conduct studies on practitioner needs based on surveys and assessments of written reports. For example, McEvoy et al. (2021) conducted an expert survey to understand the specific SLR science used by national government practitioners in Europe. Another study surveyed practitioners across the globe on their use of SLR information in local plans and policy (Hirschfeld et al., 2023). Several other studies used questionnaires to understand practitioner needs for adaptation to SLR in California, USA (Tribbia & Moser, 2008), Denmark (Madsen et al., 2019), and Italy (Geraldini et al., 2021), or among port decision‐makers (Ng et al., 2018). One recent study analyzed regional reports used in the United States to see what SLR science was being used (Garner et al., 2023). These studies, however, rely primarily on quantitative assessments, with some exceptions (Hirschfeld, Boyle, et al., 2024), there has been little qualitative assessment of practitioners' needs when it comes to translating SLR science into practice.

Recognizing the importance of elevating practitioners' voices to inform the science/policy/practice interface, the aim of this paper is to advance our understanding of practitioner needs and ways to support SLR adaptation decision‐making and implementation in the United States. We build on prior practitioner survey results of what SLR science practitioners are using (Hirschfeld et al., 2023) and investigations into the operating context of SLR practitioners (Hinkel et al., 2019; Hirschfeld, Boyle, et al., 2024) to understand more about the challenges they face and the opportunities to address them. We conduct in depth interviews and additional surveys to reveal the tools and resources practitioners are already using, as well as the kind of resources and broader supportive infrastructures that would help practitioners address the risks associated with SLR. The in‐depth interaction with practitioners represents an example of how to inform future designs of climate services and decision‐support resources (Hewitt, 2021; Penning‐Rowsell et al., 2023; Vincent et al., 2018). Our paper proceeds with a description of our methods in Section 2. In Section 3 we summarize the results and unpack them in Section 4. Our conclusion in Section 5 identifies implications of this work and future opportunities.

Materials and Methods

To better understand how SLR science is currently informing decisions and what resources could be mobilized to improve decision support, we employed a two‐round, mixed‐methods, social science approach (Teddlie & Tashakkori, 2009, 2010). This work builds on the results of a recent survey of planners around the world (Hirschfeld et al., 2023) and helps to qualitatively fill in the gaps in knowledge about climate services and decision support demand and use by those planning for and making decisions about SLR adaptation. Since the practice of adapting to SLR happens at a variety of scales and by people with a wealth of different background and job titles (Le Cozannet et al., 2017), we recruited a range of professionals at different jurisdictional scales to participate. The first round consisted of in‐depth, qualitative data collection followed by a round of quantitative data collection that built upon information gathered during the qualitative work. This methodology allowed us to dive deeply into the topics at hand with a diverse group of practitioners and then use the details we gathered to verify the impactfulness of the potential interventions with a broader group of practitioners.

Phase 1—Semi‐Structured Interviews

During the first round of data collection, we used semi‐structured interviews to gather information from practitioners. Our research team conducted a series of group interviews with 43 practitioners from 20 states, Puerto Rico, and the Mariana Islands, which represent all EPA regions with ocean coastlines (Figure 1). Participants were recruited using a snowballing technique, which can be particularly effective in hard‐to‐reach geographies (Goodman, 2011). We began by using lists of participants from the 2023 Hirschfeld et al. survey of practitioners and continued to recruit additional practitioners based on location to ensure that our sample represented all the coastal regions of the USA. We used a purposive sampling technique and stopped recruiting interviewees once we had a pool that was diverse with respect to geographic location, and after we noticed patterns emerging in interviewee responses (Tongco, 2007). We recruited our practitioners from the many different professions (e.g., coastal managers, planners, emergency managers, and other decision‐makers, etc.) and levels of decision‐making (e.g., cities, regions, states, etc.) responsible for SLR adaptation.

Map showing locations of survey and interview participants. Shaded states and territories (in blue) contain the location of a participant. Bolded lines between states indicate EPA regions of the United States.

The group interviews were a cross between a focus group and a reflective workshop, and each included a semi‐structured dialog between our research team and a group of 4–8 participants (see Text S1 in Supporting Information S1). These sessions drew upon participant expertise related to their own SLR work and relevant context. Through a collaborative inquiry process modeled on science education (Bell et al., 2010), we generated insights that are useful to us as researchers and to participants as decision‐makers. Group discussions explored a variety of questions related to the use of science in SLR planning and implementation with participants who offered diverse perspectives from around the country. We heard about working processes that they have found important in their particular context and ways in which behind‐the‐scenes support structures facilitated their success. We asked participants to be reflective and analytical and to speak to what has worked, as well as what has not. We also asked participants to provide us with information on tools and resources they are using in planning for SLR. We did not expect the groups to reach a consensus on any given topic as we value differences in perspective and honor them as revealing insight, though disagreement among participants was rare. Together, these multiple sources of qualitative data enabled us to develop a rich and nuanced picture of SLR adaptation, which allows us to make conceptual connections across SLR planning projects and between SLR planning projects and those of other sectors also engaged in planning for adaptation to climate change.

The majority of SLR adaptation research in the USA focuses on highly visible and densely populated coastal areas (Buchanan et al., 2019; Hirschfeld et al., 2021). However, some regions and specific geographic areas have received comparatively less attention. Recognizing the greater impacts of SLR on underserved and disadvantaged populations (Cutter et al., 2008; Hill et al., 2023; Martinich et al., 2013), we made deliberate efforts to include participants from areas that have been previously overlooked. For example, we focused on getting participants from more rural, remote, and less‐developed coastal communities that have limited resources and capacity to address the challenges posed by SLR. Another geographic focus was Alaska, where vast and remote coastal areas are highly susceptible to SLR, coastal erosion, and thawing permafrost, which is of particular concern to indigenous communities (Bronen, 2013). We also put effort into recruiting participants from the Gulf Coast, where some states are experiencing land subsidence (Dokka, 2011; May et al., 2023), and there are concerns about the impacts of SLR on industries like oil and gas. Finally, we focused on the southeastern coastal areas, including parts of North and South Carolina and Georgia, which have not been as extensively studied as other coastal regions like Florida and the Mid‐Atlantic and are experiencing high rates of SLR (Horton et al., 2008; Kemp et al., 2011; Valle‐Levinson et al., 2017).

Group interviews took place on Zoom in 90‐min sessions on mutually agreeable dates and times. Before each session, we built a portal that included Zoom info, researcher and participant bios, potential discussion questions, and a copy of the IRB‐reviewed informed consent. These portals allowed us to avoid spending time on long introductions and allowed participants the chance to reflect on the topics in advance so they had thoughts ready to share. Informed consent was distributed to each participant via DocuSign before the discussion. All participants either signed the consent ahead of time or were allowed to verbally consent at the beginning of the discussion before we started the recording. We used services from Otter for real‐time transcription that both researchers and participants had access to (“Otter.AI [automated technology],” 2023). Providing this type of access greatly improves the accessibility of online discussions for all participants (Chen et al., 2023). Otter transcripts (both text and audio) were used in our analysis, which began immediately after the interviews were completed.

These discussions generated more than 800 min of interview data, including transcripts and video recordings. Our initial research questions provided the basis for a thematic analysis (Kiger & Varpio, 2020; Saldana, 2021), which was guided by our understanding of the relevant literature on this topic. Sourcing predominantly from the transcripts, we used an inductive approach to articulate common themes and concepts (Thomas, 2006). When we were uncertain about a particular statement, we reviewed specific clips from video recordings. Then, using a deductive approach, we connected specific themes to the existing literature on SLR science, adaptation planning, the integration of science into planning, and coastal management. Through this coding, we observed commonalities between participants regarding the biophysical‐challenges and the social and institutional challenges they faced in adapting to SLR (Section 3.1). We also identified resources available and commonly used by practitioners (Table S1 in Supporting Information S1). Our coding approach allowed us to identify needs across sessions that we used in our survey (Section 2.2). We report on results from the survey in Section 3.

Phase 2—Survey

During the group interviews with practitioners (phase 1), we asked participants to provide specific examples of information, tools, or decision‐support resources that would enable them to more effectively make decisions related to SLR. Upon completion of the discussions, a list of 38 “needs” was compiled based on participant responses. Using thematic coding (Thomas, 2006), these needs were divided into three broad categories of needs found to be important in the literature: (a) information needs, (b) decision‐support needs, and (c) communication tools/educational opportunity‐related needs (Archie et al., 2012, 2014; Biagini et al., 2014; Hirschfeld & Hill, 2022; Nordgren et al., 2016). Our interview process included perspectives from a diverse group of practitioners. Thus, this list of 38 needs identified by practitioners represents an extensive set of resources that fill important gaps in the translation of science to practice. To report on the level of impactfulness of each potential intervention, we surveyed a broader group of practitioners.

Using the list of 38 participant‐identified “needs,” we designed a survey that allowed practitioners to rank each potential intervention on a five‐point Likert scale from “not at all impactful” to “extremely impactful” according to the level of impact they believed it would have on coastal adaptation planning. The survey was distributed anonymously via Google Forms. It also included demographic questions and an opportunity for respondents to include other potentially impactful examples of information, tools, or decision support that they believed were missing from the list.

The survey was sent to 478 practitioners and was administered from August 2023 to October 2023. One hundred seventeen practitioners from 23 states, Puerto Rico, and Barbados completed the entire survey (Figure 1). This sample represents every EPA region with coastlines and makes for a ∼24% response rate. Note that because the survey link was sharable, it is impossible to calculate an exact response rate. Group interview participants were invited to participate in the survey, and 18 of them chose to take part. When the two samples are combined, this study includes the perspectives of 142 practitioners from 24 states, Puerto Rico, the Mariana Islands, and Barbados. Figure 1 includes the locations of all responses to both the survey and the semi‐structured interviews.

Results

The practitioners who participated in our work are a geographically diverse (Figure 1), highly educated, and highly experienced group of practitioners (Figure 2). 79% of survey respondents have either a Master's Degree (60%) or a PhD (19%). Another 20% of the respondents have a Bachelor’s degree, and only 1% of respondents have a high school diploma as their highest degree. In terms of years of experience, nearly half of the respondents have been working in coastal management for more than 11 years; specifically, we find that 30% have been working for more than 15 years, and another 16% have been working for between 11 and 15 years. The remaining 44% break down into 25% at 6–10 years, 20% at 2–5 years, and 9% at less than 2 years. We did not specifically ask interviewees to provide answers to these questions; however, based on a review of their biographical data, we observed a similar level of education and experience.

The distribution of educational attainments (a) and years employed in coastal management (b) of our survey participants.

Our sample also varied in terms of job title and jurisdictional scale (Figure 3). We collected job titles for all but one of our 142 interview and survey participants. Slightly more than a quarter (27%) of our participants were scientists/researchers/coastal specialists, and another 27% were municipal officers responsible for topics connected to coastal management (i.e., sustainability, resilience, emergency management, etc.). 21% of our participants were planners or planning directors, and 16% were coastal managers or directors. A small number of consultants (4%) and extension agents (5%) also provided their perspectives. Using job titles and biographical information provided by participants, we were able to establish the scales at which 61 of our participants make SLR decisions (Figure 3b). 39% of our participants are responsible for making decisions at the state level with another 36% making decisions at the city or town level. 10% of participants work at both the regional (e.g., watershed) and county level with 3% and 2% making decisions at a national and port level respectively.

Biophysical, Social, and Institutional Challenges

To inform practitioners’ use of science, it helps to understand the types of biophysical challenges they face (e.g., saltwater intrusion, higher storm surges, etc.) and their associated decision context (e.g., low risk tolerance) (Hinkel et al., 2019). It is also important to know what is currently preventing practitioners (i.e., their social and institutional challenges) from making progress toward SLR adaptation (Ekstrom & Moser, 2014). Participants identified specific biophysical challenges related to SLR 137 times during our eight group discussions. Biophysical challenges were sorted into 11 broad categories (Figure 4), the most commonly cited of which are: storm surge (18%), saltwater intrusion (13%), coastal erosion/loss of beaches (12%), more frequent storm events (10%), and compound flooding/stormwater drainage issues (10%).

The relative number of mentions of the most common biophysical challenges related to sea‐level rise (SLR) as identified by interview participants.

During the group interviews, participants mentioned 136 social and institutional challenges to adaptation which we distilled into four categories based on the literature (Figure 5) (Aylett, 2015; Carmin et al., 2012). We find that most (33%) of the social and institutional challenges relate to resources. The other three categories are relatively evenly distributed, with information being 24%, commitment/support at 22%, and political environment at 21%. Those in the resource category include two of the three most commonly cited social and institutional challenges—lack of funding (15% of responses) and lack of staff or internal capacity (13% of responses). The information category includes lack of useful information (12%), difficulty translating information into action (7%), and conflicting science (4%). Conflicting concerns (13%) and lack of public understanding (7%) are the two largest contributors to the commitment/support category. The political enabling environment includes political realities (9%), lack of regulatory certainty (8%), and multiple actors (4%).

A sunburst graphic depicting social and institutional challenges to SLR adaptation planning articulated by interview participants in a hierarchical format. The inner ring shows four categories drawn from the literature: (1) Resources, (2) Information, (3) Commitment/support, and (4) Political environment. The outer ring shows a greater level of detail, providing a comprehensive visualization of the data.

Through our interviews and survey we compiled a list of resources identified by practitioners as useful for planning to adapt to SLR. This helps to identify a baseline of what is already available and being used by practitioners. Practitioners identified 50 specific resources that are particularly useful in their work on SLR adaptation (see Table S1 in Supporting Information S1). These resources are highly varied both in terms of topics and scale. For example, some practitioners provided state level SLR viewers (e.g., State of Hawaiʻi Sea Level Rise Viewer), while others provided state level hazards information (e.g., the Texas Shoreline Change Project). Other practitioners provided focused studies relevant to their specific jurisdiction. We also found that some participants pointed us to national resources such as NOAA Digital Coast, Ecosystem Effects of Sea Level Rise, and CoastAdapt Shoreline Explorer.

Needs to Overcome Challenges

In phase 1 of the research we asked participants to provide specific examples of information, tools, or decision‐support resources that would allow them to more effectively make decisions related to SLR. Upon completion of the interviews, a list of 38 “needs” was compiled based on participant responses, and these were divided into three broad categories based on factors that have been previously identified in the literature (Archie et al., 2012, 2014; Biagini et al., 2014; Hirschfeld & Hill, 2022; Nordgren et al., 2016): information needs, decision‐support needs, and communication tools and educational opportunities. Figure 6 shows the number of survey respondents who consider each option either very or extremely impactful for planning or implementing SLR adaptation. All 38 options were considered to be either very or extremely impactful by at least 30% of respondents, and eight out of the 38 options were considered either very or extremely impactful by more than 75% of respondents.

Percent of survey respondents who reported that the provided information needs, decision support needs, and communication tools and educational opportunities would be either very or extremely impactful.

The three options the respondents considered most impactful all fell under the information needs category. Local‐scale data collection and on‐the‐ground monitoring that is updated on a regular basis (LIDAR, groundwater levels, tide gauges, satellite imagery, SLR sensors, etc.), locally calibrated models that capture interactions leading to compound flooding and increased risks and information about the relative effectiveness of nature‐based solutions were considered to be either very or extremely impactful by 91%, 91%, and 87% of respondents respectively. Communication tools that translate the impacts of SLR into economic value (86%), reliable assessments of the potential long‐term impacts of large infrastructure projects (81%), information about the influence of waves on flooding (79%), tools that include scenarios of the future that can be used to explore the impacts of alternative decisions (78%), and communication materials to use with the public that show local impacts of sea level rise (maps, graphic representations of models, story maps, etc.) (76%) were the other five options that at least three‐quarters of respondents consider to be either very or extremely impactful.

Discussion

Our findings provide insights to inform science translation, decision support, and other boundary work in the SLR adaptation space. Unlike studies that rely exclusively on surveys (Geraldini et al., 2021; Hirschfeld et al., 2023; Madsen et al., 2019; McEvoy et al., 2021; Ng et al., 2018; Tribbia & Moser, 2008) or document analysis (Garner et al., 2023; Hirschfeld & Hill, 2022), our qualitative approach highlights very specific needs of practitioners (Figure 6). We also unpack both biophysical challenges (Figure 4), and social and institutional challenges (Figure 5) that practitioners face. This information provides critical insights for the ongoing conversation around improving adaptation to SLR and reducing risks. In the following subsections we highlight practitioners’ shared and conflicting needs related to information needs (Section 4.1), decision‐support (Section 4.2), communication tools, and educational opportunities (Section 4.3). We also discuss insights from this empirical study that pertain to the decision contexts of our participants (Section 4.4).

Information Needs

We found information needs were shared most by our interview participants and are the highest priorities from the survey (Figure 6). Practitioners in our study however were not interested in more information for the sake of information itself, but rather they mentioned very specific information needs that continue to be unmet. Our findings match recent qualitative global research, which also identified in situ monitoring and local calibration of models as primary needs from science (Hirschfeld, Boyle, et al., 2024). These findings also reflect categories that contribute to local adaptive capacity such as data availability and data quality (Fu, 2020; Hirschfeld et al., 2020). Scientists also acknowledge the issue around calibration and the challenge of localizing future SLR projections (Kopp et al., 2019). Meeting these needs would fundamentally shift the climate service enterprise making it a demand‐driven process as per recent recommendations by Findlater et al. (2021).

Our survey results reveal another important emerging trend—a focus on nature‐based solutions and a call to use them to effectively adapt to SLR (Feagin et al., 2021). Specifically, in our survey we found information about nature‐based solutions' relative effectiveness to be the third highest—87% of survey respondents reported that this type of information would be either very or extremely impactful for making decisions about SLR. Additionally, 60% of survey respondents also consider improved riparian models for building buffers to be either very or extremely impactful for making decisions about SLR (Figure 6). Interestingly this need was not limited to a specific type of participant but rather cuts across different job types, different jurisdictional scales, and different coastal settings. We think this need derives from the fact that studies over the last decade have highlighted the potential for nature‐based solutions (Debele et al., 2023; Feagin et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2020) and yet their implementation on the ground remains low (Reilly‐Moman et al., 2023). This disconnect between nature‐based solutions potential and their implementation can lead to maladaptation (La Peyre et al., 2022) and thus this call from practitioners ought be heeded and more work should be done to develop an understanding of nature‐based solutions' effectiveness under different hazard conditions and for different coastal environments (Sutton‐Grier et al., 2015, 2018).

We observed that practitioners who have on‐the‐ground monitoring and/or are more familiar with the various sources of data, the problem is not with the quantity of information provision but rather the usability of the information—a finding that matches previous semi‐structured interviews with 27 experts on climate services (Findlater et al., 2021). An example of this concern were timing‐related issues that can impede the use of information in the context of planning and implementation. As one said, “The lag from ‘your’ models to this conversation is too long. And then the translation back to whoever’s creating the models, that lag is too long too.” In other words, practitioners are not able to use the modeled information because the time to get that information is too slow and the time for the feedback on the model is too slow. Another timing‐related issue has to do with the funding horizon of some tools. “Too often, these interactive tools die with the end of their grant funding. Very few tools (e.g., Risk Rating) exist beyond their original funding period, and those that do require intensive post‐funding period investment, policy/regulation to solicit usage and regular updating.” For these reasons, many practitioners cautioned the promotion of additional tool development as opposed to other solutions. Some of the solutions they would rather see are solutions oriented such as on‐the‐ground demonstration projects, policy and regulation guidance, and case studies.

Importantly, we noticed that multiple discussion participants and survey respondents consider a lack of coordination between information sources to be the biggest barrier to effective information use. This need was clearly expressed by one planner who said: “we need to get the federal agencies to agree on one projection for SLR for practitioners’ use. NASA, USGS, USACE, and NOAA each having their own different projection is ridiculous and confusing to users.” This need was corroborated by our survey data, where 60% of respondents said that a nationally recognized source of data would be either very or extremely impactful (Figure 6). It is important to note that not everyone expressed this need. Qualitatively it seemed that those who were farther along in their adaptation efforts were more concerned by this challenge. This could be because the transition from a general plan to specific design interventions requires more financial resources and greater specificity around the actual SLR number a seawall or levee is being designed for.

This concern echoes previous research into general climate change information needs (Nordgren et al., 2016) and SLR specifically (Le Cozannet et al., 2017). This finding also matches recent international work where practitioners articulated confusion about different information sources (Hirschfeld, Boyle, et al., 2024). On the other hand, not all places around the world share this challenge. For example, coastal adaptation is thought to be much more advanced in the UK and the Netherlands (Le Cozannet et al., 2017; Tol et al., 2008). In the United States, there are some strong interagency collaborative efforts, such as the 2022 Sea Level Rise Technical Report Data Files (NOAA, 2002) and the Interagency Sea Level Rise Scenario Tool (NASA, 2023). We take this need to suggest that a better pipeline could be developed to translate these interagency projects into practitioner benefits. Additionally, bolstering local adaptive capacity, such as through regional networks, can help local governments better collaborate with national agencies (Fu, 2020; Hirschfeld et al., 2020; Woodruff, 2018).

Decision Support Needs

Beyond information needs, practitioners yearned for deeper forms of support for their difficult decisions. As one interviewee said, “we have generally good data that is often very hard to translate into implementable policy.” This reflects the shifting balance of attention from proliferating more information or resources branded as decision‐support tools to more focused attention on the actual decisions at hand and how to improve them (Findlater et al., 2021). Practitioners expressed a need for tools that would help evaluate the implications and tradeoffs between future decisions and to weigh risks over different timescales. For instance, we heard numerous examples of practitioners wrestling over how to grapple with short‐term needs relative to long‐term risks. This finding resonates with work from researchers in the field of decision support focusing on SLR who acknowledge the importance of scenarios (Hinkel et al., 2019) and integrating high‐end SLR into policy (Stammer et al., 2019; Van De Wal et al., 2022).

While a variety of decision‐support needs were expressed, underlying these stated needs seemed to be a more fundamental change in how decision support resources are produced and sustained. As one practitioner notes, “They want to develop a new tool, right? We got tools coming out of our ears! … I think there needs to be a big paradigm shift […]if somebody implements a tool or creates a tool, those scientists should be responsible for implementing the tool, responsible for the tool getting down to the local level.” This quote reflects concerns raised by researchers about how tools are produced and their overall quality and sustainability (Nordgren et al., 2016; Wong‐Parodi et al., 2017). One way to address this concern would be to build trusted and lasting relationships between consumers (e.g., practitioners) and producers (e.g., researchers) of climate services (Jacobs & Street, 2020).

Communication and Education Needs

We find that communication needs also feature strongly at the top of the list with two of the top 10 needs (Figure 6). This matches with previous global qualitative research where practitioners specifically stated that communication tools and techniques were critical to their work (Hirschfeld, Boyle, et al., 2024). While the adaptive capacity literature does not specifically call attention to communication needs, it does highlight the importance of collaboration, leadership, and political environment (Fu, 2020; Hirschfeld et al., 2020). The communication needs we found articulated by practitioners would help in these areas. For example, communication tools that translate the impacts of SLR into economic values and communication materials to use with the public that show local impacts of SLR could help practitioners improve their political environments by encouraging constituents to support local policy changes and the implementation of controversial projects. Similarly, communication tools to use specifically with elected officials would help those in leadership positions support needed changes to adapt to SLR.

Interestingly, we found that education and training did not rise to the top of our survey results with the four educational needs falling in the bottom half of the needs. This is in contrast with the findings on adaptive capacity, which overall finds that current capacity is lower than the gravity of the situation (Fu, 2020). Our finding may be an indication of the highly educated and highly experienced group of practitioners in our particular sample. While not at the top of the list, two‐thirds of survey respondents consider training and education programs to develop SLR expertise to be highly impactful (Figure 6). Filling this need would address the lack of internal capacity that interview participants identified as a major institutional challenge to SLR adaptation planning (Figure 4) and that many others have previously identified as a barrier to action (Archie et al., 2018; Carmin et al., 2012; Ekstrom & Moser, 2014; Measham et al., 2011; Nordgren et al., 2016).

Looking at the types of educational opportunities provides further insights into how to support practitioners. We observe that deeper and more engaged training opportunities rise to the top with in‐person workshops ranking 15% points higher than webinar series (Figure 6). This matches earlier research into the best ways to develop skills and operate in highly complex settings (Nursey‐Bray & Harvey, 2013). Educational opportunities need not be limited to formal degree seeking options. Coastal manager fellowship programs and workshops with scientists and SLR experts were considered highly impactful by more than half of all survey respondents (Figure 6). Another approach to the question of education highlighted by participants was a need for “opportunities for communities to learn from each other.” Networking opportunities can expand the reach of SLR training programs as trainees share their knowledge and experiences with other coastal managers who haven't taken part in the formal training (Nursey‐Bray et al., 2016; Wenger et al., 2002).

Practitioners' Operating Context

For climate information to be actionable for practitioners, the decisions the users are facing and the context they are situated in must be considered (Jones et al., 2014; Kleindorfer et al., 1993; Simpson et al., 2016). Recent work suggests that practitioners and their needs from SLR science are impacted directly by place (i.e., the nature of the coast, the density of their city, etc.), resources (i.e., finances, staffing, etc.), and culture (i.e., local values and traditions) (Hirschfeld, Boyle, et al., 2024). Additionally, in the context of SLR it's important to consider the user's uncertainty tolerance, which refers to the level of uncertainty stakeholders are willing to accept. It also means understanding the decision horizon, which refers to the time needed for planning and implementing adaptation measures (Hinkel et al., 2019). For example, planning, designing, and building critical infrastructure necessitates a lower uncertainty tolerance than public parks, where some level of failure is socially acceptable.

We find that the biophysical challenges practitioners face go far beyond the singular impact of rising seas to intersect with a myriad of other substantial climate risks depending on their particular location (Figure 3). Our 43 interviewees mentioned, on average, three different biophysical challenges corroborating prior findings that compound risks are important to practitioners (Hirschfeld, Boyle, et al., 2024). We observed that places are unique with some participants pointing out that in their location, infrastructure is being held to a higher standard in regard to where they can be built. Others are working toward a comprehensive capital improvement plan that would include future SLR estimates. Some communities have limited development in at‐risk coastal areas to single‐family detached residences, thus ensuring critical infrastructure to public safety is not in such risky locations. However, since practitioners are not simply facing a single challenge (i.e., just changing storm surge), the conversation really needs to recognize their complex reality. One participant captured the reality of these challenges when they said: “I will share a little bit of Hawaiian Knowledge that I picked up and it’s grandma’s coming home. And what that means is that you know, when grandma comes home, that is, the ocean, you don’t lock the door, you open the door, you make Grandma feel welcome. You know, you sort of move out of the way let her do her thing. She’s there to help one way or another, whether you understand it or not.” Practitioners are on the front lines of the reality that the seas are rising and are compelled to navigate a complex landscape of adaptation and resilience‐building, striving to safeguard coastal communities and ecosystems for generations to come.

Our findings also match the previous literature, which suggests that a lack of resources is a primary social and institutional challenge to climate change adaptation broadly and SLR adaptation specifically (Aylett, 2015; Carmin et al., 2012; Ekstrom & Moser, 2014; Hirschfeld & Hill, 2022). Our qualitative approach allows us to dig deeper and unpack the data presented in Figure 4. For example, we heard that a lack of staff and internal capacity impacts their ability to obtain and interpret information. It also complicates their ability to “evaluate proposals from consultants and bring them up to speed” on the local context of the problems at hand. One participant said “A lack of data is not our issue anymore. It’s just moving beyond the data now to the long‐term planning and decision‐making and setting the policies in place.” Our survey data substantiates this finding. A number of practitioners feel that the biggest gap exists in the translation of technical knowledge into actionable information, with more than two‐thirds of survey respondents reporting that assistance translating data into policy and policy to implementation is either very or extremely impactful (Figure 6). Our findings also match the literature where bibliometric research finds a shift away from pure science analysis toward solution‐focused topics such as declining ecosystem services and flood hazards (Khojasteh et al., 2023).

Conclusions

Practitioners must address the current and future SLR threats to build the resilience of coastal communities. To do so, they need to incorporate SLR science into local and regional policies and plans. Our mixed methods approach identified multiple ways that science and boundary work could better support processes required for effective adaptation. In an effort to make this research actionable, we conclude by describing concrete ways to foster coastal resilience and to meet expressed practitioner needs.

SLR information exists and is being used, however a demand‐driven process could improve the flow of this information into actionable plans and policies. Critical work that would lead to real improvements is increased local‐scale data collection and on‐the‐ground monitoring (e.g., LIDAR, groundwater levels, tide gauges, satellite imagery, SLR sensors, etc.) and locally calibrated models that are updated on a regular basis. More work is needed to establish a clear, nationally recognized source of SLR data as confusion at the local government level remains high. Scientists can also fill identified gaps related to the interactions between SLR and other local processes such as rainfall, waves, and groundwater.

Practitioners articulated a need for a fundamental change in how decision support resources are produced and sustained. Specifically there is a need for resources that would enable practitioners to evaluate the implications and tradeoffs between future decisions and to weigh risks. For example, it would be very helpful to create a clear repository of coastal adaptation best practice examples and to connect those with suitability tools allowing practitioners to understand what physical designs can be used and where they would provide the greatest benefits. Those interested in filling the decision support gap can focus on building trusted and sustained relationships by offer in‐person workshops that allow practitioners to ask questions about models/data sets. Similarly, individual experts could consider offering one‐on‐one office hours with decision‐makers to address more specific questions.

Improved communication can help with over 40% of all the social, and institutional challenges mentioned by group interview participants including conflicting perceptions of risk, lack of understanding related to climate change, or issues related to political realities and collective action. Co‐production of the science could lead to more translation of the science into communication tools that can be used with critical audiences and thereby build local capacity.

Municipalities (especially small, rural, or disadvantaged ones) lack the internal staff capacity to access and interpret relevant SLR information, and to implement on the ground data gathering. Thus, existing science is not always translated into local adaptation action. To address this need, professional development and educational programs that build internal capacity for accessing information and translating the information into analysis of possible solutions is critical. As practitioners noted, these program could build or expand upon existing successful efforts such as NOAA Coastal Management Fellowship program or FEMA's Higher Education Program.

Ultimately, we observe a fundamental shift from general needs about future sea levels that can inform vulnerability assessments to highly specific needs about complex interactions between different possible adaptation strategies, the built environment, and social dynamics. Sustained efforts and an improved bridge between SLR science and practice can build local capacity and enable practitioners to make necessary difficult decisions.
