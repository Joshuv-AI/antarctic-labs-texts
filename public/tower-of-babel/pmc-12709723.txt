Effects of blood flow restriction interval training on aerobic, anaerobic capacity, and muscle strength in healthy adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xin Zheng, Yong Li, Jun Chen, Zhangyuting Shang, Tong Jiang, Ziren Zhao, Nijiao Deng, Kaixiang Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlood Flow Restriction (BFR) training is increasingly recognized as an effective method for enhancing physical performance. BFR combined with low-intensity continuous or resistance training could improve aerobic capacity, muscle hypertrophy, and muscle strength just as effectively as high-intensity training in healthy adults. However, the effects of BFR combined with interval training (BFRIT) on aerobic capacity, anaerobic capacity, and muscle strength are not clear, and experts do not agree in the literature.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across six databases by the PRISMA guidelines. Data were extracted from the included studies, and random-effects meta-analyses were conducted to synthesize the effect size (ES), expressed as standardized mean differences (SMD). The heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic. Additionally, the GRADE framework was employed to evaluate the quality of the evidence derived from this meta-analysis.ResultsTwenty-four studies consisting of 607 participants were included. Aerobic capacity was measured using maximal oxygen uptake (V̇O2max) or peak oxygen uptake (V̇O2peak). Anaerobic capacity was assessed through peak power output (PPO) or mean power output (MPO). Muscle strength was quantified via peak force or torque in knee extension; if unavailable, knee flexion data or one-repetition maximum (1RM) were used instead. The ES of BFRIT on aerobic capacity was significant and small (SMD = 0.33, p < 0.001, I2 = 5.20%); the ES of BFRIT on muscle strength was significant and moderate (SMD = 0.61, p < 0.001, I2 = 29.76%); but the ES of BFRIT on PPO was not significant and trivial(SMD=-0.07, p = 0.74, I2 = 0.0%); the ES of BFRIT on MPO was not significant and small (SMD = 0.23, p = 0.25, I2 = 0%). Subgroup analysis revealed that the effects of BFRIT on aerobic capacity and muscle strength were impacted by the training intensity (high vs. low to moderate), training duration (≥ 6 weeks vs. < 6 weeks), training status (untrained vs. trained), cuff pressure modes (fixed vs. incremental), cuff pressure types (absolute vs. relative), and cuff application phases (during exercise vs. rest intervals alone).ConclusionsBFRIT is beneficial for healthy adults in improving aerobic capacity and muscle strength. However, the impact of BFRIT on anaerobic capacity remains uncertain due to the limited sample sizes and statistical power in current studies. Future research should focus on larger sample sizes and more robust designs to clarify the potential anaerobic adaptations related to BFRIT.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD42024609590.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01413-x.

Background

Blood flow restriction (BFR) training has gained increasing attention as an effective method for enhancing athletic performance and facilitating rehabilitation [1–6]. BFR training involves performing exercises using an external constriction device, such as blood pressure cuffs or elastic wraps, applied to the proximal limb muscles to restrict arterial blood flow and slow venous return [7]. The principal physiological mechanisms underlying BFR training include the induction of localized muscular hypoxia and the resultant accumulation of metabolic byproducts, notably lactate, due to controlled reductions in muscular perfusion [8]. Such physiological alterations activate metabolic and neuromuscular signaling pathways, ultimately enhancing protein synthesis and augmenting aerobic metabolic adaptations [9]. The main benefit of BFR with exercise is its capacity to produce physiological adaptations similar to those achieved through high-intensity training, even when using low to moderate training loads. Systematic reviews have demonstrated that BFR combined with low-intensity resistance training or continuous aerobic exercise elicits comparable effects to high-intensity training in enhancing aerobic capacity and muscle functions in healthy adults [1, 2, 10, 11]. BFR combined with resistance training (BFR-RT) primarily leads to localized physiological changes within the musculoskeletal system, also known as the peripheral system [12–14]. In contrast, BFR combined with continuous aerobic exercise enhances aerobic capacity by creating mechanical stress through proper blood flow restriction, which also challenges the cardiovascular system [2]. This unique characteristic makes BFR a valuable tool for improving aerobic capacity, muscle hypertrophy, and muscular strength, particularly in populations unable to engage in high-intensity exercise.

Given the proven efficacy, researchers have sought to expand the application of BFR with interval training (BFRIT). Acute studies have suggested that BFR combined with high-intensity interval training (HIIT) could accelerate neuromuscular and metabolic response during exercise in healthy adults through both peripheral and central mechanisms [15–22]. Currently, the chronic effects of BFRIT on physical performance in healthy adults are still under discussion. Tangchaisuriya et al. have demonstrated that a 12-week program of BFR combined with HIIT significantly improved maximal oxygen uptake (V̇O2max), maximal aerobic power (MAP), and muscle strength in master road cyclists[23]. Similarly, McKee et al. have shown that three weeks of BFR interval sprint training significantly improved MAP and 30-second anaerobic power, but not V̇O2max in semiprofessional team-sport players[24]. Conversely, other studies have suggested that 4–5 weeks of BFRIT is effective in enhancing V̇O2max in elite endurance athletes, but fails to improve 30-second anaerobic power and muscle strength[25, 26]. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in training duration, participant characteristics, exercise intensity, or variations in BFR application protocols across studies. Chua et al. (2022) conducted the first systematic review examining both the acute and chronic effects of BFR combined with HIIT in healthy adults[27]. Their findings suggested that combining BFR with HIIT could enhance physiological adaptations, improving aerobic capacity, muscular strength, and, to some degree, anaerobic capacity. However, their systematic review lacked meta-analytic methods for quantitatively assessing changes and did not examine the effects of moderating variables like training intensity, training duration, participant training status, cuff pressure, and cuff application phases. The investigation of these moderating variables has become a significant area of research in contemporary literature on BFR training[28]. Additionally, emerging published studies may provide grounds to re-evaluate existing conclusions regarding the effects of BFRIT on anaerobic performance in healthy adults. For example, Bourgeois et al. (2025) showed that BFR with HIIT enhanced sprinting ability and anaerobic power (3.6%↑) in male endurance athletes[29]. Given these inconsistencies and recently published studies, a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis are required to quantitatively synthesize existing evidence and assess the effects of BFRIT on aerobic capacity, anaerobic capacity, and muscle strength in healthy adults.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis sought to quantitatively evaluate the effects of BFRIT on aerobic capacity, anaerobic capacity, and muscular strength in healthy adults. Furthermore, it sought to explore the impact of various moderating factors, such as training intensity, training duration, participant training status, cuff pressure, and the application phase of the cuff. This meta-analysis offers a thorough and up-to-date synthesis of the existing evidence, providing valuable insights to inform practical training interventions and guide future research design.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This study was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [30], and it has been registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD42024609590).

Data sources, searches, and study selection

Two authors (K.Z. and X.Z.) independently searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, Sport-Discus, and EBSCO databases from inception to October 1, 2024, using a detailed search strategy (Table S2 in the Supplementary Materials). Manual searches of the reference lists in the related publications were also performed.

Studies were included if they met al.l the following criteria: (1) Population: participants were healthy adults with a mean age of ≥ 18 years, not using dietary supplements or medications; (2) Intervention: the intervention involved the use of elastic or non-elastic bands to compress arterial flow in the limbs during interval training, incorporating techniques such as pneumatic cuffs (e.g., KAATSU training), arterial occlusion pressure (AOP) regulation, or similar methods; interval training, characterized by repeated bouts of exercise alternated with periods of rest or recovery, can include either active or passive rest intervals [31]; (3) Comparison: the control group engaged in interval training without the use of compression techniques; (4) Outcome: the outcomes included aerobic capacity (e.g., V̇O2max), anaerobic capacity (e.g., power output), and muscular strength (e.g., force, torque); (5) Study design: the study design adopted randomized controlled trials (RCT) or controlled clinical trials to ensure methodological rigor in assessing intervention effects.

Studies were excluded if they were:1) Population: participants were adolescents or patients with conditions like knee osteoarthritis and cruciate ligament reconstruction;2) Intervention: the intervention was no interval training model or BFR combined with resistance training;3) Comparison: the control group either participated in non-interval exercises, like continuous aerobic exercise or resistance training, or did not exercise at all;4) Outcome: the outcome did not include aerobic, anaerobic, and muscle metrics; 5) Study design: case-control studies, review and conference articles, and animal trials; 6) Others: articles written in non-English languages and duplicate publications.

Data extraction, outcomes, and risk of bias assessment

The data extraction was carried out by two authors, Z.S. and X.Z., while a third party, T.J., provided additional verification. The extracted data included details such as the authors, publication year, sample size, participant characteristics, cuff application management protocol, exercise design, and outcome measures. The mean and standard deviation of each outcome in the post-tests were extracted for every included study. If any relevant data were missing, we contacted the authors to request it [32]. We obtained relevant data using WebPlotDigitizer (version 4.6) for studies when the authors could not be contacted for the data [34].

The outcome for aerobic capacity, as reported in the included studies, was V̇O2max during an incremental load exercise test or peak oxygen uptake (V̇O2peak) when V̇O2max was not available[35, 36]. For anaerobic capacity, the outcomes were peak power output (PPO) or mean power output (MPO) of the Wingate anaerobic test. For muscle strength, the outcome measure was the peak force or torque during knee extension. When data on knee extension were unavailable, alternative indicators, such as the peak force or torque during knee flexion or the one-repetition maximum (1RM), were used as substitutes.

Two investigators (T.J. and Z.X.) independently evaluated the risk of bias in the included studies using the Cochrane Collaboration’s risk of bias tool [37]. The assessment was based on the following criteria: (1) selection bias, (2) performance bias, (3) detection bias, (4) attrition bias, (5) reporting bias, and (6) other sources of bias. A study was classified as having a high risk of bias if one or more of these criteria indicated a high risk. Conversely, studies were categorized as having a low risk of bias if all criteria were rated as low. In cases where some criteria were rated as low and others as high, the studies were classified as having a moderate risk of bias.

Statistical analysis and grading the evidence

To assess the effect size (ES) of the intervention, we calculated the standardized mean difference (SMD; Hedges’ g) of the outcomes [38], accompanied by a 95% confidence interval (CI). The effect size was categorized as trivial (< 0.2), small (0.2 to 0.49), moderate (0.5 to 0.79), or large (>0.8) [39]. A meta-analysis using the inverse variance method was conducted using Stata version 18.0 (STATA Corp., College Station, TX). The heterogeneity was evaluated by assessing the inconsistency (I² statistic) of intervention effects across the trials. Heterogeneity quantification relied on I² statistics, with values of 75%, 50%, and 25% indicating high, moderate, and low heterogeneity, respectively. A random-effects model was employed to estimate the pooled effect, considering the heterogeneity among studies due to participant variations and intervention characteristics. The publication bias was evaluated using a funnel plot and Egger’s test. When significant heterogeneity was present, we conducted subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. If a significant asymmetry was detected, we used the Trim and Fill method to analyze the results. Meanwhile, we applied the Leave-One-Out method, which involved systematically removing individual studies to evaluate their impact on the overall results and to verify the robustness of the findings. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. To further support the interpretation of the results, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1) to assess whether the statistical power achieved was sufficient across the primary outcome measures.

Additionally, to evaluate the overall quality of evidence, we employed the GRADE (Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) approach. Eligible studies were assessed for risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, publication bias, and other relevant factors. The overall quality of evidence was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low for each outcome [40, 41]. Any disagreements between the two reviewers (Z.Z. and N.D.) were resolved through discussion, with input from a third reviewer (Z.S.) when necessary.

The flow diagram of screening was shown in Fig. 1. A total of 2053 relevant studies were retrieved (PubMed n = 1047, Web of Science n = 210, Embase n = 183, EBSCO n = 336, Sport-Discus n = 153, Cochrane Library n = 117, Manual search n = 7), and 2021 studies were excluded after reviewing the titles and abstracts. After the evaluation of full texts, 8 of the 32 studies were removed, and thus, 24 studies were included in the following analyses (Table 1).

Participant characteristics

A total of 607 healthy participants, with mean ages ranging from 20.1 to 51.9 years, were included in the analysis (Table 1). Regarding training status, 241 participants were classified as untrained, whereas 366 were identified as trained (e.g., cyclists or triathletes).

BFR protocol

The included studies employed various devices to apply pressure to the proximal thigh, including pneumatic cuffs [23, 24, 26, 29, 31, 42–55], elastic bands [25, 56, 57], and custom-designed devices [58, 59]. The cuffs ranged in width from 10 cm to 18 cm and in length from 67.5 cm to 200 cm. Pressure modes included fixed pressures (e.g., 230 mmHg) [23–26, 31, 44–46, 50–57], and incremental pressures [29, 42, 43, 47–49, 58, 59] (e.g., starting at 110% of systolic pressure and increasing by 10%). Based on a previous study [7], we categorized BFR pressure types into absolute and relative. Absolute pressure is defined as a fixed value (e.g., 200 mm Hg) applied uniformly to each individual, independent of limb size or vascular characteristics. Relative pressure was individualized and typically determined either as a percentage of the arterial occlusion pressure (AOP) (e.g., 30%–100% AOP) or based on the individual’s subjective perception of pressure (e.g., 7 out of 10 on a perceived pressure scale). Specifically, eight studies [26, 31, 44, 47, 49, 52, 58, 59]used absolute pressures and another 16 studies [23–25, 29, 42, 43, 45, 46, 48, 50, 51, 53–57]used relative pressures. BFR was applied during exercise in twenty studies [23–25, 29, 42–49, 51, 52, 54–59]and during rest intervals alone in two studies [26, 31], two studies [50, 53] examined the use of BFR during and between exercise. BFR protocols

Exercise protocol

Thirteen [23, 24, 26, 29, 31, 42, 43, 48, 50, 52–54, 57] included studies utilized BFR during HIIT (repeated bouts of exercise ≥ 80%HRmax, ≥ 60%V̇O2max, or “all-out effort”[27, 60–62] interspersed with rest periods. Ten [25, 44–46, 49, 51, 55, 56, 58, 59] included studies employed BFR in low- to moderate-intensity interval training (L-MIIT) (< 80%HRmax, < 60%V̇O2max), while one study[47] incorporated both HIIT and L-MIIT protocols. Eight of the included HIIT studies utilized pedal cycling, with five studies [23, 24, 50, 52, 53] utilizing submaximal intensity (80–90% of maximum) pedaling and three studies [26, 29, 31] focusing on 5–30 seconds of “all-out” pedaling. Additionally, two studies [42, 43] involved small-sided games (SSG), one study [42, 43, 54] examined maximal intensity sprinting during swim training, one study[57]utilized repetitive sprint training (RST) on a treadmill, and another one[48] incorporated RST into basketball training. Among the included L-MIIT studies, four studies [49, 55, 58, 59] focused on interval running, four studies on interval walking, and one study [25] on interval rowing; another study[51]used low-intensity interval swimming training. One study[47] investigated both HIIT and L-MIIT cycling. The intervention period lasted from 2 to 12 weeks, with training frequencies of 2 to 6 sessions each week.

Outcome measurements

Three studies [24, 48, 59] evaluated aerobic capacity, anaerobic capacity, and muscular strength. Aerobic capacity was assessed using metrics such as V̇O2max and MAP. Anaerobic capacity was measured through tests including the anaerobic performance test (suicide run), the Wingate anaerobic test, and a 30-second bicycle sprint test. Muscular strength was evaluated using the 1-RM for the deadlift and half-squat, as well as maximal voluntary isometric contraction force (MVIC) for knee extension. Six studies [26, 29, 31, 43, 50, 52] evaluated aerobic and anaerobic capacity. Aerobic capacity was evaluated using metrics such as V̇O2max, V̇O2peak, MAP, and running economy (RE). Anaerobic capacity was assessed through PPO, MPO, and minimum power. Additionally, eight studies [23, 25, 44–47, 49, 58] investigated aerobic capacity, measured by V̇O2max, and muscular strength. Muscular strength was evaluated through knee flexion and extension strength, submaximal repetitions, and MVIC. Four studies [51, 53, 55, 57] focused on assessing aerobic capacity. One study investigated [56] 100-meter sprint performance, MVIC, rate of force development (RFD), and muscle thickness. Another study [54] investigated Tethered force and mean speed during swimming. Additionally, one study [42] evaluated peak torque, muscle activation, and performance specific to futsal. Other outcomes, including heart rate, blood metabolite concentrations, ventilation, and body composition, were also investigated (Table 1).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to investigate the effects of BFRIT on aerobic capacity, anaerobic capacity, and muscle strength in healthy adults. The findings suggest that BFRIT effectively enhances V̇O2max and muscle strength but does not significantly improve PPO or MPO. Subgroup analyses identified several key moderating factors, including exercise intensity, training status, cuff pressure, and the phases of cuff application. These factors provide valuable insights for optimizing have demonstrated future BFRIT protocols.

Potential mechanisms of BFRIT on aerobic capacity and muscle strength

These findings are consistent with previous research [2, 6, 27] indicating that integrating BFR into HIIT may facilitate long-term aerobic capacity and muscle strength improvements. Evidence suggests that BFRIT enhances ventilation per minute [52, 59]and improves oxygenation responses in muscle tissue [63], pointing toward potential peripheral adaptations. Nevertheless, certain studies have reported no significant effects of BFRIT on skeletal muscle capillary density, mitochondrial protein levels, or oxygenated hemoglobin (O₂Hb) in participants [23, 26, 52], leading to the hypothesis that increases in V̇O2max might primarily arise from central adaptations. Tangchaisuriya et al. demonstrated that a 12-week BFRIT program enhanced maximal cardiac output in road cyclists [23]. Similarly, Willis et al. observed that BFRIT elicited notable changes in tissue perfusion, reflected by alterations in total hemoglobin concentration (Δ[tHb]), which may indicate improved cerebral blood flow regulation and augmented central anti-fatigue mechanisms [21]. Future research should investigate the relative contributions of central and peripheral adaptations to the observed improvements in aerobic capacity following BFRIT. Advanced imaging and molecular techniques should be employed to quantify changes in cardiovascular function, cerebral perfusion, and skeletal muscle structure. The enhancement of muscle strength through BFRIT is principally attributed to altered metabolic stress [43, 47, 58, 63, 64], which stimulates muscle hypertrophy and facilitates the promoted recruitment of fast-twitch muscle fibers [56, 65]. Studies have shown that BFR induces metabolic stress, which enhances the secretion of anabolic hormones like growth hormone (GH) and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) [46, 64, 66]. This process also activates signaling pathways, such as the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, that regulate muscle protein synthesis and the differentiation of microsatellite cells, ultimately promoting muscle hypertrophy[66, 67]. Amani-Shalamzari et al. demonstrated that incorporating BFR into futsal training may yield greater neuromuscular benefits by enhancing muscle activation and promoting elevated hormonal responses [42]. For team sports that demand high-intensity interval sprinting, such as soccer, basketball, and badminton, BFRIT may prove to be a more effective training method. The included studies have primarily focused on muscle hypertrophy and maximal strength performance, with molecular mechanisms yet to be investigated. The post-hoc power was 0.99, indicating high reliability and minimal risk of type II error.

Effects of moderating variables on BFRIT

Effects of training intensity and duration on aerobic capacity

BFR training has been considered most effective when combined with low-intensity exercise protocols [7]. This perspective is primarily supported by empirical studies indicating that intermittent BFR does not significantly augment the adaptive responses of limbs subjected to high-intensity training [68–70]. BFR combined with high-intensity training can increase discomfort and fatigue, potentially reducing adherence and disrupting muscle coordination [71]. Padulo et al. found that higher movement velocity resulted in significant bilateral deficits (BLD), indicating decreased neural control efficiency despite unchanged muscle excitation [72, 73]. HIIT combined with BFR-induced metabolic fatigue may impair neural integration and lead to minimal training improvements. Our findings align with previous perspectives, demonstrating that combining BFR with L-MIIT results in greater improvements in V̇O2max compared to BFR combined with HIIT. This discrepancy may stem from variations in control groups, as the L-MIIT control groups showed minimal improvements in V̇O2max [47], leading to a greater SMD in the BFR + L-MIIT condition. Oliveira et al.[47]directly compared four training protocols—L-MIIT (with or without BFR) and HIIT (with or without BFR)—and reported V̇O2max improvements in the BFR + L-MIIT (6%), HIIT (9%), and BFR + HIIT (6%) groups, with no significant differences among them. This observation suggests that BFR + HIIT enhances physiological stress but does not yield greater aerobic capacity gains. Additionally, subgroup analyses showed that extending the duration of interventions (e.g., ≥ 6 weeks) did not lead to further gains in aerobic capacity after BFRIT, which supports this observation. From a safety and load management perspective, BFR + L-MIIT may represent the most effective strategy for enhancing aerobic capacity in healthy adults. However, further studies are required to confirm this hypothesis. Notably, the Egger test indicated potential publication bias, but after the Trim and Fill method correction, the effect size remained significant. This suggests that the positive effect of BFRIT on aerobic capacity, though further validation is needed for its practical significance.

Effects of training intensity, training status, and duration on muscle strength

One interesting finding is that both BFR + L-MIIT and BFR + HIIT exhibited a moderate effect size regarding muscle strength benefits. This also accords with a systematic review by Jing et al. [74], which showed that BFR training can be used as an alternative training method for low-load resistance training to improve muscle hypertrophy and strength. The mechanisms underlying muscle strength enhancement may differ between the BFR + L-MIIT and BFR + HIIT. BFR + L-MIIT enhances muscle strength primarily through muscle fiber hypertrophy [23, 58], whereas BFR + HIIT predominantly facilitates the recruitment of type II muscle fibers [56]. For instance, McKee et al. found that “all-out” sprint interval training enhances the rate of force development (RFD) but does not induce muscle hypertrophy [24]. Li et al. reported that BFRIT significantly increased GH levels compared to controls [53]. Additionally, BFR training showed similar biological effects, including changes in gene expression, enhanced muscle protein synthesis, improved satellite cell activity, reduced myostatin levels, and decreased E3 ubiquitin ligase activity [69, 75–78]. These findings suggest potential biological mechanisms through which BFRIT may enhance muscular strength. However, it is essential to note that this evidence mainly comes from studies on BFR resistance training, and there is currently no direct evidence linking BFRIT to improvements in skeletal muscle function. Additionally, Elena Mainer-Pardos et al. (2024) found moderate improvements in vertical jump and sprint performance from resisted sprint training, similar to the strength gains seen with BFRIT in this study [79]. This finding highlights the need to compare different training methods to assess their effectiveness. Surprisingly, BFRIT was less effective in improving muscle strength among untrained individuals than trained athletes. This discrepancy may be due to untrained individuals’ lower tolerance for high-intensity physiological stress or their reduced neuromuscular efficiency, which may limit adaptation to BFRIT [47, 52, 57, 58, 80]. Conversely, BFR combined with HIIT may effectively challenge the physiological limits of athletes with higher fitness levels, promoting physical performance gains. BFRIT lasting more than six weeks is more effective for enhancing muscle strength. This indicates that BFRIT may improve physical performance by enhancing skeletal muscle function. A study by Linhares et al. (2023) found that low-load BFR resistance training effectively improved muscle strength and hypertrophy in older adults, along with enhancing protein synthesis and inflammatory markers (e.g., P3NP, IGF-1, IL-10) [81]. This indicates that BFR interventions hold significant potential for adaptation in the elderly. Future research should explore the efficacy of BFRIT across diverse populations, including older adults and individuals with reduced physical capacity, to better understand its potential applications and limitations.

Effects of pressure strategies on aerobic capacity and muscle strength

In early studies, cuff pressures were generally applied as a uniform absolute value for all participants, without regard for individual differences in physiological characteristics [7, 82, 83]. For example, the study has shown that individuals with larger limb circumferences require higher occlusion pressures [84]. In contemporary practice, individualized relative pressures—most commonly expressed as a percentage of arterial occlusion pressure (AOP), such as 40% AOP—are frequently employed. This approach is theoretically advantageous, as it accounts for individual variability and may enhance the overall effectiveness of training interventions. However, subgroup analyses have indicated that relative pressure does not confer significantly greater improvements in aerobic capacity or muscular strength compared to absolute pressure. These findings may be influenced by methodological inconsistencies across studies, particularly variations in the types of equipment used, which could result in inconsistent achievement of target occlusion pressures. Future research should consider the implementation of automated pressure regulation during interval exercise protocols to improve standardization. Additionally, the physical pressure applied to the limbs during BFRT may enhance proprioception in participants, which could improve postural motor control. This mechanism may be similar to the effect of core training in enhancing neuromuscular control in the lower limbs [85]. This implies that adaptations from BFRT also involve neuromuscular control. Future studies should explore neural drive and motor control to better understand the adaptations associated with BFR training. Subgroup analyses have indicated that the progressive application of incremental pressure may be beneficial for enhancing individual aerobic capacity; however, it does not appear to contribute to improvements in muscular strength. This inconsistency may be due to variations in the way pressure implementation protocols are applied. For example, Oliveira et al. [47] used a protocol starting with an initial pressure of 140 mmHg, followed by 20 mmHg increments until a maximum of 200 mmHg was achieved. In contrast, Amani-Shalamzari et al. [42, 43] initiated the pressure at 110% of systolic blood pressure, with a 10% increase every two sessions, culminating with the final session at the initial pressure level. Notably, one study found that female participants showed progressive improvements in aerobic capacity, anaerobic performance, and muscle strength under a constant training load as incremental pressure increased [86]. Furthermore, subgroup analyses revealed that the application of BFR during the rest interval phase may elicit effects comparable to those observed when BFR is combined with exercise. Currently, this protocol (BFR during the rest interval only) is mainly used during the interval phase of sprint interval training at maximal effort [26, 31], suggesting that BFR could serve as a passive training method to enhance aerobic capacity. To enhance the training stimulus, progressively increase the intensity of BFRIT sessions by adjusting occlusion pressure, exercise intensity, or cuff application. Given the numerous variables associated with BFR and interval training, further research is needed to standardize and provide evidence-based guidelines for the effective application of BFR methodology.

Effects of interval types and participant characteristics on aerobic and muscle strength

It is important to note that the interval training types may affect the effectiveness of BFRIT.

In terms of aerobic capacity, applying BFR to high-intensity sprint-type intervals (e.g., sprint interval training [SIT]) usually results in larger V̇O2max gains [26, 31, 48]. In contrast, submaximal-intensity intervals (e.g., short intervals [SI] or SSG), although they had larger V̇O2max gains, did not differ significantly from the CON group [43, 57].Various types of interval training (e.g., long intervals [LI], SSG, SI, and RST) have demonstrated enhancement in muscle strength [43, 44, 48], 56).This indicates that BFRIT could be applied to all types of interval training to improve skeletal muscle function. The studies included did not adequately consider gender in response to hypoxic stimulation. Some studies with males showed a significant increase in V̇O2max [31, 46, 59], while other studies with mixed male and female showed no improvement[52, 57]. This inconsistency may arise from hormonal differences between males and females, which affect training results. Additionally, participants were adults aged 21.1 to 51.9 years, which led to a gap in research targeting older adults and limiting the applicability of the results to this population. Further studies, which take gender and age variables into account, will need to be undertaken.

Effects of BFRIT on anaerobic capacity

In contrast to the findings reported by Chua et al. in their systematic review [27], the current analysis did not observe a statistically significant enhancement in anaerobic capacity following BFRIT. The discrepancy may stem from BFRIT-specific hemodynamic limitations that restrict the recruitment of fast muscle fibers, impairing maximal power output and anaerobic adaptation [87]. Some studies used uniform or fixed pressure values [24, 31, 50], leading to varying levels of muscle restriction. Most interventions lasted only 2 to 6 weeks and were insufficient for significant adaptations [88]. For instance, Elgammal, Taylor, and Mitchell et al. found no improvements in PPO or performance in short-term interventions [26, 31, 48]. Behringer et al. observed significant gains in 100-meter performance after a 6-week intervention [56]. For high-level individuals, BFRIT may not provide enough stimulus to surpass the existing limits of anaerobic metabolism and neuromuscular adaptations, leading to a plateau effect. Recent studies have primarily used laboratory tests, such as the Wingate test, demonstrating good reliability. However, these tests may not accurately reflect real athletic situations, potentially underestimating the true effectiveness of BFRIT. Gadea-Uribarri et al. [89] suggest that wearable devices (e.g., IMUs) should be combined with task-specific tests in the future to improve the assessment’s ecological validity. The post-hoc power was 0.10 for PPO and 0.30 for MPO, indicating low reliability and a high risk of type II error. Therefore, these non-significant results should be interpreted cautiously and need validation in larger trials.

Practical implications

The findings suggest that BFRIT can enhance fitness without high mechanical loads, making it suitable for those with time constraints, limited equipment, or who cannot handle high-intensity training, such as beginners. Trained individuals benefit more from BFRIT, which improves muscle metabolism and aerobic adaptations without increasing training load. It can also serve as a pre-transitional program for those with a weaker fitness base, paving the way for future high-intensity training. Subgroup analyses indicate better adaptation with intervention durations of ≥ 6 weeks, particularly using relative AOP and phased pressurization. Although BFRIT shows small to moderate gains in V̇O2max and strength, inconsistencies in protocols and outcomes across studies limit the reliability of these findings. Therefore, the clinical significance of these findings remains to be confirmed. We propose standardized BFRIT recommendations: For improving aerobic capacity, combine L-MIIT with individualized occlusion pressures, gradually increasing them with training adaptations. For strength outcomes, interventions should last over six weeks with progressive pressure dosing. Untrained individuals should use BFR + L-MIIT, while trained populations may opt for BFR + HIIT.

Limitations

Several limitations in the current analysis should be noted. This meta-analysis did not provide strong evidence for the enhancement of anaerobic capacity by BFRIT. This could be due to the small sample size and the limited number of studies included, which weakened the reliability of the statistical analysis. While subgroup analysis can offer a more detailed view of the research findings, it is important to interpret these results with caution. This is because some comparisons may be limited by statistical efficiency and are considered exploratory analyses rather than central to this study. Additionally, most interventions lasted less than six weeks, making it hard to achieve significant anaerobic adaptations. Due to the limited sample size, factors such as gender and age could not be thoroughly examined. Future studies should include a larger number of older adults and females to enhance understanding of how age and gender affect physiological adaptations to BFRIT. The studies included in this meta-analysis also faced inconsistencies in BFR protocols concerning equipment and pressure settings, which complicate the interpretation of results. Future research should standardize these factors, using individualized pressure settings based on each participant’s AOP.

Conclusions

The findings of this review indicate that BFRIT is beneficial for enhancing aerobic capacity and muscle strength in healthy adults; however, the evidence on BFRIT effects on anaerobic capacity is unclear due to small sample sizes and limited statistical power in current studies. This review highlights several key moderating factors influencing the effectiveness of BFRIT, including training intensity, duration, status, cuff pressure, and the phases of cuff application. Future research should focus on optimizing these variables to enhance the efficacy and applicability of BFRIT across different populations and training modalities. Establishing standardized protocols will be essential for maximizing the benefits of BFRIT in both athletic performance and rehabilitation settings.
