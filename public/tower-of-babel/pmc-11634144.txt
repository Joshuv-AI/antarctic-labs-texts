Dynamic gene expression pattern in zebrafish gallbladder: an experimental study
Authors: Pranay Tanwar, Debashruti Bhattacharya, Abhilash Dasari, Manthan Bijwe, Rashmi Rana, Ishaan Gupta, Shilpi Minocha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is one of the common biliary tract malignancies that is often associated with late presentation of clinical symptoms resulting in poor prognosis, thereby considerably increasing the mortality rate. Geographic and ethnic variations are one of the major causes of GBC incidence. However, in addition to genetic susceptibility to GBC, other factors like age, obesity, sex, and exposure to certain heavy chemicals may also lead to the incidence of GBC. Most of GBC research has so far used traditional cell culture systems. Recently, newer approaches using 2D and 3D cultures, xenograft models, and organoids are also becoming popular. However, whole organism model systems for GBC research have not yet been established. In this study, the authors propose the use of zebrafish as a model organism to study GBC. However, the zebrafish gallbladder is relatively unexplored besides the anatomical features. Here, the authors unravel and then compare the unique transcriptomic profile of zebrafish gallbladder with humans to identify genes consistently expressed in both species. The authors provide a comprehensive list of all gallbladder specific genes in zebrafish that also have their orthologous counterparts in humans. Therefore, they can be used as potential biomarkers for gallbladder transcriptomic profile. To the best of our knowledge, this is the only study so far to provide a complete genomic expression profile of zebrafish gallbladder along with an exhaustive cross-species comparison, thus paving the path for using zebrafish as a model organism to study GBC and help identify novel biomarkers for its early detection and diagnosis.

Gallbladder cancer (GBC), one of the common biliary tract malignancies, is often associated with late presentation of clinical symptoms resulting in poor prognosis, thereby escalating the mortality rate to 1.7%1,2. The incidence of this multifactorial disease is characterized by geographic and ethnic variations in addition to other predisposing factors like age, sex, and family history1. Chronic infections like gallbladder polyps, primary sclerosis cholangitis, congenital biliary cysts, and obesity-related metabolic disorders also heighten the risk of GBC2. The most effective treatment strategy for this disease involves radial resection of gallbladder2. However, most patients are diagnosed at a very advanced metastasized stage when surgery becomes infeasible. Alternative nonsurgical treatment options include chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapies like immune-mediated, vaccine-mediated, and nanoparticle-mediated biotherapies3. Traditionally, most GBC research has so far utilized cell culture systems to study the molecular underpinnings of GBC development, pathogenesis and treatment strategies. Several cell lines, such as G-415, GB-d1, ZJU-0430, and KMG-A, have been used extensively for high-throughput in vitro drug screening4. However, the inability of these 2D models to replicate the in vivo tumor microenvironment effectively has led to the emergence of 3D co-culture systems like organoids and spheroids5. Xenograft models, adept in replicating characteristic histological and molecular features of GBC, have recently become the most sought-after models for drug sensitivity and personalized drug development studies6. In addition, stem cell therapies involving patient-specific pluripotent stem cells (PSPSC) are also gaining traction in the field of personalized treatment strategies7. Albeit these models have proven to be valuable in elucidating the pathophysiology of this multifaceted disease, there exists no in vivo model for GBC research, making it difficult to study the intricate complexities of this disease in its native tumor microenvironment.

Here, we propose the use of zebrafish as a model organism to study GBC. The genome of this little fish consists of 26,206 protein-coding genes and shares significant homology with the human genome8. Seventy-one percent of human genes are known to have at least one orthologue in zebrafish. Furthermore, 82% of all human disease related genes and most signaling pathways are conserved in zebrafish, making it a suitable model organism to recapitulate several complex aspects of human diseases8. Although zebrafish has been extensively used to study neuroblastomas, pancreatic adenocarcinomas, leukemia, and hepatocellular carcinomas, no GBC model has been developed so far in zebrafish9. In fact, the zebrafish gallbladder is rather unexplored besides its anatomical features. Therefore, we sought to investigate the transcriptomic profile of the zebrafish gallbladder and then compare this unique transcriptomic profile with that of the human gallbladder to identify genes consistently expressed in both species.

In this study, we conducted a comparative transcriptomic analysis of human and zebrafish gallbladders and livers to unravel the unique gallbladder- specific transcriptomic profile and to identify both conserved and species-specific gene expression patterns. Gallbladder samples were collected from adult zebrafish (n=4, each sample contained three gallbladders pooled together) and human patients (n=28) followed by RNA isolation and sequencing. Additionally, datasets for 3 zebrafish (Bio project ID: PRJNA714665) and 10 human liver RNA samples (Bio project ID: PRJNA558102) from the NCBI SRA database were also analyzed. Following sequencing, the raw data reads were processed through a standardized pipeline of quality assessment, trimming to exclude low-quality reads, alignment with reference genome, differential gene expression analysis10, and finally, functional annotation through gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis11.

Upon comparing the zebrafish and human liver and gallbladder transcriptomic profiles, we found distinct gene expression patterns, thus highlighting significant differences in the expression profiles of the two adjacently placed and intimately linked organs (Supplementary Fig 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D574). In comparison to the liver tissue, 1329 genes were found to be downregulated, and 2435 genes were upregulated in the zebrafish gallbladder (Fig. 1). Likewise, 2162 downregulated and 7329 upregulated genes were identified in human gallbladder samples compared to liver (Supplementary tables 1, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D575 and 2, Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D576). This difference in gene expression patterns further attests the disparate functional and regulatory roles played by the two organs.

Next, we explored the conservation of gene expression between human and zebrafish gallbladders using Ensembl BioMart. A comprehensive catalog of 13,200 orthologous genes specific to zebrafish and humans was compiled and subsequently used for cross-species comparison. We identified a subset of 295 downregulated and 491 upregulated genes (Fig. 1) with similar expression profiles across both species, among the orthologous genes. The differentially expressed genes from both human and zebrafish gallbladders were then subjected to GO and KEGG pathway analysis, to infer the associated biological processes (Fig. 2). Notably, genes associated with signaling pathways and membrane transport were observed to be upregulated in human gallbladders, indicating a pronounced response to extracellular signals and a complex regulation of bile secretion. Zebrafish, on the other hand, exhibited upregulation of genes involved in cell cycle, DNA replication, and cytoskeleton organization, indicative of rapid cellular proliferation. Downregulated genes in humans were associated with nucleotide metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, thermogenesis, complement cascades, and small molecule biosynthesis pathways, suggesting towards a lower metabolic activity in the gallbladder compared to the liver. Zebrafish, alternatively, showed decreased expression of lipid metabolism-related genes, possibly reflecting a species-specific metabolic adaptation. Interestingly, the conserved dysregulated orthologous genes between humans and zebrafish were found to cluster together (Fig. 2, Supplementary Tables 3, Supplemental Digital Content 4, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D577 and 4, Supplemental Digital Content 5, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D578). GO and KEGG pathway analysis revealed the upregulated genes in both the species to be involved in G-protein coupled receptor signaling pathways, calcium signaling, focal adhesion, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and motor proteins. These pathways are pivotal for cellular communication and response to external stimuli, suggesting a conserved mechanism for bile acid regulation and sensory functions of the gallbladder across species. The downregulated genes in both human and zebrafish gallbladders, on the contrary, were associated with the PPAR signaling pathway, co-factor biosynthesis, and lipid and amino acid catabolism, suggesting a conserved basal metabolic feature in gallbladder function, emphasizing the organ’s role in energy conservation and nutrient recycling across both species. Moreover, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and GO analysis of cellular components and molecular functions also revealed similar functional enrichment patterns in both species for upregulated and downregulated genes (Supplementary Figs 2–4, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D574).

Finally, we validated the findings from our bioinformatic analysis by performing quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) on a selection of gallbladder-specific and liver-specific genes (list of primers in Supplementary Fig 5, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D574). We chose a subset of six candidate genes, out of which four genes were gallbladder-specific (and known to be implicated in GBC), while two were liver specific. qPCR results revealed significant increase in the mRNA levels of gall-bladder specific genes cep55l, esrp1, nek2, and myb compared to the liver as observed in the transcriptomic data. The validation of these conserved expression patterns further reinforces our bioinformatic analysis’s robustness and underscores these genes’ relevance in gallbladder-specific functions (Fig. 3). Moreover, we observed a significant decrease in the expression of liver-enriched genes gapdh (Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) and fabp10a (Fatty Acid Binding Protein 10a) in the gallbladder relative to the liver, further confirming the unique transcriptomic profile of the gallbladder.

In summary, our findings reveal the unique transcriptomic profile of zebrafish gallbladder and establish a strong resemblance with the human gallbladder gene expression pattern, thus making zebrafish a reliable and clinically relevant model for GBC research. The conserved expression of a distinct set of genes across both species highlights their potential utility as biomarkers for gallbladder health and disease. Additional research could explore regulatory mechanisms and the functional roles of these genes in gallbladder physiology and pathology, which could ultimately lead to better diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
