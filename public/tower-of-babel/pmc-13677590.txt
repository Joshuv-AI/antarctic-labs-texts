Epstein–Barr virus-associated T/NK-cell lymphoproliferative diseases with heterozygous IL10RA variants: two cases with pedigree analysis and a literature review
Authors: Yixin YAN, Ge SONG, Liang HUANG, Wenjie XIONG, Yao YAO, Yujia ZHAI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo summarise the clinical and familial characteristics of two patients with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-associated T/NK-cell lymphoproliferative diseases carrying heterozygous IL10RA variants and to explore their potential genetic significance.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed the clinical courses, pathological findings, flow cytometry results, EBV detection in sorted cell populations, genetic testing results, and familial segregation data of the two patients, supplemented by a review of the relevant literature.ResultsPatient 1 was a 34-year-old man who presented with fever, gastrointestinal bleeding, and multiple colonic ulcers. EBV was predominantly detected in NK cells, followed by persistent expansion of abnormal NK cells, bone marrow involvement, and progression to NK/T-cell lymphoma complicated by haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Genetic testing identified a heterozygous IL10RA c.85 C>G (p.Pro29Ala) variant, which was also detected in his mother and one sister. Patient 2 was a 32-year-old man with long-standing hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenia, and abnormal liver function. Histopathological examination and EBV testing of sorted cell populations indicated predominant T-cell involvement, with concurrent NK-cell involvement. He carried a heterozygous IL10RA c.301 C>T (p.Arg101Trp) variant, which was also identified in his mother. The p.Pro29Ala variant is currently classified as a variant of uncertain significance, whereas p.Arg101Trp is a known pathogenic allele associated with autosomal recessive IL10RA-related disease.ConclusionBoth adult patients with EBV-associated T/NK-cell lymphoproliferative diseases carried a single heterozygous missense variant in IL10RA. However, the same variants were also present in family members without a similar phenotype, and the available evidence is insufficient to establish a direct causal relationship. IL10RA genetic variation warrants further investigation as a potential host susceptibility factor for EBV-associated T/NK-cell diseases.Keywords: Epstein–Barr virus, T/NK cells, Lymphoproliferative disease, IL10RA

Introduction

Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection is highly prevalent in the general population, with B lymphocytes serving as the principal reservoir for viral latency. In a small subset of individuals, however, EBV may persist in T cells or NK cells, giving rise to a heterogeneous group of EBV-associated T/NK-cell lymphoproliferative diseases with diverse clinical and biological features. These disorders may present with persistent active EBV infection, hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenia, tissue or organ infiltration, and clonal expansion of abnormal T/NK cells; some patients may subsequently develop haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) or progress to aggressive T/NK-cell neoplasms [1], [2], [3]. Compared with classical EBV-associated B-cell diseases, the host determinants underlying long-term persistence and clonal expansion of EBV-infected T/NK cells remain incompletely understood.

Previous studies of susceptibility to EBV infection have demonstrated an important role for host genetic background in antiviral immune control. A range of monogenic defects affecting T-cell or NK-cell activation, cytotoxic function, or immune regulation have been associated with persistent EBV viraemia, EBV-associated HLH, or lymphoproliferative disease, indicating that the development of EBV-associated disorders depends not only on viral factors but also on host immune homeostasis [4]. The IL-10/IL-10R pathway is another important regulatory axis involved in maintaining immune tolerance and limiting excessive inflammatory responses. Biallelic loss-of-function variants in IL10RA or IL10RB are established causes of very-early-onset inflammatory bowel disease and have also been associated with susceptibility to lymphoma [5], [6], [7]. In addition, associations between host IL10 polymorphisms and susceptibility to EBV infection or EBV-associated HLH have been reported [4], [8], [9]. However, previous studies have focused predominantly on severe biallelic IL-10R deficiency or IL10 promoter polymorphisms, and evidence remains insufficient to determine whether a single heterozygous IL10RA variant may be associated with EBV-associated T/NK-cell lymphoproliferative diseases in adults.

Here, we report two unrelated adult patients with EBV-associated T/NK-cell lymphoproliferative diseases. In both patients, cell-sorting or histopathological evidence demonstrated predominant EBV involvement of T/NK cells. Heterozygous IL10RA variants, c.85 C>G (p.Pro29Ala) and c.301 C>T (p.Arg101Trp), were identified in the two patients, respectively, and the same variants were also detected in family members without similar clinical phenotypes. We systematically describe the clinical courses, EBV-infected cell profiles, pathological findings, and genetic characteristics of these two patients and, in the context of previous studies on IL-10/IL-10R deficiency, host susceptibility to EBV, and EBV-associated T/NK-cell diseases, discuss the clinical significance of these IL10RA variants and their potential relevance to host susceptibility.

Both probands were male and presented with persistent Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-associated T/NK-cell lymphoproliferative diseases. Heterozygous IL10RA variants were identified through genetic testing and subsequent familial investigations. Family 1

Proband 1 was a male patient who developed symptoms at 34 years of age. In May 2020, he developed intermittent fever without an apparent precipitating cause, with a maximum temperature of 40°C, followed by melena. Laboratory testing revealed a haemoglobin level of 89 g/L, a platelet count of 84 × 109/L, a peripheral blood EBV-DNA level of 3.78 × 105 copies/mL, and a serum ferritin level of 909.9 ng/mL. Gastroscopy revealed no significant abnormalities, whereas colonoscopy demonstrated multiple colonic ulcers. Inflammatory bowel disease or infectious colitis was initially suspected; however, recurrent fever persisted despite anti-infective and antiviral therapy, intravenous immunoglobulin, and supportive treatment. In June 2020, bone marrow cytology demonstrated an increased proportion of abnormal lymphocytes, while bone marrow biopsy showed markedly active haematopoiesis with an increased proportion of mature lymphocytes. Bone marrow flow cytometry identified an aberrant NK-cell population accounting for approximately 0.9% of nucleated cells. These cells expressed CD159a and CD94, with weak CD7 expression in a subset, but lacked CD57 and CD161 expression and showed low expression of Ki-67 and perforin. No monoclonal rearrangement was detected by Ig/TCR gene rearrangement analysis. Further assessment of NK-cell function showed CD107a positivity of 3.5% at baseline and 26.5% after stimulation, with an NK-cell cytotoxic activity of 6.1%; all parameters were below their respective reference ranges. EBV testing following peripheral blood cell sorting demonstrated predominant EBV detection in NK cells, with an EBV copy number of 2.21 × 106 per 2 × 105 cells in the sorted NK-cell fraction.

Contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) demonstrated segmental bowel wall thickening involving the ileocaecal region, ascending colon, and sigmoid colon. PET/CT showed focal bowel wall thickening with increased metabolic activity in the ascending colon and ileocaecal region, accompanied by an increased number of small adjacent lymph nodes. Colonoscopic biopsy demonstrated an EBV-positive T/NK-cell proliferative lesion. In conjunction with the clinical findings, the pathological findings were considered consistent with extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (early-stage, small-cell type), although an EBV-positive T/NK-cell lymphoproliferative disease could not be completely excluded. The patient subsequently received L-GemOx and P-GemOx regimens in combination with a PD-1 inhibitor. Repeat colonoscopy in August 2020 revealed no significant abnormalities, and plasma EBV-DNA became undetectable. PD-1 inhibitor maintenance therapy was subsequently continued. The patient had no previous history suggestive of chronic enteritis, including recurrent abdominal pain, diarrhoea, mucopurulent or bloody stools, or perianal disease. No recurrent diarrhoea or gastrointestinal bleeding occurred after treatment. Together with the subsequent colonoscopic findings, the overall clinical course was not consistent with typical inflammatory bowel disease. Although plasma EBV-DNA temporarily became undetectable, EBV-DNA remained persistently detectable in peripheral blood mononuclear cells and reached 2.38 × 104 copies/mL in June 2021, indicating persistent cell-associated EBV.

From September 2023, the patient again developed intermittent fever, with a poor response to anti-infective treatment. In February 2024, bone marrow cytology showed that abnormal lymphocytes accounted for 6% of cells, while flow cytometry demonstrated an aberrant NK-cell population comprising approximately 30.3% of nucleated cells, representing a marked increase compared with the 2020 findings. Concurrent bone marrow biopsy showed relatively active haematopoiesis and a substantial increase in abnormal lymphocytes expressing CD3−, CD7+, CD20−, and CD56+. The pathological findings were considered indicative of bone marrow involvement by NK/T-cell lymphoma. The patient subsequently received HLH-94 therapy in combination with the P-GemOx regimen, followed by additional chemotherapy. In May 2024, he was readmitted with fever and dyspnoea. Serum ferritin exceeded 50,000 μg/L, IL-2R exceeded 7500 U/mL, IL-10 exceeded 1000 pg/mL, and EBV-DNA in peripheral blood mononuclear cells increased to 2.48 × 106 copies/mL. He subsequently developed severe cytopenia, coagulopathy, liver failure, pulmonary haemorrhage, and respiratory and circulatory failure. EBV-associated NK-cell neoplasia complicated by haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) was diagnosed clinically, and the patient ultimately died of multiple organ failure.

Genetic testing identified a heterozygous missense IL10RA variant, NM_001558.4:c.85 C>G (p.Pro29Ala; rs201980658), in the proband. ClinVar currently classifies this variant as a variant of uncertain significance. The variant is rare in gnomAD v4.1.1, with no homozygous individuals reported. Subsequent familial testing showed that his mother and one sister carried the same heterozygous IL10RA variant, whereas another sister, one brother, and his daughter did not carry the variant (Fig. 1). Information on the location of this variant within the IL-10Rα protein, its population frequency, and database annotations is summarised in Table 2. No definite family history of a similar disorder was documented.

Family 2

Proband 2 was a 32-year-old man. Hepatosplenomegaly accompanied by abnormal liver function was first detected during a routine medical examination approximately 7 years earlier, but the underlying cause remained undetermined. In August 2025, repeat liver function testing showed a γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT) level of 217 U/L and an alkaline phosphatase (ALP) level of 787 U/L. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed diffuse echogenic abnormalities of the hepatic parenchyma, marked splenomegaly, and splenic vein dilatation. Repeat ultrasonography in September 2025 demonstrated dilatation of the portal, superior mesenteric, and splenic veins, while abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, and dilatation of the portal venous system. PET/CT demonstrated marked enlargement of the liver and spleen, with multiple small lymph nodes in the cervical, mediastinal, retroperitoneal, pelvic, and inguinal regions, some of which showed mildly increased metabolic activity. Peripheral blood smears demonstrated an increased proportion of granular lymphocytes. Liver biopsy revealed scattered or focal necrosis within the hepatic lobules, with scattered or clustered lymphocytes in the portal areas. Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated scattered or clustered CD3-positive cells, only a small number of CD20-positive and CD56-positive cells, and a Ki-67 proliferation index of approximately 5%. EBER in situ hybridisation demonstrated scattered or clustered positive cells. Dual staining for CD3/EBER revealed scattered or small clusters of double-positive cells, whereas CD20/EBER dual staining was negative, indicating predominant EBV infection of T cells. The pathological findings were consistent with EBV-positive T/NK-cell lymphoproliferative disease.

In December 2025, a complete blood count showed a white blood cell count of 2.07 × 109/L, a haemoglobin level of 104 g/L, and a platelet count of 105 × 109/L. Abdominal ultrasonography showed that the spleen measured approximately 33 cm in longitudinal diameter and 7.6 cm in thickness. Peripheral blood EBV-DNA was 3.93 × 105 copies/mL. Subsequent EBV quantification following sorting of T, B, and NK cells showed EBV-DNA levels of 807 copies/mL in B cells, 379,443 copies/mL in T cells, and 111,553 copies/mL in NK cells, indicating predominant EBV involvement of T cells with concurrent NK-cell involvement. Peripheral blood flow cytometry identified a clonal CD3dimCD4 + T-cell population accounting for approximately 3.17% of all nucleated cells and 27.17% of lymphocytes. These cells expressed CD2, CD4, CD26, and CD45RO; partially expressed CD38; showed weak expression of CD3 and CD5; and lacked expression of CD8, CD7, CD56, CD16, CD57, CD30, CD10, and PD-1. Concurrent bone marrow morphology and flow cytometry revealed no definite abnormal clonal population, while bone marrow biopsy showed only a small number of scattered EBER-positive cells.

The patient sequentially received a PD-1 inhibitor in combination with the GemOx regimen and DEP combined with AZA; however, neither splenic enlargement nor EBV load showed substantial improvement. Treatment was therefore switched to the EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat in combination with the IE chemotherapy regimen. During follow-up, EBV-DNA levels in T cells and NK cells remained consistently higher than those in B cells. In March 2026, sorted-cell testing showed EBV-DNA levels of 433, 105,914, and 79,918 copies/mL in B, T, and NK cells, respectively. Although these levels had decreased compared with pretreatment values, EBV infection remained predominantly associated with T/NK cells. By June 2026, peripheral blood EBV-DNA had fallen below the detection limit; however, the patient continued to have marked hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenia, and abnormal liver function and remained on EZH2 inhibitor therapy with ongoing follow-up.

Whole-exome sequencing identified a heterozygous IL10RA variant, NM_001558.4:c.301 C>T (p.Arg101Trp), in the proband. Testing of 43 genes associated with HLH identified no definite pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants. His mother, who underwent genetic testing as a potential haematopoietic stem cell donor, was also found to carry the heterozygous IL10RA c.301 C>T (p.Arg101Trp) variant, with a variant allele frequency of 52.71% (Fig. 1). Information on the location of this variant within the IL-10Rα protein, its population frequency, and database annotations is summarised in Table 2. Both parents were alive, and no definite family history of a similar disorder was documented.

Discussion

IL-10 is a key regulatory cytokine involved in maintaining intestinal mucosal and systemic immune homeostasis. Upon binding to the heterodimeric IL-10 receptor, composed of IL-10Rα and IL-10Rβ, IL-10 exerts its anti-inflammatory effects predominantly through the JAK1/TYK2–STAT3 signalling pathway. IL-10Rα is expressed primarily on haematopoietic cells, whereas IL-10Rβ has a broader tissue distribution [10]. Glocker et al. first demonstrated in humans that homozygous loss-of-function variants in IL10RA or IL10RB can abolish STAT3 phosphorylation following IL-10 stimulation and cause severe, treatment-refractory enterocolitis beginning in infancy [5]. Kotlarz et al. subsequently established the genetic and functional association between impaired IL-10 signalling and infantile inflammatory bowel disease in a larger cohort of patients with biallelic IL10RA or IL10RB deficiency [6]. A systematic review published in 2022 included 286 patients with IL-10/IL-10R deficiency and reported a median age at disease onset of approximately 1 month; c.301 C>T (p.R101W) and c.537 G>A (p.T179T) were among the most frequently reported IL10RA variants [11]. In the present study, we describe two unrelated adults with EBV-associated T/NK-cell lymphoproliferative diseases. Although their clinical manifestations and EBV-infected cell profiles differed, both patients, as well as some of their family members, carried heterozygous IL10RA variants. Patient 1 presented with recurrent fever, gastrointestinal bleeding, and multiple intestinal ulcers. Cell-sorting studies indicated predominant EBV involvement of NK cells, followed by persistent expansion of abnormal NK cells, bone marrow involvement, and eventual progression to NK/T-cell lymphoma complicated by haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Patient 2 presented primarily with long-standing hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenia, and abnormal liver function. Dual EBER/CD3 staining of liver tissue and peripheral blood cell-sorting studies both indicated predominant EBV involvement of T cells, with concurrent NK-cell involvement and an abnormal clonal CD4 + T-cell population. The two patients carried heterozygous IL10RA c.85 C>G (p.Pro29Ala) and c.301 C>T (p.Arg101Trp) variants, respectively, and the same variants were also identified in family members without similar clinical phenotypes. These findings do not support interpreting a single heterozygous IL10RA variant as the genetic cause of disease; rather, they raise the possibility that partial genetic variation affecting IL-10/IL-10R signalling may contribute to a host genetic background that influences the persistence and clonal expansion of EBV-infected T/NK cells.

The strength of the available evidence differs substantially between the two IL10RA variants identified in our patients. The IL10RA c.301 C>T (p.Arg101Trp; R101W) variant carried by Patient 2 is a well-established pathogenic allele and has been reported as a recurrent variant among Chinese patients with very-early-onset inflammatory bowel disease (VEO-IBD) associated with IL-10Rα deficiency [12]. In a 2017 study by the Chinese VEO-IBD Collaboration Group, numerous compound heterozygous and homozygous IL10RA genotypes were identified in children with IL-10/IL-10R deficiency, with c.301 C>T (p.R101W) and c.537 G>A (p.T179T) among the most frequently observed variants [13]. Subsequent systematic structural and functional studies further demonstrated that R101W represents an important pathogenic IL10RA allele in East Asian populations, particularly among Chinese patients [14]. In ClinVar (Variation ID: 39432), IL10RA c.301 C>T (p.Arg101Trp; R101W) is currently classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic for autosomal recessive inflammatory bowel disease 28 (IBD28; OMIM 613148), with three submitters classifying it as pathogenic and one as likely pathogenic. The variant has been repeatedly identified in patients with VEO-IBD in either the homozygous state or in compound heterozygosity with another pathogenic variant [12], [15], [16]. Thus, substantial genetic evidence supports R101W as a pathogenic allele underlying autosomal recessive IL10RA-related disease. IL-10Rα comprises 578 amino acids, with an extracellular domain spanning residues 22–235; R101 is located within a β-sheet structure encompassing residues 96–104 of the extracellular region [14], [17]. Mao et al. identified compound heterozygous IL10RA variants, including R101W, in a patient with neonatal-onset Crohn's disease and demonstrated that variants affecting the extracellular region do not necessarily abolish receptor expression or completely prevent IL-10 binding, but may impair downstream signalling following receptor activation, leading to abnormal IL-10Rα phosphorylation, impaired STAT3 activation, and reduced anti-inflammatory activity of IL-10 [18]. Aschenbrenner et al. subsequently performed a systematic functional assessment of multiple IL10RA missense variants and found that R101W disrupted the secondary structure of the extracellular region and reduced IL-10-induced STAT3 phosphorylation [14]. The c.85 C>G (p.Pro29Ala; P29A) variant carried by Patient 1 is likewise located within the extracellular domain of IL-10Rα, but the available clinical and functional evidence is considerably weaker than that supporting R101W. ClinVar currently classifies P29A as a variant of uncertain significance for IBD28, based on a single submission [19]. The variant is extremely rare in gnomAD v4.1.1, with an overall allele frequency of 3.717 × 10-⁶ and no reported homozygous individuals [20]. To date, P29A has not been repeatedly reported in patients with classical IL10RA deficiency, and no direct functional studies of this variant are available.

Nevertheless, whether the heterozygous state is entirely functionally neutral warrants further consideration. Aschenbrenner et al. compared IL-10 pathway function among healthy non-carriers, healthy heterozygous carriers of pathogenic IL10RA variants, and patients with biallelic deficiency. As expected, patients with biallelic deficiency showed a marked loss of STAT3 phosphorylation following IL-10 stimulation. Notably, however, healthy heterozygous carriers, including individuals carrying R101W, also exhibited right-shifted dose–response curves and attenuated STAT3 phosphorylation responses in peripheral blood mononuclear cells, T cells, and monocytes [14]. These findings support the established concept that classical IL10RA deficiency is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic loss of function, while also suggesting that heterozygous carriage of certain pathogenic alleles may be associated with measurable alterations in IL-10 signalling.

The long-term clinical phenotype of IL-10R deficiency is not restricted to intestinal inflammation. Neven et al. reported five children with IL-10RA or IL-10RB deficiency who developed B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma as early as 5–6 years of age. These tumours were of germinal-centre B-cell origin and exhibited features resembling diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [7]. Subsequent larger cohort studies further suggested that lymphoma risk may vary according to the specific type of IL-10R deficiency. Zheng et al. reported B-cell lymphoma in 9 of 28 patients (32.1%) with IL10RB deficiency, a higher proportion than that observed among patients with IL10RA deficiency [16]. Notably, all five tumours initially reported by Neven et al. were EBV-negative [7]. By contrast, analysis of clinical outcomes in the cohort of patients with IL-10/IL-10R deficiency reported by Engelhardt et al. documented the occurrence of secondary EBV-positive lymphoma [21], and a subsequent case report likewise described EBV-positive B-cell lymphoma in the setting of IL-10R deficiency [22]. Most previously reported tumours occurred in children with severe biallelic loss-of-function variants and were predominantly of B-cell origin, representing a substantially different clinical and biological context from that of our study, which involved adults carrying single heterozygous variants and EBV infection predominantly involving T/NK cells.

Another line of evidence supports a potential association between host genetic variation in the IL-10 axis and the clinical course of EBV infection. Helminen et al. found that the distribution of the IL-10 promoter -1082G/A polymorphism differed among EBV-seronegative individuals, otherwise healthy EBV-seropositive individuals, and patients with severe EBV infection. They proposed that the capacity for host IL-10 production might influence susceptibility to EBV infection or disease severity, with higher IL-10 production associated with reduced susceptibility and lower IL-10 production associated with an increased risk of severe EBV infection [23]. The same group subsequently analysed haplotypes comprising the −1082, −819, and −592 promoter polymorphisms and found that specific IL-10 haplotypes were associated with susceptibility to primary EBV infection and with circulating IL-10 levels [8]. In Chinese children, the IL-10 −592 A/C polymorphism has also been reported to be associated with EBV-associated haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) [24], while a subsequent broader study of cytokine gene polymorphisms similarly identified associations between IL-10 loci and haplotypes and susceptibility to paediatric EBV-HLH [9]. Collectively, these findings suggest that host genetic variation within the IL-10 axis may influence the course and outcome of EBV infection. The capacity of EBV itself to modulate the IL-10 pathway provides additional biological evidence linking this signalling axis to virus–host interactions. The EBV BCRF1 gene encodes viral IL-10 (vIL-10), which shares substantial sequence homology with human IL-10 (hIL-10) [25], [26]. vIL-10 exhibits IL-10-like immunoregulatory activity and can exert selective biological effects through IL-10Rα; for example, its ability to inhibit IL-2 production by human CD4 + T cells is approximately 1,000-fold weaker than that of hIL-10 [27]. In classical B-cell infection models, vIL-10 functions as a bona fide latency-associated gene and directly contributes to B-cell growth and transformation following EBV infection [28]. In patients with chronic active Epstein–Barr virus infection (CAEBV), Kanegane et al. also reported abnormalities in hIL-10 and vIL-10 [29], suggesting that persistent EBV infection may be accompanied by alterations in the IL-10-related immunoregulatory milieu.

IL-10 signalling may also play an important role in EBV-positive T/NK-cell proliferative diseases, rather than being relevant solely to B-cell infection. Ho et al. found that cytokines including IL-10 were more frequently expressed in EBV-positive peripheral T-cell lymphomas than in EBV-negative cases [30], suggesting a distinct cytokine milieu in EBV-positive T-cell neoplasms. Shen et al. similarly observed prominent expression of human IL-10 in EBV-associated nasal NK/T-cell lymphoma and proposed that IL-10 might contribute to tumour immune evasion [31]. Mechanistically, Kis et al., using experimental systems that included NK-lymphoma-derived cells, demonstrated that both hIL-10 and vIL-10 could induce expression of the EBV latent membrane protein LMP1 [32]. Takahara et al. subsequently investigated the EBV-positive NK-cell lines SNK6 and KAI3 and found that IL-10 increased the expression of both LMP1 and CD25, while enabling these cells to sustain enhanced growth at lower concentrations of IL-2 [33]. Thus, once EBV-positive NK cells are established, IL-10 may represent more than a cytokine that changes in parallel with disease severity; it may also modulate the viral latency programme and influence the survival and proliferative state of infected cells. This possibility is particularly relevant to Patient 1, in whom NK cells constituted the predominant EBV-infected population.

With regard to the failure of the host immune system to eliminate abnormal EBV-positive T/NK cells over time, Collins et al. used FlowRNA to simultaneously characterise cellular phenotype and intracellular EBV RNA and directly demonstrated clonal expansion of EBV-infected CD4 + T cells, as well as concurrent T- and NK-cell involvement, in patients with EBV-associated lymphoproliferative diseases [34]. Notably, some patients retained EBV-specific T cells capable of recognising EBV antigens and exerting cytotoxic activity, yet EBV-infected clones persisted over prolonged periods. At the same time, substantial numbers of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, which were capable of markedly suppressing T-cell proliferation, were detected in peripheral blood [34]. These findings suggest that persistence of EBV-positive T/NK cells does not necessarily reflect a complete absence of anti-EBV effector cells, but may instead result from ineffective immune surveillance within an immunosuppressive microenvironment. This observation is relevant to the hypothesis proposed in the present study: if certain heterozygous IL10RA states exert modest functional effects on IL-10 signalling, their potential influence may lie in modifying long-term host control of abnormal EBV-positive T/NK cells rather than in affecting the initial entry of EBV into these cells.

Once EBV-positive T/NK cells evade immune clearance, they may acquire additional survival advantages through cell-intrinsic signalling and alterations in the immune microenvironment. Onozawa et al. demonstrated constitutive STAT3 activation in EBV-infected T/NK cells from patients with CAEBV and showed that JAK1/2 inhibition reduced cell survival and the production of several inflammatory cytokines. Because STAT3 is also a major downstream effector of IL-10/IL-10R signalling, these two pathways are biologically interconnected. However, that study did not establish that STAT3 activation in CAEBV was driven by IL-10 or by abnormalities in IL10RA and therefore does not establish a complete linear pathway [35]. In 2024, Chen et al. further reported increased expression of IL-10 and PD-L1 in tissues from patients with EBV-associated T/NK-cell lymphoproliferative diseases, which correlated with STAT3 phosphorylation; in vitro experiments suggested that EBV-related intercellular signalling could promote immune evasion through a STAT3/IL-10/PD-L1 axis [36]. More recent work in EBV-NK-LPD also demonstrated increased expression of LINC02446 in patient-derived NK cells, which was associated with higher EBV-DNA and ferritin levels and with the occurrence of HLH, and was shown to positively regulate IL-10 expression in cellular models [37]. Collectively, these studies support the possibility that an IL-10-rich regulatory network contributes to the persistence and progression of EBV-positive T/NK-cell diseases.

IL-10 may exert apparently contrasting effects at different stages of disease. On the one hand, population genetic studies suggest that normal variation in IL-10 regulatory capacity may influence primary EBV infection and excessive immune responses [8], [9], [23], [24]. On the other hand, once EBV-positive T/NK-cell clones have become established, a tumour- or virus-associated IL-10-rich microenvironment may be exploited by abnormal cells to promote LMP1 and CD25 expression, STAT3-dependent survival and disease progression, and PD-L1-mediated immune escape [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37]. Thus, the relationship between IL-10 levels and EBV infection, tumour initiation, and disease progression cannot be interpreted uniformly across different disease stages. The markedly elevated IL-10 level observed in Patient 1 during the terminal stage may have reflected EBV-related tumour burden, HLH, and systemic cytokine dysregulation and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of preserved or impaired IL-10Rα function. In the context of a germline heterozygous IL10RA state, a more pertinent question may be whether it alters the responsiveness of specific immune-cell populations to IL-10 under particular conditions, rather than whether it simply predicts higher or lower circulating IL-10 concentrations.

The development and progression of EBV-associated T/NK-cell diseases are also likely to involve multiple sequential steps. Through integrated analysis of viral and host genomes, Okuno et al. showed that EBV-infected cells in CAEBV and related haematological malignancies can acquire somatic host mutations, including mutations in DDX3X, and undergo clonal evolution across multiple cell lineages. They also identified characteristic intragenic deletions within the EBV genome in CAEBV and several EBV-associated malignancies that are uncommon in ordinary infectious mononucleosis. Some of these viral genomic deletions can promote the expression of lytic-cycle-associated genes while preventing complete viral replication and cell lysis, thereby creating conditions conducive to neoplastic expansion of infected cells [38]. These findings indicate that progression from EBV infection to persistent T/NK-cell lymphoproliferative disease and ultimately to lymphoma likely reflects the combined effects of viral factors, acquired alterations within infected cells, and the host immune environment. This multistep framework may help explain why the same germline IL10RA variant was present in healthy relatives in both families, whereas severe disease developed only in the probands. Even if a single heterozygous IL10RA variant exerts a partial functional effect, it may represent only one component of the host genetic background within a complex multistep disease process.

Taken together, we propose that the heterozygous IL10RA variants identified in this study may fit a multistep model of “host susceptibility–viral persistence–clonal evolution” in EBV-associated T/NK-cell lymphoproliferation, rather than a simple linear causal model. In this framework, if certain heterozygous IL10RA variants have biological effects, they may modestly alter IL-10-mediated immune regulation without causing classical IL-10Rα deficiency and may not directly increase the likelihood of EBV infection of T/NK cells. Once abnormal EBV-positive T/NK cells emerge, impaired host immune surveillance and immunosuppressive factors such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells may facilitate their persistence. IL-10-related signalling, together with the LMP1/CD25, JAK/STAT3, and PD-L1 pathways, may subsequently promote the survival and immune evasion of infected cells. Over time, viral genomic abnormalities and somatic genetic alterations acquired by infected cells may further drive clonal selection and neoplastic evolution, ultimately resulting in persistent EBV-associated T/NK-cell lymphoproliferation, lymphoma, or HLH. The key unresolved question is whether a single heterozygous IL10RA state can meaningfully alter long-term immune control of EBV-infected T/NK cells in a subset of individuals; addressing this question will require further clinical observation and experimental validation.

This study has several limitations. First, only two unrelated families were included, precluding any statistical assessment of the association between heterozygous IL10RA variants and EBV-associated T/NK-cell disease and preventing estimation of penetrance. Second, functional studies, including assessment of IL-10Rα surface expression, STAT3 phosphorylation following IL-10 stimulation, and downstream anti-inflammatory responses, were not performed. We therefore cannot determine whether the heterozygous states in these two patients resulted in biologically meaningful alterations in IL-10 signalling. This limitation is particularly relevant to P29A, for which no direct functional evidence is currently available. Third, the two variants differ substantially in the strength of the supporting evidence: R101W is a well-established pathogenic allele for classical autosomal recessive IL10RA-related disease, whereas P29A remains a variant of uncertain significance and cannot currently be considered equivalent to R101W with respect to pathogenicity. Fourth, the same variants were identified in family members without similar EBV-associated disease, suggesting that, even if a genetic effect exists, it would more likely represent an incompletely penetrant modifying effect than a sufficient monogenic cause. In addition, previously reported lymphomas associated with IL-10R deficiency have occurred predominantly in children with severe biallelic loss-of-function defects and have been primarily of B-cell origin; these findings therefore cannot be directly extrapolated to the adult EBV-associated T/NK-cell diseases described here. Despite these limitations, both patients demonstrated the coexistence of persistent T/NK-cell-associated EBV infection, abnormal clonal lymphocyte expansion, and a single heterozygous IL10RA variant. Taken together with previous evidence linking IL-10R deficiency to lymphoma susceptibility, host IL-10 genetic background to the outcome of EBV infection, and IL-10-related signalling to the biology of EBV-positive T/NK cells, our observations suggest that the IL-10/IL-10Rα axis, although not established as a monogenic cause, warrants further investigation as a potential host susceptibility pathway in EBV-associated T/NK-cell lymphoproliferative diseases.
