MicroRNA-29b-5p regulates cell proliferation and cancer stemness via TGF-β/p300 pathway in human synovial sarcoma
Authors: HuiLing Xu, ShuangShuang Dong, Zhen Zhang, Ning Wang, MingYu Xie, LiJuan Pang, Feng Li, Yan Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Synovial sarcoma (SS) is a rare and aggressive soft tissue malignancy characterized by t(X;18)(p11.2;q11.2) translocation, which results in the SYT-SSX fusion gene. SS exhibits cancer stem cell-like properties that contribute to its invasiveness. Recent evidence implicates microRNA-29b (miR-29b) could regulate TGF-β pathway and its downstream gene EP300 may be involved in these traits. Bioinformatics tools (miRDB, TargetScan, and miRanda) were used to predict the interaction between miR-29b-5p and EP300, which was validated via dual-luciferase reporter assays and RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation. The expression levels of miR-29b-5p and EP300, along with those of cancer stem cell markers, LIF-LIFR signaling, and Ki-67 proliferation indices, were analyzed in SS tissues, parental cells, and spheroid cells. Spheroids derived from SW982 and SSX1 cells cultured in serum-free suspension presented elevated levels of cancer stem cell markers and increased proliferation, migration, and invasion capacities, particularly SYT-SSX1-derived spheroids. Compared with parental cells, spheroids presented reduced miR-29b-5p expression and increased EP300 expression. The overexpression of miR-29b-5p suppressed spheroid formation; stemness marker expression; and cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas its inhibition had the opposite effect. Compared with individual silencing, co-silencing of miR-29b-5p and EP300 further attenuated cancer stem cell-like traits, underscoring their regulatory interplay. In conclusion, miR-29b-5p may suppresses cancer stemness and aggressiveness in SS to some extent by targeting EP300, with a relatively more pronounced effect in SSX1 cells. Preliminarily these fundings suggest that miR-29b-5p-mediated modulation of EP300 and the TGF-β signaling pathway may provide a potential research data for synovial sarcoma studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04348-6.

Introduction

Synovial sarcoma (SS) is a rare and aggressive soft tissue malignancy that accounts for approximately 7–10% of all soft tissue tumors [1]. It primarily affects young adults and commonly arises in the extremities [2]. SS is characterized by a distinct biphasic histology and is closely associated with the SYT-SSX fusion gene, resulting from the t(X;18)(p11.2;q11.2) chromosomal translocation, which is found in > 90% of cases [3]. The SYT-SSX fusion gene has several isoforms, namely, SYT-SSX1, SYT-SSX2, and SYT-SSX4 [4], with SYT-SSX1 specifically linked to the biphasic variant and associated with increased malignancy and elevated Ki-67 expression [5]. SS is still considered to have an uncertain histological origin according to the fifth WHO soft tissue classification. Despite advancements in surgical and adjuvant treatments, the 5-year survival rate of patients experiencing recurrence or metastasis remains low, underscoring the need for novel biomarkers and targeted therapies [6, 7].

Molecular studies have revealed critical signaling pathways involved in SS pathogenesis, particularly those involving the SYT-SSX fusion protein and its role in tumorigenesis and metastasis [8]. Emerging evidence suggests that SS may originate from cancer stem cells (CSCs), which drive tumor initiation, metastasis, and recurrence [9, 10]. Epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT) also plays a central role in the aggressive behavior of SS, with metastasis likely linked to EMT, accounting for approximately 90% of patient mortality [6]. Recent research has highlighted the importance of noncoding RNAs, particularly miRNAs, in SS progression; however, comprehensive investigations remain limited [11]. The miR-29 family, in particular, has demonstrated tumor-suppressive activity across various malignancies [12] and can play a role in cell proliferation, invasion and metastasis by regulating target genes [13–15], and miR-29 can participate in the TGF-β signaling pathway and the EMT process [16].

Our previous studies have shown that TGF-β pathway-related factors are closely related to the EMT process of SS [17]. Exome sequencing revealed significant gene expression differences between the epithelial and mesenchymal components of SS, establishing a strong association with the TGF-β pathway. The DEGs were further analyzed, and six closely associated miRNAs (including miR-29b-5p) were predicted. Through database analysis, we identified the target genes of these six miRNAs and reported that miR-29b-5p directly targets EP300, a key molecule in the TGF-β signaling pathway [18]. This study aimed to preliminarily investigate the potential regulatory role of miR-29b-5p via the TGF-β/p300 pathway in modulating cell proliferation and cancer stemness in SS. This study may provides new ideas and preliminary theoretical basis for the targeted treatment of synovial sarcoma.

Materials and methods

Patients and specimens

Archival specimens comprising fifteen synovial sarcoma tissues and eight adjacent non-tumor tissues (defined as morphologically normal soft tissues obtained from the same patient’s surgical specimen at a distance of ≥ 5 cm from the tumor margin, with histopathological confirmation of absence of tumor cells, significant inflammation, or fibrosis by hematoxylin and eosin staining) were sourced from the Department of Pathology, First Affiliated Hospital of Shihezi University Medical College (China), collected over the period 1968–2020. All the samples were paraffin-embedded tissue. Three independent pathologists classified and graded specimens adhering to the most recent WHO criteria for bone/soft tissue tumors. Detailed clinical information of the synovial sarcoma (SS) patients from whom these tissue samples were derived is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Cell culture

The human synovial sarcoma cell line SW982, cataloged as ATCC HTB-93™, was procured from the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology. These cells were originally isolated from a 25-year-old female patient with primary biphasic SS. The SW982 cell line used in this study was negative for the SS18-SSX fusion gene. SW982 cells were stably transfected with a lentivirus expressing the SS18-SSX1 fusion gene to establish the SSX1 cell line, as previously describedThe SW982 cell line used in this study is negative for the SS18-SSX fusion gene. We set the stably expressing the SS18-SSX1 fusion gene in SW982 cells by transfection with lentivirus, called SSX1 cell as described previously [17], was used in this study. Cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ (humidified) in L-15 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, CA, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Tumorsphere formation assays were performed according to previously described protocols [19].

RNA isolation and real-time qRT‒PCR

Total RNA from fresh specimens was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), while formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues underwent extraction with the miRNeasy FFPE system (QIAGEN). Reverse transcription employed divergent strategies: mRNA conversion via the RevertAid cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher) contrasted with miRNA processing through the miScript II RT platform (QIAGEN). Sangon Biotech (China) synthesized miR-29b-5p-specific primers. Quantitative PCR amplification was performed using miScript SYBR Green chemistry (QIAGEN) for miRNA detection and Ultra SYBR Mixture containing Low ROX (CWBIO) for mRNA analysis. Relative expression was quantified using the 2^-ΔΔCt method, normalized to GAPDH for mRNA or U6 snRNA for miRNA targets. Custom primer sequences (Sangon Biotech) are provided in supplementary Table 2.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Cellular proteins from SSX1 and spheroid cultures were solubilized, resolved through SDS-PAGE, and electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes (Solarbio, China). Membranes were blocked for 2.5 h at room temperature with 5% skim milk (Yili Group, China) in PBST, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: mouse anti-EP300 (Abcam, UK; 1:500), rabbit anti-pSmad2/3 (Abcam; 1:500), rabbit anti-TGF-β1 (Abcam; 1:1000), rabbit anti-SOX-2 (Abcam; 1:1000), rabbit anti-Nanog (CST, USA; 1:1000), rabbit anti-OCT-4 (Abcam; 1:1000), and mouse anti-β-actin (ZSGB-BIO, China; 1:1000). Post-wash, membranes were exposed to HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZSGB-BIO; 1:10,000) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein signals were visualized using ultrasensitive ECL substrate (Thermo Fisher).

Oligonucleotide transfection

Cell transfections were performed using Lipofectamine™ 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, Foster City, CA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfection efficiency was evaluated via RT‒PCR. The oligonucleotides used were as follows: miR-29b-5p mimic (sense 5’-GCUGGUUUCAUAUGGUGGUUUAGA-3’ and antisense 5’-UAAACCACCAUAUGAAACCAGCUU-3’; GenePharma Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; Catalog No. miR10000608-1–5); miR-29b-5p inhibitor (5’-CAGUACUUUUGUGUAGUACAA-3’; GenePharma Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; Catalog No. miR20000608-1–5); corresponding negative controls (m-NC for mimics, sense 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’, antisense 5’-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3’, and i-NC for inhibitor, 5’-UCUAAACCACCAUAUGAAACCAGC-3’); and siRNA-EP300 (sense 5’-CCAUGAAUCCAGGGCCUAATT-3’ and antisense 5’-UUAGGCCCUGGAUUCAUGGTT-3’).

Cell proliferation assay

Cellular proliferation was quantified using a CCK-8 kit (Dojindo). Briefly, 3 × 10⁵ cells/well were plated in 96-well plates. Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded at 0, 24, 48, and 72-hour intervals with a Bio-Rad spectrophotometer. Proliferation rates were calculated from triplicate measurements across three independent experiments.

Colony formation assay

Cells were plated in 12-well flat-bottom plates containing 2 ml medium with 15% FBS (or RPMI-1640/10% FBS). Following 4–5 days of culture, fresh medium was applied. Colonies developed over 3 weeks, then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% ethidium bromide, imaged, and quantified.

Wound healing assay

Three reference parallel lines were drawn at 1 cm intervals on the backs of the 6-well plates. Cells (5 × 10^4/well) were seeded, and a scratch was created using a pipette tip. The detached cells were carefully rinsed, the medium was replaced to remove any remaining cell debris, and the samples were further incubated at 37 °C. Images were obtained at 0, 24, and 48 h to assess cell migration. Scratch areas were measured via microscopy, with 0 h as the baseline.

Cell migration and invasion analyses

Pre-thawed Matrigel (4 °C overnight) was diluted 1:8 in basal medium, and 40 µL aliquots were dispensed into Transwell chambers using pipette tips precooled at −20 °C for 30 min, followed by polymerization at 37 °C for 2 h. After aspirating residual liquid, chambers were conditioned with 100 µL L-15 basal medium (37 °C, 30 min), then seeded with 200 µL cell suspension (~ 6 × 10³ cells) in the upper compartment while the lower chamber received 600 µL L-15 supplemented with 20% FBS as chemoattractant. Cells were allowed to migrate or invade for 24–48 h before sequential processing: chambers were rinsed twice with PBS, fixed with 600 µL of 4% paraformaldehyde (4 °C, 20 min), air-dried in an inverted position, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (15 min), and washed twice with PBS to remove excess dye. Non-migratory cells were removed by cotton swab abrasion, and migrated cells adherent to the membrane’s lower surface were quantified microscopically across five randomized fields per chamber.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The EP300 3′-UTR wild-type (PGL3-EP300-wt) and mutant (PGL3-EP300-mut) sequences were cloned into luciferase reporter vectors (GenePharma, Shanghai, China). Cells were then co-transfected with these reporter constructs and either miR-29b-5p mimics or a negative control miRNA (miR-NC). After incubation for 24 h, cells were harvested and lysed. Relative luciferase activity was quantified via dual-luciferase reporter assays, employing Renilla luciferase as an internal reference.

RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP)

The cells were harvested and washed twice with ice-cold PBS. After centrifugation at 1500 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min, the cells were resuspended in 50 µL of cell lysis buffer (containing 0.5 µL of PMSF and 0.25 µL of protease inhibitor per 100 µL of buffer) and incubated on ice for 5 min. Magnetic beads (CS203178) were gently resuspended by inverting the tube, and 50 µL was transferred to a new Eppendorf tube. Subsequently, 500 µL of RIP buffer (CS203177) was added, followed by brief vortexing. The tube was placed on a magnetic rack to allow the beads to adhere to the tube wall, and the supernatant was discarded. Washing with 500 µL of RIP buffer was performed twice. The beads were then resuspended in 100 µL of RIP buffer and incubated with 5 µg of Ago2 (Millipore, USA) and IgG antibodies (Millipore, USA) at room temperature for 30 min to 1 h. The tubes were centrifuged briefly to remove the supernatant. The RIP wash buffer (0.5 mL) was then added and mixed, and the supernatant was discarded. Washing was performed twice, followed by the final addition of RIP wash buffer (0.5 mL), which was mixed and reserved for further use. After washing, the beads were incubated with 100 µL of cell lysate in 900 µL of RIP buffer overnight at 4 °C. The supernatant was then discarded, and 500 µL of RIP buffer was added. Washing with precooled RIP wash buffer was repeated five times, and the supernatant was discarded after each wash. After the final wash, 150 µL of proteinase K was added for resuspension. The mixture was incubated at 55 °C for 30 min with gentle shaking to ensure complete protein digestion. The tube was then briefly centrifuged, and the supernatant was transferred to a new Eppendorf tube, where 250 µL of RIP wash buffer was added. The RNA from the RNA isolation and qRT‒PCR methods was extracted via a kit (700 µL of TRIzol was added and shaken for 15 s, followed by the addition of 280 µL of chloroform, centrifugation to separate the aqueous phase, the transfer of 300 µL of the aqueous phase to a new tube, and the addition of 5 µL of precipitation enhancer with 800 µL of absolute ethanol). The RNA was quantified via qRT‒PCR.

Bioinformatics

Potential miRNA targets were screened using microarray data. Bioinformatics analysis utilized four established databases: TargetScan, miRDB, StarBase, and miRTarBase.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM, USA) and GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Numerical data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using appropriate tests according to experimental design (including Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons and ANOVA or non-parametric tests for multi-group comparisons), with significance defined as p < 0.05.

Results

miR-29b-5p is downregulated in SS and negatively correlated with EP300

Using laser capture microdissection and exon array analysis, we examined genomic SNP variations in both the epithelial and mesenchymal components of biphasic SS tissues. We identified significantly differentially expressed genes, including EP300, in both components of the SS tissues. Data analysis revealed that six miRNAs potentially regulate these genes, including miR-29b-5p. Figure 1A highlights a cross-referencing analysis of gene lists from multiple datasets, highlighting EP300, a downstream gene of the TGF-β pathway, as a candidate target of miR-29b-5p. To confirm this, we extracted total RNA from SS tissues and performed quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‒PCR) to measure the miR-29b-5p and EP300 expression levels. As shown in Fig. 1B, miR-29b-5p was downregulated in SS tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues, whereas EP300 was upregulated. A negative correlation trend was observed between miR-29b-5p and EP300 expression levels in synovial sarcoma tissues, although this correlation did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 1C). These results indicate that miR-29b-5p is downregulated in SS and may suppress tumor progression by targeting EP300.

SYT-SSX1 fusion gene promotes CSC-like properties in SS

Studies have suggested that SS may be a stem cell malignancy driven by the SS18-SSX fusion protein [20]. To explore the role of cancer stemness in SS, we measured the mRNA expression of CSC-related markers (Nanog, CD44, OCT-4, and SOX-2), the self-renewal factor LIF and its receptor LIFR, and the proliferation marker Ki-67 in SS and adjacent normal tissues. All the markers were upregulated in the SS tissues (Fig. 2A).

We used SSX1 cells stably expressing the SYT-SSX1 fusion gene and SW982 cells for subsequent experiments and compared their stem cell characteristics. On day 3, sphere formation assays revealed that, compared with SW982 cells, SSX1 cells formed more and larger spheres (Fig. 2B). Additionally, SW982 cells presented lower mRNA and protein expression levels of CSC-related markers than did SSX1 cells (Fig. 2C and D). Migration assays revealed that sphere cells demonstrated greater migration ability than did SSX1 cells, and this trait was further enhanced by SYT-SSX1 expression (Fig. 2E). These findings suggest that the SYT-SSX1 fusion gene promotes CSC-like properties in SSX1 cells.

miR-29b-5p suppresses malignant biological behavior in SSX1 cells

To further clarify the role of miR-29b-5p in SS, we modulated its expression in SW982 and SSX1 cells using miR-29b-5p mimics and a miR-29b-5p-inhibitor. Transfection was confirmed successful by qRT-PCR, which showed increased miR-29b-5p levels with miR-29b-5p mimics and decreased levels with miR-29b-5p-inhibitor (Fig. 3A). Compared with the negative control, the overexpression of miR-29b-5p significantly inhibited cell proliferation in the CCK-8 and colony formation assays, whereas its knockdown had the opposite effect (Fig. 3B and C). Wound healing and Transwell assays revealed that miR-29b-5p overexpression reduced migration and invasion, whereas its knockdown had the opposite effect (Fig. 3D and E). These findings indicate that miR-29b-5p functions as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting SSX1 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

miR-29b-5p suppresses stemness and malignancy in SSX1 spheroid cells

We next examined miR-29b-5p expression in SS and sphere cells. Compared with that in SW982 cells, miR-29b-5p expression was lower in sphere cells than in SSX1 cells, particularly in those formed by SSX1 cells (Fig. 4A). The overexpression of miR-29b-5p reduced both the number and size of spheres, whereas its inhibition promoted sphere-forming capacity (Fig. 4B).

miR-29b-5p targeteds EP300 in spheroid cells in vitro

To verify whether EP300 is a potential target of miR-29b-5p, we conducted dual-luciferase reporter assays and immunoprecipitation experiments to verify the interaction between miR-29b-5p and EP300. A luciferase reporter plasmid was constructed on the basis of the predicted miR-29b-5p binding site in the EP300 3’ UTR (Fig. 6A). Compared with that in the other groups, the luciferase activity in the EP300 wild-type group was significantly lower (Fig. 6B), indicating direct binding.

Anti-Ago2 immunoprecipitation demonstrated that EP300 was enriched in SSX1 cells treated with the Ago2 antibody, further confirming the interaction between EP300 and miR-29b-5p (Fig. 6C). The overexpression of miR-29b-5p significantly reduced EP300 mRNA and protein levels, whereas its inhibition increased EP300 levels (Fig. 6D and E).

EP300 expression was also elevated in sphere cells, especially those derived from SSX1 cells (Fig. 6F). These findings suggest that EP300 is a direct target of miR-29b-5p, which regulates EP300 expression at both the transcriptional and posttranscriptional levels.

miR-29b-5p/p300 axis regulates stemness via the TGF-β pathway in SSX1 cells

To investigate whether miR-29b-5p regulates SS malignancy through EP300, we conducted rescue experiments using SS and spheroid cells. qRT‒PCR confirmed that miR-29b-5p antagonized EP300 expression (Fig. 7A). Functional assays demonstrated that EP300 upregulation reversed the inhibitory effects of miR-29b-5p on cell migration and invasion (Fig. 7B).

We then examined whether this regulation occurs through the TGF-β pathway. miR-29b-5p overexpression inhibited TGF-β and phosphorylated Smad2/3 levels (Fig. 7C), whereas EP300 upregulation counteracted this effect in SSX1 cells (Fig. 7D).

Sphere formation assays revealed that miR-29b-5p inhibition increased the sphere-forming ability (Fig. 7E) and the expression of CSC-related markers, self-renewal factors, and Ki-67 (Fig. 7F). However, reducing EP300 levels reversed these effects. Similar trends were observed in the migration and invasion assays with sphere cells (Fig. 7G and I).

These results suggest that the miR-29b-5p/EP300 axis may regulate malignant behavior and stemness in SSX1 cells by modulating the TGF-β signaling pathway.

Discussion

Synovial sarcoma is an aggressive and metastatic soft-tissue tumor characterized by the SYT-SSX fusion gene [21, 22]. Although this fusion protein is a key factor in tumorigenesis, no targeted therapies are currently available, and SS exhibits distinct epigenetic features [23]. Recent studies suggest that SS may originate from stem cells, with SYT-SSX strongly correlated with elevated expression of stemness-associated markers. These findings suggest that SS initiation may involve stem-like cellular activity triggered by the SYT-SSX fusion gene [24]. Therefore, exploring the molecular mechanisms underlying stem-like phenotypes in SS remains a worthy direction for understanding tumorigenesis.

miRNAs are small, endogenous, noncoding RNAs that play key roles in cancer progression and the regulation of stem-like phenotypes. Abnormal miRNA expression causes widespread transcriptional dysregulation, which is associated with multiple cancers[25]. For example, miRNAs and lncRNAs regulate stem-like phenotypes in breast cancer cells with stemness features [26]; miR-486-5p inhibits stem-like phenotypes in colorectal cancer [27]; overexpression of miR-487b-3p inhibits the formation of osteosarcoma cell spheroids (a common in vitro surrogate for stem-like properties) and enhances doxorubicin efficacy [28]; miR-338-3p plays a critical role in regulating the self-renewal ability and stem-like phenotypes of liver cancer cells in vitro. Its loss promotes malignant features, while its overexpression inhibits in vitro spheroid formation and may affect drug sensitivity by targeting SOX4[29]. These findings highlight the central role of miRNAs in regulating in vitro stem-like phenotypes and malignant progression across diverse cancers, suggesting their potential as candidate targets for further exploration.

However, studies on the miRNA-mediated regulation of fusion oncogenes in sarcoma remain scarce [11]. In Ewing sarcoma, EWS-FLI1 downregulates miR-145, thereby increasing the repression of genes associated with undifferentiated phenotypes (e.g., SOX2) [30]. In SS, our study revealed that SYT-SSX1 modulates the miR-29b-5p-mediated targeting of EP300, with associated changes in the TGF-β signaling pathway that may be linked to the regulation of stem-like phenotypes of SSX1 cells in vitro. This study provides a theoretical basis for determining the tissue origin, invasion, and metastasis of SS and offers preliminary insights into the molecular regulatory network underlying SS progression, particularly the association with stem-like phenotypes.

EP300 functions as a transcriptional coactivator in the TGF-β signaling cascade by enhancing Smad3-driven transcription. The phosphorylation of Smad3 by TGF-β further stabilizes the interaction between EP300 and Smad3. Wang et al. demonstrated that EP300 facilitates the nuclear import of Smad2/3 and TAZ, even in the absence of nuclear localization signals, emphasizing its transport and regulatory roles [31]. EP300 also acetylates histones, remodels chromatin, and promotes TGF-β1 expression. In leukemia, EP300 cooperates with Smad3 to activate Foxp3 via TGF-β signaling [32]. In esophageal cancer, TGF-β1 stimulates the phosphorylation of Smad2/3, directly interacting with EP300/CBP in the nucleus, which increases the histone acetyltransferase activity of EP300. Wang J et al. reported that allicin inhibits the EP300 and TGF-β1 signaling pathways, suppressing esophageal cancer metastasis [33]. These studies support the oncogenic role of EP300 and its association with downstream Smad2/3 in the TGF-β pathway.

TGF-β signaling is essential for the maintenance of stem-like phenotypes and cellular differentiation, with similar roles in normal stem cells. In lung cancer, it cooperates with NPM1 to promote transformation and stem-like properties [34]. In liver cancer cells with stem-like features, cyclin D1 interacts with Smad2/3/4 to regulate self-renewal [35]. In colorectal CSCs, CD51 binds to the TGF-β receptor, increasing TGF-β/Smad signaling [36]. The upregulation of TGF-β1 also increases Smad4, p-Smad2/3, and CD133 expression in liver cancer cells with stem-like features [37]. and regulates PFKFB3 expression in glioblastoma cells with stem-like phenotypes via the p38 MAPK and PI3K/Akt pathways [38]. Moreover, silencing ShcA activates STAT4 in breast cancer cells with stem-like features[39]. Despite limited studies, the TGF/Smad pathway remains a critical regulator of stem-like phenotypes in cancer cells.

EP300 expression is positively correlated with stemness-associated markers in triple-negative and basal-like breast cancer [40]. However, the impact of EP300 on the expression of stemness-associated markers and cellular differentiation through the TGF-β pathway remains underexplored. In this study, we observed for the first time that EP300 expression in SS spheroid cells was significantly greater than that in parental cells, with greater expression in SYT-SSX1-positive spheroid cells. EP300 silencing led to marked reductions in TGF-β1 expression. Our preliminary results suggest that SS tissues with high EP300 expression also exhibit elevated TGF-β1 expression, indicating that EP300 may be involved in regulating stem-like phenotypes of SS in association with the TGF-β signaling pathway.

miR-29b is a crucial regulator of stem-like phenotypes in various cancers. It reduces the in vitro spheroid formation ability (a surrogate for stem-like self-renewal) of glioblastoma multiforme by targeting BCL2L2 [41] and inhibits the growth, migration, and invasion of triple-negative breast cancer cells by modulating the Wnt/β-catenin and AKT pathways [42]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, miR-29b regulates stem-like phenotypes through EIF5A2, with its downregulation sustaining in vitro stem-like properties [43]. Consistent with these findings, our results show that miR-29b-5p may inhibits EP300 expression, which was accompanied by marked downregulation of the expression of TGF-β1 and its downstream effector p-Smad2/3, and concurrent attenuation of the invasiveness, migration, and in vitro stem-like phenotypes (e.g., spheroid formation) of SSX1 cells. In contrast, miR-29b-5p suppression upregulated EP300 and was associated with activation of the TGF-β/Smad pathway and increased expression of genes associated with stem-like properties (e.g., Nanog, SOX2, and LIF), which in turn promoted in vitro self-renewal, spheroid formation, and invasive capacity. These effects were more pronounced in SYT-SSX1-positive cells, indicating that miR-29b-5p may regulate in vitro spheroid formation, the expression of stemness-associated markers, and malignant behaviors of SSX1 cells in a manner potentially associated with the SYT-SSX1 fusion gene. Furthermore, EP300 silencing reversed the changes in stem-like phenotypes induced by miR-29b-5p suppression, suggesting that miR-29b-5p may regulate the in vitro stem-like phenotypes of SSX1 cells through an EP300-associated TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway.

Our study identifies potential associations in a regulatory network in SS, where the SYT-SSX1 fusion gene may suppresses miR-29b-5p expression, which could be associated with reduced repression of EP300 and changes in TGF-β/Smad pathway activity. These observed associations include upregulation of the genes associated with in vitro stem-like properties (Nanog, SOX2, and LIF), which correlates with changes in the in vitro stem-like phenotypes, proliferation, and invasiveness of SSX1 cells. These findings provide preliminary insights into a potential epigenetic mechanism by which the fusion oncogene may regulate stem-like phenotypes in SS. However, we acknowledge that the current evidence remains correlative; definitive regulatory relationship between miR-29b-5p and TGF-β/p300 pathway in the cancer stemness of SS requires further functional interrogation (e.g., TGF-β pathway modulation and rescue experiments). Future studies should explore the mechanistic interplay between miR-29b-5p and the SYT-SSX fusion gene and assess whether SWI/SNF (BAF complex) and polycomb domain factors may synergize with miR-29b-5p in SS, which will provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms of SS.
