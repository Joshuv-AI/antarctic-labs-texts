Strength and balance asymmetries in children and adolescents: comparison between single- and multi-sport participants
Authors: Bartosz Wilczyński, Jakub Buler, Rafa l Buler, Micha l Grochowski, Bartosz Puchalski, Katarzyna Zorena
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrior research has suggested links between youth sport special-ization, inter-limb asymmetries, and injury risk, but findings remain inconsistent regarding whether single-sport athletes show greater asymmetries than their multi-sport peers. This study examined differences in inter-limb asymme-try (global, balance, strength) between single- and multi-sport athletes, and explored the prevalence of exceeding clinical thresholds, sex- and sport-specific interactions, and associations with training volume and injury history.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted on 76 youth athletes (24 single-sport, 52 multi-sport; mean age 12.6 ± 1.7 years) using standardized assessments of dynamic balance (Y-Balance Test) and isometric strength (hand-held dynamometry). Inter-limb asymmetry was calculated using Index-9 in three domains: global asymmetry, balance asymmetry, and strength asymmetry. Differ-ences between single-sport specialists and multi-sport athletes were tested using linear mixed effect models, adjusted for sex, maturity status, BMI, training vol-ume, and sport type. Sensitivity and interaction analyzes (e.g., sport symmetry, sex, injury history) were also performed.ResultsInter-limb strength asymmetries greater than 10% were observed in more than half of the participants, especially for ankle plantarflexion and hip abduction. Balance asymmetries were smaller, with fewer than 15% of athletes exceeding the 10% threshold. Training volume was higher in the single-sport group, but was not associated with greater asymmetry. No significant differences were found between single and multisport athletes for global (β = 0.039, p = .597), balance (β = 0.037, p = .745) or strength asymmetry (β = 0.013, p = .879). These null results persisted across the sensitivity analyses and interaction models.ConclusionsDespite higher training volumes, single-sport youth athletes did not exhibit greater balance or strength asymmetries than their multi-sport peers. Inter-limb asymmetries were common across the cohort but were not associated with sport specialization. These findings highlight the need for cautious, individ-ualized interpretation of asymmetry measures in youth athletes, and underscore the importance of further research to clarify their developmental and clinical significance.Trial registrationsClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06325228); registered on 10/03/2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01420-y.

Introduction

In recent decades, youth sport has shifted from unstructured play toward highly orga-nized, sport-specific training systems [1, 2]. This trend toward early specialization has raised concerns about potential risks including increased injury rates, psychological burnout, and impaired motor development when training volumes exceed young ath-letes’ adaptive capacity [3–11]. An emerging concern in youth sport is the development of excessive inter-limb asymmetries, particularly in lower-limb strength and dynamic balance. For example, strength asymmetry—defined as a lack of equality between limbs or muscle groups—has been reported across both asymmetric (e.g., soccer, vol-leyball) and symmetric (e.g., running, cycling) sports [12–15]. Repetitive unilateral loading can lead to neuromuscular imbalances and altered movement strategies that affect functional performance profiles[13, 16, 17]. Research suggests that single-sport athletes may exhibit greater balance asymmetries than their multi-sport peers, likely due to reduced motor variability and sport-specific adaptations [18].

Functional asymmetries have frequently been linked to increased injury risk, decreased performance, and delayed return to sport[19–22]. However, the interpreta-tion of asymmetry magnitudes remains debated. While excessive inter-limb differences may indicate maladaptive deficits, smaller or sport-specific asymmetries can represent functional adaptations, particularly among trained athletes [23–25]. Consequently, the clinical relevance of any single “threshold” (e.g., > 10–15%) may be uncertain and likely context dependent, varying by task, metric, and population [26, 27]. For example, Domínguez -Navarro et al. [28]. reported that inter-limb asymmetries exceeding 10% did not impair sprint, agility, or jump performance in youth basketball players, under-scoring that threshold values may be population- and sport-specific. For transparency and comparability with previous research, we report the proportion of participants exceeding three exploratory cut-offs (10%, 15% and 20%) representing commonly cited and progressively stricter definitions of imbalance [20, 29]. Among the numerous avail-able asymmetry indices, Index-9 is regarded as one of the more robust options, as it avoids arbitrary reference-limb selection [25]. To objectively assess these asymme-tries in youth athletes, standardized field-based tools are essential. The Y-Balance Test (YBT) provides a reliable measure of dynamic postural control and directional balance deficits, while hand-held dynamometry (HHD) enables valid quantification of lower-limb strength asymmetries across muscle groups [25, 30]. Females and males may differ in neuromuscular control, strength development, and injury risk profiles during adolescence [31], while sport-specific demands—such as unilateral kicking in soccer versus symmetrical propulsion in swimming—are thought to shape the magnitude and direction of inter-limb differences [13, 32]. Exploring these interactions therefore provides additional insight into how specialization interacts with developmental and sport-related factors.

Traditional definitions, such as that of The American Orthopaedic Society for Sports Medicine (AOSSM), describe single-sport specialization as intensive, year-round training in one sport before puberty [33]. To standardize classification, Bell et al.[34] proposed a Delphi-based consensus definition:‘intentional and focused participation in a single sport for a majority of the year that restricts opportunities for engagement in other sports and activities’.

We adopted this definition to distinguish single- and multi-sport athletes, as it reflects current best practice. However, others argue that risks stem less from specialisation itself and more from factors such us insufficient rest, lack of movement variability, or external pressures—features of so-called premature specialisation [33, 35, 36]. Given these measurement considerations, understanding how sport participation patterns influence asymmetry development becomes critical.

Despite growing concern, few studies have objectively examined the relationship between youth sport specialization, training volume, and inter-limb asymmetry [37]. Some evidence suggests that single-sport athletes may exhibit greater dynamic balance asymmetry [18], although findings are inconsistent, with Gorman et al. [38]. reporting no significant differences in balance scores between single- and multi-sport athletes. The primary aim of our study was to compare inter-limb asymmetry in single-sport and multi-sport youth athletes across three outcomes: global asymmetry, dynamic balance, and isometric strength, while accounting for key covariates. A secondary aim was to explore whether training volume or injury history were associated with asymmetry.

Methods

This study used a cross-sectional design in alignment with the STROBE guide-lines. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Bioethics Commit-tee for Scientific Research, Medical University of Gdan´sk (application number: NKBBN/241/2023). This study was prospectively registered in the ClinicalTrials.gov registry (Clinical trial number: NCT06325228) on 10/03/2024. All procedures conformed to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from both participants and their parents or legal guardians before any involvement. Participation was voluntary and confidentiality was main-tained throughout the study. The manuscript has been evaluated using the CHecklist for statistical Assessment of Medical Papers (CHAMP) (see Supplementary File 1).

Recruitment

Recruitment took place between June 2023, and April 2024, using voluntary response sampling. Young athletes and their parents/coaches were invited to participate during structured educational sessions led by the project team as part of the initiative titled”Science of Healthy Sport for Children and Adolescents” (educational sessions about health-related issues in sports). Participants and their coaches, parents/guardians were recruited from local sports organizations, clubs, and schools. Invitations to participate in the study were sent by email and telephone. Attendance at the education sessions was open to all willing participants, whereas participation in the study was subject to eligibility requirements.

Inclusion criteria were: (i) participants aged 8–16 years; (ii) active involvement in structured sports programs, defined as regular participation in organized, coach-supervised training in a recognized sport discipline at least twice per week for a minimum of six consecutive months; and (iii) current medical clearance for sports participation from a licensed physician. Exclusion criteria were: (i) self-reported mus-culoskeletal pain or discomfort during a standardized pre-test screening interview, (ii).

lower extremity or trunk surgery within the past six months, (iii) inability to correctly execute the required functional tests (e.g., Y-Balance Test or hand-held dynamom-etry trials) after standardized verbal instruction and demonstration., and (iv) any diagnosed neurological or systemic condition affecting movement.

Questionnaire

A custom-designed questionnaire (see Supplementary File 2) was developed to col-lect demographic information and training-related data. It included items on average weekly hours spent on the main sport, other sports, and physical education. Adherence to the weekly hours-to-age training rule was assessed by comparing total weekly hours to chronological age and classified as compliant or non-compliant. Sport specialization was evaluated using a three-point scale based on criteria from Jayanthi et al. [39]: (1) prioritization of one main sport over others, (2) participation in the main sport for more than eight months per year, and (3) quitting another sport to focus on the main sport. Participants scoring 3/3 were classified as single-sport specialists, whereas those scoring (≤ 2/3) were classified as multi-sport athletes, in line with consensus defini-tions [34]. The questionnaire also included items on the age at which the athlete began their main sport, and on injury history within the past 12 months. Injury history was assessed using a binary item (yes/no) based on the consensus definition of Bahr et al. [40]. An “injury” was defined as tissue damage or other derangement of normal phys-ical function due to participation in sports, resulting from rapid or repetitive transfer of kinetic energy [40]. The item did not include information on time-loss from training or competition. To provide descriptive context, participants (together with coaches or guardians) were also asked open-ended questions regarding the location (classified as upper limb, lower limb, or torso) and type (acute or overuse) of injuries, as well as whether they had experienced multiple injuries (yes/no). For the purpose of the present analyses, only the dichotomous injury history variable was used. Questionnaires were completed during organized meetings using paper forms and face-to-face interviews. Researchers provided standardized verbal explanations to clar-ify key concepts (e.g., specialization, injury, training volume) and were available at all times to assist participants. Coaches and parents/guardians were present to support younger athletes, especially with retrospective questions such as injury history. This approach was intended to minimize recall bias and reporting errors [41]. Completed questionnaires were signed by participants and guardians, digitized, anonymized, and securely stored for analysis.

Anthropometric measurement

Body composition (weight, body mass index) was used by foot-to-foot bioelectrical impedance analysis (SC-240, Tanita Cooperation, Tokyo, Japan). The data were col-lected at a frequency of 50 Hz using the standard setting, following the manual input of the patient’s sex, age, and height. Body height was measured with a standard centimetre measure twice to record the average result. The lower limbs length was measured for the purpose of standardizing the dynamic balance scores on the therapy table. The measurement was taken from the upper anterior iliac spine to the media malleolus of the lower limb and recorded in centimetres [42]. Limb dominance was determined through a two-step procedure. First, participants indicated in the ques-tionnaire which leg they would use to kick a ball or to throw a ball the greatest distance. The same questions were then repeated verbally during testing to confirm consistency. If responses differed across questions, a follow-up item was asked: “If you were to shoot a ball at a target, which leg would you use to kick?”[43].

Isometric lower limb muscle strength

The maximal (peak) isometric strength of the hip abductors (HAbd), knee flexors (KF) and extensors (KE), and ankle plantarflexion (AP) muscles was assessed using a Lafayette Hand-Held Dynamometer (Lafayette Instrument Company, Lafayette IN, USA). All testing positions were performed exactly as illustrated in (Fig. 1)of Men-tiplay et al.[44], which provides open-access visual documentation of the standardized procedures. For HAbd, participants were positioned supine with the hip and knee extended. For AP, the ankle was placed in neutral, and the dynamometer applied to the forefoot (plantarflexion) and dorsum (dorsiflexion). KF and KE were measured in a seated position with the knee at 90◦ flexion. Participants were instructed to exert maximal push/pull for five seconds with the examiner maintaining stable counter-resistance. For AP and KE, examiner support was standardized using elbow bracing against a wall to maintain resistance stability. For each muscle group, participants first performed one submaximal familiarization trial (50% of perceived effort) to ensure they understood the required contraction. This served as a standardized warm-up. For maximal trials, participants were instructed: “At the count of three, push (or pull) as hard and as fast as you can and hold until I say relax.” Constant verbal encouragement (e.g., “push, push”/“pull, pull”) was provided throughout each trial to maximize effort, consistent with prior recommendations [44]. Each test was conducted three times, with a 90-s rest interval between trials to minimize fatigue. The highest value from three trials was used as the peak strength score. Isometric strength was measured in kilograms-force (kgf) using a Lafayette handheld dynamometer. To ensure comparability across participants, values were normalized to body mass and expressed in Newtons per kilogram (N/kg) by multiplying the kgf/kg ratio by 9.81 [45]. Dynamic balance

The dynamic balance was assessed using the Y-Balance Test kit (Functional Movement Systems, Lynchburg, VA, USA) following the standardized methodology described in our previous research [46, 47]. Participants maintained a single-leg stance with hands on hips and the support foot aligned to the platform’s center line. The non-weight-bearing leg was used to reach in three directions—anterior (ANT), posterolateral (PL), and posteromedial (PM)—by pushing a block as far as possible before returning. Participants performed the test barefoot, having first received standardised verbal instructions(“Stand on one leg, reach with the opposite leg as far as possible in the indicated direction, and return under control without touching down”) and completed practice trials. Testing was conducted with alternating legs and directions using the fixed order recommended by Plisky et al. [48]: right ANT, left ANT, right PM, left PM, right PL, left PL. Controlled movements of the supporting foot, such as heel or forefoot lifts, were permitted. Trials were invalidated if balance was lost, return to start was incomplete, or movements were uncontrolled. Each participant completed three successful trials per direction and leg. Reach distances were recorded to the nearest 1 cm and normalized to leg length (LL%). For each direction, the mean of the three trials was first calculated [42]. Composite reach (COMP) was then computed as the average of these three mean reach distances across three directions, expressed as a percentage of LL. The YBT demonstrates high reliability (ICCs = 0.75–0.91) [42, 49]. Standard error of measurement ranged from 1.77% to 5.81%, with minimal detectable change (MDC) varying from 4.90% to 16.10%, depending on direction and age group [49].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in Python (v3.11) using pandas (data manipu-lation), NumPy (numerical computations), SciPy (statistical tests), scikit-learn (data preprocessing and imputation), and statsmodels (linear mixed-effects modeling and multiple comparison corrections). The primary aim was to compare inter-limb asym-metry between single-sport specialists (score 3/3 on the Bell–Delphi specialization scale) and multi-sport athletes (≤ 2/3) across three primary outcomes: Global Asym-metry – average asymmetry across seven unilateral motor tests (YBT: ANT, PL, PM; HHD: HAbd, KF, KE, AP), Balance Asymmetry – mean asymmetry across three Y-Balance Test (YBT) directions (ANT, PM, PL), and Strength Asymmetry – mean asymmetry across four muscle groups (HAbd, KE, KF, and AP). We used asymmetry Index-9 [25], as it normalizes the absolute difference to the stronger limb value, avoiding arbitrary reference limb selection. This approach aligns with recommendations for unilateral tests where there is limb comparison between dominant/nondominant and stronger/weaker [20, 25]. Asymmetry was quantified using a normalized difference formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Index-9=\frac{|A-B|}{max(A, B)}\times 100$$\end{document}where A and B represent left and right limb values. This index expresses the absolute inter-limb difference relative to the stronger limb, producing a percentage scale where higher values indicate greater asymmetry. All three outcome variables were log(x + 1) transformed due to non-normal, right-skewed distributions (Shapiro–Wilk p < 0.05), allowing for variance stabilization and standardized coefficient interpretation. Homogeneity of variances was confirmed (Lev-ene’s test p > 0.05), and no influential outliers were identified (Cook’s Distance < 4/n), aside from one borderline case in the strength model. Covariates were selected based on a directed acyclic graphs-derived minimal adjustment set [50] and included sex, BMI, biological maturity offset, and weekly total training volume. Biological maturity off-set (MO), defined as the estimated number of years from peak height velocity (PHV), was calculated using the sex-specific regression equations proposed by Mirwald et al. [51]. The formula uses standing height, sitting height, leg length, chronological age, and body mass to estimate maturity status. Sport type (soccer, swimming, handball,gymnastics) was added as a fixed factor due to the small number of levels. All con-tinuous predictors were z-standardized to facilitate interpretation of standardized β coefficients. Primary group comparisons were performed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) with participant ID as a random intercept. Each model was fit separately for the three asymmetry outcomes. We reported standardized β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals, marginal R2 (fixed effects), and conditional R2 (total model variance) using the Nakagawa method. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d from model-adjusted marginal means, with interpretation thresholds set at: d: small ≈ 0.20, medium ≈ 0.50, large ≈ 0.80, R2: small ≈ 0.02, medium ≈ 0.13, large ≈ 0.26. Multiple testing correction was applied across the three outcomes using the Ben- jamini–Hochberg procedure (FDR threshold q = 0.10). Only corrected p-values were used to interpret statistical significance. Three pre-specified sensitivity analyses were conducted: 1) Excluding athletes training > 12 h/week in non-primary sports, 2) Excluding athletes in symmetric sports (e.g., swimming), and 3) Re-running mod-els without the injury history covariate. In addition, within each sport symmetry type (Symmetric vs. Asymmetric), group comparisons (Single vs. Multi-sport) were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests, with Cohen’s d effect sizes.

Results

Participant characteristics

Out of the initial 373 participants, a total of 76 athletes met the criteria described in Sect. 2.1 and were included in the analysis (52 multi-sport, 24 single-sport specialists, Table 1). The sample consisted of 72.4% male and 27.6% female, with no significant difference in sex distribution between the single-sport and multi-sport groups (χ2 = 0.532, p = 0.467, V = 0.07). The mean chronological age of participants was 12.6 ± 1.7 years and did not differ significantly between groups (single-sport: 12.9 ± 1.5 years; multi-sport: 12.4 ± 1.7 years; t = 0.48, p = 0.251, d = 0.29).The most rep-resented sports were soccer (57.9%), followed by swimming (23.7%), handball (10.5%), and gymnastics (7.9%). Significant between-group differences were observed in weekly training volumes, both for the main sport and for total participation across all sports. Single-sport athletes reported higher weekly training hours in their main sport (10.96 ± 3.61) compared to multi-sport athletes (9.07 ± 3.44), with a small-to-moderate effect size (U = 1.89, p = 0.040, r = − 0.29). Prevalence of interlimb asymmetries

A considerable proportion of young athletes exceeded commonly used asymmetry thresholds, particularly in strength-based measures (Fig. 1, Table 2). More than half of the participants surpassed the 10% asymmetry threshold for ankle plantarflexion (56.6%, n = 43), hip abduction (51.3%, n = 39), knee extension (50.0%, n = 38), and knee flexion (51.3%, n = 39). Alarmingly, 27.6% (n = 21) and 22.4% (n = 17) exceeded the 20% threshold in ankle plantarflexion and knee flexion, respectively. In contrast, Y-Balance Test (YBT) asymmetries were generally lower. Specifically, 30.3% (n = 23) of participants exceeded 10% threshold in the anterior reach, with 10.5% (n = 8) sur-passing 15% and 2.6% (n = 2) surpassing 20%. For the posteromedial direction, 13.2% (n = 10) exceeded 10%, 2.6% (n = 2) exceeded 15%, and 1.3% (n = 1) exceeded 20%. In the posterolateral direction, 11.8% (n = 9) exceeded 10% and 3.9% (n = 3) exceeded 15%, but no participants surpassed 20%. Discussion

Building on concerns about early specialization and asymmetry, our study aimed to test whether single-sport athletes exhibited greater neuromuscular imbalances than their multi-sport peers using standardized field-based assessments. We found no significant differences between single- and multi-sport athletes in global inter-limb asymmetry, strength asymmetry, or balance asymmetry—despite higher training volumes in the single-sport group. Over half the cohort exceeded the 10% asymme-try threshold in at least one strength task, particularly ankle plantarflexion and hip abduction, while balance asymmetries were generally lower and rarely surpassed clin-ical cut-offs. No interaction effects were observed by sex or sport symmetry type. Taken together, these findings indicate that, within this cohort, sport specialization status was not associated with inter-limb asymmetry as measured by Y-Balance Test performance and isometric strength assessments.

Interpretation of findings

In this study, “inter-limb asymmetry” was operationalized via Index-9 across seven unilateral tests (YBT ANT/PM/PL and isometric HAbd, KE, KF, AP); interpreta-tions are limited to these constructs. Contrary to our hypothesis, specialization status did not influence interlimb asymmetry in strength, balance, or combined domains. This challenges the assumption that early sport specialization promotes maladap-tive neuromuscular patterns. While previous studies emphasize the role of repetitive, unilateral loading in asymmetry development [13, 16], in our sample, sport specializa-tion alone—based on existing classification schemes [34, 39]—was not associated with greater interlimb asymmetries when assessed using isolated isometric strength tests and the Y-Balance Test.

Instead, asymmetry appears to be a common feature of youth athletic develop-ment, regardless of sport background. In our cohort, over 50% of athletes exceeded 10% strength asymmetry, particularly in ankle and hip musculature, consistent with findings from Fousekis et al. [13]. In contrast, balance asymmetries were smaller and infrequently surpassed clinical thresholds, supporting the notion that postural control is less sensitive to training exposure or limb dominance [18]. These findings reinforce the view that strength and balance asymmetries are distinct constructs with differing developmental and clinical implications [25, 46].

Furthermore, a substantial number of asymmetry values fell within reference ranges reported in healthy, uninjured populations. This questions the validity of universal thresholds such as the 10–15% rule and suggests that asymmetry should be interpreted contextually rather than dichotomously [20]. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that these findings were robust when accounting for training volume, sport type, and injury history. No moderating effects of sex or sport symmetry emerged, indicating that typical modifiers may exert minimal influence in early adolescence. The neuromuscular system of youth athletes may be more adaptable than previously assumed, potentially compensating for unilateral sport loading through movement variability or informal cross-training. Alternatively, current classification tools may oversimplify exposure by ignoring factors such as age of specialization and cumulative volume.

Comparison with existing literature

Our findings align with literature suggesting that asymmetry is prevalent in youth athletes but not necessarily problematic. Maloney et al. [52]. and Kliethermes et al. [53]. highlighted the widespread occurrence of strength asymmetries across sports and sexes. Similarly, Chapelle et al. [29] reported asymmetries ranging from 3.2–15.4% in elite youth tennis players across multiple lower-limb performance tasks, including single-leg countermovement jumps, single-leg forward hop tests, a 6 m single-leg hop test, and a 505-change-of-direction test. Iglesias-Caaman˜o et al. [54]. observed explo-sive strength asymmetries exceeding 10% in junior athletes during single-leg jumping tasks, while Fousekis et al. [13]. reported greater knee and ankle strength asymmetries using isokinetic concentric and eccentric testing, particularly in early training stages. Furthermore, Greenberg et al. [55]. demonstrated that fewer than half of healthy youth athletes achieved ≥ 90% limb-symmetry index across four single-leg hop tests (sin-gle, triple, crossover, and timed hops), questioning the validity of fixed return-to-sport cut-offs in this population. This may help explain our findings, as many athletes in both groups likely had moderate training ages. Regarding balance, our mean composite asymmetry (∼ 6%) is similar to those reported by Iglesias-Caaman˜o et al. [54]. and lower than the thresholds typically associated with elevated injury risk. In the literature, both absolute reach differences (e.g. > 4 cm) and normalized (% leg length) values have been used to quan-tify Y-Balance or SEBT asymmetries when predicting injury risk [56, 57]. Although anterior reach differences > 4 cm on the Y-Balance Test have been linked to injury in some studies [58, 59], other work (Lai et al., 2017) found poor predictive value of such cut-offs. A recent systematic review also concluded that associations between inter-limb asymmetries and injury risk remain inconsistent, highlighting methodolog-ical variability in how asymmetry is defined and reported [60]. This supports recent calls to use standardized indices (Index-9) and interpret asymmetry in a continuous, individualized manner [25].

Our results also speak to the concept of “sporting asymmetries” [51], where side-dominant adaptations reflect sport-specific demands rather than dysfunction. Sports involving repeated unilateral actions—e.g., kicking in soccer—may naturally lead to strength asymmetry. In our sample, substantial asymmetries were observed across athletes, with more than half exceeding a 10% threshold in all four strength measures. In our study, however, athletes from asymmetric sports did not differ significantly in asymmetry levels from their peers. This indicates that while asymmetry was common across the sample (with over half exceeding the 10% threshold), it was not uniquely elevated in athletes from asymmetric sports. Short-term or moderate sport-specific exposure alone may therefore be insufficient to explain functional imbalances, and other factors (e.g., training age, motor variability) may moderate these effects.

Implications of the study

Clinically, our results suggest that strength asymmetries are common in youth ath-letes, but not necessarily maladaptive or specialization-driven. Rather than assuming that early single-sport participation is an asymmetry red flag, practitioners should assess athletes individually. While average asymmetries were within acceptable ranges, a notable subgroup exceeded 20–30%, especially in ankle plantarflexion and hip abduction—suggesting these athletes may benefit from targeted bilateral or unilat-eral strength training. We support the use of field-based tools like HHD and YBT but urge caution in applying rigid thresholds in uninjured, developing athletes. Interpreta-tion should account for limb dominance, sport demands, growth status, and training history. Additionally, it should be noted that our findings may not generalize to ath-letes from lateral-dominant sports, such as badminton, tennis, baseball or fencing, where repetitive unilateral actions could produce larger or sport-specific asymmetries. Ultimately, youth development programs should emphasize training quality, load man-agement, and movement variability—rather than limiting specialization per se—as core strategies for promoting functional symmetry and long-term athletic development. It is also important to recognize that our single-sport group was primarily composed of soccer, swimming, handball, and gymnastics athletes. Whether similar findings would be observed in sports with highly lateralized demands (e.g., fencing, badminton, tennis) remains uncertain. Such contexts may amplify asymmetry development and warrant targeted investigation in future research.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the use of a con-venience sample recruited through educational sessions can limit the generalizability of the findings to all youth athletes. Second, although we assessed injury history through interviews and questionnaires, we did not gather detailed data on injury type, severity, or rehabilitation, which may have influenced performance outcomes. Third, while we included questions about quitting other sports as part of specialization scoring, we did not capture the specific age at which athletes discontinued multi-sport participation, which could provide additional insight into developmental timing effects. Furthermore, although we controlled for several covariates (e.g., age, sex, BMI, maturity offset), unmeasured confounding (e.g., training intensity, psychosocial factors) remains pos-sible. Our study did not include a sufficient number of athletes for lateral-dominant sports to determine whether early specialization in these contexts may lead to greater asymmetries. The sample covered a broad age range (8–16 years), spanning prepubertal to post-

PHV stages, and included athletes from multiple sports with unequal group sizes between single- and multi-sport participants. This heterogeneity reflects real-world diversity but limits the feasibility of robust subgroup analyses and reduces generaliz-ability. In addition, while the Y-Balance Test and handheld dynamometry are widely used, clinically feasible tools with good reliability in youth, they are not gold-standard measures. Handheld dynamometry may underestimate absolute strength compared with isokinetic dynamometry, and the YBT offers only a proxy of dynamic balance rather than direct neuromuscular control. Thus, findings should be interpreted within the context of these task-specific assessments, and future studies with larger, stratified samples are needed to better examine age- and sport-specific patterns.

Suggestions for future research and specialization classification

In our cohort, single-sport athletes started earlier and trained more, yet did not show greater asymmetry. This discrepancy highlights the need to refine the way sport spe-cialization is defined and measured. Despite extensive literature, classification remains inconsistent [36], and many studies rely on binary groupings that overlook nuances of training volume, intensity, and duration. Baker et al. [35] noted that although the discourse on early specialization has grown, little empirical progress has been made in understanding its actual mecha-nisms—such as reduced movement variability, excessive load, or psychosocial strain. They, along with Mosher et al. [16], advocate for prospective, multivariate studies spanning the full spectrum of participation—especially high-exposure and multi-sport athletes—to clarify developmental impacts.

A key limitation is the lack of standardized exposure thresholds. The Delphi con-sensus by Bell et al. [34] defines specialization as “intentional, focused participation in a single sport for the majority of the year that restricts other sports,” but does not specify the load or frequency. This vagueness may contribute to conflicting findings across studies. Emerging views also challenge the idea that early specialization is inherently harm-ful. The IOC consensus [33] emphasizes that risk arises not from specialization per se, but from poor program design. When properly structured with varied movement, recovery, and psychosocial support, single-sport environments may still foster healthy development. In our sample, single-sport athletes reported higher weekly volumes both in their main sport and across all activities. This raises an important question: does specialization reduce sport diversity, or simply reflect greater total training commit-ment? Differentiating these factors is critical, as volume alone may drive neuromuscular adaptation.

Future research should move beyond binary classification. Exposure-based met-rics—such as dominant-side load, asymmetry burden, and cumulative hours—are may be appropriate. Longitudinal studies are needed to track asymmetry trajectories and determine if high asymmetry predicts injury (especially in terms of thresholds). Additionally, research should evaluate whether targeted interventions reduce injury risk or improve function, and whether these benefits relate to asymmetry itself or general neuromuscular improvements. Further work should also examine these ques-tions in more heterogeneous cohorts—for instance, by comparing athletes across clubs with distinct training cultures. Moreover, investigations should examine sport-specific effects, particularly in highly lateralized single-sport activities to clarify whether these sports disproportionately contribute to interlimb asymmetries. Furthermore, repeated within-subject assessments (e.g., every 6–12 months over multiple seasons) may reveal individual developmental pathways and clarify whether functional asymmetries fluc-tuate meaningfully over time. Such designs could test the hypothesis that sinle-sport specialization is not”bad” [35], provided training is structured with adequate vari-ation, recovery, and psychosocial support. Group-level averages, while informative, often obscure this individual variability. Finally, studies should explicitly track injury incidence and type, as the relationship between asymmetry and injury may depend on the anatomical region and the injury mechanism.

Conclusion

Single-sport youth athletes, despite reporting higher training volumes, did not demon-strate greater interlimb asymmetry in strength or balance compared with their multi-sport peers. Strength asymmetries exceeding 10% were common across the cohort, particularly in ankle plantarflexion and hip abduction, whereas balance asym-metries remained generally low. These findings indicate that asymmetry is prevalent in youth athletes but not evidently linked to specialization status. Future work should clarify whether these asymmetries reflect normative development, sport-specific adaptations, or potential risk factors for later injury.
