Channel Estimation for Full-duplex Multi-tag Ambient Backscatter Communication Systems with I/Q Imbalance
Saeed Abdallah, Mahmoud A. Albreem, Bassel Al Homssi, Mohamed Saad, Abdulmalik Alwarafy

Abstract
Ambient backscatter communication (AmBC) has emerged as a highly attractive paradigm for energy-efficient communication. Full-duplex multi-tag AmBC systems provide the scalability and efficient spectrum utilization essential for next generation Internet-of-Things (IoT) networks. However, the presence of multiple tags, self-interference and hardware impairments such as inphase/quadrature (I/Q) imbalance, makes accurate channel estimation indispensable for efficient interference management. The large number of channel parameters and the presence of mirror images of each signal component necessitate careful design of the channel estimation phase to prevent performance degradation. In this work, we propose a novel three-stage training protocol and pilot-based estimation scheme that ensure signal orthogonality and successfully avoid error floors. We also propose two semi-blind estimators, one based on decision-directed (DD) criterion and the other on the expectation conditional maximization (ECM) framework. By exploiting both pilots and data symbols, these two estimators achieve higher estimation accuracy than pilot-based estimation, at the cost of additional complexity. Cramer-Rao bounds (CRBs) for both types of estimation are also derived. The pilot-based estimator and the ECM estimator approach their respective CRBs, while the DD estimator performs mid-way between them. The three proposed solutions support different use cases by offering distinct tradeoffs between performance and computational complexity.



I
Introduction

The rising demands of the Internet-of-Things (IoT) have especially highlighted the need for more robust wireless communication
[
1
]
, to handle the pervasiveness of connected devices and the massive volumes of data transmitted in real time. Future networks are expected to provide ultra-low latency, very high throughput, and continuity. Next-generation wireless networks, such as 5G and beyond, are being developed to fulfill these varied needs. They implement features including massive MIMO, mmWave communications, and powerful coding to enhance data throughput and extend network coverage
[
2
]
. While these techniques improve the performance of wireless systems, energy efficiency is usually lagging behind
[
3
]
. Most IoT deployments involve battery-operated devices that have a small energy source. Consequently, future communication paradigms must be designed to meet the performance-power trade off in order to enable next-generation networks to operate efficiently in an energy-constrained manner.

Ambient backscatter communication (AmBC) is a novel paradigm with the potential to revolutionize wireless communication by enabling ultra-low-power connected pervasive IoT devices
[
4
]
. AmBC enables devices to transmit data by modulating and reflecting ambient RF signals that are always present in the environment
[
4
]
. A tag uses these signals—Wi-Fi, cellular, and satellite signals—to create its own signal by altering the reflectivity of the device’s antenna according to the data to be sent, where nearby receivers can then pick up these variations
[
5
,
6
]
. AmBC systems can be operated with low cost and very low power consumption, making them ideal for long-lifetime IoT applications.

While non-coherent operation has been considered in traditional AmBC, both cooperative and full-duplex AmBC systems require accurate channel state information (CSI). In the former, the CSI is needed to recover the information of both the RF source and the tag from the superposed signal. In the latter, channel estimation is additionally needed to handle the residual self-interference (RSI). The need for accurate CSI becomes even more evident in the multi-tag case where it is essential to separate the overlapping backscattered signals.

I-A

Related Work

Researchers have sought to provide efficient solutions to the AmBC channel estimation problem. For traditional AmBC systems, blind channel estimation was proposed in
[
7
]
using the expectation maximization (EM) algorithm. In cooperative AmBC systems
[
8
]
, the RF source can collaborate with the tags and the reader, enabling the transmission of pilot sequences. Hence, in
[
9
]
pilot sequences and an iterative estimator were proposed for AmBC systems where the reader is equipped with a large intelligent surface. EM-based semi-blind estimation for the cooperative scenario was proposed in
[
10
]
. The case of a massive-antenna equipped reader was studied in
[
11
]
. Using pilot transmission from the source, a two-stage algorithm was proposed to jointly estimate the channels and direction-of-arrival (DoA). Pilot-based channel estimation was cast as a denoising problem in
[
12
]
, and convolutional neural network (CNN)-based deep residual learning was employed, achieving close performance to the minimum mean squared error (MMSE) method. Pilot-based channel estimation was also cast as a denoising problem in
[
13
]
, where conditional generative adversarial networks were employed to leverage the spatial and temporal features of the pilot signals, leading to improved performance, especially at low SNR. Channel estimation for full-duplex AmBC systems with multi-antenna readers was studied in
[
14
]
. Both the AP and the legacy user (LU) were assumed to operate in full-duplex mode, which resulted in the backscattering of both their signals. In addition to a pilot-based least-squares (LS) estimator, two semi-blind estimators were explored, one based on the decision-directed (DD) strategy and the other on the EM framework. The semi-blind methods yielded superior accuracy and required lower training overhead, but had higher computational complexity.

The above methods did not take into consideration the impact of hardware impairments such as inphase/quadrature (I/Q) imbalance and carrier frequency offset (CFO). Ambient backscatter readers and tags are typically built around low-cost direct-conversion (zero-IF) radios to minimize size, cost, and power. In such radios, the RF signal is mixed directly to baseband using quadrature oscillators, making them particularly vulnerable to I/Q imbalance. Specifically, any gain or phase mismatch between the in-phase and quadrature branches directly creates image-frequency interference at baseband. This impairment is intrinsic to the front-end architecture and cannot be eliminated by filtering. I/Q imbalance is particularly noteworthy for introducing mirror images of signals, which further exacerbate interference and increase the number of parameters to be estimated
[
15
]
. This issue was addressed in
[
16
]
, where the channel, I/Q imbalance and CFO were jointly estimated using both pilot-based and EM-based techniques. Joint estimation of the channel and I/Q imbalance was also studied for OFDM-based full-duplex AmBC systems in
[
15
]
, where appropriate training was designed and both a pilot-based LS method and a DD semi-blind method were proposed. The former had lower complexity, while the latter yielded higher estimation accuracy. Joint estimation of the CFO and the sampling frequency offset (SFO) for AmBC systems was considered in
[
17
]
, where appropriate pilot sequences were designed to optimize the joint estimation.

The above works mostly assume a single tag. The multi-tag scenario is more challenging since it requires the estimation of multiple interfering cascaded channels from the superposition of backscattered signals. Although it is possible to apply pilot-based single-tag algorithms by activating only a single tag at a time, this is highly inefficient and imposes an impractical training overhead
[
18
]
. Consequently, the authors of
[
18
]
proposed several pilot designs to simultaneously estimate the channel in the multi-tag scenario, showing that the joint scheme outperforms single-tag techniques.

Contribution and Organization

Existing works on multi-tag channel estimation have mainly considered pilot-based estimation

1

The authors published a preliminary study on semi-blind channel estimation for multi-tag AmBC systems in the conference paper
[
19

, however the work therein considered a half-duplex system with perfect I/Q balance.

[

]
. Semi-blind estimation is capable of achieving higher accuracy, lower pilot overhead and superior spectrum utilization
[

. Furthermore, the work in
[
18

does not consider the impact of I/Q imbalance, which can introduce mirror images of the direct component and backscattered component, resulting in significant performance degradation and requiring accurate compensation. Moreover, the work in
[
18
]
considers half-duplex AmBC. Full-duplex AmBC would add extra complexity due to the presence of RSI and the backscattering of both the AP and LU signals.

In this work, we address the problem of channel estimation in a multi-tag full-duplex AmBC system that is affected by both transmit (TX) and receive (RX) I/Q imbalance. In our work (like
[
18
]
), we assume that the tags transmit PSK symbols, whereby they can modify the phase of the backscattered signal. The use of complex (phase-shifted) tag symbols in the presence of TX and RX I/Q imbalances completely transforms the received signal. In particular, the received signal corresponding to each backscattered component involves a total of four components, in contrast to the two components considered in
[
15
]
and
[
21
]
. This necessitates the estimation of four channel components for each tag. Additionally, the fact that both the LU and AP operate in full-duplex mode means that the tags backscatter the signals from both the LU and the AP, as in
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
. Therefore, for each tag a total of eight channel components need to be estimated. The resulting estimation problem is highly challenging and cannot be solved using simple combinations of existing methods. Designing appropriate pilot sequences is necessary to guarantee orthogonality among the signal components during the training and avoid potential error floors.

In addressing the above challenges, we consider both pilot-based and semi-blind estimation strategies. We first develop a training protocol consisting of three phases, and select the training sequences in each phase to guarantee orthogonality among all signal components. We also develop a pilot-based estimation algorithm that goes hand-in-hand with the above protocol. As a benchmark, the Cramer-Rao bound (CRB) for pilot-based estimation is obtained. Importantly, the performance of the proposed algorithm coincides with the CRB.

In addition, we develop two semi-blind estimation methods, which offer different performance/complexity tradeoffs. The first semi-blind estimator is based on the DD estimation strategy, whereby the pilot-based estimator is used to detect a sequence of transmitted data symbols, and then another estimation stage is applied using both the original pilots and the detected data symbols. Alternating maximization is used to facilitate the estimation of all parameters, and the resulting scheme is referred to as the AMDD estimator. While it yields improved accuracy over the pilot-based estimation, it can suffer from error propagation, which limits the performance improvement at low SNR. As a remedy, another semi-blind algorithm is proposed based on the expectation conditional maximization (ECM) approach. This iterative method uses the posterior probabilities of the data symbols, allowing it to achieve superior performance. The semi-blind modified CRB (MCRB) is also obtained as a benchmark for semi-blind estimators. The ECM performs closest to the MCRB, almost coinciding with it at high SNR.

The contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

A novel training protocol is developed for full-duplex multi-tag AmBC systems with I/Q imbalance and a multi-antenna reader, guaranteeing orthogonality among all signal components during the training.

•

A pilot-based estimator is developed that goes hand-in-hand with the above protocol and enables the estimation of all channel parameters without error floors.

•

A DD-based semi-blind estimator is proposed, achieving higher accuracy than the pilot-based estimator.

•

Another semi-blind estimator is developed based on the ECM criterion. This estimator yields the highest accuracy.

•

As theoretical benchmarks, both the pilot-based and semi-blind CRBs are obtained. The pilot-based estimator coincides with the pilot-based CRB, while the ECM converges to the semi-blind CRB at high SNR.

•

Extensive simulation results are used to illustrate the performances of proposed algorithms, and investigate various tradeoffs.

III
Pilot-Design and Pilot-based Channel Estimation

In this section, we propose a pilot-based estimation scheme and an appropriate design of pilot sequences that together enable the estimation of all the required channel coefficients. It is very difficult to estimate the channel coefficients simultaneously in one shot, due to the interference and coupling among the different components. The transmitted LU signal appears both individually, as well multiplied by the signals of all the tags, and their mirror images. The same applies to the transmitted AP signal. Orthogonality also needs to hold across the signals of the multiple tags. To effectively address this problem, we divide our proposed scheme into three stages.

III-
1
Pilot Training Phase 1

The first training phase aims to estimate the direct and self-interference channels. To avoid interference from the backscattered signals, all the tags are assumed to be in the absorbing state. We let
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}\triangleq\big[s_{1}[1],\ldots,s_{1}[N_{1}]\big]^{T}
be the sequence of training symbols transmitted by the LU. We also let
{\boldsymbol{r}}_{m}\triangleq\big[r_{m}[1],\ldots r_{m}[N_{1}]\big]^{T}
be the sequence of training symbols transmitted by the
m
th antenna of the AP during the same itnerval. We also define the
N_{1}\times M
matrix
{\boldsymbol{R}}\triangleq\big[{\boldsymbol{r}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{r}}_{M}\big]
. The corresponding
M\times N_{1}
received signal at the AP is expressed as

{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{1}=\bar{{\boldsymbol{h}}}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}^{T}+\check{{\boldsymbol{h}}}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}^{H}+\bar{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}{\boldsymbol{R}}^{T}+\check{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}{\boldsymbol{R}}^{H}+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{1},

(6)

where the matrix
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{1}
represents AWGN during Phase 1. Letting
{\boldsymbol{H}}\triangleq[\bar{{\boldsymbol{h}}}\ \ \check{{\boldsymbol{h}}}]
,
{\boldsymbol{Q}}\triangleq[\bar{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}\ \ \check{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}]
,
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}\triangleq[{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}\ \ {\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}^{*}]
and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{R}}}\triangleq[{\boldsymbol{R}}\ \ {\boldsymbol{R}}^{*}]
, we rewrite (
7
) as

{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{1}={\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}^{T}+{\boldsymbol{Q}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{R}}}^{T}+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{1}.

(7)

To represent the signal in vector form, we let
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{1})
,
{\boldsymbol{h}}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}({\boldsymbol{H}})
,
{\boldsymbol{q}}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}({\boldsymbol{Q}})
and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{1}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}({\boldsymbol{W}}_{1})
. We also define the matrix
{\boldsymbol{P}}\triangleq[{\boldsymbol{S}}\ \ \tilde{{\boldsymbol{R}}}]
and the matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{P}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{I}}_{M}
. The desired channel parameters are collected into the vector
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\triangleq[{\boldsymbol{h}}^{T},{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}]^{T}
.
It can be verified that

{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}={\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{1}.

(8)

Hence, we can estimate the vector
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
using the LS criterion as

\hat{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}={\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}^{\dagger}{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}
. We can extract from
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}
the estimates
\hat{\bar{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}
,
\hat{\check{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}
,
\hat{\bar{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}}
,
\hat{\check{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}}
.

Following Theorem 2 in
[
18
]

{\boldsymbol{P}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{P}}
is a diagonal matrix. Hence, optimal pilot design requires
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}^{*}

{\boldsymbol{r}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{1}^{*},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{r}}_{M},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{M}^{*}

M+1

Other types of complex sequences, like ZC sequences, violate some of the orthogonality requirements (e.g., orthogonality between the sequence and its complex conjugate), which would result in non-resolvable error floors. Hence, the pilot sequences are obtained by selecting different columns of the DFT matrix. We let
{\boldsymbol{F}}
be the
N_{1}\times N_{1}

element is
1/\sqrt{N_{1}}e^{-\jmath 2\pi(i-1)(j-1)/N_{1}}

(N_{1}-2)/2
columns. Since we need to select

{\boldsymbol{F}}

(N_{1}-2)/2\geq M+1

{\boldsymbol{T}}_{k}\triangleq\mbox{Diag}({\boldsymbol{t}}_{k})
. The resulting
M\times N_{1}
received signal matrix at the AP is

\begin{split}&{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{2}^{(1)}=\bar{{\boldsymbol{h}}}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}^{(1)T}+\check{{\boldsymbol{h}}}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}^{(1)H}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\Big(\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}^{(1)T}{\boldsymbol{T}}_{k}\\
&+\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}^{(1)H}{\boldsymbol{T}}_{k}^{*}+\dot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}^{(1)T}{\boldsymbol{T}}_{k}^{*}+\ddot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}^{(1)H}{\boldsymbol{T}}_{k}\Big)+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}^{(1)}_{2},\end{split}

(9)

where
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}^{(1)}_{2}
represents the AWGN matrix.

We set the LU signal as
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}^{(1)}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{1}}_{N_{2}}
. Hence,
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}^{(1)T}{\boldsymbol{T}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{1}}_{N_{2}}{\boldsymbol{T}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{t}}_{k}^{T}
. Furthermore, using the channel estimates from Phase 1, we cancel the direct signals from
{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{2}^{(1)}
, obtaining

\begin{split}&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{2}^{(1)}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{2}^{(1)}-\hat{\bar{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}^{(1)T}-\hat{\check{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}^{(1)H}\\
&=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\left(\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}+\ddot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}\right){\boldsymbol{t}}_{k}^{(1)T}+\left(\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}+\dot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}\right){\boldsymbol{t}}_{k}^{(1)H}+{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(1)}_{2}+{\boldsymbol{E}}^{(1)}_{2},\end{split}

(10)

where
{\boldsymbol{E}}^{(1)}_{2}
is the residual error from the estimation of
\bar{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
and
\check{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
. This makes it possible to estimate the aggregate vectors
\tilde{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}\triangleq\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}+\ddot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}
and
\tilde{\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}\triangleq\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}+\dot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}
.
We let

{\boldsymbol{T}}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{t}}_{1}&\ldots&{\boldsymbol{t}}_{K}&{\boldsymbol{t}}_{1}^{*}&\ldots&{\boldsymbol{t}}_{K}^{*}\end{bmatrix},

(11)

and
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{2}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{T}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{I}}_{M}
. We also define the vector
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{(1)}\triangleq\big[\tilde{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{1}^{T},\ldots,\tilde{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{K}^{T},\tilde{\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{1}^{T},\ldots,\tilde{\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{K}^{T}\big]^{T}
. Hence, the vector
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{2}^{(1)}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}\big(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{2}^{(1)}\big)
can be expressed as

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{2}^{(1)}={\boldsymbol{A}}_{2}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{(1)}+{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(1)}_{2}+{\boldsymbol{e}}^{(1)}_{2}.

(12)

where
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(1)}_{2}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}({\boldsymbol{W}}^{(1)}_{2})
and
{\boldsymbol{e}}^{(1)}_{2}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}({\boldsymbol{E}}^{(1)}_{2})
.
We thus estimate the vector
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{(1)}
by

\hat{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}^{(1)}={\boldsymbol{A}}_{2}^{\dagger}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{2}^{(1)}
.

As in Phase 1, we need to ensure that
{\boldsymbol{T}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{T}}
is a diagonal matrix. This can be achieved by selecting the vectors
{\boldsymbol{t}}_{k}
from the columns of an
N_{2}
-point DFT matrix. To have a sufficient number of orthogonal columns, we need
N_{2}\geq 2K+2
.

In the second sub-phase, we use the same training sequences for the tags, but use LU signal
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}^{(2)}=\jmath{\boldsymbol{1}}_{N_{2}}
. After removing the direct channel interference, the received signal is

\begin{split}&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{2}^{(2)}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\jmath\left(\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}-\ddot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}\right){\boldsymbol{t}}_{k}^{T}+\jmath\left(\dot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}-\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}\right){\boldsymbol{t}}_{k}^{H}+{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(2)}_{2}+{\boldsymbol{E}}^{(2)}_{2},\end{split}

(13)

where
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}^{(2)}_{2}
represents the AWGN matrix and
{\boldsymbol{E}}^{(2)}_{2}
is the residual error from the estimation of
\bar{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
and
\check{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
. It is now possible to estimate the vectors
\bar{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}\triangleq\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}-\ddot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}
and
\bar{\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}\triangleq\dot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}-\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}
.
Defining the vector
\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{(2)}\triangleq\big[\bar{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{1}^{T},\ldots,\bar{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{K}^{T},\bar{\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{1}^{T},\ldots,\bar{\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{K}^{T}\big]^{T}
and letting
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{2}^{(2)}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{2}^{(2)})
, we obtain

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{2}^{(2)}=\jmath{\boldsymbol{A}}_{2}\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{(2)}+{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(2)}_{2}+{\boldsymbol{e}}^{(2)}_{2}.

(14)

where
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(2)}_{2}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}({\boldsymbol{W}}^{(1)}_{2})
and
{\boldsymbol{e}}^{(2)}_{2}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}({\boldsymbol{E}}^{(1)}_{2})
. We may thus estimate the vector
\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{(2)}
as

\hat{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}^{(2)}=(\jmath{\boldsymbol{A}}_{2})^{\dagger}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{2}^{(2)}
.

Once the vectors
\tilde{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}
,
\tilde{\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}
,
\bar{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}
and
\bar{\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}
have been estimated, the estimates for the individual vectors
\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}
,
\ddot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}
,
\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}
and
\dot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}
can be readily obtained by noting that
\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}=\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}+\bar{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k})
,
\ddot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}-\bar{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k})
,
\dot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}+\bar{\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k})
and
\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}-\bar{\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k})
.

III-
3
Pilot Training Phase 3

In this phase, we estimate
\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}
,
\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}
,
\dot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}
and
\ddot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,K
, all of which are
M\times M
matrices. We assume that the AP transmits while the LU remains silent. If the tags transmit together, it is extremely challenging to find training sequences that guarantee orthogonality among all the channels, since all the tags’ signals are multiplied by the same AP’s sequence. Using DFT sequences will not ensure orthogonality in this case because of the matrix channels. Therefore, we divide this phase into
K
stages, during each of which only one tag is active while the others are silent. Furthermore, each stage is further divided in two sub-stages, during each of which the tag transmits a pilot sequence of length
N_{3}
.

During the first sub-stage of the
k
-th tag’s transmission, the tag transmits the
N_{3}\times 1
training sequence
{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}_{k}
. The training sequences transmitted by the AP’s
M
antennas during the same interval are collected into the
N_{3}\times M
matrix
{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}\triangleq\big[{\boldsymbol{r}}_{1}^{(3)},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{r}}_{M}^{(3)}\big]
. We also let
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{R}}}_{3}\triangleq[{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}\ {\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{*}]
. The corresponding received signal is

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{3}^{(k,1)}&={\boldsymbol{Q}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{R}}}_{3}^{T}+\Big(\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{k}+\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{H}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{k}^{*}\\
&+\dot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{k}^{*}+\ddot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{H}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{k}\Big)+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{3}^{(k,1)},\end{split}

(15)

where
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{k}\triangleq\mbox{Diag}({\boldsymbol{\varrho}}_{k})
and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{3}^{(k,1)}
is the AWGN matrix. We cancel the self-interference terms from
{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{3}^{(k,1)}
, obtaining

\begin{split}&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{3}^{(k,1)}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{3}^{(k,1)}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{R}}}_{3}^{T}\\
&=\Big(\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{k}+\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{H}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{k}^{*}+\dot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{k}^{*}\\
&+\ddot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{H}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{k}\Big)+{\boldsymbol{W}}_{3}^{(k,1)}+{\boldsymbol{E}}_{3}^{(k,1)},\end{split}

(16)

where
{\boldsymbol{E}}_{3}^{(k,1)}
represents the residual error from the cancellation. Furthermore, we use as the tag’s training sequence
{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{1}}_{L}
, resulting in
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{I}}_{N_{3}}
. Hence,

\begin{split}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{3}^{(k,1)}&=(\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}+\dot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}){\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{T}+(\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}+\ddot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}){\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{H}+{\boldsymbol{W}}_{3}^{(k,1)}+{\boldsymbol{E}}_{3}^{(k,1)}.\end{split}

(17)

We let
\tilde{\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k}\triangleq\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}+\dot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}
,
\tilde{\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k}\triangleq\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}+\ddot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}
,
\tilde{\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}_{k}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}(\tilde{\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k})
,
\tilde{\check{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}_{k}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}(\tilde{\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k})
and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{k}\triangleq\big[\tilde{\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}_{k}^{T},\tilde{\check{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}_{k}^{T}\big]^{T}
. We also let
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{3}^{(k,1)}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}\left(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{3}^{(k,1)}\right)
,
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{3}^{(k,1)}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}\left({\boldsymbol{W}}_{3}^{(k,1)}\right)
and
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{3}^{(k,1)}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}\left({\boldsymbol{E}}_{3}^{(k,1)}\right)
. Moreover, we let
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{3}\triangleq\tilde{{\boldsymbol{R}}}_{3}\otimes{\boldsymbol{I}}_{M}
. Therefore, we can express
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{3}^{(k,1)}
as

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{3}^{(k,1)}={\boldsymbol{A}}_{3}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{k}+{\boldsymbol{w}}_{3}^{(k,1)}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{3}^{(k,1)}.

(18)

Hence, we estimate
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{k}
by

\hat{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{A}}_{3}^{\dagger}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{3}^{(k,1)}
.
To ensure that
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{R}}}_{3}^{H}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{R}}}_{3}
is diagonal, we select the vectors
{\boldsymbol{r}}_{1}^{(3)},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(3)}_{M}
as columns of the
N_{3}
-point DFT matrix, where
N_{3}\geq 2M+2
.

In the second sub-stage, the
k
th tag’s pilot sequence is set to
\jmath{\boldsymbol{1}}_{N_{3}}
. We also let
\bar{\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k}\triangleq\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}-\dot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}
and
\bar{\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k}\triangleq\ddot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}-\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}
. The corresponding received after interference cancellation is

\begin{split}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{3}^{(k,2)}&=\jmath\bar{\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{T}+\jmath\bar{\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{H}+{\boldsymbol{W}}_{3}^{(k,2)}+{\boldsymbol{E}}_{3}^{(k,2)},\end{split}

(19)

where
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{3}^{(k,2)}
is the AWGN matrix and
{\boldsymbol{E}}_{3}^{(k,2)}
is the residual error. We also let
\bar{\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}_{k}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}(\bar{\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k})
,
\bar{\check{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}_{k}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}(\bar{\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k})
and
\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{k}\triangleq\big[\bar{\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}_{k}^{T},\bar{\check{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}_{k}^{T}\big]^{T}
. Furthermore, letting
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{3}^{(k,2)}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}\left(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{3}^{(k,2)}\right)
,
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{3}^{(k,2)}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}\left({\boldsymbol{W}}_{3}^{(k,2)}\right)
and
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{3}^{(k,2)}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}\left({\boldsymbol{E}}_{3}^{(k,2)}\right)
, we have

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{3}^{(k,2)}=\jmath{\boldsymbol{A}}_{3}\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{k}+{\boldsymbol{w}}_{3}^{(k,2)}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{3}^{(k,2)}.

(20)

Hence, we estimate
\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{k}
by
\hat{\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}_{k}=(\jmath{\boldsymbol{A}}_{3})^{\dagger}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{3}^{(k,1)}
. After estimating
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{k}
and
\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{k}
, it is straightforward to obtain the estimates of the channels
\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}
,
\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}
,
\dot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}
and
\ddot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}
.

provides a flowchart showing the sequence and inputs/outputs of each stage of the pilot-based estimator. The total number of pilot transmission slots for all three phases is given by
N_{1}+2N_{2}+2KN_{3}
. Considering a typical channel coherence time of
100
ms
[

]
and that AmBC systems have been shown to achieve data rates in the range of 1-5 Mbps
[

, the required number of pilots does not represent a significant overhead or bottleneck. For instance, considering
M=4
antennas and

tags, the number of required pilots is
N_{tot}=96
. Assuming a backscattering rate of

Mpbs, the pilot transmission would represent less than
0.1\%
of the coherence time.

IV
Semi-blind Alternate Maximization Decision-Directed Estimator

In this section, we propose a semi-blind estimator based on the DD criterion. Specifically, we use the pilot-based estimates acquired in Section
III
to detect
D
data transmissions. Another estimation stage is then applied whereby both the pilot sequences and the detected data symbols are used to re-estimate the channel coefficients. The detected data symbols are treated as if they are known pilots, enabling more accurate channel estimation, despite the possibility of error propagation from erroneous decisions. Due to the intractability of simultaneously estimating all the channel parameters, the re-estimation stage is performed by alternately maximizing the likelihood function with respect to the various channel vectors/matrices until all the parameters have been estimated. We refer to the proposed scheme as the alternate maximization DD (AMDD) estimator.

The LU transmits
D
data symbols, collected into the vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}\triangleq\big[x[1],\ldots,x[D]\big]^{T}
, where
\mathbb{E}\left\{|x[i]|^{2}\right\}=P_{x}
. The LU symbols are assumed to be generated from the set
\mathcal{S}
, of cardinality
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
. We also let
{\boldsymbol{X}}\triangleq[{\boldsymbol{x}}\ \ {\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}]
. In the same interval, each tag transmits
D
symbols, collected into the vector
{\boldsymbol{d}}_{k}\triangleq\big[d_{k}[1],\ldots,d_{k}[D]\big]^{T}
. We let
{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}\triangleq\mbox{Diag}({\boldsymbol{d}}_{k})
. The tag symbols are generated from the PSK set
\mathcal{D}
, of cardinality
\bar{\mathcal{D}}
. In contrast to the data symbols of the tags, those of the LU and the AP can be drawn from any constellation. Operating in full-duplex mode, the AP transmits the signals collected in the
D\times M
matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}\triangleq\big[{\boldsymbol{c}}[1],\ldots{\boldsymbol{c}}[D]\big]^{T}
, where
{\boldsymbol{c}}[n]
is the
M\times 1
vector transmitted during the
n
th time slot. We let
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}\triangleq[{\boldsymbol{C}}\ \ {\boldsymbol{C}}^{*}]
. The received signal at the AP is

\begin{split}&{\boldsymbol{Z}}={\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}+{\boldsymbol{Q}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}^{T}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\Big(\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}+\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}^{*}\\
&+\dot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}^{*}+\ddot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}\Big)+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\Big(\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}+\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}^{*}\\
&+\dot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}^{*}+\ddot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}\Big)+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}},\end{split}

(21)

where
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}
represents the AWGN matrix. Before proceeding to the estimation stage, we need to detect the data symbols. Maximum-likelihood (ML) detection is discussed below.

IV-A

ML Detection

In this step, we detect the vectors
{\boldsymbol{x}}
,
{\boldsymbol{d}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{d}}_{K}
. We begin by canceling the self-interference from
{\boldsymbol{Z}}
, using the pilot-based estimate
\hat{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}
obtaining
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{Z}}}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{Z}}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}^{T}
. Subsequently, ML data detection is given by

\begin{split}&\{\hat{x}[\ell],\hat{d}_{1}[\ell],\ldots,\hat{d}_{K}[\ell]\}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{S},d_{1},\ldots,d_{K}\in\mathcal{D}}\Big\|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{z}}}[\ell]-\hat{\bar{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}x-\hat{\check{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}x^{*}\\
&-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\Big(\hat{\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}xd_{k}+\hat{\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}x^{*}d_{k}^{*}+\hat{\dot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}xd_{k}^{*}+\hat{\ddot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{k}x^{*}d_{k}\Big)\\
&-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\Big(\hat{\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{c}}_{k}^{T}d_{k}+\hat{\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{c}}_{k}^{H}d_{k}^{*}+\hat{\dot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{c}}_{k}^{T}d_{k}^{*}+\hat{\ddot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{c}}_{k}^{H}d_{k}\Big)\Big\|^{2},\end{split}

(22)

for
\ell=1,\ldots,D
, where
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{z}}}[\ell]
is the
\ell
th column of
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{Z}}}
. The detected symbols are collected into the vectors
\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
,
\hat{{\boldsymbol{d}}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{{\boldsymbol{d}}}_{K}
.

IV-B

AMDD Estimation

To simplify our notation, we let

{\boldsymbol{U}}_{k}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}&\check{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}&\ddot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}&\dot{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}\end{bmatrix},{\boldsymbol{V}}_{k}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}&\check{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}&\ddot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}&\dot{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{k}\end{bmatrix}.

(23)

Furthermore, we let

\bar{{\boldsymbol{D}}}_{k}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}^{T}{\boldsymbol{C}}&{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}^{H}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{*}&{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}^{T}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{*}&{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}^{H}{\boldsymbol{C}}\end{bmatrix}^{T},

(24)

and

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{D}}}_{k}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}^{T}{\boldsymbol{x}}&{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}&{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}^{T}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}&{\boldsymbol{D}}_{k}^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

(25)

We may thus express
{\boldsymbol{Z}}
as

\begin{split}&{\boldsymbol{Z}}={\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}+{\boldsymbol{Q}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}^{T}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}{\boldsymbol{V}}_{k}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{D}}}_{k}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{k}\bar{{\boldsymbol{D}}}_{k}+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}.\end{split}

(26)

Estimation is performed via alternate maximization. We begin by re-estimating the matrices
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,K
. We illustrate the estimation for
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}
. Prior to the estimation step, we cancel interference using the existing estimates, obtaining

\begin{split}&{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{u}^{(1)}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{Z}}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{H}}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{T}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}^{T}-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\hat{{\boldsymbol{V}}}_{k}\hat{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{D}}}}_{k}-\sum\limits_{k=2}^{K}\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}\hat{\bar{{\boldsymbol{D}}}}_{k}\\
&={\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}\bar{{\boldsymbol{D}}}_{1}+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{E}}}_{u}^{(1)},\end{split}

(27)

where
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{E}}}_{u}^{(1)}
is the residual error from the prior estimation and ML detection. We let
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{u}^{(1)}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}\left(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{Z}}}^{(1)}\right)
,
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}\left({\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}\right)
,
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{e}}}_{u}^{(1)}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}\left(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{E}}}_{u}^{(1)}\right)
and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}})
. We also let
{\boldsymbol{B}}^{(k)}\triangleq\bar{{\boldsymbol{D}}}_{k}^{T}\otimes{\boldsymbol{I}}_{M}
. Hence, we obtain

{\boldsymbol{z}}_{u}^{(1)}={\boldsymbol{B}}^{(1)}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{e}}}_{1}+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}.

(28)

In addition to
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{u}^{(1)}
, the pilot-carrying vectors
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{3}^{(1,1)}
and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{3}^{(1,2)}
also bear information about
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
. Letting

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{3}^{(1)}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}&{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{*}&{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{*}&{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}\end{bmatrix},

(29)

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{3}^{(2)}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\jmath{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}&-\jmath{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{*}&\jmath{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}^{*}&-\jmath{\boldsymbol{R}}_{3}\end{bmatrix},

(30)

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{3}^{(i)}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{P}}_{3}^{(i)}\otimes{\boldsymbol{I}}_{M}
, for
i=1,2
, we express
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{3}^{(1,i)}
as

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{3}^{(1,i)}=\tilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{3}^{(i)}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}+{\boldsymbol{w}}_{3}^{(1,i)}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{3}^{(1,i)},

(31)

for
i=1,2
. We now re-estimate
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
through the minimization

\begin{split}\hat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{1}^{(DD)}&\triangleq\arg\min_{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}}\big\|{\boldsymbol{z}}_{u}^{(1)}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{B}}}^{(1)}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}\big\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\big\|{\boldsymbol{y}}_{3}^{(1,i)}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{3}^{(i)}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}\big\|^{2}.\end{split}

(32)

The resulting AMDD estimator of
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
is given by

\begin{split}\hat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{1}^{(DD)}&=\Big(\hat{{\boldsymbol{B}}}^{(1)H}\hat{{\boldsymbol{B}}}^{(1)}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{3}^{(i)H}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{3}^{(i)}\Big)^{-1}\\
&\times\Big(\hat{{\boldsymbol{B}}}^{(1)H}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{u}^{(1)}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{3}^{(i)H}{\boldsymbol{y}}_{3}^{(1,i)}\Big).\end{split}

(33)

After estimating
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
, the remaining vectors
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{u}}_{K}
, are estimated in a similar fashion, using the most up-to-date estimates to cancel the interfering signal components.

We next estimate
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{K}
. We define the matrices

{\boldsymbol{V}}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{V}}_{1}&\ldots&{\boldsymbol{V}}_{K}\end{bmatrix},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{D}}}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{D}}}_{1}^{T}&\ldots&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{D}}}_{k}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

(34)

Removing the interference from
{\boldsymbol{Z}}
, we obtain

\begin{split}&{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{v}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{Z}}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{H}}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{X}}}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{k}^{(DD)}\hat{\bar{{\boldsymbol{D}}}}_{k}\\
&={\boldsymbol{V}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{D}}}+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{E}}}_{v},\end{split}

(35)

where
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{E}}}_{v}
is the residual error. Letting
{\boldsymbol{v}}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}({\boldsymbol{V}})
,
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{v}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}({\boldsymbol{Z}}_{v})
,
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{v}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}({\boldsymbol{E}}_{v})
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{v}\triangleq\tilde{{\boldsymbol{D}}}^{T}\otimes{\boldsymbol{I}}_{M}
, we obtain

{\boldsymbol{z}}_{v}={\boldsymbol{B}}_{v}{\boldsymbol{v}}+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{e}}}_{v}+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}.

(36)

Moreover, since the vectors
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{2}^{(1)}
and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}_{2}^{(2)}
also bear information about
{\boldsymbol{v}}
, they will be incorporated into the estimator of
{\boldsymbol{v}}
. We let
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{T}}}^{(1)}\triangleq[\tilde{{\boldsymbol{T}}}^{(1)}_{1},\ldots\tilde{{\boldsymbol{T}}}^{(1)}_{K}]\otimes{\boldsymbol{I}}_{M}
, where

V
Expectation Conditional Maximization Estimator

In this section, we propose another semi-blind estimation scheme based on the ECM criterion. The ECM is an iterative approach that avoids hard detection of the data symbols. Instead, it evaluates the expectation of the complete-data log-likelihood function (LLF) using the posterior probabilities of the data symbols, computed in each iteration based on the currently available estimates. This constitutes the expectation step (E-step), which is followed by a maximization step (M-step), where estimates of the unknown parameters are updated.

The vectorized versions of all the channel parameters are collected into the
(2M+2M^{2}+4KM+4KM^{2})\times 1
vector
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\triangleq[{\boldsymbol{h}}^{T},{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{T},{\boldsymbol{u}}^{T}]
. We also let
{\boldsymbol{d}}\triangleq[{\boldsymbol{d}}_{1}^{T},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{d}}_{K}^{T}]^{T}
. The complete data LLF of the received signal (if the data were perfectly known) is given by

\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{Y}},{\boldsymbol{Z}}\ |{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{d}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{Y}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{Z}}\ |{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{d}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

(58)

where
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
is the
M\times(N_{1}+2N_{2}+2KN_{3})
matrix aggregating all the received signals during pilot training, and the LLF for the pilot-carrying signals is given by

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{Y}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=-(N_{1}+2N_{2}+2KN_{3})\log(\pi\tilde{\sigma}^{2})\\
&-\frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\big\|{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}^{T}-{\boldsymbol{Q}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{R}}}^{T}\big\|^{2}\\
&-\frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\big\|{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{2}^{(1)}-{\boldsymbol{H}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{2}^{T}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\boldsymbol{V}}_{k}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{T}}}_{k}\big\|^{2}\\
&-\frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\big\|{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{2}^{(2)}-\jmath{\boldsymbol{H}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{2}^{T}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\boldsymbol{V}}_{k}\bar{{\boldsymbol{T}}}_{k}\big\|^{2}\\
&-\frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big(\big\|{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{3}^{(k,1)}-{\boldsymbol{Q}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{R}}}_{3}^{T}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{3}^{(1)T}\big\|^{2}\\
&+\big\|{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{3}^{(k,2)}-{\boldsymbol{Q}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{R}}}_{3}^{T}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{3}^{(2)T}\big\|^{2}\Big).\end{split}

(59)

Moreover, the LLF for the data-carrying signals is given by

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{Z}}|{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{d}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=-D\log(\pi\tilde{\sigma}^{2})-\frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{D}\big\|{\boldsymbol{y}}[n]-{\boldsymbol{H}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}[n]^{T}\\
&-{\boldsymbol{Q}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{c}}}[n]-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}{\boldsymbol{V}}_{k}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{d}}}_{k}[n]-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{k}[n]\big\|^{2},\end{split}

(60)

where
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}[n]\triangleq\big[x[n]\ x[n]^{*}\big]
,

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{d}}}_{k}[n]\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}x[n]d_{k}[n]\\
x[n]{*}d_{k}[n]^{*}\\
x[n]^{*}d_{k}[n]\\
x[n]d_{k}[n]^{*}\end{bmatrix},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}}_{k}[n]\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{c}}[n]d_{k}[n]\\
{\boldsymbol{c}}^{*}[n]d_{k}[n]^{*}\\
{\boldsymbol{c}}^{*}[n]d_{k}[n]\\
{\boldsymbol{c}}[n]d_{k}[n]^{*}\end{bmatrix}.

(61)

{\boldsymbol{V}}_{k}

{\boldsymbol{H}}

\ell

\ell

(62)

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}\left({\boldsymbol{\theta}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(\ell)}\right)&\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{Y}},{\boldsymbol{Z}}\ |{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{d}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\ |{\boldsymbol{Y}},{\boldsymbol{Z}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(\ell)}\right\}\\

When updating the estimate of a desired parameter (or group of parameters), the other parameters are fixed to their most up-to-date values, instead of being treated as unknown. The process is repeated alternately until all the parameters have been estimated.

We start with the estimation of
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}}^{(\ell)}

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}^{(\ell+1)}

{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}^{*}

{\boldsymbol{q}}^{(\ell+1)}

{\boldsymbol{q}}^{*}

\begin{split}&{\boldsymbol{q}}^{(\ell+1)}=\left({\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{22}+\bar{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{1}^{H}\bar{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{1}+2K{\boldsymbol{A}}_{3}^{H}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{3}\right)^{-1}\times\\

VI
Computational Complexity

In this section, we analyze the computational complexities of the three proposed estimators. The operations that can be performed off-line are excluded from the complexity calculation.

VI-A

Complexity of the Pilot-based Estimator

The complexity of the pilot-based estimator depends on the three training phases. The complexity of phase 1 is dominated by the LS estimation of
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
. However, the matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{1}
is entirely dependent on the fixed pilot sequences. Therefore, its pseudo-inverse needs to be computed only once and can be done off-line. Hence, the run-time complexity of Phase 1 is
O(M^{3}N_{1})
. Phase 2 complexity is also dominated by the LS estimation of
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{(1)}
the
\bar{{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{(2)}
, which are
O(KM^{2}N_{2})
. Phase 3 consists of
2K
sub-phases. It is dominated by the complexity of the LS estimation of
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{k}
and
\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{k}
, which are
O(M^{3}N_{3})
. Since these operations are repeated
K
times, the complexity will be
O(KM^{3}N_{3})
. Combining all three phases, the total complexity of the pilot-based estimator is
O(M^{3}N_{1}+KM^{2}N_{2}+KM^{3}N_{3})
.

VI-B

Complexity of the AMDD Estimator

The AMDD estimator involves a data detection stage prior to the estimation. The overall complexity of the ML data detection as outlined in (
22
) is
O(D\bar{\mathcal{S}}K^{\bar{\mathcal{D}}+1}M^{2})
.

For the estimation part, the complexity of the estimation in (
33
) is
O(MD^{2}+M^{6}+M^{3}D+M^{3}N_{3})
. Moreover, this step is repeated
K
times resulting in an complexity of
O(K(MD^{2}+M^{6}+M^{3}D+M^{3}N_{3}))
. For the estimation of
{\boldsymbol{v}}
in (
41
), the complexity is
O(KM^{2}D+K^{3}M^{3}+KM^{2}N_{2})
. For the estimation of
{\boldsymbol{q}}
in (
48
), the complexity is
O(M^{6}+M^{3}D+M^{3}N_{1}+KM^{3}N_{3})
. As for the estimation of
{\boldsymbol{h}}
in (
57
), the complexity is
O(M^{3}+M^{2}(D+N_{1}+N_{2}))
. Hence, the overall complexity of the AMDD estimator including both stages is
O(D\bar{\mathcal{S}}K^{\bar{\mathcal{D}}+1}M^{2}+KM^{6}+KM^{3}D+KM^{3}N_{3}+KMD^{2}+K^{3}M^{3}+KM^{2}N_{2}+M^{3}N_{1})
.

VI-C

Complexity of the ECM Estimator

The ECM involves three steps, 1) computing the posterior probabilities, 2) computing the expectation in the E-step and 3) performing the conditional maximization in the M-step.

It can be verified that the computation of the joint posterior probability in (
66
) is
O(M\bar{\mathcal{S}}+KM^{2}D\bar{\mathcal{D}}^{K}+D\bar{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{D}^{K})
. Moreover, the computation of the marginal probabilities needed for the E-step is
O(K^{2}D\bar{\mathcal{D}}^{K}\bar{\mathcal{S}})
.

The E-step involves obtaining the expectations in (
71
). The complexity of the E-step is
O(K^{2}MD^{2}\bar{\mathcal{S}}\bar{\mathcal{D}}^{2}+K^{2}M^{2}D)
.

The last part of the ECM estimator is the M-step. Based on (
75
), the estimation of
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
is
O(KM^{6}+K^{2}M^{4}+KM^{3}D+KM^{3}N_{3})
. Based on (
76
), the complexity for the estimation of
{\boldsymbol{v}}
is
O(K^{3}M^{3}+KM^{2}D+KM^{2}N_{2})
. Based on (
77
), the complexity of estimating
{\boldsymbol{q}}
is
O(M^{6}+M^{3}D+M^{4}K+M^{3}N_{1}+M^{3}N_{3}+KMN_{3})
. Based on (
78
), the complexity of estimating
{\boldsymbol{h}}
is
O(KM^{3}+M^{2}D+M^{2}N_{1}+M^{2}N_{2})
. Hence, the overall complexity of the M-step is
O(KM^{6}+K^{3}M^{3}+KM^{3}D+KM^{3}N_{3}+KM^{2}N_{2}+M^{3}N_{1})
.

The overall complexity for an iteration of the ECM estimator including all three stages is thus
O(KM^{2}D\bar{\mathcal{D}}^{K}+K^{2}M^{2}D+K^{2}D\bar{\mathcal{D}}^{K}\bar{\mathcal{S}}+K^{2}MD^{2}\bar{\mathcal{S}}\bar{\mathcal{D}}^{2}+KM^{6}+K^{3}M^{3}+KM^{3}D+KM^{3}N_{3}+KM^{2}N_{2}+M^{3}N_{1})
.

VII
Pilot-based Cramer-Rao Bound

In this section, we derive the pilot-based CRB. Letting
{\boldsymbol{y}}\triangleq\mbox{Vec}({\boldsymbol{Y}})
, we can express
{\boldsymbol{y}}
in terms of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
as

{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\check{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}},

(79)

where

{\boldsymbol{A}}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{R}}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{T}}}^{(1)}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
\jmath{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{T}}}^{(2)}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{A}}_{3}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{3}^{(1)}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\ldots&\ldots&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{A}}_{3}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{3}^{(2)}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\ldots&\ldots&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{A}}_{3}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{3}^{(1)}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\ldots&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{A}}_{3}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{3}^{(2)}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\ldots&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ldots&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{A}}_{3}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\ldots&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{3}^{(1)}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{A}}_{3}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\ldots&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{3}^{(2)}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(80)

and
\check{{\boldsymbol{w}}}
is the concatenated AWGN vector.

Letting
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\triangleq[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{T},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{H}]^{T}
, the FIM for our signal model is

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}&\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{y}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{y}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{H}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}}\right\}=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}&{\boldsymbol{I}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}\\
{\boldsymbol{I}}^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}&{\boldsymbol{I}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{*}\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

(81)

Furthermore, it can be verified that
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{A}}
, while
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
. Hence,
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{A}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\end{bmatrix}
.

We now define the real parameter vector
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\triangleq[\Re\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}^{T},\Im\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}^{T}]^{T}
. We also let
{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
be the corresponding FIM matrix for
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
. Then
{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
is related to
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}
by

{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}=\mathcal{M}^{H}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}\mathcal{M}
, where

\mathcal{M}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\bar{L}}&\jmath{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\bar{L}}\\
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\bar{L}}&-\jmath{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\bar{L}}\end{bmatrix}
, and
\bar{L}\triangleq 2M+2M^{2}+4KM+4KM^{2}
. Therefore,

{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{A}}+{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}&\jmath({\boldsymbol{A}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{A}}-{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*})\\
-\jmath{{\boldsymbol{A}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{A}}-{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}}&{\boldsymbol{A}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{A}}+{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\end{bmatrix}.

(82)

The pilot-based CRB is then

\mbox{CRB}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=\mbox{tr}\left({\boldsymbol{J}}_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}^{-1}\right)
.

VIII
Semi-Blind Cramer-Rao Bound

Semi-blind estimation which incoporates both pilot and data symbols, requires the derivation of the semi-blind CRB. Since it is very tedious to obtain the exact semi-blind CRB, we consider the modified CRB (MCRB)
[
28
]
, which is a looser bound. The MCRB first obtains the FIM conditioned on the data symbols, then applies the expectation with respect to the data symbols. Hence, the FIM in the MCRB is given by

\begin{split}&{\boldsymbol{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{Y}},{\boldsymbol{Z}},{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{d}}}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{Y}},{\boldsymbol{Z}}|{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{d}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{Y}},{\boldsymbol{Z}}|{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{d}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{H}}{{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}}\right)\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{d}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{Y}},{\boldsymbol{Z}}|{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{d}}}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{Y}},{\boldsymbol{Z}}|{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{d}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{Y}},{\boldsymbol{Z}}|{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{d}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{H}}{{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{*}}\right)\hskip-2.15277pt.\end{split}

(83)

It can be verified that
{\boldsymbol{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}&{\boldsymbol{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}\\
{\boldsymbol{J}}^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}&{\boldsymbol{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{*}\end{bmatrix}
. Moreover,
{\boldsymbol{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
, while

{\boldsymbol{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}={\boldsymbol{I}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{J}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
,
where

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{J}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}DP_{x}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{2M}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{B}}_{c}^{H}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{c}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&DP_{x}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{4KM}&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\tilde{{\boldsymbol{B}}}\end{bmatrix},

(84)

where
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{B}}}
is the
4KM^{2}\times 4KM^{2}
matrix given by

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{B}}}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\check{{\boldsymbol{C}}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\ldots&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&\check{{\boldsymbol{C}}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\ldots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&\ldots&\ldots&\check{{\boldsymbol{C}}}\end{bmatrix},

(85)

where

\check{{\boldsymbol{C}}}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{C}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{C}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{*}&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{C}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{*}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{C}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{C}}\\
{\boldsymbol{C}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{C}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{C}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{*}&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{C}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{*}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{C}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{C}}\end{bmatrix}.

(86)

The semi-blind FIM for the real parameter vector
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is then

{\boldsymbol{J}}^{(SB)}_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\triangleq\mathcal{M}^{H}{\boldsymbol{J}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\mathcal{M}
, and

\mbox{CRB}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{(SB)}=\mbox{tr}\left(\left({\boldsymbol{J}}^{(SB)}_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\right)^{-1}\right)
.

X
Conclusions

In this paper, we considered the problem of joint estimation of the channel coefficients and I/Q imbalance for a multi-tag full-duplex AmBC system. The resulting estimation problem is highly challenging due to the large number of parameters, the coupling between them, the multiple mirror signal images and the presence of RSI. We proposed a training protocol and pilot-based estimator that guarantee orthogonality between the different signal components during pilot transmission, making it possible to estimate all cascaded channels without error floors. In addition, two semi-blind estimators were proposed, based on the AMDD strategy and the ECM framework. The pilot-based and semi-blind CRBs were obtained as performance benchmarks. Our simulations showed that the pilot-based estimator performance coincides with the corresponding CRB, while the ECM approaches its CRB.

Both semi-blind estimators offered substantially higher estimation accuracy than the pilot-based estimator. An interesting tradeoff emerges, whereby the pilot-based estimator has the lowest complexity, the ECM has the best accuracy and highest complexity, and the AMDD offers an intermediate option. This offers valuable flexibility for the user to select the solution that best matches their needs and resources.
