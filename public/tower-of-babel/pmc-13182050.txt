Latent profiles of oral health–related quality of life in temporomandibular disorders: implications for nurse-led stratified care
Authors: Qingying Zhu, Junjie Wang, Yanhong Zhang, Qianju Wu, Xiaping Wang, Yi Zhang, Liqing Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTemporomandibular disorders (TMD) are common chronic orofacial pain conditions that substantially impair oral health–related quality of life (OHRQoL). The multidimensional burden of TMD, involving pain, functional limitation, psychological distress, and maladaptive behaviors, is highly heterogeneous. However, nursing practice lacks empirically derived frameworks that support early risk stratification and profile-informed, nurse-led care.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in a tertiary TMD specialty clinic between February 2024 and August 2025. A total of 515 adults diagnosed with TMD according to the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD) were enrolled. OHRQoL was assessed using the OHIP-TMD-22. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was performed based on seven OHIP-TMD-22 domain scores to identify distinct OHRQoL profiles. Pain intensity (NRS), jaw functional limitation (JFLS-8), psychological distress (DASS-21), and oral-related behaviors were compared across profiles. Multinomial logistic regression was used to examine factors associated with profile membership from a nursing assessment perspective.ResultsFour latent OHRQoL profiles with a clear gradient structure were identified: minimal impact (46.80%), mild–moderate impact (31.26%), psychological–functional high impact (16.89%), and severe generalized impairment (5.05%). Progressive increases in pain, functional limitation, psychological distress, and behavioral risk clustering were observed across profiles. Pain intensity, jaw functional limitation, and stress emerged as the most consistent factors associated with profile membership, while depressive symptoms were additionally associated with the most severe profile. These patterns suggest that pain, function, and stress may serve as practical candidate indicators for nurse-led early screening of patients with higher multidimensional burden.ConclusionsThis study suggests that TMD is a nursing-sensitive chronic pain condition characterized by substantial heterogeneity in OHRQoL. The identified latent profiles may offer a preliminary framework for nurse-led risk stratification and stratified care planning. Integrating OHRQoL, pain, functional, and psychological assessments into routine nursing practice may help improve early identification of vulnerable subgroups and inform more tailored nursing care; however, the practical utility of this approach warrants further validation in longitudinal and multicenter studies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) are among the most common chronic orofacial pain conditions. They are typically characterized by jaw pain, chewing difficulty, limited mouth opening, and sleep disturbance. These symptoms can interfere with eating, speaking, sleeping, and other daily activities, restrict social participation, and contribute to emotional distress, thereby substantially reducing oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) [1–3]. Evidence suggests that the prevalence of TMD is approximately 30%, with a higher risk reported among women [4–6]. The adverse impact of TMD on oral health–related quality of life (OHRQoL) has therefore become a prominent concern in public oral health and nursing practice [7–9]. Prior research indicates that impaired OHRQoL in patients with TMD is usually driven by the combined effects of pain, functional limitations, and psychological distress, rather than by any single factor [10, 11]. Psychological burdens such as anxiety, depression, and stress may amplify pain experiences, reduce coping capacity, and undermine treatment adherence, thereby further exacerbating quality-of-life impairment [12, 13].

In the context of chronic orofacial pain, OHRQoL is a core patient-centered outcome that sensitively captures the real-world consequences of symptoms on functioning and social participation [14, 15]. The OHIP-TMD-22, an OHRQoL instrument developed specifically for TMD, has been validated across multiple countries and reliably reflects the multidimensional structure of pain, oral functional limitation, and psychological discomfort in this population [16–18]. However, most existing studies rely on total scores or single-domain analyses, which may obscure within-group heterogeneity among patients with TMD [19, 20] and provide limited support for effective risk stratification in nursing assessment and decision-making.

Latent profile analysis (LPA) offers a methodologically robust framework to characterize clinically meaningful subgroups within heterogeneous patient populations. Emerging evidence suggests that patients with TMD may exhibit structured burden gradients across pain intensity, functional limitation, and psychological distress, and that these patterns are closely related to illness experiences, care needs, and prognosis [21, 22]. From a nursing perspective, identifying OHRQoL-based profiles that differentiate risk levels could enable more precise and individualized care planning, including initial assessment, psychological support, self-management coaching, and follow-up scheduling [23, 24].

TMD represents a nursing-sensitive pain condition in which nurses play pivotal roles across key points of care [9], including symptom screening, health education, management of parafunctional behaviors, recognition of stress and emotional problems, and facilitation of adherence to self-management. Increasing evidence supports that nurse-led health education, psychological support, and self-management interventions can improve function and quality of life among patients with chronic orofacial pain [25–27]. Nevertheless, current nursing practice lacks an evidence-based model grounded in the multidimensional OHRQoL structure that allows nurses to rapidly identify high-burden patients and implement stratified care pathways. This gap constrains the contribution of nursing to early identification, risk stratification, and intervention matching in TMD, and may reduce the efficiency of nursing resource allocation.

To address this gap, the present study extends prior LPA work by examining whether OHRQoL-based latent profiles can help characterize clinically meaningful heterogeneity in patients with TMD and by exploring their potential relevance to nurse-led stratified assessment. Specifically, we aimed to identify latent OHRQoL profiles in patients with TMD using the OHIP-TMD-22 and to examine profile-specific differences in pain, oral function, psychological distress, and behavioral factors. Rather than establishing a definitive clinical pathway, this study sought to provide preliminary profile-informed evidence that may support nurse-led assessment, self-management support, and psychosocial screening in routine care.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This cross-sectional study was conducted between February 2024 and August 2025 in the TMD specialty clinic of the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Xiamen Medical College. Participants were recruited using consecutive sampling during the study period. The study protocol was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee of the hospital (approval No. KS20240218001), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

A total of 515 patients with TMD aged 18–65 years were included. All patients were diagnosed by qualified clinicians according to the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD). The inclusion criteria were: meeting the DC/TMD diagnostic criteria, ability to independently complete the questionnaires, and provision of written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were: acute odontogenic pain; maxillofacial trauma; tumors; systemic joint diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis; severe psychiatric or cognitive disorders; and incomplete questionnaire data. The participant selection process is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 534 patients were screened during the study period. Nineteen patients were excluded due to incomplete questionnaire data. Ultimately, 515 patients were included in the final analysis.

Sample size estimation

There is no universally accepted formula for sample size determination in latent profile analysis (LPA). Previous simulation studies have suggested that, when models include 5–7 continuous indicators and the expected number of profiles does not exceed four, a sample size of approximately 300–500 participants is sufficient to achieve high classification accuracy (entropy ≥ 0.80) [28, 29].In the present study, the seven OHIP-TMD-22 domains were used as continuous indicators, and the model complexity was considered moderate. Accordingly, a target sample size of at least 500 patients was prespecified. Ultimately, 515 patients were enrolled, which met the prespecified requirement for model stability and provided adequate support for subsequent between-profile comparisons.

Survey content and measurement instruments

Sociodemographic and general information

A structured questionnaire was used to collect data on age, sex, educational level, marital status, employment status, and disease course (months). In addition, participants were asked whether they had each oral-related behavior (yes/no), including unilateral chewing, mouth breathing, nocturnal bruxism, excessive mouth opening, sleeping after midnight, prolonged chewing of gum, preference for hard foods, prone sleeping position, and chin-supporting habits; these variables were treated as brief screening indicators of behavioral exposure.

Pain intensity

Pain intensity over the past 30 days was assessed using an 11-point numeric rating scale (NRS; 0–10), with higher scores indicating more severe pain [30].

Psychological distress

Psychological distress was evaluated using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) [31, 32], which contains 21 items divided into three subscales: depression, anxiety, and stress. Each item is rated from 0 to 3, and the total score for each subscale is multiplied by two to yield the final score, with higher scores indicating greater psychological distress. In the present sample, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the depression, anxiety, and stress subscales were 0.892, 0.853, and 0.902, respectively.

Jaw functional limitation

Mandibular functional limitation was assessed using the Jaw Functional Limitation Scale-8 (JFLS-8) [33, 34], which evaluates limitations in chewing, mouth opening, and jaw movement. Each item is rated on an 11-point scale (0 = no limitation, 10 = severe limitation), and higher total scores represent greater degrees of functional impairment. The Cronbach’s alpha for the JFLS-8 in the present sample was 0.801.

Oral health–related quality of life

The Oral Health Impact Profile for Temporomandibular Disorders-22 (OHIP-TMD-22) [17, 35, 36] was used to assess TMD - specific quality of life. This 22-item instrument comprises seven dimensions—pain discomfort, functional limitation, psychological discomfort, physical disability, psychological disability, social disability, and handicap. Each item is rated on a 5 - point likert scale (0 = never, 4 = always), with higher scores indicating greater impairment of oral health–related quality of life. The Cronbach’s alpha for the OHIP-TMD-22 in the present sample was 0.953.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in several steps. Latent profile models with one to five classes were fitted in Mplus 8.7 using the seven OHIP-TMD-22 domain scores as indicators. The optimal number of profiles was determined by jointly considering the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy, and the Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT) as well as the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT).

Subsequent analyses were conducted in SPSS 29.0. As most continuous variables did not meet the assumption of normality, between-group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ² test or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test, as appropriate. Variables that were statistically significant in univariable analyses were considered for entry into a multinomial logistic regression model (with Class 1 as the reference category) to examine associations between each factor and profile membership. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Latent profile model fit

The model fit indices for the latent profile analyses are summarized in Table 1. As the number of latent classes increased from one to five, the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC) showed a progressive decline, indicating improved overall model fit with increasing model complexity. The Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT) demonstrated a statistically significant improvement when comparing the one-class and two-class solutions (P < 0.001). However, the LMR-LRT was not significant for the three-class and five-class models, while it remained statistically significant for the four-class solution (P = 0.0032).

In addition, the four-class model yielded high classification accuracy, as reflected by an entropy value of 0.916, and produced reasonably balanced class sizes with clear and clinically meaningful distinctions between profiles. Taken together, these statistical and interpretative considerations supported the four-class solution as the optimal and final latent profile model.

OHRQoL characteristics across latent profiles

Based on the seven OHIP-TMD-22 domain scores, four distinct OHRQoL profiles were identified: Class 1, minimal-impact (46.80%); Class 2, mild–moderate impact (31.26%); Class 3, psychological–functional high-impact (16.89%); and Class 4, severe generalized impairment (5.05%). These labels are descriptive and are intended to facilitate interpretation, rather than to denote diagnostic or mechanistic categories. As shown in Table 2; Fig. 2, a clear stepwise increase was observed across all OHIP-TMD-22 domains, indicating a continuum of worsening OHRQoL from Class 1 to Class 4.

This graded pattern may be relevant to nursing assessment, as it helps differentiate between patients with relatively lower burden and those with more complex multidimensional needs. Patients in Class 1 (minimal-impact) exhibited a low pain burden, relatively stable psychological status, and near-normal jaw function, and may be suitable for brief nurse-led education and routine monitoring. Class 2 (mild–moderate impact) was characterized by noticeable increases in pain and functional limitation, accompanied by emerging emotional or sleep-related disturbances; for this group, early stress-management guidance and scheduled follow-up may be worth consideration in nursing care.

Patients in Class 3 (psychological–functional high-impact) demonstrated pronounced psychological distress, including elevated stress and depressive symptoms, in combination with marked functional limitation. This profile may indicate the need for enhanced nursing assessment, with particular attention to psychosocial screening and behavioral counselling. Class 4 (severe generalized impairment) represented a small but highly vulnerable subgroup, characterized by the highest levels of pain, functional impairment, and psychological distress across all domains. These patients may warrant closer monitoring, coordinated multidisciplinary referral, and more intensive supportive care. Overall, the identified OHRQoL profiles may offer a preliminary framework to inform nurse-led stratified care planning for patients with TMD.

Sociodemographic comparison across latent profiles

As presented in Table 3, no statistically significant differences were observed among the latent profiles with respect to age or sex distribution. In contrast, patients in Class 4 were more likely to have lower educational attainment, to be married, and to be engaged in full-time employment. These associations may reflect unmeasured contextual or social burdens; however, this interpretation remains speculative and should be confirmed in future studies. From a nursing perspective, such contextual factors may be worth considering when tailoring self-management education, follow-up scheduling, and supportive counselling.

No significant differences were found in disease duration across the four profiles, suggesting that the severity of OHRQoL impairment may not be fully explained by symptom chronicity alone. This finding highlights the potential importance of comprehensive assessment that goes beyond disease duration and incorporates functional, psychological, and social dimensions of patient experience.

Behavioral, functional, and psychological differences across latent profiles

As shown in Table 4, clear and consistent gradients were observed across the four latent profiles. Pain intensity, as measured by the NRS, and jaw functional limitation, assessed using the JFLS-8, increased progressively from Class 1 to Class 4, indicating a stepwise escalation in physical burden. Psychological distress also followed a distinct pattern, with stress and depressive symptoms measured by the DASS-21 being particularly pronounced in Class 3 and Class 4.

In addition, oral-related behavioral risks—including prolonged gum chewing, chin-supporting habits, excessive mouth opening, and unilateral chewing—were significantly more prevalent among patients in the higher-burden profiles. This clustering of maladaptive behaviors alongside elevated pain, functional restriction, and psychological distress suggests that these factors may co-occur within higher-burden profiles and may be clinically relevant to the overall quality-of-life impact.

From a nursing perspective, these findings suggest that pain severity, jaw function, and stress levels may serve as practical candidate indicators for rapid nurse-led screening in outpatient TMD care. Moreover, the concentration of behavioral risks in Classes 2–4 suggests a potentially relevant target for tailored behavioral counselling and self-management education.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

In the multivariable logistic regression model (Table 5), pain intensity, jaw functional limitation, and stress emerged as the most consistently associated factors in relation to latent profile membership. The observed gradient across profiles suggests that these indicators may be relevant to nurse-led early identification of patients with higher burden. Specifically, patients in the mild–moderate impact profile showed early signs of functional limitation accompanied by elevated stress levels. Membership in the psychological–functional high-impact profile was primarily associated with pronounced pain and functional restriction together with high stress, which may point to the potential value of targeted psychosocial assessment and support in nursing care. The severe generalized impairment profile showed the highest burden across pain, functional limitation, and psychological distress, with depressive symptoms also independently associated with profile membership.

Several oral-related behaviors, such as unilateral chewing and chin-supporting habits, were significantly associated with profile membership in univariable analyses but did not retain statistical significance in the multivariable model. This finding suggests that such behaviors may be better interpreted as components of a broader symptom- and stress-related pattern rather than as isolated risk factors.

Overall, pain severity, jaw functional limitation, and stress may be considered candidate nursing assessment indicators for exploratory stratified care approaches. Taken together, the identified latent OHRQoL profiles demonstrated clear and graded differences in pain intensity, jaw functional limitation, psychological distress, and behavioral risk patterns. To illustrate the potential nursing relevance of these empirically derived profiles, we proposed a preliminary profile-informed stratified nursing care pathway, aligning the intensity of assessment and supportive care with profile-specific burden levels (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Building on the multidimensional structure of the OHIP-TMD-22, this study identified four distinct latent OHRQoL profiles, demonstrating systematic between-group differences in pain burden, jaw functional limitation, psychological distress, and oral-related behaviors. These findings support the presence of marked heterogeneity in TMD and suggest a potentially important role for nurses in early identification, risk stratification, and patient-centered supportive care. In the following sections, we discuss the implications of the profile structure, the interrelationships among pain, function, and psychological factors, the contribution of behavioral risks, and the relevance of psychosocial and self-management pathways for nurse-led care.

Profile distribution and its significance for nursing assessment

The four identified profiles formed a clear gradient ranging from minimal impact to severe generalized impairment, suggesting that patients with TMD may exhibit distinct and stratifiable patterns of burden across pain experience, functional limitation, and psychological distress. This finding suggests that reliance on patient-reported complaints or single symptom indicators alone may be insufficient to identify individuals with higher multidimensional burden. In particular, some patients with substantial impairment in quality of life but relatively inconspicuous symptom presentation may be at risk of being overlooked, potentially representing a group with less obvious but substantial burden [37].

Compared with conventional approaches based on mean scores or regression analyses, profile-based classification may help identify clinically meaningful subgroups and may provide nurses with more structured information to support risk-informed assessment [38]. This approach may support individualized nursing assessment and care planning by capturing the overall impact of TMD on patients’ lives rather than isolated symptom severity. For nurses involved in outpatient triage and initial screening, the profile structure may offer a clearer framework for risk identification, shifting attention from single symptoms toward a more comprehensive, quality-of-life–centered perspective.

By integrating OHRQoL measures, pain ratings, and functional assessments into routine initial consultations, nurses may be able to complete preliminary stratification at an earlier stage and more readily identify patients who may benefit from further psychosocial or multidisciplinary evaluation. However, the practical utility of this approach requires further validation before routine implementation.

Interactions among pain, function, and psychological factors: implications for integrated nursing care

This study showed broadly consistent gradient patterns of pain intensity, functional limitation, and psychological stress across the identified profiles, which is compatible with multidimensional chronic pain frameworks reported in previous research [39–41]. These findings suggest that quality-of-life impairment in TMD may be associated with the co-occurrence of multiple domains of burden rather than any single factor alone [42].

In routine clinical practice, however, pain assessment often receives greater emphasis than evaluation of functional status and psychological well-being [43–45]. From a nursing perspective, multidimensional assessment remains important because limitations in daily functioning, emotional distress, and reduced social participation may all contribute to patients’ lived burden. The present findings suggest that assessment strategies focusing on pain alone may be insufficient to address the broader quality-of-life burden experienced by some patients with TMD [2].

As nurses are typically the first healthcare professionals to engage with patients in the early stages of care, they are well positioned to assume responsibility for cross-domain assessment. Integrating pain experience, functional status, and psychological condition into a unified nursing judgement provides a more comprehensive basis for care planning. By routinely incorporating psychosocial screening—particularly stress assessment—together with brief functional measures such as the JFLS-8, nurses can more effectively identify high-risk profiles without substantially increasing workload, and can subsequently formulate integrated nursing care plans tailored to patients’ multidimensional needs.

Behavioral factors: a key entry point for nurse-mediated self-management

This study revealed marked differences in the distribution of maladaptive oral behaviors across latent profiles. Some behaviors may be associated with symptom aggravation or may reflect adaptive responses to pain and functional limitation; however, these relationships cannot be disentangled in a cross-sectional design [46–48]. Given their potentially modifiable nature, these behaviors may represent a relevant target for nurse-mediated education and self-management support [49].

Previous nursing research has demonstrated that nurse-led behavioral education and self-management guidance can effectively reduce symptom burden and improve quality of life in patients with chronic pain conditions [50–52]. In light of the profile structure identified in this study, nurses can more precisely target behavioral interventions toward patients with moderate to high levels of burden. For example, nurse-mediated self-management programs can deliver tailored behavioral education to help patients reduce maladaptive chewing patterns, improve posture, and avoid excessive joint loading, thereby enhancing intervention efficiency.

For patients with limited motivation to change behaviors, strategies such as motivational interviewing may be used to strengthen engagement and adherence [53]. In addition, systematically incorporating behavioral assessment into nursing documentation and follow-up processes can enable nurses to monitor behavioral change over time and to evaluate the effectiveness of behavioral interventions in routine care.

Behavioral counselling primarily operationalizes nurse-mediated self-management, whereas the following section focuses on psychosocial care pathways for emotional distress.

Self-management and psychological interventions: strengthening the nursing role in psychosocial care

Patients with TMD in high-burden profiles commonly present with substantial levels of stress, anxiety, or depressive symptoms, indicating care needs that extend beyond physical symptoms to psychological and behavioral domains [54]. Growing evidence suggests that nurses can effectively deliver a range of psychosocial interventions in chronic pain management, including psychoeducation, self-management support, brief cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) components, and mindfulness-based strategies [55–58]. In the context of TMD, several nurse-led or nurse-deliverable non-pharmacological interventions have also demonstrated beneficial effects [26, 59].

It is important to emphasize that these nurse-delivered psychosocial interventions are not intended to replace specialist psychological therapy. Rather, they function as early identification tools and brief supportive measures embedded within routine care pathways, while facilitating timely referral when more intensive psychological treatment is required.

Compared with interventions led exclusively by mental health specialists, nurse-led psychological support is more readily integrated into everyday nursing practice and is particularly well suited to outpatient and community settings with limited resources. The latent profile structure identified in this study provides empirical guidance for prioritizing psychosocial interventions in nursing care. By routinely implementing emotional screening (e.g. using the DASS-21 or brief screening tools) for patients in moderate- and high-burden profiles, and by incorporating brief CBT elements and mindfulness strategies within outpatient encounters, nurses can enhance the timeliness and precision of psychosocial care without imposing a substantial additional referral burden. For patients in high-burden profiles, nurses may also assume a care coordination role, facilitating interdisciplinary collaboration among dental, physiotherapy, and psychological services.

Toward a profile-informed stratified nursing framework

Based on the identified latent profiles and their associated care needs, this study proposes a profile-informed stratified nursing framework that may help reframe TMD management from a uniform approach toward a more needs-oriented perspective [37, 38]. Rather than relying on single symptoms or pain intensity alone, this framework integrates quality of life, functional status, psychological distress, and behavioral factors to help inform differentiated nursing priorities across patient subgroups [11, 38].

The potential value of this framework lies in offering nurses a more structured way to align assessment intensity and supportive care planning with patient burden. However, its practical utility and generalizability require further validation before routine implementation [57, 60].

Because the proposed framework is based on brief assessment tools and existing nursing workflows, it may be feasible for future testing in outpatient and community settings, including resource-constrained environments. Further validation studies are needed to determine whether profile-informed reassessment and follow-up can improve consistency of care delivery and support efficient resource allocation.

Study limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow examination of temporal changes in latent profile membership. Longitudinal studies are needed to explore profile transitions over time and to examine whether profile-informed nursing strategies are associated with changes in patient outcomes. Second, because the sample was drawn from a single tertiary center, the generalizability of the findings to other settings, particularly primary care and community settings, remains uncertain.

Third, oral-related behaviors were assessed using dichotomous yes/no items, which may have limited measurement precision and introduced misclassification bias, particularly for behaviors that vary in frequency, duration, or intensity. These behavioral variables should therefore be interpreted as brief screening indicators rather than precise behavioral measures. Fourth, some conceptual overlap may exist between certain OHIP-TMD-22 domains and the external variables used in the analyses, particularly pain-related and psychological constructs. This overlap may have partially contributed to the observed associations and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Fifth, the most severe latent profile was relatively small (n = 26), while the multinomial regression model included multiple predictors. This imbalance may have reduced coefficient stability, increased the risk of overfitting, and limited generalizability, particularly for estimates involving the smallest subgroup. In addition, variables were selected on the basis of univariable screening, which is pragmatic but may introduce selection bias and model instability. Accordingly, the regression findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and future studies should apply more robust modeling and validation strategies.

Finally, reliance on self-administered questionnaires may have introduced recall bias. Future studies could enhance data accuracy by incorporating nursing assessment records and objective functional measures. Multicenter, longitudinal, and cross-cultural studies are needed to validate the profile structure and its potential nursing relevance and to determine whether profile-informed care is associated with improved patient and service outcomes.

Conclusion

Using the multidimensional structure of the OHIP-TMD-22, this study identified four latent OHRQoL profiles with clear gradient characteristics among patients with TMD, revealing distinct combinations of pain burden, jaw functional limitation, psychological distress, and behavioral factors across patient subgroups. By linking these latent profiles to a preliminary nursing perspective, the present study may help inform more consistent and patient-centered assessment approaches in TMD care.

The findings support viewing TMD as a nursing-relevant chronic pain condition characterized by substantial heterogeneity, and suggest that reliance on single symptoms or pain intensity alone may be insufficient to capture patients’ full burden. The profile-based framework proposed in this study may help inform multidimensional, nurse-led assessment and earlier recognition of patients with greater multidimensional burden across clinical and community settings.

Overall, this profile-informed framework may offer a useful direction for future nursing research and service development; however, its clinical utility, generalizability, and implementation value require further validation before broader application.
