Age- and step-length-dependent alterations in muscle synergies and joint coordination during unexpected gait termination
Authors: Woohyoung Jeon, Ashley Dalby, Xuanliang Neil Dong, Melinda Hermanns
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFalls in older adults often occur during unexpected gait termination, when rapid deceleration is required. Age-related declines in strength, proprioception, and coordination impair efficient distal-to-proximal braking, raising fall risk. Variable step lengths, ranging from “normal” steps to larger “big” steps used when hurrying, better reflect real-world walking demands and pose greater mechanical and neuromuscular challenges. This study investigated how age and step length affect muscle synergy complexity and joint coordination during unexpected gait stops.MethodsTwelve healthy younger and twelve healthy older adults performed normal- and big-step stops in response to random visual cues during walking. Surface EMG from distal to proximal leg muscles, whole-body kinematics, and kinetic force data from the stopping leg during the 1-second period immediately following the gait stop were collected. Muscle synergies, negative work at the knee and hip, joint coordination (Full Width at Half Maximum [FWHM] and Standard Deviation of Continuous Relative Phase [SD CRP]) for ankle–knee, knee–hip, and ankle–hip pairs, and postural sway were analyzed. A two-way mixed ANOVA (Step × Age) evaluated main and interaction effects.ResultsBig steps increased the number of synergies in both groups, with older adults requiring more synergies than younger adults. All participants shared a distal synergy (ankle muscles and knee extensor); however, older adults recruited additional proximal synergies (hip or back muscles) with overlapping temporal synergy activation. Younger adults generated more negative work at the knee, whereas older adults shifted effort proximally, increasing hip joint work. Older adults demonstrated longer FWHM and higher SD CRP across all joint pair. Postural sway was greater in older adults overall, especially during big steps, indicating an interaction effect.ConclusionsUnexpected stopping highlights age-related changes in movement strategy. Older adults shift from a distal-driven braking to proximal, multi‐joint co‐contraction strategy, characterized by increased hip work, reduced knee work, and greater muscle synergy complexity. This proximal engagement reflects a tradeoff along the stability–efficiency continuum: it enhances safety but increases metabolic cost and postural sway. By disrupting the distal‐to‐proximal control sequence, this compensatory strategy likely increases postural sway and fall risk. Targeted rehabilitation focusing on eccentric quadriceps and ankle control, such as stand-to-sit and step-down drills, may help restore distal-driven balance recovery and reduce fall risk during adaptive (reactive) walking.

Background/Introduction

Falls represent a significant health concern for older adults, contributing to injuries, loss of independence, and reduced quality of life [1]. Age-related declines in muscle strength, proprioception, reaction time, and neuromuscular coordination impair the ability to respond to sudden environmental demands, thereby increasing fall risk [2, 3]. These deficits are particularly pronounced during dynamic tasks requiring rapid motor control adjustments, such as stopping walking abruptly to avoid an obstacle or respond to an unexpected external cue [4–6]. Understanding the neuromuscular and biomechanical mechanisms underlying these adjustments in gait termination, particularly knee extensor control and leg joint coordination [7], is critical for designing targeted rehabilitation strategies to reduce fall risk and improve mobility in older adults.

Unexpected gait termination, the abrupt cessation (stopping) of walking in response to a sudden cue such as a traffic or brake light, is a critical motor task for fall prevention. It requires rapid, precise adjustments to decelerate the body’s forward momentum and maintain balance, with the stopping leg executing a sequential, “distal-to-proximal” braking pattern [8]. First, the ankle and knee eccentrically absorb ground impact, then the hip and trunk stabilize the descending center of mass (CoM), ensuring efficient energy dissipation and a secure balance recovery. In real-life scenarios, such as crossing a street or navigating a crowded sidewalk, this ability to stop abruptly is essential for safety. Failure to execute an unexpected stop effectively can result in loss of balance and an increased risk of falls, particularly in older adults with impaired neuromuscular control.

Walking patterns vary naturally: individuals take “normal” steps during leisurely ambulation and larger (“big”) steps when hurrying or stepping over obstacles. Step length directly influences the biomechanical and neuromuscular demands placed on the stopping leg, as longer steps generate higher CoM velocities and impact forces, requiring greater joint excursions and muscle activation to stabilize [9]. For older adults, whose knee extensor strength and joint range of motion are often diminished [4, 5], the additional demands of stopping after a big step may further strain their balance system and elevate fall risk. However, the effect of step size on control strategies during unexpected stops in older adults remains poorly understood, highlighting a gap in current knowledge.

Effective gait termination depends on the stopping leg’s capacity to absorb kinetic energy and arrest CoM motion [10]. Eccentric contraction of the knee extensors (the quadriceps) is critical, leveraging a favorable moment arm to resist tibial rotation and decelerate the body, with subsequent fine-tuning by ankle muscles [11]. Concurrently, coordinated action among the hip, knee, and ankle joints ensures whole-body stability during the critical single-limb support phase [12]. In older adults, declines in eccentric strength, proprioceptive feedback, and inter-joint coordination may compromise these processes, leading to delayed braking, increased postural sway, and higher fall susceptibility. While research on planned gait termination has revealed that older adults often take more steps and display altered muscle activation compared to younger adults [13], unexpected gait termination, which better reflects real-world, reflexive stops, remains understudied. The distinct neuromuscular and biomechanical demands of unplanned gait stops, combined with the unexplored influence of step length (normal vs. big), represent critical gaps in understanding fall risk factors.

The present study fills these gaps by examining how the stopping leg’s neuromuscular control and biomechanics adjust to normal versus big steps during unexpected gait termination, and how these adjustments differ between young and older adults. We examine muscle synergies—coordinated patterns of muscle activation by the central nervous system that reveal motor control complexity and efficiency—along with knee extensor eccentric work, inter-joint coordination, and postural sway in the final stopping phase following a stop cue. Our objectives are to:

(1) Investigate how age and step length influence muscle synergy complexity and spatial patterns, emphasizing the contributions of knee extensors (and ankle muscles) versus proximal (hip/trunk) muscles, (2) Quantify joint-level coordination deficits in older adults, and relate these to compensatory control strategies, and (3) Inform rehabilitation by identifying how eccentric quadriceps and ankle control training can restore a more efficient, distal-focused strategy—ultimately reducing fall risk and enhancing mobility in aging populations.

We hypothesize that muscle synergy complexity will increase with both age and step length, such that older adults and big-step conditions will exhibit a greater number of synergies, including additional proximal modules reflecting compensatory control. We further hypothesize that older adults will demonstrate impaired distal joint coordination, characterized by reduced knee extensor eccentric work, prolonged FWHM, and increased SD CRP, which will be associated with greater reliance on proximal muscles. Finally, we hypothesize that targeted interventions focusing on eccentric quadriceps and ankle control can help restore a distal-focused, efficient braking strategy, thereby improving balance recovery in older adults.

Methods

Participants

Twelve healthy younger adults (age range: 20–27, mean ± SD: 24 ± 3 years, 6 Male/6 Female) and twelve healthy older adults (age range: 65–81, mean ± SD: 74 ± 8 years, 7 Female/5 Male) were recruited. Habitual physical activity was assessed via the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-14) [14], and participants qualified if they walked unassisted for 10 m and met at least “moderate” activity criteria (≥ 3 days × 20 min vigorous activity, or ≥ 5 days × 30 min moderate‐intensity/walking, or any combination totaling ≥ 600 min/week). Exclusion criteria included any vestibular, neurological (e.g., Parkinson’s, stroke), musculoskeletal, or systemic disorders affecting balance; history of dizziness. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Texas at Tyler (IRB #2023-064), and each participant provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Experimental protocol

Participants walked at their self-selected comfortable pace along a 10 m walkway. A white LED, positioned at the end of the walkway at approximately head height, served as an unexpected stop cue, which was triggered randomly during the single-limb stance phase of the leading (stopping) leg (Fig. 1). Two step-length conditions were tested: normal step and big step. Each condition included 10 trials, with 5 trials involving the stop cue (light on) and 5 trials without the cue (light off). Post hoc kinematic analysis confirmed that the stop cue occurred during the mid- to terminal-swing phase in 94% of stop trials. Each participant contributed 5 stop-cue trials, where the cue either occurred within or outside this swing phase. Because this outcome is categorical (either ‘in-swing phase’ or ‘out-of-swing phase’), and percentages based on 5 trials are coarse (restricted to 0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, or 100%), we compared the distribution of trial outcomes between groups using a chi square test. This trial-level approach retains all available information and directly tests whether the distribution of cue timing differed between groups. The analysis showed no significant group difference (χ² test, p = 0.82), confirming comparable task difficulty across participants.

Data acquisition

Kinetics

Ground reaction forces (GRFs) and center-of-pressure (CoP) were measured using an AMTI OPTIMA force plate (Advanced Mechanical Technology, Inc., Watertown, MA, USA) at a sampling rate of 1,000 Hz. Three-dimensional GRFs (anterior-posterior [AP], medial-lateral [ML], and vertical) were recorded and normalized to body mass (N/kg). Two force plates were installed within the walkway modules, creating a continuous flat surface at ground level: during the single-limb support (SLS) phase, the leading leg stepped on the first force plate, followed by the trailing leg stepping on the second force plate. The light cue appeared while the leading leg was in SLS on the first force plate, initiating the trailing leg to step onto the second force plate and begin the stopping movement phase.

Kinematics

Three-dimensional whole-body motion was captured at 100 Hz using a Vicon Nexus 2.12 system (Oxford Metrics Ltd., Oxford, UK) with 39 reflective markers placed according to Vicon’s Plug-in-Gait full-body model.

EMG

Surface electromyography (EMG) was recorded at 1,000 Hz using a BIOPAC system (BIOPAC Systems, Inc., Goleta, CA, USA) to measure bilateral muscle activity from the tibialis anterior (TA), medial gastrocnemius (mGas), vastus lateralis (VL), biceps femoris (BF), gluteus maximus (Gmax), gluteus medius (Gmed), and erector spinae (ES). Paired Ag/AgCl electrodes (11 mm diameter, 20 mm inter-electrode distance) were placed per SENIAM guidelines [15]. Maximum voluntary isometric contractions (MVICs) were recorded for EMG normalization.

Data processing

All signals were processed in MATLAB R2024b with custom scripts. The stopping phase was defined from the instant vertical GRF on the force plate exceeded 20 N (foot-strike) [16] to 1 s afterward, encompassing the critical knee-extensor eccentric braking and subsequent stabilization.

Kinematics & kinetics

Marker trajectories and GRFs were filtered (4th-order Butterworth; cut-offs: 6 Hz and 25 Hz, respectively) [17]. Joint angles, moments, and powers were computed via inverse dynamics. Negative work at the knee and hip was obtained by integrating negative power over the stopping phase [7, 18].

Calculation of stopping time

Stopping time was calculated using kinematic data from the Vicon Nexus 2.12 system (100 Hz) and kinetic data from the AMTI OPTIMA force plate (1,000 Hz). The stop cue was presented at the point of maximum vertical GRF during SLS of the leading leg, and the participant initiated the stopping movement with the trailing leg.

Statistical evidence and justification of the stopping phase analysis window

Our data showed that stopping times ranged from 0.5 to 0.8 s across subjects and conditions (normal and big steps). Across all participants (n = 24; 12 young and 12 older adults), the mean stopping time was 0.66 ± 0.10 s, with a coefficient of variation of 15%. Statistical analysis revealed no significant effect of age group (p = 0.48) or step condition (p = 0.29), and no interaction. These results confirm that stopping time is consistent across groups and conditions. Based on these results, we selected a 1-second analysis window starting from foot strike on the second force plate to ensure capture of the complete gait termination process with minimal movement and stabilization.

EMG processing & synergy analysis

EMG pre-processing

Signals were detrended, median-filtered, band-pass (20–450 Hz), full-wave rectified, and low-pass filtered (2nd-order Butterworth, 20 Hz) to obtain linear envelopes.

Synergy extraction

Envelopes were RMS-smoothed (100-sample window), selected to balance temporal responsiveness with smooth amplitude estimation. This window length aligns with standard EMG processing recommendations (10–150 ms), facilitates high signal stationarity during dynamic tasks, and minimizes variance without obscuring relevant signal features [7, 20, 21]. Envelopes were then concatenated across five trials per condition. Non-negative matrix factorization (NNMF) decomposed the EMG matrix into W (spatial weights) and C (temporal activations) such that EMG ≈ W·C; residual variance (VAF) guided selection of k synergies (VAF >90%) [7, 22].

W specifies the spatial pattern of the relative activation level of each muscle in the muscle synergy. Each muscle’s contribution is relatively weighted within this spatial structure. The spatial components are multiplied by a scaling (synergy recruitment) coefficient C. Statistical analysis

A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) to determine the required sample size for a mixed ANOVA with one within-subjects factor (step length: Normal vs. Big) and one between-subjects factor (age group: Young vs. Older adults). The analysis assumed a medium effect size (f = 0.25) for the main effects and interaction, based on prior biomechanical studies of balance recovery and gait. The significance level (α) was set to 0.05, and the desired power (1 - β) was set to 0.80, consistent with standard conventions in biomechanical gait research. A correlation of 0.67 among repeated measures was used, derived from our previous pilot study measuring stopping leg biomechanical outcomes across Normal and Big step conditions. The non-sphericity correction (ε) was set to 1, as sphericity is not applicable with two levels of the within-subjects factor. The analysis indicated a total sample size of approximately 24 participants (12 per age group) is required to achieve 80% power to detect a medium effect size for the interaction or main effects.

Data normality for each dependent variable was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk tests, conducted separately for each combination of step length (Normal, Big) and age group (Young, Older) to ensure the assumptions of the mixed ANOVA were met. If normality was violated, data transformations (logarithmic) were applied, or non-parametric alternatives were considered. For each participant, dependent variables were averaged over three valid trials per condition (Normal and Big step) to reduce trial-to-trial variability and enhance measurement reliability.

Two-way mixed ANOVAs were performed to evaluate the effects of step length (within-subjects: Normal vs. Big) and age group (between-subjects: Young vs. Older) on the following dependent variables: peak flexion angles (ankle, knee, hip), negative work (energy absorption at knee and hip joints), full width at half maximum (FWHM) of joint angle, standard deviation of continuous relative phase (SD CRP) as a measure of joint coordination variability, and center of pressure (CoP) and center of mass (CoM) metrics.

Post-hoc analyses with Bonferroni corrections were conducted to address multiple comparisons for significant main effects or interactions, ensuring control of the family-wise error rate. For significant interactions, simple effects analyses compared step length conditions within each age group and age groups within each step length condition. The significance level (α) was set at 0.05 for all tests, with p-values reported to three decimal places for precision. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) to ensure robust and reproducible results.

Muscle synergy statistics

After NNMF decomposition, spatial weight vectors (W, 0–1 scale) were L1-normalized and grouped across participants using k-means clustering with cosine distance. This procedure consistently identified three clusters: a common synergy, a young-adult–specific synergy, and an older-adult–specific synergy. Cluster quality was evaluated with cosine similarity (mean ± SD), silhouette coefficients, and intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC, two-way random, average measures).

For each cluster, a template synergy was created by averaging the spatial weights of all assigned participants. Individual synergies were compared to their template using cosine similarity, with group differences assessed via permutation tests (10,000 iterations) and confirmed with independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. Cluster prevalence across groups was evaluated using Fisher’s exact tests.

Temporal activations (C, amplitude 0–0.5 over 0–1000 ms) were time-aligned by maximizing cross-correlation within ± 10% of the stopping phase and normalized to the task cycle. From each activation, we extracted mean activation, peak timing (ms), and peak amplitude. These metrics were analyzed using two-way mixed ANOVAs (age: young vs. older; step: normal vs. big), with multiple comparisons across synergy features controlled by false discovery rate correction.

Results

Muscle synergies

Muscle synergy analysis revealed both task- and age-dependent changes in neuromuscular modularity during unexpected gait termination.

Synergy count

Normal step: Young adults required 2–3 synergies (2.55 ± 1.20 synergies), whereas older adults needed 4 synergies (3.53 ± 1.06). Big step: Synergy count increased to 3 in young (2.86 ± 1.06) and 4–5 synergies (4.13 ± 0.95) in older adults. These results confirm that big steps and aging both augment synergy complexity.

Spatial pattern differences

Common distal module

Younger-adult-specific knee extensor module

Younger adults exhibited a greater spatial synergy contribution from knee extensors (VL) while reducing the contribution of hip joint muscles (gluteus maximus [Gmax], gluteus medius [Gmed]). This module showed high within-cluster reliability (ICC = 0.76) and strong agreement with the cluster template (cosine similarity = 0.84 ± 0.06), indicating consistent spatial patterns across participants (Fig. 3, middle column).

Older-adult–specific proximal module

Older adults exhibited additional synergies dominated by Gmax, Gmed, and sometimes erector spinae (ES)—module largely absent in younger participants. These synergies were also reliably clustered (ICC = 0.77) and closely matched the template (cosine similarity = 0.83 ± 0.07), demonstrating stable and reproducible spatial patterns within this group (Fig. 3, right column).

Temporal activation profiles

Temporal activations (0–0.5 amplitude over 0–1000 ms) revealed distinct age-related patterns. The young-adult–specific synergy showed a sharp, high-amplitude peak early in the stopping phase (0–300 ms), followed by sustained low-level activation (~ 0.15) for the remainder of the phase. In contrast, the older-adult–specific synergy displayed broader and more distributed activation, with moderate-level bursts (~ 0.25) at multiple time windows (0–100 ms, 300–600 ms, 700–800 ms, 800–1000 ms) (see Fig. 3, second row).

Statistical analysis of mean activation levels across the stopping phase confirmed these differences: older adults exhibited higher average activation than young adults (0.32 ± 0.07 vs. 0.24 ± 0.06; p = 0.02, two-way ANOVA main effect of age). Peak timing was significantly earlier in young adults (median 125 ms) compared with older adults (median 280 ms; p = 0.03). Step condition had a main effect on early-phase peak amplitude (p = 0.04), with higher peaks during big steps.

These results indicate that older adults rely on prolonged and distributed proximal muscle activation to support gait termination, whereas young adults concentrate activation in a brief early-phase peak, primarily reflecting eccentric control of the knee extensors.

Angular displacement and joint work

Angular displacement of the stopping leg revealed significant differences at the knee joint based on step length and age group. Big steps required greater knee flexion than normal steps (p = 0.02), and older adults exhibited significantly reduced knee flexion compared to younger adults (p < 0.01). No significant differences were observed at the ankle (dorsiflexion) or hip (hip flexion) joints (see Table 1 for full data).

Joint work analysis showed age-related differences in how force was controlled during stopping. Temporal overlap

Young adults showed a sequential distal→proximal activation pattern: ankle/knee modules peaked early, followed by brief hip/trunk engagement (Figs. 4C, 3rd panel “Dominant Joint by Delta Angle”). In contrast, older adults exhibited extensive temporal overlap among ankle, knee, and hip angular displacements (p = 0.03, see Table 1 for full data), reflecting a multi-point braking strategy that emphasizes co-activation across all three joints rather than a phased, sequential transfer of control.

Discussion

This study provides significant insights into how aging and task demands, such as step length, influence muscle synergy complexity and neuromuscular control during unexpected gait termination. The findings reveal distinct age-related adaptations in motor control strategies, particularly in response to increased mechanical perturbations, and highlight potential avenues for targeted rehabilitation to reduce fall risk in older adults.

Increased muscle synergy complexity with age and task demand

Our study demonstrated that big-step walking, which imposes greater mechanical demands due to higher CoM velocity and ground reaction forces, significantly increased the number of spatial muscle synergies in both young (from 2 to 3 to 3–4) and older adults (from 4 to 5 to 5–6). This increase reflects the adaptive redistribution of motor control across multiple joints and muscles to manage elevated task demands, rather than being indicative of dysfunction. Larger perturbations require additional neuromuscular modules to distribute braking and stabilization efforts across the limb, ensuring effective balance recovery under greater mechanical challenge.

Research shows that larger perturbations, such as those experienced during big-step walking, require the recruitment of more muscle synergies, meaning coordinated groups of muscles working together, to ensure effective braking and balance recovery [24]. In older adults, we found that reliance on additional synergies becomes even more evident during big-step stopping, reflecting a strategic adjustment of motor control to compensate for age-related changes in distal muscle strength. The central nervous system (CNS) compensates for weaker distal leg muscles, such as the quadriceps and ankle muscles, which struggle to handle the braking demands independently.

Our findings demonstrated that younger participants primarily utilized a distal synergy, combining the vastus lateralis with ankle stabilizers (TA, mGas), alongside a second module to fine-tune balance. Younger adults demonstrated greater knee extensor control to absorb ground impact and stronger plantar flexor engagement to slow forward progression, enabling smoother braking through a distal-first strategy compared with older adults.

In contrast, older adults retained this distal synergy but incorporated additional proximal modules, dominated by the gluteus maximus, gluteus medius, and erector spinae. These proximal modules can be understood as adaptive “redistributions” of control, serving as “backup generators,” stiffening the hip and trunk to manage residual forward momentum and bolster stability.

Older adults consistently required more synergies than younger counterparts, reflecting reduced motor automaticity or an adaptive expansion of neuromuscular modularity that allows a broader “search” for stable coordination patterns [25]. Research has shown that older adults display greater complexity and variability in muscle synergy patterns during overground walking, likely as a response to age-related physiological constraints. Specifically, older adults demonstrate greater inter-subject variability in temporal synergy activations [25] and a heightened reliance on hip-motion-related synergy clusters [26]. These features can be viewed as alternative strategies to achieve the same control objectives under higher task demands. Aging motor systems may adopt a more distributed policy, spreading braking and stabilization across multiple joints and muscles to maintain balance in complex or uncertain situations.

This interpretation aligns with our findings that older adults use compensatory mechanisms to offset reduced distal control at the ankle and knee joints. Prior studies have also shown that aging can disrupt the automaticity and flexibility of modular motor synergies in balance tasks [25, 27, 28]. Consistent with this, older adults in our study demonstrated broader and more distributed activation, along with greater variability in temporal synergy activations, suggesting that they adopt more intricate and individualized neuromuscular strategies to maintain mobility and postural stability.

We observe that although older adults showed more postural sway than younger adults during the stopping phase, both groups required a similar amount of time to come to a complete stop following an unexpected light cue. This suggests that, despite potential age-related changes in muscle strength or other neuromuscular and biomechanical factors, older adults were able to achieve a comparable functional outcome through compensatory mechanisms.

Together, these findings suggest that aging refines synergy structures, increasing their complexity to flexibly meet task demands. Additional proximal involvement and broader activation windows appear to be adaptive strategies for handling challenging gait tasks, highlighting the motor system’s ability to reorganize control rather than signaling disorder or impairment.

Neuromuscular coordination: synergies, FWHM and CRP

Our muscle-synergy analysis revealed that older adults not only required more synergies, especially under big‐step conditions, but also showed higher weighting across multiple muscles, indicative of co‐contraction, rather than a focused knee extensor eccentric control. This pattern aligns with prior reviews demonstrating that, although the number of synergies may remain similar, older adults exhibit greater co‐contraction and temporal variability during gait [27].

Consistent with these synergy findings, we observed that full width at half maximum (FWHM) of joint-pair moments was significantly prolonged in older adults for ankle–knee, knee–hip, and ankle–hip joint moments, indicating sustained, overlapping torque generation. Previous research supports our findings showing that increased FWHM during gait push‐off and toe‐clearance phases has been linked to age‐related prolongation of muscle activation and less precise (blurred) timing [29].

We also found that the standard deviation of continuous relative phase (SD CRP) was increased in older adults across all joint pairs, with a pronounced Age X Step-Length interaction in knee–hip coupling under big-step stops. Similar increases in knee–ankle SD CRP have been reported in fall‐prone older adults, reflecting inconsistent phase relationships [30]. Our finding suggests that compensatory proximal recruitment—engaging hip musculature—disrupts precise timing and leads to noisier coordination, especially under higher movement demands. Additionally, this high-gain, overlapping co-contraction increases metabolic cost, delays peak torque timing (as shown by prolonged FWHM), and limits adaptability regarding in how and when each joint contributes to braking torque (indicated by high SD CRP).

Together, increased FWHM and SD CRP indicate a loss of precise and phased control: the CNS spreads activation over longer durations; as a result, the inter-joint phasing becomes noisier. This multi‐point braking—simultaneous co‐contraction of ankle, knee, and hip—may improve immediate stability but sacrifices energy efficiency and adaptability, increasing fall risk in older adults. These inefficiencies likely underlie the greater postural sway we observed including higher mean CoP velocity, larger CoP area, and increased CoM acceleration variability. This stability-efficiency trade-off is a hallmark of aging-related neuromuscular adaptations; indeed, gait perturbation study has shown that unpredictable conditions prompt more pronounced modifications in synergy recruitment in older adults, with temporal adjustments across multiple recovery steps [31].

Distal-to-proximal braking and compensatory proximal recruitment

Distal-to‐proximal braking maximizes balance control by exploiting the mechanical and neural advantages of the ankle and knee before engaging more proximal segments [32]. Early activation of distal joints allows for a rapid, localized absorption of impact energy and fine‐tuned adjustments at the base of support, which stabilizes the body quickly and minimizes the need for extensive compensatory motions elsewhere [33].

In line with this principle, we observed universal reliance on an early distal synergy dominated by the vastus lateralis and ankle musculature (tibialis anterior, medial gastrocnemius). Upon foot strike, the tibialis anterior and gastrocnemius eccentrically control tibial rotation and modulate the center of pressure to stabilize the foot, while the quadriceps resist knee flexion to absorb the bulk of forward and downward momentum (See Fig. 5 left). This eccentric knee-extensor contraction converts kinetic energy into muscle‐tendon strain, controlling the rate of knee flexion and smoothly arresting the forward and descending momentum of the CoM in a controlled manner [34]. However, in addition to this core distal synergy, we found that older adults recruited supplementary proximal synergies involving the gluteus maximus, gluteus medius, and erector spinae, indicating a compensatory reliance on hip and trunk co-activation. This finding aligns with prior gait research showing that older adults often employ multi-step responses and altered muscle recruitment, especially during ground perturbations or high-speed walking, due to reduced knee extensor torque [4, 35, 36].

Older adults appear to strategically reweight control priorities toward stability when distal muscles or sensory estimates are constrained or when task demands increase (e.g., big-step stops). Age-related declines in quadriceps eccentric strength and ankle proprioception likely prompt the central nervous system (CNS) to integrate hip and trunk control into the braking response [37, 38]. This broader proximal engagement represents a deliberate tradeoff along a stability–efficiency continuum: while it raises metabolic cost and slightly increases postural sway but enhances safety by distributing braking forces and maintaining comfortable postural control. This approach broadens the functional support base, enhancing stability margins, especially during larger perturbations like big-step stops or unexpected ground challenges (e.g., slips or surface drops) [39]. Early active proximal recruitment in older adults likely compensates for weakened distal muscles, prioritizing trunk stability to prevent forward falls (See Fig. 4 right C).

Consistent with this compensatory shift, our findings showed that older adults exhibit less synchronized knee–hip coordination, especially under high-demand (big step) conditions, reflecting compensatory proximal adaptations that evidenced by prolonged FWHM and increased SD CRP. This can be interpreted as a flexible control strategy: the motor system increases joint stiffness and distributes braking forces to maintain stability under mechanical and sensory constraints. However, this strategy comes at the cost of reduced coordination efficiency and higher neuromuscular demand, requiring greater reliance on proximal control. Consequently, these trade-offs likely compromise smooth dynamic balance and ultimately increase fall risk in older adults.

Implications for rehabilitation and fall prevention

Findings from recent research have important implications for the rehabilitation and prevention of falls among older adults or patients with neuromuscular disease through the perspective of neuromuscular control and joint-level coordination. Our findings highlight that targeted interventions that strengthen the quadriceps eccentrically and improve ankle control, such as controlled stand-to-sit movements, step-downs, and adaptive (reactive) stepping drills, can foster distal-driven balance recovery, resulting in better proprioceptive feedback and minimizing reliance on compensatory proximal (hip and trunk) muscles [4, 5, 7, 18].

For example, ruptures of the anterior cruciate ligament compromise knee extensor control and allow excessive tibial anterior movement. After surgical reconstruction, rehabilitation strategies should systematically integrate progressive eccentric quadriceps training early in the post-injury phase using modalities such as eccentric cycle ergometers, functional leg presses, and step-down drills. This approach improves distal-driven balance recovery, resulting in better proprioceptive feedback and ensures graft integrity (or injury protection) while promoting muscle hypertrophy and strength gains [40]. In addition to restoring distal control, it trains flexible motor strategies along the stability–efficiency continuum, enabling safe movement under varying task demands.

In addition, rehabilitation programs should incorporate variable step length and unpredictable walking scenarios to simulate the dynamic challenges of real-world environments, which prepare older adults to adapt to obstacles or crowded settings [41]. These rehabilitation strategies benefit a range of individuals with movement deficits, including those with Parkinson’s disease [42] and stroke survivors [43].

Similarly, individuals experiencing peripheral neuropathy of the toes and/or feet, commonly seen with adult-onset diabetes and after chemotherapy treatments, may also benefit from this targeted gait and balance training. Individuals with peripheral neuropathy often compensate for decreased lower limb somatosensory input by walking more slowly and with reduced variability, which increases joint stiffness; this, in turn, increases their risk of falling when faced with unexpected ground challenges or when real-world situations require faster walking speeds, such as using a traffic crosswalk [44, 45]. Balance training in this population has been shown to improve functional status and reduce postural sway [46–48].

Individualized, functionally relevant regimens that blend eccentric strengthening with balance and coordination exercises align with contemporary best‑practice guidelines and ensure that patients are equipped to execute smooth, sequential braking efforts across the kinetic chain, ultimately enhancing postural stability and decreasing the likelihood of injurious falls in the clinical population [49].

Limitations

A key limitation of this study is that we focused only on the leading limb during the SLS phase, from the light cue to stopping movement, without directly assessing the trailing limb during push-off or the preceding double support phase. Trailing limb mechanics and inter-limb coordination could affect gait termination, and future studies should address these factors. Nevertheless, SLS of the leading limb is critical for evaluating fall risk in older adults, as it relies on knee extensor eccentric control, ankle dorsiflexion, and coordinated ankle-knee-hip movements. Analyzing the leading limb during SLS provides meaningful insight into the neuromuscular deficits most relevant to balance and fall prevention.

Conclusions

Young adults utilize an efficient distal-first braking strategy during gait termination, engaging rapid eccentric control of the quadriceps and ankle muscles to absorb impact, followed by minimal hip involvement for precise balance adjustments. In contrast, older adults adopt a proximal, multi-joint co-contraction approach, characterized by increased hip work, decreased knee contribution, prolonged torque overlap (FWHM), and inconsistent timing (SD CRP), representing a deliberate stability–efficiency tradeoff: it increases metabolic cost and postural sway but enhances safety under high-demand conditions.

Considering this age-related change, rehabilitation should focus on enhancing eccentric control of the quadriceps and improving ankle stability through targeted exercises such as controlled stand-to-sit descents, step-down drills, and adaptive (reactive) walk-and-stop training. This training not only reinforces distal joint control but also develops adaptive motor strategies, allowing individuals to dynamically balance postural stability and efficiency across different task demands. These interventions aim to restore efficient eccentric control of the quadriceps and sequential joint coordination, which are essential for absorbing ground impact effectively, reducing reliance on compensatory proximal muscle recruitment, and ultimately lowering fall risk in older adults.
