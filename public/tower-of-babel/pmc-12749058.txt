Miniaturized broadband lowpass filter based on the integrated square dumbbell and meander structure spoof surface plasmon polaritons
Authors: Lingling Yang, Wentao Ye, Bin Cai, Can Cao, Yongzhi Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA novel miniaturized broadband low-pass filter (LPF) is proposed based on the integrated square dumbbell and meander (SDBM) structure spoof surface plasmon polaritons (SSPPs). Compared with the conventional H-shaped and square dumbbell structure SSPP unit-cells, the integrated SDBM structure can effectively reduce its asymptotic frequency, revealing much better slow-wave characteristics. A broadband LPF is designed by integrating two types of SDBM structure SSPP unit-cell to enhance out-of-band suppression. Finite element method (FEM) simulations demonstrate that the SDBM structure SSPPs-based LPF exhibits excellent lowpass characteristics across a broadband range of 0–2.24 GHz, achieving a remarkably high out-of-band rejection level of − 27 dB in a broadband range of 2.39–8.7 GHz. An equivalent LC circuit model is established for the proposed SDBM-structured SSPPs-based LPF, exhibiting a reasonable consistency with the FEM results. The designed LPF is skillfully realized utilizing conventional PCB manufacturing techniques, achieving with a size of only 0.238λc × 0.12λc (λc is the wavelength at cut-off frequency). The experimental results exhibit a remarkable consistency with the simulated data, thereby validating the soundness and practicality of the proposed design. The proposed broadband LPF featuring robust out-of-band rejection, demonstrates significant potential for integration into compact microwave and terahertz (THz) circuits, paving the way for advanced applications in these frequency bands.Keywords: Lowpass filter (LPF), Spoof surface plasmon polaritons (SSPPs), Square dumbbell and meander (SDBM), High rejectionSubject terms: Engineering, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Low-pass filters (LPFs) are essential components in modern RF front-end modules, effectively suppressing high-frequency noise and harmonics while maintaining crucial baseband signals. As wireless communication systems advance towards greater integration and wider operational bandwidths, the need for compact, high-performance LPFs with sharp roll-off and deep out-of-band rejection becomes more critical1. Traditional LPFs, often based on planar transmission lines (TLs) like microstrip and coplanar waveguides, struggle to achieve both miniaturization and superior filtering performance at the same time2,3. Spoof surface plasmon polaritons (SSPPs) as artificial surface electromagnetic (EM) waves mimicking optical surface plasmons at lower frequencies (microwave to terahertz regimes) have emerged as a revolutionary platform for designing compact and high-performance microwave devices, owing to their unique ability to confine and manipulate EM waves at subwavelength scales4–7. SSPPs have garnered considerable attention for their utilization in microwave and terahertz devices, including antennas8,9, waveguides10, power dividers11, sensors12, and filters4,13–25, which exhibit significant promise for modern communication systems owing to their compact form factor, low propagation loss, and exceptional field confinement characteristics.

Specifically, SSPP structures inherently exhibit low-pass dispersion characteristics, strong field confinement, low transmission loss and reduced interline crosstalk, making them natural candidates for high-performance LPFs without requiring complex additional filtering networks4,14,18,20,22. For instance, Li et al. designed a novel broadband LPF based on SSPP derived from traditional coplanar waveguides (CPWs), demonstrating a cut-off frequency of 8.9 GHz4. Xu et al. developed two broadband LPFs utilizing compact folded transmission lines based on groove structures, exhibiting experimentally measured cut-off frequencies of 17.55 GHz and 19.52 GHz14. The substantial number of unit-cells in these SSPP TLs based LPF enable steep roll-off characteristics and achieves superior upper stopband attenuation exceeding − 48 dB. Moznebi et al. implemented a groundless LPF architecture by integrating butterfly-shaped SSPP cells into the center conductor of a CPW structure20. This design achieved a 33% reduction in cutoff frequency compared to conventional rectangular SSPP designs, while maintaining the same lateral dimensions. Xu et al. introduced a compact broadband LPF utilizing a fishbone-shaped SSPP structure, which demonstrated insertion loss below 3 dB and return loss over 13 dB across an operational bandwidth spanning 0 to 10 GHz24. Despite these advantages, achieving both miniaturization and broadband performance remains a challenge. The current reported SSPP-based LPFs often suffer from prohibitively large footprints, primarily due to the longitudinal dimensions required for effective momentum-matching transition sections and the lateral extent needed for multiple periodic units to achieve steep roll-off and sufficient stopband suppression4,15. This size limitation poses significant challenges for integration into miniaturized and highly integrated circuits. Consequently, achieving significant miniaturization while maintaining broad passband performance and seamless integration with planar TLs remains a critical hurdle.

In this work, we introduce a novel, highly miniaturized broadband SSPP LPF leveraging a combined square dumbbell and meander (SDBM) structure to overcome these limitations synergistically. The proposed SDBM structure offers comparable bandwidth and enhanced filtering performance compared to previous designs, while significantly reducing the size of the SSPP LPF. First, a specialized SDBM structure SSPP unit-cell was designed, and its dispersion properties were thoroughly simulated with the aim of gaining deeper insights into the intricate relationship between its various parameters and the resultant asymptotic frequencies. Subsequently, a complete LPF was designed by integrating two types of SDBM-based SSPP unit cells, each exhibiting distinct comparable asymptotic frequencies to constitute the matching and waveguide sections. Then, the optimal design for the SSPP LPF was determined by analyzing the impact of key parameters through simulations and optimizing the S-parameters for the full structure LPF. The equivalent LC circuit model for the SSPP LPF was developed, demonstrating excellent agreement with full-wave EM simulations. Finally, the designed SSPP LPF was fabricated using conventional PCB manufacturing techniques and measured with a network analyzer. Experimental results confirm that the fabricated LPF achieves a − 3 dB cutoff frequency of 2.24 GHz, a passband extending from DC to 2.24 GHz with insertion loss below − 3 dB, and the out-of-band rejection level surpasses − 27 dB from 2.39 to 8.7 GHz. Crucially, it attains a footprint of only 0.238λc × 0.12λc (λc is the wavelength at cut-off frequency), representing a reduction of over 50% compared to state-of-the-art designs with similar electrical performance6,7,12,13. This breakthrough in miniaturization, coupled with robust broadband characteristics, positions the proposed SSPP LPF as a vital enabler for advanced highly integrated RF systems.

Design and analysis of the SSPPs-based LPF

The conventional SSPPs structure typically adopts an H-shaped structure configuration, labeled as Type A, as depicted in Fig. 1a. In this design, a 0.813 mm thick low-loss dielectric material, Rogers RO4003C featuring a relative permittivity (εr) of 3.55 and a tanδ of 0.0027, was commonly preferred for its optimal foundation. Both the metal structure and the ground plane were made of copper films, each with a 0.035 mm thickness and a conductivity of 5.8 × 107 S/m, ensuring optimal performance and reliability in the resulting structure. The surface dispersion characteristics of the conventional H-shaped structure SSPP are simulated utilizing CST Microwave Studio Suite employing the finite element method (FEM). The final optimized geometrical parameters of H-shaped structure SSPP unit-cell were determined as: l0 = 16 mm, w0 = 3.6 mm, l1 = 12.72 mm, w1 = 3.008 mm, w2 = 0.2 mm, t = 0.813 mm.

(a) The unit-cell of SSPP with normal H-shaped, (b) the unit-cell of SSPP with square dumbbell structure, (c) the unit-cell of SSPP with SDBM structure, (d) the corresponding dispersion curves of three SSPPs unit-cells.

From Fig. 1d, Type A indicating the simulated dispersion curve of the H-shaped structure SSPP exhibits an asymptotic frequency of 5.85 GHz. Drawing inspiration from previous designs15,27, two distinct structures were proposed for comparison: a square dumbbell and a SDBM-based SSPP unit-cell, respectively labeled as Type B and Type C, as depicted in Fig. 1b, c. These structures were designed using the same materials and parameters l0, w0, l1, w1, w2, and t as the conventional H-shaped configuration. The remaining parameters were optimized, resulting in l2 = 2.96 mm, l3 = 4.5 mm, w3 = 0.4 mm, w4 = 0.34 mm. It is a widely acknowledged fact that cut-off frequency of a two-port LC network is fundamentally inversely proportional to , where L represents the equivalent inductance and C signifies the equivalent capacitance. The improvement in capacitance helps to explain the reduction in the asymptotic frequency to be 3.55 GHz for the square dumbbell structure SSPPs unit-cell, as clearly shown in Type B of Fig. 1d. As illustrated in Fig. 1c, a meander line structure was incorporated and finally formed a SDBM structure to further reduce the asymptotic frequency. The equivalent inductance of the meander-line structure within the SDBM-based SSPP unit-cell is demonstrably greater than that of the straight-line configurations in conventional H-shaped and square dumbbell-structured SSPP unit-cells. The significant increase in both equivalent inductance and capacitance accounts for the substantial reduction in the asymptotic frequency to be 2.43 GHz for the SDBM structure SSPPs unit-cell, as evident from the simulated dispersion curve depicted in Type C of Fig. 1d.

The proposed SDBM structure SSPP unit-cell depicted Fig. 1c comprises two integrated components: (1) a square dumbbell geometry, which enhances capacitive coupling and electric field confinement, thereby reducing the asymptotic frequency and improving subwavelength confinement relative to simpler geometries, as validated by prior studies15, and (2) an embedded meander line, which elongates the effective current path within a compact footprint, significantly enhancing inductive effects and slow-wave propagation characteristics beyond those achievable with conventional rectangular or spiral grooves15,26,27. This hybrid approach achieves unprecedented miniaturization while preserving essential wideband performance. Compared with the traditional H-shaped and square dumbbell structure SSPPs unit-cell in Fig. 1a, b, the SDBM structure offers greater adjustability due to the increased number of parameters on both sides, indicating a higher degree of design flexibility.

As depicted in Fig. 2, the impacts of the crucial geometrical parameters of the SDBM structure on the dispersion relations are analyzed in depth. In this study, we focus exclusively on four pivotal structural parameters: side length (l1, l2) of the square dumbbell structure, length (l3) of meander wire, and gap width (w3) between the two L-shaped arms in adjacent two square dumbbells. All other parameters remain unchanged, as detailed in the preceding analysis. In order to guarantee precise analytical outcomes, we employ a rigorous simulated approach. This methodology involves altering a single structural parameter while keeping all other variables constant.

The dispersion curves of the SDBM structure SSPPs with different (a, b) side length (l1, l2) of the square dumbbell structure, (c) transverse length (l3) of meander line structure, and (d) gap width (w3) between the two L-shaped arms in adjacent two square dumbbells.

As shown in Fig. 2a, when increases l1 from 11 to 14 mm, the asymptotic frequency of the SDBM structure SSPPs unit-cell decreases from 2.67 to 2.21 GHz, which is attributed to the enhanced equivalent capacitance. As shown in Fig. 2b, when the value of l2 is varied from 1 to 4 mm, the elongated structure of the square dumbbell leads to an increase in the equivalent capacitance. Consequently, the asymptotic frequency experiences a corresponding decrease. When l3 of meander line structure increases from 1.5 to 4.5 mm, as depicted in Fig. 2c, the asymptotic frequency of the SSPP unit-cell is decreased accordingly. The impact of l3 on performance can be fully explained theoretically. The transverse length of the meander line, l3 directly governs the total physical length of the conductive path within a fixed unit cell period. An increase in l3 elongates this current path, which results in a proportional increase in the total equivalent inductance (L) of the SDBM unit cell. This enhanced inductance has two primary, interconnected effects on the dispersion characteristics: Firstly, reduction of asymptotic frequency: the asymptotic frequency () of a SSPP unit-cell is fundamentally governed by its equivalent LC circuit resonance, approximately following . The significant increase in L caused by a larger l3, with only a minor associated change in capacitance, is the dominant factor that lowers the LC product. This physically explains the observed redshift of the asymptotic frequency in the dispersion relation (Fig. 2c). Secondly, the enhancement of slow-wave effect, the increased inductance per unit length, without a substantial increase in the unit-cell’s physical period, leads to a higher phase constant (β). This results in a lower guided wavelength (λg = 2π/β), thereby strengthening the slow-wave effect. This is evidenced by the dispersion curve in Fig. 2c shifting downwards and becoming flatter for larger l3 values, indicating a reduced group velocity over a wider bandwidth. In Fig. 2d, as the width w3 is increased from 0.1 to 0.4 mm, the gap between the two L-shaped arms in the adjacent square dumbbells widens, leading to a reduction of equivalent capacitance. Consequently, the asymptotic frequency rises from 1.67 to 2.23 GHz. However, a smaller w3 necessitates greater fabrication precision, thereby increasing production costs, which is undesirable for this project.

Based on the SDBM-based SSPPs unit-cell, the schematic of a compact LPF is meticulously depicted in Fig. 3a, which consists of three distinct section regions. Region I is a microstrip line, distinguished by its 50Ω impedance, and functions as the input and output ports. Regions II and III constitute the waveguide sections, integrating two distinct types of SDBM-based SSPPs unit-cells. These unit-cells are meticulously engineered based on the parametric studies presented in the preceding section to serve as the matching and mode conversion sections. The detail number of SDBM-based SSPP unit-cells in the waveguide section was optimized to enhance out-of-band suppression. The LPF incorporates an optimized gradient transition for efficient mode conversion from a 50 Ω microstrip line feed to the SSPP mode, ensuring broad passband transmission with minimal insertion loss.

(a) Schematic of the SDBM structure SSPPs-based LPF, (b) equivalent LC circuit model, (c) the S-parameters (S11 and S21) obtained from EM and circuit simulation.

To showcase the filtering mechanism and assess the performance of the designed LPF, comprehensive EM simulations have been conducted using the FEM with CST Microwave Studio. In these simulations, open boundaries along the direction of EM wave propagation are assigned boundary conditions simulating an external area, while the PEC boundaries are established in the x- and y-axis directions. According to simulation and optimization, the final remaining parameters of the whole LPF were optimized, resulting in lp = 2.5 mm, ls = 31.62 mm, lg = 4.31 mm, ws = 16 mm.

To further analyze the transmission performance of the proposed SDBM-based SSPPs LPF, as shown in Fig. 3b, the corresponding equivalent LC circuit model is constructed with TL theory. In order to streamline the analysis process, the issue of loss is temporarily set aside. The utilization of the equivalent LC circuit model emerges as a pivotal instrument in forecasting and enhancing the filtering efficacy23,28. The structural components are intricately intertwined with the circuitry elements, influencing the inductive and capacitive attributes of the filter configuration. This approach confers a profound comprehension of the filtering dynamics, thereby expediting the development of robust filters tailored for diverse applications. To facilitate a more precise analysis of the equivalent LC circuit, the SDBM structure SSPP is divided into three distinct components, as depicted in Fig. 3b. L1 and C1 are correspond to the equivalent capacitance and inductance of middle TL structure, respectively, the L2 and C3 represent the square dumbbell structure, while the L3 and C4 model the meander line structure. The target low-pass filtering properties of the proposed LPF can be efficiently achieved using the optimization capabilities of Advanced Design System (ADS 2022), a software platform for high-frequency circuit simulation. The structural parameters of the SDBM structure SSPP LPF are systematically derived through an equivalent LC circuit model, which is rigorously validated against both reflection coefficient and transmission coefficient metrics. ADS streamlines this validation process, ensuring circuit model precisely emulates the EM response of the physical filter design. Iterative refinement of the circuit components within the ADS environment enables the engineering of a tailored low-pass filtering band that aligns with predefined specifications and performance benchmarks. Based on the above analysis, the finalized equivalent LC circuit model component parameters for the proposed LPF are extracted and optimized as C1 = 4.61 pF, C2 = 0.96 pF, C3 = 5.82 pF, C4 = 0.72 pF, C5 = 1.61 pF, C6 = 72.62 pF, L1 = 0.12 nH, L2 = 1.31 nH, L3 = 0.72 nH.

The S-parameters, specifically S11 and S21, of the designed LPF based on the SDBM structure SSPPs, as obtained from EM and circuit simulations, are vividly presented in Fig. 3c. The simulation outcomes of the circuit model are in substantial concurrence with those of the FEM-based EM simulation, thereby validating the anticipated performance of the design. The subtle discrepancy in return loss (S11) can be largely ascribed to the oversight of parasitic coupling in the LC circuit model and the authentic losses sustained by the middle dielectric layer. The cut-off frequency of the proposed LPF from EM simulation is 2.23 GHz, which is marginally lower than the result obtained through the equivalent LC circuit simulation. Additionally, the EM simulation demonstrates an insertion loss (S21) of less than − 3 dB across the frequency range from DC to 2.24 GHz. Moreover, within the frequency ranging from 2.39 to 8.7 GHz, an out-of-band rejection exceeding − 27 dB is achieved. The S11 is better than − 13 dB from DC to 2.24 GHz. Both EM and equivalent LC circuit simulation results validate the highly efficient low-pass transmission characteristics and wide operational bandwidth of the proposed SDBM structure based LPF.

To gain intuitive insight into the transmission characteristics of the LPF based on the SDBM structure, Fig. 4 shows the electric field (|Ez|) distributions at frequencies of 0.8 GHz, 1.6 GHz, and 3.2 GHz. The color scale demarcates the electric field amplitude, with bright blue regions indicating the minima and bright red regions denoting the maxima, respectively. As seen from Fig. 4a, b, the |Ez| are uniformly distributed across the entire LPF structure when the incident guided wave propagates along the + x axis direction at 0.8 GHz and 1.6 GHz. This demonstrates that the EM wave energy can efficiently penetrate the SDBM structure LPF within the passband range, and the guided wave to SSPPs wave conversion process is seamless. This also suggests that the guided mode of the incident EM wave in the microstrip line can be seamlessly converted into the SSPPs mode, thus leading to a passband characterized by exceptional transmission properties. As depicted in Fig. 4c, a substantial portion of the z-component electric fields are densely concentrated at the input segment of the LPF near 3.2 GHz. This signifies that the incident guided wave energy is sent by microstrip line and effectively impeded by the SDBM structure within the operational frequency, as it exceeds the cut-off frequency of SSPPs. Consequently, the majority energy is significantly attenuated, culminating in the inability of the EM wave to traverse the SDBM structure-based LPF unobstructed.

The simulated electric field (|Ez|) of the proposed LPF at (a) 0.8 GHz, (b) 1.6 GHz, and (c) 3.2 GHz.

To gain a deeper insight into the flexibility and precise controllability of the proposed SDBM structure LPF, we delve into exploring the intricate effects of various structural parameters on the overall low-pass filtering performance. In this study, we also concentrate solely on four crucial parameters: the side lengths (l1, l2) of the square dumbbell structure, length (l3) of meander line, and gap width (w3) between two L-shaped arms of adjacent square dumbbells. As shown in Fig. 5a, b, a subtle redshift of cut-off frequency emerges, while the S21 maintains largely unchanged as the values of l1 and l2 increase, which aligns with the variations evident in the dispersion curves (see Fig. 3a, b). Figure 5c demonstrates that as l3 increases, the cut-off frequency shifts towards the lower frequency region, while S11 remains relatively constant. It is evident that the value of l3 allows for more extensive adjustment compared to the cut-off frequencies associated with l1 and l2. It is noteworthy that a spurious harmonic appearing in the high-frequency portion (around 8 GHz) of the stopband is appeared because of higher-order resonance in Fig. 5c. Its origin is directly linked to the periodic nature of the SSPP waveguide section. While the fundamental SSPP mode is cut off beyond approximately 2.24 GHz, the finite periodic structure of the SDBM unit-cells can support higher-order standing-wave resonances at specific frequencies within the stopband. The resonance near 8 GHz arises from the coherent interaction and coupling of these higher-order Bloch modes within the bounded periodic structure. Unlike the other parameter changes, as shown in Fig. 5d, the cut-off frequency of passband increases gradually while the S11 remains nearly unchanged with the increase of w3.

Simulated the insertion loss (S21) of the proposed LPF with different geometric parameters of SDBM structure SSPPs: (a, b) side length (l1, l2) of the square dumbbell structure, (c) length (l3) of meander wire, and (d) gap width (w3) of adjacent two square-dumbbells.

Therefore, by modifying the aforementioned structural parameters, cut-off frequencies and passband frequency of the LPF can be adjusted adaptively, and the width of the low-pass frequency band can also be subtly fine-tuned. This implies that bandwidth and cut-off frequency of the designed LPF can be independently controlled by altering structural parameters. It is further inferred that the LPF possesses flexible and adaptable characteristics, enabling it to meet the diverse requirements of different application scenarios.

Fabrication and measurement

To further ascertain the performance of the proposed SDBM structure SSPPs-based LPF, microwave experimental verification is also essential. Initially, a prototype of the designed LPF was fabricated using conventional PCB manufacturing technology, based on the optimized simulation results. Subsequently, the input and output ports of the LPF test specimen are skillfully welded with two SMA connectors, culminating in the finalized LPF test sample measuring 31.8 mm × 16 mm × 0.813 mm, as shown in Fig. 6a. The insertion and return losses (S21 and S11) of the proposed LPF are measured using a network analyzer (3656D), as depicted in Fig. 6b.

(a, b) The fabricated LPF test sample and the test setup, (c) the simulation and measurement insertion and return losses (S21 and S11).

Figure 6c compares the measured S21 and S11, which largely align with the simulation results, despite some minor deviations. The discrepancy between the experimental data and the simulation results can be attributed to fabrication errors, such as imperfections in the filter processing or inaccuracies in the SMA joint welding process. However, despite these discrepancies, the overall experimental findings exhibit a basic agreement with the numerical simulations of the designed LPF, thus corroborating the its remarkable transmission property within low-pass band and its notable suppression of out-of-band signals in high-frequency range. The experimental measurement cut-off frequency of the fabricated LPF is 2.24 GHz, slightly lower than the simulation one due to the limited fabrication precision and test environment. Meanwhile, the measured S21 of the designed LPF remains − 1.02 dB on average and below − 3 dB from DC to 2.24 GHz, while demonstrating an out-of-band rejection is below − 27 dB from 2.39 to 8.7 GHz. In addition, the measured S11 is below − 13 dB on average from DC to 2.24 GHz, and the minimal return loss is up to − 53 dB. All experimental results confirm the high-efficiency low-pass transmission characteristics and broad operational bandwidth of the proposed SDBM structure based LPF.

This analysis highlights the essential characteristics of the previously reported LPFs, such as their cut-off frequency (fc), minimal return loss (Min.RL), rejection, and size. From Table 1, our designed LPF has some obvious advantages compared with the previous ones4,14,18,20,22,24. Firstly, our design utilizes a straightforward SSPP structure, thus simplifying and expediting the manufacturing process. Secondly, the proposed LPF exhibits exceptional performance in terms of very low cut-off frequency, achieving minimal return loss and maintaining a high rejection level. Thirdly, and more significantly, the designed LPF boasts a remarkably compact size, measuring just 0.238λc × 0.12λc, which significantly enhances its space efficiency and portability. Fourthly, the designed LPF exhibits flexible and adaptable characteristics, thereby effectively accommodating the diverse requirements of various application scenarios.

Conclusion

In summary, we have designed an ultra-compact and miniaturized LPF based on SDBM structure SSPPs. The dispersion properties of SDBM-based SSPPs unit-cell are numerically analyzed, which has the lower asymptotic frequency compared the conventional H-shaped and square-dumbbell structures. The designed LPF is constructed and composed of microstrip line and two distinct types of SDBM-based SSPP unit-cells, middle dielectric layer and the metal ground plane. FEM simulation results indicate that the designed SDBM structure SSPPs-based LPF exhibits an S21 over − 3 dB across the frequency range from DC to 2.24 GHz, and an out-of-band rejection below − 27 dB from 2.39 to 8.7 GHz, which is basically agrees well with equivalent LC circuit model simulation. Moreover, the further simulation results demonstrate that bandwidth and cut-off frequency of the designed LPF can be independently adjusted by modifying its structural parameters. Finally, the designed SDBM structure SSPP-based LPF with compact size of 0.238λc × 0.12λc is realized and fabricated through traditional PCB technology. This compact footprint demonstrates a size reduction exceeding 50% in comparison with state-of-the-art LPF designs exhibiting comparable electrical performance characteristics6,7,12,13. The experimental measurement results exhibit a remarkable consistency with simulation ones, thereby validating the designed LPF’s performance. This LPF demonstrates an extensive − 3 dB bandwidth, encompassing frequencies from DC up to 2.24 GHz, while maintaining a return loss lower than − 13 dB throughout this range. Additionally, the measured out-of-band rejection level surpasses − 27 dB up to 3.88fc. These results underscore the effectiveness and reliability of the designed LPF in achieving the desired filtering characteristics. The proposed compact LPF is well-positioned for widespread use in increasingly communication systems, due to its outstanding low-pass filtering performance, improved integration capabilities, and cost-effective nature.
