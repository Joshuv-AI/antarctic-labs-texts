Benchmarking Vision LLMs in fetal ultrasound interpretation: a five-point expert evaluation of standard vs. custom prompts
Authors: Wala ELsharif, Mahmood Alzubaidi, Muhammad Tukur, Abdullatif Magram, Fatima Anver, Aysha Hamza, Safia Said, Rukhayya Khan, Mowafa Househ, Marco Agus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeFetal ultrasound imaging is critical for prenatal care, demanding accurate anatomical interpretation. This study evaluates the potential of Vision Large Language Models (LLMs) in interpreting fetal ultrasound images, exploring whether tailored prompts can enhance performance compared to standard prompts, and assessing their utility in clinical settings.MethodsNine fetal ultrasound images were analyzed using six advanced Vision LLMs via the Chatbot Arena platform. Standard prompts were compared against expert-crafted tailored questions. Three expert sonographers assessed the models’ outputs across five criteria—anatomical recognition, biometric potential, picture quality, normalcy assessment, and clinical recommendations—using a Likert scale (1–5).ResultsStandard prompts yielded limited interpretative accuracy. In contrast, custom prompts significantly improved performance, with Claude Sonnet 3.5 and ChatGPT4o achieving median scores of 19 and 18, respectively. Models excelled in analyzing fetal femur and trans-cerebellum images, with clinical advice being the easiest to identify. Challenges persisted in precise anatomical identification and image quality assessment, revealing limitations in visual recognition. Smaller models like pixtral-12b showed notable improvement with tailoring, suggesting fine-tuning potential, while larger models did not consistently outperform smaller ones, indicating factors beyond model size influence efficacy.ConclusionTailored prompts markedly enhance Vision LLMs’ ability to interpret fetal ultrasound images, supporting their potential as aids in prenatal diagnosis and education. However, limitations in anatomical precision and image quality assessment persist. Future research should focus on refining models with specialized datasets, optimizing architectures, and advancing prompt engineering to maximize clinical utility.

Introduction

Fetal ultrasound imaging is a cornerstone of prenatal care, offering critical insights into fetal development and maternal health. As a noninvasive, real-time imaging modality, it enables clinicians to monitor fetal growth, anatomy, and well-being throughout pregnancy [1]. Regular ultrasound examinations are crucial for early detection of congenital disabilities, facilitating timely interventions [2]. However, interpreting fetal ultrasound images is complex and requires specialized expertise. Challenges such as fetal mobility, maternal obesity, and operator dependency can affect accuracy and consistency [3]. Achieving high-quality standard planes and precise biometric measurements is time-consuming and highly dependent on skilled operators, underscoring the need for tools to enhance efficiency and diagnostic accuracy.

Advancements in prenatal imaging coincide with the revolution in natural language processing driven by Large Language Models (LLMs) [4]. Models like GPT-4 and PaLM have demonstrated remarkable proficiency in various language tasks, including translation, summarization, and complex question answering [5]. Vision Large Language Models (Vision LLMs) extend these capabilities by integrating visual perception with language understanding [6]. These models process and interpret visual data alongside textual information, enabling them to tackle complex tasks involving both images and language.

The integration of Vision LLMs into medical imaging presents a promising avenue for enhancing diagnostic processes [7]. Vision LLMs can assist in interpreting medical images by identifying key features, detecting anomalies, and generating descriptive reports [8]. Their multimodal capabilities allow them to comprehend complex visual content and relate it to medical terminology, improving diagnostic accuracy and efficiency. For example, Vision LLMs can detect subtle patterns or abnormalities in imaging data that may be challenging for human observers, supporting early disease detection and personalized treatment planning [9].

Several studies have explored the application of Vision LLMs in the medical domain. The Segment Anything Model (SAM) has been adapted for medical image segmentation tasks, assisting in delineating anatomical structures and lesions across various modalities [10]. Yan et al. [11] investigated multimodal ChatGPT-4V for medical visual question answering, highlighting the model’s ability to interpret medical images when appropriately prompted. Other works have focused on using Vision LLMs for medical image captioning, effectively translating visual data into descriptive textual reports [12]. While these studies demonstrate the potential of Vision LLMs, they often concentrate on specific applications or require significant domain-specific training, indicating a need for further research into their generalizability across diverse medical imaging tasks.

Beyond generic multimodal models, recent task-specific approaches in fetal ultrasound imaging demonstrate high clinical utility. For example, a rule-guided interpretable lightweight framework was proposed for unified standard plane capture and biometric measurement [13], emphasizing efficiency and suitability for real-time deployment in clinical practice. Similarly, a contrastive prototype federated learning method against noisy labels has been introduced [14], enabling robust and privacy-preserving standard plane detection across distributed hospital datasets. In addition, ScanAhead demonstrated a video-based approach to predict fetal head standard planes during probe navigation, facilitating faster and more reliable plane acquisition at the bedside [15].

These task-focused pipelines underscore the progress of dedicated fetal ultrasound models toward integration into real-world clinical workflows. However, they remain specialized for narrowly defined tasks and often require curated training datasets. By contrast, our work investigates the broader potential of general-purpose Vision-Language Models (VLMs), which—while not optimized for any single task—offer distinctive advantages such as zero-shot adaptability, the ability to handle free-text clinical questions, and integration of textual reasoning alongside visual interpretation. Thus, VLMs may complement specialized models, particularly for flexible tasks such as generating counseling-oriented narratives or rapidly adapting to novel clinical queries. Direct benchmarking with these advanced systems will be an important next step to characterize performance gaps, and we highlight this comparative analysis as a priority for future research.

Evaluating and benchmarking Vision LLMs with expert clinicians is crucial for integrating these models into clinical practice [16]. Expert evaluation ensures that models meet the stringent standards required in healthcare, particularly in tasks where diagnostic accuracy is vital. Previous efforts have introduced multimodal benchmarks to assess Vision LLM capabilities, but evaluations often remain limited to specific tasks or datasets [17]. Collaborations between AI researchers and medical professionals are essential to develop evaluation frameworks that reflect real-world clinical scenarios. Benchmarking against expert interpretations allows for a rigorous assessment of whether Vision LLMs can reliably assist in medical decision-making, identifying strengths and areas needing improvement before clinical implementation [18].

The primary objective of this study is to investigate whether expert-driven custom prompts can enhance the performance of Vision LLMs in interpreting fetal ultrasound images. By assessing models under both standard and custom prompts, we aim to determine the impact of prompt engineering on model accuracy and reliability. Performance of models on the custom prompt

The custom prompt produced dramatic improvements (Table 1; Fig. 1b). Claude Sonnet 3.5 achieved the highest total (19 [3]), followed by ChatGPT4o (18 [5]) and llama-3.2-vision-90b (17 [3]). Even Gemini 1.5 Pro-002, the weakest baseline model, nearly doubled its score to 11 [2]. Question-level medians rose to 2–5, indicating enhanced coverage across all five evaluation criteria.

This responsiveness highlights the importance of prompt engineering: tailored instructions unlocked diagnostic plane identification (Q3), recognition of abnormalities (Q4), and provision of clinical recommendations (Q5), tasks where baseline responses were weakest.

Discussion

In this study, our primary objective was to investigate whether expert-driven custom prompts can enhance the performance of Vision LLMs in interpreting fetal ultrasound images. By assessing models under both standard and custom prompts, we evaluated the impact of prompt engineering on model accuracy and reliability. Our findings confirm that expert-driven custom prompts significantly enhance the performance of Vision LLMs in interpreting fetal ultrasound images. All models exhibited limited interpretative abilities using standard prompts, with low median total scores ranging from 5.0 to 8.5 (see Table 4). This suggests that standard prompts do not provide sufficient guidance for the models to effectively interpret complex medical images. When provided with custom prompts, the models showed substantial improvements. For instance, Claude Sonnet 3.5 improved from a median total score of 6.0 to 19.0, and ChatGPT4o from 7.5 to 18.0 (refer to Fig. 1b). This enhancement underscores the critical role of prompt engineering in eliciting accurate and detailed responses from Vision LLMs, directly addressing our primary research question.

While exploring this main question, we also considered related aspects: The ability of Vision LLMs to identify anatomical structures and provide clinical recommendations. The models demonstrated varying proficiency in identifying anatomical structures. Under custom prompts, higher median scores were achieved in Q5 (clinical recommendations) and Q4 (normality assessment and visible abnormalities), with median scores increasing to approximately 3.0 and 2.5, respectively (see Fig. 4). This indicates that models are more adept at generating clinical recommendations and normality assessments when provided with specific guidance. However, challenges remained in Q1 (Anatomical Structures Identification), with median scores around 2.0 even under custom prompts. Interpretation of findings

Performance of small open-source models

Notably, small open-source models like pixtral-12b and reka-core demonstrated moderate but consistent improvements when provided with custom prompts. While their overall performance was less impressive compared to top-tier models, the radar charts show balanced enhancements across evaluation criteria. This suggests that with further fine-tuning on specialized fetal ultrasound datasets, these accessible models have the potential to enhance their interpretative capabilities significantly. Their performance underscores the possibility of developing effective medical imaging tools without relying solely on large proprietary models.

Performance of larger models

Larger models such as Gemini 1.5 Pro-002 and llama-3.2-vision-90b presented varied results. Gemini 1.5 Pro-002 showed strong baseline performance under standard prompts and notable improvements with custom prompts, as indicated by the substantial area coverage in its radar chart. This model exhibited particularly robust performance in Q2 (biometric assessment) and Q4 (normality assessment), suggesting inherent capabilities in these domains.

llama-3.2-vision-90b demonstrated significant improvements with custom

prompts, especially in Q4, scoring up to 4.5, which indicates strong abilities in detecting visible abnormalities. However, its performance was more variable across other evaluation criteria, reflecting specialized strengths but also highlighting areas needing improvement. These findings suggest that while larger models possess substantial potential, their effectiveness in specialized tasks like fetal ultrasound interpretation depends on factors beyond size alone, such as training data relevance and architectural optimization.

Importance of prompt engineering

The substantial improvements observed across all models with custom prompts emphasize the critical importance of domain-specific prompt engineering. Tailored prompts provided necessary context and guidance, enabling models to better understand and interpret the complex features present in fetal ultrasound images. The radar charts visually confirm that custom prompts lead to expanded and more symmetrical performance profiles, indicating balanced enhancements across evaluation criteria. This finding underscores the crucial role of prompt customization in maximizing the capabilities of AI models in medical imaging, bridging the gap between general-purpose language models and specialized clinical applications.

Implications for clinical practice

The findings have significant implications for the integration of AI models into clinical practice, particularly in fetal ultrasound interpretation. The enhanced performance with custom prompts suggests that AI models, when guided appropriately, can serve as supportive tools for healthcare professionals.

Enhanced diagnostic support: AI models can assist sonographers and clinicians by providing preliminary analyses, aiding in the identification of fetal anatomical structures and potential abnormalities. The models’ proficiency in generating clinical recommendations (Q5) and normality assessments (Q4) indicates their potential usefulness in supporting diagnostic decision-making processes.

Educational applications: These models can be valuable educational resources for training sonographers and medical students. By offering detailed analyses and interpretations, they can enhance learning experiences and provide trainees with immediate feedback on image assessments.

Resource optimization: In areas with limited access to expert sonographers, AI assistance could improve prenatal care quality by offering guidance on image interpretation, especially for standard views such as the fetal femur and trans-cerebellum. The ability of models to perform well on standardized images suggests they could help bridge gaps in healthcare accessibility.

However, the limitations observed, particularly in complex image interpretation tasks involving detailed anatomical recognition and biometric estimations, underscore the necessity for cautious implementation. AI models should complement, not replace, professional clinical judgment. Their integration into clinical workflows should ensure rigorous validation, maintain human oversight, and adhere to ethical considerations regarding patient safety and data privacy.

Limitations of the study

While the study provides valuable insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. Foremost, the evaluation was conducted on a very small and heterogeneous dataset of only nine fetal ultrasound images obtained from anonymized archival teaching datasets. This sample size, chosen deliberately to keep the expert evaluation workload manageable, constrains the statistical robustness of our findings and limits their generalizability. As such, the study should be interpreted as a pilot feasibility analysis rather than a definitive validation.

The restricted dataset further meant that pathological cases were not included; consequently, model performance in detecting abnormalities could not be assessed. In addition, while the dataset spans gestational ages from 18 to 40 weeks based on first-trimester crown–rump length measurements, precise gestational ages for each selected image were not available. This prevents stratification of performance by developmental stage and represents an additional limitation.

Finally, our reliance on median expert scores and predefined evaluation criteria may not fully capture nuanced interpretive errors or rare but clinically significant mistakes. To address these gaps, future research should validate findings on larger and demographically diverse cohorts, such as the HC18 benchmark, incorporate pathological cases, and employ extended evaluation metrics. Such efforts will be essential to establish the clinical robustness and equity of Vision Language Models in fetal ultrasound interpretation.

Clinical safety and model hallucinations: Another critical limitation relates to the potential for model hallucinations. In the medical AI context, hallucinations occur when a system generates plausible but factually incorrect outputs, such as describing anatomical structures or pathologies not present in the ultrasound image [19]. Such errors may appear convincing and fluent, yet they carry significant risk for misinforming clinicians and trainees.

Recent work has underscored the prevalence of hallucinations across Vision-Language Models in healthcare applications. Chen et al. introduced Med-HallMark, the first benchmark designed for hallucination detection in medical multimodal settings, emphasizing how unverified outputs can compromise safety [20]. Similarly, Das et al. demonstrated that hallucinations can manifest both in medical image interpretation and in text-to-image generation, producing clinically implausible content with potential to mislead diagnostic reasoning [21]. These findings echo broader concerns from clinical practice, where hallucinations represent a central challenge to building trustworthy AI [22].

In our qualitative observations, some Vision LLM outputs overstated confidence in anatomical identification or suggested biometric measurements with insufficient visual evidence. Though we did not perform a systematic analysis of hallucination frequency in this preliminary study, these observations illustrate the need for vigilance before clinical adoption. Mitigation strategies may include targeted domain fine-tuning, uncertainty estimation, and human-in-the-loop feedback mechanisms. Future work should also explore structured benchmarks to quantify hallucination severity and its clinical implications, ensuring that VLMs deployed in sensitive domains such as fetal ultrasound meet stringent safety standards.

Fairness and potential for bias: In addition to hallucinations, fairness and bias present fundamental challenges in applying Vision-Language Models to medical imaging. Bias in AI refers to systematic errors that disproportionately affect specific demographic or clinical subgroups, potentially exacerbating existing health inequities [23, 24]. In medical imaging, such biases may stem from imbalanced datasets, annotation differences, or limited geographic representation, leading to disparate performance across populations [25].

Fetal ultrasound is particularly vulnerable to these issues. Wang et al. recently demonstrated that widely used fetal ultrasound datasets exhibit embedded ethical biases linked to demographic representation, acquisition protocols, and institutional practices [26]. Similarly, recent analyses of foundation models have revealed demographic biases in large vision-language systems trained on diverse medical imaging corpora, raising concerns about generalizability across underrepresented groups [27].

Our own study, which relied on a very small and heterogeneous convenience sample, is especially limited in this respect. The findings cannot be assumed to generalize across diverse maternal demographics, geographical settings, or imaging conditions. Ensuring fairness in future work will require not only larger and more diverse ultrasound datasets but also methods to detect, monitor, and mitigate demographic and institutional biases throughout the model development lifecycle. Addressing these challenges is essential for building VLM-based tools that can be equitably and safely integrated into diverse clinical environments.

Future research directions

Building on these findings, future research should focus on several key areas:

Fine-tuning open-source models: Further training small models like pixtral-12b and reka-core on specialized fetal ultrasound datasets could enhance their interpretative capabilities. Evaluating their performance after fine-tuning will assess their potential as accessible and cost-effective tools in clinical settings.

Enhancing model architectures: Investigating architectural improvements for models that showed variable performance, such as llama-3.2-vision-90b, can help optimize their ability to process and interpret complex medical images. This includes exploring multimodal training approaches that more effectively integrate visual and textual information.

Expanding evaluation datasets: Utilizing larger and more diverse datasets encompassing a wider range of fetal anatomical variations, gestational ages, and pathological conditions will improve the robustness of model assessments. Inclusion of pathological cases is particularly important for evaluating models’ clinical applicability and safety.

Advanced prompt engineering: Exploring automated prompt generation methods and assessing the impact of different prompt structures on model performance can optimize interpretative accuracy. Research into adaptive prompts that adjust based on model outputs may further enhance performance.

Clinical integration studies: Conducting real-world trials to evaluate how AI models can be effectively integrated into clinical workflows is essential. Studies should focus on user experience, reliability, regulatory compliance, and the impact on diagnostic outcomes and clinician workload.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the capabilities of six state-of-the-art Vision Language Models (Vision LLMs) in interpreting fetal ultrasound images, focusing on the impact of expert-driven custom versus standard prompts across five evaluation criteria on nine diverse images. Custom prompts significantly enhanced the diagnostic performance of AI vision models. All models showed substantial improvements with tailored prompts, underscoring the critical role of prompt engineering in medical imaging tasks. Top-performing models, such as Claude Sonnet 3.5 and ChatGPT4o, achieved median total scores of 19.0 and 18.0 under custom prompts, compared to much lower scores with standard prompts. Notably, smaller open-source models like pixtral-12b and reka-core also exhibited appreciable improvements with custom prompts, indicating that accessible models can become valuable tools in specialized medical applications with appropriate fine-tuning and domain-specific guidance. While their overall performance was less robust than top-tier models, their balanced enhancements across evaluation criteria suggest significant potential, especially in resource-constrained settings. In contrast, larger models such as llama-3.2-vision-90b and Gemini 1.5 Pro-002 showed variable results. Gemini 1.5 Pro-002 displayed strong baseline performance and notable improvements with custom prompts, particularly in biometric assessment (Q2) and normality assessment (Q4). However, llama-3.2-vision-90b exhibited more specialized strengths with significant improvements in normality assessment but variable performance in other criteria. These observations suggest that model size alone does not guarantee superior performance; factors such as training data relevance and architectural optimization are critical determinants of effectiveness. The study highlights the importance of domain-specific customization and prompt engineering in maximizing AI models’ capabilities. Tailored prompts provided essential context and guidance, enabling models to better interpret complex features in fetal ultrasound images. Models were most proficient in providing clinical recommendations (Q5) and normality assessments (Q4), while challenges remained in anatomical recognition and image quality assessment (Q1–Q3). This underscores the need for further development to enhance models’ abilities in detailed anatomical and technical evaluations. Our findings have significant implications for clinical practice. AI models, when guided by expert-driven prompts, can support sonographers by enhancing diagnostic accuracy and efficiency, particularly in interpreting standard fetal ultrasound views. They also hold promise as educational resources for training medical professionals, especially in regions with limited access to expert clinicians. However, the limitations observed in complex image interpretation tasks highlight the necessity for cautious implementation. AI models should complement, not replace, professional clinical judgment. Integration into clinical workflows should ensure rigorous validation, maintain human oversight, and adhere to ethical considerations regarding patient safety and data privacy.

Expert scoring

A panel of three experienced fetal ultrasound sonographers, each with over ten years of clinical experience, evaluated the model-generated reports. The reports were distributed randomly among the sonographers. In order to increase the reliability of the scoring, all three sonographers re-evaluated the initial responses together and adjusted scores where needed. This ensured that there was no bias in scoring based on personal preference. They assessed the quality and accuracy of responses for each of the five key evaluation points using a Likert scale ranging from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent). This scoring system provided an ordinal measure of performance across models, prompts, images, and evaluation criteria.
