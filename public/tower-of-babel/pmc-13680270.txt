Mapping genetic modifiers of epimutation rates identifies VIM2/4 as dosage-sensitive negative regulators of CG methylation maintenance
Authors: Zhilin Zhang, Wilma Wanney, Yangyang Xu, Johan Zicola, Angela M Hancock, Robert J Schmitz, Frank Johannes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpontaneous epimutations are stochastic gains and losses of cytosine methylation that arise from imperfect maintenance across cell divisions. At CG sites, such epimutations can be inherited across generations in plants and constitute a major source of CG methylation (mCG) diversity. However, why the fidelity of mCG inheritance varies among genotypes, and how this variation relates to steady-state mCG levels, remains poorly understood. Here we tracked DNA methylation over 10 generations in ~400 mutation-accumulation lines derived from ~70 Arabidopsis thaliana Ler × Cvi recombinant inbred founders. By treating methylation gain and loss rates as quantitative molecular traits, we mapped a major-effect locus to a Cvi-derived deletion between VARIANT IN METHYLATION (VIM)2 and VIM4, two key components of the METHYLTRANSFERASE 1-dependent mCG maintenance pathway. Lines carrying this deletion showed elevated VIM2/4 (VIM2 and VIM4) expression, a rapid shift of genome-wide mCG towards a lower steady state and reduced fidelity of methylation inheritance across generations. Complementary overexpression and loss-of-function experiments identify VIM2/4 as dosage-sensitive negative regulators of mCG maintenance, in contrast to the canonical positive role of VIM-family proteins in mCG. Together, our results support a punctuated-equilibrium model of DNA methylome evolution, in which naturally segregating modifiers of mCG homeostasis can produce abrupt shifts in methylation state and alter the rate at which heritable epigenetic variation accumulates in plant genomes.Subject terms: Epigenomics, Plant evolution, Plant genetics

Main

DNA cytosine methylation is a central epigenetic modification in plants, where it contributes to transposable element (TE) silencing, chromatin organization and gene regulation1. Natural populations harbour extensive methylome variation2,3, and in some cases, this variation is associated with stable heritable phenotypic differences4–6. In plants, methylation occurs in three sequence contexts (CG, CHG and CHH, where H = A, T, C), which are maintained by partly distinct pathways1,7. Among these, CG methylation (mCG) is particularly well defined at the mechanistic level. During DNA replication, mCG is copied by METHYLTRANSFERASE 1 (MET1, AT5G49160, the plant homologue of mammalian DNMT1) together with VARIANT IN METHYLATION (VIM, homologues of mammalian UHRF1) proteins, which recognize hemimethylated CG sites and promote restoration of full methylation on the newly synthesized strand1,8–16. Thus, the MET1/VIM system lies at the molecular core of mCG inheritance.

Despite the fidelity of this maintenance machinery, mCG states are not transmitted perfectly across cell divisions and generations. Multigenerational studies show that stochastic gains and losses of mCG can arise without underlying DNA sequence change17–23. These events, termed ‘spontaneous epimutations’, are thought to reflect imperfect copying by the mCG maintenance system24. They occur at rates far higher than DNA sequence mutation19–22 and accumulate in a largely clock-like manner over evolutionary timescales17,25. Genome-wide analyses further show that epimutation rates vary strongly across genomic features17,18,22,26 and that these biases help explain standing patterns of mCG diversity among natural accessions22,23. Together, these findings suggest that natural mCG variation should be viewed not simply as a static molecular trait but as the outcome of an ongoing gain–loss process27,28.

This dynamic view is important because the steady-state level of mCG and the stability of that level are not the same property. The relative balance of methylation gain and loss determines the equilibrium mCG level observed in a given plant genome, whereas the overall rate of turnover determines how rapidly epigenetic drift accumulates around that equilibrium22,27. As a result, two genotypes can show similar methylation levels while differing markedly in maintenance fidelity or conversely differ in methylation level without large differences in ongoing turnover. Previous methylation quantitative trait locus (meQTL) mapping studies in natural populations have relied on cross-sectional methylome data and therefore captured methylation states at one timepoint2,3,29,30. Such data are powerful for mapping genetic loci that affect steady-state methylation differences among genotypes, but they cannot reveal whether a genetic variant primarily shifts the equilibrium, alters the fidelity of transgenerational copying or couples both effects.

Because MET1 and VIM proteins form the core mCG maintenance machinery, natural variation in mCG dynamics could, in principle, arise from altered activity, dosage or stoichiometric balance within this complex. However, changes in CG inheritance can also arise indirectly through perturbations outside the canonical MET1/VIM pathway. Previous work showed, for example, that mutants in CHROMOMETHYLASE 3 (CMT3, AT1G69770) gene, which encodes a DNA methyltransferase primarily responsible for maintaining CHG methylation31, can display increased accumulation of spontaneous CG epimutations despite CMT3 not being a canonical component of the MET1/VIM copying machinery18,32. Thus, a key challenge is to determine whether natural variation in mCG stability reflects direct perturbation of the core maintenance machinery or secondary effects from other epigenetic pathways.

Natural methylation outliers provide an attractive entry point to address this problem. Such genotypes may carry alleles that perturb mCG maintenance strongly enough to generate pronounced methylation phenotypes yet subtly enough to remain compatible with long-term viability in natural populations. We reasoned that the Arabidopsis thaliana accession Cvi-0 is particularly informative in this regard. Cvi-0 is a striking natural outlier with globally reduced mCG levels3, but the molecular basis of this phenotype, and its relationship to spontaneous epimutation dynamics, are unknown. By combining genetic mapping of steady-state methylation with direct estimates of transgenerational mCG gain and loss rates, this system provides an opportunity to genetically dissect whether natural variation in mCG arises primarily through direct effects on the core MET1/VIM maintenance complex or through indirect perturbations elsewhere in the epigenetic network. More generally, it allows us to determine whether the Cvi phenotype reflects a simple shift in methylation equilibrium or a broader change in maintenance fidelity.

To address this, we generated a large mutation accumulation mapping population derived from Ler × Cvi recombinant inbred lines (RILs) as founders to dissect the genetic and mechanistic basis of natural variation in mCG maintenance. Treating both steady-state mCG levels and spontaneous epimutation rates as quantitative molecular traits, we identify a major-effect locus on chromosome 1 that influences both properties. We show that the causal variant is a Cvi-specific structural deletion between VIM2 (AT1G66050) and VIM4 (AT1G66040) that elevates VIM2/4 (VIM2 and VIM4) expression. Unexpectedly, an increased VIM2/4 dosage does not enhance CG maintenance but instead lowers the mCG steady state and reduces the fidelity of methylation inheritance across generations. These findings identify the mechanistic basis of a natural mCG outlier, reveal previously unrecognized functions of VIM2/4 and show how naturally segregating variants can jointly reshape both the level and long-term stability of DNA methylation in plants.

Results

Steady-state mCG levels and epimutation rates are genotype dependent

To quantify natural variation in mCG, we re-analysed published DNA methylomes from 850 A. thaliana accessions3. Genome-wide mCG levels were ~2.5-fold more variable among accessions than non-mCG (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1), emphasizing the relevance of this cytosine context for natural variation. To assess genotype-specific differences in mCG stability across generations, we previously established mutation accumulation (MA) pedigrees for four selected accessions (Col-0, Kn-0, Mt-0 and Tsu-0) that span the natural range of mCG levels and propagated them for up to 16 selfings17 (Fig. 1a,b). Epimutation analysis revealed up to 1.4-fold differences in the combined mCG gain (α) and loss (β) rates, indicating that some genotypes are more error prone at maintaining mCG states transgenerationally (Fig. 1c,d). Predicted mCG levels based on these rates closely matched empirical observations in each accession (Fig. 1e), implying that all genotypes are at methylation equilibrium22,27. These findings demonstrate that steady-state mCG levels and epimutation rates are determined, in part, by genetic background.

Construction of MA-mapping line population

To identify genetic factors contributing to variation in mCG maintenance dynamics, we constructed a population of MA mapping lines (MA-ML) using Landsberg erecta (Ler-0) × Cape Verde Islands (Cvi-0) RILs as founder genotypes33. Each RIL was used to initiate eight independent MA lineages, propagated by single-seed descent for nine generations (Fig. 1f). The Ler × Cvi RILs were chosen because their parental accessions have highly contrasting steady-state mCG levels, with Cvi-0 scoring in the first percentile of the natural distribution and Ler-0 ranking substantially higher (Fig. 1a). We expected that this wide cross maximizes our ability to detect genetic variations underlying mCG differences. Moreover, the Ler × Cvi RILs have been extensively characterized in prior quantitative trait locus (QTL) studies of both classical and molecular phenotypes, including transcript, metabolite and protein abundance34,35, thus providing a rich framework for integrative analysis. To capture methylation dynamics across generations, we performed whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) on early- and late-generation individuals from 68 MA-ML pedigrees as well as from MA lines derived from the Ler-0 and Cvi-0 parental accessions, yielding 371 WGBS datasets in total (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Table 2). Using these data, we saturated the existing genetic map with additional single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) called from the WGBS reads (Methods). This resulted in a genetic map comprising 732 SNP markers, with an average spacing of 1.3 centimorgans (cM) and a maximum interval of 8.0 cM, substantially improving on the previously published map (Fig. 1g, Supplementary Fig. 1, and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4).

Mapping genetic mediators of mCG steady-state levels

Genome-wide DNA methylation profiling of MA-ML revealed a 1.4-fold variation in global mCG levels across genotypes, ranging from 14.7% to 21.3%. The parental accessions Ler-0 and Cvi-0 scored towards the extremes of this distribution (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 5), consistent with limited transgressive segregation36. Comparisons of mCG levels between G2 individuals and their G9 descendants for each genotype revealed no significant differences (Fig. 2b), which suggests that each lineage had reached mCG steady state already at the onset of MA-ML construction. This stability contrasts with the progressive methylation loss typically observed in many DNA methylation mutants37–39. On this basis, we pooled genome-wide mCG data from all samples within each pedigree. Treating the mCG steady-state level of each genotype as a quantitative molecular trait, we performed linkage-based meQTL mapping. This analysis identified a single major-effect meQTL on chromosome 1 that accounted for 44% of the variance in global mCG (logarithm of the odds (LOD), 8.62; CI, 23.7–25.8 Mb) (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). To explore whether additional annotation-specific effects were masked in the global analysis, we performed stratified mapping across distinct genomic features. The chromosome 1 meQTL exhibited its strongest effect on mCG levels within gene body-methylated (gbM) genes, explaining approximately 59% of the variance (LOD, 13.11; CI, 24.6–25.7 Mb), and also revealed two minor-effect loci on chromosomes 3 and 5. In addition, a distinct, strong-effect meQTL was detected at the distal end of chromosome 2, specifically influencing mCG levels in TEs, TE-like methylated (teM) genes and intergenic regions, accounting for 23%, 30% and 26% of the variance in these features, respectively (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6).

Given the frequent coregulation of mCG with non-mCG (mCHG and mCHH) in these later annotation contexts through shared epigenetic pathways1,40, we hypothesized that the chromosome 2 meQTL might also affect non-mCG. Supporting this, mapping of global mCHG and mCHH levels identified a major-effect locus that colocalized with the chromosome 2 mCG meQTL. The strongest association was observed for CHH methylation, particularly at non-CWA (CWA refers to CAA or CTA) contexts (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6), hinting at an involvement of the RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) pathway. Interestingly, FBX5 (AT2G44900) emerged as a significant cis-eQTL (where, eQTL is expression QTL) target located within the QTL CI in our analysis (Supplementary Table 7), which is consistent with its recent identification by Zicola et al. as a novel modifier of DNA methylation in natural Cvi accessions41. The target specificity of the two major meQTL was further supported by repeating the analysis using chromatin state (CS) annotations of the A. thaliana genome18,42. The chromosome 2 locus was specifically associated with four CSs enriched in intergenic and TE regions, while the chromosome 1 locus was predominantly associated with mCG levels in CS characteristic of gbM subregions18 (Extended Data Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 8).

The strong effects of the chromosome 1 and chromosome 2 meQTL on both global and feature-specific mCG levels suggested that they act primarily in trans to modulate methylation at distal targets. To test this directly, we conducted a genome-wide cis–trans analysis by segmenting the genome into 5-kb windows and treated regional mCG levels as quantitative traits. This revealed two prominent trans bands colocalizing with chromosomes 1 and 2 meQTL (Fig. 2e). As expected, the chromosome 1 trans band was enriched for gbM, whereas the chromosome 2 trans band was enriched for TEs, teM genes and intergenic regions (Fig. 2f), consistent with prior annotation-stratified results (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 1). We examined the directionality and haplotype specificity of these trans-acting effects in detail. We found that the Cvi allele at the chromosome 1 locus was associated with reduced steady-state mCG at distal targets to levels approximating those of the Cvi-0 parent but only within Ler-derived genomic segments (Fig. 2g,h and Supplementary Table 5). This suggests that the Cvi allele induces hypomethylation primarily at premethylated Ler-inherited loci. To confirm the transgenerational stability of genetic effects on mCG steady-state levels, we independently performed meQTL mapping in the G2 and G9 generations. Both analyses revealed the same genetic architecture underlying the hypomethylation phenotypes, indicating that these effects are established early and persist across generations (Fig. 2i and Extended Data Fig. 2a).

Together, these findings demonstrate that the meQTL elicits rapid and stable transitions to new methylation steady states in MA-ML genomes, primarily by acting in trans to reduce methylation on Ler-derived genomic segments.

A major QTL modulates the spontaneous CG epimutation rates

We asked whether the genetically induced shifts in steady-state mCG are accompanied by secondary changes in the transgenerational dynamics of CG maintenance; that is, whether perturbations that reset the equilibrium also increase maintenance flux and reduce transmission fidelity across generations. Prior work in cmt3 mutants showed that even modest perturbations of genome-wide methylation can destabilize epigenetic inheritance, increasing the accumulation of spontaneous epimutations over successive generations despite apparently robust steady-state levels18 (Methods). To test this in the MA-ML population, we estimated genotype-specific epimutation rates across all pedigrees using established tools (Methods). Rates varied widely among RILs, with the most hyperepimutable line reaching 14.5 × 10−4 and the most stable line 3.3 × 10−4 per site per haploid genome per generation, a 4.4-fold difference in epigenomic fidelity (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 9). These differences were mirrored in the rate of mCG divergence over generations, with hyperepimutable lines showing markedly faster epigenetic drift (Fig. 3b). These results align with findings from our MA lines in natural accessions (Fig. 1c,d) and confirm that the fidelity of mCG maintenance is inherently genotype dependent.

To dissect the genetic basis of this variability, we treated genotype-specific epimutation rates as molecular traits and performed QTL mapping. This analysis identified a single major-effect locus on chromosome 1 that influenced both gain and loss rates. The QTL explained 22% of the variance in global CG gain rates and 34% in loss rates, with particularly pronounced effects in gbM genes, where it accounted for 36% and 64% of the variance, respectively (Fig. 3c, Extended Data Fig. 2b and Supplementary Tables 9–11). This locus precisely colocalized with the previously mapped QTL for steady-state mCG levels. By contrast, the minor-effect QTL on chromosomes 3 and 5, as well as the major chromosome 2 locus associated with non-mCG (CHG/CHH) and TE/intergenic mCG, was not associated with variation in epimutation rates (Figs. 2c–f and 3c, Extended Data Figs. 1 and 2, and Supplementary Tables 6 and 8–11). We previously showed that CG sites within or proximal to regions targeted by RdDM are less prone to accumulate epimutations18,22, likely owing to periodic reinforcement during gametogenesis and early embryogenesis43. Thus, QTLs that co-segregate with mCG levels do not necessarily influence transgenerational maintenance fidelity, indicating that these are distinct molecular phenotypes with partly non-overlapping genetic architectures.

To explore the haplotype specificity of these QTL effects, we examined epimutation patterns in Ler- and Cvi-derived chromosomal segments in more detail. We found that the elevated loss rates associated with the Cvi allele at the chromosome 1 QTL were largely restricted to Ler-derived genomic regions (Fig. 3d,e and Supplementary Table 9), consistent with earlier findings that the Cvi allele drives hypomethylation of these segments (Fig. 2g,h and Supplementary Table 5). Interestingly, however, Ler segments also displayed a higher propensity for methylation gain in the presence of the Cvi allele (Fig. 3d,e and Supplementary Table 9), pointing to a more general destabilization of mCG maintenance in these segments across generations. We reasoned that increased epigenetic drift might also promote downstream functional divergence over time. To test this directly, we generated RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) data across multiple generations of MA-ML pedigrees with contrasting spontaneous CG epimutation rates (Extended Data Fig. 3a, Supplementary Tables 12 and 13, and Methods). Across gene expression and chromatin accessibility, neither the high-rate lines carrying the Cvi genotype at the chromosome 1 QTL nor the low-rate lines showed significant functional divergence over generations (RNA-seq: P = 1; F-test; ATAC-seq: P = 0.87) (Extended Data Fig. 3b,c and Methods). This conclusion was further supported by a more targeted promoter-level analysis, which revealed that variation in promoter methylation was not correlated with transcriptome variation across samples (Extended Data Fig. 3d–f). Thus, the enhanced methylation drift does not translate into measurable regulatory divergence in these data (Extended Data Fig. 3). This observation is in line with previous work showing that spontaneous CG epimutations accumulate in a selectively neutral manner17,19,22. It is also consistent with evidence that natural and experimental perturbations of gbM, the primary genomic target of the QTL, often have limited44 or no detectable45 functional consequences.

Taken together, our findings support a model in which the Cvi-derived chromosome 1 haplotype not only shifts mCG to a lower steady state but also renders this state intrinsically less stable across generations. Over the timescale assayed here, the cumulative gains and losses of mCG remain largely buffered from measurable changes in gene expression and chromatin accessibility at the genome-wide scale.

A chromosome 1 QTL harbours a large deletion associated with VIM2/4 overexpression

To identify the mechanistic basis of the chromosome 1 QTL, we searched for candidate genes by performing eQTL mapping using previously generated transcriptome data from the Ler × Cvi RIL population34,35. This analysis identified VIM2/4 (combined expression of VIM2 and VIM4) as a significant cis-eQTL target within the QTL CI (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Tables 14 and 15). The VIM gene family encodes proteins that recruit MET1 to hemimethylated DNA templates during replication, thereby promoting restoration of mCG on the newly synthesized strand1,8–10. Public expression atlases demonstrate that VIM2/4 are robustly expressed across diverse tissues and developmental stages46,47 (Supplementary Fig. 2), consistent with a systemic role in methylation maintenance.

VIM4 lies approximately 2.6-kb downstream of VIM2 (Fig. 4a) and, as a paralogue, shares strong sequence and structural similarity with VIM2, suggesting possible coregulation. Re-analysis of published long-read assemblies for Ler-0 and Cvi-048–50, together with the Col-0 reference, showed that the coding and protein sequences of VIM2 and VIM4 are >99% identical across all three accessions (Supplementary Tables 16 and 17). In Col-0, the two paralogues differ by only five amino acid residues and in Ler-0 by four residues. In Cvi-0, the VIM4 protein is identical to VIM2 except for a 59-amino-acid C-terminal truncation caused by a premature stop codon at position 563 (ref. 41) (Extended Data Fig. 4a and Supplementary Tables 16 and 18). As this stop codon lies only approximately nine nucleotides upstream of the final exon–exon junction, within the canonical escape window for nonsense-mediated decay (NMD), the transcript is likely to evade degradation. Moreover, domain mapping (Extended Data Fig. 4a) indicated that the truncation leaves all major functional domains intact, including the N-terminal RING, SRA and C-terminal RING domains. The truncated Cvi VIM4 protein is therefore predicted to retain mCG-binding activity through its SRA domain, suggesting that it remains functional.

A recent genome-wide association studies of natural Cvi accessions from the Cape Verde Islands likewise identified a strong association with mCG variation at this locus41. Our read-coverage analysis revealed a Cvi-specific deletion of approximately 2.7 kb between VIM2 and VIM4, which segregates both in the MA-ML population (Fig. 4a) and among natural Cvi accessions41. By leveraging published long-read assemblies of Cvi-0 from multiple independent sources48–50, we confirmed the presence of this deletion in the Cvi-0 accession (Extended Data Fig. 4b). A broader survey of published long-read assemblies across Arabidopsis accessions did not detect this deletion outside the Cvi lineage50–52, including in accessions phylogenetically or geographically close to Cvi-0 (Can-0, Elh-2 and Toufl-1)50,53, consistent with a relatively recent, Cvi-lineage-specific polymorphism.

The deleted interval spans three small TEs: AT1TE80750 (~100 base pairs (bp); ATREP15), AT1TE80755 (~1 kb; ATREP3) and AT1TE80760 (~20 bp; ATREP15). All three belong to the RC/Helitron family. Among them, AT1TE80755 is strongly CG methylated in the reference accessions Col-0 and Ler-0 (Fig. 4a,b), consistent with a repressive chromatin environment at this locus. RILs carrying the Cvi haplotype, which lacks these elements, showed significantly elevated VIM2/4 expression relative to lines carrying the intact Ler haplotype (Fig. 4a–d and Supplementary Table 14). Published chromatin data further showed strong Polycomb-associated H3K27me3 enrichment in the same deleted interval, proximal to or overlapping VIM2/454 (Fig. 4a). Re-analysis of H3K27me3-pathway mutants supports a functional contribution of this mark, as reduction of H3K27me3 in the region is accompanied by significantly elevated VIM2 and VIM4 expression55 (Fig. 4e). Together, these results point to a local repressive module within the deleted interval. Both the methylated Helitron AT1TE80755 and the associated H3K27me3 domain may contribute to repression of VIM2 and VIM4. The Cvi deletion therefore likely derepresses both genes by removing both features at once.

To validate this structural variant directly in the MA-ML population, we performed PCR genotyping in selected RILs and confirmed genotype-specific segregation of the deletion (Fig. 4f and Extended Data Fig. 5a). We then quantified the individual expression levels of VIM2 and VIM4 by quantitative PCR (qPCR) and observed consistent upregulation of both genes in deletion-containing lines (Fig. 4g, Extended Data Fig. 5b and Methods). Notably, VIM2/4 expression was highly predictive of both mCG levels and spontaneous epimutation rates in the genome as a whole and within gbM regions (Fig. 4c,d, Extended Data Fig. 5c,d, and Supplementary Tables 5, 9 and 14), indicating a strong functional impact on epigenomic stability. On average, the Cvi-specific deletion reduced global mCG levels by 1.7 percentage points (~9% relative reduction) and gbM mCG levels by 5.5 percentage points (~22% relative reduction) and was associated with an approximately 1.5-fold increase in genome-wide epimutation rates and an approximately 1.9-fold increase within gbM regions (Fig. 4c,d,h,i, Extended Data Fig. 5c,d, and Supplementary Tables 5, 9 and 14).

To test whether this effect extends beyond the Ler × Cvi RIL, we re-analysed published methylome data from natural Cvi accessions41 and an independent Col × Cvi RIL population56. The Cvi deletion was again associated with reduced global DNA methylation in these systems (Fig. 4j,k, Extended Data Fig. 5e–g, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 19), demonstrating that deletion-driven VIM2/4 overexpression produces hypomethylation regardless of the genetic background.

VIM2/4 act as dosage-sensitive negative regulators of mCG maintenance

As VIM proteins are canonical regulators of mCG maintenance that recognize hemimethylated DNA and recruit MET1 during replication, elevated VIM2/4 expression in the Cvi background would be expected to enhance maintenance fidelity and promote CG hypermethylation. However, our haplotype-specific QTL effects suggested the opposite. To test directly whether increased VIM2/4 expression instead causes global CG hypomethylation, we generated transgenic lines overexpressing VIM2, VIM4 or VIM24 (both genes together) in Col-0 (Supplementary Table 20 and Methods). To distinguish dosage-dependent from allele-specific effects, we generated constructs carrying either the Col-0 or Cvi-0 allele of VIM2/4.

Consistent with the RIL mapping results, overexpression of VIM2, VIM4 or VIM24 caused global CG hypomethylation, reducing global mCG levels from 29.1% in wild type to as low as 8.0% in the strongest hypomethylated overexpression line (~72% relative reduction), irrespective of whether the transgenes carried the Col-0 or Cvi-0 alleles (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). CHG and CHH methylation were comparatively less affected (Extended Data Fig. 6c,d). Consistent with our QTL mapping (Figs. 2c and 3c), elevated VIM2/4 dosage preferentially disrupted gbM regions compared with TEs (causing up to an 84% versus 54% relative reduction, respectively), confirming the preferential sensitivity of gbM loci to VIM2/4 perturbation (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). These results show that increased VIM2/4 expression, rather than allelic variation per se, is the primary cause of the observed methylation defects. They also indicate that VIM2 and VIM4 are functionally similar in their ability to perturb mCG maintenance and that the Cvi-0 VIM4 allele remains active despite the premature stop codon described above. This is in line with our structural prediction that the truncated Cvi VIM4 retains functionality (Extended Data Fig. 4a). Notably, however, global mCG levels did not decrease monotonically with VIM2/4 expression. Moderate overexpression reproducibly caused CG hypomethylation, whereas a subset of lines with extremely high VIM2/4 expression (>1,000-fold increased relative to wild type) retained near-wild-type or slightly elevated mCG levels (Fig. 5a,b). Thus, VIM2/4 expression levels appear to affect mCG in a strongly nonlinear manner, consistent with a dosage window in which increased VIM2/4 expression is the most disruptive to methylation maintenance (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 4).

If elevated VIM2/4 expression reduces mCG, then disrupted VIM2/4 activity should be expected to exert the opposite effect. To test this prediction, we re-analysed published WGBS data from vim1, vim2 and vim3 single mutants and the vim1/2/3 triple mutant7. We found that vim2 exhibits pronounced global CG hypermethylation, whereas vim1 and vim3 show only mild hypomethylation (Fig. 5c and Extended Data Fig. 6e–h). To validate this inverse dosage effect experimentally, we grew independent vim2 and vim4 T-DNA insertion mutants and generated WGBS data from multiple biological replicates (Supplementary Table 20 and Methods). These new data confirmed that mutations in either VIM2 or VIM4 cause statistically significant global CG hypermethylation (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 6i,j), in sharp contrast to the hypomethylation associated with vim1 and vim3 (Fig. 5c and Extended Data Fig. 6e,f). Non-mCG levels were largely unaffected in vim2 or vim4 (Extended Data Fig. 6k,l), mirroring the overexpression lines and indicating that VIM2/4 expression primarily affects mCG maintenance (Fig. 5a–e and Extended Data Figs. 6 and 7).

Together with the overexpression results, these findings establish a novel role of VIM2/4 as dosage-dependent negative regulators of mCG maintenance (Fig. 5a–e and Extended Data Figs. 6 and 7). These findings challenge the prevailing view that VIM1 (AT1G57820), VIM2 and VIM3 (AT5G39550) are largely redundant7,9,57–59. Instead, there may be a functional division within the Arabidopsis VIM family, with VIM2/4 and VIM1/3 (VIM1 and VIM3) forming two distinct subgroups that exert opposing effects on mCG.

Dysregulation of VIM2/4 perturbs DNA methylation pathways

To assess whether VIM2/4 dosage affects the broader epigenetic regulatory network beyond CG maintenance, we examined transcriptional relationships among core methylation-pathway components. In both the MA-ML population and natural accessions (1001 transcriptome data3), VIM2 and VIM4 were strongly co-expressed with each other and with central components of the DNA methylation machinery, including MET1, VIM1, VIM3, CMT3 and the DNA demethylase ROS1 (AT2G36490) (Fig. 5f, Extended Data Fig. 8a and Supplementary Table 14). This transcriptional association places VIM2/4 within the central DNA methylation network, consistent with protein functional association analysis from the STRING database60 (Supplementary Fig. 5). This network relationship was disrupted in MA-ML lines carrying the Cvi-derived VIM2/4 haplotype. Despite elevated VIM2/4 expression, these lines exhibited reduced transcriptional connectivity with other methylation-related genes and ROS1 downregulation (Fig. 5f,g). The downregulation of these components was also evident in independent transcriptome comparisons between Cvi-0 and Col-056 (Fig. 5h), and ROS1 downregulation was further confirmed in new RNA-seq data from RILs with contrasting epimutation rates (Extended Data Fig. 8b). Thus, the Cvi VIM2/4 haplotype is associated not only with altered mCG maintenance but also with transcriptional rewiring of the broader methylation network (Fig. 5f–h and Extended Data Fig. 8a–c).

To test whether this rewiring is driven by VIM2/4 dosage itself, we generated RNA-seq data from VIM2/4 overexpression and loss-of-function lines (Supplementary Table 21). The hypomethylated VIM2/4 overexpression lines recapitulated key features of the Cvi deletion background, including frequent downregulation of VIM1, VIM3 and ROS1 (Fig. 5a,g–n, Extended Data Fig. 8d–h, and Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). The resulting hypomethylation could not be explained by transgene-induced post-transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS) of VIM1/3 because some overexpression lines maintained or even increased VIM1/3 expression while still showing mCG maintenance defects and there was no PTGS evidence for increased CG and non-mCG in these genes (Fig. 5a,k,l, Extended Data Fig. 8d,e,h, and Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Conversely, vim2/4 mutants showed reciprocal upregulation of these same components (Fig. 5k–n, Extended Data Fig. 8i,j and Supplementary Figs. 6–8). Analogous feedback was not observed in vim1 (Fig. 5i–n and Extended Data Fig. 8k–m), indicating that VIM2/4 have a distinct regulatory role within the maintenance network. Together, these results show that altered VIM2/4 dosage is sufficient to perturb the stoichiometric balance of DNA methylation components and to trigger secondary transcriptional changes in interacting pathways.

Among the affected genes, ROS1 provided the clearest mechanistic link between methylation perturbation and transcriptional response. ROS1 was consistently downregulated in globally hypomethylated backgrounds, including high-epimutation-rate RILs, Cvi-0, VIM2/4 overexpression lines, vim1/2/3 and met1, whereas it was upregulated in hypermethylated vim2/4 mutants10,57 (Fig. 5n). This bidirectional response is consistent with the known methylation-sensitive ROS1 promoter rheostat, formed by the adjacent Helitron element and DNA methylation monitoring sequence (MEMS)61,62. Concordantly, reduced ROS1 expression in globally hypomethylated backgrounds was accompanied by hypomethylation across the MEMS–Helitron sequence, while vim2/4 showed the reciprocal methylation state at the same region (Extended Data Fig. 9a,b).

As ROS1 encodes a DNA demethylase, its downregulation should increase methylation at ROS1-sensitive loci, whereas RILs carrying the Cvi deletion haplotype show predominant genome-wide CG hypomethylation, most pronounced across gbM genes. To resolve this apparent contradiction, we examined canonical ROS1 target regions defined by differentially methylated region (DMR) analysis of ros1 mutants relative to wild type63 (Extended Data Fig. 9c and Methods). As expected under reduced ROS1 activity, these regions showed increased relative CHG methylation in RILs carrying the chromosome 1 Cvi haplotype. However, relative mCG at the same loci was lower in Cvi-haplotype than in Ler-haplotype RILs (Extended Data Fig. 9c). Moreover, DMR annotation analysis showed that VIM2/4 overexpression and mutant DMRs are predominantly enriched in gbM regions, whereas ROS1-target DMRs represent a distinct class of loci that lie largely outside these gbM regions (Extended Data Fig. 9d,e). Thus, ROS1 repression is a detectable downstream consequence of VIM2/4 dysregulation, with methylation effects at specific ROS1-sensitive regions (Extended Data Fig. 9f) but it does not explain nor contradict the genome-wide CG maintenance defect.

Together, these findings identify the Cvi VIM2/4 haplotype as a naturally segregating variant that perturbs DNA methylation-network balance, providing a framework for understanding how genetic modifiers can reshape both the steady state and the long-term dynamics of epigenome inheritance.

Discussion

Genetic mapping studies of DNA methylation usually treat steady-state methylation differences as static end points. Here, we show that natural genetic variants can also modify the fidelity with which methylation states are transmitted across generations, thereby altering how DNA methylomes evolve over time in a fixed genetic background. Earlier efforts to identify genetic modifiers of spontaneous methylation variation relied on somatic deviations from expected methylation patterns in an F2 population64 and could therefore not observe transgenerational epimutation dynamics directly. By contrast, the MA-ML experimental design allowed us to link genetically induced perturbations in global mCG levels to increased CG epimutation rates and accelerated epigenetic drift over multiple generations. This coupling supports a punctuated-equilibrium-like view of mCG evolution, in which genetic perturbations of the maintenance machinery can produce abrupt shifts in methylation equilibrium while also changing the rate at which methylation states fluctuate over time. It further suggests that CG maintenance fidelity may decline when the methylation system is displaced from its normal homeostatic range, as also observed in DNA methylation-pathway mutants18.

Our mapping identified a Cvi-derived deletion at a major meQTL locus flanking VIM2 and VIM4, which was associated with elevated expression of both genes (Extended Data Fig. 10a). Overexpression and loss-of-function experiments confirmed that this dosage change is functionally relevant. Increased VIM2/4 dosage lowered the mCG steady state, whereas reduced VIM2/4 activity caused reciprocal CG hypermethylation (Extended Data Fig. 10b). A recent study by Shin et al.65 attributed Cvi-associated CG hypomethylation to an RNAi-based mechanism initiated by the Cvi-specific VIM4–VIM2 structural configuration. In their model, the shortened intergenic region between the oppositely oriented VIM4 and VIM2 genes permits extended or read-through transcription across highly homologous sequences, generating stem-loop or double-stranded RNA substrates that are processed into VIM-derived siRNAs. On the basis of these data in seed tissue, they proposed that these siRNAs silence VIM1 in trans, thereby reducing VIM1/3–MET1-dependent CG maintenance and causing genome-wide hypomethylation.

Schematic summary of the genetic and dosage effects of the VIM2/4 on DNA methylation maintenance.

a, Schematic overview of the genetic mechanism identified through MA-ML, Ler × Cvi RILs, Col × Cvi RILs, and natural Cvi accessions. In the Ler-0 and Col-0, a repressive cis-regulatory module comprising a methylated Helitron transposable element and H3K27me3 chromatin signatures maintains basal VIM2/4 expression, ensuring faithful CG methylation maintenance (stable steady state and basal epimutation rate). The Cvi-0-derived deletion removes this repressive module, causing a loss of cis-repression and elevating VIM2/4 expression. This VIM2/4 upregulation drives a rapid shift toward a lower global mCG steady state, increases global epimutation rate, and perturbs DNA methylation pathways. b, Summary diagram of VIM transgenic and mutant experiments establishing the functional division within the VIM family. Under wild-type conditions, balanced stoichiometry between VIM2/4 and VIM1/3 maintains normal global CG methylation levels. VIM2/4 overexpression shifts the balance toward a higher relative VIM2/4 dosage, leading to reduced global mCG levels. Conversely, vim2 and vim4 single mutants lower VIM2/4 dosage and cause reciprocal CG hypermethylation. These reciprocal effects establish VIM2/4 as dosage-sensitive negative regulators of mCG maintenance. In contrast, loss of VIM1 and VIM3 (vim1 and vim3 single mutants) reduces global mCG levels, confirming their canonical roles as positive regulators. Arrows and equal signs indicate the direction of change relative to the wild type. The symbols =, >, and < indicate a balanced, higher, or lower relative stoichiometric dosage of VIM2/4 compared to VIM1/3, respectively.

Several observations argue that this RNAi-based model is not sufficient to explain the methylation phenotype observed here. First, VIM2/4 overexpression was sufficient to induce hypomethylation in both Cvi and Col-0 backgrounds (Figs. 4 and 5 and Extended Data Figs. 5–8), indicating that the Cvi-specific inverted-repeat configuration is not required for the phenotype. Second, across multiple datasets carrying the Cvi deletion, we did not observe consistent downregulation of VIM1 or VIM3 in leaf, nor evidence of PTGS at the methylation level of these genes, arguing against reduced VIM1/3 expression as the primary cause of the methylation defect in this tissue (Fig. 5, Extended Data Fig. 8, and Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Although leaf methylation is not itself the substrate of transgenerational inheritance, leaf VIM2/4 expression levels predicted both the inherited hypomethylated state and the associated epimutation instability across generations, indicating that the leaf phenotype reflects a dosage state relevant to inherited methylome dynamics rather than an isolated terminal-tissue effect. Finally, the non-monotonic dosage response observed in some overexpression lines, where extremely high VIM2/4 expression retains near-wild-type mCG levels, is difficult to reconcile with a simple RNAi-mediated loss-of-function model (Fig. 5a,b and Supplementary Fig. 4).

Together, our observations identify VIM2/4 as dosage-sensitive negative regulators of mCG maintenance, functionally distinct from the canonical positive roles of VIM1/3 (Extended Data Fig. 10b). These findings challenge the prevailing view that VIM1, VIM2 and VIM3 are largely redundant7,9,57–59. Although the underlying biochemical mechanism remains unresolved, one possible explanation is that VIM2/4 compete with VIM1/3 for hemimethylated CG templates or MET1 binding but differ in their ability to recruit, stabilize or functionally couple with MET1-dependent methylation copying (Fig. 6). In this genetic dosage model, moderate increases in VIM2/4 dosage would shift maintenance-complex stoichiometry away from a more faithful VIM1/3-dominated configuration, thereby lowering mCG. Conversely, reduced VIM2/4 activity would relieve this competition and increase maintenance efficiency. The observation that some lines with extremely high VIM2/4 expression (>1,000-fold higher than wild type) retained near-wild-type mCG levels is also consistent with this framework because the absolute number of VIM2/4-bound hemimethylated templates or MET1-recruitment events may become sufficient to compensate for lower per-complex efficiency (Fig. 5a,b). This model is also consistent with the previous observation that the vim1/2/3 triple mutant exhibits more severe global hypomethylation than the vim1/3 double mutant9. Future work should determine whether VIM2/4 directly compete with VIM1/3 for hemimethylated templates, differ in their interaction with MET1, associate with one another through intrafamily dimerization or with chromatin, undergo concentration-dependent condensate formation or alter the stability and turnover of maintenance complexes.

Working genetic model: dosage-sensitive competition among VIM paralogues modulates MET1-dependent CG maintenance.

Schematic illustrating how the stoichiometric balance between VIM protein subgroups is proposed to modulate the efficiency of MET1-dependent mCG copying at hemimethylated CG sites after DNA replication. VIM2/4 (green) are modelled as attenuated cofactors of MET1-dependent CG maintenance that compete with VIM1/3 (pink), the more efficient cofactors, for shared components of the maintenance machinery, whether hemimethylated CG substrates, MET1 binding or both. a, In wild-type plants, a balanced stoichiometry between VIM1/3 and VIM2/4 ensures efficient MET1 recruitment to hemimethylated DNA, resulting in faithful mCG maintenance and stable epigenetic inheritance. b, Elevated VIM2/4 dosage shifts this balance. Moderate overexpression creates a VIM2/4-biased stoichiometry that, through competition, leads to reduced CG maintenance, elevated epimutation rates and global CG hypomethylation (left). Conversely, extremely VIM2/4-biased stoichiometry (>1,000-fold relative to wild type) can overcome this inefficiency through a ‘mass-action’ effect when the system is flooded with VIM2/4, driving retention of near-wild-type or even slightly hypermethylated CG levels (right), consistent with the nonlinear dosage relationship observed in overexpression lines (Fig. 5b). c, In vim2/4 mutants, the relief of competitive binding results in a VIM1/3-dominant stoichiometry, which causes CG hypermethylation (Fig. 5c,d). d, In vim1/3 mutants, the attenuated VIM2/4 proteins dominate the maintenance complex, causing CG hypomethylation, analogous to moderate VIM2/4 overexpression (Fig. 5a,c). This schematic represents a genetic dosage model. The biochemical properties of the VIM proteins, including their associations with one another, with MET1, and with chromatin, probably contribute to MET1 recruitment and the observed mCG phenotypes through mechanisms that remain to be explored.

This dosage-sensitive logic may extend beyond plants. Mammalian UHRF1, the homologue of plant VIM proteins9,66–68 (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 22), recruits DNMT1, the mammalian homologue of MET1, to hemimethylated CG sites during DNA methylation maintenance1,11–16. UHRF1 is frequently overexpressed in human cancers69–71, where elevated expression has been linked to disruption of DNMT1-dependent maintenance, widespread DNA hypomethylation and tumorigenesis72–74. Although plant VIM proteins and mammalian UHRF1 are not mechanistically equivalent, these parallels suggest that the dosage of maintenance-associated recognition factors must be tightly regulated to preserve epigenomic stability.

Our study also shows that VIM2/4 perturbation is not confined to CG copying itself but propagates into the broader methylation network, most notably to the DNA demethylase ROS1 (Fig. 5f–n, Extended Data Figs. 8 and 9, and Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). The association between the Cvi deletion, elevated VIM2/4 expression and reduced ROS1 expression points to substantial crosstalk between maintenance and demethylation pathways. However, ROS1 repression neither explains nor contradicts the predominant methylation phenotype of the Cvi haplotype. Reduced ROS1 activity is expected to promote methylation gain at ROS1-sensitive loci, whereas the major QTL effect is genome-wide CG hypomethylation, the strongest across gbM genes. Consistently, canonical ROS1 target regions showed signatures of reduced demethylase activity but represented a distinct class of loci from those driving broad gbM-associated hypomethylation (Extended Data Fig. 9). ROS1 is therefore best viewed as a downstream component of VIM2/4 perturbation rather than as the primary cause of the CG maintenance defect.

Whether environmental perturbations engage the same VIM2/4–ROS1 axis remains an important question. Biotic and abiotic stress can affect DNA methylation pathways but it is unclear whether such responses intersect with VIM2/4-dependent CG maintenance, ROS1-mediated demethylation or both63. Our recent observations that loss of ROS1 modifies the effect of multigenerational biotic stress on DNA methylation maintenance fidelity are consistent with such crosstalk. However, these effects occur mainly outside gbM genes, whereas VIM2/4-dependent epimutation-rate changes primarily target gbM-associated mCG. This distinction suggests that genetic and environmental perturbations may converge on shared methylation-network components while affecting different genomic compartments.

Although the Cvi-derived VIM2/4 haplotype accelerates mCG drift, this increased epigenetic divergence did not translate into detectable divergence in chromatin accessibility or gene expression over the timescale examined here (Extended Data Fig. 3). This is consistent with the view that many spontaneous CG epimutations, particularly in gbM regions, behave largely neutrally or have weak immediate regulatory effects24. At the same time, this global neutrality does not imply that all methylation changes are functionally buffered. The ROS1 locus illustrates how specific regulatory nodes can remain highly sensitive to methylation perturbation through local feedback mechanisms, including the MEMS–Helitron rheostat. Thus, cumulative CG epimutational drift may be largely neutral at the genome-wide level while still engaging local epigenetic feedback loops at responsive loci. Over longer timescales, increased epimutational turnover may therefore influence the standing epigenetic variation on which drift, selection or genetic interactions can act75, although evidence for this remains controversial76.

Together, our findings establish spontaneous CG epimutation rate as a heritable quantitative trait and reposition VIM2/4 from presumed redundant members of the VIM family to dosage-sensitive negative modulators of both the equilibrium and fidelity of mCG maintenance. This genetic–epigenetic interplay provides a mechanistic basis for a punctuated-equilibrium-like model of methylome evolution, in which naturally segregating modifiers of mCG homeostasis can produce abrupt shifts in methylation state and alter the rate at which heritable epigenetic variation accumulates in plant genomes.

Methods

Construction and omics profiling of the MA-ML

To be able to identify specific genetic loci contributing to intraspecific variation in epimutation rates, we generated selfing-derived MA-ML in the model plant A. thaliana (Fig. 1f). The MA-ML comprise 68 genetically distinct MA pedigrees (with eight lineages each), which are derived from 68 different founders RILs. These RILs were initially obtained from a cross between Landsberg erecta (Ler-0) and Cape Verde Islands (Cvi-0) accessions.

The MA-ML were grown in climate-controlled chambers for ten selfing generations (long-day conditions, 16-h day lengths, 18 °C). Seeds from each generation were stored. To obtain plant material for WGBS, we grew plants from G2 and G9 seeds in a common growth chamber, following the sampling scheme shown in Fig. 1f. Samples were collected from young above-ground rosette leaf tissue. Leaf tissue was flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and DNA was extracted using a Qiagen Plant DNeasy kit (Qiagen) on the basis of the manufacturer’s instructions. MethylC-seq libraries were prepared on the basis of the protocol described by Urich et al.77. Libraries were sequenced at Genewiz on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) in a paired-end 150-bp format.

ATAC-seq was performed across generations in selected pedigrees with contrasting epimutation rates. ATAC-seq samples were collected from the immediate progeny of the sibling plants used for WGBS (Extended Data Fig. 3a). The lines were grown under long-day conditions (16 h light/8 h dark) on 1/2 MS plates at 25 °C for 7–9 days. For the nuclei extraction, approximately 200 mg bulk seedlings were collected. The samples were chopped down in 1 ml prechilled lysis buffer (10 mM MES-KOH pH 5.4, 10 mM NaOH, 250 mM sucrose, 0.1 mM spermine, 0.5 mM spermidine, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 1% BSA, 0.5% TritonX-100) and filtered through a 40-µm and a 20-µm strainer, with a centrifugation step in between (500g, 5 min). The nuclei were finally pelleted with a gradient solution (35% Percoll in lysis buffer) in a swing centrifuge at 500g for 10 min and resuspended in tagmentation buffer (10 mM TAPS-NaOH pH 8.0, 5 mM MgCl2). Nuclei quality and concentration were checked with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining at a cell counter. 50k–100k purified nuclei were incubated with 3 µl Tn5 enzyme in 40 µl tagmentation buffer (described above) at 37 °C for 30 min. The products were purified using a PCR-extraction kit (NEB Monarch PCR & DNA Clean-up, #T1030) and then amplified for 10–12 cycles using Q5 polymerase (NEBNext HiFi 2x PCR Mastermix, #M0541). PCR conditions were followed as described previously78. Amplified libraries were purified with AMPure beads, and concentrations were determined using Qubit and qPCR (Roche KAPA Library Quantification Kit) before sequencing. Paired-end 150-bp sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

RNA-seq was performed on siblings of the plants used for ATAC-seq following the same growth conditions. Total RNA was extracted from 7- to 9-day-old bulk seedlings following the TRIzol extraction method. Libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Kit (Number 20020594, Illumina) with a custom dual index workflow of iTruSeq primer set and iTruSeq adapter79. Paired-end 150-bp sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Construction and omics profiling of VIM2/4 overexpression lines

For VIM2/4 overexpression lines in the Col-0 background, five plasmids were constructed (VIM2 and VIM4 carrying either the Col-0 or Cvi-0 allele and a combined VIM2 and VIM4 construct carrying the Col-0 allele) using Golden Gate cloning80. The coding sequences of VIM genes were synthesized by GeneArt (Thermo Fisher), using the TAIR10 sequence for Col-0 and the proposed sequence from the long-assembly data for Cvi-0 (see the ‘Published datasets’ section). To induce gene overexpression, the CaMV 35S promoter and NOS terminator were used but no tags were added. Final constructs were confirmed by ONT-Whole Plasmid sequencing (Eurofins Genomics) before being transformed into A. thaliana Col-0 using the floral dip method and the Agrobacterium strain GV3101 (pSoup-P19)(CC-125-5×50, Goldbio). Detailed cloning strategy and construct information is presented in Supplementary Table 23. Transgenic lines were selected in the presence of 50 µg ml−1 kanamycin. T1 plants were tested for overexpression by qPCR and used for further analyses. The qPCR followed the same protocol used for the MA-ML, with a different primer pair specific to VIM2, VIM3 and VIM4 in Col and Cvi, yielding a single 206-bp amplicon with an efficiency of 92%. Detailed primer information is presented in Supplementary Table 24.

For WGBS, genomic DNA was extracted from mature rosette leaves of qPCR-confirmed T1 overexpression plants once sufficient leaf tissue was available. For each library, 200 ng genomic DNA was sheared into 350-bp fragments using a Covaris R230 ultrasonicator. Libraries were generated following the protocol of NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep kit (E765S, NEB) in combination with NEBNext EM-seq Adaptor (E7165AVIAL, NEB) and NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Enzymatic Methyl-seq (E7140L, NEB). Bisulfite conversion was performed with the EZ-96 Methylation-Gold MagPrep kit (D5042, Zymo Research) before the PCR enrichment step, where the NEB Q5 polymerase was substituted by KAPA HiFi HotStart Uracil+ReadyMix (KK2801, Roche) to enable the amplification of uracil-containing DNA. Size selection and cleanup were performed with magnetic beads (AMPure XPbeads, A63881, Beckman Colter), and the quality of the final libraries was assessed with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Q32851, Invitrogen). Sequencing was performed by Novogene using an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (PE150) platform. Furthermore, SNPs extracted from the WGBS data were utilized to verify the uniform Col-0 genetic background of all VIM2/4 overexpression lines, as well as the correct identity of the introduced Col or Cvi allele of the VIM2/4 transgene in each line.

For RNA-seq, total RNA was extracted from mature rosette leaves of qPCR-confirmed T1 overexpression plants once sufficient leaf tissue was available, using the Monarch Total RNA Miniprep Kit (N2010, NEB). RNA concentration was quantified using the Qubit RNA BR Assay Kit (Q33223, Thermo Fisher), and samples were sent to Novogene for library preparation and paired-end 150-bp sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform.

T-DNA insertion vim2 and vim4 single-mutant lines

Homozygous T-DNA insertion mutant lines for vim2 (stock number SALK_141723C; NASC ID: N664186; T-DNA insertion in VIM2, AT1G66050) and vim4 (stock number SALK_073322C; NASC ID: N675753; T-DNA insertion in VIM4, AT1G66040) were obtained from the Nottingham Arabidopsis Stock Center (NASC; https://arabidopsis.info/). Both lines are in the Col-0 background and were confirmed as homozygous by the SALK Institute before distribution. The T-DNA insertion lines were independently verified by PCR-based genotyping using OneTaq DNA Polymerase (#M0480, NEB) as previously described81 and were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Detailed primer information is presented in Supplementary Table 24. Three biological replicates for each line were used for WGBS and RNA-seq, following the same procedures as for VIM2/4 overexpression lines.

Published datasets

This study integrates newly generated data with previously published datasets spanning WGBS, RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) and long-read genome assemblies from multiple Arabidopsis genetic backgrounds, mutant systems and natural accessions. See the ‘Data availability’ section and Supplementary Table 25 for accession numbers.

Raw WGBS data for 927 samples were downloaded3. SRA files were converted to FASTQ format using fastq-dump (v2.9.2). For samples with multiple FASTQ files associated with the same GSM accession, individual BAM files were first generated and then merged using ‘samtools merge -n’ (Samtools v1.11). Only samples derived from leaf tissue were retained. To account for redundant accessions, we selected the sample (GSM ID) with the highest sequencing depth when multiple samples corresponded to the same ecotype ID. This filtering resulted in a final set of 850 unique accessions (Supplementary Table 1).

We previously generated MA lines using four different A. thaliana natural ecotypes as founders17. Further details concerning the construction of these pedigrees and DNA extraction protocols are provided in the original publication17.

To assess the functional contribution of H3K27me3 to VIM2/4 regulation, published RNA-seq data for H3K27me3-deficient clf swn double mutants and their corresponding wild-type controls were obtained from Mozgová et al.55.

Published long-read assemblies for Ler-0 and Cvi-0 were obtained from Wlodzimierz et al.49 and Baduel et al.48, and an additional Cvi-0 assembly was obtained from Lian et al.50, together with the Col-0 reference genome (TAIR10)82.

SNP data

Polymorphisms between Ler-0 and Cvi-0 were obtained from the 1001 Arabidopsis Genomes project53 and used to cross-reference SNPs identified from WGBS for genetic map construction (see the ‘Identification of SNP markers from WGBS for genetic mapping’ section).

ros1 mutant DMR data

Canonical ROS1 target regions were defined on the basis of DMR analysis of ros1 mutants relative to wild type from Zhang et al.63.

WGBS data processing

To ensure consistent processing across multiple Arabidopsis WGBS datasets, including our newly generated MA-ML, VIM2/4 overexpression lines and mutants, as well as previously published datasets such as natural accessions and MA accessions, we used the MethylStar pipeline83,84 with TAIR1082 as the reference genome. MethylStar is a fast and robust preprocessing pipeline for bulk WGBS data84. Cytosine-level methylation states were inferred using METHimpute83, which classifies each site into one of three states: unmethylated (‘U’), intermediate (‘I’) or methylated (‘M’), on the basis of a three-state hidden Markov model. It is capable of making accurate methylation calls even for cytosines with missing or low read counts. Another advantage of METHimpute is its ability to identify not only epihomozygous unmethylated (UU) or epihomozygous methylated sites (MM) but also epiheterozygous sites (MU). Knowledge of epiheterozygous sites is necessary when studying epimutation accumulation in clonal species/lineages, as most epimutations are of the form UU ↔ MU or MM ↔ MU. To ensure high-quality methylation state calls, only cytosines with a maximum posterior probability larger than 0.99 were retained. Sequencing metrics for each MA-ML sample, including sequencing depth, mapping efficiency and coverage, are presented in Supplementary Table 2. The same metrics for the VIM2/4 overexpression lines and mutants are presented in Supplementary Table 20.

Enrichment of annotations

Gene and TE annotation files in GFF3 format were downloaded from Ensembl Plants85. The gbM, teM and LM gene sets were obtained from previous work86,87. In this study, genes previously labelled as unmethylated (UM) were referred to as lowly methylated (LM), following Zhang et al.63. The ‘other_gene’ category was defined by subtracting gbM, teM and LM genes from the total gene set. Intergenic regions were defined as genomic intervals not overlapping with genes, TEs or promoter regions, using bedtools complement18,88. The ‘others’ category includes genomic regions not assigned to any of the above annotations. The genome coordinates and corresponding epigenetic marks for the 36 CSs were obtained from the PCSD database42. Clustering of these CSs into four major groups was performed according to Hazarika et al.18, corresponding to green, red, blue and purple states.

Calculation of average methylation levels

Average observed methylation levels were computed using methylome data, considering only sites with posteriorMax ≥0.99 to ensure high-confidence calls. The proportions of unmethylated (U) and intermediate (I) states were calculated relative to the total number of high-confidence sites (n_total). The methylation level was then defined as 1 − nU/n_total − 0.5 × nI/n_total, where nU and nI represent the number of sites classified as unmethylated and intermediate states, respectively. Predicted methylation levels were obtained by applying the mathematical model formulated by van der Graaf et al.22, which relates steady-state methylation to the gain and loss rates.

Metaplot visualization of DNA methylation profiles

DNA methylation profiles across genes and TEs were visualized using scaled metaplots. Each feature body was divided into 20 equally proportioned windows, and 1,000-bp upstream and downstream flanking regions were each divided into 10 windows of 100 bp. For each window, the mean recalibrated methylation level (derived from METHimpute posterior probabilities, posteriorMax ≥0.99) was averaged across all features within the same annotation group. Plots were generated separately for CG, CHG and CHH contexts using the R package ggplot2.

Estimation of epimutation rates

To obtain accurate estimates of epimutation rates in the MA-ML and MA-accessions pedigrees, global and specific annotated estimates of the CG epimutation gain rate (α) and the loss rate (β) at the level of individual cytosines were acquired using the R package AlphaBeta (version 1.10.0)19. AlphaBeta is a generalization of our previous modelling-based approach for estimating epimutation rates in self-derived MA lines22. AlphaBeta takes pedigree-based multigenerational WGBS data as input and output estimates of the unknown gain (α) and loss rates (β), along with their CIs. To quantify mCG divergence between samples, we applied the divergence estimation function implemented in the AlphaBeta package, which leverages pedigree structure to model changes in methylation states. The underlying methodology and mathematical framework are detailed in the original publication19. For all analyses, we fitted the neutral model (‘ABneutral’). AlphaBeta estimates gain and loss rates from the observed state transitions while accounting for measurement error and finite-generation dynamics. A key advantage of this framework is that the inferred rates can be used to predict steady-state mCG levels, providing an internal consistency check that simple transition counts do not provide.

For comparison, we also applied a simpler count-based method to estimate epimutation rates on MA-ML. Our pipeline categorizes methylation states into methylated (M) and unmethylated (U), excluding intermediate (I) states. To quantify epimutation rates, we tracked methylation state transitions between early and late generations (that is, G2 and G9) within the main lineage. Methylation gain was assessed by calculating the ratio of U to M transitions, defined as the number of sites that changed from U to M relative to the total number of U sites in the early generation. Similarly, methylation loss was quantified by the ratio of M to U transitions, calculated as the number of sites transitioning from M to U relative to the total M sites in the early generation. Although the count-based approach successfully maps the major-effect QTL for methylation to the same locus on chromosome 1 as AlphaBeta, we show the critical advantages of the AlphaBeta computational framework in robustly predicting steady-state methylation levels and capturing epimutation rates in Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11.

Identification of SNP markers from WGBS for genetic mapping

To construct a genetic map for Ler × Cvi RILs, we identified polymorphic SNPs from WGBS data of MA-ML. BAM files were first extracted from the bismark-deduplicate output generated by Methylstar83,84, followed by sorting and indexing with samtools (v1.17). Genome-wide SNPs were identified using bcftools (v1.17) with default settings, using TAIR10 as the reference genome while excluding indels. To retain informative parental polymorphisms, we cross-referenced SNPs identified from WGBS with polymorphisms between Ler-0 and Cvi-0 obtained from the 1001 Arabidopsis Genomes project53. Only SNPs that overlapped between these datasets were retained.

We subsequently retained homozygous biallelic SNPs, required consistent genotypes across all samples from the same RIL and restricted SNP occurrence frequencies to 30–70% across RILs to exclude rare or nearly fixed variants. To avoid ambiguity caused by bisulfite-induced C-to-T conversion, only A/G SNPs were retained.

To reduce marker redundancy and noise, SNPs were summarized using sliding windows of 200 SNPs with a 5-SNP step. Windows containing >50% Ler SNPs were classified as Ler, those containing <50% Ler SNPs as Cvi and windows with equal Ler and Cvi proportions as missing data. This yielded 12,479 window-based markers.

Further refinement was performed using the qtl R package (v1.70)89, where redundant markers were removed with the ‘findDupMarkers’ function, and markers with Ldiff >2 were excluded using the ‘droponemarker’ function to improve map quality. This resulted in a final set of 732 SNP markers (Supplementary Table 4), which were subsequently used to construct a high-density genetic map.

QTL mapping approach

Using DNA methylation levels as molecular quantitative traits, we performed meQTL. Treating the genotype-specific estimates for the gain (α) and loss rate (β) as molecular quantitative traits, we performed a search for QTL (that is, αβ-QTL) underlying rate variation using classical linkage analysis. QTL scans were performed for genome-wide as well as annotation-specific epimutation rates. We re-analysed the molecular data and searched for specific linkage association with our αβ-QTL and meQTL. This approach can uncover genes and their products that act in candidate pathways or co-expression networks90. Owing to the high sequence similarity and close physical proximity (~2.6 kb apart) between the paralogous genes VIM2 and VIM4, their transcripts are difficult to reliably distinguish by RNA-seq. As a result, the raw eQTL dataset reports expression values only for VIM4, which probably reflect the combined expression of both VIM2 and VIM4 (hereafter referred to as VIM2/4).

QTL mapping was performed using the qtl R package (v1.70) to identify loci associated with epigenetic variation89. The genetic map was constructed using est.map(), incorporating an optimized error probability of 1 × 10−7 for improved accuracy. Single-QTL genome scans were conducted using Haley–Knott regression for multiple phenotypic traits, including global and annotation-specific methylation levels and epimutation rates. Significance thresholds (α = 0.05) were determined via 1,000 permutations. LOD CIs were defined using a drop threshold of 2.0 via the ‘lodint()’ function, and the most significant QTL peak LOD for each trait was identified.

Calling Cvi- and Ler-derived chromosomal segments

To define genomic segments inherited by the Ler × Cvi RILs, we classified each genomic region into Ler segments and Cvi segments on the basis of the previously mentioned 12,479 SNP markers, which exclusively comprised Cvi and Ler genotypes. For each RIL, markers assigned to Ler were grouped into contiguous segments. The start and end positions of each Ler segment were determined using the first and last marker of the segment, with the midpoint taken as the representative position. Cvi segments were identified independently using the same approach, on the basis of markers assigned to Cvi.

DMR calling

DMRs were identified using the jDMR pipeline (https://github.com/jlab-code/jDMRgrid), adapted from the protocol described by Hazarika et al.18. The genome was partitioned into 200-bp sliding windows with a 50-bp step size, retaining only those bins containing a minimum of ten cytosines. For each sequence context (CG, CHG and CHH), per-window methylation states (methylated or unmethylated) were inferred using METHimpute83. We then constructed a comprehensive methylation call matrix to facilitate pairwise comparisons between the wild-type and treatment groups. Finally, invariant methylation patterns across samples were filtered out and contiguous differentially methylated bins were merged to generate the DMR set.

RNA-seq data processing

RNA-seq data were newly generated for high- and low-epimutation-rate Ler × Cvi RILs, VIM2/4 overexpression lines, vim2 and vim4 T-DNA insertion mutants and Col-0 wild-type controls as described in the respective sections above. All libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. When multiple FASTQ files existed for the same sample, they were concatenated before downstream processing. Raw reads were quality-checked with FastQC (v0.11.9) and trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.38). Trimmed reads were aligned to the TAIR10 reference genome using STAR (v2.7.6a), and alignments were sorted and indexed using SAMtools (v1.17). Gene-level read counts were obtained using HTSeq-count (v0.13.5) with the Araport11 annotation.

In addition, published RNA-seq data for vim1, vim1/2/3 and met1 mutants from Shook and Richards57 were re-analysed using the same pipeline. For published datasets of H3K27me3-deficient clf swn double mutants from Mozgová et al.55, Col-0 and Cvi-0 accessions, Col × Cvi RILs from Picard et al.56 and natural Cvi accessions from Zicola et al.41, precomputed gene-level raw count matrices were obtained from the original publications and re-analysed from the count stage.

All count matrices were restricted to nuclear genes on chromosomes 1–5 and analysed separately using DESeq2 v1.30.1. Counts were regularized-log transformed with rlog for visualization. For each comparison, log2 fold changes and nominal two-sided Wald-test P values for candidate DNA methylation pathway genes were extracted from the DESeq2 output. As these genes were selected a priori on the basis of their established roles in DNA methylation maintenance and demethylation pathways, significance was assessed using nominal P values (P < 0.05), whereas genome-wide comparisons used the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR <0.05).

To quantify gene expression divergence across generations of RILs using the same pedigree-based framework applied to methylation data, we converted rlog-normalized gene expression values from DESeq2 into a discrete gene-level state matrix compatible with AlphaBeta. For each RNA-seq sample, expression values were minimum–maximum scaled to an interval of (0, 1). Genes were then discretized into three ordinal states on the basis of within-sample tertile thresholds. Genes in the lowest tertile were assigned to the low-expression state U, genes in the middle tertile to the intermediate-expression state I and genes in the highest tertile to the high-expression state M. This three-state encoding mirrors the methylation state calls from METHimpute and enables direct application of AlphaBeta to each MA-ML pedigree. Subsequently, gene-level state files were generated containing chromosome, start, end, the assigned expression state and the scaled expression value. We then applied AlphaBeta independently to each MA-ML pedigree. For each RIL, the neutral accumulation model (ABneutral) was fitted and compared with the null model of no accumulation (ABnull) using the built-in F-test for the statistical significance of transcriptome divergence accumulation over time.

ATAC-seq data processing

Sequencing reads were processed using a standard ATAC-seq pipeline as described below. Adapters were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.39, and paired-end reads were aligned to the TAIR10 genome using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17). Sorted BAM files were deduplicated using Picard MarkDuplicates (v2.23.8; REMOVE_DUPLICATES=true). Peaks were called with MACS2 (v2.2.9.1) using BEDPE input mode and Arabidopsis genome size (1.35 × 108), with parameters keep-dup all, SPMR and -q 0.05. All downstream analyses were restricted to chromosomes 1–5.

To quantify chromatin accessibility divergence across generations, we converted ATAC-seq peak calls into a genome-wide binary presence/absence matrix. The genome was divided into non-overlapping 100-bp bins. Each bin was scored as ‘M’ (accessible) in a sample if at least 50% of its length overlapped with a called ATAC-seq peak and as ‘U’ (inaccessible) otherwise. This binarization approach mirrors the construction of the methylation matrix from WGBS data. All bins were retained regardless of accessibility to ensure full genome coverage. Chromatin accessibility divergence between samples across generations was inferred using AlphaBeta19, with model fitting and significance testing as described for the RNA-seq data.
