Age-stratified association between AST/ALT ratio and all-cause mortality in asthma patients: a retrospective cohort study from the NHANES database
Authors: Xuliang Chen, Xiongbin Li, Guangbin Liang, Rihui Guan, Xinwei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsthma is strongly associated with metabolic diseases. Especially concerning liver disease, the correlation between the aspartate aminotransferase to alanine aminotransferase (AST/ALT) ratio, a marker of liver function, and the prognosis of asthma is not clearly defined.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study, using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2001–2018, investigated the relationship between the AST/ALT ratio and all-cause mortality, with mortality follow-up completed through December 31, 2019. The association between the AST/ALT ratio and mortality was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models. In addition, restricted cubic spline (RCS), subgroup analyses, and sensitivity analyses were employed to further elucidate the relationship between the AST/ALT ratio and mortality.ResultsThe study ultimately included 2,137 people living with asthma with a median follow-up time of 8.83 years. After accounting for possible confounding variables, multivariate Cox regression analyses showed that the AST/ALT ratio was positively associated with the risk of all-cause mortality among people living with asthma (HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.09–1.65). Specifically, asthma patients in the highest quartile (Q4) had a significantly higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.30–2.95) compared with the lowest quartile (Q1) of the AST/ALT ratio. In addition, the association between the AST/ALT ratio and the risk of death in people living with asthma varied across populations, and this association was more pronounced in people living with asthma older than 40 years (HR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.51–2.27). Sensitivity analyses, including the exclusion of patients who died early in the follow-up period and those with cancer diagnosed during the follow-up period, confirmed the robustness of the results of the main analysis.ConclusionIn people living with asthma, the AST/ALT ratio was positively associated with the risk of all-cause mortality, and this association was more pronounced in those over 40 years of age. This finding highlights the significance of the liver index in predicting asthma prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04296-x.

Introduction

Asthma is a heterogeneous chronic inflammatory airway disease characterized by fluctuating symptoms—including wheezing, dyspnea, chest tightness, and cough—alongside variable expiratory airflow limitation and airway hyperresponsiveness [1, 2]. Globally, this condition affects approximately 358 million individuals, with a prevalence of 1%–18% that continues to rise; mortality rates range from 1.6 to 36.7 per 100,000 population [3]. Common triggers include genetic predisposition, viral infections, gastroesophageal reflux, allergen exposure, smoking, abrupt temperature changes, stress, and physical exertion [4–8]. Previous studies have confirmed that asthma is closely associated with metabolic dysfunction [9, 10]. Approximately 60% of patients with severe asthma in the United States are obese, and this population exhibits distinct clinical features, including worse asthma control, increased exacerbation frequency, and reduced responsiveness to first-line controller therapies such as inhaled corticosteroids [11, 12]. Notably, weight reduction interventions have been demonstrated to ameliorate asthma symptoms in obese patients [13]. Beyond obesity, individual components of metabolic syndrome have been independently associated with asthma pathogenesis. Dyslipidemia, characterized by elevated triglycerides, promotes airway hyperresponsiveness through inflammatory cascades [14, 15]. Longitudinal cohort studies have confirmed that higher blood insulin levels in childhood are associated with increased risk of active asthma through adolescence and adulthood [16]. Furthermore, insulin resistance has been demonstrated to be associated with accelerated lung function decline and increased risk of future asthma exacerbations [17]. Given the substantial impact of metabolic factors on asthma morbidity, mortality, and economic burden, elucidating their effects on patient prognosis is of considerable clinical significance.

The ratio of serum aspartate aminotransferase to alanine aminotransferase (AST/ALT ratio) is a widely recognized biochemical marker that is important in the diagnosis and management of various diseases [18]. This biomarker facilitates the assessment of hepatic function and disease progression by reflecting the relative release of these two enzymes in response to hepatocellular injury. Previous studies have established strong associations between the AST/ALT ratio and various metabolic disorders, including metabolic syndrome, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease [18–20]. Specifically, an elevated AST/ALT ratio has been associated with an increased risk of diabetic retinopathy [21]. Additionally, in children and adolescents, elevated ALT levels have demonstrated significant associations with heightened cardiometabolic risk factors [22]. In the respiratory field, AST/ALT has been demonstrated to be associated with increased mortality risk in patients with pulmonary embolism and acquired pneumonia [23, 24]. Moreover, the AST/ALT ratio has been identified as an independent risk factor for 90-day prognosis in patients with acute exacerbation of chronic liver disease [25]. Prior research has established a strong association between liver disease and asthma [26, 27]. Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome, has emerged as a novel metabolic comorbidity with significant implications for asthma. A nationwide prospective cohort study of 160,603 Korean adults demonstrated that NAFLD, defined by elevated fatty liver index, was associated with a 25% increased incidence of asthma compared to controls without hepatic steatosis [27]. However, the relationship between the AST/ALT ratio and clinical outcomes in asthma patients remains unexplored.

Given the substantial burden of asthma-related morbidity and mortality, the implementation of standardized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies is imperative to optimize disease control and patient outcomes. Consequently, this study aimed to elucidate the association between the AST/ALT ratio and clinical outcomes in patients with asthma utilizing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Method

Study description

Study participants were derived from the NHANES, a nationally representative database established by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). The primary purpose of NHANES is to evaluate the health and nutritional conditions of adults and children in the US, serving as an ongoing health assessment conducted biennially. Employing a multi-center stratified sampling approach, it is divided into four stages, aiming for a comprehensive population health survey in non-institutional settings across the US. The survey incorporates both questionnaires and physical assessments of participants. Questionnaires focus on demographics, socioeconomic factors, lifestyle inquiries, dietary reviews, family and personal medical histories, and medication usage. Physical exams consist of standard physiological measurements and laboratory index tests. Ethical approval for this endeavor was obtained from the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Committee, and informed consent was provided by all participants.

Study population

In our study, we selected sample data from the period between 2001 and 2018. Out of 91,351 respondents, we excluded 79,599 patients without asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), 4,953 patients who currently do not have asthma, 3,417 were excluded due to incomplete mortality data, and an additional 383 participants were excluded due to missing AST or ALT measurements. After excluding patients with missing covariate data, a total of 2,137 adult subjects with comprehensive asthma data were ultimately enrolled. (Fig. 1). This study incorporated data from 9 consecutive NHANES cycles. The number of participants with asthma in each original survey cycle was as follows: 2001–2002 (n = 178), 2003–2004 (n = 202), 2005–2006 (n = 216), 2007–2008 (n = 230), 2009–2010 (n = 248), 2011–2012 (n = 259), 2013–2014 (n = 318), 2015–2016 (n = 265), and 2017–2018 (n = 221).

Definition of AST/ALT, asthma, and outcome

AST/ALT ratio: During collection, blood was collected from fasted participants using dry tubes without anticoagulants to ensure asepsis during sampling. After blood collection, serum was rapidly separated and stored at -30 °C in a cryogenic environment. The rate of change of absorbance at 340 nm, proportional to the activity of ALT or AST in the sample, was monitored by the kinetic rate method of the DxC800. The ratio of AST to ALT is determined by dividing AST by ALT.

Asthma: Data on asthma were collected by trained interviewers using standardized questionnaires. The study used self-reported questionnaire responses to define asthma. Asthma was retained only for participants who answered “yes” to both (i) “Has a doctor ever told you that you had asthma?” and (ii) “Do you still have asthma?”. Missed interviews, refusal to answer, or do not know responses were considered invalid data.

Mortality: The study endpoint was all-cause mortality, defined as death from any cause during the follow-up period. Eligible asthma patients were linked to National Death Index (NDI) records matched with NHANES respondent sequence numbers (SEQNs) until 31st December 2019 to ascertain their survival status and duration of follow-up.

Covariate selection

Based on previous literature on mortality in the asthma population, several predetermined covariates linked to mortality were identified as potential confounders and were assessed or interviewed at the baseline survey [28–30]. (1) Demographic data: age, sex (male/female), race (Mexican American/non-Hispanic black/non-Hispanic white/other Hispanic/other race), education (less than high school/high school/more than high school), marital status (married/widowed/ separated), poverty income ratios (PIR), body mass index (BMI), and health insurance. (2) Laboratory data: blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, uric acid, white blood cell (WBC), lymphocytes, eosinophils, and neutrophils. (3) Lifestyle: smoking (former/never/current), and drinking (participants were categorized into three groups: never [no alcohol in past year], moderate drinkers [men: 1–2 drinks/drinking day; women: 1 drink/drinking day], and heavy drinkers [men: ≥3 drinks/drinking day; women: ≥2 drinks/drinking day]). (4) Comorbidities: use of anti-asthma medications (yes/no), cancer (yes/no), diabetes mellitus (DM) (yes/no), hypertension (yes/no), heart failure (yes/no), and liver disease (yes/no). For further information on how variables are collected, consult the NHANES survey methods and analysis guide.

Statistical analysis

According to the quartiles of the AST/ALT ratio, the baseline characteristics of the participants were statistically detailed. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard error, and categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages). Analysis of variance was used to compare continuous variables, and the chi-square test was used to test the statistical differences of categorical variables between groups. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to calculate hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three models were constructed to explore the relationship between the AST/ALT ratio and all-cause mortality in asthma patients. Model 1: no adjustment. Model 2: adjusted for age, sex, and race. Model 3: adjusted for age, sex, BMI, PIR, race, smoking, drinking, DM, hypertension, heart failure, cancer, anti-asthma drugs, BUN, creatinine, uric acid, WBC, neutrophils, eosinophils, lymphocytes, liver disease, and health insurance. Model diagnostics confirmed absence of severe multicollinearity (all generalized variance inflation factors < 2.5) and no significant violation of the proportional hazards assumption (global test P = 0.433; Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Bootstrap validation (B = 1000) demonstrated minimal overfitting (original C-index: 0.868, bias: 0.005). Additionally, after controlling for multiple variables, the restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was applied to investigate the nonlinear connection between the AST/ALT ratio and all-cause mortality in individuals with asthma. Finally, Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to affirm the stability of the relationship between the AST/ALT ratio and mortality from all causes in individuals with asthma.

For the pooled 2001–2018 NHANES cycles, survey weights were scaled following NHANES official guidelines. Specifically, the 2-year mobile examination center (MEC) weights (WTMEC2YR) from each participant’s respective survey cycle were divided by 9 (the number of 2-year cycles pooled) to create final weights. We incorporated the Survey design masked variable strata (SDMVSTRA) and SDMV primary sampling units (SDMVPSU) variables provided in NHANES. These were included in all Cox models. We employed the svycoxph function from the survey package, which implements the Taylor series linearization method for variance estimation in the presence of sampling weights, strata, and clustering. All analyses were completed using R software (version 4.3.1); statistical significance is determined by a P value of less than 0.05.

Result

Baseline characteristics of participants

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of 2,137 patients with asthma stratified by AST/ALT quartiles. The study population had a mean age of 43.57 years. Participants in the highest AST/ALT quartile (Q4) were predominantly female and included higher proportions of non-Hispanic Black individuals compared with the lowest quartile. Additionally, this group exhibited a lower prevalence of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and liver disease. Notably, individuals in the higher AST/ALT quartile demonstrated lower BMI and decreased laboratory values—including WBC, lymphocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils, and uric acid. Regarding medication use, the prevalence of anti-asthma drug use was highest in Q1 and lowest in Q3. Regarding mortality outcomes, the all-cause mortality rate increased significantly with the elevation of the AST/ALT ratio.

Association between AST/ALT ratio and all-cause death in patients with asthma

Table 2 shows the results of the multivariate Cox regression analyses in the asthmatic population. After adjusting for covariates in Model 3, the AST/ALT ratio was positively associated with all-cause mortality in patients with asthma (HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.09, 1.65). When patients with asthma were divided by AST/ALT quartile and adjusted for all covariates, the results of Model 3 showed that all-cause mortality was significantly higher in the Q4 quartile of patients with asthma compared with the Q1 quartile (HR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.30–2.95). Furthermore, the association between AST/ALT quartiles and mortality was variable in the population of people living with asthma (P for trend < 0.05).

Subgroup analysis

The association between AST/ALT ratio and asthma prognosis showed significant differences across demographic characteristics: age, sex, BMI, smoking, drinking, hypertension, DM, and heart failure (Fig. 3). Notably, the correlation between the AST/ALT ratio and all-cause death in people living with asthma remained positive in most subgroups of the population. In addition, interaction tests showed a stronger correlation between the AST/ALT ratio and all-cause death in asthmatic populations older than 40 years (HR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.51–2.27) (P for interaction < 0.05). Furthermore, we further stratified asthma patients according to age subgroups and used the RCS to describe the dose-response associations between AST/ALT ratio and all-cause mortality in different age subgroups (Fig. 4). An association between AST/ALT ratio and mortality was not observed in people living with asthma younger than 40 years of age, whereas this association was significant in people living with asthma older than 40 years of age (P for overall < 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis

To validate the robustness of our findings, we performed three aspects of sensitivity analyses. First, we excluded patients who died within two years of follow-up from our analyses to reduce potential survival bias. After excluding this data, the results of our analyses were consistent with our previous main findings (Table 3). Second, we also excluded patients diagnosed with cancer during follow-up and found that the association between AST/ALT ratio and all-cause mortality remained consistent with our previous main analysis (Table 4). Third, we also excluded participants with liver disease and found that the association between AST/ALT ratio and all-cause mortality remained consistent with our previous main analysis (Table 5).

Discussion

A cohort study of 2,137 asthma patients showed that after adjusting for factors such as demographics, lifestyle, and health status, the mortality rate for asthma patients in the United States increased significantly with the rising AST/ALT ratio. In particular, patients with the highest AST/ALT ratio faced a risk of death that was approximately 68% higher than patients in the lowest quartile. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses validated the reliability of these results. These results emphasize the key role of liver indicators in the prevention and prognosis in asthma patients.

The AST/ALT ratio has emerged as a clinically significant biomarker, with accumulating evidence demonstrating its associations with diverse pathological conditions ranging from hepatic disorders to cardiovascular diseases, thereby offering valuable insights into disease progression and therapeutic management [31]. Currently, this ratio serves as a diagnostic and severity assessment tool for NAFLD, a cross-sectional analysis conducted in the United States demonstrated that this biomarker outperforms conventional liver enzymes in predicting NAFLD [32]. Subsequent investigations have further validated the predictive utility of the AST/ALT ratio in NAFLD and underscored its value in evaluating disease severity [33]. Additionally, a population-based study in China revealed significant associations between the AST/ALT ratio and insulin resistance, a central component of metabolic syndrome; specifically, lower ratios were indicative of greater insulin resistance, particularly among individuals with elevated BMI [34]. The clinical relevance of this biomarker extends to cardiovascular pathology. A prospective cross-sectional study involving 100 participants demonstrated elevated AST/ALT ratios in patients with chronic heart failure [35]. Collectively, the utility of the AST/ALT ratio in predicting and monitoring various diseases has gained increasing recognition. The present study extends these findings by demonstrating that a higher AST/ALT ratio is associated with poorer prognosis in patients with asthma, thereby expanding the clinical applicability of this biomarker from hepatic and cardiovascular conditions to respiratory diseases.

Age-stratified analyses revealed that elevated AST/ALT ratios were associated with increased mortality risk among patients aged > 40 years compared to younger counterparts. This observation may be attributed to age-related physiological alterations and the cumulative burden of comorbidities. Older adults frequently present with multiple chronic conditions that may aggravate asthma severity and compromise hepatic function [36, 37]. Consequently, age-tailored management strategies are clinically imperative.

The association between elevated AST/ALT levels and increased mortality in patients with asthma can be attributed to the following possible mechanisms: AST and ALT are sensitive indicators of abnormal liver function, which may reflect a systemic inflammatory response [38]. In the case of asthma, chronic inflammation can lead to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which can affect not only airway inflammation but also liver function [39]. This liver dysfunction can exacerbate the symptoms of asthma and increase the risk of death.

This study was a large-sample exploration of the relationship between AST/ALT and asthma prognosis. The rigorous data collection of NHANES ensured the reliability of the data and provided comprehensive details of confounders, reducing measurement bias. However, this study has limitations, such as the fact that it was a retrospective study; future prospective cohort studies are still needed to assess the prognostic role of AST/ALT in asthma patients further. In addition, although adjusting for covariates minimizes confounding, the complete elimination of confounders remains unattainable. For example, AST is not exclusively hepatic in origin; skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, and erythrocytes also contain substantial AST activity [40]. Intense physical activity in healthy males can lead to a significantly greater increase in serum AST levels compared to ALTwithin 7 days post-exercise, which may interfere with the relationship between AST/ALT ratio and mortality [41]. Finally, the measurement of either AST or ALT is based on the results of a single examination, and although large-scale studies can avoid the bias introduced by a single occasion, studies of the association between the AST to ALT ratio and mortality in patients with asthma based on multiple measurements are still warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings suggest that the AST/ALT ratio is positively and independently associated with the risk of all-cause mortality in asthma patients and that this relationship is age-dependent in asthma patients. Further elaboration of the mechanisms underlying this association is still needed in the future.
