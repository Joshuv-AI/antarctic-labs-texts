Endovascular Management of a Large Isolated Systemic to Pulmonary Artery Collateral (Type B Malinosculation) in a 13-Year-Old: A Case Report
Authors: Meghal Sancheti, Saif Rehman, Alexander V Chalphin, Matthew Bramlet, Boban P Abraham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractType B malinosculation is a rare congenital malformation and a substrate for left-to-right shunting. We discuss a case of a 13-year-old boy with left-heart enlargement and incidental finding of a large isolated systemic arterial collateral to normal left lower lung. After review of computed tomography angiography of chest and risk-benefit assessment, the patient underwent transcatheter occlusion. This case highlights the importance of investigating aortopulmonary collaterals as an etiology for unexplained left-heart volume overload. We also discuss different treatment strategies used in this rare condition.Keywords: case report, malinosculation, systemic to pulmonary artery collateral, transcatheter occlusion, unifocalization

Introduction

Malinosculation is defined as the establishment of abnormal communications by means of small openings or anastomoses. Type B malinosculation is a rare congenital anomaly where a systemic collateral artery supplies a lung segment with normal tracheobronchial tree and pulmonary venous return1 (Figure 1). It results in a hemodynamically significant left-to-right shunt.2 Fewer than 60 cases are reported in the literature, posing challenges to both diagnosis and determining optimal treatment strategy. Generally, pediatric patients present with pulmonary overcirculation and left-heart volume overload, and adults with hemoptysis.3,4 We present a unique case of a 13-year-old with resolved pulmonary overcirculation and without pulmonary hemorrhage who was managed by transcatheter occlusion of the collateral vessel.

Case presentation

A 13-year-old boy (height 159 cm, weight 81 kg) was incidentally diagnosed with a large systemic arterial collateral to the left lower lobe. The patient was initially investigated for a murmur at age 3 years. An echocardiogram revealed severe left-heart dilation, left ventricular hypertrabeculation, and preserved systolic function. Holodiastolic flow reversal in the abdominal aorta Doppler seen on that echo was not reported at the time. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging confirmed the same, but the imaging did not include descending aorta. The patient was then treated for a genetic test-negative left ventricular noncompaction cardiomyopathy with beta-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and diuretics. By the age of 12 years, despite medication noncompliance, serial echocardiograms unveiled eventual resolution of the left-heart dilation. At age 13 years, a follow-up cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was performed to confirm the echo findings of resolved left ventricular dilation and persistent hypertrabeculation. It confirmed the same but also incidentally detected a 12-mm-diameter anomalous systemic artery originating from the descending thoracic aorta and supplying the left lower lobe.

Chest computed tomography angiography was performed, followed by 3D segmentation and analysis on a stereoscopic display to generate a high-fidelity anatomic digital twin. It confirmed the 12-mm-diameter tortuous aortopulmonary collateral. Tracheobronchial tree and pulmonary venous return were normal (type B malinosculation). The images suggested sole source pulmonary blood flow from the collateral (complete type). Cardiac catheterization was performed under general anesthesia with a dual-lumen endotracheal tube for selective right lung ventilation in case of inadvertent bleeding in the left lung. Left ventricular end-diastolic pressure was found to be 20 mm Hg, presumed to be the result of prior long-standing volume overload. Right ventricular systolic pressure, pulmonary artery pressure, transpulmonary gradient, and pulmonary vascular resistance were normal at baseline and postcollateral occlusion. Systemic pressures were noted within the deep branches of the collateral. Selective left pulmonary artery and collateral angiograms with and without wedge balloon occlusion confirmed single-source blood flow from the collateral to the medial basal segments. The left lower pulmonary artery supplied the lateral basal and superior segments of the left lower lobe. This confirmed a complete type B malinosculation. Collateral branches were highly tortuous, and the capillary phase showed microvascular arteriovenous malformations (AVMs). Pulmonary venous return from the left lower lobe was normal (Figure 2).

A and B show collateral branches to the left lower lobe by 3D reconstruction and by selective cath angiogram, respectively. C and D show native left lower lobe pulmonary artery branches by 3D reconstruction and by selective cath angiogram, respectively.

The collateral artery was entered from the left axillary artery using a 6F long sheath, considering the inferiorly directed takeoff of the collateral from the descending aorta. Because the sheath could not traverse the “U” turn, access to the distal collateral was achieved using a coaxial 5F Amplatzer TorqVue Low Profile sheath. Two Amplatzer duct occluder type 2, 6/6 devices (Abbott) were deployed distally to serve as a “backstop” for the coils. Proximal embolization was achieved using 3 Azur coils (Terumo) and a 14-mm Amplatzer Vascular Plug II. Postintervention angiography confirmed complete occlusion of the collateral and preservation of native left lower lobe pulmonary artery flow (Figure 3). Chest x-ray at 1-month follow-up showed no signs of major complications (Figure 4). At 5 months postcatheterization, the patient remains asymptomatic without evidence of pulmonary infarction clinically.

Discussion

Type B malinosculation represents a rare entity within the spectrum of congenital bronchopulmonary vascular malformations.1 Seven types have been described (Figure 1). Type A is an isolated bronchial malinosculation with normal arterial supply and venous connections. Examples include congenital tracheal stenosis, congenital tracheal bronchus, congenital bridging bronchus, bronchogenic cyst, bronchial stenosis, and atresia. Type B is an isolated arterial malinosculation with normal venous and bronchial anatomy. Examples include a collateral artery arising from the descending aorta to the normal left lower lobe of the lung. Type C is an isolated venous malinosculation, with the scimitar vein being an example. Type D is a mixed bronchoarterial malinosculation with normal pulmonary venous connections, such as a typical intralobar sequestration. Type E is a mixed bronchovenous malinosculation with normal arterial supply, such as congenital pulmonary airway malformation with partial anomalous pulmonary venous return. Type F is an arteriovenous malinosculation with normal bronchial anatomy, such as pulmonary AVMs as seen in hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia. Type G is a mixed bronchoarteriovenous malinosculation. Extralobar sequestration is an example.

Recent literature differentiates between “isolated” or “complete” type B, in which the collateral provides sole supply, and “incomplete” type B, which denotes dual supply from the collateral and native pulmonary arteries.2,3

The pathophysiology of complete type B malinosculation is driven by a large extracardiac left-to-right shunt. Hitherto unexplained continuous murmurs, unilateral increase in pulmonary blood flow, and left-heart enlargement have been documented especially in pediatric patients and should prompt a diagnostic workup for large aortopulmonary collaterals using cross-sectional imaging.2,3 If left untreated, this collateral can result in segmental pulmonary hypertension and heart failure, and rare complications like aneurysm formation and pulmonary hemorrhage.3, 4, 5

Computed tomography angiography is the gold standard to delineate vessel origin (most commonly the descending thoracic aorta) and to map its distribution (most commonly the left lower lobe). 3D segmentation and analysis on a stereoscopic display can augment the understanding of the anatomy and assist in preprocedural planning.

In complete types, unifocalization of the collateral artery to the native pulmonary artery is pursued in infants and young patients.6 However, in this patient, the abnormal vasculature, evidence of segmental pulmonary hypertension, AVMs, and technical challenges for unifocalization made it a nonviable management strategy. Furthermore, elevated left ventricular end-diastolic pressure and risk for pulmonary hemorrhage necessitated occlusion.

Historically, occlusion was performed surgically by ligation of the anomalous artery with or without lobectomy.3,4 Thoracic endovascular aortic repair has also been reported in the literature.7

More recently, transcatheter embolization is reported successfully in multiple case reports.2,8, 9, 10 Chest pain is the most common post procedural complication.8 Pulmonary infarct was reported in one instance after embolization, although no additional intervention was needed.8,9 The risk of pulmonary infarct is low because the nutritive supply to the lung is from bronchial arteries.

Conclusion

We present a 13-year-old who was incidentally found to have complete type B malinosculation. This case highlights that persistent, unexplained left-heart enlargement should prompt investigation for aortopulmonary collaterals. Unifocalization of such collateral is beneficial in a young child with predominant pulmonary overcirculation. The collateral in this case exhibited predominant features of segmental pulmonary hypertension with future risk for pulmonary hemorrhage and was managed endovascularly. This case demonstrates that transcatheter embolization is a successful management strategy for this anomaly and potentially prevents long-term sequelae like pulmonary hemorrhage.
