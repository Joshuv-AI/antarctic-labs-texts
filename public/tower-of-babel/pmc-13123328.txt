A novel 2-piperazino-pyrimidine compound exhibits asexual antimalarial activity by targeting Plasmodium falciparum plasmepsin X
Authors: Jing Hong, Takaya Sakura, Naoya Asada, Kouki Fuchino, Daiki Kuwana, Rina Kaki, Ryuta Ishii, Teruhisa Kato, Kiyoshi Kita, Kenichi Matsumura, Daniel Ken Inaoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although the number of cases of malaria has declined since the introduction of artemisinin-based combination therapy in endemic countries, the emergence of resistant parasites in the field has led to an alarming increase in treatment failure, making the search for novel drug candidates with precise targets critical. Here, we report the identification, from Shionogi's chemical library, of a 2-piperazino-pyrimidine compound (FPSA hereafter), with antimalarial activity; the compound shows a Plasmodium falciparum-killing effect resulting from blockage of merozoite release from infected erythrocytes. Parasites treated with FPSA halted as schizonts and exhibited a defect in the egress process. After extended selection, we generated FPSA-resistant parasites and obtained five clones showing modest EC50 shifts. Whole-genome sequencing of these isolates identified 2 clones harboring distinct single-nucleotide variants in the plasmepsin X (PMX)-encoding gene (PfDd2_080013400), changes that were predicted to result in I252V or E55G substitutions in PMX; another 3 clones harbored duplications of the genomic region spanning PfDd2_080013400. Genome editing parasites harboring the corresponding amino acid substitutions exhibited EC50 fold changes similar to those observed in the resistant parasites. Furthermore, FPSA inhibited the proteolytic activity of recombinant PMX in vitro and also the cleavage of PMX substrate proteins in parasites. Transcriptomic analysis confirmed that parasites exposed to FPSA exhibit gene expression patterns similar to those observed in parasites exposed to WM382, a validated plasmepsin IX (PMIX)/PMX dual inhibitor. These results indicate that FPSA inhibits merozoite egress by targeting P. falciparum plasmepsin X. Therefore, this work identifies a novel class of antimalarial compounds that target PMX. Our results are expected to contribute to the development of new drugs for the treatment of malaria.

Introduction

Malaria is among the most devastating of parasitic diseases, with 263 million estimated cases in 85 endemic countries and 597,000 deaths in 2023 (W.H.O., 2024). Plasmodium falciparum is the most common and most lethal causative pathogen of human malaria. According to World Health Organization's (WHO) most recent annual report, infection with P. falciparum is predominant world-wide, accounting for approximately 94% of malaria deaths in Africa, a region wherein approximately 94% of estimated global cases and 95% of deaths were reported (W.H.O., 2024). With the goal of controlling malaria, interventions such as insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS) are routinely performed to disrupt the spread of this vector-borne disease (Bhatt et al., 2015). In addition, since 2001, thanks to the wide-spread recommendation and use of artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) as the first-line treatment, malaria cases and mortality have decreased drastically. However, the disease remains a significant threat due to the emergence of parasites resistant to approved drugs, a pattern that had led to treatment failure exceeding 10% in some endemic areas (Ashley et al., 2014; (W.H.O., 2024). Therefore, there remains an urgent need for new anti-malarial drugs; global efforts continue to be made to discover druggable new candidate molecules that might circumvent the emergence of resistance to artemisinin and partner drugs used in ACT.

P. falciparum has a complex life cycle, alternating between female Anopheles mosquitoes and vertebrate hosts. Once sporozoites in the mosquito's salivary gland infect a vertebrate host during a blood meal, these cells are carried by the blood stream to the liver, where hepatocytes are infected and merozoites are generated (Cowman et al., 2016). The merozoites then are released into the blood stream and the asexual cycle starts, whereby the merozoites invade erythrocytes, develop through the trophozoite and schizont stages, and merozoites finally are released back into the blood stream (Cowman et al., 2016) via a process called egress. The mechanism of egress appears to be conserved across different Plasmodium species, constituting a critical step in the parasite life cycle (Dvorin and Goldberg, 2022). Blocking egress effectively traps the parasite inside the red blood cells (RBCs), preventing further infection and decreasing parasite burden, thereby limiting the severe symptoms associated with malaria, including anemia and inflammation (Crunkhorn, 2018).

Phenotypic screening is one of the most effective approaches in drug research, and is commonly used in drug discovery to identify chemical starting points; this method allows direct assessment of the parasite-killing activity of compounds irrespective of their targets (Guiguemde et al., 2012; Hovlid and Winzeler, 2016). The combination of phenotypic screening with target identification provides great opportunities for discovering compounds with novel modes of action (MoAs) (Gamo et al., 2010). In recent decades, despite the identification (through phenotypic screening) of numerous antimalarial molecules as effective candidate compounds, precise knowledge of the drug targets of these compounds has remained limited (LaMonte et al., 2020), impeding further development. Furthermore, many compounds initially reported to have P. falciparum-killing activities subsequently were demonstrated to target one of several common pathways, such as mitochondrial function and hemoglobin degradation (Flannery et al., 2015; Mishra et al., 2019; Siqueira-Neto et al., 2023). Consequently, elucidating the precise MoA of drug candidates emerging from phenotypic screening is crucial for the further development of compounds via target-based evolution, a strategy that aims to decrease cytotoxicity while improving biochemical activity.

We previously conducted a comprehensive in vitro phenotypic screening of Shionogi's chemical library, which consists of approximately 80,000 chemically diverse compounds, seeking compounds with activity against asexual blood-stage P. falciparum. This screening identified several chemical series, one of which was a group of chemicals with a shared backbone structure; this group was designated Cluster 322. For the above reasons, we attempted to validate the MoA of Cluster 322. The combination of in vitro evolution of resistant strains and subsequent whole-genome sequencing (WGS) analysis remains one of the best-established methods for target identification. In other work, this method has led to the discovery of targets such as acetyl-coenzyme A synthetase (AcAS) and eukaryotic elongation factor 2 (eEF2) (Baragana et al., 2015; Summers et al., 2022).

In the present study, using a 2-piperazino-pyrimidine chemical (FPSA hereafter) as a representative compound from Cluster 322, we identified and validated the P. falciparum aspartic protease plasmepsin X (PMX) as a target. This enzyme is one of the key proteases that processes proteins and proteases essential for merozoite egress from the schizont and for invasion of host erythrocytes (Nasamu et al., 2017; Pino et al., 2017; Mukherjee et al., 2023; Withers-Martinez et al., 2024). PMX was recently identified as a potential drug target in antimalarial development, based on the identification of small molecule inhibitors that block the proteolytic maturation of merozoites (Nasamu et al., 2017; Pino et al., 2017; Favuzza et al., 2020).

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The use of the human blood and of animals was approved by the Ethics Committees of the School of Tropical Medicine and Global Health (Approval Number NU_TMGH_2024_019_15) and of the Institute of Tropical Diseases, Nagasaki University (Approval Number, 191226226-5), respectively. The human blood samples used in this study were kindly provided by the Japan Red Cross Blood Center (Nagasaki, Japan); individual donors granted permission for the use of blood products for malaria research, and these products were microbiology tested for safety prior to laboratory use.

Chemicals

FPSA, the representative chemical from Cluster 322, was designed, synthesized, and provided by Shionogi & Co., Ltd. (Osaka, Japan), as described in Fig. S1. Stock solutions of FPSA were formulated at the indicated concentrations in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) before use. WM382, a dual inhibitor of plasmepsin IX (PMIX) and PMX (Favuzza et al., 2020), also was provided by Shionogi. A stock solution of WM382 was formulated at 10 mM in DMSO before use.

Malaria parasite strains and in vitro cultivation

This study employed two strains of P. falciparum: Dd2, a multidrug-resistant strain and 3D7, a wild-type strain. Type O+ human RBCs were used for in vitro cultivation of asexual blood-stage parasites. Every two weeks, RBCs from different donors were isolated from freshly drawn whole blood and pooled among donors; the pooled cells then were washed three times with Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) and resuspended at 50% hematocrit before use. The parasites were maintained at 2% hematocrit in filter-sterilized complete medium, which was composed of RPMI1640 medium supplemented with 25 mM N-2-hydroxyethylpiperazine-N-2-ethane sulfonic acid (HEPES), 23.8 mM sodium hydrogen bicarbonate, 50 mg/L hypoxanthine (Millipore/Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), 25 mg/L gentamycin (Millipore/Sigma-Aldrich), and 0.5% (w/v) Albumax II (Invitrogen). Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a gaseous mixture of 90% N2, 5% O2, and 5% CO2. Parasites were sub-cultured in fresh culture medium every two days while maintaining 2% hematocrit and parasitemia of less than 5%. Parasitemia and parasite developmental stages were classified using a flowcytometry-based automated hematology analyzer Sysmex XN-30 (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan) (Tougan et al., 2018) according to nucleic acid content and light scatter parameters.

Diaphorase-nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT)-coupled lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay

An LDH assay was performed to confirm the antimalarial activity of the tested compounds. P. falciparum Dd2 parasites were synchronized by exposure to 5% (w/v) D(−)-sorbitol (Fujifilm Wako, Osaka, Japan) for 10 min at room temperature. Test compound was dissolved at 40 μM, and this starting concentration then was subjected to serial dilution in final culture. The synchronized parasites (at 2% hematocrit and 0.3% parasitemia) were cultured in 96-well plates with FPSA (test article), artemisinin-atovaquone (reference article control), or DMSO (negative control); the resulting cultures contained DMSO at final concentrations of 0.4%. The plates were incubated at 37 °C in a moist gaseous mixture. After 72 h, the parasites were frozen overnight at −30 °C upon first thaw. Growth of the parasite was assessed by a diaphorase-NBT coupled LDH assay, which was performed as previously described (Sakura et al., 2024). Briefly, supernatant from the lysed culture was combined with modified PfLDH buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM lithium L-lactate, and 0.25% Triton X-100) supplemented with 75 μM oxidized 3-acetylpyridine adenine dinucleotide (APAD+), 0.2 mg/mL NBT, and 1 U/mL diaphorase, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 20 min. The absorbance at 655 nm of each well was measured using a SpectraMax Paradigm® Multi-Mode microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, USA). The inhibition rate was calculated by normalizing the absorbance obtained from cultures incubated in the presence of 1 μM artemisinin +1 μM atovaquone (defined as 100% inhibition).

Statistics

For calculation of the growth inhibition rate, DMSO wells were defined as negative control wells, and 1 μM artemisinin +1 μM atovaquone wells were defined as positive control wells. The half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) value of test compounds was determined using triplicate or quadruplicate experimental replicates, with curve-fitting using Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, USA) to conduct non-linear regression using the log(inhibitor) vs. normalized response, variable slope model, with least squares regression fitting method and no weighting method. Data visualization for the other figures was performed using Prism 9.0.

In vitro evolution of parasite resistance to FPSA

Cultures of 1 × 109 of P. falciparum Dd2 parasites (in triplicate) were incubated in the presence of gradually increasing concentrations of FPSA, starting from a baseline concentration of 1.5 × EC50. Spent medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the compound every 2 days. Parasitemia was confirmed by microscopic observation of Giemsa-stained blood smears of the cultures. When the parasitemia was attenuated to the point that live parasites were no longer observed, compound was removed from the medium. When parasite growth returned to a parasitemia rate exceeding 1%, compound again was added to the medium. These steps were repeated for 60 days. The resulting resistant strains, which were able to grow in the presence of FPSA at concentrations of 3∼5 × EC50, then were diluted and dispensed in 96-well plates at a density of 0.25 parasites/well, generating monoclonal cultures. The EC50 of each clone was determined; the 5 clones showing the highest shift in EC50 (compared to the parent) were expanded, and genomic DNA was purified from each using the QIAGEN Blood and Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Holland) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

WGS and bioinformatics analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from the five drug-resistant monoclonal strains, as well as from the parent Dd2 strain, using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Sample quality control, Illumina library preparation, and sequencing were outsourced (Novogene Japan Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). WGS libraries of 350-bp fragment length were generated for paired-end sequencing and sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq6000/NovaSeq X Plus instrument. The quality of the resulting sequences was confirmed; reads with adaptor contamination were removed. The resulting fastq files were aligned to the Dd2 reference genome (PlasmoDB-64_PfalciparumDd2_Genome, downloaded from https://plasmodb.org/common/downloads/release-64/PfalciparumDd2/fasta/data/) using BWA-MEM (Li and Durbin, 2009) (v. 0.7.18) and format-converted files were created using SAMtools (Li et al., 2009) (v. 1.18). Duplicate reads were removed using Picard tools MarkDuplicates (v. 3.1.0). Calling of single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) was performed with GATK HaplotypeCaller (v. 4.4.0), and the variants shared with the parent Dd2 strain were removed. Variants were filtered using BCFtools (Li et al., 2009) (v. 1.18) using parameter values of QUAL >1000, QD > 1.5, MQ > 30, and DP > 5. Variant annotation was performed using SnpEff (Cingolani et al., 2012) (v. 5.1f) with genome annotation reference (PlasmoDB-64_PfalciparumDd2_AnnotatedCDSs); the annotated variants with evaluations of HIGH and MODERATE were accepted as output. Copy-number analysis was performed using the R package QDNaseq (Scheinin et al., 2014) (v. 1.40.0). Regions of interest were inspected using the Integrated Genome Viewer (v. 2.16.2) (Thorvaldsdottir et al., 2013).

Transcriptomic analysis

P. falciparum 3D7 cells were synchronized by incubating late-stage schizonts in complete medium with 2% hematocrit RBCs for 3 h at 37 °C with shaking, followed by exposure to 5% (w/v) sorbitol to isolate ring-stage parasites. The parasites then were cultured at an initial parasitemia of 0.2% in complete medium supplemented with 10 μM FPSA, 10 μM WM382, or an equivalent volume of the DMSO vehicle; The concentrations of the compounds used exceeded 10 × EC50, ensuring effective induction of transcriptional changes; on the resulting cultures contained DMSO at final concentrations of 0.1%. These cultures were incubated for another 23 and 35 h at 37 °C. After incubation, RBCs were collected and lysed in TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen). RNA extraction was performed using Phasemaker™ Tubes (Invitrogen) and the PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration of total RNA was measured using a NanoDrop LITE spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Sample quality control, library preparation, and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) were conducted at Novogene. Directional cDNA libraries (polyA enrichment) were prepared for paired-end sequencing using an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus instrument. The quality of the resulting RNA-seq data was assessed, and reads containing adaptor contamination were removed. The resulting fastq files were aligned to the P. falciparum 3D7 reference genome (PlasmoDB-68_Pfalciparum3D7_Genome) using HISAT2 (Pertea et al., 2015) (v. 2.2.0). Format conversion was performed using SAMtools (Li et al., 2009). cDNA assembly and quantification were conducted using StringTie (Pertea et al., 2015) (v. 2.2.3), based on gene annotations. Differential gene expression analysis was performed and visualized using DEseq2 (Mukherjee et al., 2023) (v. 1.46.0).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the R package fgsea (v. 1.32.0) on gene sets for metabolic pathways that were manually curated from PlasmoDB (Release 68) (Aurrecoechea et al., 2009) (Table S4). The ranked gene list was generated based on log-fold change (log2FoldChange), and low-expression genes (baseMean <5) were excluded.

The statistical significance of the overlap of differentially expressed gene (DEGs was evaluated using a hypergeometric test implemented in the R function phyper.

Docking protocol

The crystal structure of PMX (PDBID:7TBC) was used for the docking of FPSA. The protein preparation wizard was applied to add hydrogen atoms, to remove steric clashes, and to identify intra-hydrogen bond networks within the protein. Three-dimensional (3D) structure generation and conformational search of FPSA were performed using Ligprep and Macromodel modules. Generated 3D conformers of FPSA were docked into the PMX binding pocket with Glide SP mode; we selected the top score docking pose for the binding-mode analysis. All calculations were performed with the 2022-3 version of the Schrödinger Suite (Schrödinger, New York, USA).

Data and code availability

Raw sequencing data of WGS are available in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject PRJNA1294478. Raw sequencing data of RNA-seq are available in the NCBI SRA under BioProject PRJNA1297349.

Expression and purification of recombinant PMX

For expression of the recombinant PMX in a wheat germ cell-free protein synthesis system (Fig. S2), the codon-optimized full-length of PMX gene was synthesized by GENEWIZ (Azenta Life Sciences, Tokyo, Japan). The synthesized PMX gene was cloned into the SpeI/XhoI site of pEU-E01-MCS-TEV-His-C1 plasmid (CellFree Sciences, Matsuyama, Japan) containing a His6 tag at the C-terminal. The expression of PMX was conducted using WEPRO7240H Core Kit (CellFree Sciences) following the manufacturer's protocol. The translated solution was loaded on cOmplete His-tag purification resin (Sigma-Aldrich) equilibrated with buffer. The unbound proteins were removed by washing with 10 resin volume of wash buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 300 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, pH8.0). The resin-bound His-tag PMX was eluted with buffer containing 150 mM imidazole and concentrated by passing Amicon-Ultra 4 (30 K, Merck Millipore, Germany). The purified PMX was buffer substituted in 25 mM HEPES buffer (pH 8.0) and kept at 4 °C.

Inhibition assay of PMX

A synthetic peptide Rh2N (Dabcyl)-HSFIQEGKEE-(Edans) was used to check the enzyme activity of purified PMX (Favuzza et al., 2020). The peptide cleavage by an active enzyme leads to an increase in fluorescence over time. PMX was mixed with assay buffer (25 mM sodium acetate, 0.005% Tween-20, pH 5.5) and diluted FPSA ranging from 5 nM to 50 μM, while WM382 (50 μM) was used as positive control of inhibitor to achieve complete inhibition of PMX activity. PMX was incubated with compounds for 15 min, followed by incubation with 25 μM substrate peptide for 4 h. The fluorescence was measured at the excitation wavelength 335 nm and emission wavelength 492 nm and recorded with SpectraMax M2e-TUY (Molecular Devices).

For calculation of the enzyme activity, DMSO were defined as 100% activity, and wells without enzyme were defined as 0% activity. The half-maximal inhibition concentration (IC50) value of FPSA was determined using duplicate or triplicate experimental replicates, with curve-fitting using Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, Inc.) to conduct non-linear regression using the log(inhibitor) vs. normalized response, variable slope model, with least squares regression fitting method and no weighting method.

Generation of PMX mutants

Plasmid pUF-Cas9-pre-sgRNA was a gift from Josh Beck (Addgene plasmid # 137845) (Nessel et al., 2020) and used for CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing. The sgRNA (Fasmac, Japan) was inserted into the BtgZI site of the plasmid. Donor DNA fragments were amplified from P. falciparum Dd2 genomic DNA by PCR with introducing targeted mutations in primers. Amplified donor DNA fragments were inserted into the NcoI site using In-Fusion HD cloning kit (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan). The plasmid constructs were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Fifty μg of constructed plasmid was buffered in cytomix (120 mM KCl, 0.2 mM CaCl2, 2 mM EGTA, 10 mM MgCl2, 25 mM HEPES, 5 mM K2HPO4, 5 mM KH2PO4; pH 7.6 adjusted with KOH) and transfected into erythrocytes using a Gene Pulser Xcell (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, USA). The transfected erythrocytes were mixed with P. falciparum Dd2 strain and cultured until the parasitemia reached 2%. The transgenic parasites were selected in a complete medium containing 1 μM DSM1 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 days and then maintained in a drug-free complete medium until parasite regrowth. Transgenic parasite clones were obtained by limiting dilution and genotyped by diagnostic PCR and Sanger sequencing. Primers used in this experiment are listed in Table S1.

Processing inhibition assays

Synchronized P. falciparum Dd2 parasites were cultured with either 10 × EC50 of PMX inhibitors WM382, FPSA or same volume of DMSO for 40 h. Parasites were released by lysing RBCs with saponin treatment. Collected parasites were adjusted to 2.5 × 105 parasites/μL. Total proteins from 1.0 × 106 parasites were dissolved in reducing sample buffer, separated on 4–12% acrylamide gels (NuPAGE, Invitrogen) and then transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Merck Millipore). The plotted total proteins were labeled by No-Stain™ Protein Labeling Reagent (Invitrogen) for quantification. After blocking with Blocking One (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), the membrane was probed with rabbit anti-AMA1 antibody or rabbit anti-MSP1-19 antibody (a kind gift from Dr. Eizo Takashima). After washing the membrane with TBS-T three times, it was probed with HRP-linked goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, US). Bands were visualized by an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system and densitometric analyzed by LuminoGraph I (ATTO Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Densitometric analysis was performed using Fiji software (version 2.16.0).

Results

FPSA has modest blood-stage antimalarial activity

FPSA, a representative compound of Cluster 322 (Fig. 1A), showed modest in vitro activity against blood-stage P. falciparum parasites, with an EC50 of 463 nM in Dd2 and 404.3 nM in 3D7 strains, respectively (Fig. 1B), as determined by an LDH-based growth inhibition assay. Parasite growth did not resume over six days following exposure to 10 μM FPSA (Fig. 1C); notably, exposure resulted in an increased proportion of trophozoite- and schizont-stage parasites among the total infected RBCs compared to vehicle-exposed cultures (Fig. 1D). Although exposure to FPSA did not inhibit the development of ring, trophozoite, or schizont stages, exposure did block the parasite proliferation cycle after schizont formation. In vitro evolution and WGS identifies plasmepsin X as the target of FPSA

To identify the target of FPSA, an in vitro evolution experiment was conducted using P. falciparum Dd2 parasites. The parasites were cultured under continuous or pulse exposure to FPSA at concentrations ranging from 1.5 × to 6 × EC50. Over the course of 60 days of exposure, viable parasites emerged in two out of three independent selections when the concentrations of FPSA reached 6 × EC50 (Fig. 2A). Bulk parasite populations were harvested, and their resistance to FPSA was confirmed using a growth inhibition assay. Each population exhibited an approximately two-to three-fold increase in EC50 compared to the parent strain (Fig. 2B). A total of 15 resistant clones was isolated via monoclonal cultivation. Clones selected under FPSA exposure exhibited a two-to four-fold changes in EC50; five clones with the highest EC50 shifts were chosen for analysis by WGS (Fig. 2C and Fig. S3). Sequencing revealed that two clones (2D1 and 3E5) carried single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) in the same gene, PfDd2_080013400, which encodes the aspartic protease PMX (Table 1). Although the two clones exhibited similar levels of resistance to FPSA, 2D1 and 3E5 harbored distinct mutations: PMX was predicted to carry an I252V substitution (caused by thymine to cytosine substitution at 414,115 site of chromosome 8) in Clone 2D1 and an E55G substitution (caused by thymine to cytosine substitution at 414,705 site of chromosome 8) in Clone 3E5. These mutations correspond to I264V and E55G substitutions in the PMX protein encoded by the 3D7 strain, given that the amino acid sequences of Dd2 and 3D7 are not identical (exhibiting 97.56% identity and 97.91% similarity; Fig. S4). No other resistant clones exhibited SNVs predicted to cause missense mutations in PMX. Therefore, copy-number-variant (CNV) analysis was performed using alignment files from all of the resistant clones. Three clones (3B4, 3A12 and 3E2) were found to harbor an amplification of a 103,500-bp region on Chromosome 8, a span that includes PfDd2_080013400 (encoding PMX) and additional protein-coding genes, including loci encoding the serine protease DEGP (PfDd2_080012900), a tyrosine tRNA ligase (PfDd2_080013100), and asparagine-rich antigen Pfa55-14 (PfDd2_080014400). Predicted binding mode of FPSA

We next employed computer-assisted 3D docking to predict the binding mode of FPSA to PMX (Fig. 4). Specifically, FPSA was docked into the PMX binding pocket well, in which the compound showed good shape complementarity. Several hydrogen bonds and van der Waals interactions were observed between FPSA and PMX. Notably, the piperazine moiety is predicted to form a hydrogen bond with D445 (correspond to D457 in 3D7 strain) in the same manner as the known inhibitor, WM382 (Hodder et al., 2022). Apical membrane antigen 1 (AMA1) and subtilisin-like protease 1 (SUB1) are known substrates of PMX. FPSA exposure induces a DEG signature resembling that seen with WM382 exposure

We also compared the transcriptomes of P. falciparum 3D7 parasites following exposure to the two compounds. This analysis was based on the hypothesis that if two compounds share the same target, the two should induce similar transcriptomic changes. A time line of compound treatment and sample collection were exhibited in Fig. S5A mRNA abundance data were obtained for over 8400 transcripts in each sample, and functional analyses were performed to identify DEGs (Fig. S5B). Although WM382 has been reported as a PMIX/PMX dual inhibitor, no significant changes in the transcript levels of PF3D7_1430200 (encoding PMIX) or PF3D7_0808200 (encoding PMX) were observed in WM382-exposed parasites. Additionally, with a few exceptions, PMIX/PMX-associated genes identified through STRING Protein-Protein Interaction Networks Functional Enrichment Analysis did not show significant differential expression compared to the DMSO control. However, the expression profiles of these genes exhibited similar regulatory trends in FPSA-exposed parasites, with DEGs corresponding to those observed in the WM382-exposed group (Fig. S5C). When we analyzed the overlap between the DEGs in FPSA- and WM382-exposed parasites, DEGs were significantly overlapped in both the trophozoite stage (278 genes, 14.6% of total trophozoite-stage DEGs; p = 7.3 × 10−76 with a hypergeometric test) and schizont stage (282 genes, 32.8% of schizont-stage DEGs; p = 2.1 × 10−155 with a hypergeometric test) (Fig. S5D, Table S3); almost all of the shared DEGs exhibited concordant expression trends between the two exposure groups (Fig. S5E).

We also performed GSEA to analyzing the altered expression levels of genes encoding compounds of metabolic pathways of the schizont stage. In both groups, whether exposed to FPSA or WM382, genes encoding proteins related to selected pathways such as citrate cycle (ec00020), steroid hormone biosynthesis (ec00140), and sphingolipid metabolism (ec00600) were upregulated. However, genes encoding proteins related to other pathways like selenocompound metabolism (ec00450), carbapenem biosynthesis (ec00332), and cysteine and methionine metabolism (ec00270) were downregulated in both (Fig. S6). These results demonstrate that the transcriptional response to FPSA exposure closely mirrors that observed with WM382, supporting the hypothesis that the two compounds share a common molecular target.

Discussion

In the present study, we confirmed the in vitro antimalarial activity of FPSA, a representative compound from Cluster 322, against asexual blood-stage P. falciparum. FPSA exhibited potent inhibition of schizont-stage parasites, leading to failed egress, and, consequently, the disruption of the parasite replication cycle. In vitro evolution, WGS, and sequence variant analysis of five FPSA-resistant clones identified PMX as the molecular target candidate of FPSA.

PMX is a member of the plasmepsin family of aspartic acid proteases that are responsible for processing essential proteins such as subtilisin-like protease 1 (SUB1) and P. falciparum reticulocyte binding protein homolog 5 (PfRh5) (Yeoh et al., 2007; Mukherjee et al., 2023; Triglia et al., 2023). PMX is localized in exoneme and microneme vesicles (Nasamu et al., 2017; Pino et al., 2017). Through its role in mediating SUB1 pre-domain cleavage, PMX activates and facilitates the release of SUB1 into parasitophorous vacuole (PV). This activation triggers the proteolytic processing of downstream effectors, including serine repeat antigens (SERAs) and merozoite surface proteins (MSPs), that play crucial roles in the rupture of the PV membrane and the RBC membrane, leading to merozoite egress (Ruecker et al., 2012; Das et al., 2015; Thomas et al., 2018). PfRh5 has been reported to interact with the cysteine-rich protective antigen (CyRPA) (Dreyer et al., 2012), the PfRh5-interacting protein (PfRipr) (Chen et al., 2011; Reddy et al., 2015; Wong et al., 2019), the thrombospondin-related apical merozoite protein (PfPTRAMP) (Scally et al., 2022), and the cysteine-rich small secreted protein (CSS) to form the PfPTRAMP–PfCSS–PfRipr–CyRPA–PfRh5 (PCRCR) complex, which has an indispensable function in erythrocyte invasion (Scally et al., 2022; Triglia et al., 2023). Given that PMX is involved in the proteolytic processing and activation of PfRh5, an essential subunit of the PCRCR complex, PMX subsequently enables the PCRCR complex to bind to basigin on the erythrocyte membrane to facilitate erythrocyte invasion.

Together, these observations have led to the recognition of PMX as a viable and validated druggable target for antimalarial drug development. Several PMX inhibitors, including WM4 and WM382 (Favuzza et al., 2020; de Lera Ruiz et al., 2022), UCB7362 (Lowe et al., 2022), CWHM-117 (Nasamu et al., 2017), and 49c (Pino et al., 2017), have been shown to demonstrate antimalarial activity in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, an inhibitor of cyclic GMP-dependent protein kinase (PKG), a protein that induces SUB1 release from the exoneme, also has been validated for anti-malarial efficacy (Crunkhorn, 2018).

In the present study, we identified (among five FPSA-resistant clones) two SNVs in the PMX-encoding gene of P. falciparum Dd2 parasites; these SNVs are predicted to encode PMX with I252V or E55G substitutions in Dd2 strain (correspond to I264V and E55G substitutions in 3D7 strain). These amino acid substitutions in PMX were demonstrated to contribute to the resistance to FPSA in genome editing parasites. In the crystal structure of PMX from P. falciparum 3D7 (reported by Kesari et al., 2022), D266 and D457 are key active-site residues required for PMX catalytic activity (Kesari et al., 2022). The I252V mutation in Dd2 protein (corresponding to I264V in the 3D7 protein) is proximal to D266 in the active site. According to docking simulation results, FPSA binds within the active site pocket through interaction with I252 and D445 (corresponding to I264 and D457 in the 3D7 protein), a pattern that is similar to the binding between the WM382 and PMX. Given that PMX undergoes auto-activation via sequential self-cleavage of its pro-segment to generate the active enzyme, our findings suggest that FPSA binding may obstruct the salt bridge predicted to form between R244 and D266 (in 3D7 protein), preventing proteolytic auto-activation. Consequently, immature PMX would remain enzymatically inactive, impairing the protein's ability to cleave essential downstream proteins such as SUB1 and PfRh5. This mechanism is consistent with that of verified PMX inhibitor 49c, which has been reported to bind to the D266 residue within the active-site pocket to inhibit PMX activity (Dreyer et al., 2012; Nasamu et al., 2017). On the other hand, the E55G mutation is located in the first loop region of Domain 2 (R28p–K68p) of PMX's pro-segment. This loop forms a twisted structure that interacts with and occludes the mature PMX active-site residues D266 as well as D457 in the 3D7 protein (Kesari et al., 2022). Structural details of this loop region remain limited, making precise docking simulations of FPSA at E55 challenging; however, we hypothesize that the E55G mutation may result in an allosteric mutation that changes the protein confirmation, weaken the interaction between FPSA and PMX active-site and thereby contributing to resistance. However, this hypothesis will need to be confirmed by structural analysis of a co-crystal of PMX (with precise information on the loop structure) and FPSA.

Although no SNVs were identified in the remaining three FPSA-resistant clones, CNVs of the genomic region spanning the PfDd2_080013400 gene were detected. This finding is consistent with a previous report on the PMX inhibitor WM382, where CNVs spanning Pfpmx gene also were observed in WM382-resistant clones (Favuzza et al., 2020; Carmo et al., 2022).

In the present study, we also validated inhibition of PMX-mediated cleavage of substrate peptides by FPSA both in vitro and in parasites (inhibited processing of AMA1 and MSP1). These findings indicate that FPSA functions as an inhibitor of PMX. However, the IC50 of FPSA lies in the micromolar range and is therefore substantially higher than those of previously reported PMX inhibitors, such as WM4 and WM382, which exhibit nanomolar potency (Favuzza et al., 2020). Accordingly, further medicinal chemistry efforts will be required to optimize the chemical structure and improve its activity.

To compare the effects of FPSA and WM382 on gene expression, we analyzed the transcriptomes of P. falciparum parasites exposed to either of these compounds. We found a significant overlap in DEGs between FPSA- and WM382-exposed parasites, at both the trophozoite and schizont stages. Moreover, DEGs shared between the FPSA- and WM382-exposed groups included upregulated genes (e.g., Pfsub2, PF3D7_0301700 (Plasmodium exported protein of unknown function; Pfptp7), and histone acetyltransferase Pfgcn5 (Table S3)) whose expression is associated with stress responses triggered by PMX inhibition. SUB2, another protease activated by PMX cleavage, is essential for the shedding of apical membrane antigen-1 (AMA1), which is required for successful merozoite egress and erythrocyte invasion (Olivieri et al., 2011). PTP7 plays a role in the presentation of Plasmodium-derived protein erythrocyte membrane protein-1 (EMP1) on the surface of infected RBCs, facilitating adhesion to these target cells (Carmo et al., 2022). GCN5 is involved in transcriptional regulation during intraerythrocytic development and is essential for parasite invasion and virulence (Miao et al., 2021). Meanwhile, downregulation and upregulation of some metabolic pathways also were shared between schizonts exposed to either FPSA or WM382, suggesting some similar metabolic regulatory events in response to these two compounds. However, this similarity was restricted, given that transcript levels in other gene sets differed between these two groups (Fig. S6). This observation suggests that the targets of these two compounds do not completely overlap. Specifically, while WM382 is a dual inhibitor of PMX and PMIX, FPSA may target only PMX.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we reported that FPSA, a phenotypic hit compound, exhibited antimalarial activity against asexual blood stages of P. falciparum; furthermore, using genomic analysis of FPSA-resistant clones, we identified PMX as the molecular target of this compound. Although PMX is a well-established target for antimalarial drug development (Baragana et al., 2015; Nasamu et al., 2017; Pino et al., 2017; Cheuka et al., 2020; Favuzza et al., 2020; Hodder et al., 2022; Mukherjee et al., 2023), simultaneous inhibition of PMIX is considered advantageous when targeting PMX, as dual inhibition prevents functional redundancy and presents a higher barrier to resistance (Favuzza et al., 2020). FPSA introduces a novel chemical scaffold targeting the active-site pocket of PMX. Further efforts should focus on modifying this compound to target PMIX as well. This finding is expected to contribute to future drug development efforts aimed at PMX inhibition and expands the range of potential drug combination therapies for malaria treatment.
