Abstract
. Diversity maximization problem is a well-studied problem where the goal is to find k k diverse items. Fair diversity maximization aims to select a diverse subset of k k items from a large dataset, while requiring that each group of items be well represented in the output. More formally, given a set of items with labels, our goal is to find k k items that maximize the minimum pairwise distance in the set, while maintaining that each label is represented within some budget. In many cases, one is only interested in selecting a handful (say a constant) number of items from each group. In such scenario we show that a randomized algorithm based on padded decompositions improves the state-of-the-art approximation ratio to log ⁡ ( m ) / ( 3 ​ m ) \sqrt{\log(m)}/(3m) , where m m is the number of labels. The algorithms work in several stages: ( i i ) a preprocessing pruning which ensures that points with the same label are far away from each other, ( i ​ i ii ) a decomposition phase, where points are randomly placed in clusters such that there is a feasible solution with maximum one point per cluster and that any feasible solution will be diverse, ( i ​ i ​ i ) (iii) assignment phase, where clusters are assigned to labels, and a representative point with the corresponding label is selected from each cluster. We experimentally verify the effectiveness of our algorithm on large datasets.

Techniques.

Our algorithm
Breach
uses a similar high-level approach as
(
Addanki et al. 2022
)
and
(
Moumoulidou et al. 2020
)
. The idea is to construct well-separated clusters that are far apart, from which we select at most one point per cluster.

Moumoulidou et al. 2020
construct a graph where each connected component corresponds to such a cluster. Our approach also constructs a graph, but we further decompose each connected component by using Calinescu-Karloff-Rabani (CKR) random partitions
(
Calinescu et al. 2005
)
. From the obtained decomposition, we remove all
guard
vertices (certain boundary points) which ensures the final clusters are disconnected and thus diverse. We bound the probability that a fair solution is present in the obtained decomposition.
Our flow procedure from Section
3.3
will find such a feasible solution, if it exists. By repeating the algorithm enough times, we find a solution with good probability.

Related Work

MMD and MSD.
The most fundamental problem settings for diversity maximization assume the input is given by a metric space
(V,d)
with nonnegative distance function
d:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and an integer
k
.
The goal is to select a set
S\subseteq V
of fixed size
|S|=k
which maximizes a certain objective function.

The two most popular objective functions are
max-sum diversification
(MSD) and
max-min diversification
(MMD).
MSD maximizes the sum of the pairwise distances in
S
(also known as
remote-clique
), while MMD maximizes the minimum pairwise distances in
S
(also known as
remote-edge
or
p
-dispersion
).
It has been argued that the MMD objective is more suited for tasks like summarization and feature selection, because MSD has a tendency to select outliers and include highly similar items, whereas MMD covers the data range more uniformly
(
Wang et al. 2023b
;
Moumoulidou et al. 2020
)
.
In this work, we focus on MMD.

The classic MMD problem asks for a set of
k
items under the max-min objective, without any fairness constraints.
Both MMD and MSD are shown to be
\mathbf{NP}
-complete by
(
Erkut 1990
)
and
(
Wang and Kuo 1988
)
.

Tamir 1991
and
Ravi et al. 1994
showed that the greedy “next-furthest-point-strategy” yields a
\frac{1}{2}
-approximation for MMD, which is the well-known GMM algorithm.
The GMM algorithm is identical to the Gonzalez-heuristic
(
Gonzalez 1985
)
which gives a 2-approximation for the
k
-center problem.
Ravi et al. 1994
showed that for any
\epsilon>0
MMD is
\mathbf{NP}
-hard to approximate within a factor of
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
, thereby showing the optimality of the Gonzalez-heuristic in terms of worst-case performance.
Additionally,
Ravi et al. 1994
showed that MMD without the triangle inequality constraints is
\mathbf{NP}
-hard to approximate within any multiplicative factor.
Amagata 2023
consider MMD in the presence of outliers.
Kumpulainen et al. 2024
have studied the MMD problem with asymmetric distances between the points, while still retaining the directed triangle inequality. They provided a
\frac{1}{6k}
approximation for this asymmetric generalization.
MMD has also been studied in the dynamic setting
(
Amagata and Hara 2016
;
Drosou and Pitoura 2013
)
.

Fair MSD.
Fairness for the MSD objective is well-understood and admits a
1/2-\epsilon
guarantee via local search algorithms in polynomial time
(
Abbassi et al. 2013
;
Borodin et al. 2017
)
.
This is in contrast with the fair MMD problem formulations, which appear to be more difficult to approximate.
Attaining a ratio larger than
1/2
for fair MSD is hard under the planted clique hypothesis
(
Bhaskara et al. 2016
)
.
Most authors provide results for general (partition) matroids constraints
(
Bhaskara et al. 2016
;
Borodin et al. 2017
;
Abbassi et al. 2013
;
Ceccarello et al. 2018
)
, which includes the fairness case when exactly
k_{i}
items are required from each color
(
Moumoulidou et al. 2020
;
Addanki et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023a
)
.

Cevallos et al. 2017
extend the setting to distances of negative type and also use local search to design a
\mathcal{O}(1-1/k)
approximation.

Fair MMD.
The first to incorporate fairness constraints into the MMD problem was
Moumoulidou et al. 2020
.
They study a variant of
FMMD
which considers exact size group fairness constraints (see Section
1
).

Moumoulidou et al. 2020
provided a
\frac{1}{4}
approximation for the case of
m=2
colors, a
\frac{1}{5}
approximation for the case where
m=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\sum_{i}k_{i}=o(\log n)
, and a
\frac{1}{3m-1}
approximation for the general case.
This was subsequently improved to the current state-of-the-art
\frac{1}{m+1}
approximation guarantee by
Addanki et al. 2022
.
The authors also showed a
\frac{1}{2}
approximation using an LP-based randomized rounding method, but which only satisfies the fairness constraints in expectation.
Using concentration bounds—which requires that each
k_{i}
is large enough—the same randomized rounding method can be used to obtain a
\frac{1}{6}
approximation while guaranteeing that
(1-\epsilon)k_{i}
points are selected from each color.

Specifically for the case for Euclidean metrics,
Addanki et al. 2022
proved that the problem can be solved exactly in one dimension.
For constant dimensions, they give a
1+\epsilon
approximation that runs in time
\mathcal{O}(nk)+2^{\mathcal{O}(k)})
where
k=\sum_{i}k_{i}
.
They showed how to reduce the running time to a polynomial in
k
at the expense of only picking
(1-\epsilon)k_{i}
points from each color.
The running time was recently improved by
Kurkure et al. 2024
to linear in
n
and
k
using the multiplicative weight update on an associated linear program in combination with geometric data structures.
The exact-size
FMMD
variant has also been studied in the context of streaming and sliding window models
(
Wang et al. 2022
;
Addanki et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023a
)
.

The (general)
FMMD
problem (Problem
1
) was introduced by
Wang et al. 2023b
.
They propose an exact algorithm with runtime
\mathcal{O}(n^{k}\log n)
, by reducing it to the problem of finding a maximum independent set in an undirected graph (subject to fairness constraints), which is solved by an integer linear program.
Additionally, using coreset constructions they obtain a
\frac{1-\epsilon}{5}
-approximation that runs in exponential time. They empirically show their algorithm scales to large datasets when
m
and
k
are not too large.

We should note that

Moumoulidou et al. 2020
also argued that their algorithm extends to (partition) matroid constraints. However, since the
FMMD
fairness constraints do not have matroid structure, the algorithms by
Addanki et al. 2022
and
Moumoulidou et al. 2020
are not directly applicable to the
FMMD
problem.

Interestingly, the best lower bounds for approximating these fair MMD problem formulations are still identical to bounds for unconstrained MMD. There remains a large gap between the polynomial time approximation ratios from Table
1
and the
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
lower bound.

when

.
This assumption includes cases where one requires at most a constant number of items from each color.
Such fairness is closely related to finding an

3.
Algorithm

Our algorithm consists of 3 phases:

(i)
Given a universe
V
we select a subset
U
of
V
such that
U
still has an approximate solution and any two points in
U
of the same color are far away from each other.

(ii)
We randomly partition
U
in clusters such that the points of a feasible solution, with a certain probability, will be in their own clusters and the points from different clusters are far away.

(iii)
We use maximum flow to assign clusters to colors, and extract points of those colors from the clusters. It follows that, if the second step was successful, the resulting set will satisfy the constraints.

We present two variants of our algorithm: a slower version with a better guarantee and a faster version with a worse guarantee. The difference between the two algorithms is the preprocessing step.
We assume that we know the optimal score
OPT
.
In Section
3.4
we use grid search to find an (approximate) guess for
OPT
.

Assuming that we can compute the distance
d(u,v)
in unit time, the running time of
\mathit{\textsc{Preprocess}}\mathopen{}\left(V,d,2\tau/5,k\right)
is in
\mathcal{O}(nkm)
. The resulting set contains
\mathcal{O}(km)
points.

3.2.
Random decomposition of the pruned space

Our next goal is to decompose
U
into clusters that are far enough from each other and that a feasible solution
O
will have at most one point in each cluster.

Assume that we have guessed the score of the optimal solution in
U
, say
\gamma
. Now let
\alpha=\sqrt{\log(m)}/m
, and define a graph
G=(U,E)
, consisting of the points and edges
(u,v)
for which
d(u,v)<\gamma\alpha
.

Denote
d_{G}(u,v)
to be the shortest path distance in
G
between two points
u,v
. Let

B_{G}(u,R)=\left\{v\in U\mid d_{G}(u,v)\leq R\right\}

be the points in
G
with shortest path distance of at most
R
from
u
.

Let
{\pi}:{[n]}\to{U}
be a random permutation of
U
. Let us define
\Delta_{1}=\max({\left\lfloor 1/(4\alpha)\right\rfloor},1)
and
\Delta_{2}={\left\lfloor 1/(2\alpha)\right\rfloor}
. It is easy to verify that
\Delta_{1}\leq\Delta_{2}
for
m\geq 1
. Select
R
uniformly between
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
.

To define the decomposition we first partition
U
into clusters
C_{j}
for
j\in[n]
. Each
C_{j}\subseteq B_{G}(\pi(j),R)
consists of those points in
B_{G}(\pi(j),R)
that have not been included in any
B_{G}(\pi(i),R)
, where
i<j
. Note that some
C_{j}
might be empty.

This step is known as a Calinescu-Karloff-Rabani (CKR) random partition of a metric space
(
Calinescu et al. 2005
)
, with a slight parameter modification to fit our needs.
CKR partitions play a pivotal role in metric embedding theory
(
Fakcharoenphol et al. 2004
;
Mendel and Naor 2006
)
.

Our final decomposition is
D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
, where
D_{j}
contains vertices in
C_{j}
that have
d_{G}(\pi(j),u)<R
. The vertices in
C_{j}\setminus D_{j}
are refered to as
guard
vertices and are removed from the graph.

The algorithm is given in Algorithm
2
.

The following proposition states that with a certain probability a fair solution can be found in clusters
D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
.

Proposition 4.

Assume
(U,d)
with
m
colors,
U=U_{1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{m}
. Assume
\gamma>0
such that there is
O\subseteq U
with
\mathit{div}\mathopen{}\left(O\right)\geq\gamma
. Moreover, assume that for any
u,v\in U_{i}
, it holds that
d(u,v)\geq\gamma
. If
k\leq m
, then with probability of
\Omega(1/m)
,

\mathit{\textsc{Decompose}}\mathopen{}\left(U,d,\gamma\right)
yields
\left\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}\right\}

such that
{\left|O\cap D_{i}\right|}\leq 1
for every
i
and
O\subseteq\bigcup{D_{i}}
.

Proof.

Adopt the notation used in Algorithm
2
.
Let
u,v\in C_{j}
. Note by definition, the shortest path from
u
to
v
in
G
is at most
2\Delta_{2}
. Due to the triangle inequality,
d(u,v)<\gamma
. This immediately implies that
u
and
v
must have different colors, and that
{\left|C_{j}\cap O\right|}\leq 1
.

Next we bound the probability that
O
does not have any guards.

First note that since
\alpha\leq 1/2
for any
m
, we have
\Delta_{2}\geq 1
, which guarantees
\Delta_{1}\leq\Delta_{2}
.
Let
o\in O
. A known result, see, for example, Lemma 1.2 by
Har-Peled and Rahul 2018
, states that

p(o\text{ is a guard})\leq\frac{1}{\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{1}+1}\log\frac{{\left|B_{G}(o,\Delta_{2})\right|}}{{\left|B_{G}(o,\Delta_{1}-1)\right|}}\quad.

Note that

\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{1}+1={\left\lfloor 1/(2\alpha)\right\rfloor}-\max({\left\lfloor 1/(4\alpha)\right\rfloor},1)+1\geq 1/(4\alpha).

Let
u,v\in B_{G}(o,\Delta_{2})
. The shortest path from
u
to
v
in
G
is at most
2\Delta_{2}
. Due to the triangle inequality,
d(u,v)<\gamma
. Therefore,
u
and
v
must have different colors, and consequently,
{\left|B_{G}(o,\Delta_{2})\right|}\leq m
.

If
o
is in a connected component, say
W
, of
G
with less than
\Delta_{1}
vertices, then
W
has no guards, and therefore
o
cannot be a guard. Assume
{\left|W\right|}\geq\Delta_{1}
. Then
{\left|B_{G}(o,\Delta_{1}-1)\right|}\geq\Delta_{1}\geq 1/(4\alpha)
.

In summary, if we
let
\omega=4\alpha\log(4m\alpha)
, then

p(o\text{ is a guard})\leq\omega\quad.

Note that
o
being a guard depends solely on the order of nodes in
B_{G}(o,\Delta_{2})
. Let
o,o^{\prime}\in O
be two distinct nodes. Then since
B_{G}(o,\Delta_{2})
and
B_{G}(o^{\prime},\Delta_{2})
are disjoint, the event of
o
being a guard is independent of
o^{\prime}
being a guard. Thus

p(O\text{ not having guards})=\prod_{o\in O}p(o\text{ is not a guard})\leq(1-\omega)^{k}\quad.

There is a constant
M
such that for any
m\geq M
, it holds that

\omega<1
,
1-\omega<1/2
, and
\log m\geq 2\omega m
. For such
m
, we have

\begin{split}(1-\omega)^{k}&\geq(1-\omega)^{m}\geq\exp\left(\frac{-\omega m}{1-\omega}\right)\\
&\geq\exp\left(-2\omega m\right)=m^{-2\omega m/\log m}\geq 1/m,\\
\end{split}

where the first inequality uses the assumption
k\leq m
. ∎

Next we show that the clusters
D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
are far away from each other.

Proposition 5.

Let
\left\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}\right\}
be given by
\mathit{\textsc{Decompose}}\mathopen{}\left(U,d,\gamma\right)
. Let
S
be any set for which
{\left|S\cap D_{i}\right|}\leq 1
for every
D_{i}
, then
\mathit{div}\mathopen{}\left(S\right)\geq\gamma\alpha
.

Proof.

Adopt the notation used in Algorithm
2
.
Assume a cross-edge
(u,v)
in
G
with
u\in C_{i}
and
v\in C_{j}
where
i<j
. Then
u
must be a guard as otherwise
v\in C_{i}
.
Thus, the clusters without the guards are disconnected. Assume now that
u\in D_{i}
and
v\in D_{j}
where
i<j
, then
d(u,v)\geq\gamma\alpha
.
∎

Let us now look at the computational complexity of
Decompose
. The bottleneck of the algorithm is computing
B_{G}(\pi(j),R)
.
Computing
B_{G}
independently leads to a running time of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
. We can speed-up computing
B_{G}(\pi(j),R)

by ignoring the nodes

that have shorter distance
d_{G}(\pi(i),v)
than the distance
d_{G}(\pi(j),v)
for some
i<j
. Then each node is only visited
\mathcal{O}(R)
times, leading to computational complexity
\mathcal{O}(Rn^{2})\subseteq\mathcal{O}(mn^{2})
.
In practice, a naive approach is reasonable.

The algorithm first preprocesses the space using

Both propositions require
\tau
, the optimal score. A naive option would be to test all existing distances, multiplying the running time by a factor of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
. A faster alternative option is to do a grid search by considering candidates of form
\tau_{i}=\tau_{0}(1+\epsilon)^{i}
, where
\tau_{0}
is a distance between any two points and
i
is an integer. We can lower bound
i
as we know that if
\tau_{i}
is the smallest non-zero possible distance, then
Breach
will find a solution. On the other hand, if
\tau_{i}
yields
\gamma_{2}
that is larger than the largest distance in the pruned space, then there is no solution. Let
\rho
be the ratio of the largest distance and the smallest non-zero distance in

\mathcal{O}(\log_{1+\epsilon}\rho)\subseteq\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}\log\rho)

This comes at a cost to the quality of the solution by a factor of

, proving the approximation results stated in Table

.

The

regime.

We can extend

to work with

by adding

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\log(m^{\prime})}/(3m^{\prime}))
, where
m^{\prime}=\max(m,k)
. Moreover, the running times of the two algorithms change to

\mathcal{O}(Tmn^{2}m^{\prime})

and

\mathcal{O}(kmn+Tm^{2}k^{2}m^{\prime})
.

Alternatively, we can obtain the original guarantees, but with a slower algorithm. To this end, note that
Proposition
4
proves a success probability of
\Omega(1/m)
for a single repetition of line 3 in Algorithm
4
, in case
k\leq m
. Assume that
k
is proportional to
m
, say
k\leq\beta m
for some
\beta\geq 1
. Proposition
4
would then yield a success probability of
\Omega(1/m^{\beta})
.
Therefore, Algorithm
4
(line 3) would need
T\times m^{\beta}
repetitions to ensure that Algorithm
4
succeeds with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(T)}
(see Propositions
7
and
8
). The running time of such algorithm is polynomial only when
\beta
remains a constant.

3.5.
Practical improvements

In order to improve the quality of the solution in practice, we do the following additional steps.

During preprocessing we do not treat colors individually. Instead we always select the point of a color that we need that is furthest from already added points. This increases the preprocessing time by
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
due to sort.

When forming
G
in
Decompose
it turns out that the threshold
\gamma_{2}\alpha
is too small, resulting in subpar results. To combat this we vary
\gamma_{2}
independently, instead of having it fixed to
\tau
. More specifically, we vary
\gamma_{2}
between
\gamma_{1}/2
and
\gamma_{1}/\alpha
using a similar
(1+\epsilon)
grid as with
\tau
.
This increases the running time of the last two steps by
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}\log\alpha)\subseteq\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}\log m)
.

Finally, we found out that in practice
Breach
yields good results after only a few iterations. Thus in experiments, we perform
Decompose
only a constant number of times.

when compared to the other baselines. This is mainly due to two reasons. First, our prepossessing step is repeated for each optimal guess
\gamma
(Section
3.2
), while
FairFlow
only does a greedy preprocessing once to obtain
km
nodes, independent of the guess.
Secondly, the CKR partitions from the decomposition phase (Section
3.2
)
require a BFS search for each of the
km
nodes. This is rather costly for increasing values of
m
.
Nonetheless,
Breach
achieves better diversity scores than the other polynomial time approximation algorithms
FairFlow
and
GreedyFlow
, and is slightly worse than
Core
.

Core
is tailored for performance and scalability to large datasets (by using fast ILP solvers), but has exponential running time in the worst case.

5.
Conclusions

We proposed a novel randomized approximation algorithm for
FMMD
called
Breach
.
Our algorithm uses random CKR partitions to find well-separated clusters and a novel maximum flow procedure to extract feasible solutions.
We theoretically prove our algorithm outperforms existing methods in a regime of few representatives per color, and also give a guarantee for the general problem.
In practice, our algorithm outperforms all other polynomial time approximation algorithms, while achieving slightly worse diversity score than the best exponential time algorithm.
A direction for future work is to further narrow down the gap between the
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
inapproxabimility lower bound and the approximation ratios from Table
1
.
