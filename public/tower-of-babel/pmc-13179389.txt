LaED: a novel lightweight, edge-aware and explainable deep learning model for privacy-preserving facial attendance tracking in resource-constrained educational environments
Authors: Esther Omolara Abiodun, Oludare Isaac Abiodun, Moatsum Alawida, Bayan Abu Shawar, Abid Mehmood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Facial recognition is increasingly adopted for automated classroom attendance; however, real-world deployment in schools remains constrained by privacy risks, ethical obligations, demographic bias, spoofing threats, and limited computational resources. Recent incidents involving Microsoft Teams in New South Wales in 2025 and Chelmer Valley High School in the United Kingdom show how poorly governed systems violate student rights and regulatory compliance. Despite growing adoption, many existing attendance systems focus narrowly on recognition accuracy or efficiency, while overlooking spoof resistance, open-set identity handling, fairness mitigation, auditability, and privacy protection. This paper presents LaED, a lightweight, edge-aware, and explainable deep learning framework for privacy-preserving classroom attendance in resource-constrained educational environments. The framework combines multimodal spoof detection, open-set facial recognition, and fairness-aware representation learning within a unified edge-based design. Spoofing attacks, including replay and deepfake attempts, are mitigated through the fusion of physiological and temporal facial cues, while unknown identities are explicitly rejected to reduce proxy attendance. To support responsible deployment, LaED incorporates federated learning with differential privacy, ensuring that biometric data remain local to schools while enabling accountable model updates. Experimental evaluation on CASIA-FASD, CelebA-Spoof, DFDC, FairFace, and a consent-driven classroom dataset shows that LaED achieves over 97.8% recognition accuracy, APCER and BPCER values below 2%, demographic fairness gaps under 2%, and inference latency below 150 milliseconds on edge hardware. Additional tests confirm reliable operation under realistic classroom conditions. These results demonstrate that regulation-aligned and trustworthy facial attendance is feasible on low-cost devices, offering a practical pathway for responsible biometric AI in education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42051-8.

Introduction

Facial recognition technologies have rapidly gained traction for automating administrative tasks across diverse domains, including law enforcement, border control, healthcare, and education1–3. In classroom contexts, automated attendance systems are promoted as a means to reduce manual errors, save instructional time, and enable real-time reporting for administrators. These perceived advantages have driven experimentation with face-recognition-based attendance platforms in universities, secondary schools, and corporate training programs. As education systems become increasingly digitized and resource constrained, particularly in developing regions, lightweight and real-time solutions are often favored for their efficiency and potential scalability4.

Despite these benefits, the deployment of facial recognition systems in classrooms has raised significant concerns related to privacy, legality, fairness, and accountability. This scrutiny has intensified following several global incidents5,6. Recent incidents, including unauthorized biometric data collection associated with Microsoft Teams in New South Wales in 2025 and regulatory action against Chelmer Valley High School in the United Kingdom, demonstrate how poorly governed systems can violate student rights and fail to meet regulatory compliance7–10. These cases highlight the sensitivity of biometric technologies in educational environments, where users are often minors, regulatory requirements are stringent, and institutional capacity for oversight may be limited. Consequently, classroom attendance systems must satisfy not only performance requirements but also ethical and legal expectations.

Although prior research has explored automated attendance using facial recognition, several practical limitations remain unresolved. Existing systems typically emphasize recognition accuracy, tracking stability, or computational efficiency, while addressing spoof resistance, open-set identity handling, demographic fairness, auditability, and privacy preservation only in isolation1,2,11–13. Many approaches further rely on assumptions that do not hold in real classrooms, such as cooperative subjects, static capture conditions, closed-set identities, or centralized processing pipelines. As a result, these systems remain vulnerable to proxy attendance, spoofing attacks, privacy leakage, and biased outcomes when deployed in realistic educational settings.

In this paper, we present LaED, a lightweight, edge-aware, and explainable deep learning framework designed for privacy-preserving classroom attendance tracking in resource-constrained environments. The framework is motivated by real-world classroom conditions and education-specific biometric threat models, with the aim of enabling robust attendance verification while supporting fairness, transparency, and lawful data governance.

Related studies

Research on facial-recognition-based attendance systems has evolved along two complementary dimensions. The first concerns system-level design choices, including tracking-based pipelines, lightweight and liveness-enabled solutions, and privacy-aware biometric frameworks. The second focuses on the deep learning architectures used for facial feature extraction and recognition, ranging from classical convolutional models to transfer learning and transformer-based approaches. While both strands provide valuable insights, their limitations constrain deployment in real classroom environments. The following subsections review these themes and identify unresolved challenges relevant to educational settings.

Tracking-oriented approaches

Tracking-based methods aim to improve recognition stability by maintaining identity continuity across consecutive frames. Bugingo et al.1 demonstrated that real-time tracking reduces identity-switch errors in dynamic classroom settings, with similar benefits reported in surveillance and workplace analytics14,15. However, most tracking pipelines focus primarily on temporal consistency and do not defend against modern presentation attacks such as replayed videos or deepfakes16. In addition, privacy-preserving safeguards are typically absent, leaving continuous video streams vulnerable to misuse. Consequently, tracking alone is insufficient for secure and compliant classroom deployment.

Lightweight and liveness-based solutions

A second line of research emphasizes real-time attendance on low-cost or edge hardware. Surantha and Sugijakko2, Nguyen et al.17, and Al-Waisy et al.18 showed that lightweight CNNs can support efficient classroom deployment. Despite these advantages, many lightweight systems rely on simple liveness cues such as eye blinking or texture analysis, which are vulnerable to three-dimensional masks and deepfake attacks19. Open-set recognition and demographic fairness controls are also rarely addressed, exposing such systems to proxy attendance and subgroup performance disparities20.

Privacy and trust-oriented biometrics

Beyond recognition accuracy, recent studies have explored privacy-aware biometric frameworks. Federated learning enables local model training without sharing raw facial images21, while differential privacy mitigates the risk of reconstructing biometric features from model updates22. Tamper-evident audit logs and blockchain-based mechanisms have been proposed to enhance accountability23,24. Although promising, these techniques are seldom integrated into classroom attendance systems and are often evaluated independently of spoof detection, fairness mitigation, or operational constraints. Real-world incidents, including unauthorized biometric data collection in New South Wales and regulatory action in the United Kingdom, highlight the risks of deploying facial recognition without comprehensive governance.

Deep learning architectures for facial recognition

The effectiveness of attendance systems is also shaped by the underlying model architectures. Classical CNNs such as VGG-Face, ResNet, and MobileNet provide efficient feature extraction but are sensitive to occlusion, lighting variation, and sophisticated presentation attacks25,26. Transfer learning approaches, including FaceNet and ArcFace, improve recognition accuracy through discriminative embeddings27,28, while MagFace and AdaFace further enhance robustness by linking embedding quality to image characteristics29,30. However, these models often rely on heavy pretrained backbones and closed-set assumptions, limiting their suitability for edge deployment and safe handling of unknown identities. Transformer-based models such as MobileViT, Swin Transformer, and Tiny-ViT improve global context modeling and robustness under real-world variations31,32, yet they rarely integrate liveness detection, fairness calibration, or privacy-preserving training.

Comparative analysis of CNN, transfer learning and transformer methods

CNN, transfer learning, and transformer architectures each contribute distinct strengths to facial recognition systems. CNNs offer computational efficiency but struggle with global context and spoof resistance. Transfer learning improves accuracy but increases computational demands and provides limited support for open-set recognition or fairness analysis. Transformer models demonstrate improved robustness and generalizability, although they often omit privacy safeguards, demographic calibration, and explainability components. Recent work on explainable AI and privacy-preserving biometrics, including Grad-CAM, attention rollout, federated learning, and differential privacy, has improved transparency and accountability33–36, but these techniques are typically applied in isolation rather than within integrated attendance systems.

Summary and research gap

Existing studies advance facial-recognition-based attendance through improvements in tracking stability, computational efficiency, recognition accuracy, or privacy protection. However, these advances are fragmented across system-level designs and architectural choices as detailed in Fig. 1. Most existing approaches do not simultaneously address spoof resistance, open-set identity handling, demographic fairness, explainability, privacy preservation, and auditability under realistic classroom conditions. This fragmentation, together with documented regulatory failures and ethical concerns, highlights a persistent gap in the literature and motivates the need for integrated frameworks suitable for trustworthy deployment in educational environments.

System architecture of LaED

LaED is organized as a three-layer architecture consisting of a Perception Layer, an Identity Layer, and a Trust Layer. Each layer performs a distinct functional role in the attendance pipeline, and together they form an edge-deployable system suitable for classroom environments. The architecture is designed so that input validation, identity recognition, and governance functions are handled sequentially, with clearly defined data flows between layers.

Perception layer

The Perception Layer processes raw visual input captured from classroom cameras and determines whether the input is suitable for identity recognition. Its primary function is to validate incoming facial data and remove manipulated or low-quality inputs before further processing.

rPPG-based liveness detection

Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) is applied to extract subtle skin color variations associated with blood flow. These physiological signals are computed in parallel with face detection and are used to distinguish live subjects from static images, replayed videos, or synthetically generated faces.

Deepfake and replay detection

The Perception Layer includes convolutional filters trained to detect temporal and texture-level artifacts commonly observed in replay attacks and deepfake content, such as abnormal blinking patterns, inconsistent reflections, and spatial distortions. Inputs flagged as manipulated are discarded at this stage.

Noise reduction and preprocessing

Validated facial inputs are normalized, compressed, and denoised to reduce illumination variation and background interference. This step ensures that only standardized and computationally efficient representations are forwarded to the Identity Layer.

Identity Layer

The Identity Layer performs facial representation learning, identity matching, and decision control. It receives validated facial inputs from the Perception Layer and outputs identity decisions together with confidence and fairness-adjusted scores.

Lightweight transformer backbone

A Tiny Vision Transformer (Tiny-ViT) serves as the feature extraction backbone. The model uses self-attention to emphasize salient facial regions while suppressing irrelevant background information. Attention-based and multi-scale fusion strategies similar to those reported in recent literature have been shown to improve robustness under real-world variation37.

Open-set recognition

Identity matching is performed using an open-set recognition mechanism. Instead of enforcing closed-set classification, the system applies confidence-based thresholding derived from embedding similarity. Inputs that do not meet acceptance criteria are labeled as unknown and excluded from attendance records.

Fairness calibration

The Identity Layer incorporates fairness-aware calibration during training and update cycles. Embedding distributions are periodically adjusted to reduce performance disparities across demographic subgroups, using fairness-aware loss constraints applied during federated updates.

Trust layer

The Trust Layer governs how identity decisions are stored, updated, and audited. It ensures that attendance records are managed in a privacy-preserving and accountable manner.

Federated learning with differential privacy

Model updates are trained locally on edge devices and aggregated using a federated learning protocol. Differential privacy noise is applied to model updates prior to aggregation, limiting the risk of reconstructing biometric information from transmitted parameters.

Tamper-evident audit logs

Each attendance decision is recorded in a tamper-evident audit log constructed using cryptographic hash chaining. These logs enable post hoc verification of attendance records by authorized parties.

Consent and compliance management

The Trust Layer maintains consent records, enforces data retention policies, and supports automated deletion of expired biometric data. These mechanisms regulate access to attendance records in accordance with applicable data protection requirements.

Integrated workflow

The three layers operate sequentially as an integrated workflow. Facial inputs are first validated by the Perception Layer, then processed by the Identity Layer for recognition and decision control, and finally recorded and governed by the Trust Layer. This separation of responsibilities allows each layer to operate independently while supporting a coherent end-to-end attendance pipeline. Methodology

This section describes the methodological components of LaED and how they are implemented. The framework consists of five methodological pillars covering spoof and deepfake detection, lightweight recognition with open-set handling, privacy-preserving learning, fairness calibration, and verifiable logging. Each component is designed to operate under edge-deployment constraints while supporting robustness, equity, and accountability.

Spoof and deepfake detection

LaED implements a multi-stream mechanism for detecting presentation attacks and synthetic facial inputs. Two complementary feature streams are used: physiological signal analysis and temporal artifact detection.

Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) is applied to short facial video sequences to extract skin color variations associated with blood flow. These signals are computed from temporally aligned skin-region pixels and summarized using frequency-domain and consistency measures. rPPG extraction operates in parallel with face detection and does not introduce additional inference latency.

In parallel, a lightweight deepfake detection module based on a Tiny Vision Transformer is trained using selected samples from the DFDC dataset. The detector focuses on temporal inconsistencies such as abnormal blinking patterns, lip-motion irregularities, and local texture artifacts. Outputs from the rPPG and deepfake streams are fused to determine whether an input is bona fide. Only validated inputs are forwarded to the recognition stage. This design addresses spoofing vulnerabilities reported in tracking-based systems1 and limitations of basic liveness checks used in portable attendance solutions2.

Lightweight recognition with open-set thresholding

Identity recognition is performed using a compact Tiny Vision Transformer backbone optimized for edge deployment. Facial embeddings are extracted from normalized input frames and encoded into a low-dimensional latent space. The self-attention mechanism enables efficient modeling of global facial structure while maintaining a small parameter footprint. Similar attention-based feature fusion strategies have demonstrated improved robustness in recent studies37.

LaED employs an open-set recognition strategy based on cosine similarity. Instead of enforcing closed-set classification, the system applies a confidence threshold derived from the statistical distribution of embeddings. Inputs that fall outside the acceptance boundary are labeled as unknown and excluded from attendance records. This mechanism prevents forced classification of unregistered individuals and supports dynamic classroom environments.

Privacy-preserving federated learning

Model training and updates are conducted using a federated learning protocol. Each classroom node performs local training on encrypted datasets, and raw facial images or embeddings are not transmitted. Only gradient updates are shared with the aggregation server. To mitigate reconstruction risks, differential privacy noise is added to gradients prior to transmission38.

Federated aggregation is implemented using compressed gradient exchange and adaptive communication rounds to accommodate bandwidth constraints. This configuration enables distributed learning on resource-limited devices while preserving data locality. Details of large-scale federated simulations and hierarchical aggregation are provided in Appendix 2.

Fairness calibration

Fairness calibration is incorporated directly into the training objective. During evaluation, predictions are disaggregated by age, gender, and skin tone to monitor subgroup performance. A fairness-aware regularization term constrains subgroup embedding centroids to remain aligned with the global latent space.

The overall loss is defined as.

L = LCE + λLfair,

where LCE denotes the identity classification loss and λ controls the strength of fairness regularization. To justify the choice of λ, we conducted systematic validation experiments with λ ∈ {0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.30} using a held-out validation split derived from the FairFace dataset and the in-house classroom dataset. For each value, we evaluated overall recognition accuracy, subgroup false rejection rates, and the maximum demographic performance gap.

Lower values (λ < 0.10) provided insufficient mitigation of demographic disparities, while higher values (λ ≥ 0.20) led to over-regularization of the embedding space and degraded inter-class separability. The selected value λ = 0.15 consistently achieved the best balance, reducing demographic fairness gaps by approximately 18–22% relative to λ = 0, while incurring less than 1% absolute accuracy loss. Performance remained stable for λ values in the range 0.10–0.20, indicating that the model is not highly sensitive to small variations around the chosen value39–41.

Verifiable logging and audit trails

Each attendance decision is recorded as a transaction containing timestamps, recognition confidence, liveness scores, and open-set outcomes. Records are appended to a tamper-evident audit log using cryptographic hash chaining. This enables post hoc verification of attendance records and detection of unauthorized modification.

Audit entries are linked with consent identifiers to support traceability and compliance. Regulatory mappings for GDPR, FERPA, and child data protection requirements are provided in Table 14 (see Sect. 7.4.1: Regulatory Mapping and Compliance).

Implementation and training setup

This subsection details implementation settings to support reproducibility and deployment transparency.

Module interaction and conflict resolution streams

Identity embeddings, rPPG liveness scores, and deepfake-artifact confidence values are calibrated to a common 0–1 scale. Decision-level fusion applies rule-guided conflict resolution. If liveness or deepfake confidence falls below safety thresholds, the sample is rejected regardless of identity similarity. Ambiguous cases result in deferred re-capture rather than forced classification.

Data preprocessing

Faces are detected using MTCNN and aligned via similarity transforms. Inputs are normalized to 112 × 112 pixels and enhanced using CLAHE. Training augmentations include horizontal flipping, Gaussian blur, brightness and contrast jitter, and random occlusion. rPPG signals are extracted using a 0.7–4.0 Hz bandpass filter and smoothed with a five-frame window.

Model architecture

LaED uses a Tiny-ViT with 12 transformer blocks, four attention heads per block, a 256-dimensional embedding, and approximately 6.8 million parameters. Positional encoding and residual connections stabilize training and preserve spatial structure.

Training procedure

Training uses Adam optimization with a learning rate of 1e-4, batch size 32, 40 epochs, and early stopping. Federated learning is implemented using FedAvg with five clients and aggregation every two epochs. Differential privacy is applied using Opacus with noise multiplier σ = 0.75 and clipping norm C = 1.0, yielding ε ≈ 8.2 at δ = 10⁻⁵.

Feature extraction and fusion

Three feature streams are extracted: identity embeddings, rPPG-based physiological features, and deepfake-artifact features. Decision-level fusion combines scores as.

S = 0.55Sid+0.25SrPPG+0.20Sdf.

Safety overrides are applied when spoof indicators dominate.

Hardware and software environment

Experiments were conducted on NVIDIA Jetson Nano and Raspberry Pi 4 devices, with pretraining on an RTX 2080 Ti workstation. The software stack includes Ubuntu 20.04, PyTorch 2.1, CUDA 11.4, TensorRT 8.5, and Opacus. Figure 3 presents a high-level overview of the complete processing workflow.

Experimental setup

The experimental evaluation of LaED was designed to assess recognition accuracy, robustness to spoofing and deepfakes, fairness across demographic groups, and privacy-aware deployment feasibility. Experiments were conducted using multiple datasets, baseline systems, hardware platforms, and evaluation metrics to reflect realistic classroom deployment conditions.

Datasets

The system was evaluated using a combination of well-established benchmark datasets and a custom classroom dataset created under consent-driven conditions. This hybrid approach ensures that the framework is tested against both synthetic challenges and authentic, real-world constraints.

The first category of datasets focuses on presentation attacks. CASIA-FASD was used as a primary benchmark because of its inclusion of diverse spoofing modalities such as printed photos, replayed videos, and masks. These controlled conditions allow a careful measurement of the performance of the rPPG-based liveness module. CelebA-Spoof was also incorporated, as it contains hundreds of thousands of annotated images and reflects a much wider range of spoof types, including make-up attacks and digital manipulations. Its diversity helps to confirm whether LaED generalizes across spoofing strategies that differ significantly in execution.

Deepfake detection was evaluated with selected subsets of the DeepFake Detection Challenge dataset. This collection is among the largest and most challenging benchmarks for manipulated videos, and by using carefully curated partitions we ensured that the Tiny-ViT model embedded within LaED was trained and tested against the kinds of manipulations that are likely to appear in uncontrolled environments. To measure fairness, the FairFace dataset was employed. It provides a balanced distribution across gender, age, and racial groups, enabling precise monitoring of subgroup performance disparities.

Finally, an in-house classroom dataset was collected to capture the conditions that are not present in public repositories. It includes 50 participants across 12 sessions, and reflects natural variations such as students wearing masks or glasses, changes in lighting, partial occlusions, and non-cooperative head poses. We emphasize that this in-house dataset is intended to evaluate real-world classroom deployment conditions rather than to serve as the primary source of algorithmic generalization, which is assessed using large-scale public benchmarks.

In addition to real-world facial images, we incorporated synthetic samples generated using StyleGAN342. We generated 10,000 synthetic face images using StyleGAN3. These were curated to balance underrepresented demographic groups (e.g., darker skin tones, age groups under 18) and to augment fairness calibration. Importantly, synthetic samples do not correspond to real identities, enabling privacy-preserving augmentation without increasing surveillance risk. Quality control was performed to filter out artifacts such as blurring or uncanny textures. A sample of the synthetic faces is provided in Fig. 4.

Baselines

A fair and informative comparison requires more than simply reporting LaED’s results in isolation. We selected baselines that represent both the state of the art and older but still influential recognition approaches.

The first baseline is the real-time face tracking system of Bugingo and colleagues published in 20251. This study demonstrated how continuous tracking of facial landmarks can speed up attendance verification in classrooms. However, the system was not designed to resist spoofing or synthetic manipulations, and it stored embeddings in a centralized way that posed privacy risks. It therefore provides an important reference point for showing how LaED closes these vulnerabilities.

The second baseline is the portable lightweight recognition system with liveness detection proposed by Surantha and Sugijakko in 20242. Their model achieved efficiency on mobile hardware and represented an important step toward practical deployment. Nevertheless, the absence of open-set recognition and the lack of fairness considerations left the system exposed to unauthorized use and demographic biases.

To extend the comparison beyond these recent works, we also included traditional recognition systems such as OpenFace and FaceNet. These models were selected not because they are competitive with LaED, but because they provide historical baselines for recognition accuracy. Including them helps to demonstrate the trajectory of progress and situates LaED clearly within the larger arc of face recognition development.

Finally, an extended Benchmarking of LaED against State-of-the-Art Models such as MagFace, AdaFace, ElasticFace and ViT-FaceNet was conducted and presented as part of the result in Table 2 (see Sect. 6.2: Broader Benchmarking with State-of-the-Art Models).

Evaluation metrics

The performance of LaED was measured using a wide range of indicators. Accuracy alone was not considered sufficient, since a system that is highly accurate in one condition but fails under attack or discriminates across groups cannot be considered reliable. Instead, the evaluation relied on multiple dimensions.

Recognition quality was measured using accuracy, precision, recall, and the F1-score. To evaluate resilience against spoofing attacks, we relied on the two metrics standard in presentation attack detection: the Attack Presentation Classification Error Rate (APCER) and the Bona Fide Presentation Classification Error Rate (BPCER). These allow a precise distinction between the cost of false acceptances and false rejections. For the deepfake detection module, we reported Equal Error Rate (EER) and ROC-AUC, which capture the balance between sensitivity and specificity.

Latency and efficiency were evaluated in terms of the average inference time per frame, measured in milliseconds, and memory footprint in megabytes. To ensure that the model could be realistically deployed, these values were collected not only on standard laptops but also on low-power devices such as Raspberry Pi and Jetson Nano. In addition, energy consumption per frame was calculated to reflect battery requirements in portable contexts. Fairness was measured as the maximum subgroup performance gap across gender, racial, and age categories in the FairFace dataset. A fairness gap above 5% was considered unacceptable, and LaED’s calibration was tested against this threshold.

Finally, scalability indicators such as communication cost in federated training and throughput in multi-user sessions were measured. These indicators were particularly important to demonstrate that LaED can be deployed across campuses or across institutions without creating bottlenecks.

Ablation studies

Ablation experiments were conducted to evaluate the contribution of individual components. Disabling rPPG-based liveness detection increased vulnerability to replay and photo attacks. Removing deepfake detection reduced robustness to synthetic manipulations. Excluding open-set recognition resulted in false acceptances of unregistered individuals. Omitting fairness calibration increased subgroup error disparities. Replacing federated learning with centralized training eliminated privacy guarantees. These results demonstrate the complementary role of each module.

Deployment environment

The deployment of LaED was tested across several hardware platforms to simulate the variety of environments where an attendance system might be expected to operate. On the low end, experiments were run on Raspberry Pi 4 devices with eight gigabytes of RAM. These tests demonstrated whether the system could function in schools with minimal computing resources. At the mid-tier level, we used NVIDIA Jetson Nano devices, which offer GPU acceleration but remain within the power and cost limits of edge devices commonly used in IoT networks. For comparison, standard laptops with Intel i5 processors and 16 gigabytes of RAM were used to measure performance under typical administrative setups.

Federated training was simulated across five classroom nodes using a Kubernetes cluster. Each node performed local training on a separate partition of the dataset, and the server aggregated the updates asynchronously. To reflect realistic conditions, communication bandwidth was throttled during some experiments, allowing us to measure resilience under unstable networks. Differential privacy libraries were integrated into the PyTorch 2.1 framework to guarantee that individual contributions were obfuscated before aggregation. TensorRT acceleration was employed for inference on the edge devices, ensuring low latency.

By conducting experiments across this spectrum of environments, the study demonstrates that LaED is not confined to laboratory conditions. It can operate within the limitations of resource-constrained schools while maintaining consistency with the requirements of privacy regulations and ethical expectations.

Results and comparative analysis

This section provides an extensive evaluation of the LaED framework across recognition accuracy, spoof and deepfake resistance, robustness to adversarial perturbations, cross-domain generalization, computational efficiency, fairness, federated learning performance, and usability. The goal is to determine whether LaED meaningfully improves on existing lightweight and tracking-based approaches, and whether the gains are consistent across demographics, datasets, device types, and learning configurations. The evaluation integrates both tabular and graphical analysis and includes comparison with strong baselines used in real-world attendance systems.

Additional scalability and communication-efficiency analyses are presented in Appendix 2.

Overall recognition performance

LaED achieved the strongest overall performance across all recognition metrics, maintaining both high accuracy and low statistical variance under classroom-like conditions. As shown in Table 1, LaED reached an accuracy of 97.8% with a 95% confidence interval of 97.1 to 98.4 and a low standard deviation of 0.67. This indicates that performance remains consistent across bootstrap resamples and is not sensitive to particular subsets of the data. Figure 5 shows a visual presentation of the result. In comparison, prior systems such as Bugingo et al.1 and Surantha and Sugijakko2 show wider confidence intervals and higher variance, reflecting less stability when illumination changes, partial occlusions occur or students move rapidly. ArcFace remained competitive in raw accuracy but exhibited greater spread in its CI and SD values, suggesting less reliability when tested under the same classroom conditions.

Beyond accuracy, LaED also recorded the highest precision, recall and F1-scores, demonstrating that improvements are not limited to a single metric but extend to balanced classification performance. This is particularly important in classroom deployments where false positives can inflate attendance incorrectly and false negatives can penalize present students. The narrow confidence bounds for LaED across all metrics reinforce that the model behaves predictably under varied capture conditions. These results confirm that integrating Tiny-ViT embeddings with physiological liveness cues and deepfake-artifact analysis improves both recognition stability and robustness in real-world classroom scenarios.

In contrast, LaED unifies lightweight feature extraction, multimodal spoof resistance, fairness calibration and privacy-aware learning in a single deployable architecture. These elements support stable operation on constrained devices and help maintain trustworthiness under real-world classroom conditions.

Adversarial robustness tests

To complement spoofing and deepfake evaluations, we conducted preliminary adversarial robustness tests using gradient-based attacks: Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM, ε = 0.01–0.05) and Projected Gradient Descent (PGD, 10 steps, ε = 0.03). LaED maintained 83.5% accuracy under FGSM (ε = 0.01) and 78.2% under PGD (ε = 0.03), outperforming a ResNet baseline which dropped to 62.4% and 55.8% respectively. While accuracy degraded under stronger perturbations (PGD ε = 0.05 → 68.1%), the results indicate that LaED exhibits resilience against moderate adaptive adversarial attacks, reinforcing its suitability for high-stakes and real-time educational environments. Transformer-based architectures appear more resilient to adversarial noise because self-attention layers distribute feature importance across spatial regions. LaED maintains substantially higher accuracy under gradient-based attacks, which suggests that its use of Tiny-ViT embeddings, temporal cues and physiological signatures provides additional implicit defences against noise-driven perturbations. Beyond FGSM and PGD, we analyzed how LaED behaves under a broader class of adversarial threats that commonly affect face-recognition systems. These include spatially transformed perturbations, color-channel distortions, adversarial patches, and non-gradient attacks such as boundary and noise optimization methods. Although these were not formally benchmarked in this study, we provide a qualitative assessment based on the multi-modal nature of LaED. The integration of rPPG-based liveness signals and temporal-artifact detectors helps LaED resist perturbations that distort only the RGB stream, since physiological cues are difficult to falsify with pixel-level noise. This stands in contrast to conventional CNN-based systems, which often collapse when even small spatial or color modifications are introduced.

To strengthen the comparison, we evaluated how trends in LaED’s robustness compare descriptively with state-of-the-art models such as ArcFace, MagFace and AdaFace under the same FGSM and PGD budgets. These models typically show faster degradation because their single-stream RGB embeddings are easier to destabilize. LaED’s blended features slow this performance drop. This advantage is especially visible at perturbation levels up to ε = 0.03, where LaED retains considerably more discriminative structure than the baselines.

We also acknowledge that the current adversarial experiment suite remains preliminary. Important threat categories, such as physically realizable adversarial stickers, adversarial glasses, infrared LED attacks and unrestricted semantic transformations, are not yet included in the current evaluation. These missing attack vectors represent crucial next steps. In response, the manuscript now outlines plans for a more comprehensive adversarial test suite, including red-team evaluations and cross-model transfer attacks. This expanded roadmap provides a clearer pathway toward deployment-grade adversarial resilience.

Fairness and demographic robustness

Ensuring equitable performance across demographic groups is a critical requirement for biometric-based attendance and identification systems, particularly in educational environments characterized by diverse age, gender, and skin-tone distributions. To evaluate the fairness of the proposed LaED framework, we conducted a detailed demographic robustness analysis using the FairFace benchmark, focusing on subgroup-level recognition accuracy and inter-group performance disparities.

Table 8 presents the recognition accuracies of LaED and representative baseline models across key demographic subgroups, including gender, skin tone, and age-related categories. The results indicate that conventional attendance-oriented baselines exhibit noticeable variability across demographic groups. For instance, prior methods report reduced performance for darker-skinned and female subgroups, reflecting well-documented bias patterns in face recognition systems. ArcFace demonstrates improved overall accuracy compared to earlier baselines; however, performance disparities across demographic strata remain observable.

While subgroup accuracies provide important insight, they do not directly quantify fairness disparity across models. To enable a clear and consistent baseline fairness comparison, we further compute the fairness gap, defined as the maximum difference between subgroup recognition accuracies (max–min) for each model. This metric captures the extent of demographic imbalance in model performance and is widely adopted in fairness evaluation of biometric systems.

The resulting baseline fairness comparison is summarized in Table 9, which consolidates fairness gaps for traditional baselines, ArcFace, and state-of-the-art embedding-based methods (MagFace and AdaFace), alongside the proposed LaED framework. The fairness gap for ArcFace is derived directly from the subgroup accuracies reported in Table 8, whereas MagFace and AdaFace gaps are obtained from their respective baseline evaluations under the same fairness definition.

As shown in Table 9, earlier attendance-focused baselines exhibit fairness gaps exceeding 4%, indicating substantial demographic performance imbalance. ArcFace reduces this disparity but still records a fairness gap of 3.7%, reflecting residual bias across skin tone and gender groups. More recent embedding-based approaches, MagFace and AdaFace, further improve demographic robustness, achieving fairness gaps of 2.3% and 2.0%, respectively.

In contrast, the proposed LaED framework achieves the lowest fairness gap of 1.8%, demonstrating superior demographic consistency across all evaluated subgroups. This improvement is attributed to LaED’s fairness-aware embedding calibration and adaptive decision mechanism, which jointly optimize recognition accuracy while explicitly minimizing demographic performance disparities. Notably, these gains are achieved without sacrificing overall recognition performance, highlighting LaED’s suitability for real-world deployment in fairness-sensitive educational attendance systems.

Ethical, legal, and practical implications

The deployment of automated facial recognition systems in classrooms is no longer a purely technical matter. Recent global events have placed these technologies under intense scrutiny, and institutions that adopt them without safeguards risk facing legal sanctions and public backlash. The results presented in the previous section demonstrate that LaED achieves measurable improvements in accuracy, fairness, and security. However, the broader significance of the framework lies in its ability to respond to the ethical and legal challenges that have derailed many other initiatives.

Privacy and consent

One of the strongest criticisms of facial recognition in education has been the lack of informed consent. High-profile cases, such as the unauthorized biometric data collection by Microsoft Teams in New South Wales in March 2025, have highlighted how easily privacy rights can be violated when consent protocols are not respected. In another case, Chelmer Valley High School in the United Kingdom faced reprimand after deploying a face-based lunch payment system without completing proper data protection assessments. These examples reveal that even well-intentioned deployments can cross legal boundaries if they neglect privacy safeguards.

LaED incorporates privacy-preserving mechanisms directly into its design. By using federated learning, student data never leaves the local classroom environment. The model updates, not the images, are aggregated at the global level, ensuring that personal biometric data remains confined. Furthermore, the inclusion of consent tokens within the verifiable logging system creates a record that each student’s data use has been authorized. In this way, LaED transforms privacy from an afterthought into a foundational component of system design.

Fairness and equity

Ethical concerns also extend beyond privacy into the question of fairness. Face recognition models have historically been prone to demographic bias, disproportionately misclassifying students from minority backgrounds. If left unaddressed, these disparities can reinforce existing inequalities and undermine trust in the technology. Our evaluation demonstrated that without calibration, subgroup performance gaps exceeded 9%, a level that could produce unequal educational outcomes.

By embedding fairness calibration into every training cycle, LaED ensures that subgroup performance differences are minimized and monitored. Importantly, the fairness process is transparent, allowing administrators to view disaggregated results rather than only aggregate accuracy. This design choice responds to growing calls from policymakers and advocacy groups who argue that algorithmic systems should be auditable for equity. The framework therefore does not only enhance technical reliability but also safeguards the principle of equal treatment.

Synthetic data justification

Synthetic augmentation played both an ethical and technical role. Ethically, it reduced reliance on collecting sensitive biometric data from children, respecting privacy norms. Technically, it improved demographic balance: the inclusion of StyleGAN3 faces reduced the accuracy gap between lighter and darker skin tones from 7.4% to 2.1%. This aligns with recent works leveraging GANs for fairness-aware augmentation43–46. While concerns about “GAN artifacts” are valid, careful curation and exclusion of low-quality generations minimized risks. Hence, synthetic data served as both a fairness equalizer and a privacy-preserving strategy, integral to LaED’s design philosophy.

Legal and regulatory compliance

Regulatory frameworks such as the General Data Protection Regulation in Europe and the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act in the United States impose strict requirements on the handling of student data. Compliance with these regulations is not optional; institutions that fail to meet standards risk legal penalties and reputational harm. Baseline systems did not include compliance mechanisms and would likely face challenges if deployed at scale.

LaED takes a proactive stance by embedding compliance markers into its verifiable logs. Each attendance record is tied to a consent statement, and all logs are tamper-evident, making it possible to demonstrate adherence to regulations during audits. Beyond technical compliance, this transparency creates a climate of accountability, giving stakeholders confidence that the system can withstand legal and regulatory examination.

A further ethical consideration involves the use of synthetic data for fairness calibration. While synthetic augmentation helps reduce demographic imbalance without collecting additional biometric data from students, it also introduces potential risks if the generative model embeds biased or unrealistic facial characteristics. To mitigate this, all StyleGAN3-generated faces were screened for visual artifacts, over-representation of particular phenotype traits, and distributional skew. Synthetic samples were used strictly as non-identifiable fairness-balancing inputs and were never mixed with real student embeddings in the attendance database. Moreover, subgroup performance was evaluated on real faces only, ensuring that fairness gains observed in LaED are not artefacts of synthetic data alone. These safeguards help ensure that synthetic faces improve equity without introducing new ethical vulnerabilities or reinforcing hidden biases in the model.

Deploying facial recognition in education involves navigating a wide range of regulatory, cultural and institutional environments. Some regions operate under comprehensive child-data governance frameworks such as GDPR or FERPA, while others have fragmented or minimal oversight. This variability affects how schools interpret consent, how they store biometric data and how violations are investigated or penalized. LaED’s privacy-preserving architecture was designed to accommodate these differences by keeping all biometric processing on local devices, providing cryptographically verifiable logs and supporting granular consent tracking. Even so, technical safeguards cannot fully compensate for weak institutional governance. Schools with limited digital literacy or inconsistent enforcement practices may struggle to maintain robust access control, incident handling and parental communication procedures. These challenges highlight that LaED can help reduce risk but must operate within a broader ecosystem of policy, training and regulatory accountability. Scaling the system across diverse jurisdictions will require adaptation to local norms, integration with region-specific legal requirements and ongoing monitoring to ensure that privacy commitments are upheld in practice.

Scalability in educational environments extends beyond initial deployment on low-power devices. Sustaining performance over time requires mechanisms that can adapt to demographic changes, evolving attack strategies, and hardware degradation. New deepfake generation techniques, mask materials, or adversarial manipulation methods may emerge, and demographic composition in classrooms may shift as student cohorts change. LaED addresses part of this challenge through its modular design, which allows periodic recalibration of open-set thresholds, incremental federated re-training without transferring raw data, and continuous monitoring of subgroup performance. These capabilities help maintain fairness, privacy and robustness as conditions evolve. Nevertheless, long-term sustainability also depends on institutional practices such as scheduled model audits, routine hardware maintenance and governance structures that ensure consent records and access permissions remain accurate. The system’s technical design supports long-term resilience, but effective deployment will require ongoing collaboration between technical teams, educators and regulatory bodies.

Practical deployment considerations

While technical performance and ethical safeguards are essential, practical usability determines whether a system succeeds in real-world classrooms. Teachers, administrators, and students must be able to interact with the system without excessive disruption. Our deployment studies showed that LaED can operate on modest hardware such as Raspberry Pi or Jetson Nano devices, ensuring accessibility in both resource-rich and resource-limited environments. Latency remained low enough for real-time use, and energy consumption was within tolerable limits for continuous operation.

The design also acknowledges the social realities of classroom use. By rejecting unregistered individuals through open-set thresholding, the system prevents unauthorized attendance marking, a problem that was repeatedly observed in previous deployments. At the same time, the fairness calibration and consent management modules reduce the risk of disputes, ensuring smoother adoption by both students and parents. LaED therefore demonstrates that technical robustness and social acceptance can be pursued simultaneously.

Second, integration with existing school IT systems is supported via modular APIs and lightweight clients deployable on standard classroom devices (e.g., laptops, tablets). Third, the system is optimized for resource constraints: on-device embedding computation requires minimal GPU/TPU acceleration and can run on mid-range hardware without degrading latency. Broader implications

The layered defense approach embodied in LaED carries implications that go beyond the classroom. As institutions around the world consider the adoption of biometric systems in education, healthcare, and public administration, the principles tested here may serve as a model for responsible deployment. The system shows that it is possible to design recognition technologies that respect privacy, provide fairness guarantees, and remain legally accountable, all without sacrificing efficiency.

By addressing the loopholes left open by earlier studies, LaED positions itself as more than a technical improvement. It becomes a statement about how biometric technologies should be developed and governed in sensitive environments. In a period where public trust is fragile and legal scrutiny is rising, such an approach is not simply desirable but necessary.

Conclusion and future work

This paper introduced LaED, a layered, lightweight, and explainable framework for classroom attendance tracking that goes beyond conventional face recognition pipelines. By integrating multimodal spoof and deepfake defenses, open-set recognition, fairness calibration, federated privacy-preserving learning, and tamper-evident auditability, LaED closes critical loopholes left by existing attendance systems.

The experimental results demonstrate that LaED achieves:

• A high recognition accuracy (> 97%) even under occlusions and variable classroom conditions.• Strong resilience against spoofing and deepfake attacks, maintaining low APCER and BPCER.• Equitable outcomes with subgroup error disparities reduced to below 1%.• Privacy-preserving scalability, with federated learning and differential privacy ensuring sensitive student data remains local.• Compliance-ready accountability, with cryptographically verifiable logs and consent tokens to align with global child-data regulations.
