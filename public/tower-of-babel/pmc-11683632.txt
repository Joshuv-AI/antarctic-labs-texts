Novel, Rapid, and Simple Isocratic UPLC-UV Method for Estimating Nitrate and Nitrite Contents in Environmental Water Samples Using an Analytical Quality by Design Approach
Authors: Pradeep Kumar Gollapudi, Kranthi Kumar Gollapudi, Dr Nimmagadda Padmaja
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this study, we reported a novel reverse-phase UPLC method for the simultaneous estimation of nitrite and nitrate ions using a Quality by Design (QbD) approach. Nitrite and nitrate ions were separated on an ACQUITY CSH Fluoro-Phenyl column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) in isocratic mode with a mobile phase composition of solvent A and solvent B in a ratio of 980:20 v/v. Solvent A consisted of 100% Milli-Q water, while solvent B comprised 0.5 mL of formic acid in 1000 mL of methanol. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the flow rate was 0.6 mL/min. Detection was carried out at 214 nm with an injection volume of 5.0 μL. The method was developed and validated in accordance with ICH Q2 (R2) guidelines Validation parameters, including system suitability, sensitivity, precision, linearity, and accuracy, were within acceptable ranges. These findings suggest that the developed RP-UPLC method is highly sensitive and suitable for the estimation of nitrite and nitrate ions in various environmental water samples.

Introduction

Nitrosamines form in drinking water as byproducts of the disinfection process when nitrate or nitrite ions or nitrogen-containing compounds react with chlorine or chloramine.8,9 Because nitrosamines are not federally regulated, fewer water tests are conducted for them. If tested, it is commonly for NDMA, with a recommended criterion of 8.0 ng/L. The safe level for nitrate in drinking water is 10 ppm,10,11 and levels above this threshold are considered unsafe. A single exposure above 10 ppm can adversely affect the health. Nitrate water pollution is a major problem worldwide. Elevated nitrate levels in wastewater lead to the eutrophication of natural water bodies, causing algal blooms that severely harm water quality and aquatic environments. The maximum contaminant level (MCL) for nitrate is 10 mg/L (10 ppm) and for nitrite is 1 mg/L (1 ppm).12,15 Nitrate and nitrite are forms of nitrogen found in the environment, both from natural and anthropogenic sources. Large amounts of nitrate in drinking water can be harmful because nitrate can convert to nitrite in the human body. Common sources of increased nitrate and nitrite levels include fertilizers, livestock waste, failing septic tanks, drain fields, and dry wells.15 Laboratory reports may present these measurements in units of mg/L or ppm. Based on the above data and studies, it is crucial to control nitrate and nitrite levels in water bodies to below 1–10 ppm. Therefore, there is a need for a simple method to estimate nitrate and nitrite ion contents without resorting to the tedious analytical methods currently available in the literature (phosphatidyl columns,15 octylamine mobile phase,16 and amperometric detection).2 In this study, we aimed to develop a simple, cost-effective analytical method for estimating nitrate and nitrite contents in water samples using UPLC.1,3,4,7,8

An Analytical Quality by Design (AQbD) approach20 was employed to develop the method. AQbD is an extension of QbD, providing a systematic approach to the development of analytical procedures throughout the life cycle. The AQbD process includes defining the analytical target profile (ATP), selecting critical material or method attributes (CMAs), performing risk assessment, and identifying critical method parameters (CMPs).5,20 It also involves screening and optimization using Design Expert software, robustness testing, defining the method operable design region (MODR),6 and establishing a method control strategy. As the term suggests, the analytical procedure life cycle is a cyclic process, resulting in continuous improvement of the method. To date, no method has been developed according to the ICH Q14 guidelines for the estimation of nitrate and nitrite ion contents. Therefore, there is a need for a simple method to estimate nitrate and nitrite ion contents without resorting to the tedious analytical methods currently available in the literature.10−16 This developed method is useful for regular analysis with shorter run times and aligns with the green chemistry approach toward solvent consumption (using methanol as the elution solvent, which is less costly compared to acetonitrile and other solvents).

Materials and Methods

Nitrate and nitrite standards were procured from Inorganic Ventures, with a label claim of 1000 ppm solutions. These solutions were directly used as stocks for further dilutions. Milli-Q water, generated in-house from a Millipore unit, was used as solvent A and for diluent preparations. Methanol, HPLC gradient grade, was obtained from Standard Reagents. The column was procured from Waters Technologies, USA, Milford. The UPLC system, equipped with a quaternary solvent manager (QSM), flow-through needle (FTN), and UV detector, was sourced from Waters Corporation, USA.

UPLC Instrumental Attributes and Analytical Setup

The chromatographic separation analysis of the nitrate and nitrite contents was performed using a Waters UPLC-H Class system equipped with a UV detector. The instrument consists of a QSM, FTN, a column oven with a thermostat, and a variable dual wavelength UV detector. Data processing was carried out using Empower Version 3.0 software. The column used for the separation of nitrate and nitrite ions was an ACQUITY UPLC CSH fluorophenyl (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) from Waters Corporation. The mobile phase comprised solvent A and solvent B in a ratio of 980:20 v/v. Solvent A consisted of 100% Milli-Q water, while solvent B consisted of 0.5 mL of formic acid in 1000 mL of methanol. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the sample cooler temperature was set at 15 °C. The injection volume for the analysis was 5.0 μL, and the detection wavelength for nitrate and nitrite ions was 214 nm. Samples were filtered through 0.45 μm filters before injection into the chromatographic system to avoid contamination and extend the column life.

Results and Discussion

For the estimation of nitrate and nitrite content, various literature sources describe the use of Synergy Polar columns, Purospher RP-8 columns, and ion exchange columns.10−18 In this study, we developed a new chemistry using a Fluoro-Phenyl column with a CSH technology particle substrate, having UPLC dimensions of 100 × 2.1 mm and 1.7 μm. Different mobile phases have been used in the literature10−18 for estimating these two ions, including ion pairs and ion chromatography buffers. However, our aim was to develop a simple isocratic method and mobile phases without any complex mobile phase conditions and columns, ensuring user-friendliness and adherence to green chemistry principles in the development of analytical methods.

Sample Preparations for Method Screening

Diluent Selection

Nitrate and nitrite ions are easily soluble in water, and the samples that we are analyzing are water-based. Therefore, Milli-Q water was used as the diluent for preparing standard and sample solutions.

Nitrite and nitrite peaks are scanned in the PDA system to get UV plots. Based on the UV spectral data, nitrate and nitrite were analyzed at a wavelength of 214 nm, chosen due to the strong absorption characteristics of both compounds at this wavelength, facilitating precise quantification (Figure 1).

Analytical Target Profile

The ATP is the predefined intended purpose of the procedure and establishes the criteria for the analytical procedure performance characteristics that are required to an intended use (Figure 2), which includes the quality attributes to be measured (e.g., accuracy and precision for a quantitative procedure).10 The analytical method should quantify nitrate and nitrite ions in water samples over a range of LOQ–150% of the target concentration with an accuracy of 90–110% and repeatability not exceeding 10.0%. The method should separate nitrate and nitrite ions with a resolution greater than 3.0 for accurate quantification, and sensitivity should meet the signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio acceptance criteria, i.e., LOD S/N ≥ 3 and LOQ S/N ≥ 10. ATPs are considered as per ICH Q14 guidelines.10 Based on the ATP, a UPLC method with UV detection was chosen as the analytical technique. The critical method attribute selected was the resolution between the nitrate and nitrite ion peaks.

ATP and QbD approach.

Method Development

Existing HPLC methods for determining nitrate and nitrite ions use Synergy Polar, Purospher RP-8 columns, and ion exchange columns with different ion pair mobile phases.10−18 However, no UPLC methods with simple mobile phases or a QbD approach are available. The aim of this study is to develop a simple, cost-effective analytical method for regular analysis of nitrate and nitrite content. Method development was initiated for UPLC with four different UPLC columns featuring various chemistries, column temperatures, and flow rates as variables (Table 2). The mobile phase used was a combination of water and acidified methanol (1.0 mL of formic acid in 1000 mL of methanol). Columns used for the scouting which are orthogonal stationary phases are Kromasil C4 column, ACQUITY HSS C18 column, ACQUITY CSH Fluoro-Phenyl column, and ACQUITY CSH C18 column of 100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm used (Table 1). Based on the initial understanding, method parameters were studied using design of experiments by considering 4 factors, namely, mobile phase B composition %, flow rate, column temperature, and column type. Scouting was conducted according to a set of experiments provided by Design Expert software. The study employed a response factor approach, utilizing a type-I-optimal design and a quadratic model to evaluate the impact of each factor on resolution.

Initial Screening and Findings

After the initial screening of the above experiments, it was concluded that the CSH Fluoro-Phenyl column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) provided a resolution greater than 3.0 (Table 5) at both higher and lower flow rates. The mobile phase composition did not significantly affect the resolution. However, during method screening, it was observed that the nitrite peak shape was distorted in some injections. The literature19 suggests that formic acid in the methanol mobile phase may cause this distortion for the nitrite peak.

Optimization Study

Risk Assessment

As part of the risk assessment, the effect of formic acid on the response and peak shape of the nitrite ion was investigated. Experimental trials were conducted using an ACQUITY CSH Fluoro-Phenyl column with a 0.4 mL/min flow rate and a mobile phase composition of 98:2 v/v (water). The formic acid volume in solvent B (methanol) was varied to observe its effect on the nitrite ion. Different volumes of formic acid (0.125 mL, 0.25 mL, 0.5 mL, 0.625 mL, 0.75 mL, and 1.0 mL per 500 mL methanol) were tested, and it was found that lower concentrations of formic acid resulted in higher responses for the nitrite peak (Figures 7 and 8) and vice versa.

Chromatograms of nitrite ion peak response in different concentrations of formic acid.

Effect of formic acid strength on nitrite ion peak response.

To explore alternatives, acetic acid and trifluoroacetic acid were tested as additives under the same chromatographic conditions. Acetic acid resulted in peak fronting and lower resolution (Figure 10) compared to formic acid. Trifluoroacetic acid (Figure 11) yielded a similar resolution to that of formic acid. Methanol 100% was also used, but no separation was observed (Figure 9). Based on this risk assessment study, the mobile phase composition was finalized as 0.5 mL of formic acid in 1000 mL of methanol (solvent B, 2%) and Milli-Q water (solvent A, 98%). This isocratic mobile phase was prepared for further optimization and robustness studies.

Method Optimization

Flow rate and column temperature were considered variables for method optimization. The MODR was determined based on these variables, and the CMPs affecting the CMAs were identified as the strength of formic acid, flow rate, column temperature, and mobile phase composition (Figure 12). The monitored criteria included the number of peaks observed, resolution greater than 2.0, and tailing factor of the two peaks (Table 5). A broader set of criteria were chosen to achieve the best results and gain comprehensive knowledge of the method.

System Suitability

System suitability is essential in chromatography to ensure reproducibility and adequacy of the method for the intended use. The test was conducted at MCL (1 ppm) concentration with six replicate injections. The results were evaluated based on RT, peak area, and peak tailing. Acceptance criteria include % RSD not exceeding 10%, resolution between nitrite and nitrate peaks not less than 3.0, and USP tailing for each peak not exceeding 2.0 (Table 6).

Specificity

Specificity is the ability to assess the analyte unequivocally in the presence of other components. It was evaluated by comparing the chromatograms of individual nitrite and nitrate peaks with their blank solutions at test concentrations (1 ppm). No interference was observed from the blank at the RT of both nitrate and nitrite peaks (Figures 14 and 15), confirming the method’s specificity/selectivity.

Sensitivity (LOD and LOQ Establishment)

The detection limit (LOD) is the lowest amount of analyte detectable but not quantifiable (Figure 16). The quantitation limit (LOQ) is the lowest amount of analyte that can be quantitatively determined with suitable precision and accuracy. The LOQ is particularly important for determining the impurities and degradation products (Figure 17).

Linearity and Range

Linearity is the method’s ability to produce results directly proportional to the analyte concentration in the sample within a given range. The range is the interval between the upper and lower concentrations of the analyte for which the method has demonstrated a suitable precision, accuracy, and linearity. For this method, the range is 0.3 ppm to 3 ppm based on experimental data (Tables 7 and 8). Calibration plots for nitrite and nitrate mixture solutions were created for concentrations from the LOQ to 150% of the test concentrations. Regression equations were calculated by plotting the individual calibration curves using concentration and peak area. 300% linearity solution was performed for additional information only (Figure 18).

Accuracy

Accuracy expresses the closeness of agreement between the accepted true value or reference value and the value found. Accuracy for nitrite and nitrate peaks was evaluated by measuring % recovery at three concentration levels (LOQ level, 100% level, and 150% level). Each analyte was injected in triplicate, and % recovery was measured. The acceptance criteria for % variation are 80–120% (Table 9).

Precision

Precision expresses the closeness of agreement (scatter degree) between a series of measurements obtained from multiple samplings of the same homogeneous sample. Precision is usually expressed as the variance, standard deviation, or coefficient of variation of a series of measurements. The acceptance criteria for % RSD are not more than 10.0 for six replicate injections (Tables 10 and 11).

Robustness

Robustness measures an analytical procedure’s capacity to remain unaffected by small, deliberate variations in method parameters (Table 12), indicating its reliability during normal usage.

Filter Validation Study

Nitrite and nitrate content studies were performed on water samples collected from different sources. Given that the method employs UPLC with small micrometer columns, it is crucial to filter samples to prevent dirt particles from clogging the column and causing high pressures. Thus, the compatibility of various syringe filters available in the market—nylon, PVDF, and PTFE 0.45 μm filters—was evaluated. The suitability of the syringe filters for sample preparation was determined by comparing the peak areas of filtered and unfiltered injections (Figure 19). Based on the data, nylon filters were found to be suitable, as all filters provided consistent responses for nitrate and nitrite peaks.

Conclusions

This research proposes a novel RP-UPLC-UV method successfully developed and validated for the simultaneous estimation of nitrite and nitrate contents in environmental water samples. This study is particularly valuable for sewage treatment plants and the pharmaceutical industries, where water is a major solvent used for process development and cleaning activities. The use of AQbD and DoE experimental design (Figure 13) significantly improved the method’s performance and robustness, facilitating the successful separation and estimation of nitrite and nitrate ions. The method proved to be efficient and effective for routine testing, offering quality results with well-defined sensitivity. The use of Design Expert software in this approach reduced the number of experiments required compared with OFAT trials. There is no criticality in preparing mobile phases and setting up a UPLC system. Method validation was performed as per ICH Q2 (R2) guidelines, and all parameters considered for analytical method validation were found to be within the acceptable range. The proposed method is thus confirmed to be affordable, precise, sensitive, and linear. This method can be used for estimation of nitrite and nitrate content in water, which is used in the process of manufacturing pharmaceutical products, i.e., APIs and formulation products. This is very helpful in controlling the nitrosamine screening in APIs which are manufactured using different water sources.
