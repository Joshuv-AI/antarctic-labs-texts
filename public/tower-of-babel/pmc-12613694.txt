Background

It is the intention of the authors to share their reflections on the experience of public involvement and engagement of young adults (18–40) with early onset type 2 diabetes, which is a stigmatised condition [1], from communities with high levels of racial and ethnic diversity, and high levels of deprivation. This is intended to support colleagues, especially clinical academics, project managers and public involvement and engagement professionals, wishing to undertake involvement with this group specifically and potentially inform practice in groups with similar characteristics of deprivation and diversity.

Public involvement in health research

Public involvement refers to the inclusion of members of the public in the process and lifecycle of a research project and its governance [2]. It involves patients or other individuals with relevant lived experiences contributing to a range of activities, such as prioritising research questions, supporting the design of studies and interventions, selecting the most important or relevant outcome measures, collecting, analysing and interpreting data, and disseminating and implementing results. The overarching goal of public involvement is to ensure that the research conducted is relevant, acceptable and appropriate to those directly affected by its outcomes, thus maximising its impact [3, 4]. Public involvement is increasingly a priority and requirement of funders. It is acknowledged that, for public involvement to be more effective, it is necessary to expand existing models of public involvement, incorporate equality diversity and inclusion considerations, and assess impact with a focus on empowerment [3].

A focus on equality, diversity and inclusion is particularly pertinent in Leicester. As the largest city in the East Midlands, Leicester comprises an extremely diverse population. 43% of Leicester’s population are of Asian ethnicity, and there is also a notable prevalence of people of Eastern European, Black African, and Black Caribbean ethnicities. 41% of Leicester’s population were born outside the UK, meaning Leicester has a high diasporic population. Levels of social deprivation are also particularly high in Leicester – of the 317 local authorities in the UK, Leicester is ranked as the 32nd most deprived [5].

People of working age have also historically been underserved by approaches to public involvement within research undertaken at the NIHR Leicester Biomedical Research Centre (BRC) [6] and more widely [7]. Indeed, this echoes the lack of inclusion of young people with type 2 diabetes in research generally [8]. No existing accounts of public involvement of the target group could be found which may contribute to this lack of inclusion, although one account of public involvement of children and young people with early-onset type 2 diabetes was identified [9]. Further, a number of accounts of public involvement of adolescents and young people were considered, though generally tending in age towards the lower end of that considered in this case [10–13]. These detailed a number of factors that enabled involvement including dedicated staffing [10], flexibility, building trust, provision of support, respect for resource constraints (time, financial), empathy [11], consideration of the public contributors preferences and how these may change over the course of the project [12], whole-team commitment, senior leadership, communication and dialogue [12, 13]. These mostly focussed on cancer patients, though one on adolescents and young people with type 1 diabetes. Lack of diversity amongst public contributors was noted in one case [10] and generally, as indeed with this paper, the groups considered were small. It was also noted that a diagnosis of cancer in an adolescent or young person may lead to a decrease in obstacles to involvement that are pertinent to people with early onset type 2 diagnosis, such as demands associated with employment, education and relationships [14].

The gap in knowledge pertaining to the specific requirements of young adults, clinically and in terms of involvement and engagement in research, is widely recognised [7, 8, 12] and predicated on the unique experiences of young adulthood including navigating identity formation, educational advancement, relationship development, and family and community responsibilities [7]. The demographic face age-related barriers including feeling intimidated by healthcare professionals [15], particularly in the context of avoidance associated with suboptimal outcomes [13], and low confidence [15] likely compounded by condition specific stigma [1]. They are likely to be facing multiple demands, in the context of limited life experience, such as demanding and often insecure employment, academic pressures, and young families [16–18]. These challenges demand consideration of best practice for public involvement of young adults in research [19–21].

This paper seeks to contribute by identifying best practices and key recommendations for impactful public involvement in early-onset type 2 diabetes (EOT2D) research, ensuring that involvement is not only meaningful but also measurable in terms of its contribution to research quality, intervention design, and patient outcomes.

The M3 research programme

The M3 Research Programme is funded by an NIHR Programme Grant for Applied Research (NIHR201165) [22], and aims to develop and test the effectiveness of an innovative multifactorial management intervention to address multimorbidity in young adults (aged 18–40 years) diagnosed with type 2 diabetes (‘early-onset type 2 diabetes’). Type 2 diabetes is traditionally recognised as a disease which mainly effects older people, although the prevalence of EOT2D continues to rise alarmingly [23]. EOT2D is associated with a high risk of vascular complications when compared with type 2 diabetes diagnosed at an older age [24, 25], ultimately leading to increased risk of mortality [26–28]. People with EOT2D are more likely to have experienced socioeconomic deprivation [29], experience mental health comorbidities [30, 31], and have busy lives and other circumstances which make it challenging to access care [1]. Interventions for the management of EOT2D are currently the same as for older adults, as young adults are typically severely under-represented in research [8]. There is a clear need to develop new models of care for people with EOT2D.

In summary, individuals with EOT2D have historically been excluded from research and public involvement, which is of particular concern given the increased risk of mortality and morbidity associated with EOT2D. Better ways of including individuals with EOT2D in research are sorely needed in order to create better treatments for this at-risk and underrepresented population.

Public involvement in The M3 Programme faced two main challenges from the outset: 1. Inability to meet in-person due to the risk of COVID-19 infection, particularly within a vulnerable group. 2. Creating accessible opportunities to reach sufficient numbers of people effected by EOT2D in light of the busy, changeable and stressful lives of those individuals.

Public involvement activities were undertaken roughly twice a month. Activities were facilitated by technologists, researchers, managers and administrators, as well as by a Community Engagement Officer and a Public Involvement Manager. Activities included focus and advisory groups consulting and collaborating on development of the intervention, the research project and the approach to public involvement; provision of remote feedback; and collaborative alpha and beta testing through which the team were able to adopt technological development methodology albeit not in the format of a consensus conference. In light of the circumstances of the COVID-19 pandemic, activity was moved to online spaces for much of the project.

As opportunities to meet in person became more viable, we saw an increase in community engagement work and a need to reinvigorate the online peer support (hub) sessions. This helped balance exclusion related to the digital divide. Thus we protected the relationships and trust developed through online hub sessions and expanded opportunities to build relationships and trust within our local diverse communities.

Peer support opportunities were consistently integrated into advisory group sessions, initially taking the form of online discussions featuring expert speakers. During the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic the hybrid advisory group and peer support sessions (which were dubbed hub sessions) were particularly popular, reflecting the widespread shift towards digital engagement in health research [33]. As the project evolved, peer support activities were iteratively adapted to reflect changing social and cultural priorities. Key shifts occurred during the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, the transition to the “new normal,” and in the post-pandemic period. Notably, in the post-pandemic phase, there was a marked increase in community engagement, when the team collaborated with seven community organisations in Leicester. This shift highlighted a transition from primarily digital engagement to a stronger emphasis on local, community-based interactions, potentially reflecting broader cultural changes post-pandemic [17, 32]. However, digital approaches remained integral, with a recent expansion into podcast content creation in response to public contributor feedback.

While impact assessment data on public involvement remains relatively scarce or inconsistently collected [3], efforts within the NIHR Leicester infrastructure increasingly draw on action research approaches [34] and reflection to refine and improve practice. This paper does not aim to provide a formal impact assessment but rather offers a reflective account of the successes and challenges encountered by the M3 Programme Team in delivering their public involvement workstream.

The team sought to reflect on the public involvement in The M3 Programme in order to derive good practice recommendations for working with people who have EOT2D that may also inform wider work with stigmatised young adults experiencing intersectional exclusion. Reflective discussion groups

Eight personnel participated in the reflection, and five provided personal characteristics. Two participants were men, and three women. Two participants were aged 20–29, one 30–44, and two 45–59. All were White British or White Other.

The research team reflection lasted approximately 90 minutes and was facilitated by an independent facilitator. Seven topics were discussed by participating team members which can be seen in Supplementary Table 1.

Four public contributors aged 30–44, comprising 2 men, 1 woman and 1 non-binary person, gave feedback on their experience. Their ethnicities were given as 2 Indian, 1 Asian and White, and 1 other. 2 were Muslim, 1 Sikh and 1 preferred not to say. No participants were pregnant, and 1 experienced disability (mental ill health).

To incorporate public contributor’s perspective in the reflection, the M3 public contributor community was invited to attend a reflective discussion group facilitated by the Community Engagement and Inclusion Manager for the Centre for Ethnic Health Research, who was functionally independent. Four people attended. Four topics were discussed as listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Public contributors were also invited to provide feedback using a collaborative online forum, on the same topics plus two additional, which are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Themes identified in reflections

The UK Standards for Public Involvement are communication, working together, inclusive opportunities, impact, governance, and support and learning [35]. Therefore, we will summarise our findings within the context of these overarching themes. Supplementary Table 4. Summary of Themes Identified from Reflective Discussion Groups summarises the themes identified, which are described in greater depth in the following sections.

Communication

Effective communication across the team was pivotal in embedding a whole-team approach to public involvement, ensuring that it was not confined to a single public involvement professional which in turn required sufficient investment in staffing and management capacities. Regular interdisciplinary meetings allowed for shared insights and collective problem-solving, fostering a sense of joint ownership over public involvement initiatives. However, challenges in communication occasionally hindered agility and responsiveness. To enhance communication, the team could implement routine dissemination of public involvement summaries across all workstreams, ensuring transparency and fostering a shared understanding of ongoing activities. As the whole team were involved at different times in delivering involvement, there was a need for considerable communication across the team about what was planned and what had been undertaken. By adopting agile and flexible approaches i.e. not restricting involvement to a rigid plan or structure, but delivering in a way that met the evolving needs of the research team and project and, importantly the needs of public contributors as they arose, there was an element of uncertainty that is atypical in research approaches. The team felt that they could have negotiated agreement of shifting responsibilities related to agile and flexible practice better.

The research team and public contributors found tangible impact, particularly where public involvement led to changes or consensus in the intervention, motivated their efforts. In the tech development team where collaboration with public contributors directly supported design and testing, the research team personnel experienced working in partnership on a joint project evaluating early versions and implementing public contributor recommendations to improve subsequent user experience.

Public contributors stated that they valued informality in communication and felt this made sessions accessible.

Working together

The importance of starting public involvement early and not underestimating the staffing commitment required to build networks was emphasised. The research team agreed that previous work on public involvement with the target demographic (pre-project) had helped inform practice and particularly inform the resource requirements. Despite this, there was still a sense that public involvement was under resourced based on the perceived value of more resource-intense approaches (for example, whole-team delivery required extra administration and organisation, and flexible delivery required working outside of office hours).

Fundamental to the effectiveness of public involvement with the target demographic was the approach to embedding it across all personnel in the various workstreams of the M3 Research Programme, including technologists, researchers, managers, administrators, and professorial staff. Having senior personnel work with public contributors helped ensure public involvement remained a priority throughout. Adopting a whole-team approach created a requirement to invest in resourcing, training and consistent values-based practice. The whole team participation in public involvement could potentially be in tension with the preference expressed by public contributors for working with a single consistent person, however this model facilitated public involvement that led to tangible impacts that were also valued by public contributors. Communication across the team, including with public contributors, was considered important in order to support opportunities for building effective relationships, consistency and managing demand on public contributors. Relationship building may also mitigate the preference for a single point of contact.

Public involvement benefitted from offering a variety of options for involvement, such as different roles (e.g., community researcher, co-applicant, public contributor) and different media (e.g., collaborative online forums, discourse, email). Low structure options including open discussions and focus groups were valued by public contributors, highlighting a preference for coproduction approaches. Virtual options were significantly preferred, as were weekday evenings, reflecting the working-age population that we involved in this project. This has implications for staff flexibility. In person meetings were not as popular with this demographic, though some did prefer them. There was enthusiasm for ‘forum’ spaces, like WhatsApp groups, or chat room style spaces, but there were some ongoing concerns about how these spaces would be moderated from both public contributors and researchers. Supporting public contributors to lead sessions was effective. Public contributors liked having ongoing contact with team members with whom they were familiar, and continuity was important. A balance between structure and flexibility, and skilled facilitation of sessions was desirable.

Reinvigoration was required throughout the project. Against a backdrop of rapid social change (pre-COVID, pandemic new normal, and post-pandemic) and during the extensive 5-year lifecycle of a programme grant delivering different phases of research there was a need to change how we worked together with public contributors. This was necessary to meet the needs of public contributors, the research and comply with external factors. Public involvement was subject to cycles of evaluation, adaptation and review. This was most effective when the focus was on coproducing ongoing involvement with public contributors.

Several approaches were taken to initially building a community of individuals with lived experience of EOT2D interested in becoming public contributors. Overwhelmingly, letters through clinic teams were the most effective form of invitation. However, this presented a problem with site engagement as sites felt they were unable to adopt this approach locally due to the administrative burden. A few members signed up through social media, events and existing NIHR infrastructure networks. An online sign-up form considerably helped building a community of public contributors.

Alignment with other projects seeking to engage the same community within the research centres working on M3 (Leicester Diabetes Centres and the NIHR Leicester Biomedical Research Centre) was both creative and a site of conflict in seeking to manage the burden on public contributors. Content and discussion around the work of other projects provided a wider range of topics to interest the group, but there were occasions when there was a risk of over-burdening public contributors with additional requests for meetings to support work outside the M3 project. Our ability to support other projects was therefore agreed as subject to consideration of the existing burden, and prioritising the M3 work.

Inclusive opportunities

The overall approaches to public involvement sought to create inclusive opportunities for a demographic the team recognised were facing barriers to involvement, and who are often under-represented within research. The success of the team indicated that our approaches did achieve this, although the team recognised there was no ‘perfect solution’ that would be accessible to all. Offering a suite of options for involvement as described in the preceding section helped increase accessibility, but it is important to recognise that the strong drive towards digital approaches may exclude some people.

Reimbursement for time and commitment was highly important to public contributors in this demographic. Therefore, it was crucial to have clarity around reward and recognition payments, both for the public contributors and for the team members facilitating the involvement.

Cultural competence, in which many team members are trained, was valued by public contributors. Some noted that they would also like to see more diversity within the team. Whilst some wanted the research team to reflect the communities with whom they were working, others felt that this was not necessary, as long as the research team had a good understanding of and willingness to learn about other cultures, and openness to seeing their experiences as embedded within their cultural reality.

Impact

Public involvement was felt to be purposeful and multifunctional across the different facets of the research team e.g., physical activity, tech development, and this helped provide motivation for the whole-team approach to public involvement by emphasising the benefits. It was easiest for the team to perceive tangible impacts, such as those on the development of novel technologies (i.e., digital health reports and self-management programmes) and the wider components of the M3 Intervention. It was more difficult to measure and perceive less tangible impacts, such as how working with public contributors throughout the project effected the team’s cultural competence and understanding of stigma.

Public contributors also felt that involvement was most rewarding when there was tangible impact, for example developing study resources. The opportunity to share experiences and support each other was also a highly valued impact.

Governance

The M3 public involvement plan and the logistics of public involvement were affected by external factors including restrictions associated with the COVID-19 pandemic, and difficulty recruiting staff. A greater focus on contingency and risk planning at the project development stage may have helped.

Policies and procedures to support consistency in public involvement were considered important, particularly around reward and recognition.

Disappointing engagement in the public involvement work by personnel in other sites highlighted that contracting could be important in outlining site responsibilities more widely. Whilst sites had agreed to support public involvement during study planning, particularly by inviting patients to join the public contributor mailing list, there was ultimately little support with this work leaving Leicester, as lead site, to deliver.

Support and learning

The research team stated that they would like public involvement training to support decentralised approaches tailored to address the needs of the target population. This is likely to support a values based approach underpinning consistent public involvement across the team.

The key topic areas summarised from the reflections also suggest that public involvement professionals would benefit from training in contingency and risk planning.

The opportunity for public contributors to learn about their condition through involvement was valued, supporting the importance of coproducing public involvement approaches and considering impact according to the needs and wants of public contributors as well as the research team. This made public involvement more intrinsically rewarding for public contributors and motivated involvement.

Discussion

In the M3 Programme, a high level of engagement with public contributors was evident across the team, noted as important for meaningful involvement of young adults in research [7]. The model ultimately adopted included a strategic lead working alongside the programme team to guide their approach to delivering public involvement, and a dedicated coordinator who supported administration and delivery of hub sessions alongside team personnel. This balanced the value of getting technologists and academics involved directly with the skills and guidance of a strategic expert and the support of a coordinator in overcoming administrative and organisational burdens.

A survey in seven European countries, exploring motivations and deterrents to public involvement and engagement identified that enabling research personnel to undertake public involvement and engagement work is a recognised challenge [36]. The same survey also identified that time and resources represented the primary barriers faced by science personnel. Subsequent to the social upheaval of the second world war, universities were subject to decades of shifting expectations. The focus of universities expanded from education alone to encompass research, social impact, communication, engagement and commercial partnerships. This expansion of role has left academia unsurprisingly subject to competing demands on limited resource [37]. Some academics continue to question the legitimacy of public engagement, and barriers to public engagement are consistent including resourcing, esteem of the activity, capacity, ability and skills [3, 38]. There are strong political drivers for implementation of public involvement and engagement, including the requirement of some funders for public involvement. In the last decade there has been a marked increase in personnel in operational and strategic public involvement roles. However, progress towards implementing public engagement is slow [3, 39] and often restricted to particular areas and siloed with those in professional support service roles. Whilst impact assessment that can help guide good practice is increasingly evident, it remains rare and is often of low quality [40, 41].

Delivery of public involvement by personnel across the team (rather than ‘siloed’ public involvement by public involvement professionals) represented an important success; involvement was well integrated across project processes, although most input was around the intervention development as a more tangible area of output, with diverse personnel, including academics, participating. This created an exponential increase in staffing requirements as greater intra-team communication was required. It also created a need for work on values-based alignment within the team, as well as efforts to influence policy and procedure beyond the team and in the wider institutions. The approaches that worked and the areas where the team felt improvement was possible, demonstrated demand for significant resourcing and development of complex management capacities. The approaches found to be effective required development of new skills, inter-professional working and working outside of office hours. There are therefore implications for what is considered realistic resourcing for decentralised, agile approaches to public involvement.

Personnel highlighted a tension between formalisation (e.g., the desire for clarity of roles and purpose) and the need for agility and flexibility (e.g., the need to adapt to different circumstances or challenges). This reflected the findings of previous research that suggest there is uncertainty over the function of public involvement and engagement in roles and institutions. This is often evident in a tension between willingness to undertake public involvement and engagement activity and availability of time and resource to do so within a system that does not tend to esteem the activity [42]. Communication helped the team negotiate through this tension and greater resourcing could have supported expansion of this and subsequently facilitated greater success. In the context of competing demands for resource within academic institutions [37] this is inevitably challenging.

The reflective feedback highlighted the multi-functional importance of assessing and communicating impact of public involvement, and the importance of embedding governance processes to achieve this, for both public contributors and research personnel. Both groups identified that hearing about and seeing the impacts of public involvement was motivating, especially where these impacts were tangible e.g. changes to the app interface. This suggests that inclusion of meaningful impact assessment can support the shift in academic scepticism favourably towards involvement and engagement activities.

Training and skills development was highlighted in several instances including directly in relation to engagement and involvement skills, and also in relation to the broad management capacities identified as important. This aligns to previous research that has found that skills and confidence underpin willingness to participate in involvement and engagement activity by researchers [43].

The feedback also highlighted the importance of values-based alignment to successfully engaging this demographic. The team focussed on developing an acceptance of the importance and value of public involvement to ensure a whole-team approach and commitment to placing the individual at the centre of all aspects of the project. This was achieved through leadership of senior personnel delivering involvement, guidance and support from the dedicated public involvement personnel, and consistent emphasis of involvement (often by senior personnel) in all project management considerations. A focus on good practice was grounded in the use of the UK National Standards as the foundation of public involvement in M3. However, there is also an implied value to accepting uncertainty and agility that extends beyond the research team and notably to funders. There are therefore implications for how funders should characterise success in public involvement in research and how they should approach public involvement plans to allow for experimentation and adaptation that ultimately improve our understanding and practice.

Implications for policy

Resource implications are not restricted to funded research. The need to start early and have opportunities to fail (particularly working with a target demographic who face barriers to involvement) suggests that considerable funding is required for pre-bid work. This is typically either in the form of pre-bid small grants, such as those facilitated by the Research Support Service (RSS), or investment in core functions within infrastructure e.g. ARC or BRC funding. The RSS small grant has notably recently been reduced, minimising opportunities.

Study limitations

The reflection is restricted to the population engaged and may be subject to limited generalisability and bias from the research team. In light of the need to establish good practice in the emergent field of public involvement however, the work is still considered by the team as of value to our colleagues in the field.

Future studies

The NIHR infrastructure in Leicester is committed to sharing good practice with colleagues and embedding reflective evaluation aligned with the action research model of Sagor [34]. Future studies will therefore include similar reflective evaluation in other projects.
