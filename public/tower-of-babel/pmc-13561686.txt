Serotyping and Seroprevalence Estimation of Pasteurellosis in Ovine and Caprine in Selected Districts of Afar Region, Ethiopia
Authors: Teshager Dubie, Biniam Abera, Muhammed Hamid, Tesfaw Molla, Gizachew Fentahun, Fanuel Bizuayehu Yihunie, Wossene Negash, Sadam Mohammed, Bereket Desalegn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDespite a widespread periodic vaccination with inactivated Pasteurella multocida biotype A is usually practiced, pasteurellosis is among the most prioritized infectious diseases of small ruminant that causes huge economical loss through reduced productivity and death.MethodA cross‐sectional study was conducted with the objective of serotype identification and seroprevalence estimation of ovine and caprine pasteurellosis in selected districts of Afar region, Ethiopia, from December 2021 to June 2023. A total of 293 serum samples were collected using multistage sampling approach. Based on the selection of the study districts, individual animals were randomly selected for sample collection from apparently healthy sheep and goats. The samples were analyzed using the indirect haemagglutination test (IHAT) for serotyping and overall seroprevalence estimation. Descriptive statistics and Pearson's chi‐square (χ 2) test were employed to estimate the seroprevalence and assess the associated factors of ovine and caprine pasteurellosis in the study areas.ResultsIn the present study, the estimated overall seroprevalence of pasteurellosis in small ruminant was found to be 97.61% (n = 286/293; 95% CI: 95–100), of which 98.11% of the sheep (156/159) and 97.01% (130/134) of the goats tested positive for Pasteurella infection. Moreover, four serotypes of Pasteurella (A1, A2, A7 of Pasteurella haemolytica and serotype A of Pasteurella multocida) were serotyped, and 89.16% of the seropositive animals had co‐infection with more than three serotypes. Accordingly, M. haemolytica serotype A7 (97.27%) had the highest prevalent circulating serotypes of Pasteurella in the study areas, followed by M. haemolytica serotype A1 (95.22%), P. multocida serotype A (92.83%), and M. haemolytica serotype A2 (91.47%). There was a statistically significant difference across sex (p = 0.014) of the study animals with the occurrence of the ovine and caprine pasteurellosis in serum samples.ConclusionsThe present study revealed that ovine and caprine pasteurellosis is highly prevalent in small ruminants and circulating in the study areas. The diversity of serotypes observed here would indicate that currently available vaccines are unlikely to provide adequate protection against pasteurellosis. Therefore, developing a multivalent vaccine that protects against multiple Pasteurella serotypes in a single dose is highly recommended.Clinical Trial RegistrationClinical trial registration was not required because the present study was a cross‐sectional observational study and did not involve any clinical intervention or experimental treatment.

Introduction

Livestock production is the main source of livelihood for the majority of human population residing in Sub‐Saharan Africa, particularly for smallholders in the lowland and highland areas of Ethiopia (Abdilatif et al. 2018). According to the data estimation of Central Statistical Agency (CSA 2021), Ethiopia is the most populous country in Africa with its livestock population of about 70 million heads of cattle, 42.9 million sheep, 52.50 million goats, 8.1 million camels, 13.33 million equines, and 57 million chickens. Although the country has huge livestock resource potential, livestock production and productivity have been extremely below the potential due to extremely rampant livestock diseases, lack of an appropriate disease control strategy, recurrent drought, inadequate grazing land and water resource, policy challenges, and low animal genetic improvement in the livestock sector (Abdela 2017; Ayele et al. 2009).

Small ruminants are valuable assets for livestock keepers because of their faster growth rates, low investment needs, high fertility, and better adaptability, even in tough environments, compared to large ruminants. They can, on average, supply over 30% of the country's meat needs and generate profit from exporting meat, live animals, and skins. The annual domestic consumption of sheep and goats in Ethiopia is estimated at 1,078,000 and 1,128,000, respectively (Hirpa and Abebe 2008). Among infectious diseases affecting small ruminants, pasteurellosis is considered one of the priority diseases threatening sheep and goat production in many regions of the country (Alemu et al. 2019) and leading to reduced productivity through economic losses associated with mortality, decreased body weight, market constraints, treatment expenses, and poor thriftiness among surviving animals (Ozbey et al. 2004; Daniel et al. 2006). This is further corroborated by multiple reports from various regions of Ethiopia, emphasizing the need to better elucidate the epidemiology of the disease and to design effective control measures (Berhe et al. 2017; Haji and Abunna 2016).

Although nearly 20 species have been identified in the genus Pasteurella, the two most important respiratory pathogens involved in sheep and goat diseases are Mannheimia haemolytica and Pasteurella multocida (Kehrenberg et al. 2001; Duarte and Hamdan 2004). Pasteurella multocida strains are categorized into five capsular serogroups (A, B, D, E, and F), based on Carter's 1955 indirect hemagglutination test. Moreover, these strains are divided into 16 somatic or lipopolysaccharide (LPS) serotypes using Heddleston's gel diffusion precipitation assay (Heddleston et al. 1972). A notable characteristic of these bacterial pathogens is their extensive serotype diversity, which has important clinical and immunological implications (Fodor et al. 1999; Ilhan and Kele 2007).

When the vaccine was initially developed, its primary objective was to control pasteurellosis caused specifically by P. multocida in sheep and goats. In the field, however, veterinary practitioners often claim that the vaccine is used against any form of pneumonic pasteurellosis in sheep and goats. The identification and their distribution of serotypes for vaccine production requires a thorough understanding of the distribution of highly virulent serotypes and their association with the disease. Because currently available vaccine does not include all serotypes of M. haemolytica, investigating the predominant serotypes linked to respiratory disease is essential for the development of more effective control strategies. In the Afar region, information on the circulating serotypes, seroprevalence, and associated risk factors of M. haemolytica and P. multocida in small ruminants is very limited. Therefore, this study was intended to identify circulating serotypes, estimate seroprevalence, and assess associated risk factors of small ruminant pasteurellosis in selected districts of the Afar region, Ethiopia.

Materials and Methods

Description of the Study Areas

The study was conducted from December 2021 to June 2023 in three districts of Afar region namely (Asayita, Dubti, and Mille), which are located in the administrative zone one of Afar region, Ethiopia. The Afar region is located in northeast of Ethiopia between 39°34’ and 42°28’E longitude and 8°49’ and 14° 30’ N latitude (Figure 1) with a total of annual rainfall fluctuating from 150 to 500 mm, annual mean maximum and minimum temperature of 41 and 22°C, respectively, and the altitude ranging from 120 to 1500 m above sea level. The region shares common international boundaries with Eritrea in the Northeast and Djibouti in the East, and it is characterized by an arid and semi‐arid climate with low and erratic rainfall. The human population is 1.5 million in which the majority are pastoralists who largely depend on livestock production for their livelihood. There are about 4.5 million sheep and 8.8 million goats in the Afar region, most of which are managed under the pastoral production system and few are in the agro‐pastoral production system (CSA 2021).

Study Design and Period

A cross‐sectional study was conducted from December 2021 to June 2023 in selected districts of the Afar region, northeast Ethiopia.

Study Population

The study population comprised sheep and goats older than 6 months belonging to pastoral and agro‐pastoral communities of the Afar region, Ethiopia. Blood samples were collected from animals that had not been vaccinated during the preceding year. For serotyping and seroprevalence analysis, apparently healthy animals of both sexes with no history of vaccination against pneumonic pasteurellosis were included. Animals were categorized into two age groups: young (>4 months to <2 years) and adults (>2 years), according to Tewodros and Anania (2016). Age determination was performed based on eruption patterns of permanent incisor teeth as described by Desta (2009).

Sample Size Determination

The sample size for the serotyping and seroprevalence of this study was determined using the formula described by Thrusfield (2005) using a 95% confidence level, 5% desired absolute precision, and an expected prevalence of 90.17% (Getnet et al. 2022) as indicated below: n=Z2×Pexp(1−Pexp)d2 where n is the required sample size, Z is the multiplier from normal distribution at 95% confidence interval (1.96), Pexp is the estimated (expected) prevalence 90.17% (0.9017), (1‐P) is the probability of having no disease 9.83% (0.0983), and d is the desired absolute precision 5% (0.05).

Accordingly, minimum limits of 136 serum samples were calculated for both species. However, in order to increase the level of precision, a total of 293 serum samples were collected.

Sampling Techniques

The study districts were randomly selected from eligible districts, which were identified based on a large livestock population, accessibility by road, prior reports of respiratory diseases from livestock owners, the absence of vaccination during the preceding year, no reported disease outbreaks, and the willingness of pastoralists to participate in the study. A multistage sampling approach was employed: districts constituted the first stage, kebeles were the second stage, and villages/herds were the third stage. Each village was considered a single flock of sheep or goats (single‐level cluster) sharing common grazing areas and watering points. Lists of sheep and goat owners were obtained from each selected kebele, from which farmers were systematically selected using random sampling. Finally, individual animal from the herd was selected from the herd using a simple random sampling technique for biological sample collection. Selection bias was minimized by using a complete sampling frame from epidemiological units (veterinary records) of the districts. The number of animals to be selected was proportionally allocated to each district on small ruminant population within the districts. A total of 293 sheep and goats fulfilling the inclusion criteria (healthy, non‐vaccinated, greater than 6 months of age) were sampled from the study districts: Asayita (n = 130), Mille (n = 93), and Dubti (n = 70). Sampling was limited to apparently healthy sheep and goats, which may have introduced selection bias, underestimated the true seroprevalence, and limited the generalizability of the findings to the broader population.

Sample Collection and Laboratory Analysis

Blood Sample Collection

Approximately 10 mL blood samples were collected from the jugular vein of each non‐vaccinated sheep and goat using plain vacutainer tubes (BD Vacutainer, Becton, Dickinson and Company (BD), USA) and sterile needles (Stainless steel, BD PrecisionGlide, Becton, Dickinson and Company (BD), USA). Blood samples containing tubes were kept horizontally and allowed to clot without agitation for 2 h at room temperature, and then samples were transported in an icebox to the microbiology laboratory of the college of Veterinary Medicine and Animal Sciences at Samara University, where they were placed tilted overnight at 4°C. Then, sera samples were decanted and aliquoted into cryovials (polypropylene; Corning Cryogenic Vials, Corning Incorporated, USA) and kept frozen at −20°C until shipment to the National Veterinary Institute (NVI), Bishoftu, Ethiopia, for serotyping and seroprevalence estimation purposes.

Serotyping of Pasteurella Species

Sera were serotyped for M. haemolytica and P. multocida using an indirect haemagglutination test (IHAT). Briefly, first, an antigen from stock was seeded in tryptose serum broth and incubated at 37°C for 20 h. The resulting culture was harvested by centrifugation at 2000 revolution per minute (rpm) for 20 min and resuspended in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). The suspension was heated in a water bath at 60°C for 1 h to inactivate viable organisms. It was centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was collected into sterile bottles and used as a capsular extract antigen. Second, to obtain a packed cell volume (PCV), 10 mL of blood was drawn from the jugular vein of sheep in a sterile bottle containing 5 mL Alsevers solution and kept overnight at +4°C. Next, 3 mL of blood was taken and centrifuged three times in 10 mL of PBS at 2000 rpm for 15 min. This was done to wash out the Alsevers solution and other blood cells leaving only the erythrocytes. Third, to sensitize sheep red blood cells, 10µL of capsular antigen, 100µL packed RBC, and 50µL of 50% glutaraldehyde were added. Then, it was incubated at 37°C for 1 h and agitated manually at 20‐minute intervals. After incubation, we centrifuge the suspension and wash it three times with PBS. As a final step, 10µL of PBS was added to the final sediment as a sensitized sheep red blood cell solution. To detect the serotype, 90µL of PBS was added to all wells of V‐bottomed micro‐plates. The first 10 wells of row ‘A’ were then loaded with 10µL of serum sample and serially diluted by pipetting 50µL up to row ‘H’. Then, 50µL of sensitized RBC were added to each well and the plate was incubated for 1 h at 37°C. The plate was kept at +4°C overnight for better readings. Positive reactions were indicated by the presence of a dense clot at the bottom of the V‐microtiter plate wells, while negative reactions were indicated by a uniform suspension of red blood cells. Greater than 50% agglutination was taken as positive, and those showing a hemagglutination reaction at 1/20 dilution and above were taken as positive samples (Ayelet et al. 2004). Each sample was tested for against four serotypes, namely A1, A2, and A7 of M. haemolytica and serotype A of P. multocida.

In this study, an indirect haemagglutination (IHA) test kit for the detection of antibodies against P. multocida was used (P. multocida IHA kit, Cypress Diagnostics, Belgium) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The controls used in the study were serums obtained from NVI, Bishoftu, Ethiopia.

Questionnaire Survey

A semi‐structured questionnaire was employed to gather information on the predisposing factors contributing to the occurrence of ovine pasteurellosis in the study areas. The survey collected socio‐demographic data of respondents and explored factors potentially influencing disease prevalence. Data were obtained through face‐to‐face interviews conducted in the local language (Afar af). Before the main data collection, the questionnaire was pretested to identify and address any gaps. Respondents were randomly selected from each herd, and key epidemiological information, such as species, sex, age of animals, herd size, and location, was documented.

Data Management and Statistical Analysis

The raw data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using STATA version 14.2 (Stata Corp College Station, TX 77845, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the frequency, percentage, and serotypes distribution data, and the seroprevalence was calculated as the number of seropositive samples divided by the total number of samples multiplied by the decimal values by 100%. Differences in antibody seroprevalence among categories of explanatory variables were assessed using Pearson's chi‐square (χ 2) test. Statistical significance was set at p <0.05.

Overall Seroprevalence of Pasteurellosis

In the current study, out of 293 sera collected from the study population and tested using indirect IHAT to check the presence of serotype‐specific antibody reactions against M. haemolytica and P. multocida, the overall seroprevalence of ovine and caprine pasteurellosis was found to be 97.61% (n = 286/384; 95% CI: 95–100). Out of 159 sheep and 134 goats sampled, the seropositivity of Pasteurella infection was found to be 98.11% (n = 156) and 97.01% (n = 130) among sheep and goats, respectively, as indicated in Figure 2 in the study districts of the Afar region. Among the districts, the highest prevalence was recorded in Mille (98.92%), followed by Dubti (97.142%) and Asayita (96.92%).

Individual Level Serotype Prevalence of Pasteurellosis

In this study, four different serotypes were identified (M. haemolytica serotypes A1, A2, and A7, as well as a single P. multocida serotype PA). All animals were found to be seropositive for at least one of the serotypes, with the exception of 2.39%, which were sero‐negative for all serotypes. The analysis also revealed that serotypes A1 and A7 were statistically associated with sex (p < 0.05), with a higher seroprevalence in females (98%). In addition, serotypes A1 and A2 were statistically associated with the district (p = 0.03) and age (p = 039) variables, respectively (Table 2).

Associated Risk Factors With Seroprevalence of Pasteurellosis

Pasteurellosis is a serious bacterial infection affecting both domestic and wild animals. Its severity can be exacerbated by factors that compromise the immune system, thereby increasing susceptibility to infection. In this study, potential risk factors including species, sex, age, and study districts were examined to assess their influence on the prevalence of pasteurellosis. Among the assessed risk factors, only sex was significantly associated with disease occurrence in the univariable logistic regression analysis (p = 0.014). Female animals showed a slightly higher seroprevalence (99.03%) than males (94.25%). Based on the predefined inclusion criterion for multivariable analysis (p < 0.25), no other explanatory variables met the threshold for entry into the multivariable logistic regression model. Consequently, multivariable logistic regression analysis was not conducted (Table 3).

Discussion

Pasteurellosis is a major infectious disease of sheep and goats in the study districts, causing high mortality, reduced weight gain, delayed marketing, increased treatment costs, and overall decreased productivity. Pasteurella multocida and M. haemolytica are the primary bacterial agents responsible for respiratory pasteurellosis across all age groups (Deressa et al. 2010). Given that these animals are raised in extensive, free‐ranging smallholder systems and face numerous environmental and management challenges, pasteurellosis poses a significant threat in pastoral and agro‐pastoral settings (Mohammed and Abdeselam 2008; Engdaw and Alemneh 2015). The present study revealed an overall seroprevalence of ovine and caprine pasteurellosis of 97.61% (286/293), with 98.11% in sheep and 97.01% in goats, indicating widespread circulation of the disease in the study areas. These findings are consistent with previous reports from the Western Amhara sub‐region, which documented a seroprevalence of 90.17% (Getnet et al. 2022) and in Tigray region (92.45%) (Berhe et al. 2017). In contrast, the current study result was higher than previous reports of 83% in Wollo (Sisay and Zerihun 2003), 31.4% in the Tanqua Aberegelle and Kola Tembien districts, northern Ethiopia (Assefa and Kelkay 2018; Ferede et al. 2013) in northwest Ethiopia [A1 (33.1%), A7 (31.8%), A2 (28.5%) and PA (6.6%)], (Abdulkadir et al. 2024) in Lume District, East Shewa Zone of Oromia region, Berhe et al. (2017) [A1 (23.3%), A2 (42.6%), A7 (32%), and PA (14.6%)], [A1 (79·4%), A2 (73·7%), A7 (65·5%) and PA (66·9%)], (Assefa and Kelkay 2018) in Tanqua Aberegelle and Kola Tembien Districts, northern Ethiopia [A1(26.6%), A2(18.5%), A7 (16.1%) and PA (16.9%)], Getnet et al. (2022) [A1 (39.17%), A2 (74.83%), A7 (77.83%) and PA (60%)] and the reports of (Ilhan and Keleş 2007) in Turkey [A1 (16.6%), A2 (21.2%), A7 (6%)]. This very high seroprevalence observed in this study (97.01% in goats and 98.11% in sheep), in the absence of a reported vaccination history, is most plausibly explained by antibodies acquired through natural exposure to Pasteurella spp. Given the cross‐sectional design and single time‐point sampling, the detected antibodies represent cumulative lifetime exposure rather than recent infection alone. Antibodies can persist for extended periods following subclinical or mild infections, thereby contributing to high seropositivity among apparently healthy animals. Although no confirmed vaccination was reported, the possibility of an unrecognized or recent outbreak prior to sampling cannot be excluded, as respiratory infections in small ruminants are frequently underreported or informally managed at the field level, potentially boosting population immunity through natural infection. In endemic settings, repeated exposure via close animal contact, communal grazing, and environmental contamination promotes widespread seroconversion even in the absence of overt clinical disease. Moreover, regional variation in seroprevalence likely reflects differences in husbandry and health management practices, including feed and water availability, housing quality and ventilation, stocking density, vaccination coverage, access to veterinary services, and surveillance capacity, as well as methodological and ecological factors such as sample size, diagnostic test performance, timing of sampling, agro‐ecological conditions, and environmental stressors (World Organisation for Animal Health 2022; Thrusfield 2018).

Serotyping remains an important step in delineating P. multocida and M. haemolytica isolates, as antigens for vaccines are still based on capsular types. The identification of the most prevalent serotypes in cases of pasteurellosis has paramount importance, as immunity between different serotypes is non‐cross‐reactive protective (Fodor et al. 1996). This study reported four different serotypes of M. hemolytica as A1 (95.22%), A2 (91.47%), A7 (97.27%), and P. multocida serotype A (92.83%) and noted that multiple serotypes were found in individual animals, which suggested the presence of co‐infection among the study population in the study areas of the Afar region, Ethiopia. Consistent with our findings, a previous study has reported mixed‐serotype infection on a single animal in Tigray region districts (Berhe et al. 2017). This could be due to the co‐existence of the agents in the respiratory tract of healthy animals, and they may cause disease upon stressful conditions (Mohammed and Abdeselam 2008).

According to the findings of this study, sex was significantly associated with seropositivity of pasteurellosis (p < 0.05). This could be attributed to the physiological vulnerability of female animals during gestation, lambing, and lactation (Quinn et al. 2002). Additionally, factors such as stress, hormonal differences, social behaviour, and genetic variation might contribute to this association. However, other risk factors, including species, age, and study districts, did not show a statistically significant association with seropositivity (p > 0.05). Variations in these factors appeared to have no notable effect on the seroprevalence of most serotypes detected in the present study.

Conclusion

The present serological study revealed a higher prevalence of ovine and caprine pasteurellosis in the study areas, confirming the disease is circulating in the Afar region. The study also identified four different serotypes of Pasteurella bacteria, with a higher seroprevalence of M. haemolytica serotypes than P. multocida serotype A. The diversity of serotypes observed here indicates that currently available vaccines are likely to be inadequate to protect against pasteurellosis. Among the associated risk factors considered in this study, only species was found to be statistically associated with the seropositivity of pasteurellosis among the study population. Therefore, a multivalent vaccine capable of conferring protection against multiple Pasteurella serotypes in a single administration would be a practical and effective strategy; additionally, regular vaccinations with the entire area coverage ought to take place for controlling pasteurellosis endemic areas.

Limitation of the Study

The present study has some limitations, including its cross‐sectional study design, which captures only a single time point and limits the ability to assess seasonal or temporal variation in seroprevalence. Financial and logistical constraints restricted serotype identification to only four serotypes, potentially underestimating the full diversity of circulating strains. In addition, the sensitivity and specificity of the diagnostic tools used may have influenced the estimated prevalence in this study. Furthermore, restricting sampling to apparently healthy animals may have introduced selection bias, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true seroprevalence in the study population.
