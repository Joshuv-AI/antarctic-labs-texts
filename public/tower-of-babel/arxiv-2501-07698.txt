Circle graphs and the automorphism group of the circle
Agelos Georgakopoulos

Abstract
We prove that Aut ⁡ ( 𝕊 1 ) \mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{S}^{1}) coincides with the automorphism group of the circle graph 𝒞 \mathcal{C} , i.e. the intersection graph of the family of chords of 𝕊 1 \mathbb{S}^{1} . We prove that the countable subgraph of 𝒞 \mathcal{C} induced by the rational chords is a strongly universal element of the family of circle graphs, and that it is invariant under local complementation. The only other known connected graphs that have the latter property are K 2 K_{2} and the Rado graph.



Keywords:
circle graph, universal graph, automorphism, Rado graph,

local complementation, Ivanov’s metaconjecture, sphere graph.

MSC 2020 Classification:
05C63, 05C25, 05C10, 05C62, 05E18.

1
Introduction

It is well-known that for every group
\Gamma
there is a graph
G\
such that
\Gamma
coincides with the automorphism group
\mathrm{Aut}(G)
of
G

[
5
,
17
]
. The construction of such a graph
G\
is rather ad-hoc in this generality: it boils down to decorating the edges of a Cayley graph of
\Gamma
in a way that eliminates unwanted symmetries. But it is sometimes desirable to find such a
G\
with additional properties that will help us draw conclusions about
\Gamma
. A prime example is a well-known theorem of Ivanov
[
11
]
, stating that the mapping class group
Mod(S)
of each topological surface
S
of finite type and genus at least 2 coincides with
\mathrm{Aut}(C(S))
, where
C(S)
stands for the so-called curve graph on
S
. This theorem has found many applications, establishing
C(S)
as a central tool in the study of
Mod(S)
; see
[
7
]
and references therein. Ivanov stated the influential
metaconjecture
that one can replace
C(S)
by any rich enough graph naturally associated to
S
in the above theorem
[
12
]
.

Our first main result is similar in spirit to Ivanov’s metaconjecture, and provides a natural graph
\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{C})
coincides with
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, i.e. the group of homeomorphisms of the unit circle. The group
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is on its own a rich and classical topic, a melting pot of algebra, analysis, geometry & dynamics. Several surveys and monographs have been devoted to it, and we refer to them for further study
[
1
,
10
,
14
]
. Several other important (families of) groups are subgroups of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, e.g.
PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
and Thompson’s group.

The aforementioned graph
\mathcal{C}
is what we will call
the circle graph
: its vertices are the chords of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and two chords form an edge of
\mathcal{C}
whenever they intersect (either at a point of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, or one inside it).

Note that each homeomorphism
g
of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
canonically induces an automorphism
\pi(g)
of
\mathcal{C}
. Our first theorem provides a converse that makes it similar to Ivanov’s aforementioned theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

The map
\pi
is an isomorphism from
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
onto
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{C})
.

For the rest of this introduction we will focus on the
rational circle graph
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, i.e. the (countable) subgraph of
\mathcal{C}
induced by the chords with both end-points being rational. The proof of Theorem
1.1
adapts to show that
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}})
coincides with the subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
that fixes
\mathbb{Q}
(Corollary
3.3
).

But
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
are interesting from a purely graph theoretic perspective as well. To begin with, we remark that they are vertex-transitive by construction. However, we will observe that they are not edge-transitive (Remark
3.2
).

Their finite induced subgraphs, which are called
circle graphs
, are studied for a variety of reasons. They are similar to interval graphs as well as circle-arc graphs, which are classical topics. (In fact I expect many of the constructions and results of this note to extend to other such graph classes.) Charactering such classes in terms of their forbidden induced subgraphs is a mainstream question, which remains open for circle graphs; see
[
8
]
for a survey. Nevertheless, Bouchet
[
2
]
obtained a characterization in terms of just three forbidden
vertex minors
. We say that a graph
H
is a vertex minor of a graph
G
, if
H
can be obtained from an induced subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
G\
by a sequence of
local complementation
, where the local complementation
G^{\prime}_{v}
of
G^{\prime}
at a vertex
v
is obtained by swapping edges and non-edges in the neighbourhood
N(v)
. We will show that Bouchet’s result extends to the infinite case (Observation
4.1
).

There has been renewed interest in circle graphs recently, due to a conjectural structure theorem of Geelen
[
15
]
for vertex minors analogous to that of Robertson & Seymour
[
16
]
for minors. The circle graphs thereby play an important role analogous to that of planar graphs in the theory of Robertson & Seymour. Our next result is

Theorem 1.2
.

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
is invariant under local complementation.

This is a rather rare property of graphs, and the only other connected graphs I know that enjoy it are the Rado graph (Observation
5.1
) and
K_{2}
.

Recall that a graph
U
is
strongly universal
for a graph class
\mathcal{C}\ni U
, if every
G\in\mathcal{C}
is an induced subgraph of
U
. Understanding which classes of graphs admit strongly universal elements is a classical topic in infinite graph theory, see
[
13
]
for a survey. The best known such construction is the
Rado graph
, a strongly universal element of the class of all countable graphs. But there are natural classes of graphs that fail to have a universal element even under much weaker containment notions, e.g. the rayless graphs
[
9
]
. Our next main result is

Theorem 1.3
.

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
is a strongly universal element for the class of countable circle graphs.

Problem 1.1
.

Is there a non-empty, connected, countably infinite (vertex-transitive) graph which is invariant under local complementation and is not isomorphic to the Rado graph or
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
? Are there infinitely many such graphs?

In particular, is
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
the unique, up to isomorphism, non-empty, connected, countable circle graph which is invariant under local complementation? It could also be interesting to look for finite (circle) graphs that are invariant under local complementation. I do not know of any connected example with more than one edge.

We prove Theorem
1.1
in Section
3
. The aforementioned facts about
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
are proved in Section
4
. In Section
5
we prove that the Rado graph is invariant under local complementation. In Section
6
we consider a higher-dimensional analogue of circle graphs and conclude with related open problems.

2.1
Orders and the rationals

The
d
-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is the set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid||x,(0,\ldots,0)||=1\}
, where
||\cdot||
stands for euclidean distance. A
chord
of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is a line segment joining two distinct points of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

We let
\mathbb{Q}^{1}
denote the ‘rational’ points of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. The most convenient way to make this precise is by identifying
\mathbb{S}^{1}
with the space obtained from
[0,1]
by identifying
0
with
1
.

The ordering of the set
\mathbb{Q}
of rationals has the property that

for every
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}
there is

c\in\mathbb{Q}
with
a<c<b
.

(1)

In fact this property determines the order type of
(\mathbb{Q},<)
: any countable order
(Q,<^{\prime})
satisfying (
1
) is order-isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Q},<)
. This fact easily extends to cyclic orderings by considering
\mathbb{Q}^{1}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\chi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a bijection that preserves nestedness of pairs of distinct points. Then
\chi
is a homeomorphism.

Proof.

We just need to check that
h^{\prime}
is continuous, and for this it suffices to check that it preserves the cyclic ordering of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
up to inversion. This is an easy and well-known consequence of the fact that
h^{\prime}
preserves nestedness of pairs of distinct points of
\mathbb{S}^{1}

[
3
, §3.4]
.
∎

2.2
Graphs

We follow the terminology of Diestel
[
6
]
. We use
V(G)
to denote the set of vertices, and
E(G)
the set of edges of a graph
G
. For
S\subseteq V(G)
, the subgraph
G[S]
of
G\
induced
by
S
has vertex set
S
and contains all edges of
G\
with both end-vertices in
S
.

Let
G
be a graph, and
v\in V(G)
. The neighbourhood
N(v)=N_{G}(v)
of
v
is the set of vertices
u
such that
uv\in E(G)
. We write
G_{v}
for the graph obtained from
G
by complementing the subgraph induced by
N(v)
, i.e. by deleting all edges with both end-vertices in
N(v)
, and introducing all edges within
N(v)
that were missing from
G
. We say that
G_{v}
is obtained from
G\
by
local complementation
at
v
.

The
intersection graph
of a family of sets
\mathcal{H}
is the graph with vertex set
\mathcal{H}
in which two vertices form an edge whenever their intersection is non-empty.

Remark 3.2
.

(

) implies that
\mathcal{C}
is not edge-transitive: no

can map an edge of

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

It is straightforward to check that

\pi(h^{\prime})=h

To define

h(K_{x})=K_{y}

We claim that

To see that

is injective, suppose

h^{\prime}(x)=h^{\prime}(z)=y

To see that

coincides with some boundary clique

using Lemma

h^{\prime}(x)=y

Easily, for every

By (

4
Properties of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

In this section we prove the facts about
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
mentioned in the introduction. We start with universality.

Proof of Theorem
1.3

.

Let
G\
be a countable circle graph, and let
v_{0},v_{1},\ldots
be an enumeration of
V(G)
. We will realise
G\
as an induced subgraph of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

For each
v_{i}
, let
p_{i},q_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
be the two endpoints of the chord
v_{i}
. We can recursively map the
p_{i},q_{i}
to
\mathbb{Q}
so that their cyclic-ordering is preserved. Indeed, having defined images
\pi(p_{i}),\pi(q_{i})
for every
i<j
, we can use (
1
) to define
\pi(p_{j}),\pi(q_{j})
so that the cyclic ordering of the
p_{i},q_{i}
coincided with that of
\pi(p_{i}),\pi(q_{i})
.

We can then map
V(G)
to
V(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}})
by letting
\pi(v_{i})
be the chord spanned by
\pi(p_{i}),\pi(q_{i})
. The subgraph of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
induced by
\pi(V(G))
is then isomorphic to
G
, because
v_{i},v_{j}
intersect if and only if
\pi(v_{i}),\pi(v_{j})
do by the construction of
\pi
.
∎

Some of the ideas used in the proof of Theorem
1.3
imply that being a circle graph is a finitary property in the following sense:

Observation 4.1
.

Let
G\
be a countable graph every finite subgraph of which is a circle graph. Then
G\
is a circle graph.

Proof (sketch).

Let
v_{0},v_{1},\ldots
be an enumeration of
V(G)
, and let
G_{i}:=G[\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{i}\}]
be the subgraph induced by the vertices up to
v_{i}
. Pick a realisation
R_{i}
of each
G_{i}
as a circle graph, and note that the ordering of the points of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
used by
R_{i}
determines
G_{i}
. By an elementary compactness argument, there is a subsequence of
(R_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
along which these orderings converge, to a sub-order of
\mathbb{Q}^{1}
. This limit ordering defines a realisation of
G\
as a circle graph. We leave the details to the interested reader.
∎

In particular, Bouchet’s characterization of the finite circle graphs in terms of forbidden vertex minors extends to the countably infinite case.

5
On the Rado graph

We record the following basic fact about the Rado graph, which may be obvious to some readers:

Observation 5.1
.

The Rado graph
R
is invariant under local complementation.

Proof.

It is well-known that
R
is, up to isomorphism, the unique countable graph with the following extension property:

for every two disjoint finite subsets
U,W
of
V(R)
, there is a vertex
x\in V(R)\backslash(U\cup W)
such that
N(x)\cap(U\cup W)=U
.

(4)

Pick
v\in V(R)
. Our task is to prove that
R_{v}
is isomorphic to
R
, and so it suffices to check that
R_{v}
satisfies (
4
) by the aforementioned uniqueness property. So let
U,W
be disjoint finite subsets of
V(R_{v})(=V(R))
. We distinguish two cases:

If
v\not\in U
, we pick a vertex
w
satisfying (
4
) in
R
with respect to the sets
U
and
W^{\prime}:=W\cup\{v\}
. Thus
wv\not\in E(R)
, and therefore
N_{R}(w)=N_{R_{v}}(w)
, which means that
w
satisfies (
4
) in
R_{v}
with respect to
U
and
W
.

If
v\in U
, we choose

\displaystyle U^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=(U\backslash N(v))\cup(W\cap N(v))\text{ and }

\displaystyle W^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=(U\cap N(v))\cup(U\backslash N(v)),

and pick a vertex
w
satisfying (
4
) in
R
with respect to
U^{\prime}
and
W^{\prime}
. Then
wv\in E(R)
since
v\in U^{\prime}
, and after the complementation of

, we have

as desired.
∎

6
Further problems

It is known that every finite distance-hereditary graph is a circle graph
[
18
]
. This motivates the following:

Problem 6.1
.

Is there a strongly universal (countable) distance-hereditary graph? If yes, is there a vertex-transitive one?

The definition of circle graphs can be generalised to higher dimensions as follows. A

d
-sphere graph
is an intersection graph of a subfamily of

\mathcal{H}_{d}:=\{\mathbb{D}^{d}\cap H\mid H\text{ is a hyperplane of $\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$}\},

where the
d
-disc
\mathbb{D}^{d}
is the set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\mid|x,0|\leq 1\}
, and
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is its frontier. Thus
\mathcal{H}_{2}
consists of the flat discs with boundary on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

These graph classes are studied in
[
4
]
