Curcumin-loaded tetrahedral framework nucleic acids reshape intestinal homeostasis in ulcerative colitis by coordinating barrier repair, microbiota reconstruction, and immune modulation
Authors: Meng Li, Xiaomeng Yue, Qiangwei Liu, Jifang Cui, Yandi Liu, Liujie Zhao, Lin Yang, Zejie Liu, Muran Li, Jiaojiao Yu, Shao-Kai Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUlcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) primarily driven by oxidative stress, sustained inflammation, barrier dysfunction, and gut dysbiosis. Current therapeutic drugs face limitations including complex synthetic processes, significant side effects, and suboptimal efficacy. Herein, we propose a simple, safe, and high-efficiency therapeutic strategy using curcumin-loaded tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs-Cur) to reshape intestinal homeostasis in UC by coordinating barrier repair, microbiota reconstruction, and antigen presentation modulation. The synthesized tFNAs-Cur demonstrated high Cur loading, stability, water solubility, and biocompatibility. At the cellular level, tFNAs-Cur is efficiently taken up by lipopolysaccharide-stimulated RAW264.7 cells, exerting potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. In the dextran-sulfate-sodium-induced UC mouse model, tFNAs-Cur shows enhanced colon accumulation, attenuates weight loss and disease activity index, preserves colon length, repairs intact crypt architecture, and thereby restores intestinal barrier function. Further transcriptomic and biochemical analyses reveal that tFNAs-Cur alleviates IBD by upregulating H2-DMb1, potentially enhancing antigen presentation, while concurrently suppressing pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6, NO) and promoting anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. Additionally, 16S rRNA sequencing shows that tFNAs-Cur restores a healthy gut microbiota by enriching probiotic Muribaculaceae and suppressing pro-inflammatory bacteria. Collectively, tFNAs-Cur presents a safe, readily translatable, and efficient clinical candidate for UC by coordinating multiple synergistic mechanisms.Keywords: Ulcerative colitis, Tetrahedral framework nucleic acids, Curcumin, Antigen presentation, Intestinal barrier

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, lifelong inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) affecting the rectum and colon [1]. The past three decades have seen a 37.8% surge in global UC prevalence and a 68.7% rise in related deaths, with projections indicating up to 30 million cases worldwide by 2025 [2]. The disease is clinically characterized by bloody diarrhea and weight loss, which are direct consequences of progressive intestinal pathology, including mucosal architectural alterations, changes in lamina propria cellularity, immune cell infiltration, and epithelial abnormalities [1]. The pathogenesis of UC is complex, but is currently believed to involve multiple pathways, including oxidative stress [[3], [4], [5], [6]], defects in the intestinal epithelial barrier [7], dysbiosis of the microbiota [8,9] and abnormal immune responses [7,10], triggered by environmental factors in genetically susceptible individuals.

Current treatment strategies for UC primarily encompass several major approaches [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA) remains the first-line, well-tolerated and topically active option for mild to moderate UC, yet demonstrates limited effectiveness for severe UC [12]. Although glucocorticoids are potent anti-inflammatory agents, their long-term use poses a significant risk of adverse effects, such as osteoporosis [18]. Immunosuppressants are recommended for prolonging remission in steroid-refractory or steroid-dependent patients, but suffer from slow onset, bone marrow suppression, and infection risk [11,13]. Biologics such as anti-TNF-α agents precisely treat moderate-to-severe cases, but carry high costs, risk of primary or secondary resistance, and allergic reactions [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Surgical intervention serves as a curative option for severe UC unresponsive to medication or with associated complications, but carries significant trauma and potential quality-of-life impacts [[19], [20], [21]]. Overall, current clinical treatments primarily focus on immune suppression, fail to restore the epithelial barrier and microbial homeostasis, and cause substantial immune-related systemic side effects [22]. To solve the thorny problem, basic research on UC is shifting from traditional monotherapy toward targeted and multi-mechanism synergistic treatment [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Nanomaterials serve as an optimal platform for synergistic therapy, owing to their inherent capacity for multifunctional integration. Currently developed nanomedicines for the treatment of IBD include nanozymes [2], polymeric nanoparticles [27], hydrogels [25], cell- or bacterial-derived extracellular vesicles [28], plant exosomes [29], and engineered bacteria [30]. However, current methods still face multiple challenges, including long-term in vivo accumulation, poor biodegradability, immunogenicity, risks associated with probiotic application, and complex synthesis processes [31]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for simple, efficient, low-immunogenicity and biocompatible therapeutic strategies against UC.

Curcumin (Cur), the key bioactive compound in turmeric, has been reported to combat UC through attenuating oxidative stress [32], suppressing pro-inflammatory cascades [32,33], restoring gut microbial balance and repairing the intestinal barrier [33,34]. However, the clinical translation of Cur is primarily constrained by its inherently poor water solubility and low bioavailability. To address this issue, diverse drug delivery systems, such as natural polysaccharides [32], proteins [33], synthetic polymers [6], inorganic nanoparticles [35], hydrogels [34], and DNA-based nanostructures have been designed. Among them, tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs), a novel and programmable DNA nanomaterial platform, combine high biocompatibility, mechanical robustness and facile modification, and have shown therapeutic promise in multiple inflammatory diseases [36]. tFNAs have been reported to intrinsically scavenge ROS, suppress cytokine release and repolarize macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. Typically, functionalized tFNAs with specific targeting moieties without loading cargos were employed to treat intestinal inflammation [41]. They also serve as vehicles for siRNA [42,43], miRNA [[44], [45], [46]] or small-molecule drugs that inhibit NF-κB signaling [[47], [48], [49], [50], [51], [52], [53]], and have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in multiple diseases. Thus, tFNAs offered an ideal platform to deliver Cur with improved stability, bioavailability and therapeutic potential. Specially, tFNAs-Cur has shown broad therapeutic potential in radiation-induced oral mucositis, diabetic and postmenopausal osteoporosis, and myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, exerting antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects through the Nrf2/GPX4, NF-κB, TNF-α and Nrf2/KEAP1 signaling pathways [[50], [51], [52], [53], [54]]. Therefore, tFNAs-Cur represents a promising therapeutic strategy for UC driven by oxidative stress and inflammation.

Herein, we proposed the amelioration of UC using tFNAs-Cur through coordinately restoring intestinal barrier integrity, reconstructing the gut microbiota, and modulating antigen presentation to reshape intestinal homeostasis (Scheme 1). The synthesized tFNAs-Cur demonstrated high Cur loading capacity, robust stability, markedly improved water solubility, and favorable biocompatibility. In cellular experiments, tFNAs-Cur can be internalized by lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced mouse monocyte-derived macrophage leukemia cells (RAW264.7) and exhibited significant antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. In mice with dextran-sulfate-sodium (DSS)-induced UC, tFNAs-Cur administration markedly ameliorated disease severity, as evidenced by alleviated weight loss, improved disease activity index (DAI), maintained colon length, restored crypt architecture, and reduced histopathology scores, consequently promoting the recovery of intestinal barrier function. Both transcriptomic and biochemical evidence indicated that tFNAs-Cur exerted therapeutic effects through regulating IBD-related pathways, which involved upregulating the H2-DMb1 gene, modulating antigen presentation, while simultaneously downregulating key pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6, NO) and upregulating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. Additionally, 16S rRNA sequencing demonstrated that tFNAs-Cur increased the abundance of the beneficial bacterium Muribaculaceae, modulated gut microbiota composition, and restored microbial homeostasis. By concurrently addressing multiple disease mechanisms, the synthesized tFNAs-Cur offers a simple, efficient, and biocompatible strategy with promising clinical translational potential for UC.

In summary, tFNAs-Cur ameliorates UC by coordinately restoring intestinal barrier integrity, reconstructing the gut microbiota, and modulating antigen presentation to reshape intestinal homeostasis.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of tFNAs-Cur

Through the specific complementary base pairing of DNA, we first successfully constructed tFNAs using four single-stranded DNAs via self-assembly at 95 °C for 10 min and then 4 °C for 20 min (sequence information in Table S1). Subsequently, at room temperature in the dark, Cur was loaded onto tFNAs by stirring for 6 h, followed by ultrafiltration to yield stable, purified tFNAs-Cur. UV-visible absorption spectroscopy revealed characteristic absorption peaks at 260 nm for tFNAs, while free Cur exhibited maximum absorption at 435 nm. tFNAs-Cur exhibited characteristic double absorption peaks at 260 nm and 420 nm, indicating the successful synthesis of tFNAs-Cur (Fig. 1A). Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) further validated the intact assembly of tFNAs, with Cur loading showing no interference with their structural integrity (Fig. 1B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images demonstrated that tFNAs-Cur were approximately 30 nm in size and retained the characteristic tetrahedral morphology, confirming their structural integrity after Cur loading (Fig. 1C). Analysis by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta-potential measurements indicated the hydrodynamic diameter increased from 16.21 ± 0.27 nm (tFNAs) to 34.17 ± 1.18 nm (tFNAs-Cur) and the surface charge shifted from −7.30 ± 0.72 mV to −15.90 ± 0.14 mV, further confirming its successful formation (Fig. 1D).

At a fixed tFNAs concentration of 200 nM, the encapsulation efficiency (EE) decreased with increasing Cur concentration. The Cur loading efficiency (LE) plateaued at a Cur : tFNAs ratio of 1000 : 1, which was consequently established as the optimal loading condition. At this ratio, EE was 7.06 ± 0.25% and LE was 24.99 ± 0.66%), as indicated by the black arrow in Fig. 1E and F. Release kinetics experiments revealed that tFNAs-Cur exhibited pH-dependent controlled release properties. At pH 7.4, the cumulative release of Cur was approximately 40% within 144 h, whereas at pH 6.0, the release increased to about 70% over the same time period (Fig. 1G–S1). Stability testing showed that after 72 h of incubation in various physiologically relevant media, including water, normal saline (NS), DMEM, and 2% or 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) containing DMEM, intact tetrahedral structures of tFNAs-Cur remained detectable via native PAGE, confirming their robustness in complex physiological environments (Fig. 1H). Furthermore, UV-based stability evaluation of the Cur cargo within tFNAs-Cur in 2% FBS containing DMEM revealed that approximately 50% of Cur remained intact after 72 h (Fig. S2), further supporting the protective role of the tetrahedral framework against premature drug degradation. Crucially, Cur was soluble in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) but existed almost entirely as an insoluble precipitate in TM buffer, whereas tFNAs markedly enhanced its solubility in aqueous solution (Fig. 1I). Collectively, this study demonstrated that tFNAs can served as an excellent nanocarrier for Cur, significantly improving its water-phase solubility.

Cell viability assessment of tFNAs-Cur

To evaluate the biocompatibility of tFNAs, Cur, and tFNAs-Cur, the MTT assay was employed to assess their cytotoxicity toward RAW264.7 cells. The cells were exposed to different concentrations of tFNAs (0 to 250 nM), Cur (0 to 17.5 μM), and tFNAs-Cur (where Cur concentrations ranged from 0 to 17.5 μM corresponding to tFNAs concentrations of 0-250 nM). The results of the MTT assay showed that RAW264.7 cell viability remained above 75% across all tested concentrations, confirming the favorable cytocompatibility of tFNAs, Cur and tFNAs-Cur at the cellular level (Fig. S3A–C). Additionally, LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells (Fig. S4A–C), NCM460 cells (Fig. S5A–C) and mouse embryonic fibroblasts (3T3) (Fig. S6A–C) exhibited similar trends in viability upon treatment with various tFNAs, Cur, and tFNAs-Cur concentrations. These findings demonstrated that the synthesized tFNAs-Cur possessed favorable cytocompatibility.

Internalization and in vivo distribution of tFNAs-Cur

The cellular uptake of tFNAs-Cur was assessed next in LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells, a common model for oxidative stress-induced inflammatory injury. The cellular uptake of Cy5-labeled S1, tFNAs, and tFNAs-Cur was evaluated in LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells. Confocal microscopy and flow cytometry showed that the internalization of all materials increased over time, with uptake gradually rising at 1, 6, and 12 h (Figs. S7–12). At 12 h, flow cytometry analysis revealed that RAW264.7 cells treated with tFNAs-Cur exhibited significantly higher fluorescence intensity compared to those treated with tFNAs alone, indicating more efficient internalization of the Cur-loaded complex, whereas negligible uptake was observed for S1 (Fig. 1J–L).

Ex vivo fluorescence imaging was then used to track the distribution and clearance of tFNAs-Cur in mice. In DSS-induced UC, colonic fluorescence was detected at 10 min after injection, rose to a maximum at 1 h, and remained significantly higher than in control mice for at least 6 h, showing that tFNAs-Cur accumulated more effectively in inflamed colon (Fig. 1M). We further examined the cellular localization and distribution of tFNAs-Cur in the colon tissue. As shown in Fig. S13, clear red fluorescence was observed in CD86-positive macrophages in the colonic mucosa, indicating co-localization of Cy5-tFNAs-Cur with inflammatory macrophages. The increased tissue accumulation is primarily due to heightened vascular permeability in damaged colon tissue [55], which facilitates easier penetration of tFNAs-Cur across blood-tissue barriers and subsequent accumulation within the tissue. In both control and UC mice, tFNAs-Cur was observed mainly in the liver and kidneys, but clearance was slower in the UC model. In both groups, kidney signal was almost gone by 3 h and liver signal was markedly reduced by 12 h, indicating that tFNAs-Cur was eliminated primarily through the liver and kidneys (Fig. S14). Collectively, these results demonstrated that tFNAs-Cur accumulated in colonic tissue, with significantly higher levels observed in the DSS-induced UC group.

Intracellular antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of tFNAs-Cur

To systematically evaluate the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory capacities of tFNAs-Cur, we assessed its in vitro radical-scavenging activity, intracellular antioxidant capability, and suppression effect on LPS-induced inflammation (Fig. 2A). The two commonly used radicals, 2, 2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and 2, 2′-Azinobis-(3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulphonate) (ABTS), were employed to quantify the in vitro radical-scavenging capacity of tFNAs-Cur. UV-visible absorption spectra revealed that the absorbance of DPPH and ABTS in the group treated with tFNAs-Cur were significantly lower than the groups treated with Cur or tFNAs alone, confirming that tFNAs-Cur exhibited stronger radical scavenging capacity than its individual components (Fig. 2B and C). To further validate the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory potential of tFNAs-Cur at the cellular level, we constructed an oxidative stress-induced inflammatory injury model by stimulating RAW264.7 cells with LPS. For antioxidant capacity assessment, intracellular ROS levels were observed using DCFH-DA as fluorescent indicator via fluorescence microscopy. Fluorescence imaging revealed that untreated cells exhibited only physiological ROS levels as extremely low green fluorescence, whereas LPS stimulation significantly enhanced fluorescence intensity, indicating elevated oxidative stress. In contrast, the green fluorescence intensity was markedly attenuated in the tFNAs-Cur-treated group, indicating its superior intracellular ROS scavenging capacity (Fig. 2D). Fluorescence quantification demonstrated that tFNAs-Cur treatment significantly lowered intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 2E).

Given the intrinsic link between inflammatory pathophysiology and oxidative stress, we next assessed the anti-inflammatory potential of tFNAs-Cur by quantifying cytokine release in LPS-treated RAW264.7 macrophages. After treatment with LPS, these cells exhibited significantly elevated secretion of TNF-α, NO, and IL-6, and decreased secretion of IL-10, relative to untreated controls (Fig. 2F–H, S15), confirming robust inflammatory activation. tFNAs-Cur subsequently lowered the three pro-inflammatory factors and promoted the anti-inflammatory factor to levels below those achieved by either Cur or tFNAs alone, evidencing superior anti-inflammatory activity. Collectively, these findings indicated that tFNAs-Cur attenuated LPS-induced inflammation by restoring redox homeostasis and inhibiting key inflammatory signaling pathways, highlighting their dual antioxidant and anti-inflammatory therapeutic potential.

Macrophages are essential components of the immune system and exhibit high plasticity [56], playing critical roles in mediating intestinal inflammation. Flow cytometry was employed to quantitatively analyze the proportions of CD86+ (M1-like pro-inflammatory macrophages) and CD206+ (M2-like anti-inflammatory macrophages) (Fig. 2I–S16A, S16B). First, LPS stimulation was used to induce a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Following tFNAs-Cur treatment, the percentage of CD86+ cells significantly decreased from 84.0% to 37.7%, while that of CD206+ cells significantly increased from 2.92% to 35.9%. These results indicated that tFNAs-Cur treatment effectively suppressed the activity of the M1 phenotype and promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby enhancing their anti-inflammatory capacity. The polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype is crucial for the intestinal mucosal repair process, as it helps suppress inflammatory responses and promote tissue repair and regeneration, thus facilitating effective intestinal mucosal repair [57].

Therapeutic effect of tFNAs-Cur in DSS-induced UC

Based on the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory capabilities of tFNAs-Cur, we evaluated its therapeutic potential in a murine model of DSS-induced UC. Mice were randomly assigned to five groups: Healthy control group, DSS-induced UC group, DSS-induced UC mice treated with 5-ASA (100 mg/kg/day by oral gavage) and DSS-induced UC mice treated with tFNAs (UC + tFNAs, 1 μM) and tFNAs-Cur (UC + tFNAs-Cur, 1 μM tFNAs with 70 μM Cur) (Fig. 3A). Treatments were administered via tail vein injection every other day (four injections over 7 days), except for 5-ASA which was given orally (Fig. 3A). Compared to control mice, DSS-induced UC mice displayed significant weight loss and sustained elevated DAI scores at higher levels, indicating more severe disease progression and confirming successful establishment of the UC model. As shown in Fig. 3B, tFNAs-Cur significantly mitigated weight loss induced by DSS-induced UC. Additionally, tFNAs-Cur-treated UC mice showed a trend toward lower DAI scores (Fig. 3C–Table S2 for the scoring criteria) and significantly improved colon length (Fig. 3D and E). Despite tFNAs demonstrated therapeutic effects in UC mice, tFNAs-Cur proved more effective in controlling weight loss and mitigating colonic damage (Fig. 3B–E).

Colonic pathological changes across groups were further evaluated via H&E staining. As shown in Fig. 3F, the UC group exhibited loss of basic glandular architecture, near-complete crypt atrophy, focal intestinal gland atrophy, and submucosal interstitial edema. 5-ASA, tFNAs, and tFNAs-Cur restored glandular morphology, with tFNAs-Cur achieving the most normal and complete structure and a trend toward reduced colonic pathology scores (Fig. 3G–Table S3 for the scoring criteria). As shown in Fig. S17, tFNAs-Cur still exerted therapeutic effects when administered after disease onset. tFNAs significantly improved body weight, while 5-ASA significantly improved body weight and colon length and showed a trend toward reduced DAI and colonic pathology scores. Notably, tFNAs-Cur outperformed 5-ASA, exhibiting the most pronounced improvements in body weight, colon length, and overall disease activity. Collectively, these results demonstrated that the tFNAs-Cur significantly alleviated UC in mice in the aspect of reversing weight loss, reducing disease activity scores, increasing colon length, restoring glandular morphology.

Restoring intestinal barrier function in DSS-induced UC mice via tFNAs-Cur

Tight junctions (TJs) are critical structures regulating cellular activity and maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, and their function is essential for preventing intestinal bacterial translocation and infection [58]. TJs integrity is primarily maintained through interactions among multiple membrane proteins, including Occludin, zona occludens-1 (ZO-1), and Claudin-1 (CLDN1) [59]. Thus, the normal expression of Occludin, ZO-1, and CLDN1 plays a vital role in intestinal barrier homeostasis. Based on preliminary H&E staining results, tFNAs-Cur demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy in repairing colonic tissue architecture (Fig. 3F and G). To determine the molecular mechanisms, we assessed the expression and distribution of Occludin, ZO-1, and CLDN1 in colonic tissue via immunofluorescence and Western blot (WB). Immunofluorescence results revealed that all three proteins were localized within the glandular structures of colonic tissue. Consistent with histological observations, DSS treatment severely disrupted glandular morphology, leading to disorganized distribution and reduced expression of these proteins. In contrast, in colonic tissues, tFNAs-Cur restored glandular architecture and normalized protein localization, reestablishing near-normal junctional distribution patterns comparable to controls (Fig. 4A–D). WB analysis further confirmed that Occludin, ZO-1, and CLDN1 expression was significantly reduced in the UC group than in the control (Fig. 4E–J). Both tFNAs and tFNAs-Cur interventions partially elevated expression levels of these three proteins, with tFNAs-Cur demonstrating the highest efficacy. To further confirm that these structural changes translate into functional recovery, we performed in vivo fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran (FITC-D) permeability assays, which demonstrated a significant reduction in intestinal permeability in the tFNAs-Cur group compared with the UC model (Fig. S18). These findings indicated that tFNAs-Cur effectively restored the structural and functional integrity of the intestinal barrier by promoting expression and proper localization of key tight junction proteins.

Therapeutic effects of tFNAs-Cur in UC mice by mediating IBD pathway

Taken together, these findings demonstrated the therapeutic effect of tFNAs-Cur in a murine model of UC. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, transcriptomic analysis was performed on colon tissues from control and UC mice, with or without tFNAs-Cur treatment (n = 6). This analysis enabled a comprehensive assessment of differentially expressed genes and associated pathway alterations. The volcano plot analysis revealed 5348 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between Control and UC group, while only 1754 DEGs were identified between the Control and UC + tFNAs-Cur group, indicating that tFNAs-Cur profoundly impacted on the gene expression profile in UC model mice (Fig. 5A and B). A further comparison between the UC group and the treatment group (Fig. 5C) identified only 74 DEGs, with 2 genes upregulated and 72 genes downregulated (Fig. 5C). This suggested that although tFNAs-Cur directly modulated a limited number of genes, it can still induce significant biological effects by targeting key genes. Venn analysis further confirmed that transcriptional changes were primarily driven by the UC disease model itself (Fig. 5D). The Control vs UC comparison showed 3634 unique DEGs, whereas the Control vs treatment and UC vs treatment comparisons showed only 83 and 5 unique DEGs, respectively. The majority of treatment-associated DEGs overlapped with the disease signature, as 1671 of the 1754 DEGs from Control vs treatment and 69 of the 74 DEGs from UC vs treatment were also altered in Control vs UC. Notably, a core set of 26 genes common to all three comparisons remained dysregulated after treatment (Fig. 5D). Together, these findings demonstrated that although tFNAs-Cur reversed only a small subset of disease-associated genes, this limited reversal served as the key driver for activating downstream pathways, restoring mucosal integrity, and ultimately achieving the functional recovery (Fig. 3, Fig. 4).

To explore the functional significance of DEGs after tFNAs-Cur treatment, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted. The GO analysis showed that the DEGs were significantly enriched in terms such as immune/ inflammatory response, defense response, MHC class II complex and MHC class II binding, indicating that tFNAs-Cur modulated antigen-presentation-related pathways in UC (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was executed, which demonstrated pronounced alterations in the IBD-associated pathway (Fig. 5F), which is strongly associated with antigen presentation. Further dysregulation of other key pathways implicated in inflammation was also identified through KEGG enrichment analysis, such as Th17 cell differentiation, Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, and the TNF pathway. To elucidate the mechanisms underlying UC therapy, a heatmap analysis was performed on all DEGs, revealing that tFNAs-Cur led to the upregulation of multiple genes. Among these genes, H2-DMb1 was downregulated in the UC group but showed upregulated expression in both the Control and UC + tFNAs-Cur groups (Fig. 5G). This expression pattern aligns with the established understanding that the pathological progression of UC is strongly linked to an imbalance in intestinal mucosal immune homeostasis [60]. Notably, H2-DMb1 is a key macrophage-specific MHC class II molecule, and its downregulation represents a central event in this immune dysregulation [61,62]. In the chronic inflammatory microenvironment, macrophages polarize toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and undergo epigenetic alterations, such as aberrant histone deacetylase activity or hypermethylation of the H2-DMb1 gene promoter, thereby suppressing H2-DMb1 transcription [[63], [64], [65], [66]]. As shown in Fig. S19, H2-DMb1 mRNA expression was significantly downregulated in the UC group but partially restored by tFNAs-Cur treatment, indicating a modulation in antigen presentation capacity. WB analysis further revealed significantly increased H2-DMb1 protein expression in colonic tissue from the UC + tFNAs-Cur group compared to both the UC and UC + tFNAs groups (Fig. 5H and I). Moreover, consistent with this in vivo finding, the same trend was observed in LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells (Fig. S20A and S20B), suggesting that the therapeutic effect of tFNAs-Cur may be associated with upregulation of H2-DMb1 expression. This upregulation correlated with a shift toward the M2 phenotype, potentially reflecting partial restoration of MHC class II function and promotion of macrophage repolarization [[67], [68], [69]]. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) results quantitatively confirmed that the phenotypic shift induced by tFNAs-Cur was accompanied by a reduction in pro-inflammatory factors, including TNF-α, IL-6, and NO, along with an increase in anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10, thereby demonstrating its potent anti-inflammatory activity (Fig.5J and K, S21, S22). These anti-inflammatory factors act through autocrine and paracrine pathways to further suppress the activation of key inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB, establishing a vicious cycle that reinforces anti-inflammatory responses (Fig. 5E) [70,71]. Consequently, this process restored the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory signals, systematically mitigating mucosal immune activation and promoting tissue repair. However, that direct functional evidence linking H2-DMb1 upregulation to antigen presentation was not assessed in this study, and the relationship remains correlative. Future investigations targeting H2-DMb1 will be required to establish causality.

Reconstruction of the gut microbiota induced by tFNAs-Cur

Recent studies indicated that the gut microbiota, functioning as an ecosystem, played a role in antagonizing pathogens, regulating immunity, digesting nutrients, and generating metabolites [72]. To investigate whether tFNAs-Cur may influence microbial diversity and composition, fecal 16S rRNA gene V3-V4 region sequences were analyzed. The results revealed significant differences in operational taxonomic units (OTUs) across all groups, indicating a potential increase in bacterial richness following drug intervention (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to examine β-diversity indices, which showed trends toward distinct gut microbiota patterns between the tFNAs-Cur group and other groups (Fig. 6B). We then investigated specific compositional factors influencing gut microbiota profiles at the family, OTU, and genus levels. The results revealed that all samples exhibited similar gut microbiota compositions, but relative abundances varied among groups (Fig. 6C–E). Comparative analysis at the genus level across all groups revealed increased absolute abundance of the pro-inflammatory bacteria Proteus and Acinetobacter in UC mice, which appeared to be reduced by tFNAs-Cur treatment (Figs. S23 and S24). Additionally, tFNAs-Cur treatment was associated with an increase in the absolute abundance of Muribaculaceae (Fig. 6F), the keystone fiber-degrading taxon that supports host metabolic homeostasis through the production of short-chain fatty acids, including succinate and acetate, which may contribute to macrophage regulation and maintenance of intestinal epithelial barrier integrity [[73], [74], [75]]. These results suggested that tFNAs-Cur may promote gut microbiota restructuring, characterized by the suppression of pro-inflammatory bacteria and the enrichment of the probiotic Muribaculaceae (Fig. 6G), although causality between microbiota changes and therapeutic effects remains to be further established.

Biosafety assessment of tFNAs-Cur

The biocompatibility of tFNAs-Cur was systematically evaluated through a series of in vitro and in vivo assays. Hemolysis assays demonstrated that tFNAs-Cur, even at higher concentrations (1 μM tFNAs with 70 μM Cur), induced a hemolysis rate consistently below 2.5%, confirming its blood compatibility (Fig. 7A). Body weight progression in treated mice paralleled that of the control group (normal saline), suggesting no adverse systemic effects on growth or metabolism (Fig. 7B). Serum biochemical parameters, including hepatic, renal, and electrolyte markers, remained within normal ranges at multiple time points post-administration compared to controls (Fig. 7C). H&E-stained sections from vital organs displayed no pathological alterations, confirming the absence of tissue damage (Fig. 7D). Together, these data support the favorable biosafety profile of tFNAs-Cur.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we proposed a clinically translatable therapeutic strategy for ulcerative colitis (UC) using tFNAs-Cur, which features high curcumin encapsulation, excellent stability, water solubility, and biocompatibility. In the LPS-induced RAW264.7 inflammatory cell model, tFNAs-Cur demonstrated excellent cellular uptake efficiency and exerted significant antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. In a DSS-induced UC mouse model, tFNAs-Cur treatment effectively alleviated characteristic disease symptoms, including inhibiting weight loss, improving disease activity indices, maintaining normal colonic length and crypt architecture, and markedly reducing histopathological scores, thereby promoting intestinal barrier function repair. Further mechanistic studies indicated that tFNAs-Cur upregulated the immunomodulatory gene H2-DMb1 to enhance antigen presentation, thereby modulating IBD-associated signaling pathways and leading to the suppression of key pro-inflammatory factors (including NO, TNF-α and IL-6) while upregulating the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10. In addition, 16S rRNA sequencing analysis demonstrated that tFNAs-Cur modulated gut microbiota composition, significantly increasing the abundance of the beneficial bacterium Muribaculaceae and promoting the restoration of microbial community homeostasis. Our proposed tFNAs-Cur offers a simple, efficient, and biocompatible strategy for UC treatment through targeting multiple pathways including that barrier repair, microbiota reconstruction, and antigen presentation modulation. These results were based on a single acute DSS model in male mice, providing only partial support for broad therapeutic claims; further studies in chronic or relapse models and in female mice are needed to confirm translational potential.

DMSO was purchased from Tianjin Concord Technology Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). 5-ASA was provided by Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The RAW264.7 cell line was acquired from Nation Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Beijing, China). (Shanghai, China). Ultrapure water was provided by Hangzhou Wahaha Group Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). All chemical reagents and raw materials were used directly without further purification. All experimental mice were purchased from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Preparation of tFNAs-Cur: tFNAs were prepared by self-assembly of four ssDNAs. Briefly, equimolar amounts of ssDNAs were dissolved in TM buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 50 mM MgCl2), heated to 95 °C for 10 min, and then slowly cooled to 4 °C for 20 min. The assembled tFNAs (200 nM) were then incubated with varying concentrations of Cur (10-300 μM) in the dark under gentle stirring for 6 h to form the tFNAs-Cur at room temperature. To remove unencapsulated Cur, the mixture was subjected to ultrafiltration at 2500 rpm for 15 min, repeated three times. The absorbance of Cur in the resulting purified tFNAs-Cur was used for subsequent experiments. The EE and Cur LE were calculated by the following formula:

Characterization of tFNAs-Cur: The formation of tFNAs and tFNAs-Cur was confirmed by PAGE. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the samples were determined using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). Morphology was examined by TEM (HT7700, Hitachi, Japan). To evaluate stability, tFNAs-Cur (1 μM tFNAs, 70 μM Cur) was incubated with different media (DMEM, 2% FBS in DMEM, 5% FBS in DMEM, Saline, PBS, Water) at 37 °C (0-72 h) and analyzed by PAGE.

In vitro Drug Release:The synthesized tFNAs-Cur was concentrated 5-fold and then diluted with PBS containing 0.2% Tween 20 at pH 7.4 or pH 6.0. The solutions were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm. At scheduled time points (15, 30, 45 min, 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 9, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144 h), aliquots were transferred to 30 kDa ultrafiltration tubes for centrifugation, and the filtrate absorbance at 435 nm was measured to calculate cumulative Cur release. Filtrates were returned to maintain constant volume.

In vitro Antioxidant Assays: For the DPPH assay, samples at varying concentrations were mixed with DPPH solution. After 1-4 h, absorbance at 517 nm was recorded. For the ABTS assay, ABTS and potassium persulfate were reacted to generate the radical cation. Cur (35 μM), tFNAs (500 nM), and tFNAs-Cur (500 nM tFNAs, 35 μM Cur) were mixed with the ABTS solution, respectively, and absorbance at 734 nm was measured after 1-3 h.

Cell Culture: The mouse macrophage RAW264.7 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Cell Uptake Analysis: RAW264.7 cells were first treated with LPS (1 μg/mL, 24 h), then incubated with Cy5-labeled S1 (250 nM), Cy5-labeled tFNAs (250 nM) and Cy5-labeled tFNAs-Cur (250 nM tFNAs, 17.5 μM Cur) for 1, 6, and 12 h, respectively. Cells were fixed with 4% PFA, and then nuclei were stained with DAPI. Untreated RAW264.7 cells as the control. Images were captured with using a confocal microscope (A1HD25, Nikon, Japan). For flow cytometry analysis, RAW264.7 cells were incubated with Cy5-labeled S1 (250 nM), Cy5-labeled tFNAs (250 nM) and Cy5-labeled tFNAs-Cur (containing 250 nM tFNAs and 17.5 μM Cur) for 1, 6, and 12 h, respectively, with untreated RAW264.7 cells as control. Cells were subsequently collected and further analyzed by a flow cytometer (FACSVerse, BD, USA).

Cell Viability Assay: RAW264.7 cells at an appropriate confluence were incubated with MTT solution (0.25 mg/mL, 4 h), with or without treated with LPS (1 μg/mL). Following dissolution of the formazan crystals in DMSO, absorbance was measured at 490 nm.

Cellular Anti-inflammatory Activity Assay: RAW264.7 cells were treated under the following conditions for 24 h: Control (culture medium only), LPS (1 μg/mL), LPS + tFNAs (250 nM), LPS + Cur (17.5 μM), and LPS + tFNAs-Cur (250 nM tFNAs + 17.5 μM Cur). After treatment, cell culture supernatants were collected for the measurement of cytokine protein levels (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10) using commercial ELISA kits and of NO (by Griess assay), respectively.

Cellular Antioxidant Activity Assay: For ROS detection, cells were treated for 6 h, and then incubated with DCFH-DA probe (10 μM, 20 min) in the dark. After washing with serum-free DMEM, inverted fluorescence microscopy was employed to observe intracellular ROS levels (IX73, Olympus, Japan).

Animal Model of UC: All animal experiments were conducted according to the institutional guidelines and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University (TMUaMEC 2024012). After a one-week acclimation, C57BL/6 mice (male, aged 6-8 weeks) were allocated into five groups as follows: Control (normal water), UC (4% DSS water + Saline), UC + 5-ASA (DSS + 5-ASA, 100 mg/kg/day, administered orally), UC + tFNAs (DSS + 1 μM tFNAs), and UC + tFNAs-Cur (DSS + tFNAs-Cur, 1 μM tFNAs + 70 μM Cur). On days 1, 3, 5, and 7, tFNAs and tFNAs-Cur treatments were administered via tail vein injection, while 5-ASA was given by oral gavage daily.

FITC-dextran intestinal permeability assay: Intestinal barrier function was evaluated using FITC-D. Mice were fasted for 12 h with free access to water, then administered FITC-D by oral gavage at 600 mg/kg body weight. Four hours post-gavage, blood samples were collected via retro-orbital bleeding, and serum was obtained by centrifugation. Serum fluorescence was measured on a microplate reader (Ex: 492 nm, Em: 517 nm).

Sample Collection and Processing: On day 8, feces were collected and analyzed by 16S rRNA sequencing. Mice were anesthetized, and serum and colon tissues were collected. Then. colon length was measured, and distal colon segments were fixed for H&E staining and immunofluorescence or frozen for WB.

Biodistribution and Biosafety: For biodistribution, Cy5-labeled tFNAs-Cur was injected intravenously into Control and UC mice. Major organs (heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, spleen, stomach, cecum, small intestine, colon) were obtained at specified time points (10 min-12 h) and imaged using an IVIS system (AMI-934M, PerkinElmer, USA). For biosafety, healthy mice received tFNAs-Cur or saline intravenously. Body weight was tracked for 7 days, followed by serum biochemistry and histopathological analysis of organs. Hemolysis was assessed by incubating red blood cells with tFNAs-Cur (125-1000 nM tFNAs, 8.75-70 μM Cur).

Immunofluorescence Analysis: Following permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100, colon sections were blocked using 5% BSA and then incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies (4 °C, overnight) against ZO-1 (1:300), Occludin (1:300), Claudin-1 (1:300) or CD86 (1:200). The sections were then probed with species-appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor dyes, and images were observed by inverted fluorescence microscope.

WB analysis: Protein lysates were extracted from cells or colon tissues After separation via SDS-PAGE and transfer to PVDF membranes, the membranes were blocked for 30 min. Subsequently, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies: anti-ZO-1 (1:10, 000), anti-Occludin (1:25, 000), anti-Claudin-1 (1:4, 000), and anti-H2-DMb1 (1:1, 000). Following washes, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. HRP-conjugated GAPDH antibody was used as a loading control and was incubated for 30 min prior to washing. Signals were detected using an ECL chemiluminescent system.

Transcriptome Sequencing Analysis: RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was conducted by AZENTA Life Sciences. Total RNA was used for library preparation, which included mRNA enrichment using poly (A) selection or ribosomal RNA depletion, fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, and strand-specific library construction with dUTP marking. The multiplexed libraries were sequenced on platforms such as Illumina or MGI platforms using a 2 × 150 paired-end configuration. Quality control was performed using FastQC (v0.11.9) and Cutadapt (v1.9.1). Raw sequencing reads were processed by alignment to the reference genome with HISAT2 (version 2.2.1). The resulting alignments were used to calculate gene expression levels with HTSeq (v0.6.1), followed by differential expression analysis conducted in DESeq2 (version 1.34.0). Functional enrichment of GO terms and KEGG was analyzed using GOSeq (v1.34.1) and hypergeometric tests, respectively.

16S rRNA Gene Sequencing Analysis: To profile the gut microbiota composition, the V3-V4 regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified from total genomic DNA (primers: 338F & 806R) and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq/NovaSeq platform (2 × 250/300 bp) by AZENTA Life Sciences. To process the sequencing data, reads were clustered into OTUs at 97% identity using VSEARCH (v1.9.6) and taxonomically classified against the SILVA 138 database via the RDP classifier. To compare community structures, beta diversity was examined through PCoA based on Bray-Curtis distances. Finally, to identify significant differences in microbial profiles between experimental groups, LEfSe and ANOSIM analyses were conducted.

Statistical Analysis: Quantitative data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1.2), applying an unpaired t-test for comparisons between two groups, or one-way/two-way ANOVA for comparisons among multiple groups, as dictated by the experimental design. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test for all variables except DAI and histopathology scores, before applying parametric statistical analyses. DAI and histopathology scores were not subjected to statistical analysis because the data are highly discrete and do not conform to normal distribution (Fig.3C and G, S16C, S16G). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with significance levels designated as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Complete statistical information was detailed in the figure legends.
