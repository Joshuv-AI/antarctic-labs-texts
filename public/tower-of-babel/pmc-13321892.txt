The association between patent ductus arteriosus and adverse outcomes in preterm infants
Authors: Yaqi Tang, Xiuxiang Liu, Dongxu Wei, Zhixian Ji, Silin Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe impact of patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) and its management on necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) and bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) risk in preterm infants remains debated.MethodsThe present single-center study enrolled a total of 769 preterm infants with a gestational age of less than 37 weeks. The logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors for PDA, NEC, and BPD based on maternal conditions during pregnancy, delivery details, and postnatal neonatal characteristics. Furthermore, the present study evaluated the effects of drug treatment for PDA on neonatal outcomes.ResultsThe incidence of NEC was not associated with the presence of PDA in preterm infants (P = 0.15). However, infants with PDA had a 2.12-fold higher likelihood of developing BPD compared to those without PDA (P < 0.01). Pharmacotherapy for PDA was associated with a 49% lower incidence of NEC and a 59% higher incidence of BPD (P < 0.01). Assisted reproductive technology and maternal diabetes were identified as factors independently associated with PDA in preterm infants. Additionally, infants delivered by cesarean section had a 42% lower incidence of BPD compared to those delivered vaginally (P < 0.01).ConclusionPharmacotherapy for PDA was independently associated with a lower risk of NEC but a higher risk of BPD in preterm infants. ART was an independent risk factor for PDA and served as a confounding factor for NEC. Cesarean delivery was independently associated with a lower risk of BPD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06966-5.

Introduction

The initiation of pulmonary respiration and the removal of the low-resistance placental circuit trigger the process of ductus arteriosus closure after birth [1]. However, the closure is frequently delayed in preterm infants, resulting in a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) [2]. The left-to-right shunt caused by PDA compromises blood flow in the superior mesenteric artery, middle cerebral artery, and renal arteries, leading to tissue hypoperfusion. This hemodynamic disturbance can result in several adverse outcomes, including pulmonary hemorrhage, bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), and necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) [3]. The management of PDA remains a subject of considerable debate in both domestic and international literature, particularly concerning the optimal timing of intervention and the efficacy of closure in reducing the incidence and severity of NEC and BPD. Some investigators contend that early targeted treatment of PDA should be initiated in the immediate postnatal period to mitigate the risk of systemic malperfusion-related complications [4]. A contrary perspective advocates for a more prudent conservative strategy, which involves deferring intervention until the ductus exhibits definite signs of clinical compromise [5].

A widely employed first-line pharmacotherapy for PDA in preterm infants involves the use of cyclooxygenase inhibitors, notably indomethacin and ibuprofen. Although pharmacological intervention with these agents achieves ductal closure rates exceeding 70%, the utilization is associated with an elevated risk of gastrointestinal complications [6]. A study investigating pharmacotherapy for large PDA in extremely low birth weight infants demonstrated that indomethacin achieved ductal closure in 77.7% of cases. Furthermore, no statistically significant difference in the incidence of NEC was observed between the treated and untreated groups [7]. Conversely, findings from another study indicated that the utilization of continuous ibuprofen infusion coupled with early surgical ligation was associated with higher rates of ductal closure and a concomitant reduction in the severity of NEC symptoms [8, 9].

From a pathophysiological perspective, closure of the PDA would be expected to reduce the incidence of BPD. This premise is supported by several animal studies, which have demonstrated that pharmacological closure of the ductus arteriosus improves pulmonary mechanics, reduces total lung water, and attenuates the adverse effects of prematurity on alveolarization [10]. However, certain clinical studies have failed to demonstrate a corresponding reduction in the incidence of BPD following PDA closure [11]. These contentions suggest that the role of PDA in the pathogenesis of BPD may be more complex than previously thought.

Given these divergent findings, it is evident that the relationship between the presence of PDA, its management, and the incidence of both BPD and NEC is multifaceted and likely influenced by a spectrum of factors. Physiologically, the timely closure of the patent ductus arteriosus benefits preterm infant development. In contrast, some studies have reported findings that contradict this assumption. Thus, whether early closure is necessary warrants further systematic investigation. The present study aims to investigate the correlations among PDA, NEC, and BPD in preterm infants, identify associated risk factors, and compare the incidence of NEC and BPD following pharmacological intervention for PDA.

Methods

Case collection

We conducted a retrospective study including neonates with a gestational age (GA) less than 37 weeks who were admitted to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit. The study protocol conforms to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and has been approved and informed by the institutional review board of the ethics committee. The study cohort comprised all infants born before 37 weeks of gestation and hospitalized between January 1, 2022, and July 1, 2023. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) death or withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment within the first 3 days after birth; (2) transfer to another medical facility; (3) presence of major congenital anomalies, including congenital intestinal anomalies, respiratory malformations, and complex congenital heart diseases; (4) missing key clinical data related to PDA, BPD, or NEC. The diagnostic criteria for a PDA requiring intervention, defined as a hemodynamically significant PDA (hsPDA), were as follows: the presence of a continuous left‑to‑right shunt through the ductus arteriosus, with a PDA diameter > 1.5 mm or a PDA‑to‑left pulmonary artery diameter ratio ≥ 0.5, along with at least one of the following: (1) Increased left heart volume load (left heart enlargement), with a left atrium‑to‑aortic root ratio > 1.4; (2) PDA flow velocity ≤ 2.5 m/s or a mean pressure gradient across the duct ≤ 8 mmHg; (3) Left ventricular output > 300 ml/(kg•min); (4) Decreased, absent, or reversed flow throughout diastole in the descending aorta or abdominal aorta. The dosage regimen for ibuprofen is 10 mg/kg for the first dose, followed by 5 mg/kg for the second and third doses. The dosage regimen for acetaminophen is 15 mg/kg every 6 h.

The diagnosis of NEC was based on the modified Bell’s staging criteria: Stage I (suspected NEC) presents with mild, nonspecific symptoms, with radiographic findings that may be normal or show only intestinal dilatation. Stage II (definite NEC) is characterized by distinct clinical manifestations and radiographic signs, including pneumatosis intestinalis or portal venous gas. Stage III (advanced NEC) involves intestinal perforation, peritonitis, or systemic compromise, necessitating surgical intervention. For preterm infants, BPD was diagnosed and graded as follows based on the need for supplemental oxygen at 28 days of life and at 36 weeks’ postmenstrual age (PMA) or discharge (whichever occurred first): Mild BPD was defined as oxygen requirement at 28 days but not at 36 weeks’ PMA or discharge; Moderate BPD as oxygen requirement at 28 days and requiring < 30% oxygen at 36 weeks’ PMA or discharge; Severe BPD as oxygen requirement at 28 days and requiring ≥ 30% oxygen at 36 weeks’ PMA or discharge, and/or requiring positive pressure support (nasal continuous positive airway pressure or mechanical ventilation).

Statistical methods

Data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0). According to the study objectives, cases were stratified into groups based on the presence or absence of PDA, NEC, and BPD. Intergroup comparisons were performed with respect to baseline characteristics, mode of delivery, method of conception, and the incidence of maternal conditions, including gestational diabetes mellitus, gestational hypertension, and eclampsia. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally or approximately normally distributed variables, with group comparisons performed using the two-sample T-test. Non-normally distributed data are summarized as median and interquartile range and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Variables with P < 0.1 in univariate logistic regression were included in a multivariate binary logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (with P > 0.01 indicating good fit), and overall model significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline data of the study population

A total of 769 preterm infants were included in this study. Among them, 430 (56.0%) were male. The median GA at birth was 30 weeks. Of the total cohort, the GA distribution was as follows: late preterm infants (32 < GA < 37 weeks) comprised 5.6% (n = 43), very preterm infants (28 ≤ GA ≤ 32 weeks) represented 75.8% (n = 583), and extremely preterm infants (GA < 28 weeks) constituted 18.6% (n = 143) of the study population. The median birth weight was 1320 g. In terms of weight categories, preterm infants with a birth weight of 1500 ≤ weight < 2500 g accounted for 26.9% (n = 207) of the total cohort, those with 1000 ≤ weight < 1500 g accounted for 51.8% (n = 398), and those with a weight < 1000 g accounted for 21.3% (n = 164). The median head circumference at birth was 28 cm. The most common cause of preterm birth was spontaneous preterm birth (n = 373, 48.5%), with detailed information presented in Table 1.

Potential confounders of primary outcomes

In the present study, a total of 588 infants with PDA received intervention, of whom 392 met the diagnostic criteria for hsPDA, and 196 did not fully meet the hsPDA criteria. However, given the presence of positive echocardiographic findings (PDA > 1.5 mm) and systemic symptoms, we administered pharmacological intervention to all 588 infants with PDA. Among the 588 infants with PDA who received pharmacological treatment, 581 received ibuprofen, and only 7 received acetaminophen. Among the 588 infants with PDA who received pharmacological treatment, 489 (83.1%) ultimately met the criteria for PDA closure on ultrasound examination before discharge from NICU. Based on the presence or absence of PDA, infants were stratified into two groups, and differences in postnatal status, maternal pregnancy complications, and delivery conditions were compared between these groups (Table 1). A significant difference was observed in birth weight and head circumference between infants with and without PDA (P < 0.01). In addition, maternal diabetes and mode of delivery were also significantly associated with PDA (P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Among the cohort, 72 preterm infants were diagnosed with stage II or greater NEC. However, no significant difference was found between the occurrence of stage II or higher NEC and the presence of a PDA (P = 0.15). The mean diameter of PDA before treatment was 2.375 mm, with 274 infants (45.2%) receiving pharmacological therapy. The incidence of NEC was significantly lower among infants with pharmacotherapy compared to those who were not treated (n = 18, 6.6% vs. n = 42, 13.4%; P < 0.01). No significant differences in the incidence of stage II or higher NEC were found between infants born to mothers with gestational diabetes and those not, or between infants conceived via assisted reproductive technology (ART) and those conceived naturally. Infants with larger head circumferences and higher birth weights exhibited a lower incidence of NEC. There were no significant differences observed between the incidence of NEC and maternal pregnancy complications, mode of delivery, or causes of preterm birth (Table 2).

The preterm infants with BPD affected 36.0% of all (n = 277). Of the infants born before 28 weeks of gestation, 91 (63.3%) developed BPD, compared to 182 cases (31.2%) among those born between 28 and 32 weeks of gestation (P < 0.01). The same pattern was observed for weight and head circumference. When the weight was less than 1500 g, the number of BPD cases was 156 (39.2%), and when the weight was less than 1000 g, the number was 93 (56.7%) (P < 0.01). 239 infants with BPD were diagnosed with PDA (86.3%, P < 0.01). Of the 588 infants diagnosed with PDA, those who received pharmacologic PDA closure were observed to have a higher incidence of BPD (n = 131, 22.3%; P < 0.01). Infants diagnosed with NEC developed BPD at a significantly higher rate (n = 36, 50.0%) than those without NEC (n = 241, 34.6%; P < 0.01). The prevalence of BPD was significantly lower in infants delivered via cesarean delivery (CD) (30.9%, n = 167) compared to those born by vaginal delivery (48.2%, n = 110; P < 0.01) (Table 3).

Potential associated factors and independent risks for clinical outcomes

Univariate logistic regression was performed on variables demonstrating significant intergroup differences (P < 0.05) to identify potential confounding factors associated with PDA, BPD, or NEC. Our findings indicate that lower birth weight, smaller head circumference, and lower GA were significantly associated with an increased risk of PDA. Additionally, the use of ART, preterm birth due to eclampsia or placental abruption, and maternal gestational diabetes were also significantly correlated with PDA (Table S1). Therefore, these variables were included in a multivariate regression analysis to further evaluate their independent effects. The findings revealed that very preterm infants weighing < 1000 g and those weighing 1000–1500 g had 2.28-fold and 1.51-fold increased risk of PDA, respectively, compared to infants weighing > 1500 g. Additionally, preterm infants with ART and maternal gestational diabetes had 1.675-fold and 1.54-fold increased risk of PDA, respectively (Table S2). Hence, birth weight, assisted conception, and gestational diabetes were identified as independent risk factors for PDA in preterm infants (Fig. 1).

Independent factors associated with PDA, NEC, and BPD

Based on Table 2, univariate regression analysis was performed on variables associated with NEC in preterm infants. The results indicated that head circumference, pharmacological treatment for PDA, and ART were significantly correlated with NEC (Table S3). Subsequent multivariate regression analysis revealed that for each unit increase in head circumference, the odds of developing NEC decreased by 22%. Moreover, infants who received pharmacological treatment for PDA had a 49% lower risk of developing NEC compared to those who did not receive treatment (Table S4). Therefore, PDA pharmacological treatment and larger head circumference were identified as independent protective factors against NEC in preterm infants.

Univariate analysis indicated that multiple factors were associated with BPD (Table S5). Variables with P < 0.1 were included in the multivariate regression model. The results demonstrated that both head circumference and birth weight remained protective factors against BPD. Preterm infants with PDA had a 2.12-fold increased risk of developing BPD than those without PDA. Among infants with PDA, those who received pharmacological treatment had a 1.59-fold increased risk of BPD compared to untreated infants (Table S6). A better postnatal respiratory status was associated with a reduced risk of BPD. Additionally, CD is an independent protective factor against BPD, whereas NEC is an independent risk factor for BPD (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Optimal management of PDA in preterm infants continues to pose challenges. Current evidence presents a lack of consensus on whether closure influences subsequent adverse outcomes [12]. A study by Sung et al. indicated that non-intervention for PDA was linked to a marked reduction in BPD rates among very low birth weight infants [13]. Others reported that preterm infants with PDA treated with ibuprofen had comparable outcomes to those receiving a placebo [14]. Our findings indicate that in preterm infants with PDA (N = 588), the use of ibuprofen for PDA was associated with a lower incidence of NEC but a higher incidence of BPD when compared to no pharmacological treatment. These results are consistent with those reported in some previous studies [13, 15, 16].

Multivariate regression analysis indicated that pharmacological treatment of PDA was associated with a reduced incidence of NEC, whereas the incidence of BPD was higher among infants who received such treatment. Furthermore, NEC was found to be independently associated with an increased incidence of BPD in our cohort. These findings suggest that the relationship between NEC and BPD is complex and may not be solely explained by shared hemodynamic mechanisms. Previous research indicates that both NEC and BPD share multiple common risk factors unrelated to PDA—such as breastfeeding status and oxidative stress-mediated tissue injury [17, 18]—which were not adjusted for in our regression model. Therefore, while our analysis concluded that NEC is an independent risk factor for BPD, a more rigorous interpretation would characterize NEC as a confounding risk factor. Besides, the closure medication used in this study was primarily ibuprofen, with minimal use of acetaminophen (< 1%). Ibuprofen is regarded as a preferable agent to indomethacin for lowering the risk of NEC [15, 19]. Our findings indicate that NEC is not correlated with the occurrence of PDA. In contrast, pharmacological treatment of PDA appears to reduce the incidence of NEC. Ibuprofen may confer a more direct protective effect on NEC than the benefit achieved solely through PDA closure. However, some studies have also indicated a potential association between ibuprofen use and an increased risk of BPD in preterm infants [20, 21]. While ibuprofen inhibits angiogenesis, it also reduces pulmonary inflammation [22, 23]. This dual nature of ibuprofen may be a key reason for the contradictory conclusions across different studies on its use for PDA closure [24].

Differences in PDA diagnostic criteria, choice of pharmacologic agents and dosing regimens, as well as patient selection across studies, may contribute to the observed inconsistencies in research outcomes. Therefore, we need to refine the clinical application of ibuprofen through more stratified evaluations, aiming to achieve a balance between PDA closure and reduction in the incidence of both NEC and BPD. Although surgical ligation and transcatheter closure are also available as preventive interventions for PDA, current evidence does not support the use of prophylactic surgical ligation in the management of preterm infants [25, 26]. Early catheter intervention may become a key strategy to prevent severe adverse outcomes in preterm infants [27].

In the present study, ART emerged as a factor independently associated with PDA, a finding that warrants further investigation. This conclusion must be interpreted with caution, as potential confounding factors associated with PDA have been adjusted for in the multivariate regression analysis, such as low birth weight, multiple gestation, and lower GA [28, 29]. Additionally, preeclampsia and placental abruption, as indications for preterm delivery, may confound the observed association between preterm birth and PDA. One potential explanation is that these conditions expose the fetus to hypoxia and distress, which are known to influence ductal closure—a hypothesis that warrants further investigation [27].

From a pathophysiological perspective, PDA is hypothesized to alter intestinal blood flow perfusion, thereby compromising intestinal mucosal integrity and function, and potentially increasing the risk of NEC [30]. However, our findings do not support this hypothesis. The development of NEC is a multifactorial process, and other factors such as gut dysbiosis, infection, and feeding practices may play more critical roles. Additionally, ART may act as a confounding risk factor for NEC, likely mediated through its association with lower birth weight, multiple gestation, and preterm birth, which are themselves established risk factors for NEC.

It is worth noting that, GA had little effect on the presence of PDA in infants (P > 0.05), whereas general research suggests that preterm infants with a younger gestational age are at higher risk of developing PDA. Although our results indicate that different gestational ages do not significantly affect the presence of PDA, this may be related to our use of a three group comparison statistical method, or to the fact that we did not strictly restrict the enrollment of infants with hsPDA.

Furthermore, in the present study, CD emerged as a factor independently associated with a lower incidence of BPD in preterm infants. Some researchers have suggested that vaginal delivery may present a higher risk than abdominal delivery for very low birth weight infants [31, 32], and our findings further support this perspective. The CD may reduce mechanical compression during passage through the birth canal, thereby avoiding potential lung injury caused by intrapartum pressure changes. In addition, it may decrease the risk of aspiration of bacteria-containing amniotic fluid or maternal blood, thus reducing the opportunity for pulmonary infection and ultimately lowering the incidence of BPD [33, 34]. However, the precise relationship between CD and BPD requires further validation through large-scale, multi-center studies.

The present study analyzed adverse outcomes in preterm infants with a GA of less than 37 weeks born at a single center over two years and conducted a risk prediction analysis based on maternal conditions during pregnancy, intrapartum abnormalities, and neonatal status after birth. Given that it is not possible to accurately determine whether PDA closure resulted from pharmacological treatment or spontaneous closure, and that not all infants achieved PDA closure after treatment, we cannot precisely assess the efficacy of ibuprofen in closing the PDA. Nor can we fully assume that PDA closure will inevitably occur following ibuprofen treatment, thereby directly affecting the incidences of NEC and BPD. In future studies, we will more precisely identify infants with spontaneous PDA closure and those with closure following pharmacological intervention, and conduct a relevant analysis of adverse outcomes between these two groups.

Conclusion

The present study investigated the association between pharmacologic treatment of PDA and adverse neonatal outcomes. Results showed that pharmacologic treatment for PDA was independently associated with a lower incidence of NEC and a higher incidence of BPD in preterm infants. Specifically, we observed a 49% lower incidence of NEC and a 59% higher incidence of BPD among preterm infants who received pharmacologic treatment for PDA. This finding highlights the complex relationship between PDA management and the development of NEC and BPD, which may be influenced by multiple underlying mechanisms. Further research is needed to optimize PDA treatment strategies in this vulnerable population. Additionally, ART was found to be independently associated with an increased risk of PDA and may act as a confounding factor in the relationship with NEC, while CD was independently associated with a lower incidence of BPD. Overall, this study contributes to a better understanding of the associations between pharmacologic PDA closure and neonatal outcomes and expands the spectrum of factors associated with these conditions.
