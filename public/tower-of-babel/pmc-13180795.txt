Type 2 Diabetes, Glycemic Traits and Overall Cancer Risk: A Large‐Scale Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Aiping Zhang, Xia Zhu, Zhe Li, Yaoyao Li, Meng Zhu, Caiwang Yan, Guangfu Jin, Pengpeng Cai, Min Zhang, Yanbing Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEvidence concerning the associations of T2D and glycemic traits with overall cancer risk and their combined effect with genetic susceptibility remains inconsistent and unexplored.MethodsThis large‐scale prospective cohort study included 475,637 eligible participants from the UK Biobank. We identified 25,887 participants with T2D by clinical data, baseline self‐report data, and biochemistry data. HbA1c and random blood glucose were measured at baseline. The associations of T2D, HbA1c, and random blood glucose with the risk of overall cancer and 20 site‐specific cancers, stratified by gender, were evaluated by the Cox regression models. We calculated the cancer site‐specific polygenic risk scores (PRS) to define genetic risk for each cancer and evaluated the joint effects of genetic risk and T2D on cancer risk. Additive and multiplicative interaction models were established to assess the gene‐T2D interaction.ResultsDuring a median follow‐up of 10.95 years, 41,650 incident cancer cases were observed, including 21,957 males and 19,693 females. For all cancers combined, a higher risk was found in females with T2D (HR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.13–1.28) or high HbA1c levels (≥ 48 mmol/mol) (HR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.10–1.31). Among site‐specific cancers, individuals with T2D or high glycemic traits had a higher risk of pancreatic, bladder, kidney, colorectal, gastric, lung and endometrial cancer. In contrast, we observed inverse associations of T2D and glycemic traits with prostate cancer. Additive interactions between high PRS and T2D were observed in pancreatic cancer risk (with relative excess risk of interaction (RERI) of 2.39 (95% CI: 0.34 to 4.72) and 4.41 (95% CI: 1.09 to 8.62), in males and females respectively), and in prostate cancer risk (with RERI of −0.94 (95% CI: −1.51 to −0.37) in males).ConclusionsOur study suggests that T2D and high glycemic traits are associated with a higher risk of multiple malignant cancers, particularly in females.

Introduction

Cancer poses a significant and growing global burden on public health systems and socioeconomic development. According to the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) 2022 database, there were nearly 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million deaths in 2022 [1]. Type 2 diabetes (T2D), a chronic metabolic disorder marked by insulin resistance and hyperglycemia (such as elevated glycosylated hemoglobin and random blood glucose) [2], constitutes a substantial portion of the worldwide disease burden [3]. The Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 estimates the global prevalence of diabetes at 529 million people. A large number of epidemiological studies have observed that T2D is associated with a higher risk of several malignant tumors, such as pancreatic, bladder, and endometrial cancer [4, 5]. However, the findings regarding the associations between T2D and many other cancers continue to be inconsistent. Moreover, the impact of glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and random blood glucose, two glycemic traits that are more stable and can be measured in a non‐fasting state [6, 7], on the development of overall cancer has not been fully assessed.

Polygenic risk scores (PRS) integrating the cumulative effect of genetic risk by combining multiple loci together have become strong predictors for cancer risk and clinical outcomes [8, 9]. Our group has constructed 20 site‐specific cancer PRSs to assess the effect of genetic risk on certain cancers [10]. Large‐scale studies examined the combined effect of established lifestyle or clinical risk factors and genetic risk on overall cancer risk [11, 12, 13]. However, evidence regarding the joint effect and interaction of PRS and T2D on the risk of some site‐specific cancers, such as pancreatic and upper gastrointestinal cancer [14, 15], remains limited. How the combined effect of T2D and genetic susceptibility contributes to the incidence of other malignant tumors is still unclear.

Leveraging data of the large‐scale prospective UK Biobank cohort, we performed a pan‐cancer analysis to systematically investigate the association of T2D and glycemic traits with the incidence of 16 and 18 site‐specific cancers in males and females, respectively. To identify individuals who are susceptible to certain specific cancers, particularly in patients with T2D, we further investigated the combined association of genetic susceptibility and T2D on cancer risk.

Methods

Study Population

This analysis used the publicly available data from the UK Biobank study. Details about the UK Biobank study design and methods have been described previously [16]. Briefly, the UK Biobank is a large‐scale prospective cohort study that recruited over 500,000 participants aged 40–70 years during 2006–2010. The UK Biobank collects biomedical information through 22 assessment centers from England, Wales and Scotland. The assessment visit included an electronically signed consent, a touch‐screen questionnaire, a brief interview, physical measurements and blood sample collection. Participants also provided consent for the linkage of health information to electronic medical records. Web‐based questionnaires and associated materials can be accessed through their official website (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/enable‐your‐research/about‐our‐data/questionnaire‐data) [16].

After excluding 23,750 participants with a prevalent cancer diagnosis at baseline, those with missing data for diabetes diagnosis (n = 266), and those with non‐type 2 diabetes (n = 2759), a total of 475,637 participants were included in the final analysis (Figure S1).

Ascertainment of Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) and Glycemic Traits

T2D was defined using the following criteria: (1) clinical diagnosis from hospital records identified by the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) code E11; (2) self‐reported diabetes mellitus data; (3) blood HbA1c level ≥ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol); (4) random blood glucose level ≥ 11.1 mmol/L; or (5) taking antidiabetic medications at baseline [17]. In addition, pre‐diabetes were defined as the HbA1c level of 5.7%–6.5% (39–48 mmol/mol) [18].

Biological samples were collected at baseline for detecting HbA1c and random blood glucose levels. HbA1c and glucose levels were categorized using established criteria. HbA1c was classified as low (< 5.0% or 31 mmol/mol), normal (5.0%–5.7% or 31–39 mmol/mol), intermediate (5.7%–6.5% or 39–48 mmol/mol) or high (≥ 6.5% or 48 mmol/mol) [19]. Glucose was categorized as low (< 3.9 mmol/L), normal (3.9–11.1 mmol/L), or high (≥ 11.1 mmol/L) based on established criteria of hypoglycemia and diabetes [15, 20].

Assessment of Covariates

The following covariates were included in our study (Table S1): age (continuous), Townsend Deprivation Index (continuous), ethnicity (white or non‐white), qualification (university graduate or no university degree), Body Mass Index (BMI) (normal/overweight/obese), smoking status (current/previous/never smoker), alcohol consumption (current/previous/never drinker), family history of cancer (no/yes), physical activity (low/moderate/high), dietary intake (including fruit, vegetable, fish, red meat, processed meat, and whole grains), female menopausal status (no/yes), the first ten principal components of ancestry (PCA) and genotyping batch. Sociodemographic factors, including age, ethnic background, qualification, and Townsend Deprivation Index, were assessed at baseline. BMI was classified into three categories: normal weight (< 25 kg/m2), overweight (25–30 kg/m2), and obese (≥ 30 kg/m2) based on the World Health Organization. Family history of bowel, lung, breast, and prostate cancer was ascertained through self‐reports provided by the participants regarding their biological parents or siblings. The UKB Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) assessed the dietary status to determine whether it met the following criteria: ≥ 4 servings a day of fruits and vegetables, ≥ 2 servings a week of fish, ≤ 1 serving a week of processed meats, ≤ 1.5 servings a week of red meats, and ≥ 3 servings a day of whole grains. Alcohol consumption was also assessed by the FFQ. Smoking status and female menopausal status were determined by self‐reports. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was used to assess physical activity levels. For covariates with missing data, a missingness indicator was generated for categorical variables, while missing values in continuous variables were replaced by the median stratified by sex.

Polygenic Risk Scores for Cancers

PRSs for cancers were detailed previously (10), which conducted 20 cancer site‐specific PRSs, including 16 PRSs for males and 18 PRSs for females. Briefly, PRSs for cancers were created by genetic variants on the basis of the largest published GWASs of cancers of European ancestry. We included variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.01 and genome‐wide significance (p < 5 × 10−8). Unavailable variants in the UKB were replaced by their highly correlated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (r 2 > 0.8). When multiple correlated SNPs were identified within the same locus, we selected the SNPs with the lowest p value using the linkage disequilibrium clumping procedure (r 2 < 0.2) in PLINK. PRSs of eight cancers used in this study were created by summing up the identified SNPs (Supporting Information) weighted by effect size on genetic liability to cancers following the model described before [21]. In the analysis on the association of the joint effect and interaction of genetic risk and T2D among cancers, 14,552 participants with missing genetic data were excluded, with 461,085 individuals left for analysis.

Ascertainment of Follow‐Up and Outcome

Cancer diagnosis data from the UK Biobank was ascertained through electronic record linkage with the National Health Service central registers and death registers in England, Wales, and Scotland. We investigated 20 common site‐specific cancers and identified them using the 10th Revision of International Classification of Diseases (ICD‐10). Detailed information about ICD‐10 codes for each cancer is listed in (Table S2). Follow‐up was conducted from the date of baseline examination until the first occurrence of current study outcomes, death, loss to follow‐up, or the censoring date (February 29, 2020, for England & Wales and January 31, 2021, for Scotland), whichever came first. For site‐specific cancer analyses, an additional censoring rule was applied, where individuals were also censored at the diagnosis of any other invasive cancer (excluding non‐melanoma skin cancer) as a competing event.

Statistical Analysis

We fitted Cox proportional hazards regression models to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the associations of T2D and glycemic traits with the risk of total cancer and 20 site‐specific cancers. We used follow‐up time as the underlying timescale, with enrolment as origin, with entry at the date of enrolment assessment and exit at the earliest of cancer diagnosis, death, loss to follow‐up, or censoring. Multivariable models were constructed and progressively adjusted. Covariates were increasingly allocated to 4 models by their attributes: Model 1 included age, Townsend Deprivation Index, ethnic background, and qualification. Model 2 further included body mass index; Model 3, smoking status and alcohol consumption; and Model 4, family history of cancer, physical activity, dietary intake, and menopausal status (female). The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals, and no violations of this assumption were detected.

We applied restricted cubic splines (RCS) within Cox proportional hazards regression (Model 4) to investigate potential non‐linear associations between glycemic traits and the risk of overall and site‐specific cancers [22]. The number of knots (ranging from 3 to 6) were chosen according to Akaike's information criterion (AIC) to prevent overfitting while ensuring a good model fit (Table S15) [23]. The selected knots were positioned at equal percentiles of the HbA1c and random blood glucose distributions.

We categorized participants according to quintiles of PRS as having low (the bottom quintile), intermediate (quintiles 2–4), or high (the top quintile) genetic risk for cancers. Cancer‐specific prediction models were developed using Cox models to analyze the combined effect of genetic risk and T2D. Further, we used an interaction term between PRS and T2D to evaluate the effect modification on a multiplicative scale, with additional adjustment for the first 10 principal components of ancestry and genotyping batch. Multiplicative interaction was assessed using the likelihood ratio (LR) test. The relative excess risk due to the interaction (RERI) and the attributable proportion (AP) were calculated to assess the additive interaction between PRS and T2D on cancer risk [24]. The calculations were based on the hazard ratios (HRs) as follows: RERI=HR11−HR10−HR01+1 AP=RERI/HR11 where HR11 represents the HR for individuals exposed to both risk factors (PRS and T2D), HR10 represents the HR for those exposed only to PRS, and HR01 represents the HR for those exposed only to T2D. The 95% CIs of the RERI and AP were determined by drawing 5000 bootstrap samples from the estimation dataset [25].

We conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our findings. First, we treated incident T2D as a time‐varying covariate. This approach was implemented in a sensitivity analysis rather than a main analysis because T2D as a condition may precede its formal clinical diagnosis and may be inadequately reported at baseline. Our other sensitivity analyses included reclassifying incident T2D by resetting random blood glucose standard as ≥ 7.8 mmol/L, further adjusting for antidiabetic medications use, and excluding T2D defined by HbA1c or random blood glucose level alone. Additionally, we excluded cancer cases diagnosed within the first 2 years of follow‐up. To assess the impact of extreme values, we also performed a sensitivity analysis excluding participants with HbA1c ≥ 15% (or 140 mmol/mol). In sensitivity analyses for the gene‐T2D interactions, the polygenic risk score (PRS) was dichotomized into high and low risk groups, defined as either the top quintile vs. quintiles 1–4, or the top quartile vs. quartiles 1–3. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.1), and two‐sided p values of ≤ 0.05 were considered as significant.

Results

Description of the Study Population

Among the 475,637 eligible participants included in this study, 218,610 were male and 257,027 were female (Table S3). Within this cohort, we identified 25,887 patients with prevalent type 2 diabetes (T2D), of whom 16,314 were male and 9573 were female. We observed higher prevalence of T2D in males (males 7.46% vs. females 3.72%). Compared to participants without T2D, those with prevalent T2D were generally characterized by older age, a higher rate of overweight and obesity, a worse economic status, a lower level of education, and less physical activity (Table 1).

Associations of Type 2 Diabetes With Cancer Risk

41,650 incident cancer cases were included in our study, with 21,957 males and 19,693 females, with a median follow‐up of 10.95 years (IQR: 10.11–11.68). Incident cases of 20 site‐specific cancers were listed in Figure S2. The associations between T2D and cancer risk were progressively attenuated as covariates were incrementally adjusted for in Models 1–4 (Tables S4 and S5). In both sexes, a history of T2D was significantly associated with high risk of pancreatic cancer and bladder cancer when compared to those without T2D (Figure 1). The HR (95% CI) for pancreatic cancer was 1.65 (1.32–2.07) in males and 2.06 (1.54–2.76) in females, while the HR (95% CI) for bladder cancer was 1.38 (1.12–1.69) in males and 1.65 (1.05–2.61) in females (Figure 1). Similar associations were observed for cancer sites of colorectum (HR: 1.16, 95% CI: 1.03–1.30) in males; and stomach (HR: 2.18, 95% CI: 1.38–3.44), lung (HR: 1.38, 95% CI: 1.15–1.66), kidney (HR: 1.91, 95% CI: 1.40–2.61), corpus uteri (HR: 1.50, 95% CI: 1.23–1.82) in females (Figure 1). Additionally, the positive association between T2D and overall cancer risk was only observed in females (HR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.13–1.28) (Figure 1). Furthermore, T2D was significantly associated with lower prostate cancer risk (HR: 0.76, 95% CI: 0.70–0.82) in males (Figure 1). The results remained consistent after redefining T2D by random blood glucose ≥ 7.8 mmol/L, excluding T2D defined by HbA1c or random blood glucose level alone or excluding cancer cases within the first 2 years of follow‐up (Tables S6 and S7). Treating incident T2D as a time‐varying covariate or further adjusting for antidiabetic medications use slightly attenuated the association between T2D and colorectal, bladder or gastric cancer (Tables S6 and S7).

Associations of Glycemic Traits With Cancer Risk

We further investigated the associations of two glycemic traits with overall cancer risk, including glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and random blood glucose (Table 2, Tables S8–S13). After adjusting for all potential confounders, we observed a significant association between high HbA1c level and total cancer risk in females (Table S11). Figure S4 showed the linear association between HbA1c and total cancer risk in females, with each SD increase in HbA1c corresponding to a 3% higher risk of overall cancer (HR: 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.04) (Table 2). We further examined site‐specific cancers. In both sexes, we observed positive associations of the two glycemic traits with pancreatic and gastric cancer, with adjusted HRs ranging from 1.02 (0.94–1.12) to 1.13 (1.07–1.19) (Table 2). For bladder cancer, a positive association with HbA1c was observed in both males (HR: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.01–1.10) and females (HR: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.00–1.17) (Table 2). In sex‐specific analyses, positive associations between HbA1c and cancer risk were observed in kidney (HR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.06–1.16) and endometrial cancer (HR: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.04–1.12) in females (Table 2). Conversely, inverse associations were observed between glycemic traits and prostate cancer risk in males, with HRs (95% CI) of 0.94 (0.92–0.96) for HbA1c and 0.93 (0.91–0.95) for glucose (Table 2). The associations remained consistent when HbA1c and random blood glucose were analyzed as categorical variables (Table S10–S13). Notably, the positive association between HbA1c and lung cancer risk appeared to be driven by participants with pre‐diabetes. Specifically, individuals with pre‐diabetes had a significantly higher risk of lung cancer compared to those with normal HbA1c levels (HR: 1.46, 95% CI: 1.32–1.63 for males; 1.31, 1.17–1.47 for females). These associations remained unchanged after removing cancer cases within the first 2 years of follow‐up, further adjusting for antidiabetic medication use or excluding participants with HbA1c ≥ 15% (or 140 mmol/mol) (Table S8–S14). Excluding patients with T2D slightly attenuated the association between glycemic traits and cancer risk, suggesting that the observed associations were mainly driven by the subgroup with the most extreme glycemic levels (Table S8–S13).

Restricted cubic splines (RCS) curves were employed to flexibly model and visualize the association of glycemic traits with cancer risk (Figures S3–S6). Linear associations between glycemic traits and pancreatic cancer, as well as kidney cancer, were observed in females. Similar associations were noted for prostate cancer in males. We also observed a linear association between HbA1c and gastric cancer risk in both sexes. The level of HbA1c showed nonlinear associations with the risk of lung and bladder cancer in both sexes, and with endometrial cancer risk specifically in females.

The Joint Effect and Interaction of Type 2 Diabetes and Polygenic Risk Score With Cancer Risk

We examined the combined effect of genetic risk and T2D on the eight site‐specific cancers which were associated with T2D in our study (Figure 2). Overall, T2D patients with high genetic risk showed the strongest risk of cancer sites of colorectum, pancreas, lung, kidney, bladder, and corpus uteri compared with the reference group. The HRs (95% CIs) of cancer sites of colorectum, pancreas, and bladder in males were 3.54 (2.82–4.44), 5.70 (3.80–8.56), and 2.92 (1.99–4.30) for T2D patients with high genetic risk (Figure 2). In females, the HRs (95% CIs) of cancer sites of pancreas, lung, kidney, bladder, and corpus uteri in the high joint exposure groups were 7.45 (4.54–12.22), 2.02 (1.36–3.02), 2.57 (1.35–4.88), 4.82 (2.27–10.22), and 3.13 (2.13–4.61), respectively (Figure 2).

Discussion

This large‐scale prospective cohort study provides evidence for the associations of T2D and high glycemic traits with high risk of overall and several site‐specific cancers. Notably, the association of T2D and HbA1c levels with higher risk of total cancer was only found in females. Besides, we identified a positive additive interaction between T2D and PRS for pancreatic cancer and a negative additive interaction for prostate cancer. By integrating T2D, glycemic traits and overall cancer risk within a single, large‐scale analysis, our study offers a uniquely holistic perspective on these relationships, with a specific focus on sex‐specific differences.

In the present study, we found that females with T2D faced a higher risk of total cancer compared to those without. Meanwhile, for per SD increase in HbA1c, the cancer risk increased by 3%, which could be the explanation. Notably, high risk was identified in females but not in males. This sex‐specific finding is consistent with a large‐scale meta‐analysis. That analysis, which pooled data from 151 cohorts comprising over 32 million people, reported that the associations between T2D and cancer incidence and mortality were modestly higher in females compared to males (4). Consistent with previous studies, we observed positive associations between T2D and the risk of pancreatic, bladder, kidney, colorectal, gastric, lung, and endometrial cancer [14, 15, 26, 27]. Moreover, our study provides additional evidence that the positive association for gastric, pancreatic, lung, and kidney cancer was stronger in females compared to males. Similar sex‐specific differences have been reported in previous prospective studies examining lung [26], gastric, and kidney cancers [4]. Our findings underscore the critical need for T2D prevention, with a particular emphasis on females.

Several explanations may account for these sex‐related associations. A systematic review and meta‐analysis of the transcriptomic landscape in T2D revealed that some oncogenic factors, such as VAX2, are specifically upregulated in females [28]. Dysregulation of ovarian steroid hormones and chronic inflammation can be another common underlying mechanism [29, 30]. Specifically, in females with T2D, higher bioavailable estrogen, along with other ovarian steroid hormones, can activate the NF‐kB signaling pathway. This activation leads to the induction of inflammatory mediators such as IL‐1, TNFα, and metalloproteinases (MMPs), ultimately driving inflammation [31]. Moreover, given that T2D is often an obesity‐related disease, gender differences may influence pro‐inflammatory cell subsets in adipose tissue and lead to alterations in oncogene expression [32, 33]. Insulin resistance can induce or exacerbate these biological processes. These mechanisms may be responsible for the observed sex‐related associations.

We also found the significant inverse association between T2D and prostate cancer risk within the UK Biobank, after adjusting for a comprehensive range of confounding factors. Although some studies have linked diabetes to a lower prostate cancer risk [34, 35], the overall evidence remains inconsistent. Our findings may be supported by several potential mechanisms. It has been suggested that T2D and prostate cancer risk may share a common genetic basis, as candidate T2D susceptibility genes like MTNR1B and HNF1B have been implicated in the inhibition of prostate cancer [36, 37]. Furthermore, T2D‐related endocrine changes may play a role. Males with T2D often experience reduced levels of testosterone and androgen, potentially leading to diminished growth and stimulation of prostate cancer cells [38]. Additionally, antidiabetic medications themselves may be protective. For example, a molecular study has demonstrated that PPARγ agonist Pioglitazone inhibits prostate cancer cell proliferation and induces metabolic reprogramming [39].

Several potential mechanisms may support our findings for the positive association between T2D and overall cancer risk. Individuals with T2D often exhibit high levels of insulin‐like growth factor (IGF) [40], which is considered a key factor in oncogenesis [41]. The insulin receptor (IR) is overexpressed in many cancers as the IR‐A isoform. IR‐A can be activated by excess insulin or IGF2 and mediates mitogenic effects and cancer cell proliferation [42]. Hyperglycemia, another critical characteristic of T2D, may directly contribute to oxidative stress in cancer cells [43]. The Warburg effect suggests that cancer cells have higher glucose uptake and ferment glucose to lactate to generate energy even in the presence of sufficient oxygen [44]. Meanwhile, glucose also has a direct effect on cancer cell proliferation, anti‐apoptosis, and invasiveness [45, 46]. Most studies have focused on the T2D‐cancer association, while some investigated the association of HbA1c and cancer risk [47, 48]. However, evidence of random blood glucose with cancer incidence is limited. In our study, the results from high glycemic traits were generally in line with the reports from T2D, although the association between high glycemic traits and colorectal cancer was not statistically significant. Our findings indicated that the associations of T2D with cancer risk may be driven by impaired glycemic homeostasis and emphasized the importance of glycemic control, especially among individuals with T2D.

Polygenic risk scores (PRS) can be used for risk stratification in cancer screening to identify individuals with high genetic cancer risk, thereby improving early cancer detection and enabling precise prevention [8]. A previous study demonstrated that a PRS for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is effective in identifying individuals at higher risk, especially among those with diabetes [49]. Another recent study reported that T2D patients with high genetic risk had more than two times the hazard of incident upper gastrointestinal cancer, compared to non‐T2D participants at low genetic risk [15]. However, the combined effect and interaction between genetic risk and T2D on overall cancer risk have not been fully explored. In our study, we found that the risk of certain site‐specific cancers (including colorectum, pancreas, gastric, lung, kidney, bladder, and corpus uteri) was higher among participants with both T2D and high genetic susceptibility compared to the reference group. In our study, we observed a positive additive interaction between genetic susceptibility and T2D for pancreatic cancer risk. Pancreatic cancer exhibits the highest prevalence of KRAS mutations among various malignant tumors [50]. Emerging evidence suggests that T2D may contribute to pancreatic carcinogenesis by inducing metabolic reprogramming that supports tumor growth. Specifically, T2D can activate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway—an essential downstream effector of KRAS—thereby promoting tumor proliferation in the context of underlying genetic alterations [51]. These findings suggest a possible synergistic mechanism whereby the metabolic disturbances associated with T2D amplify the oncogenic effects of pre‐existing genetic risk, particularly in pancreatic tissue. However, further studies are needed to elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms. Moreover, our study identified a negative additive interaction between genetic susceptibility and T2D for prostate cancer risk. Our findings provide new insight into potential common genetic pathways leading to cancers and support the need for early cancer screening in T2D patients.

There are several potential limitations of this study. First, UK Biobank data has inherent limitations. One significant limitation is the healthy volunteer bias, where participants tend to be healthier than the general UK population. Furthermore, the initial recruitment age range of 40–70 years limits the generalizability of findings to individuals outside this age bracket. These limitations may potentially result in an underestimation of the prevalence of T2D cases. However, the assessment of exposure‐disease relationships may remain generalizable despite participants not being fully representative of the larger population [52]. Second, it should be noted that a portion of T2D cases were ascertained on HbA1c or random blood glucose levels measured at baseline. In the absence of symptom data, relying on a single baseline measurement of plasma biomarkers for the diagnosis of T2D may lead to misclassification. This is because such measurements are susceptible to short‐term variation and may introduce bias due to the randomness in a one‐time assessment. Third, the generalizability of our findings to other ethnicities is limited, as our study was based on participants from the UK Biobank and PRS derived from European ancestry populations. Consequently, external validation in diverse patient populations is required. Fourth, although we discuss several potential mechanisms that could account for our sex‐specific findings, the definitive molecular pathways have yet to be identified. Further investigation into the underlying biology is warranted to fully elucidate these observations. Fifth, the limited statistical power, stemming from both intrinsically low case numbers for certain cancers and the reduction in sample sizes when stratified by gender and further by glycemic level category or PRS, is a non‐negligible limitation of our study. For instance, our analysis of PRS‐T2D interaction in gastric and bladder cancer among females was constrained by the low number of events within the interaction‐specific strata. This increases the risk of model overfitting and producing imprecise risk estimates. Consequently, the hazard ratios for these associations are highly unstable and should be considered preliminary findings that require validation in larger studies. Finally, it is crucial to note that the observational nature of this study precludes any definitive conclusions about causality. Our results therefore reflect statistical associations rather than causal relationships. Although we adjusted for a range of potential confounders, the influence of unmeasured confounding factors cannot be entirely excluded.

In summary, this study reveals a substantial variability in the association between T2D, glycemic traits, and cancer risk across 16 and 18 cancer sites in males and females. In particular, T2D and high glycemic traits had a more pronounced impact on females than on males. We also observed the additive interactions between genetic susceptibility and T2D for pancreas and prostate cancer. Our findings provide strong epidemiological evidence that T2D patients face a higher risk of cancer and highlight the importance of T2D prevention and glycemic control in reducing the risk of many common malignant tumors, especially for individuals with high susceptibility.
