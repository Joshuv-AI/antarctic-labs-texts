Fourier Levels and Almost Sure Bounds on Higher-Order Derivatives in First-Passage Percolation
Ivan Matić, Radoš Radoičić, Dan Stefanica

Abstract
. The variance of first-passage percolation admits a decomposition into Fourier levels indexed by the order of environment derivatives. These Fourier levels capture how local perturbations of different orders contribute to global fluctuations. In this paper, we investigate higher-order environment derivatives and their Fourier-level structure. We prove that derivatives of orders k ∈ { 2 , 3 , 4 } k\in\{2,3,4\} are almost surely bounded below by − ( k − 2 ⌈ k − 2 2 ⌉ ) -\binom{k-2}{\lceil\frac{k-2}{2}\rceil} and above by ( k − 2 ⌈ k − 2 2 ⌉ ) \binom{k-2}{\lceil\frac{k-2}{2}\rceil} . We conjecture that these are the correct bounds for all k k , and we construct explicit environments showing that these extreme values can indeed be attained.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Model

We use the same model and definitions as in
[
24
]
. We refer the reader to that paper for a more comprehensive historical discussion and overview of the literature. For completeness, we briefly recall the model and state the results needed for our proofs.

Let
a<b
be two fixed positive real numbers, and let
p\in(0,1)
be fixed. We consider the finite subgraph of the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(with
d\geq 2
) induced by the box
[-2n,2n]^{d}
. Two vertices
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
and
(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})
are connected by an edge if and only if they are nearest neighbors, that is,

|x_{1}-y_{1}|+\cdots+|x_{d}-y_{d}|=1.

Each edge
e
is independently assigned a random passage time, equal to
a
with probability
p
and to
b
with probability
1-p
. Let
W_{n}
denote the set of all edges in this graph, and define the sample space
\Omega_{n}=\{a,b\}^{W_{n}}
, whose elements
\omega\in\Omega_{n}
represent configurations of passage times.

Given a configuration
\omega
and a path
\gamma
(a sequence of adjacent edges), the passage time
T(\gamma,\omega)
is defined as the sum of the passage times of the edges in
\gamma
. For vertices
u,v
, we write
f(u,v,\omega)
for the minimal passage time among all paths connecting
u
to
v
in the environment
\omega
.

When the destination vertex is fixed, we write
f_{n}(\omega)
, or simply
f_{n}
, for
f(0,nv,\omega)
.

A path
\gamma
is called a
geodesic
if the minimum
f_{n}(\omega)
is attained at
\gamma
, that is, if
f_{n}(\omega)=T(\gamma,\omega)
.

1.2.
The torus model

For some of our results, we work with a simplified and more symmetric model introduced in
[
11
]
, in which first-passage percolation is considered on the discrete torus
\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{d}
. We use the superscript
\tau
to indicate this setting, writing
f_{n}^{\tau}(\omega)
,
f_{n}^{\tau}
, or simply
f^{\tau}
when the dependence on
n
is clear.

The
d
-dimensional discrete torus is the graph with vertex set
\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{d}
, in which two vertices
u
and
v
are connected by an edge if and only if they differ by
\pm 1
modulo
n
in exactly one coordinate.

Let
\Gamma
denote the collection of all nearest-neighbor paths on
\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{d}
that wrap once around the torus in the
x_{1}
-direction. More precisely,
\gamma=(v_{0},\dots,v_{m})\in\Gamma
if
v_{0}
and
v_{m}
have identical coordinates except possibly in the first coordinate, with
(v_{0})_{1}=0
and
(v_{m})_{1}=n-1
.

We define
f_{n}^{\tau}
to be the minimal passage time among all such paths,

\displaystyle f_{n}^{\tau}(\omega)=\min\left\{T(\gamma,\omega):\gamma\in\Gamma\right\}.

1.3.
Environment derivatives

If we denote by
W_{n}
the set of all edges, then the sample space is
\Omega_{n}=\{a,b\}^{W_{n}}
. We will often omit the subscript
n
, when there is no danger of confusion. For each edge
j
and each
\omega\in\Omega
, we define
\sigma_{j}^{a}(\omega)
as the element of
\Omega
whose
j
-th coordinate is changed from
\omega_{j}
to
a
, regardless of what the original value
\omega_{j}
was. The operation
\sigma_{j}^{b}
is defined in analogous way. Formally, for
\delta\in\{a,b\}
, we define
\sigma_{j}^{\delta}:\Omega\to\Omega
with

\displaystyle\left[\sigma_{j}^{\delta}(\omega)\right]_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\omega_{k},&k\neq j,\\
\delta,&k=j.\end{array}\right.

If
\varphi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is any random variable, then the
first order environment derivative

\partial_{j}\varphi
is the random variable defined as

\displaystyle\partial_{j}\varphi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\circ\sigma_{j}^{b}-\varphi\circ\sigma_{j}^{a}.

For two distinct vertices
k
and
l
, we will give the name
second order environment derivative
to the quantity
\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\varphi
. In general, if
S
is a non-empty subset of
W
, the operator
\partial_{S}\varphi
is defined recursively as

\displaystyle\partial_{S}\varphi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{S\setminus\{j\}}\left(\partial_{j}\varphi\right),

(2)

where
j
is an arbitrary element of
S
. The definition (
2
) is independent on the choice of
j
, since a simple induction can be used to prove that for
S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\}
, the following holds

\displaystyle\partial_{S}\varphi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta_{1}\in\{a,b\}}\cdots\sum_{\theta_{m}\in\{a,b\}}(-1)^{\mathbf{1}_{a}(\theta_{1})+\cdots+\mathbf{1}_{a}(\theta_{m})}\varphi\circ\sigma_{s_{1}}^{\theta_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{s_{m}}^{\theta_{m}}.

(3)

The function
\mathbf{1}_{a}:\{a,b\}\to\{0,1\}
in (
3
) assigns the value
1
to
a
and
0
to
b
.

1.4.
Variance decomposition and Fourier levels

A thorough understanding of environment derivatives would lead to a complete understanding of the variance. As shown in
[
24
]
, the variance admits the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{M\subseteq W,\,M\neq\emptyset}\left(p(1-p)\right)^{|M|}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{M}f\right]\right)^{2},

(4)

which highlights the central role of the
L^{2}
norms of the derivatives
\partial_{M}f
in determining fluctuation behavior. Despite their importance, general bounds for these quantities are not yet available.

We rewrite (
4
) as

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}(f)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(p(1-p))^{k}\,\Sigma_{k}(f),

(5)

where
\Sigma_{k}(f)
is the level-
k
Fourier sum of
f
, defined by

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}(f)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\subseteq W\\
|M|=k\end{subarray}}\left(\mathbb{E}\big[\partial_{M}f\big]\right)^{2}.

In this paper, we prove the following theorem, which provides bounds on the first and second Fourier levels in the torus model.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
a
is an integer multiple of
b-a
, then there exists a constant
C
, depending only on
a
,
b
,
p
, and
d
, such that the level-
1
and level-
2
Fourier sums of
f^{\tau}
satisfy

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}(f^{\tau})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cn^{2-d},

(6)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}(f^{\tau})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cn^{3-d}.

(7)

The variance decomposition (
4
) is
essentially Parseval’s identity for the Walsh–Fourier expansion
of
f
; the Fourier coefficients can be expressed in terms of
environment derivatives via a discrete integration by parts, as
noted in
[
44
]
and made explicit in
[
24
]
.
The companion paper
[
24
]
further establishes a
generalization of Talagrand’s and Tanguy’s inequalities

[
45
,
46
]
.

1.5.
What the Fourier-level bounds suggest

Theorem
1.1
casts the variance
conjecture in a new light, not by bringing it closer to resolution,
but by making it more mysterious. In dimension
d=3
, the first two
Fourier levels contribute at most
O(1)
to the variance. If the
widely conjectured bound
\mathrm{var}(f)=O(n^{2\chi})
with

\chi>0
holds in dimension
3
, then the divergence cannot come
from the first two levels. It must come entirely from higher Fourier
levels. This would be a striking phenomenon: the contributions of
individual edges and pairs of edges to the variance are negligible,
yet the cumulative effect of higher-order interactions produces
polynomial growth. On the other hand, if higher Fourier levels are
also well-controlled, as we conjecture, particularly in dimensions

d\geq 3
where the
L^{2}
norms
\|\partial_{M}f\|_{2}
may decay
exponentially in
k
, then the variance itself would be
O(1)
,
implying
\chi=0
in dimension
3
. Either conclusion would be
remarkable, and both remain out of reach.

1.6.
Almost sure bounds on environment derivatives

The sequence
(\mathcal{U}_{1}
,
\mathcal{U}_{2}
,
\dots)
represents the optimal upper bounds for environment derivatives. The number
\mathcal{U}_{k}
is defined as the best upper bound on the
k
-th order environment derivative, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b-a}\max\left\{\partial_{S}f_{n}(\omega):n\in\mathbb{N},S\subseteq W_{n},|S|=k,\omega\in\Omega_{n}\right\}.

(8)

We define an analogous sequence
(\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2},\dots)

of optimal lower bounds. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b-a}\min\left\{\partial_{S}f_{n}(\omega):n\in\mathbb{N},S\subseteq W_{n},|S|=k,\omega\in\Omega_{n}\right\}.

(9)

Theorem 1.2
.

In dimensions
d\geq 3
, the first four values of
(\mathcal{U}_{k})
and
(\mathcal{L}_{k})
are shown in the table below.

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{|c|r|r|r|r|}\hline\cr k&{\quad}1&{\quad}2&{\quad}3&{\quad}4\\
\hline\cr\mathcal{U}_{k}&1&1&1&2\\
\hline\cr\mathcal{L}_{k}&0&-1&-1&-2\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Theorem 1.3
.

The sequences
(\mathcal{U}_{k})
and
(\mathcal{L}_{k})
satisfy

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{k+1}\leq\mathcal{U}_{k}-\mathcal{L}_{k}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{L}_{k+1}\geq\mathcal{L}_{k}-\mathcal{U}_{k},

(14)

for all
k\geq 1
. Moreover, for all
k\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{k}\leq 2^{k-2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left|\mathcal{L}_{k}\right|\leq 2^{k-2}.

(15)

Also, for all integers
k\geq 2
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{k}\geq\binom{k-2}{\lceil\frac{k-2}{2}\rceil}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left|\mathcal{L}_{k}\right|\geq\binom{k-2}{\lceil\frac{k-2}{2}\rceil}.

(16)

Theorem
1.3
implies that
(\mathcal{U}_{k})
and
(\mathcal{L}_{k})
grow exponentially in
k
. Due to Stirling’s formula, these sequences are between
\frac{2^{k-2}}{k-2}
and
2^{k-2}
.

We conjecture that (
16
) are equalities. However, we have formal proofs only for
k\leq 4
and we believe
that it is possible to obtain at least a computer-assisted proof for
k=5
. We don’t have the proof yet.

1.7.
The Edge Perturbation Conjecture

Conjecture 1.4

(Edge Perturbation Conjecture)
.

For all integers
k\geq 2
, the following equalities hold

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{k}=\binom{k-2}{\lceil\frac{k-2}{2}\rceil}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{L}_{k}=-\binom{k-2}{\lceil\frac{k-2}{2}\rceil}.

(17)

The inequalities (
16
) provide a lower bound of
\mathcal{U}_{k}
and an upper bound for
\mathcal{L}_{k}
. The conjectured equalities hold for
k\in\{2,3,4\}
. Moreover, the following easy combinatorial identity provides a really miraculous jump from odd
k
to even
k
.

Namely, if
k\geq 3
is an odd integer, then

\displaystyle\binom{k-2}{\lceil\frac{k-2}{2}\rceil}+\binom{k-2}{\lceil\frac{k-2}{2}\rceil}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{k-1}{\lceil\frac{k-1}{2}\rceil}.

(18)

The equality (
18
) makes the conjecture even more believable: if (
17
) holds for an odd
k
, then it would immediately hold for
k+1
due to the Fibonacci-type bounds
(
14
) and the established bounds (
16
).

The bounds (
16
) were obtained by constructing specific configurations on which the derivatives attain these extreme values. Of course, there is no guarantee that some other configurations won’t violate the Edge Perturbation Conjecture. In our research, we considered several possible families of configurations and wrote computer programs that would go over the members of the families and search for extreme derivatives. The best that we were able to find are the families of configurations that we call
disjoint lanes
, which are surprisingly simple. We have considered variations in which the numbers of lanes was higher than 2, but these turned out to be sub-optimal.

The extremal configurations require
n
to be large, making exhaustive computational verification infeasible.

3.
Disjoint lanes

Our goal is to construct special, extreme environments in which the derivatives attain very large positive values and very small negative values. These environments will be used to establish the bounds in (
16
) from Theorem
1.3
.

The proofs involve somewhat lengthy algebraic calculations. Such calculations are omitted here and are presented in the Appendix.

In this section we will consider the first-passage percolation between two vertices, that we will call source and sink. With minimal modifications, the arguments apply to the first-passage percolation time on torus model from

[

]
.

Fix non-negative integers

,

,

, and

,

6.

6.1.
Influential and essential sets

The definitions of essential and influential edges can be generalized to essential and influential sets. These notions were already introduced in
[
24
]
. We briefly recall the definitions and their main properties, since we will need them in greater detail here.

On an environment
\omega
, a set of edges
S
is called
essential
if every geodesic in
\omega
passes through all elements of
S
. We denote this event by
E_{S}
. As noted in
[
24
]
, we have

E_{S}\;=\;\bigcap_{i\in S}E_{i}.

We say that, on the environment
\omega
, the set of edges
S
is
influential
if
\partial_{S}f(\omega)\neq 0
. The event that
S
is influential will be denoted by
A_{S}
. As shown in
[
24
]
, in general we do not have
E_{S}\subseteq A_{S}
, even for two-element subsets
S
. Moreover,
A_{\{i,j\}}\not\subseteq A_{i}\cup A_{j}
.

Compared to essential sets, influential sets are more complex and less elegant generalizations of their one-dimensional counterparts. Positive and negative values of
\partial_{S}f
correspond to vastly different configurations of geodesics, so we will study them separately. We decompose
A_{S}
as a union of
A_{S}^{+}
and
A_{S}^{-}
, defined by

A_{S}^{+}\;=\;\{\omega:\partial_{S}f(\omega)>0\},\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_{S}^{-}\;=\;\{\omega:\partial_{S}f(\omega)<0\}.

Proposition 6.1
.

For every set of edges
S
, every edge
i\in S
, and every
\xi\in\{a,b\}
,

\displaystyle\left(\sigma_{i}^{\xi}\right)^{-1}(A_{S}^{+})=A_{S}^{+},\quad\left(\sigma_{i}^{\xi}\right)^{-1}(A_{S}^{-})=A_{S}^{-},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left(\sigma_{i}^{\xi}\right)^{-1}(A_{S})=A_{S}.

(122)

Proof.

Let us first provide a generalization of (
21
). Take any function
\varphi
and apply (
21
) to
\partial_{S\setminus\{i\}}\varphi
:

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{S}\varphi\right)\circ\sigma_{i}^{\xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{i}\left(\partial_{S\setminus\{i\}}\varphi\right)\right)\circ\sigma_{i}^{\xi}=\partial_{i}\left(\partial_{S\setminus\{i\}}\varphi\right)=\partial_{S}\varphi.

(123)

Since
A_{S}^{+}=\left(\partial_{S}f\right)^{-1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\sigma_{i}^{\xi}\right)^{-1}(A_{S}^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sigma_{i}^{\xi}\right)^{-1}\left(\left(\partial_{S}f\right)^{-1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\right)=\left(\left(\partial_{S}f\right)\circ\sigma_{i}^{\xi}\right)^{-1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{S}f\right)^{-1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})=A_{S}^{+}.

This completes the proof of the first equality in (

\displaystyle\sum_{\overrightarrow{\xi}\in\{a,b\}^{m}}(-1)^{\mathbf{1}_{a}(\xi_{1})+\cdots+\mathbf{1}_{a}(\xi_{m})}\partial_{k}f\left(\sigma^{\overrightarrow{\xi}}_{\overrightarrow{t}}(\omega)\right)=0.

This contradicts the assumption

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\partial_{S}f(\omega)\neq 0\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\overrightarrow{\xi}\in\{a,b\}^{m}}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\partial_{S}f(\omega)\neq 0\right\}\cap\left\{\sigma^{\overrightarrow{\xi}}_{\overrightarrow{t}}(\omega)\in A_{k}\right\}\right).

Let us analyze the event

\omega
that correspond to the edges of
\overrightarrow{t}

\overrightarrow{\xi}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{\overrightarrow{t}}^{\overrightarrow{\xi}}(\omega)=\omega\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{m}p^{1_{a}(\xi_{i})}\cdot(1-p)^{1_{b}(\xi_{i})}\geq\left(\min\{p,1-p\}\right)^{m}.

The inequalities (

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{\overrightarrow{t}}^{\overrightarrow{\xi}}(\omega)=\omega\right).

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\partial_{S}f(\omega)\neq 0\right)

\displaystyle\rho\sum_{\overrightarrow{\xi}\in\{a,b\}^{m}}\mathbb{P}\Big(\left\{\partial_{S}f\left(\sigma_{\overrightarrow{t}}^{\overrightarrow{\xi}}(\omega)\right)\neq 0\right\}\cap\left\{\sigma^{\overrightarrow{\xi}}_{\overrightarrow{t}}(\omega)\in A_{k}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cap\left\{\sigma_{\overrightarrow{t}}^{\overrightarrow{\xi}}(\omega)=\omega\right\}\Big)

and (

Proposition 6.12
.

be a fixed positive real number. Assume that the Conjecture
6.11
is true. Assume that there exists a positive real number
\alpha_{m}
and a positive real number

such that for all dimensions

, the following holds

\displaystyle\sum_{|T|=m}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\partial_{T}f\neq 0\right)\right)^{1+\zeta}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{-\alpha_{m+1}d}.

(171)

Proof.

We can re-write the summation as

\displaystyle\sum_{|S|=m+1}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\partial_{S}f\neq 0\right)\right)^{1+\zeta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|T|=m}\sum_{k}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\partial_{S}f\neq 0\right)\right)^{1+\zeta}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{|T|=m}\sum_{k}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\partial_{T}f\neq 0\right\}\cap E_{k}\right)\right)^{1+\zeta}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{|T|=m}\mathbb{P}\left(\partial_{T}f\neq 0\right)^{\zeta}\sum_{k}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\partial_{T}f\neq 0\right\}\cap E_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{|T|=m}\mathbb{P}\left(\partial_{T}f\neq 0\right)^{1+\zeta}\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.1_{E_{k}}\right|\left\{\partial_{T}f\neq 0\right\}\right].

(172)

Each geodesic can have at most

\displaystyle\sum_{|S|=m+1}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\partial_{S}f\neq 0\right)\right)^{1+\zeta}

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\frac{b}{a}\cdot n^{1-\alpha_{m}d}.\quad

(173)

We now need to prove that there exist real numbers

and

Appendix A
Evaluations of derivatives in extreme environments

Proposition A.1
.

If
A
and
B
are two integers such that
0\leq A\leq B
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{k=A}^{B}(-1)^{k}\binom{B}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(-1)^{A}\cdot\binom{B-1}{A-1},&A\geq 1,\\
0,&A=0.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

In the case
A=0
, the sum on the left-hand side becomes
(1-1)^{B}
, which is
0
.

It remains to consider the case
A\geq 1
.
Notice that for
B-1\geq k
the following holds:
\binom{B}{k}=\binom{B-1}{k}+\binom{B-1}{k-1}
.

\displaystyle\sum_{k=A}^{B}(-1)^{k}\binom{B}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{B}\binom{B}{B}+\sum_{k=A}^{B-1}(-1)^{k}\binom{B}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{B}+\sum_{k=A}^{B-1}(-1)^{k}\binom{B-1}{k}+\sum_{k=A}^{B-1}(-1)^{k}\binom{B-1}{k-1}.

We substitute
k^{\prime}=k-1
in the second summation.

\displaystyle\sum_{k=A}^{B}(-1)^{k}\binom{B}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{B}+\sum_{k=A}^{B-1}(-1)^{k}\binom{B-1}{k}-\sum_{k^{\prime}=A-1}^{B-2}(-1)^{k^{\prime}}\binom{B-1}{k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{B}+(-1)^{B-1}\cdot\binom{B-1}{B-1}-(-1)^{A-1}\cdot\binom{B-1}{A-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{A}\cdot\binom{B-1}{A-1}.

This completes the proof of (
A.1
).
∎

Proposition A.2
.

If
n
and
k
are non-negative integers, and
A
an integer such that
n+A\in[0,k+n]
, the following holds

\displaystyle\sum_{i=\max\{0,A\}}^{\min\{k,n+A\}}\binom{k}{i}\cdot\binom{n}{n+A-i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{n+k}{n+A}.

(179)

Proof.

This is a famous cats-and-dogs identity. Assume that there are
k
cats and
n
dogs and that we want to count the number of ways to choose a committee consisting of
n+A
animals. One obvious way to count committees is to ignore the differences between cats and dogs. The number becomes
\binom{n+k}{n+A}
. However, we can also do a counting by doing the case-work. If we denote by
i
the number of cats in the committee, then the number
i
must range from
\max\{0,A\}
to
\min\{k,n+A\}
. The number of
(n+A)
-member committees with exactly
i
cats is
\binom{k}{i}\cdot\binom{n}{n+A-i}
.
∎
