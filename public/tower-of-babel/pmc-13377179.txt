Deciphering electrochemomechanical interplay in rechargeable aqueous Zn||MnO2 batteries
Authors: Canbin Deng, Yizhan Xie, Jiayue Tang, Xibin Lu, Yiqing Li, Lu-Tao Weng, Biao Li, Kaikai Li, Jiaqiang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical reactions are generally accompanied by mechanical evolutions, which, in turn, play a critical role in the performance of the electrochemical system. In aqueous Zn||MnO2 batteries, the intrinsically structural instability of MnO2 and rampant side reactions create considerable strain/stress changes in operation. However, the electrochemistry-mechanics-performance relationship of the Zn||MnO2 cell is still missing. Herein, we decode the electrochemomechanical interplay of Zn||β-MnO2 pouch cells with different electrolytes via optical fiber sensors. The operando stress monitoring provides proof of the prevailing proton intercalation and Mn dissolution/deposition mechanisms in the system, where the basic zinc salts play a dominant role in stress evolution. Additionally, the non-monotonic stress variation during discharge implies Zn-compensated reaction. For cycling ageing, a negative correlation is found between early stress amplitudes and capacity retention in long cycles. For calendar ageing, greater stress variation during open-circuit ageing is linked with more severe self-discharge. Altogether, this work provides a deep understanding of the electrochemistry-mechanics-performance correlation in aqueous Zn||MnO2 batteries, offering additional tools for electrolyte screening and battery design.

Introduction

Aqueous rechargeable batteries, as a candidate for renewable energy storage, attract more and more interest from both academia and industry1. Among them, aqueous rechargeable Zn||MnO2 batteries have emerged as a promising alternative for large-scale energy storage due to the abundance of both Zn and MnO2, low cost, and high safety of the battery system2,3. Despite various electrolyte4,5, separator, electrode design and optimization strategies6,7 to improve the battery performance, the reaction mechanism of the Zn||MnO2 system remains controversial8. Therefore, sophisticated techniques have been applied to study the reaction mechanism elaborately, including the in situ electrochemical quartz crystal microbalance technique9, and multimodal synchrotron X-ray characterization approach10, etc. These works mainly focused on the composition-structure-chemistry correlation to study the reaction mechanisms and performance degradation of this system. However, the volume variation inside the cell related to different reaction processes has long been ignored in this system, which will lead to different strain/stress evolution and may provide extra information or evidence for the relevant reaction mechanism. Besides, operando stress monitoring in extended cycles has high potential to reflect the performance decay of the batteries. Relevant research on the electromechanics of the Zn/MnO2 system is lacking and needs exploration to better understand the reaction mechanism and performance degradation. Moreover, we conducted our operando experiments with pouch cells to unravel the reaction mechanisms under the working conditions closer to real applications.

Early in 1986, ZnSO4-based aqueous electrolyte has been used to improve the reversibility of the battery2. The effectiveness of this strategy was reiterated in 2012, where a Zn2+ intercalation/deintercalation mechanism was proposed for the Zn||α-MnO2 battery to form ZnMn2O4 during discharge11. In 2016, MnSO4 additive was added to the electrolyte to modulate the dissolution equilibrium of Mn2+ from the MnO2 positive electrodes and improve the stability of the electrodes12, where a reversible conversion reaction mechanism between α-MnO2 and H+ was demonstrated. In 2021, Liu et al. proposed a proton conversion and Mn ion dissolution/deposition mechanism of β-MnO2, where they claimed that the β-MnO2 will be transformed to MnOOH and Mn2+ during the first discharge and deposition will take place to form ε-MnO2 during charge13. The pH change of the electrolyte was highlighted, which increased during discharge and decreased during charge, leading to the formation/disappearance of Zn4SO4(OH)6•5H2O (zinc hydroxy sulfate, ZHS)13. In 2024, Wu et al. reported a dominated H3O+ insertion mechanism in Zn||α-MnO2 batteries15 and a dominated proton de/intercalation mechanism in Zn||β-MnO2 batteries16. As seen above, different reaction mechanisms have been proposed in different electrolytes. Chemical reactivity at the electrode/electrolyte interface induces structural degradation of active materials and further leads to deteriorated capacity retention17. The obvious variation in reactants and products during the reaction processes will intuitively lead to volumetric change and thus strain/stress change in the batteries. This may provide an essential clue for the reaction pathways in Zn||MnO2 cells and deserves in-depth research.

The mystery of strain/stress evolution waits to be unveiled since mechanical integrity is crucial to battery performance, as seen in many other systems. Severe volume change will cause contact loss and cracking of the electrode, resulting in mechanical failure and deteriorated performance. Recently, some techniques have been applied to investigate the mechanical behavior in batteries, such as the pressure sensors for operando pressure measurement in Li-ion cells18,19, and an operando digital image correlation-based technique for chemical strain measurement of the VOOH positive electrode20. However, the pressure or strain measurements of the aforementioned research still encounter some limitations, either the pressure sensors have to be placed outside the cells or specially designed cells are required. Hence, there is an urgent need for nondisruptive techniques for interior operando strain/stress monitoring in traditional cell configurations. Recently, the fiber Bragg grating (FBG) sensor has aroused great interest due to its versatile capability to monitor multiple physical variables, including temperature (T)21,22, pressure (P), and strain (ε)23,24, pioneered by Pinto et al.25 and PARC26. The small diameter, anti-electromagnetic interference and high chemical-corrosion resistance allow facile insertion of the FBG sensor in the batteries and internal information collection during electrochemical tests27. Albero Blanquer et al. applied the optical FBG sensor to probe the chemo-mechanical stress evolution at the electrode/electrolyte interface during battery cycling, providing material-level information that cannot be sensed by external force sensors28. In addition, Huang’s group also elucidated the correlation between structure change and stress evolution of sulfur positive electrode27 and polycrystalline LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (P-NCM811) positive electrode via FBG sensor monitoring29.

Inspired by the aforementioned research, we unveil the correlation between electrochemical reactions, stress evolution, and cell performance in the Zn||MnO2 system with different electrolytes via operando FBG sensor monitoring. In combination with in situ/ex situ X-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman, pH, inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES), transmission electron microscope (TEM), scanning electron microscope (SEM) and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), the composition and structure evolution of the electrodes and electrolytes are meticulously analyzed. Based on the comprehensive information, the reaction mechanism of Zn||MnO2 batteries with different electrolytes is elucidated. It turns out that this system follows a dominant proton conversion and MnO2 dissolution/deposition mechanism in ZnSO4, Zn(OTf)2 and ZnSO4 + MnSO4 electrolytes. Simultaneously, the basic zinc salts will appear/disappear during cycling due to the varied pH of the electrolytes. The stress monitoring via FBG sensors not only helps better understand the reaction mechanism from a chemo-mechanical view but also links the mechanics with battery performance (both cycling and calendar ageing), providing critical insights into the battery design and optimization.

Results

In order to better understand the reaction mechanism of the Zn||MnO2 system in commercial applications, we decided to implement stress monitoring with an FBG sensor in pouch cells rather than coin cells. Different from the relatively low-loading positive electrodes in coin cells, a high-loading (~10 mg cm−2) positive electrode was prepared for the pouch cells in this work. Three typical aqueous electrolytes (2 M ZnSO4, 2 M Zn(OTf)2, and 2 M ZnSO4 + 0.1 M MnSO4) were employed, and their effects on the electrochemistry-mechanics-performance of the cells are compared (Fig. 1a). The structures of H2O and anions in Fig. 1a were obtained from the VESTA software30. According to the XRD pattern (Fig. 1b), the MnO2 powder used for the positive electrode shows a pure phase of β-MnO2 (PDF#72-1984) with micron-scale particles (inset in Fig. 1b). The crystallographic volumes of main phases involved in the reactions of the Zn/MnO2 system are normalized against Mn or Zn and listed in Fig. 1c, d. The volume parameters of different crystalline phases are shown in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. The volume change between the Mn-based phases can exceed 30%. The formation of ZHS as a by-product will affect the volume of the battery, apparently (Fig. 1d). The cyclic voltammetry (CV) test was carried out in Zn||β-MnO2 cells with different electrolytes. In all three electrolytes, a pair of redox peaks appears in the first cycle (Fig. 1e), which gradually evolves into two pairs of peaks in the following cycles (Fig. 1f, g). The emerging two pairs of redox peaks suggest a two-step charge storage process (Fig. 1f, g)31. In Fig. 1g, the peak voltages are exactly the same in ZnSO4 and ZnSO4 + MnSO4 electrolytes, while the peak area in ZnSO4 + MnSO4 is larger than that in ZnSO4, suggesting increased capacity with MnSO4 addition32. The two different pairs of redox peaks can be attributed to the MnO2 → MnOOH process (1.37/1.61 V) and MnOOH → Mn2+ process (1.27/1.56 V)11. Compared with the two electrolytes, the voltages of the paired redox peaks (1.37/1.59 V, 1.15/1.54 V) are slightly lower in the Zn(OTf)2 electrolyte (Fig. 1g), which is due to the anion effect. Compared to SO42− anion, OTf− features a higher electrostatic potential and lower molecular polarity index, therefore higher hydrophobicity. This causes H2O depopulation near the positive electrode surface, kinetically impeding the protonation process required for the Mn dissolution process, thus leading to a lowered discharge voltage plateau14. The evolution of the CV curves is consistent with the galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) curves in Supplementary Fig. 1. The different redox peaks in CV curves or different voltage plateaus in GCD curves suggest varied reaction processes with phase/volume changes, which will definitely lead to diversified stress evolution in the cells. According to the fitting results (Supplementary Fig. 2) of the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), the charge transfer resistance (Rct) of the coin cell with ZnSO4, Zn(OTf)2, and ZnSO4 + MnSO4 electrolytes is 717.5, 2286, and 413.4 Ω, respectively. After 10 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 3), the Rct of the coin cell with ZnSO4, Zn(OTf)2, and ZnSO4 + MnSO4 electrolytes is 10.8, 6.9, and 10.7 Ω, respectively, which is smaller than that before cycling (Supplementary Fig. 2). It has been reported that the ZHS formation can lead to high Rct of the cells33. The weaker signal of ZHS/ZHT at the MnO2 electrodes after 10 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 34a) compared with those before cycling (Supplementary Fig. 47a) contributes to the smaller Rct of the cells. Besides the basic zinc salt by-product, the activation of electrode materials34 and increased roughness of the electrode surface35,36 are also possible reasons for the decreased Rct after cycling.

The cycling performance of pouch cells with different electrolytes is compared (Supplementary Fig. 4). Fast capacity decay is observed in all three electrolytes, suggesting poor cell stability. The reasons behind the poor performance should be carefully studied before the successful application of the high-loading MnO2 electrodes. Nevertheless, the use of Zn(OTf)2 and ZnSO4 + MnSO4 electrolytes still brings some improvement in the capacity retention (15.7 and 14.5%) after 100 cycles compared with the ZnSO4 electrolyte (5.5%). According to the XRD patterns after 100 cycles (Fig. 1h), a new phase, ZnMn2O4 (or Mn3O4), was formed on the positive electrodes, which was considered irreversible and contributes to the capacity decay10. Note that the formation of ZnMn2O4 (or Mn3O4) was already found in the 10th cycle (Supplementary Fig. 36a). The morphologies of the cycled electrodes (100th cycle) were analyzed with SEM. The cycled positive electrodes in different electrolytes become finer with newly formed spherical particles (Supplementary Fig. 5a–c, g–i). Apparently, ZHS product is found on the cycled Zn negative electrodes in both ZnSO4 and ZnSO4 + MnSO4 electrolytes (Fig. 1i). Based on the EIS results of the cycled pouch cells (Supplementary Fig. 6), the larger charge transfer resistance (13.6 Ω) of the cell with ZnSO4 electrolyte after 100 cycles compared with the other two (5.7 Ω for Zn(OTf)2 and 7.2 Ω for ZnSO4 + MnSO4) should stem from the more obvious signal of residual basic zinc salt (ZHS) on both the positive and negative electrodes (Fig. 1h, i). The results above indicate that the electrolytes can influence electrochemical behaviors, electrode structure, and cell performance. To explore how electrolytes affect the cell electrochemomechanical property, the FBG sensors were then introduced to monitor the stress evolution of the Zn||β-MnO2 cells with different electrolytes under electrochemical operation.

As shown in Fig. 2a, the FBG sensor is placed between the MnO2 positive electrode and the separator in the pouch cell to track the stress evolution. X-ray computed tomography (CT) shows the internal cross-sectional structure of the pouch cell with an implanted FBG sensor (Fig. 2a, bottom left). The cross-sectional EDS of the optical fiber shows a uniform distribution of Si and O, while the core is distinguished by the Ge element (diameter of ~9 μm). The doping of Ge is used to increase the refractive index of the core to confine the light within the core37. The experimental setup for stress monitoring in pouch cells with an implanted FBG sensor is depicted in Fig. 2b. The optical interrogator can serve as both the light source and the data acquisition system. The pouch cell fixed by a compressed air clamp (A constant pressure of 0.6 MPa, Supplementary Fig. 7b) was cycled with a battery test system. Internal stress variation will be detected by the FBG sensor, and the signal will be collected by the interrogator. Though the constant pressure of 0.6 MPa applied on the pouch cell would contribute to the internal stress, however, as seen in Fig. 2d, the relative stress changes induced by the electrochemistry were captured by the sensor during cycling, offering critical information into the chemomechanics of our systems. For the pouch cell, a gas bag18 around the central electrode region was designed to accommodate possible gas generation (Supplementary Fig. 7a) so that the generated gas will not contribute to the stress at the electrode region. The temperature calibration of the FBG sensor is carried out in an oven (const-660) from 10 to 60 °C. The peak wavelength of the spectrum gradually increases with increased temperature (Supplementary Fig. 8a). A positive linear relationship can be found between temperature and the shifts of λB (Supplementary Fig. 8b).

To attain the stress variation information, the wavelength response of the FBG sensor to temperature and stress needs to be decoupled. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9, two FBG sensors are implanted in the pouch cell, one of which is sealed in a stainless-steel tube before accessing the cell. The one without a tube is used to monitor the temperature and stress, while the one using a tube to avoid the influence of stress is for temperature tracking only. While the signal of the sensor with the tube barely changes, suggesting a trivial temperature change in the Zn||MnO2 cell during cycles. Therefore, the influence of temperature on Δλ is neglected in this work. Instead, obvious stress change (converted from Δλ via Eqs. 6 and 7) can be detected by the sensor without a tube during cycles.

The performance of the cells with or without the FBG sensor is compared (Fig. 2c), which shows that the FBG insertion barely affects the electrochemical performance of the batteries. Additional evidence can also be found in the relatively consistent results of the voltage profiles, capacity-voltage profiles and CV curves (Supplementary Fig. 10). The wavelength response of FBG in the Zn||β-MnO2 pouch cell is presented in Fig. 2d. Δλ and Δσ increase during discharge and decrease during charge. The variation of Δλ and Δσ becomes smaller with increased specific currents, corresponding to decreased specific capacity at high rates, possibly due to the intrinsic low conductivity and sluggish reaction kinetics of MnO243. As shown in Fig. 2d, the envelope of the stress curve is similar to the shape of the specific capacity, which can be considered an indicator of capacity variation. However, the correlation between the stress envelope and specific capacity becomes poor at higher specific current (0.2 A g−1). To be more specific, though the stress envelope maintains relatively stable, the specific capacity keeps decreasing. When cycled under a high current density, issues inside the battery become more severe, including dendrite growth and corrosion from the Zn side44, irreversible Mn dissolution from the MnO2 side and inert by-product formation, which consumes electrolyte, increases interfacial resistance and voltage polarization, leading to fast capacity decay45,46. Under this situation, with more irreversible processes involved, the stress evolution cannot solely or mainly reflect the specific capacity evolution resulting from reversible reactions, which leads to the poor correlation between the two at 0.2 A g−1.

The reproducibility of stress monitoring via FBG sensor is validated in two Zn||β-MnO2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 11), where similar trends of stress evolution can be seen, and some subtle stress change is also repeatable. To determine where stronger stress may happen, we put two FBG sensors in the pouch cell, with one near the negative electrode surface and the other near the positive electrode surface (Supplementary Fig. 12). As a result, the increase/decrease of Δσ during discharge/charge can be observed at both the negative and positive electrode sides, where a significant magnitude difference can be observed. It suggests that the stress distribution in the cell is not uniform. The variation of Δσ at the positive electrode side is much greater than that at the negative electrode side, indicating more significant structure and volume change at the positive electrode. The huge stress variation was not reflected in the stress signal detected at the negative electrode side. The underlying mechanism is likely attributed to the poor lateral stress transfer efficiency from the positive electrode to the negative electrode through the separator. A comprehensive simulation-based analysis detailing how this inadequate lateral stress propagation leads to the pronounced stress mismatch is included in Supplementary Note 2. Thus, unless specially specified, the FBG sensor was placed near the positive electrode surface for stress surveillance.

Stress evolution in symmetric Zn cells was also analyzed (Supplementary Figs. 13–19), where the FBG sensor was placed between the negative electrode and the separator. The stress increases during discharge and decreases during charge (Supplementary Fig. 15). When cycled at the same areal current density and capacity as the Zn||β-MnO2 cell, the stress variation within a cycle in the symmetric Zn cell is about 0.13 MPa (Supplementary Fig. 17), which is smaller than that in the Zn||β-MnO2 cell (4–5 MPa, Fig. 2d, 0.05 A g−1). Interestingly, the stress increases as the deposition capacity increases (Supplementary Fig. 18, middle). When cycled with high deposition capacity (15 mA h cm−2), the symmetric cell encountered soft shorts47. In situ resistance result shows a significant decrease in this situation (Supplementary Fig. 18, bottom), which can also be supported by the apparent resistance drop in the EIS results (Supplementary Fig. 19). The stress exhibited an obvious decline when the soft shorts happened (Supplementary Fig. 18, middle), which can serve as an indicator for soft shorts.

Meticulous investigation of electrochemistry-mechanics-composition evolution in Zn||β-MnO2 cells with 2 M ZnSO4 electrolyte is shown in Fig. 3. The reflected optical spectra from the FBG sensor in the cell were recorded during cycling. The spectra of the initial two cycles are presented in chronological order (from bottom to top) in a 2D stack-view (Fig. 3a). The Bragg wavelength peak gradually shifts to the right upon discharge and then left upon charge. This change is repetitive at successive cycles, which reveals the mechanical reversibility of the battery system. In the first cycle, the stress (converted from Δλ) gradually increases during discharge and reaches its maximum (3.4 MPa) at the fully discharged state (Fig. 3b). During charge, the stress decreases and reaches its minimum (0.8 MPa) at the fully charged state. The stress is not fully released, suggesting partially irreversible volume expansion38. The stress evolution of the subsequent cycles is different from the initial cycle, where more twists appear. This suggests irreversible reactions in the initial cycle27. Take the second cycle as an example, the stress curve was divided into seven regions according to different change trends of stress (Fig. 3b). The deduced change rate of stress (dσ/dt) provides information for understanding internal electrochemical mechanisms and explaining structural evolution38. The dσ/dt is not constant during discharge/charge, suggesting the existence of competing reactions that cause different volume changes. Compared with other regions, the value of dσ/dt is bigger in regions IV and V (near the crest of the stress peak), implying greater stress variation. Different from the monotonically increasing in the first discharge, we can see a local decline of dσ/dt in the second discharge and even negative values of dσ/dt in the third discharge. This should not happen if it simply follows a H+ intercalation or Mn dissolution mechanisms accompanied by considerable ZHS-induced stress increase. Consequently, we propose that the electrochemical reactions in region III are the redox of Mn4+/Mn3+ via the formation of Zn-Mn-O compounds3 or Mn3O4 oxide48 without the charge compensation by protons. To further verify the composition, the MnO2 electrode was extracted at the end of region III for further characterization of TEM-EDS elemental mapping. The voltage and stress evolution, along with cell capacity in the first two cycles, is depicted in Fig. 3c, d. Generally, for the first cycle, the consistency of the stress evolution route between discharge and charge at the low state of charge (SOC) is better than that at the high SOC. This indicates lower mechanical reversibility at high SOC. In comparison, the consistency of the stress evolution in the first cycle is worse than that in the second cycle, which may stem from the irreversible reactions in the first cycle (Fig. 3c). More importantly, the nearly superimposed stress profiles in the second cycle (Fig. 3d) highlight the reversible and mirrored reaction pathways in Zn||β-MnO2 system, while the loss of stress segment at the high SOC underline the remaining electrochemical irreversibility.

What are these reversibility and irreversibility? An in-operando XRD test was thus carried out to track the phase change of the positive electrode during the initial two cycles. As depicted in Fig. 3e, the peaks located at 8° and 16.1° are indexed to the (001) and (002) crystal planes of the ZHS product. While the peaks at 28.6° and 37.2° belong to the (110) and (101) crystal planes of β-MnO2. The peak intensity of (001) indexed to ZHS and (110) indexed to β-MnO2 is presented over time.

Apparently, the gradually increased intensities of peaks at 8° and 16.1° during discharge indicate the formation of ZHS, while the decreased intensity during charge suggests its disappearance. This is consistent with the ex situ XRD results in Supplementary Fig. 21b. The proton conversion and dissolution of MnO2 consume H+ and cause the pH increase of the electrolyte, resulting in ZHS precipitation10,49. According to the ex situ XRD results (Supplementary Fig. 21b), the MnOOH formation overlaps with ZHS. After charge, the characteristic peaks of ZHS did not completely disappear (Supplementary Fig. 21b). The partial irreversible ZHS decomposition accounts for the obvious stress hysteresis at the first cycle (Fig. 3c).

The intensity of the (110) plane of β-MnO2 gradually declines during the first discharge and maintains relatively stable afterward without recovering to the initial intensity. Besides, the peak position barely shifts during cycling. These phenomena imply the irreversible β-MnO2 dissolution during the first discharge, which also accounts for the huge stress hysteresis at the first cycle (Fig. 3c). According to the inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) results, the increasing Mn concentration in the electrolyte during discharge (Fig. 3g) indicates the Mn dissolution process. Combined with previous research2,13,50, the re-deposited MnO2 with low crystallinity or amorphous feature should be ε-MnO2. To further verify the composition of the re-deposited product on the positive electrode, a Zn||carbon paper (CP) cell and a Zn||stainless steel (SS) cell with 2 M MnSO4 electrolyte were charged to 1.8 V at 5 mA cm−2 and maintained at 1.8 V for 12 h. After that, the cells were disassembled for further investigation, from which a black (or dark brown) product was found on the CP or SS electrode (Supplementary Fig. 22a, c). From the XRD patterns (Supplementary Fig. 22b, d), a new phase of ε-MnO2 was found after charging. Transmission electron microscope (TEM) analysis was carried out to further verify this product on the SS electrode. According to the high-resolution TEM (HRTEM), we could identify the lattice fringes of 0.266 nm corresponding to the (100) planes of ε-MnO2 (Supplementary Fig. 23a, b). Moreover, the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) further confirms the ε-MnO2 phase (Supplementary Fig. 23c). These results suggest the Mn2+ from Mn dissolution during discharge can be re-deposited to ε-MnO2 during charge.

Eight points were selected in the initial two cycles (Supplementary Fig. 21a) for ex situ Raman. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 21c, the two peaks at around 535 and 654 cm−1 are assigned to Eg and A1g modes of Mn-O, respectively51,52. Compared with the pristine state, the emerged peak of the Eg mode can be attributed to the appearance of MnOOH53. The peak of Eg mode strengthened during discharge and weakened during charge, which reflects the deformation of MnO2 during discharge and recovery during charge, indicating the reversible conversion between MnO2 and MnOOH. The deformation is evidenced by the longer Mn-O bond (2.2 Å) of MnOOH compared with that of β-MnO2 (1.9 Å, Supplementary Fig. 24). After charge, instead of returning to the pristine position, the peak of A1g mode shifted to a higher wavenumber, which should be related to the re-deposited ε-MnO254,55. As for the Zn negative electrodes, ZHS appearance/disappearance can also be detected in the XRD patterns during discharge/charge (Supplementary Fig. 21d). This is also evidenced by the growing Raman peaks of Zn-OH and SO42- in discharge and decreasing of them in charge (Supplementary Fig. 21e).

Macroscopically observed from the cycled electrodes, the emergence of white products on the positive electrode during discharge and its vanishment during charge also reflect the ZHS variation (Supplementary Fig. 25a). Moreover, the brown product emerged on the positive shell in charge and vanished in discharge support the deposition/dissolution of MnO2 (Supplementary Fig. 25b). From the Raman spectrum, the brown product should be the deposited ε-MnO22,54 (Supplementary Fig. 26). The surface and cross-sectional morphologies and element distribution of the electrodes after the first discharge and first charge were explored by SEM and EDS. Lots of big flakes appear on the surface of both the positive electrode and negative electrode after discharge and disappear after charge (Supplementary Fig. 27a, b, g, h), reflecting the variation of ZHS. The cross-sectional EDS shows increased Mn ratio and decreased O, Zn, and S ratios at the charged state, indicating the deposition of MnO2 and disappearance of ZHS after the charge (Supplementary Fig. 27m, n). The vanished white product on the positive electrode in pouch cells after charge also reveals the disappearance of ZHS (Supplementary Fig. 28a).

Since H+ and OH- will participate in the reaction processes, in-operando pH measurement was carried out in the Zn||β-MnO2 cell with 2 M ZnSO4 (Supplementary Fig. 29). As shown in Fig. 3f, the pH of the electrolyte increases gradually during the first discharge when the Mn dissolution happens, and it increases abruptly at the end of discharge and decreases suddenly at the end of charge. The sudden change of pH at the discharge/charge switching point should be related to the concentration gradient variation of H+ and OH− under the electric field effect. The Mn dissolution process consumes H+ and produces OH−, which is combined with the Zn2+, SO42−, and H2O to form ZHS during discharge. The ZHS can also consume H+ generated from the Mn deposition process to stabilize the pH value during the charge process56. From the 2nd to 5th cycle, the pH increases from 5.05 to 5.31 (ΔpH = 0.26, Supplementary Fig. 30).

Based on the comprehensive results and analysis above, the reaction mechanisms and stress evolution in the Zn||β-MnO2 cells with the aforementioned three electrolytes are summarized in Fig. 6a. In general, they follow a proton conversion and Mn dissolution/deposition mechanism with basic zinc salts formation/disappearance happened parallelly. In ZnSO4 and ZnSO4 + MnSO4 systems, during the first discharge, β-MnO2 is converted to MnOOH and then Mn2+, which transforms back to MnOOH and then ε-MnO2 in the following charge. For the subsequent cycles, ε-MnO2 will convert to MnOOH and then Mn2+ during discharge, and the reaction process will be reversed during charge. Besides, the remainder of the crystalline β-MnO2 will continue to dissolve to Mn2+ during discharge. Meanwhile, ZHS will gradually form in discharge and disappear in charge, which is a pH-dependent process. For the Zn(OTf)2 system, the evolution is similar to the other two electrolytes, with the basic zinc salt being ZHT. On the whole, the stress will increase during discharge and decrease during charge. Particularly, after the first cycle, the slowing rise or decline of stress during discharge is attributed to the redox of Mn4+/Mn3+ via the formation of Zn-Mn-O compounds3 without the charge compensation by protons. In addition to cyclic ageing, aqueous batteries’ voltage will keep evolving toward an equilibrium value within hours or even days at an open-circuit state of the battery, pertaining to the relaxation and possibly calendaring ageing with side reactions62. What are the chemomechanics of aqueous batteries without currents? To answer this question, the open-circuit states of the Zn||MnO2 cells are investigated with different electrolytes. As depicted in Fig. 6b, after the first discharge in ZnSO4 electrolyte, the voltage increases and converges to ~1.33 V during a one-hour open-circuit process (noted as D-rest). The relaxation voltage in this process is marked as ΔVD. Meanwhile, during the one-hour open circuit after the first charge (noted as C-rest), the voltage decreases and converges to ~1.53 V, which is noted as ΔVC. In the initial cycles, both the ΔVD and ΔVC gradually decrease, suggesting reduced polarization. During the open-circuit period, ion diffusion and redistribution together with side reactions will evolve toward an equilibrium state, accompanying volume changes of the electrodes63,64. As shown in Fig. 6b (bottom), the cell showed considerable stress changes during the open-circuit period with the ZnSO4 electrolyte, implying highly unstable states of materials and/or electrodes with the ZnSO4 electrolyte. In comparison, during the open-circuit period, the stress evolutions in both Zn(OTf)2 (Fig. 6c, bottom) and ZnSO4 + MnSO4 (Fig. 6d, bottom) electrolytes are less intensive (also in 10-cycle results, Supplementary Fig. 48), indicating these two electrolytes may improve the stability of materials and/or electrodes in the calendar ageing.

From the XRD patterns, we can observe the emergence of ZHS on both the positive electrodes and negative electrodes in ZnSO4 and ZnSO4 + MnSO4 electrolytes after a 4-hour rest (Supplementary Fig. 49). It indicates the easy formation of ZHS in the two electrolytes. While the XRD patterns of the positive electrode and negative electrode in Zn(OTf)2 electrolyte are unchanged, suggesting that the ZHT formation is relatively not easy to happen. This should also explain the smallest stress variation in Zn(OTf)2 electrolyte in comparison to others.

We further carried out in situ XRD/EIS tests to investigate the influencing factors of stress variation during the open-circuit process. According to the in situ XRD of the MnO2 electrode in ZnSO4 electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 50a), the peak intensity evolution of ZHS is not stable during the open-circuit processes (blue and red shadows), which contributes to the unstable stress evolution during these processes (Fig. 6b). The stress decrease during the first D-rest process could be related to the internal stress relief of the positive electrode after a high stress accumulation in the first discharge. In comparison, the peak intensity of ZHT in Zn(OTf)2 (Supplementary Fig. 50b) and ZHS in ZnSO4 + MnSO4 (Supplementary Fig. 50c) is relatively stable during the open-circuit processes (blue and red shadows), leading to a more stable stress level during these processes.

Besides, according to the in situ EIS results in all three electrolytes, the charge transfer resistance (Rct) gradually increases during the open-circuit processes (Supplementary Fig. 51b, d, f, h, j, l). And the Rct after discharge (Supplementary Fig. 51b, f, j) is larger than that after charge (Supplementary Fig. 51d, h, l), suggesting that the formation of ZHS/ZHT can lead to increased resistance to charge transfer.

What is the relationship between stress and cell performance? According to Supplementary Figs. 46 and 47, the correlation between the capacity retention (2nd to 100th cycle) and the average stress amplitude (σA, see Fig. 5b, middle) of the first two cycles in the three electrolytes is shown in Fig. 6e (cycling ageing). A relatively negative relationship can be observed between the two under different specific currents. In other words, cells that suffer from a bigger stress amplitude at early cycles show greater capacity decay. We need to clarify that the mechanical stress here is more of an indicator of underlying chemical reactions (e.g., Mn dissolution and ZHS formation), rather than the direct cause of the capacity loss. Inspired by the better cell performance in ZnSO4 + MnSO4 compared with ZnSO4 (Fig. 6e), we further study the cycling performance and stress evolution of the Zn||β-MnO2 cell with Zn(OTf)2 + Mn(OTf)2 electrolyte. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 52, the cycling stability of the cell was improved with Mn(OTf)2 addition. Besides, the stress variation is relatively lower (~1 MPa, Supplementary Fig. 53) than that in Zn(OTf)2 electrolyte (~2 MPa, Fig. 4b). According to Fig. 6e and Supplementary Figs. 52 and 53, the cells with Zn(OTf)2, ZnSO4 + MnSO4, and Zn(OTf)2 + Mn(OTf)2 electrolytes show lower capacity loss and lower stress amplitude compared with that using ZnSO4 electrolyte, which indicates that anion modulation and additives are two effective ways to mitigate stress variation and optimize battery performance. To be more specific, the modulated electrolytes help suppress the side reactions, thus reducing the mechanical stress and enhancing the structural integrity and battery performance. The stress variation can serve as an indicator or feature to assess the effectiveness of the electrolytes for side reaction suppression and battery performance improvement. Besides, the correlation between the average capacity retention (CR) in the open-circuit cycles (first 10 cycles) and average absolute stress variation during the D-rests (|ΔσD|, see Fig. 6b, bottom) is analyzed (Fig. 6f, open-circuit ageing). Note that CR is defined as Q-cha/Q-dis (charge capacity divided by the discharge capacity) in a cycle to evaluate the calendar ageing65. Interestingly, a relatively negative relationship is found between average CR and average |ΔσD|. Namely, cells that exhibit higher |ΔσD| during relaxation show lower CR in cycles, indicating more severe side reactions in the cells65. Stress-buffer-wise, using the porous carbon paper as a current collector, minimized stress and improved cycling stability were achieved (Supplementary Fig. 54) compared to the cells using Ti foil current collectors. Compared with the traditional current collector, the current collector with porous structure can provide an effective electron-conductive network and a buffer space for shortening the ion diffusion path, and they can also offer sufficient mechanical strength against the strain/stress resulting from the large volume change of the electrodes66. Besides, the porous electrode can also minimize the stress difference at the interface between the current collector and the active material coating, decreasing the energy release rate related to interface delamination67.

Discussion

In conclusion, we have investigated the electrochemomechanical evolution at the electrolyte/electrode interface in aqueous Zn||β-MnO2 pouch cells with different electrolytes via operando stress monitoring by FBG sensors. The monitored stress variation during cycling matches well with the composition characterization results and calculations of the electrode volume changes, which provides strong proof of the dominant proton intercalation and Mn dissolution/deposition mechanism in the Zn||β-MnO2 battery system. The local pH changes arising from these electrochemical reactions lead to the formation/decomposition of the basic zinc salt by-product (ZHS/ZHT), which induces large stress variation during cycling and damages the mechanical integrity of the battery. Namely, side reactions may play a dominant role in the mechanical failure mechanism of the battery. Interestingly, we also found a partial slowing increase of the stress or even a decrease of stress during the discharge process, which is supposed to be the redox of Mn4+/Mn3+ via the formation of Zn-Mn-O compounds3 by ruling out the charge compensation with protons. Besides, a cell exhibiting bigger stress amplitudes at early cycles would experience worse capacity loss in long cycles, which can serve as a feature for cell performance prediction. In addition, more significant stress variation during the calendar ageing process reflects more severe side reactions and worse cell stability in an electrolyte. The use of OTf− anion and Mn2+ additive helps lower the pH of the electrolyte and alleviate the side reactions of basic zinc salts formation, thus lowering stress and improving cell performance. These results unravel a mechanism in which electrolyte engineering (e.g., anion regulation, additives) is effective in controlling mechanical integrity to improve electrochemical performance.

In brief, internal operando stress monitoring via FBG sensors shows high potential in better understanding the electrochemistry-mechanics-performance relationship of the battery and giving guidance to battery design. For instance, electrolyte optimization should favor less side reaction and a high-reversible charge storage process, avoiding large strain/stress and maintaining high electrode integrity. In addition, developing a 3D-porous electrode/current collector helps accommodate volume change and minimize stress, enhancing the cycling stability of batteries.

Here, some details need to be highlighted for the application of FBG sensors for stress monitoring. First, the clamps should guarantee a constant external force on the pouch cell so that the FBG sensor can access more stable and reliable stress information inside the cells. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 55, compared with the compressed air clamp, the peak wavelength signal of the FBG sensor using a screw clamp exhibits bigger noise and disturbance due to the relatively unstable fastening of the clamp. Second, the mechanical properties and volume change of the reactants and products should be analyzed with quantified reaction processes that pertain to the stress variation. Extended applications of this work include stress evolution investigation in colloid, hydrogel and solid-state electrolytes and the 2D or 3D stress field distribution mapping. We expect these findings can help better understand the reaction process, rationalize the battery design, and promote the commercial application of aqueous zinc batteries.

Methods

Material and electrode preparation

Preparation of the β-MnO2-based powder. β-MnO2 (99%, Macklin), Ketjen black (KB, EC-300J, Canrd Technology Co. Ltd.), carbon nano tube (CNT-3213, Canrd Technology Co. Ltd.) were mixed in a mass ratio of 7:1.5:1, followed by ball milling via Pulverisette 7 (FRITSCH). This process was carried out at a speed of 400 r/min for 4 h. Finally, the β-MnO2-based powder with a smaller particle size and improved conductivity was attained.

Preparation of the β-MnO2 positive electrode. The above β-MnO2-based powder was mixed with polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF 5130, Canrd Technology Co. Ltd.) at a mass ratio of 9.5:0.5, using 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP, 99.5%, with molecular sieves, Macklin) as solvent. To be specific, 2 g PVDF solution (5 wt.% in NMP solvent) was added to 1.9 g β-MnO2-based powder, followed by 3 g NMP. The mixture was stored in a glass bottle and stirred with a magnetic stirrer (Hei-Tec, Heidolph) at room temperature at 350 r/min for 24 h. The resulting slurry was coated on one side of a Ti foil (0.01 mm thick) through a doctor-blade method with a set thickness of 500 μm. Then the Ti foil with the coating was dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h. The coating thickness of the dried electrode is about 89.2 μm. The mass loading of the positive electrode is about 10 mg cm−2. Besides, some disc-shaped (Φ = 12 mm) carbon papers (H-060, hydrophilic, TORAY) were also prepared by a slicer (MSK-T10, SHENZHEN KEJING STAR TECHNOLOGY COMPANY) and used as the substrates for the β-MnO2 positive electrodes in coin cells. The mass loading of the positive electrode used in the coin cell is about 5 mg cm−2.

Preparation of different aqueous electrolyte solutions. The chemicals involved include ZnSO4 •7H2O (AR, ≥99.5%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd), MnSO4•H2O (AR, ≥99.0%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd), zinc trifluoromethanesulfonate (C2F6O6S2Zn, 98%, Macklin) and manganese(II) trifluoromethanesulfonate (C2F6MnO6S2, ≥98%, Aladdin). 2 M ZnSO4, 2 M Zn(OTf)2, 2 M ZnSO4 + 0.1 M MnSO4, 2 M Zn(OTf)2 + 0.1 M Mn(OTf)2 aqueous solutions were prepared through an electromagnetic stirring process under room temperature, which were used as the electrolytes in the cells in this work.

Cell assembly and testing

Coin cells assembly with β-MnO2 electrodes and different electrolytes. The β-MnO2 with carbon paper substrate was used as the positive electrode (Φ = 12 mm), with glass fiber (Φ = 19 mm, 2.7 μm for pore diameter, 675 μm for thickness, GF/D, Whatman) as the separator and bare Zn (Φ = 16 mm, 0.1 mm thickness, 99.99% purity, Canrd Technology Co. Ltd.) as the negative electrode. The Zn electrodes were cleaned with ethanol before use. The disc-shaped electrodes and separators were prepared by a slicer (MSK-T10). One hundred and fifty microlitres of the above aqueous solutions were used as the electrolytes. The CR2025 coin cells were assembled with these components. The material of the case and spring of the coin cell is 304 stainless steel.

FBG sensor implementation in pouch cells with β-MnO2 electrodes and different electrolytes. The β-MnO2 electrode was cut into a specific size (effective area: 3.5 cm × 3.5 cm) with a protruding part (0.5 cm × 0.7 cm) to weld with the battery tab (nickel tab, Canrd Technology Co. Ltd.). The Zn negative electrode was prepared with a similar size. The optical fiber was placed between the separator (4 cm × 4 cm, GF/D, Whatman) and the positive electrode, with the FBG part positioned at the central region of the electrode. The double-layer pouch cells containing two layers of each component (positive electrode, negative electrode and separator) were prepared. The N/P ratio for the pouch cell is about 10.2. Then, a hot melt adhesive polymer tape was placed at the exit of the optical fiber near the edge of the pouch cell, which helps fix the optical fiber and ensures the airtightness of the pouch cell. 2.4 ml of the corresponding electrolyte was injected into the pouch cell before the last vacuum sealing step. The pouch cell was fixed by a compressed air clamp with a set air pressure of 0.6 MPa when cycling.

FBG sensor implementation in symmetric pouch cells with Zn electrodes. The Zn electrode was cut into a specific size (effective area: 3.5 cm × 3.5 cm) with a protruding part (0.5 cm × 0.7 cm) to weld with the battery tab (nickel tab, Canrd Technology Co. Ltd.). The optical fiber was placed between the separator (4 cm × 4 cm, GF/D, Whatman) and Zn electrode, with the FBG part positioned at the central region of the electrode. Then, a hot melt adhesive polymer tape was placed at the exit of the optical fiber near the edge of the pouch cell, which helps fix the optical fiber and ensures the airtightness of the pouch cell. 1.2 ml of the 2 M ZnSO4 electrolyte was injected into the pouch cell before the last vacuum sealing step.

Electrochemical tests

Cycling of the cells was carried out with a multichannel battery test system (LAND G340A/V5C5AT8, China) at a constant temperature (25 °C) inside climatic chambers (Memmert, accuracy ±0.1 °C, convection heating). The voltage window for the galvanostatic discharge/charge cycling is 0.8–1.8 V for the Zn||β-MnO2 cells. The cycling of the symmetric Zn cells was limited by time. The electrochemical tests of the cells started after a resting time of 4 hours. EIS and CV curves data of the cells were recorded on a VMP-3e potentiostat (Bio-Logic, France). The sweep rate for the CV test is 0.1 mV s−1, and the potential range applied is 0.8–1.8 V. For EIS measurements, the type of perturbation applied is potentiostatic, the sinus amplitude is 10 mV, the frequency range is 100 kHz to 10 mHz, the number of data points per decade of frequency is 6, and the time applied during open-circuit potential before carrying out the measurement is 4 hours. The EIS data were analyzed using the ZView software. In situ resistance monitoring of the symmetric Zn pouch cell was performed on a multimeter (DMM6500, Keithley) and the data were collected by KickStart software. The mass of MnO2 of the positive electrode was used to calculate the specific current (A g−1) and specific capacity (mA h g−1) in the related electrochemical tests. The area of the positive electrode was used to calculate the current density (mA cm−2) and areal capacity (mA h cm−2) in the related electrochemical tests.

Optical measurements

The peak wavelengths and reflected spectra were collected with the interrogator LUNA Si155 (Micron Optics, USA). The wavelength accuracy/resolution of this device is 1 pm. The FBG sensors (10 mm length, 125 μm diameter) were purchased from Shenzhen Guangchi Technology Co., Ltd (China).

Characterization techniques

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS). An ultra-high resolution scanning electron microscope (Hitachi Regulus 8230, at 5 kV) was used to analyze the morphologies of pristine and cycled electrodes. Element analysis of the samples was performed on an environmental SEM (ThermoFischer, Prisma E, at 10 kV) with an UltraDry 129 eV 30 M EDS detector or on Hitachi Regulus 8230 with an Oxford AZtecLive UltimMax 170 detector. The electrode samples were harvested, sampled and transported at room temperature and in an air atmosphere.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) and energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS). A transmission electron microscope (TEM, FEI Talos F200X) was employed to record scanning transmission electron microscopy, HRTEM and SAED images, combined with an energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS, super-X) for elemental mapping.

X-ray computed tomography (CT). Internal structure of the pouch cell was investigated by a Nano-micro CT scan system (FF 35CT, YXLON, Germany).

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD). Ex situ XRD was performed on a high-throughput powder X-ray diffractometer (Empyrean 3.0) with Cu Kα radiation.

In situ X-ray diffraction. In situ XRD was performed on a multipurpose X-ray diffractometer (Empyrean 3.0) with Cu Kα radiation. The cell for in situ XRD tests was modified from a CR2025 coin cell. A hole was created (8 mm in diameter) at the center of the positive electrode shell, which was then sealed with a polyimide film (15 μm in thickness). A customized cell holder was used for the in situ XRD tests.

Raman microscopy. The Raman analysis was performed on a confocal Raman microscope (inVia-Qontor, Renishaw). The spectra were collected with a 50× objective, a 532 nm laser (at 10% power), and an exposure time of 60 s.

In situ pH. The in situ pH measurement was carried out in a cuvette cell, containing a MnO2 electrode (effective area: 1.3 cm × 2 cm), a Zn electrode (effective area: 1.5 cm × 2 cm) and 3 ml electrolyte. The pH measurement system consists of a pH probe (APS-200T), a WQ1000-pH monitor (Shenzhen Jide Automation Equipment Co., Ltd.) and software.

ICP-OES measurement. The measurement was performed using an ICP spectrometer (Agilent 720ES(OES), USA). The samples were obtained by collecting the electrolytes of cycled Zn||MnO2 cuvette cells at room temperature and in an air atmosphere. The cell contains a MnO2 electrode (effective area: 0.8 cm × 2 cm), a Zn electrode (effective area: 0.9 cm × 2 cm) and 1 ml electrolyte.
