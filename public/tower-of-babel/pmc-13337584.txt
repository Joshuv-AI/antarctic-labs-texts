Scientific Objectivity and the Pursuit of the Public Good
Authors: Sadie Hundemer, Jamie Loizzo, Anna L Peterson, Stephanie Stoutamire, S B Paxson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractScientists are increasingly expected to not only produce objective science but also to use their science and expertise to support the public good. Prior studies indicate that scientists see both responsibilities as important; however, it is not clear how scientists manage these responsibilities when they are perceived to be in tension. In this study, scientists at land-grant institutions (n = 87) were presented with binary choices to identify where tensions may exist and how scientists make decisions between competing responsibilities. We examined nine categories of potential tensions: direct conflicts between objectivity and public impact, engagement in public outreach, communication priorities and approach, political engagement and perceived bias, the media, funding and disclosure, supporting/protecting specific causes, diversity, and ethical decision making. Results indicate that scientists belong to three cultures of ethical thought: arbiters, advocates, and brokers. These cultures roughly align with the scientist roles described by Pielke and suggest different prioritizations of commitment to public service relative to commitment to objective science, though both commitments were valued. There was variation across cultures of thought on whether scientists should openly support policies based on their research. Broad areas of agreement across cultures of thought were also identified. Scientists of all cultures of thought indicated a willingness to see themselves in a public role, communicating openly with non-experts on issues where scientific research intersects with the public good. Introduction

Among the many responsibilities assigned to or assumed by scientists are responsibilities to objectivity and public impact. In public discourse, and often among scientists themselves, objectivity is associated with being neutral or independent from personal values, while public impact is linked to addressing societal needs (Gibbons, 1999; Ladd et al., 2009; Schnittker, 2009). When these assumptions are taken together, they create a perceived tension: efforts to remain valueneutral may seem at odds with efforts to engage societal concerns (Donner, 2014; Ladd et al., 2009; Nisbet, 2016).

When surveyed, scientists report that they prioritize both objectivity and public impact; however, such assessments examine the responsibilities independently not accounting for scenarios in which the ideals could conflict (Ladd et al., 2009; Wyndham et al., 2021). Consider a scientist sharing their research with the public – should the scientist use the most accurate language possible, or should they use language that helps people with opposing viewpoints “hear” them? When advising policymakers, should the scientist only respond to specific inquiries, or should they proactively prompt policy makers to considerations absent from their discussions? And is it appropriate for the scientist to place inherent value in reducing global poverty, protecting U.S. energy independence, or eradicating sexism and homo/transphobia? Answering each of these questions requires weighing objectivity and public impact.

At a time when trust in science is threatened and when science is increasingly central to public decision making, the way scientists attend to their ethical responsibilities shapes perceptions of their work, their institutions, and science at large (Campbell & Kay, 2014; Lewandowsky & Oberauer, 2016; Rosenbaum, 2014). Our study places responsibilities to objectivity and public impact in tension, requiring scientists to make choices between ethical ideals. In doing so, we find three cultures of ethical thought and identify the areas on which these cultures agree and differ. We assert no preference among these schools of thought but suggest that scientists use these perspectives to reflect upon their own ethical decisions.

Literature Review

Scientist Objectivity and Social Responsibility

Objectivity in science is often associated with the value-free ideal (VFI) which posits that scientific reasoning should remain independent of personal or societal values (Brown, 2024; Stamenkovic & Tuboly, 2025). The conception of science as objective and value free is central to the way many scientists understand their work and its legitimacy. However, understandings of objectivity and the VFI are evolving. Historians and philosophers of science point out that science is a human practice which can never be entirely separated from the social context in which it is conducted or from scientists’ own understandings and values (Kuhn, 1970; Haraway, 1988). In this view, ethical and epistemic values are intertwined in scientific practice (Hoyningen-Huene, 2023). This entanglement can create tensions for scientists, who do not always make explicit (to themselves or others) the ways that their circumstances and values, and those of their colleagues, shape scientific work. More explicit attention to science ethics – beyond simply adhering to professional standards – can help scientists retain intellectual integrity while understanding and managing the role of values in their work (Brown, 2024; Resnik & Elliott, 2019).

Before going further, we must define objectivity and values as we have conceptually applied them. Objectivity is the notion that science aims to produce knowledge independent of individual biases, emotions, or social ideals (Tsou et al., 2015; Van Dongen & Sikorski, 2021). Objective science uses scientific evidence and fair methods to reach conclusions and allows for judgements when those judgements are supported by evidence (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2017; Reiss & Sprenger, 2020). In this way, objectivity differs from neutrality – while objectivity permits judgement, neutrality emphasizes non-advocacy and restraint from normative commitments, even when evidence points toward particular conclusions (Weber, 1949).

Values are central beliefs and principles, such as honesty, community, compassion, freedom, and equity (S. H. Schwartz, 1992). A person’s values may be a product of many factors including their experiences, religion, or politics (S. Schwartz, 2012). Values, such as honesty, can support objectivity by reinforcing norms of evidence use and methodological rigor (Longino, 1990; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2017). However, values can undermine objectivity when they intentionally or unintentionally bias the selection, production, or application of evidence (Douglas, 2009; Longino, 1990). The value-free ideal specifically regards ethical (moral) values, not epistemic values related to the reliability of scientific knowledge.

Scientists live in the societies they serve, participate in civic life, and are impacted by the same cultural, political, and ethical structures as the public (Akkermans, 2024; Pruzan, 2016). They attempt to balance institutional pressures, such as grant funding and the responsible conduct of research (RCR), with personal factors, including personalities, backgrounds, and experiences (Lilienfeld, 2017; Sato, 2016). Scientists often make value judgements across the aforementioned factors as they decide what problems to investigate, formulate research objectives, and select technologies and analysis methods (Douglas, 2009). Each of these decisions can be viewed as value choices. The selection of a research project, for example, suggests that the scientist or institution believes the topic has value and that the actions that could stem from the research are desirable. Values also emerge in the selection of stakeholder partners and collaborators and in the advice scientists give and who they give it to (Nisbet, 2016).

Linville et al. (2023) found that as scientists strive to achieve objectivity amidst complex environments and pressures, they frequently navigate epistemic and ethical values. However, scientists in Linville et al.’s study (2023) viewed epistemic and ethical values to be mutually reinforcing rather than in conflict with one another. For instance, the ethical values of transparency and trustworthiness support the research practice of maintaining detailed data collection and analysis procedural notes. Similarly, Biddle (2023) stated that the application of social values to science could increase objectivity as scientists critically consider RCR and ethics in scientific inquiry processes. As scientists take a reflective approach that considers their values, the biases they identify could prompt changes that increase adherence to professional ethical codes while also meeting society’s needs.

Prior research indicates public trust in science depends not only on the reliability of scientific knowledge but also on confidence that scientists are socially responsible (Rolin, 2021). Social responsibility in science refers to scientists’ ethical obligation to ensure their research is conducted with transparency, aligns with societal values, and positively affects public well-being and policy (Bradshaw & Bekoff, 2001; Weed & McKeown, 2003). The American Association for the Advancement of Science (AAAS) found that while many scientists recognize the importance of their social responsibilities, there is no clear consensus about how they should be enacted in practice (Wyndham et al., 2021). Hence, AAAS recommended steps such as integrating social responsibility into scientific training, offering clearer ethical guidance, increasing diversity in science, and encouraging community engagement.

Balancing Objectivity, Public Engagement, and Advocacy

Historically, the scientific community held the ideal of a “committed objectivity” that separated science from societal and political influence to ensure data integrity and research rigor (Duarte et al., 2022). However, committed objectivity is often difficult for scientists to achieve as they can be influenced by social, financial, and political pressures (Douglas, 2009; Elliott & Resnik, 2014). Public, stakeholder, and political groups appear to be calling for scientists to step out of their labs and into dialogue and advocacy (Editorial, 2025). As public skepticism in science and misinformation have escalated, scientists are expected to increase their participation in civic life through conversations that reflect shared values and diverse perspectives (American Association for the Advancement of Science [AAAS], 2018). Publicly engaged scientists are encouraged to be transparent, empathetic, and responsive to societal and cultural contexts as well as manage their values and biases to remain ethical and objective in their research (Nisbet, 2018). Online platforms are one way for scientists to communicate their findings, counteract misinformation, and foster transparency (Editorial, 2025). Yet, scientists have expressed hesitation to participate in online and in-person engagement due to barriers such as time constraints, lack of communication training, fear of heated conversations, and public engagement not being a job requirement (Loizzo et al., 2019; Nisbet, 2018; Editorial, 2025).

While grant funders require scientists to follow objective and ethical research practices, they also often expect them to report the broader impacts of their research on society, which can include issues of equity, justice, and public welfare (MacFadden, 2019; National Science Foundation [; National Science Foundation, 2022). The shift toward broader impacts acknowledges that scientific knowledge does not stand on its own and researchers have a social responsibility to consider the implications of their work (American Association for the Advancement of Science, 2018; National Science Foundation, 2022). Examples of broader impacts may include fostering partnerships between academia and industry, improving national security and public health, and creating educational resources or public engagement initiatives such as museum exhibits, citizen science projects, or K–12 outreach programs (Smith, 2020; National Science Foundation, 2022).

Another dilemma scientists face is whether they should participate in policy advocacy (Horton et al., 2016; Nelson, 2009). Scientist participation in advocacy can be described as moving beyond typical academic communication outlets and sharing results more publicly. Advocacy can also entail taking a position on a scientific subject and encouraging policy change on the basis of their research results (Cockrell et al., 2018; Nelson & Vucetich, 2009). Some scientists argue that advocacy could compromise scientific objectivity and scientists’ credibility (Nielsen, 2010). On the other hand, others both within and outside the scientific community support scientist advocacy as essential to translating research into effective public policy (American Association for the Advancement of Science, 2018; Nisbet, 2018). Supporters of scientist advocacy maintain that policy advocacy is a scientist’s social responsibility, while opposers maintain that science and advocacy should remain separate (Duarte et al., 2022). As epistemic and ethical values are further entangled, scientists must determine their approach to navigate complexities of objectivity and social responsibilities.

Philosophical studies indicate scientists take on inductive risks as they strive to remain objective (Brown, 2013; Douglas, 2003; Havstad, 2022). For instance, they make value-laden judgements within scientific processes to determine how much evidence is sufficient to support or reject a hypothesis, especially when errors could cause societal consequences (Brown, 2013; Douglas, 2009). Decisions about how strongly to state conclusions, when to disclose uncertainties, and how to communicate potential risks to public audiences involve ethical and epistemic considerations (Havstad, 2022). Hence, advocacy tensions about whether scientists should take a position on public policy are increased as scientists determine how to manage inductive risk to cautiously engage in public communication (Douglas, 2009; Elliott & Resnik, 2014). At an institutional level, scientist organizations such as the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) also contend with objectivity vs. advocacy tradeoffs (Havstad & Brown, 2017; Pearce et al., 2018). Havstad and Brown (2017) determined the IPCC’s mission to be “policy-relevant yet policy neutral, never policy-prescriptive” to be potentially confusing to stakeholders, policymakers, and the public. The researchers recommend scientist organizations can maintain credibility as they navigate inductive risks through transparent communication of how their judgements shape evidence, uncertainty, and viable policy options, even when they refrain from endorsing specific policy or courses of action. As scientists consider whether to engage in public communication, broader impacts, or policy advocacy, it is valuable to understand the ways they attempt to manage competing responsibilities for socially responsible public impact. To this end, our research examines scientists’ perspectives on this balancing act.

Theoretical Framework

Many scientific philosophers have offered opinions on the proper (and improper) ways for scientists to attend to objectivity while also engaging stakeholders to achieve public impact (Bird, 2014; Chan, 2008; Han, 2015; Keohane et al., 2014; Krogsgaard-Larsen et al., 2011; Lackey, 2007; Nisbet, 2016; Scott et al., 2007; Scott & Rachlow, 2011; Sedlak, 2016; Tuana, 2010; Wilhere, 2012). Of the many perspectives proposed frameworks, Pielke’s (2007) has emerged as one of the most highly cited and useful. Pielke (2007) suggests four roles a scientist can assume: pure scientist, issue advocate, science arbiter, and honest broker. In tradeoffs between objectivity and public impact, the pure scientist always chooses objectivity, focusing exclusively on knowledge production with “absolutely no consideration for its use or utility” (Pielke, 2007, p. 15). The issue advocate, in contrast, conducts research for the purpose of a particular societal outcome. From this perspective, public impact is the driving purpose of research. Science arbiters intentionally serve the public interest but limit the direction they provide to the scientific questions directly asked by decision-makers. Finally, honest brokers provide more directed support “by clarifying and, at times, seeking to expand the scope of choice available to decision-makers” (Pielke, 2007 p. 17).

The difference in these four roles lies not in how scientists conduct research – presumably all scientists aim to maintain the core standards of ethical conduct of research including the scientific method. The difference, instead, lies in the way scientists facilitate the use of their research in value-based applications. Issue advocates embrace their personally held value perspectives and use their science to promote their societal preferences. Pure scientists, science arbiters, and honest brokers seek to limit the influence of ethical and socialvalues on the application of science but employ different strategies to do so. These three roles can be conceptualized as lying on a spectrum of engagement with the users of science ranging from no engagement (pure scientist) to actively facilitating the interpretation and application of science (honest broker). As the level of interaction between the scientist and science user increases, so does the opportunity for scientists to unintentionally become advocates. In providing guidance, scientists risk interjecting their personal biases and encouraging the uses of science that aligns with their personal preferences. Scientists also risk the perception of bias even where none exists. This is the “paradox of scientific advice” (Pamuk, 2021, p. 63) – scientists can support the use of their research or they can maintain the highest levels of objectivity, but it is difficult to do both.

Research Questions

This exploratory study sought to determine how scientists perceived their responsibilities to objectivity and public impact, particularly where these responsibilities may lie in tension. Further, we sought to identify groups of scientists who differently perceived their responsibilities and tradeoffs between responsibilities.

RQ1

What are scientists’ cultures of thought on their responsibilities to objectivity and public impact?

RQ2

How do scientists with different cultures of thought perceive tradeoffs between objectivity and public impact?

RQ3

What are the demographic characteristics of scientists of different cultures of thought?

Methods

Sampling

Scientist participants were recruited from land-grant colleges and universities (National Institute of Food and Agriculture [NIFA], 2024). The land-grant mission includes conducting research for the benefit of the communities they serve, which suggests that the work of scientists at land-grants should have a public impact. Using website directories, we developed a list of researchers in faculty positions at the ten largest U.S. land-grants and the four largest land-grant Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs) by student population according to U.S. News and World Report (2024). The ten largest land-grant institutions in 2024 were the University of Florida, Pennsylvania State University, the University of Wisconsin, Texas A&M, Rutgers University, The Ohio State University, University of California – Berkley, the University of Arizona, Purdue University, and Michigan State University. The four largest land-grant HBCUs in 2024 were Florida A&M University, Prairie View University, Southern A&M University, and Tennessee State University.

Within these institutions, we narrowed our scope to agricultural, natural resources, and life science departments because these disciplines typically conduct applied research on topics related to economic, environmental, and social outcomes. Included in our sample were researchers from departments of the following sub-disciplines: animal science; agricultural/biosystems engineering; social sciences; agricultural economics; entomology; food science and nutrition; plant science and plant pathology; family, youth, and community services; agronomy, soil science, and crop science; horticulture and landscape; natural resource/environmental science/forest, fisheries, and range ecology; ecology, conservation, and evolution; and agricultural leadership, education, and communication. Participants were further limited to those holding a doctorate or master’s degree and at least a partial research appointment. A $50 Amazon gift card was provided to all participants who completed both the survey used in this study and a subsequent mental modeling activity.

Data collection occurred between November 2024 and February 2025 under [University Name] IRB ET00020790. We employed several methods of participant recruitment. We directly emailed faculty on our contact list and also requested department heads (n = 121) distribute the survey to their faculty. Additionally, we sent separate emails to faculty (from the same contact list) that previously participated in interviews conducted by our research team on the topic of scientist roles and responsibilities. We purposefully included these previous research participants because their engagement in in-depth conversation on social responsibility was hypothesized to generate more robust and thoughtful perspectives on objectivity and public impact. This strategic decision was based on our research goal of identifying the range of cultures of thought, which we presumed would be broadened with the inclusion of participants who had mentally attended to the topics both recently and robustly. However, this sampling method means that the results should not be interpreted as a distribution of cultures of thought in the land-grant scientist population.

Eighty-seven scientists at land-grant institutions completed the survey. Demographic information for the sample is provided in Table 1. Most participants had research (88.5% of participants) and teaching appointments (83.9%). Extension (37.9%) and administration (39.1%) appointments were also well represented. “Other” appointment types (39.9%) were typically written in as “service.” All career stages were represented: early career (24.1%), mid-career (34.5%), and advanced career (41.1%). All surveyed fields of study were represented with the largest percentage of participants from “social sciences and agricultural economics” (26.4%) and “natural resource/environmental science/forest, range, and fisheries” (18.4%). The majority of participants identified as female (56.3%) and White (87.4%).

Instruments

Survey items presented participants with binary choices related to direct conflicts between objectivity and public impact; scientist engagement in public outreach; communication priorities and approach; political engagement and perceived bias; the media; funding and disclosure; supporting/protecting specific causes; diversity; and ethical decision making. These categories reflect tradeoffs presented in the literature on scientist ethical responsibility as well as interviews our team previously conducted with 79 US scientists at land-grant institutions on their roles and responsibilities. We selected the format of binary responses, as opposed to scale responses, to force participants to make difficult choices between ethical ideals. This is contrary to the approach taken in many prior studies in which tradeoffs between responsibilities were not considered. We piloted the survey with six scientists at land-grant institutions who aligned with the sample criteria. After completing the survey, we conducted a think-aloud activity in which pilot participants explained their thought processes and identified confusing or unclear survey items. Questions were revised based on the pilot participants’ feedback. The full list of survey items is provided in supplementary materials. Item options were randomized. Demographic information was collected in a separate survey used to qualify participants for inclusion in the study.

Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted with JMP Pro version 18.0.2. Individual survey responses were scored −1 or +1, indicating selection of the first or second option respectively. K Means cluster analysis was performed on the full set of tradeoff items. As a reliability test on the application of K Means clusters for binary variables, we compared the results to a latent model which produced similar clusters. Chi Square analysis was conducted on the resultant clusters. Descriptive demographic data was analyzed by cluster and for the full sample.

Limitations

There are several limitations to consider when interpreting our results. First, we relied on binary questions that forced participants to choose between competing ethical ideals. This approach simplifies what, in real world contexts, may be a highly nuanced and conditional decision-making process. Second, our focus on scientists at land-grant institutions in specific disciplines limits the generalizability of our results. Our findings, therefore, should not be viewed as representative of scientists working in institutions without commitments to public impact or with less formal or cultural expectations of neutrality. Furthermore, science associated with agricultural and natural resources has potentially large implications for regional economies and policies, which may have influenced participants’ responses in ways that are not reflective of scientists more generally. Third, some of the participants participated in a prior interview with our research team. As with the other demographic decisions, this decision was intentionally made to increase the robustness of the thought process behind participants’ selections, but it additionally conditions the results to be more representative of scientists who have considered their ethical positions. Fouth, some priming effects from the interview may be reflected in the results. The interview was structured to encourage participants to examine their own attitudes and behaviors, but the prompting of domains of thought could have affected responses. The order of the questions could have influenced responses in a similar manner, with earlier questions triggering reflection that influenced responses to later questions.

Based on responses to the tradeoff questions, the remaining three clusters were named Arbiters, Advocates, and Brokers, to reflect their cultures of thought. The final solution allocated 28 participants to Arbiters, 28 participants to Advocates, and 30 participants to Brokers.

Arbiters prioritized objectivity. They limited interactions with stakeholders that could threaten objectivity, even if those interactions would increase their public impact. Arbiters did not perceive personal and social values as relevant to the practice of science. In contrast, advocates’ foremost commitment was to public impact. They believed science should be used to support certain inherent goods. Advocates value objectivity but believe that value-neutrality is not achievable in science. Brokers believe values are inseparable from science, but they also believe that values can be managed to maintain objectivity. Like advocates, brokers believe it is appropriate for science to support certain inherent goods but are more cautious of bias and perceived bias. Table 2 summarizes the differences between groups across nine domains.

Direct Conflicts Between Objectivity and Public Impact

All questions in the category of direct conflicts between objectivity and public impact demonstrated statistically significant differences between clusters. Relative to brokers and advocates, arbiters were more likely to believe that 1) applied science should not involve advocacy (Q4) and 2) the choice of research topics is not a reflection of values (Q6). Arbiters were less likely to believe that too few scientists engage in advocacy related to their work (Q10). Relative to brokers and arbiters, advocates 1) had more concern about not doing work that has a public impact than concern about not being scientifically objective (Q9), 2) were more likely to believe that it is impossible for scientists to remain neutral and objective when considering public impact (Q7), and 3) perceive that applied science cannot be value-neutral (Q5). The broadest range of perspectives regarded the priority of neutrality and serving the public interest, with advocates placing more importance on doing work that serves the public(s) interest and arbiters placing more importance on maintaining the highest level of neutrality (Q8).

Scientist Engagement in Public Outreach

On items pertaining to scientist engagement in public outreach, participants of all groups similarly indicated a need for scientists to engage in outreach beyond their stakeholder groups (Q2). The largest difference between groups was on perceived risks and benefits of outreach, with advocates and brokers strongly indicating that the benefits usually outweigh the risks and arbiters expressing a middle-ground perspective (Q21). Advocates very strongly believed researchers have a responsibility to anticipate how their science could be misused to cause societal harm and to take steps to avoid misuse (Q11) –brokers and arbiters agreed to a lesser extent. Although there was not a statistically significant difference between groups, advocates and brokers strongly indicated that public outreach should be part of scientists’ training and responsibilities (Q3) and that scientist credibility is developed through public engagement; arbiters also held these perspectives to a lesser degree.

Communication Priorities and Approach

Groups possessed largely similar views on items related to communication priorities and approach. There was very strong consensus that communication of science should be a two-way conversation between the expert and the public (Q16). When science has some uncertainty but is generally accepted, all groups strongly indicated that is better to discuss uncertainties so the public is prepared to accept potential changes in science than to not talk about uncertainty because the public may think the science is unsettled (Q42). Though there were not statistically significant differences between groups, brokers prioritized being highly accurate above being highly engaging when communicating with the public; arbiters and advocates indicated these options were of similar importance. Also without statistically significant differences between groups (Q43), all groups indicated somewhat more importance for public communication that gets the main points across even if some nuance and limitations are sacrificed compared to making sure all nuances and limitations are understood even if it is more difficult for the public to understand (Q35). Only one question in this category yielded statistically significant differences – while advocates moderately indicated that scientists should use language that helps people with opposing viewpoints “hear” them, arbiters indicated a weak preference for scientists using the most accurate language possible (Q15).

Political Engagement and Perceived Bias

On most items related to political engagement and perceived bias, groups differed in the degree of their perspectives though the direction of perspectives was largely similar. The largest statistically significant differences between groups were on two items. First, advocates strongly perceived it to be acceptable for scientists to be politically active in a personal capacity; arbiters weakly disagreed and brokers were in the middle (Q29). The second item of large difference was on policy considerations, with advocates and brokers believing that scientists should proactively prompt policy makers to considerations absent from their policy discussions and arbiters assuming a middle ground between this perspective and only responding to specific inquiries (Q26).

Statistically significant differences coupled with similar across group means were identified on the following items. When policy options are being considered, all groups felt it was appropriate for scientists to advise on proposed policy options and suggest alternative policy options, with brokers most strongly supporting this perspective (Q25). All groups believed that the current political climate means scientists need to engage with the public more, with arbiters and brokers most strongly agreeing (Q27). Participants also agreed that scientists should feel free to engage in advocacy unrelated to their science, with advocates most strongly supporting this perspective (Q28).

Statistically significant differences were not present on the following items. Participants were moderate on their perceptions of which risk is more important, scientists being seen as politically motivated or political bias in science, with brokers indicating the former and advocates indicating the latter (Q23). Participants believed that scientists in the public sphere can present themselves and their science in a way that mitigates perceived bias, with brokers most strongly indicating this perspective (Q24). Participants also asserted that when a scientist’s work is misrepresented by a politician, the scientist has a responsibility to correct the misrepresentation (Q12).

The Media

Though there was not a statistically significant difference between groups, advocates moderately expressed the perspective that scientists should be able to communicate directly with the media, even if they are not the best communicators, whereas brokers moderately expressed that scientists should always seek communications specialists when speaking to the media (Q14). All groups indicated moderate to strong support for the perspective that scientists cannot trust media to represent/use science accurately (Q13).

Funding and Disclosure

On items related to funding and disclosure, all groups strongly agreed that scientists should always disclose their funding source to public(s) (Q20). All groups occupied a middle position in weighing the choice between the positions that scientists should not accept funding that could make their work appear biased and perceptions of bias need not influence acceptance of funding (Q46). Statistically significant differences were identified on the following two items. Arbiters most strongly perceived that if a scientist is going to engage in advocacy related to their area of scientific expertise, they should clearly state that they are engaging in a non-official, private capacity; brokers and advocates held this perspective to a lesser extent (Q19). Arbiters indicated that when engaging in public forums on a topic related to their science, scientists should keep their policy preferences private; brokers agreed to a lesser extent, while advocates weakly indicated that scientists should explicitly state their policy preferences (Q18).

Supporting/Protecting Specific Causes

Statistically significant differences across groups were identified on all items related to supporting/protecting specific causes, with advocates and brokers often possessing more similar positions than arbiters. Advocates and brokers strongly indicated that it is appropriate for a climate scientist to speak in support of carbon neutral policies (Q30), public health scientists to speak in support of mandatory vaccine requirements (Q31), and science to place inherent value on the reduction of global poverty (Q36); arbiters assumed a middle ground between perceiving these actions as appropriate or inappropriate. On placing inherent value on defending national security (Q41), the appropriateness of scientists placing inherent value on securing clean drinking water (Q39), and supporting environmental policies even when they negatively impact agriculture (Q32) all groups indicated agreement, with stronger agreement from brokers and advocates. Placing inherent value on protecting US energy independence was moderately supported by brokers, weakly supported by advocates, and neither supported nor not supported by arbiters (Q40).

Two items yielded strongly opposing results across groups –advocates and brokers strongly indicated that it is appropriate for science to place inherent value on the eradication of sexism (Q38) and homo/transphobia (Q37), whereas arbiters indicated inappropriateness. Advocates indicated that applied science has too much focus on industry, a view weakly held by objective scientists and not indicated by arbiters (Q17). Arbiters indicated that scientists have a responsibility to publish results regardless of potential societal harm, a view held to a lesser extent by advocates and not held by brokers (Q33).

Diversity

No statistically significant differences between groups were identified on diversity questions. All groups strongly indicated that science improves when scientists have diverse backgrounds (Q45) and moderately indicated that scientific knowledge shouldn’t be put above other types of knowledge (Q44).

Ethical Decision Making

On ethical decision making, no statistically significant differences were identified across groups. All groups moderately agreed that scientists need substantially more training than what is provided by IRB to prepare them for ethical decisions (Q47). Brokers moderately indicated that ethical decision making should be uniform within a discipline, whereas arbiters and advocates indicated a middle position between this view and the view that ethical decision making should be different for everyone (Q48).

Overall, arbiters had larger research appointments and were more likely to be White and male. Brokers held larger administration appointments and were more likely to have participated in a prior interview with our team on scientist roles and responsibilities.

Discussion

Tensions between objectivity and public impact have important implications for both the work of scientists and for society. Our research provides an early step toward empirically understanding these tensions and identifying scientists’ cultures of thought. In our discussion, we analyze key findings, provide preliminary suggestions for addressing identified tensions, and point to key questions and issues for further research.

Our research reveals that the presumed tensions between objectivity and public impact are not solely topics for philosophical speculation, but issues of practical difference among scientists. Scientists’ responses make clear that even if they have analyzed the problem systematically, they have come to distinct ethical stances. In addition, they feel the need for better training and tools to manage conflicting responsibilities.

This basic point – that these tensions are real and scientists want to understand and address them better – provides context for examining the three cultures of thought that emerged in our analysis: arbiters, advocates, and brokers. These clusters roughly align with those created by Pielke, with some key distinctions. Notably, we eliminated one of Pielke’s categories, pure scientist, as this type did not emerge in our cluster analysis.

Our advocate group closely aligned with Pilke’s issue advocates. Scientists in this group were strongly motivated by public impact and sought to increase perceived inherent goods through their work. Advocates also expressed the most comfort with political and media engagement. Our arbiter group similarly aligned with Pilke’s science arbiters. Arbiters prioritized objectivity and protected this objectivity by limiting the types of advice and outreach they offered. They perceived their personal preferences as largely irrelevant to the conduct of science. In contrast to Pilke’s science arbiters, however, our arbiters were supportive of public engagement as well as some forms of advocacy. Finally, our broker group reflected characteristics of Pilke’s honest brokers, but with a key distinction. Like Pilke’s honest brokers, our brokers sought to proactively support decision making while, at the same time, preserving objectivity. However, unlike Pilke’s honest broker type, our brokers indicated that science should support certain perceived inherent goods, such as carbon neutral policies, vaccine requirements, and gender equity. We cannot, with our data, explain how brokers account for this apparent inconsistency between objectivity and value preferences in the application of science. Future research with larger and more diverse participant pools may lead us to nuance or modify these categories further.

Within our three types, we find real differences and areas of substantial agreement. Some of the most prominent differences among cultures regard their commitment to public service and commitment to objective science. Advocates and arbiters differ in the priority they place on each, with the former emphasizing service to the public and the latter prioritizing scientific objectivity. In a sense, this tension marks a key distinction. The position of brokers between the two other groups reaffirms this frame. However, it is important not to view this difference as a polarity. All groups appreciate both values, so it may be more accurate to frame their differences as points on a continuum rather than as binary opposites. Even though our research design forced respondents to make binary choices, the issues addressed are rarely so simple.

Just as participants differed in the relative priority they placed on objectivity and public impact, they disagreed, to a lesser extent, on whether scientists should openly support policies based on their research. Advocates were likely to indicate that policy advocacy is appropriate and perhaps even required, while arbiters were more inclined to indicate that scientists should not engage in policy advocacy. Brokers, again, occupied the middle ground.

There were also areas of significant agreement. Most participants, in all groups, agreed that scientists should engage in public outreach and that they should receive training so that they can accomplish that outreach effectively. Most also agreed that the communication of science should involve two-way conversations between experts and the public, rather than unidirectional provision of information. In addition, there was widespread agreement that scientists should be willing to discuss the uncertainties of their research with the public. These findings all point to scientists’ willingness to see themselves in a public role, communicating openly with non-experts on issues where scientific research intersects with the public good. The results also point to a need for more effective training of scientists to communicate with broader publics.

Another important element of scientists’ commitment to public service is a widespread conviction that they should anticipate potential problems emerging from their research, including misuses of it. This finding points to the importance of communication with non-experts and with policy makers. It reflects the value, held by many scientists, of communicating effectively and accurately about their research and its public implications.

While we do not want to over-emphasize differences, there are significant disagreements among participants. These fall to some extent along disciplinary lines, with more engineers and agronomists, for example, in the arbiter category, and more social scientists in the advocate category. Additional research can increase both the number of disciplines surveyed and the sample size, to determine whether these initial characterizations hold. Further, qualitative research can probe potential reasons for these distinctions.

While our research is preliminary, it makes several important contributions. First and perhaps most noteworthy, it can help scientists understand the conflicts they face and recognize that they are not alone in feeling these tensions. Further, it provides conceptual categories and alternative perspectives that can help scientists understand their own positions and the positions of others. By presenting “arbiter,” “advocate,” and “broker” as points of orientation, our research can enable scientists to reflect on their own identities and their relationships with other scientists. Absent this information, scientists may either assume similarity – that all scientists agree with them – or overemphasize differences, imagining polarization when in fact there is substantial common ground. Recognition of commonality can provide a foundation for constructive conversations and progress toward more effective public roles for scientists. Awareness of diversity, on the other hand, can remind scientists that their positions are not universally held and that well-intended and well-informed people can hold quite different ethical perspectives. This information can be used to help scientists self-assess where they fall and to make informed, defensible decisions as they wrestle with the tensions between objectivity and public impact in their work.

From a philosophical perspective, our findings reinforce that objectivity-advocacy tensions include inductive risk, objectivity, and the appropriate relationship between science and policy at individual scientist and organizational levels (Douglas, 2009; Pielke, 2007). The distinctions among arbiters, advocates, and brokers can be interpreted as differing judgments about how scientists should weigh the consequences of potential error, manage uncertainty in public communication, and assume social responsibility implications of their work (Brown, 2013; Havstad, 2022). Funding and political forces may also add another layer of challenges in shaping research agendas, public interpretation, and scientific consensus as disagreements can become structurally embedded within institutional contexts (Kuykendall, 2022; Oreskes & Conway, 2010). By empirically mapping how scientists distribute themselves across arbiter, advocate, and broker orientations, this study bridges science philosophy with lived professional practice to demonstrate how balancing value-ladenness and social responsibility is manifested in contemporary scientific cultures of thought.

Participants in our study expressed a desire for better tools and training to address the tensions between objectivity and public impact. While our preliminary research is not adequate to offer a basis for creating such tools, this is a goal of future research. It is important to determine both the necessary content and the best method for this training. We suspect that a range of methods, including formal training, informal networking, and mentoring will all be helpful. The more difficult question concerns content. Many respondents said there was a need for more ethical training. Much ethics training in science is narrow and limited to explanation of professional codes of conduct, IRB and IACUC rules, and other highly specific guidelines. The guidance scientists need to address the tensions between objectivity and public impact are, however, more amorphous.

Future research can investigate the kinds of conflicts that scientists face and the resources, training, and tools that would help them negotiate these conflicts effectively and ethically. In addition, further research could examine the factors that condition difference. For example, if administrators are more likely to be brokers, what does that tell us? And what might lie behind the disciplinary disagreements – do disciplines have different institutional cultures that affect attitudes about public impact? One way to answer those questions will be to delve more deeply into the ways scientists in different disciplines and roles are trained. We also suggest inquiry about respondents’ views of the other frameworks. Do arbiters think advocates are completely wrong, for example, or do they think scholar advocacy is another way of doing science that is acceptable but just not for them? This will help us understand the nuances and gray areas that initial research has not been able to explore. More important, it can contribute to the design and implementation of training, tools, and resources that can help scientists in various fields and at various career levels identify, assess, and negotiate the tensions surrounding public impact.

Ethical training and reflection will not eliminate the tensions of scientific work but can provide better understanding of the relationship between objectivity and public impact, potential pitfalls, and ways to identify and analyze ethical challenges in science. Currently, the primary ethical preparation received by scientists is the responsible conduct of research (RCR) training required by federal agencies including the National Science Foundation (NSF). These mandates do not specify course content, which has resulted in considerable variation in ethics instruction across institutions (DuBois et al., 2008; Kalichman & Plemmons, 2007; Steneck & Bulger, 2007). In practice, many RCR programs focus on black-and-white rules and clear-cut examples of scientific misconduct to illustrate ethical expectations (Pennock & O’Rourke, 2017). The emphasis on rules over reflection could suggest to scientists that scientific ethics can be achieved simply by adhering to outlined steps (Bielefeldt et al., 2018; Pennock & O’Rourke, 2017).

With robust professional preparation, scientists can gain greater confidence for navigating ethical gray areas both in their science and their public outreach, which in turn can help them become more effective advisors and advocates, to the extent that they wish to be – or to be clearer about why they choose not to embrace these roles. Training that fosters reflection and moral awareness, particularly related to discipline-specific issues, can increase engagement and ethical skill development (Antes et al., 2009; Davis, 2002; McDonald, 2004; Pimple, 2002). Case studies are often ideal for learning how to interrogate and navigate ethical complexities that scientists could encounter in practice (Fisher, 2003), particularly when the case studies are coupled with structured reflection (Colby & Sullivan, 2008). This more nuanced approach to ethics education, which acknowledges uncertainty and tradeoffs, is preferred by faculty in science and engineering (Bielefeldt et al., 2018).

We, the authors, have applied these recommendations in our classrooms and workshops, using the survey questions, ethical scenarios, and reflection to help new and seasoned scientists develop personal ethical philosophies. We begin these sessions by acknowledging that there is no “right” ethical philosophy, but that it is important to develop a personally defensible philosophy in advance of ethical challenges. Then, when faced with a dilemma, scientists are better prepared to take a considered approach and, if necessary, defend that approach to others. In some applications, we use the survey questions from this study to prompt initial thinking about the unstated tradeoffs in scientific work. We ask participants to share the reasons for their perspectives and consider peers’ perspectives and the reasons behind those perspectives. Ultimately, participants write their own beginning set of ethical principles with the understanding that their principles may change over time as they encounter new information and experiences.

In this study we specifically examined scientists at land-grant institutions working in agriculture, natural resources, or life sciences because these scientists are expected to have a public impact and a commitment to objectivity, and they often conduct applied research on topics with economic, environmental, and social implications. While this sample was well-suited for comparing tradeoffs related objectivity and public impact, the results are not generalizable to all scientists. In particular, tradeoffs faced by scientists in our sample may be shaped by local political economies that focus on agricultural development. In other disciplines, tensions could vary. For example, a scientist working in basic physics may see little relation to advocacy in their work. Therefore, in response to tradeoff questions related to advocacy (e.g., Q4, Q6, Q10), the physicist may behave as an arbiter, though their placement in this group could be more a reflection of the type of work they do than of their ethical perspective. In contrast, a scientist working on politically controversial topics such as gene editing or vaccine development may perceive advocacy as necessary to maintain the relevance and application of their science. This perceived need could position their responses as reflective of an advocate. It is therefore important to condition the application of the results of this study. A potentially fruitful line of future research is the application of the binary questions to discipline-specific samples, and possible refinement of the questions to those disciplines. Additionally, for this study we sought an approach that would allow us to identify cultures of thought among scientists. Binary questions provided a means for measuring potential tradeoffs on a broad set of objectivity and public impact considerations assumed or hypothesized in literature. However, this approach limits depth of interpretation of the results and can suggest that direct tradeoffs are required between paired items, which is not necessarily reflective of scientists’ experiences. We therefore encourage use of these results to guide the selection of domains for future qualitative inquiry.
