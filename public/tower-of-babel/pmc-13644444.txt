Differential diagnostic value of PAX1 and JAM3 methylation for cervical intraepithelial neoplasia between premenopausal and postmenopausal women: a retrospective clinical study in China
Authors: Peixin Wang, Mengyu Zhang, Yucheng Lai, Tao Song, Yan Shen, Haiying Chen, Xiaoyan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPAX1/JAM3 methylation are promising biomarkers for high-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) detection, but menopausal status may affect diagnostic accuracy. This retrospective study compared their clinical performance between premenopausal and postmenopausal women undergoing colposcopy.Methods705 women undergoing colposcopy for abnormal screening results and/or clinical symptoms were stratified by menopausal status, including 192 postmenopausal and 513 premenopausal women. Subjects were further classified into ≤CIN1 and CIN2+ groups according to histopathology. Diagnostic and triage utility of PAX1/JAM3 methylation for identifying CIN2+ lesions were compared between premenopausal and postmenopausal women.ResultsPAX1/JAM3 methylation demonstrated superior diagnostic performance for CIN2+ detection in postmenopausal women compared with premenopausal women, either alone or in combination. Sensitivities of PAX1m and JAM3m in postmenopausal women (90.00%, 95% CI: 76.34–97.21%; 82.50%, 95% CI: 67.22–92.66%) were higher than those in premenopausal women (61.98%, 95% CI: 52.71–70.65%; 60.33%, 95% CI: 51.04–69.11%) (all p < 0.05). The combination improved sensitivity to 72.73% in premenopausal women and maintained 92.50% in postmenopausal women. Besides, PAX1/JAM3 methylation outperformed cytology and HPV16/18 testing in both groups (p < 0.001). In HPV16/18-positive or cytology triage, methylation increased positive predictive values (PPVs) for CIN2+/CIN3+ and reduced colposcopy referrals . However, CIN2+ missed rates in premenopausal women (27.54% for HPV16/18-positive triage and 23.85% for cytology triage) were higher than those in postmenopausal counterparts.ConclusionsThe diagnostic performance of PAX1/JAM3 methylation for high-grade cervical lesions differs distinctly between premenopausal and postmenopausal women undergoing colposcopy. Menopausal status should be considered when applying methylation biomarkers, particularly as a valuable auxiliary tool to improve diagnostic accuracy in elderly women.Keywords: PAX1/JAM3 methylation, cervical intraepithelial neoplasia, menopause, colposcopy, diagnostic performance, triage utility

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) is a malignancy with a substantial global impact on women’s health. It ranks as the second most common gynecological tumor worldwide, with approximately 662,301 new cases and 348,874 deaths reported in 2022 according to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) [1–4]. Notably, China is among the countries with high incidence rates of cervical cancer, accounting for nearly 20% of global cases [5–7]. Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) is the precancerous lesion of cervical cancer. Timely detection and intervention are crucial to reduce the disease-related mortality rate. Given the accelerating population aging, the number of elderly patients with CIN has been progressively rising. Due to distinct physiological and anatomical characteristics, postmenopausal women, in particular, tend to face unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [8].

Cervical carcinogenesis is a gradual and multistep process characterized by a prolonged development period of approximately 10–20 years, with over 90% of cases attributed to HPV infection [9]. Currently, the combination of liquid-based cytology (LBC) and high-risk human papillomavirus (hrHPV) testing represents the mainstream cervical cancer screening strategy globally [10,11]. Persistent hrHPV infection is considered the driving force for neoplastic transformation, and HPV16/18 account for approximately 40–60% of clinically significant CIN [12–14]. TheThinPrep cytological test (TCT), a widely used LBC method, is incorporated into risk-based screening guidelines [15,16]. However, the transient nature and high prevalence of HPV infections limit the specificity, with varying false-positive rates across different age groups [17]. Cervical cytology, conversely, is inherently subjective and highly operator-dependent and can be further compromised by cervical atrophy, potentially leading to a greater number of missed CIN2+ lesions [18,19]. There is also a potential problem of excessive referral when conducting colposcopy for all women with HPV16/18-positive or cytology ≥ ASC-US [20,21]. The above issues are particularly prominent among postmenopausal women, a key group facing unique challenges in the screening and diagnosis of cervical cancer, adding to the complexity of clinical management. This population exhibits lower screening participation yet experiences disproportionately high cervical cancer mortality, attributable to factors such as uneven screening access, reduced test sensitivity, and potential reactivation of latent HPV infections [8,22]. Besides, the postmenopausal period is characterized by the decline of ovarian function and a significant decrease in estrogen levels, leading to a series of anatomical and physiological changes in the reproductive system [23]. Cervical atrophy and the inward migration of the transformation zone compound the risk of missed diagnosis, and colposcopy with a transformation zone type 3 was reported to miss more than 50% of CIN2 + lesions [19,24]. Reductions in protective lactobacilli and weakening of local immune function collectively contribute to impaired hrHPV clearance and higher rates of persistent infection [25]. However, current protocols for postmenopausal women largely adhere to guidelines established for the general population, lacking specific consensus or high-quality evidence for this demographic [26,27].

Epigenetics, defined as alterations in gene expression regulated by chemical modifications without changing the DNA sequence, is closely associated with carcinogenesis [28]. DNA methylation, as a core mechanism, exhibits highly specific abnormal patterns across different tumor types, and their accumulation in specific genes serves as an indicator of malignant transformation [29]. Aberrant DNA methylation is an early epigenetic event during cervical carcinogenesis [30,31]. Paired box gene 1 (PAX1) and junctional adhesion molecule 3 (JAM3) have recently emerged as promising molecular biomarkers demonstrating significant advantages in CIN prediction [32]. PAX1, an important transcription factor, participates in embryonic development by regulating cell proliferation and migration [33]. JAM3, a member of the junctional adhesion molecule family within the immunoglobulin superfamily, plays a critical role in regulating cell migration and motility [34]. The PAX1/JAM3 methylation (PAX1m/JAM3m) assay has been validated in large multicenter studies. Combined methylation analysis of two genes exhibits notable complementary effects, which are closely related to the severity of cervical lesions and may be involved in the development of cervical cancer [35]. Previous research has demonstrated that PAX1m/JAM3m analysis has superior performance to conventional methods in both primary cervical cancer screening and triage of hrHPV-positive women [36]. In non-16/18 hrHPV -positive women, PAX1m/JAM3m outperformed cytology in detecting CIN3+, reducing colposcopy referral rates without compromising diagnostic sensitivity [37]. In addition, the methylation levels of JAM3 and PAX1 were reported to be significantly higher in individuals with HPV infection lasting for more than 3 years, suggesting their potential as cumulative indicators of infection duration prior to precancerous development [38]. Furthermore, a recent study confirmed that PAX1 serves as a promising biomarker for predicting the pathological progression of CIN lesions, and that a ΔCt threshold of PAX1 lower than 4.34 indicates a higher risk of pathological progression [39].

Currently, a critical, yet underexplored frontier in the clinical application of methylation-based diagnose is the influence of host factors, specifically age and menopausal status. A large body of epidemiological data indicates that age is a significant factor influencing the occurrence of cervical cancer [26,40]. Global DNA methylation patterns undergo age-related alterations, which are closely related to the malignant transformation of cervical epithelial cells [41]. Furthermore, the hormonal upheaval of menopause creates a distinct cervical microenvironment, and the decline in estrogen may indirectly influence the activity of DNA methyltransferases [42]. However, previous clinical observations were conducted in mixed-age populations, applying uniform biomarker thresholds and strategies while ignoring the biological differences between women at distinct physiological stages. Few studies have systematically compared the diagnostic and triage performance of PAX1 and JAM3 methylation in populations stratified by menopausal status. It remains unclear whether menopause affects the accuracy of PAX1/JAM3 methylation detection and how to optimize cutoff values for different populations.

Our research hypothesizes that the positive rate and clinical value of epigenetic methylation biomarkers may differ between premenopausal and postmenopausal women. We stratified subjects by menopausal status, aiming to compare the diagnostic performance and triage utility of PAX1/JAM3 methylation for CIN2+/CIN3+ prediction between the two populations referred for colposcopy. This approach may help assess its potential as a supplementary tool to improve diagnostic efficiency in postmenopausal women. These findings focus on providing further evidence for the individualized application of epigenetic biomarkers in cervical cancer screening.

Materials and methods

Participants and study design

This retrospective cross-sectional study enrolled patients referred for colposcopy due to abnormal cervical screening or clinical symptoms in Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital between December 2020 and April 2025. Subjects were further stratified into premenopausal and postmenopausal groups by menopausal status according to standard clinical criteria. All cytological and pathological specimens were independently evaluated by at least two expert pathologists. The diagnosis and management of CIN and cervical cancer cases adhered to established clinical guidelines. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were as follows:

Inclusion criteria

① Age ≥ 21 years, sexually active, including premenopausal and postmenopausal women. ② Concurrent availability of cervical biopsy results alongside HPV testing, cytology, and methylation assay findings ③ Agreement to participate in the study and signed the informed consent form.

Exclusion criteria

① Pregnancy or lactation; ② History of total hysterectomy or prior treatment for gynecological malignancies (e.g., surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy). ③ Congenital malformations of the reproductive system, concurrent other malignancies, or immunocompromised status. ④ Severe cardiovascular diseases, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, or hematological system diseases.

Clinical definition of menopausal status

(1) Premenopausal group: regular menstruation or perimenopausal women without continuous amenorrhea ≥12 months;

(2) Postmenopausal group: natural menopause with amenorrhea ≥12 months.

Cytological and HPV detection

Cervical exfoliated cell samples were collected using Cervex Brush (Rovers Medical Devices, North Holland, The Netherlands) and stored in ThinPrep PreservCyt solution (Hologic, MA, USA) for liquid-based cytology, HPV DNA testing, and methylation analysis. ThinPrep Cytologic Test (TCT) and an imaging system were used for cytological evaluation [43]. Cytological results were evaluated by two qualified cytopathologists according to the Bethesda System (TBS), including negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy (NILM), atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASC-US), low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL), atypical squamous cells cannot exclude high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (ASC-H), high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL), and cervical cancer. In this study, ASC-US or higher was defined as positive. The HPV DNA detection was performed using the Cobas 4800 HPV test (Roche, Shanghai, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The test results included individual identification of HPV types 16 and 18, as well as 12 other hrHPV types (non-HPV16/18): 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, and 68 [44].

Colposcopy and cervical biopsy

All enrolled subjects underwent colposcopy based on the following referral criteria: (1) abnormal cervical screening (HPV16/18-positive, persistent positivity for other high-risk HPV subtypes, TCT ≥ ASC-US); (2) abnormal clinical symptoms (suspicious cervical ulcers or mass, unexplained contact bleeding or vaginal discharge, etc.). During colposcopy, the cervix was treated with 5% acetic acid or Lugol’s iodine solution to assess morphological changes, and biopsies were conducted under colposcopic guidance for abnormal lesions. Colposcopic impressions were made according to the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology (ASCCP) guidelines, with multiple biopsies were taken from areas showing acetowhitening, metaplasia, or other suspicious findings [45–48]. In our practice, we meticulously documented transformation zone type, biopsy sites, performance of endocervical curettage (ECC) , and colposcopy adequacy. For postmenopausal women, we performed ECC whenever clinically feasible. Moreover, even in the absence of positive findings on acetic acid or iodine staining, we conducted random biopsies to reduce the risk of missed lesions—a step we believe can further strengthen our diagnostic accuracy. The histological classification of specimens was assessed by two qualified pathologists in accordance with international standards, which included normal (no CIN findings), CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, and invasive cervical cancer. The most severe histological finding was considered the final pathology result for analysis.

PAX1/JAM3 methylation analysis

The residual cervical cell samples in ThinPrep PreservCyt solution were used for methylation detection. The methylation detection of PAX1 and JAM3 was conducted using the Human PAX1 and JAM3 Gene Methylation Detection Kit (Real-time PCR) [Class: III medical devices: approved by the National Medical Products Administration (No. 20233400253)] with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as an internal control (OriginPoly Bio-Tec Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) [32]. The analyses were performed using the SLAN-96S automatic medical PCR analysis system (Shanghai Hongshi Med-Tech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Detailed methodology for DNA methylation detection is provided in our previous publications [49]. The methylation level of the PAX1 and JAM3 was calculated as the difference between the two Ct values (ΔCtPAX1 = CtPAX1-CtGAPDH and ΔCtJAM3 = CtJAM3-CtGAPDH). According to the manufacturer’s instructions: the positivity of the PAX1/JAM3m test was defined as ΔCtPAX1 ≤ 6.6 or ΔCtJAM3 ≤10.0. This is based on the standards set by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) of China and has been clinically verified. Multiple independent studies have reported comparable detection thresholds and performance characteristics using the same kit [50].

Statistical analysis

The data analysis for this study began on November , 2025. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc 23.0 (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium). Categorical variables were presented as numbers with percentages (%). Intergroup comparisons were conducted using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate while within-group comparisons were conducted using the McNemar test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (Q1–Q3), with intergroup comparisons analyzed by the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test for two groups. Using cervical histopathology as the gold standard for patient stratification, the diagnostic performance of each method in different populations was evaluated by constructing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the curves (AUCs). DeLong analysis was used to compare the AUCs between different strategies. Additionally, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV), along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated by MedCalc software. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The primary endpoint of this study was the cervical biopsy pathological diagnosis.

Ethics statement

This retrospective study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2025273). The need for informed consent was waived by the committee because this study involved only the analysis of fully anonymized medical records and did not include any direct patient intervention or contact. All data were de-identified prior to analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Characteristics of participants and main findings

A total of 705 subjects were finally enrolled in this study and grouped based on their menopausal status, including 513 premenopausal and 192 postmenopausal women. A schematic diagram of this study was shown in Figure 1. The results of cytology, HPV testing, methylation and cervical histopathology were summarized in Table 1. The median ages were 35 years (range 29–42 years) in the premenopausal cohort and 58 years (range 54–62 years) in the postmenopausal cohort. Among premenopausal patients, 392 (76.41%) were reported as normal tissue or CIN1, 61 (11.89%) were CIN2, 44 (8.58%) were CIN3 and 16 (3.12%) were diagnosed with cervical cancer. While in the postmenopausal group, results showed 152 (79.17%) women had normal histology or CIN1, 11 (5.73%) cases had CIN2, 12 (6.25%) cases had CIN3 and 17 (8.85%) cases had cervical cancer. The positive rates of HPV16/18 and cytology were 37.43% (192/513) and 62.38% (320/513), respectively, in premenopausal women, while 31.25% (60/192) and 55.21% (106/192) in postmenopausal women. Besides, PAX1m/JAM3m(+) was 20.86% (107/513) in premenopausal women while 27.60% (53/192) in postmenopausal women.

After stratifying the two groups according to pathological findings, no significant differences were observed in the sensitivity and specificity of traditional cytology or HPV16/18 for the diagnosis of ≤CIN1 or CIN2+ between the two groups (Supplementary Table 1). Notably, HPV16/18 demonstrated higher specificity than cytology in diagnosing ≤ CIN1, whereas cytology ≥ASC-US showed higher sensitivity in predicting CIN2+ (Supplementary Table 2). Furthermore, methylation appeared to be more valuable. The specificity of PAX1m/JAM3m(-) for diagnosing ≤ CIN1 in premenopausal women was higher than that in postmenopausal women (95.2% vs 89.5%, p = 0.015), while PAX1m/JAM3m(+) in the postmenopausal group exhibited higher sensitivity for predicting CIN2+ (92.5% vs. 72.7%, p = 0.009) (Supplementary Table 1).

PAX1/JAM3 methylation in premenopausal and postmenopausal women with different grades of cervical lesions

Based on the current criteria for PAX1/JAM3 methylation, we analyzed the results across different grades of CIN in the two groups. As shown in Table 2 and Figure 2, 95.2% (373/392) of premenopausal women and 89.5% (136/152) of postmenopausal women in the ≤CIN1 groups showed no methylation of neither PAX1 nor JAM3 gene. Notably, the positive rate of methylation in the premenopausal group increased with the severity of lesions, with 59.0% (36/61) and 84.1% (37/44) of women exhibiting methylation in at least one gene in the CIN2 and CIN3 subgroups, respectively. In contrast, the positive rate of methylation in postmenopausal women with high-grade CIN was significantly elevated (90.9% [10/11] in CIN2 lesion and 91.7% [11/12] in CIN3 lesion), indicating a more specific aberrant methylation pattern in postmenopausal progressive cervical lesions. Furthermore, PAX1m/JAM3m(+) in cervical cancer further increased to 93.8% (15/16) in premenopausal women, while it remained at 94.1% (16/17) in postmenopausal women.

Differences in the diagnostic performance of various methods for detecting CIN2+ lesions between premenopausal and postmenopausal women.

We further evaluated the diagnostic performance of PAX1/JAM3 methylation and other approaches for detecting CIN2+ in premenopausal and postmenopausal groups, aiming to establish stratified management strategies. The ROC curves of specific methods were shown in Figure 3. The sensitivity, specificity, and AUC values of each method in both groups were presented in Table 3. Traditional cervical screening methods (cytology and HPV16/18) demonstrated relatively low diagnostic performance for CIN2+ prediction in both groups compared with methylation (p < 0.001) (Table 4). The AUC of cytology ≥ASC-US was 0.681 (95% CI: 0.639–0.721) in premenopausal women and 0.672 (95% CI: 0.601–0.738) in postmenopausal women, while the AUC of HPV16/18 was 0.628 (95% CI: 0.585–0.670) and 0.650 (95% CI: 0.578–0.717), respectively. Notably, HPV16/18 alone showed low sensitivity across all women, which may lead to false negative results. Additionally, its PPV was relatively low, potentially resulting in unnecessary referrals. In contrast, cytology ≥ASC-US alone exhibited relatively higher sensitivity but lower specificity, increasing the likelihood of overdiagnosis. Methylation detection, however, demonstrated greater advantages. In premenopausal women, the AUC of combined PAX1m/JAM3m was significantly higher than that of PAX1m alone (0.839 [95% CI: 0.805–0.870] vs. 0.796 [95% CI: 0.758–0.830], p < 0.01) or JAM3m alone (0.839 [95% CI: 0.805–0.870] vs. 0.784 [95% CI: 0.746–0.819], p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 3). In postmenopausal women, PAX1m alone achieved the highest AUC (0.920 [95% CI: 0.873–0.954]), while the combination of PAX1m/JAM3m did not significantly improve diagnostic performance (p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 3). As shown in Table 3, methylation, whether used alone or in combination, showed significantly better diagnostic performance in postmenopausal women compared with premenopausal women (p < 0.05). Additionally, the sensitivities of PAX1m and JAM3m in postmenopausal women were 90.00% (95% CI: 76.34–97.21%) and 82.50% (95% CI: 67.22–92.66%) respectively, both significantly higher than those in premenopausal women [(61.98% (95% CI: 52.71–70.65%) for PAX1m and 60.33% (95% CI: 51.04–69.11%) for JAM3m (p < 0.05))]. Combining the two markers increased the sensitivity to 72.73% (95% CI: 63.88–80.43%) in premenopausal women, while maintaining a high level of 92.50% (95% CI: 79.61–98.43%) in postmenopausal women. Furthermore, methylation showed high specificity in both cohorts, the specificities of JAM3m alone and PAX1m/JAM3m were 96.43% (95% CI: 94.08–98.03%) and 95.15% (95% CI: 92.53–97.06%) in premenopausal women, respectively, which were higher than those in postmenopausal women (p < 0.05), whereas no significant difference in PAX1m specificity between the two groups.

Clinical performance of PAX1/JAM3 methylation for HPV16/18-positive triage in premenopausal and postmenopausal groups

As shown in Table 5, HPV16/18-positive patients were referred for colposcopy without triage. Among them, the PPVs of CIN2+ and CIN3+ were 35.9% (95% CI: 29.5–42.9%) and 19.8% (95% CI: 14.8–26.0%) in premenopausal women, requiring an average of 2.78 and 5.05 referrals to detect one case of CIN2+ and CIN3+, respectively. In contrast, postmenopausal women required 2.73 referrals to detect one case of CIN2+ and 3.75 referrals to detect one case of CIN3+. Incorporating cytology≥ ASC-US as a criterion for colposcopy referral would reduce the referral rates for CIN2+ to 65.1% (125/192; 95% CI: 58.1–71.5%) in premenopausal women and 55.0%, (33/60; 95% CI: 42.5–66.9%) in postmenopausal women, , with corresponding CIN2+ missed rates of 13.0% (95% CI: 7.0–23.0%) and 13.6% (95% CI: 4.7–33.3%). It is worth noting that methylation-based triage significantly improved the PPVs for CIN2+ and CIN3+ in premenopausal women compared with cytology (Supplementary Table 4). By triaging with PAX1m/JAM3m, the PPV for CIN2+ in premenopausal women significantly increased to 90.9% (95% CI: 80.4–96.1%), while the number of women referred for colposcopy decreased from 192 to 55, representing a 71.4% reduction. However, this approach came with a high CIN2+ missed rate of 27.5% (95% CI: 18.4–39.0%) and CIN3+ missed rate of 13.2% (95% CI: 5.8–27.3%), which for PAX1m or JAM3m alone is even higher. In contrast, the CIN2+ missed rate by methylation-based triage in postmenopausal women was significantly lower in premenopausal women (p < 0.05). Notably, PAX1m alone also exhibited a favorable triage effect in postmenopausal women, the PPVs for CIN2+ and CIN3+ were 87.5% (95% CI: 69.0–95.7%) and 62.5% (95% CI: 42.7–78.8%), with lower missed rates of 4.5% (95% CI: 0.8–21.8%) and 6.3% (95% CI: 1.1–28.3%), respectively.The referral rate for colposcopy dropped to 40% (95% CI: 28.6–52.6%), while the combination of JAM3m did not enhance its efficacy (Supplementary Table 5). Additionally, no cervical cancer cases were missed by PAX1m/JAM3m in premenopausal patients in the present research.

Clinical performance of PAX1/JAM3 methylation for cytology≥ ASC- US triage in premenopausal and postmenopausal women

In terms of patients with cytology ≥ ASC-US, the PPVs for CIN2+ and CIN3+ in premenopausal women were 34.1% (95% CI: 29.1–39.4%) and 18.8% (95% CI: 14.9–23.4%), respectively, requiring an average of 2.94 and 5.33 colposcopy referrals to detect one case of CIN2+ and CIN3+. In contrast, postmenopausal women required 3.21 colposcopy referrals to detect one case of CIN2+ and 4.42 referrals to detect one case of CIN3+. If HPV16/18 -positive is used as an additional criterion, the colposcopy referral rates decreased significantly to 39.1% (125/320; 95% CI: 33.9–44.5%) in premenopausal women and 31.1% (33/106; 95% CI: 23.1–40.5%) in postmenopausal women, respectively. At the same time, there were high CIN2 + missed rates [45.0%, (95% CI: 35.9–54.3%) and 42.4% (95% CI: 27.2–59.2%)] and CIN3 + missed rates [36.7% (95% CI: 25.6–49.3%) and 37.5% (95% CI: 21.2–57.3%)].

Methylation-based triage significantly increased the PPV for CIN2+ in both premenopausal and postmenopausal women compared with HPV16/18 (p<0.05) (Supplementary Table 6). As shown in Table 6, the PPVs of PAX1m/JAM3m for CIN2+ and CIN3+ in premenopausal patients were 85.6% (95% CI: 77.2–91.2%) and 53.6% (95% CI: 43.7–63.2%), while the corresponding values were 81.1% (95% CI: 65.8–90.5%) and 59.5% (95% CI: 43.5–73.7%) in postmenopausal patients, respectively. Furthermore, the colposcopy referral rates decreased to 30.3% (95% CI: 25.5–35.6%) in the premenopausal group and 34.9% (95% CI: 26.5–44.4%) in the postmenopausal group, with no significant difference between groups. Notably, methylation-based triage significantly reduced the CIN2+ missed rate in postmenopausal women compared with HPV16/18 (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 6). The missed diagnosis rates of CIN2+ for PAX1m alone and PAX1m/JAM3m in postmenopausal women were both 9.1%. Additionally, the CIN2+ missed rates using PAX1m or JAM3m alone in postmenopausal women were lower than those in premenopausal women (p < 0.05).

Discussion

Clinical strategies for cervical cancer, especially risk- stratified management for women at different physiological stages, represent a major focus in gynecologic oncology. Accurate diagnosis and triage of high-grade cervical lesions in postmenopausal women have long been a major clinical challenge. DNA methylation is a stable epigenetic change that occurs in the early stage of cervical lesions, providing a more objective molecular approach [51,52]. However, the diagnostic performance of methylation assays is not static but varies significantly across different age groups. Changes in cervical tissue morphology or immune microenvironment associated with menopause status may significantly affect patterns of PAX1/JAM3 methylation, necessitating a shift from a ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach to a population-specific model.

In this retrospective study, we innovatively categorized the population referred for colposcopy based on menopausal status, and systematically evaluated the clinical performance of PAX1/JAM3 methylation across different grades of cervical lesions. We demonstrated distinct methylation profiles between the two groups and ROC curve analysis revealed that PAX1m and JAM3m exhibited superior diagnostic performance for CIN2+ detection in postmenopausal women compared to premenopausal women, whether used alone or in combination. The combined assay outperformed single markers in the premenopausal group, whereas no significant difference was observed in the postmenopausal group. The sensitivities of PAX1m and JAM3m for detecting CIN2+ lesions in postmenopausal women (90.00%, 95% CI: 76.34–97.21%; 82.50%, 95% CI: 67.22–92.66%) were both higher than those in premenopausal women (61.98%, 95% CI: 52.71–70.65%; 60.33%, 95% CI: 51.04–69.11%) (all p < 0.05). Moreover, PAX1/JAM3 methylation was significantly superior to conventional cytology and HPV16/18 testing in both groups (p < 0.001). In terms of triage value, methylation-positive results increased the PPVs for CIN2+/CIN3+ in both populations compared with conventional strategies, with reduced colposcopy referral rates. Notably, PAX1/JAM3 methylation exhibited a higher CIN2+ missed rate in premenopausal women while postmenopausal women achieved relatively lower missed rates and fewer colposcopy referrals simultaneously. These findings may have important implications for optimizing cervical cancer diagnostic and triage strategies in older women.

Previous studies have shown that PAX1/JAM3 methylation exhibits superior diagnostic performance across transformation zone (TZ) types, particularly in TZ3, enabling improved CIN3+ risk discrimination [52,53]. PAX1/JAM3 methylation detection has also been proven to be an excellent triage tool for identifying cervical precancerous lesions in HPV16/18-positive or hrHPV-positive women [20,54]. However, the subjects in the above studies were all from mixed-age populations, and most of them ignored the impact of menopausal status on the clinical value of methylation biomarkers. The decline in estrogen levels and inward retraction of the cervical squamocolumnar junction in postmenopausal population predispose them to missed diagnosis. Besides, long-term persistent infection of hrHPV is more likely to progress to high-risk CIN or even cervical cancer [25,55,56]. These factors have changed the clinical presentation spectrum of cervical lesions and underscore the urgent need for enhanced attention to cervical cancer prevention and management in this group. At present, there are relatively few studies on the diagnostic performance of cervical cancer screening strategies in postmenopausal women. A recent cohort study of women over 50 years old showed that PAX1/JAM3 methylation was more effective for high-grade CIN detection in elderly women than traditional screening methods. Moreover, its sensitivity for CIN2+ lesions was higher than that in women under 50 years old, which is consistent with the results of our study [50]. Compared with existing studies, this retrospective research focuses on the stratified differences of epigenetic biomarkers between premenopausal and postmenopausal women, addressing the limitations of unified population analysis in traditional studies and clarifying the clinical value of PAX1/JAM3 methylation in different populations based on systematic menopausal-stratified results. These findings may provide a new theoretical basis and auxiliary tools for personalized and precise diagnosis of cervical precancerous lesions.

Notably, we focused on stratified analyses according to menopausal status and comprehensively compared the PAX1/JAM3 methylation characteristics across different grades of CIN between premenopausal and postmenopausal groups. The positive rate of PAX1/JAM3 methylation in premenopausal women increased progressively with disease severity, likely reflecting their stronger cervical cellular renewal and repair capacity. By contrast, methylation positivity in postmenopausal women was significantly elevated in CIN2+ lesions but did not differ markedly with further disease progression. We speculate that the aging-associated epigenetic drift may lead to a general decrease of methylation and predispose promoter CpG sites to rapid hypermethylation once they exceed a threshold in postmenopausal women. The epigenetic alterations related to high-grade lesions may be more prominent in this population, which may help explain the improved diagnostic performance of CIN2+ identification in this population [57]. In addition, the principal finding of this study is that PAX1m and JAM3m demonstrated superior diagnostic value for CIN2+ detection in postmenopausal women compared to premenopausal women as evidenced by significantly higher AUC values, whether used alone or in combination. The combined assay outperformed single markers in the premenopausal group, whereas PAX1m alone achieved relatively ideal diagnostic performance, with the addition of JAM3m providing no significant gain in the postmenopausal group. We hypothesize that the methylation pathways of PAX1 and JAM3 may be influenced by the periodic hormonal fluctuations during the perimenopausal cycle and the active state of the cervix, which might represent complementary oncogenic pathways [58,59]. This synergistic effect indicates that a multi-gene combination in this population is more advantageous to reduce the inherent false-negative risk. Moreover, PAX1m/JAM3m showed high sensitivity and specificity in postmenopausal women, proving to be a reliable auxiliary diagnostic tool. In contrast, the low sensitivity in the premenopausal group constitutes a major limitation, necessitating combination with other tests or more aggressive follow-up strategies. Furthermore, when used as a triage tool for HPV16/18-positive or ASC-US (+) cases, the missed rate of PAX1m/JAM3m was significantly higher in the premenopausal group while postmenopausal women achieved relatively lower missed rates and fewer colposcopy referrals simultaneously. Our findings underscore menopausal status as a critical determinant in the application of epigenetic biomarkers for enhancing the accuracy of high-grade lesions diagnosis.

From a clinical standpoint, these findings carry profound implications. The superior performance of PAX1/JAM3 methylation in postmenopausal women addresses a persistent challenge in cervical screening—its reliability is unaffected by atrophic morphological ambiguities, positioning it as a promising auxiliary tool for older women. These phenomena and differences reveal a complex interaction between molecular biology and the host’s physiological environment, and we propose following hypotheses regarding the possible reasons: Firstly, the decline in estrogen after menopause leads to a relatively metabolic quiescent state of the cervical microenvironment, which may promote ‘epigenetic stability’ and distinguish pathological excessive methylation more clearly [2,19]. Secondly, the nature of HPV infection differs significantly between the two groups [17,60,61]. Premenopausal women are prone to transient hrHPV infections, while PAX1m/JAM3m acts directly on host genes involved in precancerous transformation and marks an irreversible step towards malignancy. It is generally low or absent in these transient states,resulting in a reduced sensitivity [62]. On the contrary, persistent hrHPV infections drive the clonal selection of cells with stable epigenetic alterations, which is more likely to be detected by methylation and provides a robust safety net for postmenopausal women [63]. Future research needs to explore the specific biological mechanisms underlying the differences in the clinical performance of PAX1/JAM3 methylation based on menopausal status.

This study has several important limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the patients enrolled in our study were from a ‘colposcopy referred population’ rather than a ‘general screening population’, which means this cohort may have verification-bias and the data cannot be directly generalized to primary screening settings. Second, although the overall sample size was adequate, further stratification by menopausal status and disease severity resulted in relatively smaller case numbers in certain postmenopausal subgroups, which may limit the stability of the findings and render the estimation less generalizable. Third, the findings are derived from a single-center dataset, and the accuracy of methylation may be influenced by factors such as detection platform, reagents, and operational procedures. Fourth, this study employed commercial kits with fixed cut-off values. Although they have been adopted in several independent studies, the direct comparison of the single-gene detection is prone to algorithmic biases. Therefore, the generalizability of our conclusions requires validation through subsequent multicenter prospective studies. Finally, the retrospective nature of this analysis precluded complete control for all potential confounding factors, which may introduce selection bias and potentially affect the assessment of diagnostic performance. In summary, although we have provided preliminary evidence for the impact of menopausal status on the diagnostic performance of PAX1/JAM3 methylation and hope to formulate personalized strategies in the future, the translation of these findings into clinical practice will require rigorously designed, large-scale prospective cohorts to overcome these limitations and to facilitate further mechanistic exploration.

Conclusions

PAX1/JAM3 methylation demonstrates superior diagnostic efficiency for distinguishing high-grade cervical precancerous lesions in postmenopausal women compared with premenopausal women. This molecular model can effectively compensate for the insufficient accuracy of traditional methods in special menopausal populations, which may provide a theoretical basis for optimizing personalized strategies and serve as a particularly valuable auxiliary tool for improving diagnostic accuracy, especially in elderly women.
