Development and External Validation of an Interpretable Machine Learning Model Using Routine Blood Biomarkers for Differentiating Malignant Breast Nodules
Authors: Longmei Chen, Xiaoyan Teng, Jiale Tian, Yuzhen DU, Wanchao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrecise identification of malignant breast nodules is critical for clinical intervention. This study aimed to develop and externally validate an interpretable machine learning model using routine blood biomarkers.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter study included 899 women with pathologically confirmed breast nodules in a derivation cohort from Shanghai Baoshan Hospital (March 2022–December 2024). After missing-value imputation and LASSO selection, eight models were developed. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). The models were subsequently validated in an independent temporal validation cohort (n = 205, recruited from the same center between January 2025 and September 2025) and an external validation cohort (n = 290, Tongji Hospital, Shanghai, between January 2025 and November 2025). Model interpretability was evaluated using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsEleven predictors (age and ten biomarkers) were selected. The Random Forest (RF) model exhibited the best performance, yielding AUCs of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.77–0.87), 0.78 (95% CI: 0.71–0.86), and 0.72 (95% CI: 0.66–0.78) in the internal, temporal, and external validations cohorts, respectively, indicating a moderate decline yet acceptable discriminative ability in independent validation cohorts. In the temporal validation cohort, its diagnostic performance was comparable to ultrasound (AUC = 0.76) and mammography (AUC = 0.74). SHAP analysis identified hs-CRP, RBC, and age as the most influential predictors. A web-based calculator was developed to facilitate future evaluation of the model.ConclusionRoutine blood biomarkers can support interpretable machine-learning models for differentiating malignant from benign breast nodules. The proposed model may serve as a complementary pre-biopsy risk-assessment tool alongside conventional imaging modalities; however, prospective validation across broader populations and healthcare settings remains necessary before routine clinical implementation.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women under 50 years of age.1–3 In China, the reported 370,000 new cases and 78,000 deaths in 2024 underscore a formidable public health challenge.3 Precise detection is paramount to facilitating timely therapeutic intervention, which significantly reduces mortality and optimizes long-term prognosis.4,5 Consequently, there is a clinical need to develop reliable and accessible diagnostic strategies tailored to Chinese populations.6,7

Currently, mammography and ultrasonography serve as the primary screening modalities. However, mammography demonstrates diminished sensitivity for small tumors (≤ 10 mm) and reduced accuracy in dense breasts tissue—a common phenotype in Asian women—alongside potential risks from ionizing radiation.8–10 While ultrasonography is indispensable for dense breasts, its suboptimal specificity frequently leads to excessive false positives and unnecessary biopsies.11,12

To address these limitations, machine learning (ML) models have been developed. Nevertheless, many existing models are derived from Western cohorts and tend to overestimate risk in Asian individuals.13–15 Several Chinese-specific models have also been proposed, but earlier studies primarily relied on demographic, imaging features,16,17 or comprehensive lifestyle factors.18 More recently, blood-based ML models have emerged as promising non-invasive approaches, using genetic variants,19,20 and immune-related markers.21 In particular, Qiu et al developed a diagnostic model based on 11 autoantibodies against tumor-associated antigens, achieving AUCs of 0.88 and 0.85 in the training and validation cohorts, respectively.22 Similarly, Chen et al reported AUCs ranging from 0.78 to 0.86 using routine blood biomarkers and machine-learning algorithms.23 Despite these encouraging findings, many existing models lack temporal validation, independent external validation, or sufficient interpretability for clinical implementation. Furthermore, access to high-quality breast imaging remains uneven across China, particularly in primary and rural healthcare settings. Therefore, a low-cost diagnostic tool based on routinely available blood tests could provide a practical adjunct for risk stratification.

Growing evidence indicates that chronic inflammation plays a pivotal role in BC pathogenesis, as persistent proinflammatory states drive tumor-associated inflammatory processes.24,25 This inflammatory milieu facilitates tissue remodeling, angiogenesis, and tumor progression.26,27 Systemic inflammatory markers, such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP)28 and fibrinogen (Fib), are closely associated with increased incidence and metastasis.29,30 Inflammation also contributes to cancer-related anemia (CRA), characterized by reduced RBC and hemoglobin levels and altered red blood cell distribution width (RDW-CV).31 Platelet activation further promotes tumor growth through angiogenesis and immune escape.26 In parallel, dysregulated lipid metabolism—including elevated total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) alongside reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C)—has been implicated in tumor progression.32,33

Tumor markers like CA153, CA125, and CEA reflect tumor burden, their individual diagnostic specificity remains limited.34,35 Age is a well-established risk factor.36 Therefore, integrating age with inflammatory markers, CRA-related parameters, lipid metabolic indices, and tumor markers may provide complementary diagnostic information.

Composite inflammatory indices such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) have been widely studied; however, they are derived from overlapping hematological measurements. To minimize redundancy among candidate predictors and allow machine-learning algorithms to identify the most informative features directly from routinely available laboratory data, we focused on primary laboratory variables rather than derived inflammatory ratios. Although some biomarkers show inconsistent associations in isolation, they may provide complementary information when integrated within machine-learning frameworks across multiple biological pathways.

Despite recent progress, three major challenges remain in blood-based diagnostic models for breast cancer. First, many models lack rigorous temporal and external validation, limiting their generalizability. Second, the biological contributions of individual biomarkers are often difficult to interpret due to limited model transparency. Third, few studies have translated predictive models into clinically accessible tools. Given the growing emphasis on transparency and interpretability in clinical artificial intelligence, explainable machine-learning models are increasingly advocated to facilitate clinical trust, model evaluation, and implementation in routine practice.

In this study, we developed and externally validated a dual-center diagnostic model to differentiate malignant from benign breast nodules using routinely available hematological biomarkers. The model is intended as a complementary pre-biopsy risk-assessment tool rather than a replacement for imaging. The candidate predictors including inflammatory markers (hs-CRP,30 Fib37), CRA-related parameters (RBC, Hb,38 RDW-CV,39,40 PLT26), lipid metabolic indices41,42 (TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C), and tumor markers (AFP,43 CEA,44,45 CA199,43 CA125,35 CA153,46 SCC47). Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility, and subsequently compared with conventional imaging modalities. Finally, an interpretable web-based calculator was established to facilitate future clinical evaluation and potential implementation.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

The overall study workflow, delineated in Figure 1, comprises five sequential phases: model development, internal validation, temporal validation, external validation, and model interpretation. The derivation cohort was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%) for model construction and an internal validation set (30%). To evaluate clinical generalizability and robustness of the developed models, we utilized an independent temporal validation cohort from our institution, alongside an external validation cohort sourced from a collaborating center (Tongji Hospital). This study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was formally approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Shanghai Baoshan Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine (Approval No. 2023K42). Study Participants

The derivation, temporal and external validation cohorts followed identical inclusion and exclusion criteria (Figure 2). Inclusion criteria were: (1) female patients with pathologically confirmed benign fibroadenoma or BC; (2) primary cases with no history of prior breast surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy; and (3) availability of complete clinical, laboratory, and pathological records within the electronic medical record (EMR) system. Exclusion criteria included: (1) male patients; (2) diagnosis of other benign conditions (eg, mastitis, breast cysts, mammary duct ectasia), accessory breast tissue, or secondary malignancies; (3) history of prior anti-tumor therapies; or (4) missing pathological or critical laboratory data. Additionally, a healthy control group (n = 68) was recruited from individuals undergoing routine physical examinations with no history of breast disease or malignancy. The healthy control group was included only for descriptive baseline comparison and was not used for model development or validation. Baseline clinical characteristics are detailed in Supplementary Table S1, and the number of participants with missing data for each variable of interest is detailed in Supplementary Figure S1. The screening process was conducted by two independent researchers who cross-referenced clinical keywords with pathological codes to ensure data integrity. Laboratory Measurements

Fasting blood samples were collected upon admission prior to clinical intervention. Complete blood counts (CBC) were performed following a 30-minute room-temperature incubation. Remaining specimens were centrifuged at 3,500 × g for 10 minutes, with serum processed within 4 hours.

Whole blood parameters (RBC, Hb, RDW-CV, and PLT) were analyzed using a Sysmex XN-9000 automatic hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corp., Kobe, Japan). Fib levels were assessed using a Sysmex CS-5100 coagulation analyzer (Sysmex Corp., Kobe, Japan), while hs-CRP levels were determined via a Genrui PA200 specific protein analyzer (Genrui Biotech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Serum lipid profiles (TC, TG, HDL-C, and LDL-C) were quantified on a Beckman Coulter AU5800 biochemical analyzer. Tumor markers (AFP, CEA, CA199, CA125, CA153, and SCC) were measured using a Beckman Coulter DXI800 chemiluminescent immunoassay system (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA). All assays were performed using manufacturer-recommended reagents in strict accordance with standard operating procedures, maintaining intra-assay coefficients of variation (CV) below 10%. Comprehensive definitions and standardized abbreviations for all parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Variable Selection

Missing values (<30%) were handled separately in the training, internal and temporal validation cohorts before feature selection and model development, using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) with a random forest-based imputation method. Five imputed datasets (m = 5) were generated, and the mean of the five imputed values for each missing observation was used as the final imputed value in the subsequent analyses. Continuous variables were standardized via Z-score normalization. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation was performed exclusively within the training cohort to screen optimal features, and the selected predictors were subsequently applied for model development and validation. The predictive significance of selected features was further corroborated using univariate logistic regression to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Model Development and Validation

Predictive models were constructed within the Tidymodels framework in R (version 4.5.1). Eight machine learning algorithms were implemented: Logistic Regression (Logistic) utilizing the logistic_reg function with the glm engine; Naive Bayes (NB) via naive_Bayes and the naivebayes engine; Random Forest (RF) using rand_forest with the ranger engine; Support Vector Machine (SVM) through svm_rbf and the kernlab engine; Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) via boost_tree with the xgboost engine; Decision Tree (DT) using decision_tree and the rpart engine; K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) via nearest_neighbor with the kknn engine; and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) using the mlp function with the nnet engine.

For Random Forest, hyperparameter tuning was performed using Latin hypercube sampling over predefined parameter spaces, including mtry (3–10), number of trees (100–800), and minimum node size (2–15), with 15 candidate combinations evaluated. For XGBoost, a similar Latin hypercube sampling strategy was applied, tuning number of trees (100–800), learning rate (0.01–0.30), maximum tree depth (3–10), and minimum node size (2–15).

All hyperparameter optimization was conducted within a stratified five-fold cross-validation framework, and the optimal parameter set was selected based on the highest mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC). Logistic regression, naïve Bayes, support vector machine, decision tree, k-nearest neighbor, and multilayer perceptron models were implemented using default hyperparameter settings within their respective R packages to ensure a consistent and fair comparison across algorithms. Following optimization, each model was retrained on the full training cohort to generate final locked models for evaluation in the internal, temporal, and external validation cohorts. The optimal probability threshold for binary classification was determined using the Youden index based on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve in the training cohort. This threshold was subsequently applied to all validation cohorts to generate class labels for performance evaluation.

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were computed for the optimal Random Forest model using the fastshap package in R. A model-agnostic permutation-based SHAP approach was applied to estimate feature contributions to predicted probabilities of malignancy. The prediction function was explicitly defined to extract class-specific probabilities from the ranger model outputs. SHAP values were estimated using 100 Monte Carlo simulations (nsim = 100) to balance computational efficiency and estimation stability. Both global and local explanations were generated based on SHAP values, and feature importance was summarized using mean absolute SHAP values across all observations.

Statistical Analysis

The derivation cohort included 236 malignant cases and 17 candidate predictors, yielding approximately 14 events per variable, which exceeds commonly recommended minimum thresholds for prediction model development and reduces the risk of model overfitting. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed data are reported as median (interquartile ranges, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and compared using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Variable Selection

The derivation cohort was randomly partitioned into training (70%) and internal validation (30%) sets. Following MICE-based imputation, LASSO regression was applied to the training set to identify the most parsimonious predictors and minimize confounding. Ten features were retained at the optimal lambda (λ): age, RBC, RDW-CV, hs-CRP, Fib, AFP, CEA, CA199, HDL-C, and LDL-C (Figure 3A). CA153 was additionally retained because of its established clinical relevance in breast cancer and widespread clinical use. Multicollinearity analysis of these 11 variables yielded Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values below 2, indicating the absence of significant collinearity (Supplementary Table S3). Univariate logistic regression was performed to describe the direction and magnitude of associations between the selected variables and malignant breast nodules (Supplementary Figure S2), identified age (OR = 1.069, 95% CI: 1.05–1.09) and CEA (OR = 1.597, 95% CI: 1.29–1.96) as positively associated with malignancy. The stability of features selected by LASSO was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation (Figure 3B), the SHAP importance ranking (Figure 3C) confirmed that these 11 variables contributed most substantially to the model’s overall predictive performance. Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated an interpretable diagnostic framework for the identification of malignant breast nodules, leveraging a dual-center clinical dataset to emphasize the diagnostic value of a multi-panel blood biomarker set integrating tumor-associated inflammatory indicators, tumor antigens and lipid profiles. Utilizing a RF algorithm, our model demonstrated acceptable diagnostic performance across internal, temporal, and external validation cohorts (Supplementary Figure S8), requiring only patient age and ten routine hematological parameters. Compared to Qiu et al’s immunodiagnostic model (AUC = 0.849)22 and Chen et al’s four-marker model (AUC = 0.834),23 our integrated approach achieves comparable performance with broader biomarker coverage with model interpretability supported by SHAP analysis. In the temporal validation cohort, the RF model (AUC = 0.78) achieved comparable discriminative performance to ultrasound (AUC = 0.76) and mammography (AUC = 0.74), with DeLong’s test demonstrating non-significant differences in AUC across all comparisons. Notably, the model demonstrated higher specificity (69.8%) than ultrasound (58.4%) and mammography (65.1%), although this was accompanied by lower sensitivity. This favorable specificity profile helps minimize false-positive referrals, rendering the model a useful adjunct to routine imaging. However, BI-RADS 4 subcategories (4A–4C) were not consistently incorporated into the retrospective imaging analysis and therefore could not be evaluated separately. This limitation may have reduced the discriminatory performance of the imaging-based comparison and should be considered when interpreting the reported imaging AUCs. These findings support the potential complementary role of inflammation-associated biomarkers in breast nodule evaluation. Furthermore, the model’s retained acceptable performance during external validation (AUC = 0.72) and superior net benefit in DCA underscore its potential as a complementary risk-assessment tool rather than a replacement for conventional imaging assessment.

This study strictly follows the TRIPOD-AI guidelines, ensuring a high level of transparency and methodological rigor.48 By benchmarking clinical diagnoses against gold-standard pathology and handling missing data via random forest-based MICE, we established a high-fidelity dataset that minimizes bias and preserves statistical power. Additionally, we performed a sensitivity analysis on the training set to assess the robustness of our missing data handling strategies, and the results are presented in Supplementary Table S5. To maximize clinical utility, variable selection was anchored in biological plausibility rather than a purely data-driven approach, a strategy that may help reduce overfitting while enhancing model interpretability. The framework was further substantiated through dual-center external validation, with calibration plots and Brier scores employed to ensure reliable risk estimation. Crucially, the integration of DCA and SHAP-based feature analysis—deployed via a user-friendly web platform—may facilitate future clinical evaluation and implementation.

Nevertheless, several methodological considerations warrant attention. Due to moderate imbalance (26% malignant cases), no additional resampling strategy was implemented. This may have compromised model optimization even with comprehensive performance assessment, and relevant resampling approaches can be explored in future work. In addition, CA153 was retained because of its established clinical relevance in breast cancer. Although this approach may improve clinical interpretability, it introduces a degree of subjectivity and may represent a potential source of selection bias. Future studies should further validate the incremental contribution of CA153 in independent cohorts. Although LASSO regularization, cross-validation, and independent temporal and external validation cohorts were employed to reduce overfitting risk, residual model optimism cannot be entirely excluded. In addition, although calibration was assessed using calibration curves, Brier scores, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, future studies may benefit from incorporating newer calibration metrics, such as calibration intercepts, calibration slopes, and the Integrated Calibration Index, to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of model calibration. The reduction in specificity observed during temporal and external validation is not unexpected and likely reflects differences in patient characteristics, disease spectrum, and laboratory measurement variability between cohorts. Such performance attenuation is commonly observed when prediction models are evaluated in independent datasets and may indicate reduced overfitting rather than model instability. Finally, the external validation cohort achieved an AUC of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.66–0.78), indicating residual uncertainty in performance estimates and underscoring the need for further prospective validation in larger and more diverse populations. Collectively, these practices provide a robust, interpretable framework with potential clinical utility.

To identify the optimal predictive architecture, we systematically evaluated eight machine learning algorithms, including Logistic, DT, MLP, RF, SVM, XGBoost, KNN, and NB. Following 5-fold cross-validation, the RF model emerged as the most robust architecture, demonstrating a superior balance between sensitivity and specificity. This enhanced performance may be related to the RF algorithm’s ability to accommodate complex nonlinear relationships among predictors, which may not be fully captured by traditional linear models. However, the specific interactions underlying model performance were not formally evaluated in the present study. Furthermore, the model’s generalizability was substantiated by an acceptable AUC of 0.72 in the independent external cohort.

SHAP analysis identified hs-CRP, RBC, age, CA153, RDW-CV as the five most influential predictors, underscoring the critical role of systemic inflammation, CRA, age and tumor burden in our diagnostic framework. Specifically, elevated hs-CRP levels reflect the systemic inflammatory milieu characteristic of mammary carcinogenesis,28 where tumor-derived cytokines such as IL-6 may stimulate hepatic CRP synthesis.49

This persistent inflammation state likely suppresses erythropoiesis and accelerates erythrocyte turnover,39 as evidenced by the high predictive weight assigned to RBC and RDW-CV. CA153, a widely used breast cancer biomarker, may provide complementary information regarding tumor burden,49 whereas age remains a well-established risk factor for breast cancer development.36 Furthermore, the strategic inclusion of lipid metabolic markers (HDL-C, LDL-C) and secondary tumor antigens (CEA, CA199 and AFP)46,50 bolsters the model’s robust performance. Although these variables contributed less individual weight, their collective integration enables the RF model to map the multi-dimensional interactions between bioenergetic reprogramming and oncogenic signaling. Collectively, these results suggest that integrating heterogeneous biomarkers may enhance risk stratification and provide information complementary to conventional imaging assessments.

This study presents two key innovations. First, we developed a diagnostic model by focusing on readily accessible clinical indicators, namely tumor-associated inflammatory markers, serum tumor biomarkers and blood lipid profiles—a strategic focus that remains largely underexplored in existing literature, where models often rely on conventional demographic data. Second, it is highly encouraging that this simplified framework, utilizing only routine blood parameters, demonstrated diagnostic performance comparable to traditional imaging modalities. This discovery underscores that hematological profiling can capture critical systemic signals overlooked by imaging, thus serving as a potential adjunct to current screening protocols.

However, several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, although externally validated, all participants were recruited from tertiary-care institutions in Shanghai. Furthermore, the study population primarily consisted of patients with pathologically confirmed breast nodules, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of our findings to broader screening populations, other healthcare settings, and different ethnic groups. Second, the retrospective design precludes assessment of whether these biomarkers can detect malignancy earlier than conventional imaging. Furthermore, the nine-month temporal validation period may not fully capture long-term temporal drift. Third, while performance degraded in external validation, the AUC remained above 0.70, suggesting potential utility with further refinement. The observed performance drop may stem from inter-institutional disparities in patient demographics, clinical management protocols, or laboratory testing metrics. Accordingly, additional refinement and multi-center validation are required to improve this model. Finally, while the current model relies exclusively on routinely available hematologic biomarkers without incorporating molecular, genetic, imaging, or radiomic features, this design was intentionally chosen to maximize accessibility, cost-effectiveness, and clinical applicability. Future studies integrating multimodal data may further improve diagnostic performance while preserving interpretability. Although the model demonstrated encouraging diagnostic performance, its impact on clinical risk stratification, healthcare costs, and biopsy utilization was not evaluated in the present study. Future prospective implementation studies are needed to determine whether integration of this tool into routine clinical workflows may reduce unnecessary biopsies and improve resource utilization while maintaining diagnostic safety.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed and externally validated an interpretable RF model using routine hematological biomarkers for differentiating malignant breast nodules. The model demonstrated acceptable diagnostic performance across multiple validation cohorts and was further translated into a web-based application to facilitate clinical research and evaluation. As a low-cost and accessible approach, the model may provide additional risk-assessment information for women with breast nodules and serve as an adjunct to conventional imaging in pre-biopsy evaluation. Nevertheless, prospective multicenter studies are required to validate its generalizability and clinical utility before routine implementation.
