Abstract
. Large language models (LLMs) typically generate direct answers, yet they are increasingly used as learning tools. Studying instructors’ usage is critical, given their role in teaching and guiding AI adoption in education. We designed and evaluated TeaPT , an LLM for pedagogical purposes that supports instructors’ professional development through two conversational approaches: a Socratic approach that uses guided questioning to foster reflection, and a Narrative approach that offers elaborated suggestions to extend externalized cognition. In a mixed-method study with 41 higher-education instructors, the Socratic version elicited greater engagement, while the Narrative version was preferred for actionable guidance. Subgroup analyses further revealed that less-experienced, AI-optimistic instructors favored the Socratic version, whereas more-experienced, AI-cautious instructors preferred the Narrative version. We contribute design implications for LLMs for pedagogical purposes, showing how adaptive conversational approaches can support instructors with varied profiles while highlighting how AI attitudes and experience shape interaction and learning.



1.
Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) tools, particularly large language models (LLMs), are increasingly positioned as companions for learning and professional development. Their promise lies in delivering personalized support: helping learners of all kinds, from students to working professionals, reflect, practice, and grow at scale. If designed well, such systems could extend opportunities for lifelong learning by not only providing efficient access to information but also supporting deeper processes of reasoning, reflection, and skill development
(
Luckin and Holmes 2016
)
.

Despite this promise, two major obstacles remain.
First, most LLMs, by design, generate direct answers rather than facilitate teaching and learning. This makes them efficient but not inherently pedagogical: they excel at delivering solutions but rarely foster understanding
(
Koedinger et al. 2012
;
Collins and Halverson 2018
)
. Recent product efforts, such as ChatGPT’s
Study Mode

(
OpenAI 2025
)
introduced in July 2025, signal movement toward learning-focused interactions through socratic-style questioning, yet it remains unclear whether such approaches can meaningfully support learning.

Second, instructor-facing generative AI tools are proliferating, such as syllabus generators, assignment creators, and grading assistants, but empirical studies of how instructors actually engage with these tools remain limited. Prior work shows that AI literacy and attitudes are becoming critical barriers: some instructors experiment actively, while others remain skeptical
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
. Because instructors are responsible not only for adopting AI themselves but also for setting norms and serving as role models in how AI should be used, this heterogeneity creates uneven capacity to address students’ AI practices
(
Luckin and Holmes 2016
;
Kamali et al. 2024
)
.
Instructors’ choices and behaviors ripple outward, influencing how students interpret what counts as responsible or effective use. This underscores the need for empirical evidence on how design choices in AI systems intersect with instructors’ professional backgrounds and responsibilities.

We address these challenges by designing and evaluating
TeaPT
, an LLM-based prototype with two conversational approaches for teaching pedagogical strategies. The
Socratic
approach emphasizes reflective questioning, aligning with theories of dialogic and interactive learning that highlight the value of reasoning through guided dialogue
(
Chi and Wylie 2014
;
Wegerif 2007
;
Mercer 2000
)
. The
Narrative
approach emphasizes elaboration and breadth, aligning with theories of externalized cognition, where knowledge is extended through rich external suggestions and representations
(
Hutchins 1995
;
Clark and Chalmers 1998
;
Scardamalia and Bereiter 2006
)
. Together, they offer multiple processes for professional learners to engage with LLMs. In a mixed-method study with 41 higher-education instructors, we examined how these two approaches shaped engagement, survey ratings, and qualitative perceptions, including variation by AI attitude and teaching experience.

Summary of contributions.

We empirically show how conversational approach (
socratic
vs.
narrative
) shapes engagement and reflective learning opportunities for professional learners, specifically higher education instructors.
We demonstrate how instructors’ backgrounds mediate these effects. For example, the
socratic
version was especially useful for junior instructors and those more optimistic about AI.
We contribute design implications for LLMs for pedagogical purposes.

2.
Related Works

2.1.
LLMs for Learning and Tutoring

The use of AI as a teaching and learning technology has a long history in education, most notably through intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) designed to foster reasoning, practice, and reflection rather than simply deliver answers
(
Koedinger et al. 2012
;
Collins 2013
;
Chi and Wylie 2014
;
Luckin 2018
)
. These systems emphasized scaffolding through guided practice and feedback, with frameworks such as cognitive apprenticeship
(
Collins 2013
)
and ICAP
(
Chi and Wylie 2014
)
showing that active engagement and reflection are critical to durable learning. This work established AI not only as a source of information but also as a partner that shapes how learners reason and practice.

The LLM has more recently been studied as a teaching and learning technology with mixed results. EducationQ
(
Shi et al. 2025
)
evaluates instructional effectiveness across models, showing that teaching quality does not scale linearly with size; PACE
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
simulates learner profiles and adapts Socratic feedback to personalize mathematics tutoring; and Agent4Edu
(
Gao et al. 2025
)
uses profile and memory modules to simulate learner responses for large-scale evaluation. These studies highlight the potential of LLMs for interaction and personalization, yet most LLMs remain optimized to provide direct answers rather than to scaffold reasoning or reflection. Their actual effects on learning outcomes, and the design choices needed to make them pedagogically effective, remain open questions.

At the same time, generative AI applications such as ChatGPT are already being adopted as teaching and learning tools across contexts. Students use them for studying and homework, often as a supplement to traditional resources
(
Tang et al. 2025
)
. Instructors adopt them for lesson preparation and teaching support, though surveys report ambivalence and uneven patterns of uptake
(
Center 2024
;
Mamo et al. 2024
;
Cabellos et al. 2024
)
. Beyond formal education, adults in the workforce are using generative AI for professional learning, such as developing new skills, practicing workplace communication, and pursuing upskilling and reskilling opportunities
(
Pew Research Center 2025
;
Poquet and De Laat 2021
)
. These trends show that generative AI is making learning accessible to a wide range of learners, while also raising new challenges and opportunities for the design of systems that act not only as problem solvers but as effective pedagogical partners. This motivates our work on
TeaPT
, which examines how conversational design choices can make LLMs more effective for learning.

2.2.
Instructor-Facing AI Tools and Literacy

Instructors are important professional learners: they use AI in their own teaching and also guide how students engage with it. Recent years have seen a rise in instructor-facing generative AI tools such as syllabus generators, grading assistants, and assignment creators. However, studies on how instructors actually use these tools are still limited. Early surveys show mixed views: K–12 teachers report ambivalence about classroom AI
(
Center 2024
)
, while higher-education faculty show mostly neutral-to-positive attitudes and gradual adoption
(
Mamo et al. 2024
;
Cabellos et al. 2024
)
. These patterns align with broader models of technology adoption such as UTAUT
(
Venkatesh et al. 2003
)
, which emphasize factors like usefulness, effort, social influence, and available support. Yet the lack of detailed evidence leaves open how design choices can better meet instructors’ professional learning needs.

Research also shows that instructors’ AI skills, attitudes, and responsibilities strongly shape adoption. Some experiment actively, while others remain skeptical, a divide consistent with broader findings on algorithm aversion and appreciation
(
Dietvorst et al. 2015a
;
Dietvorst et al. 2015b
;
Logg et al. 2019
)
. Work in human–automation emphasizes trust as a central factor in deciding when to rely on AI, with reviews detailing what influences trust and how it can be maintained
(
Lee and See 2004
;
Hoff and Bashir 2015
)
. HCI studies add that complementary performance depends on users’ mental models and calibrated reliance, and that design patterns such as cognitive forcing functions can reduce overreliance
(
Bansal et al. 2021
;
Buçinca et al. 2021
)
. For instructors, this means that attitudes and prior experience will directly affect how they respond to different AI interaction styles.

Design guidelines in HCI emphasizes that human–AI systems should anticipate, support, and adapt to diverse users rather than assume one-size-fits-all interactions
(
Amershi et al. 2019
)
. In parallel, AI literacy work argues for scaffolding competencies so users can engage critically and productively with AI
(
Long and Magerko 2020
)
. Taken together, these studies show that instructors differ widely, some being early adopters and others skeptical, and highlight the importance of supporting them both as professional learners and as leaders in shaping students’ practices. This motivates our work on
TeaPT
, which explores how different conversational designs can adapt to instructor profiles and better support professional learning.

2.3.
Dialogic and External Cognition Processes

Our research is informed by two theoretical perspectives that have been widely applied in education and HCI research: dialogic learning and external cognition. We focus on these perspectives because they correspond to two prevalent forms of interaction in conversational approaches (short reflective questions and extended lists) and have been shown to support distinct learning processes. While other theories address related aspects of learning, such as motivation or self-regulation, dialogic and external cognition frameworks are particularly relevant here because they directly capture the tradeoff between reflection and efficiency that emerges in Human-LLM interaction.

Dialogic learning.
Socratic questioning builds on traditions of dialogic teaching, where interaction and reasoning are prioritized over the direct transmission of information. The ICAP framework shows that interactive and constructive activities, such as explaining concepts to others, generating examples, or answering guiding questions, lead to more durable learning than passive reception
(
Chi and Wylie 2014
)
. Dialogic teaching similarly highlights how questions that encourage learners to articulate and reason, for instance through debate, collaborative problem-solving, or reflective questioning, can deepen understanding and support transfer
(
Wegerif 2007
)
. HCI and CSCW studies have drawn on these perspectives to design systems such as collaborative learning platforms and tutoring interfaces that engage users in active reflection rather than immediate solution-finding, underscoring the broader relevance of dialogic principles in technology-mediated learning.

External cognition.
A complementary perspective emphasizes how cognition extends through external representations and artifacts. Clark and Chalmers’ theory of the extended mind
(
Clark and Chalmers 1998
)
and Hutchins’ work on distributed cognition
(
Hutchins 1995
)
argue that reasoning and memory are often supported by external tools, such as notes, diagrams, or shared displays, that make knowledge more accessible and actionable. In education, knowledge-building theory highlights the importance of elaborated external contributions. For example, learners building on each other’s written ideas in online forums for collective and individual growth
(
Scardamalia and Bereiter 2006
)
. These ideas have informed HCI research on systems such as visualization tools, collaborative document editors, and shared dashboards that scaffold decision-making and support collaborative knowledge construction, illustrating how externalized representations can broaden access to strategies while also raising concerns about information overload.

3.
Formative Study

3.1.
Process

We conducted a two-stage formative study to understand instructor-support strategies and how pedagogy expert perceive AI-driven instructional support. pedagogy expert

Activity 1: Expert Interviews
We first interviewed five pedagogy experts to understand current strategies used to support instructors and to surface opportunities and risks associated with AI. A pedagogy expert has deep expertise in guiding instructors, especially around AI and broader technology adoption. Each interview lasted approximately 60 minutes.
Activity 2: Design Session and Evaluation
We then conducted a design session with ten pedagogy experts, who reviewed a storyboard of chatbot features tailored to instructors with varying levels of AI literacy and differing attitudes toward AI. Each session lasted about 60 minutes. Experts also evaluated LLM-generated suggestions for 20 common instructional challenges curated from actual instructors. The goal of this rating task was to assess how well ChatGPT could support teachers and to identify areas for improvement. Suggestions were rated on a 1–10 scale (1 = worst, 10 = best) across four dimensions, informed by participants’ design session feedbacks: Relevance, Actionability, Alignment, and Reliability. Results showed mean scores of 7.05, 6.22, 6.76, and 6.76, respectively, indicating both promising performance and opportunities for further refinement. Interview and design session transcripts were thematically analyzed to identify recurring concerns and suggestions. Two researchers independently coded the transcripts and refined themes through discussion until consensus was reached, following Braun and Clarke’s guidelines for thematic analysis
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
)
.

3.2.
Design Insights

D1: Accommodating a Wide Range of Instructors’ AI Attitudes and Readiness
Pedagogy experts noted a wide spectrum of instructor readiness for AI adoption, ranging from early adopters to those highly cautious of technological change. To accommodate this spectrum, AI systems should incorporate clear mechanisms for transparency and control, such as options to track, manage, and delete data. Two pathways emerged as particularly salient: onboarding processes that establish initial preferences, and ongoing conversation logs that can be curated or removed through a dashboard. Instructors also expressed greater willingness to engage with AI when suggested changes to teaching practices were modest and incremental. Because AI literacy and attitudes are closely intertwined, and because instructors often seek support in areas beyond their expertise, sweeping shifts in pedagogy frequently generated resistance. Design efforts should therefore emphasize low-barrier entry points and gradual integration into existing instructional practices.

D2: Emphasizing Scaffolding Rather than Direct Solutions
Participants highlighted a tension between AI’s ability to provide quick answers and its usefulness for professional learning. While tools like ChatGPT were valued for fact checking, pedagogy experts noted that direct solutions offered little opportunity for reflection or growth. They preferred questions that encouraged deeper engagement with their own reasoning. To address this, AI systems should emphasize scaffolding strategies that foster multi-turn reflection. Contextualized questions, rather than final answers, allow instructors to articulate their thinking, test alternatives, and refine their practices. We explored this contrast in our prototype by comparing user experience when interacting with the standard ChatGPT API versus a fine-tuned model that was tuned to promote reflection and metacognitive engagement.

D3: Fostering Calibrated Trust in LLMs’ Pedagogical Use
Pedagogy experts emphasized that while LLMs are powerful, their pedagogical grounding and domain expertise remain uncertain. The aim is not to simply increase or decrease trust, but to align it with the system’s actual capabilities and limitations. In higher education, even courses with the same title often differ in emphasis and design, making pedagogy experts cautious about AI-generated domain knowledge. To support calibrated trust, systems should avoid presenting themselves as authoritative experts. Rather than producing course-specific content upfront, they should prompt instructors to draw on their own expertise while grounding suggestions in trusted, verifiable sources. In this way, AI functions as a proxy that connects instructors to reliable resources and opportunities for human collaboration, rather than as a substitute for their or all human expertise.

4.
TeaPT Prototype Design and Development

4.1.
Prototype Overview

We introduce TeaPT (Teaching Excellence and Pedagogical Transformation), an LLM-based chatbot designed to enhance teaching through a conversational interface. The TeaPT prototype was designed based on insights collected from the formative study. The prototype’s architecture includes three core components, as illustrated in Fig.
1
: a one-time onboarding process, a chatbot interface, and a user dashboard. TeaPT utilizes two approaches: a
narrative
version, which provides elaborated strategies and resources, and a
socratic
version, which offers concise, reflective questions to scaffold improvement rather than directly supplying solutions, aligning with the scaffolding needs identified in (D2).

The initial user interaction begins with the onboarding process, completed upon the user’s first login. This process collects foundational user data through four initial questions, with the option for users to skip questions they are uncomfortable answering. All data collected at this stage is stored in the user’s profile in the database. After onboarding, users are directed to the chatbot interface, where they are first guided through a walkthrough. The primary mode of interaction is a text box (F1), with all conversational history visible in the sidebar. Users can also associate a challenge with a specific course by selecting it via the plus button (F2).

As shown in Fig.
1
, the data collected during the onboarding is utilized by the conversational agent to provide a personalized experience. To address the challenge of initial user hesitancy (D1) and foster trust in the LLM, the agent is designed to progressively ask the user about their instructional background. This gradual data collection strategy minimizes the initial data burden on the user. The subsequently collected information is then stored in the database.

The final component is the user dashboard, detailed in Appendix
A
, which serves as a centralized hub for managing the TeaPT experience. From the dashboard, users can access their teaching resources, review demographic information that was extracted from their conversation with the prototype, and identify course-specific challenges. They can also schedule live consultations with human pedagogy experts, in order to calibrate trust and connect with other verifiable resources (D3). After completing each conversation with the conversational agent, the TeaPT prototype leverages ChatGPT to summarize the user’s primary challenge from the interaction and create a tailored resource to help them address it, these data are then saved to the dashboard for easier access.

4.2.
Chatbot Interface - Two Conversational Approaches

The
TeaPT
prototype incorporates two different conversational approaches designed to support professional learning: a
socratic
version and a
narrative
version, as shown in Fig.
2
. The prototype was deliberately scoped to focus on core instructional challenges, such as classroom management, student engagement, and assessment design, rather than broader institutional concerns (e.g., policy, academic integrity, or the governance of AI in education).

The
socratic
version is driven by a fine-tuned Llama model
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
and is designed to impart knowledge through a series of questions. This approach prioritizes depth of knowledge and encourages user reflection. The
socratic
Version is characterized by shorter, more frequent conversational turns, fostering a dynamic exchange and reinforcing concepts through iterative dialogue.

In contrast, the
narrative
version is powered by calls to ChatGPT and leverages stories and examples to deliver information. This version prioritizes the breath and context of a topic. The
narrative
version generates significantly longer and more detailed responses, which can be more challenging for users to fully process and comprehend.

4.2.1.
Socratic Version

We fine-tuned the
meta-llama/Llama-2-13b-chat-hf
model using a structured
socratic
dataset consisting of 406,183 chat examples (user–assistant pairs), along with a held-out validation set of 4,156 examples. Each example began with a standardized system prompt defining the assistant’s role as an expert teaching consultant (see Appendix
E.1
).

To support structured, step-by-step pedagogical reasoning, we introduced two special tokens,
<step>
and
</step>
, into the training data. These tokens enclosed a short descriptor that explicitly marked the assistant’s current instructional phase—for example,
<step>Current Step: Step 1</step>
. Before generating a response, the model was expected to recognize this step label and condition its output based on the corresponding guidance provided in the system prompt (see Appendix
E.1
). For instance, if the label was
<step>Current Step: Step 1</step>
, the model focused on identifying the user’s instructional challenge; if the label indicated Step 2, it aimed to explore reasons underlying that challenge. This explicit step-labeling mechanism enabled the model to scaffold its responses across dialogue turns in a structured and pedagogically coherent manner. During training, the model learned to associate each step with distinct instructional behaviors, internalizing both the structure and intent of Socratic teaching interactions (see Appendix
E.2
). As a result, the model demonstrated a clearer understanding of instructional flow and was able to guide users through complex teaching scenarios using expert-like scaffolding. Additional details about the training configuration—such as hardware setup, optimizer parameters, and learning schedule—are provided in Appendix
E.3
.

Our scaffold adapts a three-step problem-solving framework—identify the problem, explore reasons, and develop strategies—into an expert–instructor dialogue
(
Eberly Center for Teaching Excellence and Educational Innovation [n. d.]
)
. We added an initial step to greet users and gather background before moving to diagnosis and tailored strategies. The dialogues are synthetic and grounded in Small Teaching
(
Lang 2016
)
and Small Teaching Online
(
Darby and Lang 2019
)
, two of the most widely adopted faculty development resources in higher education in the US. Small Teaching has sold over 100,000 copies and is regularly used in faculty workshops and teaching centers, while Small Teaching Online has been adopted at over 200 institutions, particularly following the COVID-19 pandemic. To ensure validity, the generated synthetic data underwent three rounds of expert review (50 conversation per round), involving the books’ authors as well as additional pedagogy experts, confirming alignment with the pedagogical intent of the original texts. The detailed procedures for synthetic data generation and validation are beyond the scope of this paper and will be presented in a separate publication.

4.2.2.
Narrative Version

The
narrative
version was implemented through API calls to ChatGPT. The prompt, detailed in Appendix
F.2
, incorporated the user’s instructional background and instructed the model to respond as an experienced teaching expert, for example, “You are an experienced teaching expert guiding instructors to solve teaching challenges based on concepts and examples.” This framing consistently produced extended, information-rich responses, often structured as lists or step-by-step strategies.

4.2.3.
Teaching Resource Generation (Same in the two versions)

The TeaPT prototype also includes a resource generator feature, which is activated towards the end of a conversation. This feature provides a tailored resource, such as a rubric or a survey template, to help the instructor implement the strategies discussed. These resources are created through an API call to ChatGPT, utilizing the prompt detailed in Appendix
F.4
. When a resource is generated, users can click to open and view it within the resource window (F3). This allows users to easily review the content before saving it to their personalized dashboard, where all collected resources are accessible (F5).

4.3.
Pilot Testing

After the creation of the TeaPT prototype, the research team held a pilot study with six experienced pedagogy experts in July 2025, different from those that participated in the formative study. Each expert participated in a two to three-hour session, during which they evaluated the prototype’s usability and the quality of its responses for both the
socratic
version and
narrative
version. The experts were compensated 40 USD per hour for their time. Based on the feedback from the pilot study, the research team was able to make minor design modifications, refine the rating scale used in the later user study (see Appendix
B
), and adjust the API prompts to better align with the users’ needs.

5.
User Study and Results

5.1.
Study Design

The research team conducted a mixed-method Zoom study in August 2025. Each participant interacted with two versions of our tool for 10 minutes each: one with the core QA in the
narrative
version and the other in the
socratic
version, with only the core QA component differing, seeking suggestions on the same initial questions under both versions. The order of exposure was counterbalanced across participants. After interacting with each version, participants rated the version they had just used, followed by a 15-minute post-study interview. Two co-authors alternated in leading the study. We address the following research questions:
RQ1
: How do instructors perceive and engage with Socratic versus Narrative pedagogical chatbot?

RQ2
: How do instructor characteristics (e.g., AI attitudes, teaching experience) influence preferences and perceived learning across the two versions?

5.1.1.
Participants Recruitment and Demographics

We recruited 41 higher education instructors through social media and public posts. Recruitment was open and not selective, drawing individuals from a wide range of U.S. institutions, including research universities, community colleges, and liberal arts colleges. Demographic information was self-reported during sign-up and screening and further confirmed during the Zoom study sessions. The sample included 22 current faculty and 19 teaching assistants or staff. Participants varied in years of teaching experience: 1–2 years (n=13, 31.7%), 10+ years (n=11, 26.8%), 0–1 year (n=8, 19.5%), 3–5 years (n=5, 12.2%), and 5–10 years (n=4, 9.8%). Attitudes toward AI were nearly evenly divided, with 21 participants identifying as
Open and Optimistic
and 20 as
Curious but Cautious
. Although the sign-up form included an additional option (
Not for me
), no participant selected it, indicating that our sample only represents individuals who are at least somewhat open to AI. With informed consent obtained at the start, each session lasted approximately 45 minutes in total, and each participant received 35 USD as compensation. There is no evidence of association between AI attitude and Role using a Chi-square test. Similarly, there is no significant association between YOE (0-2/3+) and AI attitude (
Curious but Cautious
vs
Open and Optimistic
).

5.1.2.
Within-Subject Study Procedure Details and Analysis

Each participant interacted with both versions of the tool sequentially within a single session and completed two surveys right after they interacted with each version. The onboarding process was repeated for each version, with only the core question–answering component differing. This design allowed participants to more clearly understand and evaluate the differences between the two versions. Participants were told that there were two versions but were not informed which was which or how they differed. Participants were encouraged to think-aloud during the interactions with each version. Participants were asked to identify a current teaching challenge they wished to improve upon and interact with the chatbot to seek possible solutions. Interaction with each tool version was set to 10 minutes, based on pilot testing which indicated that this duration was sufficient to meaningfully discuss at least one teaching challenge. Participants could end the session earlier if they wished, were not required to use the entire time, and were free to raise multiple challenges as long as they completed discussion of at least one.

Conversation Log

5.1.3.
Post Study Interview

Towards the end of the study, participants completed a semi-structured interview designed to elicit reflections on their experience with the two chatbot versions and overall experience. Questions probed perceived differences between the
narrative
and
socratic
versions, reasons for preferences, and how participants’ backgrounds (e.g., teaching experience, AI attitudes) may have shaped their perceptions. To examine potential instructional impact, we also asked an application-oriented question:
“What did you learn today by talking to the two chatbots that you would change or use in your next teaching session, and how did you learn that?”
. We treated these responses as a proxy for learning outcomes, linking perceptions of the chatbots to potential instructional practices. Interview transcripts were analyzed thematically using an inductive coding approach, with two co-authors independently coding and iteratively reconciling differences to ensure agreement. For the application-oriented responses, the same coders further classified whether participants attributed their learning to the
narrative
version, the
socratic
version, both, or if attribution was unclear, allowing us to assess how each version contributed to perceived instructional takeaways.

The cross-tabulation (Table

Participants consistently contrasted the two chatbot versions along three key dimensions: conversational approach, breadth and actionability of suggestions, and efficiency of interaction, which explains the differences observed in the conversation log analysis and survey analysis.

Conversational Engagement.

The

In contrast, the

5.3.3.
Interview Insights on Background Impact and Preferences

Our interview analysis highlights three aspects of user profiles that shaped whether participants found the AI versions helpful for their learning: how they framed the role of AI (partner vs. tool), how their teaching experience shaped their information needs, and how their prior exposure to generative AI influenced expectations of interaction.

Viewing AI as Partner vs. Tool.

Participants varied in whether they approached the AI as a thought partner or as a source of information, which qualitatively seems to be intertwined with their AI attitude.

Open & Optimistic
instructors tended to treat the AI as a thought partner, appreciating the
socratic
version for its conversational questions that encouraged them to rethink their own practices. One participant explained,
“It was more conversational…we were actively thinking of ways instead of just information dumping”
(P5, 3–5 YOE).
Others noted how the lists made them feel they had more to work with:
“The narrative is a list and generally longer than the Socratic, so people feel like they know more when they see it all laid out.”
Another participant connected this directly to generational differences:
“The list enabled me to skim which is more like what I would want and typically do with ChatGPT, so I can think through it myself. It is a tool for me. Maybe for younger generations—it might feel more natural to talk to AI, like they are the natives…but I am not one of them”
(P30, 0–1 YOE). Overall, instructors’ AI attitudes shaped whether they engaged with the prototype as a reflective partner for dialogue or as a practical tool for quickly extracting ideas.

Interaction Expectations from Prior AI Use.

Participants’ history with GenAI also shaped their expectations. Those already familiar with ChatGPT anticipated
low-effort, option-rich interactions
and sometimes resisted interfaces that required more reflection. For instance, one instructor described being confused when the
Socratic
version slowed them down:
“I didn’t think it was fully understanding what I was communicating…I had to push it to get the answers I wanted”
(P5, 3–5 YOE). Others appreciated the familiar design that mirrored existing tools:
“These designs are quite similar to ChatGPT…they are easy to use”
(P6, 1–2 YOE). While this familiarity lowered the learning curve, it also limited openness to using AI for deeper engagement, reinforcing habits of scanning and skimming rather than sustained dialogue. One participant went further, critiquing how common LLM systems seem to exploit human tendencies by overproducing and creating a sense of knowing:
“If the chance of a good response is 50%, Narrative gives ten and maybe five are good; Socratic gives only two or three, maybe one is good. It feels easier to find something in the five, but that just makes people feel like they know more—it doesn’t mean ChatGPT is actually doing the job effectively or supporting long-term learning.”

Teaching Experience-Shaped Information and Learning Needs.

Teaching experience influenced what participants sought from the tool.

Junior instructors
(fewer than 3 YOE) favored the
socratic
version’s reflective coaching, describing it as similar to talking with a senior colleague. As one noted,
“The follow-up questions made me reflect on my own teaching practices”
(P5, 3–5 YOE).
By contrast,
senior instructors
leaned toward
narrative
’s comprehensive lists. They valued breadth, reminders of established practices, and occasional novel ideas:
“What’s this tool gonna teach me that I don’t already know?…I want it to give me reminders or new strategies quickly”
(P18, 5–10 YOE).
These differences illustrate how experience shapes the kinds of conversation approaches instructors find useful: reflection and coaching for novices, versus coverage and creativity for experts.

Summary (RQ2).
Instructor profiles shaped engagement and preferences. Junior and
Open & Optimistic
instructors leaned toward the
Socratic
version for reflective coaching, while senior and
Curious but Cautious
instructors preferred the
Narrative
version for actionable lists. Interviews explained these patterns, showing how AI attitude influenced whether it was treated as a partner or a tool, and how prior AI use shaped expectations for option-rich interactions.

6.
Discussion

Our study examined how conversational approaches (
Socratic
vs.
Narrative
) and instructor profiles (experience, AI attitude, prior use) shaped engagement with LLMs for pedagogical purposes, specifically to help instructors improve their teaching. Taken together, RQ1 and RQ2 highlight that differences between versions cannot be interpreted in isolation: how instructors responded to each approach depended strongly on their background and expectations.

6.1.
Conversational Approaches Channel Cognitive Processes

RQ1 showed that instructors contributed more turns and words in the
socratic
version, consistent with its design for dialogic reflection. In contrast, the
narrative
version produced longer system outputs that participants skimmed for actionable ideas. These patterns illustrate how AI systems channel users into different kinds of cognitive work. The
socratic
approach aligns with constructive engagement and dialogic learning
(
Chi and Wylie 2014
;
Wegerif 2007
)
, supporting deeper reasoning but requiring more effort. The
narrative
approach reflects externalized cognition
(
Hutchins 1995
;
Clark and Chalmers 1998
)
, offering breadth efficiently but sometimes creating information overload. This trade-off explains why many participants expressed ambivalence, simultaneously valuing
socratic
’s depth and
narrative
’s efficiency.

6.1.1.
Prior AI Experience and Effort Avoidance

RQ2 revealed that prior AI use strongly shaped expectations. Instructors accustomed to ChatGPT anticipated low-effort, option-rich interactions and sometimes resisted
socratic
prompts that slowed them down. Participants critiqued how
narrative
’s overproduction created an
illusion of knowing
, echoing concerns about automation bias and overreliance
(
Buçinca et al. 2021
)
. Attitudes further moderated these effects:
Open & Optimistic
instructors were more receptive to
socratic
version, while
Curious but Cautious
instructors treated AI as a tool for extracting options. Together, these findings suggest that prior AI experience fosters habits of skimming and selection, while attitudes determine whether instructors embrace or avoid reflective engagement. Without careful design, LLMs for pedagogical purposes risk reinforcing “lazy” usage patterns that privilege immediate output over deeper learning.

6.1.2.
Experience Shapes Engagement with AI

Our findings show that professional experience shaped how instructors engaged with AI: juniors often saw the
socratic
approach as mentorship, while seniors leaned on
narrative
lists for reminders or quick inspiration. Prior work on teacher learning similarly finds that novices seek structured guidance, whereas experts emphasize efficiency and reinforcement of established practices
(
Eraut 2000
;
Putnam and Borko 2000
)
. Recent studies on generative AI echo this divide: less experienced instructors use AI for learning, while more experienced ones treat it as a productivity tool or idea generator
(
Kaplan-Rakowski et al. 2023
;
Kasneci et al. 2023
)
. LLM for pedagogical purposes may therefore reproduce, or even widen, experience-based divides, supporting reflection for some while encouraging shortcuts for others. Recognizing these dynamics is essential, as instructor-facing AI is not a neutral productivity aid but a technology that can either close or exacerbate gaps in professional growth
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
.

6.2.
Design Implications: Flexible and Adaptive Modes

RQ2 showed clear differences by experience and attitude: junior and optimistic instructors benefited from
socratic
version, while senior and cautious instructors favored
narrative
lists. A one-size-fits-all design risks widening these divides. Interviews also revealed a key tension: what instructors
preferred
was not always what they learned the most from. Many chose
narrative
for efficiency yet credited
socratic
with deeper reflection. Preference, therefore, is not a reliable proxy for pedagogical value. Designing LLMs for pedagogical purposes requires balancing user choice with mechanisms that sustain reflection and growth. We argue that systems should combine adaptation with user control:

System adaptation:
The system can use simple profiling (e.g., teaching experience, AI attitude, prior use) to recommend approaches dynamically. Importantly, alternating approaches can also help the same user avoid overreliance. For example, moving from
narrative
lists to
socratic
prompts to sustain reflection rather than reinforcing passive skimming.

User choice:
Instructors should always have the ability to switch approaches themselves. Providing a visible toggle empowers users to align the interaction style with their immediate needs (e.g., quick ideas vs. reflective coaching).

Encouraging balance:
While novices may need more
socratic
scaffolding and experts may prefer
narrative
efficiency, adaptive systems should also nudge instructors outside their comfort zone, encouraging experts to reflect and novices to explore breadth, so that no group becomes locked into a single way of engaging with AI.

6.3.
Why Focus on Instructors in the Age of AI: New Responsibilities and Challenges

Instructor-facing AI deserves attention because instructors are not only adopters of technology but also role models whose practices shape student learning cultures. Their responsibilities now extend beyond preparing content to include evaluating AI tools, setting classroom norms for responsible use, and guiding learners in developing productive habits of engagement. This dual role creates new challenges: instructors must adapt their own workflows while also helping students navigate rapidly evolving technologies. Our findings show that instructors approach AI in varied ways: some treat it as a reflective partner, others as a tool for quick ideas, and these orientations cascade into how students engage with AI. If instructors normalize reliance on list-style outputs, students may replicate low-effort strategies; if instructors demonstrate dialogic reflection, students may develop deeper habits of reasoning. How instructors think about AI therefore shapes how it is integrated into their workflow and, by extension, how students structure their own learning processes. Studying instructor-facing AI is thus essential not only for improving professional development but also for ensuring that the adoption of AI contributes to equitable and sustainable learning practices. In this way, instructor-facing AI is a lever for shaping the future culture of lifelong learning at scale.

6.4.
Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, our evaluation focused on a single prototype with two conversational approaches. While this controlled comparison provided insight into how dialogic and list-based interactions shape engagement, it does not capture the full design space of LLMs for pedagogical purposes or the diversity of instructional contexts in which they might be applied. Second, our sample consisted of higher-education instructors who self-selected into the study. Although participants varied in teaching experience and AI attitudes, the results may not generalize to instructors in K–12 settings, informal learning environments, or institutions with different cultural and technological infrastructures. Third, the study captured short-term interactions in a controlled setting. Longitudinal work is needed to examine how instructor perceptions, habits, and pedagogical practices evolve as AI tools are integrated into daily teaching. Finally, we did not directly assess student outcomes. While instructors’ attitudes and practices clearly influence how students experience AI, further research is required to understand how these dynamics affect learning in classrooms. These limitations highlight opportunities for future work to broaden the scope of evaluation and explore the long-term role of LLM for pedagogical purposes across educational settings.

7.
Conclusion

This paper introduced
TeaPT
, a pedagogical chatbot with two conversational approaches: a
Socratic
approach that scaffolds dialogic reflection and a
Narrative
approach that offers elaborated suggestions. In a mixed-method study with 41 higher-education instructors, we found that these approaches channel distinct cognitive pathways: Socratic questioning fostered more turns and reflective engagement, while Narrative lists provided breadth and immediacy but risked overload.
RQ2 showed that instructor profiles mediated these effects. Junior and AI-optimistic instructors leaned toward Socratic dialogue, treating the AI as a thought partner, whereas senior and AI-cautious instructors preferred Narrative breadth, treating the AI as a tool. Interviews revealed a misalignment: what instructors preferred was not always what they credited with learning.
These findings underscore that pedagogical AI is not a neutral productivity aid but a mediator of professional growth, conditioned by prior AI experience, attitudes, and expertise. Designing instructor-facing AI therefore requires supporting multiple interaction pathways, balancing efficiency with reflection, and offering adaptive flexibility that prevents overreliance on either depth or breadth. Situating conversational design in both dialogic and external cognition theories contributes to responsible AI systems that not only deliver solutions but also foster sustained learning and professional development.

STRUCTURED PROBLEM-SOLVING STEPS:

0. Greeting & Teaching Background

- Acknowledge instructor’s context and teaching environment.

- Ask clarifying questions (e.g., experience, methods, class size, course level).

1. Identify the Problem

- Instructor states full, specific challenge using first-person (“I”, “my”).

- Expert helps clarify with probing, non-repetitive questions.

2. Explore Reasons

- Expert helps uncover causes across student needs, course structure, methods, and external factors.

- Expert can ask if other challenges have occurred that could be related.

3. Develop Strategies

- Expert suggests practical solutions and guides implementation planning.

- Do not use general knowledge or unsupported information.

- Avoid incomplete sentences, trailing thoughts, or ellipses (“…”).

- Avoid repeating phrasing from earlier turns; vary your language.

- Problem-clarifying: “Could you describe a specific example…?”, “How does this affect…?”, “What have you noticed about when this occurs?”

- Reason-exploring: “What do you think might be causing this?”, “How do your students respond…?”, “What factors might be contributing…?”

- Strategy-developing: “What resources…?”, “How might you implement…?”, “What would success look like…?”

\in

E.3.
Training Configuration Details

Training was conducted on 4
\times
NVIDIA A100 GPUs (each with 80GB memory) using
DeepSpeed ZeRO-3
optimization. To improve memory efficiency, we adopted
bfloat16
precision and enabled activation checkpointing to reduce GPU memory usage during backpropagation. The total training duration was approximately 11.99 days.

We employed a per-device training batch size of 12 and an evaluation batch size of 8, without applying gradient accumulation. The model was fine-tuned using the
AdamW
optimizer, configured with the following hyperparameters:

•

Learning rate:
2\times 10^{-5}

•

Weight decay: 0.01

•

Gradient clipping: 1.0

A cosine decay learning rate schedule with restarts was utilized, with 761 warmup steps to stabilize training in its early phase. The model was trained for a total of 3 epochs, resulting in 25,386 optimization steps across the full training dataset.

Evaluation was performed every 500 steps on a held-out validation set. The best-performing checkpoint—identified by the lowest validation loss—was observed at step 16,000. This checkpoint was used for all downstream evaluation and deployment.

Appendix F
TeaPT Prototype Details

When deploying different large language models, we designed prompts that were largely consistent across ChatGPT and LLaMA, while making some adjustments to reflect differences in training. Both models are instruction-tuned and capable of following natural language inputs, but ChatGPT’s extensive reinforcement learning from human feedback makes it more robust to conversational prompts
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
. In contrast, LLaMA tends to benefit from slightly more explicit, stepwise instructions and structured formatting to ensure stable outputs
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
.

In practice, the Narrative version invited open-ended guidance, while the Socratic version required explicit step tracking on the back end (not displayed to the user) and probing questions. This design aimed to ensure that any observed differences in outcomes could be attributed to the presence of conversational scaffolding rather than other prompt variations or differences in pedagogical knowledge encoded by the models. We selected the ChatGPT version to meet this requirement and to approximate a comparable parameter size based on limited, publicly disclosed information. ChatGPT has never provided an official public announcement regarding its model size, so any comparison is necessarily based on incomplete or potentially inaccurate assumptions about closed-source commercial models. The purpose of this work is not to compare models directly, but rather to examine how different conversational styles shape user learning and engagement.

Multiple versions of the ChatGPT model were provided to experts in the pilot study. In a series of blind evaluations, the experts compared the versions over several rounds until no clear preference emerged. The final two were then selected, with the ultimate choice made based on the lower cost. A pilot study with pedagogy experts confirmed that, apart from the addition of explicit stepwise scaffolding in the Socratic version, the two prompts were designed to be equivalent in all other respects. Both versions consistently framed the assistant as an experienced teaching expert, included identical instructor and course context fields, and avoided vague terminology or unsupported claims. We also experimented with prompting ChatGPT to adopt a Socratic style directly; however, its responses did not consistently reach the expected level of structured scaffolding or probing depth.

The conversational scaffold follows the structured problem-solving framework developed by Carnegie Mellon University’s Eberly Center for Teaching Excellence and Educational Innovation. This framework consists of three iterative steps: (1) Identify the Problem, (2) Explore Reasons, and (3) Develop Strategies
(
Eberly Center for Teaching Excellence and Educational Innovation [n. d.]
)
. Our design adapts these stages into an expert–instructor dialogue format, beginning with contextual grounding and clarifying questions, moving toward collaborative diagnosis of underlying causes, and concluding with actionable strategy development tailored to the instructor’s teaching environment. We further added a step (0) that greets the users and ask for their background.
