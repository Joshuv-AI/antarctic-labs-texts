“I Wondered if I Would Live or Not”: A Qualitative Study of Women’s Experiences of Traumatic Childbirth
Authors: Samar M Alotaibi, Asrar S Almutairi, Rayhanah R Almutairi, Anwar N AlKhunaizi, Areej G Al-Otaibi, Hatice Bulut, Turki S Alqurashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo gain an in-depth understanding of Saudi women’s experiences of traumatic childbirth that occurred at any point within the previous five years.MethodsA qualitative study using Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis, an idiographic approach supporting in-depth exploration of lived experience. Fifteen women who had experienced traumatic childbirth occurring at any point within the previous five years were recruited through social media and publicly accessible locations frequented by women. Semi-structured interviews were conducted between January and March 2026. The data were analysed following the steps of Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis to explore women’s lived experiences in depth.ResultsThree experiential themes were identified: Experiencing distressing childbirth; living with the emotional aftermath; and the lasting impact of birth trauma on motherhood. These findings highlight the substantial impact of traumatic birth on women’s lives and suggest enduring psychological and relational consequences, particularly when care is perceived as dismissive.ConclusionThere is a need for maternity care to be delivered in a respectful, woman-centred, and supportive manner to reduce the occurrence and impact of traumatic birth experiences. Insight into women’s birth experiences can inform the development of interventions to prevent birth trauma and reduce its consequences within the Saudi healthcare system.

Introduction

Birth is often considered a positive and rewarding life transition. The experience of childbirth entails profound and complex physical and psychological changes, and is widely regarded as one of life’s most significant events.1 In many cases, this process involves severe pain and may result in physical and psychological trauma.2 Ongoing emotional distress after a traumatic birth experience, often called birth trauma, can lead to post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) and, in some cases, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).3 Traumatic childbirth has been understood from a woman-centred perspective as childbirth-related events or interactions that cause overwhelming distress and have short- or long-term negative effects on women’s health and wellbeing.4 The estimated global prevalence of women who experience childbirth as traumatic is 9 to 44%.2

Women may experience childbirth as difficult or distressing for a variety of reasons, including fear of childbirth, earlier exposure to traumatic experiences, insufficient support, and health complications or barriers to care during pregnancy and birth, all of which can increase risks to maternal and foetal health.5,7,8 Childbirth is more likely to be perceived as traumatic when women experience a lack of control, inadequate support, or labour-related complications.6 Feeling excluded from decision-making or lacking a sense of control during labour or birth can contribute to traumatic experiences.9,10 Additionally, adverse interactions with healthcare providers, such as mistreatment and obstetric violence, are associated with a heightened risk of birth trauma and birth-related PTSD.11

Individuals who experience a traumatic event may develop feelings of fear and helplessness, and many find it difficult to recover or move forward afterwards. Evidence from a recent systematic review indicates a consistent association between traumatic childbirth experiences and postpartum psychiatric disorders, including depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, and anxiety.12 There can be a range of consequences resulting from psychological birth trauma, including physical and mental health effects on mothers, mother-infant relationships, intimate partnerships, and potentially health professionals, as well.13,14 Evidence suggests that traumatic birth experiences are associated with breastfeeding difficulties, influenced by psychological, hormonal, and contextual factors.15 Therefore, greater attention to this issue is crucial to safeguard maternal wellbeing and societal health.

A recent review found that women in the Middle East were generally dissatisfied with the care they received during childbirth, with many describing their experiences as distressing and traumatic.16 According to a recent cross-sectional study in Saudi Arabia, 21.4% of women reported a negative childbirth experience during their last birth, and these women commonly reported poor postpartum mental health outcomes, including anxiety, sadness, and frequent crying; additionally, 24.1% described their last birth as more psychologically or physically traumatic than expected.17 A study of 358 Saudi women found widespread instances of non-dignified maternity care, including physical mistreatment, lack of consent, and breaches of confidentiality, highlighting the need for further qualitative research to explore women’s lived experiences and the psychological impact of such care.18

A recent qualitative study in Saudi Arabia investigated women’s traumatic birth experiences within the first 18 months postpartum. It revealed that birth trauma, experiences of obstetric mistreatment, and an inability to experience positive emotions negatively affected women’s psychological wellbeing.19 Although the previous qualitative study explored women’s experiences of birth trauma within the first 18 months postpartum, there remains a limited understanding of how women describe and make sense of these experiences over a longer time span. Therefore, in response to the limited qualitative evidence on birth trauma in Saudi Arabia, this study seeks to gain an in-depth understanding of Saudi women’s experiences of traumatic childbirth within the past five years and identify implications for clinical care and postpartum support.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

A qualitative design was used, adopting an Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) approach.20 IPA is well-suited to exploring individuals’ experiences and the ways they express them. Qualitative research captures rich and detailed experiences.

Recruitment, Setting and Participants

Participants were recruited through social media and flyers distributed in publicly accessible community locations frequented by women, including beauty salons and shopping centres in Riyadh. This approach was chosen to enable women to participate in a private and accessible manner. Purposive sampling was used to recruit eligible Saudi women aged 18 years or older who had given birth in a Saudi healthcare facility at any point within the previous five years and self-identified their birth experience as traumatic. No PTSD screening tool or minimum symptom threshold was applied, as the study aimed to explore women’s lived experiences rather than diagnose PTSD or assess symptom severity. IPA is an idiographic qualitative approach that supports detailed exploration of lived experience using small, purposive samples.

Interested women completed an online survey that provided details about the study and indicated their willingness to participate. Those who wished to take part provided their contact details, including a WhatsApp number, enabling the research team to follow up with them. The first author contacted potential participants via WhatsApp, explained the aims of the study, and provided information about participation. No prior relationship existed between the interviewer and participants before the study. Consent forms were shared and completed digitally via WhatsApp before interviews were arranged. Completed consent forms were stored securely in password-protected files, separately from anonymised interview transcripts, and were accessible only to the research team. A telephone interview appointment was scheduled based on the participant’s preference. Interviews were conducted between January 2026 and March 2026. Recruitment continued until rich and detailed accounts were obtained to support in-depth idiographic analysis, consistent with IPA guidance that emphasises depth of engagement with individual cases.20

Data Collection Process

The first author conducted semi-structured telephone interviews to gain in-depth insight into women’s experiences of childbirth. Telephone interviews were chosen to support accessibility, privacy, and participant comfort when discussing a sensitive experience. The semi-structured format allowed flexibility while providing space for participants to discuss their experiences in their own words, consistent with IPA’s focus on lived experience. The Interview Guide

Interviews were conducted sensitively to reduce the risk of distress. Before each interview, participants were reminded that they could pause, decline to answer any question, or withdraw at any time without giving a reason. The first author’s prior experience conducting interviews on sensitive women’s health topics in Saudi Arabia supported the interviewing process. The first author also shared gender, language, and cultural background with participants, which helped foster trust and open communication during the interviews.

Field notes were recorded during and after each interview to document tone, pauses, emotional expressions, contextual details shared by participants, and the researcher’s reflections. Interviews lasted 28–52 minutes and were digitally audio-recorded with participants’ permission, transcribed verbatim, and anonymised. Audio recordings, consent forms, transcripts, and field notes were stored securely in password-protected files, with identifying information kept separately from anonymised transcripts and access limited to the research team. Four potential participants did not proceed to interview participation due to incorrect contact details, non-response to follow-up communication, or personal time commitments and responsibilities. No repeat interviews were conducted, and transcripts were not returned to participants.

Data Analysis

All interviews were transcribed by the first author, anonymised, and securely managed, facilitating close engagement with the data throughout analysis. The data were analysed following the IPA guide.20 Data analysis was conducted manually using Microsoft Word files and analytic tables to organise exploratory notes, experiential statements, and developing themes. Audio recordings and transcripts were stored in password-protected files accessible only to the research team. Identifying information was removed during transcription, and anonymised participant codes were used throughout analysis.

The first author became fully immersed in the dataset through repeated, detailed readings of each transcript and repeated listening to the interview recordings. Next, exploratory notes were made. These notes were then condensed into brief experiential statements representing the central meanings expressed by participants. Subsequently, related statements were examined and organised into personal experiential themes that captured the essence of each account. Each case was analysed separately before moving to the next to preserve the idiographic focus of IPA and allow new themes to emerge. In the final stage, patterns and shared meanings across all cases were integrated to generate group experiential themes. All authors contributed to the analytic process. ASA, RRA, and ANA were involved in reviewing developing codes, interpretations, emerging themes, and supporting quotations, while the wider author team reviewed and discussed the developing analysis and contributed to refinement of the final thematic structure. Differences in interpretation, where they arose, were resolved through discussion until agreement was reached. No qualitative data analysis software or artificial intelligence tools were used in data analysis or theme development.

Ethical Considerations

Ethics approval was obtained from Princess Nourah bint Abdulrahman University Ethics Committee (approval number 26–0016). Ethical approval was obtained on 14 January 2026. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent before taking part in the study, which included permission for anonymized responses and direct quotations to be used in publications. Participants received complete information regarding the study procedures and were invited to take part on a voluntary basis. They were reminded that they could withdraw from the study at any stage without penalty or adverse consequences. They were also informed that all responses would remain confidential and would be accessible only to the research team involved in the study. All stages of the study were carried out in accordance with ethical principles governing research involving human participants.

Rigour and Reflexivity

The research was conducted in accordance with the trustworthiness criteria established by Guba and Lincoln (1994), which consist of the principles of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. The research team included seven authors, six of whom hold PhDs in nursing with specialised expertise in obstetrics, gynaecology, and mental health. One author had a background in social care with expertise in family health. All authors had prior experience in qualitative research using an IPA approach.

To reduce the risk of confirmation bias, the first author used open-ended questions, avoided leading prompts, maintained reflexive notes, and discussed developing interpretations with co-authors. Participants self-referred to the study, participation was voluntary, and the first author had no clinical or supervisory relationship with participants, which helped minimise potential power-related concerns. Field notes captured participants’ tone, pauses, emotional expressions, contextual details and researcher reflections. Reflexivity was maintained throughout the study. Reflexive notes were kept to minimise the influence of pre-existing assumptions on data interpretation. Credibility was enhanced through repeated reading of transcripts, detailed field notes, and peer debriefing with the research team. Dependability was supported through clear documentation of recruitment, interview, transcription, and analytic decisions. Confirmability was supported through reflexive notes, team discussions, repeated engagement with participants’ accounts, and the presentation of data excerpts in the Findings section to show how interpretations were grounded in the data.

Results

Fifteen women who met the inclusion criteria were included. The women’s ages ranged from 25 to 45 years (mean age 34.27). The majority had a bachelor’s degree (n = 12), while two had completed high school and one held a master’s degree. The time since their birth experience ranged from 4 months to 5 years. Theme 1: Experiencing Distressing Childbirth

Some participants described emotional shock during childbirth, arising from pregnancy and birth complications or poor care experiences, which contributed to their later understanding of the birth as traumatic.

Facing the Shock and Distress of Complicated Childbirth

This subtheme was evident across all participants’ accounts, with childbirth pain described as extremely intense and unfamiliar, almost like facing death. When birth occurred without an epidural, participants described feeling emotionally overwhelmed, and some found it difficult to overcome the aftermath of the experience. When the baby was born, they put him on my chest, but I said, “Take him away! Theme 2: Living with the Emotional Aftermath

Participants had complex feelings about caring for their baby, along with the expectation to engage in social gatherings postpartum despite physical and emotional fatigue from the birth experience. Many participants experienced recurrent memories of their difficult birth, which negatively affected their psychological wellbeing.

Fear and Concern for the Baby

This subtheme was reflected in the accounts of four participants, who described intense fear and uncertainty about whether their baby would survive during and immediately after childbirth. For some women, this distress was intensified by separation from their newborn when the baby was admitted to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit NICU, making the early postpartum period particularly traumatic. The baby spent three whole months in the nursery, and this was a second shock to me because there was a possibility that my child might live or not…I cried a lot and my sleep was interrupted before my child was discharged from the hospital. because I was lost, stressed, and unwell. This subtheme was reflected in the accounts of five participants, who described persistent emotional distress following traumatic childbirth. Some participants reported emotional irritability and flashbacks, with the hospital remaining a powerful reminder and trigger of their difficult and emotionally painful birth experiences. Theme 3: The Lasting Impact of Birth Trauma on Motherhood

Birth trauma had a lasting impact on many participants, influencing their relationship with their children as well as their decisions about future pregnancy.

Impact on the Mother–Infant Relationship

Accounts from four participants contributed to this subtheme, describing how emotional distress following traumatic birth affected their relationships with their infants. Some participants reported that feelings of irritability, depression, or fear made breastfeeding difficult, and for some this contributed to discontinuing breastfeeding. My friends encouraged me. I continued for a while and then stopped because I didn’t see results, because mental health is very important. P1

Fear of Future Pregnancy

This subtheme was reflected in the accounts of six participants, who expressed concerns about future pregnancy following birth trauma. For some, this fear extended to routine gynaecological or reproductive health procedures. I am afraid of getting pregnant again. The doctor asked me if I wanted to have an IUD inserted as a contraceptive method, and I told her I don’t want anything near the uterus and that I would use any other method. P1

I am afraid of getting pregnant again. The doctor asked me if I wanted to have an IUD inserted as a contraceptive method, and I told her I don’t want anything near the uterus and that I would use any other method. P1

Some participants described how the trauma from their previous birth shaped their reaction to subsequent pregnancy, leaving them feeling emotionally terrified and overwhelmed. Discussion

An interpretative phenomenological research design was utilised to gain an in-depth understanding of Saudi women’s experiences of traumatic childbirth within the sociocultural and healthcare context of Saudi Arabia. The analysis identified three primary themes and eight subthemes: experiencing distressing childbirth, living with the emotional aftermath, and the lasting impact of birth trauma on motherhood. The results highlight the significance of birth trauma among Saudi women.

Participants reported emotional shock and distress in their childbirth experience, which they attributed to pregnancy and birth complications or to poor care experiences. These findings are consistent with those of Alghamdi et al,24 who observed that many mothers expressed feelings of shock due to the sudden and unexpected nature of premature birth and traumatic childbirth experiences. Participants in the current study also described a lack of responsiveness from healthcare professionals during labour, which intensified the challenges of the birth experience. This lack of attention left participants feeling dismissed, unheard, and as though their pain was disregarded. These results align with previous research on women’s negative labour and birth experiences, including studies by Henriksen et al25 and Reed et al,26 which emphasise the importance of continuous, supportive, and compassionate care from healthcare professionals during labour and birth.

Accounts of being laughed at, ignored, or threatened with withdrawal of care suggest that poor care experiences extended beyond limited support to include intimidating and disrespectful communication, which may undermine women’s dignity, autonomy, and sense of safety during childbirth. These poor care experiences reflect broader concerns about respectful maternity care in Saudi Arabia, where women in government hospitals have reported lower levels of respect than those in private hospitals, and 21.8% reported physical abuse during childbirth.27 Similarly, Vedeler et al28 identified insufficient attention to emotional needs as a key factor contributing to women’s reports of negative childbirth experiences. A qualitative case study of a birth trauma survivor highlighted the importance of trauma-informed, person-centred perinatal care, particularly therapeutic relationships, respectful communication, and shared decision-making.29 Therefore, trauma-informed care training could be integrated into midwives’, nurses’, and obstetricians’ continuing education, addressing informed consent processes, respectful communication during labour, and immediate recognition of psychological signs of birth trauma.

The findings underscore the complex emotions participants experienced while caring for their infants. Many participants reported recurrent memories of challenging birth experiences, which adversely affected their psychological wellbeing. Participants exhibited heightened emotional sensitivity and irritability after difficult births, indicating the emotional strain associated with feeling isolated while managing new responsibilities. These emotions may be further intensified for first-time mothers. These findings are consistent with previous research. A cross-sectional study by Çapik and Durmaz30 identified that postpartum post-traumatic stress is a significant concern, as it can negatively affect women’s welfare, mother-infant attachment, and child development. Previous research has shown a link between traumatic birth and symptoms such as emotional irritability and flashbacks. AlKhunaizi et al19 found that women’s traumatic childbirth experiences and distressing news were associated with fear, loneliness, emotional exhaustion, postpartum depression, and lasting physical and psychological impacts. These findings highlight the need to minimise birth trauma in the first place through respectful, woman-centred maternity care, clear communication, informed consent, and emotional support during labour and birth.

The episiotomy-related finding has clinical significance, as one participant linked her traumatic birth experience to later concern about genital change and a desire for future cosmetic surgery. Although idiographic, this finding highlights how birth trauma may affect women’s sense of bodily integrity and postnatal body image, underscoring the need for sensitive assessment, clear communication about perineal healing, and appropriate referral when distress persists. Accordingly, Saudi health policymakers could consider integrating routine screening for postpartum psychological distress into standard postnatal care pathways, particularly for women who have experienced preterm birth, unplanned caesarean birth, procedures without informed consent, or ongoing distress related to perineal trauma or bodily changes.

Birth trauma exerted a sustained influence on many participants, affecting both their relationships with their children and their decisions regarding future pregnancies. Numerous participants reported apprehension about planning additional pregnancies due to previous traumatic experiences. In certain instances, this apprehension extended to the avoidance of reproductive procedures. These findings are consistent with previous research showing that traumatic or complicated birth can contribute to anxiety about subsequent pregnancies, avoidance of future pregnancy, and decisions not to have more children. This may also develop into severe fear of childbirth, which is often rooted in previous negative birth experiences and was reflected in participants’ accounts in the present study.31–35

The findings show that women experienced feelings of detachment from their newborns as a result of their birth trauma. The current study’s findings regarding disrupted mother–infant relationships are supported by previous qualitative work showing that traumatic birth may lead to emotional distancing and impaired bonding with the infant.32,36 Building on this, when traumatic experiences do occur, trauma-informed postpartum assessment and early support are needed to support maternal mental health, breastfeeding, and the early mother–infant relationship. These findings have important implications for nursing practice, particularly in relation to trauma-informed communication, respectful maternity care, early recognition of psychological distress, and support for mother–infant bonding after difficult birth experiences. Although distress was central across participants’ accounts, women’s experiences were not uniform. Some participants also described moments of reassurance, support, or relief, suggesting that traumatic childbirth experiences could coexist with mixed or partially positive aspects of care.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

This exploratory study sought to understand Saudi women’s experiences of birth trauma in the past five years, thereby contributing to the limited existing literature on Saudi women’s accounts of traumatic birth.

The use of self-identification to define traumatic childbirth may have resulted in variation in the severity and nature of participants’ experiences. In addition, self-selection into a study specifically focused on traumatic childbirth means that the findings do not capture the full spectrum of postpartum experiences and should not be considered representative of childbirth experiences among Saudi women more broadly. The use of telephone interviews may have limited observation of non-verbal cues; however, this approach supported privacy and participant comfort when discussing sensitive childbirth experiences. As participants were recruited through social media, they were likely to be digitally savvy or more likely to share their experiences, potentially reducing the diversity of perspectives represented. However, due to the sensitive nature of birth trauma, recruitment via social media may have enhanced accessibility and participant comfort. Additionally, it may have reduced the fear that disclosing negative experiences would negatively impact future interactions with healthcare providers.

Further longitudinal qualitative research is necessary to examine the progression of birth trauma beyond the five-year time frame explored in this study. In addition, comparative studies across public and private healthcare settings would further clarify how structural influences shape women’s experiences of birth trauma.

Conclusion

The phenomenon of birth trauma among Saudi women may be multifaceted and persistent, extending beyond the actual moment of giving birth. The experiences of the fifteen participants in this study suggest that traumatic childbirth can unfold across three interrelated dimensions: the initial emotional distress associated with the childbirth experience itself, the psychological burden of its emotional aftermath, and the longer-term disruption to maternal identity and future reproductive decisions. Although the study specifically recruited women who self-identified their childbirth as traumatic and was not intended to represent childbirth experiences among Saudi women more broadly, their accounts provide important insight into how birth trauma may continue to shape women’s wellbeing beyond the immediate postpartum period.

The findings suggest that both clinical complications and poor care experiences within the healthcare system shaped how women experienced birth trauma in Saudi Arabia, particularly in relation to communication with healthcare providers, whether women’s preferences were considered, and whether they felt acknowledged or ignored at their most vulnerable. Addressing these issues aligns with current Saudi health priorities that emphasise women’s health, safe and supportive healthcare environments, culturally responsive services, and improved health outcomes. Within the context of Saudi Vision 2030, addressing these issues offers an important opportunity to strengthen trauma-informed maternity care, postnatal support, and provider training focused on respectful and woman-centred care.
