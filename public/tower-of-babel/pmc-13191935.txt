Effect of 16-gauge needle as needle-over-needle guidance introducer in single-operator real-time ultrasound-guided spinal anesthesia: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Qingqing Qian, En yao Qi, Hongyue He, Rui Ding, Qilian Tan, Hongguang Bao, Tao Shan, Yongjun Li, Hongwei Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWe aimed to evaluate the effect of a 16-gauge needle as an introducer in single-operator real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anesthesia.MethodsOne hundred and two parturients undergoing cesarean section were randomized to undergo real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anesthesia either with (group T) or without (group C) a 16-gauge needle as an introducer. The primary outcome was the number of needle passes required for a successful dural puncture. Secondary outcomes included the number of attempts, first pass/attempt success rate, procedure time, parturients’ pain, puncture depth, periprocedural complications, and the quality of sonographic images.ResultsThe median number of needle passes and attempts were significantly lower in group T than in group C [1 (1–2) vs. 2 (1–3), median difference (95% CI): 1 (0–1), P < 0.001; 1 (1–1) vs. 1 (1–1), median difference (95% CI): 0 (0–0), P = 0.042]. The first pass/attempt success rates in group T were significantly higher than in group C (70.6% vs. 35.3%, 100% vs. 88.2%; both, P < 0.001). The total procedural time was shorter in group T [108 (94–130) vs. 123 (100–163) seconds, median difference (95% CI): 15 (3–33), P < 0.016]. No intergroup differences of pain, puncture depth, periprocedural complications and quality of sonographic images were seen.ConclusionsThe application of a 16-gauge needle as an introducer showed improved efficacy in terms of the number of needle passes/attempts, first pass/attempt success rates, and procedural time in real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anesthesia.Trial registrationThe study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration date February 27, 2023 registration number ChiCTR2300068702).Keywords: Anesthetic techniques, Spinal anesthesia, Subarachnoid, Ultrasonography, Paramedian transverse approach

Introduction

Spinal anesthesia is the preferred method of anesthesia in cesarean section for minimal trauma, rapid onset, precise anesthesia effect, and optimal muscle relaxation [1]. Preprocedural ultrasound assistance has been widely used, especially in obese parturients, due to the accurate identification of the subarachnoid space [2, 3]. The paramedian transverse approach is commonly used for ultrasound-assisted spinal anesthesia, achieving a high success rate on the first pass and attempt [4, 5]. Previous studies have shown that ultrasound-assisted spinal anesthesia improves the successful first attempts and needle pass, and reduce the time for a successful dural puncture compared to landmark-palpation methods [6–8]. It is believed that the real-time ultrasound guided approach may further improve upon the limitations of the ultrasound-assisted pre-puncture techniques by visualizing the needle tip and needle trajectory, and allowing for appropriate adjustments during the procedure [9, 10]. However, the real-time ultrasound-guided spinal anesthesia technique is still considered difficult and complex with a single-operator. Previous studies have concluded that it is less effective than ultrasound assistance [11]. Promoting this technology in routine clinical practice remains challenging due to the following technical difficulties. Firstly, the subarachnoid space is relatively deep, making needle visualization challenging. Secondly, the intraspinal structures are surrounded by bone, which can hinder the ultrasound beam and needle penetration. Finally, unlike traditional two-handed puncture techniques, the ultrasound-guided method requires one hand to hold the probe while manipulating the needle with the other, turning the procedure into a one-hand task.

The 25-gauge pencil-point spinal needle, known for its slender and flexible design, offers a higher success rate, reduced tissue damage, a lower risk of injury and complications, and faster postoperative recovery. It is widely used in subarachnoid anesthesia [12, 13]. Nevertheless, its flexibility makes repeated redirection of the needle difficult with only one hand, potentially leading to failure of real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anesthesia. This may result in decreased patient comfort, an increased incidence of spinal hematoma, postdural puncture headache and trauma to neural structures. Improving the efficacy of real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anesthesia is a pending issue we, as clinician, should address.

An introducer needle is often used to stabilize the placement of a small-gauge needle during spinal anesthesia. Commercially available kits that include 25-gauge needles (or smaller) usually include a 16- or 18-gauge short (1–1.5 inch) needle that is placed into the interspinous space before placement of the spinal needle through it in an attempt to guide the smaller-gauge needle, which is likely to bend during insertion through soft tissue. Whether a 16-gauge needle as an introducer would improve the real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anesthesia procedure remains unclear.

Therefore, our study aimed to compare the mean number of needle passes reqired for real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anesthesia with and without a 16-gauge needle used as an introducer in partureints undergoing cesarean section. We further investigated the first-pass success rate, procedure time, the pain score, adverse reactions and complications.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethics

This was a single-center, randomized, prospective study conducted at Nanjing Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital. Our study was approved by the ethics committee of the Nanjing Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital on December 3, 2021 (approval number: 2021KY-092-01). The study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration date: February 27, 2023; registration number: ChiCTR2300068702, http://www.chictr.org.cn/) prior to parturient enrollment. Written informed consent was obtained from all parturients before the trial. Our study adheres to the CONSORT guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Parturients and randomization

We recruited parturients aged 18–45 years, BMI 18–28 kg.m− 2, and ASA I or II. The inclusion criteria were as follows: pregnancy ≥ 37 weeks, receiving elective cesarean section. Exclusion criteria were (1) history of lumbar trauma or lumbar surgery; (2) infection at the puncture site; (3) coagulation dysfunction; (4) severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction or hypovolemia; (5) know allergy to local anesthetics.

All parturients were randomized into two groups using a computer-generated sequence of random numbers, which were securely enclosed in opaque sealed envelopes. The allocation of parturients into either the group T or group C was performed by the same anesthesiologist, who was not involved in the study. In group T, parturients underwent real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anesthesia using a 16-gauge needle as an introducer. In group C, the introducer was not applied. All parturients were blinded to group allocation.

Study intervention

The spinal anesthesia was performed by the same experienced anesthesiologist who had performed single-operator real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anesthesia with paramedian transverse approach over 30 times.

After the parturients arriving in the operating room, routine monitoring was implemented, including non-invasive arterial pressure, ECG, heart rate, and SpO2. Venous access was then established and no sedative was used before the procedure. All parturients are routinely given 250–500 ml crystalloid solution for volume expansion. All parturients were placed in the left lateral position. We used a low-frequency convex array probe (3–5 MHz, Philips, Netherlands). The full aseptic technique was implemented, including a gown, gloves, mask, sterile ultrasound gel, and sterile transducer cable sheath. We used the paramedian transverse approach to peform single-operator real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anesthesia in both two groups [14]. Firstly, a paramedian sagittal oblique view was used to identify the subarachnoid space at L3-L4. Then, the probe was rotated 90 degrees to obtain the transverse view. The transverse process, anterior complex, and posterior complex were identified in this imaging. By carefully moving the transducer away from the midline, the articular process was repositioned to the lateral side of the image. The skin was infiltrated with 2% lidocaine prior to the procedure. In group T, a 16-gauge needle was inserted into subcutaneous tissue as an introducer using an in-plane technique, with the needle tip directed towards the intervertebral space. The 16-gauge introducer was advanced only into the subcutaneous tissue or erector spinae muscle (approximately 2–3 cm depth), remaining proximal to the ligamentum flavum. Subsequently, a 25-gauge pencil-point subarachnoid anesthesia needle was inserted through the hub of the 16-gauge needle. The 25-gauge pencil-point needle was advanced stepwise under real-time ultrasound guidance until reaching the interlaminar space. Whenever the tip contacted bone or the trajectory deviated, the needle was retracted into the sheath and the insertion angle was corrected before re-advancing. When the pencil-point needle tip successfully penetrated the ligamentum flavum and the dura mater into the subarachnoid space, the needle tip might be visualized as a bright spot within the cerebrospinal fluid on ultrasonography (Fig. 1). During the procedure, tissue movements or the needle shaft may help identify the needle trajectory. When the needle tip (a bright spot) was not visible, a feeling of loss to resistance could be helpful to identify dura penetration. In group C, the parturients underwent single-operator real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anesthesia directly using a 25-gauge pencil-point subarachnoid puncture needle, following the aforementioned procedure without an introducer. In this group, the 25-gauge needle trajectory passed through the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and ligamentum flavum to reach the subarachnoid space. Upon successful dural puncture, which was confirmed by the free flow of cerebrospinal fluid, hyperbaric ropivacaine 0.5% (15–18 mg) was injected into the subarachnoid space according to previous literature [15]. After procedure, all parturients were placed in a 30-degree inclined position on the left side to avoid the supine position syndrome. Hypotension during the operation is handled according to the standard protocol. The same attending anesthesiologist evaluated the level of anesthesia and determined whether the parturient was ready for surgery.

During the procedure, each parturient was allowed a maximum of three skin punctures in each vertebral space and each skin puncture accommodated up to five needle passes (any forward needle advancements, meaning needle withdrawal and redirections without removing needle from skin). The procedure was considered a failure if the total permitted number of needling attempts were exhausted without obtaining CSF, and in such cases, the operator could consider alternative techniques (landmark-guided technique, pre-puncture ultrasound assistance, or general anesthesia).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the number of needle passes required for a successful dural puncture (defined as the number of any forward needle advancements, i.e., the number of needle withdrawal and redirections without removing the needle from the skin, including the first pass). Secondary outcomes were as follows, (1) the number of attempts (defined as the number of individual skin punctures); (2) success rate of first pass (success rate of dural puncture with a single needle pass), (3) success rate of first attempt (success rate of dural puncture with a single skin puncture with one or more needle passes); (4) procedure time (the time taken from the initial needle insertion of the 16-gauge introducer needle in Group T or the 25-gauge spinal needle in Group C to successful dural puncture); (5) pain score during the procedure (NRS rated on a scale of 0–10; 0, no pain; 10, the most severe pain conceivable); (6) puncture depth (the vertical distance from the skin surface to the subarachnoid space). (7) periprocedural complications (paresthesia, radicular pain or hematoma); (8) quality of sonographic images (defined as follows, good, both posterior and anterior complexes were visible; intermediate, the posterior or anterior complex was visible; poor, neither complex was visible). All outcomes were recorded by a separate, independent anesthesiologist who was not involved in performing the procedure.

Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

The calculation of the sample size was based on the results of our pre-experiment and in accordance with previous literature [4]. Based on data from our institution, the mean number of needle passes of the single-operator real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anesthesia was assumed to be 3.1 (1.7). An absolute difference of > 1 in the mean number of needle passes between the two groups was considered clinically relevant. With an alpha error of 0.05 and a power of 0.8, 46 patients were required per group. We randomized 51 patients to each group to allow for 10% dropout.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 22.0. Continuous variables were tested for normal distribution using Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean (SD) and were analyzed using Student’s t-test to determine inter-group differences. Nonparametric variables were presented as median(interquartile range (IQR)), and analyzed between the groups using the Mann-Whitney U test. For categorical variables, the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was applied as appropriate. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effect of a 16-gauge needle as an introducer in single-operator real-time ultrasound-guided spinal anesthesia using a 25-gauge pencil-point needle. Our results showed that the number of needle passes and attempts requried for successful dural puncture decreased significantly with the use of a 16-gauge needle as an introducer.

Although real-time ultrasound-guided spinal anesthesia is an attractive technique, its clinical application is less common than other ultrasound-guided nerve block techniques [16]. This is partly because the landmark-based blind technique, which has a high success rate [17], remains the standard approach. Moreover, the aseptic preparation of the ultrasound probe is time-consuming [18]. The complex ultrasonic anatomy of the lumbar spine and the bony encasement of the intrathecal space also pose challenges to real-time ultrasound guidance.

However, real-time ultrasound guidance may offer additional advantages by visualizing the needle tip and the needle trajectory and can allow for adjustments for any change in patient position during the procedure [19, 20]. Trials of single-operator real-time ultrasound-guided spinal anaesthesia are uncommon compared with pre-puncture ultrasound. This could be atrributed to technical difficulties and complexities. The paramedian approach is preferred for single-operator real-time ultrasound-guided spinal anesthesia compared to the palpation method, as it result in a reduced number of attempts, an improved success rate of a single needle pass, and a shorter procedural time [20, 21]. Although previous studies comparing the paramedian transverse and sagittal approach for single-operator real-time ultrasound-guided neuraxial puncture in elderly patients showed comparable results in almost all perspectives, the paramedian approach could achieve better needle-beam alignment and allow the spinal puncture to be performed with the dominant hand and an ideal direction of needle advancement, regardless of whether patients were in lateral or sitting position [11]. Thus, we used the paramedian transverse routinely in our center.

The direct puncture technique using a 25-gauge pencil-point spinal needle is highly skill-dependent and often challenging to perform successfully [22]. This difficulty may be attributed to the needle’s fine and flexible shaft, which is prone to deviation during insertion. The use of higher-gauge needle is often associated with a tendency to deflect and bend, which can complicate the procedure. Additionally, the use of these needles raises concerns about potential needle breakage due to their metallic weakness. Long, narrow-gauge needles are more susceptible to bending and fracturing [23]. A systematic review has identified that obese patients with difficult anatomical landmarks and the use of smaller gauge needles (25-gauge or smaller) are at a higher risk of needle fracture [24]. Additionally, calcification of the ligamentum flavum and even partial calcification of the interspinous ligament can impede the needle’s passage, making it difficult to enter the sac. Meanwhile, the holding point must be moved closer to the needle tip to prevent needle deviation, which may increase the risk of infection. To minimize the risk of needle breakage, employing a needle-over-needle introducer is advisable [23]. Standard introducer needles are typically 18–20 gauge and 3.2–3.8 cm long. The larger gauge introducer facilitates the insertion of non-cutting needles and is particularly beneficial when using fine needles of 25 gauge or smaller [25]. In our study, number of needle passes in the needle sheath group was significantly lower than those in the control group, which may be attributed to the directional guiding efficacy of the introducer, reducing repeated adjustments caused by needle tip deviation.

The innovation of our study resides in the integration of a 16-gauge needle as an introducer and the single-operator real-time ultrasound technology. The 16-gauge needle may compensate for the deviation in puncture direction cauesd by the lack of rigidity in 25-gauge needles. This approach allows adjustment of the puncture direction via the sheath tube under visual observation with a single hand, thereby reducing the injury caused by repeated punctures. Previous studies have used a size 8 needle (outer diameter 0.81 mm, approximately 20–21 gauge) needle as an introducer for single-shot subarachnoid anesthesia and showed significantly reduced incidence of postoperative lumbar pain in elderly patients compared to the use of 18-gauge epidural needle [26]. This improvement is likely due to the 16-gauge needle causing less soft tissue damage, providing more precise needle direction, and requiring fewer adjustments during puncture.

Our study used ultrasound transverse scanning technology to guide the puncture by visualizing the anterior and posterior complex. Successful spinal anesthesia on the first attempt is highly likely when a clear ultrasound image captures both the posterior and anterior complexes on the same plane. If only the posterior complex is visible, the procedure remains viable. However, if only the anterior complex is visualized, the approach is still possible but may require more time to access the subarachnoid space. If neither complex can be clearly visualized, the probability of failure has significantly increased. The quality of the sonographic images were evaluated in both groups. Our results demonstrated superior image quality in the parturients. This indicate the feasibility of real-time ultrasound-guidance spinal anesthesia in cesarean section.

Our study also acknowledges certain limitations. Firstly, the inclusion criterion for BMI was restricted to ≤ 28 kg/m², which may potentially underestimate the challenges associated with puncture procedures in obese patients. Since the ultrasound-guided approach showed greater advantages rather than the anatomical landmark technique, our results in non-obese parturients may actually represent a conservative estimate of the technique’s efficacy. Secondly, we did not evaluate the effect of a 16-gauge needle as an introducer in elderly patients. Thirdly, we did not assess the efficacy of using a 16-gauge needle in the paramedian saggittal approach. Finally, all real-time ultrasound guided subarachnoid anesthesia procedures were performed by a single experienced anesthesiologist not blinded to group allocation. This could introduce bias into our trial. Future research should address these limitations and validate the generalizability of this technique.

In conclusion, single-operator real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anaesthesia via a paramedian transverse approach was performed more efficiently when a 16-gauge introducer needle was used. Compared with a 25-gauge pencil-point spinal needle alone, this technique significantly reduced the number of needle passes and attempts required for successful dural puncture and shortened the overall procedure time. The application of 16-gauge needle as an introducer is a promising technique in real-time ultrasound-guided subarachnoid anaesthesia.
