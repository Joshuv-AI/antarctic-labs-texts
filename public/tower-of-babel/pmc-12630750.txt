Machine learning and bifurcation analysis in a discrete predator-prey model with neem-induced mortality
Authors: Tayyaba Mehmood, Muhammad Rafaqat, Salman Saleem, Feyisa Edosa Merga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study develops a discrete-time predator-prey model for guava pest management using the piecewise constant argument (PCA) scheme. The model incorporates logistic prey growth, neem-induced mortality, and predator crowding. Analytical and numerical results establish conditions for flip and Neimark-Sacker bifurcations, supported by bifurcation diagrams, Lyapunov exponents. Ecologically, small neem-induced mortality (d) destabilizes prey-predator coexistence, whereas larger d restores stability. The intervention frequency further shapes dynamics, with moderate values maintaining stability and large values inducing oscillations. As a proof-of-concept, machine learning (random forest and decision tree classifiers) was explored to efficiently approximate the analytically derived stability regions. Both classifiers successfully replicated the stability map, with Random Forest providing smoother boundaries and higher accuracy, demonstrating the potential of ML as a computational surrogate for more complex models. Parameter importance analysis revealed that prey dynamics are mainly driven by prey-related parameters (r, a, b), while predator persistence is strongly influenced by conversion efficiency (c) and natural mortality (s). These findings highlight that balanced neem application, appropriate timing of interventions, and conservation of natural enemies are key for sustainable guava pest control.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Ecology, Ecology, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The origins of mathematical ecology date back to the pioneering contributions of Lotka1 and Volterra2, who first described predator-prey interactions in a mathematical framework. Their work was later extended by May3, who revealed that even simple models can generate complex and chaotic dynamics, and by Murray4, who provided a comprehensive treatment of population biology using nonlinear models. Such foundations motivated the study of ecological interactions through both continuous and discrete frameworks5.

Discrete-time models, in particular, are especially relevant because many insect populations reproduce in seasonal or generational cycles and because management interventions such as pesticide spraying or harvesting typically occur periodically. Moreover, discretization can induce complex behaviors such as flip and Neimark-Sacker bifurcations6–8. These bifurcations are not only of mathematical interest but also reflect ecological thresholds where stable coexistence breaks down into oscillations or chaos, directly influencing pest management outcomes9.

The guava fruit borer (Conogethes punctiferalis) is a serious pest of guava (Psidium guajava), causing significant yield losses across Asia and Africa. Conventional synthetic pesticides, while effective, have generated ecological and health concerns, driving the adoption of sustainable approaches such as neem-based bio-insecticides (Azadirachta indica). Neem has been shown to suppress larval survival and reduce pest populations while being eco-friendly10. At the same time, natural predators such as parasitoid wasps and beetles play a crucial role in regulating pest populations, making predator-prey interactions central to integrated pest management11,12.

Recent studies have highlighted how crowding effects in prey or predator populations, delay, or nonlinear responses can stabilize or destabilize dynamics and even suppress chaos11,13,14. Similarly, external mortality through pesticides or environmental factors can shift system stability and trigger bifurcations15. However, neem-induced mortality has rarely been incorporated explicitly into discrete predator-prey models, despite its practical importance for guava pest management.

A further gap is the lack of integration between bifurcation analysis and machine learning. While bifurcation theory provides rigorous conditions for qualitative shifts in dynamics6,16, machine learning can act as a computational surrogate to classify stability regions efficiently8,9. Recent ecological studies have also emphasized the importance of tipping points and critical transitions17,18, highlighting the need for predictive tools that can detect early warning signals.

Motivated by these ecological and methodological gaps, we propose and analyze a discrete-time predator-prey model tailored for guava pest management. The model incorporates logistic prey growth, neem-induced mortality, predator crowding, and is discretized using the piecewise constant argument scheme14. We perform fixed point stability and bifurcation analysis (flip and Neimark–Sacker) and complement it with machine learning (Random Forest and Decision Tree classifier) for stability classification. This dual approach provides both theoretical insights and computational tools for sustainable guava pest control.

The model is initially derived in continuous time and then discretized using the piecewise constant argument scheme for numerical and bifurcation analysis.

Limitations and practical applicability

While the proposed discrete-time predator-prey model provides valuable theoretical and ecological insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the model parameters have not yet been validated using field or experimental data from guava orchards. The absence of empirical calibration means that our results should be interpreted qualitatively rather than quantitatively. Second, the current framework considers only two species, a pest and its predator, with neem-induced mortality, whereas real guava ecosystems involve multiple interacting pests, predators, and parasitoids. Future extensions of the model should incorporate such multispecies interactions to better reflect ecological complexity. Third, although we established positivity and boundedness of the system, high sensitivity to parameter values suggests that small uncertainties could lead to qualitatively different dynamics. This highlights the importance of parameter estimation and sensitivity analysis in practical applications.

From a methodological perspective, the machine learning approach employed in this study was intentionally designed as a proof-of-concept. The classifier was trained on data labeled using the analytical Jacobian test, making the process methodologically circular. Therefore, while it efficiently replicates the known stability map, it does not, in this specific implementation, yield new biological or mathematical insights. Its primary value is computational, demonstrating a framework that could be applied to more complex systems or empirical data where analytical solutions are unavailable. Furthermore, while the bifurcation analysis provides deep theoretical insight, the model parameters have not been calibrated with field data from guava orchards. Consequently, the results should be interpreted as qualitative trends and theoretical guidance rather than quantitative predictions. The model’s sensitivity to parameters also implies that practical application would require robust parameter estimation to avoid unexpected dynamical regimes.

Despite these limitations, the study offers important implications for guava pest management. The results suggest that:

By linking bifurcation thresholds to ecological processes, the study contributes to understanding how neem intensity and intervention timing jointly affect guava pest dynamics. These insights can inform more sustainable practices, balancing chemical and biological control to maintain orchard health.

Bifurcation

In discrete-time systems, a bifurcation marks a qualitative shift in dynamics as parameters vary, typically when Jacobian eigenvalues cross the unit circle. This can lead to cycles, quasiperiodicity, or chaos. Ecologically, bifurcations signal thresholds where population stability breaks down, making their detection crucial for effective pest management.

Flip bifurcation

A flip bifurcation occurs when a real eigenvalue crosses , causing a fixed point to lose stability and a period-2 cycle to emerge. This can cascade into higher-order cycles and chaos. Ecologically, it marks the onset of alternating pest-population cycles, often triggered by aggressive interventions or poor timing, complicating control efforts and requiring more adaptive strategies.

Neimark-sacker bifurcation

A Neimark-Sacker bifurcation occurs when a complex eigenvalue pair crosses the unit circle, causing a stable fixed point to transition into a quasiperiodic orbit. This leads to smooth, non-repeating oscillations. Ecologically, it reflects persistent population cycles triggered by small parameter changes, making pest control less predictable and requiring careful tuning of intervention strategies.

Machine learning approach

Although analytical bifurcation analysis provides exact conditions for stability, exhaustively scanning the high-dimensional parameter space of ecological models can be computationally intensive. For more complex future extensions where analytical solutions may be intractable, machine learning (ML) offers a promising complementary tool. In this study, we employ ML not as a replacement for rigorous mathematics, but as an exploratory proof of concept to demonstrate its potential for efficiently approximating stability landscapes.

Our approach was intentionally designed as a methodological demonstration. We generated training data by sampling the parameter space for neem-induced mortality (d) and intervention frequency . For each pair, we numerically computed the system’s fixed point and used the Jacobian eigenvalue test, the analytical ground truth, to classify the local stability as a sink, source, saddle, or non-hyperbolic point. A Random Forest and Decision Tree classifier was then trained on this labeled dataset.

After hyperparameter tuning, the classifier reproduced the analytical stability map with high accuracy , successfully approximating the boundaries between different dynamical regimes. This demonstrates that ML can act as a computationally efficient surrogate model. It is crucial to emphasize the exploratory and circular nature of this specific exercise, the classifier learned to replicate the known analytical rules it was trained on. Therefore, in this context, it does not yield new biological insights but validates the computational method.

Conclusion

This study developed and analyzed a discrete-time predator-prey model for guava pest management, incorporating logistic prey growth, neem-induced mortality, and predator crowding. Bifurcation analysis confirmed the existence of flip and Neimark-Sacker bifurcations, while numerical simulations revealed transitions from stability to oscillations and chaos under varying parameter values.

Ecologically, the results show that small neem-induced mortality (d) destabilizes prey and predator populations, whereas increasing d restores stability by suppressing oscillations. The intervention frequency () also plays a critical role: moderate values promote stability, while larger ones can trigger bifurcations and complex dynamics. These findings underline the need for balanced neem application and carefully tuned intervention schedules.

The machine learning component served as an exploratory proof of concept. It confirmed that Random Forests can efficiently approximate complex stability landscapes derived from bifurcation theory. While this approach is circular in the context of this study, as it learned to replicate existing analytical rules, it successfully demonstrates a methodology that could be invaluable for future work. This includes analyzing high-dimensional models that are analytically intractable or classifying dynamics directly from empirical time-series data where stability criteria are unknown.

Overall, the study provides both theoretical and practical insights, suggesting that sustainable pest management requires moderate neem use, appropriate timing of interventions, and conservation of natural enemies.
