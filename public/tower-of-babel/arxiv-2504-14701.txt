Connecting Parameter Magnitudes and Hessian Eigenspaces at Scale using Sketched Methods
Andres Fernandez a.fernandez@uni-tuebingen.de, Frank Schneider f.schneider@uni-tuebingen.de, Maren Mahsereci maren.mahsereci@uni-tuebingen.de, Philipp Hennig philipp.hennig@uni-tuebingen.de

Abstract
Recently, it has been observed that when training a deep neural net with SGD, the majority of the loss landscape’s curvature quickly concentrates in a tiny top eigenspace of the loss Hessian, which remains largely stable thereafter. Independently, it has been shown that successful magnitude pruning masks for deep neural nets emerge early in training and remain stable thereafter. In this work, we study these two phenomena jointly and show that they are connected: We develop a methodology to measure the similarity between arbitrary parameter masks and Hessian eigenspaces via Grassmannian metrics. We identify ​ o ​ v ​ e ​ r ​ l ​ a ​ p \emph{overlap} as the most useful such metric due to its interpretability and stability. To compute ​ o ​ v ​ e ​ r ​ l ​ a ​ p \emph{overlap} , we develop a matrix-free algorithm based on sketched SVDs that allows us to compute over 1000 1000 Hessian eigenpairs for nets with over 10 10 M parameters—an unprecedented scale by several orders of magnitude. Our experiments reveal an ​ o ​ v ​ e ​ r ​ l ​ a ​ p \emph{overlap} between magnitude parameter masks and top Hessian eigenspaces consistently higher than chance-level, and that this effect gets accentuated for larger network sizes. This result indicates that top Hessian eigenvectors tend to be concentrated around larger parameters , or equivalently, that larger parameters tend to align with directions of larger loss curvature . Our work provides a methodology to approximate and analyze deep learning Hessians at scale, as well as a novel insight on the structure of their eigenspace.

Hessians, Parameters and Early Crystallization

We consider a supervised classification/regression setup with train set
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{train}}\!\coloneqq\!\{({\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}
of labeled data
({\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\!\in\!{\mathbb{X}}\!\times\!{\mathbb{Y}}
, from an unknown data-generating distribution
P
.
The neural net
f_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}})\!:\!{\mathbb{X}}\!\to\!{\mathbb{Y}}
maps inputs
{\bm{x}}
to predictions
\hat{{\bm{y}}}
via parameters
{\bm{\theta}}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
A loss function
\ell\!:\!{\mathbb{Y}}\!\times\!{\mathbb{Y}}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
penalizes differences between prediction
\hat{{\bm{y}}}
and true label
{\bm{y}}
.
The goal is to minimize the inaccessible
risk

L_{P}({\bm{\theta}})\!\coloneqq\!\int\ell(f_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{y}})dP
via the proxy
empirical risk

L_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{train}}}({\bm{\theta}})\!\coloneqq\!\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ell(f_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{n}),{\bm{y}}_{n})
.
For large
N
, we approximate
L_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{train}}}({\bm{\theta}})
using
mini-batches

{\mathcal{B}}\!\mathrel{\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\vbox to0.07967pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\text{iid}$}\vss}}}\!{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{train}}
of
B\!\ll\!N
samples.
For
f
,
\ell
twice differentiable, we use the
gradient

{\bm{g}}({\bm{\theta}})\!\coloneqq\!\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}L({\bm{\theta}})\!\in\!{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
and the
Hessian

{\bm{H}}({\bm{\theta}})\!\coloneqq\!\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{2}L({\bm{\theta}})\!\in\!{\mathbb{R}}^{D\!\times\!D}
.
With
{\bm{g}},{\bm{H}}
we refer to
any
loss gradient or Hessian.
To emphasize the data domain, we use a subindex, e.g.
{\bm{H}}_{{\mathcal{B}}}
for the Hessian of the mini-batch loss.

2.1
The Hessian in Deep Learning

The Hessian plays a prominent role in
DL
applications. A useful characterization of
{\bm{H}}
is its eigendecomposition
{\bm{H}}\!=\!{\bm{U}}{\bm{\Lambda}}{\bm{U}}^{\top}\!=\!\sum_{i=1}^{D}\lambda_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top}
. Here,
{\bm{U}}
is orthogonal, with
eigenvectors

{\bm{u}}_{i}
, and
{\bm{\Lambda}}
is diagonal and real-valued with (ordered)
eigenvalues

\lvert\lambda_{1}\rvert\!\geq\!\makebox[10.00002pt][c]{.\hfil.\hfil.}\!\geq\!\lvert\lambda_{D}\rvert
.
We call
{\bm{U}}^{(k)}\!\coloneqq\!\{{\bm{u}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
the
top-
k
eigenbasis
of
{\bm{H}}
, and
\Span({\bm{U}}^{(k)})
the
top-
k
eigenspace
.
The top-
k
eigendecomposition
{\bm{H}}^{(k)}\!=\!\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top}
minimizes
\lVert{\bm{H}}-{\bm{H}}^{(k)}\rVert
for all unitarily invariant norms
(
Golub & Van Loan 2013
, Th. 2.4.8)
.

Recent literature has extensively investigated the Hessian
spectrum
of neural nets, revealing that the eigenvalues are typically clustered into two parts: (1) The
bulk
of eigenvalues with near-zero magnitude and (2) a few
top
eigenvalues with significantly larger magnitude
(
Sagun et al. 2018
;
Papyan 2019
, e.g.)
.
Thus,
{\bm{H}}
can be well-approximated by its top eigenpairs.
For the Hessian
eigenspace
,
Li et al. 2018a
showed that projecting the whole space onto a few random, fixed dimensions still allows
SGD
(
SGD
) to perform competitively—provided enough dimensions are given—leading to the idea of an intrinsic dimensionality of problems.

Gur-Ari et al. 2018
observed that this restriction to a lower-dimensional, fixed subspace seems to happen spontaneously anyway: After a brief initial training period, the gradient predominately lies within a small subspace spanned by the few top Hessian eigenvectors and this space changes little over the remaining training process.
However, these phenomena might rely on optimizer and model choices
(
Li et al. 2018a
;
Ghorbani et al. 2019
)
.

One fundamental issue greatly limiting the use of
{\bm{H}}
for
DL
is its prohibitively large size, with
D^{2}
entries. Consequently, most scalable methods are
matrix-free
, relying on
HVP
to compute linear maps
{\bm{w}}\!=\!{\bm{H}}\!{\bm{v}}
in just
\mathcal{O}(D)
memory and time
(
Pearlmutter 1994
)
.
Examples are the computation of spectral densities
(
Yao et al. 2020
;
Papyan 2018
)
or top-
k
eigendecompositions
(
Gur-Ari et al. 2018
;
Dangel et al. 2022
)
.
To make Hessian properties more accessible, specialized
DL
libraries have been developed recently
(
Dangel et al. 2020
;
Yao et al. 2020
;
Elsayed & Mahmood 2022
;
Dangel et al. 2025
, e.g.)
, but efficiently accessing large portions of the Hessian remains a major challenge (see
Section

5
).

2.2
Parameter Masks and Early Crystallization

Binary parameter masks
{\bm{m}}_{k}\!\in\!{\mathbb{B}}^{D}
, with
{\mathbb{B}}\!=\!\{0,1\}
, consisting of
k
ones and the rest zeros, can be used to define subsets of parameters from a given model.
A mask is
k
-sparse if it has
k
non-zero elements.
We measure mask sparsity using the ratio
\rho\!\vcentcolon=\!\nicefrac{{k}}{{D}}
.
For non-binary vectors
{\bm{v}}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{D}
, we instead measure whether a small subset of indices
\iota
contains a large proportion of the total norm of the vector.
When
\iota
is known, this can be directly expressed as the ratio:
\kappa({\bm{v}})\!\vcentcolon=\!\nicefrac{{\lVert{\bm{v}}_{\iota}\rVert_{2}^{2}}}{{\lVert{\bm{v}}\rVert_{2}^{2}}}

(
Hurley & Rickard 2009
, see)
.
A popular choice for
{\bm{m}}_{k}
is to take the
k
-largest parameters by magnitude: it is computationally cheap, and has been shown to be effective for neural net pruning
(
Han et al. 2015
)
, leading to smaller models that can often achieve competitive performances with
\rho\!\in\![1\%,10\%]

(
Gale et al. 2019
;
Blalock et al. 2020
;
Hoefler et al. 2021
, e.g.)
.

In this work, we do not perform any pruning, but rather focus on a key feature of parameter magnitude masks. Not only can they lead to competitive performance when used for pruning and be found very early in training
(
Frankle & Carbin 2019
;
Frankle et al. 2020
)
but they also
stabilize soon afterwards
:

You et al. 2020
compared Hamming distances between periodically extracted pruning masks and found they stop changing early in training, aligning with the loss of information plasticity reported in
Achille et al. 2019
.
This
early crystallization
bears a striking parallel with observations made for the top Hessian subspace, and motivates our investigation: Are these two phenomena connected? How would one measure this connection? What would such a connection tell us about the parameters and the Hessian?

4
Measuring Subspace Similarity via Grassmannian Metrics

We set out now to quantify the similarity between a
k
-sparse parameter mask and the top-
k
Hessian eigenspace.
Specifically, we are only interested in the similarity of their spans, since the
spaces
are the ones reported to undergo early crystallization.
To connect parameter spaces to loss curvature, one may consider randomly perturbing the parameters, and empirically measuring the impact on the loss. This turns out to be problematic due to the non-PSD nature of
{\bm{H}}
(see
Section

A.1
).
Various workarounds to obtain nonnegative measurements can lead to promising results, but they also entail tradeoffs (see
Section

A.2
).
Instead, Grassmannian metrics provide a natural and theoretically grounded way of achieving our goal, given that
k
-sparse parameter masks and the top-
k
Hessian eigenspace can both be cast as elements of the same Stiefel manifold (
Section

3
).
We review popular Grassmannian metrics in
Section

4.1
, and analyze them in more depth in
Section

4.2
, finding that the
\emph{overlap}
metric, highlighted here, is both interpretable and stable:

\displaystyle\emph{overlap}({\bm{Q}}_{1},\!{\bm{Q}}_{2})\!=\!\frac{1}{k}\lVert{\bm{Q}}_{1}^{\top}\!{\bm{Q}}_{2}\rVert_{F}^{2}\!=\!\frac{1}{k}\lVert\cos({\bm{\sigma}})\rVert_{F}^{2}\!\in\![0,\!1]\,.

(23)

Intuitively, we can see how a zero
\emph{overlap}
between
{\bm{Q}}_{i}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{j}
indicates that the spans of both matrices are orthogonal to each other, and an
\emph{overlap}
of 1 indicates that both spaces are identical.
We can also interpret
\emph{overlap}
as the rotation-invariant cosine similarity between
{\bm{Q}}_{1}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{2}
, as induced by the standard Euclidean metric on matrices.

4.1
Grassmann Manifolds and their Metrics

Grassmann manifolds are extensively studied
(
Witten 1988
;
Absil et al. 2004
;
Bendokat et al. 2020
, e.g.)
and have recently been applied to
DL

(
Gur-Ari et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Dangel et al. 2022
, e.g.)
.
A Grassmann manifold
{\mathcal{G}}_{k,D}
is the set of all
k
-dimensional subspaces of a given
D
-dimensional Euclidean space.
Two orthogonal matrices
{\bm{Q}}_{1},\!{\bm{Q}}_{2}\!\in\!{\mathbb{O}}^{D\!\times\!k}
map to the same element
\mathfrak{g}\!\in\!{\mathcal{G}}_{k,D}
if and only if their column span is identical.
Thus, the subset of all matrices in
{\mathbb{O}}^{D\!\times\!k}
that map to
\mathfrak{g}_{i}\!=\!\Span({\bm{Q}}_{i})
forms an
equivalence class

{\mathcal{S}}^{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i}\!\coloneqq\!\{{\bm{Q}}_{j}\!\!:\penalty\ \!\!{\bm{Q}}_{j}{\bm{Z}}_{j}\!=\!{\bm{Q}}_{i},\,{\bm{Z}}_{j}^{\top}{\bm{Z}}_{j}\!=\!{\bm{I}}_{k}\}

(
Edelman et al. 1998
, e.g.)
.

The
geodesics
(i.e. shortest paths) between two elements in
{\mathcal{G}}_{k,D}
are available in closed-form and can be characterized in terms of the
principal angles

{\bm{\sigma}}\!\in\![0,\frac{\pi}{2}]^{k}
, i.e. the “amount of rotation” required to transition from one space to another.
The principal angles between two matrices in
{\mathbb{O}}^{D\!\times\!k}
can be obtained via an
SVD
of their product, satisfying the invariance to changes within
{\mathcal{S}}^{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i}
(see
Section

A.3
).
Based on this, there are several
Grassmannian metrics
capturing different notions of distance between subspaces
(
Edelman et al. 1998
;
Qiu et al. 2005
, e.g.)
.
In the following, we highlight a few.
We abbreviate
\dist_{\raisebox{-0.6pt}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\ast$}}}(\mathfrak{g}_{i},\mathfrak{g}_{j})\!=\!f({\bm{\sigma}}_{i\shortrightarrow j})
as
\dist_{\raisebox{-0.6pt}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\ast$}}}\!=\!f({\bm{\sigma}})
, where
\ast
here parametrizes any unitarily invariant norm:

a)

Geodesic distance:
This is the
arc length
of the geodesic between the respective spaces in
\mathcal{G}
, defined as
\dist_{g}\!=\!\lVert{\bm{\sigma}}\rVert_{2}\!\in\![0,\penalty\ \frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{k}]
.

b)

Chordal norm:

\dist_{c,\raisebox{-0.6pt}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\ast$}}}
, obtained by minimizing
\lVert{\bm{Q}}_{i}{\bm{Z}}_{i}-{\bm{Q}}_{j}{\bm{Z}}_{j}\rVert_{\raisebox{-0.6pt}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\ast$}}}
over orthogonal matrices
({\bm{Z}}_{1},{\bm{Z}}_{2})
(for that reason it is also called
Hausdorff distance
). The
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{F}
norms admit a closed-form solution in terms of principal angles:
\dist_{c,\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{2}}}\!=\!\lVert 2\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\sigma}}\right)\rVert_{\infty}\!\in\![0,\sqrt{2}]
and
\dist_{c,\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{F}}}\!=\!\lVert 2\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\sigma}}\right)\rVert_{2}\!\in\![0,\sqrt{2k}]
.

c)

Projection norm:
Also called the
gap metric
, it uses the unique orthogonal projector representation of a given subspace, i.e.
{\bm{\Psi}}_{i}={\bm{Q}}_{i}{\bm{Q}}_{i}^{\top}
, as follows:
\dist_{p,\raisebox{-0.6pt}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\ast$}}}\!=\!\lVert{\bm{\Psi}}_{i}\!-\!{\bm{\Psi}}_{j}\rVert_{\raisebox{-0.6pt}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\ast$}}}
. Here, we also have closed-form expressions for the
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{F}
norms:
\dist_{p,\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{2}}}\!=\!\lVert\sin({\bm{\sigma}})\rVert_{\infty}\!\in\![0,1]
and
\dist_{p,\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{F}}}\!=\!\lVert\sin({\bm{\sigma}})\rVert_{2}\!\in\![0,\sqrt{k}]
.

d)

Fubini-Study:
This quantity is a measure of the
acute angle
between both spaces, generalized to higher dimensions:
\dist_{a}\!=\!\arccos\left(\lvert\det({\bm{Q}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}_{j})\rvert\right)\!=\!\arccos\left(\prod_{i}\cos(\sigma_{i})\right)\!\in\![0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
.

e)

Overlap:
The
\emph{overlap}\!=\!\frac{1}{k}\lVert{\bm{\Psi}}_{i}{\bm{Q}}_{j}\rVert_{F}^{2}\!\in\![0,1]
quantity was used in
Gur-Ari et al. 2018
to measure subspace
similarity
. It is not a metric
per se
, since it is highest for equivalent subspaces and decreases with their distance, but it is a bijection of
\dist_{p,\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{F}}}
, as follows:
\frac{1}{k}\lVert{\bm{\Psi}}_{i}{\bm{Q}}_{j}\rVert_{F}^{2}\!=\!\frac{1}{k}\lVert{\bm{Q}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}_{j}\rVert_{F}^{2}\!=\!\frac{1}{k}\lVert\cos({\bm{\sigma}})\rVert_{F}^{2}\!=\!1\!-\!\lVert\cos({\bm{\sigma}})\rVert_{F}^{2}\!=\!1\!-\!\frac{1}{k}\dist_{p,F}^{2}
.

While the above metrics apply to any pair of matrices from
{\mathbb{O}}^{D\!\times\!k}
, there are also relevant metrics specific to
{\mathbb{M}}^{D\!\times\!k}
(boolean subset of
{\mathbb{O}}^{D\!\times\!k}
defined in
Section

3
), that can be characterized in a similar manner. Consider an arbitrary pair of
k
-sparse masks
({\bm{m}}_{i},{\bm{m}}_{j})
, and their corresponding orthogonal projectors
{\bm{\Phi}}\coloneqq\diag({\bm{m}})
. Then we have:

i)

IoU
(
IoU
):
Typically used as an evaluation metric, it is defined as the relative number of entries present in
both
masks, i.e.
\IoU\!\coloneqq\!\frac{{\bm{m}}_{i}\cap{\bm{m}}_{j}}{{\bm{m}}_{i}\cup{\bm{m}}_{j}}\!\in\![0,1]
. Then we have
{\bm{m}}_{i}\!\cap\!{\bm{m}}_{j}\!=\!\lVert{\bm{\Phi}}_{i}{\bm{\Phi}}_{j}\rVert_{F}^{2}=k\!\cdot\!\emph{overlap}
, and if both masks are
k
-sparse, we also have
{\bm{m}}_{i}\!\cup\!{\bm{m}}_{j}\!=\!2k\!-\!({\bm{m}}_{i}\!\cap\!{\bm{m}}_{j})=k(2-overlap)
, yielding the bijection
\emph{overlap}\!=\!\frac{2\IoU}{1\!+\!\IoU}
.

ii)

Hamming distance:
This quantity, defined as the minimum number of bit-flips needed to pass from one mask to another, was used in
You et al. 2020
to measure distances between pruning masks. It is in fact a Grassmannian metric:
\dist_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{H}}}\!\coloneqq\!\lVert{\bm{m}}_{i}-{\bm{m}}_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2}\!=\!\lVert\Phi_{i}\!-\!\Phi_{j}\rVert_{F}^{2}\!=\!\dist^{2}_{p,\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{F}}}\in[0,k]
, which means that the bijection
\emph{overlap}\!=\!1\!-\!\frac{1}{k}\dist_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{H}}}
also holds.

Sketched Hessian Eigendecompositions to Measure Overlap

To compute the
\emph{overlap}
between parameter magnitude masks and top-
k

3

{\bm{Q}}_{1}

{\bm{Q}}_{2}
the correspondingly permuted top eigenspace
\tilde{{\bm{U}}}^{(k)}

\emph{overlap}({\bm{I}}_{D,k},\tilde{{\bm{U}}}^{(k)})\!=\!\frac{1}{k}\lVert{\bm{I}}_{D,k}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{U}}}^{(k)}\rVert_{F}^{2}\!=\!\frac{1}{k}\lVert{\bm{V}}\rVert_{F}^{2}\,,

(24)

i.e. our problem reduces to computing the Frobenius norm of
{\bm{V}}
, a
k\!\times\!k
submatrix of
\tilde{{\bm{U}}}^{(k)}
(
Section

3
).

A case for eigendecompositions:

We aim to estimate
\lVert{\bm{V}}\rVert_{F}^{2}
, where
{\bm{V}}
is part of
\tilde{{\bm{U}}}^{(k)}
, a
D\!\times\!k
orthogonal matrix spanning the top eigenspace. Since all standard algorithms to find such matrices (Gram–Schmidt, Householder transformation, Givens rotation) require to keep track of at least
k
full vectors
(
Golub & Van Loan 2013
)
,
a memory requirement of
\mathcal{O}\!\left(kD\right)
seems inescapable in general
. Still, one may recognize that any orthogonal matrix
\hat{{\bm{Q}}}
with the same span as
\tilde{{\bm{U}}}^{(k)}
yields the same Frobenius norm (due to unitary invariance). However, we need to ensure that the span of said
\hat{{\bm{Q}}}
does not overlap with the bulk eigenspace: It needs to be exclusively associated with the top-
k
eigenvalues. As a consequence,
knowledge of the eigenvalues also seems inescapable in general
. In conclusion, to measure the top-
k

\emph{overlap}
, we argue that a rank-
k
eigendecomposition is generally needed.

A case for
sketched
decompositions:

Among the methods that satisfy the memory requirement of
\mathcal{O}\!\left(kD\right)
,
orthogonal iterations

(
Golub & Van Loan 2013
)
, an extension of Rayleigh’s power method, is a popular and effective one, requiring
\mathcal{O}\!\left(k\right)
measurements per iteration and
\tau
iterations, to converge at a rate proportional to
\lvert\lambda_{k+1}/\lambda_{k}\rvert^{\tau}

(
Golub & Van Loan 2013
, Th. 7.3.1)
. This leads to large
\tau
for smoothly decaying spectra, likely scenario for
{\bm{H}}
, thus
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tau k\right)

HVP
(
HVP
) can become infeasible. Ritz accelerations provide better convergence but still suffer from this issue
(
Golub & Van Loan 2013
, 10.1)
.
Alternatively,
Lanczos iterations
convergence faster
(
Saad 1980
)
,
(
Golub & Van Loan 2013
, 10.1.6)
, and can be used to approximate eigenpairs via Ritz pairs
(
Golub & Van Loan 2013
, 8.1.4)
as well as spectral densities of e.g. large-scale deep learning Hessians via Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature
(
Papyan 2018
)
.
The main issue here is that measurements must be done in sequential form, which can get very slow for
{\bm{H}}
, and they involve matrix powers, numerically unstable for rank-defficient matrices like
{\bm{H}}
.

Sketched methods

(
Halko et al. 2011
, e.g.)
, based on random measurements, not only satisfy
\mathcal{O}\!\left(kD\right)
memory, they also exhibit
good convergence for
\mathcal{O}\!\left(k\right)
measurements
and are

(
Halko et al. 2011

. This, combined with the ability to perform matrix-free random measurements
(
Tropp et al. 2019
, 3.2)
, makes it possible to compute Hessian eigendecompositions at unprecedented scales, since the main bottleneck is now the
\mathcal{O}\!\left(kD\right)
memory requirement
(
Tropp et al. 2019

(for example, storing
1000

Last but not least, affordable

The core idea behind the sketched SVD is that, given a linear operator

Leveraging Symmetry:

While previous works studied Hermitian extensions
(
Halko et al. 2011
;
Clarkson & Woodruff 2017
;
Tropp et al. 2017

, none of them proposes a single-pass sketched eigendecomposition via oversampled and uncorrelated inner matrix.
In this work, we propose

(

SSVD
that retains core oversampling while leveraging conjugate symmetry to drastically reduce the required measurements and memory.
Without loss of generality
(
Halko et al. 2011
)

{\bm{Q}}\!=\!{\bm{P}}
, so only
{\bm{Q}}
needs to be computed via
{\bm{A}}{\bm{\Omega}}

{\bm{C}}
is also Hermitian and can be eigendecomposed:

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}\approx{\bm{Q}}({\bm{Q}}^{*}{\bm{A}}{\bm{Q}}){\bm{Q}}^{*}\!\approx\!{\bm{Q}}({\bm{\Upsilon}}_{I}^{*}{\bm{Q}})^{\dagger}\!\underbrace{{\bm{\Upsilon}}_{I}^{*}{\bm{A}}{\bm{\Omega}}_{I}}_{{\bm{M}}_{I}=\bar{{\bm{U}}}\bar{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\bar{{\bm{V}}}^{*}}\!\big[({\bm{\Omega}}_{I}^{*}{\bm{Q}})^{\dagger}\big]^{*}{\bm{Q}}^{*}\!=\!{\bm{Q}}\underbrace{({\bm{\Upsilon}}_{I}^{*}{\bm{Q}})^{\dagger}\bar{{\bm{U}}}\bar{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\bar{{\bm{V}}}^{*}\big[({\bm{\Omega}}_{I}^{*}{\bm{Q}})^{\dagger}\big]^{*}}_{{\bm{C}}={\bm{C}}^{*}={\bm{U}}{\bm{\Lambda}}{\bm{U}}^{*}\penalty\ \penalty\ (\eigh)}\!{\bm{Q}}^{*}

{\bm{\Upsilon}}_{I}\!=\!{\bm{\Omega}}_{I}

inner measurements to obtain
{\bm{Q}}
, since this is a major bottleneck for the Hessian. But recycling measurements and setting
{\bm{\Upsilon}}_{I}\!=\!{\bm{\Omega}}_{I}
would lead to correlated measurements between
{\bm{Q}}
and
{\bm{M}}_{I}
which, as mentioned, has negative impact in the quality of this procedure
(
Tropp et al. 2019
, 2.8.1)
. We opt to recycle the measurements, while keeping the uncorrelated
{\bm{\Upsilon}}_{I}\!=\!{\bm{\Omega}}_{I}
and the two pseudoinverses. This way, only

measurements are needed, memory is roughly halved, and arithmetic is reduced by one QR decomposition and increased by one inner SVD. With
SEIGH
, we are now able to approximate
\lVert{\bm{V}}\rVert_{F}^{2}
for large Hessians, leading to the
sketched

\emph{overlap}
approximation.

7
Conclusion

We started with the observation that, at the early stages of neural network training, both parameter magnitude masks and loss Hessian eigenspaces collapse and crystallize.
To connect these two phenomena throughout the training process, we proposed a principled methodology that compares these two fundamental deep learning quantities using Grassmannian metrics, and identified
\emph{overlap}
as a particularly advantageous one.
We further developed
SEIGH
, a matrix-free sketched eigendecomposition that works for Hessians of over
$10$^{7}
parameters, allowing us to approximate
\emph{overlap}
.
Our experiments reveal an
\emph{overlap}
between parameter magnitude masks and top Hessian eigenspaces well above chance level for all observed problems, training steps and dimensionalities, indicating that, in
DL
,
top Hessian eigenvectors tend to be concentrated around larger parameters
, or equivalently, that
large parameters tend to align with directions of large loss curvature
.

While the obtained
\emph{overlap}
may be considered small in absolute terms, the fact that it is orders of magnitude above chance could play a crucial role at large scales, analogously to how mini-batch gradients produce very noisy fluctuations in the short term, but lead to qualitative performance and generalization over larger training spans.
The relevance of this connection could also bear potential for downstream tasks where the Hessian plays a prominent role (e.g. by approximating expensive Hessian quantities via cheap parameter inspection).

One main limitation of this method is that it still does not scale up to contemporary network sizes (see
Section

B.2
).
In this sense, we emphasize that our methodology is primarily meant as an
analysis
technique to understand and leverage the connection between parameters and loss curvature, and not necessarily part of the downstream applications themselves.
In
Section

A.2
, we have also explored alternative approaches that show potential as more scalable alternatives to computing
\emph{overlap}
.

A.1
Perturbing the Parameters to Measure their Importance

In order to prove that a certain subset of parameters is “sensitive” without using the theoretical framework we propose, one may consider a perturbation study where large parameters are perturbed, and the resulting loss variation is measured.
More variation would then relate to more sensitivity.
Here we show that, in general, such an experiment does not yield an informative quantity towards this goal (unlike our proposed Grassmannians), due to the non-PSD nature of
{\bm{H}}
, (i.e. negative and positive directions of curvature may cancel out for arbitrary sets of parameters) and the lack of details about the loss landscape regularity.

At any parameter vector
{\bm{\theta}}\!\in\!{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
, and under additive perturbations
{\bm{\delta}}\!\in\!{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
, the loss can be expressed via its Taylor expansion (for gradient
{\bm{g}}
, Hessian
{\bm{H}}
and Lagrange remainder
R_{3}
):

\displaystyle L({\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\delta}})=L({\bm{\theta}})+{\bm{g}}^{\top}{\bm{\delta}}+\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\delta}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}{\bm{\delta}}+R_{3}({\bm{\delta}})

(26)

Then, given a parameter mask
{\bm{m}}\!\in\!{\mathbb{B}}^{D}
, the proposed perturbation study would consist in drawing random perturbations
{\bm{\delta}}
from any i.i.d. distribution
\mathcal{N}
with zero mean
\mu
and unit variance, and then mask said perturbations via
{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}\!=\!({\bm{m}}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\cr$\displaystyle\cdot$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\cr$\textstyle\cdot$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\cr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\cr}}}}{\bm{\delta}})
and add them to
{\bm{\theta}}
in order to estimate the expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[L({\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}})\right]=L({\bm{\theta}})+\underbrace{{\bm{g}}^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\bm{H}},\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}^{\top}\right]\rangle+\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[R_{3}({\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}})\right]}_{\varepsilon}

This way, we are perturbing only the selected parameters in
{\bm{m}}
, and measuring their impact on the (known) loss
L({\bm{\theta}})
:
If
\lvert\varepsilon\rvert
is larger, this can be interpreted as the subset
\xi
being more “sensitive".

Since we assumed
{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{m}}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}\right]=$0$
(otherwise reparametrize
({\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}})\!\eqqcolon\!({\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{m}}})+({\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}-{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{m}}})
such that the reparametrized mean of the perturbations will be
0
), and we also assumed a (masked) unit covariance
\mathrm{Cov}\left[{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}\right]={\bm{I}}_{{\bm{m}}}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left[{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}\right]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[({\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}-{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{m}}})({\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}-{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{m}}})^{\top}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}^{\top}\right]={\bm{I}}_{{\bm{m}}}

Then, the expectation of the perturbed loss simplifies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[L({\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}})\right]

\displaystyle=L({\bm{\theta}})+\underbrace{{\bm{g}}^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}\right]}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\bm{H}},\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}}^{\top}\right]}_{{\bm{I}}_{{\bm{m}}}}\rangle+\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[R_{3}({\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}})\right]

\displaystyle=L({\bm{\theta}})+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\Tr_{{\bm{m}}}({\bm{H}})+\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[R_{3}({\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}})\right]}_{\varepsilon}

We see now how, in general, this technique cannot be relied upon: since
{\bm{H}}
is non-PSD in general, any of its subtraces could cancel
R_{3}
, leading to
\varepsilon=0
.
Therefore, this procedure is not guaranteed to be informative in measuring the impact of parameter subsets on the loss curvature.
In contrast, our proposed method is guaranteed to yield larger similarities whenever the selected parameters in
{\bm{m}}
tend to align with directions of larger loss curvature, as shown in the paper.

Alternatively, one could consider to sample perturbations for parameters
one by one
, i.e. by setting
{\bm{m}}
to equal
1
at exactly
m_{i}
, and
0
otherwise. Then, the expectation of the perturbed loss becomes:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[L({\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}})\right]=L({\bm{\theta}})+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}{\bm{H}}_{ii}+\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}}\left[R_{3}({\bm{\delta}}_{{\bm{m}}})\right]}_{\varepsilon_{i}}

(27)

The idea here is that the aggregated perturbation
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\sum_{i}\lvert\varepsilon_{i}\rvert
is now more likely to be informative, since positive and negative diagonal entries don’t cancel anymore.
This still entails a problematic trade-off that favours our Grassmannian characterization, for the following reasons:
(1) In
DL
, the loss landscape may be far from convex or regular
(e.g.
Li et al. 2018b
, Fig. 1)
, and to interpret
\varepsilon^{\prime}
meaningfully we would still require some characterization of
R_{3}
similar in spirit to the theoretically founded Grasmannian that we use.
(2) This method requires to estimate one expectation per scalar parameter. For a set of
k
parameters and
n
samples per expectation, this means performing
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot k)
forward propagations over a given dataset.
In contrast, our method is theoretically grounded and our proposed sketched method requires just
\mathcal{O}(k)

HVP
(see
Section

5
and
Halko et al. 2011
for more discussion).

A.2
Beyond Perturbing the Parameters to Measure their Importance

Despite the various sources of error and computational issues related to parameter perturbations (see
Section

A.1
), one may still feel compelled to explore related strategies, seeking a more efficient and conceptually simple alternative to our proposed Grassmannian methodology.
In this section, we introduce three such alternatives which, despite of some issues, constitute a promising line of investigation. We discuss here some of their main aspects in terms of model error, computation and empirical behaviour.

Nonnegative Gauss-Newton Subtraces:

A main issue with
{\bm{H}}
is its non-PSD nature. Instead, the idea here is to target the Gauss-Newton matrix
{\bm{G}}
, which is a PSD approximation to
{\bm{H}}

(
Schraudolph 2002
)
.
Since PSD matrices have nonnegative diagonal entries,
{\bm{G}}_{ii}
could serve as a proxy measurement, denoting parameter sensitivity for
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
.
In that case, a larger trace for a given subset of indices could mean that the corresponding parameter space is associated with larger loss curvature.

Instead of casting this as a parameter perturbation study, which would be exposed to the
R_{3}
approximation error as well as the impossibility of disentangling
{\bm{G}}
from
{\bm{H}}
(see
Section

A.1
), we propose here to
measure the diagonal entries of
{\bm{G}}
one by one using
GNVP
. Since we are just interested in the entries corresponding to the
k
largest parameters by magnitude, this just requires
\mathcal{O}(D)
memory and
\mathcal{O}(k)

GNVP
, thus being fully parallelizable and substantially faster than computing
\emph{overlap}
.
Furthermore,
GNVP
are roughly twice as fast as
HVP
, and require half the memory
(
Martens 2010
, 4.2)
.

Once we have obtained the top-
k
diagonal entries (i.e. the diagonal entries of
{\bm{G}}
corresponding to the
k
-largest parameters), we need a way to compare them with
\emph{overlap}
. For this, we propose to compute the following
normalized trace summation
:

\displaystyle\xi_{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Tr({\bm{G}})}\sum_{i\in\{i_{1}\makebox[7.97224pt][c]{.\hfil.\hfil.}i_{k}\}}{\bm{G}}_{ii}\quad\in[0,1]

(28)

Where the
i_{1},\makebox[10.00002pt][c]{.\hfil.\hfil.},i_{k}
indices run over the parameters by descending magnitude.
We can see that
\xi
increases monotonically with
k
, reaching
\xi_{D}\!=\!1
.
Furthermore, we define the baseline corresponding to a uniformly distributed trace as
\bar{\xi}_{k}\!\coloneqq\!\nicefrac{{k}}{{D}}
.
This baseline behaves analogously to our
“chance-level”

\emph{overlap}
baseline from
Section

4.2
: if
\xi_{k}\!>\!\bar{\xi}_{k}
, the top-
k
parameters are associated with an above-chance trace (which, as already mentioned, we treat as a proxy for curvature in the case of
{\bm{G}}
).
See
Figures

6
and
7
for an illustration.

Nonnegative Squared-Hessian Subtraces:

The rationale and computations involved here are identical as for the previously described
{\bm{G}}
, but using
{\bm{H}}^{2}
instead.
This is viable, since
{\bm{H}}^{2}
is also PSD and
[{\bm{H}}^{2}]_{ii}
can be efficiently and exactly computed with a single
HVP
as follows:

\displaystyle[{\bm{H}}^{2}]_{ii}={\bm{e}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{H}}^{2}{\bm{e}}_{i}=\lVert{\bm{H}}{\bm{e}}_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}

This technique is also substantially faster than
\emph{overlap}
, requiring only
\mathcal{O}(D)
memory and
\mathcal{O}(k)
parallelizable
HVP
.
Although we do not assume
{\bm{H}}^{2}\!\approx\!{\bm{H}}
, this experiment aims to explore whether subtraces of
{\bm{H}}^{2}
can also be treated as proxy for curvature, in the same fashion as previously discussed for
{\bm{G}}
.

Recall from
Section

A.1
and

that a random perturbation study would require
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot k)
forward propoagations, for potentially large
n
.
Instead of random sampling, the idea here is to
use a single “optimal” perturbation, such that curvature is maximized in that direction
. This is precisely the idea behind

(

In our setting from

\displaystyle\Delta_{\lambda,i}=|L({\bm{\theta}})-L({\bm{\theta}}+\lambda({\bm{e}}_{i}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\cr$\displaystyle\cdot$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\cr$\textstyle\cdot$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\cr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\cr}}}}\epsilon^{*}))|

This doesn’t rely on PSD-ness anymore, since

It is also very fast to compute, since we just need

Once we have the

A.3
Main Properties of Grassmannian Metrics

A desirable property of Grassmann manifolds is the availability of closed-form expressions for their
geodesics
(i.e. the shortest paths between any two elements
\mathfrak{g}_{i},\mathfrak{g}_{j}\in{\mathcal{G}}
) and metric functions. We can thus measure distances between subspaces in an interpretable and computationally amenable manner: geodesics from
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
to
\mathfrak{g}_{j}
follow circular trajectories, and therefore their
distance
can be interpreted as the “amount of rotation” needed to go from one space to another.
Such rotations can be succinctly expressed in terms of
principal angles

{\bm{\sigma}}_{i\shortrightarrow j}\!\in\![0,\frac{\pi}{2}]^{k}
, and they can be efficiently obtained from
{\bm{Q}}\in{\mathbb{O}}^{D\times k}
matrices via their
SVD
(
SVD
)
(e.g.
Edelman et al. 1998
, s. 4.3)
:

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}_{j}

\displaystyle\eqqcolon{\bm{L}}_{i\shortrightarrow j}\diag\big(\cos({\bm{\sigma}}_{i\shortrightarrow j})\big){\bm{R}}_{i\shortrightarrow j}^{\top},\quad{\bm{L}},{\bm{R}}\text{\penalty\ orthogonal.}

(30)

Since
{\bm{Q}}_{i},\!{\bm{Q}}_{j}
have orthonormal columns,
\cos({\bm{\sigma}}_{i\shortrightarrow j})\in[0,\!1]^{k}

(
Neretin 2001
, e.g.)
, and more similar spans will yield larger singular values, which translate to smaller rotations. Importantly, singular values are invariant under similarity:

\displaystyle\diag\big(\cos({\bm{\sigma}}_{i\shortrightarrow j})\big)

\displaystyle={\bm{L}}_{i\shortrightarrow j}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}_{j}{\bm{R}}_{i\shortrightarrow j}

(31)

\displaystyle={\bm{L}}_{i\shortrightarrow j}^{\prime\top}({\bm{Z}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}_{i}^{\top})({\bm{Q}}_{j}{\bm{Z}}_{j}){\bm{R}}_{i\shortrightarrow j}^{\prime}.

(32)

In other words, they are invariant under the action of any orthogonal
{\bm{Z}}
, which means that
{\bm{\sigma}}
does not change if we replace an input matrix with any other matrix from the same equivalence class (see definition for
{\mathcal{S}}^{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i}
in
Section

4.1
). The family of functions that satisfy this invariance, plus the axioms of metric spaces, form the family of
Grassmannian metrics

(
Qiu et al. 2005
, e.g.)
, each capturing a different notion of distance between subspaces (largest principal angle, sum of principal angles
. . .
). See
Section

4.1
for more details on specific metrics.

A.5
Expectation of
\emph{overlap}
for Uniformly Random Matrices

In
Section

4.2
we empirically observed that the expectation under uniformly distributed random matrices becomes
predictable
for all reviewed metrics, converging to a baseline value. Here we show that, for
\emph{overlap}
, such expectation equals exactly
\rho=\frac{k}{D}
. This lemma depends on a standard calculation that was communicated to us by Joel A. Tropp.

Lemma A.1

Let
{\bm{Q}}_{1},{\bm{Q}}_{2}
be random matrices drawn uniformly from the Stiefel manifold
{\mathbb{O}}^{D\times k}\coloneqq\{{\bm{Q}}\!:\!{\bm{Q}}\!\in\!{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times k},\,{\bm{Q}}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}\!=\!{\bm{I}}_{k}\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\emph{overlap}(\Span(Q_{1}),\Span(Q_{2}))\right]=\frac{k}{D}

(33)

Proof.

We start by rewriting the definition of
\emph{overlap}
, presented in
Section

4.1
, in terms of the trace of orthogonal projectors
{\bm{\Psi}}={\bm{Q}}{\bm{Q}}^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times D}
. We make use of the idempotence of
{\bm{\Psi}}
and the unitary invariance of the Frobenius norm:

\displaystyle\emph{overlap}(\Span({\bm{Q}}_{1}),\Span({\bm{Q}}_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\lVert{\bm{Q}}_{1}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}_{2}\rVert_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{k}\lVert{\bm{\Psi}}_{1}{\bm{\Psi}}_{2}\rVert_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{k}\Tr({\bm{\Psi}}_{1}{\bm{\Psi}}_{2}^{2}{\bm{\Psi}}_{1})

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\Tr({\bm{\Psi}}_{1}{\bm{\Psi}}_{2}{\bm{\Psi}}_{1})

(35)

We further observe that, if
{\bm{Q}}
is drawn uniformly from
{\mathbb{O}}^{D\times k}
, the marginal distribution of every column
{\bm{q}}
is the uniform distribution over the Euclidean unit sphere, hence it is isotropic:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{q}}{\bm{q}}^{\top}\right]=\frac{1}{D}{\bm{I}}_{D}

(36)

Where
I_{D}
is the rank-
D
identity matrix. Then, leveraging linearity of expectation, the orthogonal projector
{\bm{\Psi}}={\bm{Q}}{\bm{Q}}^{\top}
can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{\Psi}}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{q}}_{i}{\bm{q}}_{i}^{\top}\right]=\frac{k}{D}{\bm{I}}_{D}

(37)

Now, given two independent realizations
{\bm{Q}}_{1},{\bm{Q}}_{2}
, we form the associated orthogonal projectors
{\bm{\Psi}}_{1},{\bm{\Psi}}_{2}
. Write
\mathbb{E}_{1},\mathbb{E}_{2}
for the expectations of the respective distributions of
{\bm{Q}}_{1}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{2}
. Then, leveraging independence of
{\bm{Q}}_{1}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{2}
, idempotence of
{\bm{\Psi}}
, linearity of expectation, and
\Tr({\bm{\Psi}})\!=\!k
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr({\bm{\Psi}}_{1}{\bm{\Psi}}_{2}{\bm{\Psi}}_{1})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[\Tr({\bm{\Psi}}_{1}\mathbb{E}_{2}\left[{\bm{\Psi}}_{2}\right]{\bm{\Psi}}_{1})\right]=\frac{k}{D}\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[\Tr({\bm{\Psi}}_{1}{\bm{I}}_{D}{\bm{\Psi}}_{1})\right]=\frac{k}{D}\Tr({\bm{\Psi}}_{1})=\frac{k^{2}}{D}

(38)

Replacing in the definition of
\emph{overlap}
concludes the proof.
∎
