Scalably Enhancing the Clinical Validity of a Task Benchmark with Physician Oversight
Junze (Tony) Ye 1, BS; Daniel Tawfik 2, MD, MS; Alex J. Goodell 3, MD, MS; Nikhil V. Kotha 4, MD; Mark K. Buyyounouski 4, MD, MS; Mohsen Bayati 1,4,5, PhD

Abstract
Automating clinical risk-score computation promises to reduce administrative burden, but rigorous evaluation requires benchmarks whose labels reflect clinical ground truth. Increasingly, such benchmarks are constructed with partial LLM assistance (e.g., feature extraction and code-based aggregation), which means the resulting “gold” labels can encode historical model and tooling errors. Treating these artifacts as static oracles is especially hazardous when they are reused as reward signals for reinforcement learning (RL), where label noise becomes a teacher rather than a yardstick. We propose viewing complex clinical workflow benchmarks as living documents that require periodic, transparent re-auditing and clinician-guided maintenance. We introduce a scalable physician-in-the-loop maintenance pipeline that combines agentic LLM verifiers with automated triage to concentrate scarce clinician attention on the most contentious instances. In a case study on the original MedCalc-Bench release, our audit and independent recomputation identify a substantial fraction of test labels that diverge from medical ground truth due to extraction errors, calculator logic mismatches, and task underspecification (including clinically unanswerable cases). Physician single-blind review indicates higher agreement for the maintained labels. Finally, in a controlled GRPO fine-tuning experiment on Qwen3-8B, changing only the reward labels yields an 8.7% absolute improvement in test accuracy when evaluated against physician-aligned labels, demonstrating that benchmark maintenance materially affects downstream alignment. Together, these results argue that in safety-critical domains, benchmark stewardship—auditing, versioning, and clinician oversight, is prerequisite infrastructure for reliable model evaluation and training.

Introduction

A clinician’s daily workflow is fundamentally an exercise in resource allocation, split between high-value complex clinical judgment and repetitive administrative tasks involving the retrieval and synthesis of fragmented data from the Electronic Health Record (EHR). This clerical burden creates a significant operational bottleneck; it is a primary driver of physician burnout, which has been linked to significant patient safety risks and estimated costs to the U.S. healthcare system reaching billions of dollars annually

(
24
;
53
;
43
;
56
)
. Integrating technology to optimize these workflows and alleviate cognitive load has shown promise in improving clinician well-being, operational efficiency, and clinical outcomes. In particular, the advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) has opened new avenues for such enhancement, ranging from ambient AI scribes that reduce documentation time
(
58
;
59
)
to “copilots” that verify medication prescriptions
(
46
)
or selectively trigger safety alerts in live care
(
32
)
.

In this paper, we focus on one such high-frequency administrative task: the calculation of standardized medical risk and stratification scores. These calculations are ubiquitous in hospital settings, for instance, determining pneumonia severity (CURB-65) upon a patient’s arrival at an Emergency Department, or assessing stroke risk (CHA
2
DS
2
-VASc) to guide anticoagulation therapy
(
34
;
36
)
. These metrics synthesize patient history, vitals, and laboratory results into actionable scalars that directly inform high-stakes operational decisions: prioritizing patient flow in triage, determining the appropriate level of care (e.g., admission planning), and applying preventative interventions (prophylaxis).

Traditionally, these scores are computed manually: a clinician reads the EHR, extracts the necessary criteria, and inputs them into a bedside calculator such as
MDCalc
, a web app that has between three and five million visits per month and is used by 65% of physicians in the US
(
42
)
. However, the manual extraction of clinical criteria from unstructured notes is time-consuming and prone to mistakes. The emergence of LLMs offers a transformative vision: automated systems that can read EHR notes, extract relevant clinical features, and reliably compute these scores end to end
(
65
;
22
;
26
;
62
;
49
)
. But as we move toward deploying or even researching such systems, a fundamental question of quality assurance arises:
how do we ensure these automated systems are computing the “right” scores, specifically, scores that align with the ground truth judgment of a licensed physician
?

To answer this question, the research community requires an accessible evaluation standard. In this domain,
MedCalc-Bench

(
29
)
stands as a foundational contribution. By curating de-identified patient notes from PubMed Central and mapping them to 55 widely calculated medical scores on MDCalc web app,
29
established the first large-scale public testbed, involving 11,000 samples, for end-to-end clinical score computation. To generate labels at scale, the authors employed a two-stage pipeline: using GPT-4 to extract clinical features from text, followed by Python scripts to aggregate those features into final scores. This effort provided the field with a standardized definition of the task and a shared dataset, enabling subsequent research to benchmark progress in medical reasoning
(
44
;
37
)
. Consequently, the labels provided by MedCalc-Bench have effectively become the ground truth against which new models are evaluated and, increasingly, the target distribution on which they are trained.

However, the reliance on largely LLM-generated “gold standard” labels introduces a unique systemic risk: these labels inherently echo the model’s capabilities and flaws at the time of creation. The original MedCalc-Bench labels relied on the extraction capabilities of GPT-4 in early 2024 and the assumption that programmed calculators perfectly mirror clinical intent. When such a benchmark is treated as a static oracle, errors in the original extraction or calculator logic become enshrined as systemic biases in the evaluation signal. This creates a dangerous feedback loop, particularly when benchmarks transition from evaluation sets to training signals. In modern Reinforcement Learning (RL) setups
(
2
)
, inaccurate labels are not just a mislabeled yardstick, but a biased teacher that actively steers models away from clinical reality.

While benchmarks have been instrumental in accelerating progress, this challenge of latent inaccuracies necessitates a more rigorous approach to their lifecycle, grounded in principles of continuous quality control. We argue that LLM-assisted benchmarks for complex clinical workflows are not static measurement instruments; they are socio-technical artifacts whose labels inherit the failure modes of the models, tools, and procedural assumptions used at the time of creation. They should therefore be treated as
living documents
with explicit maintenance practices (auditing, clinician adjudication, versioning, and documentation). We do not position our improved labels as a definitive replacement for MedCalc-Bench; rather, we use the original release as a case study to motivate transparent, repeatable benchmark stewardship and to quantify how label fragility affects downstream model development.

In this work, we approach benchmark maintenance not as an ad-hoc error correction task, but as an optimized quality assurance process. We design a systematic, replicable collaboration between advanced agentic LLMs (as verifiers) and human physicians, focused on the central operational constraint: scarcity of expert attention. Our approach proceeds in three steps: (1) an automated audit where an LLM verifier flags potential errors in the original labels; (2) an independent maintenance pipeline that recomputes scores from scratch to identify systematic divergences; and (3) a targeted physician adjudication of divergent cases. In short, we leverage agentic AI to scale re-auditing and triage, thereby concentrating scarce clinician time on the most consequential disagreements.

The above approach shows that a non-trivial fraction of the original labels diverge from physician judgment due to feature extraction errors, calculator logic mismatches, or clinical ambiguity (e.g., unanswerable questions). Furthermore, we demonstrate the downstream impact of unmaintained labels through a controlled LLM fine-tuning experiment. By fine-tuning a Qwen3-8B model
(
48
)
via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
51
)
, a commonly used RL algorithm, we show that changing only the reward labels (original versus maintained) yields an 8.7% absolute difference in test accuracy when evaluated against physician-aligned labels. The goal of this experiment is not to claim superior benchmark performance; rather, it is to test whether label noise materially impacts trained model accuracy, a meaningful question because one plausible outcome was that the noise would be “washed out” by the strength of signal in the data. Our results indicate otherwise, confirming that benchmark maintenance has tangible consequences for downstream alignment.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We design and implement a physician-in-the-loop system that operationalizes scalable oversight by optimizing the allocation of expert attention. By aggregating evidence from two independent, agentic LLM pipelines, the system triages the most contentious instances in MedCalc-Bench for targeted physician expert review (§

Literature Review

Integrating LLMs into clinical workflows.

LLM-based systems are increasingly embedded in routine care for documentation, medication safety, and clinical decision support. At health-system scale, ambient AI scribes have been deployed to draft visit notes inside the EHR and are associated with reduced documentation burden and high clinician adoption
(
58
;
59
)
. For medication safety,
46

22

29

32

These systems motivate our focus on MedCalc-style calculators as a concrete use case where LLM systems and domain-specific evaluations need to be co-designed.

Healthcare cost of burnout.

A larger problem we hope to solve is the reduction of clerical burdens and its cost for the U.S. healthcare system. Clinician burnout carries substantial economic consequences:
24
and
53
both estimate excess annual U.S. healthcare expenditures attributable to physician turnover to be in the billion USD scale, with a sizable share specific to burnout-related turnover, while
43
show that nurse burnout-attributed turnover substantially increases hospital costs in a Markov modeling analysis.
56
;
57
show a correlation between EHR-related work burden and primary care physician burnout. These results underpin the motivation for clerical support tools such as ambient AI scribes and for our narrower focus on automating repetitive, text-based decision support as one contributor to documentation and cognitive load.

Our framework views benchmark maintenance not merely as curation, but as an operational decision process, allocating scarce physician time to maximize data quality. 2.1
Mathematical Formalism

A typical workflow of computing a medical score can be decomposed into two stages: (1) evaluating a fixed set of score-specific clinical features (a.k.a. clinical criteria) based on the patient’s medical history, then (2) formulaically aggregating the feature values into a scalar score, either by hand or a custom calculator app like
MDCalc
.
In symbols, we may denote the patient context (natural language texts interleaved with tabular lab data) by
C
and a description of the medical score to compute by
q
. The feature vector typically used to answer
q
can be denoted by
\bm{x}\triangleq(x_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
where
m\in\mathbb{N}
is uniquely determined by
q
.
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
can be thought of as answers to
(q_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
where
q_{i}
is a sub-question of
q
, described in natural language, that a clinician or LLM needs to answer. For each
i
, the per-feature domain
\mathcal{X}_{i}
contains the following:

1.

real numbers, e.g. blood pressure, Likert scale response;

2.

categorical values, ranging from True/False to symptom groups—we call this a set
\mathcal{C}
;

3.

a fallback undefined token denoted by
\mathsf{NA}
, indicating unextractability of a feature.

In sum,
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{i}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\mathcal{C}\cup\{\mathsf{NA}\}
. Let
\bm{\mathcal{X}}\triangleq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{X}_{i}
denote the feature space, a Cartesian product of individual per-feature domains. Lastly, for each
(C,q)
let
y
be
any
answer predicted by an algorithm, and
y^{*}
be the (latent) clinically correct ground truth answer;
y^{*}
is a scalar if the question
q
is answerable given
C
, and
\mathsf{NA}
if
C
lacks crucial information or is not clinically suited for answering
q
. Perfectly observing
y^{*}
is costly, so an estimator
\hat{y}
(
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
for MedCalc-Bench,
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
for ours) is used.

Stage (1): Extracting Features.

With the above notations, and let
\mathcal{T}
be the set of all displayable computer texts. The first stage of the workflow is abstracted as applying a highly flexible language processing function
f_{\theta}:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\bigcup_{i}\mathcal{X}_{i}
for each one of
x_{i}=f_{\theta}(C,q_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq m
, i.e.,
m
times applying
f_{\theta}
. The language processor
f_{\theta}
is traditionally instantiated by a human healthcare provider who reads patient EHR notes to determine the
q_{i}
’s, but nowadays the rise of generative AI makes it possible to augment human-only processing with LLM auto-completions.

Stage (2): Aggregating Features.

Given the feature vector
\bm{x}
extracted in Stage (1), the second stage applies a rule-based, deterministic reduction operator
g_{\phi}
(Of course,
g_{\phi}
is specific to each score question
q
, but for notational simplicity, we do not include
q
in the notation) that maps the feature tuple
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
to a scalar output
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. The parameter
\phi
represents the score-specific rule, e.g. arithmetic formula, look-up table, or piecewise-defined condition, that defines how the features jointly contribute to the final score. Formally:

g_{\phi}:\bm{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\mathcal{Y},\quad y=g_{\phi}(\bm{x})=g_{\phi}(x_{1},...,x_{m})

where
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}\cup\{\mathsf{NA}\}
is the space of possible score values. If at least one feature in
\bm{x}
is out-of-range or
\mathsf{NA}
, the final score is not computable and
\mathsf{NA}
is returned.

Putting the two stages together, we can visualize the overall workflow of computing a medical score as a compositional pipeline:

(C,q)\xrightarrow[\text{extracts}]{f_{\theta}}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m})\xrightarrow[\text{aggregates}]{g_{\phi}}y.

The end-to-end pipeline can be abstracted by a composed function
G_{\phi,\theta}[C,q]:\mathcal{T}\mapsto\mathcal{Y}
, specifically

G_{\phi,\theta}[C,q]\triangleq g_{\phi}(f_{\theta}(C,q_{1}),\ldots,f_{\theta}(C,q_{m})).

Remark 2.1

(Insights from the Formalism)

The above formalism abstracts the
sequential modularity
of a typical medical computing workflow and the
graphical structure
of information flows: the first stage (
f_{\theta}
) interprets and extracts structured intermediate variables from unstructured data, while the second stage (
g_{\phi}
) performs a rule-based routine to aggregate the intermediate bits into a clinically verifiable and actionable scalar output. Of course, not all end-to-end workflows follow this specific pipeline structure; a system designer may as well eliminate
g_{\phi}
and use a single call of
f_{\theta}
to directly output the scalar output. That said,
29
employed the former, structured data generation approach, so understanding this allows us to attribute any deviation in the final output
y
to a parameter extraction error in
f_{\theta}
, or a formulaic mistake in
g_{\phi}
, or both.

2.2
Current task evaluation standard

To our best knowledge, the only large-scale public benchmark dataset for evaluating an agentic system’s end-to-end score computing capability is MedCalc-Bench
(
29
)
, created and released by researchers at NIH/NLM and five US medical schools. Their benchmark task asks an LLM model to read a patient note and answer a medical score computation question, and the model’s final answer
y
is graded correct if it is within a
\pm 5\%
margin of a gold label value
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
that the benchmark authors synthesized; and in cases where
y
should be a datetime, exact string match is required. A model’s performance is quantified as the average accuracy on the benchmark’s test data set, which are 1047 instances manually reviewed by those authors.

MedCalc-Bench authors curated the benchmark data and labels in the following manner:

1.

Patient notes are real, deidentified patient cases they scraped from journal-published medical papers that are archived in the
PubMed Central
database. There are altogether 10,053 + 1,047 = 11,100 notes in the train and test sets. We note that each of these patient notes can be seen as a unique context
C
in the notation of §
2.1
.

2.

The medical score questions (
q
’s), e.g. “What is the patient’s Glasgow Coma Score?”, are sourced as the 55 unique calculators listed as “popular” on
MDCalc.com
, a web app widely used by U.S. physicians. One can think of each train or test instance in the MedCalc-Bench dataset as uniquely identified by a pair
(C,q)
. Many instances may share the same score question
q
, though their patient context
C
will differ.

3.

\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}\in\mathcal{Y}
, MedCalc-Bench’s ground truth label of each
(C,q)
, was synthesized in a procedure analogous to the compositional pipeline we presented in
2.1
. They used
GPT-4
as
f_{\theta}
and themselves hard-coded the logic of
g_{\phi}
of each unique
q

individually as 55 Python scripts
.

2.3
Concerns about MedCalc’s Original Labeling Approach

MedCalc-Bench represents a significant contribution to evaluating AI for healthcare workflows. By sourcing real, de-identified patient notes and pairing them with 55 popular calculation tasks from MDCalc.com, the benchmark provides a far more realistic simulation of clinical tasks than traditional multiple-choice question answering. This curation represents a substantial effort by the authors to balance patient privacy with the need for accessible, high-quality evaluation data.

However, we have concerns about the procedure through which MedCalc-Bench synthesized the ground truth values
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
’s, particularly regarding the reliability of their chosen
f_{\theta}
and
g_{\phi}
. First, for both the train and test sets, the MedCalc authors used GPT4 as their feature extractor (
f_{\theta}
) to produce the clinical features vector
\bm{x}
, instructing it to output a JSON dictionary directly without intermediate reasoning. Although the authors reported manually checking this parameter extraction, their paper provides no statistics on the level of disagreement between their human reviewers and the GPT4-extracted values. Such disagreement seems probable, especially given that earlier versions of GPT4 were more prone to factual hallucinations than contemporary models.

Second, the level of alignment between their 55 Python functions (their
g_{\phi}
) and the formulas used by MDCalc.com is unclear. Given that MDCalc.com is used daily by many US physicians, its formulas represent a de facto standard. The authors did not detail the derivation process for their Python scripts, making it difficult to know if they were all fact-checked by licensed physicians.

As explained in Remark
2.1
, each of these potential discrepancies in
f_{\theta}
and
g_{\phi}
could contribute to an accumulation of error in the final gold label. This motivates our work in §
3
to systematically examine the true label quality of the benchmark.

Remark 2.2

(Aligning Ground Truth Labels)

If ground truth
y^{*}
were to be thought of as a point value and
\hat{y}
be a benchmark’s estimate of it, the alignment objective could be loosely interpreted as minimizing the estimator bias

B(\hat{y}):=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{y}\sim D(\theta,\phi)}[\hat{y}]-y^{*}
,
where
D(\theta,\phi)
emphasizes the sampling distribution’s dependence on both on the LLM weights
\theta
and the rule-based aggregation logic
\phi
stipulated by the human researcher. If there is high heterogeneity in how human clinicians themselves interpret a clinical criterion (low inter-rater reliability),
y^{*}
might be better modeled as a distribution; and the alignment objective be approximated as minimizing some statistical distance, e.g. KL divergence or Wasserstein distance, between
y^{*}
and
\hat{y}
. In this paper, we adopt the former, point-estimate view of
y^{*}
with a
\pm 5\%
margin for real-valued outputs, reflecting the evaluation guideline given by MedCalc-Bench. That said, we want to acknowledge the possible suitability of the second approach for certain medical score questions.

3
Systematic Alignment of MedCalc Benchmark Labels

To address reliability concerns identified in §
2.3

3.1
Phase 1: Auditing Test Labels

Our pilot study focused on checking the label correctness of MedCalc’s test set, as this is the subset ultimately used to benchmark an LLM’s capability. The audit pipeline’s backbone uses the June 2025 version of
Gemini-2.5-Pro

(
16
)
, which was one of the most advanced reasoning LLMs when we conducted the audit in July and August 2025. Figure
1
summarizes the structure of the audit pipeline. In words, Gemini is applied five times separately to clinically validate how the MedCalc label
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
was originally derived for each and every
(C,q)

The overall pipeline design is partly motivated by the
generation-verification gap

(
54
)
; we believe that
Gemini-2.5-Pro
, enhanced with the said agentic abilities, serves as a strong verifier for outputs previously generated by a non-reasoning LLM in a single-turn, closed-book fashion (e.g. clinical features
\bm{x}
extracted by
GPT-4
, or Python scripts codifying the
g_{\phi}

\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}

3.2
Phase 2: Relabeling and Clinical Validation

Motivated by the findings in Phase 1, we proceeded to systematically relabel the dataset to establish a more reliable ground truth. We subdivided the Phase 2 experiment process into three parts: pipeline setup, automated error definition, and independent physician validation.

Pipeline 2 Setup.

We developed a separate relabeling pipeline (Fig.
4
; for prompts we used, see Appendix Fig.
7
) using a
Gemini-2.5-Pro
agent with access to Google Search and Python execution capabilities. Unlike Phase 1, the agent was provided only the patient context and question
(C,q)
, without access to the original MedCalc-Bench metadata including
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
. We applied this pipeline to the full MedCalc test set (
n=1{,}047
) and a stratified random sample of the training set (
n=5{,}183
, 50% of total), stratified by question type
q
. To ensure label stability, each instance was processed by five independent copies this Gemini agent. If at least 4 of the 5 outputs agree in type and value (up to two decimal places for reals, exactly for datetimes, or all abstain), we keep that supermajority value as the new label
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
; otherwise the instance is left unlabeled by Pipeline 2. This yields a set of “high-confidence” new labels.

Flagging likely label errors.

Once a high-confidence
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
is collected from Pipeline 2 for a test instance
(C,q)
, we compare it to
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
.
We define a symmetric discrepancy metric

\operatorname{rel\_err}(\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}},\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}):=\frac{\left|\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}-\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}\right|}{\max\!\bigl(\left|\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}\right|,\left|\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}\right|\bigr)}.

Because the denominator uses the maximum of the two estimates,
\operatorname{rel\_err}
is a lower bound on the true relative error that would be obtained if either
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
or
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
exactly equaled the latent clinical ground truth
y^{\star}
.
We classify an instance as
Likely Error
if
\operatorname{rel\_err}(\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}},\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}})>0.05
, mirroring the
\pm 5\%
tolerance used by MedCalc-Bench for grading model outputs. Instances where either label is
\mathsf{NA}
or datetime are handled respectively by exact match and
>1
day difference.

Physician validation protocol.

To obtain an external clinical reference, we asked three licensed physicians (pediatrics, anesthesiology, oncology) to independently recompute a small subset of labels. The validation proceeds in three steps.
First, we sort the potentially erroneous instances flagged by the above automated procedure in descending order of relative disagreement, counting
\mathsf{NA}
’s as the highest disagreement.
Second, physicians screened the score questions
q
to identify those matching their clinical specialty. Third, we selected 50 instances from the sorted list, assigning cases to reviewers based on domain alignment to ensure that every instance was validated by an expert in that specific calculator type. For each
(C,q)
they review, a physician saw only
(C,q)
and were asked to compute the score manually, without seeing either
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
or
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
(a single-blind design); physicians were allowed to label an instance as
\mathsf{NA}
if they considered
(C,q)
clinically unanswerable / a mismatch.
Each of the 50 instances was labeled once by one of the three physicians, which we treat as the gold-standard answer
y^{\star}
. We then quantify the relative deviation of
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
and
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
against
y^{\star}
by computing two metrics: (i) sMAPE on the instances a physician deemed answerable where sMAPE is short for symmetric Mean Absolute Percentage Error
(
3
)
, defined as

\text{sMAPE}(y^{*},\hat{y})=\frac{100\%}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{2\times|y^{*}_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}|}{|y^{*}_{i}|+|\hat{y}_{i}|}\,,

and (ii) count of “agreement” that counts, across all 50 instances, how often each synthetic label matches
y^{\star}
within a clinically reasonable threshold, defined as
\pm 5\%
for real numbers, exact match if
y^{*}=\mathsf{NA}
, or
\pm 1
for ordinal numbers less than 20.

3.2.2
Phase 2 Qualitative Analysis

Single-blind physician recomputations suggest that a substantial share of label disagreement is driven not by arithmetic mistakes, but by
task underspecification
: many instances do not contain enough contextual qualifiers to define a unique clinical “ground truth” score. Two patterns recur. First, the benchmark rarely specifies a scoring
timepoint
. Many notes describe multi-visit trajectories over weeks or months, but the question does not clarify whether the score should be computed at presentation, peak severity, discharge, or another reference time (e.g. rows 138, 334, 341, 509, 569, 739, 761, 785). Second, scoring rules can vary across time and jurisdiction, so the “official” aggregation rule (our
g_{\phi}
) is not uniquely describable by a static benchmark label. For example, Morphine Milligram Equivalent (MME) conversions for methadone depend on which guideline is assumed: pre-2023 CDC guidance used a tiered rule, i.e. a multiplier of 12 for methadone doses
>60
mg/day
(
14
)
, whereas the current CDC guideline adopts a flatter multiplier (4.7) while cautioning that conversion becomes unreliable at higher doses
(
19
)
; some state-level guidance still reflects the earlier schedule
(
41
;
60
)
. Under either ambiguity, multiple defensible answers exist, so a single numeric gold label does not cleanly measure an LLM’s generalizable clinical capability.

Beyond underspecification, physician review also surfaced outright
(C,q)
mismatches that induce a
distribution shift
: the patient context falls outside the population for which the calculator was derived or validated. A clear example is row 328, where the question asks for maintenance fluids for a 1.1 kg cat, despite the underlying dosing heuristic being estimated on humans. In such cases, forcing computation produces a clinically meaningless quantity. These instances are better treated as abstentions (NA) or excluded from accuracy-based evaluation, and the benchmark prompt should explicitly allow safe non-response when
(C,q)
is clinically incompatible.

Practically, these failure modes motivate adding metadata (timepoint + guideline/version) and expanding the label space to include abstention.

4
When Labels Become Rewards and a Yardstick: A Controlled Study of Label Choice in Model Alignment

§
3
argued that MedCalc-Bench’s original labels are often misaligned with clinical reality and introduced a relabeled version with closer agreement to physicians. Because this benchmark is also used to train LLMs,
label alignment
is not only an evaluation concern: it directly shapes the behavior of next-generation models that are optimized to match these labels.

The primary goal of this section is to investigate whether the label discrepancies documented above have a meaningful impact on downstream model training, or whether such noise is effectively “washed out” by the strength of signal in the data. This is not a foregone conclusion: when training on thousands of examples, one might expect that moderate label noise would be absorbed and have little effect on final model accuracy. Conversely, if label choice does materially affect trained model performance, this would underscore the practical importance of benchmark maintenance efforts like ours.

To test this, we view model alignment as a reinforcement learning (RL) stage in which a base model is iteratively updated to maximize a reward defined with respect to benchmark labels. Changing from MedCalc’s original labels
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
to our corrected labels
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
is therefore equivalent to changing the reward function and, by extension, the model’s implicit alignment objective. We run a controlled comparison study in which the same RL training pipeline is applied to Qwen3-8B with only the reward labels varied, holding all other factors constant. Crucially, in our experimental design, both the training rewards and the evaluation metric are defined with respect to the corrected labels
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
. This means the comparison is not strictly apples-to-apples if one believes the two label sets differ meaningfully. However, this is precisely the point: if
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
and
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
were interchangeable up to inconsequential noise, we would expect both training conditions to yield similar test accuracy. A substantial performance gap would rule out this null hypothesis and demonstrate that the label discrepancies have real downstream consequences.

We emphasize that our goal is
not
to claim state-of-the-art benchmark performance. Rather, we seek to establish that label quality has tangible consequences for model alignment. That said, if one accepts the evidence from §
3
that the corrected labels more closely reflect clinical ground truth, then a model trained on these labels would, in principle, be better aligned with physician judgment. We consider this a secondary implication and leave more rigorous validation of this claim—including broader physician evaluation of model outputs—to future work. The primary contribution of this section is simply to demonstrate that the choice of benchmark labels is not merely a measurement detail, but a consequential design decision that shapes the models we build.

4.1
Model alignment as policy optimization

We adopt a standard sequential decision-making formalism for model alignment, following the RL literature on policy-gradient methods and recent work on LLM post-training.

In this view, the interaction between an LLM agent and its digital environment is a Markov decision process (MDP)

(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},R,P,\mu)
: at each discrete time
t=1,\dots,T
the agent observes a state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
, samples an action
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
from a stochastic policy
\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
, receives a reward
r_{t}=R(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})
, and the environment transitions to
s_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid s_{t},a_{t})
. A trajectory (i.e. a token sequence for an LLM) is

\tau\;=\;(s_{1},a_{1},s_{2},a_{2},\dots,s_{T},a_{T},s_{T+1}),

with initial state
s_{1}\sim\mu
. The policy objective is to maximize the expected return

J(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_{\theta}(\tau)}[R(\tau)]\;=\;\int p_{\theta}(\tau)R(\tau)\,d\tau,\quad R(\tau)\;=\;\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t},

(1)

where
p_{\theta}(\tau)
is the trajectory distribution induced by
\pi_{\theta}
and the MDP dynamics.

Instantiating the MDP for MedCalc-Bench.

For an LLM, the state
s_{t}
is the current token sequence cached in the GPU memory (prompt, EHR snippet, and previously generated tokens), the action
a_{t}
is the next token, and the transition is deterministic:
s_{t+1}=(s_{t},a_{t})
. The initial state
s_{1}\sim\mu
corresponds to a prompt i.e.
(C,q)
randomly sampled from the set of MedCalc-Bench instances. In classical MDP formulations, returns are often written as a sum of stepwise rewards like the second equation in (
1
). However, in LLM post-training a simpler,
outcome-based
interpretation is more often assumed. Given an input
(C,q):=x
and a final answer
y(\tau)
parsed from a completed text trajectory
\tau
, we set
r_{t}=0
for all
t<T
such that the trajectory-level reward becomes

R(\tau)\;=\;r_{T}\;:=\;\underbrace{\lambda_{\mathrm{f}}\,\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\,\hat{y}(\tau)\text{ is parsable}\,\}}_{\text{format reward}}\;+\;(1-\lambda_{\mathrm{f}})\,\cdot\underbrace{\mathbf{1}\{\,\operatorname{rel\_err}(\hat{y}(\tau),y^{*})\leq\epsilon\,\}}_{\text{answer reward}}

(2)

that combines correctness of the computed score and adherence to the required output format for regex parsability, evaluated against a gold label
y^{*}
(surrogated by either
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
or
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
). In this abstraction, varying
y^{*}
’s value corresponds exactly to changing the reward function
R
in the MDP.

Policy gradient and REINFORCE.

To optimize (
1
), we differentiate under the integral and apply the log-derivative identity
\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)=p_{\theta}(x)\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(x)
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)\;=\;\int\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(\tau)\,R(\tau)\,d\tau\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_{\theta}(\tau)}\!\left[R(\tau)\,\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(\tau)\right].

Because
p_{\theta}(\tau)
factors as

p_{\theta}(\tau)=\mu(s_{1})\prod_{t=1}^{T}\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\,P(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{t}),

we obtain the vanilla REINFORCE policy gradient

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_{\theta}(\tau)}\left[R(\tau)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\right].

(3)

A standard variance-reduction trick is to subtract a zero-mean baseline
b
that does not depend on the realized value of
\tau
:

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_{\theta}(\tau)}\left[\big(R(\tau)-b\big)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\right].

(4)

This is the
REINFORCE with baseline
estimator in the trajectory-level reward setting we use: every token on a trajectory shares the same scalar credit signal
R(\tau)-b
.

Group-relative policy optimization (GRPO).

GRPO specializes (
4
) by using a
group-relative
baseline at the level of each initial state
s_{1}
(think a
(C,q)
tuple in MedCalc-Bench). Fix a group size
G
(e.g.
8
), then for every
s_{1}
we sample
G
trajectories

\{\tau^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{G}
under the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
, compute their trajectory-level rewards
R^{(i)}=R(\tau^{(i)})
, form the group average
\bar{R}
and define advantages
A^{(i)}

\bar{R}\;=\;\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}R^{(i)},\quad A^{(i)}\;=\;R^{(i)}-\bar{R},

(5)

so that trajectory samples with above-average reward receive positive credit and those with below-average reward receive negative credit, finally back-propagated into LLM weight updates.

In our simplified on-policy implementation, the GRPO loss for updating
\theta
is

L_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=-\,\mathbb{E}_{\{\tau^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{1})}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{G}A^{(i)}\sum_{t=1}^{T^{(i)}}\log\pi_{\theta}\!\big(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid s_{t}^{(i)}\big)\right]\;+\;\beta\,\cdot{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\middle\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right),

(6)

where
T^{(i)}
is the length of trajectory
\tau^{(i)}
,
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
is a fixed reference policy, and
\beta>0
controls the strength of KL regularization. Minimizing (
6
) corresponds to a REINFORCE-style policy update that (i) increases the log-probability of completions whose reward is higher than their group mean and (ii) keeps the updated policy close to
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
to avoid catastrophic forgetting.

In summary, under this MDP and GRPO formalism, instantiating the benchmark labels
y^{*}
by
\hat{y}_{\mathrm{original}}
or
\hat{y}_{\mathrm{new}}
shapes the reward assignment
R(\tau)
in (
2
), and therefore the advantages
A^{(i)}
in (
5
). The rest of the policy-optimization pipeline is held fixed, so any difference in the fine-tuned
\pi_{\theta}
’s test accuracy can be attributed to the choice of labels used to define the alignment objective.

4.2
Controlled Study Setup

Goal and overall design.

Our goal is to isolate the causal effect of label choice on an LLM’s model alignment when RL is used. To this end, we run two GRPO training experiments on the same base model that are identical in all respects except for the labels used to define the training reward. In the
original-label
arm, training rewards (as assigned by (
2
)) are computed against MedCalc-Bench’s original labels
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
; in the
new-label
arm, training rewards are computed against our corrected labels
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
. At test time, the 0-1 accuracy of both models are always graded against
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
, which our Phase 2 study (§
3.2.1
) found to be more clinically accurate than
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
.

Dataset and label conditions.

Both RL runs are trained on the same subset of
4{,}593
MedCalc-Bench train instances for which Pipeline 2 produced high-confidence
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
labels. The held-out test set consists of
887
instances whose
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
labels were also obtained from Pipeline 2 and partially audited by physicians. In both arms, the policy LLM sees exactly the same collection of prompts and calculator types; the only difference is whether the training reward’s
y^{*}
is instantiated using
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
or
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
. The underlying system and user prompts, including chain-of-thought and answer-format instructions, are held fixed across runs; we provide the full prompt template in the Appendix.

Policy initialization and interaction environment.

In both arms, the trainable policy
\pi_{\theta}
and the frozen reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
are initialized from the same Qwen3-8B base model
(
48
)
, chosen as a widely used open model for academic post-training.
\pi_{\text{ref}}
, being used for KL regularization, remains fixed throughout training, while
\pi_{\theta}
is updated iteratively by gradient descent to minimize the GRPO loss in Eq.
6
. We use the same updated system prompt (which allows for abstention) and require chain-of-thought style completions with a final scalar answer token; no external tools are used during RL training, and the interaction is purely text-based.

Key training hyperparameters.

We instantiate the policy objective from §
4.1
using the trajectory-level reward
r(\tau)
in Eq.
2
with
\lambda_{f}=0.1
. For the comparison experiment in §
4.3
, both runs share the same optimizer and hyperparameters: Adam with learning rate
\text{lr}=10^{-5}
, KL regularization coefficient
\beta=10^{-3}
, and no reward standardization or length normalization to ensure advantage estimation is unbiased
(
38
)
. Each GRPO minibatch consists of

5
Discussion

In this work, we showed that a widely used, mostly LLM-labeled clinical computation benchmark has between one-quarter and one-third of test instances that appear mislabeled, affecting both measured performance and downstream model alignment. Our formal analysis and empirical audits explain how the original data-generating process for MedCalc-Bench’s ground truth causes systematic output errors. The physician-in-the-loop system, instantiated via two automated pipelines, identifies substantially more likely label errors per unit of physician time than uniform sampling, and yields revised labels that physicians judge as more clinically aligned.

Our controlled RL experiment was designed not to claim superior benchmark performance, but to test whether label discrepancies have meaningful downstream consequences or are simply absorbed as noise during training. The substantial accuracy gap we observe (8.7%) rules out the latter hypothesis, demonstrating that benchmark label quality is not merely a measurement detail but a consequential factor in model alignment. If one accepts the evidence from §
3
that the corrected labels better reflect clinical ground truth, then it follows that models trained on these labels would be better aligned with physician judgment—but we consider this a secondary implication and leave rigorous validation to future work.

Together, these findings suggest that clinical benchmarks built with partial LLM inputs should be treated as
living documents
, whose labels co-evolve with both physician feedback and model capabilities. In safety-critical domains, such ongoing maintenance is not optional: benchmarks must be revisited as clinical practice and model capabilities change.

5.1
Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the human expert reference used to validate label alignment is high quality but small. Only three physicians from a single healthcare system participated in the blinded review, and they independently computed labels for
50
contentious test instances. While these labels provide a valuable sanity check that our relabeled
\hat{y}_{\textsf{new}}
’s are much closer to expert judgment than the original
\hat{y}_{\textsf{original}}
’s, a larger panel of physician experts, with the same instances receiving multiple independent labels, would be needed to robustly quantify systematic variability.

Second, our automated pipelines depend on a particular family of LLM verifiers (Gemini-2.5-Pro with search and tools) instantiated with a fixed prompting and tool-use strategy. If Gemini’s failure modes are correlated with those of GPT-4 (used in the original MedCalc-Bench labels) or with the downstream models we wish to evaluate, some systematic error patterns may never be surfaced. Our estimates of the mislabel rate are therefore conditional on this specific verifier.

Third, our automated label verification has incomplete coverage. Pipeline 2 produces high-confidence labels for 85% of MedCalc’s test set and 89% of the sampled train set; the remaining instances where the pipeline fails to reach a supermajority are plausibly the hardest cases, and may be precisely those where physician input matters most. We exclude these deferred instances from our main analyses, meaning that both the estimated mislabel rate and the measured effect of label choice on RL alignment are computed on a more auditor-tractable subset.

Finally, our experiments focus on a narrow but important slice of clinical AI: MDCalc-style risk and severity scores derived from English-language case reports. Real-world EHRs are substantially noisier, often interleaving templated text, missing or conflicting entries, and non-English content. We also instantiate our RL study with a single base model (Qwen3-8B) and one algorithm (GRPO). Although our sensitivity analysis suggests the relative benefit of aligned labels is robust across hyperparameter settings, we do not claim that the magnitude of the effect will transfer unchanged to other architectures or task families.

5.2
Future Work

A central lesson from our study is that the real bottleneck in benchmark maintenance is not computation, but carefully allocated human attention. GPU hours are elastic; physicians’ hours are not. This naturally leads to a design question: given a fixed budget of expert feedback, how should we allocate human evaluation to learn as much as possible about benchmark quality?

Conceptually, there is a latent error structure describing where labels are wrong and which calculators are fragile, and each physician review is a costly observation about that structure. Future work could formalize benchmark maintenance as choosing queries that maximize expected information gain about this latent structure per unit of expert time. Our two-pipeline system can be viewed as a heuristic approximation: LLM auditors concentrate mass on contentious cases, and physicians act as high-leverage adjudicators rather than bulk labelers. The same information-efficient perspective applies upstream to benchmark
creation
—recent work such as GDPval
(
47
)
illustrates how task selection and expert effort allocation shape benchmark design in several domains. Coupling explicit uncertainty models over labels with active-learning or bandit-style policies for expert review is a promising direction we leave to future work.
