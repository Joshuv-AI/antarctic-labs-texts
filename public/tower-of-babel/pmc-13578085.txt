Experiences and coping strategies of time toxicity in young and middle-aged patients with lymphoma: a descriptive qualitative study
Authors: Danyang Li, Kexin Zheng, Lizhen Chen, Meiyun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith advancements in lymphoma treatment leading to improved survival rates, the time burden associated with cancer care has emerged as a significant concern. Time toxicity can disrupt daily life and impact treatment decisions; however, lymphoma-specific evidence regarding patients’ subjective experiences remains limited. This study aimed to investigate the experiences of time toxicity and the coping strategies employed by young and middle-aged lymphoma patients in China.MethodsThis descriptive qualitative study took place from June to December 2025 in the haematology department of a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou, China. We employed purposive sampling with maximum variation to recruit patients aged 18–50 years who were undergoing lymphoma treatment. The study was informed by Time-Geography Theory. Data collection involved face-to-face semi-structured interviews, and the data were analysed using conventional content analysis.ResultsThe study involved fifteen participants, revealing five interconnected themes: (1) the multidimensional time burden driven by treatment; (2) the disruption of social roles and restructuring of daily life rhythms; (3) healthcare mobility constrained by time and space; (4) emotional distress associated with time toxicity; and (5) multilevel coping strategies for managing time toxicity. Time toxicity manifested not only as the cumulative time spent on healthcare activities but also as disruptions to daily routines, role functioning, healthcare mobility, and psychological well-being. Participants reported substantial time burdens due to frequent hospital visits, prolonged waiting times, and complex care pathways, particularly for those requiring cross-city care. Despite these challenges, patients employed various coping strategies, including active time management, Health-oriented self-management, cognitive adjustment, and family support.ConclusionsTime toxicity in young and middle-aged lymphoma patients is a multidimensional and contextually embedded experience shaped by the interaction between healthcare demands and everyday life. These findings highlight the importance of considering patients’ temporal and spatial circumstances when coordinating lymphoma care and developing supportive interventions.Keywords: Time toxicity, Lymphoma, Time geography, Coping strategies, Qualitative research

Background

Lymphoma, a diverse group of malignancies originating from lymphocytes and lymphoid tissues, is broadly classified into Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) [1]. In 2022, lymphoma constituted approximately 3.2% of all cancer diagnoses [2] worldwide, with its incidence and mortality rates continuing to rise, presenting an escalating public health challenge [3]. In China, lymphoma is the most common haematological malignancy, with incidence and mortality rates increasing [4]. Advances in treatment, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, have improved survival outcomes for patients with lymphoma [5]. However, as survival rates increase, there is growing concern about the broader impact of treatment on patients’ daily lives. In this context, time toxicity has emerged as a crucial yet underexplored aspect of cancer care, increasingly recognised as a significant factor influencing patients’ treatment decisions [5].

Time toxicity refers to the considerable time patients dedicate to cancer-related care, encompassing frequent medical appointments, hospital visits, treatments, caregiving, and recovery. This cumulative time burden can profoundly disrupt patients’ daily lives and adversely affect their well-being. Emerging evidence indicates that, in certain instances, the time costs of cancer treatment may outweigh the survival benefits [6, 7] and are linked to increased healthcare utilisation, reduced productivity, and heightened psychosocial and economic burdens [7]. Previous studies have explored factors influencing time toxicity, such as disease characteristics, treatment modalities, patient demographics, and healthcare system efficiency [8–11]. Interventions like decentralised care and telemedicine have been proposed to alleviate this time burden [12, 13].

Despite this growing attention, previous research has commonly operationalised time toxicity using quantitative measures such as healthcare contact days, treatment visits, and time spent receiving care. In lymphoma, analyses of the CCTG LY.12 trial demonstrated that treatment regimens with broadly comparable oncologic outcomes could differ in healthcare contact days and home days, highlighting time use as a potentially relevant consideration in treatment decision-making [14]. Similarly, a nationwide Japanese database study showed that the time toxicity associated with R-CHOP is influenced by treatment setting, supportive care, and patient-related factors [15]. More recently, lymphoma-specific studies of bispecific antibody therapy have broadened the assessment of time toxicity to include travel, care coordination, recovery time, daily activities, and treatment preferences [16, 17].

Qualitative evidence is also emerging. A recent concept-elicitation study among patients with relapsed/refractory non-Hodgkin lymphoma and haematologists identified patient-, disease-, treatment-, and visit-related factors as important components of time toxicity [17]. Beyond lymphoma, qualitative studies conducted in the USA, Australia, and China have shown that cancer-related time burdens can extend beyond healthcare encounters to disrupt daily routines, work and family responsibilities, caregiver roles, and psychosocial well-being [18–20]. However, lymphoma-specific qualitative evidence remains limited and has largely focused on relapsed/refractory disease and specific treatment contexts such as bispecific antibody therapy. Less is known about how young and middle-aged patients undergoing diverse lymphoma treatments experience and negotiate time toxicity across work, family, healthcare mobility, emotional well-being, and everyday life, particularly within the Chinese healthcare context.

Against this background, this study employed a descriptive qualitative design informed by Time-Geography Theory [21] to explore the experiences of time toxicity and coping strategies among young and middle-aged patients with lymphoma in China. By examining how treatment demands interact with temporal, spatial, and social constraints, this study seeks to provide contextually grounded evidence to inform patient-centred nursing care.

Methods

Design

A descriptive qualitative design was employed to investigate the experiences of time toxicity and coping strategies among young and middle-aged lymphoma patients undergoing treatment. This method facilitated a comprehensive understanding of participants’ perceptions and interpretations of time-related challenges in the context of their daily lives. The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [22].

Theoretical framework and its application

Time-Geography Theory [21], developed by Hägerstrand, explains how everyday activities are shaped by temporal and spatial conditions. It identifies three key constraints: capability constraints, related to individuals’ physical or biological capacities; coupling constraints, requiring presence or interaction at particular times and places; and authority constraints, arising from institutional or organisational restrictions on access to time and space.

In this study, the theory served as a sensitising framework. It informed the interview guide by directing attention to daily schedules, healthcare-related mobility, competing work and family responsibilities, and restrictions on patients’ use of time and space. Data analysis remained inductive, with themes and subthemes generated through conventional content analysis rather than predefined theoretical categories. The theory was subsequently used as an interpretive lens to understand the interactions among temporal demands, spatial mobility, physical capacity, and healthcare-system constraints.

Participants

This qualitative study took place from June to December 2025 in the haematology department of a tertiary Grade A hospital in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China. Participants were selected through purposive sampling with maximum variation to ensure a diverse representation in terms of age, employment status, family circumstances, place of residence, lymphoma subtype, disease stage, treatment modality, economic status, and type of medical insurance.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants comprised young and middle-aged adults aged 18 to 50 years who had been diagnosed with lymphoma and were undergoing treatment. They needed to be capable of communicating their experiences and willing to participate with written informed consent. Patients were excluded if they were too unwell to engage in interviews, suffered from severe dysfunction of major organs that hindered cooperation, or faced barriers to verbal or written communication.

Data collection and management

Data collection employed face-to-face semi-structured interviews, guided by an interview framework collaboratively developed by the research team. The framework was informed by Time-Geography Theory [21], the study objectives, and relevant literature, with attention to patients’ time arrangements, healthcare mobility, and competing life responsibilities. After pilot interviews with two patients, the interview guide was refined and finalised (Table 1).

Interview guide

LDY conducted all interviews, having completed a specialised training course and possessing over 17 years of clinical experience in haematology-oncology nursing, alongside prior qualitative research experience. The interviews took place in a consultation room at a prearranged time. Before each session, participants were informed of the study’s purpose and provided written informed consent. Each participant engaged in a single interview.

All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ informed consent, ensuring that all data remained anonymised. Field notes were taken during the interviews to capture non-verbal cues such as physical movements, vocal tone, and facial expressions. The sample size adhered to the principle of code saturation. Sampling continued until code saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new codes relevant to the study objectives emerged [23]. Code saturation was reached by the 13th interview, and two additional interviews were conducted to confirm saturation. Interviews lasted 25–58 min, with a total interview duration of 546 min.

Data analysis

Data collection and analysis occurred simultaneously, employing conventional content analysis as outlined by Hsieh and Shannon [24]. Time-Geography Theory [21] was not used to generate a priori codes or categories; instead, codes and categories emerged inductively from the data. To ensure thorough immersion in the data, interview transcripts and reflective notes were read multiple times. Meaning units were identified, condensed, and initially coded through open coding. Similar codes were then compared and organised into subthemes and themes. The final thematic structure was refined by elucidating relationships across participants’ accounts.

Data collection concluded upon reaching saturation, as no new information emerged from further interviews. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim into Word documents within 24 h by two researchers, LDY and ZKX, who also cross-referenced these transcripts with field notes to ensure completeness and accuracy. Three researchers, LDY, ZKX, and CLZ, independently engaged in coding. Notably, ZKX and CLZ were haematology nurses with prior qualitative research experience. The research team reviewed all themes, resolving any analytical discrepancies through discussion until consensus was achieved. Participants consented to be re-contacted if clarification was required.

Ethical Considerations

All research procedures adhered strictly to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, approved this study (Approval No. 2025B-0583).

Rigour and reflexivity

To ensure rigour, various strategies were employed to bolster credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. Data collection and analysis proceeded simultaneously until saturation was achieved. Three researchers independently coded the data, resolving any discrepancies through discussion until consensus was reached. Reflective and field notes facilitated reflexivity by identifying potential biases stemming from the interviewer’s dual role as both nurse and researcher. The findings were grounded in participants’ accounts to ensure authenticity. Maximum variation sampling and detailed contextual descriptions enhanced transferability. Collectively, these strategies reinforced the trustworthiness of the findings.

The multidimensional time burden driven by treatment

Participants consistently reported that medical treatment dominated their daily schedules. Regular hospital visits, numerous medical tasks, and ongoing treatment cycles consumed significant portions of their time. Additionally, extended waiting periods and intricate processes further increased their time within the healthcare system. These demands disrupted daily routines, leaving less time for rest, recovery, and personal activities.

Frequent healthcare visits and fragmented medical tasks

Participants frequently indicated that the treatment process necessitated regular hospital visits for various medical procedures, such as blood tests, catheter maintenance, and injections to manage side effects. These tasks often recurred several times a week, compelling patients to travel repeatedly between hospitals and clinics. Consequently, their daily routines were often disrupted by treatment-related activities.

“Pre-admission tests and hospital stays typically last approximately one week, while follow-up appointments generally require about two days each week.” (P3)

“Following chemotherapy, I must visit the local hospital twice weekly for blood tests. Should my white blood cell count be low, I am required to attend a tertiary hospital for injections. Consequently, I spend several days each week travelling between hospitals.” (P4)

“Following chemotherapy, I require weekly care for my PICC line and regular blood tests. Should my white blood cell count be low, I need daily injections for several consecutive days.” (P8)

“I must visit the hospital every few days for blood tests, injections to boost white blood cells, and follow-up appointments. It seems as though my life centres around the hospital.” (P9)

Hidden time costs driven by waiting and healthcare process friction

Participants often noted extended waiting times and intricate clinical procedures, which lengthened their hospital visits. Despite the brevity of actual medical consultations or treatments, patients indicated that registration, queuing, waiting for examinations, and delays in obtaining test results consumed a significant part of their time at the hospital. These waiting periods and disjointed clinical processes imposed additional time burdens during their treatment.

“Occasionally, I find myself waiting for several hours, yet the actual consultation lasts merely a few minutes. The waiting is the most draining aspect.” (P8)

“From morning to evening, nearly the entire day is occupied with queuing and waiting at the hospital.” (P11)

“When I developed a fever, I initially visited the fever clinic for tests, including those for COVID-19 and influenza. The results took approximately two hours to be processed. Once infectious diseases were excluded, I was referred to the emergency department for further treatment.” (P13)

Continuous treatment cycles and insufficient recovery time

Several participants noted that treatment cycles were continuous, frequently overlapping with the period required for physical recovery. They often perceived the interval between treatment rounds as insufficient for patients to fully regain their strength. Consequently, patients reported commencing the subsequent treatment phase before completely recovering from the previous one.

“Even before my body has recuperated from the previous treatment, the next round is already arranged.” (P1)

“The entire treatment cycle is lengthy, necessitating a recovery period following each round.” (P2)

Complex care pathways shaped by treatment uncertainty and complications

Several participants recounted how uncertainties in treatment, evolving clinical conditions, and complications led to increasingly complex treatment pathways. Alterations in treatment plans, referrals to various departments or hospital sites, and unforeseen medical events frequently required additional consultations, diagnostic procedures, and hospital visits. These processes prolonged the time spent navigating the healthcare system and heightened the overall time burden during treatment.

Participant 9 noted that their treatment plan was abruptly altered following the PET-CT results, necessitating multiple visits to various hospital locations.

“Due to the port infection, I needed frequent dressing changes and ultimately underwent another surgery.” (P9)

“Following chemotherapy, I experienced a fever and acute gastrointestinal symptoms, necessitating another hospital admission.” (P12)

“Because of my fever, I needed to undergo several additional steps before receiving treatment. These included visiting the fever clinic for screening and waiting for the test results.” (P13)

The disruption of social roles and restructuring of daily life rhythms

The treatment of the disease significantly disrupted participants’ social roles and altered their daily routines. Individuals indicated that they had to pause their occupational duties, renegotiate family responsibilities, and withdraw from social activities. As time progressed, managing the disease began to dictate the structure and priorities of their daily lives, resulting in a fundamental reorganisation of time, roles, and routines centred around therapeutic requirements.

Career disruption accompanied by financial and developmental toxicity

Participants faced notable career interruptions due to frequent treatment schedules and cancer-related fatigue, which required them to take extended leave or completely stop working. These disruptions arose during crucial stages of their careers, leading to missed professional opportunities, a loss of occupational identity, and increased financial strain. The combined burden of developmental stagnation and economic insecurity heightened psychological distress, underscoring the interplay of temporal, financial, and emotional challenges within this group.

“Despite having insurance, the out-of-pocket expenses have continued to accumulate. My illness has undermined our financial security.” (P1)

“Due to the illness, I missed a very important promotion opportunity.” (P3)

“In the fashion industry, remaining relevant requires daily immersion. Without this, I risk losing both income and professional value.” (P4)

“Sick leave pay constitutes merely a fraction of my salary, and we have depleted all our savings. The financial strain surpasses even the stress caused by the illness.” (P6)

“It occurred just as my career was gaining momentum, and then everything abruptly stopped.” (P12)

Redistribution of family responsibilities and reorganisation of caregiving roles

Participants indicated that the physical demands of treatment and the necessity for frequent hospital visits diminished their capacity to meet prior family obligations. Consequently, family members adapted their schedules and assumed extra caregiving responsibilities, such as childcare, household chores, accompanying patients to hospital appointments, and offering emotional support.

“There are numerous tasks I am no longer able to perform. My husband now manages the household chores and collects our child from school.” (P4)

“My parents often stay with me and assist with my daily life.” (P9)

Compression of social time and shrinking life space

Participants reported a significant decline in social activities during treatment. Owing to weakened immunity, physical limitations, and fears of infection, many patients avoided crowded areas and curtailed social interactions. Consequently, their daily activities became more restricted, confining their life primarily to the hospital and home.

“I hardly go out anymore. I avoid places where there are many people.” (P8)

“There is virtually no social life. When my blood counts are low, going out becomes impossible.” (P3)

Reprioritisation of life around treatment

As the treatment advanced, managing the disease emerged as the primary organising principle in the participants’ daily lives. Medical appointments and health-related considerations dictated most decisions, prompting a deliberate reprioritisation of activities according to their perceived benefit or risk to recovery.

“My life is now entirely centred on medical treatment. I lack the energy for other activities and must continually assess whether any action will benefit or worsen my condition.” (P9)

Healthcare mobility constrained by time and space

Participants highlighted that, beyond the time required for medical treatment, geographical distance, transportation conditions, and treatment-induced physical weakness imposed further time–space constraints. These elements significantly affected patients’ healthcare mobility, influencing their travel to and from hospitals during treatment.

Cross-city healthcare and multi-site medical navigation

Several participants indicated that they travelled considerable distances to access specialised medical care. Often, they had to utilise various modes of transport and occasionally visit multiple hospitals in a brief timeframe. These journeys frequently resulted in extended travel times and heightened physical exhaustion.

“Travelling from Taizhou to the hospital typically requires approximately three hours, encompassing both a high-speed train journey and a taxi ride.” (P3)

“The journey alone exceeds three hours each way, leaving me feeling exhausted upon reaching the hospital.” (P9)

“Occasionally, I find myself travelling between multiple hospitals within the same day.” (P11)

Urban transportation friction and spatial barriers

Participants reported challenges associated with urban transportation. Traffic congestion, parking difficulties, and peak travel periods frequently extended their commuting time when accessing medical care.

“Finding a parking space can be even harder than seeing the doctor.” (P4)

“Despite the short distance, traffic congestion often extends the journey to over an hour.” (P8)

Double burden of physical weakness and travel demands

Treatment-related physical symptoms significantly complicated patients’ travel experiences. Participants noted that fatigue, dizziness, and other side effects of chemotherapy rendered long-distance travel particularly challenging during treatment.

“After chemotherapy I could barely stand, let alone travel a long distance.” (P3)

“On one occasion, I fainted on my way home, and since that incident, I’ve been apprehensive about going out.” (P12)

Mobility restriction driven by infection risk

Concerns regarding infection during treatment have influenced patients’ travel decisions. Several participants have indicated that they avoid crowded transport or reduce the frequency of long-distance travel to minimise potential infection risks.

“I worry about getting an infection from going to the hospital every week to maintain my PICC line because it’s so crowded there.” (P2)

“Throughout the six treatment cycles, I returned home merely three or four times. Predominantly, I resided in a hotel close to the hospital, fearing the risk of infection from travelling on the high-speed train amidst large crowds.” (P3)

Economic burden associated with healthcare mobility

Participants noted an increased financial burden stemming from frequent travel. Owing to physical weakness and concerns about infections, many opted for taxis over public transport, resulting in additional expenses.

“I have to take a taxi every time, and the cost adds up.” (P14)

Emotional distress associated with time toxicity

Participants reported various emotional challenges throughout the treatment process. Regular hospital visits, extended treatment cycles, and interruptions to daily routines consistently evoked feelings of anxiety and uncertainty. Moreover, alterations in work, social engagement, and life paths caused some patients to feel socially disconnected during their treatment.

Anxiety related to treatment uncertainty

Numerous participants expressed anxiety regarding the uncertain duration and effectiveness of their treatment. The anticipation of examination results and the necessity of frequent hospital visits further exacerbated their emotional stress.

“I frequently ponder the duration and efficacy of this treatment.” (P14)

“Every time the blood test results come out, I feel very nervous.” (P6)

Social disconnection during treatment

Participants reported feeling disconnected from their former social roles, as the demands of treatment restricted their involvement in work and social activities. This disruption led to a sense of being out of sync with normal life trajectories and diminished social engagement.

“During treatment, I clearly feel disconnected from society.” (P3)

“Everyone else is working, but I can only stay at home.” (P4)

Emotional exhaustion and irritability

Extended treatment requirements, frequent waiting, and continuous physical fatigue led to cumulative emotional exhaustion. Participants noted diminished emotional resilience and heightened irritability, especially during prolonged waiting periods or persistent discomfort, illustrating the compounding impact of sustained time demands.

“After waiting for a long time and feeling exhausted, I become very irritable.” (P12)

“Prolonged waiting and frequent trips to the hospital can be emotionally exhausting.” (P13)

Multilevel coping strategies for managing time toxicity

Despite the significant time burden imposed by treatment, participants reported employing various strategies to manage these demands and maintain their daily routines. These strategies functioned on multiple levels, encompassing individual time management, Health-oriented self-management, cognitive adjustments, and support from family members.

Active time management and optimisation of healthcare processes

Several participants reported that they actively planned their medical appointments and utilised digital healthcare services to minimise unnecessary travel and waiting times. By scheduling appointments in advance, communicating with physicians online, and arranging examinations via internet-based hospital platforms, patients enhanced the efficiency of their medical visits.

“For subsequent examinations like PET-CT, I schedule an appointment via the online hospital platform and arrive promptly at the designated time.” (P3)

Participant 5 expressed a preference for planning their schedule ahead of time to avoid unnecessary travel.

“After the blood test, I send the results to my doctor through WeChat.” (P8)

Health-oriented self-management and lifestyle adjustment

Participants indicated that they adjusted their lifestyles and actively monitored their health to more effectively manage the demands associated with treatment. Several reported learning the patterns of blood count fluctuations following chemotherapy. They also modified their sleep and dietary habits and avoided crowded environments to minimise the risk of infection.

“Now, I am aware of when my white blood cell count is expected to decrease following chemotherapy. Consequently, I schedule a blood test earlier and receive injections if necessary.” (P4)

“I avoid crowded places and try to maintain a regular routine. I go to bed earlier and exercise every day.” (P5)

“My diet has become lighter and my daily routine is much more regular now.” (P8)

Cognitive reframing and psychological adjustment

Participants altered their perceptions of illness and time to manage the treatment-related time burden. This adjustment involved both cognitive reframing and daily psychological regulation. For instance, some employed distraction techniques to ease anxiety and divert attention during treatment. Others reported a shift in life priorities, placing greater importance on personal well-being and family relationships.

“I listen to music during the infusion—it helps me relax and takes my mind off things.” (P9)

“This illness made me slow down and rethink my life.” (P10)

“Now I value my time with my family much more.” (P14)

Family-supported redistribution of time and responsibilities

Family members play a crucial role in assisting patients to manage the time burdens associated with treatment. Participants have reported that relatives help with daily tasks, childcare, and hospital visits, enabling patients to concentrate more on their treatment and recovery.

“My family assists with numerous daily tasks, allowing me to focus on my treatment.” (P4)

“My cousin helps look after my child.” (P6)

“My mother came to Hangzhou to cook and help with daily life.” (P7)

Discussion

This study provides a contextualised account of time toxicity among young and middle-aged patients with lymphoma, encompassing treatment-related demands, disruption of social roles, constraints on healthcare mobility, emotional distress, and adaptive coping. These findings are broadly consistent with emerging lymphoma-specific and cross-cancer evidence showing that time toxicity extends beyond treatment administration alone. From a time-geographical perspective, our findings further illustrate how healthcare demands are experienced within patients’ everyday temporal, spatial, family, and social contexts.

The multidimensional time burden driven by treatment

Treatment-related activities occupied substantial portions of participants’ daily schedules, including frequent healthcare visits, fragmented medical tasks, waiting, supportive care, and additional visits arising from complications. These findings are consistent with lymphoma-specific studies showing substantial healthcare contact associated with treatment. In the CCTG LY.12 trial, salvage regimens with comparable oncologic outcomes differed in healthcare contact and home days [14], while a nationwide Japanese study showed that R-CHOP-related time toxicity was influenced by hospitalisation, supportive care, and patient-related factors [15].

Our findings further highlight less visible time costs beyond scheduled treatment, particularly waiting, fragmented care processes, and insufficient recovery time between treatment cycles. Recent patient-reported evidence in relapsed/refractory FL and DLBCL similarly identified travel, healthcare visits, care coordination, and recovery as important components of time burden [16]. Our qualitative findings complement this evidence by illustrating how these cumulative demands disrupt patients’ daily rhythms and compress the time available for recovery.

These findings suggest that reducing time toxicity requires attention not only to treatment schedules but also to potentially modifiable care-process factors, including waiting, fragmented appointments, and coordination of supportive care.

The disruption of social roles and restructuring of daily life rhythms

Time toxicity extended beyond healthcare encounters into participants’ work, family responsibilities, and social participation. Career interruptions were particularly salient in this young and middle-aged population, resulting in lost income, missed professional opportunities, and disrupted career trajectories. This is consistent with lymphoma-specific qualitative evidence showing trade-offs between treatment time and work or other life obligations [17], and with broader qualitative studies from the USA, Australia, and China demonstrating that cancer-related time burdens disrupt daily routines and affect both patients and caregivers [18–20].

Treatment-related time demands were also redistributed within families as relatives assumed childcare, household, and other responsibilities. This relational burden may be incompletely captured by healthcare contact days alone; an economic analysis of the CCTG LY.12 trial found only weak associations between contact days and indirect costs such as lost productivity and informal caregiving [25]. Our findings further illustrate how these time-related burdens intersect with employment and family roles, which may be particularly relevant for young and middle-aged patients. These findings support considering role functioning and family responsibilities when assessing and addressing time toxicity.

Healthcare mobility constrained by time and space

Time toxicity was closely intertwined with the spatial organisation of healthcare. Cross-city care, transportation difficulties, treatment-related physical weakness, and infection concerns collectively increased the time and effort required to access treatment. This is consistent with lymphoma-specific evidence identifying proximity to treatment centres and travel and logistical coordination as important contributors to time toxicity [17]. Recent patient-reported data also showed longer travel times among rural than urban or suburban patients with relapsed/refractory lymphoma [16].

Viewed through a time-geographical lens, our findings further illustrate how geographical distance interacts with physical capacity, transportation conditions, and financial resources to constrain healthcare mobility. Travel time therefore represents more than time in transit; it forms part of a broader time–space burden shaping patients’ healthcare journeys. These findings support coordinated scheduling, telehealth or decentralised supportive care where appropriate, and targeted navigation or transportation support to reduce avoidable time–space burdens.

Emotional distress associated with time toxicity

Anxiety, social disconnection, emotional exhaustion, and irritability emerged as important consequences of time toxicity. This is consistent with recent qualitative studies showing that cancer-related time burdens can cause psychosocial distress, disrupt daily life, and reduce patients’ sense of control [18–20]. Our findings further illustrate how these emotional experiences are linked to temporal conditions, including treatment uncertainty, repeated waiting, limited control over scheduling, and a perceived loss of synchrony with others’ everyday lives. Thus, psychological distress may partly reflect disrupted temporal rhythms and reduced control over personal time.

These findings suggest that approaches to reducing time toxicity should consider emotional as well as logistical consequences, particularly for patients experiencing prolonged treatment or substantial disruption of work and social roles.

Multilevel coping strategies for managing time toxicity

Participants actively used behavioural, health-related, psychological, and family-based strategies to manage time toxicity. This is consistent with qualitative evidence from China showing that patients and family caregivers adopt multiple strategies to cope with cumulative time demands [20]. Our findings similarly show that patients sought to regain control over time through advance scheduling, digital communication, anticipatory health monitoring, cognitive reframing, and redistribution of family responsibilities.

Importantly, patient-led strategies should be complemented by healthcare-system support. Recent qualitative research has proposed tailored scheduling, improved administrative and logistical support, home-based care where feasible, transparent communication about expected time demands, and innovative care models to reduce time toxicity [26]. Taken together, these findings support multilevel approaches involving patients, families, and healthcare systems rather than relying solely on patients to adapt to time-related burdens.

Conclusion

Time toxicity among young and middle-aged patients with lymphoma was experienced not only through time spent receiving healthcare but also through disruptions to daily routines, social roles, healthcare mobility, and emotional well-being. Patients actively adopted behavioural, health-related, psychological, and family-based strategies to manage these time-related burdens. These findings highlight the importance of considering patients’ temporal and spatial circumstances when coordinating lymphoma care and developing supportive interventions. Further research is needed to evaluate strategies for reducing avoidable time burdens across diverse healthcare settings.

Theoretical contribution

Time-Geography Theory provided a sensitising and interpretive lens for understanding how treatment-related time demands interacted with spatial mobility, physical capacity, healthcare-system constraints, and competing life responsibilities. As the analysis remained inductive, the theory interpreted rather than predetermined the findings. This perspective highlights the value of considering temporal and spatial contexts when examining time toxicity.

Nursing implications

Nurses may play an important role in identifying patients with substantial time burdens, particularly those requiring frequent visits, cross-city care, or balancing treatment with work and caregiving responsibilities. Nursing support may include anticipatory information about treatment-related time demands, coordinated scheduling and supportive care, appropriate digital follow-up, and self-management and family support. Nurse-led navigation and care coordination may further reduce avoidable waiting and fragmented visits. Future studies should evaluate the effectiveness and safety of these approaches in reducing patient-reported time burden.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, this single-centre study was conducted in a tertiary hospital in eastern China, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other healthcare settings. Second, although maximum variation sampling was used, participants were limited to adults aged 18–50 years and had heterogeneous lymphoma subtypes, disease stages, and treatments; experiences may differ in other patient populations. Third, the study relied on subjective accounts without objective measures of healthcare contact, travel, waiting, or recovery time. Fourth, single-time-point interviews could not capture changes in time toxicity across the disease and treatment trajectory. Finally, the interviewer’s clinical background may have influenced data collection and interpretation, although reflexive notes, independent coding, and team discussions were used to enhance rigour.

Future directions

Future multicentre, longitudinal, and mixed-methods studies should examine time toxicity across diverse lymphoma populations and integrate patient-reported experiences with objective measures of time use. Further research is also needed to refine and validate patient-centred measures of time toxicity. Interventions that consider patients’ temporal and spatial circumstances should be developed and evaluated for their potential to reduce avoidable time burdens.
