Who Wins the Multi-Structural Game?
Ronald Fagin, Neil Immerman, Phokion Kolaitis, Jonathan Lenchner, Rik Sengupta

Abstract
Combinatorial games played between two players, called Spoiler and Duplicator, have often been used to capture syntactic properties of formal logical languages. For instance, the widely used Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé (EF) game captures the syntactic measure of quantifier rank of first-order formulas. For every such game, there is an associated natural decision problem: “given an instance of the game, does Spoiler win the game on that instance?” For EF games, this problem was shown to be PSPACE-complete by Pezzoli in 1998. In this present paper, we show that the same problem for the multi-structural (MS) games of recent interest is PSPACE-hard, but contained in NEXPTIME. In the process, we also resolve an open problem posed by Pezzoli about the dependence of the hardness results for EF games on the arity of the schema under consideration. Our techniques combine adaptations of Pezzoli’s constructions together with insights from the theory of inapproximability of optimization problems, as well as the recently developed technique of parallel play for MS games.

Introduction

Combinatorial games are one of the main tools for studying the expressive power of logics on classes of structures. In particular, these games are indispensable in the study of logics on classes of finite structures, since the compactness theorem and other basic results of mathematical logic fail in the finite realm. Examples of such combinatorial games include the Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game
[
11
,
7
]
, the pebble game
[
1
,
17
]
, and the existential pebble game
[
21
]
. The Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game captures the quantifier depth of first-order formulas,
and thus it is used to analyze the expressive power of first-order logic. The pebble game captures the number of distinct variables in finite-variable infinitary logic, and thus it is used to analyze the expressive power of these logics,
as well as the expressive power of least fixed-point logic since, on classes of finite structures, the latter is a fragment of finite-variable infinitary logic. Finally, the existential pebble game captures the existential fragment of finite-variable infinitary logic,
and thus it can be used to analyze the expressive power of Datalog on finite structures.
Detailed expositions of these games are in the books
[
13
,
19
,
22
]
.

Each of these games is not a single game, but rather a family of games parameterized by a positive integer. Concretely, Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games are parameterized by the number
m
of moves in the game, while pebble games and existential pebble games are parameterized by the number
k
of pebbles. Furthermore, each such parameterized game is played by two players, called Spoiler and Duplicator, on a pair
({\bf A},{\bf B})
of structures. For each of these games, it can be proved that Duplicator wins the game at hand on
({\bf A},{\bf B})
if and only if the structures
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
cannot be distinguished by a sentence of a certain logic (this is the logic that the game is said to
capture
).

Each of the three games mentioned above gives rise to a natural algorithmic problem; namely, given an input to the game, determine the winner of the game on the given input. In fact, for each fixed schema
\tau
, there are two versions of this algorithmic problem, depending on whether or not the parameter associated with the game is part of the input. Specifically, the Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game gives rise to the following decision problem
WIN(EF)
: given two finite
\tau
-structures
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
, and a positive integer
m
, does Spoiler win the
m
-move Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game on
({\bf A},{\bf B})
? Furthermore, for each fixed
m\geq 1
, the Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game gives rise to the following decision problem
\mbox{{\sc WIN(EF)}}_{m}
: given two
\tau
-structures
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
, does Spoiler win the
m
-move Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game on
({\bf A},{\bf B})
?
In a similar manner, the pebble game gives rise to the decision problems
WIN(Pebble)
and
\mbox{{\sc WIN(Pebble)}}_{k}
, for
k\geq 1
, while the existential pebble game gives rise to the decision problems
WIN(
\exists
Pebble)
and
\mbox{{\sc WIN($\exists$Pebble)}}_{k}
, for
k\geq 1
.

The state of affairs concerning the computational complexity of these decision problems is as follows. For the Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game, the results in
[
11
]
imply that
\mbox{{\sc WIN(EF)}}_{m}
is in
\mathrm{LOGSPACE}
, for each
m\geq 1
. In contrast, Pezzoli
[
23
]
showed that
WIN(EF)
is a
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-complete problem. For the pebble game, Grohe
[
14
]
showed that
\mbox{{\sc WIN(Pebble)}}_{k}
is a
\mathrm{PTIME}
-complete problem, for each
k\geq 2
. The problem
WIN(Pebble)
is in
\mathrm{EXPTIME}
, but its exact computational complexity remains open. Finally, for the existential pebble game, Kolaitis and Panttaja
[
20
]
showed that

\mbox{{\sc WIN($\exists$Pebble)}}_{k}
is a
\mathrm{PTIME}
-complete problem for each
k\geq 2
, while
WIN(
\exists
Pebble)
is an
\mathrm{EXPTIME}
-complete problem.

In this paper, we focus on a different combinatorial game, called the
multi-structural
(
MS
) game. This game was introduced by Immerman
[
16
]
, who showed that the
MS
game captures the number of quantifiers in first-order formulas. There are two main differences between the Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game and the
MS
game: first, the
MS
game is played on a pair
({\cal A},{\cal B})
of
sets
of structures, instead of being played on a pair
({\bf A},{\bf B})
of structures; second, in every round of the
MS
game, Duplicator can make an arbitrary number of copies of structures on the side that she plays in, effectively simulating all possible
responses simultaneously, and only has to exhibit her win on a single pair of structures at the end, one on either side.
Because of these differences,
MS
games turn out to be significantly harder to analyze than
Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé
games. In fact, no substantive progress in the analysis of
MS
games had been made prior to
[
8
]
, which provided the impetus for further investigations of
MS
games, including
[
10
,
3
,
4
]
. Furthermore, several different games that capture the number of quantifiers in first-order formulas (and hence, equivalent to
MS
games) were studied in
[
15
]
.

Here, we investigate the algorithmic problem of determining the winner of the
MS
game. More formally, for each fixed schema
\tau
, we consider the decision problem
WIN(MS)
: given two finite sets
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
of finite
\tau
-structure, and a positive integer
m
, does Spoiler win the
m
-move
MS
game on
({\cal A},{\cal B})
? In addition, for each fixed
m\geq 1
, we consider the decision problem
\mbox{{\sc WIN(MS)}}_{m}
: given two finite sets
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
of finite
\tau
-structure, does Spoiler win the
m
-move
MS
game on
({\cal A},{\cal B})
?

We first observe that, for each fixed
m\geq 1
, the problem
\mbox{{\sc WIN(MS)}}_{m}
is in
\mathrm{LOGSPACE}
(Proposition
5
). We next explore the computational complexity of
WIN(MS)
, and establish an upper bound and a lower bound for this problem. For the upper bound, it is relatively straightforward to show that
WIN(MS)
is in
\mathrm{NEXPTIME}
, i.e.,
WIN(MS)
is solvable in non-deterministic exponential time (Proposition
6
).

Establishing lower bounds for this problem, on the other hand, turns out to be a much more challenging task. Our main result (Theorem
17
) asserts that
WIN(MS)
is a
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-hard problem, which implies that
WIN(MS)
is at least as hard as
WIN(EF)
.

Our approach involves combining an adaptation of the sophisticated gadgets from
[
23
]
(which in turn were inspired by the gadgets introduced in Cai, Fürer, and Immerman
[
2
]
to study the expressive power of first-order logic with counting) with two critical new ingredients: (i) the recently developed technique of
parallel play
from
[
4
]
, which enables us to simulate several parallel
MS
games without wasting too many moves; and (ii) the inapproximability results for
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-hard problems in
[
6
]
.
We exhibit a natural reduction from
WIN(MS)
to an optimization variant of
QBF
known as
Max-Q3SAT
, which is known to be
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-hard to approximate within a constant multiplicative factor. Furthermore, we show that our reduction holds even for directed graphs. In particular, along the way, we answer a question raised by Pezzoli in
[
23
]
in the positive, namely, whether the
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-hardness of the Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game holds for schemas in which each relation has arity at most two; Pezzoli
[
23
]
established the
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-hardness of
WIN(EF)
only for schemas containing a ternary relation symbol.

We leave open the problem of closing the gap between
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-hardness and containment in
\mathrm{NEXPTIME}
. We note that our new techniques establishing the
\mathrm{PSPACE}
lower bound do not carry over to
\mathrm{NEXPTIME}
-hardness in a straightforward way. We conjecture that
WIN(MS)
is a
\mathrm{NEXPTIME}
-hard problem (and therefore
\mathrm{NEXPTIME}
-complete as well). We hope that this conjecture will stimulate research in this fundamental algorithmic problem.

Preliminaries and Background

A
schema
is a tuple

\tau=(R_{1},\ldots,R_{t},c_{1},\ldots,c_{s})
of relation symbols
R_{i}
and constant symbols
c_{j}
, where each
R_{i}
has a designated positive integer as its arity. A
\tau
-structure
is a tuple
{\bf A}=(A,R_{1}^{\bf A},\ldots,R_{t}^{\bf A},c_{1}^{\bf A},\ldots,c_{s}^{\bf A})
, where
A
is a set, called the
universe
of
{\bf A}
, each
R_{i}^{\bf A}
is a relation on
A
whose arity matches that of the relation symbol
R_{i}
, and each
c_{j}^{\bf A}
is an element of
A
. We say that a structure
{\bf A}
is
finite
if its universe is a finite set.
In what follows, we will denote
\tau
-structures in boldface (e.g.,
{\bf A}
,
{\bf B}
), their universes in capital letters (e.g.,
A
,
B
), and sets of pebbled
\tau
-structures (defined next) in calligraphic typeface (e.g.,
{\cal A}
,
{\cal B}
). We adopt the terminology and notation of recent papers on multi-structural games (e.g.,
[
4
]
).

2.1
Pebbled Structures

We assume that we have a palette
{\cal C}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}
of
colors
, and that for each color, we have infinitely many pebbles of that color available. A
\tau
-structure
{\bf A}
is
pebbled
if some elements in the universe
A
of
{\bf A}
have pebbles on them, provided there is at most one pebble of each color on an element. In general, an element in
A
may have multiple pebbles (of different colors) on it.

If pebbles of colors
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
are placed on (not necessarily distinct) elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
in the universe of a
\tau
-structure
{\bf A}
, the resulting pebbled
\tau
-structure is called
k
-pebbled
, and is denoted by
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle
. Note that
{\bf A}
can be viewed as the
0
-pebbled structure
\langle{\bf A}~|~\rangle
. If the number of pebbles is clear from the context, we
write
\langle{\bf A}~|~\vec{a}\rangle
for a pebbled structure.

We remark at this juncture that the colors are designated
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
deliberately: colored pebbles will turn out to correspond to variables; thus, when a pebble colored
x_{i}
is placed on an element
a_{i}\in A
, we will implicitly have that
x_{i}
is interpreted as the element
a_{i}
on
{\bf A}
.

We say that two pebbled structures
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle
and
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{\ell}\rangle
are a
matching pair
if
k=\ell
, and the function
\iota:A\to B
defined by:

\iota(a_{i})=b_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq k
and
\iota(c^{\bf A})=c^{\bf B}
, for every constant
c
in
\tau
,

is an isomorphism between the substructures of
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
induced by
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
and
b_{1},\dots,b_{k}
.

2.2
Multi-Structural Games

An instance of the multi-structural game consists of a natural number
m
, and two finite sets
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
of pebbled finite
\tau
-structures, each pebbled with the
same
subset
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\subseteq{\cal C}
of colors.
The
m
-round multi-structural (MS) game on
({\cal A},{\cal B})
is a game played by two players, called Spoiler (
S
, he/him) and Duplicator (
D
, she/her), over
m
rounds. In a run of the game, the
i
-th round, where
1\leq i\leq m
, consists of two steps:

•

First,
S
chooses either
{\cal A}
(the “left side”) or
{\cal B}
(the “right side”), and an unused color
x_{t}\in{\cal C}
; he then places (“plays” or “moves”) a pebble of color
x_{t}
on an element of
every
pebbled structure in the chosen side.

•

Next,
D
makes as many copies as she wants of each pebbled structure on the other side, and then places a pebble of color
x_{t}
on an element of each such copy.

D
wins this run of the game if at the end of round
m
, there is a pebbled structure in
{\cal A}
and a pebbled structure in
{\cal B}
forming a matching pair. Otherwise,
S
wins the run. We say
S
has a
winning strategy
on an instance
\langle m,({\cal A},{\cal B})\rangle
if he has a way to respond to the moves made by
D
so that he wins the resulting run of the game, regardless of the moves made by
D
.
We define a winning strategy for
D
analogously. It is easy to see that the game is
determined
, i.e., exactly one of
S
and
D
has a winning strategy on a given instance. Therefore, every instance of the game can be classified as either
S
-winnable
or
D
-winnable
.

We adopt the convention of always calling the two sides
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
, even though they change over the course of a run of the game, since the Duplicator adds new copies and since more pebbles are places on each pebbled structure.

For sets
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
described as above and with all structures in
{\cal A}\cup{\cal B}
pebbled with the same subset
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\subseteq{\cal C}
of colors, we say that a FO-formula
\varphi

separates

({\cal A},{\cal B})
if

•

\mathsf{free}(\varphi)=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
(i.e.,
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
is the set of the free variables of
\varphi
);

•

{\bf A}\models\varphi[a_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,a_{k}/x_{k}]
, for every
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle\in{\cal A}
;

•

{\bf B}\models\lnot\varphi[b_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,b_{k}/x_{k}]
, for every
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\rangle\in{\cal B}
.

We call
\varphi
a
separating sentence
for
({\cal A},{\cal B})
if
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
are sets of unpebbled structures.

The following key theorem
[
16
,
9
]
yields the precise sense in which multi-structural games capture the number of quantifiers of FO-formulas.

Theorem 1

(Fundamental Theorem of MS Games,
[
16
,
9
]
)
.

Let
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
be two finite sets of pebbled finite
\tau
-structures, each pebbled with the same subset of colors. Then the following statements are equivalent:

1.

S
has a winning strategy in the
m
-round MS game on
({\cal A},{\cal B})
.

2.

There is a separating formula for
({\cal A},{\cal B})
with at most
m
quantifiers.

The
MS
game can also be regarded as a single-player game, since
D
has a clear optimal
oblivious
strategy: in each round,
D
can make enough copies of each pebbled structure on her side in order to play all possible responses (one on each copy). In
D
-winnable instances, it can be easily checked that the oblivious strategy is a winning strategy.

The following simple observation helps keep our proofs clean.

Lemma 2
.

At any point during a run of an
MS
game, if there is a pebbled structure
\langle{\bf A}~|~\vec{a}\rangle
not forming a matching pair with any pebbled structure on the other side, then
\langle{\bf A}~|~\vec{a}\rangle
can be discarded from the game with no effect on the final outcome of the game.

2.3
Parallel Play

The technique of
parallel play
, introduced in
[
4
]
, allows the Spoiler to partition an MS game into MS sub-games corresponding to distinct isomorphism classes (on the pebbles played so far), and then play different
MS
games on those isomorphism classes in parallel, thereby potentially saving himself a large number of redundant moves that he might have needed with a naïve strategy. The basic idea is straightforward: suppose Spoiler is faced with a game on
({\cal A},{\cal B})
, where
{\cal A}={\cal A}_{1}\sqcup{\cal A}_{2}
,
{\cal B}={\cal B}_{1}\sqcup{\cal B}_{2}
, and the followin hold:

•

no pebbled structure in
{\cal A}_{1}
forms a matching pair with a pebbled structure in
{\cal B}_{2}
, and

•

no pebbled structure in
{\cal A}_{2}
forms a matching pair with a pebbled structure in
{\cal B}_{1}
.

Suppose further that Spoiler can win the
r
-round
MS
game on
({\cal A}_{1},{\cal B}_{1})
, and he can win the
r
-round
MS
game on
({\cal A}_{2},{\cal B}_{2})
,
following the same sequence of sides to play on for these
r
rounds
. Then, the principle of parallel play asserts that he can win the
r
-round
MS
game on
({\cal A},{\cal B})
as well, just by following his strategies on the two sub-games simultaneously. The fact that he follows the same sequence of sides to play on for the
r
rounds means that there is never any ambiguity about which side he needs to play on in the (larger) game.

Parallel play turned out to be a powerful technique to save redundant moves for Spoiler. Using Theorem
1
, this led to showing in
[
4
]
that many natural FO-properties on
ordered
structures can be expressed using exponentially fewer quantifiers than the naïve upper bound.

Since we will use parallel play in this paper, we formally state it as a lemma below.

Lemma 3

(Parallel Play Lemma,
[
4
]
)
.

Let
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
be two sets of pebbled structures, and fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
can be partitioned as
{\cal A}={\cal A}_{1}\sqcup{\cal A}_{2}
and
{\cal B}={\cal B}_{1}\sqcup{\cal B}_{2}
in such a way that
S
has a winning strategy for the
r
-round MS game on
({\cal A}_{i},{\cal B}_{i})
, where
i=1,2
, satisfying the following conditions:

1.

Both strategies follow the same sequence
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{r})
of left or right sides that Spoiler plays on, where
S_{i}\in\{L,R\}
for
1\leq i\leq r
.

2.

At the end of the sub-games, both of the following are true:

•

There do not exist pebbled structures from
{\cal A}_{1}
and
{\cal B}_{2}
forming a matching pair.

•

There do not exist pebbled structures from
{\cal A}_{2}
and
{\cal B}_{1}
forming a matching pair.

Then
S
wins the
r
-round
MS
game on
({\cal A},{\cal B})
with the same sequence
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{r})
of sides he plays on.

3
The Upper Bound

Suppose that the schema
\tau=(R_{1},\ldots,R_{t},c_{1},\ldots,c_{s})
is such that the maximum arity of the relation symbols
R_{1},\ldots,R_{t}
is
r
, where
r\geq 2
.
We start with a lemma, which establishes an upper bound on the number of FO-sentences over
\tau
(up to logical equivalence) with
m
quantifiers.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\tau=(R_{1},\ldots,R_{t},c_{1},\ldots,c_{s})
be a schema such that the maximum arity of the relation symbols in
\tau
is
r
, where
r\geq 2
. Then, up to logical equivalence, the number of
FO
-sentences over
\tau
with
m
quantifiers is at most
2^{m+2^{(t+1)(m+s)^{r}}}
, and each such sentence has a quantifier-free part with at most
(t+1)(m+s)^{r}\cdot 2^{(t+1)(m+s)^{r}}
(positive or negative) atomic formulas.

Proof.

Fix
m
, and let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
be
m
distinct
variables. For each
i
, the number of atomic formulas using the relation symbol
R_{i}
(say of arity
r_{i}
) is
(m+s)^{r_{i}}\leq(m+s)^{r}
. Furthermore, there are
(m+s)^{2}
atomic formulas involving the equality symbol
=
. Therefore, the total number of atomic formulas over
\tau
is at most
(t+1)(m+s)^{r}
.

By definition, a
type
over
\tau
is a conjunction of the form
\theta_{1}\land\ldots\land\theta_{d}
, where
d\leq(t+1)(m+s)^{r}
by the argument above, and each
\theta_{i}
is either an atomic formula or a negated atomic formula, and for each atomic formula, either the formula itself or its negation appears exactly once as a conjunct. Therefore, the total number of types is at most
2^{(t+1)(m+s)^{r}}
(corresponding to each choice of positive or negative appearance of each atomic formula).

Now, every FO-sentence
\varphi
in prenex normal form with
m
quantifiers is logically equivalent to an FO-sentence
\psi
of the form
Q_{1}x_{1}\ldots Q_{m}x_{m}\chi
, where
\chi
is a disjunction of types. Note that the quantifier-free part
\chi
has at most
(t+1)(m+s)^{r}\cdot 2^{(t+1)(m+s)^{r}}
atomic formulas (positive or negative), since each type has at most
(t+1)(m+s)^{r}
atomic formulas.
Also, there are
2^{m}
choices for the quantifier block
Q_{1}x_{1}\ldots Q_{m}x_{m}
, and there are
2^{2^{(t+1)(m+s)^{r}}}
choices for the subset of types needed to define
\chi
. By combining these two facts, we obtain the desired result.
∎

We now establish the
\mathrm{LOGSPACE}
upper bound for
\mbox{{\sc WIN(MS)}}_{m}
, for each fixed
m\geq 1
.

Proposition 5
.

For each fixed
m\geq 1
, the problem
\mbox{{\sc WIN(MS)}}_{m}
is in
\mathrm{LOGSPACE}
.

Proof.

Assume first that we have unpebbled structures, and so we are looking for separating
sentences
rather than formulas with free variables. When
m\geq 1
is fixed, the number of pairwise inequivalent sentences over
\tau
with
m
quantifiers is constant; this follows directly from Lemma
4
, by observing that the entire expression for the upper bound is a constant since
\tau
and
m
are fixed. Furthermore, each such sentence is equivalent to one of the form
\psi=Q_{1}x_{1}\ldots Q_{m}x_{m}\chi
, where
\chi
has constant length (also by Lemma
4
, since
t
,
s
,
r
, and
m
are all constants).

Consider the following model-checking problem: given a finite
\tau
-structure
{\bf A}
, and a FO-sentence
\psi
of the form above, is it the case that
{\bf A}\models\psi
? This problem can be solved by cycling through each of the
|A|^{m}
possible instantiations of the quantified variables
Q_{1}x_{1}\ldots Q_{m}x_{m}
, and examining whether each such instantiation satisfies one of the types in the quantifier-free formula
\chi
.
This is easily seen to be a
\mathrm{LOGSPACE}
protocol, since we only need: (i) space to store each instantiated tuple of
m
variables, which takes space
O(m\log(|A|))
; (ii) space to keep a counter to cycle through all the instantiations of the
|A|^{m}
tuples, which takes space
O(m\log(|A|))
; and (iii) space to check the that the instantiated tuple satisfies the constant-length quantifier-free formula
\chi
, which is well-known to be in
\mathrm{LOGSPACE}
.

Therefore, given an instance
({\cal A},{\cal B})
of
\mbox{{\sc WIN(MS)}}_{m}
, we can consider each of the FO-sentences

\psi
in prenex normal form with
m
quantifiers (there are a constant number of such sentences), and test whether, for each
{\bf A}\in{\cal A}
, we have
{\bf A}\models\psi
, and for each
{\bf B}\in{\cal B}
, we have
{\bf B}\models\lnot\psi
. As argued above, this takes space logarithmic in the maximum size of a structure in
{\cal A}\cup{\cal B}
.

When the input instance has pebbled structures, the proof goes through in exactly the same way, by considering pairwise inequivalent formulas over
\tau
with free variables corresponding to the pebbles played in the input instance.
∎

The same ideas can be adapted to yield an upper bound for
WIN(MS)
.

Proposition 6
.

The problem
WIN(MS)
is in
\mathrm{NEXPTIME}
.

Proof.

We can follow similar ideas as in the proof of Proposition
5
. Once again, assuming unpebbled structures first, we need to only consider the distinct FO-sentences
\psi
(up to logical equivalence) with
m
quantifiers, but this time the number of these sentences is not constant, since
m
is part of the input. Nevertheless, as before, we know from Lemma
4
that there are at most
2^{m+2^{(t+1)(m+s)^{r}}}
such sentences.

Now, consider our model-checking problem once again: given a finite
\tau
-structure
{\bf A}
, and a FO-sentence
\psi
of the form above, is it the case that
{\bf A}\models\psi
? Again, by cycling through the instantiations of the quantified variables, and examining whether each such instantiation satisfies one of the (at most)
2^{(t+1)(m+s)^{r}}
types in the quantifier-free formula
\chi
, we can conclude that the model-checking problem takes deterministic time
O(|A|^{m}2^{(t+1)(m+s)^{r}})
for any
\tau
-structure
{\bf A}
.

So now, consider the following nondeterministic algorithm for verifying that
S
wins an instance
({\cal A},{\cal B})
of the
m
-round
MS
game.

1.

Guess a FO-sentence
\psi
with
m
quantifiers. Without loss of generality, assume that
\psi
is of the form
Q_{1}x_{1}\ldots Q_{m}x_{m}\chi
, where
\chi
is a disjunction of conjunctions
\theta_{1}\land\cdots\land\theta_{d}
, with
d\leq(t+1)(m+s)^{r}
, as in the proof of Lemma
4
.

2.

For each
{\bf A}\in{\cal A}
and each
{\bf B}\in{\cal B}
, check that
{\bf A}\models\psi
and
{\bf B}\not\models\psi
.

The guess of
\psi
is a non-deterministic step that takes time
O(2^{(t+1)(m+s)^{r}})
. Each subsequent model-checking step takes time
O(N^{m}2^{(t+1)(m+s)^{r}})=O(2^{m\log(N)+(t+1)(m+s)^{r}})
, where
N
is the maximum size of a structure in
{\cal A}\cup{\cal B}
. Thus, the second step of the algorithm takes time
O(|{\cal A}|\cdot|{\cal B}|\cdot 2^{m\log(N)+(t+1)(m+s)^{r}})
. Therefore, this is a nondeterministic exponential time algorithm.

Once again, the proof goes through similarly in the case of pebbled structures (by guessing a separating formula this time with free variables corresponding to the pebbles placed in the input instance).
∎

We remark here that the two “obvious” approaches for showing an upper bound for
WIN(MS)
– either simulating the game, or guessing a separating sentence – both seem to result in an exponential blow-up of the problem, resulting in a
\mathrm{NEXPTIME}
upper bound at best. If we try to simulate the game directly, even on the game instances starting with singleton sets
(\{{\bf A}\},\{{\bf B}\})
, the number of copies on either side goes up in general to
|A|^{O(m)}
by the end of the game.
Therefore, the number of objects to keep track of seems to grow exponentially in the length of our input. Guessing a separating sentence achieves
\mathrm{NEXPTIME}
, as shown in Proposition
6
. In each case, this seems to happen primarily because of Duplicator’s ability to play obliviously; when she does so, she increases the number of possibilities exponentially, and this seems to be an inherent attribute of the problem.

We remark here that Hella and Luosto
[
15
]
have introduced several different games that capture the number of quantifiers, hence each of them is equivalent to the
MS
game. None of these games, however, seems to yield a better upper bound than the one derived here, at least in a straightforward way. In particular,
one of these games is the
Monotone Prefix Game
in which, unlike the
MS
game, the Duplicator does
not
have an oblivious strategy. A naïve analysis of this game, however, only yields
\Sigma_{2}^{\rm exp}
as an upper bound, where
\Sigma_{2}^{\rm exp}
is the second level of the exponential hierarchy (and contains
\mathrm{NEXPTIME}
).

4
The Lower Bound

In this section, we prove our main results, through our lower bound constructions. For ease of exposition, we shall do this in two parts; first, we will prove that the
WIN(MS)
problem is
\mathrm{NP}
-hard, which shall contain all the essential ingredients of the
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-hardness proof, albeit on slightly simpler gadgets. In particular, this proof will illustrate the main ideas from inapproximability in complexity theory that we will use. We shall then prove
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-hardness through substantially more involved gadgets.

4.1
Warm-Up: NP-Hardness of EF Games

We start by providing a self-contained proof that
WIN(EF)
is
\mathrm{NP}
-hard, a result already shown in
[
24
]
. We first recall the rules of the
Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé
(EF) game briefly.

An instance of the EF game consists of a natural number
m
, and a pair of finite pebbled
\tau
-structures
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle
and
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\rangle
, each pebbled with the same subset
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
of colors. We shall now describe a run of the
m
-round EF game on
(\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle,\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\rangle)
, by describing inductively what happens in round
i
, for
1\leq i\leq m
. Assume that in rounds
1
through
i-1
, pebbles of colors
x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{i-1}
have been played on the elements
a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{i-1}\in A
respectively, and on elements
b^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,b^{\prime}_{i-1}\in B
respectively, resulting in the two pebbled structures
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{i-1}\rangle
and
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{k},b^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,b^{\prime}_{i-1}\rangle
at the end of round
i-1
(and hence, at the beginning of round
i
). In round
i
,
S
chooses one of these pebbled structures,
say, the first one, i.e.,
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{i-1}\rangle
.
He then plays a pebble of an
unused
color
x^{\prime}_{i}
on an element
a^{\prime}_{i}\in A
, creating
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{i}\rangle
.

In response,
D
plays a pebble of color
x^{\prime}_{i}
on an element
b^{\prime}_{i}\in B
, creating the pebbled structure
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{k},b^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,b^{\prime}_{i}\rangle
. The run ends after
m
rounds, at which points,
D
wins this run if
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{m}\rangle
and
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{k},b^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,b^{\prime}_{m}\rangle
form a matching pair, and
S
wins otherwise. We define a winning strategy for
S
and
D
as in
MS
games, and the notion of a separating formula also carries over analogously. As in the
MS
game, we can adopt the convention of calling the two sides consistently
left
and
right
throughout. Finally, we have the fundamental theorem of EF games as well, showing that these games capture quantifier rank.

Theorem 7

(Fundamental Theorem of EF Games,
[
7
,
11
]
)
.

S
has a winning strategy in the
m
-round EF game on
(\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle,\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\rangle)
iff there is a separating formula (with
k
free variables) for
(\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle,\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\rangle)
with quantifier rank at most
m
.

To show the
\mathrm{NP}
-hardness of
WIN(EF)
, we adopt a similar gadget to the one in
[
24
]
, with several important modifications, including a reduction from
Dominating-Set
instead of one from
Clique
, which establishes the hardness even for schemas with predicates of arity at most
2
. However, first we need a few definitions.

Definition 8
.

Let
\langle{\bf A}~|~\vec{a}\rangle,\langle{\bf B}~|~\vec{b}\rangle
be
\tau
-structures, and fix elements
u\in A
and
v\in B
, and natural numbers
m,m^{\prime}
. In the
m
-round EF game on
(\langle{\bf A}~|~\vec{a}\rangle,\langle{\bf B}~|~\vec{b}\rangle)
, we say
S

forces
the pair
(u,v)
(within
m^{\prime}
additional rounds) if
S
has a strategy such that the following hold:

•

he can play on
u\in A
, and if
D
does not respond to this play by playing on
v\in B
, then she loses within
m^{\prime}
additional rounds;

•

he can play on
v\in B
, and if
D
does not respond to this play by playing on
u\in A
, then she loses within
m^{\prime}
additional rounds.

Usually

is understood from the context, and not specified. Furthermore,

can often force a pair without actually playing on that pair. We can extend this definition for the
MS
game as well.

Finally, we define the problem that we shall reduce from.

Definition 9
.

For an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, a
dominating set
is a subset
U\subseteq V
such that every
v\in V-U
has a neighbor in

U
. Define the decision problem:

\mbox{{\rm\sc Dominating-Set}}:=\{\langle G,k\rangle~:~G\mbox{ has a dominating set of size }k\}.

It is well known that
Dominating-Set
is a canonical
\mathrm{NP}
-complete problem
[
12
]
. Note also that
Dominating-Set
is the decision problem underlying the optimization problem

Min-Dominating-Set
: given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, find the minimum size of a dominating set of
G
(and a dominating set
U
of that size). The
\mathrm{NP}
-hardness of
Dominating-Set
implies that there is no polynomial-time algorithm for computing the minimum value
\mathrm{opt}(G)
of
Min-Dominating-Set
on
G
unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
.
In fact, it is known that
Min-Dominating-Set
is hard even to approximate. More precisely, suppose that
\mathscr{A}
is an algorithm such that, given a graph
G
, the algorithm returns a value
\mathscr{A}(G)
as an approximate value of
\mathrm{opt}(G)
. It is well known and not hard to see that if
\mathscr{A}
is the greedy algorithm, then

\mathscr{A}(G)/\mathrm{opt}(G)\leq 1+\log(|V|)
.
However, the following inapproximability result has been established by Raz and Safra
[
25
]

Theorem 10

(Inapproximability of Dominating Set,
[
25
]
)
.

Unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
, there is a constant
c>0
with the following property: there is no polynomial-time algorithm
\mathscr{A}
that, given an arbitrary undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, returns an approximate value
\mathscr{A}(G)
for
Min-Dominating-Set
on
G
such that
\mathscr{A}(G)/\mathrm{opt}(G)\leq c\log(|V|)
.

As a consequence of Theorem
10
, no polynomial-time algorithm
\mathscr{A}
and no constant
\varepsilon
exist such that
\mathscr{A}(G)/\mathrm{opt}(G)\leq\varepsilon
, unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
.

In Section
4.2
, we will leverage the inapproximability results for
Min-Dominating-Set
to prove that
WIN(MS)
is
\mathrm{NP}
-hard. In what follows in this section, we will give a polynomial-time reduction of
Dominating-Set
to
WIN(EF)
.

Given an instance
\langle G,k\rangle
of

has

-structure

with vertices

\langle G,k\rangle\in\mbox{{\rm\sc Dominating-Set}}\iff\langle k+1,(\langle{\bf A}~|~a\rangle,\langle{\bf A}~|~a^{\prime}\rangle)\rangle\in\mbox{{\sc WIN(EF)}}.

(1)

Here, schema

has a single binary relation symbol

4.3
The PSPACE Construction

In this section, we present our main result, that of
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-hardness for the problem
WIN(MS)
. We shall use several ideas from Section
4.2
, but the gadgets and the reduction problem will be different.

A
quantified Boolean formula
(QBF) is an expression of the form
Q_{1}x_{1}\cdots Q_{k}x_{k}\psi
, where each
Q_{i}
is either the existential quantifier
\exists
or the universal quantifier
\forall
, and
\psi
is a propositional formula in CNF whose variables are among
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
. Each such formula gives rise to a game, called the
QBF game associated with
Q_{1}x_{1}\cdots Q_{k}x_{k}\psi
, played between two players,
\mathsf{Player}_{\exists}
and
\mathsf{Player}_{\forall}
, as follows: during a run of the game, in the
i
-th move, and depending on whether
Q_{i}
is
\exists
or
\forall
,
\mathsf{Player}_{\exists}
or
\mathsf{Player}_{\forall}
plays and assigns a truth value to the variable
x_{i}
.
\mathsf{Player}_{\exists}
wins this run if the truth assignment produced by the two players at the end of the
k
-th move satisfies
\psi
; otherwise,
\mathsf{Player}_{\forall}
wins the run. We say that the formula
Q_{1}x_{1}\cdots Q_{k}x_{k}\psi
is
true
if
\mathsf{Player}_{\exists}
has a winning strategy that allows him to win every run of the QBF game in which she plays according to this strategy.
Stockmeyer
[
26
]
showed that the following problem,
QSat
, is
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-complete: given a QBF formula
Q_{1}x_{1}\cdots Q_{k}x_{k}\psi
, is it true? In fact, this problem is
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-complete even when restricted to QBF formulas

Q_{1}x_{1}\cdots Q_{k}x_{k}\psi
in which
\psi
is a 3CNF-formula.

Here, we are interested in the following variant of
QSat
, which we call
Clause-Q3Sat
:
given a QBF formula

Q_{1}x_{1}\cdots Q_{k}x_{k}\psi
, where
\psi
is a 3CNF-formula, and a positive integer
t
, does
\mathsf{Player}_{\exists}
have a strategy in the QBF game associated with
Q_{1}x_{1}\cdots Q_{k}x_{k}\psi
such that at least
t
clauses of
\psi
are simultaneously satisfied at the end over every run? In other words:

Clause-Q3Sat

\displaystyle:=\{\langle Q_{1}x_{1}\cdots Q_{k}x_{k}\psi,t\rangle~:~\psi\text{ has }t\text{ simultaneously satisfiable clauses}\}.

Clearly,
Clause-Q3Sat
is a
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-complete problem. Furthermore,
Clause-Q3Sat
is the decision problem underlying the optimization problem
Max-Q3Sat
: given a quantified
3
-CNF formula
Q_{1}x_{1}\cdots Q_{k}x_{k}\psi
, find the maximum number of clauses that are simultaneously satisfiable under optimal play by both players.
The optimization problem
Max-Q3Sat
is
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-hard to approximate up to a fixed constant, as the following result (stated without proof) of Condon, Feigenbaum, Lund, and Shor
[
6
]
asserts. The main ideas behind the proof can be found in Condon’s excellent exposition
[
5
]
.

Theorem 15

(Inapproximability of Quantified SAT,
[
6
]
)
.

Unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{PSPACE}
, there is a constant
0<c<1
with the following property: there is no polynomial-time algorithm
\mathscr{A}
that, given an arbitrary quantified
3
