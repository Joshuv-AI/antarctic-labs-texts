FOVA: Offline Federated Reinforcement Learning with Mixed-Quality Data
Nan Qiao, Sheng Yue, Ju Ren, and Yaoxue Zhang

Abstract
Offline Federated Reinforcement Learning (FRL), a marriage of federated learning and offline reinforcement learning, has attracted increasing interest recently. Albeit with some advancement, we find that the performance of most existing offline FRL methods drops dramatically when provided with mixed-quality data, that is, the logging behaviors (offline data) are collected by policies with varying qualities across clients. To overcome this limitation, this paper introduces a new vote-based offline FRL framework, named FOVA. It exploits a vote mechanism to identify high-return actions during local policy evaluation, alleviating the negative effect of low-quality behaviors from diverse local learning policies. Besides, building on advantage-weighted regression (AWR), we construct consistent local and global training objectives, significantly enhancing the efficiency and stability of FOVA. Further, we conduct an extensive theoretical analysis and rigorously show that the policy learned by FOVA enjoys strict policy improvement over the behavioral policy. Extensive experiments corroborate the significant performance gains of our proposed algorithm over existing baselines on widely used benchmarks. Code is available at: https://sites.google.com/view/fova .

III
Preliminaries

In this section, we first introduce preliminaries for FRL and offline FRL, followed by the system model and problem formulation.

III-A

Federated Reinforcement Learning

In RL, the environment is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), defined by the tuple
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},T,r,\mu_{0},\gamma)
. Here,
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the action space,
T
represents state transition probabilities,
r
is the reward function,
\mu_{0}
is the initial state distribution, and
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor.

In the
actor-critic
methods, a policy (denoted as
\pi(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})
) is explicitly trained to maximize the expected return
J(\mathcal{M},\pi):=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})\sim d_{\mathcal{M}}^{\pi}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})}[r(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})]
, where
d_{\mathcal{M}}^{\pi}
represents the state-action visitation distribution, through alternating phases of policy evaluation and policy improvement.
We assume
|r(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})|\leq r_{\mathrm{max}}

.

During policy evaluation, the Q-function
Q^{\pi}
is updated using the Bellman operator,
\mathcal{B}^{\pi}Q=r+\gamma P^{\pi}Q.

Specifically,
P^{\pi}
denotes the transition probability matrix under policy
\pi
, defined as
P^{\pi}Q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\sim T(\cdot|\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a}),\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot|\mathbf{s}^{\prime})}[Q(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{a}^{\prime})].

In the policy improvement phase, the policy is refined to favor actions with higher expected Q-values.

FRL carries out this process in a distributed manner, leveraging the paradigm of FL, without exchanging local data.
The objective of FRL is to accelerate policy search and enhance sampling and communication efficiency through client collaboration while maintaining strict adherence to information privacy requirements.

III-B

Offline Federated Reinforcement Learning

The objective of offline FRL is to learn a global policy
\bar{\pi}
from offline data
\{\mathcal{D}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
distributed across clients without interacting with the environment. Each offline dataset
\mathcal{D}_{k}
is collected using a behavior policy
\pi_{\beta_{k}}
, with its discounted marginal state distribution denoted as
d_{\pi_{\beta_{k}}}
.
The offline mixed-data over all clients can therefore
be regarded as induced by a mixture of behavior policies
\{\pi_{\beta_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}

[
12
]
.
We characterize the data quality of client
k
by the
expected return
J(\mathcal{M},\pi_{\beta_{k}})
.

Furthermore, offline FRL faces significant challenges due to distribution shifts, which include discrepancies between the distributions of local policy
\pi_{k}
and the behavior policy
\pi_{\beta_{k}}
, as well as variations in behavior policy distributions across different clients

[
59
,
73
,
47
]
.
To overcome these challenges, current approaches design dual regularization during local updates, that is,

\displaystyle\max_{\pi_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}\sim\mathcal{D}_{k},\mathbf{a}\sim\pi_{k}(\cdot|\mathbf{s})}\big[\hat{Q}^{\pi_{k}}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})\big]-\alpha_{1}D(\pi_{k},\pi_{\beta_{k}})-\alpha_{2}D(\pi_{k},\bar{\pi}),

where
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are positive coefficients, and
D(\cdot,\cdot)
denote a divergence measure such as the total variance (TV) distance or the L2-norm distance for the constrained policy distribution shift
[
69
,
47
]
.
These regularization terms attempt to strike a balance between alignment with the local behavior policy
\pi_{k}
and the global policy
\bar{\pi}
aggregated in the server.

However, the dual regularization approaches address the aforementioned distribution shift without taking into account the quality of local datasets. This results in poor performance in the case of mixed-quality behavioral data.
Moreover, existing methods only seek to find multiple local policies instead of optimizing the global policy generated from server aggregation. The global policy should have been the optimization objective of offline FRL. This leads to the problem of inconsistent optimization objectives.
In Appendix
B
, we delve deeper into these problems.

III-C

System Model

The system model depicted in Fig.
2
consists of a collection of clients, indexed by
\mathcal{K}
, representing
K

k\in\mathcal{K}
, possesses an individual offline dataset
\mathcal{D}_{k}=\{(s_{i},a_{i},r_{i},s^{\prime}_{i},a^{\prime}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{D_{k}}
collected using its own behavior policy
\pi_{\beta_{k}}
. Specifically, during the local update phase, we have designed a vote mechanism and an AWR method.
In the local policy evaluation phase, we implement the vote mechanism to identify and select better behavior across
\bar{\pi}
,
\pi_{k}
, and
\pi_{\beta_{k}}
, thereby training the local
Q_{k}
function. In the local policy improvement phase, we develop the AWR method, which fully utilizes
\bar{\pi}
,
Q_{k}
, and
\pi_{\beta_{k}}
to train the local policy function
\pi_{k}
. Subsequently, the parameters
\pi_{k}
and
Q_{k}
generated in the client
k
are transmitted to the central server. The server then performs global aggregation to obtain the global parameters
\bar{\pi}
and
\bar{Q}
, which are distributed back to the local clients.

IV
Proposed Method

In this section,
we introduce FOVA, detailing two primary mechanisms in local updates: the vote mechanism and the AWR method.
These approaches address the problems observed in existing methods and provide a more effective framework for policy learning in offline FRL.
Additionally, we provide theoretical analyses for each of the two methods.

IV-A

Local Policy Evaluation with Vote Mechanism

As illustrated before, existing methods often experience performance degradation in scenarios with mixed-quality data due to a lack of the mechanism to assess behavior from policies.
To this end, we design the vote policy
{\pi^{v}_{k}}
, which
selects the best behavior from mixed-quality policies. Specifically, vote policy
{\pi^{v}_{k}}
chooses
the behavior with the max Q-value from
\pi_{k}
,
\pi_{\beta_{k}}
, and
\bar{\pi}
, which is defined as follows:

\pi^{v}_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})={\arg\max}_{\pi^{\prime}_{k}\in\{\pi_{k},\pi_{\beta_{k}},\bar{\pi}_{k}\}}Q_{\mathbf{a}\sim\pi^{\prime}_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a}),~~\forall\mathbf{s}.

(1)

Notably, our method does not require training a new policy
{\pi^{v}_{k}}
. Instead, it selects the optimal behavior and its corresponding Q-value by identifying the maximum Q-value.
This can be implemented with just a few lines of code.

From an expectation perspective, the expected Q-value under the state–action distribution induced by a policy recovers the previously defined return
J(\mathcal{M},\pi)
, which we use to measure data quality. Therefore, choosing the action with the largest Q-value at each state can be viewed as locally selecting behaviors from higher-value policies.

By integrating evaluations of additional
\pi_{\beta_{k}}
and
\bar{\pi}
, this approach effectively enhances Q-function performance.
The Q-learning with vote policy is:

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{\tau+1}\leftarrow\arg\min_{Q}~{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}_{k}}\left[\left(Q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})-\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi^{v}_{k}}}\hat{Q}^{\tau}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})\right)^{2}\right]}.

(2)

However, such a Q-learning update fails to effectively address the problem of extrapolation error. Extrapolation error poses a significant challenge in local policy evaluation because offline RL training lacks interaction with the environment
[
43
]
.
Consequently, there is a mismatch between the distribution of state-action pairs accessed by the current policy and those sampled from the dataset
[
22
]
.
To mitigate this problem, we incorporate conservative Q-learning (CQL) regularization terms into the Q-learning update
[
21
]
.
These terms effectively penalize Q-values for actions outside the dataset policy, serving as a classical regularization approach in offline RL. Additionally, we integrate the vote policy into the CQL regularization terms.
To this end, the vote-based conservative Q-learning (VCQL) is as follows (changes from CQL in
red

):

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{\tau+1}\leftarrow\arg\min_{Q}~{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}_{k}}\left[\left(Q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})-\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi^{v}_{k}}}\hat{Q}^{\tau}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})\right)^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle+{{\alpha}\left(\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D}_{k},\mathbf{a}\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\pi^{v}_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})}}[Q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a}\sim\mathcal{D}_{k}}[Q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})]\right)},

(3)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}_{k}}\left[\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{\pi^{v}_{k}}Q^{\tau}_{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})\right]=\gamma\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\mathbf{a^{\prime}}\sim\pi^{v}_{k}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{s^{\prime}})}}\left[Q^{\tau}_{k}\left(\mathbf{s^{\prime}},\mathbf{a^{\prime}}\right)\right]+r(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})
.

Such vote policy significantly modifies the policy evaluation function of CQL, potentially rendering the original conservatism guarantee in
[
21
, Theorem 3.2]
invalid.

Thus, we provide a novel theoretical analysis of conservatism in Appendix
A-A
.

IV-B

Local Policy Improvement with AWR

As mentioned in Section
I
, current efforts suffer from a decline in policy performance because of the inconsistent optimization objective.
Moreover, due to privacy constraints, the server cannot access offline datasets to optimize the global policy directly, making it challenging to address this problem on the server side.
To this end, an intuitive strategy lies in directly optimizing the global policy on local policy updates.
The objective is mathematically expressed as follows:

\displaystyle~~\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim d_{\bar{\pi}_{k}}({\mathbf{s}}),{\mathbf{a}}\sim\bar{\pi}_{k}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})}\left[\hat{A}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right],

(4)

where advantage
\hat{A}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})=\hat{Q}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-\hat{V}({\mathbf{s}})
is a widely recognized metric to evaluate policy performance
[
38
]
.

\hat{Q}=\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\hat{Q}^{\tau}
,
\hat{V}(\mathbf{s})=\mathbb{E}_{{\pi^{v}_{k}}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})}[\hat{Q}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})]
,
and
\bar{\pi}_{k}=\frac{1}{K}\sum\nolimits^{K}_{i=1}\pi_{i}
is aggregated in the same way as the global policy, whereas updated and optimized at the local end.
We slightly abuse notation by using
\hat{Q}
and
\hat{V}
to denote
\hat{Q}^{{\pi^{v}_{k}}}
and
\hat{V}^{{\pi^{v}_{k}}}
, respectively.
Subsequently, we design the objective as maximizing an advantage function with respect to policy
\pi_{k}
:

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\pi_{k}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim d_{\bar{\pi}_{k}}({\mathbf{s}}),{\mathbf{a}}\sim\bar{\pi}_{k}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})}\left[\hat{Q}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-\hat{V}({\mathbf{s}})\right]

(5)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim d_{\bar{\pi}_{k}}({\mathbf{s}})}\left[\mathrm{D_{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}_{k}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}}),\pi_{\beta_{k}}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})\right)\right]\leq\epsilon,

(6)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{a}}\ \bar{\pi}_{k}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})d{\mathbf{a}}=1,~~\forall{\mathbf{s}},

(7)

\displaystyle\bar{\pi}_{k}=\frac{1}{K}\sum\nolimits^{K}_{i=1}\pi_{i}.

(8)

The constraint in Eq. (
6
) ensures that the global policy
\bar{\pi}_{k}
remains closely aligned with the local behavioral policy
\pi_{\beta_{k}}
, thereby reducing extrapolation errors during the policy improvement process. Eq. (
7
) ensures policy
\bar{\pi}_{k}
normalization.
Eq. (
8
) describes
\bar{\pi}_{k}
as defined previously.
Further, we convert the constraint (
6
) into a Lagrangian constraint with a coefficient
\beta
, which is a common transformation in RL
[
17
,
41
]
.
Combining the fact of
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim d_{\bar{\pi}_{k}}({\mathbf{s}})}\left[\mathrm{D_{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}_{k}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}}),\pi_{\beta_{k}}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})\right)\right]=\int_{{\mathbf{s}}}d_{\bar{\pi}_{k}}({\mathbf{s}})\mathrm{D_{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}_{k}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}}),\pi_{\beta_{k}}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})\right)d{\mathbf{s}}
,
we have

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\pi_{k}}\quad

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbf{s}}d_{\bar{\pi}_{k}}({\mathbf{s}})\int_{\mathbf{a}}\bar{\pi}_{k}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})\left[\hat{Q}({\mathbf{s}},\bar{\pi}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}}))-\hat{V}({\mathbf{s}})\right]\ d{\mathbf{a}}\ d{\mathbf{s}}\right)

\displaystyle+\beta\left(\epsilon-\int_{{\mathbf{s}}}d_{\bar{\pi}_{k}}({\mathbf{s}})\mathrm{D_{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}_{k}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}}),\pi_{\beta_{k}}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})\right)d{\mathbf{s}}\right)

s.t.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{a}}\ \bar{\pi}_{k}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})\ d{\mathbf{a}}=1,\quad\forall\ {\mathbf{s}},

\displaystyle\bar{\pi}_{k}=\frac{1}{K}\sum\nolimits^{K}_{i=1}\pi_{i},

(9)

which provides a form of reasonable transition,
following the method in
[
41
]
.
Additionally, since our goal is to derive an improved aggregated policy, we directly consider
\bar{\pi}_{k}
as the target variable.

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\bar{\pi}_{k}}\quad

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{s}}d_{\bar{\pi}_{k}}({\mathbf{s}})\int_{\mathbf{a}}\bar{\pi}_{k}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})\left[\hat{Q}({\mathbf{s}},\bar{\pi}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}}))-\hat{V}({\mathbf{s}})\right]\ d{\mathbf{a}}\ d{\mathbf{s}}

\displaystyle+\beta\left(\epsilon-\int_{{\mathbf{s}}}d_{\bar{\pi}_{k}}({\mathbf{s}})\mathrm{D_{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}_{k}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}}),\pi_{\beta_{k}}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})\right)d{\mathbf{s}}\right)

s.t.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{a}}\ \bar{\pi}_{k}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})\ d{\mathbf{a}}=1,\quad\forall\ {\mathbf{s}}.

(10)

Rearranging terms, we derive the Lagrangian of this constrained optimization problem over
\bar{\pi}_{k}

Consistent Optimization Objective

To evaluate the role of the consistent optimization objective in our method compared to the baseline methods, we utilize the Walkerd-medium-expert dataset to analyze the differences between overall server-side return and average client-side return, as illustrated in Fig.
4(a)
. In our experiments, we fixed the number of clients at five and varied the data size from 1,000 to 50,000 state-action pairs. The results demonstrate that FOVA consistently outperforms the client-side return in terms of server-side policy performance. Although FOVA does not always surpass the baseline methods in client-side policies, its server-side policies frequently exceed those of the corresponding baseline methods. This observation suggests that the baseline methods may allocate computational resources toward enhancing client-side policies, whereas FOVA focuses on directly optimizing the final target server-side policy. Consequently, FOVA effectively maintains a consistent optimization objective, leading to superior performance in this domain.

In Fig.

In general, a consistent optimization objective always leads to a better server policy as the output policy.

VI
Limitations and Future Work

We note that the vote mechanism’s requirement for multiple Q-value evaluations incurs non-negligible GPU memory overhead. Furthermore, certain hyperparameters currently lack adaptive tuning capabilities. These limitations motivate two concrete directions for future investigation: developing memory-efficient vote through selective value estimation or distributed computation, and designing an adaptive hyperparameter tuning approach tailored to offline FRL.

In addition, we plan to enhance privacy protection in FOVA by exploring techniques such as differential privacy and secure multi-party computation to ensure data confidentiality during aggregation. Another important direction is adapting FOVA to dynamic environments by developing algorithms that can detect and respond to changes in real time, incorporating memory or recurrent structures to handle sequential dependencies. Furthermore, we will work on improving the scalability of FOVA to cope with large-scale FL with many clients and extensive datasets, optimizing communication and computational efficiency for broader practical applications.

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce FOVA, an offline FRL framework that effectively addresses the challenges of mixed-quality data.

By employing vote mechanism and AWR, FOVA significantly improves overall performance. Theoretical analysis shows that FOVA guarantees policy improvement, and extensive experiments demonstrate its superiority over existing methods, particularly in scenarios with mixed-quality data.

Appendix E
Safe Policy improvement for global policy

This section establishes safe policy improvement for FOVA in heterogeneous offline FRL. We first lay out the problem setup and a formal
Preliminary on Heterogeneity
, fixing notation for client MDPs, discounted occupancy, and advantages. We then derive a
global improvement bridge
via advantage decomposition and averaging, and, under an
offline estimation/shift assumption
, transfer bounds from empirical to true MDPs. Finally, we prove a
local regularized policy-improvement lower bound

\lambda
and

.

E-A

Preliminary on Heterogeneity

Offline FRL heterogeneity stems from different initial states and transition dynamics.
The performance of a policy on
\mathcal{M}_{k}
depends jointly on the initial state distribution
\mu_{k}
and the transition dynamics
T_{k}
, both of which can be stochastic or deterministic. Variations in either of these factors can lead to different learned policies, resulting in the heterogeneity problem.
For each client
k
, we have the local objective as

J_{k}(\pi)=J(\widetilde{M}_{k},\pi),

where

\widetilde{M}_{k}
denotes the empirical Markov Decision Process (MDP) constructed from the client’s local dataset
D_{k}
. Specifically,
\widetilde{M}_{k}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\widetilde{T_{k}},\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{k}},\gamma,\widetilde{\mu_{k}})
consists of the estimated transition kernel
\widetilde{T_{k}}
, estimated reward function
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{k}}\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\max}_{k}
, and estimated initial state distribution
\widetilde{\mu_{k}}
, all derived from
D_{k}
.
The value
J_{k}(\pi)
for a given policy
\pi
can be expressed as:

J_{k}(\pi)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{\mathbf{s}}d^{\pi}_{\widetilde{M}_{k}}(\mathbf{s})\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\pi(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})\mathcal{R}_{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a}),

where
d^{\pi}_{\widetilde{M}_{k}}(\mathbf{s})
represents the normalized discounted state distribution, defined as:

d^{\pi}_{\widetilde{M}_{k}}(\mathbf{s})=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\Pr(\mathbf{s}_{t}=\mathbf{s}\mid\pi,\widetilde{\mu_{k}},\widetilde{T_{k}}).

In offline federated reinforcement learning, the global objective aggregates the local objectives uniformly across
K
clients. The primary goal is to learn a policy based on the set of offline datasets
\{\widetilde{M_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{K}
:

\max_{\pi}\;J(\pi)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}J_{k}(\pi).

To quantify the heterogeneity between MDPs, we introduce the following quantity on the
level of heterogeneity. Similar definitions are widely used in the FL/FRL work

[
24
,
53
,
64
,
63
]

Definition 1

(Level of Heterogeneity)
.

For any policy
\pi
, the heterogeneity level of client
n
is measured by the Frobenius norm
\lVert\mathbf{H}_{k}\rVert_{F}
. The heterogeneity matrix is constructed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{k}=\sum_{n=1}^{K}\frac{1}{K}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k})^{-1}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{n}\mathbf{A}_{n}-\mathbf{A}_{k},

(58)

where
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k}
denotes the diagonal state visitation frequency matrix for dataset
k
, while
\mathbf{A}_{k}
represents the advantage matrix under transition dynamic
T_{k}
.

This matrix
\mathbf{H}_{k}
captures the deviation between client
n
’s local performance and the federated average performance. Since policy and Q-value directly determine gradient directions, significant deviations indicate potential optimization conflicts in federated learning. In IID settings,
\mathbf{H}_{k}
reduces to the zero matrix and
\lVert\mathbf{H}\rVert_{F}=0
. Under non-IID conditions, the norm’s magnitude quantifies the client’s environmental divergence.
The matrix
\mathbb{A}_{k}
is a
\left|\mathcal{S}\right|\times\left|\mathcal{A}\right|
matrix, with the
(i,j)
-th entry denoted by
A_{k}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{a}_{j})
. This entry represents the advantage function for the
i
-th state and
j
-th action, capturing the difference between the action-value function and the state-value function. It quantifies the benefit of taking action
\mathbf{a}_{j}
at state
\mathbf{s}_{i}
within the local environment of client
k
.
The policy advantage
\mathbb{A}_{k}(\pi^{\prime})
can be expressed as:

\mathbb{A}_{k}(\pi)=\sum_{\mathbf{s}}d^{\pi}_{\widetilde{M}_{k}}(\mathbf{s})\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\pi(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})A_{k}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a}).

\displaystyle\mathop{\geq}_{\text{(a)}}\mathbb{A}_{k}(\pi_{k}^{t+1})-\frac{2\|\mathbf{H}_{k}\|_{F}}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}\sim d^{\pi_{\beta_{k}}}_{\widetilde{M}_{k}}}\left[D_{TV}(\pi_{k}^{t+1}(\cdot|\mathbf{s})\|\pi_{\beta_{k}}(\cdot|\mathbf{s}))\right],

where (a) follows from norm inequalities. Let

The lemma impolies that after client

the improvement in terms of the global expected discounted reward. For that purpose, we have the following Lemma.

Remark 4
.

Building upon our prior analysis, particularly that of local policy improvement,
we emphasize that the results pertain exclusively to the local level.
Although heterogeneity introduces variations in local data distributions,

By contrast, the situation is more intricate

(l_{k})
are uniformly controlled, ensuring that global updates do not reduce performance.
∎

Remark 5
.

The theorem certifies that the global update is safely improving up to
explicit penalty terms tied to heterogeneity. In particular, the
aggregation-induced deviation
l_{k}
is damped by the aggregation
regularizer
\lambda
, while the behavior-mismatch (dataset heterogeneity)
term
h_{k}
is damped by the behavior regularizer
\beta
. Thus:
when cross-client aggregation dominates (
l_{k}
large), increase

\lambda
to tighten the bound; when dataset mismatch dominates
(
h_{k}
large), increase
\beta
to tighten the bound.
In summary, larger
(\lambda,\beta)
yield a stronger safety
global policy improvement.

Lemma 7

(Local compact policy set)
.

Fix finite state/action sets
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}
and
\eta\in(0,1/|\mathcal{A}|]
.
For client
k
, define

\Pi_{k}\;:=\;\Big\{\pi_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}:\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S},\ \sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})=1,\ \pi_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})\geq\zeta\Big\},

and endow
\Pi_{k}
with the
\ell_{\infty}
norm. Then
\Pi_{k}
is nonempty, convex, and compact.

Proof.

Take any
\pi_{k},\tilde{\pi}_{k}\in\Pi_{k}
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
. For all
s,a
we have

\alpha\pi_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})+(1-\alpha)\tilde{\pi}_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})\ \geq\ \alpha\zeta+(1-\alpha)\zeta=\zeta,

and for every
s
,

\sum_{a}\!\big[\alpha\pi_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})+(1-\alpha)\tilde{\pi}_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})\big]=\alpha\sum_{a}\pi_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})+(1-\alpha)\sum_{a}\tilde{\pi}_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})=\alpha+(1-\alpha)=1,

hence
\alpha\pi_{k}+(1-\alpha)\tilde{\pi}_{k}\in\Pi_{k}
, which proves convexity.
Now let
(\pi_{k}^{(m)})_{m\geq 1}\subset\Pi_{k}
converge to
\pi_{k}
in
\ell_{\infty}
.
Then
\pi_{k}^{(m)}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})\to\pi_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})
for each
s,a
, and since
\pi_{k}^{(m)}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})\geq\zeta
for all
m
, the limit satisfies
\pi_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})\geq\zeta
. Moreover,

\sum_{a}\pi_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})=\sum_{a}\lim_{m\to\infty}\pi_{k}^{(m)}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{a}\pi_{k}^{(m)}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})=\lim_{m\to\infty}1=1,

thus
\pi_{k}\in\Pi_{k}
, which shows that
\Pi_{k}
is closed.
For any feasible
\pi_{k}
and any
s,a
, the normalization condition together with nonnegativity implies
0\leq\pi_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})\leq 1
, so
\|\pi_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, hence
\Pi_{k}
is bounded.
In finite-dimensional spaces, closed and bounded sets are compact (Heine–Borel), therefore
\Pi_{k}
is compact. Finally, since
\eta\leq 1/|\mathcal{A}|
, the uniform policy
\pi_{k}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})=1/|\mathcal{A}|
is feasible, hence
\Pi_{k}
is nonempty.
∎

Lemma 8

(Global compact feasible set)
.

Let
\Pi:=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\Pi_{k}
and equip it with the max product norm

\|\pi\|_{\max}:=\max_{k\in[K]}\|\pi_{k}\|_{\infty}
. If each
\Pi_{k}
is compact (resp. convex), then
\Pi
is compact (resp. convex).

Proof.

If each
\Pi_{k}
is convex, then their Cartesian product is also convex, so
\Pi
is convex.
To prove compactness, take any sequence
(\pi^{(m)})_{m\geq 1}\subset\Pi
. Since
\Pi_{1}
is compact, there is a subsequence along which the first component converges. From this subsequence, compactness of
\Pi_{2}
ensures the existence of a further subsequence with a convergent second component. Repeating this argument up to
k=K
yields a diagonal subsequence
(\pi^{(m_{j})})_{j}
such that
\pi^{(m_{j})}_{k}\to\bar{\pi}_{k}\in\Pi_{k}
for every
k
. Therefore

\|\pi^{(m_{j})}-\bar{\pi}\|_{\max}=\max_{k\in[K]}\|\pi^{(m_{j})}_{k}-\bar{\pi}_{k}\|_{\infty}\;\longrightarrow\;0,

so
\pi^{(m_{j})}\to\bar{\pi}=(\bar{\pi}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\pi}_{K})\in\Pi
, hence
\Pi
is sequentially compact, and thus compact since it is a metric space. This completes the proof.
∎

Assumption 3

(Feasibility-preserving update)
.

The update operator
\mathcal{U}
satisfies
\mathcal{U}(\Pi)\subseteq\Pi
.

Remark 6
.

Assumption
3
is met if, e.g., (i) each round solves a constrained subproblem over
\Pi
,
(ii) the server aggregates by convex combination of feasible local policies,
or (iii) one projects/clips and renormalizes back to
\Pi
after local updates.

Lemma 9

(Continuity Properties)
.

The following continuity properties hold for the offline FRL framework:

1.

(State visitation) The discounted visitation distribution
d^{\pi}_{M}
is continuous in the policy
\pi
.

2.

(Q-function) For any policy
\pi
, the state–action value function
Q^{\pi}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})
is Lipschitz-continuous in
\pi
.

3.

(Derived quantities) As a consequence of
(2)
, the state value
V^{\pi}(\mathbf{s})
, the advantage
A^{\pi}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})
, and the expected return
J(M,\pi)
are all Lipschitz-continuous in
\pi
.

4.

(Cross-policy value) For any policies
\pi
and
\hat{\pi}
, the functional
{Q}^{\hat{\pi}}(\pi)
is continuous in
(\pi,\hat{\pi})
.

5.

(Heterogeneity) For every client
k=1,\dots,K
, the heterogeneity level
\|\mathbf{H}_{k}\|_{F}
is continuous in
\pi
.

6.

(Local objective) For every client
k=1,\dots,K
, the local policy-improvement objective is continuous in
\pi
.

Proof.

Items
(1)
–
(4)
follow directly from
[
18
, Lemmas 4–6 and Corollary 1]
. In particular, these results give continuity (and, in fact, Lipschitz continuity where stated) of
d_{M}^{\pi}
,
Q^{\pi}
, and the derived quantities, as well as continuity of cross-policy value functionals.
Let
\|\mathbf{H}_{k}\|_{F}
denote the Frobenius norm of the client-
k
heterogeneity matrix. For concreteness, write

\|\mathbf{H}_{k}\|_{F}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{n=1}^{K}\sum_{\mathbf{s}}\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\Bigg(\frac{d^{\pi_{\beta_{n}}}_{\widetilde{M}_{n}}(\mathbf{s})}{d^{\pi_{\beta_{k}}}_{\widetilde{M}_{k}}(\mathbf{s})}A^{\pi_{\beta_{n}}}_{T_{n}}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})-A^{\pi_{\beta_{k}}}_{T_{k}}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})\Bigg)^{2},

where the precise form of
\mathbf{H}_{k}
is built from ratios of discounted visitation distributions and advantage functions across clients. By Item
(1)
,
d^{\pi}_{M}
varies continuously in
\pi
; by Item
(3)
, so do the advantages
A^{\pi}
. Under the standing reachability assumption (so that
d^{\pi_{\beta_{k}}}_{\widetilde{M}_{k}}(\mathbf{s})>0
on the relevant support), the ratio of two continuous maps with nonzero denominator is continuous. Finite sums and products preserve continuity, hence
\|\mathbf{H}_{k}\|_{F}
is continuous in
\pi
. This proves
(5)
.
Consider the local policy-improvement objective

f(\pi)=\lambda\,\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})\sim\mathcal{D}_{k}}\!\left[\log\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{s})\;\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\big(\hat{Q}(\mathbf{s},\bar{\pi}(\mathbf{s}))-\hat{V}(\mathbf{s})\big)\right)\right]\;+\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}\sim\mathcal{D}_{k},\;\mathbf{a}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid\mathbf{s})}\big[\hat{Q}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})\big].

By Items
(2)
–
(4)
, the quantities
\hat{Q}
,
\hat{V}
, and any cross-policy evaluation such as
\hat{Q}(\mathbf{s},\bar{\pi}(\mathbf{s}))
vary continuously with
\pi
(and with any auxiliary policy arguments). The functions
\exp(\cdot)
and
\log(\cdot)
are continuous on their domains; we work on the support where
\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{s})>0
(so
\log\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{s})
is well-defined). The first expectation is taken w.r.t. a fixed dataset distribution
\mathcal{D}_{k}
, hence it is a finite (or dominated) linear functional of a continuous integrand, and therefore continuous in
\pi
. For the second term, the map

\pi\;\mapsto\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid\mathbf{s})}[\hat{Q}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})]=\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{s})\,\hat{Q}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})\quad(\text{or the corresponding integral})

is continuous whenever
\hat{Q}
is continuous and bounded on the relevant domain; linearity (or dominated convergence) then implies continuity after averaging over
\mathbf{s}\sim\mathcal{D}_{k}
. The scalar weights
\lambda
and
\beta
are fixed (hence trivially continuous); if they are chosen adaptively as continuous functions of
\pi
, the conclusion remains unchanged. Consequently,
f(\pi)
is continuous in
\pi
. This proves
(6)
.
∎

We further show the lower bound of
J(\bar{\pi}^{t+1})-J(\bar{\pi}^{t})
.

Lemma 10
.

For all
t
, Algorithm FOVA is guaranteed to generate a monotonically improving global policy, i.e.

J(\bar{\pi}^{t+1})-J(\bar{\pi}^{t})\geq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}\eta_{k},\quad\forall t,\quad\eta_{k}>0,\forall k,

with

{\;\lambda\;\geq\;\frac{\,w\big(C+\eta_{k}\big)\;+\;\sqrt{\,w^{2}\big(C+\eta_{k}\big)^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}(l_{k}+2\sigma)^{2}\,\mathrm{KL}_{1}\,}\,}{\mathrm{KL}_{1}},}

{\;\beta\;\geq\;\frac{\,\big(1-w\big)\big(C+\eta_{k}\big)\;+\;\sqrt{\,\big(1-w\big)^{2}\big(C+\eta_{k}\big)^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\,\mathrm{KL}_{2}\,}\,}{\mathrm{KL}_{2}},}

\mathrm{KL}_{1}:=\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1}\big),\mathrm{KL}_{2}:=\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}}\big),

C=h_{k}\varepsilon_{k}+l_{k}D_{t}
,
and

w\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Recall the definition

\displaystyle f_{k}=

\displaystyle\lambda\,\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1}\big)+\beta\,\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}}\big)-l_{k}\,\mathrm{TV}\!\big(\pi_{k}^{t+1},\bar{\pi}^{\,t}\big)-h_{k}\,\mathrm{TV}\!\big(\pi_{k}^{t+1},\pi_{\beta_{k}}\big)

\displaystyle-2\sigma\,\mathrm{TV}\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1}\big)-\sigma\,\mathrm{TV}\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}}\big).

Suppose
\mathrm{TV}(\pi_{k}^{t+1},\pi_{\beta_{k}})=\varepsilon_{k}>0
and both

\KL(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1})
and
\KL(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}})
are strictly positive.
By the triangle inequality,

\mathrm{TV}\!\big(\pi_{k}^{t+1},\bar{\pi}^{\,t}\big)\leq\mathrm{TV}\!\big(\pi_{k}^{t+1},\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*}\big)+\mathrm{TV}\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\bar{\pi}^{\,t}\big).

Writing
D_{t}:=\mathrm{TV}(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\bar{\pi}^{\,t})
and substituting
\mathrm{TV}(\pi_{k}^{t+1},\pi_{\beta_{k}})=\varepsilon_{k}
into the definition of
f_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle f_{k}\ \geq

\displaystyle\lambda\,\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1}\big)+\beta\,\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}}\big)

\displaystyle-(l_{k}+2\sigma)\,\mathrm{TV}\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1}\big)-\sigma\,\mathrm{TV}\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}}\big)-\big(h_{k}\varepsilon_{k}+l_{k}D_{t}\big).

By Pinsker’s inequality
\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\KL(p,q)}
and Young’s inequality, for any
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0
,

\displaystyle(l_{k}+2\sigma)\,\mathrm{TV}\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}\,\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1}\big)+\frac{(l_{k}+2\sigma)^{2}}{4\epsilon_{1}},

\displaystyle\sigma\,\mathrm{TV}\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{2}\,\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}}\big)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\epsilon_{2}}.

Choosing
\epsilon_{1}=\lambda
and
\epsilon_{2}=\beta
yields

\displaystyle f_{k}\ \geq\ \frac{\lambda}{2}\,\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1}\big)+\frac{\beta}{2}\,\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}}\big)-\Bigg(\frac{(l_{k}+2\sigma)^{2}}{4\lambda}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\beta}+h_{k}\varepsilon_{k}+l_{k}D_{t}\Bigg).

(83)

Let
C:=h_{k}\varepsilon_{k}+l_{k}D_{t}
. For any target margin
\eta_{k}>0
, condition

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1}\big)+\frac{\beta}{2}\,\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}}\big)\ \geq\ \frac{(l_{k}+2\sigma)^{2}}{4\lambda}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\beta}+C+\eta_{k}

(84)

guarantees
f_{k}\geq\eta_{k}
by Eq. (
83
). To meet Eq. (
84
), fix any weight
w\in[0,1]
, decompose
C+\eta_{k}
as

a:=w(C+\eta_{k}),\qquad b:=(1-w)(C+\eta_{k}),

and impose the pair of inequalities

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1}\big)\ \geq\ \frac{(l_{k}+2\sigma)^{2}}{4\lambda}+a,\qquad\frac{\beta}{2}\,\KL\!\big(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}}\big)\ \geq\ \frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\beta}+b.

(85)

Each inequality in Eq. (
85
) is quadratic in the corresponding parameter. Solving the first inequality yields

\lambda\ \geq\ \frac{a+\sqrt{\,a^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}(l_{k}+2\sigma)^{2}\,\KL(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1})}}{\KL(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1})}.

Similarly, the second inequality is satisfied for

\beta\ \geq\ \frac{b+\sqrt{\,b^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\,\KL(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}})}}{\KL(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}})}.

Thus, by selecting any
w\in[0,1]
and choosing
\lambda,\beta
not smaller than the respective minimal values above, condition (1) holds and hence

f_{k}\ \geq\ \eta_{k}\ >0.

Because both
\KL(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{t+1})
and
\KL(\bar{\pi}_{k}^{*},\pi_{\beta_{k}})
are assumed strictly positive, the minimal feasible
\lambda,\beta
given above are finite. Therefore, one can always tune
(\lambda,\beta)
to guarantee
f_{k}\geq\eta_{k}
for any desired
\eta_{k}>0
, in particular ensuring
f_{k}>0
.
∎

Next, we show the convergence of policy updates in Algorithm FOVA.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose Assumption
2
and Assumption
3
hold, with parameters chosen as in Lemma
10
.
Then the algorithm converges in terms of the performance values
\{J(\bar{\pi}^{t})\}_{t\geq 0}
.
Moreover, every accumulation point of the policy sequence
\{\bar{\pi}^{t}\}
corresponds to an optimal solution.

Proof.

By Lemma
10
, for all
t
there exist admissible local parameters that yield per-round client improvements
\{\eta_{k}^{(t)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
with

J(\bar{\pi}^{t+1})\;\geq\;J(\bar{\pi}^{t})+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\eta_{k}^{(t)}\;\geq\;J(\bar{\pi}^{t}),

so
\{J(\bar{\pi}^{t})\}
is nondecreasing. Using
\widetilde{R}_{k}\leq\widetilde{R}_{k}^{\max}
and
\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}=(1-\gamma)^{-1}
, for any
\pi
,

J(\pi)\;\leq\;\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\widetilde{R}_{k}^{\max}\;=\;J_{\max}.

Thus,
\{J(\bar{\pi}^{t})\}
is bounded above and hence converges to some
J^{\star}\leq J_{\max}
. Denote
\Delta_{t}:=J(\bar{\pi}^{t+1})-J(\bar{\pi}^{t})\geq 0
; by convergence we have
\Delta_{t}\to 0
.
By Lemma
7
each
\Pi_{k}
is compact (and nonempty), and by Lemma
8

\Pi=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\Pi_{k}
is compact. Assumption
3
ensures
\bar{\pi}^{t}\in\Pi
for all
t
when initialized with
\bar{\pi}^{0}\in\Pi
, so
\{\bar{\pi}^{t}\}\subset\Pi
admits accumulation points. Let
\bar{\pi}^{\star}
be any accumulation point and suppose, for contradiction, that it is not a solution. Then at
\bar{\pi}^{\star}
there exists an admissible update that achieves a strict improvement
\delta>0
. By Lemma
9
(continuity of the improvement value in
\bar{\pi}
), there is a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{\pi}^{\star}
and a constant
\underline{\eta}\in(0,\delta)
such that for every
\bar{\pi}\in U
, applying the same improvement construction yields

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\eta_{k}\;\geq\;\underline{\eta}.

Since
\bar{\pi}^{\star}
is an accumulation point,
\bar{\pi}^{t}\in U
for infinitely many
t
, hence
\Delta_{t}\geq\underline{\eta}
infinitely often, contradicting
\Delta_{t}\to 0
. Therefore every accumulation point is a solution.
In particular, if the solution set is a singleton
\{\bar{\pi}^{\star}\}
, then the whole sequence
\{\bar{\pi}^{t}\}
converges to
\bar{\pi}^{\star}
.
Finally, if there exists a uniform
\underline{\eta}>0
such that at every round one can select admissible local parameters with
\eta_{k}^{(t)}\geq\underline{\eta}
,
\forall k
,

J(\bar{\pi}^{t+1})-J(\bar{\pi}^{t})\;\geq\;\underline{\eta}>0\quad\forall t.

We see that the process must end in at most

N\;\leq\;\frac{J_{\max}-J(\bar{\pi}^{0})}{\underline{\eta}}

rounds; otherwise
J(\bar{\pi}^{t})
would eventually exceed
J_{\max}
, a contradiction.
∎

Remark 7
.

Theorem
4
highlights the practical implication that the algorithm converges once the regularization parameters
(\lambda,\beta)
are chosen sufficiently large.
Intuitively, this condition enforces that the induced global policy from local updates remains sufficiently close to the local behavioral datasets (measured in KL divergence), and that local policies stay close to the induced global policy.
Under these proximity constraints, the iterative process guarantees monotone improvement of
J(\bar{\pi}^{t})
and thus ensures convergence.
