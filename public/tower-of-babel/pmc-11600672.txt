Causal relationship between major depressive disorder, anxiety disorder and constipation: a two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: ZhiYu Deng, XiaoYu Zeng, HanYu Wang, Wei Bi, YuMei Huang, Hao Fu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpidemiological and other studies have shown correlations among major depressive disorder (MDD), anxiety disorder (AXD) and constipation. However, no consensus has been reached regarding their interdependence and pathogenesis. Herein, we sought to further explore the causal associations between them.MethodsBidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was performed to confirm the causal link between MDD, AXD and constipation. Genetic instrumental variables for MDD, AXD, and constipation were obtained from publicly available genome-wide association studies (GWASs). In this MR analysis, inverse variance weighting (IVW) was used as the primary analysis method to evaluate the causal effect. Additionally, we employed Cochran’s Q test, MR‒Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO analysis to examine heterogeneity and pleiotropy. Moreover, leave-one-out analysis was employed to investigate the stability of the associations. Finally, a reverse analysis of Mendelian randomization was conducted.ResultsThe results revealed a causal relationship between MDD and an increased risk of constipation (p = 0.0001), whereas AXD (p = 8.52 × 10–1) did not increase the risk of constipation. In the inverse MR analysis, no causal associations were found (constipation to MDD: p = 9.37 × 10–1; constipation to AXD: p = 8.51 × 10–1).ConclusionThis MR study revealed genetic evidence supporting a causal relationship between MDD and constipation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-024-03526-y.Keywords: Mendelian randomization, Major depressive disorder, Anxiety disorder, Constipation, GWAS

Introduction

Depression and anxiety disorders rank among the most prevalent mood disorders globally and significantly impact the physical and mental well-being of individuals and exacerbate societal and economic burdens [1]. Depression, often referred to as major depressive disorder (MDD), is one of the most frequently encountered psychiatric disorders [2], with reported 12-month and lifetime prevalence rate of 7.2% and 10.8%, respectively [3]. And in the United States, the lifetime rate of MDD in adults is 17% [4]. An increasing body of research has revealed a link between MDD and constipation [5–7]. In a cross-sectional study by Wang et al., depression of all severities was significantly associated with constipation [8]. The incidence of constipation in patients with varying severity of depression was 7.8% (mild depression), 13.0% (moderate depression), and 22.6% (moderately severe to severe depression) [8].

Constipation is a prevalent global health concern [9]. The primary clinical manifestations involve infrequent or difficult evacuation of feces. The occurrence of constipation is linked to disorders in the nervous system, psychology, gastrointestinal motility, intestinal smooth muscle, immunity, and intestinal flora. Research indicates that individuals with chronic constipation experience a greater prevalence of psychological issues, particularly anxiety and depression [10]. Two extensive, population-based studies have assessed factors linked to constipation. In a cross-sectional study involving 12,352 participants, individuals with constipation exhibited a significantly greater likelihood of having MDD (20.9%) than did those without constipation (7.9%) [2]. Another study conducted in Iran examined factors associated with chronic constipation and revealed that depression was significantly associated with chronic constipation [11].

The current research on the causal relationships among MDD, AXD and constipation is inadequate. In addition, randomized controlled studies (RCTs) on this topic are time-consuming and laborious. In recent years, the advent of MR methods has offered a novel approach for conducting reliable causal inference studies based on observational data. Given that genetic variants are randomly assorted during meiosis, MR analyses are less prone to confounding factors than traditional observational studies [12]. Moreover, as genetic variants are fixed at conception and impervious to subsequent disease processes, MR analyses are immune to reverse causation bias [12].

There is currently a lack of clarity regarding the causal relationships among MDD, AXD and constipation. Consequently, this study employed two-sample MR to unveil the causal effects of MDD, AXD and constipation risk, utilizing aggregated statistics from an extensive GWAS encompassing MDD, AXD and constipation.

Materials and methods

Study design

To investigate the genetic causality between MDD, AXD and constipation, we used bidirectional MR analysis. In this study, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were chosen as instrumental variables (IVs) for MR analysis. For SNPs to serve as IVs for causal inference in MR studies, they must adhere to three fundamental assumptions [13–15]. Assumption 1: All chosen SNPs must exhibit a high correlation with the exposure. Assumption 2: The confounding factors of exposure and outcome have no effect on any of the chosen SNPs. Assumption 3: Each SNP influences outcomes only through exposure and not through other pathways. Figure 1 shows the research design and the analysis process for this MR analysis.

Data Sources

GWAS summary data encompass a variety of information reflecting the phenotypic effects of SNPs. Genetic data associated with MDD and AXD were obtained from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC) database (https://www.med.unc.edu/pgc). MDD GWAS summary statistics were compiled from a meta-analysis of data on 807,553 individuals (246,363 cases and 561,190 controls) from the three largest genome-wide association studies of depression [16]. However, GWAS summary statistics from the 23andMe Company remain unavailable to the public. Consequently, our MR analysis relied on a cohort consisting of 170,756 individuals diagnosed with MDD and 329,443 control subjects [16]. The AXD data were derived from a meta-analysis involving 77,096 individuals (33,640 cases and 43,456 controls) conducted by Otowa et al. [17]. The GWAS summary statistics for constipation were obtained from the FinnGen consortium (https://www.finngen.fi/fi), with a sample size of 377,277 European individuals, including 36,022 cases and 341,255 controls. The details of the data sources are outlined in Table 1. All original studies underwent ethical review, and participants provided informed consent.

Instrumental variable selection

Conducting a two-sample MR analysis requires meeting three assumptions: correlation, independence, and exclusion. First, we used P < 5 × 10–8 as the genome-wide significance threshold to obtain SNPs that were significantly associated with MDD [18]. To obtain more SNPs contributing to AXD and constipation, we applied a more relaxed threshold (P < 5 × 10–6), and this threshold adjustment has been reported in other studies [19, 20]. Second, the presence of linkage disequilibrium (LD) among SNPs may introduce biased results. In this MR analysis, we specified that the LD of SNPs significantly associated with exposure should meet the criteria of r2 = 0.001 and KB > 10,000 to ensure independence [21]. Additionally, our MR analysis excluded palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies. Moreover, we used the F-statistic to ensure the strength of the association between IVs and exposure. The equation for the F value used in this study is F = Beta2/Se2, where Beta is the allele effect value, and Se is the estimated standard error of Beta. Generally, an F-statistic greater than 10 is considered to satisfy the requirement for a strong association [22]. Finally, SNPs meeting the above criteria were uploaded to the PhenoScanner database (www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk) to examine the presence of a second phenotype that could lead to confounding [23].

Mendelian randomization analysis

In this MR analysis, MR‒Egger, weighted median and inverse variance weighted (IVW) methods were used to investigate the genetic associations among MDD, AXD and constipation. Among these methods, the IVW method assumes that all SNPs are valid IVs and is the method with the strongest ability to detect causality [24]. Therefore, the IVW method served as the primary analytical approach in this study, complemented by methods such as MR‒Egger and weighted median methods. Additionally, random-effect IVW yields more conservative causal inferences than fixed-effect IVW and accounts for the uncertainties associated with pleiotropic effects. Therefore, in this study, all references to “IVW” denote random-effects IVW unless otherwise specified. The MR‒Egger method, which assumes that all genetic variants are null IVs, serves as a valid test for the null causality hypothesis [25]. This method involves weighted regressions with an intercept term, enabling the assessment of pleiotropy among the IVs. The slope represents the estimate of the causal effect [26]. When the proportion of invalid IVs is as high as half and the precision of the estimates varies considerably between IVs, the weighted median approach still provides credible effect estimates [27]. Subsequently, to ensure the stability and reliability of the results, we conducted separate tests for heterogeneity and pleiotropy. First, we applied Cochran’s Q test to determine the presence of heterogeneity among the included IVs, with P < 0.05 indicating heterogeneity [28]. However, it is noteworthy that the presence of heterogeneity did not impact the results of the random-effects IVW estimation when the overall heterogeneity was balanced [29]. Therefore, when using random effects IVW for MR analysis, the presence of heterogeneity did not affect the reliability of the IVW results. Second, MR‒Egger methods allowing for nonzero intercepts were employed to detect horizontal pleiotropy. Additionally, we conducted leave-one-out analysis to determine whether the removal of a single SNP has a significant impact on the results by comprehensively assessing the stability of the MR results. Finally, the MR-PRESSO method was used to detect outliers, which were promptly rejected upon detection. After removing the outliers, MR analysis was performed again to further evaluate the robustness of the results by comparing the effects before and after removal [30].

For statistical analysis, we utilized the R programming language (version 4.3.1), and the software packages “TwoSampleMR” and “MR-PRESSO” were used for MR analysis. Visualization was conducted using the “forestplot” package. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. It is important to note that this study involved secondary analysis of published data, and no modifications were made to the original dataset.

Results

A bidirectional two-sample MR analysis was used to investigate the causative link between MDD, AXD and constipation. Our MR findings demonstrated that genetic vulnerability to MDD was associated with an elevated risk of constipation.

Instrumental variable selection

In this study, we included 49 SNPs substantially linked to MDD (p < 5E-08) that met independence criteria (r2 < 0.001 and KB > 10,000). Additionally, 6 SNPs strongly associated with AXD (p < 5E-06) and fulfilling the independence criteria (r2 < 0.001 and KB > 10,000) were included. Subsequently, intermediate allele frequency palindromes were excluded (MDD palindromic SNPs: rs2247523, rs2876520, rs4730387; AXD did not have palindrome SNPs), and SNPs that could not be matched and 46 and 6 IVs representing MDD and AXD, respectively, were obtained for MR analysis. Next, we calculated the F statistic, and all SNPs exhibited F values greater than 10, indicating the absence of weak instrumental bias (detailed F values in Supplementary Table 1). Next, we uploaded all screened SNPs to the PhenoScanner V2 database (www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk, retrieved on December 13, 2023), and no SNPs were excluded. Additionally, after the MR analysis, we conducted the MR-PRESSO test and identified an outlier, MDD (rs150346963). Upon eliminating this outlier, we reran the MR analysis, resulting in the extraction of 45 and 6 SNPs as IVs for MDD and AXD, respectively. Detailed SNP information is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis

We used three methods (MR‒Egger, weighted median, IVW) to evaluate the causal relationships among MDD, AXD and constipation, with IVW random effects as the primary analytical method. The results indicated that MDD independently increased the risk of constipation (MDD to constipation: OR = 1.24; 95% CIs 1.11–1.39; p = 0.0001). Furthermore, the weighted median also supported a genetic causal relationship between MDD and constipation (OR = 1.25; 95% CIs 1.09–1.44; p = 0.0013). The corresponding ORs (MR‒Egger, weighted median, IVW) for AXD for constipation were 0.86 (95% CIs 0.76–0.98; p = 8.47 × 10–2), 0.99 (95% CIs 0.93–1.05; p = 7.68 × 10–1), and 0.99 (95% CIs 0.94–1.05; p = 8.52 × 10–1), respectively. Figure 2 illustrates the main results of our MR analysis. Overall, these findings suggested that MDD may increase the risk of constipation from both an epidemiological and genetic standpoint, while there was no genetic causation between AXD and constipation. Cochran’s Q test was used to detect heterogeneity, and the results are presented in Supplementary Table 3. Although heterogeneity was observed in the results of Cochran’s Q test (p < 0.05), it was deemed acceptable with random effects IVW analysis as the primary outcome [31]. The funnel plot used to visualize heterogeneity is displayed in Supplementary Figure S1. The P values of the MR‒Egger intercepts were all greater than 0.05, indicating the absence of interference caused by pleiotropy. Additionally, leave-one-out analysis was conducted to eliminate one SNP at a time to determine whether causality was induced by a single IV, and the final results showed that the MR analysis results were robust (Supplementary Figure S2). Furthermore, outliers were identified during MR-PRESSO tests. However, after correcting for the outliers, we reran the MR analysis and found that the results remained consistent.

Reverse two-sample MR analysis

To investigate the reverse causality of constipation on MDD and AXD, we conducted a reverse MR analysis. In the reverse two-sample MR, constipation served as the exposure factor, whereas MDD and AXD were the outcome factors. To obtain more IVs, we set the p value to less than 5 × 10–6. Additionally, after removing linkage disequilibrium (r2 < 0.001 and KB > 10,000), we ensured that the included SNPs followed the core assumptions of MR and removed palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies. Finally, we separately included 15 (constipation to MDD) and 11 (constipation to AXD) SNPs strongly associated with constipation. All SNPs exhibited F values greater than 10 (see Supplementary Table 1 for details). The results of this inverse MR did not support an association between genetic susceptibility to constipation and increased risk of MDD and AXD causation (IVW, Constipation to MDD: OR = 1.00, 95% CIs 0.94–1.06, p = 9.37 × 10–1; Constipation to AXD: OR = 1.05, 95% CIs 0.60–1.82, p = 8.51 × 10–1) (Supplementary Figure S4). For heterogeneity and pleiotropy tests, Cochran’s Q test, the MR‒Egger intercept test and the MR-PRESSO test did not detect any abnormalities in the analysis between constipation and MDD risk or AXD risk (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, publicly available aggregate GWAS data were utilized for our two-sample MR analysis to evaluate the causal relationships among genetically predicted MDD, AXD and constipation through bidirectional analysis. SNPs served as IVs unaffected by confounding factors or reverse causation [32]. Our findings suggested that MDD has a potential causal relationship with constipation. Specifically, MDD was significantly associated with an increased risk of constipation. However, we did not find a causal relationship between the AXD and constipation, and we found no inverse causal relationships between MDD, the AXD and constipation. It is worth noting that the results of our study are consistent with the previous studies of Liu et al. and Wu et al. [33, 34], which once again proves the reliability of the results of this study. In addition, Wu et al. conducted an observational study to explore the link between depression and constipation by extracting data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, and their results also support our results [34].

In most observational studies, researchers typically use statistical methods to address confounders by incorporating known and measurable variables into the regression model. Nonetheless, when confounders remain unobserved or unknown, regression methods may fail to produce unbiased estimates of the genuine connection between an exposure and an outcome. Consequently, the interpretation of findings can potentially introduce biases [35]. Moreover, it should be noted that some observational studies, especially cross-sectional studies, may not be able to definitively establish a chronological relationship between constipation and psychiatric disorders and are thus unable to elucidate cause and effect. To overcome this limitation and elucidate the causal relationship, we used MR Method to explore the relationship between mental disorders such as depression and anxiety and constipation.

Nevertheless, no studies have fully elucidated the exact mechanisms underlying the associations between MDD and constipation. On the one hand, the relationship between MDD and constipation is associated with interactions between the central nervous system and the gastrointestinal tract (also known as the brain-gut axis). For instance, studies investigating neuronal stress pathways have identified corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF) in the brain as a significant mediator of the relationship between emotional distress and changes in both upper and lower gastrointestinal motor function [36, 37]. Research has shown that in functional gastrointestinal disorders such as irritable bowel syndrome, functional dyspepsia, and constipation, autonomic nervous system dysfunction plays a role in altering bowel habits and gastric emptying by directly acting on the CRF [38]. Clinical evidence strongly supports the notion that hypothalamic and extrahypothalamic neurons in depressed patients overproduce CRF [39]. Notably, CRF receptors have been suggested as possible treatment targets for both depression and gastrointestinal disorders [40, 41]. It is possible that consistent activation of the stress pathways mentioned above may lead to dysfunction in the brain-gut axis, making individuals with MDD more prone to developing constipation symptoms. Several animal experiments have observed significant effects of CRF receptor antagonists against stress-induced defecation [42, 43]. Further studies are needed in the future to investigate the key role of CRF in brain-gut axis dysfunction and between MDD and constipation.

On the other hand, the association between MDD and constipation may be related to dysregulation of the intestinal flora. Some studies have shown that the intestinal microbes of MDD patients mainly belong to the genus Bacteroides [44]. Overgrowth of Bacteroides may lead to increased degradation of mucins and their protective and lubricating effects, resulting in thinner mucus layers and impaired intestinal barrier function [45]. As the core bacterium in the interaction network of constipation, Bacteroides is positively correlated with a high-protein/low-fiber diet [46] and produces the inhibitory neurotransmitter γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) [47], which can affect gastrointestinal motility through different GABA receptors [48], leading to constipation. Similarly, an increase in Bacteroides has been suggested to be a potential cause of functional constipation [49]. The potential impact of Bacteroides on constipation was emphasized in studies by Fan et al. and Wang et al.,who both found that functional constipation exhibited significantly higher levels of Bacteroidetes compared to a healthy cohort [49, 50]. In addition, a Mendelian randomization study found Bacteroides to be associated with an increased risk of constipation [51]. However, due to the complexity of the gut microbiota as an ecosystem, there are regulatory networks and confounding factors among various bacterial types. We look forward to further experimental studies in the future to refine the mechanisms linking the gut microbiota to MDD and constipation.

Notably, in addition to the dangers of MDD itself, the use of antidepressant medications may also cause constipation symptoms. Studies have confirmed that approximately 25% of every 500 patients using antipsychotics may experience symptoms of constipation [52]. Our MR findings strengthen the evidence for a causal relationship between MDD and constipation. Hence, clinicians should be aware that MDD may increase the risk of constipation, and proactive prevention, comprehensive medication and multidisciplinary approaches should be taken to treat constipation symptoms in patients with MDD.

The advantages of our two-sample bidirectional MR analysis are as follows. First, we adopted the MR analysis method, which can simulate randomized controlled experiments in an observational environment and obtain higher-level experimental evidence through simple and inexpensive test methods. At the same time, the MR research method effectively avoids the reversal of causality and confounding factors. Second, all IVs analyzed by MR were strictly screened, and the F value was greater than 10, ensuring the accuracy of the results. Third, as the data were collected from different tissues, there was little overlap between exposures and results in the samples. Finally, the results of our analysis may have implications for health care policy. Uncovering the causal relationship between MDD and constipation may influence public health policies regarding prevention and treatment.

Nevertheless, our study inevitably has several limitations. Our study relied on pooled GWAS data, which could not be stratified based on factors such as country, age, or sex due to data limitations. Furthermore, given that we used aggregated GWAS data from individuals of European descent, the applicability and generalizability of our findings to other ethnicities are limited due to potential interracial genetic variation. Therefore, we look forward to a larger sample base with a more diverse population, wider regional coverage and a larger sample size to verify our conclusions.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings not only shed new light on the causal relationship between MDD and constipation but also help generate new hypotheses about the development of MDD and constipation. This advancement lays the foundation for future clinical decisions regarding the treatment of constipation in patients with MDD, the validation of intervention trials, and the exploration of new therapeutic targets.
