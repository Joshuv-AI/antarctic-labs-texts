Inferior alveolar nerve relocation and immediate dental implant placement in severely resorbed mandibles: a prospective clinical case series
Authors: Hisham Suliman Hwaiti, Yasser Ahmed Al-Rubidi, Ahmed Al-Ashwal, Yahya Al-Hadi, Taghreed Al-Kibsi, Mohammed Ali Al-Wesabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvaluation of neurosensory disturbances (ND) and the success rate of dental implants related to inferior alveolar nerve lateralization (IANL) and immediate dental implant placement in the posterior atrophic mandible.MethodsA prospective clinical case series was conducted among five edentulous patients with posterior mandibular atrophy. The study included 9 IANL sites. Nerve function and implant success rate were assessed during follow-up. Differences between pre- and post-operative outcomes were analyzed.ResultsThe study included patients aged 24–72 years, with a mean age of 56.22 ± 16.49 years and a male predominance (77.7%). Surgical procedures were performed on the right side in 55.56% of cases and on the left side in 44.44%. By one month, 44.4% reported full recovery, 33.3% reported moderate hypoesthesia, and 22.3% had mild hypoesthesia. At three months, 33.3% had full recovery, 55.6% experienced mild hypoesthesia, and 11.1% (1 patient) reported anesthesia. At six months, 88.9% had recovered, with only one patient (11.1%) still reporting anesthesia. A statistically significant difference in ND was observed across follow-ups at day one, week one, month one, month three, and month six (χ² = 30.3, p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons revealed significant improvements from day one to month one (p = 0.03), day one to month three (p = 0.008), and day one to month six (p = 0.005), while the difference between day one and week one was not significant (p = 1.00). No significant correlations were found between ND recovery and patient age or membrane type at any follow-up interval (p > 0.05).ConclusionThe study showed that IANL with immediate implant placement is an effective technique for rehabilitating atrophic posterior mandibles.Trial registrationThe study protocol has been registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier NCT07104994, 5/8/2025), and the date of registration was retrospectively determined.Keywords: Inferior alveolar nerve lateralization, Mandibular atrophy, Dental implant, Alveolar ridge resorption

Background

Alveolar bone resorption is initiated following tooth extraction or loss as a consequence of diminished mechanical loading and disruption of the physiologic bone remodeling balance, leading to progressive atrophy of the residual ridge. Significant interindividual variability as well as intraindividual variations over time and across various regions of the residual ridge are observed in the rate and extent of alveolar bone resorption after tooth loss [1].

A recent systematic review evaluated the dimensional changes in the hard and soft tissues of the alveolar process up to 12 months following tooth extraction [2]. Because of anatomical and biomechanical differences, the maxilla and mandible show different patterns and magnitudes of alveolar bone resorption after tooth loss. Studies have shown that bone loss in the maxilla usually happens more quickly, with an average vertical reduction of 1.5–2 mm in the first six months and up to 40–60% of ridge width over the course of three to five years. On the other hand, cortical bone resorption in the mandible is typically slower but more noticeable, especially in the anterior region, where height loss can reach 4–5 mm in the first year. Nonetheless, there is a great deal of variation based on variables like systemic health, periodontal phenotype, and occlusal forces. Because the buccal bone plates in the posterior regions of both jaws are thinner, they tend to resorb more quickly, whereas the mandibular anterior ridge, supported by dense cortical bone, often retains greater residual volume [3].

Several classification systems have been proposed to describe the extent and pattern of mandibular ridge resorption. Atwood’s [1], Cawood and Howell’s [4], Lekholm and Zarb’s Classification, and Seibert’s Classification [5]. Cawood and Howell’s classification is the most widely used in general assessment in dental implantology and describes six stages of ridge resorption: Class I: Dentate ridge, Class II: Immediately post-extraction, Class III: Well-rounded ridge (favorable for dentures), Class IV: Knife-edged ridge (reduced width), Class V: Flat ridge (loss of height and width), and Class VI: Depressed ridge (severe resorption with basal bone loss) [4]. Placement of implants in severely resorbed posterior mandibles (Cawood-Howell categorization classes V and VI) is problematic due to inadequate bone volume, reduced attached and unattached mucosa, and the superficial positioning of the inferior alveolar nerve [6]. Numerous therapeutic modalities have been suggested in the literature to tackle this issue, including the insertion of short implants, sandwich osteotomy, vertical ridge augmentation, and distraction osteogenesis. An alternative approach involves exposing the IAN by laterally displacing it from the canal through nerve lateralization or transposition [7, 8].

The inferior alveolar nerve, a major branch of the mandibular nerve, innervates the mandibular teeth, periodontium, lower lip, and jaw. It traverses the lower jaw via the mandibular foramen, encased in firmly bound connective tissue, and subsequently proceeds downward and forward within the mandibular canal, typically situated behind the root apex [9]. Within the mandibular canal, it accompanies the inferior alveolar artery (IAA), vein, and lymphatic vessels, forming the inferior alveolar neurovascular bundle [10, 11]. The assessment of the infra-alveolar nerve canal’s relative position and its association with mandibular anatomical landmarks can be therapeutically advantageous in reducing the risk of surgical problems, such as nerve damage, that may arise following invasive mandibular surgeries. Furthermore, the evaluation of bone height and width includes the Cone Beam Computed Tomographic (CBCT) study of the trajectory and location of the mandibular canal [12].

Two techniques pertain to the alveolar nerve; the initial technique involves inferior alveolar nerve transposition (IANT) via a bone window fashioned in the cortical bone of the jaw, posterior to the mental foramen, excluding it. The second approach incorporates the mental foramen inside the established bone window and is referred to as IANL [9, 13]. In contrast to graft-based reconstruction methods, the lateralization procedure eliminates the need for donor sites, thereby reducing patient morbidity, lowering expenses, facilitating the immediate placement of long implants (as it utilizes all available jawbone), and averting a six to eight-month waiting period for treatment [14]. IAN lateralization is a complex surgical operation that necessitates the exposure of the neurovascular bundle from its dense bone compartment and sufficient retraction, with the prompt implantation of an implant. This operation necessitates substantial clinical expertise, anatomical understanding, and the capability to address any complications [15–18].

Varying methods of IANL and immediate implant placements that have used drills, burs, or saws have been reported [19, 20]. Disturbances in postoperative neurosensory function of the IAN are common. The risk of IAN morbidity created some controversy, which resulted in the limited use of this classic technique [21].

The key benefits of IAN relocation encompass the utilization of standard-length implants with bicortical anchorage, hence augmenting primary stability, which is essential for effective osseointegration. Implants accompanied by nerve relocation have a reduced risk of bone loss when compared to short implants implanted under analogous conditions [22]. In instances when the vertical bone height is below 5 mm—insufficient for even small implants—IAN repositioning is the advised procedure [23]. This treatment enhances resistance to occlusal stresses and guarantees an appropriate implant-to-prosthesis connection. In comparison to bone grafting, IAN relocation presents multiple advantages: it can be executed under local anesthetic, eliminates the need for a donor site, incurs cheaper expenses, and is linked to diminished morbidity [7, 24]. The present study aimed to investigate the efficacy of the IANL procedure for dental implants in atrophied mandibles.

Patients and methods

This is a prospective clinical case series. The study was carried out in patients with posterior mandibular atrophies among selected cases from patients attending the Faculty of Dentistry dental polyclinics, and some private dental clinics in Sana’a city from 10 July 2024 to 20 April 2025 by convenience methods. The sample size was calculated using GPower software for a fixed model with 7 degrees of freedom, an alpha of 0.05, and a power of 0.80. The analysis indicated a minimum sample size of 9 participants [25]. The age of patients was above the age of 18 years according to [25]. Patients included in the study were those who had a bone height above the IAN that ranged from 0.5 to 7.0 mm, and patients with a bone width of less than 5 mm in the planned dental implant sites. All patients were informed about alternative treatments and given information regarding all steps of the procedure, the risks of the surgery, and possible postoperative neurosensory dysfunction. All explanations were given to the patients verbally and in a written manner. Patients with active infections, uncontrolled diabetes, a history of radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or oral cancer surgery were excluded from this study. Demographic data name, age, gender, occupation, address, phone number, and habits) were collected using a preformed questionnaire. Present and past medical and dental history were also recorded for all patients. All patients received a thorough dental examination (extra-oral and intra-oral examination) in dental clinics using a dental mirror, ball-tipped WHO dental prob, and dental tweezers. Sterile examination tools by autoclave class B were used. Clinical examination included Oral health and oral hygiene status, interarch distance, dimensions of the edentulous ridge, and soft tissue condition. Panoramic radiographs were taken to assess the condition of the patients’ teeth and jawbone, as well as to detect any oral infections requiring treatment. These images also served as baseline documentation for comparison before and after treatment. CBCT revealed significant vertical and horizontal bone loss (bone quantity) and bone density (bone quality) in the mandibular premolar and molar areas, with the residual bone above the IAN ranging from 0.5 to 7.0 mm (Fig. 1). All cases’ mandibles were classified as class V (flat ridge form, inadequate in height and width) or class VI (depressed ridge form, with some basilar loss evident) according to Howell-Cawood classification [4]. All patients were provided written informed consent and received a modified questionnaire of that proposed by Hashemi to register any ND and the success rate of dental implant after the operation [26].

Radiographic examination before operation (bone quality and quantity, and canal position)

Surgical protocol

Local anesthesia was administered using lidocaine with adrenaline at a concentration of 1:80,000. A 27-gauge long needle was used with a stainless-steel syringe to perform IAN, lingual nerve, and long buccal nerve blocks. A triangular mucoperiosteal flap was performed, a crestal incision as well as an envelope with vertical releasing incision began from the retromolar region and was carried forward to the distal for canine, were used by scalpel and blade to expose the alveolar crest and lateral body of the mandible of the posterior area. Then the mucoperiosteal flap was reflected by a periosteal elevator, and the mental nerve was then identified and released from the underlying tissue to free the mucoperiosteal flap during reflection. Then all flaps were reflected by a Minnesota retractor. Fig. 2 shows that the mental bundle was located and secured using a specially fabricated instrument (nerve retractor).

Flap reflection and mental nerve identification

Piezoelectric osteotomy

For inferior alveolar nerve lateralization, a linear occlusal cortical osteotomy was performed with a piezoelectric device (Mectron Piezosurgery Device, Mectron) by Piezosurgery osteotomy burs from the prepared implant bed into a medial and distal direction, extending 3–4 mm in length bilaterally, with a depth compatible with the existing thickness of the cortical bone [27].

The osteotomy separates the implant bed into 2 relatively equal parts. Subsequently, anterior and posterior vertical osteotomies (8–15 mm in length) are performed on the buccal surface of the mandible with the piezoelectric device to a depth compatible with the thickness of the cortical bone. A horizontal osteotomy is then performed with the piezoelectric device at the apical level to unite the vertical osteotomies and define a square bone window (Fig. 3), [27].

Osteotomies of a square bone window

Nerve relocation

The cortical bone block is removed with the aid of a periosteal elevator and kept hydrated in saline solution. After osteotomy, the mandibular canal is exposed, and the IAN is gently manipulated to remove cancellous bone fragments that may hinder a full mobilization of the IAN. The nerve was retracted laterally by a specially fabricated instrument (elastic tape), according to [28], and carefully protected in a way to allow implant bed preparation through its medial surface up to the desired bone depth (Figs. 4a, b).

a, b: nerve retracted laterally

Implant placement

The AnyRidge MegaGen dental implants were then inserted to the ideal locking depth, in accordance with the predetermined position and direction. The selected implants should be long enough to allow anchorage in the basal cortical bone of the mandibular body and provide adequate locking and stability [27].

Nerve repositioned and PRF Preparation

Blood has been drowned from the patient using a canula or directly by syringe and filling a plain tube. Place the tube in a centrifuge at 3000 rpm for 8–10 min. IAN was carefully repositioned to rest gently against the implants. On one side, a platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) membrane was placed between the implant surface and the neurovascular bundle, while on the contralateral side, a resorbable membrane was used in the same position.

Inlay autogenous bone graft repositioned

The inner surface of the previously harvested bone block can be contoured using specialized piezoelectric tips to ensure precise adaptation to the defect site at the end of the surgery, promoting close bone-to-implant contact. This approach results in an optimal inlay autogenous bone graft, which is the basis for the technique’s name. The bone block is then repositioned and secured in its original location to effectively close the bone defect (Figs. 5a and b) [27]. In the final step, soft tissues were carefully approximated and sutured using interrupted stitches with 3 − 0 silk sutures.

a and b: The bone block is adapted and restored to close the bone defect

Post-operative care

Patients received standard postoperative care instructions and were advised to avoid chewing on the operated side. A soft and liquid diet was recommended during the implant osseointegration period. Anti-inflammatory drugs, analgesics, and antibiotics were administered according to established protocols. Neurostimulatory drugs (methycobal amin) can be administered shortly after surgery, depending on the patient’s sensory perception and the IAN trauma extension. Sutures are removed only after confirmation of edge repair, usually 12 days after surgery. Implants are loaded 90 to 120 days after insertion [27].

Post-operative follow-up

Testing of nerve function and implant success rate during the scheduled follow-up visits was scheduled on the day after the procedure, and at 1, 3, and 6 months, immediately following the surgery [29]. Patients were asked about pain and neurosensory disturbance classified as positive (including paresthesia, dysesthesia, and hyperesthesia) or negative (such as hypoesthesia and anesthesia). The incidence and duration of ND were evaluated by the light touch test using cotton or gauze. A cotton tip applicator is used to determine sensation (Static Touch Detection Tests), and the Pin-prick test by an insulin needle and the tip of an explorer prob at predetermined intervals of time. Neurosensory evaluation was carried out on the 1 st and 7th postoperative day and every month thereafter until up to 6 months. The areas of impaired nerve function were mapped and followed during each follow-up appointment. The evaluation of nerve function was performed by two independent, calibrated examiners [23]. Implant success was reported when there was primary stability during insertion by ratchet. In addition, radiographs (OPGs and CBCT) (Figs. 6a, b, and c) were taken at 3 and 4 months after implant insertion to detect the marginal bone loss (MBL), if any. Painless, immobile under torque and while loading, and being free from any pathology around it on radiograph, were considered osseointegrated implants [23].

a: panoramic x-ray after operation, b: CBCT x-ray after operation, c: CBCT x-ray after operation

Abutments and prosthodontic procedures

The multi-unit abutments, designed by the manufacturer, were selected based on implant angulation and soft tissue thickness, with both straight and angled options (15° and 25°) and various collar heights available to accommodate individual anatomical variations. These abutments helped standardize the prosthetic platform, simplify the restoration delivery, and improve long-term outcomes (Fig. 7). All prostheses were cement-retained, except for one case where a screw-retained restoration was employed. The use of multi-unit abutments played a key role in managing implant angulation and facilitating a smooth and accurate prosthodontic alignment (Figs. 8a and b).

Data management and statistical analysis

All data were collected and then analyzed using statistical software (SPSS Version 27; SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). Descriptive analysis was performed as frequency and proportion for categorical variables, and the mean and standard deviation of quantitative variables. Analytical statistics were performed to find out the association between variables (Demographic characteristics, neurosensory disturbance, and success rate of implant) with IANL. As the normality test results were significant, indicating abnormally distributed quantitative data (Shapiro-Wilk test (P < 0.05)), Non-parametric tests were performed to measure the differences between pre- and post-times of operation (Friedman and Wilcoxon tests). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Five patients received a total of nine surgeries, with one patient receiving unilateral surgery and the other four receiving bilateral surgery. A total of 31 implants were inserted, averaging 6.2 implants per patient, or three implants each procedure. 77.7% of the patients were male, with only one patient (22.2%) being female. The ages of the patients ranged from 24 to 72 years, with a mean age of 56.22 ± 16.49. The procedures were conducted on the right side (55.5%) and the left side (44.4%). All patients had adverse symptoms, notably hypoesthesia or anesthesia, after the surgical treatment. All patients (100%) attended follow-up appointments and had numbness on the first day and week post-surgery.

Discussion

The main aim of this prospective clinical case series was to assess the incidence and development of ND after IANL. Prior research has highlighted the predominantly positive patient perception of IANL, with numerous individuals indicating minimal disruption to daily activities and a readiness to repeat the procedure owing to the substantial functional and aesthetic enhancements attained [19, 30]. This research provides significant insights into the occurrence of neurosensory disturbances following rapid implant insertion in patients with atrophic posterior mandibles. In accordance with prior research, all patients (100%) in this study experienced numbness on the first day and week following surgery. This observation aligns with prior investigations [23, 26, 31], which also reported universal early neurosensory complaints, confirming that transient sensory modification is a predictable consequence of the technique due to the direct manipulation of the inferior alveolar nerve during surgery. The occurrence of SD after IANL is common, and most of the studies included in a recent systematic review reported ND with all patients included in those studies [32]. Another case series reported the same findings in this regard [33].

After one month, a significant enhancement was noted in our group; nonetheless, 44.4% of patients persisted in experiencing neuropathic discomfort, with 33.3% reporting moderate hypoesthesia and 22.3% mild hypoesthesia. This trend indicates a more gradual recovery trajectory in contrast to one previous study [23], which documented a decline in ND to 20% after one month and 5.2% by the second month, and another study that noted a reduction to 26% at one month and 3% by six months. This gap may be ascribed to variations in surgical techniques, nerve protection measures, patient characteristics, or methodologies employed to evaluate sensory function.

At the three-month interval, the majority of patients (55.6%) reported mild hypoesthesia, 33.3% achieved complete recovery, and one patient (11.1%) persisted with anesthesia. At six months, 88.9% of patients attained neurosensory recovery, whereas only one patient exhibited enduring anesthesia; these results align favorably with Rosenquist et al. study [34], which indicated that one week of implantation, 21% of patients exhibited normal sensation, 32% demonstrated decreased function, and 47% experienced a complete lack of function. At six months, 77% exhibited normal function, 21% showed reduced function, and merely 2% retained no sensation. At 18 months, 94% of patients had restored normal sensation, whereas just 4% exhibited reduced function and 1% suffered from enduring loss. These findings corroborate the prevailing view that the majority of neurosensory deficits following IANL exhibit substantial improvement within six months and continue to enhance subsequently.

Our findings significantly differ from those of Atef, whose research in Egypt, involving a limited sample of seven patients, indicated no evidence of lasting neurosensory damage at six months postoperatively. This implies that complete recovery may be achievable within this period; nonetheless, the inconsistency highlights the possible impact of sample size, patient variability, and subjective assessment standards. The persistence of ND in one instance (11.1%) highlights the potential for individual variability in nerve repair, indicating the necessity for tailored patient counseling [35].

In this study, 30 of 31 dental implants successfully achieved osseointegration, resulting in a high success rate of 96.8%. These findings validate that the placement of dental implants subsequent to inferior alveolar nerve lateralization in the posterior segment of the mandible is a dependable and efficacious alternative. The results align with those documented by Campos et al. [36], who similarly noted elevated success rates, thereby reinforcing the dependability of this surgical method when executed according to appropriate clinical guidelines. Other studies have reported similar results. For example, Atef and Mounir [35] indicated that all implants exhibited clinical stability and effective osseointegration at the six-month follow-up, resulting in a 100% success rate. Correspondingly, Díaz and Gías reported an implant success rate of 97.3%, closely aligning with the findings of the current investigation. The data shows the success of implants with simultaneous nerve lateralization, particularly good case selection, and accurate procedures performed [23]. Additional long-term results from the study by Deryabin and Grybauskas provide significant insights, indicating an implant survival rate of 95.8% across a follow-up duration of 1 to 10 years. The monitoring period deemed all remaining implants clinically effective, notwithstanding the loss of one additional implant after five years of functional loading. This study illustrates both the initial efficacy of these therapies and their sustained performance under functioning conditions over time [26].

When examining comprehensive literature data, dental implant survival rates after IANL vary from 86.9% to 100%, with a reported average survival rate of 90.1%. This heterogeneity underscores disparities in surgical technique, follow-up duration, and patient-specific characteristics, while also reinforcing the general success of the strategy. Significantly, four studies failed to disclose the number of implants implanted [25, 37–39]. Collectively, these findings confirm that IANL, when properly suggested and carefully performed, can enhance high implant success and survival rates in both the short and long term. Despite potential transient nerve complications, the outcomes regarding the implant’s efficacy and weight-bearing capacity remain highly favorable. This highlights the technique’s effectiveness in situations where insufficient bone height would otherwise prevent the insertion of standard-length implants.

The present study findings indicate that the lack of a substantial difference in recovery outcomes between platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) and artificial collagen membranes is a crucial consideration in therapeutic decision-making. Although both membrane types showed comparable efficacy in facilitating sensory neuron regeneration, it is crucial to examine their comprehensive impacts on health and recovery. This finding is different from that in the systematic review by Vinci et al., who reported that the PRF was associated with accelerated nerve recovery [32]. From a biological standpoint, PRF provides numerous inherent benefits that surpass its structural function. In contrast to artificial collagen membranes that mostly function as passive barriers, PRF serves as an active support system capable of altering the circumstances surrounding a wound. The findings of the present study are also consistent with prior research that reported favorable outcomes following the use of PRF to enhance IAN regeneration during elective surgical procedures [40–42].

Conclusions

This study provides significant insights into the occurrence, development, and determinants of ND after mandibular surgical interventions involving implant insertion. The study concludes that neurosensory abnormalities are prevalent yet predominantly temporary outcomes of mandibular surgery. Ongoing follow-up is crucial to assess nerve healing and guarantee optimal patient results. As this study included a small sample size, these results are preliminary, and it is advisable to do larger, multicenter studies with prolonged follow-up durations to corroborate these findings and further investigate the long-term impacts of various biomaterials on nerve regeneration. Performing the research within a solitary clinical environment in Yemen may constrain the external validity across diverse populations and healthcare systems. The absence of a control group, such as individuals undergoing vertical bone augmentation treatments, complicates direct comparisons across the various treatment modalities.
