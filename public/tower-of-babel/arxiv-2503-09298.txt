Fractional currents and Young geometric integration
Philippe Bouafia

Abstract
. We introduce a class of flat currents with fractal properties, called fractional currents, which satisfy a compactness theorem and remain stable under pushforwards by Hölder continuous maps. In top dimension, fractional currents are the currents represented by functions belonging to a fractional Sobolev space. The space of α \alpha -fractional currents is in duality with a class of cochains, α \alpha -fractional charges, that extend both Whitney’s flat cochains and α \alpha -Hölder continuous forms. We construct a partially defined wedge product between fractional charges, enabling a generalization of the Young integral to arbitrary dimensions and codimensions. This helps us identify α \alpha -fractional m m -currents as metric currents of the snowflaked metric space ( ℝ d , d Eucl ( m + α ) / ( m + 1 ) ) (\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{Eucl}}^{(m+\alpha)/(m+1)}) .

1.
Introduction

In this article, we define a class of currents in Euclidean space,
referred to as fractional currents, on which a form of Hölder
non-smooth calculus can be performed. By this, we mean, in particular,
the ability to push forward fractional currents by Hölder maps
(subject to certain restrictions on the Hölder exponent) or to
integrate “rough” differential forms over fractional currents. We
summarize our main results.

1.1.
Fractional currents

To motivate the definition of fractional currents, we begin by
highlighting the importance of fractional Sobolev spaces in the Hölder
analysis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Notably, a recent result of De Lellis and
Inauen
[
8
]
demonstrates the fractional Sobolev regularity
of the Brouwer degree
\operatorname{deg}(f,U,\cdot)
for a Hölder
continuous map
f\colon\operatorname{cl}U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a
bounded open set
U
with a fractal boundary (under some restrictions
on the Hölder exponent of
f
and the regularity of
\partial U
).
This result suggests that, in order to define an appropriate class of
currents for which pushforwards by Hölder maps can be meaningfully
constructed, such currents should be representable in codimension
0

by fractional Sobolev functions.

It is in fact possible to characterize the fractional Sobolev space

W^{1-\alpha,1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with
0<\alpha<1
(the subscript
c

stands for compact support), in the language of currents. Recall that
a function
u\in L^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
belongs to
W^{1-\alpha,1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if
its Gagliardo norm

\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{d+1-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y

is finite. It was noted in
[
6
,
28
]
that

\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
represents an intermediate quantity
between the total variation
\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the
L^{1}
norm of

u
. Specifically, it satisfies the interpolation inequality

\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant C\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1-\alpha}\|u\|_{1}^{\alpha}

which, in fact, almost characterizes the Gagliardo norm
\|\cdot\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
in the sense that we now describe.

Let
N\colon L^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to[0,\infty]
denote the largest
seminorm that satisfies

(A)

N(u)\leqslant\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1-\alpha}\|u\|_{1}^{\alpha}
for all
u\in L^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

(B)

Lower semicontinuity:
N(u)\leqslant\liminf_{k\to\infty}N(u_{k})
for all sequences
(u_{k})
in
L^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that

L^{1}
-converge to
u
, with the additional condition that there is a
compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\spt u_{k}\subseteq K
for all

k
.

It is easy to prove that
N
is given by the formula

N(u)=\inf\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|Du_{k}\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1-\alpha}\|u_{k}\|_{1}^{\alpha}

where the infimum is taken over all decompositions of
u
as an

L^{1}
-convergent series
u=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}u_{k}
into components

u_{k}
of bounded variation and supported in a common compact subset of

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We will establish in Section
4
that
N
is
Lipschitz equivalent to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
. Note that this result is closely linked to
the idea presented in
[
27
]
, which considers the fractional
Sobolev space
W^{1-\alpha,1}
(
0<\alpha<1
) as an interpolation
space between
L^{1}
and
BV
.

In positive codimension, we obtain a suitable definition of a
fractional current by replacing, in the discussion above, the total
variation and the
L^{1}
-norm by higher-codimensional analogs, namely
the normal mass
\mathbf{N}
and Whitney’s flat norm
\mathbf{F}
. More precisely,
an
\alpha
-fractional
m
-current is a flat current
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that admits a decomposition into an
\mathbf{F}
-convergent
series
T=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}T_{k}
, where the components
T_{k}
are
normal
m
-currents supported in a common compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}<\infty.

The space of
\alpha
-fractional currents
\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
equipped with an adequate norm, lies between the space of normal
currents and the space of flat currents. This space extends the already
quite general class of fractal currents introduced by Züst
in
[
35
]
, see Subsection
3.3
.

1.2.
Related spaces of cochains

We establish a compactness theorem for fractional currents, extending
the classical Federer-Fleming compactness theorem for normal currents

[
14
, 4.2.17]
. This result will play a key role in the
construction of the pushforward operator by Hölder maps.

The proof of the compactness theorem partly relies on
functional-analytical arguments. We show indeed that

\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)
, the space of
\alpha
-fractional
m
-currents
supported in a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is a dual space
(hence, a form of weak compactness is provided by the
Banach-Alaoğlu theorem). As any predual of a Banach space
X
is
necessarily embedded in
X^{*}
, this calls for a description of the
dual space
\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)^{*}
. Elements of
\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)^{*}
are
cochains, that are of independent interest. Loosely speaking, they can
be viewed as Hölder versions of Whitney’s flat cochains. The next
paragraph provides an alternative description of
\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)^{*}
.

An
\alpha
-fractional charge
\omega
over
K
is a linear functional
on
\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
, the space of normal
m
-currents supported in
K
, that
satisfies the continuity condition

|\omega(T)|\leqslant C\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\alpha}

(1)

for all
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
. When
K
is convex,
\omega
extends by
density to a continuous linear functional on

\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)
. Conversely, any element of
\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)^{*}
,
restricted to
\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
, satisfies (
1
). Thus, the
space
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
of
\alpha
-fractional
m
-charges is
(isomorphic to) the dual of
\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)
in the case where
K
is
convex.

One important class of
\alpha
-fractional charges consists of

\alpha
-Hölder continuous differential forms. More precisely, to each
such form
\eta\in\rmLip^{\alpha}(K;\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one associates
the functional
\Lambda(\eta)
that acts on normal currents
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
by

\Lambda(\eta)(T)=\int_{K}\langle\eta(x),\vec{T}(x)\rangle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\|T\|(x).

In the simplest case where
m=0
, the map
\Lambda
is in fact a
Banach space isomorphism between
\rmLip^{\alpha}(K)
and
\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(K)
. As a result, we can identify
\alpha
-fractional

0
-charges over
K
with
\alpha
-Hölder continuous functions on

K
. In this case, it is known that
\rmLip^{\alpha}(K)
is the double
dual of the little Hölder space, described for example in

[
32
, Chapter 4]
. We treat the general case
m\geqslant 0
by
analogy. We define the space of little
\alpha
-fractional
m
-charges

\mathbf{ch}^{m,\alpha}(K)
as the closure in
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
of

\{\Lambda(\omega_{\mid K}):\omega\text{ smooth}\}
, and we
establish that
\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)\cong\mathbf{ch}^{m,\alpha}(K)^{*}
. Thus any
bounded sequence in
\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)
possesses a weakly convergent
subsequence (in the sense of currents), by the Banach-Alaoğlu
theorem. Convergence in flat norm is later obtained by adapting the
classical deformation theorem to the setting of fractional currents.

Our initial motivation for introducing fractional charges was to
provide a generalization in positive codimension of the Hölder charges
introduced in
[
2
]
and further studied in
[
3
]
.
The reader is referred to the survey
[
4
]
for
an overview of these works, as well as for examples of fractional
charges arising from stochastic processes.

1.3.
Generalized Stokes’ formula

The exterior derivative operator for fractional charges is
well-defined as the adjoint of the boundary operator. Specifically,

\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega
is the
\alpha
-fractional
(m+1)
-charge over
K
,
given by

\langle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega,T\rangle=\langle\omega,\partial T\rangle

(2)

for
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
. If
K
is convex, this formula extends more
generally to
\alpha
-fractional currents over
K
.

A particular case of this formula deserves attention, as it results in
a generalized Gauss-Green formula for Hölder vector fields over
fractal boundaries. It extends the work of Harrison and
Norton
[
19
]
(it should be however stressed that
formula (
2
) is purely definitional, contrary to the
more constructive approach taken in
[
19
,
18
]
). Indeed, we
can take for
U
a bounded open set with finite
(1-\alpha)
-perimeter
(meaning that
\mathbbm{1}_{U}\in W_{c}^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, or equivalently,

\llbracket U\rrbracket
is
\alpha
-fractional). We shall prove that
this condition is weaker than the
(d-1+\alpha)
-summability
(see
[
19
, §3]
or Subsection
3.2
)
of
\partial U
that is required in the Gauss-Green formula of
Harrison-Norton. In this case, formula (
2
) applies
for
T=\llbracket U\rrbracket
and
\omega
that is associated to
an
\alpha
-Hölder continuous
(d-1)
-form.

1.4.
Pushforward by Hölder maps

The pushforward of currents by Hölder maps
f
has been explored in
the literature, notably in
[
15
]
and

[
33
]
. The results in
[
33
]
complement ours, as they
address the case where the components
f_{i}
of
f
have different
Hölder exponents.

Our contribution is to provide a functional-analytical setting to this
construction by showing that
f_{\#}
is a bounded linear operator between
spaces of fractional currents. Indeed, in Section
8
,
we define the pushforward
f_{\#}T
of an
\alpha
-fractional

m
-current
T
by a
\gamma
-Hölder continuous map
f
. The
construction makes sense as long as
m+\alpha<\gamma(m+1)
. Under
this assumption, we show that
f_{\#}T
is
\beta
-fractional, where

\beta
is given by the equation

\frac{m+\alpha}{\gamma}=m+\beta.

The above inequality provides an interpretation of the parameter

\alpha
as an upper bound for the fractional part of the fractal
dimension of both
T
and its boundary
\partial T
.

In top dimension,
i.e.
, whenever
T
is a
d
-dimensional
current in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
f
is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued, the pushforward is defined
under the less restrictive condition
d-1+\alpha<\gamma(d-1)
. When applied to
T=\llbracket U\rrbracket
, this
pushforward construction allows us to recover the result of De Lellis
and Inauen on the fractional Sobolev regularity of the Brouwer degree

\operatorname{deg}(f,U,\cdot)
, and even strengthens it slightly by
treating the critical case where
U
has finite fractional perimeter.

1.5.
Young geometric integration

Züst introduced in
[
34
]
a notable generalization of the
one-dimensional Young integration to Hölder forms, making sense of
geometric integrals like

\int_{[0,1]^{d}}g^{0}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}g^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}g^{d}

(3)

where
g^{0},\dots,g^{d}
are Hölder-continuous functions over
[0,1]^{d}
, with Hölder exponents
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{d}\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{]}
such that

\alpha_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}>d.

(4)

The integral (
3
) is there defined via approximation by
Riemann sums, and the wedge products that appear in (
3
)
are purely formal. The condition (
4
) was shown to be
sharp for the convergence of these sums; see

[
34
, Section 3.2]
. In the context of (zero-codimensional)
charges, a generalization of the Züst integral was proposed
in
[
3
]
. Recently, T. Jaffard described precisely the
regularity of the differential forms in
3
in
[
20
]
,
in terms of negative Hölder spaces.

Our goal in Section
9
is to set up a rough exterior
calculus apparatus in which differential forms, such as the ones
in (
3
), are well-defined. In fact, the elements
g^{0}
,

\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}g^{1},\dots,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}g^{d}
can be seen as fractional charges. We
construct more generally a wedge product between fractional charges:
if
\omega
and
\eta
are respectively
\alpha
- and

\beta
-fractional charges (of any order), we make sense of
\omega\wedge\eta
provided that the Young-type condition
\alpha+\beta>1
is met. The resulting product
\omega\wedge\eta
is an
(\alpha+\beta-1)
-fractional charge. Using this wedge product,
g^{0}\wedge\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}g^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}g^{d}
is then a well-defined

(\alpha_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}-d)
-fractional charge, which can
therefore be integrated not only over
[0,1]^{d}
, but over any
fractional
d
-current. More generally, the wedge product extends the
Züst integral in any codimension.

Recently, Chandra and Singh undertook a similar effort to develop a
rough geometric integration theory in
[
7
]
. Their approach
is probabilistic in nature, relying on a multidimensional sewing
lemma. Establishing a connection between their space of chains and
cochains and ours is an open question.

1.6.
Outline of the paper

Section
2
contains preliminaries, mostly on normal and
flat currents. Unlike the introduction, we reverse the order of
exposition: we begin in Section
3
with the general
definition of fractional currents and their fundamental properties
before turning to the top-dimensional case in
Section
4
, where we show the equivalence between

\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
W^{1-\alpha,1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Sections
3.2

and
3.3
present examples of fractional currents; they
are not essential for the remainder of the paper.

In Section
5
, we study the space of
m
-charges

\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
over a compact
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose elements are linear
functionals on
\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
such that for any sequence
(T_{n})
in

\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
,

\omega(T_{n})\to 0\text{ if }\sup_{n\geqslant 0}\mathbf{N}(T_{n})<\infty\text{
and }\mathbf{F}(T_{n})\to 0.

This continuity condition is weaker than (
1
). The
inclusion
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)\subseteq\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
can be informally

. This section contains essentially no new results. Fractional

.

Our main results are in the subsequent sections: the compactness

Sections
7
and
8
. Young geometric
integration is the object of Section
9
. Finally, we show
in Section
10
that fractional currents are

two sections do not depend on Sections
7

and
8
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notations

We will work in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of dimension
d\geqslant 1
,
endowed with the standard Euclidean norm
|\cdot|
. For any compact
set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\varepsilon>0
, the compact

\varepsilon
-tubular neighborhood of
K
is denoted

B(K,\varepsilon)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\operatorname{dist}(x,K)\leqslant\varepsilon\right\},

where
\operatorname{dist}(x,K)
denotes the distance between
x
and

K
. The Lebesgue (outer) measure is denoted
\mathscr{L}^{d}
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
(E,\|\cdot\|)
be a
finite-dimensional normed linear space. For any
f\colon X\to E

and
\alpha\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{]}
, we denote

\rmLip^{\alpha}f=\sup\left\{\frac{\|f(x)-f(y)\|}{d(x,y)^{\alpha}}:x,y\in X,x\neq y\right\}

and by
\rmLip^{\alpha}(X;E)
the space of maps
f\colon X\to E
such
that
\rmLip^{\alpha}f<\infty
. We abbreviate
\rmLip^{\alpha}(X;\mathbb{R})=\rmLip^{\alpha}(X)
.

The space of continuous functions
X\to E
is denoted
C(X;E)
. We
abbreviate
C(X;\mathbb{R})=C(X)
. For any
f\in C(X;E)
and any subset

K\subseteq X
, we write

\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup\left\{\|f(x)\|:x\in X\right\}\text{ and }\|f\|_{\infty,K}=\sup\left\{\|f(x)\|:x\in K\right\}.

Throughout the paper, multiplicative constants are denoted by
C
; their value may change from line to line, and even within the same line.

2.2.
Currents

We will work within the setting of Federer-Fleming’s currents. For an
in-depth exploration of this subject, we refer the reader to the
treatise
[
14
]
. In this preliminary part, our focus will be on
defining common notations, highlighting those that deviate from

[
14
]
, and revisiting a few definitions.

The spaces of
m
-vectors and
m
-covectors are
\wedge_{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and they are respectively given the mass and comass
norm described in
[
14
, 1.8.1]
, both of which will be denoted
here by
\|\cdot\|
. We will use the bracket notation
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for the duality between
m
-covectors and

m
-vectors.

All our currents will be compactly supported, defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
their order will be typically denoted by the integer
m
. We recall
that a compactly supported
m
-current is a member of the topological
dual of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the space of smooth

m
-forms, equipped with the locally convex topology described
in
[
14
, 4.1.1]
. The support
\spt T
of a compactly supported

m
-current
T
is the smallest compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

T(\omega)=0
for all smooth
m
-forms supported in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\spt T
.

A sequence of compactly supported currents

(T_{n})
is said to converge weakly to
T
whenever
T_{n}(\omega)\to T(\omega)
for every compactly supported smooth
m
-form
\omega
.

For any compactly supported
m
-current
T
and any smooth
k
-form

\eta
, where
k\leqslant m
, we define the compactly supported

(m-k)
-current
T\>{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}\hskip-0.2pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt,width=7.0pt,depth=0.0pt}\,\eta
by
(T\>{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}\hskip-0.2pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt,width=7.0pt,depth=0.0pt}\,\eta)(\omega)=T(\eta\wedge\omega)
.

The mass of a compactly supported current
T
is

\mathbf{M}(T)=\sup\left\{T(\omega):\omega\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})\text{ and }\|\omega\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1\right\}.

When
\mathbf{M}(T)<\infty
, the
m
-current
T
is representable by
integration: there is a unique Radon measure
\|T\|
, a

\|T\|
-measurable unit
m
-vector field
\vec{T}
, unique

\|T\|
-almost everywhere, such that for all
\omega\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

T(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\omega(x),\vec{T}(x)\rangle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\|T\|(x).

Additionally, one has
\spt\|T\|=\spt T
.

In case
m\geqslant 1
, we define the boundary
\partial T
to
be the compactly supported current
\omega\mapsto T(\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega)
. The normal mass of
T
is

\mathbf{N}(T)=\mathbf{M}(T)+\mathbf{M}(\partial T)

if
m\geqslant 1
and
\mathbf{N}(T)=\mathbf{M}(T)
if
m=0
. A compactly supported
current
T
is said to be normal whenever
\mathbf{N}(T)<\infty
, and the
space of normal
m
-currents is denoted
\mathbf{N}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Examples of normal
m
-currents are provided by compactly supported
smooth
m
-currents. Those are the currents of the form

\omega\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\omega(x),\xi(x)\rangle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\mathscr{L}^{d}(x)

where
\xi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\wedge_{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a compactly supported
smooth
m
-vector field. Such a current will be denoted
\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\xi
.

We define the flat norm of a normal current
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in a
way which departs slightly from Federer’s exposure:

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(T)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{T(\omega):\omega\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})\text{ and }\max\left\{\|\omega\|_{\infty},\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\|_{\infty}\right\}\leqslant 1\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\mathbf{M}(S)+\mathbf{M}(T-\partial S):S\in\mathbf{N}_{m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right\}.

The proof of the above equality is similar to

[
14
, 4.1.12]
. From the first equality, it is clear that
convergence in flat norm implies weak convergence.

A compactly supported
m
-current
T
is said to be flat whenever
there is a sequence
(T_{n})
of normal
m
-currents that is

\mathbf{F}
-Cauchy, weakly converges to
T
, and such that
\bigcup_{n}\spt T_{n}
is relatively compact. The flat norm of
T
is then defined to be

\mathbf{F}(T)=\lim\mathbf{F}(T_{n})
. When
T
is not flat, we set
\mathbf{F}(T)=\infty
. The space of flat
m
-currents is denoted by
\mathbf{F}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

It may be worth noting that, for every
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one has

\mathbf{F}(T)\leqslant\mathbf{M}(T)\leqslant\mathbf{N}(T),

(5)

a fact that will be used repeatedly.

The pushforward
f_{\#}T
of a flat
m
-current
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by a
locally Lipschitz map
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
is the flat

m
-current
f_{\#}T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
defined as
in
[
14
, 4.1.14]
. It depends only on the values of
f
on
\spt T
, so we may write
f_{\#}T
if the domain of
f
contains
\spt T
and
the restriction
f_{\mid\spt T}
is Lipschitz continuous.

For any compact subset
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)

\displaystyle=\{T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\spt T\subseteq K\}

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{m}(K)

\displaystyle=\{T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\spt T\subseteq K\}

The construction of charges ultimately relies on the Federer-Fleming’s
compactness theorem of normal currents in flat norm. The following
version uses the flat norm
\mathbf{F}
(rather than
\mathbf{F}_{K}
as in

[
14
, 4.2.17(1)]
). It can be easily deduced from the original
version. Alternatively, it is possible to reproduce the arguments in
the proof of Federer-Fleming, as done in

[
11
, Theorem 4.2]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Compactness)
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact. For all
c\geqslant 0
, the ball
\{T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K):\mathbf{N}(T)\leqslant c\}
is
\mathbf{F}
-compact.

In this article, convolutions will play an important role in
regularizing various chains or cochains. Here, we simply recall how
convolution works at the level of currents. The convolution of a
compactly supported
m
-current
T
with a function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the compactly supported
m
-current defined by

(T*\phi)(\omega)=T(\check{\phi}*\omega)\qquad\text{for all }\omega\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})

where
\check{\phi}(x)=\phi(-x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Throughout the article, we fix a
C^{\infty}
function
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with compact support in the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is
nonnegative and such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi=1
. For the sake of
simplicity, we will further assume that
\Phi
is even. For all

\varepsilon>0
, we define
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-d}\Phi(\varepsilon^{-1}x)
.

For the reader’s convenience, we collect here several well-known facts concerning normal and flat currents that will be used throughout the paper.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
K
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
f,g\colon K\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
be two
Lipschitz maps and
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}(K)
.

(A)

If
m\geqslant 1
, one has

\mathbf{N}(f_{\#}T)\leqslant\max\{(\rmLip f)^{m},(\rmLip f)^{m-1}\}\mathbf{N}(T).

If
m=0
then
\mathbf{N}(f_{\#}T)\leqslant\mathbf{N}(T)
.

(B)

\mathbf{F}(f_{\#}T)\leqslant\max\{(\rmLip f)^{m},(\rmLip f)^{m+1}\}\mathbf{N}(T)
.

(C)

\spt f_{\#}T\subseteq f(\spt T)
.

(D)

If
m\geqslant 1
, one has

\mathbf{F}(f_{\#}T-g_{\#}T)\leqslant\|f-g\|_{\infty,K}\max\{(\rmLip f)^{m},(\rmLip f)^{m-1},(\rmLip g)^{m},(\rmLip g)^{m-1}\}\mathbf{N}(T).

If
m=0
then
\mathbf{F}(f_{\#}T-g_{\#}T)\leqslant\|f-g\|_{\infty,K}\mathbf{N}(T)
.

(E)

\mathbf{M}(T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})\leqslant\mathbf{M}(T)
,
\mathbf{F}(T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})\leqslant\mathbf{F}(T)
and
\mathbf{N}(T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})\leqslant\mathbf{N}(T)
.

(F)

\mathbf{N}(T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})\leqslant C\varepsilon^{-1}\mathbf{F}(T)
if
\varepsilon\leqslant 1
.

(G)

\mathbf{F}(T-T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})\leqslant\varepsilon\mathbf{N}(T)
.

(H)

\spt(T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})\subseteq B(\spt T,\varepsilon)
.

Specifically, we refer to
[
14
, 4.1.13, 4.1.14]
for properties (A) to (D), while (E) to (G) follow mainly from
[
14
, 4.1.2]
. In (F), the constant
C
depends only on the dimension
d
(through the choice of
\Phi
). Recall from (
5
) that the flat norm
\mathbf{F}
is weaker than the norm
\mathbf{N}
. This provides a useful heuristic for the appearance of the coefficients: an
\varepsilon^{-1}
factor in (F), where one estimates a stronger quantity in terms of a weaker one, and an
\varepsilon
factor in (G), reflecting the converse situation.

3.
Fractional currents

3.1.
Definition and first properties

Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
. We say that a flat
m
-current
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is
\alpha
-fractional
whenever one can find a
sequence
(T_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
of normal currents satisfying the
following properties:

(A)

there is a compact set
K
such that
\spt T_{k}\subseteq K

for all
k
;

(B)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}<\infty
;

(C)

T=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}T_{k}
.

We recall that
\mathbf{F}\leqslant\mathbf{M}\leqslant\mathbf{N}
, which ensures that

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}<\infty

by (B). This guarantees that the series in (C) is in fact

\mathbf{F}
-convergent, and it is in this sense that it should be
understood. A sequence
(T_{k})
that satisfies (A), (B) and (C) is
called an
\alpha
-fractional decomposition
of
T
. This
paragraph makes it clear that any permutation of the sequence
(T_{k})

is still an
\alpha
-fractional decomposition of
T
. In the remainder
of the paper, we may at times consider decompositions indexed over a
countable set other than
\{0,1,2,\dots\}
.

We will denote the space of
\alpha
-fractional currents by

\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and associate to each
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

the
\alpha
-fractional norm

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)=\inf\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}

(6)

where the infimum is taken over all
\alpha
-fractional decompositions
of
T
(the fact that
\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}
is a norm will become apparent in
Proposition
3.1
). If
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is not

\alpha
-fractional, we set
\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)=\infty
.

Additionally, for compact sets
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we will denote by

\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)
the space of
\alpha
-fractional currents
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\spt T\subseteq K
. In case
K
is
compact convex and
T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(K)
, the infimum
in (
6
) can be taken only over
\alpha
-fractional
decompositions
(T_{k})
of
T
into normal currents that are all
supported in
K
(as we can replace each
T_{k}
by its pushforward by
the orthogonal projection onto
K
). This fact will be often used
implicitly.

The following comments are in order.

•

The boundary cases where
\alpha=0
or
\alpha=1
correspond
to well-established classes of currents. Specifically, it can be
easily verified that
\mathbf{F}_{m}^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the space of normal currents
(with
\mathbf{F}^{0}
being the normal mass
\mathbf{N}
) while
\mathbf{F}^{1}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is
the space of flat currents (with
\mathbf{F}^{1}=\mathbf{F}
). As we will observe
later, many properties of fractional currents hold exclusively for

0<\alpha<1
, and the extreme cases demand closer scrutiny.

•

The definition of fractional currents can be readily extended to
the metric setting, with coefficients in a complete normed Abelian
group, as defined in
[
10
]
. In this context, the sequence
of normal currents
(T_{k})
is to be replaced with rectifiable

G
-chains. However in this work, we focus exclusively on Euclidean
fractional currents with real coefficients, the only case for which
a compactness theorem is currently known.

•

We will aim to convince the reader that an
\alpha
-fractional
current, while being an
m
-dimensional object (in the sense that it
can be integrated against
m
-forms), is characterized by the
property that its “fractal dimension” (a loosely defined notion)
is at most
m+\alpha
, and that the fractal dimension of its
boundary at most
m-1+\alpha
. Thus, the parameter
\alpha
can be
understood as representing an upper bound of the fractional part of
the fractal dimension.

In the next proposition, we gather some immediate properties of
fractional currents.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact set. The following hold.

(A)

If
\beta
is such that
\alpha\leqslant\beta\leqslant 1
,
then
\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and

\mathbf{F}^{\beta}\leqslant\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}
.

(B)

For
m\geqslant 1
, the boundary operator
\partial
takes

\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\mathbf{F}_{m-1}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(\partial T)\leqslant\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)
for all
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

(C)

\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a linear space, normed by

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}
.

(D)

(\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K),\mathbf{F}^{\alpha})
is a Banach space.

(E)

In case
K
is convex,
\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
is dense in

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K)
.

(F)

If
T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
f\colon\spt T\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
is a Lipschitz map, then
f_{\#}T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
and

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(f_{\#}T)\leqslant\max\{(\rmLip f)^{m-1+\alpha},(\rmLip f)^{m+\alpha}\}\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)

(7)

if
m\geqslant 1
, and

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(f_{\#}T)\leqslant\max\{1,(\rmLip f)^{\alpha}\}\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)

if
m=0
.

Proof.

(A) and (B) are easy consequences of the inequalities

\mathbf{F}(T)\leqslant\mathbf{N}(T),\qquad\mathbf{F}(\partial T)\leqslant\mathbf{F}(T),\qquad\mathbf{N}(\partial T)=\mathbf{M}(\partial T)\leqslant\mathbf{N}(T)

that hold for any normal current
T
.

(C). As
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{F}^{1}\leqslant\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}
by (A) and
\mathbf{F}
is a norm, the
equality
\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)=0
readily implies that
T=0
.

The set
\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is closed under addition, for if

(T_{k})
and
(S_{k})
are
\alpha
-fractional decompositions of
T

and
S
, we can construct the decomposition
(R_{k})
of
T+S
, with

R_{2k}=T_{k}
and
R_{2k+1}=S_{k}
. Furthermore, one has

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T+S)\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(R_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(R_{k})^{\alpha}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(S_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(S_{k})^{\alpha}

Taking the infimum on the right-hand side over all decompositions

(T_{k})
and
(S_{k})
yields the triangle inequality for

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}
.

(D). To prove that
\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)
is complete, it is sufficient
to prove that an
\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}
-absolutely convergent series
\sum_{n}T^{(n)}
actually converges in
\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K)
. The proof of this
is fairly standard; we do it by considering for each
T^{(n)}
an

\alpha
-fractional decomposition
(T_{k}^{(n)})_{k}
with

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k}^{(n)})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k}^{(n)})^{\alpha}<\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T^{(n)})+\frac{1}{2^{n}}

Without loss of generality, we can assume that all of
T_{k}^{(n)}

are supported in the same compact set (
K
is contained in a closed
ball, then replace if necessary
T_{k}^{(n)}
by its projection onto
this ball). Then define

T=\sum_{n,k}T_{k}^{(n)}

This series is well-defined (it is in fact absolutely convergent in
flat norm). Clearly,
T
is
\alpha
-fractional, as

(T_{k}^{(n)})_{k,n}
is an
\alpha
-fractional decomposition, and

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}\left(T-\sum_{n=0}^{N}T^{(n)}\right)\leqslant\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\left(\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T^{(n)})+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)\to 0

as
N\to\infty
.

(E). Let
T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K)
and
(T_{k})
be an

\alpha
-fractional decomposition of
T
such that all
T_{k}
are
supported in
K
. It is clear that

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}\left(T-\sum_{k=0}^{N}T_{k}\right)\leqslant\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}\to 0

as
N\to\infty
.

(F). First extend
f
to a Lipschitz map
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, while
preserving the Lipschitz constant
L=\rmLip f
. Let
(T_{k})
be an

\alpha
-fractional decomposition of
T
. Observe that
(f_{\#}T_{k})

is an
\alpha
-fractional decomposition of
f_{\#}T
. Thus,
f_{\#}T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
and, if
m\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(f_{\#}T)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(f_{\#}T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(f_{\#}T_{k})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\max\{L^{(m-1)(1-\alpha)},L^{m(1-\alpha)}\}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\max\{L^{m\alpha},L^{(m+1)\alpha}\}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\max\{L^{m-1+\alpha},L^{m+\alpha}\}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}

Taking the infimum over all
\alpha
-fractional decompositions of

T
results in the inequality (
7
). The case
m=0

is treated similarly.
∎

3.2.
A first example

Many fractal-like objects can be seen as fractional currents. Although
a complete characterization of fractional currents with codimension

0
will be provided in Section
4
, we introduce a
preliminary example here. Recall that a
dyadic
k
-cube
(in
short:
k
-cube) is a set of the form

Q=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left[\frac{\ell_{i}}{2^{k}},\frac{\ell_{i}+1}{2^{k}}\right]

where
k,\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
are integers. Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be
a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For any integer
k
, we let
N_{A}(k)
be
the number of
k
-cubes that intersect
A
. Following
[
19
]
, we
say that
A
is
m
-summable
(where
m\geqslant 0
is real) whenever

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}N_{A}(k)2^{-km}<\infty

Summability is related to the
upper box dimension
of
A
, which is defined by

\dim_{\mathrm{box}}A=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log N_{A}(k)}{k\log 2}

Indeed, one can easily check that
A
is
m
-summable for all
m>\dim_{\mathrm{box}}A
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded open set of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\partial U
is
(d-1+\alpha)
-summable, for
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Then
\llbracket U\rrbracket
is
\alpha
-fractional.

Proof.

This proof is heavily influenced by
[
19
, Section 3]
and

[
35
, Lemma 4.4]
. We assume that
U
is nonempty, otherwise
there is nothing to prove. The main ingredient will be the Whitney
decomposition of
U
, that we now describe.

As
U
is bounded and nonempty, there is a smallest integer
k_{0}\geqslant 0
such that some
k_{0}
-cube and its
3^{d}-1
neighbors are
contained in
U
. Let
\mathscr{Q}_{k_{0}}
be the collection of such

k_{0}
-cubes. Then we define inductively the collections

\mathscr{Q}_{k_{0}+1},\mathscr{Q}_{k_{0}+2},\dots
, where for all
k\geqslant k_{0}+1
, the collection
\mathscr{Q}_{k}
consists of all
k
-cubes
Q
such that

(A)

Q
and all of its
3^{d}-1
neighbors are contained in
U
;

(B)

Q
is not contained in any cube of
\mathscr{Q}_{\ell}
, for
some
k_{0}\leqslant\ell<k
.

Let
k\geqslant k_{0}+1
. By construction, the parent
(k-1)
-cube of
any
Q\in\mathscr{Q}_{k}
, or one of its neighbors, intersects
\partial U
. Therefore,

\operatorname{Card}\mathscr{Q}_{k}\leqslant 3^{d}\times 2^{d}N_{\partial U}(k-1)\leqslant CN_{\partial U}(k-1)

Note that
\mathscr{Q}_{k_{0}}
is finite as well by boundedness of
U
. We
define, for each
k\geqslant k_{0}
, the normal current

T_{k}=\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{Q}_{k}}\llbracket Q\rrbracket

Moreover, one clearly has

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}(T_{k})\leqslant\operatorname{Card}\mathscr{Q}_{k}\left(2^{-kd}+2d2^{-k(d-1)}\right)\leqslant C\left(\operatorname{Card}\mathscr{Q}_{k}\right)2^{-k(d-1)}

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(T_{k})\leqslant\mathbf{M}(T_{k})\leqslant\left(\operatorname{Card}\mathscr{Q}_{k}\right)2^{-kd}

Consequently,

\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}\leqslant C\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}(\operatorname{Card}\mathscr{Q}_{k})2^{-k(d-1+\alpha)}<\infty

Hence the sequence of normal currents
(T_{k})
satisfies
condition (B) of Definition
3.1
.
∎

3.3.
Züst’s fractal currents

In this subsection, we exhibit another class of currents that are
proven to be
\alpha
-fractional. It was introduced
in
[
35
, Definition 4.1]
. The definition is adapted here to the
Euclidean setting, using Federer-style currents instead of metric
currents.

Let
m\leqslant\gamma<m+1
and
m-1\leqslant\delta<m
. An

m
-dimensional flat current
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is said to be

(\gamma,\delta)
-fractal
in the sense of Züst whenever there
are sequences
(R_{k})
in
\mathbf{N}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
(S_{k})
in

\mathbf{N}_{m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and parameters
\sigma,\rho>1
(depending on
T
) such that

(A)

there is a compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the
supports of
R_{k}
,
S_{k}
all lie in
K
;

(B)

one has

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{M}(S_{k})\sigma^{k(m+1-\gamma)}<\infty,\qquad\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{M}(\partial S_{k})\sigma^{k(m-\gamma)}<\infty

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{M}(R_{k})\rho^{k(m-\delta)}<\infty,\qquad\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{M}(\partial R_{k})\rho^{k(m-1-\delta)}<\infty

(C)

T=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(R_{k}+\partial S_{k})
weakly.

A helpful way to think about
T
is as having a fractal dimension less
than
\gamma
, while its boundary can be seen as having a fractal
dimension less than
\delta
. One limitation of Züst’s fractal currents, compared to fractional currents, is that they do not form a linear space. Fractional currents are more general, as we now show:

Proposition 3.3
.

A
(\gamma,\delta)
-fractal current (in the sense of Züst) is
\alpha
-fractional for
\alpha=\max\{\gamma-m,\delta-(m-1)\}
.

Proof.

Let
T
be a
(\gamma,\delta)
-fractal current. We adopt the notations introduced at the beginning of the subsection.
First we estimate

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}(\partial S_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(\partial S_{k})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{M}(\partial S_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{M}(S_{k})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\mathbf{M}(\partial S_{k})\sigma^{-k(m+1-\gamma)\alpha/(1-\alpha)}\right)^{1-\alpha}\left(\mathbf{M}(S_{k})\sigma^{k(m+1-\gamma)}\right)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant(1-\alpha)\mathbf{M}(\partial S_{k})\sigma^{-k(m+1-\gamma)\alpha/(1-\alpha)}+\alpha\mathbf{M}(S_{k})\sigma^{k(m+1-\gamma)}

by Young’s inequality. Using
\alpha\geqslant\gamma-m
and
\sigma>1
,
one further obtains

\mathbf{N}(\partial S_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(\partial S_{k})^{\alpha}\leqslant(1-\alpha)\mathbf{M}(\partial S_{k})\sigma^{k(m-\gamma)}+\alpha\mathbf{M}(S_{k})\sigma^{k(m+1-\gamma)}

(8)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}(R_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(R_{k})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant(1-\alpha)\mathbf{N}(R_{k})\rho^{-k(m-\delta)\alpha/(1-\alpha)}+\alpha\mathbf{F}(R_{k})\rho^{k(m-\delta)}

\displaystyle\leqslant(1-\alpha)\mathbf{N}(R_{k})\rho^{k(m-1-\delta)}+\alpha\mathbf{F}(R_{k})\rho^{k(m-\delta)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{M}(R_{k})\rho^{k(m-\delta)}\left(\alpha+\frac{1-\alpha}{\rho^{k}}\right)+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{M}(\partial R_{k})\rho^{k(m-1-\delta)}

(9)

The inequalities (
8
), (
9
) and (B) show
that
(T_{k})
, where
T_{2k}=\partial S_{k}
and
T_{2k+1}=R_{k}
, is
an
\alpha
-fractional decomposition of
T
. Hence
T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This shows, by the way, that the sum in (C) is

\mathbf{F}
-convergent.
∎

4.
Fractional Sobolev regularity of zero-codimensional fractional currents

It is well-known that normal currents of top dimension have the form

\llbracket u\rrbracket
, where
u
is a compactly supported
BV

function, that is, a
L^{1}_{c}
function such that the De Giorgi
variation

\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\operatorname{div}v:v\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\text{ and }\|v\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1\right\}

is finite, in which case
\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\mathbf{M}(\partial\llbracket u\rrbracket)
, see
[
14
, Section 4.5]
. As for flat currents, they are the currents of the form

\llbracket u\rrbracket
, where
u
is a compactly supported
integrable function, see
[
14
, 4.1.18]
. We will characterize in Theorem
4.1

the functions
u
such that
\llbracket u\rrbracket\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as members of a fractional Sobolev
space.
We will assume throughout this section that
0<\alpha<1
.

4.1.
Fractional Sobolev space

Here, we will simply recall the definition of fractional Sobolev
spaces, referring the reader to
[
24
]
for a
comprehensive discussion on the subject.

The
W^{1-\alpha,1}
-norm
of an
L^{1}
function
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined to be

\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{d+1-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y.

This quantity defines a genuine norm (and not merely a seminorm) on

L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, since the only constant function belonging to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

is the zero function.

In the case where
u
is the indicator function of a Lebesgue
measurable set
A
, we call it the
(1-\alpha)
-fractional
perimeter
of
A
:

\operatorname{Per}_{1-\alpha}(A)=\|\mathbbm{1}_{A}\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=2\int_{A}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus A}\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{d+1-\alpha}}

We let
W^{1-\alpha,1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the space of all compactly
supported integrable functions
u
such that
\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty
.

4.2.
A norm equivalent to
\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}

We observe that the
W^{1-\alpha,1}
-norm behaves well under
rescaling. Indeed,

\|u\circ\varphi_{r}^{-1}\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=r^{d-1+\alpha}\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

for
r>0
, where
\varphi_{r}(x)=rx
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

On the space
\mathbf{F}_{d}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of top-dimensional
\alpha
-fractional currents, it will be more convenient to work with a
norm that has better homogeneity properties than
\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}
. We
define

\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\alpha}(T)=\inf\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{M}(\partial T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{M}(T_{k})^{\alpha}

where the infimum is taken over all
\alpha
-fractional decompositions
of
T
. One checks that
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\alpha}(\varphi_{r\#}T)=r^{d-1+\alpha}\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\alpha}(T)
.

The norms
\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\alpha}
are equivalent, in a
sense that we now describe. Of course, one has
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\alpha}\leqslant\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}
, as
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{M}
for top-dimensional currents. As for
the converse inequality, we will prove that

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)\leqslant C(K)\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\alpha}(T)\qquad\text{for all }T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{d}(K)

(10)

where
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact and
C(K)
is a constant depending
only on
K
.

We lose no generality in assuming that
K
is convex, as we can
replace it with its closed convex hull. Consider an

\alpha
-fractional decomposition
(T_{k})
of
T
, with
\spt T_{k}\subseteq K
for all
k
. Each
T_{k}=\llbracket u_{k}\rrbracket
is
represented by a
BV
function
u_{k}
, supported in
K
. By the Hölder
and Sobolev inequality (see
[
13
, Theorem 5.10(i)]
), one has

\mathbf{M}(T_{k})=\|u_{k}\|_{1}\leqslant\|u_{k}\|_{d/(d-1)}|K|^{1/d}\leqslant C\|Du_{k}\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})|K|^{1/d}

where
C
denotes here the Sobolev constant. Hence

\mathbf{N}(T_{k})\leqslant(1+C|K|^{1/d})\|Du_{k}\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and

\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}\leqslant\left(1+C|K|^{1/d}\right)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{M}(\partial T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{M}(T_{k})^{\alpha}

Summing over
k
and then passing to the infimum
yields (
10
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
u
be a compactly supported integrable function. The current

\llbracket u\rrbracket
is
\alpha
-fractional if and only if
u\in W^{1-\alpha,1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, in which case

\frac{1}{C}\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\alpha}(\llbracket u\rrbracket)\leqslant\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant C\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\alpha}(\llbracket u\rrbracket)

for some constant
C=C(d,\alpha)>0
.

Proof.

Step 1. For
u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant C\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1-\alpha}\|u\|_{1}^{\alpha}
, where
C=C(d,\alpha)
is a constant.

To our knowledge, this step and the next one were first proven
by Brasco, Lindgren and Parini in
[
6
, Proposition 4.2]
. For the reader’s convenience, we reproduce their proof.

We can assume
that
u\neq 0
and
\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})<\infty
. Let
\eta>0
be a real
number, to be chosen adequately later. By a change of variables,

\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(x)-u(x-z)|}{|z|^{d+1-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z

We then split

\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\int_{\{|z|\leqslant\eta\}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(x)-u(x-z)|}{|z|^{d+1-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z+\\
\int_{\{|z|>\eta\}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(x)-u(x-z)|}{|z|^{d+1-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z

The second term in the right-hand side is controlled by

\int_{\{|z|>\eta\}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(x)-u(x-z)|}{|z|^{d+1-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z\leqslant\int_{\{|z|>\eta\}}\frac{2\|u\|_{1}}{|z|^{d+1-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z=C\eta^{\alpha-1}\|u\|_{1}

As for the first term, we estimate

|u(x)-u(x-z)|\leqslant|z|\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla u(x-tz)|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t

Thus, by Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle\int_{\{|z|\leqslant\eta\}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(x)-u(x-z)|}{|z|^{d+1-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\{|z|\leqslant\eta\}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|\nabla u(x-tz)|}{|z|^{d-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\{|z|\leqslant\eta\}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla u(x-tz)|}{|z|^{d-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\{|z|\leqslant\eta\}}\frac{\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})}{|z|^{d-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=C\eta^{\alpha}\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Summing up, we find

\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant C\left(\eta^{\alpha}\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})+\eta^{\alpha-1}\|u\|_{1}\right).

Choosing
\eta=\|u\|_{1}/\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we finish the proof of
the first step.

Step 2. For
u\in BV(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have
\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant C\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1-\alpha}\|u\|_{1}^{\alpha}
, where
C=C(d,\alpha)
is a constant.

Indeed, by the approximation theorem
[
13
, Theorem 5.2.2]
,
there is a sequence
(u_{n})
in
BV(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

u_{n}\to u
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\|Du_{n}\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We may also assume that
u_{n}\to u
pointwise. We next
apply Fatou lemma to infer that

\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|u_{n}\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant C\|Du\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1-\alpha}\|u\|_{1}^{\alpha}.

Step 3. Let
u
be an integrable compactly supported
function. If
\llbracket u\rrbracket\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant C\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\alpha}(\llbracket u\rrbracket)
.

Let
\left(\llbracket u_{k}\rrbracket\right)
be an

\alpha
-fractional decomposition of
\llbracket u\rrbracket
. The

L^{1}
-convergence of the series
\sum u_{k}
implies that some
subsequence of the sequence of partial sums converges almost
everywhere to
u
. It follows that
|u(x)-u(y)|\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|u_{k}(x)-u_{k}(y)|
for almost all
x,y
. Thus,

\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|u_{k}\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant C\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|Du_{k}\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1-\alpha}\|u_{k}\|_{1}^{\alpha}.

We conclude by taking the infimum in the right-hand side over all
possible decompositions of
\llbracket u\rrbracket
.

Step 4. If
u\in W^{1-\alpha,1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
\llbracket u\rrbracket\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\alpha}(\llbracket u\rrbracket)\leqslant C\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
.

Some elements here are inspired by the proof
of
[
5
, Theorem A.1]
. As the norms
\|\cdot\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\alpha}
behave
identically under rescaling and translation, we may assume without
loss of generality that
\spt u\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
. For each integer

k\geqslant 0
, we let
\mathscr{P}_{k}
be the collection of dyadic
k
-cubes
in
[0,1]^{d}
. We define

v_{k}=\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}_{k}}\left(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}u\right)\mathbbm{1}_{Q}

It is well-known that
v_{k}\to u
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We define the
following
BV
functions

u_{0}=v_{0}\text{ and }u_{k}=v_{k}-v_{k-1}\text{ for all }k\geqslant 1

all of them being compactly supported in
[0,1]^{d}
.

We wish to prove that the decomposition
\left(\llbracket u\rrbracket=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\llbracket u_{k}\rrbracket\right)
is
an
\alpha
-fractional decomposition of
\llbracket U\rrbracket
.

We deal with the function
u_{0}=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}u\right)\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1]^{d}}
separately. We have

\|Du_{0}\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})\leqslant\left|\int_{[0,1]^{d}}u\right|\|D\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1]^{d}}\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})\leqslant\|u\|_{1}\cdot 2d.

Therefore

\|Du_{0}\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1-\alpha}\|u_{0}\|_{1}^{\alpha}\leqslant(2d)^{1-\alpha}\|u\|_{1}.

Moreover, recalling that
\spt u\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
, one has

\displaystyle\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{d+1-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus[-1,2]^{d}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{d+1-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\right)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|u(y)|\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus[-1,2]^{d}}\frac{1}{|y-x|^{d+1-\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\right)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geqslant C\|u\|_{1}.

Therefore,

\|Du_{0}\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1-\alpha}\|u_{0}\|_{1}^{\alpha}\leqslant C\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(11)

Now consider any
k\geqslant 1
. As the function
u_{k}
has a
decomposition

u_{k}=\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}_{k}}\alpha_{Q}\mathbbm{1}_{Q}

we infer that

\|Du_{k}\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})\leqslant\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}_{k}}|\alpha_{Q}|\|D\mathbbm{1}_{Q}\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})=2d(2^{-k})^{d-1}\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}_{k}}|\alpha_{Q}|=C2^{k}\|u_{k}\|_{1}

This leads to

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|Du_{k}\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1-\alpha}\|u_{k}\|_{1}^{\alpha}\leqslant C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{(1-\alpha)k}\|u_{k}\|_{1}

(12)

Next we intend to estimate the right-hand side
of (
12
). For any
Q\in\mathscr{P}_{k}
, we let
\hat{Q}\in\mathscr{P}_{k-1}
be the parent cube of
Q
. Almost everywhere on a

k
-cube
Q\in\mathscr{P}_{k}
, the function
u_{k}
is constant and takes the value

\displaystyle\alpha_{Q}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathscr{L}^{d}(Q)}\int_{Q}u(x)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{\mathscr{L}^{d}(\hat{Q})}\int_{\hat{Q}}u(y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathscr{L}^{d}(Q)\mathscr{L}^{d}(\hat{Q})}\int_{Q}\int_{\hat{Q}}\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y

Consequently,

\displaystyle\|u_{k}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}_{k}}\frac{1}{\mathscr{L}^{d}(\hat{Q})}\int_{Q}\int_{\hat{Q}}|u(x)-u(y)|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{(k-1)d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|u(x)-u(y)|\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{Q}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\hat{Q}}(y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y

This implies that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{(1-\alpha)k}\|u_{k}\|_{1}\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{d+1-\alpha}}a(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y

(13)

where

a(x,y)\leqslant C|x-y|^{d+1-\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{k(d+1-\alpha)}\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{Q}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\hat{Q}}(y)

It is clear that, for all
k\geqslant 1
and almost all
(x,y)
,

\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{Q}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\hat{Q}}(y)\leqslant 1

and that this sum is zero if
|x-y|
is greater than the diameter

\sqrt{d}2^{-(k-1)}
of a
(k-1)
-cube. Thus, we can bound
a(x,y)
by

C|x-y|^{d+1-\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}2^{k(d+1-\alpha)}

where
k_{0}
is the greatest integer such that
\sqrt{d}2^{-(k_{0}-1)}\geqslant|x-y|
. This proves that the function
a
is bounded by
a constant depending only on
d
and

\alpha
. Finally, (
11
), (
12
)
and (
13
) show that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|Du_{k}\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1-\alpha}\|u_{k}\|_{1}^{\alpha}\leqslant C\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

This proves that
\llbracket u\rrbracket\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{\alpha}(\llbracket u\rrbracket)\leqslant C\|u\|_{W^{1-\alpha,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
.
∎

In light of Subsection
3.2
and the previous
theorem, we can assert that if
U
is a bounded open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

then,

d-1+\inf\{\alpha\in[0,1]:\llbracket U\rrbracket\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}\leqslant\dim_{\mathrm{box}}(\partial U)

or, equivalently,

d-\dim_{\mathrm{box}}(\partial U)\leqslant\sup\{s\in(0,1):\mathbbm{1}_{U}\in W^{s,1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}

(where, in the right-hand side, the supremum is zero if there are no

s\in(0,1)
such that
\mathbbm{1}_{U}\in W^{s,1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
). The idea of
using the fractional Sobolev exponent
s
as a way to measure the
fractional codimension of the boundary of
U
has been first
introduced in
[
31
]
, where the above formula is proven to hold
if
U
is any bounded Lebesgue measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the
topological boundary
\partial U
is replaced by a measure-theoretic
analog.

5.
Charges in middle dimension

5.1.
Definition

In this section, we present a self-contained introduction to charges
in middle dimension and establish their fundamental properties. This
concept was initially developed by De Pauw, Moonens, and Pfeffer
in
[
11
]
, where charges were defined over arbitrary
subsets of Euclidean space. The theory was later extended to the more
general context of metric spaces in
[
9
]
. In this work,
however, we restrict our attention to charges defined on compact
subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, later on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
itself in
Subsection
5.5
. This narrower focus allows us to
simplify many of the underlying arguments.

An
m
-charge
over a compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a linear
map
\omega\colon\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies one of the
following equivalent continuity properties:

(A)

\omega(T_{n})\to 0
for any bounded sequence
(T_{n})
in

\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
that converges in flat norm to
0
;

(B)

the restriction of
\omega
to the unit ball of

\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
is
\mathbf{F}
-continuous;

(C)

for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\theta\geqslant 0

such that

|\omega(T)|\leqslant\varepsilon\mathbf{N}(T)+\theta\mathbf{F}(T)

holds for any normal current
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
.

Proof of the equivalences.

One clearly has (A)
\iff
(B) and (C)
\implies
(A). The only non
trivial implication (A)
\implies
(C) can be derived as a short
consequence of the compactness theorem. Indeed, suppose by
contradiction that (A) holds and (C) is false. In this case, there
is
\varepsilon>0
and a sequence
(T_{n})
of normal currents
supported in
K
, with normal masses
\mathbf{N}(T_{n})=1
such that

|\omega(T_{n})|>n\mathbf{F}(T_{n})+\varepsilon

(14)

for all
n
. Some subsequence
(T_{n_{k}})
converges to a normal
current
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
in flat norm. Property (A) then implies
that
\omega(T_{n_{k}})\to\omega(T)
. Consequently,
\mathbf{F}(T_{n_{k}})\leqslant n_{k}^{-1}|\omega(T_{n_{k}})|
tends to
0
as
k\to\infty
,
which implies that
T=0
and
\omega(T_{n_{k}})\to 0
. This is in
contradiction with (
14
).
∎

The space of
m
-charges over
K
is denoted
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
. As
\mathbf{F}\leqslant\mathbf{N}
, the continuity property (C) above implies that charges are

\mathbf{N}
-continuous,
i.e.

\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
is a subspace of the dual

\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)^{*}
. The space
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
is equipped with the operator norm

\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sup\left\{\omega(T):T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)\text{ and }\mathbf{N}(T)\leqslant 1\right\}.

(15)

In fact,
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
is a closed subspace of
\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)^{*}
, for if

(\omega_{n})
is a sequence in
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
converging towards
\omega\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)^{*}
, then
\omega_{n}\to\omega
uniformly on the unit ball
of
\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
. We conclude by (B) that
\omega
is a charge. As a
result,
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
is a Banach space.

In addition, there is also a notion of
weak convergence of
charges
: we say that
\omega_{n}\to\omega
weakly whenever

\omega_{n}(T)\to\omega(T)
for all
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
.

5.2.
Continuous differential forms

Any continuous
m
-form on
K
defines an
m
-charge, via the map

\Lambda\colon C(K;\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)

defined by

\Lambda(\omega)(T)=\int_{K}\langle\omega(x),\vec{T}(x)\rangle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\|T\|(x)

(16)

for
\omega\in C(K;\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
. Note that,
in the special case where
\omega
has a smooth extension to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
still denoted
\omega
, then
\Lambda(\omega)(T)=T(\omega)
.

Let us check that
\Lambda(\omega)
, defined by (
16
)
for
\omega\in C(K;\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is indeed an
m
-charge.

To this end, let us fix
\varepsilon>0
and choose a compactly
supported smooth form
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

|\omega(x)-\phi(x)|\leqslant\varepsilon
for all
x\in K
. Then, for
all
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
,

|\Lambda(\omega)(T)-\Lambda(\phi_{\mid K})(T)|\leqslant\left|\int_{K}\langle\omega(x)-\phi(x),\vec{T}(x)\rangle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\|T\|(x)\right|\leqslant\varepsilon\mathbf{M}(T)\leqslant\varepsilon\mathbf{N}(T)

(17)

whereas

|\Lambda(\phi_{\mid K})(T)|\leqslant\theta\mathbf{F}(T)

(18)

for
\theta=\max\{\|\phi\|_{\infty},\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\phi\|_{\infty}\}
. The map
\Lambda(\phi_{\mid K})\colon T\mapsto T(\phi)
is clearly linear. As
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary,
inequality (
17
) shows the linearity of

\Lambda(\omega)
. As for continuity, it follows
from (
17
), (
18
)
and
5.1
(C).

We warn the reader that
\Lambda
is, in general, not injective. Indeed, flat
m
-chains cannot be excessively concentrated. More precisely, it is known that a nonzero flat chain
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
must have a support of Hausdorff measure
\mathscr{H}^{m}(\spt T)>0
. We refer to
[
14
, 4.1.20]
for a more general statement involving the integralgeometric measure. Consequently, if
K
is nonempty and
\mathscr{H}^{m}(K)=0
, then
\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{m}(K)=\{0\}
, which in turn implies
\mathbf{CH}_{m}(K)=\{0\}
. In this situation,
\Lambda
cannot be injective. For this reason, we will not identify a continuous
m
-form with its associated
m
-charge.

Only in the simplest case
m=0
do charges correspond to continuous functions, as we prove now.

Proposition 5.1
.

The map
\Lambda\colon C(K)\to\mathbf{CH}^{0}(K)
is a Banach space
isomorphism.

Proof.

We define the map
\Gamma\colon\mathbf{CH}^{0}(K)\to C(K)
by

\Gamma(\omega)(x)=\omega(\llbracket x\rrbracket)
for all

\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{0}(K)
and
x\in K
. The continuity of

\Gamma(\omega)
follows from
5.1
(A). It is clear that

\Gamma\circ\Lambda
is the identity operator. Therefore, it
suffices to show that
\Gamma
is a Banach space isomorphism.

First, we check that
\Gamma
is a continuous. For all
x\in K
, we
have

|\Gamma(\omega)(x)|\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{0}(K)}\mathbf{M}(\llbracket x\rrbracket)=\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{0}(K)}.

Thus,
\|\Gamma(\omega)\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{0}(K)}
.

Next, we claim that
\Gamma
is injective. Let us call
\mathbf{P}_{0}(K)

the space of polyhedral
0
-currents supported in
K
,
i.e.

the linear space spanned by the
\llbracket x\rrbracket
, for
x\in K
. By an easy corollary of the deformation
theorem
[
14
, 4.2.9]
, every
T\in\mathbf{N}_{0}(K)
is the

\mathbf{F}
-limit of a sequence
(T_{n})
in
\mathbf{P}_{0}(K)
such that
\mathbf{M}(T_{n})\leqslant\mathbf{M}(T)
. Observe that if
\omega\in\ker\Gamma
, then

\omega
vanishes on
\mathbf{P}_{0}(K)
. By the preceding result and the
continuity property of charges,
\omega=0
. This proves that

\Gamma
is injective.

Next we prove the surjectivity of
\Gamma
. Let
g\in C(K)
. We
define the function
\omega
, on polyhedral
0
-currents, by

\omega\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\llbracket x_{k}\rrbracket\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}g(x_{k}).

Let
\varepsilon>0
. There is a smooth compactly supported
function
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\|f-g\|_{\infty,K}\leqslant\varepsilon
. Setting
\theta=\max\{\|f\|_{\infty},\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}f\|_{\infty}\}
, we have

\displaystyle|\omega(T)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}f(x_{k})\right|+\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}(f-g)(x_{k})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\theta\mathbf{F}(T)+\varepsilon\mathbf{M}(T)

(19)

for every polyhedral
0
-current
T
. We extend
\omega
to

\mathbf{M}_{0}(K)
with

\omega(T)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\omega(T_{n})

(20)

where
T\in\mathbf{M}_{0}(K)
and
(T_{n})
is any sequence of polyhedral

0
-currents that
\mathbf{F}
-converges to
T
, with
\mathbf{M}(T_{n})\leqslant\mathbf{M}(T)
. By (
19
), the sequence
(\omega(T_{n}))
is Cauchy,
which ensures that the limit in (
20
) exists and
does not depend on the choice of an approximating sequence. It is
also straightforward that
\omega
is linear and (
19
) holds
now for any
T\in\mathbf{M}_{0}(K)
. Hence
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{0}(K)
and

\Gamma(\omega)=g
. This proves that
\Gamma
is onto.

Finally,
\Gamma^{-1}=\Lambda
is continuous by the open mapping
theorem.
∎

5.3.
Operations on charges

Operations on normal currents, such as pushforwards by Lipschitz maps,
taking the boundary, have a counterpart in term of charges, defined by
duality. We first define the
exterior derivative

\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m+1}(K)
of a charge
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
, by setting

\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega(T)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\omega(\partial T)

for all
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
. That
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega
is continuous, in the
sense of charges, is a consequence of the identities

\mathbf{N}(\partial T)\leqslant\mathbf{N}(T),\qquad\mathbf{F}(\partial T)\leqslant\mathbf{F}(T)

(21)

that furthermore implies that the operator
\mathrm{d}\colon\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)\to\mathbf{CH}^{m+1}(K)
is bounded with norm less than or equal to
1
.

This allows us to introduce other examples of charges, namely the
differentials of continuous forms.

Next, if
f\colon K\to L
is a Lipschitz map between two compact
subsets
K
and
L
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, respectively, we define
the
pullback

f^{\#}\colon\mathbf{CH}^{m}(L)\to\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)

by

f^{\#}\omega(T)=\omega(f_{\#}T)

for all
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m}(L)
and
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
.

Most results from differential calculus extend to charges, by
duality arguments. We state some of them for the sake of completeness.

(A)

\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\circ\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}=0
.

(B)

The exterior derivative commutes with Lipschitz pullbacks.

(C)

(f\circ g)^{\#}=g^{\#}\circ f^{\#}
for Lipschitz maps

f,g
with compatible domains and codomains.

5.4.
Relative compactness in
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)

We now state a criterion for relative compactness in
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
. It
will prove useful in the next section for establishing the basic
properties of the space of fractional charges. This result seems to
be new.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
. The following are equivalent:

(A)

\Omega
is relatively compact;

(B)

for all
\varepsilon>0
, there is
\theta\geqslant 0
such that

|\omega(T)|\leqslant\varepsilon\mathbf{N}(T)+\theta\mathbf{F}(T)

holds for all
\omega\in\Omega
. We stress that
\theta
does not depend on
\omega
.

Proof.

(A)
\implies
(B). We prove this implication by
contradiction. Suppose there are
\varepsilon>0
and two sequences

(\omega_{n})
in
\Omega
and
(T_{n})
in
\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
such that

|\omega_{n}(T_{n})|>\varepsilon\mathbf{N}(T_{n})+n\mathbf{F}(T_{n})

for all integers
n
. We can also suppose
\mathbf{N}(T_{n})=1
for all

n
. As
\Omega
is relatively compact, it is bounded, consequently

n\mathbf{F}(T_{n})<\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)}<\infty

which implies that
(T_{n})
converges to
0
in flat norm. On the
other side, there is a subsequence
(\omega_{n_{k}})
that converges
to
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
. Hence

|\omega_{n_{k}}(T_{n_{k}})|\leqslant|\omega(T_{n_{k}})|+\|\omega-\omega_{n_{k}}\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)}\to 0

which contradicts that
|\omega_{n_{k}}(T_{n_{k}})|>\varepsilon
.

(B)
\implies
(A). Denote by
B_{\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)}
the unit ball of

\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
, metrized by
\mathbf{F}
, and let
\iota\colon\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)\to C(B_{\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)})
be the linear map that sends a charge to its
restriction to
B_{\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)}
. Here,
C(B_{\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)})
is given the
supremum norm. Since
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
is complete and
\iota
is an
isometric embedding, we only need to show that
\iota(\Omega)
is
relatively compact in
C(B_{\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)})
.

First, the inequality in (B) (for
\varepsilon=1
) entails that

\iota(\Omega)
is pointwise bounded. Now, for an arbitrary

\varepsilon>0
there is
\theta\geqslant 0
as in (B). If
T,S\in B_{\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)}
satisfy
\mathbf{F}(T-S)\leqslant\varepsilon/\theta
, then
for any
\omega\in\Omega
, one has

|\iota(\omega)(T)-\iota(\omega)(S)|\leqslant\theta\mathbf{F}(T-S)+\varepsilon\mathbf{N}(T-S)\leqslant 3\varepsilon.

This proves that
\iota(\Omega)
is equicontinuous, thus relatively
compact by the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem. The proof is then finished.
∎

5.5.
Charges over
\mathbb{R}^{d}

An
m
-charge
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a linear map
\omega\colon\mathbf{N}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies the continuity condition

\omega(T_{n})\to 0\quad\text{whenever }\mathbf{F}(T_{n})\to 0,\,\,\sup_{n\geqslant 0}\mathbf{N}(T_{n})<\infty\text{ and }\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\spt T_{n}\text{ is bounded}

Alternatively,
\omega
is an
m
-charge over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whenever its
restriction to
\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
for every compact set
K
is an
m
-charge over

K
. We denote by
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of
m
-charges over

\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

For each compact set, we define

\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m},K}=\sup\left\{\omega(T):T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)\right\}

This definition closely resembles (
15
), with the
difference that
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m},K}
is now only a seminorm on

\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The collection of these seminorms, as
K
ranges over
all compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defines a vector space topology on

\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Clearly, only a countable subset of these seminorms is
required to determine this topology (for example, by letting
K
range
over the closed ball centered at the origin with positive integer
radius). Furthermore, it follows directly from the fact that the
spaces
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
are Banach spaces and that
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a
Fréchet space.

Many definitions and properties carry over easily to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(A)

Weak convergence
: a sequence
(\omega_{n})
converges
to
\omega
weakly in
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
whenever
\omega_{n}(T)\to\omega(T)
for any
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

(B)

Exterior derivative
: the operator
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\colon\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbf{CH}^{m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined by
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega(T)=\omega(\partial T)
is linear and continuous;

(C)

Pullback by locally Lipschitz maps
: for any locally
Lipschitz map
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
and
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
, we define the pullback
f^{\#}\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
f^{\#}\omega(T)=\omega(f_{\#}T)
. This
definition has a natural adaptation in the domain or the codomain of

f
is a compact subset of the Euclidean space.

In particular, we will frequently use pullbacks as a tool to extend
charges, in the following context: if
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact
convex and
p\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to K
is the orthogonal projection, then

p^{\#}\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
agrees with
\omega
on
\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
. This is because
p_{\#}T=\operatorname{id}_{\#}T=T
for all
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
. Recall that the pushforward of a flat chain
T
by a map
f
depends only on the restriction of
f
to the support
\spt T
.

(D)

Continuous differential
m
-forms over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
: as before, a
continuous
m
-form can be naturally regarded as an
m
-charge,
through the map
\Lambda\colon C(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, defined by

\Lambda(\omega)(T)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\omega(x),\vec{T}(x)\rangle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\|T\|(x)

for all
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

This map is injective. Indeed, suppose that
\Lambda(\omega)=0
. For every compactly supported smooth
m
-current
\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\xi
, one has

\Lambda(\omega)(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\omega(x),\xi(x)\rangle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x=0.

Since this holds for every
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge_{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one infers that
\omega=0
by the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations.

Consequently, we will identify continuous
m
-forms with
m
-charges over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
allowing us to consider that

C(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})\subseteq\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

In particular, in the case
\omega
is a smooth
m
-form over
\mathbb{R}^{d}

and
T
is a normal
m
-current, both expressions
\omega(T)
and

T(\omega)
are well-defined and interchangeable.

When
\omega\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the meaning of
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega
is unambiguous, whether we think of
\omega
as an
m
-form
or an
m
-charge. This follows from the classical Stokes’ formula.

(E)

Continuity of
0
-charges
: the map
\Lambda\colon C(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbf{CH}^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a Fréchet space isomorphism, whose
inverse is the map
\Gamma
defined by
\Gamma(\omega)(x)=\omega(\llbracket x\rrbracket)
for
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here,
C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is given the Fréchet topology induced
by the family of seminorms
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,K}
, where
K
can
be any compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(F)

Relative compactness in
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
: a subset

\Omega\subseteq\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is relatively compact in

\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
whenever for all compact subsets
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\varepsilon>0
, there exists

such that

for all

.

5.6.
Regularization of charges

Operating on the entire space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
enables the regularization of
charges through convolution. The
convolution
of a charge
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with a smooth compactly supported function

\phi
is the linear map
\mathbf{N}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
that sends
T
to

\omega(T*\check{\phi})
. It satisfies the continuity property of
charges because the map
T\mapsto T*\check{\phi}
is both
\mathbf{N}
-
and
\mathbf{F}
-continuous.

This provides an example of weak convergence of charges. Indeed, for

T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one has
T*\Phi_{\varepsilon}\to 0
in flat norm
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, whereas the normal masses of the
T*\Phi_{\varepsilon}
are bounded by
\mathbf{N}(T)
, by
Proposition
2.2
. (Recall that we fixed a regularization
kernel
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
in Section
2
). This entails
that
\omega*\Phi_{\varepsilon}\to\omega
weakly.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then

\omega*\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

We will actually prove the explicit formula

(\omega*\phi)(z)=\sum_{I\in\Lambda(d,m)}\omega\left(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\phi(z-\cdot)\boldsymbol{e}_{I}\right)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x_{I}\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(22)

where
\Lambda(d,m)
is the set of increasing subfamilies of
\{1,\dots,d\}
of cardinal
m
, and for
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})
,

\boldsymbol{e}_{I}=e_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_{m}}\text{ and }\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x_{I}=\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x_{i_{m}}

and
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Call
\tilde{\omega}(z)
the right-hand side
in (
22
). First we check that
\tilde{\omega}

is a smooth
m
-form. This is done by ensuring that, for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
and for any sequence
(h_{n})
of nonzero real numbers
tending to 0,

\frac{\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\phi(z+h_{n}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\cdot)\boldsymbol{e}_{I}-\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\phi(z-\cdot)\boldsymbol{e}_{I}}{h_{n}}\to\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{i}}(z-\cdot)\boldsymbol{e}_{I}

in flat norm with uniformly bounded normal masses. An argument by
induction finishes the proof that the component functions
z\mapsto\omega\left(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\phi(z-\cdot)\boldsymbol{e}_{I}\right)

are smooth.

Next, in order to prove that the charges
\omega*\phi
and

\tilde{\omega}
coincide, we need only do so on currents of the
form
\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\xi
, where
\xi=\sum_{I\in\Lambda(d,m)}\xi_{I}\boldsymbol{e}_{I}
is a compactly supported smooth

m
-vector field. This is because, for all
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(\omega*\phi-\tilde{\omega})(T)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(\omega*\phi-\tilde{\omega})(T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})

using the continuity property of the charge
\omega*\phi-\tilde{\omega}
and Proposition
2.2
(E) and (G). As
T*\Phi_{\varepsilon}
has the form
\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\xi
by

[
14
, 4.1.2]
, the claim follows.

We begin by evaluating

(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\xi)(\tilde{\omega})=\sum_{I\in\Lambda(d,m)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\phi(z-\cdot)\boldsymbol{e}_{I})\xi_{I}(z)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z.

On the other hand, one has

\omega(T*\check{\phi})=\sum_{I\in\Lambda(d,m)}\omega\left(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\xi_{I}*\check{\phi}\boldsymbol{e}_{I}\right).

Following
[
14
, 4.1.2]
, we introduce, for every
n\geqslant 1
, a
partition
A_{n,1},\dots,A_{n,p_{n}}
of
\spt\xi
into Borel sets
of diameter less than
n^{-1}
and choose points
z_{n,k}\in A_{n,k}
for
1\leqslant k\leqslant p_{n}
. Then

\sum_{k=1}^{p_{n}}\xi_{I}(z_{n,k})\left(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\phi(z_{n,k}-\cdot)\boldsymbol{e}_{I}\right)\mathscr{L}^{d}(A_{n,k})\to\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\xi_{I}*\check{\phi}\boldsymbol{e}_{I}

in flat norm with uniformly bounded normal masses. Thus,

\displaystyle\omega(T*\check{\phi})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{I\in\Lambda(d,m)}\sum_{k=1}^{p_{n}}\xi_{I}(z_{n,k})\omega(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\phi(z_{n,k}-\cdot)\boldsymbol{e_{I}})\mathscr{L}^{d}(A_{n,k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\in\Lambda(d,m)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\xi_{I}(z)\omega(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\phi(z-\cdot)\boldsymbol{e_{I}})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\xi)(\tilde{\omega}).\qed

6.
Fractional charges and duality with fractional currents

6.1.
Definition

Let
\alpha\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{]}
. An

\alpha
-fractional charge
over a compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a linear functional
\omega\colon\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)\to\mathbb{R}
for
which there is a constant
C\geqslant 0
such that

|\omega(T)|\leqslant C\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\alpha}\text{ for all
}T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)

It is clear that the above requirement is stronger than the continuity
condition of Subsection
5.1
. We adopt the notation

\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
to represent the space of
\alpha
-fractional

m
-charges, normed by

\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}=\inf\left\{C\geqslant 0:|\omega(T)|\leqslant C\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\alpha}\text{ for all
}T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)\right\}.

We also define
\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}=\infty
if

\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)\setminus\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
.

The parameter
\alpha
represents regularity. One clearly has
inclusions

\mathbf{CH}^{m,\beta}(K)\subseteq\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)\subseteq\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)

(that are continuous) whenever
\beta\geqslant\alpha
. In addition, the
reader may use the continuity of the second embedding and the lower
semicontinuity of
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}
with respect to
weak convergence to check that
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
is a Banach
space.

When
\alpha=1
and
K
is convex, we encounter a well-known
object. Indeed, in this case, a
1
-fractional charge
\omega
is

\mathbf{F}
-continuous and
\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
is
\mathbf{F}
-dense in
\mathbf{F}_{m}(K)
. As such,

\omega
can be uniquely extended so as to become an element of

\mathbf{F}_{m}(K)^{*}
, the space of
flat
m
-cochains over
K
,
introduced by H. Whitney. This result will be generalized in
Theorem
6.4
.

More generally, we can think of
\alpha
-fractionality as a regularity
that is intermediate between that of mere charges and that of flat
cochains.

We observe that, as a consequence of (
21
), the
exterior derivative of an
\alpha
-fractional
m
-charge is again

\alpha
-fractional (and the map
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\colon\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)\to\mathbf{CH}^{m+1,\alpha}(K)
is continuous). As for pullbacks, if
m\geqslant 1
,
f\colon K\to L
denotes a Lipschitz map and
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(L)
,
then for all
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
,

\displaystyle|f^{\#}\omega(T)|

\displaystyle=|\omega(f_{\#}T)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(L)}\mathbf{N}(f_{\#}T)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(f_{\#}T)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\max\{(\rmLip f)^{m-1+\alpha},(\rmLip f)^{m+\alpha}\}\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(L)}\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\alpha}.

Accordingly,
f^{\#}\omega
is
\alpha
-fractional and

\|f^{\#}\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}\leqslant\max\{(\rmLip f)^{m-1+\alpha},(\rmLip f)^{m+\alpha}\}\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(L)}.

In case
m=0
, one proves similarly that
f^{\#}\omega
is
\alpha
-fractional and

\|f^{\#}\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(K)}\leqslant(\rmLip f)^{\alpha}\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(L)}

6.2.
Hölder differential forms

In this subsection, we claim that
\alpha
-Hölder continuous
m
-forms
are
\alpha
-fractional charges. More precisely, the map
\Lambda

defined in Subsection
5.2
restricts to a continuous
linear map

\Lambda\colon\rmLip^{\alpha}(K;\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)

(23)

Indeed, let
\omega\in\rmLip^{\alpha}(K;\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
. It is
possible to extend
\omega
to an
\alpha
-Hölder continuous
m
-form
\tilde{\omega}

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\rmLip^{\alpha}(\tilde{\omega})\leqslant C\rmLip^{\alpha}(\omega)\text{ and
}\|\tilde{\omega}\|_{\infty}=\|\omega\|_{\infty}.

Such an extension can be obtained, for instance, via the McShane extension theorem (see
[
32
, Theorem 1.33]
).
For any
\varepsilon\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{]}
, the smooth

m
-form
\omega_{\varepsilon}=\tilde{\omega}*\Phi_{\varepsilon}

satisfies

\|\omega_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\infty},\qquad\|\tilde{\omega}-\omega_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\leqslant C\rmLip^{\alpha}(\omega)\varepsilon^{\alpha}\text{ and }\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{1-\alpha}}\rmLip^{\alpha}(\omega)

For an arbitrary
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
, we have

\Lambda(\omega)(T)=\int_{K}\langle\omega(x)-\omega_{\varepsilon}(x),\vec{T}(x)\rangle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\|T\|(x)+T(\omega_{\varepsilon})

From the above inequalities, we can control the first term

\left|\int_{K}\langle\omega(x)-\omega_{\varepsilon}(x),\vec{T}(x)\rangle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\|T\|(x)\right|\leqslant C\rmLip^{\alpha}(\omega)\varepsilon^{\alpha}\mathbf{N}(T)

whereas,

\displaystyle|T(\omega_{\varepsilon})|

\displaystyle\leqslant\max\left\{\|\omega_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty},\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\right\}\mathbf{F}(T)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{1-\alpha}}\max\left\{\|\omega\|_{\infty},\rmLip^{\alpha}(\omega)\right\}\mathbf{F}(T)

Combining the two preceding inequalities yields

|\Lambda(\omega)(T)|\leqslant C\max\left\{\|\omega\|_{\infty},\rmLip^{\alpha}(\omega)\right\}\left(\varepsilon^{\alpha}\mathbf{N}(T)+\frac{\mathbf{F}(T)}{\varepsilon^{1-\alpha}}\right)

(24)

In case
\alpha\neq 1
, we choose
\varepsilon=\mathbf{F}(T)/\mathbf{N}(T)

(which is indeed less than or equal to
1
), so that

|\Lambda(\omega)(T)|\leqslant C\max\left\{\|\omega\|_{\infty},\rmLip^{\alpha}(\omega)\right\}\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\alpha}

(25)

thereby showing that
\Lambda(\omega)
is
\alpha
-fractional and

\Lambda
is continuous, with

\|\Lambda(\omega)\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}\leqslant C\max\{\|\omega\|_{\infty},\rmLip^{\alpha}(\omega)\}.

(26)

Inequalities (
25
) and (
26
) are
obtained in case
\alpha=1
by letting
\varepsilon
tend to
0

in (
24
).

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{]}
. The map
\Lambda\colon\rmLip^{\alpha}(K)\to\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(K)
is a Banach space
isomorphism.

Proof.

Recall the map
\Gamma\colon\mathbf{CH}^{0}(K)\to C(K)
from the proof of
Proposition
5.1

\Gamma(\omega)(x)=\omega(\llbracket x\rrbracket)\text{ for all }\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{0}(K)\text{ and }x\in K.

It was proved that it is inverse to the map
\Lambda\colon C(K)\to\mathbf{CH}^{0}(K)
. It suffices to prove that
\Gamma
restricts to a Banach
space isomorphism
\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(K)\to\rmLip^{\alpha}(K)
.

Let
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(K)
. The function
\Gamma(\omega)

is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous, as for all
x,y\in K
, one has

\displaystyle\left|\Gamma(\omega)(x)-\Gamma(\omega)(y)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\omega(\llbracket x\rrbracket-\llbracket y\rrbracket)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(K)}\mathbf{N}(\llbracket x\rrbracket-\llbracket y\rrbracket)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(\llbracket x\rrbracket-\llbracket y\rrbracket)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(K)}|x-y|^{\alpha}.

Furthermore, for any
x\in K
,

|\Gamma(\omega)(x)|=|\omega(\llbracket x\rrbracket)|\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(K)}.

This shows that
\max\{\|\Gamma(\omega)\|_{\infty},\rmLip^{\alpha}(\Gamma(\omega))\}\leqslant C\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(K)}
, giving the continuity of
\Gamma
.

Let
f\in\rmLip^{\alpha}(K)
. In particular,
f\in C(K)
so there
is a charge
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{0}(K)
such that
\omega=\Lambda(f)
. Let us prove that
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(K)
. First extend
f
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
while preserving the
Hölder constant. As usual, we consider
\varepsilon\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{]}
and define
f_{\varepsilon}=f*\Phi_{\varepsilon}
. We have

\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|f\|_{\infty,K},\|f-f_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\varepsilon^{\alpha}\rmLip^{\alpha}(f)\text{ and }\rmLip f_{\varepsilon}=\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}f_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\alpha-1}\rmLip^{\alpha}f.

Let
T=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\llbracket x_{k}\rrbracket
be a

0
-polyhedral current, supported in
K
.

\omega(T)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}f_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})+\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\varepsilon}(x)\right)

Therefore,

\displaystyle|\omega(T)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|T(f_{\varepsilon})|+\varepsilon^{\alpha}\rmLip^{\alpha}(f)\mathbf{N}(T)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\max\{\|f\|_{\infty,K},\rmLip^{\alpha}f\}\left(\varepsilon^{\alpha-1}\mathbf{F}(T)+\varepsilon^{\alpha}\mathbf{N}(T)\right).

Choosing
\varepsilon=\mathbf{F}(T)/\mathbf{N}(T)
yields

|\omega(T)|\leqslant C\max\{\|f\|_{\infty,K},\rmLip^{\alpha}(f)\}\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\alpha}.

The preceding inequality holds as well for an arbitrary
T\in\mathbf{N}_{0}(K)
, as it is the
\mathbf{F}
-limit of as sequence of
0
-polyhedral
currents
(T_{n})
with
\mathbf{M}(T_{n})\leqslant\mathbf{M}(T)
. This proves that

\omega
is
\alpha
-fractional. Since
\Gamma(\omega)=f
, this
concludes that the map
\Gamma
, restricted to
\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(K)\to\rmLip^{\alpha}(K)
, is surjective. By the open mapping theorem,
it is a Banach space isomorphism, and so is
\Lambda=\Gamma^{-1}\colon\rmLip^{\alpha}(K)\to\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(K)
.
∎

6.3.
Fractional charges over
\mathbb{R}^{d}

An
\alpha
-fractional
m
-charge
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a linear functional

\omega\colon\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for all compact
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there is a constant
C_{K}\geqslant 0
such that

|\omega(T)|\leqslant C_{K}\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\alpha}\text{ for all }T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K).

In other words, an
\alpha
-fractional charge is an
m
-charge whose
restriction to each
\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
belongs to
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
. We
denote by
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of
\alpha
-fractional
charges over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We define, for each compact
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the following seminorm
on
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},K}=\inf\left\{C\geqslant 0:|\omega(T)|\leqslant C\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\alpha}\text{ for all }T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)\right\}.

The family of such seminorms induces a Fréchet topology on

\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We summarize the following facts, each of
which is a straightforward adaptation of previous arguments to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(A)

Exterior derivative
: the operator
\mathrm{d}\colon\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbf{CH}^{m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
restricts to a linear
continuous operator
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbf{CH}^{m+1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

(B)

Pullback by locally Lipschitz maps
: let
f\colon\operatorname{dom}f\to\operatorname{codom}f
be a locally
Lipschitz map, where the domain is either
\mathbb{R}^{d}
a compact subset
thereof, and likewise, the codomain is either
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
a compact
subset thereof. Then the pullback
f^{\#}
is a linear continuous map

\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\operatorname{codom}f)\to\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\operatorname{dom}f)
.

(C)

Hölder continuous differential forms
: the map
\Lambda\colon C(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
restricts to a
continuous linear map
\rmLip^{\alpha}_{\rmloc}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, that is still injective. As such, we
consider that

\rmLip^{\alpha}_{\rmloc}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})\subseteq\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(D)

Hölder continuity of fractional
0
-charges
: the map

\Lambda\colon\rmLip^{\alpha}_{\rmloc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbf{CH}^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a Fréchet space isomorphism, whose inverse is
given by the map
\Gamma
defined by
\Gamma(\omega)(x)=\omega(\llbracket x\rrbracket)
.

We end this subsection with two technical results that give the

1
-fractional seminorm of a smooth form. They will be used in the
proof of Theorem
9.1
and their reading may be postponed
until that point.

Proposition 6.2
.

Suppose
\omega\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\bigwedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a compact convex set with positive Lebesgue measure. Then

\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,1},K}=\max\{\|\omega\|_{\infty,K},\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\|_{\infty,K}\}
.

Proof.

The convexity of
K
guarantees that for all
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(T)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\mathbf{M}(A)+\mathbf{M}(B):T=A+\partial B\text{ with }A\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\text{ and }B\in\mathbf{N}_{m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{\mathbf{M}(A)+\mathbf{M}(B):T=A+\partial B\text{ with }A\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)\text{ and }B\in\mathbf{N}_{m+1}(K)\}

Indeed, one can always replace
A
and
B
by their pushforwards
p_{\#}
and
p_{\#}B
by the orthogonal projection
p
onto
K
. Since
p
is
1
-Lipschitz, this operation does not increase the mass and the identity
T=p_{\#}A+\partial(p_{\#}B)
is valid.

Therefore, we consider
A\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
and
B\in\mathbf{N}_{m+1}(K)
such that
T=A+\partial B
and compute

|\omega(T)|=|A(\omega)|+|B(\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega)|\leqslant\left(\mathbf{M}(A)+\mathbf{M}(B)\right)\max\{\|\omega\|_{\infty,K},\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\|_{\infty,K}\}.

Taking the infimum over
A,B
, one obtains that
|\omega(T)|\leqslant\mathbf{F}(T)\max\{\|\omega\|_{\infty,K},\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\|_{\infty,K}\}
. This means that
\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,1},K}\leqslant\max\{\|\omega\|_{\infty,K},\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\|_{\infty,K}\}
.

As the Lebesgue measure of the convex set
K
is nonzero, one can
replace the
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,K}
seminorms with essential
suprema. One has then

\displaystyle\|\omega\|_{\infty,K}=\sup_{\zeta}\int\langle\omega(x),\zeta(x)\rangle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x=\sup_{\zeta}\omega(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\zeta)

\displaystyle\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\|_{\infty,K}=\sup_{\xi}\int\langle(\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega)(x),\xi(x)\rangle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x=\sup_{\xi}\omega\left(\partial(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\xi)\right)

where
\zeta
(resp.
\xi
) ranges over the summable

m
-vector fields (resp.
(m+1)
-vector fields) supported in
K
of

L^{1}
-norm 1. As

\mathbf{F}(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\zeta)\leqslant\mathbf{M}(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\zeta)\leqslant 1\text{ and }\mathbf{F}\left(\partial(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\xi)\right)\leqslant\mathbf{M}\left(\partial(\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge\xi)\right)\leqslant 1,

one finally proves the desired inequality.
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

If
\omega\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\bigwedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\eta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\bigwedge^{m^{\prime}}\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
K
is a compact set that
satisfies (A) and (B), then
\|\omega\wedge\eta\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m+m^{\prime},1},K}\leqslant C\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,1},K}\|\eta\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m^{\prime},1},K}
, for some constant
C
.

Proof.

It is a consequence of the identity
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}(\omega\wedge\eta)=\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\wedge\eta+(-1)^{m}\omega\wedge\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\eta
. The
reader interested in estimating the constant may consult

[
14
, 1.8.1]
.
∎

6.4.
Duality and a generalized Gauss-Green formula for fractal boundaries

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
K
be a compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
is (isometrically isomorphic to) the dual space
of
\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)
. The corresponding duality bracket
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\colon\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)\times\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\langle\omega,T\rangle=\omega(T)

(27)

for all
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
and
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
.

Proof.

First we define the duality bracket. For
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
and
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)
we set

\langle\omega,T\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\omega(T_{k})

(28)

where
(T_{k})
is a decomposition of
T
as in the
definition
3.1
and all the normal currents
T_{k}
are
supported in
K
. The right-hand side series is convergent, as

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|\omega(T_{k})|\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}<\infty

We need however to show that the right-hand side
of (
28
) does not depend on the choice of the
decomposition
(T_{k})
. We reduce to the case where
T=0
. First we
define
\hat{\omega}=p^{\#}\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
where
p\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to K
is the orthogonal projection onto

K
. For
\varepsilon>0
, we know that the convolution

\hat{\omega}*\Phi_{\varepsilon}
is a smooth
m
-form. As
\sum_{k}T_{k}=0
in flat norm (and therefore weakly), one has

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hat{\omega}*\Phi_{\varepsilon}(T_{k})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\omega}*\Phi_{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}T_{k}\right)=0

In addition,

\displaystyle|\hat{\omega}*\Phi_{\varepsilon}(T_{k})|

\displaystyle\leqslant|\omega(p_{\#}(T*\Phi_{\varepsilon}))|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}\mathbf{N}(p_{\#}(T_{k}*\Phi_{\varepsilon}))^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(p_{\#}(T_{k}*\Phi_{\varepsilon}))^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}

and for all
k\geqslant 0
, one has

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\hat{\omega}*\Phi_{\varepsilon}(T_{k})=\hat{\omega}(T_{k})=\omega(T_{k})

This follows from the continuity of charges, as the normal currents

(T_{k}*\Phi_{\varepsilon})_{0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1}
have uniformly
bounded normal masses, are supported in
B(\spt T_{k},1)
and
T_{k}*\Phi_{\varepsilon}\to T_{k}
in flat norm by Proposition
2.2
.
By the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem,

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\omega(T_{k})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hat{\omega}*\Phi_{\varepsilon}(T_{k})=0

as desired.

The map
T\mapsto\langle\omega,T\rangle
is

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}
-continuous. Indeed, by (
28
), one has

|\langle\omega,T\rangle|\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}

and by passing to the infimum
|\langle\omega,T\rangle|\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)
. Hence, the map

\Upsilon\colon\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)\to\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K)^{*}\colon\omega\mapsto\langle\omega,\cdot\rangle

is well-defined and
\|\Upsilon\|\leqslant 1
.

Finally,
\Upsilon
is surjective. Indeed, consider a linear
continuous functional
\varphi\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K)^{*}
and denote by

\omega
its restriction to
\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
. Then for all
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
, one has

|\omega(T)|=|\varphi(T)|\leqslant\|\varphi\|\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)\leqslant\|\varphi\|\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\alpha}

Accordingly,
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
and it is
straightforward that
\varphi=\Upsilon(\omega)
by density of

\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
in
\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K)
and
\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}\leqslant\|\Upsilon(\omega)\|
. We conclude that
\Upsilon
is a
surjective isometry.
∎

Observe that if
K
is convex, Stokes’ formula

\langle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega,T\rangle=\langle\omega,\partial T\rangle,

which is valid by definition for
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m-1,\alpha}(K)
and
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
, extends by density to all
T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K)
. Setting
m=d
,
T=\llbracket U\rrbracket
, where
U
is a bounded open set with finite

(1-\alpha)
-perimeter, and choosing
\omega
as the

\alpha
-fractional charge associated with an
\alpha
-Hölder
continuous
(d-1)
-form, one arrives at a generalized Gauss-Green
theorem. Notably, this framework encompasses the case where
U

is
(d-1+\alpha)
-summable, because of
Proposition
3.2
, thereby extending the generalized
Gauss-Green formula established by J. Harrison and A. Norton in

[
19
]
.

6.5.
Weak* convergence of fractional charges

We say that a sequence
(\omega_{n})
in
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)

converges weakly-*
to
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
whenever

(A)

the sequence
(\omega_{n})
is bounded in
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
;

(B)

\omega_{n}\to\omega
in
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
.

One could prove that this notion of weak* convergence corresponds to the duality formulation given in Theorem
6.4
whenever
K
is convex; however, we shall not rely on this more precise statement.

The next proposition, which establishes a compactness property
of
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
, implies that condition (B) can be replaced with

(B’)

\omega_{n}\to\omega
weakly in
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
, that is,

\omega_{n}(T)\to\omega(T)
for all
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
.

Proposition 6.5
.

Any bounded sequence in
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
has a subsequence that
converges in
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
to an
\alpha
-fractional charge.

Proof.

First observe that
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}
(defined on

\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
with values in
[0,\infty]
) is lower semi-continuous
with respect to weak convergence. Therefore, we only need to check
that a sequence
(\omega_{n})
that satisfies

M\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sup_{n}\|\omega_{n}\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}<\infty

has a convergent subsequence in
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
. This is an easy
consequence of the compactness criterion
(Proposition
5.2
), as for any
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
, any
integer
n
and
\varepsilon>0
, one has, by Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle|\omega_{n}(T)|

\displaystyle\leqslant M\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\varepsilon\mathbf{N}(T)\right)^{1-\alpha}\left(\frac{M^{1/\alpha}}{\varepsilon^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}}\mathbf{F}(T)\right)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant(1-\alpha)\varepsilon\mathbf{N}(T)+\frac{\alpha M^{1/\alpha}}{\varepsilon^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}}\mathbf{F}(T)

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon\mathbf{N}(T)+\frac{\alpha M^{1/\alpha}}{\varepsilon^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}}\mathbf{F}(T).\qed

The definition of weak* convergence is adapted for charges over

\mathbb{R}^{d}
as follows. We say that
\omega_{n}\to\omega
weakly-* in

\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
whenever

(A)

(\omega_{n})
is bounded in
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. As the
topology of
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is induced by the family of
seminorms
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},K}
, this means for

\sup_{n}\|\omega_{n}\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},K}<\infty\text{ for any
compact }K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d};

(B)

\omega_{n}\to\omega
in
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

As before, one proves that (B) can be replaced with the weaker

(B’)

\omega_{n}\to\omega
weakly in
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The smoothing of charges provides an example of weak*
convergence. Precise estimates are given in the next proposition. We recall that
B(K,\varepsilon)
denotes the closed tubular
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of a compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, see Subsection
2.1
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be
compact and
\varepsilon\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{]}
. We have

(A)

\|\omega*\Phi_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},K}\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},B(K,\varepsilon)}
;

(B)

\|\omega*\Phi_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,1},K}\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\alpha-1}\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},B(K,\varepsilon)}
;

(C)

\|\omega-\omega*\Phi_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m},K}\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\alpha}\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},B(K,\varepsilon)}
.

Proof.

Let
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
be arbitrary. Regarding (A), we have

|\omega*\Phi_{\varepsilon}(T)|=|\omega(T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})|\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K_{\varepsilon})}\mathbf{N}(T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})^{\alpha}

(29)

because
\spt(T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})\subseteq B(K,\varepsilon)
. By
Proposition
2.2
(E),

|\omega*\Phi_{\varepsilon}(T)|\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},B(K,\varepsilon)}\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\alpha}.

Therefore
\|\omega*\Phi_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},K}\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},B(K,\varepsilon)}
.

(B) is obtained by combining (
29
) with
Proposition
2.2
(F).

(C). This time,

\displaystyle|(\omega-\omega*\Phi_{\varepsilon})(T)|

\displaystyle=|\omega(T-T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},B(K,\varepsilon)}\mathbf{F}(T-T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})^{\alpha}\mathbf{N}(T-T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})^{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},B(K,\varepsilon)}\varepsilon^{\alpha}\mathbf{N}(T)^{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{N}(T)+\mathbf{N}(T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})\right)^{1-\alpha}

Prop.
2.2
(F)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},B(K,\varepsilon)}\varepsilon^{\alpha}\mathbf{N}(T)

Prop.
2.2
(E)

We conclude with the arbitrariness of
T
.
∎

Another example of weak* convergence is given by the following proposition.

Proposition 6.7
.

Let
(\omega_{n})
be a sequence in
\rmLip^{\alpha}_{\rmloc}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that, for all compact subsets
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
one has

\sup_{n}\rmLip^{\alpha}(\omega_{n\mid K})<\infty\text{ and }\omega_{n}\to\omega\text{ uniformly on }K.

Then
\omega_{n}\to\omega
weakly-* in
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

Let
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. From

\omega_{n}(T)-\omega(T)=\int_{\spt T}\langle\omega_{n}(x)-\omega(x),\vec{T}(x)\rangle\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\|T\|(x)

we infer
|\omega_{n}(T)-\omega(T)|\leqslant\|\omega_{n}-\omega\|_{\infty,\spt T}\mathbf{M}(T)\to 0
. This proves that
\omega_{n}\to\omega
weakly in
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

For any compact subset
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one has

\|\omega_{n}\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},K}\leqslant C\max\{\|\omega_{n}\|_{\infty,K},\rmLip^{\alpha}(\omega_{n\mid K})\}

by (
26
). This guarantees that the sequence

(\omega_{n})
is bounded in
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
∎

6.6.
A Littlewood-Paley type lemma

The following interpolation lemma, though simple, will prove crucial
in the remainder of this paper. Indeed, it will be an important
ingredient in the proof of our three main results and constructions:
the compactness theorem, pushforwards by Hölder maps and the wedge
product of fractional charges.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
K
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
0<\alpha<\beta\leqslant 1
and
\kappa\geqslant 0
. Suppose
(\omega_{n})
is a sequence in

\mathbf{CH}^{m,\beta}(K)
such that, for all
n\geqslant 0
, one has

\|\omega_{n}\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\beta}(K)}\leqslant 2^{-n(\alpha-\beta)}\kappa\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\omega_{n}\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)}\leqslant 2^{-n\alpha}\kappa

(30)

Then
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\omega_{n}\colon T\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\omega_{n}(T)
is well-defined, and is a
\alpha
-fractional charge
such that

\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\omega_{n}\right\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}\leqslant C\kappa

where
C=C(\beta,\alpha)
is a constant.

This lemma calls for several remarks:

•

First, the convergence of the series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\omega_{n}
is only weak* in
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
. Indeed, by applying
the same lemma to the truncated sequence
(\omega_{0},\dots,\omega_{N},0,\dots)
for all
N\geqslant 0
, one obtains

\sup_{N}\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{N}\omega_{n}\right\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}\leqslant C\kappa

therefore ensuring that the sequence of partial sums is bounded in

\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
. Moreover, the sequence of partials sums
converges weakly in
\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)
, as already asserted in
Lemma
6.8
.

•

Let us consider the case
m=0
and
\beta=1
. According to
Propositions
5.1
and
6.1
, charges,

\alpha
-fractional charges and
1
-fractional charges can be
identified with continuous,
\alpha
-Hölder continuous, and
Lipschitz functions, respectively. In this setting,
Lemma
6.8
establishes the
\alpha
-Hölder
continuity of a uniformly convergent series
f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}
. The estimates in (
30
) translate to
\rmLip f_{n}\leqslant C2^{-n(\alpha-1)}\kappa
and
\|f_{n}\|_{\infty,K}\leqslant C2^{-n\alpha}\kappa
, which aligns with the expected behavior of
the Littlewood-Paley components of
f
. Moreover, the weak*
convergence of the sum
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\omega_{n}
echoes with the
weak orthogonality properties observed in the Littlewood-Paley
decomposition in harmonic analysis. In the context of metric spaces,
and
m=0
, the existence of such a decomposition is proved
in
[
17
, Appendix B, 2.6]
.

Proof of Lemma
6.8
.

Let
T\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
. Using (
30
), we can estimate

|\omega_{n}(T)|
in two different ways

|\omega_{n}(T)|\leqslant 2^{-n(\alpha-\beta)}\kappa\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\beta}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\beta}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\omega_{n}(T)|\leqslant 2^{-n\alpha}\kappa\mathbf{N}(T)

Let
N
be a nonnegative integer, to be determined later. We have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N}\omega_{n}(T)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\kappa\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\beta}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\beta}\sum_{k=0}^{N}2^{-n(\alpha-\beta)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\kappa\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\beta}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\beta}2^{-N(\alpha-\beta)}

and

\left|\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\omega_{n}(T)\right|\leqslant\kappa\mathbf{N}(T)\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}2^{-n\alpha}\leqslant C\kappa\mathbf{N}(T)2^{-N\alpha}

Since
\mathbf{F}(T)\leqslant\mathbf{N}(T)
, we choose
N
to be a nonnegative
integer such that

2^{-(N+1)}\leqslant\frac{\mathbf{F}(T)}{\mathbf{N}(T)}\leqslant 2^{-N}

Using the preceding inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\omega_{n}(T)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\kappa\left(\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\beta}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\beta}\left(\frac{\mathbf{F}(T)}{\mathbf{N}(T)}\right)^{\alpha-\beta}+\mathbf{N}(T)\left(\frac{\mathbf{F}(T)}{\mathbf{N}(T)}\right)^{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\kappa\mathbf{N}(T)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T)^{\alpha}\qed

When adapted to charges over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the preceding proposition takes
the following form. We state it only for the case
\beta=1
, which
will be used.

Corollary 6.9
.

Suppose
0<\alpha<1
. Let
(\omega_{n})
be a sequence in

\mathbf{CH}^{m,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that, for each compact
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
there is
\kappa(K)\geqslant 0
such that

\|\omega_{n}\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,1},K}\leqslant\kappa(K)2^{n(1-\alpha)}\text{ and }\|\omega_{n}\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m},K}\leqslant\frac{\kappa(K)}{2^{n\alpha}}

for all
n
. Then
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\omega_{n}
converges weakly-* to
a charge in
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In addition, for all compact
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\omega_{n}\right\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha},K}\leqslant C\kappa(K)

(31)

Of course, it is enough to check the hypothesis of
Corollary
6.9
when
K
ranges over (non degenerate) closed
balls. A Littlewood-Paley type decomposition of a fractional charge

\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
will be obtained by convolution

\omega=\omega*\Phi_{1}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\omega*\Phi_{2^{-(n+1)}}-\omega*\Phi_{2^{-n}}\right).

This decomposition will play a pivotal role in the forthcoming proof
of Theorem
9.1
.

7.
Compactness theorem

The purpose of this section is to prove the following compactness
theorem

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact subset,
0\leqslant\alpha<1
and
c\geqslant 0
. Then

\{T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K):\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)\leqslant c\}

is
\mathbf{F}^{\beta}
-compact, for all
\beta\in\mathopen{(}\alpha,1\mathclose{]}
.

7.1.
Little fractional charges

Our first step towards the compactness theorem is to prove that

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K)
is itself a dual space, specifically the space of
so-called little fractional charges. This will help us show that the
ball
\{T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K):\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)\leqslant c\}
is closed in
flat norm.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. We define the map

\iota_{K}\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)\colon\omega\mapsto(T\mapsto T(\omega))

Note that
\iota_{K}(\omega)=\Lambda(\omega_{\mid K})
for all smooth
forms
\omega
, where
\Lambda
is the map
from (
23
). For all
S\in\mathbf{N}_{m+1}(K)
, one has

\displaystyle|\iota_{K}(\omega)(T)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|(T-\partial S)(\omega)|+|S(\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\mathbf{M}(T-\partial S)+\mathbf{M}(S)\right)\max\{\|\omega\|_{\infty,K},\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\|_{\infty,K}\}

In case
K
is convex, we take the infimum over all
S\in\mathbf{N}_{m+1}(K)
and deduce that

\|\iota_{K}(\omega)\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,1}(K)}\leqslant\max\{\|\omega\|_{\infty,K},\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\|_{\infty,K}\}

and

\|\iota_{K}(\omega)\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}\leqslant\|\iota_{K}(\omega)\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,1}(K)}\leqslant\max\{\|\omega\|_{\infty,K},\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\|_{\infty,K}\}.

(32)

The space
\mathbf{ch}^{m,\alpha}(K)
of
little
\alpha
-fractional
charges
is defined to be the closure in
\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
of

\operatorname{im}\iota_{K}
. It is given the norm inherited from

\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
K
be a compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Then
\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K)
is isomorphic to the dual of
\mathbf{ch}^{m,\alpha}(K)
. The corresponding duality bracket is the restriction
of (
27
) to
\mathbf{ch}^{m,\alpha}(K)\times\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K)
.

Proof.

Let
\Upsilon\colon\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K)\to\mathbf{ch}^{m,\alpha}(K)^{*}
be
the map
T\mapsto\langle\cdot,T\rangle
. It is clear that

\Upsilon
is linear and continuous, as the bracket satisfies the
continuity condition

|\langle\omega,T\rangle|\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)=\|\omega\|_{\mathbf{ch}^{m,\alpha}(K)}\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)

for all
\omega\in\mathbf{ch}^{m,\alpha}(K)
and
T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K)
.

Moreover,
\Upsilon
is injective, for if
\Upsilon(T)=0
, then
for all compactly supported smooth
m
-form
\omega
, one has

T(\omega)=\langle\iota_{K}(\omega),T\rangle=\Upsilon(T)(\iota_{K}(\omega))=0

Next we turn to surjectivity of
\Upsilon
. Let
\varphi\in\mathbf{ch}^{m,\alpha}(K)^{*}
. We define
T\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
by

T(\omega)=\varphi(\iota_{K}(\omega))

It is clear that
T
is linear. By the continuity of
\varphi

and (
32
), one infers

|T(\omega)|\leqslant\|\varphi\|\max\{\|\omega\|_{\infty,K},\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\|_{\infty,K}\}

This implies in particular that
T
is an
m
-current, compactly
supported in
K
. But at this point, it is not even clear that
T
is a flat chain (for this claim, we need to prove that
T
is a limit of normal currents under the flat norm).

For each integer
n\geqslant 1
, we define
T_{n}=T*\Phi_{2^{-n}}

which is a smooth
m
-current. Let us denote
L=B(K,1)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\operatorname{dist}(x,K)\leqslant 1\}
. We
remark that all the currents
T_{n}
belong to
\mathbf{N}_{m}(L)
. Observe
that
L
is still a compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let us denote
by
p\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to L
the orthogonal projection onto
L
.

Claim
.

For all
n
, there is a smooth
m
-form
\theta_{n}
such that

(T_{n+1}-T_{n})(\theta_{n})\geqslant\frac{\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T_{n+1}-T_{n})}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\|\iota_{L}(\theta_{n})\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(L)}\leqslant 1

(33)

To make notations shorter, let us abbreviate
R=T_{n+1}-T_{n}

during the proof of this claim. By Theorem
6.4
, there
exists a charge
\theta\in\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(L)
such that

\theta(R)\geqslant\frac{2}{3}\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(R)\text{ and }\|\theta\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(L)}=1

Let us define the smooth form
\theta_{n}=p^{\#}\theta*\Phi_{\varepsilon}
, where
\varepsilon
is to be determined
shortly. We have

\displaystyle R(\theta_{n})

\displaystyle=\theta\left(p_{\#}(R*\Phi_{\varepsilon})\right)

\displaystyle=\theta\left(R*\Phi_{\varepsilon}\right)

if
\varepsilon+2^{-n}\leqslant 1
(this ensures that
\spt(R*\Phi_{\varepsilon})\subseteq L
). By Proposition
2.2
(E)
and (G), one has

\displaystyle\left|\theta(R)-\theta\left(R*\Phi_{\varepsilon}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{N}(R-R*\Phi_{\varepsilon})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(R-R*\Phi_{\varepsilon})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\alpha}\mathbf{N}(R)

so that

\displaystyle(T_{n+1}-T_{n})(\theta_{n})

\displaystyle=R(\theta_{n})

\displaystyle=\theta(R*\Phi_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\geqslant\theta(R)-|\theta(R)-\theta(R*\Phi_{\varepsilon})|

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{2}{3}\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T_{n+1}-T_{n})-C\varepsilon^{\alpha}\mathbf{N}(R)

and the first inequality in (
33
) is achieved if

\varepsilon
(depending on
R
) is small enough. As for the second one, one computes,
for
S\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(L)
,

\displaystyle|\iota_{L}(\theta_{n})(S)|

\displaystyle=|S(\theta_{n})|

\displaystyle=|\theta(p_{\#}(S*\Phi_{\varepsilon}))|

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{N}(p_{\#}(S*\Phi_{\varepsilon}))^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(p_{\#}(S*\Phi_{\varepsilon}))^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{N}(S)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(S)^{\alpha}

which guarantees that
\|\iota_{L}(\theta_{n})\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(L)}\leqslant 1
.

Claim
.

For every smooth
m
-form
\omega
and
0<\varepsilon<1
, one
has

(T*\Phi_{\varepsilon})(\omega)=T(p^{\#}\iota_{L}(\omega)*\Phi_{\varepsilon})

Indeed, let
S
be a normal
m
-current supported in the set
U=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\operatorname{dist}(K,x)<1-\varepsilon\}
. The charge
p^{\#}\iota_{L}(\omega)*\Phi_{\varepsilon}

appearing in the right-hand side satisfies

\displaystyle(p^{\#}\iota_{L}(\omega)*\Phi_{\varepsilon})(S)

\displaystyle=\iota_{L}(\omega)(p_{\#}(S*\Phi_{\varepsilon}))

\displaystyle=\iota_{L}(\omega)(S*\Phi_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=S*\Phi_{\varepsilon}(\omega)

\displaystyle=S(\omega*\Phi_{\varepsilon})

It follows that the smooth forms
\omega*\Phi_{\varepsilon}
and

p^{\#}\iota_{L}(\omega)*\Phi_{\varepsilon}
agree on
U
(here we use that
U
is open). As
T
is
supported in
K\subseteq U
, the claim follows.

It follows from the two preceding claims that, setting

\eta_{n}=p^{\#}\iota_{L}(\theta_{n})*\Phi_{2^{-(n+1)}}-p^{\#}\iota_{L}(\theta_{n})*\Phi_{2^{-n}}

one has

\varphi(\iota_{K}(\eta_{n}))=T(\eta_{n})=(T_{n+1}-T_{n})(\theta_{n})\geqslant\frac{\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T_{n+1}-T_{n})}{2}

(34)

Our last goal is to apply Lemma
6.8
with
\beta=1
, and for this, we need to estimate the norms
\|\iota_{K}(\eta_{n})\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,1}(K)}
and
\|\iota_{L}(\eta_{n})\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)}
.

Let
S\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
. Then

\displaystyle|\iota_{K}(\eta_{n})(S)|

\displaystyle=|S(\eta_{n})|

\displaystyle=\left|\iota_{L}(\theta_{n})\left(p_{\#}(S*\Phi_{2^{-(n+1)}}-S*\Phi_{2^{-n}})\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\iota_{L}(\theta_{n})\left(S*\Phi_{2^{-(n+1)}}-S*\Phi_{2^{-n}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{N}\left(S*\Phi_{2^{-(n+1)}}-S*\Phi_{2^{-n}}\right)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}\left(S*\Phi_{2^{-(n+1)}}-S*\Phi_{2^{-n}}\right)^{\alpha}

as
\|\iota_{L}(\theta_{n})\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(L)}\leqslant 1
. Using
Proposition
2.2
(E) and (F), one first estimates

|\iota_{K}(\eta_{n})(S)|\leqslant C2^{-n(\alpha-1)}\mathbf{F}(S)

which entails that
\|\iota_{K}(\eta_{n})\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,1}(K)}\leqslant 2^{-n(1-\alpha)}
. On the other hand, Proposition
2.2
(G)
implies that

\mathbf{F}\left(S*\Phi_{2^{-(n+1)}}-S*\Phi_{2^{-n}}\right)\leqslant\mathbf{F}(S-S*\Phi_{2^{-(n+1)}})+\mathbf{F}(S-S*\Phi_{2^{-n}})\leqslant C2^{n}\mathbf{N}(S)

thus
|\iota_{K}(\eta_{n})(S)|\leqslant C2^{n\alpha}\mathbf{N}(S)
. Hence
\|\iota_{K}(\eta_{n})\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m}(K)}\leqslant C2^{n\alpha}
.

Finally, by applying Lemma
6.8
to the sequences of
charges

(\iota_{K}(\eta_{1}),\iota_{K}(\eta_{2}),\dots,\iota_{K}(\eta_{N}),0,0,\dots)

where
N
is an arbitrary integer, we obtain that

M=\sup_{N\geqslant 1}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\iota_{K}(\eta_{n})\right\|_{\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(K)}<\infty

Hence, by (
34
)

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T_{n+1}-T_{n})\leqslant 2\varphi\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\iota_{K}(\eta_{n})\right)\leqslant 2M\|\varphi\|_{\mathbf{ch}^{m,\alpha}(K)^{*}}

As
N
is arbitrary, it follows that
(T_{n})
is a

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}
-Cauchy sequence in the Banach space

\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(L)
. Additionally,
(T_{n})
converges weakly to
T
,
and thus
T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K)
.

Finally, the linear continuous forms
\Upsilon(T)
and
\varphi

coincide on all little fractional charges of the form

\iota_{K}(\omega)
, for
\omega\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
. By density,
\varphi=\Upsilon(T)
, which concludes the
proof of the surjectivity of
\Upsilon
.
∎

Corollary 7.3
.

The ball
\{T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K):\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)\leqslant c\}
is

\mathbf{F}^{\beta}
-closed in
\mathbf{F}^{\beta}_{m}(K)
for all
\beta\in\mathopen{(}\alpha,1\mathclose{]}
.

Proof.

There is no loss in generality in assuming that
K
is compact
convex. Let us show that
\mathbf{ch}^{m,\alpha}(K)
is separable. The
inequality (
32
) guarantees that
\iota_{K}
factorizes
through

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C(K;\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})\times C(K;\wedge^{m+1}\mathbb{R}^{d})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{\iota}_{K}}

where
\varphi
is the map
\omega\mapsto(\omega_{\mid K},(\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega)_{\mid K})
and
\tilde{\iota}_{K}
is continuous. By the
separability of the middle space, we infer that
\operatorname{im}\iota_{K}
is separable, and so is
\mathbf{ch}^{m,\alpha}(K)
.

By the Banach-Alaoğlu theorem, the closed ball
B:=\{T\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(K):\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)\leqslant c\}
is weakly*
compact. Furthermore, the induced weak* topology on
B
is
metrizable, since
\mathbf{ch}^{m,\alpha}(K)
is separable,
by
[
23
, 2.6.23]
.

Let
(T_{n})
be a sequence in
B
that
\mathbf{F}^{\beta}
-converges to some

T\in\mathbf{F}^{\beta}_{m}(K)
. By the weak* sequential compactness of
B
,
we suppose, up to extracting a subsequence, that
(T_{n})
weak*
converges to
S\in B
. In particular, for each
\omega\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\wedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one has

T_{n}(\omega)=\iota_{K}(\omega)(T_{n})\to\iota_{K}(\omega)(S)=S(\omega)

hence the convergence
T_{n}\to S
is also weak (in the sense of
currents). As
\mathbf{F}^{\beta}
-convergence implies convergence in flat
norm, that in turn implies weak convergence, we conclude that
T=S\in B
by uniqueness of the weak limit. This finishes the proof.
∎

7.2.
Deformation theorem for fractional currents

The following result shows how well fractional currents can be
approximated by polyhedral currents. It is the version of the
deformation theorem (see
[
29
, Chapter 6,
Theorem 5.3]
) for
\alpha
-fractional currents. It gives the
“totally boundedness” part of Theorem
7.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. For every subset
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}
of cardinal
m
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we define the map

h_{I,a}\colon[0,1]^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon x\mapsto\varepsilon\left(a+\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{i}e_{i_{k}}\right)

where
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}
are the distinct elements of
I
. Here,
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
denotes the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that a current
P
is a
polyhedral
m
-current on the standard
\varepsilon
-grid
whenever it is a finite linear combination of currents of the form
h_{I,a\#}\left\llbracket[0,1]^{m}\right\rrbracket
.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
K
be a compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
0\leqslant\alpha<\beta\leqslant 1
. There is a constant
C=C(\alpha,\beta,K)\geqslant 0

such that for all
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)
and
\varepsilon>0
, one
can find a polyhedral
m
-current
P
on the standard
\varepsilon
-grid
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mathbf{F}^{\beta}(T-P)\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\beta-\alpha}\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)\text{ and }\spt P\subseteq B(K,C\varepsilon).

Proof.

Consider a decomposition
T=\sum_{k}T_{k}
into normal currents
such that

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}\leqslant 2\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)\text{ and }\spt T_{k}\subseteq K.

(35)

For each
k
, introduce two normal currents
A_{k}\in\mathbf{N}_{m}(K)
and

B_{k}\in\mathbf{N}_{m+1}(K)
such that
T_{k}=A_{k}+\partial B_{k}
and

\mathbf{M}(A_{k})+\mathbf{M}(B_{k})\leqslant 2\mathbf{F}(T_{k})
. In particular,
\mathbf{M}(A_{k})\leqslant 2\mathbf{M}(T_{k})
. We apply the deformation theorem
[
29
, Chapter 6,
Theorem 5.3]
at scale
\varepsilon
to
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
,
which provides decompositions

A_{k}=Q_{k}+\partial R_{k}+S_{k}\text{ and }B_{k}=\tilde{Q}_{k}+\partial\tilde{R}_{k}+\tilde{S}_{k}

where
Q_{k}
and
\tilde{Q}_{k}
are polyhedral currents on the standard
\varepsilon
-grid, and
R_{k},S_{k},\tilde{R}_{k},\tilde{S}_{k}
are normal currents
with the estimates

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(R_{k})\leqslant C\varepsilon\mathbf{M}(A_{k})\leqslant C\varepsilon\mathbf{M}(T_{k})

(36)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(S_{k})\leqslant C\varepsilon\mathbf{M}(\partial A_{k})=C\varepsilon\mathbf{M}(\partial T_{k})

(37)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(Q_{k})\leqslant C\mathbf{M}(A_{k})\leqslant C\mathbf{F}(T_{k})

(38)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(\partial Q_{k})\leqslant C\mathbf{M}(\partial A_{k})=C\mathbf{M}(\partial T_{k})

(39)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(\tilde{S}_{k})\leqslant C\varepsilon\mathbf{M}(\partial B_{k})\leqslant C\varepsilon(\mathbf{M}(T_{k})+\mathbf{M}(A_{k}))\leqslant C\varepsilon\mathbf{M}(T_{k})

(40)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(\tilde{Q}_{k})\leqslant C\mathbf{M}(B_{k})\leqslant C\mathbf{F}(T_{k})

(41)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(\partial\tilde{Q}_{k})\leqslant C\mathbf{M}(\partial B_{k})\leqslant C\left(\mathbf{M}(T_{k})+\mathbf{M}(A_{k})\right)\leqslant C\mathbf{M}(T_{k})

(42)

and with
\spt Q_{k},\spt\tilde{Q}_{k}\subseteq B(K,C\varepsilon)
.
Let
n
be such that

\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\beta}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\beta}\leqslant\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha}\leqslant\varepsilon^{\beta-\alpha}\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)

(43)

and define

P=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(Q_{k}+\partial\tilde{Q}_{k})

which is clearly a polyhedral current on the
\varepsilon
-grid, with
support in
B(K,C\varepsilon)
. We have

\mathbf{F}(Q_{k}+\partial\tilde{Q}_{k})\leqslant\mathbf{M}(Q_{k})+\mathbf{M}(\tilde{Q}_{k})\leqslant C\mathbf{F}(T_{k})

by (
38
) and (
41
). This clearly implies that

\mathbf{F}(T_{k}-Q_{k}-\partial\tilde{Q}_{k})\leqslant C\mathbf{F}(T_{k})

(44)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(T_{k}-Q_{k}-\partial\tilde{Q}_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}(S_{k}+\partial(R_{k}+\tilde{S}_{k}))

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{M}(S_{k})+\mathbf{M}(R_{k})+\mathbf{M}(\tilde{S}_{k})

\displaystyle\leqslant C\varepsilon\mathbf{N}(T_{k})

(45)

by (
37
), (
36
) and (
40
). Now,
by (
44
) and (
45
),

\mathbf{F}(T_{k}-Q_{k}-\partial\tilde{Q}_{k})\leqslant C\varepsilon^{1-\alpha/\beta}\mathbf{N}(T_{k})^{1-\alpha/\beta}\mathbf{F}(T_{k})^{\alpha/\beta}.

(46)

Furthermore, one has

\mathbf{N}(T_{k}-Q_{k}-\partial\tilde{Q}_{k})\leqslant\mathbf{N}(T_{k})+\mathbf{M}(Q_{k})+\mathbf{M}(\partial Q_{k})+\mathbf{M}(\partial\tilde{Q}_{k})\leqslant C\mathbf{N}(T_{k})

(47)

by (
38
), (
39
) and
(
42
). Therefore,

8.
Pushforward by Hölder maps

8.1.
Main result

The following theorem shows that it is possible to make sense of
pushforwards of fractional currents by Hölder maps. In the subsequent
subsection, we will extend this construction in the top-dimensional
case
m=d^{\prime}
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact set. Let
0\leqslant\alpha<\beta<1
and
0<\gamma<1
such that

\frac{m+\alpha}{\gamma}=m+\beta.

For all
\gamma
-Hölder continuous maps
f\colon K\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
,
there is a unique linear map

f_{\#}\colon\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)\to\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})

such that

(A)

in case
f
is a Lipschitz map,
f_{\#}
is the usual
pushforward operator;

(B)

for all
T\in\mathbf{F}_{m}^{\alpha}(K)
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{\beta}(f_{\#}T)\leqslant C\max\left\{\left(\rmLip^{\gamma}f\right)^{m-1+\beta},\left(\rmLip^{\gamma}f\right)^{m+\beta}\right\}\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)\qquad\text{if }m\geqslant 1

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{\beta}(f_{\#}T)\leqslant C\max\left\{1,\left(\rmLip^{\gamma}f\right)^{\beta}\right\}\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}(T)\qquad\text{if }m=0,

where the constant
C=C(d,\alpha,\beta)
;

(C)

if
(f_{n})
is a sequence in
\rmLip^{\gamma}(K;\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})

that converges pointwise to
f
with
\sup_{n}\rmLip^{\gamma}(f_{n})<\infty
, then
f_{n\#}T\to f_{\#}T
in flat norm, and therefore
weakly.

This theorem is of course also true in the case
\gamma=1
where
f

is Lipschitz continuous. In fact, this has already been treated in
Proposition
3.1
(F). In this special case,
\alpha=\beta
can be equal to
1
.

The following example shows, however, that the restriction
\beta<1

is natural in the purely Hölder case
\gamma<1
. Suppose
m=1
,

\alpha=0
, and
\gamma\in(1/2,1)
. Suppose
f_{1},f_{2}
are two

\gamma
-Hölder continuous functions
[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, and let

f=(f_{1},f_{2})
. By the preceding theorem, the pushforward
f_{\#}\llbracket 0,1\rrbracket
defines a
\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}-1\right)
-fractional current.

In particular, we can evaluate this current against the smooth
differential form
x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y
, which yields the Young integral

\int_{0}^{1}f_{1}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}f_{2}=:f_{\#}\llbracket 0,1\rrbracket(x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y).

However, in the critical case
\gamma=1/2
(corresponding to
\beta=1
),
Young’s theory breaks down and the above expression cannot, in general,
be given a meaningful interpretation. In stochastic settings, most notably
when
f_{2}
is a sample path of Brownian motion and
f_{1}
belongs to a suitable
class of processes, the integral can instead be interpreted in the sense of
Itô. This construction, however, is intrinsically probabilistic and is not
determined solely by the sample paths. We refer to
[
16
]
for a
detailed discussion of how the integral can be defined pathwise in certain cases by enhancing

f_{2}
to a rough path.

(61)

The basic properties of the pushforward operator are summarized in the
following proposition.

Proposition 8.2
.

Let

as in

8.1
and

.

(A)

\spt f_{\#}T\subseteq f(\spt T)
.

(B)

f_{\#}\partial T=\partial f_{\#}T
for
m\geqslant 1
.

(C)

Let

be a compact set such that
f(K)\subseteq L
and
g\colon L\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}
a
\gamma^{\prime}
-Hölder continuous map, where
0<\gamma^{\prime}<1
. We suppose that
\beta^{\prime}\in(\beta,1)
is such that

\frac{m+\alpha}{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}=\frac{m+\beta}{\gamma^{\prime}}=m+\beta^{\prime}.

Then
(g\circ f)_{\#}T=g_{\#}f_{\#}T
.

(D)

The current
f_{\#}T
depends only on
T
and the
restriction of
f
to
\spt T
.

Proof.

(A). Let
(f_{n})
be a sequence of Lipschitz maps
K\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}

that converge pointwise to
f
with equi-bounded Hölder
constants. Then, it is clear that, for all

and
n\geqslant n_{0}
,
one has

\spt f_{n\#}T\subseteq\bigcup_{n\geqslant n_{0}}B\left(f(\spt T),\|f-f_{n}\|_{\infty,K}\right).

As
f_{n\#}T
converges weakly to

by

8.1
(C), we deduce that

\spt f_{\#}T\subseteq\bigcap_{n_{0}\geqslant 0}\bigcup_{n\geqslant n_{0}}B\left(f(\spt T),\|f-f_{n}\|_{\infty,K}\right)=f(\spt T).

(B). First we observe that the left-hand side
f_{\#}\partial T

makes sense, as
\partial T
is an
\alpha
-fractional

(m-1)
-current and

\frac{m-1+\alpha}{\gamma}=m+\beta-\frac{1}{\gamma}<m+1-1=m.

The equality

is obviously true
if

is Lipschitz continuous, and the Hölder case is obtained by a

We next prove (C) in the special (unmentioned) case
\gamma=1
and

\beta=\alpha
where
f
is Lipschitz continuous. Let
(g_{n})
be a
sequence in
\rmLip^{\gamma^{\prime}}(L;\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}})

to

is a

\rmLip^{\gamma^{\prime}}(K;\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}})

to

(D). Let

We remark that the Young-type integral defined in

corresponds to a special case of this construction, namely the product
of a Hölder function (seen as a fractional

-charge.

Using the weak* density of smooth forms, it is easy to extend the
well-known formulae of exterior calculus to fractional charges. Among
them, we have, for
\omega\in\mathbf{CH}^{m,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\eta\in\mathbf{CH}^{m^{\prime},\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(A)

\wedge
is bilinear;

(B)

The last item uses the weak*-to-weak* continuity of the exterior

Regarding the product of many fractional charges, we notice that

\omega_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\omega_{k}
makes sense (and the product

\omega_{i}

\alpha_{i}
-fractional and

(\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}-(k-1))

Pullbacks of fractional charges by locally Hölder maps can also be
defined via duality with the pushforward operator on fractional
currents. However, this operation and its compatibility with the wedge
product is not studied in this work.

10.
Fractional currents as metric currents

We conclude this paper with a section that discusses some questions
related to the characterization of metric currents of snowflaked
Euclidean spaces. This problematic was introduced in
[
34
]
,
upon which we further build.

Ambrosio and Kirchheim introduced metric
currents in
[
1
]
, while Lang provided in
[
22
]
a
definition of metric currents in locally compact metric spaces without
relying on a finite mass assumption. Here, we reproduce the
definition of Hölder currents, a variant of Lang’s definition, that
was introduced by Züst in
[
35
, Definition 2.2]
. For simplicity,
we consider currents in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with compact support.

Definition 10.1
.

Let
(\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m})\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{]}^{m+1}
. An
m
-dimensional
(\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m})
-Hölder current is a multilinear map

T\colon\prod_{k=0}^{m}\rmLip^{\alpha_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}

that satisfies:

(A)

Locality:
T(g^{0},g^{1},\dots,g^{m})=0
whenever some there
is
1\leqslant k\leqslant m
such that
g^{k}
is constant on an open
neighborhood of
\spt g^{0}
.

(B)

Compact support: there is a compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that
T(g^{0},g^{1},\dots,g^{m})=0
whenever
\spt g^{0}\cap K=\emptyset
.

(C)

Continuity: for any sequences
(g^{k}_{n})_{n}
(
0\leqslant k\leqslant m
) that converge uniformly to
g^{k}
with equi-bounded

\alpha_{k}
-Hölder constants, one has

\lim_{n\to\infty}T(g^{0}_{n},\dots,g^{m}_{n})=T(g^{0},\dots,g^{m}).

The space of such currents is denoted
\mathscr{D}_{m}^{(\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m})}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The particular case
\alpha_{0}=\cdots=\alpha_{m}=1
corresponds to
the compactly supported currents in the sense of Lang. More generally,
for any
\theta\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{]}
, the

m
-dimensional
(\theta,\dots,\theta)
-Hölder currents are the
compactly supported currents of the snowflaked Euclidean space

(\mathbb{R}^{d},d_{\mathrm{Eucl}}^{\theta})
, in the sense of Lang.

We expect that the class of
(\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m})
-Hölder
currents possesses a geometric description that depends solely on the
parameter

\alpha=\alpha_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{m}-m.

If
\alpha\leqslant 0
, then it is known (see
[
34
, Theorem 4.5]
)
that
\mathscr{D}_{m}^{(\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m})}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=0
. If
\alpha=1
,
i.e.

\alpha_{0}=\cdots=\alpha_{m}=1
, then it was
conjectured in
[
22
]
that the corresponding currents are in
correspondence with Federer-Fleming’s flat currents. However, this has
been recently disproved by J. Takáč in
[
30
]
.

Next we treat the case
0<\alpha\leqslant 1
. If
g^{k}\in\rmLip^{\alpha_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
0\leqslant k\leqslant m
, then the functions

g_{k}
can be seen as
\alpha_{k}
-fractional
0
-charges over

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore, the product of charges

g^{0}\wedge\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}g^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}g^{m}

makes sense, and is, according to Section
9
, an

\alpha
-fractional charge. Therefore, to each
S\in\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we may associate the multilinear map

\Upsilon(S)\colon\prod_{k=0}^{m}\rmLip^{\alpha_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}\colon(g^{0},\dots,g^{m})\mapsto\langle g^{0}\wedge\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}g^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}g^{m},S\rangle.

It is easily proven that
\Upsilon
is injective, and that, using the
continuity properties of
\wedge
,
\Upsilon(S)
is an
m
-dimensional

(\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m})
-Hölder current. We believe that

\Upsilon
is in fact a bijection
\mathscr{D}_{m}^{(\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m})}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if
\alpha\neq 1
. This
assertion may be regarded as a fractional variant of the flat chain
conjecture.

In particular, taking all
\alpha_{k}
to be equal, we may regard

\alpha
-fractional currents as metric currents in the sense of Lang
of the snowflaked space
(\mathbb{R}^{d},d_{\mathrm{Eucl}}^{\frac{m+\alpha}{m+1}})
.
