Adaptive and non-adaptive randomized approximation of high-dimensional vectors
Robert J. Kunsch, Marcin Wnuk

Abstract
We study approximation of the embedding ℓ p m ↪ ℓ q m \ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m} , 1 ≤ p < q ≤ ∞ 1\leq p<q\leq\infty , based on randomized algorithms that use up to n n arbitrary linear functionals as information on a problem instance where n ≪ m n\ll m . By analysing adaptive methods we show upper bounds for which the information-based complexity n n exhibits only a ( log ⁡ log ⁡ m ) (\log\log m) -dependence. In the case q < ∞ q<\infty we use a multi-sensitivity approach in order to reach optimal polynomial order in n n for the Monte Carlo error. We also improve on non-adaptive methods for q < ∞ q<\infty by denoising known algorithms for uniform approximation.



Keywords:
Monte Carlo, information-based complexity, upper bounds,
adaption, confidence

Introduction

We continue our study from
[
14
,
15
]
on the
\ell_{q}
-approximation of vectors
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
relative to their
\ell_{p}
-norm
via algorithms
A_{n}
that use at most
n
adaptively chosen randomized linear measurements
of
\boldsymbol{x}
.
We consider
1\leq p<q\leq\infty
and write
\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}
as a short-hand for the problem.
Studying these sequence space embeddings
is foundational for understanding the approximation of many other more complicated linear problems
such as function approximation, see for instance
[
4
,
19
]
in the context of randomized approximation.

A randomized
algorithm
(
method
,
scheme
)
A=(A^{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}

for the approximation problem
\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}

is a family of mappings
A^{\omega}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

indexed by elements of an underlying probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,{\mathbb{P}})
,
for any fixed
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
the output
A(\boldsymbol{x})\colon\omega\mapsto A^{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})
is a random variable.
We restrict to mappings where the output is generated
based on finitely many pieces of information
y_{i}=L_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})\in{\mathbb{R}}
with linear functionals
L_{i}
that may depend on
\omega\in\Omega
, thus being random.
In addition we allow for
adaptivity
, that is, the choice of
L_{i}
(or the decision whether we even want to continue collecting information) may also depend on previously obtained information
y_{1},\ldots,y_{i-1}
.
Writing
k=k(\omega,\boldsymbol{x})\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
for the number of evaluated information functionals,
the output of the algorithm is of the form
A(\boldsymbol{x})=\phi(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
,
where the reconstruction map
\phi
may also depend on
\omega\in\Omega
.
We consider algorithms with a strict bound on the number of pieces of information,
the
cardinality
of
A
(also called
information cost
) is defined as

\card A:=\sup_{\omega,\boldsymbol{x}}k(\omega,\boldsymbol{x})\,.

The error of
A
is defined by the worst case expectation

e(A,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}):=\sup_{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{x}-A(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{q}

(1)

with the classical
\ell_{p}
-norms (
1\leq p<\infty
)

\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}:=\left(|x_{1}|^{p}+\ldots+|x_{m}|^{p}\right)^{1/p},\quad\text{and}\quad\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}:=\max_{i=1,\ldots,m}|x_{i}|\,.

We aim for the error of optimal randomized algorithms, hence, the quantity of interest is

e^{\textup{ran}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}):=\inf_{A_{n}}e(A_{n},\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\,,

(2)

where the infimum is taken over algorithms
A_{n}
with cardinality at most
n
.
Conversely, for
\varepsilon>0
we define the (Monte Carlo)
\varepsilon
-complexity of a problem by

n^{\textup{ran}}(\varepsilon,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}):=\inf\left\{\card A\;\middle|\;\text{algorithms $A$ with }e(A,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

The key finding of
[
14
,
15
]
was that under certain circumstances
the minimal error (
2
) can only be achieved with
adaptive
methods.
In other words, the error will be considerably larger if we restrict to
non-adaptive
methods
where
all information functionals
L_{i}
are chosen independently of the input
\boldsymbol{x}
and
the information can be written as
\boldsymbol{y}=N^{\omega}\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with a random matrix
N^{\omega}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
.
Analogously to (
2
),
we define
e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})

and
n^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(\varepsilon,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})

with the infimum taken over non-adaptive methods.

In
[
14
, Thm 2.7]
a lower bound for non-adaptive methods was shown, namely,
with suitable constants
C,a>0
,

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\geq e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{1}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m})\geq\frac{1}{100}\qquad\text{for }m\geq C\cdot e^{an^{2}}.

(3)

In other words, for large problem size
m
relative to the cardinality
n

(or for small cardinality
n\leq c\sqrt{\log m}
compared to the dimension
m
, with suitable
c>0
)
the error of non-adaptive methods
cannot be significantly smaller than the initial error

e^{\textup{ran}}(0,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})=1
.
The follow-up paper
[
15
]
was dedicated to adaptive algorithms for uniform approximation (
q=\infty
),
showing

e^{\textup{ran}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m})\preceq\min\left\{1,\,\left(\frac{\log n+\log\log m}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\right\}\qquad\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2\,,

(4)

see
[
15
, Thm 3.3]
. Together with the lower bounds (
3
),
this lead to a gap of order
n
(up to logarithmic terms) between adaptive and non-adaptive approximation
[
15
, Thm 4.1]
:

\frac{e^{\textup{ran}}(n,\ell_{1}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m})}{e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{1}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m})}\preceq\frac{\log n}{n}\qquad\text{for $m=m_{n}:=\left\lceil C\,e^{an^{2}}\right\rceil$.}

(5)

The algorithm presented in
[
15
]
is based on ideas of Woodruff and different co-authors
[
9
,
17
,
18
]
who studied related problems of
stable sparse recovery
.
Already in
[

, Thm 3.1]
we cited results from
[

,
17
,
18
]
to show

e^{\textup{ran}}(n,\ell_{1}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{2}^{m})\preceq\min\left\{1,\,\sqrt{\frac{\log\log\frac{m}{n}}{n}}\right\},

(6)

leading to a gap of order
\sqrt{n}
up to logarithmic terms
[
14

\frac{e^{\textup{ran}}(n,\ell_{1}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{2}^{m})}{e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{1}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{2}^{m})}\preceq\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\qquad\text{for $m=m_{n}:=\left\lceil C\,e^{an^{2}}\right\rceil$.}

(7)

In fact, the gaps (
5
) and (

[

.
In this paper we continue the presentation of

,

.
By this we obtain gaps between adaptive and non-adaptive methods for a broader range of regimes, see Theorem

Asymptotic notation

We use asymptotic notation to compare functions
f
and
g
that depend on variables
(\varepsilon,m)
or
(n,m)
, writing
f\preceq g
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
f\leq Cg
holds for “small
\varepsilon
and large values of
m\varepsilon^{p}
”
(say, for
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}
and
m\varepsilon^{p}\geq 16
),
or for “
n\ll m
” (say,
\frac{m}{n}\geq 16
), respectively.

Weak asymptotic equivalence

f\asymp g
means
f\preceq g\preceq f
.
We also use
strong asymptotic equivalence

f\simeq g
to state
f/g\to 1
for
\varepsilon\to 0
and
m\varepsilon^{p}\to\infty
, or
n\to\infty
and
\frac{m}{n}\to\infty
, respectively.
The implicit constant
C
or the convergence
f/g
is to be understood for fixed values of the parameters
p
and
q
.

A recurring theme in this paper are bounds that contain a double logarithm
\log\log x
as a factor.
This is defined for
x>1
and monotonically increasing, but it only exhibits positive values for
x>e
.
However, we also need that such a factor is greater than a positive constant,
thus we restrict ourselves to
x\geq 16
which ensures
\log\log x>1
,
at the price of constraints on the domain of asymptotic relations.
Asymptotic relations under varying restrictions can be challenging,
so in Appendix
A
we prove a
less obvious result.

2
Toolkit for adaptive approximation

The numerical problem under consideration is
\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}
for
1\leq p<q<\infty
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
The adaptive approximation scheme described in this paper
will identify the most important entries of the given vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and measure them directly.
In precise terms, we determine a set
K\subseteq[m]:=\{1,\ldots,m\}
and yield an output
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}_{K}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with

z_{j}:=\begin{cases}x_{j}&\text{for }j\in K,\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

(8)

How do we come up with a set
K
?
We start by splitting
[m]
into smaller sets
J_{d}
,
d\in[D]
, so-called buckets, see Section
2.1
.
From each set
J_{d}
we then adaptively find one element to be included in
K
, see Section
2.2
.
The precise combination of these two steps is studied in Section
2.3
.
Further, Section
2.4
adds a step that allows for much larger buckets
J_{d}
to begin with,
reducing this bucket to a significantly smaller set
S_{d}\subseteq J_{d}
from which we are to identify one supposedly important element.
The whole process will be repeated several times in the final approximation algorithm, see Section
3.1
.

Notation:
Given a vector
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{j})_{j\in[m]}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}={\mathbb{R}}^{[m]}
and a set
J\subset[m]
we define the sub-vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{J}:=(x_{j})_{j\in J}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{J}
.

2.1
Hashing

Let
D\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\boldsymbol{H}=(H_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
be a family of random variables with values in
[D]
.
(
\boldsymbol{H}
is a so-called
hash function
or
hash family
.)
This defines disjoint (random) buckets

J_{d}=J_{d}^{\boldsymbol{H}}:=\{i\in[m]\colon H_{i}=d\}\subseteq[m]\,.

For
j\in[m]
we denote by
B_{j}
the bucket containing
j
, that is,

B_{j}=B_{j}^{\boldsymbol{H}}:=\{i\in[m]:H_{j}=H_{i}\}\,.

(9)

Usually,
\boldsymbol{H}
is chosen with pairwise independent values
H_{i}\sim\Uniform[D]
, each uniformly distributed on
[D]
. Pairwise independence ensures
{\mathbb{P}}(H_{i}=H_{j})=\frac{1}{D}
for
i\neq j
,
allowing for a probabilistic bound on the norm of the sub-vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{B_{j}\setminus\{j\}}
,
see
[
15
, Lemma 2.1]
.
For the precise asymptotic error decay of the algorithm presented in this paper,
it will be also necessary to bound the cardinality
of the bucket
B_{j}
, but with pairwise independent hashing we have some uncertainty therein.
Alternatively, we can define a random hashing where each hash value
d\in[D]
occurs roughly the same number of times.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
D,m\in\mathbb{N}

and let
\pi\sim\Uniform(\Sym(m))
be a uniformly chosen random permutation of
[m]
. Consider the random vector

\boldsymbol{H}:=\left(\left\lceil\frac{\pi(i)\cdot D}{m}\right\rceil\right)_{i=1}^{m}.

We call the distribution of
\boldsymbol{H}
the equi-hash distribution with parameters
m,D
and denote it by
\EquiHash(m,D)
.

Note that a random vector distributed according to
\EquiHash(m,D)
takes values in
[D]^{m}
and satisfies two crucial properties:

1.

Each entry value appears either
\lfloor m/D\rfloor
or
\lceil m/D\rceil
times.
In particular, a bucket
B
obtained via
\boldsymbol{H}\sim\EquiHash(m,D)
satisfies

\#B\leq 1+\frac{m}{D}\,.

2.

The entries are pairwise negatively dependent in the sense that for
i\neq j
one has

{\mathbb{P}}(H_{i}=H_{j})\leq\frac{1}{D}\,.

The statement of
[
15
, Lemma 2.1]
and its proof transfer to hashing by
\EquiHash
.
For the convenience of the reader we restate it now as Lemma
2.2
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
j\in[m]
,
1\leq p<\infty
, and

\alpha\in(0,1)
.
If buckets
B_{j}
, see (
9
),
are generated by a hash vector
\boldsymbol{H}
with
{\mathbb{P}}(H_{i}=H_{j})\leq\frac{1}{D}

for
i\neq j
, then we have the probabilistic bound

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{B_{j}\setminus\{j\}}\right\|_{p}>\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}_{[m]\setminus\{j\}}\|_{p}}{(\alpha D)^{1/p}}\right)\leq\alpha\,.

This applies in particular to

•

hash vectors
\boldsymbol{H}
with pairwise independent entries
H_{i}\sim\Uniform[D]
,

•

hash vectors
\boldsymbol{H}\sim\EquiHash(m,D)
.
In this case
\#B_{j}\leq\left\lceil\frac{m}{D}\right\rceil
.

The analysis of random measurements naturally leads to estimates that involve the
\ell_{2}
-norm.
In particular, by hashing we aim to isolate important coordinates
x_{j}
in a way that,
with sufficiently high probability,
it satisfies a so-called
heavy-hitter condition
with
heavy-hitter constant

\gamma>1
on the corresponding bucket
B_{j}
:

\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{B_{j}\setminus\{j\}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{|x_{j}|}{\gamma}\,.

(10)

For this requirement the appropriate choice of
D
is as follows.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty

and
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
.
Further let
\gamma>1
,
\varepsilon,\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
, and assume that
|x_{j}|\geq\varepsilon
.
If we take

D:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left\lceil\left(\gamma/\varepsilon\right)^{p}\cdot\delta_{0}^{-1}\right\rceil&\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2,\\
\displaystyle\left\lceil m^{1-2/p}\cdot\left(\gamma/\varepsilon\right)^{2}\cdot\delta_{0}^{-1}\right\rceil&\text{for }2<p<\infty,\end{cases}

and draw a hash vector
\boldsymbol{H}
as in Lemma
2.2
,
then

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{B_{j}\setminus\{j\}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{|x_{j}|}{\gamma}\right)\geq 1-\delta_{0}\,.

Moreover, with

\boldsymbol{H}\sim\EquiHash(m,D)

we have

\#B_{j}\leq\begin{cases}\left\lceil m\cdot(\varepsilon/\gamma)^{p}\cdot\delta_{0}\right\rceil&\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2,\\
\left\lceil m^{2/p}\cdot(\varepsilon/\gamma)^{2}\cdot\delta_{0}\right\rceil&\text{for }p>2.\end{cases}

Proof.

We apply Lemma
2.2
with
\alpha=\delta_{0}
to guarantee
\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{B_{j}\setminus\{j\}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{|x_{j}|}{\gamma}
with probability
1-\delta_{0}
.
First, in the case
1\leq p\leq 2
we have
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{B_{j}\setminus\{j\}}\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}_{B_{j}\setminus\{j\}}\|_{p}
, and the choice of
D
with
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{[m]\setminus\{j\}}\|_{p}\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
leads to a probabilistic guarantee for

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{B_{j}\setminus\{j\}}\|_{p}\leq\frac{1}{(\delta_{0}D)^{1/p}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\gamma}\,.

In the case
p>2
we use
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}\leq m^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
The choice of
D
and
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
leads to the probabilistic bound

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{B_{j}\setminus\{j\}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{m^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}}{\sqrt{\delta_{0}D}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\gamma}\,.

This shows the assertion.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

While a hash vector
\boldsymbol{H}\sim\EquiHash(m,D)
gives desirable theoretical guarantees,
generating a hash vector
\boldsymbol{H}
with pairwise independent entries might be cheaper
(e.g. taking fewer random bits).
With pairwise independent entries we have
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\#B_{j}]=1+\frac{m-1}{D}
,
but we would need a probabilistic guarantee for the cardinality
\#B_{j}
to work with.
Applying Lemma
2.2
to the vector
(1,\ldots,1)
with
p=1
, we find an estimate for the cardinality of the bucket
B_{j}
:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\#B_{j}>1+\frac{m-1}{\alpha D}\right)\leq\alpha\,.

With fully independent hashing
\boldsymbol{H}\sim\Uniform([D]^{m})
, even better estimates are possible as
\#B_{j}
concentrates around its expectation.

In any event, we can use Lemma
2.2
with failure probability
\alpha=\delta_{0}/2

for estimating the
p
-norm of the sub-vector and the cardinality of
B_{j}
.
By a union bound, the probability that both estimates hold is at least
1-\delta_{0}
.
In detail, in the case of
1\leq p\leq 2
, if we take

D:=\left\lceil\left(\frac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}\right)^{p}\cdot\frac{2}{\delta_{0}}\right\rceil,

and draw a hash vector
\boldsymbol{H}
with pairwise independent entries
H_{i}\sim\Uniform[D]
, then
for
\boldsymbol{x}
with
x_{j}
as in the assumptions of Corollary
2.3
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{B_{j}\setminus\{j\}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{|x_{j}|}{\gamma}\quad\text{and}\quad\#B_{j}\leq 1+(m-1)\cdot\left(\varepsilon/\gamma\right)^{p}\right)\geq 1-\delta_{0}\,.

2.2
Spotting a single heavy hitter

Details of the adaptive routine
\spot
described in this section can be found in
[
15
, Sec 2.3]
,
here we only describe essential aspects to address small adjustments necessary in our context.
The original idea of the algorithm stems from
[
9
, Sec 3.1]
.

Having split the domain
[m]
into buckets
J_{d}
,
d\in[D]
, on each bucket
J=J_{d}
we run a routine
\spot_{\delta_{2},k^{\ast}}(\boldsymbol{x},J)
to identify the most important coordinate
j\in J
.
The routine succeeds in detecting a single coordinate
j\in J

with probability at least
1-\delta_{2}
,
provided this coordinate satisfies the heavy hitter condition

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{J\setminus\{j\}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{|x_{j}|\cdot\delta_{2}}{129\sqrt{2\log\frac{16}{\delta_{2}}}}\,,

(11)

see
[
15
, Lem 2.9 with Rem 2.10]
.
This routine produces a nested sequence of sets

J=S_{0}\supseteq S_{1}\supseteq\ldots\supseteq S_{k^{\ast}}

via iterated shrinking
where each shrinking step
S_{k}\leadsto S_{k+1}
is based on a subdivision of
S_{k}
into up to

D_{k}=D_{k}(\delta_{2}):=\left\lceil 2^{4\cdot(9/8)^{k}+k+2}\delta_{2}^{-1}\right\rceil>2^{4\cdot(9/8)^{k}}

(12)

smaller buckets via a random hash vector
\boldsymbol{H}^{(k)}
that is independent of previous hash vectors (here, hashing with pairwise independent entries will do the job). We identify a small bucket
S_{k+1}:=\shrink_{\boldsymbol{H}^{(k)}}(\boldsymbol{x},S_{k})
via a shrinking subroutine that takes two randomized measurements of
\boldsymbol{x}
and with high probability ensures
j\in S_{k+1}
for
k=0,\ldots,k^{\ast}-1
.
If we obtain
\#S_{k}=1
or
S_{k}=\emptyset
for some
k\leq k^{\ast}
, then this is the output of
\spot
,
otherwise, in a final step we yield the set

\spot_{\delta_{2},k^{\ast}}(\boldsymbol{x},J):=\shrink_{\boldsymbol{h}^{\ast}}(\boldsymbol{x},S_{k^{\ast}})

where
\boldsymbol{h}^{\ast}
provides a trivial hashing of
S_{k^{\ast}}
into one-element sets.
In
[
15
, eq (11)]
we chose a stopping index
k^{\ast}=k^{\ast}(m)

that guaranteed
D_{k^{\ast}}\geq m
,
hence, the hash vector
\boldsymbol{h}^{\ast}:=(1,\ldots,m)

was suitable for the final shrinkage step.
Here now, in order to reduce the cost of the algorithm
we exploit that after initial hashing of
[m]
via
\EquiHash(m,D)
,
we control the size of the bucket,
\#J\leq\left\lceil\frac{m}{D}\right\rceil
, which is significantly smaller than
m
.
Hence, a hash vector
\boldsymbol{h}^{\ast}
that enumerates the elements of
S_{k^{\ast}}
will exist already
if we ensure
D_{k^{\ast}}\geq\left\lceil\frac{m}{D}\right\rceil
, namely, by (
12
) it suffices to put

\displaystyle k^{\ast}=k^{\ast}(m/D)

\displaystyle:=\max\left\{0,\,\left\lceil\log_{\frac{9}{8}}\frac{\log_{2}\left\lceil\frac{m}{D}\right\rceil}{8}\right\rceil\right\}

(13)

\displaystyle\simeq\underbrace{\frac{1}{\log\frac{9}{4}}}_{\approx 8.4902}\cdot\log\log\frac{m}{D}\qquad\left(\text{for }\frac{m}{D}\to\infty\right).

Since the choice of
k^{\ast}
does not only depend on
m
,
in this paper we include the parameter
k^{\ast}
in the description of
\spot
in contrast to the notation in
[
15
]
.
By construction,
\spot_{\delta_{2},k^{\ast}}(\boldsymbol{x},J)
will be a one-element set
(or the empty set in case of failure), and according to the analysis in
[
15
, Sec 2.3]
,
if (
11
) holds then we have

{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl(\spot_{\delta_{2},k^{\ast}}(\boldsymbol{x},J)=\{j\}\Bigr)\geq 1-\delta_{2}\,.

The overall information cost of
\spot
is bounded by

n^{\ast}=2(k^{\ast}(m/D)+1)\asymp\log\log\frac{m}{D}\qquad\text{for }m\geq 16D.

(14)

Obviously,
n^{\ast}\geq 2
. On the other hand, recall that
m\geq 16D
ensures
\log\log\frac{m}{D}>1
.

Remark 2.5
.

If we use hashing of
[m]
into buckets
J_{1},\ldots,J_{D}
with pairwise independent hash values rather than using
\EquiHash(m,D)
,
then we only have a stochastic guarantee that
\#J_{d}\leq 1+\frac{2(m-1)}{\delta_{0}D}
.
Instead of
k^{\ast}=k^{\ast}(m/D)
we choose

k^{\ast}=k^{\ast}\left(1+\frac{2(m-1)}{\delta_{0}D}\right)=\max\left\{0,\,\left\lceil\log_{\frac{9}{8}}\frac{\log_{2}\left\lceil 1+\frac{2(m-1)}{\delta_{0}D}\right\rceil}{4}\right\rceil\right\},

deterministically bounding the cost of
\spot
,
while with sufficient probability we have
\#J_{d}\leq D_{k^{\ast}}

in which case we can rely on the probabilistic guarantees for
\spot
.
If we happen to end up with
D^{\ast}:=\#S_{k^{\ast}}>D_{k^{\ast}}
,
the attempt is considered a failure.
In that case we may still perform a last shrinking step with an injective hashing

\boldsymbol{h}^{\ast}\in[D^{\ast}]^{S_{k^{\ast}}}

without any probabilistic guarantee of recovery whatsoever,
or, alternatively, we may simply return the empty set.

2.3
Discovering important entries – simple version

We combine the previous two steps in the spirit of Hash-and-Recover by Woodruff et al.
[
17
, Sec E.2.2]

to form an algorithm that will (in expectation)

discover
around half
of the important coordinates when being run once.
In Section
3.1
we will see how several independent executions of
\discover
lead to a set of coordinates that—when measured directly—provide an approximation with small expected error.

For a suitable choice of
D\in{\mathbb{N}}

we pick a hash vector
\boldsymbol{H}\sim\EquiHash(m,D)
,
resulting in a decomposition
J_{1},\ldots,J_{D}
of
[m]
, see Section
2.1
.
For each bucket
J_{d}
we apply the adaptive
\spot
algorithm
with parameters
\delta_{2}=\frac{1}{3}

and
k^{\ast}=k^{\ast}(m/D)
, see Section
2.2
and (
13
).
Each instance of
\spot
has the information cost
n^{\ast}=2(k^{\ast}+1)
as described in (
14
),
moreover, its random parameters are independent of the initial hash vector
\boldsymbol{H}
.
In a simple version with only these two stages, our coordinate finding
algorithm for
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
returns the set

\discover_{D}^{0}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\bigcup_{d=1}^{D}\spot_{\delta_{2},k^{\ast}}(\boldsymbol{x},J_{d})

(15)

with at most
D
elements.
The superindex
0
indicates that we are talking about the basic version of
\discover
without preconditioning, see Section
2.4
for more details.
The choice of
D
depends on the
sensitivity

\varepsilon>0
we are interested in,
that means, coordinates
j
with
|x_{j}|\geq\varepsilon
shall exhibit at least a
50\%
chance of being detected by
\discover
. The precise value for
D
is given in the following Lemma.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
1\leq p<\infty
,
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
If we take

D:=\begin{cases}\left\lceil 4\cdot\left(387\sqrt{2\log 48}\right)^{p}\cdot\varepsilon^{-p}\right\rceil&\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2,\\
\left\lceil 1\,198\,152\cdot\log 48\cdot m^{1-2/p}\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\right\rceil&\text{for }p>2,\end{cases}

and perform
\spot_{\delta_{2},k^{\ast}}
with
\delta_{2}=\frac{1}{3}

and iteration depth
k^{\ast}
as in (
13
), namely,

k^{\ast}(m/D)\asymp\log\log\frac{m}{D}\simeq\log\log(m\varepsilon^{p})\,,\qquad\text{for $m\geq 16D$ or $m\varepsilon^{p}\to\infty$,}

then for every
coordinate
j\in[m]
with
|x_{j}|\geq\varepsilon
we have

{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(j\notin\discover_{D}^{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{2}\,.

The information cost of
\discover_{D}^{0}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\card(\discover^{0}_{D})

\displaystyle\leq D\cdot 2\bigl(k^{\ast}(m/D)+1\bigr)

\displaystyle\asymp\begin{cases}\varepsilon^{-p}\cdot\log\log(m\varepsilon^{p})&\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2\,,\\
m^{1-2/p}\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot\log\log(m\varepsilon^{p})&\text{for }p>2,\end{cases}

where the asymptotic equivalence holds for
m\varepsilon^{p}\geq 16
.

Proof.

If an execution of
\spot_{\delta_{2},k^{\ast}}

shall have failure probability at most
\delta_{2}=\frac{1}{3}

for detecting a coordinate
j\in[m]
with
|x_{j}|\geq\varepsilon
,
we require
j
to fulfil a heavy hitter condition (
10
)
with heavy hitter constant

\gamma=\frac{1}{\delta_{2}}\cdot 129\sqrt{2\log\frac{16}{\delta_{2}}}=387\cdot\sqrt{2\log 48}\approx 1076.8\,,

see (
11
).
Hashing shall provide this condition with failure probability at most
\delta_{0}=\frac{1}{4}
,
which by Corollary
2.3
leads to the choice of
D
as stated in the lemma.
In this setup the success for detecting a coordinate
j\in[m]
with
|x_{j}|\geq\varepsilon

can be computed as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(j

\displaystyle\in\discover_{D}^{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{x}_{B_{j}\setminus\{j\}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\gamma}\right)\cdot{\mathbb{P}}\left(\spot_{\delta_{2},k^{\ast}}(\boldsymbol{x},B_{j})=\{j\}\;\middle|\;\|\boldsymbol{x}_{B_{j}\setminus\{j\}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\delta_{0})\cdot(1-\delta_{2})=\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{2}{3}=\frac{1}{2}\,.

Finally, observe
\frac{m}{D}\asymp m\varepsilon^{p}
for
1\leq p\leq 2
, and
\frac{m}{D}\asymp m^{2/p}\varepsilon^{2}=\left(m\varepsilon^{p}\right)^{2/p}
for
p>2
,
compare the bound for the bucket size
\#B_{j}
in Corollary
2.3
.
In any event, within
the cost bound we find
\log\frac{m}{D}\asymp\log(m\varepsilon^{p})
.
∎

2.4
Preconditioning

The constant for
D
in Lemma
2.6
is quite big
(namely,
D\approx 4307\,\varepsilon^{-1}
for
p=1
, or
D\approx 4\,638\,287\,\varepsilon^{-2}
for
p=2
).
Improving upon this constant is irrelevant for weak asymptotic error and complexity bounds, yet smaller constants are desirable because the routine
\discover
requires
D
instances of
\spot
, hence the constant is roughly proportional to the total cost.
The sole aim of this section is to provide a preconditioning routine that helps to reduce the constant for
D
.
If one is interested only in the asymptotic behaviour
of the error,
one may skip this section and directly proceed with Section
3
.

We review
[
18
, Lem 49]
without using coding theory in our proof.
Starting from a relatively moderate heavy-hitter condition (
10
) on a set
J\subseteq[m]
with heavy-hitter constant
\gamma_{0}=\sqrt{5}
,
we may reduce the candidate set by a so-called preconditioning procedure that uses
k
independent measurements
where
k
is a parameter of this routine.
Namely, we use a Rademacher measurement matrix

A=(a_{ij})_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\ldots,k\\
j\in J\end{subarray}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times J},\qquad a_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{iid}}}{{\sim}}\Uniform\{\pm 1\}\,,

and we only retain the signs of these measurements:

\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{i})_{i=1}^{k}:=\sgn(A\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad\text{that is,}\quad s_{i}=\sgn\left(\sum_{j\in J}a_{ij}x_{j}\right)\in\{\pm 1\}\,.

(We put
\sgn(0)=1
at the price of asymmetry.)
For vectors
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}
we consider the Hamming distance

d_{H}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}):=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{1}\,.

Let
\boldsymbol{a}_{j}:=(a_{ij})_{i=1}^{k}
denote the
j
-th column of
A
.
We then define the output of the preconditioning algorithm as follows:

\precond_{k}(\boldsymbol{x},J):=\left\{j\in J\mid d_{H}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j},\boldsymbol{s})\leq\frac{k}{6}\;\vee\;d_{H}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j},-\boldsymbol{s})\leq\frac{k}{6}\right\}.

Lemma 2.7
.

For
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
j^{\ast}\in J\subset[m]
assume the heavy hitter condition

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{J\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{|x_{j^{\ast}}|}{\sqrt{5}}\,.

Let
\gamma>1
and
\delta_{1}\in(0,1)
.
If we choose

k:=\left\lceil 36\log\left(\frac{1+\frac{2}{5}\gamma^{2}}{\delta_{1}}\right)\right\rceil,

then, with
S:=\precond_{k}(\boldsymbol{x},J)\subseteq J
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(j^{\ast}\in S\quad\text{and}\quad\|\boldsymbol{x}_{S\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{|x_{j^{\ast}}|}{\gamma}\right)\geq 1-\delta_{1}\,.

Proof.

The idea is that
\boldsymbol{s}\approx\sgn(x_{j^{\ast}})\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{j^{\ast}}

in the sense that likely only few entries of these two vectors differ.
In fact, if

Y_{i}:=\sum_{j\in J\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}}\frac{x_{j}}{x_{j^{\ast}}a_{i,j^{\ast}}}\cdot a_{ij}>-1

holds then
s_{i}=\sgn(x_{j^{\ast}})\cdot a_{i,j^{\ast}}
.
Here,
Y_{i}
is the sum of independent random variables with square-summable absolute bounds
\frac{|x_{j}|}{|x_{j^{\ast}}|}
. By Hoeffding’s inequality we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(s_{i}\neq\sgn(x_{j^{\ast}}a_{i,j^{\ast}})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i}\leq-1)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{x_{j^{\ast}}^{2}}{2\|\boldsymbol{x}_{J\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}}\|_{2}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{5}{2}\right)<\frac{1}{12}\,.

It follows that

\mu:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[d_{H}(\boldsymbol{s},\sgn(x_{j^{\ast}})\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{j^{\ast}})\right]<\frac{k}{12}\,.

Since

\frac{1}{2}|s_{i}-\sgn(x_{j^{\ast}})\cdot a_{i,j^{\ast}}|\in\{0,1\}

are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables,
we may use a Chernoff bound for binomially distributed random variables,
namely, with
\delta=\frac{k}{6\mu}-1\geq 1
we find

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(d_{H}(\boldsymbol{s},\sgn(x_{j^{\ast}})\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{j^{\ast}})>\frac{k}{6}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\bigl(d_{H}(\boldsymbol{s},\sgn(x_{j^{\ast}})\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{j^{\ast}})>(1+\delta)\mu\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\min\{\delta,\delta^{2}\}\cdot\mu}{3}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{k/6-\mu}{3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{k}{36}\right)=:\alpha\,.

This gives a guarantee for correctly identifying a subset of
J
that still contains the important coordinate
j^{\ast}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(j^{\ast}\in\precond_{k}(\boldsymbol{x},J)\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(d_{H}(\boldsymbol{s},\sgn(x_{j^{\ast}})\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{j^{\ast}})\leq\frac{k}{6}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{k}{36}\right)=1-\alpha\,.

(16)

Now suppose that
j^{\ast}\in S=\precond_{k}(\boldsymbol{x},J)
. Then, by the triangle inequality we further know

S\subseteq\left\{j\in J\colon\min\{d_{H}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j},\boldsymbol{a}_{j^{\ast}}),d_{H}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j},-\boldsymbol{a}_{j^{\ast}})\}\leq\frac{k}{3}\right\}=:\overline{S}\,.

For
j\in J\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}
, the quantity
d_{H}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j},\boldsymbol{a}_{j^{\ast}})
follows a symmetrical binomial distribution with expectation
\mu:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[d_{H}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j},\boldsymbol{a}_{j^{\ast}})\right]=\frac{k}{2}
.
Another Chernoff bound,
with
\delta=\frac{1}{3}
,
gives

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(d_{H}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j},\boldsymbol{a}_{j^{\ast}})\leq\frac{k}{3}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\bigl(d_{H}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j},\boldsymbol{a}_{j^{\ast}})\leq(1-\delta)\mu\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}\mu}{2}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{k}{36}\right).

We can use this to estimate the mean squared norm of these coordinates:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\overline{S}\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in J\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}}{\mathbb{P}}\left(d_{H}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j},\boldsymbol{a}_{j^{\ast}})\leq\frac{k}{3}\text{ or }d_{H}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j},-\boldsymbol{a}_{j^{\ast}})\leq\frac{k}{3}\right)\cdot|x_{j}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{k}{36}\right)\cdot\|\boldsymbol{x}_{J\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}}\|_{2}^{2}\,.

By Markov’s inequality we find for
\gamma>1
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\overline{S}\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}}\|_{2}>\frac{|x_{j^{\ast}}|}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\overline{S}\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}}\|_{2}>\frac{\sqrt{5}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{x}_{J\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}}\|_{2}}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{5}\,\gamma^{2}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{k}{36}\right)=:\beta\,.

(17)

Finally, combining the findings (
16
) and (
17
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle\left(j^{\ast}\in S\quad\text{and}\quad\|\boldsymbol{x}_{S\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{|x_{j^{\ast}}|}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(j^{\ast}\in S\quad\text{and}\quad\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\overline{S}\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{|x_{j^{\ast}}|}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-{\mathbb{P}}\left(j^{\ast}\notin S\right)-{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\overline{S}\setminus\{j^{\ast}\}}\|_{2}>\frac{|x_{j^{\ast}}|}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\alpha-\beta\,.

The choice of
k
in the lemma ensures

\alpha+\beta=\left(1+\frac{2}{5}\,\gamma^{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{k}{36}\right)\leq\delta_{1}
.
∎

With the help of preconditioning we may define the following modification of the basic version of
\discover
, compare (
15
):

\discover_{D}^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\bigcup_{d=1}^{D}\spot_{\delta_{2},k^{\ast}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{x},\precond_{k}(J_{d})\bigr)\,.

(18)

Again, the three random components, namely hashing,
\spot
, and
\precond
shall be independent.
The parameters
D
,
\delta_{2}
,
k^{\ast}
, and
k
are to be chosen differently now.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
1\leq p<\infty
,
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
If we take

D:=\begin{cases}\left\lceil 6\cdot 5^{p/2}\cdot\varepsilon^{-p}\right\rceil&\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2,\\
\left\lceil 30\cdot m^{1-p/2}\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\right\rceil&\text{for }p>2,\end{cases}

and perform
\precond_{k}
with
k=551

as well as
\spot_{\delta_{2},k^{\ast}}
with
\delta_{2}=\frac{1}{4}

and iteration depth
k^{\ast}
as in (
13
), namely

k^{\ast}(m/D)\asymp\log\log\frac{m}{D}\simeq\log\log(m\varepsilon^{p})\,,

then for every
coordinate
j\in[m]
with
|x_{j}|\geq\varepsilon
we have

{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(j\notin\discover_{D}^{+}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{2}\,.

The information cost of
\discover_{D}^{+}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\card(\discover^{+}_{D})

\displaystyle\leq D\cdot\bigl(553+2k^{\ast}(m/D)\bigr)

\displaystyle\asymp\begin{cases}\varepsilon^{-p}\cdot\log\log(m\varepsilon^{p})&\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2,\\
m^{1-2/p}\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot\log\log(m\varepsilon^{p})&\text{for }p>2.\end{cases}

Proof.

We choose failure probabilities
\delta_{0}=\frac{1}{6}
,
\delta_{1}=\frac{1}{5}
, and
\delta_{2}=\frac{1}{4}
such that we can bound the success probability for any given heavy hitter by multiplying the success probabilities of hashing, preconditioning, and spotting:

(1-\delta_{0})(1-\delta_{1})(1-\delta_{2})=\frac{1}{2}
.
In this setup,
\spot_{\delta_{2},k^{\ast}}
requires the heavy hitter condition (
10
) with

\gamma=\frac{1}{\delta_{2}}\cdot 129\sqrt{2\log\frac{16}{\delta_{2}}}=516\cdot\sqrt{2\log 64}\approx 1488.2\,,

see (
11
).
From Lemma
2.7
we find the parameter choice

k:=\left\lceil 36\log\left(\frac{1+\frac{2}{5}\gamma^{2}}{\delta_{1}}\right)\right\rceil

for
\precond_{k}
.
Initial hashing, though, is cheaper now
since we choose
D
according to Corollary
2.3
but with the much smaller heavy hitter constant
\gamma_{0}=\sqrt{5}
.
The choice of the stopping index
k^{\ast}
for
\spot
follows from (
13
),
the change due to the smaller constant is marginal because of the double logarithm.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

With
k=551
we can estimate the expected cardinality of the preconditioned bucket

S:=\precond_{k}(\boldsymbol{x},B_{j})
where
j\in[m]
is a fixed (important) coordinate:

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\#(S\setminus\{j\})]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{551}{36}\right)\cdot\#(B_{j}\setminus\{j\})<\frac{\#(B_{j}\setminus\{j\})}{2\,218\,648}\,.

This means that if we have initial buckets with cardinality
\#B_{j}\leq 10^{7}
, then we will very likely end up with a preconditioned bucket
S
that contains only one element, so performing
\spot
would not be needed anymore.
If, however,
m
is larger, spot is still relevant and we need to know the size of a preconditioned bucket
S
to decide on the iteration depth
k^{\ast}
of
\spot
.
In the lemma we simply chose
k^{\ast}
on the basis of
\#S\leq\#B_{j}\leq\lceil\frac{m}{D}\rceil
, but one could also take into account a much smaller probabilistic bound on
\#S
.
In any event, the iteration depth of
\spot
will be

k^{\ast}\simeq\log_{\frac{9}{8}}\log(m\varepsilon^{p})
.

The advantage of preconditioning is most striking in the case
p=2
:
Here we roughly spend

551\cdot 30\,\varepsilon^{-2}=16\,530\,\varepsilon^{-2}

measurements in total
for preconditioning on
D\approx 30\,\varepsilon^{-2}
buckets.
This is still significantly smaller than the number of
D\approx 5\cdot 10^{6}\,\varepsilon^{-2}
buckets we would need to deal with in the basic version
\discover^{0}

without preconditioning.

Remark 2.10
.

Preconditioning could also be included in uniform approximation, i.e. the problem
\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m}
, see the prior work
[
15
]
.
In contrast to
\ell_{q}
-approximation with
q<\infty
,
for uniform approximation with expected error
\varepsilon
we require that with probability
1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

all coordinates with
|x_{j}|>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
are detected
in one run
of the algorithm, provided
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
for
1\leq p\leq 2
.
The modified method would have the following structure:

1.

Hashing
[m]
into
D
buckets.

2.

Selecting
k\asymp\varepsilon^{-p}
important buckets.

3.

Optionally: Preconditioning of the selected buckets.

4.

Applying
\spot
on each of the selected (preconditioned) buckets.

Preconditioning will help to significantly reduce
D
,
for instance, in case of
p=2
we could take

D\simeq 4096\,\varepsilon^{-4}

instead of
D\approx 7\cdot 10^{10}\cdot\varepsilon^{-14}

without preconditioning.
However, the information cost of the bucket selection step is only roughly proportional to
\log D
, and this is the only stage where
D
affects the cost.

3
Adaptive randomized approximation

In Section
3.1
we describe and analyse the final adaptive randomized algorithm
for finite-dimensional sequence space embeddings
\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}
.
In Section
3.2
we draw conclusions for the complexity and error rates.

3.1
A multi-sensitivity algorithm

Adaptive approximation of
\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}
for

1\leq p<q<\infty

is based on a repeated independent execution of
\discover
with different hashing parameters
D
.
The particular approach we are taking directly corresponds to the first phase of
[
17
, Alg 3]
.
Our error criterion allows for this simplified approach and a straightforward analysis.
For
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
, indicating different sensitivity levels of the algorithm, define hashing parameters

D^{(l)}:=\begin{cases}\left\lceil C_{p}\cdot 2^{l}\right\rceil&\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2,\\
\left\lceil C_{2}\cdot m^{1-2/p}\cdot 2^{l}\right\rceil&\text{for }p>2,\end{cases}

(19)

with the constant
C_{p}:=4\cdot\left(387\sqrt{2\log 48}\right)^{p}

if we work with the basic version
\discover^{0}
, compare Lemma
2.6
,
or
C_{p}:=6\cdot 5^{p/2}
if we work with the version
\discover^{+}
which features preconditioning, compare Lemma
2.8
.
From now on, we simply write
\discover
for any of the two versions.
The choice (
19
) of the hashing parameter
D^{(l)}

means that for vectors
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
, the routine
\discover_{D^{(l)}}

achieves sensitivity

\varepsilon_{l}:=\begin{cases}2^{-l/p}&\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2,\\
2^{-l/2}&\text{for }p>2,\end{cases}

meaning that, in expectation, at least half of the coordinates with
|x_{j}|\geq\varepsilon_{l}
will be discovered.
The algorithm is governed by two parameters
L,R\in{\mathbb{N}}
and first computes a set

K_{L,R}:=\bigcup_{l=1}^{L}\bigcup_{r=1}^{R}\discover_{D^{(l)}}^{(r,l)}(\boldsymbol{x})\,,

where by the superscripts
(r,l)\in[R]\times[L]
we indicate independent calls of
\discover
.
As a final output we return

A_{L,R}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\boldsymbol{x}_{K_{L,R}}^{\ast}

by
\#K_{L,R}
direct evaluations of entries of
\boldsymbol{x}
,
see (
8
) for the definition of
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}_{K}
given
K\subset[m]
.
The parameter
L
is the number of sensitivity levels,
the parameter
R
is the number of repetitions at each sensitivity level.
We may thus call the method a “multi-sensitivity algorithm”.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
m,L\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
1\leq p<q<\infty
, and
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
.
If we choose
R:=\lceil q/\min\{2,p\}\rceil
,
the algorithm
A_{L,R}
achieves accuracy

\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{x}-A_{L,R}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{q}^{q}\right)^{1/q}\leq 3^{1/q}\cdot\begin{cases}2^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\cdot L}&\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2,\\
2^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)\cdot L}&\text{for }p>2.\end{cases}

The information cost is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\card A_{L,R}

\displaystyle\preceq\begin{cases}\displaystyle 2^{L}\cdot\log\log\frac{m}{2^{L}}&\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2,\\
\displaystyle m^{1-2/p}\cdot 2^{L}\cdot\log\log\frac{m^{2/p}}{2^{L}}&\text{for }p>2,\end{cases}

the asymptotic relation holding for
m^{\max\{1,\,2/p\}}\geq 16\cdot 2^{L}
.

Proof.

For convenience we will write
p^{\prime}:=\min\{2,p\}
and
t_{+}:=\max\{0,t\}
for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}

to accommodate for both regimes at once.
We classify the coordinates according to the sensitivity levels
\varepsilon_{l}=2^{-l/p^{\prime}}

by forming index sets

I_{l}:=\left\{j\in[m]\colon\varepsilon_{l}<|x_{j}|\leq\varepsilon_{l-1}\right\}.

By definition of the sensitivity levels and
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
,
we have
\#I_{l}\leq\varepsilon_{l}^{-p}=2^{l\frac{p}{p^{\prime}}}
.
Further, the hash parameter
D^{(l)}
is chosen such that
for all
j\in I_{1}\cup\ldots\cup I_{l}
we have

{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(j\notin\discover_{D^{(l)}}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{2}\,,

see Section
2.3
.
In other words, for
1\leq l_{0}\leq L
and
j\in I_{l_{0}}
we know

{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(j\notin K_{L,R}\bigr)\leq\prod_{l=l_{0}}^{L}\prod_{r=1}^{R}{\mathbb{P}}\left(j\notin\discover_{D^{(l)}}^{(r,l)}\right)\leq 2^{-(L-l_{0}+1)\cdot R},

hence, we expect the following cardinality for the set of entries that have not been discovered:

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\#(I_{l}\setminus K_{L,R})\right]\leq 2^{l\frac{p}{p^{\prime}}-(L-l+1)\cdot R}.

We do not expect to recover any of the less important coordinates from the complementary set

C_{L}:=\{j\in[m]\colon|x_{j}|\leq\varepsilon_{L}\}\,,

though it might happen for some.
The
q
-norm of
\boldsymbol{x}_{C_{L}}
can be bounded by interpolation between the
\ell_{p}
- and the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm,
namely,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{p}+\frac{1-\lambda}{\infty}\quad\text{with }\lambda=\frac{p}{q}\in(0,1)\,,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{x}_{C_{L}}\|_{q}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}_{C_{L}}\|_{p}^{\lambda}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{x}_{C_{L}}\|_{\infty}^{1-\lambda}\leq 1^{\lambda}\cdot\varepsilon_{L}^{1-\lambda}\leq\varepsilon_{L}^{1-\frac{p}{q}}\leq 2^{-\frac{L}{p^{\prime}}\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)},

(20)

see for instance
[
16
, Lem 2.4]
.
This leads to the desired error estimate

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{x}-A_{L,R}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{q}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}_{C_{L}}\|_{q}^{q}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\#(I_{l}\setminus K_{L,R})\right]\cdot\varepsilon_{l-1}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\frac{L}{p^{\prime}}\left(q-p\right)}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}2^{l\frac{p}{p^{\prime}}-(L-l+1)\cdot R}\cdot 2^{-(l-1)\cdot\frac{q}{p^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{L}{p^{\prime}}\left(q-p\right)}\left(1+2^{\frac{q}{p^{\prime}}-R}\sum_{l=1}^{L}2^{-\left(\frac{p}{p^{\prime}}+R-\frac{q}{p^{\prime}}\right)\cdot(L-l)}\right).

With
\frac{p}{p^{\prime}}\geq 1
and by the choice of
R=\lceil q/p^{\prime}\rceil
we have

2^{\frac{q}{p^{\prime}}-R}\sum_{l=1}^{L}2^{-\left(\frac{p}{p^{\prime}}+R-\frac{q}{p^{\prime}}\right)\cdot(L-l)}\leq 1\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-k}=2\,,

hence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{x}-A_{L,R}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{q}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq 3\cdot 2^{-\frac{L}{p^{\prime}}\left(q-p\right)}.

Taking this to the power of
\frac{1}{q}
we find precisely the accuracy we claimed.

We use Lemma
2.6
or
2.8
, respectively,
to bound the combined cost for all calls of
\discover
as long as
m\geq 16D^{(L)}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{L}R\cdot

\displaystyle\card\left(\discover_{D^{(l)}}\right)\preceq\sum_{l=1}^{L}D^{(l)}\cdot\log\log\frac{m}{D^{(l)}}

\displaystyle\asymp m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot\sum_{l=1}^{L}2^{l}\cdot\log\log\frac{m^{p^{\prime}\!/p}}{2^{l}}\qquad\qquad\text{(using $(1-2/p)_{+}=1-p^{\prime}\!/p$)}

\displaystyle=m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot 2^{L}\log\log\frac{m^{p^{\prime}\!/p}}{2^{L}}\cdot\sum_{l=1}^{L}2^{-(L-l)}\cdot\frac{\log\left(\left(\log\frac{m^{p^{\prime}\!/p}}{2^{L}}\right)+(L-l)\log 2\right)}{\log\log\frac{m^{p^{\prime}\!/p}}{2^{L}}}

\displaystyle\preceq m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot 2^{L}\log\log\frac{m^{p^{\prime}\!/p}}{2^{L}}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-k}\cdot\log(2+k)

\displaystyle\asymp m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot 2^{L}\log\log\frac{m^{p^{\prime}\!/p}}{2^{L}}\,.

(21)

The algorithm
A_{L,R}
will finally return a vector with at most

\#K_{L,R}\leq\sum_{l=1}^{L}R\cdot D^{(l)}=R\sum_{l=1}^{L}\lceil C_{p^{\prime}}\cdot m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot 2^{l}\rceil\simeq C_{p}R\cdot m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot 2^{L+1}

directly measured coordinates and all other coordinates set to zero.
The information cost of this final step
is clearly dominated by (
21
)
which leads to the overall asymptotic cost bound as stated.
Note that (
21
) holds as a weak asymptotic upper bound of the cost
even if we extend the domain of comparison to
m^{p^{\prime}\!/p}\geq 16\cdot 2^{L}
, see Example
A.2
for the case
1\leq p\leq 2
.
∎

If we perform the instances of
\discover
sequentially,
we will find a growing sequence

\emptyset=K^{(0)}\subseteq\ldots\subseteq K^{(RL)}=K_{R,L}

of candidate sets,
and it is natural to apply the
i
-th instance of
\discover

\boldsymbol{x}_{[m]\setminus\{K^{(i)}\}}

as it has been suggested in

]
.
With this modification we might find better approximations,

Remark 3.3

(Homogeneity)
.

Note that
A_{L,R}
is homogeneous in the sense of

A_{L,R}(t\boldsymbol{x})=t\cdot A_{L,R}(\boldsymbol{x})
for any scalar
t\neq 0
,
see
[
11
]
for a general reference on homogeneous algorithms.
This is due to the fact that all adaption decisions in the scheme are always based on the ratio of measurements
but not on absolute values.
Homogeneity implies in particular that, for
1\leq p\leq 2
and all
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
we can state

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{x}-A_{L,R}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{q}\leq 3^{1/q}\cdot 2^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\cdot L}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\,.

3.2
Complexity

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
m\geq 16
,

1\leq p<q<\infty
.
Then for

\left(\frac{16}{m}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\leq\varepsilon<1

we have

n^{\textup{ran}}(\varepsilon,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\\
\preceq\begin{cases}\varepsilon^{\left.-1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\cdot\log\log\left(m\cdot\varepsilon^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\right)&\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2,\\
m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot\varepsilon^{\left.-2\middle/\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)\right.}\cdot\log\log\left(m\cdot\varepsilon^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\right)&\text{for }p>2.\end{cases}

Conversely, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
m\geq 16n
we have

e^{\textup{ran}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\preceq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\min\left\{1,\left(\frac{\log\log\frac{m}{n}}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\right\}&\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2,\\
\displaystyle\min\left\{1,\left(\frac{m^{1-2/p}\cdot\log\log\frac{m}{n}}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)}\right\}&\text{for }p>2.\end{cases}

Proof.

From the
q
-moment error stated in Theorem
3.1
and Jensen’s inequality we can directly conclude
on the Monte Carlo error as defined in (
1
), namely

e(A_{L,R},\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\leq 3^{1/q}\cdot 2^{-\frac{L}{p^{\prime}}\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)},

for
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
R=\lceil q/p^{\prime}\rceil
where we write
p^{\prime}=\min\{2,p\}
again.
If this shall be smaller or equal than a given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
then we need to choose

L:=\left\lceil\frac{\log_{2}\frac{3^{1/q}}{\varepsilon}}{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)}\right\rceil,

which implies

\varepsilon^{\left.-p^{\prime}\middle/\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)\right.}\leq 2^{L}\leq 2\cdot 3^{\left.p^{\prime}\middle/\left(q-p\right)\right.}\varepsilon^{\left.-p^{\prime}\middle/\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)\right.}.

(22)

This relation is independent of
m
and holds for all
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
(Overhashing with
2^{L}\geq m
would lead to error
0
because
\EquiHash(m,D)
would then put all coordinates into single element buckets,
hence all entries are measured and so the upper bound trivially holds.)
From Theorem
3.1
we know that for
m\geq 16\cdot 2^{L}
and with a suitable constant
C>1
we have
the following estimate, and by (
22
) it can be bounded in terms of
\varepsilon

(again with the notation
t_{+}=\max\{0,t\}
):

\card A_{L,R}\leq C\cdot m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot 2^{L}\cdot\log\log\frac{m^{p^{\prime}\!/p}}{2^{L}}\\
\leq\underbrace{C\cdot 2\cdot 3^{\left.p^{\prime}\middle/\left(q-p\right)\right.}}_{C^{\prime}}\,\cdot\,m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot\varepsilon^{\left.-p^{\prime}\middle/\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)\right.}\cdot\log\log\left(m\cdot\varepsilon^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\right).

(For
p>2
we also omitted the exponent
\frac{p^{\prime}}{p}=\frac{2}{p}<1
in the argument of the double logarithm.)
The constraint
m\cdot\varepsilon^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\geq 16

is less restrictive than
m^{p^{\prime}\!/p}\geq 16\cdot 2^{L}
,
but analogously to Example
A.2
we may extend the validity of the asymptotic estimate.

We are now passing to the asymptotic
n
-th minimal error.
By construction, the error of the method
A_{L,R}
is always bounded by
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
.
Aiming for better bounds,
we want to make sure that the cost does not exceed a given limit of
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, hence:

C\cdot m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot 2^{L}\cdot\log\log\frac{m^{p^{\prime}\!/p}}{2^{L}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{\leq}}n\,.

(23)

We will show that there exists a constant
c\in(0,1)
such that for
m\geq 16n
the choice

L:=\max\left\{0,\left\lfloor\log_{2}\left(c\cdot\frac{n}{m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot\log\log\frac{m}{n}}\right)\right\rfloor\right\}

ensures (
23
) if
L\geq 1
, that is, if

n\geq\frac{2}{c}\cdot m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot\log\log\frac{m}{n}\,.

(24)

If
n
violates (
24
), we have
L=0
and
we resort to the zero algorithm with cost
0
and error
1
,
the so-called
initial error
.
If, however, (
24
) holds, then we have

\frac{c}{2}\cdot\frac{n}{m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot\log\log\frac{m}{n}}<2^{L}\leq c\cdot\frac{n}{m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot\log\log\frac{m}{n}}\,,

(25)

which (with
(1-2/p)_{+}=1-p^{\prime}\!/p
) leads to

C\cdot m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot 2^{L}\cdot\log\log\frac{m^{p^{\prime}\!/p}}{2^{L}}<C\cdot c\cdot\frac{n}{\log\log\frac{m}{n}}\cdot\log\log\left(\frac{2}{c}\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log\log\frac{m}{n}\right).

(26)

For
x:=\frac{m}{n}\geq 16
and
0<c\leq 2
, observe

c\cdot\frac{\log\log\left(\frac{2}{c}\cdot x\cdot\log\log x\right)}{\log\log x}\leq c\cdot\frac{\log\log\left(\frac{2}{c}\cdot x^{2}\right)}{\log\log x}\leq c\cdot\frac{\log\log\left(\frac{512}{c}\right)}{\log\log 16}\xrightarrow[c\to 0]{}0\,.

This shows that the right-hand side of (
26
) is smaller or equal
n
if we take the constant
c
sufficiently small.
Now, combining (
25
) with the error bound of Theorem
3.1
, we obtain

e^{\textup{ran}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\leq 3^{1/q}\cdot\left(\frac{2}{c}\cdot\frac{m^{(1-2/p)_{+}}\cdot\log\log\frac{m}{n}}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)}.

(27)

This was shown assuming (
24
),
but if this is violated then the right-hand side of (
27
)
is larger than
3^{1/q}
, which is a trivial upper bound exceeding the initial error.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

We have put some effort into proving upper bounds with
\log\log\frac{m}{n}
instead of a much simpler bound with the factor
\log\log m
.
This difference is very subtle: If we take, for instance,
m=m(n):=\lfloor n\log n\rfloor
,
then

\frac{\log\log\frac{m}{n}}{\log\log m}\leq\frac{\log\log\log n}{\log\log\lfloor n\log n\rfloor}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\,.

If, however, we restrict to, say,
n\leq m^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then

\log\log m\geq\log\log\textstyle\frac{m}{n}\geq\log\log m^{1-\alpha}=\log\log m-\log\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\asymp\log\log m\,.

The error bound (
4
) for uniform approximation we found in
[
15
]

appears with the factor
\log n+\log\log m
.
For
n\geq\log m
this is clearly of order
\log n
.
For
n\leq\log m
, however, we are in a situation where
\log\log m\asymp\log\log\frac{m}{n}
.
To summarize,

\log n+\log\log m\asymp\log n+\log\log\textstyle\frac{m}{n}\,,

so the neglecting the reduced size of the buckets in our previous work on uniform approximation
did
not
lead to worse asymptotic bounds.

Remark 3.6

(Probabilistic error criterion)
.

In the context of uniform approximation the analysis of the algorithm started
in the probabilistic setting of a “small error with high probability”,
see
[
15
, Thm 3.1]
.
In the current paper, however, we directly go for the expected error, see Theorem
3.1
.
It is well known that by independently repeating an algorithm and combining the results in an appropriate way one can amplify the probability of success
(compare literature on the median trick, e.g.
[
21
]
).
In
A_{L,R}
, however, repetition is inherent to the algorithm,
namely, by choosing
R
proportional to
\log\delta^{-1}
we can achieve

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\\
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(\|A_{L,R}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{q}>\varepsilon\bigr)\leq\delta

in a very cost-effective way.
Here,
1-\delta
is the confidence level we aim for.

Remark 3.7

(Lower bounds)
.

Heinrich
[
4
]
proved that for
1\leq p<q<\infty
we have

e^{\textup{ran}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\succeq n^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}.

This lower bound shows that the
n
-dependence of the error rate in Theorem

is optimal,
If we consider

It remains an open problem to show that for

the
m
-dependence of our adaptive upper bounds is optimal as well.

We contrast this with lower bounds for non-adaptive methods from

For the sake of completeness,

4
Non-adaptive methods

In Sections
4.1
and
4.2
we will consider two basic algorithms:
\linsketch
which proves useful in the case
p\geq 2
, and
\countsketch
which is appropriate for
1\leq p<2
.
We need to modify these algorithms by denoising their outputs to achieve optimal rates if
q<\infty
, see Section
4.3
. The results will be summarized in Section
4.4
.

4.1
A linear Monte Carlo method

A fairly simple randomized non-adaptive method for approximating the embedding

\ell_{2}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m}
was described by Mathé
[
19
]
.
The information mapping can be represented by a matrix
N\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
with independent standard Gaussian entries,
and for
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
we define the output

\linsketch_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\frac{1}{n}N^{\top}N\boldsymbol{x}\,.

This method is linear, for
m\geq 2
we have

e(\linsketch_{n},\ell_{2}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m})\leq 2\,\sqrt{\frac{2\log m}{n}}\,.

More generally, for
p\geq 2
, exploiting
\|\cdot\|_{2}\leq m^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\|\cdot\|_{p}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we find

e(\linsketch_{n},\ell_{p}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m})\leq 2\,\sqrt{\frac{2\,m^{1-2/p}\log m}{n}}\,.

(28)

This bound is improving over the initial error
1
only for

n\geq 8\,m^{1-2/p}\log m
.

\linsketch
can also be analysed for the
\ell_{q}
-error with
2<q<\infty
giving

e(\linsketch_{n},\ell_{2}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\asymp\frac{m^{1/q}}{\sqrt{n}}

(29)

with
q
-dependent implicit constants.
The upper bound relies on
[
13
, Prop 3.1]
and
results on the expected
\ell_{q}
-norm of Gaussian vectors.
The lower bound is obtained by considering

\boldsymbol{x}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
.
As it turns out, via a non-linear modification (”denoising”) of this method we can get rid of the polynomial
m
-dependence for
q<\infty
.
We will describe this approach later after introducing
\countsketch
,
another non-adaptive method that is already non-linear from the beginning.

4.2
Count sketch

For the problem
\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m}
with
p<2
,
we use
\countsketch
, a non-linear randomized approximation method
first developed in
[
1
]
.
This is method is closely related to the bucket selection scheme from
[
15
, Sec 2.2]
, see also
[
17
, Lem 54]
, and has been mentioned in
[
15
, Rem 2.6]
.

For algorithmic parameters
R,G\in{\mathbb{N}}
where
R
is odd,
we generate independent hash vectors
\boldsymbol{H}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{H}^{(R)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{iid}}}{{\sim}}\Uniform[G]^{m}

and draw random signs

\sigma_{ri}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{iid}}}{{\sim}}\Uniform\{\pm 1\}
,
r\in[R]
,
i\in[m]
.
The algorithm takes
R\cdot G
Rademacher measurements:

Y_{r,g}=L_{r,g}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sum_{i\in[m]\colon H_{i}^{(r)}=g}\sigma_{ri}\cdot x_{i}\,,\qquad r\in[R],\,g\in[G].

That way we execute

R
repetitions of a grouped measurement where for each repetition the coordinates are
randomly sorted into
G
groups on which we perform individual measurements.
Hence, each coordinate
i\in[m]
exerts influence on exactly
R
measurements
Y_{r,g}
.
We use these to define the following variables:

\hat{Y}_{r,i}:=\sigma_{ri}Y_{r,H_{i}^{(r)}}\,,\qquad r\in[R].

The output
\boldsymbol{Z}:=\countsketch_{R,G}
of the method is defined using the median for each component in the following way:

Z_{i}:=\median\left\{\hat{Y}_{r,i}\;\middle|\;r\in[R]\right\}.

We provide a precise
error analysis for this method in our setting.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
1\leq p\leq 2
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
.
For
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
let
G:=2^{4+L}
and pick
R
as the smallest odd number such that

R\geq\max\{5,2+3\log_{2}m\}\,.

Then we obtain the error bound

e(\countsketch_{R,G},\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m})\leq 4\cdot 2^{-\frac{L}{p}}

while the cardinality of the method is

\card(\countsketch_{R,G})=R\cdot G\asymp 2^{L}\cdot\log m\,.

Proof.

For
k\in[m]
define
\varepsilon_{k}:=k^{-1/p}
.
Then, for any given vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
,
we find that at most
k
entries can have an absolute value larger than
\varepsilon_{k}
.
Let
Q_{k}\subseteq[m]
be a (not necessarily unique) set of
k
coordinates with the largest absolute values, i.e.
\#Q_{k}=k
and

\min_{i\in Q_{k}}|x_{i}|\geq\max_{i\in[m]\setminus Q_{k}}|x_{i}|\,.

Consider a fixed coordinate
i\in[m]
and for each round
r\in[R]
define the set of companion coordinates that are measured together with the
i
-th coordinate in that round:

C_{i}^{(r)}:=\left\{j\in[m]\setminus\{i\}\colon H_{i}^{(r)}=H_{j}^{(r)}\right\}.

By a union bound we estimate the probability that large coordinates are among the companion coordinates of
i
:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(Q_{k}\cap C_{i}^{(r)}\neq\emptyset\right)\leq\frac{k}{G}\,.

(30)

Obviously,
\hat{Y}_{r,i}
is unbiased:

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\hat{Y}_{r,i}=x_{i}
.
We compute the variance of
\hat{Y}_{r,i}
conditioned on the event that no large entries occur among the companion coordinates of
i
.
We do this by analysing
\hat{Y}_{r,i}
as a sum of independent centred random variables

\mathds{1}_{[{H_{j}}^{(r)}=H_{i}^{(r)}]}\cdot\sigma_{ri}\sigma_{rj}\cdot x_{j}

for
j\in[m]\setminus(Q_{k}\cup\{i\})
:

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\hat{Y}_{r,i}-x_{i}\right)^{2}\;\middle|\;Q_{k}\cap C_{i}^{(r)}=\emptyset\right]\leq\frac{1}{G}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{x}_{[m]\setminus Q_{k}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{k^{1-2/p}}{G}\,.

(31)

(In the last inequality of (
31
) we used
a well-known result on best
k
-term approximation which states

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{[m]\setminus Q_{k}}\|_{q}\leq k^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}
for
p<q
,
see e.g.
[
2
, eq (2.6)]
,
here with
q=2
and for
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
.)
With Chebyshev’s inequality applied to (
31
), and together with (
30
), we find

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(|\hat{Y}_{r,i}-x_{i}|>\varepsilon_{k}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left(|\hat{Y}_{r,i}-x_{i}|>\varepsilon_{k}\;\middle|\;Q_{k}\cap C_{i}^{(r)}=\emptyset\right)\cdot{\mathbb{P}}\left(Q_{k}\cap C_{i}^{(r)}=\emptyset\right)

\displaystyle\quad+{\mathbb{P}}\left(|\hat{Y}_{r,i}-x_{i}|>\varepsilon_{k}\;\middle|\;Q_{k}\cap C_{i}^{(r)}\neq\emptyset\right)\cdot{\mathbb{P}}\left(Q_{k}\cap C_{i}^{(r)}\neq\emptyset\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{k^{1-2/p}}{G}\cdot\varepsilon_{k}^{-2}\right)\cdot 1+1\cdot\frac{k}{G}=\frac{2k}{G}=:\alpha\,.

Taking the median of several independent estimates amplifies the probability of success.
In detail, from
[
21
, eq (2.6)]

we conclude that for
0<\alpha<\frac{1}{4}
, thus for
G>8k
,
with
Z_{i}
being the median of
R
independent copies of
\hat{Y}_{r,i}
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(|Z_{i}-x_{i}|>\varepsilon_{k}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(4\alpha\right)^{R/2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{8k}{G}\right)^{R/2}.

A union bound over all coordinates
gives the following result on the uncertainty for uniform approximation:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{Z}-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon_{k}\right)\leq\frac{m}{2}\left(\frac{8k}{G}\right)^{R/2}.

On the other hand, we know an absolute bound for the error of Rademacher measurements,
namely
\|\boldsymbol{Z}-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}\leq m^{1-1/p}
.
Altogether, for
G=2^{4+L}
and with values
k=2^{l}
for
l=0,\ldots,L
,
the expected error can be estimated as follows:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{Z}-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq m^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{Z}-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon_{1}\right)+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\varepsilon_{2^{l-1}}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{Z}-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon_{2^{l}}\right)+\varepsilon_{2^{L}}

\displaystyle\leq m^{2-\frac{1}{p}}\cdot 2^{-\frac{(L+1)R}{2}-1}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}m\cdot 2^{-\frac{l-1}{p}-\frac{(L-l+1)R}{2}-1}+2^{-\frac{L}{p}}

\displaystyle=m^{2-\frac{1}{p}}\cdot 2^{-\frac{(L+1)R}{2}-1}+m\cdot 2^{-\frac{L-1}{p}-\frac{R}{2}-1}\cdot\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(2^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{R}{2}}\right)^{L-l}+2^{-\frac{L}{p}}.

If we take
R>4
, then the sum is bounded by
2
.
Further,
R\geq 2+3\log_{2}m
ensures that the first term is bounded by
2^{-L/p}
,
as well as the pre-factor of the sum, leading to

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{Z}-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}\leq 4\cdot 2^{-L/p}
.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
1\leq p\leq 2
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
m\geq 2
.
Given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we find the following asymptotic cardinality bound for non-adaptive Monte Carlo methods:

n^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(\varepsilon,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m})\preceq\varepsilon^{-p}\cdot\log m\,.

Conversely, for
n<m
we find the following error rate:

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m})\preceq\left(\frac{\log m}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Note that for
p=2
the rate obtained with
\countsketch
is identical to the rate of
\linsketch
, but
\linsketch
is the simpler algorithm with smaller constant in the error estimate.

4.3
Denoising the output

The problem of the above uniform approximation algorithms is
that the output is quite noisy (with large
\ell_{2}
-norm),
potentially leading to an unnecessarily large error
if measured in the
\ell_{q}
-norm with
q<\infty
.
We solve this problem by keeping only
k
entries of the reconstruction with the largest absolute values and putting all other coordinates to zero.
Note that by this we introduce a bias to the method,
see
[
20
]
for a study of unbiased approximation methods.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
1\leq p<q\leq\infty
and let
A
be an algorithm with

e(A,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m})\leq C\cdot\varepsilon

where
C\geq 1
and
\varepsilon>0
.
For
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
denote the corresponding output
\boldsymbol{z}:=A(\boldsymbol{x})
.
Define
k:=\min\{\lfloor\varepsilon^{-p}\rfloor,m\}

and choose a
k
-element set
I_{k}=I_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})\subseteq[m]
satisfying

\min_{i\in I_{k}}|z_{i}|\geq\max_{i\in[m]\setminus|I_{k}}|z_{i}|\,.

Based on this we define the denoised algorithm
D
with output
\boldsymbol{w}=D(\boldsymbol{x})
where

w_{i}=\begin{cases}z_{i}&\text{if }i\in I_{k}(\boldsymbol{z}),\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Then

\textstyle\frac{1}{3}\,\varepsilon^{1-\frac{p}{q}}\leq e(D,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\leq(1+5C)\cdot\varepsilon^{1-\frac{p}{q}}\,

where the lower bound holds under the additional assumption
2k+1\leq m
.
Moreover,

\card D=\card A\,.

Proof.

The fact that

\card D=\card A

is obvious as

D
is only adding a post-processing step without any extra measurements.

We start by showing the upper error bound.
We obtain the
\omega
-wise
\ell_{q}
-error by interpolating between
\ell_{p}
- and
\ell_{\infty}
-error, compare (
20
):

\|D^{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{q}\leq\|D^{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}^{p/q}\cdot\|D^{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}^{1-p/q}.

(32)

Given an
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

denote the
\ell_{\infty}
-error at the current instance by

\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathcal{E}^{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{x}}:=\|A^{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}\,.

Thus
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a random variable
with
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\leq C\cdot\varepsilon
.
For
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
, by the choice of
k
, there are at most
k
entries of
\boldsymbol{x}
with
|x_{i}|\geq\varepsilon
.
Hence, there are at most
k
entries of
\boldsymbol{z}^{\omega}=A^{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})
with
|z_{i}^{\omega}|\geq\varepsilon+\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\omega}
.
Therefore, if for an entry of
\boldsymbol{w}^{\omega}=D^{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})
we have
w_{i}^{\omega}=0
, then
|z_{i}^{\omega}|<\varepsilon+\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\omega}
and

|w_{i}^{\omega}-x_{i}|\leq|w_{i}^{\omega}-z_{i}^{\omega}|+|z_{i}^{\omega}-x_{i}|<\varepsilon+2\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\omega}
.
If, however,
w_{i}^{\omega}\neq 0
, then
w_{i}^{\omega}=z_{i}^{\omega}
and
|w_{i}^{\omega}-x_{i}|=|z_{i}^{\omega}-x_{i}|\leq\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\omega}
.
This shows

\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\omega}-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}=\|D^{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon+2\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\omega}.

(In particular,
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|D(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}\leq(1+2C)\cdot\varepsilon
, so denoising does not deteriorate the error guarantees for uniform approximation by much.)
Furthermore,

\|D^{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}^{p}=\sum_{j\in I_{k}}|z_{j}^{\omega}-x_{j}|^{p}+\|\boldsymbol{x}_{I_{k}^{c}}\|_{p}^{p}\leq k\cdot(\mathcal{E}^{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{x}})^{p}+1\,.

(33)

Substituting (
33
) into (
32
),
taking the expectation, and keeping in mind that
k\leq\varepsilon^{-p}
,
we find

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|D(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{q}

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[(k\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{p}+1)^{1/q}\cdot(\varepsilon+2\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}})^{1-p/q}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[(k^{1/q}\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{p/q}+1)\cdot(\varepsilon^{1-p/q}+(2\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}})^{1-p/q})\right]

\displaystyle=k^{1/q}\varepsilon^{1-p/q}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{p/q}]+2k^{1/q}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}+\varepsilon^{1-p/q}+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[(2\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}})^{1-p/q}]

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{1-2p/q}\cdot\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)^{p/q}+2\varepsilon^{-p/q}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}+\varepsilon^{1-p/q}+\left(2\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)^{1-p/q}

\displaystyle\leq\left(C^{p/q}+2C+1+(2C)^{1-p/q}\right)\varepsilon^{1-p/q}

\displaystyle\leq(1+5C)\cdot\varepsilon^{1-p/q}.

Since this holds for all
\boldsymbol{x}
with
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
,
we proved the upper bound.

We now show the lower bound, starting
with the case
q=\infty
.
Taking as an input vector
\boldsymbol{x}
with some
(k+1)

entries set to

(\lfloor\varepsilon^{-p}\rfloor+1)^{-1/p}

and all the other
entries set to
0
,
we have
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}=1
and for
\boldsymbol{w}=D(\boldsymbol{x})
we get

\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}\geq(\lfloor\varepsilon^{-p}\rfloor+1)^{-1/p}\geq 2^{-1/p}\,\varepsilon\,.

To prove the lower bound in the case
q<\infty
consider an input vector
\boldsymbol{x}
having some
2k+1
entries set to

(2k+1)^{-1/p}=(2\lfloor\varepsilon^{-p}\rfloor+1)^{-1/p}

and all the other entries set to
0
.
Once again
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}=1
and

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{q}

\displaystyle\geq\left((k+1)\cdot(2k+1)^{-q/p}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle=\left((\lfloor\varepsilon^{-p}\rfloor+1)\cdot(2\lfloor\varepsilon^{-p}\rfloor+1)^{-q/p}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\geq 3^{-1/p}\,\varepsilon^{1-p/q}.

This finishes the proof
∎

We apply the above lemma to the non-adaptive algorithms we introduced before.
For
1\leq p<2
we take
\countsketch
with the
\ell_{\infty}
-error rates from Corollary
4.2
,
and, combined with the choice
k:=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\log m}\right\rfloor

for the denoised algorithm, we obtain

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\preceq\left(\frac{\log m}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}.

(34)

For
2\leq p<\infty
, employing
\linsketch
with the error rates for
\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m}
from (
28
),
and with
k:=\left\lfloor\left(\frac{n}{m^{1-2/p}\log m}\right)^{p/2}\right\rfloor
,
we find

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\preceq\left(\frac{m^{1-2/p}\cdot\log m}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)}.

(35)

Note that for
n<m^{1-2/p}\log m
we have
k=0
, that is, we fall back on the zero algorithm
which is essentially the best we can do in this setting.
Alternatively we could use the relation

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\leq m^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\cdot e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{2}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\,,

(36)

exploiting
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}\leq m^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
If we combine (
36
) with

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{2}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\preceq\left(\frac{\log m}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}},

the result will be consistently worse than (
35
).
If, however, we use the direct
\ell_{q}
-error analysis of undenoised
\linsketch
(
29
),
together with (
36
) we find

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\leq m^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,.

(37)

This bound is better than (
35
) only for the narrow window

n\succ\frac{m}{(\log m)^{\frac{q}{p}-1}}\,,

so for
n\ll m
in the sense of
n\leq m^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
the bound (
37
) does not play any role.

4.4
Summary on non-adaptive results

We summarize our findings for non-adaptive methods in the following theorem.
Here, for the sake of simplicity,
we focus on the best results results we know for the regime
n\ll m
in the sense that
n\leq m^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
For larger
n
close to
m
, in particular
m=2n
, we find better results in
[
4
, Sec 4]
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
1\leq p<q\leq\infty
,
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
m\geq 2
.
For non-adaptive randomized methods we have the following asymptotic upper bounds where the implicit constant may depend on
p
and
q
:

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\preceq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\min\left\{1,\,\left(\frac{\log m}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\right\}&\text{for $1\leq p\leq 2$,}\\
\displaystyle\min\left\{1,\,\left(\frac{m^{1-2/p}\cdot\log m}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)}\right\}&\text{for $p>2$.}\end{cases}

Conversely, for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we find

n^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(\varepsilon,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\preceq\begin{cases}\varepsilon^{-\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\cdot\log m&\text{for }1\leq p\leq 2,\\
\varepsilon^{-\left.\frac{2}{p}\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\cdot m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot\log m&\text{for }p>2.\end{cases}

Remark 4.5

(Deterministic methods for
q\leq 2
)
.

For
1\leq p<q\leq 2
it is well known that there exist non-linear deterministic (non-adaptive) methods achieving

e^{\textup{det}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\asymp\min\left\{1,\;m^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\cdot\left(\frac{\log\frac{m}{n}}{n}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{q}}\right\},

(38)

which is due to
[
10
,
3
]
(originally formulated in terms of the dual quantity of Kolmogorov numbers).
It was unknown so far if randomized algorithms can achieve better rates.
For
p=1
, the deterministic rate (
38
) is already slightly better than denoised
\countsketch
if
n
is close to
m
, see (
34
).
However, if we restrict to
n\ll m

in the sense of
n\leq m^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
both error rates are of the same order
because
\log m\asymp\log\frac{m}{n}
in that regime.
Significantly, it seems that for
1=p<q\leq 2
randomization does not help as long as we restrict to non-adaptive methods.
For
p>1
, in contrast, it turns out that already non-adaptive randomized algorithms can perform significantly better than deterministic methods if
n\ll m
.

Appendix A
Some results on asymptotic relations

Asymptotic relations that involve logarithms are often quite surprising.
For instance, if we have weakly asymptotically equivalent functions
f(m)\asymp g(m)
with
f(m),g(m)\to\infty
for
m\to\infty
,
then their logarithms are strongly asymptotically equivalent,
\log f(m)\simeq\log g(m)
.
This is what we used in (
13
) to simplify the order of
k^{\ast}(m/D)
.
Asymptotic results with iterated logarithms in particular pose the problem that we need to be aware of the domain on which the logarithm takes positive values,
which is why the definition (
13
) of
k^{\ast}
works for general
m>D

but the weak asymptotic cost bound (
14
) for
\spot
, namely
\log\log\frac{m}{D}
, is stated for
m\geq 16D
.
Later on we want to state error bounds with a factor
\log\log\frac{m}{n}
.
Since
D
is much smaller than
n
, the natural restriction
m\geq 16n
is stronger.
On other occasions, however, after changing the argument of the double logarithm we want to relax the restrictions for the validity of the asymptotic relation to keep statements as simple as possible.
The following abstract result shows how the domain of an asymptotic estimate can be extended under certain circumstances, we subsequently provide an example from this paper.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
h_{1},h_{2}\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
with
h_{1}(n)\geq h_{2}(n)
, let
c_{1},c_{2}>0
, and consider functions

\displaystyle e\colon

\displaystyle{\mathbb{N}}^{2}\to[0,\infty)\,,

\displaystyle f_{1}\colon

\displaystyle\left\{(n,m)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}\mid m\geq h_{1}(n)\right\}\to[0,\infty)\,,

\displaystyle f_{2}\colon

\displaystyle\left\{(n,m)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}\mid m\geq h_{2}(n)\right\}\to(0,\infty)\,.

Assume that
e(n,m)
,
f(n,m)
, and
g(n,m)
are all
monotonically increasing in
m
.
Further assume

b:=\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\frac{f_{2}(n,h_{1}(n))}{f_{2}(n,h_{2}(n))}<\infty\,.

Then the following implication holds:

\displaystyle e(n,m)\preceq f_{1}(n,m)

\displaystyle\preceq f_{2}(n,m)\qquad\text{for }m\geq h_{1}(n)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\qquad e(n,m)

\displaystyle\preceq f_{2}(n,m)\qquad\text{for }m\geq h_{2}(n)\,.

Proof.

The premise states that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

e(n,m)\leq C\cdot f_{1}(n,m)\quad\text{and}\quad f_{1}(n,m)\leq C\cdot f_{2}(n,m)\quad\text{for }m\geq h_{1}(n)\,,

in particular
e(n,m)\leq C^{2}\cdot f_{2}(n,m)
for
m\geq h_{1}(n)
.
Monotonicity in
m
and the definition of
b
imply
that for
h_{1}(n)\geq m\geq h_{2}(n)
we have

e(n,m)\leq e(n,h_{1}(n))\leq C^{2}\cdot f_{2}(n,h_{1}(n))\leq C^{2}\,b\cdot f_{2}(n,h_{2}(n))\leq C^{2}\,b\cdot f_{2}(n,m)\,.

Hence, with
b\geq 1
, we even find

e(n,m)\leq C^{2}\,b\cdot f_{2}(n,m)\qquad\text{for }m\geq h_{2}(n)\,,

which proves the assertion.
∎

We exemplify this result by a particular application from this paper:

Example A.2
.

Consider the cost function

e(L,m):=\card A_{L,R}

from Theorem
3.1
,
with asymptotic bound
f_{1}(L,m):=D^{(L)}\cdot\log\log\frac{m}{D^{(L)}}

for
m\geq h_{1}(L):=16\,D^{(L)}
where
D^{(L)}:=\lceil C_{p}\cdot 2^{L}\rceil
with
C_{p}>1
for
1\leq p\leq 2
.
Further, we know that
f_{1}(L,m)\leq(C_{p}+1)\cdot f_{2}(L,m)
where
f_{2}(L,m):=2^{L}\cdot\log\log\frac{m}{2^{L}}
,
but
f_{2}(L,m)
can be regarded on the domain
m\geq h_{2}(L):=16\cdot 2^{L}
.
Now, with
\log\log 16>1>0
, we find

\frac{f_{2}(L,h_{1}(L))}{f_{2}(L,h_{2}(L))}=\frac{\log\log\frac{16\lceil C_{p}\cdot 2^{L}\rceil}{2^{L}}}{\log\log 16}\leq\frac{\log\log(16(C_{p}+1))}{\log\log 16}<\infty\,.

Thus, by Lemma
A.1
we conclude

e(L,m)\preceq 2^{L}\cdot\log\log\frac{m}{2^{L}}
for
m\geq 16\cdot 2^{L}
.
