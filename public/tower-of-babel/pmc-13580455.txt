Identifying Patient Portal Messages from Care Partners of People with Dementia
Authors: Daniel Martin, Jason A Lyons, John David Powers, Andrea E Daddato, Rebecca S Boxer, Elizabeth A Bayliss, Kelly T Gleason, Hillary D Lum, Jennifer D Portz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient portals provide a centralized platform for accessing health information and communicating with healthcare providers, offering benefits for care partners of people with dementia. However, uptake of shared proxy access remains limited, with many care partners informally using patient credentials. This study aimed to identify portal messages sent from care partners of people with dementia and describe associated portal use.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at Kaiser Permanente Colorado involving N=521 people with dementia ≥60 years. Data extracted from electronic health records included standardized contact information and unstructured portal messages. Names of potential care partners, termed here “known contacts”, were identified through structured fields (emergency contacts, patient contacts, healthcare proxies). A rules-based approach was developed to identify messages from known contacts and validated through manual review. Messages were linked to portal activities performed the same day to describe patterns of use.ResultsAmong the cohort (N=521), 402 (77%) people with dementia had messages sent on their behalf from a known contact, predominantly children and spouses. Known contacts sent a mean of 13.7 messages. Of all threads, 7% were from proxy accounts; 53% of messages did not identify a known contact, and 40% did. 67% of the cohort had portal activity on the same day as a known contact message. Known contacts more often accessed “Visits and appointments” and less often “Medications” after sending messages.ConclusionKnown contacts may use patients’ portal accounts for broader care management beyond messaging. Identifying known contact portal use could ultimately help health systems to recognize care partners, provide timely support, improve portal privacy and accuracy, and facilitate formal proxy registration.Keywords: care partner, patient portal, name-matching algorithm, caregiving, digital health

Introduction

Patient portals provide a centralized platform for accessing health information, facilitating communication with healthcare providers, managing appointments, and obtaining educational resources, which can improve care coordination for people with dementia and their care partners. Shared patient portal access (proxy access), where patients officially authorize an individual to access their account, allows the proxy to view health information and communicate with providers on the patient’s behalf. Proxy portal registration and use are associated with improvements in care partner engagement, satisfaction, and confidence in managing care.1 However, despite the potential benefits of care partner engagement through the portal, uptake of shared proxy access remains limited, with many care partners opting to use patient credentials informally.2

Informal use of patient credentials by care partners is common yet difficult to identify and complicates providers’ ability to recognize patient vs care partner communication.3,4 This study aims to identify portal messages sent from care partners of people with dementia and describe portal use associated with the messages (ie, care partner use while logged in as the patient). Identification of care partners through messages and associated use will inform portal improvements to include and enhance care partner support such as access to caregiving resources, timely assessments, and improved proxy registration.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Kaiser Permanente Colorado (KPCO), involving N=521 people with dementia with behavioral disturbances, as defined in Supplemental Material Table 1, aged 60 or older as of November 1, 2022, with at least one portal message sent from their account between January 1, 2020 and January 1, 2023. Because advanced dementia is often associated with substantial assistance needs and behavioral symptoms,5 we included behavioral disturbance codes to identify patients more likely to need and have a care partner. A summary of data extraction, name-matching, and rules-based approach are summarized in Figure 1. Structured patient contact information and unstructured text from portal messages were sourced from KPCO’s electronic health records (EHR). Using our previously published methods,6 structured care partner data, ie, standardized fields documenting care partner information such as name and phone number, from the patient contacts, advance directive, and patient relationship tables were examined to identify names of emergency contacts, listed family members, healthcare agents, and legal guardians (hereafter termed “known contacts”). Unstructured data extraction involved text fields from portal messages sent from the person with dementia’s account between January 1, 2020, and January 2023. Approval for the research was obtained from the KPCO Institutional Review Board (1956520). Because the study was a secondary data analysis in which informed consent would be unattainable, waivers of informed consent and HIPAA authorization were approved. All study procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Matching Known Contact Names to Names in Portal Messages

We searched the text of portal messages to identify messages containing the name of a known contact. Given potential misspellings or abbreviations, we employed an approximate matching algorithm using the SPEDIS function in SAS v9.04, (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC) which calculates a spelling distance between strings to account for slight variations (Supplemental Material Figure 1). Each word in a text string was compared with known contact names, and the closest match was retained when the SPEDIS score was consistent with common spelling variations, including single-letter transposition or a missing letter. We flagged instances where first names or first-last name pairs matched. To validate this approach, two team members (DM and AD) manually reviewed a random sample of 100 messages to confirm known contact identification.

Rules-Based Approach to Identify Messages Sent by Known Contacts

Extracted messages were parsed using message-break indicators to distinguish individual messages within a thread, including replies. Each message segment was evaluated to distinguish messages sent by known contacts from replies or incidental mentions. Threads were classified as known contact-initiated if any message within the thread was identified as being sent by a known contact. Messages identifying the name of a known contact as the sender had to be distinguished from replies addressed to known contacts and messages containing incidental references to known contacts. These messages were identified using a rules-based approach focusing on the line where the name was found (hereafter “target line”) accounting for keywords or phrases, the length of the target line and the position of the name within that line, the position of the target line within the message, and the content on the lines directly above and below the target line. These rules were iterated based on observations of message patterns and manual review of the results until a final rule set was deemed sufficient for correctly identifying messages from a known contact.

The rules for this identification process accounted for the following scenarios. A message was identified as sent by a known contact if a sign-off word such as “sincerely” was adjacent to or on the previous line from the contact name, or if the target line is: in the last four lines, contains four or fewer words, and is seven or more lines from the start of the message. Mirrored criteria were used to identify messages addressed to known contacts (name found next to a greeting word such as “hello”, target line in the first four lines of a message, etc). Conflicts could occur where a message was flagged as both to and from a known contact, especially for short messages. In those cases, if the name was positioned with four or more words before it without a greeting word, it was identified as from a known contact. If there were four or fewer words with a comma directly after the name, it was identified as sent to the known contact. The phrases “this is [contact name]” or “[contact name] on behalf of” identified the known contact as the sender, taking priority over other rules. All other cases were deemed ambiguous references to known contacts.

A sample of messages (n=333) from a known contact were manually reviewed, and 95% were verified by the reviewer (AD). Messages from known contacts were further flagged based on whether the sender had a healthcare agent or medical power of attorney relationship with the patient. Messages identified as replies to a known caregiver or ambiguous references were not the focus of the analysis. Messages were flagged based on whether they were sent from the patient’s account or a proxy account. Messages were also categorized based on topics selected from a drop-down list provided in the portal: appointments, existing health problem, medical records and forms, medication refill, medication questions and concerns, new health problem, non-urgent medical questions, test result question, other/multiple concerns, and no category selected.

Portal Activity on the Same Day a Message is Sent from a Known Contact

All portal activity excluding log in/out and messaging were pulled for the dementia cohort during the study period, and activity was flagged that occurred on the same day that messages were sent. We categorized whether the activity occurred on the same day as messages sent from: 1) proxy accounts, 2) from the patient account that did not identify a known contact as the sender, and 3) messages sent from the patient account that identified a known contact as the sender. Activity on the same day as a message was sent from a known contact logged in as the patient with dementia was assumed to be from that contact. This activity was then grouped into eight healthcare categories based on our previous work:7,8 visits and appointment scheduling, medical record, medications, health management tools and guides, labs and test results, care team information, cost and coverage, and other.

Results

Cohort

We identified a cohort of N=521 people with dementia who sent at least one portal message, who were on average 84 years of age and mostly female (63%). Among the cohort, 402 (77%) patients had at least one message from a known contact sent within their portal. 41 (8%) patients had a message from a proxy account, and 381 (73%) had a message from a known contact while logged in as the person with dementia. Known Contacts

Based on name-matching with standardized contact fields, a total of N=478 known contacts sent a portal message on behalf of the patient. 58% of these known contacts were the children of the person with dementia and 20% were spouses; 73% were emergency contacts, 37% were healthcare agents, and 12% of the known contacts had registered portal proxy accounts. Each known contact sent a mean of 13.7 (SD=21.8) messages during the 36-month study period.

Message Threads

There were 12,925 total message threads (Mean=24.8, SD=32) sent by patient or proxies accounts during the study period: 7% of messages were sent from a proxy account, 40% were sent from the patient’s account and identified a known contact as the sender, and 53% were sent from the patient’s account but did not identify a known contact as the sender. The most common message topic used by the message author was “Existing Health Problems”. A summary of messages is provided in Table 1.

Portal Activity

Regarding portal activity, 10% of the cohort had additional activity beyond messages from proxy accounts, and 67% had additional portal activity on the same day that a known contact sent a message while logged in as the patient. The cohort had a mean of 95.5 (SD=100.4) unique days with activity per person with dementia, including a mean of 5.9 (SD=10.7) days on which a known contact sent a message while logged in as the patient. Portal features accessed on days when known contacts sent messages are shown in Table 2. Compared with days without messages from known contacts, the overall distribution of portal activity types differed significantly (Chi2 = 91.4, p<0.001). On days when known contacts sent messages, portal use included more visits and appointment scheduling (89% vs 83%) and included fewer medication views (27% vs 34%).

Discussion

Care partners play a significant role in dementia care yet are largely unrecognized and undocumented in the EHR.9,10 Despite EHRs’ power to identify patients through standardized data fields, a clinical gap in identifying care partners through the EHR persists due to the absence of structured care partner fields.11,12 In this preliminary study, we combined name-matching with a rules-based algorithm to identify portal messages from individuals listed in the patient’s EHR, who may provide care, and characterize their corresponding portal use. Known contacts appear to use the portal for multiple care-management activities while logged in as the patient.

In response to the Caregiver Advise, Record, Enable (CARE) Act,13 hospitals are required to provide patients the opportunity to self-identify a care partner. Efforts are in place across the US to standardize care partner EHR fields, yet these fields are not widely available14 nor are care partner requests and documentation common practice.15 Although the CARE Act requirements may support care partner identification, the policy is limited to hospital care and not necessarily available in ambulatory settings. These gaps highlight the need for approaches that can identify care partners through their interactions with the patient portal, including those who are not formally registered as proxies.

Previous work has successfully deployed rules-based models and NLP to determine if portal messages are sent by a patient or a non-registered proxy.16,17 Our study expands on this work by linking messages sent by non-registered proxies to specific known contacts listed in the EHR, and connecting known contacts to caregiving activities, ie, sending messages to providers, accessing health tools, and exploring resources, on behalf of the person with dementia. Future research should include further investigation of message text to characterize caregiving roles and responsibilities (transportation, activities of daily living support, decision-making, etc) of the known contacts listed in the EHR.

This study corroborates previous work that care partners are frequent users of the portal for dementia care, yet currently do so mostly through patient credentials, so their identification is not formally captured.2,8,18,19 This is evident by the number of messages sent from known contacts and additional portal activity while logged in using patient credentials. Only 8% of the cohort had a care partner with a proxy account, highlighting the underutilization of proxy accounts and the need for improved access to proxy registration. Proxy assignment processes are typically arduous, suggesting a need for improved proxy access education initiatives, streamlined registration procedures, and mechanisms for user identification upon logging in.2

There are emerging efforts to design portal interventions specifically for care partners.20–22 Known contacts in this study who sent messages were most interested in using the scheduling and visit tools. However, accessing health management tools and guides were also popular, indicating a potential need for these types of resources. The portal may be an excellent tactic for delivering high-quality, validated resources directly to known contacts, particularly if users are offered a nudge for possible caregiving support during their log-in. Overall, the portal remains an underutilized tool to support people with dementia and their care partners. Identification of known contacts through messages is an opportunity to better include and enhance interventions for individuals who may be helping with care, including access to caregiving resources, timely assessments, and technical support for proxy registration.

Several limitations related to the study population, portal activity, and validation of our approach should be considered when interpreting these findings. The study sample was limited to a majority white and non-Hispanic cohort in a single integrated health system. KPCO offers extensive portal access, while small practices and low-resource health systems may have less capacity to offer EHR portal messaging. We purposefully selected people with dementia with behavioral disturbances who we considered most likely to need and have a caregiver of some sort. However, this limits our findings to individuals with more advanced dementia and may not generalize to those in earlier stages of cognitive impairment or various types of dementia. Because our objective was to examine the relationship between messages from known contacts and corresponding portal activity, our analytic cohort was also restricted to people with dementia who had portal messages. Therefore, our findings do not represent overall portal use among people with dementia. Portal activity by non-proxy users or contacts who could not be identified using our approach was also not captured. Although we observed differences in portal activity types between days with and without known-contact messages, these individual activity types were not tested separately and should therefore be interpreted descriptively. The study period included the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have influenced patient and known contact use of the patient portal message system and tools.

Our approach was developed and evaluated as a preliminary step to ultimately identify care partners in the EHR that are also using the patient portal, yet it has not undergone comprehensive validation. Although manual review provided initial evidence supporting the name-matching and known-contact identification approaches, the evaluation was limited to relatively small samples. In addition, there is currently no established gold standard for identifying care partners in the EHR.11,23 While we were able to link known contacts to messages and corresponding portal activity, we cannot confirm if the names identified have a caregiving role in the patient’s life or consider themselves care partners. Our immediate next steps are to further validate this approach by establishing a gold standard for care partner identification, examining message content to better characterize care partner roles and responsibilities, and validating identified known contacts against self-reported caregiving roles and clinical assessments of caregiving involvement.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study integrated structured and unstructured EHR data to identify and characterize patient portal messages and corresponding portal use involving known contacts who may be providing care for people with dementia. Known contact involvement in the patient portal extended beyond messaging, with additional portal activity occurring on days when known contacts sent messages while logged in as the patient. These findings suggest that known contacts may use patient portal accounts to participate in multiple aspects of care management. Recognizing this broader pattern of non-registered proxy use may help health systems identify care partners while they are actively engaged in care management. This could create opportunities to provide timely support, improve portal privacy and accuracy, and facilitate formal proxy registration.
