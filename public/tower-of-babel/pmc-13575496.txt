The Relationship Between Preoperative COVID‐19 Fear and Anxiety Level Undergoing Elective Surgery Patients: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Tansu Ayik, Yasemin Eda Tekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study was conducted to investigate the relationship between patients' preoperative Coronavirus Disease 2019 fear level and anxiety level.DesignThe study was conducted using a cross‐sectional design.MethodsThe study was carried out with 479 patients who underwent elective surgery at a university hospital between January and June 2021. The ‘Sociodemographic and Medical Characteristics Information Form’, ‘Coronavirus Disease 2019 Fear Scale’ and ‘State/Trait Anxiety Scale’ were used. Descriptive methods, independent samples t‐test, ANOVA and Pearson correlation analysis were used in data analysis.ResultsThe mean fear scale score was 14.27 ± 5.46, the mean State Anxiety Scale score was 31.05 ± 7.92, and the mean Trait Anxiety Scale score was 38.69 ± 8.81. Coronavirus Disease 2019 fear was found to be higher in single individuals, those with poor sleep and those without caregiving responsibilities. State anxiety was found to be lower in those who believed they had sufficient information about the surgery. A very weak positive correlation was found between fear and trait anxiety. A strong positive correlation was found between patients' state anxiety and trait anxiety.ConclusionPreoperative anxiety during the COVID‐19 pandemic appeared to be more closely associated with patients' underlying anxiety predisposition than with COVID‐19‐related fear. Structured preoperative education may contribute to reducing situational anxiety.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareRoutine assessment of both state and trait anxiety, together with individualized preoperative education and psychosocial support, may improve patients' psychological preparedness for surgery. Particular attention should be given to patients with elevated trait anxiety, as they may benefit from targeted nursing interventions aimed at reducing preoperative distress and enhancing perioperative care.Reporting MethodThis study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement for reporting cross‐sectional studies.Patient or Public ContributionThe findings of this study highlight the importance of routinely assessing psychosocial factors, such as anxiety, sleep quality and social support, during preoperative care. Structured patient education and individualized psychosocial support provided by nurses may reduce patients' perceived uncertainty, improve psychological adaptation to surgery and enhance perioperative outcomes.

Introduction

Surgical procedures are invasive interventions that disrupt tissue integrity and elicit physiological stress responses, carrying risks including bleeding, pain, morbidity and mortality. Globally, more than 300 million surgical procedures are performed annually (Raslan et al. 2021; World Health Organization 2023). Despite substantial progress in surgical techniques, anaesthesia and perioperative safety protocols, preoperative anxiety persists as a prevalent clinical issue among surgical patients worldwide (Vermişli et al. 2024).

Preoperative anxiety is a common psychological response among patients awaiting surgery, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 11% to 80% and typically falling between moderate and high levels (Ni et al. 2023; Vermişli et al. 2024; Balkaya et al. 2021; Ongün et al. 2022; Dayılar et al. 2017; Buonanno et al. 2022; Prakash et al. 2024). Anxiety represents both a psychological and physiological reaction to perceived internal or external threats and serves as an adaptive mechanism for coping with danger (Vermişli et al. 2024). However, excessive or prolonged anxiety can adversely affect surgical outcomes. High anxiety levels are associated with increased postoperative morbidity, higher complication rates, extended recovery periods and greater healthcare costs (Arunalatha et al. 2023; Bakker‐Bons et al. 2025; Raslan et al. 2021).

The preoperative period represents a critical phase that elicits substantial emotional, cognitive and physiological stress responses in patients. Activation of the autonomic nervous system leads to increased catecholamine and cortisol release, which is linked to haemodynamic instability, elevated anaesthetic requirements and a higher risk of postoperative complications (Buonanno et al. 2022; Prakash et al. 2024). This neuroendocrine response may also delay wound healing and increase infection risk by inducing metabolic irregularities and immunosuppression. Consequently, these physiological alterations can directly impact postoperative recovery (Buonanno et al. 2022; Cici et al. 2024; Maheshwari et al. 2025). Anxiety during the perioperative period can further intensify the stress response, thereby negatively influencing surgical outcomes. Understanding factors associated with preoperative anxiety may help healthcare professionals, particularly nurses involved in perioperative care, identify patients requiring additional education and psychosocial support, thereby facilitating individualized perioperative care (Association of periOperative Registered Nurses [AORN] 2024; Balkaya et al. 2021; Baş and Ayhan 2024; Vermişli et al. 2024).

The COVID‐19 pandemic has introduced an additional psychological burden for surgical patients by heightening infection risk and uncertainty about healthcare safety. Hospitals are perceived as high‐risk settings due to required interactions with medical personnel, other patients and visitors, as well as the need for postoperative monitoring, treatment and mobilization. During infectious disease outbreaks, patients experience not only the inherent stress of surgery but also increased fear of infection, compounding psychological distress (Al‐Masri et al. 2025; Prakash et al. 2024).

Studies have documented increased clinical deterioration and higher mortality rates in surgeries conducted during the pandemic (Lei et al. 2020; Lim et al. 2022). The continual threat of COVID‐19 and emerging variants has become a major environmental stressor, adversely affecting patients' psychological well‐being during the perioperative period (Raslan et al. 2021; Vermişli et al. 2024; Balkaya et al. 2021; Çetinkaya and Aslan 2023). As a result, elective surgery patients during the pandemic faced a psychological dilemma: postponing treatment could worsen their prognosis, yet proceeding with surgery exposed them to infection risk within the hospital. This conflict likely contributed to increased preoperative anxiety by intensifying the tension between the necessity of surgery and fear of infection.

Existing literature includes studies that assess preoperative anxiety during the pandemic (Buonanno et al. 2022; Yıldız et al. 2024; Viola et al. 2021; Doglietto et al. 2020; Balkaya et al. 2021; Upreti et al. 2024) and those that examine preoperative COVID‐19‐related fear (Arıkan and Erden 2025). Although some research has considered both variables, most have relied primarily on the preoperative anxiety scale (Çetinkaya and Aslan 2023; Aloweidi et al. 2022). Only a few studies (Cici et al. 2024) have simultaneously evaluated the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) and COVID‐19 fear. Employing the STAI, which differentiates between state and trait anxiety, provides a considerable advantage for understanding the psychological responses associated with environmental stressors such as the COVID‐19 pandemic. Accordingly, the conceptual framework of this study is based on Spielberger's State–Trait Anxiety Theory, which distinguishes between state anxiety as a transient emotional response to situational stressors and trait anxiety as a relatively stable predisposition to perceive situations as threatening (Spielberger 1989). Within this framework, pandemic‐related fear represents a situation‐specific stressor that may influence patients' psychological responses during the preoperative period. However, the relative contribution of COVID‐19‐related fear and underlying trait anxiety to patients' preoperative anxiety remains unclear. Therefore, examining these constructs together may contribute to a better understanding of the psychological factors associated with preoperative anxiety during the pandemic.

Perioperative nurses play an important role in assessing patients' psychological status, identifying anxiety and concerns, addressing informational needs and planning appropriate support, in addition to ensuring patients' physical preparation during the preoperative period (AORN 2024; Balkaya et al. 2021; Baş and Ayhan 2024). Anxiety experienced during the preoperative period may not solely represent a transient response to surgery or external stressors such as COVID‐19; an individual's more persistent predisposition to anxiety may also shape their psychological response during this period. Therefore, assessing situation‐specific fear alongside underlying anxiety predisposition may help perioperative nurses identify patients who require greater psychological support at an early stage, tailor education and support to individual needs and initiate appropriate referrals when necessary. In this respect, psychological assessment is an important component of holistic and individualized perioperative nursing care.

Although studies have investigated preoperative anxiety and COVID‐19‐related fear during the pandemic, relatively few have examined COVID‐19 fear together with both state and trait anxiety. Furthermore, it remains unclear how distinguishing the associations of a situation‐specific stressor such as COVID‐19 fear from those of an individual's more persistent anxiety predisposition with preoperative psychological status may contribute to perioperative nursing assessment and clinical decision‐making. Addressing this knowledge gap is important for identifying patients who may require greater psychological support during the preoperative period and for planning nursing care according to individual psychological needs.

This study aimed to examine the relationship between COVID‐19‐related fear and state and trait anxiety levels in patients undergoing elective surgery during the preoperative period. The findings may contribute to clinical practice by supporting more comprehensive assessment of patients' psychological needs by perioperative nurses, identification of patients who may require additional psychological support and planning of individualized preoperative care. From a nursing education perspective, the concurrent assessment of state and trait anxiety may enhance perioperative nursing knowledge regarding the recognition of psychological risk, consideration of individual anxiety predisposition and the importance of patient‐centred psychological care. Furthermore, this study may provide a basis for future prospective and intervention studies evaluating the effectiveness of preoperative psychological assessment, individualized patient education and nurse‐led psychosocial support approaches.

Research Questions

1. What are the levels of state anxiety, trait anxiety and COVID‐19–related fear among patients in the preoperative period during the COVID‐19 pandemic?

2. Is there a correlation between preoperative anxiety levels (state and trait anxiety) and fear of COVID‐19 among surgical patients?

3. Is there a difference in state anxiety, trait anxiety and COVID‐19–related fear according to patients' sociodemographic and health‐related characteristics?

The Study

Design

A descriptive and correlational design was employed to investigate the relationship between patients' preoperative COVID‐19 fear and anxiety levels.

Instrument With Validity and Reliability

Data collection instruments included the Sociodemographic and Medical Characteristics Form, the COVID‐19 Fear Scale and the State–Trait Anxiety Scale.

Sociodemographic and Medical Characteristics Form

The Sociodemographic and Medical Characteristics Information Form was developed by the researchers following a literature review. It comprised 11 items addressing participants' age, gender, marital status, education level, income status, chronic diseases, history of COVID‐19, previous surgery status, knowledge about surgery, sleep status the night before and caregiving responsibilities (Bayrak et al. 2019; Ahorsu et al. 2022).

COVID‐19 Fear Scale (FCV‐19S)

The COVID‐19 Fear Scale is a one‐dimensional instrument developed by Ahorsu et al. (2022) to assess fear related to COVID‐19 (Ahorsu et al. 2022). Bakioğlu et al. (2021) validated the scale for use in Turkey. The scale consists of 7 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 7 to 35. Higher scores indicate greater fear of COVID‐19. Bakioğlu et al. (2021) reported a Cronbach's alpha of 0.82 for the scale, while the present study found a value of 0.81.

State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI)

The State–Trait Anxiety Inventory was developed by Spielberger and colleagues, with its validity and reliability in Turkish established by Öner and Le Compte in 1985 (Spielberger 1989; Öner and Le Compte 1985). The inventory comprises 40 items: 20 assess state anxiety and 20 assess trait anxiety. The State Anxiety Scale measures acute changes in emotional responses, whereas the Trait Anxiety Scale evaluates the general tendency to experience anxiety. Each item is rated on a 4‐point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 20 to 80. Higher scores reflect greater anxiety. Scores between 20 and 39 indicate mild anxiety, 40 to 59 moderate anxiety, 60 to 79 high anxiety and above 80 panic anxiety (Cici et al. 2024; Öner and Le Compte 1998). Öner and Le Compte (1998) reported Cronbach's alpha values of 0.83 and 0.92. In the present study, Cronbach's alpha values for the scale items ranged from 0.75 to 0.72.

Sampling and Recruitment

The study was conducted between January and June 2021 in the General Surgery and Cardiovascular Surgery departments of a university hospital. A non‐probability convenience sampling method was used, and eligible patients were recruited consecutively throughout the data collection period.

Sample Size and Power

During the specified study period, 1208 patients underwent surgery and constituted the target population. Based on a 99% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 428 patients. To compensate for potential attrition and incomplete questionnaires, the calculated sample size was increased by 15%, resulting in a target sample of 492 participants. Following eligibility assessment, 532 patients met the inclusion criteria and were invited to participate. Of these, 40 patients declined participation, and 13 were excluded because they returned incomplete questionnaires. Consequently, the final sample consisted of 479 patients who met the inclusion criteria and were included in the statistical analysis (von Elm et al. 2008) (Figure 1). Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 18 years or older, were able to communicate, had an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification of I, II or III, were scheduled for elective surgery in the General Surgery or Cardiovascular Surgery departments, were in the preoperative period (the evening before surgery) and provided written informed consent to participate. Patients were excluded if they underwent emergency surgery, had an ASA physical status classification of IV or higher, were scheduled for surgery in departments other than General Surgery or Cardiovascular Surgery, had diagnosed psychological and/or psychiatric disorders or were unable to communicate.

Data Collection

Data collection was conducted between January and June 2021 during the COVID‐19 pandemic, when nationwide restrictions and lockdown measures were in effect. To minimize the risk of infection for both participants and researchers, all interviews were conducted in accordance with infection prevention protocols. Researchers wore face masks throughout the interviews, physical distancing was maintained whenever possible, and the interview rooms were ventilated after each session.

Eligible patients were identified in the General Surgery and Cardiovascular Surgery departments and were approached individually by the researchers on the evening before their scheduled surgery. Patients who met the inclusion criteria were informed verbally and in writing about the purpose and procedures of the study and were invited to participate on a voluntary basis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. The questionnaires were completed on the evening before surgery, and each interview lasted approximately 20 min.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) version 25.0. Descriptive statistics included frequencies, percentages, means and standard deviations. Prior to inferential analyses, the normality of continuous variables was assessed using skewness and kurtosis statistics. Variables with skewness and kurtosis values between −1.5 and +1.5 were considered to have an approximately normal distribution, supporting the use of parametric statistical tests (George and Mallery 2010). Accordingly, the Independent Samples t‐test was used to compare two independent groups (Table 3), one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among more than two groups (Table 3), and Pearson's correlation analysis was performed to examine relationships between continuous variables (Table 4). The internal consistency of the study scales was assessed using Cronbach's alpha coefficients. To assess not only the statistical significance but also the practical importance of differences between groups, effect size analyses were conducted (Table 3). Cohen's d was used for comparisons between two independent groups (independent samples t‐test), while eta‐squared (η 2) was employed for comparisons involving three or more groups (one‐way ANOVA). Cohen's (1988) criteria were applied to interpret Cohen's d values: d < 0.20 was considered negligible, 0.20 ≤ d < 0.50 small, 0.50 ≤ d < 0.80 medium and d ≥ 0.80 a large effect. For η 2, values of η 2 < 0.01 were regarded as negligible, 0.01 ≤ η 2 < 0.06 as small, 0.06 ≤ η 2 < 0.14 as medium and η 2 ≥ 0.14 as large (Cohen 1988).

Missing data were assessed before statistical analysis. Thirteen questionnaires containing incomplete responses were excluded from the study before analysis. Therefore, only participants with complete datasets were included in the final analyses (complete‐case analysis/listwise deletion). No data imputation procedures were performed because the amount of missing data was small and resulted solely from incomplete questionnaires rather than missing responses within otherwise eligible datasets. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The mean age of the patients was 59.1 ± 15.7 years. Of the sample, 39% were female, 79.5% were married, and 39% had a university degree or higher. Additionally, 62% had a chronic condition, 10% had a history of COVID‐19 infection, 52.2% had undergone previous surgery, 17.1% reported poor sleep quality on the night before surgery, and 30.7% had caregiving responsibilities (Table 1). Overall, 68% of patients reported experiencing preoperative anxiety. The most commonly reported causes of anxiety were fear of death (32.7%), fear of pain (13.5%), concern about repeat surgery (10.0%), fear of developing complications (8.3%) and fear of infection (3.5%). Regarding surgical procedures, 31% of the patients underwent cardiac surgery and 23.6% underwent oncological surgery.

Discussion

The present study has revealed that participants' fear of COVID‐19 is at a moderate level. This suggests that patients do not perceive the pandemic as entirely low‐risk but that their psychological coping capacities remain relatively unaffected. In contrast, previous studies involving patients who had undergone various surgical procedures reported higher levels of fear of COVID‐19 (Soydan et al. 2023; Arıkan and Erden 2025; Raslan et al. 2021; Baş and Ayhan 2024), while some studies show similar findings (Haq et al. 2022; Ulubay et al. 2025; Cici et al. 2024; Tanrıverdi and Söylemez 2024). This difference may be due to sample characteristics, the type of surgical procedure, the course of the pandemic during the data collection period, infection control measures implemented in healthcare settings and differences in patients' experiences of COVID‐19. Furthermore, the fact that the majority of participants in the present study were patients undergoing major surgical procedures may have contributed to their primary concerns being directed more towards the success of the surgical procedure, the anaesthesia process and post‐operative recovery, rather than a COVID‐19 infection. These findings highlight the importance of providing clear and reassuring information about infection control practices in pre‐operative nursing care, as well as systematically integrating the assessment of patients' individual concerns and psychosocial support into the pre‐operative care process.

This study found that single individuals had higher levels of fear of COVID‐19 than married individuals. This may reflect the uncertainties experienced, particularly regarding having a close relative available to provide post‐operative care and the perceived lack of social support due to pandemic restrictions. Indeed, studies conducted during the pandemic indicate that a perceived lack of social support is associated with increased levels of psychological stress, anxiety and fear (Labrague and De Los Santos 2021; Alnazly et al. 2021). However, while Ulubay et al. (2025) reported higher levels of fear of COVID‐19 among married individuals, some studies found no significant difference based on marital status (Çetinkaya and Aslan 2023; Soydan et al. 2023; Maheshwari et al. 2025; Ertan and Gürçayır 2025). These discrepancies between studies may stem from differences in the socio‐demographic characteristics of the sample, variations in caregiving responsibilities across countries, data collection during different phases of the pandemic and differences in infection control measures implemented in healthcare settings.

In the present study, it was found that patients reporting poor sleep quality had significantly higher levels of COVID‐19 related fear. This finding is consistent with other studies conducted in different populations (Siddique et al. 2021; Duong 2021). In support of this finding, it has been reported that fear of COVID‐19 activates the physiological stress response, thereby increasing sympathetic nervous system activity; this, in turn, may contribute to difficulties in falling asleep and maintaining sleep via hyperarousal and rumination (Siddique et al. 2021; Duong 2021; Jahrami et al. 2021). In elective surgery patients, this cycle may become more apparent due to the influence of the existing surgical stress and may adversely affect preoperative psychological preparation. These findings highlight the importance of nurses routinely assessing patients' sleep patterns during the preoperative assessment process and identifying factors that impair sleep quality at an early stage.

This study revealed that individuals with caregiving responsibilities reported lower levels of COVID‐19‐related fear. According to the caregiving appraisal model, caregiving responsibilities can influence how individuals cognitively evaluate stressors, thereby shaping their perception of threat and coping process (Wang et al. 2025). Similarly, the literature reports that higher psychological resilience among carers is associated with more effective coping with stress and uncertainty (Wu et al. 2025; Hou and Chen 2024; Herrero and Díaz 2025).

The moderate level of state anxiety among patients suggests that the surgical procedure is not perceived as entirely threatening. State anxiety is defined as a temporary emotional response to a specific situation and is known to be closely related to the perception of uncertainty, loss of control and threat assessment during the preoperative period (Buonanno et al. 2022; Cici et al. 2024; Maheshwari et al. 2025; Öner and Le Compte 1985). In this context, moderate levels of anxiety may represent an adaptive response that facilitates cognitive preparation for surgery; however, it may also be regarded as a clinical risk indicator carrying the risk of increasing when adequate psychosocial support is not provided. The findings of the present study are consistent with research conducted during the preoperative period in the pandemic (Buonanno et al. 2022; Yıldız et al. 2024; Doglietto et al. 2020; Balkaya et al. 2021). Given that preoperative anxiety is a temporary emotional response specific to the surgical context, the consistent reporting of moderate levels of anxiety in studies using different measurement tools to assess preoperative anxiety during the pandemic supports this conclusion (Çetinkaya and Aslan 2023; Aloweidi et al. 2022; Vermişli et al. 2024).

The findings indicate that participants' levels of state anxiety were also moderate. As trait anxiety reflects an individual's relatively persistent tendency to experience anxiety in response to stressful life events, the findings suggest that the psychological responses observed during the surgical process are not limited to the situational stress caused by the surgical procedure only (Cici et al. 2024; Öner and Le Compte 1998). Similarly, studies conducted during the pandemic have also reported anxiety profiles ranging from low to moderate levels, and it has been noted that these levels may reflect the process of adapting to pandemic conditions rather than a pathological condition (Buonanno et al. 2022; Yıldız et al. 2024; Vermişli et al. 2024; Balkaya et al. 2021). In comparison, studies conducted prior to the pandemic reported higher levels of anxiety; this discrepancy may stem from differences within the surgical population, data collection conditions and changes in healthcare delivery (Mulugeta et al. 2018; Dayılar et al. 2017; Bayrak et al. 2019). These findings support the systematic assessment of not only state anxiety but also an individual's trait anxiety during preoperative nursing assessment. The early identification of patients with high trait anxiety may contribute to the planning of individualized psychoeducation and psychosocial support interventions.

In this study, the fact that patients who reported having sufficient information about their surgery exhibited lower levels of state anxiety suggests that pre‐operative information plays an important role in patients' psychological preparation. Providing sufficient information regarding the surgical process may have reduced patients' perception of uncertainty, thereby making the surgical process more predictable and strengthening their sense of control. This finding can be considered one of the key factors explaining the moderate level of state anxiety observed during the preoperative period. Findings reported by Idris (2023) and Gürler et al. (2022) support these results. Consistent with these findings, studies conducted across various surgical specialties show that preoperative anxiety is influenced by the level of uncertainty patients perceive regarding their health status and the care environment (Vermişli et al. 2024; Balkaya et al. 2021; Ongün et al. 2022; Doglietto et al. 2020).

This study found a statistically significant, but very weak, positive association between fear of COVID‐19 and trait anxiety. This finding suggests that as individuals' levels of trait anxiety increase, their fear of COVID‐19 may also increase to a limited extent. The fact that the data for this study were collected in 2021, during the period of full lockdown in the COVID‐19 pandemic, a time when uncertainty about the disease, the risk of infection and social restrictions were particularly acute, may partly explain the association found between fear of COVID‐19 and trait anxiety. However, the limited association between fear of COVID‐19 and trait anxiety also suggests that trait anxiety is influenced more by relatively stable individual characteristics, such as personality traits, past life experiences, stress coping skills and psychological resilience. Our findings are consistent with the study by Doğan and Yıldırım (2022), which utilized the same measurement tools. In that study, a very weak but positive and statistically significant association was again reported between fear of COVID‐19 and persistent anxiety among parents whose children had been hospitalized with suspected COVID‐19. Furthermore, numerous studies conducted among the general population during the pandemic have reported positive correlations between fear of COVID‐19 and general anxiety levels (Ahorsu et al. 2022; Erbiçer et al. 2021; Garcia 2022; Huarcaya‐Victoria et al. 2020; Nikopoulou et al. 2020; Şimşir et al. 2021). Unlike state anxiety, trait anxiety is defined as a relatively stable personality trait that reflects an individual's general predisposition to anxiety rather than temporary environmental stressors (Öner and Le Compte 1998). Consequently, the fact that a situation‐specific stressor such as COVID‐19 does not show a strong association with state anxiety is, in theory, to be expected. This finding indicates that preoperative psychological assessment should take into account not only fear of COVID‐19 but also the individual's general tendency towards anxiety and other psychosocial risk factors.

Our study found no significant association between fear of COVID‐19 and situational state anxiety. Cici et al. (2024) also reached a similar result in their study. In contrast, some studies have reported a positive correlation between the perceived threat of the pandemic or COVID‐19‐related anxiety and preoperative anxiety (Balkaya et al. 2021; Çetinkaya and Aslan 2023; Doglietto et al. 2020). The literature indicates that state anxiety during the pre‐operative period is influenced not only by fear of COVID‐19 but also by numerous situational factors, such as the surgical procedure itself, concerns regarding anaesthesia, the outcome of the operation, expectations of pain and worries about potential complications (Eberhart et al. 2020; Çelik and Edipoğlu 2018; Mulugeta et al. 2018). This finding is further supported by the other correlation findings obtained in our study.

Our study found a strong correlation between state and trait anxiety. This finding suggests that fears specific to the pandemic are insufficient on their own to explain the pre‐operative psychological state, whilst individual psychological characteristics play a more defining role. In other words, although the COVID‐19 pandemic constitutes a significant cause of environmental stress, the state anxiety experienced prior to surgery is shaped more by the individual's prior psychological characteristics. The fact that individuals with high levels of state anxiety exhibit higher levels of state anxiety in stressful situations such as surgical procedures is also consistent with Spielberger's state–trait anxiety theory (Spielberger 2010). Consequently, in a highly stressful process such as surgery, one of the key factors determining an individual's state anxiety level may be this underlying predisposition to anxiety. Our findings are consistent with previous studies reporting a strong association between state and trait anxiety in preoperative patients (Vermişli et al. 2024; Kil et al. 2012). When these findings are evaluated together, the fact that fear of COVID‐19 is associated with state anxiety only to a very weak level and shows no association with trait anxiety, whereas a strong correlation is found between state and trait anxiety, suggests that the preoperative psychological response is primarily influenced by the individual's prior anxiety predisposition. These findings highlight the importance of routinely assessing not only situational fears but also the individual's general anxiety predisposition during preoperative psychological assessment and planning individualized psychological support interventions for high‐risk patients.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, data were collected from only two surgical departments of a single university hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings and patient populations. Second, a non‐probability convenience sampling method was used. As participation was voluntary, selection bias may have occurred because patients who declined participation or those with poorer clinical conditions, communication difficulties or higher levels of psychological distress may have been underrepresented. Consequently, the study sample may not fully reflect the characteristics of the broader population of elective surgical patients. Third, the cross‐sectional design precludes any conclusions regarding causal relationships between COVID‐19 fear and anxiety levels. Finally, psychological variables were assessed using self‐report questionnaires, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias, and the internal consistency coefficient of the State/Trait Anxiety Scale in the present study was relatively low. Despite these limitations, the study has several important strengths. It included a relatively large sample that exceeded the minimum required sample size, providing adequate statistical power. Data were collected during the COVID‐19 pandemic under real clinical conditions, allowing the assessment of patients' psychological responses within a unique healthcare context. Furthermore, the simultaneous evaluation of COVID‐19 fear together with both state and trait anxiety provides a more comprehensive understanding of preoperative psychological status than studies assessing only a single dimension of anxiety. Future studies should employ probability‐based sampling methods, recruit participants from multiple healthcare institutions and different surgical specialties and use longitudinal designs to better clarify the temporal and causal relationships between pandemic‐related fear and preoperative anxiety.

Implications for Nursing Practice

The findings of this study highlight the importance of considering not only fears related to surgery or situation‐specific stressors such as COVID‐19, but also patients' general anxiety predisposition during preoperative nursing assessment. The absence of a significant association between COVID‐19 fear and state anxiety, together with the strong association between state and trait anxiety, suggests that psychological assessment should not be limited to situational fears. Perioperative nurses can assess anxiety and psychosocial needs to identify patients who may require additional support and, when necessary, provide individualized support or appropriate referral.

The lower state anxiety observed among patients who reported having sufficient information about the surgical process highlights the importance of assessing patients' informational needs. In addition to providing standard information, perioperative nurses can assess whether patients perceive the information as sufficient, identify remaining uncertainties and determine the need for additional information. Tailoring education to individual needs may contribute to strengthening patient‐centred preoperative care.

For implementation in practice, structured assessment of psychological status and informational needs could be integrated into existing preoperative nursing processes. Institutional preoperative assessment forms and care protocols could incorporate assessment of anxiety, psychosocial support needs and perceived information adequacy, thereby supporting systematic identification of patients requiring additional support and clinical decision‐making. However, the effectiveness of these approaches should be evaluated in future prospective and intervention studies.

Recommendations for Further Research

Future longitudinal studies are needed to examine the long‐term relationship between trait anxiety, state anxiety and psychological outcomes in surgical patients. In addition, intervention studies evaluating the effectiveness of structured preoperative education and nurse‐led psychosocial support programs in reducing anxiety among patients with high trait anxiety are warranted. Research involving multicenter and more diverse surgical populations would also improve the generalizability of these findings.

Conclusion

This study suggests that preoperative anxiety in elective surgery patients during the COVID‐19 pandemic appears to be more strongly associated with individual anxiety predisposition than with pandemic‐related fear. Although COVID‐19 fear was not associated with state anxiety and demonstrated only a very weak positive association with trait anxiety, trait and state anxiety were strongly correlated, indicating that patients' underlying anxiety predisposition plays a more important role in shaping preoperative psychological responses than pandemic‐related fear. The association between sufficient surgical information and lower state anxiety further highlights the value of structured preoperative education in reducing uncertainty and improving psychological preparedness. Patients with elevated trait anxiety may particularly benefit from targeted psychosocial support interventions, especially during periods of heightened environmental stress such as a pandemic. These findings suggest that perioperative nurses should routinely assess patients' anxiety predisposition during the preoperative period, identify individuals with elevated trait anxiety, provide structured preoperative education to reduce uncertainty and implement individualized psychosocial support when needed. Incorporating these evidence‐based interventions into routine preoperative nursing care may improve patients' psychological preparedness for surgery.
