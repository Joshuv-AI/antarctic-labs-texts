Unification of Deterministic Higher-Order Patterns
Johannes Niederhauser (🖂), Aart Middeldorp

Abstract
We present a sound and complete unification procedure for deterministic higher-order patterns, a class of simply-typed lambda terms introduced by Yokoyama et al. which comes with a deterministic matching problem. Our unification procedure can be seen as a special case of full higher-order unification where flex-flex pairs can be solved in a most general way. Moreover, our method generalizes Libal and Miller’s recent functions-as-constructors higher-order unification (FCU) by dropping their global restriction on variable arguments, thereby losing the property that every solvable problem has a most general unifier. In fact, minimal complete sets of unifiers of deterministic higher-order patterns may be infinite, so decidability of the unification problem remains an open question. Nevertheless, our method can be more useful than FCU in practice.



1
Introduction

Given two simply-typed
\lambda
-terms
s
and
t
, higher-order
unification tries to answer the question whether they can be made
equivalent up to
\beta\eta
-conversion by applying some substitution

\theta
to both of them, i.e.,
s\theta\leftrightarrow_{\beta\eta}^{*}t\theta
.
The first practical higher-order unification procedure has been
introduced by Huet in 1975
[
8
]
. Since then, higher-order
unification has evolved into an extensively studied topic which plays
a central role in research areas such as higher-order automated
theorem proving
[
1
,
16
,
3
]
, interactive theorem proving

[
12
]
, higher-order logic programming
[
11
]
and
higher-order rewriting
[
10
]
. In contrast to first-order
unification, which is decidable and unitary (most general unifiers
exit), higher-order unification is undecidable and infinitary
(infinitely many incomparable unifiers may exist)
[
5
]
. Since
neither of these properties makes higher-order unification a
well-behaved subroutine for tasks such as theorem proving, more
well-behaved subclasses of higher-order unification have been
identified and investigated in the past. One important subclass is
higher-order pattern unification due to Miller
[
11
]
where the
arguments of
free variables are restricted to
\eta
-equivalent representations of
distinct bound variables. Even though this rules out many simply-typed

\lambda
-terms, the unification procedure is sufficient for many
practical purposes
[
10
,
11
]
while being both decidable and
unitary. Furthermore, higher-order pattern unification has linear
time complexity
[
14
]
.

Example 1
(taken from
[
13
]
)

Let
\mathsf{c}
be a constant and
F
,
G
be variables. The
higher-order patterns
\lambda x,y.F\>x
and

\lambda x,y.\mathsf{c}\>(G\>y\>x)
are unifiable with

\theta=\{\hskip 0.85358ptF\mapsto\lambda z.\mathsf{c}\>(H\>z),G\mapsto z_{1},z_{2}.H\>z_{2}\}

where
H
is a fresh variable:

(\lambda x,y.F\>x)\theta=\lambda x,y.\mathsf{c}\>(H\>x)=(\lambda x,y.\mathsf{c}\>(G\>y\>x))\theta

In fact,
\theta
is a most general unifier.

Recently, Libal and Miller have generalized higher-order pattern
unification to
functions-as-constructors higher-order unification (FCU)
[
9
]
.
In this subclass, arguments of free variables may contain constants
and distinctness is generalized to the higher-order subterm relation.
In addition, it is required that an argument of a free variable
is not a strict higher-order subterm of any other argument of some
variable in the unification problem (global restriction)
in order to retain unicity and decidability of the unification problem.

Example 2
(taken from
[
9
]
)

Let
\mathsf{fst}
,
\mathsf{snd}
be constants and
X
,
Y
be variables. The
simply-typed
\lambda
-terms

\lambda l_{1},l_{2}.X\>(\mathsf{fst}\>l_{1})\>(\mathsf{fst}\>(\mathsf{snd}\>l_{1}))
and

\lambda l_{1},l_{2}.\mathsf{snd}\>(Y\>(\mathsf{fst}\>l_{2})\>(\mathsf{fst}\>l_{1}))
are
unifiable with

\theta=\{\hskip 0.85358ptX\mapsto\lambda z_{1},z_{2}.\mathsf{snd}\>(H\>z_{1}),Y\mapsto\lambda z_{1},z_{2}.H\>z_{2}\}
where
H
is a fresh variable:

(\lambda l_{1},l_{2}.X\>(\mathsf{fst}\>l_{1})\>(\mathsf{fst}\>(\mathsf{snd}\>l_{1})))\theta=\mathsf{snd}\>(H\>(\mathsf{fst}\>l_{1}))=(\lambda l_{1},l_{2}.\mathsf{snd}\>(Y\>(\mathsf{fst}\>l_{2})\>(\mathsf{fst}\>l_{1})))\theta

In fact,
\theta
is a most general unifier.

Note that unlike higher-order pattern unification, FCU does not give
rise to a corresponding class of simply-typed lambda terms: Due to the
global restriction, the question whether

\lambda l_{1},l_{2}.X\>(\mathsf{fst}\>l_{1})\>(\mathsf{fst}\>(\mathsf{snd}\>l_{1}))

is in the scope of FCU depends on the remaining terms in the
unification problem. In our previous example, it worked, but if we
had chosen

\lambda l_{1},l_{2}.\mathsf{snd}\>(Y\>(\mathsf{fst}\>l_{2})\>(\mathsf{snd}\>l_{1}))

(which is also fine on its own) as the second term, FCU could not be
used as
\mathsf{snd}\>l_{1}
is a higher-order subterm of

(\mathsf{fst}\>(\mathsf{snd}\>l_{1}))
. In
[
9
]
, FCU is compared to
Yokoyama et al.’s deterministic second-order patterns (DSPs), a class of
simply-typed

\lambda
-terms which have a deterministic matching problem, i.e.,
given a DSP
s
and a simply-typed
\lambda
-term
t
, there is at
most one substitution
\theta
such that
s\theta=t

[
19
]
.
Even though it is not discussed in
[
9
]
,
Yokoyama et al. also introduced deterministic higher-order
patterns (DHPs)
[
18
]
which is a generalization of
DSPs to terms of any finite order. DHPs are very
similar to the terms used in FCU, but since one is only interested in
matching, the global restriction of FCU is not needed. Furthermore,
the set of DHPs strictly includes the set of terms which can in
principle be used in FCU. Since there is no global restriction
as in FCU, unification of DHPs is not unitary.

Example 3
(taken from
[
18
]
)

Let
\mathsf{f}
be a constant and
M
,
N
be variables. The DHPs

\lambda x,y.M\>(\mathsf{f}\>x)\>(\mathsf{f}\>y)
and

\lambda x,y.\mathsf{f}\>(N\>y\>x)
admit the three unifiers

(i)

\{\hskip 0.85358ptM\mapsto\lambda z_{1},z_{2}.z_{1},N\mapsto\lambda z_{1},z_{2}.z_{2}\}
,

(ii)

\{\hskip 0.85358ptM\mapsto\lambda z_{1},z_{2}.z_{2},N\mapsto\lambda z_{1},z_{2}.z_{1}\}
, and

(iii)

\{\hskip 0.85358ptM\mapsto\lambda z_{1},z_{2}.\mathsf{f}\>(Z\>z_{1}\>z_{2}),N\mapsto\lambda z_{1},z_{2}.Z\>(\mathsf{f}\>z_{2})\>(\mathsf{f}\>z_{1})\hskip 0.85358pt\}
,

which constitute a complete set of unifiers. Here,
Z
is a fresh
variable.

We think that it is worthwhile to investigate unification of DHPs
because it occupies a special position where matching is still
deterministic but most general unifiers do not exist
anymore. Interestingly, instead of moving from unitary to finite
unification problems, we immediately jump to infinitary problems as we
will show in Example
10
. Another reason for the usage
of DHP unification is that the definition of DHPs
as a class of simply-typed
\lambda
-terms makes it easier to use it as a
subroutine than FCU since one cannot get in a situation where the
problem is undefined due to the global restriction.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: After
setting the scene in Section
2
, we define
DHPs in Section
3
and introduce an inference system to solve
their unification problem in Section
4
.
Correctness of the inference system is proved in Section
5
.
A sufficient criterion to get minimal solutions is
described in Section
6
before we conclude in Section
7
.

3
Deterministic Higher-Order Patterns

The goal of DHPs is to approximate the class of terms which
have a deterministic matching problem while keeping the definition
simple and the corresponding matching procedure efficient. In the
following examples, we leave out type information as it is not
important.

Example 4

Consider the term
s=F(G)
. Given a term
t
there cannot be at
most one substitution
\theta_{t}
such that
s\theta_{t}=t
as it is
not clear how to split the substitution between
F
and
G
. For
example, if
t=\mathsf{f}(x)
then we have
s\theta_{1}=t
and

s\theta_{2}=t
for

\theta_{1}=\{\hskip 0.85358ptF\mapsto z.\mathsf{f}(x),G\mapsto y\hskip 0.85358pt\}
and

\theta_{2}=\{\hskip 0.85358ptF\mapsto z.\mathsf{f}(z),G\mapsto x\hskip 0.85358pt\}
. In fact, it is
easy to see that there are actually infinitely many possible
matching substitutions.

Hence, nested free variables will not be allowed in DHPs. On the other
hand, arguments of free variables have to contain bound variables as
the following example shows.

Example 5

Consider the term
s=F(\mathsf{c})
. Again, matching is not
deterministic: For
t=\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{c})
we have
s\theta_{1}=t
and

s\theta_{2}=t
where
\theta_{1}=\{\hskip 0.85358ptF\mapsto z.\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{c})\hskip 0.85358pt\}

and
\theta_{2}=\{\hskip 0.85358ptF\mapsto z.\mathsf{f}(z)\hskip 0.85358pt\}
.

If the arguments of free variables contain at least one bound
variable, then, due to capture-avoidance, a matching substitution is
forced to use one of these arguments in order to construct terms with
bound variables. Furthermore, bound variables can only be used in the
contexts specified by the arguments of the free variable.

Example 6

Consider the term
s=x,y.F(\mathsf{f}(x))
. For

t=x,y.\mathsf{g}(\mathsf{f}(x))
there is a unique substitution

\theta=\{\hskip 0.85358ptF\mapsto z.\mathsf{g}(z)\hskip 0.85358pt\}
such that
s\theta=t
. Note that
s
does not match
x,y.\mathsf{g}(x)
because the
bound variable
x
does not occur in the context specified by the
argument of
F
in
s
. Moreover,
s
does not match
x,y.y

because the bound variable
y
does not occur in the arguments of

F
.

Finally, if a free variable has multiple arguments, we have to ensure
that they are different enough such that there is always a unique
argument to choose while constructing a term during matching.

Example 7

Consider the term
s=x.F(\mathsf{f}(x,\mathsf{c}),z.\mathsf{f}(x,z))
.
For
t=x.\mathsf{f}(x,\mathsf{c})
we have
s\theta_{1}=t
and

s\theta_{2}=t
where
\theta_{1}=\{\hskip 0.85358ptF\mapsto z_{1},z_{2}.z_{1}\}

and
\theta_{2}=\{\hskip 0.85358ptF\mapsto z_{1},z_{2}.z_{2}(\mathsf{c})\hskip 0.85358pt\}
.

Hence, DHPs as introduced in
[
18

are defined via

\beta\eta

variable is a higher-order subterm of another argument of the same
free variable. In the following, we present a definition of DHPs for
our definition of terms, i.e.,
\lambda
-terms in
\beta\eta
-
long

normal from. To that end, we introduce the notion of expanded terms as
well as the expanded subterm relation which replaces the higher-order
subterm relation on

Definition 1

A term
\overline{x}_{n}.s
is

\overline{x}_{n}.s=\overline{x}_{n},\overline{y}_{k}.h(\overline{s}_{m},\overline{y}_{k}{\uparrow})

\bigl(\mathsf{FV}(\overline{s}_{m})\cup\{\hskip 0.85358pth\hskip 0.85358pt\}\bigr)\cap\{\hskip 0.85358pt\overline{y}_{k}\}=\varnothing
. Consider a term
\overline{x}_{n}.s
as well as an expanded
term
\overline{x}_{n}.t=\overline{x}_{n},\overline{y}_{k}.h(\overline{t}_{m},\overline{y}_{k}{\uparrow})

We say that
\overline{x}_{n}.t
is an

\overline{x}_{n}.s

(
\overline{x}_{n}.s\trianglerighteq_{\mathsf{e}}\overline{x}_{n}.t
) if there are

and terms

\overline{x}_{n^{\prime}}.t_{m+1},\dots,\overline{x}_{n^{\prime}}.t_{m+k}
such that

\overline{x}_{n}.s\trianglerighteq\overline{x}_{n^{\prime}}.h(\overline{t}_{m+k})
. We write

\overline{x}_{n}.s\vartriangleright_{\mathsf{e}}\overline{x}_{n}.t
if
\overline{x}_{n}.s\trianglerighteq_{\mathsf{e}}\overline{x}_{n}.t
and

4

We already showed in Example

unitary. Therefore, instead of using most general unifiers as in
higher-order pattern unification and FCU, we have to work with

present a unification procedure which can produce complete sets of
unifiers which are minimal in the sense that the produced
substitutions are orthogonal.

6
Minimality

In this section, we show that implementations of the unification
inference system which adhere to the mild restrictions given in the
following definition are guaranteed to produce minimal complete
sets of unifiers if successful.

Definition 7

A
strategy

\mathtt{s}
is a mapping from unification pairs

(E,\theta)
with
E\neq\varnothing
to a particular element

\mathtt{s}((E,\theta))=u\approx v\in E

should be applied. In particular,

(E,\theta)\Longrightarrow_{\mathtt{s}}(E^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})
denotes a step in the
inference system restricted to
\mathtt{s}
. A derivation with

is called

if (
rem
) and (
var

in a deterministic way.

7
Conclusion

We introduced a sound and complete inference system to compute
complete sets of unifiers of higher-order unification problems
containing DHPs. Furthermore, we showed that suitable restrictions of
implementations guarantee minimal complete sets of unifiers if
successful. Our inference system is closely related to the one given
in
[
15
]
for full higher-order unification, but with notable
differences such as most general solutions to flex-flex
pairs. Furthermore, DHPs are closely related to the terms used in FCU
which is decidable and unitary. However, these nice properties
of FCU are achieved by a global restriction on the unification problem,
so it can be hard to predict when it is actually usable as a subroutine
in practice. While FCU can solve unification goals
directly, in unification of DHPs one has to resort to partial bindings
as in full higher-order unification to keep the problem
manageable. In fact, the existence of infinite minimal complete sets
of unifiers renders a termination proof of our derivation relation
impossible and leaves decidability of the problem as a nontrivial open
question. Note that a way of detecting situations which require an
infinite minimal complete set of unifiers would not be enough since
the inference system would still be infinitely branching without a
clever representation of infinite sets of unifiers. The idea to use
(regular) grammars as stated in the conclusion of
[
17
]
seems to
be a promising step in that direction.
