Structure Matters: Dynamic Policy Gradient
Sara Klein, Xiangyuan Zhang, Tamer Başar, Simon Weissmann, Leif Döring

Abstract
In this work, we study γ \gamma -discounted infinite-horizon tabular Markov decision processes (MDPs) and introduce a framework called dynamic policy gradient (DynPG). The framework directly integrates dynamic programming with (any) policy gradient method, explicitly leveraging the Markovian property of the environment. DynPG dynamically adjusts the problem horizon during training, decomposing the original infinite-horizon MDP into a sequence of contextual bandit problems. By iteratively solving these contextual bandits, DynPG converges to the stationary optimal policy of the infinite-horizon MDP. To demonstrate the power of DynPG, we establish its non-asymptotic global convergence rate under the tabular softmax parametrization, focusing on the dependencies on salient but essential parameters of the MDP. By combining classical arguments from dynamic programming with more recent convergence arguments of policy gradient schemes, we prove that softmax DynPG scales polynomially in the effective horizon ( 1 − γ ) − 1 (1-\gamma)^{-1} . Our findings contrast recent exponential lower bound examples for vanilla policy gradient.



Keywords:
reinforcement learning, policy gradient, dynamic programming.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

The overarching goal in reinforcement learning (RL) is to train an agent that interacts with an unknown environment. This is mathematically modeled through a Markov decision process (MDP), where the agent actively learns a policy that, given a state, executes actions and receives rewards in return. The objective is to find a policy that maximizes the expected discounted reward. We categorize RL algorithms into two groups.
Rather than the conventional separation between value-based and policy-based algorithms, we distinguish between algorithms that leverage the model’s structure and those that do not. The first class utilizes the dynamic programming (DP) principle, such as value iteration, policy iteration, or Q-learning. Those algorithms optimize essentially single-period problems, where a single reward feedback is used in the update procedure and the future payoff is estimated by evaluation. The second class considers the entire multi-period problem at once and solves it using classical optimization, as in policy gradient (PG) methods
(
Williams, 1992
;
Sutton et al., 1999
;
Konda and Tsitsiklis, 1999
;
Kakade, 2001
)
.
Over time, hybrid approaches integrating both methods, such as actor-critic (AC) methods, have consistently demonstrated superior performance in practical applications even though dynamic programming is used rather indirectly. In AC, the critic suggests a baseline, estimating either the value or action-value function. Dynamic programming becomes essential during the critic update process, effectively diminishing variance in gradient estimation. The essence of employing dynamic programming lies in the fact that parameter updates are not solely reliant on sampling new trajectories, new information is bootstrapped. Efforts of a broad research community have led to tremendous success of AC type algorithms (such as natural policy gradient (NPG), trust region policy optimization (TRPO), proximal policy optimization (PPO)) but these have not yet been backed by a sufficient theoretical understanding. This paper contributes to the basic theory of PG methods by studying the following questions:

To what extent can the Markov property of an MDP be exploited more directly for improved convergence behavior of PG methods?
How much improvement is gained compared to vanilla PG?

This paper addresses both points by merging dynamic programming and policy gradient into dynamic policy gradient (DynPG) and analyzing its convergence behavior. DynPG can be seen as a hybrid RL algorithm that utilizes policy gradient to optimize a sequence of contextual bandits. In each iteration, the algorithm extends the horizon of the MDP by adding an additional epoch in the beginning and shifting trained policies to the future, as in applying the Bellman operator. A policy for the newly added epoch is trained by policy gradient where previous policies are used to determine future actions (cf.,
Figure

1
). DynPG is not an AC method. It reduces the variance as far as possible by applying the previously trained, and therefore fixed, policies to generate a trajectory. This leads to stable estimates of the Q-values and improves convergence behavior. In this work, we first present a general error analysis which is compatible with any optimization scheme to address each contextual bandit problem such as PG, NPG or policy mirror descent
(
Xiao, 2022
)
. We then employ vanilla PG to present an explicit complexity bound under softmax parametrization.

1.2
Related work

The idea of using dynamic programming in searching the optimal policy is not new and dates back to the early 2000s, having been revisited several times in recent decades. Policy search by dynamic programming (PSDP)
(
Bagnell et al., 2003
;
Kakade, 2003
;
Scherrer, 2014
)
is most closely related to DynPG and searches for optimal policies in a restricted policy class without explicitly using gradient ascent to find the optimal policy.
There is a line of similar algorithms like approximate policy iteration (API), gradient temporal difference, policy dynamic programming and numerous other variations
(
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis, 1996
;
Sutton et al., 2008
;
Sutton et al., 2009
;
Azar et al., 2012
;
Kakade and Langford, 2002
;
Scherrer et al., 2015
)
. In these works, however, PG was not used as policy optimization step.
In
Klein et al. (2024)
, a similar concept also called dynamic policy gradient was employed to tackle finite-time MDPs without discounting. Their motivation to utilize dynamic programming stemmed from seeking non-stationary optimal policies in finite-time MDPs. In contrast to their findings, our objective is to identify a stationary optimal policy and incorporate discounting into the framework.
Lastly,
Zhang and Başar (2023)
;
Zhang et al. (2023a)
;
Zhang et al. (2023b)
proposed the receding-horizon policy gradient algorithm, which integrates dynamic programming-based receding-horizon control with policy gradient methods, to solve linear-quadratic continuous control and estimation problems.

The discount factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
plays an essential role in the convergence behavior of RL algorithms, as they explicitly depend on the contraction property of the Bellman operator. The closer
\gamma
to one, the slower the Bellman operator contracts. Similarly, the convergence of PG methods also heavily depends on
\gamma
but establishing a clear dependence is generally challenging due to the non-convex optimization landscape. Early works only concern convergence to stationary points
(
Pirotta et al., 2013
;
Schulman et al., 2015
;
Papini et al., 2018
)
. More recently, specific to tabular settings and the softmax parametrization, in
Agarwal et al. (2021)
;
Mei et al. (2020)
upper bounds on the convergence rate to a global optimum was derived by utilizing a gradient domination property
(
Polyak, 1963
)
. As shown in
Mei et al. (2020)
, vanilla softmax PG has a sublinear convergence rate of
O(\epsilon^{-1})
with respect to the error tolerance
\epsilon
. Dependencies on other salient but essential parameters of the MDP crucially affect the overall convergence behavior of PG methods, e.g., the effective horizon
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
. Notably,
Li et al. (2023)
constructed a counterexample such that vanilla PG could take an exponential time (with respect to
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
) to converge. Although the optimal solution can be reached in just
|\mathcal{S}|
steps of exact value iteration in the counter example, vanilla softmax PG is very inefficient by not leveraging the inherent structure of the MDP. In contrast, for softmax DynPG, we establish an explicit dependency on the discount factor. The convergence rate under exact gradients scales with
(1-\gamma)^{-4}
up to logarithmic factors; thus, we delineate the unknown model-dependent constant
\mathcal{C}(\gamma)
in the rate of vanilla softmax PG
(
Mei et al., 2020
)
. As a result, DynPG can efficiently address the counterexample in
Li et al. (2023)
; see
Section

5.1
.

For completeness we also include a sample based analysis of DynPG and provide complexity bounds for high probability convergence. A related stochastic analyses can be found in
Klein et al. (2024)
and for entropy regularized PG in
Ding et al. (2022)
.

1.3
Main contributions and outline

The main contribution of this article is to introduce and analyze a way to directly combine policy gradient and dynamic programming ideas. The algorithm we introduce in Section
3
is called DynPG (dynamic policy gradient method). The algorithm (provably) circumvents recent worst case lower bound problems for standard PG that prove impracticability of plain vanilla PG in delicate environments. In Section
4
we provide a detailed error decomposition to show the convergence behavior of DynPG in general frameworks. For the tabular softmax parametrization we prove rigorous upper bounds on the convergence rate, Section
4.2
for the theoretical setting of exact gradients and Section
4.3
for the sample based setting. Most importantly, the
\gamma
-dependence is harmless, DynPG does not suffer from exponential dependencies in
\gamma
! A simple example is presented in Section
3.2
to show in a sample based setting how DynPG can beat PG in environments that trick PG into so-called committal behavior. To turn our theoretical algorithm into practice a more memory efficient actor-critic variant of DynPG is presented in Section
5.2
.

2
Preliminaries

Let the tuple
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r,\gamma)
denote an MDP, where
\mathcal{S}
represents the state space,
\mathcal{A}
represents the action space,
p:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the transition kernel mapping each state-action pair to the probability simplex of all possible next states
\Delta(\mathcal{S})
,
r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is the reward function mapping each state-action pair to a reward,
\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
denotes the initial state distribution, and
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor. We assume that the reward is bounded such that
r(s,a)\in[-R^{\ast},R^{\ast}]
for all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
and the state and action spaces are finite.
Hereafter, we use capital and lowercase letters to distinguish random state and actions from deterministic ones. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote its supremum norm by
\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{i=1,\dots,d}|x_{i}|
; we refer to
Appendix

A
for complete notation conventions.

A policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
is a mapping from a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
to a distribution over the action space, i.e.,
\pi(\cdot|s)\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})
. The set of all policies is denoted by
\Pi
. We refer to
h\in\mathbb{N}
as the deterministic time-horizon, where the case
h=\infty
corresponds to the standard infinite-horizon MDP. Non-stationary policies with time-horizon
h
are denoted by
\bm{\pi}_{h}:=\{\pi_{h-1},\dots,\pi_{0}\}\in\Pi^{h}
. Let
V_{0}\equiv 0
and, for all
h>0
, define the
h
-step value function of policies
\bm{\pi}_{h}\in\Pi^{h}
as

\begin{split}V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}(\mu)&=V_{h}^{\{\pi_{h-1},\dots,\pi_{0}\}}(\mu)=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{0}\sim\mu,A_{t}\sim\pi_{h-t-1}(\cdot|S_{t})\\
S_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|S_{t},A_{t})\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{h-1}\gamma^{t}r(S_{t},A_{t})\Big].\end{split}

(1)

When
\mu
is a Dirac measure at
s
we let
V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}(s):=V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}(\delta_{s})
. Subsequently, we interpret a function
V:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
. Additionally, we use
V_{h}^{\pi}(\mu):=V_{h}^{\{\pi,\dots,\pi\}}(\mu)
to denote the value function of the stationary policy
\pi
being applied
h
times in a row. For
h=\infty
the resulting infinite-horizon discounted MDP admits a stationary optimal policy
(
Puterman, 2005
)
. We define
V_{\infty}^{\ast}(\mu):=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}V_{\infty}^{\pi}(\mu)
and use
\pi^{*}
to denote a stationary policy that achieves
V_{\infty}^{\pi^{\ast}}(\mu)=V_{\infty}^{\ast}(\mu)
. In contrast, when
h
is finite, the finite-horizon MDP optimization problem needs non-stationary optimal policies; thus, we define
V_{h}^{\ast}(\mu):=\sup_{\bm{\pi}_{h}\in\Pi^{h}}V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}(\mu)
and use
\bm{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}:=\{\pi_{h-1}^{\ast},\dots,\pi_{0}^{\ast}\}\in\Pi^{h}
to denote a sequence of policies that achieves
V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}^{*}}(\mu)=V_{h}^{\ast}(\mu)
.

For any function
V\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
and stationary policy
\pi
, the Bellman expectation operator
T^{\pi}:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
is defined for every
s\in\mathcal{S}
by

\displaystyle T^{\pi}(V)(s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)\Big(r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})\Big).

The Bellman’s optimality operator
T^{\ast}:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
is then for every
s\in\mathcal{S}
given by

\displaystyle T^{\ast}(V)(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big\{r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})\Big\}.

The operators
T^{\pi}
and
T^{\ast}
are
\gamma
-contracting with unique fixed points denoted by
V_{\infty}^{\pi}
and
V_{\infty}^{\ast}
, respectively.
In addition, the optimal
h
-step value functions
V^{\ast}_{h}
can be obtained by iteratively applying
T^{\ast}
and, as
h\to\infty
,
V^{\ast}_{h}
converges to
V^{\ast}_{\infty}
(c.f.
Lemma

C.1
).

Vanilla Policy Gradient.

Policy gradient is a policy search method for which a family of differential parameterized policies
(\pi_{\theta})_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is fixed, say
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|s):=\pi(\cdot|s,\theta)
, and the optimal policy is searched using gradient ascent:

\displaystyle\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}+\eta\cdot\nabla_{\theta_{n}}V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta_{n}}}(\mu)\,.

(2)

Here,
\eta>0
is the step size. Analyzing the convergence of vanilla PG is generally non-trivial due to the non-convexity of
V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}
and the approximation error of the policy approximation family. Specialized to tabular MDPs,
Agarwal et al. (2021)
has shown global convergence of vanilla PG under the softmax parametrization, and
Mei et al. (2020)
established a non-asymptotic convergence rate. It should be emphasized that convergence is slow and constants can easily dominate convergence rates.

Tabular Softmax Policy.

Policy gradient can be applied in the tabular setting (i.e. considering separately each state-action pair). Although the tabular setting is not used in practical applications, it is the most tractable setting for a complete mathematical analysis and sheds light on general principles. We introduce the logit function
\theta:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
and the softmax policy parametrized by
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}
as

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta}(a|s)=\frac{\exp({\theta(s,a)})}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\exp({\theta(s,a^{\prime})})},\quad s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}.

(3)

The tabular softmax policy can represent any deterministic and therefore optimal policy, whereas other parametrizations such as neural networks may induce an approximation error. This error needs to be considered in the convergence analysis.

Policy Search Framework.

Inspired by dynamic programming,
Bagnell et al. (2003)
;
Kakade (2003)
proposed Policy Search by Dynamic Programming to search policies
\{\pi_{H-1},\cdots,\pi_{0}\}\in\tilde{\Pi}^{H}
, where
H
is the problem horizon given as an input to the algorithm and
\tilde{\Pi}\subseteq\Pi
is the set of all deterministic policies. Formally, given
H
and
\tilde{\Pi}
, PSDP computes

\begin{split}\tilde{\pi}^{*}_{h}&=\textrm{argmax}_{\pi_{h}\in\tilde{\Pi}}~V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{h},\tilde{\bm{\pi}}^{*}_{h}\}}(\mu)=\textrm{argmax}_{\pi_{h}\in\tilde{\Pi}}~V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{h},\tilde{\pi}^{*}_{h-1},\cdots,\tilde{\pi}^{*}_{0}\}}(\mu),\end{split}

(4)

for
h=0,\dots,H-1
. PSDP solves an
(h+1)
-step MDP initialized at an
S_{0}\sim\mu
in the iteration indexed by
h
. Specifically, it finds the optimal deterministic policy for selecting the first action
A_{0}
, denoted by
\tilde{\pi}^{*}_{h}
, but then all the remaining actions in the episode
\{A_{1},\cdots,A_{h-1}\}
are selected according to the sequence of deterministic policies
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{*}:=\{\tilde{\pi}^{*}_{h-1},\cdots,\tilde{\pi}^{*}_{0}\}
. Note that for all
h
,
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{*}
have been computed in previous iterations and are kept fixed. Compared to vanilla PG, PDSP exploits the Markovian property of the environment, rendering each iteration into solving a contextual bandit problem.
While considering deterministic policies may suffice in tabular MDPs, no explicit computational procedures have been provided in
Bagnell et al. (2003)
;
Kakade (2003)
to solve the optimization problem in (
4
). Further, determining the policy to be applied post-training is not immediately evident.
Scherrer (2014)
proposes to apply the non-stationary policy
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}
in a loop. Still, to solve the discussed infinite horizon MDP, a stationary policy is sufficient. We provide answers to these issues using DynPG.

3
Dynamic Policy Gradient

3.1
The DynPG Algorithm

DynPG starts by solving a one-step contextual bandit problem and then incrementally extends the problem horizon by one in each iteration, which is done by appending the new decision epoch in front of the current problem horizon (see the illustration in Figure
1
).

Figure 1
:
DynPG solves a sequence of contextual bandit problems, iteratively storing the convergent policies to memory and applying them accordingly as fixed policies in later iterations.

In each iteration, the parametrized (stochastic) policy responsible for sampling action
A_{0}
from the newly-added epoch is optimized using gradient ascent. In subsequent time steps, DynPG applies the previous convergent policies to sample actions. Upon convergence of the current iteration, the policy is stored in a designated memory location, which will be utilized in future iterations. We define
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}:=\{\hat{\pi}^{*}_{h-1},\cdots,\hat{\pi}^{*}_{0}\}\in\Pi^{h}
with convention
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{0}^{\ast}=\emptyset
to denote the learned policies. DynPG returns the first policy in
\Lambda
when certain user-defined convergence criteria have been met. For example, this is the case if the relative value improvements between two consecutive DynPG iterations are small i.e.,
\lVert V_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h+1}^{\ast}}-V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
.

which will be employed in the analyses of the convergence rate.

Compared to vanilla PG in (
2
), DynPG dynamically adjusts the episode horizon during the execution of the algorithm. This results in two notable advantages. Firstly, we observe that DynPG effectively reduces the variance in gradient estimation through the utilization of non-changing future policies.
In contrast, the estimation of the gradient in vanilla PG involves assessing Q-values based on the stationary policy
\pi_{\theta}
, which changes during training. Secondly, DynPG requires significantly fewer samples, a topic we discuss in detail later in this section. Moreover, each DynPG iteration has more benign optimization landscapes, as they are essentially contextual bandit problems. Specifically, DynPG has a simpler PG theorem (cf.
Theorem

C.3
) and under softmax parametrization a smaller smoothness constant, enabling the selection of a more aggressive step size,
\eta_{h}
, to enhance convergence.

In contrast to PSDP, we specify the set of policies
\tilde{\Pi}
to be a parameterized class of differentiable policies and a computational procedure, namely PG, for the inner loop. It is straight forward to incorporate function approximation (e.g. using neural networks) to the DynPG algorithm. This preserves the model-free optimization characteristic of gradient methods, while still exploiting the underlying structure of MDPs by Dynamic Programming. DynPG can be further modified with additional enhancements such as regularization, natural policy gradient or policy mirror descent in ever optimization epoch.
In addition, we show that applying the policy of the last training epoch as stationary policy is sufficient. This is a non-trivial result and our analysis in Section
4
reveals that in general it requires additional training to obtain a good stationary policy compared to using the non-stationary policy
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}
in a loop as proposed in
Scherrer (2014)
. In the tabular softmax case we specify these additional computational cost explicitly.

On the total sample complexity.

For each gradient step in standard policy gradient, one must run the MDP until termination or up to a stochastic horizon
H\sim\textrm{Geom}(1-\gamma)
to obtain an unbiased sample of the gradient
(
Zhang et al., 2020
)
.
DynPG, on the other hand, only requires
h
interactions with the environment to sample an unbiased estimator of the gradient in epoch
h
.
Thus, comparing other policy gradient methods to DynPG solely based on the number of gradient steps is inadequate.
Instead, for fairness, the number of samples (interactions with the environment) should be compared in practical implementations. For DynPG the total sample complexity is given by
\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}(h+1)N_{h}
. This results in a trade-off between increasing samples required for estimation and more accurate training to obtain convergence (cf.
Section

4.1
).

4
Convergence Analysis

The numerical example suggests improved convergence properties for DynPG. In this section we provide estimates for exact gradient and sample based algorithms. Specifically, Section
4.1
introduces four different layers of approximations that the analysis of DynPG employs in solving the infinite-horizon discounted MDP. Based on this we present the asymptotic global convergence of DynPG under the assumption of small enough optimization errors and rich enough parametrization class. In Section
4.2
, we establish non-asymptotic global convergence rates of DynPG for the softmax parametrization under the assumption that exact gradients are available. We provide suitable choices of
H
,
N_{h}
and
\eta_{h}
such that DynPG achieves an error in the value functions of at most
\epsilon
. In Section
4.3
we extend the previous analysis to gradient estimates obtained from MDP samples. We defer the proofs of all results to
Appendix

D
.

4.1
Error Decomposition and General Convergence

The analysis of DynPG employs four different layers of approximations, including 1) adopting parametrizations incapable of modeling the optimal policy perfectly (approximation error), 2) truncating the infinite time horizon to a finite horizon (truncation error), 3) utilizing a finite number of gradient updates to approximately solve each optimization problem (accumulated optimization error), and 4) applying the first policy of
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}
as a stationary policy in solving finite-horizon MDP (stationary policy error).
We formally quantify these errors in the following lemma where the terms on the right-hand side of (
6
) correspond to the aforementioned errors, respectively.

Proposition 4.1
.

The overall error of DynPG after
H
iterations can be decomposed as follows

\begin{split}&\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{\infty}^{\hat{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\\
&\leq\Big\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-\sup_{\theta}V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}\Big\rVert_{\infty}+\Big\lVert\sup_{\theta}V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}-\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}\Big\rVert_{\infty}\\
&\quad+\Big\lVert\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\Big\rVert_{\infty}+\lVert V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}-V_{\infty}^{\hat{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\\
&\leq\Big\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-\sup_{\theta}V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}\Big\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}\\
&\quad+\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\Big\lVert\sup_{\theta}T^{\pi_{\theta}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})-V_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h+1}^{\ast}}\Big\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\lVert V_{H+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H+1}^{\ast}}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}.\end{split}

(6)

The error decomposition gives clear insights into algorithm design, for which it is necessary to discuss the practical implications of the four summands.

(i)

To control the approximation error a rich enough policy parametrization is required.

(ii)

To keep the truncation error small, DynPG should run at least
H\approx\frac{\log(1-\gamma)}{\log(\gamma)}
rounds.

(iii)

The third summand shows that approximation errors in earlier iterations are discounted more than approximation errors in later iterations. To achieve optimal training efficiency, we will thus require a geometrically decreasing optimization error across the iterations of DynPG. Recall the sample complexity discussion at the end of Section
3
to note that a trade-off between more accurate training and increasing samples required for estimating the gradient must be made to obtain the best performance of DynPG.

(iv)

DynPG approximates the value function by truncation at a fixed time
H
and then replaces the optimal time-dependent policy by a stationary policy. As mentioned for PSDP, one could also apply the non-stationary policy
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}
to approximate the value function. This would cause the fourth error term to vanish. Thus, in what follows, we distinguish between the
overall error
in (
6
) and the
value function error
:

\begin{split}&\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\\
&\leq\big\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}\big\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}+\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\big\lVert\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}T^{\pi_{\theta}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})-V_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h+1}^{\ast}}\big\rVert_{\infty}.\end{split}

(7)

It is important to note that the factor
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
in the stationary policy error causes an additional dependence on the effective horizon in the complexity bounds for softmax DynPG.

For the remainder of this section, we will proceed under the assumption of zero approximation error stemming from the parametrization. This condition generally holds true when the class of policies
(\pi_{\theta})
can effectively approximate all deterministic policies, such as achieved through tabular softmax. Under this circumstance, it follows that
T^{\ast}=\sup_{\theta}T^{\pi_{\theta}}
and
\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-\sup_{\theta}V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}\rVert_{\infty}=0
.
Subsequently, we demonstrate that a certain reduction in the optimization error is adequate for achieving global convergence within the parametrized policy space.

Assumption 4.2
.

The class
(\pi_{\theta})
has zero approximation error and there exists a positive sequence
\epsilon_{h}
such that

•

\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}\to 0
for
H\to\infty
,

•

the policies obtained by DynPG satisfy
\lVert T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})-V_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h+1}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{h}
for all
h\geq 0
.

As an example for such a sequence one might think of
\epsilon_{h}=c\gamma^{h}
for some
c>0
. Under this assumption we prove convergence directly from the error decomposition in
Proposition

4.1
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let Assumption
4.2
hold. Then, Algorithm
1
generates a sequence of non-stationary policies
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}\in\Pi^{H}
that satisfy

\displaystyle\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\to 0\quad\textrm{ for }H\to\infty.

Furthermore, the overall error vanishes in the limit:

\displaystyle\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{\infty}^{\hat{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\to 0\quad\textrm{ for }H\to\infty.

Remark 4.4
.

The condition
\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}\to 0
for

implies that the optimization error must decrease with increasing

4.2
Convergence Rates under Tabular Softmax Parametrization - Exact Gradients

In this section, we assume that all gradients are known explicitly, a strong assumption for practical use but standard for a rigorous analysis. The analysis is extended in the next section to sample based estimates.

4.3
Convergence Rates under Tabular Softmax Parametrization - Sampled Gradients

For this part of the paper we drop the exact gradient assumption and analyze DynPG for sample based gradient estimates (generated by MDP roll-outs). We emphasize that the main insight into DynPG (breaking the (possibly) exponential
\gamma
-dependence) is part of the exact gradient analysis. Even though the estimates provided here are not relevant for practical use, we add the discussion for completeness.

For a fixed future policy
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}
, consider
K_{h}
trajectories
(s_{k}^{i},a_{k}^{i})_{k=0}^{h}
, for
i=1,\dots,K_{h}

,

and

is defined by

\widehat{G}_{K_{h}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{K_{h}}\sum_{i=1}^{K_{h}}\nabla\log(\pi_{\theta}(a_{0}^{i}|s_{0}^{i}))\hat{Q}_{h+1}^{i},

(9)

where
\hat{Q}_{h+1}^{i}=\sum_{k=0}^{h}\gamma^{k}r(s_{k}^{i},a_{k}^{i})
is an unbiased estimator of
Q_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}}(s_{0}^{i},a_{0}^{i})
. Then the stochastic PG update for training the parameter
\theta
in epoch

is given by

\bar{\theta}_{n+1}=\bar{\theta}_{n}+\eta_{h}\widehat{G}_{K_{h}}(\bar{\theta}_{n}).

(10)

Our main result for the optimization of the contextual bandits in DynPG is given as follows.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let Assumption
4.5
hold true and DynPG be used with stochastic updates from (

and

\mathbb{P}\Big(T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\Lambda})(\mu)-V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\bar{\theta}_{n}},\Lambda\}}(\mu)\geq\epsilon_{h}\Big)\leq\delta_{h}.

From the previous contextual bandit result, we can derive a sample complexity result under which we obtain convergence in stochastic DynPG with arbitrary high probability.

5
Application of DynPG

5.1
Breaking the lower bound example with DynPG

In
Li et al. (2023)
a lower bound example was given for which softmax PG takes exponential time in the expected horizon
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
to converge. More precisely, it is shown that at least
|\mathcal{S}|^{2^{\Omega((1-\gamma)^{-1})}}
gradient steps are required to approximate the optimal value function with
\epsilon=0.15
accuracy.
The constructed MDP is designed such that the unknown constant
C(\gamma)
in the upper bound on softmax PG (see
Table

1
) is exponential in the effective time horizon
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
.
To understand this behavior, we take a closer look at the definition of
C(\gamma):=\big(\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\inf_{n\geq 1}\pi^{\theta_{n}}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)\big)^{-1}
in
Mei et al. (2020)
,
where
a^{\ast}(s)
denotes the optimal action in state
s
.
To ensure small
C(\gamma)
the probability of choosing the best action should not get close to
0
during training.
But by construction in
Li et al. (2023)
, finding the best action in state
s
decreases as long as the probability of choosing the best action a previous state is not close enough to 1. This results in the phenomenon that the gradient steps required to converge towards
a^{\ast}(s)
grows at least geometrically as
s
increases.
However, using DP one can solve the MDP within
|\mathcal{S}|
steps of exact value iteration
(
Li et al., 2023
, Lemma 1)
. This already implies that DynPG easily circumvents this exponential convergence time by employing DP. As future actions are determined by the previously trained policies, DynPG can evaluate the MDP under a non-stationary policy during training and thereby avoid the above described phenomenon.
The upper bound on the complexity of softmax DynPG provides a theoretical proof that the needed gradient steps scale at most polynomial in
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
!

5.2
DynPG in Large or Complex Environments

In the following, we aim to discuss a challenge that may arise when applying DynPG in practice.
In very complex MDPs, where the policy parametrization needs to be rich, the DynPG approach in Algorithm
1
can suffer from a storage problem. The number of policy parameters that need to be stored for future decisions scale linearly with the number of training steps. In order to circumvent this problem for practical applications we propose an actor-critic based modification of DynPG, called Dynamic Actor-Critic (DynAC). The general idea behind actor-critic in vanilla PG is to introduce a so called critic, a second parametrized class of functions
(Q^{w})_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{l}}
, which approximates the Q-function
Q^{\pi^{\theta}}
in the policy gradient theorem.
Using the critic as estimator of the Q-values no more roll-outs are needed to estimate the rewards-to-go. In the actor-critic variant of DynPG, we include an additional training procedure after optimizing policy
\hat{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}
to update the critic based on the old critic,
Q^{w_{h}}\approx\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{\hat{\pi}_{h-1}^{\ast},\dots,\hat{\pi}_{0}^{\ast}}
, and the newly trained policy,
\hat{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}
, using the relation:

\displaystyle Q_{h+1}^{\hat{\pi}_{h}^{\ast},\dots,\hat{\pi}_{0}^{\ast}}(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\sum_{a^{\prime}}\hat{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{\hat{\pi}_{h-1}^{\ast},\dots,\hat{\pi}_{0}^{\ast}}.

The critic is used in the policy gradient theorem as an estimator for
Q_{h+1}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}
such that the new gradient in DynAC is given by

\displaystyle G^{\text{Dyn-AC}}=\nabla_{\theta}\log(\pi^{\theta}(A|S))Q^{w_{h+1}}(S,A),

where
S\sim\mu
and
A\sim\pi^{\theta}(\cdot|s)
and
\pi^{\theta}
is the policy we are currently training for epoch
h+1
. This way there is no need to store all trained policies only the currently trained policy and the current critic are needed. For applications where the performance of vanilla PG is poor and the parametrized policy needs to be very rich such that storing many policies might cause storage issues, we suggest to keep this variant of DynPG in mind.
We call this approach DynAC for dynamic actor-critic and summarize the steps in Algorithm
2
.

A theoretical analysis of this approach would require an assumption on the approximation error to train the Q-functions. As the gradients are no longer unbiased, convergence towards the global optimum cannot be guaranteed and the bias errors will appear in the overall error. We leave further investigations of DynAC to future work, as an in-depth exploration would exceed the scope of this paper.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper contributes to the theoretical understanding of PG methods by directly introducing dynamic programming ideas to gradient based policy search. To this end, we have introduced DynPG, an algorithm that directly employs DP to solve
\gamma
-discounted infinite horizon MDPs using gradient ascent methods. We provide mathematically rigorous performance estimates (for exact and estimated gradients) in simplified situations which are typical for theory papers on PG methods. The main strength of the algorithm, in contrast to vanilla PG, is to avoid exponential
\gamma
-dependence.

In this article we have provided theoretical evidence that dynamic programming, combined with PG methods, can address well-known issues inherent to vanilla PG. This opens up a range of further avenues for future research, such as:

•

Investigate whether the complexity bounds in the exact gradient setting are tight (numerically or theoretically) and explore the possibility of eliminating the log-factor.

•

Analyze the variant DynAC.
Kumar et al. (2023)
present a sample complexity analysis of AC methods. Combining their results to control the critic error with the SGD analysis could result in convergence rates to achieve a small value function error in DynAC.

•

Perform a simulation study of DynPG and DynAC on standard tabular environments and explore how numerical instability issues in standard PG can be overcome.

•

Combine DynPG and DynAC with deep RL implementations for PG algorithms such as PPO, TRPO.

C.2
Dynamic Programming - Finite Time Approximation

Lemma C.1
.

Let
V_{0}\equiv 0
. For any
h\geq 1
, it holds that

(i)

V_{h}^{\ast}(s)=T^{\ast}(V_{h-1}^{\ast})(s)
, for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
,

(ii)

\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{h}^{\ast}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma^{h}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast}.

Proof.

C.f.
[
Bertsekas, 2001
, Prop. 1.2.1]

The first claim, follows directly from the definition of the Bellman optimality operator

\displaystyle V_{h}^{\ast}(s)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\bm{\pi}_{h}\in\Pi^{h}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{0}(a|s)\left(r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)V_{h-1}^{\bm{\pi}_{h-1}}(s^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\sup_{\bm{\pi}_{h-1}\in\Pi^{h-1}}V_{h-1}^{\bm{\pi}_{h-1}}(s^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=T^{\ast}(V_{h-1}^{\ast})(s).

For the second part, we fix
h\geq 0
. Recall that
\pi^{\ast}\in\Pi
denotes a stationary optimal policy for the infinite-time problem and
\bm{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}\in\Pi_{h}
an optimal non-stationary policy.

We divide the proof of the second claim into two cases.

Case 1:
Assume that
V_{\infty}^{\ast}(s)-V_{h}^{\ast}(s)\leq 0
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
Note that
V_{\infty}^{\ast}(s)\geq V_{\infty}^{(\bm{\pi}_{h}^{\ast})_{\infty}}(s)
, where
(\bm{\pi}_{h}^{\ast})_{\infty}
denotes that we apply the finite time policy
\bm{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}
in a loop for the infinite time problem. We have

\displaystyle V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}}(s)-V_{\infty}^{\ast}(s)

\displaystyle\leq V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}}(s)-V_{\infty}^{(\bm{\pi}_{h}^{\ast})_{\infty}}(s)

\displaystyle=-\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{0}=s,A_{t}\sim\pi_{h-(t\textrm{ mod }h)-1}(\cdot|S_{t})\\
S_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|S_{t},A_{t})\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=h}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(S_{t},A_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=h}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R^{\ast}=\frac{\gamma^{h}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast},

where
R^{\ast}
bounds the absolute value of rewards.

Case 2:
Assume that
V^{\ast}(s)-V_{h}^{\ast}(s)>0
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Note that
V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}}(s)\geq V_{h}^{\pi^{\ast}}(s)
due to the optimality of
\bm{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}
over the finite-time horizon. Further, by the definition of
V_{\infty}^{\ast}(s)=V_{\infty}^{\pi^{\ast}}(s)
, we have

\displaystyle V_{\infty}^{\ast}(s)-V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}}(s)

\displaystyle\leq V_{\infty}^{\pi^{\ast}}(s)-V_{h}^{\pi^{\ast}}(s)

\displaystyle=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{0}=s,A_{t}\sim\pi^{\ast}(\cdot|S_{t})\\
S_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|S_{t},A_{t})\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=h}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(S_{t},A_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=h}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R^{\ast}=\frac{\gamma^{h}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast}.

Hence, we arrive at

\displaystyle\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{h}^{\pi_{h}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|V_{\infty}^{\ast}(s)-V_{h}^{\pi_{h}^{\ast}}(s)|\leq\frac{\gamma^{h}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast}.

∎

C.3
Error bound for stationary policy

The following Lemma is inspired by error bounds presented in
Bertsekas [2001, Sec. 1.3]
. The purpose of this result is to establish validation of applying the last policy trained in DynPG as stationary policy.

Lemma C.2
.

For any
V\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
and policy
\pi\in\Pi
it holds that

\displaystyle\lVert V-V_{\infty}^{\pi}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\lVert T^{\pi}(V)-V\rVert_{\infty}}{1-\gamma}.

Proof.

Consider any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Since
V_{\infty}^{\pi}
is the unique fixed point of the operator
T^{\pi}
, we have

V_{\infty}^{\pi}(s)=T^{\pi}(V_{\infty}^{\pi})(s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)V_{\infty}^{\pi}(s^{\prime}).

This implies that

\displaystyle V_{\infty}^{\pi}(s)-V(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)V_{\infty}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})-V(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)(V_{\infty}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})-V(s^{\prime})+V(s^{\prime}))-V(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)(V_{\infty}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})-V(s^{\prime}))-V(s)

\displaystyle=T^{\pi}(V)(s)-V(s)+\gamma\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)(V_{\infty}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})-V(s^{\prime}))\,,

for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
. We define the mappings
s\mapsto g_{\pi}(s)=T^{\pi}(V)(s)-V(s)
and
s\mapsto J_{\pi}(s)=V_{\infty}^{\pi}(s)-V(s)
,
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Then, the above equation simplifies to

J_{\pi}(s)=g_{\pi}(s)+\gamma\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\pi(a|s)p(s^{\prime}|s,a)J_{\pi}(s^{\prime}).

By definition
J_{\pi}
satisfies

\displaystyle J_{\pi}(s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)\left(g_{\pi}(s)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)J_{\pi}(s^{\prime})\right)

for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, and therefore is a solution of the Bellman equation with an auxiliary reward function
(s,a)\mapsto\tilde{r}(s,a)=g_{\pi}(s)
. Note that this reward function is also bounded and by the uniqueness of the solution of the Bellman equation it has to hold that

\displaystyle J_{\pi}(s)=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{0}=s,A_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|S_{t})\\
S_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|S_{t},A_{t})\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}g_{\pi}(S_{k})]=g_{\pi}(s)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{0}=s,A_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|S_{t})\\
S_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|S_{t},A_{t})\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}[g_{\pi}(S_{k})].

Define
\underline{\beta}=\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}g_{\pi}(s)
and
\overline{\beta}=\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}g_{\pi}(s)
. Then,

\frac{\underline{\beta}}{1-\gamma}\leq g_{\pi}(s)+\frac{\gamma\underline{\beta}}{1-\gamma}\leq J_{\pi}(s)\leq g_{\pi}(s)+\frac{\gamma\overline{\beta}}{1-\gamma}\leq\frac{\overline{\beta}}{1-\gamma}.

It follows that for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
,

|J_{\pi}(s)|\leq\frac{\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|g_{\pi}(s)|}{1-\gamma}.

By definition of
g_{\pi}
and
J_{\pi}
this yields the claim.
∎

C.4
Policy Gradient Theorem for DynPG

Theorem C.3
.

Suppose that
\bm{\pi}_{h}\in\Pi^{h}
is fixed for any
h\geq 0
, with the convention
\bm{\pi}_{0}=\emptyset
. Then, for any differentiable parametrized family, say
(\pi_{\theta})_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
(i.e.
\theta\mapsto\pi_{\theta}(s,a)
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is differentiable for all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
), it holds that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(s)=\underset{S=s,A\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|S)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log(\pi_{\theta}(A|S))Q_{h+1}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}(S,A)\right].

Proof.

The proof can be found in
Klein et al. [2024, Thm A.6]
. However, we will need an index shift and additionally to consider the discount factor
\gamma
.
An
h
-step trajectory
\tau_{h}=(s_{0},a_{0},\dots,s_{h-1},a_{h-1})
under policy
\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}
and initial state distribution
\delta_{s}
occurs with probability

\displaystyle p_{s}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(\tau_{h})=\delta_{s}(s_{0})\pi_{\theta}(a_{0}|s_{0})\prod_{k=1}^{h-1}p(s_{k}|s_{k-1},a_{k-1}){\pi}_{k}(a_{k}|s_{k}).

Then, the log trick yields that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\log(p_{s}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(\tau_{h}))

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\Big(\log(\delta_{s}(s_{0}))+\log(\pi_{\theta}(a_{0}|s_{0}))+\sum_{k=1}^{h-1}\log(p(s_{k}|s_{k-1},a_{k-1}))+\log({\pi}_{k}(a_{k}|s_{k}))\Big)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\log(\pi_{\theta}(a_{0}|s_{0})).

Let
\mathcal{W}_{h}
be the set of all trajectories from
0
to
h-1
. Then, the set
\mathcal{W}_{h}
is finite due to the assumption that state and action space are finite. For
s\in\mathcal{S}
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\nabla_{\theta}V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(s)\\
&=\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{\tau_{h}\in\mathcal{W}_{h}}p_{s}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(\tau_{h})\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\gamma^{k}r(s_{k},a_{k})\\
&=\sum_{\tau_{h}\in\mathcal{W}_{h}}p_{s}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(\tau_{h})\nabla_{\theta}\log(p_{s}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(\tau_{h}))\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\gamma^{k}r(s_{k},a_{k})\\
&=\sum_{\tau_{h}\in\mathcal{W}_{h}}p_{s}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(\tau_{h})\nabla_{\theta}\log(\pi_{\theta}(a_{0}|s_{0}))\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\gamma^{k}r(s_{k},a_{k})\\
&=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{0}=s,A_{0}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|S)\\
S_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|A_{t},S_{t}),A_{t}\sim\pi_{h-t-1}(\cdot|S_{t})\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\nabla_{\theta}\log(\pi^{\theta}(A_{0}|S_{0}))\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\gamma^{k}r(S_{k},A_{k})\Big]\\
&=\underset{S_{0}=s,A_{0}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|S)}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\nabla_{\theta}\log(\pi^{\theta}(A_{0}|S_{0}))\underset{S_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|A_{t},S_{t}),A_{t}\sim\pi_{h-t-1}(\cdot|S_{t})}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\gamma^{k}r(S_{k},A_{k})\big|S_{0},A_{0}\Big]\Big]\\
&=\underset{S=s,A\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|S)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log(\pi_{\theta}(A|S))Q_{h+1}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}(S,A)\right].\end{split}

∎

C.6
Convergence under Gradient Domination

Lemma C.5
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be
L
-smooth (i.e.
L
-Lipschitz continuity of the gradient),
f^{\ast}=\sup_{x}f(x)<\infty
. Denote the gradient ascent procedure by
x_{n+1}=x_{n}+\alpha\nabla f(x_{n})
for some
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\alpha=\frac{1}{L}
and assume that
f
satisfies the following gradient domination property for some
b>0
along the gradient trajectory,

\lVert\nabla f(x_{n})\rVert_{2}\leq b(f^{\ast}-f(x_{n}))\quad\forall n\geq 1.

Then the convergence rate

f^{\ast}-f(x_{n})\leq\frac{2}{\alpha b^{2}n},

holds true if
f^{\ast}-f(x_{1})\leq\frac{2}{\alpha b^{2}}
.

Proof.

We apply the descent lemma of smooth functions
[
Beck, 2017
, Lem 5.7]
on
-f
and obtain

\displaystyle f(y)\geq f(x)+\nabla f(x)^{T}(y-x)-\frac{L}{2}\lVert y-x\rVert^{2}.

Now for gradient ascent updates we have that

\displaystyle f(x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\geq f(x_{n})+\nabla f(x_{n})^{T}(x_{n+1}-x_{n})-\frac{L}{2}\lVert x_{n+1}-x_{n}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=f(x_{n})+\alpha\lVert\nabla f(x_{n})\rVert^{2}-\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2}\lVert\nabla f(x_{n})\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=f(x_{n})+\Big(\alpha-\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2}\Big)\lVert\nabla f(x_{n})\rVert^{2}.

It follows that

\displaystyle f^{\ast}-f(x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq f^{\ast}-f(x_{n})-\Big(\alpha-\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2}\Big)\lVert\nabla f(x_{n})\rVert^{2}.

Exploiting the gradient domination along the gradient trajectory results in

\displaystyle f^{\ast}-f(x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq f^{\ast}-f(x_{n})-\Big(\alpha-\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2}\Big)b^{2}(f^{\ast}-f(x_{n}))^{2}.

As
\alpha=\frac{1}{L}
, we have

\displaystyle f^{\ast}-f(x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq f^{\ast}-f(x_{n})-\frac{b^{2}}{2L}(f^{\ast}-f(n_{k}))^{2}.

When
f^{\ast}-f(x_{1})\leq\frac{2}{\alpha b^{2}}
, then
f^{\ast}-f(x_{n})\leq\frac{2}{\alpha b^{2}n}
by
Klein et al. [2024, Lem. B.7]
.

∎

Before we prove the second inequality, note that

\displaystyle\Big|\sup_{\theta}f(\theta)-\sup_{\theta}g(\theta)\Big|\leq\sup_{\theta}|f(\theta)-g(\theta)|

for any two functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. If
\sup_{\theta}f(\theta)-\sup_{\theta}g(\theta)\geq 0
, then

\Big|\sup_{\theta}f(\theta)-\sup_{\theta}g(\theta)\Big|=\sup_{\theta}f(\theta)-\sup_{\theta}g(\theta)\leq\sup_{\theta}(f(\theta)-g(\theta))\leq\sup_{\theta}|f(\theta)-g(\theta)|.

If
\sup_{\theta}f(\theta)-\sup_{\theta}g(\theta)<0
, then the role of
f
and
g
are swapped.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\sup_{\theta}T^{\pi_{\theta}}(V)-\sup_{\theta}T^{\pi_{\theta}}(G)\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\sup_{\theta}\big(T^{\pi_{\theta}}(V)-T^{\pi_{\theta}}(G)\big)\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma\lVert V-G\rVert_{\infty}.

(13)

We treat the second, third and fourth terms separately.

For the second term
, we prove that
\Big\lVert\sup_{\theta}V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}-\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}\Big\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}
similar to
Lemma

C.1
.
Let
s\in\mathcal{S}
be arbitrary but fixed. If
\sup_{\theta}V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}(s)-\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}(s)\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta}V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}(s)-\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta}V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}(s)-\sup_{\theta}V_{H}^{\pi_{\theta}}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta}(V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}(s)-V_{H}^{\pi_{\theta}}(s))

\displaystyle=\sup_{\theta}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{0}=s,A_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|S_{t})\\
S_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|S_{t},A_{t})\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=H}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(S_{t},A_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=H}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R^{\ast}=\frac{\gamma^{H}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast},

where we used (
13
) in the second inequality.
On the other hand, if
\sup_{\theta}V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}(s)-\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}(s)<0
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}(s)-\sup_{\theta}V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}(s)-\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{\infty}^{(\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\})_{\infty}}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}\Big(V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}(s)-V_{\infty}^{(\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\})_{\infty}}(s)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}-\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{0}=s,A_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta_{H-(t\textrm{ mod }H)-1}}(\cdot|S_{t})\\
S_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|S_{t},A_{t})\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=H}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(S_{t},A_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=H}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R^{\ast}=\frac{\gamma^{H}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast}.

Collecting these together, we obtain that
\Big\lVert\sup_{\theta}V_{\infty}^{\pi_{\theta}}-\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}\Big\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}
, since
s\in\mathcal{S}
was chosen arbitrary.

For the third term
, we show that

\displaystyle\lVert\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\lVert\sup_{\theta}T^{\pi_{\theta}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})-T^{\hat{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})\rVert_{\infty}

(14)

holds for all
H\geq 1
by induction. For
H=1
we have by (
5
) that

\displaystyle\lVert\sup_{\theta}V_{1}^{\pi_{\theta}}-V_{1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{1}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}=\Big\lVert\sup_{\theta}T^{\pi_{\theta}}(V_{0}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{0}^{\ast}})-T^{\hat{\pi}_{1}^{\ast}}(V_{0}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{0}^{\ast}})\Big\rVert_{\infty},

with the convention
V_{0}\equiv 0
and
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{0}^{\ast}=\emptyset
. So assume that
Equation

14
holds for some
H\geq 1
; then for
H+1
we have

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H}}V_{H+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}-V_{H+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H+1}^{\ast}}\Big\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\Big\lVert\sup_{\theta_{H}}T^{\pi_{\theta_{H}}}\big(\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}\big)-T^{\hat{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}(V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}})\Big\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lVert\sup_{\theta_{H}}T^{\pi_{\theta_{H}}}\big(\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}\big)-\sup_{\theta_{H}}T^{\pi_{\theta_{H}}}(V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}})\Big\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\quad+\Big\lVert\sup_{\theta_{H}}T^{\pi_{\theta_{H}}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}})-T^{\hat{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}(V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}})\Big\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\Big\lVert\sup_{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{H-1}}V_{H}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{H-1}},\dots\pi_{\theta_{0}}\}}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\Big\rVert_{\infty}+\Big\lVert\sup_{\theta_{H}}T^{\pi_{\theta_{H}}}(V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}})-T^{\hat{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}(V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}})\Big\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\lVert\sup_{\theta}T^{\pi_{\theta}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})-T^{\hat{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})\rVert_{\infty}+\Big\lVert\sup_{\theta}T^{\pi_{\theta}}(V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}})-T^{\hat{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}(V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}})\Big\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=0}^{H}\gamma^{H-h}\lVert\sup_{\theta}T^{\pi_{\theta}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})-T^{\hat{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})\rVert_{\infty},

where we used (
5
), as well as (
13
), and the induction assumption. This yields the desired claim (
14
) for all
H\geq 1
.

For the fourth error term
, we have to deal with the error of applying the final policy as stationary policy.
We use
Lemma

C.2
to arrive at

\displaystyle\lVert V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}-V_{\infty}^{\hat{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\lVert T^{\hat{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}(V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}})-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\lVert V_{H+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H+1}^{\ast}}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty},

where we used again (
5
) in the last line.
∎

Proof of Corollary
4.3
.

Adjusting (
7
) to zero approximation error and exploiting
Assumption

4.2
we obtain

\displaystyle\rVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}+\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}\to 0\quad\textrm{for }H\to\infty.

For the second part of the theorem note that
V_{H}^{\bm{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}
converges in the supremum norm to
V_{\infty}^{\ast}
by the first part. This implies that
V_{H}^{\bm{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}
is a Cauchy sequence with respect to the supremum norm, i.e.
\lVert V_{H+1}^{\bm{\pi}_{H+1}^{\ast}}-V_{H}^{\bm{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\to 0
for
H\to\infty
. The claim follows directly from
Proposition

4.1
.
∎

D.2
Proof of Theorem
4.6

Before we prove
Theorem

4.6
, we will discuss the similarities and differences between DynPG and the finite-time Dynamic Policy Gradient (FT-DynPG) Algorithm proposed in
Klein et al. [2024]
.

(i)

FT-DynPG has a prefixed time horizon
H
and trains policy backwards in time. In contrast, DynPG adds arbitrarily many policies in the beginning and can therefore be applied without prefixed
H
.

(ii)

Given a fixed time horizon
H
, FT-DynPG returns a non-stationary policy
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}
for an
H
-step MDP, but without discounting
\gamma=1
. When DynPG is run for the same fixed number of iterations
H
, then FT-DynPG and DynPG only differ by the discount factor: The value functions
V_{h,H}
defined in
Klein et al. [2024]
for FT-DynPG are by the strong Markov property equivalent to
V_{0,H-h}
via index shifting. The function
V_{0,H-h}
differs from our
H-h
-step value function
V_{H-h}
defined in (
1
) only by the discount factor
\gamma
.

In order to prove
Theorem

4.6
, we have to adapt the proof for
Klein et al. [2024, Lem. 3.4]
to an additional discount factor. Note, that we cannot use
Mei et al. [2020, Thm. 2]
for bandits, as we consider contextual bandits. Further, we cannot use
Mei et al. [2020, Thm. 4]
with
\gamma=0
as they just consider positive rewards in
[0,1]
which is inconvenient for our contextual bandit setting where the maximal rewards grows when we add a new time-epoch in the beginning.

Therefore, we will adapt the proofs in
Mei et al. [2020]
,
Klein et al. [2024]
and start with deriving the smoothness of our objective functions.

Lemma D.1
.

Suppose
\pi_{\theta}={\textrm{softmax}}(\theta)
. Then, for arbitrary
\bm{\pi}_{h}\in\Pi^{h}
and
\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
, the function
\theta\mapsto V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(\mu)
is
L_{h}
-smooth with
L_{h}=\frac{2R^{\ast}(1-\gamma^{h+1})}{(1-\gamma)}
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to
Klein et al. [2024, Lem. B.8]
. Note that we can interpret
V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(\mu)
as a contextual bandit problem, i.e. a discounted (infinite-time) MDP with discount factor
0
. The reward of the contextual bandit problem is almost surely bounded in
[-\frac{1-\gamma^{h+1}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast},\frac{1-\gamma^{h+1}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast}]
, because

\sum_{t=0}^{h}\gamma^{t}R^{\ast}=\frac{1-\gamma^{h+1}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast}.

We can apply
Yuan et al. [2022, Lem. 4.4 and Lem. 4.8]
with
R_{\max}=\frac{1-\gamma^{h+1}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast}
,
G^{2}=1-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\leq 1
and
F=1
to obtain the smoothness constant
L_{h}=\frac{2R^{\ast}(1-\gamma^{h+1})}{(1-\gamma)}
.
∎

Second, we obtain the following gradient domination property.

Lemma D.2
.

Under
Assumption

4.5
it holds for any
\bm{\pi}_{h}\in\Pi^{h}
that

\lVert\nabla_{\theta}V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(\mu)\rVert\leq\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{\theta}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)\big(T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}})(\mu)-V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(\mu)\big),

where
a^{\ast}(s)
denotes the (unique) action taken after the greedy policy
\pi^{V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}}
.

Remark D.3
.

Without loss of generality, we assume that the action
a^{\ast}(s)
is unique for any fixed future policy
\bm{\pi}_{h}
, otherwise one can consider
\min_{a\textrm{ optimal action in }s}\pi_{\theta}(a|s)
.

Proof.

First note from
Theorem

C.3
that under the tabular softmax parametrization we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(\mu)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(s)\underset{S=s,A\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|S)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log(\pi_{\theta}(A|S))Q_{h+1}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}(S,A)\right].

Hence, with the derivative of the softmax function

\frac{\partial\log(\pi_{\theta}(a|s))}{\partial\theta(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}=\mathbf{1}_{\{s=s^{\prime}\}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{a=a^{\prime}\}}-\pi_{\theta}(a^{\prime}|s)\right)

it holds that

\begin{split}\frac{\partial V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(s)}{\partial\theta(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}&=\mathbf{1}_{\{s=s^{\prime}\}}\underset{S=s^{\prime},A\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|S)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{A=a^{\prime}\}}-\pi_{\theta}(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\right)Q_{h+1}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}(S,A)\right]\\
&=\mathbf{1}_{\{s=s^{\prime}\}}\left(\pi_{\theta}(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})Q_{h+1}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-\pi_{\theta}(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\underset{S=s^{\prime},A\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|S)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[Q_{h+1}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}(S,A)\right]\right)\\
&=\mathbf{1}_{\{s=s^{\prime}\}}\pi_{\theta}(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\left(Q_{h+1}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(s^{\prime})\right)\\
&=\mathbf{1}_{\{s=s^{\prime}\}}\pi_{\theta}(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})A_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}).\end{split}

(15)

We deduce from Lemma
C.4
, Equation (
12
), that

\displaystyle T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}})(s)-T^{\pi_{h}}(V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}})(s)=\underset{S_{0}=s,A_{0}\sim\pi_{h}^{\ast}(\cdot|S_{0})}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[A_{h+1}^{\bm{\pi}_{h+1}}(S_{0},A_{0})\Big].

Finally, the rest of the proof is derived as in
[
Klein et al., 2024
, Lem. B.9]

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla_{\theta}V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(\mu)\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\Big\lVert\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(s)\frac{\partial V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(s)}{\partial\theta}\Big\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\Big[\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(s)\frac{\partial V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(s)}{\partial\theta(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\right)^{2}\Big]^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big[\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(s)\pi_{\theta}(a^{\prime}|s)A_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(s,a^{\prime})\right)^{2}\Big]^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\Big|\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(s)\pi_{\theta}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)A_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(s)\pi_{\theta}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)\underset{S=s,A\sim\pi^{V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}}(\cdot|s)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[A_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\bm{\pi}_{h}\}}(S,A)\right]\Big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(s)\pi_{\theta}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)\left(T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}})(s)-T^{\pi_{\theta}}(V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}})(s)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{\theta}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)\left(T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}})(\mu)-T^{\pi_{\theta}}(V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}})(\mu)\right),

where
a^{\ast}(s)
denotes the action taken after the greedy policy
\pi^{V_{h}^{\bm{\pi}_{h}}}
(c.f.
Remark

D.3
). ∎

The next step is to show that the term
\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{\theta}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)
can be bounded (uniformly in
s\in\mathcal{S}
) from below by
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}
along the gradient ascent trajectory, when softmax is initialized uniformly.

Lemma D.4
.

Let Assumption
4.2
hold and denote by
(\theta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
the gradient ascent sequence in epoch
h\geq 0
of DynPG under the fixed future policy
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\in\Pi^{h}
. Suppose further that
\eta_{h}=\frac{1-\gamma}{2R^{\ast}(1-\gamma^{h+1})}
is chosen as in Theorem
4.6
. Then, for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
, it holds that

\min_{n\geq 0}\pi_{\theta_{n}}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}.

where
a^{\ast}(s)
denotes the (unique) action taken after the greedy policy
\pi^{V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}}}
(see
Remark

D.3
).

Proof.

The proof is adapted from
Mei et al. [2020, Lem. 5]
and
Klein et al. [2024, Lem. B.10, Prop. 3.3]
.

First, we define the sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}(s)=\{\theta:\pi_{\theta}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)\geq\pi_{\theta}(a|s)\forall a\neq a^{\ast}(s)\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{2}(s)=\Big\{\theta:\frac{\partial V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)}{\partial\theta(s,a^{\ast}(s)}\geq\frac{\partial V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)}{\partial\theta(s,a^{\ast}(s)}\forall a\neq a^{\ast}(s)\Big\}.

Claim 1:
It holds that
\theta_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}\implies\theta_{n+1}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}
.

To prove this claim, we first deduce from (
15
) that

\begin{split}\frac{\partial V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)}{\partial\theta(s,a^{\ast}(s))}&\geq\frac{\partial V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)}{\partial\theta(s,a^{\ast}(s))}\\
\Longleftrightarrow\,\pi_{\theta_{n}}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)A_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))&\geq\pi_{\theta_{n}}(a|s)A_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s,a).\end{split}

(16)

Let
a\neq a^{\ast}(s)
be arbitrary. We consider two cases to proof claim 1:

(i)

Suppose that
\pi_{\theta_{n}}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)\geq\pi_{\theta_{n}}(a|s)
for any
a\neq a^{\ast}(s)
. Then, is holds that
\theta_{n}(s,a^{\ast}(s))\geq\theta_{n}(s,a)
by the definition of softmax. Next, as
\theta_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}^{\ast}
, we derive

\displaystyle\theta_{n+1}(s,a^{\ast}(s))

\displaystyle=\theta_{n}(s,a^{\ast}(s))+\eta_{h}\mu_{h}(s)\frac{\partial V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{n}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)}{\partial{\theta_{n}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))}

\displaystyle\geq\theta_{n}(s,a)+\eta_{h}\mu_{h}(s)\frac{\partial V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)}{\partial\theta(s,a^{\ast}(s))}

\displaystyle=\theta_{n}(s,a).

Thus, by the definition of the softmax function,
\pi_{\theta_{n+1}}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)\geq\pi_{\theta_{n+1}}(a|s)
for any
a\neq a^{\ast}(s)
. Further, because
a^{\ast}(s)
is the greedy action, we arrive at

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta_{n+1}}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)A_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{n+1}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))

\displaystyle\geq\pi_{\theta_{n}}(a|s)A_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{n+1}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s,a),

such that
\theta_{n+1}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}(s)
by (
16
).

(ii)

Suppose that
\pi_{\theta_{n}}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)<\pi_{\theta_{n}}(a|s)
for any
a\neq a^{\ast}(s)
. Then,
\theta_{n}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-\theta_{n}(s,a)<0
by definition of the softmax function. As
\theta_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}(s)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\theta_{n+1}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-\theta_{n+1}(s,a)

\displaystyle\geq\theta_{n}(s,a^{\ast}(s))+\eta_{h}\mu(s)\frac{\partial V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)}{\partial\theta(s,a^{\ast}(s))}-\theta_{n}(s,a)-\eta_{h}\mu(s)\frac{\partial V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)}{\partial\theta(s,a)}

\displaystyle\geq\theta_{n}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-\theta_{n}(s,a).

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle(1-\exp(\theta_{n+1}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-\theta_{n+1}(s,a)))\leq(1-\exp(\theta_{n}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-\theta_{n}(s,a)))<1.

By the ascent lemma for smooth functions
[
Mei et al., 2020
, Lem. 18]
it follows monotonicity in the objective function (due to small enough step size
\eta_{h}=\frac{1}{L_{h}}
with
L_{h}
the smoothness constant in
Lemma

D.1
) such that
V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{n+1}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)\geq V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{n}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)
. So,

\displaystyle(1-\exp(\theta_{n+1}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-\theta_{n+1}(s,a)))\Big(Q_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{n+1}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\exp(\theta_{n}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-\theta_{n}(s,a)))\Big(Q_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{n}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)\Big)

We rearrange (
16
) and obtain that
\theta\in\mathcal{R}_{2}(s)
is equivalent to

\displaystyle Q_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-Q_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}}(s,a)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\exp(\theta_{n}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-\theta_{n}(s,a)))A_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s,a^{\ast}(s)).

We deduce by
\theta_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}(s)
that

\displaystyle(1-\exp(\theta_{n+1}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-\theta_{n+1}(s,a)))\Big(Q_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{n+1}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\exp(\theta_{n}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-\theta_{n}(s,a)))\Big(Q_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{n}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq Q_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))-Q_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}}(s,a),

and thus
\theta_{n+1}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}(s)
.

This proves claim 1.

Claim 2:
If
\theta_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}(s)
, then it holds that
\pi_{\theta_{n+1}}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)\geq\pi_{\theta_{n}}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)
.

Line by line as in Claim 2 of
Klein et al. [2024, Lem. B.10]
.

Claim 3:
It holds that
\theta_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}(s)\implies\theta_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}(s)
.

Let
\theta_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}(s)
. As
a^{\ast}(s)
is optimal we have for any
a\neq a^{\ast}(s)
that

\displaystyle A_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{n}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))\geq A_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{n}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s,a).

Further by
\theta_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}(s)
it holds

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta_{n}}(a^{\ast}(s)|s)A_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{n}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))\geq\pi_{\theta_{n}}(a|s)A_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta_{n}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s,a).

Hence, by (
15
), we deduce that
\theta_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}(s)
.

Conclusion of the proof by combining claim 1, claim 2 and claim 3:

Since
\theta_{0}
is initialized such that softmax is the uniform distribution, we have that
\theta_{0}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. By claim 3, we have that
\theta_{0}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}(s)
and by claim 1 it follows that
\theta_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}(s)
for all
n\geq 0
and all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
Finally, by claim 2, it follows that
\min_{n\geq 0}\pi_{\theta_{n}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))=\pi_{\theta_{0}}(s,a^{\ast}(s))=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}
for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
∎

We finally we collect all preliminary results to prove
Theorem

4.6
.

Proof of
Theorem

4.6
.

First, note that the tabular softmax parametrization can approximate any deterministic policy arbitrarily well. As optimal policies in finite horizon MDPs are deterministic, we have that
\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}}V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(s)=T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
(zero approximation error induced by the parametrization).
Moreover, for any
s\in\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(s)-T^{\hat{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(s^{\prime})\frac{\mathbf{1}_{s^{\prime}=s}}{\mu(s^{\prime})}\Big(T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(s^{\prime})-T^{\hat{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(s^{\prime})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}\Big(T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(\mu)-T^{\hat{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(\mu)\Big).

For any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds that

\displaystyle T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(s)-T^{\pi_{\theta}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(s)\leq 2\frac{1-\gamma^{h+1}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast}=L_{h}=\frac{1}{\eta_{h}}\leq\frac{2|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{\eta_{h}}.

Moreover, by Lemma
D.1
the function
\theta\mapsto V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}\}}(\mu)
is
L_{h}
-smooth and fulfills the gradient domination property along the gradient ascent steps with
b=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}
(Lemma
D.2
and Lemma
D.4
).
Hence, we can apply Lemma
C.5
with
b=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}
,
\alpha=\eta_{h}=\frac{1-\gamma}{2R^{\ast}(1-\gamma^{h+1})}
and
n=N_{h}
, such that

\displaystyle T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(\mu)-T^{\pi_{\theta_{N_{h}}}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(\mu)\leq\frac{4|\mathcal{A}|^{2}R^{\ast}(1-\gamma^{h+1})}{(1-\gamma)N_{h}}.

By the choice of
N_{h}
we deduce for any
s\in\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(s)-T^{\hat{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(s)

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}\Big(T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(\mu)-T^{\pi_{\theta_{N_{h}}}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})(\mu)\Big)\leq\epsilon_{h},

which proves the claim.
∎

D.3
Proof of
Theorem

4.8

We will treat the two cases of value function error and overall error separately and start with the value function error.

Value function error.

Note that under the tabular softmax parametrization we have zero approximation error and the can upper bound in (
7
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}+\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h},

where
\epsilon_{h}
are upper bounds on one step optimization errors
\big\lVert\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}T^{\pi_{\theta}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})-V_{h+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h+1}^{\ast}}\big\rVert_{\infty}
according to
Theorem

4.6
.

In order to minimize the accumulated number of gradient steps, we have to solve the optimization problem:

\begin{split}&\min_{H,(\epsilon_{h})_{h=0}^{H-1},\epsilon_{h}>0}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}N_{h}=\frac{4R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\frac{(1-\gamma^{h+1})}{\epsilon_{h}}\\
&\textrm{subject to }\quad\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}+\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}\leq\epsilon.\end{split}

(17)

In
Weissmann et al. [2022, Sec. 3.2]
a similar optimization problem was considered and it was shown that asymptotically (as
\epsilon\to 0
) it suffices to bound both error terms in the constraint by
\frac{\epsilon}{2}
.
The first condition, i.e.
\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
, leads to the criterion
H\geq\log_{\gamma}\big(\frac{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}{2R^{\ast}}\big)
. To minimize the number of gradient steps we fix
H=\big\lceil\log_{\gamma}\big(\frac{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}{2R^{\ast}}\big)\big\rceil
.
It remains to solve the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{(\epsilon_{h})_{h=0}^{H-1},\epsilon_{h}>0}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}a_{h}\epsilon_{h}^{-1}\quad\textrm{ subject to }\quad\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2},

(18)

with
a_{h}=\big\lVert\frac{4R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{1-\gamma}\frac{1}{\mu}\big\rVert_{\infty}(h+1)(1-\gamma^{h+1})
.

We provide a solution of the optimization problem (
18
) by solving the following more general one:

Lemma D.5
.

Fix
H>0
. Let
(a_{h})_{h=0}^{H-1}
and
(b_{h})_{h=0}^{H-1}
be strictly positive sequences. For any
d>0
the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{(c_{h})_{h=0}^{H-1}}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}a_{h}c_{h}^{-1}\quad\textrm{ subject to }\,\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}b_{h}c_{h}\leq d,

is optimally solved for
c_{h}=C_{d,H}\left(\frac{b_{h}}{a_{h}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, with
C_{H,d}=d\Big(\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}(a_{h}b_{h})^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{-1}
.

Hence, the minimum of the optimization problem is given by
\frac{1}{d}\left(\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}(a_{h}b_{h})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}
.

Proof.

The result and the proof is inspired by
Weissmann et al. [2022, Lem. 3.8]
.

We solve the constrained optimization problem by employing the Lagrange method, i.e

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda,(c_{h})_{h=0}^{H-1}}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}a_{h}c_{h}^{-1}+\lambda\left(\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}b_{h}c_{h}-d\right).

The first order conditions are given by

\displaystyle-a_{h}c_{h}^{-2}+\lambda b_{h}=0\quad\forall h=0,\dots,H-1,\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}b_{h}c_{h}-d=0.

We deduce from the first equations, that there exists a constant
C_{H,d}
such that
c_{h}=C_{H,d}\left(\frac{b_{h}}{a_{h}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
for all
h=0,\dots,H-1
. Using this in the second equation we can solve for the constant

\displaystyle C_{H,d}=d\Big(\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}b_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}a_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{-1}.

Using the minima
c_{h}=C_{H,d}\left(\frac{b_{h}}{a_{h}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
in the optimization function
\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}a_{h}c_{h}^{-1}
, we obtain the minimum

\displaystyle\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}a_{h}c_{h}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}a_{h}C_{H,d}^{-1}\left(\frac{b_{h}}{a_{h}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=C_{H,\epsilon}^{-1}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}(a_{h}b_{h})^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{d}\left(\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}(a_{h}b_{h})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}.

∎

Finally, we are ready to state the detailed version of Theorem
4.8
for the value function error.

Theorem D.6
.

[Detailed version of Theorem
4.8
(2.)]

Let Assumption
4.5
hold true and the gradient be accessed exactly. Choose
\epsilon>0
and set

\displaystyle H=\Big\lceil\log_{\gamma}\Big(\frac{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}{2R^{\ast}}\Big)\Big\rceil,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{h}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\Big(\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}((1-\gamma^{t+1})\gamma^{H-t-1})^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{-1}\Big(\frac{\gamma^{H-h-1}}{(1-\gamma^{h+1})}\Big)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\eta_{h}=\frac{1-\gamma}{2R^{\ast}(1-\gamma^{h+1})}

\displaystyle N_{h}=\Big\lceil\frac{4R^{\ast}(1-\gamma^{h+1})|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)\epsilon_{h}}\big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\big\rVert_{\infty}\Big\rceil,

for
h=0,\dots H-1
. Then, the non-stationary policy
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}
obtained by DynPG under exact gradients achieves
\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
. The total number of gradient steps are given by

\displaystyle\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}N_{h}=\frac{8R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}\epsilon}\Big\lceil\frac{\log(2R^{\ast}(1-\gamma)^{-1}\epsilon^{-1})}{\log(\gamma^{-1})}\Big\rceil\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}.

Proof.

First, note that the choice of
\epsilon_{h}
in the theorem is the solution of the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{(\epsilon_{h})}\frac{4R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\frac{(1-\gamma^{h+1})}{\epsilon_{h}}\textrm{ subject to }\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Therefore, choose
a_{h}=(1-\gamma^{h+1})
,
b_{h}=\gamma^{H-h-1}
,
c_{h}=\epsilon_{h}
and
d=\frac{\epsilon}{2}
in
Lemma

D.5
, where we excluded the constant
\frac{4R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}
. Then,

\epsilon_{h}=C_{H,d}\left(\frac{b_{h}}{a_{h}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\Big(\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}((1-\gamma^{t+1})\gamma^{H-t-1})^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{-1}\Big(\frac{\gamma^{H-h-1}}{(1-\gamma^{h+1})}\Big)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

is the optimal solution to this problem.
For
H=\lceil\frac{\log(\frac{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}{2R^{\ast}})}{\log(\gamma)}\rceil
we have that
\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
.

Using these
(\epsilon_{h})_{h=0}^{H-1}
in Theorem
4.6
results in a value function error for DynPG bounded by

\displaystyle\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}+\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon.

(19)

For the optimal complexity bound we ignore the Gauss-brackets in
N_{h}
and derive

\displaystyle\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}N_{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{4R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}a_{h}c_{h}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{4R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}C_{H,d}^{-1}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}a_{h}\Big(\frac{b_{h}}{a_{h}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}C_{H,d}^{-1}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\big(a_{h}b_{h}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}\frac{1}{d}\Big(\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\big(a_{h}b_{h}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{8R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}\Big(\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\big((1-\gamma^{h+1})\gamma^{H-h-1}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{8R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}\Big(\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\big((\gamma^{H-h-1}-\gamma^{H})\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}H\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}(\gamma^{H-h-1}-\gamma^{H}),

where we used Jensen’s inequality in the last step. Further it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}N_{h}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}H\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}(\gamma^{H-h-1}-\gamma^{H})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}H\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8R^{\ast}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}H\frac{1}{1-\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{8R^{\ast}H|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}\epsilon}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{\mu}\Big\rVert_{\infty}.

Finally, note that we can rewrite
H
to be

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\Big\lceil\log_{\gamma}\Big(\frac{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}{2R^{\ast}}\Big)\Big\rceil=\Big\lceil\frac{\log\Big(\frac{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}{2R^{\ast}}\Big)}{\log(\gamma)}\Big\rceil=\Big\lceil\frac{\log((1-\gamma)^{-1}\epsilon^{-1}2R^{\ast})}{\log(\gamma^{-1})}\Big\rceil

∎

Overall error.

To deal with the overall error, we first derive the following upper bound.

Lemma D.7
.

Assume zero approximation error, i.e.
T^{\ast}=\sup_{\theta}T^{\pi_{\theta}}
. Further, assume bounded optimization errors
\lVert T^{\ast}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})-T^{\hat{\pi}_{h}^{\ast}}(V_{h}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{\ast}})\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{h}
for all
h=0,\dots,H
. If
\epsilon_{H}\leq(1-\gamma)\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}
, then the overall error of DynPG is bounded by

\displaystyle\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{\infty}^{\hat{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{3}{1-\gamma}\Big(\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}+\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}\Big).

Proof.

First, we have by triangle inequality

\displaystyle\lVert V_{H+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H+1}^{\ast}}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert V_{H+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H+1}^{\ast}}-V_{\infty}^{\ast}\rVert_{\infty}+\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}.

By Proposition
4.1
we obtain, under zero approximation error, that

\displaystyle\lVert V_{H+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H+1}^{\ast}}-V_{\infty}^{\ast}\rVert_{\infty}+\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma^{H+1}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}+\sum_{h=0}^{H}\gamma^{H-h}\epsilon_{h}+\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}+\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}.

By the assumption
\epsilon_{H}\leq(1-\gamma)\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}
, it holds further that

\displaystyle\sum_{h=0}^{H}\gamma^{H-h}\epsilon_{h}\leq\gamma\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}+\epsilon_{H}\leq\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}.

We obtain

\displaystyle\lVert V_{H+1}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H+1}^{\ast}}-V_{H}^{\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}+2\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}.

We deduce for the overall error (by Proposition
4.1
and under zero approximation error) that

\displaystyle\lVert V_{\infty}^{\ast}-V_{\infty}^{\hat{\pi}_{H}^{\ast}}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}+\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}+\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\left(\frac{2\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}+2\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{1-\gamma}\Big(\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}+\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}\Big).

∎

It is important to notice that the overall error is upper bounded by the same error terms,
\frac{\gamma^{H}R^{\ast}}{1-\gamma}+\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{H-h-1}\epsilon_{h}
, as the value function error (under zero approximation error) up to the constant
\frac{3}{1-\gamma}
.
Thus, we can obtain the result for the overall error by substituting
\epsilon
with
\frac{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}{3}
.

Theorem D.8
.

[Detailed version of Theorem
4.8
(1.)]

Let Assumption
4.5
hold true and the gradient be accessed exactly. Choose
\epsilon>0
and set

\displaystyle H=\big\lceil\log_{\gamma}\big(\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}\epsilon}{6R^{\ast}}\big)\big\rceil,

Then, the stationary policy

\epsilon
with
\frac{(1-\gamma)\epsilon}{3}
. Moreover, note that

Thus,

D.7
to hold, and we obtain that using these

’s in Theorem

Thus, the complexity bounds for the optimization epochs

∎

Lemma D.9
.

The term
\log\big(\gamma^{-1}\big)=-\log(\gamma)
is asymptotically equivalent to
(1-\gamma)
for
\gamma\uparrow 1
.

Proof.

By the definition of asymptotic equivalence for two functions
f
and
g
we have to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{x\uparrow 1}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=1.

It holds by L’Hospital rule that

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\uparrow 1}\frac{-\log(\gamma)}{(1-\gamma)}=\lim_{\gamma\uparrow 1}\frac{-\gamma^{-1}}{-1}=1.

∎

D.4
Proof of
Theorem

To prove

We first verify that the gradient estimator in

Lemma D.10
.

Consider the estimator
\hat{G}_{K_{h}}(\theta)
from

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\Lambda\}}[\widehat{G}_{K_{h}}(\theta)]=\nabla_{\theta}V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\Lambda\}}(\mu)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\Lambda\}}[\lVert\widehat{G}_{K_{h}}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\Lambda\}}(\mu)\rVert^{2}]\leq\frac{5(\frac{1-\gamma^{h+1}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast})^{2}}{K_{h}}=:\frac{\xi_{h}}{K_{h}}

By the definition of
\widehat{G}_{K_{h}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\Lambda\}}[\widehat{G}_{K_{h}}(\theta)]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\Lambda\}}\Big[\nabla\log(\pi_{\theta}(A_{0}|S_{0}))\sum_{k=0}^{h}\gamma^{k}r(S_{k},A_{k})\Big],

For the second claim, we first obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\Lambda\}}\Bigl[\lVert\widehat{G}_{K_{h}}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\Lambda\}}(\mu)\rVert^{2}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K_{h}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\Lambda\}}\Bigl[\lVert\nabla\log(\pi_{\theta}(A_{0}|S_{0}))\hat{Q}_{h+1}(S_{0},A_{0})-\nabla_{\theta}V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\Lambda\}}(\mu)\rVert^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K_{h}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\Lambda\}}\Big[\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big(\mathbf{1}_{s=S_{0}}(\mathbf{1}_{a=A_{0}}-\pi_{\theta}(a|s))\sum_{k=0}^{h}\gamma^{k}r(S_{k},A_{k})-\mu(s)\pi_{\theta}(a|s)Q_{h+1}^{\Lambda}(s,a)\Big)^{2}\Big].

Note that we have nearly the same term as in the proof of
Klein et al. [2024, Lem. D.6]
. We can follow line by line the arguments there and have to replace the bounds
\sum_{k=h}^{H-1}r(S_{k},A_{k})\leq(H-h)R^{\ast}
with our upper bound
\sum_{k=0}^{h}\gamma^{k}r(S_{k},A_{k})\leq\frac{1-\gamma^{h+1}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast}
.

So replacing
(H-h)
with
\frac{1-\gamma^{h+1}}{1-\gamma}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\Lambda\}}\Bigl[\lVert\widehat{G}_{K_{h}}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}V_{h+1}^{\{\pi_{\theta},\Lambda\}}(\mu)\rVert^{2}\Bigr]\leq\frac{5(\frac{1-\gamma^{h+1}}{1-\gamma}R^{\ast})^{2}}{K_{h}}.

∎

Proof.
