Comprehensive GWAS and transcriptome analysis discovered new candidate genes for powdery mildew response in pea (Pisum sativum L.)
Authors: Xiaojuan Zhong, Mei Yang, Xin Li, Shuangyu Yang, Yuanfang Fan, Xianshu Wang, Chao Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPea powdery mildew is a major disease that causes substantial yield losses and reduces crop quality. Identifying new resistance genes is essential for developing cultivars with durable resistance and high productivity.ResultsIn this study, we applied two complementary approaches, genome-wide association study (GWAS) and transcriptomics, to identify genetic loci and candidate genes involved in the pea response to powdery mildew. For GWAS, infection type (IT) and disease severity (DS) were evaluated in 376 genetically diverse pea accessions grown across four environments. Ten significant single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) loci were detected for IT in three environments, and eleven loci were detected for DS in two environments; in total, 70 genes were located near these peak SNPs. Transcriptome analysis of one susceptible accession and five resistant accessions was carried out at four infection time points. By integrating GWAS with weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and series test of cluster analysis, we identified eight candidate genes likely contributing to powdery mildew resistance.ConclusionsThese results provide new SNP loci and candidate genes for studying the resistance mechanism of pea powdery mildew and for breeding resistant cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07924-6.Keywords: Pea, Powdery mildew, Genome-wide association study (GWAS), Transcriptome, WGCNA

Backgroud

Pea (Pisum sativum L.), the model plant for Mendel’s laws of heredity, is the fourth largest edible legume crop worldwide, valued for its high protein content, nitrogen-fixing capacity, and adaptability to diverse climates. Powdery mildew of pea, caused by the biotrophic fungus Erysiphe pisi, is one of the most damaging diseases, leading to yield losses of 25%–50% and reducing the quality of produce [1]. Chemical control is both costly and environmentally unsustainable, highlighting the urgency of identifying and exploiting natural resistance genes. The discovery of new resistance genes and the development of resistant cultivars through genetic improvement remain the most effective strategies for enhancing resistance to powdery mildew.

To date, only three resistance genes for pea powdery mildew have been identified. These include two recessive genes, er1 and er2, and one dominant gene, Er3, and are derived from “Huancabamba,” “JI 2480,” and Pisum fulvum, respectively [1–3]. The er1 gene blocks invasion of powdery mildew into epidermal cells, conferring immunity or high resistance with almost no visible necrotic spots on leaves [4, 5]. The er2 and Er3 genes provide resistance through hypersensitive reactions (post-penetration cell death) [6]. Resistance mediated by er2 depends on temperature and leaf age, whereas Er3 is not temperature dependent [7, 8]. Moreover, all three genes have been mapped with molecular markers. The er1 gene is located on linkage group VI (LG VI), with two markers, OPD10650 (random amplified polymorphic DNA, RAPD) and ScOPD-10,650 (sequence characterized amplified region, SCAR), reported at 2.1 cM and 3.4 cM from er1, respectively [5, 9]. Multiple allelic variants of er1 have since been identified, and kompetitive allele-specific PCR (KASP) markers developed [10, 11]. The er2 gene is located on linkage group III (LG III), with the SCAR marker ScX17-1400 positioned 2.6 cM away [12, 13]. The Er3 gene is located on linkage group IV (LG IV), with the simple sequence repeat (SSR) marker AD61 identified 0.39 cM away [14].

However, resistance conferred by these genes has been eroded or bypassed by other erysiphaceae fungi. For example, Ondřej et al. [15] and Attanayake et al. [16] demonstrated that E. baeumleri and E. trifolii can overcome er1-mediated resistance to airborne powdery mildew. Similarly, Fondevilla et al. [17] showed that E. trifolii can overcome Er3-mediated resistance. Thus, although many studies have screened pea germplasm with resistance to powdery mildew, no additional resistance genes have yet been identified.

In an earlier study, we evaluated resistance to powdery mildew in 400 pea germplasm accessions at the seedling stage in greenhouse conditions. Building on this work, resistance in 376 accessions was assessed at the adult stage over three years at two locations. Among these, five local accessions displayed resistance at both stages. In the present study, these 376 accessions and the five resistant accessions of them were analyzed by simplified genome sequencing and transcriptome sequencing to identify resistance genes and to provide new genetic resources for pea breeding.

Results

Phenotypic statistics

A total of 376 pea accessions collected from at least 12 countries and nine provinces of China (Table S1) were used to evaluate the response to powdery mildew infection consecutively for three years (2018–2020) at two experimental fields (Xindu and Jianyang). The infection type (IT) and disease severity (DS) of the 376 accessions were evaluated for the GWAS analysis (Table 1; Fig. 1a and b). The mean IT values ranged from 1.36 to 3.00, and the mean DS values ranged from 13.05 to 71.40 across the four environments. The average coefficient of variation (CV) of IT was 32.8%, ranging from 25.48% to 44.03%, and the average CV of DS was 37.2%, ranging from 22.95% to 63.38%. The CV of both IT and DS in 2020_Jianyang was the highest among the four environments.

The genotype main effects and genotype × environment interaction (GGE) biplot analysis was employed to evaluate the genotype-by-environment interaction for powdery mildew resistance in 376 accessions across these four environments (Fig. 1c-h). For IT, the polygon-view plots grouped the test environments and identified vertex genotypes with high susceptibility (Fig. 1c). The functional diagram of “inter-environmental relationships” revealed that there was a positive correlation among the four environments (the angle between two environments was acute angles), indicating that the four environments had consistent sorting of genotypes (Fig. 1d). The lengths of the four environmental vectors indicated that the three environments of Xindu had stronger discriminatory power compared to the 2020_jianyang environment (Fig. 1d). The “discriminatory power and representativeness” view demonstrated that the three environments of xindu possessed the stronger discrimination ability (longest vector) than the 2020_jianyang environment. However, the 2020_jianyang environment exhibited greater representativeness than the 2018_xindu and 2020_xindu environments, whereas 2019_xindu was the most representative environment (smallest angle with the average environment axis) (Fig. 1e). A parallel analysis for DS (Fig. 1h) confirmed these patterns, with the first two principal components explaining a high cumulative variance (89.88%), underscoring the model’s reliability. In conclusion, the GGE analysis revealed a generally consistent ranking of the 376 accessions across environments, and this stability in phenotypic data provides a solid foundation for subsequent GWAS to identify reliable genetic loci for powdery mildew resistance of pea.

Genotyping and population structure

Re-sequencing of the 376 accessions yielded more than 520.4 Gb of paired-end reads with an average coverage of approximately 45.54×, and per-sample coverage ranging from 0.2% to 0.69% (Table S2). The average Q30 and Guanine-Cytosine (‌GC) contents were 88.25% and 42.42%, respectively. After SNP calling and filtering, a total of 17,128 SNPs and 730 insertions/deletions (InDels) were obtained for further analysis.

The phylogenetic tree generated using the neighbor-joining approach divided the 376 accessions into five main clusters (Fig. 2a). Genetic diversity of the accessions was also assessed by principal component analysis. The top three principal components accounted for 6.63%, 4.25%, and 1.76% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 2b). Accessions from China could be clearly distinguished from those originating from other countries. In addition, the results of population structure showed that the cross-validation (CV) error was lowest at K = 5 (Fig. 2c), suggesting that the 376 accessions could be divided into five subpopulations (Fig. 2d), consistent with the phylogenetic analysis.

Genome-Wide association study (GWAS)

GWAS was conducted using a mixed linear model (MLM). A total of 10 significant SNP loci for IT were detected in three environments, located on chromosomes 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6 (Table 2; Fig. 3a–d). For IT and DS, one and 11 significant SNP loci were detected in two environments, respectively (Table 2; Fig. 3e–h). In addition, one SNP locus (chr4LG4-88487329) was identified in both IT and DS across two environments (i.e., 2019_Xindu_IT, 2020_Xindu_IT, 2019_Xindu_DS, and 2020_Xindu_DS), and five SNP loci were simultaneously identified in 2020_Xindu_IT and 2020_Xindu_DS (Table 2).

Transcriptome analysis

To further understand the changes in transcript levels of pea during powdery mildew response and to jointly screen candidate genes with GWAS analysis, six accessions (one susceptible accession (CW8) and five accessions (i.e., SCPA098, SCPA163, SCPA245, SCPA310, and SCPA361)) among these 376 accessions were selected to conduct RNA sequencing and transcriptome analysis at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after inoculation based on their contrasting allele at the significant SNP loci and their divergent phenotypic responses to powdery mildew resistance (Table S3). The detailed sequencing data showed that more than 496.5 billion clean bases of 150 bp were generated from 72 cDNA libraries after raw-read filtering and quality control (Table S4). Overall, approximately 93.57% of the reads mapped to the reference genome, and 90.57% were uniquely mapped. The GC content was 42.79%, while more than 94% of the reads had quality scores of Q30 or higher. These results demonstrated high-quality sequencing and indicated that the transcriptome dataset could be used for further analysis.

To analyze the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among the six accessions at different infection time points, 38 comparison groups were constructed (Table S5). In these comparisons, the numbers 1, 2, 3, and 4 represented the time points 0 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, respectively; the susceptible accession CW8 was labeled as “C,” and resistant accessions were designated as “S” plus a number. The number of DEGs in each comparison ranged from 2669 (i.e., S098-2_vs_S098-1) to 6668 (i.e., S245-2_vs_C-2). In all comparisons between pre-infection and post-infection, the number of down-regulated DEGs was greater than the number of up-regulated DEGs, indicating that more genes were suppressed in response to disease stress following infection with powdery mildew. In addition, Venn diagram results showed that 233 (comparisons with the susceptible material as control) and 239 (comparisons with samples before infection as control) DEGs were shared by all comparisons in two different comparison modes, respectively (Fig. S1a and b). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes(KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis indicated that the 239 DEGs identified in the second mode were mainly enriched in pathways related to stress responses, such as zeatin biosynthesis, sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (Fig. S1c and d). Finally, to validate RNA-seq data, 10 DEGs were randomly selected, and their relative expression levels were measured by the quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) in all samples (Fig. S2a–j). Correlation analysis showed that the qRT-PCR results and RNA-seq data for these 10 DEGs were highly consistent (Fig. S2k).

Combined GWAS and transcriptome analysis

To identify the most promising candidate genes involved in the regulation of powdery mildew, all DEGs from the transcriptome analysis were subjected to weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). A total of 22 modules were identified (Fig. 4a), of which 11 modules were significantly correlated with powdery mildew traits (Fig. 4b). KEGG pathway enrichment of these 11 modules was analyzed (Table S6). These results revealed that these modules were significantly enriched in metabolic pathways associated with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, carbon metabolism, and plant–pathogen interactions.

Next, the vennset was used to screen for candidate genes shared between GWAS and the WGCNA modules, and a total of 15 genes were identified (Fig. 4c). In addition, a series test of cluster analysis was performed on all DEGs to identify genes that co-segregated with the phenotypes. All DEGs were divided into 10 clusters with distinct expression patterns (Fig. S3). Among these, genes in cluster 1 were expressed significantly higher before infection than after infection in all materials, while cluster 2 displayed the opposite expression pattern. The expression patterns of clusters 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 showed higher expression in at least one material compared with the others. The 15 genes identified by GWAS and WGCNA were further compared with the genes in clusters 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8, and 12 genes were finally selected (Fig. 4d). The expression patterns of these 12 genes are shown in Fig. 4d.

Candidate gene analysis

Gene annotation of the 12 genes identified by GWAS and transcriptome analysis was carried out, and the detailed information is provided in Table S7. Among the 12 genes, four were located on chromosome 1, two on chromosome 2, one on chromosome 5, and four on chromosome 6. All gene sequences were blasted in the NCBI database to determine related gene information. The BLAST results showed that three genes (i.e., Psat6g035000, Psat6g035040, and Psat6g036040) were uncharacterized and had unknown functions. Of the remaining eight genes, Psat1g188680 encoded an outer envelope membrane protein, Psat1g191400 belonged to the pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR)-containing protein family, Psat1g191440 encoded a calcium permeable stress-gated cation channel 1(CSC1)-like protein, Psat1g195720 encoded the cellulose synthase A catalytic subunit, Psat2g170880 and Psat5g027160 belonged to the lateral organ boundaries (LOB) family, Psat2g170960 encoded a BTB/POZ (Broad-Complex, Tramtrack and Bric a brac/POxvirus and Zinc finger) and TAZ (Transcription Adaptor putative Zinc finger) domain-containing protein, and both Psat6g036200 and Psat6g036840 encoded amino acid permeases. We speculate that these eight genes, encoding different functional proteins, may participate in the response of pea to powdery mildew.

Discussion

GWAS studies in pea

GWAS is a powerful tool that enables the identification of genetic variants associated with important agronomic traits, and is therefore important for plant genetics and breeding. In pea, GWAS has been applied to identify loci associated with multiple agronomic traits [18], seed protein composition [19], heat stress resistance [20], frost tolerance [21], and drought and heat resistance [22]. Powdery mildew is one of the most serious diseases of pea, inflicting heavy losses in both yield and quality. From a breeding perspective, a major objective is to mine new powdery mildew resistance genes. In this study, we conducted an integrated analysis of GWAS and transcriptomics to identify SNP markers and candidate genes associated with resistance using 376 genetically diverse pea accessions.

Comparison between the known resistance sites or genes and the SNP loci identified in this study

To date, several studies have investigated the inheritance of resistance to pea powdery mildew. However, only three resistance genes, er1, er2, and Er3, have been identified and mapped to pea chr1LGVI, chr5LGIII, and chr4LGIV, respectively, using various molecular markers [4, 14, 23, 24]. For er1, a total of nine different molecular markers, including random amplified RAPD, SCAR, restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP), and SSR markers, have been reported. These markers were distributed between 0.0 cM (Sc-OPO-181200, RAPD/SCAR) and 25.8 cM (PSMPSAD51, SSR) from er1 [4]. In addition, the previous study has confirmed that resistance provided by er1 is due to a loss of function a mildew resistance locus O1 (PsMLO1) gene [25]. In our study, four significant SNP loci from the 2020_Xindu environment were identified on chr1LG6 (Table 2). One SNP (chr1LG6-139245238) was associated with IT, and three (chr1LG6-342962843, chr1LG6-340179630, and chr1LG6-346996363) were associated with DS. Comparison of the genome location of the PsMLO1 gene and marker positions for er1 showed that the four SNPs on chr1LG6 detected here did not overlap with the known marker segments (Fig. S4).

For er2, the marker OPX-17_1400/ScX17_1400 is linked at 2.6 cM [12]. By BLASTing against the reference genome, we determined that the RAPD fragment OPX-17_1400 was located in chr5LG3:241616853–241,617,397; this location did not overlap with the SNP loci identified in this study (Table 2 and Fig. S4). For Er3, two RAPD markers, OPAH06_539 and OPAB01_874/SCAB1874, were mapped at 7.7 cM and 2.8 cM from the Er3 gene, respectively [26]. Based on marker positions, the chromosome position of the SNP loci (i.e., chr4LG4-88487329 and chr4LG4-118238272) on chr4LGIV identified in this study were compared with Er3 (Fig. S4). This comparison result represented that the two SNP loci on the chr4LGIV were new loci different form the Er3. Overall, comparison of the genome position for er1, er2, and Er3 with the SNPs identified here showed that the 16 significant loci (Table 2) from GWAS did not include the previously known resistance genes.

Putative candidate gene analysis

Although chr4LG4-88487329 was significantly detected in two environments and associated with both IT and DS, the genes linked to this locus were not detected among DEGs in the transcriptome dataset (Table 3). Therefore, these genes were not considered candidates for powdery mildew resistance. Further, the set of candidate genes was narrowed down to 12 genes by comprehensively comparing GWAS, WGCNA, and the series test of cluster analysis in this study, and eight genes encoding different functional proteins were considered as candidates responding to powdery mildew (Fig. 4 and Table S7). Sequence blast analysis showed that these eight genes encoded seven types of protein.

First, the Psat1g188680 gene was annotated as an outer envelope membrane protein. Previous studies have shown that outer envelope membrane proteins play important roles in signal transduction, protein import, lipid biosynthesis and remodeling, exchange of ions and metabolites, plastid division, movement, and host defense [27, 28]. Next, we found that both Psat1g191400 and Psat1g191440 were homologous to the PPR-containing protein At3g25210. The PPR family, one of the largest in plants, has been implicated in crucial functions broadly involving organelle biogenesis and plant development [29, 30]. A genome-wide analysis of the poplar PPR gene family showed that 154 PtrPPR genes were induced by biotic and abiotic treatments, including Marssonina brunnea, salicylic acid, methyl jasmonate, mechanical wounding, cold, and salinity stress [31].

Several candidate genes identified in this study belonged to families of transcription factors or cell wall–related enzymes that have been implicated in plant defense. For example, the Psat1g195720 gene was predicted to encode a cellulose synthase A catalytic subunit. Cellulose synthase affects pathogen invasion by regulating hemicellulose content in cell walls, as shown in rice. The OsCSLC3 gene, encoding cellulose synthase, was specifically expressed in vascular tissues, and its overexpression increased hemicellulose content and significantly improved resistance to rice blast [32]. The Psat2g170880 gene belonged to the LOB domain-containing family, a group of plant-specific transcription factors with essential roles in growth, development, and disease resistance. In Arabidopsis, the LBD20 protein acted as a repressor of a subset of jasmonate-mediated defenses and contributed to susceptibility to the root-infecting fungal pathogen Fusarium oxysporum [33]. Furthermore, the Psat2g170960 gene was a member of the BTB/POZ family of proteins (i.e., BR–C, ttk, and bab or pox virus and zinc finger). The BTB-TAZ subfamily is plant-specific and functions as transcription factors [34]. In maize and soybean, BTB-TAZ proteins were shown to participate in disease resistance by mediating jasmonate synthesis or salicylic acid-related pathways [35, 36].

The remaining two genes, Psat6g036200 and Psat6g036840, encoded amino acid permeases, a subfamily of amino acid transporters [37]. In rice, OsAAP1 was shown to regulate tillering by affecting nitrogen transport and metabolism, as well as auxin, cytokinin, and strigolactone signaling [38]. Another gene, OsAAP5, regulated tillering bud growth through cytokinin levels [39]. Although no direct evidence links amino acid permeases to plant disease responses, amino acid transport is an important component of plant defense. Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that these two genes encoding amino acid permeases may also contribute to powdery mildew resistance in pea. In summary, previous studies provide evidence that the homologous genes of the eight candidate genes identified in this study are involved in disease responses in other crops which indicates that the eight candidate genes may closely be related to the pea response to powdery mildew.

Conclusions

In this study, two complementary approaches, GWAS and transcriptomics were applied to identify genetic loci and candidate genes involved in the pea response to powdery mildew. For GWAS, IT and DS were evaluated in 376 genetically diverse pea accessions grown across four environments. Ten significant SNP loci were detected for IT in three environments, and eleven loci were detected for DS in two environments; in total, 70 genes were located near these peak SNPs. Transcriptome analysis of one susceptible accession and five resistant accessions was carried out at four infection time points. By integrating GWAS with WGCNA and series test of cluster analysis, eight candidate genes likely contributing to powdery mildew resistance were identified. These belonged to families of transcription factors or cell wall–related enzymes that have been implicated in plant defense. Therefore, this research presents new candidate genes for exploring resistance mechanisms. Future research should investigate the function of each gene and its interactions with other proteins involved in powdery mildew pathways to further clarify the genetic architecture of resistance and to provide new anti-powdery mildew genes for pea breeding.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and phenotypic evaluation

A total of 376 pea germplasm resources collected from different countries and from several provinces in China were used (Table S1). All materials were grown in two experimental fields of the Sichuan Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Xindu: 104.2067° E and 30.6235° N, elevation 476 m, mean annual temperature 16.1℃, mean annual precipitation around 828.3 mm; and Jianyang: 104.5882° E and 30.2422° N, elevation 490 m, mean annual temperature 17.5℃, mean annual precipitation around 799.1 mm) during 2018 and 2020. For each accession, 20–30 seeds were sown per row with 50 cm spacing between rows. The method of inoculation and the evaluation of resistance to powdery mildew were followed the ‘Descriptors and Data Standard for Pea (Pisum sativum L.)’ [40]. The artificial spray inoculation method was used to inoculate the pea powdery mildew fungus. The powdery mildew spores on the leaves of the infected plants were washed with distilled water to prepare a spore suspension of the fungus at a concentration of 8 × 104 sporangia per ml, which was then used to spray inoculate the pea leaves. Field irrigation was carried out after vaccination to keep the soil under high humidity conditions to create environmental conditions suitable for the disease. Disease investigation was conducted when the highly susceptible line CW8 was fully infected. Disease severity (DS) was evaluated as the percentage of leaves infected by powdery mildew per plant. For each accession, 20 individual plants were randomly selected, and the number of leaves showing visible white fungal mycelium was recorded for each plant. The percentage of infected leaves per plant was calculated, and the DS score for each accession was determined as the average percentage across the 20 plants. The infection type (IT) of each line were evaluated based on the DS, and was scored on a 0–4 scale: 0, immune (DS = 0); 1, highly resistant (0 < DS ≤ 30%); 2, resistant (30% < DS ≤ 50%); 3, susceptible (50%< DS ≤ 70%); 4, highly susceptible (DS > 70%).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean, median, standard deviation (SD), and coefficient of variation (CV), were calculated for IT and DS trait in each environment. The correlation between IT and DS was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Broad-sense heritability (H²) was estimated on an entry-mean basis using the following formula: H² = σ²g / (σ²g + σ²ge/n + σ²e/rn), where σ²g is the genotypic variance, σ²ge is the genotype-by-environment interaction variance, σ²e is the error variance, n is the number of environments, and r is the number of replications. GGE bioplot analysis were performed using Genstat (v24th edition) [41].

Genomic DNA extraction and SNP genotyping

Total genomic DNA was extracted from young pea leaves for library construction. The DNA was digested with PstI-HF and MspI enzymes, and libraries were constructed using SuperGBS technology [42]. Paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq Xten platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at Oebiotech (Qingdao, China). Sequencing quality was assessed by guanine–cytosine (GC) content and Q20 and Q30 values, where Q20 and Q30 represent error probabilities of 1% and 0.1%, respectively. To generate clean reads, Stacks software was used to remove adapter-containing reads and demultiplex pooled samples according to barcodes. The Fastx toolkit was then applied to trim restriction sites and discard bases with quality scores < 20 at the 3′ end. High-quality reads were aligned to the pea reference genome [43] (Pisum sativum v1a; https://urgi.versailles.inra.fr/Species/Pisum) using Bowtie2 (version 2.4.2) [44].

Based on these alignments, population SNP detection and screening were performed with Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, version 4.6.0.0) [45]. To reduce false positives, SNP and InDel calls were filtered in VCFtools (version 0.1.16) [46] using the following thresholds: read depth (DP) > 4; minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.01; missing rate ≤ 20%. The resulting SNP set was annotated with SnpEff (version v4.3t) [47] to identify genomic locations, predicted amino acid changes, and other effects.

Population characteristics and genome-wide association study (GWAS)

Population characteristics of the 376 accessions were assessed by constructing a phylogenetic tree and analyzing population structure. A neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was generated in MEGA-X (version 10.2) [48] with 1000 bootstraps. Population structure was analyzed in Admixture (version 1.3.0) [49] with K values from 2 to 10, each tested with 10 different seeds and replicated 10 times. Cross-validation (CV) error was calculated for each K, and the K value with the lowest CV was selected as the optimal number of populations. Next, GWAS was conducted using the GAPIT (version 3) [50] R package with a mixed linear model (MLM). The MLM accounted for population structure and genetic relatedness by incorporating principal components (PCs) as fixed effects and a kinship matrix as a random effect (the PCA + K model). SNP markers with MAF < 1% and missing data > 5% were excluded. Significant marker–trait associations were identified at p ≤ 0.05. Results were visualized with Manhattan plots [–log10(p)] and quantile–quantile (QQ) plots. Using the Bonferroni correction, the significance threshold was set at p < 2.919 × 10 − 6 (0.05/n, where n = total markers; equivalent to –log10(p) = 5.53).

RNA-sequencing and transcriptome analysis

Six materials, including the control accession CW8 (susceptible to powdery mildew) and five local germplasm resources showing resistance, were selected to analyze transcriptome responses to powdery mildew. Each material was planted and inoculated at the fourth to fifth leaf stage with three replicates in a greenhouse (temperature: 15℃-25℃; photoperiod: 16 h light/8 h darkness; relative humidity: 50–70%). The pathogen was collected from pea plants in the experimental field, suspended in 0.01% Tween-20, and sprayed evenly onto the inoculated plants. Young leaves from the six materials were sampled at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after inoculation. For each time point, leaves from three plants were pooled, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80℃ for RNA-seq and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR).

Total RNA from each sample was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). RNA purity, concentration, and integrity were assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. Library construction and sequencing were performed with a VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. More than 230 Gb of paired-end reads were generated from 72 RNA-seq libraries. After filtering, high-quality reads were mapped to the reference genome (Pisum sativum v1a, EnsemblPlants) using TopHat2 in the HISAT2 (version 2.2.1) [51] package (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/index.shtml). Gene expression was quantified as fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments (FPKM). DEGs were identified with DESeq2 (version 1.4.5) [52] under thresholds of p < 0.05 and |log2FoldChange| > 1. Functional annotation of DEGs, including Gene Ontology terms and KEGG pathway enrichment, was performed with Blast2GO (version 3.0.8) [53] and the KEGG Automatic Annotation Server.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) validation

The expression of 12 randomly selected DEGs was validated by RT-qPCR. The cDNA was synthesized with the Maxima Reverse Transcriptase kit (EP0743, Thermo Scientific, USA). RT-qPCR was performed using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Amplifications were carried out with the 2X SG Fast qPCR Master Mix (B639271, BBI, Roche) on a LightCycler480 II system (Roche, Rotkreuz, Switzerland). The TubA1 (U12589.1) and GAPDH (U34988.1) genes of pea served as internal references. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method.
