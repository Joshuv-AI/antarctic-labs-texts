The role of deer keds (Diptera: Hippoboscidae: Lipoptena and Neolipoptena) in occupational and public health
Authors: Markus Braun, Doris Klingelhöfer, Dörthe Brüggmann, Isabelle Marie Kramer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeer keds (Diptera: Hippoboscidae: Lipoptena and Neolipoptena) are bloodsucking arthropods that primarily feed on deer (family Cervidae) but can occasionally bite humans. The knowledge of their potential harm to wildlife, livestock, and pets is fragmentary. These ectoparasites have been historically understudied, as they were only considered nuisances to various occupational groups and the public. However, there is also mounting evidence that they may be pathogen vectors as several infectious disease agents, including Acinetobacter, Anaplasma, Bartonella, Borrelia, Coxiella, Francisella, Mycoplasma, Rickettsia, Chlamydia-like organisms, Trypanosoma, Babesia, and viruses such as Sigmavirus lipoptenae and Thrombocytopenia Syndrome Virus, have been detected in deer keds. In this context, deer keds should be considered a highly probable vector for Bartonella schoenbuchensis. Persons at risk include anyone who travels or resides in the habitats of deer keds, primarily forests and their immediate surroundings in the Northern Hemisphere, whether for leisure or work purposes, particularly in temperate regions of Europe, Siberia, China, and North America. They also occur in Central and South America, the Russian Federation, South Korea, Thailand, Japan, Algeria, and Southern Spain. The ongoing rise in temperatures due to climate change is likely to favor the spread of deer keds, as it alters host distributions and increases their activity levels. Measures to prevent bites include avoiding areas with a high occurrence of deer keds, wearing protective clothing, including permethrin-treated clothing, and conducting a thorough body check for deer keds and bite sites after spending time or working in a risk area. Medical professionals should consider deer ked bites in their differential diagnoses. To ensure occupational and public health safety, it is crucial to educate and train at-risk workers (such as woodsman, foresters, employees of national parks, naturalists, farmers, and veterinarians) and those recreating in forests (such as walkers, hikers, hunters, or mushroom pickers) about deer ked bites, their symptoms, and possible zoonotic implications. Introduction

Blood-feeding arthropods, including ticks, mosquitoes, fleas, black flies, and lice, are the primary potential carriers of pathogens in both animals and humans. Nonetheless, other lesser-known biting insects may pose a risk to people and pets [1–3]. One such group is flat flies (Diptera: Hippoboscidae), which are ectoparasitic flies that suck blood from vertebrates. While most flat flies primarily feed on birds, deer keds in the genera Lipoptena and Neolipoptena primarily target deer and other members of the Cervidae family. Typically, deer keds are light to dark brown, measuring between 3 and 5 mm in length, and have a dorsoventrally flattened body shape [4, 5].

While there are 30 species of Lipoptena globally, only a few have been investigated, and their medical significance recognized (Fig. 1, Table S1) [6]. European deer keds (Lipoptena cervi) (Fig. 2) are distributed throughout most of Europe, as well as in Siberia, the Russian Federation, China, Korea, and the USA, where they were first detected in 1907 [6–9]. Lipoptena cervi is considered an “Old World dipteran ectoparasite” [9]. Lipoptena fortisetosa is native to the Eastern Palaearctic but has been introduced into Europe [6, 10]. In 2023, a new Lipoptena species, L. andaluciensis, was found in Southern Spain [11]. Besides, the neotropical deer ked (L. mazamae) occurs in the southeastern USA south through Central and South America, the western deer ked (L. depressa) and the Pacific deer ked (Neolipoptena ferrisi) both occur in the west and Midwest USA and in Canada [6, 7, 10, 12, 13].

Deer keds primarily host are deer species (family Cervidae), including red deer, white-tailed deer, moose, reindeer, and roe deer, as well as bovids, primarily goats. Additionally, they have been found on accidental hosts such as horses, dogs, and humans [12, 14, 15]. Adult deer keds are winged but are unable to fly longer distances ( > 50 m) in search of a host as well as putative accidental hosts like humans and dogs [6, 16]. They employ a sit-and-wait tactic, waiting for a host and flying from a close distance [16]. Adult deer keds shed their wings after host acceptance, meaning they cannot switch the host unless another suitable host is very nearby. Paradoxically, this also applies after accepting an accidental host [16].

Wingless adults live the rest of their lives in the coat of their host and regularly feed on the host’s blood. Between blood meals, it looks for a partner to reproduce. Female adults produce a single larva at a time, which develops inside the mother until it reaches full maturity. Soon after the mature larva is born, the pupation begins, and the pupa drops to the ground and develops into a winged imago. Each female deer ked is capable of producing about 20 to 30 pupae throughout its lifespan (Fig. 3) [17–19]. To date, the reproduction of deer keds on the accidental human host has not been reported [16]. However, deer keds can bite humans, preferably on the neck and scalp, and are considered an occupational hazard or at least a nuisance in rural areas of their range [12].

Life cycle of deer keds: The winged adults (1) fly to suitable hosts (2), primarily deer (Cervidae), but sometimes to accidental hosts (6), e.g., humans or dogs (no reproduction on the accidental host), where they shed their wings (3) before feeding on blood. Female adults produce a single pre-pupa (4), which then drops to the ground and develops into a pupa (5), remaining there until a new adult deer ked emerges (1) to seek out new hosts

Deer keds threaten wild animals, farm animals, and pets, creating a pathway for zoonotic pathogens [6, 20]. In humans, skin lesions, dermatitis, pruritic papules, and occupational allergic rhinoconjunctivitis have been reported [21–24]. Meanwhile, it is suspected that deer keds may be vectors for several zoonotic pathogens, such as Bartonella spp., Borrelia spp., or Rickettsia spp. to humans [12, 25, 26]. Due to the short time they feed on humans, the bites are often painless, leaving only minor marks at the bite site, and it can be difficult to associate them with later symptoms, such as skin lesions or infection caused by pathogens [27].

Climate change is influencing the host range and flight times of deer keds, altering the situations in which humans may be at risk. In Poland, for example, the exposure time to bites is from September to December, the period when adult deer keds are active in this area [28]. In Finland, where the time of activity is a little earlier (late summer and early autumn), there has been a growing number of Finns suffering from persistent and recurring dermatitis linked to deer ked bites since the 1970s [29]. Not only in Finland, hunters, mushroom pickers, and others who work in or frequent forests are particularly susceptible to accidental deer ked infestations [29]. Due to climate change and the resulting continuous temperature rise, which leads to changes in the host’s distribution and increasing activity, the spread of deer keds is expected to increase. Warmer years in the Northern Hemisphere prolong the growth season in higher latitudes and altitudes, leading to the possibility of the expansion of the host’s distribution towards the north and upwards. Additionally, warmer spring, summer, and autumn can prolong the development period as well as the host-seeking time of deer keds [20, 30–32]. The time of activity differs among species. For instance, the usual flight period of L. cervi is autumn, but that of L. fortisetosa is earlier in the year, so the species profit in various ways due to climate change [6, 33].

Despite recent interest, deer keds remain largely neglected [34]. This review summarizes the knowledge about the health hazards of deer keds, both for occupational groups and all those who come into contact with deer keds in their geographic range. Signs and symptoms of deer ked bites

Because deer keds feed on humans for a brief period, their bites may be painless, and the initial mark at the bite site is often minimal. Patients may therefore not connect their reported symptoms with deer ked bites. Understanding the skin lesions that occur after bites may be valuable in clinical practice in deer ked endemic areas [27].

A deer ked bite in humans may go unnoticed for a short time (up to a few hours) until the body responds, likely to sensitizing antigens in the saliva of the deer ked. Deer ked dermatitis is characterized by the presence of several to 20–50 red or pink papules, primarily found on the scalp, neck, and upper back. These papules typically emerge 6 to 24 hours after the bites [29]. According to a case report, the erythematous papules were irregularly shaped and scattered [29]. The itchy papules can last for at least eight weeks but may endure for as long as a year [28]. Within a few days after a bite, the bite sites may transform into firm, red welts, and a severe itch can last for several weeks or even months in some individuals [23, 29, 35]. Another well-documented case report of a bitten patient described that the first symptoms around the bitten area included urticarial red or pink patches, itching, and burning papules up to 25 mm in size on an erythematous base. The bite site of the deer ked was evident in the center of the lesions. Twenty-five hours after the bite, the papules became more uniform, though accompanied by a significant local allergic reaction. The skin lesions resulted in escalating burning pain with an inflammatory response. Although the inflammation subsided within five days, hyperpigmented papules remained for three months [27]. In addition to causing skin inflammation characterized by distinctive clots and itching, the skin lesions can also result in secondary bacterial infections through the wound [23, 35].

For clinical differentiation, in contrast to a deer ked bite, a tick bite produces an erythematous-infiltrated lesion that lacks clear borders, featuring a centrally located bite mark. A tick bite typically resolves within a few days [28]. In the case of Lyme disease by Borrelia burgdorferi, 3 to 30 days after a tick bite, a typical erythema migrans rash occurs most commonly [36].

Rhinoconjunctival allergy – an uncommon presentation

In 2005, a Finnish case report confirmed IgE-mediated occupational allergic rhinoconjunctivitis in a patient attacked by hundreds of deer keds [24]. The forestry worker developed typical symptoms, such as sudden episodes of sneezing, watery nasal discharge, and severe redness and itching of the eyes while working in regions with high deer ked activity. The authors described that the patient avoided most bites from the deer keds by wearing protective equipment. The eye symptoms could be triggered by hand-to-eye contact or by airborne particles (antigens from deer keds). Deer keds may release allergenic substances that function as outdoor aeroallergens, particularly in cases of significant exposure [24].

Although most allergic reactions to deer keds primarily present as skin issues due to probable sensitizing antigens in the salivary secretions, occasional occupational or incidental exposure may lead to allergic rhinoconjunctivitis [23, 24, 27]. Healthcare providers in endemic regions should be vigilant for these symptoms, especially in outdoor workers, and consider suitable diagnostic and preventive measures.

Pathogens of medical importance

Vectors are an integral aspect of the One Health concept as they facilitate the transmission of pathogens among wildlife, livestock, pets, and humans [3]. A distinction must be drawn between a biological vector, where the pathogen replicates or grows within the vector before transmission, and a mechanical vector, where pathogens are transferred between hosts externally or via contaminated mouthparts of the vector [37]. The vector competence of deer keds is not well known. Deer keds have been historically ignored by research, leading to a lack of study on their role as carriers of infectious agents to humans and domestic animals. That is partly because most species mainly affect wildlife, with few reports on domestic animals or humans. Consequently, our understanding of their biology, ecology, behavior, epidemiology, and vector competence is limited [38]. However, several infectious disease agents have been detected more frequently by molecular detection methods in deer keds over the last decade (Table 1).

Potential bacterial pathogens

Anaplasma spp.

Anaplasma spp. are obligate intracellular bacteria. DNA of Anaplasma spp., often identified as A. phagocytophilum, has been found in deer keds (L cervi, L. depressa, L. fortisetosa) collected from various cervids in Europe, Canada, and the USA [3, 12, 26, 39–47]. The detection of Anaplasma DNA in L. fortisetosa collected from Eld’s deer in a zoo in Thailand was reported for the first time in 2023, and collected from sika deer in Japan in 2025 [48, 49]. The vector competence of deer keds for Anaplasma spp. is potentially mechanical. The transmission of A. phagocytophilum to humans seems unclear and needs further research [39, 42, 49].

DNA of A. ovis, an intraerythrocytic pathogen of sheep, goats, and ruminants, was detected in L. cervi in Hungary [26]. Anaplasma ovis is linked to ovine anaplasmosis, with the most frequently seen symptoms being weakness, pale mucous membranes, and substantial weight loss [50]. In a patient, A. ovis or an A. ovis-like Anaplasma species was detected after a tick bite. However, A. ovis transmission to humans is not assured [51].

Anaplasma phagocytophilum (formerly known as Ehrlichia phagocytophilum) is the pathogen responsible for anaplasmosis in animals and human granulocytic anaplasmosis (HGA) [40, 52]. HGA is an emerging disease in the Northern Hemisphere with an estimated high number of undetected cases not reported by public health departments [53]. Patients with HGA have, after an incubation time of 5–21 days, non-specific febrile symptoms, often with headache, myalgia, and shaking chills, commonly presenting thrombocytopenia, lymphopenia, left shift (sometimes more than 50%), and mild to moderate transaminase activities. In rare cases, severe disease develops, leading to respiratory failure, coagulopathy, and a high risk of organ failure and death. Risk factors for severe disease are advanced age, delayed treatment, and a weakened immune system [54, 55].

Early-stage HGA can be laboratory-diagnosed in a peripheral blood smear, by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) methods, isolation of A. phagocytophilum in promyelocytic leukemia cell cultures, or an indirect fluorescent antibody test for A. phagocytophilum immunoglobulin G (IgG). The vague symptoms of HGA aggravate the differential diagnosis to various infectious or noninfectious diseases, including vector-borne zoonoses. Antibiosis with tetracycline doxycycline is the treatment of choice [53, 54].

Bartonella spp.

Bartonella spp. are gram-negative, facultative intracellular bacteria and commonly transmitted by bloodsucking arthropods. In several studies, Bartonella spp. have been detected in deer keds (L. cervi, L. fortisetosa, L. mazamae, Neolipoptena ferrisi) in several European countries, Thailand, Japan, and the USA [2, 3, 12, 25, 35, 41, 44, 56–72]. Deer keds are regarded as potentially effective vectors for specific Bartonella spp., particularly B. schoenbuchensis and B. bovis, due to molecular detection, evidence of transstadial and vertical transmission, and ecological associations. Lipoptena cervi is considered a potential vector for B. schoenbuchensis. That is, meanwhile, also suspected in the case of L. mazamae [2, 61, 62, 64, 68]. Reeves et al. found Bartonella DNA identical or very similar to the DNA of B. henselae in L. mazamae [60]. Nonetheless, further research is needed to provide direct experimental confirmation of their vector competence and a better understanding of their overall role in zoonotic transmission patterns. Zoonotic infections with Bartonella spp. caused by tick bites, have been reported [73].

It has been suggested that B. schoenbuchensis causes deer ked dermatitis in humans [29, 35]. At the bite site, the dermatitis manifests as a hard, reddened urtica within three days of the deer ked bite. The concomitant itching is intense and usually lasts for 14 to 20 days. Occasionally, an itchy papule may persist for as long as a year. In patients, the immunoglobulin E (IgE) serum level is often elevated [21–23]. Other Bartonella spp. are associated with certain human infections, among others, prolonged intra-erythrocytic bacteremia [74]. Bartonella quintana, for example, is the agent of trench fever [74]. Bartonella henselae is the pathogen of the cat-scratch disease in humans, a lymphadenopathy with fever, normally transmitted by a bite or scratch from a bacteremic domestic cat [75]. Bartonella henselae is also an agent for endocarditis, bacillary angiomatosis, and hepatic peliosis [74]. However, there is insufficient evidence directly linking deer keds to B. quintana or B. henselae. Usually, infections by Bartonella spp. proceed benignly. However, systemic involvement may occur in some patients. Atypical symptoms occur particularly in immunocompromised patients.

The antibiosis is not uncontroversial, and treatment should be associated with an accurate differential diagnosis [76].

Borrelia spp.

The spirochete (motile gram-negative bacterium) Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato is the pathogen of Lyme disease. Borrelia spp. DNA was detected in L. cervi and L. fortisetosa in Poland, the Czech Republic, and the USA [3, 12, 42, 45, 58, 66, 77]. In recent studies, Borrelia spp. DNA was detected in L. cervi in Canada and in L. fortisetosa in Japan [47, 49]. Deer keds often contain Borrelia DNA, including strains that can infect humans. However, there is currently no direct experimental evidence showing that deer keds are effective biological or mechanical vectors capable of transmitting viable Borrelia spp. to mammals. Their involvement in the epidemiology of Lyme disease has not been established and additional research is necessary to determine whether deer keds can transmit Borrelia spp. in natural settings or under controlled experimental conditions [47, 49, 58, 77].

Lyme disease is a tick-borne disease, as infected hard ticks (Ixodes ricinus complex) are usually responsible for transmitting the pathogen [78]. In most cases, Lyme disease is limited to a manifestation of the characteristic erythema rash with a median diameter of 16 cm (5 to 70 cm) around the bite site three to 30 days after the bite with virus-like illness symptoms (e.g., fatigue, fever, malaise, myalgia, headache). If left untreated. the disease can disseminate with multiple erythema migrans lesions, arthritis, and/or neurologic and cardiac manifestations [79].

Diagnosis of Lyme disease can be made by the identification of the typical erythema rash after a bite. Preferential laboratory tests are standard two-tier testing (STTT) with enzyme immunoassay (EIA) or immunofluorescence assay (IFA), or modified STTT using two EIAs [80]. Antibiosis with doxycycline is often the treatment of choice [79]. For additional information, we refer to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [81] or the German Robert Koch Institute [82] webpages.

Rickettsia spp.

Hematophagous arthropods, such as ticks and fleas, spread the obligate intracellular, globally occurring bacteria [83]. Rickettsia spp. DNA was detected in deer keds collected from cervids in Hungary, Poland, Italy, and the USA [3, 25, 26, 41, 44]. Certain studies indicate that deer keds could function as mechanical vectors of Rickettsia spp. [3, 25, 26, 44]. However, this does not equate to biological vector competence. Although deer keds often harbor Rickettsia DNA, there is currently no definitive proof that they are competent biological vectors. Additional research is necessary for a better understanding of their involvement in the epidemiology of rickettsial diseases [3, 25, 26, 44]. Deer keds may carry rickettsial endosymbionts, not necessarily infectious to mammals [26, 84].

Pathogenic Rickettsia spp. can cause Rickettsioses, clinically presenting as an undifferentiated febrile illness [83, 85]. Spotted fever group (SFG) rickettsioses, such as Rocky Mountain spotted fever (RMSF), Mediterranean spotted fever, and Siberian tick typhus, are generally transmitted by infected ticks, while typhus group rickettsioses, such as epidemic typhus, are transmitted by lice and fleas. The clinical manifestations of SFG rickettsioses vary from relatively mild (e.g., tick-borne lymphadenopathy by R. slovaca) to severe infections with a high fatality rate (RMSF by R. ricketsii). Infections manifest with fever, myalgia, headache, and, often in the case of RMSF, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain. Severe infections can show pulmonary involvement with cough and dyspnea up to respiratory arrest requiring ventilation, or prerenal azotemia with acute tubular necrosis requiring dialysis, or neurological disorder with seizures, delirium, or stupor up to coma [83, 85]. Risk factors for severe processes are older age, alcoholism, favism, and intake of sulfonamide antibiotics.

Accurate clinical identification of a rickettsial illness is crucial for effective treatment and appropriate medication, as certain antibiotics, such as penicillins, sulfonamides, and cephalosporins, are not effective against Rickettsia [83]. A prompt diagnosis at an early stage using the now available molecular diagnostic tests, and an early antibiotic treatment usually ensures recovery without complications. Unfortunately, serological tests often cannot identify the causative Rickettsia because, after the acute phase of the disease (7–10 days after infection), other cross-reactive antibodies are detected [85].

Coxiella spp.

Coxiella are tiny, obligate intracellular bacteria classified as Gammaproteobacteria [86]. Coxiella DNA was detected in L. fortisetosa in Poland and South Korea [3, 87]. DNA of C. burnetii was detected recently in L. andaluciensis in Southern Spain [11]. The ability of deer keds to act as biological vectors for Coxiella spp., particularly in natural transmission cycles involving mammals, remains unproven and needs more investigation. While it is possible that deer keds could function as mechanical vectors or incidental carriers, this has not yet been validated in controlled studies [3, 87].

Coxiella burnetii is the pathogen of the Query fever (Q fever), a worldwide zoonosis of mammals, birds, and arthropods, and can be transmitted airborne or by infected animals and bloodsucking arthropods like ticks [88]. Q fever is considered an occupational disease for all individuals who have contact with infected livestock or inhale contaminated dust or aerosols with infected animal products. It is also a potential bioweapon due to its extreme infectivity, airborne nature, and the broad spectrum of symptoms [88]. Q fever in humans shows varying clinical signs from asymptomatic or mild infection with flu-like symptoms up to acute severe disease with pneumonia and/or hepatitis with the risk of becoming chronic, resulting in, among others, endocarditis, vascular disease, chronic fatigue syndrome, premature birth, or abortion [89, 90]. While rarely fatal, the disease can be severely debilitating, even with treatment [90]. Q fever infections are more common in men and adults and are associated with an increased risk of endocarditis in patients with preexisting valvular heart disease [91].

Diagnosis can be made primarily by serological tests, but also by PCR-based assays or cell culture. Antibiotic treatment with doxycycline is recommended for symptomatic infections. In doxycycline intolerance, other antibiotics (minocycline, cotrimoxazole, fluoroquinolones) are an alternative. For asymptomatic infections, treatment is only recommended in the case of pregnancy [91].

Francisella spp.

Francisella are gram-negative, facultative intracellular bacteria [92]. Francisella DNA was detected in L. fortisetosa in Poland [3]. Francisella-like endosymbionts (FLEs) differ from the traditional Francisella tularensis pathogen. They have been found in various blood-feeding arthropods and exhibit strong genetic connections to pathogenic Francisella, but they generally possess inactive virulence genes. Additionally, endosymbionts are not inevitably infectious to mammals [84]. The role of deer keds is uncertain and likely limited to incidental mechanical transmission rather than active biological vectoring [3].

Francisella tularensis is the pathogen of tularemia or rabbit fever, a zoonotic arthropod-, food-, water-, and airborne disease [92]. Francisella tularensis is a potential bioweapon because of its airborne transmission and strong virulence, even caused by a low infectious bacterial load. Tularemia is mainly prevalent in the Northern Hemisphere and Australia. The pathogen is divided into three subspecies, types A, B, and C. Type A is responsible for more severe disease processes, type B (F. tularensis holarctica) for milder courses. Type C (F. tularensis mediasiatica) has not yet been detected in humans [92].

If the correct diagnosis is not made promptly, the mortality rate is up to 60%, with the poorest prognosis in pneumonic and typhoidal forms. For severe courses of tularemia, gentamicin is the first-line therapy, and for milder courses, tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones. To date, there are no vaccines against tularemia. Vector control and insect protection during outdoor activities are the most important preventive measures, alongside monitoring drinking water and ensuring that laboratory staff and medical professionals handle potentially infected clinical samples or autopsies with extreme care [92].

Acinetobacter spp.

Acinetobacter is a group of gram-negative bacteria frequently found in the environment, such as soil and water [93]. DNA of Acinetobacter was detected in L. cervi collected from hunted roe and fallow deer in Germany. One sample was confirmed as Acinetobacter baumannii DNA [2]. Based on this study, L. cervi is possibly a potential vector for Acinetobacter spp. [2]. However, the vector competence for Acinetobacter spp. is unproven and can currently be considered rather unlikely based on available data.

Infections attributed to Acinetobacter primarily arise in healthcare settings. Germs in the A. baumannii family are responsible for most Acinetobacter infections in humans, often referred to as the “Acinetobacter baumannii complex.” Acinetobacter baumannii can lead to infections in various areas, including the bloodstream, urinary tract, lungs (pneumonia), and wounds. In certain instances, individuals may harbor bacteria without being infected. Of particular concern is the ability of Acinetobacter spp. to accumulate various resistance mechanisms and the emergence of strains that are resistant to all commercially available antibiotics [93]. Consequently, the treatment of infections with Acinetobacter and the choice of agents depend on the degree of antibiotic resistance. Highly resistant Acinetobacter spp. poses a significant challenge to public health [94].

Mycoplasma spp.

Mycoplasmas are very small parasitic bacteria without a cell wall, and more than 200 species have been identified in humans, animals, arthropods, and plants. Mycoplasma DNA identified as M. erythrocervae and M. ovis was detected in L. fortisetosa in Poland [3]. No experimental or field studies have validated the vector competence of deer keds for Mycoplasma spp. to date [3].

Only a few species, mainly Mycoplasma pneumoniae, M. genitalium, M. hominis, M. penetrans, M. fermentans, M. pirum, and Ureaplasma urealyticum, can cause disease in the respiratory tract and urogenital system of humans and animals [95]. Haemotropic M. ovis is now recognized as an emerging zoonotic pathogen that is prevalent in sheep and goat-rearing regions of the tropics and subtropics, where the disease is almost endemic [96]. Human infections are very unusual but have been documented in pregnant women, immunocompromised individuals, and those in close contact with animals and arthropods [96, 97]. The number of reported cases is too limited to establish distinct clinical syndromes. However, co-infections with other zoonotic agents can complicate the outcomes [97].

Chlamydia-like organisms

Chlamydia-like organisms (CLOs) are a collection of gram-negative bacteria within the order Chlamydiales, which also includes the Chlamydiaceae family. The Chlamydiaceae include, among others, the well-known Chlamydia trachomatis (human pathogen of sexually transmitted infection, trachoma, neonatal pneumonia) and C. pneumoniae (bronchitis, pneumonia) [98, 99]. CLOs exhibit considerable diversity and have been identified in various environments and hosts, including protozoa, insects, reptiles, fish, and humans [77]. Chlamydiales are obligate intracellular bacteria characterized by their distinctive developmental cycle and their requirement to live inside host cells [100]. The identification of CLO DNA in the early developmental stages of L. cervi in the Czech Republic indicates the possibility of transgenerational transmission of CLOs [77]. It is still uncertain whether CLOs are actual pathogens or benign symbionts in deer keds. Additional research is needed to assess their viability, pathogenic potential, and the likelihood of transmission to deer or other hosts [77].

Some CLOs outside the Chlamydiaceae family, such as Parachlamydia, Waddlia, and Simkania, are associated with human diseases, just like respiratory diseases and miscarriage, most commonly involving epithelial cells [100]. As the detection of CLOs for diagnosis by classical cell culture, serology, or immunohistochemistry is frequently difficult and sometimes inconclusive, fast detection by molecular tests (PCR methods) is recommended [101]. Similar antibiotic treatment (Doxycycline, Azithromycin) as published in the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines for the treatment of C. trachomatis is recommended for CLOs [102, 103].

Protozoa detected in deer keds

Protozoa are unicellular eukaryotes with a nucleus, in contrast to bacteria, which are prokaryotic microorganisms lacking a nucleus [104]. Protozoa of interest from a human health perspective are parasitic and typically belong to the phyla Sacromastigophora and Apicomplexa [105]. To our knowledge, there is no confirmed evidence that deer keds directly transmit protozoan diseases to humans. Most protozoa found in deer keds are mainly harmful to animals, including livestock.

Trypanosoma spp.

Trypanosoma are flagellated parasitic protozoa, class Kinetoplastida, transmitted by hematophagous insects. The most relevant diseases in humans are sleeping sickness in Africa caused by T. brucei, which is transmitted by the Tsetse Fly, and Chagas disease, an endemic disease in the United States caused by T. cruzi, transmitted by the Triatomine “kissing” bug [106, 107].

Trypanosoma DNA was detected in both L. cervi and L. fortisetosa collected from different localities in Poland [108]. The authors assume that the DNA is from species that are harmless to humans, as only Trypanosoma have been found in Poland to date that are pathogens to European bison, cattle, roe and red deer, and water shrew [108]. In Venezuela, the DNA of Pan-American Trypanosoma trinaperronei n. sp. was detected collected from white-tailed deer [109]. In a 1991 study, trypanosomatids were found in the midgut and hindgut of L. cervi collected from red deer in Northern Germany. The authors concluded that L. cervi seems to be a potential vector of Trypanosoma (Megatrypanum) spp., which infects red deer [110]. Meanwhile, L.fortisetosa may be a potential vector for Trypanosoma spp. [108]. It appears that no Trypanosoma DNA pathogenic to humans has been detected in deer keds to date.

Babesia spp.

Babesia are parasitic protozoa belonging to the phylum Apicomplexa [111]. Molecular studies confirmed Babesia DNA in L. fortisetosa collected in Poland. It is highlighted that although deer keds may serve as biological indicators of pathogens in wildlife populations, their ability to act as vectors for Babesia spp. has yet to be demonstrated [3].

Human Babesiosis is an intraerythrocytic infection by Babesia and is usually transmitted by hard ticks in endemic areas and, less commonly, through blood transfusion with erythrocytes, perinatally, or organ transplantation [111, 112]. The infection ranges from subclinical symptoms to death. It can manifest with many symptoms, e.g., malaise, fatigue, usually fever with chills and sweats, myalgia, arthralgia, headache, cough, nausea, anorexia, occasionally sore throat, abdominal pain, vomiting, weight loss, conjunctival injection, photophobia, emotional lability, depression, or hyperesthesia. The spleen and liver can be enlarged. The severity of babesiosis is mainly determined by the patient’s immune status and the species of Babesia responsible for the infection, with B. microti being the most relevant. Besides, B. duncani and B. divergens (especially in Europe) are of concern. Severe cases of babesiosis that necessitate hospital admission are frequently in patients who have had a splenectomy, as well as in individuals with cancer, HIV infection, hemoglobin disorders, or chronic heart, lung, or liver diseases [111].

The gold standard for the diagnosis of babesiosis is still the microscopic pathogen detection. There are additional diagnosis techniques like digital microscopy connected with artificial intelligence (AI), immunodiagnostic, or molecular biological tests, such as PCR and Loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), to name a few [111, 113]. The preferred treatment for immunocompetent individuals with mild to moderate babesiosis is a combination of atovaquone and azithromycin. However, severely immunocompromised patients may experience persistent or recurring cases of babesiosis, even after following the standard 7 to 10 days of antimicrobial treatment. For these patients, achieving a cure typically necessitates a minimum of 6 weeks of antibiosis with high doses of azithromycin combined with atovaquone, including 2 weeks after the Babesia parasites are no longer visible on blood smears [111, 114].

Viruses detected in deer keds

Although viruses are studied less frequently in deer keds than bacteria, recent studies have identified both a new sigmavirus and the Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome Virus (SFTSV), expanding the range of known pathogens associated with deer keds.

The novel sigmavirus (Sigmavirus lipoptenae) was detected for the first time within the microbiome of L. cervi in Southern England through mass sequencing [71]. Sigmaviruses represent a distinct group of rhabdoviruses that primarily infect arthropods, especially Drosophila species [115]. It is also hypothesized that this virus may be capable of infecting vertebrates. However, additional research is needed to assess the risk of sigmavirus infection in vertebrates [71].

A 2025 study presented the first molecular evidence of SFTSV in L. fortisetosa gathered from wild ungulates in South Korea [116]. SFTSV is a tick-borne virus that poses significant risks to veterinary and public health [117]. This discovery indicates that deer keds might also be involved in its ecological dynamics.

SFTSV is a highly pathogenic virus that can lead to hemorrhagic fever in humans, with a mortality rate of up to 30% [116].

Risk assessment and recommendations for occupational safety

Persons at risk are all those who move or stay in the habitat of deer keds, whether for leisure or work purposes, mainly forests and the immediate surroundings at the Northern Hemisphere, commonly in temperate areas of Europe, Siberia, China, and North America, but also in Central and South America, the Russian Federation, South-Korea, Thailand, Japan, Algeria, and Southern Spain, to name the current known habitats [6, 7, 9–13, 47–49, 109]. The ectoparasitic deer keds can be found wherever their hosts, commonly cervids, are present [12].

Preventive measures can be similar to those for ticks, including avoiding seasons and areas with a high occurrence of deer keds, wearing protective clothing, including permethrin-treated clothing, and conducting a thorough body check for keds and bite sites after spending time or working in a risk area [79]. When selecting protective clothing, it is wise to ensure that it is suitable for the environmental conditions. For example, protective clothing can cause discomfort and problems when working at elevated temperatures [118]. Informing and involving employees in the selection process of protection measures can increase their acceptance [119].

A 2024 study found that clothing treated with permethrin is highly effective in killing deer keds soon after exposure. The median survival time for deer keds exposed to permethrin was roughly 35 minutes, with all keds succumbing within about an hour. The commercially available repellants Diethyltoluamide (DEET), Ethyl Butylacetylaminopropionate (brand name IR3535), oil of lemon eucalyptus, and Picaridin (aka Icaridin; brand names Bayrepal or Saltidin, e.g.) killed the deer keds more slowly. However, a repellent effect was lacking for all compounds tested [120].

A 2009 study ascertained that deer keds are more likely to land on dark and red clothing than on white. The authors inferred that wearing bright clothing seems to reduce the risk of attacks by deer keds. [16]. Another study from 2021 showed that L. fortisetosa preferred blue panels. Fewer flies were captured on the red, green, black, and white panels, while the yellow panels recorded the lowest number of captures [121].

Monitoring the occurrence of deer keds would be beneficial, but it is often challenging, as their primary hosts are wild animals [6]. An approach, involving the collection of adult insects either from their hosts or while they are in flight searching for hosts, can be useful; however, it is not always practical given the extensive host populations and their frequent migrations [12]. The occurrence of so-called “hotspots” of deer keds has been observed in Pennsylvania and Ontario (USA). It would be helpful to determine the prevalence of such “hotspots” and the environmental conditions that lead to their formation through further survey efforts [13]. To reduce populations in which deer keds pose a significant nuisance, population control methods such as the sterile insect technique (SIT), which is successfully applied to other arthropods, need to be further investigated in the case of deer keds [6, 122]. The treatment of the natural hosts with antiparasitic drugs, such as Ivermectin or Deltamethrin, is an evident method for reducing the deer keds population [123]. The economic sustainability of such a vector control strategy is certainly doubtful [6]. Deer keds are highly host-specific, relying exclusively on deer throughout their life cycle. Reducing the density of deer can lead to a decrease in available hosts, limiting the breeding opportunities for deer keds and consequently restricting their population [124]. Co-feeding of deer keds and other ectoparasites, e.g., ticks, on the same host may facilitate pathogen transfer. A low population density of ticks or a spatial separation of ticks and deer keds on host bodies reduces this risk [44]. As a result, controlling other ectoparasites also reduces the health risk posed by deer keds.

The vaccination against pathogens possibly transmitted by deer keds (Table 1) would be reasonable. However, as of 2025, there are only a few approved and licensed vaccines for human use against those pathogens. A licensed vaccine (Q-VAX®) targeting Coxiella burnetii is available. Q-VAX® is mainly utilized in Australia, where C. burnettii is endemic, for at-risk groups, such as livestock workers, abattoir staff, veterinarians, wildlife rehabilitators, and individuals who have contact with animal birthing materials, waste, or slaughter settings. However, its use worldwide is constrained by safety concerns regarding reactogenicity in individuals who have been previously exposed. Ongoing research aims to create safer vaccines that can be more broadly applied [125]. In the case of Lyme disease caused by Borrelia burgdorferi, a promising vaccine candidate named VLA15 is in a clinical trial phase [126]. For Rickettsia infections, research on vaccine candidates is ongoing, too [127].

For occupational safety, it is essential to raise awareness and provide training for at-risk workers (such as woodsman, foresters, employees of national parks, naturalists, farmers, and veterinarians) regarding deer ked bites, associated symptoms, and their potential zoonotic implications. Prompt removal of adherent deer keds and vigilance regarding skin reactions can help avert dermatitis. Medical professionals should include deer ked bites in their differential diagnoses in case workers exhibit skin or allergic symptoms or infectious diseases after exposure. In summary, a strategy that encompasses protective clothing, education, and clinical vigilance is the most effective way to manage the occupational hazards posed by deer keds [24, 27, 128].

Discussion

The current understanding of the impact of deer keds on wild animals is limited, and their significance in veterinary contexts is incomplete [3]. However, it is recognized that severe infestations of deer keds can lead to significant hair loss in moose, especially in Scandinavia. This hair loss can result in higher mortality rates during winter due to reduced insulation [129]. Infestations of deer keds are significant for reindeer farming, as they can influence the behavior and overall condition of the animals [130]. The vector competence of deer keds has not yet been confirmed but is meanwhile suspected, at least for some pathogens [12, 65].

Even at the beginning of the 21st century, the public health importance of hippoboscoid species was considered poor, as only a few affected individuals reported symptoms ranging from mild redness and swelling to severe pain or dermatitis at the bite site [131]. Not so long ago, a study assumed that only Bartonella and Anaplasma DNA had been detected in deer keds up to 2015 [20]. That is meanwhile out-of-date as recent studies have also detected DNA of Borrelia, Rickettsia, Coxiella, Francisella, Acinetobacter, Mycoplasma, Chlamydia-like organisms, Trypanosoma, Babesia, and Theileria (important for veterinary medicine) in deer keds [2, 3, 58, 77, 108, 132]. For example, Andreani et al. found a diverse array of bacteria in the pupae and both winged and wingless adults of L. fortisetosa, ranging from harmless endosymbionts to pathogens that pose a potential risk to human health [25]. In addition, two types of viruses have since been identified [71, 116]. The detection of DNA of pathogens in deer keds has increased significantly in recent years, likely due to several factors. The range of deer keds is expanding, partly due to climate change, which is also causing their hosts to spread, for instance, further north [20]. The detection methods of DNA of pathogens have become increasingly sophisticated, and the number of studies in this field has increased in recent years. This trend could be investigated more intensely in a bibliometric study.

It should be noted that the presence of putative pathogenic DNA in deer keds may correspond to endosymbiotic bacteria rather than pathogenic strains. Certain endosymbionts have close evolutionary ties to tick-borne pathogens such as Rickettsia parkeri and Francisella tularensis, which cause diseases in humans and animals. Endosymbionts are not necessarily infectious to mammals [84].

Since deer keds shed their wings upon locating a suitable host and remain there for the duration of their lives (approx. one year), the likelihood of transmitting a pathogen between hosts is considered to be relatively low [7, 108]. However, deer keds are meanwhile acknowledged as a significant group of blood-feeding insects with implications for both veterinary and human health. From a human health perspective, the most relevant are certainly skin lesions caused by deer ked bites and/or subsequent scratching, as well as the resulting infections. Beyond, it is discussed that deer keds are (more or less) likely capable of transmitting Bartonella spp., Borrelia spp., Acinetobacter spp., and Trypanosoma (Megatrypanum) spp., at least to cervids [2, 42, 65, 108–110]. Deer keds should be regarded as a highly probable vector for Bartonella schoenbuchensis, because the vertical transmission from wingless adult females to larvae of L. cervi was ascertained [41]. Additionally, B. schoenbuchensis has been demonstrated to inhabit the midgut of deer keds [35]. The ability to replicate within the arthropod is essential for deer keds to function as a natural reservoir host or vector. Multiple studies have detected Bartonella DNA in deer ked pupae, as well as in both pupae and adult winged deer keds [61, 62]. Furthermore, no Bartonella spp. were isolated from moose that were free of deer ked infestations [62]. These findings support the hypothesis that L. cervi serves as a vector for B. schoenbuchensis, with the bacteria capable of being transmitted transstadially. In contrast, a 2025 study diminished the potential vector capacity of L. cervi for B. schoenbuchensis, as the researchers found no significant increase in anti-B. schoenbuchensis IgG titers among German forest workers who are probably regularly exposed to deer keds, compared to those who do not work in forested areas [133]. Given that the symptoms associated with B. schoenbuchensis infections are not well studied, it could be valuable to systematically investigate whether B. schoenbuchensis transmitted by deer keds plays a role in infectious diseases affecting both animals and humans [2]. Given that multiple pathogens were found in not only the wingless adult deer keds that fed on their hosts, but also in their larvae, pupae, and winged adults (Table 1), all evidence for the possibility of transstadially transmission, it is not unlikely that that deer keds may serve as vectors for the one or other pathogen [3, 11, 41, 58, 61, 62, 77, 108]. Deer keds could potentially act as vectors for Coxiella burnetii, Coxiella-like bacteria (CLB), and Theileria spp., hemoparasitic protozoa that cause theileriosis in both wild and domestic animals, which are currently not identified as zoonotic [49, 87, 132, 134]. It must be said that the vector competency of deer keds for Anaplasma spp. and Rickettsia spp. remains unclear, and further field and xenodiagnostic studies are necessary [39, 41, 43]. Deer ked bites are frequently overlooked, and Anaplasma infections present with vague symptoms that can make diagnosis challenging [27, 53].

Recent information indicates that the potential for deer keds to act as mechanical vectors is not unlikely, particularly since they may transmit parasites between hosts through contaminated mouthparts, similar to other blood-feeding ectoparasites. However, the efficiency and importance of this transmission route have yet to be assessed [3, 49]. Additional research focusing on pathogen viability, host-switching behavior, and human exposure is crucial to evaluate the zoonotic risk [135]. In the meantime, deer keds are not believed to be major vectors compared to ticks. Ticks possess adaptations that allow them to stay attached to a host for several days to consume blood, whereas deer keds feed sporadically. They have been noted to take meals 15 to 20 times each day, consuming only small amounts of blood with each feeding [44, 136]. It is plausible that this feeding biology reduces the probability of pathogen intake or transmission compared to ticks.

Conclusion

Deer keds not only pose a significant nuisance to humans by limiting outdoor activities, but they also warrant attention due to potential health risks, as they may have a greater capacity for vectoring diseases than previously believed [38]. To ensure occupational and public health safety, it is crucial to provide education and training for at-risk workers and anyone who may encounter deer keds, regarding their bites, their symptoms, potential zoonotic risks, and preventive measures. An expanded monitoring of deer keds and related pathogens of public and animal health importance is necessary [57]. Due to climate change, deer keds are on the move to migrate to new regions, potentially raising occupational risks in previously unaffected territories [32]. If deer keds are confirmed to be effective carriers of pathogens, it will be crucial to determine their specific locations and the variations in their prevalence across their habitats. Additionally, understanding the frequency of deer ked “hotspots” and the environmental factors that contribute to the formation of these “hotspots” will require further survey research [13]. That serves to protect affected occupational groups and all those who spend time in the habitat of deer keds.

In conclusion, deer keds represent a significant yet often underestimated health issue for those working or tarrying in environments associated with deer keds, necessitating tailored preventive measures and increased awareness of exposure to those biting arthropods. There is a need for research in understudied parts of the world where deer keds occur and possibly bite humans, potentially transmitting diseases.
