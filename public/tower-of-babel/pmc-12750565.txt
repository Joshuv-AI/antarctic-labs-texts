Patterns of human-wildlife conflicts and management: insights from areas adjacent to Mid Zambezi protected areas
Authors: Jeremiah Chakuya, Roseline M. Chikerema, Patmore Ngorima, Innocent Mahakata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Human-Wildlife Conflicts (HWC) are common challenges in communal areas adjacent to protected areas. The objectives of the study were to: (i) determine the key depredated domestic animals over five years, (ii) determine the key wildlife species causing HWC, trends in conflicts and the nature of HWC and (iii) establish management measures employed to minimise HWCs. Secondary data was collected from seven (7) protected areas and three (3) rural district council areas’ annual reports. The data were obtained from field reports between January 2017 and December 2021. Goats (Capra aegagrus hircus) 512 (47%) and cattle (Bos taurus) 409 (38%) were the main domestic animals depredated by wildlife. The study showed that out of the five (5) species involved in the conflicts, the top two (2) species reported were lions (Panthera leo) and hyenas (Crocuta crocuta). To mitigate HWC in the study area, a multi-action approach has been proposed.

Introduction

Human-Wildlife Conflicts (HWCs) are as old as the co-existence of humans and wildlife in different landscapes [1–3]. HWCs are defined as interactions between humans and wildlife with negative outcomes on both sides [1, 2, 4]. HWC occurs in most continents where wildlife and humans interact and share the same landscape [5–7]. HWC can have direct and indirect impacts on communities living near protected areas. Direct conflicts occur when humans are injured or killed, while indirect conflicts involve wildlife causing damage to property, crops and livestock [8–11]. Communities living adjacent to protected areas are the most affected by such conflicts as they are on the frontline of the park-community interface [12–14]. Such communities suffer huge economic losses resulting mainly from property damages, livestock depredation, crop damages, human injury and deaths [5, 8, 13, 15].

Besides economic losses, HWCs are associated with terror in communities, psychological stress, fear of travelling and ultimately lead to reduced human activities and negative attitudes towards wildlife [13, 15, 16]. Continuous HWCs in communities result in negative attitudes toward wildlife and may result in retaliation through persecution [12, 17]. Human beings often retaliate against wildlife due to the losses incurred from hunting them for bush meat and destroying their habitat [5, 18]. This negative attitude towards wildlife has led to retaliatory killings of certain species, exacerbating conflicts in many cases [4, 19, 20].

The increase in HWCs is primarily caused by habitat loss and land fragmentation due to human population growth and encroachment into protected areas. This issue has been discussed in various studies [4, 11, 13, 15]. In these circumstances, understanding HWC patterns of HWC is critical in ensuring better management of the conflicts and reducing negative impacts on both communities livelihoods and biodiversity [12, 14, 21, 22].

Zimbabwe like most African developing countries continue to experience cases of HWC [4, 12, 14, 22]. Communities adjacent to protected areas and areas where agriculture is the mainstay of livelihoods are seriously affected by HWC [23–25]. In northern Zimbabwe, the land use of Mid Zambezi was planned for the protection of wildlife [26, 27]. The area comprises protected areas such as Mana Pools National Park, Charara, Hurungwe, Chewore, Sapi and Dande Safari Area [26]. Areas adjacent to these protected areas have the Communal Areas Management Programme for Indigenous Resources (CAMPFIRE) [21, 22, 26]. The major CAMPFIRE areas include Nyaminyami, Hurungwe, Mbire and they are administered by their respective Rural District Councils. CAMPFIRE was initiated by Zimbabwe’s Department of National Parks and Wildlife (Zimbabwe Parks and Wildlife Management Authority) with the aim to help manage HWC, mitigate disasters caused by HWC and correct discriminations in natural resources utilisation [23, 28]. In Zimbabwe, mitigating and resolving HWC through effective planning and management remains a top priority conservation agenda [5, 21, 28, 29].

Several measures have been attempted to mitigate HWC with varying levels of success [5, 13, 30]. One common method has been the killing of problem animals through Problem Animal Control (PAC) [2, 4, 8, 13, 15]. However, killing endangered problem animals may drive such animals to extirpation [2; 4; 24]). Translocation of problem animals has also been attempted but it has been observed that translocated animals may cause problems in their new areas where they are translocated [5, 21, 29]. Local authorities have made efforts to compensate victims and affected communities, primarily aiming to provide economic support and relief to the victims of HWC [3, 24, 31]. While compensation programs are mainly intended to promote tolerance and reduce retaliation against wildlife, they have been vulnerable to abuse through corruption as evidenced by [3, 23, 31].

Practical measures to mitigate HWC can be established through understanding the behaviour of wild animals and trend in these incidences. For instance, the behaviour of wild herbivores and predator may differ depending on the environment they inhabit as well as seasonal variations which defines the magnitude of the conflict. The Zambezi valley area is known because of its wildlife populations that occur in protected areas as well within the CAMPFIRE areas [5]. Around protected areas, human presence continue to increase while previous known CAMPFIRE areas, are being converted into farmland and settlements. With increased habitat loss and fragmentation, HWCs may also increase as a result of competition for space and resources. Additionally, livestock may also be killed by large carnivores while crop raiding is also eminent. Incidences of livestock losses through depredation has been recorded in Dande as a result of increased human-wildlife interactions [4, 5].

Given the intensity and magnitude of HWC, the study attempted to enhance our scientific understanding and knowledge of HWC patterns in communal areas adjacent to Mid Zambezi protected areas. The objectives of the study were to: (i) determine the key depredated domestic animals over five years, (ii) determine the key wildlife species causing HWC, trends in conflicts and the nature of HWC and (iii) establish management measures employed to minimise HWCs. Understanding the dynamics of HWCs is critical for improving response and management interventions, promoting co-existence and protection of wildlife [10, 18].

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in areas adjacent to Mid Zambezi protected areas (Fig. 1). Currently, the Zimbabwe Parks and Wildlife Management Authority (Zimparks) has eight administrative regions and the Mid Zambezi region is one of the second biggest regions after the Hwange region. Mid Zambezi region has seven safari areas (Charara, Hurungwe, Chewore, Dande, Sapi and Doma), one National park (Mana Pools) two recreational parks (Chinhoyi Caves and Kariba). The area study covers five districts (Hurungwe, Kariba, Karoi Urban, Makonde, Nyaminyami and Mbire).Fig. 1Mid Zambezi Valley protected areas and communities adjacent to protected areas

Mid Zambezi Valley protected areas and communities adjacent to protected areas

The study area is characterised by diverse land use which includes small-scale communal farming lands, resettled farming areas, medium and large-scale commercial farming areas, urban centres, safari and national park areas. The areas lie within agro-region III-IV with an annual rainfall of between 450 and 750 mm characterised by mid-season dry spells [26]. The area experiences high temperatures, with mean maximum and minimum temperatures ranging between 30 °C and 22.5 °C in October and July, respectively [26]. The soils are characterised by ferisialitic and pockets of lithosol soils and local farmers effectively grow maize (Zea mays), tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum), sugar beans (Phaseolus vulgaris), sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) and groundnuts (Arachis hypogaea). Vegetation varies from one area to the other, however, the dominant vegetation comprises of Miombo Woodland which is dominated by zebrawood (Brachystegia speciformis), mountain acacia (Brachystegia glaucescens), white seringa (Kirkia acuminate), Julbernardia globiflora, Sterculia quinqueloba, Brachystegia bohemii, Commiphora sp, Combretum sp [26, 27]. Farmers domesticate livestock such as cattle, goats, sheep (Ovis aries), chickens (Gallus gallus domesticus) and many others. The protected areas adjacent to communal areas are endowed with several wildlife species which include the elephant (Loxodonta Africana), leopard (Panthera pardus), lion (Panthera leo), buffalo (Syncerus caffer), Hyena (Crocuta crocuta), crocodile (Crocodylus niloticus), hippopotamus (Hippopotamus amphibius), baboon (Papio ursinus) among others [26, 27, 32]. The area has rivers with diverse species, birds, several reptiles and insects.

Data collection

The study obtained research ethics clearance from the Zimbabwe Institute of Wildlife Conservation (Ethics Committee). Rural District Council (RDC) Chief Executive Officers were informed of the planned research and the RDC helped to inform the Chiefs and Headman and their respective communities of the planned research. Before data collection, respondent’s consent was sought and responds who did not wanted to participate were allowed to leave or terminate the discussions without being victimised. All HWC were recorded daily at the stations (Safari areas and National parks) by rangers over five (5) years. Some HWC were reported to the respective Rural District Councils (RDCs) by the residents. The Zimparks officials at the respective stations collated the HWC data including data from RDC to the Mid Zambezi regional office as weekly, monthly, quarterly and annual reports. The collated on HWC included: (i) type of HWC, (ii) dates and time, (iii) location of HWC, (iv) wildlife species involved in HWC, (v) action taken to mitigate the conflict, (vi) losses incurred and other relevant information. In this study, we used the regional dataset of HWC records collated by Zimparks and respective RDC for the period covering January 2017 to December 2021. The main dataset used includes the daily HWC incidences related to livestock depredation, human depredation and injury, property damages and crop raiding. To validate the secondary data collected, a questionnaire was used to collect data on the extent of damages and common species involved in HWC. Twenty-five members of the communities from five districts were interviewed after getting clearance from the RDC, local chiefs and headman. Members of the communities were selected on the basis of their proximity to a protected area and participation was voluntary. Thirteen focus group discussions were conducted with RDC (5) and protected area (8) officials to provide valuable insights into the HWC from more experienced personnel. All the collected information was translated to Microsoft Excel sheets. The study applied the reductionist conceptual framework and this enabled reduction of big and complex issues into small compressed interactions [33]. In some cases HWC direct and indirect effects may be difficult to establish and understand the reductionist approach was used to identify the cause. The approach allowed assessment of the background of the problem and made an effort to relate the cause and its effects.

Data analysis

The data was analysed and summarised using descriptive statistics, such as frequency, means, graphs and tables. Descriptive statistics allows for HWCs data to be presented in a meaningful and understandable way for a simplified interpretation of the findings. Additionally, the method also facilitated the identification of data distribution, outliers, and similarities. In order to compare trend changes, a one sample t-test was conducted, determining whether the sample average differed from the known population average. A Pearson Correlation was used to examine the variable relationships using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20 for Windows (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). A p value ˂ 0.05 was considered significant. The statistical method effectively assessed the presence or absence of correlation between variables and provided precise insights into the extent of their correlation. Trend analysis was used to assess trends in HWC over a period of five years. A regression model generated trend line to indicate whether conflicts levels were increasing, decreasing or stable. The coefficient of determination (R2 values) quantified the strength of temporal trends.

Management measures employed to minimise HWCs

Rural District Councils and Zimparks applied different techniques to manage HWCs in both communal areas and protected areas. Non-lethal techniques were dominantly used for the total number of HWCs reported (n = 1 288; 65 %) to manage HWCs. Non-lethal means varied from firing warning shots, driving wildlife out of the communities, capture and translocation, fencing of fields and communities, awareness and education campaigns, guiding the fields and kraals and management of encroachment of both domestic animals and humans. In extreme cases, lethal means (n = 1 288; 35%) were used to manage HWCs. Lethal means involved the physical elimination of wildlife species as problem animals.

Discussion

The study suggests that although HWC levels fluctuate widely, the overall average remains statistically consistent with long term patterns. The study indicates persisten underlying drivers of conflict that remains stable over time, such as blockage of wildlife corridors, poor human livelihood activities and poor land use planning. This study revealed that lions were the primary cause of livestock predation, resulting in the loss of 324 cattle. A total of 409 herds of cattle were lost over 5 years due to predation from different predators. Three hundred and twenty-four herds of cattle would translate to a substantial financial loss of US$245,400 at US$600 per herd for the farmer. Currently, Zimbabwe does not have a compensation policy and such loss of revenue by communal farmers contributes to poverty. Besides that, lions had the highest number of kills on donkeys and goats of communal farmers’ livestock. Donkeys are a source of draught power whereas goats are a source of protein and income for communal farmers. A study by Patterson et al. [7] conducted between two neighbouring ranches adjoining Tsavo East National Park in Kenya also reviewed the same notion that lions were responsible for 86% of livestock attacks and the remaining were by hyenas and other predators. In this study, hyenas were the second most common livestock predator. The study also highlighted the challenges of human encroachment into protected areas and the straying of livestock which remain as one of the major causes of conflicts. In Sengwa, research has also established that livestock left out of bomas at night and human settlement encroachment into the protected areas also worsens the incidence of HWC [34, 35].

The decline in prey within protected areas, coupled with drought and ongoing loss of natural habitats and biodiversity may have caused carnivores to move into communal areas in search of prey and water thus causing more HWC incidents [16, 26, 36, 37]. On the other hand, poor animal husbandry practices could have contributed to increased predation. Livestock that strays and wanders into protected areas are vulnerable to predation and in some cases, some farmers do not have appropriate infrastructures (kraals/pens) to protect their livestock from predatory threat [36, 38].

Trends in wildlife-inflicted depredation on domestic animals showed diverse patterns over five years. The variations in the extent of livestock depredation may have been influenced by several different factors. An increased interaction mainly as a result of wildlife encroachment into communal lands or human and livestock intrusion into protected areas often results in high depredation [23, 36, 39]. Predators mainly stray into communal lands in search of prey, water, suitable habitats and territorial expansion [3, 26, 31]. In most cases, these factors are driven by seasonal variations and such seasonal variations are also associated with wildlife straying into communal areas thus increasing HWC. Similarly, findings by [34] and [35] established that the incidence of livestock depredation varies with season and area. In addition, variations in awareness campaigns and preparedness by communal farmers to protect their livestock annually affect livestock depredation levels. Some farmers tend to relax in safeguarding their livestock, especially during the dry season.

Generally, the findings imply stability in human injury rates, with no significant increase or decrease relative to comparison mean. Humans were mainly attacked by lions, elephants and crocodiles. Incidents involving lion attacks occurred when lions strayed and encroached into communal lands. In some cases, humans were attacked by lions while doing illegal activities within protected areas [2, 16]. People were exposed to crocodile attacks while drawing water from rivers, dams and Lake Kariba. Fishing either subsistence or commercial exposed many fishermen to crocodile attacks. Fishing is regarded as a quick way of earning income and in a country with high unemployment, more people are involved in informal and illegal fishing practices [4]. The habitat for crocodiles will continue to be constrained due to increased encounters between crocodiles and humans [2, 4]. Generally, there were more injuries caused to humans per year than deaths. Since 2019, there has been an increase in cases of human injuries and deaths as a result of HWC, indicating a pressing need to intensify efforts to manage these conflicts. Besides the elimination of wildlife, there is also a great need to increase educational awareness. Human attitudes, negligence could be contributing to the high number of conflicts, emphasizing the necessity for education to foster co-existence.

The study observed a significant increase in wildlife elimination as a result of HWC. The number of wildlife eliminated could not tally with the domestic animals lost due to HWC. It was noted that in some instances, PACs are driven by the desire for meat of certain species, while in some cases, they are done to appease communities affected by HWCs-related losses. Apart from that, there are no clear guidelines or protocols to be used in deciding when to kill an animal after causing damage [40]. The common assumption is that if wildlife causes human fatality it would be killed in retribution [36, 41]. However, it is not clear on the type and number of livestock depredation, the extent of crop loss and property damages before a decision could be made to kill a problem animal and what numbers to be killed for each incidence. In some cases, a pride of lions may terrorise communities and kill livestock, one would need to decide whether to kill the whole pride or kill the leading lioness [36]. HWC management has many subjective matters which need a good sense of judgment. However, in this study, PAC was perhaps done to maintain cordial relationships with communities and ensure the sustenance of CAMPFIRE. This could have forced Zimparks or the RDC to resort to the lethal method thus causing increased numbers of wildlife eliminated than domestic animals depredated. In the absence of clear guidelines on PAC in HWC management, the use of the lethal method will continue to be the most politically sound method of handling HWC incidents. Concurrently, there is a need to implement non-lethal methods to mitigate significant wildlife depredation. The use of non-lethal methods can be more effective in sustainable wildlife conservation. However, the outcome can be influenced by how the affected community collaborates with the authorities responsible for controlling the problem [42].

In this study, not all reported cases of HWC were attended. Attendance to reports of HWC was above 50%. Generally, the norm is all HWC reports should be attended by either Zimparks or the respective RDC with the appropriate authority in the land. The more they delay or fail to attend to reported cases of HWCs, the greater the threat to the communal people’s livestock, crops, and property or their lives will be in danger. In some cases, failure or a delay in attending to HWC incidences, communities will end up using their unorthodox means of PAC and some wildlife will be killed through retaliation killings [2, 13]. To reduce such delays, a wide range of approaches can be developed to manage HWC and these include establishing response teams to be stationed in the communal areas and wildlife monitors who help to alert the community about the presence of wild animals.

Human-wildlife coexistence is both a lived philosophy and a set of daily practices in communities that lives with wildlife [43]. Probing coexistence from the perspective of people who live with wildlife reveals how coping in woven into social life at multiple system levels. In this case, the iceberg model gives a simple and practical way of understanding Human-wildlife coexistence and the underlying causes and challenges [43].What we commonly observe is the tip of the iceberg, which is, supported deeper patterns, structures, and mental models (much of them beneath the surface).

The iceberg model clearly explains the human-wildlife coexistence experienced in areas adjacent to wildlife areas [43].The event level reveals what most people notices. In this case, it was clear that communities domestic animals and wildlife had a significant effect on their livelihood through predation. Crops, infrastructures were destroyed and in some cases humans were killed by wildlife. Such events triggers emotions, fear, anger, sadness and attract immediate attention from communities and wildlife management officials [43]. In most cases, wildlife management at this level tends to be reactive and action such as fencing, capture and translocation or compensation are recommended as the solution [44.] Such short response are important, however treating only events risks repeatedly treating symptoms instead of preventing recurrences [43, 44].

The pattern level of the iceberg model reveals recurring trends of human-wildlife conflicts. As the model explains, besides the recorded events, same recorded challenges of crop raids, livestock depredation, infrastructure and human lives loss develops predictable trends and patterns [43, 44]. For example, dry season livestock depredation. Recognising patterns allows wildlife managers to forecast where and when human-wildlife conflicts are likely and design preventative measures such as targeted fencing, seasonal night watches among others [43, 45]. This approach enables avoiding responding only when there is a crisis.

In this study, since 2019 reports of HWCs were on the rise and respective authorities had to scale up their efforts to attend to such reports to manage such conflicts. In many cases, HWC management is associated with high costs in terms of labour, fuels, ammunition, in some cases capture and translocation. Due to the high costs involved, some reports of HWC are not attended thus causing communities to live with a burden of wildlife [2, 7, 30]. The situation has been worsened by the constrained operation of the CAMPFIRE as the communities do not realise any benefits of wildlife but more problems. Resuscitation of CAMPFIRE or the development of Community Conservancies will help to reduce cases reported as problems as communities begin to derive benefits from what were previously perceived to be problems. The structure level of the iceberg model explains the causes of patterns. Repeated livestock depredation often results from a combination of factors such as (physical) lack secure kraals and straying livestock, organisational factors such as (policy) land use zonation and compensation rules and (habits) ingrained practices of letting cattle freely roam especially during the non-farming season [43]. In addition, predators requires suitable space, water, shelter and prey availability to maintain their viable populations. Predators such as lions, leopards and hyenas need extensive home ranges which in most cases overlaps with communal lands where livestock graze. As the natural prey declines due to poaching, competition, habitat degradation predators population may increasingly target domestic animals to meet their needs. This ecological pressure, often invisible to most local communities is a major component of the submergerd part of the iceberg.

The surge in human-wildlife conflict in the study area may have been significantly influenced by the impact of drought. Between the 2018–2019 seasons, the Mid Zambezi region faced a severe drought [26] prompting wildlife to venture out of protected areas and into nearby communal areas in search of sustenance [9, 12, 19, 26]. As drought mitigation measures, some CAMPFIRE areas constructed dams, introduced feed and salt licks as feed supplements for wildlife and this could have helped to increase wildlife populations in CAMPFIRE areas at the same time bringing the problem close to the people. Since 2020, instances of HWCs have been on the rise in communities adjacent to protected areas. Management must assess water distribution in both CAMPFIRE areas and water in communal areas to ascertain if this may be a key factor attracting wildlife from protected areas into communal areas [26]. Drought in communal areas increases poverty and many people go into protected areas to look for fruits, honey, nuts, vegetables and poaching of wildlife to sustain their livelihood.

Following the iceberg model, the mental-model level states the beliefs that sustain the system. The shared beliefs, values, expectations and assumptions makes the structures appear to be normal [43]. In most communities, there is a tragedy of the common when it commons to wildlife. Some communities believe that wildlife should either be removed from their areas or be killed and be given as food. Such subconscious narrative determines what people accept, resist or prioritise in human-wildlife conflict management.

Conclusion

The study reviewed HWCs which are widespread in most areas adjacent to protected areas in Africa. Based on the study, we conclude that goats and cattle in communal areas adjacent to protected face a high threat of depredation. Lions and hyenas were found to be the primary predators responsible for numerous HWCs. The main HWCs prevalent incidents involved depredation of livestock and attacks on humans resulting in fatalities. To manage HWCs and associated losses, Rural District Councils and ZimParks have employed several loss-alleviation measures which include the use of lethal and non-lethal means. The intensity of HWCs has created an inhospitable environment for both humans and wildlife and the study proposed a multi-action approach to mitigate HWCs. The study recommended: (i) appropriate land use planning which zones communal areas from protected areas, protection of wildlife corridors and control of human encroachment, (ii) resuscitation of wildlife buffer zones, removal of hard boundaries created by human encroachment and (iii) increase in awareness campaigns, promotion of co-existence projects to support communities affected by HWCs.
