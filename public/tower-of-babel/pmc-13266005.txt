Mapping the multi-domain allosteric network for CKAMP44 modulation of AMPA receptors
Authors: Pratibha Bharti, Shantanu Visal, Rajesh Vinnakota, Janesh Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Most fast excitatory neurotransmission is mediated by AMPA-type ionotropic glutamate receptors, whose gating properties are fine-tuned by a wide range of auxiliary subunits. While bulky, multi-pass auxiliary subunits such as transmembrane AMPAR regulatory proteins (TARPs) and cornichons (CNIHs) dominate current structural models of receptor regulation by forming a stabilizing girdle around the ligand-binding and transmembrane domains, the modulatory mechanisms of simpler, single-pass transmembrane proteins like cysteine-knot AMPA receptor-modulating protein 44 (CKAMP44), also known as Shisa9, remain a critical missing link in understanding allosteric control. To identify the molecular drivers of CKAMP44 regulation, we utilized our previously characterized library of reciprocal chimeric receptors containing swapped domains between the CKAMP44-sensitive AMPAR subunit GluA2 and the CKAMP44-insensitive kainate receptor GluK2. Using whole-cell patch-clamp electrophysiology, we demonstrate that CKAMP44 modulation is not localized to a single interface but relies on a distributed allosteric network. The findings indicate a novel and essential role for the receptor’s amino-terminal domain (ATD) in facilitating the CKAMP44-mediated enhancement of desensitization entry, thereby pinpointing a new site for gating regulation. Furthermore, we find that CKAMP44 functionally rescues previously non-conductive transmembrane-carboxy-terminal chimeras, highlighting a critical role in stabilizing compromised receptor assemblies. Conversely, the modulation of receptor’s recovery from desensitization appears mechanistically distinct, governed primarily by the stability of the ligand-binding domain and transmembrane (TMD) interface. We propose a hierarchical dual-action model of allosteric regulation wherein the canonical gating machinery of the ligand-binding and transmembrane domains cassette is distinctly fine-tuned by distal interactions at the ATD, establishing a newly identified regulatory layer for auxiliary subunit engagement.

In the central nervous system, the precise timing and strength of excitatory neurotransmission are essential for learning, memory, and synaptic plasticity. This mechanism is controlled mainly by α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid-type ionotropic glutamate receptors (AMPARs), which are tetrameric ligand-gated ion channels that open a non-selective cation-permeable pore upon glutamate binding (1).

Although the genome encodes only for AMPAR subtypes, extensive functional diversity results from alternative splicing, RNA editing, and most importantly, the fact that they are modulated by a wide range of auxiliary subunits (1, 2, 3, 4, 5). These auxiliary proteins co-assemble with the core AMPAR tetramer to fundamentally alter its kinetics, biosynthesis and trafficking to its precise gating and pharmacological characteristics at the synapse (6, 7, 8). The trans membrane AMPAR regulatory proteins (TARPs) (9, 10, 11, 12), cornichon homologs (CNIHs) (13, 14, 15) and germ cell-specific gene 1-like (GSG1L) (16, 17) auxiliary proteins are well characterized and have been shown to form a girdle around receptor’s ligand-binding domain (LBD) and transmembrane domain (TMD) layers, thereby altering channel kinetics and pharmacology. However, this "girdle" model, predicated on extensive transmembrane contacts utilizing four TM helices, may not apply to single-pass auxiliary subunits.

The cystine-knot AMPA receptor-modulating protein (CKAMP) family, especially CKAMP44 (also called Shisa9), is a structural and functional puzzle. CKAMP44 is a single-pass transmembrane protein with a unique extracellular N-terminal cystine-knot domain and an intracellular C-terminus with a PDZ-binding motif (18, 19, 20, 21). CKAMP44 has been demonstrated to influence synaptic transmission by expediting AMPAR desensitization, significantly delaying recovery from the desensitized state, and modifying short-term synaptic plasticity (20, 22). These unique modulatory effects imply a mode of action that is markedly different from those of other auxiliary subunits. Classical auxiliary modulation frequently operates via a "bipartite interaction" model, wherein intracellular domains anchor the complex to scaffolds (such as PSD-95 or PICK1) while extracellular loops modulate gating kinetics. While the intracellular PDZ interactions of CKAMP44 have been documented (18, 20, 23), the precise extracellular domains on the AMPAR that facilitate these kinetic interactions are yet to be identified. Previous biochemical investigations utilizing deletion mutagenesis have established that a highly conserved, ∼20-amino acid arginine-rich sequence located immediately downstream of the CKAMP44 transmembrane domain is an absolute structural requirement for physical tethering to the GluA1 subunit (20). However, whether this juxtamembrane anchor is sufficient for kinetic modulation, or whether distal structural elements are required to transmit allosteric force, remains a critical unresolved question.

This study utilizes a robust methodology based on chimeric receptor engineering to delineate the molecular determinants of CKAMP44 modulation (24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29). Building upon our recent work characterizing a complete library of 14 reciprocal chimeric receptors between the AMPAR subunit GluA2 with the kainate receptor subunit GluK2 (30), we systematically mapped the functional footprint of CKAMP44. In this context, this verified toolset is utilized to examine the impact of co-expression with CKAMP44 on the functionality of each chimera. The primary idea is that by methodically replacing GluA2 domains with their CKAMP44-insensitive equivalents from GluK2, the important regions needed for modulation can be functionally separated. We report that unlike TARPs, which primarily engage the ligand-binding and transmembrane domain (LBD-TMD) interface, CKAMP44 also requires GluA2 amino-terminal domain (ATD) to modulate the rate of desensitization, while simultaneously acting as a chaperone to rescue functional gating in structurally compromised chimeras.

Results

To systematically delineate the regions of the GluA2 receptor necessary for functional regulation by CKAMP44, each previously characterized GluA2/GluK2 chimeric receptor (30) was co-expressed with CKAMP44 in human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293) cells. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were utilized to assess critical gating and permeation characteristics, including the desensitization rate, the recovery time from desensitization, and the current-voltage (I-V) relationship. Here, we accurately measured CKAMP44's effects on each construct by directly comparing the test outcomes with the baseline features of the chimeras expressed alone. These control data were obtained from our previously reported recordings performed under identical conditions (30). The GluA2 amino-terminal domain is a key determinant for CKAMP44-mediated modulation of desensitization

Initially, we examined the modulatory effect of CKAMP44 on the rate at which receptors entered desensitization upon 100 ms application of 10 mM glutamate. In line with earlier studies, co-expressing CKAMP44 with WT GluA2 slightly accelerated desensitization (Figs. 1A and S2), lowering the mean weighted time constant τdes from 5.8 ± 1.23 ms (n = 9) to 4.90 ± 0.56 ms (n = 5) (Table 1). Furthermore, we observed an opposing trend when measuring the mean τdes of GluA2 in the presence of 1 mM AMPA (mean weighted time constant τdes = 4.88 ± 0.68 ms; n = 10), where desensitization was notably slower compared with the condition in which CKAMP44 was co-expressed (mean weighted time constant τdes = 7.03 ± 1.30 ms; n = 4) (Table 1; Fig. S1A). The investigation of CKAMP44 modulation on A2(K2ATD) chimera, which substituted the ATD of the GluK2 receptor into that of the GluA2 receptor, yielded a remarkable finding. This one-domain change completely abolished CKAMP44's ability to accelerate desensitization (Figs. 1B and S2). The τdes for chimera A2(K2ATD) co-expression with CKAMP44 was 6.09 ± 0.92 ms (n = 7), which is not significantly different from its baseline of 6.49 ± 1.62 ms (n = 10) without CKAMP44 (Table 1). This finding is the first functional evidence indicating that the native GluA2 ATD is necessary for CKAMP44-mediated regulation of the transition into the desensitized state. This gives us a glimpse into the significance of ATD as an effector site for AMPAR auxiliary subunit interaction, which has not been observed till date.

Conversely, the reciprocal K2(A2ATD) showed no significant effect in glutamate-evoked desensitization 5.13 ± 1.10 ms (n = 5) and co-expression CKAMP44, 4.57 ± 0.55 ms (n = 6)), suggesting that although the ATDs play a role in CKAMP44 modulation of receptor kinetics, it is not the sole region of interaction, thereby failing to produce the expected functional effects in the GluK2 background (Figs 1C and S2). This suggests that while the ATD acts as a vital allosteric wedge, the kinetic output requires mechanical continuity with the required juxtamembrane binding determinants located at the LBD-TMD interface. However, significantly enhanced desensitization was observed in presence of 1 mM kainate in K2(A2ATD) co-expressed with CKAMP (τdes = 3.72 ± 0.21 ms; n = 5) compared with K2(A2ATD) alone (τdes = 6.25 ± 1.59 ms; n = 7) (p = 0.0058) (Table 1; Fig. S1C). Interestingly, the CKAMP44-induced acceleration of desensitization was abolished in the A2(K2ATD) chimera when measured in 1 mM AMPA when compared to its reciprocal K2(A2ATD), further highlighting the essential contribution of the ATD to CKAMP44-mediated modulation. Under these conditions, the mean weighted desensitization time constants were τdes = 6.17 ± 1.08 ms (n = 8) for A2(K2ATD) and τdes = 6.86 ± 0.77 ms (n = 6) under CKAMP44 co-expression, confirming the loss of CKAMP44-dependent enhancement (Table1; Fig. S1B).

ATD as a key regulator of recovery kinetics in CKAMP44-Mediated modulation

Analysis of the chimeric constructs revealed that the structural determinants governing recovery kinetics are distinct from those controlling desensitization. Chimera A2(K2 ATD), which showed no CKAMP44-dependent modulation of desensitization, was nevertheless strongly affected in its recovery behavior. Co-expression with CKAMP44 slowed recovery from desensitization by 2.77-fold, increasing the recovery time constant (τrec from 31 ms (27–37; n = 8) to 88 ms (67–110; n = 7)) (Table 2; Fig. 1, E and H). This functional dissociation indicates that the ATD is primarily required for controlling the rate of entry into desensitization, rather than determining the stability or exit kinetics of the desensitized state. Additionally, the its reciprocal chimera K2(A2ATD) does not alter the recovery kinetics co-expressed with CKAMP44. Recovery time constants were 730 ms (620–850; n = 5) for K2(A2-ATD) alone and 990 ms (790–1200; n = 6) when co-expressed with CKAMP44, indicating only a very small effect on recovery (Table 2; Fig. 1, F and I).

To validate this gating behavior, we generated a GluA2(ΔATD) construct by deleting residues 1 to 377 of the mature polypeptide (31). Consistent with prior reports (32), removing the ATD elevated steady-state currents (Fig. 2, A–C). While mean weighted τdes was unchanged, the steady-state/peak ratio was significantly increased, suggesting that CKAMP44 alters the equilibrium of desensitization without affecting the rate of entry into the desensitized state. Structurally, the native GluA2 ATD exists in a dynamic equilibrium between a tightly cross-linked compact state and a highly flexible splayed state (33, 34, 35, 36). In a WT receptor, the tightly associated ATD dimer-of-dimers exerts a mechanical constraint on the LBDs, physically forcing conformational tension downward into the TMD to facilitate rapid channel closure. Excision of the ATD relieves this upper physical constraint, fundamentally altering the energy landscape of the gating transition. Notably, ΔATD receptors recovered rapidly from desensitization (complete recovery within 100 ms), whereas CKAMP44 markedly slowed recovery (Fig. S4, A and B), suggesting that CKAMP primarily stabilizes desensitized states without affecting the rate of entry into desensitization when the ATD is absent. The persistence of CKAMP modulation in ΔATD receptors suggests that CKAMP mainly acts through the LBD–TMD gating machinery to stall recovery. While high-resolution empirical structures of the AMPAR–CKAMP44 complex remain elusive, our AlphaFold multimer predictions suggest that CKAMP44 extends far enough to reach the ATD-LBD interface (Fig. S5). Specifically, the maximal vertical extension of the CKAMP44 extracellular domain measures approximately 87.5 Å from the juxtamembrane boundary. Given the known height of the AMPA receptor extracellular domains (approx. 120 Å), this vertical span provides absolute biophysical feasibility for CKAMP44 to project beyond the LBD and directly wedge into the ATD recess. This strongly implicates direct physical contacts at the ATD level as an absolute requirement for CKAMP44 to stabilize the active pre-desensitized conformation.

To better approximate native synaptic assemblies, we extended our electrophysiological recordings to tripartite complexes comprising the receptor, CKAMP44, and a TARP-γ8.

Interestingly, while the difference in desensitization kinetics between WT-GluA2 + CKAMP44 and the ATD-swapped chimera (A2(K2ATD)) + CKAMP44 was non-significant in isolation, the co-expression of a TARP revealed a striking functional divergence. Within this tripartite assembly, the ATD-swapped chimera exhibited significantly faster desensitization kinetics (40.09 ± 13.21 ms, n = 14) compared to the WT counterpart (59.92 ± 14.25 ms, n = 7; Two-way ANOVA: ATD swap p = 0.0305, TARP co-expression p < 0.0001, interaction p = 0.0157) (Fig. 2, D–F). This remarkable shift demonstrates that the functional impact of the GluA2 ATD extends beyond isolated CKAMP44 modulation, playing a critical role in mediating the complex allosteric crosstalk when multiple auxiliary subunits are engaged. We hypothesize that TARP- γ 8 and CKAMP44 likely establish competing mechanical anchor points at the base (LBD-TMD) and apex (ATD) of the receptor, respectively. By rigidifying the native ATD, CKAMP44 likely creates a state of high structural tension that partially counteracts the stabilization conferred by the TARP girdle. When the native ATD is substituted with that of GluK2, this upper anchor is lost, allowing the TARP- γ8 kinetics to dominate.

Together, these findings identify the GluA2 ATD as an important determinant of CKAMP44-mediated modulation of AMPA-type glutamate receptor gating. Replacing the native GluA2 ATD with that of GluK2 was sufficient to abolish CKAMP44’s ability to enhance the desensitization, suggesting that the GluA2 ATD plays an essential role in the rate at which receptors enter and exit the desensitization state. In contrast, the reciprocal chimera, in which the GluK2 ATD was replaced with the GluA2 ATD, showed slight modulation with CKAMP44 under saturated glutamate activation but regained a clear modulatory effect under kainate, indicating that ATD-dependent coupling is to the ligand environment. However, the fact that CKAMP44 continued to slow recovery in A2(K2ATD) suggests that while the ATD gates the "entry" modulation (desensitization), the "exit" (recovery) is governed by downstream domains.

The S2 lobe and transmembrane domains govern desensitization stability and surface rescue

The critical role of the S2 lobe and Transmembrane (TM) domains became evident in subsequent swaps. For Chimera A2(K2S2), the control receptor produced unstable seals and exhibited pronounced rundown, limiting data quality. Therefore, we restricted our measurements to desensitization kinetics. CKAMP44 co-expression rescued functional surface expression, revealing a rapid desensitization phenotype (τdes = 2.07 ± 0.33 ms; n = 4) and a substantial Glutamate/AMPA ratio of 23.21 ± 4.0; n = 4 (Fig. 4; Table 1). Importantly, this rescue allowed us to resolve recovery kinetics (τrec = 2.054 s (1.81–2.32); n = 4) (Table 2), indicating a stable desensitized state. When the substitution was extended to both lobes in Chimera A2(K2S1S2), the entry into desensitization remained rapid and insensitive to modulation (Control τdes = 3.37 ± 1.21 ms vs. +CKAMP44 τdes = 3.15 ± 0.61 ms) (Fig. 5A; Table 1). This pattern of stabilization was further corroborated by Chimera A2(K2TM), where the TM swap rendered the receptor non-functional in isolation, yet CKAMP44 rescued activity with a recovery time constant of 446 ms (330–600); n = 4 (Table 2). GluK2 backbone locks desensitization speed but allows modulation of recovery

In sharp contrast to the A2 backbone, the GluK2 backbone exhibited a dominant kinetic signature that largely occluded CKAMP44-mediated modulation of desensitization entry, while retaining sensitivity in recovery kinetics. Chimera K2(A2S1) displayed rapid desensitization kinetics (τdes = 2.28 ± 0.63 ms; n = 11) that were refractory to CKAMP44 modulation (τdes = 2.01 ± 0.52 ms; n = 5) (Fig. 3E; Table 1). This resistance extended to kainate-evoked responses, where τdes and the Glutamate/Kainate amplitude ratio (Control: 3.1 ± 1.39 ms; +CKAMP44: 3.43 ± 1.14 ms) remained virtually unchanged (Table 1). Unexpectedly, however, CKAMP44 exerted a differential effect on recovery in this chimera; rather than the typical slowing observed with AMPARs, CKAMP44 accelerated recovery by roughly 30%, reducing τrec from 780 ms (540–1180) to 480 ms (400–580), representing a 1.6-fold change in the opposite direction to established models (Fig. 3, F and G). The introduction of the complete GluA2 LBD in K2(A2S1S2) further highlighted the decoupling of gating entry and exit. Despite possessing the native GluA2 binding pocket, the receptor’s desensitization rate remained dictated by the K2 backbone (Control τdes = 9.38 ± 1.96 ms; +CKAMP44: 10.84 ± 1.76 ms). However, CKAMP44 induced a dramatic collapse in relative agonist efficacy, reducing the Glutamate/AMPA current ratio from 23.21 ± 4.0 in controls to 1.67 ± 0.29 (Table 1) in the presence of the auxiliary subunit. Furthermore, the presence of the S2 lobe in this context restored the canonical slowing of recovery, with τrec increasing 2.62-fold to 170 ms (140–210) (Table 2; Fig. 5, D and F). This stabilization effect was maximized when the transmembrane domains were substituted in Chimera K2(A2TM); CKAMP44 extended the already slow intrinsic recovery (τrec = 3.604 s) by nearly 3-fold to 10.44 s (7.2–17.8) (Table 2). These results reveal a fundamental dissociation: the GluK2 backbone structurally imposes a desensitization rate limit that CKAMP44 cannot overcome, yet the stability of the resulting desensitized state remains highly tunable via interactions with the transplanted LBD and TM interfaces.

The C-terminal domain actively regulates desensitization states

To isolate the specific contribution of the disordered C-terminal domain (CTD) to gating, we compared two ATD-swap chimeras that differ only in their C-terminal tails. The control construct, Chimera A2(K2ATD), retains the native GluA2 CTD, while Chimera K2(A2S1S2/TM) is identical except for the substitution of the GluK2 CTD. Replacing the ATD alone (A2(K2ATD)) caused a minor slowing of desensitization (τdes = 6.49 ± 1.62 ms; n = 10) compared to WT-GluA2 (5.8 ± 1.23 ms; n = 9), suggesting the GluK2 ATD slightly stabilizes the open or pre-desensitized conformation (Table 1).

However, incorporating the GluK2 CTD (K2(A2S1S2/TM) had a much stronger impact, prolonging desensitization to 12.80 ± 3.18 ms (n = 7). This indicates that the CTD is not merely a passive tether but an active participant in the energy landscape of gating, resisting the transition into the desensitized state (Table 1). Notably, this regulation appears limited to entry kinetics; once desensitization occurred, the stability of the state was unaffected by the tail composition, as recovery kinetics were comparable between the A2-tail (τrec = 31 ms; 27–37) (Table 2) and K2-tail constructs (τrec = 30 ms; 20–48) (Table 2).

We observed a distinct divergence in how CKAMP44 modulates these backgrounds. In the A2(K2ATD) chimera, where the native GluA2 CTD is present, CKAMP44 had negligible impact on desensitization (τdes = 6.49 ± 1.62 ms; n = 7). In contrast, for Chimera K2(A2S1S2/TM), CKAMP44 effectively "rescued" the slow kinetics driven by the GluK2 tail, accelerating desensitization back to 5.86 ± 1.20 ms (n = 6) (Table 1; Fig. 6). A plausible mechanism is that CKAMP44 interacts with transmembrane or linker regions in a manner that is dynamically coupled to the GluA2 CTD, which in turn stabilizes conformations associated with the open or partially desensitized state. The intrinsically disordered nature of the CTD may allow transient and flexible interactions with cytoplasmic linkers or scaffolding proteins, providing a structural conduit for allosteric influence. Replacement of the GluA2 CTD with the GluK2 counterpart likely disrupts this finely tuned coupling, weakening communication between the auxiliary subunit and the gating machinery and resulting in accelerated desensitization. Overall, these findings highlight that the GluA2 CTD is not merely a passive structural appendage but an active determinant of auxiliary subunit efficacy, shaping AMPAR gating dynamics through domain-specific allosteric coupling.

In our previous study, we identified that chimeras combining the transmembrane domain (TMD) of GluK2 with the C-terminal domain (CTD) of GluA2 (Chimera A2(K2TM)) were non-functional, indicating a strict requirement for structural compatibility between the TMD and CTD (30). Interestingly, when Chimera A2(K2TM) was co-expressed with the auxiliary subunit CKAMP44, we observed robust glutamate-evoked currents, indicating functional gating rescue of this previously inactive construct (Table 1, Fig. 7A). The rescued receptor exhibited desensitization kinetics (τdes = 4.25 ± 0.21 ms, n = 3) and a recovery time constant of 446 ms (range: 330–600 ms, n = 4) (Tables 1 and 2). This unexpected result suggests that CKAMP44 may compensate for the allosteric or assembly incompatibility between the GluK2 TMD and GluA2 CTD. CKAMP44 does not alter desensitization of chimera K2(A2TM) but enhances kainate-evoked responses

Swapping the GluA2 transmembrane domain (TMD) into the GluK2 backbone (Chimera K2(A2TM)) had no significant effect on desensitization kinetics compared to the parental receptors, as previously reported (τdes = 4.97 ± 1.86 ms, n = 6; p = 0.873 versus GluA2; p = 0.4728 versus GluK2 (30)). When co-expressed with the auxiliary subunit CKAMP44, Chimera K2(A2TM) showed a similar mean weighted desensitization time constant (τdes = 3.9 ± 1.22 ms, n = 3, p < 0.0001) (Table 1), indicating that CKAMP44 does not further affect desensitization under saturating (10 mM) glutamate conditions. This was unexpected, as CKAMP44 usually interacts with AMPARs through their transmembrane segments, affecting desensitization and recovery kinetics.

Interestingly, while glutamate-evoked desensitization remained unaffected, co-expression of CKAMP44 markedly potentiated kainate-evoked responses. In the absence of CKAMP44, 1 mM kainate elicited low-amplitude currents, whereas CKAMP44 co-expression significantly enhanced the peak current amplitude (Table 1; Fig. 7, C and D). This potentiation suggests that CKAMP44 can stabilize the open conformation, even when its effect on desensitization is minimal. Such selective enhancement of kainate responses highlights a modulatory role for CKAMP44 that extends beyond direct gating kinetics, potentially involving conformational stabilization of the TMD bundle in hybrid receptor context.

Discussion

This research utilized a systematic domain-swapping approach to generate the first functional map of the interactions between the auxiliary subunit CKAMP44 and the AMPA receptor subunit GluA2. We identify three distinct modes of CKAMP44 action: (1) ATD-dependent modulation of desensitization entry, (2) LBD/TMD-dependent stabilization of the desensitized state (recovery), and (3) a chaperone-like rescue of structurally mismatched TMD-CTD interfaces. The findings elucidate a novel, unrecognized function of the ATD in regulating desensitization, illustrate the state-dependent characteristics of recovery modulation, and reveal a significant, dormant allosteric connection to the ion channel pore. These findings challenge existing models of auxiliary subunit function and provide a new framework for understanding the unique biology of CKAMP44.

A multi-domain framework for CKAMP44 interaction

Our chimera data support a model wherein CKAMP44 engages the AMPA receptor through a distributed, multi-point interaction network that is structurally distinct from other known auxiliary subunits (Fig. S5). Previous studies attempting to map this complex primarily focused on the CKAMP44 footprint itself (20). While those efforts successfully highlighted the extracellular globular cystine-knot and the cytosolic PDZ-binding motif as critical for AMPAR interaction (forming a classical bipartite interaction module), our approach provides the complementary perspective by identifying the corresponding regulatory regions on the receptor. Most notably, we establish the GluA2 ATD as a primary gatekeeper governing desensitization kinetics. The complete loss of CKAMP44 modulation upon exchanging or deleting the ATD (Chimera A2(K2ATD)) indicates that this domain either forms a direct binding interface or, more likely, serves as an essential allosteric scaffold required for CKAMP44 to influence the downstream LBD-TMD gating core. Recent structural data indicate the GluA2 ATD oscillates between a compact and splayed state (37, 38). We propose that the extracellular cystine-knot of CKAMP44 likely acts as an allosteric wedge that restricts the splaying of the ATD local dimers, locking it in a compact state. This downward mechanical force forces the receptor to rupture its LBD interface more rapidly upon ligand binding, manifesting as accelerated desensitization entry. Beneath this, the LBD, particularly the S2 lobe, appears to be a primary locus for modulating the stability of the desensitized state. The sufficiency of the GluA2 LBD to confer sensitivity to recovery modulation (K2 (A2S1S2)) combined with CKAMP44’s ability to modulate chimeras lacking the native ATD (A2(K2ATD)) and CTD (Chimera K2 (A2S1S2/TM)) for this parameter point to the LBD as a central interaction hub. This reveals a bifurcated dual-action mechanism whereby the distal cystine-knot dictates the speed of desensitization entry via the ATD, while the juxta membrane helical stalk of CKAMP44 physically restricts the LBD-TMD linkers. Once desensitized, the stalk likely acts as a clamp, trapping the receptor in the desensitized conformation, thereby drastically prolong the speed of recovery. Finally, the potent effects on the TMD in certain chimeras (e.g., K2 (A2TM)) indicate that the conformational changes induced by CKAMP44 binding can propagate across the entire receptor to control the ion channel gate, with this allosteric communication being dependent on the integrity of native domain interfaces.

The ATD as an innovative locus for auxiliary subunit regulation of gating

One of the most striking findings to emerge from this work is the crucial role of ATD in orchestrating CKAMP44-mediated desensitization. While classical auxiliary subunits, such as TARPs and Cornichons, are well known to form a "girdle" tightly restricted to the LBD-TMD interface, our data strongly suggests that CKAMP44 may be able to extend its structural reach significantly higher up the receptor complex.

Moreover, the absolute loss of CKAMP44-driven modulation in the A2(K2ATD) and A2(ΔATD) implies that the native GluA2 ATD is far from a passive distal appendage. Rather, it also participates actively in transmitting auxiliary signal to the gating core. This functional reliance makes perfect sense when considering the unique, extended cysteine knot of CKAMP44. We believe that this extracellular motif physically projects into the inter-ATD-LBD recess, acting as an allosteric wedge that dictates the receptor's kinetic state.

The importance of the ATD became even clearer when additional auxiliary proteins were involved. In receptor complexes containing GluA2, CKAMP44, and TARP-γ8, replacing the GluA2 ATD with the ATD from GluK2 noticeably changed desensitization behavior. This indicates that the ATD participates in the allosteric interactions that emerge when multiple auxiliary subunits modulate the receptor.

Overall, these results show that the GluA2 ATD is a key factor in CKAMP44-mediated modulation and support a model in which interactions between extracellular domains help control AMPA receptor gating.

Revealing hidden allosteric pathways with chimeric receptors

A significant advantage of the chimeric technique is its capacity to uncover emerging phenotypes that are not predictable from the parent molecules. The fact that CKAMP44 can either slow down recovery from desensitization (in WT-GluA2) or accelerate it (in K2(A2S2)) or stop ion flux altogether (in K2 (A2TM)) are strong examples of state-dependent regulation. These findings suggest that the functional output of auxiliary subunit binding is not an intrinsic, fixed property of the subunit itself. Instead, it is an emergent property of the entire receptor-subunit complex, critically dependent on the baseline conformational energy landscape of the receptor. By systematically altering this landscape, the chimeras serve as unique conformational probes that unmask the full dynamic range of CKAMP44's modulatory potential. This highlights the exquisite allosteric integration within iGluRs and suggests that in the native synaptic environment, the effect of CKAMP44 may be subtly tuned by factors that alter receptor conformation, such as subunit composition or post-translational modifications.

Mechanistic model of rescue and bipartite signalling

The ability of CKAMP44 to rescue Chimera A2(K2TM) (GluA2 LBD + GluK2 TMD + GluA2 CTD) is particularly illuminating. In the absence of CKAMP44, the mismatch between the GluK2 TMD and GluA2 CTD creates a non-conductive channel (28). CKAMP44 likely bridges this interface, acting as a molecular splint that stabilizes the coupling between the pore and the intracellular tail, thereby enabling channel gating. This supports the bipartite interaction model, suggesting that the transmembrane and intracellular domains of CKAMP44 coordinate with the receptor's CTD to provide a stable anchor, which is a prerequisite for the extracellular cystine-knot to properly orient and exert force on the ATD.

Limitations of the study

While the chimeric receptor approach is undeniably powerful to isolate specific functional domains, we must acknowledge its inherent structural constraints. Swapping out large receptor domains can sometimes introduce non-native conformations or potentially disrupt the more subtle intra- and inter-domain interactions that naturally occur in the WT complex. However, to mitigate the risk of interpreting folding defects as functional modulation, our analytical framework explicitly utilizes the "chimera alone" recordings as the baseline for every condition. By comparing the chimera + CKAMP44 directly to the chimera without the subunit, rather than comparing it back to WT GluA2, we ensure that any reported kinetic shifts are specifically due to the allosteric influence of the auxiliary subunit, rather than the structural stress of the domain swap itself. Moreover, given the sheer size and allosteric complexity of these receptors, this comparative strategy remains one of the viable ways to pinpoint which specific domain or lobe dictates a particular gating behavior.

Additionally, our experiments were conducted in HEK293 cells. Although this is a standard and robust heterologous system, it lacks the complex environment of a native neuronal and synaptic environment, that consists of several other interacting proteins, specific lipid compositions, endogenous scaffolding proteins, and neuron-specific post-translational modifications that might fine-tune CKAMP44-AMPAR coupling in vivo. That being said, utilizing HEK293 cells was a deliberate and necessary trade-off as it provides minimal background that allowed us to confidently correlate the observed kinetic shifts to our co-expressed CKAMP44, completely free from the confounding variables of native neuronal auxiliary subunits.

Finally, our mechanistic interpretations are fundamentally based on functional modulation, essentially assessing the gain or loss of CKAMP44 sensitivity - rather than direct biochemical binding assays like co-immunoprecipitation (presented in earlier studies (20)) or direct structural resolution. Moreover, our AlphaFold predictions (Fig. S5) suggest the CKAMP44 extracellular stalk possesses a theoretical maximum extension of ∼87.5 Å which is sufficient to physically engage the ATD, we acknowledge the limitations of computational modelling for intrinsically disordered sequences. While functional gating is ultimately the physiological output corresponding to synaptic transmission, these kinetic insights will eventually need to be paired with high-resolution structural data to map the exact physical footprint of the CKAMP44 interaction.

Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrate that CKAMP44 engages the AMPA receptor via a hierarchical allosteric network. A novel role for the amino-terminal domain is identified as a key determinant for the modulation of desensitization. This work redefines the mechanistic framework for CKAMP44 function, and highlights and highlights the ATD as a viable target for pharmacological intervention.

Experimental procedures

Receptor constructs and plasmids

The design, cloning, and expression of WT GluA2 (unedited at the Q/R site, ‘flip’ splice variant), and the chimeric receptor constructs in the pCDNA3.1 vector have been described previously, and the chimeric receptors were also used in our earlier published work (30). CKAMP-44 was cloned into the pEG-BacMam expression vector. Δ ATD GluA2 was generated from the WT GluA2/pCDNA3.1 construct as described above.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293 cells (passage 20–35) were cultured on glass coverslips in 35-mm polystyrene dishes. For whole-cell electrophysiology, cells were transiently co-transfected using Xfext reagent (Clontech). Each dish received 1 μg of the designated receptor construct DNA, 0.2 μg of a GFP reporter plasmid, and 1 μg of vector carrying the complete cDNA for CKAMP44. For GluA2 ΔATD and TARP γ8 experiments, 30 μM NBQX was added to the culture medium approximately 8 h after transfection to minimize excitotoxicity and maintain cell viability.

Electrophysiology

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were conducted 24 to 48 h post-transfection. Currents were recorded using a HEKA USB 10 amplifier, with cells held at a constant potential of −60 mV, unless stated otherwise. Data were acquired using Patchmaster software (HEKA; https://www.heka.com/downloads/downloads_main.html#down_patchmaster), which also controlled an ultra-fast perfusion system consisting of a two-barrel theta pipette mounted on a piezoelectric device (Multichannel Systems).

Recording pipettes were pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries (1.5 OD × 1.17 × 100 mm; Harvard Apparatus) using a Sutter P-1000 puller and fire-polished on a microforge to a final resistance of 2 to 3 MΩ. Pipettes were filled with an intracellular solution containing (in mM): 30 CsCl, 100 CsF, 4 NaCl, 10 Hepes, 5 EGTA, and 0.5 CaCl2 (pH adjusted to 7.2 with NaOH; 90–300mosmol/L). During recordings, cells were continually perfused (0.2 ml/min) with an extracellular solution (ECS) containing (in mM): 150 NaCl, 2.8 KCl, 10 Hepees, and 0.5 CaCl2 (pH adjusted to 7.3 with NaOH; 295–305mosmol/L).

After establishing the whole-cell configuration, cells were lifted from the coverslip and positioned near the theta pipette interface for rapid solution exchange. One barrel contained the control ECS, while the other contained ECS with the specified ligand (10 mM L-glutamate, 1 mM AMPA, or 1 mM kainate). For all chimeric receptors, WT GluA2, and GluK2, desensitization kinetics were measured using 100 ms ligand applications. Current decays for both kinetics were fitted in pClamp 10.3 (Molecular Devices) using a two-term exponential function (standard method, variable metric).

Current-voltage relationships

Current-voltage (I-V) relationships were determined by applying voltage ramps from −100 mV to +90 mV during agonist application. The rectification index was calculated as the ratio of the current amplitude at +90 mV to the current amplitude at −90 mV (rectification index = I(+90mV)/I(−90mV)).

Data analysis

Raw data files (.itx) were exported from Patchmaster and converted to.abf format using ABF Utility (Molecular Devices) for analysis in pClamp 10.3.

Kinetics

The macroscopic rate of desensitization (τdesensitization) was determined from the exponential fit of the current decay from 90% of its peak amplitude (Ipeak) to baseline. For desensitization kinetics is fitted with two-term exponential function of the form:I(t)=A1e−t/τ1+A2e−t/τ2Where, A1 and A2 represent the relative amplitudes, and τ1 and τ2 are the fast and slow time constants, respectively.

The weighted time constant (τw) was calculated as:τw=(A1·τ1)+(A2·τ2)A1+A2

Recovery

Recovery from desensitization was quantified by measuring the time constant (τ) obtained from single-exponential fits to the recovery current traces recorded using paired-pulse stimulation at varying interpulse intervals. The recovery ratio (P2/P1) was plotted as a function of interpulse interval and fitted with the equation.I(t)=I∞(1−e−t/τ)where I(t) represents the fractional recovery at time t, I∞ is the maximal recovery, and τ is the time constant of recovery. By definition, τ corresponds to the time required for approximately 63% of the current to recover to the pre-desensitized amplitude.

For each construct, recordings were performed both without and with CKAMP44 co-expression. To ensure full compliance with transparent data reporting standards, all quantitative graphical data are presented as bar graphs with superimposed scatter plots to display individual independent biological replicates. Error bars represent the SD of the mean, denoting the variability of the recorded parameters. Mean τ values and their 95% confidence intervals were determined using profile-likelihood estimation in GraphPad Prism (version 8). To quantify the modulatory effect of CKAMP44, a fold change in τ was calculated as:FoldChange=τ(+CKAMP44)/τ(Control)

A fold change greater than 1 indicates that CKAMP44 slowed recovery (increased τ), whereas a fold change less than 1 indicates faster recovery kinetics. Statistical significance was assessed primarily using unpaired, two-tailed Student's t-tests to compare mean kinetic values between control and +CKAMP44 conditions, or by comparing profile-likelihood 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance between two test groups indicated in Figure 2, D–F was determined using two-way ANOVA. Exact p-values and standardized significance thresholds (e.g., ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001) are explicitly defined wherever applicable.
