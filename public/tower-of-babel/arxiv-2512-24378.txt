Implicit score matching meets denoising score matching: improved rates of convergence and log-density Hessian estimation
Konstantin Yakovlev, Anna Markovich, Nikita Puchkin

Abstract
We study the problem of estimating the score function using both implicit score matching and denoising score matching. Assuming that the data distribution exhibiting a low-dimensional structure, we prove that implicit score matching is able not only to adapt to the intrinsic dimension, but also to achieve the same rates of convergence as denoising score matching in terms of the sample size. Furthermore, we demonstrate that both methods allow us to estimate log-density Hessians without the curse of dimensionality by simple differentiation. This justifies convergence of ODE-based samplers for generative diffusion models. Our approach is based on Gagliardo-Nirenberg-type inequalities relating weighted L 2 L^{2} -norms of smooth functions and their derivatives.

Q1

Can a score estimator trained with an implicit score matching objective escape the curse of dimensionality in the noisy setup (for instance, as in
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a

Besides score estimation, there is also an important line of research devoted to inference with generative diffusion models.

Q2
:
Can we estimate the score Jacobian matrix without the curse of dimensionality?

In what follows, we give a positive answer to this question. Moreover, we show that it is enough to take derivatives of denoising and implicit score matching estimates for this purpose.

Our contribution.
In this paper, motivated by the aforementioned research questions, we investigate the properties of feedforward neural networks with smooth activation function
(
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
)
in estimating both the score function and its Jacobian matrix without the curse of dimensionality under the noisy setting as in
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
.
Our main contributions are summarized below.

1.

We demonstrate that implicit score matching can surpass the curse of dimensionality and achieve the same rates of convergence as denoising score matching (in terms of the sample size).

2.

We further show that the Jacobian matrix of the true score function can be estimated without the curse of dimensionality by differentiating the outputs of either denoising or implicit score matching.

3.

Our main results rely on extensions of the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality for the space
L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})
.
The first inequality reveals favourable geometry of the implicit score matching loss landscape and results in sharper rates of convergence.
The second inequality links
L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})
-norm of a smooth function with its Sobolev seminorm
W^{1,2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})
and yields an upper bound on the estimation error of the score Jacobian matrix.

4.

As a byproduct, we develop a tail inequality for suprema of unbounded empirical processes, building upon
Adamczak 2008
and
Bartlett et al. 2005
.
Our new machinery extends the standard localization technique to the unbounded setting, thereby eliminating the need for an
\varepsilon
‑net argument and simplifying the analysis while maintaining the sharpness of the resulting convergence rates.

\displaystyle W^{k,p}(\Omega)=\{f\in L^{p}(\Omega):\partial^{\mathbf{k}}f\in L^{p}(\Omega)\quad\text{for every ${\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{r}$ with $|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant k$}\},

Implicit score matching.

The

Implicit score matching aims to minimize

\displaystyle\min_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{E}\,\|s(Y)-s^{*}(Y)\|^{2},

Since

\ell(s,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\|s(Y)\|^{2}+{\rm div}[s](Y).

(5)

Under regularity conditions (satisfied in our setting), integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\|s(Y)-s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\ell(s,Y)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\|s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}

Consequently, for all

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\|s(Y)-s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\ell(s,Y)-\mathbb{E}\ell(s^{*},Y).

Given i.i.d. samples

\displaystyle\widehat{s}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(s,Y_{i})\right\}.

(B_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
are independent Wiener processes on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
By construction,
Z_{T}\sim\mathsf{p}^{*}
.
Therefore, to draw samples from
\mathsf{p}^{*}
, one can first sample
Z_{0}\sim\mathsf{p}_{T}^{*}
and then model the backward dynamic (
9
).
Since we do not have access to the
score function

\nabla\log\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(y)
, it must be estimated.
For each timestamp
t>0
, this is achieved by minimizing the empirical version of the score matching loss:

\displaystyle\min_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{DSM}}\mathbb{E}\,\|s(X_{t})-s_{t}^{*}(X_{t})\|^{2}.

Here,
s_{t}^{*}(x)=\nabla\log\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(x)
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{DSM}
denotes the class of score estimates at the considered timestamp.
As in the case of implicit score matching, the score function
s_{t}^{*}
is unknown.

Vincent 2011
suggest using a surrogate objective,
\mathbb{E}[\ell_{t}(s,X_{0})]
, defined as

\displaystyle\ell_{t}(s,X_{0})=\mathbb{E}\left[\big\|s(X_{t})-\nabla_{X_{t}}\log\mathsf{p}^{*}_{t}(X_{t}\,|\,X_{0})\big\|^{2}\,\big|\,X_{0}\right].

Given the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck forward process (
8
), we have that

\displaystyle(X_{t}\,|\,X_{0})\sim{\mathcal{N}}\big(m_{t}X_{0},\sigma_{t}^{2}I_{D}\big),\quad\text{where $m_{t}=e^{-t}$ and $\sigma_{t}^{2}=1-e^{-2t}$}.

(10)

Consequently, the score function of the conditional density is tractable and, thus,

\displaystyle\ell_{t}(s,X_{0})=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|s(X_{t})+\frac{X_{t}-m_{t}X_{0}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right\|^{2}\,\bigg|\,X_{0}\right].

Furthermore,
Vincent 2011
claims that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\|s(X_{t})-s_{t}^{*}(X_{t})\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\ell_{t}(s,X_{0})+C_{t},

where
C_{t}
is a constant depending on
t
but independent of
s_{t}^{*}
and
s
.
Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\|s(X_{t})-s_{t}^{*}(X_{t})\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\ell_{t}(s,X_{0})-\mathbb{E}\ell_{t}(s_{t}^{*},X_{0}).

(11)

Finally, we define the denoising score matching estimate as the empirical risk minimizer

\displaystyle\widehat{s}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{DSM}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{t}(s,Y_{i})\right\},

(12)

where
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\sim\mathsf{p}^{*}
are independent.

3
Results

This section collects our main results. We show that both denoising and implicit score matching are able to estimate not only the score function but also its Jacobian matrix without the curse of dimensionality. The results of such type usually require the underlying density
\mathsf{p}^{*}
to have a low intrinsic dimension. Following
(
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
)
, we assume that the data distribution is a convolution of isotropic Gaussian noise with a measure supported on a low-dimensional surface.

Assumption 3.1

(
(
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
)
)
.

Let
g^{*}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}([0,1]^{d},\mathbb{R}^{D},H)
, for some
H>0
and
d,D\in\mathbb{N}
, with
\|g^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant 1
.
We assume that the observed samples
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
are independent copies of a random element
Y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, generated according to the model

\displaystyle Y_{0}=g^{*}(U)+\sigma\xi,

where
U\sim\mathrm{Un}([0,1]^{d})
and
\xi\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{D})
are independent and
\sigma\in[0,1)
.

In Assumption
3.1
, we suppose that the intrinsic dimension
d
is small compared to the ambient dimension
D
. We would like to note that it is not the only way to impose structural assumptions on the data distribution. For instance,
Koehler et al. 2023
investigated statistical efficiency of implicit score matching under the condition that
\mathsf{p}^{*}
has a small isoperimetric constant. In
Tang and Yang 2024
;
Azangulov et al. 2024
, the authors assumed the existence of a hidden smooth low-dimensional manifold. Nevertheless, Assumption
3.1
has several advantages over the aforementioned setups. First, Assumption accommodates highly multimodal distributions, where the isoperimetric approach is not applicable. Second, unlike
Tang and Yang 2024
and
Azangulov et al. 2024
, we do not require the image of
g^{*}
to have a positive reach. More importantly, the density of
g^{*}(U)
(with respect to the volume measure) does not have to be bounded away from zero. In
Zhang et al. 2024
, the authors discussed that the density lower bound assumption may be restrictive and, in particular, does not explain the true strength of generative diffusion models.

We also highlight that adding a small amount of Gaussian noise is crucial when learning the score function, especially when the data distribution lies near a low-dimensional manifold (see
(
Song and Ermon 2019
)
).
The normalization
\|g^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant 1
together with
\sigma\in[0,1)
ensures a controlled signal‑to‑noise ratio, making the score estimation problem well‑posed.
Furthermore, Assumption
3.1
encompasses the cases when the data distribution has multiple well-separated components, a typical feature real-world data
(
Brown et al. 2023
)
.

Finally, we emphasize that similar data distribution assumptions were used to analyze statistical efficiency of other generative models, including generative adversarial networks
(
Schreuder et al. 2021
;
Stéphanovitch et al. 2024
;
Chakraborty and Bartlett 2025
)
and diffusion-based generative models
(
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
)
.
Recently, a more general assumption, where the data sample is a convolution of a bounded random vector and Gaussian noise, was used to analyze the iteration complexity of diffusion models
(
Beyler and Bach 2025
)
.

3.1
Weighted Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequalities for
\mathsf{p}^{*}

We start with a key technical result, which can be considered as an extension of the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality
(
Nirenberg 1959
)
with a weight function
\mathsf{p}^{*}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose Assumption
3.1
holds.
Let also
h:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
satisfy
|h|_{W^{\alpha,2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}<\infty
for some integer
\alpha\geqslant 2
.
Then it holds that

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle\quad\|{\rm div}[h]\|_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}^{2}\leqslant\frac{\alpha D}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}^{2}+\sigma^{2\alpha}\,|h|_{W^{\alpha,2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}^{2}\right)^{1/\alpha}\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}^{2-2/\alpha},

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle\quad\big\|\|\nabla h\|_{F}\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}^{2}\leqslant\frac{\alpha D^{1-1/\alpha}}{\sigma^{2}}\left(D\,\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}^{2}+\sigma^{2\alpha}\,|h|_{W^{\alpha,2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}^{2}\right)^{1/\alpha}\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}^{2-2/\alpha}.

We postpone the proof of Lemma
3.2
to Appendix
A
and focus on its implications. First, inequality
(i)
helps us to verify the Bernstein condition (see
(
Bartlett and Mendelson 2006
, Definition 2.6)
) for the excess loss class. While for denoising score matching this was already done in
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
, this task remained challenging for implicit score matching.
This property is crucial for establishing sharp rates of convergence as it allows us to use localization technique for unbounded empirical processes (see Appendix
D
). Second, the inequality
(ii)
allows us to control the score Jacobian matrix estimation error. We just have to verify that both
s^{*}
and its estimate are sufficiently smooth.
We would like to emphasize that the dependence on the ambient dimension in Lemma
4.2
is polynomial. This aspect did not receive much attention in some previous works on statistical properties of generative diffusion models, such as
Oko et al. 2023
;
Tang and Yang 2024
, where the hidden constant in the rates of convergence is of order
{\mathcal{O}}(e^{D})
. This significantly limits applicability of those results.

3.2
Score estimation using implicit score matching

We proceed with analysis of implicit score matching. To leverage the low intrinsic dimension of
\mathsf{p}^{*}
following from Assumption
3.1
, we take a reference class
{\mathcal{S}}
of special structure. Let us note that, under Assumption
3.1
, the density
\mathsf{p}^{*}
admits the following form:

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}^{*}(y)=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-D/2}\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{*}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}\,{\mathrm{d}}u,\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}.

Therefore, the score function is given by

\displaystyle s^{*}(y)=\nabla\log\mathsf{p}^{*}(y)=-\frac{y}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{f^{*}(y)}{\sigma^{2}},\quad f^{*}(y)=\frac{\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}g^{*}(u)\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{*}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u}{\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{*}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u}.

(13)

In other words,
s^{*}
is a sum of linear and bounded nonlinear parts. We take this structure of the score function (
13
) into account when designing the reference class.

Definition 3.3

(class of score estimators)
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
and
\sigma_{\min}\in(0,1)
the class of the score estimators is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)=\Big\{s(x)=-ax+af(x)\,:\,a\in(1,\sigma^{-2}_{\min}],\,f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{D})^{\top}\in\mathsf{NN}(L,W,S,B),

\displaystyle\qquad\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant D}|f_{l}|_{W^{0,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\leqslant C_{0},\quad\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant D}|f_{l}|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\leqslant C_{1}\sigma^{-2}_{\min},\quad\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant D}|f_{l}|_{W^{\alpha,2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}\leqslant C_{\alpha}\sigma^{-2\alpha}_{\min}\Big\}.

We would like to make several remarks regarding Definition
3.3
.
The conditions on the Sobolev seminorms are motivated by Lemma
3.2
: before we apply the weighted Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequalities, we must ensure higher-order derivatives of the score estimate candidates are bounded uniformly over
{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)
. We understand that enforcing boundedness of higher-order derivatives across the entire space is a challenging task in practice.
To the best of our knowledge, only first-order regularity is achieved during training, either by adding a regularization term to the loss function
(
Yoshida and Miyato 2017
;
Gulrajani et al. 2017
;
Pauli et al. 2021
)
or through the introduction of a more sophisticated neural network architecture
(
Anil et al. 2019
;
Qi et al. 2023
)
.

In what follows, we often
{\mathcal{S}}
, instead of
{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)
, when there is no ambiguity.
Note that, as defined in Assumption
3.1
, the value of
\sigma
is unknown.
To ensure that the linear term of the score estimate aligns with the true score function, as given in (
13
), we introduce a learnable parameter
a\in(1,\sigma_{\min}^{-2}]
.
This requires that
\sigma_{\min}
is known and satisfies
\sigma_{\min}\leqslant\sigma
.
These constraints are formalized in the following assumption.

Assumption 3.4

(non-degenerate noise)
.

Assume that
\sigma_{\min}\leqslant\sigma
for a known constant
\sigma_{\min}\in(0,1)
, where
\sigma
is a noise level defined in Assumption
3.1
.

We are ready to formulate our main result on generalization properties of implicit score matching.

Theorem 3.5

(implicit score matching generalization bound)
.

Grant Assumptions
3.1
and
3.4
, and let the sample size
n
be large enough, in the sense that it satisfies

\displaystyle n\sigma_{\min}^{52+4P(d,\beta)}\gtrsim 1\vee\left\{D\sigma^{-2}(\log n+\log(\sigma_{\min}^{-2}))\log(nD\sigma^{-2}\log(\sigma_{\min}^{-2}))\right\}^{\frac{2\beta+d}{\beta\wedge 1}}.

Define
P(d,\beta)=\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}
.
Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
with probability at least
1-\delta
the risk of an empirical risk minimizer over the class
{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)
(as defined in Definition
3.3
and (
7
)) with

\displaystyle L\lesssim\log(nD\sigma_{\min}^{-2}),\quad\log B\lesssim D^{8}(\log n)^{65}(\log D)^{26}(\log(\sigma_{\min}^{-2}))^{69},

\displaystyle\|W\|_{\infty}\vee S\lesssim D^{16+P(d,\beta)}(n\sigma_{\min}^{52+4P(d,\beta)})^{\frac{d}{2\beta+d}}\sigma_{\min}^{-48-4P(d,\beta)}(\log(nD\sigma_{\min}^{-2}))^{142+17P(d,\beta)}

and
C_{0}\asymp 1
,
C_{1}\asymp(D\log n\log\sigma_{\min}^{-2})^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
,
C_{\alpha}\asymp(D\log n\log\sigma_{\min}^{-2})^{{\mathcal{O}}(\log(n\sigma_{\min}^{-2}))}
satisfies the inequality

\|\widehat{s}-s^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}^{2}\lesssim\sigma^{-8}\left(n\sigma_{\min}^{52+4P(d,\beta)}\right)^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+d}}\log(e/\delta)\mathscr{L}(\sigma_{\min},D,n),

(14)

where the logarithmic factors are contained within

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}(\sigma_{\min},D,n)=\left(D\log(n)\log(\sigma_{\min}^{-2})\right)^{{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\log n}+\sqrt{\log(\sigma_{\min}^{-2})}\right)}.

Furthermore, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\big\|\|\nabla(\widehat{s}-s^{*})\|_{F}\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}\lesssim\sigma^{-10}\sigma_{\min}^{-4}\left(n\sigma_{\min}^{52+4P(d,\beta)}\right)^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+d}}\log(e/\delta)\mathscr{L}(\sigma_{\min},D,n).

(15)

The proof of Theorem
3.5
is moved to Section
4
.
Before comparing our result with the previous work, let us highlight key implications.
First, we derive the rate of convergence
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)})
for both score and its Jacobian matrix estimation, which is determined by the effective dimension, rather than the ambient one.
Unlike kernel-based score estimators
(
Wibisono et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
and the procedures based on Stein’s method
(
Li and Turner 2018
;
Shi et al. 2018
)
, the bounds of Theorem
3.5
do not become vacuous when
D\asymp\log n
.
It also indicates that estimating the Jacobian matrix is as easy as learning the score function in terms of the sample size.
It is not surprising, because the score function
s^{*}
is analytic in our setup (see
(
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
, Lemma 4.1)
).
Second, we note that
\mathscr{L}(\sigma_{\min},D,n)
grows in the sample size
n
slower than any polynomial and, hence, it does not ruin the derived fast rate.
Moreover, the rate does not deteriorate if
D\asymp\log n
.

To the best of our knowledge, only
Shen et al. 2024
has considered a setup similar to ours. This work assumed that the data distribution is supported on a neighborhood of a Riemannian submanifold.
However, this bounded support assumption does not explain the success of score-based generative models
(
Song and Ermon 2019
)
when the data is corrupted with Gaussian noise.
In contrast, our result successfully addresses this case.
Furthermore,
Shen et al. 2024
do not provide convergence rates for the score Jacobian matrix estimation.

In
(
Sasaki et al. 2018
, Lemma 12)
, the authors establish estimation error bounds on the score function and its Jacobian matrix with respect to the infinity norm on a compact set, using a least-squares density-derivative ratio method.
However, as discussed in the introduction, these bounds may be affected by the curse of dimensionality, which is avoided in Theorem
3.5
. The same concerns the methods based on kernel density estimation
Wibisono et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
.
Furthermore, kernel density estimation methods suffer not only from the curse of dimensionality but also from deteriorating convergence rates as the derivative order increases
(
Genovese et al. 2014
)
.

Theorem
3.5
also extends the statistical guarantees of
Ghosh et al. 2025
for implicit score matching, who considered a univariate case.
Specifically, Theorem 7 in
Ghosh et al. 2025
derives fast score estimation rates under the assumption that the data is a convolution of a distribution with support contained in
[-M,M]
and a sub-Gaussian noise.
However, this work does not explicitly state the dependence on
M
or, in view of Assumption
3.1
, does not provide a dependence on the noise scale parameter
\sigma
, which is a crucial aspect for analyzing distribution estimation in diffusion models, as highlighted by
Tang and Yang 2024
;
Azangulov et al. 2024
;
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
.

By comparing the convergence rates provided by Theorem
3.5
with with those achieved by denoising score matching
(
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
)
under identical assumptions, we see that implicit score matching achieves the same rate of
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)})
.
Thus, implicit score matching is statistically efficient as denoising score matching, even though the latter has access to the original noiseless samples.
Although the dependence on the noise scale parameter in Theorem
3.5
is less favourable than in
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
, it still remains polynomial.

3.3
Score estimation using denoising score matching

Finally, we discuss our findings on statistical properties of denoising score matching.
In particular, we show that, under Assumption
3.1
, Jacobian matrix of the denoising score matching output yields a consistent log-density Hessian estimate, provided that the reference class
{\mathcal{S}}_{DSM}
is chosen properly.
To facilitate our analysis, we fix an arbitrary
t>0
and investigate the statistical efficiency of denoising score matching at this specific timestamp.
This focus is motivated by the requirement for accurate estimation of both the score function and its Jacobian matrix at designated timestamps
(
Li et al. 2024a
;
Li et al. 2024b
;
Li et al. 2024c
;
Li et al. 2025
)
.

Assumption
3.1
in conjunction with the conditional law of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process given in (
10
) implies that the density of
X_{t}
, for any
t>0
, can be written in a closed form as

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*}(y)=\big(2\pi(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})\big)^{-D/2}\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-m_{t}g^{*}(u)\|^{2}}{2(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})}\right\}\,{\mathrm{d}}u,\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}.

Now let us fix an arbitrary
t>0
.
Therefore, by differentiating the logarithm of the derived density, we arrive at

\displaystyle s_{t}^{*}(y)=-\frac{y-m_{t}f^{*}(y,t)}{m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}},\quad\text{where}\quad\quad f^{*}(y,t)=\frac{\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}g^{*}(u)\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-m_{t}g^{*}(u)\|^{2}}{2(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})}\right\}\,{\mathrm{d}}u}{\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-m_{t}g^{*}(u)\|^{2}}{2(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})}\right\}\,{\mathrm{d}}u}.

(16)

Based on the expression for the true score function given in (
16
), we formulate the following definition of its estimate.

Definition 3.6
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
and
t>0
define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{DSM}(L,W,S,B)=\bigg\{s(x)=-\frac{x}{m_{t}^{2}\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\frac{m_{t}f(x)}{m_{t}^{2}\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}\,:\,\widetilde{\sigma}\in[0,1),\,f\in\mathsf{NN}(L,W,S,B),

\displaystyle\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant D}|f_{l}|_{W^{0,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\leqslant C_{0},\,\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant D}|f_{l}|_{W^{\alpha,2}(\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*})}\leqslant C_{\alpha}\sigma_{t}^{-2\alpha}

\displaystyle\bigg\}.

Non-asymptotic high-probability upper bounds on the generalization error and the accuracy of score Jacobian matrix estimation by denoising score matching are given in the next theorem.

Theorem 3.7

(denoising score matching generalization bound)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, suppose the sample size satisfies

\displaystyle n\sigma_{\min}^{52+4P(d,\beta)}\gtrsim\left\{D(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{-1}\log^{2}(nD\sigma_{t}^{-2})\right\}^{\frac{2\beta+d}{\beta\wedge 1}}.

Define
P(d,\beta)=\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}
.
Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the empirical risk minimizer
\widehat{s}
over the class
{\mathcal{S}}_{DSM}(L,W,S,B)
(as defined in (
12
)) with

\displaystyle L\lesssim\log(nD\sigma_{t}^{-2}),\quad\log B\lesssim D^{8}(\log(Dn\sigma_{t}^{-2}))^{90},

\displaystyle\|W\|_{\infty}\vee S\lesssim D^{16+P(d,\beta)}(n\sigma_{t}^{52+4P(d,\beta)})^{\frac{d}{2\beta+d}}\sigma_{t}^{-48-4P(d,\beta)}(\log(nD\sigma_{t}^{-2}))^{142+17P(d,\beta)}

and

\displaystyle C_{0}\asymp 1,\quad C_{\alpha}\asymp(D\log n\log(\sigma_{t}^{-2}))^{{\mathcal{O}}(\log(n\sigma_{t}^{-2}))}

satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle\|\widehat{s}-s_{t}^{*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*})}\lesssim D^{25+P(d,\beta)}(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{-4}(n\sigma_{t}^{52+4P(d,\beta)})^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+d}}\log(e/\delta)\mathscr{L}(\sigma_{t},D,n),

where

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}(\sigma_{t},D,n)=(\log(Dn\sigma_{t}^{-2}))^{239+17P(d,\beta)}\exp\left\{{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\log n}+\sqrt{\log(\sigma_{t}^{-2})}\right)\right\}.

Furthermore, on the same event of probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\displaystyle\big\|\|\nabla(\widehat{s}-s_{t}^{*})\|_{F}\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*})}\lesssim\frac{(D\log n\log(\sigma_{t}^{-2}))^{{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{\log n}+\sqrt{\log(\sigma_{t}^{-2})})}\log(e/\delta)}{\sigma_{t}^{4}(m_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2})^{5}}(n\sigma_{t}^{52+4P(d,\beta)})^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+d}}.

The proof of Theorem
3.7
is postponed to Appendix
C
.
Now compare the result outlined in Theorem
3.7
with previous work.

Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
consider the identical setup and achieve the same convergence rate in terms of the sample size.
However, our work exhibits slower dependence on
\sigma_{t}
, which remains polynomial.
We argue that this is due to the approximation result given in Theorem
E.1
that offers a bit worse dependence on the noise scale when approximating higher order derivatives.
Similar to Theorem
3.5
, we establish a
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)})
rate of convergence for the score Jacobian matrix estimation.
Notably, this rate in terms of the sample size is as fast as that for the score function estimation. Similarly to Theorem

, the reason for such phenomenon is that
s^{*}
is an analytic function (see
(
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
, Lemma 4.1)
).
We also note that the condition on the
W^{\alpha,2}(\mathsf{p}_{t}^{*})
-norm in Definition
3.6
can be removed if a Jacobian matrix estimation is not required.

The existing work on statistical efficiency of denoising score matching that avoids the curse of dimensionality
(
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Tang and Yang 2024
;
Azangulov et al. 2024
;
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
)
does not address score Jacobian matrix estimation problem.
Since these studies provide no approximation guarantees for the higher-order derivatives of the score function, a straightforward application of our weighted Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality (see Lemma

) fails to establish an error bound for the score Jacobian matrix estimation.
Consequently, in
Li et al. 2024a
;
Li et al. 2024b
;
Li et al. 2024c
;
Li et al. 2025
the score Jacobian matrix estimation error is no longer a limiting factor, since it is estimated at the same rate as the score function, as indicated by Theorem
3.7
.

4
Proof of Theorem
3.5

We split the proof into several steps for convenience. We focus our attention on the upper bound

(see Steps 1–5) and postpone the proof of (
15
) to the very end (Step 6). A reader will observe that the second statement of the theorem easily follows from our findings presented on Steps 1–5.

As usually in learning theory, to control generalization error of the implicit score matching estimate we have to bound approximation and estimation errors. Fortunately, the upper bound on the former one is straightforward in view of the recent result of
(
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
, Theorem 3.2)
(see Step 5). In contrast, the study of estimation error requires more efforts. First, we make some preparatory steps (Steps 1–3) to study properties of the excess loss class

{\mathcal{F}}=\big\{\ell(s,\cdot)-\ell(s^{*},\cdot):s\in{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)\big\}.

(17)

In particular, on Step 1 we show that
{\mathcal{F}}
has a bounded
\psi_{1}
-diameter. On the second step, we use the weighted Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality (Lemma
3.2
) to show that
{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies Bernstein’s condition. Finally, on Step 3 we elaborate on the covering number of
{\mathcal{F}}
. All these steps are necessary to apply a localization argument for unbounded empirical processes (Theorem
D.3
).

Step 1:
\psi_{1}
-diameter of
{\mathcal{F}}
.

We start with an upper bound on the
\psi_{1}
-diameter of the excess loss class
{\mathcal{F}}
.
The next lemma shows that it scales linearly with
D
and
\sigma_{\min}^{-4}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Grant Assumption
3.1
.
Then for
Y\sim\mathsf{p}^{*}

Step 2: verifying the Bernstein condition.

We defer the proof of Lemma

\displaystyle\|{\rm div}[f]\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant D\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{D}\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\left(a^{i}_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{i}}\right)^{2}({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{i})!(k_{i}+1)^{\alpha}\right)^{1/\alpha}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{D}\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\left(a^{i}_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{i}}\right)^{2}({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{i})!\right)^{1-1/\alpha}.

(31)

Evidently, the second term on the right-hand-side is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{D}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}(a^{i}_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{i}})^{2}({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{i})!\leqslant\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\|a_{\mathbf{k}}\|^{2}{\mathbf{k}}!=\|f\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2},

(32)

as suggested by (
28
).
We now turn to the evaluation of the first term.
Shifting the multi-index and partitioning the series leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{D}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}(a^{i}_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{i}})^{2}({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{i})!(k_{i}+1)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}\\
1\leqslant k_{i}\leqslant\alpha\end{subarray}}(a^{i}_{{\mathbf{k}}})^{2}{\mathbf{k}}!k_{i}^{\alpha}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{D}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}\\
k_{i}>\alpha\end{subarray}}(a^{i}_{{\mathbf{k}}})^{2}{\mathbf{k}}!k_{i}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\alpha^{\alpha}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\|a_{\mathbf{k}}\|^{2}{\mathbf{k}}!+\alpha^{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}\\
k_{i}>\alpha\end{subarray}}(a_{\mathbf{k}}^{i})^{2}{\mathbf{k}}!\frac{k_{i}!}{(k_{i}-\alpha)!},

where the last inequality uses the observation that if
k_{i}\geqslant\alpha
, then
k_{i}^{\alpha}(k_{i}-\alpha)!\leqslant\alpha^{\alpha}k_{i}!
.
In view of (
28
) and (
30
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{D}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}(a^{i}_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{i}})^{2}({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{i})!(k_{i}+1)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\alpha^{\alpha}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\|a_{\mathbf{k}}\|^{2}{\mathbf{k}}!+\alpha^{\alpha}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{p}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}\\
|{\mathbf{p}}|=\alpha\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}\\
{\mathbf{k}}\geqslant{\mathbf{p}}\end{subarray}}\|a_{\mathbf{k}}\|^{2}\frac{({\mathbf{k}}!)^{2}}{({\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{p}})!}

\displaystyle\leqslant\alpha^{\alpha}\left(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}+|f|_{W^{\alpha,2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}\right).

Combining this with (
A.1
) and (
32
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\|{\rm div}[f]\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}\leqslant\alpha D\left(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}+|f|_{W^{\alpha,2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}\right)^{1/\alpha}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2(\alpha-1)/\alpha}.

The proof of claim
(i)
is complete.

Step 2: proof of statement
(ii)
.
The proof follows almost identically to that of
(i)
.
First, we note that

\displaystyle\big\|\|\nabla f\|_{F}\big\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\sum_{j=1}^{D}\|\partial^{{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}f_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{D}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\|a_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}\|^{2}(k_{j}+1)({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{j})!.

Next, applying the Hölder inequality with parameters
p=\alpha
and
q=\alpha/(\alpha-1)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\big\|\|\nabla f\|_{F}\big\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}\leqslant\left(\sum_{j=1}^{D}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\|a_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}\|^{2}({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{j})!(k_{j}+1)^{\alpha}\right)^{1/\alpha}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{D}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\|a_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}\|^{2}({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{j})!\right)^{1-1/\alpha}.

From (
28
) we find that the second term in the right-hand-side of the above bound is bounded as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{D}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\|a_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}\|^{2}({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{j})!\leqslant D\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\|a_{\mathbf{k}}\|^{2}{\mathbf{k}}!=D\|f\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}.

For the first term, we apply the same reasoning used in the previous step.
Formally,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{D}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\|a_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}\|^{2}({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{j})!(k_{j}+1)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\alpha^{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{D}\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\|a_{\mathbf{k}}\|^{2}{\mathbf{k}}!+\alpha^{\alpha}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{p}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}\\
|{\mathbf{p}}|=\alpha\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}\\
{\mathbf{k}}\geqslant{\mathbf{p}}\end{subarray}}\|a_{\mathbf{k}}\|^{2}\frac{({\mathbf{k}}!)^{2}}{({\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{p}})!}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\alpha}\left(D\|f\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}+|f|_{W^{\alpha,2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}\right),

where the last equality uses (
28
) and (
30
).
Combining both bounds, we conclude that

\displaystyle\big\|\|\nabla f\|_{F}\big\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}\leqslant\alpha D^{1-1/\alpha}\left(D\|f\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}+|f|_{W^{\alpha,2}(\phi_{D})}^{2}\right)^{1/\alpha}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\phi_{D})}^{2(\alpha-1)/\alpha}.

Thus, claim
(ii)
holds, and the proof is complete.

\square

B.1
Proof of Lemma
4.2

We divide the proof into several steps to improve readability.

Step 1: decomposing the variance.
We begin by noticing that

\displaystyle{\rm Var}[\ell(s,Y)-\ell(s^{*},Y)]

\displaystyle={\rm Var}\left[{\rm div}[s-s^{*}](Y)+\frac{1}{2}\|s(Y)\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big({\rm div}[s-s^{*}](Y)\big)^{2}}_{(A)}+\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left(\|s(Y)\|^{2}-\|s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}\right)^{2}}_{(B)}.

(33)

Step 2: Bounding term
(A)
.
First, we rewrite term
(A)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big({\rm div}[s-s^{*}](Y)\big)^{2}=\|{\rm div}[s-s^{*}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}.

Applying Lemma
3.2
with
\alpha\geqslant 2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|{\rm div}[s-s^{*}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}\leqslant\frac{\alpha D}{\sigma^{2}}\left\{\|s-s^{*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}+\sigma^{2\alpha}|s-s^{*}|^{2}_{W^{\alpha,2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}\right\}^{1/\alpha}\|s-s^{*}\|^{2-2/\alpha}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}.

(34)

Next, we evaluate

\displaystyle\|s-s^{*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left\|-aX+af(Y)\right\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left\|-\frac{Y}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{f^{*}(Y)}{\sigma^{2}}\right\|^{2},

where
X\sim\mathsf{p}^{*}
,
s(y)=-ay+af(y)
, and
s^{*}(y)=(-y+f^{*}(y))/\sigma^{2}
according to (
13
).
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\|s-s^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}^{2}\lesssim\sigma_{\min}^{-4}\,\mathbb{E}\|Y\|^{2}+\sigma_{\min}^{-4}\,\mathbb{E}\|f(Y)\|^{2}+\sigma^{-4}\,\mathbb{E}\|f^{*}(Y)\|^{2}.

Taking into account that
|f_{l}|_{W^{0,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\leqslant C_{0}
for each
1\leqslant l\leqslant D
, and
\mathbb{E}\|Y\|^{2}\lesssim 1+\sigma^{2}D
according to Assumption
3.1
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\|s-s^{*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}\lesssim\sigma_{\min}^{-4}D(C_{0}^{2}\vee 1).

(35)

Now, using the fact that
\alpha\geqslant 2
, we bound

\displaystyle|s-s^{*}|^{2}_{W^{\alpha,2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}\\
|{\mathbf{k}}|=\alpha\end{subarray}}\|a\partial^{\mathbf{k}}f-\partial^{\mathbf{k}}s^{*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}\\
|{\mathbf{k}}|=\alpha\end{subarray}}\left(\sigma_{\min}^{-4}\|\partial^{\mathbf{k}}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}+\|\partial^{\mathbf{k}}s^{*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}\right).

Recall that
|f_{l}|_{W^{\alpha,2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}\leqslant C_{\alpha}\sigma_{\min}^{-2\alpha}
for each
1\leqslant l\leqslant D
.
Moreover, applying Lemma
E.2
, we deduce that

\displaystyle|s-s^{*}|^{2}_{W^{\alpha,2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}\\
|{\mathbf{k}}|=\alpha\end{subarray}}\left(C_{\alpha}^{2}D\sigma_{\min}^{-4\alpha-4}+4^{\alpha}\alpha!\sigma^{-4\alpha-4}\right)\leqslant(D+\alpha)^{\alpha}(C_{\alpha}^{2}\vee 1)\sigma_{\min}^{-4\alpha-4}(D+4^{\alpha}\alpha!).

Substituting this and (
35
) into (
34
) yields

\displaystyle\|{\rm div}[s-s^{*}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha D\sigma^{-2}\left\{(\sigma_{\min}^{-4}D(C_{0}^{2}\vee 1))^{1/\alpha}+\sigma^{2}(D+\alpha)(C_{\alpha}^{2/\alpha}\vee 1)\sigma_{\min}^{-4-4/\alpha}(D+4^{\alpha}\alpha!)^{1/\alpha}\right\}\|s-s^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}^{2-2/\alpha}.

Finally, from Stirling’s approximation we find that

\displaystyle\|{\rm div}[s-s^{*}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}\lesssim\alpha^{2}D^{1+1/\alpha}(D+\alpha)\sigma^{-2}\sigma_{\min}^{-4-4/\alpha}(C_{0}^{2/\alpha}\vee C_{\alpha}^{2/\alpha}\vee 1)\|s-s^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}^{2-2/\alpha}.

(36)

Lemma
3.2
also implies that the above bound holds for the Frobenius norm of the Jacobian matrix
\nabla(s-s^{*})
, therefore, verifying claim
(ii)
of the current lemma.

Step 3: Bounding term
(B)
.
To evaluate term
(B)
, the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality together with the Hölder inequality, applied with
p=\alpha/(\alpha-1)
and
q=\alpha
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
with
\alpha\geqslant 2
, yields

\displaystyle(B)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\|s(Y)-s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}\,\|s(Y)-s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\mathbb{E}\|s(Y)-s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}\right)^{(\alpha-1)/\alpha}\left(\mathbb{E}\big[\|s(Y)-s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}\,\|s(Y)+s^{*}(Y)\|^{2\alpha}\big]\right)^{1/\alpha}.

The second term is bounded by

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\big[\|s(Y)-s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}\|s(Y)+s^{*}(Y)\|^{2\alpha}\big]\right)^{1/\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\mathbb{E}[(\|s(Y)\|^{2}+\|s^{*}(Y)\|^{2})^{1+\alpha}]\right)^{1/\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\|Y\|^{2}+D(C_{0}^{2}\vee 1)}{\sigma_{\min}^{4}}\right)^{1+\alpha}\right]\right)^{1/\alpha}.

Next, by Assumption
3.1
, we have
\|Y\|^{2}\leqslant 2+2\sigma^{2}\|Z\|^{2}
almost surely, where
Z\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{D})
.
Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\big[\|s(Y)-s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}\|s(Y)+s^{*}(Y)\|^{2\alpha}\big]\right)^{1/\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma_{\min}^{-4-4/\alpha}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left(\|Z\|^{2}+D(C_{0}^{2}\vee 1)\right)^{1+\alpha}\right\}^{1/\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sigma_{\min}^{-4-4/\alpha}\left\{\left(\mathbb{E}\|Z\|^{2+2\alpha}\right)^{1/(1+\alpha)}+D(C_{0}^{2}\vee 1)\right\}^{1+1/\alpha},

where the last line follows from Minkowski’s inequality.
Next, since
\big\|\|Z\|^{2}\big\|_{\psi_{1}}\lesssim D
, then from
[
Vershynin 2018
, Proposition 2.7.1]
we deduce that

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|s(Y)-s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}\,\|s(Y)+s^{*}(Y)\|^{2\alpha}\right]\right)^{1/\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma_{\min}^{-4-4/\alpha}\left\{\alpha D+D(C_{0}^{2}\vee 1)\right\}^{1+1/\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha\left(\frac{D(C_{0}^{2}\vee 1)}{\sigma_{\min}^{4}}\right)^{1+1/\alpha}.

Consequently, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\|s(Y)\|^{2}-\|s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim\alpha\{\sigma_{\min}^{-4}D(C_{0}^{2}\vee 1)\}^{1+1/\alpha}\{\mathbb{E}[\|s(Y)-s^{*}(Y)\|^{2}]\}^{1-1/\alpha}.

(37)

Step 4: combining the bound for terms
(A)
and
(B)
.
Putting together (
B.1
), (
36
), and (
37
), we obtain that

\displaystyle{\rm Var}[\ell(s,Y)-\ell(s^{*},Y)]\lesssim\frac{\alpha^{2}D^{1+1/\alpha}(D+\alpha)}{\sigma^{2}\sigma_{\min}^{4+4/\alpha}}\left(C_{0}^{2+2/\alpha}\vee C_{\alpha}^{2/\alpha}\vee 1\right)\|s-s^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}^{2-2/\alpha}.

Note that the hidden constant in the above inequality does not depend on
s
.
The proof is now complete.

\square

Proof of Lemma

The proof is quite technical, so we divide it into several steps.

(see Definition

From

Note that due to the mean value theorem it suffices to show that the absolute value of the first and the second derivative of

t\,e^{-t}

Assumption D.1

When dealing with unbounded empirical processes, the main obstacle is to relate

In the unbounded case, we slightly modify this approach and take into account sub-exponential tails of

Theorem D.3
.

Let us introduce an empirical counterpart of

Using the standard chaining technique
[

{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})\leqslant\frac{12}{\sqrt{n}}\int\limits_{0}^{\rho_{n}}\sqrt{\log{\mathcal{N}}\big(\varepsilon,{\mathcal{F}},L^{2}(\mathsf{P}_{n})\big)}\,{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon\leqslant\frac{12}{\sqrt{n}}\int\limits_{0}^{\rho_{n}\vee\rho}\sqrt{\log A+\zeta\log\frac{D_{n}}{\varepsilon}}\,{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon.

Making a substitution
\varepsilon=(\rho_{n}\vee\rho)e^{-u}
,
u\in(0,+\infty)
, we deduce that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{12(\rho_{n}\vee\rho)}{\sqrt{n}}\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}\sqrt{\log A+\zeta\log\frac{B_{n}}{\rho_{n}\vee\rho}+\zeta u}\;e^{-u}{\mathrm{d}}u

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{12(\rho_{n}\vee\rho)}{\sqrt{n}}\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\sqrt{\log A+\zeta\log\frac{B_{n}}{\rho_{n}\vee\rho}}+\sqrt{\zeta u}\right)\,e^{-u}{\mathrm{d}}u

In the last line, we used the fact that

\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}\sqrt{u}\,e^{-u}{\mathrm{d}}u=\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}.

Due to the triangle inequality and the symmetrization trick, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\rho_{n}^{2}\leqslant\rho^{2}+\mathbb{E}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left(\mathsf{P}_{n}f^{2}-\mathsf{P}f^{2}\right)\leqslant\rho^{2}+2\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}f^{2}(\xi_{i})\right|,

where
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}
are i.i.d. Rademacher random variables, which are independent of
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
.
Let us note that for each
R>0
the map
x\mapsto x^{2}
is
2R
-Lipschitz on
[-R,R]
. In view of the Talagrand contraction principle
[
Ledoux and Talagrand 2013
, Theorem 4.12]
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}f^{2}(\xi_{i})\right|\leqslant 4\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}|f(\xi_{i})|\,{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({\mathcal{F}}).

Thus, applying Hölder’s inequality, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}\rho_{n}^{2}\leqslant\rho^{2}+8\left(\mathbb{E}\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}|f(\xi_{i})|^{q/(q-1)}\right)^{(q-1)/q}\left(\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}^{q}({\mathcal{F}})\right)^{1/q}.

Let us note that, due to Lemma
E.3
and
[
Puchkin et al. 2025
, Lemma F.7]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}|f(\xi_{i})|^{q/(q-1)}\right)^{(q-1)/q}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2^{(q-1)/q}(2q-1)}{q-1}\left\|\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}|f(\xi_{i})|\right\|_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\Psi(2q-1)(1+\log_{2}n)}{q-1},

where we introduced

\Psi=\left\|\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}|f(\xi)|\right\|_{\psi_{1}}.

Substituting this bound into the previous inequality, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}\rho_{n}^{2}\leqslant\rho^{2}+\frac{16\Psi(2q-1)(1+\log_{2}n)}{q-1}\left(\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}^{q}({\mathcal{F}})\right)^{1/q},

On the other hand, according to Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}^{q}({\mathcal{F}})\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{12}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\mathbb{E}(\rho_{n}^{q}\vee\rho^{q})\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi\zeta}}{2}+\sqrt{\log A+\zeta\log\frac{D_{n}}{\rho}}\right)^{q}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{12}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(\rho_{n}^{2}\vee\rho^{2})}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi\zeta}}{2}+\sqrt{\log A+\zeta\log\frac{D_{n}}{\rho}}\right)^{2q/(2-q)}\right)^{(2-q)/(2q)}.

This yields that

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}^{q}({\mathcal{F}})\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{12\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\rho^{2}+\frac{16\Psi(2q-1)(1+\log_{2}n)}{q-1}\left(\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}^{q}({\mathcal{F}})\right)^{1/q}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left[\frac{\sqrt{\pi\zeta}}{2}+\sqrt{\log A}+\sqrt{\zeta}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\log\frac{D_{n}}{\rho}\right)^{q/(2-q)}\right)^{(2-q)/(2q)}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\chi(q,\rho)}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\rho+\sqrt{\frac{16\Psi(2q-1)(1+\log_{2}n)}{q-1}\left(\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}^{q}({\mathcal{F}})\right)^{1/q}}\right),

where

\chi(q,\rho)=12\sqrt{\frac{\pi\zeta}{2}}+12\sqrt{2\log A}+12\sqrt{2\zeta}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\log\frac{D_{n}}{\rho}\right)^{q/(2-q)}\right)^{(2-q)/(2q)}.

Hence, we showed that

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}^{q}({\mathcal{F}})\right)^{1/q}\leqslant\frac{\chi(q,\rho)\,\rho}{\sqrt{n}}+4\chi(q,\rho)\sqrt{\frac{\Psi(2q-1)(1+\log_{2}n)}{(q-1)n}\left(\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}^{q}({\mathcal{F}})\right)^{1/q}}.

It only remains to note that the inequality
x\leqslant a+b\sqrt{x}
yields
x\leqslant b^{2}+2a
. This allows us to conclude that

\left(\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}^{q}({\mathcal{F}})\right)^{1/q}\leqslant\frac{2\chi(q,\rho)\,\rho}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{16\chi^{2}(q,\rho)(2q-1)(1+\log_{2}n)\,\Psi}{(q-1)n}.

Proof of Theorem
D.3

Similarly to the standard localization argument (see, for instance,
[
Bartlett et al. 2005
]
), the proof of Theorem
D.3
uses reweighting and peeling techniques. For any
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
r>0
we define

\mathsf{k}(f)=\min\left\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}:\mathsf{P}f\leqslant 4^{k}r\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{F}}_{r}=\left\{f\in{\mathcal{F}}:\mathsf{P}f\leqslant r\right\}.

Then the triangle inequality yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left\{4^{-\mathsf{k}(f)}\left|\mathsf{P}f-\mathsf{P}_{n}f\right|\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{r}}\left|\mathsf{P}f-\mathsf{P}_{n}f\right|+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}4^{-k}\;\mathbb{E}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{4^{k}r}\backslash{\mathcal{F}}_{4^{k-1}r}}\left|\mathsf{P}f-\mathsf{P}_{n}f\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}4^{-k}\;\mathbb{E}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{4^{k}r}}\left|\mathsf{P}f-\mathsf{P}_{n}f\right|.

According to the standard symmetrization argument the summands in the right-hand side are bounded by the corresponding double Rademacher complexities:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left\{4^{-\mathsf{k}(f)}\left|\mathsf{P}f-\mathsf{P}_{n}f\right|\right\}\leqslant\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}4^{-k}\;\mathbb{E}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{4^{k}r}}\left|\mathsf{P}f-\mathsf{P}_{n}f\right|\leqslant 2\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}4^{-k}\;\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{4^{k}r}\right).

(68)

Applying Lemma
D.2
with
q=3/2
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{4^{k}r}\right)\leqslant\left(\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}^{3/2}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{4^{k}r}\right)\right)^{2/3}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\rho_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sqrt{\log A}+\sqrt{\zeta}+\sqrt{\zeta\log(1/\rho_{k})}+\left(\mathbb{E}\log^{3}D_{n}\right)^{1/6}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\Psi\log n}{n}\left(\log A+\zeta+\zeta\log(1/\rho_{k})+\left(\mathbb{E}\log^{3}D_{n}\right)^{1/3}\right),

(69)

where
\rho_{k}=\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{4^{k}r}}\sqrt{\mathsf{P}f^{2}}
.
Next, using the Bernstein condition, that is,
\mathsf{P}f^{2}\leqslant B(\mathsf{P}f)^{\varkappa}
for every
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\rho_{k}\leqslant\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{4^{k}r}}\sqrt{B}\left(\mathsf{P}f\right)^{\varkappa/2}\leqslant 2^{\varkappa k}r^{\varkappa/2}\sqrt{B}.

(70)

Introducing

\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)=\frac{1}{n}\left(\log A+\zeta+\zeta\log(1/r)+\zeta\left(\mathbb{E}\log^{3}D_{n}\right)^{1/3}+\log(1/\delta)\right)

and summing up the inequalities(
68
), (
D.2
), and (
70
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left\{4^{-\mathsf{k}(f)}\left|\mathsf{P}f-\mathsf{P}_{n}f\right|\right\}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2^{\varkappa k}}{4^{k}}\sqrt{Br^{\varkappa}\Phi_{n}(r,1)}+4^{-k}\Psi\Phi_{n}(r,1)\log n\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{Br^{\varkappa}\Phi_{n}(r,1)}+\Psi\Phi_{n}(r,1)\log n

Note that for all
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
it holds that
{\rm Var}[4^{-\mathsf{k}(f)}f]\leqslant Br^{\varkappa}
.
Indeed, due to the conditions of the theorem, we have

{\rm Var}\left[4^{-\mathsf{k}(f)}f\right]\leqslant 8^{-\mathsf{k}(f)}{\rm Var}[f]\leqslant 8^{-\mathsf{k}(f)}B(\mathsf{P}f)^{\varkappa}\leqslant 8^{-\mathsf{k}(f)}B(4^{\mathsf{k}(f)}r)^{\varkappa}\leqslant Br^{\varkappa}.

According to the concentration inequality for suprema of unbounded empirical processes
[
Adamczak 2008
]
, there exists a universal constant
C>0
and an event
{\mathcal{E}}
of probability measure at least
(1-\delta)
such that

\sup\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left\{4^{\mathsf{k}(f)}\left|\mathsf{P}f-\mathsf{P}_{n}f\right|\right\}\leqslant C\sqrt{Br^{\varkappa}\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)}+C\Psi\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)\log n\quad\text{on ${\mathcal{E}}$.}

From now on, we restrict our attention on the event
{\mathcal{E}}
. Let us fix an arbitrary
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
. There are two scenarios: either
\mathsf{k}(f)=0
or
\mathsf{k}(f)>0
.
If
\mathsf{k}(f)=0
, then

\displaystyle\left|\mathsf{P}f-\mathsf{P}_{n}f\right|\leqslant C\sqrt{Br^{\varkappa}\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)}+C\Psi\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)\log n\quad\text{on ${\mathcal{E}}$.}

(71)

Otherwise, it holds that

and, therefore, the following two inequalities hold on
{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}f-(1+\varepsilon)\mathsf{P}_{n}f

\displaystyle\leqslant-\varepsilon\,\mathsf{P}f+4^{\mathsf{k}(f)}(1+\varepsilon)C\left(\sqrt{Br^{\varkappa}\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)}+\Psi\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 4^{\mathsf{k}(f)}\left(-\frac{\varepsilon r}{4}+(1+\varepsilon)C\sqrt{Br^{\varkappa}\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)}+(1+\varepsilon)C\Psi\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)\log n\right)

(72)

and

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{n}f-(1+\varepsilon)\mathsf{P}f

\displaystyle\leqslant-\varepsilon\,\mathsf{P}f+4^{\mathsf{k}(f)}C\left(\sqrt{Br^{\varkappa}\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)}+\Psi\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 4^{\mathsf{k}(f)}\left(-\frac{\varepsilon r}{4}+C\sqrt{Br^{\varkappa}\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)}+C\Psi\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)\log n\right).

(73)

Let us choose the smallest

where we introduced

\Upsilon(n,\delta)=\frac{1}{n}\left(\log A+\zeta\log n+\zeta\left(\mathbb{E}\log^{3}D_{n}\right)^{1/3}+\log(1/\delta)\right).

Hence, due to the inequalities (

), (
D.2
), and (

\left|\mathsf{P}f-\mathsf{P}_{n}f\right|\leqslant C\sqrt{Br^{\varkappa}\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)}+C\Psi\Phi_{n}(r,\delta)\log n\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon r}{4(1+\varepsilon)}
