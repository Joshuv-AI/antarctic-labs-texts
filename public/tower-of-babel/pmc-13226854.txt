Facile production of d-arabinitol from waste cells via artificial in vitro enzymatic cascade
Authors: Xueli Zhang, Zihan Cheng, Yilin Luan, Zheng Zhang, Cuiyao Zhang, Liyang Chu, Dongdong Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstractd-Arabinitol is a high-value platform chemical widely applied in food, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical industries, yet conventional production suffers from low conversion efficiency, prolonged cycles, and considerable environmental hazards. Herein, an artificial in vitro enzymatic cascade was developed consisting of Thermotoga maritima ribose-5-phosphate isomerase (RPI) and d-arabinitol 2-dehydrogenase (Ar2DH), plus Thiobacillus sp. formate dehydrogenase (FDH), for efficient d-arabinitol production from d-ribose. Systematic optimization of RPI, Ar2DH, formate, and NAD+ loadings yielded 9.9 g/L d-arabinitol from 10.4 g/L d-ribose within 4 h, corresponding to 93.9% molar conversion. Scale-up with 102.1 g/L d-ribose as substrate, 71.8 g/L of d-arabinitol was obtained with a molar conversion rate of 69.4%. Moreover, 3.0 g/L d-arabinitol was synthesized using 70 g/L Escherichia coli waste cells as feedstock. In conclusion, this enzymatic cascade demonstrates a rapid, sustainable, and eco-friendly approach to produce d-arabinitol, providing a promising strategy for upgrading industrial waste biomass in the biomanufacturing of bio-based products.Keywords: Sugar alcohol, Sustainability, Biocatalysis, Enzymatic catalysis, In vitro synthetic biosystem

Introduction

d-Arabinitol-arabinitol, a well-characterized five-carbon sugar alcohol, is listed among the 12 value-added platform chemicals designated by the U.S. Department of Energy. This compound has established extensive applications across the food, pharmaceutical, and fine chemical industries [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. Notably, d-arabinitol exhibits approximately 70% sweetness and only 5% of calories of sucrose [7,8]. This distinctive profile renders it an ideal non-cariogenic sweetener and dietary supplement for weight management and individuals with diabetes. Beyond its nutritional applications, d-arabinitol exhibits significant cytoprotective potential through its osmo-protective, drought-resistant, and energy-storage functionalities in biological systems. Furthermore, it serves as a critical precursor for synthesizing high-value pharmaceutical excipients and intermediates, including propylene, xylitol, xylonic acids, ethylene glycol, and immunosuppressive glycolipids [9,10]. Despite the compound's growing market demand [11,12], its natural scarcity presents a major bottleneck for industrial applications. This challenge has prompted intensive research interest in developing efficient production strategies for d-arabinitol, with the aim of fulfilling the ever-growing consumer requirements.

d-Arabinitol is predominantly synthesized from d-arabinose, d-lyxonic acid, or calcium gluconate via conventional chemical methods [13]. However, these approaches suffer from several inherent limitations, including high production costs due to expensive catalysts, significant toxicity, low yields, complex purification processes, stringent reaction conditions, and substantial environmental impact [11]. In recent years, advances in synthetic biology have introduced innovative, eco-friendly alternatives that are aligned with the principle of green chemistry [11,14,15]. Notably, metabolically engineered microorganisms have been employed for the de novo biosynthesis of d-arabinitol using renewable substrates including d-glucose, xylose, lactose, or glycerol [5,10,[16], [17], [18]]. Of particular interest is the utilization of d-glucose, a cost-effective feedstock that can be readily extracted from straw hydrolysate [19,20], enabling substantial reductions in production costs. Despite these advantages, microbial synthesis platforms still face technical challenges that impede their industrial scalability. This biosynthesis process is hampered by several constraints, including stringent cultivation conditions, prolonged production cycles, low conversion rates, and exorbitant separation and purification costs [21].

The enzymatic cascade platform has emerged as a viable alternative for producing valuable chemicals, as it circumvents the stringent conditions required to maintain cell viability [22,23]. This biosynthetic strategy mimics the in vivo metabolic pathway, enabling stepwise conversion of initial substrates into target products via precisely regulated enzymatic cascade reactions [24]. The system offers distinct advantages for target product synthesis, including well-defined metabolic pathways, mild reaction conditions, rapid reaction kinetics, high product titers and yields, environmental sustainability, and the capacity to overcome limitations to traditional synthetic approaches [25]. Thermostable enzymes confer particular advantages in enzymatic cascade, which not only improves reaction efficiency but also reduces microbial contamination risk, simplifies production processes, and minimizes storage and transportation costs [[26], [27], [28]]. Tomoko Fujisawa et al. successfully achieved the conversion of 2.9 g of amylose into 2.9 g of inositol within 7 h using a thermostable four-enzyme cascade, with a molar conversion rate of 96% [29]. Similarly, Zhao et al. reported the synthesis of 15.03 g/L of allicin from 25 g/L of d-glucose via a four-enzyme system under optimized reaction conditions [12]. For d-arabinitol production, Tang et al. successfully converted 70 mM xylose into 4.4 g/L of d-arabinitol using a three-enzyme cascade, achieving a maximum conversion rate of 41% [30]. Despite these promising results, current research still faces limitations in simultaneously achieving high yield and high efficiency in d-arabinitol production. Therefore, developing efficient and environmentally sustainable artificial in vitro enzymatic cascade for this valuable platform chemical remains an urgent and challenging research priority.

In this study, an in vitro enzymatic cascade was designed for the conversion of d-ribose into d-arabinitol via ribose-5-phosphate isomerase (RPI) and d-arabinitol 2-dehydrogenase (Ar2DH), with formate dehydrogenase (FDH) facilitating NADH regeneration. By optimizing the enzyme dosages, a remarkably high conversion rate of 93.9% was achieved using 10.4 g/L d-ribose as the substrate. Furthermore, a maximum d-arabinitol concentration of 71.8 g/L was obtained under high d-ribose concentration. More importantly, value-added d-arabinitol was successfully produced from Escherichia coli waste cells, with a weight-based conversion rate of 4.3%. This work provides an alternative approach for the industrial production of high-titer and high-yield d-arabinitol, and offers guidance for the high-value utilization of waste bacterial cells from industrial fermentation.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and strains

Unless otherwise noted, all chemicals used in this work were analytical grade, procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) or Sinopharm (Beijing, China). E. coli TOP10 and E. coli BL21(DE3) were selected for plasmid construction and overexpression of recombinant proteins, respectively.

Construction of plasmids

The RPI and Ar2DH gene were amplified from T. maritima genomic DNA using two pairs of primers IF-RPI (5′-TTTAACTTTAAGAAGGAGATATACCATGAAGATCGCTATTGCATCGGACC-3′)/IR-RPI (5′-AGTGGTGGTGGTGGTGGTGCTCGAGAACCTCATCGATCTTTCTGATTCTC-3′) and IF- Ar2DH (5′-TTTAACTTTAAGAAGGAGATATACCATGAATATTCTTGAAAAGCTTTTCT-3′)/IR-Ar2DH (5′-AGTGGTGGTGGTGGTGGTGCTCGAGCCAGACGGTGTAGCCTCCGTCTATC-3′), respectively. The vector backbone of pET28a was amplified from pET28a with a primer pair of VF-28a (5′-CTCGAGCACCACCACCACCACCACT-3′) and VR-28a (5′-GGTATATCTCCTTCTTAAAGTTAAA-3′). The RPI and Ar2DH gene were then ligated with the pET28a to generate the plasmid pET28a-TmRPI and pET28a-TmAr2DH by Simple Cloning, respectively [31]. Similarly, the DNA sequence of the FDH gene was amplified by primers IF-FDH (5′-TTAACTTTAAGAAGGAGATATACATATGGCGAAGATTCTGTGTGTTCTGT-3′) and IR-FDH (5′-AGTGGTGGTGGTGGTGGTGCTCGAGGCCTGCCTTTTTGAATTTGGCCGCT-3′) using Thiobacillus sp. genomic DNA as template. The vector backbone of pET20b was amplified by primers VR-20b (5′-ACAGAACACACAGAATCTTCGCCATATGTATATCTCCTTCTTAAAGTTAA-3′) and VF-20b (5′-AGCGGCCAAATTCAAAAAGGCAGGCCTCGAGCACCACCACCACCACCACT-3′). Then, the linear fragment pET20b was ligated with the gene fragment FDH by Simple Cloning to construct the plasmid pET20b-TsFDH.

Protein expression and purification

The constructed plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) to express the recombinant proteins. The resulting strains were inoculated into Luria–Bertani (LB) medium containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin or 100 μg/mL ampicillin and cultured until the optical density at 600 nm reached 0.8 to 1.0 at 37 °C. Subsequently, isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to a final concentration of 0.1 mM to induce expression at an appropriate temperature for 16 h (RPI and ADH at 18 °C, FDH at 25 °C). Following induction, cells were collected via cryogenic centrifugation and resuspended in 50 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5). Ultrasonic disruption was performed according to a previously described method [32], followed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 20 min. The supernatant was then used to purify His-tagged RPI, ADH, and FDH using Ni-NTA resin. Finally, protein purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE, and protein concentration was determined using the Bradford method.

Enzymatic activity assays

The activity of RPI was determined by adding 10 g/L d-ribose and an appropriate amount of RPI to 100 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5) at 50 °C for 5 min. The generated d-ribulose was quantitatively detected by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and one unit of RPI activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that released 1 μmol of d-ribulose per minute.

Ar2DH activity was measured based on the NADH consumption in 100 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5) containing 10 g/L d-ribulose and 3 mM NADH at 50 °C for 5 min. The consumption of NADH was monitored at 340 nm. One unit of Ar2DH activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that consuming 1 μmol of NADH per minute.

The activity of FDH was assayed at 50 °C for 5 min in a reaction solution including 100 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5), 80 mM sodium formate, and 3 mM NAD+. The generated NADH was determined at 340 nm. One unit of FDH activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that released 1 μmol of NADH per minute. Nonlinear regression was used to calculate the Michaelis−Menten kinetic constant Km and the turnover number kcat of each enzyme at 50 °C via GraphPad Prism 8 software.

d-Arabinitol synthesis from d-ribose by in vitro three-enzyme cascade

d-Arabinitol was synthesized in one pot with a reaction solution including 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 11.1 g/L d-ribose, 80 mM sodium formate and 3 mM NAD+. The enzyme loadings in the reaction system were 1 U/mL RPI, 1 U/mL Ar2DH and 3 U/mL FDH, respectively. The concentrations of d-arabinitol and d-ribose in the samples were determined by HPLC equipped with a refractive index detector and a Waters Sugar-Pak chromatographic column. The column temperature was maintained at 80 °C, with the mobile phase of water and a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min.

Optimization of reaction conditions

The loading amount of RPI was optimized in a 50 μL reaction solution containing fixed 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 10 g/L d-ribose, 3 U/mL FDH, 80 mM formate, 3 mM NAD+ and 1 U/mL Ar2DH. To determine the optimal dosage of RPI, the yield of d-arabinitol was measured after incubating the reaction mixture in a water bath at 50 °C for 24 h with RPI dosages ranging from 1 to 13 U/mL. Under conditions where all other components remained unchanged, RPI was fixed at its optimal loading, while Ar2DH was set at 0.1 U/mL, 0.2 U/mL, 0.5 U/mL, and 1 U/mL to determine its optimal dosage of the in vitro biosystem. Then, the formate concentration was set at 60 mM, 70 mM, 80 mM, and 90 mM to determine its optimal dosage under pre-optimized concentrations of RPI and Ar2DH. Finally, the NAD+ concentration was optimized at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 5 mM with the optimal dosages of RPI, Ar2DH, and formate.

Conversion of a high concentration of d-ribose into d-arabinitol

Using 50.1 g/L d-ribose as the substrate, 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 30 U/mL RPI, 1.5 U/mL Ar2DH, 9 U/mL FDH, 400 mM formate and 3 mM NAD+ were added to the in vitro enzymatic cascade. The whole system totaled 300 μL and was reacted at 50 °C for 24 h. The yield of d-arabinitol was determined by the method described above. Similarly, the conversion of 102.1 g/L d-ribose to d-arabinitol was carried out by adding 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 60 U/mL RPI, 3 U/mL Ar2DH, 18 U/mL FDH, 800 mM formate and 6 mM NAD+ to the reaction system.

Conversion of E. coli waste cells into d-arabinitol

In this work, metabolically engineered E. coli cells for the fermentative production of 5-hydroxyectoine were used as waste cells for the bioproduction of d-arabinitol. Briefly, E. coli BL21(DE3) was transformed with the recombinant plasmid pET28a-ectA/B/C/D, which carries a four-gene cluster from Streptomyces pseudogriseolus. This gene cluster encodes enzymes responsible for converting the l-aspartate-4-semialdehyde into 5-hydroxyectoine. The cells were cultured in LB medium until the OD600 reached 0.6, followed by the addition of IPTG at a final concentration of 0.1 mM. Subsequently, the cultures were incubated at 30 °C and 220 rpm for an additional 48 h, yielding 0.36 g/L 5-hydroxyectoine. Cells were harvested from the fermentation broth by centrifugation at 6000×g for 10 min, followed by three sequential washes each with a 10-fold volume of 0.9% (w/v) NaCl solution. Then, E. coli waste cells were suspended in 0.9% NaCl solution to an OD600nm of ∼260. The cell suspension was then disrupted, and the pH was adjusted to 5.5. The resulting cell lysate was incubated in a water bath shaker operating at 80 rpm and 50 °C for 3 h, allowing endogenous enzymes to release d-ribose. This d-ribose-containing solution was mixed with 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 5 U/mL RPI, 0.25 U/mL Ar2DH, 1.5 U/mL FDH, 40 mM formate, and 0.5 mM NAD+ for d-arabinitol production. The reaction was performed at 50 °C for 6 h. Subsequently, the concentrations of d-ribose and d-arabinitol were determined by HPLC.

Data analysis

All experimental values are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Intergroup comparisons were performed via t-test using GraphPad Prism 8. Asterisks (∗), (∗∗), and (∗∗∗) indicate statistically significant differences at p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001, respectively.

Results

Design of an artificial in vitro enzymatic cascade for d-arabinitol production from d-ribose

An artificial in vitro enzymatic cascade was designed for efficient conversion of d-ribose to d-arabinitol via isomerization and hydrogenation biocatalytic steps (Fig. 1A). This streamlined biosystem comprises two functional modules encompassing three enzymatic reactions: (1) RPI-catalyzed isomerization of d-ribose to d-ribulose; (2) FDH and Ar2DH synergistically catalyze d-ribulose and formate to d-arabinitol and CO2 in the presence of NAD+. In the hydrogenation module, Ar2DH mediates the reduction of d-ribulose to d-arabinitol with the consumption of NADH, while FDH is responsible for NADH regeneration using formate as the sacrifice substrate. This innovative configuration utilizes cost-effective formate as the hydrogen donor and enables continuous NADH regeneration, thereby conferring substantial economic benefits. Thermodynamic analysis, based on the standard Gibbs free energy changes (ΔG'°) calculated from the Equilibrator database (https://equilibrator.weizmann.ac.il/) under physiological conditions (pH 7.5, ionic strength 0.1 M), revealed that while the initial isomerization is thermodynamically unfavorable (2.7 kJ/mol), the subsequent hydrogenation reactions exhibit ΔG'° values of −14.4 kJ/mol (FDH) and −14.7 kJ/mol (Ar2DH), resulting in an overall reaction ΔG'° of −26.4 kJ/mol (Fig. 1B). This thermodynamic profile confirms that the entire enzymatic cascade is thermodynamically favorable, with theoretical substrate conversion approaching 100%.

Enzyme selection and preparation

In this enzymatic cascade, Ar2DH emerged as a pivotal enzyme for final product yield. The Brenda database currently reports only three Ar2DHs: Candida albicans CaAr2DH (UniProt ID: P43066), Candida tropicalis CtAr2DH (UniProt ID: P50166), and T. maritima TmAr2DH (UniProt ID: Q9WYD3). TmAr2DH, derived from the extreme thermophile T. maritima, exhibits superior thermostability [33]. TmAr2DH exhibited a kcat of 11 s−1 and a Km of 21.1 mM for d-ribulose at 50 °C, yielding a catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) of 0.5 mM−1 s−1 (Fig. S1A), thus confirming its robust catalytic activity. Consequently, TmAr2DH was selected as the key enzyme for d-arabinitol production in this work. Following successful expression in E. coli BL21(DE3), Ni-affinity chromatography-purified TmAr2DH displayed a single band on SDS-PAGE with a molecular weight (25-31 kDa) consistent with its theoretical value of 27.9 kDa (Fig. 1C), exhibiting a specific activity of 5.7 U/mg against d-ribulose at 50 °C (Table 1).

Additionally, the Brenda database and literature confirm the presence of RPI in T. maritima, which catalyzes the isomerization of d-ribose to d-ribulose [34]. T. maritima RPI (TmRPI) was successfully expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3), purified, and verified by SDS-PAGE as a single band, demonstrating a specific activity of 6.5 U/mg at 50 °C (Table 1). Following a 4 h enzymatic reaction with 5 U/mL TmRPI, 5.9 g/L d-ribulose was produced from 14.8 g/L d-ribose, corresponding to a conversion rate of 39.9% (Fig. S1B). For NADH regeneration, FDH from Thiobacillus sp. (TsFDH) was selected based on its stability and efficiency in NADH recycling [35,36]. TsFDH was expressed and purified as a single band (Fig. 1C), and exhibited a specific activity of 1.7 U/mg at 50 °C (Table 1). TsFDH exhibited a kcat of 9.2 s−1 and Km of 7.7 mM for formate at 50 °C (Fig. S1C). The combined use of thermostable TmAr2DH, TmRPI, and TsFDH not only ensures compatibility with high temperature reaction conditions, but also inhibits nonessential microorganisms and enhances the sustainability of the d-arabinitol production cascade.

Proof-of-concept assay for production of d-arabinitol

The quantification of d-ribose, d-ribulose, and d-arabinitol was performed using a HPLC system equipped with a Waters Sugar-Pak column and a refractive index detector (RID). The retention times for these compounds were 20.3 min (d-ribose), 19.4 min (d-ribulose), and 15.4 min (d-arabinitol), respectively. The peak heights of the three standard substances all demonstrated linear correlation with compound concentrations (Fig. S2). For the enzymatic synthesis of d-arabinitol, the reaction mixture was prepared containing 11.1 g/L (73.9 mM) d-ribose, 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 1 U/mL each of RPI and Ar2DH, 3 U/mL FDH, 80 mM formate, and 3 mM NAD+. HPLC analyses revealed a gradual decrease in the peak of d-ribose, accompanied by a concomitant increase in the peak of d-arabinitol over the course of the reaction (Fig. 2A). Time course analysis showed that a maximum d-arabinitol concentration of 1.2 g/L (8 mM) was obtained at 34 h, with a productivity of 0.24 mM/h and a molar conversion rate of 10.8% (Fig. 2B). These findings confirm the feasible conversion of d-ribose to d-arabinitol by the designed in vitro enzymatic cascade.

During d-arabinitol biosynthesis, a distinct peak at 12.4 min exhibited increasing intensity over the course of the reaction upon HPLC analysis using a Aminex HPX-87H column (Fig. S3A). This compound was verified as D-ribitol, as its retention time matched that of the d-ribitol standard (Fig. S3B). Using 10 g/L d-ribose as substrate and 3 mM NADH as cofactor, the specific activity of Ar2DH was determined to be 6 mU/mg at 50 °C by quantification of NADH consumption, demonstrating the enzyme's capacity to convert d-ribose to d-ribitol. To verify this observation, a control reaction was conducted in 100 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5) containing 10 g/L d-ribose, 80 mM sodium formate, 3 mM NAD+, 1 U/mL Ar2DH, and 3 U/mL FDH. As shown in Fig. S4A, the peak corresponding to d-ribitol showed progressive accumulation. At the 24-h time point, the d-ribitol concentration was determined to be 0.7 g/L (4.6 mM) (Fig. S4B). Although d-ribitol accumulated as a byproduct, the substantial residual d-ribose was the primary cause of the low conversion efficiency (Fig. 2). This observation suggests the rate-limiting step in d-arabinitol synthesis is likely the initial conversion of d-ribose to d-ribulose. Thus, this in vitro enzymatic cascade requires further optimization to enhance the overall conversion efficient.

Optimization enzymatic cascade for efficient synthesis of d-arabinitol

Optimizing reaction conditions to enhance conversion efficiency has been identified as a critical strategy for in vitro enzymatic cascades. Liu et al. achieved a fivefold increase in inositol productivity of an in vitro enzymatic biosystem by systematically optimizing key parameters, including enzyme loading, NAD+ concentration, temperature, and pH [37]. In this study, the dosage of RPI was first optimized while maintaining the loading of Ar2DH and FDH at 1 U/mL and 3 U/mL, respectively. The reaction mixture contained 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 10 g/L d-ribose, 80 mM formate, and 3 mM NAD+. RPI concentrations were set at 1 U/mL, 4 U/mL, 7 U/mL, 10 U/mL, and 13 U/mL. As the RPI loading increased from 1 U/mL to 10 U/mL, the conversion efficiency of d-ribose to d-arabinitol exhibited a nearly proportional correlation with the enzyme dosage (Fig. 3A). Concurrently, the residual d-ribose progressively diminished with increasing RPI loading (Fig. S5), a trend consistent with preliminary speculation. Ultimately, a maximum d-arabinitol conversion rate of 95.1% was achieved at an RPI loading of 13 U/mL. Considering economic feasibility, an RPI dosage of 10 U/mL was selected for subsequent experiment, as it maintained a high conversion rate of 94.6% (Fig. 3A). To determine the optimal Ar2DH loading, reactions were performed with systematically increasing Ar2DH dosages ranging from 0.1 to 1 U/mL while maintaining RPI loading of 10 U/mL. Notably, the conversion rate of d-ribose to d-arabinitol plateaued when Ar2DH reached 0.5 U/mL, at which point a maximum conversion rate of 95.7% was achieved (Fig. 3B). Then, under the optimized RPI and Ar2DH dosages, the optimal formate dosage was determined to be 80 mM (Fig. 3C). Finally, the NAD+ concentration was identified as 1 mM (Fig. 3D). Collectively, the optimal reaction conditions for this in vitro enzymatic cascade were determined to be 10 U/mL for RPI, 0.5 U/mL for Ar2DH, 80 mM for formate, and 1 mM for NAD+.

Under the optimized enzyme loadings, d-arabinitol was efficiently synthesized using 10.4 g/L ribose (69.3 mM) as substrate (Fig. 4A). Notably, a substantial amount of 7.5 g/L d-arabinitol was produced within the initial 2 h of the reaction, with a remarkably high productivity of 3.8 g/L/h (Fig. 4B). Subsequently, the productivity declined over the next 2 h, and the d-arabinitol concentration gradually stabilized at 9.9 g/L (65.1 mM) by 4 h. Correspondingly, the residual d-ribose concentration decreased to 0.12 g/L (0.8 mM). The concentration of d-ribulose peaked at 0.79 g/L at 1 h, yet it subsequently declined to 0.12 g/L by 4 h. In this one-pot enzymatic cascade, the molar conversion rate of d-ribose to d-arabinitol reached 93.9%. These results demonstrate that systematic optimization of reaction conditions significantly improved d-arabinitol biosynthesis efficiency.

d-Arabinitol production from the high concentration of d-ribose

To evaluate the industrial application potential of this designed enzymatic cascade, scale-up biotransformation experiments were conducted using high-concentration d-ribose as substrate. Initially, the reaction was performed at 50 °C in a 100 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5) containing 50.1 g/L (333.7 mM) d-ribose, 30 U/mL RPI, 1.5 U/mL Ar2DH, 9 U/mL FDH, 400 mM formate, and 3 mM NAD+. Within the first 6 h, d-arabinitol was rapidly produced, reaching 34.4 g/L with a productivity of 5.7 g/L/h (Fig. 5A). The reaction then gradually reached equilibrium by 12 h, yielding 36.6 g/L (240.6 mM) d-arabinitol, corresponding to a molar conversion rate of 72.1%. Concurrently, the d-ribose concentration decreased from 333.7 mM to 31.3 mM over the 12-h reaction period. Subsequently, the d-ribose concentration was increased to 102.1 g/L (680.1 mM) in 100 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5) with 60 U/mL RPI, 3 U/mL Ar2DH, 18 U/mL FDH, 800 mM formate, and 6 mM NAD+. The d-ribose concentration decreased from 102.1 g/L to 9.2 g/L at 12 h, while d-arabinitol concentration reached a maximum of 71.8 g/L (471.9 mM), achieving a molar conversion rate of 69.4% and a productivity of 39.3 mM/h (Fig. 5B). These findings demonstrated that the enzymatic cascade enables efficient conversion of d-ribose into d-arabinitol under high substrate concentration through an eco-friendly and sustainable process, thereby exhibiting great potential for large-scale industrial production.

d-Arabinitol production from E. coli waste cells

E. coli cells are common byproducts of industrial fermentation, and their high-value utilization is of great significance. Converting d-ribose from the nucleic acids of waste E. coli cells into d-arabinitol represents a promising strategy for achieving high-value utilization. E. coli BL21(DE3) cells with an OD600nm of ∼260 (corresponding to a dry cell weight of 93 g/L) were disrupted by ultrasonication. The resulting supernatant was placed in a water bath shaker operated at a stirring speed of 80 rpm. Owing to endogenous hydrolytic enzymes in the cells, the cell lysate underwent self-hydrolysis, leading to the gradual release of d-ribose (Fig. S6A). Compared with the water bath incubation at 37 °C, a high d-ribose concentration of 3.2 g/L was achieved after 3 h of self-hydrolysis at 50 °C (Fig. 6A). The addition of 3 U/mL nuclease and 3 U/mL phosphatase to the cell lysate yielded a final 3.6 g/L d-ribose, albeit with low productivity; in contrast, the boil-inactivated cell lysate generated 0.14 g/L d-ribose upon self-hydrolysis at 50 °C. Subsequently, this self-hydrolyzed solution was supplemented with 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 5 U/mL RPI, 0.25 U/mL Ar2DH, 1.5 U/mL FDH, 40 mM formate, and 0.5 mM NAD+ for d-arabinitol production at 50 °C. This enzymatic system containing an initial d-ribose concentration of 2.5 g/L, corresponding to starting with a dry cell weight of 70 g/L. As shown in Fig. S6B, the peak corresponding to d-ribose gradually decreased, while d-arabinitol accumulated progressively. The maximum d-arabinitol concentration of 3.0 g/L was attained at 6 h, corresponding to a conversion rate of 4.3% based on dry cell weight (Fig. 6B). Notably, the three enzymes involved in d-arabinitol production could fully drive the self-hydrolysis of the cell lysate.

In vitro production of d-arabinitol from E. coli BL21(DE3) waste cells. (A) Production of d-ribose from 260 OD cells (93 g/L dry cell weight) via self-hydrolysis under 37 °C or 50 °C. Control experiments were conducted to assess self-hydrolysis following boiling inactivation, as well as lysis at 30 °C in the 100 mM HEPES (pH 8.0) supplemented with 3 U/mL nuclease, 3 U/mL phosphatase, and 1 mM Mg2+. (B) Production of d-arabinitol from self-hydrolyzed solution containing initial 2.5 g/L d-ribose, 5 U/mL RPI, 0.25 U/mL Ar2DH, 1.5 U/mL FDH, 40 mM formate, and 0.5 mM NAD+ at 50 °C. Values shown are means of triplicate determinations.

Discussion

In this study, an artificial in vitro enzymatic cascade, consisting of ribose-5-phosphate isomerase (RPI) and d-arabinitol 2-dehydrogenase (Ar2DH) from Thermotoga maritima, and formate dehydrogenase (FDH) from Thiobacillus sp., was constructed for the efficient conversion of d-ribose into d-arabinitol. Compared to existing d-arabinitol production methods, this system offers several advantages: (1) Thermostable enzymes enable operation at elevated temperatures, thereby minimizing microbial contamination risk, lowering the viscosity of reaction mixtures, and enhancing reaction rates. (2) An efficient one-pot synthesis of d-arabinitol from readily available d-ribose was demonstrated. Formate was employed as a sacrificial substrate to sustain continuous NADH regeneration, which markedly lowered the cofactor cost of the overall system. (3) This enzymatic cascade exhibits significant industrialization potential. In particular, it enables the high-value bioconversion of waste E. coli cells, thereby promising broad applications for circular economic development. In conclusion, this artificial in vitro enzymatic cascade provides a promising strategy for the green and efficient production of d-arabinitol and offers a feasible technical approach for the valorization of industrial fermentation waste cells.

This enzymatic cascade has achieved a high titer of d-arabinitol via the large-scale conversion of high-concentration d-ribose, yet it still faces several key bottlenecks for industrial scale-up. Notably, the reliance on costly d-ribose substantially compromises the economic feasibility of this enzymatic cascade. Developing strategies for low-cost substrate supply therefore represents a key research focus of the present study. A hybrid system integrating microbial fermentation with an in vitro enzymatic cascade can be proposed. For example, fermentation by Bacillus subtilis using low-cost corn steep liquor as a feedstock yielded 62.1 g/L d-ribose under optimized cultivation conditions [38]. This biosynthetic route can be integrated with the RPI–Ar2DH–FDH enzymatic cascade for the subsequent production of d-arabinitol. In addition, in vitro enzymatic cascades offer a robust alternative for the cost-effective supply of d-ribose. Xu et al. constructed a synthetic pentose Izumoring pathway to convert low-cost d-xylose into d-ribose [39], providing a feasible strategy for the enzymatic production of d-arabinitol starting from d-xylose. Therefore, rationally designed enzymatic cascade pathways enabling the one-pot biosynthesis of d-arabinitol directly from inexpensive, abundant carbohydrate feedstocks (e.g., starch, cellulose, and hemicellulose hydrolysates) are anticipated, thereby eliminating reliance on costly d-ribose [32,40].

Another non-negligible issue in this cascade is the mass imbalance during high-concentration substrate conversion, for example, the total concentration of d-arabinitol, d-ribose, d-ribulose, and d-ribitol (276.8 mM) at 12 h was lower than the initial d-ribose concentration (333.7 mM) in Fig. 5A. This phenomenon was attributed to the degradation of d-ribulose. Further experiments confirmed that under the conditions of 50 °C and 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), the concentration of d-ribulose decreased by 14.6% after 12 h and by 24.5% after 24 h, relative to the initial concentration of 50 g/L (Fig. S7A). Besides, non-enzymatic isomerization of d-ribose to d-ribulose via the Lobry de Bruyn–Alberda van Ekenstein (LBAE) rearrangement [41] may also cause substantial substrate degradation and loss (Fig. S7B). Furthermore, the mismatch between the reaction catalyzed by Ar2DH and FDH lead to the accumulation of the intermediate d-ribulose, which intensify mass imbalance. Although the two enzymes exhibit comparable kcat values, their catalytic efficiencies (kcat/Km) differ by 2.4-fold (Fig. S1A and S1C). Furthermore, the half-lives of NADH and NAD+ at 50 °C range from 7.9 to 12.8 h (Fig. S8), causing kinetic incompatibility within the enzymatic cascade and thereby a relatively low actual turnover number of approximately 80 for NADH recycling. Increasing enzyme loadings to accelerate the reaction rate might reduce ribulose degradation. However, considering the industrial cost and the already high enzyme dosages in this biosystem, future efforts should focus on mining enzymes with higher catalytic efficiency to address this issue.

Excessive enzyme loading hinders the industrial scalability of this enzymatic cascade, particularly limiting the high-value valorization of waste cells. A high dosage of RPI is required to maintain a rapid forward conversion efficiency (Fig. 3A). For practical industrial application, enzyme engineering strategies such as semi-rational design and directed evolution should be adopted to improve the catalytic efficiency, substrate affinity, and operational stability of both RPI and Ar2DH. These modifications would reduce enzyme loading, lower overall production costs, and maintain high reaction rates under more flexible processing conditions. However, the costs associated with enzyme preparation, including cell disruption and protein purification, along with the operational complexity of in vitro enzymatic cascades, remain critical bottlenecks for large-scale production. Enzyme immobilization and multi-enzyme co-expression represent promising strategies to address these limitations.

Conclusions

In this study, an efficient enzymatic cascade was demonstrated for sustainable one-pot d-arabinitol synthesis from d-ribose, yielding a high concentration of 71.8 g/L product with a molar yield of 69.4%. E. coli BL21(DE3) cells were successfully converted into d-arabinitol with 4.3% weight conversion offering the distinct advantage of high-value valorization of industrial waste microbial cells. Several critical bottlenecks restrict its industrial scalability, including the high cost of the d-ribose substrate, mass imbalance, excessive enzyme loading, and elevated biocatalyst expenses. Targeted strategies, including the provision of cost-effective substrates via rational design of enzymatic pathways, systematic optimization to minimize intermediate accumulation, enzyme engineering for enhanced catalytic performance, and multi-enzyme co-expression, can be implemented in future investigations. Collectively, this enzymatic cascade provides a promising d-arabinitol production strategy and a versatile model for sustainable biochemical manufacturing from waste biomass.
