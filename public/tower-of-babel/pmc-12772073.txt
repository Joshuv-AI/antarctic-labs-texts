The impact of respiratory training on diaphragmatic function in elderly COPD patients with sarcopenia
Authors: Hailian Guo, Fang Yan, Xuhong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) combined with sarcopenia is a common chronic disease combination in elderly patients, significantly affecting diaphragmatic function, pulmonary function, quality of life, and exercise capacity. This study aims to evaluate the effects of respiratory training on diaphragmatic function and multidimensional health indicators in elderly COPD patients with sarcopenia.MethodsThis single-center, randomized controlled, prospective study included 80 elderly COPD patients with sarcopenia, who were randomly assigned to the respiratory training group (40 patients) and the conventional treatment group (40 patients) in a 1:1 ratio. The respiratory training group underwent 12 weeks of diaphragmatic breathing training and expiratory resistance training, while the conventional treatment group received standard COPD care only. The primary outcomes included diaphragmatic function (diaphragmatic excursion and diaphragmatic thickness change rate), pulmonary function (FEV1, FEV1/FVC), quality of life (SGRQ total score), exercise capacity (6MWT), and clinical scores (mMRC, BODE index). Paired t-tests, independent t-tests, and multivariable linear regression analyses were used to assess intervention effects and independent influencing factors.ResultsBaseline characteristics were well-matched between the two groups, with no significant differences observed (P > 0.05). After 12 weeks, the respiratory training group showed significant improvements in diaphragmatic function: diaphragmatic excursion increased from 1.8 ± 0.3 cm to 3.2 ± 0.4 cm, and diaphragmatic thickness change rate increased from 20.0 ± 3.5% to 40.2 ± 4.2% (both P < 0.001), significantly outperforming the conventional treatment group (intergroup comparison P < 0.001). Improvements in pulmonary function, quality of life, and exercise capacity were also significantly greater in the respiratory training group (FEV1: P < 0.001; FEV1/FVC: P < 0.001; SGRQ total score: P < 0.001; 6MWT: P < 0.001). Multivariable linear regression analysis identified respiratory training as the only significant predictor of improved diaphragmatic function (P < 0.001).ConclusionsRespiratory training significantly improved diaphragmatic function, pulmonary function, quality of life, and exercise capacity in elderly COPD patients with sarcopenia, showing superior efficacy compared to conventional treatment. These findings highlight respiratory training as an effective intervention strategy, providing scientific evidence for the comprehensive management of elderly COPD patients with sarcopenia.Clinical trial number ChiCTR2500098349 (Registration Date: 2025-3-6).Keywords: Respiratory training, Diaphragmatic function, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Sarcopenia, Multidimensional function, Quality of life

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common respiratory disease characterized by airflow limitation and chronic airway inflammation, particularly prevalent in the elderly population [1–3]. Recently, COPD combined with sarcopenia (progressive loss of muscle mass and strength) has emerged as a complex comorbidity, further exacerbating respiratory dysfunction, reducing exercise capacity, and impairing quality of life [4–7]. The diaphragm, as the primary respiratory muscle, plays a crucial role in the pathophysiology of dyspnea in COPD [8]. Sarcopenia further weakens diaphragmatic strength, increasing rehabilitation difficulty, worsening disease progression, and causing a continuous decline in quality of life [9]. Thus, effectively improving diaphragmatic function in COPD patients with sarcopenia is a key clinical challenge.

Current pulmonary rehabilitation strategies, including bronchodilator use and whole-body exercise training [10, 11], lack direct interventions targeting diaphragmatic function. Moreover, COPD patients with sarcopenia, due to muscle loss and metabolic abnormalities, respond less effectively to conventional treatments than patients with COPD alone, highlighting the need for tailored interventions [12, 13]. Respiratory muscle training, which focuses on enhancing the strength and endurance of respiratory muscles, has shown potential in COPD management in recent years [14]. Specifically, diaphragmatic breathing training and expiratory resistance training can significantly improve respiratory function by strengthening diaphragmatic activity and respiratory muscle strength [15]. However, whether respiratory training can significantly improve diaphragmatic function in COPD patients with sarcopenia and its multidimensional health benefits remains to be validated.

To address this gap, this study focuses on COPD patients with sarcopenia, utilizing a randomized controlled trial design to systematically evaluate the effects of respiratory training on diaphragmatic function, pulmonary function, exercise capacity, and quality of life. The innovative aspect of this study lies in the dynamic assessment of multidimensional indicators and the use of multivariable linear regression analysis to investigate the independent contributions of respiratory training to diaphragmatic function improvement. This design not only validates the intervention effects of respiratory training but also clarifies its scientific value in disease management.

The aim of this study is to determine the efficacy of respiratory training in improving diaphragmatic function and other health outcomes in COPD patients with sarcopenia and to analyze its specific mechanisms of action across multidimensional indicators. The findings will provide scientific evidence for developing personalized rehabilitation strategies and new intervention directions to enhance the quality of life and prognosis for this patient population.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient enrollment

This single-center, prospective, randomized controlled trial was conducted at Taiyuan Second People’s Hospital. The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of a structured respiratory training program on diaphragmatic function, pulmonary function, quality of life, and exercise capacity in elderly patients with COPD and sarcopenia.

The study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) under registration number ChiCTR2500098349, and approved by the hospital’s ethics committee (approval number: ky2024-03). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Age >60 years; (2) Diagnosis of COPD based on the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) 2023 criteria [16]; (3) Sarcopenia was diagnosed according to the criteria proposed by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS, 2019) [17]. The diagnostic process included three components: low muscle mass, low muscle strength, and/or reduced physical performance. Muscle mass was assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), and appendicular lean mass index (ALMI) was calculated as appendicular lean mass (kg) divided by height squared (m²). The cut-off values for low muscle mass were ALMI < 7.0 kg/m² for males and < 5.7 kg/m² for females [4]; (4) Ability to cooperate with respiratory training and complete follow-up.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease or other life-threatening comorbidities; (2) Acute respiratory infection or hospitalization within the past four weeks; (3) Use of any respiratory support devices, including non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NPPV) even if used exclusively at night; (4) Comorbidities affecting exercise capacity or quality of life.

Patients were randomly assigned to either the respiratory training group or the conventional treatment group in a 1:1 ratio. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random number table by an independent researcher. The research team and participants were blinded to group allocation.

Interventions

The respiratory training group underwent a 12-week respiratory muscle training program designed to enhance diaphragmatic function and overall respiratory capacity. The program consisted of diaphragmatic breathing training and expiratory resistance training.

Diaphragmatic breathing training involved deep inhalation and slow exhalation exercises performed twice daily for 20 min per session, aiming to improve diaphragmatic mobility and breathing pattern control.

Expiratory resistance training was conducted using a specialized Respiratory Rehabilitation Training Device(TKBR-01, Wuxi Tianjian Medical Equipment Co., Ltd, Wuxi, Jiangsu, China). Instead of prescribing training load based on maximal expiratory pressure, which we deliberately avoided because maximal expiratory maneuvers may provoke transient airway collapse, dizziness, or fatigue in frail elderly patients with sarcopenia, resistance was initially set at the lowest tolerable level of the device and subsequently adjusted according to two criteria: (1) the patient’s ability to complete full expirations without fatigue, dizziness, or discomfort, and (2) achieving a minimum perceived exertion of ≥ 3 on the 0–10 Borg scale. This submaximal, tolerance-based approach was adopted to ensure safety while maintaining adequate respiratory muscle activation. In line with practical recommendations for COPD respiratory muscle training, load progression was guided by perceived exertion at a “somewhat hard” level (RPE 12–14 on the Borg 6–20 scale, equivalent to 3–4 on the CR10 scale), which is considered safe and effective for frail COPD populations [18–20]. The final resistance was therefore considered “low-to-moderate” in relative terms, reflecting both subjective effort and clinical tolerability.

The expiratory resistance training was performed once daily for 15 min under the supervision of medical staff. Each session consisted of three 5-minute bouts with 1-minute rest intervals. During hospitalization, sessions were fully supervised; after discharge, patients continued home-based training guided by demonstration videos and weekly follow-up calls. Adherence was verified through daily training logs and device-use counters, with no adverse events or discontinuations due to intolerance observed throughout the 12-week intervention. The parameters of the training protocol (duration, frequency, and resistance level) were determined based on previous studies of respiratory muscle training in patients with COPD [18–20] and were adapted for elderly patients with sarcopenia to ensure feasibility and safety.

To ensure methodological rigor and minimize bias, randomization and group assignment were performed by an independent statistician not involved in patient care or data collection. Respiratory training sessions were supervised by clinical staff who were not involved in outcome assessment. All outcome measurements, including diaphragmatic ultrasound, pulmonary function tests, 6-minute walk test (6MWT), and questionnaires, were conducted by trained technicians blinded to group allocation. Data entry and analysis were performed by a separate team who had no access to participant group information. The detailed procedure is illustrated in the flowchart (Fig. 1).

Outcome measures

This study employed multidimensional outcome measures to comprehensively evaluate the effects of respiratory training on elderly COPD patients with sarcopenia, focusing on diaphragmatic function, pulmonary function, quality of life, exercise capacity, and clinical scores. Measurements were conducted using standardized protocols by trained technicians to ensure accuracy and reliability. Data were collected at baseline (pre-intervention) and after the 12-week follow-up.

Diaphragmatic function, the core outcome of this study, was assessed through diaphragmatic excursion and diaphragmatic thickness change rate. Diaphragmatic excursion was measured via ultrasound imaging during quiet breathing, recording the displacement of the diaphragm apex between end-inspiration and end-expiration (unit: cm). Diaphragmatic thickness change rate reflected the relative change in diaphragmatic thickness between end-inspiration and end-expiration and was calculated using the formula: Thickness change rate = ((end-inspiratory thickness - end-expiratory thickness)/end-expiratory thickness) × 100% (unit: %). Each parameter was measured three times under relaxed conditions, and the average value was used as the final result. Each parameter was measured three times during quiet breathing under relaxed conditions, and the average value was used as the final result.

Pulmonary function was assessed using forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1, unit: liters) and the FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio (unit: %). Pulmonary function tests were conducted using standardized spirometry equipment operated by certified technicians in accordance with GOLD guidelines. Patients were instructed to refrain from using bronchodilators for at least 12 h prior to testing to ensure accurate baseline measurements.

Quality of life was evaluated using the St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) total score [21], which encompasses symptoms, activity, and the impact of disease on daily life. Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating worse quality of life. Patients completed the questionnaire under the guidance of researchers to ensure comprehension and accuracy.

Exercise capacity was measured using the six-minute walk test (6MWT), which recorded the distance a patient could walk in a flat, straight corridor within six minutes (unit: meters). Patients rested for 10 min and achieved a stable state before the test. They were allowed to pause as needed during the test, but timing continued. The total walking distance and subjective fatigue level were recorded at the end of the test.

Dyspnea was quantified using the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scale, with scores ranging from 0 to 4 (higher scores indicating more severe dyspnea). Disease severity was assessed using the BODE index, which integrates body mass index (BMI), FEV1, mMRC score, and 6MWT results [22]. The BODE index ranges from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating more severe disease.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software, with a two-sided significance level set at P < 0.05. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. Within-group pre- and post-intervention changes were analyzed using paired t-tests, while between-group differences after follow-up were analyzed using independent t-tests. Multivariable linear regression was used to evaluate the independent effect of respiratory training on diaphragmatic excursion. The dependent variable was diaphragmatic excursion at the 12-week follow-up, and independent variables included group allocation, baseline diaphragmatic excursion, FEV1/FVC ratio, 6MWT, SGRQ total score, and mMRC score. Independent contributions were assessed using unstandardized coefficients (B values) and standardized coefficients (β values), with collinearity checked (VIF < 10). There were no dropouts or missing data during the study period; all enrolled participants completed the interventions and outcome assessments as planned.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 80 elderly patients with COPD and sarcopenia were included in this study, with 40 patients randomly assigned to the respiratory training group and 40 to the conventional treatment group. Among the respiratory training group, there were 28 males and 12 females, while the conventional treatment group had 26 males and 14 females. No significant differences were found in demographic characteristics, physical parameters, diaphragmatic function, pulmonary function, or quality of life between the two groups at baseline (P > 0.05), indicating comparability between groups (Table 1).

Changes in multidimensional function and quality of life indicators

For dyspnea, mMRC scores significantly decreased in the respiratory training group after the intervention (P < 0.001), while no significant change was observed in the conventional treatment group (P > 0.05). Post-intervention inter-group comparisons revealed that the respiratory training group had significantly lower mMRC scores than the conventional treatment group (P < 0.001).

The BODE index, reflecting overall disease severity, also showed significant improvement in the respiratory training group (P < 0.001), whereas no significant change occurred in the conventional treatment group (P > 0.05). Inter-group analysis demonstrated that the decrease in BODE index was significantly greater in the respiratory training group compared to the conventional treatment group (P < 0.001).

In terms of pulmonary function, both FEV1 and FEV1/FVC ratios significantly increased in the respiratory training group (P < 0.001). No significant changes were observed in the conventional treatment group (P > 0.05). Between-group comparisons after intervention showed that improvements in FEV1 and FEV1/FVC were significantly greater in the respiratory training group (P < 0.001).

Quality of life, as assessed by SGRQ total score, improved markedly in the respiratory training group, with a significant reduction in scores after the intervention (P < 0.001). The conventional treatment group showed no significant changes (P > 0.05). Post-intervention inter-group comparisons confirmed that quality of life improvements were significantly more pronounced in the respiratory training group (P < 0.001).

Regarding exercise capacity, the 6MWT distance increased significantly in the respiratory training group (P < 0.001), while no significant changes were observed in the conventional treatment group (P > 0.05). Inter-group analysis indicated that the improvement in walking distance was significantly greater in the respiratory training group (P < 0.001).

Multivariable linear regression analysis: effects of respiratory training on primary and secondary outcomes

Respiratory training was the only variable significantly associated with diaphragmatic excursion improvement at the 12-week follow-up (P < 0.001). Baseline variables, including diaphragmatic excursion, FEV1/FVC ratio, 6MWT, SGRQ total score, and mMRC dyspnea score, were not significantly associated with post-intervention diaphragmatic excursion (P > 0.05). No multicollinearity was observed among variables, as VIF values were below 10 and tolerance values were close to 1 (Table 4).

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that respiratory training significantly improved diaphragmatic function, pulmonary function, quality of life, and exercise capacity in elderly patients with COPD and sarcopenia. The respiratory training group showed significant increases in diaphragmatic excursion and diaphragmatic thickness change rate, along with marked improvements in pulmonary function (FEV1 and FEV1/FVC) and quality of life (SGRQ total score), all of which were superior to the conventional treatment group. Multivariable regression analysis identified respiratory training as an independent predictor of diaphragmatic function improvement. These findings provide strong scientific evidence for the clinical application of respiratory training in this patient population.

In the absence of direct strength or endurance testing of the diaphragm, the observed improvements in diaphragmatic excursion and thickness change rate may be interpreted as functional enhancements in diaphragmatic motion and ventilatory mechanics rather than evidence of structural adaptation. Given that our protocol did not employ progressive overload anchored to objective pressures (e.g., MIP, maximal expiratory pressure [MEP], or sniff nasal inspiratory pressure [SNIP]), it is not physiologically justified to infer muscle fiber remodeling or hypertrophy. Therefore, the functional gains observed in this trial are more plausibly explained by improved ventilatory efficiency and better utilization of existing respiratory muscle capacity, rather than structural fiber-type adaptations.

While IMT with individualized resistance and progressive overload is the most established modality for specifically strengthening the diaphragm [23], our cohort consisted of elderly patients with COPD and sarcopenia, in whom safety, feasibility, and adherence are paramount. Accordingly, we adopted an EMT component at low-to-moderate resistances, combined with diaphragmatic breathing, as a pragmatic strategy derived from COPD respiratory muscle training literature [15, 24]. Although EMT is not a standard method for diaphragmatic strengthening, it may yield indirect benefits by reducing expiratory flow limitation, alleviating dynamic hyperinflation, and restoring a more favorable length–tension relationship of the diaphragm. Thus, the improvements in diaphragmatic excursion observed here could plausibly reflect these indirect mechanisms, rather than direct training-induced muscle hypertrophy.

Within this exploratory framework, several physiological pathways may help explain the functional gains. First, by facilitating expiratory flow and mitigating air trapping, EMT may reduce lung hyperinflation, thereby improving the mechanical environment of the diaphragm at end-expiration. Second, diaphragmatic breathing enhances diaphragmatic recruitment and may improve the coordination between inspiratory and expiratory muscles. Third, the combined effect of these interventions may optimize ventilatory efficiency, relieve dyspnea, and increase exercise tolerance. Although these pathways are consistent with the multifunctional role of the diaphragm [23, 25], they remain hypothetical in our dataset and should be confirmed in future studies using direct physiological and mechanistic assessments.

Even though the precise mechanisms remain speculative, the consistency of improvements across multiple outcome domains highlights the clinical relevance of our findings. Enhanced diaphragmatic function can directly alleviate dyspnea and improve alveolar ventilation and gas exchange, thereby leading to measurable improvements in pulmonary function [23]. Furthermore, by reducing the work of breathing and redistributing oxygen delivery to peripheral muscles, respiratory training can augment exercise capacity [25]. Compared to previous studies [26, 27], this study is the first to focus on the specific effects of respiratory training on diaphragmatic function in COPD patients with sarcopenia. It not only validates the targeted intervention efficacy of respiratory training on respiratory muscles but also reveals its potential synergistic mechanisms in systemic functional recovery, expanding its application value in managing complex comorbidities.

Despite the important findings of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although diaphragmatic breathing combined with expiratory resistance training improved diaphragmatic mobility, our protocol differed from standardized IMT, as it did not incorporate individualized load prescription or progressive overload. Similarly, the absence of objective respiratory muscle assessments (e.g., MIP, MEP, SNIP) limits our ability to determine whether strength or endurance adaptations occurred. Second, EMT should be regarded as an exploratory adjunct, not a replacement for IMT, and its role in diaphragmatic strengthening requires further validation. Third, we did not include plethysmography or direct measurements of respiratory effort, which precludes mechanistic confirmation (e.g., quantification of dynamic hyperinflation). Additional limitations include the single-center design and relatively small sample size, lack of GOLD stage stratification, absence of predicted 6MWT reference values, and the relatively short intervention period (12 weeks), which prevents assessment of long-term sustainability. Furthermore, we did not incorporate metabolic or molecular biomarkers to probe underlying biological mechanisms, nor did we conduct detailed adherence analyses, both of which may influence training outcomes.

Addressing these limitations will be critical for advancing the field. Future studies should include multicenter, large-sample trials to validate generalizability and identify the most responsive subgroups of COPD patients with sarcopenia. Incorporating objective respiratory muscle tests, plethysmographic assessment, and biomarker analyses may help delineate the physiological pathways by which respiratory training exerts its effects. Longer-term follow-up is also warranted to establish the durability of benefits. In addition, combining respiratory training with nutritional support, anti-inflammatory therapies, and novel intelligent training devices may further enhance adherence, optimize outcomes, and provide a comprehensive rehabilitation strategy.

In summary, this study provides robust clinical evidence that respiratory training significantly improves diaphragmatic function, pulmonary function, exercise capacity, and quality of life in elderly COPD patients with sarcopenia. While the precise mechanisms remain to be elucidated, the observed multidimensional benefits support its integration into comprehensive management approaches for this high-risk population.

Conclusion

This study explored whether respiratory training could improve diaphragmatic function and overall health in elderly COPD patients with sarcopenia. The results showed that a 12-week respiratory training program significantly enhanced diaphragmatic excursion and thickness change rate, improved lung function, exercise capacity, and quality of life, supporting its value as a safe and effective rehabilitation strategy for this population.
