Solving Multiagent Path Finding on Highly Centralized Networks
Foivos Fioravantes, Dušan Knop, Jan Matyáš Křišťan, Nikolaos Melissinos, Michal Opler, Tung Anh Vu

Abstract
. The Mutliagent Path Finding (MAPF) problem consists of identifying the trajectories that a set of agents should follow inside a given network in order to reach their desired destinations as soon as possible, but without colliding with each other. We aim to minimize the maximum time any agent takes to reach their goal, ensuring optimal path length. In this work, we complement a recent thread of results that aim to systematically study the algorithmic behavior of this problem, through the parameterized complexity point of view. First, we show that MAPF is NP-hard when the given network has a star-like topology (bounded vertex cover number) or is a tree with 11 11 leaves. Both of these results fill important gaps in our understanding of the tractability of this problem that were left untreated in the recent work of Fioravantes et al., Exact Algorithms and Lowerbounds for Multiagent Path Finding: Power of Treelike Topology, presented in AAAI’24. Nevertheless, our main contribution is an exact algorithm that scales well as the input grows (FPT) when the topology of the given network is highly centralized (bounded distance to clique). This parameter is significant as it mirrors real-world networks. In such environments, a bunch of central hubs (e.g., processing areas) are connected to only few peripheral nodes.

1.
Introduction

Collision avoidance and optimal navigation are crucial in scenarios that require a group of agents to move through a network in an autonomous way. The real-world applications for such problems are well documented and range from virtual agents moving through a video game level
(
Snape et al., 2012
)
, to actual robots transporting goods around a warehouse
(
Wurman et al., 2008
;
Li et al., 2021
)
. Agents can be required to move through simpler topologies that span multiple “floors”
(
Veloso et al., 2015
)
, or much more complicated topologies on a single “floor”, e.g., in Airport surface management
(
Morris et al., 2016
)
.

Many different models have been proposed to capture and solve the scenarios described above
(
Stern et al., 2019
)
. This is due in part to the ubiquity of these scenarios, but also to the variety of slightly different requirements that each real-world application demands. For example, there are models where a set of positions must be reached, regardless of which agent reaches each one
(
Ali and Yakovlev, 2024
)
, while other models require each agent to reach a specific predefined position. Many models consider the centralized setting
(
Ma et al., 2019
;
Sharon et al., 2015
)
, while others consider the decentralized setting
(
Skrynnik et al., 2024
)
.

The version we consider, usually appearing under the name of
Multiagent Path Finding
\xspace
(
MAPF
\xspace
for short), consists of finding non-colliding paths for a set of agents that move through a given graph; each agent has to move from its source to its target vertex, both of which are predefined and unique, in an optimal way. That is, the agents move in such a way that the
length
of the schedule is minimized. To the best of our knowledge, this problem first appeared more than
30
years ago
(
Hopcroft et al., 1984
;
Reif, 1979
)
.

Interestingly, the
MAPF
\xspace
problem is computationally hard. In particular, it is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete
(
Surynek, 2010
)
, and the hardness holds even in planar graphs
(
Yu, 2016
)
. Thus, the research community has mainly focused on heuristic algorithms that lead to efficiently solving the problem in practice. Some first positive results were based on the
A^{*}
algorithm
(
Hart et al., 1968
)
, while, more recently, many researchers have focused on exploiting highly efficient solvers; SAT-solvers
(
Surynek et al., 2017
)
and SMT-solvers
(
Surynek, 2019
;
Surynek, 2020
)
. There is also a multitude of greedy heuristic algorithms, such as the one based on
Large Neighborhood Search
(
Phan et al., 2024
)

. See
(
Stern et al., 2019
)
for a more comprehensive list of approaches of this flavor.

We follow a slightly different approach to attack this problem. Our goal is to design exact algorithms that are guaranteed to return an optimum solution. In view of the computational hardness of the
MAPF
\xspace
problem, this is not possible in the general case. But what if we consider restricted instances? Can we identify characteristics, henceforth called
parameters
, whose exploitation leads to efficient algorithms? This is exactly the question at the core of the parameterized analysis that has recently been initiated for this problem
(
Eiben et al., 2023
;
Fioravantes et al., 2024
)
.

Our Contribution

The first two parameters that one would hope could be exploited are the number of agents
k
and the
makespan

\ell
, i.e., the total length of the schedule; these are exactly the two natural parameters of the
MAPF
\xspace
problem. Unfortunately, the results of
Fioravantes et al. (2024)
indicate that it is highly unlikely for either of these two parameters to be sufficient on their own.
Thus, we turn towards structural parameters of the problem. Both
(
Eiben et al., 2023
)
and
(
Fioravantes et al., 2024
)
provide a plethora of infeasibility results for many topologies which we could expect to come up in practical applications. Indeed, the
MAPF
\xspace
problem remains
\mathsf{NP}
-complete even when the input graph is a tree, or is planar and the makespan is at most
3
.

Our first result addresses a gap in the tractability landscape that started being painted in
(
Fioravantes et al., 2024
)
and it concerns graphs of bounded vertex cover number, a parameter whose exploitation usually leads to efficient algorithms (see, e.g.,
(
Fellows et al., 2009
)
). Recall that the vertex cover of a graph is a set of vertices
S
such that all the edges of the graph are incident to at least one vertex of
S
. Note that the rest of the vertices of the graph form an independent set (there are no edges between them). This is the key observation, as it means that in any round of the schedule, no more than
|S|
agents can move. So, intuitively, we could hope for an algorithm that would only focus on monitoring the agents that move through the vertices of
S
during every turn. Surprisingly, our first result shows that this approach will not work.

Theorem 1.

Multiagent Path Finding
\xspace
remains
\mathsf{NP}
-hard on trees with nine internal vertices and vertex cover number 7.

We stress here that the above result is based on the tree having many leaves.
In fact, if a tree has few internal vertices and leaves, the entire instance is bounded in size, and finding a solution cannot be intractable.
Therefore, we ask the following question: What if the tree has only a few leaves?
A generalization of this is captured by the parameter known as the max leaf number: the maximum number of leaves a spanning tree of a graph can have.
Note that the max leaf number is a parameter that limits the structure of the input in a rather drastic way—the graph is essentially a subdivided tree with few edges (consequently, few internal vertices and leaves).
Graphs admitting this structure appear quite often as transportation networks, in particular, underground trains
(
Eppstein, 2015
)
.
Indeed, many problems are tractable with respect to the max leaf number (see, e.g.,
(
Fellows et al., 2009
)
); a notable exception is the
Graph Motif
problem
(
Bonnet and Sikora, 2017
)
.
Here, we obtain yet another intractability result for this parameter.

Theorem 2.

Multiagent Path Finding
\xspace
remains
\mathsf{NP}
-hard on trees with
11
leaves.

Additionally, minor modifications to the previous theorem yield a hardness result in a more general setting involving semi-anonymous agents.
The input of the
Colored Multiagent Path Finding
\xspace
(
Colored MAPF
\xspace
) problem
(
Barták and Mestek, 2021
;
Barták et al., 2021
)
consists of a graph
G
, and multiple
types
of agents. All agents have their predefined starting positions, and each type of agents has a specific subset of the vertices of
G
which are the ending positions of that type; we are satisfied as long as every agent of a type ends up on
any
ending position of that type.

Theorem 3.

Colored MAPF
\xspace
remains
\mathsf{NP}
-hard even if there are only
6
groups of agents and
G
is a tree with
11
leaves.

All the results we have so far seem to imply that having a sparse topology is not helpful. So, what about dense topologies? Our final result, which is the main algorithmic contribution of this paper, is an efficient algorithm that solves
MAPF
\xspace
on graphs of bounded
distance to clique
. A graph
G
has distance to clique
d
if there is a set of
d
vertices
S
, whose removal renders the graph complete. Such topologies can be expected to appear in real-life applications where agents are required to move through a very dense network (the clique) which is connected to few docking stations (the vertices of
S
). We stress here that this is, to the best of our knowledge, the only positive result that exists that does not rely on the number of agents or makespan as parameters. In other words, the algorithm we construct remains useful even in instances where we have a large enough number of agents and/or long enough schedules that would make any previous exact algorithm infeasible in practice.

Theorem 4.

Multiagent Path Finding
\xspace
is in FPT parameterized by the distance to clique.

Preliminaries

We follow standard graph-theoretic notation
(
Diestel, 2012
)
.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, let
[a,b]=\{c\in\mathbb{Z}\mid a\leq c\leq b\}
and
[a]=[1,a]
.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.
For a subset of vertices
U\subseteq V
and a vertex
u\in V
we denote by
N_{U}(u)
the
neighborhood of
u
in
U
, that is, the set of vertices of
U
that are adjacent to
u
.
By
N_{U}[u]
we mean
N_{U}(u)\cup\{u\}
.
As a short-hand, we write
N_{G}=N_{V(G)}
.
When clear from the context, the subscripts will be omitted.
Finally, for any
S\subseteq V
, let
G[S]
be the subgraph of
G
that is induced by
S
, i.e., the graph that remains after deleting the vertices of
V\setminus S
from
G
(along with their incident edges).
For an
m
-
dimensional integer vector
\vec{u}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
, we write
\|\vec{u}\|_{p}=\big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{p}\big)^{1/p}
, i.e., its
\ell_{p}
-
norm.
By default,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the
\ell_{1}
-
norm.
For a function
f\colon A\to B
and
C\subseteq A
we write
f(C)=\{f(c)\mid c\in C\}
.

Formally, the input of the
Multiagent Path Finding
\xspace
(
MAPF
\xspace
) problem consists of a graph
G=(V,E)
, a set of agents
A
, two functions
s_{0}\colon A\rightarrow V
and
t\colon A\rightarrow V
and a positive integer
\ell
. For any pair
a,b\in A
where
a\neq b
, we have that
s_{0}(a)\neq s_{0}(b)
and
t(a)\neq t(b)
.
Initially, each agent
a\in A
is placed on the vertex
s_{0}(a)
. At specific times, called
turns
or
rounds
(we will use both interchangeably), the agents are allowed to move to a neighboring vertex, but are not obliged to do so. The agents can make at most one move per turn and each vertex can host at most one agent at a given turn. The position of the agents in the end of turn
i
(after the agents have moved) is given by an injective function
s_{i}\colon A\rightarrow V
.

We say that a schedule
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
is a
feasible solution
of an instance
\mathcal{I}=\langle G,A,s_{0},t,\ell\rangle
of
MAPF
\xspace
if:

(1)

s_{i}(a)\in N[s_{i-1}(a)]
for all
a\in A
and every
i\in[m]
,

(2)

s_{i}(a)\neq s_{i}(b)
for all
i\in[m]
and
a\neq b\in A
, and

(3)

s_{m}=t
.

A feasible solution
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
has
makespan

m
. A feasible solution of
minimum
makespan will be called
optimum
. Our goal is to decide if there exists a feasible solution of makespan
m\leq\ell
.

It is worth mentioning here that there are two principal variations of
MAPF
\xspace
. The first, more “generous” version, allows the agents to share edges if the wish to do so. That is, in this version, two agents
a
and
b
such that
s_{i}(a)=u,s_{i}(b)=v
, are allowed to move so that
s_{i+1}(a)=v,s_{i+1}(b)=u
if
u
and
v
are connected through a unique edge. The second, more “restrictive”, version does not allow this behavior. In this work we focus on the restrictive version.

Parameterized Complexity.

Parameterized complexity is a field in algorithm design that takes into consideration additional measures to determine the time complexity. In some sense, it is about the multidimensional analysis of the time complexity of an algorithm, each dimension being dedicated to its own parameter.
Formally, a parameterized problem is a set of instances
(x,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
;
k
is referred to as the
parameter
.
The goal in this paradigm is to design
Fixed-Parameter Tractable
(FPT) algorithms, i.e., an algorithm that solves the problem
f(k)|x|^{O(1)}
time for any arbitrary computable function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
. We say that a problem
is in FPT
if it admits an FPT algorithm. We refer the interested reader to classical monographs
(
Cygan et al., 2015
;
Downey and Fellows, 2012
)
for a more comprehensive introduction to this topic.

Structural Parameters.

There are three structural parameters that we consider in this work. Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.

First we have the
vertex cover number
of
G
. A set
S\subseteq V
is called a
vertex cover
of
G
if for every edge
uv\in E
we have either
u\in S
or
v\in S
(or both). The vertex cover number of
G
is the size of a minimum vertex cover of
G
. This is a parameter that can be computed in FPT time, e.g., by
(
Chen et al., 2006
)
.

Next, we will consider the
distance to clique
of
G
. This is defined as the size of a minimum set
S\subseteq V
such that
G[V\setminus S]
is a clique (a graph where each vertex is adjacent to all other vertices of the graph); the size of such a set is denoted by
\textit{dc}(G)
. The distance to clique of
G
can be computed in FPT time by computing the vertex cover number of the
complement of

G
. Recall that the complement of a graph
G
is the graph
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})
, where
uv\in E^{\prime}
if and only if
uv\not\in E
.

Finally, the
max leaf number
of
G
is defined as the maximum number of leaves (i.e., vertices with only one neighbor) in a
spanning tree
of
G
.
Recall that
T
is a spanning tree of
G
if it is a subgraph of
G
with the same vertex set same as
G
and
T
is a tree (i.e., it is conncted and contains no cycles).
The max leaf number of a given graph can also be computed in FPT time
(
Fellows and Langston, 1992
)
.

High-level idea of the reduction.

On a high level, the reduction works as follows.
First, the bow ties play the role of gates, forcing the agents of
A^{X}
and
A^{Y}
to only move on the (shortest) path from their starting position to their terminal position, and without doing any “back and forth” once they begin traveling towards their destination.
Then, the choice of

, the agents of

Notice also that, in order for any agent that starts on a red edge to move towards their goal, they have to pass through either

Also, in order to have enough empty vertices so that the agents (that start on a red edge) can “rotate”, one of them must occupy

This forces the occupation of the vertices

This, essentially, sets a global clock according to which the vertices

are unoccupied only during specific turns.
Moreover, we are able to prove that in any optimal solution of

\varphi

This subsection is dedicated to the proof of Theorem

In this section, we prove the main positive result of this paper.
First, we prove that the

Throughout this section, there is a notion of

\xspace

m=\kappa\leq\big(3(2|M|+2)^{|M|}+2\big)
, we can compute a feasible solution
s^{\prime}=s^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,s^{\prime}_{m}
of
\mathcal{K}
such that
|s^{\prime}_{i}(A^{\prime})\cap M|=|s_{i}(A)\cap M|\geq|A|-|Q|
, for all
i\in[m]
.

Proof.

First, let
A^{M}_{\sigma,\tau}\subseteq A_{\sigma,\tau}
be the set of agents such that
\alpha\in A^{M}_{\sigma,\tau}
if and only if
s_{i-1}(\alpha)\in M
for some
i\in[m+1]
. Notice that
|A^{M}_{\sigma,\tau}|\leq\min\{\kappa,|A_{\sigma,\tau}|\}=|A^{\prime}_{\sigma,\tau}|
for all
(\sigma,\tau)\in[2^{|M|}]\times[2^{|M|}]
(since there are at most
\kappa
agents that are going to pass through a vertex of
M
throughout the schedule).

We continue with some definitions. For any
\tau\in[2^{|M|}]
, if
U_{\tau}\neq Q_{\tau}
, we will select a set
U^{\prime}_{\tau}\subseteq U_{\tau}\setminus\big(s^{\prime}_{0}(A^{\prime})\cup t^{\prime}(A^{\prime})\big)
such that
|U^{\prime}_{\tau}|=|A^{\prime}|
.
Notice that
U_{\tau}\setminus\big(s^{\prime}_{0}(A^{\prime})\cup t^{\prime}(A^{\prime})\big)
has enough vertices for such a selection and that the selection is arbitrary.
For the rest of the integers
\tau\in[2^{|M|}]
(i.e., if
U_{\tau}\neq Q_{\tau}
), we set
U^{\prime}_{\tau}=\emptyset
.
We will define an (arbitrary) enumeration of the agents
A^{\prime}
and an (arbitrary) enumeration of the vertices of each set
U^{\prime}_{\tau}
.
From now on, we will assume that
A^{\prime}=\{\alpha^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\alpha^{\prime}_{|A^{\prime}|}\}
and
U^{\prime}_{\tau}=\{u_{1}^{\tau},\ldots,u_{|A^{\prime}|}^{\tau}\}
, for each
\tau\in[2^{|M|}]
where
U^{\prime}_{\tau}\neq\emptyset
.

Now, we will show that there exists a matching between agents of
A^{\prime}
and
A
which we will use to create
s^{\prime}
. The idea behind this is that we can pair an agent
\alpha^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}
with an agent
\alpha\in A
, which allows us to use the trajectory of
\alpha
in
G
to compute the trajectory of
\alpha^{\prime}
in
G[U]
.

To do so, we first define a set of agents
B
as follows.
Initially
B
contains exactly the agents of
A_{0}
.
For each agent type
(\sigma,\tau)\in[2^{|M|}]\times[2^{|M|}]
, we construct a set
B_{\sigma,\tau}
such that
A^{M}_{\sigma,\tau}\subseteq B_{\sigma,\tau}\subseteq A_{\sigma,\tau}
and
|B_{\sigma,\tau}|=|A^{\prime}_{\sigma},\tau|
.
Notice that, by construction,
|A^{M}_{\sigma},\tau|\leq|A^{\prime}_{\sigma},\tau|\leq|A_{\sigma},\tau|
and, therefore, we have enough agents. Also, the selection of the agents of
B_{\sigma,\tau}\setminus A^{M}_{\sigma,\tau}
is arbitrary. We set
B=\bigcup_{(\sigma,\tau)\in[2^{|M|}]\times[2^{|M|}]}B_{\sigma,\tau}\cup A_{0}
.

We now define the matching we will use as a function
N\colon A^{\prime}\rightarrow B
such that:

•

|N(A^{\prime})|=|A^{\prime}|
(i.e.,
N
is a matching),

•

for all
\alpha\in A_{0}
we have
N(\alpha^{\prime})=\alpha^{\prime}
,

•

for all
A^{\prime}_{\sigma,\tau}=A_{\sigma,\tau}
, for some
(\sigma,\tau)\in[2^{|M|}]\times[2^{|M|}]
, and
\alpha^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}_{\sigma,\tau}
, we have
N(\alpha^{\prime})=\alpha^{\prime}
and
for all
A^{\prime}_{\sigma,\tau}\neq A_{\sigma,\tau}
, we have
N(A^{\prime}_{\sigma,\tau})=B_{\sigma,\tau}
.

Notice that if
A^{\prime}_{\sigma,\tau}\neq A_{\sigma,\tau}
, the matching between the vertices
A^{\prime}_{\sigma,\tau}
and
B_{\sigma,\tau}
is arbitrary.
Also, such a matching between
A^{\prime}
and
B
always exists.

We are now ready to use the matching
N
to create a feasible solution
s^{\prime}=s^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,s^{\prime}_{m}
for
\mathcal{K}
, starting from a feasible solution
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
for
\mathcal{I}
.

Let
\alpha_{i}=N(\alpha^{\prime}_{i})
for all
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\in A^{\prime}
.
To construct
s^{\prime}
we proceed as follows:

•

First, we set
s^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{i})=t^{\prime}(\alpha^{\prime}_{i})
, for each
i\in[|A^{\prime}|]
.

•

For each
i\in[m-1]
and each
p\in|A^{\prime}|
, if
s_{i}(\alpha_{p})\in M
, then we set
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s_{i}(\alpha_{p})
. Otherwise,
s_{i}(\alpha_{p})\in Q
.

•

Then, for each
i\in[m-1]
and each
p\in|A^{\prime}|
, let
\tau\in 2^{|M|}
be the vertex type such that
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha_{p})\in Q_{\tau}
.

–

If
U^{\prime}_{\tau}=\emptyset
, then we set
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s_{i}(\alpha_{p})
.

–

Otherwise, if
s_{i}(\alpha_{p})\in s_{0}(A^{\prime})\cup t(A^{\prime})
, then
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha_{p})=s_{i}(\alpha_{p})
.

–

Otherwise,
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha_{p})=u_{p}^{\tau}
.

We claim that the schedule
s^{\prime}
constructed this way is feasible. To do that, we need to show that
s^{\prime}
is collision-free and swap-free.

The schedule
s^{\prime}
is collision-free.

To start, observe that for each
i\in[0,m]
and
p\in[|A|]
, we have that either
s^{\prime}_{i},(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s_{i},(\alpha_{p})
, or the vertices
s^{\prime}_{i-1},(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})
and
s_{i-1},(\alpha_{p})
have the same vertex type
\tau\in[2^{|M|}]
(i.e.,
s^{\prime}_{i-1},(\alpha^{\prime}_{p}),s_{i-1},(\alpha_{p})\in Q_{\tau}
, for some
\tau\in[2^{|M|}]
).
This is indeed clear for all
i\in[m-1]
by the construction of
s^{\prime}_{i}
. To see that this is also true for
i\in\{0,m\}
, recall that
\alpha^{\prime}_{p}
and
\alpha_{p}
belong to the same agent type
(\mu,\sigma)\in[2^{|M|}]\times[2^{|M|}]
. That is,
\alpha_{p},\alpha^{\prime}_{p}\in A_{\mu,\sigma}
for some
(\mu,\sigma)\in[2^{|M|}]\times[2^{|M|}]
. This means that
s_{0}(\alpha_{p}),s^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})\in Q_{\mu}
and
t(\alpha_{p}),t^{\prime}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})\in Q_{\sigma}
. This covers the case
i=0
.
For
i=m
we need to additionally observe that
t(\alpha_{p})=s_{m}(\alpha_{p})
(by the feasibility of
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
) and
s^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=t^{\prime}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})
.

Thus, for each agent
\alpha^{\prime}_{p}\in A^{\prime}
and each
i\in[m]
, it holds that
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})\in N_{G[U]}[s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})]
.
Indeed, since
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})
has the same vertex type as
s_{i}(\alpha_{p})
, we have that
N_{G}[s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})]=N_{G}[s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})]
.
Similarly, we have that
N_{G}[s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})]=N_{G}[s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})]
.
Since
s_{i}(\alpha_{p})\in N_{G}[s_{i-1}(\alpha_{p})]
, we conclude that
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})\in N_{G}[s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})]
. Finally, since
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})\in U
and
N_{G[U]}[s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})]=U\cap N_{G}[s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})]
, we have that
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})\in N_{G[U]}[s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})]
.

Next, we prove that in
s^{\prime}
there is no turn where two agents occupy the same vertex. Formally, for any
i\in[m]
and pair of agents
\alpha^{\prime}_{p},\alpha^{\prime}_{q}\in A^{\prime}
, where
p\neq q
, we have that
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})\neq s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})
.
Indeed, assume that this statement is not true and let
i\in[m]
and
\alpha^{\prime}_{p},\alpha^{\prime}_{q}\in A^{\prime}
with
p\neq q
, be such that
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=w
.
Note that the claimed statement does hold for
i=m
. Indeed,
s^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=t^{\prime}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})
for all
\alpha^{\prime}_{p}\in A^{\prime}
. So, we may assume that
i\in[m-1]
.
Let
\tau\in[2^{|M|}]
be the vertex type of
w
. That is,
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=w\in Q_{\tau}
. We can assume that
U_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}
as, otherwise, by the construction of
s^{\prime}_{i}
, we would have that
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=u^{\tau}_{p}\neq u^{\tau}_{q}=s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})
(as
p\neq q
). Since
U_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}
, it follows by the construction of
s^{\prime}_{i}
that
w=s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s_{i}(\alpha_{p})
and
w=s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=s_{i}(\alpha_{q})
. This means that
s_{i}(\alpha_{p})=s_{i}(\alpha_{q})
which is a contradiction to the assumption that
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
is a feasible solution of
\mathcal{I}
.

The schedule
s^{\prime}
is swap-free.

We first claim that if
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
contains no swaps, then the potential swaps of
s^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,s^{\prime}_{m}
may appear only between the turns
s^{\prime}_{0},s^{\prime}_{1}
or
s^{\prime}_{m-1},s^{\prime}_{m}
. Formally, if
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
does not include any swaps then,
for any
i\in[m-2]
and agents
\alpha^{\prime}_{p},\alpha^{\prime}_{q}\in A^{\prime}
, where
p\neq q
, we have that
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})\neq s^{\prime}_{i+1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})
or
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})\neq s^{\prime}_{i+1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})
.

Indeed, assume that there exists a turn
i\in[m+1]
and two agents
\alpha^{\prime}_{p},\alpha^{\prime}_{q}\in A^{\prime}
, with
p\neq q
, such that
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{i+1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=v
and
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=s^{\prime}_{i+1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=u
. Let
v\in Q_{\tau}
and
u\in Q_{\sigma}
, for two (not necessarily different) vertex types
\sigma,\tau\in[2^{|M|}]
.
We claim that
U_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}
and
U_{\sigma}=Q_{\sigma}
.
Assume otherwise and, w.l.o.g., let
U_{\tau}\neq Q_{\tau}
.
Notice that at least one of
i-1
and
i
must be in
[m-1]
.
Let
j\in\{i-1,i\}\cap[m-1]
. Then either
v=s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=u^{\tau}_{p}
(if
j=i-1
) or
v=s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=u^{\tau}_{q}
(if
j=i
).
In the first case, we have
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=v=u^{\tau}_{p}
which is a contradiction since (by the construction of
s^{\prime}_{i}
)
\alpha^{\prime}_{q}
either occupies
u^{\tau}_{q}\neq u^{\tau}_{p}
or
t^{\prime}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})\notin U_{\tau}
(while
u^{\tau}_{p}\in U_{\tau}
).
In the second case, we have that
s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=v=u^{\tau}_{q}
, which is a contradiction since, by construction of
s^{\prime}_{i}
,
\alpha^{\prime}_{p}
either occupies
u^{\tau}_{p}\neq u^{\tau}_{q}
or
s^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})\notin U_{\tau}
(while
u^{\tau}_{q}\in U_{\tau}
).
So, it holds that
U_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}
and
U_{\sigma}=Q_{\sigma}
. Consider two turns
0<i-1<i<m
. By the construction of
s^{\prime}_{i-1}
and
s^{\prime}_{i}
, we have that
s_{i-1}(\alpha_{p})=s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=s_{i}(\alpha_{q})
and
s_{i-1}(\alpha_{q})=s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s_{i}(\alpha_{p})
. Therefore, this swap appears between
s^{\prime}_{i-1}
and
s^{\prime}_{i}
only if there exists a swap between
s_{i-1}
and
s_{i}
.

It remains to show that there are no swaps between
s^{\prime}_{0},s^{\prime}_{1}
or
s^{\prime}_{m-1},s^{\prime}_{m}
.
Assume that this is not true, and let
\alpha^{\prime}_{p}
and
\alpha^{\prime}_{q}
,
p\neq q
, be two agents such that
s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=w
and
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=w^{\prime}
for some
i\in\{1,m\}
. First observe that
w\notin U_{\tau}
such that
U_{\tau}\neq Q_{\tau}
. Indeed, assuming otherwise means that
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=s^{\prime}_{1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=u^{\tau}_{q}
or
s^{\prime}_{i-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{m-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=u^{\tau}_{p}
.
By the selection of
U^{\prime}_{\tau}
, either
w=u^{\tau}_{q}\neq s^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=w
or
w=u^{\tau}_{p}\neq t^{\prime}(\alpha_{q})=s^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=w
. This is a contradiction. Using the similar arguments we can prove that
w^{\prime}\notin U_{\tau}
such that
U_{\tau}\neq Q_{\tau}
.
Therefore,
w,w^{\prime}\in M\cup\{U_{\tau}\mid\tau\in[2^{|M|}]\text{ and }U_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}\}
.

The final ingredient we need in order to show that there are no swaps in
s^{\prime}_{1},\dots,s^{\prime}_{m}
is to prove that:

Claim 11.

N(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=\alpha^{\prime}_{p}
and
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=\alpha^{\prime}_{q}
.

Proof of the claim.

Notice that if
A^{\prime}=A
then this is trivially true. Therefore, we may assume that
A^{\prime}=A^{p}
.

First, if
w,w^{\prime}\in M
, then either
s^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p}),s^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})\in M
or
s^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q}),s^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})\in M
(depending on whether the swap happens in the first or the last turn). Therefore,
\alpha^{\prime}_{p},\alpha^{\prime}_{q}\in A_{0}
and thus,
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=\alpha^{\prime}_{p}
and
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=\alpha^{\prime}_{q}
(by the selection of
N
).

Therefore, we can assume that at least one of
w
and
w^{\prime}
is not in
M
. W.l.o.g., assume that
w\notin M
. Then, there exists a vertex type
\tau\in[2^{|M|}]
such that
w\in U_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}
. Therefore, either
\alpha^{\prime}_{p}\in A^{\prime}_{\tau,\sigma}
or
\alpha^{\prime}_{q}\in A^{\prime}_{\sigma,\tau}
(depending on whether the swap happens in the first or the last turn). Observe that
A^{\prime}_{\tau,\sigma}=A_{\tau,\sigma}
and
A^{\prime}_{\sigma,\tau}=A_{\sigma,\tau}
. Indeed, since
U_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}
, we have that
Q_{\tau}\leq 3|A^{\prime}|=3|A^{p}|
. Therefore, by the construction of
A^{p}
, we have that
A_{\tau,\sigma}\cup A_{\sigma,\tau}\subseteq A^{p}=A^{\prime}
. Therefore, by the selection of
N
, we have that:

•

either the swap happens in the first turn and
\alpha^{\prime}_{p}\in A^{\prime}_{\tau,\sigma}=A_{\tau,\sigma}
or

•

the swap happens in the last turn and
\alpha^{\prime}_{q}\in A^{\prime}_{\sigma,\tau}=A_{\sigma,\tau}
.

Also, by the selection of
N
we have that:

•

either the swap happens in the first turn and
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=\alpha^{\prime}_{p}
or

•

the swap happens in the last turn and
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=\alpha^{\prime}_{q}
.

\diamond

It remains to show that, if the swap happens in the first turn then
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=\alpha^{\prime}_{q}
otherwise
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=\alpha^{\prime}_{p}
.

Case a.
(the swap happens in the first turn): If
s^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=w^{\prime}\in M
, then
\alpha^{\prime}_{q}\in A_{0}
. Thus, by the selection of
N
, it follows that
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=\alpha^{\prime}_{q}
. Therefore, we may assume that there exists a vertex type
\phi\in[2^{|M|}]
such that
s^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=w^{\prime}\in U_{\phi}=Q_{\phi}
. Using similar arguments as before, we can conclude that there also exists a vertex type
\psi\in[2^{|M|}]
such that
\alpha^{\prime}_{q}\in A^{\prime}_{\phi,\psi}=A_{\phi,\psi}
. Thus,
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=\alpha^{\prime}_{q}
.

Case b.
(the swap happens in the last turn): If
t^{\prime}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=w^{\prime}\in M
, then
\alpha^{\prime}_{p}\in A_{0}
. Thus, by the selection of
N
, we have that
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=\alpha^{\prime}_{p}
. Therefore, we may assume that there exists a vertex type
\phi\in[2^{|M|}]
such that
t^{\prime}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=w^{\prime}\in U_{\phi}=Q_{\phi}
. Using similar arguments as before, we can conclude that there also exists a vertex type
\psi\in[2^{|M|}]
such that
\alpha^{\prime}_{p}\in A^{\prime}_{\psi,\phi}=A_{\psi,\phi}
. Thus,
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=\alpha^{\prime}_{p}
.

We are finally ready to show that
s^{\prime}
contains no swaps. Let us assume that this schedule contains a swap. By the discussion above, this swap is between two agents
\alpha^{\prime}_{p}
and
\alpha^{\prime}_{q}
where
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=\alpha^{\prime}_{p}
and
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=\alpha^{\prime}_{q}
, and happens either in the first turn or in the last. We will show that this leads to a contradiction.

Assume that the swap happens in the first turn.
Recall that
s^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=w
and
s^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=w^{\prime}
. We have also proven that
w,w^{\prime}\in M\cup\{U_{\tau}\mid\tau\in[2^{|M|}]\text{ and }U_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}\}
.
Since
s^{\prime}_{1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=w
and
w\in M\cup\{U_{\tau}\mid\tau\in[2^{|M|}]\text{ and }U_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}\}
, we have that
s^{\prime}_{1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=s_{1}(N(\alpha^{\prime}_{q}))=s_{1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})
. Similarly, since
s^{\prime}_{1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=w^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime}\in M\cup\{U_{\tau}\mid\tau\in[2^{|M|}]\text{ and }U_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}\}
, we have that
s^{\prime}_{1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s_{1}(N(\alpha^{\prime}_{p}))=s_{1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})
.
Also, since
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=\alpha^{\prime}_{p}
and
N(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=\alpha^{\prime}_{q}
, we have that
s_{0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})
and
s_{0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=s^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})
. Therefore, there exists a swap in the feasible solution
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
of
\mathcal{I}
. This is a contradiction.

We will prove the same happens in the case where the swap happen in the last turn. In this case we have that
s^{\prime}_{m-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=w
and
s^{\prime}_{m-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=w^{\prime}
. We have also proven that
w,w^{\prime}\in M\cup\{U_{\tau}\mid\tau\in[2^{|M|}]\text{ and }U_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}\}
.
Since
s^{\prime}_{m-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=w
and
w\in M\cup\{U_{\tau}\mid\tau\in[2^{|M|}]\text{ and }U_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}\}
, we have that
s^{\prime}_{m-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s_{m-1}(N(\alpha^{\prime}_{p}))=s_{m-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})
. Similarly, since
s^{\prime}_{m-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=w^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime}\in M\cup\{U_{\tau}\mid\tau\in[2^{|M|}]\text{ and }U_{\tau}=Q_{\tau}\}
, we have that
s^{\prime}_{m-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=s_{m-1}(N(\alpha^{\prime}_{q}))=s_{m-1}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})
.
Also, by the construction of
s^{\prime}_{m}
and
t^{\prime}
, we have that
s_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=t(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=t^{\prime}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})=s^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{p})
and
s_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=t(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=t^{\prime}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})=s^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha^{\prime}_{q})
. Therefore, there exists a swap in the feasible solution
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
of
\mathcal{I}
. This is a contradiction.

Therefore,
s^{\prime}
is a feasible solution for
\mathcal{K}
. It remains to prove that
|s^{\prime}_{i}(A^{\prime})\cap M|=|s_{i}(A)\cap M|
for all
i\in[m]
. Before doing so, notice that
|s_{i}(A)\cap M|\geq|A|-|Q|
, as otherwise there is not enough space in
|s_{i}(A)\cap Q|>|Q|
which contradicts the feasibility of
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
.

Claim 12.

For the feasible solution
s^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{K}
, it holds that
|s^{\prime}_{i}(A^{\prime})\cap M|=|s_{i}(A)\cap M|
for all
i\in[m]
.

Proof of the claim.

Notice that
s^{\prime}_{0}(A^{\prime})=s_{0}(A^{\prime})
and and
s^{\prime}_{m}(A^{\prime})=t^{\prime}(A^{\prime})=t(A^{\prime})
. Therefore, the claim is trivial for the turns
0
and
m
.

Assume that there exists a turn
i\in[m-1]
such that
|s^{\prime}_{i}(A^{\prime})\cap M|<k
. Let
A^{\prime}_{i}
be the set of agents such that
s^{\prime}_{i}(A^{\prime}_{i})=s^{\prime}_{i}(A^{\prime})\cap M
and
A_{i}
the set of agents such that
s_{i}(A_{i})=s_{i}(A)\cap M
. Also, recall that
|A_{i}|\geq k
by the selection of
k
.
Moreover, by construction,
B\supseteq A_{i}
(recall that
A^{\prime}
is matched to
B\subseteq A
by
N
).

Therefore, it suffices to prove that
|A^{\prime}\setminus A^{\prime}_{i}|\leq|B\setminus A_{i}|\leq|B|-k=|A^{\prime}|-k
. This holds if
N(A^{\prime}\setminus A^{\prime}_{i})\subseteq B\setminus A_{i}
.
To prove this, consider an agent
\alpha^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}\setminus A^{\prime}_{i}
. Since
s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime})\in Q
, we can conclude that
s_{i}(N(\alpha^{\prime}))=s^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha^{\prime})\in Q
(by the construction of
s^{\prime}_{i}
). Therefore,
N(\alpha^{\prime})\in B\setminus A_{i}
.
Thus
N(A^{\prime}\setminus A^{\prime}_{i})\subseteq B\setminus A_{i}
.

\diamond

This concludes the proof of Lemma

Lemma 0.

Proof.

Let

The following observation allows us to use

Observation 3.

Any feasible solution of

The previous observation allows us to compute an optimal solution of

5.
Conclusion

In this paper we continued down the path of studying the parameterized complexity of the
Multiagent Path Finding
\xspace
problem, an approach that has recently started gathering interest. Although the intractability of the problem had already been established even for restrictive cases, our hardness results are quite unexpected. Indeed, we stress a graph having bounded vertex cover or max leaf number has a very restricted structure, which leads to FPT algorithms the vast majority of times. Thus, our results in Theorems
1
and
2
are of great theoretical importance. Indeed,
MAPF
\xspace
has the potential to be among the few candidates to give birth to reductions that show hardness when considering these parameters for a plethora of other problems. Moreover, these results provide further motivation towards a more heuristic approach. On the other hand, the FPT algorithm we presented has the potential to be of great practical use, depending on the specific characteristics of the topology on which the
MAPF
\xspace
problem has to be solved, which can be the object of a dedicated study.
