Bayesian inference for the fractional Calderón problem with a single measurement
Pu-Zhao Kow, Janne Nurminen, Jesse Railo

Abstract
. This paper investigates the consistency of a posterior distribution in the single-measurement fractional Calderón problem with additive Gaussian noise. We consider a Bayesian framework with rescaled and Gaussian sieve priors, using a collection of noisy, discrete observations taken from a suitable exterior domain. Our main result shows that the posterior distribution concentrates around the true parameter as the number of measurements increases. Furthermore, we establish tight convergence rates for the reconstruction error of the posterior mean. A central technical challenge is to obtain refined stability estimates for both the forward and inverse problems. In particular, the required forward estimates are delicate to obtain because the fractional elliptic problems do not enjoy as strong regularity theory as their classical counterparts.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega
be a nonempty bounded smooth domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
(-\Delta)^{s}
denote the fractional Laplacian of order
s\in(0,1)
, which admits at least ten equivalent definitions under suitable interpretations
[
Kwa17
]
.
We fix any
0\not\equiv\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{e})
with
\Omega_{e}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
, and consider the following Dirichlet problem:

(1.1)

((-\Delta)^{s}+f)u=0\text{ in $\Omega$},\quad u|_{\Omega_{e}}=\phi.

In this work we study an inverse problem for the fractional Schrödinger equation which concerns the recovery of the potential
f
from the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map (DN map)

(1.2)

\Lambda_{f}\phi\equiv\left.(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f}\right|_{\mathcal{D}},

where
\mathcal{D}\subset\Omega_{e}
is a fixed open set. It was proved in
[
GSU20
]
that the potential can be uniquely determined from exterior measurements and, remarkably, a single-point measurement suffices
[
GRSU20
]
.

The nonlocal Dirichlet problem (
1.1
) has been extensively studied in, e.g.,
[
Gru15
,
RO16
]
. Nonlocal problems of this type also arise naturally in various applications. For instance, nonlocal models describe animal movement
[
VAB+96
]
, foraging patterns of marine predators
[
HQD+10
]
, the dynamics of competing populations sharing the same resource
[
MV17
]
and in quantum optics
[
KS22
,
KS23
,
OHKSW24
]
. Moreover, the fractional Laplacian appears in the linear problem of unique continuation from partial data Radon transforms in integral geometry and X-ray tomography
[
IM20
,
CMR21
,
IKS25
]
. Nonlocal problems also play a central role in physics, fluid dynamics, finance, and image processing, see
[
RO16
,
BV16
]
for further references. For inverse problems related to (
1.1
) we mention
[
CMRU22
,
FGKU25
,
FGK+25
]
and the monograph
[
LL25
]
. Further nonlinear variants are studied in
[
KRZ23
,
KMS23
,
KW23
]
.

We consider a statistical model in which we randomly choose finitely many uniformly distributed points from
\mathcal{D}
. A priori (
1.2
) is not pointwise well-defined but
Lemma

3.1
shows it to be smooth when restricted to
\mathcal{D}
for a smooth exterior value
\phi
. Then the value of the DN map at these points, with added noise, creates our measurements. We model
f
with rescaled Gaussian priors and Gaussian sieve priors. Then we study the concentration and contraction rates, also the optimality of these rates, of posterior distributions arising from these priors.

This approach to studying the Calderón problem was iniated in
[
AN19
]
where the authors introduced a statistical inverse problem for the isotropic conductivity equation. Then the approach was generalized in
[
GN20
]
in the context of determining a conductivity from a source-to-solution map. The authors of
[
GN20
]
used priors that were inspired by previous consistent inversion of noisy non-abelian
X
-ray transforms
[
MNP21
]
. Further results for various inverse problems with similar techniques can be found in
[
FKW24
,
GK20
,
Kek22
,
KW25
,
NvdGW20
]
and the references therein.

Our main results show that an estimator, here we use the posterior mean, converges to the ground truth that creates the data. The convergence rate is logarithmic and is based on the stability of the inverse problem
[
Rül21
]
. Furthermore we prove that this convergence rate is optimal in the statistical minimax sense and this is based on the instability result for the inverse problem
[
RS18
]
(see also
[
Man01
]
for the local version and
[
KRS21
]
for a more general setting for instability).
To prove these results we follow the general framework developed in
[
GN20
]
. In order to use this framework we need to analyze the forward and inverse problem in more detail.

For the forward problem we need our forward map
f\mapsto\Lambda_{f}\phi
to be uniformly bounded on its domain and a suitable
L^{2}
-based local Lipschitz forward estimate (see
Section

3.1
). The uniform boundedness is proved via the singular integral definition of the fractional Laplacian. To prove the forward estimate we analyze the DN map using the following observations. Firstly we use that the DN map is smooth in the exterior domain when we assign a smooth exterior value and thus we can use the strong definition of the DN map (see
[
GSU20
]
). We use this together with the weak definition of the DN map and the Alessandrini identity. The trick is that in the Alessandrini identity we can choose up to three different functions to appear in the identity. Choosing these in a clever way, using a stronger elliptic estimate from
[
CRZ22
]
and in the end combining this with an approximation argument using cutoffs gives the desired estimate. See
Lemma

3.3
for further details.

For the inverse problem we need a stability estimate, in particular a stability estimate for a single measurement Calderón problem (for full data this was proven in
[
RS20
]
). A stability estimate is proved in
[
Rül21
]
but we need to refine it in order to make visible the expressions for
\lVert f_{1}\rVert_{C^{s}(\Omega)}
and
\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{C^{s}(\Omega)}
. Deriving this estimate requires modifying some of the arguments in
[
Rül21
,
GRSU20
]
and using tools developed in these articles.
Once we have obtained the required estimates, we prove our main results in the spirit of
[
GN20
]
.

Rest of the paper is as follows. In
Section

2
we introduce more precisely the fractional Schrödinger equation and the statistical model for our inverse problem and furthermore the main results of our work.
Section

3
is dedicated to estimates related to the forward and inverse problems. In
Section

4
we prove the main results and finally in
Section

5
we illustrate numerically the convergence of the Bayesian procedure.

2.
Main results

Let
\Omega
be a nonempty bounded smooth domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
(-\Delta)^{s}
denote the fractional Laplacian of order
s\in(0,1)
.
We fix any
0\not\equiv\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{e})
with
\Omega_{e}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
, and consider the following Dirichlet problem:

(2.1)

((-\Delta)^{s}+f)u=0\text{ in $\Omega$},\quad u|_{\Omega_{e}}=\phi,

where
\Omega_{e}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. We assume that
0\leq f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, which ensures the existence and uniqueness of a solution
u=u_{f}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to (
2.1
), where
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the
L^{2}
-based Hilbert space defined via Fourier transform
[
GSU20
, Lemma 2.3]
. The nonnegativity of
f
plays a crucial rule in ensuring the
L^{\infty}
-bound

(2.2)

\lVert u_{f}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{e})},

which is a consequence of maximum principle
[
LL19
, Proposition 3.3]
.

It is known that
\left.(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f}\right|_{\Omega_{e}}
is well-defined in
H^{-s}(\Omega_{e})
. Accordingly, the Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DN) map
\Lambda_{f}:C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{e})\rightarrow H^{-s}(\Omega_{e})
is defined by
\Lambda_{f}\phi:=\left.(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f}\right|_{\mathcal{D}}
. Fix any open set
\mathcal{D}\subset\Omega_{e}
. It was showed in
[
GRSU20
]
that one can uniquely determine such potential
f\in C^{0}(\Omega)
from the knowledge of

(2.3)

G(f):=\Lambda_{f}\phi\equiv\left.(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f}\right|_{\mathcal{D}},

for any fixed
0\not\equiv\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{e})
, which is mainly based on the following antilocality property for fractional Laplacian
[
GSU20
, Theorem 1.2]
:

(2.4)

\begin{array}[]{c}\text{if $w\in H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ for some $r\in\mathbb{R}$ and there exists $s>0$ such that}\\
\text{$w=(-\Delta)^{s}w=0$ in some open set in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$, then $w=0$ in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$.}\end{array}

Accordingly, the operator
G:C^{0}(\Omega)\rightarrow H^{-s}(\mathcal{D})
is called the
forward operator
.
By carefully quantifying the arguments, a logarithmic stability is derived in
[
Rül21
]
when
f\in C^{s}(\Omega)
.
Later in
Lemma

3.1
below, we will show that
(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{e})
, thereby ensuring that the measurement
G(f)
is pointwise well-defined. However, it is still impractical to obtain full measurement
G(f)
on
\mathcal{D}
, therefore we develop a probabilistic approach with a discrete set of measurements for this problem.

2.1.
The statistical model

We randomly choose
N\in\mathbb{N}
points from a uniform distribution on
\mathcal{D}
, that is, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the random variables

\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\overset{\rm iid}{\sim}\mu,\quad\mu=\mathrm{d}x/\lvert\mathcal{D}\rvert,

where
\mathrm{d}x
is the usual Lebesgue measure and
\lvert\mathcal{D}\rvert
is the Lebesgue measure of
\mathcal{D}
. It is more realistic to measure

(2.5)

G(f)(x_{i})=\left((-\Delta)^{s}u_{f}\right)(x_{i}),

where
x_{i}
are sample points randomly chosen according to the random variable
X_{i}
. We assume the measurement model with a fixed noise level
\sigma>0
as follows:

(2.6)

Y_{i}=G(f)(X_{i})+\sigma W_{i},\quad\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\overset{\rm iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is the normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 1. We also assume that
W^{(N)}:=\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
X^{(N)}:=\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are independent. We are interested in the inference of
f
from the observational data
(Y^{(N)},X^{(N)})
with
Y^{(N)}:=\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be a bounded smooth domain with
\overline{\mathcal{O}}\subset\Omega
and let
M_{0}>1
be a constant and we consider the following space of parameters:

\mathscr{F}_{M_{0}}^{\beta}(\mathcal{O}):=\left\{f\in H^{\beta}(\overline{\Omega}):0<f<M_{0},f|_{\Omega\setminus\mathcal{O}}=1,\left.\partial_{\nu}^{j}f\right|_{\partial\mathcal{O}}=0\,\text{ for all $1\leq j\leq\beta-1$}\right\}

where
\partial_{\nu}
is the normal derivative in the sense of
[
LM72
, Theorem 9.4, Chapter 1]
.
Following the approach in
[
AN19
,
GN20
]
, we consider the link function:

Assumption 2.1

(
[
GN20
, Condition 1]
)
.

Let
\Phi
satisfy

(i)

\Phi:(-\infty,\infty)\rightarrow(0,M_{0})
,
\Phi(0)=1
,
\Phi^{\prime}(z)>0
for all
z
;

(ii)

for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}

\sup_{-\infty<z<\infty}|\Phi^{(k)}(z)|<\infty.

Given any link function
\Phi
as in

, by following from the same argument in
[
NvdGW20
]
, the parameter space can be realized as

\mathscr{F}_{M_{0}}^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})=\{\Phi\circ F:F\in H_{0}^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})\}.

Accordingly, we can define the
reparametrized forward map
by

(2.7a)

\mathcal{G}(F):=G(\Phi\circ F)\quad\text{for all $F\in H_{0}^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})$,}

where

is the mapping given in (
2.3
). Therefore, the model (
2.6
) can be regarded as a special case of

(2.7b)

Y_{i}=\mathcal{G}(F)(X_{i})+\sigma W_{i}\quad\text{for $i=1,\cdots,N$.}

The random vectors

are then iid with laws denoted by

with Radon-Nikodym density

p_{F}(y,x):=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{F}^{i}}{\mathrm{d}y\times\mathrm{d}\mu}(y,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(y-\mathcal{G}(F)(x))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\quad\text{for all $y\in\mathbb{R},x\in\mathcal{D}$}

where

denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. By slightly abusing the notation, we write
\mathbb{P}_{F}^{N}=\otimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{F}^{i}
for the joint law of

, and

by a Borel probability measure

. Since the map

can be shown to be jointly measurable the posterior distribution

\Pi(B|Y^{(N)},X^{(N)})=\frac{\int_{B}e^{\ell^{N}(F)}\,\mathrm{d}\Pi(F)}{\int_{C(\Omega)}e^{\ell^{N}(F)}\,\mathrm{d}\Pi(F)}

for any Borel set

, where

\ell^{N}(F)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(Y_{i}-\mathcal{G}(F)(X_{i}))^{2}

is the joint log-likelihood function (up to a constant).

In this work, we are interested in the frequentist property of the posterior distribution (

are generated through the model (

) of law

with a ground truth

. The aim here is to show that the posterior distribution arising from Gaussian process priors and Gaussian sieve priors concentrates near

and to derive a bound on the rate of contraction as in
[
GN20
]
. We will follow the terminologies in

as well as in

. One may also consult the monograph

2.5.
Remarks on the Bernstein-von Mises (BvM) theorem

Consistency alone does not suffice for uncertainty quantification. A central goal would be to establish a Bernstein-von Mises (BvM) theorem characterizing the asymptotic shape of the posterior distribution. Although this theorem generally fails in infinite-dimensional settings (see
[
Nic20
, Remark 9]
), progress has been made for the Calderón problem with piecewise-constant conductivities, corresponding to a parametric setting
[
Boh23
]
. Further results in semiparametric settings can be found in
[
Nic20
,
Nic25
]
.

3.
Estimates for the fractional Schrödinger equation

3.1.
Estimates for the forward problem

The following lemma, which can be proved using a similar argument to the one in
[
PSU23
, Theorem 1.3.16]
, establishes that
(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{e})
for
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{e})
. Let us recall that the singular support of a distribution
u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is defined as a complement of the largest set where
u
is smooth. In particular,

\ssupp(u):={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,:\,\text{$u$ is smooth near $x$}\}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
0<s<1
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a nonempty bounded smooth domain. Let
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{e})
,
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
u=u_{f}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the unique solution of (
2.1
). Then

\ssupp((-\Delta)^{s}u)\cap\Omega_{e}=\emptyset

Proof.
Let
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfy
\chi(\xi)=1
for
\lvert\xi\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
\chi(\xi)=0
for
\lvert\xi\rvert\geq 1
. Then

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\lvert\xi\rvert^{2s}(\mathcal{F}u)(\xi)\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left((1-\chi(\xi))\lvert\xi\rvert^{2s}(\mathcal{F}u)(\xi)\right)+\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\chi(\xi)\lvert\xi\rvert^{2s}(\mathcal{F}u)(\xi)\right)

\displaystyle\quad=Eu+Su.

Since
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\chi
has compact support we know that
\chi(\xi)\lvert\xi\rvert^{2s}(\mathcal{F}u)(\xi)\in L^{1}
with compact support. Hence
Su
is smooth by the properties of the Fourier transform. Also
E
is a classical elliptic pseudodifferential operator of order
2s
since its symbol
(1-\chi(\xi))\lvert\xi\rvert^{2s}
is a classical elliptic symbol of order
2s
. Hence,

\ssupp(Eu)\cap\Omega_{e}\subset\ssupp(u)\cap\Omega_{e}=\ssupp(\phi)\cap\Omega_{e},

which then concludes our lemma.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

In fact, it holds that
\ssupp\Lambda_{f}\phi\subset\ssupp\phi\cap\mathcal{D}
whenever
\phi\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

With
Lemma

3.1
at hand, we are now able to prove the following forward estimate:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
0<s<1
,
\Omega
and
\mathcal{D}
be nonempty bounded smooth domains in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\overline{\mathcal{D}}\cap\overline{\Omega}=\emptyset
. Let
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{e})
and let
f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
be such that
f_{1},f_{2}\geq 0
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(d,s,\Omega,\lvert\mathcal{D}\rvert,\dist(\Omega,\mathcal{D}),\phi)>0
,
independent of both
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, such that

(3.1)

\lVert G(f_{1})-G(f_{2})\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq C\lVert f_{1}-f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where
G
is the forward map given in (
2.5
).

Proof.
Let
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\overline{\mathcal{D}})
,
u_{k}=u_{f_{k}}
be the unique solutions to

((-\Delta)^{s}+f_{k})u_{k}=0\text{ in }\Omega,\quad u_{k}=\phi\text{ in }\Omega_{e}

and
\tilde{u}_{2}
solve

((-\Delta)^{s}+f_{2})\tilde{u}_{2}=0\text{ in }\Omega,\quad\tilde{u}_{2}=\psi\text{ in }\Omega_{e}.

Choose
\psi=\varphi(-\Delta)^{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})
where
\varphi
is a cutoff such that
\varphi\equiv 1
in
\mathcal{D}
and
\varphi\geq 0
in
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
with
\dist(\tilde{\mathcal{D}},\overline{\Omega})>0
. Next we use the weak definition of the DN map and recall the Alessandrini identity (see e.g.
[
GSU20
]
)

(3.2)

((\Lambda_{f_{1}}-\Lambda_{f_{2}})\phi,\psi)=((f_{1}-f_{2})u_{1}|_{\Omega},\tilde{u}_{2}|_{\Omega})_{\Omega}.

Now by the above
Lemma

3.1
the DN map is smooth where
\phi
is smooth and thus

(\Lambda_{f_{k}}\phi,\psi)=((-\Delta)^{s}u_{k}|_{\Omega_{e}},\psi)_{\Omega_{e}}=\int_{\Omega_{e}}(-\Delta)^{s}u_{k}|_{\Omega_{e}}\psi\,\mathrm{d}x

(notice that apriori the DN map acts as a distribution, but the above can be made precise by an approximation argument).
Therefore

\displaystyle\lvert((\Lambda_{f_{1}}-\Lambda_{f_{2}})\phi,\psi)\rvert=\lvert(\Lambda_{f_{1}}\phi,\psi)-(\Lambda_{f_{2}}\phi,\psi)\rvert=\left|\int_{\Omega_{e}}\psi(-\Delta)^{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\quad=\left|\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left((-\Delta)^{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\supp(\varphi)\setminus\mathcal{D}}\varphi\left((-\Delta)^{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\quad\geq\left|\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left((-\Delta)^{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|=\lVert(-\Delta)^{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}.

Utilizing the Alessandrini identity we have, denoting
f=f_{1}-f_{2}
,

\displaystyle\lvert((\Lambda_{f_{1}}-\Lambda_{f_{2}})\phi,\psi)\rvert={(fu_{1}|_{\Omega},\tilde{u}_{2}|_{\Omega})_{\Omega}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert u_{1}\tilde{u}_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert u_{1}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\tilde{u}_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

where in the first inequality we used Cauchy-Schwarz and in the second Hölder’s inequality.
Let us then focus on
\lVert\tilde{u}_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
. Notice that
\tilde{u}_{2}-\psi
is supported in
\Omega
,
(\tilde{u}_{2}-\psi)|_{\Omega}=\tilde{u}_{2}|_{\Omega}
and thus

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{u}_{2}-\psi\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\geq\lVert\tilde{u}_{2}-\psi\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\lVert\tilde{u}_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Using
[
CRZ22
, Lemma 5.3]
, we have that

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{u}_{2}-\psi\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C(d,s,\lvert\supp(\varphi)\rvert,\Omega,\tau)\lVert\psi\rVert_{L^{2}(\supp(\psi))}

where
\tau:=\dist(\Omega,\supp(\psi))
.
Hence by using Hölder’s inequality

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{u}_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(d,s,\lvert\supp(\varphi)\rvert,\Omega,\tau)\lVert\psi\rVert_{L^{2}(\supp(\psi))}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(d,s,\lvert\supp(\varphi)\rvert,\Omega,\tau)\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\supp(\varphi))}\lVert(-\Delta)^{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})\rVert_{L^{2}(\supp(\varphi))}.

Putting the above together we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle\lVert(-\Delta)^{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(d,s,\lvert\supp(\varphi)\rvert,\Omega,\tau)\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert u_{1}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\supp(\varphi))}\lVert(-\Delta)^{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})\rVert_{L^{2}(\supp(\varphi))}

\displaystyle\leq C(d,s,\lvert\supp(\varphi)\rvert,\Omega,\tau,\phi)\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\supp(\varphi))}\lVert(-\Delta)^{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})\rVert_{L^{2}(\supp(\varphi))}

where we used (
2.2
).

The above works for any smooth cutoff
\varphi
. Let
\Phi(x)=\chi_{B(0,1)}e^{\frac{1}{\lvert x\rvert^{2}-1}}
and
\Phi_{1}(x)=\frac{\Phi(x)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}
. Let now
\varepsilon>0
and set
\Phi_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\Phi_{1}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)
,
\varphi_{\varepsilon}=\Phi_{\varepsilon}\ast\chi_{\mathcal{D}}
. Then
\lVert\varphi_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\supp(\varphi_{\varepsilon})}\leq 1
,
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
is smooth,

\supp(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}=\{x:\dist(x,\mathcal{D})\leq\varepsilon\}.

and, by making
\varepsilon
smaller if necessary,
\supp(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\cap\overline{\Omega}=\emptyset
.
Moreover
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varphi_{\varepsilon}=\chi_{\mathcal{D}}
. For all
\varepsilon
(small enough) the estimate (
3.3
) holds with
\varphi
replaced by
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
. Thus taking the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
we have

\lVert(-\Delta)^{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}\leq C(d,s,\lvert\mathcal{D}\rvert,\Omega,\dist(\Omega,\mathcal{D}),\phi)\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert(-\Delta)^{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}

which implies (recall that
f=f_{1}-f_{2}
)

(3.4)

\lVert(-\Delta)^{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq C(d,s,\lvert\mathcal{D}\rvert,\Omega,\dist(\Omega,\mathcal{D}),\phi)\lVert f_{1}-f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

This readily gives (
3.1
).
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
0<s<1
,
\Omega
and
\mathcal{D}
be nonempty bounded smooth domains in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\overline{\mathcal{D}}\cap\overline{\Omega}=\emptyset
. Let
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{e})
,
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
be such that
f\geq 0
and let
u_{f}
be the unique solution to (
2.1
). Then there exists a constant
C=C(d,s,\lvert\Omega\rvert,\dist(\Omega,\mathcal{D}),\phi)>0
, independent of
f
, such that

\lVert G(f)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}\leq C.

Proof.
Since
\phi=0
in
\Omega
, for each
x\in\mathcal{D}
, we see that

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f}(x)=C_{d,s}\int_{\Omega}\frac{u_{f}(x)-u_{f}(y)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y+C_{d,s}\int_{\Omega_{e}}\frac{u_{f}(x)-u_{f}(y)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=C_{d,s}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\phi(x)-u_{f}(y)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y+C_{d,s}\int_{\Omega_{e}}\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(y)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=C_{d,s}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\phi(x)-u_{f}(y)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y+C_{d,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(y)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y-C_{d,s}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(y)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=C_{d,s}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\phi(x)-u_{f}(y)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y+(-\Delta)^{s}\phi(x)-C_{d,s}\phi(x)\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y,

where

C_{d,s}:=\frac{4^{s}\Gamma(d/2+s)}{\pi^{d/2}\lvert\Gamma(-s)\rvert}.

Since for each
x\in\mathcal{D}
one can estimate

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\frac{\phi(x)-u_{f}(y)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq\frac{1}{\dist(\Omega,\mathcal{D})^{d+2s}}\int_{\Omega}\lvert\phi(x)-u_{f}(y)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lvert\Omega\rvert}{\dist(\Omega,\mathcal{D})^{d+2s}}(\phi(x)+\lVert u_{f}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\lvert\Omega\rvert}{\dist(\Omega,\mathcal{D})^{d+2s}}\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{e})}

where we used (
2.2
) in the last inequality. Similarly for the last term we have

\left|\phi(x)\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq\frac{\lvert\Omega\rvert}{\dist(\Omega,\mathcal{D})^{d+2s}}\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{e})}.

Therefore we combine all the equations above to obtain

\lVert(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}\leq C(d,s,\lvert\Omega\rvert,\dist(\Omega,\mathcal{D}))\left(\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{e})}+\lVert(-\Delta)^{s}\phi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}\right),

which concludes our lemma.
∎

to conclude (
3.10
) in the case where (
3.13
) does not hold.
∎

4.
Statistical conclusions

4.1.
Posterior contraction rates

We first verify some sufficient conditions which are needed in the Bayesian approach.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Omega
,
\mathcal{O}
,
\mathcal{D}
be nonempty bounded smooth domains in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\overline{\mathcal{O}}\subset\Omega
and
\overline{\Omega}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}=\emptyset
. Let
0<s<1
,
0\not\equiv\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{e})
and let
\Phi
be the link function given in
2.1
. Let
\beta>1+d/2
be an integer and
\mathcal{G}
be the reparametrized forward map given by (
2.7a
). Then one has

\lVert\mathcal{G}(F_{1})-\mathcal{G}(F_{2})\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\lesssim\lVert F_{1}-F_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\quad\text{for all $F_{1},F_{2}\in H_{0}^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})$}

and

\lVert\mathcal{G}\rVert_{H_{0}^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})\rightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}\equiv\sup_{F\in H_{0}^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})}\lVert\mathcal{G}(F)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}<\infty.

Proof.
This proposition is an immediate consequence of
Lemma

3.3
and
Lemma

3.4
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main results.

Proof of
Theorem

2.3
.

First of all, it follows from
Proposition

4.1
that the conditions in
[
GN20
, Theorem 14]
(which can also be found in
[
KW25
, Lemma 5.1]
) are satisfied with
\tau=1
and
\kappa=0
. Hence, there exists a sufficiently large
L>0
such that

\Pi_{N}\left(F:\lVert\mathcal{G}(F)-\mathcal{G}(F_{0})\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}>L\delta_{N}|Y^{(N)},X^{(N)}\right)=O_{\mathbb{P}_{F_{0}}^{N}}(e^{-DN\delta_{N}^{2}})\quad\text{as $N\rightarrow\infty$,}

where
\delta_{N}=N^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+d}}
and, furthermore, there exists a sufficiently large
M
such that

(4.1)

\Pi_{N}\left(F:\lVert F\rVert_{C^{1}(\mathcal{O})}>M|Y^{(N)},X^{(N)}\right)=O_{\mathbb{P}_{F_{0}}^{N}}(e^{-DN\delta_{N}^{2}})\quad\text{as $N\rightarrow\infty$.}

From
Proposition

3.7
, we now see that

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}_{N}\left(f:\lVert f-f_{0}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}>C\omega(L^{\prime}\delta_{N})|Y^{(N)},X^{(N)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\Pi}_{N}\left(f:\lVert G(f)-G(f_{0})\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}>L\delta_{N}|Y^{(N)},X^{(N)}\right)

\displaystyle+\tilde{\Pi}_{N}\left(f:\lVert f\rVert_{C^{s}(\mathcal{O})}>M^{\prime}|Y^{(N)},X^{(N)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pi_{N}\left(F:\lVert\mathcal{G}(F)-\mathcal{G}(F_{0})\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}>L\delta_{N}|Y^{(N)},X^{(N)}\right)

\displaystyle+\Pi_{N}\left(F:\lVert F\rVert_{C^{1}(\mathcal{O})}>M|Y^{(N)},X^{(N)}\right)

\displaystyle=O_{\mathbb{P}_{F_{0}}^{N}}(e^{-DN\delta_{N}^{2}})\quad\text{as $N\rightarrow\infty$.}

Finally, we compute that

\displaystyle C\omega(L^{\prime}\delta_{N})=C\lvert\log(\tilde{E}L^{\prime}N^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+d}})\rvert^{-\mu}

\displaystyle=C\left|\log(\tilde{E}L^{\prime})-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+d}\log N\right|^{-\mu}\sim C(\log N)^{-\mu}\quad\text{as $N\rightarrow\infty$}

and hence we conclude our theorem.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2.4
.

The idea is to use tools from the general machinery developed in
[
GN20
,
GN21
]
. In particular, the proof is based on
[
GN20
, Theorem 6]
(almost identical to
[
FKW24
, Theorem 2.6]
). By Jensen’s inequality it is enough to show that

\mathbb{P}_{F_{0}}^{N}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\overline{F}_{N}-F_{0}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}\right]>\tilde{C}(\log N)^{-\mu}\right)\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as $N\rightarrow\infty$.}

Then one can split the expectation to two parts: For large
M>0
we look at the case with
\lVert\overline{F}_{N}\rVert_{C^{1}(\mathcal{O})}\leq M
and one with
\lVert\overline{F}_{N}\rVert_{C^{1}(\mathcal{O})}>M
.
For
\lVert\overline{F}_{N}\rVert_{C^{1}(\mathcal{O})}>M
one uses Cauchy-Schwarz,
Theorem

2.3
,
[
GN20
, Lemma 16, Lemma 23]
and
[
GN21
, Lemma 7.3.2]
to get the desired asymptotic behavior. For
\lVert F\rVert_{C^{1}(\mathcal{O})}\leq M
one reduces to the first case by using the properties of the link function together with the mean value theorem and inverse function theorem. For further details, see the proofs of
[
GN20
, Theorem 6]
. ∎

4.2.
Minimax optimality of the contraction rates

For the proof of
Theorem

2.6
we will follow the method used in the proof
[
AN19
, Theorem 2]
(see also the proof of
[
FKW24
, Theorem 2.8]
).
We shall use the exponential instability of the Calderón problem for the fractional Schrödinger equation proved in
[
RS18
, Corollary 1.2]
. We will recall the result here for the convenience of the reader (adopted to our notation).

Theorem 4.2

(
[
RS18
, Corollary 1.2]
)
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, assume that
m\in\mathbb{N}
. There is a potential
f\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
, a sequence of errors
\{\varepsilon_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
and a sequence of potentials
\{f_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{m}(\overline{B}_{1})
such that

(i)

\lVert f-f_{k}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\geq\frac{\varepsilon_{k}}{2}
and
\lVert\Lambda_{f}-\Lambda_{f_{k}}\rVert_{L^{2}(B_{3}\setminus\overline{B}_{2})\to L^{2}(B_{3}\setminus\overline{B}_{2})}\leq C\exp\left(-\varepsilon_{k}^{-\frac{d}{(2d+3)m}}\right),

(ii)

for all
0\leq m^{\prime}<m
we have
f_{k}\to f
in
C^{m^{\prime}}(\overline{B}_{1})
.

We now prove
Theorem

2.6
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.6
.

Following
[
GN21
, Section 6.3.1 and Theorem 6.3.2]
we can reduce the proof of minimax lower bound to a testing problem with two hypotheses. By using the test
\Psi=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\lVert\tilde{f}-f_{1}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}<\lVert\tilde{f}-f_{0}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\}}
we have
H_{0}\colon f=f_{0}
against
H_{1}\colon f=f_{1}
. Now from the reduction made in the beginning of
[
GN21
, Section 6.3.1]
we have

\inf_{\tilde{f}}\sup_{f\in\mathscr{F}_{M_{0}}^{\beta}}\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f}\left(\lVert\tilde{f}-f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}\geq\frac{1}{2}(\log N)^{-\mu}\right)\geq\inf_{\psi}\max\{\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{0}}(\psi\neq 0),\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(\psi\neq 1)\}.

Recall that
p_{f}^{\otimes N}
is the Radon-Nikodym density of
\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f}=\otimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}^{i}_{f}
. Next let us define an event
B=\left\{\frac{p^{\otimes N}_{f_{0}}}{p^{\otimes N}_{f_{1}}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\right\}
for which we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{0}}(\psi\neq 0)

\displaystyle=\int_{\{\psi\neq 0\}}p^{\otimes N}_{f_{0}}\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\int\mathbbm{1}_{\{\psi=1\}}\mathbbm{1}_{B}p^{\otimes N}_{f_{0}}\mathrm{d}\mu=\int\mathbbm{1}_{\{\psi=1\}}\mathbbm{1}_{B}\frac{p^{\otimes N}_{f_{0}}}{p^{\otimes N}_{f_{1}}}p^{\otimes N}_{f_{1}}\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(\psi=1)>\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(\psi=1)-\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(B^{c})\right).

Then denoting
p_{1}=\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(\psi=1)
gives

\displaystyle\max\{\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{0}}(\psi\neq 0),\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(\psi\neq 1)\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}(p_{1}-\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(B^{c}),1-p_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{p\in[0,1]}\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}(p-\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(B^{c}),1-p\right\}.

This infimum is obtained when
1-p=\frac{1}{2}(p-\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(B^{c})
and thus
p=1-\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(B)
. Hence

\max\{\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{0}}(\psi\neq 0),\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(\psi\neq 1)\}\geq\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(B).

Estimating the term
\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(B)
further we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(B)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}\left(\frac{p^{\otimes N}_{f_{1}}}{p^{\otimes N}_{f_{0}}}\leq 2\right)=1-\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}\left(\log\frac{p^{\otimes N}_{f_{1}}}{p^{\otimes N}_{f_{0}}}>\log 2\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}\left(\left|\log\frac{p^{\otimes N}_{f_{1}}}{p^{\otimes N}_{f_{0}}}\right|>\log 2\right)\geq 1-\frac{1}{\log 2}\mathbb{E}^{N}_{f_{1}}\left[\left|\log\frac{p^{\otimes N}_{f_{1}}}{p^{\otimes N}_{f_{0}}}\right|\right],

where in the last estimate we used Markov’s inequality. Using the second Pinsker inequality (see e.g.
[
GN21
, Proposition 6.1.7 (b)]
) gives

\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f_{1}}(B)\geq 1-\frac{1}{\log 2}\left(\mathrm{KL}(p_{f_{0}}^{\otimes N},p_{f_{1}}^{\otimes N})+\sqrt{2\mathrm{KL}(p_{f_{0}}^{\otimes N},p_{f_{1}}^{\otimes N})}\right),

where
\mathrm{KL}(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)
is the Kullback-Leibler distance defined by

\mathrm{KL}(p_{f_{0}}^{\otimes N},p_{f_{1}}^{\otimes N})=\mathbb{E}^{N}_{f_{0}}\left(\log\frac{p_{f_{0}}^{\otimes N}}{p_{f_{1}}^{\otimes N}}\right).

Putting the above together we have proven so far that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{f}}\sup_{f\in\mathscr{F}_{M_{0}}^{\beta}}\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f}\left(\lVert\tilde{f}-f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}\geq\frac{1}{2}(\log N)^{-\mu}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{\log 2}\left(\mathrm{KL}(p_{f_{0}}^{\otimes N},p_{f_{1}}^{\otimes N})+\sqrt{2\mathrm{KL}(p_{f_{0}}^{\otimes N},p_{f_{1}}^{\otimes N})}\right).

Let us then focus on the Kullback-Leibler distance. We have

\mathrm{KL}(p_{f_{0}}^{\otimes N},p_{f_{1}}^{\otimes N})=N\mathbb{E}^{1}_{f_{0}}\left[\log\frac{p_{f_{0}}(Y_{1},X_{1})}{p_{f_{1}}(Y_{1},X_{1})}\right]=\frac{N}{2\sigma^{2}}\lVert G(f_{0})-G(f_{1})\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})},

since
X_{i},Y_{i}
are independent in the first equality and the second equality comes from
[
GN20
, Lemma 23]
. Recall that
G
is the DN map (see (
2.5
)) and thus we have

\lVert G(f_{0})-G(f_{1})\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq\lVert\Lambda_{f_{0}}-\Lambda_{f_{1}}\rVert^{1/2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}

(see e.g. the beginning of the proof of
Lemma

3.3
). Let
\mu>\log_{\log_{e}N}2\frac{2d+3}{d}
(
N
is going to be large in the end) and
\delta=(\log N)^{-\mu}
. By
Theorem

4.2
there are
f_{0},f_{1}
such that

\lVert f_{0}-f_{1}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{\delta}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert\Lambda_{f_{0}}-\Lambda_{f_{1}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq C\exp\left(-\delta^{\frac{d}{2d+3}}\right).

Hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(p_{f_{0}}^{\otimes N},p_{f_{1}}^{\otimes N})\leq\frac{N}{2\sigma^{2}}C\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{\frac{d}{2d+3}}\right)=\frac{C}{2\sigma^{2}}\exp\left(\log N-\frac{1}{2}(\log N)^{\frac{d}{2d+3}}\right)

and for the choice of
\mu
the exponent of
\exp
is negative. Thus choosing
N
large we get that

\mathrm{KL}(p_{f_{0}}^{\otimes N},p_{f_{1}}^{\otimes N})\leq\tilde{\varepsilon}

for some
\tilde{\varepsilon}>0
. Using this in (
4.2
) gives

\inf_{\tilde{f}}\sup_{f\in\mathscr{F}_{M_{0}}^{\beta}}\mathbb{P}^{N}_{f}\left(\lVert\tilde{f}-f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}\geq\frac{1}{2}(\log N)^{-\mu}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon

for
\varepsilon>0
. Choosing
N
large enough ends the proof.
∎

5.
Numerical demonstrations of the Bayesian procedure

The main objective of this section is to numerically illustrate the convergence of the Bayesian procedure using a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm, based on the log-likelihood, rather than the faster and more accurate steepest-descent methods, based on a suitable Tikhonov functional, which are commonly employed in practice
[
Li25
,
Li24
]
. Owing to computational complexity, we restrict our consideration to the one-dimensional case
d=1
. Let
\ell
be a positive real number,
K
be a positive integer and set the mesh size
h=2\ell/K
. Based on
[
DvWZ18
, (2.10)]
(with an appropriately chosen parameter), the numerical approximation of
(-\Delta)^{s}v(x)
for
x\in(-\ell,\ell)
, under the assumption that
v(x)=0
outside
(-\ell,\ell)
, is given by
(-\Delta)_{h}^{s}v(x_{i})=A\mathbf{v}
, where
x_{i}=-\ell+ih
are grid points for
0\leq i\leq K
,
\mathbf{v}=(v(x_{1}),v(x_{2}),\cdots,v(x_{K-1}))^{\intercal}
is a column vector and the matrix
A=(A_{ij})
is a symmetric Toeplitz matrix given by

A_{ij}=\frac{c_{1,2s}}{(1-s)h^{2s}}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\begin{aligned} &\sum_{k=2}^{K}\frac{(k+1)^{1-s}-(k-1)^{1-s}}{k^{1+s}}\\
&\quad+\frac{K^{1-s}-(K-1)^{1-s}}{K^{1+s}}+2^{1-s}+\frac{1-s}{sK^{2s}}\end{aligned}&&\text{when $j=i$}\\
&-\frac{(\lvert j-i\rvert+1)^{1-s}-(\lvert j-i\rvert-1)^{1-s}}{2\lvert j-i\rvert^{1+s}}&&\text{when $j\neq i$ and $j\neq i\pm 1$,}\\
&-2^{-s}&&\text{when $j=i\pm 1$,}\end{aligned}\right.

for all
i,j=1,2,\cdots,K-1
, where
c_{1,2s}=\frac{4^{s}s\Gamma(\frac{1+2s}{2})}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma(1-s)}>0
. We also refer to
[
DZ19
]
for discretizations of
(-\Delta)^{s}
in the two- and three-dimensional cases.

For any given
f
, we aim to compute the solution

of (

) numerically. To this end, we define
