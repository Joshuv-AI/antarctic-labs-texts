The interplay of adolescent obesity and smoking on hypertension in early adulthood: insights from TLGS
Authors: Neda Izadi, Proushat Shirvani, Aram Halimi, Fereidoun Azizi, Parisa Amiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension (HTN) increasingly originates in childhood and adolescence, with obesity and smoking as modifiable risk factors. However, longitudinal evidence on their independent and joint effects on HTN risk into early adulthood remains limited in non-Western populations.MethodsUsing data from the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS), we followed 985 adolescents (mean age 15.01 years) for a median of 15.05 years. Adolescent overweight/obesity was defined using national BMI percentiles, and smoking status was self-reported. Incident adulthood HTN was defined as blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg or use of antihypertensive medication. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusting for potential confounders.ResultsOver follow-up, 95 participants developed HTN. In adjusted analyses, adolescent overweight/obesity was independently associated with higher adulthood HTN risk (HR: 1.73; 95% CI: 1.11–2.71). Adolescent smoking was not independently associated with adulthood HTN after adjustment (HR: 1.15; 95% CI: 0.63–2.09). In joint exposure analyses, overweight/obese non-smokers had a higher risk than normal-weight non-smokers (HR: 1.77; 95% CI: 1.10–2.84), whereas smokers did not show statistically significant excess risk regardless of weight status. The obesity–smoking interaction suggested a possible synergistic effect on HTN risk in adulthood.ConclusionsAdolescent overweight/obesity is an independent predictor of adulthood HTN. Although smoking was not independently associated after adjustment, the potential interaction between obesity and smoking merits further study. Prevention efforts should prioritize adolescent weight management to reduce long-term cardiovascular risk.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Adolescent obesity, Smoking, Hypertension, Early adulthood, Cardiovascular risk factors

Introduction

Hypertension (HTN), often called the “Silent Killer” is a major, modifiable risk factor for a range of serious cardiovascular and renal outcomes, including myocardial infarction, stroke, and renal failure [1]. Although traditionally considered an adult condition, substantial evidence now shows that the origins of essential HTN are rooted in childhood and adolescence [2]. Approximately 3.5% of children and adolescents have HTN, with a further 3.5% classified as prehypertensive—figures that increase significantly as youth transition into early adulthood [2]. This epidemiological shift is alarming, as elevated blood pressure (BP) in youth shows significant tracking into later life [3, 4]. Recent updates of the European Society of Hypertension (ESH) guidelines in 2023 further emphasize that hypertension in childhood and adolescence is not a transient phenomenon, but an early manifestation of lifelong cardiovascular risk, requiring early detection and modification of risk factors [5].

Among Iranian children and adolescents, overweight and obesity are already common, with a pooled prevalence of approximately 11.7% for overweight and 8.1% for obesity in those under 18 years [6]. Cigarette smoking is also notable in this age group: a systematic review of 99 studies reported that among Iranian high-school students, the pooled prevalence of current cigarette smoking was about 9% in boys and 3% in girls, with regular (almost daily) smoking around 5% in boys and 1% in girls [7]. In addition, the prevalence of HTN among children and adolescents (6–20 years) is about 9%, with a similar prevalence for prehypertension, highlighting that elevated blood pressure is already a significant problem in this young population [8]. Recent global evidence has highlighted the growing burden and accumulation of cardiovascular risk factors among adolescents and young adults, particularly in low- and middle-income countries; Where obesity, smoking, and hypertension often co-occur and contribute to future cardiovascular complications [9].

Adolescent overweight and obesity are central to the development of HTN. The association between elevated body mass index (BMI) in childhood and higher BP is among the most consistent findings in pediatric epidemiology [2, 3]. Gu et al. (2023), in their cross-sectional study of 6,339 U.S. children and adolescents, found that both overweight/obesity and tobacco exposure (serum cotinine > 0.05 µg/L or household smoker) independently increased the odds of HTN. Critically, they identified a significant joint effect: children with both obesity and tobacco exposure had the highest risk, and those with both overweight and tobacco exposure also faced substantially increased odds [3].

In addition, Hou et al. (2019), in a longitudinal cohort study of 2,095 Chinese participants, investigated how changes in weight status from childhood (6–17 years) to early adulthood (18–37 years) affect the risk of adult HTN. They found that becoming overweight or obese by early adulthood significantly increased the risk of HTN, regardless of adolescent overweight and obesity are central to the development of pathway to HTN. The association between elevated body mass index (BMI) in adolescent and higher BP is among the most consistent findings in pediatric epidemiology weight status; individuals with persistent overweight or obesity or who developed it in adulthood had similarly high risks. Conversely, individuals who were overweight or obese in adolescent but achieved a normal weight by early adulthood had no increased risk of HTN, demonstrating that the long-term cardiovascular risk of adolescent obesity is reversible with weight normalization [4].

Global data reveal concerning patterns, with smoking prevalence escalating sharply through the teenage years. It has been shown that smoking prevalence rises from 0.3% at age 10 to over 26% by age 24, with a majority of childhood initiators continuing the habit into their mid-twenties [10]. Critically, exposure during developmentally sensitive periods, such as gestation and lactation, may induce metabolic programming, potentially increasing lifelong susceptibility to obesity, HTN, and full metabolic syndrome, thereby creating a shared developmental origin for both risk factors [11]. Even passive exposure in adolescent has been meta-analytically linked to elevated BP, confirming that no level of tobacco smoke exposure is safe for cardiovascular health in young people [1]. Furthermore, studies indicate that adolescent risk factors, including BMI and smoking, independently predict increased systemic vascular resistance—a key determinant of HTN—decades later in adulthood [12]. Recent evidence from global pooled analyzes published in Lancet Global Health shows that cardiovascular risk factors established during adolescence are strongly tracked into adulthood and contribute significantly to premature cardiovascular events worldwide [13].

However, most existing evidence is either cross-sectional or examines these critical risk factors in isolation. Longitudinal data examining their combined trajectory from adolescent into early adulthood are scarce. Determining whether their co-occurrence exerts a synergistic effect—a risk greater than the sum of its parts—is crucial for refined risk stratification and targeted prevention. Despite growing evidence from systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and international blood pressure guidelines, longitudinal data from Middle Eastern populations examining the combined effects of obesity and adolescent smoking on future blood pressure remain limited [2, 5, 13]. Therefore, this study aims to examine the independent and joint effects of adolescent obesity and smoking behavior (assessed from adolescent into early adulthood) on the risk of HTN in early adulthood.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study used data from the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS), an ongoing population-based prospective cohort that began in 1999 in Tehran, Iran, with follow-up examinations every three years. The study design and sampling procedures have been described in detail in previous publications [14]. Phase II (2002–2004) was considered the baseline for the current analysis because variables related to smoking habits and physical activity were collected from this phase onwards, and participants included were aged 12–18 years at baseline. Adolescents were followed until phase VII or until the occurrence of HTN in adulthood, whichever came first. From an initial pool of 1,567 adolescents, individuals with missing baseline data for systolic or diastolic blood pressure (n = 32) were excluded. A further 193 participants who presented with prevalent HTN at baseline, defined as Stage 1 Hypertension or higher (SBP ≥ 130 mmHg or DBP ≥ 80 mmHg or physician diagnosis), were also excluded, as well as 287 individuals with fewer than three phases of follow-up data. In addition, based on smoking data from Phase III, participants who reported inconsistent smoking status between phases were excluded: those who were smokers in Phase II but non-smokers in Phase III (n = 55), and those who were non-smokers in Phase II but smokers in Phase III (n = 15). Given the variability of smoking behavior over time, these exclusions ensured that the remaining adolescents had a stable smoking status for at least 3–5 years. After these exclusions, the final analytical sample consisted of 985 adolescents followed into early adulthood.

Baseline measurements

In the TLGS database, data were collected through interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. Trained interviewers recorded socio-demographic information, including age, sex, education, occupation, lifestyle factors such as smoking and physical activity, and medical history using validated questionnaires. All anthropometric measurements were conducted by trained personnel following a standardized protocol to ensure accuracy and consistency. Participants stood upright without shoes and wore light clothing. Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a stadiometer. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a digital scale calibrated daily. Waist circumference (WC) was measured at the narrowest point between the lowest rib and the iliac crest, and hip circumference (HC) was measured at the maximum circumference over the buttocks; both were taken with a non-stretch tape to the nearest 0.1 cm at the end of normal expiration. Physical activity was assessed using the Iranian version of the Modifiable Activity Questionnaire (MAQ) [15]. The reliability and validity of this instrument were previously established in an Iranian adolescent population by Delshad et al. (2015) [16]. The study showed excellent test-retest reliability, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.97. Regarding validity, the Pearson correlation coefficient between the MAQ and physical activity records was 0.49 (p < 0.001), indicating moderate validity for assessing leisure-time physical activity in this contex. Participants reported the frequency and duration of the activities they had engaged in during the previous 12 months. Metabolic equivalent (MET) values were assigned to these activities and total MET minutes per week were calculated [17]. Based on the MET calculations, participants were categorized into two groups: low (≤ 600 MET-minutes per week) and moderate/high (> 600 MET-minutes per week). Blood pressure (BP) was measured twice for each participant after at least 5 min of seated rest, using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer with an appropriate cuff size placed on the right arm. The two measurements were taken and the mean of the two readings was recorded as the participant’s blood pressure. Fasting blood samples were collected after at least 12 h of fasting. Laboratory tests measured total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and fasting blood glucose (FBG). Family history of cardiovascular disease (CVD) was defined as a self-reported positive history of myocardial infarction and/or ischemic heart disease and/or stroke and/or ECG positive and/or medication use to treat CVD in first-degree relatives. Family history of diabetes was defined as a self-reported diagnosis of diabetes mellitus in at least one first-degree relative (parent, sibling, or offspring).

Main exposures

BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²). adolescent obesity was defined using age- and sex-specific BMI percentiles based on Iranian national growth charts [18]. For analysis, participants were categorized into a binary variable: non-obese (underweight/normal) and obese (overweight/obese). To classify participants as smokers or non-smokers, they were asked “Do you currently smoke cigarettes regularly?” with the answer options “smoke regularly”, “smoke irregularly” or “never smoked”. Those who answered " smoke regularly” or " smoke irregularly” were categorized as smokers, while the others were classified as non-smokers.

Outcome

Hypertension in adolescent (ages 12–18) was defined according to AAP 2017 guidelines as Stage 1 Hypertension or higher (SBP ≥ 130 mmHg or DBP ≥ 80 mmHg or use of antihypertensive medication) [19]. In adulthood (ages ≥ 18), HTN was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg or the use of antihypertensive medication [20]. Incident HTN was identified from Phases II to VII of the TLGS. A composite variable was created to indicate the occurrence of HTN during the follow-up period. Survival time for the time-to-event analysis was calculated from the baseline examination to the midpoint between the last non-hypertensive visit and the first hypertensive visit for incident cases. For censored participants, survival time was calculated up to the last available follow-up visit. The time-to-event was then converted into years for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata software (version 17). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), while categorical variables are reported as frequency (percentage). Group comparisons were made using chi-square tests for categorical variables and t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. The relationships between adolescent obesity, smoking status and incident HTN were assessed using Cox proportional hazards models, with adjustments for potential confounders. The proportional hazards assumption was verified for all models using Schoenfeld residuals. The interaction between obesity and smoking status was also evaluated; for these interaction terms, a P-value < 0.10 was considered statistically significant. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval declaration

This study was conducted according to the guidelines laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki and all procedures involving research study participants were approved by the Ethics Committee of Research Institute for Endocrine Sciences, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (IR.SBMU.ENDOCRINE.REC.1403.058). Written informed consent was obtained from a parent and/or legal guardian.

Results

Of the 985 study participants, with a mean age of 15.01 years (SD: 1.93) at baseline, 451 (45.79%) were male and 534 (54.21%) were female. Among them, 95 (9.64%) were diagnosed with HTN in adulthood. The median follow-up period was 15.05 years (IQR: 5.01). Several adolescent characteristics were significantly associated with the development of HTN later in life. Individuals who developed HTN were more likely to be male (16.63% vs. 3.75% in females, p < 0.001). A higher prevalence of HTN was also observed among current smokers (15.29% vs. 9.28% in non-smokers) and those with moderate/high physical activity levels compared to their less active peers (13.03% vs. 7.34 p = 0.003).

Adolescent anthropometric measures were associated with future HTN. Those who developed the condition had significantly higher baseline weight, BMI, WC, and HC (all p ≤ 0.001). Participants who were overweight or obese in adolescent had a significantly higher prevalence of HTN compared to those who were underweight or of normal weight (15.56% vs. 8.87%, p = 0.008). As expected, baseline systolic and diastolic blood pressures were also significantly higher in the group that developed HTN (p < 0.001 for both).

Regarding metabolic profiles, fasting blood glucose and total and LDL cholesterol levels showed no significant differences. However, the hypertensive group had a less favorable lipid profile in adolescent. This was characterized by significantly higher median TG levels (102 mg/dl vs. 90 mg/dl, p = 0.002) and significantly lower HDL-C levels (37.44 mg/dl vs. 39.75 mg/dl, p = 0.027). A positive family history of diabetes was associated with a higher risk of HTN (15.46% vs. 8.94% in those without, p = 0.039), whereas a family history of CVD was not (p = 0.535) (Table 1).

Adolescent characteristics by adolescent obesity and smoking

Participants who were underweight or normal-weight in adolescent (n = 755) had a significantly higher prevalence of low physical activity compared to their overweight or obese peers (60.13%vs. 50.56%, p = 0.019). As expected, all anthropometric indices were markedly higher in the overweight or obese group (all p < 0.001). This group also showed a less favorable cardiometabolic profile, with significantly higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure (p < 0.001 for both), TC (p < 0.001), and TG (p < 0.001), alongside significantly lower HDL-C levels (p = 0.001). No significant differences were observed in the distribution of sex, fasting blood glucose, or family history of diabetes and CVD between the two groups.

Current smokers (n = 85) were significantly older than non-smokers (mean age 15.44 vs. 15.00 years, p = 0.042) and were more likely to be male (48.24% vs. 31.76%, p < 0.001). Smokers showed significantly higher weight, BMI, and WC (p < 0.05). However, unlike the findings for obesity, smoking status was not associated with significant differences in blood pressure, blood glucose, lipid profiles, or family history of cardiovascular diseases (Table 2).

Adulthood HTN by adolescent obesity and smoking

Among individuals who were underweight or of normal weight in adolescent, those who smoked had a significantly higher prevalence of adulthood HTN compared to non-smokers (14.29% vs. 8.49%, p = 0.043). In contrast, among those who were overweight or obese in adolescent, smoking status was not significantly associated with HTN rates, with similar prevalence between smokers and non-smokers (23.08% vs. 15.15%, p = 0.450) (Fig. 1).

Frequency of adulthood hypertension by adolescent obesity and smoking

During a total of 13,936 person-years of follow-up, 95 incident cases of HTN were identified, resulting in an overall incidence rate of 68.17 per 10,000 person-years. Participants who were overweight or obese in adolescent had a substantially higher incidence rate (110.73 per 10,000 person-years) compared to those who were underweight or of normal weight (62.50 per 10,000 person-years). Similarly, current smokers in adolescent had a higher incidence rate (111.66 per 10,000 person-years) than non-smokers (65.43 per 10,000 person-years). When these factors were combined, a clear gradient was observed. The lowest incidence rate was found in individuals who were underweight or of normal weight and non-smokers in adolescent (59.72 per 10,000 person-years). This rate increased in underweight or normal weight individuals who were smokers (103.74 per 10,000 person-years), and further in overweight or obese non-smokers (107.36 per 10,000 person-years). The highest incidence rate was observed in those with both risk factors— adolescent overweight/obesity and smoking—at 174.12 per 10,000 person-years (Table 3).

Relationship of adolescent obesity and smoking with adulthood HTN

In the crude Cox model, adolescent overweight/obesity was significantly associated with a higher risk of adulthood HTN (HR: 1.77, 95% CI: 1.14–2.74). Although current smoking in adolescent was associated with an elevated hazard of adulthood HTN, this association was not statistically significant (HR: 1.72, 95% CI: 0.96–3.08). When the joint categories were examined, compared with underweight/normal-weight non-smokers, both overweight/obese non-smokers (HR: 1.79, 95% CI: 1.12–2.87) and overweight/obese current smokers (HR: 2.91, 95% CI: 1.01–9.30) had a significantly higher risk of adulthood HTN. In contrast, underweight/normal-weight current smokers did not show a statistically significant increased risk (HR: 1.76, 95% CI: 0.89–3.44). The highest crude risk was observed among overweight/obese current smokers, and the interaction between obesity and smoking status was statistically significant (Pinteraction=0.016).

In the fully adjusted model, adolescent overweight/obesity remained a significant predictor of adulthood HTN (HR: 1.73, 95% CI: 1.11–2.71), whereas adolescent current smoking was not significantly associated with HTN risk after adjustment (HR: 1.15, 95% CI: 0.63–2.09). In the joint exposure analysis, overweight/obese non-smokers continued to have a significantly higher hazard of adulthood HTN compared with underweight/normal-weight non-smokers (HR: 1.77, 95% CI: 1.10–2.84). However, neither underweight/normal-weight current smokers (HR: 1.21, 95% CI: 0.61–2.38) nor overweight/obese current smokers (HR: 1.69, 95% CI: 0.52–5.45) showed statistically significant associations in the adjusted model (Fig. 2). The interaction term between obesity and smoking status remained significant in the adjusted model based on the pre-defined threshold (Pinteraction=0.097).

Discussion

The present longitudinal study investigated the independent and joint effects of adolescent obesity and smoking behavior on the development of HTN in early adulthood. Our findings are consistent with recent international evidence and guideline updates showing that CVD risk pathways are formed early in life and are strongly influenced by modifiable factors in adolescence such as obesity and smoking [5, 9]. The findings showed that both obesity and smoking during adolescence were significant predictors of adult HTN, and that their co-occurrence applied a synergistic effect on risk magnitude. These results highlight the importance of early prevention strategies targeting modifiable lifestyle factors during adolescence.

Our findings are consistent with a growing body of evidence linking adolescent obesity to elevated blood pressure trajectories and adult HTN. Falkner (2018) [2], Virdis et al. (2009) [21], and Beilin and Huang (2008) [22] have emphasized that excess adiposity in youth predisposes individuals to vascular remodelling, endothelial dysfunction, and activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, ultimately elevating lifelong HTN risk. Similarly, Field et al. (2005) showed that boys with a childhood BMI above the 75th percentile were five times more likely to develop HTN in early adulthood [23]. In line with these results, Hou et al. (2019) reported that individuals with persistent overweight or obesity from childhood to adulthood, or who became obese in adulthood, had nearly a fourfold increased risk of HTN [4]. Recent systematic reviews and pooled cohort analyzes have further confirmed that high BMI in adolescence is associated with persistent vascular dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and increased lifetime risk of hypertension, even after adjustment for lifestyle factors in adulthood [4, 23, 24]. Importantly, normalization of weight by adulthood was associated with a risk comparable to that of individuals who had never been overweight, suggesting that the adverse cardiovascular consequences of adolescent obesity are reversible with weight reduction. Findings from the Young Finns Study by Kähönen et al. (2021) also revealed that adolescent BMI, blood glucose, and smoking independently predict higher systemic vascular resistance in adulthood, emphasizing that early-life metabolic and behavioral factors directly influence vascular hemodynamics [12]. The present study confirms these observations, extending them to an Iranian cohort and confirming that the obesity–HTN pathway transcends ethnic and geographical boundaries. Our findings also extend previous observations from the TLGS, which showed that obesity and metabolic abnormalities in adolescence contribute to adverse cardiovascular outcomes later in life [25, 26].

The current findings also align with evidence from Turer et al. (2018), who emphasized that obesity, HTN, and dyslipidemia in childhood are mutually reinforcing precursors of adult cardiovascular disease [27]. Similarly, Faienza et al. (2016) described childhood obesity as a “metabolic amplifier” that predicts not only adult HTN but also other metabolic imbalances, including insulin resistance and dyslipidemia [28]. Together, these studies reinforce the notion that early adiposity acts as a foundational risk factor for cardiovascular disease through both hemodynamic and metabolic mechanisms.

The observed relationship between adolescent smoking exposure and later HTN risk also supports previous findings from large cohort studies. Gall et al. (2014) demonstrated that exposure to parental smoking during childhood was independently associated with increased carotid intima-media thickness in adulthood, even after controlling for participants’ own smoking behaviors [29]. Similarly, Su et al. (2015) found that adverse child adolescent hood experiences, including household dysfunction and early tobacco exposure, were associated with accelerated increases in blood pressure in young adulthood [30]. Recent evidence from Corsi and Agbaje (2025) expands on these findings, showing that both active and passive smoking in childhood lead to structural cardiac alterations, endothelial dysfunction, and ventricular remodelling through oxidative and inflammatory mechanisms [10]. Their review highlights that early-life tobacco exposure has measurable long-term consequences for cardiac morphology and function, complementing our observations on HTN risk. These results suggest that both direct and indirect tobacco exposure during critical developmental period may have long-term vascular consequences, likely mediated by oxidative stress, endothelial injury, and autonomic imbalance. In our study, adolescents who smoked or were exposed to household smoking had a significantly higher risk of adult HTN, supporting the hypothesis that tobacco-related vascular damage may begin early and persist for decades.

Interestingly, the joint effect of obesity and smoking observed in the present analysis highlights an important synergistic interaction. Obese adolescents who smoked were at significantly greater risk of developing HTN than those with either risk factor alone. Biologically, both obesity and smoking may converge on pathways involving systemic inflammation, increased sympathetic activity, and reduced nitric oxide bioavailability. The presence of both exposures may therefore potentiate vascular stiffness and endothelial dysfunction, amplifying hypertensive risk beyond that attributable to each factor independently. This finding is consistent with previous epidemiological evidence suggesting that lifestyle risk factors often cluster and interact biologically, increasing cardiovascular risk when combined.

However, some Western cohort studies, such as those by Field et al. (2005), emphasized BMI’s predictive power for HTN primarily in males, whereas our findings demonstrated similar effects across sexes [23]. These inconsistencies may reflect cultural or environmental factors influencing physical activity, diet, and smoking behaviors across populations.

Furthermore, our analysis examined the role of leisure-time physical activity in relation to adult HTN. Interestingly, initial univariate results suggested a paradoxical association, where adolescents with higher physical activity levels showed a higher prevalence of hypertension. However, stratified analyses showed that this association was primarily driven by sex-based confounding; males in our cohort exhibited both higher levels of physical activity and a higher prevalence of HTN than females. After stratifying by sex, the association between physical activity and HTN was no longer significant, indicating that sex-specific behavioral patterns are a major contributor to the observed cardiovascular risk profiles. These findings highlight the necessity of adjusting for sex when evaluating the relationship between lifestyle habits and cardiometabolic outcomes in adolescents.

Beyond individual predictors, our study highlights several public health implications. These findings are particularly important in the context of recent global cardiovascular health reports that indicate a rapid increase in cardiometabolic cluster risk factors among adolescents in low- and middle-income countries [9]. First, interventions targeting weight management and smoking prevention in adolescence could substantially reduce the burden of adult HTN. Early screening and lifestyle modification programs, especially in school and community settings, may interrupt the trajectory from adolescent risk factors to adult disease. Second, our results support the inclusion of obesity and smoking behaviors in youth cardiovascular risk stratification models, which currently underrepresent lifestyle exposures during adolescence. The findings also provide regionally specific evidence from a Middle Eastern population, addressing the gap in data from non-Western contexts.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, self-reported smoking behavior may have resulted in misclassification and potential underestimation of true exposure levels, particularly among adolescents. Second, although we adjusted for several relevant covariates, residual confounding by unmeasured lifestyle or genetic factors cannot be completely ruled out. Third, the number of incident hypertension events in some combined exposure subgroups was small; specifically, only three events occurred in the overweight/obese current smoker group, which may have reduced statistical power and limited the precision and stability of the corresponding hazard ratio estimates. Therefore, the results related to this subgroup should be interpreted with caution.

Furthermore, the longitudinal variability of smoking and obesity status over time represents an additional limitation. Regarding smoking behavior, we attempted to mitigate this by including only participants with a stable smoking status for at least 3–5 years; nevertheless, residual changes in habits during adulthood may still influence the observed associations between early-life exposures and incident hypertension. For obesity, a similar approach was not feasible, as clinical definitions and thresholds differ significantly between adolescence and adulthood, precluding direct longitudinal harmonization. Despite these limitations, the strengths of our study include its longitudinal design, extensive follow-up (median = 15 years), and rigorous measurement of anthropometric and blood pressure variables within a well-characterized community-based cohort. Moreover, the inclusion of both sexes and repeated follow-up assessments enabled robust estimation of hypertension incidence and temporal associations.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides longitudinal evidence that adolescent obesity is a significant independent predictor of hypertension in early adulthood. Furthermore, our findings suggest that obesity and smoking during adolescence may interact synergistically to exacerbate the risk of developing HTN. While smoking did not maintain a statistically significant association independently after adjustment, the observed interaction underscores the potential danger of clustering lifestyle risk factors in youth. These findings reinforce global evidence that modifiable behaviors during adolescence have enduring consequences for cardiovascular health and emphasize the necessity for unified, early public health initiatives targeting both weight management and tobacco prevention simultaneously. Future research is required to elucidate the specific biological pathways underlying these synergistic effects and to evaluate the long-term effectiveness of early, integrated intervention programs in preventing adulthood hypertension.
