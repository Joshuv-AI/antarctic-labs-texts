Refusal in LLMs is an Affine Function
Thomas Marshall, Adam Scherlis, Nora Belrose

Abstract
We propose affine concept editing (ACE) as an approach for steering language models’ behavior by intervening directly in activations. We begin with an affine decomposition of model activation vectors and show that prior methods for steering model behavior correspond to subsets of terms of this decomposition. We then provide a derivation of ACE and use it to control refusal behavior on ten different models, including Llama 3 70B. ACE combines affine subspace projection and activation addition to reliably control the model’s refusal responses across prompt types. We evaluate the results using LLM-based scoring on a collection of harmful and harmless prompts. Our experiments demonstrate that ACE consistently achieves more precise control over model behavior than existing methods and generalizes to models where directional ablation via affine subspace projection alone produces incoherent outputs. Code for reproducing our results is available at https://github.com/EleutherAI/steering-llama3 .



1
Introduction

According to the linear representation hypothesis
(
Elhage et al. 2022
;
Park et al. 2023
)
, models represent high-level concepts as linear directions in activation space. This idea has led to the development of several techniques designed to change the behavior of large language models (LLMs) by performing linear modifications of residual stream activations. Directional ablation and activation addition are two such techniques that have both been shown to be effective. In this paper, we examine a unification and generalization of directional ablation and activation addition, which we call affine concept editing. We also test this on a recurrent neural network from the RWKV v5 family introduced by
Peng et al. 2024
.

In this paper, we point out an ambiguity that has existed in most work on the linear representation hypothesis thus far. Specifically, the notion of “linear representation” or “direction” may either refer to a
linear
function – that is, a function that does not have a constant term – or an
affine
function, with a constant (bias) term that may or may not be zero. We show that this distinction matters in practice: steering methods that assume concepts are encoded linearly can sometimes cause a model to devolve into outputting nonsense, while a generalized affine method steers the model successfully.

1.1
Linear and affine decomposition

Linear decomposition.

Given an activation vector
{\bm{v}}
and a nonzero vector
{\bm{r}}
, we can decompose
{\bm{v}}

linearly
into its orthogonal projections parallel to
{\bm{r}}
and perpendicular to
{\bm{r}}
:

{\bm{v}}=\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\perp}({\bm{v}})+\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}})

Because
\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}})
is parallel to
{\bm{r}}
, we can write it as a multiple of
{\bm{r}}
:

{\bm{v}}=\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\perp}({\bm{v}})+\alpha{\bm{r}}

If we interpret
{\bm{r}}
as a direction corresponding to some behavior or concept, then we can interpret
\alpha
as the degree to which that behavior is represented in the activation vector
{\bm{v}}
. Note that
\alpha
is a linear function of
{\bm{v}}
:

\alpha=\frac{{\bm{r}}}{\|{\bm{r}}\|^{2}}\cdot{\bm{v}}

This framework suggests some possible steering interventions. For example, we may want to shift
\alpha
by a fixed amount
\Delta\alpha
, to increase or decrease the behavior,

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\perp}({\bm{v}})+(\alpha+\Delta\alpha){\bm{r}}={\bm{v}}+\Delta\alpha{\bm{r}}

Or set
\alpha\mapsto 0
to “remove” it:

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}^{\prime}=\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\perp}({\bm{v}})={\bm{v}}-\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}}).

Or, by combining these, set
\alpha
to a new value
\alpha^{\prime}
, regardless of its original value:

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle={\bm{v}}-\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}})+\alpha^{\prime}{\bm{r}}

As we will see, these correspond to existing steering methods in the literature.

Affine decomposition.

We argue that this framework is not sufficiently general. In particular, it assumes without justification that the origin (zero vector) is a meaningful “default value” for activations, corresponding to a given behavior/concept being “absent”. In practice, this assumption fails for multiple reasons; the typical level of most behaviors in a network is not “none”, and typical activations are often far from the origin. We extend the framework by allowing our decomposition to depend on both a vector
{\bm{r}}
and a point of reference
{\bm{v}}_{0}
, corresponding to some standardized degree of behavior.

We now decompose
{\bm{v}}
as

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle={\bm{v}}_{0}+\Delta{\bm{v}}={\bm{v}}_{0}+\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\perp}(\Delta{\bm{v}})+\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}(\Delta{\bm{v}})

where
\Delta{\bm{v}}
is the displacement vector
({\bm{v}}-{\bm{v}}_{0})
from
{\bm{v}}_{0}
to
{\bm{v}}
. We again write this in terms of
\alpha
as

{\bm{v}}={\bm{v}}_{0}+\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\perp}(\Delta{\bm{v}})+\alpha{\bm{r}}

Note that
{\bm{v}}_{0}
determines the zero-point for
\alpha
: it is zero when
{\bm{v}}={\bm{v}}_{0}
, and not (in general) when
{\bm{v}}=\bm{0}
. In other words,
\alpha
is now an
affine function
of
{\bm{v}}
, with a linear term and a constant term:

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bm{r}}}{\|{\bm{r}}\|^{2}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{v}}=\frac{{\bm{r}}}{\|{\bm{r}}\|^{2}}\cdot{\bm{v}}-\frac{{\bm{r}}\cdot{\bm{v}}_{0}}{\|{\bm{r}}\|^{2}}

This also suggests a different set of steering tools. In particular, the operation that zeros out this version of
\alpha
is now given by

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle={\bm{v}}_{0}+\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\perp}(\Delta{\bm{v}})={\bm{v}}-\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}})+\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}}_{0})

And the operation that sets it to a new value
\alpha^{\prime}
is given by

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle={\bm{v}}-\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}})+\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}}_{0})+\alpha^{\prime}{\bm{r}}

Note that the
{\bm{r}}
-parallel component of
{\bm{v}}_{0}
determines the zero-point for
\alpha
. We can make this more explicit by defining
\alpha_{0}
such that

\alpha_{0}{\bm{r}}=\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}}_{0})

and therefore

{\bm{v}}^{\prime}={\bm{v}}-\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}})+\alpha_{0}{\bm{r}}+\alpha^{\prime}{\bm{r}}

(1)

This representation, derived by thinking about an affine decomposition of
{\bm{v}}
, realizes a generalization of two previously described steering methods. In the rest of this paper, we show that with sensible choices for
\alpha_{0}
and
\|r\|\neq 0
, this more complete, affine treatment of steering simultaneously gives more deterministic control over the refusal behavior and solves the problem of certain models generating nonsense when steered.

1.2
Standardized Steering

In the context of steering, we define standardization as the deterministic control of a behavior with respect to the steering parameter. In other words, we say that a steering technique is “standardized” with respect to a parameter if fixing the parameter fixes the degree to which a behavior is exhibited, regardless of input prompt. A technique is not standardized if the degree of behavior depends upon the input prompt itself along with the parameter. Furthermore, we require that for a steering strategy to be considered standardized that setting the steering parameter to 1 should induce the behavior and setting it to 0 should induce the null-behavior. Tables
1
and
2
give examples of standardized versus non-standardized steering.

In the context of refusal, we consider the behavior to be refusal and the null-behavior to be non-refusal. We feel that this choice is intuitive, but one could perform the same analysis having made the opposite choice.

Standardization may or may not be desirable in any given application. If the goal is simply to bias a model toward a certain behavior rather than totally control it, then non-standardized techniques may be preferred. However, if the desire is to totally control the behavior—as often with refusal—then a standardized technique is required. Here, we show that contrastive activation addition is not a standardized method of steering refusal whereas directional ablation fails to generalize across models. We also show that ACE helps to simultaneously both of these shortcomings, being more standardized than CAA and more general across models than directional ablation.

2
Existing Steering Techniques

2.1
Contrastive Activation Addition

Described by
Rimsky et al. 2023
, contrastive activation addition (CAA) modifies the activations of a model simply by adding a difference in means vector weighted by a scalar multiplier,

{\bm{v}}^{\prime}={\bm{v}}+\alpha{\bm{r}}

(2)

where
{\bm{r}}
is given to be the difference in means of the positive and negative examples,
{\bm{r}}^{+}
and
{\bm{r}}^{-}
respectively, and
\alpha
is a constant which mediates the strength of the intervention. As we can see, Equation
2
simply uses a subset of the terms in Equation
1
. CAA was originally shown by
Rimsky et al. 2023
to be effective in steering Llama 2 across a range of behaviors including refusal.

One of the major limitations of CAA is its inability to incorporate how much of the target behavior may already be exhibited by the model in a given context. As a result, the behavior exhibited by the model after steering may not be fully determined by the steering parameter. Thus, this type of steering is non-standardized as seen in Figure
2
(left) and Table
1
.

2.2
Directional Ablation

Directional ablation modifies the activations of a model by projecting them onto the orthogonal complement of the span of the difference of means vector:

{\bm{v}}^{\prime}={\bm{v}}-\text{proj}_{{\bm{r}}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}})

(3)

While directional ablation is more standardized than CAA, it is unable to control the degree of expression beyond ablation. Like CAA, Equation
3
takes a subset of the terms in Equation
1
.
Arditi et al. 2024
partially address this by reintroducing the difference in means vector, effectively performing CAA after directional ablation. They find that this reintroduces the refusal behavior:

{\bm{v}}^{\prime}={\bm{v}}-\text{proj}^{\parallel}_{{\bm{r}}}({\bm{v}})+{\bm{r}}

(4)

While this technique is sufficient to remove all linearly accessible information about the concept encoded by
{\bm{r}}
, as shown by
Belrose et al. 2024
, no guarantee is made that the resulting vector will end up in a meaningful location in activation space. The result is that this technique can cause certain models to collapse into producing nonsense outputs. We observe exactly this effect in
Hermes Eagle RWKV v5

(
Paulo et al. 2024
)
.

3
Affine Concept Editing

We propose affine concept editing (ACE) as a combination of CAA and a corrected directional ablation which realizes the more complete affine description outlined in Equation
1
, which we repeat here for convenience:

{\bm{v}}^{\prime}={\bm{v}}-\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}})+\alpha_{0}{\bm{r}}+\alpha{\bm{r}}

(Henceforth, for simplicity, we will use
\alpha
to denote the
new
value of
\alpha
, not the original value.)

We find that the resulting technique both produces a standardization effect in Llama 3 and corrects the problem of nonsense generation in Hermes Eagle RWKV v5.

We will begin by taking
{\bm{r}}\in\mathrm{span}({\bm{r}}^{+}-{\bm{r}}^{-})
. We need to resolve choices for
\alpha_{0}
and
\|{\bm{r}}\|
. We will choose them such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\alpha=0}[{\bm{v}}^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\text{null-behavior}}[{\bm{v}}]={\bm{r}}^{-}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\alpha=1}[{\bm{v}}^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\text{behavior}}[{\bm{v}}]={\bm{r}}^{+}

That is, the expected value of the steered activation vectors have the same mean as the example null-behavior vectors when
\alpha=0
and the same mean as the example behavior vectors when
\alpha=1
.

The first of these equations lets us solve for
\alpha_{0}
. Taking the projection along
{\bm{r}}
of each side and applying idempotence of projections and linearity of expectation:

\displaystyle\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}(\mathbb{E}[{\bm{v}}-\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}})+\alpha_{0}{\bm{r}}])=\alpha_{0}{\bm{r}}=\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{r}}^{-})

Similarly, the second equation lets us determine
\|{\bm{r}}\|
:

\displaystyle\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}(\mathbb{E}[{\bm{v}}-\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}})+\alpha_{0}{\bm{r}}+{\bm{r}}])=\alpha_{0}{\bm{r}}+{\bm{r}}=\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{r}}^{+})

Subtracting these equations and using the fact that
{\bm{r}}
is parallel to
{\bm{r}}^{+}-{\bm{r}}^{-}
:

\displaystyle{\bm{r}}

\displaystyle=\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{r}}^{+}-{\bm{r}}^{-})={\bm{r}}^{+}-{\bm{r}}^{-}

Which is to say, before we only fixed
{\bm{r}}\in\mathrm{span}({\bm{r}}^{+}-{\bm{r}}^{-})
. But now we find that our standardization criterion requires that
{\bm{r}}
is precisely equal to
{\bm{r}}^{+}-{\bm{r}}^{-}
. We may now plug this back in to Equation
1
which gives us our final definition of ACE as follows:

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle={\bm{v}}-\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{v}})+\text{proj}_{\bm{r}}^{\parallel}({\bm{r}}^{-})+\alpha{\bm{r}}

(5)

We can interpret this equation as an affine decomposition of
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}
: we are taking
{\bm{r}}^{-}
as our reference point
{\bm{v}}_{0}
and steering in the
{\bm{r}}
direction relative to this reference point.

Speaking practically,
\alpha
here holds more intuitive meaning than it did for CAA. Rather than setting the strength of the intervention, it sets the degree to which the steering vector is represented in the resulting activation vector relative to the means of known samples. In CAA,
\alpha=0
represents non-intervention, leaving model behavior unchanged. In directional ablation with CAA,
\alpha=0
represents an unpredictable level of behavior found near the origin. In ACE,
\alpha=0
sets the mean expression of
{\bm{r}}
, and thus the degree of expression of the target behavior, to a known quantity.

4
Results

We evaluate the ability of ACE to steer refusal behavior on ten different open weight language models
(
Bai et al. 2023
;
Young et al. 2024
;
Team et al. 2024
)
by using Llama 3 70B Instruct as a scoring model, asking it whether or not the prompt had been refused for a given response. Harmful and harmless examples are taken from AdvBench
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
and Alpaca
(
Taori et al. 2023
)
respectively. We always apply ACE on the activations of all tokens at the output of a single layer of the residual stream. This way, ACE interrupts the entire residual stream and no information can flow around the intervention via skip connections. We choose a layer close to the middle of the network, with the specific layer index chosen by qualitative inspection of steering results. We find that poor layer choice either produces no effect, or causes the model to produce nonsense, with the “sweet-spot” being somewhere around the midpoint of the model. Steering vectors are extracted at the last token of the example prompts, including special tokens in the chat template–in other words, as the model is producing the first token of the response. See Appendix
A
for full results and Appendix
B
for experimental details.

We show results for Llama 3 8B Instruct
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
in Tables
1
and
2
, where we find that ACE produces superior standardization than CAA. Figure
2
shows the probability of refusal across a range of steering parameters for ACE along with CAA-only steering. Results on other models also show improved standardization compared to CAA, even for models where all steering approaches struggle to fully control refusal. Strikingly, on Hermes Eagle RWKV v5, we find that ACE produces coherent text, while directional ablation causes the model to output nonsense (Table
3
).

Furthermore, while
Arditi et al. 2024
demonstrated that directional ablation was effective at controlling refusal across a number of models, we find that when we replicated the method on an RNN, specifically Hermes Eagle RWKV v5, the model produced nonsensical results. Upon manual inspection of the activation vectors involved, we found that directional ablation alone amounted to an undesirably strong intervention in Hermes Eagle RWKV v5 unlike in Llama 2.

5
Discussion

By combining corrected directional ablation with activation addition, we are able to achieve more standardized steering. In practice, because the standardization effect of ACE is not perfect, we observe that ACE parameters slightly below zero and slightly above one are the most effective. This can be seen in Figure
2
as the
P(\text{refusal})
curves for both prompt types become quite close for parameters just above one and just below zero. We would say that for Llama 3 8B Instruct at layer 15, ACE is nearly perfectly standardized at
\alpha=-0.5
and
\alpha=1.5
.

While ACE successfully produces a standardizing effect superior to CAA alone, our results show that this effect is not perfect. This could imply that the derived steering vectors are flawed or that that directional ablation does not fully erase information. Preliminary experiments using more sophisticated erasure techniques such as LEACE
(
Belrose et al. 2024
)
did not improve the standardization effect and were actively detrimental in many cases. Future work may seek to address some of these limitations by exploring whether or not its possible to fully erase concepts like refusal linearly or if nonlinear techniques are required. Furthermore, further study is needed to extend all of these techniques to work reliably on a more varied set of behaviors.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we derived and examined affine concept editing (ACE) as an alternative approach for steering behavior in large language models. ACE combines directional ablation with contrastive activation addition in an affine framework, providing more consistent and standardized control over model behavior. Our results demonstrate that, at least on the refusal behavior, ACE mitigates the limitations of CAA and directional ablation, offering greater generalizability across models and input prompts. It is especially noteworthy that ACE performs well on Hermes Eagle RWKV v5, where directional ablation alone produced incoherent results.

ACE offers notable advantages in scenarios where deterministic behavior is critical, as it allows for fine-grained control over refusals while maintaining coherent output across a variety of inputs. Future work should explore whether nonlinear approaches could further improve ACE’s standardization effect and expand its applicability to broader behavior-modification tasks.
