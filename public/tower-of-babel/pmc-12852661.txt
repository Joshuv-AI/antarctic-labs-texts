Hybrid cryo-nano MQL strategy for sustainable and high-performance machining of Monel 400
Authors: G Narsinga Rao, S Sivalingam, Dipak Patel, D T Arunkumar, S Lakshmi Sankar, Binayak Pattanayak, Shahbaz Juneja, Saurabh Aggarwal, Abhijit Bhowmik, Lema Abate
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the combined influence of advanced lubrication and cooling techniques on enhancing the machinability of Monel 400, a nickel-based superalloy widely employed in marine, chemical, and aerospace applications due to its exceptional corrosion resistance and mechanical strength. Four machining conditions—dry cutting, Minimum Quantity Lubrication (MQL), nano-enhanced MQL (NMQL), and a hybrid approach integrating cryogenic liquid nitrogen with NMQL (Cryo + NMQL)—were systematically compared. The experiments were performed using fixed machining parameters: a cutting speed of 80 m/min, feed rate of 0.15 mm/tooth, radial depth of cut of 6 mm, and axial depth of cut of 0.8 mm. Among these, Cryo + NMQL, featuring carbon nanotube (CNT)-enriched sunflower oil nanofluid combined with liquid nitrogen (LN₂) as the cryogenic medium, achieved superior outcomes, reducing cutting force, cutting temperature, surface roughness, and tool wear by 23.99%, 24.66%, 44.58%, and 54.85%, respectively, compared to dry cutting. SEM analysis confirmed adhesion and abrasion as dominant wear mechanisms, which were significantly mitigated under Cryo + NMQL. Multi-Objective Response Surface Methodology (MORSM) optimized the machining parameters to achieve an ideal balance of performance metrics, with a composite desirability index of 0.956. This study shows that combining LN₂ cryogenic cooling with CNT-based NMQL provides superior machinability of Monel 400—offering lower forces, temperatures, surface roughness, and tool wear—while contributing new insights for an alloy rarely examined under hybrid cooling environments. The findings validate Cryo + NMQL as an eco-benign, high-performance machining strategy, offering enhanced tool life, improved surface finish, and sustainable outcomes.Keywords: Monel 400; CNT nanofluid; Liquid nitrogen; Hybrid cooling, machining responses; Response surface methodologySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Nickel-based superalloys are extensively employed in critical sectors such as aerospace, nuclear energy, and marine engineering due to their exceptional mechanical strength and ability to retain structural stability under extreme thermal conditions1. However, these very attributes also make them inherently difficult to machine. Conventional machining practices often lead to excessive heat generation and rapid tool degradation, which in turn compromise surface integrity and dimensional accuracy2. As reported by Liao et al.3, the combined effects of machining-induced stresses and elevated temperatures can alter the surface microstructure of these alloys, ultimately reducing fatigue resistance, tensile strength, and creep performance during service. To address these limitations, cutting fluids have traditionally been employed as an effective means of thermal and frictional control. They help dissipate heat, reduce the coefficient of friction at the tool–workpiece interface, and facilitate smooth chip evacuation, thereby enhancing surface quality and overall machining efficiency4,5. For example, Arunachalam et al.6 demonstrated that the use of coolant during Inconel machining can suppress built-up edge (BUE) formation and alleviate thermally induced residual stresses. Despite these advantages, conventional cooling strategies are often associated with environmental concerns, health risks, and high disposal costs arising from chemical-laden fluid waste. In response to these challenges, sustainable lubrication and cooling alternatives—such as dry machining, Minimum Quantity Lubrication (MQL), and cryogenic cooling—have gained increasing attention. Dry machining eliminates fluid-related hazards entirely but subjects the cutting zone to elevated thermal loads, which can accelerate tool wear and compromise surface finish7,8. Among the sustainable approaches, MQL has emerged as a particularly promising solution. This technique delivers a precisely controlled quantity of lubricant (typically 10–500 ml/h) in the form of a fine atomized mist using compressed air. The mist evaporates rapidly upon contact with the high-temperature cutting zone, absorbing heat and thereby reducing thermal stresses. Owing to its minimal fluid consumption and reduced environmental impact, MQL not only improves machining performance but also provides economic benefits, lower energy demand, and improved occupational safety9.

Over the years, extensive research has examined the influence of various lubricants and MQL-based strategies on machining performance, revealing clear advancements in tool life, temperature control, and surface integrity. For instance, Ghatge et al.10 demonstrated that substituting mineral oil with vegetable oil in MQL systems substantially reduced flank wear and cutting temperatures while improving overall surface quality. Similar improvements were reported by Khaliq et al.11, who showed that MQL-assisted milling of titanium alloys yielded superior surface finish and longer tool life compared with dry machining. Their study also highlighted the critical importance of optimizing MQL parameters to fully realize these benefits under diverse machining conditions. Consistent findings were presented by Szczotkarz et al.12 in machining SS316 steel, where MQL application effectively reduced chip adhesion, thereby extending tool life and enhancing surface characteristics. Despite these promising outcomes, concerns remain regarding the limited cooling capacity of conventional MQL, particularly under high-stress or elevated-temperature machining environments. Yildirim et al.13 underscored this challenge, prompting researchers to explore strategies for enhancing MQL performance beyond its baseline capabilities. One emerging direction involves dispersing nanoparticles into MQL fluids to formulate nanofluids with superior heat-transfer and tribological characteristics. These nanoparticle-enriched lubricants exhibit improved thermal conductivity and form stable protective films at the tool–chip interface, enabling more effective cooling and lubrication. Among the available options, inorganic nanoparticles have gained significant traction due to their compatibility, dispersion stability, and favorable molecular interactions. Comprehensive assessments by Kulkarni et al.14 highlighted the effectiveness of various nanoparticle types—especially carbon-based particles such as graphene, carbon nanotubes, and nanodiamonds—in enhancing thermal conductivity within nanofluid formulations. Further improvements in stability and functional performance have been achieved through the incorporation of surfactants, which help maintain uniform dispersion. Building on these developments, Gong et al.15 evaluated the machining of Inconel 718 under dry, conventional MQL, and graphene-enriched nanoMQL environments and reported substantial gains in tool life and surface finish with the nanoparticle-assisted lubrication. Similarly, Yi et al.16 demonstrated that graphene oxide-based nanoMQL significantly improved the machinability and sustainability of Ti-6Al-4 V alloy, with notable benefits even in hard-turning conditions—highlighting its environmental advantages and energy-efficient behavior. Extending this line of inquiry, Sarıkaya et al.17 investigated the use of hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) nanofluids during NMQL machining of Haynes 25 and observed a marked reduction in notch wear, underscoring the reliability and robustness afforded by nano-enhanced lubrication systems.

Cryogenic cooling has emerged as a powerful strategy for enhancing the machinability of superalloys, largely owing to its ability to deliver extremely low-temperature media—such as liquid nitrogen (LN₂) or compressed carbon dioxide (CO₂)—directly into the cutting zone. By rapidly dissipating heat, these agents effectively reduce cutting temperatures, suppress tool wear, and improve surface integrity. For instance, Venugopal et al.18 demonstrated that LN₂ substantially extended tool life while minimizing adhesion and diffusion wear relative to dry machining. Consistent findings were reported by Wang and Rajukar19, who observed significant reductions in flank wear and cutting forces during the machining of hard materials like silicon nitride. Moreover, Shokrani et al.20 showed that cryogenic milling of Ti-6Al-4 V decreased surface roughness by nearly 40% while reducing chip re-deposition, reinforcing the potential of cryogenic cooling in high-performance applications. Despite these benefits, a key limitation of cryogenic fluids lies in their insufficient lubrication capability, often resulting in variability in machining outcomes. This shortcoming has motivated the development of hybrid cooling approaches that combine the thermal advantages of cryogenics with the lubricating effects of conventional or nano-enhanced cutting fluids. Shokrani et al.21 were among the first to demonstrate that integrating cryogenic cooling with MQL markedly improved tool life. Further evidence was provided by Schoop et al.22, who found that LN₂-assisted MQL significantly reduced tool wear, while standalone cryogenic cooling delivered superior surface finish. Yildirim et al.13 similarly reported that Cryo + MQL enhanced surface roughness, tool wear resistance, and thermal control compared to individual cooling modes. Recent advancements have further strengthened the case for hybrid systems. Danish et al.23 showed that a dual-nozzle Cryo + MQL configuration incorporating LN₂ and vegetable oil reduced surface roughness by 60.6% and tool wear by 19.5%, supported by SEM evidence of refined grains and minimal wear. Gajrani et al.24 applied Cryo + MQL to titanium alloys and observed lower machining forces with improved surface quality, while Naik et al.25 introduced a pulse-assisted Cryo + MQL technique that yielded smoother surfaces and reduced tool degradation. In machining Inconel 601, Korkmaz et al.26 reported that Cryo + NMQL offered the most favorable outcomes, including enhanced hardness and improved chip morphology. Collectively, these studies affirm that cryogenic and hybrid cooling strategies substantially improve tool life, surface integrity, and overall machining efficiency when dealing with difficult-to-cut materials.

Recent progress in sustainable machining has underscored the growing relevance of MQL, NMQL, and cryogenic cooling as alternatives to conventional dry machining for processing superalloys. Each method offers distinct advantages, yet their individual limitations become evident when subjected to the severe thermal and mechanical stresses encountered in nickel-based alloy machining. While cryogenic cooling with liquid nitrogen provides exceptional heat removal, it offers limited lubricity at the tool–work interface. Conversely, MQL and NMQL supply effective lubrication and friction reduction but remain inadequate in controlling the extreme heat generated during high-speed cutting of difficult-to-machine alloys. These contrasting characteristics have motivated the development of hybrid lubrication–cooling strategies that can synergistically combine the strengths of both approaches. Although hybrid techniques have gained momentum, research efforts have predominantly centered on widely used alloys such as Inconel 718 and Ti-6Al-4 V, with evaluations mostly confined to conventional responses like surface quality, cutting forces, and tool wear. In contrast, the machining behavior of Monel 400—a nickel-copper alloy valued for its corrosion resistance, toughness, and use in marine, chemical, and aerospace environments—remains insufficiently explored. Its inherent work-hardening tendency and high ductility pose additional challenges that necessitate a more nuanced investigation into advanced cooling–lubrication schemes. To address this gap, the present study introduces an integrated Cryo + NMQL strategy for milling Monel 400, leveraging the high cooling potential of liquid nitrogen together with the superior lubricating performance of carbon nanotube (CNT)-based nanofluids. Beyond assessing fundamental machining responses such as cutting force, temperature, surface roughness, and tool wear, the study places particular emphasis on enhancing surface integrity, minimizing thermo-mechanical effects, and improving tool life under hybrid conditions. Additionally, a Multi-Objective Response Surface Methodology (MORSM) framework is employed to optimize key machining parameters, providing a scientifically grounded pathway for achieving sustainable, efficient, and high-performance machining outcomes suitable for advanced manufacturing applications.

Experimental setup and methodology

Lubrication and cooling techniques

This study undertakes a comparative assessment of four lubrication and cooling strategies: dry cutting, MQL, NMQL, and a hybrid Cryo + NMQL technique. For MQL, a standard KENCO (India) unit was employed to deliver sunflower oil-based lubricant directly to the cutting zone. The NMQL approach involved dispersing 0.5 vol% CNT into the same base oil to enhance its tribological and thermal performance. The key physicochemical properties of the base oil and CNT are presented in Tables 1 and 2. In both MQL and NMQL systems, the cutting fluid was finely atomized and accurately directed toward the tool–workpiece interface. The system operated under a constant oil flow rate of 35 mL/h and 5 bar compressed air, with the spray nozzle positioned at a 45° angle and a 30 mm distance from the cutting zone. For the Cryo + NMQL trials, this configuration was augmented by simultaneously introducing a stream of LN₂ at − 196 °C. The cryogenic spray was delivered at a 30° angle and a standoff distance of 15 mm, synchronously with the MQL mist. All process parameters, including fluid delivery rates, nozzle alignment, and cryogenic integration, were established based on the methodology reported by Gajrani et al.24, ensuring consistency and alignment with prior validated procedures.

The selection of the chosen lubricating and cooling mediums in this study was a deliberate and well-informed decision, grounded in their proven alignment with eco-friendly machining practices. Numerous studies have highlighted that these alternatives support sustainable manufacturing by significantly reducing the use of toxic chemicals and limiting ecological harm27–30. In addition to their environmental benefits, these mediums offer practical advantages such as extended tool longevity, decreased power consumption, and reduced material waste—factors that directly lower operational costs. Their widespread availability further supports smooth implementation in industrial machining setups. By prioritizing such mediums, the study emphasizes not only ecological responsibility but also enhanced productivity and cost efficiency in the machining of superalloys.

Preparation and thermo-physical characterization of nanofluids

In this study, high-purity (99.9%) single-walled CNT, procured from LOBA Chemie Pvt. Ltd., India, were employed as nano-additives for preparing sunflower-oil-based NMQL lubricants. CNT were specifically selected over other commonly used solid lubricants such as WS₂ and MoS₂ because of their exceptionally high thermal conductivity (2000–6000 W/m·K), superior aspect ratio, and their proven ability to form continuous, protective tribofilms under high strain-rate machining conditions. Unlike WS₂ and MoS₂, whose self-lubricating properties primarily arise from layered shear mechanisms, CNT offer a combined effect of rapid heat dissipation, micro-rolling, and surface polishing at the tool–chip interface. These synergistic mechanisms have been widely reported to reduce interface temperature, tool wear, and frictional stress more effectively in demanding machining of superalloys—factors that strongly support their suitability for NMQL applications.

The CNTs used in this study possessed an outer diameter of 1–50 nm, a wall thickness of approximately 0.34 nm, and a specific surface area of 400–1000 m²/g, enabling efficient energy transport and stable interaction within the lubricant medium. To prepare the CNT-based nanolubricants, nine concentrations (0–0.8 vol% at 0.1 vol% intervals) were synthesized using a structured two-stage dispersion approach. Initially, CNT powder was introduced into sunflower oil—the chosen base fluid—and the mixture was subjected to magnetic stirring (Abdos®, MS-H280-Pro) at 600 rpm for 45 min, repeated twice to promote uniform pre-dispersion. A suitable surfactant, sodium dodecylbenzene sulfonate (SDBS), was then added to minimize agglomeration and enhance stability. Subsequently, the mixture underwent probe ultrasonication (ITB-32D4A76D, Johnson Plastosonic) to achieve long-term colloidal stability and homogeneous nanoparticle distribution. Machining details

For this study, Monel 400—a high-performance nickel–copper alloy known for its excellent corrosion resistance, mechanical strength, and suitability for demanding service environments—was procured from Siddhagiri Metals & Tubes, Mumbai, India. Its extensive use in aerospace, marine engineering, chemical processing, defense components, medical devices, and sports equipment underscores its industrial significance. The alloy composition consists primarily of nickel (63.0–70.0%) and copper (28.0–34.0%), with minor constituents such as iron (≤ 2.5%), manganese (≤ 2.0%), carbon (≤ 0.3%), silicon (≤ 0.5%), and sulfur (≤ 0.024%). Machining trials were performed on a CNC milling machine (MTAB, India) using a fixed set of cutting parameters: a cutting speed of 80 m/min, feed rate of 0.15 mm/tooth, radial depth of cut of 6 mm, and axial depth of cut of 0.8 mm. These parameters were selected based on a combination of tool manufacturer recommendations and a series of preliminary trial cuts, during which the ranges were iteratively refined to avoid tool chipping, prevent thermal instability, and ensure smooth chip formation when machining Monel 400. The finalized values ensured stable cutting conditions, facilitated reliable assessment of machinability across all lubrication environments, and provided a consistent basis for comparing the performance of dry, MQL, NMQL, and Cryo + NMQL conditions.

The experimental program investigated the influence of four lubrication–cooling strategies—dry machining, MQL, NMQL, and the hybrid Cryo + NMQL technique—on the machining performance of Monel 400. All machining trials were conducted using TiN PVD-coated cermet inserts supplied by TaeguTec (ISO code: TNMG 160408-TT5100), mounted on an MTJNR 2525M16 tool holder. The insert geometry consisted of a 0.8 mm nose radius, an 11° clearance angle, a −6° rake angle, and a 95° approach angle, ensuring stable cutting engagement during milling. The TiN coating was selected due to its high thermal stability, reduced adhesion tendency with nickel-based alloys, and its proven compatibility with cryogenic and MQL environments. These tool specifications were deliberately chosen to enhance reproducibility and to ensure consistent heat resistance, tool stability, and reliable performance across all lubrication–cooling conditions.

Evaluation of machining performance indicators

A comprehensive set of machining performance indicators—including cutting force, cutting temperature, surface roughness, and tool wear—was measured using calibrated instruments and standardized procedures to ensure accuracy and repeatability. Cutting forces were recorded using a KISTLER 9170 A piezoelectric dynamometer mounted beneath the workpiece fixture, enabling simultaneous acquisition of radial (Fx), tangential (Fy), and axial (Fz) forces. These signals were processed through a KISTLER 5238B multi-channel charge amplifier and DynoWare software. Steady-state force values were extracted from continuous data streams, and the resultant force (Fr) was calculated from the vector combination of the three components.

Cutting temperature was monitored using a ThermoPro TP8 infrared imaging camera, which operates in the 8–14 μm spectral band and offers a measurement range of − 20 °C to 800 °C with a thermal sensitivity better than 0.08 K. These specifications make the TP8 suitable for capturing the rapid and localized thermal variations occurring during machining of nickel-based alloys. Since oil mist generated under MQL, NMQL, and Cryo + NMQL conditions can partially obstruct the infrared field of view, the camera was positioned at an oblique angle away from the densest mist path to maintain an unobstructed line of sight. Multiple high-frequency thermal frames were captured during each milling pass, and only those exhibiting minimal mist interference were retained. From these validated frames, the maximum cutting temperature (Tmax) was extracted and used for analysis, as peak temperature most accurately reflects the thermal load influencing tool degradation and surface integrity. The emissivity of Monel 400 was set to 0.5 in accordance with established literature values for nickel-based alloys30, and a conservative uncertainty of ± 2.5% was adopted to account for camera accuracy limits, emissivity assumptions, and residual scattering from atomized mist.

Surface roughness was measured using a KLA-Tencor MicroXAM-100 3D optical profiler following ISO 4287 standards. For each machined surface, three measurement traces were recorded along the feed direction, and the arithmetic average roughness (Ra) was computed. A Gaussian filter was applied to remove high-frequency noise while retaining the intrinsic surface texture profile. Tool wear evaluation was performed using a JEOL scanning electron microscope (SEM) after every 25-minute machining cycle. Prior to imaging, the inserts were ultrasonically cleaned to ensure accurate wear characterization. Flank wear (VB) was quantified using ImageJ software in strict accordance with ISO 3685:1993, which specifies the standard procedure for wear land measurement, including the use of the maximum uniform wear land width (VBmax) as the primary criterion for assessing wear progression. This ensured consistency and methodological transparency in determining the tool’s wear limit. SEM morphology was further utilized to identify dominant wear mechanisms such as adhesion, abrasion, micro-chipping, and localized diffusion. Complementary EDX analysis confirmed the presence of adhered Monel 400 material on the tool edge—particularly under NMQL and Cryo + NMQL environments—providing additional evidence to support the interpretation of the observed wear phenomena. Results and discussion

Influence of lubri-cooling environments on cutting force

During milling, the forces exerted on the cutting tool fluctuate due to variations in tool engagement, chip thickness, and material removal rate. Any abrupt change in chip–tool interaction leads to force instability, increased frictional resistance, and localized temperature rise. To assess the influence of different lubri-cooling strategies, cutting forces were measured using a piezoelectric dynamometer. For each 25-minute machining cycle, the maximum values of Fx, Fy, and Fz were extracted from the stabilized section of the force signal, and the resultant force (Fr) was computed using Eq. (1). As shown in Fig. 4, dry cutting produced the highest resultant force (321 N). The application of MQL reduced the force to 288 N (10.28% reduction), while NMQL further decreased it to 263 N (18.07% reduction) due to improved tribological and thermal performance of CNT nanoparticles. The Cryo + NMQL condition achieved the lowest force (244 N; 23.99% reduction), demonstrating the strong synergistic effect of extreme cooling and nano-assisted lubrication.

These findings align with prior literature, where Cryo + MQL environments consistently reduce cutting forces due to reduced shear deformation, enhanced chip brittleness, and superior frictional control. Yıldırım et al.23 reported significant reductions in cutting forces during machining of Ni-based alloy 625 under Cryo-MQL conditions. Gajrani et al.24 also demonstrated that hybrid cryogenic–MQL application promotes easier chip segmentation and lowers shear resistance. Likewise, Danish et al.23 observed notable cutting-force reduction in Cryo + MQL machining of Inconel 718 due to improved cooling efficiency and minimized tool–chip adhesion.

It is important to acknowledge that cryogenic medium can sometimes increase cutting forces at lower cutting speeds owing to cryo-induced work hardening of the material. This behavior has been reported in cryogenic machining studies by Venugopal et al.18 and Wang & Rajurkar19, where increased material hardness at sub-ambient temperatures elevated cutting resistance when lower cutting speeds were used. However, at the constant cutting speed of 80 m/min, the cooling and lubrication effects were dominant, which led to a clear reduction in cutting forces.

Influence of lubri-cooling environments on cutting temperature

Cutting temperature plays a decisive role in determining tool life, chip formation, and surface integrity during the machining of Monel 400, which is inherently prone to rapid heat accumulation. Under dry machining, the peak temperature reached 365 °C, the highest among all conditions, due to the complete absence of a cooling or lubricating medium (Fig. 5). With no mechanism available to dissipate frictional heat, thermal energy is concentrated at the tool–chip interface, intensifying adhesion, accelerating abrasive interactions, and promoting built-up edge formation. This severe thermal environment establishes dry cutting as the reference baseline for evaluating the effectiveness of different sustainable lubri-cooling strategies.

Variation in cutting temperature across different lubrication and cooling conditions.

The application of MQL provided noticeable thermal relief, lowering cutting temperature to 312 °C (a 14.52% reduction). This improvement stems from the formation of a thin lubricating oil film and the partial evaporative cooling effect of atomized droplets striking the hot interface. However, the inherently low fluid volume and rapid evaporation limit its ability to sustain cooling under prolonged or high-load cutting. In contrast, NMQL further decreased the temperature to 288 °C (a 21.09% reduction), owing to the addition of CNT nanoparticles. The superior thermal conductivity of CNTs enhances heat dissipation from the shear zone, while their micro-rolling and tribofilm-forming actions reduce shear stress and friction. Together, these mechanisms offer more effective thermal management compared to conventional MQL, demonstrating the clear advantage of nano-enhanced lubrication in heat-intensive machining of nickel–copper superalloys.

The Cryo + NMQL hybrid environment produced the lowest peak temperature of 275 °C, corresponding to a 24.66% reduction compared to dry cutting. This superior performance arises from the synergistic interaction between cryogenic cooling and nano-lubrication. Liquid nitrogen provides intense convective and nucleate boiling heat transfer, rapidly extracting heat and reducing the bulk temperature of both tool and workpiece. Simultaneously, the CNT-enriched nanofluid maintains robust lubrication even under extremely low temperatures, enabling efficient thermal conduction and friction reduction at the interface—an aspect not achievable with cryogenic cooling alone. Consequently, Cryo + NMQL ensures the most stable and balanced thermal environment, effectively suppressing heat generation and accumulation while preserving lubrication integrity during the machining of Monel 400.

Influence of lubri-cooling environments on surface roughness

Surface roughness is a critical indicator of machining performance, directly affecting functional properties such as fatigue life, wear resistance, frictional behavior, and impact durability of mechanical components. In this study, surface roughness measurements were performed in accordance with ISO 4287, with three readings taken under each test condition to determine the Ra. Figure 6 presents the comparative results for surface finish achieved under various cooling and lubrication environments. Among the tested conditions, dry machining — lacking any external lubrication or cooling — produced the coarsest surface finish, recording the highest Ra value of 0.83 μm. The application of MQL significantly improved surface finish due to the micro-mist formation of oil and compressed air, which formed a thin lubricating film at the cutting interface. This film served to reduce contact temperature and tool–chip friction, resulting in smoother surfaces. MQL led to an 18.07% reduction in surface roughness compared to dry machining.

Variation in surface roughness across different lubrication and cooling conditions.

Further refinement was observed with the use of NMQL, where CNT were dispersed in the base oil. The introduction of nanoparticles enhanced the tribological performance through mechanisms such as micro-polishing, mending of minor surface irregularities, and rolling at the tool–work interface. This method yielded a Ra value of 0.5 μm, further reducing surface roughness by 39.76% relative to dry conditions. The most substantial improvement was achieved using Cryo + NMQL, which integrates cryogenic cooling with nanofluid-assisted lubrication. This hybrid method produced the smoothest machined surface, with a roughness value of 0.46 μm—representing a 44.58% reduction compared to dry machining. The dual benefits of deep cooling from the cryogenic medium and enhanced lubrication from the nanofluid contribute to minimizing thermal distortion and frictional wear. Comparable insights were reported by Ross et al.33, who analyzed 3D surface profiles during sustainable machining of Monel 400. Their findings confirmed that dry cutting led to the roughest textures, while hybrid lubrication techniques achieved superior surface integrity. The present study reinforces the conclusion that combining cryogenic cooling with advanced lubrication methods significantly enhances surface finish in high-performance machining applications.

Influence of lubri-cooling environments on tool wear

Tool wear plays a crucial role in determining essential machining outcomes such as surface finish and thermal stability in the cutting zone. Its progression is influenced by several factors, including tool substrate, coating material, cutting parameters, and the lubrication or cooling strategy employed. In the present study, tool-wear behavior is examined under a constant set of machining parameters for a fixed 25-minute cutting duration. Since the experimental design was not intended to run the tool until end-of-life, the investigation focuses on wear progression rather than deriving complete tool-life curves. However, the comparative wear trends observed under different environments provide meaningful indications of potential tool-life improvements. A detailed assessment of flank wear and notch wear—commonly encountered in the machining of nickel-based alloys—is carried out. While notch wear mechanisms remain complex, involving thermal gradients, mechanical loads, strain hardening, and chip adhesion, it is widely recognized that an effective lubrication strategy can significantly mitigate its severity.

Figure 7 presents the comparative VBmax progression under dry, MQL, NMQL, and Cryo + NMQL environments. Among the tested lubrication strategies, Cryo + NMQL demonstrates the most pronounced reduction in flank wear. Quantitatively, wear decreases by 33.52% with MQL, 41.27% with NMQL, and 54.85% with Cryo + NMQL relative to dry machining. The improvement achieved with NMQL over conventional MQL is particularly noteworthy. This enhanced performance is primarily due to the superior heat-transfer and friction-reducing capabilities imparted by nano-additives. Nanoparticles act as nano-ball bearings and boundary-film enhancers, reducing shear resistance at the tool–chip interface. Their high thermal conductivity also assists in dissipating heat more efficiently, resulting in lower interface temperatures. These combined effects lead to slower wear evolution and more stable machining conditions when compared to traditional MQL.

Progression of VBmax over machining time under different lubrication conditions.

Cryo + NMQL further elevates these advantages through a synergistic thermo-lubrication mechanism. The cryogenic jet rapidly extracts heat by convection, suppressing thermal softening and reducing oxidation-induced wear. Simultaneously, the NMQL droplets—which carry nanoparticles—promote evaporative cooling and form a persistent lubricating film over the cutting zone. By lowering the local temperature, the cryogenic medium minimizes premature evaporation of MQL droplets. This phenomenon, as emphasized by Bagherzadeh and Budak34, greatly extends the functional lifespan of the lubricant and ensures consistent lubrication even under high-temperature conditions. As a result, the Cryo + NMQL environment not only maintains the sharpness of the cutting edge for a longer period but also establishes a more thermally stable machining regime. Although the present work does not include full tool-life testing, the distinct suppression of wear progression clearly highlights the potential of this hybrid technique for significantly prolonging tool life and enhancing machining sustainability. Future studies incorporating complete tool-life assessments are recommended to validate these promising findings and quantify the long-term benefits more comprehensively.

The SEM images presented in Fig. 8 provide clear insights into the predominant wear mechanisms encountered during the milling of Monel 400 under Dry, MQL, NMQL, and Cryo + NMQL conditions. Adhesion-related wear was consistently observed across all cutting environments, as evidenced by the presence of adhered workpiece fragments on the tool surface. This observation is further supported by EDX analysis of the rake face, which confirms material transfer from the alloy to the tool—an expected phenomenon during the machining of nickel–copper-based superalloys. Similar adhesion-driven wear behavior has also been reported by Habeeb et al.35 and Ezugwu et al.36, who highlighted chip adherence as a key contributor to tool deterioration in difficult-to-cut materials. In addition to adhesion, abrasive wear was identified through the appearance of fine grooves and scratches oriented along the chip flow direction. These surface features likely result from hard alloy constituents sliding or becoming entrapped at the tool–work interface, causing micro-cutting and ploughing effects on the tool surface. Importantly, the manuscript reports only those wear features that are directly supported by the experimental SEM and EDX evidence, without inferring any transitions or compounded mechanisms that cannot be validated under the constant cutting conditions employed. Although hybrid lubri-cooling strategies—particularly NMQL and Cryo + NMQL—effectively reduced the severity of adhesion- and abrasion-driven wear, they did not eliminate these mechanisms entirely. Adhesive and abrasive wear persisted across all machining environments, reflecting the inherent challenges associated with processing tough superalloys such as Monel 400. These observations are in line with the findings of Sasi et al.37 and Danish et al.23, who similarly reported adhesion- and abrasion-dominated tool wear in nickel-based alloys due to poor thermal conductivity and work-hardening behavior. Their results further reinforce our conclusion that while enhanced cooling and lubrication can mitigate wear intensity and extend tool life, they do not fundamentally alter the primary wear mechanisms governing these materials.

ANOVA based statistical analysis

To evaluate the statistical significance and relative influence of the machining parameters on the measured responses, a comprehensive Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)38 was performed at a 95% confidence level. Four primary input variables—cutting speed, feed per tooth, radial and axial depths of cut—were analyzed. Complete ANOVA results for cutting force, cutting temperature, surface roughness, and tool wear are provided in Tables 4, 5, 6 and 7, which report the Sum of Squares (SS), Degrees of Freedom (DOF), Mean Square (MS), F-values, p-values, and Percentage Contribution Rates (PCR) for each factor. MORSM-based optimization

Optimization seeks to enhance system performance by systematically adjusting variables within defined limits to achieve the best possible outcomes. It involves either minimizing or maximizing key output parameters based on specific design goals39. Among the various techniques available, the MORSM has emerged as a robust statistical and mathematical framework for modeling complex engineering problems where multiple factors influence several performance outcomes simultaneously. MORSM is especially beneficial in mechanical engineering applications where traditional modeling struggles to capture nonlinear interactions between multiple inputs and outputs40,41.

In this study, MORSM is utilized to develop predictive models, optimize machining responses, and determine the most favorable set of process parameters. The aim is to minimize machining responses, using cutting speed, feed rate, radial depth of cut, and axial depth of cut as controllable input parameters. Figure 10 illustrates the optimized response surfaces tailored for sustainable machining. The MORSM-based optimization reveals a compromise solution that pinpoints precise values for each machining parameter, enabling a balanced trade-off among all objectives. Experiments conducted using these optimized settings confirm the reliability of the model, as demonstrated by the strong alignment between predicted and observed results (Table 8). The high composite desirability index of 0.956 further affirms the method’s efficiency and accuracy in solving multi-objective optimization tasks. Nonetheless, translating these optimized settings into real-world manufacturing environments will require additional validation. Scalable implementation may involve iterative testing across various operational scales to ensure the robustness and versatility of the proposed solution. This step is essential to maximize the industrial applicability and sustainability impact of the optimization framework.

Conclusions, limitations, and directions for future research

This study systematically examined the machinability of Monel 400 under four distinct lubri-cooling environments—dry cutting, MQL, NMQL, and the hybrid Cryo + NMQL strategy—to identify a sustainable, high-performance machining solution for this difficult-to-cut nickel–copper alloy. The findings demonstrate that the proposed Cryo + NMQL technique offers the most favorable machining conditions, primarily due to the synergistic interaction between deep cryogenic cooling and the enhanced tribological properties of the optimized 0.5 vol% CNT-based nanofluid. Relative to dry machining, the hybrid method resulted in substantial reductions in key machining responses: cutting force by 23.99%, temperature by 24.66%, surface roughness by 44.58%, and tool wear by 54.85%. SEM and EDX analyses confirmed that although adhesive and abrasive wear mechanisms persisted, their severity significantly diminished under the hybrid condition due to reduced interface temperature and improved lubrication.

The MORSM-based multi-objective optimization further validated the robustness of the hybrid strategy, achieving a high composite desirability index of 0.956 and demonstrating excellent agreement between predicted and experimental values. These results confirm the suitability of Cryo + NMQL for precision machining of Monel 400 while advancing the broader agenda of sustainable manufacturing. Importantly, this work also addresses a notable gap in existing literature: while hybrid lubri-cooling strategies have been extensively explored for alloys such as Inconel 718 and Ti-6Al-4 V, their application to Monel 400 has remained underexplored. The insights generated here provide a deeper mechanistic understanding of how cryogenic cooling and nano-lubrication jointly influence heat transfer, chip formation, tool integrity, and surface quality for this alloy.

Despite the promising outcomes, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study employed fixed cutting parameters; varying cutting speeds, feed rates, and tool geometries may reveal additional insights into the performance limits of the hybrid strategy. Second, while CNT nanofluids demonstrated superior tribo-thermal performance, their long-term stability under industrial recirculating systems and potential health or environmental considerations warrant further evaluation. Third, the experimental setup utilized a single-nozzle configuration for fluid delivery; alternative nozzle positioning or pulse-assisted cryogenic flows may further enhance cooling and lubrication synergy. Additionally, the study focused on short-duration machining trials; extended tool-life tests and real-time monitoring under production-scale conditions would be valuable for industrial adoption.

Future research should thus explore (i) alternative nanoparticle systems such as graphene, hBN, or hybrid nano-additives; (ii) advanced cryogenic–MQL architectures including dual-jet, pulse-assisted, or internally-cooled tooling systems; (iii) life-cycle assessments and cost–benefit analyses to quantify economic and environmental advantages; and (iv) integration of Industry 4.0 tools such as thermal imaging, acoustic emission sensing, and AI-driven wear prediction models for real-time process control. Extending this hybrid strategy to other nickel-based superalloys and complex multi-material components will further validate its versatility and accelerate its transition from laboratory testing to industrial machining ecosystems.
