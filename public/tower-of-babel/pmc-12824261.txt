Green CO2-capture cluster model using bioengineering of carbonic anhydrase enzyme: QM and QM/QM′ approach
Authors: Mina Ghiasi, Hanane Sadat Eghtedari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ongoing increase in anthropogenic CO2 levels and global warming demonstrate serious risks to human society and the biosphere. Green CO2 capture technologies that utilize the characteristics of enzymes such as carbonic anhydrase (CA), including low consumption, exceptional selectivity, and environmentally friendly nature, show significant promise. The hydration of CO2 by the CA model was investigated using QM/QM′ calculations. We performed a detailed theoretical study using density functional theory (B3LYP/6-31G* and B3LYP-D3/def2-TZPV/def2-ECP) by the cluster model approach. The hydration mechanisms were studied extensively in both gas and water solvent phases. Furthermore, explicit solvent effect was evaluated for water solvent using ONIOM (QM: QM′) calculations, and all thermodynamic functions through the reaction path were calculated in different phases. The mechanism of CO2 hydration exhibits the characteristics of an exothermic reaction, wherein the transition state resembles the reactant with a barrier height about of 6.35, 5.71, and 5.22 kcal.mol− 1 for TS1, TS2, and TS3, respectively, in water solvent. These results reveal low energy barriers and favorable thermodynamic profiles in solvated environments. These findings support future research on CA hydration mechanisms and the development of a synthetic enzyme model, which may lead to a significant reduction of CO2 emissions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32638-y.Keywords: Carbonic anhydrase, CO2 capture, Hydration mechanism, DFT calculation, ONIOMSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

To alleviate the atmospheric carbon emissions resulting from surging anthropogenic CO2 outputs, there exists a global initiative to identify sustainable carbon capture (CC) technologies that integrate both economic feasibility and maximal energy efficiency. Recent developments emphasize the need for a rational design framework in CO₂ capture systems, integrating enzymatic catalysis, support architecture optimization, and process-level engineering to achieve scalable and energy-efficient performance1. Within this framework, many innovative chemical and physical CC techniques have been developed2–4. Rationally designed capture systems consider the interplay between enzyme functionality, material properties, and process conditions, ensuring compatibility between catalytic activity and reactor design4.

A viable alternative to mitigate this issue encompasses the application of enzymes as biocatalysts to enhance the effective sequestration of CO2, producing bicarbonate at rates that may approach one million reactions per second5,6. A sustainable approach to CC involves transmuting CO2 into carbonate via a biomimetic pathway that utilizes CA as the catalyst7. It is important to highlight that biocatalysts present distinct advantages for CC, attributable to their exceptional specificity and operational efficiency. The utilization of CA for the sequestration of carbon dioxide presents multiple significant advantages, including (1) The catalytic properties facilitate the rapid conversion of CO2 into carbonate ion, thereby enhancing capture efficiency; (2) The specificity in targeting CO2 allows for selective capture, while concurrently reducing interference from other gaseous species; (3) CA enzyme demonstrate effective functionality under mild temperature and pressure conditions, which minimizes the CC process energy demands; (4) CA characterized by their biodegradable nature, renewability, and the potential for production through environmentally benign methodologies, which aligns with principles of sustainability2,3,8. In addition, recent research demonstrates that CA thermostability and activity in alkaline environments relevant to industrial flue gas can be greatly enhanced by rational enzyme design, either through directed evolution or computational engineering9. Additionally, when the enzyme is optimized for CC, it may present a financially advantageous alternative compared to conventional capture technologies, thereby rendering it economically feasible for extensive application. Nevertheless, many challenges, including exorbitant cost, stability, limited pH range, and complications associated with recovery and reuse, necessitate resolution. Moreover, MOF-anchored CA hybrids and biomimetic CA mimics have been reported in recent studies to enhance the stability and reusability of enzymes under harsh industrial conditions. For example, Asadi et al.10 developed CA@ZIF-8 electrospun fibers, while Zhu et al.11 introduced Fe-based CA mimics with enhanced CO₂ capture. Further reviews by Rogacka et al.12 and Xu et al.13 highlight the versatility of MOF–enzyme hybrid systems. According to modeling of enzymatic catalysis is now possible thanks to modern QM/MM methodologies14 and enhanced-sampling simulations15, which close the gap between studies of free and immobilized enzymes. Furthermore, one of the most effective strategies for resolving these problems is enzyme immobilization16–19. Controlling enzyme–support interactions, pore geometry, and surface functionalization are the main goals of rational immobilization techniques in order to preserve active-site accessibility and prevent leaching, which is essential for maintaining operational stability20. Porous silica materials (such as SBA-15, MCM-41, and KIT-16) emerge as promising candidates for enzymatic immobilization due to favorable properties that provide substantial surface area, uniform particle size, restricted pore size distribution, and remarkable thermal, mechanical, and chemical stabilities21,22. Various functional groups, including amino, chloride, thiol, carboxylic acid, epoxy, and phenyl groups, can be affixed to the surface of mesoporous molecular sieves. These functional groups facilitate diverse interactions between the support surface and the enzyme molecule. Among these, the amino group is predominantly employed for modifying the supports23,24. Furthermore, it is recognized that the selection of linker molecules (such as glutaraldehyde and 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane) is determined by rational design principles in order to reduce conformational distortion during immobilization by fine-tuning enzyme orientation and microenvironment polarity25. Although various enzymatic classes are capable in CC, including dehydrogenases, decarboxylases, and nitrogenase, none have demonstrated superior efficiency and adaptability to CC conditions compared to the CA family26. According to prior research, the model system employed in this study encompasses the immobilization of CA on porous silica (PS) with 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APTES) was achieved through the application of crosslinking enzyme with glutaraldehyde (GA)16. In addition to increasing enzyme reusability, these immobilization techniques enable incorporation into solid-state devices and membranes for ongoing CO2 absorption. Thus, this bioengineering strategy is now essential to the logical development of hybrid catalytic systems that blend the robustness of inorganic frameworks with the selectivity of enzymes27,28. Previous theoretical QM and QM/MM studies29 have proposed a reaction mechanism, as depicted in Scheme 1. The catalytic pathway for CO2 hydration facilitated by wild CA comprises four distinct stages; (1) Zn-bound hydroxide reacts with CO2 to yield Zn-bound bicarbonate through a nucleophilic attack; (2) the bicarbonate then undergoes rearrangements to form a more favorable leaving group; (3) bicarbonate is replaced by solvent water; and (4) finally, the deprotonation of Zn-bound water restores the initial structure of CA. Notably, step 1 pertains to the formation of bicarbonate, while steps 2 and 3 involve the release of HCO3−, and step 4 corresponds to the dissociation of H2O30,31. Nevertheless, despite extensive investigation into CA catalysis, a comprehensive quantum-level understanding of its electronic configuration and catalytic energetics remains insufficient. Recent advancements in multiscale modeling and QM/QM′ hybrid methods have opened novel opportunities for exploring enzyme–substrate interactions and predicting reactivity with unprecedented precision32,33 The current study aims to bridge this gap by developing a green CO₂-capture cluster model employing QM and QM/QM′ approaches to elucidate the electronic and structural determinants governing CO₂ activation in CA. This study concentrates on the catalytic behavior of immobilized CA for CO₂ capture utilizing GA/APTES/porous silica (PS) as the support. The intrinsic mechanism and activation parameters of free carbonic anhydrase have been previously delineated through a quantum mechanical cluster model25. The present research extends this mechanistic framework to the immobilized CA system to clarify how the GA/APTES/PS influences the catalytic microenvironment, energy landscape, and process-level efficacy. By integrating theoretical quantum chemistry with enzyme bioengineering, our study contributes to the broader objective of designing optimized biocatalysts for large-scale CO₂ capture. This interdisciplinary methodology not only enhances mechanistic understanding but also facilitates the rational development of sustainable materials for prospective carbon-neutral technologies. Consequently, we have focused solely on investigating the Lindskog mechanism30. This emphasizes our study to explore the CO2 hydration mechanism using theoretical methods, specifically employing a CA enzymatic model. Additionally, we highlight the practical applications and notable results stemming from this research effort by examining the complex CO2 hydration mechanism facilitated by the CA model enzyme.

Our investigation seeks to gain insights into the catalytic activity and molecular interactions underlying this vital biochemical reaction. Utilizing theoretical approaches to decipher the mechanistic complexities and identify key factors that affect the efficiency and specificity of CA-mediated CO2 hydration. Our research not only clarifies fundamental biochemical processes but also explores the practical implications. Comprehending the mechanistic details of CO2 hydration offers significant potential across various fields, including biotechnology, environmental science, and pharmaceuticals34. By illuminating the catalytic mechanism involved, our study may facilitate advancements in innovative enzymatic catalysis, biomimetic materials, and CC technologies, ultimately aiding in strategies to enhance enzymatic CO2 capture, reducing greenhouse gas emissions, and addressing climate change. By clarifying the catalytic capabilities of CA in the hydration of CO2, we seek to contribute to the formation of sustainable solutions to urgent environmental issues. Through an interdisciplinary methodology, we aspire to enhance scientific comprehension and foster the creation of innovative solutions with a tangible, real-world effect.

Computational details

Model system

Given the substantial size of enzyme molecules, analyzing these systems remains challenging, even with significant advancements in computational chemistry techniques. This challenge is exacerbated when considering the external environment, including solvent media, which is necessary for a more accurate representation of enzyme behavior. Consequently, over the past few decades, various models and computational strategies have been proposed to account for both the structural and functional aspects of enzymes. For both quantum mechanical (QM) and QM/QM′ calculations, the enzyme structure is constructed based on the crystallographic data available in the literature, specifically utilizing the PDB code 1CA235. The resulting cluster simulates the enzyme’s active site, includes a Zinc cation (Zn2+) coordinated to the nitrogen atoms of three histidine residues: His94, His96, and His119, as primary ligands. Tetra-coordinated Zinc is achieved by incorporating a hydroxyl group in the fourth ligand. It is noteworthy that the bulk buffer species were intentionally excluded to isolate the intrinsic chemical transformation (Zn–OH⁻ + CO₂ → Zn–HCO₃⁻). The Zn–bound water was modeled in its deprotonated form (Zn–OH⁻), corresponding to the catalytically active species near physiological pH (pKₐ ≈ 7). All coordinating histidines were maintained in neutral (N coordination). This level of modeling facilitates an accurate description of the electronic structure and transition state of the nucleophilic step.

Additionally, GA has been employed as a linker to immobilize the model enzyme on amine-functionalized silica support. This cluster model effectively elucidates the catalytic mechanism employed by human carbonic anhydrase II (hCA II) in the hydration of native CO2 and other isoelectronic promiscuous substrates examined in prior studies36–39, as shown in Scheme 2.

Methodology

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations, utilizing the B3LYP hybrid functional40,41, were conducted for the geometry optimization along a reaction path including substrate, intermediates (INTs), and transition states (TSs) without symmetry constraints. These calculations were executed using the standard 6-31G* basis set, with energies adjusted for solvation and zero-point vibrational effects. The QST3 procedure was employed to search for TSs, and the relationship between each TS and the two associated minima was verified using the intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC). Vibrational analyses were performed on all optimized structures with the same functional and basis set as the corresponding geometry optimizations. Frequency calculations were performed on all stationary points on the potential energy surface in order to study their nature as TSs or INTs42–44. Furthermore, all reactants, products, intermediates, and transition states were fully optimized utilizing advanced basis sets and dispersion-corrected density functional theory. Geometry optimizations were executed at the B3LYP-D3 theoretical level with the def2-TZVP basis set applied to all main-group atoms, while the Stuttgart–Dresden effective core potential and associated basis set (def2-ECP) were used for Zn(II)45. The D3 empirical dispersion correction developed by Grimme et al.46,47 was incorporated to accurately account for van der Waals interactions between the enzyme components, thereby ensuring a realistic description of metal–ligand interactions and non-covalent contacts within the active site. To validate the computational setup, single-point energy refinements were performed at the ωB97X-D/def2-TZVP theoretical level42 for key stationary points, including representative intermediates and transition states. The consistency of relative reaction barriers and energetic orderings across these levels confirms the robustness of qualitative mechanistic conclusions and the reliability of the chosen protocol for representing Zn-containing active sites. In line with prior studies on CO₂ activation in CA enzymes, and considering the catalytic mechanism of the native substrate in hCA II, the overall reaction pathway is depicted in Scheme 1. The initial step involves the formation of Zn-OH through deprotonation of the Zn-bound water molecule, which acts as a nucleophile in CO₂ interaction. The metal–hydroxide subsequently attacks CO₂ to form a bicarbonate intermediate. CO₂ is then hydrated, resulting in Zn-bound bicarbonate, a labile intermediate36. This intermediate then reacts with water, resulting in bicarbonate release into solution.

All stationary points were identified as either minima or transition states by performing frequency calculations on all optimized structures at the same theoretical level used for optimization. Vibrational frequency calculations were also employed to derive thermodynamic functions, including enthalpies and Gibbs free energies at 298.15 K and 1 atm. Moreover, the reported Gibbs free energies (ΔG and ΔG≠) were computed at 298.15 K and corrected from the gas-phase standard state (1 atm) to the solution-phase standard state (1 M) using the term RT ln(24.46) = 1.89 kcal. mol⁻¹ per species48. For the bimolecular association step, such as CO₂ + Zn–OH⁻ → TS1 and INT3 (Δn = − 1), this correction reduces the barrier by 1.89 kcal mol⁻¹. Intramolecular steps (TS₂, Int2 and TS₃) are unaffected (Δn = 0). Entropic contributions were evaluated within the rigid-rotor harmonic oscillator (RRHO) approximation and refined using the Grimme quasi-harmonic (quasi-RRHO) correction with a frequency cutoff of 100 cm⁻¹ as implemented in GoodVibes27,49. The resulting variation in ΔG≠ values does not exceed ± 2 kcal mol⁻¹, consistent with standard uncertainty estimates. All reported free energies correspond to the reactant-state concentration of 1 M. Given the significant influence of solvation on chemical and physical properties, two approaches were used to include solvent effects. The first is an implicit solvent model, treating the solvent as a continuous medium. Among these, the polarizable continuum model (PCM) was employed to simulate water, which is recognized for its reliability, adaptability, and efficiency in describing solvent interactions50. Results and discussion

Optimization of a bio-mimetic model of carbonic anhydrase enzyme

CA enzyme exhibits distinctive properties; however, its practical applications are limited due to its low stability in extreme conditions, as well as challenges related to recovery and reusability. Consequently, various immobilization techniques have been explored to address these limitations. Enzymes that are immobilized through methods such as cross-linking, covalent binding, self-assembly, adsorption, and entrapment demonstrate enhanced robustness and stability compared to their free counterparts55–58. Notably, the cross-linking technique involves the formation of covalent bonds between the enzyme and the support matrix, facilitated by functional reagents known as cross-linkers, which include glutaraldehyde, glyoxal, toluene diisocyanate, or formaldehyde59. Typically, the amino groups of lysine, the imidazole group of histidine, and the phenolic hydroxyl group of tyrosine are utilized for enzyme immobilization60. The establishment of covalent bonds between active groups of the support matrix, such as -OH, -COOH, and -NH2, and enzymes can enhance enzyme retention and reusability, as well as improve loading efficiency61,62. The functionalization of the surface significantly enhances interactions between enzymes and the support. However, specific functional groups necessary for modification are largely contingent upon the chemical composition and structural characteristics of the immobilized enzyme63,64. The active site of hCA II comprises three histidine residues that coordinate with a zinc cation. Histidine residues are crucial for bonding to the glutaraldehyde linker (GA) and its attachment to the mesoporous matrix. In this research, the active site of hCA II, obtained from the protein data bank (PDB) under code 1CA2, was immobilized onto the amine-functionalized porous silica matrix utilizing GA, as illustrated in Scheme 2. Previous studies have indicated that porous materials provide an effective surface for enzyme binding65–67. In alignment with catalytic hydration pathway of natural CA, Scheme 1, geometries of model system, which includes CA active site, GA, APTES, and PS support [CA/GA/APTES/PS], were constructed and optimized to reflect similar reaction steps, with the transition state structures for each reaction step also identified and fully optimized in the gas and solvent phase with both above menthioned methods. Figure 1 illustrates the optimized geometry of [CA/GA/APTES/PS] in water solvent as well as some geometrical parameters. The GA/APTES/PS immobilization strategy facilitates covalent anchoring and creates a stabilizing microenvironment that maintains CA’s native structure and Zn²⁺ active center integrity. APTES functionalization and glutaraldehyde activation result in a mildly polar environment that stabilizes the enzyme, preserving its structural perturbation. Based on previous spectroscopic and kinetic studies, immobilized CA on functionalized silica generally retains a substantial portion of its native activity (typically 70–90%) with only modest changes in activation parameters (ΔG≠ ∼1 kcal·mol⁻¹), suggesting that the catalytic mechanism remains largely preserved48,49 From a process perspective, immobilized CA shows enhanced dispersion and stability within porous supports, improving CO₂ effective mass transfer to active sites, which accelerates CO₂ hydration and lowers solvent regeneration energy requirements. These reported trends indicate a qualitative link between enhanced enzyme stabilization and improved CO₂ conversion efficiency in immobilized systems, although the extent of improvement may vary depending on support chemistry and operational conditions27,49.

The CO2 hydration mechanism by [CA/GA/APTES/PS] cluster model

As depicted in Scheme 1, the mechanism initiates with a nucleophilic attack by lone pairs of Zinc-bound hydroxide on the CO2 molecule, resulting in the formation of an enzyme-substrate (ES) complex structure. Since the CO2 molecule approaches oxygen atom of Zn-OH, the linear O-C-O structure begins to bend, ultimately yielding a Zn-HCO3− structure. The tetrahedral model of enzyme structure (CO2) complex, denoted as ES, is derived from isolated reactions without an energy barrier. Furthermore, CO2 molecule is fully optimized at the same computational level. The bond length of Zn-O1 increases from 1.85 Å in the reactant state to 1.86 Å in ES state. This elongation of Zn-O1 bond correlates with an enhancement in the nucleophilic character of hydroxyl ion, thereby facilitating subsequent nucleophilic attack. The measured C-O1 distance of 2.63 Å in ES complex (1.81 Å in TS1) is consistent with values reported in our prior research (1.68 Å) and those documented by Solá et al. (1.59 Å)68, Lipscomb et al. (1.50 Å)69, and Marino et al. (1.75 Å)29. Geometry of the proposed ES indicates a subsequent phase in the interaction, wherein nucleophilic attack has commenced. At this stage, the polarization of the CO₂ molecule by the hydroxide species bound to zinc becomes particularly significant. Atomic charge calculations conducted utilizing the NBO method indicate that the net atomic charge of the carbon atom increases from 1.073 to 1.123, while the disparity in net negative charges between the O3 and O2 atoms approximates 0.021. An increase in negative charge on the O2 atom implies an electrostatic interaction with the zinc ion (Table S1). According to Table S1, the Zn center shows only modest charge variation across ES → TSs → INTs, consistent with its role as a Lewis center that maintains coordination throughout the mechanism. Moreover, the C(O₂) atom shows charge changes reflecting electron redistribution during bond formation/cleavage, and finally, the histidine nitrogens display changes correlated with coordination/bonding changes and H-bonding interactions. Although there is no direct chemical bond between zinc ion and the O2 atom (2.97 Å), from a geometric standpoint, CO2 molecule can be regarded as a fifth ligand. Our results align well with those reported by Solá68, Lipscomb69, Pullman70, Jacob et al.71, and Marino et al.29. Activation of CO2 leads to the formation of the first transition state, TS1, in which the hydrogen carbonate group is nearly monocoordinated to Zinc cation. For example, the Zn-O2 bond distance is shorter in TS1 compared to ES geometry (2.12 Å in TS1 versus 2.96 Å in ES), while the Zn-OH bond distance is longer (1.89 Å in TS1 versus 1.86 Å in ES). The imaginary frequency of −363i cm− 1 associated with TS1 corresponds to C-O1 stretching. Free energy barriers for TS1 are calculated to be 12.27 kcal.mol− 1 in the gas phase and 6.35 kcal.mol− 1 in water solvent. Subsequent intermediate, INT1, involves rearrangement of the bicarbonate moiety, with C-OH bond distance stretching to 1.41 Å (refer to Fig. 2). Energetically, INT1 is situated at −5.31 kcal.mol− 1 in the gas phase and − 9.17 kcal.mol− 1 in the solvent phase relative to ES (see Fig. 3). As depicted in Scheme 1, following formation of INT1 entails a rotation around Zn-O2 bond while simultaneously breaking Zn-O1 bond (TS2 species), resulting in the rearrangement of bicarbonate fragment within the active site (see Fig. 4). Low energy barrier observed in this region, measured at 10.82 and 7.39 kcal.mol− 1 in the gas and solvent phase, respectively, relative to ES, Table 2. This interaction facilitates the attack of the water molecule, leading to the establishment of a pentacoordinate water complex at TS3, with energy values of 8.29 and 6.12 kcal.mol− 1 for the gas and solvent phase, respectively, concerning the energy of initial ES. In the subsequent step, the oxygen atom of H2O progressively approaches the Zn cation, resulting in the formation of a weakly bound Zn-OH2 structure in conjunction with HCO3−. This process results in the formation of a pentacoordinated zinc complex (INT3), which is situated at energy levels of −18.25 and − 15.22 kcal.mol− 1 in gas and solvent phase, respectively, relative to ES. Subsequently, H2CO3 is displaced from the catalyst, yielding the Zn-OH form of the catalyst, which restores the original structure of the catalyst and completes the catalytic cycle. The geometries of TSs were confirmed through IRC and QST3 methods. Figures 5, 6 and 7 illustrate IRC for three TSs. Figure 3 depicts the Gibbs free energy profile associated with the catalytic hydration of CO2 by the model system. The results indicate that the formation of INTs and TSs occurs energetically more favorably in the solvent than in the gas phase. Furthermore, as indicated in Fig. 3, TSs and INTs show some stabilization due to solvent effects during transitions from gas to the solvent phase, emphasizing the solvent’s significant impact on the stability of these species. All thermodynamic functions related to the reaction path are calculated and presented in Table 2. Moreover, our calculated results align closely with those reported by Marino and Russo29 across different phases. It is widely accepted that a lower energy barrier correlates with an increased reaction rate; thus, the reaction rate for each step can be assessed based on height of energy barrier. For comparative purposes, the Gibbs free energy of the initial system is set to zero. Throughout the catalytic CO2 hydration process, the enzyme model must overcome three energy barriers corresponding to three TSs. To ensure the reliability of our DFT results, we conducted additional full geometry optimizations at the B3LYP-D3/def2-TZVP/def2-ECP level of theory. These computations included all key intermediates (INT1–INT3) and transition states (TS1–TS3) in both gas and water solvent phases. The results from this higher-level method confirm thermodynamic and structural trends of B3LYP/6-31G* level, confirming the consistency and robustness of our findings. The optimized geometries of transition states and intermediates from both approaches follow the same patterns, suggesting the stability and method-independence of CO₂ hydration mechanism (Table S2). The alignment among computational methods provides compelling evidence for the validity of the proposed reaction pathway and validates the energetic and mechanistic trends in our DFT analysis. To further verify the accuracy of the optimized geometries, additional single-point energy calculations were performed at the ωB97X-D/def2-TZVP level for all intermediates and transition states optimized at the B3LYP/6-31G* level. This final validation step ensures that the relative energetics and mechanistic features are not affected by the choice of computational level.

In the present QM-cluster model, which excludes the His64 residue and the associated proton-shuttling pathway, the highest computed barrier corresponds to the nucleophilic attack of Zn–OH on CO₂ (TS₁). The calculated Gibbs free energy barriers are the average of 11.56–12.27 kcal mol⁻¹ in the gas phase and 5.45–6.35 kcal mol⁻¹ in solvent for both B3LYP/6-31G*-B3LYP-D3/def2-TZVP/def2-ECP methods, respectively. These values are in good agreement with previous computational studies on the hCA II active site: Solà et al. reported a barrier of 9.3 kcal mol⁻¹ using a hybrid QM/MM approach68, while Marino and Russo29 obtained barriers between 7 and 10 kcal mol⁻¹ at the B3LYP/6–311 + + G(d, p) level. Similarly, EVB and reactive MD simulations by Voth and co-workers indicated barriers of 6–9 kcal mol⁻¹ when explicit solvent and proton-transfer channels were incorporated.

It should be noted that, in contrast to the full enzymatic environment, the present cluster model omits the His64 residue and the extended proton-shuttle network. Consequently, explicit proton transfer cannot be captured within this framework. As a result, the rate-limiting step predicted for this truncated model corresponds to CO₂ activation (TS1), whereas in the native enzyme, both experimental kinetics and QM/MM studies consistently identify the proton-transfer step as rate-determining (ΔH ≠ ≈ 7.8 kcal mol⁻¹)72. Therefore, any direct comparison between the rate-determining step obtained here and experimentally observed enzymatic kinetics must be made with caution.

Despite this intrinsic limitation, the computed activation barriers and essential geometrical parameters for the Zn–OH⁻ addition to CO₂ fall within the range reported by more complete QM/MM and EVB investigations. This indicates that the local chemical reactivity at the Zn center is reasonably well represented by the cluster approach. However, this agreement should not be interpreted as full equivalence with the enzyme, since the model necessarily neglects long-range electrostatic stabilization, protein conformational dynamics, solvent reorganization, and entropic contributions—all of which can significantly influence proton-transfer energetics and overall catalytic turnover.

Accordingly, while the present results support the mechanistic relevance of the Zn–OH–CO₂ chemical step and provide guidance for bioinspired catalyst design, they should be viewed as computational indications rather than definitive predictions. Future studies incorporating explicit proton shuttling (e.g., QM/MM with His64 included or constant-pH MD) together with experimental validation will be required to fully resolve these effects.

Intrinsic Reaction Coordinate (IRC) calculations were performed for all optimized transition states (TS1–TS3) at the B3LYP/6-31G* level to confirm that each transition state connects the correct reactant and product minima, Figs. 6 and 7, and 8. For each TS, both the potential energy profile and the key geometric parameter evolution (bond lengths and/or angles relevant to bond formation and cleavage) were monitored along the mass-weighted reaction coordinate. All IRC paths demonstrate smooth and continuous variations of energy, confirming stable connectivity along the reaction pathway. The continuous evolution of mass-weighted coordinates between the reactants and products further substantiates the nature of each transition state.

Moreover, the immobilized enzyme retains significant catalytic activity and shows improved operational stability compared with the free enzyme. The activation free energy (ΔG≠) of the immobilized enzyme was calculated to be an average of 11.91 kcal·mol⁻¹ in the gas phase and about 5.90 kcal·mol⁻¹ in the aqueous (solvated) phase for both above-mentioned methods. These values are only slightly higher than those reported for the free enzyme (10.21 and 5.80 kcal·mol⁻¹, respectively) in our previous QM investigation36. The small difference of approximately 1.70–0.10 kcal·mol⁻¹ suggests that the immobilization induces only minor restrictions in conformational flexibility while largely preserving the electrostatic characteristics of the Zn–His–H₂O catalytic field. This finding indicates that the essential reaction pathway of the native enzyme is preserved in the immobilized system, affirming that [CA/GA/APTES/PS] provides a stable yet minimally perturbing microenvironment for catalysis. The observed increase in the activation barrier may result from restricted structural motion due to crosslinking, which enhances enzyme framework stability. Nonetheless, the immobilized enzyme retains approximately 80% of its intrinsic catalytic efficiency while demonstrating improved recyclability (≥ 10 operational cycles) and resistance to denaturation under process conditions. To assess the predicted results of QM-cluster model against existing free energy profiles in literature, it is essential to recognize that thermodynamic properties of our computed mechanisms are affected by the limited presence of extensive water networks within the enzyme model, unlike models such as QM/MM or QM-cluster that incorporate additional water molecules. Accurately identifying and modeling water networks for the solvent-exposed active site remains a challenging issue, further complicated by the mobility of water molecules and their generally low resolution in X-ray crystallography. Therefore, in the next step, all reaction path mechanisms were studied in the presence of approximately 1000 water molecules.

Solvent effects

Investigation of solvent effecs on hydration mechanism of CO2 catalyzed by enzyme CA is of paramount importance for several reasons that must be considered in QM calculations: (1) solvent influences the catalytic efficiency of CA, with water, as natural solvent, enhancing enzyme’s activity by facilitating proton transfer and stabilizing intermediate species; (2) dynamics of solvent molecules affect the overall reaction mechanism, thus understanding these effects is crucial for elucidating the precise pathway of CO2 hydration; (3) solvent effects are vital for design of industrial process, such as CC, where optimizing solvent conditions can improve efficiency of CO2 absorption and conversion technologies; and (4) interaction between CO2 and solvents, particularly water, significantly impacts aquatic environments, influencing CO2 dissolution and carbon cycle, which are critical for addressing climate change challenges. Consequently, this research investigates both explicit and implicit solvent effects for aforementioned reasons. Selection and design of solvents are essential in carbon cpture process, as efficiency and overall cost of the process are directly influenced by the solvent, which affects factors such as CO2 absorption capacity, equipment size, and solvent regeneration energy. Explicit solvent effect accounts for short-range electrostatic interactions and the effect of a hydrogen-bond network. Considering the significant role of solvent in calculated results, explicit solvent effect was investigated by employing the two-layer ONIOM method, QM/QM′ calculations. The ONIOM method is one of the best methods to include explicit solvation effects with a low computational cost. It should be noted that the AM1 semi-empirical Hamiltonian used to represent the explicit water shell in the ONIOM (QM: QM′) calculations has known limitations in describing hydrogen-bonding and molecular polarization in liquid water. This may affect the accuracy of absolute solvation energies. Conversely, AM1-based solvation models like AM1-SCRF and AM1-SM2 have been widely utilized in explicit or cluster–continuum solvation studies, providing an efficient computational approach to assess relative solvation trends with manageable model sizes72,73. Accordingly, our discussion focuses on relative energy trends and mechanistic insights rather than absolute solvation energy values. As the results indicate, the inclusion of explicit solvent molecules leads to a reduction in all thermodynamic quantities, which shift to more favorable (negative) values in the ONIOM calculations. Although no experimental thermodynamic data are available for direct comparison, the trends obtained here provide meaningful insights into the thermodynamic impact of solvation on the CA-catalyzed hydration of CO₂.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides a detailed examination of the full catalytic pathway of CO₂ hydration by the major physiological isoform of human carbonic anhydrase, hCA II, using QM and QM/QM′ methods. The reaction pathway—comprising nucleophilic attack, rearrangement, replacement, and deprotonation—closely reflects the mechanism known for the native enzyme. Thermodynamic parameters were evaluated along the entire reaction coordinate in both gas and solvent phases, including models containing nearly 1000 water molecules. Given the increasing urgency of climate-related challenges, biomimetic carbonic anhydrase systems may offer potential—but still preliminary—utility in CO₂ capture technologies. Similar analogs may also find use in water treatment and pH regulation. The results further show that the Zn–OH⁻ nucleophilic attack on CO₂ remains energetically favorable even within a simplified cluster model lacking an extended proton-shuttle chain. This finding suggests that the chemical step at the Zn center is inherently robust and may be transferable to engineered or synthetic catalytic systems. Future studies will focus on modeling explicit proton-transfer kinetics and evaluating the influence of immobilization on pKₐ shifts using constant-pH molecular dynamics and QM/MM methods. Comparison with our previous QM work on the native enzyme indicates that immobilization with GA/APTES/PS largely preserves the intrinsic reaction energetics, while also enhancing the structural stability and potential reusability of the catalyst. Overall, the energetics predicted here indicate that immobilized carbonic anhydrase could be viable for stable CO₂ capture and reuse; however, these conclusions remain computational and will require experimental confirmation. Additionally, immobilization appears to increase the activation barrier by approximately 1 kcal/mol relative to the free enzyme—a modest change that implies limited perturbation of the Zn–His–H₂O core, although real catalytic performance under operational conditions cannot be fully inferred from the present model. It is important to acknowledge that the current calculations rely on a finite cluster model and a simplified treatment of proton-transfer pathways. These factors introduce inherent limitations that must be considered when extending the results to experimental systems. While the immobilized system may combine near-native catalytic behavior with operational advantages, further computational and experimental work is needed to confirm its applicability in large-scale CO₂ absorption processes.

Altogether, these findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the hCA II mechanism and may help guide the design of improved biomimetic catalysts. The Zn-containing active site model presented here also provides a useful baseline for future comparisons with metal-substituted variants of hCA II.
