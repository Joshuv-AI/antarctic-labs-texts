ILAE‐YES global webinar series: Integrating clinical and basic science in epilepsy research

ABSTRACT
AbstractSummaryBridging clinical and basic research is increasingly recognized as a priority in the epilepsy field, yet opportunities for integration remain limited by the time, space, and financial constraints of scientific meetings. To address this gap, the Research Task Force of the Young Epilepsy Section of the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE‐YES) organized a free global webinar series designed to promote translational dialogue and provide accessible research education for early‐career clinicians, researchers, and physician‐scientists. Based on a preliminary ILAE‐YES community survey, eight topics of high interest were selected: (1) epigenetics, (2) EEG biomarkers, (3) ictogenesis, (4) thalamo‐cortical network, (5) sudden unexpected death in epilepsy, (6) neurodegeneration and seizures, (7) seizure‐related brain damage, and (8) neuromodulation therapy. From March to June 2025, eight live Zoom webinars were held, each featuring expert speakers representing both basic science and clinical perspectives, with recordings made available as unlisted YouTube videos to ensure on‐demand access. A total of 1199 individuals from 116 countries registered, 63.2% from low‐ and middle‐income countries. Live attendance averaged 50 participants per session, and the mean session duration was 71.6 min, including an average of 16 min of discussion. Post‐session feedback was obtained from 285 respondents; overall satisfaction was high, with 93.3% rating their experience as 4 or 5 on a 5‐point Likert scale. Speaker satisfaction was similarly high (95.4%), and 80.0% reported gaining new research ideas. Although access to YouTube and Google Forms may have limited participation in some regions, the series provided an inclusive and globally accessible platform. These findings demonstrated that free, discussion‐focused online webinars represent a scalable, low‐cost, and reproducible educational model that can effectively promote international engagement and integration between clinical and basic epilepsy research, aligning with the ILAE's global educational mission and complementing WHO IGAP priorities on capacity building and equitable access to knowledge.Plain Language SummaryBringing together basic science and clinical research is important for improving epilepsy care, but many researchers have limited opportunities to learn across these fields. INTRODUCTION

Bridging clinical and basic research through translational approaches has increasingly been recognized as a priority in the epilepsy field. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Although this topic has gained greater visibility at international scientific meetings, constraints in time, space, and program structure often limit the depth and breadth with which key mechanistic and clinical issues can be jointly addressed. In addition, participation in major conferences typically requires substantial financial and logistical resources, creating barriers particularly for early‐career clinicians and researchers. Even where virtual components exist, registration fees and scheduling constraints may continue to restrict access. As a result, opportunities for sustained dialogue and research‐oriented educational opportunities that explicitly connect basic neuroscience and clinical investigation remain unevenly distributed across regions and career stages. These challenges are especially pronounced in low‐ and middle‐income countries, where opportunities to engage in high‐quality, research‐focused, and translational epilepsy education remain limited.

Given this context, there is a pressing need for scalable, inclusive, and cost‐free educational platforms that can meaningfully facilitate dialogue between basic science and clinical practice, stimulate translational thinking, and support global capacity building in epilepsy research. To address this gap, the Research Task Force of the Young Epilepsy Section of the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE‐YES) organized a free global webinar series, designed specifically to promote translational research dialogue rather than to deliver clinical skills training or service‐oriented education, by pairing fundamental neuroscientific concepts with their clinical relevance while providing on‐demand access to accommodate diverse time zones and resource settings. This Special Report summarizes the structure, reach, and outcomes of this initiative.

METHODS

Preparation and planning of the webinar series

To identify topics of greatest interest, we conducted a preliminary survey through the ILAE‐YES community platforms (Slack and WhatsApp), targeting early‐career clinicians, researchers, and physician‐scientists. Based on the responses, members of the Research Task Force discussed the results and finalized specific topics. In selecting the final topics, the Research Task Force aimed to achieve a balance between fundamental disease mechanisms, clinically relevant questions, and emerging areas with translational potential. Priority was given to topics that (i) were highly ranked by early‐career members, (ii) allowed meaningful integration of basic science and clinical perspectives within a single session, and (iii) addressed educational concepts broadly relevant across different health‐care settings. Using these criteria, eight topics were selected: (1) Epigenetics, (2) EEG biomarkers, (3) ictogenesis, (4) Thalamo‐cortical network, (5) Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP), (6) Neurodegeneration and seizures, (7) Seizure‐related brain damage, and (8) Neuromodulation therapy. For each topic, potential speakers with expertise spanning both basic science and clinical research were identified. The selected speakers were contacted, informed about the goals of the webinar series, and formally invited to participate. Session scheduling was coordinated in consultation with speakers, and all webinars were planned to be held between March and June 2025. The overarching educational objective was to promote translational thinking by pairing basic neuroscience concepts with clinical relevance in each session.

Structure and implementation of the webinar sessions

The webinar series was conducted free of charge, and participants were required to complete a single Google Form for pre‐registration covering all eight sessions, with no restriction on the number of registrants. Each webinar was delivered live using the Zoom Webinar platform. Particular attention was paid to time zone differences when scheduling sessions, in order to maximize global accessibility. Each session generally consisted of two 20‐ to 25‐min presentations, one representing a basic science perspective and the other a clinical perspective (with the exception of session 3, in which a single 40‐min lecture covered both aspects), followed by a 10‐ to 15‐min discussion and Q&A segment to facilitate interactive engagement. In addition to sharing a common structural format, each webinar focused on a distinct translational theme. The eight sessions were entitled:

Promotion and participant recruitment

To support recruitment, one general flyer summarizing the entire webinar series and individual flyers for each session (nine flyers in total) was created (Figure 1). The ILAE‐YES Communication Task Force disseminated promotional announcements through the social media accounts of ILAE‐YES and ILAE (including Facebook, WhatsApp, Instagram, and LinkedIn), as well as via the ILAE‐YES mailing list. Announcements were typically distributed approximately 1 week to a few days prior to each session.

Evaluation and data collection

Following each webinar, participants were invited to complete an online feedback survey via Google Forms. Although the feedback survey remained open beyond the live webinar period for educational purposes, all descriptive analyses presented in this manuscript were restricted to responses submitted by July 27, 2025. Based on Kirkpatrick's model for assessing the usefulness of medical education and curriculum, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 the survey assessed overall satisfaction with the session, satisfaction with the speakers' presentations, and satisfaction with the discussion and Q&A segment, using a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = worst, 5 = best). The survey additionally asked participants whether the session inspired new research ideas. Responses of 4 or 5 were considered indicative of a positive evaluation. Completion of the survey was voluntary and anonymous. All registrants were invited to participate, regardless of whether they attended live or viewed the recording. This work reflects an educational quality‐improvement initiative summarizing the design, implementation, and outcomes of a webinar series. No patient data were used, and no identifiable personal information was included in the analytical dataset. Therefore, formal approval from an institutional review board was not required.

RESULTS

A total of 1199 individuals, from 116 countries or regions, registered for the webinar series. The geographical distribution of registrants is shown in Figure 2. The average duration of each webinar was 71.6 ± 9.0 min. The number of live attendees per session averaged 50, ranging from 33 to 75 participants. The average duration of the discussion and Q&A portion was 16.0 ± 5.2 min, accounting for 22.2% ± 5.5% of each session. Post‐webinar feedback surveys were completed by a total of 285 participants across the eight sessions. Overall session satisfaction was high, with 93.3% of respondents (266/285) rating 4 or 5 on a 5‐point Likert scale. Satisfaction with the speakers' presentations was 95.4% (272/185), while satisfaction with the discussion and Q&A segments was 90.2% (257/285). DISCUSSION

We successfully implemented a global webinar series designed to foster integration between clinical and basic epilepsy research. By leveraging the intrinsic advantages of online education, including free participation, elimination of travel barriers, and on‐demand accessibility, we were able to create a platform that promoted sustained engagement, meaningful discussion, and international participation. Importantly, scheduling each session on a separate day allowed us to avoid the time constraints often encountered in scientific meetings, thereby ensuring adequate time for both expert presentations and interactive dialogue. This balance is reflected in the high levels of participant satisfaction and the substantial proportion of attendees who reported being inspired to develop new research ideas.

From an implementation perspective, this initiative demonstrates a scalable, low‐cost, and reproducible educational model for promoting translational thinking in epilepsy research. The structured yet flexible program design, combined with proactive outreach through international epilepsy networks, enabled broad global reach, with participants representing 116 countries or regions. Notably, although the primary objective of the series was not to target specific regions, participants were distributed across high‐, upper‐middle‐, and lower‐middle‐income countries, suggesting that interest in research‐oriented translational education extends beyond high‐income settings. 1 This distribution indicates that unmet needs for platforms facilitating exposure to basic–clinical integration and research dialogue may also exist among early‐career professionals in upper‐ and lower‐middle‐income countries, even when such activities do not directly address immediate clinical care gaps.

Beyond geographic inclusivity, it is important to distinguish between gaps in clinical care delivery and gaps in research‐oriented education and dialogue. While substantial disparities persist globally in access to epilepsy care, including specialist services, medications, diagnostics, and surgery, these challenges represent a clinical and health‐system–level care gap. In contrast, the present initiative was designed to address a different, though related, gap: limited opportunities for early‐career clinicians and researchers to engage in sustained, bidirectional dialogue between basic science and clinical investigation. Importantly, regardless of the clinical setting or available resources, a sound background in basic science enables clinicians to achieve a deeper understanding of disease mechanisms, clinical phenotypes, and interrelated biological processes. Such foundational knowledge is essential for generating research ideas and for designing and implementing projects that are tailored to local clinical needs and unmet gaps, while simultaneously supporting advocacy for the adoption of evidence‐based standards of care worldwide. Accordingly, this webinar series was not intended to define region‐specific clinical priorities, nor to directly address deficiencies in service delivery. Rather, its primary aim was to promote translational research thinking by exposing participants to integrative discussions that connect mechanistic neuroscience with clinical questions across diverse settings. In addition, the series provided an educational benefit by presenting paired basic science and clinical perspectives for each topic, thereby encouraging participants to conceptualize epilepsy through an explicitly translational framework. The finding that 80% of respondents reported gaining new research ideas highlights the potential of such initiatives not only to disseminate knowledge but also to stimulate scientific creativity, collaboration, and cross‐disciplinary thinking among early‐career professionals.

Several prior initiatives have demonstrated the feasibility and educational value of web‐based and blended learning approaches in epilepsy education, particularly under the auspices of the ILAE. Previous ILAE‐led e‐learning programs, including tutored courses and interactive online resources, have achieved broad global reach, high learner satisfaction, and substantial participation from low‐ and middle‐income countries. 17 In addition, structured, competency‐oriented blended learning programs have highlighted the importance of deliberate educational design for supporting proficiency development in epileptology. 18 More recently, an ILAE‐led integrated virtual continuing medical education initiative combining webinars, case‐based learning, and microlearning modules reported high satisfaction rates and perceived clinical impact among participants. 19

Against this background, the present ILAE‐YES Global Webinar Series should be viewed as complementary to, rather than duplicative of, these existing efforts. Whereas prior programs primarily focused on structured curricula and clinically oriented education, our series was intentionally designed to prioritize live, discussion‐focused sessions that explicitly paired basic science and clinical perspectives. This format aimed to create space for bidirectional dialogue across disciplines and to stimulate translational research thinking among early‐career clinicians and researchers. The high levels of participant satisfaction observed in our study are consistent with previous reports, while the substantial proportion of respondents reporting the generation of new research ideas underscores the potential added value of extended interactive discussion as a distinct educational model within the broader ILAE e‐learning ecosystem.

This project has several limitations. First, reliance on platforms such as YouTube and Google Forms may have restricted access in certain regions (e.g., China), potentially limiting participation. Offering parallel access through alternative platforms may improve accessibility in future initiatives. Second, participation from low‐income countries remained very limited, accounting for only around 1% of registrants, indicating that additional targeted strategies are needed to reach these communities. Third, feedback data were obtained only from participants who completed surveys, raising the possibility of selection bias. Finally, although participants reported subjective educational benefit, objective learning outcomes were not assessed.

Despite these limitations, the webinar series successfully provided an accessible, sustainable, and globally engaging platform that facilitated meaningful dialogue between basic and clinical epilepsy research communities. These findings demonstrate that structured, discussion‐oriented online education can function as an effective implementation model for advancing translational epilepsy education worldwide. Beyond their immediate educational impact, and within the context of global initiatives emphasizing capacity building and knowledge exchange, these activities resonate with global policy frameworks such as the ILAE's international educational agenda and align in scope with selected elements of the WHO Intersectoral Global Action Plan on Epilepsy and Other Neurological Disorders (IGAP), which highlight capacity building, workforce strengthening, and equitable access to knowledge as essential components of improving epilepsy care and outcomes. Importantly, addressing research education gaps should not be conflated with, or viewed as a substitute for, efforts to reduce global disparities in epilepsy care. Initiatives aimed at improving access to medications, diagnostics, and specialized services remain essential, particularly in low‐resource settings. The present project should therefore be interpreted as complementary to, rather than competing with, broader global health efforts focused on clinical care delivery. Future efforts should explore methods to enhance engagement in underrepresented regions, assess longer‐term educational and research impact, and expand collaborative opportunities generated through such initiatives.
