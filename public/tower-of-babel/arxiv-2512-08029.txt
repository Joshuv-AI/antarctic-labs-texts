CLARITY: Medical World Model for Guiding Treatment Decisions by Modeling Context-Aware Disease Trajectories in Latent Space
Tianxingjian Ding, Yuanhao Zou, Chen Chen, Mubarak Shah, Yu Tian

Abstract
Clinical decision-making in oncology requires predicting dynamic disease evolution, a task current static AI predictors cannot perform. While world models (WMs) offer a paradigm for generative prediction, existing medical applications remain limited. Existing methods often rely on stochastic diffusion models, focusing on visual reconstruction rather than causal, physiological transitions. Furthermore, in medical domain, models like MeWM typically ignore patient-specific temporal and clinical contexts and lack a feedback mechanism to link predictions to treatment decisions. To address these gaps, we introduce CLARITY, a medical world model that forecasts disease evolution directly within a structured latent space. It explicitly integrates time intervals (temporal context) and patient-specific data (clinical context) to model treatment-conditioned progression as a smooth, interpretable trajectory, and thus generate physiologically faithful, individualized treatment plans. Finally, CLARITY introduces a novel prediction-to-decision framework, translating latent rollouts into transparent, actionable recommendations. CLARITY demonstrates state-of-the-art performance in treatment planning. On the MU-Glioma-Post dataset, our approach outperforms recent MeWM by 12%, and significantly surpasses all other medical-specific large language models. Our code and pre-trained models will be made publicly available once accepted.

This prediction-to-decision framework translates generative forecasts into transparent, actionable recommendations, directly emulating a physician’s adaptive reasoning. Overall, our contributions are threefold:

•

Latent-space disease forecasting.
We model progression as smooth, interpretable trajectories within a structured latent space, avoiding pixel-level artifacts and preserving anatomical consistency for trackable forecasts.

•

Temporal and clinical Contexts.
We encode continuous time and multimodal patient context (e.g., genomics, demographics, prior therapies) to generate physiologically faithful and individualized predictions.

•

Prediction-to-Decision Framework.
We integrate latent-space rollouts with an Inverse Survival Evaluation, translating predictions into transparent recommendations for adaptive, patient-tailored decisions.

Related Works

World Models.

The development of World Models has evolved along distinct paradigms. One branch includes generative-interactive models like Genie
7
, which learn to simulate controllable, pixel-level environments from unlabeled video. Another branch features latent-space planning models like DreamerV3
17
, which learn a compact Recurrent State-Space Model (RSSM,
13
). A third, non-reconstructive paradigm, exemplified by V-JEPA
6
and MuDreamer
8
, avoids pixel-level reconstruction entirely by predicting representations in latent space. In medicine, these concepts are emerging but often rely on diffusion-based generative models. For example, recent specialized frameworks like MeWM
42
, function as a generative model to synthesize and segment ‘post-treatment tumors’. Unlike these generative approaches, our method differs in two fundamental ways:

(1)
Prior medical world-models, such as diffusion-based MeWM operate in pixel space and synthesize post-treatment images, whereas we model disease evolution directly through transitions in latent space without any reconstruction objectives.

(2)
Existing approaches predict only one fixed future step, while our framework models arbitrary time gaps via time-conditioned latent dynamics.

Survival Analysis.

Traditional survival analysis relies on statistical models such as Cox regression
12
and Random Survival Forests
22
, which provide interpretable hazard estimation but assume proportional hazards and linear relations. Deep-learning extensions like DeepSurv
21
, DeepHit
24
, and Deep Survival Machines
31
relax these assumptions, learning nonlinear risk mappings from high-dimensional clinical data. Recent multimodal frameworks
16
;
29
further integrate imaging and omics features for personalized prognosis. However, most existing models remain static, predicting fixed survival outcomes without modeling temporal disease dynamics. Dynamic approaches such as Latent ODE
30
and Dynamic DeepHit
23
introduce time continuity, yet lack treatment conditioning or long-horizon modeling. Our framework extends these paradigms by forecasting patient-specific survival trajectories through a therapy-aware latent transition model.

3.3
Clinical and Temporal Contexts

As illustrated in
Fig.

2

Clinical Context
and

. These two components inject individualized and temporally consistent priors into the latent transition, enabling CLARITY to model disease progression in a physiologically grounded manner.

Clinical Context.

For each patient
p

\mathcal{C}_{p}
containing key clinical attributes,
including demographic information (
e.g
.
, age, sex),
molecular biomarkers (
e.g
.
,
IDH1/2
,
ATRX

1p19q
co-deletion,
MGMT

and treatment-related indicators.
We serialize
\mathcal{C}_{p}
into a textual prompt and encode it using
the medical text encoder
33

The resulting contextual embedding is then passed through a lightweight MLP
to produce the final clinical representation:

h^{\text{clin}}=\mathrm{MLP}_{\text{clin}}\big(\Phi_{\text{text}}(\mathcal{C}_{p})\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}},

where
d_{c}
denotes the dimension of the clinical embedding space.
This unified embedding conditions the Actor, enabling patient-specific latent prediction
and survival estimation based on the clinical profile.

Temporal Context.

The temporal component models disease evolution across arbitrary follow-up intervals.
Given imaging observations at times
t_{\text{pre}}
and
t_{\text{post}}
, we encode the time gap
\Delta t=t_{\text{post}}-t_{\text{pre}}
into a continuous representation. We encode the time gap using sinusoidal embeddings with log-spaced frequencies:

\gamma(\Delta t)=[\sin(\omega_{i}\Delta t),\cos(\omega_{i}\Delta t)]_{i=1}^{d_{t}/2},

(3)

where
\omega_{i}=1/10000^{2i/d_{t}}
, and
d_{t}
denotes the dimensionality of the temporal embedding.
This encoding provides the Post-Treatment Latent Predictor with explicit temporal awareness, enabling it to generate predictions that reflect the expected magnitude of physiological change over the elapsed interval.
The sinusoidal encoding represents elapsed time between visits, ensuring smooth interpolation across variable intervals.

Diseases Evolution Model

As shown in
Fig.

2

Post-Treatment Latent Predictor

Survival Predictor

Information Integration.

To generate a comprehensive post treatment prediction and conduct an accurate survival analysis, we feed various sources of information into the Diseases Evolution Model. Specifically, the model receives pre-treatment latent
z_{\text{pre}}
, clinical context
h^{\text{clin}}
, temporal context
\gamma(\Delta t)
and the drug combo
h^{\text{drug}}

[z_{\text{pre}},\,h^{\text{clin}},\,\gamma(\Delta t),\,h^{\text{drug}}]
.
Through this design, the model learns to simulate disease evolution trajectories that are sensitive to both molecular context and temporal dynamics, aligning with the deterministic and causal objectives of a medical world model.

Post-Treatment Latent Predictor.

After concatenating to obtain the integrated representation, we feed it into a multi-layer Transformer to predict the post-treatment latent
\hat{z}_{\text{post}}
and risk score
\hat{r}_{p}
. This process is formulated as:

\hat{z}_{\text{post}}=\mathrm{SelfAttn}([z_{\text{pre}},\,h^{\text{clin}},\,\gamma(\Delta t),\,h^{\text{drug}}])^{N}+z_{\mathrm{pre}},

(4)

where
\mathrm{SelfAttn}(\cdot)^{N}
is a
N

[z_{\text{pre}},h^{\text{clin}},\gamma(\Delta t),h^{\text{drug}}]

Self-attention enables the model to capture high-order dependencies among patient state, clinical background, and therapeutic factors, producing a temporally and physiologically consistent post-treatment latent prediction
\hat{z}_{\text{post}}

The Post-Treatment Latent Predictor is trained via a combination of latent reconstruction loss and a soft label-based contrastive loss, controlled by a coefficient
\lambda_{1}
as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}=\lambda_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{latent}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{con}}.

(5)

Specifically, the latent reconstruction loss is computed between the predicted and ground-truth latent states as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{latent}}=\|\,\hat{z}_{\text{post}}-z_{\text{post}}\,\|_{\ell_{1}},

(6)

where
\ell_{1}
represents the L1-loss. Additionally, a contrastive loss is used to structure the latent space, ensuring that predicted states generated from similar treatments remain close. However, treatments are not simply ‘
same
’ (hard label 1) or ‘
different
’ (hard label 0); they possess nuanced similarities. We therefore utilize a soft label-based contrastive loss, where the soft label for any two treatment actions
a_{i}
and
a_{j}

33

\hat{z}_{\text{post},i}
and
\hat{z}_{\text{post},j}

(i,j)
in a batch:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{con}}=-\sum_{i}^{B}\sum_{j}^{B}\left(p_{ij}\log(q_{ij})+q_{ij}\log(p_{ij})\right),

(7)

where

p_{ij}
and
q_{ij}
are the softmax-normalized similarity scores:

\displaystyle p_{ij}=\frac{\exp(\text{sim}(u_{i},u_{j})/\tau_{1})}{\sum^{B}_{k\neq i}\exp(\text{sim}(u_{i},u_{k})/\tau_{1})},

(8)

\displaystyle q_{ij}=\frac{\exp(\text{sim}(\hat{z}_{\text{post},i},\hat{z}_{\text{post},j})/\tau_{2})}{\sum^{B}_{k\neq i}\exp(\text{sim}(\hat{z}_{\text{post},i},\hat{z}_{\text{post},k})/\tau_{2})},

Survival Predictor.

To link disease dynamics with patient outcomes, we introduce a Survival Predictor that jointly processes the pre- and post-treatment latents and predicts risk score and survival probability. To enhance the understanding of both pre-treatment latent and post-treatment latent, we adopt a Two-Way Cross-Attention mechanism, allowing information to flow bidirectionally between
z_{\text{pre}}
and
\hat{z}_{\text{post}}
. Then the aggregated feature is passed to a MLP-based predction head that outputs a one-year survival probability
\hat{p}_{1\text{y}}
and a continuous risk score
\hat{r}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{MLP}\big(

\displaystyle\text{ CrossAttn}(z_{\text{pre}},\,\hat{z}_{\text{post}})^{M}

\displaystyle+\text{CrossAttn}(\hat{z}_{\text{post}},\,z_{\text{pre}})^{M}\big),

The survival branch is supervised by a Brier loss

\mathcal{L}_{\text{surv}}=\lambda_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{Brier}}(\hat{p}_{1\text{y}},y)+\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cox}}(\hat{r},\,T,\,\delta),

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cox}}=-\sum_{i:\,\delta_{i}=1}\left(\hat{r}_{i}-\log\sum_{j:\,T_{j}\geq T_{i}}e^{\hat{r}_{j}}\right),

Together, these losses enforce both calibrated survival probability prediction

This design connects imaging changes with survival reasoning in one latent space. By modeling temporal and treatment effects on embeddings, CLARITY captures disease evolution more stably and clearly.

3.5
Inverse Survival Evaluation

To close the loop between prediction and decision-making, we add a training-free inference process that iteratively refines therapy choices using updated survival risk feedback(
Fig.

2

K

Step 1: Propose Therapies based on Feedback.

Given the pre-treatment MRI
x_{0}
, the clinical context
c_{p}
(
Section

3.3
), combined survival feedback (initially this is survival analysis results from Direct Survival Evaluation)
and the high-level objective

Section

3.2
), the Therapy Policy Agent employs a textual description of the patient’s condition and goal, and outputs
a
refined action set

\mathcal{A}^{(k)}

description for the Actor to evaluate.

Step 2: Refine with Dose and Schedule Variation.

For each therapy candidate
a\in\mathcal{A}^{(k)}
,
we introduce minor, medically grounded variations to its parameters
(e.g., drug type, dosage level, cycle count, and interval schedule).
Each perturbed version
\tilde{a}
slightly modifies the original configuration, expanding the exploration space while maintaining realism. If any resulting combination violates the medical safety constraints
\Omega

(
e.g
.
exceeding the maximum cumulative dose or pairing incompatible drugs),
it is directly discarded.
This rule-based filtering ensures that all retained therapy variants remain compliant with oncology protocols,
while still broadening the search space through medically grounded diversity.

Steps 3 & 4: Score Survival and Update Feedback.

After refinement guideline-based constraints, each candidate therapy is evaluated by the Actor module one-by-one again, predicting its post-treatment latent representation and estimates the corresponding survival risk score. These results of risk score are then be integrated into the current accumulated survival feedback, yielding
\psi^{(k+1)}
.
This updated context provides survival-grounded evidence
for
\pi_{\text{MLLM}}
to refine subsequent therapy generation in iteration
k+1

Step 5: Select Final Therapy.

After
K

a^{\star}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{all}}}\hat{r}(a),\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{A}_{\text{all}}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{A}^{(k)},

(14)

where
\hat{r}(a)
represents

the risk score
\hat{r}
for drug combo

4.1
Implementations

For training and evaluation, we leverage two longitudinal brain tumor cohorts that
provide both temporal imaging trajectories and treatment information,
which are essential for modeling therapy-conditioned disease evolution in a
world-model framework. The
MU-Glioma-Post

32
cohort contains 203 patients and
654 MRI follow-ups with rich treatment logs and genomic annotations. We split the
dataset in training and validation at the patient level (4:1) to prevent temporal leakage across pairs.
For external validation, we use the
UCSF-ALPTDG

14
cohort, which includes 298
patients with longitudinal MRI sequences and corresponding survival outcomes.
Additional details are discussed in the Supplementary
.

4.2
Results on Treatment Exploration

As shown in
Table

1
, Our CLARITY framework achieves state-of-the-art results, significantly outperforming all baselines on both datasets. On the MU-Glioma-Post benchmark, our approach achieves an F1-score of 55.6%, a substantial 9.2% absolute improvement over the strongest medical-specific baseline, Huatuo-Vision (46.4%). The trend continues on UCSF-ALPTDG, where our model achieves 48.9% F1, again outperforming the second-best method (44.1%). This highlights a key finding: while general-purpose models like GPT-4o and Claude-4.5 perform poorly when prompted directly (42.1% and 41.6% F1, respectively), our framework successfully leverages the MLLM as part of a robust simulation-to-decision loop, dramatically enhancing its effective performance.

Conclusion

We introduced CLARITY, a medical world model addressing critical limitations in prior works. By forecasting disease evolution in a
latent space
, we avoid the stochasticity and high computational cost of diffusion-based models. CLARITY is the first to explicitly integrate
temporal and clinical contexts
with an
Inverse Survival Evaluation
. Experiment proves that our feedback loop is critical for optimized decisions, with results outperforming all baselines. Therefore, CLARITY represents a significant step towards computationally feasible and personalized treatment planning in oncology.

6
Additional Implementation Details

CLARITY consists of four major components: an MRI encoder, a Therapy Policies Agent, a text encoder, and an Actor module. For MRI encoder, we adopt the foundation model BrainIAC
37
, which follows a 12-layers ViT-B backbone with
d=d_{c}=d_{t}=768
. For Therapy Policies Agent, we employ GPT4o
20
with a specially designed prompt. For Text Encoder, we use the MedGemma
33
, which is fine-tuned via 4-bit quantization and Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA,
18
). For the Actor module, we apply a
N=4
layers Transformer as its Latent Predictor while a
M=4
layers Transformer with two-way cross attention as its Survival Predictor.

For the training strategy, we combine an L1 latent reconstruction loss, Cox survival
loss, Brier score loss, and a contrastive consistency loss, with empirically chosen
coefficients
\lambda_{1}=5
and
\lambda_{2}=1
.

,
\Omega

10
Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations.

•

Generalizability.
Our model is trained on two specific glioma cohorts. Its performance on new clinical centers, imaging protocols, or patient demographics is not yet validated.

•

Domain Specificity.
The framework is specialized for brain MRI and glioma modeling. Adapting it to other cancer types or imaging modalities (e.g., CT) would require substantial re-training.

•

Therapy Agent Constraints.
The policy agent (
\pi_{\text{LLM}}
) is constrained by a predefined set of medical rules (
\Omega
). An incomplete
\Omega
could lead to sub-optimal or invalid therapy proposals.

•

Computational Cost.
The iterative Inverse Survival Evaluation (
K=3
), while far more efficient than diffusion, is computationally more expensive than a single forward-pass, which may affect real-time deployment.
