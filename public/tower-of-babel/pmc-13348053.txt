Successful treatment of generalized lichen planus with deucravacitinib monotherapy
Authors: Danny Zakria, Gabriela Soto-Canetti, Alexandra K. Golant, Jordan Talia
Published: 2026

Introduction

Lichen planus (LP) is a chronic inflammatory dermatosis that typically presents as intensely pruritic, flat-topped, violaceous papules of varying sizes, typically localized to the extremities.1 In rare cases, it can become generalized to the entire body, causing significant morbidity.1 There are currently no approved therapies for generalized LP, and commonly used off-label medications such as oral corticosteroids and steroid-sparing immunosuppressants can have significant adverse effects. Herein, we report the successful treatment of generalized LP with deucravacitinib, a selective tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) inhibitor.

Case report

A 46-year-old woman presented to the clinic with diffuse, pruritic, pink papules on the trunk and bilateral upper and lower extremities. Over the course of the prior months, the eruption spread to involve 80% of the patient’s body surface area. The patient denied any new exposures, viral illnesses, or medications prior to the onset of the rash. The patient had failed topical steroids. Morphologically, the patient’s rash demonstrated pink, flat-topped papules diffusely covering the trunk and extremities, coalescing into plaques (Fig 1, A-C). Our patient expressed concern regarding the adverse effects of classically used medications such as systemic steroids. Following initiation of deucravacitinib 6 mg daily, the patient had remarkable improvement over the course of 3 months, with near-complete resolution of cutaneous lesions, residual hyperpigmentation, significant improvement in pruritus, and development of 3 new lesions on the elbows. (Fig 3, A-C). To date, the patient continues to be well-controlled with few new lesions developing. New lesions have been treated with various topical agents including tapinarof 1% cream, mometasone 0.1% ointment, and clobetasol 0.05% ointment. Discussion

In cases of diffuse cutaneous LP, the disease may cause significant quality-of-life impairment, in part due to pruritus. Historically, systemic treatment options for LP have included oral corticosteroids, acitretin, methotrexate, azathioprine, and cyclosporine.1

Deucravacitinib, a selective TYK2 inhibitor, recently demonstrated notable efficacy in treating localized hypertrophic LP.2 Another case of generalized LP significantly improved with concomitant deucravacitinib 6 mg daily and topical ruxolitinib 1.5% cream twice daily.3 Through TYK2 blockade, deucravacitinib inhibits type 1 interferons, including interferon-alfa and interferon-beta, which have been found at elevated levels in cutaneous LP.4,5 Notably, interferon regulatory factor 5, which is known to stimulate interferon-alfa and interferon-beta, was also found to be upregulated in LP, further supporting the rationale for treatment with deucravacitinib.6 TYK2 inhibition may also help modulate LP-related inflammation via the downregulation of interleukin (IL)-18, IL-22, and IL-23,7 type II interferon-associated cytokines that have also been implicated in LP pathogenesis, with IL-22 found to be elevated in cutaneous LP samples8 and IL-18 and IL-23 both demonstrating elevated levels in serum samples.9,10

Our patient demonstrated a remarkable reduction in both itch and disease burden within 3 months of deucravacitinib monotherapy. Janus Kinase (JAK) inhibitors have become a new option for the treatment of LP, with one review identifying several cases where complete resolution was obtained with tofacitinib, baricitinib, ruxolitinib, or upadacitinib.11 Notably, our patient’s new lesions were well-controlled with adjunctive topical delgocitinib. Deucravacitinib may be preferred for some patients over JAK inhibitors and steroid-sparing immunosuppressants because of its safety profile. Unlike JAK inhibitors, it does not carry a boxed warning for major adverse cardiovascular events or venous thromboembolism, which was based on observed associations in high-risk populations receiving tofacitinib, a pan-JAK inhibitor. Although TYK2 is a member of the JAK family of kinases, its mechanism differs from the JAKs, without activation of the other 3 kinases, and thus has a more favorable safety profile. Our patient has experienced no adverse effects after more than 12 months of treatment and is tolerating the medication well. Additional studies are needed to further explore the long-term efficacy and safety of deucravacitinib in the treatment of LP.
