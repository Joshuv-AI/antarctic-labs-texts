FCDM : A Physics-Guided Bidirectional F requency Aware C onvolution and D iffusion-Based M odel for Sinogram Inpainting
Jiaze E, Srutarshi Banerjee, Tekin Bicer, Guannan Wang, Yanfu Zhang, Bin Ren

Abstract
Computed tomography (CT) is widely used in industrial and medical imaging, but sparse-view scanning reduces radiation exposure at the cost of incomplete sinograms and challenging reconstruction. Existing RGB-based inpainting models struggle with severe feature entanglement, while sinogram-specific methods often lack explicit physics constraints. We propose FCDM , a physics-guided, frequency-aware sinogram inpainting framework. It integrates bidirectional frequency-domain convolutions to disentangle overlapping features while enforcing total absorption and frequency-domain consistency via a physics-informed loss. To enhance diffusion-based restoration, we introduce a Fourier-enhanced mask embedding to encode angular dependencies and a frequency-adaptive noise scheduling strategy that incorporates a soft row-wise absorption constraint to maintain physical realism. Experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets show that FCDM outperforms existing methods, achieving SSIM over 0.95 and PSNR above 30 dB, with up to 33% and 29% improvements over baselines.

Introduction

X-ray computed tomography (XCT) is a vital 3D imaging technique widely used in synchrotron facilities for high-resolution internal structure analysis. For instance, the nano-CT beamlines at the Advanced Photon Source (APS) at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) offer cutting-edge imaging capabilities, achieving sub-20 nm spatial resolution

One key limitation of conventional spatial-domain inpainting models
[

,
18
,

,

is their inability to handle feature entanglement
[

]
in sinograms. Unlike RGB images, where each pixel corresponds to a localized structure, sinogram pixels encode accumulated attenuation values along X-ray projection paths, leading to feature entanglement. Consequently, missing sinogram regions introduce ambiguities that propagate through reconstruction, making spatial-based inpainting ineffective. This issue is further compounded by the unique spectral characteristics of sinograms. While RGB images have localized frequency components, sinograms exhibit structured spectral distributions due to the Radon transform
[
31
]
, encoding long-range dependencies across projection angles (
Fig.

1
). These combined factors make traditional spatial inpainting methods ineffective, underscoring the necessity of frequency-domain processing. To address this, we introduce bidirectional frequency-domain convolutions (BFDC), which independently process frequency components along both the projection angle and detector dimensions. Unlike purely spatial convolutions, which struggle with mixed projection components, and purely frequency-based approaches, which lose spatial locality, BFDC combines the advantages of both domains. It processes spatial features to retain local structure and also applies spectral decomposition to disentangle overlapping features, enabling more effective inpainting. Instead of applying convolutions in a single dimension or fully in 2D, our bidirectional approach independently extracts features along projection angles and detector positions, balancing global and local dependencies. By assigning different weights along each dimension, BFDC dynamically preserves global correlations while ensuring local feature extractions, leading to more accurate sinogram restoration.

Another key limitation of existing methods is their failure to enforce physical consistency, particularly total absorption conservation
[
17
]
—–a fundamental X-ray imaging principle requiring the sum of attenuation values along a projection to remain stable. Most deep learning-based inpainting models treat sinograms as generic images, optimizing for pixel fidelity while neglecting physical constraints. To address this, we introduce a physics-guided loss function that preserves total absorption consistency and frequency-domain stability, ensuring that restored sinograms adhere to physical principles and further reduce artifacts.

Further, standard diffusion models use binary 0/1 masks, which are effective for RGB images where missing pixels correspond to localized features. However, in sinograms, missing regions are correlated across angles, as the same structure appears at different locations. A standard 0/1 mask does not indicate the projection angle, making it challenging for the model to infer how to restore structures. By incorporating Fourier-enhanced mask embedding (FEME), our approach enables the model to distinguish missing regions based on their projection angles, allowing it to leverage observed structures from other angles for more consistent and physically plausible restoration.

Finally, standard diffusion models apply uniform Gaussian noise across all frequency components, ignoring that recovery prioritizes global structures before fine details. To align with this process, we introduce frequency-adaptive noise scheduling (FANS), applying stronger noise to high-frequency components early on to disrupt fine details first, while later steps focus on low-frequency noise to preserve structural stability. Additionally, instead of enforcing strict absorption consistency, we introduce a soft constraint to prevent excessive deviations in total absorption. This avoids the model wasting capacity on correcting non-physical intensity shifts, allowing it to focus on meaningful sinogram inpainting. By permitting controlled absorption variations, our approach ensures both physics alignment and generalization to real-world scanning variations.

In summary, our key contributions are:

•

We improve the robustness of diffusion-based inpainting by incorporating Fourier-enhanced mask embedding and frequency-adaptive noise scheduling, enabling better generalization across varying sinogram corruption patterns.

•

3
FCDM Design and Analysis

Figure 2
:
FCDM Overview: In stage 1, the enc-dec network is trained to generate a latent representation capturing both spatial and frequency features. In stage 2, with fixed parameters from stage 1, random masking information is embedded.

FCDM
is a two-stage process model that first employs an encoder-decoder network to capture both spatial and frequency domain features, followed by a diffusion-based process for inpainting using mask information.
Fig.

2
shows a brief overview. In stage 1, the encoder encodes the input sinogram into a latent representation, which is then enhanced using BFDC to extract structural features across projection angles and detector positions. This enriched latent representation is subsequently decoded to recover the sinogram. Once this stage is trained, the encoder-decoder network’s parameters are fixed, ensuring stable feature extraction during the next phase. In stage 2, a diffusion-based model is trained on the latent space with random masks applied to simulate real-world missing data conditions. To guide the inpainting process, we incorporate FEME, allowing the model to better distinguish missing regions based on their projection geometry. Additionally, we introduce FANS, where early diffusion steps apply more noise to high-frequency components, while later steps increase noise injection into low-frequency components. This aligns the noise injection process with the natural denoising sequence, improving inpainting stability.

3.1
Bidirectional Frequency-Domain Convolutions

To enhance feature extraction in the latent space, we introduce BFDC. Given a sinogram input
s\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
, the encoder
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)
maps it to a latent representation
h\in\mathbb{R}^{C^{\prime}\times H^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}}
:

h=\mathcal{E}(s).

(1)

Next, both spatial and frequency-domain convolutions are applied to refine feature extraction. First, a standard spatial convolution
\mathcal{G}(\cdot)
is performed:

h_{s}=\mathcal{G}(h),

(2)

where
\mathcal{G}(\cdot)
represents a spatial convolutional operator. We also perform frequency-domain convolutions to complement spatial feature extraction. We apply a real Fourier transform (RFFT)
[
6
]
along both the width
W\prime
and height
H\prime
dimensions, converting the latent representation into the frequency domain:

\hat{h}_{w}=\mathcal{F}_{w}(h),\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{h}_{h}=\mathcal{F}_{h}(h).

(3)

Both transformations produce complex-valued tensors, which are decomposed into their real and imaginary components before convolution. The frequency-domain convolution is then performed using Hadamard multiplication, computationally more efficient than spatial convolution:

\hat{y}_{w}=\mathcal{K}_{w}\cdot\hat{h}_{w},\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{y}_{h}=\mathcal{K}_{h}\cdot\hat{h}_{h}.

(4)

where
\mathcal{K}_{w}
and
\mathcal{K}_{h}
are Fourier-transformed convolution kernels. The processed representations are then mapped back to the spatial domain using an inverse RFFT:

y_{w}=\mathcal{F}_{w}^{-1}(\hat{y}_{w}),\hskip 10.00002pty_{h}=\mathcal{F}_{h}^{-1}(\hat{y}_{h}).

(5)

Finally, spatial and frequency-domain features are fused, ensuring that both spatial and frequential characteristics are preserved:

y=\alpha_{s}h_{s}+\alpha_{w}y_{w}+\alpha_{h}y_{h}.

(6)

The weighting factors
\alpha_{s}
,
\alpha_{w}
and
\alpha_{h}
ensure that spatial-domain information and frequency-domain information along both projection angles and detector positions are optimally integrated, allowing the model to effectively disentangle overlapping features while maintaining projection consistency. The decoder
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
then reconstructs the inpainted sinogram:

\overline{s}=\mathcal{D}(y).

(7)

3.2
Physics-Guided Loss Functions

To ensure physically consistent inpainting, we introduce two loss functions: total projection consistency loss and frequency domain consistency loss.

3.2.1
Total Projection Consistency Loss

In CT, the total X-ray absorption should be preserved across all projection angles. This ensures that the reconstructed sinogram does not introduce non-physical intensity shifts, which could propagate into CT reconstruction. To enforce this, we define the total absorption constraint:

\sum_{\theta}\sum_{s}P_{\theta}(s)=\int_{\Omega}\mu(x,y)\,dx\,dy,

(8)

where
P_{\theta}(s)
represents the projection data at angle
\theta
and detector position
s
, while
\mu(x,y)
denotes the object’s linear attenuation coefficient. We incorporate this constraint into the loss function as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{absorp}}=\left(\sum_{\theta}\sum_{s}P_{\theta}(s)-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{R}^{-1}\{P_{\theta}(s)\}\,dx\,dy\right)^{2}.

(9)

where
\mathcal{R}^{-1}
represents the inverse Radon transform used to reconstruct the attenuation map. This loss encourages the model to maintain global absorption consistency in the inpainted sinogram.

3.2.2
Frequency Domain Consistency Loss

Since sinogram information is naturally structured in the frequency domain, ensuring spectral consistency between inpainted and ground-truth sinograms is essential. We define this constraint by minimizing the L2 norm of the frequency difference between the reconstructed and ground-truth sinograms:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{freq}}=\sum_{u}\sum_{v}\left\{\mathcal{F}(\overline{\mu}(x,y))-\mathcal{F}({\mu}(x,y))\right\}^{2}.

(10)

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the 2D Fourier transform. This loss ensures that the model accurately captures the structural characteristics of sinograms in the frequency domain, preventing spectral artifacts.

3.2.3
Overall Loss

The final loss for stage 1 training combines pixel-wise reconstruction, physics-guided constraints, and adversarial learning:

\mathcal{L}_{1}=\lambda_{\text{pixel}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{pixel}}+\lambda_{\text{adv}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}+\lambda_{\text{absorp}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{absorp}}+\lambda_{\text{freq}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{freq}}.

(11)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pixel}}
ensures pixel-wise reconstruction accuracy, and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}
is the adversarial loss. In stage 2, the diffusion model optimizes a mean squared error (MSE) loss.

3.3
Fourier-Enhanced Mask Embedding

Standard diffusion-based inpainting methods use binary 0/1 masks to indicate missing regions, which is sufficient for natural images but inadequate for sinograms. Unlike RGB images, where missing pixels can be inferred based on local spatial continuity, sinograms exhibit global feature entanglement due to the projection process. The same physical structure can appear at different positions across projection angles, yet a simple binary mask provides no information about these angular relationships. As a result, the model may treat missing regions as independent spatial holes, failing to recognize that certain missing areas correspond to the same structure observed at different angles.

To address this, we introduce FEME, where the mask is augmented with projection angle information using Fourier encoding. Given a projection angle
\theta
, we compute its Fourier representation as

\Phi(\theta)=\left[\sin(2\pi f_{1}\theta),\cos(2\pi f_{1}\theta),\dots,\cos(2\pi f_{N}\theta)\right],

(12)

where
f_{i}
represents a set of predefined frequencies. The enhanced mask representation is then obtained via element-wise Hadamard multiplication:

M^{\prime}(\theta)=M(\theta)\odot[1;\Phi(\theta)].

(13)

This transformation ensures that each missing region retains its original binary mask values while incorporating angular dependencies, making the mask projection-aware. To integrate this enhanced mask representation into the diffusion model, we employ an attention-based mechanism, where the encoded mask features interact with the latent space via key-query-value attention. By embedding this angular information, the model can better recognize correspondences between missing regions across different angles, leading to more coherent and physically consistent restorations in sinogram inpainting.

3.4
Frequency-Adaptive Noise Scheduling

In standard diffusion models, noise is applied uniformly across all frequency components, disregarding the varying importance of different spectral bands in sinogram reconstruction. To enhance the effectiveness of the denoising process, we introduce FANS that dynamically adjusts noise injection across different spectral regions over the course of diffusion.

The process begins by generating standard Gaussian noise
\epsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, followed by a two-dimensional Fourier transform to obtain the frequency representation of the noise,

\epsilon_{t}^{\text{freq}}=\mathcal{F}(\epsilon_{t}).

(14)

Instead of applying uniform noise, we modulate its intensity in the frequency domain by introducing a time-dependent frequency weighting function
m_{t}(u,v)
, which controls the distribution of noise across different frequency components. Specifically, the weighting function is formulated as

m_{t}(u,v)=1+\alpha\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right)\cdot\text{dist}(u,v)+\beta\frac{t}{T}\cdot(1-\text{dist}(u,v)),

(15)

where
\text{dist}(u,v)=\frac{\sqrt{u^{2}+v^{2}}}{d_{\max}}
represents the normalized distance of the frequency component from the lowest frequency, with

d_{\max}=\sqrt{(H/2)^{2}+(W/2)^{2}}

(16)

denoting the maximum frequency distance. The parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
control the relative enhancement of high-frequency and low-frequency noise, respectively, while
T
is the total number of diffusion steps and
t
denotes the current step.

The weighted noise is obtained by element-wise multiplication in the frequency domain,

\epsilon_{t}^{\text{weighted}}=m_{t}\cdot\epsilon_{t}^{\text{freq}},

(17)

followed by an inverse Fourier transform to recover the adjusted noise in the spatial domain,

\epsilon_{t}^{\text{adapted}}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\epsilon_{t}^{\text{weighted}}).

(18)

While this adaptive noise scheduling improves the quality of the denoising process, it does not inherently preserve the physical consistency of sinograms, particularly in terms of total absorption along projection paths. In standard diffusion models, the added noise may disrupt the total attenuation consistency, leading to inconsistencies that require the model to correct unrealistic variations rather than focus on meaningful reconstruction. To address this, we introduce a soft projection consistency constraint that prevents excessive deviation in total absorption without imposing a strict constraint that could hinder generalization.

To enforce this constraint, we first compute the row-wise sum of the adapted noise, given by

\text{noise\_sum}_{i}=\sum_{j}\epsilon_{t}^{\text{adapted}}[i,j],

(19)

where
i
indexes the sinogram rows. To mitigate large deviations, we adjust the noise using a row-wise normalization approach,

\epsilon_{t}^{\prime}=\epsilon_{t}^{\text{adapted}}-\frac{\text{noise\_sum}}{W}\cdot\mathbf{1},

(20)

where
W
is the width of the sinogram and
\mathbf{1}
represents a unit vector to ensure uniform correction across each row.

To avoid an overly rigid constraint, we introduce a soft weighting factor
\lambda
, allowing a controlled level of deviation:

\epsilon_{t}^{\text{final}}=\epsilon_{t}^{\text{adapted}}-\lambda\frac{\text{noise\_sum}}{W}\cdot\mathbf{1}.

(21)

When
\lambda=1
, the total absorption is fully preserved, while
\lambda=0
ignores consistency entirely. Selecting
0<\lambda<1
provides a balance, enabling the model to maintain reasonable physical properties while retaining flexibility in handling real-world variations.

Finally, the frequency-adaptive noise, now adjusted for projection consistency, is injected into the diffusion process following the standard formulation,

x_{t}=\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\epsilon_{t}^{\text{final}}.

(22)

[

, and frequency domain loss
{\alpha}_{freq}=0.1
. For FEME, we set the Fourier encoding frequencies as
f_{i}=i
with

. For FANS, we use

for high-frequency perturbation scaling and

, allowing limited variations in total absorption to improve generalization. During training, the mask ratio is randomly chosen within a range from

Metrics

We evaluate inpainting quality using Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM)
[
40
]
and Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR). SSIM quantifies structural preservation by measuring differences in structure, luminance, and contrast, while PSNR assesses reconstruction fidelity by comparing signal strength to noise levels. These metrics are chosen for their effectiveness in evaluating both structural accuracy and artifact reduction in sinogram restoration.

4.3
Ablation Studies

4.3.1
Impact of Bidirectional Frequency Domain Convolutions

We first examine the impact of BFDC by comparing several convolutional configurations. In addition to evaluating the performance of the full model with BFDC, we also test models that employ only spatial-domain convolutions, 2D frequency convolutions, and single-direction frequency convolutions along either the detector axis or the projection angles. Results show that removing frequency-domain convolutions leads to a performance drop, as purely spatial convolutions fail to disentangle overlapping sinogram features. While 2D frequency convolutions lack directional control and single-direction convolutions capture only partial spectral information, our bidirectional approach achieves the best balance between efficiency and feature preservation. We also compare peak memory allocation, runtime and FLOPs between
FCDM
incorporating frequency domain convolutions and without it, showing no significant difference (in Supplementary Materials).

Complexity Analysis of Different Convolution Locations

6.1
Downsampling Followed by Frequency-Domain Convolution

Downsampling reduces the input size from
H\times W
to
\frac{H}{r}\times\frac{W}{r}
, with complexity:

\mathcal{O}_{down}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{2CWH}{r^{2}}\right).

(23)

Half of the channels undergo standard convolution:

\mathcal{O}_{s}=\mathcal{O}\left(C^{2}k_{s}^{2}\frac{WH}{r^{2}}\right),

(24)

while the other half is processed via Fourier Transform, frequency convolution (Hadamard multiplication), and inverse Fourier Transform:

\mathcal{O}_{f}=\mathcal{O}\left(C\frac{WH}{r^{2}}\log{\frac{WH}{r^{2}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(C\frac{WH}{r^{2}}\right).

(25)

A restore convolution follows:

\mathcal{O}_{r}=\mathcal{O}\left(2C^{2}k_{r}^{2}\frac{WH}{r^{2}}\right).

(26)

Finally, the result is processed by an Encoder
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)
with complexity:

\mathcal{O}_{e}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{L}C_{i-1}C_{i}k_{i}^{2}\frac{H_{i}W_{i}}{r^{2}}\right).

(27)

6.2
Latent Space Frequency-Domain Convolution

The input is encoded into a latent representation of size
C_{L}\times H_{L}\times W_{L}
:

\mathcal{O}_{e}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{L}C_{i-1}C_{i}k_{i}^{2}H_{i}W_{i}\right).

(28)

Standard convolution is applied:

\mathcal{O}_{s}=\mathcal{O}(C_{L}^{2}k_{s}^{2}H_{L}W_{L}).

(29)

Frequency-domain convolution and inverse Fourier Transform follow:

\mathcal{O}_{f}=\mathcal{O}(C_{L}H_{L}W_{L}\log{H_{L}W_{L}})+\mathcal{O}(C_{L}H_{L}W_{L}).

(30)

A final channel restoration convolution is applied:

\mathcal{O}_{r}=\mathcal{O}(2C_{L}Ck_{r}^{2}H_{L}W_{L}).

(31)

7.2
Memory Usage Caused by Frequency Domain Convolution

We conduct an additional experiment to assess the impact of frequency-domain convolutions on computational efficiency by measuring peak memory allocation, inference time, and FLOPs. Results in
Table

6
indicate that incorporating frequency-domain convolutions introduces less than a 1% increase in memory usage and execution time, with no significant change in FLOPs.

This demonstrates that performing frequency-domain convolutions in the latent space minimizes overhead while preserving inpainting accuracy. Unlike applying convolutions on full-resolution images, this approach reduces computational complexity while effectively capturing structural information, making it feasible for large-scale sinogram inpainting.

7.3
Effect of Frequency-Domain Convolution Placement

We compare the placement of frequency-domain convolutions before and after the Encoder within the Encoder-Decoder component. Results in
Table

7
show that applying frequency convolutions after encoding, in the latent space, achieves better inpainting quality.

This improvement is likely due to the better abstraction and compression achieved by the Encoder, which filters out redundant noise before frequency processing. In contrast, applying frequency convolutions before encoding may introduce spectral distortions, reducing reconstruction accuracy. Processing in the latent space enables more effective feature extraction, improving structural consistency across projection angles.
