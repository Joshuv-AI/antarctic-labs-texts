Serum angiotensinogen as a biomarker for renal outcomes in patients with acute myocardial infarction: prognostic significance and clinical implications
Authors: Xiaoqin Zhang, Haoyong Zhang, Xi Liu, Chen Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsAcute kidney injury (AKI), also referred to as type 1 cardiorenal syndrome (CRS), is a frequent complication of acute myocardial infarction (AMI), with reported incidence rates in the literature ranging from 9.6% to 43.2%. Type 1 CRS has been shown to elevate the risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) in affected patients, although the precise incidence of CKD in this context remains uncertain. Prior research has indicated that certain biomarkers, including angiotensinogen and α-Klotho, may facilitate the early identification of type 1 CRS. However, whether these emerging biomarkers possess predictive value for long-term renal outcomes in patients with AMI remains to be elucidated.MethodsThis prospective observational study enrolled 149 patients with AMI from Shanghai Tongji Hospital between November 2018 and August 2019. Patients were categorized into two groups: those without acute kidney injury (non-AKI; n = 103) and those with acute kidney injury (AKI; n = 21). At admission, serum levels of angiotensinogen, α-Klotho, and fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF-23) were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The average follow-up duration was 36 months, during which the incidence of CKD among patients was monitored. The associations between the levels of these biomarkers and renal outcomes were subsequently analyzed.ResultsAmong the 149 patients, 21 (14.09%) were diagnosed with AKI, with an in-hospital mortality rate of 0.02%. Over the 3-year follow-up period, 22 patients (17.60%) developed CKD. Only serum angiotensinogen levels are associated with the occurrence of AKI and CKD. We conducted an assessment of the predictive utility of three clinical models for the occurrence of AKI and CKD. The AUC for AKI and CKD diagnosis are 0.811 (95% CI: 0.565–0.733, p < 0.001), 0.700 (95% CI: 0.579–0.821, p < 0.001), respectively. The optimal cutoff value of serum angiotensinogen for AKI and CKD is 1541.40 and 1332.88 pg/ml, respectively. The inclusion of age, serum creatinine (Scr) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in the predictive models resulted in enhanced predictive efficacy.ConclusionsIn conclusion, serum angiotensinogen levels emerge as a promising biomarker for predicting the development of AKI and CKD in patients with AMI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04573-7.Keywords: AMI, AKI, Biomarkers, CKD, Serum angiotensinogen

Introduction

Cardiorenal syndrome type 1(CRS1), characterized by acute cardiac dysfunction leading to acute kidney injury (AKI), represents a significant clinical challenge due to its high morbidity and mortality rates [1]. AKI is an important risk factor for chronic kidney disease (CKD), but the incidence of progression of AKI to CKD and end-stage kidney disease (ESRD) in CRS1 is unknown. The transition from AKI to CKD is a complex process that involves multiple biological pathways and risk factors, many of which remain to be fully elucidated. Identifying early biomarkers that can predict this progression is crucial for the development of interventions aimed at halting or slowing the deterioration of renal function.

Angiotensinogen, a circulating protein synthesized primarily in the liver, serves as the substrate for the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), which is pivotal in regulating blood pressure and fluid balance [2]. In AKI, RAAS activation increases angiotensin II levels in the kidney. Urinary angiotensinogen is a more stable marker than direct angiotensin II measurements and elevated levels predict AKI progression, especially in conditions like decompensated heart failure [3]. Sustained RAAS activation, indicated by high urinary angiotensinogen, may lead to continued kidney injury and progression to CKD [4]. In critical AKI cases, urinary angiotensinogen effectively predicts worsening AKI and the risk of renal replacement therapy or mortality.

Elevated angiotensinogen can also enhance RAAS activity, potentially increasing angiotensin II levels, blood pressure, and cardiovascular injury. The interaction between angiotensin II and aldosterone is implicated in hypertension and cardiovascular disease. In CRS1, where cardiac and renal dysfunction coexist, RAAS activation from reduced renal perfusion can worsen both conditions. Urinary angiotensinogen levels peak at admission, serving as a strong predictor for AKI in this context. Studies suggest it may be a valuable marker for acute cardiorenal syndrome [3, 5, 6], offering insights into kidney function and forecasting CKD progression. However, further research is needed to fully validate angiotensinogen’s role as a biomarker for AKI to CKD progression in CRS1 patients.

In addition to angiotensinogen, the roles of fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) and Klotho in the progression from AKI to CKD have garnered significant attention. FGF23 is a hormone produced by bone cells that regulates phosphorus and vitamin D metabolism. It binds to its coreceptor, Klotho, which helps maintain serum phosphorus levels within the normal range [7]. Elevated levels of FGF23 are independently associated with vascular calcification across various stages of CKD, contributing to the progression of cardiovascular disease.

Klotho, an endogenous anti-aging protein, serves as a coreceptor for FGF23 and plays a crucial role in regulating mineral metabolism [7]. Predominantly expressed in the kidneys, Klotho acts as a cofactor for fibroblast growth factor receptor 1, facilitating the phosphaturic effects of FGF23. Reduced expression of Klotho in CKD impairs phosphate reabsorption, leading to hyperphosphatemia [8]. Furthermore, the loss of Klotho exacerbates kidney injury by derepressing Wnt/β-catenin signaling, which is implicated in renal fibrosis and CKD progression [9].

Although Klotho is not found in myocardial tissue, it has a cardioprotective role, counteracting the harmful effects of elevated FGF23 on the heart, even in the absence of hyperphosphatemia. The elevated levels of FGF23 and decreased expression of Klotho are associated with renal dysfunction and heightened cardiovascular risk, making them potential early indicators of CRS1 [6, 10].

This study aims to investigate the utility of serum angiotensinogen, FGF23 and α-klotho in predicting AKI progression in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI). We conducted a prospective, single-center cohort study involving 149 hospitalized adults with AMI in the Cardiac Care Unit (CCU) to evaluate the utility of serum angiotensinogen and other injury biomarkers, measured at the time of diagnosis, for predicting AKI progression in CRS 1.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study included adult patients (≥ 18 years old) with AMI who were admitted to the CCU from November 2018 and August 2019 in Tongji Hospital in Shanghai, China. The exclusion criteria include: (1) patients who died within 48 h of admission; (2) cirrhosis; (3) active malignancy; (4) severe infection.

AMI refers to a group of conditions that include ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). The criteria for diagnosing AMI were based on the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction and were further confirmed by coronary angiography [11]. The diagnosis of AKI was made according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [12], which state that AKI is diagnosed if serum creatinine (Scr) increases by ≥ 0.3 mg/dL (≥ 26.5µmol/L) or if there is a 50% increase in serum creatinine from the baseline level within 48 h of hospitalization. Urine criteria (0.5 mL/kg per hour for 6 h) were not utilized for diagnosing AKI in this study due to the use of diuretics for some patients. The diagnosis of CRS1 was assigned to patients with AMI who developed AKI within 48 h [13].

All data were obtained from electronic records in hospital. All laboratory tests were performed in clinical laboratory of Tongji Hospital.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The statement confirms that all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. The study involving human participants was approved by the Ethical Committee of Tongji Hospital, and the Ethical review opinions number was KYSB-2017-116.

Procedures

Serum creatinine was measured on admission and at least twice times during the first three days. The estimated eGFR was estimated by the 2021 CKD-Epidemiology Collaboration Equation. Information about death status and date of death, as well as further CKD development, was obtained by reviewing hospital records.

Blood samples and biomarker level measurement

Blood samples were collected for the first day of hospitalization, subsequently centrifuged, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. The frozen serum samples were rapidly thawed and brought to room temperature, and serum α-Klotho, serum FGF23 and angiotensinogen concentrations were assessed using a commercial ELISA kit for human serum klotho, serum FGF-23 and serum angiotensinogen (Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Outcome definitions

The primary outcome was the development of AKI and subsequent death during hospitalization. The secondary outcome was AKI progression leading to CKD development within a three-year-follow-up.

Statistical analyses

For descriptive statistics, categorical variables were reported as frequencies (proportions), while continuous variables were expressed as medians (interquartile ranges, IQRs) or means ± standard deviations (SDs). Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t test or Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables were compared using χ2 test. All tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The ROC curve was used to calculate the area under the curve (AUC), and the cutoff value was chosen at the highest score of Youden’s index (J), where J = sensitivity + specificity − 1. Sensitivity and specificity were calculated.

The correlation between two normally distributed continuous variables was evaluated by Pearson’s correlation; otherwise, Spearman’s correlation was used. A binary univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association between CRS1 and other variables. Variables with p < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were further analyzed using multivariate logistic regression analysis to identify independent factors associated with AKI progression leading to CKD development within the three-year follow-up. All statistical calculations were performed using SPSS 21.0 software (SPSS Inc., USA).

Risk factors for the development of CKD in patients with AMI

To identify predictors of incident CKD after AMI, we constructed univariable and multivariable logistic-regression models. Univariable screening of admission variables yielded ten candidate factors: age, serum angiotensinogen, hsTnI, Hb, CRP, albumin, BUN, UA, Scr and eGFR (Table 4). Because BUN and UA exhibited variance-inflation factors > 10, and because eGFR and Scr are mathematically and physiologically coupled, we removed BUN, UA and Scr from the final model to minimise multicollinearity. Multivariable analysis then showed that only serum angiotensinogen and eGFR at admission were independently associated with post-discharge CKD (Table 4).

The value of serum angiotensinogen in diagnosing AKI or CKD in patients with AMI

We conducted an assessment of the predictive utility of three clinical models for the occurrence of AKI and CKD in patients with AMI. Model 1 was predicated on serum angiotensinogen levels alone, Model 2 incorporated both angiotensinogen and Scr levels, and Model 3 included age in addition to angiotensinogen and Scr levels. The diagnostic accuracy of these models was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The AUC for AKI diagnosis is 0.811 (95% CI: 0.565–0.733, p < 0.001), 0.935 (95% CI: 0.882–0.988, p < 0.001), and 0.955 (95% CI: 0.914–0.996, p < 0.001), respectively. ROC curves for CKD prediction using the same three models. The AUC values for these models are 0.700 (95% CI: 0.579–0.821, p = 0.003), 0.814 (95% CI: 0.701–0.928, p < 0.001), and 0.955 (95% CI: 0.921–0.990, p < 0.001), respectively. For AKI, the cutoff points are 1541.40 pg/ml for Model 1, and the sensitivity and specificity are 95.20% and 61.17%, respectively. For CKD, the cutoff point is 1332.88 pg/ml for Model 1, with a sensitivity of 95.50% and specificity of 55.30%. The AUC values for AKI and CKD across all models are significantly high (p < 0.001), the inclusion of age and admission Scr in the predictive models resulted in enhanced predictive efficacy, as shown in Fig. 3.

Discussion

Acute kidney injury is a frequent and severe complication in patients with acute myocardial infarction, occurring in approximately 14.09% of our cohort. This incidence aligns with previous studies [14], highlighting AKI as a significant contributor to morbidity and mortality in this patient population. AKI not only prolongs the hospital stay of patients with AMI and increases the mortality rate of these patients, but also raises the risk of chronic kidney disease and end-stage renal disease after discharge [15, 16]. The development of AKI in AMI patients is multifactorial, involving hemodynamic instability, inflammation, and potential nephrotoxicity from medications or contrast agents [17]. Our study underscores the importance of early identification and management of AKI to mitigate its impact on patient outcomes.

Several biomarkers have been to studied assess the severity of renal dysfunction and to accurately identify early risks of cardiorenal syndrome progression [18, 19]. Biomarkers such as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin [NGAL], fatty acid-binding proteins, G1 cell cycle arrest biomarkers, kidney injury molecule-1 [KIM-1] and IL-18 show high sensitivity for early AKI detection and provide prognostic information across multiple clinical settings [2, 20–27]. Urine angiotensinogen has recently been shown to be an early predictor for acute CRS and 1-year prognosis in patients with ADHF [5]. In our research, the assessment of serum biomarkers revealed that angiotensinogen levels were significantly higher in AKI patients compared to non-AKI patients, while no significant differences were observed for klotho and FGF23. This finding highlights the potential role of the RAAS in the pathogenesis of AKI [28]. Angiotensinogen, as a precursor of angiotensin II, is central to RAAS activation, which can contribute to renal vasoconstriction and ischemia, particularly in the setting of reduced renal perfusion [29–32]. Elevated angiotensinogen levels may reflect increased RAAS activity, predisposing patients to AKI. Further exploration of RAAS inhibitors as therapeutic agents in this context may be warranted.

Klotho and FGF23 are emerging biomarkers with potential roles in renal injury [33]. Klotho, an anti-aging protein primarily expressed in the kidneys, acts as a coreceptor for FGF23 and plays a crucial role in phosphate metabolism and renal protection [34]. FGF23 regulates phosphorus and vitamin D metabolism, and elevated levels are associated with renal dysfunction and cardiovascular risk [33]. In our study, no significant differences were observed in serum levels of klotho and FGF23 between AKI and non-AKI groups. This finding contrasts with previous studies that have shown associations between these biomarkers and renal outcomes in other contexts, such as chronic kidney disease and heart failure. The lack of significant differences in our study may be attributed to the relatively small sample size and single detection time point. In addition, the pathophysiology of AKI in AMI patients is complex and multifactorial. The lack of significant differences in klotho and FGF23 levels may reflect the heterogeneity of renal injury mechanisms, with some patients experiencing ischemic injury, while others may have tubular dysfunction or other forms of renal stress.

Our analysis identified several risk factors for AKI in AMI patients, including age, serum angiotensinogen levels, and admission Scr. These factors remained significant in multivariate logistic regression, indicating their independent predictive value. The association of age with AKI is consistent with the increased vulnerability of older individuals to renal insults due to age-related physiological changes and comorbidities. Similarly, elevated admission Scr and angiotensinogen levels likely reflect pre-existing renal dysfunction and heightened RAAS activity, respectively, both of which can exacerbate AKI risk.

For CKD development, serum angiotensinogen and eGFR at admission were significant predictors. The progression from AKI to CKD is a complex process influenced by multiple factors, including the severity and duration of AKI, underlying renal health, and systemic comorbidities. Elevated angiotensinogen levels may indicate sustained RAAS activation, which can contribute to ongoing renal injury and fibrosis, ultimately leading to CKD. The inclusion of eGFR in predictive models further underscores the importance of baseline renal function in determining long-term outcomes.

The evaluation of different clinical models for predicting AKI and CKD demonstrated that serum angiotensinogen levels alone (Model 1) provided significant predictive accuracy, with AUC values of 0.811 for AKI and 0.700 for CKD. The addition of Scr (Model 2) and age (Model 3) further enhanced model performance, achieving AUC values of 0.955 for both AKI and CKD predictions. These findings suggest that serum angiotensinogen is a robust biomarker for identifying patients at high risk of AKI and CKD development. Its integration into clinical decision-making algorithms could facilitate early intervention and improved management strategies.

Our study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment: 1) Sample Size and Generalizability: The relatively small sample size may have limited our ability to detect significant differences in klotho and FGF23 levels and even other baseline characteristics between the AKI and non-AKI groups. For example, in prior published research, the administration of contrast agents has been identified as one of the risk factors for AKI [35–37]. In our study population, we compared the doses of contrast agent used in patients who developed AKI versus those who did not.

The findings may not be generalizable to broader populations, and larger, multicenter studies are needed to validate our results. 2) Single-Time-Point Measurement: We measured biomarkers only at admission, which may not capture the dynamic changes in klotho, FGF23, and angiotensinogen levels over time. Serial measurements could provide more insights into their temporal relationship with renal injury. 3) Diagnostic Performance: Although serum angiotensinogen showed promise for predicting AKI and CKD in AMI patients, their high sensitivity but only moderate specificity suggests utility primarily for risk stratification rather than definitive diagnosis. 4) Absence of Urinary Biomarkers: Urinary indices of tubular damage or glomerular dysfunction were not assessed. Incorporating such markers could complement serum data and refine mechanistic insights. 5) Lack of Quantitative Albuminuria Data: Because MI presents acutely, 24-hour quantitative albuminuria measurements were unavailable; only qualitative dipstick results were recorded at baseline and follow-up. Future studies should integrate standardized urinary protein assessments to strengthen renal injury characterization.

Our study highlights the potential of serum angiotensinogen as a biomarker for early detection and risk stratification of AKI and CKD in AMI patients. Early identification of at-risk patients allows for timely interventions, such as optimization of hemodynamic support, careful monitoring of renal function, and judicious use of nephrotoxic agents. Future research should focus on validating these findings in larger, multicenter cohorts and exploring the mechanistic underpinnings of angiotensinogen’s role in renal injury. Additionally, studies investigating the therapeutic potential of RAAS inhibitors in preventing AKI and CKD progression in AMI patients are warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study underscores the clinical significance of serum angiotensinogen in predicting AKI and CKD development in AMI patients. Its incorporation into predictive models enhances diagnostic accuracy and may guide targeted interventions to improve patient outcomes. Further research is needed to fully elucidate the role of angiotensinogen in renal pathophysiology and to explore its therapeutic implications.
