Urban emissions and expansion intensify extreme precipitation and flood susceptibility in Texas
Authors: Giacomo Moraglia, Mohamed Aboelnour, Paola Crippa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Texas Triangle is rapidly expanding and increasingly vulnerable to extreme rainfall and floods. Using the Weather Research and Forecasting model coupled with chemistry (WRF-Chem), we simulate a cold-season frontal storm and a prolonged warm-season event, comparing a present-day configuration with an end-of-century urban-expansion scenario consistent with Shared Socioeconomic Pathway 5 projections. Across scenarios, meteorological boundary conditions are held fixed, while urban land cover is expanded and anthropogenic emissions are enhanced accordingly. In the cold-season case, urban growth enhances activation of fine-mode cloud condensation nuclei, strengthens updrafts, and produces statistically significant precipitation increases over San Antonio and Austin. In the warm-season case, the response is heterogeneous, with early enhancement on the western side of Dallas-Fort Worth followed by suppression eastward as the system evolves. Flood-risk analysis, based on the model-diagnosed runoff coefficient and a high-resolution stochastic framework (SCS-CN), reveals a systematic expansion of high-risk zones under future urbanization, amplifying existing runoff hotspots and generating new ones across metropolitan areas. Overall, the consistency between the WRF-Chem runoff diagnostics and the stochastic SCS-CN framework supports the use of WRF-Chem as a first-order, spatially explicit indicator of relative urban flood risk under extreme precipitation.

Introduction

Cities influence tropospheric circulation and have been shown to modify precipitation patterns both locally and in surrounding regions, although the magnitude of these effects varies depending on regional climate, topography, and other environmental factors1–4. This phenomenon, commonly referred to as “Urban Rainfall Effect” (URE5), arises from multiple interacting mechanisms6. A primary driver is the Urban Heat Island (UHI), which develops as natural vegetation is replaced by low-albedo surfaces that enhance solar energy absorption7,8. In combination with changes in surface roughness, UHI alters the exchange of heat and turbulent fluxes between the surface and the atmosphere, potentially enhancing low-level cloud formation and precipitation9–12. However, the influence of UHI on rainfall appears limited, or even negligible, when strong horizontal advection dominates, such as during synoptic-scale frontal passages13–16. By contrast, increased urban surface roughness associated with buildings can modify low-level flow and, in some cases, slow storm translation, increasing storm residence time over the city and enhancing rainfall accumulation and flood hazard17.

Another key component of the URE is the role of urban emissions. Primary and secondary aerosols from traffic, industry, and other anthropogenic sources act as cloud condensation nuclei (CCN), thereby altering cloud microphysics, life-cycle, and radiative balance18–20. In shallow clouds, elevated aerosol concentrations can suppress drizzle and extend cloud lifetimes (the Albrecht effect21), while in deep convective systems, the effects are more complex. High urban aerosol concentrations generally reduce droplet sizes and inhibit collision-coalescence, suppressing warm-rain formation22. However, under environments with strong convective instability, this delay can shift condensation and freezing processes to higher altitudes, modifying storm dynamics and, in some cases, enhancing mixed-phase or hail production23–27. It should be noted, however, that most evidence on the impact of urban aerosols on precipitation mainly relies on numerical simulations and theoretical frameworks, and its mechanistic understanding remains debated28.

The URE demands further investigation, especially given that ongoing and projected urban expansion poses critical questions about how future urbanization may alter precipitation patterns and associated hydrological risks29. Indeed, rapid urban growth has become a defining feature of the 21st century with suburban sprawl30–32, driven by persistent migration from rural to urban areas33. The expansion of cities inevitably increases the extent of impermeable surfaces such as roads, buildings, and pavements, which reduce infiltration and alter near-surface groundwater-surface water interactions, and heighten flood risk by placing additional stress on urban drainage systems34. In the U.S., this process is particularly evident in the Texas Triangle, the megaregion delineated by Dallas-Fort Worth (DFW), Houston, and San Antonio, with Austin positioned along its central corridor. This area contains some of the fastest-growing urban regions in the country, both at present and in long-term demographic projections35–37. Within this broader setting, DFW, San Antonio, and Austin are part of an area commonly referred to as Flash Flood Alley, where steep terrain, shallow soils, and intense precipitation can combine to generate extreme flash floods with substantial loss of life38,39. Rapid urban expansion and the associated increase in impervious surfaces make the Texas Triangle a particularly relevant testbed for investigating how future urban growth may reshape precipitation extremes, runoff generation, and flash flood risk in a highly populated region, while also addressing key gaps in our understanding of the mechanisms linking urbanization to hydro-climatic responses. Previous studies, however, report contrasting outcomes, with urban growth associated in some cases with an enhancement of the URE11,13,40–42, and in others with suppression43–45, underscoring the need for further targeted investigations46.

To address these gaps, we aim to: (1) quantify the impacts of future urban expansion on extreme precipitation events across the Texas Triangle under different seasonal conditions; (2) examine how future urban emissions may influence storm dynamics, rainfall distribution, and intensity; and (3) advance urban flood-risk assessment for future cities with a process-consistent framework that links fully coupled aerosol-cloud simulations with an event-scale hydrologic screening metric. To achieve these objectives, we adopt a numerical modeling approach that couples precipitation simulations of severe storm events with a stochastic flood-risk framework. We conduct paired simulations for each event under two land-use scenarios: a present-day “Control” configuration representing current urban extent, and a “future expansion” (Exp) configuration in which urban land cover is updated to reflect projected growth by the end of the century. In Exp, the newly urbanized areas are also assigned an urban emissions profile to represent the additional anthropogenic emissions associated with urban growth. Overall, our findings show that future urban expansion across the Texas Triangle can substantially perturb storm intensity, increasing precipitation in a cold-season frontal event over Austin and San Antonio, while producing a heterogeneous response during a warm-season event over DFW. However, the flood-risk analysis consistently indicates an expansion of high-runoff, high-risk areas under urban growth, highlighting a robust tendency toward greater flood hazard in future cities.

Results

Model evaluation

The analyzed events, described in detail in section 2, are simulated with the Weather Research and Forecasting model with Chemistry (WRF-Chem, v4.747,48). We initially perform an evaluation against a different set of observations. Specifically, the evaluation against NOAA’s Global Surface Summary of the Day (GSOD49) stations shows that WRF-Chem reasonably captures the spatial distribution and magnitude of accumulated precipitation for both analyzed events (Fig. 1a, b). For the cold-season event, the model slightly underestimates daily precipitation amounts with a domain average mean bias of −1.1 mm while reproducing wet grid cells with a hit-rate of 87%. The warm-season event exhibits a stronger negative bias (−4.2 mm) and higher root mean square error (RMSE, 6.6 mm), yet the hit-rate increases to 92.5%, indicating that the model better identifies the occurrence of rainfall events, albeit with a consistent underestimation of accumulated precipitation. Negative bias could potentially also be related to under-catch in the presence of strong horizontal winds, typical of synoptic-scale events such as those analyzed in this study, which are known to cause systematic underestimation of precipitation amounts50. An additional evaluation focused on the urban areas is reported in Table S1. During the cold-season event, urban-scale daily biases remain small (−1.3 to +2.4 mm) and RMSE values range from 2.6 to 3.3 mm, with consistently high hit-rate percentages (i.e., ≥90%). During the warm-season event, the performance remains city-dependent, but overall errors are still modest, with mean bias spanning from −2.9 mm in Austin to +2.4 mm in San Antonio and RMSE ranging from 2.1 to 4.5 mm, while hit-rate varies between 88% and 96%. Taken together, the errors remain modest relative to the storm precipitation magnitudes and intensity structure relevant for urban flood hazard, reinforcing the robustness of the subsequent hazard-based discussion. Furthermore, comparison with the Multi-RADAR Multi-Sensor system (MRMS51) reflectivity during the cold-season event shows that WRF-Chem reproduces the north-south-oriented squall line across central Texas, although simulated reflectivity values are generally weaker than observed (Fig. 1c, e). In the warm-season case, the model reproduces both the primary convective system peaking over the DFW and also the secondary squall line approaching the Austin-San Antonio corridor, in relatively good agreement with MRMS observations (Fig. 1d, f). Further evaluation of the chemical component of the model is performed through the comparison with aerosol observations from the Tracking Aerosol Convection Interactions Experiment (TRACER52,53) campaign in the Houston area on 23 August 2022 for the warm-season event. Time series of ammonium and chloride are reasonably reproduced in both magnitude and temporal variability, while nitrate is generally underestimated during the day and exhibits an evening increase not supported by the observation at the S3 site (Fig. S1a). Overall, the model evaluation indicates that WRF-Chem adequately represents the two storms’ physical and chemical features, supporting its suitability for our subsequent process-based analyses.

Impacts of urbanization on storm evolution

The cold-season event produced the largest precipitation amounts over western Texas. As shown in Fig. 2a, b, the cold front advanced rapidly across San Antonio and Austin (02:10–05:00 UTC), with precipitation intensities peaking at nearly 40 mm within just 10 min in both cities. In both cases, the Control and Exp runs exhibit a consistent eastward progression of convective cores, with reflectivity exceeding 45 dBZ during the peak phase of the event. In the Exp simulations, however, the fraction of grid cells above this threshold increases systematically by about 5% over both cities, suggesting an intensification of the convection. Although Austin is located downwind of San Antonio, the upwind corridor between the two cities shows no appreciable differences in precipitation or reflectivity prior to the frontal passage, supporting the interpretation that the two can be treated as independent for this case. In addition, Fig. 2c shows that spatial differences are heterogeneous, but decreases are rarely significant. Among grid cells with statistically significant differences, increases dominate, 78% in Austin and 84.7% in San Antonio, with decreases limited to 22% and 15.3%, respectively. Figure S2 shows a consistent set of statistically significant differences between the Control and Exp simulations for several in-cloud variables over Austin and San Antonio. Specifically, the Exp run exhibits systematically larger CCN concentrations of 0.15–0.62 μm (bin 2), with number concentrations significantly exceeding those of Control throughout the lower-mid-troposphere (Fig. S2a), approximately from near cloud base up to about 5–6 km. Specifically, bin 2 CCN concentrations in Exp are on average about 26% higher than in Control over Austin and about 38% higher over San Antonio across the 0–6 km layer. Figure S3 further indicates that the enhanced presence of CCN in bin 2 is primarily driven by a marked increase in ammonium sulfate concentrations. This enhanced activation is accompanied by greater latent heat release at the same levels due to condensation, which coincides with significantly larger in-cloud positive latent heat fluxes (LHF) for both cities (Fig. S2b), with typical increases on the order of a few tens of W m−2 in the 1–4 km layer, and larger shifts in the upper quantiles. These fluxes increase buoyancy and thereby sustain stronger updrafts (Fig. S2c), with the 0.9 quantile of w enhanced by about 2–4 m s−1 through much of the mid-troposphere, approximately 3–8 km. These results are consistent with a similar cold-season frontal case over DFW examined in our previous study15.

In the warm-season event, instead, the storm persisted over DFW for nearly 15 h, with several peaks exceeding 5 mm per 10 min (Fig. 3a). The prolonged rainfall produced substantial accumulation (Fig. 3b). Both scenarios simulate widespread precipitation, but they differ in timing and spatial extent. During the early hours (01:00–05:00 UTC), the Exp run generates stronger rainfall in the western side of DFW, whereas later in the day, the Control run sustains higher intensities in the eastern side (07:00–11:00 UTC). Indeed, relative difference maps for daily precipitation amounts (Fig. 3c) reveal a mixed response to urban expansion, with localized enhancements but an overall reduction under Exp compared to Control. By 05:00 UTC, Fig. 3c shows reduced local precipitation in the Exp scenario relative to Control on the eastern side of the metroplex. In the same regions, we notice decreased CCN activation of mixed composition again in bins 2–3, while the in-cloud mean profiles show a weaker convection, with overall lower LHF and weaker updrafts in Exp (Fig. S4). Decoupling land-use and emissions forcing

To better attribute the precipitation changes reported above, we complement the fully coupled WRF-Chem experiments with paired Physics-Only (PO) WRF simulations. These PO simulations preserve the land-use changes associated with urban expansion but exclude chemistry and aerosol interactions. Specifically, the “Control (PO)” versus “Exp (PO)” comparison isolates land-surface and dynamical impacts of urban expansion under identical atmospheric forcing, including changes in surface roughness and flux partitioning. In contrast, the original Exp versus Exp (PO) comparison isolates the incremental effects of emissions and aerosol-cloud interactions under identical land use. This first-order separation is particularly relevant for synoptically driven storms, where strong background advection can suppress UHI-related thermodynamic anomalies12,15,16, while roughness-driven dynamics can still influence low-level convergence and storm residence time17.

Figure 4 summarizes 10 min accumulated precipitation time series over the expanded urban mask for the cold-season event (Austin and San Antonio) and the warm-season event (DFW). Across all three metropolitan areas, the land-use-only perturbation does not yield statistically significant increases in precipitation relative to the Control (PO) baseline, with one-sided Wilcoxon tests indicating no significant intensification in Exp (PO) versus Control (PO) (p > 0.05). By contrast, enabling emissions and aerosol-cloud interactions produces a significant departure from the no-emissions baseline, with a clearer intensification of short-duration maxima and an upward shift in wet-cell precipitation relative to Exp (PO) (p < 0.05). Specifically, 05:00 UTC in Austin, where Exp reaches about 40 mm versus about 25 mm in Exp (PO), an increase of about 15 mm (+60%), at 03:00 UTC in San Antonio, where Exp reaches about 44 mm versus about 30 mm in Exp (PO), an increase of about 14 mm (+47%), and at 06:30 UTC in DFW, where Exp reaches about 28 mm versus about 18 mm in Exp (PO), corresponding to an increase of about 10 mm (+56%). Taken together, these paired experiments support the interpretation that, for the synoptically forced cases examined here, the precipitation intensification diagnosed under urban expansion is dominated by emissions mediated aerosol-cloud interactions, while the net land-surface forcing of expansion alone produces a weak response. This does not constitute a full factorial attribution, because land-surface and aerosol pathways can interact nonlinearly, and roughness-driven dynamical impacts may be stronger for locally forced or slower-moving storms. Runoff changes from urban expansion

In this section, we focus on the estimation of urban flood risk for the two simulated events. We note that the runoff coefficient-based metric provides an event-integrated measure of surface runoff susceptibility, rather than an explicit representation of urban pluvial or flash flooding54. The daily accumulation framework is therefore intended for comparative assessment of runoff-generating conditions under extreme precipitation and urban expansion. Accordingly, we compare results within each city and storm regime, focusing on relative changes in runoff susceptibility rather than absolute cross-city equivalence. For the cold-season case, San Antonio is identified as more severely affected than Austin55. Despite intense precipitation peaks, no major flooding was reported over Austin, and neither WRF-Chem nor the Soil Conservation Service Curve Number method (SCS-CN56–58) indicates high-risk areas there (not shown). In contrast, San Antonio exhibits elevated runoff propensity in both approaches, with WRF-Chem indicating near-saturated runoff potential and the SCS-CN method yielding η values just above the 0.65 high-risk threshold. Expressed first in terms of total high-risk area, the WRF-Chem diagnostic indicates an increase from 128 km2 in Control to 544 km2 in Exp. Using the SCS-CN method, the total high-risk area increases from 113.5 km2 in Control to 195.7 km2 in Exp. This increase reflects two contributions. On the common urban footprint (Control mask), the WRF-Chem high-risk coverage doubles from 128 km2 in Control to 256 km2 in Exp (+99.4%). In addition, within the newly urbanized area in Exp, 288 km2 exceeds the high-risk threshold. The SCS-CN method shows a more moderate increase over the common footprint, from 113.5 km2 to 133.4 km2 (+17.5%), and additionally identifies 62.3 km2 of the added urban area as high risk. Although the two approaches differ in magnitude due to their underlying formulations, both consistently delineate the northwestern part of the city as a zone of elevated surface runoff susceptibility under extreme precipitation (Fig. 5a, b). When urban expansion is accounted for, this northwestern hotspot persists, but the extent of the high-risk zone expands, and the corresponding η values increase slightly, reflecting enhanced surface impermeability rather than changes in storm forcing. For the warm-season storm over DFW, the agreement between the two approaches is stronger (Fig. 4c, d). The changes in the extent of high-risk areas within the Control mask between scenarios are relatively small: 1280 to 1344 km2 in WRF-Chem (+5.0%) and 1233.7 to 1264.1 km2 in SCS-CN (+2.5%). In the Exp scenario, however, newly urbanized areas add a substantial high-risk zone, with an additional 544 km2 (WRF-Chem) and 421.2 km2 (SCS-CN) classified as high risk. This consistency suggests that, despite the absence of explicit hydrological routing compared with WRF-Hydro60, WRF-Chem could potentially provide reliable first-order estimates of urban runoff-generating conditions associated with flood susceptibility under extreme precipitation.

Discussion

This study examines how projected urban expansion across the Texas Triangle may reshape extreme precipitation and flood hazards based on WRF-Chem simulations and a stochastic flood-risk framework. By contrasting present-day and a future-expansion scenario for two severe storms, we highlight how urbanization alters storm dynamics and hydrological outcomes. Specifically, in a cold-season event, a more extensive urban area and associated increased anthropogenic emissions systematically intensify convection over San Antonio and Austin. The Exp run displays greater activation of CCN within the accumulation mode, leading to higher LHF that sustains stronger updrafts, supports more vigorous convective cores and produces statistically significant precipitation increases. The warm-season storm shows a heterogeneous response over DFW, with early CCN-driven LHF enhancement increasing western precipitation, followed by weaker CCN activation, reduced buoyancy, and suppressed rainfall eastward relative to Control. This evolution likely reflects the longer storm residence time over the larger DFW metroplex, where the system persists for several hours and undergoes more complex development, whereas Austin and San Antonio interactions are shorter-lived. Notably, the intensification signal is attributable to aerosol perturbations rather than urban land-surface changes alone. Physics-only simulations that incorporate urban expansion without coupled anthropogenic emissions fail to produce statistically significant precipitation increases, isolating aerosol-cloud interactions as the dominant driver of convective enhancement. These findings are consistent with earlier theoretical22 and modeling studies15,24,25,27,61 that link enhanced activation of fine-mode aerosol to significant impacts on deep convection. Because our simulations do not explicitly resolve peak supersaturation at the smallest turbulent scales, the magnitude of aerosol-induced condensational invigoration is likely underestimated. High-resolution simulations that explicitly resolve supersaturation suggest stronger invigoration may occur27. Accordingly, the reported aerosol-driven storm intensity increases represent conservative lower-bound estimates of urban aerosol impacts.

Although the analyzed storms show high short-duration intensities typical of urban flash-floods, we interpret these extremes as drivers of surface runoff rather than explicit representation of pluvial flooding. By coupling storm-resolving simulations with probabilistic flood projections, this study provides an integrated framework to quantify how urbanization reshapes precipitation extremes and downstream flood risk. These results indicate that continued metropolitan growth is likely to increase hydro-meteorological vulnerability by modifying both rainfall patterns and the hydrological processes that convert precipitation into damaging floods, underscoring the need for resilient infrastructure and forward-looking adaptation in rapidly expanding regions. Both the WRF-Chem diagnostics and the stochastic SCS-CN framework identify expansion of high-runoff areas under urban growth, reflecting spatial contrasts in runoff susceptibility conditioned by local topography rather than homogeneous flood-generating behavior. This terrain sensitivity is particularly relevant along the Balcones Escarpment in Central Texas, where terrain-modified WRF experiments show that altering the escarpment produces modest yet meaningful shifts in precipitation maxima, affecting alignment between rainfall forcing and runoff-prone areas62. In San Antonio, existing high-risk zones intensify, while in DFW, eastern hotspots persist and western precipitation creates additional high-risk zones. The proposed coupled modeling framework is extendable to synthetic or climate-altered storm scenarios and provides a foundation for future attribution studies separating precipitation amplification from land-cover-driven infiltration changes.

We note that the flood-risk assessment captures relative amplification of runoff conditions, not explicit flood events, and is limited to two extreme storms, representing only a small subset of possible future scenarios. Urban environments differ substantially in geography, aerosol sources, and regional climate, each shaping aerosol-cloud-precipitation interactions in distinct ways1,2. Additional uncertainty arises from modeling choices, including parameterizations constrained by grid spacing28, reliance on a single microphysics scheme13, and simplified sectional aerosol treatments63, all of which may influence storm representation. Our future-expansion scenario assumes newly urbanized areas share the same per-area emission profile as present-day cities, although technological, regulatory, and societal changes will likely modify emission factors over time. Coarse inventories cannot resolve intra-urban variability at neighborhood or street-canyon scales and may under-represent emission heterogeneity within the urban canopy. Finally, the high computational cost of fully coupled aerosol-chemistry simulations restricts feasible ensemble size, even though finer grid spacing improves representation of aerosol processes64. Advancing confidence in projections of how urban growth and climate change jointly alter extreme precipitation and flood hazards will require continued integration of high-resolution modeling, diverse observations, and probabilistic flood-risk frameworks. Future research should extend this analysis across additional cities, seasons, and storm regimes while testing sensitivity to alternative microphysics and aerosol schemes. Field campaigns, such as the Coast-Urban-Rural Atmospheric Gradient Experiment (CoURAGE)65, offer critical constraints for model evaluation and process-level insight. Particular attention is needed in coastal cities, where land-sea contrasts and interactions between anthropogenic and biogenic aerosols may amplify rainfall perturbations66–68. Although UHI intensity may weaken in some regions as rural warming reduces temperature gradients69, greater uncertainty likely stems from evolving atmospheric chemistry. Shifts in oxidative capacity, NOx/VOC regimes, ammonia availability, and temperature-dependent biogenic emissions can alter new-particle formation and secondary organic aerosol production, thereby modulating CCN concentrations and cloud microphysics70–72. While our experiments isolate urban expansion effects, future work should examine combined urban growth and climate warming using pseudo-global warming or end-of-century ensemble nesting approaches.

Methods

Model configuration

In this study, we use the Weather Research and Forecasting model with Chemistry in a one-way nested, two-domain configuration with horizontal resolutions of 12 km and 4 km centered over Texas, US (Fig. S5). The land-use is based on the MODIS 500 m database, comprising 21 categories73. Each run includes a 72-h spin-up period with output saved every 10 min for both domains. The physics and chemistry configuration of our runs follows ref. 15, which showed good ability in capturing spatio-temporal features of similar events in the same region. Specifically, we employ the Morrison double-moment microphysics scheme with ice correction, dynamically coupled to aerosols74. We note that ref. 27 introduced an alternative implementation of the Morrison scheme with an explicit supersaturation treatment for a Houston deep convection case. Here we retain the standard formulation, which has already been tested in our previous WRF-Chem ensemble study over DFW, where urban aerosol perturbations produced an invigoration of updrafts and rainfall that was consistent in sign and order of magnitude with the response reported in their study15. Radiation is represented by the Rapid Radiative Transfer Model (RRTMG75), the planetary boundary layer by the Mellor-Yamada-Nakanishi-Niino scheme (MYNN76), and land-atmosphere exchanges by the NOAH Land Surface Model77. Sensitivity tests with both single-layer and multi-layer urban canopy models78,79 indicate negligible impacts on storm structure and precipitation at 4 km grid spacing for these strongly synoptically forced events, consistent with a previous study80. Urban canopy schemes are therefore not included to reduce computational cost and model complexity. The Grell-Dévényi cumulus scheme81 is used in the parent domain, while convection in the inner domain is explicitly resolved. Hourly meteorological boundaries are sourced from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts Reanalysis v5 (ERA582) and chemical boundaries from the Community Atmosphere Model with Chemistry every 6 h for a 2017 event (CAM-chem83) and from Whole Atmosphere Community Climate Model version 6 (WACCM84) for a 2022 event. We discretize the vertical dimension with 61 user-defined ETA levels (about 24 levels are in the lowest 2 km). We use chemical option 202, which includes aqueous-phase chemistry, full aerosol-radiation-cloud coupling, and a volatility basis set framework for secondary organic aerosol formation85. Specifically, gas-phase chemistry is described by the Model for Ozone and Related chemical Tracers (MOZART86), coupled with the Model for Simulating Aerosol Interactions and Chemistry (MOSAIC) for aerosol treatment, which includes four aerosol size bins (0.04–0.15 μm, 0.15–0.62 μm, 0.62–2.5 μm, 2.5–10 μm)87. Anthropogenic emissions are specified from the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) 2017 National Emissions Inventory (NEI), at hourly intervals88,89, biogenic emissions are described with the Model of Emissions of Gases and Aerosols from Nature (MEGAN90) and the natural aerosol sources activated include sea spray and dust.

We perform two sets of simulations to compare two urbanization scenarios: (1) present-day (Control) and (2) future expansion (Exp). The latter scenario follows ref. 91 and depicts the maximum projected urban extent within the Texas Triangle by the end of the 21st century (Fig. S6a), based on projections under the Shared Socioeconomic Pathway 5 (SSP592,93). SSP5 not only represents the high end of fossil fuel use, food demand, energy consumption, and greenhouse gas emissions, but also entails the largest projected increase in urban footprint across the contiguous United States and the study region. We adopt ref. 91 because its gridded urban expansion enables a reproducible implementation onto the WRF land-use categories used in this study. To help disentangle land-surface driven effects from aerosol influences, we also perform an additional pair of Physics-Only (PO) WRF simulations, similar to the sensitivity framework adopted in ref. 15. These PO runs use the same domain, meteorological forcing, and physical parameterizations as the WRF-Chem experiments, but with chemistry and aerosol-coupling disabled. We repeat both land use configurations within this physics-only framework, namely “Control (PO)” with present-day urban extent and “Exp (PO)” with the future expanded urban footprint, so that their difference isolates the land use, urban extent perturbation in the absence of chemistry.

For the newly defined urban grid cells, both the land-use index (LU_INDEX) and the land-use fraction (LANDUSEF) are modified to represent urban cover. The expansion primarily replaces “Grassland” and “Cropland,” which dominate the landscapes surrounding the major metropolitan areas of the Texas Triangle (Fig. S6a), thereby altering key surface characteristics such as albedo, roughness length, and soil properties11,13,16. In the newly urbanized cells, biogenic emissions are set to zero to account for vegetation loss, while anthropogenic emissions are assigned using a nearest-neighbor approach that replicates the full vertical profile of the geographically closest pre-existing urban cell. This ensures spatial continuity with the NEI inventory and produces emission patterns consistent with suburban sprawl in U.S. metropolitan areas. An example of the resulting NO2 emissions is shown in Fig. S6b. While several frameworks exist for estimating CO2 emissions from urban expansion (e.g., refs. 94–96), no standardized methods exist for short-lived species (e.g., NOx, SOx, CH4); their emissions are therefore also allocated using the nearest-neighbor approach described above.

Analyzed events, variables of interest and observations

We analyze two synoptically driven extreme precipitation events marked by intense hourly rainfall (≥10 mm h−1) across the Texas Triangle. The first, an easterly cold front on 20 February 2017, produced widespread heavy rain, with major flooding and tornado outbreaks around Austin and San Antonio55. It should be noted, however, that tornadoes are not resolved at our model resolution. The precipitation period over these two cities spans 02:10–05:00 UTC. The second event, on 22–23 August 2022, was triggered by a slow-moving cold front extending across central Texas, connected to a low-pressure system over far northern Texas. Moist southerly flow from the Gulf of Mexico converged along the boundary, producing prolonged rainfall and severe flooding over DFW on the first day59. Simulated precipitation persisted over the metroplex from 00:00 to 14:00 UTC on 22 August, before convection shifted southward along the front. As the storms progressed south, precipitation totals across the remaining part of the Texas Triangle decreased substantially.

Figure S7a shows the simulated skew-T over San Antonio on 20 February 2017, about 30 min before the frontal passage. The profile is typical of winter conditions, with a relatively cold and dry troposphere and a strong low-level jet evident in the wind profile. By contrast, Fig. S7b shows the sounding over Dallas-Fort Worth for 22 August 2022, characterized by a much warmer and more humid column and deeper moisture extending through the lower and mid-troposphere. Despite these thermodynamic differences, Convective Available Potential Energy (CAPE) values are of similar magnitude in both cases, exceeding 2000 J kg−1, indicating the presence of sufficient instability to support organized convection. The contrasting thermodynamic environments motivate our choice to divide the analysis into two cases, hereafter referred to as the cold-season (20 February 2017) and warm-season (22–23 August 2022) events.

To assess model performance, we compare daily precipitation from 212 Texas stations in NOAA’s Global Surface Summary of the Day against WRF-Chem simulations. Model skill is quantified using mean bias, root mean square error, and hit-rate, defined as the fraction of stations where both simulations and observations indicate wet conditions (≥1 mm day−1). Simulated composite reflectivity is compared with MRMS at peak rainfall times over the Texas Triangle, focusing on values ≥45 dBZ to delineate regions of deep convection97. For the chemical component, simulated concentrations of ammonium, chloride, nitrate, and sulfate are evaluated against observations from the S3 site of the TRACER campaign in Houston on 23 August 2022. In addition, CCN concentrations are validated against particle counts from the M1 site.

Aerosols and CCN loadings are analyzed through vertical integration of number concentration (column droplet, # m−2) and Liquid Water Path (LWP, kg m−2). In addition, we compute the activation rate, defined as the percentage ratio between CCN and aerosol column droplets for each grid cell at 10-min intervals. Flood-risk estimation

We estimate urban flood risk based on the daily runoff coefficient η index, defined for each grid cell (i, j) as in ref. 101:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\eta }_{i,j}=\frac{S{R}_{i,j}}{PRC{P}_{i,j}}$$\end{document}ηi,j=SRi,jPRCPi,j

Surface runoff (SRi,j, mm) in the NOAH land surface scheme is diagnosed as the fraction of precipitation (PRCPi,j) that exceeds canopy storage and soil infiltration capacity at each model timestep. This diagnostic approach, however, neglects lateral routing and channel processes, which have motivated the development of the WRF-Hydro framework to provide an advanced representation of hydrologic response60. Acknowledging potential limitations of this approach and as a sensitivity study, we also supplement our analysis with a stochastic flood-risk methodology that leverages Monte Carlo simulations of precipitation events102. In this study, we apply the stochastic formulation in an event-based manner to characterize uncertainty conditional on two representative extreme storms, rather than to represent a full stochastic flood-risk assessment. At each urban grid cell for both scenarios, we fit a Gamma distribution by estimating its shape and scale parameters from the WRF-simulated daily precipitation. Based on these fits, we generate 10,000 stochastic realizations of daily precipitation per grid cell across the study domain. Each simulation reproduces the statistical behavior of simulated rainfall while introducing variability between data, thereby allowing a probabilistic assessment of flood-inducing rainfall events103. This Monte Carlo framework propagates uncertainty from precipitation inputs into the surface runoff estimation process, offering a probability distribution of outcomes (e.g., runoff exceedance or flood occurrence) rather than a single deterministic estimate. Larger ensembles increase the accuracy of risk distributions, making this approach particularly suitable for evaluating low-probability, high-impact events104.

Subsequently, we estimate the SR of each Monte Carlo-simulated precipitation field using the Soil Conservation Service Curve Number method. The SCS-CN method accounts for land cover, soil properties, and antecedent moisture conditions to partition rainfall into infiltration and runoff. The CNi,j is derived from land-use and soil type, based on standard look-up tables (Table S2), and mapped across the urban masks of the two scenarios using the Soil Survey Geographic Database (SSURGO106). Finally, we assess flood risk based on η values following again105. Specifically, the high-risk areas within each city are identified for η ≥ 0.65 for both methods. Through the SCS-CN method, we limited the analysis to 200 m resolution, corresponding to the typical size of urban blocks, to maintain spatial consistency and reduce computational cost, although finer resolutions are possible. By incorporating this stochastic method, our analysis moves beyond the diagnostic limitations of our approach, providing a more robust and decision-relevant framework for urban flood-risk projections under present and future urban scenarios. Here, WRF-Chem provides the core physical framework for simulating precipitation and land-atmosphere interactions, while the SCS-CN method is used as a complementary runoff-based diagnostic to contextualize the hydrologic implications of the modeled precipitation.
