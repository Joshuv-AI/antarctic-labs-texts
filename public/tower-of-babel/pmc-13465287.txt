Barriers to compliance with hepatitis B vaccination: sociocultural and socioeconomic perspectives of pregnant women in the Bono East Region of Ghana
Authors: Dennis Bardoe, Robert Bagngmen Bio, Daniel Hayford, Denis Dekugmen Yar, Salma Ahmed Bardoe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-compliance with hepatitis B virus (HBV) vaccination among pregnant women poses a significant challenge to maternal health, particularly due to the risk of vertical transmission. Understanding the factors influencing non-compliance is essential for contextualising patterns of HBV vaccination uptake and the risk of infection. This study, therefore, investigated the sociocultural and socioeconomic barriers to compliance with HBV vaccination among pregnant women in the Bono East Region of Ghana.MethodsThis was a hospital-based cross-sectional study that employed the mixed method approach. A serological screening, a closed-ended questionnaire, in-depth interviews (IDIs), and focus group discussions (FGDs) were used to collect data from 1430 pregnant women. Quantitative data were analysed with the Software Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25 (IBM, USA). Logistic regression was performed to compute the odds ratios and identify the barriers that were significantly associated with HBV. Thematic analysis was used to analyse qualitative data, with a focus on four steps: transcription, profiling, coding, and thematic framework.ResultsThe prevalence of HBV infection among pregnant women was 1.8% (95% CI: 1.24–2.65). The majority of participants (74.34%) reported not taking the HBV vaccine or not completing the full vaccination regimen. Several sociocultural and socioeconomic factors were qualitatively reported as reasons for non-compliance. These included spiritual beliefs, fear of divine punishment, concerns about becoming a burden to family members, perceived cost of interventions, and time constraints. In the multivariate analysis, pregnant women who cited belief in spiritual poisoning as a reason for non-compliance had significantly higher odds of HBV infection (aOR = 8.79; 95% CI: 3.57–13.63; p < 0.001). Concern about being a burden to family members (aOR = 2.73; 95% CI: 1.03–7.21; p = 0.042) and perceived cost of intervention (aOR = 5.86; 95% CI: 1.13–9.26; p = 0.034) were also associated with increased odds of HBV infection.ConclusionThis study sheds light on the various sociocultural and socioeconomic barriers to compliance with HBV vaccination. This highlights the complexity involved in promoting essential interventions among pregnant women. Addressing these challenges requires collaborative efforts to promote responsiveness, improve healthcare accessibility, enhance health education, and strengthen community support systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09415-4.Keywords: Hepatitis B virus, Pregnancy, Barriers, Maternal health, Non-compliance, Vaccine hesitancy, Vaccination, Vertical transmission

Background

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a major public health concern, with an estimated global seroprevalence of 5.8% [1]. According to a recent report by the World Health Organisation (WHO), about 2 billion people are infected with HBV, and among them, an estimated 296 million are noted to be chronically infected [2]. The global distribution of this infection exhibited significant variations. In low-prevalence regions, such as the United States, Canada, and Western Europe, the prevalence rate is less than 2% [3]. Intermediate-prevalence regions, which include Mediterranean countries, Japan, Central Asia, the Middle East, and parts of South America, exhibit a prevalence rate between 2% and 7% [3]. Conversely, high-prevalence regions such as West Africa have prevalence rates exceeding 8% [3]. Pregnant women are a critical population for HBV prevention due to the risk of chronic infection and associated long-term health complications. These include preterm birth, pregnancy hypertensive disorders, gestational diabetes mellitus, low birth weight, asphyxia, intrauterine foetal death, and stillbirth [4]. To address these pregnancy-related complications, a series of large-scale interventions, including screening, HBV immunoglobulin administration, and vaccination, were implemented by WHO [5].

WHO has recognised the critical need for expanded immunisation coverage to combat the widespread prevalence of HBV and has, therefore, integrated HBV vaccination into routine immunisation programs worldwide [6]. This strategy is particularly vital in highly endemic areas where the risk of HBV transmission is highest [6]. The HBV vaccine regimen consists of three doses, administered at 0, 1, and 6 months. This vaccination schedule is crucial for inducing the development of adequate antibodies, which are essential for achieving seroprotection against the virus [7]. Moreover, additional doses, commonly referred to as boosters, have been shown to prompt a rapid increase in antibody levels, further enhancing protection [8]. Historically, the efficacy of the HBV vaccine was believed to provide active protection for a limited duration, typically five to seven years. This led to concerns regarding the long-term effectiveness of vaccination programs and the potential need for regular booster doses to maintain immunity [8]. However, recent studies have significantly shifted this understanding. It is now established that immunity conferred by the HBV vaccine could last for a decade or more following the completion of the vaccination course [8]. Moreover, current evidence suggests that the protection provided by the vaccine may be indefinite, negating the necessity for frequent booster doses in most individuals [8].

Despite the availability of these interventions and the proven efficacy of the HBV vaccine, challenges and several barriers remain in achieving optimal immunisation coverage, particularly in low-resource settings [9, 10]. This could be attributed to factors such as healthcare accessibility, vaccine availability, public awareness, and the complex interplay of sociocultural and socioeconomic barriers, especially in most sub-Saharan countries [11]. Sociocultural barriers may include cultural practices and social norms (such as beliefs, attitudes, and practices within communities) that tend to conflict with recommended guidelines [12]. For instance, in some cultures, there is a wide array of misconceptions regarding the causes of diseases. These misconceptions include the belief that diseases are a form of punishment from the gods or ancestors because of the violation of traditional principles [13]. Another misconception is that diseases are an act of witchcraft [14] or an outcome of spiritual poisoning. An earlier study found misconceptions regarding the aetiology of diseases to be deeply associated with non-compliance with interventions in Northern Ghana [15]. Moreover, socioeconomic factors such as unemployment, poverty, and healthcare costs could significantly hinder compliance with interventions [16]. Individuals facing economic hardships may prioritise immediate livelihood concerns over long-term health concerns [17]. This is particularly relevant for preventive measures that may require financial investments, such as serological screening, vaccination, and antiviral treatment [18]. For instance, previous to this current study, it was found that the high cost of laboratory tests was a major challenge affecting the fight against hepatitis B in low-income countries [15].

Non-compliance with HBV vaccination poses a significant public health concern, particularly because incomplete or absent vaccination increases the likelihood of vertical transmission from mother to child. Understanding this challenge requires examining why pregnant women may fail to initiate or complete the recommended three-dose HBV vaccination schedule, especially within the sociocultural and socioeconomic contexts that shape their health decisions. In the Bono East Region, before this study, there was a limited peer-reviewed study that investigated the rate of non-compliance to HBV vaccination, the sociocultural and socioeconomic barriers influencing this non-compliance, and how these barriers relate to the risk of HBV infection. In response to this gap, the study pursued two interconnected objectives. First, it assessed the rate of non-compliance with the recommended HBV vaccination schedule and identified the sociocultural and socioeconomic barriers influencing this non-compliance among pregnant women in the region. Secondly, it examined how these barriers were associated with HBV infection, which served as the main outcome variable in the regression analysis. Assessing HBV infection status among pregnant women provides an important epidemiological context and helps to illustrate the potential health implications of non-compliance. Together, these objectives provided insight into both vaccination non-compliance and the potential implications of these barriers for HBV infection risk during pregnancy. Generating this evidence was essential for informing strategies that improve vaccination uptake and reduce the burden of HBV in vulnerable populations.

Methods

Study setting and context

The study was conducted in the Bono East Region (Fig. 1). Projections from the 2021 population and housing census indicated a total of 1,203,400 people, occupying 22,952 km2 of land, with a population density of 48.75 km2 [19]. The study included seven health facilities that provide deliveries and antenatal care (ANC) in seven municipalities/districts. These municipalities/districts were Atebubu-Amantin Municipal, Kintampo South District, Kintampo North Municipal, Nkoranza South Municipal, Techiman Municipal, Pru East District, and Pru West District. The region is primarily made up of rural communities inhabited by various ethnic groups [20]. In a few urban towns, most people work in agriculture and retail. Due to the region’s agricultural activities throughout the year, a workforce from the northern part of the country temporarily travels into communities in the region to work for income. These factors significantly contribute to an increase in the population’s flow to and from all parts of the country and beyond. As a region in the heart of a country, these population dynamics have enormous public health implications, as emergencies that occur anywhere in the country or the sub-region could spread to the region and vice versa [19].

Study design

This hospital-based cross-sectional study employed a mixed-method design among 1430 pregnant women in seven selected municipal/district health facilities over a specific time frame (from September 2023 to June 2024). This design was selected to efficiently gather data across diverse patient populations across multiple health facilities.

Study population

The study population comprised consented pregnant women who attended ANC in selected healthcare facilities.

Inclusion criteria

Pregnant women who lived in the selected municipalities/districts attended ANC at seven selected health facilities at the time of the study, and those who agreed to participate were included.

Exclusion criteria

Pregnant women were excluded if they had already been vaccinated against HBV, did not receive ANC at the selected health facilities, or declined participation at any stage. Those who consented but later withdrew, experienced discomfort during questioning, or reported insufficient time to complete the interview were also excluded. In addition, women who withheld consent for personal reasons were not included.

Sample size estimation

The sample size for the study was estimated with Slovin’s formula using the population of pregnant women registered in the selected municipalities/districts for 2022 [Atebubu-Amantin (n = 5192), Kintampo North (n = 6186), Kintampo South (n = 2205), Nkoranza South (n = 3932), Techiman Municipal (n = 9334), Pru East (n = 4140), Pru West (n = 2406)]. To ensure adequate representation of pregnant women across all seven selected areas, an initial estimated sample size of 1075 was increased by 35%, resulting in a final sample size of 1452. This adjustment was made to account for the considerable variation in population sizes across the municipalities/districts and to prevent underrepresentation in smaller areas such as Kintampo South, Pru West, and Nkoranza South. The expanded sample size also strengthened the precision of subgroup estimates and ensured that each selected area contributed sufficient participants for meaningful statistical comparisons. This approach was, therefore, a methodological choice to improve representativeness and analytical reliability rather than a conventional attrition-based adjustment [21]. Each study site was then allotted a quota based on the sample size proportional to the respective municipality/districts to maintain representativeness [Atebubu-Amantin Municipal (n = 226), Kintampo North Municipal (n = 269), Kintampo South District (n = 96), Nkoranza South Municipal (n = 171), Techiman Municipal (n = 405), Pru East District (n = 180), and Pru West District (n = 105)]. Pregnant women were randomly selected until their numbers were proportional to the sample size of the representative municipalities/districts.

Sampling and enrolment of pregnant women

This study employed multistage sampling. The Bono East Region was first stratified into its municipalities and districts. From this stratification, seven health facilities that provide antenatal and delivery services were purposively selected from seven municipalities/districts. These sites were selected to ensure broad geographical representation across the region’s diverse ecological, socioeconomic, and health service delivery contexts, and because of their capacity to provide routine ANC and delivery services. Within each selected health facility, systematic random sampling was used to recruit pregnant women. On each clinic day, the ANC register served as the sampling frame. The number of eligible pregnant women present (N) was divided by the assigned daily quota for that facility (n) to obtain the sampling interval ( k), expressed as:

The first participant was selected by randomly choosing a number between 1 and k. Thereafter, every pregnant woman listed in the ANC register was approached for participation until the daily quota was met. This approach ensured that each eligible pregnant woman had an equal chance of being selected, thereby reducing the likelihood of sampling bias. Eligible pregnant women were approached after registration at the ANC unit to discuss the study’s objectives, potential risks, expected benefits, confidentiality, and voluntary nature of participation. Those who agreed to take part provided written informed consent through either a signature or a thumbprint. For participants below 18 years, assent was obtained alongside parental or guardian consent. Enrolment continued until each facility reached its quota allocated proportionally to the size of the pregnant woman population in the respective municipality or district.

A flow diagram illustrating participant recruitment, enrolment, and reasons for non-participation is presented in Fig. 2. The study initially targeted 1,452 pregnant women based on the calculated sample size. Of these, 1,430 consented to participate and were enrolled in the study. Twenty-two pregnant women (1.52%) declined participation. Reasons for non-participation included discomfort with the study procedures (40.9%, n = 9), language barriers (22.7%, n = 5), time constraints (27.3%, n = 6), and personal reasons (9.1%, n = 2). Consequently, all analyses were conducted using data from the 1,430 enrolled participants.

Variables of interest

Primary outcome variable

The primary outcome variable was HBV infection. This measured the HBV status of pregnant women at the time of data collection. The variable was categorical and assessed on a dichotomous scale, coded as 0 = HBsAg negative and 1 = HBsAg positive. This variable served as the main outcome in the logistic regression analysis used to examine associations between the assessed sociocultural and socioeconomic barriers and the risk of HBV infection.

Secondary outcome variable

The secondary variable was HBV vaccination status, which captured whether pregnant women had initiated or completed the HBV vaccination regimen. For descriptive purposes, this variable was coded as 0 = Not vaccinated and 1 = Vaccinated and was used to report the rate of non-compliance with the recommended vaccination schedule. Vaccination status was presented descriptively and was not the primary outcome in the logistic regression models.

Predictor variable

Predictor variables comprised sociocultural and socioeconomic barriers to compliance with HBV vaccination, including neglect by family members, religious prohibitions, cultural taboos, fear of divine punishment, belief in spiritual poisoning, unemployment, concerns about becoming a burden to family members, cost of the intervention, and time burden. All predictor variables were categorical and coded as 0 = No and 1 = Yes.

Description of instruments and data collection methods

All instruments used in the study were specifically developed to align with the study objectives. The obstetrics data collection guide (Supplementary File 1), the structured questionnaires (Supplementary File 2 and 3), and the qualitative interview guides (Supplementary File 4) were drafted based on existing evidence [15, 18] and the thematic focus of the study. To enhance content validity, the instruments were reviewed by two senior public health researchers and a specialist midwife with extensive ANC experience in the study region. Before data collection, the instruments were pretested among 30 pregnant women attending ANC in a facility outside the study sites. The pretesting assessed comprehension, cultural appropriateness, ease of administration, and completion time. Feedback from the pretesting informed minor revisions, including refinement of questions, wording, and sequencing. The pretest data were not included in the final analysis. These steps were taken to support minimal standardisation and ensure the suitability of the instruments for the target population.

Obstetrics data collection guide (Supplementary File 1)

This guide was developed to extract clinical and obstetric data documented in the ANC record book. It consisted of seven variables, including gravidity, parity, number of ANC visits, gestational age, Glucose-6-phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) status, blood group, and haemoglobin level. Previous Hb levels of pregnant women during the study were averaged to determine the proportion of pregnant women who were anaemic during pregnancy. The format of the guide allowed for recording each variable in a structured manner, ensuring uniformity across all seven health facilities. It also contained sections for verifying laboratory information and a prompt for obtaining any undocumented information verbally from consented pregnant women. To assess reliability, the guide underwent internal consistency testing, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.834, indicating strong reliability (Table 1).

Structured questionnaire on sociodemographic characteristics (Supplementary File 2)

This instrument captured the background characteristics of the pregnant women. It consisted of standardised closed-ended items covering age, marital status, education level, religious affiliation, employment status, occupation type, monthly income, household structure, household type, and number of people living in the household. The items were designed to be simple, brief, and easily understood. The structure enabled quantification of key demographic indicators needed to contextualise the study findings. The internal consistency of this questionnaire was examined, resulting in a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.836, demonstrating high reliability (Table 1).

Structured questionnaire on barriers to compliance with HBV vaccination (Supplementary File 3)

This instrument assessed the barriers influencing non-compliance with HBV vaccination. It consisted of closed-ended items grouped into sociocultural and socioeconomic domains. This ensured that all relevant barriers were assessed in a uniform manner across all pregnant women. The questionnaire demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.760 (Table 1).

Overall reliability of the combined quantitative instruments

When all quantitative items from Supplementary Files 1–3 were combined, they produced an overall Cronbach’s alpha [α] value of 0.821, confirming good internal consistency across the integrated dataset (Table 1).

Qualitative interview guides for FGDs and IDIs (Supplementary File 4)

Two separate but complementary interview guides were used for focus group discussions (FGDs) with pregnant women and in-depth interviews (IDIs) with unit heads of the ANC facilities. The FGD guide included open-ended questions exploring pregnant women’s perceptions of HBV, experiences with screening and vaccination, sociocultural influences on decisions, economic constraints, and general attitudes toward health interventions. Probing questions were embedded to encourage elaboration and to allow participants to describe their lived experiences in their own words. Likewise, the IDI guide was designed for health workers and focused on facility-level perspectives. It covered HBV prevention practices, availability and cost of interventions, perceived barriers to compliance among pregnant women, and the operational challenges encountered during service delivery. Both guides maintained thematic consistency to ensure complementarity between qualitative and quantitative findings while allowing participants the flexibility to express detailed insights.

In total, seven FGDs were conducted, with one session held in each of the selected facilities. Each FGD comprised 15–20 pregnant women who met the inclusion criteria and provided informed consent. Participants were selected based on their current pregnancy status, attendance at ANC services within the study facilities, and their ability to provide relevant and credible responses regarding barriers to HBV vaccination. This approach ensured that participants had lived experiences relating to ANC and decision-making around vaccination. All FGDs lasted approximately 45–60 min and were audio-recorded with participants’ permission.

Likewise, seven IDIs were conducted with the heads of the ANC units in each selected health facility. These key informants were purposively selected based on their managerial role, firsthand knowledge of service delivery, and direct involvement in HBV-related maternal health interventions. Each IDI followed a semi-structured interview guide and explored facility-level challenges, service provision, and contextual factors influencing adherence. Interviews lasted 30–45 min and were audio-recorded with consent.

Serological screening for HBV

HBV screening was performed to determine the proportion of pregnant women who were positive for HBV mono-infections. At the time of arrival at the laboratory, the left arms of the pregnant women were cleaned with 70% denatured alcohol, and approximately 5 ml of venous blood was collected into purple-top EDTA tubes following the standard operating procedure (SOP) performed by a trained medical laboratory technician. The Micropoint HBsAg Gold Rapid Screen Test Kit (Trinity Biotech Plc, Japan) was used. The Rapid test strip is used for direct detection of HBsAg in human samples, such as serum, saliva, or whole blood, using chromatographic immunoassay [22]. The kit consists of a dip strip device, a plastic pipette, and a desiccant. The collected blood samples from pregnant women were allowed to clot for 30 min at room temperature and centrifuged at 1500 revolutions per minute (RPM) for 3 min to separate the serum. Upon separation, about 2–3 drops of the serum were placed onto the serum pad of the Test Kit, and subsequently, 2–3 drops of buffer were added. The results were read after 5–20 min. For the certainty of outcomes, test strips were quality controlled using known HBsAg-positive serum samples stored in facility laboratories. In addition, all HBV-positive samples were repeated for confirmation because some test kits may be faulty, which could lead to false-positive results. A positive result was classified as HBsAg if a pink band appeared on both the control (C) and test (T) regions of the test strips. In contrast, the test was confirmed to be negative if the pink band appeared only on the control (C) region of the test strips. In addition, a test was reported as invalid if a pink band appeared on the test (T) region or if no pink band appeared on the test strips [23]. Tests were repeated on samples that were reported to be invalid upon reading on the test strips.

Data management and statistical analysis

All data were examined for completeness, consistency, and clarity as part of data management before analysis using the Software Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25 (IBM, USA).

Quantitative analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to summarise the data using frequencies and percentages, as well as means with corresponding standard deviations for continuous variables. Differences in proportions across categorical variables were assessed using chi-square (χ2) tests. Logistic regression modelling was then applied to estimate odds ratios and to identify barriers associated with HBV infection, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals. Bivariate logistic regression analyses (Model I) were first performed to explore unadjusted associations, after which variables with p-values ≤ 0.25 were selected for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model (Model II).

Minimising bias

Sampling bias could occur because of the differences between the populations of the selected municipalities and districts under study. This was addressed by allotting a quota based on the sample size proportional to the respective municipality/districts to maintain representativeness. Selection bias was addressed through randomisation, where the random sampling technique was used to ensure that every member of the population had an equal chance of being included in the sample. Another form of bias that was minimised was that related to data analysis. This bias could occur because of inappropriate statistical analyses, leading to misinterpretation of the results [24]. Data analysis bias was minimised by understanding all the statistical analyses and their associated assumptions before the data collection instruments were administered.

Results

Sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics of pregnant women

The sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics of pregnant women are presented in Table 2. Their ages ranged from 17 to 40 years, with a mean of 28.8 ± 3.73 years (95% CI: 28.63–29.02). Approximately three-quarters (76.1%) were aged between 26 and 40 years, and a similar proportion were married (76.1%). About two-fifths (42.8%) had attained primary education, while the majority were Christians (83.5%). Nearly all participants were employed (97.6%), with trading/marketing constituting the most common occupation (42.9%). Nearly half of the pregnant women (48.4%) earned a monthly income between GH¢100 and GH¢500. Over three-quarters lived in extended households (75.9%), and more than two-thirds resided in compound houses (70.6%). Most participants lived in houses constructed with blocks and iron sheets (89.1%), and nearly two-thirds (62.6%) lived in households comprising one to five members.

Discussion

Prevalence of HBV infection and interventions geared towards HBV prevention

This study found a relatively low prevalence of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection among pregnant women. This estimate is within the range reported by some facility-based studies in Ghana and similar settings, although prevalence varies considerably by geographic context and study design [25, 26]. The low prevalence observed may reflect routine antenatal screening practices and increasing awareness of HBV among pregnant women in the study area [27, 28]. Importantly, this finding provides epidemiological context for interpreting subsequent results on barriers to compliance with HBV vaccination and risk of HBV infection. Despite the relatively low prevalence, the study revealed substantial gaps in access to HBV vaccination.

Findings from the in-depth interviews (IDIs) with senior midwives indicate that a range of facility-level interventions are routinely implemented to control HBV among pregnant women. These interventions primarily include routine antenatal screening, health education, counselling, and prevention of mother-to-child transmission. Across facilities, HBV screening was described as a standard component of antenatal care (ANC), with all pregnant women tested during ANC visits. This approach is consistent with evidence from previous studies in Ghana and similar sub-Saharan African settings, which have reported ANC-based screening as the primary entry point for HBV prevention among pregnant women [10, 25, 26]. Routine screening, therefore, provides an important opportunity for early identification of HBV status and initiation of preventive counselling.

Health education also emerged as a central intervention, particularly in relation to HBV vaccination. Midwives reported that pregnant women are routinely educated about HBV transmission, risks during pregnancy, and preventive behaviours, after which vaccination is encouraged, especially among women who test negative. Similar provider-led education efforts within ANC settings have been documented elsewhere in Ghana [9, 18]. However, despite consistent counselling and encouragement, vaccination uptake was reported to be constrained by financial considerations, as the vaccine is not freely available. This mirrors findings from other sub-Saharan African contexts, where out-of-pocket costs remain a key barrier to compliance with maternal health interventions, even when services are formally integrated into routine care [15, 18, 29, 30].

For pregnant women who test positive for HBV, counselling and referral for further management were emphasised as key strategies. Midwives described a strong focus on preventing mother-to-child transmission through counselling and recommendations for hepatitis B immunoglobulin administration for newborns. Nevertheless, the high cost of immunoglobulin was repeatedly identified as a major challenge, limiting adherence to recommended preventive measures even among well-informed women. Similar constraints have been reported in other low- and middle-income country settings, where the affordability of immunoglobulin undermines efforts despite established clinical guidelines [18, 30, 31].

HBV vaccination coverage among pregnant women

The study also revealed a high level of non-compliance with HBV vaccination among pregnant women. Thus, nearly three-quarters of the pregnant women reported that they had not taken the HBV vaccine or had not completed the full vaccination regimen. This substantial gap between recommended practice and reported uptake is consistent with findings from other facility-based studies conducted in similar settings, where HBV vaccination coverage among pregnant women has been reported to be suboptimal [32, 33]. The significant difference observed between vaccinated and non-vaccinated women underlines the extent of non-compliance and highlights the need to better understand the underlying factors influencing vaccination behaviour.

Insights from the IDIs with senior midwives provide important contextual explanations for this high non-vaccination rate. Financial constraints were consistently identified as a major barrier, with midwives noting that the out-of-pocket cost of the HBV vaccine discourages many pregnant women from initiating or completing the vaccination regimen. Similar cost-related barriers to maternal vaccination uptake have been documented across sub-Saharan Africa, where inconsistent financial protection mechanisms continue to limit access to preventive health services [18, 30]. These findings suggest that affordability remains a critical determinant of vaccination compliance, even within routine ANC settings.

Moreover, fear of injections and concerns about vaccine safety also emerged as important deterrents to uptake. Midwives reported that some pregnant women declined vaccination due to fear of pain, perceived side effects, or beliefs that the vaccine could harm the unborn child. Such fears have been reported in previous studies in Ghana and other low- and middle-income countries, where misinformation and limited understanding of vaccine safety contribute to hesitancy among pregnant women [9, 10]. These concerns highlight the need for sustained, targeted health education that directly addresses misconceptions surrounding HBV vaccination during pregnancy. In addition, sociocultural factors, particularly religious and spiritual beliefs, were described as influencing vaccine refusal among some women. Preference for prayer, spiritual protection, or traditional remedies over biomedical interventions has similarly been reported as a barrier to vaccination uptake in earlier studies [34, 35]. Time-related constraints, including late initiation of ANC, competing work demands, and long travel distances to health facilities, further compounded non-compliance, echoing findings from other maternal health studies in resource-limited settings [9].

Sociocultural barriers to compliance with HBV vaccination

From the perspectives of pregnant women who participated in the focus group discussions (FGDs), sociocultural factors strongly influenced compliance with HBV vaccination. Quantitative findings showed that concerns about neglect or negative reactions from family members were the most frequently reported sociocultural barrier, and the qualitative narratives provide deeper insight into how these fears shape decision-making. Women described apprehension about blame, stigma, and possible abandonment by spouses or extended family members if they were perceived to have violated family or cultural expectations. Similar patterns of family-mediated control over maternal health decisions have been documented in studies conducted in Ghana and other sub-Saharan African contexts, where women’s autonomy during pregnancy is often negotiated within broader household and kinship structures [36, 37]. In most African cultures, the roles of families vary from being the primary unit of social organisation to financial support, social structures, care, and, most importantly, preserving the cultural values of the society [38]. Neglect by family members is likely to lead to many psychological and emotional imbalances, including resentment, stigma, isolation, shame, depression, and anxiety [39]. Consequently, pregnant women who are faced with neglect by family members and hence have associated psychological disparities are unlikely to comply with HBV vaccination. Increasing support systems that facilitate the family’s responsiveness and orientation regarding adherence and creating a supportive environment for pregnant women could help improve adherence to interventions, which in turn may decrease the rate of transmission.

Religious prohibitions were also described by pregnant women as influencing their decisions not to take the HBV vaccine. Some of them recounted faith-based teachings that framed vaccination as unnecessary, spiritually dangerous, or indicative of weak faith. These narratives reflect findings from earlier studies showing that religious beliefs and guidance from spiritual leaders could significantly shape health behaviours, particularly when biomedical explanations conflict with religious interpretations of illness and healing [40, 41]. From the women’s accounts, adherence to religious doctrine often took precedence over medical advice received during ANC. This could be attributed to fear of judgment or punishment from superior beings. For instance, in some Muslim-dominated societies, the use of vaccines containing gelatine derived from pigs is forbidden unless halal-certified alternatives are provided [42]. Likewise, some customs see compliance with vaccines as a way of challenging the decree of the supreme beings. These customs also presume that illnesses are a way of drawing people closer to divinity and appreciating the existence of supreme beings [43]. These religious and ancestral beliefs have accorded credence or supremacy to certain individuals regarded as living representatives of supreme beings or ancestors (gods) who tend to enforce certain prohibitions regarding interventions to diseases on their followers, as highlighted in the FDGs. From another perspective, some pregnant women perceived diseases, especially HBV, as an after-effect of moral shortcomings or divine retribution. This perception is likely to breed shame or guilt, which could lead to non-compliance with the intervention [15].

Similarly, beliefs related to cultural taboos, fear of divine punishment, and spiritual poisoning further illustrate how pregnant women interpret the causes of HBV through non-biomedical frameworks. Some of the pregnant women described HBV as a condition that may result from ancestral displeasure, violation of cultural norms, or spiritual attacks, which they believed required spiritual or traditional remedies rather than vaccination. Similar belief systems regarding spiritual causation of illness have been reported in maternal health research in other low- and middle-income settings, where preventive biomedical interventions may be perceived as ineffective for spiritually mediated conditions [15, 44]. Instead of complying with formal interventions, such beliefs could lead pregnant women to resort to traditional healers or prayer centres, thereby increasing the risk of transmission [45]. These echoes highlight the surge in demand for spiritual healing services, and a strong collaboration between various stakeholders in health and religious, community, or traditional leaders to endorse and promote adherence could help mitigate religious and cultural barriers. Importantly, this perception was also reflected in the quantitative analysis, which showed that pregnant women who believed that HBV could be caused by spiritual poisoning had significantly higher odds of HBV infection compared with those who did not hold this belief.

Socioeconomic barriers to compliance with HBV vaccination

From the perspectives of pregnant women, certain socioeconomic constraints were central to their decisions not to initiate or complete HBV vaccination. Although only a small proportion of women explicitly cited unemployment as a barrier, the qualitative narratives reveal how unstable or seasonal livelihoods limit women’s financial autonomy and ability to afford vaccination. Women described reliance on farming, informal labour, or family support, which made the cost of vaccination particularly burdensome during periods of low income. Similar links between precarious employment and reduced uptake of maternal health interventions have been reported in other studies conducted in Ghana and comparable low- and middle-income settings [37, 46]. For example, the HBV vaccine comes with a cost, which an individual must incur [47]. If diagnosed, individuals with low financial strength may face challenges in accessing and affording it, leading to a higher risk of diseases and their associated complications.

Concerns about becoming a burden to family members emerged as a prominent socioeconomic barrier to compliance with HBV vaccination. This was reported by over one-third of the pregnant women and strongly articulated during the FGDs. Pregnant women expressed reluctance to request financial support for vaccination or related care, particularly in households already facing economic strain. This perceived burden often led women to deprioritise their own preventive health needs, even when they recognised the benefits of vaccination. Similar findings have been documented in previous studies, where women’s health-seeking behaviour during pregnancy is shaped by social expectations around self-reliance and the moral responsibility not to impose additional costs on family members [48, 49]. This perception was also reflected in the quantitative analysis, which showed that pregnant women who reported concerns about being a burden to their families had significantly higher odds of HBV infection compared with those who did not express such concerns.

The cost of HBV-related interventions was the most frequently reported socioeconomic barrier, with nearly three-fifths of pregnant women identifying it as a reason for non-compliance. From the women’s accounts, the expense of the HBV vaccine, follow-up tests, medications, and hepatitis B immunoglobulin created a cumulative financial burden that discouraged sustained engagement with recommended care. These perspectives align with reports from other sub-Saharan African contexts, where out-of-pocket payments for maternal health services continue to undermine compliance despite routine service availability within antenatal care settings [15, 18, 29, 30]. Insights from the IDIs further corroborate these experiences, as midwives acknowledged that the high cost of immunoglobulin often limits adherence to PMTCT recommendations even among motivated families. Quantitative analysis further supports this observation, showing that pregnant women who reported cost-related barriers had significantly higher odds of HBV infection compared with those who did not identify cost as a barrier.

Meanwhile, time-related costs were also prominently described by pregnant women as a barrier to vaccination compliance. Long waiting times at health facilities, late initiation of ANC, competing work responsibilities, and long travel distances, often compounded by poor road conditions, were reported to discourage women from returning for vaccination or completing the recommended regimen. Similar logistical barriers have been identified in maternal health studies across Ghana and other LMICs, where time poverty and transportation challenges disproportionately affect women’s access to preventive services [37, 46]. These findings reinforce existing literature suggesting that improving maternal vaccination uptake will require interventions that address not only knowledge and availability but also the broader economic and structural conditions shaping women’s ability to comply with recommended care.

Recommendations for policy and practice

This study highlights the need for coordinated, multilevel policy and practice interventions to address the persistently high rate of non-vaccination against HBV among pregnant women in the Bono East Region of Ghana. The findings indicate that sociocultural beliefs and socioeconomic constraints play a central role in shaping vaccination non-compliance, while health system-level factors, particularly the cost of vaccination, interact with these barriers to sustain low uptake. Importantly, these barriers were also associated with an increased risk of HBV infection, highlighting their public health significance. In response, the Ghana Health Service (GHS), as the primary implementing authority, should prioritise the full integration of HBV vaccination into routine ANC services. Making HBV vaccination a standard component of ANC would reduce missed opportunities and normalise vaccine uptake during pregnancy. For such integration to be effective, the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) should be expanded to cover the cost of HBV vaccination and related preventive services. Eliminating out-of-pocket payments would directly address one of the strongest barriers identified in this study and ensure that financial capacity does not determine access to HBV prevention.

At the individual level, targeted efforts are needed to address belief-driven and informational barriers to vaccination. There is a need for culturally sensitive, literacy-appropriate health education delivered consistently during ANC visits. Health messages should clearly explain the causes of HBV, the safety and benefits of vaccination during pregnancy, and the risks of mother-to-child transmission, using local languages and contextually relevant examples. Beyond the health facility, broader community engagement is essential to address enabling factors such as stigma, spiritual interpretations of disease, and social norms that discourage vaccination. Collaboration with community leaders, faith-based organisations, traditional authorities, and male partners could help legitimise HBV vaccination and counter misinformation. Community-based outreach strategies, including mobile ANC services and health talks at community gatherings, may further complement facility-based interventions and reduce access barriers, particularly in underserved areas. Taken together, these policy and practice recommendations recognise that while health system integration and financial protection are immediate priorities, sustained improvements in HBV vaccination uptake will require simultaneous efforts to address sociocultural norms and belief systems that shape health-seeking behaviour among pregnant women.

Study’s strengths and limitations

This study has several important strengths. In the first instance, the mixed-methods design enabled triangulation of quantitative and qualitative findings. This approach provided a more comprehensive understanding of HBV vaccination non-compliance and its association with HBV infection among pregnant women. The quantitative component allowed estimation of HBV prevalence, vaccination status, and statistical associations. In contrast, the qualitative data offered contextual insights into the sociocultural and socioeconomic factors underlying non-compliance. Secondly, the inclusion of multiple study sites across different districts enhanced the diversity of perspectives captured. This was particularly evident through in-depth interviews with senior midwives and focus group discussions with pregnant women. Together, these features strengthened the credibility and internal validity of the findings. Similarly, the use of adjusted regression models with appropriate diagnostic checks improved the robustness of the quantitative analysis.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. The study was facility-based and included only pregnant women who attended antenatal care and consented to participate. As a result, the findings may not reflect the experiences of pregnant women who do not access ANC services. In addition, vaccination status and some sociocultural factors were self-reported. These measures may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. The cross-sectional design also limits the ability to infer temporal or causal relationships between non-vaccination and HBV infection. Finally, although the qualitative data provided rich contextual information, the findings reflect perceptions at the time of data collection. Changes in beliefs or practices over time may therefore not be fully captured.

Conclusion

This study sheds light on various barriers that pregnant women face in complying with HBV vaccination. It revealed that a significant proportion of pregnant women were not vaccinated against HBV, mainly due to certain sociocultural and socioeconomic barriers. Key barriers contributing to non-compliance included neglect by family members, religious prohibitions, taboos, divine punishment, and spiritual poisoning. In addition, unemployment, concerns about becoming a burden, perceived high cost of vaccine, and time constraints were identified as significant socioeconomic barriers to compliance with HBV vaccination. Overall, the findings of this study reinforce that while non-compliance with HBV vaccination is shaped by complex sociocultural and socioeconomic factors, failure to address these barriers may have measurable implications for the risk of HBV infection among pregnant women. Therefore, collaborative efforts from healthcare providers, policymakers, community leaders, and other stakeholders are needed to address these barriers effectively. This study recommends initiatives such as subsidising healthcare costs, improving healthcare accessibility, enhancing health education, and strengthening community support systems. These measures could help overcome these obstacles and improve compliance, leading to healthier and more resilient maternal health.
