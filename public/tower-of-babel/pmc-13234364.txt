Prevalence, risk factors, and antimicrobial resistance of Staphylococcus aureus in equines in Egypt
Authors: Marwa O Abd El-Halim, Mostafa Shoraba, Hattan S Gattan, Mohammed H Alruhaili, Abdelfattah Selim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStaphylococcus aureus is a common commensal organism in both humans and animals, frequently colonizing the skin and nasal cavities of healthy individuals, although it can also cause a wide range of severe infections. The present study investigated the prevalence and risk factors of S. aureus in equines, along with antimicrobial resistance patterns of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) isolates. The results revealed a prevalence of 38.6% in nasal samples collected from equines, highlighting the organism’s significant presence and potential to cause infections in these animals. A genotypic prevalence of 8.2% was also recorded. Horses showed a significantly higher prevalence compared to donkeys and mules, indicating species-related differences in susceptibility and management practices. Risk factor analysis demonstrated higher infection rates in young animals, those in poor body condition, animals showing respiratory signs, and those with a history of prior antibiotic therapy. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of MRSA isolates revealed high resistance to cefoxitin, cefotaxime, and meropenem, indicating the emergence of multidrug-resistant strains. These findings emphasize the role of antimicrobial misuse and microbial adaptation in resistance development. Overall, the study highlights the epidemiological importance of S. aureus in equines and underscores the need for improved management, hygiene practices, and prudent antibiotic use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55462-4.Keywords: S. aureus, MRSA, Equines, Prevalence, Risk factors, Antimicrobial resistance, Nasal colonization, EgyptSubject terms: Diseases, Microbiology

Introduction

Staphylococcus species represent a diverse group of mesophilic bacteria that are widely distributed in nature and are commonly found as part of the normal microbiota colonizing the skin and mucous membranes of both humans and animals1,2. These organisms can persist in various environmental niches and may act as commensals under normal conditions3.

Among them, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is classified as a coagulase-positive staphylococcus (CoPS) and is recognized as the most important opportunistic pathogen within the genus4,5. It is capable of causing a broad spectrum of infections in both humans and animals, ranging from mild, localized, and self-limiting cutaneous conditions to more severe and potentially life-threatening systemic diseases, depending on host factors and virulence characteristics of the strain6–8.

A significant concern associated with staphylococcal infections is the increasing emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains, most notably methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)9–11. This emergence represents a serious threat to public health across all sectors, as it limits the effectiveness and availability of antibiotics commonly used in clinical settings and complicates the management and treatment of bacterial infections12,13. From another perspective, antimicrobial-resistant bacteria impose a substantial economic burden, particularly in low-income countries, where they exacerbate disease burden and increase mortality among vulnerable populations. Projections suggest that, by 2050, antimicrobial resistance could result in up to 10 million deaths annually and incur global costs reaching 100 trillion US dollars14,15.

Studies have indicated that livestock and companion animals can serve as asymptomatic reservoirs of MRSA16,17, in addition to harboring borderline oxacillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (BORSA) strains18,19.

Methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA), MRSA, and BORSA are frequently identified in the nasal cavities of clinically healthy horses in developed countries. For example, Pusterla et al.20 reported the detection of MRSA in 22% of cases, while Hryniewicz and Garbacz18 documented a BORSA prevalence exceeding 25% in equines receiving routine veterinary care. In contrast, Mama et al.21 observed an overall staphylococcal prevalence of 90% in horses intended for human consumption.

In addition to β-lactams, S. aureus isolated from equines in various regions worldwide has shown resistance to aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, folate pathway inhibitors, fusidic acid, macrolides, and tetracyclines in recent years22. Notably, studies have reported healthy horses harboring S. aureus strains resistant to heavy metals23. as well as a high rate of nasal colonization by multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains, affecting up to one in four equines24,25. However, in contrast to the available data for other livestock species, information on the occurrence of S. aureus in low-income countries remains limited.

In this context, the study aimed to investigate the antimicrobial resistance patterns and genetic relationships of Staphylococcus aureus isolates from healthy horses, along with identifying potential risk factors associated with their prevalence.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Benha University. All methods and procedures were conducted in accordance with the committee’s relevant guidelines and regulations. The animal study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Benha University. All methods and procedures were conducted in accordance with the committee’s relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from owner. All study was performed in accordance with ARRIVE.

Study area

The study was conducted in Mansoura Governorate, Egypt, from September 2025 to April 2026. The study area is located in the Nile Delta region and is characterized by a typical Mediterranean climate, with mild winters, warm to hot summers, moderate humidity, and seasonal variations in temperature and rainfall.

Sample strategy

The present study was initiated in response to increasing reports of reduced efficacy of β-lactam antibiotics in treating respiratory and systemic infections in equines. Accordingly, the study was conducted on equines in Mansoura Governorate, Egypt, exhibiting clinical signs of respiratory distress, including nasal discharge, coughing, dyspnea, or pneumonia.

A total of 280 nasal samples were collected from equines, including 160 horses, 30 mule, and 90 donkeys. Sterile cotton swabs were gently rubbed against the anterior part of both nares in accordance with established guidelines26.

Regarding age distribution, 66 animals were younger than 2 years, 140 were between 2 and 4 years, and 74 were older than 4 years. The collected swabs were immediately transported to the laboratory in containers containing sterile normal saline.

The association between animal-related and management-related risk factors and the occurrence of S. aureus colonization in equines was assessed using a predesigned questionnaire. Various factors were evaluated, including species, age, physical condition, season, presence of respiratory signs and history of antibiotic use. The data was collected via questionnaire at time of sampling.

Bacterial isolation

Nasal specimens were streaked onto freshly prepared Mannitol Salt Agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Following visible growth, colonies were subcultured onto nutrient agar plates and incubated again at 37 °C for 24 h to obtain pure cultures, which were subsequently used for biochemical characterization.

The isolates were examined based on their morphological characteristics, Gram staining, and biochemical tests, including catalase and coagulase assays, to verify their identity. Final identification and confirmation of the bacterial isolates were performed according to Bergey’s Manual of Determinative Bacteriology [2].

For Gram staining, a smear of each isolate was prepared on clean, sterilized glass slides and allowed to air dry. The smear was stained with crystal violet for 60 s, rinsed with distilled water, and then treated with Gram’s iodine for another 60 s. Decolorization was carried out rapidly using acetone, followed by immediate washing with distilled water. The slide was then counterstained with safranin for one minute, rinsed, and air dried.

The stained slides were examined under oil immersion at ×100 magnification to observe bacterial morphology [3]. Gram-positive organisms appeared purple, whereas Gram-negative organisms appeared red [4].

Biochemical identification

This test detects the catalase enzyme, which breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, distinguishing catalase-positive Staphylococcus spp. from catalase-negative Streptococcus spp. A colony was mixed with 2 mL of 3% hydrogen peroxide; immediate effervescence indicated a positive result [3].

The coagulase test identifies Staphylococcus aureus by detecting the coagulase enzyme. For the slide test, a bacterial suspension in saline was observed for clumping within 5–10 s. For the tube test, 0.5 mL of bacterial culture was mixed with 0.5 mL citrated rabbit plasma, incubated at 37 °C for 1–4 h, and checked for clot formation; clotting indicated a positive result [3,5].

Antimicrobial susceptibility tests

Antimicrobial susceptibility was evaluated using the agar disk diffusion method following the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (Quinn et al., 1994). Briefly, isolated colonies were cultured in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth and incubated overnight. The resulting bacterial suspension was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard and evenly inoculated onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates using a sterile swab.

Antibiotic-impregnated disks were then applied to the agar surface, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. After incubation, inhibition zone diameters were measured in millimeters and interpreted accordingly.

The panel of antimicrobials tested against Gram-positive isolates included agents included β-lactams (amoxicillin/clavulanic AMC 30 µg, cefotaxim CTX 30 µg and Cefoxitin disc 30 µg), tetracycline T 30 µg, aminoglycoside (gentamycin CN 10 µg), macrolides (Azithromycin AZM 15 µg), carbapenem (meropenem MEM 10 µg. Isolates resistant to more than three antimicrobial classes were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR)27.

Molecular testing

Genomic DNA was extracted from confirmed phenotypic S. aureus isolates using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany, GmbH), with slight modifications to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 200 µL of the sample suspension was incubated with 10 µL of proteinase K and 200 µL of lysis buffer at 56 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 200 µL of absolute ethanol was added to the lysate. The mixture was then processed through washing and centrifugation steps according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, nucleic acid was eluted in 100 µL of the provided elution buffer.

Molecular confirmation of S. aureus isolates was done by PCR using an already reported primer sequence that targets the hla gene at 704 bp by using primers (F- GAAGTCTGGTGAAAACCCTGA-3′ and R- 5′- TGAATCCTGTCGCTAATGCC − 3′) and conditions described by Xiaohong and Yanjun28.

Then PCR was carried out by targeting mecA gene for genotypic confirmation of MRSA by using primers and conditions reported by Galdiero et al.29, P1: 5′-TGGCATTCGTGTCACAATCG-3′, P2: 5′-CTGGAACTTGTTGAGCAGAG- 3′, with a product size of 310 bp.

The PCR products were subjected to gel electrophoresis and bands were then visualized under UV- illuminator.

Statistical analysis

The interview data were organized and tabulated in Microsoft Excel (v. 2010) for descriptive analysis. Subsequently, a univariate logistic regression analysis was performed, using the number of coagulase-positive Staphylococcus isolates resistant to at least one antimicrobial (n = 108) as the dependent variable. Variables with a P-value < 0.2 were then included in a multivariate analysis using a backward selection method.

Variables with P ≤ 0.05 and an odds ratio (OR) greater than or less than 1 were considered risk or protective factors for antimicrobial resistance (AMR), respectively. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 24 (IBM, USA).

Discussion

S. aureus is a common commensal in both humans and animals, frequently colonizing the skin and nasal passages of healthy individuals; however, it can also lead to severe infections30. The present study found a 38.6% prevalence of S. aureus in nasal samples collected from infected equines. The high prevalence of S. aureus in equines highlights its potential to cause a wide range of infections in these animals.

The present study demonstrated a genotypic prevalence of 8.2% in equines. In contrast, a much higher prevalence of MRSA (54%) has been reported in equines in Pakistan31. Similarly, a study from Belgium documented MRSA in the nasal cavity and skin of horses, with prevalence rates of 34.5% and 30%, respectively32. Additionally, research conducted in Ontario and New York reported that MRSA colonization in horses on individual farms ranged from 0 to 45%33. This finding may be attributed to suboptimal hygiene practices and inadequate management conditions commonly observed in equine rearing systems in Egypt. Factors such as poor sanitation, overcrowding, insufficient biosecurity measures, and limited access to proper veterinary care may contribute to the persistence and transmission of infections within equine populations34–37.

The marked discrepancy observed between the phenotypic methicillin resistance rate (49.3%) and the relatively low prevalence of the mecA gene (8.2%) suggests that additional resistance mechanisms may contribute to the methicillin-resistant phenotype among the examined isolates. Similar inconsistencies between phenotypic and genotypic detection methods have been reported previously in S. aureus and other staphylococcal species. Several explanations may account for this phenomenon, including the presence of alternative resistance determinants such as the mecC gene, hyperproduction of β-lactamase enzymes, mutations in penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), or the occurrence of borderline oxacillin-resistant S. aureus (BORSA) strains38–40.

The presence of carbapenem-resistant bacteria in equines may reflect indirect exposure to resistant organisms or resistance determinants through environmental contamination, close contact with humans, inappropriate antimicrobial use, or horizontal gene transfer mediated by mobile genetic elements such as plasmids and transposons. Equines frequently interact with humans, particularly owners, veterinarians, caretakers, and workers, thereby increasing the potential risk of zoonotic transmission of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria. Consequently, horses and other working equids may act as reservoirs and disseminators of resistant pathogens, contributing to the circulation of antimicrobial resistance between animals and humans. These findings emphasize the urgent need for integrated antimicrobial stewardship programs, continuous surveillance, and strict infection control measures targeting both veterinary and public health sectors41–44.

The finding that horses have a significantly higher prevalence of Staphylococcus aureus compared to donkeys and mules suggests important species-related differences in susceptibility, exposure, and management conditions. This observation is in agreement with the findings of Anwaar et al.45, who reported similar variations among equine species.

One possible explanation is that horses are often kept under more intensive management systems, including closer human contact, frequent handling, transportation, and participation in sporting or working activities46–49. In addition, horses may receive more frequent veterinary interventions and antimicrobial treatments than donkeys and mules. This can disrupt the normal microbiota and create selective pressure that favors colonization by opportunistic and potentially resistant strains such as S. aureus50,51.

In contrast to the findings of Diaz-Méndez et al.52 have reported a significant association between age and S. aureus colonization. Many earlier studies have concentrated on foals as an important group for examining antimicrobial susceptibility53,54. This focus is mainly attributed to the increased susceptibility of young animals to infections, which often leads to more frequent antimicrobial treatments and, in turn, a greater selection pressure for the emergence of resistance.

In agreement with Anwaar et al.45, the present study demonstrated that equines in poor body condition are significantly more susceptible to S. aureus infection than those in good condition. This relationship may be explained by the fact that poor body condition is often associated with weakened immune function, reduced physiological reserves, and increased vulnerability to opportunistic pathogens. Animals in poor condition may also be more affected by nutritional deficiencies and chronic stress, both of which can impair immune responses and reduce the ability to resist bacterial colonization55,56.

Equines showing respiratory signs had a higher prevalence of Staphylococcus aureus infection, which is in agreement with Anwaar et al.45. This association may be explained by the fact that respiratory signs often reflect underlying airway inflammation or infection, which can disrupt the normal mucosal barriers of the respiratory tract. Such damage facilitates colonization and persistence of opportunistic pathogens like S. aureus in the nasal passages and upper respiratory tract57–63.

The present study demonstrated a significantly higher prevalence of S. aureus in animals with a history of prior antibiotic therapy. This finding is consistent with64, who also identified previous antimicrobial treatment as a significant risk factor for MRSA colonization in horses.

This can be explained by the fact that antibiotic use can disrupt the normal microbial balance in the body, reducing beneficial bacteria that normally compete with or suppress pathogenic organisms. As a result, S. aureus may have a better opportunity to survive, multiply, and colonize sites such as the nasal cavity65,66.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of MRSA isolates in the present study demonstrated high resistance levels to several antibiotics, including cefoxitin, cefotaxime, and meropenem (MEM). The emergence of antimicrobial resistance in recent times is primarily attributed to genetic mutations and adaptive evolutionary changes in microorganisms, as extensively documented by researchers worldwide67.

A limitation of the present study is that it was conducted exclusively in the Mansoura region, which may restrict the generalization of the findings to other areas of Egypt. Regional differences in management practices, environmental conditions, and antimicrobial use may influence resistance patterns. In addition, the high prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolates may be associated with the inappropriate or excessive use of antibiotics and the limited implementation of antimicrobial stewardship programs. These findings highlight the need for improved antibiotic use policies and continuous surveillance within a One Health framework.

Conclusion

The present study confirms that Staphylococcus aureus is widely prevalent among equines, with horses being more affected than donkeys and mules. Infection was strongly associated with age, poor body condition, respiratory signs, and previous antibiotic use, indicating the influence of both host-related and management-related factors.

The detection of significant antimicrobial resistance among MRSA isolates raises serious concerns regarding therapeutic options and public health implications. These findings highlight the urgent need for strict biosecurity measures, rational use of antibiotics, and continuous surveillance programs to control the spread of S. aureus and its resistant strains in equine populations.
