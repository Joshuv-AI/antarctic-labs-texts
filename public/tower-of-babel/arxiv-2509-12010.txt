Generalizing Behavior via Inverse Reinforcement Learning with Closed-Form Reward Centroids
Filippo Lazzati, Alberto Maria Metelli

Abstract
We study the problem of generalizing an expert agent’s behavior, provided through demonstrations, to new environments and/or additional constraints. Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) offers a promising solution by seeking to recover the expert’s underlying reward function , which, if used for planning in the new settings, would reproduce the desired behavior. However, IRL is inherently ill-posed : multiple reward functions, forming the so-called feasible set , can explain the same observed behavior. Since these rewards may induce different policies in the new setting, in the absence of additional information, a decision criterion is needed to select which policy to deploy. In this paper, we propose a novel, principled criterion that selects the “average” policy among those induced by the rewards in a certain bounded subset of the feasible set. Remarkably, we show that this policy can be obtained by planning with the reward centroid of that subset, for which we derive a closed-form expression . We then present a provably efficient algorithm for estimating this centroid using an offline dataset of expert demonstrations only. Finally, we conduct numerical simulations that illustrate the relationship between the expert’s behavior and the behavior produced by our method.

4
Our Approach

In the previous section, we saw that “on-average” methods, by balancing
optimism and conservatism through a prior over rewards

However, we also observed that the commonly used prior

In this section, we propose novel

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a novel “on-average” approach for generalizing
expert behavior and derived three efficient IRL algorithms for estimating the
corresponding reward centroids from offline data. We also provided numerical
simulations to illustrate the resulting policies’ behavior.

Limitations and future directions.
Our approach is currently limited
to tabular settings. A key direction for future work is to identify structural
assumptions that enable scaling to large or continuous spaces. Broader empirical
studies could also clarify in which application domains our method generalizes
well and when additional structure is needed.

Appendix A
Additional Related Work

In this appendix, we discuss additional related work that was omitted from the
main paper due to space constraints.

In Section
3.2
, we categorized existing IRL methods into
three groups, based on the strategy used to address the partial identifiability
of the expert’s reward function. Here, we introduce a fourth category of
methods, which we refer to as “heuristic”
methods
[
29
,
37
]
. These are closely related to
“best-case” methods. Specifically, heuristic methods select a reward function
from the feasible set using an application-specific heuristic, which is
generally not justified beyond the given context. For example,

[
29
,
37
]
employ the heuristic of
margin
maximization
to select a reward function from the feasible set, a choice mainly
justified within the IL setting.

The reward function obtained through OPT (see Eq.
16
) can be
computed using knowledge of the expert’s support
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
, making it
similar to expert support estimation methods in IL found in the
literature
[
50
,
8
]
.
These works present IL algorithms based on reward functions derived directly
from
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
. However, we emphasize that these approaches are
specifically tailored to IL and typically involve implicit structural
assumptions to scale to large or continuous state-action spaces (see also

[
38
,
41
]
).

Finally, we note that the reward derived for MCE in Eq.(
17
)
was previously discussed in Section 4 of
[
14
]
as a promising
approach for IL. However, it was shown to potentially yield errors when applied
to novel environments. This observation led
[
14
]
to introduce
additional assumptions aimed at reducing the size of the feasible set to obtain
a more transferable reward. In contrast, our work provides a principled
justification for this reward based on the minimization of average error in the
absence of additional assumptions.

Proposition B.3

Proof.

We begin by showing that every reward in the set can be written in this way.
Starting from the definition of
\mathcal{R}^{\text{BIRL},\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{M},\pi}

\displaystyle\pi(a|s)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\frac{1}{\beta}Q^{*}(s,a;p,r)}}{\sum_{a^{\prime}}e^{\frac{1}{\beta}Q^{*}(s,a^{\prime};p,r)}}

\displaystyle\beta\log\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}e^{\frac{1}{\beta}A^{*}(s,a^{\prime};p,r)}=-\beta\log\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s),

\displaystyle A^{*}(s,a;p,r)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(a|s)}{\max_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s)}.

[

\displaystyle r(s,a)=V(s)-\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})+\beta\log\frac{\pi(a|s)}{\max_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s)}.

\displaystyle\pi(a|s)=\frac{e^{\frac{1}{\beta}Q^{*}(s,a;p,r)}}{\sum_{a^{\prime}}e^{\frac{1}{\beta}Q^{*}(s,a^{\prime};p,r)}},

Proposition C.2
.

\setminus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}[\text{vol}\big(\mathfrak{R}\cap\mathcal{R}^{\text{OPT},\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{1}}\big)]=\mathbb{E}_{p}[\text{vol}\big(\mathfrak{R}\cap\mathcal{R}^{\text{OPT},\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{2}}\big)],

where
\mathbb{E}_{p}
denotes the expectation w.r.t.
p
.

Proof.

The result can be proved by showing that, for any possible pair of policies

\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
and any transition model

, there always exists another

for which:

\displaystyle\text{vol}\big(\mathfrak{R}\cap\mathcal{R}^{\text{OPT},\mathcal{S}}_{p^{\prime},\pi_{1}}\big)=\text{vol}\big(\mathfrak{R}\cap\mathcal{R}^{\text{OPT},\mathcal{S}}_{p^{\prime\prime},\pi_{2}}\big),

\displaystyle\text{vol}\big(\mathfrak{R}\cap\mathcal{R}^{\text{OPT},\mathcal{S}}_{p^{\prime\prime},\pi_{1}}\big)=\text{vol}\big(\mathfrak{R}\cap\mathcal{R}^{\text{OPT},\mathcal{S}}_{p^{\prime},\pi_{2}}\big).

where we used
p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime}
in place of
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},s_{0},p^{\prime},\gamma)
and

(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},s_{0},p^{\prime\prime},\gamma)
. To this aim, fix two policies
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
and a
transition model
p^{\prime}
, and construct the transition model
p^{\prime\prime}
such that, for
every

\displaystyle p^{\prime\prime}(s^{\prime}|s,\pi_{1}(s))=p^{\prime}(s^{\prime}|s,\pi_{2}(s)),

\displaystyle p^{\prime\prime}(s^{\prime}|s,\pi_{2}(s))=p^{\prime}(s^{\prime}|s,\pi_{1}(s)),

\displaystyle p^{\prime\prime}(s^{\prime}|s,a)=p^{\prime}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\qquad\text{if }a\neq\pi_{1}(s)\wedge\pi_{2}(s).

\pi_{1}

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{S}}\coloneqq\Big\{s\in\mathcal{S}\;\Big|\;\exists i\in\llbracket N\rrbracket,\exists h\in\llbracket H\rrbracket:\,s_{h}^{i}=s\Big\},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}\coloneqq\Big\{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\;\Big|\;\exists i\in\llbracket N\rrbracket,\exists h\in\llbracket H\rrbracket:\,s_{h}^{i}=s\wedge a_{h}^{i}=a\Big\}.

We want to find a minimum number of samples
NH
that guarantees that, w.h.p.,

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}=\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
. To this
aim, we must first show that taking
H\geq S
is sufficient for guaranteeing that
the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{E}
, made of trajectories long
H
-stages, can contain all the
states and state-action pairs in the support, i.e.,
p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}>0
:

Lemma F.1
.

If
H\geq S
, then
p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}>0
.

Proof.

Observe that, since
H\geq S
, then:

\displaystyle p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\big(\exists t\in\llbracket H\rrbracket:\,s_{t}=s\big)

\displaystyle\geq\min\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\big({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\exists t\in\llbracket S\rrbracket}:\,s_{t}=s\big)

\displaystyle\geq\min\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\min\limits_{t\in\llbracket S\rrbracket}(p^{\pi_{E}})^{t}(s|s_{0})}.

Therefore, the result can be proved by showing that, for every state
s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
, there exists a stage
t\in\llbracket S\rrbracket
for which the probability

(p^{\pi_{E}})^{t}(s|s_{0})
of being in
s
at
t
is strictly greater than 0. But
this is exactly the claim of Lemma
F.2
. This concludes
the proof.
∎

Lemma F.2
.

For every state
s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
, there exists a
t\in\llbracket S\rrbracket
such that

(p^{\pi_{E}})^{t}(s|s_{0})>0
.

Proof.

By hypothesis, since
s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
, then there always exists a

t\in\mathbb{N}
such that:
(p^{\pi_{E}})^{t}(s|s_{0})>0
. If
t\leq S
, then the result
follows immediately, so we restrict to the case where
t>S
. By
computing matrix
(p^{\pi_{E}})^{t}
, we can write:

\displaystyle(p^{\pi_{E}})^{t}(s|s_{0})=\sum\limits_{s^{(2)},s^{(3)},\dotsc,s^{(t-1)}\in\mathcal{S}}p^{\pi_{E}}(s^{(2)}|s_{0})p^{\pi_{E}}(s^{(3)}|s^{(2)})\cdot\dotsc\cdot p^{\pi_{E}}(s|s^{(t-1)})>0,

which is strictly positive as long as at least one specific path

s_{0},\overline{s}^{(2)},\dotsc,\overline{s}^{(t-1)},s
has strictly positive
probability, which is the hypothesis made by contradiction:

\displaystyle(p^{\pi_{E}})^{t}(s|s_{0})>0

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\exists\overline{s}^{(2)},\overline{s}^{(3)},\dotsc,\overline{s}^{(t-1)}:\;p^{\pi_{E}}(\overline{s}^{(2)}|s_{0})p^{\pi_{E}}(\overline{s}^{(3)}|\overline{s}^{(2)})\cdot\dotsc\cdot p^{\pi_{E}}(s|\overline{s}^{(t-1)})>0.

But this means that all these probabilities are strictly positive:

\displaystyle p^{\pi_{E}}(\overline{s}^{(i)}|\overline{s}^{(i-1)})>0\;\forall i\in\{2,t\},

where
\overline{s}^{(1)}=s_{0},\overline{s}^{(t)}=s
.

Now, if all the states
\overline{s}^{(i)}
are distinct, then
t\leq S
,
because there are at most
S
states. Otherwise, there exist
i\neq i^{\prime}
such
that
\overline{s}^{(i)}=\overline{s}^{(i^{\prime})}
, and so we can get rid of all
the terms
p^{\pi_{E}}(\overline{s}^{(j)}|\overline{s}^{(j-1)})
for

j\in\{i+1,i^{\prime}\}
. Then, iterating this reasoning, we will end up with all
distinct states, representing a path long
t^{\prime}\leq S
for which

(p^{\pi_{E}})^{t^{\prime}}(s|s_{0})>0
.

This concludes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to upper bound the sample complexity necessity for estimating

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
:

Lemma F.3
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and
H\geq S
. If
\pi_{E}
is deterministic, then it holds that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\Big(\widehat{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\wedge\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}=\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\Big)\geq 1-\delta,

with a number of trajectories:

\displaystyle N\leq\frac{\log\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}|}{\delta}}{p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}.

Proof.

If
\pi_{E}
is deterministic, then observe that
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}

implies condition
\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}=\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
. Thus, we will focus on the
former equality. We bound the opposite event:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\Big(\widehat{\mathcal{S}}\neq\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\Big(\exists s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}},\forall i\in\llbracket N\rrbracket,\not\exists h\in\llbracket H\rrbracket:s_{h}^{i}=s\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{\makebox[0.0pt]{(1)}}}{\leq}\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\Big(\forall i\in\llbracket N\rrbracket,\not\exists h\in\llbracket H\rrbracket:s_{h}^{i}=s\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{\makebox[0.0pt]{(2)}}}{=}\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\Big(\not\exists h\in\llbracket H\rrbracket:s_{h}^{i}=s\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}\bigg(1-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\Big(\exists h\in\llbracket H\rrbracket:s_{h}^{i}=s\Big)\bigg)

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{\makebox[0.0pt]{(3)}}}{\leq}\sum\limits_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}(1-p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}})

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}|(1-p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}})^{N},

where at (1) we apply a union bound, at (2) we use the fact that different
trajectories are independent of each other, at (3) we lower bound

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\Big(\exists h\in\llbracket H\rrbracket:s_{h}^{i}=s\Big)
using

p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
, which is
>0
because of
H\geq S
and Lemma

F.1
.

Forcing this probability to be at most
\delta
, and solving w.r.t.
N
, we get:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}|(1-p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}})^{N}\leq\delta

\displaystyle\iff N\log(1-p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}})\leq-\log\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}|}{\delta}

\displaystyle\iff N\geq\frac{\log\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}|}{\delta}}{\log\frac{1}{1-p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}}.

By using that, for any
x\in(0,1)
, we have:
\log\frac{1}{1-x}\geq x
, we get
the result.
∎

There are some interesting observations that can be done on this sample
complexity result. Specifically, note that there is a trade-off in the choice of

H
. If
H
is large, then
p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
is also large, and so
N

gets small. However, at the same time, increasing
H
we are using more samples
since the trajectories are longer. The number of samples is:

\displaystyle NH\leq\frac{H}{p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}\log\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}|}{\delta}.

This expression can be optimized by
H
depending on the problem at stake, by
solving:

\displaystyle\argmin\limits_{H\in\mathbb{N}:H\geq S}\frac{H}{p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}\log\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}|}{\delta}=\argmin\limits_{H\in\mathbb{N}:H\geq S}\frac{H}{p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}.

Finally, we are ready to prove the main theorem.
See
5.1

Proof.

Simply, apply Lemma
F.3
, and realize
from the definition of
r^{\text{OPT}}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
that

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\wedge\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}=\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}

suffices for having
\widehat{r}^{\text{OPT}}=r^{\text{OPT}}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
.
∎

F.2
Proof of Theorem
5.2

We begin by proving how the error in the estimation of the policy propagates to
the estimation of the centroid:

Lemma F.4
.

Assume that
\min_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\pi_{E}(a|s)\geq\pi_{\min}^{\prime}
. Let
\widehat{\pi}

be the estimate of policy computed in Algorithm
2
.
Then, it holds that:

\displaystyle\|\widehat{r}^{\text{MCE}}-r^{\text{MCE}}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\pi_{\min}}\|\widehat{\pi}-\pi_{E}\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

We can write:

\displaystyle\|\widehat{r}^{\text{MCE}}-r^{\text{MCE}}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max\limits_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\Big|\widehat{r}^{\text{MCE}}(s,a)-r^{\text{MCE}}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}(s,a)\Big|

\displaystyle=\max\limits_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\Big|\log\widehat{\pi}(a|s)-\log\pi_{E}(a|s)\Big|

\displaystyle=\max\limits_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\Big|\log\frac{\widehat{\pi}(a|s)}{\pi_{E}(a|s)}\Big|

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{\makebox[0.0pt]{(1)}}}{\leq}\max\limits_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\frac{\big|\widehat{\pi}(a|s)-\pi_{E}(a|s)\big|}{\min\{\widehat{\pi}(a|s),\pi_{E}(a|s)\}}

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{\makebox[0.0pt]{(2)}}}{\leq}\frac{1}{\pi_{\min}^{\prime}}\max\limits_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\big|\widehat{\pi}(a|s)-\pi_{E}(a|s)\big|,

where at (1) we use that
\log(1+x)\leq x
for
x\geq 0
. Indeed, when

\widehat{\pi}(a|s)\geq\pi_{E}(a|s)
, then
\widehat{\pi}(a|s)/\pi_{E}(a|s)\geq 1
, and so:
\log\frac{\widehat{\pi}(a|s)}{\pi_{E}(a|s)}=\log\Big(1+\frac{\widehat{\pi}(a|s)}{\pi_{E}(a|s)}-1\Big)\leq(\widehat{\pi}(a|s)-\pi_{E}(a|s))/\pi_{E}(a|s)
. Doing similarly in the opposite
case, we get the minimum at denominator. At (2) we use that

\min_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\pi_{E}(a|s)\geq\pi_{\min}^{\prime}
by hypothesis and the definition
of
\widehat{\pi}
.
∎

The next step consists in upper bounding the estimation error of
\pi_{E}
:

Lemma F.5
.

Let
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
and assume that

\min_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\pi_{E}(a|s)\geq\pi_{\min}^{\prime}
. Then, it holds that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}\Big(\|\widehat{\pi}-\pi_{E}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon\Big)\geq 1-\delta,

with a number of trajectories:

\displaystyle N\leq\frac{16\log\frac{4SA}{\delta}\log\frac{2SA}{\delta}}{\epsilon^{2}p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}.

Proof.

For all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, we can write:

\displaystyle\big|\widehat{\pi}(a|s)-\pi_{E}(a|s)\big|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\max\Big\{\pi_{\min}^{\prime},\frac{N(s,a)}{\max\{1,N(s)\}}\Big\}-\pi_{E}(a|s)\bigg|

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{\makebox[0.0pt]{(1)}}}{=}\bigg|\max\Big\{\pi_{\min}^{\prime},\frac{N(s,a)}{\max\{1,N(s)\}}\Big\}-\max\Big\{\pi_{\min}^{\prime},\pi_{E}(a|s)\Big\}\bigg|

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{\makebox[0.0pt]{(2)}}}{\leq}\max\bigg\{\big|\pi_{\min}^{\prime}-\pi_{\min}^{\prime}\big|,\Big|\frac{N(s,a)}{\max\{1,N(s)\}}-\pi_{E}(a|s)\Big|\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\Big|\frac{N(s,a)}{\max\{1,N(s)\}}-\pi_{E}(a|s)\Big|,

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{\makebox[0.0pt]{(3)}}}{\leq}\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{4}{\delta}}{\max\{1,N(s)\}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{\makebox[0.0pt]{(4)}}}{\leq}\sqrt{\frac{16\log\frac{4}{\delta}\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{Np^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}},

where at (1) we use that
\min_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\pi_{E}(a|s)\geq\pi_{\min}^{\prime}
by
hypothesis, at (2) we use that the max operator is 1-Lipschitz, at (3) we use
that, when
N(s)=0
, then
|\pi_{E}(a|s)|\leq 1\leq\sqrt{2\log\frac{4}{\delta}}
,
while when
N(s)\geq 1
, we apply the double-sided Azuma-Hoeffding’s inequality
(that must be applied because
N(s)
is a random variable itself), which holds
w.p.
1-\delta/2
. At (4) we note that each
N(s)\sim\text{Bin}(N,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}(\bigcup_{t=0}^{H}\{s_{t}=s\}))
is Binomially distributed, so we
can apply Lemma A.1 in
[
52
]
w.p.
\delta/2
to get
that:
\frac{1}{\max\{1,N(s)\}}\leq\frac{8\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{N\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}(\bigcup_{t=0}^{H}\{s_{t}=s\})}\leq\frac{8\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{Np^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}
, where we used the definition of

p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
in Eq. (
19
).

By applying a union bound, we have that the above bound for
s,a
holds w.p.

1-\delta
. In addition, the error above is bounded by
\epsilon
as long as:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{16\log\frac{4}{\delta}\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{Np^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}}\leq\epsilon\iff N\geq\frac{16\log\frac{4}{\delta}\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{\epsilon^{2}p^{\min,H}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}.

The proof is concluded through the application of a union bound over all the

(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
.
∎

We conclude with the main result:
See
5.2

Proof.

Combining Lemma
F.4

∎

We now show how generalization to new environments and constraints is carried
out by other approaches in this specific problem setting.

Policy transfer.

The first “baseline” that we consider here is
\pi^{\text{BC}}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}

[
3
]
, which is defined as:

\displaystyle\pi^{\text{BC}}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}(a|s)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\pi_{E}(a|s)&\text{if }s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}},\\
\frac{1}{A}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This policy is called “BC” because it represents the result of Behavioral
Cloning
[
34
,
32
]
on
\pi_{E}
.
As shown in Fig.
11
(a)-(b), directly transferring this policy
to the new environment
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
might induce quite meaningless behaviors in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

regardless of whether
s_{0}^{\prime}\neq s_{0}
(Fig.
11
(a)) or not (Fig.

11
(b)).
Moreover, note that this policy cannot even be applied in general when there are
constraints in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, because it might not satisfy them.

A “best-case” approach.

As a second “baseline”, we consider the policy
\pi^{\prime}
obtained after carrying
out (constrained) planning in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
with an
arbitrary
reward
r
in the
feasible set
\mathcal{R}^{\text{OPT}}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
:

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}\in\argmax\limits_{\pi^{\prime\prime}\in\Pi_{c,k}}V^{\pi^{\prime\prime}}(s_{0}^{\prime};p^{\prime},r).

We have constructed this reward starting from the knowledge of the policy

\pi_{E}
(see Fig.
2
(a)) in its support

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}
as follows. First, we have sampled at random a deterministic
policy
\overline{\pi}
from
[\pi_{E}]_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M},\pi_{E}}}
. Next, based on Proposition
B.1
, we have sampled uniformly at random the values of

V\in[-1,+1]^{S}
and
A\in[-0.5,+0.5]^{S(A-1)}
(the numbers 1 and 0.5 are
meaningless), and constructed
r
as:

\displaystyle r(s,a)=V(s)-\gamma\sum\limits_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})-\begin{cases}0&\text{if }a=\overline{\pi}(s),\\
A(s,a)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that this approach is representative of all the “best-case” approaches
described in Section
3.2
.
Fig.
11
(c)-(d) show the resulting policy
\pi^{\prime}
in a new
environment
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
respectively in absence and presence of constraints. As clear
from the images, the policies
\pi^{\prime}
and
\pi_{E}
are rather uncorrelated.
