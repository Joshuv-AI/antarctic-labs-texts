Evolution of maxillofacial fractures before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic: an 8-year interrupted time series analysis from South-Western Romania
Authors: Horatiu Urechescu, Ancuta Banu, Nicoleta Gena Oncescu, Felicia Streian, Cristiana Cuzic, Marius Pricop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic altered trauma epidemiology worldwide. Although reductions in maxillofacial trauma during lockdown periods have been widely reported, longitudinal changes in trauma characteristics and post-pandemic recovery remain insufficiently explored. This study evaluated temporal changes in maxillofacial trauma volume and injury characteristics across pre-COVID, COVID, and post-COVID periods using an interrupted time series (ITS) framework.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary maxillofacial surgery center in Romania, including patients treated between December 2017 and November 2025. Cases were categorized into pre-COVID, COVID, and post-COVID phases. Variables included fracture patterns, trauma etiology, associated head and neck injuries, polytrauma, and treatment modality. Comparative analyses were performed using chi-square analyses with Bonferroni correction. ITS regression analysis was applied to assess temporal changes in monthly trauma volume.ResultsA total of 914 patients were included. ITS analysis suggested a temporary reduction in trauma volume during the COVID period, followed by post-pandemic recovery; however, several model parameters demonstrated borderline statistical significance and the model showed limited explanatory power. Descriptive analyses demonstrated persistent differences in trauma characteristics across study periods, including lower proportions of associated injuries and documented polytrauma in the post-COVID period compared with pre-pandemic values. ORIF procedures decreased during the COVID period and increased thereafter.ConclusionsThe COVID-19 pandemic was associated with a temporary reduction in maxillofacial trauma volume, followed by post-pandemic recovery. Although overall trauma volume recovered, trauma characteristics differed across study periods. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the variability of trauma epidemiology and the limitations of the ITS model. Further studies incorporating standardized severity measures and more advanced time-series methodologies are warranted.

Introduction

Maxillofacial trauma represents a significant public health concern worldwide, accounting for an important proportion of severe traumatic injuries. These lesions compromise essential oral and facial functions, including mastication, speech, respiration, and facial aesthetics, frequently involving the mandible and midfacial skeletal structures. Beyond their functional and psychosocial consequences, maxillofacial fractures require substantial healthcare resources and specialized surgical management within oral and maxillofacial surgery units.

The epidemiology of maxillofacial trauma is influenced by demographic characteristics, behavioral factors, and healthcare system organization, leading to considerable geographic and temporal variability in incidence and etiology. A clear understanding of epidemiological trends is essential for prevention strategies, early diagnosis, and appropriate allocation of therapeutic resources [1]. The multicenter prospective European Maxillofacial Trauma (EURMAT) study confirmed a shift in the epidemiology of maxillofacial trauma across Europe, with assault- and fall-related injuries surpassing those caused by road traffic accidents. The authors suggested that progressive population aging and stricter traffic and occupational safety legislation may have contributed to this change. Although males remain disproportionately affected, with an overall male-to-female ratio of 3.6:1, substantial variability between centers was observed. Mandibular fractures were reported as the most frequent injury type, followed by orbito-zygomatico-maxillary fractures, with condylar fractures being the most common mandibular subtype [2].

The COVID-19 pandemic introduced unprecedented social restrictions and healthcare system reorganization, including mobility limitations, quarantine measures, and redistribution of medical resources. These changes directly influenced the frequency and pattern of facial trauma. Published evidence suggests a general decrease in the incidence of mandibular fractures during the pandemic period. A recent meta-analysis including more than 100 studies and over 23,000 cases of maxillofacial trauma reported a reduction in the prevalence of mandibular fractures from 24% in the pre-pandemic period to 20% during the pandemic. At the same time, falls and interpersonal violence increased as etiological factors, from 23% to 30% and from 22% to 31%, respectively [3]. An analysis conducted across maxillofacial trauma units in Germany confirmed a reduction in trauma volume during peak restriction periods, with a predominance of midfacial fractures and a decrease of approximately 16% in mandibular fractures during the pandemic, followed by a relative increase in the post-pandemic phase, although remaining below pre-pandemic levels [4].

In Romania, the pandemic was characterized by a state of emergency declared on March 16, 2020, followed by a prolonged state of alert until March 8, 2022, after which most restrictions were lifted. This clearly defined timeline offers a unique framework for analyzing changes in maxillofacial trauma across pre-pandemic (2018–March 2020), pandemic (April 2020–March 2022), and post-pandemic (March 2022 onward) periods. However, data from Romanian oral and maxillofacial surgery centers regarding long-term epidemiological trends across these three phases remain limited.

In Romania, oral and maxillofacial trauma care is primarily delivered through regional tertiary referral centers integrated within university-affiliated emergency hospitals. The Maxillofacial Surgery Clinic in Timisoara serves as a major referral unit for South-Western Romania, managing both isolated facial fractures and complex craniofacial trauma requiring multidisciplinary care. This regional referral structure provides a representative framework for evaluating longitudinal changes in maxillofacial trauma patterns across the pandemic and post-pandemic periods.

While numerous studies have reported fluctuations in trauma incidence during the COVID-19 pandemic, most have focused primarily on case volume and etiological distribution. Far fewer investigations have examined longitudinal changes in injury complexity, including associated head and neck injuries and polytrauma burden, across pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic phases. Moreover, evolving mobility patterns, including increased reliance on micromobility devices, may have contributed to shifts in trauma mechanisms in the post-restriction period. Interrupted Time Series (ITS) analysis provides a robust framework to distinguish temporary disruptions from sustained structural changes in epidemiological trends, yet its application in maxillofacial trauma research remains limited.

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate temporal trends in maxillofacial trauma volume before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic at a tertiary maxillofacial referral center in South-Western Romania using an interrupted time series (ITS) framework. Secondary objectives included assessing changes in trauma etiology, fracture patterns, associated injuries, polytrauma, and treatment strategies across the three study periods. By providing updated regional data, this study contributes to a better understanding of how major societal disruptions, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, may be associated with changes in the epidemiology, injury complexity, and surgical management of maxillofacial trauma.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A retrospective observational study was conducted at the Maxillofacial Surgery Clinic in Timisoara, a tertiary referral center for oral and maxillofacial trauma in South-Western Romania. The study included all consecutive patients diagnosed with maxillofacial fractures between December 2017 and November 2025. Data extraction and statistical analysis were performed during January and February 2026.

Study population

Inclusion criteria were patients with radiologically confirmed maxillofacial fractures admitted and treated in the department between December 2017 and November 2025.

Exclusion criteria comprised patients without imaging confirmation, pathological fractures unrelated to trauma, and cases with incomplete essential clinical data.

Patients were categorized into three predefined temporal periods according to the national COVID-19 timeline: pre-COVID (December 2017–15 March 2020), COVID period (16 March 2020–8 March 2022), and post-COVID (9 March 2022 – November 2025). One case was recorded on 31 December 2017, with hospitalization extending into January 2018, and was therefore included in the analysis. Period classification was derived from the date of hospital admission.

Data collection and variables

Data were retrospectively extracted from hospital electronic medical records, imaging archives, operative logs, and discharge summaries. All data were anonymized prior to analysis. Collected variables included demographic characteristics (sex and age), date of admission, etiological factors (road traffic accidents, bicycle/e-scooter injuries, assault/interpersonal violence, falls, sports-related injuries, work accidents, and other/unknown causes), fracture characteristics (main fracture type and side of injury), associated head and neck injuries (yes/no), polytrauma status (yes/no), COVID-19 status at admission (positive/negative/not tested), type of treatment (conservative management, open reduction and internal fixation [ORIF], or closed reduction), and length of hospital stay (number of days).

Main fracture types were categorized as mandibular fractures, zygomaticomaxillary complex (ZMC) fractures, Le Fort I–III fractures, naso-orbito-ethmoidal (NOE) fractures, orbital floor/medial wall fractures, maxillary alveolar fractures, combined/multiple fractures, and other fracture types. Side of injury was recorded as right, left, bilateral, or not applicable for midline fractures such as Le Fort and NOE fractures. Associated head and neck injuries were defined as concomitant traumatic lesions documented at admission in the head and neck region, including soft tissue lacerations, dental trauma, traumatic brain injury or concussion, orbital soft tissue injuries, cervical spine injuries, and other clinically recorded head and neck injuries accompanying the maxillofacial fracture. Polytrauma was defined as the presence of traumatic injuries involving at least one additional body region or organ system beyond the maxillofacial region, as documented in the admission records. It was recorded as a binary clinical variable.

Statistical analysis

Data were organized and subsequently imported into SPSS (version 26, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for statistical analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Bonferroni correction was applied for post-hoc pairwise comparisons, with an adjusted alpha level of 0.0167.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Continuous variables were reported as mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values, while categorical variables were expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages.

Given the unequal duration of the three study periods (27, 24, and 44 months), mean monthly case counts were additionally calculated to facilitate standardized descriptive comparison of trauma volume across periods.

To evaluate temporal changes in trauma volume, an interrupted time series (ITS) regression analysis was conducted using 96 monthly observations covering the period from December 2017 to November 2025. Monthly case counts were aggregated from individual-level data and grouped by calendar month. The regression model included a sequential time variable, indicator variables for the first interruption (March 16, 2020) and the second interruption (March 8, 2022), and corresponding post-interruption time variables. The post-interruption slope for the second interruption represents the change relative to the COVID-period slope rather than the baseline pre-COVID trend, consistent with standard ITS methodology [5]. A linear regression model was fitted using monthly case counts as the dependent variable. The model did not include adjustment for potential confounding factors such as seasonal variation or broader socioeconomic influences, which may contribute to unexplained variability in trauma volume and limit the precision of the estimated effects.

Differences in categorical variables related to trauma characteristics across the three study periods were assessed using chi-square analyses. When overall comparisons were statistically significant, post-hoc pairwise analyses with Bonferroni correction were performed to identify specific group differences.

Normality of distribution for length of hospital stay was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Since the distribution significantly deviated from normality in all three periods, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to compare hospitalization duration between groups.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Municipal Emergency Hospital Timisoara, Romania (approval number E-5262/17.12.2025). The study was conducted using retrospective anonymized data. No direct patient contact or intervention was involved. All patients had previously provided informed consent for the use of their anonymized clinical and radiological data for research purposes, in accordance with institutional and national regulations.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 914 patients with radiologically confirmed maxillofacial fractures were included in the study. Of these, 323 cases (35.3%) were recorded during the pre-COVID period, 181 cases (19.8%) during the COVID period, and 410 cases (44.9%) in the post-COVID period.

Male patients accounted for 82.2% of the overall cohort (n = 751), while females represented 17.8% (n = 163). The predominance of male patients remained consistent across all three periods (pre-COVID: 83.3%; COVID: 80.7%; post-COVID: 82.0%) (Table 1).

The mean age of the study population was 38.45 years (SD = 16.61), with a median age of 36 years and a range between 13 and 96 years.

Bicycle and e-scooter-related injuries showed differences in proportional distribution across study periods (χ²(2) = 19.069, p < 0.001). The number of cases increased from 11 in the pre-COVID period and 12 during COVID to 35 in the post-COVID phase. Post-hoc analysis showed significant differences between pre-COVID and post-COVID (χ² = 12.522, p < 0.001), as well as between COVID and post-COVID (χ² = 11.255, p = 0.001), while no difference was found between pre-COVID and COVID (p = 0.835).

Assault and interpersonal violence also showed differences in distribution between study periods (χ²(2) = 52.995, p < 0.001). The number of cases decreased from 157 pre-pandemic to 71 during COVID, followed by an increase to 188 in the post-COVID period. Post-hoc comparisons revealed significant differences between pre-COVID and COVID (χ² = 32.439, p < 0.001) and between COVID and post-COVID (χ² = 52.853, p < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was observed between pre-COVID and post-COVID (p = 0.095).

Falls occurring at home or in public settings similarly showed differences in distribution (χ²(2) = 26.923, p < 0.001). Cases declined from 76 pre-COVID to 36 during COVID and increased to 96 post-COVID. Significant differences were confirmed between pre-COVID and COVID (χ² = 14.286, p < 0.001) and between COVID and post-COVID (χ² = 27.273, p < 0.001), while pre- and post-COVID periods did not differ significantly (p = 0.127).

No statistically significant differences were identified for road traffic accidents (χ² = 1.357, p = 0.507), sport-related injuries (χ² = 2.976, p = 0.226), work accidents (χ² = 0.818, p = 0.366), or other/unknown causes (χ² = 0.208, p = 0.902).

ZMC fractures also showed significant variation across periods (χ² = 11.886, p = 0.003). The number of cases was 83 pre-COVID, 59 during COVID, and 103 post-COVID. After Bonferroni correction, only the comparison between COVID and post-COVID remained statistically significant (χ² = 11.951, p = 0.001).

NOE fractures also differed significantly between periods (χ² = 31.973, p < 0.001), with 47 cases pre-COVID, 9 during COVID, and 18 post-COVID. Post-hoc analysis confirmed significant reductions from pre-COVID to COVID (χ² = 25.786, p < 0.001) and from pre-COVID to post-COVID (χ² = 12.938, p < 0.001), while no significant difference was found between COVID and post-COVID (p = 0.083).

Le Fort I–III fractures, orbital floor/medial wall fractures, maxillary alveolar fractures, combined fractures, and other fracture types did not demonstrate statistically significant differences across periods (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The present study evaluated the eight-year evolution of maxillofacial fractures in a tertiary referral center from South-Western Romania across pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic periods. ITS analysis suggested a temporary disruption in trauma volume during the COVID-19 period, followed by post-pandemic recovery. However, given the multifactorial nature of maxillofacial trauma epidemiology and the absence of adjustment for seasonal variation or autocorrelation, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Although overall trauma volume recovered after the pandemic period, descriptive analyses demonstrated persistent differences in trauma characteristics across study phases, including lower proportions of documented associated injuries and documented polytrauma compared with pre-pandemic values. Treatment patterns, particularly ORIF procedures, mirrored these epidemiological changes. Overall, the findings suggest both transient temporal changes in trauma volume and sustained differences in trauma characteristics across the study periods, while highlighting the multifactorial nature of maxillofacial trauma epidemiology.

Multiple studies have documented a reduction in maxillofacial trauma during periods of lockdown and social restrictions. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis identified a consistent decline in maxillofacial trauma incidence across multiple countries during the COVID-19 period [3]. Analyses from the United States demonstrated decreased trauma volume during strict restrictions [6, 7], while German national data confirmed reductions in trauma-related surgical activity during pandemic waves [8]. Similar patterns were also reported in other settings, with additional investigations describing marked shifts in trauma trends and injury patterns during the pandemic period [9]. Similar findings were described in France through a multicenter study [10] and in Italy [11].

Our findings are broadly consistent with these previously reported pandemic-related trauma trends. However, in contrast to many reports describing gradual normalization after restrictions were lifted [4, 9], our cohort demonstrated post-pandemic recovery in trauma volume following the reduction observed during the COVID period. ITS analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant abrupt level change at the post-COVID transition, supporting the interpretation of a gradual normalization rather than a clear structural shift.

The ITS findings likely reflect the multifactorial nature of maxillofacial trauma epidemiology, which is influenced by multiple time-varying factors including seasonal variation, mobility patterns, alcohol consumption, socioeconomic conditions, and healthcare access. Consequently, the ITS findings should be interpreted as indicative of a temporary disruption in trauma patterns during the pandemic period rather than definitive evidence of a sustained structural change attributable exclusively to COVID-19-related restrictions.

One of the notable findings of this study was a lower proportion of associated injuries and polytrauma during the COVID and post-COVID periods compared with pre-pandemic values. Although case numbers increased post-pandemic, the proportion of patients with associated injuries remained lower than pre-pandemic values, while the reduction in polytrauma should be interpreted cautiously given that only the pre-COVID versus post-COVID comparison reached statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons. While numerous investigations have evaluated fluctuations in trauma volume, fewer studies have examined longitudinal changes in injury severity. Possible explanations for these observed differences in injury severity patterns may include changes in referral patterns, triage protocols, and inter-hospital transfer practices during and after the pandemic period. However, given the retrospective nature of the study, a systematic change in clinical documentation practices or coding behavior across the observation period cannot be excluded as a contributing factor, particularly regarding the recording of associated head and neck injuries. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the study was not specifically designed to distinguish true epidemiological changes from potential variations in documentation practices.

Recent studies have incorporated objective severity metrics such as Injury Severity Score (ISS) and Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) when analyzing pandemic-related shifts in craniomaxillofacial trauma [12, 13]. Marchant et al. reported variations in both incidence and severity indices following the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 [12], and similar methodological approaches were used in analyses of domestic violence-related trauma [13]. Dawoud et al. also assessed maxillofacial injuries during lockdown in a Level I trauma center, including evaluation of polytrauma patterns [14]. In contrast to studies primarily focused on short-term pandemic effects, our analysis spans pre-, intra-, and post-pandemic phases and suggests that, although trauma volume rebounded, the proportion of documented polytrauma cases remained below pre-pandemic values; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the limited post-hoc significance pattern and the absence of standardized severity scores.

Changes in epidemiology were paralleled by modifications in treatment patterns. ORIF procedures decreased significantly during the COVID period and increased post-pandemic, consistent with reports from European and North American centers documenting reduced operative volume during pandemic peaks [6–8, 10]. This increase likely reflects restoration of surgical capacity alongside increased trauma exposure. Length of hospital stay remained stable across periods, in line with previously reported European epidemiological data [2].

Significant shifts were also observed in trauma etiology, particularly regarding interpersonal violence, falls, and micromobility-related injuries. Reductions in certain categories of interpersonal trauma during periods of restricted social interaction have been described in several cohorts [6, 7], although domestic violence-related injuries demonstrated distinct patterns in some studies [13]. The increase in bicycle and e-scooter injuries observed in the post-pandemic period in our cohort is consistent with emerging literature describing a growing burden of micromobility-related craniofacial trauma in urban environments [15, 16]. The dataset did not allow consistent differentiation between conventional bicycle-related injuries and e-scooter-related trauma, which may limit the interpretation of micromobility trends observed in the post-COVID period.

Pre-pandemic epidemiological studies, including the EURMAT multicenter project, identified assault, falls, and road traffic accidents as leading etiologies of maxillofacial trauma across Europe [2]. Regional analyses, such as the Greek cohort described by Kostakis et al. [1], similarly reported a predominance of male patients and a peak incidence among young adults. Our demographic findings remain consistent with these established European patterns; however, the dynamic fluctuations observed across the pandemic phases highlight the importance of temporal context when interpreting trauma epidemiology.

The distribution of fracture types observed in the present study is broadly consistent with previously reported epidemiological patterns of maxillofacial trauma. Mandibular fractures represented the most frequent injury type throughout all study periods and demonstrated an increasing relative proportion across the three periods, particularly in the post-COVID phase. This trend may reflect changes in trauma mechanisms following the relaxation of mobility restrictions, particularly the rebound in interpersonal violence and the increased use of micromobility devices, both of which have been associated with mandibular injury patterns in recent reports.

In contrast, NOE fractures showed a marked decrease during the pandemic period compared with the pre-COVID phase. These injuries are typically associated with high-energy trauma mechanisms, suggesting that pandemic-related restrictions may have reduced exposure to such mechanisms. Reduced mobility, decreased road traffic, and changes in social activity patterns during restriction periods may have contributed to this observation, although these interpretations remain speculative. ZMC fractures remained consistently among the most frequent injury types across all periods, which aligns with findings from large European epidemiological studies of facial trauma. Finally, the overall low frequency of Le Fort fractures in our cohort suggests that the majority of injuries were produced by trauma mechanisms of moderate energy rather than severe multisystem trauma mechanisms.

The rebound in trauma volume highlights the importance of adaptable resource allocation within oral and maxillofacial surgery units. Temporary reductions in trauma burden during public health crises may be followed by increased demand. Furthermore, the persistently lower proportion of polytrauma cases observed in the post-COVID period may reflect evolving patterns of injury complexity, with potential implications for operative planning, multidisciplinary coordination, and training priorities.

This study benefits from a large sample size, extended eight-year observation period, clearly defined temporal phases, and the application of ITS analysis. The use of post-hoc statistical correction further supports the robustness of the comparisons. By integrating pre-, intra-, and post-pandemic periods within a single longitudinal framework, this study provides additional insight into medium-term trends beyond the short-term effects commonly reported in the literature.

Limitations include the retrospective single-center design and the potential for information bias inherent to this type of study. The ITS model did not include explicit adjustment for seasonal variation or autocorrelation, which may contribute to unexplained variability in trauma volume and limit the precision of slope estimates. Because the study periods differed in duration, overall trauma volume comparisons were interpreted descriptively using standardized mean monthly case counts rather than formal inferential comparisons of aggregated case totals. The study also did not incorporate standardized trauma severity scores such as Injury Severity Score (ISS) or Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS), which may limit the precision of injury severity assessment. In this context, polytrauma was recorded as a binary clinical variable based on documentation in the medical records, rather than using validated scoring systems, and should therefore be interpreted as a proxy for multisystem trauma rather than a precise measure of overall injury severity. Furthermore, the retrospective design raises the possibility that changes in clinical documentation practices, coding behavior, or reporting completeness across the observation period may have influenced the recorded frequency of associated head and neck injuries. In particular, the persistently lower proportion of associated injuries observed during both the COVID and post-COVID periods may partially reflect variations in documentation or referral practices rather than a true sustained epidemiological reduction in injury complexity. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Although comparisons were made with international cohorts [3, 4, 6–8, 10–16], the single-center design and regional variability in trauma patterns and healthcare system organization may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings. Finally, the observational and primarily descriptive nature of the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between pandemic-related factors and observed changes in trauma patterns.

Conclusions

The COVID-19 pandemic was associated with a temporary reduction in maxillofacial trauma volume, followed by post-pandemic recovery in trauma volume. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the variability in trauma volume and the limited explanatory power of the ITS model.

Although overall case volume appeared to recover, the proportion of documented associated injuries remained lower in the post-COVID period. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the potential influence of variations in documentation practices, the absence of standardized severity metrics, and the limited post-hoc significance pattern observed for polytrauma analyses.

Taken together, the results suggest that large-scale societal disruptions such as the COVID-19 pandemic may influence both trauma patterns and injury characteristics. These findings highlight the importance of adaptable resource planning within oral and maxillofacial surgery services, while underscoring the need for further studies incorporating more robust severity metrics and advanced time-series modeling approaches.
