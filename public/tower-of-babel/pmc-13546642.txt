Selection of an Appropriate Tool for Assessing Geriatric Frailty in Clinical Practice: A Narrative Review
Authors: Samuel Sládeček, Eva Topinková, Monika Laššánová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrailty is a clinically significant geriatric syndrome characterized by reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors, resulting in a higher risk of adverse health outcomes such as functional decline, hospitalization, institutionalization, and mortality. Its early identification is essential for risk stratification, care planning, and individualized intervention. As no universal biomarker of frailty exists, its assessment relies on standardized clinical assessment instruments. However, the available tools are not interchangeable, and their optimal use depends on the clinical setting, the purpose of assessment, and organizational capacity.MethodsA narrative review was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science, supplemented by manual screening of reference lists of key articles, methodological papers, and international recommendations. The literature search was current to March 2026. Ten frailty assessment instruments were included based on predefined eligibility criteria, including external validation in large cohorts, demonstrated prognostic performance in more than one independent study, or endorsement in international geriatric guidelines: the FRAIL scale (Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illnesses, and Loss of weight), the Study of Osteoporotic Fractures (SOF) index, the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), the Program of Research to Integrate Services for the Maintenance of Autonomy 7-item questionnaire (PRISMA-7), the Vulnerable Elders Survey-13 (VES-13), the Fried frailty phenotype, the Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI), the Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI), the Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS), and the Frailty Index derived from Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (FI-CGA).FindingsThe reviewed instruments differ in their conceptual background, including phenotypic, deficit-based, and multidomain approaches, as well as in assessed domains, time requirements, and staffing demands. They are therefore not directly interchangeable. Based on this synthesis, a stepwise, context-oriented pathway for instrument selection is proposed. Brief screening tools such as PRISMA-7 or the FRAIL scale may be suitable at first contact in outpatient and community care; the CFS may support rapid clinical stratification in acute hospital and emergency settings; multidomain instruments such as the EFS may be appropriate when a broader clinical overview is required but full comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) is not feasible; and CGA supplemented by a deficit accumulation index such as the FI-CGA may be most appropriate in specialized geriatric care and individualized management planning.ConclusionThe stepwise pathway proposed in this review represents a conceptual framework synthesized from the available literature rather than a prospectively validated clinical protocol. Its purpose is to support clinicians in selecting an appropriate frailty assessment instrument for a defined clinical situation. Prospective evaluation across different healthcare settings is needed before the pathway can be regarded as an evidence-based protocol for routine practice.Keywords: frailty, frail elderly, geriatric assessment, aged, mass screening, health services for the aged

Introduction

Frailty represents a multifactorial geriatric syndrome characterized by reduced physiological reserve and a diminished capacity of the organism to adapt to stressors. Clinically, it is a state of increased vulnerability in which even relatively minor stressors—such as infection, surgery, immobilization, or changes in pharmacotherapy—may lead to disproportionately severe adverse outcomes, including functional decline, hospitalization, or mortality.1,2

Frailty is highly prevalent among older adults, although reported estimates vary according to the population studied and the operational definition applied. In community-dwelling older adults, a large meta-analysis of population-level studies reported a pooled prevalence of approximately 12% when physical frailty measures were used and approximately 24% when a frailty index based on deficit accumulation was applied.3 Prevalence rises markedly across higher-acuity settings: among geriatric hospital inpatients, pooled estimates indicate frailty in approximately 47% and prefrailty in a further approximately 26%,4 whereas in nursing homes and long-term care facilities the pooled prevalence of frailty approaches 50%.5 These figures underscore both the scale of the problem and the need for assessment strategies appropriate to each care setting.

The vulnerability that characterizes frailty reflects a cumulative, multisystem decline in physiological reserve rather than a single organ deficit.6 Among the principal contributing mechanisms are sarcopenia—the age-related loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength—chronic low-grade systemic inflammation (often termed “inflammageing”), and neuroendocrine dysregulation, including alterations in cortisol, insulin-like growth factor-1, and sex-steroid signalling.6,7 Chronic elevation of circulating inflammatory mediators has been linked mechanistically to reduced anabolic signalling, increased catabolism, and progression of frailty and its adverse outcomes.7 Despite these advances in understanding, no universal laboratory or imaging biomarker of frailty has yet been established; its identification therefore remains primarily based on clinical assessment and the use of standardized instruments.

Undetected and unmanaged frailty carries substantial consequences for both patients and health systems. Frailty is a strong, independent predictor of adverse outcomes including falls, disability, recurrent hospitalization, institutionalization, and mortality.8 It is also associated with markedly higher healthcare utilization and expenditure: longitudinal analyses have demonstrated a graded increase in healthcare costs with increasing frailty severity,9 and frailty independently increases the use of primary care, emergency, and inpatient services.10 Because a substantial part of this burden may be mitigated through earlier identification and targeted intervention, timely and setting-appropriate frailty assessment represents an important clinical and public health priority.8

The heterogeneity of frailty definitions and the absence of a single diagnostic biomarker have led to the development of several conceptual models of its assessment, which can be broadly categorized into three principal approaches. The phenotypic model (biological) conceptualizes frailty as a biologically grounded clinical syndrome defined by the presence of physical criteria such as weakness, slowness, low physical activity, exhaustion, and unintentional weight loss. The deficit accumulation model (mathematical) defines frailty as the result of the cumulative burden of health deficits; frailty severity is expressed by the Frailty Index as the ratio of present deficits to their total number, thereby quantifying the degree of biological vulnerability.11 In contrast, the multidomain model (holistic) conceptualizes frailty as the interaction of physical, cognitive, psychological, and social factors; this approach underlies the clinical and multidimensional screening instruments designed for the rapid identification of patients who may benefit from comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA).12

Individual instruments differ substantially in terms of time requirements, personnel and technical resources, and the breadth and depth of information obtained. While brief screening tools enable rapid preliminary identification of patients at risk, more comprehensive multidomain and deficit-based approaches provide a more detailed basis for individualized management within the framework of CGA.

In geriatric practice, frailty assessment serves three principal purposes: rapid identification of at-risk individuals through screening, risk stratification for adverse clinical outcomes, and detailed characterization of deficits as a basis for individualized management within CGA.12 The selection of a specific instrument must therefore reflect both the purpose of the assessment and the clinical context of its use. Brief screening tools are particularly suitable at first patient contact, in outpatient and community settings, in long-term care facilities, as well as in environments with limited time and personnel capacity, where they allow rapid evaluation without the need for technical equipment.13 In acute hospital or emergency settings, a rapid global clinical estimate of functional status and multimorbidity—such as the Clinical Frailty Scale—is especially relevant.1 Conversely, in situations where more time is available and the assessment is intended to support complex clinical decision-making, multidomain instruments or approaches derived from CGA may be more appropriate.14,15

From a practical perspective, it is essential to recognize that individual instruments are not fully interchangeable and that their optimal use depends on the specific clinical question and the context in which they are applied. Brief screening cannot replace CGA, and a detailed deficit-based index is not feasible in the setting of emergency triage.12 At the same time, it is not appropriate to use time-consuming multidomain assessments in every clinical situation when the primary objective is merely the rapid identification of patients at increased risk. In clinical practice, it is therefore crucial not only to recognize the presence of frailty but also to select an instrument that best corresponds to the purpose of assessment, the characteristics of the patient population, and the organizational capacities of the healthcare setting.

The aim of this review article is therefore to summarize selected instruments for the assessment of geriatric frailty, but primarily to analyze their use from the perspective of clinical practice and the selection of appropriate tools across different settings—from first patient contact through outpatient and community care to emergency departments, inpatient hospital environments, and CGA. The central focus is thus the question of when and which instrument should be used so that frailty assessment provides clinically meaningful information for the subsequent management of the geriatric patient.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This narrative review was designed to provide a clinically oriented synthesis of selected instruments for frailty assessment in older adults, with emphasis on their applicability across healthcare settings, including first patient contact, outpatient and community care, emergency departments, inpatient wards, perioperative assessment, long-term care, and comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA). The literature search was conducted between January and March 2026.

Data Sources and Search Strategy

Three electronic databases—PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science—were searched. The database search was supplemented by manual screening of the reference lists of key articles, methodological papers, and international recommendations addressing frailty assessment. Search terms were combined using Boolean operators as follows:

(“frailty” OR “frailty assessment” OR “frailty screening” OR “comprehensive geriatric assessment”) AND (“FRAIL scale” OR “Study of Osteoporotic Fractures index” OR “SOF index” OR “Clinical Frailty Scale” OR “PRISMA-7” OR “Vulnerable Elders Survey” OR “VES-13” OR “Fried frailty phenotype” OR “Tilburg Frailty Indicator” OR “Groningen Frailty Indicator” OR “Edmonton Frail Scale” OR “Frailty Index” OR “CGA-based Frailty Index”) AND (“older adults” OR “aged” OR “geriatric”).

In PubMed/MEDLINE, the corresponding MeSH terms included “Frailty”, “Frail Elderly”, “Geriatric Assessment”, “Aged”, and “Mass Screening”. Only English-language publications were considered. No lower date limit was applied in order to include the seminal publications describing individual instruments alongside contemporary validation studies; the search was current to March 2026.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible publications addressed the development, validation, psychometric properties, prognostic value, or clinical application of the selected frailty assessment instruments. Priority was given to original validation studies, large cohort analyses, consensus documents, international recommendations, and clinically oriented review articles. The target population was adults aged ≥65 years.

Publications were excluded if they did not address frailty assessment using one of the selected named instruments, focused exclusively on populations younger than 65 years, or were published in languages other than English.

Instrument Selection

Frailty instruments were selected if they met at least one predefined objective criterion: (a) external validation in large cohorts; (b) demonstrated prognostic performance in more than one independent study; or (c) endorsement, recommendation, or use in international geriatric guidelines, consensus documents, or clinically oriented recommendations. On this basis, the following ten instruments were included in the review: the FRAIL scale, Study of Osteoporotic Fractures index, Clinical Frailty Scale, PRISMA-7, Vulnerable Elders Survey-13, Fried frailty phenotype, Tilburg Frailty Indicator, Groningen Frailty Indicator, Edmonton Frail Scale, and the CGA-based Frailty Index (CGA-FI).

Selection Process and Synthesis

Study identification and selection were performed by the first author. Methodological decisions, interpretation of the evidence, and the final structure of the synthesis were discussed with the co-authors during manuscript preparation. The review followed a structured and transparent approach to identification and selection, but was conducted as a purposive, clinically oriented narrative synthesis. It did not apply the formal methodology of a systematic review or scoping review, such as the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement or the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). Therefore, dual independent screening, inter-rater agreement statistics, formal risk-of-bias assessment, GRADE evaluation, sensitivity analyses, and a PRISMA flow diagram were not applicable to the design and purpose of this review.

Overview of Frailty Instruments

Frail Questionnaire (Frail Scale)

The FRAIL questionnaire was developed as a simple clinical interview-based screening instrument within the framework of the International Academy on Nutrition and Aging (IANA) initiative and was defined in the work of John E. Morley et al. It has been in use at least since 2012 and was also tested in the African American Health cohort in St. Louis (USA).13,16 Conceptually, it represents a hybrid model of frailty that combines elements of functional and biological approaches with the concept of deficit accumulation, as it includes an item assessing the presence of multiple chronic illnesses (“Illness”) (Figure 1).17

Each positive item scores one point; a total score of 1–2 indicates prefrailty and a score of ≥3 indicates frailty. Adapted from Morley et al.13

The FRAIL scale consists of five components: Fatigue, Resistance (difficulty climbing stairs), Ambulation (difficulty walking), Illness (presence of multiple chronic conditions), and Loss of weight (unintentional weight loss) (Table 1).13 It is administered as a brief structured interview or questionnaire without the need for performance-based testing or specialized equipment.17 Administration typically takes approximately 3 minutes and may be performed by a nurse or another healthcare professional. Each positive item is scored with one point, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 5 points: a score of 0 corresponds to a robust status, 1–2 points indicate prefrailty, and 3–5 points indicate frailty.

The FRAIL scale has been validated in several population-based cohorts, including the African American Health study (N ≈ 998), where it demonstrated the ability to predict incident disability at 3- and 9-year follow-up and, together with the deficit accumulation index, also predicted 9-year mortality.17 Analyses of discriminative performance showed comparable—and in some outcomes even superior—accuracy compared with the Fried frailty phenotype and the SOF criteria. From a clinical perspective, the FRAIL scale is particularly suitable as a rapid first-line screening instrument in situations where it is necessary to identify patients at increased risk within a limited time frame, especially at first contact, in primary care, outpatient settings, community practice, internal medicine wards, and in preoperative assessment.17 Its principal advantages include simplicity, minimal organizational requirements, and good feasibility in environments without technical equipment.

A limitation of the FRAIL scale is its focus primarily on the physical dimension of frailty and the presence of comorbidity; the instrument does not include assessment of cognitive, psychological, or social domains of patient status.17 A positive result should therefore be interpreted as an indication for subsequent targeted evaluation—for example of functional status, mobility, cognition, and nutritional status—and for the formulation of a specific intervention plan.

SOF Frailty Index (Study of Osteoporotic Fractures Frailty Index)

The SOF frailty index represents a very brief frailty phenotype derived from the conceptual framework of the Study of Osteoporotic Fractures. It has been used and validated in the United States, including in the Osteoporotic Fractures in Men Study (MrOS) cohort comprising community-dwelling men aged ≥ 67 years across six clinical centers in the USA.18 Conceptually, it is a short physical frailty phenotype based on the assessment of functional performance, energetic vitality, and unintentional weight loss.18

The index consists of three components: unintentional weight loss, inability to rise from a chair five times without using the upper extremities (Figure 2), and self-reported low energy, defined by a negative response to the question: “Do you feel full of energy?”.18 The physical performance component evaluates the ability to stand up from a chair five times without the use of the upper limbs and is scored dichotomously (able/unable). For standardization of the test procedure, the use of a chair without armrests with a height of approximately 43–46 cm is recommended, with the arms crossed over the chest and full extension achieved in the standing position; although the original index primarily evaluates inability to complete the task, timing the performance with a stopwatch is frequently incorporated in clinical practice.19

Five Times Sit-to-Stand Test (FTSST). The participant is instructed to rise from a standard chair to a full standing position and return to the seated position five times as quickly as possible, with arms crossed over the chest. The time required to complete the five repetitions is recorded as a measure of lower-extremity strength and functional performance, and serves as a physical performance component of frailty assessment. Adapted from Muñoz-Bermejo et al.19

Each positive item is assigned one point, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 3 points. Based on the total score, three categories are distinguished: 0 points – robust, 1 point – prefrail, ≥ 2 points – frail. Administration typically requires only a few minutes and minimal equipment, mainly a chair and a safe space for test performance; when standardized measurement is applied, the use of a stopwatch is also recommended.18 The assessment is feasible even outside specialized geriatric settings.

The index was validated in the MrOS cohort (N = 3132), where it demonstrated discriminative ability comparable to the Fried frailty phenotype for predicting recurrent falls (area under the curve [AUC] approximately 0.63), disability (AUC approximately 0.68), nonvertebral fractures (AUC approximately 0.63), and mortality (AUC approximately 0.71–0.72 in age-adjusted models).18 Area-under-the-curve values within this range indicate low-to-moderate discriminative performance for predicting the above clinical outcomes.

In clinical practice, the SOF index is used primarily in outpatient and community care as a brief physically oriented screening instrument, particularly in situations where the aim is a rapid assessment of physical frailty with minimal equipment requirements. Its main strengths include short and simple administration and the fact that it does not require comparison with reference norms for grip strength or gait speed, which facilitates its application across different clinical environments.18

A limitation of the index is its exclusive focus on the physical dimension of frailty; it does not include assessment of cognitive, psychological, or social domains of patient status.17 Moreover, the performance-based component may be influenced by acute pain, orthopedic disease, or other musculoskeletal limitations, which may lead to overestimation of frailty severity. The SOF index therefore represents an appropriate instrument in situations requiring a brief and easily implementable assessment of physical frailty.

Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS)

The Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) was developed within the framework of the Canadian Study of Health and Aging in Canada and was originally published as a 7-point scale in 2005.1 It was later expanded to nine categories, and in 2020 version 2.0 was released with refined descriptive criteria.20 Conceptually, the CFS is based on a global clinical assessment of the patient derived primarily from the level of functional independence, the burden of comorbidity, and cognitive status.21 It is therefore a clinical judgment–based instrument, rather than a scale derived from the summation of individual items.

The scale comprises nine categories (1–9) describing a continuum from full vitality to terminal illness: 1 – very fit; 2 – well; 3 – managing well; 4 – vulnerable; 5 – mildly frail; 6 – moderately frail; 7 – severely frail; 8 – very severely frail; 9 – terminally ill (Figure 3).22

Administration consists of assigning one of the nine categories based on the patient’s baseline level of functional independence (activities of daily living/instrumental activities of daily living - ADL/IADL), comorbidity burden, and cognitive status—that is, according to the condition preceding the acute illness.20 Version 2.0 of the CFS additionally provides methodological guidance supporting standardization of assessment and improving inter-rater agreement. Scoring is performed by assigning a single category within the range from 1 to 9, with higher values reflecting greater frailty severity.22

In clinical practice, category 4 (vulnerable) is often interpreted as corresponding to prefrailty, whereas values ≥ 5 indicate frailty; categories 1–3 are generally considered to represent the absence of frailty.23 Administration of the scale is very rapid, typically requiring only several tens of seconds up to approximately one minute. It does not require any technical equipment and, after brief training, may be performed by physicians as well as nurses.24

In the original Canadian Study of Health and Aging cohort (N = 2305), the CFS showed a strong correlation with the deficit accumulation Frailty Index (r = 0.80) and demonstrated good discriminative ability for predicting mortality (AUC 0.77 for 18-month follow-up and 0.70 for approximately 70-month follow-up).1 Each one-category increase on the scale was associated in multivariable models with an increased risk of mortality and institutionalization.

In clinical practice, the CFS is used primarily in emergency departments, internal medicine and geriatric wards, intensive care units, and in preoperative assessment as a tool for rapid risk stratification and for communicating the degree of frailty within a multidisciplinary team.21 Its principal strengths include rapid administration, good prognostic validity, and the fact that it provides a shared clinical “language” understood across multidisciplinary settings.1

A limitation of the CFS is its dependence on the clinical judgment of the assessor, which may lead to inter-rater variability, particularly outside geriatric settings or when training is insufficient.20 In acute clinical situations, there is also a risk of overestimation of frailty severity if assessment is based on the patient’s current clinical condition rather than their baseline functional status prior to the acute illness.

Program of Research to Integrate Services for the Maintenance of Autonomy (PRISMA-7)

The PRISMA-7 questionnaire was developed in Canada (Québec; Sherbrooke) by Michel Raîche, Réjean Hébert, and Marie-France Dubois and published in 2008 in Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics.12 It is a case-finding screening instrument designed to identify individuals with more significant disability and, in clinical practice, also those at increased risk of frailty who should be referred for more detailed geriatric assessment.12

The instrument consists of seven binary (yes/no) questions addressing age over 85 years, male sex, health problems limiting usual activities, the need for regular assistance, health problems requiring the individual to remain at home, availability of a close person in case of need, and regular use of mobility aids (cane, walker, or wheelchair). Each “yes” response is assigned one point, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 7 points.25

In the original validation study, a cut-off value of ≥ 3 points was established for identifying significant disability.12 In clinical practice, scores of 0–2 are interpreted as indicating low risk, whereas scores ≥ 3 indicate high risk and the need for comprehensive geriatric assessment.26 Administration typically requires approximately 1–2 minutes, does not require any technical equipment, and is suitable for use in both outpatient and community settings.27

In the original validation study including 594 community-dwelling individuals, the cut-off ≥ 3 demonstrated a sensitivity of 78.3% and a specificity of 74.7% for identifying moderate-to-severe disability.12 In acute care settings, PRISMA-7 may be associated with indicators of adverse clinical outcomes; however, its prognostic accuracy for certain short-term outcomes may be limited by the heterogeneity of acute patient populations.25

In clinical practice, PRISMA-7 is primarily used as a rapid screening instrument for identifying individuals who should be referred for comprehensive geriatric assessment, particularly in outpatient and community settings.12 Its principal strengths include exceptional simplicity, very rapid administration, and the ability to capture a social dimension of vulnerability through the item addressing the availability of support.26 A limitation of the instrument is that the result does not provide a detailed explanation of the underlying causes of frailty nor structured information across individual domains. For planning targeted interventions, a more comprehensive geriatric assessment is therefore required.12

Vulnerable Elders Survey-13 (VES-13)

The VES-13 was developed in the United States based on analyses of nationally representative data from the Medicare Current Beneficiary Survey by D. Saliba et al and was published in 2001 in the Journal of the American Geriatrics Society.28 It is a functionally oriented prognostic screening instrument designed to identify older adults at increased risk of death or functional decline. Conceptually, the instrument is derived from the construct of vulnerability, and therefore does not represent a classical frailty phenotype but rather a clinically useful proxy indicator of increased geriatric risk.28

The questionnaire consists of 13 items with a total score ranging from 0 to 10 points and includes four principal domains: age, self-rated health, physical capability, and need for assistance with everyday activities.29 Age is scored according to categories (≤ 74 years, 75–84 years, and ≥ 85 years), with higher age categories assigned higher scores. Self-rated health is considered a risk indicator when reported as fair or poor. The physical capability domain includes difficulties with stooping or kneeling, lifting objects weighing approximately 4.5 kg, reaching above shoulder level, grasping small objects, walking a distance of 400 meters, and performing heavy housework; this domain contributes a maximum of 2 points. Functional limitations include the need for assistance with shopping, managing finances, moving across a room, performing light housework, and bathing; this domain contributes a maximum of 4 points.29 The total score ranges from 0 to 10 points according to the specified scoring algorithm.

In the original validation study, a cut-off value of ≥ 3 points was established to identify vulnerability.28 This threshold identified approximately 32% of the representative older population and was associated with a significantly increased risk of death or functional decline. In the original validation cohort, a score ≥ 3 identified a subgroup with approximately a 4.2-fold higher risk of death or functional decline over a two-year period; the discriminative performance of the instrument was expressed by an AUC of approximately 0.78.28 An AUC value within this range indicates good discriminative ability for distinguishing individuals at higher versus lower risk of adverse outcomes.

In a subsequent five-year cohort of outpatient individuals aged ≥ 75 years, the instrument achieved an AUC of approximately 0.75 for the composite endpoint of functional decline or death, demonstrating stable prognostic accuracy over longer follow-up periods.29 At the threshold ≥ 3 points, sensitivity was approximately 86% and specificity 54%, indicating that the instrument identifies most high-risk patients, albeit at the cost of a higher rate of false-positive results.

Administration requires approximately 4 minutes, does not require technical equipment, and may be performed by physicians, nurses, or other healthcare professionals.29 In clinical practice, the instrument is used primarily in outpatient settings, geriatric care, and oncology as a rapid prognostic tool supporting decision-making regarding the intensity of diagnostic and therapeutic interventions.29

Its principal strengths include practical applicability, stable prognostic accuracy across validation studies, and the availability of sensitivity and specificity estimates at different cut-off values, which supports informed clinical decision-making.29 A limitation of the instrument is its predominant focus on functional vulnerability and prediction of functional decline; it does not provide information about biological mechanisms of frailty, such as reduced physical performance or sarcopenia, nor about broader social or psychological dimensions of patient status.28 The resulting score therefore reflects primarily functional status, rather than a comprehensive multidimensional representation of geriatric frailty.

Fried’s Frailty Phenotype

This instrument was defined in 2001 by Linda P. Fried et al within the Cardiovascular Health Study in the United States.30 It represents a physical frailty phenotype based on the presence of five specific criteria, in which frailty is conceptualized as a clinical syndrome resulting from dysregulation across multiple physiological systems reaching a critical threshold of reduced physiological reserve.18

The phenotype is defined by the presence of five criteria: unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, muscle weakness, slowness, and low physical activity (Figure 4). Unintentional weight loss is defined as a reduction of more than 4.5 kg during the previous year and/or at least 5% of body weight (Table 2). Exhaustion is assessed using selected items from the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D scale), with symptoms present on most days of the week considered a positive result. Weakness is determined by measurement of handgrip strength using a dynamometer, with threshold values stratified by sex and body mass index (Table 3). Slowness is assessed by measuring gait time over a standardized short distance (in clinical practice often 4 meters) at usual walking speed, allowing the use of customary walking aids (eg, a cane), with threshold values stratified by sex and body height. Low physical activity is determined based on weekly energy expenditure, typically assessed using the Minnesota Leisure Time Activity Questionnaire.31

Each of the five criteria is assigned one point, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 5 points. Based on the number of criteria present, three categories are distinguished: 0 points – robust, 1–2 points – prefrail, ≥ 3 points – frail.

Administration typically requires approximately 5–20 minutes depending on local organizational conditions. It requires measurement of handgrip strength using a dynamometer, assessment of gait speed using a stopwatch and a standardized walking distance, and evaluation of physical activity and exhaustion using questionnaire-based items.24 Compared with purely questionnaire-based screening instruments, it is therefore more demanding in terms of logistics.

The Fried phenotype represents one of the most extensively studied operational definitions of physical frailty. It has been widely validated across multiple population-based cohorts and has demonstrated the ability to predict adverse clinical outcomes, including hospitalization, disability, institutionalization, and mortality.18 Owing to its clear conceptual definition and standardized threshold values, it is frequently used as a reference model of physical frailty in research studies.

In clinical practice, the Fried phenotype is applied in both outpatient and inpatient care settings, in research projects, and in preoperative patient assessment when conditions allow performance-based measurements to be conducted.17 Its principal strengths include a clearly defined and biologically grounded conceptualization of frailty, standardized threshold values for individual criteria, and its ability to identify physical vulnerability with strong prognostic validity.17

A limitation of the phenotype is its greater logistical complexity in routine outpatient practice, as it requires performance-based measurements and specific equipment. Conceptually, the instrument is restricted to the physical dimension of frailty and does not include psychological or social domains of patient status.17 When applying the Fried phenotype, it is essential to standardize measurement procedures for handgrip strength (type of dynamometer, dominant hand, number of attempts) and gait speed distance in order to ensure comparability and reproducibility of results.

Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI)

The Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI) is a Dutch multidomain self-report instrument designed to assess the physical, psychological, and social dimensions of frailty. It was developed by Karlijn J. Gobbens et al around 2009–2010.32 The instrument consists of two parts. Part A collects information on potential determinants of frailty, whereas Part B contains 15 items assessing frailty itself. The total score (0–15 points) is calculated exclusively from Part B33 Although scoring is based only on Part B, both sections together provide a more comprehensive clinical picture of the patient’s condition.

Part B includes three frailty domains: physical (8 items), psychological (4 items), and social (3 items). The physical domain evaluates self-perceived health status, unintentional weight loss, difficulties with walking and balance, hearing and visual impairment, upper-extremity weakness, and physical fatigue. The psychological domain addresses memory problems, depressive mood, anxiety, and the ability to cope with stressful situations. The social domain assesses living alone, lack of social contacts, and perceived insufficient social support.34

The total score of Part B ranges from 0 to 15 points, with higher values reflecting greater frailty severity. Individual items are scored 0 or 1 point depending on the presence of the deficit; for items with three response options, answers indicating the presence of difficulties are considered positive. The threshold value for identifying frailty is set at ≥ 5 points.16,35

Administration of the TFI is relatively time-efficient, typically requiring less than 15 minutes. It does not require technical equipment and can be completed even without direct face-to-face contact.36 In terms of psychometric properties, the TFI demonstrates good internal consistency and construct validity compared with other self-report screening instruments.16 Predictive validity for adverse clinical outcomes in longitudinal studies has generally been rated as modest, with area-under-the-curve values ranging approximately from 0.54 to 0.67 depending on the outcome assessed.16

In clinical practice, the TFI is used primarily in community settings and primary care for screening multidomain frailty. It is also widely applied in research settings, where it serves mainly as a screening instrument prompting further assessment rather than as a definitive diagnostic tool.33 Its principal strengths include its multidomain structure, which systematically incorporates psychological and social components that are often critical determinants of overall functioning in older adults.34

A limitation of the instrument is its greater length compared with ultra-brief screening tools; in some comparisons, higher scores may also identify individuals in the stage of prefrailty.33 In clinical practice, it is advisable to define a structured follow-up pathway when a score ≥ 5 points is detected (eg, medication review, fall risk assessment, nutritional evaluation, and referral for comprehensive geriatric assessment), so that screening leads to concrete clinical interventions.

Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI)

The Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI) is a Dutch multidomain screening instrument designed to identify deficits across physical, cognitive, social, and psychological domains. It was first published in The Gerontologist in 2001.37 The instrument consists of 15 items assessing the ability to perform shopping, walking outdoors, dressing, and using the toilet; self-rated physical fitness on a 0–10 scale; the presence of visual and hearing impairment; unintentional weight loss (3 kg within 1 month or 6 kg within 2 months during the previous 6 months); polypharmacy (≥ 5 medications); subjective memory complaints; feelings of emptiness; lack of social contacts; perceived loneliness; sadness or depressive mood; and nervousness or anxiety.38

The total score ranges from 0 to 15 points. Individual items are scored as 0 or 1 point depending on the presence of the deficit. For the item assessing self-rated physical fitness on a 0–10 scale, values between 0 and 6 are considered positive (1 point), whereas values between 7 and 10 are scored as 0 points.38 A threshold value of ≥ 4 points is most commonly used to identify frailty. However, the instrument does not define additional formal categories of severity (eg, mild, moderate, or severe frailty).

Administration of the instrument is straightforward, does not require technical equipment, and consists of 15 questions, allowing rapid and feasible implementation in clinical practice.39 In terms of psychometric properties, the GFI demonstrates moderate internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha approximately 0.68–0.73) and established construct validity; when compared with the Tilburg Frailty Indicator, it shows broadly comparable psychometric characteristics.39 Predictive validity in longitudinal studies is generally modest, with area-under-the-curve values of approximately 0.54–0.67, indicating limited discriminative ability for predicting adverse clinical outcomes.16

In clinical practice, the GFI is used primarily for screening in community and outpatient settings. It has also been applied in the preoperative period for preliminary risk assessment; however, it should not be considered a standalone diagnostic instrument.33 Its principal strengths include its multidomain structure, incorporating assessment of social isolation, as well as availability in a self-report format.40

A limitation of the instrument is the additive structure of its scoring system, which aggregates heterogeneous deficits; therefore, identical total scores may correspond to substantially different clinical profiles, necessitating further evaluation using comprehensive geriatric assessment.39 In clinical practice, it is recommended that interpretation of results include documentation of which domains were positive, followed by targeted evaluation within comprehensive geriatric assessment or an individualized intervention plan.

Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS)

The Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS) was developed in Canada (Edmonton; University of Alberta) by Darryl B. Rolfson et al and published in 2006.14 Conceptually, it represents a multidomain clinical screening instrument designed to capture key domains of comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) within a brief assessment format.14

The scale consists of 11 items with a maximum score of 17 points and includes the domains of cognition (clock-drawing test) (Figure 5), general health status (hospitalizations and self-rated health), functional independence (need for assistance with ADL/IADL), social support, medication use (polypharmacy and medication adherence), nutrition (weight loss), mood, continence, and functional performance (Timed Up and Go test).14

The cognitive domain includes the clock-drawing test, in which the patient completes the numbers within a pre-drawn circle and sets the hands to “ten minutes past eleven”; scoring reflects both the accuracy of number placement and correctness of the indicated time. Functional performance is assessed using the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, in which the patient rises from a seated position, walks approximately 3 meters, turns, returns, and sits down again; scoring is graded according to the time required to complete the task or inability to perform it (Figure 6).41

The total score is calculated as the sum of individual item scores within the range of 0–17 points, with higher values reflecting greater frailty severity. Based on the total score, the following categories are distinguished according to the official EFS categorization: 0–3 points – fit (not frail); 4–5 points – vulnerable; 6–7 points – mild frailty; 8–9 points – moderate frailty; ≥10 points – severe frailty.14

Administration of the EFS typically requires less than 5 minutes and involves minimal equipment—a sheet with a pre-drawn circle, a pen, a chair, a stopwatch, and approximately a 3-meter walking distance. In the original validation study, the scale was successfully administered by healthcare professionals without specialized geriatric training.

In the original validation cohort of patients referred for comprehensive geriatric assessment (N = 158), the EFS demonstrated a moderate correlation with geriatricians’ clinical impression of frailty severity (r = 0.64), good inter-rater reliability (κ = 0.77), and acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.62).14 Administration time remained under 5 minutes.

In clinical practice, the EFS is used as a rapid multidomain screening instrument in both outpatient and inpatient settings, particularly before surgical procedures and in oncological decision-making, where it may serve as an initial screening step followed by referral for comprehensive geriatric assessment.14 Its principal strengths include its multidomain structure, which integrates assessment of function, cognition, medication use, social support, and mobility within a single brief instrument.14

A limitation of the EFS is that the resulting score represents a composite of heterogeneous domains, which may complicate interpretation without subsequent domain-specific clinical evaluation. In addition, threshold values defining frailty may vary depending on the clinical context (eg, geriatric assessment vs perioperative evaluation), requiring clear methodological specification when applied in clinical practice or research.42

The EFS represents a practical compromise between screening and comprehensive assessment; when higher scores are detected, it is advisable to translate the result into a structured intervention plan targeting the identified domains (eg, medication review in polypharmacy, fall prevention strategies in prolonged TUG performance, further cognitive evaluation after abnormal clock drawing, or assessment of social support where deficits are identified).

Frailty Index (FI-CGA)

The Frailty Index derived from Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA-FI, or FI-CGA) was operationalized in Canada by David M. Jones, Xiaowei Song, and Kenneth Rockwood and published in 2004 in the Journal of the American Geriatrics Society as an index based on standardized comprehensive geriatric assessment.15 Conceptually, it represents a frailty index grounded in the deficit accumulation model, anchored within the framework of comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA). The value of the index is calculated from the total number of identified problems across CGA domains, thereby quantifying the overall burden of deficits in an individual patient.15

In one commonly used 10-domain version, the assessment includes the following areas: cognition (eg, Mini-Mental State Examination and clinical history), emotional status (Geriatric Depression Scale), communication (vision, hearing, speech), mobility (Timed Up and Go test), balance (history of falls), bladder function, bowel continence, nutrition, ability to perform basic activities of daily living (ADL), and social problems.43 Within this framework, the Timed Up and Go test contributes to the evaluation of mobility, with risk defined according to completion time or inability to perform the test. Specific threshold values may vary depending on the protocol used; in one published study, high mobility-related risk was defined, for example, as a time > 19 seconds or inability to complete the test.43

In the 10-domain version, each domain is scored on a three-point scale: 0 (no deficit), 0.5 (minor deficit), 1 (major deficit).

The resulting index is calculated as the ratio between the sum of domain scores and the total number of domains assessed. The final value ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values reflecting a greater degree of accumulated deficits.43 Threshold values may vary depending on the specific version used. In some methodological frameworks, a value of 0.25 has been suggested as an indicative threshold separating robust status from frailty.43

In the original version published by Jones et al (2004), the FI-CGA was stratified according to the total number of deficits into three levels—mild (0–7 deficits), moderate (7–13 deficits), and severe (>13 deficits). Higher levels were associated with a significantly increased risk of institutionalization or mortality during 12-month follow-up (hazard ratio 1.9 for moderate and 5.5 for severe levels compared with the mild level).15

The time required for assessment depends on completion of the comprehensive geriatric assessment itself, as the index represents its quantitative output. Full CGA administration typically requires several tens of minutes and involves a multidisciplinary team; however, calculation of the index itself is rapid when structured data are available.

In the original study, the FI-CGA demonstrated predictive validity for institutionalization or mortality during 12-month follow-up, with higher scores associated with increased risk of adverse outcomes.15 Very high intra-rater reliability (0.95) was also reported, indicating strong stability of repeated assessments by the same evaluator. Subsequent operationalizations have consistently demonstrated a graded increase in the risk of adverse outcomes proportional to the burden of accumulated deficits, supporting the theoretical framework of the deficit accumulation model.43

In clinical practice, the CGA-FI is used primarily in geriatric outpatient clinics and inpatient wards, within preoperative comprehensive geriatric assessment, and in geriatric oncology, where it is important not only to identify the presence of frailty but also to quantify its severity and structure of deficits. It also has substantial application in research settings, where it enables precise quantification of deficit burden and analysis of its association with adverse clinical outcomes.15

Its principal strengths include its multidimensional structure, quantitative expression of deficit burden, and close integration with individualized intervention planning. The index is also strongly supported by the theoretical framework of the deficit accumulation model, which enhances its prognostic and analytical applicability.11 A limitation is its dependence on completion of comprehensive geriatric assessment, which is time-consuming and requires an experienced multidisciplinary team. In addition, threshold values may vary across different versions, which may complicate comparability between studies or clinical settings.43

Discussion

The assessment of geriatric frailty in clinical practice should not be understood as the search for a universally “best” instrument, but as the selection of the most clinically appropriate tool for a defined patient, setting, and decision. Frailty instruments differ substantially in their conceptual background, domains assessed, time requirements, need for equipment, and staffing demands (Table 4). For this reason, they are not fully interchangeable. This heterogeneity should not be viewed as a weakness of the field, but rather as an opportunity to match the assessment method to the clinical question and the organizational capacity of the healthcare setting.

The present review therefore proposes a context-oriented framework for selecting frailty assessment tools across different levels of care. In everyday practice, the first distinction should be whether the aim is rapid screening, immediate clinical risk stratification, multidomain evaluation, or detailed quantification of accumulated deficits. Brief instruments such as the FRAIL scale and PRISMA-7 are particularly suitable for first-contact screening in outpatient, community, and long-term care settings. Their value lies in their simplicity, low time burden, and ability to identify older adults who may require further assessment. However, they should not be interpreted as substitutes for comprehensive frailty evaluation.

In acute hospital and emergency settings, the clinical priority is different. Decisions are often made under time pressure, with incomplete information and a need for rapid orientation in the patient’s premorbid functional status, comorbidity burden, and cognitive condition. In this context, the Clinical Frailty Scale offers a practical method of global clinical stratification with minimal organizational demands. Extensive multidomain assessment during emergency triage is usually neither realistic nor appropriate; nevertheless, rapid identification of frailty may meaningfully inform prognosis, escalation decisions, communication with patients and relatives, and early discharge planning.

Between brief screening tools and comprehensive geriatric assessment, multidomain instruments may provide a practical compromise. The Edmonton Frail Scale combines functional, cognitive, social, and performance-based components while remaining feasible in routine clinical environments. It may be useful in inpatient wards, perioperative assessment, and outpatient geriatric practice, where frailty assessment is intended not only to identify risk but also to guide subsequent clinical decision-making. Similarly, self-reported multidomain tools such as the Tilburg Frailty Indicator and the Groningen Frailty Indicator broaden the assessment beyond physical vulnerability by incorporating psychological and social dimensions. These domains are particularly relevant in community and outpatient care, where social isolation, mood symptoms, and functional vulnerability often interact. However, positive results from these tools should generally be regarded as indications for further professional evaluation rather than definitive diagnostic conclusions.

Phenotype-based models, particularly the frailty phenotype described by Fried and the simplified Study of Osteoporotic Fractures index, provide a structured assessment of physical frailty. Their strength lies in their biological grounding and their ability to capture physical vulnerability associated with adverse outcomes such as functional decline, hospitalization, institutionalization, and mortality. They are most useful when the clinical aim is targeted evaluation of physical frailty and when performance-based testing is available. Their routine use may be limited in settings with restricted time, staffing, or equipment, especially when rapid decision-making is required.

At the more detailed end of the assessment spectrum are deficit accumulation indices derived from comprehensive geriatric assessment. These instruments allow a broader quantification of biological vulnerability and provide a closer link between frailty assessment and individualized management. Their principal value is not rapid screening, but the detailed characterization of accumulated health deficits and the support of complex clinical decision-making. They are therefore most appropriate in specialized geriatric outpatient clinics, inpatient geriatric units, and settings where comprehensive geriatric assessment and adequate staffing capacity are available.

A stepwise, context-oriented approach may therefore provide a practical framework for aligning frailty assessment with the clinical setting, the purpose of evaluation, and available resources. At first contact with patients aged 65 years or older, the clinical environment in which assessment takes place should guide tool selection. In outpatient, community, and social care settings, brief screening using instruments such as FRAIL or PRISMA-7 may represent an appropriate first step. In acute hospital or emergency settings, rapid clinical stratification using the Clinical Frailty Scale is often more suitable When screening is positive or clinical suspicion of more advanced frailty persists, multidomain evaluation using an instrument such as the Edmonton Frail Scale or targeted comprehensive geriatric assessment may be appropriate. If the objective is detailed quantification of deficit burden and individualized care planning, comprehensive geriatric assessment supplemented by a deficit accumulation index such as the FI-CGA may represent an appropriate next step. This approach also reflects the dynamic nature of frailty. Frailty status may deteriorate or improve over time, particularly during transitions between community care, acute hospitalization, rehabilitation, long-term care, and home-based support.8 A frailty score obtained in the community may inform triage and early orientation after hospital admission, while frailty status established during hospitalization may guide discharge planning, follow-up intensity, and the need for home-based or institutional post-acute care.9 Because different instruments capture related but non-identical constructs of frailty, scores obtained with one tool in one setting should be interpreted as clinically informative rather than directly interchangeable. Reassessment at key transitions of care, using the tool most appropriate for the specific setting, is therefore more realistic than attempting to apply a single instrument across the entire care pathway.

The clinical value of frailty assessment does not lie in the score alone, but in the actions that follow from it. A positive screening result should activate a clearly defined care pathway leading to comprehensive geriatric assessment, medication review, nutritional intervention, rehabilitation, management of geriatric syndromes, social support, and follow-up planning. In this sense, frailty screening functions as an entry point into a broader care process. Evidence from trials of comprehensive geriatric assessment suggests that such structured multidisciplinary intervention may improve the probability of older patients being alive and living at home at follow-up and may reduce functional decline.44 The impact of frailty assessment on outcomes therefore depends critically on whether identification is translated into meaningful clinical intervention.

Implementation remains a major challenge. Highly sensitive screening tools may identify large numbers of older adults as potentially frail, but not all healthcare systems have sufficient geriatric expertise to provide timely comprehensive assessment for every positive result. Workforce shortages, uneven access to specialist geriatric services, and the absence of standardized referral pathways may limit the practical benefit of screening.45 Sustainable implementation therefore requires more than the selection of an appropriate instrument. It requires clearly defined onward pathways, task-sharing between geriatricians and other clinicians, and integration of frailty assessment into existing clinical workflows rather than its use as an isolated additional step.

Digital health approaches may help address some of these barriers. Automated calculation of deficit accumulation indices from routinely collected electronic health record data offers a scalable method for identifying older adults at increased risk without adding substantial clinician workload. The electronic frailty index, developed and validated in United Kingdom primary care using routine health data from a large population of older adults, has shown predictive validity for mortality, unplanned hospitalization, and nursing home admission.46 Similar electronic frailty indices have since been developed and validated in other healthcare systems.47 Wider use of such tools may support large-scale frailty identification, particularly when combined with clearly defined clinical pathways for patients identified as vulnerable.

This review has several limitations. The literature on frailty assessment is characterized by substantial heterogeneity in definitions, conceptual models, validation cohorts, and outcome measures, which limits direct comparison between instruments. The review was designed as a clinically oriented narrative synthesis rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis, and no formal risk-of-bias appraisal, quantitative pooling, or Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) certainty rating was performed. Study identification and selection were carried out by a single reviewer, only English-language publications were considered, and the search was current to March 2026. The synthesis was therefore purposive rather than exhaustive, and the proposed pathway reflects the balance of the available evidence rather than a definitive comparative ranking of the instruments discussed.

Future research should focus on: (i) harmonizing frailty assessment strategies across healthcare environments and levels of care; (ii) prospective evaluation of stepwise, context-oriented pathways in real-world clinical practice; (iii) integration of frailty screening into standardized referral and care pathways, with clearly defined actions following a positive result; and (iv) wider development, validation, and implementation of digital and electronic health record–based frailty tools to support large-scale, low-burden identification of at-risk older adults.

Conclusion

Frailty is a clinically significant, multidimensional geriatric syndrome whose assessment cannot be reduced to a single universal instrument. The diversity of available tools reflects differing clinical needs, assessment objectives, and organizational capacities across healthcare settings. Selection of an appropriate instrument should therefore be context-oriented, guided by the purpose of evaluation, the characteristics of the patient population, and the intended use of the results in subsequent clinical management.

The stepwise, context-oriented pathway proposed in this review should be interpreted as a conceptual framework synthesized from the available literature, not as a prospectively validated clinical protocol. Its aim is to support clinicians in matching the assessment method to a defined clinical situation, not to prescribe a fixed diagnostic algorithm. Prospective evaluation across different healthcare settings is needed before it can be regarded as an evidence-based protocol for routine practice.

Ultimately, frailty assessment becomes clinically meaningful only when frailty identification is translated into clearly defined care pathways, comprehensive geriatric assessment, individualized intervention, and structured follow-up.
