Moving beyond disease-centered care toward whole-person health
Authors: Yangxia Wang, Ka Kit Hui
Published: 2026

Introduction

Healthcare systems worldwide are entering a period of profound transformation. Aging populations, the rising burden of chronic and complex diseases, escalating healthcare expenditures, mental health crises, and increasing recognition of social and environmental determinants of health have collectively exposed important limitations of reductionist, disease-centered medical models.1 Although modern biomedicine has achieved extraordinary advances in acute care, surgery, infectious disease control, molecular diagnostics, and precision therapeutics, many chronic conditions remain inadequately managed when approached primarily through organ-specific or symptom-centered frameworks.

At the same time, global interest in whole-person, systems-oriented, preventive, and patient-centered approaches to care continues to grow. Traditional medical systems, including Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), Ayurveda, Kampo medicine, Traditional Korean medicine and other longstanding healing traditions, are increasingly being revisited not merely as complementary therapies, but as potential contributors to a broader transformation in healthcare philosophy.2 Among these approaches, Integrated Chinese-Western medicine (ICWM) represents one of the most mature and systematically developed models of medical integration worldwide.

Over the past several decades, ICWM has evolved from a largely China-based exploratory practice into an interdisciplinary framework with expanding international relevance. Importantly, this transition is unfolding during a period of unprecedented development in evidence-based medicine, digital health, artificial intelligence (AI), quantum science, systems biology, and precision medicine. Together, these advances create new opportunities to rethink how medical evidence is generated, interpreted, translated, and implemented across different medical paradigms.

The question facing global healthcare is therefore no longer simply whether traditional medicine and western medicine can coexist. The more important challenge is how to develop scientifically rigorous, clinically meaningful, globally accessible, and person- and population-centered models that can successfully translate evidence into real-world cost-effectiveness practice.

This commentary discusses the historical evolution of collaboration between traditional Chinese medicine and western medicine, the growing emergence of whole-person health frameworks, and the future role of AI-assisted evidence ecosystems in shaping next-generation integrative healthcare.

Historical evolution of integrated Chinese-western medicine

The relationship between traditional Chinese medicine and western medicine has evolved from competition and skepticism toward increasing collaboration and structured integration. Modern ICWM formally emerged in China during the end of the 1950s, when national policies encouraged cooperation between traditional Chinese medicine practitioners and biomedically trained physicians. Initially focused on pragmatic clinical applications, ICWM gradually developed into a broader interdisciplinary field involving clinical care, rehabilitation, education, public health, and translational research.3

Over time, integrated hospitals and academic programs were established throughout China, while evidence-based medicine methodologies became increasingly incorporated into traditional Chinese medicine research. The growing use of randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, and clinical guidelines helped strengthen the scientific foundation of integrative medicine, alongside international support from organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO).

Importantly, ICWM has evolved through multiple models of integration. Some approaches emphasize biomedical interpretation of traditional Chinese medicine, whereas others seek to preserve traditional theoretical frameworks. Despite these differences, one principle has remained clear: meaningful integration requires more than simply combining therapies. It depends on interdisciplinary collaboration, coherent clinical pathways, and evidence frameworks capable of evaluating complex and individualized interventions.

The convergence of systems medicine and traditional holistic thought

One of the most significant developments in contemporary medicine is the growing convergence between systems-oriented biomedical science and traditional holistic medical philosophy. Advances in systems biology, psychoneuroimmunology, network physiology and pharmacology, microbiome research, and stress biology increasingly suggest that health and disease emerge through dynamic interactions among biological, psychological, behavioral, environmental, and social systems. For example, chronic disease is increasingly understood not as isolated organ dysfunction, but as dysregulation across interconnected physiological networks.4

These perspectives resonate strongly with longstanding concepts in Chinese medicine, including systemic balance, individualized constitution, adaptive regulation, and restoration of physiological harmony. Contemporary scientific concepts such as allostasis, resilience, and adaptive homeostasis parallel traditional ideas such as yin-yang balance as well as the abundance and smooth flow of Qi. Importantly, this convergence is not simply philosophical. It reflects a broader shift in modern medicine toward systems-based, preventive, and person-centered approaches to healthcare.

Whole person health and the redefinition of healthcare

An important emerging development in global integrative medicine is the rise of the “whole-person health” framework, recently advanced by the U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH), the Veterans Health Administration (VA), and multiple academic integrative medicine centers including the UCLA Center for East-West Medicine (CEWM).5,6 Unlike traditional disease-centered models that focus primarily on pathology, symptoms, and disease biomarkers, whole-person health emphasizes a broader understanding of health that includes physiological, behavioral, psychological, social, environmental, and existential dimensions.7

This framework reflects a deeper conceptual transition in modern healthcare. Rather than asking only “What’s the matter with you?”, whole-person health increasingly asks, “What matters to you?” The VA whole-health initiative views health not simply as the absence of disease, but as a multidimensional state encompassing wellbeing, resilience, functional capacity, self-efficacy, and life purpose.8 In this model, patients become active participants in their own healing process through lifestyle modification, stress regulation, nutrition, physical activity, mindfulness, sleep optimization, social connection, and self-care.

Importantly, Whole Person Health also challenges the assumption that healing simply represents the reversal of disease pathogenesis. Emerging systems-based models increasingly suggest that health may involve distinct “salutogenic” pathways that actively promote physiological resilience, adaptive regulation, and restoration of homeostasis.9 Research in psychoneuroimmunology, network physiology, and stress biology continues to demonstrate that emotional regulation, inflammation, autonomic function, metabolism, behavior, and chronic disease are deeply interconnected within dynamic biological networks.10 These concepts closely parallel longstanding holistic principles in traditional Chinese medicine, including adaptive harmony and individualized regulation.

Challenges in translating integrative evidence into clinical practice

Although research related to traditional Chinese medicine and ICWM has expanded rapidly in recent years, substantial barriers remain in translating evidence into clinical practice. Conventional evidence hierarchies were largely developed for evaluating single interventions targeting single disease outcomes under highly controlled conditions. Yet ICWM often involves individualized diagnosis, multimodal interventions, dynamic treatment adjustment, and whole-person care. These characteristics make conventional randomized controlled trial designs insufficient for capturing the complexity of real world integrative practice.11

At the same time, clinicians frequently struggle to access and interpret the rapidly expanding body of ICWM literature. Existing evidence remains fragmented across languages, databases, disciplines, and study designs. Variations in methodological quality and inconsistent reporting standards further complicate evidence synthesis. Interdisciplinary communication also remains challenging. Biomedically trained physicians may have limited familiarity with traditional Chinese medicine theory, while traditional medicine practitioners may have limited exposure to evidence-based medicine methodologies, clinical epidemiology, or digital health systems.

Equally important, healthcare systems in many countries continue to operate within structurally separated regulatory, educational, reimbursement, and clinical frameworks. As a result, integrated care pathways remain difficult to implement consistently. The challenge is therefore not simply generating more evidence, but creating evidence ecosystems capable of supporting real-time evidence synthesis, interdisciplinary collaboration, clinical translation, and patient-centered decision-making.

Artificial intelligence and the emergence of next-generation evidence ecosystems

Recent advances in artificial intelligence, particularly large language models (LLMs), natural language processing, and automated evidence synthesis, may substantially reshape the future of integrative medicine research and implementation. The AI-empowered Clinical Evidence for Integrated Chinese–Western Medicine (ACE-iMed) platform represents an important step toward this next-generation evidence ecosystem.

Unlike conventional evidence repositories, ACE-iMed adopts a clinical question-oriented framework that integrates clinical practice guidelines, consensus statements, systematic reviews, and randomized controlled trials across multiple Chinese and international databases.12,13 More importantly, ACE-iMed demonstrates how AI may support multiple stages of the evidence workflow, including literature screening, data extraction, risk-of-bias assessment, semantic evidence retrieval, multilingual dissemination, and clinical question matching. This capability may significantly reduce the gap between rapidly evolving evidence and real-world clinical implementation.

Additionally, this transition is not simply technological. It reflects a broader rethinking of how medical evidence is organized, translated, and applied in complex healthcare environments. Rather than functioning solely as administrative or analytical tools, AI-assisted platforms may increasingly become active facilitators of evidence translation and personalized clinical decision-making.

Nevertheless, caution remains essential. Large language models remain vulnerable to hallucinations, incomplete evidence retrieval, algorithmic bias, and variability between models.14 Human oversight, methodological rigor, interdisciplinary governance, and transparent validation will remain critical as AI becomes increasingly integrated into clinical and research ecosystems.

International perspectives and future directions

Many international healthcare systems have already begun exploring integrative medicine implementation, providing important insights into the future development of integrative medicine. In the United States, academic medical centers increasingly incorporate acupuncture, mindfulness-based interventions, lifestyle medicine, nutrition, and integrative oncology into mainstream care. European countries such as Germany and Switzerland have integrated selected complementary therapies into public healthcare systems, while Japan’s Kampo medicine has become incorporated into conventional medical education and clinical practice. Meanwhile, Hong Kong has emerged as an important international bridge connecting Chinese medicine traditions, global biomedical research, and AI-assisted evidence systems.15

Collectively, these experiences suggest that successful integration is most effective when clinically rather than ideologically driven. They also highlight the importance of patient-centered outcomes, interdisciplinary education, institutional infrastructure, and flexible evidence frameworks capable of accommodating complexity and personalization. Importantly, the goal should be to develop adaptive, evidence-informed, and globally relevant healthcare systems capable of addressing the multidimensional complexity of chronic disease and long-term health maintenance.

The next phase of ICWM will likely involve a transition from conventional evidence-based medicine toward broader evidence-ecosystem medicine. Future healthcare systems may increasingly integrate AI-assisted clinical decision support, systems biology, precision medicine, wearable technologies, real-world evidence, and individualized lifestyle interventions into more continuous, personalized, and dynamic models of care. At the same time, research methodologies will also need to evolve beyond rigid reductionist frameworks, with greater emphasis on whole systems research, pragmatic clinical trials, comparative effectiveness and cost-effectiveness research, and AI-assisted evidence synthesis for evaluating complex interventions in real-world settings.

Conclusion

The global healthcare landscape is undergoing profound transformation. Increasing recognition of chronic disease complexity, whole-person health, systems medicine, and digital health innovation is creating new opportunities for collaboration between traditional medicine and modern biomedicine. ICWM represents one of the most mature models of this integration. Its future depends not only on generating evidence, but on effectively translating evidence into improvement of patient-centered care while redefining how health is understood and sustained.
