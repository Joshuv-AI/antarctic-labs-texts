Resonant breathing in hospitalised psychiatric patients with persistent somatic symptoms: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Markus Canazei, Katharina Hüfner, Barbara Sperner-Unterweger, Astrid Lampe, Johannes Weninger, Siegmund Staggl, Verena Dresen, Elisabeth Weiss
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersistent somatic symptoms are a common feature in many psychiatric disorders. Moderate-to-severe cases often necessitate treatment in specialised inpatient psychiatric settings.AimsThis study evaluated the efficacy, safety and acceptability of resonant breathing (personalised slow breathing to maximise heart rate variability (HRV)) as adjunctive treatment for hospitalised patients with psychiatric disorders presenting with persistent somatic symptoms on admission, when patients typically present with high symptom severity.MethodsThis multicentre, randomised, placebo-controlled cross-over study included 78 patients diagnosed with somatic symptom disorder or affective disorders with persistent somatic symptoms. Participants underwent light-guided resonant breathing and sham light therapy two times per day for 5 weekdays each, starting an average of 11 days after admission. The primary outcomes were changes in self-reported somatic well-being and arousal (efficacy), and adverse effects (safety). Secondary outcomes included somatic, anxiety, depression and insomnia symptoms, HRV measures and patient satisfaction (acceptability). The trial was preregistered in the German Clinical Trial Register (ID: DRKS00027323) and funded by the Austrian Research Promotion Agency (Grant ID. F0999886082).ResultsResonant breathing rates were six breaths per minute in 60% of patients and higher in the remaining participants. The study found no intervention-specific effect on primary outcomes of self-reported somatic well-being and arousal. Patients reported no adverse effects and expressed high satisfaction with the breathing intervention. Resonant breathing significantly reduced secondary outcomes of self-reported anxiety (η²p=0.09, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.22) and insomnia symptoms (η²p=0.10, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.01 to 0.23) after 10 sessions. It also significantly improved several HRV measures acutely (η²p range: 0.23 (95% CI 0.09 to 0.38) to 0.51 (95% CI 0.35 to 0.62)).ConclusionsWhile resonant breathing did not improve somatic well-being or arousal, it was safe, well-tolerated and effective at reducing anxiety and insomnia symptoms during early hospitalisation. These findings support further investigation into resonant breathing for these specific indications over extended periods.Trial registration numberDRKS00027323Keywords: Psychosomatic Medicine, Cross-Over Studies, Depressive Disorder, Anxiety, Behavioral Research

Introduction

Persistent somatic symptoms encompass a range of distressing somatic complaints that persist for several months or longer, irrespective of their aetiology. These symptoms impose a considerable burden on patients, the healthcare system and society.1 In inpatient psychiatric settings, they are a particularly prominent feature of severe illness. Due to significant diagnostic overlap,2 this study focuses on two diagnostic categories in which somatic symptoms are especially prevalent: somatic symptom disorder (SSD) and moderate-to-severe depressive disorders. Among patients with these conditions, persistent somatic symptoms are associated with substantial functional impairment and poorer treatment outcomes,1 3 frequently necessitating multidisciplinary interventions in specialised psychiatric facilities.4

Effective treatment of patients with these diagnoses typically incorporates psychological therapies such as cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT).5 6 Relaxation techniques such as progressive muscle relaxation (PMR), autogenic training (AT) or slow breathing (SB) techniques are a fundamental component of CBT protocols.7 PMR targets the reduction of muscular tension, whereas AT employs auto-suggestive formulas to diminish psychophysiological tension. SB aims to reduce the breathing rate to approximately six breaths per minute for 5–15 min and uses visual, auditory or tactile cues to guide the breathing patterns.8 Research indicates that breathing at this frequency enhances heart rate oscillations, thereby synchronising cardiorespiratory rhythms and maximising the activity of the parasympathetic nervous system.9 A substantial body of research demonstrates the effectiveness of SB in both healthy individuals and patients with somatic diseases. In healthy individuals, it lowers blood pressure10 and decreases self-reported stress, anxiety and depressive symptoms.11 Clinical studies have shown benefits for patients with hypertension12 and chronic pain.13

Although the therapeutic benefits of SB are well established, its efficacy as an adjunctive treatment for patients hospitalised for acute psychiatric illness, particularly those presenting with persistent somatic symptoms, has not been systematically evaluated. Specifically, during the initial hospitalisation phase, the autonomic nervous system is often highly dysregulated2 14 and stress and anxiety can exacerbate somatic symptoms,1 4 creating both a critical therapeutic need and a unique implementation challenge for breathing-based interventions. However, based on its beneficial effects on the autonomic nervous system, SB provides a clear rationale for its use in hospitalised patients with persistent somatic symptoms.

The primary goal of this study was to assess the safety and efficacy of resonant breathing as an adjunctive treatment for hospitalised patients with SSD or affective disorders presenting with persistent somatic symptoms. We hypothesised that a twice-daily intervention delivered over five consecutive weekdays would be safe, enhance perceived somatic well-being following morning sessions and reduce presleep arousal following evening sessions (primary outcomes). Regarding secondary outcomes, we hypothesised that the intervention would reduce overall somatic, anxiety, depression and insomnia symptoms, while acutely improving physiological markers of autonomic function, specifically heart rate variability (HRV).

Methods

Study design

This multicentre study was a two-arm, randomised, placebo-controlled, crossover trial.

Clinical setting

The study took place at two specialised clinics. Clinic 1 was a specialised psychiatric hospital (Department of Psychiatry, Psychotherapy, Psychosomatics and Medical Psychology at the Medical University Innsbruck), while Clinic 2 was a psychiatric ward of a rehabilitation hospital (Department of Mental Health at the Rehabilitation Clinic, Schruns). Both facilities frequently treat patients with the diagnoses included in this study.

Participants

All participants were inpatients receiving treatment on a voluntary basis. Eligibility criteria included an age of 18–70 years and a primary diagnosis of SSD (International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision, ICD-10: F45) or depressive episodes with somatic symptoms (ICD-10: F32.01, F32.11, F33.01 or F33.11). The attending psychiatrist made an initial provisional diagnosis within the first 2 days after admission, based on a clinical interview, comprehensive physical examination and review of medical records. This diagnosis was then formally confirmed by clinical psychologists using a structured clinical interview (Structured Clinical Interview for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), research version).15

Exclusion criteria included dementia, acute suicidality and acute medical illnesses. We also excluded patients with active psychotic symptoms who were not yet stabilised on an appropriate medication regimen at the time of study inclusion. As this was a crossover trial, inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to all participants.

Interventions

Equipment and set-up

The interventions were delivered using a ceiling luminaire (Projektleuchten GmbH, Germany; diameter: 95 cm), with 72 warm-white (2200 Kelvin) and 72 cool-white (5300 Kelvin) Luxeon light emitting diodes (type: 3535 L) and dynamic lighting capabilities. The luminaire was mounted vertically with an integrated Raspberry Pi for dynamic lighting control. Interventions were conducted in clinic meeting rooms with controlled lighting conditions. Window screens were closed and ambient lighting was dimmed to 50 lx at desk height.

Active intervention: light-guided resonant breathing

The active intervention consisted of 15 min sessions of light-guided resonant breathing. A visual breathing pacemaker was created by dynamically adjusting the illuminance of the luminaire. Resonant breathing is a specific form of SB that identifies the individual respiration rate that generates the greatest heart rate oscillations.16 In healthy adults, resonant breathing rates typically range between 4.5 and 6.5 breaths per minute, but may be higher in untrained people and patients. In the present study, the individual resonant breathing rate was determined during screening by recording an electrocardiogram (ECG) while participants breathed at progressively slower rates (12, 10, 8 and 6 breaths/min) for 2 min each with 2 min breaks between. The rate producing the highest individual HRV (root mean square of successive difference (RMSSD) between normal heart beats) was selected as the participant’s individual resonant breathing rate.

Participants were instructed to ‘inhale as the light gets brighter and exhale as it gets darker’ with equal inhalation/exhalation phases and no breathing pauses. They were not provided with specific instructions regarding breathing techniques such as nose, mouth or abdominal breathing.

Sessions occurred two times per day (morning and evening) from Monday to Friday at consistent times. Morning sessions used cold-white light (5300 Kelvin; see figure 1, left) with illuminance varying between 0 lx and 990 lx at eye level (measured with a 100 cm distance from the light source). Evening sessions used warm-white light (2200 Kelvin; see figure 1, right) with illuminance varying between 0 lx and 18 lx.

Study luminaire with morning (left) and evening (right) sham light therapy.

Control intervention: sham light therapy

Following methodological recommendations,8 an active control condition (sham light therapy) was implemented. The control condition (sham light therapy) involved 15 min sessions using the same luminaire but with static illumination. Participants were informed they would receive light therapy and advised to keep their eyes open. The sham light therapy was also conducted in the morning and evening at identical times over 5 weekdays. Morning sessions used cold-white light (5300 Kelvin; see figure 1, left) at 50 lx; evening sessions used warm-white light (2200 Kelvin; see figure 1, right) at 52 lx.

Light doses administered during active and control interventions were substantially lower than recommended light therapy doses.17

Measures

Primary outcomes

To assess the immediate effects of the morning sessions, two subscales of the Somatic Well-Being Scale (FAW)18 were administered before and after the session on Monday and Friday. The FAW included the body perception subscale (seven items), comprising items such as ‘I feel physically balanced’ and ‘I can enjoy my current physical state’, and the vitality subscale (seven items), measuring feelings of energy and drive through items like ‘I feel a zest for action’ and ‘I feel physically fit’. Items were rated on a 5-point scale (1=not at all; 5=completely).

To measure the effects of the evening sessions, the Pre-sleep Arousal Scale (PSAS)19 was administered after the intervention. The PSAS assesses physical arousal (eight items; eg, ‘racing heart, palpitations’ or ‘a tight, tense feeling in the muscles’) and cognitive arousal (seven items; eg, ‘ruminating about daily events’ or ‘thoughts that cannot be switched off’) on a 5-point scale (1=not at all; 5=extremely).

A 12-item questionnaire (adverse events (AE)) assessed visual (eye strain; watery, itchy or burning eyes; blurred vision; pain around the eyes; glare) and respiratory adverse effects (headache; dizziness; nausea; shortness of breath; palpitations; feelings of tightness or chest pain; tiredness) on a 6-point scale (1=not at all noticeable; 6=very noticeable).

Secondary outcomes

The Insomnia Severity Index (ISI)20 assesses insomnia symptoms through seven items on a 5-point scale (0=not at all, 4=very severe problem), with scores ranging from 0 to 28. The Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI)21 evaluates psychological distress across somatisation, depression and anxiety dimensions (six items each, 5-point scale: 1=not at all; 5=very severe).

To evaluate immediate physiological effects, ECGs were recorded using a portable single-channel ECG recorder (FAROS 180; Bittium Corporation, Oulu, Finland) with a 1 kHz sampling rate. ECG quality was verified using ‘Cardiscope Pro’ software (Cardiscope Analytics; Hasiba Medical GmbH, Austria), from which time durations between successive heart beats (RR intervals) and HRV parameters were derived (time-domain: RMSSD; proportion of successive RR intervals greater than 50 ms (pNN50); short axis in Poincare diagram (SD1); long axis in Poincare diagram (SD2); frequency-domain: very low frequency band between 0.0033–0.04 hertz (VLF); low frequency band between 0.04–0.15 hertz (LF); high frequency band between 0.15–0.4 hertz (HF); total power and LF:HF ratio).22 The first 2 min and the last minute of the 15 min interventions were excluded from HRV analysis.

Other measures

After the structured clinical interview (during days 4–7 postadmission), participants completed self-report measures within a screening session, assessing psychosomatic symptoms (using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-15)23 and the Somatic Symptom Disorder Scale (SSD-12)),24 depression (Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-V))25 and anxiety (State-Trait Anxiety Inventory to Trait Anxiety (STAI-T)).26 Additionally, participants rated the anticipated effectiveness of the interventions with single items (‘I believe that this intervention can help me to start the day better’ for the morning interventions, and ‘I believe that this intervention can help me to fall asleep better’ for the evening interventions) on 5-point Likert scales (1=strongly disagree; 5=strongly agree).

Satisfaction with the intervention (SAT) was assessed after each 5-day intervention period using two items from the Patients’ Satisfaction Questionnaire (ZUF-8)27 on binary yes/no scales.

Interventions commenced between days 8 and 14 to ensure stable medication regimens. Psychopharmacological data during the 2-week study period were extracted from electronic medical records. To compare pharmacotherapies, daily antidepressant doses were converted to fluoxetine equivalents28 and antipsychotic doses were converted to olanzapine equivalents.29

Study protocol

This study used the online software ‘Research Randomizer’ (https://www.randomizer.org/) for randomisation. An independent researcher (MC) conducted the statistical analysis while blinded to group allocations. Group assignments for each participant were only revealed by the study managers after completion of all analyses. All procedures were preregistered in the German Clinical Trial Register (ID: DRKS00027323).

Study physicians recruited eligible patients and informed them about study procedures. During screening, participants completed baseline questionnaires, had their individual resonant breathing rates determined and were introduced to the two interventions, after which they rated treatment expectations. At the end of the screening session, participants provided written consent for study participation.

Interventions commenced on Mondays, typically in the second inpatient treatment week. Sessions started between 6:30 and 7:30 (before breakfast) and 19:30 and 21:30 (≥2.5 hours post-dinner; 1–2 hours before bedtime). Each intervention took place on five consecutive weekdays, with the second intervention following in the subsequent inpatient treatment week (see figure 2). Single sessions in the morning and evening lasted 25 min on Mondays and Fridays (due to ECG recordings and questionnaires) and 15 min on Tuesdays, Wednesdays and Thursdays.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software, V.3.1.9.6.30 Drawing on previous research indicating moderate-to-large effects of SB on depression and anxiety symptoms,31 we conservatively estimated a small effect size (f=0.10). To achieve 80% power at a 5% significance level, the analysis yielded a minimum sample size of 62 patients. To account for an anticipated 30% dropout rate, the study aimed to recruit a minimum of 81 participants.

For the morning sessions, a three-factorial multivariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures was used to examine both FAW scales. These ANOVAs incorporated intervention (resonant breathing vs sham light treatment), measurement time (preintervention vs postintervention) and weekday (Monday vs Friday) as factors. The effects of evening sessions were analysed using two-factorial ANOVAs with repeated measures (for ISI, the total BSI score and its three subscales and both PSAS subscales), including intervention and weekday as factors. Three-factorial ANOVAs were used to examine acute physiological effects on heart rate (RR intervals) and HRV parameters (pNN50, RMSSD, SD1, SD2, VLF, LF, HF, total power and LF:HF ratio), with intervention, daytime (morning vs evening) and weekday as factors. Post hoc tests with Bonferroni correction were performed for significant interactions. Partial eta-squared values (η²p) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported as effect sizes.

Adverse effects and satisfaction with the intervention were analysed using paired t-tests for daytime-specific intervention effects. Differences in sample characteristics were assessed using t-tests, χ2 tests and Fisher’s exact tests. Descriptive data are presented as mean (SD) or number (n). All analyses were run with the SPSS software (V.29) with two-sided testing and a 5% significance level.

Results

Study population

Between 1 December 2021 and 10 April 2023, 177 patients were invited to participate in this study. Of these, 71 individuals did not meet the inclusion criteria, and 8 individuals declined to participate. Ultimately, 98 patients (Clinic 1, n=40; Clinic 2, n=58) were allocated to the interventions, of whom 20 discontinued the study. All discontinuations occurred during the first intervention week, with 75% of them discontinuing within the first two intervention days. Due to the cross-over design, imputing outcomes was problematic and an intention-to-treat analysis could not be run. As the final sample (n=78; Clinic 1, n=32; Clinic 2, n=46) exceeded the sample size derived from the power analysis, a per-protocol analysis was performed. In addition, a sensitivity analysis revealed that sample characteristics for participants who dropped out were not statistically different compared with participants who finished the study (see online supplemental table A1).

Baseline characteristics

The final sample (n=78) consisted of inpatients with a primary diagnosis of either SSD, accounting for 71% of participants, or depressive episodes with somatic symptoms. Participants had a mean age of 47.2 years (range: 19–70 years), and 67% were female. At screening, participants reported moderate-to-severe symptoms, with the majority having somatic symptom scores above the clinical threshold (71% with PHQ-15 >9; 81% with SSD-12 >22). In addition, 64% reported severe depressive symptoms (BDI-V >34) and 81% reported severe anxiety symptoms (STAI-T >39). During the 2-week intervention period, a majority of the patients were taking medication: 76% received antidepressants, 33% received antipsychotics and 78% were taking medication for somatic illnesses. The mean inpatient treatment duration was 41.7 days (range: 21–63 days). Further details on sample characteristics are provided in table 1.

Individual resonant breathing rates, as the frequency that maximised each participant’s HRV (RMSSD), were 6 breaths/minute for the majority of participants (60%), 8 breaths/minute for 24% and 10–12 breaths/minute for the remaining 16%. The intervention typically started in the second week of hospitalisation (mean=10.8 treatment days; range=5–21 days), with morning sessions beginning on average at 7:01 and evening sessions at 20:38. During screening, participants reported similar expected benefits for both interventions for the morning (mean=3.9, both interventions) and evening sessions (mean=3.9, both interventions).

Sample characteristics are detailed in table 1, which also presents the data stratified by clinic. Statistical analyses revealed a higher proportion of patients with F32/F33 diagnoses, lower BDI-V scores, higher expectations regarding the effectiveness of the morning intervention and fewer patients were taking antipsychotics and somatic medications in Clinic 2 compared with Clinic 1. The remaining clinical and demographic parameters were similar between the two clinics. ANOVAs with clinic and diagnosis type (F45, F32/F33) as additional factors revealed no statistically significant effects on study outcomes and are therefore not reported.

Treatment outcomes

Primary outcomes

Changes in somatic well-being (morning) and arousal (evening)

ANOVAs revealed significant effects for measurement time (preintervention vs postintervention) on both FAW Scales in the morning (body perception: p=0.043, η²p=0.05 (95% CI 0.00 to 0.17); vitality: p=0.007, η²p=0.09 (95% CI 0.01 to 0.22)), with higher scores postintervention. Body perception significantly improved from Monday to Friday (p=0.008; η²p=0.09 (95% CI 0.01 to 0.22)), while vitality scores did not change across weekdays (F(1,77)=0.967, p=0.329). No significant effects of the intervention or interaction effects on either FAW Scale were observed. Detailed results are shown in online supplemental table B1.

An ANOVA for the cognitive arousal subscale of the PSAS revealed no significant main effects for intervention or weekday, nor a significant interaction effect. However, a significant moderate main effect of weekday was observed for the somatic arousal subscale (p=0.001, η²p=0.13 (95% CI 0.02 to 0.27)), indicating decreasing somatic arousal across weekdays. Neither the factor intervention nor the intervention-weekday interaction reached significance for presleep somatic arousal. Online supplemental table B2 provides a comprehensive overview of the analyses.

Adverse treatment effects

Among the 12 items evaluating adverse treatment effects, only perceived glare ratings showed significant differences between the two interventions, both in the morning (t(77)=2.925; p=0.005) and evening (t(77)=3.001; p=0.004) sessions. Glare was perceived as stronger under the brighter intervention, specifically during the morning session in the resonant breathing condition and during the evening session in the control condition. However, perceived glare ratings were low (range: 1.5 to 2.4). The remaining light-related items and all breathing-related items did not yield significant differences between the two interventions (see online supplemental table B3).

Secondary outcomes

Acute cardiac intervention effects

Significant effects of the main factor intervention were observed for the HRV parameters pNN50, RMSSD, SD1, SD2, LF, total power and LF:HF ratio (all p<0.001; see online supplemental table C1). HRV parameters were significantly higher (large effect sizes, η²p ranging from 0.23 (95% CI 0.09 to 0.38) to 0.51 (95% CI 0.35 to 0.62)) under the resonant breathing condition than under the control condition. No significant main effects of the intervention were found for RR intervals and the HRV parameter HF. The factor daytime (morning vs evening session) was significant for RR intervals (moderate effect size, η²p=0.09 (95% CI 0.01 to 0.22)), SD2 (large effect size, η²p=0.21 (95% CI 0.07 to 0.36)) and total power (moderate effect size, η²p=0.08 (95% CI 0.00 to 0.20)), with higher scores consistently observed in morning sessions (see online supplemental table C1). The remaining HRV parameters did not differ significantly between morning and evening sessions. For the factor weekday (Monday vs Friday sessions), neither RR intervals nor any HRV parameters reached statistical significance (see online supplemental table C1).

ANOVAs further revealed two significant interaction effects. First, a significant intervention—daytime interaction was observed for the HRV parameter LF (p=0.047, small effect size, η²p=0.05 (95% CI 0.00 to 0.17); see online supplemental table C1), with lower LF Scores in the evening sessions in the placebo condition than in the resonant breathing condition. Second, a significant intervention—weekday interaction was found for the LF:HF ratio (p=0.010, moderate effect size, η²p=0.08 (95% CI 0.01 to 0.22); see online supplemental table C1), with an increase under resonant breathing from Monday to Friday. Detailed descriptive and inferential statistics are presented in online supplemental table C1.

Changes in psychological distress and insomnia severity

A two-factor ANOVA with repeated measures revealed a significant reduction in psychological distress (BSI, total score) for the factor weekday, with lower scores on Friday than on Monday (p<0.001, large effect size, η²p=0.50 (95% CI 0.34 to 0.61), see online supplemental table C2). No significant effect of intervention or intervention-weekday interaction effect was observed for the total BSI Score. For the BSI subscales somatisation and depression, similar results were found: a significant effect of weekday (both p<0.001, with lower scores on Friday compared with Monday; large effect size, 0.28 (95% CI 0.12 to 0.42) ≤η²p≤0.31 (95% CI 0.15 to 0.45); see online supplemental table C1), but no significant effect for intervention. However, for the BSI subscale anxiety, a significant main effect of weekday (p<0.001; large effect size, η²p=0.38 (95% CI 0.21 to 0.51)) and a significant intervention-weekday interaction (p=0.008; moderate effect size, η²p=0.09 (95% CI 0.01 to 0.22)) were observed. Anxiety scores decreased significantly in the resonant breathing condition, whereas no changes were observed in the control condition (see online supplemental table C2).

Subjective insomnia severity (ISI) also showed a significant effect of weekday (p=0.015; moderate effect size, η²p =0.08 (95% CI 0.00 to 0.20)) and intervention-weekday interaction (p=0.005; moderate effect size, η²p =0.10 (95% CI 0.01 to 0.23)), see online supplemental table C2. Insomnia severity improved (lower ISI scores) over time in the resonant breathing condition, with no changes observed in the control condition (see online supplemental table C2).

Treatment expectancies and satisfaction

Treatment expectancies assessed during screening indicated positive expectations for both interventions, with no significant differences between the morning (t(77)=0.000, p=1.000) and evening (t(77)=−0.191, p=0.849) interventions (see online supplemental table D1). Wilcoxon tests on satisfaction items for both interventions revealed no significant differences in the proportion of participants ‘recommending the intervention to a friend’ and ‘re-using the intervention again when needed’ (z=−0.707, p=0.480; for both items). Overall, acceptance rates for both items ranged from 83% to 90% (see online supplemental table D2).

Discussion

Interpretation

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate whether adjunctive light-guided resonant breathing could improve somatic well-being and reduce arousal in hospitalised patients with psychiatric disorders and persistent somatic symptoms. Our principal finding was that the intervention did not generate a specific effect on these primary outcomes in the initial phase of inpatient treatment when compared with a sham light therapy control. While general improvements in body perception and somatic arousal were observed over the treatment week, these changes were observed in both interventions and thus likely reflect the effects of the overall inpatient treatment programme.4

Several factors related to our specific clinical population and study design may explain the lack of effect on the primary outcomes. The participants were in the acute phase of hospitalisation, presenting with moderate-to-severe symptom loads. Patients with persistent somatic symptoms often exhibit heightened attention to bodily signals and hold maladaptive cognitions about them,1 which may have hindered their perception of subtle, immediate changes in somatic well-being and arousal. It is also plausible that the intervention period of 10 sessions (each lasting 15 min) over 1 week was insufficient to alter these deeply internalised perceptual patterns.

Despite the null findings for the primary outcomes, this study found that resonant breathing led to significant improvements in several secondary outcomes. Specifically, after 10 sessions, the intervention significantly (with moderate effect sizes) reduced self-reported symptoms of anxiety and insomnia compared with the control condition. This finding aligns with prior research on the anxiolytic effects of SB and their positive impact on sleep outcomes.11 The effect cannot be attributed to patient expectations, as anticipated benefits were rated similarly for both the active and control interventions. The observed improvement in insomnia may be linked to the enhancement of parasympathetic activity by resonant breathing, which is crucial for restorative sleep.32

Our findings should be considered within the broader context of other non-pharmacological interventions. Techniques such as PMR, AT, yoga and mindfulness meditation are well-established components of care, known to be effective for reducing anxiety and depressive symptoms.33 34 While these methods are valuable, they can be multifaceted, requiring detailed instruction, physical engagement (as in yoga), or significant cognitive effort for sustained attention (as in mindfulness meditation), which can be challenging for acutely ill patients with a high symptom burden. In contrast, the light-guided resonant breathing used in the present study offers a simple, physiologically targeted approach with a very low cognitive load. This makes it a particularly accessible intervention that can be implemented early in hospitalisation, even before patients may have the capacity to engage in more complex behavioural therapies.

The intervention also generated immediate and statistically significant physiological effects. Resonant breathing acutely improved several HRV parameters with large effect sizes (η²p from 0.232 to 0.508), indicating a successful enhancement of autonomic regulation. The increase in the LF:HF ratio observed from Monday to Friday, specifically within the resonant breathing condition, suggests a potential cumulative effect on autonomic function over the 5-day period. This is consistent with research indicating that repeated sessions are necessary to modify autonomic regulation patterns in clinical populations.9 While our HRV findings confirm an acute physiological response, the values differed from those in healthy populations,35 highlighting the distinct autonomic regulation in patients experiencing an acute psychiatric episode.

Finally, the study established that resonant breathing is a safe and well-accepted intervention for this patient population. Participants reported no adverse effects related to the breathing exercise itself and expressed high satisfaction, with 83%–90% stating they would recommend or reuse the intervention. This high degree of acceptability is particularly relevant for inpatients who, early in inpatient treatment, may have limited capacity to engage in more complex psychosocial interventions.

Strengths and limitations

The randomised, placebo-controlled, crossover design is a key strength, allowing each participant to serve as their own control. This methodology minimised the influence of confounding variables like ongoing medication regimens and the general therapeutic effects of hospitalisation, thereby isolating the specific effects of resonant breathing on anxiety, insomnia and HRV.

However, the study has several limitations. First, the cross-over design over 2 weeks, although helpful to enhance statistical power, limits the evaluation of long-term intervention effects and precludes follow-up assessments, as carry-over effects cannot be fully ruled out in behavioural interventions.36 Second, this study relied on several subjective measures and acute cardiac measures. To increase the robustness of the findings, these assessments should be complemented by further objective measures, such as polysomnography for sleep quality or 24-hour HRV measurements, which are often recommended to validate subjective changes in psychiatric research.37 Third, the study did not control for specific breathing techniques, which may have introduced variability in intervention effects. Future studies should investigate the differential physiological effects of specific techniques, such as diaphragmatic breathing versus cyclic sighing, as evidence suggests they are not interchangeable.38 Fourth, the inpatient setting and the inclusion of patients with two specific disorders limit generalisability to other mental health conditions or outpatient settings. Fifth, individual factors such as age, gender and psychiatric comorbidities were not explored as potential moderators of the treatment effect, which is a key step in personalising behavioural interventions.39 Sixth, the light-based pacemaker, although delivering a subtherapeutic dose of light, may have had non-visual effects on alertness or mood that could have confounded the results.40 Seventh, the study did not assess participants’ prior experience with breathing exercises or other relaxation techniques. Previous experience could have influenced study outcomes and should be controlled for in future studies. Lastly, the specific intervention design (5 days, 15-min sessions twice daily) does not allow for recommendations on the optimal duration or frequency of breathing interventions, for which formal dose-response studies would be required.

Implications

Despite its limitations, this study provides valuable clinical knowledge by demonstrating that resonant breathing is a safe, well-accepted and effective adjunctive intervention for a challenging patient population. For clinicians working with hospitalised patients with psychiatric disorders and persistent somatic symptoms, this research offers a practical, low-demand tool that can be implemented early in treatment. Specifically, it provides evidence for targeting two common and distressing co-occurring symptoms, showing significant reductions in both anxiety and insomnia after 10 sessions. The simplicity of the intervention makes it particularly suitable for patients with a high symptom burden who may initially struggle to engage in more complex psychosocial therapies.
