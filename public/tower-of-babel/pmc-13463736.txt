Addition of malignancy into the CHADS2/CHA2DS2VASc score: better prediction of stroke risk in individuals with cancer and atrial fibrillation
Authors: Zoha Majeed, Rochell Issa, Tess Calcagno, Ryan Issa, Neehal Shukla, Rohan Prasad, Arianne Clare Agdamag, David C Kaelber, Diego Sadler, Alok A. Khorana, Mohamed Kanj, Rohit Moudgil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe CHADS₂ and CHA₂DS₂VASc scoring systems guide thromboprophylaxis in atrial fibrillation (AF), but their performance is limited in patients with cancer. This study evaluated whether malignancy functions as an independent risk factor for ischemic stroke among patients with AF.MethodsUsing the TriNetX research network, we identified patients with AF with and without malignancy between 2015 and 2022, excluding those with prior cerebrovascular disease. Propensity score matching for CHA₂DS₂VASc variables and anticoagulation yielded two cohorts of 234,428 patients each. The primary outcome was ischemic stroke; secondary outcomes included major bleeding and all-cause mortality. Outcomes were assessed at 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 5 years following the index event.ResultsAt 3 months, stroke incidence was significantly higher among patients with AF and malignancy compared with AF alone (OR 2.092; CI 1.990–2.200). This increased risk persisted at 6 months (OR 1.978), 1 year (OR 1.795), and 5 years (OR 1.585). Mortality and major bleeding rates were also consistently higher in the AF and malignancy cohort across all time intervals.ConclusionPatients with AF and malignancy demonstrated a significantly higher risk of ischemic stroke despite matching for established CHA₂DS₂VASc risk factors, supporting malignancy as a potential independent contributor to stroke risk. Mortality and major bleeding were likewise higher, reflecting the combined burden of malignancy and AF. The relative odds of ischemic stroke exceeded those of major bleeding across all time points. Future studies should evaluate whether incorporating malignancy into the CHA₂DS₂VASc score improves stroke risk stratification in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40959-026-00513-y.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a common cardiac arrhythmia and an important contributor to ischemic stroke worldwide. In a large meta-analysis, Odutayo et al. demonstrated that AF is associated with increased all-cause mortality and major cardiovascular outcomes, including stroke and heart failure, highlighting its global clinical burden [1]. Cancer and AF frequently coexist: in a nationwide Dutch cohort, Chen et al. demonstrated that each condition independently increased the risk of the other, and their coexistence was associated with higher all-cause mortality [2]. As cancer survival improves, the overlap between AF and malignancy is expected to grow, making cerebrovascular complications an increasingly important issue in cardio-oncology.

To manage stroke risk, clinical guidelines recommend risk-stratification tools such as CHADS₂ and CHA₂DS₂VASc, which incorporate factors such as heart failure, hypertension, age, diabetes, and prior stroke or thromboembolism to estimate stroke risk [3]. While widely used, these tools were developed in populations that largely excluded individuals with malignancy and may not fully capture the complex thrombotic and bleeding risks associated with cancer. Emerging data suggest that patients with cancer represent a distinct population with competing risks of thrombosis and bleeding, yet existing studies have largely been limited to short-term follow-up or inpatient settings, resulting in heterogeneous and sometimes conflicting estimates of stroke risk in this population.

Given these limitations, there remains uncertainty regarding the long-term risk of ischemic stroke in patients with AF and malignancy. Leveraging a large, propensity-matched cohort from the TriNetX global health research network, we sought to evaluate longitudinal stroke risk in patients with AF with and without malignancy across multiple time points. We additionally assessed major bleeding and all-cause mortality to provide a more comprehensive understanding of clinical outcomes in this population. By providing real-world, population-level evidence, this study aims to inform whether cancer should be incorporated into existing stroke risk stratification frameworks and guide future refinements in cardio-oncology risk assessment.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from the TriNetX platform, a global federated health research network that provides access to electronic medical records (EMRs) from large healthcare organizations (HCOs). The analysis utilized data from the U.S. Collaborative Network within TriNetX, which includes 64 HCOs across the United States. The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committees and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. The study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of each participating HCO. Patient consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data. This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [4].

Cohort selection

Two cohorts of individuals diagnosed with AF, including paroxysmal, persistent, or chronic, between January 2015 and January 2022 were identified from the TriNetX registry. Cohort A consisted of individuals with malignancy and a diagnosis of AF. Cohort B consisted of individuals without malignancy but had a diagnosis of AF. People were excluded if they had any prior history of cerebrovascular disease, including ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke. AF was defined by the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) code I48.x, which includes paroxysmal, persistent, and chronic AF. Malignancy was defined using ICD-10-CM code C80.1, which in large multi-institutional EHR systems is frequently used when an active malignant process is documented without a specified site, excluding brain and skin cancers. The end of the index window was set at January 2022 to provide current data while allowing adequate time for the selected outcomes to occur at each of the set time-points for most of the individuals entering the time window.

Propensity score matching

The inclusion and exclusion criteria yielded 234,606 individuals in Cohort A and 943,343 individuals in Cohort B. Baseline characteristics of these individuals leading up to the index event were then run to identify demographic, diagnoses, procedural, surgical, and medication information. Subsequently, propensity score matching (PSM) was utilized to balance baseline characteristics between the cohorts and minimize confounding biases in comparative analyses creating two groups of 234,428 individuals each. The matched variables included age at index, sex, race, ethnicity, T2DM, hypertensive diseases, tobacco use, heart failure, and anticoagulation.

Clinical outcomes

The primary outcome was ischemic stroke. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality and major bleeding events. Stroke was defined as any instance of cerebral infarction (ICD-10-CM I63) or transient cerebral ischemic attacks and related syndromes (ICD-10-CM G45). Hemorrhagic stroke was not included, as patients with any prior history of cerebrovascular disease were excluded at baseline. All-cause mortality was identified by “deceased” within the TriNetX database. Major bleeding was defined using a combination of diagnosis and procedure codes consistent with clinically significant bleeding events. Diagnosis codes included non-traumatic intracranial hemorrhage (I60, I61, I62), hemoperitoneum (K66.1), retroperitoneal hematoma (K68.3), gastrointestinal bleeding (K92.2, K62.5, K25.0, K25.4, K55.21), ocular hemorrhage (H11.3, H35.6, H43.1), and hemorrhage not elsewhere classified (R58). Procedure codes indicating active control of bleeding were also included (e.g., CPT 42961, 43501, 45382, 45334, 46614; ICD-10-PCS 0W3R0ZZ, 0W3R3ZZ, 0W3R4ZZ, 0W3R7ZZ, 0W3R8ZZ). This ICD and procedure-based definition aligns with the clinical categories encompassed by the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) criteria for major bleeding, although laboratory and transfusion-based components of the ISTH definition cannot be assessed within TriNetX [5]. All outcomes are reported at the time-points of 3-months, 6-months, 1-year, and 5-years during the entire duration of available follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Utilizing the compare outcomes analytic function of TriNetX, Measure of Association Analyses and Survival Analyses were run. The Measure of Association Analysis was used to calculate and compare the fraction of patients with the selected outcome between both propensity matched groups. We also generated Kaplan-Meier curves for all outcomes and used log-rank tests to compare outcomes at 3-months, 6-months, 1-year, and 5-years. Censoring was applied such that patients were removed from the risk set after the last recorded clinical encounter. In this analysis, patients were removed from the analysis (censored) after the last data entry in their record. Baseline anticoagulation use was included as a covariate in the propensity score model; however, outcomes were not stratified by anticoagulation status.

Net benefit

Net benefit was assessed by calculating the ratio of the odds of ischemic stroke to the odds of major bleeding in individuals with AF and malignancy (cancer group), providing a comparative measure of thrombotic versus bleeding risk over time. Across all time intervals, the risk of ischemic stroke consistently exceeded the risk of major bleeding. The net benefit was greatest at 3 months (1.634), indicating 63.4% higher odds of stroke compared to major bleeding. This value declined gradually over time, with net benefit ratios of 1.524 at 6 months, 1.330 at 1 year, and 1.204 at 5 years. Table 3.

Discussion

Principal findings

Our large, retrospective cohort study demonstrates that individuals with AF and cancer experience a significantly higher incidence of ischemic stroke. This was calculated as an OR of 1.585–2.092, corresponding to an approximately twofold increase in stroke risk in individuals with cancer and AF. We also observed that this cohort also exhibits an increased risk of all-cause mortality and major bleeding. Our study expands on prior studies by evaluating stroke incidence in individuals with AF across a comprehensive range of cancer types, excluding brain and skin cancers. Additionally, it evaluates hard outcomes of all-cause mortality and major bleeding events in this high-risk population, providing a broader perspective of individual outcomes while the cohorts are propensity matched for anticoagulation.

Comparison with prior studies

Evidence evaluating stroke risk in patients with atrial fibrillation and malignancy remains inconsistent, reflecting differences in study design, follow-up duration, and patient populations. El-Rayes et al. reported comparable stroke incidence between AF patients with and without cancer but observed a significantly higher risk of major bleeding, emphasizing the competing risks in this population [6]. In a national database analysis, Wahbeh et al. observed that AF among patients hospitalized with cancer-related acute ischemic stroke was associated with higher in-hospital mortality, longer length of stay, and reduced likelihood of discharge home, highlighting the adverse clinical impact of this overlap [7]. Similarly, Sheth et al. demonstrated increased rates of both thromboembolic and bleeding events among patients with active malignancy, underscoring the complexity of anticoagulation management [8]. Ullah et al. reported lower short-term stroke risk among cancer patients with AF using a 30-day outcome framework [9]. Likewise, Al Mouslmani et al. found that the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score demonstrated limited discriminatory ability for stroke prediction in patients with AF and cancer, suggesting that traditional risk stratification tools may perform sub-optimally in this population [10]. These findings, largely derived from short-term or inpatient-focused analyses, provide insight into early or acute risk but may not fully capture the longitudinal trajectory of stroke risk in this population. Taken together, these studies illustrate the heterogeneity of existing evidence. In contrast, our study leverages longitudinal real-world data with follow-up extending to 5 years and incorporates propensity matching, including anticoagulation use, demonstrating a consistently elevated risk of ischemic stroke in patients with AF and malignancy across multiple time points.

Stroke risk and CHA2DS2VASc score

The odds ratios (OR) derived from our study show a clear and persistent elevation in stroke risk among individuals with both AF and malignancy. At 3 months, the odds of stroke were over twice as high in the cancer cohort (OR 2.092, 95% CI 1.990–2.200, p < 0.001), and this increased risk remained statistically significant at 6 months (OR 1.978, 95% CI 1.895–2.065, p < 0.001), 1 year (OR 1.795, 95% CI 1.734–1.857, p < 0.001), and 5 years (OR 1.585, 95% CI 1.545–1.620, p < 0.001). The findings highlight the substantial and sustained impact of malignancy on stroke risk in individuals with AF.

The CHA₂DS₂VASc score, derived from foundational studies such as the Atrial Fibrillation Investigators (AFI) and Stroke Prevention in Atrial Fibrillation (SPAF) trials and later validated in the National Registry of Atrial Fibrillation (NRAF), identified key predictors of stroke in patients with AF but notably excluded individuals with malignancy [11–13]. While subsequent refinement from CHADS₂ to CHA₂DS₂VASc improved sensitivity, its overall discriminative performance remains modest, with reported C-statistics of approximately 0.66 [14]. In contrast, the Wells criteria for venous thromboembolism demonstrate stronger discriminatory performance (C-statistic 0.75–0.80) and explicitly incorporate malignancy as a risk factor, highlighting the potential value of including cancer in thromboembolic risk stratification models [15, 16]. More recent evidence highlights the limitations of CHA₂DS₂VASc in oncology populations: Matetic et al. demonstrated poor discriminatory ability of CHA₂DS₂VASc for ischemic stroke prediction in patients with AF and cancer (AUC ≈ 0.54) [17], while Bungo et al. showed that incorporating cancer status modestly improved predictive performance compared with the conventional model [18]. Similarly, Seth et al. emphasized that current thromboembolic and bleeding risk stratification tools lack formal validation in cancer populations, underscoring the need for refinement in this setting [19]. In contrast to prior analyses, including those evaluating cancer-modified CHA₂DS₂-VASc models, our study incorporated anticoagulation as a matched covariate, allowing for clearer delineation of malignancy’s independent contribution to stroke risk [20]. Within this framework, the observed association of malignancy with stroke risk was of similar clinical relevance to established risk factors such as diabetes and blood pressure incorporated in the Framingham stroke risk model for AF [21]. These findings suggest that malignancy may represent an important and independent contributor to stroke risk in patients with AF, supporting consideration of cancer as a modifier of existing risk stratification approaches and highlighting the need for further refinement of current tools.

Mechanistic rationale for adding cancer to the CHA2DS2VASc score

The higher stroke risk in individuals with both AF and cancer can be attributed to several factors that promote a hypercoagulable state. Cancer is a well-established risk factor for both venous and arterial thromboembolism, mediated through tumor-related hypercoagulability, systemic inflammation, and endothelial injury. The release of tumor-associated procoagulants, inflammatory cytokines, and reactive oxygen species within the tumor microenvironment promotes atrial remodeling and electrical instability, predisposing individuals to atrial arrhythmias [22].

This biologic susceptibility is reflected clinically, as captured by the cancer-specific Khorana score, which was developed to estimate the risk of chemotherapy-associated venous thromboembolism [23]. In addition, a bidirectional relationship exists between cancer and AF, with each condition contributing to worse clinical outcomes [2]. These pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic processes provide a biologically plausible basis for increased thromboembolic risk in this population.

This mechanism parallels the pathophysiology underlying hypertensive heart disease, a well-established risk factor for stroke in AF, as described by Dzeshka et al. [24]. When AF occurs in the context of malignancy, the combination of systemic hypercoagulability, vascular inflammation, and treatment-related toxicity further amplifies thromboembolic potential. Cancer therapies, including cytotoxic and targeted agents, further contribute to structural remodeling, neurohormonal changes, and electrical instability, increasing susceptibility to AF and thromboembolic events [25].

In a single-center case–control study, Khamis et al. reported that AF was more common among cancer patients who experienced stroke or transient ischemic attack and remained an independent predictor after adjustment, although the small sample size and retrospective design limit generalizability [26]. Together, these findings provide strong biologic plausibility for the observed association between malignancy and increased stroke risk in patients with AF.

Mortality

Our study finds that there is an increased incidence of all-cause mortality in individuals with AF and malignancy. Mortality rates were significantly higher in the cancer group at all time points, with a 1-year mortality rate of 16.4% compared to 10.4% in the non-cancer group (OR 1.687, p < 0.001) and a 5-year mortality rate of 33.5% compared to 24.7% (OR 1.536, p < 0.001). These results are consistent with previous research, demonstrating the adverse impact of cancer on survival in individuals with AF [2, 6, 8]. Our study provides a more granular, long-term perspective across multiple time points. The elevated mortality risk observed in the cancer cohort is likely influenced by the underlying disease process and complications of cancer-directed therapies. Among patients with AF, the coexistence of cancer was associated with significantly higher mortality compared with AF patients without cancer. This excess risk may reflect the combined impact of malignancy and AF-related complications, including stroke, heart failure, and overall arrhythmia burden. This forces us to investigate how we can modify the elevated mortality risk in this cohort; a space where more prospective research is needed.

Major bleeding risk

The increased risk of major bleeding in this cohort further complicates management decisions, particularly in the context of anticoagulation and cancer-associated thrombosis management [27]. At 3 months, 3.6% of the cancer group experienced major bleeding compared to 2.8% in the non-cancer group (OR 1.280, p < 0.001), with the gap widening at later time points. By 5 years, the cumulative incidence of major bleeding events was 12.1% in the cancer group versus 9.5% in the non-cancer group (OR 1.314, p < 0.001). This increased bleeding risk is likely multifactorial, resulting from the combined effects of cancer-associated coagulopathies, anticoagulant use, and cancer treatments that affect platelet function or induce thrombocytopenia. These findings raise important considerations for balancing stroke prevention and bleeding risk in individuals with AF and cancer, suggesting that individualized anticoagulation strategies may be warranted in this population.

Net benefit

Despite the elevated risk of bleeding, our analysis suggests a net clinical benefit of anticoagulation in individuals with both cancer and AF. As shown in Table 3, at every time point evaluated, the odds of experiencing an ischemic stroke were consistently higher than the odds of a major bleeding event. This net benefit appears to be most pronounced around the time of cancer diagnosis and gradually diminishes over time, though it remains favorable overall. These findings highlight the continued value of stroke prevention in this high-risk population, even in the presence of increased bleeding risk. Further studies are needed to identify specific cancer types and treatment regimens in which anticoagulation offers the greatest benefit, as well as those associated with higher bleeding risk. Ultimately, refined risk stratification tailored to cancer subtype and treatment may improve outcomes and reduce overall mortality in this vulnerable cohort.

Clinical implications of adding cancer to CHA2DS2VASc: CaCHA2DS2VASc

Cancer and its treatments present a double-edged sword, promoting thromboembolic disease through procoagulant factors while simultaneously increasing bleeding risk due to complications like thrombocytopenia and tumor-induced vascular invasion [27, 28]. The addition of AF to this complex interplay further exacerbates stroke risk in this population [17]. Addition of malignancy will improve the accuracy and precision of stroke risk stratification in individuals with cancer and AF. This will allow for an individualized, patient-centric approach to anticoagulation. It is known that solid tumor cancers, including lung, gastrointestinal and breast cancers, have a higher incidence of thromboembolic phenomenon. Navi et al. looked at data from the SEER-Medicare database and found that lung, colorectal and pancreatic cancers had the highest incidence of ischemic stroke [29]. Therefore, the importance of independent effect of malignancy on stroke risk in individuals with AF is substantial. This could be particularly consequential for individuals classified as low risk by traditional scoring systems as they still have significant risk of stroke. This can ultimately shift the threshold for initiating anticoagulation in individuals with cancer and AF, as current guidelines recommend anticoagulation based on the score’s calculated risk. The inclusion of malignancy would appropriately risk stratify this cohort and allow shared decision making to guide anticoagulation therapy, potentially preventing debilitating or fatal strokes. It is prudent to recognize this special population early on and make appropriate recommendations for stroke prevention. One such alternative to anticoagulation is the use of left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) devices in this cohort as it significantly reduces the risk of thromboembolic stroke from the left atrial appendage while limiting the duration these individuals need to be on anticoagulation. LAAO devices represent one such intervention that may address these competing risks. More studies are needed to investigate the use of LAAO devices and perhaps even consider them first line therapies for this unique population.

Future directions

Future research should focus on refining risk stratification in patients with AF and malignancy by incorporating cancer-specific factors, including cancer type, stage, and treatment exposure. Prospective studies are needed to validate modified risk scores and to better define the optimal balance between thromboembolic disease prevention and bleeding risk in this population. In addition, further investigation into the role of alternative strategies, such as LAAO, may help identify subgroups of patients who derive the greatest benefit from non-pharmacologic approaches. Ultimately, integrating oncologic and cardiovascular risk profiles may enable more precise, individualized management strategies in this complex population.

Limitations

Our study included individuals with various malignancies (including solid, hematologic and metastatic) whilst excluding brain and skin cancers. However, it lacks subgroup analysis across different cancer types, activity status (active cancer vs. prior history of cancer), and exposure to specific anticancer therapies (chemotherapy vs. immunotherapy). This data is not consistently available in TriNetX. It did not differentiate stroke risk between AF with solid tumors versus hematologic malignancies. Future studies should include detailed oncologic characteristics to better delineate how tumor biology and treatment influence cerebrovascular risk. Although the cohorts were propensity score–matched for baseline anticoagulation use, we could not stratify outcomes by anticoagulant type. Future studies should compare outcomes among patients treated with different direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) and vitamin K antagonists to clarify differential risks of bleeding and stroke in AF with cancer which could provide valuable insight and guide clinical decisions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that cancer is a significant, independent risk factor for stroke, major bleeding, and mortality in individuals with AF. Based on these and prior findings, inclusion of cancer in stroke risk stratification models, such as the CHA2DS2VASc score, making it CaCHA2DS2VASc is clearly warranted. Clinicians managing individuals with AF and cancer should carefully weigh the risks and benefits of anticoagulation and consider tailored therapeutic approaches to improve outcomes in this challenging population.
