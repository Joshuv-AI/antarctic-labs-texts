Elastic displacements in wedge-shaped geometries with a straight edge: Green’s functions for perpendicular forces
Abdallah Daddi-Moussa-Ider, Andreas M. Menzel

Abstract
Edges are abundant when fluids are contained in vessels or elastic solids glide in guiding rails. We here address induced small-scale flows in viscous fluids or displacements in elastic solids in the vicinity of one such edge. For this purpose, we solve the underlying low-Reynolds-number flow equations for incompressible fluids and the elasticity equations for linearly elastic, possibly compressible solids. Technically speaking, we derive the associated Green’s functions under confinement by two planar boundaries that meet at a straight edge. The two boundaries both feature no-slip or free-slip conditions, or one of these two conditions per boundary. Previously, we solved the simpler case of the force being oriented parallel to the straight edge. Here, we complement this solution by the more challenging case of the force pointing into a direction perpendicular to the edge. Together, these two cases provide the general solution. Specific situations in which our analysis may find application in terms of quantitative theoretical descriptions are particle motion in confined colloidal suspensions, dynamics of active microswimmers near edges, or actuated distortions of elastic materials due to activated contained functionalized particles.



I
Introduction

In three-dimensional (3D) incompressible, isotropic, homogeneous bulk viscous fluids, motion at small scales and low speeds is well understood in terms of low-Reynolds-number hydrodynamics
Happel and Brenner 2012
. The Green’s function is available in terms of the Oseen tensor for the corresponding linear Stokes equations
Dhont, J. K. G. 1996
;
Kim and Karrila 2013
. Analogous relations apply to elastic deformations in 3D linearly elastic, homogeneous, isotropic, possibly compressible bulk solids. There, the Green’s function for the displacement field as described by the associated linear Navier–Cauchy equations is available as well
Cauchy 1828
;
Love 2013
. In these situations, the motion of passive particles in liquid suspensions or active self-propelled microswimmers in fluids are well analyzed and quantitatively described
Lauga and Powers 2009
;
Elgeti, Winkler, and Gompper 2015
;
Lauga 2016
;
Zöttl and Stark 2016
. The same is true for displacements of actuated particles in linearly elastic solids
Huang et al. 2016
;
Puljiz et al. 2016
;
Puljiz and Menzel 2019
.

When boundaries come into play, the situation rapidly complicates. In the presence of one planar, infinitely extended no- or free-slip boundary, corresponding Green’s functions are still available both for the viscous fluid and linearly elastic solid case
Blake 1971
;
Felderhof, B. U. 2005
;
Menzel 2017
. Likewise, there are strategies of analytical or quasi-analytical solutions in the presence of two parallel, infinitely extended no- or free-slip boundaries
Liron and Mochon 1976
;
Felderhof, B. U. 2006
;
Felderhof, B. U. 2010
;
Swan and Brady 2011
;
Mathijssen et al. 2016a
;
Mathijssen et al. 2016b
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2018
;
Lutz, Menzel, and Daddi-Moussa-Ider 2024
.
Other geometries include three-dimensionally bounded domains, which are finite systems and have been intensely studied analytically, such as spherical systems
Walpole 2002
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider and Gekle 2017
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider, Lisicki, and Gekle 2017
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider, Löwen, and Gekle 2018
;
Fischer and Menzel 2019
;
Hoell et al. 2019
;
Sprenger et al. 2020
;
Kree, Rueckert, and Zippelius 2021
;
Fischer and Menzel 2020
;
Fischer and Menzel 2024a
;
Kawakami and Vlahovska 2025
.

In the present work, we consider a 3D geometry in which two infinitely extended planar boundaries meet at a finite angle. There, they bound the system by a straight edge.
Overall, the boundaries form a wedge-shaped confinement.
Problems involving corner geometries have garnered considerable interest in recent decades.
In this case, a fundamentally different mathematical approach is required once the planar boundaries are not parallel to each other.
Previously, the general Stokes flow near a two-dimensional corner has been investigated
Dean and Montagnon 1949
;
Moffatt 1964
;
Crowdy and Brzezicki 2017
.
It was observed that if one or both of the corner boundaries are no-slip walls and the corner angle is below a certain critical value, the flow near the corner exhibits a series of eddies that diminish in size and intensity with proximity to the corner.

Low-Reynolds-number flows in incompressible viscous fluids near 3D corners have been analyzed in pioneering works by Sano and Hasimoto through the study of increasingly complex wedge-shaped geometries
Sano, O. and Hasimoto, H. 1976
;
Sano and Hasimoto 1977
;
Sano and Hasimoto 1978
;
Hasimoto, H. and Sano, O. 1980
;
Sano 1977
.
Not long ago, the 3D Stokes flow problem near a corner has been revisited and explored using a complex analysis approach
Dauparas and Lauga 2018
;
Dauparas 2018
.
The associated Green’s function under free-slip wedge-shaped confinement was recently derived for commensurate opening angles of the wedge. For this purpose, an image method was applied
Sprenger and Menzel 2023
.
The Stokes flow in a semi-infinite wedge, bounded not only by sidewalls but also by a bottom wall, has been further investigated
Shankar 2000
.

More recently, in our prior article
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2025
, we addressed the more general geometry of commensurate or incommensurate opening angles of the wedge. We considered both low-Reynolds-number fluid flows or linearly elastic solids confined by the wedge, where no-slip or free-slip conditions prevailed on both boundaries or one such condition on each boundary.
This situation is severely more challenging. Therefore, we so far had only solved the simpler case of an external point force applied to the viscous fluid or elastic solid in a direction parallel to the edge of the wedge
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2025
. By point force we understand a constant force applied at only one point in the system. The mathematical expression for the resulting flow velocity field or elastic displacement field corresponds to the fundamental solution or Green’s function.

Now, in the present work, we complete this solution. We here derive the Green’s function for point forces directed perpendicular to the straight edge of the wedge. Together, due to the linearity of the underlying equations, the general case of arbitrarily directed point forces and thus the general expression for the Green’s function follows by superposition of the parallel and perpendicular cases.

We proceed in the following way. Due to possible compressibility, the description of linearly elastic solids is generally more complex than for low-Reynolds-number flows of incompressible fluids. Therefore, we focus on the equations for elastic displacement fields. The results for viscous flows can then be obtained by setting the Poisson ratio to one half, reinterpreting the displacement field as the flow field, and identifying the shear modulus of the solid with the shear viscosity of the fluid. We overview in Sec.
II
the underlying mathematical framework. In Sec.
III
, we solve the equations after transformation into the Fourier-Kontorovich-Lebedev space. Real-space expressions for the Green’s functions are provided in Sec.
IV
.
Exact analytical solutions are derived in Sec.
V
by taking the limit of planar boundaries, thereby recovering previously known results.
We conclude the paper in Sec.
VI

We analyze the Green’s function in a wedge-shaped elastic medium with a straight edge forming its tip, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. Our system is described in a cylindrical coordinate frame
(r,\theta,z)
.
z
-axis coincides with the straight edge of the wedge where its two surfaces meet. These two surfaces of the wedge are positioned along polar angles
\theta=\pm\alpha
, with
\alpha\in(0,\pi/2]
.
A point force is applied at
(r,\theta,z)=(\rho,\beta,0)
, oriented perpendicular to the
z
-axis.
We define
\delta
as the angle between the direction of the force and the radial axis. Thus,
\delta=0
corresponds to a purely radial force and
\delta=\pi/2
to a purely azimuthal force.

Our objective is to determine the displacement field induced by the point force in the wedge-shaped elastic material.
The displacement field
\bm{u}(\bm{x})
of material elements within an isotropic, homogeneous, linearly elastic medium subjected to a force density
\bm{f}(\bm{x})
is governed by the Navier-Cauchy equation,

\mu\Delta\bm{u}(\bm{x})+\left(\mu+\lambda\right)\bm{\nabla}\left(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x})\right)+\bm{f}(\bm{x})=\mathbf{0}\,,

(1)

with
\lambda
and
\mu
representing the first and second Lamé parameters, respectively, also known as the Lamé coefficients or Lamé moduli.
We define the dimensionless number

\sigma=\frac{\lambda+3\mu}{\lambda+\mu}\,,

(2)

which, physically, takes values in the interval
[1,7)
, with the limit of incompressibility corresponding to
\sigma=1
.

In the following, we examine combinations of two types of boundary conditions, no-slip (NOS) and free-slip (FRS) conditions. An NOS boundary condition indicates no relative motion between the material and the surface of the wedge.
This implies
\bm{u}=\bm{0}
at the boundary. An FRS boundary condition is characterized by impermeability. That is, the normal component of the displacement at the boundary, here given by
u_{\theta}
, satisfies
u_{\theta}=0
, along with relaxed tangential shear, given by
\partial u_{r}/\partial\theta=\partial u_{z}/\partial\theta=0
.
We consider three scenarios, (i) no-slip boundary conditions applied on both walls at
\theta=\pm\alpha
, (ii) free-slip boundary conditions on both walls at
\theta=\pm\alpha
, and (iii) mixed boundary conditions, with NOS at
\theta=-\alpha
and FRS at
\theta=\alpha
.

II.1
Solution approach

A general solution to Eq. (
1
) is expressed as

\bm{u}=\bm{\nabla}\left(\bm{x}\cdot\bm{\Phi}+\Phi_{w}\right)-(\sigma+1)\bm{\Phi}\,,

(3)

wherein
\bm{x}=(x,y,z)
denotes the position vector in the system of Cartesian coordinates and
\bm{\Phi}=\Phi_{x}\,\hat{\bm{e}}_{x}+\Phi_{y}\,\hat{\bm{e}}_{y}+\Phi_{z}\,\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}
.
Here,
\Phi_{j}
are harmonic functions satisfying Laplace’s equation,
\Delta\Phi_{j}=0
, for
j\in\{x,y,z,w\}
.
Alternative representations of the general solution to the equations of equilibrium in linear elasticity have also been reported
Papkovich 1932
;
Neuber 1934
;
Naghdi and Hsu 1961
;
Lurie 2010
;
Boussinesq 1885
;
Palaniappan 2011
.

The components of the displacement field in cylindrical coordinates are given by

\displaystyle u_{r}

\displaystyle=\left(r\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}-\sigma\right)\Phi_{r}+z\,\frac{\partial\Phi_{z}}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial\Phi_{w}}{\partial r}\,,

(4a)

\displaystyle u_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Phi_{r}}{\partial\theta}-(\sigma+1)\Phi_{\theta}+\frac{z}{r}\frac{\partial\Phi_{z}}{\partial\theta}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\Phi_{w}}{\partial\theta}\,,

(4b)

\displaystyle u_{z}

\displaystyle=r\,\frac{\partial\Phi_{r}}{\partial z}+\left(z\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-\sigma\right)\Phi_{z}+\frac{\partial\Phi_{w}}{\partial z}\,,

(4c)

where the radial and azimuthal components of
\Phi
are determined through coordinate transformations

\begin{pmatrix}\Phi_{r}\\
\Phi_{\theta}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\Phi_{x}\\
\Phi_{y}\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

In an infinitely extended, linearly elastic bulk medium, the displacement field induced by a point force
\bm{F}
positioned at
\bm{x}_{0}
is given by
Stakgold and Holst 2011

\bm{u}^{\infty}=\frac{1}{4\pi\mu\left(\sigma+1\right)s}\left(\sigma\,\bm{I}+\hat{\bm{s}}\hat{\bm{s}}\right)\cdot\bm{F},

(6)

where
\bm{I}
is the identity tensor, and
\bm{s}=\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0}
, with
s=|\bm{s}|
and
\hat{\bm{s}}=\bm{s}/s
.

In a wedge geometry, the general solution for the four harmonic functions is expressed as

\Phi_{j}=\phi_{j}^{\infty}+\phi_{j}\,,

(7)

where
\phi_{j}^{\infty}
, for
j\in\{r,\theta,z,w\}
, are the free-space harmonic functions in an unbounded elastic medium, while
\phi_{j}
denote additional harmonic fields necessary to satisfy our boundary conditions.

II.2
Integral transform analysis

The Kontorovich-Lebedev transform (KL-transform) was introduced by Soviet mathematicians Kontorovich and Lebedev in the late 1930s to solve specific boundary-value problems
Kontorovich and Lebedev 1938
;
Kontorovich and Lebedev 1939
.
The KL-transform naturally emerges when using the method of separation of variables to solve boundary-value problems formulated in cylindrical coordinates.
Transform tables have been provided by Erdélyi
et al.

(
Erdélyi et al. 1953
, p. 75)
, along with various other types of integral transforms.

The KL-transform is a well-established technique for solving partial differential equations when a radial transform is appropriate.
It is typically expressed in terms of modified Bessel functions of the second kind, although equivalent representations are also available in the literature
Lebedev, Skal’skaya, and Ufliand 1966
;
Jones 1980
.
The mathematical theory was developed later by Lebedev
Lebedev 1946
;
Lebedev 1949
.
Unlike more familiar transforms such as Fourier or Laplace transforms, the KL-transform belongs to the category of index integral transforms
Yakubovich 1996
;
Polyanin and Manzhirov 2008
.
The latter are a class of integral transforms where the transformed function depends on an index parameter, often associated with special functions.
In recent decades, Yakubovich and his collaborators have made numerous original contributions to the theory of KL-transforms
Yakubovich 2003
;
Yakubovich 2012
;
Rodrigues, Vieira, and Yakubovich 2013
;
Loureiro and Yakubovich 2013
.

Applications of the KL-transform to boundary value problems were presented by Lebedev and Kontorovich
Lebedev and Kontorovich 1939
.
The transform has been widely used for various physical problems involving wedges, primarily in solving electromagnetic scattering and diffraction problems
Lowndes 1959
;
Rawlins 1999
;
Antipov 2002
;
Salem, Kamel, and Osipov 2006
;
Hwang 2009
;
Kim, Eom, and Hwang 2009
;
Shanin and Valyaev 2011
;
Eom 2014
;
Lyalinov 2016
or studying fluid flows
Waechter 1969a
;
Waechter 1969b
.
Other applications include analyzing the Schrödinger equation
Yakubovich and Vieira 2013
;
Lyalinov 2020
and quantifying the escape probability for integrated Brownian motion with nonzero drift
Atkinson and Clifford 1994
.

In the following, we denote the hyperbolic sine and hyperbolic cosine functions by
\operatorname{sh}
and
\operatorname{ch}
, respectively. Additionally, the hyperbolic secant and hyperbolic cosecant functions are denoted by
\operatorname{sch}
and
\operatorname{csh}
, respectively, and are defined as
\operatorname{sch}(x)=1/\operatorname{ch}(x)
and
\operatorname{csh}(x)=1/\operatorname{sh}(x)
.

Combining the KL-transform with the classical Fourier transform of the axial coordinate yields the Fourier-Kontorovich-Lebedev transform (FKL-transform), defined by the double integral over the radial and axial coordinates

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(\nu,k)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{f(r,z)\right\}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k\,e^{ikz}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)K_{i\nu}(|k|r)\,r^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}r\,,

(8)

where
K_{i\nu}
denotes the modified Bessel function of the second kind
Abramowitz and Stegun 1972
, also known in the context of index transforms as Macdonald’s function, with purely imaginary order
i\nu
.
Note that for a positive argument,
K_{i\nu}
results in a real number when
\nu
is constrained to be real.
It is clear that the transform in Eq. (
8
) leaves the polar angle unaffected.
The inverse FKL-transform is given by the double integral

\displaystyle f(r,z)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}^{-1}\{\widetilde{f}(\nu,k)\}=\frac{1}{\pi^{3}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k\,e^{-ikz}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{f}(\nu,k)\,K_{i\nu}(|k|r)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\,\nu\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,.

(9)

We here use a positive exponent for the forward Fourier transform and a negative exponent for the inverse Fourier transform.

Applying this transform, we convert the partial differential equation, Eq. (
1
), that governs the displacement field in an elastic medium into a set of ordinary differential equations for the polar angle
\theta
.
We find that the FKL-transform of the Laplace equation results in a homogeneous second-order ordinary differential equation for the transformed function, given by

\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}-\nu^{2}\right)\widetilde{f}=0\,.

(10)

In the following, we utilize a key property of the FKL-transform related to division by
r
. By applying the recurrence relation that links Macdonald’s function to its adjacent orders, it can be shown that

\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{\frac{f}{r}\right\}=\frac{|k|}{2i\nu}\,\big(\mathscr{T}_{i\nu+1}\{f\}-\mathscr{T}_{i\nu-1}\{f\}\big)\,.

(11)

III
Green’s function in FKL space

III.1
Green’s function in an infinite medium

In an unbounded medium, the displacement field caused by a point force applied in the direction perpendicular to the wedge is derived from

\displaystyle\phi_{x}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=\chi\cos(\beta+\delta)\,,

(12a)

\displaystyle\phi_{y}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=\chi\sin(\beta+\delta)\,,

(12b)

\displaystyle\phi_{w}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=-\rho\chi\cos\delta\,,

(12c)

and
\phi_{z}^{\infty}=0
.
Here, we have defined

\chi=-\frac{q_{\perp}}{s}\,,\qquad q_{\perp}=\frac{F_{\perp}}{4\pi\mu\left(1+\sigma\right)}\,,

(13)

with the distance from the singularity position

s=\left(r^{2}+\rho^{2}-2\rho r\cos(\theta-\beta)+z^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

(14)

As demonstrated in Ref.
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2025
, the FKL-transform of
\chi
is given by

\widetilde{\chi}=-2\pi q_{\perp}\,\frac{\operatorname{ch}\left((\pi-|\theta-\beta|)\nu\right)}{\nu\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)}\,K_{i\nu}(|k|\rho)\,.

(15)

It follows from the coordinate transforms that the radial and azimuthal components of the free-space contribution to the displacement field are given by

\phi_{r}^{\infty}=\chi\cos(\theta-\beta-\delta)\,,\quad\phi_{\theta}^{\infty}=-\chi\sin(\theta-\beta-\delta)\,.

(16)

III.2
General solution

Since they are harmonic functions that satisfy the Laplace equation, it follows from Eq. (
10
) that the general solution for
\widetilde{\phi}_{j}
, where
j\in\{x,y,z,w\}
, is given by

\widetilde{\phi}_{j}=A_{j}\operatorname{sh}(\theta\nu)+B_{j}\operatorname{ch}(\theta\nu)\,,

(17)

with
A_{j}
and
B_{j}
as unknown coefficients. They will subsequently be determined from the boundary conditions imposed at
\theta=\pm\alpha
.

We express the coefficients in the form

\displaystyle A_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi q_{\perp}}{\nu\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)}\,\left(\Lambda_{j}+\Omega_{j}\,\rho\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)K_{i\nu}(|k|\rho)\,,

(18a)

\displaystyle B_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi q_{\perp}}{\nu\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)}\,\left(\Lambda_{j}^{\dagger}+\Omega_{j}^{\dagger}\,\rho\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)K_{i\nu}(|k|\rho)\,.

(18b)

Here,
\Lambda_{j},\Lambda_{j}^{\dagger},\Omega_{j}
, and
\Omega_{j}^{\dagger}
are functions of
\nu
and depend solely on the parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\delta
, and
\sigma
.
We note that
\Omega_{w}
and
\Omega_{w}^{\dagger}
are non-vanishing only for
j\in\{x,y\}
.
In the following, we provide their full expressions for the three types of boundary conditions considered in this study.

III.3
No-slip–no-slip boundary conditions (2NOS)

We require the displacement field to vanish at both surfaces of the wedge. Thus, we impose the condition
u_{r}=u_{\theta}=u_{z}=0
at
\theta=\pm\alpha
.
Since there are eight unknown coefficients and only six equations for the boundary conditions, we have some flexibility in choosing two of the coefficients. From the radial and axial components of the displacement field given by Eq. (
4
), we select

\phi_{w}+\phi_{w}^{\infty}=0\,,\quad\phi_{r}+\phi_{r}^{\infty}=0\,,\quad\phi_{z}=0\,,

(19)

evaluated at
\theta=\pm\alpha
.
Since the general solution for
\phi_{z}
is given by Eq. (
17
), and it must vanish at both boundaries, it follows that
\phi_{z}
is zero everywhere.

The condition for vanishing azimuthal displacement leads to

\hskip-2.84544pt\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\phi_{r}+\phi_{r}^{\infty}+\frac{\phi_{w}+\phi_{w}^{\infty}}{r}\right)-(\sigma+1)\left(\phi_{\theta}+\phi_{\theta}^{\infty}\right)=0,

(20)

evaluated at
\theta=\pm\alpha
.
Defining the abbreviation

\vartheta=\theta-\beta-\delta\,,

(21)

it follows that the corresponding equations in FKL-space are derived as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}_{w}-\rho\,\widetilde{\chi}\,\cos\delta

\displaystyle=0\,,

(22a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}_{r}+\widetilde{\chi}\,\cos\vartheta

\displaystyle=0\,,

(22b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{\phi}_{r}}{\partial\theta}-(\sigma+1)\,\widetilde{\phi}_{\theta}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\,\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{\frac{\widetilde{\phi}_{w}}{r}\right\}+\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(22c)

evaluated at
\theta=\pm\alpha
, with the right-hand side

\mathcal{R}=\sigma\widetilde{\chi}\,\sin\vartheta+\frac{\partial\widetilde{\chi}}{\partial\theta}\,\cos\vartheta-\rho\cos\delta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\,\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{\frac{\widetilde{\chi}}{r}\right\}.

(23)

The unknown coefficients are determined by substituting the forms in Eqs. (
17
) into Eqs. (
22
) and solving the resulting system of equations.
For future reference, we introduce the abbreviations

\zeta_{\pm}=\alpha\pm\beta
,

\xi_{\pm}=\alpha\pm\beta\pm\delta
,

\alpha_{\pm}=\pi\pm\alpha
,

\beta_{\pm}=\pi\pm\beta
, and

\tau=\beta+\delta
.

The coefficients associated with
\widetilde{\phi}_{w}
take a simple form because their governing equation is decoupled from the other fields. They are expressed as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{w}

\displaystyle=-\rho\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{sh}\left(\alpha_{-}\nu\right)\operatorname{csh}(\alpha\nu)\cos\delta\,,

(24a)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{w}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=-\rho\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{ch}\left(\alpha_{-}\nu\right)\operatorname{sch}(\alpha\nu)\cos\delta\,.

(24b)

In addition,
\Omega_{w}=\Omega_{w}^{\dagger}=0
.
Contrariwise, the remaining coefficients associated with

and
\widetilde{\phi}_{y}
have a more delicate structure and are written as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{x}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{+}\left(b\cos\tau+\nu\left({a}_{-}+{c}_{-}\operatorname{ch}(\alpha\nu)\right)\sin\alpha\right),

(25a)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{y}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{-}\left(b\sin\tau+\nu\left({a}_{+}-{c}_{+}\operatorname{ch}(\alpha\nu)\right)\cos\alpha\right),

(25b)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{x}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{-}\left(b^{\dagger}\cos\tau-\nu\left({a}_{+}^{\dagger}-{c}_{+}\operatorname{sh}(\alpha\nu)\right)\sin\alpha\right),

(25c)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{y}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{+}\left(b^{\dagger}\sin\tau-\nu\left({a}_{-}^{\dagger}+{c}_{-}\operatorname{sh}(\alpha\nu)\right)\cos\alpha\right).

(25d)

In addition,

\displaystyle\Omega_{x}

\displaystyle=-\Pi_{+}d_{-}\sin\alpha\operatorname{ch}(\alpha\nu)\,,

(26a)

\displaystyle\Omega_{y}

\displaystyle=\phantom{+}\Pi_{-}d_{+}\cos\alpha\operatorname{ch}(\alpha\nu)\,,

(26b)

\displaystyle\Omega_{x}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=-\Pi_{-}d_{+}\sin\alpha\operatorname{sh}(\alpha\nu)\,,

(26c)

\displaystyle\Omega_{y}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\phantom{+}\Pi_{+}d_{-}\cos\alpha\operatorname{sh}(\alpha\nu)\,,

(26d)

where

\Pi_{\pm}^{-1}=\sigma\operatorname{sh}(2\alpha\nu)\pm\nu\sin(2\alpha)\,.

(27)

For convenience, we define the following expressions to present the results in a more compact form

\displaystyle{a}_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\cos\xi_{+}\operatorname{sh}\left(\beta_{-}\nu\right)\pm\cos\xi_{-}\operatorname{sh}\left(\beta_{+}\nu\right)\,,

(28a)

\displaystyle{a}_{\pm}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\cos\xi_{+}\operatorname{ch}\left(\beta_{-}\nu\right)\pm\cos\xi_{-}\operatorname{ch}\left(\beta_{+}\nu\right)\,,

(28b)

together with

\displaystyle{c}_{\pm}

\displaystyle=Z_{\pm}\left(\cos\zeta_{-}\operatorname{ch}\left(\zeta_{+}\nu\right)\pm\cos\zeta_{+}\operatorname{ch}\left(\zeta_{-}\nu\right)\right)\,,

(29a)

\displaystyle d_{\pm}

\displaystyle=Z_{\pm}\left(\sin\zeta_{-}\operatorname{sh}\left(\zeta_{+}\nu\right)\pm\sin\zeta_{+}\operatorname{sh}\left(\zeta_{-}\nu\right)\right)\,,

(29b)

where
Z_{\pm}=2\cos\delta\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\,\Delta_{\pm}
with

\Delta_{\pm}^{-1}=\operatorname{ch}\left(2\alpha\nu\right)\pm\cos(2\alpha)\,.

(30)

Moreover,

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=2\sigma\operatorname{ch}(\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\alpha_{-}\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)\,,

(31a)

\displaystyle b^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=2\sigma\operatorname{sh}(\alpha\nu)\operatorname{ch}(\alpha_{-}\nu)\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\,.

(31b)

III.4
Free-slip–free-slip boundary conditions (2FRS)

Next, we impose free-slip conditions on both surfaces by requiring that

u_{\theta}=0\,,\qquad\frac{\partial u_{r}}{\partial\theta}=0\,,\qquad\frac{\partial u_{z}}{\partial\theta}=0\,,

(32)

evaluated at
\theta=\pm\alpha
.
A simple form of the solution is obtained by imposing that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\phi_{r}+\phi_{r}^{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle=0\,,\hskip 35.56593pt\phi_{\theta}+\phi_{\theta}^{\infty}=0\,,

(33a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi_{z}}{\partial\theta}

\displaystyle=0\,,\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\phi_{w}+\phi_{w}^{\infty}\right)=0\,,

(33b)

evaluated at
\theta=\pm\alpha
.
We use the same reasoning as for 2NOS. Since the general solution for
\phi_{z}
is given by Eq. (
17
) and since its derivative with respect to
\theta
must vanish at both boundaries,
\phi_{z}
is identically zero.
The corresponding expressions in FKL-space read

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{\phi}_{r}}{\partial\theta}-\widetilde{\chi}\,\sin\vartheta+\frac{\partial\widetilde{\chi}}{\partial\theta}\,\cos\vartheta

\displaystyle=0\,,

(34a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}_{\theta}-\widetilde{\chi}\,\sin\vartheta

\displaystyle=0\,,

(34b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{\phi}_{w}}{\partial\theta}-\rho\,\frac{\partial\widetilde{\chi}}{\partial\theta}\,\cos\delta

\displaystyle=0\,,

(34c)

evaluated at
\theta=\pm\alpha
.

The coefficients of
\widetilde{\phi}_{w}
again have a simple form because its governing equation is decoupled from the other fields and can be written as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{w}

\displaystyle=\rho\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{ch}\left(\alpha_{-}\nu\right)\operatorname{sch}(\alpha\nu)\cos\delta\,,

(35a)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{w}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\rho\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{sh}\left(\alpha_{-}\nu\right)\operatorname{csh}(\alpha\nu)\cos\delta\,,

(35b)

and
\Omega_{w}=\Omega_{w}^{\dagger}=0
.
Defining

\displaystyle W_{x}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\cos\hat{\xi}_{+}\operatorname{sh}(\beta_{-}\nu)-\cos\hat{\xi}_{-}\operatorname{sh}(\beta_{+}\nu)\right),

(36a)

\displaystyle W_{x}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\cos\hat{\xi}_{+}\operatorname{ch}(\beta_{-}\nu)+\cos\hat{\xi}_{-}\operatorname{ch}(\beta_{+}\nu)\right),

(36b)

\displaystyle W_{y}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\sin\hat{\xi}_{+}\operatorname{sh}(\beta_{-}\nu)+\sin\hat{\xi}_{-}\operatorname{sh}(\beta_{+}\nu)\right),

(36c)

\displaystyle W_{y}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\sin\hat{\xi}_{+}\operatorname{ch}(\beta_{-}\nu)-\sin\hat{\xi}_{-}\operatorname{ch}(\beta_{+}\nu)\right),

(36d)

with
\hat{\xi}_{\pm}=2\alpha\pm\beta\pm\delta
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Lambda_{x}

\displaystyle=\phantom{+}\Delta_{+}\left(W_{x}-\cos\tau\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{ch}\left(\hat{\alpha}\nu\right)\right),

(37a)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{x}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{-}\left(W_{x}^{\dagger}-\cos\tau\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{ch}\left(\hat{\alpha}\nu\right)\right),

(37b)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{y}

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{-}\left(W_{y}+\sin\tau\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{ch}\left(\hat{\alpha}\nu\right)\right),

(37c)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{y}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\phantom{+}\Delta_{+}\left(W_{y}^{\dagger}+\sin\tau\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{ch}\left(\hat{\alpha}\nu\right)\right),

(37d)

where
\hat{\alpha}=\pi-2\alpha
.
We recall that
\Delta_{\pm}
was previously defined in Eq. (
30
).
In addition,
\Omega_{j}=\Omega_{j}^{\dagger}=0
for
j\in\{x,y\}
.

III.5
No-slip–free-slip boundary conditions (NOS–FRS)

Finally, we consider the case of mixed boundary conditions by imposing an NOS condition at
\theta=-\alpha
and an FRS condition at
\theta=\alpha
. Specifically, we enforce Eqs. (
22
) at
\theta=-\alpha
and Eqs. (
34
) at
\theta=\alpha
.
The coefficients take more complex expressions due to the varied boundary conditions.
We obtain for the
w
-related coefficients

\displaystyle\Lambda_{w}

\displaystyle=\rho\,\frac{\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)+\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{ch}\left(\hat{\alpha}\nu\right)}{\operatorname{ch}(2\alpha\nu)}\,\cos\delta\,,

(38a)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{w}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\rho\,\frac{\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)-\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{ch}\left(\hat{\alpha}\nu\right)}{\operatorname{ch}(2\alpha\nu)}\,\cos\delta\,.

(38b)

In addition,
\Omega_{w}=\Omega_{w}^{\dagger}=0
.

For convenience, we now introduce the abbreviations
\hat{\zeta}_{\pm}=2\alpha\pm\beta
,
\overline{\alpha}=\pi-4\alpha
,
\varsigma=3\alpha-\beta
, and
\eta=3\alpha-\beta-\delta
.
The remaining coefficients are cast in the form

\displaystyle\Lambda_{j}

\displaystyle=Q\big(\nu\left(ZG_{j}+H_{j}\right)+\sigma K_{j}\big)\,,

(39a)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{j}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=Q\left(\nu\left(ZG_{j}^{\dagger}-H_{j}^{\dagger}\right)-\sigma K_{j}^{\dagger}\right),

(39b)

and

\Omega_{j}=-QYG_{j}\,,\quad\Omega_{j}^{\dagger}=-QYG_{j}^{\dagger}\,,

(40)

for
j\in\{x,y\}
, where we have defined

Q=\left(\sigma\operatorname{sh}(4\alpha\nu)-\nu\sin(4\alpha)\right)^{-1}\,.

(41)

The other coefficients are expressed as

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=M\left(\cos\varsigma\operatorname{ch}\left(\zeta_{+}\nu\right)+\cos\zeta_{+}\operatorname{ch}\left(\varsigma\nu\right)\right)\,,

(42a)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=M\left(\sin\varsigma\operatorname{sh}\left(\zeta_{+}\nu\right)+\sin\zeta_{+}\operatorname{sh}\left(\varsigma\nu\right)\right)\,,

(42b)

with

M=\frac{4\cos\delta\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)}{\operatorname{ch}(4\alpha\nu)+\cos(4\alpha)}\,,

(43)

and

\displaystyle G_{x}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{+}\sin\alpha\operatorname{ch}(\alpha\nu)\,,\,\quad G_{x}^{\dagger}=\Sigma_{+}^{\dagger}\sin\alpha\operatorname{sh}(\alpha\nu)\,,

(44a)

\displaystyle G_{y}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{-}\cos\alpha\operatorname{ch}(\alpha\nu)\,,\quad G_{y}^{\dagger}=\Sigma_{-}^{\dagger}\cos\alpha\operatorname{sh}(\alpha\nu)\,,

(44b)

with

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\pm}

\displaystyle=1-\operatorname{ch}(2\alpha\nu)\pm\cos(2\alpha)\,,

(45a)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\pm}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=1+\operatorname{ch}(2\alpha\nu)\pm\cos(2\alpha)\,.

(45b)

In addition,

\displaystyle H_{x}

\displaystyle=\sin\alpha\,\big(\cos\eta\operatorname{sh}(\beta_{+}\nu)+P_{+}\cos\xi_{+}\big)\,,

(46a)

\displaystyle H_{y}

\displaystyle=\cos\alpha\,\big(\cos\eta\operatorname{sh}(\beta_{+}\nu)+P_{-}\cos\xi_{+}\big)\,,

(46b)

\displaystyle H_{x}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sin\alpha\left(\cos\eta\operatorname{ch}(\beta_{+}\nu)+P_{+}^{\dagger}\cos\xi_{+}\right)\,,

(46c)

\displaystyle H_{y}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\cos\alpha\left(\cos\eta\operatorname{ch}(\beta_{+}\nu)+P_{-}^{\dagger}\cos\xi_{+}\right)\,,

(46d)

where

\displaystyle P_{\pm}

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\operatorname{ch}(\hat{\zeta}_{-}\nu)-\left(1\pm 2\cos(2\alpha)\right)\operatorname{sh}(\beta_{-}\nu),

(47a)

\displaystyle P_{\pm}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\hat{\zeta}_{-}\nu)+\left(1\pm 2\cos(2\alpha)\right)\operatorname{ch}(\beta_{-}\nu).

(47b)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}K_{x}\\
K_{x}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{1}\\
Q_{1}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\cos\tau-\begin{pmatrix}Q_{2}\\
Q_{2}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\cos\hat{\xi}_{-}\,,

(48a)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}K_{y}\\
K_{y}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{1}\\
Q_{1}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\sin\tau-\begin{pmatrix}Q_{2}\\
Q_{2}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\sin\hat{\xi}_{-}\,,

(48b)

where

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sh}\left(\overline{\alpha}\nu\right)\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)-\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\hat{\zeta}_{-}\nu)\,,

(49a)

\displaystyle Q_{1}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sh}\left(\overline{\alpha}\nu\right)\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)-\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\operatorname{ch}(\hat{\zeta}_{-}\nu)\,,

(49b)

and

Q_{2}=\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)+\operatorname{sh}(\hat{\zeta}_{+}\nu)\,,\quad Q_{2}^{\dagger}=\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)-\operatorname{ch}(\hat{\zeta}_{+}\nu)\,.

(50)

IV
Green’s functions in real space

We apply the inverse FKL-transform, as given by Eq. (
9
), to express the displacement field in terms of integrals over the radial and axial wavenumbers. The integration with respect to
k
can be evaluated analytically, reducing the final expressions to a single improper integral over
\nu
, which can be handled numerically.
Since
\widetilde{\phi}_{j}
are even functions of
k
,
j\in\{x,y,w\}
, the inverse complex Fourier transform can be expressed in terms of the inverse cosine Fourier transform.

We define the operator

\Psi_{j}(\theta,\nu)=\psi_{j}(\theta,\nu)+\varphi_{j}(\theta,\nu)\,\rho\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\,,

(51)

where

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(\theta,\nu)

\displaystyle=q_{\perp}\left(\Lambda_{j}\operatorname{sh}(\theta\nu)+\Lambda_{j}^{\dagger}\operatorname{ch}(\theta\nu)\right),

(52a)

\displaystyle\varphi_{j}(\theta,\nu)

\displaystyle=q_{\perp}\left(\Omega_{j}\operatorname{sh}(\theta\nu)+\Omega_{j}^{\dagger}\operatorname{ch}(\theta\nu)\right),

(52b)

and remark that
\varphi_{w}=0
.
The solution is represented in the form

\phi_{j}(r,\theta,z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi_{j}(\theta,\nu)\,\mathcal{K}_{i\nu}(r,z)\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,

(53)

for
j\in\{x,y,w\}
, where the kernel functions are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{i\nu}(r,z)=\left(\tfrac{2}{\pi}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(kz)K_{i\nu}(k\rho)K_{i\nu}(kr)\,\mathrm{d}k\,.

(54)

This improper integral converges. Its value is available in classical textbooks as

\mathcal{K}_{i\nu}(r,z)=\left(\rho r\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}P_{i\nu-\frac{1}{2}}(\xi)\operatorname{sch}(\pi\nu)\,.

(55)

For instance, we refer to the comprehensive table of integrals by Gradshteyn and Ryzhik
(
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2014
, p. 719)
Eq. ET I 50 (51) or Prudnikov
et al.

(
Prudnikov, Brychkov, and Marichev 1992
, p. 390)
Eq. 2.16.36 (2).
Here,
P_{n}
stands for the Legendre function of the first kind of degree
n
, and

\xi=\frac{\rho^{2}+r^{2}+z^{2}}{2\rho r}.

(56)

We have now formulated the unknown harmonic functions as integrals over the radial wavenumber
\nu
, as shown in Eq. (
53
).
While the improper integrals in these expressions could, in principle, be evaluated analytically using the residue theorem, we choose numerical integration for simplicity.
By introducing the change of variable
u=\nu/(\nu+1)
, which gives
\mathrm{d}\nu=\mathrm{d}u/(1-u)^{2}
, the infinite integrals are transformed into well-behaved integrals over the interval
[0,1]
, making them suitable for standard numerical integration techniques.

Finally, the displacement field results from Eqs. (
4
) as

\displaystyle u_{j}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=u_{j}^{\infty}(\bm{x})+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathscr{D}_{j}\,\mathcal{K}_{i\nu}(r,z)\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,,

(57)

for
j\in\{r,\theta,z\}
,
where the elements of the operator
\mathscr{D}_{j}
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{r}

\displaystyle=\Psi_{r}\left(r\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}-\sigma\right)+\Psi_{w}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\,,

(58a)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Psi_{r}}{\partial\theta}-(\sigma+1)\Psi_{\theta}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\Psi_{w}}{\partial\theta}\,,

(58b)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{z}

\displaystyle=r\Psi_{r}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+\Psi_{w}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\,,

(58c)

with the radial component
\Psi_{r}=\cos\theta\,\Psi_{x}+\sin\theta\,\Psi_{y}
and azimuthal component
\Psi_{\theta}=\cos\theta\,\Psi_{y}-\sin\theta\,\Psi_{x}
.

V
Exact solutions in the limit of one planar boundary

The semi-analytical results presented in Sec.
IV
for 2NOS and 2FRS boundary conditions are valid in the interval
\alpha\in(0,\pi/2]
. Therefore, it is possible to recover known results in the limit of one planar boundary. For this purpose, we let the semi-opening angle of the wedge approach
\pi/2
.

For the case of 2NOS, we obtain

\displaystyle\psi_{w}

\displaystyle=-\rho\,q_{\perp}\cos\delta\operatorname{ch}((\theta+\beta)\nu)\,,

(59a)

\displaystyle\psi_{x}

\displaystyle=q_{\perp}\bigg(\cos(\beta+\delta)\operatorname{ch}((\theta+\beta)\nu)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2}{\sigma}\,\cos\beta\sin\delta\,\nu\operatorname{sh}((\theta+\beta)\nu)\bigg),

(59b)

\displaystyle\psi_{y}

\displaystyle=q_{\perp}\sin(\beta+\delta)\operatorname{ch}((\theta+\beta)\nu)\,,

(59c)

together with

\varphi_{x}=-\frac{2}{\sigma}\,q_{\perp}\cos\beta\cos\delta\operatorname{ch}((\theta+\beta)\nu)\,,

(60)

and
\varphi_{y}=\varphi_{w}=0
.
In contrast to that, for the situation of 2FRS, the coefficients in the planar boundary limit become

\displaystyle\psi_{w}

\displaystyle=\rho\,q_{\perp}\cos\delta\operatorname{ch}((\theta+\beta)\nu)\,,

(61a)

\displaystyle\psi_{x}

\displaystyle=q_{\perp}\cos(\beta+\delta)\operatorname{ch}((\theta+\beta)\nu)\,,

(61b)

\displaystyle\psi_{y}

\displaystyle=-q_{\perp}\sin(\beta+\delta)\operatorname{ch}((\theta+\beta)\nu)\,,

(61c)

and
\varphi_{j}=0
for
j\in\{x,y,w\}
.

Therefore, in the limit of one planar boundary, the solution under 2NOS conditions can be written as

\displaystyle\phi_{w}

\displaystyle=-\rho\,q_{\perp}\cos\delta\,\mathscr{S}(\xi,\theta+\beta)\,,

(62a)

\displaystyle\phi_{x}

\displaystyle=q_{\perp}\big(\cos(\beta+\delta)\,\mathscr{S}(\xi,\theta+\beta)-\cos\beta\,\mathscr{Q}\big),

(62b)

\displaystyle\phi_{y}

\displaystyle=q_{\perp}\sin(\beta+\delta)\,\mathscr{S}(\xi,\theta+\beta)\,,

(62c)

with the differential operator

\mathscr{Q}=\left.\frac{2}{\sigma}\left(\sin\delta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial a}+\cos\delta\,\rho\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)\mathscr{S}(\xi,a)\right|_{a=\theta+\beta}\,.

(63)

Here, we have defined the improper integral

\mathscr{S}(\xi,a)=\left(\rho r\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{ch}(a\nu)\operatorname{sch}(\pi\nu)\,P_{i\nu-\frac{1}{2}}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,,

(64)

which converges for
a\in[-\pi,\pi]
and can be evaluated analytically as

\mathscr{S}(\xi,a)=\left(2\rho r\left(\xi+\cos a\right)\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,.

(65)

Similarly, for 2FRS conditions, we obtain

\displaystyle\phi_{w}

\displaystyle=\rho\,q_{\perp}\cos\delta\,\mathscr{S}(\xi,\theta+\beta)\,,

(66a)

\displaystyle\phi_{x}

\displaystyle=q_{\perp}\cos(\beta+\delta)\,\mathscr{S}(\xi,\theta+\beta)\,,

(66b)

\displaystyle\phi_{y}

\displaystyle=-q_{\perp}\sin(\beta+\delta)\,\mathscr{S}(\xi,\theta+\beta)\,.

(66c)

From there, the expressions for the harmonic functions can be simplified to

\displaystyle\phi_{w}

\displaystyle=-q_{\perp}\,\frac{\rho\cos\delta}{\overline{s}}\,,

(67a)

\displaystyle\phi_{x}

\displaystyle=q_{\perp}\bigg(\frac{\cos(\beta+\delta)}{\overline{s}}+\frac{2\rho\cos\beta}{\sigma\overline{s}^{3}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(r\cos(\theta+\beta+\delta)+\rho\cos\delta\right)\bigg),

(67b)

\displaystyle\phi_{y}

\displaystyle=q_{\perp}\,\frac{\sin(\beta+\delta)}{\overline{s}}\,,

(67c)

for 2NOS and to

\displaystyle\phi_{w}

\displaystyle=q_{\perp}\,\frac{\rho\cos\delta}{\overline{s}}\,,

(68a)

\displaystyle\phi_{x}

\displaystyle=q_{\perp}\,\frac{\cos(\beta+\delta)}{\overline{s}}\,,

(68b)

\displaystyle\phi_{y}

\displaystyle=-q_{\perp}\,\frac{\sin(\beta+\delta)}{\overline{s}}\,,

(68c)

for 2FRS. In these expressions

\overline{s}=\left(r^{2}+\rho^{2}+2\rho r\cos(\theta+\beta)+z^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

(69)

denotes the distance from the image system of the point force that is introduced when directly investigating the situation of a planar boundary.
It is straightforward to verify numerically that the resulting displacement fields are in good agreement with previous expressions for both no-slip and free-slip boundary conditions
Menzel 2017
.

VI
Conclusions

In summary, we present the derivation of the Green’s functions for the deformation of a linearly elastic, homogeneous, isotropic, possibly compressible solid when enclosed by a wedge-shaped confinement bounded by a straight edge where its two surfaces meet. On these surfaces, either no-slip or free-slip conditions apply, while we also address the mixed situation of one surface showing no-slip and the other one featuring free-slip conditions.

Our results for the linearly elastic solid carry over to the situation of low-Reynolds-number flows of viscous, homogeneous, isotropic, incompressible fluids, if we identify the displacement field with the flow field, the shear modulus with the shear viscosity, and set the Poisson ratio to one half. Overall, our solutions quantify the displacement or flow fields in response to a constant force applied at one point to the material within the wedge.

In a previous work, we found the associated Green’s functions for the situation of the applied force pointing into a direction parallel to the edge of the wedge. Contrariwise, we now present the solution for the force oriented perpendicular to the edge. In combination, these two cases form the general solution for the wedge-shaped geometry because of the linearity of the underlying equations. They can simply be superimposed.
Likewise, the solution for a general, continuous force density field is obtained by spatial convolution with our solution.

Our resulting expressions can be used to study abundant situations such as sedimenting colloidal particles in suspensions maintained in a vessel
Jones and R. 1999
, propulsion of active microswimmers near edges
Das et al. 2015
, or the excitation of soft elastic composite actuators
Chung, Parsons, and Zheng 2021
near a kink in their confinement. This approach can replace more extensive simulations using, for instance, multiparticle collision dynamics
Gompper et al. 2009
;
Goh et al. 2025
or finite-element methods
Kalina et al. 2023
in these specific geometries. Possibly, they can be combined with simulation methods to speed up corresponding evaluations or serve as input for optimization routines
Fischer and Menzel 2024b
.
