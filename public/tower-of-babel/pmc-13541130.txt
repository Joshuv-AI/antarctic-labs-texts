Acceptability, benefits, and challenges of teleconsultation: A qualitative study among doctors in Manipur
Authors: K. Dhinu, Brogen Singh Akoijam, Kshetrimayum Birendra Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesTeleconsultation through eSanjeevani can reduce the burden on secondary and tertiary hospitals and meet healthcare delivery challenges in rural and remote areas. This study explored doctors’ perspectives to assess the acceptability of teleconsultation and to identify perceived benefits and challenges of teleconsultation services in Manipur.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted from January to May 2024 among doctors registered with eSanjeevani in Manipur. In-depth interviews were conducted with purposively selected doctors from different districts, and data were analysed thematically using an abductive approach. Thematic saturation was achieved after 11 interviews.ResultsInvolved doctors perceived teleconsultation as acceptable to patients, doctors, and the health system. Participants reported improved access to general and specialist care in remote areas, with reduced travel, costs, and time for patients. Key benefits included early case identification, timely referral, follow up of chronic diseases, and maintenance of electronic medical records. Community health officers (CHOs) played an important role in enhancing consultation quality and acceptability. Major challenges included poor network connectivity, limited direct physical examination, limited public awareness, unclear guidelines, increased workload due to the absence of designated telemedicine doctors, the absence of regular review mechanisms, and limited availability of medicines and diagnostic facilities at peripheral facilities.Interpretation and conclusionsTeleconsultation was acceptable and beneficial in improving access to healthcare, despite persistent operational challenges. Designating dedicated telemedicine doctors with structured rosters, regular specialist scheduling, capacity-building for CHOs, improved dissemination of guidelines, IEC-based awareness generation, and stakeholder motivation are essential for sustaining teleconsultation services.

Teleconsultation, enabled by expanding digital penetration in India, is increasingly recognised as a practical approach to address persistent gaps in healthcare access caused by shortages and uneven distribution of medical professionals. India’s engagement with telemedicine has evolved over two decades, beginning with initiatives by the Indian Space Research Organisation, followed by the establishment of the National Telemedicine Task Force (2005) and the Telemedicine Society of India (2006).1,2 Despite early promise, scalable and sustainable models remained limited for many years due to technological constraints, lack of economic incentives, and limited integration into routine healthcare delivery. Recent policy initiatives, including the National Health Policy 2017 and the National Digital Health Blueprint, sought to address these gaps by envisioning a comprehensive national digital health ecosystem.3 The national telemedicine programme, eSanjeevani, launched in 2019–2020, marked a significant shift toward institutionalised teleconsultation services, supported by the release of telemedicine practice guidelines in 2020. The COVID-19 pandemic markedly accelerated the adoption of teleconsultation services in India, with eSanjeevani delivering over 100 million consultations.4 While this rapid scale-up demonstrated feasibility, it may have masked unresolved concerns about clinical adequacy, workload, medico-legal issues, and sustainability.

In States like Manipur, with difficult terrain, a dispersed rural and remote population, and limited access to specialist care, teleconsultation plays a transformative role by delivering equitable health services. Since its launch in Manipur in 2020, teleconsultation has been integrated into primary care and outreach initiatives (e.g. door-to-door screening under the Chief Minister’s Health for All scheme, Health & Wellness centres).5 Despite a prolonged internet shutdown during the conflict in 2023, teleconsultation services were gradually restored once connectivity resumed. Understanding the perspectives of doctors on teleconsultation becomes critical in assessing user satisfaction and identifying barriers, thus informing policy decisions. However, qualitative evidence capturing doctors’ experiences, perceptions, and contextual challenges in settings like Manipur are scarce. This study explores the acceptability of teleconsultation among doctors in Manipur and documents the perceived benefits and challenges of teleconsultation.

Methods

This qualitative study was conducted by the department of Community Medicine, Regional Institute of Medical Sciences, Imphal, Manipur, a northeastern Indian State with an estimated population of 3.19 million (2022 projection) across 16 districts.6 While 57.2% of the population resides in valley districts that constitute only 10% of the geographical area, most tertiary healthcare facilities are concentrated there. Services are delivered through a mix of public and private facilities, including four medical colleges. Telemedicine services operate through designated hubs and CHOs in all districts, with 114 doctors providing over 11,700 teleconsultations by July 2022.

This study was conducted between January and May 2024 among modern medicine doctors practicing in Manipur who were registered for eSanjeevani. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Research Ethics Board. At the end of the interview, participants were allowed to listen to the recording and, if they wished, delete or omit any part of the interview, but no one did. The study was conducted following a 7-mo internet shutdown between May to November 2023, which temporarily disrupted teleconsultation services. Those who could not be contacted after two attempts were excluded. Following permission from the State nodal officer for eSanjeevani, the list of doctors involved in eSanjeevani across various districts was obtained from the health directorate. Of the eligible doctors approached, two declined participation (one due to a medical emergency and being out of station, and another who could not be contacted subsequently despite follow up attempts), while one doctor was excluded as he had been transferred to another district and was no longer engaged in eSanjeevani services. Participants were purposively selected to ensure representation across different districts, genders, and specialties ( Table I).

Doctors were contacted in advance, and verbal informed consent was obtained after explaining the study objectives, including permission to tape-record the entire conversation. A semi-structured interview guide was developed based on the study objectives and review of relevant literature on telemedicine implementation. The guide included open-ended questions exploring doctors’ experiences with teleconsultation, perceived acceptability, benefits in practice, operational challenges, training experiences, and suggestions for improving teleconsultation services. Probing questions were used where necessary to obtain detailed responses. The interview guide was reviewed by faculty members with experience in public health research to ensure clarity and relevance. In-depth interviews were conducted telephonically in English using the interview guide. All interviews were conducted by a trained researcher with a background in public health and prior experience in qualitative research. Interviews lasted 30 to 80 min and were audio-recorded. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved, at which point no new codes or themes emerged from successive interviews; saturation was achieved after 11 interviews.

Data analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, and thematic analysis was done using an abductive approach informed by Braun and Clarke’s framework. Transcripts were repeatedly checked against audio recordings to ensure accuracy and to generate codes. Coding of the transcripts was performed by the first author. Similar codes were grouped into broader categories, and overarching themes were identified. The emerging codes and themes were subsequently reviewed and discussed with senior researchers experienced in qualitative research to ensure consistency and credibility of the analysis, and disagreements were resolved through discussion. Trustworthiness was enhanced through repeated listening to recordings, peer debriefing, and maintenance of an audit trail documenting coding, categorisation, and theme refinement.

THEME 1: Acceptability of teleconsultation:

Teleconsultation was widely perceived as an acceptable and satisfactory alternative to in-person care for both doctors and patients. Despite a 7-mo internet shutdown that temporarily disrupted eSanjeevani services, teleconsultations resumed and expanded across districts once connectivity was restored, reflecting sustained acceptance. Although some doctors initially expressed hesitancy due to the departure from face-to-face consultations, acceptance improved with experience. Respondents perceived strong institutional support, with the State health system expanding teleconsultation through additional hubs and integration into the Chief Minister’s Health for All (CMHA) programme ( Table II).

THEME 2: Benefits of teleconsultation:

Participants described multiple benefits of teleconsultation across patient, provider, and health-system levels. Teleconsultation was perceived to improve access to care in remote and rural areas by overcoming geographical barriers, reducing travel time, and lowering out-of-pocket expenditure. Doctors highlighted its critical role during the COVID-19 pandemic in ensuring continuity of care while minimising infection risk. It was considered effective for specialist consultations, follow up of stable chronic conditions, early identification of emergencies, and facilitating timely referral and preparedness at higher centres. Community health officers (CHOs) were viewed as pivotal in ensuring consultation quality, while electronic medical records supported continuity and retrieval of patient information.

Accessibility: ‘It’s a remote district…in the periphery, there’s hardly any public transport…. It’s very tough for patients to reach the district hospital or Imphal. Instead, we can have a direct teleconsultation on eSanjeevani.’ (P3, Male, 44 y)

‘During the pandemic, it was very helpful…the spread of COVID. The fear from the patient as well as the doctor was removed at that time. So, it was very helpful.’ (P8, Male 40 y)

Reduced costs for accessing healthcare: ‘Traveling, money… especially in periphery…people who work in farms. Unless it’s very serious... They don’t want to go to the hospital as they must spend three hundred… six hundred rupees for traveling. So, they directly consult with the CHO.’ (P10, Male, 41 y)

‘Minor issues... not necessary to burden them with travel costs… someone must accompany them. They also have their job.’ (P11, Male 43 y)

Availability of services/Coverage: ‘For State government, promoting teleconsultation is a very good initiative. They can reach out to a large number of people…provide more services. Compared to no service, it’s better.’ (P3, Male, 44 y)

Specialist consultation: ‘For hilly areas. For doctors…If the patient can’t go to the Imphal area… instead of referring the patient, they can take suggestion…Specialty services should be continued through teleconsultation.’ (P1, Male 38 y)

Timely referral: ‘We can have some idea if the patient is very serious. Suppose if a patient’s BP or sugar is very high, if medication is required… or considering the golden hour… severe chest pain…acute MI, and stroke. We can stay prepared… at least the primary drugs… anti-platelets, statins can be given in the periphery.’ (P10, Male, 41 y)

Follow up of cases: ‘Chronic patients…stable… diabetes, hypertension. They do not need to come to us. Monthly follow up through eSanjeevani is possible. In that way, it helps a lot for the patient.’ (P5, Male, 42 y)

Role of CHOs in enhancing quality of care: ‘If she is along with CHO, CHO can check his or her vitals and inform us… BP is very high. CHO being there, has many advantages.’ (P4, Male, 42 y)

‘When the patient comes to the doctor and when they’re asking history and all by the doctor, they feel afraid…they are more confident at their home and with their community health officers…reveals everything about their disease.’ (P8, Male, 40 y)

Electronic health records: ‘Electronic records…unique ID… can search the patient according to ABHA ID… it will add to record linkage and retrieving records. It will be beneficial.’ (P3, Male, 44 y)

THEME 3: Challenges of teleconsultation:

Participants described challenges at system, provider, and process levels, including poor network connectivity in hilly districts, lack of medicines and investigations at peripheral facilities, and absence of designated teleconsultation staff. Additional barriers included limited public awareness, unclear telemedicine guidelines, and absence of regular review meetings. The lack of direct patient-doctor interaction constrained physical examination and rapport building. Inadequate training for CHOs and limited incentives for specialists were also perceived as threats to service sustainability.

Poor network connectivity: ‘Majority of calls are from the valley areas. Hilly areas’ internet connection is very poor. If it’s not there, even if we have got internet in the centre, that’s not going to solve.’ (P6, Male, 42 y)

Lack of direct physical examination: ‘As a physician, physical examination is a must… No teleconsultation can replace that. Like, serious illness…not easy with teleconsultation.’ (P2, Female, 38 y)

Lack of follow up: ‘If the problem does not go with the teleconsultation, they will go to the hospital. I have not found much follow up by teleconsultation. Safety netting is not there.’ (P2, Female, 38 y)

Lack of awareness: ‘But the level of awareness given to people is very low. If I ask my family members about eSanjeevani, 90% will say that they have no idea.’ (P3, Male, 44 y)

Human resources and infrastructure constraints: ‘ If I’m asked to have my eSanjeevani tomorrow and I’ve been asked to go for a field visit, then I will miss the eSanjeevani… It’s better if there is a dedicated person, dedicated timing for the eSanjeevani; otherwise, it will hamper the OPD Hours.’ (P11, Male 43 y)

Not rewarding for specialists: ‘Like for specialist doctors, it’s not rewarding enough. So, a consultant who has much knowledge beyond that, just sitting in front of the computers… I think consultants are chained to their laptops all day. Keep a whole bunch of specialists just waiting for the call. I think it’s not worth it. It could have been done by the MBBS also…’ (P7, Female, 36 y)

Lack of separate rooms in the hub: ‘Sometimes four or five doctors used to sit at the same time. So, when do consultation… all speaking at the same time, one voice may give problem to the other. We feel that each room should be separate and soundproof.’ (P6, Male, 42 y)

Lack of investigations/medicines: ‘Even if we give medications on teleconsultation…if pharmacy is not available, they have to come to town. If the only drug that we want to give is not available, it is difficult.’ (P7, Female, 36 y)

Lack of training: ‘It was difficult for CHO… like where to enter the data… refresher training should be there. CHOs are not trained for all the diseases, but new responsibilities are coming.’ (P4, Male, 42 y)

Lack of guidelines: ‘… this medicine you can … no such guidelines…But then after telemedicine, because of not being able to diagnose meningitis that person might die also. Then the blame would be more on the doctor who has not recognised the red flag. I think a protocol should be there to identify.’ (P7, Female, 36 y)

Lack of review meetings: ‘Last Saturday every month, I proposed having a meeting. But they never listen. We should have regular review meetings…discuss the problems, the hurdles in the process, and how to improve service provisions.’ (P8, Male, 40 y)

Lack of ownership: ‘If there is properly committed staff and doctors, then it will be beneficial …especially for the patients …that ownership also has to be there rather than taking it as a burden.’ (P4, Male, 42 y)

Discussion

Telemedicine expanded rapidly during the COVID-19 pandemic due to mobility restrictions and concerns regarding infection risk. In the present study, doctors generally perceived teleconsultation as an acceptable and useful component of healthcare delivery in Manipur, particularly for improving access to care in remote areas. Participants highlighted its role in facilitating specialist consultations, reducing travel time and out-of-pocket expenditure for patients, and supporting follow up of stable chronic conditions such as hypertension and diabetes. Teleconsultation was also considered valuable for early identification of emergencies and appropriate referral to higher centres. The involvement of community health officers (CHOs) at peripheral facilities was viewed as an important operational feature that enabled clinical assessment and communication with patients, thereby improving the effectiveness of remote consultations. However, several operational challenges were identified, including poor network connectivity, limitations in physical examination, low public awareness of teleconsultation services, and constraints related to availability of medicines and investigations at peripheral facilities. Doctors involved in teleconsultation services were expected to receive orientation on the teleconsultation platform and consultation workflow as part of programme implementation. Participants reported variable experiences regarding training; some recalled receiving an initial orientation to the teleconsultation platform, while others did not recall any formal training prior to initiating teleconsultation services.

Increasing acceptance of teleconsultation among healthcare providers and patients is consistent with previous research from India.7-12 Similar to earlier studies, participants emphasised the ability of teleconsultation to reduce travel time, costs, and unnecessary referrals, thereby improving healthcare access in underserved populations.7-10,13 Teleconsultation has also been recognised as a strategy for strengthening primary healthcare by enabling specialist support at Health and Wellness centres and supporting triage and followup care.14-16 The integration of teleconsultation into routine public health activities in Manipur, including programmes such as CMHA house-to-house screening and palliative care services, further reflects evidence that digital health interventions are most effective when embedded within existing service delivery systems.14,15,17 Acceptance across diverse patient groups and use across multiple specialties such as dermatology, psychiatry, paediatrics, and diabetes care are also consistent with earlier reports.8-12,17-19 CHOs’ involvement helping to mitigate some limitations of remote consultations was also noted in studies from Jharkhand.17 Challenges reported by participants mirror barriers described in previous telemedicine studies.13,15-23

This study provides contextual qualitative insights into teleconsultation implementation from the perspective of doctors working in a geographically challenging and conflict-affected setting. Inclusion of participants from different districts and specialties enabled exploration of diverse provider experiences and operational realities of teleconsultation services in the State.

Certain limitations should also be considered. The study explored only provider perspectives and may have been influenced by recent internet disruptions. We did not include other stakeholders like patients, CHOs, and data entry operators, involved in teleconsultation delivery. The number of teleconsultation visits or duration of consultations was not assessed. Doctors who had discontinued teleconsultation services were not included in the study; in most instances discontinuation occurred due to administrative transfers to districts where teleconsultation services were already managed by other designated doctors, although their exclusion may still introduce a degree of positive selection bias. All interviews were conducted telephonically, which may have limited observation of non-verbal cues during interviews, although this approach allowed participation of doctors located across geographically dispersed districts. Coding by a single researcher may also introduce interpretive bias. In addition, limited representation of female doctors, and the inherent constraints of purposive sampling and qualitative study design limit the generalisability of findings.

Strengthening teleconsultation services will require improvements in digital infrastructure to address persistent connectivity challenges, particularly in remote areas. Structured induction and refresher training programmes for doctors and community health officers may enhance familiarity with teleconsultation platforms and telemedicine guidelines. Ensuring availability of essential medicines and basic diagnostic services at peripheral facilities would further improve the effectiveness of remote consultations and reduce unnecessary referrals. Increasing community awareness through targeted information, education, and communication activities may also enhance utilisation of teleconsultation services. Integration of teleconsultation records with unique health identifiers such as ABHA IDs and strengthening data privacy safeguards could support continuity of care and improve health information management.
