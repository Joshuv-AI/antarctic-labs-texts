Appraisal of community-based interventions for hepatitis B in underserved populations using the quadruple aim of value-based medicine: a scoping review within the VH-COMSAVAC project
Authors: Filippo Paoletti, Melissa D’Agostino, Egidio De Mattia, Andrea Adduci, Piergiorgio Salerno, Camila A Picchio, Aina Nicolàs, Delfina Boudou, Stefania Boccia, Jeffrey V Lazarus, Antonio Giulio De Belvis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic viral hepatitis remains a critical public health concern, a key focus of WHO’s 2030 elimination targets. High-risk populations, especially migrants often facing significant healthcare barriers, are disproportionately affected globally by this condition. Community-based interventions are crucial for enhancing access to screening, vaccination, and linkage to care. This scoping review, a foundational step within the EU co-funded VH-COMSAVAC project, identifies and analyses how these interventions map to the Quadruple Aim framework of value-based medicine.MethodsThis scoping review adhered to PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Five scientific databases (MEDLINE, Embase, GlobalHealth, Scopus, Web of Science) were systematically searched up to July 30, 2023. Titles and abstracts of studies focusing on community-based HBV interventions, primarily targeting migrants and refugees while also encompassing other underserved populations, were independently screened by five reviewers using the Rayyan platform. Data extraction was conducted using a structured Excel spreadsheet, mapping reported outcomes to the Quadruple Aim framework (patient experience, population health, cost reduction, care team wellbeing) and intervention types (prevention, linkage to care, treatment).ResultsOf 34 included publications, 94% (n = 32) focused on prevention, 29% (n = 10) on linkage to care, and 6% (n = 2) on treatment strategies. Patient experience was the most reported value domain (56%, n = 19 studies), followed by population health (50%, n = 17 studies). Cost reduction (9%, n = 3 studies) and care team wellbeing (3%, n = 1 study) were considerably less represented. Point-of-care (PoC) testing demonstrated significantly higher linkage to care (86% vs. 34% for venepuncture) and 76.5% lower costs (€12.7 vs. €54.0 per screened person). Screening uptake in targeted intervention groups (19.5%) was also significantly higher than in control groups (1.7%; p = 0.014). Major reported barriers included language (62%), cultural/religious issues (50%), and lack of knowledge (32%).ConclusionsThe review reveals a significant gap in the comprehensive, value-based evaluation of existing community-based interventions, with no established framework existing. The VH-COMSAVAC project seeks to bridge this by developing a value-based assessment tool, leveraging the Quadruple Aim, to systematically assess its prevention and linkage-to-care interventions and guide evidence-based optimization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25180-z.Keywords: Hepatitis, Hepatocarcinoma, Underserved populations, Value-based medicine, Community care

Introduction

Chronic viral hepatitis infection, stemming from hepatitis B and C viruses (HBV and HCV), stands as a critical public health concern due to its potential progression to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and cirrhosis if left untreated [1–3]. In 2016, the World Health Assembly aimed to eliminate HBV as a public health threat by 2030, setting targets for a 90% reduction in incidence and a 65% reduction in mortality from 2015 levels [4]. People in prisons, people who inject drugs (PWIDs), migrants, pregnant women (due to the risk of transmission to the child), and people living with HIV are particularly at risk of both spreading and being affected by chronic viral hepatitis. Collectively, these groups are often referred to as “underserved populations,” meaning individuals who experience social, economic, or structural barriers that reduce their ability to receive timely prevention, diagnosis, and treatment. Migrants, in particular, deserve special attention because they may come from countries with a high prevalence of viral hepatitis – such as those in sub-Saharan Africa, East and Southeast Asia, and parts of Eastern Europe – and often face barriers to healthcare access, including language, legal status, and limited health literacy [3]. A systematic review conducted by Rossi et al. reported that migrants from intermediate or high HBV prevalence regions living in low-prevalence countries are at significant risk for chronic HBV infection with a seroprevalence that often mirrors their region of origin [5]. According to the authors, nearly 3.5 million migrants in low-prevalence countries are chronically infected with HBV, with the largest numbers found in the United States (US) (1.6 million), Canada (285,000), and Germany (284,000) [5]. In the European Union (EU) and European Economic Area (EEA), over half of the approximately 50 million foreign-born individuals come from HBV and/or HCV high-endemic regions, notably sub-Saharan Africa, Eastern Europe, and Asia. While only around 10% of the European population are migrants or refugees, they account for approximately 25% of cases of HBV infections in the region [6, 7].

The diagnosis and treatment of HBV remain challenging, particularly among such high-risk groups (8, 9). The World Health Organization (WHO) advocates for “simplified service delivery models” by expanding community-based care for hepatitis B and C to enhance access to care, especially among underserved communities [10]. Key strategies include task-shifting to non-specialist healthcare workers, integrating hepatitis services into primary care, and utilizing point-of-care testing. Additionally, the WHO highlights the importance of community-led initiatives, mobile clinics, and partnerships with NGOs to reach high-risk populations and improve overall care delivery. These approaches are central to community-based interventions, which are delivered outside traditional healthcare settings and are specifically designed to engage underserved populations by bringing services closer to where people live and gather. The potential of community-based strategies targeting this vulnerable group has already been shown by the results of the pilot program Hepatitis B Community Screening and Vaccination in Africans (HBV-COMSAVA) using point-of-care testing. Preliminary results after 1.5 years of the HBV-COMSAVA pilot model indicate an HBsAg prevalence of nearly 10%, with almost 50% of participants lacking evidence of prior immunity [11].

Building on these findings, the ongoing EU co-funded Multicountry Viral Hepatitis COMmunity Screening, Vaccination, and Care (VH-COMSAVAC) project aims to scale up and implement community-based interventions focused on increasing viral hepatitis screening through point-of-care testing, HBV vaccination, and linkage to care in Greece, Italy, and Spain. Additionally, the project seeks to improve epidemiological data and raise both public and individual awareness of viral hepatitis [12, 13].

A key component of VH-COMSAVAC is the assessment of these interventions from a value-based perspective. In this context, value-based medicine (VBM) refers to an approach that considers the benefits of healthcare interventions across multiple dimensions—clinical, economic, and experiential—with the goal of improving quality of care while ensuring efficient use of resource. This scoping review is designed as a foundational step toward that objective. Its primary aim is to identify and analyse existing literature on community-based interventions for HBV prevention, treatment, and linkage to care, with a specific focus on mapping reported outcomes according to the “quadruple aim” framework proposed by Bodenheimer: improved patient experience, enhanced population health, reduced healthcare costs, and better care-team wellbeing (see Table 1.) [14]. While prior studies have explored interventions in community, primary care, and hospital settings [15–19], this review is the first to systematically investigate how and to what extent value-related outcomes are considered in the design and reporting of community-based programs targeting underserved populations. The insights gained will serve to inform and shape the conceptual basis for developing a dedicated tool to support value-based evaluation of these interventions.

Methods

Search strategy

The study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [20].

We searched five scientific databases – MEDLINE Ovid, Embase Ovid, GlobalHealth, Scopus and Web of Science – for studies available up to July 30, 2023. Detailed search terms for each database are outlined in the Appendix 1 of Supplementary Materials. Targeted updates were added to include key relevant literature published afterward.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria are reported in Table 2. While the primary focus of this review was on community-based HBV interventions targeting migrants and refugees, the search strategy was intentionally kept broad. This was to ensure the capture of studies that addressed both HBV and HCV/HCC, or referred more generally to viral hepatitis, and to minimize the risk of overlooking relevant interventions due to overly restrictive search terms. For the purpose of this review, the terms ‘migrants’ and ‘refugees’ will be used interchangeably to encompass foreign-born individuals from high HBV prevalence regions.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for study selection

Study selection

All records retrieved were imported into EndNote version 20, with duplicates automatically removed. For screening, the Rayyan platform was utilized [21]. Five reviewers (DM, dME, AA, FP and PS) autonomously evaluated titles and abstracts for eligibility. Discrepancies were resolved through reviewer discussions or consultation with a third party (AgDB). Full-text screening for final inclusion was conducted on studies that met eligibility criteria. To ensure comprehensive coverage, two reviewers (FP and DM) also examined the reference lists of included studies.

Data extraction and synthesis

The data analysis aimed to identify, within each included study, one or more of the four previously defined value-based healthcare domains (patient experience, population health, cost reduction, and care team wellbeing), as well as one or more of the three main areas of intervention (prevention, linkage to care, and treatment). A standardized reference table was provided to each of them to minimize subjective interpretation and ensure consistency. Because this review aimed to map the breadth of community-based HBV interventions rather than assess study quality or synthesize effect estimates, and given the heterogeneous and often poorly specified study designs, we did not classify the included studies by design type, consistent with the exploratory nature of scoping reviews. Analytical filters and cross-tabulation techniques were applied to explore emerging patterns, relationships, and variations across studies.

No formal protocol was registered for this scoping review.

Results

Figure 1 summarizes the study selection process. A total of 34 publications met the predefined inclusion criteria. The majority of these studies were conducted in the United States (n = 16; 47%), followed by the Netherlands (n = 4; 11%), Italy (n = 3; 9%), Canada (n = 2; 6%), France (n = 2; 6%), and the United Kingdom (n = 2; 6%). Spain, Germany, Colombia, Australia, and Belgium each contributed one study. Regarding the aims of the interventions described, most efforts were directed toward primary or secondary prevention and, in particular, screening initiatives. The majority of studies (n = 32; 94%) was focused on prevention strategies, while 10 (29%) on linkage to care, and only 2 (6%) on treatment strategies. In terms of value domains, patient experience was the most frequently reported (n = 19; 56%), followed by population health (n = 17; 50%). The remaining two domains—cost reduction (n = 3; 9%) and care-team wellbeing (n = 1; 3%)—were significantly less represented.

Predictably, most studies target two main populations: migrants and refugees (n = 24/34 articles; 71%) [22–45] and ethnic or religious minorities (n = 7/34 articles; 21%) [45–51]. Two articles report interventions targeting PWID or people experiencing homelessness (6%) [25, 52], and one targeting primary care physicians, assessing their tools and strategies to improve screening [53]. The most frequently reported obstacles to accessing and linking to care include language (n = 21; 62%) or logistic (n = 8; 24%) barriers, cultural or religious issues (n = 17; 50%), and lack of knowledge or self-awareness of the disease (n = 11; 32%). Other reported challenges are social or family stigma (n = 8; 24%), financial restrictions (n = 5; 15%), confidentiality concerns related to irregular legal status (n = 2; 6%), and competing priorities, such as work and family (n = 1; 3%).

To overcome these barriers, the use of questionnaires, interviews and focus-groups to enhance disease knowledge and awareness is the most commonly reported intervention (n = 14; 41%). The second most common strategy (n = 12; 35%) is the adoption of mediators, navigators, or peers who facilitate access to these communities, followed by cultural or religious outreach initiatives (n = 11; 32%), educational programs (n = 9; 26%), the use of mobile units to deliver interventions (n = 7; 20%), and the implementation of Point-of-Care (PoC) testing (n = 7; 20%). Additional strategies include the use of social media to expand reach (n = 5; 15%), as well as financial incentives (n = 1; 3%) and telemedicine services (n = 1; 3%). Value-based appraisal of HBV interventions

Patient experience

Among the four domains of value-based healthcare, patient experience stands out as the most commonly reported (n = 19; 56%) [22–26, 31, 33, 35, 37–39, 44–46, 49–51, 54, 55]. PoC testing interventions and educational campaigns emerge as the two primary types of interventions associated with improved patient experience. Outcomes are predominantly expressed in terms of attendance rates and acceptance rates. Ho et al. compared traditional venepuncture testing at clinical settings versus opportunistic PoC HBsAg testing during classes or professional activities in Asian populations in Belgium. Compared to venepuncture, PoC testing showed higher linkage to care (86% vs. 34%) and 76.5% lower costs per person screened (12.7 Euro vs. 54.0 Euro) [33]. PoC testing facilitated immediate communication of results and specialist care follow-up on-site, while venepuncture required result delivery within 20–45 days. Similar findings are presented in a study by the Asian Health Coalition, that conducted screenings in both clinical and nonclinical settings, revealing that nonclinical settings, such as health fair events organized by churches and social services agencies, screened twice as many individuals [24]. These findings align with the report by Jones et al., whose feasibility and acceptability study conducted within Haitian immigrant communities living in South Florida advocates for higher adherence rates resulting from community-based screening strategies [35].

In the realm of educational campaigns, Taylor and colleagues detailed their experience in implementing an HBV curriculum in an English as a Second Language program for Chinese immigrants in the United States [50]. Topics covered include the importance of testing for Asian descent immigrants, prevalence of HBV in Asian communities in North America, modes of transmission, and potential consequences of the infection. Despite increased awareness in the follow-up survey, the intervention had limited effectiveness in promoting HBV testing. Another initiative by Veldhuijzen et al. targeted a Chinese community in Rotterdam, involving information sessions, pre- and post-surveys, and on-site HBV testing [56]. Over 91% of participants accepted immediate testing, with 49% showing signs of past or recent HBV infection (anti-HBc positive), and 8.5% being diagnosed with chronic HBV. Thirty-five of the chronically infected patients (38%) were referred for specialist evaluation. In a distinct intervention, Sievert and colleagues addressed cultural communication needs in Afghan, Sudanese, and Rohingyan communities in Australia [57]. They implemented a culturally relevant peer-education intervention with chronic HBV-focused radio programs and community forums in respective languages. This initiative successfully increased chronic HBV knowledge and was deemed cost-effective by the authors. Primary care physicians and General Practitioners (GPs) significantly contribute to enhancing patient experience and encouraging patients to actively seek appropriate care, as demonstrated by the HepFREE trial [31]. The HepFREE trial tested screening strategies for viral hepatitis among first- or second-generation migrants from high-risk countries in 63 UK general practices. Participants were assigned to a control group (opportunistic screening) or one of four intervention groups with targeted screening, varying by care setting (hospital or community) and invitation type (standard or enhanced letters). General practitioners received incentives to encourage screening. Screening uptake was significantly higher in the intervention groups (19.5%) compared to the control group (1.7%; p = 0.014), with the interventions proving cost-effective. No significant difference was found between standard and enhanced invitation letters. Seventeen control and 220 intervention patients tested positive; hospital-based care had slightly higher adherence (87.9%) than community care (81.4%).

Population health

Population Health (PH) ranks as the second most frequently reported value-based domain among selected studies (n = 17; 50%) [22–30, 36, 42, 43, 46–48, 53, 54]. While there is significant overlapping with patient experience, PH interventions prioritize community well-being and reducing health disparities. For this aim, qualitative approaches, such as semi-structured interviews and focus groups [25, 27, 28, 30, 36, 42, 47, 48, 53], are employed to provide deeper insights into cultural nuances, communication strategies, and group dynamics. Daughtridge and colleagues detail the implementation of participatory investigation and peer-led interventions, introducing the Fénix peer leader method to promote an HBV vaccination campaign among sex workers in Bogota, Colombia [54]. The peer leaders operate with a dual purpose: to mitigate risks and catalyse positive decision-making. The spectrum ranges from “embedded peer leaders” deeply rooted in the sex workers community to “congruent peer leaders” from the same socio-cultural background but not engaged in sex work. This diverse network enhances both understanding of the target population and the reach of the vaccination program, achieving a 37.7% adherence rate. The use of navigators or mediators – either peers or not – is explicitly reported in one-third of PH-related studies [24, 28, 30, 36, 54]. Bolutayo et al. illustrate the engagement of gatekeepers and community leaders in crafting a culturally targeted HBV group educational program for West African-born immigrants in New York City, US [28]. Gatekeepers and community leaders were actively involved in focus groups and tasked with refining existing educational materials to better suit the target community. To our knowledge, this study is the only one reporting navigators/mediators receiving financial incentives to participate in the process —a departure from the norm where such incentives are typically provided to study participants. Gatekeepers and community leaders identified crucial improvements during focus groups, emphasizing the need to address fear of disclosure, community stigma, and lack of HBV knowledge.

Resource allocation

Only two studies were found that thoroughly addressed the development, implementation, and budget impact of these interventions [33, 39]. Other economic analyses focused mainly on comparing different prevention, linkage to care, or treatment strategies, leading to their exclusion from this scoping review. Sievert et al. developed radio broadcasts on chronic HBV and assert their cost-effectiveness [39]. However, they did not provide data on economic evaluation, preventing its integration into our analysis. Conversely, Ho et al. explored PoC strategies, emphasizing their impact on linkage to care and cost reduction. Their analysis showed improved outcomes, with 76.5% lower costs per screened person (12.7 Euro vs. 54.0 Euro per person screened [33].

Care team wellbeing

We identified only one study suitable for inclusion within this domain. Brown et al.‘s study investigated the participation of healthcare providers in designing and implementing an App aimed at helping them overcome language and communication barriers when engaging with migrants and refugees, particularly in settings like HIV or HBV prevention programs [53]. The intervention also sought to highlight their attitudes toward the implementation of the app. The methods involved semi-structured interviews to record opinions and feedback from healthcare providers. The expectations were for a tool that could present information in simplified French or another chosen language while also fostering a positive attitude toward screening. Health professionals also expressed a desire for the technology to assist in collecting health data. From a value-based perspective, this study holds a dual significance: it aims to improve screening strategies among migrants and refugees while also facilitating the integration and collaboration of physicians and nurses.

Discussion

This scoping review found that no identified study explicitly cited or reported having directly incorporated a value-based healthcare framework in their planning, implementation, or reporting. Our findings confirm that interventions predominantly focus on strategies to overcome persistent systemic barriers in prevention, linkage to care, and access to appropriate care settings. A striking pattern emerged in the geographic distribution of evidence, with the vast majority of studies originating from high-income, English-speaking Western countries, including the United States (47%), Canada (6%), and the United Kingdom (6%). This highlights a potential global inequity not only in access to care but also in research generation for vulnerable populations, suggesting a significant gap in evidence from low- and middle-income settings where viral hepatitis burden, particularly HBV, is often highest, accounting for over 80% of global chronic HBV infections [58].

The application of the Quadruple Aim framework to the included studies revealed a critical disparity: while many interventions reported positive impacts on patient experience (56% of studies) and population health (50% of studies), there was a pronounced underrepresentation of explicit consideration for resource allocation (only 9% of studies) and, most notably, care-team well-being (only 3% of studies). This oversight suggests that even effective community-based models may not be fully optimizing value if long-term sustainability, equitable resource distribution, or provider support are not systematically integrated.

The consistent identification of fear (especially among undocumented migrants regarding legal repercussions and data sharing), coupled with language barriers (62% of studies), cultural insensitivity (50% of studies), and lack of health insurance, as principal barriers, reveals a complex interplay of social determinants of health that community interventions strive to mitigate. While many interventions successfully improved screening uptake, the persistent challenge of achieving consistent linkage to follow-up care (addressed by only 29% of studies) and treatment initiation (addressed by only 6% of studies) despite initial engagement, indicates a ‘leakage’ in the care cascade, with drop-off points often occurring post-diagnosis [55, 56]. Simply increasing awareness and screening is insufficient: robust, integrated pathways addressing ongoing socioeconomic/systemic hurdles are paramount. Successful strategies like mobile clinics, culturally tailored education, and peer-led outreach underscore the indispensable role of trust-building and accessible, decentralized service. Point-of-Care (PoC) testing emerges as a particularly promising strategy, as its use of rapid diagnostic tests can significantly improve screening accessibility [22, 30, 33, 46, 54]. For example, PoC testing demonstrated significantly higher linkage to care (e.g., 86% vs. 34% for traditional venipuncture) and reduced costs per person screened (e.g., €12.7 vs. €54.0) by minimizing follow-up losses [59]. Although this study refers to HCV programs, similar results can be expected from HBV interventions.

For projects like VH-COMSAVAC, translating these insights into specific EU contexts (Greece, Italy, Spain) utilizes the global evidence as a strength for transferable principles, while also highlighting the critical need for contextual adaptation. While community-based approaches are universally valuable, the specific socio-legal landscapes, migrant demographics, and healthcare system structures within each target country will necessitate tailored implementation strategies, particularly concerning policies for undocumented migrants and sustainable funding models. The low engagement with value domains like resource allocation and care-team well-being in the current literature also presents a unique opportunity for VH-COMSAVAC to pioneer more holistic evaluation models within its own framework, thereby contributing novel insights.

We acknowledge several limitations in our study. Due to limited prior research on the topic, a systematic literature review was not feasible. Instead, we conducted a scoping review to capture a broad range of studies and enrich our analysis. However, this approach introduced greater heterogeneity across the included studies and a degree of subjectivity during screening, data extraction, and analysis. While our primary focus was on community-based HBV interventions targeting migrants and refugees, we intentionally applied a broad search strategy to also include studies addressing HCV, HCC, or viral hepatitis more generally. This inclusivity helped avoid missing relevant interventions but also contributed to the overall variability. Additionally, we included some primary care interventions with a community component, as in several countries, community-based care is integrated into primary care. Excluding all studies labelled as ‘primary care’ might have resulted in the omission of relevant linkage-to-care strategies.

Another significant limitation involves categorizing studies based on four value-based domains. Despite efforts to provide clear definitions to reviewers, some degree of interpretation and discretion was unavoidable. This subjective understanding could result in inconsistent categorization, leading to the exclusion of articles if a reviewer found none of the four domains applicable. To address this, we implemented a double-blind screening and reassessment process to enhance consistency and reliability.

A methodological limitation relates to our inclusion criteria, specifically the decision to only include complete articles on implemented interventions for HBV prevention, linkage to care and treatment. This choice stemmed from our desire to understand how researchers consider values when planning interventions for HBV in marginalized populations. We therefore excluded articles like economic evaluations that analyse interventions after completion, providing a different perspective on resource allocation. Additionally, some interventions implemented by public health initiatives or national/international agencies exist only as abstracts or protocols, and these were also excluded. As a final observation, we acknowledge that the decision to include only studies published in English may introduce a selection bias, particularly considering the disproportionately higher representation of English-speaking countries in the included literature. Nonetheless, this limitation is widely recognized in scientific research—especially in reviews—and, provided it is transparently reported, we consider it acceptable and not invalidating to the overall findings of this study.

Although the database search concluded on July 30, 2023, the risk of missing evidence likely to alter our conclusions is low. Community-based HBV interventions evolve gradually and major practice-changing studies are uncommon. As this scoping review aimed to map the range of interventions rather than estimate effects, the descriptive synthesis remains valid. Targeted updates were also performed to capture key publications released after the initial search.

Conclusions

Viral hepatitis is a significant public health concern, particularly among migrants and refugees, where it represents a disproportionately high burden of infection compared to the general population [7]. Understanding the dynamics that hinder access to care and engagement, as well as identifying strategies to overcome these barriers, is crucial for guiding targeted efforts to reduce hepatitis-related morbidity and mortality among high-risk migrant groups and to align with WHO’s goal of eradication by 2030 [4]. Our review of the current literature highlights a notable gap in the development of interventions that comprehensively address and evaluate all perspectives, from patients to decision-makers. At present, no value-based framework exists for implementation in such initiatives. VH-COMSAVAC is addressing this gap by developing a value-based assessment tool to evaluate the four domains within the specific context of the project and explore opportunities for further improvement and implementation. Specifically, all prevention and linkage-to-care interventions conducted by VH-COMSAVAC members will be assessed using a value-based evaluation tool. This tool will measure impact across four domains—patient experience, population health, cost reduction, and care team well-being—using a questionnaire that will be developed, pilot-tested, and implemented with support from the insights gained through this review. Beyond its direct contribution to VH-COMSAVAC, this scoping review is also novel in its inclusion of multiple vulnerable populations beyond migrants and refugees and in its systematic mapping of intervention gaps and geographic disparities, thereby providing a broader evidence base to guide future community-based strategies and value-based evaluation efforts.
