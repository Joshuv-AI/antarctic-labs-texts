Dual-targeted phosphorus and COD removal by engineered amine-modified zeolite with pH self-regulation and competitive adsorption selectivity
Authors: Yunnen Chen, Qinglong Liu, Yuting Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractElevated total phosphorus (TP) and chemical oxygen demand (COD) drive dissolved oxygen depletion and black-odorous water formation in surface waters. To address this dual contamination challenge, this study engineered an amine-modified zeolite (AMZ) adsorbent. The functionalization process was meticulously refined using orthogonal experimental design, systematically optimizing critical parameters like amine type, concentration and temperature to maximize performance. The resulting AMZ demonstrated exceptional co-removal capabilities, achieving > 90% TP and > 80% COD removal efficiency across an exceptionally wide pH range (3–10) in both single-component and competitive binary systems. Crucially, AMZ uniquely stabilized treated effluent pH near neutrality (7.2 ± 0.2), a significant operational advantage. NaCl-enabled regeneration maintained > 90% TP and > 80% COD removal efficiency over multiple cycles. AMZ demonstrated robust performance in authentic wastewater matrices. The cost-performance advantage of AMZ becomes evident when benchmarked against commercial alternatives. SEM revealed a multilayered flake morphology enhancing active site accessibility. This study provides the first demonstration of site-specific selectivity in competitive adsorption of TP and COD on amine-functionalized zeolite: electrostatic sites (quaternary ammonium groups) favored HPO42− binding, while ion-exchange sites exhibited selectivity toward phthalate anions (dominant COD component). This work validates AMZ’s applicability in complex water remediation scenarios.Keywords: Amine-modified zeolite, Phosphorus removal, Chemical oxygen demand, Competitive adsorption, Ion exchange, Electrostatic attractionSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Eutrophication fueled by excessive total phosphorus (TP) and chemical oxygen demand (COD) threatens global water resources1–3. China’s Ministry of Ecology and Environment consistently reports non-compliant TP/COD levels in lakes, reservoirs, and urban waterways4, necessitating efficient co-remediation technologies.

Current TP and COD purification strategies encompass biological (e.g., microbial degradation, phytoremediation, aquatic faunal remediation)5,6, physical (e.g., sediment dredging, aeration, dilution)7,8, and chemical (e.g., oxidation, adsorption)9,10 approaches. Nevertheless, these methods face substantial limitations including protracted treatment durations, operational instability, high costs, sludge production, and chemical dependency. Consequently, developing efficient, low-cost, and eco-friendly TP/COD removal technologies is imperative. Adsorption emerges as a particularly promising approach due to its operational simplicity, scalability, and economic viability.

Natural zeolite (Z) offers notable advantages over conventional adsorbents (e.g., activated carbon), including low cost, abundant availability, high physicochemical stability, and scalability11,12. However, its primary application remains cation removal due to high cation-exchange capacity13–15. Although inorganic modification (i.e., the dual-adsorbent approach) has been effectively used for the separate removal of TP16–18 or COD19–21, research on organic modification remains relatively scarce. To date, only sporadic studies have reported the use of amine-modified zeolites for the removal of TP and heavy metals22, while systematic investigations on the simultaneous removal of both TP and COD from aqueous systems using a single adsorbent have not yet been documented.

In this study, orthogonal experiments were employed to optimize the amine functionalization modification scheme of zeolite. The performance of the modified adsorbent (AMZ) for the removal of TP and COD was systematically evaluated in both single-component and binary-component systems. The regeneration capability of AMZ was investigated through cyclic adsorption–desorption experiments, and its treatment efficiency was further validated using representative water samples with high TP/COD concentrations. Meanwhile, the mechanisms involved in the removal of TP and COD by AMZ were elucidated through comprehensive characterization techniques including scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), specific surface area (SBET), surface point of zero charge (pHpzc), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR).

Optimization of amino modification conditions of Z by orthogonal experiment

With the dosage of base material Z fixed, the solid-to-liquid ratio is determined by the dosages of EPI, DMF, DETA, and TEA. Consequently, five experimental factors were selected: EPI (A), DMF (B), DETA (C), TEA (D), and reaction temperature (E). Using the TP and COD removal rates achieved by the amino-modified material (AMZ) as performance metrics, a five-factor, five-level orthogonal experiment (L25(56)) was designed.

Modification process of Z

Amino-functionalization of zeolite was performed according to Cui et al. (2022). Pretreated Z, EPI, DMF, DETA, and TEA were sequentially added to a conical flask at a defined solid-to-liquid ratio. The mixture was reacted in a temperature-controlled shaker at a defined temperature and 150 rpm for 4 h. The solid product was then rinsed sequentially with absolute ethanol and deionized (DI) water until the chemical oxygen demand (COD) of the washing solution stabilized at a low value (< 10 mg/L), followed by drying in a forced-air oven at 70 °C. Then the dried solid was mixed with DI water and shaken under identical conditions (2 h, same conditions). Finally, the product was vacuum-filtered, rinsed repeatedly with DI water until neutral pH, and dried at 70 °C to yield amino-modified zeolite (AMZ).

The core contradiction in the development of environmental functional materials is considered the necessity to ensure environmental friendliness while high efficiency is pursued.

This study was initiated from material design and immobilization strategies, whereby the crosslinking agent epichlorohydrin (EPI) was introduced during the modification process. A dense and stable three-dimensional polymer network structure was formed, through which quaternary ammonium groups were covalently immobilized onto both the network and the zeolite surface, thereby significantly reducing their leaching and release.

Furthermore, a thorough washing procedure was implemented, wherein repeated stirring washes were performed using solvents such as absolute ethanol and deionized (DI) water until the chemical oxygen demand (COD) of the washing solution stabilized at a low value (< 10 mg/L).

Batch adsorption tests

Single-component purification by AMZ: Solutions containing either TP (1 mg/L) or COD (100 mg/L) were treated with amino-modified zeolite (AMZ) to evaluate purification performance. After pH value adjustment (range: 3–10), AMZ was added to the solution in a conical flask at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 0.6 g/L. The mixture was reacted in a thermostatic shaker at 25 °C and 150 rpm for a reaction time (0–120 min). The effect of adsorbent dosage on AMZ adsorption performance was also investigated. Post-reaction, samples were filtered through 20 μm medium-speed qualitative filter paper. Residual TP/COD concentrations in the filtrate were measured to determine the effects of pH and reaction time.

To further determine whether the ion exchange (displacement) of Cl− or the electrostatic attraction of quaternary ammonium group (-N+R3) plays a dominant role, real-time data experiment on the contents of TP and Cl− during the adsorption process by AMZ was performed.

TP and COD were determined according to Chinese National Standards as GB 11893-89 (Ammonium molybdate spectrophotometric method) and HJ/T 399–2007 (Rapid digestion spectrophotometric method), respectively. The Cl− concentration in the supernatant was determined using a chloride ion-selective electrode.

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean values ± standard deviation.

pH-dependent adsorption behavior of AMZ

Figure 3 illustrates phosphate speciation (H2PO4−/HPO42−/PO43−) distribution across the tested pH range.

Figure 3 reveals three distinct phosphate speciation regimes. (1) pH 0-4.6, dominance of H3PO4 and H2PO4−. As the pH value undergoes a step-by-step increase, the proportion of H3PO4 gradually decreases, while that of H2PO4− gradually increases. (2) pH 4.6–9.6, co-dominance of of H2PO4− and HPO42−. With the increase of the pH value, the proportion of H2PO4− gradually decreases, whereas that of HPO42− gradually increases. (3) pH > 9.6, transition to HPO42− gradually decreases, while the proportion of PO43− gradually increases.

Figure 4 pH-dependent adsorption behavior of AMZ. (TP0 = 1 mg/L, COD0 = 100 mg/L, AMZ = 0.6 g/L, T = 25 °C, t = 20 min)

Delineates pH-governed adsorption behavior of AMZ in aqueous solutions.

Figure 4 reveals pH-insensitive adsorption characteristics of AMZ: TP removal > 98% across pH 3–10, COD removal > 80% with slight increase at higher pH. This may be because the zwitterionic AMZ surface (positively-charged quaternary ammonium groups and negatively-charged Cl−). Under acidic conditions, the concentration of Cl− in the solution increases to some extent, which will inhibit the exchange of COD with Cl− on the surface of AMZ and also compete for electrostatic attraction. However, since TP has two electrons, the influence of Cl− on it is weaker than that on COD. Based on the fact that the pH value of actual water sample is 8.19, and combined with Fig. 3, it can be known that the phosphate in the water mainly exists in the form of HPO42−. Therefore, in the subsequent experiments, the TP is all expressed in the form of HPO42−.

Figure 5 shows the surface point of zero charge (pHpzc) of AMZ.

Zero charge point diagram of AMZ.

As can be seen from Fig. 5, the pHpzc of AMZ was determined to be approximately 2.5. When the solution pH (3–10) was higher than 2.5, the surface of AMZ was positively charged, which was favorable for the adsorption of TP and COD. This also explains why high removal efficiencies of both TP and COD by AMZ can be achieved across a broad pH range, as shown in Fig. 4.

Figure 6 pH-shift signatures of interfacial proton transfer during AMZ adsorption. (TP0 = 1 mg/L, COD0 = 100 mg/L, AMZ = 0.6 g/L, T = 25 °C, t = 20 min)

Characterizes proton transfer dynamics during adsorption via pH-shift profiles.

Figure 6 demonstrates AMZ’s intrinsic pH buffering capability: post-adsorption pH consistently converges to 6.9–7.3 (± 0.2) regardless of initial pH, indicating proton exchange equilibria at surface functional groups.

Figure 3 also demonstrates that phosphate speciation varies significantly with pH. As a triprotic weak acid, phosphoric acid undergoes stepwise dissociation in aqueous solution, establishing multiple equilibria. Consequently, phosphate species act as a buffer system. This buffering capacity explains why the solution pH remains near 7.2 following HPO42− adsorption by AMZ.

Dosage of adsorbent on AMZ adsorption performance

As shown in Fig. 9, with the increase of AMZ dosage, the removal efficiencies of both HPO42− and COD were observed to initially increase and then level off. When the AMZ dosage was increased from 0.05 g/L to 0.4 g/L, the removal efficiencies of HPO42− and COD by AMZ rapidly rose from 59.61% to 2.59% to 99.01% and 79.21%, respectively. As the AMZ dosage was further increased from 0.4 to 0.6 g/L, the removal efficiency of COD continued to climb, from 79.21% to 82.69%. Subsequently, when the AMZ dosage was increased from 0.6 g/L to 1.2 g/L, the removal efficiencies of both HPO42− and COD remained almost unchanged.

Desorption and regeneration of AMZ

The desorption efficacy of NaCl, NaOH, and HCl on saturated AMZ and the regenerated adsorbent’s re-adsorption performance were investigated (Fig. 10).

Adsorption performance of AMZ in the first and second cycles. (TP0 = 1 mg/L, COD0 = 100 mg/L, AMZ = 0.6 g/L, pH 8, T = 25 °C, t = 20 min)

Figure 10 indicates that AMZ regenerated using HCl and NaCl maintains high removal efficiency for both HPO42− and COD in subsequent cycles. This performance retention is likely due to Cl− ions effectively exchanging with adsorbed HPO42− and COD during desorption, thereby regenerating the active sites on the AMZ surface. Consequently, the removal rates achieved in the second adsorption cycle closely match those of the first cycle. In contrast, NaOH regeneration resulted in significantly reduced adsorption capacity. The poor performance suggests that OH− ions are ineffective in displacing the adsorbed HPO42− and COD, leaving active sites blocked. Based on comprehensive evaluation of desorption efficacy, environmental impact, and cost-effectiveness, NaCl was selected as the optimal regenerant.

Mechanism of HPO42− and COD removal by AMZ

Z and AMZ were characterized using EDS and FTIR to examine compositional differences before and after modification, as well as after adsorption, thereby revealing the mechanism of AMZ for HPO42− and COD removal.

EDS analysis

Figure 12 presents EDS spectra of Z, AMZ, and AMZ after adsorption of HPO42− or COD.

EDS spectra of Z and AMZ before and after adsorption.

EDS analysis in Fig. 12 reveals significant elemental changes after modification. Surface atomic percentages for N and Cl increased from 1.71% to 0.02% in pristine Z to 12.01% and 8.15% in AMZ, confirming that the cross-linking agent EPI has successfully loaded quaternary ammonium groups and chlorine onto the Z. Concurrently, carbon content surged from 23.89% to 59.71%, while oxygen, aluminum, and silicon decreased sharply (O: 49.75% → 18.93%; Al: 3.49% → 0.14%; Si: 20.85% → 0.54%). These shifts demonstrate encapsulation of Z by organic layers formed during reaction with EPI, DMF, DETA, and TEA, which dominate the AMZ surface.

Post-adsorption EDS analysis reveals key mechanistic insights (Fig. 11). For AMZ-HPO42−, Cl⁻ decreased from 8.15% to 2.47% and P increased from 0.49% to 2.58%. For AMZ-COD, Cl⁻ dropped sharply from 8.15% to 0.05%. This confirms anion exchange where surface Cl− is replaced by HPO42− or phthalate anions (C8H5O4−). Crucially, N% increased in both AMZ-HPO42− (15.81% vs. 12.01%) and AMZ-COD (14.28% vs. 12.01%), demonstrating retention of quaternary ammonium groups via electrostatic attraction to anions – consistent with their non-participation in ion exchange.

FTIR analysis

FTIR analysis was performed to identify functional group and bonding differences between Z and AMZ, and between AMZ before and after adsorption of HPO42− or COD. Results are presented in Fig. 13.

FTIR spectra of Z, AMZ and AMZ before and after adsorption.

FTIR spectra in Fig. 13 reveal that all four materials (Z, AMZ, AMZ-HPO42−, AMZ-COD) show characteristic bands at ~ 3430 cm− 1 (H-bonded O-H stretch) and 1080 cm− 1 (Si-O-Si asymmetric stretch)32,33. Amino-functionalization significantly enhanced the 3625 cm− 1 band (isolated O-H/N-H) while attenuating the 1080 cm− 1 Si-O-Si vibration.

New C–Cl stretching vibrations appear at 777 cm− 1 in AMZ spectra, persisting after adsorption. Notably, AMZ-COD shows that weakened peak intensity and blue-shifted position, indicating ion exchange between Cl− and phthalate anions (C8H5O4−). Conversely, AMZ-HPO42− maintains near-identical peak intensity and position versus AMZ, confirming phosphate adsorption occurs primarily through electrostatic attraction with minor ion exchange contribution.

New peaks at 1460 cm− 1 in AMZ spectra (pre/post-adsorption) confirm quaternary ammonium groups via alkyl asymmetric bending34–36. Adsorption-induced intensity changes reveal distinct anion interactions that HPO42− adsorption enhances 1460 cm⁻¹ intensity and phthalate (C8H5O4−) adsorption reduces it, indicating variable electrostatic binding geometries with quaternary ammonium groups37,38. Material-specific adsorption signatures confirm successful uptake: AMZ-HPO42− shows unique 528 cm− 1 band (HPO42− vibration), AMZ-COD exhibits carboxyl stretches at 1550 cm− 1 (asymmetric) and 1382 cm− 1 (symmetric)39.

Effects of amino groups on selective adsorption

Diethylenetriamine (DETA) is a polyamine that provides a large number of primary amine (-NH2) and secondary amine (-NH-) groups. These amino groups are immobilized onto the zeolite surface through cross-linking with epichlorohydrin (EPI), forming positively charged active sites. The amino groups on AMZ become protonated and carry positive charges (-NH3+, -NH2+-). In aqueous solution, phosphate ions and many organic compounds that contribute to COD typically carry negative charges. The strong attraction between these opposite charges is recognized as the primary driving force for adsorption. Meanwhile, the amino groups serve as strong sites for hydrogen bonding formation, through which a hydrogen bonding network can be established with oxygen atoms or hydroxyl groups (-OH) in phosphate ions or organic pollutant molecules, further enhancing the adsorption effect.

Effects of the autonomous pH regulation capability on selective adsorption

“pH self-regulation” is referred to as the inherent ability of AMZ to stabilize the pH of its immediate micro-environment within a range that is favorable for adsorption to occur. The mechanism is based on the proton buffering capacity of the amino groups cross-linked onto the zeolite. Under acidic conditions, protons (H+) are captured by the amino groups, resulting in their protonation (-NH2 + H+ → -NH3+), thereby preventing a sharp decrease in pH. Under alkaline conditions, protons are released from the protonated amino groups (-NH3+ + OH− → -NH2 + H2O), which mitigates the rise in pH.

The speciation and charge of phosphate ions are highly dependent on pH. Within the range of approximately pH 5–10, the dissolved phosphate species are predominantly dominated by negatively charged forms such as H2PO4− and HPO42− (Fig. 3). The strongest electrostatic interaction with protonated amino groups (-NH3+) is achieved under these conditions. The pH self-regulating capability of AMZ is thus utilized to maintain the local pH within this optimal range, thereby ensuring that the amino groups remain persistently protonated while the phosphate ions retain their negative charge. Consequently, highly efficient and stable adsorption is realized.

Many organic acids that contribute to COD are protonated and become neutral under lower pH conditions, whereby electrostatic interaction with the adsorbent is reduced. By maintaining the pH at a weakly alkaline level, these organic substances are ensured to remain in an ionized state (negatively charged), thus enabling them to be effectively captured by the amino groups through electrostatic attraction.

Exploration of the purification mechanism of AMZ

Collectively, experimental and characterization data demonstrate that AMZ removes HPO42− and COD through dual mechanisms: electrostatic attraction and ion exchange. The detailed pathways are illustrated in Fig. 14.

Mechanism diagram of AMZ removes HPO42− and COD.

Electrostatic attraction is evidenced by: (1) EDS-detected increased N% on AMZ surfaces post-modification, with persistent N% post-adsorption despite P increase; (2) FTIR 1460 cm− 1 peaks confirming quaternary ammonium groups33–35; (3) Adsorbate-specific FTIR signatures (HPO42− at 528 cm− 1; COD carboxyl stretches at 1550/1382 cm− 1) confirming contaminant binding. These observations collectively demonstrate electrostatic binding between AMZ’s quaternary ammonium groups and HPO42−/C8H5O4− anions, as mechanistically depicted in Eqs. (5)–(6).

Ion exchange is confirmed by: (1) EDS: Significant Cl− increase on AMZ surfaces post-synthesis (vs. Z), followed by substantial Cl− decrease post-adsorption with corresponding P rise; (2) FTIR: Persistent C–Cl stretch at 777 cm− 1 confirming surface Cl− loading; (3) Adsorbate-specific FTIR signatures post-adsorption (HPO42− at 528 cm− 1; COD carboxyl vibrations at 1550/1382 cm− 1). These complementary data demonstrate stoichiometric exchange between Cl− and HPO42−/phthalate anions (C8H5O4−), as mechanistically detailed in Eqs. (7)–(8).

Under co-contaminant conditions (HPO42− + COD), competitive adsorption occurs at AMZ’s active sites: Electrostatic sites (quaternary ammonium groups) exhibit preferential affinity for HPO42− over COD constituents. Ion-exchange sites (Cl− carriers) demonstrate superior selectivity toward phthalate anions (C8H5O4−, primary COD component) versus HPO42−.

Conclusions

This study addresses critical water quality challenges in China—specifically, elevated TP/COD levels in lakes/reservoirs and black-odorous water bodies in small watersheds. Through orthogonal design optimization, we developed amino-modified zeolite (AMZ) exhibiting exceptional purification performance: achieves Class III Surface Water Standards (GB 3838 − 2002) for binary systems (1 mg/L TP + 100 mg/L COD), effective across pH 3–10 with only 0.6 g/L dosage and 20 min contact time. AMZ reduces material costs by > 95% versus activated carbon/resins while maintaining competitive removal efficiencies (> 95% TP, > 89% COD) and regenerability. This positions it as a technically and economically viable solution for decentralized water treatment.

Key mechanistic insights reveal that significant N/Cl enrichment on AMZ surfaces (vs. pristine Z), and quaternary ammonium groups enable dual adsorption pathways. Electrostatic sites preferentially adsorb HPO42− over COD constituents, and ion-exchange sites selectively remove phthalate anions (primary COD component). These findings position AMZ as a rapid, pH-robust solution for co-contaminant remediation in impaired water bodies.

Future work will investigate AMZ’s dynamic adsorption performance in complex aquatic systems exhibiting TP/COD co-pollution, focusing on: (1) Long-term efficacy in natural water matrices; (2) Regeneration potential under field conditions; (3) Scaling considerations for practical implementation.
