The regulation of COX-2, ABCA1, and ABCG1 by the lncRNA PACERR links the inflammatory response and cholesterol homeostasis
Authors: Elizabeth J. Hennessy, Takae Tanosaki, Ronan Lordan, Soon Yew Tang, Vladimir V. Shuvaev, Soumita Ghosh, Ujjalkumar S. Das, Robin Joshi, Hu Meng, John N. Snouwaert, Hanna Winter, Lars Maegdefessel, Beverly H. Koller, Garret A. FitzGerald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYCyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) plays a central role in inflammation, but its inhibition is associated with increased cardiovascular risk. Here, we investigate the COX-2 antisense long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) PTGS2 antisense complex-mediated expression regulator RNA (PACERR) and its potential role in pathways linking inflammation and cholesterol homeostasis. PACERR expression is induced by high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and is associated with changes in COX-2, ABCA1, and ABCG1 expression in vascular (smooth muscle and endothelial) and immune cells (macrophages). PACERR interacts with regulatory proteins p300, hnRNPL, and IκBζ, and its perturbation alters their association with target loci. Silencing PACERR enhances HDL-induced cholesterol efflux and modifies COX-2 expression and prostaglandin production. PACERR is also associated with regulatory elements near PLA2G4A (cPLA2), and its perturbation influences cPLA2 expression and arachidonic acid release. In inflammatory settings, PACERR silencing reduces COX-2 expression, prostaglandin biosynthesis, and systemic inflammatory responses. Together, these findings support a role for PACERR in coordinating inflammatory and cholesterol-responsive pathways.

INTRODUCTION

NSAIDs are among the most widely used drugs in adults, relieving pain by inhibiting cyclooxygenase (COX) activity and reducing prostaglandin (PG) production. However, they carry significant risks, including gastrointestinal lesions and cardiovascular complications such as myocardial infarction, hypertension, stroke, and heart failure.1–4 These risks arise partly because NSAIDs suppress not only pro-inflammatory PGs but also cardioprotective ones, particularly prostacyclin (PGI2), which inhibits platelet aggregation, macrophage activation, and vascular smooth muscle cell (vSMC) contraction and growth.5,6 Whether regulated COX-2 expression influences NSAID (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug) efficacy or toxicity remains unknown.

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by lipid-rich plaque accumulation in arterial walls. Studies in mice show that COX-2 deletion in endothelial cells (ECs) or vSMCs accelerates atherosclerosis, while myeloid-specific deletion slows it, highlighting the cell-type-specific roles of COX-2 in disease progression.7–9 High-density lipoprotein (HDL) has long been inversely associated with cardiovascular risk in epidemiological studies, though Mendelian genetics have not confirmed a protective causal role.10–12 One proposed mechanism is that HDL upregulates COX-2 and PGI2 production via CREB activation in vitro,13–17 though the significance of this pathway in vivo is unknown.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are promising therapeutic targets due to their cell- and tissue-specific expression. Unlike protein-coding genes, lncRNAs evolve rapidly with little sequence conservation across species. They regulate gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms including DNA methylation, histone modification, and chromatin remodeling.18–25 Although thousands of lncRNAs have been identified, the function of only a small number has been characterized.26–31

To explore the relationship between COX-2, HDL signaling, and vascular inflammation, we investigated lncRNAs that mediate these responses. We find PACERR (PTGS2 antisense complex-mediated expression regulator RNA), previously characterized in a study of macrophage inflammation,32 as a dual regulator of COX-2 expression and function. PACERR is induced by both lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and HDL in macrophages, vSMCs, and ECs, and is linked to PG regulation. Depending on the stimulus and cell type, PACERR modulates PG output to either promote or restrain inflammation and cholesterol homeostasis.

Although PACERR shows functional orthology between mice and humans, the transcripts share minimal sequence homology, only a single NF-κB binding motif. To study human PACERR in vivo, we generated a knockin mouse model replacing the mouse Cox-2/Pacerr locus with its human counterpart. When PACERR is silenced using an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO), COX-2 expression and PG production increase in response to HDL but decrease in response to LPS. Through interactions with RNA-binding proteins, including p300 and hnRNPL, PACERR regulates COX-2 in cis and the cholesterol transporters ABCA1 and ABCG1 in trans, balancing inflammation and intracellular cholesterol homeostasis. These findings support a model in which human PACERR differentially modulates COX-2 in response to distinct stimuli, influencing both PG production and cholesterol metabolism with potential implications for the therapeutic index of NSAIDs.

RESULTS

PACERR is expressed in carotid plaques and is induced by HDL-dependent CREB transcription

Previous studies have implicated COX-2 inhibition in the progression of atherosclerosis, so we first examined if PACERR is differentially expressed in early-stage (“control”) versus more advanced human carotid plaques.33,34 When compared to control plaques, PACERR expression is reduced in more advanced plaques, suggesting a role in the initiation of early inflammatory events such as EC dysfunction that diminishes as the disease progresses (Figure 1A). Single-cell RNA sequencing from the same human carotid atherosclerotic samples revealed that PACERR was primarily expressed in ECs and inflammatory macrophages (Figure 1B). Because of these observations, we treated human THP-1 monocytes differentiated into macrophages with various ligands involved in cholesterol metabolism and inflammation that are known to modulate COX-2 expression.18–23 Both PACERR and COX-2 are concomitantly induced by the HDL precursor apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), HDL, and CD36-dependent oxidized LDL (oxLDL), but SR-BI-dependent acetylated low-density lipoprotein (acLDL) did not induce either PACERR or COX-2. The COX-2 downstream enzyme microsomal PG E synthase-1 (mPGES-1) was also induced in response to ApoA1 and HDL, signifying a role for it in cholesterol metabolism (Figure S1A). Consistent with prior studies, LPS also induced PACERR expression32 (Figure 1C). Previous studies have shown a differential impact of HDL subfractions on the expression of COX-2 and production of PGI2, potentially affording restraint on thrombogenesis, atherogenesis, and hypertension.24,25 HDL3 induced higher PACERR expression, COX-2 protein levels, and PG production than HDL2 (Figures 1D and 1E; Figure S1B).7,9 HDL3 was associated with increased PON1 and reduced F2-isoprostanes compared to HDL2, indicating greater antioxidant capacity, and we hypothesize that differences in particle oxidative state will contribute to the divergent signaling responses; PON1 depletion or oxidized lipid supplementation would be needed to test it directly (Figures S1C and S1D).

ABCA1 and ABCG1 mediate cholesterol efflux to ApoA1 and HDL, and ABCA1 also regulates inflammatory signaling.35–40 To test whether PACERR induction by HDL depends on these transporters, macrophages were treated with small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting ABCA1, ABCG1, or both (Figures S2A and S2B). Combined knockdown reduced HDL-induced COX-2 expression more than either alone, while PACERR induction was similarly reduced by each condition. Rather than observing reciprocal upregulation predicted by a compensatory model involving the two transporters, ABCA1 knockdown led to a concomitant decrease in ABCG1 expression, suggesting a co-regulatory mechanism (Figure 1F; Figures S2C and S2D).

To elucidate further the regulation of PACERR by HDL, we examined the impact of MAP kinase signaling by treating macrophages with inhibitors of p38 and ERK, known regulators of COX-2 by various stimuli.16,24 Pre-treatment with either inhibitor followed by treatment with HDL inhibited the induction of PACERR expression (Figures S3A and S3B). Because the transcription start site of PACERR is 364 base pairs from the COX-2 transcription start site, we anticipated co-regulation of the two genes and that PACERR would be dependent on the activity of COX-2. Using the COX-2 selective NSAID celecoxib to inhibit PG synthesis, we found that PACERR is induced to the same degree by either HDL or LPS (Figure S3C). COX-2 expression is attenuated when cells were treated with celecoxib before LPS, signifying a described feedback loop where the suppression of PGs affects COX-2 expression but interestingly, not PACERR.41,42

As the COX-2 promoter region includes multiple NF-κB and CREB sites, we anticipated a shared transcription factor paradigm between the two genes.43 LPS induces COX-2 expression via binding of the NF-κB subunits p65 and p50 to transcription factor sites in its promoter region.44 The transcription of COX-2 is followed by production of pro-inflammatory PGs together with cytokines.45–48 Inhibition of NF-κB with the NF-κB inhibitor BAY-117082 reduced COX-2 expression but did not affect PACERR levels after LPS or HDL stimulation, indicating that PACERR is regulated independently of NF-κB49 (Figure 1G).

The lipoproteins ApoA1 and HDL can induce COX-2 expression through CREB signaling independent of NF-κB.24,46,50 We used siRNAs targeting both the CREB and the CREM transcription factor binding sites due to redundancy and compensatory effects51,52 to silence the transcription factors in THP-1 cells (Figures S4A and S4B). In contrast to NF-κB, silencing CREB reduced both COX-2 and PACERR expression in response to HDL or LPS, demonstrating CREB-dependent regulation (Figure 1H; Figures S4C and S4D). We did not observe the reciprocal upregulation predicted by a compensatory model when CREB and CREM were silenced; instead, CREB silencing decreased CREM expression in untreated cells, while CREM silencing had no effect on CREB. Stable CREB knockdown (Figures S4E–S4G) confirmed this effect. These findings suggest that HDL induces PACERR through CREB signaling, which may suppress NF-κB activity and limit inflammation. Previous studies have shown that CREB promotes an anti-inflammatory immune response through the inhibition of NF-κB while also inducing IL-10.14 Thus, we hypothesize that CREB-dependent PACERR induction by HDL inhibits NF-κB activation, limiting inflammation.

PACERR regulates COX-2 expression and HDL-induced PGs

PACERR is expressed in ECs and inflammatory macrophages within early carotid plaques and is enriched in arterial tissues (GTEx) (Figures S5A and S5B), prompting us to examine its regulation by HDL across vascular cell types. HDL induced both PACERR and COX-2 expression in primary macrophages, vSMCs (from three independent donors), and ECs (HUVECs) subjected to laminar flow (10 dyn/cm2) and exposed to HDL (Figures 2A–2C; Figures S6A–S6C). To ensure HUVECs responded to shear stress, KLF2 and ICAM were measured and accordingly increased and decreased in response to laminar flow. Proper endothelial response to shear stress was confirmed by increased KLF2 and decreased ICAM expression. (FiguresS7A and S7B). Silencing PACERR using an ASO enhanced HDL-induced COX-2 expression as well as the release of PGI2 (6-keto(k)PGF1α—its stable hydrolysis product) and PGE2 by macrophages and SMCs. In ECs, HDL increased PGE2, but PACERR knockdown further increased its production, consistent with higher COX-2 expression. Additional PGs showed similar patterns, and macrophages also exhibited increased PGD2 following PACERR silencing Figures S8A–S8C. Importantly, COX-1 and mPGES-1 levels were unchanged (Figures S9A and S9B), indicating that PACERR specifically regulates COX-2-dependent pathways.

PACERR impacts cholesterol metabolism gene expression and cholesterol efflux

The canonical role for HDL in the body is an acceptor molecule in the removal of excess cholesterol.39 To assess the role for PACERR in this process, THP-1 macrophages were transfected with the PACERR ASO, and we observed an increase in cholesterol efflux to HDL (Figure 3A) along with augmented expression of ABCA1 and ABCG1 (Figure 3B; Figure S6A). PACERR was also induced by the LXR agonist T0901317, suggesting it participates in regulating the LXR-responsive cholesterol transport genes.

Because the human and mouse COX-2/PACERR loci differ substantially, a humanized mouse model was generated by replacing the mouse locus with the human genomic region (Figures S10A and S10B). Elevated expression of hCOX-2 and hPACERR was seen in the lungs of humanized (hu/hu) mice compared to wild type (wt) (Figure S10C). Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from the hu/hu mice and treated with LPS to confirm that the humanized COX-2 and PACERR genes were responsive to LPS (Figure S10D). (Mouse and human COX-2 share ~89% sequence homology, an explanation for the increase in expression in wt). Consistent with in vitro findings, macrophages from humanized mice transfected with PACERR ASO showed increased HDL-mediated cholesterol efflux and higher ABCA1 and ABCG1 expression following PACERR knockdown (Figures 3C and 3D). When these macrophages were injected into mice, PACERR silencing increased plasma and liver cholesterol levels, indicating enhanced cholesterol transfer from cells to circulating HDL (Figure 3E). Lungs isolated from hu/hu mice treated with the ASO showed a decrease in PACERR expression. The PACERR ASO also depressed expression of human, but not mouse, COX-2 (Figures S11A and S11B).

p300 and hnRNPL bind to PACERR RNA to mediate effects on COX-2, ABCA1, and ABCG1

Many lncRNAs regulate the expression of genes and pathways through their interactions with proteins, such as transcription factors, ribonucleoproteins and chromatin modifying molecules. To identify RNA binding protein (RBP) partners of PACERR, we focused on nuclear interactions due to predominant nuclear expression of PACERR (Figure S12A) and performed an unbiased mass spectrometry proteomic screen (Figure S12B; Table S2).53 Using an antisense PACERR sequence as a negative control for binding, PACERR interacts with the histone acetyltransferase p300, and heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein L (hnRNPL), proteins involved in inflammation and cholesterol metabolism (Figure 4A).

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) confirmed PACERR interacts with chromatin, as indicated by enrichment with histone H3, and directly interacts with p300 and hnRNPL, and their associations decreased following HDL treatment (Figure 4B). Control RNAs, lncRNA NEAT1 and GAPDH, showed no enrichment, supporting specificity (Figure S12C). Similar interactions were observed in humanized mouse macrophages (Figure S12D), indicating a conserved mechanism of PACERR-mediated regulation.

Because PACERR and COX-2 are both CREB-dependent and p300 enhances CREB-mediated transcription, we examined whether PACERR regulates COX-2 through p300.24,54 Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) showed that HDL increases p300 binding and H3K27 acetylation at the COX-2 promoter, and these effects were further enhanced when PACERR was silenced (Figure 4C; Figure S13A). In contrast, no p300 binding sites were found at the ABCA1 or ABCG1 loci. Instead, hnRNPL was found to bind intronic regions of both genes, as seen previously using CLIP (Figures S14A, S14B, S15A, and S15B).55 ChIP confirmed hnRNPL association with these loci, and its binding increased when PACERR was silenced (Figure 4D). These results suggest that PACERR limits transcriptional activity of cholesterol transporters by modulating hnRNPL availability.

The hnRNPL motif in PACERR RNA is required for ABCA1 and ABCG1 expression and cholesterol efflux

A strong hnRNPL binding site motif was found within the 825 bp PACERR RNA sequence (Figures S16A and S16B). When this was deleted, the interaction between PACERR RNA and hnRNPL was abrogated both at baseline and following HDL treatment of THP-1 macrophages (Figure 5A). To assess its function, THP-1 macrophages were engineered to overexpress either full-length PACERR or a mutant lacking the hnRNPL binding site (ΔhnRNPL). Overexpression of intact PACERR reduced hnRNPL binding to the ABCA1 and ABCG1 loci, whereas ΔhnRNPL restored hnRNPL binding, indicating that PACERR normally sequesters hnRNPL and prevents its association with these genes (Figure 5B). Functionally, PACERR overexpression decreased COX-2, ABCA1, and ABCG1 expression. In contrast, deletion of the hnRNPL binding site restored ABCA1 and ABCG1 levels, consistent with increased hnRNPL binding (Figures 5C and 5D). Transcriptomic analysis showed that PACERR overexpressing cells are enriched in inflammatory pathways, whereas ΔhnRNPL cells show greater enrichment in lipid metabolism pathways (Figures S17A and S17B). ΔhnRNPL cells also exhibit an overall increase in transcript number, suggesting altered splicing due to hnRNPL release from PACERR. Both PACERR and ΔhnRNPL cells show increased expression of inflammatory genes (IL-23, IL-10, TNFAIP2, and NFKBIZ), but only ΔhnRNPL cells display elevated ABCA1, indicating a specific effect of hnRNPL binding (Figure 5E).

PACERR interacts with cPLA2 to mediate LXR-dependent expression of ABCA1 and ABCG1

HDL increases PACERR transcription and releases p300 and hnRNPL, suggesting that newly produced PACERR may regulate additional pathway components. We, therefore, examined its interaction with cPLA2 (cytosolic phospholipase A2—PLA2G4A), an enzyme located near the PACERR/COX-2 locus that releases arachidonic acid (AA). HDL increased cPLA2 expression and AA release in macrophages and ECs,56,57 and both effects were reduced by PACERR knockdown, indicating that PACERR promotes cPLA2 activity (Figures 6A and 6B). This effect was specific, as expression of a related phospholipase GX sPLA2 (PLA2G10) implicated in the regulation of ABCA1 and ABCG1 was unchanged58,59 (Figure S18A).

Chromatin interaction analysis (GeneHancer60) showed enhancer regions linking PACERR and cPLA2. Chromosome conformation capture (3C) confirmed their interaction at one enhancer site, suggesting PACERR can influence cPLA2 transcription (Figures 6C and 6D; Figures S19A and S20A–S20C). While PACERR and cPLA2 do not interact at baseline, HDL induces their association, indicating an additional regulatory layer, possibly affecting cPLA2 localization and AA release (Figure 6E).

Because AA can inhibit LXR-driven transcription of ABCA1 and ABCG1,61,62 we examined LXR binding to their promoters. HDL increased LXR binding to both promoters, and this effect was further enhanced by PACERR knockdown (Figure 6F), consistent with increased transporter expression (Figure 3). As these observations demonstrate associated observations rather than a causal relationship, pharmacological inhibition or knockdown of cPLA2 would be required to establish whether it is necessary for the observed AA release. Together, these findings suggest that PACERR and cPLA2 provide an additional level of control over LXR-mediated regulation of cholesterol efflux genes.

PACERR regulates the COX-2-mediated inflammatory response

Single-cell RNA sequencing from human carotid plaques (Figure 1B) showed PACERR is enriched in inflammatory macrophages, so we examined its role under inflammatory conditions. LPS-induced PACERR enhances COX-2 transcription by preventing the repressive NF-κB p50/p50 homodimer from occupying NF-κB sites in the promoter, thereby enabling recruitment of the activating p65/p50 heterodimer and the histone acetyltransferase p300, which collectively promote a more transcriptionally permissive chromatin state.32,63–65 While HDL-induced COX-2 expression increases when PACERR is silenced, LPS-induced COX-2 expression is reduced (Figures S21A and S21B), indicating context-dependent regulation. Similarly, under oscillatory shear stress in ECs, PACERR knockdown decreased COX-2 expression and production of 6-kPGF1α and PGE2, consistent with an inflammatory role (Figures 7A and 7B). KLF2 expression decreased, and ICAM increased, consistent with a stress response to OSS (oscillatory shear stress) (Figures S22A and S22B).

In vivo, humanized mice treated with a PACERR-targeting ASO localized to the endothelium via platelet EC adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1) showed reduced PACERR and COX-2 expression in lungs, spleens, and aorta (Figures 7C and 7D; FiguresS23A and S23B), along with decreased systemic biosynthesis of PGE2 and PGI2 as reflected by their urinary metabolites (PGE-M and PGI-M) (Figure 7E) and systemic inflammatory markers, IL-6 and MCP-1 (Figure 7F) following LPS stimulation. Consistent with previous studies,66–68 we found there is a significant decrease in PGE-M and IL-6 in response to LPS in females compared to males. COX-2 was not induced in response to LNP-ASO alone (Figure S23C), but it was evident that PACERR ASO decreased LPS-induced COX-2 protein when directly compared to untreated tissues (Figure S23D).

Mechanistically, PACERR interacts with the NF-κB regulator IκBζ (Figure S12B), a repressive co-factor of NFκB-mediated target gene expression that regulates the binding of p50.69–71 LPS increases this interaction (Figure S24A), promoting inflammatory gene activation. Silencing PACERR enhances IκBζ binding at the COX-2 promoter, suppressing transcription. Together, these findings suggest that PACERR promotes COX-2 expression during inflammation, and its inhibition reduces both inflammatory signaling and cholesterol accumulation.

DISCUSSION

This study identifies a mechanism by which the lncRNA PACERR coordinates PG synthesis with cholesterol efflux in vascular cells, potentially influencing atherogenesis. PACERR is preferentially expressed in inflammatory macrophages and ECs in human carotid plaques, particularly in early, developing lesions. Unlike most lncRNAs, PACERR is expressed in both mice and humans from the same genomic locus. Although the COX-2 gene is highly conserved (~89%) across primates and rodents, the COX-2 promoter and PACERR have diverged substantially, retaining only a single NF-κB binding site. Notably, LPS- and HDL-induced PACERR expression is driven by CREB, not NF-κB. To study human PACERR in vivo, we generated a humanized mouse model replacing a 35 kb mouse locus with the syntenic 59 kb human region.

Under basal conditions, PACERR binds p300 (a histone acetyltransferase) and hnRNPL (a splicing factor). HDL stimulation triggers CREB-dependent PACERR upregulation, releasing p300 to acetylate H3K27 at the COX-2 promoter and increase COX-2 transcription. Simultaneously, hnRNPL is released from PACERR and relocates to ABCA1 and ABCG1 pre-mRNAs, altering their splicing and boosting cholesterol efflux. Silencing PACERR enhances both pathways, increasing COX-2 expression and PG production, together with cholesterol transporter expression, confirming PACERR’s role as a checkpoint on these two processes. Deletion of the hnRNPL binding motif within PACERR recapitulates this effect, demonstrating interaction specificity.

An additional regulatory layer involves cPLA2/PLA2G4A, a PACERR neighboring gene. HDL-induced PACERR promotes cPLA2 expression and AA release, which inhibits LXR/RXR heterodimerization and suppresses ABCA1/ABCG1 transcription. Silencing PACERR reduces cPLA2 activity, relieving this inhibition and further enabling LXR-driven cholesterol efflux. Finally, PACERR basally sequesters IκBζ, a negative regulator of NF-κB. Upon PACERR silencing, free IκBζ binds the NF-κB site in the COX-2 promoter, repressing LPS-driven COX-2 expression and attenuating systemic inflammation in vivo, a mechanism analogous to glucocorticoid-mediated COX-2 suppression.69–72 Together, these findings show how a single lncRNA integrates inflammatory and metabolic signals to regulate cardiovascular homeostasis.

Limitations of the study

A major limitation of this study is its reliance on cell-based systems, primarily macrophages, which may not fully capture the complexity of in vivo biology. We examined regulation of COX-2, ABCA1, and ABCG1 under defined acute stimuli (HDL and LPS), and it remains unclear whether similar mechanisms operate in other immune cell types or under chronic inflammatory conditions. In vivo validation is limited to a humanized mouse model, which does not fully recapitulate human physiology. Additionally, ASO-mediated knockdown may have off-target effects and does not necessarily mirror complete genetic loss of PACERR.

Interpretation is further complicated by the nature of lncRNA biology. It remains difficult to distinguish effects mediated by the PACERR transcript itself from those arising from transcriptional activity at the locus or from local DNA regulatory elements. Consequently, some observed phenotypes may reflect locus-dependent rather than RNA-specific functions. Although our data support interactions between PACERR and multiple RNA-binding proteins and show effects on chromatin occupancy and gene expression, these findings do not definitively establish direct causality or exclude indirect mechanisms.

Finally, the translational relevance of these findings remains uncertain. While PACERR perturbation influences inflammatory signaling and cholesterol efflux, its effects on chronic disease outcomes, such as atherosclerosis progression, and the safety or efficacy of therapeutic targeting in humans have not been addressed in this study.

Human studies

See Paloschi et al. and Pauli et al. for specifics on processing and single cell RNA sequencing of human carotid endarterectomy samples.33,34,76 In brief, human carotid plaques were selected based on morphology, including remnant media and macroscopically assessed and dissected into advanced atherosclerotic (plaque) and adjacent early lesion (control) tissue from the same patient (matched paired diseased-control samples). Bulk RNA-Sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis compared advanced plaques and early lesion control tissues in an unpaired (n = 232, n = 65) design. Data was normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values normalization method (TMM) and transformed with Voom, resulting in log2-counts per million with associated precision weights. TMM uses the raw data to estimate appropriate scaling factors (i.e., library size) that can be used in downstream statistical analysis procedures, thus accounting for the sampling properties of RNA-seq data. The aim of normalization was to remove systematic technical effects that occur in the data to ensure that technical bias has minimal impact on the results. Normalized data provided the input for statistical hypothesis testing, in which genes that are significantly different between sample groups were identified. Statistical comparisons were performed using empirical Bayes moderated t-tests. Significance values (p-values) were adjusted for multiple testing by controlling the false discovery rate. Sex was not considered in the study design. After selecting available samples of patients, sex was added as a clinical variable. No sex-specific differences were observed.

In vivo animal studies

Ten-to twelve-week-old male and female mice were kept under 12:12 light:dark conditions with environmental enrichment (nestlet, shepherd shacks) unless otherwise specified. All mice were housed with ad libitum access to standard chow diet and water. Studies were performed in accordance with the protocol approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Generation of mouse ESCs and mouse lines lacking the entire 35 kb mouse Cox-2/Pacerr locus, extending from base pair 150,073,658 to base pair 150,108,528 on chromosome 1 (GRCm38/mm10) and replaced with 59 kb of the human COX-2/PACERR locus from base pair 186,671,772 to base pair 186,716,901 on chromosome 1 (GRCh38/hg38) was performed as previously described.77–79 The human gene is present as a single copy, the orientation and relative distance of COX-2 to the endogenous murine upstream and downstream genes is preserved, and, with the exception of a mutant loxP and frt site, no vector or marker sequences remain in the humanized locus. The ESCs were used to generate co-isogenic 129S6-COX-2/PACERR mice. Mice were backcrossed to C57/BL6J mice purchased from Jackson Laboratories 10 generations. Using standard nomenclature, the human gene is capitalized COX-2/PACERR whereas Cox-2/Pacerr refers to the mouse gene. Humanized mice are referred to as hu/hu throughout the study. Genotyping primers are found in Table S1.

For the in vivo reverse cholesterol transport (RCT) assay, bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) were prepared from hu/hu mice as previously described.80,81 Bone marrow was isolated, and cells were plated overnight in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 15% L-929 conditioned media. Non-adherent cells were removed and cultured for an additional 6 days to allow for macrophage differentiation. Following transfection (described below), cells were washed twice and incubated with 37.5 μg/mL acLDL (Kalen Biomedical, LLC 770201-6) and 5 μCi/mL 3H-cholesterol (PerkinElmer, NET139250UC) for 24 h a described previously.37,82 Cells were resuspended in ice-cold DMEM, and 2–3 × 106 cells were injected subcutaneously into individually housed hu/hu mice. Prior to injection, an aliquot of cells was counted using liquid scintillation counting to measure baseline radioactivity. Blood was obtained by retro-orbital puncture at 24 h following BMDM injection and by cardiac puncture after 48 h at sacrifice. An aliquot of plasma was used for liquid scintillation counting immediately at each time point. Feces were collected for 48 h after injection and homogenized in 50% NaOH overnight, after which an aliquot was used for liquid scintillation counting. At sacrifice, liver samples were collected and incubated with hexane/isopropanol (3:2) for 48 h and then dried overnight. Lipids were resolubilized in liquid scintillation fluid, and radioactivity was counted. RCT to plasma, feces, and liver was calculated as a percentage of total radioactivity injected at baseline.

Ultrapure Lipopolysaccharide from Escherichia coli O111:B4 (LPS, L2630, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to induce sepsis in 12-week-old humanized mice. LPS was dissolved in PBS at a concentration of 0.25 mg/mL. Mice were injected with LPS at a dose of 1 mg/kg body weight intraperitoneally. Urine was collected at 6 h, and urinary prostaglandins were measured as described below. Sex differences were observed in the production of urinary PGs and this is described in the corresponding results section.

Cell culture

HEK293T (CRL-3216) and THP-1 (TIB-202) cell lines were obtained from American Type Tissue Collection, authenticated with standard American Type Tissue Collection methods (morphology check under a microscope and growth curve analysis) and tested monthly for mycoplasma contamination. HEK293T were maintained in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, 11965084) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cytiva, SH30071.03HI) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122) and never used beyond passage 20. THP-1 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, 11875093) with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122), 10 mM HEPES (Gibco, 15630080), 1 mM Sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11360070), and 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, 21985023) according to ATTC. Cells were not used beyond passage ten and maintained at 1 × 106 cells/mL. THP-1 cells were differentiated into macrophages in the presence of 10 nM phorbol-12-myristate acetate (PMA, Sigma, P1585–1MG) for 48–72 h. Vascular aortic smooth muscle cells (vSMCs) were obtained from Lonza from 3 different male donors (cat # CC-2571). vSMCs were grown in SmGM-2 Smooth Muscle Cell Growth Medium-2 BulletKit (cat # CC-3182) and not used for experiments beyond passage five. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were obtained from Lonza (cat # CC-2519) and grown in EGMTM EC Growth Medium BulletKit (cat # CC-3124) and not used beyond passage three.

Culture of primary cells

Primary peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from blood collected in a BD vacutainer CPT (362753). Whole blood was centrifuged through a Ficoll-Paque PLUS (Cytiva, 17-1440-03) density gradient centrifugation, and PBMCs were collected from the buffy coat. Macrophages were expanded and cultivated in the presence of 50 ng/mL recombinant human M-CSF (R&D, 216-MC-025) to generate monocyte derived macrophages (MDMs). Cells were grown in RPMI-1640, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 10% FBS, 25 mM HEPES, 1X nonessential amino acids (Gibco, 11130051), 1X GlutaMax (Gibco, 35050061), 1 mM Sodium-pyruvate, 10 μg ciprofloxacin (Acros, AC456880050).

METHOD DETAILS

Cellular response to lipoproteins and inflammatory ligands

ApoA1 was (molecular cloning laboratories, APO-100) and used at 50 μg/mL. High density lipoproteins (HDL, HDL2 and HDL3) were purchased from Lee Biosolutions (cat # 361-10-0.1) or academy biomedical solutions (80P-HD-101, 80P-HD2-101, 80P-HD3-101) and used at 50 μg/mL. Concentrations of HDL typically used in vitro (25–100 μg/mL) correspond to physiological levels seen in people (40–60 mg/dL).83 Average plasma HDL levels of 50 mg/dL translates into an HDL protein concentration of about 125 mg/dL as HDL particles consist of roughly 50% protein, 30% phospholipid, and 20% cholesterol.84,85 The concentration of HDL that tissues encounter in the interstitial space is substantially lower than circulating levels. The HDL protein concentration that a typical cell will encounter is around 125 μg/mL. AcLDL (Kalen Biomedical, LLC, 770201-6) was used at 37.5 μg/mL.18 Oxidized LDL (Lee Biosolutions, 360-31-1) was used at 50 μg/mL19 LPS (L2630, Sigma-Aldrich) was used at 100 ng/mL.22,23 Cells were incubated for 24 h, unless otherwise indicated. For inhibitor experiments, cells were pretreated with 10 μM celecoxib (Carbosyth FC11 30), 5 μM Bay 11-7082 (Sigma B5556), 10 μM PD98059 (Cayman 10006726), 1 μM SB203580 (APExBIO A8254).

RNA isolation, cell fractionation and real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen 15596026) and Direct-zol RNA MicroPrep columns (Zymo Research R2051). Tissues were lysed with stainless steel beads (Qiagen 69989) using the Qiagen tissue lyser II in the presence of Trizol. For cell fractionation experiments RNA was isolated from separate cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions using the RNA Subcellular Isolation Kit (Active Motif 25501). Upon isolation, RNA was reverse transcribed using High-capacity cDNA Synthesis kit (Applied Biosystems 4374967) and quantitative PCR analysis was conducted using Applied Biosystems FAST SYBR green (4385612) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and quantified on the ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Fold change in mRNA expression was calculated using the comparative cycle method (2−ΔΔCt) normalized to the housekeeping gene HPRT. A list of primers used in this study can be found in supplement.

Gain- and loss-of-function studies

Stable knockdown of CREB was done by designing shRNAs targeting a region common to all CREB gene variants or a non-targeting control shRNA and cloned into the pLKO vector (Addgene, 8453) and transfected into HEK293T cells with packaging vectors psPAX2 (Addgene, 12260) and pMD2.G (Addgene, 12259). After 48 h, the culture medium was isolated and used to transduce THP-1 cells. Cells stably expressing the shRNAs were selected with 5 μg/mL puromycin (Santa Cruz sc-108071). To create PACERR-overexpressing cell lines, PACERR gene block from IDT was cloned into the pMSCV-PIG vector (Addgene, 21654) and transfected into HEK293T cells with packaging vectors pCMV-VSV-G (Addgene, 8454) and pCMV-Gag-Pol (Cell Biolabs, RV-111). After 48 h, the culture medium was isolated and used to transduce THP-1 cells which were then selected with puromycin (5 μg/mL).

For knockdown of PACERR in PMA-differentiated THP-1 cells or hBMDMs, 50 nM locked nucleic acid GapmeRs (IDT) targeting PACERR or Negative Control GapmerRs were transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Life Technologies 13778075) as described.30 Knockdown of ABCA1, ABCG1, CREB1 and CREM in THP-1 cells was acquired by transfecting 50 nM Dharmacon ON-TARGET plus siRNAs directed against ABCA1 (J-004128-06-0002), ABCG1 (J-008615-05-0002), CREB1 (L2-003619-01-0005) or CREM (L2-009528-01-0005) or non-targeting control (D-001810-01-05) using Lipofectamine RNAiMax. After the 24 h of incubation, cells were treated with HDL or LPS and then lysed and RNA was extracted and used for qPCR analysis.

To mutate the predicted binding site for hnRNPL (ΔhnRNPL) in the PACERR sequence, the hnRNPL motif was deleted with the QuikChange XL kit (Stratagene 200521) on the pMSCV-PIG, by using the primers indicated in. All constructs were confirmed by sequencing.

RNA-sequencing

THP-1 monocyte cell lines (n = 3) were stably transfected with pMSCV-PIG overexpression vector containing PACERR sequence or PACERR ΔhnRNPL sequence or empty vector. Cells were differentiated with 10 nM PMA for 72 h and treated with 50 μg/mL HDL for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and Direct-zol RNA MicroPrep columns (Zymo Research). For each RNA sequencing dataset, total RNA was sequenced by Azenta Life Sciences. Sequence reads were trimmed to remove possible adapter sequences and nucleotides with poor quality using Trimmomatic v.0.36. The trimmed reads were mapped to the Homo sapiens GRCh38 reference genome available on ENSEMBL using the STAR aligner v.2.5.2b. Unique gene hit counts were calculated by using FeatureCounts from the Subread package v.1.5.2. The hit counts were summarized and reported using the gene_id feature in the annotation file. Only unique reads that fell within exon regions were counted. Using DESeq2, a comparison of gene expression between samples was performed. The Wald test was used to generate p-values and log2 fold changes. Genes with an adjusted p-value <0.05 and absolute log2 fold change >1 were called as differentially expressed genes for each comparison. A gene ontology analysis was performed on the statistically significant set of genes by implementing the software GeneSCF v.1.1-p2. The goa_human GO list was used to cluster the set of genes based on their biological processes and determine their statistical significance. Metascape was used for further pathway analysis and heatmaps displayed.86 Data has been deposited to GEO: GSE328984.

Lipid nanoparticle preparation and characterization

LNPs used in this study were similar in composition to those described previously,75 an ionizable cationic lipid, proprietary to Acuitas/phosphatidylcholine/cholesterol/PEG-lipid.

(50:10:38.5:1.5 mol/mol) SM-102 was used. The nitrogen to phosphate ratio (N/P ratio) was 5 and they were encapsulated at an RNA to total lipid ratio of ~0.05 (wt/wt). Formulation was prepared using microfluidic mixing of lipids (in ethanol) and ASO (in sodium acetate), and buffer exchanged against DPBS buffer supplemented with 8% w/v of sucrose. They had a diameter of ~80 nm as measured by dynamic light scattering using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, UK) instrument. mRNA-LNP formulations were stored at −80°C at a concentration of mRNA of ~1 μg/μL. Targeting antibody for PECAM-1 (BD Biosciences MEC-13.3) was conjugated to LNP particles via SATA–maleimide conjugation chemistry using the previously established method.75

Application of shear stress

HUVECs were seeded on gelatin-coated μ-slides 0.6 Luer (Ibidi 50-305-756) for 3 h and transfected with 10 nm LNP-ASOs. Flowing medium was applied by using the Ibidi pump system to generate unidirectional laminar flow (10 dyn/cm2), oscillating flow (0.5 + 4dyn/cm2), or static control for 24 h in an incubator. The system was maintained at 37°C and ventilated with 95% humidified air containing 5% CO2.87–91

Preparation of cells for chromatin immunoprecipitation

ChIP-immunoprecipitation was performed using SimpleChIP Plus Sonication Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology #56383) following manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were cross-linked with 37% formaldehyde (Sigma 252549) for 10 min at room temperature. Isolated chromatin was fragmented using the Diagenode Bioruptor (ON 30 s, OFF 30 s) for 30 min to generate 200–500 bp chromatin fragments. In each experiment, chromatin was first processed by agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm DNA shearing to 200–500 bp fragments, and the DNA concentration was measured by NanoDrop 2000. Equal quantities of sheared chromatin (10 μg per immunoprecipitation) were diluted 1:5 in sonication buffer to the final volume of 1 mL, and immunoprecipitated overnight with 1 μg antibody targeting human hnRNPL (novus NB120-6106), P300 (Abcam ab275378), Histone H3K27Ac (Active Motif, 39685), IκBζ (Cell Signaling 9244S), cPLA2 (Cell Signaling 2832S) and LXR-α and LXR-β (Active motif 61175 and 61177). Negative site primers targeted regions where there was no predicted binding of protein of interest. An equal amount of chromatin from each sample was immunoprecipitated with normal rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling #2729P) at 4°C overnight. Chromatin complexes were captured using 20 μL Dynabeads protein G (Invitrogen 10003D) at 4°C for 1 h.

Western blots

Protein was extracted in RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific 89901) with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche 11836153001 and 4906845001) and subsequently normalized with a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific 23225). Samples (30 μg per well) were electrophoresed on NuPAGE 4–12% BT gels (Invitrogen NP0322BOX) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes at 40 V for 2 h. Membranes were incubated overnight with the specified antibodies directed against COX-2 (Cayman 160106), ABCA1 (Novus Biologicals, NB400-105), ABCG1 (Novus NB400-132), CREB1 (Abcam ab32515), KLF-2 (Cell Signaling 51221T), ICAM-1 (Cell Signaling 67836T), β-actin (Sigma A5441), Tubulin (Novus NBP2-54687). Proteins were visualized by using appropriate secondary antibodies from Jackson Laboratories and scanned with an Odyssey Imaging System (Li-Cor Biosciences). Quantification of western blots was performed in Fiji.92

Cholesterol efflux assay

For cholesterol efflux assays, THP-1 cells stably expressing PACERR or transfected with PACERR ASOs were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells, and THP-1 cells were treated with PMA as described above. Cells were labeled with 0.5 μCi/mL of 3H-cholesterol (PerkinElmer) and 37.5 μg/mL acetylated LDL (Kalen Biomedical, LLC 770201-6) for 24 h, washed twice with PBS and then incubated with 2 mg/mL fatty-acid-free BSA (Sigma-Aldrich A8806) in medium for 24 h. To induce efflux, we added 50 μg/mL human HDL (Lee Biosolutions 361-10-0.1) in fatty-acid-free medium, and supernatants were collected after 24 h. HDL-dependent efflux was expressed as a percentage of total cell 3H-cholesterol content.

Mass spectrometry screen for RNA binding proteins

NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents (Thermo Scientific 78833) was used to isolate nuclear proteins from THP-1 macrophages. We used PACERR cloned into pGEM-7Z+ (Promega P2251) as the template to in vitro-transcribe (IVT) using T7 RNA polymerase, and biotin-16-UTP was incorporated at approximately every 20th to 25th nucleotide of the transcript. Pierce Magnetic RNA-Protein Pull-Down Kit (Thermo Scientific 20164) was used to identify proteins bound to PACERR IVT RNA. Biotinylated RNA was incubated with nuclear cellular extracts from THP-1 macrophages unstimulated or treated with 50 μg/mL HDL. Streptavidin magnetic beads were used to pull-down biotinylated PACERR and proteins bound to PACERR were identified using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) as previously described.93 We used an antisense PACERR RNA sequence and GFP sequence as controls for specificity of the in vitro transcribed product. GFP is approximately the same size as the human PACERR transcript, 715 bp versus 825 bp. RNA binding proteins identified using CatRapid, oRNAment and RBPmap were used to focus the list.94–96 Mass spectrometry results are found in s–S2F. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD082116.74

Measurement of HDL paraoxonase activity and F2-Isoprostane levels

Paraoxonase or PON1 activity was measured by hydrolysis of paraoxon. 10 μL of the HDL sub-fractions were added to 95 μL buffer solution (100 mM Tris-HCl, 2 mM CaCl2, pH 8.0) and then 95 μL of 5.5 mM paraoxon-ethyl (Sigma-Aldrich, 36186) (190 μl +10 μl = 200 μl reaction). Absorbance was recorded at 412 nm and 25°C over a period of 12 min (at 20-s intervals). Formation of p-nitrophenol/4-nitrophenol was measured. Blanks contained substrate without enzyme and were used to correct for the spontaneous hydrolysis of the substrate. Enzyme activity was calculated with a molar extinction coefficient of 18,290 M−1 cm−1. One unit of paraoxonase activity produced 1 nmol of p-nitrophenol per minute. 1 nmol of paraoxon hydrolyzed per mL per minute.

Then 10 μL 5% D, L-dithiothreitol solution was added to samples along with 100 μL of 15% KOH. The mixture was incubated at 40°C for 1 h. The solution was then acidified to pH 3 with 6 N HCl. Mass spectrometric analysis of cell culture media prostanoids

Prostanoids in cell culture media were measured, as described by Meng et al.97 Values were normalized to total RNA concentration for each sample to adjust for variations in cell numbers and plotted as fold change relative to control ASO transfected samples.

Mass spectrometric analysis of urinary prostaglandin metabolites

Urinary prostanoid metabolites were measured by liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry as described.98 Such measurements provide a noninvasive, time-integrated measurement of systemic prostanoid biosynthesis.99

RNA immunoprecipitation

Human histone H3 (Cell signaling, 9715), hnRNPL, p300, IκBζ and cPLA2 (antibodies used as above in ChIP) were immunoprecipitated from PMA-differentiated THP-1 macrophages. All immunoprecipitations were done using the MagnaRIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (EMD Millipore 17-700) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Briefly, an antibody targeting protein of interest, or an isotype matched control IgG antibody were bound to magnetic beads and incubated with lysed cells at 4°C for 24 h. Beads were isolated and cleaved from the bound proteins by proteinase K, and coprecipitated RNA was purified. qPCR analysis of total RNA was performed to detect enrichment of PACERR and control genes in the protein-of-interest precipitated fraction was determined as percentage of 1% input control.

Chromosome conformation capture

Chromatin interactions were determined, as described by Joniec et al.100 and Hagège et al.101 Briefly, chromatin was crosslinked and nuclei isolated. Chromatin was digested DpnII restriction enzyme (NEB R0543T) and chromatin was then ligated (NEB M0202S). Proteins were digested and cross-links reversed. Chromatin was purified using phenol-chloroform and presence or absence was determined using qPCR and combinations of primers targeting different regions of PACERR and PLA2G4A regions. Undigested and digested DNA that had not been ligated were used as controls as well as primers that were not located in regions containing DpnII sites.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

All statistical tests and data representations were performed using GraphPad Prism 11 (GraphPad Software Prism v11 (93)). Data are presented as biological replicates and as means with error bars representing the standard error of the mean (SEM). Data distribution and variances were considered for deciding on appropriate statistical tests. Performed tests are indicated in figure legends. Unpaired t tests or Mann-Whitney tests were performed when comparing two groups. two-way ANOVA with correction for multiple testing was performed when comparing two or more groups with two independent variables. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 with the levels of significance indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. The statistical significance is not shown in all graphs or between all groups to ensure clarity of the figures. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments. Sample size was calculated by power analysis or based on availability.

All animals compared were the stated age (10–12 weeks old). Post hoc analysis was performed by pairwise t-tests. two-Way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc multiple comparisons test was used to compare more than two groups containing one factor. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 11. All significant tests were further validated by the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U and Wilcoxon tests to make sure that they were not biased by parametric assumption. A significance threshold of 0.05 was used for all tests after correcting for multiple tests. Sample sizes were based on power analysis of the test measurement and the desire to detect a minimal 10% difference in the variables assessed with α = 0.05 and the power (1 – β) = 0.8.
