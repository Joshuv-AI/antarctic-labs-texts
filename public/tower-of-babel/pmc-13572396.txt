Isotropic-carrier-transport perovskite single crystal enables high-photoresponsivity solar-blind photodetectors for selective flame monitoring
Authors: Mengyao Zhang, Fei Wang, Zhuangzhuang Ma, Dongwen Yang, Jingli Ma, Xinzhen Ji, Meng Wang, Lejin Li, Huifang Jiang, Conghui Dun, Ruoting Yang, Yanbing Han, Mochen Jia, Xu Chen, Yongtao Tian, Jibin Zhang, Ying Li, Xinjian Li, Zhifeng Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLow-dimensional halide perovskites are promising candidates for solar-blind ultraviolet photodetectors. However, simultaneously achieving high photoresponsivity and low dark current remains challenging, due to the restricted transport and collection of photogenerated carriers caused by strong electronic localization. Here, guided by theoretical calculations of electronic structures, we report a solar-blind family of CsCdCl3 perovskite single crystals, which features a 3D structural framework, delivering high carrier mobility of 7.54 cm2 V−1 s−1 and a long carrier diffusion length of 6.96 μm. These achievements stem from the 3D electronic structure, enabling balanced carrier effective mass isosurfaces and enhanced band dispersion. CsCdCl3-based solar-blind photodetectors yield a photoresponsivity of 35.4 A W−1 and dark current of 1.0 × 10−12 A. Moreover, the devices demonstrate a narrowband spectral response of only 20 nm with a spectral-rejection ratio of 1.96 × 103, which enables spectral-selectivity flame detection, distinguishing various types of flames and showcasing potential in offshore oil flame monitoring.Subject terms: Photonic devices, Optoelectronic devices and components

Introduction

Solar-blind photodetectors operating in the wavelength range of 200–280 nm hold significant potential for applications in flame detection, missile plume tracking, and early warning systems1–5. Conventional solar-blind photodetectors based on wide-bandgap semiconductors like Ga2O3, MgZnO, AlxGa1−xN, and diamond have demonstrated high sensitivity and fast response speed, but they still face challenges in achieving large-scale manufacture, cost control, and high signal-to-noise ratio readout6,7. Recently, metal halide perovskites have emerged as promising candidates for solar-blind detection due to their excellent absorption coefficient, direct band gaps, low cost, and compatibility with low-temperature solution processing8–10. However, the representative lead-based perovskites typically exhibit absorption edges that fall short of the solar-blind region, and their poor environmental stability remains a critical concern. In contrast, low-dimensional perovskite derivatives, such as newly emerging Cu+-, Ag+-, and Cd2+-based metal halides, possess intrinsically wide band gaps exceeding 3.8 eV, making them suitable for solar-blind detection11,12. In addition, the low-dimensional structures (such as 0D isolated octahedra, 1D chains, and 2D layered frameworks) can enhance environmental stability by reducing the exposure of active sites to oxygen and moisture. Nevertheless, such structures also increase the electronic localization, restrict the band dispersion, and induce severe anisotropy in carrier effective mass, thereby inhibiting the transport and extraction of photogenerated carriers13,14. These limitations deteriorate the device performance, making it difficult to meet the requirements of practical applications. Despite extensive efforts to improve these electronic properties through various strategies, such as ion doping, surface/interface modification, and defect engineering, the state-of-the-art low-dimensional perovskites still exhibit inferior carrier mobilities and carrier diffusion lengths that are two to three orders of magnitude lower than those of conventional solar-blind semiconductors13,14.

According to the k⃗⋅p⃗ perturbation theory, carrier effective mass plays a decisive role in governing optoelectronic performance, which is inversely related to the curvature of the band edges15. 3D electronic structures, particularly those comprising corner-sharing octahedral networks that form continuous lattices, provide synergistic benefits for band dispersion optimization and density of states distribution16,17. Such structures also facilitate isotropic carrier transport pathways, resulting in extended carrier lifetimes and superior charge transport properties18. Nonetheless, 3D wide-bandgap perovskites that simultaneously possess strong solar-blind responsivity, high carrier mobility, and excellent stability remain scarce, limiting the realization of high-performance solar-blind photodetectors.

Herein, we report a solar-blind family of CsCdCl3 perovskite single crystals (SCs) designed via 3D structure engineering, which shows a unique 3D structural framework constructed from the mixed face-shared and corner-shared octahedra. Theoretical calculations reveal that the 3D electronic structure of CsCdCl3 with continuous lattices enables a more balanced carrier effective mass isosurface and enhanced band dispersion compared to their low-dimensional counterparts (layered Cs2CdCl4 (2D), chain-like CsCdBr3 (1D), and isolated Rb4CdCl6 (0D)). As a result, 3D CsCdCl3 SCs yield a high carrier mobility of 7.54 cm2 V−1 s−1 and a long carrier diffusion length of 6.96 μm, surpassing most solar-blind detection semiconductors. These exceptional properties enable the fabrication of high-performance solar-blind photodetectors showing simultaneously: (1) a high photoresponsivity of 35.4 A W−1, (2) low dark current 1.0 × 10−12 A and large specific detectivity of 2.13 × 1013 Jones, and (3) excellent solar-blind imaging capability. Leveraging the narrowband spectral response of only 20 nm with high spectral-rejection-ratio of 1.96 × 103, a flame detection and early warning system constructed with these photodetectors achieves spectral selectivity monitoring, effectively distinguishing various flame types, and further expands their applications in offshore oil flame detection.

Results

Electronic structure

High carrier mobility and long carrier diffusion length are prerequisites for efficient collection of photogenerated carriers in solar-blind photodetectors, as the former enables rapid carrier drift while the latter reflects suppressed recombination19. Our proposed CsCdCl3, with a 3D electronic structure, achieves both, placing it within the high-performance regime beyond most perovskites and oxide semiconductors (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). To rationalize the superior optoelectronic properties of CsCdCl3, we compared its structure and electronic characteristics with those of representative Cd-based perovskites with different structural dimensions (Figs. 1b–m). CsCdCl3 forms a 3D bonding framework, in which two octahedrons share a triangular face to form [Cd2Cl9]5− (face-shared C3v symmetry), and [Cd2Cl9]5− is connected with six [CdCl6]4− octahedrons to share vertices (corner-shared D3d symmetry), which enables continuous orbital overlap and isotropic carrier pathways (Fig. 1b). In contrast, the reduced dimensionality in layered Cs2CdCl4 (2D), chain-like CsCdBr3 (1D), and isolated Rb4CdCl6 (0D) limits electronic connectivity and suppresses long-range transport of photogenerated carriers (Figs. 1c–e).

First-principles calculations reveal that both the conduction and valence bands of CsCdCl3 exhibit high band dispersion along multiple high-symmetry directions, with balanced carrier effective mass isosurfaces, indicating isotropic carrier transport (Fig. 1f). However, low-dimensional Cd-based perovskites show significantly reduced band-edge dispersion and pronounced carrier localization due to their structural confinement. Specifically, 2D Cs2CdCl4 is characterized by pronounced anisotropy arising from weakened interlayer coupling (Fig. 1g); 1D CsCdBr3 shows limited band dispersion along its chain direction (Fig. 1h); and 0D Rb4CdCl6 exhibits strongly localized electronic states in all three dimensions (Fig. 1i). Band structure analysis indicates that CsCdCl3, CsCdBr3, and Rb4CdCl6 possess direct bandgaps, whereas Cs2CdCl4 exhibits an indirect bandgap (Fig. 1j–m). Projected density of states (PDOS) of four materials shows that the conduction bands are dominated by halogen (Cl or Br) p and Cd s orbitals, while the valence bands primarily originate from halogen (Cl or Br) p and Cd d states, with minimal contribution from Cs or Rb cations (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Preparation of CsCdCl3 SCs

The CsCdCl3 SCs were synthesized through a straightforward hydrothermal method20. We systematically investigated the effects of precursor concentration and cooling rate on the nucleation density and morphology of CsCdCl3 SCs (Supplementary Figs. 2, 3). By adjusting the reaction temperature, we further controlled the growth of CsCdCl3 SCs with different exposed crystal planes (Supplementary Fig. 4). As the reaction temperature decreases from 180 to 120 °C, the crystal morphology evolves from highly symmetric, diamond-shaped polyhedra to well-defined, thin platelets (Fig. 2a, b). To clarify this phenomenon, we established the predicted morphology of CsCdCl3 SCs using the Bravais–Friedel–Donnay–Harker (BFDH) morphology21. The predicted morphology consists of the (002), (101), (011), (−111), (1-10), and (010) planes, with the (002) plane occupying the largest portion of the total surface area (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 2). Based on the hexagonal crystal geometry, we further calculated the interfacial angles between these facets. The BFDH-predicted morphology is consistent with the preferred orientations observed in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of CsCdCl3 SCs (Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating that the exposed planes are closely related to the temperature-dependent growth behavior.

According to Wulff’s theorem, a crystal’s external surface adheres to the principle of minimizing surface free energy (ES)22. Based on the surface-energy-dependent crystal growth model23, the ES of (002), (100), and (101) planes is calculated to be 0.0023, 0.0067, and 0.0078 eV Å−2, respectively. At the low temperature of 120 °C, the exponential dependence of the growth rate ratio (exp[(ES2 − ES1)/(kBT)], where ES1 and ES2 are the surface energies of normal and along the plane of the crystal, respectively) amplifies the kinetic contrast between planes. As a result, the lateral high-energy (100) and (101) planes grow rapidly, whereas the low-energy (002) plane remains preferentially exposed, giving rise to sheet-like SCs with a pronounced (002)-oriented growth feature (Supplementary Figs. 7,8). When the temperature increases to 180 °C, the increase in kBT reduces the magnitude of (ES2−ES1)/(kBT), thereby weakening the exponential amplification of plane-dependent energy differences, and the (002) plane grows quickly, resulting in bulk-like SCs. XRD patterns of CsCdCl3 SCs grown at different reaction temperatures reveal that the (002)-oriented diffraction peak is more intense at 120 °C, indicating an enhanced crystal growth along the (002) direction and a pronounced lateral expansion of SCs (Supplementary Fig. 9).

We selected the CsCdCl3 SCs with thin-layered platelets grown at 120 °C as a representative sample, which exhibit sharp, intense diffraction peaks in the XRD patterns exclusively at (004), (006), (008), and (0012) planes (Fig. 2c), consistent with the calculated pole figure for the (101) plane (WinWulff software), thus confirming the CsCdCl3 SC’s sixfold symmetry (Supplementary Fig. 10). The inset of Fig. 2c presents the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of (006) diffraction peak from the high-resolution X-ray rocking curve, showing a value as low as 0.012°, which is much lower than the previously reported CH3NH3PbBr3 (0.113°) and Cs3Bi2I9 (0.058°) SCs24,25, indicating an excellent crystalline quality of CsCdCl3 SCs26. Single-crystal XRD patterns show that the diffraction spots of CsCdCl3 SCs are intense and well-aligned, further validating the single-crystalline nature (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Table 3). Moreover, the fabrication of CsCdCl3 SCs possesses excellent reproducibility, batch-scale growth scalability, and low cost (Supplementary Fig. 12), offering significant advantages for large-scale manufacturability compared with conventional oxide-based semiconductors that mainly rely on complex vacuum-based fabrication techniques.

Morphological characteristics

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of CsCdCl3 SCs (Supplementary Fig. 13a) shows clear edges, a smooth and homogeneous surface without obvious grain boundaries, consistent with intrinsic single crystallinity. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping verifies a uniform distribution of Cs, Cd, and Cl, and the quantitative EDS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 13b) yields an atomic ratio of Cs:Cd:Cl = 1.34:1.32:4.01, matching the stoichiometry of CsCdCl3. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements reveal a smooth surface with a root-mean-square roughness of 0.67 nm (Supplementary Fig. 14). Raman spectroscopy mapping shows that the CsCdCl3 SCs exhibit uniform intensity distribution throughout the selected areas, with identical uniformity observed at 77, 134, and 252 cm−1 (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 15). In addition, spatially resolved Raman measurements collected from multiple positions along the CsCdCl3 SCs show highly consistent characteristic vibrational modes without peak shift or broadening, confirming good lattice vibrational uniformity and structural consistency (Supplementary Fig. 16). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) image in Fig. 2e exhibits clear lattice fringes, and two sets of lattice planes with d-spacings of 0.37 and 0.63 nm were observed, which can be attributed to the (110) and (100) planes of CsCdCl3, respectively. The selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Fig. 2f) exhibits sharp and regularly spaced spots, verifying the single-crystalline structure of the resulting sample. Notably, the CsCdCl3 SCs also demonstrate excellent environmental stability, confirmed by the fact that the structural integrity and absorption spectra can be retained after one year of storage under ambient conditions (20 − 25 °C, 40 − 60% humidity; Supplementary Fig. 17).

Trap density and carrier transport properties

To investigate the electronic properties of CsCdCl3 SCs, we fabricated electron-type devices to calculate the trap density (ntraps) and carrier mobility (μe). According to the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) method27,28, the ntrap of CsCdCl3 SCs was calculated as 8.89 × 108 cm−3 (Fig. 2g) through the equation of Nt = 2εε0VTFL/eL2 (ε = 15.6 for CsCdCl3 SCs, Supplementary Fig. 18), which is approximately two orders of magnitude lower than that of reported perovskite SCs29,30. The μe of CsCdCl3 SCs is extracted by the Mott-Gurney law of JD = 9ε0εrμV2/8L3, where JD and V are the current density and applied voltage, respectively31. It is deduced that the μe of CsCdCl3 SCs reaches 7.54 cm2 V−1 s−1, which is significantly higher than that of the low-dimensional Cs2CdCl4 (1.12 cm2 V−1 s−1), CsCdBr3 (0.77 cm2 V−1 s−1), and Rb4CdCl6 (0.72 cm2 V−1 s−1) counterparts (Supplementary Figs. 19a−f). The recombination dynamics of photogenerated carriers in CsCdCl3 SCs were investigated with time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectroscopy (Fig. 2h). The PL decay curves were fitted by a biexponential model, yielding an average lifetime (τ) of 2.48 μs. Carrier diffusion length (LD) was then calculated by the following formula of LD = (Dτ)1/2 = (μτkBT/q)1/2, where τ is the photogenerated carrier lifetime32,33, and LD was calculated as 6.96 µm that is significantly higher than that of the Cs2CdCl4 (2.6 µm), CsCdBr3 (1.3 µm), and Rb4CdCl6 (0.83 µm) (Supplementary Figs. 19g−i).

High resistivity typically results in low dark current, which is advantageous for detecting weak optical signals34. The resistivity of CsCdCl3 SCs was determined by measuring the current density under varying applied voltages, showing a high value of 7.45 × 1011 Ω cm (Fig. 2i), which is 1000 times higher than that of the CH3NH3PbI3 SCs (~108–109 Ω cm)35,36. Spatiotemporal PL mapping was performed under focused point excitation to visualize the carrier transport behavior in CsCdCl3 SCs. As shown in Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 20, with the delay time increased, the PL spot exhibited a progressive broadening, consistent with the diffusion of photogenerated carriers away from the excitation site. The PL profiles near the crystal edges displayed a larger FWHM and a slight peak shift, confirming the lateral diffusion behavior. To determine the diffusion coefficient, the cross-sectional profiles of PL spots at different delay time was fitted using the equation of σx2(t) = σ0,x2 + 4Dt, where Gaussian variance (σx2 (t)) reflects the size of PL spot. The carrier diffusion coefficient (D) was then calculated from a linear fit of the measured Gaussian variance as a function of time (Fig. 2k), yielding a high value of 0.0997 cm2 s–1.

The corresponding energy band diagram of device is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 21, where the energy level values of the CsCdCl3 photoactive layer were confirmed by ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy combined with the optical bandgap. The influence of the thickness of the CsCdCl3 layer on device performance is shown in Supplementary Fig. 22, where the device with an 800 μm-thick CsCdCl3 layer exhibits the best performance and was therefore employed for subsequent photodetection characterizations. We systematically investigated the effects of electrode thickness (Supplementary Fig. 23), area (Supplementary Fig. 24), and geometry (Supplementary Fig. 25) on device performance and clarified the optical shadowing effect of our electrode design. Moreover, we compared the photoresponse performance of lateral device architecture and semi-transparent ITO electrode configurations to demonstrate the rationality of device structure (Supplementary Fig. 26).

Current–voltage (I–V) characteristics of devices (Fig. 3b) reveal that the photocurrent increases with the increase of incident light power densities, because the generation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs is directly proportional to the incident photon flux37,38. Correspondingly, the on/off ratio gradually increases as the light intensity rises (Fig. 3c), with the maximum switching ratio reaching up to 38,150. Figure 3d presents the normalized spectral responsivity curves of CsCdCl3 SCs photodetectors with different thicknesses. As the crystal thickness increases from 0.2 to 1.0 mm, the response spectral bandwidth of devices narrows significantly, with the minimum FWHM being only 20 nm. The mechanism of this narrowing spectral response is illustrated in Fig. 3e. Specifically, the optical absorption characteristics of CsCdCl3 SCs determine that the short-wavelength photons with a high extinction coefficient (brown curve in Fig. 3e) are absorbed within an ultrashallow region near the illuminated Au electrode. In this region, the density of surface trap states is high, and the photogenerated carriers are rapidly quenched by surface-charge recombination. Moreover, the large carrier mobility of CsCdCl3 SCs allows carriers generated slightly deeper in the crystal to diffuse quickly toward these surface recombination sinks, further suppressing the short-wavelength responsivity. In contrast, long-wavelength photons exhibit a much smaller extinction coefficient (pink curve in Fig. 3e), enabling them to penetrate deeper into the crystal and generate carriers far from the surface recombination region. Overall, the spectral response of the device is governed by the interplay between the optical penetration depth and the effective charge-collection length (Ld), which accounts for both the intrinsic diffusion length and the drift length induced by the applied electric field. In thin crystals or under strong internal fields, the Ld is increasing, thereby improving charge collection at the electrodes and producing a broadband response. In contrast, in thick crystals or under a weaker field, Ld becomes much smaller. Only carriers generated in sufficiently deep regions, where long-wavelength photons are able to penetrate, fall within the collection range and can be extracted. In contrast, carriers generated by short-wavelength absorption are confined to the near-surface region, and therefore do not contribute to the responsivity. Consequently, the charge collection efficiency is much higher for long-wavelength excitation than for short-wavelength excitation, resulting in a narrowband responsivity spectrum. The spectral response curve in logarithmic scale shown in Fig. 3f exhibits a sharp peak at 240 nm with a cutoff wavelength at 267 nm (where responsivity drops to 1/10 of the peak value). The corresponding spectral-rejection-ratio (R240/R280) of the solar-blind band reaches 1.96 × 103, demonstrating the strong spectral selectivity of the device for solar-blind light41–44.

Figure 3h, i shows the solar-blind detection performances of photodetectors composed of various materials, evaluated by their photoresponsivity, dark current, and spectral-rejection ratio of solar-blind band. The figure establishes a dark current threshold of below 10−12 A, as many commercial testing devices (such as the Keithley 2400 tester) face challenges in accurately detecting dark currents below this level. The comparison shows that the CsCdCl3 SC-based photodetector achieves a high photoresponsivity of 35.4 A W−1 while maintaining a low dark current of 1.0 × 10−12 A and a high spectral-rejection ratio of 1.96 × 103, outperforming many comparable systems (Supplementary Table 4).

We comprehensively evaluated the operational stability of the photodetector under multiple test conditions45,46 (Supplementary Fig. 30), including high humidity (RH = 60%), elevated temperature (353 K), ambient air (21% O2), illumination exposure (240 nm, 42.6 μW cm−2), encapsulation configuration, and long-term ambient storage. These results show that the devices exhibit stable and repeatable photoresponse under different external conditions. Furthermore, the device retains constant photoresponse over a long time span of 50,000 s under continuous operation, with reversible on/off switching and no detectable degradation of photocurrent, dark current, and EQE (Supplementary Fig. 31). The excellent stability of device is attributed to the inherent tolerance of CsCdCl3 SCs to humidity and light irradiation, which inhibits photo-oxidation decomposition, outperforming conventional lead-halide perovskites (Supplementary Figs. 32,33)47,48. Temperature dependence of devices was also assessed to validate the harsh-environment suitability. Under a light illumination of 42.6 μW cm−2, both photocurrent and dark current increase with temperature, which can be attributed to enhanced carrier density, reduced barrier heights, and improved conductivity at elevated temperatures49. Importantly, when the working temperature was lowered to 293 K, the currents fully recover to the initial levels, demonstrating reversible thermal stability and resilience to temperature fluctuations (Supplementary Fig. 34). XRD patterns of CsCdCl3 SCs remain nearly unchanged from 293 to 573 K, excluding phase transition or structural decomposition during heating process (Supplementary Fig. 35a). The temperature-dependent conductivity follows the Arrhenius relation, yielding an activation energy of 0.652 eV (Supplementary Fig. 35b), which is higher than those of many reported perovskites (Supplementary Fig. 35c). This relatively high activation energy suggests suppressed ion migration in CsCdCl3 SCs. Therefore, the increased current at elevated temperature mainly arises from reversible thermally activated carrier transport rather than irreversible ion defect formation. Consistently, the photocurrent, dark current, and responsivity recover close to their initial values after cooling back to 293 K (Supplementary Fig. 35d).

We further explored its solar-blind imaging capacity using a large-area photodetector array (10 × 10) (Fig. 3j), each with an effective area of 6400 μm2. The current mapping of the device array (Supplementary Fig. 36) reveals highly uniform dark current and photocurrent distributions with minimal fluctuations, confirming excellent performance consistency across the array. Under solar-blind light illumination, the device array produces high-contrast images, with distinct current differences between exposed and unexposed pixels (Fig. 3k). Imaging results of the “tree” pattern under different incident wavelengths of solar-blind light with a consistent light power density of 42.6 μW cm−2. A tree-shaped target image under 240 nm laser illumination yields a remarkable current ratio of 14,280, enabling high-resolution visualization. In contrast, under 220 and 280 nm illumination, the current ratios drop to only 2.2 and 2.4, respectively, consistent with the intrinsic spectral selectivity of CsCdCl3 SCs. In addition, we integrated the CsCdCl3 SCs photodetectors with a commercial thin-film transistor circuit for imaging, which delivers an outstanding imaging quality under a light illumination of 240 nm (Supplementary Fig. 37), demonstrating its potential for practical integrated optoelectronic applications.

Spectral selective flame detection

According to the chemiluminescence reaction mechanism of the combustion process, various intermediate radicals emit distinct UV and visible bands, forming unique optical signatures for different fuels. These emissions span the solar-blind region (200–270 nm) and extend into the broader UV–visible range, serving as spectral fingerprints that enable selective flame identification (Supplementary Fig. 38). Taking advantage of the narrowband solar-blind photoresponsivity and strong spectral selectivity of CsCdCl3 SCs-based photodetectors, we developed a spectrally selective flame detection system (Fig. 4a). The photodetector converts flame-emitted UV photons into electrical signals, which are wirelessly transmitted to a cloud server for analysis and storage, then relayed to mobile devices for gas-type identification and fire warning. According to the optical signatures for different fuels, the spectral response peak of CsCdCl3 closely corresponds to the characteristic emission of the CO* Cameron band system, while the OH* radicals emission band is located near 280–320 nm and falls outside the detection window (Fig. 4b). This spectral overlap enables selective detection of specific flame species, enhancing discrimination between different combustion environments50–54. Photographs of various gas flames (Fig. 4c) and corresponding photocurrent responses (Fig. 4d) demonstrate the device’s discriminatory capability. Due to the inherent spatial inhomogeneity and temporal fluctuations of flame radiation sources, the response and recovery speed of flame detection are slower than those of direct laser irradiation devices (Supplementary Figs. 39,40). Methane (CH4), containing approximately 74.9% carbon, and butane (C4H10), with about 82.7% carbon, produce strong UV emission in the 200–270 nm range associated with the CO* Cameron bands during combustion. In contrast, ethanol, with about 52.1% carbon, yields a weaker photocurrent response. Furthermore, the photodetector does not respond to OH* radical emission generated by hydrogen combustion, resulting in negligible photocurrent output, which is consistent with the spectral characteristics of flames and the material’s intrinsic selectivity. To avoid deviations caused by differences in flame intensity, all gas fuels were supplied at equivalent flow rates during measurements. Consequently, the system can identify different gases by identifying photocurrent signals of different intensities. The distance-dependent flame detection was further evaluated (Supplementary Fig. 41). With the increase of the distance between the device and the flame source, the device exhibits a clear and stable response throughout the test distance range, and reliable flame detection can still be achieved at a detection distance of 120 cm. Fifty consecutive measurements show highly reproducible response with negligible signal fluctuation, confirming the reliable and repeatable detection performance of device (Supplementary Fig. 42). We further tested the switch characteristics of C4H10 flames at four time points (10:00, 12:00, 18:00, and 22:00), measuring both dark current and combustion-induced signals (Fig. 4e). The consistent performance across different time periods confirm the system’s reliability for continuous environmental monitoring.

During offshore oil extraction, associated natural gas is frequently released alongside crude oil, with CH4 typically accounting for the vast majority (≈80% or more) of the gas composition55. The CsCdCl3 SCs-based photodetector possesses rapid and accurate identification of CH4 combustion via its intrinsic sensitivity to flame-emitted UV radiation, facilitating timely detection of oil/gas leak-induced fires, activation of early-warning systems, and prompt emergency responses to protect platform operations (Fig. 4f). We evaluated the seawater tolerance of CsCdCl3 SCs to verify their suitability in marine working environments. XRD results reveal that the CsCdCl3 SCs maintained its structural integrity after prolonged immersion in seawater for seven days without the formation of any impurities (Supplementary Fig. 43). Importantly, the CsCdCl3 SCs-based device exhibits effective detection of CH4 flame even when fully submerged in seawater, confirming its robustness and feasibility for continuous underwater monitoring (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 44). In addition, the device demonstrates a reliable underwater optical communication characteristic. Underwater wireless data transmission was successfully demonstrated by encoding the word “ZZU” into ASCII, with case sensitivity preserved, in which binary “1” and “0” were represented by high and low light intensities, respectively. The signal was received by an underwater photodetector, converted to corresponding electrical levels, and demodulated back to text, faithfully reconstructing the entire message “ZZU” (Supplementary Fig. 45 and Supplementary Movie 1).

To evaluate the structural resilience of CsCdCl3 SCs in the presence of seawater, we conducted ab initio molecular dynamics simulations to examine water-induced degradation pathways56, exposing the surface of CsCdCl3 to H2O molecules. The simulations show that water adsorption leads to a small fluctuation in total energy, indicating stable surface interactions without inducing lattice disruption or decomposition (Fig. 4h). Notably, relaxed structural snapshots confirm that H2O does not penetrate into the CsCdCl3 lattice, supporting the material’s intrinsic stability under seawater. The low intercalation energies of H2O molecules on CsCdCl3 indicate that H2O incorporation exerts a minimal perturbation on the crystal lattice, suggesting that the structural integrity of CsCdCl3 remains preserved upon exposure to moisture (Supplementary Fig. 46a). Moreover, the calculated decomposition enthalpy of CsCdCl3 is higher than that of lead-halide perovskites such as CsPbCl3 and its hydrated analog (CsPbCl3·H2O), providing energetic evidence of its superior water resistance (Supplementary Fig. 46b)57.

Discussion

In summary, we have demonstrated a wide-bandgap perovskite, CsCdCl3, featuring a 3D mixed bonding structure of face-shared and corner-shared octahedrons, for solar-blind detection. Theoretical calculations reveal that the 3D electronic structure of CsCdCl3 affords more balanced carrier effective mass isosurfaces and enhanced band dispersion compared to other low-dimensional Cd-based perovskites, enabling isotropic charge diffusion in the continuous lattice. The resultant CsCdCl3 SCs demonstrated a high carrier mobility of 7.54 cm2 V−1 s−1 and a long carrier diffusion length of 6.96 μm. Consequently, we fabricated solar-blind photodetectors reaching a high photoresponsivity of 35.4 A W−1, low dark current of 1.0 × 10−12 A, excellent solar-blind imaging capability, and outstanding operational stability. Encouraged by the narrowband spectral response of 20 nm with high spectral-rejection-ratio of 1.96 × 103, we constructed a spectral-selectivity flame monitoring system, effectively distinguishing various types of flames, showcasing its significant potential in offshore oil flame monitoring. These findings establish a wide-bandgap perovskite design concept that couples electronic structure with carrier transport dynamics, providing a path for reliable solar-blind photodetectors.

Methods

Materials

All chemicals were used as purchased without further purification, which include Au (99.999%, ZhongNuo Advanced Material (Beijing) Technology Co., Ltd), cadmium chloride (CdCl2, >99.99%, Macklin), Cadmium bromide (CdBr2, >99.99%, Macklin), cesium chloride (CsCl, >99.9%, Macklin), and Rubidium chloride (RbCl, >99.9%, Macklin). Ethyl alcohol was purchased from Aladdin Chemistry Co., Ltd. (China).

Preparation of CsCdCl3 SCs

The CsCdCl3 SCs with a centimeter size were prepared by the hydrothermal technique. CsCl (6 mmol) and CdCl2 (6 mmol) were dissolved in 5 mL aqueous solution. The obtained mixture was transferred into a 25 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated in an oven at 180 °C for 12 hours. Subsequently, the temperature of the drying chamber was slowly cooled to room temperature at the rates of 4 °C h−1, 8 °C h−1, 16 °C h−1, and 21 °C h−1. For comparative experiments, varying amounts of CsCl (2 mmol, 4 mmol, 8 mmol, and 10 mmol) and CdCl2 (2 mmol, 4 mmol, 8 mmol, and 10 mmol) were each dissolvedin 5 mL of deionized water. The resulting mixtures were transferred into 25 mL nPTFE-lined stainless-steel autoclaves and heated at 180 °C for 12 h. After the reaction, the autoclaves were cooled to room temperature at a rate of 4 °C h−1. Then, the aqueous solution was removed with a rubber-tipped dropper, and the SCs were washed with ethyl alcohol. Subsequently, the prepared SCs were dried in a vacuum drying oven.

Fabrication of the devices

The device was fabricated using a metal-semiconductor-metal sandwich structure. The method involved depositing a 30 nm thick gold electrode onto a CsCdCl3 SC under a high vacuum of ~1.0 × 10–4 Pa using a high-vacuum metal evaporator. The active area of each device is 0.04 mm2. A 10 × 10 array mask was applied to the top surface of a centimeter-scale CsCdCl3 SCs, with each pixel having a dimension of 800 μm. A 30 nm-thick gold layer was subsequently deposited by vacuum evaporation. Another 50 nm-thick gold layer was then deposited on the entire back surface of the centimeter-scale CsCdCl3 SCs.

Theory calculations

DFT calculations were carried out using the projector augmented wave (PAW) method implemented in the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP)58. AIMD simulations were run in an NVT ensemble at a temperature of 300 K with a 1 fs time step. To simulate the perovskite surfaces, 2 × 2 slabs were cut from the CsCdCl3, which exposed the CsCl- and CdCl2-terminated (002) surfaces after relaxation. The vacuum regions above and below the perovskite slabs were populated with water molecules, whose density was kept consistent with the experimental density of liquid water.

Surface energy calculations

All the calculations were carried out using VESP. A slab model was established based on the crystal structure of CsCdCl3. Geometry and energy optimization were carried out using the PBE functional within the GGA to handle the exchange-correlation energy. The convergence criteria for energy and force were set to 10−5 eV and 0.005 eV Å−1, respectively, with a k-point grid of 3 × 1 × 1. To avoid interactions between adjacent molecular layers, a vacuum layer of 15 Å was applied. The surface energy can be calculated using the following equation of Esurface = (Eslab − nEbulk)/A, where Eslab is the total energy of the surface slab model, n is the number of formula units of bulk phase contained in the slab model, Ebulk is the energy of a unit in the bulk phase, and A represents the surface area of the surface slab.

Material characterizations

The morphology was investigated by field emission SEM (Jeol-7500F, 15 keV). The crystallinity characterizations were analyzed using XRD (Panalytical X’Pert Pro). The crystal texture was characterized by XRD 2θ-scans, φ-scans, and omega-scans with Cu Ka radiation. The element composition was analyzed using EDS. UPS measurements were performed to analyze the energy band properties of CsCdCl3 SCs, and the UPS radiation was generated by a He-gas discharge lamp (He I = 21.22 eV). The microstructure and crystallography were carried out by TEM (JEOL, JEM-3010). The vibration modes were analyzed by Raman (Horiba, LabRAM HR). Carrier diffusion imaging was measured by a FILM300 confocal microscopic system. The PL generated by the samples was collected through the same objective and relayed onto an image plane through a telescopic relay lens system. The transmitted epifluorescence image was then collected by a single-photon detector (PicoQuant PMA Hybrid) through an optical fiber that was connected to a precise x-y piezo motor stage (PI U-751.24) with a linear step resolution of 100 nm. A 360-nm pulsed laser with a spot size of around 1 mm was used to measure the surface recombination lifetime. For the transient photovoltage (TPV) measurements, a 245-nm picosecond pulsed laser diode (pulse width less than 120 ps) served as the transient excitation source. The laser excitation frequency was 32 kHz. The transient voltage, under open-circuit conditions, was measured using an oscilloscope that was externally triggered by the laser pulse. The absorption spectra were recorded using a UV−visible absorption spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, UV-3600i Plus). The optoelectrical tests of SCs were demonstrated by a digital source meter (Keithley, 2636B) in the air.

Devices characterizations

For testing the array device, a tunable monochromatic light source was employed to generate UV light at specific wavelengths (220, 240, and 280 nm). A collimated beam combined with an X-Y-Z displacement stage was used to illuminate the array pixel by pixel. The top electrodes of the array were connected to the grounded Au layer on the back side, serving as a common electrode. The photocurrent and dark current of each pixel were measured using a Keithley 2636B digital source meter. A 10 × 10 response matrix was obtained after the complete scanning process. In the flame detection process, dimethylamine, methane, butane, and ethanol were ignited sequentially inside a fume hood using the same combustion nozzle under identical flow and pressure conditions. The distance between the flame and the device, as well as the incident angle, was fixed at 12 cm. The dark current and photocurrent were recorded using a Keithley 2636B digital source meter under a bias voltage of 2 V. All flame-irradiation measurements for different gaseous fuels were conducted in ambient air at room temperature inside a fume hood. The gas flow rate was maintained at 3.5 L·min−1, and all gaseous fuels were supplied under identical flow conditions. For ethanol combustion, the liquid feeding rate was regulated to be equivalent to the gas flow rate used for the other fuels. For the underwater flame detection, the exposed metal and lead ends of the Au electrodes on both sides were encapsulated with epoxy resin to prevent water ingress. The device was mounted in a PTFE fixture with the incident surface facing upward, and the back lead was connected to the source meter through a resin-sealed through-hole. Water was added to an acrylic bath, and a methane flame was ignited using a spray gun inside a fume hood. The I–t characteristics were recorded using a Keithley 2636B source meter.
