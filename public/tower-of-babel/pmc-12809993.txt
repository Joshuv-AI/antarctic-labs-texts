Total coronary revascularization with anterior thoracotomy: it’s not just about sternotomy
Authors: Huseyin Gemalmaz, Ahmet Arif Aglar, Ahmet Yavuz Balcı
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery, various forms of minimally invasive approaches have been defined. The research evaluates TCRAT technique against traditional CABG methods through analysis of their effects on surgical results and postoperative clinical performance.MethodsWe report a comparative analysis of 75 TCRAT and 75 conventional CABG cases that we performed using cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and cardioplegic arrest between February 2021 and August 2022. The patients received clinical and echocardiographic follow-up for an average period of 17.05 ± 4.13 months.ResultsWe compared our first 75 TCRAT cases to the 75 conventional CABG cases we performed during the same time period. Mean CPB duration (113.9 ± 24.3 vs. 95.7 ± 20.1 min, p = 0.001) and mean cross-clamp duration (71.5 ± 18.2 vs. 58.3 ± 14.7 min, p = 0.003) of the TCRAT group were found to be significantly longer than those of the conventional CABG group. Hospital length of stay was significantly shorter in the TCRAT group (5.2 ± 1.8 vs. 6.8 ± 2.3 days, p = 0.003). Throughout the follow-up period, patients in both groups experienced a 47% reduction in the mean New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class. The study followed patients for 17.05 ± 4.13 months without detecting any signs of graft blockage through clinical or echocardiographic assessments although angiographic verification was not standard practice. In employed patients, the time to return to work was an average of 16 days in the TCRAT group, while in the conventional CABG group, this duration exceeded 2 months.ConclusionsTCRAT has proven its ability to deliver results similar to traditional CABG in the short term through our first experience with the procedure while providing sternotomy-free benefits that lead to reduced hospitalization duration and quicker recovery of daily activities. The technique requires additional research to determine its cost-effectiveness and identify suitable patient groups through formal economic assessments and extended follow-up studies.Keywords: Coronary artery bypass, Thoracotomy, Minimally invasive surgical procedures, Myocardial revascularization, Cardiopulmonary bypass

Introduction

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) has been established as an effective treatment for coronary artery disease (CAD) since the early reports in the 1960 s [1]. The technique has evolved through multiple decades of development [2] yet conventional CABG with median sternotomy continues to be a highly invasive procedure that restricts physical activity and diminishes both quality of life and treatment satisfaction during the first months after surgery [3]. The development of less invasive CABG procedures has shown limited progress despite advances in surgical methods and postoperative care. The development of minimally invasive CABG techniques remains ongoing because there exists no unified terminology system and no established protocols for these procedures. The current minimally invasive coronary surgery methods including MIDCAB grafting and TECAB grafting and HCR face multiple restrictions which prevent them from replacing traditional CABG procedures. The current minimally invasive coronary artery bypass procedures face multiple challenges which include high expenses and narrow patient eligibility requirements and insufficient treatment of complex coronary artery disease and challenging learning processes.

Babliak and colleagues introduced an innovative surgical technique involving a small left anterior thoracotomy. This method draws upon established principles from cardiac surgery for multivessel coronary disease revascularization and incorporates techniques commonly used in minimally invasive valve surgery. They coined this procedure “total coronary revascularization via left anterior thoracotomy (TCRAT)” [4]. We have incorporated this minimally invasive approach into our clinical practice. The research evaluated perioperative data and hospital results between TCRAT patients and CABG patients who received standard treatment at our facility during the same time frame.

Methods

We report a comparative analysis of 75 TCRAT and 75 conventional CABG cases that we performed using cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and cardioplegic arrest between February 2021 and August 2022. Descriptive characteristics of the patients are provided in Table 1. Prior to their procedures, all patients underwent a heart team evaluation [5] which included recommendations rooted in established guidelines [6] for elective or urgent isolated surgical revascularization.

Patient selection and group allocation

The research design used consecutive non-randomized observational methods for this study. The surgeons decided which patients received TCRAT or conventional CABG based on the order of patient arrival and their personal treatment choices instead of using propensity score matching. The first 75 patients underwent traditional CABG during the study period but the following 75 patients received TCRAT because our institution started using this technique. The study included all eligible patients who received their assigned treatment without any form of randomization. The study design lacked propensity score matching and randomization which created selection bias during group distribution [7]. Our study results might be less reliable because of this limitation yet we used consecutive patient enrollment and adjusted for initial differences through demographic and clinical variable analysis.

The researchers used EuroSCORE II to evaluate surgical risks and compare patient characteristics between groups [8]. The EuroSCORE II system uses patient-related and cardiac-related and operation-related factors to predict surgical mortality rates. The EuroSCORE II risk assessment showed similar predicted mortality rates between groups since the conventional CABG group had 1.82 ± 0.91% and the TCRAT group had 1.76 ± 0.88% (p = 0.683).

The selection process for TCRAT or conventional CABG focused on excluding patients with particular contraindications rather than selecting patients with specific indications. Patients were considered suitable for TCRAT if they did not have emergency status, substantial atherosclerotic involvement of the ascending aorta on preoperative imaging, concomitant valve pathology requiring intervention, or need for reoperation. The surgical team made the decision to perform TCRAT during preoperative planning sessions. The selection process for TCRAT cases might have been biased because the team did not use risk stratification or matching procedures which could have led to choosing simpler cases for the procedure. However, our comparable EuroSCORE II values between groups suggest that overt bias toward selecting more complex cases for conventional CABG was limited.

This study excluded patients who were classified as emergency cases (those who underwent catheterization and surgery on the same day), individuals with substantial atherosclerotic involvement of the ascending aorta, patients with concomitant valve pathology requiring intervention and those in need of reoperation.

Data was extracted from patient records in a retrospective manner and is presented as either the mean (± standard deviation) or numerical counts.

This study obtained approval from the local ethics committee under the reference number 5410–01/18, ensuring its adherence to the ethical principles outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions.

In addition to the routine preoperative assessments, every patient underwent a computed tomography (CT) scan to assess the condition of the aorta and its major branches. These findings helped in clarifying any existing atherosclerotic disease that could impact intraoperative handling, the intended clamping of the ascending aorta, and the determination of the perfusion strategy (either transaxillary or transfemoral) during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).

Standard cardiac anesthesia techniques were employed for both the induction and maintenance of anesthesia. Additionally, standard arterial and venous lines were used for invasive monitoring.

Patients were placed in a supine position in preparation for the surgery. The saphenous vein graft (SVG) harvesting was conducted with small, atraumatic incisions under direct surgical visualization. These harvested grafts were then preserved in a solution containing heparinized cold blood-saline mixture. For TCRAT procedures, a dedicated retractor (MIDAccess IMA Retractor; Delacroix-Chevalier, Paris, France) (Fig. 1) was placed in the chest through a minithoracotomy made well forward in the fourth intercostal space, with an incision length ranging from 6 to 10 cm (Fig. 1). The left internal mammary artery (LIMA) was harvested with direct surgical vision extending beyond the origin of the left mammary vein, and the entire surgical procedure was conducted using the same retractor (Fig. 2).

Peripheral arterial cannulation in TCRAT cases was carried out in accordance with the findings from preoperative CT scans and physical examinations. In 72 patients, this involved the right femoral artery and vein, while in 3 patients, it was done via the left femoral artery and vein. To access the artery and vein, a minor cutdown procedure was performed, and intravenous heparin at a dosage of 400 IU/kg was administered before cannula insertion. Even in cases where the body surface area exceeded 2.0 m², no additional venous cannula was placed in the jugular vein to augment venous return. Instead, a vacuum-assisted venous return was employed to optimize heart decompression. For conventional CABG, standard central cannulation technique was used. Throughout cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), patients were maintained at normothermic conditions.

In all cases, the pericardium was longitudinally incised from the apex to the ascending aorta and its sides. The ascending aorta was separated from the pulmonary trunk and encircled with a tape. This allowed for the exposure and manipulation of the ascending aorta – especially in TCRAT cases, in the direction of the thoracotomy, facilitating the placement of a small cannula for administering antegrade blood cardioplegia and venting the left ventricle during cardiac arrest.

In TCRAT cases, a separate minor skin incision was made in the anterior axillary line at the level of the second intercostal space. Through this incision, a transthoracic aortic clamp (Chitwood DeBakey clamp) was inserted. By gently pulling the aortic tape, the aorta was repositioned toward the thoracotomy site, allowing for the placement of an aortic cross-clamp under direct visualization. In all cases, cardiac arrest was initiated by infusing cold blood cardioplegia and sustained through periodic reinfusions of it every 20 min.

Individual tapes were looped around the left pulmonary veins and the inferior vena cava following cardiac arrest and heart decompression. Gently pulling on these tapes expose the heart and facilitates appropriate access to all coronary targets, particularly the right coronary artery (RCA) and circumflex (Cx) territories located on the lateral and inferior walls of the ventricle. Coronary anastomoses were conducted using standard anastomotic techniques, involving continuous 7–0 polypropylene sutures and the usual coronary surgical instruments. The left internal mammary artery (LIMA) was anastomosed as an insitu graft to the left anterior descending (LAD) artery. Saphenous vein grafts (SVGs) were proximally anastomozed to the ascending aorta using a side-biting aortic clamp.

Results

Demographic and preoperative characteristics

21.0% of the cases (n = 31) are female, while 79.0% (n = 119) are male. Their ages range from 40 to 83, with an average of 59.77 ± 9.14 years (Table 1). The age and gender distributions by groups do not show statistically significant differences (p > 0.05).

Among the cases included in the study; 37.3% (n = 56) had diabetes mellitus, 62.6% (n = 94) had essential hypertension, 12.0% (n = 18) had hyperlipidemia, and 8.0% (n = 12) had chronic kidney disease. The rate of tobacco use was found to be 64.0% (n = 96) (Table 1). The preoperative risk assessment showed mean EuroSCORE II values of 1.82 ± 0.91% for the conventional CABG group and 1.76 ± 0.88% for the TCRAT group (p = 0.683). There were no statistically significant differences between the rates of diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, tobacco use, chronic kidney disease, and EuroSCORE II in the sternotomy group and the thoracotomy group (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Operative data

The number of bypassed vessels averaged 2.96 ± 0.89 per patient without any statistical difference between treatment groups (p = 0.111). The TCRAT group required longer operative times than conventional CABG patients with an average of 234.52 ± 48.76 min (p < 0.001). The TCRAT group needed extended durations for cardiopulmonary bypass (101.29 ± 32.70 min vs. 69.41 ± 20.36 min, p = 0.001) and aortic cross-clamp (49.45 ± 15.89 min vs. 42.56 ± 11.83 min, p = 0.003). All patients received LIMA-to-LAD grafting (100%) but only 5.3% (n = 8) of cases required additional radial artery grafts with no group differences. The surgical team used saphenous vein grafts (SVG) to connect diagonal branches in 30% of cases and obtuse marginal branches in 52% and right coronary artery/posterior descending artery (RCA/PDA) territories in 63.3% of procedures without any intergroup variations. The TTFM results showed equivalent graft flow rates between LIMA (42.3 ± 12.5 ml/min) and SVG (58.7 ± 18.2 ml/min) for both treatment groups (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Postoperative outcomes

Postoperative exploration was performed on 2.7% of the cases (n = 4). The postoperative atrial fibrillation (AFib) incidence was determined to be 8.0% (n = 12) (Table 4). No intraoperative conversion to sternotomy was required in the TCRAT group. The peak troponin-I levels measured 8.42 ± 4.31 ng/ml in conventional CABG patients and 9.18 ± 4.67 ng/ml in TCRAT patients (p = 0.308) without any values reaching the threshold for perioperative myocardial infarction (> 35 ng/ml). While there was no statistically significant difference between postoperative exploration and postoperative AFib rates among the groups (p > 0.05), more postoperative explorations were carried out in the conventional CABG group.

The amount of bleeding varies between 150 ml and 1700 ml, with an average of 579.00 ± 276.14 ml. Red blood cell transfusion requirements averaged 1.48 ± 1.21 units overall, with a median of 0 units, as 52.0% of patients (n = 78) required transfusion while 48.0% (n = 72) required no transfusion. There was no statistically significant difference in the amount of bleeding or transfusion requirements between the groups (p > 0.05). In terms of the duration of hospital stay, there is a statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.003; p < 0.01), with the conventional CABG group having a longer hospital stay than the thoracotomy group. Peripheral cannulation-related complications occurred in 3 patients (4.0%) in the TCRAT group, including 2 cases of femoral hematoma and 1 lymphocele, all managed conservatively. The postoperative renal dysfunction (creatinine rise > 0.5 mg/dl) occurred in 6.0% of patients in total with no difference between groups (p = 1.000). The conventional CABG group had one patient (0.7%) who needed dialysis treatment after surgery. The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) results for cognitive assessment showed no statistical difference between the groups at both preoperative (28.3 ± 1.3 vs. 28.5 ± 1.1, p = 0.312) and 3-month postoperative (28.1 ± 1.5 vs. 28.3 ± 1.3, p = 0.388) assessments. The MMSE decline of 2 points or more indicated clinically important cognitive deterioration in 5.3% of patients (n = 8) without any group differences (6.7% vs. 4.0%, p = 0.719) (Table 5).

The study recorded zero deaths during the first thirty days of postoperative care and the total readmission rate reached 4.7% (n = 7) without any group differences (5.3% vs. 4.0%, p = 1.000). The patients maintained an average follow-up duration of 17.05 ± 4.13 months throughout the study period (12–30 months) without any group-related variations (17.71 ± 4.15 vs. 16.39 ± 4.04 months, p = 0.066). The clinical assessment of graft patency occurred in 92.0% of patients (n = 138) while CT angiography scans were used for symptomatic patients (8.0% or n = 12) at equal rates between groups. Suspected graft occlusion based on clinical or imaging findings occurred in only 1.3% of cases in each group (n = 2 total, 1 per group) (Table 5).

The use of arterial grafts was limited to LIMA-to-LAD in 94.7% of cases, with only 5.3% receiving additional radial artery grafts. This conservative approach to arterial grafting was consistent between both groups and was primarily due to surgeon preference and the learning curve associated with the TCRAT technique rather than technical limitations.

Discussion

In patients who require revascularization for significant left main coronary artery disease (CAD) with high-complexity CAD or for multivessel CAD with complex or diffuse CAD (e.g., SYNTAX score > 33), coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) remains the most robust form of myocardial revascularization [6, 9]. The enduring positive impact of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) in the treatment of stable multivessel coronary disease is believed to be rooted in the concept of “surgical collateralization.” [10]. This concept asserts that using a vascular conduit to bypass diseased proximal coronary artery segments can prevent future myocardial infarctions and subsequently contribute to the survival. Achieving long-term success in this context involves several crucial surgical considerations, including complete anatomical revascularization, precise construction of coronary anastomoses, and the patency of conduits. In this sense, median sternotomy has conventionally been the gold standard.

The CABG operation, which has been performed for over fifty years [1], has been continuously improved from a technical perspective in parallel with the increasing awareness of its value. When compared to percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), the adoption of minimally invasive approaches has emerged as the most significant step to be taken in adapting CABG to complement its significant advantages in major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) and survival.

Initially, in the mid-90s, the minimally invasive direct coronary artery bypass grafting (MIDCAB) procedure made its debut through an anterior mini-thoracotomy. The first group of 155 patients with isolated lesions in the left anterior descending artery (LAD) was documented by Antonio Calafiore in Italy and subsequently, numerous studies have emphasized the advantages of minimally invasive coronary procedures, underscoring it as an appealing alternative to traditional CABG by eliminating the necessity for sternotomy [11]. In the nature of things, this method initially shifted towards the traditional thoracoscopy approach and then logically advanced into robotics, ultimately resulting in the emergence of totally endoscopic coronary artery bypass grafting (TECAB), which was first described by Loulmet et al. [12]. Hybrid coronary revascularization (HCR), which combines the advantages of MIDCAB or TECAB and PCI to non-LAD lesions is also preferred as an alternative in minimally invasive coronary surgery.

Babliak et al. have made a significant breakthrough in minimally invasive coronary artery bypass grafting surgery with an extremely practical and elegant method that eliminates such disadvantages as limitations in patient selection, complex technological requirements, long procedural time, and a lengthy and challenging learning process [4]. Since February 2021, we have been offering this practical and easily adaptable minimally invasive method as an alternative to patients who have applied to our clinic for aortocoronary bypass graft surgery. For patients who have given informed consent, we have been performing aortocoronary bypass graft surgery using this method.

We compared our first 75 TCRAT cases to the 75 conventional CABG cases we performed during the same time period. Mean CPB duration (113.9 ± 24.3 min vs. 95.7 ± 20.1 min, p = 0.001) and mean cross-clamp duration (71.5 ± 18.2 min vs. 58.3 ± 14.7 min, p = 0.003) were significantly longer in the TCRAT group. These extended operative times can be attributed to the learning curve associated with this new technique and the technical demands of working through a limited thoracotomy incision [13]. Notably, the disproportionate increase in CPB time (30 min) compared to cross-clamp time (13 min) reflects the additional time required for peripheral cannulation, patient positioning, and establishing CPB through femoral access, as well as weaning from bypass and decannulation procedures that must be performed under CPB support in the TCRAT approach. The study found that neither CPB nor cross-clamp durations exceeded thresholds linked to higher morbidity rates and the authors expect these times to decrease as the institution gains more experience [4].

A critical aspect of our study was the comparable baseline surgical risk between groups, as demonstrated by similar EuroSCORE II values (1.82 ± 0.91% vs. 1.76 ± 0.88%, p = 0.683) [8]. The patient selection process shows minimal evidence of bias because of the way patients were distributed between groups. The study design lacks randomization and propensity score matching which makes it possible for hidden selection factors to impact patient group distribution [14]. The study results about patient selection need careful interpretation because the small number of participants restricts our ability to confirm that TCRAT works for all patients. The study requires additional research at multiple centers to determine specific patient selection criteria.

The TCRAT group required less hospital time for recovery because the sternotomy-free procedure led to decreased surgical trauma and better early patient movement [15]. Throughout the follow-up period in the study, patients in both groups experienced a 47% reduction in the mean New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class. TCRAT demonstrates equivalent functional results to traditional CABG procedures according to this study. During this period, no complaints or echocardiographic findings suggestive of graft occlusion were encountered. The intraoperative transit-time flow measurement showed that all grafts achieved optimal patency through flow rates above 20 ml/min and pulsatility indexes below 5 in both groups thus proving technical success [16]. The study results match previous research on minimally invasive surgical techniques which have shown equivalent results for revascularization procedures. Guangxin et al. conducted a propensity-matched analysis between MICS CABG and conventional CABG in diabetic patients which showed that MICS CABG required longer surgical times (4.25 vs. 4.00 h, p = 0.028) but produced less blood loss (600 vs. 700 mL, p = 0.032) and shorter hospital stays (5.0 vs. 6.0 days, p = 0.068). The study by Guangxin et al. showed that MICS CABG achieved equal one-year graft patency rates as conventional CABG methods (94.2% vs. 90.2%, p = 0.761) while maintaining equivalent major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular event rates during postoperative follow-up [17].

In employed patients, the time to return to work was an average of 16 days in the TCRAT group, while in the conventional CABG group, this duration exceeded 2 months. The significant difference between the two groups shows a crucial result that benefits patients. The fact that patients return to work earlier does not automatically make the procedure cost-effective. The study did not perform a cost-effectiveness assessment which would have included expenses for equipment and surgical time and hospital resource usage. The cost-effectiveness of TCRAT compared to conventional CABG remains uncertain because the procedure uses less expensive robotic systems but may require additional resources from peripheral cannulation supplies and longer surgical times. The study results do not support a definitive conclusion about TCRAT cost-effectiveness because it lacks complete economic evaluation. Future research needs to perform complete cost-effectiveness assessments [18].

The study results showed no substantial variations in blood loss during surgery or blood transfusions or postoperative cognitive performance (MMSE) between the two groups indicating TCRAT does not raise these risks despite its technical difficulties. The peripheral cannulation site complications in TCRAT patients numbered 3 (4%) and all presented as minor groin hematomas which required conservative treatment. The main advantage of minimally invasive techniques, compared to conventional CABG, arises from the avoidance of sternotomy. Utilizing a minimally invasive thoracotomy approach has shown improved thoracic stability, smaller incision size, and a decreased risk of wound infection. These elements have already been demonstrated to expedite early recovery and hasten the return to normal physical activity in different cardiac surgery procedures [19, 20]. The main constraint in all previously described minimally invasive coronary grafting techniques has been identified as technical difficulty. These complexities have led to a highly selective approach, involving patients with specific precautions and contraindications, consequently limiting the widespread adoption of these techniques in the field of coronary surgery.

The TCRAT technique represents a significant advancement by addressing several limitations of other minimally invasive approaches, including avoiding high equipment costs of robotic systems while allowing complete multivessel revascularization. The TCRAT system needs peripheral cannulation for its operation but this method does not work for patients who have severe peripheral vascular disease.

Limitations of the study

This study is limited due to its retrospective nature and the absence of randomization or propensity score matching, which may have introduced selection bias relatively small sample size from a single institution limits generalizability and precludes definitive conclusions regarding optimal patient selection criteria. The study faces two major limitations because it lacks data about graft patency assessment through angiography and major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events during mid- and long-term follow-up periods. The study fails to provide formal cost-effectiveness analysis which prevents researchers from determining the economic effects of TCRAT. The results from our first cases may improve as our team gains more experience with this procedure.

Conclusion

The TCRAT method shows promise as a new approach for minimally invasive coronary artery bypass surgery. The TCRAT procedure delivers similar clinical results to traditional CABG surgery but provides the benefit of sternotomy avoidance which leads to reduced hospitalization duration and faster work resumption.

Multiple essential restrictions need to be recognized in this study. The research study does not contain any evaluation of cost-effectiveness and it lacks data about long-term patient outcomes. The study results indicate TCRAT benefits but additional research with extensive economic assessments and long-term graft patency data is needed to establish definitive conclusions about cost-effectiveness and patient selection and long-term graft success. The main problem extends beyond sternotomy because researchers need to study both long-term outcomes and extensive clinical practice outcomes.
