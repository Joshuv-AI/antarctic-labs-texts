Scalable cooperative lane-change management for connected autonomous vehicles using MPC-based decision coordination
Authors: Hamed Kouhi, Georg Schildbach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The advancement of connected autonomous vehicles (CAVs) enables cooperative decision-making for traffic efficiency and safety. This study proposes a centralized lane-change decision coordination framework for multiple CAVs on highways, extending cooperative driving beyond traditional car-following strategies. The controller jointly optimizes speed adaptation and lane-change decisions over a finite prediction horizon to maximize overall traffic utility while ensuring collision-free maneuvers. Assuming reliable vehicle-to-infrastructure (V2I) communication, the planner computes acceleration, braking, and lane-change commands for all vehicles simultaneously. The underlying decision process is formulated as a mixed-integer optimization problem, which is computationally prohibitive for real-time deployment. To address this challenge, a priority-aware search strategy is developed to evaluate only the most promising lane-change combinations at each time step, integrated within a Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework enhanced with Artificial Potential Fields (APFs) for safety assurance and motion guidance. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed framework effectively balances safety, mobility, and system-level coordination while achieving real-time feasibility through significantly reduced computational complexity. The approach offers a scalable solution for future intelligent transportation infrastructures and real-time traffic management applications.

Introduction

In recent years, automated driving technologies have advanced rapidly, such as cooperative automatic parking1, critical overtaking for obstacle avoidance2, and cooperative maneuver for hunting a moving target3.

There are many contributions to the control of self-driving cars individually, i.e., as autonomous systems in an exogenic environment. To improve traffic efficiency, reduce traffic congestion, and increase safety, a promising approach is to use the potential of platooning4.

However, because of the complexity and uncertainty involved in the environment, autonomous vehicles have the tendency to behave in a conservative manner. If the vehicles can communicate their intentions and coordinate their actions, a much better performance can be expected. Such a cooperative approach requires the existence of a reliable communication channel in each vehicle, with the infrastructure (V2I) or directly with other vehicles (V2V). Recent advances in wireless communications enable multiple connected vehicles to collaborate for automated driving5,6.

Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC) has been widely employed as a high-potential approach for reducing traffic congestion and fuel consumption, and especially bringing the speeds of vehicles to their reference speeds on highways7–9. CACC automatically adjusts the speeds of vehicles to track the reference speeds. But the complexities and safety-critical nature of coordinating multiple autonomous ground vehicles (AGVs) in overtaking on a multi-lane road requires a deeper focus.

Research on cooperative control of self-driving vehicles, however, is still relatively sparse. Many approaches have been proposed in the direction of traffic management. Here, individual vehicles receive high-level commands or reference values from a centralized planner, e.g., a speed limit depending on the current traffic flow. However, the potential of V2I or V2V (called V2X) goes further, as it can also be used to resolve individual traffic situations, e.g., during a forced lane merging or at intersections. A centralized controller can significantly improve overall efficiency, and potentially even replace the need for traffic rules.

Literature review

A review on trajectory planning and tracking for autonomous overtaking was done in10. Lane changing, overtaking, and control for highway collision avoidance using active front steer and wheel torque control was done in11.

One of the important problems in the control of autonomous vehicles is overtaking and lane changing. In12, an autonomous vehicle collision avoidance system using active front steering and wheel torque control, and based on MPC, was proposed for lane change and overtaking. Optimal state feedback control for obstacle avoidance and lane changing was proposed in13, where a four-wheel steering (4WS) vehicle was studied. Emergency path planning of an active collision-avoidance system for an autonomous vehicle was designed in14, where lane-keeping and overtaking were studied. In15, for collision avoidance and stabilization in lane-changing maneuvers, an emergency steering control of the autonomous vehicle has been addressed. A path and speed planning method for lane changing and collision avoidance based on a cubic 3-D Bezier curve was proposed in16. In17, the overtaking problem based on active front steering (AFS) is investigated, and path planning and stability control of the collision avoidance system were addressed. In which authors plan an evasive trajectory to bypass the obstacle and avoid crashes.

Currently, because of the high potential of MPC, it is starting to be used also in the automotive industry18. Lane changing and collision avoidance steering at high speed using Nonlinear MPC (NMPC) has been addressed in19, in which the car is equipped with rear and front steering. A survey on the control of a wheeled mobile robot using MPC has been done in20. Simultaneous braking and steering control method based on NMPC for lane changing and obstacle avoidance was addressed in21. Lane changing maneuver using MPC theory and front steering was investigated in22. Path planning and path tracking for collision avoidance based on MPC with multiple constraints have been investigated in23. Obstacle avoidance in real-time with NMPC of autonomous vehicles was addressed in24, in which a bicycle model is used for the prediction of future robot behavior. In15, a longitudinal collision avoidance and lateral stability adaptive control system based on MPC was addressed by using emergency braking. Obstacle avoidance of autonomous vehicles based on MPC was studied in25. In26, a robust MPC is designed to track the desired path, and robust overtaking control of an autonomous electric vehicle with parameter uncertainties was addressed.

In27, for decision-making of a twin-vehicle cooperative driving in a stochastic dynamic highway, an algorithm based on reinforcement learning has been proposed. In28, an algorithm for overtaking an ego vehicle among multiple vehicles has been proposed. In29, based on deep reinforcement learning, the ego vehicle is controlled to pass through the other vehicles with a safe maneuver. In30, the aim is that the ego vehicle passes through the surrounding vehicles based on a reinforcement learning approach. Guaranteed collision-free overtaking has been proposed in31, in which an automated vehicle tries to overtake another vehicle with uncertain motion. In32, a motion planning technique based on the artificial potential field is introduced for overtaking of the autonomous vehicle. Obstacle avoidance control is also designed to avoid collisions during overtaking. Autonomous vehicles and human-driven vehicles are connected by V2V communication on a highway system.

In contrast to the control of a single autonomous vehicle, less progress has been made in investigating how multiple autonomous vehicles interact with each other on a multilane road. Existing approaches typically assume bilateral (V2V) or centralized (V2I) communication between vehicles. The motivation is to improve traffic efficiency, fuel efficiency, and the safety of the passengers.

Cooperative autonomous driving in highways based on dynamic coordination graphs and learning is addressed in33, where a two-lane one-way road with only one lane available for overtaking is considered. The trajectory planning problem for autonomous vehicles in traffic based on reinforcement learning is addressed in34; however, it does not include an index for optimal motion planning. To cut down the number of accidents, a cooperative collision avoidance scheme at lane changing maneuver has been proposed in35.

In32, the authors have proposed an optimization-based algorithm for solving the overtaking problem of multiple vehicles on a two-lane road. They have used many Artificial Potential Fields (APFs) to ensure safe overtaking of autonomous vehicles. These types of optimization problems may get stuck in a local minimum, so the optimization result is not reliable and robust. Given that, in the actual optimization problems belonging to the overtaking motion planning, there are many constraints (e.g., driving forces, steering angles, etc.) that do not allow an explicit solution.

In36, overtaking decision-making, motion planning, and control of autonomous vehicles have been proposed. In which, according to the safe distances criteria, the possibility of lane changing is investigated. As the authors mentioned, the interactive lane change logic needs to be proposed in the future.

APF is a simple and efficient method to design a path that meets the obstacle avoidance and tracking criteria. To get a collision-free path for obstacle avoidance of a single vehicle, in37, model predictive control and APF are combined. To do autonomous high-speed overtaking, a trajectory planning method based on robust MPC and APF has been proposed in38.

In automated vehicle research, lane change maneuver for overtaking maneuver or obstacle avoidance is one of the critical decisions to be considered in control algorithms39. Automatic lane change plays an important role in the optimality of the maneuver and convergence of actual speeds to the desired speeds to obtain efficient CACC for autonomous vehicles. Active lane change logic for an ego vehicle with human factor considerations has been proposed in40. To implement CACC for multiple autonomous vehicles, intelligent and automated lane changes should be performed with consideration of collision avoidance with surrounding vehicles.

Content and contributions

This paper considers the design of a centralized controller for cooperative automatic lane change maneuvers on a highway to extend the traditional CACC methods. As a cooperative approach, the goal is to increase overall efficiency, i.e., to maximize the performance of all vehicles simultaneously. To this end, a new hierarchical control algorithm is proposed. The basic idea is to select a subset of relevant vehicles, for which a suboptimal control problem is formulated and solved over a finite prediction horizon. The decisions to be optimized include lane changes and speed profiles for all vehicles. The decisions are then applied in a receding-horizon fashion. The performance of the algorithm is validated in a simulation study.

In this paper, it is assumed that the connected vehicles are communicating directly with other vehicles via a suitable V2X communication protocol to exchange information and enhance future safety assist functions. The vehicles obey a centralized planner/decision maker, which tries to maximize the overall utility for all vehicles, while respecting safety constraints. The algorithm we design is for decision making (lane changes) and trajectory planning for all vehicles.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

While a significant body of research has focused on lane-changing and overtaking maneuvers for a single ego vehicle [e.g.12,16,19,22,29,41], or on cooperative car-following within a platoon7–9, the problem of interactive and simultaneous lane-changing for multiple vehicle in a multi-lane environment has received considerably less attention. This paper addresses this gap by formulating a centralized control problem that jointly optimizes lane assignments and speed profiles for all vehicles in a highway segment.

Centralized coordination of multiple vehicles inherently leads to a mixed-integer optimization problem whose complexity grows exponentially with the number of vehicles, making real-time implementation challenging. To overcome this, we propose a priority-based lane-change search strategy integrated within an MPC framework. This approach evaluates only the most promising lane-change combinations (e.g., the top three priority vehicles) at each time step. This differs from methods that solve the full combinatorial problem [e.g.32., or rely on purely reactive, rule-based logic [e.g.36,40., . Proposed method offers a scalable trade-off between global optimality and real-time feasibility, significantly reducing the number of decision variables and the computational burden.

This work introduces a novel central controller that combines receding horizon optimization with an APF-based penalty function. The cost function is specifically designed not only to track desired speeds and avoid collisions but also to manage system-level coordination, for instance, by preventing deadlocks during lane changes through the inclusion of a penalty for vehicles in the left lane. This provides a flexible foundation for future research in cooperative traffic management.

In summary, the key novelty lies in the development of a computationally efficient, centralized coordination algorithm for interactive, multi-vehicle lane changes. By limiting the search space of the mixed-integer problem to a priority-based subset. The proposed framework provides a practical and scalable solution for real-world deployment, balancing safety, efficiency, and system-level coordination in a way that previous single-vehicle or non-interactive multi-vehicle approaches do not.

Lane changing dynamics

While the primary focus of this paper is on high-level decision-making for longitudinal control and lane-change coordination rather than detailed lateral dynamics, the instantaneous lane-change assumption commonly used in strategic planning has been replaced with a realistic finite-duration model. This model incorporates lateral trajectory planning, maneuver duration, and explicit constraints on lateral acceleration and jerk to ensure physical feasibility.

As established in the literature42,43, appropriate control inputs enable accurate trajectory tracking for safe lane-change execution. Among the various functions available for trajectory design, the sigmoid function is widely adopted due to its ability to naturally accommodate acceleration and jerk constraints while producing smooth, continuous transitions43.

The sigmoid function offers several desirable properties for autonomous vehicle trajectory generation: 1-smooth transition between lanes, 2-continuous velocity and acceleration profiles, 3- zero slope at start and end points (eliminating sudden steering inputs), and 4-adjustable aggressiveness through parameter tuning. These characteristics ensure passenger comfort, vehicle stability, and dynamic feasibility throughout the maneuver.

Algorithms for automatic lane changing of multiple vehicles

In the following, an innovative lane changes algorithm with low computational burden, based on priority lane changes is proposed that can be adjusted according to the central processor requirements to meet the real-time implantation.

Here, to better introduce the proposed algorithm, among all possible lane changes, three lane changes have been selected as priority. However, the algorithm is flexible to select a suitable number of automatic lane changes in priority according to the central processor requirements to obtain a real-time implementation.

The proposed algorithm is based on the model predictive control method. In the MPC, two important parameters control horizon and prediction horizon should be tuned. In this work, not only control inputs are considered in the prediction horizon but also vehicle lanes are variable in the prediction horizon.

To solve the automatic lane changing problem and find the suboptimal control inputs for all vehicles the following main stages should be executed.

Stage A: Producing the set of all possible lanes in the prediction horizon

Before proceeding to the general case of n vehicles, we first solve the problem of optimal lane changing for three vehicles. In the next sections, it will describe how 3 out of n vehicles are selected to solve the suboptimal lane changing problem.

In this stage, all possible lanes in the prediction horizon for three vehicles are created and saved in the set named SetL with the number of nset, L as all possible lanes number for each vehicle. In this section, number 0 and 1 indicate the left lane and right lane, respectively. As an example, SetL for a vehicle with the two-step prediction horizon will be achieved by executing Algorithm 1. It means that in the MPC, all future lanes should be checked to find the best future lanes.

The choice of a two-step prediction horizon for generating lane options for a vehicle in Algorithm 1 is just a special case with nset, L=4 however for the future lanes number, many possible options can be considered. If we have three possible lane changes for a vehicle then nset, L=8 cases should be investigated in the prediction horizon.

Stage B: Selecting the lane changes in priority among n vehicles

To choose the best lane changing maneuver, the proposed approach is to optimize the cost function for the whole lane change cases and then select the case with the smallest cost. But for real-time implementation, it is not possible to solve the full optimization problem for the whole lane changing cases. Instead, we need a suboptimal algorithm that is fast enough.

Because the proposed algorithm is based on the lane changing of three vehicles, the first three lane changing in priority should be determined. The weights α₂ and α₃ in the lane-change priority cost function Jvec play a critical role in balancing two key objectives: 1-responding to lane density (using α₂) and 2-ensuring target lane safety (using α₃).

Tuning the Weight α₂ (lane density penalty): weight controls the sensitivity of the priority calculation to congestion in the current lane. A higher α₂ gives greater priority to vehicles in dense traffic conditions, encouraging them to change lanes to balance lane utilization. Through iterative simulations, it is observed that setting α₂ too high (e.g., >>1) caused overly aggressive lane-change attempts even when target lane gaps were insufficient, leading to frequent aborted maneuvers. Conversely, setting α₂ too low (e.g., <<1) resulted in vehicles remaining in congested lanes even when empty adjacent lanes were available, reducing traffic flow efficiency. The value α₂=1 was selected as it provided a balanced response to moderate density variations without inducing unnecessary lane-change attempts.

Tuning the Weight α₃ (target lane safety penalty): This weight determines the importance of target lane availability in the priority calculation. A higher α₃ prioritizes vehicles that have a clear and safe gap in the adjacent lane, reducing the risk of collision during lane-change execution. Tuning experiments revealed that α₃=1 effectively identified vehicles with sufficient gaps while avoiding excessive prioritization that could starve vehicles in congested lanes of lane-change opportunities. This balanced approach ensures that safety considerations are adequately weighted without completely overriding density-based priorities.

The selection of these weights was performed through systematic simulation-based tuning.

A two-step prediction horizon for generating lane options is chosen in Algorithm 1 that represents a deliberate trade-off between computational tractability and the ability to anticipate near-future lane-change opportunities. The rationale for selecting a one-step prediction horizon in this section is as follows:

Computational Efficiency: The number of possible lane combinations grows exponentially with the prediction horizon. The one-step horizon thus keeps the optimization within real-time feasible limits while still providing meaningful future information.

Literature Support: The use of short prediction horizons for lane-change decision-making is consistent with related work in cooperative driving. For instance38, employs a prediction horizon of 1.5 s for high-speed overtaking maneuvers, while32 uses a receding horizon approach with limited look-ahead to maintain tractability. The one-step horizon adopted in this work falls within the range commonly reported in the literature for real-time trajectory planning applications.

Therefore, the one-step prediction horizon in Algorithm 1 is carefully selected to maximize predictive benefit while maintaining the computational efficiency required for real-time deployment in intelligent transportation systems. This horizon length ensures that the set of possible lane combinations remain manageable (nset, L=2 possibilities per vehicle) while providing sufficient look-ahead to avoid myopic decision-making.

The global lane-change coordination problem can be formulated as a mixed-integer optimization problem whose complexity grows exponentially with the number of vehicles and the prediction horizon. For n vehicles and horizon \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:H$$\end{document}, the number of lane-change combinations increases combinatorially, making global optimization computationally prohibitive for real-time traffic management. The proposed priority-based strategy reduces the search space to a fixed number of candidate lane-change combinations independent of the total number of vehicles, enabling scalable real-time implementation at the expense of global optimality.

Runtime analysis

This section analyzes the runtime of the proposed centralized controller as a function of the number of vehicles and the prediction horizon Nc, aiming to characterize the algorithm’s computational scaling behavior. Table 2 summarizes the impact of key parameters on runtime performance.

To illustrate the relationship between lane-change planning and computational load, a baseline scenario is examined with six vehicles, Nc=1, and nset, L=2 future lane-change possibilities (Fig. 8 and Fig. 9. Figure 8 shows the runtime when only longitudinal control (acceleration and braking) is considered, with no lane-change planning. Figure 9 presents the total runtime when both future lane assignments and control inputs are optimized over all iterations.

According to the proposed priority logic, three vehicles are selected as candidates for lane-change evaluation at each time step. With nset, L=2 possible lane choices per vehicle, this yields 23=8 distinct lane-change combinations to evaluate. Consequently, the MPC optimization in Fig. 8 must be executed eight times within each time step to assess all combinations. As expected, the total runtime values in Fig. 9 are approximately eight times those in Fig. 8. The minor discrepancy between the measured total runtime and eight times the MPC runtime corresponds to the execution time of Algorithm 2 (priority calculation). Thus, the total runtime of the complete algorithm can be estimated as:

Total Runtime ≈ (nset, L)3 × (MPC Runtime for longitudinal control) + (Priority Logic Runtime).

Unlike distributed approaches that rely on local decision making32,33, the proposed framework performs global coordination through a centralized planner. Compared with centralized trajectory planning approaches36,38, the proposed method explicitly addresses scalable multi-vehicle lane-change decision coordination using priority-based screening.

This study assumes ideal V2X communication with perfect, instantaneous data transmission between vehicles and the centralized controller. While this assumption is common in foundational work on cooperative driving algorithms7,9,32, real-world deployment must contend with communication imperfections such as packet loss, variable latency, and bandwidth limitations5,6.

Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented a centralized cooperative lane-change management framework for connected autonomous vehicles on multi-lane highways. The proposed approach extends traditional CACC by jointly optimizing speed profiles and lane-change decisions for multiple vehicles simultaneously, addressing a gap in the literature where most existing work focuses on single-vehicle maneuvers or platoon-based car-following.

The core contribution lies in the development of a priority-based search strategy integrated within an MPC framework enhanced with APF penalties. By evaluating only the three most promising lane-change combinations at each time step, the proposed method reduces the exponential complexity of mixed-integer optimization to a fixed and manageable search space, enabling real-time feasibility for up to ten vehicles. This represents a scalable trade-off between global optimality and computational tractability, distinguishing our approach from fully combinatorial methods that require offline computation and purely reactive rule-based strategies that lack predictive capability.

The controller design incorporates two critical safety objectives: convergence of actual speeds to arbitrary desired speeds for all vehicles, and collision-free operation throughout all maneuvers. The instantaneous lane-change assumption common in high-level coordination studies has been replaced with a finite-duration sigmoid trajectory model that respects lateral acceleration and jerk limits, ensuring physical feasibility and passenger comfort. The cost function is specifically structured to prevent deadlocks and unnecessary lane changes, with penalties for vehicles in the left lane and smoothness terms that minimize jerk.

Comprehensive simulation results demonstrate that the actual speeds of all vehicles converge asymptotically to their desired speeds while maintaining safe inter-vehicle distances.

Future work will focus on three main directions: 1-extending the priority mechanism to larger traffic scenes through hierarchical decomposition, 2-quantifying the optimality gap between the proposed heuristic and global optima from commercial solvers, and 3-investigating robustness to communication imperfections, including latency and packet loss. Addressing these challenges will bridge the gap between idealized assumptions and real-world deployment requirements.
