Clinical accountability in fragmented primary care for older adults: a practical framework for minimal multidimensional assessment and targeted testing in community and home-based settings
Authors: Joomong Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFragmented care in older adults often results in duplicated testing, delayed decision-making and unclear clinical responsibility. These challenges are particularly pronounced in community and home-based primary care settings, where multiple providers contribute to care without formal coordination structures. Although comprehensive multidimensional geriatric evaluation is recommended, its complexity limits routine implementation. This article proposes a pragmatic framework integrating assessment of daily functioning, cognitive screening, mood assessment and mobility evaluation with targeted laboratory testing guided by clinical findings. A central principle of this model is clinical accountability, defined as the responsibility of the clinician who performs assessment and interprets findings to integrate diagnostic results into patient management and follow-up. In this framework, accountability refers specifically to responsibility for documenting findings, initiating appropriate follow-up evaluation and ensuring communication of clinically significant results. By linking brief multidimensional assessment, problem-oriented testing and explicit responsibility for follow-up, this practice-informed communication offers a pragmatic, adaptable approach for family physicians and community-based clinicians working in fragmented care environments. It is presented as a practice-informed proposal rather than a validated framework, consensus statement or guideline.Keywords: Primary Health Care, Geriatrics, Continuity of Patient Care, Health Services for the Aged

Introduction

Fragmented care is a defining challenge in the management of older adults, frequently resulting in duplicated investigations, unclear clinical ownership and delayed or inconsistent decision-making. These problems are most pronounced in community and home-based care settings, where multiple providers contribute episodically without a single coordinating clinician, and where the absence of institutional structures makes clinical accountability especially difficult to assign. As populations age and multimorbidity becomes the norm, the consequences of fragmented care are increasingly clinically and economically significant.1

Comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) is widely regarded as the standard multidimensional approach for evaluating older adults.2–5 However, translating CGA principles from specialist geriatric practice into routine community care remains difficult.6 Similarly, although laboratory testing is frequently used in older adults, there is no consensus on a minimal essential laboratory panel, nor on which clinician should assume responsibility for ordering and interpreting these tests in shared-care settings.

Older adults are a heterogeneous population in whom disease burden reflects interacting biological, functional and social determinants,7 and multimorbidity is common, further complicating care coordination.8 Existing principles for the care of older adults with multimorbidity emphasise individualised prioritisation and coordinated decision-making, but do not fully operationalise who should assess, test and act in fragmented primary care.5

This article therefore proposes a clinically grounded framework for minimal multidimensional assessment and targeted laboratory evaluation in older adults, translating established geriatric assessment principles into a brief, responsibility-linked pathway specifically designed for community and home-based primary care settings where institutional coordination structures are absent or insufficient. The framework emphasises clinical accountability by linking assessment, diagnostic testing and management responsibility within a single clinical pathway. It is structured around three interconnected components: a minimal multidimensional clinical assessment, a targeted laboratory evaluation strategy and a set of principles for assigning clinical responsibility in fragmented care settings. To inform this framework, a narrative search of PubMed was conducted using the terms “geriatric assessment primary care”, “fragmented care older adults”, “clinical accountability”, “polypharmacy elderly” and “fall risk assessment”, with priority given to systematic reviews, clinical guidelines and widely cited validation studies published in English. The search covered publications available through April 2026, without a formal date restriction, and was intended to identify established, widely accepted instruments and principles rather than to provide an exhaustive systematic synthesis. The framework was additionally informed by the author’s clinical experience delivering primary and home-based care to older adults in a resource-limited community setting. Accordingly, this article is presented as a practice-informed scholarly communication rather than a systematically developed guideline, formal consensus (eg, Delphi) statement, or validated implementation model.

Minimal multidimensional clinical assessment

As part of the proposed framework, a minimal multidimensional assessment (hereafter, the minimal assessment) is defined to capture the essential domains of CGA while remaining feasible in real-world primary care.

Functional status: Activities of Daily Living

Activities of Daily Living (ADL) provide a direct measure of patient independence and are strongly associated with prognosis, institutionalisation, and mortality.9

Cognitive function: brief cognitive screening (Montreal Cognitive Assessment, Mini-Cog or Mini-Mental State Examination)

Brief cognitive screening is essential for detecting cognitive decline, assessing decision-making capacity and guiding treatment adherence. Contemporary practice increasingly favours the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) or the Mini-Cog, which offer greater sensitivity for mild cognitive impairment and, in the case of the Mini-Cog, brevity suited to time-limited encounters.10 11 The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) remains widely recognised but is subject to copyright and licensing restrictions and is therefore not presented here as a default or preferred instrument.12 Clinicians should apply tool-specific cut-offs (eg, MoCA<26) rather than assuming direct equivalence between instruments, and the choice of tool should reflect local availability, licensing and purpose.

Psychological status: Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS)

Depression is common but underdiagnosed in older adults and significantly impacts functional outcomes and healthcare utilisation.13

Mobility and fall risk: Timed Up and Go

The Timed Up and Go (TUG) test is a rapid and reliable tool for assessing mobility and fall risk.14 15

This structured approach ensures that medical context directly informs risk stratification and management decisions.

Together, these four validated instruments and the structured clinical history constitute a ‘minimal multidimensional assessment’ that enables rapid, clinically meaningful assessment in settings where full CGA is impractical.

The selection of these five domains was guided by three criteria: high clinical prevalence in community-dwelling older adults, availability of a brief validated instrument and direct actionability within a primary care encounter. Pain and nutritional screening were considered but excluded as they exceed what is feasible in a standard visit and are often prompted by abnormalities already captured within the core domains. Gait speed, although a strong predictor of adverse outcomes, overlaps substantially with the mobility information the TUG already captures, while sensory impairment and medication appropriateness are addressed within the structured clinical history rather than as standalone instruments. Frailty-specific indices such as the Clinical Frailty Scale and the FRAIL scale were considered valuable but not adopted as the core screen; where frailty stratification is a specific goal, they can be added.

Feasibility in primary care

A key strength of this minimal assessment is its relative feasibility compared with full CGA. ADL assessment relies on patient or caregiver history, brief cognitive screening and GDS requires structured but brief questioning, and TUG can be completed within a standard clinical space. In total, the core assessment battery may require approximately 15–20 min, which may not be feasible within all consultation formats.

Feasibility will therefore vary depending on consultation structure, visit duration, reimbursement systems, staffing and home-care infrastructure. In high-volume outpatient settings, full completion in a single visit may require scheduling adaptation or delegation of specific components to trained support staff. In home-based care, where the visiting clinician may be the sole provider, the assessment can be integrated into a structured home visit without additional resources. This framework is best understood as a scalable model whose implementation should be adapted to the specific constraints of each clinical setting, rather than a universally time-neutral intervention.18 Importantly, the framework is intended to support prioritised assessment rather than mandate completion of all components during every encounter.

Minimal laboratory evaluation

In older adults, indiscriminate laboratory testing may lead to overdiagnosis and unnecessary cascades of care. Therefore, laboratory evaluation should be structured, minimal, and clinically directed.

Core laboratory panel

The following baseline panel is proposed:

Complete blood count (CBC).

Complete blood count (CBC).

Blood urea nitrogen and creatinine (BUN/Cr)±electrolytes.

Blood urea nitrogen and creatinine (BUN/Cr)±electrolytes.

Glycaemic assessment (HbA1c or glucose).

Glycaemic assessment (HbA1c or glucose).

Liver function tests (LFT).

Liver function tests (LFT).

This baseline panel is not intended as a mandatory checklist but as a pragmatic starting point, to be applied selectively based on clinical findings, multimorbidity burden and medication risk. These components were selected because they provide broad yet clinically actionable screening for conditions that are highly prevalent and functionally relevant in older adults. CBC addresses anaemia, which is a common and treatable contributor to fatigue and functional decline. BUN/Cr and electrolytes screen for renal dysfunction and electrolyte imbalance, both of which influence medication safety and fall risk. Glycaemic assessment identifies uncontrolled or undiagnosed diabetes, which contributes to neuropathy, cognitive impairment and functional decline. LFTs assess hepatic function relevant to drug metabolism and polypharmacy safety. Lipid profile is not included in the core panel, as it is less directly linked to acute geriatric vulnerability, functional decline, cognition, mood or falls; it may be added selectively when cardiovascular risk stratification is an explicit management goal, consistent with routine chronic-disease care. Vitamin D, calcium, serum albumin and electrocardiography were likewise kept out of the core panel to preserve its minimal scope and are better ordered selectively when clinical findings warrant.

Responsibility for laboratory testing in fragmented care

One of the most under-recognised challenges in geriatric care is determining who should order laboratory tests; in fragmented systems, multiple providers may be involved without clear ownership, leading to duplicated testing or missed abnormalities.

This framework proposes a central organising principle: clinical responsibility for diagnostic testing should rest with the clinician who is able to interpret and act on the results within the context of the patient’s overall care.

This includes generating formal documentation when required for referral, care certification, care planning or inter-clinician communication, and aligns with emerging principles of diagnostic stewardship and clinical ownership.19 Clinical accountability here functions as a default coordinating principle rather than a legally or institutionally enforceable responsibility structure. Where ownership of findings is negotiated, delegated or variable, the framework does not override those dynamics but establishes a clear default—the clinician performing and interpreting the assessment assumes integrative oversight—reducing ambiguity that leads to gaps in follow-up.

Within this framework, laboratory decision-making follows three practical rules: the clinician providing longitudinal care performs baseline and follow-up testing; problem-focused testing may be done when clinical status changes, when access to that clinician is limited (eg, home-based care) or when results will directly influence immediate management; and where clinical uncertainty is high or specialised care is required, referral should precede extensive testing. Clinical application in home and primary care settings

This framework is particularly suited to home-based care, where the visiting clinician may be the sole provider with direct contact and where assigning accountability is therefore critical: without a clear responsible clinician, findings from even a brief assessment may go unacted on. The minimal multidimensional assessment can be completed within a standard home visit using no specialised equipment, with the visiting clinician assuming responsibility for documenting findings, initiating targeted laboratory evaluation where indicated and communicating results to the broader care team—directly addressing the coordination gaps most pronounced outside institutional settings.

Discussion

This framework extends the principles of CGA by translating them into a simplified, clinically actionable model tailored for routine primary care. Its distinctive emphasis lies not in reducing assessment complexity alone, but in making clinical responsibility explicit—an aspect that, while related to established concepts such as continuity of care, care coordination and diagnostic stewardship, is often left implicit in fragmented systems.19 20

A key strength is the framework’s alignment with real-world primary care workflows, where clinicians must decide rapidly with limited time and resources. By focusing on high-yield domains—functional status, cognition, mood, mobility and structured history—it enables meaningful risk stratification without full CGA implementation.

Importantly, this framework also addresses a critical but underexplored issue in geriatric care: the lack of clarity regarding clinical responsibility in fragmented care systems. While existing guidelines emphasise what should be assessed, they rarely define who should act on the findings. For example, the AGS Guiding Principles for the Care of Older Adults with Multimorbidity explicitly prioritise individualised goal-setting and care coordination, yet do not specify which clinician in a fragmented care system should assume responsibility for ordering, interpreting and acting on diagnostic findings.5 Similarly, CGA frameworks as summarised by Stuck and Iliffe2 and Ellis et al3 focus on the domains and outcomes of assessment but do not operationalise accountability for diagnostic integration in shared-care or community-based settings. More recent frameworks have advanced structured approaches to older-adult care: the WHO Integrated Care for Older People approach operationalises community-level screening across intrinsic-capacity domains21; NICE multimorbidity guidance (NG56) emphasises individualised assessment and reduction of treatment burden22; and the British Geriatrics Society and Canadian frailty pathways provide frailty-oriented identification and management routes. These frameworks increasingly define what to assess and how to structure care, but they generally stop short of specifying which clinician holds integrative responsibility for findings in fragmented, multi-provider community and home-based settings—the specific point this communication seeks to make explicit.

By supporting problem-oriented investigation guided by abnormal findings rather than routine testing, this model addresses two common diagnostic errors in community care—over-testing, which drives unnecessary cascades and under-recognition of significant conditions—both highlighted as patient-safety priorities.23

Beyond individual patient care, this framework may improve inter-provider communication, streamline referral processes and enhance continuity of care—addressing the systemic consequences of fragmented healthcare that have long been recognised as detrimental to older adults,1 20 and align with broader health-system goals of improving outcomes, efficiency and cost-effectiveness.24

Implementation may face practical barriers, including limited electronic-record interoperability, information-sharing challenges across providers and reimbursement constraints. It is acknowledged that proposing clinical accountability as a principle does not, in itself, guarantee that the responsible clinician will act on findings—a limitation that is particularly relevant in settings where role boundaries are informal or poorly enforced. To move from principle to practice, this framework would need to be embedded within existing clinical infrastructure. Concretely, this might involve the use of structured assessment templates that automatically assign a named responsible clinician at the point of documentation, integration with electronic referral pathways that require acknowledgement of findings before handover is complete, or institutional protocols that designate accountability based on the nature of the clinical encounter (eg, home visit vs outpatient consultation), supported where needed by digital health tools and system-level integration.25 Implementation may also be strengthened by aligned financial and reputational incentives for care coordination and quality improvement.26 Prospective evaluation of these implementation strategies, alongside their impact on clinical outcomes and care coordination, represents an important direction for future research. This priority is underscored by evidence that even well-specified preventive-care frameworks for older adults are adopted unevenly by primary care physicians in routine practice, indicating that defining what to assess does not, by itself, ensure that findings are acted on.27 It is also reinforced by patients’ own accounts, in which those with frequent hospital admissions describe fragmented, discontinuous care as eroding the experience of being cared for, whereas relational continuity and clear clinical responsibility help restore it.28

Its core principles—explicit accountability and targeted, problem-oriented testing—are conceptually transferable across primary care models, but the organisation of primary care differs substantially across health systems, from Medicare-based care in the USA to the National Health Service in the UK and to lower-resource settings; the mechanisms for assigning and documenting accountability would need adaptation to each context. Broad applicability is therefore proposed at the level of principles rather than as a uniform, directly transferable protocol.18

Limitations

This framework has several limitations. First, it is not a substitute for a full CGA in complex patients who require highly specialised multidisciplinary care. Second, effective application of this model still depends on the clinician’s sound clinical judgement rather than rigid adherence to predefined thresholds. Third, the framework does not itself guarantee accountability in fragmented multi-provider systems, particularly when communication pathways and role allocation remain poorly defined across institutions. Finally, this framework has not yet been prospectively validated against objective patient outcomes or formal care-assessment systems.

Conclusion

In fragmented care settings, the challenge is not only what to assess or test, but who should act on the results. A minimal multidimensional assessment combined with targeted laboratory evaluation and explicit clinical responsibility may provide a practical and scalable approach for structuring care in fragmented primary care.

Because it is brief and adaptable, this approach may be particularly suited to settings where full CGA is not feasible. Prospective validation is needed to determine whether it improves follow-up, care coordination, patient outcomes or resource use.
