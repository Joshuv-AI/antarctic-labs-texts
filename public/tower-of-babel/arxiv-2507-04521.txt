Every real number is a sum of two real numbers with diverging partial quotients
Dmitry Gayfulin, Erez Nesharim

Abstract
We show that every irrational number is a sum of two real numbers with diverging partial quotients. The proof is constructive. The key towards these results is an algorithm which was recently developed by Nikita Shulga [ 8 ] , and our study of this algorithm is of independent interest.



1
Introduction

Define the set

G:=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}\textrm{ or }a_{n}(\alpha)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\infty\}\,,

where
a_{n}(\alpha)
is the
n
th partial quotient in the continued fraction expansion of a real number
\alpha
, whenever its continued fraction expansion is
longer than
n
. The set
G
was first studied by Good
[
3
]
, where it is proved that its Hausdorff dimension is

1/2
.
In this paper we resolve a question suggested by Shulga
[
8
, Problem 6.2]
and show that every real number is a sum of two real numbers in
G
.

Theorem 1
.

For every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
there exist
\beta,\gamma\in G
such that

\alpha=\beta+\gamma\,.

(1)

Decompositions of real numbers as a sum of two numbers satisfying a Diophantine property are abundant in the literature. Arguably, the most notable examples of this sort are the theorem of Hall
[
4
]
, where
G
is replaced by

F(a):=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,a_{n}(\alpha)\leq a\textrm{ for every $n\geq 1$}\}\,,

for
a=4
, and the theorem of Erdős
[
2
]
, where
G
is replaced by the set of Liouville numbers. Another Diophatine property was studied by Cusick
[
1
]
. Let

S(a):=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,a_{n}(\alpha)\geq a\textrm{ for every $n\geq 1$}\}\,,

where
a\geq 2
is a fixed integer. Following Cusick’s result for
a=2
, Shulga
[
8
]
proved that for any
a\geq 2
and any
0\leq\alpha\leq\frac{1}{a-1}
, there exists a decomposition (
1
) with
\beta,\gamma\in S(a)
.

Our proof of Theorem
1
is constructive. In fact, our decomposition (
1
) is given by an algorithm that was developed and analyzed by Shulga
[
8
]
, and the proof relies on complementing its analysis. Shulga’s algorithm is described in the next subsection, and the analysis we carry out is of independent interest. The reader is referred to Section
2
for more background on continued fractions.

1.1
Shulga’s decomposition

In
[
8
]
Shulga established a nice and simple algorithm which decomposes real numbers into a sum of two real numbers with remarkable properties.

Definition 2

(Shulga)
.

For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
define sequences
\{b_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{c_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, and real numbers
\beta
and
\gamma
as follows:

1.

For every
n\geq 0
, if the integers
b_{k}
and
c_{k}
are defined for every
1\leq k\leq n
, then if

\alpha=[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}]\,,

we put

\beta:=[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]\,,\quad\textrm{and}\quad\gamma:=[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}]\,,

and finish the algorithm. Otherwise, define

\displaystyle b_{n+1}

\displaystyle:=a_{n+1}(\alpha-[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}])+1\,,\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle c_{n+1}

\displaystyle:=a_{n+1}(\alpha-[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n},b_{n+1}])\,.

2.

If
b_{n}
and
c_{n}
are defined for every
n\geq 1
, i.e., the algorithm takes infinitely many steps, then we put

\beta:=[0;b_{1},b_{2},\ldots]\,,\quad\textrm{and}\quad\gamma:=[0;c_{1},c_{2},\ldots]\,.

The following properties of the algorithm were established in
[
8
]
:

Theorem 3

(Shulga)
.

For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
the numbers
\beta
and
\gamma
in Definition
2
are well defined and satisfy

\alpha=\beta+\gamma\,.

Moreover, the sequences
\{b_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{c_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
in Definition
2
satisfy

\displaystyle b_{k}

\displaystyle=a_{k}(\alpha-[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}])\,\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle c_{k}

\displaystyle=a_{k}(\alpha-[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}])\,,

for any
1\leq k\leq n
.

As will be seen below, a key step in Shulga’s proof of Theorem
3
is to show that sequences
\{b_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{c_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of Definition
2
satisfy the inequality (see Section
3
)

c_{n}\geq b_{n}\,,

(2)

for every
n\geq 1
. Likewise, a key step in the proof of Theorem

1
is a strengthening of (
2
):

Theorem 4
.

For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
n>1
the Shulga decomposition satisfies

c_{n}>b_{n}\,.

(3)

In Section
4
we will show that Theorem
4
implies the following corollary, which implies Theorem
1
:

Corollary 5
.

For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
the Shulga decomposition (
1
) satisfies
\beta,\gamma\in G
. Moreover, if
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
then
\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Remark 1
.

If
\alpha=p/q\in\mathbb{Q}
, one can ask what is the length of the continued fraction expansions of the
\beta
and
\gamma
in the Shulga decomposition. It follows from the proof of Corollary
5
that this length is
O\left(q^{2}\right)
. Numerical evidence seem to suggest that the maximal length is
O(\log{q})
, but the study of this phenomenon lies beyond the scope of the current paper.

The key towards Theorem
4
is a detailed analysis of the sets of reals with a given first
n
steps of Shulga’s algorithm. This analysis allows us to prove further properties regarding the growth rate of the partial quotients of
\beta
and
\gamma

Theorem 6
.

For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
n\geq 1
the Shulga decomposition satisfies

\displaystyle b_{n+1}

\displaystyle\geq b_{n}\,,

(4)

\displaystyle b_{n+2}

\displaystyle\geq b_{n}+1\,,

(5)

\displaystyle b_{n}

\displaystyle\geq n\,.

(6)

The estimates in Theorems
4
and

6

are non-improvable, as one can see from the Shulga decomposition

531/629=[0;2,2,3,5]+[0;2,3,4,8]\,,

and the inequalities (
4
) and (
5
) have no analogues for the sequence
\{c_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
. For example, the Shulga decomposition

28244/141973=[0;6,27]+[0;30,29]\,,

shows that

is not necessarily monotonic. This gives a negative answer to
[
8

. In fact, one can show that for every

, there exists

such that

. Here is an example with

9974074083712426/149649898029019789=[0;16,227,231,235]+[0;240,229,233,237]\,.

Numerical evidence suggest that (

, and more specifically, that for every

b_{n}>2n
for all

large enough. However, the following theorem shows that a linear growth rate is best possible.

Theorem 7
.

There exists an irrational number such that the partial quotients of its Shulga decomposition (
1
) satisfy the inequalities

4n-2\leq b_{n}<c_{n}<5n.

(7)

Theorem

is proved by an explicit construction.

Remark 2
.

It is possible to deduce that the number constructed in the proof of Theorem

satisfies

for every

Basic properties of continued fractions

In this section, we recall some basic
facts about continued fractions that we use in the article. For a more detailed background, we refer to the classical literature on the subject (e.g.,
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
). Any real number
\alpha
can be represented as a continued fraction as follows

\alpha=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]:=a_{0}+\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cdots}}\,,

where
a_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
for
i\geq 1
. This representation is finite for rational
\alpha
, and infinite and unique for irrational
\alpha
. Each rational number has exactly two different representations

[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}]=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}-1,1]\,,\quad a_{n}\geq 2\,.

Thus, without loss of generality we will assume that the last partial quotient of rational numbers is greater than
1
.

For any
n\geq 1
we consider
a_{n}(\cdot)
as a function
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{N}
that returns the
n
th partial quotient of the continued fraction expansion. Of course, if
\alpha
is rational, then
n
should not exceed the length of the continued fraction expansion of
\alpha
; otherwise,
a_{n}(\alpha)
is undefined.

The convergents of a continued fraction are the rational numbers

\frac{p_{n}(\alpha)}{q_{n}(\alpha)}:=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}]

whose numerators and denominators are defined by the recurrence relations

\displaystyle p_{0}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=a_{0}\,,\;

\displaystyle p_{1}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=a_{1}a_{0}+1\,,\;

\displaystyle p_{n}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=a_{n}p_{n-1}(\alpha)+p_{n-2}(\alpha)\,,\quad\textrm{and}

(8)

\displaystyle q_{0}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=1\,,\;

\displaystyle q_{1}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=a_{1}\,,\;

\displaystyle q_{n}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=a_{n}q_{n-1}(\alpha)+q_{n-2}(\alpha)\,.

The following properties are well known

q_{n}(\alpha)p_{n-1}(\alpha)-p_{n}(\alpha)q_{n-1}(\alpha)=(-1)^{n}\,,

(9)

\frac{q_{n}(\alpha)}{q_{n-1}(\alpha)}=[a_{n};a_{n-1},\ldots,a_{1}]\,.

(10)

It is well known that the sequence of even convergents
\frac{p_{2n}(\alpha)}{q_{2n}(\alpha)}
is monotonically increasing, converging to
\alpha
from the left, while the sequence of odd convergents
\frac{p_{2n-1}(\alpha)}{q_{2n-1}(\alpha)}
is monotonically decreasing, converging to
\alpha
from the right. In our argument, we will frequently use intervals with endpoints of the form
\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}
. The order of those endpoints will depend on the parity of
n
. To avoid considering multiple cases, we will use the following convention:

Convention 1
.

For any
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}
, the
half-open-half-closed interval whose endpoints are
x+z
and
y+z
is

[x,y)+z:=\begin{cases}\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,x+z\leq\alpha<y+z\}&\text{if $x<y$},\\
\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,y+z<\alpha\leq x+z\}&\text{if $x>y$}.\end{cases}

Let
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
be arbitrary positive integers. Then the set of real numbers whose continued fraction expansion begins with
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
is the interval

\left\{\alpha\in[0,1]\,:\,a_{k}(\alpha)=a_{k}\textrm{ for every $1\leq k\leq n$}\right\}=\bigl[[0;a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}]\,,[0;a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}+1]\bigr)\,.

(11)

Note that

\displaystyle[0;a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}]

\displaystyle<[0;a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}+1]\,,\quad\textrm{for even $n$, and}

\displaystyle[0;a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}]

\displaystyle>[0;a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}+1]\,,\quad\textrm{for odd $n$},

so for any
n
odd Convention
1
is applied in (
11
). Lastly, recall that for any
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}
and
a
integers, the length
n
continued fraction
[0;a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1},a]
converges monotonically to
[0;a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}]
as
a
grows to infinity. In fact,

\displaystyle[0;a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1},a]

\displaystyle\underset{a\to\infty}{\nearrow}[0;a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}]\,,\textrm{ for even $n$, and}

(12)

\displaystyle[0;a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1},a]

\displaystyle\underset{a\to\infty}{\searrow}[0;a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}]\,,\textrm{ for odd $n$}.

Shulga intervals and their intersections

In this section we rephrase the Shulga algorithm in Definition
2

[

For every
n\geq 1
and positive integers
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\geq 2
and

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle:=\bigl[[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}-1],[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}]\bigr)+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{k-1}]\,,

(13)

\displaystyle C_{k}

\displaystyle:=\bigl[[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}],[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}+1]\bigr)+[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}]\,.

Note that for odd
k
the left and right endpoints of these intervals interchange according to our Convention

A_{n}:=\bigcap\limits_{k=1}^{n}(B_{k}\cap C_{k})\,,

and set
A_{0}:=B_{0}:=C_{0}:=[0,1]

Lemma 8
.

For every
n\geq 0
and positive integers
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\geq 2
and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}

then the first
n
steps in Definition
2
are well defined and yield the two sequences
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}

Proof.

By induction on
0\leq k\leq n-1
. The base case
k=0
is trivial. Assume that the first
k
steps in Definition
2
are
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}
. Since
\alpha\in B_{k+1}
one has

\alpha-[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}]\in\bigl[[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{k+1}-1],[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{k+1}]\bigr),

and hence by (
11
) the
(k+1)
th partial quotient of
\alpha-[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}]
is well defined and satisfies

b_{k+1}=a_{k+1}(\alpha-[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}])+1\,.

In particular, we have
\alpha-[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}]\neq[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}]
, and the
(k+1)
th digit of the first sequence in Definition
2
is well defined and equals
b_{k+1}
. Similarly, since
\alpha\in C_{k+1}
, one has

\alpha-[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{k+1}]\in\bigl[[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{k+1}],[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{k+1}+1]\bigr)

and hence by (
11
) the
(k+1)
th partial quotient of
\alpha-[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{k+1}]
is well defined and satisfies

c_{k+1}=a_{k+1}(\alpha-[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{k+1}])\,,

which again agrees with Definition
2
. This finishes the induction.

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{q_{n-1}(q_{n-1}-q_{n-2})}\frac{c_{n}t_{n-2}}{t_{n}+{t_{n-1}}}\,.

Hence, by (
10
),

\displaystyle\frac{q_{n}}{q_{n-1}}\frac{q_{n-1}}{q_{n-1}-q_{n-2}}>\frac{t_{n}+t_{n-1}}{c_{n}{t_{n-2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(c_{n}+1)t_{n-1}+t_{n-2}}{c_{n}{t_{n-2}}}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{c_{n}}\right)[c_{n-1};c_{n-2},\ldots,c_{1}]+\frac{1}{c_{n}}\,.

Applying (
10
) once again results in

\displaystyle\frac{[b_{n};b_{n-1},\ldots,b_{1}]}{1-[0;b_{n-1},\ldots,b_{1}]}

\displaystyle>\left(1+\frac{1}{c_{n}}\right)(c_{n-1}+1+[0;c_{n-2},\ldots,c_{1}])-1\,,

thus establishing the inequality (
17
).

∎

We are now ready to rephrase and prove Theorem

.

Lemma 10
.

Given
\alpha\in[0,1]
, either there exist unique
n\geq 0
and finite sequences
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
such that
\alpha\in A_{n}
and

\alpha=[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}]

or there exist unique infinite sequences
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots
and
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots
such that

\alpha\in\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}\,.

Proof.

By induction on
n
. Let
n\geq 1
,
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\geq 2
and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
satisfy
\alpha\in A_{n}
. Recall that by definition (
1
) one has

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle=\bigl[[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}-1],[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]\bigr)+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1}]\,,

\displaystyle C_{n}

\displaystyle=\bigl[[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}],[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}+1]\bigr)+[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]\,,

and for every two integers
b\geq 2
and
c\geq 1
let this notation be extended by setting

\displaystyle B_{n+1}(b)

\displaystyle=\bigl[[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n},b-1],[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n},b]\bigr)+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}]\,,

\displaystyle C_{n+1}(b,c)

\displaystyle=\bigl[[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n},c],[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n},c+1]\bigr)+[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n},b]\,.

It is enough to show that

\displaystyle C_{n}

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup\limits_{b=2}^{\infty}B_{n+1}(b)\cup\{[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}]\}\,,\quad\textrm{and}

(26)

\displaystyle B_{n+1}(b)

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup\limits_{c=1}^{\infty}C_{n+1}(b,c)\,,\quad\textrm{for any $b$ such that $B_{n+1}(b)\cap C_{n}\neq\emptyset$}\,.

(27)

Since the unions in (
26
) and (

) are disjoint, this also implies the uniqueness in the statement of the theorem. It follows from (

) that the right-hand sides of (

) and (

) are the intervals

\displaystyle\bigl[[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}],[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}+1]\bigr]+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}]\,,\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle\bigl([0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}],[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}+1]\bigr]+[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n},b]\,.

respectively. Thus, the containments in (

) are equivalent to the inequalities

\displaystyle[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}+1]+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}]

\displaystyle\geq[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}+1]\,,\quad\textrm{and}

(28)

\displaystyle[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n},b]+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n},1]

\displaystyle>[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n},b-1]+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}]\,.

(29)

Similarly to the way it was done in the proof of Lemma
9
, the inequality (
28
) can rewritten as

\frac{1}{t_{n}(t_{n}+t_{n-1})}\leq\frac{1}{q_{n}(q_{n}+q_{n-1})}\,.

(30)

Lemma
9
(
4
) implies that
c_{k}\geq b_{k}
for all

. Therefore, from the recurrence law (
8
) one can easily deduce that
t_{k}\geq q_{k}
for every
1\leq k\leq n
. In particular, this holds for
k=n
and
k=n-1
, which implies (

As for (
29
), fix any
b
that satisfies
B_{n+1}(b)\cap C_{n}\neq\varnothing
. Setting
b_{n+1}=b
and arguing as before, shows that (
29
) can be rewritten as

\frac{1}{q_{n+1}(q_{n+1}-q_{n})}<\frac{1}{t_{n}(t_{n}+t_{n-1})}\,.

The last inequality follows from Lemma
9
(
3
) with
n+1
instead of

. Indeed, as
b_{n+1}\geq 2
, one has
q_{n+1}-q_{n}>q_{n}
and therefore

\frac{1}{q_{n+1}(q_{n+1}-q_{n})}<\frac{1}{q_{n+1}q_{n}}\,.

.
∎

Starting from this point and until the end of this section and through Sections
4
and
5
, given a real number

, the sequences

and

stand for the corresponding digits that arise from Shulga’s algorithm with input

. We will omit the dependency on

when it does not create ambiguity.

Now we are ready to prove Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem 4.

Assume by contradiction that for some
n\geq 2
the partial quotients do not satisfy
c_{n}>b_{n}
. By (
2
) this means that
c_{n}=b_{n}
. Denoting

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=[0;b_{n-1},\ldots,b_{1}]\,,

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=[0;c_{n-1},\ldots,c_{1}]\,,\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=c_{n}=b_{n}\,,

and substituting it into Lemma
9
(
4
) and Lemma
9
(
5
) gives

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle>(1+y)(z-1+x)\,,\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle>\left(1+\frac{1}{z}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{y}\right)(1-x)-1\,,

respectively. This is a contradiction since these inequalities can be rearranged into

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle<\left(1+\frac{1}{y}\right)(1-x)\,,\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle>\left(1+\frac{1}{y}\right)(1-x),

respectively.
∎

4
Rational or divergent decomposition

In this section we use Theorem
4
in order to deduce Corollary
5
. Fix a number
\alpha\in[0,1]
and let
b_{n}
and
c_{n}
be as defined in Defition
2
. Also, let
p_{n},q_{n},s_{n},t_{n}
be as defined in (
14
).

Lemma 11
.

The sequence of ratios
\frac{t_{n}}{q_{n}}
is strictly increasing.

Proof.

Indeed, since
b_{n}
and
c_{n}
are integers, the inequality (
3
) implies that
c_{n}-b_{n}\geq 1
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{t_{n}}{t_{n-1}}\frac{q_{n-1}}{q_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{[c_{n};c_{n-1},\ldots,c_{1}]}{[b_{n};b_{n-1},\ldots,b_{1}]}

(31)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{(c_{n}-b_{n})+([0;c_{n-1},\ldots,c_{1}]-[0;b_{n-1},\ldots,b_{1}])}{b_{n}+[0;b_{n-1},\ldots,b_{1}]}>1+\frac{1}{2b_{n}+1}\,.

The inequality in (
31
) follows from the trivial estimates

[0;c_{n-1},\ldots,c_{1}],\;[0;b_{n-1},\ldots,b_{1}]<1/2\,,

which follow from the fact that
c_{n-1},b_{n-1}\geq 2
.
∎

Lemma 12
.

If the algorithm takes infinitely many steps then

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{t_{n}}{q_{n}}=\infty\,.

(32)

Proof.

Suppose that the sequence
c_{n}
is unbounded. From (
15
) one can see that

\frac{c_{n}}{2t^{2}_{n}}<\frac{1}{2t_{n-1}t_{n}}<\frac{2}{q^{2}_{n}}\,.

Thus,

\frac{t^{2}_{n}}{q^{2}_{n}}>\frac{c_{n}}{4}\,.

Therefore, the divergence (
32
) follows directly from Lemma
11
.

Considering the remaining case, if all the numbers
c_{n}
, and hence
b_{n}
, do not exceed some positive constant
N
, then (
31
) implies that

\frac{t_{n}}{q_{n}}>\left(1+\frac{1}{2N-1}\right)^{n-1}\,,

thus finishing the proof.
∎

Remark 3
.

In fact, it follows from the growth rate estimates of Section
5
that the situation in the second paragraph of the proof of Lemma
12
cannot occur, namely, that if
\{c_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is an infinite sequence then it is unbounded. However, since the above argument is short we decided to present it here and keep the more involved analysis for the next section.

Now we are ready to prove Corollary
5
.

Proof of Corollary
5
.

First, we show that if
\alpha=p/q\in\mathbb{Q}
then the algorithm converges in finitely many steps.
Let
C_{n}
be the interval obtained on the
n
th step of the algorithm. The fact that
\alpha\in C_{n}
is equivalent to the property

\frac{p}{q}-\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\in\bigl[[0;c_{1},\ldots c_{n}],\ [0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}+1]\bigr).

Therefore, it follows from (
11
) that the denominator of
\frac{p}{q}-\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}
is at least
t_{n}
. On the other hand, this denominator does not exceed
q_{n}q
. Thus, we have the inequality

\frac{t_{n}}{q_{n}}\leq q\,.

Due to Lemma
12
, the algorithm must stop at a finite step.

If
\alpha
is irrational, one can easily see that the numbers
[0;b_{1},b_{2},\ldots]
and
[0;c_{1},c_{2},\ldots]
must lie in the set
G
. Indeed, from (
3
) we have

q_{n+1}q_{n}>t_{n}^{2}\,.

Thus,

b_{n+1}>\left(\frac{t_{n}}{q_{n}}\right)^{2}-1\,.

Due to Lemma
12
, the sequences
\{b_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, and therefore
\{c_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, both tend to infinity as
n
tends to infinity.
∎

5
Growth rate

In the previous section, we showed that the sequences
b_{n}
and
c_{n}

tend to infinity as
n\to\infty
. In this section, we provide some quantitative estimates on the growth rate of these sequences. First, we establish the inequality (
4
).

Lemma 13
.

For all
n\geq 1
one has
b_{n+1}\geq b_{n}
.

Proof.

We suppose the contrary, and denote
b_{n}=t
. Then
c_{n}\geq t+1
and
b_{n+1}\leq t-1
. From (
17
), applying the trivial estimates
1+\frac{1}{c_{n+1}}>1
and
[0;c_{n-1},\ldots,c_{1}]>0
, we get

t>\left(t+2\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{t}\right),

and thus obtain a contradiction, as
t\geq 2
.
∎

Lemma 14
.

Suppose that
b_{n}\geq 8
. If
b_{n+1}<c_{n}
, then
c_{n+1}>\frac{3}{2}b_{n}
.

Proof.

Denote
c_{n}=t
, hence
b_{n+1}\leq t-1
. Applying (
17
), we obtain

t>\left(1+\frac{1}{c_{n+1}}\right)\left(t+1\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{b_{n}}\right).

Thus, we have

1>\left(1+\frac{1}{t}+\frac{1}{c_{n+1}}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{b_{n}}\right),

which is equivalent to

\frac{c_{n+1}}{b_{n}-1}>1+\frac{c_{n+1}}{t}\,.

(33)

If
c_{n+1}\geq t
then

\frac{c_{n+1}}{b_{n}}>2\frac{b_{n}-1}{b_{n}}\geq\frac{7}{4}\,.

If
c_{n+1}<t
then it follows from (
33
) that

c_{n}=t>2(b_{n}-1)\,.

From (
17
) with
n+1
instead of
n
and Theorem
4
, it follows that

\displaystyle c_{n+1}

\displaystyle\geq b_{n+1}+1>\left(1+\frac{1}{c_{n+1}}\right)\left(c_{n}+1+[0;c_{n+1},\ldots,c_{1}]\right)\left(1-[0;b_{n},\ldots,b_{1}]\right)

\displaystyle>\left(2(b_{n}-1)+1\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{b_{n}}\right)>2(b_{n}-1)\left(1-\frac{1}{b_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=2b_{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{b_{n}}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{49}{32}b_{n}\,.

The lemma is proved.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
6
.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

The first inequality (
4
) is already established in Lemma
13
. One can easily check (e.g., by a computer program) that (
5
) and (
6
) hold if
n\leq 6
. In fact, all
\alpha\in[0,1]
for which
b_{6}
is defined satisfy

b_{6}\geq 8\,.

(34)

Note that for all
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
we have
b_{n+k}\geq b_{n}
,
c_{n+k}>b_{n}
, so for
n\geq 6
we may apply the consequence of Lemma
14
.

We start with the second inequality (
5
). Denote
b_{n}=t
. Then
c_{n}\geq t+1
. If
b_{n+1}\geq t+1
then
b_{n+2}\geq t+1=b_{n}+1
by Lemma
13
, so (
5
) is established. Otherwise, by Lemma
14
we have
c_{n+1}\geq\frac{3}{2}b_{n}
, and therefore, applying (
17
) for index
n+2
instead of
n
, we obtain

b_{n+2}>\frac{3}{2}b_{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{b_{n+1}}\right)-1\geq\frac{3}{2}b_{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{b_{n}}\right)-1=\frac{3}{2}(b_{n}-1)-1>b_{n}+1\,.

Thus, we established (
5
).

Let us now prove the last inequality (
6
). Let
n>6
be an arbitrary integer greater than
6
. If
b_{k+1}\geq c_{k}
for all
6\leq k\leq n-2
then Theorem
4
implies by induction that
b_{n-1}\geq n+1
. Therefore, Lemma
13
gives
b_{n}\geq n+1
. Otherwise, let
l
be the smallest index
6\leq l\leq n-2
satisfying
b_{l+1}<c_{l}
. Due to the definition of the index
l
we conclude as above that
b_{l+1}\geq b_{l}\geq l+1
. By Lemma
14
, one has

c_{l+1}>\frac{3}{2}b_{l}\,,

and therefore, applying (
17
) once again we have

b_{l+2}>\frac{3}{2}b_{l}\left(1-\frac{1}{b_{l+1}}\right)-1\geq\frac{3}{2}b_{l}\left(1-\frac{1}{b_{l}}\right)-1=\frac{3}{2}(b_{l}-1)-1\geq b_{l}+\frac{3}{2}\geq l+\frac{7}{2}\,.

Since
b_{l+2}
and
l
are integers this implies that
b_{l+2}\geq l+4=(l+2)+2
. This argument can be repeated with the index
6
replaced by
l+2
, so it follows by induction with base (
34
) that (
6
) holds for every
n\geq 1
. Thus, the proof of Theorem
6
is completed.
∎

5.1
Proof of Theorem

In this subsection, we construct an irrational number

. In our construction, we define the infinite sequences

and
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots

(35)

Using (

By Lemma

.

We put

and

(40)

\displaystyle[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{6},\ldots]

The proof of the nesting property (

Lemma 15
.

For every

(41)

The proof goes by induction. For

\displaystyle x:=c_{n}-\left(\frac{t_{n}}{q_{n}}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle z:=c^{(3)}_{n+1}-\left(\frac{t^{(3)}_{n+1}}{q_{n+1}}\right)^{2}.

\displaystyle\left(\frac{t^{(2)}_{n+1}}{q_{n+1}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{t_{n}}{q_{n}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{c_{n}+4+[0;c_{n},\ldots,c_{1}]}{c_{n}+2+[0;b_{n},\ldots,b_{1}]}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle>c_{n}+4-\left(\frac{c_{n}+4}{c_{n}+2}\right)^{2}(c_{n}-x)=(c_{n}+4)\left(1-\frac{c_{n}+4}{c_{n}+2}\frac{c_{n}-x}{c_{n}+2}\right)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{t^{(3)}_{n+1}}{q_{n+1}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{t^{(2)}_{n+1}}{q_{n+1}}\right)^{2}\left(1+\left[0;c^{(2)}_{n+1},c_{n},\ldots,c_{1}\right]\right)^{2}

. On the other hand, since

\left(\frac{t^{(2)}_{n+1}}{q_{n+1}}\right)^{2}>\left(1+\frac{1.9}{c_{n}+2.1}\right)^{2}(c_{n}-x)=\left(\frac{c_{n}+4}{c_{n}+2.1}\right)^{2}(c_{n}-x)\,.

(44)

Thus, we obtain the following upper bound on

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle<c_{n}+4-\left(1+\frac{1.9}{c_{n}+2.1}\right)^{2}(c_{n}-x)

\displaystyle<c_{n}+4-\left(1+\frac{3.8}{c_{n}+2.1}\right)(c_{n}-x)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{t^{(3)}_{n+1}}{q_{n+1}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{t^{(2)}_{n+1}}{q_{n+1}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{t^{(2)}_{n+1}+t_{n}}{t^{(2)}_{n+1}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle>\left(c^{(2)}_{n+1}-3/2\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{c^{(2)}_{n+1}+1}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle>\frac{\left(c^{(2)}_{n+1}-3/2\right)\left(c^{(2)}_{n+1}+3\right)}{c^{(2)}_{n+1}+1}

\displaystyle>c^{(2)}_{n+1}=c^{(3)}_{n+1}-1\,,

where the last inequality follows from

Now we are ready to prove Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

Due to the definition of
b_{n}
and

in (

) and (

), one can see that the inequality (

) is satisfied. Hence, it suffices to establish the nesting property (

.

With out loss of generality, we assume that

is even. This containment is equivalent to (recall Figure

)

[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1},b_{n}]+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1},c_{n}]>[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1},b_{n}-1]+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1}].

(45)

The inequality (
45
) is equivalent to

\frac{1}{t_{n}t_{n-1}}<\frac{1}{q_{n}(q_{n}-q_{n-1})}\,,

which can be rewritten as

c_{n}+[0;c_{n-1},\ldots,c_{1}]<\left(\frac{t_{n}}{q_{n}}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{1-[0;b_{n},\ldots,b_{1}]}\,.

Applying (
41
), one can see that it suffices to show

c_{n}+\frac{1}{c_{n-1}}<\frac{c_{n}-1}{1-[0;b_{n},\ldots,b_{1}]}\,,

which is equivalent to

1-[0;b_{n},\ldots,b_{1}]<1-\frac{1+1/c_{n-1}}{c_{n}+1/c_{n-1}}\,.

Taking into account that
b_{n-1}\geq 2
, we see that it is sufficient to show

\frac{c_{n-1}+1}{c_{n}c_{n-1}+1}<\frac{2}{2b_{n}+1}\,.

(46)

As
c_{n}\geq b_{n}+2=c_{n-1}+4
, the inequality (
46
) follows from

\frac{c_{n-1}+1}{c^{2}_{n-1}+4c_{n-1}+1}<\frac{2}{2c_{n-1}+5}\,.

(47)

One can easily see that (
47
) holds whenever
c_{n-1}>3
. Thus, we established that
B_{n}\supseteq C_{n}
.

Now we show that
C_{n}\supseteq B_{n+1}
.
This containment is equivalent to

[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n},b_{n+1}-1]+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}]<[0;b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]+[0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}+1]\,.

(48)

The inequality (
48
) is equivalent to

\frac{1}{q_{n}(q_{n+1}-q_{n})}<\frac{1}{t_{n}(t_{n}+t_{n-1})}

(49)

which can be written as

\frac{1}{b_{n+1}-1+[0;b_{n},\ldots,b_{1}]}\left(\frac{t_{n}}{q_{n}}\right)^{2}<\frac{1}{1+[0;c_{n},\ldots,c_{1}]}\,.

Taking into account (
41
), we obtain that it is sufficient to show that

\frac{c_{n}}{c_{n}+1+[0;b_{n},\ldots,b_{1}]}<\frac{1}{1+[0;c_{n},\ldots,c_{1}]}\,.

(50)

Note that the left-hand side of (
50
) is less than
\frac{c_{n}}{c_{n}+1}
, while the right-hand side of (
50
) is greater than this quantity.
Thus, we established the property
C_{n}\supseteq B_{n+1}
and Theorem
7
is proved.
∎
