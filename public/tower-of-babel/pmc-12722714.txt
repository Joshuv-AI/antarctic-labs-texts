Peripheral cyclical expression of GABA-A receptor subunit genes and menstrual cycle affective change: a dimensional, transdiagnostic study
Authors: Jordan C. Barone, Raffaele Romano, Anisha Nagpal, Antonio Salerno, Anna Patterson, Elizabeth S. Wenzel, Gabriela Guzman, Viraja Alluri, Pauline Maki, Graziano Pinna, Tory A. Eisenlohr-Moul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Menstrual cycle affective change (MCAC), including conditions like Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD), is triggered by ovarian hormone fluctuations, but its neurobiological mechanisms remain poorly understood. A leading hypothesis implicates abnormal GABA-A receptor (GABAAR) subunit regulation in altering sensitivity to neuroactive steroids, a theory difficult to test in humans. We examined peripheral mRNA expression of GABRD, GABRG2, and GABRA5 in a transdiagnostic sample of 112 participants (75 psychiatric outpatients with suicidality; 37 controls) to capture a dimensional range of MCAC severity. Participants provided daily affect ratings and five LH-timed blood samples using PAXgene tubes for RT-PCR analysis. Replicating preclinical findings, GABRG2 expression decreased during the midluteal phase. Critically, individual differences in both person-mean expression and perimenstrual change (AUCi) were correlated with cyclical symptom trajectories. Greater perimenstrual increases or higher average expression were associated with classic luteal-phase worsening of anxiety (GABRG2, GABRA5), irritability (GABRG2), and suicidal ideation (GABRG2, GABRA5, GABRD). However, relative to those with moderate or stable expression, perimenstrual decreases or lower average expression were also linked to cyclical worsening of anxiety and suicidal ideation that emerged around menses and persisted into the follicular phase. Overall, more stable subunit expression was associated with less cyclical symptom change. These findings could be consistent with a loss of homeostatic stability in GABAergic plasticity, rather than a simple unidirectional differences in levels or changes, as a potential correlate of MCAC. Peripheral GABAAR gene expression warrants further investigation, particularly in experimental studies, to determine whether there are causal associations between subunit gene expression and MCAC.

Introduction

Menstrual Cycle Affective Change (MCAC) refers to cyclical changes in mood and behavior triggered by cyclical fluctuations in estradiol (E2) and progesterone (P4). Sensitivity to these hormonal shifts varies widely, with many in the general, healthy population experiencing no hormone-triggered symptoms or mild symptoms (i.e., Premenstrual Syndrome) [1–4]. More severe, clinically significant points on this spectrum include Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD)—mood symptoms that emerge in the luteal phase and remit shortly after menstruation [5]—and Perimenstrual Exacerbation (PME)—cyclical worsening of psychiatric symptoms without complete follicular remission [3, 6, 7]. Indeed, these extreme manifestations of MCAC are linked to substantial functional impairment [2, 8] and risk for suicidal thoughts and behaviors [9–12]. Human experimental models of the menstrual cycle [13, 14], pregnancy [15], and menopause [16] support a hormone sensitivity framework in which typical ovarian hormone fluctuations elicit symptoms via underlying neurobiological vulnerability.

A leading mechanistic hypothesis implicates altered GABAergic signaling in response to fluctuations in progesterone-derived neuroactive steroids (NAS) like allopregnanolone (ALLO), which modulate inhibition via the γ-aminobutyric acid type A receptor (GABAAR) [17]. This pentameric chloride channel is assembled from five subunits encoded by 19 genes (α1–6, β1–3, γ1–3, δ, ε, θ, π, and ρ1–3) [18, 19]. Subunit composition governs receptor localization and NAS sensitivity: α/β/γ configurations are typically synaptic, mediate phasic inhibition, and are relatively NAS-insensitive, whereas α/β/δ configurations are extrasynaptic, mediate tonic inhibition, and are highly NAS-sensitive [20–22].

In animals, GABAAR subunit expression shifts in response to hormonal state [23–27]. Accordingly, one hypothesis for MCAC pathophysiology is that altered expression of GABAAR subunits—either as stable traits or cyclical trajectories—modifies neurobiological responses to normal hormonal rhythms, triggering MCAC [17, 26, 28].

The present study begins to test this hypothesis in humans by examining cyclical changes in peripheral gene expression for GABAAR δ, γ2, and α5 subunits. We selected these because they are each hormonally regulated and linked to affect. The δ subunit generally appears in extrasynaptic GABAARs and mediates tonic inhibitory current [29, 30]. In preclinical models, fluctuations (increases or decreases) in P4 and ALLO precipitate δ subunit increases in the hippocampus [31], nucleus accumbens [32], and periaqueductal gray matter [33]. This upregulation reduces excitability, protecting against anxiety and seizures during hormonal fluctuations [34, 35]. Further, plasticity in δ subunit expression [36] during peripartum hormonal fluctuations appears crucial in reducing postpartum depressive-like symptoms [28, 37]. GABAARs containing the γ2 subunit are generally synaptic and mediate phasic inhibitory current [38]. γ2 is also regulated by P4 and ALLO, but generally in the reverse direction of the δ subunit: increases in P4 and ALLO predict γ2 subunit decreases in preclinical models in the estrus cycle or pregnancy [28, 34, 39, 40]. While some studies suggest a positive correlation between P4 and the γ2 subunit, this is either not replicated in females [32], or holds for P4 but not ALLO [41], further supporting that increased ALLO and decreased γ2 subunit concentrations may be specific to NAS fluctuation in females. However, evidence is mixed regarding the γ2 subunit and affective symptoms [31, 32, 42, 43]. Although the α4 subunit is known to be hormonally sensitive and to modulate GABAergic sensitivity to NAS [44, 45], we excluded it given technical difficulties in detecting GABRA4 in peripheral blood. Instead, we included the α5 subunit (GABRA5), which shares several functional properties with α4, including NAS sensitivity. Although less is known about α5 hormonal regulation [40, 43, 46, 47], its expression —at both synaptic and extrasynaptic sites —has been shown to increase with environmental adversity [48–51], aggressive behavior [52], and depressive phenotypes [53]. Together, alterations in any of these subunits may shift inhibitory tone [29, 30, 38], influencing affect and behavior.

This study is the first to assess peripheral GABAAR subunit gene expression longitudinally across the menstrual cycle, doing so in a large, diverse sample of healthy individuals and psychiatric outpatients with suicidal ideation (SI). We test the hypothesis that altered overall levels or menstrual cycle trajectories of subunit expression in peripheral blood will correspond to dimensionally greater cyclical affective changes (MCAC).

Materials and methods

This study, including recruitment of healthy controls (HCs), is a supplemental investigation to the “CLEAR-3” RCT (NCT04112368). Hypotheses, power calculations, and planned analyses—including the preregistered shift from categorical to dimensional modeling of MCAC if categorical reliability checks failed—were preregistered at https://osf.io/m9u5r.

Participants

Patient recruitment – transdiagnostic suicidal ideation

Psychiatric outpatients reporting past-month SI were recruited as described previously [8]. Recruitment targeted SI and natural menstrual cycles, not pre-diagnosed MCAC, to ensure a broad MCAC severity range.

Healthy control recruitment

HCs were recruited via ads and listservs for “individuals with no history of mental health diagnoses for a clinical research study.” Eligibility matched patient criteria except for exclusion of psychiatric care and lifetime SI.

This investigation was approved by the University of Illinois Chicago Institutional Review Board (IRB), and all participants provided informed consent.

Study design

Enrollment included the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5) [54], and the Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behaviors Interview (SITBI-R) [55, 56]. Starting the next day, participants completed the Daily Record of Severity of Problems (DRSP; [57]), which captures DSM-5 PMDD symptoms, and the Adult Suicidal Ideation Questionnaire (ASIQ; [58]). Medication, substance use, and illness were recorded. After one baseline cycle, participants completed at-home urine ovulation tests during expected ovulation windows (luteinizing hormone (LH), ClearBlue®; threshold 40 mIU/mL). After a positive test (day 0), five morning lab visits were scheduled on days +7, +10, +14, +18, and +22. Patient visits occurred during two randomized conditions: active hormone manipulation or placebo (bisected by a washout cycle; full study protocol can be found on clinicaltrials.gov). The present study analyzes only placebo-condition data alongside HC observational visits.

RNA extraction and Real-Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR)

At each visit, 35 ml of blood was drawn, including 5 ml in PaxGene RNA tubes. Whole blood was prepared according to manufacturer’s instructions (PreAnalytiX, Hombrechtikon, Switzerland). After RNA isolation and extraction, total RNA was reversed transcribed into cDNA, followed by RT-PCR with primer pairs for β-actin and the δ, γ2, and α5 subunits of the GABAAR. Full methods and primer pair sequences (Table S1) can be found in Supplemental Materials.

Variables and data preparation

Menstrual cycle time

Visit scheduling targeted midluteal (LH + 7), perimenstrual (LH + 14), and midfollicular (LH + 22) phases, with LH + 10 and LH + 18 as intermediates. For visit-based analyses, lab visits were dummy-coded (visit 1 at LH + 7/midluteal, visit 3 at LH + 14/perimenstrual, and visit 5 at LH + 22/midfollicular, with the perimenstrual visit as the reference group in phase contrasts). Then, we re-scaled from −1– +1 based on individual LH surge and menses onset (at time = 0) to create a standardized, continuous cycle time variable. Time was modeled with two splines (luteal and follicular), scaled −1– +1. Detailed time scaling methods are described in [59].

Gene expression

GABRG2, GABRD, and GABRA5 gene expression (coding for the γ2, δ, and α5 subunits of the GABAAR, respectively) was measured as fold-change or 2-ΔCt (Ct: Cycle Threshold, reference β-actin). Assays were run in triplicate, with outliers (2-ΔCt > 0.1) re-assayed in triplicate. For limit of detection (LOD), a two-step approach ensured reliability: 1) an analytical threshold based on the quantification cycle (Cq ≥32) excluded samples with unreliable amplification, 2) we applied a normalized biological LOD, defined as 2^–ΔCt < 0.00001, corresponding to approximately 100,000-fold lower expression than the reference gene. This threshold was empirically derived from standard curves and replicate analyses, reflecting the lowest cDNA concentration consistently detected across replicates. Our approach aligns with MIQE guidelines, which define LOD as the lowest concentration at which ≥95% of replicates yield detectable amplification. Any sample with a non-detectable value in all assays was recorded as 0.00 and included in analyses. Outliers (> 95th percentile) were Winsorized to the 95th percentile value. We then log-transformed the Winsorized value + 1 to approximate normality.

Two trait-level indices of gene expression were calculated per gene as level 2 moderators in multilevel models: 1) person-mean, and 2) perimenstrual change (increase or decrease from the midluteal to midfollicular phase) as area under the curve with respect to increase, AUCi). Person-mean is the average fold-change of that gene across all five blood draws per individual, providing one value per person that represents person-to-person “baseline” differences in gene expression. AUCi was calculated across the perimenstrual weeks per [60] (see Equation 1). Briefly, AUCi summarizes repeated measures change (overall increase or decrease) across a timeline into one comparable index between individuals. For each person, AUCi is the area between the lowest value datapoint and all higher values (y-axis) across each independent time observation (x-axis), approximating a trapezoidal shape. In our design, AUCi is the sum of the areas between visit 1 and 2, visit 2 and 3, and so on, where the distance between visits was scaled based on individual cycle phase length, to standardize hormonal meaning. We interpolated missing values as the mean between neighboring visits. If a participant was missing two consecutive values, we removed that participant. Symptoms

We examined daily depressed mood, anxiety, and irritability on the DRSP. For patients, we calculated mean SI from eight ASIQ items adapted for daily use. See Tables S2-3 for item text.

Covariates

Sensitivity analyses included age (linear, z-scored), recruitment group (0 = HC, 1 = Suicidal Patient), history of DSM-5 diagnosis (0 = no lifetime diagnoses, 1 = at least one lifetime DSM-5 diagnosis), SSRI use (0 = no use, 1 = any use during study), and parity (0 = never given birth, 1 = given birth).

Analytic plan

Analyses examined: 1) how GABAAR subunit expression varies across the cycle, and 2) whether baseline differences in overall levels (the person-mean moderator) or cycle trajectories of subunit expression (the AUCi moderator) associate with cyclical symptom trajectories.

Subunits across the cycle

Multilevel models with random intercepts by participant tested the relationship between cycle phase and gene expression by comparing the LH + 7 (midluteal), LH + 14 (perimenstrual), and LH + 22 (midfollicular) lab visits. Next, we characterized subunit trajectories across visits using standardized cycle time variables and spline-based multilevel growth models. Splines were specified as linear or quadratic piecewise functions (see Supplemental Methods, Tables S4-S5, for model fit statistics). We tested model fit improvement with random slopes for the luteal and follicular splines. Sensitivity analyses tested the impact of covariates.

Subunits and symptoms

To each best-fitting growth model (i.e., predicting daily symptoms from luteal and follicular time variables), we then added person-mean gene expression, perimenstrual AUCi gene expression, and the interactions of both of these gene expression moderators with the time splines. These models examined how subunit expression indices interact with cycle time to predict symptom trajectories.

Visualizing significant statistical moderation of mcac by gene expression indices

When our models indicated significant moderation (i.e., interaction between individual differences in gene expression indices with the cyclical trajectory of symptoms), we describe the shape of these associations by inspecting means plots subgrouped by levels of the gene expression moderator (lowest, middle, and highest 20% observed in the sample). First, person-centered means (with standard errors (SE)) of the symptom across the cycle were plotted in three subgroups of the sample (lowest, middle, and highest 20% of the moderator). Second, raw means across the cycle are provided for the same subgroups to provide a more direct view of the data and any intercept differences emerging across levels of the moderator.

Additional details

All primary analyses were preregistered (https://osf.io/m9u5r). As the first human test of these links, our approach was theory-driven. Best practices discourage multiple comparison corrections in this context, as they increase false negatives [61, 62], which are of concern during early translational signal detection. All tests were two-sided, α = 0.05. Analyses were completed in R (4.2.1) with packages ‘menstrualcycleR’ [59], ‘lme4’ [63] ‘stats’ [64], ‘interactions’ [65], ‘mgcv’ [66], and ‘performance’ [67].

MCAC symptoms

MCAC is often conceptualized categorically, assuming HCs show little cyclical symptom change, while some psychiatric patients experience clear PMDD/PME. However, extant work supports a continuous view: cyclical affective changes range from absent to severe, with no obvious threshold for demarcating binary diagnosis [2].

Accordingly, we hypothesized (https://osf.io/m9u5r) that MCAC would be minimal in most HCs and variable among patients. As expected, MCAC did not sort into reliable subgroups (for distributions of percent-cyclical-change across cycles, see Fig. 1). We assessed reliability of categorical MCAC using the commonly-used 30% pre/perimenstrual symptom elevation threshold for daily ratings [5]. 49 (65%) of patients with SI showed 30% elevation of at least one symptom in any cycle, but only 16 (21%) in both cycles. Surprisingly, 18 (48%) of the HC group also met this 30% threshold for at least one symptom in one cycle, despite being recruited for (and, via the SCID-5, confirmed to have) no distressing/impairing pattern of psychiatric symptoms; however, only 3 HCs (8%) showed this pattern repeatedly. These findings demonstrate the unacceptable reliability of categorical MCAC cutoffs in this sample and highlight the continuous nature of MCAC. Accordingly, we modeled cycle-affect associations as linear outcomes across participants, consistent with prior work showing that categorical MCAC variables, like many psychiatric diagnoses, have poor reliability and specificity. It also aligns with our work showing dimensional variability in MCAC degree and timing [8, 12], even in prospectively-confirmed PMDD samples [2]. As preregistered, we therefore modeled MCAC as a continuous variable, utilizing our sample’s wide range of MCAC severity. Cross-level interactions tested whether person-level factors (e.g., person-mean subunit expression or AUCi) were associated with within-person symptom trajectories across cycle time [71] regardless of recruitment group.

Subunit data: basics

Detailed descriptive statistics for raw and transformed subunit expression can be found in Table S6. ICCs were 0.18 for GABRD, 0.30 for GABRG2, and 0.15 for GABRA5. Low ICCs indicate that most variance occurred at the visit level, not between person. Between-person correlations were significant between person-mean GABRD and GABRG2 (r = 0.26, p < 0.01) and GABRD and GABRA5 (r = 0.92, p < 0.001); there was a nonsignificant association between GABRG2 and GABRA5 (r = 0.17, p = 0.07). Repeated measures correlations were significant between GABRD and GABRG2 (rmcorr = 0.34, p < 0.001), GABRD and GABRA5 (rmcorr = 0.88, p < 0.001), and GABRG2 and GABRA5 (rmcorr = 0.28, p < 0.001). Full correlation matrices are in Table S7. In sum, correlations among subunit expression were generally significant—but particularly strong between GABRD and GABRA5.

GABAAR subunit gene expression as a biological correlate of MCAC

ICCs for symptoms ranged from 0.47–0.73, indicating that 47–73% of variance reflected between-person differences, while 27–53% reflected within-person fluctuations (i.e., that could potentially be explained by the menstrual cycle). Recruitment groups (Patients with SI vs. HC), used to broaden variability in MCAC, did not significantly differ on mean or AUCi gene expression indices, although nonsignificant trends suggest somewhat greater detectability, person-mean levels, and perimenstrual change (absolute value of AUCi) in patients with SI that may be observable in larger samples (Figure S1; Table S9). Across all moderation models, trajectories were best fit with a quadratic luteal phase and rectilinear follicular phase patterns (Tables S10-S12).

GABRG2 expression as a moderator of cyclical symptom trajectories

Discussion

This preregistered study offers the first direct human test of the longstanding hypothesis that MCAC is linked to altered GABAAR subunit gene expression in response to NAS fluctuations. While decades of preclinical studies have implicated altered GABAAR regulation by hormones as a core mechanism for hormone-sensitive mood symptoms [17, 72–76], translation to humans remains limited. Here, we show for the first time that peripheral expression of GABRG2 fluctuates across the cycle and that both mean levels and cyclical dynamics of GABAAR subunit expression are associated with cycle-related changes in psychiatric symptoms. Despite the known efficacy of SSRIs for PMDD [77], we found that SSRI use did not correspond to GABAAR subunit expression at baseline or across the cycle.

Subunit genes and the menstrual cycle

We found that GABAAR subunit expression varied more within individuals across the cycle than between them, with heterogeneity in expression trajectories, suggesting dynamic hormonal regulation. While causality and concordance with central expression remain to be demonstrated, these preliminary findings are consistent with animal studies showing that GABAAR composition shifts with steroid fluctuations, producing downstream changes in affect and behavior [17, 73–75].

GABRA5 and GABRD expression were strongly correlated both within and between individuals. Postmortem studies in suicide victims have shown that these genes tend to be co-upregulated or co-downregulated [78], supporting the potential for coordinated regulation of these subunits across brain and peripheral systems and highlighting the value of examining GABAAR subunit co-expression more broadly.

Results also suggested reduced GABRG2 expression during the high-P4 midluteal phase, consistent with many preclinical studies linking P4 and its metabolite ALLO to γ2 downregulation. This may represent an adaptive mechanism reducing receptor sensitivity to ALLO’s sedating effects ([28, 34, 35, 39, 40]. A prior human study similarly found lower GABRG2 expression in peripheral monocytes during late pregnancy—another high-P4 state—compared to non-pregnant women [72].

Peripheral GABAAR subunit gene expression may be a biological correlate of MCAC

Peripheral GABAAR subunit expression was associated with cycle-related fluctuations in anxiety, irritability, and SI, though not depression. Pending replication, this specificity of findings to higher-arousal symptoms is theoretically consistent with the recent Dimensional Affective Sensitivity to Hormones across the Menstrual Cycle (DASH-MC) framework [1], which posits that luteal phase NAS change sensitivity may trigger high-arousal symptoms confined to the luteal phase [79], whereas lower-arousal symptoms like depression may be more closely linked to later-onset perimenstrual sensitivity to E2 withdrawal [80, 81]. DASH-MC, informed by both experimental studies [14] and trajectory subgrouping studies in patients with prospectively-confirmed PMDD [82], may be a useful lens for understanding the timing and content of symptom correlations in the current study.

Consistent with their co-fluctuation, GABRD and GABRA5 showed similar associations with SI, mirrored in anxiety for GABRA5. Across models, moderate levels and changes in subunit expression appeared protective, whereas individuals with more extreme levels or changes demonstrated greater MCAC.

Higher overall expression in GABRA5 and greater perimenstrual increases in GABRD expression were associated with luteal elevations in anxiety and SI that resolved rapidly with menses onset. Conversely, lower levels and larger perimenstrual reductions in GABRD and GABRA5 expression were associated with increased cyclicity of anxiety and SI. Lower levels or greater decreases in expression of these subunits were linked with a late luteal anxiety onset or SI that often persisted through the follicular phase, failing to resolve until the next luteal phase, aligning with prior work linking GABRD to anxiety in animals [34, 35], and to suicidality in humans [78, 83], and suggesting that expression differences may causally shape perimenstrual affective trajectories. Similarly, these findings converge with evidence that altered α5 subunit expression, particularly in hippocampal circuits, regulates cognition and emotion [84, 85], associating in complex ways with adverse affective and behavioral outcomes [48–50, 53]—including behavioral anxiety across the estrous cycle [86]. Future studies should replicate these results and clarify whether there may be causal relationships between 1) increased GABRA5/GABRD expression and luteally-confined affective symptoms or 2) decreased GABRA5/GABRD expression and perimenstrual- or follicular affective symptoms.

GABRG2 showed the strongest and most consistent associations with symptoms across the cycle, correlating with anxiety, irritability, and SI trajectories. Similar to the results for GABRD and GABRA5, more extreme levels or changes in GABRG2 expression were associated with greater cyclicity of anxiety and SI; cyclical irritability change appeared to be solely linked to perimenstrual increases. Given irritability’s prominence in PMDD [87] and the limitations of animal models for midluteal onset irritability [88], further validation of peripheral correlates like GABRG2 is important. Participants with marked GABRG2 decreases also demonstrated cyclical increases in anxiety and SI (though not irritability), with symptoms again emerging later in the perimenstrual phase and persisting into the follicular phase. These findings echo prior studies linking lower γ2 expression to early life adversity and chronic stress [50, 52, 89]. Of note, the dynamic pattern of GABRG2 regulation in our sample (reduced in the midluteal phase compared to perimenstrual and midfollicular phases) mirrored the timing of suicidal risk observed in hormone-sensitive samples [12]. Given the lack of validated time-varying biomarkers for SI, future work is needed to replicate and extend these findings in causal paradigms.

Cyclical depression change was not significantly associated with either mean expression or perimenstrual change in GABRD, GABRG2, or GABRA5, possibly reflecting the lower arousal profile of depression compared to anxiety and irritability—symptoms that have shown stronger links to ovarian hormone fluctuations in both PMDD [90, 91] and peripartum mood disorders [91]. Our prior work in an experimental perinatal model similarly found that high-arousal symptoms, particularly anger and anxiety, responded rapidly to steroid surges, while depressive symptoms emerged weeks later [91]. These findings suggest that hormone sensitivity may initially manifest through high-arousal dimensions like irritability and anxiety, with depression reflecting downstream or cumulative effects. Greater focus on irritability in PMDD and related models is warranted, given its luteal specifity and functional impact [87, 92].

Limitations and future directions

This translational study has several limitations. It remains uncertain whether peripheral GABAAR subunit expression reflects central receptor dynamics, though peripheral expression has been linked to symptoms in migraine [93], Tourette Syndrome [94], and depression [95]. Our spline-based model with a knot at menses captured cyclical inflections, but generalized additive modeling could offer complementary insights. While our preregistered approach focused on three subunits and four symptoms to reduce false positives, exploratory analyses may reveal additional candidate genes or symptoms. We were unable to reliably measure α4, a key subunit in NAS sensitivity [44, 45]. Additionally, observational HCs were compared to placebo-phase data from patients. While no placebo effects on GABAAR expression are expected, this design choice limits interpretability. Finally, unmeasured factors such as stress or trauma—which can alter expression of γ2 and δ subunits [96]— should be addressed in future studies.

Conclusions

This study is the first to test whether peripheral expression of GABAAR subunit genes varies across the menstrual cycle and relates to hormone-sensitive mood symptoms. In a naturally-cycling transdiagnostic sample of female psychiatric outpatients with suicidality alongside HCs, we found that GABRG2 expression fluctuated across the cycle and that interindividual differences in cyclicity of GABRD, GABRA5, and GABRG2 were linked to menstrual cycle changes in anxiety, irritability, and SI. Broadly, larger absolute perimenstrual levels and changes correlated with greater MCAC, highlighting the possibility that lower reactivity of subunit expression to hormone fluctuations could be adaptive. While these findings require replication and cannot be assumed to reflect central processes, they offer preliminary support that peripheral GABAAR gene expression could represent a potential mechanistic correlate of MCAC.
