Green synthesis, characterization, evaluation of anticancer and antioxidant potentials of gold and copper oxide nanoparticles using jujube fruit extract
Authors: Hatice Demir Becerekli, Filiz Akbaş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetallic nanoparticles synthesized via plant extracts under green chemistry principles exhibit unique physicochemical and biological properties. In this study, for the first time, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) and copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO NPs) were synthesized using Ziziphus jujuba Mill. fruit extract as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent. The biosynthesized nanoparticles were comprehensively characterized by UV–Vis, SEM–EDX, TEM, FTIR, XRD, and DLS analyses, confirming their nanoscale size, crystalline structure, and surface functionalization by plant-derived biomolecules. The antioxidant potential of the nanoparticles was evaluated using DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assays, revealing effective free radical scavenging activity, with AuNPs exhibiting comparatively higher antioxidant performance. Cytotoxicity studies performed on A549, MDA-MB-231, and SH-SY5Y cancer cell lines demonstrated a significant, concentration-dependent reduction in cell viability, while relatively lower toxicity was observed in healthy L929 fibroblast cells. Notably, CuO NPs showed stronger cytotoxic effects compared to AuNPs, highlighting differences in their biological behavior. This study emphasizes the novelty of jujube fruit extract-mediated synthesis of both AuNPs and CuO NPs and demonstrates their promising antioxidant and anticancer potential, underscoring the role of plant-derived phytochemicals in the development of sustainable nanomaterials for biomedical applications.Keywords: Green synthesis, Jujube, AuNP, CuO NPs, Characterization, Antioxidant, AnticancerSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Cancer, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Nanotechnology can be defined as a discipline that conducts research on the synthesis of nanoparticles with various sizes, shapes, optical, electrical, biological, magnetic, thermal, mechanical and chemical compositions and their effective use in human benefit, environmental and technological applications. Particles 100 nm or smaller are called nanoparticles (NPs). Nanoparticles have different properties than larger particles of the same composition1,2. Their large surface area allows them to undergo diverse reactions and, therefore, enables their use in new applications. In addition to their large surface area, NPs are also highly resistant to high temperatures, making them used in many fields, including electronics, medicine, agriculture, pharmacy, and healthcare. Their distinctive chemical, physical, and biological properties have attracted global attention. The properties of NPs are important for antimicrobial, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, surfactant, drug delivery, and pharmacological applications, depending on the synthesis methods used. However, physical and chemical synthesis methods are costly and can lead to the formation of toxic byproducts3–5.

Environmentally friendly approaches are becoming increasingly important in nanotechnology. Green synthesis is a more cost-effective and environmentally friendly method that eliminates the use of toxic chemicals. Therefore, it is emerging as an alternative to chemical and physical methods. Green or biological synthesis can utilize biological organisms such as fungi, bacteria, yeast, molds, and algae, as well as plants and their products6. Plant extracts, in particular, provide a more advantageous synthesis environment compared to microorganisms due to the biologically active compounds they contain. Molecules and pigments found in plants or microorganisms, such as proteins, enzymes, phenolic compounds, amines, alkaloids, are used as reducing agents in the synthesis of NPs7.

Recent mechanistic studies have demonstrated that phytochemicals such as phenolic acids, flavonoids, and other redox-active biomolecules present in plant extracts play a dual role in green nanoparticle synthesis by reducing metal ions and simultaneously stabilizing the formed nanoparticles through surface capping interactions. These biomolecules bind to the nanoparticle surface via functional groups such as hydroxyl, carbonyl, and amine groups, thereby preventing agglomeration and controlling particle growth8.

Metal and metal oxide nanoparticles are intensely studied in multidisciplinary fields. Such as nanotechnology, materials science, electronics, biomedicine, energy systems, environmental engineering, catalysis and biosensor technologies due to their high surface area, unique optical properties and chemical reactivity9,10. Metal nanoparticles include silver (AgNPs), gold (AuNPs), platinum (PtNPs), palladium (PdNPs), copperoxide (CuO NPs), zinc (ZnNPs), selenium (SeNPs), and Fe-based nanoparticles. These nanoparticles are synthesized by a wide variety of approaches, including photochemical methods, electrochemical techniques, and chemical reduction processes. Each synthesis method has a direct impact on the morphology, size, distribution, and surface functionality of the nanoparticles11,12.

Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) are widely investigated in various fields such as materials science and medicine due to their non-toxicity, controlled size and shape, high surface reactivity, amenability to surface modification, and superior optical properties. The properties of these particles can be tuned by controlling their size, shape, and surface modifications through synthetic methods13,14.

Compared to silver nanoparticles, which often exhibit strong antimicrobial activity but raise concerns regarding cytotoxicity, AuNPs are generally considered more suitable for biomedical applications due to their lower toxicity and ease of surface functionalization12,17.

Copper (Cu) is an easily accessible metal and one of the essential trace elements for most living organisms, playing a critical role in cellular energy production15,16. CuO NPs with different morphologies can be synthesized with low-cost methods using Cu2O, a p-type semiconductor17–19. In recent years, CuO NPs have come to the fore as an alternative to noble metals such as silver, gold and platinum due to their low production costs, high surface/volume ratios, strong anticancer, antioxidant, antimicrobial properties and especially strong catalytic performance20. They also exhibit higher redox activity compared to materials such as ZnO and Fe3O4 nanoparticles. While ZnO nanoparticles are known for their photocatalytic and antibacterial properties, CuO nanoparticles generally show enhanced biological activity due to their ability to generate reactive oxygen species and interact with cellular components. These properties make AuNPs and CuO NPs complementary nanomaterials for investigating antioxidant and anticancer performance21. Although metal nanoparticles have shown promising antioxidant and cytotoxic responses, their long-term stability, environmental fate, and overall sustainability—especially for CuO NPs—should be carefully evaluated. Over time, metal oxide nanoparticles can aggregate, undergo surface modifications, and/or release ions; furthermore, interactions with natural organic matter and biomolecules in aqueous or biological environments can further alter their surface chemistry and stability, potentially affecting both bioactivity and environmental behavior. Therefore, scalability optimization, life cycle assessment, and ecotoxicological evaluation should be considered to develop metal nanomaterials responsibly22,23.

Jujube (Ziziphus jujuba) is an important species in the Rhamnaceae family, noted for its high nutritional and pharmacological value. 33 different fatty acids have been identified in jujube’s dried fruit. It is rich in vitamin C and also contains vitamins A and B, as well as essential minerals such as zinc, magnesium, potassium, and phosphorus. It is also rich in sugars such as glucose, fructose, sucrose, and rhamnose, as well as organic acids such as malic, citric, and succinic acid, and various amino acids. Its high level of phenolic compounds makes it a powerful antioxidant; these compounds protect against cellular damage caused by oxidative stress24,25. Additionally, ursolic and oleic acids found in jujube have been experimentally proven to exhibit anticancer effects in lung, cervical (HeLa), and hepatocellular carcinoma cells26.

Although numerous plant extracts have been employed for the green synthesis of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles, the scientific potential of jujube (Ziziphus jujuba Mill.) fruit extract remains largely unexplored in this context. Jujube fruit is known to be rich in phenolic acids, flavonoids, polysaccharides, and other bioactive compounds with strong reducing and antioxidant properties27–29. Unlike many commonly used leaf or peel extracts, the fruit matrix of jujube provides a complex and abundant phytochemical profile that can simultaneously facilitate nanoparticle reduction, stabilization, and surface functionalization. Despite these advantageous characteristics, no systematic study has yet reported the green synthesis of both gold nanoparticles and copper oxide nanoparticles using jujube fruit extract. This gap in the literature forms the basis of the present study.

In this study, JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs were synthesized using jujube fruit extract and characterized using UV-Vis, DLS, TEM, XRD, SEM-EDX, and FTIR techniques. The antioxidant and cytotoxic properties of JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs were investigated for their potential use in the medical field.

Results and discussion

UV–visible spectroscopy

The position and broadening of absorption bands in UV-Vis spectra can provide indirect information on size distribution and morphology, as demonstrated in comparative mechanistic studies of green-synthesized nanomaterials30. The UV-visible spectra of the synthesized JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs are shown in Fig. 1. When JFE-Au NPs were examined, a characteristic but relatively broad surface plasmon resonance (SPR) band centered around 650 nm was observed. This broad peak range indicates the agglomeration formation of gold nanoparticles. The surface plasmon resonance band observed at 650 nm suggests the formation of anisotropic gold nanostructures and/or strong interparticle coupling due to aggregation. This agglomeration is a characteristic surface plasmon resonance (SPR) band of gold nanoparticles and is a clear indication that the nanoparticles have been successfully synthesized31. On the other hand, when the data in the study are examined in Fig. 1, it is reported that the UV-vis spectra of CuO NPs synthesized with JFE extract have two peaks, one for copper oxide at 265 nm and the other for copper oxide at 359 nm, as reported by both Rabiee et al32. and Badawy et al.33. In contrast to these data in the literature, Yin et al34. obtained three absorption peaks at 260, 340 and 630 nm when they took UV-vis spectrum measurements of CuO nanocrystals. They stated that the peaks observed at 260 and 340 nm could be due to the band-to-band transition of the Cu2O nanostructure, and the peak obtained at 630 nm was attributed to the band gap transition of the CuO nanostructured material35. The absence of this peak in the extract confirms the successful synthesis of the nanoparticles.

UV-Vis Spectra of JFE extract, JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs.

In UV–visible spectroscopy, not only the presence but also the position and width of absorption bands can provide indirect insights into nanoparticle size distribution, morphology, and surface environment. In particular, slight shifts in the SPR band position and peak broadening are often associated with variations in particle size, polydispersity, shape anisotropy, and surface capping effects. Comparative studies have shown that changes in nanoparticle morphology, such as the formation of spherical or polyhedral structures, can influence the optical response observed in UV–Vis spectra. Therefore, UV–Vis spectral features are commonly interpreted in conjunction with complementary techniques such as TEM and DLS rather than as standalone indicators of particle size30. In the present study, the observed UV–Vis characteristics are consistent with the nanoparticle sizes and morphologies determined by TEM and the hydrodynamic diameters obtained from DLS analysis, further supporting the successful formation and stabilization of JFE-mediated nanoparticles.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

FTIR serves as an evaluation technique for nanoparticle production and provides information about the spectrum and vibrational patterns of the molecules present; this allows for the identification of useful and potential phytochemical components affecting the reduction and stability of the extract and NPs36. The FTIR spectra of JFE, JFE-Au, and JFE-CuO NPs are shown in Fig. 2. Examining the figure, characteristic absorption bands are seen for JFE at various wavelengths. The broad peak around 3200–3300 cm⁻¹ is attributed to O-H stretching vibrations. This broad peak seen in the figure represents the hydroxyl groups of polyphenols, polysaccharides, and possibly adsorbed water molecules in the jujube fruit present in the extract. Peaks in the range of 2900–3000 cm⁻¹ show aliphatic C-H stretching vibrations and represent the methyl and methylene groups of organic compounds in the jujube fruit. These compounds protect the structure of nanoparticles and prevent agglomeration37. The prominent peaks around 1600–1750 cm⁻¹ represent C = O stretching vibrations of the amide I and/or carboxylic acid groups of the jujube plant, possibly proteins. Peaks in the 1400–1450 cm⁻¹ region can be attributed to symmetrical stretching or C-H bending vibrations of the carboxylate groups (-COO⁻) present in the extract. Peaks between 1000 and 1250 cm⁻¹ indicate C-O-C and C-OH stretching vibrations and confirm the presence of polysaccharides. Peaks between 500 and 850 cm⁻¹ are significant and may originate from metal-oxygen bonds and out-of-plane bending of aromatic rings.

FTIR spectrum of JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs.

Peaks in the range of 500–600 cm⁻¹ show the characteristic vibrations of Cu-O bonds, confirming the synthesis of JFE-CuO NPs38. Previous studies have shown that green-synthesized CuONPs possess various reactive functional groups and therefore have sufficient capacity to be used in agriculture against phytopathogens39,40. FTIR study confirmed that green-synthesized CuONPs exhibit potential functional groups and can effectively disrupt pathogenic growth.

Furthermore, bands observed in the 500–750 cm− 1 region may be associated with metal–ligand interactions at the nano–bio interface and thus support the presence of an extract-derived organic coating on the AuNP surface. These features are consistent with interactions between the gold surface and oxygen- and/or nitrogen-containing functional groups (hydroxyl, carbonyl, and amine moieties) originating from the corona, which can contribute to colloidal stabilization39. As a result, the FTIR results demonstrate that specific biomolecules—particularly phenolic compounds, flavonoids, proteins, and polysaccharides—play a crucial role in both the reduction and stabilization of JFE-mediated nanoparticles. Such biomolecule-driven capping mechanisms have been widely reported in recent mechanistic studies on green-synthesized nanoparticles, where functional groups containing hydroxyl, carbonyl, and amine moieties govern nanoparticle surface chemistry and long-term stability41,42.

Although the same jujube fruit extract was used for the synthesis of both nanoparticle systems, the FTIR spectrum of JFE-AuNPs exhibited fewer characteristic bands compared to that of JFE-CuO NPs. This difference can be attributed to the distinct surface chemistry of metallic gold and copper oxide nanoparticles. Gold nanoparticles are chemically inert and interact selectively with specific functional groups, resulting in fewer detectable surface-bound biomolecules. In contrast, CuO nanoparticles possess a highly reactive oxide surface capable of forming multiple interactions with phenolic, carboxylic, and hydroxyl-containing compounds present in the extract. Consequently, a broader range of organic functional groups is adsorbed onto the CuO NP surface, leading to a higher number of FTIR absorption bands.

Transmission electron microscope analysis (TEM)

Figure 3 presents the TEM micrographs of JFE-Au NPs and JFE-CuO NPs. As shown in Fig. 3a–b, the gold nanoparticles are predominantly spherical and are, overall, fairly uniformly dispersed, although minor localized agglomeration is observed. The relatively bright/low-contrast regions surrounding the particles are attributable to extract-derived biomolecules (polysaccharides, flavonoids, and proteins), indicating the presence of an organic coating/corona. These biomolecular species are inferred to contribute to nanoparticle formation by acting as reducing agents during synthesis and by providing steric/electrostatic stabilization that mitigates particle–particle aggregation. The electron-dense, dark contrasts correspond to the metallic Au cores, confirming the formation of well-crystallized gold nanoparticles with a comparatively narrow size distribution. Based on TEM measurements, the mean particle size of JFE-Au NPs was determined to be 78.74 ± 6.40 nm.

TEM images of JFE-AuNPs (a, b) and JFE-CuO NPs (c, d) at different scales.

Figure 3c–d demonstrates that the JFE-CuO NPs exhibit a markedly heterogeneous morphology. The copper oxide nanoparticles are largely non-spherical, irregular in shape, and span a wider size range, with some particles forming larger agglomerates while others remain more isolated. The light gray background/halo-like regions are consistent with an organic matrix composed of JFE-derived biomolecules (polyphenols, carbohydrates, and proteins), supporting the formation of a nano–bio interface. The darker regions represent CuO cores, and the observation of lattice fringes/crystal planes in selected particles provides additional evidence of crystallinity, consistent with the monoclinic CuO phase. Overall, these observations confirm the successful synthesis of JFE-CuO NPs, with an average TEM-derived particle size of 159.14 ± 22.53 nm.

X-Ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

XRD is primarily used to determine the phases of JFE-Au NPs and JFE-CuO NPs and to characterize their crystal structure. Figure 4 shows the XRD pattern of JFE-Au NPs biosynthesized using aqueous JFE solution. A series of Bragg peaks with 2θ values ​​of 38.22˚, 44.32˚, 64.62˚, 77.56˚ and 81.72˚ were detected; these peaks correspond to lattice planes (111), (200), (220), (311) and (222), respectively, and can be indexed to face-centered cubic structures for gold. The obtained data are in compliance with Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) file number 04–0784. The broadening of the Bragg peaks indicates that the nanoparticles were successfully synthesized. The sharp peaks indicate the presence of some biological-organic compounds in the nanoparticle structure during nanoparticle synthesis43,44. The average crystalline diameter of JFE-Au NPs was calculated from the XRD pattern according to the line width of the peak via the Debye-Scherrer Eq. (111) using the formula D = Kλ/(β cosθ). Here, D is the average crystalline size, K is the constant value (1.504), λ is the X-ray wavelength value (0.90), β is half the FWHM value of the peak with maximum height, and θ is the Bragg angle of the high peak (JFE-Au NPs = 0.21742). As a result of the calculation, the particle size of JFE-Au NPs was calculated to be approximately 40.42 nm.

XRD patterns of JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs.

Figure 4 shows the XRD pattern of CuO NPs biosynthesized using a JFE aqueous solution. A series of Bragg peaks with 2θ values ​​of 36.34˚, 43.22˚, 61.22˚ and 73.44˚ were detected; these peaks correspond to lattice planes (111), (200), (220) and (311), respectively. The obtained data are in compliance with Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) file number 05–0661. The broadening of the Bragg peaks indicates that JFE-CuO NPs were successfully synthesized. The sharp peaks indicate the presence of some biological-organic compounds in the nanoparticle structure during nanoparticle synthesis. The microstructural crystallite size of JFE-CuO NPs was calculated using the Debye-Scherrer formula D = Kλ/(β cosθ). Here, D is the crystallite size, λ is the wavelength of the incident Cu-Kα X-rays, and β is the half-maximum width (in radians) of the selected peak (111) at the diffraction angle θ. The calculation results show that the average crystallite size of JFE-CuO NPs is close to 13.61 nm45,46.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-Ray spectroscopy with energy dispersive (EDX)

SEM and EDX images of JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs are seen in Fig. 5. The gold and copper percentages presented in the EDX analysis were determined from a small portion of the sample, assuming a uniform composition throughout the sample. Figure 5a shows that JFE-AuNPs are generally spherical and polygonal in shape. Under high magnification 2 , information is obtained regarding particle size, the overall distribution of nanoparticles, and the surface coating density. SEM analysis reveals that the morphological features of JFE-AuNPs produced through green synthesis are nanometric. The EDX results of JFE-AuNPs (Fig. 5a) successfully reveal the presence of gold in the synthesized sample. The presence of a sharp peak around 2.4 keV indicates a high percentage of gold in the composition. Furthermore, the presence of gold, carbon, and oxygen elements is clearly identified. The presence of oxygen and carbon can be attributed to the components of the extract adsorbed on JFE-AuNPs. The results obtained in our study are similar to those reported by some researchers using green synthesized AuNPs from different plant sources47. Quantitative EDX analysis revealed that JFE-AuNPs consist predominantly of carbon (65.08 at%), oxygen (21.66 at%), and gold (12.26 at%). The relatively high carbon and oxygen content further supports the effective surface functionalization of gold nanoparticles by plant-derived biomolecules. Similar elemental compositions have been reported for green-synthesized gold nanoparticles using various plant extracts, confirming the consistency of the present results with previous studies.

SEM images, EDX elemental analysis and compositions of the synthesized JFE-AuNPs (a) and JFE-CuO NPs (b).

The SEM image in Fig. 5b shows that the JFE-CuO NPs are generally spherical and, in some cases, rod-like in their morphology. An examination of the shape reveals that some of the particles are singly dispersed, but some regions exhibit a pronounced tendency toward aggregation and agglomeration. Figure 5b shows that the JFE-CuO NPs obtained by green synthesis were successfully synthesized and their surface morphology was suitable for functional applications. Figure 5b shows close agreement with the results of the green synthesized CuONPs previously reported by Pugazhendhi et al.48.

EDX spectra of JFE-CuO NPs (Fig. 5b) show the presence of copper metal, thus confirming metal impregnation. The EDX results of JFE-CuO NPs (Fig. 5b) successfully reveal the presence of copper in the synthesized sample. The presence of a sharp peak around 1.00 keV indicates a high copper content in the composition. Furthermore, the presence of copper, carbon, and oxygen elements was clearly determined. The presence of oxygen and carbon can be attributed to the adsorbed components of the extract on JFE-CuO NPs. Quantitative EDX analysis further revealed that JFE-CuO NPs are mainly composed of copper and oxygen, with atomic percentages of approximately Cu: 8.43 at% and O: 53.44 at%, while carbon (38.13 at%) originates from surface-bound phytochemicals. These results confirm the formation of copper oxide nanoparticles and demonstrate effective organic capping by jujube-derived biomolecules.

Dynamic light scattering method (DLS) analysis

The size distribution, DLS analysis, and zeta potentials of JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs synthesized from JFE are shown in Fig. 6. The average particle sizes of the obtained JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs were measured as 78.8 ± 3.85 nm and 153 ± 4.58 nm, respectively. These values were determined to be different or even larger than those observed by electron microscopy. The dynamic light scattering (DLS) technique measures the hydrodynamic diameter, which includes not only the core size of the nanoparticles but also the surrounding biomolecules and solvent layer. Therefore, the particle sizes determined by DLS may appear larger than the actual core size. It has been stated in reports that nanoparticles synthesized in this manner may exhibit better biological activity due to their higher surface-to-volume ratio interactions49–51. In addition, recent studies emphasize that the formation of a nanoparticle corona composed of surface-adsorbed biomolecules plays a crucial role in increasing the hydrodynamic diameter measured by DLS. This biomolecular corona, together with the surrounding solvation layer, contributes to the dynamic behavior of nanoparticles in solution and explains the systematic size discrepancy observed between DLS and TEM measurements, particularly for green-synthesized nanoparticles52,53.

Zeta potential of JFE-Au NPs and JFE-CuO NPs

The zeta potential and electrical charge value of nanoparticles synthesized from the JFE plant, JFE-Au NPs and JFE-CuO NPs, were determined (Fig. 7). Very similar zeta potential values ​​were detected by reading JFE-Au NPs and JFE-CuO NPs with 3 repeats. This result explains the high stability of green synthesized JFE-Au NPs and JFE-CuO NPs and indicates the presence of a electrostatic repulsive force that prevents the aggregation of nanoparticles.

Antioxidant activity

The DPPH and ABTS free radical scavenging activities of green-synthesized JFE-CuO NPs and JFE-AuNPs are given in Fig. 8. In both antioxidant activity methods, AuNP showed higher activity than CuO NPs, but when compared to ascorbic acid, BHA and BHT used as standards, it was observed that the antioxidant activities of the nanoparticles were lower. When comparing the methods, CuO NPs and AuNPs were observed to have higher activity in the ABTS method, with an IC50 value of 83.48 ± 1.66 µg/mL, and 70.32 ± 2.73 µg/mL, respectively. In the DPPH assay, JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs exhibited IC₅₀ values of 138.96 ± 1.89 µg/mL and 161.21 ± 2.66 µg/mL, respectively, indicating lower radical scavenging efficiency compared to the ABTS method. The IC50 values of both JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs are now explicitly reported in the Results section for both DPPH and ABTS assays. In addition, the observed higher antioxidant activity in the ABTS assay has been mechanistically explained by considering the broader reactivity of the ABTS•+ radical toward hydrophilic and lipophilic antioxidants and its higher sensitivity to electron transfer mechanisms involving surface-bound phytochemicals54.

Consistent with previous findings, studies have determined the DPPH activity IC50 of CuO NPs derived from Impatiens chinensis to be 95.24 µg/mL and 85.23 µg/mL for ABTS; and the DPPH activity IC50 of AuNPs derived from Mangifera indica to be 256 µg/ml and 62 µg/ml for ABTS55,56. Examples of bioactive substances found in plant extracts such as flavonoids, polyphenols, terpenoids, and alkaloids play a vital role in nanoparticle stabilization. Similarly, Ali et al. reported notable DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities for green-synthesized metal and metal oxide nanoparticles, which were attributed to the presence of phenolic and flavonoid compounds on the nanoparticle surface. The antioxidant performance observed in the present study is consistent with these findings, further confirming the contribution of plant-derived biomolecules to free radical scavenging efficiency57,58. The IC50 values ​​reported for AuNPs and CuO NPs obtained via green synthesis generally vary depending on the phytochemical composition of the extract and the synthesis conditions. Based on this information, and referencing the IC50 values ​​obtained in our study, we can say that jujube fruit extract provides effective surface functionalization and exhibits similar or better antioxidant properties compared to nanomaterials obtained in the studies of other researchers. In this case, the antioxidant activity of JFE-mediated nanoparticles can be explained by the phenolic acid-rich composition of jujube fruit extract. This is because phenolic acids and flavonoids contribute to radical scavenging through hydrogen atom transfer and single electron transfer mechanisms facilitated by hydroxyl (-OH) functional groups. It has been shown that nanoparticles synthesized using phenolic-rich extracts exhibit enhanced antioxidant activity by increasing redox accessibility and reaction kinetics due to synergistic interactions between the inorganic core and surface-bound phytochemicals. This mechanistic behavior is consistent with the antioxidant performance observed in the present study64–66.

If the plant extract is richer in flavonoids and phenolic molecules, NPs show strong scavenging effect67. The antioxidant effect of these substances is enhanced by the O–H functional group. The unbound O–H group in the aromatic ring constitutes the antioxidant capacity68. The hydrogen from the O–H group in the aromatic ring was transferred to the free radical, stabilizing the free radical molecule69. These compounds reduce the oxidation state of nanoparticles and act as antioxidants70,71. Recently, the ability of nanoparticles to scavenge free radicals has attracted great attention and provides important implications for a variety of potential therapeutic and health-promoting applications72,73.

Cytotoxic effects of JFE-CuO and JFE-Au nanoparticles

To evaluate the cytotoxic effects of CuO and Au nanoparticles, A549 human lung cancer cell line, MDA-MB-231 human breast cancer cell line, SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cell line were used as malignant cell lines. L929 mouse fibroblast cell line was preferred as healthy cell. When the morphological images of A549 cells to which CuO and Au nanoparticles were applied were examined, it was determined that the intactness of the cells decreased compared to the control group and their morphology began to change (Figs. 9 and 10).

In Fig. 9a, it is observed in the microscope images that the cells in the control group maintained their healthy morphology, while in the JFE-Au NPs applied group, cell morphology was deteriorated and cell density decreased. This shows that JFE-Au NPs have cytotoxic effects on cells. The % cell viability rates of A549, MDA-MB-231, SH-SY5Y and L929 cells treated with 1000 µg/mL Au nanoparticles were calculated as 32.57 ± 8.30 (p < 0.0001), 16.96 ± 0.77 (p < 0.0001), 14.98 ± 1.48 (p < 0.0001) and 25.83 ± 0.20 (p < 0.0001), respectively.

Figure 9c presents the MTT analysis results showing the effects of JFE-Au NPs application at different concentrations (1, 10, 100, 1000 µg/mL) on cell viability in L929, A549, MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cell lines. Statistically significant decreases in cell viability occurred at increasing nanoparticle concentrations (ns: not significant, * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001).

The cytotoxicity of NPs varies depending on parameters such as particle size, surface area and surface reactivity74. Numerous studies have investigated the cytotoxic effects of Au nanoparticles on various cancer cell lines. While these studies generally demonstrate the biocompatibility of metal NPs, some provide contradictory opinions. Some researchers reported that Au nanoparticles had a cytotoxic effect on cell lines75–78; some researchers reported that they did not have a cytotoxic effect79 and were not biocompatible80. In line with this, Ali et al. demonstrated that green-synthesized metal-based nanoparticles induced concentration-dependent cytotoxicity in various cancer cell lines, primarily through oxidative stress generation and mitochondrial dysfunction. The dose-dependent decrease in cell viability observed in the present study is consistent with these insights81. The results obtained in our study, in accordance with the literature, reveal that JFE-Au NPs exhibit concentration-dependent cytotoxic effects and cause a significant decrease in viability, especially in cancer cell lines.

To statistically substantiate the observed preferential cytotoxicity, direct comparisons were performed between cancer cell lines (A549, MDA-MB-231, and SH-SY5Y) and the L929 healthy fibroblast cell line at corresponding nanoparticle concentrations. Statistical analysis revealed that the reduction in cell viability induced by both JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs was significantly greater in cancer cells than in L929 cells (p < 0.05–0.0001, depending on concentration and cell line). These results suggest that, although some cytotoxicity was observed in normal fibroblast cells, the nanoparticles exhibit statistically significant preferential cytotoxicity toward cancer cells, thereby supporting their potential application as anticancer agents.

In Fig. 10a, it is observed in the microscope images that the cells in the control group maintained their healthy morphology, while in the JFE-CuO NPs applied group, the number of cells decreased and the cellular morphology deteriorated. This shows that nanoparticles have cytotoxic effects on cells. The % cell viability rates of A549, MDA-MB-231, SH-SY5Y and L929 cells treated with 1000 µg/mL JFE-CuO NPs were calculated as 13.71 ± 0.93 (p < 0.0001), 11.98 ± 0.42 (p < 0.0001), 47.31 ± 0.93 (p < 0.0001) and 7.58 ± 0.57 (p < 0.0001), respectively.

Figure 10c shows the MTT analysis results showing the effects of JFE-CuO NPs application at different concentrations (1, 10, 100, 1000 µg/mL) on cell viability in L929, A549, MDA-MB-231 and SH-SY5Y cell lines. Compared to the control group, a statistically significant decrease in cell viability was observed as the nanoparticle concentration increased (ns: not significant, * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001). The results reveal that JFE-CuO NPs exhibit a concentration-dependent cytotoxic effect and cause a significant decrease in viability in cancer cell lines.

Recently, the use of nanomaterials in cancer treatment has begun to replace chemotherapeutic therapy. In general, CuO NPs have anticancer effects through various signaling pathways and exhibit excellent synergistic effects when used alone or in combination with antibiotics, thus offering a significant alternative in the treatment of some types of cancer. However, little research has been conducted on the anticancer activities of CuO NPs and their oxides. Depending on the method and source of Cu/CuO NP synthesis and the cell line used, the mode of action of CuO NPs may vary82–84. The results obtained in our study, in accordance with the literature, reveal that JFE-CuO NPs exhibit a concentration-dependent cytotoxic effect and cause a significant decrease in viability in cancer cell lines.

Despite the pronounced anticancer activity observed for both nanoparticle systems, particularly JFE-CuO NPs, the reduction in cell viability detected in healthy L929 fibroblast cells highlights an important biosafety consideration. This partial loss of viability in non-malignant cells may be associated with non-selective oxidative stress mechanisms, such as excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, which can affect normal cells when cellular antioxidant defense systems are overwhelmed81. Future studies focusing on dose refinement, surface functionalization, and targeted delivery strategies will be essential to improve the biosafety profile of these nanomaterials for biomedical applications.

The graph shown in Fig. 11 reveals that cell viability analyses performed at a concentration of 1000 µg/mL show that AuNP and CuONP exhibit different cytotoxic profiles depending on the cell type. CuONP application caused a significant loss of viability in A549, MDA-MB-231, and L929 cells, and the fact that the viability rate in healthy fibroblast cells, especially L929, fell below 10% suggests that CuONPs may be disadvantageous in terms of selectivity. In contrast, the high viability rate observed against CuONPs in SH-SY5Y cells (47.31 ± 0.93%) suggests that this cell line has developed a more resistant biological response to copper oxide nanoparticles. AuNPs, on the other hand, significantly reduced cell viability in all cell lines, but showed a higher viability rate compared to CuONPs, especially in L929 cells. This situation reveals that AuNPs have a relatively limited cytotoxic effect on healthy cells and offer a more advantageous profile in terms of biocompatibility. The findings show that AuNPs produced by green synthesis may be effective against cancer cells while exhibiting more protective behavior in healthy cells, while CuONPs are potent cytotoxic agents with limited cell selectivity.

Conclusion

In this study, for the first time, JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs were successfully synthesized using jujube fruit extract (JFE) via an environmentally friendly green synthesis approach and comprehensively characterized. UV–Vis spectroscopy confirmed successful nanoparticle formation through the characteristic surface plasmon resonance band of JFE-AuNPs and the distinct absorption features of JFE-CuO NPs. FTIR analyses demonstrated the presence of bioactive phytochemicals such as polyphenols, flavonoids, and polysaccharides on the nanoparticle surfaces, indicating their role in reduction and stabilization. Morphological and structural analyses revealed spherical and relatively homogeneous JFE-AuNPs, whereas JFE-CuO NPs exhibited irregular morphology and heterogeneous size distribution, with XRD confirming their respective crystalline structures. DLS measurements further indicated larger hydrodynamic sizes due to the presence of surface-bound JFE-derived biomolecules.

Antioxidant activity studies showed that JFE-AuNPs exhibited higher free radical scavenging efficiency than JFE-CuO NPs, while both nanoparticle systems demonstrated promising antioxidant potential. Cytotoxicity assays revealed a concentration-dependent decrease in viability in A549 lung cancer, MDA-MB-231 breast cancer, and SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cell lines. Although reduced viability was also observed in L929 healthy fibroblast cells, the effect was comparatively lower than that in cancer cells, suggesting a degree of selective cytotoxicity.

Overall, these findings indicate that jujube fruit extract serves as an effective natural reducing and stabilizing agent, enabling the synthesis of biologically active nanoparticles with notable antioxidant and anticancer properties. The results highlight the translational potential of JFE-mediated AuNPs and CuO NPs as sustainable nanomaterials for biomedical applications. However, further studies focusing on in vivo validation, long-term biosafety assessment, mechanistic pathways, and dose optimization are essential to advance these nanoparticles toward practical therapeutic use.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents used

All chemicals and reagents used were of analytical grade and purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and Merck. Tetrachloroauric acid (HAuCl4, 99.8%) was used for the synthesis of gold nanoparticles, and copper (II) sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4.5H2O, 99.0%) was used for the synthesis of copper nanoparticles.

Preparation of jujube fruit extract for nanoparticles biosynthesis

The study used jujube (Ziziphus jujuba) fruit as material. The fruit was obtained from a local producer in the Çivril district of Denizli province and was collected from 14-year-old fruit trees (September 2024). The fruits were washed with demineralized water and dried in a cool, dry room away from sunlight. After drying, it was ground in a rotary mill and turned into powder. 20 mg of the powdered jujube was mixed with 200 ml of pure water and extracted at 90 o C for 15–20 min. The prepared extract was filtered with Whatman No: 1. The filtered extract was stored at + 4 o C for use85.

Gold nanoparticles and copper oxide nanoparticles synthesis

A 10 mM solution of tetrachloroauric acid was prepared and reacted with jujube extract at a 1:1 ratio at room temperature. The reaction resulted in a JFE-AuNP solution. A 200 ml solution of 0.5 M copper (II) sulfate pentahydrate (Cu2SO4.5H2O) and 40 ml of JFE were allowed to react at 100 °C for approximately 2 h on a magnetic stirrer. At the end of the specified time, the JFE-Cu ONP solution was obtained (Fig. 12). To determine the formation of nanoparticles, a distinct peak was observed at approximately 530–560 nm for AuNPs and 350–360 nm for CuO NPs by examining the UV-vis spectrum. The synthesized AuNPs and Cu ONPs were centrifuged separately at 6000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate remaining at the bottom was washed three times with pure water and a final wash with ethyl alcohol to remove any impurities. The resulting nanoparticles were dried in a lyophilizer and stored in the dark for use in experimental studies and characterization86,87.

Characterization of JFE-AuNPs ve JFE-CuO NPs

The optical properties and absorption spectra of the synthesized JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs were analyzed using UV-Visible spectroscopy. UV-visible spectra were measured on an Agilent Cary 60 spectrophotometer in the wavelength range of 200–900 nm at 1 nm resolution. To evaluate the morphology, size, and shape of the nanoparticles, TEM images were obtained with a Hitachi HT-7700 instrument in the 40–120 kV range. An XPERT-PRO diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) was used to obtain the XRD pattern, which determines the crystal size, properties, purity, and structure of the biosynthesized NPs. An Agilent Cary 630 FTIR spectrometer was used for functional group analysis of JFE, JFE-AuNPs, and JFE-CuO NPs. SEM-EDX images were obtained using a Zeiss Sigma 300 instrument to determine the size, shape, and chemical composition of JFE-AuNPs and JFE-CuO NPs. Additionally, particle size (DLS) and zeta potential measurements of the nanoparticles were performed using the Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZSP instrument.

Cell culture

The A549 human lung cancer cell line, the MDA-MB-231 human breast cancer cell line, the SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cell line, and the L929 mouse fibroblast cell line obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) were grown in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, L-glutamine, and HEPES in 25 cm2 vials at 5% CO2 and 37 °C. When the cells filled the vials to 80–90%, they were passaged into new 25 cm2 and 75 cm2 vials to increase stocks for use in experiments.

Cytotoxicity test

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h at 37 °C in a CO₂ incubator. JFE-CuO and JFE-Au nanoparticles were applied at concentrations of 1, 10, 100, and 1000 µg/mL for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h.
