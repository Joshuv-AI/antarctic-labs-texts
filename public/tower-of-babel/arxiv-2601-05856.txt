Q ⁡ ( n ) Q(n) is divisible by 4 4
Hugo Møller Nielsen

Abstract
We consider the classical n n -queens problem, which asks how many ways one can place n n mutually non-attacking queens on an n × n n\times n chessboard. We prove that the total number of solutions to the n n -queens problem Q ⁡ ( n ) Q(n) is divisible by 4 whenever n ≥ 6 n\geq 6 .



keywords
n-queens problem, arithmetic properties, divisibility

1
Introduction

An
n
-queens configuration is a placement of
n
mutually non-attacking queens on an
n\times n
chessboard. That is, no
two queens are contained in the same row, column, or diagonal. The
n
-queens problem, as posed by Max Bezzel in
1848
2
, asks: for a given board size
n
, how many distinct
n
-queens configurations exist. We
denote this number by
Q(n)
.

The problem has attracted attention from notable mathematicians including Gauss, Pólya, and Lucas

6
. At first, the values of
Q(n)
were computed by hand, or at least without digital assistance.
These were the values of
Q(n)
for
n\leq 13
, see
1
.
With the advent of computers, computing
Q(n)
for larger
n
became feasible;
the current record being
Q(27)=234907967154122528
, which required an immense amount of time and computational power to
compute
7
.
Today the
n
-queens problem is often introduced at universities in the context of computation, where it
serves as an example problem to be tackled by backtracking, constraint programming, or genetic algorithms,
but theoretical results pertaining to
Q(n)
remain an active area of research
8
;
3
.

Existence of solutions to the
n
-queens problem for
n\geq 4
was shown using a constructive proof
in 1874 by Pauls
5
. The simple result that
Q(n)
is divisible by
2
for all

n\geq 2
has been known for at least as long
9
;
4
,
a short proof of which will be provided in this work. More recently,
the asymptotic behavior of
Q(n)
was characterized and proven in 2021 to be of the form
Q(n)=((1+o(1))ne^{-\alpha})^{n}

by Simkin
8
.

In addition to recalling a short proof that
Q(n)
is even for all
n\geq 2
, we prove the stronger divisibility statement
in Theorem
1
.

Theorem 1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,4,5\}
we have
Q(n)\equiv 0\pmod{4}
.

We prove Theorem
1
in Section
3
. In the next section, Section
2
, we
provide the tools and context necessary for the proof.

Lemma 2
.

There is no
n
-queens solution

that is fixed by one of the reflections
s,rs,r^{2}s
or

Proof.

We have
Q(2)=Q(3)=0

holds true for

so consider now the case
n\geq 5
. Fix any
n
-queens configuration

be a queen with

of which is guaranteed by

and the constraints given in (

being an
n

\displaystyle sQ

\displaystyle=Q\implies(i,n+1-j)\in sQ=Q\implies|Q\cap R_{i}|>1,

(10)

\displaystyle r^{2}sQ

\displaystyle=Q\implies(n+1-i,j)\in r^{2}sQ=Q\implies|Q\cap C_{j}|>1,

(11)

\displaystyle rsQ

\displaystyle=Q\implies(j,i)\in rsQ=Q\implies|Q\cap D_{j+i-1}^{+}|>1,

(12)

\displaystyle r^{3}sQ

\displaystyle=Q\implies(n+1-j,n+1-i)\in r^{3}sQ=Q\implies|Q\cap D_{n+j-i}^{-}|>1,

(13)

each of which is a contradiction according to (

Proposition 3
.

Q(n)
is even for all
n\geq 2
.

Proof.

Fix
n\geq 2
and let
S_{n}
denote the set of all
n
-queens configurations on the
n\times n
board, so that

\lvert S_{n}\rvert=Q(n)
.
By Lemma
2
, no configuration
Q\in S_{n}
is fixed by the reflection
s
, that is,
sQ\neq Q
for all

Q\in S_{n}
. Moreover, for two distinct configurations
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in S_{n}
we have
sQ_{1}\neq sQ_{2}

by injectivity of the map
Q\mapsto sQ
.
Thus
S_{n}
decomposes into disjoint orbits of the form
\{Q,sQ\}
, each containing exactly two distinct
configurations. It follows that
\lvert S_{n}\rvert
is even, and hence
Q(n)
is even.
∎

As per Lemma
2
, an
n
-queens configuration can only possibly be fixed by one of the symmetry
actions
e,r,r^{2},r^{3}
. The identity
e
trivially fixes all
n
-queens configurations, leaving us with the actions

r,r^{2}
and
r^{3}
. If an
n
-queens configuration
Q
is fixed under
r
, then it is fixed under
r^{3}
since

r^{3}Q=r^{2}(rQ)=r^{2}Q=r(rQ)=rQ=Q
, and if
Q
is fixed under
r^{3}
, then it is fixed under
r
since

rQ=r(r^{3}Q)=r^{4}Q=eQ=Q
, hence the set of
n
-queens configurations that are fixed under
r
is the same
set of
n
-queens configurations that are fixed under
r^{3}
.

Thus, we conclude that we can partition
the set of all
Q(n)

n
-queens configurations into those solutions that are fixed under the
r
-symmetry,
F_{r}
,
those solutions that are fixed under the
r^{2}
-symmetry, but not the
r
-symmetry,
F_{r^{2}\setminus r}
, and
those solutions that are only fixed by the identity symmetry operator
e
,
F_{e}
. By this partition, we must have that

\displaystyle Q(n)=|F_{r}|+|F_{r^{2}\setminus r}|+|F_{e}|.

(14)

See Figure
2

for an example in
F_{r}
and
F_{e}
respectively, for the case
n=5
. Now define
C_{x}
as a set of equivalence class representatives under
D_{4}

of the solutions in
F_{x}
for
x\in\{r,r^{2}\setminus r,e\}
.

The final result of this section, Theorem
4
, shows that the cardinality of the set of solutions fixed under
F_{r}
,
F_{r^{2}\setminus r}
and

F_{e}
respectively are divisible by
2,4
and
8
. This result is crucial in the proof of Theorem
1
, but is also of independent
interest.

Theorem 4
.

For
n\geq 2
we have
|F_{r}|=2|C_{r}|
,
|F_{r^{2}\setminus r}|=4|C_{r^{2}\setminus r}|
, and
|F_{e}|=8|C_{e}|
, and hence

\displaystyle Q(n)=2|C_{r}|+4|C_{r^{2}\setminus r}|+8|C_{e}|.

(15)

Proof.

Fix
n\geq 2
. For an
n
-queens configuration
Q
, define its orbit and stabilizer (under the actions of
D_{4}
) by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Orb}(Q)

\displaystyle=\{\,yQ:y\in D_{4}\,\},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Stab}(Q)

\displaystyle=\{\,y\in D_{4}:yQ=Q\,\}.

(17)

First, for each
x\in\{r,\,r^{2}\setminus r,\,e\}
and each
Q\in C_{x}
, we have
\mathrm{Orb}(Q)\subseteq F_{x}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle F_{x}=\{\,yQ:Q\in C_{x},\ y\in D_{4}\,\}.

(18)

Therefore, using the decomposition of
F_{x}
into
D_{4}
-orbits, we obtain

\displaystyle|F_{x}|=\sum_{Q\in C_{x}}|\mathrm{Orb}(Q)|.

(19)

Next, by the definition of the sets
F_{x}
, the stabilizer size is constant on each
F_{x}
(and hence on each
C_{x}
). More precisely:

\displaystyle Q\in F_{r}

\displaystyle\iff rQ=Q

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\ \mathrm{Stab}(Q)=\{e,r,r^{2},r^{3}\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\ |\mathrm{Stab}(Q)|=4,

(20)

\displaystyle Q\in F_{r^{2}\setminus r}

\displaystyle\iff r^{2}Q=Q\text{ but }rQ\neq Q

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\ \mathrm{Stab}(Q)=\{e,r^{2}\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\ |\mathrm{Stab}(Q)|=2,

(21)

\displaystyle Q\in F_{e}

\displaystyle\iff\text{$Q$ is fixed only by $e$}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\ \mathrm{Stab}(Q)=\{e\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\ |\mathrm{Stab}(Q)|=1.

(22)

Combining (
19
) with the orbit–stabilizer theorem yields, for each
x\in\{r,\,r^{2}\setminus r,\,e\}
,

\displaystyle|F_{x}|=\sum_{Q\in C_{x}}|\mathrm{Orb}(Q)|=\sum_{Q\in C_{x}}\frac{|D_{4}|}{|\mathrm{Stab}(Q)|}=\frac{8}{|\mathrm{Stab}(Q)|}\,|C_{x}|,

(23)

where
|\mathrm{Stab}(Q)|
is the constant stabilizer size for configurations in
C_{x}
.

Finally, using (
14
), we obtain

\displaystyle Q(n)=|F_{r}|+|F_{r^{2}\setminus r}|+|F_{e}|=2|C_{r}|+4|C_{r^{2}\setminus r}|+8|C_{e}|.

(24)

∎
