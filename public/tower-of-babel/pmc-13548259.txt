Clinical outcomes and economic burden of bacterial meningitis in Africa: a protocol for systematic review and meta-analysis

Introduction

Bacterial meningitis (BM) remains a major global public health concern, caused by a range of aetiological agents that vary across age groups and geographical regions.1 2 Regionally, BM is one of the most challenging and severe infectious diseases in Africa, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, especially in paediatric populations.3 BM is an acute inflammatory condition of the meninges, characterised by rapid clinical progression, high mortality rates and a significant risk of long-term neurological sequelae.4 Despite substantial advances in antimicrobial therapy and vaccination strategies, the disease remains a major public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income settings.5 Globally, meningitis accounts for hundreds of thousands of deaths annually and contributes substantially to different complications and years of life lost, reflecting both its acute lethality and long-term consequences.6 Due to a combination of factors, these burdens are disproportionately concentrated in developing regions, particularly in Africa.7

The burden of bacterial meningitis is disproportionately distributed across countries worldwide.8 9 Low-income countries are experiencing a markedly higher burden, up to approximately 17 times greater than in high-income countries.10 Africa is among the most affected regions, particularly within the so-called ‘meningitis belt’, a zone extending from Senegal to Ethiopia, where recurrent epidemics and sustained endemic transmission are common.9 The epidemiology of BM in this region is largely driven by Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis and Haemophilus influenzae type b.11 The introduction of conjugate vaccines, particularly the MenAfriVac vaccine targeting N. meningitidis serogroup A across the African meningitis belt, has dramatically reduced serogroup A meningococcal epidemics.12 However, outbreaks caused by other meningococcal serogroups (eg, C, W and X) and persistent disease due to S. pneumoniae continue to contribute substantially to the burden of BM across the continent.13

Beyond incidence and prevalence, clinical outcomes of BM in Africa remain poorer than in high-income settings.14 Clinical outcomes include both adverse and favourable measures, such as complications (including death) and recovery.15–18 Among hospitalised patients in Ethiopia, unfavourable outcomes were reported at around 36%, with an overall mortality rate of 22.2%.19 Although this study was conducted in specialty hospitals expected to have adequate resources and skilled healthcare providers, outcomes remained poor, likely due to persistent constraints such as delayed diagnosis and limited access to effective treatment. Survivors often experience a range of complications, including hearing loss, cognitive impairment, seizures, motor deficits and behavioural disorders.20 21 Evidence suggests that up to 25% of survivors in African settings develop long-term sequelae, highlighting the substantial individual and societal burden of the disease.22 These outcomes can be compounded by structural challenges, with complications prolonging hospitalisation and increasing financial burden.

This financial burden emerged from multiple sources, including direct, indirect and intangible components.23 Direct medical costs such as hospitalisation, diagnostics and medications are often compounded by indirect costs (productivity loss, long-term disability care) and direct non-medical costs (transportation, accommodation or house adjustment).24 BM imposes a considerable economic burden globally, with an estimated annual cost of US$20 676 per patient in Mexico25 and a 3-month mean societal cost of €4985.32 per patient in the Netherlands over 3 months.26 In low-resource settings, such costs can be catastrophic, pushing vulnerable households into poverty.

Despite the substantial clinical and economic consequences of BM, synthesised evidence from Africa remains limited. Existing reviews in the region have largely focused on aetiology,2 prevalence and epidemiology27 or clinical outcomes and complications.28 29 However, a review addressing complications in African settings is likely outdated, having been conducted more than a decade ago.22 Furthermore, a global review including African studies did not comprehensively capture economic burden, nor did it systematically synthesise clinical outcomes.9 30 Consequently, there is a paucity of evidence in Africa that simultaneously evaluates both clinical outcomes and economic burden.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA) aim to synthesise the available evidence on the clinical outcomes and economic burden of BM in Africa. Specifically, it will quantitatively synthesise clinical outcomes, including mortality and acute and chronic complications, where appropriate, and systematically review the direct and indirect costs of BM and its complications, while examining methodological variations in cost estimation. The findings will provide robust evidence to inform policy, guide resource allocation, improve clinical practice and identify priorities for future research in the region.

Methods

Protocol

The protocol was not prospectively registered. However, it is being published in a peer-reviewed journal to ensure transparency of the planned review methods.

Design

This protocol for the SRMA was developed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA), and the findings will be reported in line with these recommendations31 . This protocol is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) checklist available in online supplemental file 1. The methodological procedures for this SRMA will include formulating the research question(s), identifying appropriate search terms and databases, conducting a comprehensive search, screening eligible studies, extracting relevant data, synthesising and reporting the findings. Inclusion criteria

The eligibility criteria were informed by the Population, Intervention, Comparator and Outcome framework. As this review includes observational and non-interventional studies, the intervention and comparator components were not applicable. Therefore, the eligibility criteria were defined based on the population and outcome components.

Populations

All studies involving individuals with a suspected or confirmed diagnosis of BM and its associated complications will be eligible for inclusion, irrespective of demographic characteristics, including age and sex. Including all population groups enables a comprehensive assessment of differences in economic and clinical outcomes across groups through subgroup analysis.

This SRMA will include studies conducted in Africa. Eligible study settings will include a range of healthcare facilities, such as public institutions (eg, specialised hospitals, general hospitals and health centres) and private healthcare facilities.

Outcome

Clinical outcomes will include seizure/status epilepticus, hydrocephalus, hyponatraemia, cerebral abscess, hearing loss, cognitive impairment, mortality and length of hospital stay. Economic burden studies will include those reporting direct and/or indirect costs associated with BM and/or its complications. In addition, studies reporting intangible costs will also be included, as these have a significant impact on patients and their families.

Study design

Observational studies (cross-sectional, case–control and cohort)cost-of-illness studies and economic evaluations.

Publication types: peer-reviewed publications and grey literature.

Patient and public involvement

As this study will use secondary data from published literature, no patients or members of the public were involved in the development of this protocol. There are also no plans to involve patients or the public in conducting the review or disseminating the findings, as the results are primarily intended to inform policymakers, researchers and clinical practice rather than individual participants.

Exclusion criteria

The following records will be excluded: economic evaluation studies (eg, cost-effectiveness analyses, cost-utility analysis, cost-minimisation analysis) that do not report annual or per-event costs associated with BM and/or its related complications; studies published before September 2008, as earlier evidence has been addressed in a previous review22; and studies focusing on meningitis of non-bacterial aetiology (eg, fungal meningitis), particularly when occurring concomitantly with tuberculosis or HIV infection. Systematic reviews and/or meta-analyses will be excluded to avoid double-counting of data, as they synthesise evidence from primary studies rather than provide original data relevant to the current research question. Although no language restrictions were applied during the search, due to a lack of experts, only studies conducted in English-language will be included; limited resources for translation and verification of non-English studies. In addition, case reports, case series, conference proceedings, letters, editorials and opinion pieces will be excluded, as these sources may not adhere to rigorous methodological standards and could compromise the validity of the review findings.

Databases and search strategies

Three babilographic databases, including PubMed, Scopus and ERIC, will be searched studies published from September 2008 to March 2026 using predefined search terms and database-specific search syntax. Details of the search terms and database-specific search strategies are provided in online supplemental table 1. Backward citation searching will also be performed by reviewing the reference lists of all included studies to identify additional relevant articles. In addition, manual (hand) search will be conducted using key terms such as “economic burden” or “cost of illness,” “clinical outcomes” or “complications” and meningitis using Google and Google Scholar. These approaches are intended to enhance the comprehensiveness of the search strategy and minimise the risk of publication bias.

Data screening

Study selection will be conducted in three sequential stages: title screening followed by abstract screening and full-text review. After duplication removed using EndNote, first, two review authors (BK and BB) will screen the titles of all retrieved records to identify potentially relevant studies. Second, the same review authors will assess the abstracts of selected records to determine their relevance to the research question. Finally, full-text articles will be evaluated against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to confirm eligibility.

All stages of the screening process will be performed independently by the two reviewers to minimise bias. Any discrepancies between reviewers will be identified and discussed. If consensus cannot be reached, a third review author (BA) will be consulted to resolve disagreements. This rigorous screening approach is essential to ensure the accuracy, consistency and overall quality of the included data, thereby enhancing the validity of the review findings.

Data extraction

Following study selection, data will be extracted independently from all eligible full-text articles by two review authors (BK and BB) using a standardised data extraction form. Any discrepancies between the reviewers will be identified and resolved through discussion; if consensus cannot be reached, a third review author (BA) will adjudicate. Where essential information is missing or unclear, we will review the full article and Online supplemental materials and, if necessary, contact the corresponding author for clarification.

Data quality assessment

Two separate quality appraisal tools will be used for clinical outcome studies and economic burden studies. The methodological quality of all included full-text articles will be assessed.

For clinical outcome studies, quality assessment will be conducted using the Joanna Briggs Institute Quality Assessment tools for cohort, case–control and cross-sectional studies.32–34 These standardised instruments evaluate methodological rigour and risk of bias across key domains, including selection (eg, representativeness of the cohort and ascertainment of exposure), comparability and outcomes (eg, outcome assessment, adequacy and duration of follow-up).

For economic burden studies, a Consensus-based Cost-of-Illness Checklist (2023) will be applied.35 This tool assesses study quality across multiple domains, including study characteristics, methodological approach, cost estimation and analysis, results and reporting standards. All assessments will be conducted systematically to ensure the reliability and validity of the evidence included in this review. Findings from studies with a high risk of bias will be interpreted cautiously, and their potential impact on the findings will be considered during the synthesis.

Qualitative data synthesis

This review will include both qualitative and quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis), where appropriate. A descriptive (qualitative) synthesis will summarise study characteristics, including geographic distribution, publication trends, methodological features, clinical outcomes and the economic burden of BM.

Economic data will be extracted and reported as cost per patient or per event and, where available, standardised to monthly or annual estimates. If the cost year is not reported, the data collection year will be used as a proxy. If the cost is not presented in local currency in the paper, we will convert to local currency before adjusting to the standard cost. The reported cost will be adjusted to 2024 international dollars (I$). All cost adjustments will be performed using the Campbell and Cochrane Economics Methods Group-Evidence for Policy and Practice Information-Centre Cost Converter,36 which applies data from the International Monetary Fund to account for purchasing power parity and inflation, enabling standardised comparisons across countries and time periods.

Meta-analysis

If clinical outcomes have consistent effect sizes across studies, a meta-analysis will be conducted using R V.4.6.0 to generate pooled estimates of factors associated with clinical outcomes (mortality) in BM. Study-specific proportions and corresponding 95% CIs will be calculated and combined using appropriate statistical models. First, a general meta-analysis on clinical outcomes, including death, will be conducted. If studies report more than one effect size from the same sample, the mean of the combined effect sizes will be calculated. In cases of overlapping samples, the study with the largest sample size will be included to avoid duplication of data and potential bias in the pooled estimate. For studies reporting only test statistics, such as χ2, t-test, regression beta and F-test values, these will be converted to the appropriate effect size.

A random-effects model is anticipated to be used to estimate the pooled mean effect size, given the expected heterogeneity across studies, particularly in terms of population characteristics (eg, paediatric, adult and geriatric groups). However, heterogeneity will be assessed using the tau test and the I² statistic, with values <25% indicating low, 25–50% moderate and >50% substantial heterogeneity. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the second step, moderator (subgroup) analyses will be conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Independent effect sizes reported for distinct subgroups within a study will be included as separate entries in the meta-analysis, provided that participants are not double-counted across estimates. Examples include effect sizes reported separately by sex, age group, comorbidity status and timing of presentation to healthcare.

Subgroup analyses will also be performed based on variables that may influence clinical outcomes, including study period, study design and geographic region. Studies conducted during earlier periods may report different outcomes compared with more recent studies due to advances in vaccination, diagnostic approaches, treatment strategies and healthcare delivery. Similarly, differences between prospective and retrospective study designs may influence the reported number of events because retrospective studies often rely on existing records and may capture more severe or documented cases. Regional differences will also be explored, as variations in healthcare infrastructure, access to medications, health-seeking behaviour and timeliness of presentation to healthcare facilities may affect patient clinical outcomes.

To assess whether outlier studies influence the overall estimates, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis will be conducted by sequentially excluding each study and examining its impact on the pooled estimate. Studies will be considered outliers if their effect sizes fall outside the 95% CI of the overall pooled estimate.

Publication bias will be assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test (p<0.05),37 with cautious interpretation in the presence of heterogeneity. Findings from the review and meta-analysis will be presented through tables, figures and narrative summaries aligned with the study objectives.

Discussion

This SRMA will be the first to comprehensively synthesise and characterise the available evidence on both clinical outcomes and the economic burden of BM in Africa. To date, no prior review has specifically addressed the economic burden in this context. The use of broad eligibility criteria is expected to enhance the generalisability of the review findings, although the extent of generalisability will depend on the characteristics of the included studies.

Although a previous systematic review exists, it did not include a meta-analysis and was limited to studies published up to 2008. Therefore, an updated synthesis incorporating more recent evidence, alongside a quantitative meta-analysis, is warranted. By integrating both clinical and economic perspectives, this SRMA aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the burden of BM in Africa.

In addition, this review will identify gaps in the existing literature, including limitations in study design, methodology and reporting quality, assessed against current recommended appraisal tools. BM-related complications impose a substantial burden on patients, families and healthcare systems. Accordingly, this review will characterise clinical outcomes, including complications and mortality, and identify populations most vulnerable to adverse outcomes.

Furthermore, the review will examine the economic implications of BM, considering direct medical costs (eg, medications, hospitalisation, laboratory investigations and medical services), direct non-medical costs (eg, transportation, food and accommodation for patients and caregivers) and indirect costs (eg, productivity losses due to absenteeism and presenteeism).

Where data permit, subgroup analyses will be conducted to explore factors associated with adverse outcomes, such as duration of illness, timing of antibiotic initiation, disease severity, treatment regimens and patient characteristics (eg, age). The review will also distinguish between acute and chronic complications and identify factors associated with favourable outcomes. Overall, the findings are expected to provide important insights into the clinical and economic burden of BM and inform future research, policy and practice in the African context.

Implications of SRMA

The findings of this SRMA are expected to have several important contributions. First, the review will help advance research by identifying key evidence gaps and generating priority research questions, particularly in relation to the economic burden and clinical outcomes of BM, where data remain limited. As such, it will provide a foundational reference for future systematic reviews and primary studies.

Second, the review will offer clinically relevant insights by identifying populations at higher risk of adverse outcomes and those disproportionately affected by the costs of BM management. It will also highlight the full spectrum of cost components, including direct medical, direct non-medical and indirect costs, thereby supporting healthcare professionals in understanding the broader economic impact of the disease and informing more comprehensive patient management strategies.

Third, by synthesising evidence on barriers and facilitators to care reported across studies, the review will inform policy, advocacy and healthcare planning aimed at improving access to timely and effective BM management and reducing financial hardship for patients and their families.

Finally, the review is expected to reveal variations in healthcare access, outcomes and costs across settings, particularly within Africa. This will enable cross-country learning and support the adaptation of effective, context-specific strategies to improve healthcare delivery and reduce the overall burden of BM.

Anticipated strength and limitations

This review will include a meta-analysis, with subgroup and sensitivity analyses conducted to examine the impact of potential variables on outcomes and to assess between-study differences. The economic burden will be standardised and reported in I$ to facilitate comparability across studies. In addition, study quality will be assessed using appropriate critical appraisal tools tailored to both economic and clinical studies, according to study design.

Potential limitations of this SRMA include heterogeneity across study designs, populations and outcome measures, as well as inconsistencies in the reporting of clinical and economic outcomes. In addition, the availability of high-quality data may be limited in certain regions.

For economic evaluations, some studies may omit important cost components, resulting in incomplete estimates of the total economic burden. Variations in the types of costs included across studies may further limit comparability and hinder a comprehensive assessment of the societal burden. Moreover, many studies report costs on a per-event basis, with limited data on long-term or national-level economic impacts.

Finally, the review may be subject to publication bias and the exclusion of non-English studies, which could affect the completeness and generalisability of the findings.

Dissemination plan

The findings of this review will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presentation at scientific conferences. Where appropriate, the results will also be shared with local policymakers to inform decision-making and support alignment with evidence-based priorities. By making these findings accessible, the review is expected to contribute to the scientific community and serve as a valuable resource for clinical practice, policy development, and future research.

Protocol amendment

If protocol amendments become mandatory due to unforeseen or methodological considerations, they will be made transparently. All changes will be clearly documented and justified in the final report, including the rationale for each amendment and the sections of the review affected.
