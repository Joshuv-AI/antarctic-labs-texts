A Cubic Regularization Method for Multiobjective Optimization Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.

Abstract
This work introduces a new cubic regularization method for solving nonconvex unconstrained multiobjective optimization problems. At each iteration of the method, a model associated with the cubic regularization of each component of the objective function is minimized. This model allows approximations for the first- and second-order derivatives, which must satisfy suitable error conditions. One interesting feature of the proposed algorithm is that the regularization parameter of the model and the accuracy of the derivative approximations are jointly adjusted using a nonmonotone line search criterion. Implementations of the method, where derivative information is computed using finite difference strategies, are discussed. From a theoretical perspective, it is shown that, under the assumption that the Hessians of the components of the multiobjective function are globally Lipschitz continuous, the method requires at most 𝒪 ( C ϵ − 3 / 2 ) \mathcal{O}(C\epsilon^{-3/2}) iterations to generate an ϵ \epsilon -approximate Pareto critical, where C C is related to the Lipschitz constant and the inexactness parameters. In particular, if the first- and second-order derivative information is computed using finite differences based solely on function values, the method requires at most 𝒪 ( n 1 − β ϵ − 3 / 2 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{1-\beta}\epsilon^{-3/2}) iterations, corresponding to 𝒪 ⁡ ( m ​ n 3 − β ​ ε − 3 2 ) \mathcal{O}\left(mn^{3-\beta}\varepsilon^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right) function evaluations, where n n is the dimension of the domain of the objective function, m m is the number of objectives, and β ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \beta\in[0,1] is a constant associated with the stepsize used in the finite-difference approximation. We further discuss the global convergence and local convergence rate of the method. Specifically, under the local convexity assumption, we show that the method achieves superlinear convergence when the first derivative is computed exactly, and quadratic convergence when both first- and second-order derivatives are exact. From a practical perspective, numerical experiments are presented to illustrate the behavior of the proposed method and compare it with a multiobjective Newton method.

Introduction

Consider the unconstrained multiobjective optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(x),\\

(1)

where
\allowbreak F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is twice continuously differentiable. Our analysis will be conducted under the following assumptions:
for every
j\in\mathcal{J}:=\{1,\ldots,m\}
,

(A1)

the Hessian of
F_{j}
is
L_{j}
-Lipschitz continuous, i.e.,

\|\nabla^{2}F_{j}(y)-\nabla^{2}F_{j}(z)\|\leq L_{j}\|y-x\|,\quad\forall y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n};

(For convenience, we denote
L:=\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}}L_{j}.
)

(A2)

there exists
F_{j}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
F_{j}(y)\geq F_{j}^{*}
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In multiobjective optimization, the concept of optimality is replaced by Pareto optimality or efficiency. A point
x^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is considered Pareto optimal or efficient if there is no other point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
F(x)\leq F(x^{\ast})
and
F(x)\neq F(x^{\ast})
, where the inequality
\leq
is understood component-wise. Similarly, a point
x^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called weakly Pareto optimal or weakly efficient if there is no point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
F(x)<F(x^{\ast})
.
A point
x^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is locally Pareto optimal (or locally weakly Pareto optimal) if there is a neighborhood
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
around
x^{\ast}
where
x^{\ast}
is Pareto optimal (or weakly Pareto optimal) for
F
restricted to
V
. A necessary condition for local Pareto optimality of
x^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by:

{\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\langle\nabla F_{j}(x^{*}),y-x^{*}\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.}

(2)

A point
x^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that satisfies this condition is called Pareto critical or stationary.
Consequently, if a point
x
is not Pareto critical, there exists a vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
y-x
is a descent direction for
F
at
x
, meaning that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
F(x+t(y-x))<F(x)
for any
t\in(0,\varepsilon]
.

In this work, we propose and analyze a multiobjective cubic regularization method (M-CRM) for solving (
1
). Basically, at each iteration of the method, a model associated with the cubic regularization of each component of the objective function is minimized. More specifically, the next iterate

(x^{+},\gamma^{+},\lambda^{+})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}

is an approximate KKT point of the following smooth optimization problem with cubic objective function and quadratic constraints

\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\min_{(y,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}}&\;\gamma+\frac{\sigma}{6}\|y-x\|^{3}\\
\mbox{s. t.}&\langle{\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x)},{y-x}\rangle+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle{\nabla^{2}\bar{F}_{j}(x)(y-x)},{y-x}\rangle\leq\gamma,\quad\forall j\in\mathcal{J},\end{array}

(3)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\sigma>0
is a regularization parameter, and
\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x)
and
\nabla^{2}\bar{F}_{j}(x)
are approximations to the gradient
\nabla F_{j}(x)
and the Hessian
\nabla^{2}F_{j}(x)
, respectively.
Note that, this smooth problem is equivalent to the non-smooth scalar-value one

\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}M_{x,\sigma}(y):=\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}}M_{x,\sigma}^{j}(y),

(4)

where

M_{x,\sigma}^{j}(y):=\langle\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x),y-x\rangle+\dfrac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}\bar{F}_{j}(x)(y-x),y-x\rangle+\dfrac{\sigma}{6}\|y-x\|^{3},\;\forall j\in\mathcal{J}.

(5)

It is worth pointing out that the exact conditions for the derivative approximations in the M-CRM, specifically the inequalities (
16
) below, depend only on the previous information, and hence they are implementable; for further discussion on this issue in the scalar case, see, for example,
[

Implementations of the method, where information about derivatives are computed through finite difference strategies, are presented.
One interesting feature of the M-CRM is that the regularization parameter
\sigma

From a theoretical perspective, it is shown that the M-CRM requires at most

Preliminary material

In this section, we present the key results that form the foundation of the proposed algorithm. These results are essential for understanding both the structure of the algorithm and its theoretical analysis.

We begin by noting, from (
2
), that
x
is a Pareto critical point of (
1
) if and only if it is a minimum point of
\phi(y):=\max_{j}\langle\nabla F_{j}(x),y-x\rangle
, whose the optimal value is zero. Hence, since
\phi
is a convex function given by the maximum of affine functions
\phi_{j}(y):=\langle\nabla F_{j}(x),y-x\rangle
, with gradient
\nabla F_{j}(x)
, it follows that
x
is a Pareto critical point if and only if
0
belongs to the subdifferential of
\phi
, which is equivalent to the existence of
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
such that
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda_{j}=1
and
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda_{j}\nabla F_{j}(x)=0
.
This motivates the following concept of an approximate Pareto critical point.

Definition 1
.

Given a tolerance
\varepsilon>0
, we say that a point
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an
\varepsilon
-approximate Pareto critical point of (
1
) if and only if there exists
\bar{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
such that
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\bar{\lambda}_{j}=1
and
\left\|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\bar{\lambda}_{j}\nabla F_{j}(\bar{x})\right\|\leq~\varepsilon
.

Since problems (
3
) and (
4
) are equivalent, and the objective function of problem (
4
) is coercive, they have a solution.
Additionally, problem (
3
) satisfies the Mangasarian-Fromovitz constraint qualification; for example,
d=(0,1)
is a strictly feasible direction.
Thus, there exists a multiplier
\lambda^{+}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
such that the triple
(y,\gamma,\lambda):=(y^{+},\gamma^{+},\lambda^{+})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
satisfies the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions of (
3
), given by:

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}=1,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\left[\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x)+\nabla^{2}\bar{F}_{j}(x)(y-x)\right]+\frac{\sigma}{2}\|y-x\|(y-x)=0,

(6)

and, for all
j\in\mathcal{J}
,

\langle{\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x)},{(y-x)}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\nabla^{2}\bar{F}_{j}(x)(y-x)},{y-x}\rangle\leq\gamma,

\lambda_{j}\left[\langle{\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x)},{y-x}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\nabla^{2}\bar{F}_{j}(x)(y-x)},{y-x}\rangle-\gamma\right]=0.

Note that, in view of the latter two relations,
\gamma^{+}
is uniquely determined in terms of
(x^{+},\lambda^{+})
. Hence, we will consider only the pair
(x^{+},\lambda^{+})
when referring to an approximate KKT solution of problem (
3
). Indeed, in our algorithm, we will consider an approximate KKT pair
(x^{+},\lambda^{+})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
of problem (
3
)
in the sense that

M_{x,\sigma}(x^{+})\leq 0,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda^{+}_{j}=1,\quad\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda^{+}_{j}\nabla M_{x,\sigma}^{j}(x^{+})\right\|\leq\theta\|x^{+}-x\|^{2},

(7)

for some
\theta\geq 0
, where
M_{x,\sigma}
is as in (
4
).

Next, we review some well known properties of functions whose Hessians are Lipschitz continuous. In view of
(A1)
, it can be shown that for all
j\in\mathcal{J}
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

|F_{j}(y)-F_{j}(x)-\langle\nabla F_{j}(x),y-x\rangle-\dfrac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}F_{j}(x)(y-x),y-x\rangle|\leq\dfrac{L_{j}}{6}\|y-x\|^{3},

(8)

and

\|\nabla F_{j}(y)-\nabla F_{j}(x)-\nabla^{2}F_{j}(x)(y-x)\|\leq\dfrac{L_{j}}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}.

(9)

We now establish a key result.

Lemma 2
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be given and let
\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x)
and
\nabla^{2}\bar{F}_{j}(x)
be approximations of the gradient
\nabla F_{j}(x)
and the Hessian
\nabla^{2}F_{j}(x)
, respectively. Define the following residuals

E^{G}_{j}(x):=\nabla F_{j}(x)-\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x),\quad E^{H}_{j}(x):=\nabla^{2}F_{j}(x)-\nabla^{2}\bar{F}_{j}(x),\quad\forall j\in\mathcal{J}.

(10)

Assume that
(A1)
holds and let a pair
(x^{+},\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{++}
be such that

M_{x,\sigma}(x^{+})\leq 0,
where
M_{x,\sigma}
is as in (
4
).
Then, for any
\kappa_{G}>0
and
j\in\mathcal{J}
, there holds

\displaystyle F_{j}(x^{+})\leq F_{j}(x)+\|E^{G}_{j}(x)\|\|x^{+}-x\|+\frac{1}{2}\left\|E^{H}_{j}(x)\right\|\|x^{+}-x\|^{2}+\frac{(L-\sigma)}{6}\|x^{+}-x\|^{3}.

(11)

Proof.

It follows from (
8
) with
y=x^{+}
and the definitions of
M^{j}_{x,\sigma}
, and
E^{G}_{j}(x)
and
E^{H}_{j}(x)
in (
5
) and (
10
) that

\displaystyle F_{j}(x^{+})\leq

\displaystyle F_{j}(x)+\langle\nabla F_{j}(x),x^{+}-x\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}F_{j}(x)(x^{+}-x),x^{+}-x\rangle+\frac{L_{j}}{6}\|x^{+}-x\|^{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{j}(x)+M^{j}_{x,\sigma}(x^{+})+\langle E^{G}_{j}(x),x^{+}-x\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle E^{H}_{j}(x)(x^{+}-x),x^{+}-x\right\rangle+\frac{L_{j}-\sigma}{6}\|x^{+}-x\|^{3}.

Hence, using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, property of norms operator and the definition of
M_{x,\sigma}
in (
4
), we have

\displaystyle F_{j}(x^{+})\leq

\displaystyle F_{j}(x)+M_{x,\sigma}(x^{+})+\|E^{G}_{j}(x)\|\|x^{+}-x\|+\frac{1}{2}\left\|E^{H}_{j}(x)\right\|\|x^{+}-x\|^{2}+\frac{L_{j}-\sigma}{6}\|x^{+}-x\|^{3}.

Therefore, (
11
) follows from the above inequality,
M_{x,\sigma}(x^{+})\leq 0
for all
j\in\mathcal{J}
and
L=\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}}L_{j}
.

The next lemma provides a recursive formula for the functional value
F_{j}(x^{+})
, assuming that the errors in the gradient and Hessian of
F_{j}
at the current point—i.e.,
\|E^{G}_{j}(x)\|
and
\|E^{H}_{j}(x)\|
—are controlled by quantities depending on two parameters,
\kappa_{G}
and
\kappa_{H}
, and a pair
(\hat{x},x)
, where
\hat{x}
represents the previous iterate in our algorithm. It is important to note that this error condition will be iteratively ensured in our algorithm through a finite difference procedure.

Corollary 3
.

Let
(x,\hat{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
be given and assume that
E^{G}_{j}(x)
and
E^{H}_{j}(x)
as in (
10
) satisfy

\|E^{G}_{j}(x)\|\leq\kappa_{G}\|x-\hat{x}\|^{2},\quad\|E^{H}_{j}(x)\|\leq\kappa_{H}\|x-\hat{x}\|,\quad\forall j\in\mathcal{J},

(12)

for some
\kappa_{G},\kappa_{H}\geq 0
.
Assume that
(A1)
holds and let a pair
(x^{+},\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{++}
be such that

M_{x,\sigma}(x^{+})\leq 0

and

\sigma\geq 2[L+3\rho(2\kappa_{G}+\kappa_{H})],

(13)

for some
\rho\geq 1
. Then, the following inequality holds

F_{j}(x^{+})-F_{j}(x)\leq\dfrac{\sigma}{12\rho}\|x-\hat{x}\|^{3}-\dfrac{\sigma}{12}\|x^{+}-x\|^{3},\quad\forall j\in\mathcal{J}.

(14)

Proof.

In view of Lemma
2
, (
12
), and the fact that
a^{2}b\leq a^{3}+b^{3}
for any
a,b\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle F_{j}(x^{+})-F_{j}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\|E^{G}_{j}(x)\|\|x^{+}-x\|+\frac{1}{2}\|E^{H}_{j}(x)\|\|x-x^{+}\|^{2}+\frac{(L-\sigma)}{6}\|x^{+}-x\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{G}\|x-\hat{x}\|^{2}\|x^{+}-x\|+\frac{\kappa_{H}}{2}\|x-\hat{x}\|\|x-x^{+}\|^{2}+\frac{(L-\sigma)}{6}\|x^{+}-x\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{G}\|x-\hat{x}\|^{3}+\frac{\kappa_{H}}{2}\|x-\hat{x}\|^{3}+\frac{(L+6\kappa_{G}+3\kappa_{H}-\sigma)}{6}\|x^{+}-x\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2\kappa_{G}+\kappa_{H})}{2}\|x-\hat{x}\|^{3}+\frac{[L-\sigma+3\rho(2\kappa_{G}+\kappa_{H})]}{6}\|x^{+}-x\|^{3},

where the last inequality is due to the fact that
\rho\geq 1
.
Therefore, (
14
) follows from the above inequality and by noting that (
13
) implies that

L-\sigma+3\rho(2\kappa_{G}+\kappa_{H})\leq-\frac{\sigma}{2},\qquad 2\kappa_{G}+\kappa_{H}\leq\dfrac{\sigma}{6\rho}.

Our next result essentially shows that by computing an approximate KKT pair
(x^{+},\lambda^{+})
of the cubic approximation subproblem as in (
7
), we can control the convex combination of the gradients of the components of the multiobjective function
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda^{+}_{j}\nabla F_{j}(x^{+})
in terms of the residuals defined in (
10
) and the displacement
\|x^{+}-x\|
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be given and consider
E^{G}_{j}(x)
and
E^{H}_{j}(x)
as in (
10
). Assume that
(A1)
holds and let a triple
(x^{+},\lambda^{+},\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{++}
be such that
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda^{+}_{j}=1
and
\left\|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda^{+}_{j}\nabla M_{x,\sigma}^{j}(x^{+})\right\|\leq\theta\|x^{+}-x\|^{2}
, for some
\theta\geq 0
. Then, we have

\left\|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda^{+}_{j}\nabla F_{j}(x^{+})\right\|\leq\frac{L+\sigma+2\theta}{2}\|x^{+}-x\|^{2}+\max_{j}\left(\|E_{j}^{G}(x)\|+{\|E_{j}^{H}(x)\|\|x^{+}-x\|}\right).

Proof.

Let
\Phi_{x,j}
denote the quadratic approximation given by

\Phi_{x,j}(y):=\langle\nabla F_{j}(x),y-x\rangle+\dfrac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}F_{j}(x)(y-x),y-x\rangle,\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},j\in\mathcal{J}.

In view of (
9
) and the fact that
L=\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}}L_{j}
, we have

\left\|\nabla F_{j}(x^{+})-\nabla\Phi_{x,j}(x^{+})\right\|\leq\frac{L}{2}\|x^{+}-x\|^{2},\qquad\forall j\in\mathcal{J}.

(15)

On the other hand, from the definition of
M_{x,\sigma}^{j}
in (
5
), (
10
), and the triangle inequality for norms, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\Phi_{x,j}(x^{+})-\nabla M_{x,\sigma}^{j}(x^{+})\right\|\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla F_{j}(x)-\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x)\right\|+\|\nabla^{2}F_{j}(x)-\nabla^{2}\bar{F}_{j}(x)\|\|x^{+}-x\|

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma}{2}\|x^{+}-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|E_{j}^{G}(x)\|+\|E_{j}^{H}(x)\|\|x^{+}-x\|+\frac{\sigma}{2}\|x^{+}-x\|^{2}.

It follows by using the triangle inequality for norms, the fact that
\lambda^{+}_{j}\geq 0
, and (
15
) that

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda^{+}_{j}\nabla F_{j}(x^{+})\right\|\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda^{+}_{j}\left(\left\|\nabla F_{j}(x^{+})-\nabla\Phi_{x,j}(x^{+})\right\|+\left\|\nabla\Phi_{x,j}(x^{+})-\nabla M_{x,\sigma}^{j}(x^{+})\right\|\right)

\displaystyle+\left\|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda^{+}_{j}\nabla M_{x,\sigma}^{j}(x^{+})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda^{+}_{j}\left(\frac{L+\sigma+2\theta}{2}\|x^{+}-x\|^{2}+\|E_{j}^{G}(x)\|+\|E_{j}^{H}(x)\|\|x^{+}-x\|\right).

The proof of the lemma then follows from the above inequality and the fact that
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda^{+}_{j}=1
.

3
Multiobjective Cubic Regularization Method

In this section, we first introduce the multiobjective cubic regularization method for computing approximate Pareto critical points of problem (
1
). We then establish its iteration complexity and global convergence properties. The local behavior of the sheme is also studied.
Finally, we discuss implementations of the method where the approximation of the gradient and Hessian of each function component
F_{j}
are computed using finite difference strategies.

Algorithm 1.
Multiobjective Cubic Regularization Method (M-CRM)

Step 0.
Choose
x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\sigma_{1}>0
,
\theta\geq 0
,
\alpha>1
,
\bar{\kappa}_{G},\bar{\kappa}_{H}\geq 0,
and set
t:=1
.

Step 1.
Find the smallest integer
i\geq 0
such that
\alpha^{i-1}\sigma_{t}\geq\sigma_{1}
.

Step 1.1.
For every
j\in\mathcal{J}
, let
\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x_{t})
and
\nabla^{2}\bar{F}_{j}(x_{t})
be such that
E^{G}_{j}(\cdot)
and
E^{H}_{j}(\cdot)
, defined in (
10
), satisfy

\|E^{G}_{j}(x_{t})\|\leq\frac{\bar{\kappa}_{G}}{\alpha^{i-1}}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2},\quad\|E^{H}_{j}(x_{t})\|\leq\frac{\bar{\kappa}_{H}}{\alpha^{i-1}}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|.

(16)

Step 1.2.

Compute an approximate KKT point
(x_{t,i}^{+},\lambda_{t,i}^{+})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
of subproblem (
3
) with
x:=x_{t}
and
\sigma:=\alpha^{i}\sigma_{t}

such that

3.2
Local convergence rates

In this subsection, we establish local convergence rates for the sequence
\{x_{t}\}
generated by Algorithm 1 under some additional assumptions. One of the key assumptions is that
F_{j}
is strongly convex on a specific level set, as stated next.

(A3)
There exists
\mu>0
and an index
t_{0}
such that, for every
j\in\mathcal{J}
,
\nabla^{2}F_{j}(x)\succeq\mu I
whenever

F_{j}(x)\leq F_{j}(x_{t_{0}})+\dfrac{\sigma_{1}}{12}\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|^{3},

where
\sigma_{1}
is as in Step 0 of Algorithm 1.

First note that, by similar arguments as in (
23
), we have,
for all
t>t_{0}
,

\displaystyle F_{j}(x_{t})-F_{j}(x_{t_{0}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=t_{0}}^{t-1}F_{j}(x_{i+1})-F_{j}(x_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma_{1}}{12}\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|^{3}-\sum_{j=t_{0}}^{t-1}\dfrac{(\alpha-1)\sigma_{1}}{12}\|x_{j+1}-x_{j}\|^{3}-\dfrac{\sigma_{t}}{12}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma_{1}}{12}\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|^{3}.

Thus, it follows from
(A3)
that

\nabla^{2}F_{j}(x_{t})\succeq\mu I,\quad\forall t\geq t_{0}.

(27)

The local convergence rate will require the gradient
\nabla F_{j}
to be computed exactly and imposes a slightly stronger condition on the approximations of Hessian
\nabla^{2}F_{j}
. For clarity, these conditions are presented below as an assumption.

(A4)
For every
j\in\mathcal{J}
, the gradient of
F_{j}
is exactly computed (i.e.,
\nabla\bar{F}_{j}=\nabla F_{j}
), and the Hessian approximation
\nabla^{2}\bar{F}_{j}
satisfies the following condition:

\|E^{H}_{j}(x_{t})\|\leq\frac{\bar{\kappa}_{H}}{\alpha^{i-1}}\min\left\{\|\Delta x_{t}\|,\zeta\left\|g_{t}\right\|\right\}.

(28)

where
E^{H}_{j}
is as in (
10
),
\bar{\kappa}_{H}
is as in Step 0 of Algorithm 1,
\zeta>0
and

g_{t}:=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}(\lambda_{t})_{j}\nabla F_{j}(x_{t}),\qquad\Delta x_{t}:=x_{t}-x_{t-1}.

(29)

The next result establishes key inequalities that will be used to demonstrate the superlinear or quadratic convergence of
\{x_{t}\}
. In particular, the sequence
\{g_{t}\}
converges quadratically to zero, and the sequence
\{x_{t}\}
converges to a local Pareto solution of (
1
).

Theorem 10
.

Assume that
(A1)-(A4)
hold and let
\left\{x_{t}\right\}

t_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}

\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}=1

\|\Delta x_{t+1}\|\leq\frac{4}{\mu}\left\|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda_{j}\nabla F_{j}(x_{t})\right\|,\quad\forall t\geq t_{1},

(30)

\left\|g_{t+1}\right\|\leq C\left\|g_{t}\right\|^{2},\quad\forall t\geq t_{1},

C:=8\left(L+\alpha\sigma_{max}+2\theta\right)/\mu^{2}+4\alpha\bar{\kappa}_{H}\zeta/\mu

It follows from Corollary

\{g_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}

Hence, by (

\nabla^{2}\bar{F}_{j}(x_{t})\succeq\nabla^{2}F_{j}(x_{t})-\dfrac{\mu}{2}I\succeq\dfrac{\mu}{2}I.

Proof.

3.3
Approximations of the gradients and Hessians using finite differences

In this section, we discuss implementations of the M-CRM where the approximations of the gradient and Hessian of each function component
F_{j}
are computed using finite difference procedures; see, for example,
[
30
,
24
,
13
]
. We provide bounds for the derivative approximations, whose proofs are given in the Appendix, to ensure the completeness of our presentation.

It is worth noting that in these implementations, the constants
\bar{\kappa}_{G}
and

depend on the Lipschitz constants

. However, knowing the exact values of these constants are not necessary, as the first and/or second conditions in (

3.3.1
Approximations for the gradient using finite differences

In this subsection, we discuss sufficient conditions under which the first condition in (
16
) is met by suitable finite-difference approximations of
\nabla F_{j}
. We begin by discussing the approximations of
\nabla F_{j}
using central finite differences under hypothesis

Lemma 13
.

Suppose that
(A1)
holds. Given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
h>0
, let
\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be defined by

\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x)=\left(\dfrac{F_{j}(x+he_{1})-F_{j}(x-he_{1})}{2h},\ldots,\dfrac{F_{j}(x+he_{n})-F_{j}(x-he_{n})}{2h}\right),\quad\forall j\in\mathcal{J}.

(44)

Then, for every
j\in\mathcal{J}

\|E^{G}_{j}(x)\|\leq\dfrac{\sqrt{n}L_{j}}{6}h^{2},

(45)

where

10

As a consequence, by taking
h:={\sqrt{6}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|}/(n^{\beta}\alpha^{(i-1)})^{1/2}

\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x_{t})

\bar{\kappa}_{G}:=Ln^{(1-2\beta)/2}

.

We now discuss suitable finite-difference approximations of
\nabla F_{j}
under an additional hypothesis, which trivially holds when the gradient

is Lipschitz continuous.
The first and second approaches use forward and backward finite differences, while the third employs central finite differences.

Lemma 14

Assume that, for every

(46)

Given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
h>0
, let

be defined, for every

\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x)=\left(\dfrac{F_{j}(x+he_{1})-F_{j}(x)}{h},\ldots,\dfrac{F_{j}(x+he_{n})-F_{j}(x)}{h}\right),

\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x)=\left(\dfrac{F_{j}(x)-F_{j}(x-he_{1})}{h},\ldots,\dfrac{F_{j}(x)-F_{j}(x-he_{n})}{h}\right),

The power

4.1
Exact derivatives

In the first set of experiments we provide analytic expressions for the gradient and Hessian of the objectives, such that
\bar{\kappa}_{G}=\bar{\kappa}_{H}=0
. We consider two versions of Algorithm 1: one that finds a KKT point in Step 1.2 “exactly”, i.e,
\theta:=10^{-8}/\|x_{t,i_{t}}^{+}-x_{t}\|^{2}
, and another where the subproblems are solved inexactly, with
\theta:=0.9
in (
17
). We changed the stopping criteria of the inner solver (Algencan) to meet those in (
17
). These two versions shall be called M-CRM-E (exact) and M-CRM-I (inexact).

Based on Definition
1
, we consider the stopping criterion

\left\|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda_{t,i_{t}}^{+}\nabla F_{j}(x_{t,i_{t}}^{+})\right\|^{2}\leq 10\times{\tt eps}^{1/2},

(62)

where
{\tt eps}=\sqrt{2^{-52}}
is the machine precision. We also set a maximum number of iterations to 1,000.

M-CRM-E and M-CRM-I were compared with a Multiobjective Newton Method with safeguards (M-NS) from
[
22
]
on the 44 problems listed in Table
1
for 100 different starting points (resulting in 4,400 instances). The default parameters of the M-NS defined in
[
22
]
were used. We remark that M-NS implementation
1
1

1

https://github.com/lfprudente/newtonMOP
also employs Algencan for solving the subproblems.

Performance profiles of outer iterations, function evaluations, and running time are reported in Figure
1
.
We observe that both versions of M-CRM are more efficient in terms of outer iterations than M-NS. When it comes to robustness, given the budget of 1,000 iterations, M-NS solved 97.25% of the instances whereas M-CRM-E and M-CRM-I solved 99.65% and 99.63%, respectively.
When it comes to function evaluations, the profile follows almost the same trend as that of outer iterations, which indicates that M-CRM-I and M-CRM-E are not demanding an excessive number of backtrackings in Step 1.3. In terms of running time, M-CRM-I is more efficient than both M-NS and M-CRM-E for this set of problems/instances. It is interesting to note that, in general, even though M-CRM-E requires less iterations than M-NS, the iterations of the former are more expensive, which has a negative impact in the total running time. M-CRM-I mitigates this higher cost per iteration by allowing the subproblems to be solved inexactly.

4.2
Finite difference approximations

Now, we consider a derivative-free version of M-CRM-I given by Algorithm 2, where the gradients and Hessians are approximated by finite differences. We will refer to this version as M-CRM-I-DF.

Since M-CRM-I-DF is designed for problems where the derivatives are not readily available, we used as stopping criterion

\left\|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda_{t,i_{t}}^{+}\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x_{t,i_{t}}^{+})\right\|\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\|x_{t,i_{t}}^{+}-x_{t}\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon

(63)

where
\nabla\bar{F}_{j}(x)
denotes the finite difference approximation of
\nabla F_{j}(x)
given in Lemma
13
and
\varepsilon^{2}=10\times{\tt eps}^{1/2}
; see discussion in Remark
19

4.3
An application to logistic regression

This last experiment is inspired by regularized logistic regression problems of the form

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f_{\mu}(x):=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[b^{(i)}\log(\sigma_{x}(a^{(i)}))+(1-b^{(i)})\log(1-\sigma_{x}(a^{(i)}))\right]+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2},

(64)

where
\{(a^{(i)},b^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0,1\}
is the dataset,
\sigma_{x}(a):=1/(1+e^{-\langle a,x\rangle})
is the logistic model and
\mu>0
is a regularization parameter.

Problem (
64
) can be interpreted as a scalarization for a multiobjective optimization problem (MOP) with two objectives

F_{1}(x)=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[b^{(i)}\log(\sigma_{x}(a^{(i)}))+(1-b^{(i)})\log(1-\sigma_{x}(a^{(i)}))\right],\quad F_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}.

(65)

Performing analysis of points on the Pareto front of such a MOP allows one to find a good trade-off between goodness of fit (represented by
F_{1}(x)
) and simplicity of the model (represented by
F_{2}(x)
).

As dataset, we consider the Wisconsin Breast Cancer Dataset
2
2

2

Dataset freely available in the UCI Machine Learning Repository
(
https://archive.ics.uci.edu/ml
).

[
31
]
. We used only the first 10 features and scaled the data so that each feature is in the interval [0,1]. For scaling the two objectives we considered
\gamma_{1}=0.1
and
\gamma_{2}=1.0
. The dataset has 568 records which were split: the first
m=468
for training and the last 100 for test.

We decided to test M-CRM-I-DF for training so we do not need to provide gradients and Hessians for the objectives. Then, we ran M-CRM-I-DF from 300 random starting points in
\mathbb{R}^{10}
according to (
61
) with
\ell=(-10,\dots,-10)^{\top}
and
u=(10,\dots,10)^{\top}
.

Figure

illustrates the recovered Pareto front (and its zoomed version). “A” represents the point with smallest training error (smallest
F_{1}(x))
, but it is the point with the largest Euclidean norm (

Even with a value of

5
Conclusion

This work proposed a novel cubic regularization method tailored for nonconvex unconstrained multiobjective optimization problems. The method relies on regularized models for each objective component, incorporating inexact first- and second-order derivative information within controllable error bounds. A distinctive aspect of the algorithm is the joint adjustment of the regularization parameter and derivative approximation accuracy through a nonmonotone line search strategy, enhancing its practical robustness. Theoretical analysis established iteration-complexity bounds of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-3/2})
under mild assumptions, with specific results for finite-difference implementations showing favorable complexity in terms of function evaluations. Furthermore, the method exhibits global convergence, and under local convexity, achieves superlinear or quadratic local convergence, depending on the accuracy of the derivative information.

Numerical experiments on standard test sets from the literature showcase the effectiveness of the proposed methods and their competitive performance with respect to a safeguarded multiobjective Newton method. We also remark the robustness of the derivative-free version of M-CRM in these test problems and its applicability in real problems as illustrated by the example in regularized logistic regression.
