Radiation therapy, cytogenetic prognostication, novel systemic chemotherapy, and patient perspective in an adult with choroidal melanoma
Authors: Kevin Card, Rizwan Haq, John Mignano, Shilpa J Desai
Published: 2026

CASE PRESENTATION

Initial presentation

LF, a woman aged 50 years, presented initially in April of 2021 to a local provider with new flashes and floaters in her right eye. She was told she had a posterior vitreous detachment (a benign separation of the vitreous gel from the underlying neurosensory retina) and to return as needed. Her past medical history was notable only for hypertension and hyperlipidemia. Her family history was negative for malignancy. She had an active prior history of smoking with a 29‐pack‐year history and was consuming one pack per day. Over the next months, the patient noted continued flashes and floaters and represented in October of 2021 with further loss of peripheral vision. At this visit, her vision was 20/20 in both eyes with normal intraocular pressures and no afferent pupillary defect. Dilated examination showed a temporal wedge‐shaped pigmented choroidal lesion measuring 18 × 14 mm in greatest basal dimension with an associated serous retinal detachment present temporally and inferiorly in the right eye (Figure 1A). Ocular ultrasound of her right eye revealed a 4.27‐mm isoechoic lesion with an overlying serous retinal detachment (Figure 1B). Her examination and testing in the left eye were within normal limits. The patient was clinically diagnosed with a medium‐sized choroidal melanoma. The risks, benefits, alternatives, and indications of fine‐needle aspiration biopsy with genetic testing were discussed, and she elected to proceed. Radiation therapy using plaque versus external‐beam radiation therapy were discussed, and she elected to proceed with external‐beam radiation therapy with gamma knife stereotactic radiosurgery (GKSRS). The patient underwent screening abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with and without contrast, which was negative for liver metastasis. Her initial tumor staging was Stage IIB (T3a, Nx, M0).

(A) Wedge‐shaped, pigmented choroidal melanoma measuring 18 × 14 mm in greatest basal dimension with an associated serous retinal detachment present temporally and inferiorly. (B) B‐scan ultrasound of the right eye demonstrating a 4.27‐mm lesion with a serous retinal detachment.

LF underwent a chorioretinal biopsy and genomic testing using an institutional next‐generation sequencing assay of 447 cancer‐related genes (OncoPanel; Brigham and Women’s Hospital), which revealed a missense mutation in GNA11 (c.626A>T, p.Q209L), KRAS (c.601C>T, p.G12V), and GNAS (c.601C>T, p.R201C). Notable structural variants included deletion of BAP1 (BRCA‐associated protein 1), a uveal melanoma tumor suppressor gene associated with metastasis. 1 The molecular signature was identified as class 2, preferentially expressed antigen (PRAME)‐negative, correlating with a 41.7% risk of liver metastasis (based on liver biopsy) at 5 years after diagnosis. She then underwent gamma knife stereotactic radiation surgery on December 12, 2021, using contrast‐enhanced MRI for treatment planning (Figure 2). At that time, the right choroidal melanoma had a volume of approximately 0.42 mL. She received a minimum peripheral tumor dose of 22 grays (Gy) and a maximum tumor dose of 33 Gy. The maximum optic nerve tolerance dose is between 8 and 10 Gy; and, in this patient, the maximum optic nerve dose was 3.5 Gy.

SUBSEQUENT CARE

LF was followed with stable ocular tumor measurements and resolution of her serous retinal detachment until July 27, 2023. Her fundus photography then showed tumor growth at the superior aspect of the lesion (Figure 3A) without obvious increase in thickness of the original mass (Figure 3B). The patient was offered enucleation, repeat GKSRS, or plaque brachytherapy (PBT). She elected to repeat GKSRS, which was completed on August 13, 2023. At that time, the area of recurrent choroidal melanoma had a volume of approximately 0.34 mL (Figure 4). GKSRS treatment delivered a dose of 20 Gy to the 50% isodose line using a plan consisting of seven isocenters, which encompassed the entire lesion volume.

First recurrence. (A) Choroidal melanoma with tumor growth at the superior aspect of the lesion (arrow). (B) B‐scan ultrasound demonstrating no obvious increase in thickness of the original mass.

Magnetic resonance imaging‐guided gamma knife stereotactic radiosurgery planning for treatment of the first recurrence.

Given the high risk of metastasis associated with choroidal melanoma, she was followed annually with surveillance abdominal MRI. In October 2023, three lesions on the liver (1.3, 0.8, and 0.5 cm) were detected, and liver biopsy confirmed the presence of liver metastases. Several treatment options were considered, but she ultimately was enrolled in a clinical trial combining darovasertib and crizotinib because of promising results (see Medical oncology perspective, below). This option was also influenced by the restriction of this clinical trial to the front‐line setting. She was not eligible for treatment with tebenafusp because human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genotype did not reveal HLA‐A*02:01. She began treatment with IDE196 (darovasertib) along with crizotinib, which she generally tolerated despite some nausea and lower extremity edema, both of which resolved.

After the repeat GKSRS, her ocular tumor measurements were again stable until August 8, 2024, when another area of tumor extension was noted at the superotemporal edge of the original tumor (Figure 5A), with ultrasound demonstrating thickness of the extension at 2.70 mm (Figure 5B). We again discussed enucleation, repeat GKSRS, or PBT. Observation was not an option because, without local control, the ocular melanoma would continue to grow, leading to a blind and painful eye. Therefore, despite visual acuity of 20/30, the patient elected for enucleation. She underwent enucleation of the right eye on September 9, 2024. Tissue pathology showed epithelioid cell melanoma, which extended to the anterior ciliary body without involvement of the sclera or the optic nerve (Figure 6A,B).

Ocular oncologist perspective

The uveal tract encompasses the iris, ciliary body, and choroid. The choroid is a vascular layer of tissue between the sclera and the retina. Choroidal melanoma originates from melanocytes in the uveal tract. Choroidal melanomas account for 90% of uveal melanoma. 2 Uveal melanoma is the most common primary intraocular malignancy with a prevalence of 5.6 per 1,000,000 per year. 3 The incidence of uveal melanoma is 30% greater in men than in women, with a peak age of diagnosis between 72 and 79 years and A median age at diagnosis of 62 years. 4 , 5

The occurrence of uveal melanoma is thought to be either a sporadic event or, rarely, caused by a preexisting germline mutation. 6 Risk factors include light iris color, light‐colored skin, and northern European descent. 7 The HERC2 gene is related to blue eye color and is associated with an increased risk of choroidal melanoma. 8 Patients with known choroidal nevi are at risk of malignant transformation at a rate of one in 8845 per year. 9 It is estimated that up to 6% of the Caucasian population has a choroidal nevus. 10 There are known characteristics of choroidal nevi seen on examination and on imaging that may increase the risk of malignant transformation. These choroidal nevi typically require closer surveillance to monitor for evidence of transformation. 11 However, even in those with zero known risk factors, patients with choroidal nevi are recommended to have at least annual screening for transformation.

The diagnosis of uveal melanoma is made clinically. 2 Choroidal melanomas typically present as a brown, dome‐shaped or mushroom‐shaped mass beneath the retina. In some patients, uveal melanomas can be amelanocytic. Choroidal melanomas typically have low to medium echogenicity on ultrasound, appearing acoustically hollow. 12 Choroidal melanomas are also often associated with orange subretinal pigment because of overlying, lipofuscin‐laden macrophages. They may also present with a concomitant serous retinal detachment caused by invasion of the tumor into the choroidal space. 13 Patients may experience symptoms of blurry vision, flashing lights, visual field defect, pain, and metamorphopsia, but up to 30%–40% of patients are asymptomatic. 14

Multiple groups have worked to improve the understanding of genetic signatures present in choroidal melanomas and how they may prognosticate metastasis and death. Robertson et al. studied the genetic signatures present in choroidal melanoma from the eyes of 80 patients to identify molecular pathways involved in choroidal melanoma pathogenesis. 15 Jager et al. then applied these molecular pathways to a four‐tier stratification system for metastatic potential termed The Cancer Genome Atlas, based on chromosomal aberrations that give way to relevant pathogenic mutations involved in choroidal melanoma. 16 Harbour also explored the genotype markers that may predict the potential for metastatic disease and developed systems of collecting tumor samples through fine‐needle aspiration biopsy to classify groups based on their gene expression and corresponding risk stratifications for metastatic disease. 17 Notably, it has been described that the status of single genes is inferior as a predictive index to the combined status of the multiple known oncogenes in uveal melanoma. Dollar et al. recently performed genetic profiling of 1140 uveal melanomas to describe which mutations are the most reliable predictors of metastatic potential and also to define mutation profiles that exist between tumors transitioning between low‐risk and high‐risk states. 18 Their results indicate that tumors classified as high‐risk for metastasis and death appear to arise from tumors that were at one point in the melanoma cycle comprised of a low‐risk cytogenetic profile. 19 Specifically, abnormalities in chromosomes 1, 3, 6, and 8 are highly associated with choroidal melanoma. 20 Monosomy of chromosome 3 is associated with a higher risk of metastasis, whereas disomy of chromosome 3 has an excellent survival rate. 19 Mutations in GNAQ and GNA11 are identified in almost all choroidal melanomas. 20 These mutations lead to dysregulated signaling, leading to uncontrolled cell growth. 21 Recently, mutations in the gene TNC (encoding Tenascin C, an extracellular matrix protein) were associated with higher mortality rates. 22 In addition, mutations in BAP1, which is identified in up to 50% of choroidal melanomas, are associated with a higher risk of metastasis. 23 SF3B1 (splicing factor 3b subunit 1) and E1F1AX (eukaryotic translation initiation factor 1A X‐linked gene) are also frequently seen in choroidal melanoma but are associated with a lower risk of metastasis. 1 , 24 , 25

The DecisionDx‐UM gene expression profile test (Castle Biosciences) is an RNA‐based test performed on tumor biopsy samples to determine which gene changes are present in an individual choroidal melanoma. The class (1 or 2) is based on 15 genes to determine the risk of metastasis. 26 In addition, PRAME testing evaluates the expression of an antigen expressed in choroidal melanoma cells that can aid in distinguishing malignant melanoma from suspicious‐appearing choroidal nevi or other choroidal tumors. With these molecular signatures, a risk assessment can be provided to the patient. 27 , 28 Risks of performing the choroidal melanoma biopsy include hemorrhage, infection, and retinal detachment, along with the standard risks of the required anesthesia.

Radiation oncology perspective

Enucleation was the standard of care for curative intent‐treatment of uveal melanomas from the late 1800s until the mid‐1900s. 22 , 29 Attempts at organ‐preserving brachytherapy were performed as early as the 1920s with radon‐222 seeds. In the 1960s, brachytherapy was performed with cobalt‐60. Neither radon‐222 nor cobolt‐60 had properties ideal for ocular brachytherapy. Therefore, the Collaborative Ocular Melanoma Study (COMS) group conducted multicenter trials using iodine‐125 (I‐125) for temporary PBT from the 1980s through 2003. COMS Report 28 was published in 2006 and compared PBT versus enucleation. Patients enrolled in that trial had a maximum basal dimension of no more than 16 mm and an apical height of no more than 10 mm. 30 In total, 1317 eligible patients were enrolled. The authors reported an all‐cause mortality at 12 years of 43% for the PBT arm and 41% for the enucleation arm (nonsignificant). Local control in the PBT arm was 87.5%. 30 Preservation of visual acuity in the patients who underwent PBT depended on several factors, although the most important was tumor location at least 5 mm from the fovea. 30 , 31

After the favorable treatment outcomes from the COMS report, organ‐preserving treatment with PBT became more common. The plaque applicator was typically loaded with I‐125 seeds such that the tumor apex would receive approximately 85 Gy over approximately 7 days. More recently, palladium‐103 seeds have been used instead of I‐125 because of the more favorable shorter half‐life (17.0 vs. 59.4 days, respectively) and radiobiologic properties. 32

As an alternative to PBT, external‐beam radiotherapy may be used. This technique is especially useful for situations in which the melanoma is peripapillary in location and/or too large for PBT (i.e., >10 mm in thickness or >18 mm in maximum basal dimension). 33 One option is cobalt‐60 stereotactic radiosurgery (GKSRS, most commonly with the Elekta gamma knife). The Boston Gamma Knife Center has used the gamma knife for the successful treatment of select uveal melanomas for approximately 25 years. 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 Another option, used since the 1960s, is proton‐beam radiotherapy, which is similarly useful for large tumors, tumors abutting the optic nerve, or tumors in a difficult‐to‐reach location with PBT. 38 Proton‐beam radiotherapy also boasts excellent local control rates. 39

A retrospective analysis of patients with uveal melanoma treated at Tufts Medical Center with PBT or GKSRS included data on 121 and 139 patients treated with brachytherapy or stereotactic radiosurgery, respectively, between January 2000 and March 2025. 40 Our analysis demonstrates that GKSRS yields the same treatment outcomes for brachytherapy regarding the prevention of local recurrence and metastases, overall survival, and progression‐free survival. The rates of adverse events (such as visual acuity decline, optic neuropathy, retinal detachment) were similar, but GKSRS resulted in less vision loss than PBT.

Medical oncology perspective

Approximately 40% of patients with uveal melanoma will develop metastasis despite effective management of the eye lesion. 41 In contrast to other subtypes of melanoma, uveal melanoma metastasis at diagnosis has a strong predilection for the liver. Recurrence is also notable for its latency, with the development of overt metastasis occurring up to 40 years after the initial diagnosis. 42 , 43 Based on clinical evidence, it has been estimated that micrometastasis was present >5 years before treatment of the primary tumor. 44 Furthermore, 9.6% of patients died more than 15 years after treatment of the primary tumor, and micrometastasis remained the leading cause of death in this population over the course of 35 years. 45 Accordingly, surveillance strategies of patients at risk typically emphasize lifelong monitoring, which is predominantly focused on the liver. Gadolinium‐enhanced MRI of the liver has greater sensitivity than ultrasound or computed tomography scans. 46 , 47 Fluorodeoxyglucose‐positron emission tomography avidity can be mixed for uveal melanoma and has relatively low sensitivity for liver lesions. 45 There is growing interest in using circulating tumor DNA in surveillance, although these assays still lack the sensitivity to be widely used for surveillance. 48

Historically, there has been a paucity of effective therapies upon the development of metastasis. 49 Because liver‐only or liver‐predominant disease is common among patients newly diagnosed with metastatic disease, resection or ablation of metastasis has been used in patients with limited burden, oligometastatic disease. Liver metastases depend relatively more on the hepatic artery for vascular supply than nonmalignant liver tissue, enabling the use of embolization approaches such as transarterial chemoembolization or immunoembolization. 50 , 51 Definitive evidence for their use has been limited because of the single‐institution design, retrospective design, or selection bias in these studies. For example, a randomized study showed a nonsignificant higher response rate of immunoembolization compared with bland embolization (21.2% vs. 16.7%). 52 Nevertheless, because most patients have multifocal or miliary hepatic metastasis, perfusion‐based therapies, such as isolated hepatic perfusion or percutaneous hepatic perfusion (PHP) have been evaluated. 53 Isolated hepatic perfusion was found to be associated with improved responses compared to best alternative care (best available treatment for patients not getting the experimental intervention). 53 Percutaneous hepatic perfusion was tested in the FOCUS trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02678572), which demonstrated a significant improvement in objective response rates (51.1% vs. 22.2%), progression‐free survival (11.3 vs. 5.8 months), and overall survival (26.7 vs. 15.4 months) with percutaneous hepatic perfusion versus the best alternative care, leading to FDA approval in 2023. 54

Unfortunately, few systemic treatments for metastatic uveal melanoma have proven to be efficacious. Overall response rates to most chemotherapies have been <10%. Molecularly targeted therapies against KIT, MET, IGF1R, EGFR, and other cell‐surface proteins have historically been ineffective. 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 Preclinical evidence previously suggested dependence on the MAPK pathway, leading to several trials evaluating selumetinib, an MEK1/2 inhibitor. 61 , 62 Phase 2 studies indicated that selumetinib showed improvements in progression‐free survival either alone or in combination with chemotherapy. 63 , 64 However, a confirmatory phase 3 study of dacarbazine with selumetinib versus placebo failed to demonstrate significant differences. 65

The relative dependency of uveal melanoma cells on protein kinase C led to the design of early phase studies of the PKC inhibitor darovasertib either alone or in combination with the MET inhibitor crizotinib. 66 , 67 , 68 , 69 , 70 Objective response rates were observed in 34% of patients, with disease control rates exceeding 90% in both previously treated and treatment‐naive patients. Across all patients treated, the median overall survival was 21.1 months, and the median progression‐free survival was 7.0 months. A registrational study of darovasertib with crizotinib in HLA‐201–negative patients is underway (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05987332). 71

Immune checkpoint inhibitors, despite their impressive activity in cutaneous melanoma, have had limited activity in patients with uveal melanoma. Combinations of anti‐CTLA4 and anti–PD‐1 inhibition with ipilimumab and nivolumab, respectively, have shown overall response rates of approximately 11.5%–18%. 72 , 73 , 74 , 75 Although LAG‐3 was identified as a checkpoint in uveal melanoma, overall responses are infrequent. 76 , 77 , 78 , 79 However, patients with somatic mutations in MBD4 (encoding methyl‐CpG‐binding domain protein 4, which regulates base‐excision repair DNA damage) may have improved responses to checkpoint inhibitors. 80 Explanations for their limited activity include the relatively low tumor mutational burden, the immunosuppressive effects associated with liver metastasis, or lower expression of intratumoral checkpoints.

Tebentafusp, a first‐in‐class, bispecific T‐cell engager targeting CD3 and the glycoprotein 100 expressed in an HLA‐A*02:01–restricted fashion, was tested in the IMCgp100‐202 study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03070392) compared with the investigator's choice. 81 , 82 An improvement in overall survival was observed with tebentafusp (21.6 vs. 16.9 months; hazard ratio for death, 0.68), yet progression‐free survival was more modest (3.4 vs. 2.9 months; hazard ratio, 0.76), indicating that parameters other than radiologic evaluation may be better biomarkers of response. Several studies have indicated that a reduction in circulating tumor DNA may better define patients who are benefiting from treatment. 82 , 83 The results led to FDA approval of tebentafusp in 2022.

Several therapeutic strategies are being developed with early evidence of activity. First, combined regional and systemic therapies are currently in clinical trials. For example, immune checkpoint inhibitors with embolization or hepatic perfusion approaches are under investigation. The CHOPIN trial combined PHP with systemic the immune checkpoint inhibitors ipilimumab and nivolumab (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04283890). 84 , 85 Compared with patients randomized to PHP alone, the combination was associated with improved progression‐free and overall survival. The use of agents targeting PRAME are currently in clinical testing, with preliminary efficacy reported. 86

UPDATE STATUS

LF currently uses an ocular prosthesis in place of the right eye. Repeat MRI of the abdomen in January 2026 showed progression of her liver metastasis and a rising circulating tumor DNA tumor fraction after relative stability since October 2023 on darovasertib and crizotinib. Scans show no evidence of extrahepatic disease, thus she has been referred for consideration of liver‐directed therapy.

Patient perspective

It all started back in 2021. I had some floaters and went to my regular eye doctor and thought maybe I had a detached retina. He did the initial evaluation, looked in my eyes, and, at the end, he didn't verify anything. He said I needed to see a specialist, who then told me it was definitely choroidal melanoma. That was very upsetting and surprising to find out that I was only one of about 1400 in the United States that gets it a year.

I was scared and nervous. When I met with Dr Desai (ocular oncologist), we went over our options: gamma knife radiation or the plaque brachytherapy surgery. From there, I went to see Dr Mignano (radiation oncologist). We discussed the options, and I went with the gamma radiation because I really didn't want two surgeries. I figured it was more precise, the gamma radiation. I also had a biopsy of the tumor in my eye, which was the scariest thing. They did tell me that, because my biopsy showed class 2 PRAME‐negative, there was an extremely high risk for the tumor to spread to other parts of the body.

Of course, my family and I were nervous and a little scared, but we went over everything, and all the teams were amazing. We had the first gamma knife radiation and kept going and having the MRIs and the CAT scans, and everything was going along fine. Then, in 2023, the choroidal melanoma came back. It was crazy to hear that only 210 in the United States get it a second time. So, you know, can't win the lottery. We already knew what the procedures were and what was involved, so we went with gamma knife radiation because, again, it was only one surgery. I didn't want to go through plaque and have two different surgical procedures. And then, I kept having the MRIs and the CAT scans.

In 2023, we found out it spread to my liver. That was extremely scary and challenging, especially when I went over to Dr Haq (medical oncologist). They told me there was basically no standard of care for my treatment, which made it even scarier and more difficult to deal with. However, I was accepted for a clinical trial, and I eventually learned that I was selected for the trial drug and not the placebo.

I started the trial meds around November 17th, 2023. I had to go every month for MRI and CAT scan imaging along with blood work and other testing. The trial was going great for a year and a half. But then, in 2024, at one of my follow‐ups with Dr Desai, my eye scans showed that the choroidal melanoma had come back for a third time.

So, at this point, now we're dealing with liver cancer and the choroidal melanoma. By now, they didn't recommend the gamma knife because we've already done that twice. Plaque surgery, with everything going on, felt so aggressive. I really didn't want to have a plaque surgery and then, a year later, find out it's spread to my brain. So I decided to have the eye removed on September 9th, 2024, which of course, was also scary. But afterwards, the pathology confirmed that they did get the whole cancer out along with the eye. So I didn't need to worry about that anymore and could concentrate on the liver. Up to this point, can I tell you, all the teams involved have been incredible.

In January 2026, while being on the trial meds, they found some new liver lesions. I ended up having a consultation at Massachusetts General Hospital for a chemotherapy that will be directed only to my liver. This is scheduled for February 19th, 2026.

So far, I've stayed positive the whole time, even when I had my eye removed, I would say now, you spell my name, ***, with one i. My whole attitude is that I can't sit on the couch and cry because it's not going to do anything. So I just keep moving forward and plugging along and doing what I can. Right now, I’m feeling fine and doing well.
