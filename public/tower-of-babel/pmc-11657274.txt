The relationship between academic motivation and moral sensitivity in nursing students
Authors: Parisa Nezhadhoseini, Seyed Kazem Mousavi, Ali Javadzadeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA Bachelor’s degree in nursing is one of the most challenging programs in the field of medical sciences. As a result, maintaining students’ academic motivation at the desired level is a constant concern for policymakers and educational administrators. Furthermore, tackling complex ethical dilemmas is inherent in nursing, making the educational period an important moment to instill moral sensitivity and reinforce professional ethics in students. This study aimed to investigate the association between academic motivation and moral sensitivity among undergraduate nursing students.MethodsIn this descriptive-correlational study, 265 undergraduate nursing students from the Abhar School of Nursing at Zanjan University of Medical Sciences in Iran were chosen using a census approach in 2024. The data collection tools included demographic surveys, the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS), and the Moral Sensitivity Questionnaire (MSQ), all completed online. The data were analyzed with SPSS version 16 software, which used descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation) and inferential statistics (independent t-test, ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and multiple regression) with a significance level of 0.05.ResultsNursing students had average academic motivation and moral sensitivity ratings of 79.24 ± 14.05 and 121.12 ± 16.33, respectively. Furthermore, a significant relationship was found between the overall scores and all dimensions of academic motivation and moral sensitivity (p < 0.001). Additionally, 29.1% of the variance in moral sensitivity was explained by the dimensions of the student’s academic motivation.ConclusionThis study’s findings revealed a link between academic motivation and moral sensitivity among nursing students. As a result, it is advised that levels of academic motivation be continually checked during the program. This will assist in identifying students at danger of losing motivation and allow for the development and execution of effective initiatives to improve their academic engagement.Keywords: Motivation, Morals, Students, Nursing

Introduction

The primary mission of nursing is to provide care, treatment, and rehabilitation services at the highest possible level to ensure, maintain, and promote the health of society. Therefore, adhering to nursing ethics and maintaining a high moral standard in this profession is crucial [1]. In nursing, compliance with ethical principles is emphasized above all else, as these principles guide the best practices for patient care [2]. As a result, nurses at every stage of their professional lives must cultivate and develop their moral sensitivity to enhance their understanding of professional ethics [3].

Moral sensitivity is a quality that enables an individual to recognize moral conflicts, understand vulnerable situations both emotionally and intellectually, and be aware of the moral consequences when making decisions affecting others [4]. Lutzen defines moral sensitivity as nurses’ awareness of patients’ vulnerability and their insight into the moral implications of decisions made on behalf of patients [5]. Thus, moral sensitivity and a sense of responsibility are essential, particularly for nurses [4]. As future members of the nursing workforce, nursing students will encounter the challenging ethical conditions inherent in the profession. Therefore, they must develop moral awareness and sensitivity to provide appropriate care in complex situations based on sound moral decision-making skills [6]. Consequently, teaching the principles of professional ethics to medical professionals, especially nursing students, has become essential [7].

Since ethics is a complex and multifaceted issue, it is crucial to identify the factors that influence its teaching [4, 6]. The factors affecting the development of ethics and moral sensitivity can be categorized into individual and contextual factors. Personal factors relate to a person’s internal personality traits, such as generosity, responsibility, and interest, and are primarily shaped by culture. Conversely, contextual factors pertain to external conditions and are often influenced by the surrounding environment [8]. Studies indicate that individual factors play a more significant role in forming moral sensitivity, as ethical activity in a profession is rooted in genuine interest and intrinsic motivation for the work [8, 9].

Undoubtedly, one of the most critical factors for people to move forward and achieve any goal is motivation [10]. According to the definition, motivation is a process that stimulates and energizes physical and mental activities to achieve a specific goal [11]. Academic motivation is commonly used in educational environments and refers to behaviors related to learning and progress [10]. Deci and Ryan described academic motivation as the learner’s desire to engage in learning activities and their ongoing efforts to complete them. In their theory, they identified three types of motivation: intrinsic motivation, which is the desire to engage in an activity primarily for the inner pleasure and satisfaction it brings; extrinsic motivation, which refers to the desire to pursue an activity without an inherent sense of connection to it; and motiveless, which indicates a lack of desire and enthusiasm to engage in an activity [12].

According to this theory, motivation rarely involves a single phenomenon. Individuals differ not only in the quantity of their motivation but also in its direction and type, and this conflict in motivation types is connected to their fundamental beliefs and goals [13]. One common issue in educational systems is low academic motivation among students, which leads to significant educational, cultural, and economic losses for governments [14]. This challenge is notably pronounced in nursing education, where providing quality nursing services necessitates students with sufficient motivation to acquire knowledge and implement clinical skills [15]. However, statistics indicate that 20% of nursing students drop out yearly, and many need more academic motivation throughout their studies [9].

It is important to note that a decrease in motivation adversely affects nurses, patients’ health, and society as a whole, resulting in considerable economic losses for governments [14]. Moreover, disturbances in motivation can lead to emotional and behavioral issues, creating an environment conducive to pessimism, anxiety, depression, and mental health problems, along with a significant decline in personal, social, and occupational performance [16].

It can be inferred from the content presented that academic motivation and moral sensitivity are fundamental in nursing. Although separate studies have been conducted on these two concepts, their interrelationship has received less attention from nursing researchers. Therefore, by conducting studies that provide a suitable platform for a deeper understanding of each concept and how they are interconnected, it is possible to attract the attention of educational planners and managers to this issue, facilitating more appropriate and informed planning for its enhancement. Consequently, the researchers planned and implemented a study investigating the relationship between academic motivation and moral sensitivity among undergraduate nursing students.

Materials and methods

Study design and sampling

This descriptive-correlational study was undertaken in 2024 to examine the relationship between academic motivation and moral sensitivity among undergraduate nursing students. The research population comprised all second- to eighth-semester nursing students at Abhar Nursing School, associated with Zanjan University of Medical Sciences in Iran. A census-based sampling method was used, and 265 pupils were chosen to participate. The study’s inclusion criteria included willingness to participate and full-time attendance in the nursing program. Exclusion criteria included a lack of clinical work experience, a history of psychiatric problems, usage of associated drugs (self-reported), and incomplete questionnaire submissions.

Instruments

Data collection tools included demographic questionnaires, the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS), and the Moral Sensitivity Questionnaire (MSQ). The demographic questionnaire gathered information on age, gender, marital status, semester, grade point average, and whether the students were interested in or not interested in nursing.

Harter et al. developed the AMS, consisting of 33 items: 17 for intrinsic and 16 for extrinsic motivation. These items are rated using a five-point Likert scale (1 = never, 2 = seldom, 3 = occasionally, 4 = mostly/often, and 5 = always). Items 27–21, 19, 16, 15, 10, 9, 5, 4, 3, and 31 are scored reversely. The overall score for this questionnaire varies from 33 to 165, with higher scores suggesting greater academic motivation. A score between 33 and 66 indicates poor academic motivation, 66 to 99 shows average academic motivation, while scores above 99 indicate high academic motivation [17]. Bahrani conducted a psychometric evaluation of the AMS in Iran, confirming its structural validity and reliability in Persian. The construct validity indicated two factors: internal and external motivation. The scale’s reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and test-retest reliability, obtaining coefficients of 0.85 and 0.86 for the internal motivation factor and 0.69 and 0.72 for the external motivation factor, respectively [18]. In this investigation, Cronbach’s alpha was used to calculate the AMS’s dependability value, which was 0.89.

The 28-item MSQ, created and psychometrically evaluated by Kim Lützén et al. in Sweden, assessed nursing students’ moral sensitivity. This tool’s items are rated on a seven-point Likert scale, with “totally agree” (7 points) and “disagree” (1 point). The overall MSQ score ranges from 28 to 196, with higher levels suggesting stronger moral sensitivity. The questionnaire comprises six dimensions: relational orientation, structuring moral meaning, expressing benevolence, modifying autonomy, experiencing moral conflict, and following the rules [19]. In this study, scores of 28–84 were categorized as low moral sensitivity, 85–140 as moderate moral sensitivity, and 141–196 as high moral sensitivity. This questionnaire’s face and content validity were examined for the first time in Iran by Abbaszadeh et al. in Kerman, achieving a validity rate of 97%. Additionally, its reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, resulting in a coefficient of 0.81 [20]. Cronbach’s alpha was used in the current investigation to calculate the MSQ’s dependability, which came out to be 0.91.

Data collection and statistical analysis

After acquiring the ethical code and permission to conduct the research, the questionnaires, the consent form to participate in the study, and the researchers’ contact information for any inquiries were made available to the students online via the Porsline system (www.porsline.ir). The students completed the questionnaires at their convenience. Data analysis was performed with the help of SPSS version 16, using descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation) and inferential statistics (independent t-test, ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and multiple regression) at a significance level of 0.05.

Results

Out of the 265 students who participated in the study, 16 were removed due to incomplete questionnaire completion. As a result, the analysis was based on data collected from 249 participants. According to the data, the students’ average age was 22.16 years, and 66.8% were female. Most participants were single (75.4%), and 43.8% had a grade point average between 16 and 18. Additionally, 76.2% of the students indicated an interest in nursing. Nursing students’ total academic motivation and moral sensitivity scores were reported as 79.24 ± 14.05 and 121.12 ± 16.33, respectively. Discussion

This study examined the association between academic motivation and moral sensitivity in undergraduate nursing students. The primary variables were first analyzed, followed by an investigation of their relationship. The findings demonstrated that nursing students’ motivation was average, consistent with results from other studies conducted in Iran and other countries [21–24]. However, several studies reveal conflicting findings, indicating that nursing students’ intellectual motivation was either high [10, 25, 26] or poor [27]. According to a review of the research, social, political, economic, and cultural issues can all impact nursing students’ academic motivation. For example, during the COVID-19 epidemic, levels of academic interest rose, most likely due to the change to virtual schooling at the time [28, 29]. Furthermore, an analysis of linked studies shows that academic motivation among Iranian nursing students has declined in recent years. This could be attributed to the continued economic and occupational issues that the nursing profession faces. This circumstance can have a negative impact on academic motivation for students who have selected nursing as a fulfilling career [30, 31]. More focused research is required to investigate this assumption more completely. Furthermore, the study found a substantial association between demographic variables—such as age, gender, semester, grade point average, and interest in the field—and academic motivation in students, which is consistent with the findings of most studies in this area [21, 23, 25, 26].

Furthermore, the total moral sensitivity score of nursing students in this study was average. Similar findings have been observed in some Iranian investigations, including those by Kohensal et al. [32] and Mohammadi et al. [33]. Furthermore, foreign research, like those by Chen et al. in China [34], Alnajjar et al. in Saudi Arabia [35], and Uncu et al. in Turkey [36], have yielded similar results. However, some investigations provide different outcomes. For example, Tahmasabi et al. found a high moral sensitivity among nursing students in their study [37], mainly focusing on final-year nursing students enrolled in practical courses. Bagheri et al. discovered poor moral sensitivity among second-semester nursing students [38]. The current study found a substantial association between academic semesters and students’ moral sensitivity, which indirectly supports earlier findings. Shadi et al. emphasized the role of clinical environment exposure in developing moral sensitivity in nursing students [39]. Aside from academic semesters, other demographic characteristics like age, gender, grade point average, and field interest also substantially impacted students’ moral sensitivity. Specifically, older students, married individuals, students with higher overall grade point averages, and students with a greater interest in the area demonstrated higher levels of moral sensitivity. In line with this, Shadi et al. observed that married and older nursing students are more responsible and sensitive to ethical concerns, most likely due to their greater life experience, and hence exhibit more suitable actions when addressing these issues. Furthermore, because conversations about ethical issues are interwoven into most nursing undergraduate courses, it is reasonable to expect that students who do better academically and have a larger interest in the subject will be more morally sensitive [39].

The study’s most notable conclusion was a link between the overall score and all variables of academic motivation and moral sensitivity in nursing students. In this setting, pupils with stronger academic motivation demonstrated greater moral sensitivity. Ghafari et al. found that conducting motivational interviews increases moral sensitivity among nurses working in specialized areas [40]. Similarly, Hosseini et al. found that introducing motivational activities in the workplace boosts nurses’ moral sensitivity [41]. Aflah et al. found a strong link between motivation and adherence to professional ethics at work. Academic motivation and moral sensitivity are reciprocal. Individuals with high moral sensitivity exhibit greater conscientiousness, professional devotion, and a drive to thrive. In contrast, students with higher academic motivation regard ethical performance as a key priority, encouraging them to strive for the best performance in their assigned activities while considering ethical consequences [42]. Additionally, Grabowski et al. proposed that encountering ethical difficulties in the workplace can motivate people to improve their learning. They emphasized that when confronted with moral quandaries, people become aware of their theoretical and practical deficiencies, which pushes them to get the information and skills required to overcome these obstacles [43]. In contrast, Zeng et al. defined extrinsic motivation as an individual’s completion of tasks and learning new abilities primarily for external rewards or to avoid punishment. On the other hand, internal motivation stems from the satisfaction and attraction of the activity itself. Regardless of the distinctions, both types of motivation are influenced by an individual’s moral convictions, as the fundamental goal in both cases is to do tasks to the best of one’s ability [44].

Furthermore, the latest findings of this research indicated that approximately one-third of the level of moral sensitivity in nursing students could be attributed to their intrinsic and extrinsic motivations. In this regard, Taylor elaborated on the relationship between ethics and motivation, proposing that at least two perspectives exist to understand the correlation between moral judgments and motivation. The first approach, motivational introversion, holds that generating appropriate moral judgments necessitates the motivation to act on them. The second perspective, motivational extroversion, holds that moral judgments influence our motivation to act. These moral challenges and judgments motivate people to improve their talents and excel in their careers, implying a natural relationship between them [45].

Limitations

The study’s first weakness was the possibility of incomplete or erroneous questionnaire replies, which the electronic data collection technology and the large number of questions could have influenced. To address this issue, researchers gave participants their contact information during the data collection phase, allowing them to ask any queries they had. Another disadvantage was the use of self-report techniques and the fact that participants’ psychological circumstances were not considered while measuring the study variables. This feature was outside the researchers’ control and may have influenced the results.

Conclusion

This study found an association between academic motivation and moral sensitivity in undergraduate nursing students. As a result, it is advised that students’ academic motivation be evaluated during their studies to identify those at risk of dropping out. This allows for developing and implementing specific and successful strategies for increasing their academic motivation. Given that academic motivation and clinical competence are complicated variables influenced by various factors, it is recommended that qualitative research be done to understand these linkages better.
