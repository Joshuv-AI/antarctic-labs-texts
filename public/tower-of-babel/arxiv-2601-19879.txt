Abstract
. For any integer d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 and prime power q q , we construct unexpectedly large induced matchings in the point-line incidence graph of 𝔽 q d \mathbb{F}_{q}^{d} by leveraging a new connection with the Furstenberg-Sárközy problem from arithmetic combinatorics. In particular, we significantly improve the previously well-known baselines when q q is prime, showing that 𝔽 q 2 \mathbb{F}_{q}^{2} contains matchings of size q 1.233 q^{1.233} and 𝔽 q d \mathbb{F}_{q}^{d} contains matchings of size q d − o d ​ ( 1 ) q^{d-o_{d}(1)} . These results and their proofs have several applications. First, we also obtain new constructions for finite field Nikodym sets in dimension d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , improving recent results of Tao by polynomial factors. For example, when q q is prime, we show the existence of Nikodym sets in 𝔽 q d \mathbb{F}_{q}^{d} of size q d − q d − o d ​ ( 1 ) q^{d}-q^{d-o_{d}(1)} . Second, we construct a new minimal blocking set in PG ⁡ ( 2 , q ) \mathrm{PG}(2,q) , solving a longstanding problem in finite geometry. Third, we obtain new constructions for the minimal distance problem (in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} and also in higher dimensions), improving a recent result of Logunov-Zakharov. We also obtain analogous results for general finite fields with large characteristics. In particular, in one of our constructions we introduce a new special set of points inside the norm hypersurface in 𝔽 q d \mathbb{F}_{q}^{d} , which directly generalizes the classical Hermitian unital and which may be of independent interest for applications.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Induced matchings and Nikodym sets

For an integer
d\geq 1
and prime power
q
, let
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
denote the
d
-dimensional vector space over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of order
q
. Let
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(d)}
be the bipartite graph whose left vertex set is the set of points in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, whose right vertex set is the set of affine lines in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, and where a point is adjacent to a line if and only if it lies on that line. The object that we will be studying in this paper is the following parameter.

Definition 1.1

(Induced matchings)
.

Let
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)
be the maximum size of an induced matching in
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(d)}
.

Equivalently,
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)
denotes the largest
m
for which there exist pairs

(p_{1},\ell_{1}),\dots,(p_{m},\ell_{m})

with
p_{i}\in\ell_{j}
if and only if
i=j
. We will be interested in the regime where the dimension
d
is fixed and
q
is large.

The problem of estimating
\mathrm{IM}(2,q)
was recently highlighted by Cohen–Pohoata–Zakharov
[
11
]
, in a surprising connection with the Heilbronn triangle problem. As noted in
[
11
, §1.5]
, a Szemerédi–Trotter type incidence theorem of Vinh

[
45
]
implies the general upper bound

\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\leq q^{3/2}+q.

(1)

When
q=p^{2}
is a square, (
1
) is sharp up to constants, because of the so-called Hermitian unitals defined on the affine plane by

P:=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}:\ a^{p+1}+b^{p+1}=1\}.

(2)

It is well-known that
|P|=p^{3}-p=q^{3/2}-q^{1/2}
. Through each
(a,b)\in P

there is a unique tangent line
\ell_{a,b}
meeting
P
only at
(a,b)
; in affine coordinates one may take

\ell_{a,b}=\{(a,b)+t(b^{p},-a^{p}):t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}:\ a^{p}x+b^{p}y=1\}.

(3)

Thus the pairs
\{((a,b),\ell_{a,b}):(a,b)\in P\}
form an induced matching in
\mathcal{I}_{q}
of size
|P|
, giving

\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\geq q^{3/2}-q^{1/2}
. Hermitian unitals have recently been featured in several constructions in extremal combinatorics, most notably in the recent paper of Mattheus–Verstraëte
[
33
]
on the asymptotics of the off-diagonal Ramsey
R(4,t)
.

That being said, the unital construction is not applicable when
q
is not a perfect square, and the best known lower bound for
\mathrm{IM}(2,q)
prior to this work has been of the form
\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\gg q\log q
1
1

1

Throughout the paper, we will write
f\gg g
or
f=\Omega(g)
or if there exists a positive constant
C
such that
|f(x)|\geq Cg(x)
for all
x
. We will also write
f\ll g
or
f=O(g)
if
g\gg f
, and
f\asymp g
if
f\gg g
and
f\ll g
both hold. We say
f=o(g)
if for any
\varepsilon>0
,
|f(x)|\leq\varepsilon g(x)
for all sufficiently large
x
.
. This is achieved by either choosing the point set randomly or lifting a clique from the Paley graph (see Section
2
for the important backstory).

Our foundational result improves upon this “baseline bound” by a polynomial factor for all prime
q
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For all primes
q
, we have

\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\gg q^{1.2334}.

For
d\geq 3
, no analogue of (
1
) is known, and it is in fact an open problem to show
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)=o(q^{d})
. This question is typically stated in a different language, in terms of so-called
Nikodym sets
. We next formally define Nikodym sets and state their connections.

Fix
d\geq 2
and a prime power
q
.
For
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
and
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
,
write

\ell(x,v):=\{x+tv:\ t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}

for the affine line through
x
with direction
v
, and

\ell(x,v)^{\!*}:=\ell(x,v)\setminus\{x\}

for the corresponding punctured line.

Definition 1.3

(Nikodym set)
.

A set
N\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
is a
Nikodym set
if for every
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
there exists

v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\ell(x,v)^{\!*}\subset N
. Let
\mathrm{Nikodym}(d,q)
be the size of the smallest Nikodym set in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
.

Nikodym sets are a finite-field analogue of the classical Euclidean Nikodym problem, and are closely related
(via projective transformations) to Kakeya sets. See, for instance, Dvir’s beautiful survey
[
13
]
and the references therein.

From this perspective, point–line matchings can be regarded as a weaker version of Nikodym sets, where only points outside
N
are required to have a punctured line contained in
N
.

Definition 1.4

(Weak Nikodym set)
.

A set
N\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
is a
weak Nikodym set
if for every
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\setminus N

there exists
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\ell(x,v)^{\!*}\subset N
.

To keep a clear picture, we summarize the relationship between Nikodym sets and weak Nikodym sets in the following simple proposition. See Section
7
for the complete proof.

Proposition 1.5
.

1) Any Nikodym set is a weak Nikodym set, but not vice versa.

2) A set in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
is a weak Nikodym set if and only if its complement is contained in an induced matching in
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(d)}
.

3) If a set
N\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
is a weak Nikodym set, then the set
N\times\mathbb{F}_{q}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d+1}
is a Nikodym set.

Part 2) and 3) shows the implications

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)=o(q^{d})\Longrightarrow\mathrm{Nikodym}(d,q)=q^{d}-o(q^{d})\Longrightarrow\mathrm{IM}(d-1,q)=o(q^{d-1}).

The problem of showing
\mathrm{Nikodym}(d,q)=q^{d}-o(q^{d})
is a well-known open problem in the area, see for example
[
30
]
.

We will next establish new lower bounds for the quantity
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)
when
d\geq 3
, which due to Proposition
1.5
will also come with new constructions for Nikodym sets in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
.

1.2.
Large induced matchings in higher dimensions

In higher dimensions, the “baseline bound” is
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\gg q^{d-1}\log q
. We will discuss the history of this bound in the next subsection. Before our work, the only known improvement upon the baseline bound when
q
is a prime is a constant factor improvement due to a recent result of Tao
[
43
]
on Nikodym sets.

Before we talk more about Nikodym sets, let us start with the simple observation that by Proposition
1.5
(part 2 and 3), we can already derive the inequality

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\geq q\mathrm{IM}(d-1,q).

(4)

Hence, for example, Theorem
1.2
already gives us a polynomial improvement of
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\geq q^{d-0.7776}
over the trivial bound when
q
is a prime.

Our next results significantly improve upon such bounds in high dimensions. In particular, when
q
is a prime, we show that as
d\to\infty
, the exponent can be improved to
d-o_{d}(1)
.

Theorem 1.6
.

There exist constants
\varepsilon_{d}>0
with
\varepsilon_{d}\ll(\log d)^{-1}
, such that for any dimension
d
and prime
q
, we have

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\gg_{d}q^{d-\varepsilon_{d}}.

In other words, while Theorem
1.6
does not refute the possibility that
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)=o(q^{d})
holds, it shows that a dimension–independent power saving is not possible.

1.3.
The story for prime powers
q

The story here technically starts with the work of Szőnyi et. al.
[
40
]
who showed that for any
k\geq 2
and
q=p^{k}
one can use ovoids to construct a minimal blocking set in
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
of size
p^{k+1}+1
. Their construction contains only one point at infinity, and removing that point immediately gives a point–line matching of size
p^{k+1}
, proving
\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\geq q^{1+1/k}
. When
k=2
, this recovers the lower bound
\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\geq q^{3/2}
from earlier. For general
k\geq 3
, the inequality (
4
) readily gives

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\geq q^{d-1+1/k}.

(5)

As a first new observation, we record that Theorem
1.6
above directly implies an improvement for prime powers with small exponent compared to the dimension.

Corollary 1.7
.

For
s\geq 2
and
d\geq 3
, there exist constants
\varepsilon_{d,s}>0
with
\varepsilon_{d,s}\ll s/\log d
, such that for any prime power
q=p^{s}
with
p
a prime greater than
s
, we have

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\gg_{d,s}q^{d-\varepsilon_{d,s}}.

More substantially, we can also show a large polynomial improvement over (
5
), for any power that is less than the dimension.

Theorem 1.8
.

For every positive integers
k
and
d
with
d\geq k\geq 2
, if
q=q_{0}^{k}
is a prime power with prime
q_{0}
, then we have

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\ \gg_{d}\ q^{d-1/k}.

Instead of considering ovoids, the proof of Theorem
1.8
will proceed by constructing a set of points inside a norm hypersurface, which will directly generalize the Hermitian unital construction from (
2
) and (
3
). For these reasons, we believe this construction is interesting for independent reasons and should have more applications. We discuss the proofs of Corollary
1.7
and of Theorem
1.8
in Section
5
.

Finally, by combining Corollary
1.7
, Theorem
1.8
and a new argument, we achieve bounds of the form
q^{d-o_{d}(1)}
whenever the base of
q
is much larger than the exponent.

Theorem 1.9
.

There exists constants
\varepsilon_{d}\ll(\log\log d)^{-1}
, such that if
q=p^{t}
is a prime power, then

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\gg_{d}q^{d-\varepsilon_{d}-2\log t/\log p}.

1.4.
Small Nikodym sets

Combining our point–line matching construction with Proposition
1.5
, we can immediately generate improved constructions of Nikodym sets in dimensions
\geq 3
. Before we make this more precise, let us first review the literature on this problem.

For
d=2
, the best known lower and upper bounds are of the form

q^{2}-q^{3/2}-1\leq\mathrm{Nikodym}(2,q)\leq\begin{cases}q^{2}-q^{3/2}+O(q\log q),\text{$q\ $is a perfect square}\\
q^{2}-O(q\log q),\text{$q\ $ is not a perfect square}\end{cases}.

For
d\geq 3
, there is a significant gap between lower and upper bounds on
\mathrm{Nikodym}(d,q)
. A sequence of works starting from Dvir’s breakthrough
[
2
,
12
,
30
]
showed the general lower bound

\mathrm{Nikodym}(d,q)\geq\frac{q^{d}}{2^{d-1}}+O(q^{d-1}).

If the characteristic
q_{0}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is bounded by a constant, then a much stronger lower bound is known
[
20
]
: for some
\epsilon=\epsilon(q_{0},d)>0
, we have

\mathrm{Nikodym}(d,q)\geq q^{d}-O(q^{(1-\epsilon)d}).

The problem of upper bounds is highlighted in recent works
[
14
,
43
]
of Tao in collaboration with Georgiev, Gómez-Serrano, Wagner and the AI tools
AlphaEvolve
and
Deep Think
operated by Google DeepMind. It is the first of 67 mathematical problems considered by the collaboration. Tao
[
43
]
first noted that for any
q,d_{1},d_{2}
we have the “product construction”

\mathrm{Nikodym}(d_{1}+d_{2},q)\leq\mathrm{Nikodym}(d_{1},q)\mathrm{Nikodym}(d_{2},q).

By taking the product of
2
-dimensional Nikodym sets, we have

\mathrm{Nikodym}(d,q)\leq q^{d}-\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor q^{d-1/2}+O(q^{d-1}\log q),\text{when $q$ is a perfect square}.

The focus of Tao’s paper is on non-square
q
with unbounded characteristics. Tao notes that a simple probabilistic argument, i.e. picking each point independently at random, shows that for every odd prime power
q
, we have

\mathrm{Nikodym}(d,q)\leq q^{d}-(d-1+o(1))q^{d-1}\log q.

Via a combination of
AlphaEvolve
(for
q
prime),
Deep Think
, and human guidance, Tao identified a construction based on deleting random quadratic varieties, which improves the baseline by a constant factor

\mathrm{Nikodym}(d,q)\leq q^{d}-\left(\frac{d-2}{\log 2}+1+o(1)\right)q^{d-1}\log q.

As an application of our results on point–line matchings, we improve Tao’s bound by polynomial factors when
q
is a prime or most prime powers. Again, the only regime where we cannot improve Tao’s bound is when
q
is a high power of a small prime.

Theorem 1.10
.

1) For
d=3
and every odd prime power
q=p^{t}
, we have

\mathrm{Nikodym}(3,q)\leq q^{3}-\Omega_{t}(q^{2.1167})

2) For sufficiently large
d\geq 3
and
q=p^{t}
a prime power, we have

\mathrm{Nikodym}(d,q)\leq q^{d}-\Omega_{d}\left(q^{d-\epsilon_{d}-2\log t/\log p}\right).

where
\epsilon_{d}\ll(\log\log d)^{-1}
.

However, as our construction requires going up one dimension, this method does not construct small Nikodym sets in dimension
2
. Nevertheless, there is an embedding trick one can use, taking advantage of additional extra properties of our high-dimensional matchings, that will allow us to also get the following.

Theorem 1.11
.

There exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that for prime
q
, we have

\mathrm{Nikodym}(2,q)\leq q^{2}-q^{1+c}.

1.5.
Minimal blocking sets

Let
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
denote the projective plane over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We say
B\subset\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
is a
blocking set
if for every affine line
\ell\subset\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
,
\ell\cap B\neq\emptyset
. We say
B
is a minimal blocking set, if
B^{\prime}
is not a blocking set for every proper subset
B^{\prime}\subset B
. Equivalently, every point
of a minimal blocking set is
essential
: for each
p\in B
there exists a
tangent line

\ell
through
p
such that
\ell\cap B=\{p\}
. Blocking sets and their extremal variants have a long history
and appear throughout finite geometry, coding theory, and incidence combinatorics; see, for instance,
the survey of Blokhuis
[
6
]
.
In particular, Bruen–Thas
[
7
]
showed that minimal blocking sets can have size on the

q^{3/2}
scale (and that this scale is sharp when
q
is a square, as witnessed by the Hermitian unital).

The induced-matching problem considered in this paper is in fact closely related to the “tangent line”
characterization above: the affine part of a minimal blocking set automatically supports an induced matching. In a remarkable paper
[
39
]
, Szőnyi constructed large minimal blocking sets in
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
using (what can be rephrased as) independent sets in Paley-type
Cayley graphs. This construction will be the starting point of our proof of Theorem
1.2
. In Section
2
, we will start by isolating the induced-matching aspect of this lift, and then observe that using dense sets without square differences in the integers rather than Paley graphs leads to significantly larger point-line induced matchings. This will be the content of Theorem
1.2
. Subsequently, we will generalize this new lift to higher dimensions in Theorems
1.6
and
1.9
.

Before we get to that, in this section we would like to record the following simple connection between minimal blocking sets and Nikodym sets in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
.

Proposition 1.12
.

Let
B(2,q)
denote the maximum cardinality of a minimal blocking set in
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
. Then,

B(2,q)\geq q^{2}-\mathrm{Nikodym}(2,q).

Combining this with Theorem
1.11
, we settle a longstanding problem in finite geometry: We construct a polynomially larger blocking set in
\operatorname{PG}(2,q)
when
q
is a prime.

Theorem 1.13
.

There exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that the following holds: for every prime
q
, there exists a minimal blocking set in
\operatorname{PG}(2,q)
of size at least
\Omega(q^{1+c})
, i.e.

B(2,q)\gg q^{1+c}.

1.6.
The minimal distance problem

Determining
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)
may be viewed as a finite-field analogue for Euclidean point–line separation problems. In particular, in their most recent work on the Heilbronn triangle problem
[
11
]
, Cohen, Pohoata and Zakharov introduced the following
minimal distance problem
:
given pairs
\{p_{i}\in\ell_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with
p_{i}\in[0,1]^{2}
and
\ell_{i}
a line through
p_{i}
, what is the maximum value of

\delta:=\min_{i\neq j}d(p_{i},\ell_{j}),

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes Euclidean distance. Observe that this can be easily rephrased induced matching problem between points and
\delta
–tubes. In
[
11
]
, they showed that necessarily
\delta\lesssim n^{-2/3+o(1)}
,
which in turn implied the current record of
\Delta(n)\lesssim n^{-7/6+o(1)}
for the Heilbronn triangle problem
(
\Delta(n)
being the smallest triangle area determined by
n
points in the unit square); see
[
11
]
and the discussion therein.

In
[
31
]
, Maldague, Wang and Zakharov also recently proposed higher-dimensional analogues of the minimal distance problem for point–line pairs, which we denote by
\mathrm{PL}_{d}(\gamma)
.

Definition 1.14
.

Let
\mathrm{PL}_{d}(\gamma)
be the following proposition: for every large
n
and any points
p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}\in[0,1]^{d}
and lines
\ell_{1},\cdots,\ell_{n}
with
p_{i}\in\ell_{i}
, there exists
i\neq j
with

d_{\infty}(p_{i},\ell_{j})\leq n^{-\frac{1}{d-\gamma}+o(1)}.

Here the
d_{\infty}
–distance between point and line is defined as

d_{\infty}(p,\ell):=\min_{q\in\ell}\lVert{p-q\rVert}_{\infty}.

This choice of norm is convenient for product-type constructions and is standard in this context (recall that all norms are equivalent up to dimension-dependent constants). We also note that the statement
\mathrm{PL}_{d}(0)
is trivially true in any dimension
d
: it simply reflects the fact that among any
n
points inside
[0,1]^{d}
there always exist two points within distance
2n^{-1/d}
from each other (a simple consequence of the pigeonhole principle).

In dimension
2
, the main result from
[
11
]
established precisely the validity of
\mathrm{PL}_{2}(1/2)
. In
[
31
]
, Maldague, Wang, and Zakharov recently extended the methods from
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
and also established the
3
–dimensional analogue
\mathrm{PL}_{3}(\gamma_{0})
, for some absolute constant
\gamma_{0}>0
. In turn, this implied the first polynomial improvement for the Heilbronn triangle problem in three dimensions. In dimension
d\geq 4
, determining whether
\mathrm{PL}_{d}(\gamma)
holds for any
\gamma>0
is an open problem with connection to higher–dimensional Heilbronn triangle problems.

In this paper, we find new constructions that show
\mathrm{PL}_{d}(\gamma)
cannot hold for certain
\gamma
. Prior to this work, the best result in this direction comes from a recent elegant construction of Logunov and Zakharov
[
29
]
. In this paper, they introduce a self-affine (“fractal-like”) family of point–line pairs
(p_{i},\ell_{i})
in
[0,1]^{2}
with

d_{\infty}(p_{i},\ell_{j})\ \gg\ n^{\eta-1},\quad\forall i\neq j

(6)

for some absolute, yet inexplicit, constant
\eta>0
. This translates to the fact that
\mathrm{PL}_{2}(1-\eta^{\prime})
fails for any constant
\eta^{\prime}\in(0,\frac{\eta}{1-\eta})
. We note that

\mathrm{PL}_{d}(\gamma)\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathrm{PL}_{d-1}(\gamma),

(7)

since one can embed a configuration in
[0,1]^{d-1}
into a coordinate hyperplane in
[0,1]^{d}
and take well–separated translates in the new coordinate. Hence (
6
) also implies that
\mathrm{PL}_{d}(1-\eta^{\prime})
fails in every dimension
d\geq 2
.

Our contribution is a general mechanism for producing well-separated Euclidean point–line configurations from
large
integer
point–line matchings with
bounded slopes
.

Theorem 1.15
.

Fix
d\geq 1
and let
N,M,L\geq 1
be integers with
N\geq ML
.
Let
P\subset[N]^{d}\times[M]\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
be a set of lattice points.
Assume that for each
p\in P
we are given an integer direction vector

s_{p}=(u_{p},1)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}\qquad\text{with}\qquad\|u_{p}\|_{\infty}\leq L,

such that for all distinct
p,p^{\prime}\in P
one has

p^{\prime}\notin p+\mathbb{R}s_{p}

which, due to the last coordinate of
s_{p}
being
1
, is equivalent to
p^{\prime}\notin p+\mathbb{Z}s_{p}
. Then there exists a set of points
p_{1},\cdots,p_{|P|}\in[0,1]^{d+1}
and lines
\ell_{1},\cdots,\ell_{|P|}
with
p_{i}\in\ell_{i}
such that for each
i\neq j
, we have

d_{\infty}(p_{i},\ell_{j})\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2N}.

Using this framework, we can pass from our finite-field induced matchings (after choosing convenient integer representatives)
to counterexamples for various
\mathrm{PL}_{d}(\gamma)
. In dimension
2
, using our proof of Theorem
1.2
, we significantly improve on the Logunov and Zakharov’s result. Furthermore, we discover a novel connection between the minimal distance problem and the Furstenberg–Sárközy problem.

Corollary 1.16
.

(1)

\mathrm{PL}_{2}(0.7666)
is false.

(2)

If
\mathrm{PL}_{2}(0.5+c)
is true for any
c>0
, then any square–difference–free subset of
[N]
has size at most
N^{1-c+o(1)}
.

We refer to Section
10
for a discussion of the Furstenberg–Sárközy problem and more context around Part 2) of Corollary
1.16
.

In higher dimensions, we can leverage the proof of Theorem
1.6
to prove the following.

Corollary 1.17
.

For any
\gamma>0
, there exists some
d_{0}=d_{0}(\gamma)
such that
\mathrm{PL}_{d_{0}}(\gamma)
fails. Quantitatively, we have
d_{0}(\gamma)=\exp(O(\gamma^{-1}))
.

By (
7
), Corollary
1.17
also implies that
\mathrm{PL}_{d}(\gamma)
fails for every

2.
Lifting Paley graphs and Ruzsa sets

In this section, we discuss the “baseline” lower bound
\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\gg q\log q
. Now there are a few different constructions of induced matchings of size
q\log q
in
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(2)}
, but we would like to show an argument inspired by an old paper of Szőnyi
[
39
]
. Then we shall use a new (but related) argument to prove our first Theorem
1.2
. This new argument will serve as the foundation of almost all our constructions.

First, recall that if
q
is a prime power with characteristic
q_{0}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, the
Paley graph

G_{q}
is the (undirected) graph on vertex set
\mathbb{F}_{q}
in which
distinct
x,y
are adjacent iff
x-y
is a nonzero square. It is well known that
G_{q}
is self-complementary (multiplication by a fixed nonsquare induces an isomorphism
to the complement), and hence the independence number
\alpha(G_{q})
and the clique number
\omega(G_{q})
satisfy the relation
\alpha(G_{q})=\omega(G_{q})
. See for example
[
37
]
for more details.

In
[
39
]
, Szőnyi observed that it is possible to use large independent sets in
G_{q}
in order to construct large
minimal blocking sets
in the finite projective plane
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
. From a minimal blocking set, it is then easy to construct an induced matching in the point-line incidence graph
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(2)}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
q
is a prime power with characteristic
q_{0}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, then

\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\ \geq\ q\,\alpha(G_{q})\;=\;q\,\omega(G_{q}).

Proof.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}
be an independent set in
G_{q}
, i.e.
(I-I)\cap(D_{2}^{\times})=\emptyset
. We define the set

P=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}:\ x+y^{2}\in I\right\}.

Clearly,
|P|=q|I|
. For each such point
(x,y)\in P
, let
\ell_{x,y}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
be the line

\ell_{x,y}:=(x,y)+\{\,\lambda(-2y,1):\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\,\}=(x-2\lambda y,\ y+\lambda).

Note that
(x,y)\in\ell_{x,y}
. Crucially, note also that all the points of
\ell_{x,y}
satisfy

(x-2\lambda y)+(y+\lambda)^{2}=(x+y^{2})+\lambda^{2}=t+\lambda^{2},

for some
t\in I
. However, then
t+\lambda^{2}
can’t be in
I
unless
\lambda=0
. This means that the line
\ell_{x,y}
intersects
P
only at the point
(x,y)
. We conclude that
\{((x,y),\ell_{x,y})\}_{(x,y)\in P}
is an induced matching in
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(2)}
.
∎

Some history.

Determining the clique number
\omega(G_{q})
of the Paley graph is a classical problem at the intersection of several topics in combinatorics and number theory. The Delsarte–Hoffman eigenvalue method yields the standard “square-root barrier”

\omega(G_{q})\leq\sqrt{q}.

See, for instance,
[
47
]
for all the relevant background.
When
q=q_{0}^{2}
, this barrier is sharp: indeed
\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}^{\times}\subset(\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}^{2}}^{\times})^{2}
, so the subfield
\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}^{2}}
spans a clique of size
q_{0}=\sqrt{q}
, and hence
\omega(G_{q_{0}^{2}})=\sqrt{q}
.
For prime
q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, Hanson–Petridis proved the best known constant-factor improvement,

\omega(G_{q})\leq\sqrt{q/2}+1

[
22
]
. Thus, Proposition
2.1
highlights the difficulty of asymptotically improving the upper bound
\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\ll q^{3/2}
when
q
is prime. We will discuss more about this problem in Section
10
.

On the lower-bound side, a standard application of Ramsey’s theorem produces the lower bound
\omega(G_{q})=\Omega(\log q)
(see for example
[
9
]
), and
work of Graham–Ringrose implies that for infinitely many primes
q
one has

\omega(G_{q})=\Omega(\log q\,\log\log\log q)

[
18
]
, improving to

\Omega(\log q\,\log\log q)
under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (see, e.g.,
[
34
]
).
Therefore, Proposition
2.1
produces the lower bound
\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\gg q\log q
for all primes
q
, and improves it to
\mathrm{IM}(2,q)=\Omega(q\log q\log\log\log q)
for infinitely many primes
q
.

We now discuss the polynomial improvement over Szőnyi’s construction.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
. The idea is that instead of lifting an independent set in the Paley graph, it is more efficient to lift a so-called
square–difference–free
set over
\mathbb{Z}
. A set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}
is square–difference–free if for any distinct
a,a^{\prime}\in A
, the difference
a-a^{\prime}
is not a square. The problem of finding the largest size of a square–difference–free of
[N]
is known as the Furstenberg–Sárkőzy problem
[
15
,
38
]
. While there have been recent exciting developments regarding the upper bound
[
19
]
, here we will only need an older non–trivial lower bound construction due to Ruzsa
[
36
]
, which was subsequently improved slightly by Beigel–Gasarch and Lewko
[
1
,
27
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists a square–difference–free subset of
[N]
with size at least
N^{0.7334}
.

We will use such a set to construct a large point-line induced matching in
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(2)}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
q
be a prime, and
A
be a square–difference–free subset of
[\lfloor q/10\rfloor]
. Then we have

\mathrm{IM}(2,q)=\Omega(q^{1/2}|A|).

One can regard Proposition
2.3
as a direct analogue of Proposition
2.1
, where we lift a square–difference–free subset of
[q/10]
, instead of an independent set
I\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}
in the Paley graph, to an induced matching in the point-line incidence graph
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(2)}
.

Proof.

Let
N=\lfloor q/3\rfloor
and
M=\lfloor\sqrt{q}/2\rfloor
. We define the set

P=\{(x,y)\in[N]\times[M]:2x-y^{2}\in A\}

and for each
p=(x,y)\in P
, define the associated line
\ell_{p}=\{(x,y)+t(y,1):t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

We claim that
\{(p,\ell_{p}):p\in P\}
is an induced matching in the point–line incidence graph of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
. For the sake of contradiction, assume that distinct
p=(x,y)\in P
and
p^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in P
satisfies
p^{\prime}\in\ell_{p}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then there exists
t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that

(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\equiv(x+ty,\ y+t)\pmod{q}.

In particular, from the second coordinate equality we have
t=y^{\prime}-y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, which after substitution in the first equality gives

x^{\prime}\equiv x+y(y^{\prime}-y)\pmod{q}.

Because
x,x^{\prime}\in[M]
and
y,y^{\prime}\in[N]
, we have

|x+y(y^{\prime}-y)-x^{\prime}|\leq|x-x^{\prime}|+|y|\,|y^{\prime}-y|\leq(M-1)+(N-1)^{2}<\frac{q}{3}+\frac{q}{4}<q.

so we have the equality over
\mathbb{Z}

x+y(y^{\prime}-y)=x^{\prime}.

(8)

Next, let
2x-y^{2}=a\in A
and
2x^{\prime}-y^{\prime 2}=a^{\prime}\in A
(recall that
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in P)
. Using (
8
), it follows that

a-a^{\prime}=2(x-x^{\prime})+y^{\prime 2}-y^{2}=2y(y-y^{\prime})+y^{\prime 2}-y^{2}=(y-y^{\prime})^{2},

which is a perfect square. Since
a,a^{\prime}\in A
and
A
is square–difference–free, we have
a=a^{\prime}
, so
y=y^{\prime}
and
x=x^{\prime}
, contradiction.

We have proved
\ell_{p}\cap P=\{p\}
for every
p\in P
. In particular, distinct points of
P
give distinct lines,
and the pairs
\{(p,\ell_{p})\}_{p\in P}
form an induced matching. Hence
\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\ \geq\ |P|
. It remains to estimate the size of
A
. Recall that
A\subset[q/10]
. For each
a\in A
and
y\in[M]
with the same parity, we have
(a+y^{2})/2\in[N]
, so the point
((a+y^{2})/2,y)
is an element of
P
. Therefore, we obtain

\left|P\right|\geq(N-1)/2\cdot|A|=\Omega(q^{1/2}|A|)

as desired.
∎

Theorem
1.2
now follows directly from Lemma
2.2
and Proposition
2.3
.

3.
Lifting to higher dimensions

Next, we show how Szőnyi’s Paley lifting can be generalized to higher dimensions, leading us to the proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Fix an integer
d\geq 2
. Write

D_{d}:=\{t^{d}:t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad D_{d}^{\times}:=D_{d}\setminus\{0\}.

The baseline lower bound
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\gg q^{d-1}\log q
comes from the following more general construction.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})>d
.
Let
I\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}
satisfy

(I-I)\cap D_{d}^{\times}=\emptyset.

Then we have

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{(d-1)!}\,|I|\,(q-1)^{d-1}.

Proof.

Set

\Phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}):=x_{1}+x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{d}.

Our goal is to construct a set of points
P\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
and, for each
p\in P
, a line
\ell_{p}
such that
\ell_{p}\cap P=\{p\}
. The pairs
(p,\ell_{p})
would then form an induced matching in
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(d)}
of size
|P|
.

To do so, we will first build
(d-1)
-tuples
(x_{2},\dots,x_{d})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{d-1}
together with a direction vector

v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{d-1}\times\{1\}

(a vector
v
which will be a function of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
) such that for all
x_{1},\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we have

\Phi(x_{1}+\lambda v_{1},\dots,x_{d}+\lambda v_{d})=\Phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})+\lambda^{d}.

(9)

Expanding by the binomial theorem, (
9
) is equivalent to requiring that all coefficients
of
\lambda^{r}
for all
1\leq r\leq d-1
vanish, while the
\lambda^{d}
coefficient equals
1
(the coefficient of
\lambda^{d}
is
v_{d}^{d}=1
).

For
1\leq r\leq d-1
, the
\lambda^{r}
coefficient equals

v_{r}^{r}\;+\;\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}\binom{i}{r}\,x_{i}^{\,i-r}v_{i}^{r},

so we want

v_{r}^{r}=-\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}\binom{i}{r}\,x_{i}^{\,i-r}v_{i}^{r}.

(10)

We choose the variables in decreasing order
r=d-1,d-2,\dots,2
, and handle
r=1
at the end. When
r=d-1
, the sum in (
10
) contains only the term
i=d
. This gives

v_{d-1}^{\,d-1}=-{d\choose d-1}x_{d}^{\,1}v_{d}^{\,d-1}=-dx_{d},

since
v_{d}=1
. This provides the base case of the recursion.

For
2\leq r\leq d-2
, now let us assume that the variables
x_{r+2},\dots,x_{d}
and
v_{r+1},\dots,v_{d}
have already been chosen, and are nonzero. Then in (
10
) the only occurrence of the new variable
x_{r+1}
comes from the term with
i=r+1
. For the reader’s convenience, we single out this term

-\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}\binom{i}{r}\,x_{i}^{\,i-r}v_{i}^{r}=-\binom{r+1}{r}\,x_{r+1}\,v_{r+1}^{r}\;-\;\sum_{i=r+2}^{d}\binom{i}{r}\,x_{i}^{\,i-r}v_{i}^{r}.

This allows us to emphasize that the right-hand side of (
10
) is an affine-linear function of
x_{r+1}
of the form

A\,x_{r+1}+B,\ \ \ \ \ \text{where}\qquad A:=-(r+1)v_{r+1}^{r},\quad B:=-\sum_{i=r+2}^{d}\binom{i}{r}\,x_{i}^{\,i-r}v_{i}^{r}.

Note that
B
is already determined by the previously chosen variables.
Moreover
A\neq 0
because
v_{r+1}\neq 0
and
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})>d\geq r+1
.
Therefore the map
x_{r+1}\mapsto Ax_{r+1}+B
is a bijection of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, so as
x_{r+1}
ranges over
\mathbb{F}_{q}

the right-hand side of (
10
) ranges over all of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

In particular, we may choose
x_{r+1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
so that the right-hand side lies in
D_{r}^{\times}
(recall
D_{r}^{\times}=\left\{t^{r}:t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\right\}
),
and then pick
v_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
satisfying (
10
).
Note that

\left|D_{r}^{\times}\right|=\frac{q-1}{\gcd(r,q-1)}\geq\frac{q-1}{r},

giving at least
(q-1)/r
valid choices of
x_{r+1}
for which the right-hand side
of (
10
) lies in
D_{r}^{\times}
. For each of these, we can then choose
v_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
satisfying (
10
).

After choosing
v_{2},\dots,v_{d}
, we choose
v_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
so that the
\lambda^{1}
coefficient vanishes;
this is always possible because the
r=1
equation (
10
) is linear in
v_{1}
.

We have thus obtained a set
S\subset(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{d-1}
of admissible tuples
(x_{2},\dots,x_{d})
, and for each
(x_{2},\dots,x_{d})\in S
we fixed one corresponding direction
v(x_{2},\dots,x_{d})
satisfying (
9
). Moreover, we have also shown along the way that

|S|\ \geq\ \prod_{r=1}^{d-1}\frac{q-1}{r}=\frac{(q-1)^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}.

We can define finally the point set

P=\left\{p=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}:\ (x_{2},\cdots,x_{t})\in S\ \ \text{and}\ \ \Phi(p)=x_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}x_{i}^{i}\in I\right\}.

By construction, we have

|P|=|I|\,|S|\ \geq\ \frac{1}{(d-1)!}\,|I|\,(q-1)^{d-1}.

For
p=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{t})\in P
arising from
(x_{2},\dots,x_{d})\in S
, define the affine line

\ell_{p}:=\{p+\lambda v(x_{2},\dots,x_{d}):\ \lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}.

By (
9
), for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
we have

\Phi(p+\lambda v)=\Phi(p)+\lambda^{d}
.
If
p^{\prime}\in\ell_{p}\cap P
, then
\Phi(p^{\prime}),\Phi(p)\in I
and

\Phi(p^{\prime})-\Phi(p)=\lambda^{d}\in D_{d}.

By the hypothesis
(I-I)\cap D_{d}^{\times}=\emptyset
, this forces
\lambda=0
, hence
p^{\prime}=p
.
Thus
\ell_{p}\cap P=\{p\}
for every
p\in P
, and
\{(p,\ell_{p})\}_{p\in P}
is an induced matching of size
|P|
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The condition from Proposition
3.1
simply means that
I
is an independent set in the Cayley graph
G_{q,d}
on the additive group of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, generated by the nonzero
d
th powers.
Equivalently, this is the graph on vertex set
\mathbb{F}_{q}
in which two distinct elements are adjacent if and only if their difference is a nonzero
d
th power. When
q
is a prime power, this Cayley graph is highly symmetric and behaves in many respects like the usual Paley graph.

When
q
is a prime power,
G_{q,d}
is a normal Cayley graph and its spectrum is controlled by classical
character-sum estimates; in particular, all nontrivial eigenvalues are
O_{d}(\sqrt{q})
.
The Delsarte–Hoffman bound then gives

\alpha(G_{q,d})\ \ll_{d}\ \sqrt{q},

matching the familiar “square-root barrier” for Paley-type graphs.
Moreover, in many extension-field situations this is sharp up to constants; for example, when
q=q_{0}^{2}
and

D_{d}^{\times}
contains
\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}^{\times}
(equivalently
\gcd(d,q-1)\mid(q_{0}+1)
), the subfield
\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}

forms a clique of size
q_{0}
, and by scaling by an element outside
D_{d}^{\times}
one obtains an independent set of
size
q_{0}
as well (see, e.g.,
[
47
]
).

In the prime-field case, much less is known about the extremal behaviour of
G_{q,d}
.
First, if
\gcd(d,q-1)=1
then
D_{d}^{\times}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, so the condition
(I-I)\cap D_{d}^{\times}=\emptyset
forces
|I|=1
,
and Proposition
3.1
yields only the trivial lower bound
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\gg_{d}q^{d-1}
.
Otherwise
\gcd(d,q-1)>1
, so
D_{d}^{\times}
is a proper multiplicative subgroup.
In the undirected situation (
-1\in D_{d}^{\times}
), one can convert cliques into independent sets by dilation:
if
C
is a clique and
\xi\notin D_{d}^{\times}
, then
\xi C
is an independent set (since

\xi D_{d}^{\times}
is a disjoint coset of
D_{d}^{\times}
).
Thus
\alpha(G_{q,d})\geq\omega(G_{q,d})
, and combining this with the standard Ramsey bound

\max\{\alpha(G_{q,d}),\omega(G_{q,d})\}\gg\log q
produces an independent set
I\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}
of size
\gg\log q
.
Consequently Proposition
3.1
gives the baseline estimate (for such primes
q
)

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\ \gg_{d}\ q^{d-1}\log q.

Obtaining substantially larger sets
I
in prime fields appears comparable in difficulty to the classical
problem of finding large independent sets in Paley graphs, and Proposition
3.1
by itself does not lead to any substantial improvements for
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)
.

On the other hand, Proposition
3.1
naturally leads to the idea of a “higher power analogue” of Lemma
2.3
. We next establish such a result and prove Theorem
1.6
.

We will mostly work with integers, reducing modulo
q
only in the final step. All equations in the rest of this section, unless otherwise indicated, hold over
\mathbb{Z}
.

We need several preliminary results.

Lemma 3.3

(Waring’s problem)
.

For each positive integer
k
, there exists a positive integer
G(k)
and a threshold
T(k)
such that for any non–negative integer
n\geq T(k)
, the equation in
G(k)
variables

x_{1}^{k}+\cdots+x_{G(k)}^{k}=n

has a solution in non–negative integers.

Classical results of Vinogradov
[
46
]
show that we can take
G(k)=O(k\log k)
, which has remained the best known upper bound to date, up to lower order terms. See for example the survey of Vaughan and Wooley
[
44
]
for further progress.

The next lemma is the higher power analogue of Lemma
2.2
. It is established alongside Lemma
2.2
(with exponent
0.7330\cdots
) in Ruzsa’s paper
[
36
]
, and we reproduce its short proof here for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
N
be a positive integer, and let
D_{k}(N)
be the size of the largest
A\subset[N]
such that
A-A
avoids non–zero
k
–th powers. If
p
is the smallest prime congruent to
1
modulo
2k
, then we have

D_{k}(N)=\Omega_{k}\left(N^{c_{k}}\right)

where

c_{k}=1-\frac{1}{k}+\frac{\log k}{k\log p}.

In particular, if
2k+1
is a prime, then we have

c_{k}=1-\frac{1}{k}+\frac{\log k}{k\log(2k+1)}\geq 1-\frac{2}{k\log k}.

Proof.

Let
Q
be the set of nonzero
k
-th powers in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. As
p\equiv 1\bmod{2k}
, we have
Q=-Q
and
|Q|=\frac{p-1}{k}
. By the greedy algorithm, we can sample
A_{p}=\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{k}\}
in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
such that
a_{i+1}
does not lie in
(a_{1}+Q)\cup\cdots\cup(a_{i}+Q)
. Hence, we have
|A_{p}|\geq k
and
A_{p}-A_{p}
contains no nonzero
k
-th powers in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
L=\lfloor{\log_{p}N\rfloor}
. Let
A\subset[N]
be the set of positive integers

n=1+\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}c_{i}p^{i}

where
c_{i}\in\{0,1,\cdots,p-1\}
, and
c_{d}\in A_{p}
whenever
k\mid d
. It is simple to check that
A-A
contains no nonzero
k
–th power. Setting
s=\lceil L/k\rceil
, we have

|A|\geq p^{L-s}|A_{p}|^{s}\gg_{k}N\cdot\left(\frac{k}{p}\right)^{\log_{p}N/k}=N^{c_{k}}

as desired.
∎

We now begin the proof of Theorem
1.6
. Fix
k
to be a positive integer such that
2k+1
is a prime. Set
\ell=k^{2}
. We define an index set

I:=I_{k}=\{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma):\alpha\in[k-1],\beta\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)],\gamma\in[2G(\alpha)]\}\cup\{0\}.

Here
G(\cdot)
is the number of variables in Lemma
3.3
, and we use the notation

[n]:=\{1,2,\cdots,n\},\quad[0,n]:=\{0,1,2,\cdots,n\}.

Let
N
be a positive integer and set
M=\lfloor N^{1/\ell}\rfloor
. We define a polynomial
\Phi_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{I}]
by

\Phi_{N}(x_{I})=x_{0}^{k}+\sum_{\alpha\in[k-1]}\sum_{\beta\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)]}M^{\beta}\left(\sum_{\gamma=1}^{G(\alpha)}x_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}-\sum_{\gamma=G(\alpha)+1}^{2G(\alpha)}x_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}\right).

This polynomial satisfies the following lemma, which adapts (
9
) to the integer setting.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any
x_{I}\in[N]^{I}
, there exists some
y_{I}\in\mathbb{Z}^{I}
such that, as a polynomial identity in
h
, we have

\Phi_{N}(x_{I}+y_{I}h)=\Phi_{N}(x_{I})+h^{k},

and furthermore, we have
\lVert y_{I}\rVert_{\infty}=O_{k}(M)
.

Proof.

For each
\alpha\in\{1,\cdots,k-1\}
. Let
I_{\alpha}=\{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma):(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in I\}
and
y_{\alpha}=y_{I_{\alpha}}
. Note that the term in
\Phi_{N}(x_{I}+y_{I}h)
with index
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
only contribute to the coefficients of
h^{\alpha^{\prime}}
with
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
.

We set
y_{0}=1
, and iteratively choose
y_{\alpha}
in decreasing order of
\alpha=k-1,\cdots,1
, such that the coefficient of
h^{\alpha}
in
\Phi_{N}(x_{I}+y_{I}h)
is zero. This coefficient is given by

\binom{k}{\alpha}x_{0}^{k-\alpha}y_{0}^{\alpha}+\sum_{\epsilon=\alpha}^{k-1}\sum_{\beta\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)]}M^{\beta}\left(\sum_{\gamma=1}^{G(\alpha)}\binom{\epsilon}{\alpha}x_{\epsilon,\beta,\gamma}^{\epsilon-\alpha}y_{\epsilon,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}-\sum_{\gamma=G(\alpha)+1}^{2G(\alpha)}\binom{\epsilon}{\alpha}x_{\epsilon,\beta,\gamma}^{\epsilon-\alpha}y_{\epsilon,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}\right).

Isolating the term corresponding to
I_{\alpha}
, we need to ensure that

\sum_{\beta\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)]}M^{\beta}\left(\sum_{\gamma=1}^{G(\alpha)}y_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}-\sum_{\gamma=G(\alpha)+1}^{2G(\alpha)}y_{\epsilon,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}\right)=R

where

R=-\binom{k}{\alpha}x_{0}^{k-\alpha}y_{0}^{\alpha}-\sum_{\epsilon=\alpha+1}^{k-1}\sum_{\beta\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)]}M^{\beta}\left(\sum_{\gamma=1}^{G(\alpha)}\binom{\epsilon}{\alpha}x_{\epsilon,\beta,\gamma}^{\epsilon-\alpha}y_{\epsilon,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}-\sum_{\gamma=G(\alpha)+1}^{2G(\alpha)}\binom{\epsilon}{\alpha}x_{\epsilon,\beta,\gamma}^{\epsilon-\alpha}y_{\epsilon,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}\right).

Estimating the size of
R
using the triangle inequality, we have

\left|R\right|\ll_{k}N^{k-\alpha}+\sum_{\epsilon>\alpha}^{k-1}M^{\ell(k-\alpha)}\lVert y_{\epsilon}\rVert_{\infty}^{\alpha}\ll_{k}N^{k-\alpha}(1+\max_{\epsilon>\alpha}\lVert y_{\epsilon}\rVert_{\infty}^{\alpha}).

Expressing
R
in base
M
, we can write

R=\sum_{\beta\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)]}R_{\beta}M^{\beta}

where we have

\left|R_{\beta}\right|\leq\max\left(M,\frac{\left|R\right|}{M^{\ell(k-\alpha)}}\right)\ll_{k}\max\left(M,\max_{\epsilon>\alpha}(1+\lVert y_{\epsilon}\rVert_{\infty}^{\alpha})\right).

Now Lemma
3.3
shows that there exists
\{y_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in[2G(\alpha)]}
that satisfy

\sum_{\gamma=1}^{G(\alpha)}y_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}=\max(R_{\beta},0)+T(\alpha)

and

\sum_{\gamma=G(\alpha)+1}^{2G(\alpha)}y_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}=\max(-R_{\beta},0)+T(\alpha).

So we have

\sum_{\gamma=1}^{G(\alpha)}y_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}-\sum_{\gamma=G(\alpha)+1}^{2G(\alpha)}y_{\epsilon,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}=R_{\beta}

and therefore

\sum_{\beta\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)]}M^{\beta}\left(\sum_{\gamma=1}^{G(\alpha)}y_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}-\sum_{\gamma=G(\alpha)+1}^{2G(\alpha)}y_{\epsilon,\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha}\right)=\sum_{\beta\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)]}M^{\beta}R_{\beta}=R

as desired. Furthermore, we have
\left|y_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\right|^{\alpha}\leq(\left|R_{\beta}\right|+T(\alpha))^{1/\alpha}
, so we obtain

\lVert y_{\alpha}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\max_{\beta}(\left|R_{\beta}\right|+T(\alpha))^{1/\alpha}\ll_{k}\max\left(M^{1/\alpha},\max_{\epsilon>\alpha}(1+\lVert y_{\epsilon}\rVert_{\infty})\right).

Telescoping, we obtain
\lVert y_{\alpha}\rVert_{\infty}\ll_{k}M^{1/\alpha}
for each
\alpha\in[k-1]
, and in particular
\lVert y_{I}\rVert_{\infty}\ll_{k}M
, as desired.
∎

We are now ready to construct the induced point–line matching. Let
q
be a large prime, and set
N=\lfloor{q/4\rfloor}
. Let
A\subset[q^{k}]
be the largest subset of
[q^{k}]
such that
A-A
avoids non–zero
k
–th powers. As before, set
I=I_{k}
. For a shift
s\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define the set

\Gamma_{s}=\{x_{I}\in[N]^{I}:\Phi_{N}(x_{I})\in A+s\}.

We observe that for any
x_{I}\in[N]^{I}
, we have

\left|\Phi_{N}(x)\right|\ll_{k}x_{0}^{k}+\max_{\alpha\in[k-1]}M^{\ell(k-\alpha)}N^{\alpha}\ll_{k}N^{k}.

Hence,
\left|\Gamma_{s}\right|=0
unless
s\ll_{k}q^{k}
(recall
N\asymp q
), and we have

\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\Gamma_{s}\right|=|A|N^{|I|}.

Therefore, we can choose some
s\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\left|\Gamma_{s}\right|\gg_{k}\frac{|A|N^{|I|}}{q^{k}}.

By Lemma
3.5
, for any
x_{I}\in[N]^{I}
, there exists some
y_{I}\in\mathbb{Z}^{I}
such that

\Phi_{N}(x_{I}+y_{I}h)=\Phi_{N}(x_{I})+h^{k}

and
\lVert y_{I}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C_{k}M
, where
C_{k}>0
is some constant depending only on
k
.
We now consider the subset of
[q]\times[q]^{I}
defined by

P:=\{(z,x_{I})\in[q]\times[q]^{I}:z\in[N/C_{k}M],x_{I}\in\Gamma_{s}\}.

For each
p=(z,x_{I})
in
P
, define the line
\ell_{p}=\{(z+h,x_{I}+y_{I}h):h\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. We claim that the reduction of
P
and
\{\ell_{p}\}_{p\in P}
modulo
q
is an induced point–line matching.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that for some distinct points
p=(z,x_{I})
and
p^{\prime}=(z^{\prime},x_{I}^{\prime})
in
P
, we have
p^{\prime}\in\ell_{p}
when reduced modulo
q
. Let
h=z^{\prime}-z
. Then we have
\left|h\right|\leq N/C_{k}M
. For each coordinate
i\in I
, we have

x^{\prime}_{i}\equiv x_{i}+hy_{i}\bmod{q}.

Furthermore, we have

\left|x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i}-hy_{i}\right|\leq N+N+\frac{N}{C_{k}M}\cdot C_{k}M=3N<q.

So we must have
x_{i}^{\prime}=x_{i}+hy_{i}
over
\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore, we have
x_{I}^{\prime}=x_{I}+hy_{I}
over
\mathbb{Z}
. By definition, we obtain (over
\mathbb{Z}
)

\Phi_{N}(x_{I}^{\prime})=\Phi_{N}(x_{I})+h^{k}.

However, both
\Phi_{N}(x_{I})
and
\Phi_{N}(x_{I}^{\prime})
are elements of
A+s
, and
A-A
avoids non–zero
k
–th powers. Therefore, we must have
h=0
and
p=p^{\prime}
, contradiction.

We conclude that the reduction of
P
and
\{\ell_{p}\}
modulo
q
is an induced point–line matching in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{|I|+1}
. Its size is given by

\left|P\right|\gg_{k}\frac{N}{M}\cdot\frac{|A|N^{|I|}}{q^{k}}\gg_{k}q^{|I|+1-k(1-c_{k})-\ell^{-1}}=q^{|I|+1-k(1-c_{k})-k^{-2}}

where
c_{k}
is the constant in Lemma
3.4
. Substituting the value of
c_{k}
in that lemma, we conclude the following.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
k
be a positive integer such that
2k+1
is a prime, and let
d=\left|I_{k}\right|+1
. For prime field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we have

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\gg_{k}q^{d-k(1-c_{k})-k^{-2}}\geq q^{d-\frac{2}{\log k}-\frac{1}{k^{2}}}.

We have
\mathrm{IM}(d+1,q)\geq q\mathrm{IM}(d,q)
by (
4
).
Hence, Proposition
3.6
also holds when
d\geq\left|I_{k}\right|+1
. Thus for each dimension
d
, we have

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\gg_{d}q^{d-\epsilon_{d}},\ \ \text{with}\ \epsilon_{d}:=\frac{2}{\log k}+\frac{1}{k^{2}},

where
k
is the greatest integer such that
2k+1
is prime and
d\geq\left|I_{k}\right|+1
.

By the construction of
I_{k}
and Vinogradov’s estimate for the Waring number
G(k)
, we have
\left|I_{k}\right|=k^{O(1)}
. Bertrand’s postulate shows that
k=d^{\Omega(1)}
and thus
\epsilon_{d}\ll(\log d)^{-1}
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.6
.

4.
Lifting for prime powers: a warm-up

We now shift the attention to base fields
\mathbb{F}_{q}
where
q
is a prime power. The goal of this section is to prove the subsequent Proposition
4.1
, as well as Corollary
1.7
. Both of these results admit relatively short proofs, and serve as motivations for the remaining arguments.

In previous sections, we used the isomorphism
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cong\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
to reduce various constructions over
\mathbb{Z}
to the desired constructions over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. While this isomorphism no longer holds when
q=p^{t}
is a prime power, we instead have

\mathbb{F}_{q}\cong\mathbb{Z}[X]/(p,\phi(X))

where
\phi
is an irreducible polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
of degree
t
. One natural strategy is to do constructions over
\mathbb{Z}[X]
, and reduce them via this isomorphism to constructions over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This strategy leads to the following Proposition
4.1
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
t\geq 1
be an
odd
integer, let
p
be an odd prime such that
p>100t
, and set
q=p^{t}
. Let
A
be a square–difference–free subset of
[\lfloor p/(20t)\rfloor]
.
Then,

\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\gg_{t}p^{\frac{3t-1}{4}}\,|A|^{\frac{t+1}{2}}=q^{\frac{3t-1}{4t}}\;|A|^{\frac{t+1}{2}}.

In light of Lemma
2.2
, we thus have

\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\gg_{t}q^{\frac{3t-1}{4t}}\;p^{\frac{t+1}{2}\cdot 0.7334}\gg_{t}q^{1.1167}.

For
t=1
, note that this recovers Proposition
2.3
. It is perhaps important to add that a similar “degree-barrier” construction can be carried out for even
t
as well (taking
\deg f<t/2
so that
\deg(f^{2})<t
).
However, when
t
is even the field size
q=p^{t}
is a square, and the Hermitian unital construction already yields induced matchings of size
\asymp q^{3/2}
, which is substantially larger than what the Ruzsa-type lifting provides in this regime. For this reason we focus on odd
t
, where no unital-type construction is available.

Proof.

Choose
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that
\mathbb{F}_{q}=\mathbb{F}_{p}(\alpha)
and let
\phi\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
be the minimal monic polynomial of
\alpha
.
Then
\deg\phi=t
, and every element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has a unique representative polynomial of degree
<t
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]

via the identification
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]/(\phi)
.

Let
s=\frac{t+1}{2}\leq t
,
M=\left\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{p}{64s}}\right\rfloor
, and define
X(s,M)
to be the set of integer polynomials

f(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}f_{i}X^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]\qquad\text{with}\qquad|f_{i}|\leq M.

Furthermore, let
Y_{even}(t,A)
be the set of integer polynomials

g(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}g_{i}X^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]

with
g_{2i}\in A
for
i=0,1,\ldots,s-1
and
g_{2i+1}\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\right\}
for
i=0,1,\ldots,s-2
. In other words, the even coefficients of
g
come from
A
, while the odd coefficients of
g
are arbitrary elements of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. For each pair
(f,g)\in X(s,M)\times Y_{even}(t,A)
, we can now define

p_{f,g}:=\left(\frac{g(\alpha)+f(\alpha)^{2}}{2},f(\alpha)\right)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}.

Note that
p_{f,g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
indeed holds by design (and also because
p
is odd). We also would like to emphasize that if
p_{f,g}=(x,y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
then
f(\alpha)=y
and
g(\alpha)=2x-y^{2}
. Finally, let

P:=\left\{p_{f,g}:\ f\in X(s,M),g\in Y_{even}(t,A)\right\}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}.

This will be the point set defining our large induced matching in
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(2)}
. Before, we define the set of lines, we take a moment to note that the map
X(s,M)\times Y_{even}(t,A)\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
defined by
(f,g)\mapsto p_{f,q}
is injective. Indeed, this is because
\deg f<s\leq t
,
\deg g<t
, and
M<p/2
, so the values of
f(\alpha)
and
g(\alpha)
uniquely recover
f
and
g
. Hence

|P|=|X(s,M)|\cdot|Y_{\mathrm{even}}(t,A)|=(2M+1)^{s}\cdot(p^{s-1}|A|^{s})\gg_{t}p^{\frac{3t-1}{4}}\,|A|^{\frac{t+1}{2}}=q^{\frac{3t-1}{4t}}\;|A|^{\frac{t+1}{2}}.

For
p=(x,y)\in P
, let us now define the affine line

\ell_{p}:=\{(x,y)+\lambda(y,1):\ \lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}=\{(x+\lambda y,\ y+\lambda):\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}.

We claim that
\{(p,\ell_{p}):p\in P\}
is an induced matching in the point–line incidence graph of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
. By the calculation from (
4
), this will complete the proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Like before, the key point is that for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
we have the identity

2(x+\lambda y)-(y+\lambda)^{2}=(2x-y^{2})-\lambda^{2},

(11)

so moving along
\ell_{p}
changes
2x-y^{2}
by a square.

Fix
p=p_{f,g}\in P
and suppose
p^{\prime}=p_{f^{\prime},g^{\prime}}\in P
lies on
\ell_{p}
.
Then
p^{\prime}=(x+\lambda y,\,y+\lambda)
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and by (
11
) we get

g(\alpha)-g^{\prime}(\alpha)=(2x-y^{2})-(2x^{\prime}-y^{\prime 2})=\lambda^{2}.

Also
y^{\prime}=y+\lambda
implies

\lambda=y^{\prime}-y=f^{\prime}(\alpha)-f(\alpha)=h(\alpha),\qquad\text{where }h:=f^{\prime}-f.

Thus

(g-g^{\prime})(\alpha)=h(\alpha)^{2}.

(12)

Because
\deg h<s
and
t=2s-1
, we have
\deg(h^{2})\leq 2(s-1)<t
.
Since evaluation at
\alpha
is injective on degree-
<t
polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
,
(
12
) implies the polynomial identity in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
:

g(X)-g^{\prime}(X)\equiv h(X)^{2}\pmod{p}.

(13)

Now let
i\geq 0
be the smallest index with the
X^{i}
coefficient of
h
nonzero in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
Write that coefficient as
d_{i}\in\{-2M,\dots,2M\}\subset\mathbb{Z}
(using
2M<p/2
so representatives are unambiguous).
Then the coefficient of
X^{2i}
in
h^{2}
is
d_{i}^{2}
, and it is the first nonzero coefficient of
h^{2}
.

Consequently, the first nonzero coefficient of
g-g^{\prime}
must also occur in degree
2i
.
In particular,
g_{2j}=g^{\prime}_{2j}
for all
j<i
, and at degree
2i
we have

g_{2i}-g^{\prime}_{2i}\equiv d_{i}^{2}\pmod{p}.

But
g_{2i},g^{\prime}_{2i}\in A\subset[\,p/(20t)\,]
, so
g_{2i}-g^{\prime}_{2i}\in A-A\subset(-p/2,p/2)
as an integer.
Also
|d_{i}|\leq 2M
and
4M^{2}\leq p/(16s)<p/2
, hence
d_{i}^{2}\in(-p/2,p/2)
.
Therefore the congruence mod
p
lifts to the integer equality

g_{2i}-g^{\prime}_{2i}=d_{i}^{2}\in\mathbb{Z}.

If
g\neq g^{\prime}
, then
g_{2i}-g^{\prime}_{2i}\neq 0
and we have produced a nonzero perfect square in
A-A
,
contradicting the square–difference–free hypothesis on
A
.
Hence
g=g^{\prime}
. With
g=g^{\prime}
, (
13
) gives
h^{2}\equiv 0
, so
h\equiv 0
and thus
f=f^{\prime}
.
Therefore
p^{\prime}=p
.

We have shown
\ell_{p}\cap P=\{p\}
for every
p\in P
, so the pairs
\{(p,\ell_{p})\}_{p\in P}

form an induced matching of size
|P|
in
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(2)}
.
∎

We now turn our attention to Corollary
1.7
. We prove it using the isomorphism
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}^{t}
as
\mathbb{F}_{p}
–vector spaces.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose
q=p^{s}
. Then for
d_{0}\geq 1
, we have
\mathrm{IM}(d_{0}s,q)\geq\mathrm{IM}(d_{0},p)^{s}\cdot q^{(s-1)d_{0}}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}[T]/(f)
for some irreducible
f
of degree
s
.
Each element
a
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
can be uniquely written as
a=\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}c_{i}T^{i}+(f)
with
c_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Let
\pi_{i}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
map
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
to
c_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Note that
\pi_{i}
is a
\mathbb{F}_{p}
–linear map.

Now let
M
be an induced matching in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d_{0}}
with point-set
P
, and each
p\in P
having some direction
v_{p}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d_{0}}\setminus\{0\}
which spans a line
\ell_{p}
with
P\cap\ell_{p}=\{p\}
. We then define an induced matching
M
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{sd_{0}}
as follows. For each
a=(a_{j})_{j\in[sd_{0}]}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{sd_{0}}
and each
i\in\{0,1,\cdots,s-1\}
, define
\phi_{i}(a)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d_{0}}
as

\phi_{i}(a):=(\pi_{i}(a_{j}))_{j\in\{id_{0}+1,\dots,(i+1)d_{0}\}}.

Our point-set shall be

P^{\prime}=\left\{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{sd_{0}}:\phi_{i}(a)\in P\text{ for all }i\in\{0,\dots,s-1\}\right\}.

Given a point
a\in P^{\prime}
, let its corresponding line
\ell_{a}^{\prime}
have slope equal to

v_{a}^{\prime}=(v_{\phi_{1}(a)},v_{\phi_{2}(a)},\cdots,v_{\phi_{s}(a)})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{sd_{0}}.

Now suppose for the sake of contradiction that, for some
a\in P^{\prime}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\backslash\{0\}
, we have
a+\lambda v_{a}^{\prime}\in P
. For each
i\in\{0,1,\cdots,s-1\}
and
j\in\{id_{0}+1,\dots,(i+1)d_{0}\}
, we have

\pi_{i}((a+\lambda v_{a}^{\prime})_{j})=\pi_{i}(a_{j})+\pi_{i}(\lambda(v_{a}^{\prime})_{j}).

Since
(v_{a}^{\prime})_{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, we have

\pi_{i}((a+\lambda v_{a})_{j})=\pi_{i}(a)+(v_{a}^{\prime})_{j}\pi_{i}(\lambda).

Hence, we obtain

\phi_{i}(a+\lambda v_{a})=\phi_{i}(a)+\pi_{i}(\lambda)v_{\phi_{i}(a)}.

Therefore, we have
\phi_{i}(a)+\pi_{i}(\lambda)v_{\phi_{i}(a)}\in P
. Since
P\cap\ell_{\phi_{i}(a)}=\{\phi_{i}(a)\}
, we obtain
\pi_{i}(\lambda)=0
for each
i\in\{0,1,\cdots,s-1\}
, so
\lambda=0
, contradiction.

We conclude that
(a,\ell_{a}^{\prime})_{a\in P^{\prime}}
forms an induced point–line matching in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d_{0}s}
. Its size is given by

\left|P^{\prime}\right|=(|P|\cdot p^{d_{0}(s-1)})^{s}=|P|^{s}\cdot q^{d_{0}(s-1)}

as desired.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.7
.

If
s\geq\log d
, we may take
\epsilon_{d,s}=1
and apply the trivial bound
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\geq q^{d-1}
. So we may assume that
s<\log d
. Let
d_{0}=\lfloor d/s\rfloor
. By (
4
), we have

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\geq\mathrm{IM}(d_{0}s,q)\cdot q^{d-d_{0}s}.

By Proposition
4.2
and Theorem
1.6
, we have

\mathrm{IM}(d_{0}s,q)\geq\mathrm{IM}(d_{0},p)^{s}\cdot q^{(s-1)d_{0}}\gg_{d}p^{sd_{0}s-s\epsilon_{d_{0}}}q^{(s-1)d_{0}}.

Combining the estimates, we conclude that

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\gg_{d}q^{d-\epsilon_{d_{0}}s}.

As
d_{0}=\lfloor d/s\rfloor\gg d^{1/2}
, Corollary
1.7
holds with

\epsilon_{d,s}:=\epsilon_{d_{0}}s\ll\frac{s}{\log d}

as desired.
∎

5.
Norm hypersurfaces over finite fields

In this section we prove Theorem
1.8
by constructing large induced matchings over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
when
q=q_{0}^{k}
is a prime power with
k\leq d
. We first describe the proof strategy. Recall that in Section
3
, the key ingredient is a polynomial
\Phi:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that for certain
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, there exists some
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
such that

\Phi(x+yh)=\Phi(x)+h^{d}.

Here, we instead consider the
norm trace polynomial

\Phi^{\prime}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}
given by

\Phi^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Norm(x_{i}).

Related norm–trace varieties appear in finite geometry and coding theory, e.g.
[
17
]
. We prove that for many
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, there exists some
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}^{d}
such that the following relations hold for every
h\in\mathbb{F}_{q}

\Phi^{\prime}(x+yh)=\Phi^{\prime}(x)+\Norm(h).

Hence, we can adapt the proof of Proposition
3.1
in a much more efficient way.

Assume that
q=q_{0}^{k}
with
k\geq 2
and
q_{0}
a prime (in fact, our proof actually also works when
q_{0}
is a prime power).
Let
\sigma:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
be the Frobenius automorphism
\sigma(x)=x^{q_{0}}
.
Write

\Norm(x):=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\sigma^{j}(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}

for the relative norm map
\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}
.
For
\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
0\leq r\leq k
set

e_{r}(\mu):=\sum_{0\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq k-1}\sigma^{i_{1}}(\mu)\cdots\sigma^{i_{r}}(\mu)\in\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}},

with the conventions
e_{0}(\mu)=1
and
e_{k}(\mu)=\Norm(\mu)
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For every
\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and every
t\in\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}
one has

\Norm(1+\mu t)=\sum_{r=0}^{k}t^{r}e_{r}(\mu).

Proof.

Since
t\in\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}
we have
\sigma(t)=t
, hence

\Norm(1+\mu t)=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\bigl(1+\sigma^{j}(\mu)\,t\bigr).

Expanding the product and collecting terms according to the power of
t
yields the stated identity,
with the coefficient of
t^{r}
equal to the
r
th elementary symmetric polynomial in the conjugates
\sigma^{j}(\mu)
.
∎

Let
S\subset\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}^{k-1}
consist of the
(k-1)
-tuples
(t_{1},\cdots,t_{k-1})
with pairwise distinct entries none of which being in
\{0,1\}
. Let
t_{k}=1
. We now define a key map
\varphi:S\to\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}^{k}
. For each
(t_{1},\cdots,t_{k-1})\in S
, let

A_{i}:=\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{-t_{j}}{t_{i}-t_{j}}=\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{t_{j}}{t_{j}-t_{i}}\qquad(i=1,\dots,k)

and define

\varphi(t_{1},\cdots,t_{k-1})=(A_{1},\cdots,A_{k}).

This definition is motivated by the following lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
(t_{1},\dots,t_{k-1})
be an element of
S
, and let
(A_{1},\cdots,A_{k})=\varphi(t_{1},\cdots,t_{k-1})
. Recall that
t_{k}=1
. Then we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}t_{i}^{r}=0\ \ \text{for every}\ \ 1\leq r\leq k-1.

Moreover, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}t_{i}^{k}=(-1)^{k+1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}.

Proof.

Let
L_{i}(T)
be the Lagrange basis polynomials for the nodes
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
,

L_{i}(T):=\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{T-t_{j}}{t_{i}-t_{j}},

so that any polynomial
f
of degree at most
k-1
satisfies
f(T)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(t_{i})L_{i}(T)
.
Evaluating at
T=0
gives

f(0)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(t_{i})L_{i}(0).

Since
A_{i}=L_{i}(0)
, taking
f(T)\equiv 1
gives
\sum_{i}A_{i}=1
, while taking
f(T)=T^{r}
for
1\leq r\leq k-1
gives
\sum_{i}A_{i}t_{i}^{r}=0
.

For the final identity, let
g(T)
be the (unique) interpolation polynomial of degree at most
k-1

such that
g(t_{i})=t_{i}^{k}
for all
i\in[k]
.
Then the polynomial
T^{k}-g(T)
has roots
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
, hence

T^{k}-g(T)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(T-t_{i}).

Evaluating at
T=0
gives
-g(0)=(-1)^{k}\prod_{i}t_{i}
, i.e.
g(0)=(-1)^{k+1}\prod_{i}t_{i}
.
Finally, by interpolation at
T=0
we have
g(0)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}t_{i}^{k}
, completing the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

With the notation above, for every
\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\,\Norm(1+\mu t_{i})=1+c\,\Norm(\mu),

where
c=\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}t_{i}^{k}=(-1)^{k+1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}^{\times}
.

Proof.

Combine Lemma
5.1
with Lemma
5.2
.
All intermediate coefficients
e_{r}(\mu)
for
1\leq r\leq k-1
vanish after weighting, leaving only the constant term
e_{0}(\mu)=1

and the top term
e_{k}(\mu)=\Norm(\mu)
.
∎

We now show how Corollary
5.3
leads to an induced matching.
Define the
k
-fold norm hypersurface
as

\mathcal{H}_{k}:=\Bigl\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}:\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\Norm(x_{i})=1\Bigr\}.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
(t_{1},\dots,t_{k-1})\in S
and let
(A_{1},\cdots,A_{k-1})=\varphi(t_{1},\cdots,t_{k})
. Let
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
satisfy

\Norm(a_{i})=A_{i}\qquad(i=1,\dots,k).

Then the affine line

\ell_{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}}:=\{(a_{1}(1+\mu t_{1}),\dots,a_{k}(1+\mu t_{k})):\ \mu\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}

satisfies
\ell_{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\}
.

Proof.

Since
\sum_{i}A_{i}=1
by Lemma
5.2
, we have
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
For
\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, by multiplicativity of
\Norm
and Corollary
5.3
,

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Norm\bigl(a_{i}(1+\mu t_{i})\bigr)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Norm(a_{i})\,\Norm(1+\mu t_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\,\Norm(1+\mu t_{i})=1+c\,\Norm(\mu).

Because
c\neq 0
and
\Norm(\mu)=0
if and only if
\mu=0
, the right-hand side equals
1
precisely when
\mu=0
.
Thus
\ell_{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}}
meets
\mathcal{H}_{k}
only at
\mu=0
, i.e. only at
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
.
∎

Therefore, the subset of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
defined by

P:=\{(a_{1},\cdots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}:(N(a_{1}),\cdots,N(a_{k}))\in\varphi(S)\}

together with the lines
\ell_{a_{1},\cdots,a_{k}}
forms an induced point–line matching. It remains to show that the matching is large, which is equivalent to lower bounding the size of
\varphi(S)
.

We show that

Lemma 5.5
.

For each
A=(A_{1},\cdots,A_{k})\in\varphi(S)
, its preimage
\varphi^{-1}(A)
has size at most
(k-1)!
.

In order to prove this lemma, we need to following “overdetermined” form of Bezóut’s theorem. The theorem was first explicitly stated by Heintz in
[
23
]
, and a self-contained proof was published by Tao in his webblog
[
42
]
.

Theorem 5.6

(Bezóut’s theorem)
.

Assume
d\geq m\geq 0
. Let
k
be a field. Let
f_{1},\cdots,f_{d}\in k[x_{1},\cdots,x_{m}]
be polynomials, and let
V
be their common zero locus. Let
V^{0}
be the union of the
0
-dimensional irreducible components of
V
. If
f_{i}
has degree
D_{i}
, then we have

|V^{0}|\leq D_{1}\cdots D_{d}.

Proof of Lemma
5.5
.

Recall that
A=(A_{1},\cdots,A_{k})
lies in
\varphi(S)
.

Let
f_{1},\cdots,f_{k-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}[x_{1},\cdots,x_{k-1}]
be defined by

f_{i}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k-1})=A_{1}x_{1}^{i}+\cdots+A_{k-1}x_{k-1}^{i}+A_{k}.

By Lemma
5.2
, any
(t_{1},\cdots,t_{k-1})\in\varphi^{-1}(A)
lies in the common zero locus
V
of the
f_{i}
’s. We now claim that it lies in a
0
-dimensional irreducible component.

By the Jacobian criterion, it suffices to check that the Jacobian
J=\{\partial_{j}f_{i}\}_{i,j=1}^{k-1}
is non-singular at
(t_{1},\cdots,t_{k-1})
. Note that

\partial_{j}f_{i}(t_{1},\cdots,t_{k})=A_{j}\cdot i\cdot t_{j}^{i-1}.

Hence we have

\det J=(k-1)!\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}A_{j}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(t_{j}-t_{i}).

As
q_{0}>k
, we have
k!\neq 0
. By the definition of
\varphi
, the
A_{i}
’s are nonzero. By the definition of
S
, the
t_{i}
’s are nonzero. We conclude that
\det J\neq 0
, thus any element in
\varphi^{-1}(A)
lies in a
0
-dimensional irreducible component of
V
. By Theorem
5.6
, we conclude that
\left|\varphi^{-1}(A)\right|\leq(k-1)!
.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Set

P:=\{(a_{1},\cdots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}:(N(a_{1}),\cdots,N(a_{k}))\in\varphi(S)\}

Recalling the definition of
S
, we have

\left|S\right|=(q_{0}-2)(q_{0}-3)\cdots(q_{0}-k)\gg_{k}q_{0}^{k-1}.

By Lemma
5.5
, we have

\left|\varphi(S)\right|\gg_{k}\left|S\right|\gg_{k}q_{0}^{k-1}.

Since the norm map
\Norm:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\to\mathbb{F}_{q_{0}}^{\times}
is surjective with fibres of size
(q-1)/(q_{0}-1)
(see
[
28
, Ch. 2]
), we obtain

\left|P\right|=\left(\frac{q-1}{q_{0}-1}\right)^{k}\left|\varphi(S)\right|\gg_{k}\frac{1}{q_{0}}q^{k}=q^{k-1/k}.

For each
p=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in P
choose the associated line
\ell_{p}
from Lemma
5.4
.
Since
\ell_{p}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}=\{p\}
and
P\subset\mathcal{H}_{k}
, we have
\ell_{p}\cap P=\{p\}
.
Thus
\{(p,\ell_{p}):p\in P\}
is an induced matching in
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(k)}
of size
|P|\gg_{k}q^{k-1/k}
.

For
d>k
we take the Cartesian product with
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d-k}
:
replace each
p\in P
by all points
(p,u)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d-k}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
and replace
\ell_{p}
by
\ell_{p}\times\{u\}
.
This multiplies the matching size by
q^{d-k}
and yields an induced matching in
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(d)}
of size

\gg_{k}q^{k-1/k}q^{d-k}=q^{d-1/k},

as desired.
∎

Remark 5.7

(The case
k=2
)
.

When
k=2
and
q=q_{0}^{2}
, the hypersurface
\mathcal{H}_{2}=\{(x_{1},x_{2}):\Norm(x_{1})+\Norm(x_{2})=1\}
is a Hermitian curve,
and lines meeting
\mathcal{H}_{2}
in a unique point are its tangents.
Thus, for
d=2
the norm–interpolation construction is closely related to the classical Hermitian unital and yields

\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\gg q^{3/2}
, matching (
1
) up to constants in the square-field case.

6.
Ruzsa lift for prime powers

In this section, we finally complete the proof of Theorem
1.9
. We will prove the following result, which deals with the prime powers
q=p^{t}
with
t>d
. The remaining prime powers are covered by Corollary
1.7
and Theorem
1.8
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
k
be an odd positive integer such that
2k+1
is a prime. Then there exists some constant
f(k)=k^{O(k)}
such that the following holds. Let
d,t
be positive integers with
\min(d,t)>f(k)
, and let
q=q_{0}^{t}
be a prime power. Then we have

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\geq(c_{k})^{t}t^{-t}\cdot q^{d-\delta_{k}}

where
c_{k}>0
depends on
k
only and
\delta_{k}=\frac{3}{\log k}+\frac{3}{k}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.9
assuming Lemma
6.1
.

We may assume that
d
is sufficiently large. For a prime power
q=p^{t}
, we divide into three cases based on the exponent
t
.

(1)

If
t<\sqrt{\log d}
, then Corollary
1.7
gives

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\gg_{d,t}q^{d-\epsilon_{d,t}}

where
\epsilon_{d,t}\ll t/\log d\ll(\log d)^{-1/2}\ll(\log\log d)^{-1}
, as desired.

(2)

If
t\in[\sqrt{\log d},d]
, then Theorem
1.8
gives

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\gg_{d}q^{d-1/t}\gg q^{d-(\log d)^{-1/2}}

as desired.

(3)

If
t>d
, then Lemma
6.1
gives

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\geq(c_{k})^{t}t^{-t}\cdot q^{d-\delta_{k}}

where
k
is the maximum odd positive integer such that
(2k+1)
is a prime and
f(k)<d
. As
f(k)=k^{O(k)}
, we have
k\gg\sqrt{\log d}
, hence
\delta_{k}\ll(\log\log d)^{-1}
. Furthermore, we have

(c_{k})^{t}t^{-t}\ll_{k}t^{-2t}=q^{2\log t/\log p}.

Hence, taking
\epsilon_{d}=\delta_{k}
, we conclude that

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\gg_{d}q^{d-\epsilon_{d}-2\log t/\log p}.

Thus, we have proved Theorem
1.9
in all cases.
∎

Our approach to Lemma
6.1
is an analogue to the proof of Theorem
1.6
with
\mathbb{Z}
replaced by the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[T]
(in this proof, we use
T
as the variable for polynomials). We need the following analogue of Waring’s theorem for polynomials (see
[
8
,
49
]
). For the reader’s convenience, we include a proof in the Appendix.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[T]
. then

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{k-1}{i}\left(f+\frac{k-1}{2}-i\right)^{k}=k!\cdot f.

We set
\ell=(k+1)!
and define an index set

J:=J_{k}=\{(\alpha,\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma):\alpha\in[k-1],\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)],\gamma\in[0,\alpha-1]\}\cup\{0\}.

It is clear that
\left|J_{k}\right|=k^{O(k)}
. We take
f(k)=\max(\ell^{3},\left|J_{k}\right|+1)
. Then the condition
\min(d,t)>f(k)
implies that

t>\ell^{3},d>f(k)+1.

Let
r=\lceil t/\ell\rceil
. As in the proof of Proposition
4.1
, let
X(s,M)
denote the polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[T]
with degree at most
(s-1)
, whose coefficients have absolute value at most
M
. We note the relation that

X(s,M)\cdot X(s^{\prime},M^{\prime})\subset X(s+s^{\prime},(s+s^{\prime})MM^{\prime}).

(14)

Let
N
be a positive integer and set
M=\lfloor N^{1/\ell}\rfloor
. We define the following polynomial

\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J}):=(k!)^{k}x_{0}^{k}+\sum_{\alpha\in[k-1]}\sum_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)]}(k!)^{\alpha}M^{\beta}T^{r\beta^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{\gamma=0}^{\alpha-1}(-1)^{\gamma}\binom{\alpha-1}{\gamma}x_{\alpha,\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}^{\alpha}\right).

This polynomial satisfies the following analog of Lemma
3.5
in the ring
\mathbb{Z}[T]
.

Lemma 6.3
.

For any
x_{J}\in X(t,N)^{J}
, there exists some
y_{J}\in\mathbb{Z}[T]^{J}
such that, as a polynomial identity in the single variable
h
, we have

\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J}+y_{J}h)=\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J})+(k!)^{k}\cdot h^{k},

and furthermore, we have
y_{j}\in X(t^{\prime},N^{\prime})
for every
j\in J
, where
t^{\prime}\leq 2k!\cdot(r+(k+1)!)
,
N^{\prime}=O_{k}(N^{1/k})
.

Proof.

For each
\alpha\in\{1,\cdots,k-1\}
. Let
J_{\alpha}=\{(\alpha,\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma):\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)],\gamma\in[0,\alpha-1]\}
and
y_{\alpha}=y_{J_{\alpha}}
. Note that the term in
\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J}+y_{J}h)
with index
(\alpha,\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma)
only contribute to the coefficients of
h^{\alpha^{\prime}}
with
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
.

We set
y_{0}=1
, and also define
t_{k}:=1,N_{k}:=1
. We then iteratively choose
y_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}[T]^{J_{\alpha}}
and integers
t_{\alpha},N_{\alpha}
in decreasing order of
\alpha=k-1,\cdots,1
, such that the coefficient of
h^{\alpha}
in
\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J}+y_{J}h)
is zero, and
y_{j}\in X(t_{\alpha},N_{\alpha})
for each
j\in J_{\alpha}
.

This coefficient is given by

(k!)^{k}\binom{k}{\alpha}x_{0}^{k-\alpha}+\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha}^{k-1}\sum_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha^{\prime})]}(k!)^{\alpha^{\prime}}M^{\beta}T^{r\beta^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{\gamma=0}^{\alpha^{\prime}-1}(-1)^{\gamma}\binom{\alpha^{\prime}-1}{\gamma}\binom{\alpha^{\prime}}{\alpha}x_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}^{\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha}y_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}^{\alpha}\right).

Isolating the term corresponding to
I_{\alpha}
, we need to ensure that

\sum_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)]}(k!)^{\alpha}M^{\beta}T^{r\beta^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{\gamma=0}^{\alpha-1}\binom{\alpha-1}{\gamma}(-1)^{\gamma}y_{\alpha,\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}^{\alpha}\right)=R

where

R=-(k!)^{k}\binom{k}{\alpha}x_{0}^{k-\alpha}-\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha+1}^{k-1}\sum_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha^{\prime})]}(k!)^{\alpha^{\prime}}M^{\beta}T^{r\beta^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{\gamma=0}^{\alpha^{\prime}-1}(-1)^{\gamma}\binom{\alpha^{\prime}-1}{\gamma}\binom{\alpha^{\prime}}{\alpha}x_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}^{\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha}y_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}^{\alpha}\right).

We now recall our assumption that

x_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}\in X(t,N),y_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}\in X(t_{\alpha^{\prime}},N_{\alpha^{\prime}}).

Applying (
14
), we have

x_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}^{\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha}y_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}^{\alpha}\in X\left((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)t+\alpha t_{\alpha^{\prime}},O_{k}\left(N^{\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha}N_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{\alpha}\right)\right).

Therefore, for each
\alpha^{\prime}\in[\alpha,k-1]
, the summand

\sum_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha^{\prime})]}(k!)^{\alpha^{\prime}}M^{\beta}T^{r\beta^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{\gamma=0}^{\alpha^{\prime}-1}(-1)^{\gamma}\binom{\alpha^{\prime}-1}{\gamma}\binom{\alpha^{\prime}}{\alpha^{\prime}}x_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}^{\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime}}y_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}^{\alpha}\right)

lies in

X\left((\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)t+\alpha t_{\alpha^{\prime}}+r\ell(k-\alpha^{\prime}),O_{k}\left(M^{\ell(k-\alpha^{\prime})}N^{\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha}N_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{\alpha}\right)\right).

Furthermore, we have

-(k!)^{k}\binom{k}{\alpha}x_{0}^{k-\alpha}\in X(t(k-\alpha),N^{k-\alpha}).

Recalling the assumption that
r=\lceil{t/\ell}\rceil\leq t/\ell+1
and
M=\lfloor N^{1/\ell}\rfloor
, we conclude that

R\in X\left((k-\alpha)t+\ell k+\alpha\max_{\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha}t_{\alpha^{\prime}},O_{k}\left(N^{k-\alpha}\max_{\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha}N_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{\alpha}\right)\right).

We also make the observation that
(k!)^{-\alpha-1}R
has integral coefficients.

We now write the coefficients of
(k!)^{-\alpha-1}R
in base
M
, and write each monomial
T^{i}
as
T^{(i-r\beta^{\prime})+r\beta^{\prime}}
, where
\beta^{\prime}
is the largest element of
[0,\ell(k-\alpha)]
such that
i\geq r\beta^{\prime}
. Thus, there exists
R_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{Z}[T]
such that

R=\sum_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)]}(k!)^{\alpha+1}M^{\beta}T^{r\beta^{\prime}}R_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}}

and as
r\ell(k-\alpha)\geq t(k-\alpha)
and
M^{\ell(k-\alpha)}\geq 2^{-k}N^{k-\alpha}
, we have

R_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}}\in X\left(\max(r,\ell k+\alpha\max_{\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha}t_{\alpha^{\prime}}),\max(M,O_{k}(\max_{\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha}N_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{\alpha}))\right).

Finally, recalling our assumption that
k
is odd, we can take

y_{\alpha,\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}=R_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}}+\frac{k-1}{2}-\gamma

so that by Proposition
A.1
, we have

k!R_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}}=\sum_{\gamma=0}^{\alpha^{\prime}-1}\binom{\alpha-1}{\gamma}(-1)^{\gamma}y_{\alpha,\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}^{\alpha}

and thus we conclude that

R=\sum_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)]}(k!)^{\alpha}M^{\beta}T^{r\beta^{\prime}}\sum_{\gamma=0}^{\alpha^{\prime}-1}\binom{\alpha-1}{\gamma}(-1)^{\gamma}y_{\alpha,\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}^{\alpha}

as desired. Furthermore, we have

y_{\alpha,\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}\in X\left(\max(r,\ell k+\alpha\max_{\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha}t_{\alpha^{\prime}}),k+\max(M,O_{k}(\max_{\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha}N_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{\alpha}))\right).

Thus, we can take

\begin{cases}t_{\alpha}=r+\ell k+\alpha\max_{\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha}t_{\alpha^{\prime}}\\
N_{\alpha}\ll_{k}M+\max_{\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha}N_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{\alpha}\end{cases}.

Solving this recursion, we conclude that for each
\alpha
, we have

t_{\alpha}\leq t^{\prime}=2k!\cdot(r+\ell k)

and

N_{\alpha}\ll_{k}M^{(k-1)!}\ll_{k}N^{(k-1)!/\ell}.

Recalling our choice that
\ell=(k+1)!
, we conclude the desired result.
∎

We are now ready to construct the induced point–line matching over
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)
. In light of (
4
), and recall that we assume
d>|J_{k}|+1
, it suffices to show that

\mathrm{IM}(|J_{k}|+1,q)\geq(c_{k})^{t}t^{-t}q^{|J_{k}|+1-4(\log k)^{-1}}.

Recall our choice of parameters:

\ell=(k+1)!,\quad t>\ell^{3},\quad r=\lceil t/\ell\rceil.

Let
N=\lfloor{p/4\rfloor}
. Let
A\subset[N^{k}]
be the largest subset of
[N^{k}]
such that
A-A
avoids non–zero
k
–th powers. Let
Y_{k}(r\ell k,N^{k},A)
denote the set of polynomials in
X(r\ell k,N^{k})
, whose coefficient of
T^{ki}
lies in
A
for each non–negative integer
i
.

For a shift
s\in\mathbb{Z}[T]
, we define the set

\Gamma_{s}=\{x_{J}\in X(t,N)^{J}:\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J})\in Y_{k}(r\ell k,N^{k},A)+s\}.

Recall the definition of
\Psi_{N,t}
as

\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J})=(k!)^{k}x_{0}^{k}+\sum_{\alpha\in[k-1]}\sum_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in[0,\ell(k-\alpha)]}(k!)^{\alpha}M^{\beta}T^{r\beta^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{\gamma=0}^{\alpha-1}(-1)^{\gamma}\binom{\alpha-1}{\gamma}x_{\alpha,\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma}^{\alpha}\right)

where
M=\lfloor{N^{1/\ell}\rfloor}
. By (
14
), we see that for any
x_{J}\in X(t,N)^{J}
, we have

\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J})\in X\left(r\ell k,O_{k}(N^{k})\right)

where the bound on the degree following from that fact that for any
\alpha\in[k-1]
, we have

\ell(k-\alpha)r+\alpha t\leq r\ell k.

Hence,
\left|\Gamma_{s}\right|=0
unless
s\in X(r\ell k,C_{k}N^{k})
for some constant
C_{k}>0
depending on
k
only. On the other hand, we have

\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}[T]}\left|\Gamma_{s}\right|=\left|X(t,N)\right|^{\left|J\right|}\left|Y_{k}(r\ell k,N^{k},A)\right|.

Therefore, we can choose some

such that

\left|\Gamma_{s}\right|\geq\frac{\left|X(t,N)\right|^{\left|J\right|}\left|Y_{k}(r\ell k,N^{k},A)\right|}{\left|X(r\ell k,C_{k}N^{k})\right|}.

By Lemma

, for any
x_{J}\in X(t,N)^{J}
, there exists some
y_{J}\in\mathbb{Z}[T]^{J}
such that (for every

\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J}+y_{J}h)=\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J})+(k!)^{k}h^{k}

such that for each

y_{j}\in X(t^{\prime},N^{\prime})

with
t^{\prime}=2k!\cdot(r+(k+1)!)
,
N^{\prime}=D_{k}N^{1/k}
and
D_{k}>0
is some constant depending only on
k
.

Now let
\alpha
be a primitive element of the field extension
\mathbb{F}_{q}/\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
We consider the subset of
\mathbb{F}_{q}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{J}
defined by
2
2

2

Here
(h,x_{J})(\alpha)
means evaluating every coordinate of
(h,x_{J})
, being a polynomial in
T
, at
T=\alpha
.

P:=\{(z,x_{J})(\alpha):z\in X(t-t^{\prime},N/2tN^{\prime}),x_{J}\in\Gamma_{s}\}.

For each
p=(z,x_{J})(\alpha)
in
P
, define the line
\ell_{p}=\{(z(\alpha)+h,x_{J}(\alpha)+y_{J}(\alpha)h):h\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}
. We claim that the reduction of
P
and
\{\ell_{p}\}
modulo

is an induced point–line matching.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that for some distinct points
p=(z,x_{J})(\alpha)
and
p^{\prime}=(z^{\prime},x_{J}^{\prime})(\alpha)
in
P
, we have
p^{\prime}\in\ell_{p}
. Let
h=z^{\prime}-z
. Then we have
h\in X(t-t^{\prime},N/tN^{\prime})
. For each coordinate
j\in J
, we have

x^{\prime}_{j}(\alpha)=x_{j}(\alpha)+hy_{j}(\alpha).

Note that each of the polynomials
x_{j}^{\prime}
,
x_{j}
,
hy_{j}
lies in
X(t,N)
. As the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
has degree

x_{j}^{\prime}-x_{j}-hy_{j}\equiv 0\bmod{p}.

Furthermore, the coefficients of
x_{j}^{\prime}-x_{j}-hy_{j}
have absolute value at most

. Hence, we must have

x_{j}^{\prime}-x_{j}-hy_{j}=0

over
\mathbb{Z}[T]

Therefore, we have

\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J}^{\prime})=\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J}+hy_{J}).

Expanding the right hand side using Lemma

, we have

\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J}^{\prime})=\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J})+(k!h)^{k}.

On the other hand,
\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J}^{\prime})-\Psi_{N,t}(x_{J})
lies in
Y_{k}(r\ell k,N^{k},A)-Y_{k}(r\ell k,N^{k},A)
. The non–zero term of

with the least degree must be of the form

for some non–negative integer

. In any element of

We conclude that

P=\{(z,x_{J})(\alpha):z\in X(t-t^{\prime},N/2tN^{\prime}),x_{J}\in\Gamma_{s}\}

Different choices of

\left|P\right|\geq\left|X(t-t^{\prime},N/2tN^{\prime})\right|\left|\Gamma_{s}\right|.

Substituting our estimate for

\left|P\right|\gg_{k}\left|X(t-t^{\prime},N/2tN^{\prime})\right|\cdot\frac{\left|X(t,N)\right|^{\left|J\right|}\left|Y_{k}(r\ell k,N^{k},A)\right|}{\left|X(r\ell k,C_{k}N^{k})\right|}.

Hence, we obtain

\left|P\right|\geq c_{k}^{t}\cdot(N/2tN^{\prime})^{t-t^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{N^{t\left|J\right|}\cdot N^{k\cdot r\ell(k-1)}\cdot\left|A\right|^{r\ell}}{N^{r\ell k^{2}}}.

Again, recall our choice of parameters

Recalling that

7.
New Nikodym sets and new minimal blocking sets

In this section, we will first justify the simple correspondence between induced matchings in point–line incidence graphs and (weak) Nikodym sets, which was discussed in Section
1
. In dimensions
d\geq 3
, this dictionary immediately turns any large induced matching in dimension
(d-1)
into a small Nikodym set by taking a
Cartesian product. In dimension
2
, the same dictionary only gives
weak
Nikodym sets. In order to prove Theorem
1.11
, we will start instead with a high-dimensional matching constructed in Section
3
, and then develop a projection mechanism that will produce a Nikodym set in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
.

Last but not least, we will use the new Nikodym sets we construct to get new minimal blocking sets, establishing Theorem
1.13
.

7.1.
Small Nikodym sets in dimension
d\geq 3

Recall that for
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
and
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
we write

\ell(x,v):=\{x+\lambda v:\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ell(x,v)^{\ast}:=\ell(x,v)\setminus\{x\}.

A set
N\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
is a
Nikodym set
if for every
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
there exists
v\neq 0
with

\ell(x,v)^{\ast}\subset N
. A set
N\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
is a
weak Nikodym set
if for every
x\notin N
there exists

v\neq 0
with
\ell(x,v)^{\ast}\subset N
.

Proof of Proposition
1.5
.

(1) Nikodym
\Rightarrow
weak Nikodym, and the converse fails for
d=2
.

The implication is immediate from the definitions.
To see that the converse can fail in the plane, consider

N:=(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}:\ x\neq 0,\ y\neq 0\}.

If
p\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\setminus N
, then either
p=(a,0)
with
a\neq 0
, or
p=(0,b)
with
b\neq 0
, or
p=(0,0)
.
In these three cases one checks that the punctured line is contained in
N
by choosing respectively

v=(0,1),\qquad v=(1,0),\qquad v=(1,1).

Hence
N
is weak Nikodym.
On the other hand, if
p=(a,b)\in N
and
\ell
is any affine line through
p
, then
\ell
contains a point with

x
-coordinate
0
(unless
\ell
is vertical, in which case it contains a point with
y
-coordinate
0
).
Thus every line through
p
meets
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\setminus N
, so
N
is not Nikodym.

(2) Weak Nikodym sets and induced matchings are complements.

Suppose
N\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
is weak Nikodym.
For each
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\setminus N
, choose a line
\ell_{x}
with
\ell_{x}^{\ast}\subset N
.
If
x\neq x^{\prime}
are two points outside
N
, then
x^{\prime}\notin\ell_{x}
(since
\ell_{x}^{\ast}\subset N
but
x^{\prime}\notin N
),
so the pairs
\{(x,\ell_{x})\}_{x\notin N}
form an induced matching in the point–line incidence graph.
Conversely, if
\{(p,\ell_{p})\}_{p\in M}
is an induced matching, then with
N:=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\setminus M
we have

\ell_{p}^{\ast}\subset N
for each
p\in M
, i.e.
N
is weak Nikodym.

(3) Product trick: weak Nikodym
\Rightarrow
Nikodym one dimension up.

Assume
N\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
is weak Nikodym and set
N^{\prime}:=N\times\mathbb{F}_{q}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d+1}
.
Fix
(x,a)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d+1}
.
If
x\notin N
, pick
v_{x}\neq 0
with
\ell(x,v_{x})^{\ast}\subset N
and take
v^{\prime}:=(v_{x},0)
; then

\ell((x,a),v^{\prime})^{\ast}\subset N\times\{a\}\subset N^{\prime}
.
If
x\in N
, take
v^{\prime}:=(0,\dots,0,1)
; then
\ell((x,a),v^{\prime})^{\ast}=\{(x,a+\lambda):\lambda\neq 0\}\subset N^{\prime}
.
Thus
N^{\prime}
is a Nikodym set in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d+1}
.
∎

We now derive Theorem
1.10
.

Proof of Theorem
1.10
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d-1}
be the point set of an induced matching in
\mathcal{I}_{q}^{(d)-1}
,
and set
N_{0}:=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d-1}\setminus M
.
By Proposition

is weak Nikodym in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d-1}

Applying Proposition

is a Nikodym set. Its size is

We now choose

from the appropriate induced-matching theorem.

Three Dimensions.
Take

and let

be the point set of the induced matching from Proposition

. This gives

Higher dimensions.
Let

This gives

∎

7.2.
Nikodym sets in
2
dimensions

We now explain how to obtain the
polynomial
improvement for planar Nikodym sets from Theorem
1.11
. The main idea will be to use our high-dimensional point-line matching from Theorem
1.9
in order to produce a large set of lattice points in
[N]^{d}\times[M]
, each endowed with a suitable “escape line” (one should think of this as a certain Nikodym-like property in
\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
). We will then be able to project such a set to
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
.

We start by first recording this projection mechanism.

Proposition 7.1
.

Suppose
L,M,N
are positive integers with
LM\leq N
.
Let
P\subset[N]^{d}\times[M]
be a set of points with the following property:
for every
v\in[N]^{d}\times[M]
there exists a direction

s_{v}\in[-L,L]^{d}\times\{1\}

such that the (integer) punctured line
\ell(v,s_{v})^{\ast}:=\{v+ts_{v}:t\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}
is disjoint from
P
.

Then for every prime
q>(3N)^{d}
we have

\mathrm{Nikodym}(2,q)\ \leq\ q^{2}-|P|.

Proof.

Define a map
\phi:\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
by

\phi(n_{1},\dots,n_{d},m):=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}(3N)^{i-1}\bmod q,\ m\bmod q\Bigr).

Since
q>(3N)^{d}
, the base-
(3N)
expansion in the first coordinate is unique on the box

B:=\bigl[-(N-1),\,2N\bigr]^{d}\times\bigl[-(M-1),\,2M\bigr],

hence
\phi
is injective on
B
. In particular
\phi|_{P}
is injective and therefore

|\phi(P)|=|P|
.

Set

\mathcal{N}:=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\setminus\phi(P).

We claim that
\mathcal{N}
is a Nikodym set; since
|\mathcal{N}|=q^{2}-|P|
, this will prove the proposition.

Fix
w=(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
. Let

X:=\Bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}(3N)^{i-1}\bmod q:\ n_{i}\in[N]\Bigr\},\qquad Y:=\{1,2,\dots,M\}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q},

so that
\phi([N]^{d}\times[M])\subset X\times Y
.

Case 1:
w\notin X\times Y
.

If
w_{1}\notin X
, then the vertical line through
w
is disjoint from
X\times Y
and hence from
\phi(P)
.
If instead
w_{2}\notin Y
, then the horizontal line through
w
is disjoint from
X\times Y
and hence from
\phi(P)
.
In either case
w
has a punctured line contained in
\mathcal{N}
.

Case 2:
w\in X\times Y
.

Then there exists
v\in[N]^{d}\times[M]
with
w=\phi(v)
.
Let
z_{w}:=\phi(s_{v})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
. We claim that
\ell(w,z_{w})^{\ast}\cap\phi(P)=\emptyset
.

Take any
t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.
If
t\not\equiv t_{0}\pmod{q}
for every integer
t_{0}\in\{-M,\dots,M\}
, then

w_{2}+t\notin Y
(since
Y-Y\subset\{-M,\dots,M\}\bmod q
), and hence
w+tz_{w}\notin X\times Y
,
so certainly
w+tz_{w}\notin\phi(P)
.

Otherwise, write
t\equiv t_{0}\pmod{q}
with
t_{0}\in\{-M,\dots,M\}\setminus\{0\}
.
By linearity of
\phi
we have

w+tz_{w}=\phi(v)+t\phi(s_{v})=\phi(v+t_{0}s_{v})=:\phi(v^{\prime}).

Since
|t_{0}|\leq M
and
\|s_{v}\|_{\infty}\leq L
with
LM\leq N
, we have
v^{\prime}\in B
.
Moreover, by hypothesis
\ell(v,s_{v})^{\ast}\cap P=\emptyset
, so
v^{\prime}\notin P
.
Injectivity of
\phi
on
B
then implies
\phi(v^{\prime})\notin\phi(P)
, i.e.
w+tz_{w}\notin\phi(P)
.

Thus for every
t\neq 0
we have
w+tz_{w}\in\mathcal{N}
, proving that
\mathcal{N}
is Nikodym.
∎

We next explain how to produce sets
P\subset[N]^{d}
with many points and many “private” lines, as required above.
The following statement can be extracted from the high-dimensional induced-matching construction in Section
3
.

Proposition 7.2
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
d=O_{\varepsilon}(1)
such that for every
N\geq 1
one can find a set

P\subset[N]^{d}
with

|P|\ \gg_{\varepsilon}\ N^{d-\varepsilon},

and such that for each
p\in P
there exists a non-zero direction
s_{p}\in[-N^{\varepsilon},N^{\varepsilon}]^{d}
with

\ell(p,s_{p})^{\ast}\cap P=\emptyset.

Moreover, one may take
d\leq\exp(O(\varepsilon^{-1}))
.

Proof.

We recall the proof of Theorem
1.6
in Section
3
. Let
k
be a positive integer so that
2k+1
is prime and
\frac{2}{\log k}+\frac{1}{k^{2}}\leq\varepsilon
(thus
k\leq\exp(O(\varepsilon^{-1}))
), and let
I=I_{k}
be the index set introduced in Section
3
. Set
d=|I_{k}|+1
(thus
d\leq k^{O(1)}\leq\exp(O(\varepsilon^{-1}))
).

Observe that we may assume
N
is sufficiently large, by changing the implicit constant in
|P|\gg_{\varepsilon}N^{d-\varepsilon}
. At the price of further constants, we may assume
N=\lfloor q/4\rfloor
for some prime
q
(we do this only to match the notation used to prove Theorem
1.6
).

Now, for each large prime
q
, write
N=\lfloor{q/4\rfloor}
and
M=\lfloor{N^{1/k^{2}}\rfloor}
. We constructed a set
\Gamma_{s}\subset[N]^{I}
, and then considered a subset of
[q]\times[q]^{d-1}
defined by

P:=\{(z,x_{I})\in[q]\times[q]^{I}:z\in[N/C_{k}M],x_{I}\in\Gamma_{s}\}

with size

\left|P\right|\gg_{k}q^{d-\frac{2}{\log k}-\frac{1}{k^{2}}}\gg_{\varepsilon}N^{d-\varepsilon}.

For each
p=(z,x_{I})
in
P
, we define the line
\ell_{p}=\{(z+h,x_{I}+y_{I}h):h\in\mathbb{Z}\}
with slope
s_{p}=(1,y_{I})
, where
||y_{I}||_{\infty}\leq C_{k}M\leq N^{\varepsilon}
(using that
N
is large for the final inequality).

We checked that if
p,p^{\prime}\in P
satisfied
p^{\prime}\equiv p+t\cdot s_{p}\pmod{q}
for some
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, that
p=p^{\prime}
. This in particular implies that
\ell_{p}\cap P=\{p\}
or equivalently
\ell(p,s_{p})^{*}\cap P=\emptyset
, as desired.
∎

We now upgrade Proposition
7.2
to the stronger hypothesis needed in

7.1
, namely obtaining an escaping line for
every
point of the ambient box whose slope

has ‘
1

Corollary 7.3
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
d=O_{\varepsilon}(1)
such that for all
N\geq 1
, with

M:=N^{1-\varepsilon},\qquad L:=N^{\varepsilon},

there exists a set
P\subset[N]^{d}\times[M]
with

|P|\ \gg_{\varepsilon}\ N^{d+1-2\varepsilon},

and such that for every
v\in[N]^{d}\times[M]
there exists
s_{v}\in[-L,L]^{d}\times\{1\}
satisfying

\ell(v,s_{v})^{\ast}\cap P=\emptyset.

In fact, we may take
d=\exp(O(\varepsilon^{-1}))
.

Proof.

Let
d_{0}
be large enough so that Proposition
7.2
holds for
\varepsilon
. Now consider
N\geq 1
.
Let
P_{0}\subset[N]^{d_{0}}
be a set of size
|P_{0}|\gg_{\varepsilon}N^{d_{0}-\varepsilon}
and for each
u\in P_{0}
fix a slope

s_{u}^{(0)}\in[-L,L]^{d_{0}}
with
\ell(u,s_{u}^{(0)})^{\ast}\cap P_{0}=\emptyset
.

Define
P_{1}:=P_{0}\times[N]\subset[N]^{d_{0}+1}
. For
w=(u,n)\in P_{1}
set

s_{w}^{(1)}:=(s_{u}^{(0)},0)\in[-L,L]^{d_{0}+1}.

Then
\ell(w,s_{w}^{(1)})^{\ast}
projects onto
\ell(u,s_{u}^{(0)})^{\ast}
in the first

coordinates, which is disjoint from

(the projection of

onto the first

coordinates). Whence

\ell(w,s_{w}^{(1)})^{\ast}\cap P_{1}=\emptyset
.

If
w=(u,n)\in([N]^{d_{0}}\setminus P_{0})\times[N]
, take instead

s_{w}^{(1)}:=(0,\dots,0,1)\in[-L,L]^{d_{0}+1}.

Then
\ell(w,s_{w}^{(1)})^{\ast}
varies only in the last coordinate and never meets

.

Finally set
P:=P_{1}\times[M]\subset[N]^{d_{0}+1}\times[M]
and for

define

s_{v}:=(s_{w}^{(1)},1)\in[-L,L]^{d_{0}+1}\times\{1\}.

By construction,
\ell(v,s_{v})^{\ast}
projects onto
\ell(w,s_{w}^{(1)})^{*}
in the first

coordinates, whence
\ell(v,s_{v})^{*}\cap P=\emptyset
.
Moreover,

|P|=|P_{0}|\cdot N\cdot M\ \gg_{\varepsilon}\ N^{d_{0}-\varepsilon}\cdot N\cdot N^{1-\varepsilon}=N^{(d_{0}+1)+1-2\varepsilon}.

Renaming

completes the proof.
∎

Finally, we combine Corollary

with Proposition

to obtain planar Nikodym sets.

Proof of Theorem

.

Take

. By Corollary

such that for all

there is

Now let

be a large prime and set

. Then

, so Proposition

applies (with

and

∎

7.3.
Minimal blocking sets from Nikodym sets

Recall that
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
is the projective plane over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and that
B(2,q)
is the maximum cardinality of a minimal blocking set in
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
. In this short subsection, we verify Proposition
1.12
.

To this end, we use a change in perspective. While a blocking set is a set of points
P
which intersects every line in
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
, taking the dual yields a set of lines
L
where
\bigcup_{\ell\in L}\ell=\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
. We call such a set of lines a
cover
, and say
L
is a
minimal cover
if every
L^{\prime}\subsetneq L
is not a cover. Then, an equivalent way to define
B(2,q)
is as the maximum cardinality of a minimal cover of
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
.

With this established, we can prove our reduction.

Proof of Proposition
1.12
.

Fix a Nikodym set
N\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
of size
\mathrm{Nikodym}(2,q)
. We write points of
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
in projective notation
[x:y:z]
, with
[x:y:z]=[\lambda x:\lambda y:\lambda z]
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Recall the following basic facts about
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
.

(1)

\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
consists of the
q^{2}

affine points
of the form
[x:y:1]
which we identify with
(x,y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
, and the
(q+1)

points at infinity
of the form
[x:y:0]
.

(2)

The points at infinity lie on a single
line at infinity

\ell_{\infty}
, with equation
z=0
.

Henceforth, let us identify any point
(x,y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
with the corresponding affine point
[x:y:1]
.

For each
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
, there exists some direction
z_{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
so that
\ell(v,z_{v})^{*}\subset N
. Set
\ell_{v}:=\ell(v,z_{v})
. The union of
\{\ell_{v}:v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\}
contains all affine points of
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
. Hence, the line family

L_{0}=\{\ell_{v}:v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\}\cup\{\ell_{\infty}\}

covers
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
.

We observe that for each
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\backslash N
, the only element of
L_{0}
that contains
v
is
\ell_{v}
: indeed, for any
w\notin v
, all affine points of
\ell_{w}\backslash\{w\}
lies in
N
, so
v\notin\ell_{w}
. The line at infinity
\ell_{\infty}
consists of points at infinity, so it also cannot contain
v
.

Let
L\subset L_{0}
be any minimal subfamily of
L_{0}
that covers
\mathrm{PG}(2,q)
. In light of the preceding observation, we must have

L\supset\{\ell_{v}:v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\backslash N\}.

Therefore,
L
is a minimal cover with
\left|L\right|\geq q^{2}-\left|N\right|
, as desired.
∎

8.
Minimal distance configurations

In this section, we prove our results on minimal distance problem. First, we show our main observation, Theorem
1.15
.

Theorem 8.1

(Restate of Theorem
1.15
)
.

Fix
d\geq 1
and let
N,M,L\geq 1
be integers with
N\geq ML
.
Let
P\subset[N]^{d}\times[M]\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
be a set of lattice points.
Assume that for each
p\in P
we are given an integer direction vector

s_{p}=(u_{p},1)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}\qquad\text{with}\qquad\|u_{p}\|_{\infty}\leq L,

such that for all distinct
p,p^{\prime}\in P
one has

p^{\prime}\notin p+\mathbb{R}s_{p}

which, due to the last coordinate of
s_{p}
being
1
, is equivalent to
p^{\prime}\notin p+\mathbb{Z}s_{p}
. Then there exists a set of points
p_{1},\cdots,p_{|P|}\in[0,1]^{d+1}
and lines
\ell_{1},\cdots,\ell_{|P|}
with
p_{i}\in\ell_{i}
such that for each
i\neq j
, we have

d_{\infty}(p_{i},\ell_{j})\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2N}.

Proof.

Let
\varphi:[N]^{d}\times[M]\to[0,1]^{d+1}
be the linear map

\varphi(n_{1},\dots,n_{d},m):=\left(\frac{1}{N}n_{1},\cdots,\frac{1}{N}n_{d},\frac{L}{N}m\right)

where the image lies in
[0,1]^{d+1}
by the assumption
LM\leq N
. Let our point set be
Q=\{\varphi(p):p\in P\}
in
[0,1]^{d+1}
, and let the line
\ell_{q}
corresponding to
q=\varphi(p)\in Q
be defined by the direction vector
\varphi(s_{p})
.

It suffices to check that, for distinct
q=\varphi(p),q^{\prime}=\varphi(p^{\prime})\in Q
and
r=\varphi(p)+t\cdot\varphi(s_{p})
with
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\lVert{q^{\prime}-r\rVert}_{\infty}\geq\frac{1}{2N}.

This implies that
d_{\infty}(q^{\prime},\ell_{q})\geq 1/2N
, as desired.

We first suppose
\left|p_{d+1}^{\prime}-p_{d+1}-t\cdot(s_{p})_{d+1}\right|>\frac{1}{2L}
. Then we have that

||\varphi(p^{\prime})-\varphi(p)-t\cdot\varphi(s_{p})||_{\infty}\geq|\varphi(p^{\prime})_{d+1}-\varphi(p)_{d+1}-t\cdot\varphi(s_{p})_{d+1}|>\frac{L}{N}\cdot\frac{1}{2L}=\frac{1}{2N}.

Now we suppose
\left|p_{d+1}^{\prime}-p_{d+1}-t\cdot(s_{p})_{d+1}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2L}
. By definition, we have

(s_{p})_{d+1}=1
, so

\left|t-(p_{d+1}^{\prime}-p_{d+1})\right|\leq\frac{1}{2L}.

Set
\tilde{t}:=p_{d+1}^{\prime}-p_{d+1}
. Observe that

||\tilde{t}\cdot\varphi(s_{p})-t\cdot\varphi(s_{p})||_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2L}||\varphi(s_{p})||_{\infty}=\frac{1}{2L}\cdot\frac{L}{N}<\frac{1}{2N}

where we have
||\varphi(s_{p})||_{\infty}\leq\frac{L}{N}
by the assumptions that
s_{p}=(u_{p},1)
and
||u_{p}||_{\infty}\leq L
. Then, we note that
p^{\prime}-p-\tilde{t}\cdot s_{p}\neq 0
since
p^{\prime}\not\in p+\mathbb{R}s_{p}
, and each of the vectors
p^{\prime},p,s_{p},\tilde{t}
are integral. Hence, we must have

||p^{\prime}-p-\tilde{t}\cdot s_{p}||_{\infty}\geq 1.

Therefore, we have

||\varphi(p^{\prime})-\varphi(p)-\tilde{t}\cdot\varphi(s_{p})||_{\infty}\geq\frac{1}{N}||p^{\prime}-p-\tilde{t}\cdot s_{p}||_{\infty}\geq\frac{1}{N}

Finally, by the triangle inequality, we conclude that

||\varphi(p^{\prime})-\varphi(p)-t\cdot\varphi(s_{p}))||_{\infty}\geq||\varphi(p^{\prime})-\varphi(p)-\tilde{t}\cdot\varphi(s_{p})||_{\infty}-||(t-\tilde{t})\varphi(s_{p})||_{\infty}\geq\frac{1}{N}-\frac{1}{2N}=\frac{1}{2N}

as desired.
∎

Corollaries
1.16
and
1.17
follow by observing that the constructions in Theorems
1.2
and
1.6
happen to satisfy the “bounded slope criterion”.

Proof of Corollary
1.16
.

Part 1) follows immediately from part 2) and Lemma
2.2
. So it suffices to establish 2).

Recall the proof of Lemma
2.3
: Let
q
be any large prime. Let
N=\lfloor q/3\rfloor
and
M=\lfloor\sqrt{q}/2\rfloor
. Let
A
be the largest square–difference–free subset of
[\lfloor q/10\rfloor]
. We define the set

P=\{(x,y)\in[N]\times[M]:2x-y^{2}\in A\}

and for each
p=(x,y)\in P
, define the associated line
\ell_{p}=\{(x,y)+t(y,1):t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
with slope
s_{p}=(y,1)
. We showed that
\{(p,\ell_{p}):p\in P\}
is an induced matching in the point–line incidence graph of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
. In particular, this implies that
p^{\prime}\notin p+\mathbb{R}s_{p}
for each distinct
p,p^{\prime}\in P
. Hence, we can apply Theorem
1.15
with
L=M
, noting that
LM<N
by definition. We conclude that there exists points
p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}\in[0,1]^{2}
and lines
\ell_{1},\cdots,\ell_{p}
with
p_{i}\in\ell_{j}
and for distinct
i\neq j

d_{\infty}(p_{i},\ell_{j})\leq\delta

where
n=M|A|
and
\delta=\frac{1}{2N}
.

Thus assuming
\mathrm{PL}_{2}(0.5+c)
does hold, we must have

M|A|\leq(2N)^{1.5-c+o(1)}

which given our choice of
M
and
N
gives

|A|\leq N^{1-c+o(1)}.

Thus, any square–difference–free subset of
[\lfloor q/10\rfloor]
has size at most
q^{1-c+o(1)}
. Since this holds for any prime
q
, by Bertrand’s postulate this also holds with
\lfloor q/10\rfloor
replaced by any positive integer.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.17
.

Given
\gamma>0
, let
d
be the dimension given by Corollary
7.3
with
\varepsilon:=\gamma/3
(thus in particular
d\leq\exp(O(\gamma^{-1}))
). We will show that
\mathrm{PL}_{d+1}(\gamma)
fails, meaning we can take
d_{0}(\gamma)=d+1
.

By definition of
d
, given any
N
, we can find
P\subset[N]^{d}\times[N^{1-\varepsilon}]
of size
|P|\gg_{\varepsilon}N^{d+1-2\varepsilon}
, and slopes
s_{p}\in[-N^{-\varepsilon},N^{\varepsilon}]^{d}\times\{1\}
for
p\in P
so that
\ell(p,s_{p})^{*}\cap P=\emptyset
(recall that this means that there is no
t\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
so that
p+t\cdot s_{p}\in P
). In particular, we have that for distinct
p,p^{\prime}\in P
, that
p^{\prime}\not\in p+\mathbb{Z}s_{p}
.

Thus, we can apply Theorem
1.15
to
P
with parameters

N:=N,M:=N^{1-\varepsilon},L:=N^{\varepsilon}.

We conclude there exists a set of points
p_{1},\dots,p_{|P|}\in[0,1]^{d+1}
and lines
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{|P|}
with
p_{i}\in\ell_{i}
such that for each

we have

d_{\infty}(p_{i},\ell_{j})\geq\frac{1}{2N}.

Recalling

and
2\varepsilon<\gamma
, it is impossible for the bound “
n<(2N)^{d+1-\gamma+o(1)}
” to hold, meaning

∎

9.
Point–hyperplane matchings

The induced matching problem extends naturally beyond point-line incidences. For example, for an integer
d\geq 2
, let

\mathrm{IM}_{\mathrm{PH}}(d,q)
denote the maximum size of an induced matching in the point–hyperplane incidence graph of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
: the left vertex set is
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}

(points), the right vertex set is the set of affine hyperplanes in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, and a point is adjacent
to a hyperplane if and only if it lies on it.

The same eigenvalue method underlying (
1
) gives a uniform upper bound for all
d
.

Proposition 9.1

(Point–hyperplane upper bound)
.

Let
q
be a prime power and
d\geq 2
. If
P=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
and

H=\{\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}\}
is a family of affine hyperplanes in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
such that

p_{i}\in\pi_{j}\ \text{ holds if and only if }\ i=j,

then

n\leq q^{\frac{d+1}{2}}+q.

Equivalently,
\mathrm{IM}_{\mathrm{PH}}(d,q)\leq q^{\frac{d+1}{2}}+q
.

Proof.

Vinh
[
45
]
proved more generally that for any sets of points
P\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
and affine hyperplanes
H
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
,
the number of incidences satisfies

\Bigl|I(P,H)-\frac{|P||H|}{q}\Bigr|\leq q^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\sqrt{|P||H|},

see
[
45
]
. In our situation
|P|=|H|=n
and
I(P,H)=n
, so

\bigl|n-n^{2}/q\bigr|\leq q^{\frac{d-1}{2}}n.

If
n\leq q
there is nothing to prove. Otherwise
n^{2}/q-n\geq 0
, and the last inequality gives

\frac{n^{2}}{q}-n\leq q^{\frac{d-1}{2}}n,\qquad\text{i.e. }\qquad n\leq q\bigl(q^{\frac{d-1}{2}}+1\bigr)=q^{\frac{d+1}{2}}+q.

∎

For
d=3
, Proposition
9.1
asserts
\mathrm{IM}_{\mathrm{PH}}(3,q)\leq q^{2}+q
.
Perhaps surprisingly, in contrast with the planar case, we note that the correct scale in three dimensions is already achieved by a classical quadratic construction, for every prime or prime power
q
.

Assume
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\neq 2
and fix
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
such that
-a
is a non-square.
Consider the (elliptic) paraboloid

P\ :=\ \{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}:\ x=y^{2}+az^{2}\}\ =\ \{(y^{2}+az^{2},\,y,\,z):\ y,z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}.

For a point
p=(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})\in P
define the affine plane

\pi_{p}\ :=\ \Bigl\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}:\ x-2y_{0}y-2az_{0}z=-y_{0}^{2}-az_{0}^{2}\Bigr\}.

The next proposition will show that
\pi_{p}
is the tangent plane to
P
at
p
, in the sense that
\pi_{p}\cap P=\left\{p\right\}
for every
p\in P
. Since
|P|=q^{2}
, this example will thereby provide a lower bound construction for
\mathrm{IM}_{\mathrm{PH}}(3,q)
.

Proposition 9.2
.

With notation as above, the family of point–plane pairs

\{(p,\pi_{p}):\ p\in P\}

is an induced matching in the point–plane incidence graph of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
.
In particular,

\mathrm{IM}_{\mathrm{PH}}(3,q)\geq q^{2}.

Proof.

Fix
p=(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})\in P
. Since
x_{0}=y_{0}^{2}+az_{0}^{2}
, we have

x_{0}-2y_{0}y_{0}-2az_{0}z_{0}=-y_{0}^{2}-az_{0}^{2}

and hence
p\in\pi_{p}
.

Now let
p^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in P\cap\pi_{p}
. Substituting
x^{\prime}=y^{\prime 2}+az^{\prime 2}
into the plane equation gives

y^{\prime 2}+az^{\prime 2}-2y_{0}y^{\prime}-2az_{0}z^{\prime}\ =\ -y_{0}^{2}-az_{0}^{2},

or equivalently

(y^{\prime}-y_{0})^{2}+a(z^{\prime}-z_{0})^{2}\ =\ 0.

Since
-a
is a non-square, the only solution is
y^{\prime}=y_{0}
and
z^{\prime}=z_{0}
. It follows that
p^{\prime}=p
, so

\pi_{p}\cap P=\{p\}
.

Therefore, no point in
P
lies on the plane corresponding to a different point, and the set of
pairs
\{(p,\pi_{p}):p\in P\}
is an induced matching.
∎

Remark 9.3
.

The construction in Proposition
9.2
is an affine model of the classical
Brown construction/elliptic quadric (ovoid) in
\mathrm{PG}(3,q)
; see, for instance,
[
21
, Chapter 4.3]
.
Combined with Proposition
9.1
, it yields

\mathrm{IM}_{\mathrm{PH}}(3,q)=q^{2}+O(q).

It is tempting to try to imitate the construction from Proposition
9.2
in higher dimensions by hyperplanes which are tangent to a quadratic hypersurface. The preceding proof, however, is quite specific to dimension
d=3
: the “error term” is controlled by a binary quadratic form
u^{2}+av^{2}
with no non–trivial zeros, which is only available in two variables. Determining the correct order of magnitude of
\mathrm{IM}_{\mathrm{PH}}(d,q)
for
d\geq 4
seems to be an interesting
finite-geometric question, related to the existence of large minimal blocking sets in higher dimensions
[
32
]
.

10.
Concluding remarks

This paper develops several new lower bounds for induced matchings in point–line incidence graphs over finite fields.
We close by highlighting a few directions where we think the induced-matching viewpoint can keep paying dividends.

Simple things we don’t know

On the upper-bound side our understanding is still rather limited.
For
d=2
, Vinh’s finite-field Szemerédi–Trotter theorem
[
45
]
implies that for every prime power
q
,

\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\ \leq\ q^{3/2}+q.

(15)

This
q^{3/2}
exponent is sharp up to constants when
q
is a square, thanks to the Hermitian unital.
Beyond this, however, essentially no nontrivial upper bounds are known for point–line induced matchings in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
once
d\geq 3
.
In contrast to the point–hyperplane setting (where one still has strong spectral/incidence tools),
we do not currently know any interesting analogue of (
15
) for points and lines in higher dimensions. Even a qualitative asymptotic improvement over the trivial bound
\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\leq q^{d}
would be a major advance.

Conjecture 10.1
.

For every fixed
d\geq 3
,

\mathrm{IM}(d,q)\ =\ o(q^{d})\qquad\text{as }q\to\infty.

In the planar case, the sharp examples for (
15
) are fundamentally extension-field phenomena.
Naturally, this motivates the following strengthening for induced matchings over a prime field.

Conjecture 10.2
.

There exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that for all sufficiently large
primes

q
,

\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\ \leq\ q^{3/2-c}.

Morally, Conjecture
10.2
asserts that one cannot realize a “unitary-like” configuration over a prime field which would support induced matchings on the
q^{3/2}
scale. Two rather remarkable consequences of such a prime-field saving are worth recording.

Cliques in Paley graphs

Assume
q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
is prime and let
G_{q}
be the Paley graph on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
As observed in Proposition
2.1
, Paley cliques lift to induced matchings:

q\,\omega(G_{q})\ \leq\ \mathrm{IM}(2,q).

(16)

Consequently, Conjecture
10.2
would immediately yield a matching power saving for Paley clique numbers,
with the
same
exponent:

\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\ \ll\ q^{3/2-c}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\omega(G_{q})\ \ll\ q^{1/2-c}.

Breaking the square-root barrier for Paley graphs of prime order by a polynomial factor is a classical open problem
at the intersection of additive combinatorics, pseudorandomness, and analytic number theory; see, e.g.,

[
48
,
26
]
and the references therein.
The strongest unconditional improvement in the prime case remains only a constant-factor gain due to Hanson–Petridis
[
22
]
.

The Furstenberg–Sárközy problem

Our Ruzsa set lifting (Proposition
2.3
and its prime-power variants) shows that induced matchings
interact naturally with the Furstenberg–Sárközy problem.
Let

s(N)\ :=\ \max\Bigl\{|A|:\ A\subset[N],\ (A-A)\cap\{m^{2}:\ m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}=\emptyset\Bigr\}

denote the largest size of a subset of
[N]=\{1,\dots,N\}
with no
nonzero
square difference.
The Furstenberg–Sárközy theorem asks about the asymptotic behavior of
s(N)
, as a function of
N
.
The current record for the upper bound is due to Green–Sawhney
[
19
]
:

s(N)\ \ll\ N\exp\!\bigl(-c\sqrt{\log N}\bigr).

(17)

A well-known folklore goal is to strengthen this to a
power saving
:

s(N)\ \ll\ N^{1-c}

(18)

for some absolute
c>0
.

Conjecture
10.2
would imply such a power saving (and with the same exponent).
Indeed, Proposition
2.3
shows that if
A\subset[\lfloor q/10\rfloor]
is square-difference-free, then

\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\ \gg\ q^{1/2}|A|.

Therefore, if
\mathrm{IM}(2,q)\ll q^{3/2-c}
holds for primes
q
, we obtain

|A|q^{1/2}\ \ll\ \mathrm{IM}(2,q)\ \ll\ q^{3/2-c}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad|A|\ \ll\ q^{1-c}\ \asymp\ N^{1-c},

which is exactly (
18
).
Thus Conjecture
10.2
would provide a rather unexpected route to the long-sought power-saving regime
for Furstenberg–Sárközy.

Euclidean point–line separation and Heilbronn

Induced matchings are also a finite-field model for Euclidean point–line separation questions.
Given
n
pairs
\{p_{i}\in\ell_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
in the unit square, define

\delta:=\min_{i\neq j}d(p_{i},\ell_{j}),

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes Euclidean distance.
Cohen–Pohoata–Zakharov
[
11
]
proved that one always has
\delta\lesssim n^{-2/3+o(1)}
,
and this implies the upper bound
\Delta(n)\lesssim n^{-7/6+o(1)}
for Heilbronn’s triangle problem.

A natural open problem is to improve the
2/3
exponent in the minimal-distance problem to
2/3+c
.
Such an improvement would generate a further new record of
\Delta(n)\lesssim n^{-7/6-c+o(1)}
for the Heilbronn triangle problem. In Corollary
1.16
, we also observed that improvements for the minimal distance problem have nontrivial implications for square-difference-free sets (via a geometric encoding of the type used in Proposition
2.3
). We view this as further evidence that induced matchings provide a useful organizing principle for the intersection of finite-field incidence combinatorics, Euclidean separation, and additive number theory.

Ramsey theory and forbidden configurations

Finite-geometric incidence structures have long supplied extremal and Ramsey constructions via their incidence graphs. A striking recent example is the work of Mattheus–Verstraëte
[
33
]
on
R(4,t)
:
they exploit the Hermitian unital and its “no O’Nan configuration” property to build very dense graphs
avoiding the one-subdivision of
K_{4}
, leading to a nearly sharp lower bound for
R(4,t)
.

Our norm-hypersurface construction from Theorem
1.8
can be viewed as higher-degree analogues of unitary geometry: we obtain large point sets
P
lying on an algebraic hypersurface together with a distinguished line through each
p\in P
meeting
P
only at
p
. It would be very interesting to understand whether these higher-
k
objects also satisfy local forbidden-configuration phenomena in the spirit of the O’Nan property, and whether such structure could be leveraged to build new extremal and Ramsey constructions.

Appendix A
Polynomial identities via Lagrange interpolation

In this appendix, we record the proof of Proposition
A.1
that we used in Section
6
in the proof of Theorem
1.9
.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Then

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{k-1}{i}\,\bigl(f(x)-i\bigr)^{k}=k!\,f(x)-\frac{k!(k-1)}{2}.

(19)

Equivalently,

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{k-1}{i}\left(2f+k-1-2i\right)^{k}=k!\cdot 2^{k}\cdot f.

Proof.

We use the following fairly well-known polynomial identity.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
n\geq 0
, and let

p(t)=a_{0}t^{n}+a_{1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{n-1}t+a_{n}

be a polynomial with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
. Then, for all
a,b
with
b\neq 0
, we have that

\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{\,n-j}\binom{n}{j}\,p(a+jb)=a_{0}\,b^{n}\,n!.

(20)

Lemma
A.2
is typically attributed to Boole and can be proved, for example, using Lagrange interpolation formula. See
[
35
]
and the references therein. To derive Proposition
A.1
from Lemma
A.2
, we will work in the coefficient ring
\mathbb{Q}[x]
, and set
c:=\dfrac{k-1}{2}
.
Consider the polynomial in
t
,

P(t):=\bigl(f(x)-t\bigr)^{k}-\bigl(c-t\bigr)^{k}\in\mathbb{Q}[x][t].

As a polynomial in
t
, both
(f(x)-t)^{k}
and
(c-t)^{k}
have leading term
(-t)^{k}
,
so the
t^{k}
terms cancel and hence
\deg_{t}P\leq k-1
.

We now compute the coefficient of
t^{k-1}
in
P(t)
. Using the binomial theorem,

(f(x)-t)^{k}=\sum_{r=0}^{k}\binom{k}{r}f(x)^{k-r}(-t)^{r},

so the
t^{k-1}
-term is
\binom{k}{k-1}f(x)(-t)^{k-1}=kf(x)(-1)^{k-1}t^{k-1}
.
Similarly, the
t^{k-1}
-term of
(c-t)^{k}
is
kc(-1)^{k-1}t^{k-1}
.
Therefore the leading coefficient of
P(t)
(as a degree
\leq k-1
polynomial in
t
) is

a_{0}=(-1)^{k-1}k\bigl(f(x)-c\bigr).

Apply Lemma
A.2
to
P(t)
with
n=k-1
,
a=0
,
b=1
to get:

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{\,k-1-i}\binom{k-1}{i}\,P(i)=a_{0}\cdot 1^{k-1}\cdot(k-1)!=(-1)^{k-1}k\bigl(f(x)-c\bigr)(k-1)!.

Multiplying both sides by
(-1)^{k-1}
yields

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{k-1}{i}\,P(i)=k!\bigl(f(x)-c\bigr).

(21)

Expanding
P(i)=(f(x)-i)^{k}-(c-i)^{k}
in (
21
) gives

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{k-1}{i}\,(f(x)-i)^{k}\;-\;\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{k-1}{i}\,(c-i)^{k}=k!\bigl(f(x)-c\bigr).

(22)

It remains to show that the second sum in (
22
) is
0
. Let

T:=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{k-1}{i}\,(c-i)^{k}.

Pair the summand with index
i
with the summand at index
k-1-i
. Since

\binom{k-1}{k-1-i}=\binom{k-1}{i}
and
c-(k-1-i)=\frac{k-1}{2}-(k-1-i)=-(c-i)
, we obtain

(c-(k-1-i))^{k}=\bigl(-(c-i)\bigr)^{k}=(-1)^{k}(c-i)^{k}.

Moreover,
(-1)^{k-1-i}=(-1)^{k-1}(-1)^{i}
. It follows that the paired summand equals

(-1)^{k-1-i}\binom{k-1}{k-1-i}(c-(k-1-i))^{k}=-\,(-1)^{i}\binom{k-1}{i}(c-i)^{k},

Thus each pair cancels, and if
k
is odd the
unique fixed point
i=c=(k-1)/2
contributes
(c-c)^{k}=0
. Therefore
T=0
.

With
T=0
, equation (
22
) becomes

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{k-1}{i}\,(f(x)-i)^{k}=k!\bigl(f(x)-c\bigr)=k!\,f(x)-k!\,\frac{k-1}{2},

which is exactly (
19
).
∎
