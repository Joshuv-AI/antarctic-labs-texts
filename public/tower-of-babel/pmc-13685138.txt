Analysis of the association between low back and neck pain with cardiovascular risk factors according to different domains of physical activity: longitudinal study
Authors: Anna Amélia P Motta-Oishi, William R Tebar, Claudiele CM Silva, Rafael Z Pinto, Italo R Lemes, Danilo RP Silva, Gabriel G Cucato, Eduardo R de Victo, Gerson Ferrari, Diego GD Christofaro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical activity (PA) is promoted for treating and preventing low back and neck pain and cardiovascular risk factors, but which specific levels and domains are associated with cardiovascular risks in these individuals remain unclear. The aim of this study is to evaluate the association between individuals with persistent low back pain and neck pain (pain in baseline and follow-up) and cardiovascular risk factors, considering different domains and levels of PA.MethodsThis prospective cohort study with a 2-year follow-up analyzed the association between different levels and domains of PA and cardiovascular risk factors in 331 adults followed longitudinally with persistent low back and/or neck pain. Data were collected through in-person interviews covering age, sex, socioeconomic status, PA, low back and neck pain, and self-reported cardiovascular risk factors. PA was assessed using a validated questionnaire and participants were categorized as persistently active, persistently inactive, or inactive at one time point (baseline or follow-up) across different PA domains. Binary logistic regression was used for analysis, with all models adjusted for age, sex, and socioeconomic status.ResultsIn the sport PA domain, individuals inactive at one time point with neck pain showed an association with hypertension (OR = 2.96 [95%CI = 1.13; 7.78] p = 0.027), while an association between low back pain and high cholesterol was observed among persistently inactive individuals (OR = 3.70 [95%CI = 1.27; 10.75] p = 0.016). In the leisure-time PA and locomotion domain, persistently active individuals with neck pain showed an association with diabetes (OR = 8.46 [95%CI = 1.26; 56.73] p = 0.028), whereas individuals with low back pain who were persistently inactive were associated with high triglycerides (OR = 4.22 [95%CI = 1.21; 14.70] p = 0.028) and high LDL cholesterol (OR = 4.96 [95%CI = 1.82; 13.53] p = 0.001). In the occupational PA domain, individuals inactive at one time point with low back pain showed an association with high LDL cholesterol (OR = 3.09 [95%CI = 1.06; 8.98] p = 0.040).ConclusionPersistent physical inactivity was more frequently associated with unfavorable cardiovascular risk factors among adults with low back pain, whereas associations among individuals with neck pain varied according to PA domain and level.Keywords: Musculoskeletal pain, Dyslipidemias, Exercise, Public Health

Introduction

Low back pain is one of the most prevalent musculoskeletal problems in the adult population and is estimated to affect approximately 840 million people by 2050. Neck pain had a global prevalence of 203 million people, and it is expected that by the year 2050 there will be an increase to 269 million people [1]. Neck and lower back pain are among the leading causes of years lived with disability in the world. Furthermore, studies have shown an increased prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension and obesity in individuals with chronic pain [2]. Oliveira et al., in a systematic review, reported that participants who had chronic musculoskeletal pain were approximately twice as likely to develop cardiovascular disease [3]. Similarly, Yin et al. followed 4,445 participants for 13.7 years and found that individuals with chronic pain were at greater risk of developing cardiovascular risk factors [4]. Some explanations for this association are related to the increase in stressors generated by neck pain and chronic low back pain, which are also present in the presence of cardiovascular risk factors [5–7]. This complex and potentially bidirectional interaction between chronic pain and cardiovascular risk may be further modified by behavioral factors, such as physical activity (PA) patterns, which can differentially influence neuroendocrine, inflammatory, and autonomic pathways [8].

Some authors propose that individuals with neck pain and chronic low back pain are also more likely to have lower levels of PA which is widely known as a risk factor for the development of cardiovascular diseases [9]. Booth et al. reported that inactive individuals have a 45% higher risk of developing cardiovascular disease and 26% to 35% more likely to develop type II diabetes [10–13]. Analyzing the association of specific domains with cardiovascular risk factors, there are different associations. Pitanga et al. conducted a cross-sectional study in Brazil where they observed an inverse association between cardiovascular risk factors and leisure-time PA but not observed in the active locomotion domain [14].

However, Viktorisson et al. in a longitudinal study found a lower risk of having a stroke in physically active individuals in the leisure and active locomotion domain [15]. In the occupational domain, Fan and collaborators reported that an increase in the level of PA in this domain presented a lower risk of developing cardiovascular risk factors [16]. Different domains of PA can exert distinct effects on health due to differences in intensity, autonomy, psychosocial stress, and recovery patterns [17, 18]. Leisure-time PA is often voluntary, while occupational PA may involve prolonged exertion, limited recovery, and higher stress levels, which can lead to different responses. These findings indicate that PA may act as a key behavioral pathway linking chronic musculoskeletal pain to cardiovascular risk, potentially mitigating or exacerbating this relationship depending on activity level and domain.

The heterogeneity observed in previous studies across different PA domains highlights an important knowledge gap in populations with persistent musculoskeletal pain, particularly regarding how domain-specific activity patterns may influence cardiovascular risk. Understanding these associations is essential to better inform preventive strategies and interventions. Given these gaps, this study aimed to investigate whether associations between persistent low back and neck pain and cardiovascular risk factors differ according to PA domains and levels.

Materials and methods

Study population and procedures

A prospective cohort study with 2-year follow-up in the general community was conducted through a random sampling process that considered the division of the city into geographic regions and the randomization of all streets in each region, and all homes on the selected streets were visited. Representativeness was enhanced through a stratified multistage cluster sampling design. Stratification by city regions ensured coverage of heterogeneous urban and socioeconomic contexts. The random selection of streets within each stratum minimized investigator-driven selection and reduced the likelihood of convenience sampling.

Data collection for all study periods ranged from April 2016 to October 2019 and was conducted by experienced evaluators in face-to-face interviews in a single day at the participant’s home. To collect information, the Data Kit (ODK) digital interface was used. Additional methodological details, including the full structure of the interview instrument, have been previously published elsewhere and are available in a prior publication [19].

The baseline sample for this study consisted of 843 participants. After two years, baseline participating households were visited and 449 baseline participants were contacted and included in the longitudinal study. Of the 449 participants, 105 did not agree to participate in the evaluation, 7 reported not having the physical or psychological conditions to participate, and 6 died between the study phases. Thus, the final sample including all participants in the longitudinal wave was 331 participants. Individuals were included in the study if they were 18 and older of age, non-institutionalized individuals, living in the urban and no current physical limitations that make it impossible for the participant to stand up (e.g., wheelchair users, bed-ridden). Informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study. The exclusion criterion was not responding to all items in the questionnaire.

The calculation of the minimum sample size for this study was carried out using an equation for a finite population [20]. Considering the prevalence of low back pain of 28.8% [21] a tolerable sampling error of 5% and a confidence level of 95%, the minimum size required for the longitudinal sample was established at 316 individuals. The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee.

Low back pain and neck pain

To assess low back pain and neck pain, the Nordic questionnaire was used [22–24].

We emphasize that the Nordic questionnaire does not indicate the intensity or possible interference of pain on functionality. For the present analyses, we considered symptoms reported during the previous 7 days to minimize recall bias and reduce possible overestimation of pain prevalence in this longitudinal follow-up study. Therefore, participants who reported having some type of pain at baseline and at follow-up were considered to have persistent neck pain and/or low back pain.

Physical activity

To evaluate PA practice, the Baecke questionnaire [25] previously validated for the Brazilian adult population was used [26]. This instrument considers the different domains of physical activity [occupational (physical exertion such as standing, walking, and carrying weights in the work routine), leisure (sports or exercise programs during leisure time) and commuting physical activity(active commuting to work, shopping, etc.)], and the sum of the different domains provides a dimensionless score where the higher the score, the more active the participant tends to be. Tertiles were calculated separately at baseline and at follow-up for each physical activity domain of the Baecke questionnaire. Participants in the highest tertile were classified as most active, whereas those in the lower tertiles were classified as least active in each moment (baseline and follow-up). The longitudinal physical activity patterns were defined by combining classifications from the both time points, so that participants classified as most active at both baseline and follow-up were considered as persistently active, whereas those least active at both assessments were considered as persistently inactive, and participants who were most active at only one moment were classified as inactive at one time point. The baseline cutoff values for tertiles were: ≤1.500, 1.501–2.000, > 2.000 for sport domain; ≤1.750, 1.751–2.250, > 2.250 for leisure and commuting domain; and ≤ 2.625, 2.626–3.250, > 3.250 for occupational domain. The follow-up cutoff values for tertiles were: ≤1.750, 1.751–2.000, > 2.000 for sport domain; ≤2.000, 2.001–2.500, > 2.500 for leisure and commuting domain; and ≤ 2.500; 2.501–3.000; >3.000 for occupational domain.

We adopted the tertile to classify the participants’ physical activity because Baecke’s questionnaire has a dimensionless score, where the higher the scores in the different domains, the greater the practice of physical activity.

Cardiovascular risk factors

Cardiovascular risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, high cholesterol, high triglycerides and hypertension were assessed by self-report, which reported the presence or absence of some of these diseases through medical diagnosis, time of diagnosis and whether the participant uses medication for this condition [27].

Covariates

The variables age, sex, socioeconomic status was included as covariates in the present study (i.e., confounding parameters). To assess the socioeconomic status of the participants, the Brazilian Economic Classification Criteria was used [28]. This instrument provides a continuous score ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating higher socioeconomic status, which was analyzed as a continuous variable in the present study. According to established cutoff points, scores correspond to socioeconomic classes A (45–100 points), B1 (38–44 points), B2 (29–37 points), C1 (23–28 points), C2 (17–22 points), and D–E (0–16 points).

Statistical analysis

The sample characterization variables were described as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range based on data normality. The association between cardiovascular risk factors and persistent neck and/or low back pain was also analyzed using Binary Logistic Regression considering stratification by levels of PA (persistently inactive, active at one time and persistently active) also adjusting for sex, age and socioeconomic status. The statistical significance adopted was 5% and the confidence interval was 95%.

Persistent neck pain is reported by 20.8% of the sample, and persistent low back pain by 34.7%. In relation to health conditions, hypertension affects 46.6%, diabetes 18.2%, high triglycerides 9.7%, and high LDL cholesterol 22.7% of the participants. Baseline characteristics were compared between participants retained in the follow-up (n = 331) and those who were lost (n = 511). Retained participants were older (59.7 ± 17.3 vs. 54.5 ± 18.7 years, p < 0.001), more frequently female (68.3% vs. 57.3%, p = 0.001), and presented lower leisure-time and commuting physical activity scores (2.01 ± 0.67 vs. 2.20 ± 0.73, p < 0.001). No differences were observed in proportions of socioeconomic status, neck pain, low back pain, hypertension, diabetes, triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, or in mean values of occupational and sport physical activity (p > 0.05 for all).

Figure 1 presented the proportions of persistent neck pain and low back pain according to the presence of the cardiovascular risk factors. When compared to participants without persistent pain, a higher prevalence of hypertension was observed (59.4% vs. 45.8% [p = 0.044]) and high LDL cholesterol (31.9% vs. 20.2% [p = 0.040]) among participants with persistent neck pain, as well as a higher prevalence of high LDL cholesterol among participants with persistent low back pain (33.0% vs. 17.2% [p = 0.001]), when compared to participants without low back pain.

Discussion

The results of this study revealed that individuals with neck pain and persistent low back pain have an association with some cardiovascular risk factors when compared to individuals without persistent neck and low back pain during the two-year follow-up. To our knowledge, this is the first study that evaluates the association between individuals with persistent low back and neck pain considering occupational, sport and leisure domains and locomotion at different levels of PA. This is important to understand the impact that different domains and levels of PA have on the association between persistent neck and low back pain and cardiovascular risk factors.

In the domain of PA in sport, individuals with neck pain and inactive at one moment indicated an increased risk for hypertension and persistently inactive individuals showed an association with high LDL cholesterol. In the domain of leisure-time PA and locomotion, persistently inactive individuals with low back pain were associated with high triglycerides and LDL cholesterol, and persistently active individuals with neck pain showed an association with diabetes. In the domain of occupational PA, only individuals with low back pain who are inactive at one moment are associated with high LDL cholesterol.

Bruhel et al. conducted a study evaluating the relationship between chronic pain and hypertension. These authors found that 39% of individuals with chronic pain had hypertension compared to 21% of individuals without chronic pain [27]. Another study conducted by Al-Ghamdi et al. with 1031 in Saudi Arabia also observed similar results [29].

These results partially corroborate with the present study, since neck pain was associated with a greater chance of hypertension in inactive individuals when practicing sports. One of the possible mechanisms is that persistent neck pain could cause a reduction in PA. Furthermore, Skrzypek et al. in a study with hypertensive patients observed that those with chronic back pain would have a higher prevalence of atherosclerotic plaques in the carotid artery, which could contribute to higher values of arterial hypertension [30].

When analyzing the association of chronic neck pain with diabetes, some authors observed that individuals with chronic neck pain are associated with type II diabetes [31, 32].

Pozzobon et al. conducted a systematic review where they observed that individuals with diabetes have a greater risk of developing chronic neck pain [33]. Some possible explanations are related to the biochemical changes present in both diabetes and chronic pain mechanism that can lead to tissue damage [34, 35]. However, our study aimed, through a timeline, to identify whether individuals with persistent neck pain are at greater risk of developing diabetes by analyzing different levels and domains of PA.

Our study observed that the association between neck pain and diabetes was statistically significant in individuals persistently active in active commuting. However, this association should be interpreted with caution due to the wide confidence interval, suggesting limited precision, possibly due to the reduced number of diabetes cases in the specific subgroup analyzed. These results contradict several studies that demonstrated that 2.5 h of brisk walking per week (which would be possible in the commuting domain) would be enough to reduce the risk of diabetes by 30% [36–38]. Divney et al. conducted a study that evaluated active commuting and diabetes. In this study they observed that individuals who have a higher level of PA while commuting had a lower risk of developing diabetes.

However, after adjusting by race and socioeconomic factors, this protective effect decreased.

Therefore, the authors reported that the relationship between type II diabetes and PA during commuting is highly influenced by socioeconomic factors [39]. Furthermore, a possible explanation for our findings may be related to the concept that type II diabetes has a long latency period, and the individuals evaluated could already have diabetes but would not be diagnosed [12]. It is also known that PA is just one of the pillars for the treatment of diabetes, which must be associated with diet, medications, weight loss and healthy habits [40], which may explain why, despite being persistently active, these individuals presented an association with diabetes. Another relevant aspect is related to the fact that at the time of diagnosis, the health team involved could start with recommendations to increase the level of PA to control the disease. Another point to consider is that this pattern is consistent with protopathic bias [41], in which persistent physical activity reflects an early behavioral change in response to emerging metabolic dysfunction and related musculoskeletal symptoms that precede a formal diagnosis of diabetes. Finally, even though participants were active, the association between neck pain and the risk of developing diabetes could be explained by several mechanisms. Although physical activity is a protective factor for metabolic outcomes, persistent pain may be associated with chronic inflammatory processes, neuroendocrine dysfunction, and a sustained increase in the physiological stress response, including alterations in cortisol levels [7, 42]. These factors could be related to insulin resistance and impaired glucose metabolism. Additionally, the presence of chronic pain can negatively influence sleep quality and movement levels throughout the day, as well as the effectiveness of physical activity, especially regarding the intensity and regularity necessary for metabolic protection [43].

Individuals with persistent low back pain were associated with high cholesterol and triglycerides exclusively. In the occupational domain, individuals who were inactive at one time had a greater chance of developing high cholesterol. In the domain of PA in sport, persistently inactive individuals had an increased risk of developing high cholesterol and in the active commuting domain, persistently inactive individuals with persistent low back pain had a higher risk of developing triglycerides and high cholesterol. These findings indicate that individuals with persistent low back pain and who are inactive, regardless of the domain, are at increased risk of developing dyslipidemia. Episodes of pain have been related to a greater inflammatory profile, mainly with an increase in cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-alpha. These mechanisms could contribute to the relationship between low back pain and dyslipidemia, since an increased inflammatory profile has also been observed associated with dyslipidemia [44, 45]. Furthermore Kim et al. has also shown that this increased inflammatory profile could also contribute to the acceleration of disc degeneration, effectively contributing to low back pain [46].

However, no associations were observed between low back pain and cardiovascular risk factors in persistently active participants. This result observed over the follow-up period of this study may suggest the protective role of physical activity, which could improve the metabolic profile, decrease systemic inflammation, and enhance musculoskeletal function, potentially attenuating the coexistence of low back pain and cardiovascular risk. In this sense, Bourke et al. conducted a systematic review in which they observed that a level of PA on average of 600 METs/week can reduce high cholesterol (LDL cholesterol) by 3.2% [47]. An interesting finding aligned with our results is that for a person who uses active commuting for 1 min per day, a 10-minute increase in active commuting is associated with a decrease in total cholesterol [48]. Therefore, as the main message of the present study, subjects who have back pain should try to increase PA levels, whatever the domain, to prevent cardiovascular risk factors. Furthermore, other possible mechanisms could also contribute to the observed findings. While stress and psychosocial factors might play a greater role in cervical pain [49, 50], mechanical factors could be more significant in low back pain and, theoretically, more responsive to physical activity [51].

Considering potential implications for public health, the work environment seems to be the most difficult to modify, due to the specific demands of each work activity. However, simple strategies such as getting up every thirty minutes and breaking sedentary behavior can help, since breaking sedentary behavior has been associated with less skeletal pain and cardiovascular risk factors [52, 53]. Regarding locomotion, this area is often susceptible to changes in urban infrastructure, which can be more difficult to alter due to its complexity. In this context, the area where health promotion actions can be emphasized is that of physical activity during leisure time. Shiri et al., in a meta-analysis with over 158,000 participants, showed that leisure-time physical activity was a protective factor against low back pain [54].

Similar findings were also observed in another meta-analysis, however, examining the role of leisure-time physical activity in cardiovascular risk factors [55]. From this perspective, interventions should emphasize low-barrier, accessible forms of physical activity, such as walking, cycling, and recreational activities adapted to individual pain tolerance and functional capacity, which can be an interesting alternative. Community-based programs, the use of public spaces (especially in areas with lower socioeconomic status), and structured group activities may further facilitate engagement and long-term adherence.

This present study has some limitations. Initially, data collection on the presence of cardiovascular risk factors was through self-report. This type of measure may be subject to recall bias and misclassification, since participants may inaccurately report previous diagnoses, medication use, or BP values. Consequently, the prevalence of some cardiovascular risk indicators may have been underestimated or overestimated. Another limitation to consider is the loss of participants between the initial and follow-up assessments.

Although participants were recruited using a probability sampling strategy, the reduction in sample size may have introduced attrition bias. Furthermore, despite being widely used, the level of PA was assessed through questionnaires, which may be susceptible to recall bias. We emphasize that when classifying participants by tertiles based on physical activity levels, there may be cases of participants with moderate physical activity classified as inactive. In addition, other relevant confounders, such as body mass index, smoking, dietary factors, and medication use, were not included as adjustment variables. The multiple additional adjustment variables in the relatively small analytical groups resulting from stratification analyses could further compromise model stability and increase the risk of overfitting, so that only core demographic and socioeconomic confounders were included (age, sex and socioeconomic status). Therefore, the residual confounding cannot be excluded, especially for the lipid-related outcomes. Another limitation is the relatively small number of participants within some PA subgroups, which may have resulted in unstable estimates and wide confidence intervals for certain associations or bounds close to the null value, which reflected limited statistical precision and should be interpreted cautiously. Given the number of stratified models performed, the possibility of inflated Type I error also cannot be excluded.

Moreover, the lack of objective measures of PA (e.g., accelerometry) and biological markers (e.g., blood biomarkers; absence of inflammatory markers) limits the ability to strengthen causal inferences, and the possibility of attrition bias cannot be excluded due to observed differences in age, sex, and leisure-time physical activity between participants retained and those lost to follow-up. However, the present study presents positive aspects that deserve to be highlighted, including the longitudinal design, which avoids reverse causality. Another point refers to random sample selection, which avoids homogeneities likely to occur in samples selected for convenience. The analysis of the association of neck and low back pain with cardiovascular risk factors considering the different domains of PA is another factor to be considered positively. Another point to consider is that most studies on this topic have been conducted in developed countries, while the information from our study comes from the reality of a developing country. The use of a standardized and widely validated instrument to assess musculoskeletal pain strengthens the reliability and comparability of the findings across studies.

Conclusion

The results of this prospective cohort study indicate that persistent physical inactivity is more frequently associated with unfavorable cardiovascular risk factors among adults with low back pain. In contrast, among individuals with neck pain, associations with cardiovascular risk factors varied according to the domain and level of PA. Health promotion strategies should be targeted at individuals with persistent neck and back pain to reduce the risk of cardiovascular risk factors.
