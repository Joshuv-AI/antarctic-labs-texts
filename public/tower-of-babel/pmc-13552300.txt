Reconciling the Formal and Hidden Curricula in General Practice Training
Authors: Ross Munro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis reflective letter explores the relationship between the formal curriculum and the hidden curriculum (HC) in UK general practice (GP) training. Drawing on personal experience, it highlights a perceived disconnect between curriculum content and the realities of day-to-day GP practice. While trainees acquire a broad range of knowledge, some learning feels only indirectly relevant to independent practice.The HC – comprising implicit values, behaviours and cultural norms – emerges as a powerful influence on professional identity formation. Observed features include perceived underappreciation of general practice, narratives of unsustainable workload, and role modelling of burnout. Tensions between policy and practice, particularly around digital innovation, further reinforce this gap.This divergence risks positioning the formal curriculum as aspirational and the HC as reality. Greater recognition of the HC within curriculum reform may help align training with clinical practice. Making its influence explicit could support more constructive learning and professional development in GP training.KEYWORDS: Hidden curriculum, medical education, curriculum

Dear Editor,

As my GPST1 year draws to a close, I have found myself reflecting on the effectiveness of general practice training. In accordance with my placements and the curriculum, I have managed intrapartum care on night shifts and supported acutely psychotic patients in crisis. At other times, I have revised lysosomal storage diseases, genomic imprinting disorders and porphyrias for the AKT. While each has value, much of this has felt only indirectly relevant to preparing for independent general practice. Alongside this, I have become increasingly aware of the influence of the hidden curriculum (HC) on my learning.

Hafferty defines the HC as the organisational and institutional contexts and cultural subtexts that shape how and what students learn outside the formal curriculum [1]. In practice, it encompasses the implicit values and behaviours transmitted through everyday clinical environments and plays a central role in professional identity formation [2].

In my experience, salient features of the HC include the perceived underappreciation of general practice by secondary care colleagues, narratives of overwhelming workload in primary care, and observing GP role models routinely working beyond scheduled hours while describing burnout and compassion fatigue. Trainees are also exposed to tensions between policy and practice; for example, the expectation that digital innovation will transform primary care, while experiencing significant administrative and IT burdens with minimal curriculum-based training in digital skills.

A 2021 study found that UK undergraduate medical students are frequently exposed to negative cues about GP careers, largely mediated through the HC [3]. Despite entering training with enthusiasm, I have recognised similar influences in postgraduate practice. A persistent gap between stated curricular values and trainees’ lived experiences risks positioning the formal curriculum as aspirational and the HC as reality, with potential negative consequences for professional identity formation.

In the 2026 Medical Training Review: Phase 1 Diagnostic Report, authors Professors Whitty and Powis acknowledge conflicting views regarding current curricula but don’t explicitly acknowledge the underappreciated significance of the HC. However, the report’s recommendations do support the need for curriculum reform to embed interdisciplinary learning, leadership, digital literacy and system working from early postgraduate stages [4]. This builds on existing themes within The RCGP curriculum: Being a General Practitioner (2025) including the capabilities “team working” and “organisation management and leadership”. My hope is that evolving postgraduate training reforms seek to address the tensions between current curricula and the realities of current clinical practice and training within the NHS which are often negatively experienced within the HC.

To conclude, the HC cannot be eliminated, nor should it be. Many of its lessons such as professionalism, resilience and clinical judgement are essential. However, making the negative HC influences more visible allows them to be examined constructively and improve the curricula where needed. In the meantime, acknowledging the gap between curricular ideals and clinical reality may in itself be an important educational intervention for GP training at present.
