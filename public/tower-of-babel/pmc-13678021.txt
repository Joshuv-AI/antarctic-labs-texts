Dynamic quantitative photoacoustic tomography under sparse sampling via residual-driven Kalman filter assisted reconstruction
Authors: Ye Wu, Wenxing Bai, Pengfei Zhang, Limin Zhang, Dongyuan Liu, Feng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mechanically scanned photoacoustic tomography (PAT) provides high detection sensitivity and flexible system configurations, but its application to dynamic quantitative imaging is limited by the trade-off between temporal resolution and spatial sampling density. We propose a coordinated acquisition–estimation–reconstruction (AER) framework for dynamic quantitative PAT under sparse-pattern sampling. Sparse-pattern cyclic (SPC) acquisition collects baseline-referenced differential signals, while residual-driven adaptive Kalman filtering (RDAKF) adapts location-specific state-transition matrices at sampled locations and uses neighborhood-weighted transition information to propagate states at unsampled locations. The recovered spatially complete differential PA waveforms are then used to reconstruct absorption-coefficient perturbations through adjoint-based, PDE-constrained optimization. The framework was evaluated in numerical simulations, dynamic phantom experiments, and ex vivo tissue experiments using a single-element transmission-mode PAT system. Across multiple sparse-pattern configurations in the dynamic phantom experiment, structural similarity index values of 0.81–0.84 and root-mean-square errors of 0.017–0.023 mm⁻¹ were obtained. In the single-location-per-pattern configuration, the mean sparse-pattern acquisition rate was approximately 0.84 Hz. The framework tracked gradual, abrupt, periodic, and motion-associated variations, supporting the feasibility of dynamic quantitative PAT under measurement-limited conditions.

Introduction

Photoacoustic tomography (PAT) combines optical absorption contrast with ultrasonic detection, enabling structural and functional imaging at depths that are generally inaccessible to purely optical techniques [[1], [2], [3]]. Through sensitivity to endogenous chromophores such as hemoglobin and melanin, PAT has been investigated for oxygen-metabolism assessment, hemodynamic monitoring, and tumor imaging [[4], [5], [6]].

Building on these capabilities, dynamic quantitative photoacoustic tomography (QPAT) aims to recover the temporal evolution of optical absorption properties and thereby characterize time-varying physiological or experimental processes [7,8]. Mechanically scanned systems, however, face a pronounced trade-off between temporal sampling rate and spatial measurement density. Multi-element arrays enable faster acquisition but entail greater hardware cost, system complexity, and calibration requirements [9]. Single-element scanning systems offer high receive sensitivity and comparatively simple instrumentation, but sequential acquisition intrinsically couples spatial sampling density to imaging speed: increasing the number of spatial measurements reduces the achievable temporal sampling rate [10,11].

Under dynamic conditions, sparse sampling can produce spatially incomplete measurements and temporal inconsistency among reconstructed frames, exacerbating the ill-posedness of the inverse problem and degrading quantitative accuracy [[12], [13], [14]]. A central challenge in dynamic QPAT is therefore to recover temporally consistent signals and stable quantitative images under severe undersampling.

Current approaches to dynamic photoacoustic imaging with sparse measurements include interpolation-based completion, spatiotemporal regularization, low-rank reconstruction, recursive state estimation, and deep learning. Interpolation is straightforward but relies on local spatial or temporal smoothness and may attenuate rapid or localized transients. Spatiotemporal regularization and low-rank methods exploit inter-frame correlations to stabilize sparse-data reconstruction [[15], [16], [17]], but they typically require batch optimization and may suppress weak dynamics when the imposed prior is overly restrictive. Kalman-type methods recursively combine model predictions with current observations and have been applied to photoacoustic denoising, state estimation, and dynamic-sequence filtering [18,19]. Their performance, however, depends strongly on prescribed state-transition and observation models, which may not represent spatially heterogeneous and nonstationary dynamics adequately [20,21]. Existing QPAT studies have largely focused on stabilizing optical-parameter inversion at individual states [22]. Deep-learning approaches provide strong representational capacity, but their use may be limited by dependence on training data, insufficient generalization, and limited physical interpretability [[23], [24], [25]].

To address the coupled challenges of sparse spatial sampling and spatially heterogeneous, nonstationary waveform dynamics, we propose an acquisition–estimation–reconstruction (AER) framework for dynamic QPAT. The framework integrates three sequentially coupled and functionally complementary stages. First, sparse-pattern cyclic (SPC) acquisition is implemented in a mechanically scanned, single-element, transmission-mode PAT system to collect differential PA signals. Each sparse pattern contains only a small subset of candidate locations, and the predefined sparse patterns are acquired cyclically in sequence, progressively providing direct measurements across the candidate field while maintaining a high pattern-level acquisition rate. Second, a residual-driven adaptive Kalman filter (RDAKF) recovers spatially complete differential PA waveforms by combining recursive state prediction, residual-driven adaptation of location-specific state-transition matrices at sampled locations, and neighborhood-weighted transition fusion to support state propagation at unsampled locations. As the sparse patterns cycle, direct residual-driven adaptation progressively reaches the entire candidate field. Finally, the recovered full-field differential waveforms are supplied to PDE-constrained dynamic QPAT, in which a physical forward model links absorption-coefficient perturbations to differential PA observations and adjoint-based optimization enables quantitative inversion. This coordinated workflow supports temporally consistent waveform recovery and spatially resolved quantitative reconstruction under severe undersampling, including the limiting configuration of S= 1, where each sparse pattern contains only one direct spatial measurement.

The framework was evaluated through numerical simulations, dynamic phantom experiments, and ex vivo tissue experiments. Across the tested sparse-pattern configurations, it provided stable dynamic tracking and model-consistent quantitative reconstruction, supporting further investigation of measurement-efficient dynamic PAT.

Methods

System configuration

Fig. 1(a) presents the mechanically scanned, single-element, transmission-mode two-dimensional (2D) photoacoustic imaging system. A Q-switched Nd:YAG laser operating at 1064 nm served as the excitation source, with a pulse duration of 7 ns and a repetition rate of 20 Hz (Dawa-200, Beamtech Optronics, China). Mirrors M1 and M2 directed the beam to a beam-expansion and diffusion (BE-DF) module, which expanded and homogenized the illumination over the phantom surface. The illumination spot was approximately 3 cm in diameter, and the incident surface fluence was approximately 7.69 mJ⋅cm−2 per pulse. PA signals were detected with a single-element unfocused ultrasonic transducer centered at 2 MHz (UG2P25S, ULSO Tech, China). The single-channel configuration avoids interelement sensitivity and timing variations while retaining the high receive sensitivity of single-element detection. The transducer was mounted on a three-axis motorized translation stage for precise positioning and 2D planar scanning. The commanded translation speed was 50 cm s−1, with acceleration and deceleration of 200 and 50 cm/s2, respectively. The lower deceleration was used to reduce mechanical vibration and positioning disturbances during stopping. The acquired PA signals were amplified by a preamplifier (ATA-5560, Aigtek, China) and digitized at 40 MS/s (USB8912, ART Technology, China). At each sampled location, ten PA waveforms generated by consecutive laser pulses were acquired over 0.5 s and averaged to form one measurement. Stage motion and waveform acquisition were performed sequentially: the stage remained stationary during acquisition and moved to the next location only after waveform averaging and data transfer. Data acquisition using sparse-pattern cyclic sampling

As illustrated in Fig. 1(b), the scanning plane contains L candidate spatial locations, denoted by Ξ=ξll=1L, corresponding to the discrete positions visited by the single-element transducer. To promote progressive spatial coverage under sparse-sampling constraints, these locations are ordered into a global sequence using farthest-point sampling. At each selection step, the next location maximizes its minimum Euclidean distance from the previously selected set, thereby distributing the sampled positions across the measurement domain.

The ordered sequence is partitioned into sparse sampling patterns Ωii=1I, each containing S locations, except possibly the final pattern when L is not exactly divisible by S. The patterns partition the candidate location set, satisfying Ωi∩Ωj=∅ for i≠j and ⋃i=1IΩi=Ξ. During dynamic acquisition, the transducer sequentially scans the locations in each predefined sparse pattern. The predefined pattern sequence is then repeated cyclically, and completion of one pattern defines one sparse acquisition step. Accordingly, each candidate location is directly measured once during one complete pass through all predefined sparse patterns. With one-based indexing, the pattern acquired at the k-th step is Ωik, where ik=modk−1,I+1. At the k-th acquisition step, PA waveform measurements at the locations within pattern Ωik are acquired sequentially, and the pattern-completion time Tk, corresponding to the completion time of the measurement at the final visited location, is assigned as the time coordinate of the corresponding estimated and reconstructed frame. For S>1, measurements acquired earlier within the same pattern therefore have finite temporal offsets relative to Tk.

At the k-th acquisition step, only locations ξl∈Ωik are directly sampled. The PA waveform acquired at location ξl is denoted by pl,k∈RM, where M is the number of data points along the acoustic time-of-flight axis. A full reference scan is acquired before the dynamic sequence, and the baseline waveform at location ξl is denoted by pl,ref. The sparse differential observation is then defined as ∆pl,k=pl,k−pl,ref for ξl∈Ωik. After each sparse-pattern acquisition, RDAKF combines the new observations with recursively propagated states to estimate differential PA waveforms at all L candidate locations, producing a spatially complete waveform set for dynamic QPAT reconstruction.

The SPC acquisition rate is defined as the pattern-level sparse acquisition rate, rather than the full-field direct-measurement rate. The sparse acquisition rate is approximated as fspc≈1/Tsamp+Ttravel, where Tsamp is the mean signal-acquisition time per pattern and Ttravel is the mean mechanical-travel overhead. Together, these terms account for waveform acquisition at all locations in Ωi, point-to-point stage motion, and inter-pattern transition latency. Each candidate location is directly measured once during one complete pass through all predefined sparse patterns, and S controls the trade-off between direct spatial sampling density and pattern-level temporal resolution. In the limiting single-location-per-pattern configuration (S=1), each sparse pattern contains one directly observed location, providing the highest pattern-level acquisition rate but the most severe spatial undersampling.

Residual-driven adaptive Kalman filtering

After the k-th sparse-pattern acquisition step, RDAKF estimates the full-field differential photoacoustic (PA) waveforms using the measurements acquired in the current pattern, providing a spatially complete waveform dataset for PDE-constrained dynamic QPAT reconstruction at Tk. At sampled locations, pre-adaptation model residuals are used to update the local state-transition matrices; the priori waveform state and covariance are then recomputed with the updated matrix and corrected through the Kalman measurement update. At unsampled locations, each retained transition matrix is relaxed toward a distance-weighted estimate formed from valid neighboring transition matrices. The waveform state is propagated from the preceding acquisition step without measurement correction. The resulting states, transition matrices, and covariance matrices are propagated recursively to the next acquisition step, yielding a dynamic sequence of full-field differential PA waveforms.

Upon completion of the k-th sparse-pattern acquisition, RDAKF estimates the full-field differential PA waveform matrix Xk, expressed as(1)Xk=x1,k∣k,x2,k∣k,…,xL,k∣kT∈RL×M,where xl,k∣k∈RM denotes the posterior differential PA waveform-state estimate at candidate location ξl, and M is the number of data points in each PA waveform.

For each candidate location, RDAKF maintains a location-specific transition matrix Al,k∈RM×M that describes the temporal evolution of the differential waveform state between successive acquisition steps. At initialization, all transition matrices Al,0 are assigned the same scaled identity matrix as(2)Al,0=a0IM,l=1,…,L,where a0 is the transition-initialization coefficient and IM is the M×M identity matrix. In the absence of prior information on waveform dynamics, a0 is set to 1, corresponding to an identity transition model with no initial amplification or attenuation.

The location-specific state-transition matrix Al,k−1 retained after the (k−1)-th acquisition step is used to predict the waveform state at step k, yielding the provisional a priori estimate x~l,k∣k−1,(3)x~l,k∣k−1=Al,k−1xl,k−1∣k−1,where xl,k−1∣k−1∈RM is the posterior waveform-state estimate retained after the (k−1)-th acquisition step, and x~l,k∣k−1∈RM is the provisional a priori estimate for step k predicted from the preceding posterior state.

The corresponding provisional a priori state-error covariance P~l,k|k−1 is propagated as(4)P~l,k|k−1=Al,k−1Pl,k−1|k−1Al,k−1T+Q,where P~l,k|k−1∈RM×M is the provisional a priori state-error covariance for step k, conditioned on information available through step k−1 at candidate location ξl; Pl,k−1|k−1∈RM×M is the posterior state-error covariance retained after the (k−1)-th acquisition step; Al,k−1T denotes the transpose of Al,k−1; and Q∈RM×M is the process-noise covariance accounting for model uncertainty and unmodeled waveform variations [21,26]. At initialization, the posterior covariance and process-noise covariance are set to the scaled identity matrices Pl,0|0=P0IM and Q=Q0IM, respectively.

For each sampled location ξl∈Ωik, the local state-transition matrix is adapted before the Kalman measurement update. The pre-adaptation model residual el,k is defined as(5)el,k=yl,k−Hx~l,k|k−1,where yl,k is the differential PA waveform observed at sampled location ξl during the k-th acquisition step. In the reported implementation, the observation matrix is fixed as H=IM, so adaptation is confined to the state-transition dynamics.

Before Kalman measurement correction, the pre-adaptation residual is used to update the local state-transition matrix. At the k-th acquisition step, the regularized prediction-error objective Jl,kAl,k−1, evaluated at the transition matrix retained from the preceding step, is defined as(6)Jl,kAl,k−1=12yl,k−HAl,k−1xl,k−1|k−122+λ2Al,k−1F2.

The gradient of this objective with respect to the retained transition matrix Al,k−1 is(7)∇Al,k−1Jl,k=−HTyl,k−HAl,k−1xl,k−1|k−1xl,k−1|k−1T+λAl,k−1.

Here, ‖⋅‖2 denotes the Euclidean norm, ‖⋅‖F denotes the Frobenius norm, and ∇Al,k−1 is the gradient operator with respect to Al,k−1. The coefficient λ controls the Frobenius-norm penalty, limiting excessive growth of the state-transition matrix and reducing sensitivity to instantaneous residual fluctuations. In this study, λ was set to 1×10−3.

To scale the transition update according to the current prediction mismatch, the normalized residual magnitude rl,k is defined as(8)rl,k=‖el,k‖2‖yl,k‖2+ϵ.

The corresponding residual-dependent learning rate ηl,k is(9)ηl,k=η0rl,kcscale+rl,k+ϵ，where η0 is the base learning rate, cscale is a dimensionless residual-saturation parameter controlling how rapidly ηl,k approaches η0 as rl,k increases, and ϵ is a small positive numerical floor used to prevent unstable normalization when a denominator approaches zero. In this study, η0= 0.06, cscale= 0.8, and ϵ= 10−6.

Using recursive prediction-error identification and normalized stochastic-gradient adaptation, the current-step transition matrix Al,k is updated through one normalized gradient step as(10)Al,k=Al,k-1-ηl,k∇Al,k-1Jl,k∇Al,k-1Jl,kF+ϵ.

After adaptation, the updated matrix Al,k is retained as the location-specific transition matrix for the k-th acquisition step and is used to recompute the a priori waveform-state estimate xl,k|k−1 and state-error covariance Pl,k|k−1 as(11)xl,k|k−1=Al,kxl,k−1|k−1,(12)Pl,k|k−1=Al,kPl,k−1|k−1Al,kT+Q.

The updated matrix Al,k is then used to recompute the a priori state and covariance in Eqs. (11) and (12) for the subsequent Kalman measurement correction. Thus, the pre-adaptation residual el,k is used for local state-transition-matrix adaptation, whereas the Kalman innovation νl,k, computed from the a priori estimate obtained with the updated matrix Al,k, is used for measurement correction.

For an unsampled location ξl∉Ωik, neither residual-driven transition adaptation nor measurement correction is performed. Instead, the waveform state and state-error covariance are propagated using spatially shared transition information. A fixed neighborhood graph is constructed from the physical coordinates of the candidate scanning locations. At the k-th acquisition step, the active neighbor set Nl,k includes only neighboring locations that have received at least one valid direct observation through the current step. At the k-th acquisition step, the waveform state xl,k|k−1 at the unsampled location is predicted using the fused transition matrix, and the associated state-error covariance Pl,k|k−1 is propagated as(21)xl,k|k−1=Al,kxl,k−1|k−1,(22)Pl,k|k−1=Al,kPl,k−1|k−1Al,kT+Q.

At an unsampled location, no current observation is available; therefore, measurement correction is skipped. The propagated a priori quantities xl,k∣k−1 and Pl,k|k−1 are assigned directly to the posterior quantities xl,k∣k and Pl,k|k at the k-th step as(23)xl,k∣k=xl,k∣k−1,Pl,k|k=Pl,k|k−1.

After each sparse-pattern acquisition, the posterior states are assembled into the full-field waveform matrix Xk as(24)Xk=x1,k|k,x2,k|k,…,xL,k|kT∈ℝL×M.

This assembly produces a spatially complete differential PA waveform matrix for subsequent PDE-constrained dynamic QPAT reconstruction. The location-specific transition and state-error covariance matrices are retained in full M×M form, allowing cross-sample dependencies to be represented during state prediction and the corresponding uncertainty correlations to be preserved during measurement correction.

Adjoint-based nonlinear differential reconstruction

The absorption-coefficient perturbation Δμa,k∈RN is represented by nodal values on the finite-element mesh at the k-th acquisition step, where N is the number of mesh nodes. It is reconstructed by solving a PDE-constrained nonlinear inverse problem using the spatially complete differential PA waveforms estimated by RDAKF as observations. The nonlinear forward operator FΔμa,k maps the absorption perturbation to predicted differential PA waveforms through optical-fluence calculation, differential initial-pressure generation, and acoustic propagation. At each acquisition step, the perturbation is reconstructed by minimizing the following data-misfit objective function:(25)LΔμa,k=12‖FΔμa,k−∆pk‖22,where ∆pk denotes the vectorized recovered full-field differential PA waveform data obtained after the k-th sparse-pattern acquisition at completion time Tk.

For a given absorption-coefficient perturbation Δμa,k, the total absorption-coefficient distribution is μa,k=μa,ref+Δμa,k, where μa,ref∈RN is the reference distribution. The optical fluence Φk∈RN is obtained by solving the finite-element system K(μa,k)Φk=q subject to a Robin boundary condition, where K(μa,k)∈RN×N is the optical stiffness matrix assembled from the current absorption-coefficient distribution and q∈RN is the source vector. The differential initial-pressure distribution relative to the reference state is δpinit,k=Γμa,k⊙Φk−μa,ref⊙Φref, where Γ∈RN is the nodal Grüneisen-parameter distribution, Φref∈RN is the reference fluence, and ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication. The predicted differential PA waveforms are given by FΔμa,k=Hδpinit,k, where H is the acoustic forward operator that maps differential initial pressure to the detected waveforms.

The minimization problem is solved using an iterative adjoint-state method [[27], [28], [29]]. At reconstruction iteration l for the k-th acquisition step, the data residual is defined as dkl=FΔμa,kl−∆pk. The acoustic adjoint field pk*,(l) is computed by applying the transpose of the discrete acoustic forward operator to the data-misfit vector as(26)pk*,(l)=HTdk(l),where HT denotes the transpose of the discrete acoustic forward operator, and the superscript * identifies an adjoint variable.

The reference-state contribution is fixed throughout reconstruction and therefore has zero derivative with respect to Δμa,k. At the l-th iteration, the optical adjoint field Φk*,(l)∈RN is obtained by solving the transposed finite-element optical system as(27)Kμa,k(l)TΦk*,(l)=−Γ⊙μa,k(l)⊙pk*,(l).

The objective-function gradient with respect to the absorption-coefficient perturbation is then evaluated as(28)∇Δμa,kL(l)=Γ⊙Φk(l)⊙pk*,(l)+Φk(l)⊙Φk*,(l).

The absorption-coefficient perturbation Δμa,k(l+1) is updated by gradient descent as(29)Δμa,k(l+1)=Δμa,k(l)−σ∇Δμa,kL(l),where σ is the relaxation step size. At each iteration, the total absorption distribution is updated, after which the optical forward field, predicted differential PA waveforms, acoustic adjoint variable, and optical adjoint field are recomputed. A node-wise nonnegativity projection is applied after each update because all absorption perturbations considered in this study are nonnegative relative to their reference states.

Results

Numerical simulation

To evaluate the AER framework numerically, a three-dimensional phantom containing three dynamically varying cylindrical absorbers was constructed (Fig. 2(a)). The phantom dimensions were 20 × 20 × 30 mm3. The absorbers had diameters of 2 mm and were centered at (5,12,11), (15,12,11), and (10,7,18) mm, respectively. Their absorption coefficients followed distinct temporal profiles (Fig. 2(b)), generating spatially separated PA responses with different dynamics. The background absorption and reduced scattering coefficients were μa,bg=0.005 mm−1 and μs,bg′=1.0 mm−1, respectively. Optical fluence was calculated using a finite-element solution of the diffusion equation with a Robin boundary condition on a mesh containing N=105895 nodes. The resulting initial-pressure distributions were propagated acoustically using k-Wave on a 40 × 40 × 60 computational grid. The sound speed, mass density, and nodal Grüneisen-parameter vector were c=1500 m∙s−1, ρ=1000 kg∙m−3, and Γ=[Γ1,Γ2,…,ΓN]T∈RN, respectively, with Γi=0.0375 for all i=1,…,N. A finite-bandwidth detector response with a center frequency of 2 MHz and a fractional bandwidth of 80% was included. Frequency-dependent attenuation was modeled as αf=α0(f/1MHz)β, with α0=0.75 dB/cm at 1 MHz and β=1.5. Linear interpolation exhibited relatively large fluctuations because its estimates were determined directly by adjacent sparse observations and were therefore sensitive to measurement noise. The identity transition matrix in fixed-A KF caused the state estimate to remain near its most recently updated value until a new observation became available. Although this behavior suppressed recursive overshoot, it produced a step-like trajectory, temporal delay, and loss of detail during nonstationary variations. RDAKF w/o WF adapted the local transition matrix whenever the location was sampled; during long unsampled intervals, however, prediction relied recursively on the retained historical model. If that model was affected by measurement noise or no longer representative of the current dynamics, errors accumulated and could produce overshoot, particularly at the higher noise level. Additional evaluations of abrupt, multi-peak, and sinusoidal dynamics for RDAKF without neighborhood transition fusion (γA= 0) and with nonzero fusion strengths are provided in Supplementary Note S2.8 and Fig. S7; these results further support the stabilizing role of neighborhood transition fusion.

The evolution of G-SER and L-SER over the complete acquisition sequence (Fig. 3) confirmed the representative results in Fig. 2. Full RDAKF maintained comparatively high and stable recovery across sparse-sampling configurations and noise levels, indicating that its performance was not confined to selected time points or a single local trajectory. At ηnoise= 3.16%, differences among methods were more pronounced. Linear interpolation yielded lower G-SER and L-SER because noise propagated across interpolated intervals. RDAKF w/o WF also performed worse overall than fixed-A KF, showing that recursive propagation of an imperfect historical transition model can introduce additional error without neighborhood fusion. Fixed-A KF achieved competitive global recovery during some intervals, but its local dynamic recovery remained constrained by the fixed transition model. Full RDAKF maintained competitive or superior G-SER and consistently high L-SER for S= 1, 3, and 6.

Around Tk≈ 900 s, both G-SER and L-SER decreased for all methods as the abrupt change in Target 1 caused the differential PA waveforms to vary rapidly over a short interval, increasing the difficulty of prediction and missing-data recovery. The decline was more pronounced for Kalman-filter-based methods, consistent with recursive prediction from the preceding state estimate and the need for progressive correction after the change. Nevertheless, under the limiting condition S=1, full RDAKF maintained the highest G-SER and L-SER throughout the transition, indicating that neighborhood-weighted transition fusion reduced transient performance loss under extreme undersampling.

The effect of the number of sampled locations per pattern was evaluated using the same estimation method and noise level. Larger S resulted in longer pattern acquisition times and lower pattern-level update rates. For S>1, the sequential acquisition of locations within a pattern introduced finite temporal offsets relative to the pattern-completion time Tk. As shown by the temporal amplitude profiles in Fig. 2(e), larger S was associated with increased temporal smoothing, weaker transient responses, and temporal delay. Thus, S=1 provided the highest pattern-level temporal responsiveness, whereas S= 3 and S= 6 provided more direct observations per pattern at the cost of greater temporal aggregation and weaker transient capture.

The heterogeneous simulation illustrates the complementary roles of the three sequentially coupled AER components. SPC acquisition cycles through the predefined sparse sampling patterns in sequence to obtain direct observations at different candidate locations; RDAKF recovers spatially complete differential PA waveforms through residual-driven transition adaptation and neighborhood-weighted transition fusion; and PDE-constrained QPAT converts the recovered waveforms into spatially resolved absorption-coefficient perturbations. Under the limiting condition S= 1, the integrated framework preserved the distinct abrupt, periodic, and motion-induced target responses without evident inter-target crosstalk and recovered the trajectory of the slowly moving target. These findings support dynamic tracking and quantitative reconstruction under controlled heterogeneous dynamics with one directly observed location per sparse pattern.

Three solutions generated the dynamic absorption contrast: high-absorption solution SA (μa,A= 0.2 mm−1), low-absorption solution SB (μa,B= 0.014 mm−1), and a mixing reservoir SC , initially containing the background solution (μa,C= 0.014 mm−1). Their reduced scattering coefficient of all solutions was fixed at μs′= 1 mm−1, such that PA variations primarily reflected absorption changes. Before dynamic acquisition, the phantom was filled with 50 mL of SC and all valves were closed to establish a stable no-flow baseline. During dynamic acquisition, two peristaltic pumps and control valves (Fig. 6(b, c)) were used to maintain balanced inflow and outflow while SA and SB were alternately introduced into SC. A flow-switching event occurred at approximately 540 s, followed by an empirically measured transport delay of approximately 60 s. The pump flow rate was 11.50 mL/min, and a magnetic stirrer operated continuously to promote homogeneous mixing and prevent sedimentation. A flow-weighted mixing model described the effective channel absorption coefficient and provided the theoretical reference trajectory. Independent photon-counting calibration assessed agreement between measured and model-predicted absorption dynamics; details are provided in Supplementary Note S4 and Figs. S11–S13.

Signal-estimation and reconstruction results for the different sparse-pattern configurations are shown in Fig. 6(d–h). RDAKF parameters were fixed at P0= 10, Q0= 0.023, R0= 1, η0= 0.06, and γA= 0.6 for all phantom experiments. The stopping criterion yielded approximately 18 reconstruction iterations per frame on average. Even for S = 1, RDAKF recovered a spatially complete set of differential PA waveforms while preserving the dominant temporal features (Fig. 6(d)). For quantitative evaluation, the known target geometry defined target-specific ROIs. At each acquisition time, voxels within an ROI whose reconstructed Δμa values were at least 50% of the local peak were retained, and their mean defined the target-specific Δμa trajectory. Target-wise SSIM was calculated within a fixed three-dimensional evaluation region obtained by expanding the full target ROI by 1 mm in each direction; reconstructed and reference distributions were normalized independently by their positive peaks. CNR used the full target ROI and a surrounding local background, with a one-voxel dilated boundary layer excluded. SSIM and CNR in Fig. 6(g) were averaged over the two targets. Temporal evolution rate V was calculated from the first derivative of the reconstructed Δμa profile, and fspc was the reciprocal of the mean acquisition time per sparse pattern. RMSE was calculated between reconstructed and reference Δμa trajectories over their common interval. The residual coefficient of variation (ResCV) was the standard deviation of the reconstruction residual divided by the absolute mean of the reference trajectory.

Fig. 6(e) shows that the principal spatial distribution of Δμa was recovered with limited geometric distortion even under severe undersampling. The corresponding SSIM and CNR trajectories are shown in Fig. 6(g). During the initial period (Tk< 170 s), both metrics were low and fluctuated, particularly for S= 1, because few direct observations were available and reconstruction constraints were weak. SSIM and CNR then increased rapidly and stabilized after approximately 170 s. In the steady state, SSIM remained within 0.81–0.84, and CNR was consistently high with little variation among sampling configurations, indicating stable structural recovery and target-to-background contrast. CNR gradually decreased later in the dynamic sequence, whereas SSIM remained stable, suggesting preservation of structure as target contrast diminished.

Fig. 6(f) shows that the framework tracked mean Δμa during wash-in and wash-out. Compared with larger S, S=1 more closely reproduced the theoretical rising and falling slopes, indicating reduced temporal-integration effects and improved transient capture. Fig. 6(h) summarizes the associated trade-offs. Mean sparse-pattern acquisition rate, defined as the reciprocal of the mean pattern acquisition time including waveform acquisition, mechanical translation, and stage settling, decreased from 0.84 Hz at S= 1–0.10 Hz at S= 8. Accordingly, temporal evolution velocity V, which reflects responsiveness to rapid perturbations, was highest at S= 1. RMSE between reconstructed Δμa and the theoretical reference increased from 0.017 to 0.023 mm−1 with increasing S, indicating greater error from temporal integration and delay. Conversely, ResCV of the reconstructed Δμa trajectory decreased slightly from 0.051 to 0.044 as S increased, indicating improved numerical stability. Thus, increasing the number of locations per pattern improved stability but reduced temporal resolution and increased smoothing and delay.

Discussion

The AER framework coordinates cyclic sparse acquisition, adaptive full-field waveform estimation, and PDE-constrained quantitative reconstruction. SPC cycles through the predefined sparse sampling patterns in sequence to provide direct observations at different candidate locations, RDAKF adapts location-specific transition matrices and propagates states at unsampled locations, and PDE-constrained inversion converts the recovered waveforms into absorption-coefficient perturbations. Baseline differencing suppresses static background contributions and focuses the inversion on dynamic absorption changes.

The method comparisons clarify the contribution of each estimation mechanism. Linear interpolation recovered overall trends but required observations acquired both before and after each missing state, precluding causal online estimation and allowing interpolation error to propagate into quantitative inversion. Fixed-A KF suppressed noise but introduced smoothing and delay, as its time-invariant transition model had limited responsiveness to rapid local changes. Residual-driven adaptation improved tracking in RDAKF w/o WF, but local predictions without neighborhood-weighted transition fusion remained susceptible to transient overshoot. Neighborhood-weighted transition fusion reduced these instabilities, and full RDAKF provided the most consistent balance across waveform accuracy, structural preservation, target contrast, amplitude recovery, and temporal localization. Its comparatively stable performance at the higher tested additive-noise level supports empirical robustness within the evaluated conditions.

The number of locations per pattern, S, governs a trade-off among direct spatial information, temporal fidelity, and estimation stability: larger S provides more direct observations but prolongs pattern acquisition, with the resulting within-pattern temporal offsets potentially contributing to temporal integration, smoothing, and delay, particularly when the underlying dynamics vary appreciably during one pattern acquisition, whereas smaller S increases the pattern-level acquisition rate and better preserves rapid dynamics but requires estimation at more locations. The integrated AER framework remained effective under the limiting condition S=1. In the heterogeneous simulation, abrupt, periodic, and motion-induced target responses remained qualitatively separable, and the trajectory of the slowly moving target was recovered despite its constant prescribed absorption coefficient.

Several limitations remain. First, temporal resolution is constrained by mechanical travel and positioning, as well as by the laser repetition rate. Faster scanning, reduced mechanical displacement, parallel detection, and higher-repetition-rate excitation could further increase acquisition speed.

Second, residual-driven adaptation requires sufficient observations and may show an initialization transient or temporary degradation during abrupt changes. Robust residual weighting, adaptive initialization, and change-sensitive transition updates could improve responsiveness. The present study also did not model temporally varying or spatially heterogeneous noise within a monitoring sequence. Under such nonstationary conditions, fixed process- and measurement-noise covariances may be suboptimal. Future work could combine Sage–Husa-type online covariance estimation with noise-change detection, adaptive forgetting factors, and bounded covariance constraints to update the process-noise covariance Q and measurement-noise covariance R while balancing model prediction and current observations.

Third, differential reconstruction assumes that the pre-acquired reference remains valid throughout monitoring. Reference drift or mismatch can bias both estimated differential PA waveforms and quantitative reconstruction; periodic or interleaved reference acquisition and online baseline-state estimation could mitigate this limitation. The finite-element optical model and numerical acoustic solver also impose substantial computational cost, and mismatch in sound speed, optical properties, or transducer response may reduce quantitative accuracy. Future work should investigate accelerated forward–adjoint solvers, heterogeneous acoustic and optical models, and detector-response calibration.

Overall, the AER framework mitigates the trade-off between spatial sampling density and temporal fidelity by integrating cyclic sparse acquisition, adaptive full-field waveform estimation, and physically constrained quantitative inversion. Performance under the limiting S= 1 condition supports the feasibility of dynamic tracking, preservation of heterogeneous temporal responses, recovery of slow target motion, and quantitative reconstruction with measurement-efficient mechanically scanned photoacoustic systems.

Conclusions

This study developed an AER framework for dynamic QPAT under sparse-pattern sampling. The framework integrates cyclic sparse acquisition, residual-driven full-field waveform estimation, and PDE-constrained adjoint inversion of absorption-coefficient perturbations. Numerical and experimental results showed stable dynamic tracking and quantitative reconstruction across the tested sparse-pattern configurations, including the limiting condition S=1, and comparatively stable performance at the higher tested additive-noise level. In simulation, the framework recovered abrupt, periodic, and motion-induced dynamics and tracked a slowly moving target. These findings support the feasibility of dynamic QPAT with measurement-efficient mechanically scanned systems, while highlighting the need for faster acquisition, accelerated reconstruction, improved robustness to model mismatch and experimental disturbances, and future in vivo validation.
