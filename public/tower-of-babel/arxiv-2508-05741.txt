Factorization and resummation of QED radiative corrections for neutron beta decay
Zehua Cao, Richard J. Hill, Ryan Plestid, Peter Vander Griend

Abstract
Details of the two-loop analysis of long-distance QED radiative corrections to neutron beta decay are presented. Explicit expressions are given for hard, jet, and soft functions appearing in the factorization formula that describes the small mass/large energy limit. Power corrections, cancellation of singularities in the small mass expansion, renormalization scheme dependence, and bound state effects are discussed. The results impact the determination of | V u ​ d | |V_{ud}| from the measured neutron lifetime.

2
Soft-hard factorization

Neutron beta decay is described by a low-energy effective Lagrangian involving spin-1/2 heavy particle fields:
[
29
]

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=-\bar{h}_{v}^{(p)}\left({\cal C}_{V}\gamma^{\mu}+{\cal C}_{A}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}\right)h_{v}^{(n)}\bar{e}\gamma_{\mu}(1-\gamma_{5}){\nu}_{e}+{\rm H.c.}\,,

(1)

where
h^{(n)}_{v}
and
h^{(p)}_{v}
are heavy-particle spinors that annihilate neutrons and protons of velocity
v
, with
v^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)
in the laboratory frame.
This Lagrangian corresponds to the leading order in an expansion around the static limit in which the nucleon masses are taken to infinity while their mass difference,
\Delta=m_{n}-m_{p}
, is fixed.
Higher-order terms in the expansion encode recoil corrections and are suppressed by powers of the inverse nucleon mass
M=(m_{n}+m_{p})/2
; the effective theory Lagrangian can be easily extended to include such corrections
[
2
]
.
The scheme dependent Wilson coefficients

\mathcal{C}_{V,A}\approx(G_{F}/\sqrt{2})\,V_{ud}\,g_{V,A}
encode hadronic structure
and are determined by experimental measurement and/or nonperturbative
QCD matching calculations (e.g. chiral perturbation theory or lattice QCD).

The QED corrections discussed in the Introduction originate from long-distance scales and can be computed in the effective theory described by Eq. (
1
).
The separation of the hadronic scale (
\Lambda_{\rm UV}\sim m_{\pi}\sim 100\,{\rm MeV}
) from kinematic scales of neutron beta decay
(
p\sim\Delta\sim m\sim{\rm MeV}
), and
the soft scale (
\lambda\sim\varepsilon_{\gamma}\ll p
, where
\lambda
and
\varepsilon_{\gamma}
denote photon mass regulator and photon energy cutoff parameter, respectively)
imply the factorization of the decay amplitude schematically as

\displaystyle{\cal M}\sim{\cal M}_{\rm UV}(\Lambda_{\rm UV}/\mu_{\rm UV}){\cal M}_{H}(p/\mu_{\rm UV},p/\mu){\cal M}_{S}(\lambda/\mu)\,,

(2)

where
\mu_{\rm UV}
and
\mu
are factorization scales separating the UV, hard, and soft regions.
For an electron with momentum
p
and a neutrino with momentum
k
, we write the
complete amplitude as

\displaystyle{\cal M}

\displaystyle=-\bar{u}_{v}\left({\cal C}_{V}\gamma^{\mu}+{\cal C}_{A}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}\right)u_{v}\,\bar{u}(p)\mathcal{M}_{H}\mathcal{M}_{S}\gamma_{\mu}(1-\gamma_{5})v(k)\,,

(3)

where
u_{v}
is the heavy particle spinor wavefunction.
The UV amplitude
{\cal M}_{\rm UV}
is thus given simply in terms of
{\cal C}_{V}
and
{\cal C}_{A}
, and the soft amplitude
{\cal M}_{S}
exponentiates
[
29
,
28
]
. In the following subsections, we discuss the hard amplitude
{\cal M}_{H}
.

2.1
One loop computation

Let us study the one-loop corrections to neutron beta decay in order to better understand the origin of the
term proportional to
\pi\alpha/\beta
.
In what follows, we denote the amplitude with a proton in the final state by
{\cal M}_{H}(w,\mu^{2})
, whereas the crossed amplitude is denoted by
[{\cal M}_{H}]_{v\rightarrow-v}
.
As we will see in what follows, the large “
\pi
-enhanced” radiative correction is present in
{\cal M}_{H}(w,\mu^{2})
but absent in
[{\cal M}_{H}]_{v\rightarrow-v}
.
We trace the origin of this term to an infrared divergence in the hard amplitude, which is in turn related to an ultraviolet divergence in the soft effective theory, where the electron is also treated as a heavy particle. Such terms can therefore be handled with renormalization group techniques.

The Lagrangian (
1
) implies that, to all orders in perturbation theory, the hard function
{\cal M}_{H}
takes the form (suppressing
\mu_{\rm UV}
dependence)

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{H}(w,\mu^{2})={\cal A}_{H}(w,\mu^{2})+\frac{1}{w}v\!\!\!/\!\,\,{\cal B}_{H}(w,\mu^{2})\,,

(4)

for scalar functions
{\cal A}_{H}
and
{\cal B}_{H}
,
where

w=v\cdot p/m
, and
p^{\mu}
and
m
are the electron four-momentum and mass.
A straightforward one loop computation, including wavefunction renormalization factors, yields

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{H}(w)

\displaystyle=1+{\alpha\over 2\pi}\bigg[\frac{3}{4}\log{\mu_{\rm UV}^2\over m^2}+\log{\mu^2\over m^2}(\tilde{w}j(\tilde{w})-1)+\tilde{w}j(\tilde{w})-\tilde{w}J(\tilde{w})\bigg]\,,

(5)

\displaystyle{\cal B}_{H}(w)

\displaystyle={\alpha\over 2\pi}\left[-\tilde{w}j(\tilde{w})\right]\,,

(6)

where
\tilde{w}=-w-{\rm i}0
and the functions
j
and
J
are defined by

\displaystyle xj(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dy\,\frac{x}{1+y^{2}+2yx}\,,

\displaystyle xJ(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dy\,\frac{x}{1+y^{2}+2yx}\log(1 + y^2 + 2yx)\,.

(7)

The term
\alpha/(2\pi)\times\frac{3}{4}\log(\mu^2_{\rm UV}/m^2)
is related to the renormalization group flow of the Wilson coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{A,V}
, or equivalently
\mathcal{M}_{\rm UV}
, as denoted explicitly in
Eq.

2
.

Let us consider the related quantity
[{\cal M}_{H}]_{v\to-v}
,
which describes the spacelike decay of a heavy negatively charged particle decaying into an electron.
Using, for
w>1
,

\displaystyle wj(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{w}{\sqrt{w^{2}-1}}\log{(w+\sqrt{w^{2}-1})}\,,

\displaystyle wJ(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{w}{\sqrt{w^{2}-1}}\left({\rm Li}_{2}(1-(w-\sqrt{w^{2}-1})^{2})+\log^{2}{(w+\sqrt{w^{2}-1})}\right)\,,

(8)

we find

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{H}(-w)

\displaystyle=1+{\alpha\over 2\pi}\bigg[\frac{3}{4}\log{\mu_{\rm UV}^2\over m^2}+\log{\mu^2\over m^2}(wj(w)-1)+wj(w)-wJ(w)\bigg]\,,

\displaystyle{\cal B}_{H}(-w)

\displaystyle={\alpha\over 2\pi}\left[-wj(w)\right]\,.

(9)

To recover the timelike process corresponding to neutron beta decay,
we use that

\begin{split}\tilde{w}j(\tilde{w})=&wj(w)-{\rm i}\pi\frac{w}{\sqrt{w^{2}-1}},\\
\tilde{w}J(\tilde{w})=&wJ(w)-{\rm i}\pi\frac{w}{\sqrt{w^{2}-1}}\log(-4(w^{2}-1)-\iu 0).\end{split}

(10)

We note that the spacelike amplitude is finite at
w\to 1

\displaystyle\lim_{w\to 1}{\cal A}_{H}(-w)

\displaystyle=1+{\alpha\over 2\pi}\bigg[\frac{3}{4}\log{\mu_{\rm UV}^2\over m^2}-1\bigg]\,,

\displaystyle\lim_{w\to 1}{\cal B}_{H}(-w)

\displaystyle={\alpha\over 2\pi}(-1)\,.

(11)

In contrast, the timelike amplitude contains an enhancement

\log(-1-\iu 0)\log[(-4\bm{p}^{2}-{\rm i}0)/\mu^{2}]\sim-\pi^{2}

(at
\mu^{2}=4\bm{p}^{2}
), and diverges at
w\to 1
:

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{H}(w)-{\cal A}_{H}(-w)

\displaystyle={\alpha\over 2\pi}\bigg[{{\rm i}\pi w\over\sqrt{w^{2}-1}}\left(\log\left(-4\bm{p}^{2}-{\rm i}0\over\mu^{2}\right)-1\right)\bigg]\,,

\displaystyle{\cal B}_{H}(w)-{\cal B}_{H}(-w)

\displaystyle={\alpha\over 2\pi}\bigg[{{\rm i}\pi w\over\sqrt{w^{2}-1}}\bigg]\,.

(12)

Evaluating at
\mu^{2}=\hat{\mu}^{2}=-4\bm{p}^{2}-{\rm i}0

minimizes such large logarithms; equivalently,
enhancements present in the timelike amplitude can be obtained
with a renormalization group resummation

{\cal M}_{H}(w,\mu^{2})={\rm exp}\left[\int_{\hat{\mu}}^{\mu}{d\mu^{\prime}\over\mu^{\prime}}\,\,\gamma_{H}\right]{\cal M}_{H}(w,\hat{\mu}^{2})\,,

(13)

where
\gamma_{H}
is the anomalous dimension of the hard coefficient.

2.2
Anomalous dimension and resummation of large logarithms

Since
{\cal M}_{H}
represents the coefficient function of a soft operator,
its
\mu
dependence is given, to all orders in perturbation theory, by the cusp anomalous dimension
[
30
,
31
]
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{H}=-\frac{{\rm d}\log{\cal M}_{S}(\mu^{2})}{{\rm d}\log\mu}={\alpha\over\pi}\left[-1+{1\over 2\beta}\log{1+\beta\over 1-\beta}-{{\rm i}\pi\over\beta}\right],

(14)

with solution

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{H}(\mu^{2})

\displaystyle=\exp[\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\left(-1+{1\over 2\beta}\log{1+\beta\over 1-\beta}\right)\log{\mu^2 \over\hat{\mu}^2}\bigg]\exp[{-{\rm i}\alpha\over 2\beta}\log{\mu^2\over\hat{\mu}^2}\bigg]{{\cal M}_{H}(\hat{\mu}^{2})}.

(15)

For
\mu^{2}=4\bm{p}^{2}
and
\hat{\mu}^{2}=-4\bm{p}^{2}-{\rm i}0
, we have

\log{\mu^2 \over\hat{\mu}^2}=+{\rm i}\pi
.
The first exponential in
Eq.

15
is then a phase that does not
impact rate observables and the second exponential
leads to a numerical enhancement for the associated hard function,

1
1

1

The hard function is given by the appropriate average of the squared magnitude of
{\cal M}_{H}
. For example, in the electron energy spectrum for neutron decay (cf. Eq. (14) or Ref.
[
28
]
),

H(\mu^{2})=\langle\,|{\cal M}_{H}(\mu^{2})|^{2}\,\rangle
,
where the angle brackets denote contracting with lepton and nucleon spinors,

\mathcal{M}_{H}\to\bar{u}(p)\mathcal{M}_{H}\gamma_{\mu}(1-\gamma_{5})v(k)\bar{u}_{v}\left({\cal C}_{V}\gamma^{\mu}+{\cal C}_{A}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}\right)u_{v}

squaring, summing over final state spins and integrating over final state phase space (apart from the electron energy),
and dividing by the same expression at tree level.

\displaystyle H(\mu^{2})=\exp[{\pi\alpha\over\beta}\bigg]H(\hat{\mu}^{2})\,.

(16)

We emphasize that this result is true to all orders and makes no reference to non-relativistic kinematics.
Compared to the expression involving the naive application of the traditional Fermi function for nuclear beta decay, the expression (
16
) differs starting at two-loop order (cf. Sec. 6 of Ref.
[
28
]
).

3
Soft-collinear-hard factorization

Here we analyze the large-energy/small-mass limit characterized by
E\gg m
.
This is sufficient to compute the two-loop corrections for neutron beta decay to the requisite
10^{-4}
precision, and provides an all-orders derivation of the result (

3.1
Factorization formula

Let us decompose the hard function in
Eq.

2
, neglecting contributions suppressed by powers of
m/E
,

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{H}(w)\approx{\cal A}_{H}(w)\approx F_{R}(w,m)F_{J}(m)F_{H}(E)\,.

(17)

Power corrections are discussed in Appendix
A
.
We will analyze the remainder
F_{R}
, jet
F_{J}
and hard
F_{H}
amplitudes, using dimensional regularization and the
\overline{\rm MS}
scheme.
Following the discussion in the previous section, we consider first the quantity
{\cal M}_{H}|_{v\to-v}\approx{\cal A}_{H}(-w)=F_{R}(-w,m)F_{J}(m)F_{H}(-E)
, and then analyze the ratio
{\cal A}_{H}(w)/{\cal A}_{H}(-w)
using renormalization arguments to resum enhanced contributions.
Note that the product
F_{S}F_{R}F_{J}F_{H}
is equal to the
heavy-light Dirac form factor with heavy-particle initial state, at leading power.
The renormalized soft amplitude is given, to all orders in the coupling, as

\displaystyle F_{S}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[{\bar{\alpha}_{0}\over 4\pi}\left(2(L-1)\log{\lambda^2\over\mu^2}\right)\right]\,,

(18)

where
L\equiv\log(2E/m)=\log(2w)
and
\bar{\alpha}_{0}\to\alpha
is the
\overline{\rm MS}
coupling for
n_{e}=0
, which reduces to the on-shell coupling in
d=4

4
Renormalization

Enhanced contributions result from the continuation
F_{H}(-E)\to F_{H}(E)
. To see the all-orders properties of this continuation, we recognize that (again setting
\mu_{\rm UV}=\mu
)
F_{H}

depends only on the dimensionless ratio
E/\mu
, and that
the
\mu
dependence is determined by renormalization. In particular, accounting for the
\mu
dependence of
C(\mu)
, the hard amplitude obeys

\displaystyle{{\rm d}\log F_{H}(E,\mu)\over{\rm d}\log\mu}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{\rm UV}+\gamma^{h}(\bar{\alpha})+\gamma^{\psi}(\bar{\alpha})+\gamma_{\rm cusp}(\bar{\alpha})\tilde{L}_{E}

\displaystyle={\bar{\alpha}\over 4\pi}\left(4\tilde{L}_{E}-2\right)+\left({\bar{\alpha}\over 4\pi}\right)^{2}\left[n_{e}\left(-{80\over 9}\tilde{L}_{E}+{160\over 27}+{2\pi^{2}\over 3}\right)-4+{14\pi^{2}\over 3}-24\zeta_{3}\right]+\dots\,,

(29)

with
\tilde{L}_{E}=\log[(-2E-{\rm i}0)/\mu]
.
Here we integrate this equation to obtain

\displaystyle{F_{H}(E,\mu)\over F_{H}(-E,\mu)}={F_{H}(E,\mu)\over F_{H}(E,-\mu-{\rm i}0)}\,.

(30)

4.1
Coupling constant renormalization

To compute the solution to
Eq.

We have carried out a calculation of long-distance radiative corrections to the neutron lifetime at

In this section, we analyze
\beta\to 0

{\cal O}(\alpha^{3})
and
m/\Delta\to 0
singularities appearing at
{\cal O}(\alpha^{2})

5.1
Small-velocity expansion

Our treatment of
\beta
as an order unity parameter is justified over the bulk of the neutron decay phase space (cf. Fig.
1
).
Starting at
{\cal O}(\alpha^{3})
, the small-
\beta
region is no longer kinematically suppressed [for
m/\Delta=O(1)
].
Here we discuss the third order correction from this region.

Let us consider the beta decay spectrum,
\mathrm{d}\Gamma/\mathrm{d}E
as the electron energy goes to the electron mass,
E\to m
, or equivalently,
\mathrm{d}\Gamma/\mathrm{d}\beta
as the electron velocity goes to zero,
\beta\to 0
.
It is well known that photon exchanges with a non-relativistic final-state proton lead to enhancements by powers of
\pi^{2}/\beta
leading to contributions of order
(\frac{\alpha}{\pi})^{n}\times(\frac{\pi^{2}}{\beta})^{n}\sim(\frac{\pi\alpha}{\beta})^{n}
. These terms are captured by the non-relativistic Fermi function and lead to the factorization theorem,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\Gamma}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\approx\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\Gamma}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\right)_{\rm tree}\times F_{\rm NR}(\pi\alpha/\beta)~,

(42)

where

F_{\rm NR}(\pi\alpha/\beta)=\frac{2\pi\alpha/\beta}{1-\exp(-2\pi\alpha/\beta)}~.

(43)

A naive expansion in
\alpha
of
F_{\rm NR}
leads to terms whose average over phase space diverges. Yet, when treated non-perturbatively,
\langle F_{\rm NR}\rangle
is finite. Subtle issues begin at
O(\alpha^{3})
which we will now discuss.

We begin with the infinite series representation
[
42
]
,

F_{\rm NR}(\pi\alpha/\beta)=1+\frac{\pi\alpha}{\beta}+\frac{\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}}{3\beta^{2}}-\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\frac{(\alpha/n)^{2}}{\beta^{2}+(\alpha/n)^{2}}~.

(44)

Integrating over phase space, each of the first three terms yields a finite contribution at
\beta\to 0
. However,
in the remaining series all terms must be retained when
\beta\sim\alpha
, and sum to a contribution that is
{\cal O}(\alpha^{3})
.

Let us consider the contribution to the phase space integral from the region
\beta<\beta_{c}
where
\beta_{c}
is a cut-off velocity chosen such that
\alpha/\beta_{c}\sim O(\sqrt{\alpha})
, i.e.,
\beta_{c}
is small enough that a non-relativistic expansion is justified but large enough that
\alpha/\beta_{c}
is still perturbative. In this small-
\beta
region, we can expand the phase space measure

\mathrm{d}\Phi\simeq(\Delta-m)^{2}m^{3}\beta^{2}\mathrm{d}\beta+O(\beta^{4})~.

(45)

Let us compute the contribution to the phase space integral from the infinite series in
Eq.

44
:

\begin{split}(\Delta-m)^{2}m^{3}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{0}^{\beta_{c}}\mathrm{d}\beta(-2\alpha^{2})\frac{(\alpha/n)^{2}}{\beta^{2}+(\alpha/n)^{2}}&=(\Delta-m)^{2}m^{3}|\alpha|^{3}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-2)}{n^{3}}\arctan\quantity(\frac{n\beta_c}{|\alpha|})\\
&=(\Delta-m)^{2}m^{3}|\alpha|^{3}\times(-\pi\zeta_{3})+\ldots~,\end{split}

(46)

where in the final line we expanded
\arctan(n\beta_c/|\alpha|)
about
\beta_{c}/|\alpha|\to\infty
. Notice that the left-hand side is manifestly even in
\alpha
, requiring the right-hand side to be the even and non-analytic function
|\alpha|^{3}
.

At
O(\alpha^{3})
, one must also include bound-state contributions from
s
-wave hydrogenic orbitals formed by the
e^{-}p^{+}
final state.
The contribution is given by the sum over squared wavefunctions evaluated at the origin, with a relative factor of
(2\pi)^{3}/(4\pi)
related to 2-body vs. 3-body phase space. The relevant quantity is

2\pi^{2}(\Delta-m)^{2}\sum_{n}|\psi_{n}(\mathbf{x}=0)|^{2}=2\pi^{2}(\Delta-m)^{2}\sum_{n}\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha^{3}+|\alpha|^{3}\right)\frac{m^{3}}{\pi n^{3}}=(\Delta-m)^{2}m^{3}(\alpha^{3}+|\alpha|^{3})\times(\pi\zeta_{3})\,.

(47)

In particular, when
\alpha<0
(corresponding to equally charged particles in the final state), the bound state contribution vanishes.
For both positive and negative values of
\alpha
, the sum of
Eqs.

46
and
47
gives

(\Delta-m)^{2}m^{3}|\alpha|^{3}\times(-\pi\zeta_{3})+(\Delta-m)^{2}m^{3}(\alpha^{3}+|\alpha|^{3})\times(\pi\zeta_{3})=(\Delta-m)^{2}m^{3}\alpha^{3}\times(\pi\zeta_{3})~,

(48)

leaving only the analytic-in-
\alpha
term. This cancellation, and the origin of
|\alpha|^{3}
vs.
\alpha^{3}
, is discussed in detail in Ref.
[
42
]
where a similar problem appears in the theory of the electron’s anomalous magnetic moment.

If
\beta_{\rm max}
(as set by kinematics) were to be very small,
\beta_{\rm max}\sim\alpha
, then the “large velocity” region does not exist, one replaces
\beta_{c}\rightarrow\beta_{\rm max}
in the above analysis, and the full functional dependence of
\arctan(\alpha/n\beta_{\rm max})
must be kept in each term of the sum. Such a scenario requires a highly tuned value of
(\Delta-m)
satisfying
(\Delta-m)\lesssim m\alpha^{2}
. In this regime, one could imagine that
-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}m\lesssim(\Delta-m)<0
, in which case continuum contributions would be kinematically forbidden but hydrogenic bound states would be present.
In practice, such small energy splittings do not occur in Nature and are certainly not relevant for neutron beta decay.

An alternative formulation describes the impact of Coulomb photon exchange on the inclusive rate via the optical theorem. For non-relativistic energies, the result is

\displaystyle{\rm d}\Phi

\displaystyle\to{\rm d}\Phi\times\bigg[\left(-2\pi\over m^{2}\beta\right){{\rm Im}\,G(E_{\rm NR})}\bigg]

\displaystyle\approx m^{3}(\Delta-m)^{2}{\rm d}E_{\rm NR}\,\bigg[\left(-2\pi\over m^{3}\right){{\rm Im}\,G(E_{\rm NR})}\bigg]\,,

(49)

where

and

\displaystyle G(E_{\rm NR})

\displaystyle=\int{\mathrm{d}^{3}p\over(2\pi)^{3}}\int{\mathrm{d}^{3}p^{\prime}\over(2\pi)^{3}}\,\langle\vec{p}|{1\over E_{\rm NR}-H_{\rm Coul.}+{\rm i}0}|\vec{p}^{\,\prime}\rangle\,,

(50)

A dispersive analysis of

\displaystyle G(E)

\displaystyle={1\over\pi}\int_{-E_{0}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}E^{\prime}{{\rm Im}\,G(E^{\prime})\over E^{\prime}-E}\,,

(51)

\displaystyle\int_{-E_{0}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}E^{\prime}\,{\rm Im}\,G(E^{\prime})=\lim_{E\to-\infty}\big[-\pi EG(E)\big]\,,

(52)

where

is the ground state energy.
Subtraction of the
\alpha^{0}

\alpha^{1}
and
\alpha^{2}

[
42

\displaystyle G-[G^{(0)}+G^{(1)}+G^{(2)}]

\displaystyle={m^{4}\alpha^{3}\over\pi p^{2}}\bigg[\zeta_{3}+{m\alpha\over\sqrt{-p^{2}-{\rm i}0}}\zeta_{4}+\left({m\alpha\over\sqrt{-p^{2}-{\rm i}0}}\right)^{2}\zeta_{5}+\dots\bigg]\,,

(53)

with

reproduces the sum of continuum and bound state contributions (

In conclusion, we find that the total decay rate has a well defined expansion in
\alpha

(\Delta-m)\gg\alpha^{2}m

\beta

6
Discussion

Control of QED corrections is an important theoretical challenge for neutron beta decay. Part of this challenge relates to non-perturbative hadronic structure but, at present levels of accuracy, is confined to one-loop order. Long-distance radiative corrections, by way of contrast, are insensitive to hadronic structure but are required at two-loop order. It is well known that these corrections contain certain large numerical enhancements (proportional to
\pi^{2}
), and their origin has historically been ascribed to the Fermi function (i.e., a resummation of diagrams involving a background Coulomb field). A shortcoming of this argument is that very little of the neutron decay electron spectrum shown in
Fig.

1
satisfies the non-relativistic kinematics that make the Fermi function a controllable resummation of enhanced contributions.

In this work we have argued, and explicitly demonstrated, that these numerical enhancements stem from the infrared region of the amplitudes, or the “soft-hard boundary”.
We have shown that these numerical enhancements can be controlled and resummed using renormalization group methods, making no reference to the non-relativistic limit.

We have used our results to supply the most precise determination of long-distance radiative corrections to neutron beta decay
[
28
]
.
We ascribe an uncertainty for an extraction of
|V_{ud}|
at the level of
3\times 10^{-5}
from the long-distance region.
Our results set a target for uncertainty reductions in the experimental and short-distance inputs, and supply a firm foundation for estimating higher order radiative corrections in processes involving numerically enhanced Coulomb exchanges for both relativistic and quasi-relativistic kinematics.

In Ref.

\displaystyle={(4\pi)^{2}\over(2\pi)^{5}}\Delta^{5}\frac{1}{30}\bigg[1-5\delta^{2}+\left(-\frac{15}{8}-\frac{15}{2}\log{\delta\over 2}\right)\delta^{4}+\frac{5}{8}\delta^{6}+\frac{5}{128}\delta^{8}+\frac{1}{128}\delta^{10}+\dots\bigg]

(62)

A.2
One loop correction

Although the convergence of the

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{d}\Gamma\over\mathrm{d}E\right)=\left(\mathrm{d}\Gamma\over\mathrm{d}E\right)_{\rm tree}\bigg[1+{\alpha\over 2\pi}\left(3\log{\mu_{\rm UV} \over m}+{2\pi^{2}\over\beta}+\dots\right)\bigg]\,.

A.3

For two-loop radiative corrections, we expect a further suppression by
\alpha\sim 10^{-2}

The enhanced power correction, proportional to

Appendix D
Renormalization schemes

We have worked throughout with dimensional regularization in

d=4-2\epsilon
spacetime dimensions, and have expressed renormalized quantities in the modified minimal subtraction (
\overline{\rm MS}
) renormalization scheme. In this scheme, the renormalized coupling is defined to subtract commonly-occurring factors of
\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\log{4\pi}-\gamma_{\rm E}
.
This is achieved by defining the renormalized coupling

\overline{\alpha}(\mu)=Z_{\alpha}^{-1}{e_{\rm bare}^{2}\over 4\pi}\left(\frac{4\pi}{\mu^{2}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\gamma_{\rm E}}\right)^{\epsilon}\,,

(81)

e_{\rm bare}
.
The coupling renormalization factor
Z_{\alpha}
, and other operator renormalization factors, are specified in the
\overline{\rm MS}
scheme to contain only negative powers of
\epsilon
at each order in an expansion in
\bar{\alpha}

The precise choice of factors in Eq. (
85
) do not impact physical observables, but do impact the conventional separation between different components of factorized expressions.
As an example, we denote by
\overline{\rm MS}^{\prime}
the scheme where

\overline{\alpha}_{\overline{\rm MS}^{\prime}}(\mu)=Z_{\alpha,\overline{\rm MS}^{\prime}}^{-1}{e_{\rm bare}^{2}\over 4\pi}\left(\frac{4\pi}{\mu^{2}}\Gamma(1+\epsilon)\right)^{\epsilon}\,.

(82)

\overline{\rm MS}^{\prime}
scheme we find instead of Eq. (
4.3
), (using that
\bar{\alpha}=\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}
at
\epsilon=0
)

\displaystyle F_{H}^{(1)}(\hat{\mu}^{2})|_{\overline{\rm MS}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-2-2\zeta_{2}\,,

\displaystyle F_{H}^{(2)}(\hat{\mu}^{2})|_{\overline{\rm MS}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=n_{e}\bigg[{656\over 81}+12\zeta_{2}\bigg]-8+64\zeta_{2}-15\zeta_{3}-55\zeta_{4}\,,

(83)

leading to [cf. Eq. (
40

\displaystyle\big(|F_{H}(\hat{\mu}^{2})|^{2}\big)|_{\overline{\rm MS}^{\prime}}
