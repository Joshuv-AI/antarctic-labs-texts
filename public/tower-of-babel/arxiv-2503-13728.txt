Abstract
. A linear order A A is called strongly surjective if for every nonempty suborder B ⪯ A B\preceq A , there is an epimorphism from A A onto B B (denoted by B ​ ⊴ ​ A B\trianglelefteq A ). We show that under 𝖬𝖠 ℵ 1 \mathsf{MA}_{\aleph_{1}} there is a strongly surjective Countryman line, answering some questions of Dániel T. Soukup. We also study the general structure of the class of Aronszajn lines under ⊴ \trianglelefteq , and compare it with the well known embeddability relation ⪯ \preceq . Under 𝖯𝖥𝖠 \mathsf{PFA} the class of Aronszajn lines and the class of countable linear orders enjoy similar nice properties when viewed under the embeddability relation; both are well-quasi-ordered and have a finite basis. We show that this analogy does not extend perfectly to the ⊴ \trianglelefteq relation; while it is known that the countable linear orders are still well-quasi-ordered under ⊴ \trianglelefteq , we show that already in 𝖹𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{ZFC} the class of Aronszajn lines has an infinite antichain, and under 𝖬𝖠 ℵ 1 \mathsf{MA}_{\aleph_{1}} an infinite decreasing chain as well. We show that some of the analogy survives by proving that under 𝖯𝖥𝖠 \mathsf{PFA} , for some carefully constructed Countryman line C C , C C and C ⋆ C^{\star} form a ⊴ \trianglelefteq -basis for the class of Aronszajn lines. Finally we show that this does not extend to all uncountable linear orders by proving that there is never a finite ⊴ \trianglelefteq -basis for the uncountable real orders.

A two element basis for the Aronszajn lines

Although we have shown that the analogy between countable linear orders

\trianglelefteq

Recall that in

4

it is shown that

\omega+1
and

and

form a
\trianglelefteq
-basis for the countable

Using the results of Section

, fix an
\aleph_{1}
-dense

.

Lemma 7.1
.

Assume
\mathsf{MA}_{\aleph_{1}}
.

and

form a
\trianglelefteq
-basis for the Countryman lines.

Proof.

It is enough to prove that if

is uncountable, then

.
First observe that

for some
\aleph_{1}
-dense
Aronszajn line

. This can be proven by taking the usual
quotient on
A
defined by
a\sim b
if there are countably many points between

6.2
implies that

which finishes the proof.
∎

Under
\mathsf{PFA}
we can say more. Recall that Moore
proved
[

that under
\mathsf{PFA}

any non-Countryman Aronszajn line contains a copy of

and

for any given Countryman line

.

.

Assume
\mathsf{PFA}
.

and

form a
\trianglelefteq
-basis for the
Aronszajn lines.

Proof.

Taking into account Lemma
7.1

it is enough to prove that if

is any Aronszajn line, then

surjects onto some Countryman line.

By Theorem
2.9
there is

that is an

\aleph_{1}
-dense Countryman line with the induced order.
For

, define

.

Suppose not, then by

7.1.
On a basis for all uncountable linear orders

Theorem
7.2
suggests the following question. Under
\mathsf{PFA}
,
is there a finite
\trianglelefteq
-basis for the uncountable linear orders?
It is easily seen that if
L
is not short,
then
L
surjects onto
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{1}+1
,
\omega_{1}^{\star}

or
1+\omega_{1}^{\star}
. However we will see that no matter what
set-theoretic hypotheses are in use, this cannot be extended to all uncountable
linear orders. This will be done by proving (in

\mathsf{ZFC}
) the following theorem.

Theorem 7.3
.

Any
\trianglelefteq
-basis for the uncountable real orders has at
least
\mathfrak{c}^{+}
elements.

Let
\mathscr{F}
be the family of epimorphisms between subsets of reals. That
is,
f\in\mathscr{F}
iff
\dom(f),\ran(f)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and for all
x<y
in
\dom(f)
,
f(x)\leq f(y)
. For
f\in\mathscr{F}
let
f\!\uparrow
denote the set of
g\in\mathscr{F}
such that
f\subseteq g
. Define

\bar{f}
as the set of
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that for all

g\in f\!\uparrow
with
x\in\dom(g)
,
g(x)=y
. Morally, we want

\bar{f}
to be the largest monotone function whose values are
uniquely determined by
f
. In practice, it will be convenient to have the
following characterization.

Lemma 7.4
.

For every
f\in\mathscr{F}
and
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

(x,y)\in\bar{f}
.

(b)

\sup(f(\dom(f)\cap\left]-\infty,x\right]))=y=\inf(f(\dom(f)\cap\left[x,+\infty\right[))
.

Proof.

Assume that

(b)
fails. Then there

y_{0}<y_{1}
such that
y\in[y_{0},y_{1}]
,

f(\dom(f)\cap\left]-\infty,x\right])\subseteq\left]-\infty,y_{0}\right]
and

f(\dom(f)\cap\left[x,+\infty\right])\subseteq\left[y_{1},+\infty\right[
.
Note then that
x\notin\dom(f)
.
Then one easily sees that both
f\cup\{(x,y_{0})\}
and

f\cup\{(x,y_{1})\}
are in
f\!\uparrow
, which implies
that
(x,y)\notin\bar{f}
, so
(a)
fails.

Assume
(b)
. If
x\in\dom(f)
then clearly
y=f(x)
and thus

(a)
holds. Assume
that
x\notin\dom(f)
. Then it
should be clear that
f\cup\{(x,y)\}\in f\!\uparrow
.
Now suppose that
(a)
fails, and thus take
g\in f\!\uparrow
such that

y^{\prime}:=g(x)\neq y
. We may assume that
y<y^{\prime}
. By
(b)
there is

x_{0}\in\dom(f)
such that
x<x_{0}
and
y\leq f(x_{0})<y^{\prime}
. But since
g
extend
f
,
we have that
x<x_{0}
and
g(x_{0})<g(x)=y^{\prime}
, which contradicts that

g
, being in
f\!\uparrow
, is monotone.
∎

Note that this implies that for every
f\in\mathscr{F}
,

\bar{f}
is indeed a monotone function extending

.

The following three lemmas are essentially due to Baumgartner (see

[
3
]
). We give a proof of the following because
Baumgartner only deals with monotone injective functions, and
passing to all monotone functions requires a small tweak.

Lemma 7.5
.

For every

there is a countable

such that

\bar{g}\in f\!\uparrow
. Moreover, if

is injective then so is

.

Proof.

Let

be a countable subset of
\dom(f)

such that every

is either in

, or is a limit point (in

) of both

and
\{a\in A:x<a\}
. Let

be
defined similarly. Now using the ccc of
\mathbb{R}
and

is monotone, one sees that there are only
countably many

such that

has

and

we may assume

contains every such

and

. We claim that

Note that the previous lemma implies
that

, and that

is enough to show that

Case 1:

∎

Since there are only

is also injective.

We now construct by recursion
a sequence

of pairwise disjoint

. The construction closely follows
that of
[

]
, we only generalize it as to allow

At step

has been defined for every
\xi<\alpha
,
that they are pairwise disjoint, and that for each
\xi<\alpha
,

. Note then
that

has cardinality less
than
\mathfrak{c}
. Let

be the closure of

under
\mathrm{id}
,
and every

and

(when

is injective)
for

. Then

and

.

be any subset of

disjoint from

,
and such that

as to achieve a particular

For

, let

. The following

in
[

]
.

Lemma 7.6
.

Suppose that

, and that

is such that

.

In
[
3
]
(Theorem 1.2 (a)), it is proven that there is

of size
\mathfrak{c}^{+}
of subsets of
\mathfrak{c}
such that for every

in

,
|X|=|Y|=\mathfrak{c}
and
\sup(X\cap Y)<\mathfrak{c}
. For
the rest of this section we fix such a family. The following
is Theorem 2.4 (b) of
[

]
. We give the proof because
is not given there.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
X\neq Y
be in
S
. If

, then
|B|<\mathfrak{c}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
|B|=\mathfrak{c}
and that
B\preceq A(X)
, we may assume in fact that

B\subseteq A(X)
. Since for each
\alpha
,

|A_{\alpha}|<\max(\aleph_{0},|\alpha|)
, we conclude that

is cofinal. Since

,

is also cofinal, so by

.
∎

Note that this already implies Theorem

under
\mathsf{CH}
,
since any
\trianglelefteq
-basis is an
\preceq
-basis. What we show now, is that
for a particular way of choosing the

we can achieve the
following property: for
X\subseteq\mathfrak{c}
and

,
if
\aleph_{0}<|B|<\mathfrak{c}=|X|
, then

.
Together with the previous lemma, this immediately
yields a proof of Theorem

.

Let
L
be a linear order. Consider a pair
of subsets
(X,Y)
such that

is exactly the
set of lower bounds of

, and
Y
is the set of
upper bounds of
X
. We furthermore ask

and

to be nonempty. Such a pair is called a

of

if

. Note that in this case

has no right endpoint, and

has no left endpoint.
Let

be the set of gaps of

. This is naturally
linearly ordered by letting
(X,Y)\leq(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
iff

. By taking preimages the following
is easily proved (or see

).

Proposition 7.8
.

If
B\trianglelefteq A
, then
G(B)\preceq G(A)
.

Lemma 7.9
.

Let

.

Proof.

Let

witness that

, so

. Because

|\mathbb{R}\setminus A|<\mathfrak{c}
,
\mathbb{R}=A\cup E
where

|E|<\mathfrak{c}
. Since
\mathbb{R}
is complete (i.e., has no

|G(A)|\leq|E|<\mathfrak{c}
. Then the previous proposition yields

|G(B)|<\mathfrak{c}
also. Now suppose towards a contradiction
that
\aleph_{0}<|B|<\mathfrak{c}
.

Let

be countable and (order) dense in

, i.e.,
for all

in

,

. Since

is uncountable,

must contain a copy of
\mathbb{Q}
.
Since
\mathbb{Q}
has
\mathfrak{c}
gaps, and

is countable,
we deduce that
|G(Q)|=\mathfrak{c}
.

For every

let

.
Since
|B|<\mathfrak{c}
,
|G(Q)\setminus\ran(g)|=\mathfrak{c}
,
and it is clear that any

maps to a gap in

(\{b\in B:b\leq\inf(Y)\},\{b\in B:\sup(X)\leq b\})
,
and that this applications is injective. Thus

\mathfrak{c}\leq|G(B)|
, a contradiction.
∎

We now describe the way to choose the

.
At step

according to the following:

(1)

For each

we add any element of

to

.

(2)

For each
\xi<\alpha
we ask if
|\ran(f_{\xi})|=\mathfrak{c}
.
If the answer is yes, we add to
A_{\alpha}
any

such that

.

If neither step (1) or (2) adds elements to

, simply let

be singleton and disjoint from

. Note that clearly

|A_{\alpha}|\leq\max\{\aleph_{0},|\alpha|\}
.

Lemma 7.10
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathfrak{c}
be such that
|X|=\mathfrak{c}
, and

B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be such that
\aleph_{0}<|B|<\mathfrak{c}
.
Then
A(X)\ntrianglerighteq B
.

Proof.

Suppose
A(X)\trianglerighteq B
and that
\aleph_{0}<|B|<\mathfrak{c}
. Let
f:A(X)\twoheadrightarrow B
be
an epimorphism, and find
\alpha
such that

f\subseteq f_{\alpha}
.

First note that
|\mathbb{R}\setminus\dom(f_{\alpha})|=\mathfrak{c}
is impossible:
suppose this is the case, then taking

\beta>\alpha
in
X
, we see that the step (1) of the construction implies
that
A(X)
contains some
a\notin\dom(f_{\alpha})
,
but then also
a\notin\dom(f)
which is a contradiction. So,

|\mathbb{R}\setminus\dom(f_{\alpha})|<\mathfrak{c}
, and thus by
Proposition
7.9
, and using the fact that
B
is uncountable,
we conclude that
|\ran(f_{\alpha})|=\mathfrak{c}
.

Let
X^{\prime}:=X\setminus(\alpha+1)
, and note that by hypothesis,

|X^{\prime}|=\mathfrak{c}
. Now, for each
\beta\in X^{\prime}
pick

a_{\beta}\in A_{\beta}\cap\dom(f_{\alpha})

such that
f_{\alpha}(a_{\beta})\notin Z_{\beta}
. This exists by
step (2) of the construction. We claim that if
\beta<\gamma

are in
X^{\prime}
, then
f_{\alpha}(a_{\beta})\neq f_{\beta}(a_{\gamma})
.
This finishes the proof because then

|f(A(X))|=|f_{\alpha}(A(X))|\geq|X^{\prime}|=\mathfrak{c}>|B|
, which
is a contradiction.

To see this, note that since
\alpha<\beta<\gamma
,

a_{\beta}\in A_{\beta}\subseteq Z_{\gamma}
, thus
f_{\alpha}(a_{\beta})\in Z_{\gamma}
,
and by construction
f_{\alpha}(a_{\gamma})\notin Z_{\gamma}
.
∎

8.
On a question on Countryman lines

In this section we take a detour from epimorphisms, and study
the following question on Countryman lines, which in our opinion is quite
natural, and to the best of our knowledge, has not been considered
in the literature.
Recall that if
C
is a Countryman line then it enjoys the property
of having no two uncountable reverse isomorphic suborders.
We ask if this characterizes the Countryman lines in the class
of Aronszajn lines.

Usually a linear order
L
is called
reversible
if

L\cong L^{\star}
, otherwise it is called irreversible. In
this spirit we make the following definition.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
L
be a linear order. For a cardinal
\kappa
we say that
L
is

\kappa
-irreversible
if
|L|\geq\kappa
and

L
contains no two uncountable reverse
isomorphic suborders of cardinality
\kappa
.

Observe that the
\omega
-irreversible linear orders
are exactly the infinite ordinals and their reverses.
Easy examples of
\omega_{1}
-irreversible uncountable orders are

\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{1}^{\star}
and Countryman lines. As mentioned,
we are interested in the following question. Is there a non-Countryman

\omega_{1}
-irreversible Aronszajn line?
We have not seen an example of such
a line in the literature.

Moore
[
16
]
proved that under

\mathsf{PFA}
, every non-Countryman Aronszajn line contains both orientations
of any given Countryman line. Thus under
\mathsf{PFA}
there is no such
example. It is well known that under
\mathsf{PFA}
there are no Suslin trees, and that
any lexicographic ordering of a Suslin tree is a non-Countryman
Aronszajn line. Thus it is natural to look for an example that is
the lexicographic ordering of a Suslin tree.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be the forcing for adding a single Cohen real. Here
we take it as the finite partial functions from
\omega
to itself,
ordered by reverse inclusion. It is well known that if
G
is a generic
filter for
\mathbb{P}
, then in
V[G]
there is a Suslin tree. The
original proof is due to Shelah (see
[
18
]
). We will
prove that in this same model there is an example of an Aronszajn
line with the desired properties.

Theorem 8.2
.

If
\mathbb{P}
is the forcing for adding a single Cohen real, then in

V[G]
there is a
\omega_{1}
-irreversible lexicographically ordered Suslin
tree.

Our proof will be a modest modification of Todorcevic’s proof of Shelah’s theorem
(see
[
22
]
). For details
see
[
7
, Proposition 20.7]
.

Fix any uncountable and coherent
T\subseteq\omega^{<\omega_{1}}
of finite-to-one
functions such as the one used in the proof of Theorem
4.5
. As
mentioned in Lemma
4.3
, the lexicographic ordering
of
T
is a Countryman line.
We also need the following lemma, for a proof see the claim
of the mentioned proposition in
[
7
]
.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
K\subseteq T
be uncountable, and let
n\in\omega
. Then
there is an uncountable
K^{\prime}\subseteq K
such that
for all
s,t\in K^{\prime}
, if
\xi\in\dom(s)\cap\dom(t)
,
and
t(\xi)<n
or
s(\xi)<n
, then
t(\xi)=s(\xi)
.

We turn now to prove Theorem
8.2
.
Fix
G
a generic filter for
\mathbb{P}
, and let
\mathring{c}
be the canonical
name for
\bigcup G
. Then clearly
\mathbbm{1}\Vdash\mathring{c}:\check{\omega}\to\check{\omega}
.
Now working in
V[G]
let
T^{c}:=\{c\circ t:t\in T\}
. Todorcevic
proved that this is always a Suslin tree. Also a proof of this
is easily extracted from what follows, and not by chance, but because
the proof here follows that one.

Proposition 8.4
.

V[G]\models

(T^{c},<_{\mathrm{lex}})
is
\omega_{1}
-irreversible.

Proof.

Suppose towards a contradiction
that
V[G]\models T^{c}\text{ is not $\omega_{1}$-irreversible}
.
Then there must be
p\in G
and names
\mathring{A}
,
\mathring{f}
such
that

p\Vdash\mathring{A}\text{ is uncountable and }f:\mathring{A}\subseteq T^{\mathring{c}}\to T^{\mathring{c}}\text{ reverses }<_{\mathrm{lex}}.

To arrive to a contradiction it is enough to find some extension
of
p
that forces

(\exists t,s\in\mathring{A})(t<_{\mathrm{lex}}s\land\mathring{f}(t)<_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathring{f}(s))
.

For each
q\leq p
, consider the
set
A_{q}:=\{t\in T:q\Vdash\mathring{c}\circ\check{t}\in\mathring{A}\}
. Since
\mathbb{P}
is
countable, there is some
q\leq p
such that
A_{q}
is uncountable. Fix such a
q
.
In similar fashion, there is
q^{\prime}\leq q
, an uncountable
K\subseteq A_{q}
,
and
g:K\to T
in
V
such that for all
t\in K
,

q^{\prime}\Vdash\mathring{f}(\mathring{c}\circ\check{t})=\mathring{c}\circ\check{g}(\check{t})
. We may
assume that this is already forced by
q
. Let
n
be greater
than any element in
\dom(q)
. By going to an uncountable
subset of
K
, we may assume that
K
and
\{g(t):t\in K\}
satisfy
the conclusions of Lemma
8.3
.

Since
T
is Countryman we see that
T
is

\omega_{1}
-irreversible, and thus there are
t,s\in K

such that
t<_{\mathrm{lex}}s
and
g(t)<_{\mathrm{lex}}g(s)
. There are four
cases depending on the extension relation of
t
with
s
and
of
g(t)
with
g(s)
. In
case that
t\not\sqsubset s
we let
x
and
y
be such that

t(\xi)=x
and
s(\xi)=y
, where
\xi
is the first such that

t(\xi)\neq s(\xi)
. Otherwise we let
x
and
y
undefined.
In similar fashion, when
g(t)\not\sqsubset g(s)

we let
a=g(t)(\xi)
and
b=g(s)(\xi)
where

\xi
is the first such that
g(t)(\xi)\neq g(s)(\xi)
.
Note that when they are defined,

a,b,x,y>n
,
x<y
and
a<b
.

If
t\sqsubset s
and
g(t)\sqsubset g(s)
, then clearly

q\Vdash\mathring{c}\circ\check{t}\sqsubset\mathring{c}\circ\check{s}
and

q\Vdash\mathring{c}\circ\check{g}(\check{t})\sqsubset\mathring{c}\circ\check{g}(\check{s})
.
This arrives gives us our desired contradiction, since

q\Vdash\mathring{c}\circ\check{t},\mathring{c}\circ\check{s}\in\mathring{A}
and

q\Vdash\mathring{c}\circ\check{g}(\check{t})=\mathring{f}(\mathring{c}\circ\check{t})\land\mathring{c}\circ\check{g}(\check{s})=\mathring{f}(\mathring{c}\circ\check{s})
.

If
t\sqsubset s
and
g(t)\not\sqsubset g(s)
, then
letting
q^{\prime}:=q\cup\{(a,0),(b,1)\}
we see that
q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}
,

q^{\prime}\leq q
and

q^{\prime}\Vdash\mathring{c}\circ\check{t}\sqsubset\mathring{c}\circ\check{s}\land\mathring{c}\circ\check{g}(\check{t})<_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathring{c}\circ\check{g}(\check{s})
,
which is again a contradiction.
If
t\not\sqsubset s
and
g(t)\sqsubset g(s)
the argument is identical.

Assume now that
t\not\sqsubset s
and
g(t)\not\sqsubset g(s)
.
If
x=b
,
then
a<x=b<y
and thus

q^{\prime}:=q\cup\{(a,0),(x,1),(y,2)\}
is in
\mathbb{P}
. But note then

q^{\prime}\leq q
and

q\Vdash\mathring{c}\circ\check{t}<_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathring{c}\circ\check{s}\land\mathring{c}\circ\check{g}(\check{t})<_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathring{c}\circ\check{g}(\check{s})
,
and we arrive at a contradiction as before. Assume now
x\neq b
.
If
y=a

then
q^{\prime}:=q\cup\{(x,0),(a,1),(b,2)\}
works, and if
y\neq a

then
q^{\prime}:=q\cup\{(x,0),(y,1),(a,0),(b,1)\}
works.
∎

Remark 8.5
.

It was pointed out to us by Moore (and then also by the anonymous referee)
that that any coherent Aronszajn
tree
T\subseteq\omega^{<\omega_{1}}
is
\omega_{1}
-irreversible
with the natural lexicographic ordering. Note that this
gives an alternative proof of Theorem
8.2
, and it also
shows that
\diamondsuit
implies the existence of an

\omega_{1}
-irreversible lexicographically ordered Suslin
tree. This is because
\diamondsuit
implies the existence of a
coherent Suslin tree (see for
example
[
21
, Theorem 6.9]
).

To see that the claim is true one proves that the ccc forcing for
specializing
T
(see
[
8
, Theorem 16.17]
) makes

(T,<_{\mathrm{lex}})
Countryman in the extension
(see
[
23
, 4.4]
). Thus in the extension

(T,<_{\mathrm{lex}})
has no two uncountable reverse isomorphic
suborders. But since
\omega_{1}
is preserved, this must already hold
in the ground model.

9.
Concluding remarks and questions

As mentioned in Section
3
, this work leaves open
Question
6
of whether
\eta_{C}
(or any other universal Aronszajn line)
is strongly surjective under
\mathsf{PFA}
.
Recently in
[
6
]
, answering a question by
Baumgartner, a
\preceq
-minimal Countryman line was constructed under

\diamondsuit
. As mentioned in the introduction,
in
[
19
]
it is proved that
\diamondsuit^{+}
implies
the existence of a strongly surjective Suslin line. Does

\diamondsuit
imply the existence of a strongly surjective Aronszajn
line?

The work in Sections
5
and
6
shows
that already the class of Countryman lines is not well-quasi-ordered by

\trianglelefteq
. This is done by considering Aronszajn lines that have
decompoisitions with a stationary set of endpoints. It seems that a natural
question is whether the class of
normal
Aronszajn lines is
well-quasi-ordered by
\trianglelefteq
under
\mathsf{PFA}
. By Theorem
2.8

this is clearly true for the class of Countryman lines.

Regarding Section
8
, Theorem
8.2
and
Remark
8.5
suggest the following question. Does the
existence of a Suslin tree suffices to show the existence of a
non-Countryman
\omega_{1}
-irreversible Aronszajn line?
