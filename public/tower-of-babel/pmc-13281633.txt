Glaucoma following cataract surgery in pediatric patients: a systematic review of incidence, risk factors, and management strategies
Authors: Seham S Alhemaidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlaucoma following pediatric cataract surgery is a sight-threatening complication with reported incidence rates of 3.3–25.5%, influenced by surgical factors, patient age, and anatomical characteristics. This review synthesizes evidence from 36 studies, identifying younger age at surgery (< 9 months), aphakia, microcornea, and sulcus intraocular lens (IOL) implantation as key risk factors, while in-the-bag IOL placement with posterior optic capture demonstrates the lowest glaucoma risk. A bimodal onset pattern (early postoperative vs. delayed presentation) necessitates lifelong monitoring, particularly in high-risk cases. Surgical management with goniotomy shows efficacy, though heterogeneity in study designs and diagnostic criteria limits consensus. These findings underscore the need for standardized protocols, meticulous surgical techniques, and risk-adapted follow-up. Introduction

Glaucoma following cataract surgery (GFCS), formerly known as aphakic or pseudophakic glaucoma, remains the most frequent and visually threatening complication of pediatric cataract surgery [1]. Recognized as a distinct entity by the World Glaucoma Association and the Childhood Glaucoma Research Network, this secondary glaucoma poses significant challenges due to its insidious onset and potential for lifelong progression [2, 3].

The reported incidence of GFCS varies widely, from 6% to 32%, primarily influenced by the age at surgery and the duration of follow-up [4]. For instance, the Infant Aphakia Treatment Study (IATS) documented an increase in GFCS incidence from 17% at five years to 22% at ten years post-surgery [5, 6]. The risk is particularly pronounced in infants; surgery before nine months of age increases the risk of developing secondary glaucoma by up to 7.2 times compared to later surgery [7, 8]. This association is consistently supported by long-term cohort studies, which report glaucoma rates of 37% in children operated on by nine months versus 6% in those operated on later, and 29.2% in infants under nine months compared to 3.4% in older children [9, 10]. The heightened risk with early intervention creates a clinical dilemma, as early surgery is often necessary to prevent irreversible amblyopia from visual deprivation [11].

Beyond the timing of surgery, several anatomical and surgical factors have been identified as key contributors to GFCS. Anatomical risk factors include shorter axial length, microcornea (corneal diameter ≤ 10 mm), and the presence of persistent fetal vasculature (PFV), which may indicate an inherently compromised anterior segment [12–14]. Surgical factors such as postoperative inflammation, the use of topical corticosteroids, retained lens material, and the need for secondary intraocular procedures further elevate the risk [5, 6, 11, 15, 16]. The choice of intraocular lens (IOL) placement is also critical, with sulcus fixation associated with a significantly higher glaucoma risk compared to in-the-bag implantation.

The pathophysiology of GFCS is multifactorial. Proposed mechanisms include postoperative obstruction of the trabecular meshwork by inflammatory debris or retained lens proteins, steroid-induced ocular hypertension, and anatomical changes such as a reduction in Schlemm’s canal diameter and narrowing of the anterior chamber angle [17–24]. While angle-closure glaucoma has become less common with modern surgical techniques, late-onset open-angle glaucoma remains the predominant form in both aphakic and pseudophakic patients [25, 26].

GFCS is notoriously difficult to manage, often requiring multiple medical and surgical interventions over a patient’s lifetime, and the risk of its development persists for years after the initial cataract surgery [24, 27–32], . Consequently, the literature universally advocates for early and lifelong ophthalmic surveillance for all children undergoing cataract surgery. Given the significant clinical burden and the complexity of managing GFCS, this systematic review aims to synthesize and critically evaluate the existing evidence on its incidence, risk factors, pathophysiology, diagnostic approaches, and management strategies in the pediatric population.

Methodology

Research

This systematic review was conducted following the international guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA).

A search of relevant publications was conducted with the use of the following electronic databases: Medline (via PubMed), Scopus and the Cochrane Library and was carried out between January 2015 and September 2025.

The literature presented has been vetted through the formulation of a search string common to the following databases: Medline, Scopus and Cochrane Library containing keywords as follows: ((Glaucoma) AND (Cataract Surgery)) AND (Pediatric).

Study selection

The selection process was conducted in two steps:

Screening Titles and Abstracts – Two independent reviewers screened the retrieved articles based on their title and abstract. Duplicate studies were removed, and only relevant publications discussing the incidence, risk factors, and management strategies of glaucoma following pediatric cataract surgery were considered.

Screening Titles and Abstracts – Two independent reviewers screened the retrieved articles based on their title and abstract. Duplicate studies were removed, and only relevant publications discussing the incidence, risk factors, and management strategies of glaucoma following pediatric cataract surgery were considered.

Full-Text Review – Articles that met the initial screening criteria were subjected to a full-text review to ensure they met the eligibility criteria. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved by consulting a third reviewer.

Results and discussion

General characteristics of included studies

This systematic review analyzed 28 studies investigating glaucoma following pediatric cataract surgery (GFCS). Supplementary Table S1 provides comprehensive details of all included studies, including country, design, sample size, follow-up duration, and quality assessment scores.

This research analyzed a wide range of research designs, geographical distributions, and methodological approaches. In terms of study design distribution, observational studies comprised the majority (n = 23, 82.1%), primarily featuring retrospective cohorts or case series with sample sizes ranging from 20 to 2,421 patients. Randomized controlled trials accounted for 17.9% (n = 5) and focused on comparative surgical techniques, such as in-the-bag versus sulcus IOL implantation, posterior optic capture, and IOL material comparisons. Geographically, Asian studies predominated (n = 18, 64.3%), reflecting high-volume pediatric cataract surgical centers in China, India, and Taiwan, while Western studies (n = 10, 35.7%) contributed long-term outcome data from the United States, Australia, Europe, and the United Kingdom. Follow-up durations varied from 1 to 13.6 years across studies, with only 6 studies (21.4%) exceeding 10 years of follow-up, indicating a critical evidence gap for late-onset glaucoma. Additionally, there was substantial heterogeneity in glaucoma definitions; 14 studies (50%) utilized intraocular pressure (IOP) thresholds alone (≥ 21 mmHg or ≥ 22 mmHg), while the other 14 studies (50%) included additional criteria such as optic nerve cupping, corneal enlargement, and axial length changes. This methodological diversity highlights the necessity for standardized diagnostic protocols in future research.

The analysis revealed moderate overall quality across the included studies. Among observational studies, quality ratings ranged from low to high, with common limitations including retrospective designs (65% of observational studies) and single-center settings. High-quality evidence emerged from six retrospective cohort studies that achieved the maximum NOS score of 9/9, providing robust data on long-term glaucoma outcomes. These high-quality studies include the French retrospective study [33], US Visual Outcomes study [34], Australian retrospective study [35], Danish study[36], Chinese Zhang study [37], and the Chinese IOL comparison study [38]. Most observational studies (n = 13, 56.5%) were rated as moderate quality, while four studies (17.4%) were classified as low quality due to fundamental design constraints such as small sample sizes, short follow-up durations, or lack of multivariate adjustment.

Among the five randomized controlled trials [39–43] bias assessment using RoB 2 revealed that one RCT demonstrated low risk of bias across all domains, providing the highest level of evidence for surgical technique comparisons. The remaining four RCTs had “some concerns” primarily due to incomplete blinding of surgeons or outcome assessors, which is an inherent challenge in surgical trials. No RCT was rated as high risk of bias, and there were no concerns regarding missing outcome data or selective reporting.

Overall, the evidence base provides moderate confidence in the findings, with the strongest support for established risk factors (young age, microcornea, sulcus IOL placement) derived from six high-quality observational studies and one low-bias RCT, while weaker evidence characterizes emerging risk factors identified in single studies.

Incidence of glaucoma following pediatric cataract surgery

The reported incidence of GFCS ranged from 6.1% to 25.5% across the 28 included studies, with variability primarily attributable to differences in age at surgery, follow-up duration, and diagnostic criteria. Studies with longer follow-up consistently reported higher cumulative incidence, highlighting the importance of lifelong surveillance (Table 2).

Discussion

Pediatric glaucoma encompasses a wide range of conditions characterized by ocular structural damage and vision impairment linked to elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) [53]. The World Glaucoma Association classifies pediatric glaucoma as either primary or secondary, with primary congenital glaucoma (PCG) representing a distinct genetic entity often associated with CYP1B1 mutations and autosomal recessive inheritance, particularly prevalent in consanguineous populations [2, 3, 54, 55]. While these genetic insights are crucial for understanding primary glaucomas, GFCS represents a distinct secondary form with its own unique risk profile. Unlike PCG, which arises from developmental abnormalities of the anterior chamber angle, GFCS develops after otherwise successful cataract extraction and is influenced by a complex interplay of patient-related and surgically-modifiable factors. This distinction is critical, as the prevention and management strategies for these two forms of childhood glaucoma differ substantially.

This systematic review aimed to synthesize and critically evaluate the existing evidence on the incidence, risk factors, pathogenesis, diagnostic approaches, and management strategies for GFCS in pediatric patients. Specifically, the review summarized reported incidence rates and factors contributing to their variability, analyze associated risk factors (anatomical, genetic, and surgical), evaluate the effectiveness of current management strategies, and identify gaps in the literature to guide future research.

Incidence and temporal pattern

This review of 28 studies confirms that GFCS remains a significant long-term complication with incidence rates ranging from 6.1% to 25.5%. The wide variability is primarily attributable to differences in age at surgery, follow-up duration, and surgical techniques across studies, highlighting the need for standardized reporting.

The bimodal distribution observed in the French study-with early-onset cases at a mean of 2.5 months and late-onset cases at a mean of 5.7 years corroborated by the Greek study reporting mean onset at 4.9 years, suggests that different pathophysiological mechanisms may dominate at different postoperative stages. Early cases may reflect acute angle-related mechanisms or steroid-induced ocular hypertension, while late-onset cases likely represent chronic trabecular dysfunction [33, 46]. The cumulative risk increasing steadily over time, with 12% at 5 years and 28% at 10 years, underscores the necessity for lifelong surveillance beyond the early postoperative period.

Age at surgery

Age at surgery emerged as the most potent and consistently confirmed risk factor. The Swedish study provided precise timing data, demonstrating that surgery within the first month of life carries a 48.3% glaucoma risk, which drops dramatically to 6.7% when surgery is performed between 5 weeks and 2 years of age [45]. This refines the established cut-point of 9 months (37% vs. 6% risk) [47] and suggests that when clinically feasible, delaying surgery by even a few weeks beyond the first month may substantially reduce glaucoma risk. However, this must be balanced against amblyopia risk, which drives the need for early intervention [46]. Importantly, the Chinese RCT demonstrated that early surgery (~ 3 months) combined with optimal technique (posterior capsulorhexis and anterior vitrectomy) can achieve excellent outcomes (2% glaucoma rate) [9], while the Indian Miraj study reported 0% glaucoma in early-operated unilateral cases with meticulous technique [40]. These findings suggest that surgical technique may be more impactful than absolute timing, and that early surgery need not be avoided if accompanied by complete capsule management and careful IOL placement.

Anatomical risk factors

Anatomical vulnerability plays a critical role. Microcornea was confirmed as an independent predictor in five studies, including the Australian cohort (HR 6.30) [35] and the Chinese Zhang study (HR 3.92) [37]. The Zhang study further identified novel anatomical predictors: preoperative central corneal thickness (HR 1.021 per micron), preoperative horizontal corneal diameter (HR 3.92), and preoperative lens thickness (HR 3.75) [37]. Most strikingly, family history of congenital cataract emerged as the strongest individual risk factor identified to date (HR 50.46) [37]. Pre-existing glaucoma-associated conditions increased risk to 57.1% in the Swedish study [45]. These findings suggest that inherently compromised anterior segments are less tolerant of surgical disruption and warrant closer postoperative surveillance.

Surgical risk factors and technique comparisons

Surgical factors demonstrated clear gradients of risk. Primary in-the-bag IOL implantation consistently showed the lowest glaucoma risk (0% in Zhang study [37], while secondary IOL implantation carried substantially higher risk (18.8%) and aphakia carried intermediate risk (7.9%).

Context for Zhang et al. study: In the Zhang et al. study [37], it is important to note that the groups differed in age at surgery: the primary IOL group (n = 111) had a median age of 34.0 months (IQR 15.0–46.0), the aphakia group (n = 63) had a median age of 3.0 months (IQR 2.0–4.0), and the secondary IOL group (n = 85) had primary cataract removal at a median age of 4.0 months (IQR 3.0–5.0) with secondary IOL implantation at a median age of 34.0 months (IQR 24.0–37.0). Despite these age differences, the study reported glaucoma-related adverse events in 0% of the primary IOL group, 7.9% of the aphakia group, and 18.8% of the secondary IOL group, suggesting that primary IOL implantation may be associated with lower glaucoma risk.

Sulcus placement increased risk 14-fold compared to in-the-bag placement [38]. Reintervention was confirmed as an independent risk factor (OR 4.1) [33]. The Danish study provided novel evidence that high-dose glucocorticoids increase risk specifically in eyes with axial length ≥ 18 mm, supporting personalized steroid protocols based on ocular biometry [36]. Notably, IOL material (hydrophobic acrylic vs. silicone) did not differentially affect glaucoma risk, indicating that surgical technique and patient factors outweigh material effects [39].

Alternative surgical techniques offer promising options. Posterior optic capture demonstrated comparable safety (3.3% glaucoma rate) with additional benefits in preventing visual axis opacification [51]. The bag-in-the-lens technique showed excellent safety (1.3% glaucoma) when performed after 5 weeks of age [45]. For traumatic cases, posterior chamber IOLs demonstrated a slightly better safety profile than iris-claw IOLs (0% vs. 4%) [50].

Pathophysiological insights

Pathophysiological insights from the UK Schlemm canal study provide a biomechanical explanation for GFCS, demonstrating that reduced canal size and impaired accommodation-mediated dilation contribute to IOP elevation [19]. This mechanistically supports angle-based surgeries over trabecular bypass procedures and validates the clinical success of goniotomy observed in this review.

Management of established GFCS

Management of established GFCS remains challenging. Over 50% of affected eyes eventually require surgical intervention. Goniotomy emerged as a promising first-line option (81.8% IOP control over 9–15 months) [52], though longer follow-up is needed to confirm durability. Trabeculectomy demonstrated high failure rates in the pediatric population [46], while glaucoma drainage devices showed variable success with many eyes requiring additional interventions [34]. Triamcinolone-assisted vitrectomy proved safe, maintaining stable IOP while minimizing postoperative complications [36].

Clinical significance

GFCS remains a significant and challenging complication that can lead to irreversible visual impairment despite advances in surgical techniques. The pathogenesis is multifactorial, involving anatomical, genetic, and surgical factors. Early diagnosis and effective management are critical, but GFCS often presents insidiously and requires lifelong surveillance. The lack of a standardized classification system and the heterogeneity of pediatric glaucoma make it difficult to predict outcomes and tailor treatment strategies (10–13). This review addresses this need by synthesizing current evidence and highlighting actionable insights for clinicians.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths, including a comprehensive search strategy, inclusion of high-quality studies from diverse geographic regions, and rigorous quality assessment using standardized tools. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the heterogeneity of included studies in design, follow-up durations (1–13.6 years), and glaucoma definitions precluded meta-analysis. Second, the predominance of retrospective, single-center studies (65%) introduces potential selection bias. Third, only 21.4% of studies exceeded 10 years of follow-up, which is concerning given the late-onset nature of GFCS. Fourth, the exclusion of non-English studies may have introduced language bias. Fifth, the variability in surgical techniques and postoperative protocols across centers and eras may confound risk factor analyses.

Future directions

To advance the field, future research should prioritize: (1) multicenter prospective cohorts with standardized, protocolized glaucoma screening and follow-up exceeding 10 years; (2) RCTs comparing angle-based surgeries (e.g., goniotomy) versus traditional filtering procedures for managing established GFCS; (3) development of consensus definitions for GFCS to harmonize incidence reporting; (4) integration of genetic and biomechanical markers into risk stratification models; and (5) investigation of optimal timing for IOL implantation in high-risk infants, balancing glaucoma risk against visual outcomes.

Conclusion

Glaucoma following pediatric cataract surgery is a common, complex, and potentially blinding complication that demands lifelong vigilance. This review confirms that younger age at surgery, anatomical anomalies (microcornea, family history, abnormal biometry), and sulcus IOL placement are the most robust risk factors. In-the-bag IOL implantation with posterior optic capture offers the lowest glaucoma risk and should be prioritized when anatomically feasible. Management requires a stepwise approach, with goniotomy emerging as an effective first-line surgical intervention. However, the high long-term treatment burden and persistent evidence gaps underscore the urgent need for standardized protocols, multicenter collaborations, and extended follow-up to optimize outcomes for these vulnerable children.
