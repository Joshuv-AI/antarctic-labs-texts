Flank-free modified supine vs. prone position for pediatric nephrolithotripsy: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Breno C Porto, Roberto N Santana, Ingrid M S Duarte, Carlo C Passerotti, Rodrigo A S Sardenberg, Ronaldo S Maia, Jose P Otoch, Jose A S da Cruz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPercutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is widely performed as the preferred treatment for kidney stones larger than 20 mm in pediatric patients, with current research focusing on comparing outcomes between prone and supine positions to determine optimal procedural positioning. Therefore, the aim of this study is to compare the efficacy of prone versus supine positioning in pediatric PCNL, providing clarity on this critical aspect of the procedure to guide clinical decision-making.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane, Web of Science and Google Scholar. We included studies that compared PCNL in prone vs supine positions for pediatric patients. Our primary outcome was stone-free rate (SFR). Secondary outcomes included operative time, length of hospital stay and overall complications rate. The statistical analysis was performed using Review Manager 5.4.ResultsWe retrieved 8 articles, with 269 patients in the prone group and 223 patients in the supine group. The mean age of all patients was 7.92 years old. Our findings presented no statistically significant difference in SFR between the two positions (OR 0.67; CI95 0.38, 1.18; p = 0.17; I2 = 0%). Additionally, we noted a significant reduction in operative time in the supine position group (MD 13.75; CI95 4.35, 23.15; p = 0.004; I2 = 84%). At the same time, the length of hospital stay after the procedure was lower in supine group (MD 0.61; CI95 0.34, 0.88; p < 0.0001; I2 = 21%). No difference was observed regarding the total complication rate (OR 1.47; CI95 0.88, 2.47; p = 0.15; I2 = 0%).ConclusionOur meta-analysis suggests that PCNL performed in the prone position is equivalent to supine PCNL in terms of SFR. However, mainly in the RCT studies, we could observe benefits of the supine position in comparison of prone position in terms of lower operative time, as well as a reduced postoperative hospital stay.Keywords: Pediatrics, Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy, Prone position, Supine position

Introduction

Over the past two decades, the incidence of pediatric nephrolithiasis has increased by an estimated 4 to 10% annually [1]. The first pediatric percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) was performed by Woodside [2] in 1985. Although, the technique has been modified in recent decades to become a less invasive and well-implemented procedure for pediatric nephrolithiasis [3].

The European Association of Urology (EAU) Pediatric guidelines endorse PCNL as the primary treatment option for large kidney stones (> 20 mm) and those stones exceeding 10 mm in the lower renal pole [4]. Similarly, the American Urological Association (AUA) concurs with this recommendation, advocating for PCNL and Shock Wave Lithotripsy (SWL) as suitable treatment modalities in symptomatic pediatric patients with a total renal stone burden exceeding 20 mm [5].

While PCNL remains the gold standard for large stone treatment procedures, it is associated with a significant morbidity rate [6]. These complications can range from fever and hemorrhage to wound infections and, in severe instances, even sepsis [7, 8]. The prone technique was initially introduced by Goodwin in 1955 within the field of urological surgery [9]. Subsequently, in 1987 Valdivia Uría pioneered the technique of the supine position, which aimed to minimize complication rates and decrease the overall morbidity associated with PCNL [10].

Since the last meta-analysis by Ramez et al. [11], new studies and perspectives have emerged. It is not merely the expansion in cohort size, but also the necessity for subgroup analyses comparing the validity of act and non-act trials that highlight the importance of further investigation. Multiple centers have conducted comparative studies on prone and supine positions, primarily in adult populations. However, despite well-established indications for PCNL in current guidelines, there is a notable absence of formal recommendations regarding optimal patient positioning—prone or supine—for pediatric patients undergoing PCNL. This gap, coupled with the scarcity of data on the most effective surgical approach for this group, makes our updated meta-analysis particularly significant. It aims to assess the latest evidence and provide insights into the best practices for pediatric patient positioning during PCNL, ensuring that clinical recommendations remain aligned with evolving evidence and support informed decision-making in urological practice.

Thus, the purpose of this study is to conduct an update meta-analysis of existing studies that investigate the outcomes associated with prone and supine positions in PCNL for pediatric kidney stones.

Material and methods

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis were performed and reported in accordance with the Cochrane Collaboration Handbook for Systematic Review of interventions and the Preferred reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Statement guidelines [12].

We searched MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane, Web of Science and Google Scholar from April 2023 to September 2023 for comparative trials of prone vs supine PCNL in pediatric patients. Our search strategy was: ("ultra mini percutaneous" OR UMP OR UMPCNL OR "mini-percutaneous" OR "miniaturized percutaneous" OR percutan* OR PNL OR nephrolithotomy) AND (supine OR dorsal OR valdivia OR Galdakao OR "flank free" OR "free modified" OR FFMS) AND (prone OR "split leg" OR ventral) AND (pediatric* OR child* OR paediatrics).

The references from all included studies, previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses were also searched manually for any additional studies. The prospective meta-analysis protocol was registered on PROSPERO under protocol CRD42023449354.

Eligibility criteria for study selection

We included: (1) Randomized trials (RCTs) or non-randomized cohorts (non-RCTs); (2) comparing percutaneous nephrolithotomy, and its variants, in pediatric patients (< 18 years old); (3) diagnosed with single or multiple renal or calyx stones (2–3 cm). In addition, studies were included only if they reported any of the clinical outcomes of interest.

We excluded studies with (1) adult patients; (2) anatomical abnormalities (e.g. solitary kidney, ureteral or urethral stricture); (3) patients with active urinary tract infection; (4) skeletal anomalies; (5) or patients with bleeding disorders. Additionally, in order to obtain better accuracy of the articles and relevance of the presented data, articles with any of these characteristics were excluded: (a) case report, (b) systematic review, and (c) bibliographic review.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint is stone-free rate (SFR) of prone versus supine mini-PCNL. Secondarily we analyzed the operative time, length of postoperative hospital stay and complications rate.

Screening

After deduplication, where we used Endnote online™ 20 (Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA) [13], two independent researchers (BP and RS) screened the studies by title and abstract, and disagreements were solved by a third (JC). Following this process, full text screening was performed.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two authors (BP and RS) independently extracted the data based on a predefined protocol and disagreements were solved by a third (JC). Risk of bias was assessed in randomized studies using version 2 of the Cochrane Risk of Bias assessment tool (RoB 2) [14]. Non-randomized studies were assessed with the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies – of Interventions tool (ROBINS-I) [15]. Two independent authors completed the risk of bias assessment (BP and RS). Disagreements were resolved through a consensus after discussing reasons for discrepancy.

Statistical analysis

Continuous outcomes are presented as a mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Dichotomous data are presented as odds ratio (OR) with 95% CI. Pooled estimates were calculated with the random-effects model, considering that the patients came from different populations. We considered a study to exhibit considerable heterogeneity if, following the statistical analysis, the I2 statistic is equal to or greater than 30%.

Review Manager 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Denmark, Copenhagen) was used for statistical analysis [16].

Results

Study selection and characteristics

After performing our screening, we retrieved 390 articles. Following the deduplication and screening process, 8 articles [3, 17–23] were deemed relevant and included in our analysis (Fig. 1/PRISMA flow chart). For a comprehensive overview of the patient demographics and the outcome data across all included studies, please refer to Table 1. Regarding the design of the included studies, 4 of them was RCT, while the remaining 4 were non-RCTs. We should point out that the plots were performed through a subgroup analysis of RCT studies and not-RCT studies. Combining the data from these articles, we analyzed a total of 269 patients in the prone PCNL branch and 223 patients in the supine branch. The mean age of all patients was 7.92 years old. Although all studies employed the same general technique, there was slight variation in invasiveness in terms of ureteroscope size. Most studies used a mini-percutaneous technique, while only Desoky et al [3] utilized the ultra-mini-percutaneous approach (6–7.5F) (Table 2).

Meta-analysis

When comparing the overall SFR of prone versus supine, we observed no difference between the two approaches (OR 0.67; CI95 0.38, 1.18; p = 0.17; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 2).

Regarding operative time, we observed a reduced in supine group mainly in overall comparison and RCT analysis (overall comparison: MD 13.75; CI95 4.35, 23.215; p = 0.004; I2 = 91%;—Subgroup RCT: MD 17.15; CI95 6.07, 28.24; p = 0.002; I2 = 94%) (Fig. 3A). Quality assessment

The 4 RCT trials were assessed by the Rob-2 tool. Three of them presented low risk, and only one, the cohort from Abouhashem et al [24] presented a moderate risk. (Fig. 6A). On the other hand, the non-RCT, that were assessed through Robins-I score trials, presented two trials of low risk, the Hajiyev [23] trial regard some concerns, and Campobasso [22] showed a serious risk of bias, mainly due to deviations from intended interventions (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

Our meta-analysis included 8 studies (Table 1) and aimed to compare prone versus supine PCNL for kidney stones in pediatric patients, which is a recent technique, since it was first defined by Helal et al. in 1997 [25]. The primary outcome assessed in our work was the SFR, and no difference could be found. The operative time and the rate of postoperative complications were also evaluated, and no differences were seen between both techniques.

Currently, various guidelines indicate that both prone and supine positions in PCNL are safe and effective for pediatric patients, with each position offering specific advantages based on individual patient needs. The EAU/European Society for Paediatric Urology guidelines [4] recommend minimally invasive approaches in pediatric urolithiasis management but do not favor one position over the other, allowing flexibility based on surgical context. Similarly, the International Alliance of Urolithiasis guidelines [26] do not provide a formal preference for prone or supine positioning, citing a lack of significant differences in safety, SFRs, and complication rates between the two methods in pediatric patients. At the same time, a review article conducted by Güven et al [27] concluded that both positions are viable and effective for pediatric patients undergoing PCNL, in some way that the choice between prone and supine positions depend on individual patient factors. Each position presents distinct advantages: the prone position offers improved access for multiple punctures and enhanced percutaneous options, whereas the supine position provides better cardiovascular and airway management, along with the possibility of simultaneous retrograde access to the collecting system [4, 26, 28]. Although the conclusions supporting these positions primarily derive from adult studies, there is a notable lack of pediatric-specific evaluations. This gap in pediatric research underpins our study’s purpose to contribute further evidence regarding optimal positioning in pediatric PCNL.

Our findings revealed that there were no significant differences in SFR between the prone and supine positions, with rates of 85.1% and 90.1%, respectively. This suggests that both positions are equally effective in achieving stone clearance in pediatric patients undergoing PCNL. These results align with current guidelines in the field, which generally conclude that there is no clear superiority of one position over the other in adult patients, reinforcing the necessity to evaluate case-by-case, and the importance to surgeons to perform the approach they are more used and comfortable with.

Although the operative time did not reach statistical significance in the non-RCT subgroup analysis, the overall results favored the supine position, as it was associated with reduced surgical time. Graphically, we can observe that Campombasso's [22] data diverge from the other studies, contributing to the higher heterogeneity (80%). This elevated heterogeneity can be attributed to the varying methods of defining surgical time, such as the start of surgery in the operating room, the time of puncture, or lithotripsy initiation. Nevertheless, the overall trend of reduced operative time in the supine position is consistent with findings in the literature. This can likely be explained by the additional time required for repositioning the patient from supine to prone after anesthetic induction [17, 29–32].

In terms of postoperative hospital stay, the supine group once again demonstrated a more favorable outcome, with a reduced length of stay compared to the prone group. This result can be attributed to several factors. For instance, while the rates of Clavien-Dindo grade 2 and 3 complications are generally similar between groups, grade 1 complications are more frequent in the prone group. Many of these complications, such as position-related pain and fever due to position-induced atelectasis [21, 33], are associated with the prone position. It is also important to note that the morbidity of the procedure is reduced with the convenience of using smaller caliber (> 20 Fr) devices. As a result, the more complications a patient experiences, the longer their hospital stay is likely to be. Common complications include wound infection, fever, hematuria, bleeding, urine leakage, and more serious events like ureteral injury, urosepsis and acute renal failure, albeit rare [34, 35]. It's worth mentioning that the prone position in PCNL is associated with an increased cardiopulmonary risk, as noted in the literature [36]. Our meta-analysis indicated a complication rate of 17.9% in the prone group and 13.2% in the supine group. It should be noted that, due to the small sample size of patients and the absence of representation in each Clavien-Dindo grade across the included studies, we opted for an overall comparison rather than a stratified one. Additionally, performing a stratified analysis with such limited data would significantly reduce statistical power, potentially leading to unreliable or inconclusive results. An overall comparison allows us to maintain a more robust statistical framework and draw meaningful conclusions from the available evidence.

When comparing the results of the latest systematic review and meta-analysis on the preference for prone or supine positions in PCNL for the pediatric population (Ramez et al., 2024 [11]) with our findings, it is important to highlight that although our study included two additional studies, thereby increasing the cohort size, the overall result aligns with the previous analysis. However, our updated meta-analysis brings several enhancements. Not only were we able to more clearly define the outcomes, but we also included a new RCT, and performed a subgroup analysis, particularly for operative time and length of hospital stay. These analyses underscore the weaker evidential strength of the retrospective studies, contributing significantly to the observed heterogeneity, which was also evidenced through the leave one out analysis and Baujat graphic in Fig. 3. Furthermore, the inclusion of an RCT strengthens our ability to make more reliable conclusions, as randomized studies tend to mitigate bias and provide higher-quality evidence.

The comparison of positions in PCNL has been investigated in adults for other authors. Yuan et al. [37] conducted a meta-analysis with 6881 patients assessing prone and supine for PCNL. It was found a higher SFR in the supine group, while all the other outcomes presented no difference. Another study, this one a multicenter randomized controlled trial, performed by Perrela et al., [38] included 112 patients and showed a similar SFR between both positions and a shorter operative time in supine group, and agreed upon the justification of the increased time caused by the repositioning of the patient.

Our study has limitations. Firstly, we counted with a small sample of patients due to the scarcity of studies in literature. Secondly, it is crucial to acknowledge that the surgeon's preferences regarding positioning may influence the ultimate choice of placement method. Thirdly, associated with the paucity of existing literature on this topic, the eight studies included exhibited variations in study design, ranging from RCTs to non-RCT. This diversity, to some extent, may limit the generalizability of the meta-analysis results. This difference was more present in the operative time and postoperative hospital stay, since in both these analysis, the overall comparison and RCT subgroup analysis differs from the non-RCT analysis.

Looking ahead, future research in this area could benefit from comparing PCNL with alternative procedures, as well as conducting controlled studies that consider stone density and caliceal localization. Additionally, assessing a greater quantity of RCT studies could provide valuable insights, and even finally determine which approach is better, or if there is in fact no difference between then and the chose for the position should the doctors preference.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis indicates that both prone and supine positions for PCNL in pediatric patients achieve similar outcomes in SFRs and complication rates, with the supine position showing potential benefits of reduced operative time and shorter hospital stay.

Clinically, these findings suggest that either position can be safely and effectively employed, allowing the choice of position to be tailored based on patient characteristics, surgical team expertise, and operating room logistics. To further support clinical decision-making, particularly in cases where patient anatomy or comorbidities may influence positioning, additional large-scale RCTs are recommended. These future studies could provide more definitive guidance for optimizing patient outcomes in pediatric PCNL procedures.
