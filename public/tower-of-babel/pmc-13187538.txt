Bone microenvironment-driven macro-galvanic coupling of Zn alloys: Mediating immune response and osteogenesis
Authors: Chaoyang Sun, Bo Jia, Shuang Li, Xinhua Qu, Xuyang Sun, Kai Yan, Jiahui Shi, Yufeng Zheng, Hongtao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biodegradable metals exhibit heterogeneous degradation patterns within complex physiological microenvironments, which frequently culminate in premature implant sequestration or failure. While traditional research paradigms have prioritized enhancing material homogeneity, the impact of site-specific physiological variability remains insufficiently addressed. This study investigated the degradation kinetics and osteogenic efficacy of Zn-0.8Mg and Zn-0.8Fe alloys implanted into distinct anatomical sites: the medullary cavity and the bone matrix. Our findings reveal that microenvironmental fluctuations trigger macro-galvanic coupling within the Zn alloys, inducing localized crevice corrosion. The spatiotemporal flux of Zn2+ was identified as a decisive factor in modulating bone regeneration. In the well-perfused medullary cavity, the Zn alloy functioned as a cathodic site, exhibiting suppressed degradation rates. This sustained Zn2+ release within a pro-osteogenic therapeutic window, significantly enhancing bone volume; notably, the Zn-0.8Mg alloy elicited a 10-fold increase in new bone formation relative to pure Titanium (Ti) after one month. Conversely, within the transport-restricted bone matrix, the alloy acted as an anode, undergoing accelerated degradation. The resultant Zn2+ overload triggered pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization and impaired osteoblast viability. Histological analysis at one-month post-implantation revealed am non-ossified zone surrounding the implants, measuring approximately 50 μm for Zn-0.8Mg and 100 μm for Zn-0.8Fe. These insights underscore that the implant microenvironment dictates the material's biological fate, providing new understanding for the design of next-generation, microenvironment-adaptive orthopedic implants.

Introduction

Biodegradable zinc (Zn) alloys have emerged as a leading candidate for next-generation bone repair materials, distinguished by balanced degradation kinetics, favorable mechanical integrity, and inherent osteogenic bioactivity. Current researches focused on optimizing these properties through strategic alloying (e.g., Zn-Mg, Zn-Li) [[1], [2], [3]] and advanced manufacturing techniques like laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) for fabricating porous scaffolds [[4], [5], [6], [7]], aiming to achieve mechanical compatibility with native bone and stimulate regeneration. Preliminary studies have confirmed that Zn alloy implants can provide sufficient mechanical support during the critical healing period and eventually degrade safely in vivo, demonstrating the feasibility of its clinical transformation [8,9]. However, a significant limitation persists in conventional research paradigms, the osteogenic capacity of Zn alloys is often perceived as an intrinsic and uniform biological property, largely overlooking the decisive regulatory role of the inherently heterogeneous bone repair microenvironment on the material's biological functions [10]. This gap critically limits the precision and reliability of Zn-based implants in complex clinical settings.

Clinically successful osseointegration necessitates synchronized and balanced osteogenesis across distinct skeletal compartments surrounding the implant. Specifically, the highly vascularized, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-rich medullary cavity and the mechanically demanding, structurally dense bone matrix zone [11,12]. In the repair of segmental defects or arthrodesis, the ideal healing outcome involved stable intramedullary support coupled with external callus bridging in the cortical zone and concurrent new bone ingrowth within the medullary cavity, ensuring comprehensive structural restoration [[13], [14], [15]]. Failure of Zn alloys implant to adapt to these disparate biological microenvironments, particularly resulting in impaired osteogenesis in the bone matrix zone, significantly elevated the risk of non-union. Consistent evidences indicated that Zn2+ exhibited a biphasic dose-response effect, demonstrating an optimal range (0.1–0.5 mM) for enhanced osteogenesis, beyond which became toxic to cells [[16], [17], [18]]. Owning to the dynamic and active feature, the medullary cavity microenvironment enabled effective removal of degradation products, thereby confining local Zn2+ levels within a biocompatible range [19,20]. Conversely, the diffusion and transport-restricted prevalent in the bone matrix may lead to degradation pathways different from those in the medullary cavity. More importantly, crevice corrosion inevitably occurs at the Zn alloy-bone interface despite their close contact. This localized, self-accelerating process creates an acidic microenvironment, which critically shortens the implant lifespan by accelerating corrosion and potentially causing premature fracture [21,22]. Concurrently, the nearly closed and static microenvironment causes​ a substantial accumulation of Zn2+, which quickly exceeds​ the toxicity threshold and consequently hindered​ osseointegration [23,24]. Currently, research on crevice corrosion in biodegradable Zn alloys remains limited, particularly when the material is exposed to heterogeneous microenvironments simultaneously.

The implantation of Zn alloys triggers a foreign body response. This process begins with rapid serum protein adsorption on the implant surface, followed by the recruitment and activation of immune cells. Among these, macrophages play a pivotal role as both sensors of implant properties and central regulators directing the subsequent repair process [25,26]. In this context, the outcome of osteogenesis is largely determined by the functional state of macrophages, particularly their polarization into the pro-inflammatory or the pro-reparative phenotype. Previous studies have shown that appropriate Zn2+ concentrations can modulate intracellular signaling pathways (e.g., STAT6/NFκB) and promote the transition to the pro-healing phenotype [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. This promotes the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10) and osteoinductive cytokines (e.g., BMP-2), thereby promoting the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs [31,32]. However, in the diffusion and transport-restricted bone matrix, excessive Zn2+ accumulation can paradoxically reverse this beneficial effect, potentially triggering chronic inflammation and ultimately suppressing bone integration [33,34]. This biphasic immunomodulatory effect underscores the paramount importance of precisely controlling Zn2+ release kinetics at the implant-tissue interface and explains the inconsistent outcomes often observed with homogeneous material designs in heterogeneous microenvironments.

To investigate how the bone microenvironment precisely regulates material degradation and ultimately determines biological outcomes, this study employed two model alloys: Zn-0.8Mg and Zn-0.8Fe. This selection was grounded in prior evidence indicating that Zn-Mg alloys possess superior osteogenic capacity compared to pure Zn, whereas Zn-Fe alloys exhibit inferior performance. Furthermore, in vivo degradation kinetics differ significantly between them, with Zn-Fe alloys degrading faster than Zn-Mg alloys [1,8]. By systematically comparing the degradation behavior and osteogenic response of these two alloys within the distinct bone microenvironments (the medullary cavity and the bone matrix), this research elucidated the pivotal role of microenvironment-driven macro-galvanic coupling degradation in orchestrating bone regeneration. The results demonstrated that in the well-perfused and diffusion-dominated medullary cavity, the Zn alloy acted as the cathode. While the diffusion-restricted bone matrix zone functioned as the anode and initiated crevice corrosion, accelerating the degradation process. Concurrently, the dynamic microenvironment of medullary cavity facilitated the timely diffusion and metabolic clearance of Zn2+, maintaining local ion concentrations within a safe and osteogenic threshold range, thereby effectively supporting osteoblast activity and new bone deposition. In contrast, within the diffusion-limited bone matrix, Zn2+ tended to accumulate locally, rapidly reaching cytotoxic levels that triggered an inflammatory response and impeded bone regeneration.

Materials and methods

Compliance with ethical standards

All surgical procedures were conducted by the ARRIVE guidelines and received approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of the National Research Institute for Family Planning (NRIFH 21-2203-18).

Materials preparation

Zn alloy ingots were prepared by melting high-purity Zn (99.99%), Mg (99.99%), Fe (99.99%) with a vacuum induction melting furnace. The as-cast material was first hot-extruded at 200 °C into rods measuring 10 mm in diameter, using an extrusion ratio of 16:1. To enhance the mechanical strength of the implant and prevent deformation during cortical bone penetration, an equal-channel angular extrusion (ECAP) process is employed (Fig. S1A). The extruded rods are sectioned into 10 cm segments and subsequently subjected to eight extrusion cycles at 250 °C. Following ECAP, the material was cut into 1 mm thick discs for further experimentation. All discs were ground with 2000-grit silicon carbide paper and ultrasonically cleaned in anhydrous ethanol. For cell culture studies, samples were sterilized by immersion in 75% ethanol followed by ultraviolet irradiation. For animal implantation, Φ1 × 10 mm rod-shaped specimens were sterilized with ethylene oxide.

Microstructure characterization

The test samples were sequentially polished using 2000 and 5000-grit sandpaper followed by a final polishing step with 0.1 μm diamond paste to achieve a smooth surface. Microstructural and crystallographic characterizations were performed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM 7200F) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). EBSD analysis was employed to determine the grain size distribution, phase mapping, grain orientation, and texture information. Phase composition was identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD; Rigaku DMAX 2400, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation operated at 40 kV and 100 mA. XRD patterns were collected over a 2θ range from 10° to 90° with a scanning rate of 2°·min−1 and a step size of 0.02°. Surface potential mapping of pure Zn and Zn alloys, as well as the potential difference between the second phase and the Zn matrix, was conducted using the Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) mode of an atomic force microscope (AFM; Bruker Dimension ICON, Germany).

Electrochemical characterization

The electrochemical measurements were conducted using a conventional three-electrode system connected to an electrochemical workstation (Autolab, Metrohm, Switzerland). A platinum electrode served as the counter electrode and a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) was used as the reference electrode. All tests were performed at 37 °C in a simulated body fluid (SBF) with the following composition: NaCl 8.035 g L−1, NaHCO3 0.355 g L−1, KCl 0.25 g L−1, K2HPO4·3H2O 0.231 g L−1, MgCl2·6H2O 0.311 g L−1, HCl (36–38%) 39 mL L−1, CaCl2 0.292 g L−1, Na2SO4 0.072 g L−1, Tris 6.118 g L−1, adjusted to pH 7.4. The open-circuit potential (OCP) was monitored for 5400s for each sample prior to polarization measurements. Localized electrochemical activity was assessed using the scanning vibrating electrode technique (SVET) in Minimum Essential Medium α (MEM-α) for 24h. The SVET system was supplied by Applicable Electronics Inc. (USA).

In vitro simulated immersion experiment

To simulate different microenvironments, in vitro immersion tests were conducted in a physiological saline solution at pH 4.0. To create a confined microenvironment, a segment of the metal rod was embedded in polyurethane channels with an internal diameter of 1.2 mm, while an adjacent segment was left freely exposed to the bulk solution. After immersion, the corrosion products were chemically removed with a CrO3 solution, and the underlying surface morphology was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Cytocompatibility

The preparation standard for the extract was 1.25 mL/cm2 with Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (D-MEM), and cultured for 24h. Subsequently, the concentrations of metal elements in the solution were determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), the results were shown in Fig. S2.

The cytotoxicity of Zn alloys was evaluated using a mouse macrophage cell line (RAW264.7) in the presence of Zn alloy extracts. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 104 cells/mL and cultured for 24h to allow cell adhesion. Then, the culture medium was replaced with 100 μL of sample extracts at concentrations of 100%, 50%, 25%, and 12.5%. After another 24h culture, the extract was removed, replaced with fresh medium, and 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) was added to each well. The plates were further cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 1h, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Bio-RAD680 microplate reader. Each experimental condition was repeated in at least three independent replicates. To explore the underlying mechanisms of macrophage polarization in response to Zn2+, transcriptome analysis was performed based on RNA sequencing. RAW264.7 cells were treated with 50% Zn-0.8 Mg alloy extract for 24h, with untreated cells serving as the control. Three biological replicates were included per group. Total RNA was extracted and subjected to RNA sequencing to identify differentially expressed genes. Bioinformatics analysis was conducted using the online platform http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn.

Surgical procedure

All surgical procedures were performed under sterile conditions using New Zealand white rabbits (6-month-old, average weight: 2.8 kg). The rabbits were anesthetized via intramuscular injection of ketamine (35 mg kg−1) and 2% xylazine (5 mg kg−1). Following anesthesia, the right hind limb was shaved, and the knee joint was fixed in maximum flexion. A 1.2 mm diameter cylindrical hole was drilled perpendicularly through the femoral cortex into the medullary cavity. After irrigation with physiological saline, a single metal rod was implanted to ensure contact with both the medullary cavity and bone matrix, following which the wound was sutured. Only one implant was placed per rabbit. A minimum of six implants (n = 6) were tested for each alloy type, with a total of 36 male rabbits randomly assigned to experimental groups. Postoperatively, all animals were housed in a well-ventilated room with free access to water and standard feed.

Histological preparation and histomorphometric analysis

For decalcified sections, the femur containing the implant was subjected to decalcification in EDTA decalcifying solution (pH 7.2) for 6-8 weeks until reaching an elastic state. During this process, the specimens were regularly replaced with fresh decalcification solution every week. Following decalcification, the specimens underwent serial dehydration and paraffin embedding. Longitudinal sections of the femur tissue, each measuring 5 μm in thickness, were prepared for subsequent histological analysis.

Inflammatory responses, tissue integration, and bone regeneration at defect sites were evaluated by immunoluminescence, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome staining. For immunoluminescence analysis, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against iNOS (1:200 dilution, Proteintech; 22226-1-AP; Polyclonal), CD206 (1:200, Abcam; ab3163) a Zn2+-sensitive fluorescent probe (FluoZin™-3, AM, 1:200), followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. The sections were imaged using Pannoramic MIDI (DHISTECH).

Osteogenesis analysis

Non-decalcified sections were stained with methylene blue-acid fuchsin and imaged using the Pannoramic MIDI slide scanner (3DHISTECH). For quantitative analysis, a zone of interest (ROI) with a diameter of 1.5 mm, encompassing the implant, was defined. For elemental analysis, MMA-embedded sections were examined using an X-ray fluorescence spectrometer (XRF, Bruker M4 Tornado, Germany). Data acquisition and quantitative analysis were conducted using Bruker's proprietary software. Cross-sectional samples were prepared by sequential grinding with 7000-grit silicon carbide (SiC) paper, followed by final polishing using a 0.1 μm diamond suspension to obtain a smooth, artifact-free surface. Prior to SEM analysis, all polished specimens were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance conductivity. SEM imaging was performed in combination with EDS to assess the elemental composition of the samples.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Origin Pro 2016B (Data Analysis Software, Northampton, MA, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical significance between two independent groups was evaluated using an unpaired two-sided Student's t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post-hoc test was applied. Prior to applying these parametric tests, normality of the data distribution was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. If the data were not normally distributed, employed non-parametric test (Kruskal-Wallis test). Differences were considered statistically significant at ∗p< 0.05, ∗∗p< 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.005.

Results

Osteogenesis in different microenvironments in vivo

To assess the osteogenic performance of Zn-0.8Mg and Zn-0.8Fe alloys in different bone tissue microenvironments, a critical femoral bone defect model in rabbits was established. As shown in Fig. 1A, a defect was designed along the vertical direction of the femoral defect, spanning both the cortical bone and medullary cavity. Two Zn alloys, with medical-grade Ti as the control, were implanted into the defect zone, ensuring simultaneous contact with both the medullary cavity and bone matrix. In vivo results showed, no new bone formed within the medullary cavity around any implant at two weeks (Fig. S3A). However, distinct patterns were observed at the bone-implant interface. The pure Ti group exhibited new bone formation directly adjacent to the implant, indicating good osseointegration. In contrast, for both Zn alloy groups, new bone formed only in zones distant from the implant, with no bone tissue in immediate contact. After one month implantation, histological analysis of osteogenesis in the defect zone was performed using methylene blue-acid fuchsin staining. In the medullary cavity zone, tissue sections revealed that no significant new bone formation was observed in the Ti group, while varying degrees of new bone deposition were seen in the Zn-0.8Mg and Zn-0.8Fe groups (Fig. 1B). Notably, the Zn-0.8Mg group exhibited the most significant osteogenic activity, with the amount of new bone mass increased more than 10-fold compared to pure Ti (Fig. 1C). In the bone matrix zone, the Ti implant was well-integrated with bone, exhibiting intimate contact at the interface. To further evaluate the mineral deposition surrounding different implants, XRF was used to perform elemental surface distribution scanning of the implantation zones (Fig. 1E). In the medullary cavity, the Ti implant showed no detectable Ca or P signals. However, distinct enrichment of both elements was observed around the Zn-0.8Mg and Zn-0.8Fe implants, indicating active Ca and P deposition and bone matrix mineralization. This confirmed the clear osteoinductive capacity of the Zn alloys in the medullary cavity microenvironment. In contrast, XRF analysis of the bone matrix revealed signal-deficient zones around both Zn alloys, with average thicknesses of 50 μm for Zn-0.8Mg and approximately 100 μm for Zn-0.8Fe (Fig. 1D). These zones correspond to the unossified areas observed in hard tissue sections, further indicating that Zn alloys inhibited bone regeneration in the bone matrix. Extensive studies have demonstrated that the Zn2+ released from Zn alloys during degradation exhibits a dose-dependent effect on bone tissue regeneration [16,29]. A pronounced promotive activity has observed at lower concentrations, which transitions to a significant inhibitory effect once a critical threshold was exceeded. Thus, we hypothesized that in the bone matrix zone, the relatively fast degradation of the Zn alloys may lead to elevated local Zn2+ concentrations, which temporarily inhibited the maintenance of surrounding bone tissue or the deposition of new bone.

Degradation behavior in different microenvironments in vivo

To validate the hypothesis that the degradation rate of Zn alloys and the local Zn2+ concentration was microenvironment-dependent, this study conducted a systematic material analysis of rabbit femoral defect samples one month after implantation. Macroscopic observation of the retrieved implants revealed that the surface of the Zn-0.8Fe implant was tightly encased by a yellow tissue layer (Fig. 2A). After removing the residual tissue and degradation products, distinct differences in the degradation morphology were observed between the bone matrix and medullary cavity. Overall, implants in the bone matrix exhibited more severe degradation. Specifically, the Zn-0.8Fe implant in the bone matrix exhibited deep and large corrosion pits, exposing coarse grains and nearly losing the integrity of the material's matrix. While the Zn-0.8Mg implant, though less degraded, still exhibited rough surfaces and matrix fragmentation in this zone. To further assess the mineral deposition around the implants, SEM-EDS elemental mapping of the implant-bone interface cross-sections was performed. After two weeks implantation, no Zn was detected outside the implant site in either location, indicating no significant degradation of the Zn alloy had occurred in early stage (Fig. S3B). The one-month postoperative data indicated that in the medullary cavity, both Zn-0.8Mg and Zn-0.8Fe implants showed significant enrichment of Ca and P elements, indicating active mineral deposition and osteogenic activity (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, in the Zn-0.8Fe group, only trace amounts of Zn were detected diffusing into the medullary cavity. However, both Zn alloys exhibited Ca and P signal depletion zones in the bone matrix, suggesting inhibition of new bone mineralization. Furthermore, significant Zn diffusion signals were observed in the bone matrix, particularly in the Zn-0.8Fe group, where the surrounding tissue showed significantly elevated Zn levels. These results substantiated the initial hypothesis: within the diffusion-restricted bone matrix microenvironment, the degradation of Zn alloys was substantially accelerated. This rapid degradation leaded to a swift accumulation of Zn2+, the local concentration of which could readily surpass the cytotoxic threshold​ for surrounding osteoblasts, consequently impairing the bone regeneration process.

Degradation products analysis in different microenvironments in vivo

To further explore the intrinsic mechanisms underlying the differences of degradation behavior of Zn alloys in the bone matrix and medullary cavity microenvironments, we analyzed the degradation products of the implants in both zones. As shown in Fig. 3A, the Zn 2p peak in the bone matrix was significantly higher compared to the medullary cavity, indicating that material degradation was faster in the bone matrix, resulting in the accumulation of more degradation products. Additionally, the Cl 2p peak was present in both groups in the bone matrix. High-resolution spectra revealed that the binding energy of this peak was located at 198.5 eV (Fig. 3B), suggesting chloride may be involved in reactions within the bone matrix microenvironment [35]. Further analysis revealed that the O 1s main peak for all samples was positioned around 531-533 eV (Fig. 3C), which strongly pointed to the presence of hydroxyl groups (-OH) and adsorbed water on the sample surfaces [36]. This was a typical feature of degradation products in biological microenvironments and indicated the formation of a hydroxide or hydrated oxide layer on the Zn alloy surface. The C 1s spectra for all groups showed a characteristic peak at approximately 289 eV, attributed to carbonate or carboxylate groups. Further analysis of the O peak at ∼532 eV confirmed the presence of carbonate in the degradation products [37].Fig. 3Degradation products analysis in different microenvironments in vivo. For the Zn 2p3/2 spectrum, both Zn-0.8Mg and Zn-0.8Fe alloys in the bone matrix, as well as Zn-0.8Fe in the medullary cavity, showed two characteristic peaks, which were well fitted. The peak at ∼1022.0 eV corresponded to Zn(OH)2 [24]. The characteristic peak for ZnCO3 showed a noticeable shift toward lower binding energy (approximately 0.5-1.0 eV) in the bone matrix groups compared to the medullary cavity groups. This shift, along with the presence of the Cl 2p peak, suggested the formation of electronegative chlorine-containing metal compounds [38]. This was an important clue to crevice corrosion may occur in bone matrix with limited diffusion. Notably, the Zn 2p3/2 spectrum for Zn-0.8Mg in the medullary cavity showed no typical parabolic shape compared to the other groups, suggesting a significantly lower amount of degradation product. The detection of a Zn0 signal at ∼1020 eV implied a relatively thin degradation product layer that failed to fully shield the substrate [24]. These results confirmed that the local microenvironment played a crucial role in determining the nature and extent of degradation.

The effects of two simulated microenvironments in vitro on material degradation

Considering the complexity of the in vivo environment, a simplified in vitro model was used to further validate the impact of the microenvironment on the degradation behavior of the implants. As shown in Fig. 4A, this process employed a polyurethane block with a three-dimensional interconnected porous structure to simulate the bone matrix, whose interconnected porosity exceeding 80% closely matches the structural characteristics of cancellous bone [39,40]. By implanting the alloy into a compact 1.2 mm channel, the model successfully reproduced the extremely constrained gap geometry between the implant and hard tissue. After 14 days' immersion, both Zn-0.8Mg and Zn-0.8Fe alloys inside the polyurethane exhibited more significant corrosion than those in the solution microenvironment, indicating that restricted diffusion conditions and crevice corrosion significantly accelerated the material degradation (Fig. 4B). Further SEM analysis of the surface morphology after corrosion product removal revealed that Zn-0.8Mg underwent mild and uniform corrosion in the solution microenvironment, with its grain boundaries still clearly visible, with clear grain boundaries still visible (Fig. 4C). However, inside the polyurethane, the surface displayed uneven morphology with localized corrosion pits. The corrosion behavior of Zn-0.8Fe was more pronounced. In the solution, specimens exhibited incomplete surfaces, with large grain exposure, and the surrounding shallow matrix was corroded while the deeper layers remained relatively intact. In the polyurethane, the material exhibited a foam-like porous structure, with large grains detached due to corrosion, forming deep pits, indicating severe material failure. These results further confirmed that crevice corrosion was induced in the closed diffusion microenvironment simulating bone tissue, accelerating the localized corrosion process. In contrast, under the free diffusion conditions simulating medullary cavity, the alloy's degradation process was more uniform and slower.

Microstructure and electrochemical characteristics analysis

To investigate the differences in corrosion behavior between Zn-0.8Mg and Zn-0.8Fe alloys from the composition, their intrinsic electrochemical properties and microstructural characteristics were analyzed using a multi-scale characterization approach. The addition of Mg and Fe resulted in the formation of secondary phases, Mg2Zn11 and FeZn13, respectively (Fig. S1B). As shown in Fig. 5A, the secondary phase Mg2Zn11 in the Zn-0.8Mg alloy was uniformly dispersed within the Zn matrix, with a fine average grain size of 3 ± 1 μm. In contrast, the FeZn13 phase in the Zn-0.8Fe alloy was much larger (40 ± 15 μm) and more irregularly distributed. EBSD orientation imaging maps visually illustrated the crystallographic orientation distribution of the grains, further revealing differences in grain morphology and texture between the two alloys. Zn-0.8Mg exhibited a more uniform fine-grained structure, while Zn-0.8Fe showed localized grain coarsening and orientation clustering (Fig. S1C).Fig. 5Microstructure and electrochemical performance analysis of Zn-0.8Mg and Zn-0.8Fe alloys. The surface potential distribution was measured using SKPFM, as shown in Fig. 5B. In the Zn-0.8Mg alloy, the average potential of Mg2Zn11 secondary phase was lower than Zn matrix, indicating that it acted as the anodic phase and preferentially dissolved. In contrast, in the Zn-0.8Fe alloy, the potential of the FeZn13 phase was significantly higher than Zn matrix, making it acted as the cathodic phase, thereby accelerating the galvanic corrosion of the surrounding Zn matrix during the initial stages of corrosion.

To further evaluate the corrosion resistance in physiological microenvironments, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests were conducted in SBF solution (Fig. 5C). The Nyquist plot showed that the capacitive arc radius of Zn-0.8Mg alloy was significantly larger than Zn-0.8Fe alloy, indicating higher charge transfer resistance and superior corrosion resistance. Additionally, SVET was employed to monitor the surface current density distribution in situ, providing three-dimensional spatiotemporal corrosion evolution data (Fig. 5D). Initially, both alloys exhibited minor galvanic corrosion activity points on their surfaces, with no significant corrosion behavior differences. However, as immersion time increased, the corrosion current density on the Zn-0.8Mg alloy surface decreased significantly, likely due to the formation of a dense corrosion product layer that passivated and inhibited further corrosion. In the Zn-0.8Fe alloy, the FeZn13 phase, acting as a stable cathode, continuously promoted the anodic dissolution of the surrounding Zn matrix, resulting in a nearly 4-fold increase in local corrosion current density. The corrosion area expanded outward from its initial position, showing a clear trend of corrosion propagation. These results indicated that the electrochemical differences between the secondary phase and the matrix are key factors in controlling the degradation behavior of Zn alloys. The uniformly distributed anodic secondary phase in Zn-0.8Mg promoted a more uniform degradation pattern and the formation of a protective product layer, while the cathodic secondary phase in Zn-0.8Fe induced localized galvanic corrosion, accelerating material failure.

Excessive release of Zn2+ in bone matrix causes inflammation

The results above have verified that the bone matrix and medullary cavity microenvironments significantly influenced the degradation behavior of Zn alloys, ultimately leading to distinct degradation rates and degradation products. As biodegradable metal implants, Zn alloys trigger a foreign body response in the host, with macrophages playing a central role in this process. Typically, low concentrations of Zn2+ promote the polarization of anti-inflammatory macrophages, whereas excessive concentrations drive the overactivation of pro-inflammatory macrophages [16,41,42]. Therefore, to elucidate the impact of the implantation site microenvironment on the biological response to Zn alloys, this study performed immunofluorescence staining on tissue from the defect zone at day 14 post-surgery. The analysis focused on evaluating the local Zn2+ distribution and its regulatory effects on macrophage polarization.

As shown in Fig. 6A, the local Zn2+ concentration in the medullary cavity remained low. Discussion

This study systematically investigated the degradation behavior of Zn-0.8Mg and Zn-0.8Fe alloys in two distinct intraosseous microenvironments: the medullary cavity and the bone matrix. After implantation, the alloys were simultaneously exposed to both zones, which established a macro-galvanic coupling where the bone matrix acted as the anode and the medullary cavity as the cathode. Driven by this microenvironmental difference, the corrosion rate was significantly higher on the bone matrix zone than medullary cavity zone, leading to spatial differences in new bone formation. Consequently, osteogenesis was promoted in the medullary canal but inhibited within the bone matrix.

The implantation microenvironment-driven macro-galvanic coupling played a dominant role in the degradation of Zn alloys

Specifically, within the well-perfused medullary cavity microenvironment, the abundant vascular network and dense cell populations establish a highly dynamic and active setting. This zone not only promotes the efficient transport of reactants and products but also maintains the implant surface in a continuously renewing microenvironment. Owing to the ample blood supply, the oxygen partial pressure in the medullary cavity remains relatively stable, a key factor enabling it to function as an effective cathode (Fig. 8A), where oxygen reduction reactions (Equation (2)) predominantly took place [45,46]. The generated Zn2+ can readily diffuse and react with environmental OH− to form Zn(OH)2 (Equation (3)), which subsequently transformed into stable basic zinc carbonate [Zn2(OH)2CO3] (Equation (4)) [47,48]. This process helped slow down the local corrosion on the medullary canal side to some extent. The oxygen consumed during the reaction cannot be replenished efficiently in this zone, leading to a gradual decrease in oxygen content that fell below levels in the medullary canal. As a result, the implant acted as the anode of a galvanic couple in this zone. More importantly, the narrow gaps between the Zn alloy and the bone promoted crevice corrosion, which further accelerated material degradation and may even lead to premature fracture. In this process, Zn2+ generated from alloy dissolution accumulated within the confined space due to restricted diffusion. To maintain electroneutrality, anions such as Cl− migrated inward. The presence of Cl− inhibited the formation of Zn(OH)2, leading to an increase in the local concentration of free Zn2+ [49,50]. Furthermore, when the concentration reached a certain level, the Zn2+ underwent hydrolysis, resulting in local acidification (Equation (4)). At this point, oxygen reduction corrosion transformed into hydrogen evolution corrosion, further exacerbating the corrosion of Zn alloys [24]. Over time, the self-buffering capacity of the surrounding tissues gradually neutralized the local microenvironment, ultimately leading to the precipitation of stable Zn5Cl2 (OH)8 [51]. Furthermore, degradation is further modulated by micro-galvanic coupling originating from the material's inherent electrochemical properties, which are dictated by its composition. SKPFM analysis showed that in Zn-0.8Mg, the electrochemical potential of the Mg2Zn11 s phase was lower than Zn matrix, causing it to act as the anode and preferentially degrade in the initial stage of degradation (Fig. 5B). As degradation progressed, a dense degradation product layer formed on the surface of Zn-0.8Mg, generating a passivation effect that suppresses further degradation. Moreover, the gradual consumption of the Mg2Zn11 phase weakened the micro-galvanic effect, leading to uniform degradation of the Zn matrix and effectively controlling the degradation rate (Fig. 5D). While in Zn-0.8Fe, the high potential of the FeZn13 phase acted as a strong cathode, continuously accelerating the anodic dissolution of the surrounding Zn matrix, leading to a burst release of Zn2+. This explained why alloys with different compositions exhibited different degradation behaviors, even in the same microenvironment. However, when microenvironmental heterogeneity is pronounced, its influence outweighs that of material composition. In open, stable microenvironments, degradation is governed primarily by the micro-galvanic coupling, such as the protective effect conferred by Mg and Li [52,53] or the accelerated degradation induced by Fe and Cu [8]. In contrast, within complex and confined microenvironments like the bone matrix, local microenvironment dictates the degradation pathway. This microenvironment-driven macro-galvanic coupling is especially evident at tissue interfaces, such as the cortex-medullary junction or the soft-tissue-bone contact surface, where degradation rates are significantly elevated compared to those within homogeneous tissue zones [54,55]. Therefore, in the confined microenvironment of bone matrix, even Zn-0.8Mg showed significant accumulation of degradation products (Fig. 3A).

Therefore, it can be predicted that this microenvironment-driven macro-galvanic coupling degradation mode extends beyond Zn-Mg and Zn-Fe alloys to Zn alloys in general, and potentially to Mg alloys. This is because the core driving force fundamentally stems not from the material, but from the physiological heterogeneity of bone tissue. The reaction direction is governed primarily by the oxygen concentration gradient and diffusion conditions. The well-vascularized medullary cavity maintains a stable oxygen partial pressure, naturally serving as a cathodic zone. In contrast, the dense bone matrix, with its limited oxygen supply, tends to become anodic. Moreover, compared to the confined space within the bone matrix, the open and dynamic medullary cavity offers greater potential for metabolic exchange and molecular diffusion. Therefore, this microenvironments distinction leads to comparable outcomes for biodegradable metals. While our in vivo and in vitro data provide a fundamental understanding of the degradation tendencies of biodegradable metals, it is crucial to acknowledge the significant roles of other external factors, such as mechanical stimulation. As the primary load-bearing tissue, bone can experience cracking or detachment of the corrosion product layer on the implant surface (when it reaches a certain thickness) under mechanical stress. Crack formation leads to the loss of local passivation protection, triggering deep pitting corrosion, severely compromising the structural integrity of implants, and even causing premature fracture. The confined microenvironments of the bone matrix zone, coupled with mechanical loading, further accelerates the material failure process [24].

Moreover, in vitro simulation experiments further confirmed the decisive influence of microenvironment variations on the degradation behavior of Zn alloys. Results have shown that both Zn-0.8Mg and Zn-0.8Fe alloys exhibited pronounced propensity for localized corrosion when evaluated in a polyurethane model designed to mimic diffusion-limited conditions (Fig. 4). The formation of abundant oxygen-containing corrosion products and deep pits on the alloy surfaces represented a morphological characteristic, which aligned closely with the degradation mode observed within the bone matrix in vivo (Fig. 2). These results collectively indicated that the restricted mass transport conditions prevalent on the bone matrix served to exacerbate the localized degradation process, which was primarily governed by macro-galvanic coupling and crevice corrosion, ultimately resulting in a significantly elevated degradation rate in this zone relative to medullary cavity. Although the polyurethane model has limitations in simulating the self-buffering capacity of real bone tissue and the complex Havers system, it demonstrated that mass transfer limitation can induce autocatalytic crevice corrosion, accelerating the degradation of Zn alloys.

The decisive role of implantation microenvironments in modulating osteogenesis and inflammatory responses to Zn alloys

Zn2+ exhibited a typical biphasic dose-dependent biological effect. Studies have shown that at low concentrations (10.91–27.15 μM) Zn2+ promoted osteoblast differentiation by activating PI3K/AKT and Wnt signaling pathways, while high concentration (128.58 μM) significantly inhibited ALP activity [56,57]. At concentrations below 90 μM, it further polarizes macrophages toward the anti-inflammatory phenotype by inhibiting the HIF-1α/NF-κB and JAK-STAT pathways, establishing an immune microenvironment conducive to bone regeneration [[58], [59], [60]]. Zn alloys exhibit controlled release behavior in the medullary cavity microenvironment, with their ion concentration consistently maintained within the osteogenic threshold range (especially Zn-0.8Mg), effectively promoting new bone formation. Conversely, in the bone matrix, rapid degradation and restricted diffusion leading to excessively high local ion concentrations, which can trigger inflammation and inhibit osteogenic signaling pathways. In addition, the local pH acidification caused by the hydrolysis of Zn2+ and inflammation can also affect the activity of alkaline phosphatase and matrix mineralization, ultimately leading to the inhibition of osteogenic formation. Proteomics analysis confirmed the above arguments, the high concentrations of Zn2+ upregulated reactive oxygen species (ROS)-related pathways while suppressing the PPAR signaling pathway, resulting in sustained inflammatory responses (Fig. 7D). Simultaneously, immunofluorescence staining results confirmed that, in the bone matrix microenvironment, both Zn-0.8Mg and Zn-0.8Fe implants accumulated large numbers of pro-inflammatory macrophages (Fig. 6B), accompanied by fibrotic tissue formation, ultimately hindering the bone integration process. These results further emphasized that the implantation microenvironment will determine the ultimate direction of the material's biological function, and also illustrate the critical importance of precisely modulating Zn2+ release within bone tissue for effective repair.

Future directions and limitations

Future directions

This study highlighted an important message, microenvironmental conditions at the implantation site can dominate over alloy composition in determining degradation behavior and biological response, which has clear implications for the design of future Zn-based implants. The following presents a perspective on future Zn alloy design aimed at advancing bone repair within complex microenvironments:1.Structural gradient design: Designing high-porosity scaffolds in the medullary cavity can further enhance the blood perfusion and mass transfer characteristics of this zone. Employing a dense structure at the bone-implant interface reduces the material's exposed surface area, thereby lowering the risk of localized ion release due to crevice corrosion.2.Surface functionalization gradient: The surface properties of different parts of the implant are changed by adding coatings through physical or chemical means to match the local microenvironment.3.Composition gradient design: Balance the electrochemical response by employing tailored alloy compositions in different zones. For instance, incorporate a higher proportion of corrosion-resistant elements (such as those that form stable passivation layers) in the anodic bone matrix portion, while utilizing components that enhance bone activity in the medullary cavity.

Limitations

Despite this study provided insights into the mechanisms of microenvironment-dependent material degradation and osteogenic heterogeneity, several limitations remain to be addressed in future research:1.Temporal constraints of in vivo observations: The current in vivo evaluation was restricted to a relatively short-term period of one month. While this result captured the critical initial phases of Zn2+ release and early bone formation, it does not fully account for the long-term degradation kinetics of Zn-Mg and Zn-Fe alloys or the secondary bone remodeling process. Future studies with extended observation periods (e.g., 6-12 months) are essential to track the ultimate fate of the implants and ensure the stability of the regenerated bone tissue.2.Translational gap of the rabbit model: Despite being one of the few small animal models possessing a Haversian canal system similar to humans, rabbits differ in key physiological parameters, such as bone turnover rate, mineral density, and perfusion rate. These differences present challenges for translating research findings. Consequently, the results reported in this study must therefore be validated in larger animal models or direct clinical studies.3.Polyurethane models possess inherent limitations in simulating the complex biological microenvironment: While the polyurethane in vitro model employed is effective for isolating parameters related to mass transfer, it cannot capture the complexity of the native bone microenvironment. Future studies should incorporate more physiologically relevant conditions, including fluid shear stress, cyclic loading, the dynamic interplay of proteins and multi-lineage cells. This integration is essential to better capture the synergistic effect of physiological flow and biological factors on biodegradable metal interface degradation.4.In-situ or real-time monitoring of Zn2+ in vivo: This study investigated crevice corrosion induced by different in vivo microenvironment led to the massive release of Zn2+ in the bone matrix, which inhibited bone regeneration. However, the characterization of Zn2+ concentration remains qualitative. It is necessary to develop advanced real-time, in-situ monitoring technologies to quantify local ion concentrations in vivo, which will provide a more detailed understanding of biodegradable metal implants-microenvironment interactions.

Conclusion

This study revealed that the degradation behavior and osteogenic effects of Zn alloys were profoundly influenced by microenvironmental conditions, which was more pronounced than that caused by the material composition. In the medullary cavity, both Zn alloys exhibited osteogenic activity. Zn-0.8Mg alloy demonstrated excellent biocompatibility and osteogenic activity, due to the anodic second phase and uniform degradation characteristics. However, the Zn-0.8Fe alloy exhibited relatively weaker osteogenic activity due to localized corrosion induced by the cathodic second phase. In the bone matrix, even Zn-0.8Mg can rapidly degrade with the macro-galvanic coupling and crevice corrosion induced by the microenvironment. This will release a large amount of Zn2+, which induced a strong inflammatory response, ultimately inhibiting osteogenesis. These findings emphasized that the design of future biodegradable Zn alloys need to take into account the microenvironmental characteristics of the implantation site, and achieved a precise match between degradation rate and bone regeneration process through means, such as component optimization, structural design and surface functionalization.
