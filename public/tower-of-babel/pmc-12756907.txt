Probable scrub typhus-like infection in Colombia: seroconversion and serological reactivity to Orientia spp. among patients with acute undifferentiated febrile illness in Villeta municipality
Authors: Carlos Ramiro Silva-Ramos, Nicole L Mendell, Patricia A Crocquet-Valdes, Peter C Melby, Patricia V Aguilar, Miguel M Cabada, Marylin Hidalgo, David H Walker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Scrub typhus, caused by Orientia spp., is a mite-borne disease historically restricted to the Asia-Pacific region. Autochthonous cases have been confirmed in southern Chile, while serological evidence has been reported in Peru, Honduras and Colombia, suggesting wider distribution in Latin America. Identifying recent exposure among febrile patients is essential to evaluate its role in acute undifferentiated febrile illness (AUFI) etiology.Aim:To assess the possible implication of Orientia infection as a cause of AUFI in Villeta, Colombia.Methods:Between September and December 2021, patients with AUFI were recruited. Whole-blood samples were tested for Orientia DNA by real-time PCR targeting the 16S rRNA gene. Acute and convalescent serum samples were analyzed for IgG antibodies by IFA and ELISA, with paired samples titration to assess seroconversion. Reactive sera were further confirmed by Western blot.Results:Forty-one acute whole-blood samples were tested by qPCR; no Orientia DNA was detected. Serology identified antibodies in 27 % (11/41) of patients: six positive by ELISA, two by IFA, and three by both methods. Based on paired sera, eight cases (20 %) were classified as recent infections, including cases of seroconversion, and three (7 %) as previous exposures. Western blot confirmed specific reactivity against Orientia antigens in all positive samples.Conclusions:This study provides serological evidence of Orientia circulation among febrile patients in Villeta, Colombia, including clear cases of seroconversion. Western blot validated reactivity against immunodominant proteins. Findings suggest a local scrub typhus–like illness and highlight the need for pathogen isolation and genetic characterization to clarify its contribution to AUFI.Keywords: Orientia, Scrub typhus, Seroconversion, Febrile illness, Colombia, Latin America

Introduction

Orientia (order Rickettsiales, family Rickettsiaceae) is a bacterial genus composed of obligately intracellular, small, Gram-negative, pleomorphic rods [1]. The principal vector and reservoir of Orientia spp. are trombiculid mites, commonly known as chiggers, and transmission to vertebrate hosts occurs exclusively through the larval stage, which is the only developmental stage that attaches and feeds on host skin [2]. Orientia tsutsugamushi is the prototype species and a biosafety level (BSL)-3 pathogen, which comprises several antigenically different strains with high variability of its major outer membrane proteins. The Karp, Kato, Gilliam, Boryong, Ikeda, TA716 and Kawasaki strains cause most human disease [3,4]. Humans and animals are considered accidental dead-end hosts in the natural cycle, which primarily involves many small mammalian orders, especially murids, as key hosts for chiggers [5].

Orientia species cause scrub typhus, an emerging mite-borne infectious disease and probably the most neglected global etiology of acute undifferentiated febrile illness (AUFI) [1,6]. Scrub typhus is associated with rash and the formation of an eschar at the site of the chigger bite. Although the disease typically responds to appropriate antibiotic treatment, some cases progress to severe illness with multiple organ involvement and life-threatening outcomes due to delayed antibiotic therapy [7]. While Orientia spp. can infect a wide range of cell types, endothelial cells are the primary target in severe infections, and dermal dendritic cells and macrophages play a key role during infection at the chigger feeding site [8,9]. Consequently, systemic vascular damage, caused by both direct pathogen-mediated damage and the host immune response, comprises the predominant pathogenic event, leading to respiratory, neurological, renal and gastrointestinal manifestations [7,10, 11]. Severe complications can include a wide spectrum of neurological presentations, including meningoencephalitis, cranial polyneuropathy, and diaphragmatic myoclonus, among others, particularly in patients from endemic areas [12–17].

Confirmatory diagnosis of scrub typhus is primarily based on serologic assays such as indirect immunofluorescence assays (IFA) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) [12]. A four-fold rise or seroconversion of IgG antibody titers between acute and convalescent samples is still the gold-standard for confirmation of scrub typhus [18]. Although molecular methods, such as PCR, are fast and more specific, bacterial DNA cannot always be amplified from whole blood samples due to the transient and low-level bacteremia. Therefore, it is preferable to use an eschar biopsy or a swab from the uncrusted lesion, as these specimens have a higher likelihood of yielding detectable DNA [19]. Isolation of Orientia by culture can also be performed, but is more difficult as it is restricted to BSL-3 reference laboratories [18].

Scrub typhus was thought to be limited to the Asia-Pacific region, within a geographic area known as “the tsutsugamushi triangle” established as the endemic region for this disease [20]. However, since 2006, following the discovery of an autochthonous case of scrub typhus in United Arab Emirates caused by a novel species, Orientia chuto [21], the detection of previous exposure to Orientia spp. in several African countries [22] and detection of O. chuto in chiggers [23], has confirmed that scrub typhus is no longer restricted to the Asia-Pacific region [20].

In Latin America, a new endemic region for scrub typhus has been identified in the continental subantarctic region of southern Chile [24, 25]. In this region, the first case was reported in 2011 on Chilöe Island [26], and during the following years, several cases were diagnosed among inhabitants from the island, confirming its endemicity [27]. The disease in this area is believed to be associated with a novel Orientia species, provisionally named Candidatus Orientia chiloensis [28]. Since then, additional studies have been conducted in two other regions of Latin America. A retrospective study among febrile patients from Iquitos, Loreto Department, in the Peruvian Amazon, provided serological evidence of exposure against Orientia spp., including one documented case of seroconversion [29]. Another study involving military personnel who had traveled to Honduras, in Central America, demonstrated seroconversion to Orientia spp. [30]. These findings support the hypothesis that the distribution of Orientia is more widespread in Latin America than previously thought, and that scrub typhus could be an underdiagnosed and neglected etiology of AUFI in the region [31].

In Colombia, no attempts to investigate the circulation of Orientia had been undertaken until 2023, when a study reported evidence of antibodies against Orientia spp. among rural inhabitants of four municipalities in Cauca department [32]. One year later, evidence of exposure was also documented by serology among indigenous population from the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta [33]. These findings suggest that at least one Orientia species is actively circulating in the country; however, it is unknown whether Orientia is one of the etiologies of AUFI in the country. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to assess the possible implication of Orientia infection as a cause of AUFI in Villeta municipality, Colombia.

Materials and methods

Study area

Villeta (5°00′46″ N, 74°28′23″ W), is a municipality located at an elevation of 850 m above sea level (MASL), in the Gualivá Province, Cundinamarca Department, 84 km from Bogotá D.C. It covers 140 km2 distributed in 22 villages. The annual mean temperature is 26 °C, with a relative humidity ranging from 80 % to 97 %. Eco-tourism and agriculture are the main economic activities, with sugarcane cultivation and “panela” production being the predominant economic activities in rural areas (www.villeta-cundinamarca.gov.co). According to the 2018 national population and housing census conducted by the “Departamento Administrativo Nacional de Estadística - (DANE)”, Villeta has a total population of 25,957 inhabitants, of which 17,751 reside in the urban area, 743 in suburban settlements, and 7463 in rural areas (https://www.dane.gov.co).

Samples

Samples were collected as part of a larger multisite and multi-country study of AUFI conducted in six regions across four different countries, all of which followed a common protocol framework and data collection system [34]. Briefly, between September and December 2021, active surveillance was carried out in “Salazar de Villeta” Hospital, located in the municipality of Villeta, Colombia. Patients presenting to the emergency department with febrile illness without an apparent cause of infection were enrolled. For each recruited patient, acute whole-blood, acute serum, and convalescent serum samples were obtained [35]. Samples of patients who consented to the use of their left-over blood and serum specimens in future studies beyond the scope of the parent project were processed in the present study. A total of 41 patients provided such consent, representing the available sample for this exploratory study. No formal sample size calculation was conducted, as the study relied on the available cohort of recruited febrile patients. All samples were stored at the Laboratorio de Bacteriología Especial of the Pontificia Universidad Javeriana in Bogotá D.C., Colombia, and aliquots were subsequently shipped to the University Texas Medical Branch in Galveston, Texas, United States, for further analyses.

The study protocol, informed consent and assent procedures were approved by the ethics committee of the Pontificia Universidad Javeriana. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients, and from parents or legal guardians in the case of minor-age patients. All data were deidentified by assigning a unique numeric code to each patient. All procedures adhered to the resolution 8430 from 1993 of the Ministry of Health of Colombia and the declaration of Helsinki for ethical principles in human research.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from 200 μL of acute whole-blood sample using the DNeasy® Blood & Tissue Kit (250) (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After each extraction procedure, to assess DNA integrity and rule out the presence of inhibitors, the extracted DNA was quantified spectrophotometrically using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE, United States), and its quality was further evaluated through conventional PCR targeting a 289-base pair fragment of the β-actin encoding gene (ACTB) using the primers XAHR 17 (CGGAACCGCTCATTGCC) and XAHR 20 (ACCCACACTGTGCCCATCTA) following a previously reported protocol [36]. Each amplification included a no template control (molecular grade water) and a positive control (human DNA). PCR products were visualized by electrophoresis on an 1 % agarose gel stained with SYBR™ Safe DNA Gel Stain (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA).

Molecular detection of Orientia spp. infection

The presence of Orientia spp. DNA was determined by qualitative genus-specific real-time PCR (qPCR) targeting a 94-bp fragment of the 16s rRNA gene, with a limit of detection of 10 copies per reaction, using the primers O16s-563F (GCCTGATCCAGCAATG) and O16s-656R (GGCTTTTTCTGTAGGTAC), along with the TaqMan probe O16s-636 (FAM-TCATTATCATCCCTACTAAAAGAGCTTTACA-BHQ1) [19]. Reactions were performed following a previously described protocol [19], using 1 μL of DNA extracted from 200 μL whole-blood samples and amplified using the Platinum™ Quantitative PCR SuperMix-UDG mastermix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Samples with a cycle threshold (Ct) value ≤ 45 were considered positive. Each reaction included a positive control (O. tsutsugamushi Karp strain DNA) and a no template control (molecular grade water). This analysis was exploratory since only whole-blood samples were available for testing through qPCR. Therefore, the molecular screening was considered preliminary, and negative findings do not rule out the possibility of Orientia infection.

Serological detection of Orientia spp. infection

Antibodies against Orientia spp. were screened initially on each patient’s convalescent-phase serum sample, and if reactive, the corresponding acute-phase serum sample was also tested. Screening was performed by two different methods: indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

IFA was performed using antigen-coated slides fixed with whole-cell antigens of O. tsutsugamushi Karp strain. Samples were first screened at a 1:128 dilution. Alexa Fluor® 488 goat anti-human IgG (H + L) (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) was used as secondary antibody at a 1:2000 dilution. Slides were examined at 100x magnification using an Olympus BX61 Fully Motorized Fluorescence Microscope (Evident Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Samples reactive at 1:128 were further titrated, along with their paired acute-phase serum sample, by two-fold serial dilutions until disappearance of specific fluorescence compatible with bacterial morphology, which was established as the sample endpoint titer. A case was considered positive for recent Orientia infection if antibodies were absent in the acute-phase but present in the convalescent-phase sample, or a four-fold antibody titter increase was observed between acute and convalescent sera. Samples that did not fulfill these criteria but showed the presence of antibodies were considered as having evidence of previous exposure to Orientia.

ELISA was performed using the commercial Scrub Typhus Detect™ IgG ELISA kit (InBios International, Inc., Seattle, WA, United States), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, serum samples were tested at 1:100 dilution in an antigen-coated 96-well plate containing a mixture of recombinant P56-kDa type-specific antigens from O. tsutsugamushi Kato, Karp, Gilliam and TA716 strains. The plate was read at 450 nm using a Versamax microplate reader with Softmax Pro V.6.5 software (Molecular Devices, LLC., San Jose, CA, United States). A sample was considered reactive when the optical density (OD) exceeded the cut-off value of 0.370. Reactive convalescent-phase serum was paired with its corresponding acute-phase sample. Recent Orientia infection was defined as a change from non-reactive to reactive between acute and convalescent sera. Samples that did not fulfill these criteria but showed an OD value above 0.370 were classified as evidence of previous exposure to Orientia. Serum negative and positive controls provided by the kit were used in both IFA and ELISA screening methods.

Confirmation of Orientia spp. infection

To further confirm the specificity of reactive samples, a Western blot assay was performed using whole Vero cell protein extracts and whole-cell antigens of O. tsutsugamushi Karp strain. In brief, 10 μg of antigen per well was loaded into a NuPAGE™ Bis-Tris Mini Protein Gels, 4–12 %, 1.0 mm × 12 wells (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Electrophoresis and transfer were carried out using an XCell SureLock™ Mini-Cell system (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, United States) and a Trans-Blot SD Semi-Dry Transfer Cell module (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, United States), respectively. Then, nitrocellulose membranes were blocked with 5 % skim milk to prevent nonspecific binding. Serum samples at a 1:100 dilution were used as primary antibodies, incubated separately overnight. Donkey anti-human IgG antibodies conjugated with alkaline phosphatase were used as the secondary antibodies at a 1:5000 dilution. Bound antibodies against O. tsutsugamushi Karp strain antigens were detected with BCIP/NBT, stable alkaline phosphatase substrate (MP Biomedicals, LLC, Irvine, CA, USA) until bands became visible. The assay was validated with a positive serum sample identified in a previous study [32] and a negative serum sample (non-reactive by IFA or ELISA).

Results

Molecular detection of Orientia spp. infection

A total of 56 febrile patients were recruited during the parent project, of whom, 41 provided consent for the use of their samples in future studies. DNA was successfully extracted from all 41 whole-blood samples collected during the acute phase of the disease. Integrity and quality of the extracted DNA were confirmed by the amplification of the expected ACTB fragment in all samples, indicating suitability for down-stream molecular analyses. All 41 samples were subsequently screened by genus-specific qPCR targeting a fragment of the 16s rRNA gene; however, no amplification was observed in any of the samples. The positive control included in each run was successfully amplified and no amplification was seen in negative controls.

Serological detection of Orientia spp. infection

Antibodies against Orientia spp. were detected in 27 % (11/41) of the samples processed. Among these, six samples were positive only by ELISA, two were positive only by IFA, and three were positive by both methods (Fig. 1). ELISA OD values for positive convalescent samples screened ranged from 0.371 to 2.001, while IFA endpoint titers ranged between 1:256 and 1:1024.

Distribution of serological reactivity against Orientia spp. by IFA and ELISA. A) IgG antibody titers measured by IFA with dashed lines indicating the cut-off value obtained for each sample. B) OD values measured by ELISA, with the cut-off value indicated by a dashed line and values obtained for each sample displayed above each bar. Acute samples are shown in light blue and convalescent samples in dark blue.

Based on paired acute and convalescent samples, eight of the positive samples were classified as recent exposure to Orientia spp., while the remaining three were considered as evidence of previous exposure. Recent exposure was identified in five samples by showing a change from non-reactive to reactive status by ELISA, in one sample through four-fold seroconversion by IFA, and in two samples by fulfilling both criteria. The distribution of ELISA OD values and IFA end-point titers for each positive sample is shown in Fig. 1.

The eleven seropositive patients ranged in age from 12 to 72 years, with the majority being young adults (36 % [4/11]). Sex distribution was nearly balanced, with six males (55 % [6/11]) and five females (45 % [5/11]). All patients originated from urban areas of Villeta (100 % [11/11]), and most were engaged in small-scale commerce (5/11 [45 %]). Fever duration ranged from 1 to 3 days, typically with gradual onset (55 % [6/11]) and intermittent (73 % [8/11]) pattern often associated with total activity limitation (45 % [5/11]). Common accompanying clinical manifestations included malaise (82 % [9/11]), chills (73 % [8/11]), headaches, sweats and fatigue (64 % [7/11] each). Most patients recovered without complications (82 % [9/11]), although two patients (18 % [2/11]) were transferred to higher-level hospitals for clinical management. A summary of the most relevant demographic, epidemiological, and clinical data obtained during the enrollment of febrile patients who tested seropositive for Orientia spp. is presented in Table 1. No statistically significant differences were observed between seropositive and seronegative patients considering age, sex, origin, occupation or clinical presentation.

Discussion

Scrub typhus is probably one of the most neglected and underrated causes of AUFI around the world [1,6]. For decades, it was thought to be geographically restricted to the Asia-Pacific region, where it represents a major public health problem [20]. This assumption, coupled with the lack of interest in Orientia as a cause of AUFI in other parts of the world, led to its systematic underdiagnosis outside the endemic region in southeast Asia. The report of the first autochthonous cases in the United Arab Emirates [21] challenged this long-standing paradigm of geographic restriction and encouraged further research to evaluate the circulation of scrub typhus in other regions. To date, the most important findings have been obtained in Chile, where several studies demonstrated the presence of a novel endemic region in a subantarctic area [24–27], contrasting with the classical tropical distribution described in the Asia-Pacific region [6]. Prompted by these pioneering findings, a subsequent study identified one confirmed seroconversion case in the Peruvian Amazon [29], strengthening the hypothesis that scrub typhus may be more widely distributed across the region [31]. In this context, the present study provides novel evidence of recent exposure through seroconversion, as well as past exposure, against Orientia spp. in febrile patients from Villeta municipality, Colombia, suggesting that scrub typhus may also be circulating in the country.

In Colombia, knowledge about Orientia and scrub typhus is limited. To date, only two seroepidemiological studies have evaluated exposure to Orientia in the human population, reporting 13.8 % (67/486) seror-eactivity among rural inhabitants of Cauca department [32] and 4.3 % (23/539) among indigenous populations from the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta [33]. However, both studies were conducted among an apparently healthy population, and none of them assessed recent exposure, limiting possible inferences connecting Orientia infection and local cases of AUFI. In contrast, in the present study eight individuals were classified as having a recent exposure to Orientia, of which three exhibited clear seroconversion by IFA. Considering that these findings were observed in febrile patients, the results strongly suggest that Orientia could be associated with the clinical presentation of AUFI in Colombia. Seroconversion cases are highly relevant since it indicates ongoing Orientia transmission in the region and mirrors the early findings reported in the Peruvian Amazon [29]. These data together rein-force the notion that Latin America harbors underrecognized endemic hotspots of scrub typhus-like illness extending beyond the southern Chilean region.

To confirm the serological reactivity, a Western blot assay demonstrated compatible reactivity to some of the major immunodominant proteins of Orientia in all patient serum samples tested, mainly the 47- and the 56-kDa. Serological studies previously found that O. tsutsugamushi has five immunodominant proteins detectable by Western blot, which include the 22-, 47-, 56-, 58-, and 110-kDa proteins [37–39]. While the 56-kDa protein has been identified as the major immunodominant surface antigen of O. tsutsugamushi, it exhibits high genetic diversity among strains due to its four hypervariable regions [40]. On the other hand, the 47-kDa protein, an outer membrane serine protease, is comparatively more conserved among O. tsutsugamushi strains [41], together with the 22- and 110-kDa proteins, which have also been identified to elicit consistent antibody recognition with also lower diversity, but less antigenic than the 56- and the 47-kDa [42,43]. The study of these immunodominant proteins has been largely restricted to O. tsutsugamushi, and although homologous proteins may exist in other Orientia species, their degree of diversity remains uncertain. It is highly plausible that the lineage circulating in Colombia corresponds to a novel and undescribed taxon with an uncharacterized antigenic profile; nevertheless, the detection of serological reactivity against both variable and conserved proteins strongly suggests the presence of shared epitopes across Orientia species and provides antigen-specific evidence of local human exposure among febrile patients in Colombia.

Molecular screening by qPCR was attempted; however, no evidence of Orientia DNA was detected. Previous studies have shown that PCR assays on whole-blood samples display variable sensitivity, with significantly higher performance when using buffy coat or eschar samples [44–47]. Orientia is an obligately intracellular bacterium with a primary tropism for endothelial cells, that can also infect mononuclear and dendritic cells at the inoculation site [47], which explains why eschar or skin biopsy samples provide greater diagnostic yield than whole-blood as observed with other intracellular pathogens with similar tropism [48]. Given that the main project was not specifically designed to investigate Orientia, eschars were not systematically sought, detected, or documented, and no specimens were collected from recruited patients. DNA extraction was originally performed from whole-blood samples, and consequently, buffy coat preparations were not analyzed. High bacterial loads of Orientia in the bloodstream are usually associated with a severe disease [49]; however, the window for DNA detection typically occurs early in the illness, around days 3 and 4, before the development of an effective antibody response that begins to control the infection. In the present study, qPCR screening was performed only as an exploratory approach, and future investigations should systematically address eschar-associated AUFI cases to improve the chances of Orientia DNA detection.

Although the results found during the present study provide important novel evidence suggesting recent exposure to Orientia spp. among febrile patients in Colombia, it has several limitations that must be acknowledged such as the relatively small sample size, that eschars were not systematically sampled, and molecular evidence was not achieved. Results obtained in the present study cannot definitively confirm the presence of scrub typhus in Colombia; nevertheless, the identification of seroconversion in febrile patients provides strong evidence of recent infection and raises the possibility that the disease or a closely related illness may be locally circulating. Therefore, clinicians should be aware that unexplained AUFIs could be associated with Orientia spp. infection, even in the absence of classical features such as the presence of an eschar or rash development. In Chile, where more extensive evidence has been gathered from autochthonous cases [29,50], zoonotic hosts, and vectors [51,52], including the molecular characterization of a novel species [28], the isolation of Candidatus Orientia chiloensis is still pending. This underscores that even in regions with stronger data, definitive confirmation requires pathogen isolation and characterization. In this sense, our study should encourage further research aimed at isolating and genetically characterize the Orientia species potentially circulating in Colombia, as well as expanding ecological and epidemiological investigations. Only through these efforts it will be possible to conclude whether scrub typhus is indeed present in the country and to clarify its potential contribution to the local etiology of AUFI. Although preliminary, findings retrieved in the present study highlight the importance of maintaining clinical awareness of Orientia spp. infection as a possible cause of unexplained febrile illness in the region and encourage healthcare professionals to consider this pathogen within the differential diagnosis of AUFI when other common etiologies are excluded.

Conclusion

The present study provides strong serological evidence of the circulation of Orientia spp. infection among febrile patients from Villeta municipality, Colombia. More than a quarter of the samples showed serological reactivity and almost a fifth met criteria for recent exposure, including clear cases of seroconversion. Our Western blot analyses further confirmed reactivity against several Orientia immunodominant proteins, providing additional antigen-specific evidence of infection. These results strongly suggest that a scrub typhus–like illness may be locally circulating. Future studies should aim at pathogen isolation and genetic characterization to determine the taxonomic identity of the circulating lineage and clarify its contribution to the etiology of AUFI in Colombia.
