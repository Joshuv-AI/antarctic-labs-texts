Abstract
. Generative AI systems are increasingly adopted by patients seeking everyday health guidance, yet their reliability and clinical appropriateness remain uncertain. Taking Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) as a representative chronic condition, this paper presents a two-part mixed-methods study that examines how patients and physicians in China evaluate the quality and usability of AI-generated health information. Study 1 analyzes 784 authentic patient questions to identify seven core categories of informational needs and five evaluation dimensions— Accuracy, Safety, Clarity, Integrity , and Action Orientation . Study 2 involves 7 endocrinologists who assess responses from four mainstream AI models across these dimensions. Quantitative and qualitative findings reveal consistent strengths in factual and lifestyle guidance but significant weaknesses in medication interpretation, contextual reasoning, and empathy. Patients view AI as an accessible “pre-visit educator,” whereas clinicians highlight its lack of clinical safety and personalization. Together, the findings inform design implications for interactive health systems, advocating for multi-model orchestration, risk-aware fallback mechanisms, and emotionally attuned communication to ensure trustworthy AI assistance in chronic disease care.



1.
INTRODUCTION

As generative AI systems increasingly permeate consumer health contexts, their potential role in chronic disease management has garnered substantial interest from the HCI and medical informatics communities. Large language models (LLMs) have shown strong capabilities in encoding biomedical knowledge
(
Samimi et al. 2025
;
Hernandez et al. 2023
)
, yet questions remain about their real-world effectiveness in delivering health information that is not only factually correct, but also contextually safe, actionable, and emotionally resonant
(
Akrimi et al. 2025
)
.

In this work, we take Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) as a representative chronic condition to examine how generative AI systems interact with real-world patient needs and clinician expectations. T2DM affects over 500 million people globally and demands continuous self-management involving medication, diet, exercise, and psychosocial care
(
Gong et al. 2020
)
. In countries like China—where patient volumes are high and access to endocrinologists is uneven—generative AI tools are increasingly adopted by patients as always-available “health companions”
(
Hernandez et al. 2023
;
Lanshan et al. 2025
;
Guo et al. 2025
)
.

However, despite their growing use, little is known about how well current generative AI systems align with the lived realities and risk sensitivities of chronic disease management. Existing work often relies on benchmark-style evaluations or guideline-checking pipelines
(
Hernandez et al. 2023
;
Akrimi et al. 2025
;
Antonie et al. 2025
;
Rajagopal et al. 2025
)
, while overlooking the situated experiences and expectations of end users. More broadly, within HCI, relatively few studies have examined how AI-generated content performs across the emotional, behavioral, and clinical complexity of long-term care trajectories
(
Burgess et al. 2023
;
Mirbabaie et al. 2025
;
Mitchell et al. 2021
)
.

To address this gap, we present a two-part mixed-methods study exploring how T2DM patients and physicians in China evaluate and experience generative AI tools in the context of diabetes self-management. First, we conducted an observational study with 35 T2DM patients to understand their actual usage patterns, question types, and unmet needs when interacting with AI tools. Second, we ran a structured evaluation study with 7 endocrinologists, who assessed AI-generated responses to 66 authentic patient questions using a five-dimensional framework:
Accuracy, Safety, Clarity, Integrity
, and
Action Orientation
. These dimensions were empirically grounded in Study 1 and refined through physician co-design.

Our contributions are fourfold:

•

We introduce a real-world, chronic disease–grounded benchmark comprising 66 high-priority patient questions across seven categories, along with AI-generated responses from four popular LLMs;

•

We propose a five-dimensional evaluation rubric that combines medical criteria (e.g., accuracy, safety) with usability-oriented qualities (e.g., clarity, actionability), co-developed with clinicians and grounded in patient needs;

•

We provide mixed-methods insights into how generative AI systems are currently perceived and rated by patients and physicians, revealing a tension between accessibility and appropriateness;

•

We derive concrete IUI design implications for chronic care support systems, highlighting opportunities for multi-model routing, risk-sensitive fallback, and empathetic response scaffolding.

Ultimately, we argue that chronic illness contexts—such as T2DM—require a paradigm shift in IUI design: from general-purpose conversational agents to domain-sensitive, context-aware, and trust-calibrated support systems. By foregrounding both patient and physician perspectives, our study offers actionable insights for building safer, more adaptive AI interfaces in real-world healthcare scenarios.

2.
RELATED WORK

2.1.
Information Needs in T2DM Self-Management

Type 2 diabetes mellitus represents one of the most information-intensive chronic conditions, requiring patients to make continuous, data-driven decisions in everyday life. Beyond medical treatment, effective management demands a combination of dietary control, physical activity, medication adherence, and emotional regulation—each involving complex behavioral routines and substantial informational burden
(
Gong et al. 2020
;
Gerstenberg et al. 2025
;
Cui et al. 2025
)
. Prior HCI and health informatics research has shown that patients frequently struggle to interpret medical guidelines, contextualize numerical data (e.g., HbA1c levels), and translate abstract principles into actionable behaviors
(
Bhattacharya et al. 2023a
;
Hernandez et al. 2023
;
Mayberry et al. 2019
;
Preum et al. 2019
)
.

In many healthcare systems, particularly those with limited consultation time, patients turn to online sources, social media, and increasingly, AI-powered assistants to fill the gap between visits
(
Desai et al. 2019
;
Peimani et al. 2024
;
Hughes et al. 2024
)
. However, such unregulated information channels often produce fragmented or inconsistent advice, amplifying confusion and anxiety rather than reducing it. Studies have highlighted that diabetic patients not only seek factual knowledge—such as understanding insulin mechanisms—but also request guidance on lifestyle adaptation and emotional coping
(
Akrimi et al. 2025
;
Dsouza et al. 2024
;
Biernatzki et al. 2018
)
. This pattern underscores the need for health information systems that can support both cognitive understanding and behavioral decision-making in a continuous, personalized manner.

Within the HCI community, T2DM has long been treated as a model condition for designing and evaluating intelligent health technologies
(
Bhattacharya et al. 2023b
;
Ayobi et al. 2021
;
Ayobi et al. 2023
)
. Prior work on diabetes education tools and interactive self-tracking interfaces has explored how feedback visualization, goal-setting, and conversational support can improve adherence and motivation. Yet, most existing systems remain rule-based and rely on pre-scripted educational content, limiting their flexibility and responsiveness to real-world questions. As generative AI becomes increasingly integrated into health information platforms, T2DM offers a unique and clinically grounded context to examine how LLMs can meet, or fail to meet, the nuanced information and interaction needs of chronic disease patients.

Our study builds on this foundation by focusing specifically on the communication dynamics between generative AI systems, diabetic patients, and physicians. By examining authentic patient questions and clinician evaluations in the T2DM domain, we aim to provide a situated understanding of how AI-mediated information exchange reshapes self-management practices and what design opportunities arise for intelligent user interfaces in chronic care.

2.2.
Evaluating AI-Generated Health Information

The emergence of LLMs has transformed how patients access and interpret health information. Systems such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and ERNIE Bot have demonstrated impressive linguistic fluency and general knowledge coverage, enabling patients to obtain instant answers about chronic disease management, including diet, exercise, and medication
(
Bedmutha et al. 2024
;
Lanshan et al. 2025
;
Rebitschek et al. 2025
;
Reddy 2024
)
.
However, recent studies have revealed that these models often produce clinically inaccurate, incomplete, or misleading statements when applied to domain-specific scenarios such as diabetes care
(
Guo et al. 2025
;
Agarwal et al. 2024
;
Vishwanath et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2025
)
. While medical question–answering benchmarks such as MedQA and PubMedQA have been widely used to assess factual accuracy
(
Antonie et al. 2025
;
Jin et al. 2019
;
Jin et al. 2021
;
Kim et al. 2024b
)
, they typically emphasize correctness at the sentence level rather than the interpretive or behavioral utility of information for real patients.

Within the HCI and IUI communities, researchers have called for a shift from purely accuracy-based evaluation toward multidimensional frameworks that capture how users make sense of AI-generated health guidance
(
Burgess et al. 2023
;
Jörke et al. 2025
;
Kim et al. 2024a
;
Rong et al. 2023
)
. Prior work on mental health chatbots and digital coaching systems, for instance, has highlighted the importance of evaluating not only accuracy and clarity, but also safety, empathy, and actionability
(
Sanjeewa et al. 2024
;
Trinh et al. 2025
;
Cuadra et al. 2024
)
. Yet, these frameworks have seldom been applied in the context of chronic disease education, where patient needs are longitudinal, emotionally charged, and clinically diverse.

Specifically in the T2DM domain, existing assessments of AI-assisted education tools remain limited in scope and depth. Most focus on usability or patient satisfaction
(
Akrimi et al. 2025
;
Swallow et al. 2025
;
Tseng et al. 2025
)
, with little attention to the informational integrity or contextual appropriateness of AI-generated advice. Few studies involve clinicians as evaluators, despite their critical role in identifying subtle risks—such as when a model provides generic advice that contradicts comorbidity-specific precautions. As a result, we still lack systematic understanding of how generative AI performs across the heterogeneous question types encountered in diabetes management, from factual explanations to symptom interpretation.

Our study addresses this gap through a physician-based, multi-model comparative evaluation that examines AI-generated health information across five clinically relevant dimensions: accuracy, safety, clarity, integrity, and action orientation. By combining structured quantitative scoring with qualitative interviews, we provide a human-centered framework for evaluating the reliability and limitations of generative AI in chronic care. This approach complements existing automatic and crowd-based assessments by incorporating expert judgment, clinical reasoning, and contextual awareness—dimensions essential for understanding how AI guidance may shape patient decision-making in T2DM and beyond.

2.3.
Designing IUI Systems for Chronic Care Support

Chronic disease management, including T2DM, presents distinct challenges for intelligent user interface design due to its long-term, behavior-intensive, and emotionally loaded nature. Unlike acute care settings, where decisions are immediate and directive, chronic care requires sustained information support, personalization, and motivational scaffolding
(
Mirbabaie et al. 2025
;
Mitchell et al. 2021
;
Mack et al. 2022
)
.
Prior work in health informatics and IUI has explored digital coaching systems, context-aware reminders, and shared decision-making tools that assist patients in navigating complex treatment regimens
(
Oh et al. 2023
;
Hwang et al. 2025
)
.
However, few studies have investigated how generative AI can be safely and effectively integrated into such ecosystems—especially when its outputs are not curated by clinicians.

Designing for trust and safety remains a major concern in health-AI interfaces.
Existing research emphasizes the importance of risk-sensitive messaging
(
Schömbs 2025
;
Kandel et al. 2025
)
, such as triggering disclaimers, enforcing deferral to professionals, and dynamically adjusting language based on user history or query severity.
While some conversational agents implement these features manually, automated approaches that adapt to real-time user input, clinical context, or model limitations are still rare.

In addition, most IUI designs assume a single AI engine or backend service.
Recent studies have begun to explore multi-agent architectures that combine generative and retrieval-based models, or route user queries to specialized modules based on intent classification
(
van Bekkum et al. 2021
;
Echeverria et al. 2023
)
. Yet, such orchestration strategies have not been empirically grounded in physician evaluation or tailored to the multidimensional demands of chronic disease scenarios.

Finally, personalization in chronic care interfaces has largely relied on static user profiles or rule-based tailoring
(
Bedmutha et al. 2024
;
Jörke et al. 2025
)
. With generative AI, new opportunities arise for dynamic, dialogue-driven scaffolding that adjusts to a patient’s literacy level, cultural context, or emotional state.
However, the design implications of physician-validated strengths and weaknesses across different AI models remain underexplored.

Our study contributes to this space by synthesizing physician judgments and model-specific performance profiles into actionable design implications. These include fallback mechanisms for high-risk topics, adaptive routing based on task type, and scaffolded interaction strategies that balance automation with human judgment. By grounding design in both quantitative evidence and qualitative insight, we advance a more nuanced framework for building IUI systems that are trustworthy, flexible, and context-aware in chronic care settings such as T2DM.

3.
OBSERVATIONAL STUDY OF PATIENT USE AND ATTITUDES

This formative study explores how Chinese patients with T2DM interact with generative AI tools in daily self-management. We examine common usage channels, question categories, and user expectations—laying the foundation for the physician evaluation study in Study 2.

3.1.
Method

3.1.1.
Participants

We recruited participants through a combination of online and offline channels. Online calls were posted on widely used Chinese social platforms, including Xiaohongshu, Bilibili, and Baidu Tieba. Offline recruitment took place in community centers and public spaces across four provinces in Northern China: Henan, Hebei, Shanxi, and Shandong. Eligibility criteria required participants to be at least 18 years old, clinically diagnosed with T2DM, and to have prior experience using AI tools—such as ChatGPT, Wenxin Yiyan, or other generative systems—for diabetes-related information seeking or self-management. All participants were required to have basic communication abilities and voluntarily sign an informed consent form. Individuals were excluded if they had not been diagnosed with T2DM, were experiencing severe complications or other serious illnesses, or had recently participated in other clinical trials that might interfere with the current study.

3.1.2.
Procedure

In the observational study, all participants were informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential implications before enrollment. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. They were explicitly reminded of their rights to withdraw from the study at any time or to request deletion of their data without penalty.

The study followed a multi-stage protocol. First, participants completed two structured instruments: the AI Attitude Questionnaire (Appendix A) and the AI Usage Questionnaire (Appendix B). The former comprised ten items on a 10-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 10 = strongly agree), assessing perceived convenience, personalization, psychological support, risk, and overall satisfaction. The latter collected participants’ actual usage behaviors, including preferred AI platforms, interaction modalities (e.g., mobile apps, embedded assistants, smart speakers), and self-reported examples of past interactions. Open-ended fields allowed participants to record unlisted tools and describe notable queries or use cases.

To validate and contextualize the questionnaire data, we conducted 35–55 minute semi-structured interviews using the protocol outlined in Appendix C. Interviews explored participants’ medical and demographic background, real-world use contexts, attitudes toward AI-generated information, perceived benefits and risks, and expectations for future AI capabilities. Interviews were conducted either in person or via phone, depending on participant availability. With consent, all interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed within 24 hours. Each transcript underwent at least two rounds of cross-checking to ensure accuracy and completeness.

All research staff received standardized training in qualitative interviewing and passed a knowledge review on T2DM management to ensure ethical and context-aware data collection. Research data were stored securely on the institutional cloud drive of [Anonymous University], with access restricted to authorized personnel. All personal identifiers were removed prior to analysis to ensure confidentiality.

3.1.3.
Data Analysis

We adopted a mixed-methods approach to analyze both structured survey responses and semi-structured interview data. Data sources included two patient questionnaires (Appendix A and B) and transcribed interviews (Appendix C). Quantitative data from the attitude and usage questionnaires were processed using descriptive statistics to summarize participant backgrounds and overall engagement patterns. The attitudinal items, measured on 10-point Likert scales, were organized into five predefined dimensions. Usage data included categorical responses on platforms, frequency, and interaction scenarios. Qualitative data, including patient-submitted AI queries and interview transcripts, were analyzed using thematic analysis
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
)
, supported by NVivo for data management. Two researchers independently coded an initial subset of interviews to construct a shared codebook, which was iteratively refined through discussion. Inter-coder agreement exceeded 85%, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. Open-ended responses and transcripts were coded using both inductive and deductive strategies. Inductive coding captured emergent patterns in patient information-seeking behavior and AI interaction experiences. Deductive coding was informed by key analytical dimensions developed to guide subsequent evaluation in Study 2. All transcripts were double-coded to enhance analytic rigor and support cross-participant comparisons.

3.2.2.
Patient Attitudes Toward AI and Perceived Boundaries

Patients generally expressed cautious optimism about using generative AI in diabetes management. Many described it as convenient and responsive, particularly for answering routine questions and preparing for doctor visits. As one participant noted,
"After chatting with AI, I know what exactly to ask my doctor—it saves time."
Several participants viewed AI as a useful supplement to overburdened clinics, especially for addressing everyday confusion around diet, glucose levels, and treatment logistics.

Despite these benefits, participants consistently emphasized that AI should not replace medical professionals. Physician authority remained the ultimate point of trust, especially in cases of conflicting information. One user explained,
"If AI says one thing and my doctor says another, I will always follow the doctor."
This belief reflects safety concerns and the perceived need for human accountability in medical decision-making.

Participants also pointed to a lack of personalization and context-awareness in AI responses. Generic advice like “eat a balanced diet” felt too abstract without adjustments for comorbidities such as kidney disease. Others found AI unable to offer concrete, actionable steps tailored to their lifestyles—underscoring limitations in
Action Orientation
. Additionally, emotional support was widely seen as inadequate. While AI could deliver polite and encouraging phrases, users described its tone as robotic and unempathetic when discussing anxiety, burnout, or stigma. These gaps highlighted the importance of
Clarity
and emotional resonance, especially for long-term disease self-management.

Overall, participants valued AI as a reliable source for factual information, but viewed it as insufficient for individualized, judgment-based, or emotionally sensitive support. These perspectives directly informed the five-dimensional evaluation criteria used in Study 2, reinforcing the need to assess not only correctness, but also contextual safety, completeness, clarity, and behavioral relevance.

3.2.3.
Expectations for Future AI Health Assistants

While participants generally acknowledged the value of generative AI for knowledge access and self-management support, they expressed clear expectations for how these systems should evolve. Their visions converged around three themes—personalization, transparency, and ecosystem integration—each addressing shortcomings observed in current AI interactions.

First, participants strongly emphasized the need for individualized responses. Rather than generic templates, they hoped for systems that account for their unique medical history, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors. As P3 explained,
"I hope AI can truly understand my condition, not just repeat a fixed template."
This desire reflects a growing expectation for enhanced
Action Orientation
and contextual responsiveness in AI recommendations.

Second, trust emerged as a key concern. Participants stated that AI-generated content should include citations from credible medical sources or clinical guidelines. As P13 put it,
"If AI could show where its advice comes from—like official guidelines—I would feel more reassured."
Such transparency was seen as essential for building confidence in both the
Accuracy
and
Safety
of AI advice.

Finally, many envisioned an AI system embedded in a broader, data-connected ecosystem. Suggestions included integration with wearables and sensors to automatically interpret physiological data (P10), image-based interfaces to reduce input burden (P8), and optional physician review mechanisms to ensure professional oversight (P13). These ideas point toward improved
Integrity
and real-world utility.

Together, these expectations constitute a patient-centered roadmap for future AI health assistants—systems that are personalized, trustworthy, and embedded within a larger health data infrastructure. They also offer empirical support for the five-dimensional evaluation framework deployed in Study 2.

EVALUATION STUDY OF AI-GENERATED HEALTH INFORMATION BY PHYSICIANS

Building on insights from our formative study, we conducted a follow-up evaluation to assess the quality of AI-generated health information for T2DM management. This second study shifts the analytic lens from patients to physicians, leveraging expert clinical judgment to evaluate the
accuracy
,
clarity
,
safety
, and
actionability
of AI responses to real-world patient questions. Our aim is to identify system-level strengths and limitations as perceived by domain experts, and to derive design implications for safer, more personalized AI tools in chronic disease care in China.

4.1.
Participants

We recruited participating physicians through online outreach on major Chinese platforms, including Xiaohongshu, Bilibili, and Baidu Tieba. All participants were required to provide written informed consent. Physicians were excluded if they were directly involved in the development of AI tools for diabetes care or held financial interests in related companies, to avoid conflicts of interest. 4.2.
Procedures

The evaluation study consisted of three sequential phases designed to assess the quality, safety, and actionability of AI-generated health information from a clinical perspective: (1) physician-led selection of patient-generated questions, (2) expert evaluation of AI responses, and (3) post-evaluation interviews and reflections. This end-to-end process—from corpus curation to expert judgment—enabled a systematic investigation of AI’s role in T2DM management.

Phase 1: Question Selection

In the first phase, the physician team reviewed all 784 real-world questions collected from patients in Study 1. Given the large volume and heterogeneity of the dataset, three criteria were used to curate a representative subset: (1) frequency of occurrence, (2) clinical significance (i.e., relevance to core management decisions), and (3) potential risk if answered incorrectly. Through iterative discussion and consensus, the team finalized a question set that balanced high-frequency queries with low-frequency but high-risk items.

Questions were then categorized into six domains:
factual knowledge
,
diet management
,
exercise advice
,
medication guidance
,
symptom interpretation
, and
complications & psychological well-being
. This taxonomy builds on the "five pillars" of diabetes self-management—education, diet, exercise, medication, and monitoring—while extending to psychosocial dimensions in line with the biopsychosocial model of care. The final question set, along with frequency and category labels, is presented in Appendix D.

Phase 2: AI Output Evaluation

In the second phase, each question from the curated set (Appendix D) was entered into four mainstream generative AI platforms. To ensure independence and comparability, a new session was initiated for each query, and memory or context-continuation features were disabled where possible. All AI-generated responses were saved in text format, anonymized, and archived by question ID and platform type to support consistent analysis.

Each physician independently evaluated the AI outputs using a five-dimensional rubric (120 scores total) co-developed by the clinical team. The rubric, grounded in both clinical guidelines and the textual nature of LLM outputs, consisted of the following weighted dimensions:

•

Accuracy (30)
: Alignment with current clinical guidelines and evidence-based practice.

•

Safety (30)
: Absence of misleading or potentially harmful advice (e.g., unsupervised drug discontinuation, unverified therapies).

•

Clarity (20)
: Use of accessible, non-technical language and clear explanatory structure.

•

Integrity (20)
: Inclusion of all key components; avoidance of partial or fragmented information.

•

Action Orientation (20)
: Provision of concrete, practical suggestions for health self-management.

Physicians could optionally add qualitative comments to highlight strengths, deficiencies, or safety concerns. All scores and written feedback were compiled and cross-validated to ensure consistency. These expert evaluations served as the basis for Phase 3 interviews, enabling in-depth reflection on patterns and concerns observed during rating.

Phase 3: Expert Interviews and Reflective Analysis

To gain deeper insights into physicians’ evaluation processes and their broader perspectives on AI-generated content, we conducted semi-structured interviews following the scoring phase. The interview protocol (Appendix E) was developed based on the evaluation framework and quantitative results, structured to progress from overall impressions to dimension-specific reasoning.

Each interview began with open-ended reflections on the perceived utility, reliability, and appropriate use cases of generative AI in clinical practice. Physicians were then guided through a focused discussion on the five evaluation dimensions—
Accuracy, Safety, Clarity, Integrity
, and
Action Orientation
. Interviewers used prompts such as “Can you recall an example where an AI response felt unsafe?” or “What would make you trust an AI’s recommendation in your workflow?” to elicit concrete examples and decision rationales.

The conversation then transitioned to the seven question domains used in the study (e.g., dietary advice, medication guidance, complication interpretation), prompting physicians to comment on domain-specific variation in AI performance. Finally, participants were invited to reflect on how AI tools might fit into real-world medical workflows, suggest design improvements, and share their willingness to collaborate with AI in future clinical settings.

All interviews were conducted by researchers trained in qualitative interviewing and medical communication. Each session lasted 30–60 minutes, was audio-recorded with consent, and transcribed within 24 hours. Transcripts were manually verified in two rounds to ensure fidelity. This phase complemented the structured ratings from Phase 2 by surfacing clinicians’ mental models, evaluative heuristics, and design expectations for trustworthy health AI systems.

4.3.
Data Analysis

The quantitative analysis aimed to evaluate the quality of AI-generated health information across four generative AI models (GPT-4-turbo
(
OpenAI 2025
)
, DeepSeek-R1
(
DeepSeek 2025
)
, Kimi K2
(
AI 2025
)
, ERNIE Bot 4.5
(
Baidu 2025
)
) and to examine performance variability across five evaluation dimensions and seven question categories. All physician rating data (N = 5 raters × 4 models × 66 questions) were first cleaned and screened for completeness. For each model, we computed overall scores by aggregating five dimension-level sub-scores (Accuracy, Safety, Clarity, Integrity, and Action Orientation), yielding descriptive statistics including means (M), standard deviations (SD), and interquartile ranges (IQR) to assess central tendencies and variability.

To examine model-level performance differences, we conducted one-way repeated-measures ANOVA
(
Girden 1992
)
with AI system as the within-subject factor. Mauchly’s test was used to assess sphericity assumptions, and Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied where violations occurred. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction, and effect sizes were reported using generalized eta squared (
\eta^{2}_{G}
) and Hedges’ g. To investigate interactions between model type and evaluation dimension, we conducted two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with within-subject factors of Model (4 levels) and Dimension (5 levels), followed by simple effects tests to examine differential strengths and weaknesses across dimensions. Similarly, to assess how model performance varied across question categories, we applied a separate two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Model and Category (7 levels) as within-subject factors.

Beyond inferential statistics, we visualized score distributions through boxplots, radar charts, and matrix-style heatmaps to highlight performance asymmetries and model-specific strengths and weaknesses across dimensions and categories. These visualizations enabled triangulation with qualitative feedback, supporting more robust interpretation of physician assessments and informing subsequent design discussions.

The qualitative data from post-evaluation interviews were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
)
. Transcripts were manually coded in two iterative rounds. Initial open coding was conducted independently by two researchers who reviewed all seven interview transcripts to generate a preliminary codebook, which was then refined through collaborative discussion. Codes were subsequently organized into dimension-aligned themes (e.g., perceptions of safety, critiques of clarity, emotional response gaps) and emergent design expectations (e.g., transparency, contextual reasoning, model orchestration). Representative quotes were selected to illustrate key physician attitudes and to provide interpretive depth to quantitative trends. Thematic development was validated through peer debriefing and reviewed by senior clinical experts to ensure conceptual validity.

5.2.
Qualitative Findings

5.2.1.
Perceptions of AI as a Health Information Source

Physicians in our study expressed cautious optimism regarding the use of generative AI as a health information resource. While acknowledging its limitations in clinical reasoning, participants consistently recognized AI’s strengths in factual explanation, patient education, and linguistic clarity—particularly for newly diagnosed individuals navigating complex concepts. As P3 observed,
"As an information aggregator, it performs remarkably well. I can imagine a newly diagnosed patient who knows nothing about ’insulin resistance’ coming in after consulting AI, asking more specific questions like, ’Is my insulin resistance related to obesity?’"
. This role as a “pre-visit primer” was perceived as beneficial for raising patients’ baseline understanding and improving the efficiency of doctor–patient communication.

However, physicians drew clear boundaries between educational scaffolding and clinical authority. Several participants emphasized that AI could not replace human expertise in contextualized care planning or decision-making. P2 stated,
"I’m okay with patients bringing AI-printed materials to the clinic, but I can’t accept AI directly telling a patient ’your insulin dosage should be adjusted.’"
P4 similarly cautioned,
"Diabetes—and most chronic conditions—require highly contextualized management. AI sees a single data point; physicians see a multi-dimensional picture."
These perspectives underscore a critical distinction: AI may inform, but it should not prescribe.

This boundary became especially salient when AI responses approached borderline clinical decisions. As P5 noted,
"It correctly listed the ’dawn phenomenon’ and ’Somogyi effect’ as causes of fasting hyperglycemia, but didn’t suggest 3 a.m. blood glucose testing—which is key for differentiation."
Similarly, P6 described AI’s neutrality in high-risk queries as potentially dangerous:
"When asked about ketogenic diets, AI gives pros and cons but fails to state clearly that it’s contraindicated for patients with kidney disease. That kind of neutrality could mislead high-risk users."
Such examples reveal a critical gap between linguistic competence and clinical decisiveness—particularly in scenarios that demand strong safety framing.

Physicians generally acknowledged AI’s tendency to err on the side of caution. P4 remarked,
"It consistently recommends ’150 minutes of moderate exercise’ and includes warnings for retinopathy or neuropathy. Honestly, it’s more cautious than some doctors."
Yet concerns remained about incomplete or de-emphasized warnings. P2 recounted a subtle but clinically relevant omission:
"It mentioned metformin side effects but downplayed that it must be stopped before contrast imaging. For elderly patients getting coronary CTs, this omission could be dangerous."
These accounts reflect how AI’s surface-level thoroughness may still fall short of the prioritization expected in clinical contexts.

In terms of communicative design, physicians consistently praised the structure and clarity of AI-generated responses. Metaphors such as
"a key and a lock"
(for insulin and receptors) or
"a photo album of your blood sugar memory"
(for HbA1c) were cited by P7 as helpful for patient comprehension. However, such fluency was not always matched by completeness or contextual integration. As P3 explained,
"It tells you ’spinach is good’ and ’oatmeal is good,’ but doesn’t integrate that into a personalized plan that considers culture, budget, and comorbidities. It gives data points, not strategies."

Finally, physicians raised concerns about the emotional flatness and behavioral rigidity of AI interactions. P1 described its action-oriented advice as overly scripted:
"It might say ’test your blood sugar four times a day,’ but won’t say ’test if you feel dizzy—even if it’s unscheduled.’"
Several participants emphasized the lack of empathetic resonance. P6 noted,
"When a patient says ’I’m terrified after diagnosis,’ AI responds with checkbox lines like ’stay positive, seek support.’ But patients need to feel understood. AI can’t do that."
P4 echoed this sentiment:
"It might say ’I understand your anxiety,’ but there’s no feeling behind those words. Patients can tell."

These findings reinforce and extend our quantitative analysis. While models like ChatGPT excel in factual clarity and procedural structure, physicians cautioned that emotional nuance, contextual judgment, and safety signaling remain underdeveloped. These observations highlight the importance of interface-level guardrails and hybrid interaction strategies—where AI serves as a first-pass educator but defers to clinician involvement in ambiguous or affectively charged scenarios.

5.2.2.
Comparative Evaluation of the Four AI Models

When comparing the four evaluated models—ChatGPT, DeepSeek, ERNIE Bot, and Kimi Chat—physicians expressed clear and consistent preferences. Across all participants, ChatGPT was regarded as the most reliable, well-rounded system, frequently described as "the most trustworthy" or "closest to professional standards." As P2 noted,
"ChatGPT not only delivers accurate content but also includes physiological and clinical context—almost at the level of professional medical writing."
In addition to its factual correctness, physicians appreciated its structured delivery and well-calibrated tone, especially regarding safety. P3 pointed out,
"It tends to include appropriate disclaimers, like reminding users to consult a doctor. That kind of caution matters a lot."
These attributes, particularly in high-stakes health contexts, contributed to clinicians’ stronger confidence in deploying ChatGPT as part of patient-facing systems.

DeepSeek was generally viewed as a competent but clearly second-tier model. Physicians acknowledged its strengths in handling standardized factual queries, especially in topics such as glucose metabolism or nutritional guidelines. However, multiple interviewees emphasized its limitations in handling ambiguity or clinical nuance. As P5 observed,
"It performs well on textbook-style questions, but once contextual reasoning is needed, the gap with ChatGPT becomes clear."
Others noted that DeepSeek occasionally lacked precision in tone, sometimes offering overly optimistic or vague reassurance. This undermined its reliability for sensitive topics, where cautious phrasing is essential. For interface design, these issues suggest that while DeepSeek may be suitable for knowledge delivery, additional guardrails may be necessary when extending it to complex or ambiguous health interactions.

Compared to the top two models, ERNIE Bot received mixed reviews. Physicians characterized it as action-oriented and procedurally helpful but often lacking in depth and accuracy. For instance, P7 remarked,
"It’s good at listing steps—like how to rotate insulin injection sites—but the underlying explanation isn’t always sound."
This pattern was consistent across multiple interviewees, who saw ERNIE’s directness as a double-edged sword: helpful for simple behavioral prompts, but potentially risky if it leads users to over-trust incorrect advice. From a design standpoint, this calls for tighter integration of model strengths and weaknesses into system architecture, potentially using ERNIE Bot to support structured task workflows while delegating open-ended reasoning tasks elsewhere.

Kimi Chat was consistently rated as the weakest among the four models. Physicians pointed to deficiencies across all five evaluation dimensions—accuracy, clarity, safety, integrity, and action orientation. P1 commented,
"It underperforms across the board… there’s no part where I’d feel confident letting it advise a patient."
Of particular concern were its inconsistent safety messages and guideline-inconsistent responses. P4 shared,
"Its answer on hypoglycemia missed basic life-saving steps. That kind of omission is unacceptable in health advice."
These limitations rendered Kimi unsuitable for independent deployment, but potentially useful for behind-the-scenes text generation if paired with strict verification mechanisms.

Across all interviews, physicians emphasized that factual correctness alone was insufficient. Their judgments incorporated tone, structure, and perceived empathy, all of which shaped their willingness to endorse a model in clinical contexts. While ChatGPT excelled in integrating these elements, other models lagged behind in different ways—some too vague, some too risky, and others simply lacking coherence. This comparative perspective reinforces the need for model-aware interface strategies. Rather than relying on a single system, future AI health assistants could benefit from multi-model orchestration frameworks that route queries based on content type, safety requirements, or complexity—leveraging each model’s relative strengths while mitigating their individual weaknesses.

5.2.3.
Performance Patterns Across Question Types

Beyond differences among models, physicians identified clear performance trends across question types, revealing how task characteristics shaped the perceived strengths and limits of generative AI. While AI systems were generally seen as competent in conveying structured factual knowledge, they consistently struggled when questions demanded contextual judgment, personalized guidance, or emotional sensitivity.

Factual knowledge was regarded as the most consistently well-handled category. Physicians agreed that questions with clear biomedical definitions or standardized explanations—such as diagnostic criteria, biomarker functions, or physiological mechanisms—played to AI’s strengths. P3 illustrated this by noting,
"For standardized questions like ’What is HbA1c?’ or ’How does insulin resistance work?’, AI—especially ChatGPT—can deliver accurate, well-structured, and accessible explanations. It’s fully capable of serving as an entry-level educational tool for patients."
This educational framing was echoed by others, who viewed AI as a promising scaffold for basic health literacy, particularly in early stages of diagnosis.

However, enthusiasm diminished when discussions turned to lifestyle guidance. Although physicians acknowledged the breadth of content coverage in domains like diet and exercise, they noted a persistent lack of contextual tailoring. As P5 explained,
"AI can list glycemic indices and general food principles, but when asked ’How should a diabetic patient with kidney disease adjust their diet?’, the answer is vague and lacks actionable specificity."
This gap between information breadth and actionable depth raised concerns about practical usefulness, especially for patients with comorbid conditions or cultural dietary constraints. Safety considerations also emerged. P6 emphasized that,
"AI reliably recommends ’150 minutes of moderate exercise per week,’ but often misses crucial warnings for patients with retinopathy or cardiovascular issues. The lack of tailored precautions poses real risks."
These observations suggest that while AI can serve as a lifestyle coach in general terms, it may require complementary safeguards or physician review before offering personalized health plans.

Medication-related questions marked a sharper decline in confidence. While factual summaries of drug mechanisms were often accurate, physicians grew increasingly uneasy when AI attempted to interpret symptoms or suggest treatment adjustments. P1 remarked,
"Explaining how a drug works is fine. But once the question becomes ’Should I change my dose?’ or ’Can I switch medications?’, AI quickly exceeds its capacity."
Even more troubling was the lack of appropriate clinical disclaimers. P4 warned,
"AI often fails to strongly emphasize: ’Any medication change must be discussed with a doctor.’ That should be a non-negotiable message."
These limitations point to a critical weakness in AI’s judgment scaffolding, reinforcing the need for design interventions that clearly delineate the boundary between education and clinical decision-making.

Medication interpretation presented similar challenges. While AI responses were often encyclopedic, they lacked diagnostic direction. P2 offered a telling example:
"If a patient reports feeling constantly fatigued, AI lists everything from poor glucose control to thyroid disorders. But this ’comprehensive’ list offers no guidance—it just adds confusion."
Physicians stressed that such ambiguity could lead to increased anxiety or misinformed self-management behaviors, particularly in patients with limited medical training. These findings suggest that future interfaces should move beyond completeness toward prioritization and diagnostic scaffolding, perhaps by dynamically structuring outputs based on patient intent or severity cues.

Complication-related questions drew more polarized reactions. Some physicians appreciated the system’s cautious framing, interpreting its emphasis on risks as a helpful awareness-raising tool. Others, however, found the language overly alarmist. As P7 noted,
"When describing complications like diabetic foot or retinopathy, the language is often alarmist. It fails to convey that these are preventable and manageable, which might lead to heightened patient anxiety."
These observations raise important questions about tone calibration—how to balance urgency with reassurance—particularly in emotionally vulnerable users. For IUI design, this highlights the potential value of tone-tuning modules that adapt messaging style based on query context or user preferences.

Finally, in the domain of psychological and emotional support, AI was seen as superficially helpful but emotionally disconnected. Physicians acknowledged that AI could provide practical tips—such as how to pack insulin for travel or manage eating out—but found its responses to emotional distress generic and impersonal. P3 remarked,
"When it comes to things like diabetes burnout, the answers feel templated. Patients can tell there’s no real understanding behind it."
P6 added,
"When a patient says ’I’m terrified after diagnosis,’ AI responds with checkbox lines like ’stay positive, seek support.’ But patients need to feel understood."
These accounts reveal a core design gap: while AI can mimic empathy linguistically, it lacks the affective responsiveness that builds trust in emotionally charged interactions.

Taken together, these findings suggest that generative AI is most reliable when operating within bounded informational domains—those where knowledge is structured, generalizable, and low-risk. Its performance deteriorates when clinical nuance, contextual judgment, or emotional resonance becomes essential. For interactive health systems, this underscores the need for task-aware mediation mechanisms—such as routing queries based on topic sensitivity, flagging potentially high-risk responses, or explicitly signaling the system’s scope and limitations.

5.2.4.
Expectations for Future Health-AI Systems

In addition to assessing current capabilities, physicians also articulated forward-looking expectations for the next generation of AI-powered health assistants. These expectations centered on three recurring themes: personalization, transparency, and system integration.

First, several participants emphasized the need for deeper personalization in AI-generated recommendations. Rather than issuing generalized suggestions, future systems should account for a patient’s comorbidities, medication history, and even psychosocial background. As P6 described,
"I want AI to stop answering like a textbook. If it could say ’Because this patient has nephropathy, avoid high-protein diets,’ that’s when it becomes truly useful."
This desire for tailored, context-sensitive responses highlights a key design direction: embedding structured patient data—when ethically and securely permitted—into the AI reasoning pipeline.

Second, physicians called for greater transparency and traceability in AI outputs. As P2 remarked,
"I need to know where the information is coming from. Was it a national guideline? A journal article? A user forum?"
Multiple physicians indicated that when AI systems cite authoritative sources or flag uncertainties explicitly, their trustworthiness improves substantially. This suggests that future interfaces might incorporate citation panels, source tags, or confidence visualizations as default features in health dialogue systems.

Third, some participants envisioned integration between AI systems and real-time physiological or behavioral data. For instance, P5 imagined,
"If AI could pull my patient’s continuous glucose monitor readings or even step counts, it could explain why their blood sugar spiked last night—and what to do next."
Others pointed to the potential of image-based triage, such as uploading a photo of a foot wound to evaluate infection risk. These visions signal a demand for multi-modal interaction and richer data fusion, supporting more responsive and holistic care pathways.

Finally, physicians also proposed governance mechanisms to constrain AI autonomy in high-stakes decisions. P7 suggested,
"It should be a draft assistant. Let it write, but require a doctor to review before the patient sees anything involving medication changes."
This human-in-the-loop orientation aligns with ongoing debates about safe deployment and shared control in clinical AI systems.

Together, these forward-looking expectations underscore the limitations of current generative models while pointing toward actionable design trajectories. Rather than treating AI as a static answer engine, future IUI systems should evolve into adaptive, explainable, and physician-collaborative agents—augmented by patient data, governed by transparency, and embedded in clinical workflows.

6.
DISCUSSION

6.1.
Generative AI as a Scalable Health Educator

Our findings underscore the value of generative AI as an accessible and scalable tool for patient education, particularly in the management of chronic diseases such as T2DM. Across both quantitative and qualitative analyses, physicians consistently acknowledged the ability of LLMs to deliver structured, accurate, and digestible explanations for factual knowledge and general lifestyle guidance. This aligns with recent work demonstrating that LLMs can support patient understanding of medical terminology, risk factors, and self-care protocols in controlled settings
(
Akrimi et al. 2025
;
Hernandez et al. 2023
)
.

What distinguishes our study, however, is the ecological validity of this finding in the context of real-world patient queries and localized healthcare constraints. Unlike prior work that focuses on static prompts or expert-authored question sets
(
Shin et al. 2020
;
Mou et al. 2024
)
, our question corpus emerged directly from patient-initiated information-seeking behavior, collected through observational and participatory methods. This grounding enables a more nuanced understanding of where AI succeeds—as a “pre-visit primer” that prepares patients to ask better-informed questions—and where it fails. Several physicians in our study noted that AI-enhanced literacy improves clinical encounters by enabling patients to articulate symptoms or concepts more precisely, reducing communication overhead.

However, our results also clarify the boundaries of this educational value. Even in its strongest domains, AI does not substitute for clinical reasoning. Physicians emphasized that factual explanations—no matter how clear—are not equivalent to judgment. This echoes concerns raised in prior work about the dangers of misperceived authority when AI-generated outputs are overly fluent or confident
(
Burgess et al. 2023
;
Rajagopal et al. 2025
;
Asgari et al. 2025
)
.

Taken together, these findings support the design of intelligent interfaces that explicitly frame generative AI as an educational tool rather than a diagnostic engine. Future IUI systems could emphasize this distinction through role-based messaging (“AI educator” vs. “clinical decision-maker”), scaffolding features that encourage users to explore questions without assuming correctness, and interface cues that promote follow-up discussions with human professionals. Such systems could position AI as a “health literacy amplifier” embedded in the patient journey, improving not just knowledge access but also downstream communication quality.

6.2.
Performance Gaps Across Tasks and Models

Despite the growing enthusiasm for deploying large language models in health contexts, our results reveal significant and persistent performance disparities—both between different AI systems and across task categories. This aligns partially with prior work showing that LLMs vary widely in factual correctness and language fluency
(
Samimi et al. 2025
;
Hernandez et al. 2023
)
. However, our study extends these findings by offering a fine-grained, physician-rated evaluation that captures how these differences manifest across clinically relevant question types.

First, we observed a consistent model hierarchy: ChatGPT outperformed all others in accuracy, clarity, and integrity, while Kimi Chat and ERNIE Bot exhibited frequent deficiencies, especially in high-stakes tasks such as medication guidance or symptom interpretation. This suggests that not all commercially available LLMs meet the same threshold of reliability—an important caveat often underemphasized in prior benchmark studies, which tend to focus on a single model in isolation
(
Jörke et al. 2025
;
Gaber et al. 2025
;
Jahan et al. 2024
)
.

Second, our task-level analysis revealed a domain-dependent performance gradient. Factual and lifestyle-related questions were answered well by most models, while categories requiring clinical reasoning (e.g., medication adjustment, complications) or emotional sensitivity (e.g., psychological support) showed consistent underperformance. These findings corroborate early qualitative studies suggesting that LLMs perform better on structured or rule-based inputs
(
Antonie et al. 2025
;
Singhal et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
, but add empirical depth by linking these limitations to specific design risks—such as inappropriate generalizations, lack of tailored disclaimers, or emotionally hollow language.

Physicians in our interviews were particularly attuned to these gaps. While appreciative of AI’s structured explanations in low-risk domains, they uniformly cautioned against using the same tools in high-risk scenarios. For instance, missing dosage warnings or failing to emphasize critical precautions (e.g., stopping metformin before contrast imaging) were seen as serious flaws. This speaks to a growing concern in human-centered AI: how users might conflate general fluency with medical validity, especially when tools are deployed without context-aware safeguards
(
Bhattacharya et al. 2025
;
Reis et al. 2024
;
Abdelwanis et al. 2024
)
.

These results call for task-aware interface designs that go beyond general-purpose prompting. One avenue is to implement automatic confidence calibration or risk-level tagging, where the system adjusts the interface tone or flags answers for clinician review based on question type. Another approach is dynamic model routing: given the observed dimensional strengths of different models, systems might route medication-related questions to more conservative models and lifestyle queries to those with stronger communicative clarity. Together, these strategies move IUI design toward a more modular, risk-sensitive paradigm—matching the affordances of each model to the complexity of each task.

6.3.
Designing for Trust and Appropriate Use Boundaries

Our findings highlight an essential distinction between trust and overreliance in the use of generative AI for health information. Physicians in our study consistently described AI as a valuable educational assistant but rejected the notion of it serving as a clinical authority. This position aligns with a broader concern in human-centered AI research that system usability and fluency may inadvertently foster misplaced confidence among users
(
Rosbach et al. 2025
;
Shekar et al. 2024
;
Wysocki et al. 2023
)
. In practice, the boundary between "helpful" and "hazardous" AI advice is often blurred—not because users consciously disregard risks, but because system design fails to communicate its limitations clearly.

Prior research has explored how interface transparency, uncertainty expression, and provenance cues can help users calibrate trust in AI-generated content
(
Reyes et al. 2025
;
Bhatt et al. 2021
)
. Yet, few studies have examined how these mechanisms should operate in consumer-facing health contexts, where users are often laypeople interpreting AI responses without professional oversight. Our physician participants repeatedly emphasized that AI’s linguistic confidence and smooth presentation—while improving readability—can mask substantive weaknesses in logic or clinical relevance. As P4 explained,
"It might sound professional, but sometimes it misses the one thing that truly matters—the context that makes an answer safe."
This “fluency illusion” complicates users’ ability to judge the reliability of AI-generated advice.

These observations point to a critical design challenge: balancing informativeness and humility. In our study, doctors proposed explicit strategies for framing AI outputs as provisional rather than prescriptive. Some suggested that systems should adopt role-based communication, e.g., labeling responses as “educational summaries” instead of “recommendations.” Others recommended that high-stakes topics—such as medication adjustment or symptom interpretation—trigger mandatory safety disclaimers or physician referral prompts. Such mechanisms would anchor AI within an assistive role, reinforcing its value while preventing overreach.

From an interaction design perspective, these insights call for interfaces that convey layered trust signals. For example, uncertainty-aware outputs could include confidence visualizations or textual qualifiers (“This answer is based on general guidelines; consult your doctor for personalized advice”). Provenance markers could trace factual statements to verified sources or guideline editions, enabling users to differentiate evidence-based information from generative speculation. Building on
(
Merry et al. 2021
;
Schrills and Franke 2020
;
Bauer et al. 2023
)
, such features may help cultivate a form of calibrated trust—where users neither dismiss AI advice outright nor accept it uncritically.

Ultimately, establishing clear trust boundaries is not about reducing user confidence in AI but about shaping appropriate reliance. By making system uncertainty visible, contextualizing authority, and integrating escalation pathways to human experts, future IUI systems can support safer, more transparent, and ethically grounded human–AI collaboration in health communication.

6.4.
Limitations in AI’s Capacity for Human-Centered Information Interfaces

While generative AI systems demonstrate strong potential in factual information delivery, our findings highlight important limitations in their usability and reliability as health information interfaces. Physicians consistently observed that, beyond lexical fluency, current AI models fall short in providing context-aware, personally relevant, and emotionally resonant responses—especially in tasks where trust, safety, and interactional sensitivity are critical. These findings shift the analytical lens away from evaluating AI as a proxy for clinical decision-making, and instead underscore its limitations as a communicative interface in chronic care contexts.

A core limitation lies in personalization. Participants noted that while models could accurately define terms like HbA1c or list general dietary principles, they failed to tailor recommendations to individual comorbidities or behavioral constraints. Generic responses such as “eat a balanced diet” were perceived as vague or even misleading when key risk factors—like kidney impairment—were not acknowledged. This points to the absence of user modeling and real-time contextual adaptation, long emphasized in IUI research
(
Lewis et al. 2022
;
Torkamaan and Ziegler 2022
;
Jacobs et al. 2018
)
.

Actionability was another concern. Physicians found that AI systems often stopped at listing options rather than offering navigable next steps. In symptom interpretation, AI might enumerate causes of fatigue but not suggest whether or when to seek care. In medication queries, safety disclaimers were inconsistent or overly subtle—posing risks for unsupervised behavior. These patterns reflect the “answer-delivery” rather than “decision-support” orientation of current interfaces
(
He et al. 2025
;
Song et al. 2025
)
.

Emotional responsiveness also emerged as a persistent usability barrier. Despite polished language, AI responses to affect-laden prompts—such as post-diagnosis anxiety—were frequently described as formulaic or emotionally hollow. Participants emphasized that empathy requires not just emotional language, but interactional attunement and validation. In high-stakes chronic care contexts, such affective insensitivity may erode trust or reduce engagement
(
Pan and De Graaf 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Xiao et al. 2023
)
.

Overall, these limitations suggest that current generative AI tools, while linguistically competent, lack many of the communicative and interpersonal affordances required for chronic care. As physicians noted, these systems operate more as “information broadcasters” than “interactive collaborators,” limiting their ability to scaffold understanding, promote behavior change, or build user confidence in uncertain situations.

6.5.
Toward Human–AI Collaboration in Chronic Care Interfaces

To address these challenges, we call for a paradigm shift in the design of intelligent user interfaces (IUIs) for chronic disease support—from static information presenters to adaptive, collaborative communication partners. Building on insights from physicians’ evaluations, we outline four design priorities that reflect both technical feasibility and clinical usability.

First,
task-aware model orchestration
is critical. As no single model performs consistently across all content types and risk levels, future IUI systems should support multi-model routing based on task demands—e.g., using more expressive models for patient education and more conservative models for medication-related advice. While prior work in orchestration has shown promise
(
Naik et al. 2025
;
Lee et al. 2025
)
, our study provides clinical grounding to specify which dimensions warrant dynamic delegation.

Second, systems must support
risk-aware fallback strategies
. For sensitive domains such as symptom triage or medication changes, interfaces should detect uncertainty and activate protective flows—such as inserting high-visibility disclaimers, triggering clarifying questions, or offering escalation pathways to human professionals. These mechanisms align with broader trends in safety-critical IUI design
(
Florins and Vanderdonckt 2004
;
Bhatt et al. 2021
;
Reyes et al. 2025
)
.

Third,
dynamic personalization
should move beyond user profiles to incorporate real-time behavioral and physiological signals—e.g., wearables, glucometer data, symptom logs. This would allow AI systems to adapt recommendations based on patient history and temporal context. While such multimodal adaptation is well-established in health informatics
(
Lee et al. 2025
;
Ayobi et al. 2021
)
, its integration with generative, dialog-based systems remains underexplored.

Finally,
emotionally intelligent interaction
demands more than sentiment-aware phrasing. IUIs should recognize linguistic cues of stress, confusion, or disengagement and respond with structured emotional scaffolding—e.g., validating user emotion, suggesting relevant support resources, or offering conversational pacing. These principles resonate with affective computing research but require reinterpretation in health-oriented dialogue systems
(
Genç and Verma 2024
;
Cuadra et al. 2024
)
.

In sum, effective AI-mediated interfaces for chronic disease care must balance informativeness with interactivity, personalization with safety, and fluency with emotional authenticity. By aligning interface design with real-world clinician feedback, our study outlines actionable pathways for developing AI systems that patients can trust, understand, and rely on—not just as content generators, but as credible, collaborative participants in their long-term health journeys.

6.6.
Limitations and Future Work

This study has several limitations that inform opportunities for future research. First, while we evaluated four widely adopted generative AI models, the pace of model iteration is exceptionally rapid. The outputs we analyzed represent a snapshot in time, and subsequent updates—such as fine-tuning or safety alignment—may alter performance characteristics. Longitudinal tracking of AI evolution will be essential to capture emerging strengths and risks in real-world deployments. Second, although our evaluation protocol featured rigorous scoring and expert review, it was limited to static, single-turn outputs. Real-world health interactions are inherently dynamic and often involve multi-turn clarification, user error, and real-time correction. Future work should extend this framework to simulate interactive scenarios—such as follow-up questioning, correction-seeking behavior, and adaptive clarification—to better reflect the complexities of intelligent user interfaces in clinical and self-management settings.

Third, the physician sample in our qualitative evaluation comprised seven endocrinologists, all affiliated with Chinese hospitals. While their insights offer clinical depth, this relatively small and specialized sample may not capture the broader spectrum of provider perspectives, such as those from primary care, rural practice, or nursing contexts. Broader sampling could uncover differing expectations regarding AI trustworthiness and workflow integration. Finally, although prompts were crafted with consistency across platforms, we did not employ a formalized prompt engineering protocol. Given the known sensitivity of LLMs to prompt phrasing and structure, future research should systematically compare how variations in input design affect both performance and user experience—particularly in health-critical domains where misinterpretation carries real-world risk. Together, these limitations point toward future directions for making AI-assisted health systems more robust, generalizable, and contextually adaptive.

7.
CONCLUSION

This paper presents a mixed-methods investigation into the capabilities and limitations of generative AI systems in the context of T2DM self-management. Through an observational study with patients and a structured evaluation with physicians, we surface key tensions between accessibility and appropriateness, standardization and personalization, fluency and factuality. Our five-dimensional evaluation framework—centered on
Accuracy, Safety, Clarity, Integrity
, and
Action Orientation
—provides a structured lens for assessing AI-generated health information in real-world chronic care scenarios.

Our findings suggest that while current LLMs perform well in factual and lifestyle-oriented domains, they struggle in delivering safe, context-sensitive, and emotionally resonant support. Importantly, we highlight the divergent expectations of patients and clinicians, and propose design implications for interactive health systems—ranging from multi-model orchestration to risk-aware fallback mechanisms and empathetic scaffolding. More broadly, we argue that chronic conditions such as T2DM demand new paradigms of IUI design—ones that go beyond generic QA and instead support longitudinal care, trust calibration, and adaptive information delivery. By centering both user and expert perspectives, this work contributes actionable insights toward the development of more precise, patient-centered AI health assistants.
