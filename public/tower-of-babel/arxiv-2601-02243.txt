Optimal Dispatch of Electricity and Water in Renewable-Integrated Desalination Plants
Ahmed S. Alahmed, Audun Botterud, Saurabh Amin, Ali T. Al-Awami

Abstract
We develop a mathematical framework for the optimal dispatch of flexible water desalination plants (WDPs) as hybrid generator-load resources. WDPs integrate thermal generation, membrane-based controllable loads, and renewable energy sources, offering unique operational flexibility for power system operations. They can simultaneously participate in two markets: selling desalinated water to a water utility, and bidirectionally transacting electricity with the grid based on their net electricity demand. We formulate the dispatch decision problem of a profit-maximizing WDP, capturing operational, technological, and market-based coupling between water and electricity flows. The threshold-based structure we derive provides computationally tractable coordination suitable for large-scale deployment, offering operational insights into how thermal generation and membrane-based loads complementarily provide continuous bidirectional flexibility. The thresholds are analytically characterized in closed form as explicit functions of technology and tariff parameters. We examine how small changes in the exogenous tariff and technology parameters affect the WDP’s profit. Extensive simulations illustrate the optimal WDP’s operation, profit, and water-electricity exchange, demonstrating significant improvements relative to benchmark algorithms.

I-B

Main Contributions

Our main contribution is the analytical characterization of optimal electricity-water dispatch for WDPs operating as hybrid generator-load resources, enabling efficient grid integration while maintaining water production requirements. The threshold-based structure we derive provides computationally tractable coordination suitable for large-scale deployment, offering operational insights into how thermal generation and membrane-based loads complementarily provide continuous bidirectional flexibility.

From a power system operations perspective, our results demonstrate how WDPs can provide: (i) bidirectional dispatch flexibility (import, balanced, export modes) without energy storage; (ii) combined response from coordinated generation-load control; (iii) predictable dispatch through thresholds computable offline and independent of renewables; (iv) smooth zero-crossing transitions valuable for renewable integration.

II
WDP Framework and Dispatch Problem

From a grid operator perspective, the WDP represents a controllable hybrid resource capable of both generation and consumption. Unlike conventional resources with unidirectional power flow, the WDP can smoothly transition between grid import, balanced, and export states through coordinated control of TDP and RODP units. This section formalizes the WDP’s operational characteristics and dispatch problem.

We consider the problem of a WDP that jointly optimizes water and electricity dispatch to maximize its profits from transacting the two commodities with water and electric utilities (Fig.
1
). The WDP integrates thermal and membrane-based desalination units, colocated with renewables on-site. It engages in a unilateral transaction with the water utility, whereby it supplies water at an exogenously specified price, which may vary over time. In contrast, its interaction with the electricity utility is bilateral: the WDP purchases electric power when its demand exceeds local generation and exports surplus power otherwise. This arrangement is structurally analogous to virtual power plants
[
20
]
and grid-connected microgrids
[
19
]
. In both the water and electricity markets, the WDP is modeled as a price-taking agent, with its dispatch affecting quantities but not market-clearing prices.

As shown in Figures
1
and
2
, the WDP exhibits a noteworthy complementarity between water and electricity systems. Specifically, the TDP uses a fuel source to co-generate both water and electricity, while the RODP consumes electricity to produce water. As a result, the electrical load within the WDP is the RODP, whose demand is met through a combination of local generation from the TDP, on-site renewables, and electricity imported from the grid, with contributions varying based on availability and operating conditions.

The WDP’s operational timescale is set by its measurement and dispatching frequency, while transactions with water and electric utilities follow their billing periods (e.g., 15 min or hourly). To streamline the exposition, we assume the WDP’s dispatch period matches the utilities’ billing period, allowing a single time-step formulation.

Next, we present the modeling of the TDP, RODP, payment functions to the water and electricity utilities, and the WDP dispatch problem.

III-B

Optimal Dispatch Properties

We summarize five key structural properties of the optimal dispatch of the WDP, as characterized in Theorem
1

•

Renewable-independent thresholds: The optimal dispatch is governed by the four thresholds in (
6

g

•

Monotonic and piecewise-linear dispatch: The optimal TDP output decreases monotonically with

\Gamma_{\sf{NZ}_{1}}
and
\Gamma_{\sf{NZ}_{2}}

•

Closed-form characterization: The optimal setpoints

admit explicit closed-form expressions.

•

Operational interdependence: In the net-zero mode, the TDP and RODP interchangeably use the increasing renewables to maintain energy-balancedness.

\pi^{+}

III-C

RODP Optimal Dispatch

Although the RODP variables are linear in the objective of (

The off-grid mode

Because the TDP cost is convex with increasing marginal cost, for

At

g\in[\Gamma_{\sf NZ_{1}},\Gamma_{\sf NZ_{2}}]

g=\Gamma_{\sf NZ_{2}}

VI
Conclusion

We present a tractable analytical solution to the joint water-power dispatching problem for WDPs operating as hybrid generator-load resources, yielding valuable operational insights. The optimal dispatch exhibits a threshold-based structure determined solely by technology and tariff parameters, and in some modes is independent of electricity prices. The coupled optimal dispatch of thermal and RO desalination is a function of local renewable generation, and is divided into five modes. When renewable generation is very low or very high, the WDP maintains fixed water production while using renewables to offset electricity imports or reduce exports. In the three intermediate modes, the WDP is net-zero, and the surplus renewables are allocated to water production: first by reducing thermal output, then by increasing RO output up to capacity, and finally by further displacing thermal generation when its marginal cost exceeds the export value of electricity.

The structural insights and performance benefits demonstrated through simulations showcase the potential to guide efficient dispatch of integrated water-energy systems in increasingly renewable-rich and market-responsive environments.

Several avenues for future research follow from the limitations of this work. First, incorporating energy storage would enable temporal shifting of desalinated water and electricity. Second, the model adopts rather simplified water-electricity conversion functions; in practice, energy-use depends on ambient conditions such as salinity and temperature. Third, our analysis focuses on short-run operations and abstracts from long-term investment decisions. Extending the framework to a joint planning-operation model would inform optimal sizing of desalination technologies and renewable capacity.

The Lagrangian is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\sf{IM}}

\displaystyle=-\pi^{w}(\alpha_{r}q_{r}+\eta_{h}q_{h})+(\pi^{+}-\mu^{\sf{IM}})(q_{r}-q_{h}-g)

\displaystyle+C_{h}(p_{h})\quad+\underline{\lambda}^{\sf{IM}}_{r}(\underline{w}_{r}-\alpha_{r}q_{r})+\overline{\lambda}^{\sf{IM}}_{r}(\alpha_{r}q_{r}-\overline{w}_{r})

\displaystyle\quad+\underline{\lambda}^{\sf{IM}}_{h}(\underline{w}_{h}-\eta_{h}q_{h})+\overline{\lambda}^{\sf{IM}}_{h}(\eta_{h}q_{h}-\overline{w}_{h}),

with
\mu^{\sf{IM}}=0
because

is explicitly enforced here and the active case
\mu>0
is handled in
{\cal P}^{\sf{NZ}}
.

\underline{\lambda}^{\sf{IM}}_{r},\overline{\lambda}^{\sf{IM}}_{r},\underline{\lambda}^{\sf{IM}}_{h},\overline{\lambda}^{\sf{IM}}_{h}\geq 0
are the Lagrange multipliers of the RO and thermal minimum and maximum water output limits,

Note that the objective is linear w.r.t.
q_{r}
, over a convex set, and we are given
f^{\prime}_{r}\pi^{w}\leq\pi^{+}
, hence the optimal solution is
q_{r}^{\sf{IM}}=\underline{w}_{r}/\alpha_{r}
, which yields, using the conversion function,
w_{r}^{\sf{IM}}=\underline{w}_{r}
.

Stationarity in
q_{h}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}^{\sf{IM}}}{\partial q_{h}^{\sf{IM}}}=-\eta_{h}\pi^{w}-\pi^{+}+\frac{\eta_{h}}{\alpha_{h}}C^{\prime}_{h}(p_{h})-\eta_{h}\underline{\lambda}^{\sf{IM}}_{h}+\eta_{h}\overline{\lambda}^{\sf{IM}}_{h}=0,

leading to

q_{h}^{\sf{IM}}=\max\left\{\frac{\underline{w}_{h}}{\eta_{h}},\min\left\{\frac{\alpha_{h}}{\eta_{h}}D_{h}\left(\alpha_{h}\pi^{w}+\beta_{h}\pi^{+}\right),\frac{\overline{w}_{h}}{\eta_{h}}\right\}\right\},

and
w_{h}^{\sf{IM}}:=\eta_{h}q_{h}^{\sf{IM}}
.
The solution is feasible only if the resulting

which holds for all
g<q_{r}^{\sf{IM}}-q_{h}^{\sf{IM}}=:\Gamma_{\sf{IM}}
, under which

w_{h}^{\sf{IM}}=\max\left\{\underline{w}_{h},\min\left\{\alpha_{h}D_{h}\left(\alpha_{h}\pi^{w}+\beta_{h}\pi^{+}\right),\overline{w}_{h}\right\}\right\}.

Solution to
{\cal P}^{\sf{EX}}

Under
{\cal P}^{\sf{EX}}
, the problem becomes

\displaystyle\min_{q_{r},q_{h}}

\displaystyle\quad-\pi^{w}(\alpha_{r}q_{r}+\eta_{h}q_{h})+\pi^{-}(q_{r}-q_{h}-g)+C_{h}(p_{h}(q_{h}))

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad q_{r}-q_{h}-g\leq 0,

\displaystyle\quad\underline{w}_{r}\leq\alpha_{r}q_{r}\leq\overline{w}_{r},

\displaystyle\quad\underline{w}_{h}\leq\eta_{h}q_{h}\leq\overline{w}_{h}

By symmetry to the
{\cal P}^{\sf{IM}}
problem, we obtain the optimal solutions
w_{r}^{\sf{EX}}=\overline{w}_{r}
, and

\displaystyle w_{h}^{\sf{EX}}=\max\left\{\underline{w}_{h},\min\left\{\alpha_{h}D_{h}\left(\alpha_{h}\pi^{w}+\beta_{h}\pi^{-}\right),\overline{w}_{h}\right\}\right\}.

Using the conversion functions, we have
q_{r}^{\sf{EX}}=w_{r}^{\sf{EX}}/\alpha_{r}
and
q_{h}^{\sf{EX}}=w_{h}^{\sf{EX}}/\eta_{h}
.

Feasibility of the export region requires

z^{\sf{EX}}(g)=q_{r}^{\sf{EX}}-q_{h}^{\sf{EX}}-g<0,

which holds for all
g>q_{r}^{\sf{EX}}-q_{h}^{\sf{EX}}=:\Gamma_{\sf{EX}}

Solution to
{\cal P}^{\sf{NZ}}

Under
{\cal P}^{\sf{NZ}}
, the problem becomes

\displaystyle\min_{q_{r},q_{h}}

\displaystyle\quad-\pi^{w}(\alpha_{r}q_{r}+\eta_{h}q_{h})+C_{h}(p_{h})

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad q_{r}-q_{h}-g=0,

\displaystyle\quad\underline{w}_{r}\leq\alpha_{r}q_{r}\leq\overline{w}_{r},

\displaystyle\quad\underline{w}_{h}\leq\eta_{h}q_{h}\leq\overline{w}_{h}

The net-zero region enforces the equality

q_{r}-q_{h}-g=0,

(15)

which is imposed with multiplier
\mu^{\sf{NZ}}
. The Lagrangian for the NZ region reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\sf{NZ}}

\displaystyle=-\pi^{w}(\alpha_{r}q_{r}+\eta_{h}q_{h})+C_{h}(p_{h})+\mu^{\sf{NZ}}(q_{r}-q_{h}-g)

\displaystyle\quad+\underline{\lambda}_{r}^{\sf{NZ}}(\underline{w}_{r}-\alpha_{r}q_{r})+\overline{\lambda}_{r}^{\sf{NZ}}(\alpha_{r}q_{r}-\overline{w}_{r})

\displaystyle\quad+\underline{\lambda}_{h}^{\sf{NZ}}(\underline{w}_{h}-\eta_{h}q_{h})+\overline{\lambda}_{h}^{\sf{NZ}}(\eta_{h}q_{h}-\overline{w}_{h}),

with
\underline{\lambda}_{r}^{\sf{NZ}},\overline{\lambda}_{r}^{\sf{NZ}},\underline{\lambda}_{h}^{\sf{NZ}},\overline{\lambda}_{h}^{\sf{NZ}}\geq 0
.

Stationarity conditions.

Differentiating w.r.t.
q_{r}
and
q_{h}
and using
\partial p_{h}/\partial q_{h}=1/\beta_{h}
(from
w_{h}=\alpha_{h}p_{h}=\eta_{h}q_{h}
) yields

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{r}\pi^{w}+\mu^{\sf{NZ}}-\alpha_{r}\underline{\lambda}_{r}^{\sf{NZ}}+\alpha_{r}\overline{\lambda}_{r}^{\sf{NZ}},

(16)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\eta_{h}\pi^{w}-\mu^{\sf{NZ}}+\frac{\eta_{h}}{\alpha_{h}}C_{h}^{\prime}(p_{h})-\eta_{h}\underline{\lambda}_{h}^{\sf{NZ}}+\eta_{h}\overline{\lambda}_{h}^{\sf{NZ}}.

(17)

Rearranging (
16
)–(
17
) gives the standard pair

\displaystyle\mu^{\sf{NZ}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{r}\big(\pi^{w}+\underline{\lambda}_{r}^{\sf{NZ}}-\overline{\lambda}_{r}^{\sf{NZ}}\big),

(18)

\displaystyle\mu^{\sf{NZ}}

\displaystyle=\eta_{h}\!\left(-\pi^{w}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{h}}C_{h}^{\prime}(p_{h})-\underline{\lambda}_{h}^{\sf{NZ}}+\overline{\lambda}_{h}^{\sf{NZ}}\right).

(19)

Interior NZ solution (no bounds active)

If none of the bound multipliers are active

\underline{\lambda}_{r}^{\sf{NZ}}=\overline{\lambda}_{r}^{\sf{NZ}}=\underline{\lambda}_{h}^{\sf{NZ}}=\overline{\lambda}_{h}^{\sf{NZ}}=0
,
equating (
18
) and (
19
) yields

\alpha_{r}\pi^{w}=\eta_{h}\!\left(-\pi^{w}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{h}}C_{h}^{\prime}(p_{h})\right).

Solving for
C_{h}^{\prime}(p_{h})
and applying
p_{h}=D_{h}(\cdot)
gives

p_{h}^{\sf{NZ,int}}=D_{h}\!\Big(\alpha_{h}\big(\,\pi^{w}(1+\tfrac{\alpha_{r}}{\eta_{h}})\,\big)\Big),

which yields

q_{h}^{\sf{NZ,int}}=\beta_{h}\,D_{h}\!\Big(\alpha_{h}\pi^{w}+\beta_{h}\alpha_{r}\pi^{w}\Big)=q_{h}^{\ast}

(20)

Then the NZ equality (
15
) gives

q_{r}^{\sf{NZ,int}}=q_{h}^{\sf{NZ,int}}+g.

(21)

Consequently

w_{h}^{\sf{NZ,int}}=\eta_{h}q_{h}^{\sf{NZ,int}},\qquad w_{r}^{\sf{NZ,int}}=\alpha_{r}(\,q_{h}^{\sf{NZ,int}}+g\,).

(22)

The interior NZ solution is feasible only if both

\underline{w}_{h}\leq\eta_{h}q_{h}^{\sf{NZ,int}}\leq\overline{w}_{h},\qquad\underline{w}_{r}\leq\alpha_{r}(q_{h}^{\sf{NZ,int}}+g)\leq\overline{w}_{r}.

(23)

The first condition in (
23
) depends only on parameters; if it fails the interior solution is impossible for every
g
.
Solving the second condition for
g
yields the RODP-derived interval

\frac{\underline{w}_{r}}{\alpha_{r}}-q_{h}^{\ast}\;\leq\;g\;\leq\;\frac{\overline{w}_{r}}{\alpha_{r}}-q_{h}^{\ast}.

Accordingly define

\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{1}}}:=\frac{\underline{w}_{r}}{\alpha_{r}}-q_{h}^{\ast},\qquad\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{2}}}:=\frac{\overline{w}_{r}}{\alpha_{r}}-q_{h}^{\ast}.

(24)

Thus, if the TDP bound
\underline{w}_{h}\leq\eta_{h}q_{h}^{\ast}\leq\overline{w}_{h}
holds, the interior NZ solution is feasible exactly for

g\in[\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{1}}},\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{2}}}].

If the TDP bound does not hold, the interior NZ solution is infeasible for all
g
, and the NZ optimum must be a boundary solution for every
g
.

We now characterize which boundary (RODP or TDP, lower or upper) is active depending on
g
. There are two cases.

Case A: TDP bounds satisfied for the interior candidate.

Assume

\underline{w}_{h}\leq\eta_{h}q_{h}^{\ast}\leq\overline{w}_{h}.

Then the interior NZ solution is feasible for
g\in[\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{1}}},\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{2}}}]
. For
g
outside this interval the interior candidate violates the RODP bounds and the NZ optimum lies on the corresponding RODP boundary:

•

\mathbf{g<\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{1}}}}
(RODP lower-bound violation):
The interior candidate requires
\alpha_{r}(q_{h}^{\ast}+g)<\underline{w}_{r}
. Enforcing the RODP lower bound yields the RODP lower-boundary NZ solution

q_{r}=\frac{\underline{w}_{r}}{\alpha_{r}},\qquad q_{h}=\frac{\underline{w}_{r}}{\alpha_{r}}-g,

so the NZ pair is

w_{r}^{\mathrm{NZ,lb}}=\underline{w}_{r},\qquad w_{h}^{\mathrm{NZ,lb}}(g)=\eta_{h}\!\Big(\tfrac{\underline{w}_{r}}{\alpha_{r}}-g\Big).

This solution is valid provided the computed
w_{h}^{\mathrm{NZ,lb}}(g)
respects the TDP bounds:

\underline{w}_{h}\leq w_{h}^{\mathrm{NZ,lb}}(g)\leq\overline{w}_{h}
. If it violates TDP bounds then the (TDP) bound will be active as described in Case B below.

•

\mathbf{g>\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{2}}}}
(RODP upper-bound violation):
The interior candidate requires
\alpha_{r}(q_{h}^{\ast}+g)>\overline{w}_{r}
. Enforcing the RODP upper bound yields the RODP upper-boundary NZ solution

q_{r}=\frac{\overline{w}_{r}}{\alpha_{r}},\qquad q_{h}=\frac{\overline{w}_{r}}{\alpha_{r}}-g,

so the NZ pair is

w_{r}^{\mathrm{NZ,ub}}=\overline{w}_{r},\qquad w_{h}^{\mathrm{NZ,ub}}(g)=\eta_{h}\!\Big(\tfrac{\overline{w}_{r}}{\alpha_{r}}-g\Big),

valid provided
\underline{w}_{h}\leq w_{h}^{\mathrm{NZ,ub}}(g)\leq\overline{w}_{h}
. If this TDP check fails then revert to Case B behavior.

Case B: TDP bounds violated by the interior candidate.

If either
\eta_{h}q_{h}^{\ast}<\underline{w}_{h}
or
\eta_{h}q_{h}^{\ast}>\overline{w}_{h}
, then the interior NZ candidate is infeasible for all
g
. In this situation the NZ optimal solution must make
w_{h}
equal the violated TDP bound, and the NZ equality (
15
) then pins
q_{r}
. Concretely:

•

\eta_{h}q_{h}^{\ast}<\underline{w}_{h}
: the TDP lower bound is mandatory. Set
q_{h}=\underline{w}_{h}/\eta_{h}
and enforce
q_{r}=q_{h}+g
. The NZ pair is

w_{h}^{\sf{NZ}}=\underline{w}_{h},\qquad w_{r}^{\sf{NZ}}(g)=\alpha_{r}\!\Big(\tfrac{\underline{w}_{h}}{\eta_{h}}+g\Big).

Verify whether
w_{r}^{\sf{NZ}}(g)
respects RODP bounds; if it violates RODP bounds then the solution projects to the intersection of active bounds (e.g., both lower bounds).

•

\eta_{h}q_{h}^{\ast}>\overline{w}_{h}
: the TDP upper bound is mandatory. Set
q_{h}=\overline{w}_{h}/\eta_{h}
and enforce
q_{r}=q_{h}+g
. The NZ pair is

w_{h}^{\sf{NZ}}=\overline{w}_{h},\qquad w_{r}^{\sf{NZ}}(g)=\alpha_{r}\!\Big(\tfrac{\overline{w}_{h}}{\eta_{h}}+g\Big).

Again check RODP feasibility and project onto active intersections if needed.

Selection of the Optimal Region.

Let the IM and EX KKT solutions yield feasible pairs

(q_{r}^{\sf{IM}},q_{h}^{\sf{IM}})
and
(q_{r}^{\sf{EX}},q_{h}^{\sf{EX}})
, and define

\Gamma_{\sf{IM}}:=q_{r}^{\sf{IM}}-q_{h}^{\sf{IM}},\qquad\Gamma_{\sf{EX}}:=q_{r}^{\sf{EX}}-q_{h}^{\sf{EX}}.

Let the interior NZ solution (
20
)–(
21
) be feasible for
g\in[\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{1}}},\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{2}}}]
. Each region (IM, NZ, EX) corresponds to a convex restriction of the original
problem to a fixed sign of
z=q_{r}-q_{h}-g
. By convexity and Slater’s condition,
the region-wise KKT point is globally optimal within its region. Hence the
global optimizer is the region-consistent KKT solution.

The optimal regime as a function of
g
is therefore

\begin{cases}\textnormal{IM},&g<\Gamma_{\sf{IM}},\\[5.69054pt]
\textnormal{NZ-lower boundary},&\Gamma_{\sf{IM}}\leq g\leq\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{1}}},\\[5.69054pt]
\textnormal{NZ-interior},&\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{1}}}\leq g\leq\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{2}}},\\[5.69054pt]
\textnormal{NZ-upper boundary},&\Gamma_{\sf{NZ_{2}}}\leq g\leq\Gamma_{\sf{EX}},\\[5.69054pt]
\textnormal{EX},&g>\Gamma_{\sf{EX}}.\end{cases}

Remark (Equality cases).

The derivation above assumed strict inequalities between
f^{\prime}_{r}\pi^{w}
and the boundary prices
\pi^{+},\pi^{-}
. We now treat the two equality cases, which correspond to indifference of the objective to small variations of the RODP decision and hence to possible non-uniqueness of the RODP optimum.

(i)
f^{\prime}_{r}\pi^{w}=\pi^{+}
. From the stationarity condition for the IM problem the objective is flat in
q_{r}
at interior points, so the complementary slackness conditions allow either an interior
q_{r}
or an active RODP upper bound. In particular, both the IM-region boundary solution
w_{r}=\overline{w}_{r}
and any
w_{r}
in the feasible interval that satisfies the remaining KKT conditions are optimal. Equivalently, the limiting behaviour under an arbitrarily small increase of
\pi^{+}
(so that
f^{\prime}_{r}\pi^{w}>\pi^{+}
) selects
w_{r}=\overline{w}_{r}
; under an arbitrarily small decrease of
\pi^{+}
the lower boundary becomes preferred. Thus the set of optimal RODP outputs at equality is the convex set of KKT-feasible
w_{r}
.

(ii)
f^{\prime}_{r}\pi^{w}=\pi^{-}
. A symmetric statement holds: the EX-region stationarity is flat in
q_{r}
at equality and both
w_{r}=\underline{w}_{r}
and any KKT-feasible interior value are optimal; the limit under perturbations of
\pi^{-}
breaks the tie.

∎

Proof of Theorem
2

Define the maximized profit under Theorem
1
controls as

\Pi^{\ast}(g):=\Pi\big(w_{h}^{\ast}(g),w_{r}^{\ast}(g),q_{h}^{\ast}(g),q_{r}^{\ast}(g);g\big),

with
q_{h}^{\ast}=w_{h}^{\ast}/\eta_{h}
,
q_{r}^{\ast}=w_{r}^{\ast}/f_{r}^{\prime}
, and
z^{\ast}:=q_{r}^{\ast}-q_{h}^{\ast}-g
.
We compute
\dfrac{d\Pi^{\ast}}{dg}
by substituting the region-specific expressions for

(w_{h}^{\ast},w_{r}^{\ast},q_{h}^{\ast},q_{r}^{\ast})
from Theorem
1
and differentiating.

–
g<\Gamma_{\sf{IM}}
(IM).

From Theorem
1
we have
w_{h}^{\ast}=w_{h}^{\sf{IM}}
and
w_{r}^{\ast}=\underline{w}_{r}
.
Differentiating the profit yields

\frac{d\Pi^{\ast}}{dg}=-\pi^{+}(-1)=\pi^{+}.

Hence in the IM region the maximized profit increases with
g
at rate
\pi^{+}
.

–
g\in[\Gamma_{\sf{IM}},\Gamma_{\sf{NZ}_{1}})
(NZ)

From Theorem
1
we have

w_{r}^{\ast}(g)=\underline{w}_{r},\qquad w_{h}^{\ast}(g)=\eta_{h}\Big(\frac{\underline{w}_{r}}{f_{r}^{\prime}}-g\Big),

so

q_{r}^{\ast}=\frac{\underline{w}_{r}}{f_{r}^{\prime}},\qquad q_{h}^{\ast}=\frac{w_{h}^{\ast}}{\eta_{h}}=\frac{\underline{w}_{r}}{f_{r}^{\prime}}-g,

and therefore
z^{\ast}=q_{r}^{\ast}-q_{h}^{\ast}-g=0
, and the profit becomes

\Pi^{\ast}(g)=\pi^{w}\big(w_{h}^{\ast}+\underline{w}_{r}\big)-C_{h}(p_{h}^{\ast}),

which when differentiated gives

\frac{d\Pi^{\ast}}{dg}=\pi^{w}\frac{dw_{h}^{\ast}}{dg}-C_{h}^{\prime}(p_{h}^{\ast})\frac{dp_{h}^{\ast}}{dg}.

(26)

Since
w_{h}^{\ast}=\eta_{h}(\underline{w}_{r}/f_{r}^{\prime}-g)
we have
dw_{h}^{\ast}/dg=-\eta_{h}
, and
p_{h}^{\ast}=w_{h}^{\ast}/\alpha_{h}
so
dp_{h}^{\ast}/dg=-\eta_{h}/\alpha_{h}
. Substituting into (
26
) gives

\frac{d\Pi^{\ast}}{dg}=-\eta_{h}\pi^{w}+\frac{\eta_{h}}{\alpha_{h}}C_{h}^{\prime}(p_{h}^{\ast}).

Next use the stationarity (first-order) condition for the TDP at the optimal point. Writing the Lagrangian of the inner maximization (and denoting by
\mu^{\sf{NZ}}
the multiplier associated with the power balance) yields the
p_{h}
-stationarity

\alpha_{h}\pi^{w}-C_{h}^{\prime}(p_{h}^{\ast})-\beta_{h}\mu^{\sf{NZ}}=0,

hence

C_{h}^{\prime}(p_{h}^{\ast})=\alpha_{h}\pi^{w}-\beta_{h}\mu^{\sf{NZ}}.

Substitute this into the expression for
d\Pi^{\ast}/dg
to obtain

\frac{d\Pi^{\ast}}{dg}=-\eta_{h}\pi^{w}+\frac{\eta_{h}}{\alpha_{h}}\big(\alpha_{h}\pi^{w}-\beta_{h}\mu^{\sf{NZ}}\big)=-\eta_{h}\frac{\beta_{h}}{\alpha_{h}}\mu^{\sf{NZ}}.

Using
\beta_{h}/\alpha_{h}=1/\eta_{h}
(since
w_{h}=\alpha_{h}p_{h}=\eta_{h}q_{h}
and
\beta_{h}q_{h}=p_{h}
) the prefactors cancel and we obtain the clean identity

\;\frac{d\Pi^{\ast}}{dg}\;=\;\mu^{\sf{NZ}}\;.

Thus the slope equals the electricity-balance multiplier
\mu^{\sf{NZ}}
in this net-zero region.

It remains to bound
\mu^{\sf{NZ}}
. Examining the neighbouring regions shows that the two adjacent marginal values are
\pi^{+}
(IM side) and the NZ marginal value
f_{r}^{\prime}\pi^{w}
(NZ interior). Therefore the KKT/subgradient conditions imply

\mu^{\sf{NZ}}\in[\,\pi^{+},\,f_{r}^{\prime}\pi^{w}\,],

and combining with
\dfrac{d\Pi^{\ast}}{dg}=\mu^{\sf{NZ}}
yields
\frac{d\Pi^{\ast}}{dg}\in[\pi^{+},\,f_{r}^{\prime}\pi^{w}]

–
Monotonicity in prices and conversion factors.

By the envelope theorem, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Pi^{\ast}}{\partial\pi^{+}}=-[z^{\ast}]^{+}\leq 0,\frac{\partial\Pi^{\ast}}{\partial\pi^{-}}=-[z^{\ast}]^{-}\geq 0,\frac{\partial\Pi^{\ast}}{\partial\pi^{w}}=w_{h}^{\ast}+w_{r}^{\ast}\geq 0.

Increasing conversion factors
\alpha_{h},\beta_{h},\alpha_{r}
increases feasible water output per unit fuel/energy and therefore (since revenues grow while costs are convex in fuel) cannot decrease the maximized profit; hence the profit is nondecreasing in these parameters (and strictly increasing under mild interiority assumptions). ∎
