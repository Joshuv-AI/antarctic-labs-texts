Generative mechanisms of barrier to the internalization of motivation to participate in training in youth basketball players
Authors: Junmin Zhang, Yongfeng Liu, Xiaogang Zhang, Shuiyou Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The resistance to training among Chinese youth basketball players is becoming increasingly common, leading to low training effectiveness. Despite the growing significance of this issue, existing research has not systematically analyzed its underlying causes. To address this gap, this study employs Procedural Grounded Theory to explore the psychological mechanisms behind training resistance in these athletes. The findings indicate that coaches’ controlling behaviors undermine athletes’ basic psychological needs and trigger immediate resistance. When the coach’s power becomes excessive, even to the extent of having decision-making authority over athlete deselection, these behaviors may develop into an established coach-centered interaction pattern. This pattern thoroughly suppresses the satisfaction of athletes’ basic psychological needs and compels them to submit to the coach’s authority, triggering athletes’ negative emotions. If this accumulation of negative emotions exceeds the athletes’ psychological tolerance threshold, it erupts and manifests as resistant behaviors. In turn, these behaviors are perceived by the coach as a challenge to their authority, leading to intensified control and ultimately resulting in a vicious cycle. This resistance is identified as the “internalization barrier of training motivation.” The study recommends that coaches regularly assess athletes’ psychological states, technical abilities, and physical fitness using appropriate measurement tools. Such assessment helps identify early signs of burnout and anxiety and track progress in skills and fitness. Coaches should adjust training plans based on individual progress to tailor training loads and goals. Additionally, providing personalized feedback, recognizing individual efforts, and fostering peer support through team-building activities can enhance intrinsic motivation. In conclusion, this study constructs a theoretical framework for understanding training resistance and provides practical recommendations to improve coaching efficacy in Chinese youth basketball.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24896-7.

Introduction

Chinese professional youth basketball training is primarily undertaken by basketball clubs in various provinces and regions. These clubs adopt a centralized management model for their youth training teams, which often entails youth basketball players leaving home at an early age to participate in closed training. During the training process, Chinese basketball youth coaches are often influenced by traditional educational beliefs such as “no pain, no gain” and “strict teachers produce excellent students,” which affords them decision-making and control over the athletes’ behaviors. The training content typically focuses on improving players’ competition skills and physical fitness, while neglecting mental health education and equal interaction.

Preliminary investigations have revealed that Chinese youth basketball players generally exhibit resistance to training. During sessions, they often adopt a passive learning attitude. This resistance not only limits the scope and depth of the athletes’ personal development, but also results in the elimination of many potentially talented basketball players. Consequently, it restricts the efficiency of talent development within China’s youth basketball training system, leading to a long-term imbalance between the demand for high-quality reserve talent in professional basketball leagues and the supply of the current talent development system1.

Although existing research has confirmed the impact of coaching behaviors and the training environment on athletes’ training participation and engagement levels2–4, most studies have focused on the influence of single external factors on athletes’ training motivation. These studies have not fully elucidated the complex psychological mechanisms behind athletes’ resistance to training. Moreover, many of these conclusions are derived from theoretical perspectives, failing to account for the institutional variations in basketball talent development models across countries. As a result, they provide limited guidance for Chinese basketball youth coaches seeking to depart from the traditional “strictness equals efficiency” mindset. In the coach-led, high-pressure talent development model, what specific factors contribute to the complex psychological resistance to training exhibited by athletes? What are the underlying mechanisms driving these responses? These questions remain unanswered.

This study aims to clarify these psychological mechanisms and propose corresponding strategies, with the goal of systematically revealing the intrinsic causes and pathways of this psychological resistance from a theoretical perspective. It also seeks to provide more contextually adaptive and actionable coaching solutions to assist Chinese basketball youth coaches in guiding athletes towards more proactive training.

Literature review

In the field of sports psychology, athletes’ resistance or passion for training is often explained within the concept of motivation. Motivation serves as the intrinsic driving force behind athletes’ perseverance in arduous training or competition5. The internalization of training motivation can enhance athletes’ subjective satisfaction with their performance and strengthen their willingness to persist in training6, laying the foundation for improved athletic performance7. To better understand this process of motivation internalization, Self-Determination Theory (SDT) offers a comprehensive theoretical framework. Motivation is central to SDT, representing the internal psychological process that energizes and directs an individual’s behavior8. SDT classifies motivation into three categories: intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, and amotivation, highlighting the importance of fulfilling basic psychological needs (autonomy, competence, and relatedness) in fostering the development of intrinsic motivation9.

Related research indicates that behaviors demonstrating autonomy support from coaches and a task-involving peer motivational climate can effectively facilitate athletes’ internalization of training motivation10,11, whereas controlling coach behaviors are likely to intensify athletes’ controlled motivation, amplifying their perception of the coach’s control and leading to a negative cycle, keeping athletes in a state of controlled motivation12. Although these studies have theoretically analyzed the factors influencing athletes’ training motivation, they have not fully accounted for the complexities of Chinese sociocultural contexts and the intricate power dynamics between coaches and athletes. Thus, they fall short in offering a systematic explanation of the psychological mechanisms behind the resistance to training observed in Chinese youth athletes.

In China, influenced by traditional educational concepts such as “respect for teachers,” there is a growing tendency among parents, coaches, and fans to rationalize controlling coaching behaviors. Criticism and punishment imposed by coaches are often viewed as expressions of “responsible” discipline rather than mere control. However, during adolescence, athletes’ self-awareness begins to form, and they start to crave independence and autonomy in decision-making13. The stricter the coach’s demands, the more likely they are to provoke dissatisfaction among the athletes14,15. This inherent tension between traditional educational values and the growing need for autonomy in adolescents suggests that the resistance to training among Chinese youth basketball players is not a straightforward process, but a complex interaction between traditional educational ideals and individual developmental needs.

This study aims to clarify the underlying mechanisms of this complex issue. On the one hand, theory guides the development of practice16. To prevent the resistance to training among youth athletes, it is essential to clarify the psychological mechanisms involved and develop a theoretical framework suited to China’s sociocultural background and basketball talent development model. On the other hand, theory originates from practice16. To better understand the intricate psychological mechanisms of Chinese youth basketball players’ resistance to training, it is necessary to engage deeply with practice, investigate the real experiences of these athletes, and conduct a systematic analysis. Therefore, grounded theory, with its methodological focus on “abstracting concepts and refining theories from practice,” was selected as the primary research method for this study17. This approach can build theoretical models through the gradual refinement of raw data, providing deep insights into the complex psychological mechanisms of resistance to training among Chinese youth basketball players.

Research design

Research method

Grounded theory, originally proposed by Glaser and Strauss, is a research approach that builds theory from the bottom up by inductively categorizing concepts from social phenomena and specific contexts. It includes three versions: constructivist, classic, and Procedural Grounded Theory (PGT)18. This study employs PGT as the research method, with the aim of leveraging its systematic coding process to construct a comprehensive theoretical model, thereby enabling an in-depth analysis of the complex psychological mechanisms underlying training resistance among Chinese youth basketball players. The selection of PGT is justified by the following reasons.

Firstly, PGT has a highly structured coding process that includes open, axial, and selective coding. This process guides researchers in deriving explanatory concepts and categories from rich raw data, which provides a detailed and explicit paradigm for analyzing the complex psychological mechanisms underlying training resistance among Chinese youth basketball players, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive theory.

Secondly, PGT offers a systematic approach to data analysis, incorporating a series of concepts such as causal conditions, phenomena, context, intervening conditions, action/interaction strategies, and consequences. This framework enables researchers to systematically analyze raw data and theorize the complex interrelationships within it19. Such an analytical approach facilitates a deep and holistic understanding of the complete picture of the complex psychological mechanisms involved in training resistance.

Sample selection and materials collection

Theoretical sampling is a form of sampling in qualitative research which entails jointly collecting and analyzing data through the constant comparison of concepts and categories emerging from the raw data, in order to determine what data to collect next and where to find them, thereby contributing to theory development17. Through this constant comparative analysis, theoretical sampling allowed us to deliberately seek out participants from diverse backgrounds, to ensure that the concepts and categories emerging from the data provide a deep explanation for the research problem.

This study was initiated on June 17, 2023. The first author compiled training diaries written during his participation in a Chinese youth basketball program from 2010 to 2022. These diaries contained his authentic reflections and emotional experiences. Using these materials, initial coding was performed to develop preliminary concepts and categories. Guided by these emerging categories, this study employed theoretical sampling to recruit interviewees from the provinces with mature youth training systems, large participant bases, and high competitive levels, such as Shanxi, Guangdong, and Sichuan, while also requiring all interviewees to have at least three years of training experience. This sampling approach enabled the collection of authentic experiences more comprehensively from youth basketball players across different provinces, to ensure representativeness and diversity in theory-building, thereby enhancing the explanatory depth of the primary data in addressing the research questions.

The preliminary analysis of training diaries commenced on June 17, 2023, followed by the initiation of interviews, audio transcription, and data analysis on October 26, 2023. All analytical work was completed by March 12, 2024. Ultimately, 39 athlete respondents were recruited, and three volumes of diary materials were organized, resulting in a total of over 117,000 words of textual data. The data collection was completed on March 12, 2024. It should be noted that the upper age limit for participation in the Chinese Youth Basketball League is 21 years old20. Therefore, to ensure the comprehensiveness and integrity of the raw data, this study did not strictly adhere to the World Health Organization’s age definition of adolescents (10–19 years) when recruiting respondents21. Instead, the upper age limit for respondents was set at 21 years old (see Table 1).

Interview question design

Preliminary analysis of training diaries revealed that Chinese youth basketball players do not typically begin their training with resistance. Rather, their motivation undergoes a dynamic transition from passion to resistance. Furthermore, the closed management model characteristic of China’s youth basketball training system limits athletes’ interactions primarily to their coaches, with minimal contact with individuals outside this environment. This suggests that factors influencing athletes’ training motivation may be directly related to their coaches.

During the design of the interview protocol, core concepts of motivation and basic psychological needs from SDT served as sensitizing concepts. To ensure that the interview questions comprehensively addressed the core theoretical dimensions identified in the preliminary analysis, we drew upon well-established items from empirically validated instruments, including the Student Athletes’ Motivation toward Sports and Academics Questionnaire (SAMSAQ)22, the Coach-Athlete Relationship Maintenance Questionnaire (CARM-Q)23, and the Coach-created Empowering and Disempowering Motivational Climate Questionnaire (EDMCQ-C)24. This approach aimed to provide participants with ample space for expression while maintaining the theoretical depth and validity of the interview questions, thereby laying the groundwork for obtaining rich primary materials with explanatory depth. It is crucial to emphasize that SDT was used solely as a tool to inform the question design, not as a predetermined coding or analytical framework, and the actual coding process remained strictly open and exploratory. The specific content of the interview questions is presented in Table 2.

The final interview guide consists of five thematic directions and 15 semi-structured questions. Among these, the directions “Level of Passion for Basketball” and “Initial Motivation for Playing Basketball” were designed to explore the dynamic evolution of motivation among Chinese youth basketball players and the factors influencing this process. The direction “Subjective Perceptions of the Coach-Athlete Relationship” was designed to investigate power dynamics and interaction patterns between coaches and athletes. Finally, questions in the directions of “The Most Frustrating Aspect of Training” and “Perception of the Team Atmosphere” were intended to capture athletes’ authentic experiences of training, competition, and daily life within a collective environment.

Analysis process

The coding of the raw data was completed in Microsoft Excel. The analysis of raw materials strictly follows the open coding, axial coding, and selective coding of PGT. During the open coding phase, the material content was read line by line, with initial concepts extracted from the original sentences related to the research topic and subsequently categorized. In the axial coding phase, further induction was performed based on the logical relationships between categories to identify main categories. During the selective coding phase, the core category was identified by clarifying the logical relationships between the main categories, forming a storyline that leads to the construction of the theoretical model. The coding process followed the principle of theoretical saturation, which means that when the categories generated from the material content could all be included in the existing main categories, it was considered that the coding content had reached theoretical saturation. Materials collection could then be stopped, and the theoretical framework could be constructed (see Fig. 1). Detailed information on the coding process is provided in the S1 Appendix.

Open coding

Open coding requires researchers to continuously refine and generalize key sentences in the raw materials, extracting concepts that have explanatory power for the research question and forming initial categories through the induction of these concepts19, thus laying the foundation for theory generation.

In this study, we read the empirical materials collected earlier word by word and sentence by sentence, extracted the connotations of the original sentences to form concepts, and classified the concepts by clarifying their logical relationships. Ultimately, 96 concepts and 20 initial categories were formed. For example, concepts such as “Uniform dress code” and “Confiscate cell phones” both involve coaches’ control over athletes’ daily lives and were thus categorized under the initial category of “Daily Life Control.” Concepts like “Sarcasm” and “Isolation” both pertain to emotional abuse by coaches toward athletes and were categorized under the initial category of “Emotional Abuse” (see Table 3).

Axial coding

Axial coding is the second step in the data analysis process of grounded theory. It aims to clarify the internal relationships among initial concepts and categories through classification and comparison, forming more generalized and higher-level core categories19, thereby constructing a theoretical framework.

In this study, by clarifying and analyzing the logical relationships among the 20 initial categories, a total of 8 main categories were identified. For instance, the initial categories of “Life Control” “Emotional Abuse” “Mandatory Training Management” and “Utilitarian Coaching Behavior” all involve strict control and constraints imposed by coaches on athletes, which can restrict the fulfillment of athletes’ psychological needs. Therefore, these were categorized under the main category of “Need-Restrictive Behaviors.” The initial categories of “Low Relationship Quality” “Team Alienation” and “Deficiency in Self-Value” all represent specific manifestations of athletes’ unmet belonging needs and were thus categorized under the main category of “Sense of Belonging Stripped” (see Table 4).

Selective coding

Selective coding involves identifying a core category that integrates all the main categories into a coherent whole by clarifying the logical relationships among them19, thereby establishing the logical connections between the core category and the main categories, forming a logically consistent and complete storyline, and ultimately accomplishing the construction of the theory.

In this study, through an in-depth analysis of the logical relationships among the eight main categories, the core category was determined to be “Internalization Barrier of Training Motivation in Youth Basketball Players”. The storyline constructed around this core category is as follows: coaches’ controlling behaviors undermine athletes’ basic psychological needs and trigger resistance in the short term. When the coach’s power becomes excessive, even to the extent of having decision-making authority over athlete deselection, these behaviors may develop into an established coach-centered interaction pattern, which thoroughly suppresses the satisfaction of athletes’ basic psychological needs and compels them to submit to the coach’s authority. Under such circumstances, athletes can only passively accept rigid training regimens, excessive training loads, and unrealistic goals, thereby further undermining their basic psychological needs. When the accumulation of negative emotions exceeds the athletes’ psychological tolerance threshold, it erupts and manifests as resistant behaviors. In turn, these behaviors are perceived by the coach as a challenge to their authority, leading to intensified control and ultimately resulting in a vicious cycle. This creates a vicious cycle that causes internalization barrier of training motivation in athletes.

Based on the above analysis, this study constructed a theoretical model of internalization barrier of training motivation in youth basketball players (see Fig. 2).

As indicated by the model, the internalization barrier of training motivation refers to a special psychological state in which youth trainees, influenced by both internal and external factors, experience long-term unmet basic psychological needs, and face a barrier in the internalization process of training motivation, and gradually develop aversion to participating in training. The emergence of the internalization barrier of training motivation not only leads to trainees being unwilling to think proactively during training but also has a negative impact on athletes’ mental health, thereby reducing the quality and efficiency of basketball youth training.

Trustworthiness

This study included 39 participants. During the 36th interview, no new categories emerged from the raw materials. Therefore, to ensure the completeness and validity of the result, three athletes were recruited for supplementary interviews. The subsequent materials were analyzed by the previously established procedure of applying open, axial, and selective coding. The consistency of the coding process was verified through cross-checking among the research team. All derived concepts were subsumed within the existing core categories, with no new main categories emerged, and no new relationships between the main categories were identified. Additionally, in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ)25, we engaged in self-reflection and collectively discussed the coding results with the participants to confirm that no new main categories emerged and that no main categories were omitted. Therefore, it can be inferred that the data coding in this study reached a relatively saturated state.

The model of motivation internalization barrier among youth basketball players and the connotations of its dimensions

In China’s youth basketball training system, the majority of coaches are former professional athletes who transitioned directly into coaching. Their pedagogical approach is predominantly shaped by their own training experience as players rather than formal education in sports science or pedagogy. Deeply influenced by traditional notions of authority and discipline, such as “a strict teacher makes a good student,” they often equate rigorous control with effective coaching26. In their practice, they frequently restrict athletes’ behaviors to consolidate their own authority and dominate interactions with athletes. Furthermore, within the closed management model of Chinese youth basketball training system, coaches assume dual roles as both trainers and life supervisors.

Furthermore, the Chinese youth basketball training system is marked by a high elimination rate, which fosters an intensely competitive atmosphere among athletes. A single poor performance in a match may prompt coaches to develop a negative perception of an athlete, potentially leading to diminished playing time and ultimately deselection from the team. This institutional structure amplifies their authority, thereby extending their control from the athletic domain into the realm of daily living, thereby creating a comprehensive control environment that profoundly impacts athletes’ psychological development.

Coaching level: the negative impact of coaching authority

Needs-restricting behaviors

Needs-restricting behaviors are a collective term for coaches’ emotional abuse, life management, and coercive management behaviors towards athletes, referring to a series of control measures taken by coaches to achieve their coaching goals. While extant literature has indicated that such behaviors heighten athlete burnout through controlled motivation or amotivation27, induce negative emotional states28, and cause mental health impairments29, this study extends this understanding by revealing that these behaviors primarily operate through the frustration of athletes’ basic psychological needs, particularly the need for competence.

In China’s youth basketball training system, the coaches are particularly strict with team members. In daily life, they restrict the time that team members can use their mobile phones and go out, and make rigid requirements on team members’ clothing, hairstyles, etc. In training, controlling behaviors such as corporal punishment, sarcasm, and isolation of team members have become the norm. This behavior pattern restricts the satisfaction of youth basketball players’ basic psychological needs, and leads them to resist training. As interviewees said:The coach often scolds me. I feel that no matter how hard I try, I can’t meet his requirements. Sometimes I lose confidence and don’t want to train with him anymore (A17).

Within China’s youth basketball training system, most coaches struggle to transition between their roles as sports instructors and life supervisors, consistently maintaining a stern and authoritative leadership persona. Athletes are expected to comply with coaches’ demands both during training and in daily life. In the aforementioned case, the coach’s reprimands elicit persistent negative feedback, which athletes interpret as “unattainable expectations that cannot be met through effort.” In the short term, this perception leads athletes to perceive themselves as prone to consistent failure, thereby resulting in the frustration of their competence need. Furthermore, repeated experiences of failure will also lead the athlete to perceive his efforts as futile and begin to exhibit resistance and aversion toward the coach. By stating “not wanting to train with him anymore,” the interviewee is not rejecting basketball, but rather avoiding the needs-restricting behaviors that cause psychological harm.

During adolescence, athletes possess a limited capacity to regulate negative emotions. Under such conditions, coaches’ persistent criticism continuously suppresses the satisfaction of athletes’ psychological needs, diminishes their enthusiasm for basketball participation, and gradually erodes their self-confidence. According to SDT, the internalization of an individual’s motivation depends on the degree to which the external environment satisfies their basic psychological needs30. Apparently, Chinese youth basketball training coaches mistakenly regard strict management and control of athletes as the only way to help them improve their competitive abilities, thus creating an external environment centered on coaching authority, making it difficult to meet the basic psychological needs of athletes during the interaction with them. As a result, the internalization process of athletes’ training motivation is hindered, leading to a state of controlled motivation or even amotivation.

Coach-centered interaction

Subject to the centralized and unified management paradigm, the majority of Chinese youth basketball players commence their training in a closed system environment from an early age. Throughout their professional trajectories, they typically affiliate with merely 3 to 5 professional basketball clubs. Coaches thus become the most frequently encountered and closely interacting figures in their daily lives. Whether in daily life, training, or competition, Chinese youth basketball coaches dictate all decisions and rarely tolerate any noncompliance. This relatively constrained or isolated social environment makes athletes hypersensitive to coaches’ attitudes and behaviors. As illustrated by an interviewee:With 57 s remaining in the fourth quarter of last year’s league match, trailing by 9 points, the coach instructed us to abandon the offensive play. Driven by an intense desire to win, I took successive shots, which led to my being verbally abused and physically assaulted. Since then, I have lived in constant fear of the coach’s criticism, so I rarely dare to implement my own ideas in training or competition. I am often anxious about not meeting expectations in any aspect of life or sport, and I hesitate to apply trained skills in games based on my own judgment. More often than not, I just follow the coach’s instructions. (A3).

More often than not, I just follow the coach’s instructions. (A3).

As posited by SDT, when the external environment fails to satisfy individuals’ fundamental psychological needs, external regulation and introjective regulation of motivation occur, resulting in the formation of controlled motivation31. In the aforementioned scenario, the coach perceives the athlete’s autonomous decision-making as a challenge to their authority and consequently employs escalating control behaviors to suppress the athlete and enforce compliance. In the short term, athletes may exhibit resistance toward the coach’s control. However, constrained by the coach’s decision-making power over athlete deselection and the intensely competitive environment, athletes are compelled to submit to the coach’s authority and suppress their own autonomy. This dynamic thoroughly undermines the athlete’s autonomy, leading them to unconsciously believe that autonomous initiative in life, training, or competition will elicit punitive responses from the coach. This shift marks how athletes begin to transition from passive resistance against coaches’ control to active suppression of their own needs, ultimately forming a psychological defense mechanism.

The inner demand layer: negative impacts of constrained basic psychological needs

Tendency of external attribution

External attribution is defined as the process by which individuals ascribe the causes of behaviors or events to environmental factors external to themselves, including others’ actions, task complexity, and serendipity32. When individuals attribute their circumstances to uncontrollable external elements, it triggers a cascade of persistent negative emotions, undermines their self-efficacy beliefs, and precipitates detrimental psychological states such as motivational exhaustion and learned helplessness33. Long-term exposure to a coach-dominated environment leads athletes to attribute negative outcomes in training and competition to coaches’ attitudes and behaviors. When subjected to persistent negative feedback including reprimands and physical punishment, this external attribution tendency leads to the internal accumulation of negative emotions, ultimately culminating in overt resistance toward the coach. As one interviewee remarked:My coach publicly labeled me as weak and lacking in physicality in front of the entire team. In response, I intentionally injured an opponent to prove otherwise (A24).

My coach publicly labeled me as weak and lacking in physicality in front of the entire team. In response, I intentionally injured an opponent to prove otherwise (A24).

In this case, the athlete firmly believed that being labeled as “lacking in physicality” reflected the coach’s bias rather than a deficiency in his own ability or effort, thereby generating resentment toward the coach. According to Psychological Reactance Theory, when individuals perceive that their behavioral freedom or expectations regarding outcomes are threatened, removed, or restricted, they will experience a motivational state of psychological reactance34. In the aforementioned scenario, the coach’s public disparagement of the athlete’s competitive ability exceeded the athlete’s psychological tolerance threshold, severely frustrating his need for competence and damaging his self-esteem, thereby activating a state of psychological reactance. Driven by this need to demonstrate his capability to the coach and regain competence, the athlete intentionally injured an opponent. This led the athlete to divert his focus from the game to resisting the coach. Such a pattern of external attribution heightens athletes’ susceptibility to coaches’ attitudes and behaviors, making them more prone to negative emotions and resistance to coaching practices, which severely undermines training effectiveness.

Unfortunately, Chinese youth basketball coaches place great emphasis on maintaining authority during instruction. They often interpret these negative emotions among athletes as a challenge to their own authority, which in turn leads them to intensify control over athletes, ultimately resulting in a vicious cycle. Consequently, it significantly impedes the internalization process of training motivation, creating a detrimental cycle that undermines athletes’ intrinsic drive for continuous improvement.

Mutual constraint of basic psychological needs

Autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs collectively facilitate the internalization of individual motivation through their interactive effects35. In coach-controlled training environments, youth athletes’ need for autonomy is initially suppressed, which in turn leads to the frustration of their needs for competence and relatedness. The resulting negative behaviors further prompt coaches to reinforce their controlling strategies, ultimately forming a vicious cycle that becomes difficult to break. As reported by the interviewees:Before a game, the coach said he would assign playing time based on our condition, giving everyone a chance to play. Even though I was in great shape, I ended up on the bench for most of the game barely getting to play. This made me lose trust in him, as it seemed he didn’t keep his word.

Autonomy occupies a unique position in the set of three needs. Satisfying the need for autonomy is essential for behavior to become self-determined and for achieving optimal outcomes associated with self-determination35. In the case described above, the coach’s failure to keep his promise led the athlete to develop an external attribution. Namely, the belief that diligent training does not directly lead to competitive rewards, rather it is the coach’s approval that ultimately grants the opportunity to perform, even when such recognition may not reflect true athletic capability. This controlling behavior deprived the athlete of a sense of control over behavioral outcomes, thereby undermining their need for autonomy.

Subsequently, the frustration of autonomy rapidly undermines the need for competence. When athletes who perceive themselves to be in peak condition are denied playing time, they inevitably experience self-doubt regarding their abilities. According to SDT, the need for competence refers to the sense that an individual can proficiently master a certain ability, which is manifested as the individual’s desire for self-growth and the pursuit of self-worth realization36. However, this action by the coach denies the athlete an avenue for realizing self-worth, which in turn impairs their sense of self-efficacy.

The dual frustration of autonomy and competence inhibits the athlete’s need for relatedness. As mentioned previously, coaches in the Chinese youth basketball training system often assume a dominant role. This relational dynamic makes control exerted by the coach more likely to undermine the foundation of trust between the coach and the athletes, leading the latter to experience strong feelings of alienation and perceived injustice. Athletes may come to believe that they are not recognized or accepted by the coach, and that they are not valued members of the team.

However, coaches often perceive such behaviors as challenges to their authority, prompting them to tighten their control over the athletes. This further deprives athletes of their psychological needs, exacerbates their negative emotions and behaviors, and perpetuates a self-reinforcing vicious cycle.

Training level: negative impacts of fixed training arrangement

Fixed training contents and loads

The investigation reveals that despite variations in training programs across China, most coaches at professional youth basketball teams mandate fixed training regimens. As an interviewee said:A year ago, the coach established the standard for the ‘intensive shooting’ drill for us, which was to make 12 out of 15 two-point shots or 8 out of 13 three-point shots within one minute. This standard has remained unchanged ever since. Interestingly, during the usual practice of the ‘one-minute intensive shooting’ drill, I can meet the standard and even exceed the task requirements. However, my shooting percentage in games is not high. Moreover, every time my shooting percentage in games is low, the coach will criticize me. Now, I don’t even dare to choose shooting opportunities based on my own judgment during the game (A7).

In the above case, the coach aimed to help athletes master specific basketball skills through extensive repetitive training. However, the coach failed to account for the differences between training and games and lacked reflection on the athletes’ real feelings. This oversight creates a fundamental mismatch between the rigid training model and the dynamic, unpredictable nature of basketball competition. During training, athletes’ ability to “meet or even exceed the task requirements” stems merely from their adaptation to fixed routines, not from a genuine enhancement of competitive abilities. This false impression leads athletes to equate their training level with their actual competitive level. When athletes participate in real game scenarios that require dynamic decision making, owing to the persistent disconnect between training and competition in content and load, they will struggle to adapt to the changes in the game situation, resulting in a decline in their game performance. This not only impedes the development of competitive abilities but also provokes coach dissatisfaction and criticism, which adversely affects the athletes’ motivational internalization process.

The impropriety of training goals

Specific, attainable, and challenging goals can ignite an individual’s motivation37. Nevertheless, when the coaches establish training goals, they often overlook the physical and mental development traits of youth athletes and set overly ambitious training objectives. During the training process, athletes are unable to meet these objectives despite expending all their efforts, and they are compelled to persevere by the authority of the coaches. As time elapses, it can result in sports injuries and mental health issues. As the interviewees described:During the winter training period, the small forwards, power forwards, and centers of our team practiced lower limb explosive power and jumping ability almost every day. By the end of the winter training, 7 out of the 16 athletes on the entire team suffered from fatigue-induced bone injuries of varying degrees. Based on the Long Term Athlete Development (LTAD) model, the development of athletes is a continuous and long term process38. Undoubtedly, achieving outstanding athletic performance is a key objective of competitive sports. However, in China’s youth basketball training, coaches pursue the rapid enhancement of athletes’ competitive abilities through high-intensity exercise loads, without giving due consideration to the long-term development of the athletes. In the aforementioned cases, the coaches’ pursuit of short-term competitive benefits has led to the absence of the educational function in youth basketball training. The excessively rigorous goals they set thereby transform training from an engaging challenge into a pressure to be passively endured. When athletes become aware that their self worth cannot be realized due to the constraints of external rules, they will experience intense negative emotions, amplify the negative impact of external rules on their basic psychological needs. This culminates in a range of negative psychological states, which severely impede the internalization of their training motivation.

Response strategies

Establishing an athlete-centered scientific evaluation and feedback system

Coaches should recognize athletes as the central focus of sports training and adopt an athlete-centered approach committed to facilitating their holistic development. During the coaching process, it is essential to move beyond a “win-only” mentality and instead adopt a long-term perspective. When fostering the long-term development of youth athletes, coaches should not regard personal experience as an authoritative guide. Given that individuals often perceive the same issues or phenomena differently, relying solely on experiential knowledge in training will inevitably result in a lack of scientific rigor and comprehensiveness in training content. Therefore, coaches need to implement a more scientific approach by regularly assessing athletes’ development in various athletic competencies and making targeted adjustments to subsequent training plans.

First, coaches can use established psychological measurement scales (such as the Athlete Burnout Questionnaire, ABQ39, Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale, GAD-740, Sport motivation scale-6 SMS-641), to measure specific psychological indicators such as anxiety, burnout, and training motivation every 6–8 weeks. Based on these results, coaches can take appropriate measures to assist athletes showing signs of anxiety, burnout, or other negative psychological states, encouraging them to make positive adjustments. For athletes whose training motivation is predominantly influenced by internal or external regulation, coaches should increase the frequency of positive reinforcement, encourage peer support, and ensure that training objectives are achieved without resorting to physical punishment, sarcasm, or emotional abuse. For example, during defensive drills, coaches can say, “If you maintain a high pace and communicate effectively while defending, you will be rewarded” or “You are sure to succeed,” rather than saying, “If you don’t defend quickly enough, you will have to run.” This approach helps foster a subjective sense of belonging to the team, guiding the internalization of training motivation.

Second, monthly testing sessions should be scheduled to scientifically measure athletes’ technical abilities (such as shooting accuracy, passing precision, and dribbling speed) and physical fitness (such as vertical jump height, shuttle runs, squats, bench press, deadlift, and pull-ups). Based on the testing data, the training plan should be adjusted accordingly. After every 12 testing sessions, coaches can evaluate athletes’ progress by comparing data from the most recent 6 tests with the first 6, calculating the positive or negative change and developing an individualized progress chart for each athlete. This ensures objective evaluations, avoiding subjectivity in assessments and maintaining fairness. Coaches may also hold team meetings to publicly recognize athletes who have shown significant progress, thereby further promoting the internalization of training motivation. It is important to note that progress evaluation should be based on individual data over a given time period, rather than comparing athletes, in order to avoid fostering unhealthy competition among teammates.

Lastly, coaches should allow athletes a certain degree of autonomy in their daily lives. For example, after dinner, athletes should be allowed to use their mobile phones or other entertainment devices during their rest time, with the coach retrieving them only before bedtime to ensure proper rest and recovery from fatigue.

Creating a Two-Way communication model between coaches and athletes

The motivation for players to train doesn’t come from the coach’s fear, but from their happiness42. During the training process, coach’s caring behavior and qualities, such as self-confidence and optimism, can create a positive training atmosphere, help players overcome negative emotions, stimulate their sense of cooperation and independent decision-making, and alleviate their frustration and loneliness when competing43.

During rest periods, coaches should organize short group discussions (no longer than 60 min, with each athlete having approximately 5 min to speak if there are 15 team members) to encourage athletes to express their true feelings about training, offer suggestions for the training plan, or share any thoughts they may have for their teammates, coaches, or the entire team. Coaches should actively listen, take notes, and reflect on the feedback provided. When formulating the training plan, coaches should set clear training objectives, communicate with athletes to understand and analyze their real feelings, selectively incorporate their suggestions into the training arrangements, and achieve a consensus among the team to create a targeted, comprehensive, and scientifically designed training plan that enhances training efficiency.

Before each training session, coaches should first explain which athlete’s suggestions were adopted, describe the adjustments made, and clarify the rationale behind these changes and the specific goals they expect athletes to achieve. This approach not only preserves the coach’s authority but also addresses athletes’ psychological needs. Additionally, coaches can designate a practice session on rest days or once a week for a joint competition or practice with both the coach and athletes, aiming to enhance communication and strengthen the relationship between the coach and the team.

Implementing teaching strategies focused on guiding athletes to think independently

“Teaching one how to fish is more advisable than giving them a fish”. Coaches should not only interact with players but also impart scientific training knowledge and methods. They should cultivate players’ independent thinking, their ability to discriminate right from wrong, and their capacity for independent training. They should also enhance their knowledge and comprehension of training and effectively bridge the talent cultivation of the multi-level training system. This is vital to prevent circumstances where players’ demands for attribution remain unfulfilled due to their difficulties in adapting to the new environment.

Firstly, coaches can utilize a 20-minute session after weekly lunch breaks to educate athletes on sports training theory, integrating learning with training. Building on this, once a week, a training session should be designated as a self-directed training class, where different athletes take on the role of the head coach and design their own training plans. This encourages athletes to apply both their training experience and the theoretical knowledge they have gained during these sessions.

Second, during formal training sessions, coaches can design situational simulations based on typical game scenarios, focusing on developing athletes’ decision-making abilities and adaptability. For instance, simulating a situation where the game is nearing its end with the team trailing by 2 or 3 points, and requiring the attacking team to score within 3 s. The defense must then adopt a specified defensive strategy (such as zone defense or man-to-man defense). After the attacking team successfully completes 10 offensive attempts, control of the ball is switched. During the practice, coaches should record the number of successful scores during these 10 attempts and invite the athletes who scored most frequently to share their decision-making processes after the session. Coaches should also encourage athletes to discuss strategies for responding to different defensive formations.

Conclusions

This research utilized PGT to conduct hierarchical coding and systematic analysis of the original data, constructed a model of motivation internalization barrier among youth basketball players. The findings indicate that the internalization barrier of training motivation refers to a special psychological state in which youth trainees, influenced by both internal and external factors, experience long-term unmet basic psychological needs, and face barrier in the internalization process of training motivation, and gradually develop aversion to participating in training. To address this, coaches should regularly assess athletes’ psychological well-being and competitive abilities progress, adjusting training loads and goals to meet individual needs. Providing personalized feedback and fostering a supportive team environment to help athletes enhance intrinsic motivation and reduce resistance.

In conclusion, this study provides a theoretical framework that explains how and why training resistance develops and perpetuates, offering a significant theoretical lens for future research in sports psychology, and provides practical recommendations to improve coaching effectiveness in Chinese youth basketball.

Limitations and future directions

This study is primarily situated within the context of the Chinese youth basketball system. Although we constructed a comprehensive theoretical model following the principle of theoretical saturation, its applicability to other cultural and training environments may be limited. As “All theories and models are sub-optimal to some degree44,” future research could use the Delphi method to further validate the theoretical model of barrier to the internalization of training motivation.
