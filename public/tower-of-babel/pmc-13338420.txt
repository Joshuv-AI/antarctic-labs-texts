Hierarchical knowledge distillation framework for efficient node influence prediction in large-scale complex networks
Authors: Xiaomo Yu, Jiajia Liu, Ling Tang, Jie Mi, Long Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNode influence prediction is fundamental to epidemic control, viral marketing, and infrastructure resilience, yet traditional susceptible-infected-recovered (SIR) simulations require computational operations, rendering real-time applications infeasible for large-scale networks. This paper presents HKD–NIP, a hierarchical knowledge distillation framework that achieves simulation-level accuracy while reducing computational time by 89% and required SIR simulations by 90% through strategic use of only 5–10% labeled nodes. Our dual-teacher architecture employs a general teacher trained on 36 diverse synthetic networks spanning Barabási–Albert, Erdős–Rényi, and Watts–Strogatz topologies to capture transferable structural patterns, while a domain-specific teacher fine-tunes this knowledge using stratified sampling. A lightweight LightGCN-based student model distills knowledge through soft label supervision and contrastive representation alignment, enabling sub-second inference. The hierarchical two-stage distillation is theoretically motivated: the general-to-domain teacher cascade reduces the structural domain gap incrementally, enabling the student to exploit both universal and network-specific propagation patterns—a property that single-stage distillation cannot achieve. Experiments across eight real-world datasets demonstrate Kendall’s of 0.921 (15.4% improvement over state-of-the-art AGNN) and MSE of 0.0085 (46% improvement over baselines). Statistical validation reports large effect sizes (Cohen’s versus all baselines). Scalability analysis on synthetic networks up to 500,000 nodes confirms practical execution times while traditional SIR simulation becomes prohibitively expensive. The framework successfully bridges the gap between computational efficiency and prediction accuracy for real-time deployment.Keywords: Node influence prediction, Knowledge distillation, Graph neural networks, Hierarchical teacher-student architecture, LightGCN, Complex networksSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Complex networks serve as fundamental mathematical representations of interconnected systems across diverse domains, from biological protein-protein interactions and neural connectivity to social media platforms, transportation infrastructures, and communication systems1,2. The concept of node influence quantifies how effectively a node can propagate information, disease, or cascading failures throughout the network structure, making it critical for applications in epidemic control, viral marketing, infrastructure protection, and policy decisions3–6. Current approaches to node influence prediction fall into three main categories. Centrality-based methods, including degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, PageRank, and k-shell decomposition, offer computational efficiency through closed-form calculations but treat influence as a static structural property, failing to capture dynamic spreading processes7–10. Simulation-based methods, particularly the susceptible-infected-recovered (SIR) model and independent cascade (IC) model, explicitly model propagation dynamics through Monte Carlo simulations, providing accurate ground-truth estimates at the cost of computational operations that become prohibitive for large networks11–13. Machine learning approaches, including support vector machines, random forests, and recent graph neural networks (GCN, GAT, CGNN, AGNN), attempt to learn mappings from topological features to influence scores, demonstrating improved performance but requiring extensive labeled training data14–19. Despite these advances, several critical research gaps impede practical deployment of influence prediction systems:

Related work

Centrality-based methods

Centrality-based methods constitute the earliest and most computationally efficient approaches to influence prediction, relying on topological properties to rank node importance. Degree centrality assumes that nodes with more connections exert greater influence, while betweenness centrality identifies nodes controlling information flow between network regions7,8. PageRank extends this concept by incorporating recursive importance propagation, where a node’s influence depends on the influence of its neighbors9. K-shell decomposition iteratively removes low-degree nodes to identify core influential spreaders, demonstrating superior performance in some networks compared to traditional centrality measures10. More recent developments include eigenvector centrality, which accounts for the quality rather than quantity of connections20, and local centrality metrics that balance computational efficiency with predictive accuracy21–23. Despite computational advantages with complexity typically bounded by O(m) or for different measures, these methods fundamentally treat influence as a static structural property, failing to capture temporal dynamics, heterogeneous transmission rates, and context-dependent spreading processes that characterize real-world diffusion phenomena13,24.

Simulation-based approaches

Simulation-based methods explicitly model propagation dynamics through stochastic processes that track disease or information spread across network edges. The Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR) model, originally developed for epidemiology, partitions nodes into three states and simulates transitions according to infection probability and recovery rate parameters3,25. The independent cascade (IC) model focuses on information diffusion where each infected node has a single chance to activate its neighbors with edge-specific probabilities4. Linear threshold (LT) models incorporate cumulative influence from multiple neighbors, activating nodes when aggregate influence exceeds individual thresholds11. These approaches provide ground-truth influence estimates by averaging over Monte Carlo simulations, typically requiring to 10,000 runs per node to achieve statistical reliability12,26. While simulation accuracy makes them the gold standard for evaluation, computational complexity of renders them impractical for networks exceeding thousands of nodes or applications requiring real-time predictions, leading researchers to develop approximation algorithms and sampling strategies that trade accuracy for speed27,28. Sketch-based methods such as SKIM29 and the martingale-based IMM algorithm28 reduce the cost of finding top-k spreaders to near-linear time, yet still require simulations proportional to network density and do not directly provide a full-network influence ranking without complete simulation coverage. We compare HKD–NIP against these simulation acceleration baselines in Sect. “Comparison with simulation acceleration baselines”.

Machine learning approaches

Traditional machine learning methods frame influence prediction as supervised learning, extracting handcrafted features that combine multiple centrality measures, local clustering properties, and neighborhood statistics. Early approaches employed Support Vector Machines with kernels designed for graph-structured data, achieving modest improvements over single centrality metrics14. Random Forest and gradient boosting methods demonstrated superior performance by capturing nonlinear feature interactions without manual kernel engineering15,30. Feature engineering strategies evolved to incorporate higher-order network motifs, community structure, and spreading process characteristics. Deep learning approaches including multi-layer perceptrons and recurrent neural networks attempted to learn representations directly from raw adjacency matrices, but struggled with permutation invariance and scalability to large networks31,32. A fundamental limitation across these methods is poor generalization across network types: models trained on social networks perform poorly on infrastructure networks due to different degree distributions, clustering patterns, and spreading dynamics, necessitating domain-specific feature engineering and model retraining for each network class.

Graph neural networks

Graph neural networks revolutionized network analysis by learning node representations through iterative message passing that naturally handles graph structure. Graph convolutional networks aggregate neighbor features through spectral convolutions, enabling end-to-end learning without manual feature engineering16,33. Graph Attention Networks introduce adaptive attention mechanisms that weight neighbor contributions based on learned relevance scores, improving performance on heterogeneous networks17. GraphSAGE enables inductive learning through sampling-based aggregation, allowing generalization to unseen nodes without retraining34,35. Graph isomorphism networks address expressiveness limitations by injecting multi-layer perceptrons into aggregation functions36. Specialized architectures for influence prediction include cascade-based graph neural networks that explicitly model information diffusion patterns18,37, multi-resolution convolutional neural networks operating at multiple graph scales38, and adversarial graph neural networks incorporating domain adaptation for cross-network transfer19. Recent work explores temporal graph networks for dynamic influence tracking39–41 and universal node ranking frameworks applicable across diverse network types42,43. Despite impressive performance, these methods require extensive labeled data (typically 50–80% of nodes) and substantial computational resources for training, limiting practical deployment in scenarios where simulation-based labeling is expensive44.

Knowledge distillation and transfer learning

Knowledge distillation transfers learned representations from complex teacher models to efficient student models, originally proposed for image classification where large ensembles distill knowledge into compact networks45,46. The core principle involves training students to match teacher soft labels (temperature-scaled probability distributions) rather than hard ground-truth labels, preserving relational information about class similarities47,48. Graph knowledge distillation extends these concepts to network-structured data, addressing unique challenges including permutation invariance and irregular neighborhood sizes49,50. Early graph distillation work focused on node classification, transferring knowledge from deep GCNs to shallow networks while maintaining accuracy. Link prediction distillation enables efficient recommendation systems by compressing large graph embeddings51. Self-supervised graph distillation reduces labeled data requirements through contrastive learning objectives that align student and teacher representations52–55. Recent advances explore distillation across different graph neural architectures56 and multi-teacher frameworks that combine complementary knowledge sources57. However, existing graph knowledge distillation primarily addresses classification and link prediction tasks with static node labels, not influence prediction where labels depend on complex spreading dynamics and vary with epidemic parameters, making direct application of existing techniques insufficient58–61. Unlike prior multi-teacher frameworks57 that combine teachers trained on the same domain, our general-to-domain cascade explicitly bridges two levels of knowledge abstraction: universal structural patterns and network-specific propagation dynamics. This design is theoretically justified in Section "Theoretical justification for hierarchical distillation" and empirically validated through component ablation.

Methodology

This section presents the HKD–NIP framework, a hierarchical knowledge distillation approach for efficient node influence prediction that operates through three synergistic components working in tandem to achieve both accuracy and computational efficiency.

Framework overview

HKD–NIP addresses the computational expense of simulation-based influence prediction through a hierarchical teacher-student architecture that transfers knowledge across three stages. First, a general teacher model pre-trained on diverse synthetic networks captures universal structural patterns governing influence propagation across different topologies, serving as a repository of cross-domain knowledge. Second, a domain-specific teacher adapts this general knowledge to target networks by fine-tuning on strategically selected labeled nodes, typically 5–10% of total nodes, capturing network-specific propagation dynamics while leveraging pre-learned patterns. Third, a lightweight LightGCN-based student model distills knowledge from the domain teacher through complementary mechanisms: soft label supervision transfers probabilistic influence rankings, while contrastive representation alignment preserves geometric structure of the teacher’s embedding space, enabling sub-second inference without sacrificing accuracy. The framework operates in two distinct phases. During the offline phase, the general teacher trains once on synthetic networks and the domain teacher adapts to each target network using sparse labels obtained through limited SIR simulations. During the online phase, the trained student model provides rapid influence predictions for all nodes without additional simulations, enabling real-time applications. Figure 1 illustrates the complete architecture.

The HKD–NIP framework architecture consisting of three key components: (1) General teacher trained on 36 diverse synthetic networks (BA, ER, WS) captures transferable structural patterns, (2) Domain-specific teacher adapts to target network using 5–10% strategically sampled labeled nodes, (3) Lightweight LightGCN student distills knowledge through soft labels and contrastive alignment for efficient inference. The two-stage teacher cascade incrementally reduces the structural domain gap: the general teacher bridges the synthetic-to-real network gap, while the domain teacher closes the residual gap to the specific target topology. This hierarchical decomposition of domain shift is the key theoretical advantage over single-stage distillation (see Section "Theoretical justification for hierarchical distillation").

Results

Prediction accuracy

Figure 2 presents mean squared error comparison across all methods, with each method executed 20 independent times per dataset. HKD–NIP achieves significantly lower prediction error with mean MSE of 0.0085 ± 0.0028 compared to 0.0158 ± 0.0048 for the best baseline AGNN, representing 46% error reduction. The performance ranking follows: HKD–NIP (0.0085), AGNN (0.0158), MRCNN (0.0165), CGNN (0.0172), RCNN (0.0182), GAT (0.0198), GCN (0.0215), and GIN (0.0245). HKD–NIP is the only method achieving MSE below 0.01, crossing a critical threshold for practical influence prediction applications. First, the general teacher provides strong initialization through cross-domain knowledge transfer, enabling effective learning despite sparse labels (5–10% labeled nodes). Error case analysis

To understand when HKD–NIP underperforms, we conduct a qualitative analysis of high-error nodes—those where the predicted influence rank deviates most from the ground-truth SIR rank. PowerGrid (, the lowest-scoring dataset). PowerGrid is an infrastructure network with distinctive characteristics: very low average degree (), tree-like topology (high diameter , low clustering coefficient), and strong community partitioning. In such networks, influence propagation is dominated by long-range paths rather than local centrality metrics, and the epidemic threshold is high (), making simulation outcomes highly sensitive to small structural perturbations near the threshold. Analysis of the 50 highest-error nodes reveals that 76% are “boundary nodes”—nodes with degree located at the junctions between sparsely connected sub-trees. Their influence scores are highly sensitive to the exact path connectivity of their local neighborhoods, which differs substantially from anything in the BA/ER/WS training data (all of which have denser local structure). This suggests that adding synthetic tree-like or grid-structured networks to the general teacher’s training set could substantially improve PowerGrid performance. Small dense networks (Jazz, ). In the Jazz dataset (), the highest-error nodes are high-degree “connectors” between dense cliques. Their influence scores are very similar to one another (all near-perfect spreaders), making fine-grained ranking inherently noisy even in ground-truth SIR estimates across runs. HKD–NIP correctly identifies these as top-tier nodes but struggles with their exact ordering—a limitation shared by all methods. Implications. These two failure modes suggest distinct improvement strategies: (1) for sparse infrastructure networks, augmenting synthetic training with tree/grid topologies; (2) for near-saturation dense networks, uncertainty quantification to flag low-confidence rank pairs rather than asserting a definitive ordering.

Discussion

Key findings and implications

Our comprehensive experimental evaluation across eight real-world datasets demonstrates that HKD–NIP successfully addresses the fundamental trade-off between computational efficiency and prediction accuracy in node influence prediction. The framework achieves three key breakthroughs that advance the state-of-the-art. First, HKD–NIP reduces computational requirements by 89% (from 18 hours to 2 hours for PowerGrid network) while maintaining simulation-level accuracy, enabling practical deployment in time-sensitive applications such as real-time epidemic monitoring and dynamic network analysis. Second, the hierarchical knowledge distillation approach reduces labeled data requirements by 90% (from full-network coverage to 5–10% strategically selected nodes), addressing the labeling bottleneck that limits practical application of supervised learning methods. Third, the framework achieves superior prediction accuracy with Kendall’s tau of 0.921 (15.4% improvement over state-of-the-art AGNN) and MSE of 0.0085 (46% improvement over baselines), demonstrating that knowledge distillation with cross-network transfer learning can match or exceed methods trained on complete labels. The ablation studies reveal critical insights about framework design. The general teacher trained on diverse synthetic networks (BA, ER, WS topologies) provides robust initialization enabling effective learning despite sparse labels, with removal causing 3.8% performance degradation. Contrastive representation alignment emerges as the most critical component, with removal producing 9.2% accuracy drop, confirming that preserving geometric structure of teacher embeddings is essential for capturing influence propagation patterns. The complementary nature of soft label distillation and structural alignment demonstrates that both output-level and representation-level knowledge transfer are necessary for optimal performance. The systematic feature ablation confirms that degree and k-shell are most critical for influence prediction (4.9% and 3.5% impact respectively), while the full six-feature set achieves 15–17% better performance than any single feature alone, validating our multi-faceted structural characterization approach. The practical implications extend beyond academic benchmarks. For epidemic control agencies, HKD–NIP enables rapid identification of super-spreader nodes for targeted intervention strategies without requiring weeks of simulation time, making it feasible to update predictions as networks evolve. For social media platforms, the framework supports real-time influence ranking for viral marketing campaigns or misinformation containment, processing networks with millions of users within minutes rather than days. For critical infrastructure operators, the lightweight student model can be deployed on edge devices 64 for continuous monitoring of influence cascades in power grids, transportation networks, or communication systems, enabling proactive resilience management.

Embedding interpretability

To understand what structural patterns the general teacher’s embeddings capture, we analyze the alignment between embedding dimensions and structural features using Spearman correlation. Feature-dimension alignment. We compute the Spearman correlation between each of the 64 embedding dimensions of the general teacher’s final layer and each of the six structural features (, , , , , ) across all nodes in the eight evaluation datasets. Principal component analysis of the embedding space reveals that the first two principal components explain 38.2% and 19.4% of embedding variance respectively. PC1 correlates strongly with degree () and k-shell index ()—consistent with the feature ablation finding that these are the most predictive structural features. PC2 correlates with clustering coefficient () and local efficiency (), capturing local cohesion independently of global connectivity. This suggests the embedding space organizes nodes along two primary axes: global network position (PC1) and local neighborhood structure (PC2)—precisely the two dimensions most relevant for influence propagation3,10. Cross-network embedding geometry. We project embeddings from three evaluation networks (Dolphins, Jazz, PowerGrid) onto a shared 2D embedding space using UMAP. High-influence nodes (top quintile by SIR) consistently form a compact cluster in embedding space regardless of the source network, demonstrating that the general teacher has learned a transferable representation of “influential node” topology. This geometric consistency explains why contrastive alignment (which preserves embedding geometry during student distillation) is the most critical ablation component. Limitations of interpretability. While PC analysis and correlation-based interpretability provide insight into the first two principal components, the remaining 42 dimensions that comprise the effective rank of the embedding matrix are harder to interpret. Methods such as concept activation vectors (CAVs)67 or probing classifiers applied to individual embedding dimensions could provide finer-grained interpretability and are planned as future work.

Limitations

Despite strong empirical performance, HKD–NIP has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, the framework currently uses directed-graph extensions as a post-hoc modification (Section "Directed and weighted network experiments") rather than native support; future work should incorporate directed synthetic training data and asymmetric message passing natively. Second, the general teacher trains on three random graph models (BA, ER, WS) which, while covering diverse properties, may not capture all real-world structural patterns such as hierarchical modularity, core-periphery structure, or hyperbolic geometry—as evidenced by the lower performance on PowerGrid (Section "Error case analysis"). Third, the domain teacher adaptation assumes stationary network structure, performing poorly on rapidly-evolving networks. Fourth, computational efficiency gains require upfront investment in general teacher training, making the framework most suitable for scenarios where multiple target networks will be analyzed or repeated predictions are needed. Fifth, the k-means sampling strategy assumes that structural similarity implies influence similarity, which may not hold in networks with complex spreading dynamics. Sixth, the current evaluation is limited to networks of up to 4941 nodes; million-node validation remains an open challenge. The theoretical complexity analysis predicts favorable scaling, but hardware limitations on single-GPU systems have precluded empirical verification at that scale.

Future directions

Several promising research directions could extend HKD–NIP’s capabilities. First, integrating self-supervised learning objectives could further reduce labeled data requirements through contrastive learning on unlabeled nodes. Second, uncertainty quantification through Bayesian neural networks or ensemble methods would enable the framework to express confidence in predictions. Third, federated learning approaches could enable collaborative model training across organizations without sharing sensitive network data. Fourth, incorporating temporal dynamics through recurrent graph neural networks would improve performance on evolving networks. Fifth, extending to attributed networks where nodes have demographic or behavioral features could improve prediction accuracy. Sixth, multi-task learning jointly predicting influence under different spreading models (SIR, IC, LT) could improve generalization. Seventh, incorporating stochastic block models and core-periphery synthetic networks into the general teacher training could address the PowerGrid failure mode identified in Section "Error case analysis".

Conclusion

This paper presented HKD–NIP, a hierarchical knowledge distillation framework that addresses the fundamental computational bottleneck in node influence prediction while maintaining simulation-level accuracy. Traditional SIR simulation-based methods provide accurate influence estimates but require computational operations that become prohibitively expensive for large-scale networks or real-time applications, while existing machine learning approaches demand extensive labeled data obtained through expensive simulations. Our framework resolves this dilemma through strategic knowledge transfer across three hierarchical components that separate universal structural knowledge from domain-specific propagation patterns. The proposed approach employs a general teacher trained on 36 diverse synthetic networks spanning Barabasi-Albert, Erdos-Renyi, and Watts-Strogatz topologies to capture transferable influence patterns, a domain-specific teacher that adapts this knowledge to target networks using only 5–10% strategically selected labeled nodes, and a lightweight LightGCN-based student model that distills knowledge through complementary soft label supervision and contrastive representation alignment mechanisms. The two-stage teacher cascade is theoretically justified through domain adaptation theory: the hierarchical decomposition of the structural domain gap enables the student to benefit from both universal structural knowledge and network-specific propagation dynamics, reducing the effective learning error by compared to single-stage distillation. This hierarchical separation enables the framework to achieve 89% computational cost reduction and 90% reduction in required SIR simulations while delivering superior prediction accuracy. Comprehensive experiments across eight real-world datasets from social, communication, collaboration, and infrastructure domains demonstrate that HKD–NIP achieves Kendall’s tau correlation of 0.921, representing 15.4% improvement over the previous state-of-the-art AGNN method, and mean squared error of 0.0085, a 46% improvement over baseline approaches. All improvements are statistically significant (, Friedman/Nemenyi tests) and practically significant (Cohen’s for all comparisons). Directed-network experiments confirm the framework generalizes beyond undirected graphs. Error case analysis reveals that sparse tree-like infrastructure networks represent the primary failure mode, pointing to a concrete future improvement: augmenting the general teacher with grid/tree synthetic topologies. Scalability analysis on synthetic networks up to 500,000 nodes validates practical deployment potential, with student inference requiring less than 10 minutes while traditional SIR simulation demands 1,920 hours, demonstrating 28 speedup that increases with network size.
