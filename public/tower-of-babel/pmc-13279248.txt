Seasonality of Selected Cardiovascular Disorders in Austria: A Retrospective Observational Study Between 2009 and 2023
Authors: Maria Elisabeth Leinweber, Amun Georg Hofmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronoepidemiological patterns in general and seasonality in specific have been investigated for a multitude of pathologies. Although cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarction and stroke are well researched, evidence on other vascular segments and events remains comparably small.MethodsA retrospective observational study analyzed hospital admission data from 2009 to 2023 for pulmonary embolism (PE), aortic dissection (AD), deep vein thrombosis (DVT), acute limb ischemia, and ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm.ResultsSeasonal variation was most pronounced for PE and AD, with winter peaks and summer troughs (PE 21.5 versus 17.6 admissions/day, −18.2%; AD 2.53 versus 2.06 admissions/day, −18.6%). DVT showed similar fluctuations (peak‐to‐trough ratio 1.19), whereas acute limb ischemia and ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm demonstrated smaller absolute variation. Marked weekend effects were observed, with reductions of 65.5% (DVT), 52.5% (PE), 55.2% (acute limb ischemia), and 39.2% (AD); ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm varied by 15.0%. Although admissions on daylight saving time days were lower than the annual average (eg, PE −42.9%, DVT −52.2%), differences compared with other Sundays were small (mostly <10%), and post‐daylight saving time weeks differed by <6% from control weeks.ConclusionThis 15‐year analysis confirms significant seasonal and weekend effects in vascular emergency admissions, particularly for PE, AD, and DVT. Regional differences in season manifestation and health care access limit generalizability, necessitating context‐specific research into underlying mechanisms, including air pollution, health care system characteristics, and behavioral factors, to inform targeted interventions.Keywords: acute limb ischemia, aortic aneurysm, aortic dissection, chronobiology, deep venous thrombosis, pulmonary aneurysm, seasonalitySubject Categories: Epidemiology, Vascular Disease, Aneurysm, Aortic Dissection, Thrombosis

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

This 15‐year population‐wide study in Austria reveals pronounced seasonal patterns in hospital admissions for pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, and deep vein thrombosis, with winter peaks and ∼18% reductions in summer, alongside substantial weekend troughs (up to 65%) and no influence from daylight saving time transitions, whereas acute limb ischemia and ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm show minimal temporal variation.

This 15‐year population‐wide study in Austria reveals pronounced seasonal patterns in hospital admissions for pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, and deep vein thrombosis, with winter peaks and ∼18% reductions in summer, alongside substantial weekend troughs (up to 65%) and no influence from daylight saving time transitions, whereas acute limb ischemia and ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm show minimal temporal variation.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Awareness of these patterns can inform resource planning in vascular and emergency departments, including staffing decisions and diagnostic preparedness during winter and early weekdays, to optimize timely management of urgent vascular conditions.

A growing body of evidence has established chronobiological associations with various acute cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction, stroke, and pulmonary embolism (PE) revealing distinct temporal patterns in their onset. 1 , 2 , 3 However, chronobiological research specific to other vascular territories—such as aortic aneurysm rupture, acute popliteal artery aneurysm, and acute limb ischemia (ALI)—from the central European region remains scarce. These conditions, which often require urgent surgical intervention, carry significant morbidity and mortality, yet their potential alignment with circadian, weekly, or seasonal rhythms is underexplored.

Chronobiological effects and the influence of environmental factors on the occurrence of adverse cardiovascular events have garnered significant interest over the past 3 decades. Like many physiological processes, the cardiovascular system exhibits circadian rhythms driven by fluctuating sympathetic and vagal activity, as well as cortisone and epinephrine release, which mediate intraday variations in blood pressure, heart rate, and cardiac output. 4 , 5 , 6 These rhythms correspond to differing energetic demands and nutrient availability, influencing cardiomyocyte metabolism and cellular signaling. 7 At a macroscopic level, cardiovascular diseases often follow seasonal patterns, with peak incidences frequently observed during winter and nadirs in fall. 1 , 8 For instance, acute myocardial infarction, 9 , 10 , 11 venous thromboembolism, 12 , 13 and ruptured intracranial 14 , 15 and abdominal aortic aneurysms 16 , 17 have been shown to exhibit such seasonality. Similarly, hospitalizations for critical limb ischemia have demonstrated temporal variations. 18

Weekly patterns suggest a higher occurrence on Mondays, and seasonal trends indicate increased risk during winter, particularly in December. 19 , 20 These patterns may be driven by shared pathophysiological and behavioristic mechanisms, such as increased blood pressure, sympathetic activity, and prothrombotic tendencies, which exhibit circadian and seasonal variations. However, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

This study aims to address these knowledge gaps by analyzing population data from Austria between 2009 and 2023 to investigate the seasonality of acute vascular surgery cases, including aortic dissections (AD), ruptured aortic aneurysms (RAA), deep venous thrombosis (DVT), PE, and ALI.

Methods

Study Design

The present study is a population‐wide retrospective observational study. The requirement of institutional review board approval was waived by the Austrian Ministry of Health due to the fully anonymized and aggregated nature of the extracted data.

Data

Data were obtained from the Austrian Ministry of Health through the Dokumantations‐ und Informationssysteme für Analysen im Gesundheitswesen data warehouse. The database in its current structure was established in 2009. Data between 2009 and 2023 were extracted for the present analysis. International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes were used to classify cases: RAA were defined as I71.3, 0.5, 0.8, AD as I71.1, ALI as I74, PE as I26, DVT as I82. Data can be made available only through the ministry.

Statistical Analysis

To investigate seasonal patterns in hospital admissions for PE, DVT, ALI, AD, and RAA, we calculated the mean daily admissions for each calendar month across the entire observational period (2009–2023). Additionally, to assess the stability of these patterns, we stratified the data into 5‐year time bands and computed similar monthly means within each band. For conditions exhibiting strong seasonal indications (PE, DVT, and AD), we performed time series decomposition to separate the data into trend, seasonal, and random components, allowing for visual inspection of seasonal patterns over time. To examine potential weekend effects, we calculated the mean daily admissions for each weekday and assessed variations between weekdays and weekends. Finally, to investigate the impact of daylight saving time (DST) transitions on hospital admissions, we employed a multilayered approach: we compared mean admissions on DST transition days to those on all other days and specifically to other Sundays; additionally, we compared the 7 days following DST transitions to a control period (7 days before and 8–14 days after DST). All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.2) in RStudio.

Results

Population Characteristics

Austria had between 8.3 (2009) and 9.1 million (2023) inhabitants during the observational period, corresponding to a relative increase of 9.2% in population size. The population consisted of 50% to 51% female inhabitants, and the proportion of individuals of 65 years of age and older grew to 19.6% in 2023 from 17.4% in 2009. According to Statistics Austria (Federal Statistical Office), daily smoking decreased over the study period, from 27.5% (men) and 19.4% (women) in 2006 to 23.7% and 17.9% in 2019, respectively. Seasonal Patterns

In order to investigate seasonality of acute conditions in vascular surgery and medicine, mean daily hospital admissions for all included pathologies were stratified by calendar month. (Figure 1A) The strongest indication for a seasonal pattern was identified for PE, with a peak in January (21.5 admissions/day) and a nadir in June (17.6 admissions/day), corresponding to relative decrease of 18.2%. Hospital admissions for AD showed a similar trend, with a peak in February (2.53 admissions/day) and a nadir in August (2.06 admissions/day), a reduction of 18.6%. Hospital admissions for DVT also peaked in the first quarter of the year (Figure 1B) and generally correspond to caseloads for PE, even though they are more centered around an annual mean showing smaller deviations between peaks and troughs. Even though admissions related to ALI showed differences of up to 15.0% between the maximum in February and a minimum in December, no rhythmic pattern indicative of seasonal variations emerged in the data. RAA showed the smallest variation across the calendar year with mean daily admissions ranging between 1.47 and 1.65.

We further investigated whether these observations were not only present in the aggregated data set across the full observational period but could be found in 5‐year time bands. The results remained rather stable, for example with peak caseloads for DVT and PE in the first quarter or the nadir in ALI caseloads in the fourth quarter. (Table 3).

Weekend Effects

Following the analysis of seasonal patterns, we investigated the appearance of weekend effects in hospital admissions for the conditions of interest. Mean daily admissions per weekday across the observational period are shown in Figure 3. Strong indications for weekend effects were observed for PE and DVT, where the difference between Monday (maximum mean admissions) and caseloads on the weekend were 52.5% and 65.5%, respectively. Regarding ALI, the difference between mean number of admissions on a Saturday and on Mondays were also 55.2%. In AD, the peak was not on Mondays but Tuesdays with a mean of 2.68 admissions. However, compared with Saturdays with 1.63 this was yet again a relative reduction of 39.2%. Even though caseloads for RAA reached a minimum on Saturdays and Sundays, the difference between the peak on Tuesdays and valley on Sundays was comparably small at 15.0% (1.61 versus 1.37 admissions).

Daylight Saving Time

We found no evidence for an effect of DST transitions and increased hospital admissions for any of the investigated pathologies using a multilayered analytical approach. Comparative analyses between DST days and the remaining days in the data set suggested clinically relevant reductions in caseloads. (Table 4) However, because all DST days are Sundays, the analysis was repeated comparing DST days to the remaining Sundays. Hereby, differences between DST days and controls remained <10%, except for AD, where daily caseloads are generally low and small absolute increases can generate larger relative movements. Because this comparison could potentially be also confounded by seasonal variations (as discussed previously) and a lag in the effects of DST, that is, sleep deprivation, a further comparison between the 7 days starting with DST and a combined control set of the 7 days before DST and 8 to 14 days after DST was conducted. No relevant differences were observed. Variations in caseloads after DST transitions therefore primarily replicated observed weekend effects.

Discussion

To investigate seasonal patterns in specific vascular admissions, mean daily hospital admissions were analyzed by calendar month. PE showed the clearest seasonality, peaking in January and reaching a low in June (−18.2%). Similar trends were seen for AD (−18.6%) and DVT, whereas ALI and RAA showed less distinct or minimal seasonal variation. These patterns were consistent across 5‐year intervals. A strong weekend effect was observed for PE and DVT, with Monday caseloads exceeding weekend levels by 52.5% and 65.5%, respectively. ALI and AD also showed notable weekday‐weekend differences, and RAA varied least (15.0%). No effect of DST on admissions was found. Although initial analyses suggested lower admissions on DST Sundays, follow‐up comparisons with other Sundays and surrounding weeks found no clinically relevant differences, indicating DST does not influence hospital admissions of the investigated cardiovascular disorders.

The present study employed primarily descriptive temporal analyses, including calculation of mean daily admissions by month and weekday, stratification into time bands, and time series decomposition, to identify and characterize seasonal and weekly patterns in selected vascular emergency admissions. This exploratory approach was deliberately chosen over adjusted regression‐based models, as the primary objective was to describe chronobiological variations in a population‐wide data set rather than to establish causality or adjust for potential confounders, which would be required for hypothesis‐driven pathomechanistic investigations.

The observed seasonal patterns for PE, AD, and DVT align with prior research. A meta‐analysis of ∼35 000 patients reported a winter peak in venous thromboembolism, including PE and DVT, attributing this to colder ambient temperatures, increased sympathetic activity, wintertime peaks in total and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol as well as changes in the coagulation system, which may promote prothrombotic states. 21 Similarly, a winter increase in AD incidence has been reported by several authors. 20 , 22 , 23 , 24 Our findings of an 18.2% reduction in PE admissions from January to June and an 18.6% reduction in AD from February to August are consistent with these studies.

The absence of conclusive signs of seasonality in ALI and RAA corresponds to conflicting evidence from previous studies. These include either minimal seasonal variation in RAA or significant associations with seasonality. 19 , 25 , 26 A meta‐analysis of 30 mainly retrospective studies demonstrated that the pooled proportion of RAA was highest in autumn and winter. 25 Similarly, evidence for seasonal patterns in ALI is limited. It has been recently reported that patients with symptomatic popliteal artery aneurysms predominantly (57.0%) presented to the hospital in the first half of the year, with a nadir in the fall season. 27 Additionally, an analysis of 1 276 745 hospitalizations due to critical limb‐threatening ischemia from the US Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project's National Inpatient Sample revealed that spring was the peak admission season for patients with critical limb‐threatening ischemia, again with a nadir in fall. 28

Moreover, findings on seasonal associations in vascular events from other countries may not be reproducible in other regions, given that differences in climate, the manifestation of seasons, and behavioral aspects may vary regionally. For instance, Austria's temperate climate, characterized by cold winters, may mitigate the direct impact of temperature on some vascular events, as populations are likely adapted to seasonal changes through infrastructure (eg, indoor heating) and behaviors (eg, thermoregulatory clothing). This is consistent with a meta‐analysis, which found that colder climates with robust cold‐protection measures exhibit smaller temperature‐related effects on cardiovascular risk compared with warmer regions with less adaptation. 29

However, several factors may explain the increased occurrence of adverse vascular events during winter besides the effect of cold exposure. An analysis 24 population‐based studies, involving 237 979 patients, found seasonal variations in cardiovascular risk factors, such as elevated blood pressure, changes in body measurements, higher serum cholesterol and triglyceride levels, and increased blood glucose levels, all of which worsen in winter months. 3 Additionally, seasonal changes in air pollution, such as elevated particulate matter (specifically PM 2.5) and nitrogen dioxide levels during winter due to increased heating and stagnant air, may exacerbate vascular risk. Several studies have linked higher particulate matter 2.5 concentrations to increased cardiovascular events, potentially contributing to the observed winter peaks. 30 , 31 Nonetheless, the interplay of colder ambient temperatures, levels of air pollutants, and cardiovascular events is complex, and conflicting evidence exist. 1

Beyond temperature and air pollution, factors such as variations in behavior, such as reduced physical activity, increased caloric intake, or indoor smoking during winter, could elevate vascular risk. Basnet et al. (2016) found that 70% of 4689 participants in the Finnish FINRISK study reported seasonal changes in social activity, sleep, and mood, with 40% showing variations in weight and appetite. 32 Holiday periods, including travel or reduced health care access in summer, as well as weather‐related influences on behavior could further play a role, when colder winter conditions may promote more sedentary lifestyles and reduced outdoor physical activity.

The pronounced weekend effect, with PE and DVT admissions surging by 52.5% and 65.5% on Mondays compared with weekends, corroborates previous findings observing similar reductions in venous thromboembolism hospitalizations. This may reflect both system‐level factors such as reduced access to diagnostic services or patient‐level factors such as reluctance to seek care during weekends, leading to delayed presentations. For AD, the Tuesday peak (39.2% increase compared with Saturdays) is consistent with weekend effects that have been described before, noting a peak on Monday and a nadir on Saturdays and national holidays. 33 , 34 The minimal weekend effect in RAA is consistent with its life‐threatening nature, prompting immediate presentation regardless of other conditions. However, the current data and methodological approach cannot distinguish between patient‐related delays and health care system‐related factors. These aspects might also affect varying geographic regions differently depending on available health care infrastructure and sociocultural factors.

The absence of a DST effect aligns with mixed evidence in the literature. As >1.5 billion people worldwide are affected by the DST transition each year, which potentially affects circadian rhythms and sleep quality—factors that have been investigated in relation to cardiovascular events—DST‐associated severe cardiovascular events have gained attention in recent years. 35 A large analysis of a nationwide US study involving 36 116 951 individuals showed that the spring transition to DST was only suggestively associated with a slight increase in the rate of adverse cardiovascular events. 36 A meta‐analysis of DST and the incidence of acute myocardial infarction revealed that although the risk of acute myocardial infarction increased after the spring shift (odds ratio [OR], 1.05 [95% CI, 1.02–1.07]), the incidence of acute myocardial infarction during the week following the winter DST transition was comparable to that in control phases (OR, 1.01 [95% CI, 0.98–1.04]). 37 Although a pathophysiological concept may exist regarding how disruption to circadian rhythms and sleep deprivation may accelerate the risk of cardiovascular events, it is important to note that there is limited evidence regarding the effect of short‐term circadian changes on cardiovascular events like those investigated in this analysis. The initial observation of lower admissions on transition days compared with other days, which disappeared when limiting the controls to other Sundays, underscores the importance of robust statistical controls to avoid confounding by weekend effects.

Although previous studies have demonstrated seasonal variation in individual vascular conditions, most were limited to single pathologies, regional registries, or shorter observation periods. In contrast, the present analysis integrates nationwide hospital admission data over 15 years, covering nearly the entire Austrian population and simultaneously evaluating 5 different cardiovascular conditions. This allows direct comparison of seasonal amplitude, weekday effects, and DST transitions within a uniform health care system. Importantly, the absence of a DST effect after controlling for weekend patterns adds clarifying evidence to a literature that has reported inconsistent associations for cardiovascular events.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the data are limited to hospital admissions in Austria, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other health care systems, populations, or geographic regions with different seasonal climates or health care access. Second, as with all studies using routine administrative data, there is a risk of misclassification or miscoding of diagnoses, particularly when relying on ICD‐10 codes, which may affect the accuracy of condition‐specific trends. In the case of AD, the absence of morphological details (eg, Stanford type A versus B) in the ICD‐10 system further limits clinical interpretation. Third, selection bias may have influenced the observed caseloads, as individuals receiving ambulatory care (especially for DVT or PE) or those experiencing prehospital death (particularly in AD and RAA) are not captured in hospital admission data. Additionally, the observational period of 15 years, although substantial, may not fully account for long‐term secular trends or changes in diagnostic practices. Temporal patterns may also be influenced by evolving health policies, improvements in diagnostic imaging, or seasonal variation in health‐seeking behavior. Furthermore, due to the aggregated nature of the data from the database that were used, sex‐disaggregated analyses were not feasible in this study.

Conclusions

This 15‐year analysis confirms significant seasonal and weekend effects in vascular emergency admissions, particularly for PE, AD, and DVT, with no DST impact. These findings align with broader cardiovascular research, suggesting a complex interplay of environmental, physiological, and behavioral factors driving temporal trends. Health care systems can leverage these insights to optimize resource allocation. However, regional differences in climate and health care access, along with various confounders, caution against broad generalization. Future research should explore diverse populations and detailed mechanisms, including air pollution and behavioral factors, to inform interventions.
