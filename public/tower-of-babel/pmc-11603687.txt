Mapping context of sedentary behaviour in older adults who are prefrail and frail: an analysis of secondary outcomes from a longitudinal study (MAPS-B)
Authors: Isabel B Rodrigues, Mayank Kalra, Rong Zheng, Alexander Rabinovich, George Ioannidis, Dylan Kobsar, Steven R Bray, Jonathan D Adachi, Qiyin Fang, Alexandra Papaioannou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo map the context of sedentary behaviour in older adults who are prefrail and frail during the winter and spring over 3 days (2 weekdays and 1 weekend) and to determine if certain types of sedentary behaviours are associated with health outcomes.DesignMixed methods, prospective longitudinal cohort study.SettingCommunity-dwelling older adults living in southern Ontario, Canada.ParticipantsWe recruited 21 older adults (72±7.3 years, 13 females, 13 frail) and experienced two dropouts.ResultsParticipants accumulated a total of 18.4±2.6 hours/day of sedentary time in winter and 17.7±3.1 hours/day in spring. When considering daily indoor sedentary time (excluding sleep), participants accumulated 7.6±4.0 hours/day in winter and 6.3±2.7 hours/day in spring. In the spring, 68% of participants accumulated <8 hours/day of ≥15 min bouts of indoor sedentary time compared with 63% in the winter. Although there were no differences in sedentary time between seasons, we found a significant decrease in step count in winter (1190 fewer steps, 95% CI −2228 to −153 steps). There were no differences in sedentary time or step count between the weekday and weekend or between individuals who were prefrail and frail. There were no seasonal variations in participants’ sedentary activities; the most common activities were watching television (TV), eating, napping, browsing the internet and socialising. We also found there may be gender differences in time and activities. Individuals who identified as female were more sedentary than individuals who were male. Almost all indoor behaviours occurred in the living/family room. Sedentary time was evenly spread throughout the day; however, prolonged sedentary behaviours ≥60 min mainly occurred in the evening regardless of the season. Step count in both winter and spring was positively associated with the Nottingham Activity of Daily Living assessment. There was an inverse association between sedentary behaviours ≥60 min (not including sleeping) and the 5×sit-to-stand and grip strength.ConclusionThe current definition of sedentary behaviour requires modification to capture total sedentary time, continuous bouts >60 min, the types of behaviours, time of day and season.Trial registration numberNCT05661058.Keywords: Frailty, Behavior, Aged

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY

This study provides information about the context of sedentary behaviour during different seasons (ie, winter and spring) among older adults who are prefrail and frail and the association of different types of sedentary behaviours on health outcomes including falls, cognition, frailty level, activities of daily living, health-related quality of life, depression and anxiety.

Introduction

Older adults who are frail are more likely to be sedentary.1 2 Although there is no standard definition of sedentary behaviour,3 most western countries define sedentary behaviour as any waking behaviour in a standing, seating or reclining posture with a low energy expenditure (≤1.5 metabolic equivalent of task (MET)).4 The coexistence of frailty and sedentary behaviour worsens overall health.2 Most older adults who are frail spend 60%–70% of their awake time in a seated position, which is equivalent to 9.5–11.5 hours/day of accumulated sedentary time.5 Prolonged periods of sedentary time may lead to muscle and bone unloading and are associated with declines in mobility and quality of life and increased risk of falls, fractures and death.15,8 Even older adults who meet the recommended aerobic exercise and resistance training guidelines may experience adverse effects of sedentary behaviour.5 For example, an older adult may attend a 1-hour exercise programme in the morning and then spend the rest of the day engaging in sedentary behaviours. Interventions to reduce periods of prolonged sedentary behaviour are necessary, especially among older adults who are frail.

There is robust epidemiological evidence showing the adverse effects of prolonged sedentary time on health outcomes, which cannot be compensated for by adhering to the physical activity or exercise guidelines alone.15,8 There is a dearth of evidence on effective interventions to reduce sedentary behaviour or total sedentary time in older adults, especially among individuals who are frail.2 7 9 Previous studies have attempted to reduce total sedentary time by increasing physical activity time with the assumption that sedentary time will be reallocated to physical activity.9 A 2021 Cochrane review synthesised the results from seven studies (six randomised controlled trials and one cluster-randomised controlled trial) and concluded that it is not clear what types of interventions are effective at reducing total sedentary time in older adults (mean 44.91 min/day lower, 95% CI 93.13 min/day lower to 3.32 min/day higher, low certainty of evidence, 3 studies, 397 participants).2 Moreover, the effectiveness of such interventions on physical functioning, mental health or adverse events is unknown.2 The Cochrane review also found there were no interventions focusing on environmental or organisation-level policies that could inadvertently reduce sedentary time.2 Thus, it is unclear what types of interventions are effective at reducing sedentary behaviour or total sedentary time in older adults and what the effects of such interventions are on health-related outcomes in older adults.2

Previous interventions aimed at reducing sedentary time in older adults may not be effective as there is little to no research about the context of sedentary behaviour, about when and where to intervene, and which specific sedentary behaviours should be targeted.2 7 Almost all studies in older adults have assessed total sedentary time, which does not provide enough information about the context of sedentary behaviours.2 7 The main reason to understand context over total sedentary time is that not all sedentary behaviours may be equal or should be targeted in an intervention (eg, interventions that promote sedentary behaviours that decrease social isolation). The purpose of this manuscript was to describe the context of sedentary behaviour in older adults during the winter and spring and to determine if certain types of sedentary behaviours are associated with health outcomes. Context was defined as the purpose of the sedentary behaviours (eg, reading), location in the home where the behaviours occur (eg, living room), posture of the behaviours (eg, lying, sitting), social context (eg, alone or with others) and time of day the behaviours occur.10 11 To map the context of sedentary behaviour, we used objective (ie, wearable sensor and home monitoring system) and self-report (ie, diary) measures; we chose three measures as one measure alone does not provide enough information about context.

Methods

This study is part of a larger research project, the MAPping Sedentary Behaviour (MAPS-B) study.10 12 The primary objective of the project was to report on the feasibility of using three measures to assess the context of sedentary behaviour10 and to understand participants’ perspectives of sedentary behaviour.12 The purpose of this paper was to report on the context of sedentary behaviour.

Study design

We conducted a mixed-methods longitudinal study with older adults who are prefrail and frail. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology 2007 guidelines for reporting of observational studies (online supplemental S1 table).13 We registered our study with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05661058) on 22 December 2022.

Recruitment and enrolment

Our recruitment process is described elsewhere.10 In short, we recruited participants from physicians’ offices, the local newspaper, social media and a local radio station. We also used strategies to recruit underrepresented groups, which are described elsewhere.14 We recruited participants between January and February 2023. We obtained written informed consent from each participant prior to enrolling them in the study. Participants attended two study visits; one in the winter and another in the spring at the Hamilton Health Sciences in Hamilton, Ontario, Canada. Participants were grouped into four cohorts of five participants. During the first week, we met with the first cohort at St. Peter’s Hospital (Hamilton, Ontario, Canada) where they completed a series of questionnaires and physical performance measures. We provided each participant with a wearable sensor, explained how to set up and calibrate the indoor positioning system and complete the electronic or hard copy diary. During the second week, we collected the devices and diaries and transferred the encrypted data to a McMaster University cloud, disinfected the devices between users and charged the devices. We repeated the same process in the spring with the same group of participants.

Participants

We included participants if they (1) spoke English or attended with a translator or caregiver; (2) were 60 years and older; (3) lived in the Greater Hamilton Area and (4) had a Morley Frail Scale score ≥3.15 A Morley Frail Scale score of 0 is considered robust, a score of 1 or 2, prefrail and a score of 3–5, frail.15 We excluded individuals who (1) used a wheelchair for at least 55% of the awake day due to medical conditions; (2) were not independently mobile (ie, require assistance from another individual to ambulate); (3) were unable to follow two-step instructions and (4) had travel plans or other commitments that required missing >30% of the rollout period.

Measures

To map the context of sedentary behaviour, we used objective (wearable sensor and indoor positioning system) and self-report (daily diary) measures. Our methods for equipping participants with the devices and diary are described elsewhere.10 In short, we used the activPAL4 (PAL Technologies Ltd, Glasgow, Scotland) to collect data on posture and a custom-designed indoor positioning system to obtain room-level positioning information. The indoor positioning system was designed and validated to be used by older adults in their own homes without the need for a floor plan and only minimal initial setup and calibration; the system can also be used in homes with multiple stories with multiple residents.16 Each participant was asked to complete a diary of 24-hour daily activities using an electronic diary (Activities Collected over Time over 24 hours) or a hard copy version of the electronic diary that asked participants to describe their activity, who they did the activity with, and the date and time.

Participants attended two study visits during the winter (1 February 2023–21 March 2023) and spring (10 April 2023–27 May 2023) where they completed the same assessments during both visits. Participants were equipped with the wearable sensor (activPAL4) and indoor positioning system, and trained on how to complete a diary of daily activities over 3 days (1 weekend and 2 weekdays) in winter and spring.

Outcomes

We collected several outcomes in the winter and spring. We assessed falls in the last 6 weeks17 and cognition using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA); we administered V.8.2 English in winter and MoCA Basic in spring.18 We used two different versions of the MoCA to decrease possible learning effects when the questionnaire is administered 3 months apart. MoCA scores were adjusted for age and education level. Frailty scores were measured using the Fit-Frailty Assessment and Management Application,19 activities of daily living with the Nottingham Extended Activities of Daily Living Scale20 and health-related quality of life using the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level questionnaire.21 We assessed depression scores using the Geriatric Depression Scale22 and anxiety using the Geriatric Anxiety Scale-10.23 Demographic characteristics were collected using Place of residence, Race/ethnicity, Occupation, Gender and sex, Religion, Education, Socioeconomic status and Social capital.24

Sample size

As the primary outcome is feasibility,10 we selected a sample size of 20, which was considered large enough to understand the practicality of using this novel approach to map sedentary behaviour.25 26

Data analysis

Primary analysis

The purpose of this paper was to map the context of sedentary behaviour in the winter and spring by linking data from the wearable sensor, indoor positioning system and diary for each day. To analyse our main outcome, we used summative content analysis to map the context of sedentary behaviour.27 The principal investigator (IBR) extracted data from the wearable device that were at least 15 min and we excluded outdoor sedentary behaviours (eg, driving). The research team (included researchers, trainees and patient partners) discussed that the minimum time to include in the analysis would be activites that were at least 15 min or more. We also discussed that behaviours lasting ≥60 min would be classified as continuous sedentary behaviours. Data from the diary were linked to the wearable sensor using date and time and coded using the Time Use Survey.28 We anticipated time differences between the diary and wearable device, so we linked activities within ±30 min of the wearable sensor. Data from the indoor positioning system were converted into a CSV file by MK and IBR extracted the relevant data by matching date and time to the wearable sensor. Data extraction was verified by a second reviewer (MK). Data that could not be linked were coded as ‘undetermined’. Disagreements were discussed between IBR and MK, and if consensus was not reached, we reported the activity as ‘undetermined’. We coded ‘napping’ as sleep during the awake day, while ‘sleeping’ was considered a nighttime activity and lasted several hours. We coded ‘morning activities’ between 12:00 and 11:59 o’clock, ‘afternoon’ between 12:00 and 17:59 o’clock, and ‘evening’ between 18:00 and 23:59 o’clock. We pulled data on temperature from the Government of Canada Historical Climate Data.29 If data were missing, we used temperature from the adjacent city. We used Microsoft Excel 2021 (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington) to manage the data and support our analysis.

Secondary analysis

Our secondary purpose was to conduct an exploratory analysis to determine if there was a correlation between certain types of sedentary behaviour and health-related outcomes including gait speed, the chair stand test and grip strength using the Fit-Frailty Assessment and Management Application,19 frailty scores,19 activities of daily living,20 cognition,18 health-related quality of life,21 depression22 and anxiety23 in the winter and spring. We initially planned to conduct a regression analysis, but after collecting the data, we determined a regression was not appropriate as we did not have enough data to predict if sedentary behaviours had an effect on the collected outcomes. Instead, we used the Pearson bivariate correlation or the Spearman partial correlation depending on if the data were normally distributed. We conducted a paired t-test to determine if there was a difference in sedentary behaviour in winter vs spring. A Cohen’s d was performed to determine the strength of the difference. We also conudcted an independent t-test to determine if there was a mean difference between genders for total sedentary time and step count for each season. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine normality. Missing data were not included in the analysis. Quantitative analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V.28 (IBM).

Results

We recruited 21 older adults (72±7.3 years, 13 females) from southwestern Ontario. 19 individuals were included for analysis in both winter and spring. Two individuals were lost to follow-up due to medical mistrust and worse health; one individual dropped out within hours of the first study visit and the second individual dropped out after the winter data collection period. Another individual’s wearable sensor malfunctioned during winter. Baseline characteristics are presented in table 1 and physical performance measures are shown in table 2.

Sedentary time by season

In total, 114 days from 19 participants were available for analysis. In winter, 12 of the 19 participants (63%) accumulated an average of 8 hours/day of indoor sedentary behaviour (not including sleep) while in the spring, 13 of the 19 participants (68%) accumulated an average of 8 hours/day (table 3). Nine participants had <8 hours of indoor sedentary behaviour for all 3 days in winter, while in spring, 10 individuals had <8 hours for all 3 days. We found no significant difference between total daily sedentary time in winter (includes sleep) and spring (mean difference 0.61 hours, 95% CI −0.20 to 1.42, t(17)=1.59) and there was a small effect size (d=0.46). We also found no difference between seasons for daily sedentary time that were ≥15 min (mean difference 0.75 hours, 95% CI −0.37 to 1.87, t(17)=1.42, d=0.40) and ≥60 min (mean difference 0.58 hours, 95% CI −0.29 to 1.44, t(17)=1.40, d=0.36); sleep was not included in the analysis. There was a significant difference in step count between winter and spring with a moderate effect size (mean difference of 1190 fewer steps in winter, 95% CI −2228 to −153 steps, t(17)=−2.42, d=−0.73). There were no differences in sedentary time or step count between the weekday and weekend or between individuals who were prefrail and frail.

Context of sedentary behaviour

There were no seasonal variations in participants’ sedentary activities (table 4). The most common activities include watching television (TV), eating, napping, browsing the internet and socialising with others in person, on the phone, or through a screen (figure 1). About 45% of indoor sedentary behaviours involved screen-based activities. We excluded sleep from figure 1 as it accounted for a little over 50% of indoor sedentary behaviour. Most activities occurred in the living room/family room area including eating (mainly in front of the TV), resting and napping. Four participants did not complete their hard copy diary with enough detail and as a result there were several hours of missing data that could not be linked. We found that participants spent more time socialising and eating outside of the home in spring than winter.

Sedentary time was evenly spread throughout the day; however, prolonged sedentary behaviours (ie, ≥60 min) mainly occurred in the evening (18:00–23:59 o’clock) regardless of the season. The most common evening activities were watching TV and browsing the internet. Most participants (90%) did not indicate if they completed an activity alone or with another individual.

Individuals who identified as female had a higher total daily sedentary time in both seasons than individuals who were male (mean difference in winter 1.67 hours greater for females, 95% CI -4.23 to 0.89, t(17)=-1.37, d=-0.66, mean difference in spring 0.81 hours greater for females, 95% CI -4.02 to 2.39, t(17)=-0.54, d=-0.33). Males had a higher step count in the winter (mean difference 1095 steps higher for males, 95% CI -2535 to 4724, t(17)=0.64, d=1.68), but females had a higher step count in spring (mean difference 264 steps higher for females, 95% CI -4314 to 3785, t(17)=-0.14, d=1.18). During both seasons, males spent more time napping, resting and smoking a cigar.

Correlation between sedentary behaviours and health outcomes

We found a moderate inverse association between the Nottingham Activity of Daily Living assessment and total sedentary time in spring (r=−0.46, n=19, p=0.05) and sedentary time ≥15 min in spring (r=−0.47, n=19, p=0.05). Step count in both winter (r=0.47, n=19, p=0.05) and spring (r=0.60, n=19, p<0.01) was strongly associated with the Nottingham Activity of Daily Living assessment. There was a moderate inverse association between sedentary behaviours ≥60 min (not including sleeping) and the 5×sit-to-stand and grip strength (see online supplemental table 5).

Discussion

This is the first study to objectively quantify the context of sedentary behaviour by different seasons in community-dwelling older adults who are prefrail and frail. In winter and spring, most participants accumulated <8 hours/day of indoor sedentary behaviour (not including sleep). Although we did not find a difference between total sedentary time or the types of sedentary behaviours older adults engage in between seasons, we anticipate that activities in summer versus winter may result in differences in both sedentary time and behaviour. Of notable difference, we found step count was higher in the spring compared with the winter season. In terms of context of sedentary behaviour, the most common behaviours were screen-based activities (eg, watching TV and browsing the internet), which accounted for almost 45% of participants’ indoor behaviour. Participants mainly engaged in screen-based activities in the evening between 18:00 and 23:59 o’clock. We identified gender-based differences in sedentary behaviour between males and females, with females having a higher sitting time than males. Males had a higher step count in winter, while females had a higher step count in spring. Most sedentary behaviours occurred in the living room/family room area. We found a negative association between sedentary behaviours that were 60 min or more (excluding sleeping) and grip strength and the 5×sit-to-stand test. The result of our study suggests context-specific sedentary behaviours, prolonged continuous behaviours, time of day and season may be important factors to consider when designing sedentary behaviour interventions.

Older adults are the most sedentary cohort in our society with two-in-three older adults accumulating over 8.5 hours/day in sedentary activities.30 There is robust evidence that prolonged time spent in sedentary behaviours is associated with poorer health outcomes and premature mortality.15,8 Most national and international physical activity guidelines include specific recommendations to reduce sedentary behaviour in older adults.3 In a recent review, our group compared national and international sedentary behaviour recommendations.3 All sedentary behaviour guidelines recommend reducing total sedentary time by increasing step count; a subset of the guidelines suggests breaking up prolonged sedentary behaviour every hour with light physical activity.3 A recent cohort study published by Jama Network found that individuals who regularly take 8000 steps or more were at a lower risk of mortality.31 Despite the strong evidence for higher step counts,31 exercise prescriptions when prescribed incorrectly may increase the risk of falls and fractures.32 For example, the UK sedentary behaviour guideline suggest ‘walking while on the phone’ to increase step count33; however, there is evidence that dual tasks can increase the rate and risk of falls in older adults.34 It might be necessary to reexamine sedentary behaviour guidelines which only promote increasing step count to also consider comorbidities of older adults as well as seasonal variations that impact step count. Morever, it may be of interest to target interventions in the late evening (between 18:00 to 23:59 o’clock) when most older adults engage in screen-based sedentary activities.

Our current study found that most participants do not engage in continuous sedentary behaviour. In fact, after adjusting sedentary time to consider behaviours that were at least 15 min or more, most participants accumulated less than 8 hours/day. The average number of bouts of continuous sedentary behaviours ≥60 min (excluding sleep) was 2.2±1.4 in winter and 1.9±1.3 in spring. In spring, there was a reduction in the number of continuous bouts of prolonged sedentary behaviour suggesting weather may play a role in sedentary behaviour. If we only consider total sedentary time during the awake day, participants accumulate 7.6±4.0 hours/day in winter and 6.3±2.7 hours/day in spring, while if we consider total daily sedentary time participants accumulated 18.4±2.6 hours/day in winter and 17.7±3.1 hours/day in spring. If we consider the awake time only, community-dwelling older adults may be adhering to recommendations in the sedentary behaviour guidelines. We previously conducted a review to compare national and international sedentary behaviour guidelines in older adults which suggest the following: (1) limit total sedentary time to less than 8 hours; (2) accumulate no more than 3 hours of screen time and (3) break up prolonged sitting often.3 Our data suggest most participants are breaking up their sedentary behaviours with light physical activity every 60 min.3 We may need to reevaluate the current recommendations to reduce sedentary behaviour in community-dwelling older adults as there is robust evidence for the negative health outcomes of prolonged sitting on health.15,8

The current definition of sedentary behaviour (any waking behaviour characterised by an energy expenditure ≤1.5 MET while in a sitting or reclining posture) by the Sedentary Behaviour Research Network does not distinguish ‘sedentary individuals’ from ‘not sedentary individuals’.35 Every individual accumulates some degree of sedentary behaviour for rest, eating and socialising, and so, the current definition should specify a cut-off value in hours to determine ‘sedentariness’. A cut of time in hours may be more relatable to the public than METs. Total sedentary time may not be as relevant as continuous, prolonged sedentary behaviour.10 36 While the definition provides a cut-off as 1.5 MET, it is not clear how much accumulated time in activities of 1.5 MET would be considered sedentary. Moreover, consider two older adults who both accumulate 10 hours/day of sedentary behaviour but break up their prolonged sitting at different times; one individual breaks up their behaviour every hour while the other every 3 hours. The effects of prolonged sedentary time and how to break up sedentary time on health outcomes remain unknown. A standard sedentary behaviour definition should distinguish between types of behaviour (eg, passively watching TV vs socialising with friends and family) as well as comorbidities and season.37 38 There is emerging evidence that context-specific sitting time is associated with negative biomarkers in adults, but there are less data in older adults.37 38 Our study found a negative association between continuous bouts of 60 min or more of sedentary behaviour (excluding sleeping) and muscle-related outcomes (ie, grip strength and the 5×sit-to-stand test). There is evidence from longitudinal studies and meta-analyses that handgrip strength and lower muscle function are associated with functional outcomes such as gait speed and the risk of serious falls.39,42 Although our study was not powered to conduct a regression analysis, our results suggest there may be a potential relationship between continuous sedentary behaviours of 60 min or more and important health outcomes such as falls and sarcopenia. Moreover, weather may be an important factor to control for when considering sedentary behaviour. When implementing interventions to reduce sedentary behaviour, assessments should be done within the same season. The current definition of sedentary behaviour may need to be modified to consider continuous bouts of sedentary behaviour, the types of behaviours and season.

Almost all studies to date have examined total sedentary time in older adults rather than considering context of sedentary behaviour.743,45 To our knowledge, only one other study in the UK has objectively quantified the context of sedentary behaviour in healthy community-dwelling older adults.36 Leask et al recruited 36 older adults from Glasgow, UK and mapped the context of sedentary behaviour using a camera (Vicon Revue) and activity monitor (activPAL).36 Due to charging and privacy issues, the authors were only able to collect 1 day of camera data.36 Moreover, analysing the camera data presented challenges due to it being covered or malfunctioning.36 Although our study used a combination of wearable sensors that could passively capture data, our results were similar to the UK study. Leask et al reported that the most common sedentary behaviours were non-screen activities (62%), followed by screen-based activities (26%), while the remaining 12% were undetermined. Common non-screen-based activities included driving, reading and eating, while screen-based sedentary activities included watching TV or using a computer. Our study found that participants spent about 45% of their time engaged in screen-based activities; however, we did not capture screen-based socialising or reading, which could have affected our value. The UK study also included outdoor sedentary behaviours in their analysis. Nevertheless, we need more studies to understand context of sedentary behaviours in different seasons across a continuum of care and to understand how certain behaviours are associated and predict health outcomes important to older adults.

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of our study is the unique combination of objective and subjective measures to capture context of sedentary behaviour. We also collected data in winter and spring as we anticipated weather may play a role in sedentary behaviour and over several days (weekend and weekdays). We were able to recruit female and male older adults from across southwestern Ontario who were prefrail and frail. Nevertheless, we also must acknowledge some limitations. Participants in our study may not be representative of the rest of the Canadian population. Participants stated that they often engage in multiple activities at the same time (eg, watching TV while eating). Our current methods of capturing purpose will need to be modified to identify multiple activities within the same period. Our study did not distinguish the difference between screen-based reading versus non-screen-based reading. The diary times did not match precisely to the wearable sensor, although we attempted to limit error by linking activities that were within ±30 min of the wearable sensor. We also acknowledge the risk of response bias where self-reported data were used such as with the diary. Lastly, we did not find major differences between winter and spring sedentary behaviours; however, we hypothesise that there may be differences between winter and summer that should be explored in future studies

Conclusion

Our study is the first to objectively quantify the context of sedentary behaviour during different seasons in community-dwelling older adults who are prefrail and frail. On average, participants accumulated 7.6±4.0 hours/day of daily indoor sedentary behaviour in winter and 6.3±2.7 hours/day in spring. There were no differences in sedentary time between seasons and there were no seasonal variations in participants’ sedentary activities. There was a significant difference in step count between winter and spring. We identified gender-based differences in sedentary behaviour between males and females, with females having a longer sitting time than males. Step count in winter and spring was positively associated with the Nottingham Activity of Daily Living assessment and there was an inverse association between sedentary behaviours ≥60 min (not including sleeping) and the 5×sit-to-stand and grip strength. The current definition of sedentary behaviour may need to be modified to not only consider total sedentary time, continuous bouts >60 min, the types of behaviours, time of day and season.
