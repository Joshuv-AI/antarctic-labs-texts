Relationship between medical and general gratitude among medical students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xueyan Cheng, Jing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To develop a medical gratitude scale for medical students, and to investigate the relationship between medical gratitude and general attitude. A cross-sectional study was conducted in Guangdong, China. We used a stratified cluster sampling strategy to select 500 eligible clinical medical students in Guangdong, China. A self-administered medical gratitude scale was developed to measure the medical-specific gratitude. Fornell-Larcker criterion was used to estimate the discrimination of these two scales. A hierarchical multiple regression was used to estimate the relationships. 482 valid questionnaires were finally collected. Factor analysis showed the acceptable reliability and validity of the medical gratitude scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.930). The mean scores for medical and general gratitude scales were 3.33 ± 0.42 and 3.11 ± 0.66, respectively. Fornell-Larcker criterion confirmed the discrimination between general and medical gratitude. Hierarchical multiple regression estimated the positive relationship between medical and gratitude (β = 0.46, 95%CI 0.38, 0.54, p < 0.001). Medical gratitude was also positively associated with the frequency of gratitude education received from teachers (β = 0.10, 95% CI 0.02, 0.18, p = 0.018) and from schools (β = 0.16, 95% CI 0.08, 0.25, p < 0.001). While correlated with general gratitude, medical gratitude appears to be conceptually distinct in its focus on the professional context. Integrating gratitude education into the medical school curriculum is a viable strategy to foster professionalized future physicians.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50742-5.

Introduction

Research on gratitude has proliferated over the past two decades. Generally, gratitude could be conceptualized as an emotion, a mood, affective trait, an attitude, a personality trait, a moral virtue, a habit, or a coping mechanism1,2. Although there are differing opinions on how gratitude should be conceptualized or investigated empirically3,4, the benefits of gratitude have been verified. For example, gratitude is associated with enhanced individual’s physical health5,6, quality of life7,8, and life satisfaction9. It has also been linked to higher subjective well-being10, better sleep quality and duration11, and lower levels of depression and stress12. In organizational context, gratitude has been estimated to have positive associations with performance13 and stronger workplace relationships14. Within healthcare, gratitude has been studied predominantly from the patient perspective, where expressions of thankfulness from patients and families are recognized as meaningful professional feedback, associated with increased self-esteem and motivation among clinicians15. In contrast, gratitude among healthcare professionals, termed as medical gratitude, encompasses appreciation directed specifically towards one’s professional role, rewards, and relationships, and may foster prosocial behavior and professional pride16. However, the definition of medical gratitude remains ambiguous, and no studies have systematically demonstrated how it differs from general gratitude. Clarifying this distinction is essential for understanding its unique role in health professional development and well-being.

Medical gratitude: Based on the broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions

Based on the broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions, which posits that positive emotions, such as general gratitude, may broaden cognitive and behavioral activities to build enduring personal resources17, medical gratitude may further cultivate medical-specific psychosocial and functional benefits. Different from general population, physicians often experience material benefits, social esteem, and intrinsic rewards from patient care, which deliberate reflection on professional gratitude can serve as a moral and psychological resources. Such reflection can foster humility, reinforce a sense of meaning and control in work, and mitigate the potential negative effects of materialistic pursuits18. Although gratitude cannot be mandated as an emotion, physicians can adopt a practice of ethical reflection to recognize and habituate gratitude for the benefits inherent in their profession, thereby enhancing occupational joy and purpose19. Notably, gratitude in the healthcare field is the gratitude towards health and life, which could result in positive health outcomes5,6. The medical gratitude has received growing attention in recent years due to its direct and indirect benefits for patients, family caregiver, and healthcare professionals. A mixed-method study demonstrated that gratitude for the chosen profession and relationships was aligned with a physician’s well-being and professional fulfillment20. A cohort study in the U.S. suggested that experiencing grateful affect is associated with increased longevity among older nurses21.

In the educational context, the medical gratitude could be more specific among students, which may consist of appreciation for both their formative learning environment and early clinical experiences, including gratitude towards non-living teaching resources (such as silent mentors and laboratory animals), institutional and peer support (including school and classmates) during pre-clinical training, as well as toward supervising teachers and patients encountered during clinical internships. This educational phase represents a critical period for cultivating a professional mindset before entering the workplace. Similar to physicians, medical gratitude offers students profession-focused benefits, it may positively influence the development of professionalism, which in turn could enhance future healthcare quality, patient satisfaction, and treatment adherence22. Consequently, as the future physician workforce, medical students are encouraged to cultivate both general and medical gratitude alongside their acquisition of knowledge and technical skill.

However, despite substantial evidence supporting the value of general gratitude23,24, medical gratitude among students remains under-investigated. Existing instruments, such as the Gratitude Questionnaire-Six (GQ-6) 1 and the Gratitude Resentment and Appreciation Test (GRAT)25, measure general gratitude but do not capture the profession-specific expression in medical context. Furthermore, while interventions like gratitude education have shown positive effects on general student population26, their association with medical gratitude is unknown. To address these gaps, this study aimed to develop and validate a scale to measure medical gratitude, and to examine its association between general gratitude, and other influencing factors including gratitude education.

Methods

Study design and setting and participants.

We conducted a cross-sectional study with a stratified cluster sampling strategy to select the involved medical students. One medical college in Guangdong province, China was selected using purposive sampling. The institution was selected based on research feasibility and established collaborative access. Given the variability in educational contexts across medical universities, this study was purposefully situated within a single institution to conduct an in-depth exploration of medical gratitude. Participant recruitment was conducted internally through a stratified approach by academic year (year 1 to year 5). Three classes were selected for students at each academic year, and a cluster sampling strategy was used to collect data from each class. The inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 18 years and over; (2) should be clinical medical students; (3) could be able to give consent form; (4) be able to read and write in Chinese. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Those who did not finish the questionnaire; (2) students from other majors; (3) students who cannot read and write in Chinese.

Sample size and characteristics.

As the prevalence of gratitude is not available in this study, the sample size is determined by the “rules-of-thumb” of regression analysis (at least 10 observations per variable). Finally, a total of 500 medical students were selected, and 482 valid questionnaires were collected, with an effective rate of 96.4%. Potential confounders were also investigated. Among the included clinical medical students, 111 were in year 1 (23%), 101 in year 2 (21%), 88 in year 3 (18.3%), 97 in year 4 (20.1%), and 85 in year 5 (last year) (17.6%); 213 were male students (44.2%); 282 (58.5%) were from rural area; 99 (20.5%) were from Single-Child household; and 150 (31.1%) received funding from school.

Study instruments.

Measurement for medical gratitude

We developed a medical gratitude scale based on general gratitude scales in previous studies including GQ-61 and GRAT25. The GQ-6 has demonstrated good internal consistency in prior research, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.82 to 0.8727, while the GRAT has also shown high reliability, with alpha coefficients between 0.86 and 0.92 in adult populations28. To ensure the scale was contextually grounded, we also conducted interviews with twenty medical students to identify profession-specific themes and recipients of gratitude. Building on these qualitative insights and incorporating feedback from open-ended questionnaires, we constructed a 14-item scale. Items assess gratitude toward distinctive aspects of medical training, including hospitals, medical teachers, patients, silent mentors, and experimental animals. The items of this scale were confirmed with two experts in clinical education. Responses are recorded on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = completely disagree to 4 = completely agree). Two items are reverse-scored and were recoded accordingly during data analysis.

Measurement for general gratitude

The College Student Gratitude scale (1 to 4 points) developed by Ma and Hu was used to measure general gratitude among general college students29. The scale was developed based on the GQ-61, and consisted of 14 items (including 4 reversed items). Typical items include “I am grateful for the many wonderful things that life has provided for me”. The Cronbach’s α of the general gratitude scale was 0.82 in this study.

Gratitude education questionnaire

To explore potential factor associating with medical gratitude, a gratitude education questionnaire was developed for this study to capture students’ exposure to formal gratitude-promoting activities within their medical training. The item was formulated based on the principal investigator’s observational experience with medical training curricula and was refined through consultation with two experts in medical education. A gratitude education questionnaire with 7 items was designed from four facets (family education, teacher education, school education and social education). A 5-point Likert scoring was used (1 = never, 2 = seldom, 3 = sometimes, 4 = often, and 5 = always).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 24.0. Students with missing data were deleted in the analysis. A total of 482 participants was randomly split for Exploratory factor analysis (EFA, n1 = 241) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA, n2 = 241). EFA used principal axis factoring with Promax rotation to explore the structure of the scale. The suitability of the data for the EFA was supported by the Kaiser-Meyer Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Factors were retained based on eigenvalues > 1.00 and individual items with factor loadings ≥ 0.530. To validate the factor structure derived from the EFA, a CFA was performed on the second subsample using a Maximum Likelihood Robust (MLR) estimator. Model fit was evaluated against the following established thresholds: root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.1 31, standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) ≤ 0.08, comparative fit index (CFI) ≥ 0.90, and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) ≥ 0.90 32,33. Fornell-Larker criterion was used to assess the discrimination between general and medical gratitude.

Frequencies and percentages were used to describe categorical variables, and means and standard deviations were used to describe the distributions of continuous variables. The scores differences of different groups were tested by T-test and one-way ANOVA at a significance level of p < 0.05. Correlation analysis was used to test the correlations between medical gratitude, general gratitude and gratitude education. A hierarchical regression analysis was conducted across three sequential models to examine predictors of medical gratitude. Variables that showed a statistically significant association in univariate analyses were entered into the regression in blocks. Model 1 included only general gratitude. Model 2 added demographic covariates (birthplace and single-child household status). Model 3 included all predictor variables. Estimated coefficients (βs) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were reported to display the associations. The distinct associations of four gratitude education domains (family, teacher, school, and society) with the outcome were examined by entering each variable individually. For analytical clarity, family, teacher, and society gratitude education were dichotomized (never/seldom/sometimes vs. often/always). School gratitude education was analyzed as a continuous total score.

Results

Reliability and validity of medical gratitude scale

EFA for the medical gratitude scale with 14 items (KMO = 0.887, Barlett’s test χ2 = 1136.700, p < 0.001) identified two factors. Factor loading of two factors < 0.5 and based on the significance of the item content, we eliminated 2 items. The EFA for the final medical gratitude scale included 12 items (KMO = 0.900, Barlett’s test χ2 = 1068.96, p < 0.001), and only one factor was identified (Table 1). The CFA results confirmed the single factor structure as acceptable (RMSEA = 0.085, SRMR = 0.041, CFI = 0.924, TLI = 0.910) (Fig. 1). The Cronbach’s α for the total scale was 0.930.

There were no statistically significant differences among other demographic variables such as gender, grades (p > 0.05).

Hierarchical regression analysis of medical gratitude

Table 6 indicated a consistent, positive relationship between medical gratitude and general gratitude across all models. In the final model (Model 3), which demonstrated the best predictive fit (R² = 0.370), general gratitude was a significant predictor (β = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.38, 0.54, p < 0.001). Medical gratitude was also positively associated with the frequency of gratitude education received from teachers (often/always: β = 0.10, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.18, p = 0.018) and from schools (often/always: β = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.08, 0.25, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study conducted an important attempt to assess profession-focused gratitude in medical context. We specified the medical-specific gratitude in contrast to general gratitude, and estimated the significantly positive relationship between medical gratitude and general gratitude among medical students in China. Frequent gratitude education from family, teacher and school were also identified to have potential associations with medical gratitude.

This study developed a profession-specified gratitude scale in medical education context. The creation of such targeted scales represents a persistent and current direction in the gratitude literature. A previous scoping review demonstrated that gratitude measurement has expanded into various specialized domains, such as workplace gratitude scale, existential gratitude scale, transpersonal gratitude scale34. These domain-specific scales are typically adapted from established general measures (e.g., GQ-6, GRAT) and contextualized to reflect the unique attributes of their respective settings. This enables researchers to uncover nuanced, context-dependent expressions of gratitude and to identity emerging thematic priorities within in specialized area35. Within academic and professional training settings, cultivating domain-specific gratitude should be increasingly recommended, as it may inform the design of more holistic curricula and improve key educational outcomes—a consideration of particular relevance for medical students. Consequently, future studies are warranted to not only refine measurement but also to explore the practical implications of integrating medical gratitude into training programs to enhance professional development.

This study revealed a moderate correlation between medical gratitude and general gratitude (r = 0.609), indicating both similarities and distinctions in the medical and general gratitude experienced by medical students. This pattern aligns with the development of gratitude in other specialized domains. A meta-analysis revealed that general gratitude demonstrates moderate correlation with future gratitude (r = 0.50) and other gratitude measure (r = 0.47)36. This supports the conceptualization of general gratitude as a foundational psychological trait, while domain-specific gratitude, such as in medicine, constitute a contextually shaped expression of it. In the domain-specific context in medical education, cultivation of students relies on resources involving health, life, and ethical responsibility, such as learning from patients, silent mentors, and laboratory animals. Cultivation of medical gratitude may contribute to medical education related outcomes, such as less student burnout, positive professional attitudes, and better patient care and ultimately improve healthcare quality. In response, some universities have begun to emphasize gratitude as one of the pedagogical priorities. For example, interaction with silent mentors has been proved to foster appreciation for altruism and sanctity of life37. Structured experiences such as body donation ceremonies, the Cadaveric Oath38,39, and supported simulated dying exercises40 provide reflective spaces for students to contemplate mortality and care. Programs like “Patients as Teachers”38,39 further strengthen relational gratitude. Additionally, training in the humane and ethical treatment of laboratory animals41,42 and courses on emotional labor and clinical empathy43 represent further avenues to intentionally foster gratitude, thereby supporting both student resilience and the development of a compassionate professional identity.

Beyond general gratitude, gratitude education emerged as a significant predictor of medical gratitude. Different from general gratitude, which is often related to family atmosphere44 and social process45, medical gratitude appears to be more closely linked to formal educational experiences within the medical training context. This distinction may be attributed to the fact that medical gratitude is a domain-specific construct cultivated through longitudinal professional socialization and explicit curricular design. For example, gratitude in medical education is often fostered through institutionally organized activities—such as commemorative events during symbolic occasions (e.g., Teachers’ Day or anatomical donor memorial ceremonies)—and through exposure to professional role models, including statues and narratives of medical figures like Nightingale, Sun Simiao of the Tang Dynasty. Furthermore, dedicated courses focusing on gratitude and professional identity offer a direct pedagogical avenue to strengthen this attribute.34. Future studies should therefore specifically examine the role of intentional educational interventions in enhancing medical gratitude.

Strengths and limitations

This study developed the first medical-specific gratitude scale for medical students and employed it in a cross-sectional analysis to estimate levels of medical gratitude and its relationship with general gratitude and prior gratitude education. Several limitations should be noted. First, the single-institution design may limit the generalizability of the findings to other medical schools in China, despite the nationally diverse student body at GMU, which may partially mitigate this concern. The applicability of these findings to other cultural or national contexts also requires careful consideration, warranting future multi-institutional and cross-cultural validation. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships. Longitudinal studies are needed to further explore the dynamics between general gratitude, and gratitude education and medical gratitude. Third, methodological limitations associated with the cross-sectional design, including social desirability and self-report bias, should be acknowledged. Fourth, the very high Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the medical gratitude scale, while indicating strong internal consistency, may suggest a degree of item redundancy. Further research is warranted to refine the scale’s structure and optimize its psychometric properties. Last, the gratitude education was an exploratory variable in this study, and it was developed based on the principal investigator’s experience and expert consultation rather than on established instruments. Future research should focus on developing and validating a standardized scale.

Conclusion

We developed a self-administered medical gratitude scale with acceptable reliability and validity. Moderate correlation was found between medical and general gratitude, indicating the specialty of medical gratitude among medical students. Students receiving frequent gratitude education from teacher and school would improve medical gratitude. Future studies should pay more attention on medical-specific gratitude and explore their practical implications.

Ethics approval and informed consent

All procedures utilized in this study were conducted following the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. Ethical clearance and approval letters were secured from the Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College (No: S051). Informed consent was obtained orally from all the included medical students. During the data collection, the confidentiality and privacy of participants are ensured by maintaining their anonymity.
