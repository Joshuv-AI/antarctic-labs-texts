Collegiate Pitchers’ Attitudes Toward Fastball Velocity and Ulnar Collateral Ligament Injury Risk: A Cross-Sectional Survey Study
Authors: Benjamin W. King, Evan P. Bailey, Jesse Seilern und Aspang, Mihir Patel, Kyle Hammond, Richard M. Danilkowicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Throwing velocity is among the most consistently implicated and controversial risk factors for ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) injury. Collegiate pitchers' perceptions of the importance of fastball velocity for competitive success along with the relationship between velocity and injury risk has yet to be evaluated.Purpose/Hypothesis:The purpose of this study was to describe collegiate pitchers’ attitudes toward fastball velocity and its relationship with UCL injury risk. It was hypothesized that pitchers would place substantial importance on velocity and that a significant proportion would be aware of, and willing to accept, greater UCL injury risk in exchange for increased fastball velocity.Study Design:Cross-sectional study.Methods:An anonymous online survey was distributed to National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA) baseball pitchers from August to October 2025. The final survey instrument consisted of 30 questions divided into 4 sections: (1) demographics and injury history; (2) perceived pitching effectiveness; (3) opinions on the importance of fastball velocity; and (4) relationship between fastball velocity and UCL injury risk. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages and continuous variables as means with standard deviations. Chi-square tests compared attitude responses across NCAA division levels, history of prior UCL injury, and fastball velocity groups.Results:A total of 169 collegiate pitchers completed the survey. Most agreed that velocity is critical for in-game success (89.1%), college recruitment (66.9%), and advancement to professional baseball (91.8%), while also acknowledging its association with increased UCL injury risk (67.6%). On average, pitchers were willing to accept a 10.0% ± 10.9% increase in UCL injury risk for a 1-mph gain in velocity. High-velocity pitchers were more likely to play in Division I (69.2% vs 17.6%; P < .001), be starters (63.8% vs 40.2%; P = .002), and perceive themselves as effective (98.8% vs 89.7%; P = .02). Low-velocity pitchers more often agreed they would have been recruited by a better college program had they thrown harder in high school (90.8% vs 66.3%; P < .001). Pitchers with a UCL injury history more often agreed that sustaining earlier UCL surgery was preferable (63.6% vs 36.7%; P = .001).Conclusion:This study showed that collegiate pitchers view fastball velocity as vital for competitive success and career advancement. Despite acknowledging the link between velocity and UCL injury risk, many are willing to accept added risk in exchange for enhanced performance.

The extreme biomechanical stress produced by the overhead throwing motion places the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) of baseball players at elevated risk of injury.4,11,13,17,18 UCL injuries are often career altering for pitchers, frequently requiring extended periods of rest and rehabilitation and prolonged absence from competition. 1 While a number of risk factors have been linked to UCL injuries, increased throwing velocity is among the most consistently implicated and controversial.2,3,9,12,16

In the 2024 Report on Pitcher Injuries released by Major League Baseball (MLB), 12 increasing pitch velocity was identified as a primary driver of the rising incidence of UCL injuries in professional baseball. Despite this, the average fastball velocity in professional baseball has continued to climb over the past 2 decades. This trend is likely attributable to advances in player development and training, as well as the fact that velocity remains one of the few objective measures directly linked with in-game pitching success. 21 Understanding pitchers’ attitudes toward fastball velocity and its relationship to injury is a critical step in contextualizing the contemporary rise in both velocity and UCL injuries. These attitudes may influence training behaviors, risk taking, and openness to injury prevention strategies.

Prior studies have examined pitchers’ general perceptions of UCL injury risk, but no study has specifically investigated collegiate pitchers’ views on the role of fastball velocity in performance and career advancement, its perceived association with UCL injury, or willingness to accept increased injury risk in exchange for added velocity. 20 The purpose of this cross-sectional survey study was to describe National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA) pitchers’ attitudes toward fastball velocity and its relationship with UCL injury risk. We hypothesized that pitchers would place substantial importance on velocity and that a significant proportion would be aware of, and willing to accept, greater UCL injury risk in exchange for increased fastball velocity.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study was deemed exempt by the local institutional review board (Emory University). An anonymous online survey (Qualtrics) was developed to evaluate collegiate pitchers’ attitudes toward fastball velocity and its relationship with UCL injury risk. Two sports medicine fellowship–trained orthopaedic surgeons (R.M.D. and K.H.) and a recent former collegiate pitcher (B.W.K.) developed the survey through iterative review.

Survey Instrument

The final survey instrument consisted of 30 questions divided into 4 sections: (1) demographics and injury history; (2) perceived pitching effectiveness; (3) opinions on the importance of fastball velocity; and (4) relationship between fastball velocity and UCL injury risk. A copy of the survey instrument is provided in the Appendix.

Demographics and Injury History

Respondents reported their NCAA division level (I, II, or III), year in college, handedness, and pitching role (starter vs reliever). They also were asked whether they had sustained a prior UCL injury. If yes, conditional follow-up questions assessed treatment method and time lost from competition.

Perceived Pitching Effectiveness

Respondents reported their peak and average fastball velocity (mph), perceived effectiveness as a collegiate pitcher, and current standing on their team.

Importance of Fastball Velocity

Participants rated their agreement (5-point Likert scale from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”) with statements about the importance of fastball velocity for pitching success, collegiate recruitment, and progression to professional baseball.

Relationship of Fastball Velocity and UCL Injury Risk

Using the same Likert scale, respondents evaluated the relationship between fastball velocity and usage with UCL injury risk, willingness to accept increased injury risk for added velocity, and perceived career impact of UCL surgery. Based on prior findings by Prodromo et al, 16 respondents were asked if they would be willing to accept a 15% increase in UCL injury risk for a 1 mph gain in fastball velocity. 8

Data Collection

Recruitment took place over a 2-month period from August to October 2025. An electronic survey link was distributed via email to pitching coaches and certified athletic trainers (ATCs) at 144 NCAA baseball programs across Divisions I to III, who were asked to forward the invitation to collegiate pitchers at their institution. Participation was voluntary and responses were collected anonymously through the secure online platform. No incentives were offered. A total of 169 pitchers completed the survey. Occasional question nonresponse led to minor variations in sample size for some questions.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed using Excel (Microsoft Corp) and R (R Core Team). Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographics, injury history, pitching performance, and attitude responses. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages and continuous variables as means with standard deviations.

Likert scale responses were summarized descriptively and dichotomized into 2 categories (Agree: strongly agree, somewhat agree; Not Agree: neither agree nor disagree, slightly disagree, strongly disagree) for subgroup comparison. Average fastball velocity was dichotomized into high- and low-velocity subgroups based on the sample median. Chi-square tests were used to compare attitude responses across NCAA division level, history of prior UCL injury, and fastball velocity group. Given the exploratory nature of subgroup comparisons, no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. P values < .05 were considered statistically significant.

For selected subgroup comparisons, a post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf) to estimate the minimal detectable effect sizes given the achieved sample sizes. Analyses were calibrated with a significance level (α = .05) and desired power (1–β = 0.80), assuming 2-tailed testing.

Results

The electronic survey link was distributed via email to pitching coaches and ATCs at 144 NCAA baseball programs across Divisions I to III, and a total of 169 NCAA baseball pitchers completed the survey (Table 1). Responses were generally evenly distributed across class year, and Division I was the most common level of competition (41.4%). A total of 57 respondents (33.7%) reported a prior UCL injury. Among these, 28 (49.1%) were managed nonoperatively, 13 (22.8%) underwent UCL reconstruction, and 16 (28.1%) underwent UCL repair. The mean reported absence from competition following UCL injury was 8.6 ± 5.4 months.

Perceptions of Fastball Velocity

Pitchers overwhelmingly agreed that fastball velocity is important for in-game success (89.1%) and that they would be more effective with increased velocity (66.9%) (Table 3). Two-thirds (66.9%) believed that their fastball velocity influenced their college recruitment, and more than three-quarters (75%) believed they would have been recruited to a better college baseball program if they had thrown harder in high school. Greater than 90% agreed that their fastball velocity would affect their chances of playing professional baseball (91.8%) and that professional baseball scouts would show them more attention if they threw harder (94.7%). In addition, more than half believed that increased velocity would result in more innings pitched (55.1%) and a more prominent role on their team (59.8%).

Perceptions of UCL Injury Risk

Approximately two-thirds of respondents agreed that pitchers who throw harder are at greater risk of UCL injury, and 57.9% believed that personally increasing their fastball velocity would increase their own risk of UCL injury (Table 4). Only 24.4% believed that throwing a higher percentage of fastballs increased UCL injury risk. Close to 1 in 3 (30.5%) indicated that they would be willing to accept a 15% increase in UCL injury risk for a 1-mph gain in velocity. On average, pitchers were willing to accept a 10.0% ± 10.9% (range, 0%-50%) increased risk of UCL injury for a 1-mph velocity gain.

Subgroup Analyses

The median reported average fastball velocity was 88 mph (39.3 m/s). Respondents were divided into low-velocity (≤88 mph) and high-velocity (>88 mph) groups. High-velocity pitchers were significantly more likely to compete at the Division I level (54/78 [69.2%] vs 15/85 [17.6%]; P < .001) and to be starting pitchers (51/80 [63.8%] vs 35/87 [40.2%]; P = .002). High-velocity pitchers were also significantly more likely to consider themselves an effective pitcher (79/80 [98.8%] vs 78/87 [89.7%]; P = .02) and in the top half of pitchers on their team (69/80 [86.3%] vs 64/87 [73.6%]; P = .04). Low-velocity pitchers were significantly less likely to agree that fastball velocity was an important factor in their collegiate recruitment (47/87 [54.0%] vs 64/80 [80.0%]; P < .001) but more likely to believe they would have been recruited to a better collegiate baseball program if they had thrown harder in high school (79/87 [90.8%] vs 53/80 [66.3%]; P < .001).

Division I pitchers reported significantly higher peak (40.7 ± 1.2 m/s vs 39.1 ± 1.3 m/s; P < .001) and average (39.2 ± 1.4 m/s vs 37.6 ± 1.4 m/s; P < .001) fastball velocities than Division II and III pitchers. Division I pitchers agreed significantly more frequently that fastball velocity was an important factor in their collegiate recruitment (54/70 [77.1%] vs 55/94 [58.5%]; P = .01) but agreed less frequently that they might have been recruited to a better collegiate baseball program if they had thrown harder in high school (47/70 [67.1%] vs. 82/94 [87.2%]; P = .001). Division I pitchers were also significantly more likely to believe that they would pitch more innings for their college team if they had greater velocity (46/70 [65.7%] vs 44/94 [46.8%]; P = .02).

Finally, pitchers with a history of UCL injury were significantly more likely to believe it is better to sustain a UCL early in a career and undergo surgery sooner rather than later compared with uninjured pitchers (35/55 [63.6%] vs 40/109 [36.7%]; P = .001). Other responses did not differ significantly between these groups.

Discussion

The most significant findings of this study are that the overwhelming majority of collegiate pitchers believed fastball velocity is critical for in-game success, college recruitment, and advancement to professional baseball, while simultaneously recognizing that increased velocity is associated with greater UCL injury risk. On average, pitchers were willing to accept a 10% increase in UCL injury risk for a 1-mph gain in velocity. High-velocity pitchers were more likely to play in Division I, be starters, and perceive themselves as effective, whereas low-velocity pitchers more often agreed they might have been recruited by a better college baseball program had they thrown harder in high school. Division I pitchers more frequently agreed that velocity was an important factor in their collegiate recruitment and playing time. Pitchers with a history of UCL injury were more likely to agree that sustaining a UCL injury earlier in one's career and undergoing surgery sooner was preferable. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly examine collegiate pitchers’ attitudes toward the importance of fastball velocity and its relationship with UCL injury risk.

These findings provide significant insight into the contemporary rise in fastball velocity and the attitudes of elite amateur athletes toward performance. Collegiate pitchers clearly believe that velocity directly affects competitive success, college recruitment opportunities, and professional advancement, and these attitudes could be reflective of observation, direct experience, or instruction from coaches and scouts. At the Division I level, these pressures appear amplified, with pitchers believing velocity was an important determinant of playing time. Such beliefs reinforce the current pervasive “velocity culture” within baseball identified by MLB's recent Report on Pitcher Injuries. 12

While the results show that the majority of pitchers acknowledged that greater velocity increases UCL injury risk, nearly 40% reported that they did not agree that there was a relationship between increased velocity and injury risk, highlighting a significant opportunity for improved patient education. When asked directly about their willingness to accept a 15% increase in UCL injury risk for a 1-mph increase in fastball velocity, nearly 1 in 3 responded that they would. On average, pitchers reported that they would be willing to accept a 10% increase in injury risk for this gain. These risk-tolerant attitudes may be related to the common belief of respondents that pitchers return throwing as hard or harder after UCL surgery. Together, these beliefs appear to create a “perfect storm”—pitchers feel constant pressure to increase velocity while simultaneously believing that UCL surgery is a reliable procedure for restoring performance in all patients, potentially encouraging risk-taking behaviors. In reality, outcomes of UCL surgery are less predictable. Swindell et al 19 previously reported that 15.5% of collegiate pitchers failed to return to competition postoperatively, and only 4.1% of injured pitchers went on to pitch professionally, representing a stark contrast between players’ beliefs and the current reality presented by the literature. This information is critical, as it represents a clear and identifiable avenue for improved education, particularly among amateur baseball players, that is necessary to ensure pitchers appreciate both the risks and the limitations of surgery.

The actual relationship between velocity and UCL injury risk remains unsettled. 7 Prodromo et al 16 and Mastroianni et al 10 reported 15% and 20% increases in UCL injury risk per 1-mph increase in velocity, respectively. In contrast, Chalmers et al 2 found that a regression model including velocity, age, and body mass index explained only 7% of UCL injury risk, and multiple other studies have found no association between velocity and UCL injury risk.6,14 Most recently, Fleisig et al 5 performed a prospective longitudinal study and found that velocity was not predictive of UCL injury risk, whereas elbow varus torque was the only significant risk factor. UCL injury risk is multifactorial and a number of factors outside of solely velocity, including increased workloads, poor conditioning, and biomechanical considerations, ultimately contribute to an athlete's risk of injury.

Existing biomechanical research may help to reconcile these discrepancies. While velocity and elbow varus torque are strongly correlated within an individual pitcher, the relationship is far weaker across pitchers.5,9,13,15,17,18 This suggests that some pitchers are able to achieve high velocity more efficiently without a proportional increase in elbow torque. Our findings indicate that collegiate pitchers view velocity as vital for competitive success and career advancement and are unlikely to reduce their pursuit of velocity in the name of injury prevention. Future prevention strategies should focus on identifying the biomechanical and physiological factors that allow some pitchers to throw harder without increasing elbow stress. Elucidation of these factors may ultimately provide more realistic and effective injury prevention strategies that minimize injury risk without sacrificing performance.

The findings of this study ultimately identify several areas for improved patient education and highlight the pervasive so-called velocity culture that exists within the game of baseball. From youth leagues to the professional ranks, radar gun readings dominate the minds of fans, coaches, scouts, and perhaps most importantly, pitchers themselves. For physicians, recognizing this performance-focused priority system is critical. A better understanding of the cultural importance of velocity in baseball will guide more effective education and shared decision-making with athletes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it utilized a convenience sample, and results from the respondents may not be representative of all collegiate pitchers; those with stronger views may have been more likely to participate. Despite this, the study captured responses from a broad cohort across Division I, II, and III levels, providing meaningful insights into common beliefs that likely reflect the general attitudes of collegiate pitchers. Second, responses were self-reported and may be subject to recall or reporting bias. Because the survey primarily assessed current beliefs and attitudes rather than past events, the risk of recall bias was minimized, and the anonymity of the survey likely reduced social desirability bias. Third, some respondents did not answer all questions, creating minor variations in response counts. The rate of missing responses was low and did not materially alter subgroup comparisons or main findings. Analyses were conducted using available data for each question, a common and accepted method in survey research. Finally, as a cross-sectional survey, this study reflects pitcher attitudes at a single time point and cannot capture how beliefs evolve over the course of a career. Although the survey captures attitudes at one time point, this snapshot provides clinically meaningful insight into pitchers’ perceptions during their collegiate careers, a critical stage for injury risk and development. Future longitudinal studies can build upon these results to evaluate how attitudes shift over time.

Conclusion

Our study showed that collegiate pitchers view fastball velocity as vital for competitive success and career advancement. Despite acknowledging the link between velocity and UCL injury risk, many are willing to accept added risk in exchange for enhanced performance.
