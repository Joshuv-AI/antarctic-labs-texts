Experimental and RSM simulation assessment of Gamma-ray shielding efficiency of nano-modified concrete exposed to elevated temperatures
Authors: Islam N. Fathy, Hany A. Dahish, Mohammed K. Alkharisi, Mohamed A. Abouelnour, Alaa A. Mahmoud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study experimentally investigates the radiation shielding performance, characterized by the linear attenuation coefficient (LAC) values, of concrete mixes incorporating lead monoxide (NL), nano-magnetite (NM), and nano-granodiorite (NG) as partial cement replacements (1–5%) under ambient and elevated temperatures (400–800 °C). The primary objective was to develop robust predictive models for the LAC values using Response Surface Methodology (RSM). Results showed that exposure to elevated temperatures notably reduced the LAC of the control concrete, especially after heating to 800 °C. Incorporating nanomaterials effectively mitigated this deterioration, maintaining higher shielding efficiency. NL mixes showed the greatest improvement, with LAC increases of about 42.6% and 37.4% after exposure to 600 °C and 800 °C, respectively. NM mixes ranked second, enhancing LAC by 26.7% and 24.2% at the same temperatures, while NG provided moderate improvement of around 9.9% at 800 °C (4% replacement). The superior performance of NL and NM mixes is attributed to their high density and atomic number, which promote photon absorption and scattering within the concrete matrix, as well as the improved microstructural integrity resulting from the filler effect of the uniformly dispersed nanoscale particles. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) confirmed the high statistical significance and predictive power of the developed model, demonstrating an exceptional correlation between predicted and experimental data. The response surface analysis revealed that the nanomaterial dose and type are the dominant factor for enhancing LAC values, exhibiting a strong positive correlation, whereas elevated temperature has a detrimental effect. The observed curvature in the response surfaces confirmed significant non-linear and interaction effects between the input parameters. Optimization results demonstrated that a 5% nano-dose at various temperatures maximizes shielding performance, with NL identified as the most effective material, demonstrating superior radiation shielding properties.

Introduction

Concrete is among the most widely used construction materials worldwide1. It plays a crucial role not only in conventional structures but also in specialized applications, including nuclear facilities, radiation shielding, and high-temperature environments2. Concrete is the second most widely used material in the world after water, with an annual consumption of over 10 billion tons3–5. Due to its versatility and cost-effectiveness, concrete is indispensable in nuclear facilities, medical radiation centers, and radioactive waste storage where structural safety and radiation protection are critical6.Gamma radiation poses one of the most significant hazards among ionizing radiations due to its high penetration ability and energy, which enable it to pass through most materials and biological tissues7. Prolonged or unshielded exposure to gamma rays can cause severe health effects, including cellular damage, genetic mutations, and increased cancer risk8. Therefore, effective gamma-ray shielding is crucial to protect human health, maintain environmental safety, and ensure the safe operation of facilities dealing with radioactive sources9. Shielding capability is determined mainly by a material’s density, atomic number, and its interaction profile across various radiation types and energy levels10. Therefore, the use of dense materials with high atomic numbers, such as heavy-weight concrete incorporating special aggregates, has proven to be one of the most efficient and practical approaches to attenuate gamma radiation in structural applications11,12. However, elevated temperatures significantly alter concrete’s physical, mechanical, and microstructural properties, compromising its structural performance and radiation shielding effectiveness13. Assessing concrete’s resistance to high temperatures is complicated by its composite nature and the different thermal characteristics of its constituents14. Temperature elevation affects concrete through interconnected chemical and mechanical mechanisms. Chemically, elevated temperatures progressively break down bonding materials through temperature-dependent reactions. Surface and capillary water evaporates first13. Between 100 and 110 °C, free water rapidly evaporates while thermal expansion of aggregates generates internal pressures13. The 80–150 °C range marks ettringite dehydration, followed by gypsum decomposition at 150–170 °C13. At 400 °C, chemically bound water evaporates, initiating significant strength reductions and deteriorating calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H), the primary binding phase15,16. Between 400 and 540 °C, calcium hydroxide decomposes (Ca(OH)₂ → CaO + H₂O↑), causing volumetric shrinkage17. Simultaneously, quartz phase transformation occurs in siliceous aggregates at 500–600 °C, introducing internal stresses18. Above 600 °C, C-S-H decomposes into β-C₂S, while calcium carbonate undergoes calcination, reaching complete degradation by 900 °C19. Mechanically, these transformations manifest as progressive cracking, spalling, loss of load-bearing capacity, and increased porosity20,21. Deterioration extent depends on heating rate, peak temperature, exposure duration, concrete composition and age, cooling method, and surface geometry21–23.

Thermal exposure significantly compromises mechanical properties24,25, with strength reductions of 20–30% at 400 °C escalating to 70–90% beyond 800 °C, influenced by mixture composition, heating rate, and cooling methodology18,24. Concurrently, physical characteristics experience notable alterations as porosity and permeability surge due to moisture loss, hydration product breakdown, and thermally-induced microcracking26,27. The resulting porous structure enables deeper ingress of deleterious agents, compromising both structural safety and radiation attenuation capacity. Consequently, enhanced thermal stability and post- high temperature exposure strength are key to improve concrete’s durability and fire resistance28.

Gamma-ray shielding performance is determined by the linear attenuation coefficient (LAC), which depends on material density, composition, and high-Z elements that enhance photoelectric absorption29. Traditional shielding concrete incorporates heavy aggregates such as barite, magnetite, or hematite to increase density and attenuation30,31. However, elevated temperatures cause density loss, increased porosity, and microstructural degradation that significantly reduce shielding effectiveness32. For radiation shielding applications, concrete’s temperature-dependent behavior is critical because gamma-ray attenuation effectiveness directly correlates with material density33,34. Thermal degradation and porosity development decrease bulk density, reducing LAC values and weakening shielding performance. LAC is directly proportional to material density and governed by the mass attenuation coefficient (µ/ρ), an intrinsic property determined by elemental composition and photon energy35. For gamma energies of 0.662–1.332 MeV, characteristic of common radioisotopes such as 137Cs and 60Co, concrete LAC values typically range from 0.10 to 0.18 cm⁻¹ based on composition and density36. Nanotechnology has enabled new approaches to enhance concrete through nanomaterials, which provide superior reactivity, higher packing density, and the ability to introduce beneficial elements at the nanoscale. Nano-engineered concrete shows improved mechanical strength, durability, and thermal stability compared to conventional mixtures37. For radiation shielding, nanomaterials with high-Z elements can enhance LAC values while potentially improving thermal resistance38. Selecting suitable nanomaterials for radiation shielding enhancement requires evaluating multiple factors: radiation attenuation efficiency, thermal stability, cement chemistry compatibility, cost-effectiveness, and environmental impact. Nano lead (NL) or Lead monoxide (PbO) nanoparticles provide superior gamma-ray attenuation owing to lead’s high atomic number and density, which yield large photoelectric absorption cross-sections, especially at lower gamma energies39. Nevertheless, lead’s toxicity, potential leaching risks, and stringent environmental regulations demand thorough assessment of its practical applicability40. Despite these concerns, numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of lead-based materials in enhancing concrete’s radiation shielding capabilities. Hassan et al.41 investigated the effect of incorporating two types of nano-lead compounds (PbO and PbTiO3 nano powders) on concrete’s gamma-ray shielding characteristics at varying percentages. The study compared nano-sized additives prepared via co-precipitation and oxalate precursor techniques with commercial PbO₂ powder to evaluate particle size effects on attenuation properties. Phase composition and particle size were confirmed through XRD and TEM imaging, while gamma-ray attenuation coefficients were measured at energies of 662, 1173, and 1332 keV, demonstrating the significance of both additive type and particle size in enhancing concrete’s radiation shielding performance. Mokhtari et al.42 investigated ordinary concrete reinforced with lead oxide micro/nano particles using Monte Carlo simulation and experimental methods. Results showed that lead oxide substantially enhanced mass attenuation coefficients with negligible differences between micro and nano-sized particles. Mass attenuation increased with nanoparticle weight fraction up to 10 wt%, where semi-saturation occurred. Experimental validation using 22Na, 137Cs, and 60Co sources revealed that 5 wt% nano-PbO reduced the half-value layer by 64% at 511 keV and 48% at 1332 keV, demonstrating superior efficiency at gamma energies up to 1 MeV. Mohamed et al.43 investigated NL incorporation into alkali-activated slag mortar at 0, 1, 3, and 5 wt%, evaluating mechanical properties and gamma-ray shielding at 662, 1173, and 1332 keV using Co-60 and Cs-137 sources. Results showed that flowability increased with nPbO content, while mechanical strength and aging resistance peaked at 1–3% before declining at 5%. Gamma-ray attenuation coefficients improved progressively with nPbO addition, with optimal ratios of 1–3% enhancing both mechanical and radiation shielding properties.

Nano-magnetite (NM) (Fe3O4), with iron’s moderate atomic number and density, provides reasonable radiation shielding capability while offering better environmental compatibility and potential additional benefits such as magnetic properties44,45. Magnetite’s thermal stability and established use in heavyweight concrete formulations make it a practical choice for nuclear applications46. Kunchariyakun et al.45 investigated the effect of NM additions (1–10 wt%) on gamma and neutron radiation shielding properties of mortar. Results demonstrated that nano-Fe3O4 enhanced radiation shielding through pore filling and nucleation effects, with 5 wt% providing optimal performance: density of 1,847 kg/cm3, compressive strength of 216.79 ksc at 28 days, and improved photon LAC from 0.144 to 0.155 cm−1. Sikora et al.47 examined NM in Portland cement paste at 5–30 wt% replacement levels, demonstrating enhanced gamma-ray shielding across the 0.08–2.614 MeV energy range and confirming its effectiveness as a lead-free radiation shielding additive.

In the construction industry, one of the primary concerns today is to minimize the environmental impact of traditional cementitious binders48,49. This can be achieved by utilizing supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) such as fly ash, ground granulated blast furnace slag, silica fume, and others50–53. Among these alternatives, nano-granodiorite (NG), derived from granodiorite igneous rock containing quartz, plagioclase, and potassium feldspar, has recently gained attention as a sustainable and naturally occurring SCM54. NG which is derived from granodiorite igneous rock containing quartz, plagioclase, potassium feldspar, represents a naturally occurring, sustainable alternative that introduces a complex elemental composition including silicon, aluminum, calcium, potassium, iron55. Granodiorite demonstrated enhanced radiation attenuation capability and potential pozzolanic activity in both micro and nano forms. Fathy et al.56 investigated the individual and combined effects of micro and nano lead monoxide (PbO) and granodiorite on concrete’s mechanical and radiation shielding properties, demonstrating that increasing PbO ratios directly enhanced radiation attenuation capacity. The optimal individual replacement ratios were 5% for both micro and nano PbO, achieving linear attenuation coefficient improvements of 24.78% and 39.57%, respectively. However, optimal mechanical properties were observed at lower ratios of 2% for micro-PbO and 3% for nano-PbO, with higher concentrations (5% micro-PbO) causing a 7.02% reduction in compressive strength. The combined mixture of 5% nano-PbO + 4% nano-GD demonstrated synergistic effects, increasing compressive strength, tensile strength, and linear attenuation coefficient by 25.7%, 16.2%, and 44.7%, respectively, compared to the control mix. Mahmoud et al.32 investigated granodiorite waste powder as partial cement replacements, demonstrating that 7% waste granodiorite powder (WGDP) substitution yielded optimal results with compressive strength improvements of 24.7%, 26.1%, 22%, and 28% at room temperature, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C, respectively. The optimal WGDP mix also enhanced radiation attenuation capability, contributing positively to concrete’s shielding performance against both gamma rays and neutrons.

Despite the growing interest in nano-modified concrete for radiation shielding applications, significant knowledge gaps remain regarding the combined effects of nanomaterial incorporation and elevated temperature exposure on LAC performance. Most existing studies have focused either on room-temperature radiation shielding properties of nano-modified concrete or on the thermal behavior of conventional concrete, but comprehensive investigations examining the interaction between these factors are limited. Furthermore, the development of predictive models that can accurately forecast LAC values as a function of nanomaterial dosage, temperature exposure, and material type remains an underexplored area with significant practical importance for nuclear facility design and safety assessment. Understanding these thermal degradation mechanisms and developing mitigation strategies through advanced material modifications is crucial for nuclear safety engineering and radiation protection17. The interactive effects of temperature, nanomaterial type, and replacement ratio on gamma ray LAC of the nano-modified concrete require comprehensive evaluation and optimization using combined experimental and statistical approaches.

The followed methodology in the current study utilized the Central Composite Design (CCD) within the Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to model the relationships between these factors. RSM is a statistical and mathematical technique widely used for developing, improving, and optimizing processes by exploring the interactions among multiple variables and their combined effects on a specific response. The CCD, as one of the most effective experimental designs under RSM, enables efficient estimation of both linear and quadratic effects, allowing for the generation of predictive models and optimization of the studied parameters with a minimal number of experimental trials57. Model performance was validated using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), and RSM’s numerical optimization was subsequently applied to identify the optimal mix proportions for enhancing LAC values of the investigated concrete mixes under high-temperature conditions. The choice of the RSM model depends primarily on the data characteristics and the number of independent variables. The user-defined method is advantageous as it permits model construction and analysis from a pre-established experimental matrix, granting flexibility in optimizing the experimental work58. RSM has been widely and successfully utilized in literature studies, particularly for optimizing concrete formulations, developing predictive models, and effectively reducing experimental complexity59–65, confirming its utility as a robust tool for both optimization and modeling.

Materials and methods

This section outlines the materials, mix compositions, and experimental procedures adopted in the present study. The physical and chemical characteristics of all constituent materials are described, followed by details of mix preparation, specimen casting, curing, and testing protocols. Special emphasis is given to the incorporation of various nanomaterials and their replacement ratios, as well as to the experimental setup designed to assess the gamma-ray shielding efficiency of the developed nano-modified concrete under elevated temperature conditions.

Materials properties

Cementitious materials

In the present investigation, Portland cement of classification CEM I 52.5 N, procured from commercially available sources within the local market, was employed as the primary cementitious binding agent. The cement conformed to the specifications outlined in ASTM C15066 – Standard Specification for Portland Cement, and was incorporated at a dosage of 500 kg/m³ in the control mixture formulation. The physical and time-dependent characteristics of the cement included a specific gravity of 3.15, Blaine surface area of 3600 cm²/g, mean particle size of 9 μm, initial setting time of 150 min, and final setting time of 235 min. Compressive strength assessment of standard mortar cubes, conducted in accordance with ASTM C10967, demonstrated strength development of 27.3 MPa, 37.5 MPa, and 53.4 MPa at curing ages of 3,7, and 28 days, respectively, thereby validating conformance with CEM I 52.5 grade performance criteria. The mixture design additionally incorporated nano-silica (NS), recognized for its elevated pozzolanic reactivity, as a constant supplementary additive at 2% by mass of cement across all experimental mixtures. Table 1 delineates the chemical composition of both the Portland cement and the nanomaterials utilized in this study. The NS employed herein constitutes a gel-type material characterized by an amorphous structural configuration, exceptionally refined particle dimensions, and substantially elevated specific surface area. These synergistic attributes confer pronounced pozzolanic reactivity and substantial capacity for microstructural densification in cementitious composites. The NS gel utilized in this investigation was synthesized via the sol-gel method, wherein a silicon alkoxide precursor undergoes sequential hydrolysis and condensation reactions within an alkaline medium, resulting in the formation of a colloidal silica suspension. The resultant sol was subsequently subjected to stabilization and concentration procedures to yield a homogeneous nanoscale colloidal gel exhibiting high purity and enhanced reactivity. Aggregate

The aggregate utilized in this experimental investigation consisted of dolomite-derived coarse aggregate and siliceous fine sand, meticulously selected to conform to the specifications prescribed in relevant standards. The coarse aggregate fraction possessed a nominal maximum particle size of 19 mm and exhibited the following characteristic properties. specific gravity of 2.65, bulk density of 1660 kg/m³, water absorption capacity of 0.45%, deleterious material content (clay and fines) of 1%, and an aggregate crushing value of 22%. The specific gravity and water absorption characteristics were determined following the methodology outlined in ASTM C12768. The aggregate crushing value was established in accordance with the standardized procedures delineated in BS 812 − 110:199069.

The fine aggregate fraction was characterized by a fineness modulus of 2.46, specific gravity of 2.55, bulk density of 1730 kg/m³, and water absorption of 0.9%. The specific gravity and absorption percentage were ascertained in conformity with the procedures established in ASTM C12870. Furthermore, the bulk density was quantified in accordance with the methodology prescribed in ASTM C2971. The gradation characteristics of both aggregate fractions were systematically assessed through standardized sieve analysis procedures. Particle size distribution analysis demonstrated that both coarse and fine aggregate gradations consistently fell within the upper and lower boundary limits established by ASTM C3372 specifications. This conformance validated the appropriateness of the selected aggregates for the production of high-performance concrete mixtures, ensuring optimal particle packing efficiency and enhanced mechanical properties through proper aggregate interlock and void minimization.

Water and superplasticizer

Clean potable water, meeting the specifications prescribed in the Egyptian Code for Design and Construction of Concrete Structures, was utilized for all mixing and curing procedures to ensure the structural integrity and quality of test specimens. The water-to-cement ratio was systematically optimized while preserving adequate workability through the incorporation of a high-range water-reducing superplasticizer (SP). HYMIX G-500, a polycarboxylate-based superplasticizer obtained from local commercial sources, was employed at a constant dosage of 1.8% by mass of cement throughout all mixture formulations. The liquid chemical admixture is characterized by a specific gravity of 1.16 kg/L, pH value of 5.8, and solid content of 30%, demonstrating full conformance with ASTM C49473 Type G specifications for high-range water-reducing admixtures. The incorporation of superplasticizer proved essential for achieving proper dispersion of concrete constituents and sustaining workability requirements, particularly in response to the exceptionally elevated specific surface area characteristic of the nanomaterials employed in this investigation. Beyond workability enhancement, the SP fulfills a critical function in disrupting nano-particle agglomerations and inhibiting cluster formation, thereby facilitating homogeneous distribution of nanomaterials throughout the cementitious matrix and optimizing their pozzolanic reaction efficiency.

NL

NL (PbO) nanoparticles used in this study were synthesized by Nanotech Company, Egypt. As shown in Fig. 2a, the nano powder exhibits a characteristic bright white appearance indicative of high-purity lead oxide. TEM analysis (Fig. 2b) reveals irregularly shaped, electron-dense nanoparticles with an average particle size of 50.4 nm. XRD analysis (Fig. 2c) confirms the presence of both litharge (tetragonal) and massicot (orthorhombic) PbO polymorphs. The diffraction pattern exhibits three prominent peaks at 2θ = 22.3°, 29.1°, and 30.6°, corresponding to the major crystallographic reflections of the mixed-phase PbO structure. Additional characteristic peaks observed at 2θ = 15.6°, 18.2°, 23.1°, 27.3°, 28.6°, 31.6°, 35.8°, and 48.6° confirm the polycrystalline nature of the material. The relatively broad peak profiles and moderate intensities are characteristic of nanoscale crystallites, consistent with the average particle size of 50.4 nm determined by TEM analysis, indicating successful synthesis of nanostructured lead oxide.

NM

Nano-magnetite (Fe3O4) was synthesized via chemical co-precipitation by Nanotech Company, Egypt. The synthesis involved dissolving ferrous (Fe²⁺) and ferric (Fe³⁺) salts at a 1:2 molar ratio in deionized water under an inert atmosphere. Dropwise addition of a basic solution (NH₄OH or NaOH) under vigorous stirring at 60–80 °C yielded high-purity magnetite nanoparticles with a density of 5.17 g/cm³. The comprehensive characterization is presented in Fig. 3. The as-synthesized powder exhibits a characteristic black coloration (Fig. 3a), resulting from the mixed valence iron oxide structure. TEM analysis (Fig. 3b) reveals irregularly shaped nanoparticles with some agglomeration and an average particle size of 44.5 nm. XRD analysis (Fig. 3c) confirms the cubic inverse spinel structure of Fe₃O₄ (magnetite), with characteristic diffraction peaks observed at 2θ values of 29.9°, 35.5°, and 43.1°. Additional peaks are identified at 2θ = 17.5°, 26.7°, and 39.2. The sharp, well-defined peaks indicate high crystallinity and phase purity, with no detectable secondary phases. The sharp diffraction peaks indicate high crystallinity and phase purity, with no detectable impurities, confirming successful synthesis of magnetite nanoparticles suitable for cementitious applications.

NG

Granodiorite rock samples were mechanically processed to nanoscale dimensions using planetary ball milling. After initial crushing, the material was ground with hardened steel balls at 370 RPM for 17 h, achieving effective size reduction with minimal contamination. Figure 4 presents the comprehensive characterization of the nano-granodiorite. The milled powder exhibits a characteristic fine gray appearance (Fig. 4a). TEM analysis (Fig. 4b) reveals irregularly shaped, angular particles with an average size of 52.7 nm. This morphology is typical of the mechanically fractured crystalline materials. XRD analysis (Fig. 4c) confirms the multi-phase mineralogy typical of granodiorite. Quartz, the dominant phase, exhibits characteristic peaks at 2θ = 26.6°, 20.8°, 36.6°, 39.5°, 40.3°, and 42.9°. Albite, a sodium-rich plagioclase feldspar, displays major reflections at 2θ = 27.8°, 22.0°, 24.3°, and 28.3°. Biotite, a phyllosilicate mica, shows its characteristic basal reflection at 2θ = 8.7°, along with additional peaks at 24.3°, 28.3°, 30.5°, 35.4°, and 39.3°. Actinolite, a calcium-magnesium amphibole, exhibits diagnostic peaks at 2θ = 10.8°, 20.8°, 21.95°, 22.8°, and 35.4°. Peak overlap is observed in the 20–40° 2θ range, particularly where quartz, albite, and biotite reflections coincide, which is typical for granitic rock assemblages. The sharp diffraction peaks indicate preserved crystallinity despite intensive milling, confirming particle size reduction occurred via fracture rather than amorphization which is essential for maintaining pozzolanic reactivity. The mineralogical findings are consistent with XRF analysis (Table 1), showing composition typical of granodiorite: 64.1% SiO2 and 16.32% Al2O3, with minor Fe2O3, CaO, and alkali oxides. It is important to highlight that the pozzolanic behavior and filler contribution of NG used in this study are supported by extensive prior research, where previous investigations have experimentally confirmed the significant pozzolanic activity of NG through strength development, microstructural refinement, and its reactive interaction with calcium hydroxide. These findings are documented in several peer-reviewed publications addressing both mechanical performance and radiation-shielding applications of NG-based concrete54,56,74.In addition, the chemical composition of the NG employed in the present study fully complies with the ASTM C618-22 requirements for pozzolanic materials, where the combined SiO2 + Al2O3 + Fe2O3 content must exceed 70%75. The NG used in the current study contains 85.57% of these oxides in its chemical composition as introduced in Table 1, confirming its suitability as a supplementary cementitious material. Based on this established evidence, the present study builds upon the previously validated mechanisms of NG without repeating microstructural analyses.

Mixes design

Table 2 presents the control mix proportions: 500 kg/m³ cement, 1130 kg/m3 coarse aggregate, 695 kg/m³ fine aggregate, 160 kg/m3 water, 9 kg/m3 superplasticizer, and 10 kg/m3 nano-silica. Fifteen modified mixtures were designed to evaluate three nanomaterials as partial cement replacements at 1–5% substitution levels by weight. The experimental matrix consisted of three series incorporating NL, NM, and NG, with five replacement percentages per nanomaterial type. The water-to-cementitious (w/c) ratio remained constant across all formulations. Superplasticizer content was maintained at 1.8% of cementitious material weight, the upper limit of manufacturer specifications, to maintain adequate workability despite the low water ratio and high nanomaterial surface area. It is worth noting that the most significant workability results were observed in earlier study for the investigated nanomaterials with the same mix designs54. The incorporation of NL particles led to an improvement in workability, with a 5% cement replacement yielding a 12.2% increase in slump compared to the control mix. Conversely, NM and NG particles caused slight reductions in slump at higher dosages, with 1% NM decreasing slump by 1.4%, and 2%, 3%, 4%, and 5% NM resulting in decreases of 4.1%, 6.8%, 10.8%, and 14.9%, respectively. NG particles showed a minor negative effect as well. Importantly, in all cases, whether the slump increased (NL) or decreased (NM&NG), the variations in workability remained within relatively moderate levels compared to the control mix, indicating that all mixes retained good fresh-state handling and castability. This demonstrates that the use of these nanomaterials, at the tested replacement levels, does not severely compromise workability, allowing for practical mixing and placement while still benefiting from the enhanced mechanical and radiation-shielding properties.

Specimens Preparation and curing conditions

In accordance with the mix design proportions presented in Table 2, concrete specimens were fabricated for mechanical testing. Cubic specimens (100 × 100 × 100 mm) and cylindrical specimens (100 mm diameter × 200 mm height) were cast in steel molds. A wet mixing methodology was employed to ensure uniform dispersion of nanoparticles and prevent agglomeration. The mixing procedure involved dry blending cement and aggregates for 3 min, followed by incorporation of the nanomaterial (NL, NM, or NG) pre-dispersed in 80% of the mixing water via magnetic stirring for 2 min. The remaining 20% of water, premixed with NS and superplasticizer, was then added under continued stirring. Total mixing time was approximately 3 min. Fresh concrete was cast into molds and mechanically vibrated to eliminate air voids and ensure proper compaction. Specimens were covered with polyethylene sheets and maintained at ambient laboratory conditions for 24 h before demolding. Subsequently, specimens were demolded and subjected to standard curing conditions. The specimens were cured by immersion in clean water at a controlled temperature of 23 ± 2 °C for 28 days until the age of testing, in accordance with ASTM C192/C192M specifications76. During the curing period, the relative humidity was maintained above 95% to ensure proper hydration and strength development under normal curing conditions. After completion of the curing period, the specimens were removed from the water, surface-dried, and prepared for subsequent mechanical and radiation shielding tests.

Experimental tests

Elevated temperature exposure

To evaluate the effect of elevated temperature exposure effect on gamma ray radiation shielding efficiency of the investigated nano concretes, the concrete specimens were subjected to controlled exposure at elevated temperatures of 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C for a fixed duration of two hours using an electrically controlled muffle furnace, as shown in Fig. 5a. The heating regime was carefully designed to simulate severe temperature exposure conditions while maintaining uniform temperature distribution throughout the specimens. The furnace, capable of reaching up to 1200 °C, was constructed with a thermally insulated steel chamber lined with high-temperature refractory materials to minimize heat losses and maintain temperature stability. It was equipped with multiple heating elements, strategically distributed to ensure homogenous thermal exposure, and an automatic temperature control system integrated with K-type thermocouples connected to a digital control unit. This system allowed continuous monitoring and precise adjustment of the temperature, ensuring that the internal environment followed the pre-programmed heating profile with minimal deviation.

As illustrated in Fig. 5b, the recorded temperature-time profiles during the heating process exhibited excellent agreement with the ISO 834 standard fire curve, which defines the temperature rise over time during a standard fire test. The actual heating rate in the experiments reached the target temperatures of 400, 600, and 800 °C within approximately 10–12 min. Radiation shielding assessment test

Experimental tests were conducted to evaluate the ability of concrete incorporating nano materials to attenuate gamma rays, expressed in terms of the linear attenuation coefficient (LAC). This was achieved using an experimental setup comprising three components: a gamma-ray source, the concrete barrier, and a radiation measurement device. Regarding the radiation source, as previously mentioned, two radioactive sources, uranium (U238) and cesium (Cs137), were used, as illustrated in Fig. 6a. These radioactive sources are prepared solely for research purposes and are stored securely in a vial wrapped with lead sheets to ensure safety when not in use. The Beer-Lambert equation (or Beer-Lambert law) is typically used to relate the attenuation of light or other radiation to the properties of the material through which it travels. When applied to radiation shielding, it describes the reduction in radiation intensity as it passes through an absorbing medium. For your concrete shield, which has a thickness of 10 cm, the equation is used to calculate the transmitted intensity (C) relative to the initial intensity (C0). The Beer-Lambert equation for radiation attenuation is shown in Eq. Each of these attenuation parameters offers a distinct perspective on how effectively concrete can weaken or absorb gamma-ray radiation. The mass attenuation coefficient (µ/ρ) expresses how strongly a material interacts with gamma rays relative to its mass. Physically, it reflects the probability that a gamma-ray photon will undergo absorption or scattering as it travels through a unit mass of the material. Because it separates the effect of density from the intrinsic interaction properties, it allows fair comparison between materials of different densities, making it a key parameter when selecting or engineering radiation-shielding materials. The HVT represents the material thickness needed to reduce the gamma-ray intensity to 50%. In physical terms, it is a measure of how quickly photons lose energy or are removed from the beam as they penetrate the material. A smaller HVT indicates a material in which interactions such as photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering, or pair production occur more frequently, meaning the photons are more effectively attenuated. Similarly, the TVT is the thickness required to reduce the photon intensity to just 10% of its original value. Physically, it represents a deeper level of shielding performance and reflects the material’s capacity to continue absorbing or scattering photons even after multiple interaction events. TVT is especially important when designing barriers for high-radiation environments, where substantial attenuation is necessary beyond the first few interaction layers.

The third component in the experimental setup is the device used to measure the intensity of gamma rays transmitted after passing through the concrete barrier. For this purpose, a Portable handheld gamma-ray spectrometer (RS230 BGO - SUPER-SPEC) was utilized. This sophisticated instrument, depicted in Fig. 6b, offers two distinct operational modes: assay mode and survey mode, each tailored to specific applications in gamma radiation detection and analysis. Assay mode is designed for in-depth analysis of materials, enabling the determination of potassium (K40), uranium (U238), and thorium (Th232) concentrations within the radiation source. This mode focuses on identifying characteristic energy peaks in the gamma-ray spectrum associated with these elements. It provides the concentrations of potassium as a percentage and those of uranium and thorium in parts per million (ppm). Furthermore, it measures the total radiation dose rate in grays per hour (Gy/h), quantifying the absorbed dose of ionizing radiation in nano or micro grays per hour. Conversely, survey mode measures the total gamma-ray count within a predefined energy range, reporting the count rate in counts per second (CPS). Compared to assay mode, which requires a minimum of 30 s for data acquisition, survey mode provides significantly faster results, typically within 1 to 20 s. This efficiency makes survey mode well-suited for rapid scanning of large areas to locate radioactive materials and map radiation levels, while assay mode is more suitable for in-depth analysis of specific samples. In the context of the experiments, readings from both modes were obtained for all concrete specimens, including the control mix and those incorporating nano materials. The radiation levels before and after the application of the concrete shield, expressed in Gy/h for assay mode and CPS for survey mode, were utilized to calculate key radiation shielding performance indicators The used experimental methodology in the current study has been utilized in previous studies to assess the radiation attenuation coefficient of concrete and has demonstrated high efficiency56,82. The concrete radiation shielding methodology implemented for a specific group of concrete samples is visually represented in Figs. 6c &d. The concrete specimen (10 cm thick) was placed directly on the RS230 BGO detector, and the gamma-ray source (either U-238 or Cs-137) was positioned on top of the sample. The detector was oriented directly beneath the sample, and the source was centered on the top surface to achieve a vertical transmission path through the specimen. Background radiation and (air-only) readings, with no sample present, were subtracted from all measurements to isolate the transmitted intensity. Although no long source–detector spacing was used, the setup effectively minimizes lateral scattering due to the direct vertical geometry and compact collimation provided by the vial and the detector housing. It is important to note that the experimental setup employed (as shown in Figs. 6c & d), using 10 cm cubic specimens and an uncollimated handheld source placed close to the samples, represents a practical or semi-broad-beam geometry rather than the ideal narrow-beam configuration. As a result, the values obtained using Eq. (2) inherently include the Build-up Factor, which accounts for secondary photons scattered within the specimen (mainly via Compton scattering) that still reach the detector. This effect does not compromise the validity of comparative assessments, as any systematic contribution from scattered photons would be approximately uniform across all tested specimens. Therefore, relative performance comparisons under these practical shielding conditions remain reliable and meaningful.

Results and discussions

Experimental LAC results at ambient temperatures

Tables 4 and 5 present the detailed assay and survey mode results for all concrete mixes at ambient temperatures. Two gamma-ray energies were investigated of uranium (U238) (~ 2.6 MeV) and cesium (Cs137) (0.662 MeV), and therefore two attenuation coefficients were reported: LAC₁ for U-238 and LAC₂ for Cs-137. As expected from the energy-dependent behavior of photon–matter interactions, LAC₂ values are consistently higher than LAC₁ for all mixes, reflecting the stronger attenuation of lower-energy Cs-137 photons. The results clearly show that incorporating nanomaterials substantially enhanced the attenuation capacity of concrete at both energies. This improvement is attributed to the nanoscale dimensions of the additives, which increase the probability of photon interactions due to their large specific surface area, improved packing efficiency, and enhanced filling of micro voids. The uniform dispersion of nanoparticles also leads to a denser and more homogeneous matrix, reducing potential pathways for gamma-ray transmission. Among all mixes, NL concretes exhibited the highest LAC values, demonstrating a strong positive correlation between NL dosage and gamma-ray attenuation. At a 5% replacement level, the average enhancement reached 39.67% (assay mode) and 36.47% (survey mode). This superior performance arises from the high atomic number of lead (Z = 82), which increases the likelihood of photoelectric absorption and Compton scattering, particularly at lower photon energies.

Similarly, concrete incorporating NM displayed a progressive increase in both LAC1 and LAC2 with increasing dosage. At the 5% level, improvements reached 23.97% and 23.70% under assay and survey modes, respectively. These gains reflect the beneficial influence of the high-density iron oxide composition on gamma-ray interaction probability. In contrast, NG concretes showed moderate yet consistent improvements, with the optimal performance observed at the 4% replacement level, where LAC increased by 14.88% and 18.91% for assay and survey modes, respectively. The enhancement in NG mixes is primarily linked to microstructural densification and porosity reduction rather than compositional effects, as granodiorite possesses a comparatively lower atomic number and density relative to lead- and iron-based nanoparticles. Table 6 summarizes the average LAC values and the corresponding improvement rates relative to the control mix. This table clearly illustrates the magnitude of enhancement achieved by each nanomaterial at different replacement levels and provides a concise comparison of the effectiveness of NL, NM, and NG in improving gamma-ray shielding. The data confirms that NL is the most effective additive, followed by NM, while NG contributes moderate improvements mainly through microstructural densification. The obtained results demonstrate that nanoparticle incorporation enhances gamma-ray attenuation across both investigated energy levels, with the magnitude of improvement governed by the atomic characteristics and microstructural influence of each nanomaterial.

It’s worth mentioning that the bulk density of all nano-modified concrete mixes, introduced in Table 2, exhibits notable consistency, ranging narrowly from 2.41 to 2.47 g/cm3, with the control mix at 2.42 g/cm3. This limited variation reflects the fact that the nanomaterials were introduced as a weight-based replacement for cement; therefore, their influence on the overall density is inherently marginal. Nevertheless, a slight increase in density is observed in most mixes, particularly at the 3% and 4% replacement levels, which can be attributed to the microscopic filler effect of the nanoparticles. Their ultrafine particle size enables them to occupy voids and nanopores within the cement matrix, improving packing efficiency and reducing internal porosity without significantly altering the total mass or volume of the composite. Given that the maximum increase in bulk density remains below 2%, the substantial enhancement observed in the effective linear attenuation coefficient, especially in the NL and NM mixes, cannot be explained by density changes alone. Instead, the improvement in gamma-ray shielding performance is predominantly driven by the chemical nature and high atomic number of the incorporated nanoparticles. Lead (in NL) and iron (in NM) introduce elements with higher photon interaction probabilities, thereby increasing the mass attenuation coefficient through enhanced photoelectric absorption. This confirms that the improved shielding efficiency results mainly from the elemental composition and refinement of the modified matrix, while the impact of density changes remains secondary and minimal.

Experimental LAC results after elevated temperatures exposure

Results of gamma ray LAC for all investigated nano concrete mixes at traditional and elevated temperatures are presented in Table 7. The practical values of LAC for the concrete samples were measured after exposure to increasing temperatures by assessing the amount of radiation from gamma-ray beams passing through the concrete shield. All tests were conducted using three main components: the radiation source, the concrete barrier, and the measuring device. Two radioactive sources were used: cesium (Cs137) and uranium(U238) with energies of 0.662 and 2.615 MeV, respectively. The concrete shield was represented by concrete cubes measuring 100 × 100 × 100 mm, which had been exposed to elevated temperatures conditions. The amount of transmitted radiation was measured using a portable handheld gamma-ray spectrometer (RS-230 BGO SUPER-SPEC), as previously described in the experimental work section. Figure 7 shows a set of the tested concrete samples to determine LAC values after exposure to various severe conditions, where the results are reported in Table 7, as previously mentioned.

The results showed a decrease in the control mix’s ability to attenuate gamma rays after exposure to high temperatures, where LAC value decreased by 24.17% from its initial value after being exposed to 800 °C for 120 min. This decline can be understood in light of the accompanying reduction in mechanical properties and the deterioration of the concrete’s microstructure when exposed to elevated temperatures. These changes lead to a decrease in density and an increase in voids and microcracks, which in turn allow radiation beams to penetrate through the concrete shield more easily. The incorporation of nanomaterials as partial cement replacements demonstrated a significant enhancement in concrete’s capacity to attenuate gamma radiation when subjected to extreme surrounding conditions. Figure 8 clearly illustrates the superior performance of NL in this regard. A 5% cement replacement ratio with NL optimized the concrete’s radiation shielding effectiveness, resulting in substantial increases in LAC values by 42.57% and 37.36% after exposure to temperatures of 600 °C and 800 °C, respectively. The remarkable enhancement in gamma-ray attenuation observed in concrete incorporating NL can be attributed to the material’s intrinsic properties. The high density and atomic number of lead are well-established factors contributing to efficient gamma ray scattering. Moreover, the nanoscale dimensions of NL enable optimal dispersion within the concrete matrix, increasing the probability of radiation interaction. Consequently, the observed proportional relationship between NL content and attenuation coefficient provides compelling evidence for the proposed mechanism.

Concrete mixes incorporating NM demonstrated the second-highest gamma ray attenuation performance among the tested concrete. Optimal attenuation was achieved at 5% cement replacement ratio with NM. At this ratio, the values of LAC improved by 26.73% and 24.18% when exposed to temperatures of 600 °C and 800 °C, respectively. While NM is composed of high-density metal oxide at the nanoscale of particles, exhibiting good capacity for gamma-ray attenuation, its performance was inferior to that of NL. The higher density and atomic number of NL confer superior gamma-ray shielding properties, resulting in enhanced concrete performance in this regard.

On the other side, NG exhibited the least influence on enhancing LAC values of the investigated concrete samples. Improvements in LAC values were modest, reaching 9.89% at 800 °C exposure. This improvement can be attributed to the enhancement in the microstructure of the concrete due to the use of NG. The HVL represents the thickness of the material required to reduce the intensity of incident gamma radiation to half its original value, and it is inversely proportional to the LAC83.The increase in HVL values with rising temperature can be attributed to the thermal degradation of the concrete microstructure, which leads to increased porosity and a consequent reduction in density and compactness of the matrix84. As shown, the HVL values generally increase with temperature, indicating a reduction in gamma-ray attenuation capability due to thermal deterioration of the concrete matrix. However, the magnitude of this increase varies among the mixes, reflecting the influence of the incorporated nano-additives and aggregate types on thermal stability and radiation shielding performance. For the NL mixes, the optimal mix containing 5% replacement exhibited a reduction in HVL of about 27.2% compared to the control mix after two hours of exposure to 800 °C. Similarly, the optimal NM mix at a 5% replacement rate achieved a 19.47% reduction under the same conditions. In contrast, the NG mixes were the least effective, showing only 11.65% reduction in HVL at 4% replacement rate after exposure to the extreme thermal condition of 800 °C.

Similar to the HVL behavior, the TVL values show consistent increasing trend with temperature, indicating a reduction in gamma-ray attenuation capability due to thermal deterioration of the concrete matrix. It was observed that the percentage increase in TVL values remains identical to that of the HVL values for all mixes. This consistency is expected, as both parameters are mathematically related to the LAC value according to Eqs. (4) and (5).

Recommendations, Limitations, and suggested future studies

The authors of the current study strongly recommend to exercise caution when handling lead oxides (NL) due to their inherent toxicity. Appropriate personal protective equipment, such as gloves, masks, and safety goggles, must be worn when incorporating NL into concrete mixtures. Direct contact with the environment should be strictly avoided. The use of lead-containing concrete is generally limited to specialized applications, such as radiation-shielding walls in medical imaging facilities, nuclear power plants, and research laboratories, where insulating layers are applied to prevent exposure86. For wider applications, concrete elements containing lead compounds should be coated with suitable insulating materials to prevent lead dispersion into the surrounding environment9.

The findings of the present study are limited by the specific experimental conditions under which the gamma radiation attenuation tests were conducted, including the concrete mix proportions, the type and dosage of nanomaterials incorporated, as well as the selected temperature levels and exposure durations. Therefore, the generalization of the present results should be approached with caution and supported by further investigations involving a wider range of parameters. This need is particularly significant considering the limited availability of experimental data on nanomaterials such as NG and NL, for which systematic research on their influence on radiation shielding under thermal exposure remains scarce. furthermore, it should be noted that the present investigation is limited to the specific photon energies associated with the used radiation source isotopes of uranium (U238) and cesium (Cs137). Since the interaction mechanisms of gamma rays with matter strongly depend on energy, the attenuation performance of concrete may vary at different gamma energies, particularly across the ranges where photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering, or pair production dominate. Therefore, extending future work to cover a broader energy spectrum would be essential to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of the shielding behavior of nanomaterial-modified concretes under diverse radiation environments. Additionally, conducting of SEM, XRD, MIP, or TGA analyses after high-temperature exposure can provide better validation of the thermal degradation and mitigation mechanisms of concrete exposed to elevated temperatures conditions.

Based on the outcomes of this study, several future research directions can be proposed. Similar experimental frameworks may be employed to assess the influence of various nanomaterials on the gamma-ray attenuation, neutron shielding, and microstructural integrity of different concrete types, including heavyweight, ultra-high-performance, and geopolymer concretes. Furthermore, the exploration of other nano-scale additives with high atomic numbers could provide valuable insights into enhancing the thermal stability and shielding efficiency of radiation-resistant concrete. Beyond thermal effects, future investigations could experimentally and numerically simulate the influence of harsh environmental and loading conditions, such as chemical attack, seawater exposure, and seismic loading, on the radiation attenuation performance of nanomaterial-incorporated concretes. Such studies would help elucidate the extent of degradation or loss in shielding capacity under these extreme scenarios, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the material’s long-term durability and safety in nuclear and coastal infrastructure applications. Moreover, the integration of machine learning (ML) tools offers a promising avenue for advancing predictive modelling of gamma radiation attenuation in thermally exposed concretes. Techniques such as artificial neural networks (ANNs), support vector machines (SVMs), and ensemble learning algorithms (e.g., Random Forest, Gradient Boosting) can be effectively applied to predict the linear attenuation coefficient (LAC) and optimize mixture design parameters with reduced experimental effort. In addition to these methods, metaheuristic optimization-based learning approaches, such as the Genetic Algorithm (GA) and the Water Cycle Algorithm (WCA), could be employed in future studies to enhance prediction accuracy and parameter optimization, offering robust hybrid frameworks that combine physical understanding with data-driven intelligence.

Conclusions

This study systematically examined the gamma-ray attenuation behaviour of concrete incorporating nano-lead oxide (NL), nano-magnetite (NM), and nano-granodiorite (NG) as partial cement replacements, with substitution levels ranging from 1% to 5% by cement weight. The linear attenuation coefficient (LAC) values were measured experimentally for all concrete mixes both at ambient temperature and after exposure to elevated temperatures of 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C, each maintained for two hours, to comprehensively evaluate their radiation shielding performance under thermal stress. Through the integration of experimental observations and statistical modelling, the study explored how nanomaterial type, dosage, and thermal exposure interact to affect the shielding efficiency and thermal stability of concrete. The application of RSM and ANOVA yielded a highly reliable predictive model, confirming the dominant influence of nanomaterial dosage and the adverse impact of temperature on LAC values. These results offer valuable insights and optimization guidelines for developing nano-engineered concrete with enhanced radiation attenuation capability and durability under high-temperature environments relevant to nuclear, medical, and industrial shielding applications.
