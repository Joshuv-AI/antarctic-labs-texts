Prognostic value of admission D-dimer in acute pancreatitis: association with disease severity and clinical outcomes in a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Elahe Nateghi Baygi, Niloofar Seighali, Mohammad-Sadra Gholami-Chahkand, Morteza Doustmohammadi, Fatemeh Jodeiri, Fahime Salehi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly risk stratification in acute pancreatitis (AP) is crucial for improving patient outcomes and guiding timely clinical management. D-dimer has emerged as a potential biomarker reflecting activation of coagulation and fibrinolysis and may assist in early risk stratification of patients with acute pancreatitis. However, its prognostic performance remains incompletely validated across different populations.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 200 adult patients diagnosed with acute pancreatitis between March 2023 and March 2024 at a tertiary care hospital. Disease severity was classified using the Revised Atlanta Classification. Admission serum D-dimer was measured within the first six hours after hospital presentation. Admission D-dimer levels were analyzed in relation to clinical severity, BISAP and Ranson scores, ICU admission, length of hospital stay, and in-hospital mortality. Statistical analyses included non-parametric tests, correlation analysis, logistic regression, and ROC curve analysis to evaluate predictive performance.ResultsTwo hundred consecutive patients, with a mean age of 58.1 ± 17.5 years and 54% male patients, were included in this study. Severity distribution showed 68.5% mild, 17% moderately severe, and 14.5% severe AP. Overall mortality was 7% (n = 14), and 56% (n = 112) required ICU admission. Admission D-dimer levels increased significantly with disease severity, with median values of 1534 ng/mL (mild), 1908.5 ng/mL (moderately severe), and 2960 ng/mL (severe). Non-survivors had markedly higher levels than survivors (3776.5 vs. 1663 ng/mL, p < 0.001). D-dimer showed significant positive correlations with BISAP (ρ = 0.6068), Ranson scores (ρ = 0.3425), and hospital length of stay.ROC analysis demonstrated good discriminatory ability for severe AP (AUC = 0.778; cutoff 2035 ng/mL) and good discriminatory performance for mortality prediction (AUC = 0.912; cutoff 2726 ng/mL, sensitivity 85.7%, specificity 80.1%). However, performance for predicting ICU admission was limited (AUC = 0.579). Multivariable analysis demonstrated that each 1-ng/mL increase in admission D-dimer level was independently associated with higher odds of severe AP (OR = 1.001, p < 0.001).ConclusionAdmission D-dimer is a simple and rapid biomarker associated with disease severity and mortality in acute pancreatitis. While it should not replace established scoring systems, it can serve as a valuable adjunct for early risk stratification, particularly for identifying high-risk patients. Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a common gastrointestinal disorder with an increasing global incidence and no approved targeted pharmacotherapy, so treatment remains largely supportive. Early identification of patients at risk of severe disease remains one of the most important clinical challenges because complications frequently develop during the first days after presentation [1–3]. The disease is most often caused by gallstones, alcohol use, and hypertriglyceridemia [2–4]. AP ranges from mild to moderately severe and severe disease, and worse outcomes are more likely in older patients and in those with comorbidities such as obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, renal dysfunction, and alcohol-related disease [2, 5].

Early recognition of severity is important because timely management can improve outcomes [6]. However, no single routine method is sufficiently accurate for early severity assessment in all patients [3, 7]. Established prognostic tools such as APACHE II, BISAP, and Ranson score are widely used to estimate disease severity. BISAP can be calculated within the first 24 h after admission, whereas complete Ranson scoring requires 48 h. Despite their usefulness, these systems require multiple clinical and laboratory variables and may be less practical in busy emergency settings [8–10]. In addition, computed tomography is not recommended solely for early severity prediction on admission [9]. Therefore, simple and accessible biomarkers are still needed.

One proposed mechanism in severe AP is inflammatory activation of coagulation with microvascular thrombosis and subsequent organ dysfunction [3, 11–13]. D-dimer, a fibrin degradation product, reflects activation of coagulation and fibrinolysis and may therefore help identify patients at higher risk of severe disease [11, 12, 14].

Several observational studies and recent systematic reviews have demonstrated an association between elevated D-dimer concentrations and severe acute pancreatitis [15–17]. Recent systematic and clinical studies suggest that D-dimer may be useful for early risk stratification in AP, particularly for severe disease and organ failure, although its specificity remains moderate [18, 19]. To date, there is limited data regarding the performance of D-dimer in Middle Eastern populations, including Iranian cohorts, where distinct dietary habits, metabolic profiles, and referral patterns may influence clinical outcomes. Therefore, this retrospective cohort study aimed to evaluate the association between admission serum D-dimer levels and disease severity, established prognostic scores, and important in-hospital clinical outcomes in patients with acute pancreatitis, and to assess its potential role as an adjunctive prognostic biomarker.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Imam Ali Hospital, Alborz University of Medical Sciences, Karaj, Iran. Medical records of patients admitted with a diagnosis of acute pancreatitis between 21 March 2023 and 21 March 2024 were reviewed. The study aimed to investigate the relationship between baseline admission D-dimer levels and disease severity and clinical outcome. All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Alborz University of Medical Sciences, Karaj, Iran (approval code: IR.ABZUMS.REC.1405.034). The study is reported in accordance with the STROBE Statement for observational cohort studies (Supplementary Material, Table S1).

Study population

A total of 200 consecutive adult patients with acute pancreatitis were included in the study. The diagnosis was established if at least two of the following three criteria were met: (1) characteristic acute upper abdominal pain (2), serum amylase or lipase levels at least three times the upper limit of normal, and (3) radiologic findings compatible with acute pancreatitis on abdominal imaging. Exclusion criteria were age under 18 years, chronic or recurrent pancreatitis, pregnancy, active malignancy, known thromboembolic disorders, chronic anticoagulant therapy (because these conditions may independently influence circulating D-dimer concentrations), and severe chronic liver disease (Child–Pugh class C when documented in the medical records). Patients with incomplete records preventing classification of disease severity, determination of study outcomes, or retrieval of admission D-dimer measurements were excluded. Analyses were therefore performed using a complete-case approach.

Clinical assessment

Disease severity was classified according to the 2012 Revised Atlanta Classification as mild, moderately severe, or severe acute pancreatitis. Mild acute pancreatitis was defined as the absence of organ failure and local or systemic complications. Moderately severe acute pancreatitis was defined by the presence of transient organ failure (lasting < 48 h), local complications, or systemic complications without persistent organ failure. Severe acute pancreatitis was defined by persistent organ failure lasting ≥ 48 h. Organ failure was determined according to the treating physicians’ documented clinical assessment based on the Revised Atlanta Classification as recorded in the medical records. Because of the retrospective design, detailed modified Marshall organ failure scores were not consistently available for all patients and therefore could not be analyzed separately [20]. Also, the Bedside Index for Severity in Acute Pancreatitis (BISAP) score and the Ranson score were calculated independently of the study hypothesis using routinely collected clinical and laboratory information for each patient during hospitalization. The BISAP score was calculated using the original five-variable scoring system (blood urea nitrogen > 25 mg/dL, impaired mental status, systemic inflammatory response syndrome, age > 60 years, and pleural effusion) [21]. The Ranson score was calculated according to the original published criteria using admission and 48-hour clinical and laboratory parameters [22]. The etiology of acute pancreatitis was classified according to the documented clinical diagnosis in the medical records. Biliary pancreatitis was diagnosed in the presence of gallstones or biliary sludge identified on imaging and/or evidence of biliary obstruction. Alcohol-induced pancreatitis was defined as pancreatitis occurring in patients with a documented history of significant alcohol consumption after exclusion of other likely causes. Hypertriglyceridemia-induced pancreatitis was diagnosed in patients with serum triglyceride levels ≥ 500 mg/dL in the absence of another more likely etiology. Post-operation (ERCP) pancreatitis was defined according to the documented occurrence of pancreatitis following endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography during the index admission. Drug-induced pancreatitis was assigned when the treating physicians identified a medication as the most likely cause after exclusion of alternative etiologies. Traumatic pancreatitis was diagnosed in patients with documented blunt or penetrating abdominal trauma. Cases were classified as idiopathic when no definite etiology could be identified after routine clinical evaluation.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical information was retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized data collection form. Variables recorded included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), etiology of pancreatitis, length of hospital stay, ICU admission, and all-cause in-hospital mortality. For etiology, patients’ drug history was assessed for drugs that may induce pancreatitis by the diagnosis of experts [23]. Admission laboratory findings obtained before initiation of treatment were also collected when available. The primary exposure variable was serum D-dimer (ng/mL) concentration measured during the initial evaluation within six hours after hospital admission. D-dimer was routinely measured in every patient with acute pancreatitis.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the association between admission D-dimer levels and the severity of acute pancreatitis, as defined by the Revised Atlanta Classification. Secondary outcomes included the relationship between D-dimer concentration and established clinical severity scores (BISAP and Ranson), as well as the duration of hospitalization, ICU admission, and in-hospital mortality. In addition, the diagnostic performance of D-dimer for identifying severe acute pancreatitis and predicting mortality was assessed and compared with established clinical scoring systems. Because detailed data regarding local pancreatic complications, organ-support therapies, infected necrosis, and serial laboratory measurements were not consistently available in this retrospective dataset, these outcomes could not be evaluated.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.12.3; Python Software Foundation) with the following libraries: pandas (v3.0.2) and NumPy (v2.4.4) for data management; SciPy (v1.17.1) for non-parametric hypothesis testing; statsmodels (v0.14.6) for logistic regression modelling; scikit-learn (v1.8.0) for receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis; and Matplotlib (v3.10.8) and Seaborn (v0.13.2) for data visualisation. The Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to assess the normality of continuous variables. Kruskal–Wallis test with pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests (with Bonferroni correction, adjusted α = 0.0167) were applied to the differences across the three severity groups (mild, moderately severe, and severe; per the revised Atlanta Classification) for D-dimer levels assessed at baseline or correlated with sample timing. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was calculated to assess the monotonic association between D-dimer and severity grade. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminatory ability of D-dimer for severe acute pancreatitis, ICU admission, and all-cause in-hospital mortality. Optimal cutoff values were determined using the Youden index because it identifies the threshold that maximizes the combined sensitivity and specificity and is widely used in exploratory biomarker studies. Because cutoff values were derived and evaluated within the same study cohort, they should be considered exploratory and require external validation before clinical implementation. The diagnostic performance of D-dimer was descriptively compared with BISAP and Ranson scores using ROC analysis. Spearman’s ρ was used to assess the correlations between D-dimer and hospitalisation duration, Ranson score, and BISAP score. The distribution of D-dimer was compared across aetiological subgroups with the Kruskal–Wallis test. Multivariate binary logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of severe pancreatitis, with ORs and 95% CIs reported. Variables with clinical relevance (age, gender, BMI and D-dimer) were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model using the enter method. Multivariable binary logistic regression was performed to evaluate the independent association between admission D-dimer concentration and severe acute pancreatitis. Age, sex, and body mass index were selected a priori because of their established clinical relevance and potential confounding effects. Additional variables, including inflammatory biomarkers, renal function indices, and comorbidities, were not entered into the model because these variables were either unavailable or incompletely recorded in the retrospective dataset. Residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be excluded because of the observational retrospective design. All statistical tests throughout required a p-value < 0.05 to declare significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 200 patients with acute pancreatitis were included in this study (Supplementary Figure S1). No eligible patient included in the final analytical cohort had missing values for the primary exposure or study outcomes because patients with incomplete records were excluded before analysis. The mean age of patients was 58.1 ± 17.5 years, and the mean BMI was 25.5 ± 3.5 kg/m². 108 patients were male (54%). In terms of disease severity, 68.5% (n = 137) had mild disease, 17.0% (n = 34) moderately severe disease, and 14.5% (n = 29) severe disease. Overall mortality was 7% (n = 14), while 56% (n = 112) required ICU admission (Table 1). The relatively high ICU admission rate reflects local institutional admission practices for close monitoring of patients with acute pancreatitis and should therefore not be interpreted as equivalent to persistent organ failure. D-dimer levels varied from 110 to 4500 ng/mL, with an overall median of 1756.5 ng/mL (IQR: 990–2716) and a mean of 1871.4 ± 1070.4 ng/mL for total patients (Table 2).

D-dimer levels and disease severity

An increase in D-dimer concentration was observed with increasing severity of acute pancreatitis. Patients with mild disease had a median D-dimer of 1534 ng/mL (IQR: 804–2291), whereas patients with moderately severe AP experienced a higher serum D-dimer level of 1908.5 ng/mL (IQR: 1283.2–2763). The highest levels were observed in severe AP, with a median of 2960 ng/mL (IQR: 2134–3687). This trend was supported by non-parametric testing, confirming a statistically significant difference in D-dimer levels across severity groups (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

Distribution of admission D-dimer levels according to acute pancreatitis severity. Overall differences among the three groups were statistically significant (P < 0.001). Post-hoc analysis showed significant differences between mild and severe pancreatitis (P < 0.001) and between moderately severe and severe pancreatitis (P = 0.0023), whereas the difference between mild and moderately severe disease was not statistically significant (P = 0.0542)

Clinical outcomes

D-dimer concentrations were markedly elevated in patients who did not survive compared with survivors. Median values were 3776.5 ng/mL (IQR: 2959–4083) in the non-survivor group versus 1663 ng/mL (IQR: 914.5–2515.8) among survivors, demonstrating a pronounced separation between outcome groups (p < 0.001). For mortality prediction, D-dimer showed strong diagnostic accuracy with an AUC of 0.9121. A threshold of 2726 ng/mL yielded a sensitivity of 0.857 and specificity of 0.801, indicating acceptable performance in identifying high-risk patients (Fig. 2).

Admission D-dimer levels in survivors and non-survivors

Similarly, patients requiring ICU admission exhibited higher D-dimer levels (median: 1973.5 ng/mL, IQR: 1023.2–2956.2) compared with non-ICU patients (median: 1579.5 ng/mL, IQR: 907.5–2186). However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.054), suggesting only a borderline association with intensive care requirement. Furthermore, D-dimer ability to predict ICU admission was limited (AUC = 0.5793), reflecting poor discrimination for this endpoint (Fig. 3).

Admission D-dimer levels according to intensive care unit (ICU) admission

Correlation with severity scores and clinical course

D-dimer levels demonstrated significant positive correlations with established clinical severity indices. The strongest association was observed with the BISAP score (ρ = 0.6068, p < 0.001), followed by the Ranson score (ρ = 0.3425, p < 0.001). A weaker but statistically significant correlation was also noted with duration of hospitalization (ρ = 0.1913, p = 0.0067). These findings indicate that higher D-dimer values are significantly associated with longer hospitalization stay (Fig. 4).

Correlation between admission D-dimer levels and clinical severity indices. Scatter plots demonstrate positive correlations between admission D-dimer levels and established markers of disease severity

D-dimer and etiology of pancreatitis

D-dimer levels varied across different etiological subgroups of pancreatitis, although the overall difference did not reach statistical significance (H = 11.8921, p = 0.0644). Post-ERCP and drug-induced pancreatitis tended to show higher median values compared with other causes, whereas traumatic and gallstone-related cases demonstrated relatively lower concentrations (Fig. 5).

Comparison of admission D-dimer levels among different etiological groups of acute pancreatitis. No statistically significant difference in D-dimer levels was observed between the etiological groups (Kruskal–Wallis H = 11.89, P = 0.0644). Data are presented as median (IQR)

Multivariable analysis

In logistic regression analysis, D-dimer emerged as an independent predictor of severe acute pancreatitis (OR = 1.001, 95% CI: 1.0006–1.0014, p < 0.001). Although the odds ratio per 1 ng/mL increase appears numerically small, D-dimer values varied over several thousand ng/mL in this cohort; therefore, clinically meaningful differences in D-dimer concentration correspond to substantially larger cumulative changes in estimated risk. In contrast, age, BMI, and gender did not show statistically significant associations (p > 0.05) (Fig. 6).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis for predictors of severe acute pancreatitis. Admission D-dimer was identified as an independent predictor of severe acute pancreatitis (OR = 1.001, 95% CI: 1.0006–1.0014, P < 0.001), whereas age (OR = 1.008, P = 0.528), sex (OR = 0.648, P = 0.334), and body mass index (OR = 0.961,P = 0.541) were not significantly associated with disease severity

Prognostic performance and predictive ability

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed that D-dimer had acceptable ability for severe acute pancreatitis, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.7778. A cut-off value of 2035 ng/mL provided a sensitivity of 0.828 and specificity of 0.678. When compared with other prognostic tools, BISAP demonstrated superior performance (AUC = 0.9076, sensitivity = 0.828, and specificity = 0.924), while Ranson score showed lower discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.6192, sensitivity = 0.69, and specificity = 0.52). These results place D-dimer as an intermediate-performing biomarker between established scoring systems (Fig. 7).

Comparison of the diagnostic performance of D-dimer, BISAP, and Ranson score for predicting severe acute pancreatitis

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 200 patients with acute pancreatitis, admission serum D-dimer concentrations were significantly associated with disease severity, established clinical severity scores, and all-cause in-hospital mortality. Higher admission D-dimer levels were observed in patients with severe acute pancreatitis and in non-survivors, supporting the biological relationship between activation of coagulation pathways and disease progression. Although D-dimer demonstrated moderate discrimination for severe acute pancreatitis and stronger discrimination for mortality within this cohort, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-supporting rather than definitive because they were derived from a single retrospective cohort without external validation.

The elevation of serum D-dimer in severe AP likely reflects the interaction between systemic inflammation, endothelial injury, and coagulation activation [24, 25]. In the early phase of AP, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) can induce a hypercoagulable state, with increased thrombin generation and fibrin deposition that further amplifies inflammation [26]. This thrombo-inflammatory process may promote microvascular thrombosis and capillary stasis, contributing to pancreatic ischemia and necrosis [24, 27–29]. Such vascular abnormalities are not limited to the pancreas; they may also extend to other organs, increasing vascular permeability and predisposing to multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) [27]. As a soluble degradation product of cross-linked fibrin, D-dimer reflects secondary activation of coagulation and fibrinolysis and therefore provides a biologically plausible marker of systemic vascular injury and disease progression. Nevertheless, D-dimer is a non-specific biomarker that may also be influenced by advanced age, systemic infection, recent surgery, trauma, malignancy, and other prothrombotic conditions. Therefore, interpretation should always be made within the overall clinical context [24, 27, 30].

In our cohort, admission D-dimer correlated significantly with both BISAP (r = 0.6068) and Ranson encouraging performance (r = 0.3425) scores. This is consistent with a prior study showing that D-dimer measured early after admission correlates with AP severity and with established scoring systems, including Ranson, BISAP, and APACHE II [30]. Zhang et al. similarly reported that D-dimer could distinguish mild AP from moderately severe and severe disease [26]. The stronger association with BISAP than with Ranson may reflect the fact that BISAP captures early indicators of systemic involvement and SIRS, whereas Ranson requires 48 h for full assessment and includes parameters that may be less closely aligned with immediate microcirculatory disturbance [30, 31]. Even so, D-dimer should be viewed as a complement to, rather than a substitute for, multidimensional scoring systems because it offers a focused measure of the coagulation-inflammation axis that adds to broader clinical assessment. These findings suggest that D-dimer reflects a specific component of the thrombo-inflammatory response in acute pancreatitis. Nevertheless, because BISAP demonstrated superior discrimination for severe disease in our cohort, D-dimer should be considered a complementary laboratory marker rather than a replacement for validated multidimensional clinical scoring systems [27, 30].

D-dimer also performed well for mortality prediction in this study, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.9121. Because only fourteen deaths occurred during the study period, estimates of mortality discrimination and the proposed threshold should be interpreted cautiously until validated in larger prospective cohorts. Non-survivors had substantially higher mean D-dimer levels than survivors (3506 ng/mL vs. 1748.38 ng/mL), supporting the view that pronounced elevation reflects severe systemic inflammation and extensive microvascular injury, both of which are linked to fatal outcomes [24, 27]. This finding is in line with Wan et al., who reported a markedly higher mortality rate among patients with D-dimer levels above 2.5 mg/L [24], and with He et al., who found that elevated D-dimer was associated with a more than tenfold increase in the risk of death [30]. Our proposed cutoff of > 2725 ng/mL demonstrated good discrimination within the study cohort; however, differences in assay methodology and reporting units across laboratories limit direct comparison of thresholds and underline the need for external validation before clinical adoption [24, 26].

By contrast, D-dimer showed only weak performance for predicting ICU admission in our study (AUC = 0.579). This likely reflects the fact that ICU transfer is influenced by factors that extend beyond biological severity, including physician judgment, bed availability, institutional policy, and the timing of referral relative to symptom onset [30–32]. Consequently, ICU admission should not be regarded as a surrogate for disease severity across different healthcare systems because admission thresholds vary substantially according to institutional resources and local clinical practice. Although some studies have reported significant associations between elevated D-dimer and ICU requirement, mortality remains a more objective endpoint than ICU admission, which is more vulnerable to variation in triage practice across settings [24, 31]. As a result, biomarkers may track disease biology more closely than resource-dependent clinical decisions.

These differences also help explain the variability across published studies. Our findings are broadly consistent with reports linking admission D-dimer to severe disease and mortality, yet the literature remains mixed with respect to ICU-related outcomes. Such discrepancies may arise from differences in sample size, timing of measurement, laboratory methods, unit standardization, and severity definitions [24, 26, 31]. Variations in classification systems, such as the 2012 Atlanta criteria versus older approaches, and in outcome definitions for organ failure further complicate cross-study comparisons [26]. In addition, variations in laboratory assay methodology, reporting units (fibrinogen-equivalent units versus D-dimer units), patient selection, timing of blood sampling, and differences in healthcare systems may further contribute to heterogeneity across published studies. These factors should be considered when comparing absolute cutoff values between cohorts. In this context, our data support D-dimer as a biologically meaningful marker of severity, while also highlighting the need for standardized protocols in future investigations.

From a practical perspective, D-dimer is widely available in many emergency departments and can be obtained early during the initial evaluation of patients with acute pancreatitis. Our findings suggest that it may provide additional biological information regarding activation of coagulation pathways. However, the present study was not designed to determine whether D-dimer improves prognostic performance beyond established clinical scores, and therefore its incremental clinical value remains to be established in future prospective studies [24, 26, 31]. In our study, early measurement appeared to improve risk stratification by capturing the underlying thrombo-inflammatory state more directly than some broader clinical variables. Because it correlated strongly with BISAP, D-dimer may be most useful as a biochemical adjunct to clinical scoring rather than as an independent stand-alone tool [24, 30, 31]. It should therefore complement comprehensive assessment rather than replace established multidimensional systems.

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other healthcare settings and patient populations. Second, although the overall cohort included 200 patients, the number of patients with severe acute pancreatitis and in-hospital deaths was relatively small, limiting the precision of subgroup analyses, multivariable estimates, and the proposed D-dimer cutoff values. Third, only a single admission D-dimer measurement was available; therefore, we were unable to evaluate whether serial measurements might better reflect disease progression or improve prognostic performance. Fourth, several clinically relevant variables, including the exact interval between symptom onset and blood sampling, inflammatory biomarkers such as C-reactive protein and procalcitonin, detailed modified Marshall organ failure scores, organ-support requirements, pancreatic necrosis, infected necrosis, peripancreatic fluid collections, and interventional procedures, were not consistently available in the retrospective database and therefore could not be analyzed. Fifth, although the regression model adjusted for major demographic variables, residual confounding from unmeasured clinical characteristics, comorbidities, renal function, and disease-specific factors cannot be excluded. Sixth, D-dimer cutoffs were derived and evaluated within the same study population using the Youden index; consequently, they should be considered exploratory and require independent external validation before clinical implementation. Also, it should be mentioned that because D-dimer testing was performed as part of routine clinical care, detailed assay characteristics (manufacturer, analytical platform, calibration method, and reporting units) were unavailable and could not be reported. We did not evaluate the incremental prognostic value of combining D-dimer with established clinical scores or other emerging biomarkers because these data were unavailable. In addition, potential confounding by age or comorbidities may not have been fully controlled. Finally, we did not compare D-dimer with other emerging biomarkers such as angiopoietin-2 or uPAR, which have shown promise in recent studies [27, 31].

Conclusion

In conclusion, admission serum D-dimer was associated with disease severity and all-cause in-hospital mortality in patients with acute pancreatitis. Within this retrospective cohort, D-dimer demonstrated potential as an adjunctive biomarker for early risk stratification when interpreted alongside established clinical scoring systems. However, these findings represent observational associations rather than proof of clinical utility. Larger prospective multicenter studies with standardized D-dimer assays, serial measurements, and external validation are required before specific cutoff values can be recommended for routine clinical practice.
