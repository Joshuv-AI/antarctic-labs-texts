Functional disability mediates the relationship between pain severity and quality of life in pregnant women with pregnancy-related low back pain: a moderated mediation analysis
Authors: Ya-Wen Chang, Fan-Hao Chou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnancy-related low back pain (PLBP) significantly affects functional ability and quality of life (QoL), yet the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This study examined the mediating role of functional disability between pain severity and QoL, as well as the moderating effects of gestational weeks and gestational body mass index (BMI).MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited 342 women with PLBP from medical centers and hospitals in southern Taiwan between February 2022 and January 2023. Pain severity was assessed using the Pain Visual Analog Scale (PVAS) and Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), functional disability using the Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ) and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and QoL using the World Health Organization Quality of Life Assessment-Brief (WHOQOL–BREF). Moderated mediation analyses were performed using the PROCESS macro (Models 4 and 21) with 5,000 bootstrapped samples.ResultsFunctional disability (measured by RMDQ or ODI) significantly mediated the association between pain severity (PVAS or BPI) and QoL across four regression pathways. PVAS showed full mediation, with significant indirect effects through RMDQ (B = -0.701, 95% CI [-1.098, -0.384]), accounting for 61.1% of the total effect, and through ODI (B = -0.750, 95% CI [-1.156, -0.387]), accounting for 65.3% of the total effect. BPI exhibited strong partial mediation, with significant indirect effects through RMDQ (B = -0.074, 95% CI [-0.130, -0.021]), accounting for 45.1% of the total effect, and through ODI (B = -0.082, 95% CI [-0.134, -0.033]), accounting for 50.0% of the total effect, alongside marginally significant direct effects (p = 0.044–0.051). Gestational weeks moderated the relationship between pain severity and functional disability (B = 0.003–0.066, p = 0.001–0.029), with impairment worsening as pregnancy progressed. Gestational BMI moderated the effect of functional disability on QoL (B = -0.041 to -0.032, p = 0.011–0.035), exerting a stronger moderating effect than gestational weeks. Their interaction effect was minimal.ConclusionFunctional disability is a key mechanism linking PLBP severity to reduced QoL. Gestational weeks and gestational BMI independently worsen these effects. Early functional assessment, functional rehabilitation, and weight management during pregnancy are essential strategies to minimize PLBP-related disability and promote maternal well-being.Keywords: Pregnancy-related low back pain, Functional disability, Quality of life, Gestational BMI, Gestational weeks, Moderated mediation

Background

During pregnancy, women experience numerous biochemical, physiological, and anatomical changes [1]. Of these, pregnancy-related low back pain (PLBP), is the most common [2]. Around 50–70% of pregnant women experience varying degrees of PLBP [2–4]. Although PLBP is generally temporary, if left unattended or untreated, the pain may extend into the postpartum period and progress to chronic low back pain, ultimately leading to long-term functional impairment and disability [5]. PLBP does not have a single cause. Instead, it is caused by various factors including hormonal changes, increased uterine weight, and poor posture, with pregnancy itself being a trigger [6–9].

Women with PLBP often experience limited mobility, difficulty performing daily tasks, and increased psychological distress due to pain-related disabilities, which can negatively affect their quality of life (QoL) [2, 10]. Furthermore, psychosocial factors, such as depression, anxiety, and perceived social support, may also influence the relationship between pain severity, functional disability, and QoL in pregnant women [2]. To date, most studies have focused on pain intensity. The pathways or mechanisms by which PLBP affects the QoL remain unclear [1]. Pain severity and functional disability (the degree to which pain interferes with daily activities) may be closely related to the QoL of patients with chronic LBP [11]. Moreover, health-related QoL is worse when pain severity and disability degree are higher [11]. Functional disability appears to mediate the relationship between pain severity and QoL, with greater physical limitations amplifying the negative effects of pain on overall well-being [4, 12, 13].

Furthermore, gestational stage is associated with various physiological adaptations that may exacerbate pain intensity and restricted activity as pregnancy progresses [2, 9]. Additionally, gestational body mass index (BMI) may alter weight distribution and musculoskeletal stress, thereby aggravating functional impairment [6, 8, 14]. In other words, gestational factors, such as gestational weeks and BMI, may further modulate these pathways, affecting the degree of pain-related disability, ultimately impacting QoL [3, 15, 16]. Despite these insights, few studies have systematically examined the combined effects of pain severity, functional disability, and gestational factors on maternal QoL.

Addressing the above effects is crucial for developing effective interventions to mitigate the adverse effects of PLBP and improve the QoL of women. The present study aimed to explore how functional disability and gestational factors influence the relationship between PLBP severity and QoL. Given that gestational weeks reflect cumulative physiological changes that increase biomechanical stress and pain susceptibility across trimesters [9], we conceptualized it as a moderator of the pathway from pain severity to functional disability. Likewise, we hypothesized that gestational BMI would moderate the pathway between functional disability and QoL, given its influence on mobility and increased musculoskeletal strain [16].

To ensure a comprehensive assessment, this study evaluated pain severity and functional disability using two measures for each. Employing multiple validated measures allows for a more nuanced understanding of the impact of PLBP on maternal QoL by capturing different aspects of pain intensity, pain interference, and physical limitations. Methods

Design and participants

This study was designed as a cross-sectional survey. The survey was distributed via medical centers and regional hospitals in southern Taiwan between February 2022 and January 2023. A convenience sampling method was used to collect data from pregnant women attending routine prenatal check-ups at obstetrics outpatient clinics, who experienced varying degrees of LBP. The inclusion criteria were (i) age 20 years and above in any trimester of pregnancy, (ii) self-reported chronic LBP either currently or in the past during pregnancy, (iii) capacity to complete questionnaires, (iv) willingness to provide informed consent, and (v) no diagnosed mental disorders. The exclusion criteria were (i) existing acute or chronic diseases, trauma, or previous surgeries involving the musculoskeletal or nervous system, (ii) any gynecological or urological conditions causing pain during pregnancy, such as pyelonephritis, (iii) high-risk pregnancies requiring bed rest, and (iv) communication difficulty or cognitive impairments. These exclusion criteria were implemented specifically to minimize the influence of non-pregnancy-related causes of LBP, ensuring that all included cases reflected PLBP.

Sample size

We estimated the required sample size using G*Power 3.1 to test a small effect size (f² = 0.02) in a multiple regression model with moderated mediation effects. With a significance level of α = 0.05, and a power of 1 - β = 0.80, approximately 311 participants were needed to detect significant R² changes in ten predictors, including independent variables, mediators, moderators, and covariates. Our study included 342 participants, which provided sufficient power to ensure robust and reliable results.

Measures and definitions

This study employed previously validated standardized questionnaires as assessment tools. To ensure their applicability and reliability in our context, five experts in public health, clinical obstetrics, and nursing care reviewed the questionnaire and concluded that our content validity index (CVI) was greater than 0.78 [17]. A pilot test with 30 pregnant women meeting the same inclusion and exclusion criteria confirmed acceptable internal consistency reliability, with Cronbach’s α values all above 0.7, indicating acceptable reliability [18]. Face validity was confirmed by analyzing respondent feedback regarding the clarity and comprehensibility of the questionnaire items. The following demographic variables and standardized questionnaires were employed in this study:

Demographics

A structured interview using both categorical and continuous variables was conducted to collect demographic data from the participants, including personal information, maternal status, and activity patterns.

Body mass index (BMI)

The BMI was calculated based on participants’ self-reported height and weight using the following formula: weight (kg)/[height (m)]². It was categorized as pre-gestational BMI (before pregnancy) and gestational BMI (during the survey).

Pain visual analog scale (PVAS)

The PVAS was used to measure subjective LBP severity in pregnant women based on a 10-cm scale, with higher scores indicating greater pain severity.

Brief pain inventory (BPI)

The BPI scale was used to measure pain intensity and pain interference on a scale of 0–10, with higher scores indicating greater pain severity and life interference [19]. Specifically, 4 items of the BPI scale were used to assess pain severity over the past week, while pain interference was determined using 7 items of the BPI scale to evaluate its impact on daily life during the past week. Factor analysis demonstrated good construct validity for the BPI, with Cronbach’s α values of 0.91 for pain intensity and 0.89 for pain interference [20]. Importantly, the BPI has been used to evaluate back pain during pregnancy [5]. The present study revealed a Cronbach’s α of 0.89 for pain intensity and 0.94 for pain interference, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Roland-Morris disability questionnaire (RMDQ)

The RMDQ scale was translated and adapted into Chinese by Chen et al. [21] based on the original English version [22], which was designed to assess functional impairment in individuals with LBP. The RMDQ consists of 24 items, each scored as “Yes” (1 point) or “No” (0 point), with total scores ranging from 0 (no disability) to 24 (severe disability). The RMDQ has demonstrated good psychometric properties, with good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.85) and good test-retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.81) [21]. The present study assessed LBP-related functional disability during pregnancy using the RMDQ (Cronbach’s α = 0.87, indicative of high reliability).

Oswestry disability index (ODI)

The ODI scale assesses LBP patients’ functional status with ten items rated from 0 (no impairment) to 5 (maximum impairment), with higher scores indicating more severe pain and disability [23, 24]. The Chinese version demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α = 0.90 in a previous study [24] and 0.72 in this study.

World health organization quality of life assessment-brief version (WHOQOL–BREF)

The Taiwan version of this 28-item scale assesses health-related QoL on a 5-point Likert scale [25]. It includes four domains for physical health, psychology, social relationships, and environment, as well as two local facets (respect/acceptance and eating/food), with higher scores indicating a better QoL. The WHOQOL–BREF demonstrates good expert construct validity and reliability, as evidenced by internal consistency coefficients for the four domains, ranging from 0.70 to 0.77 [25], and 0.75 to 0.82 in the present study.

Procedure

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kaohsiung Medical University (IRB NO: KMUHIRB–SV(I)–20190076). Pregnant women awaiting prenatal check-ups at the hospital were informed of this study and invited to participate if they met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Detailed information regarding research objectives, procedures, risks, and benefits was provided verbally and in writing, and their privacy was respected. Participants had sufficient time to read the consent form, ask questions, and fully understand the research before signing. The voluntary nature of participation was emphasized to ensure that participants understand that they can withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their medical care. After informed consent was obtained, participants completed the questionnaire with the researcher through a face-to-face interview. All procedures were performed as per relevant guidelines and regulations.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted using SPSS version 24.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical and continuous variables were expressed using numbers (proportions), means, and standard deviations (SDs), respectively. Prior to the main analyses, common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single factor test [26]. Regression assumptions were evaluated using diagnostic plots and statistical tests. The moderated mediation model of interest was analyzed using single mediation (Model 4) and moderated mediation (Model 21) frameworks from the PROCESS macro version 4.2. Bootstrap inference with 5000 samples was used to compute confidence intervals (CIs) and determine the significance of model coefficients, ensuring robustness without relying on normality assumptions [27]. Coefficients and indirect effects were deemed significant if the 95% CIs excluded zero. The HC3 heteroscedasticity-consistent standard error estimator was employed to address potential heteroscedasticity, further enhancing the robustness of the results [28].

To test Hypothesis 1, the PROCESS Model 4 test of mediation was used to examine the mediation effect of functional disability on the association between pain severity and QoL as shown in Fig. 2(a) in its conceptual form [27]. This analysis utilized pain severity (measured by PVAS or BPI) as the independent variable to assess the subjective perception of pain. Additionally, functional disability (assessed by RMDQ or ODI) was used as the mediation variable to measure limitations in daily activities caused by PLBP. Moreover, QoL (measured by WHOQOL–BREF) was coded as the dependent variable. As shown in Fig. 2(b) in the statistical form, the observed effect of pain severity on QoL is called the total effect (path c). The total effects comprised a direct effect pathway (path c’) of pain severity on QoL and a total indirect pathway (mediated: path a × b) of pain severity on QOL through functional disability.

The regression pathways (panels a-d) illustrating the mediating effect of functional disability on the relationship between pain severity and quality of life (QoL), based on PROCESS Model 4 (N = 342). (a) PVAS-RMDQ-QoL regression pathway showing the mediating role of functional disability (RMDQ) between pain severity (PVAS) and QoL. (b) PVAS-ODI-QoL regression pathway showing the mediating role of functional disability (ODI) between pain severity (PVAS) and QoL. (c) BPI-RMDQ-QoL regression pathway showing the mediating role of functional disability (RMDQ) between pain severity (BPI) and QoL. (d) BPI-ODI-QoL regression pathway showing the mediating role of functional disability (ODI) between pain severity (BPI) and QoL

To test Hypotheses 2, 3, and 4, we conducted the PROCESS Model 21 test of moderated mediation to examine the interactions between gestational weeks and pain severity, and those between BMI and functional disability, as well as their impacts in the mediation effect identified in Hypothesis 1 [27]. To build the Model 4 framework, gestational weeks and gestational BMI were included as moderator variables. The essence of the moderating effect was uncovered by simple slope analyses at specific regions of each interaction based on standard deviation levels (i.e., 1 SD below mean, mean, and 1 SD above mean) [27]. A statistically significant difference in the path coefficient across different levels of the moderator was considered evidence of moderation. To reduce multicollinearity of interaction models and facilitate interpretation, all continuous study variables were mean-centered in Model 4 and Model 21 prior to data analyses.

Results

Demographic characteristics

This study analyzed the demographic characteristics of 342 pregnant women and their association with QoL. As shown in Table 1, the mean ± SD for maternal age was 33.16 ± 3.97 years, pre-gestational BMI was 23.26 ± 4.70, gestational BMI was 25.63 ± 4.87, gestation was 25.34 ± 8.74 weeks, and sleep duration was 7.37 ± 1.22 h per night. Participants were distributed across all three trimesters: 16.1% were in the first trimester (0–13 weeks), 36.5% in the second trimester (14–27 weeks), and 47.4% in the third trimester (28–40 weeks). This distribution reflects a broad range of pregnancy stages, with nearly half of the participants in the third trimester, when PLBP typically peaks.

Regarding personal information, most participants were married (97.1%), had a university education or higher (82.7%), and reported a moderate income (78.4%). Additionally, 57.6% of participants had a religious belief. Notably, none of these variables were significantly associated with QoL.

Regarding maternal status, 42.1% were pregnant for the first time, 69.6% had no history of miscarriage, and 73.1% reported no history of chronic diseases. In addition, 218 participants (63.7%) had a history of LBP, which was significantly associated with lower QoL (p = 0.001).

Regarding activity patterns, most participants (73.4%) exercised regularly one year before pregnancy. About half continued exercising during pregnancy, while the remaining maintained sustained postures or engaged in repetitive movements at work. Only 8.5% of the participants engaged in weight-bearing activities, which was associated with a significantly lower QoL scores (p = 0.013).

Preliminary analysis

Harman’s single factor test showed that the first component accounted for 26.99% of the variance, well below the 40% threshold [26], suggesting no significant common method bias in the self-reported questionnaires. Regression assumptions, including the absence of outliers, normality, linearity, independence of residuals, multicollinearity, and homoscedasticity, were confirmed using diagnostic plots and statistical checks.

Table 2 summarizes the descriptive statistics and correlation analysis of the study variables. Self-perceived PLBP severity was assessed using the PVAS and BPI and showed mean scores of 3.13 ± 1.9 and 22.96 ± 18.94, respectively, with the latter comprising pain intensity (9.17 ± 6.62) and pain interference (13.80 ± 13.45) over the past week. Functional disability was evaluated using the RMDQ and ODI and the mean scores were 6.97 ± 5.32 and 9.87 ± 7.82, respectively. Additionally, QoL as assessed by the WHOQOL–BREF displayed a mean total score of 102.07 ± 11.88. The analysis revealed an absolute skewness of less than 2 and an absolute kurtosis of less than 7. These were appropriate for sample sizes greater than 300, indicating no significant deviations from normality [29].

Pain severity (assessed using PVAS and BPI) showed a significant positive correlation with functional disability, measured by RMDQ (PVAS: r = 0.448, p < 0.001; BPI: r = 0.594, p < 0.001), or ODI (PVAS: r = 0.436, p < 0.001; BPI: r = 0.572, p < 0.01). Our findings indicated that for women with PLBP, higher pain severity was associated with greater limitations in daily activities. The very strong correlation between PVAS and BPI (r = 0.744, p < 0.001) suggests the consistency between the two subjective pain measures. Likewise, the strong correlation between RMDQ and ODI (r = 0.811, p < 0.001) demonstrates good alignment between the two measures of functional disability.

The WHOQOL–BREF revealed a significant negative correlation between QoL and pain severity (PVAS: r = −0.203, p < 0.001; BPI: r = −0.277, p < 0.001), and functional disability (RMDQ: r = −0.282, p < 0.001; ODI: r = −0.298, p < 0.001). In other words, higher degrees of pain severity and functional disability were associated with poorer QoL.

As a moderator variable, gestational weeks was positively correlated with pain severity (PVAS: r = 0.149, p < 0.01; BPI: r = 0.267, p < 0.001) and functional disability (RMDQ: r = 0.280, p < 0.001; ODI: r = 0.271, p < 0.001). Similarly, gestational BMI, another moderator variable, showed a significant positive correlation with pain severity (PVAS: r = 0.202, p < 0.001; BPI: r = 0.273, p < 0.001) and functional disability (RMDQ: r = 0.167, p < 0.01; ODI: r = 0.116, p < 0.05). In contrast, neither gestational weeks nor gestational BMI demonstrated a significant association with QoL.

Table 3 presents the multivariable regression analysis results on the relationships between pain severity, functional disability, gestational weeks, gestational BMI, and QoL. To provide a comprehensive evaluation of relationships between the study variables and QoL, we analyzed four regression pathways, including PVAS–RMDQ–QoL, PVAS–ODI–QoL, BPI–RMDQ–QoL, and BPI–ODI–QoL. These assessed the robustness of the findings across different combinations of pain severity measures (PVAS and BPI) and functional disability measures (RMDQ and ODI). Notably, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values (range: 1.101 to 1.635) across all four regression pathways remained well below the stricter threshold of 3, indicating minimal multicollinearity among the predictors [30]. Similarly, the Durbin-Watson statistics (range: 1.814 to 1.833) were within the acceptable range of 1.5 to 2.5, thereby satisfying the assumption of residual independence [31].

Mediation effect analysis

Following Hypothesis 1, we analyzed the mediation models to examine the direct and indirect effects of PLBP severity on QoL through functional disability (Fig. 2). Path coefficients were derived from a series of multiple regression analyses using PROCESS (Model 4) and adjusted for covariates compromising LBP history and workload type. The results showed that all of the simple path coefficients (path a, b, and c’) were statistically significant, with p < 0.05. Of note, covariates, particularly demographic characteristics, should be theoretically justified to avoid obscuring meaningful relationships [32]. The selected covariates were chosen based on their potential influence on QoL (Table 1).

As shown in Fig. 2(a) and (b), functional disability (RMDQ or ODI) had a significant mediating effect on the relationship between pain severity (measured by PVAS) and QoL. When RMDQ was the mediator, the indirect effect was significant (B = −0.701, 95% CI [−1.098, −0.384]), whereas the direct effect was not significant (B = −0.447, 95% CI [−1.186, 0.292], p = 0.235) (Fig. 2(a)). Similarly, when ODI was the mediator, a significant indirect effect (B = −0.750, 95% CI [−1.156, −0.387]) and a non-significant direct effect (B = −0.398, 95% CI [−1.116, 0.319], p = 0.275) were identified (Fig. 2(b)). Overall, the total effect of pain severity as measured by PVAS on QoL was significant (B = −1.148, 95% CI [−1.772, −0.525], p < 0.001), demonstrating that functional disability, regardless of whether it is assessed by RMDQ or ODI, fully mediated the relationship between pain severity (PVAS) and QoL. The proportion of the total effect explained by the mediation pathway was 61.1% for PVAS-RMDQ-QoL regression pathway and 65.3% for PVAS-ODI-QoL regression pathway.

Likewise, as presented in Fig. 2(c) and (d), functional disability (RMDQ or ODI) demonstrated a significant mediating effect on the relationship between pain severity (measured by BPI) and QoL. When RMDQ was the mediator, the indirect effect was significant (B = −0.074, 95% CI [−0.130, −0.021]), while the direct effect was marginally significant (B = −0.090, 95% CI [−0.177, −0.003], p = 0.044), suggesting a weak direct effect (Fig. 2(c)). Similarly, when ODI was the mediator, the indirect effect was significant (B = −0.082, 95% CI [−0.134, −0.033]), and the direct effect was borderline significant (B = −0.082, 95% CI [−0.164, 0.001], p = 0.051), indicating a potentially weak direct effect (Fig. 2(d)). Overall, the total effect remained significant (B = −0.164, 95% CI [−0.229, −0.098], p < 0.001), demonstrating that functional disability, regardless of whether it is measured by RMDQ or ODI, served as a weak partial mediator between pain severity (BPI) and QoL. The proportion of the total effect explained by the mediation pathway was 45.1% for BPI-RMDQ-QoL regression pathway and 50.0% for BPI-ODI-QoL regression pathway.

In summary, this study shows that functional disability, whether assessed by RMDQ or ODI, plays a critical mediating role in the relationship between pain severity (measured by PVAS or BPI) and QoL.

Moderated mediation effect analysis

Given that the direct effects of pain severity on QoL were either non-significant or marginal, moderation was applied only to the indirect pathways. After adjusting for LBP history and workload type, we performed a moderated mediation analysis using PROCESS (Model 21). Based on Hypothesis 2, the moderating effect of gestational weeks on the relationship between pain severity and functional disability was examined. Likewise, the moderating effect of gestational BMI on the relationship between functional disability and QoL was assessed based on Hypothesis 3. To test Hypothesis 4, we further investigated the double moderated mediation effect to determine whether both gestational weeks and gestational BMI jointly moderated the mediated relationship between pain severity, functional disability, and QoL. Figure 1 shows the conceptual (panel a) and statistical (panel b) forms of the moderated mediation model, while Table 4, corresponding to Fig. 1(b), presents the regression analysis results for each pathway. The results showed that the moderating effect of gestational weeks (path a1) and its interaction with pain severity (path a2) on functional disability were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Similarly, gestational BMI (path b1) and its interaction with functional disability (path b2) on QoL were considered in the model, with path b2 reaching statistical significance (p < 0.05). PVAS–ODI–QoL regression pathway

As presented in Table 4(b), pain severity (PVAS) significantly predicted functional disability (ODI) (B = 1.667, t = 9.147, p < 0.001). Gestation weeks was positively associated with ODI (B = 0.193, t = 4.475, p < 0.001), and a significant interaction effect (B = 0.066, t = 3.499, p = 0.001) suggested a moderating role. ODI displayed a significant negative effect on QoL (B = −0.423, t = −4.428, p < 0.001), moderated by gestational BMI (B = −0.033, t = −2.227, p = 0.027). In contrast, PVAS showed no direct effect on QoL (B = −0.292, t = −0.800, p = 0.424). The model accounted for 15.1% of the variance in QoL (F(6, 335) = 10.242, p < 0.001) and 26.2% of the variance in ODI (F(5, 336) = 29.741, p < 0.001).

The simple slope test revealed that the association between PVAS and ODI strengthened with advancing gestation (early: B = 1.092, 95% CI [0.604, 1.579], p < 0.001; late: B = 1.532, 95% CI [1.764, 2.719], p < 0.001) (Fig. 3(b)). Moreover, the negative association between ODI and QoL was stronger in women with higher gestational BMI (low BMI: B = −0.262, 95% CI [−0.489, −0.035], p = 0.024; high BMI: B = −0.585, 95% CI [−0.830, −0.340], p < 0.001) (Fig. 4(b)).

As shown in Table 5(b), PVAS had a significant negative impact on QoL through ODI in early gestational weeks and low gestational BMI (B = −0.286, 95% CI [−0.589, −0.022]). This negative effect became most pronounced with increasing gestational weeks and gestational BMI (B = −1.311, 95% CI [−1.943, −0.714]), particularly for gestational BMI. Although gestational weeks and gestational BMI each moderated the mediating effect, their interaction failed to reach statistical significance (Index = −0.002, 95% CI [−0.005, 0.000]), suggesting minimal double moderated mediation.

BPI–RMDQ–QoL regression pathway

As presented in Table 4(c), pain severity (BPI) significantly predicted functional disability (RMDQ) (B = 0.152, t = 10.305, p < 0.001). Gestational weeks positively influenced RMDQ (B = 0.084, t = 2.951, p = 0.003), with a significant moderating effect (B = 0.003, t = 2.190, p = 0.029). RMDQ negatively affected QoL (B = −0.415, t = −2.572, p = 0.011), moderated by gestational BMI (B = −0.040, t = −2.558, p = 0.011). The direct effect of BPI on QoL was marginally significant (B = −0.088, t = −1.978, p = 0.048). The model accounted for 14.8% of the variance in QoL (F(6, 335) = 10.778, p < 0.001) and 37.9% of the variance in RMDQ (F(5, 336) = 34.557, p < 0.001).

The simple slope test showed that BPI was significantly and positively associated with RMDQ at later gestational weeks (early: B = 0.123, 95% CI [0.080, 0.166], p < 0.001; late: B = 0.181, 95% CI [0.147, 0.215], p < 0.001) (Fig. 3(c)). Moreover, the negative association between RMDQ and QoL became statistically significant in women with higher gestational BMI (low BMI: B = −0.219, 95% CI [−0.581, 0.143], p = 0.234; high BMI: B = −0.612, 95% CI [−0.953, −0.270], p < 0.001) (Fig. 4(c)).

As shown in Table 5(c), with a low gestational BMI, the negative effect of BPI on QoL increased from early (B = −0.027, 95% CI [−0.075, 0.021]) to later stages of pregnancy (B = −0.040, 95% CI [−0.106, 0.030]), though not statistically significant. Notably, the negative effect became significant and stronger at medium or high gestational BMI, with the greatest impact observed when both gestational weeks and gestational BMI were high (B = −0.111, 95% CI [−0.182, −0.041]). Although gestational weeks and gestational BMI each moderated the mediating effect, their interaction was extremely weak and had no significant impact on the indirect effect (Index = 0.000, 95% CI [0.000, 0.000]).

Moreover, the negative association between ODI and QoL was not statistically significant in women with higher gestational BMI (low BMI: B = −0.206, 95% CI [−0.444, 0.033], p = 0.090; high BMI: B = −0.513, 95% CI [−0.776, −0.250], p < 0.001) (Fig. 4(d)).

As shown in Table 5(d), with a low gestational BMI, the negative effect of BPI on QoL increased from early (B = −0.036, 95% CI [−0.080, 0.007]) to later stages of pregnancy (B = −0.052, 95% CI [−0.115, 0.010]), though not statistically significant. Notably, the negative effect became significant and stronger at medium or high gestational BMI, with the greatest impact observed when both gestational weeks and gestational BMI were high (B = −0.130, 95% CI [−0.204, −0.061]). Discussion

This study elucidated the mediating role of functional disability and the moderating effects of gestational weeks and gestational BMI, promoting the understanding of the relationship between pain severity, functional disability, and QoL in pregnant women with PLBP. The analysis included four regression pathways, evaluating different combinations of pain severity (PVAS or BPI) and functional disability measures (RMDQ or ODI). The findings demonstrated that functional disability significantly mediated the relationship between pain severity and QoL. Furthermore, gestational weeks moderated the relationship between pain severity and functional disability, while gestational BMI moderated the link between functional disability and QoL. Additionally, gestational BMI had a stronger moderating effect on disability pathways compared to gestational weeks, and their interaction had minimal influence on the mediation effect.

There was no association between demographic variables (e.g. maternal age, BMI, gestational weeks, and sleep duration) and QoL among our participants. On the contrary, a history of LBP and workload type significantly influenced QoL. Participants with prior LBP reported lower QoL, providing evidence that chronic pain history strongly predicts long-term adverse effects on QoL, particularly in pregnant women [4, 10, 33, 34]. Similarly, weight-bearing workload was found to be associated with lower QoL, highlighting its impact on pregnancy-related musculoskeletal discomfort. Physically demanding work, such as heavy lifting and prolonged standing, has been linked to adverse pregnancy outcomes, which can negatively impact QoL [35, 36]. To maintain model robustness, we adjusted for LBP history and workload type to ensure that the mediation and moderated mediation effects were not confounded.

Mediating effect of functional disability (Hypothesis 1)

In accordance with Hypothesis 1, functional disability significantly mediated the relationship between PLBP severity and QoL. Specifically, higher pain severity was associated with greater functional disability, leading to poorer QoL. Previous studies have shown that functional impairment plays a crucial role in QoL among pregnant women with musculoskeletal pain [12, 13, 37]. Pain severity assessment using PVAS revealed that the direct effect of PVAS on QoL became non-significant after functional disability was included as a mediator, indicating full mediation. This finding is consistent with disability models in chronic pain populations, in which functional impairment serves as the key mechanism linking pain perception and broader well-being outcomes [11, 38]. This suggests that subjective pain intensity alone does not directly impair QoL. Instead, the impact of pain on physical function significantly contributes to poorer QoL. Consequently addressing activity limitations, rather than solely focusing on pain intensity, is essential for effective PLBP management [38–40]. Similarly, Nordeman et al. emphasized the importance of improving physical capacity to prevent further disability and enhance QoL in women with chronic LBP [41]. In contrast, pain severity assessment using BPI showed that functional disability partially mediated the relationship with QoL. Specifically, the direct effect of BPI-assessed pain severity on QoL remained marginally significant in the RMDQ model (p = 0.044) and borderline significant in the ODI model (p = 0.051). This pattern is expected, as the BPI assesses both pain intensity and pain interference, capturing not only the severity but also the impact of pain on various aspects of daily life, such as standing, lifting, and sleeping [11, 19]. Therefore, while functional disability explains a significant portion of the relationship, pain interference may still directly impact QoL beyond its effect on functional limitations, consistent with validation findings showing that BPI interference items moderately correlate with functional disability measures in low back pain populations [42].

Importantly, we observed two distinct mediation patterns depending on the pain severity measure used. When pain was assessed using PVAS, the effect on QoL was fully mediated by functional disability. In contrast, the broader construct of BPI, which includes both intensity and interference, showed a partial mediation effect, indicating direct impact on QoL beyond functional limitations. These findings underscore the importance of prioritizing interventions targeting functional disability in PLBP management. Instead of solely addressing pain intensity, treatment approaches should focus on improving physical function and mitigating activity limitations.

From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the need to prioritize managing functional disability in PLBP management, rather than focusing solely on pain intensity [40]. Strategies that improve mobility and daily functioning, such as physical therapy, posture training, and ergonomic support, may be particularly effective in alleviating the impact of pain on QoL [39, 43, 44]. Karayannis et al. emphasized that pain interference does not always align with functional disability over time, reinforcing the importance of addressing these two aspects in treatment [45]. Importantly, treatment strategies should be tailored to pain severity profiles. For pregnant women with high pain intensity but low interference, functional rehabilitation may be appropriate. In contrast, for those experiencing high pain interference, a combination of physical therapy, assistive device use, and lifestyle modifications may be necessary to fully address the impact of PLBP on QoL [46].

Moderating effect of gestational weeks (Hypothesis 2)

In accordance with Hypothesis 2, gestational weeks significantly moderated the relationship between pain severity and functional disability. The positive association between pain severity and functional disability became stronger with increasing gestational weeks, suggesting that the physical burden of PLBP intensifies over time. These findings align with previous studies that indicated a progressive increase in mechanical strain on the lumbar spine and sacroiliac joints as pregnancy advances. The expansion of the uterus shifts the body’s center of gravity forwards, promoting pelvic tilt and lumbar hyperlordosis, which heightens stress on the lower back muscles and ligaments, thereby exacerbating functional disability [2, 3, 9]. Additionally, fetal weight gain in later pregnancy imposes an increased mechanical load on the spine, potentially leading to postural instability and sacroiliac joint dysfunction [4, 6]. This instability may be further aggravated by increased ligament laxity, predisposing pregnant women to greater mobility restrictions and discomfort as gestation advances [7].

Since gestation stage significantly moderates the impact of pain severity on functional disability, early intervention is essential. Healthcare providers should implement preventative strategies in early pregnancy, rather than waiting until pain becomes debilitating in later stages. Given that the risk of functional disability increases with pregnancy progression, routine PLBP screening should be integrated into prenatal care, especially for women in their second and third trimesters.

Moderating effect of gestational BMI (Hypothesis 3)

Similarly, in accordance with Hypothesis 3, gestational BMI significantly moderated the relationship between functional disability and QoL, with a stronger negative association observed in women with a higher gestational BMI. Notably, the negative impact of functional disability on QoL became weaker in pregnant women with a lower gestational BMI, with most regression pathways showing non-significant effects. In contrast, the detrimental effect of functional disability on QoL became more pronounced as the BMI increased to medium or high levels. Our findings suggest that excessive weight gain during pregnancy exacerbates the detrimental effects of functional impairment on QoL, presumably due to increased biomechanical strain, postural instability, and reduced physical activity [6–8]. These findings highlight the dual burden of excessive weight gain and musculoskeletal limitations, which together contribute to restricted mobility, increased fatigue, and poorer QoL in pregnant women [6, 14]. Previous studies have demonstrated that excessive gestational weight gain increases the risk of pregnancy-related musculoskeletal pain, while rising gestational BMI is associated with a greater likelihood of developing PLBP [47]. Moreover, excessive gestational weight gain has been strongly linked to a higher risk of pelvic girdle pain (PGP), suggesting that excess weight may compromise pelvic stability and contribute to persistent discomfort [10, 16].

Interaction between gestational weeks and gestational BMI (Hypothesis 4)

Hypothesis 4 proposed a double moderated mediation effect. However, our results demonstrated that the interaction between gestational weeks and gestational BMI was not statistically significant across the four regression pathways. As shown in Table 3, the VIF values for both moderators were well below the standard cutoff, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity. Furthermore, a post-hoc power analysis based on the near-zero effect size and narrow confidence interval of the interaction term (Gestational Weeks × Gestational BMI) reported in Table 5 indicated sufficient statistical power (power > 0.80). These results suggest that the non-significant interaction likely reflects a true absence of combined moderation, rather than being due to multicollinearity or insufficient statistical power. Instead, both gestational weeks and gestational BMI independently moderated different parts of the mediation effect, with gestational BMI exerting a stronger influence on QoL. At a low BMI, the negative impact of pain severity on QoL through functional disability was not significant across most regression pathways, regardless of gestational weeks. In contrast, this effect became significant and most pronounced with advancing gestational weeks and a high BMI. These findings highlight gestational BMI as a key factor in worsening QoL through amplifying functional disability. This may be because a higher gestational BMI increases biomechanical strain, postural instability, and physical exertion, making it more challenging for pregnant women to compensate for functional impairments [6, 7, 14].

Given the significant moderating effect of BMI, proper weight management during pregnancy should be prioritized, particularly in women with a higher pre-gestational BMI [16, 48]. Gestational weight gain should be adjusted based on the pre-gestational BMI, and weight gain velocity should be carefully monitored, especially in the third trimester, when excessive weight gain is most likely to exacerbate functional impairments and increase the risk of PLBP [16, 47, 49]. Implementing targeted interventions, including nutritional counseling, weight monitoring, and low-impact exercise, can support optimal weight gain, enhance biomechanics, and improve mobility, which in turn, can mitigate the adverse effects of excessive weight gain on physical function and QoL in pregnant women [50]. Given that functional disability has a greater impact on QoL in women with a higher gestational BMI and those in later stages of pregnancy, tailored interventions including physical therapy, posture training, and lifestyle modifications, should be integrated to minimize mobility limitations and musculoskeletal stress.

Strengths and limitations

This study adds to the understanding of PLBP and its impact on functional disability and QoL in pregnant women. The main strengths of the study are as follows. First, this is the first study to examine the mediating role of functional disability and the moderating effects of gestational weeks and gestational BMI in this population. By integrating a moderated mediation model, a more comprehensive understanding of how different factors interact to influence pregnancy-related musculoskeletal pain and its consequences was elucidated. Second, the use of multiple regression pathways to incorporate different pain severity measures (PVAS and BPI) and functional disability scales (RMDQ and ODI) enhanced the robustness of the findings, as evidenced by the consistency of the observed effects across different measures. Third, our findings have important clinical implications, emphasizing the need for early intervention, tailored weight management strategies, and mobility-enhancing interventions to mitigate the impact of PLBP on QoL. These insights contribute to the development of targeted, evidence-based interventions for managing pregnancy-related disability.

This study also has some limitations. First, as a cross-sectional study, causal inferences regarding the relationships between pain severity, functional disability, and QoL were not possible. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine how these relationships evolve across different stages of pregnancy and postpartum recovery. Second, self-reported measures of pain and disability may be subject to recall bias or variability in individual pain perception [3, 9]. Objective functional assessments, such as gait analysis and muscle strength testing, could complement self-reported data to provide a more accurate understanding of physical impairment. Third, the sample collected from medical centers and regional hospitals in southern Taiwan may not fully represent pregnant women from diverse geographic, healthcare, or socio-economic backgrounds. Future studies should include more varied populations to improve generalizability. Finally, while this study adjusted for potential confounders, such as LBP history and workload type, additional psychosocial factors (e.g., depression, anxiety, and social support) may also influence the observed relationships [13, 34]. Future studies incorporating additional factors including psychosocial parameters, could provide a more comprehensive understanding of PLBP and its impact on QoL.

Conclusion

This study identified functional disability as the primary pathway linking PLBP severity and QoL in pregnant women. While pain severity alone did not directly impair QoL, its impact on physical function significantly contributed to disability and well-being outcomes. Gestational weeks and BMI independently moderated these pathways, with functional limitations worsening as pregnancy progresses and excessive weight gain further exacerbating the decline in QoL. These findings highlight the need for early screening, preventative strategies, and targeted interventions to mitigate PLBP-related disability and improve QoL. Healthcare providers should tailor treatment strategies based on pain severity profiles, with functional rehabilitation potentially being beneficial for those with high pain intensity but low interference, while a combination of physical therapy, assistive device use, weight management, and lifestyle modifications may be necessary for those experiencing high pain interference.

Beyond its clinical implications, this study contributes to obstetric and musculoskeletal research by applying a moderated mediation framework to examine the complex interplay among pain, disability, and QoL during pregnancy. It emphasizes the need to move beyond assessments of pain intensity alone and to incorporate multidimensional evaluations such as pain interference and functional limitations to better understand the impact of PLBP. These insights may guide future research on psychosocial and behavioral influences in the pain-disability-QoL relationship and support the adoption of biopsychosocial frameworks in obstetric pain management.

Future studies are encouraged to expand upon these findings and advocate for the integration of comprehensive, multidimensional strategies into routine prenatal care. These strategies should include evidence-based functional rehabilitation and gestational weight management, supported by structured educational programs, standardized screening procedures, and formal referral pathways. Incorporating these components into routine obstetric practice could facilitate early detection and effective management of PLBP-related disability, ultimately improving maternal health outcomes and enhancing QoL for pregnant women experiencing PLBP.
