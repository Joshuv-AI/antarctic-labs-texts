VLM-fusion: an intelligent diagnostic system for rural teacher professional development integrating vision-language models and adaptive learning path optimization
Authors: Lumei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRural teachers face persistent professional development challenges stemming from geographic isolation, limited training resources, and scarce peer collaboration opportunities. This paper presents an intelligent diagnostic system that integrates vision-language model (VLM) capabilities with adaptive learning path optimization to deliver personalized, context-aware development support for rural educators. We first construct a four-dimensional competency model encompassing pedagogical content knowledge, practical teaching skills, information and digital literacy, and rural contextual adaptability, with weights determined via the Analytic Hierarchy Process. A multimodal feature fusion diagnostic model employing cross-modal attention mechanisms jointly reasons over classroom video, teaching design texts, artifacts, and supplementary data streams, while a gated fusion strategy ensures robustness under missing-modality conditions common in rural settings. An Actor-Critic reinforcement learning algorithm generates adaptive learning paths by optimizing a composite reward function that balances competency improvement, resource accessibility, and temporal feasibility constraints. Experiments on a dataset of 386 rural teachers from 47 schools across five provinces, with competency ground truth established through structured expert panel ratings, suggest that the proposed VLM-Fusion model attains an F1-score of 84.9% (mean ± 0.6% across five runs) and a Spearman rank correlation of 0.918 in competency diagnosis, comparing favorably against unimodal and conventional fusion baselines. A 12-week quasi-experimental field study (rather than a long-term randomized controlled design) with 96 teachers further indicates that the adaptive path recommendation is associated with a mean competency improvement of 23.7%, exceeding expert-curated (14.2%) and self-directed (8.9%) conditions, while narrowing within-group competency variance over time. We wish to flag upfront that these improvement figures were derived using the same multimodal diagnostic pipeline that generated the personalized recommendations, not an entirely independent assessment procedure; readers should therefore treat the numerical magnitude with appropriate caution, as we discuss at length in the Limitations.Keywords: Vision-language model, Rural teacher professional development, Multimodal competency diagnosis, Adaptive learning path optimization, Cross-modal attention, Reinforcement learningSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The professional growth of teachers in rural and remote areas has long been constrained by a constellation of structural barriers that differ markedly from those encountered by their urban counterparts. Geographic isolation, scarce access to high-quality training resources, and limited peer collaboration opportunities collectively hinder the continuous competence development of rural educators1. In many developing regions, rural teachers account for a substantial proportion of the teaching workforce, yet the investment in their in-service training remains disproportionately low, resulting in widening pedagogical skill gaps between urban and rural schools2. This imbalance not only undermines educational equity but also threatens the quality of foundational learning experiences available to millions of students in underserved communities. Against this backdrop, the accelerating digital transformation of education—propelled by breakthroughs in artificial intelligence and multimodal computing—has opened new avenues for rethinking how professional development support can be designed, delivered, and personalized at scale.

Within this wave of technological advancement, vision-language models (VLMs) have emerged as a particularly promising class of multimodal architectures. By jointly learning from visual and textual data, VLMs such as CLIP, BLIP-2, and their successors demonstrate a remarkable capacity to interpret complex, context-rich information spanning images, documents, and instructional scenarios3. In the educational domain, researchers have begun to explore VLM-based applications for automated classroom observation analysis, teaching behavior recognition, and multimodal learning analytics4. These efforts signal a departure from traditional text-only diagnostic approaches, enabling systems that can process heterogeneous data sources—including lesson videos, handwritten artifacts, and visual teaching materials—to generate richer, more holistic assessments of teaching practice5. Meanwhile, intelligent diagnostic systems grounded in deep learning and knowledge graph techniques have attracted growing scholarly attention. Such systems seek to identify individual competency profiles, detect latent knowledge deficiencies, and deliver targeted feedback through data-driven inference mechanisms6. Recent work has demonstrated that coupling diagnostic outputs with adaptive learning path recommendation algorithms can substantially improve the efficiency and relevance of professional training programs7.

Despite these advances, a careful examination of the existing literature reveals several notable shortcomings when it comes to addressing the unique needs of rural teachers. Most intelligent diagnostic frameworks reported to date have been developed and validated within urban or well-resourced institutional contexts, with little consideration for the infrastructural and socio-cultural realities that characterize rural teaching environments8. The data modalities employed in prior studies tend to be relatively homogeneous—predominantly survey-based or log-based—overlooking the rich but unstructured visual and contextual information that pervades everyday rural classroom practice9. Furthermore, current adaptive learning path planning methods largely rely on static prerequisite graphs or collaborative filtering, which struggle to accommodate the highly heterogeneous starting points and fragmented learning schedules typical of rural teacher populations10. The integration of multimodal perception capabilities offered by VLMs into the diagnostic loop remains underexplored, and no existing study has systematically addressed how such integration could enhance both the granularity of competency diagnosis and the responsiveness of subsequent learning recommendations for rural educators specifically.

It is precisely this gap that motivates the present research. We argue that a meaningful leap in rural teacher professional development support requires moving beyond unimodal, context-agnostic diagnostic paradigms toward a framework that can perceive, interpret, and act upon the diverse informational signals embedded in authentic rural teaching contexts. By harnessing the cross-modal reasoning strengths of contemporary VLMs and coupling them with adaptive path optimization algorithms sensitive to individual progress trajectories, it becomes feasible to construct an intelligent system capable of delivering genuinely personalized, context-aware development guidance. Such an approach holds both theoretical significance—advancing our understanding of how multimodal AI can serve equity-oriented educational goals—and practical value, offering a scalable mechanism to narrow the professional development divide between urban and rural teachers11.

This paper presents the design, implementation, and evaluation of an intelligent diagnostic system for rural teacher professional development that fuses vision-language model capabilities with adaptive learning path optimization. The research unfolds along three interrelated threads. First, we construct a multimodal data representation framework that captures classroom visual features, textual teaching reflections, and structured competency indicators, enabling comprehensive teacher profiling. Second, we propose a VLM-driven diagnostic module that performs fine-grained competency assessment by jointly reasoning over visual and linguistic evidence extracted from authentic teaching scenarios. Third, we develop an adaptive learning path optimization algorithm that dynamically adjusts recommended training sequences based on real-time diagnostic feedback, individual learning pace, and resource availability constraints characteristic of rural settings. The technical route progresses from multimodal data preprocessing through model fine-tuning and diagnostic inference to path generation and iterative refinement, forming a closed-loop system architecture12. The main contributions of this work center on three aspects: an early integrated attempt to apply VLMs to rural teacher competency diagnosis, a context-sensitive adaptive path planning mechanism that accounts for rural-specific constraints, and an end-to-end framework that bridges the gap between multimodal perception and actionable professional development recommendations. We note that, while prior studies have explored multimodal analytics and adaptive learning independently, few have combined both within a single closed-loop system tailored to rural educators; existing frameworks have generally not addressed the data incompleteness and resource scarcity that characterize rural teaching environments8,9. Through this integrated effort, we aim to contribute both a methodological advance in AI-supported educational diagnostics and an empirically grounded solution to a pressing equity challenge in teacher development.

Theoretical foundation and technical framework

Construction of the rural teacher professional development competency model

Any robust diagnostic system must rest on a clearly articulated competency framework that reflects both general pedagogical principles and the particularities of the target population. Drawing on Shulman’s foundational taxonomy of teacher knowledge and subsequent extensions by scholars working in resource-constrained educational settings, we recognize that rural teachers operate under conditions that demand a broader and more adaptive skill repertoire than conventional models typically capture13. Standard professional development frameworks—while valuable—tend to privilege content knowledge and generic instructional strategies, often neglecting the contextual competencies that prove decisive in rural classrooms, such as multi-grade teaching adaptability, community engagement capacity, and the ability to improvise with limited technological infrastructure14.

To address this gap, we constructed a multi-dimensional competency indicator system through an iterative process involving three steps. First, an initial pool of 32 candidate indicators was generated from a systematic review of teacher competency frameworks in the literature13,14. Second, seven experts participated in iterative consultation rounds to refine and finalize the indicator set. These experts comprised three university-based teacher educators with over ten years of experience in rural education research, two provincial-level teaching and research officers responsible for rural teacher training policy, and two senior rural school principals recognized for instructional leadership. Through two rounds of Delphi-style consultation, the panel reached consensus on 8 secondary indicators organized under 4 primary dimensions, discarding 24 items judged redundant, ambiguous, or insufficiently observable in rural contexts. Third, the resulting framework was piloted with 120 practicing rural teachers across three provinces, and exploratory factor analysis (EFA) confirmed a four-factor structure consistent with the theoretical design (Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure = 0.83; Bartlett’s test of sphericity p < 0.001), with all items loading above 0.45 on their intended factors. Because the four primary dimensions are expected, on theoretical grounds, to covary rather than to stand fully independent of one another, we extracted factors via principal axis factoring (PAF) and applied a promax oblique rotation with κ = 4; the inter-factor correlations fell within the range 0.28–0.41, which reinforces the appropriateness of an oblique—rather than orthogonal—rotation scheme. We acknowledge that a pilot sample of 120 teachers across 8 secondary indicators yields a subject-to-item ratio of 15:1, which is adequate but not generous by EFA conventions, and we flag this as a reason for caution when generalizing the factor structure. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the four dimensions ranged from 0.78 to 0.86, indicating acceptable internal consistency reliability. The resulting framework encompasses four primary dimensions: pedagogical content knowledge (), practical teaching skills (), information and digital literacy (), and rural contextual adaptability (). Each primary dimension is further decomposed into secondary indicators that operationalize specific observable competencies. Application mechanism of vision-language models in education

Vision-language models represent a class of multimodal architectures that learn joint representations across visual and textual modalities, enabling machines to reason about images and language in a unified semantic space. The conceptual breakthrough underlying most contemporary VLMs is contrastive pre-training on large-scale image-text pairs, an idea crystallized by the CLIP framework3 and extended at even larger scale through noisy-supervision variants such as ALIGN19, both of which demonstrated that aligning visual encoders and text encoders through a shared embedding objective could yield surprisingly transferable representations without task-specific fine-tuning. BLIP and its successor BLIP-2 refined this paradigm further by introducing generative objectives alongside contrastive ones, allowing the model not only to match images with descriptions but also to produce contextually grounded textual outputs conditioned on visual inputs20. What makes these architectures particularly compelling for educational applications is their capacity to process the kind of heterogeneous, loosely structured data that pervades real teaching environments—classroom photographs, handwritten lesson plans, slide presentations, video keyframes—without requiring rigid, predefined feature extraction pipelines.

Formally, the core alignment mechanism in contrastive VLMs can be expressed as maximizing the cosine similarity between matched image-text pairs while minimizing it for unmatched ones. In educational contexts, these capabilities translate into three concrete application pathways that are directly relevant to the diagnostic system proposed in this paper. The first concerns classroom behavior recognition. Recent studies have shown that fine-tuned VLMs can classify teacher-student interaction patterns from video frames with accuracy rates exceeding those of conventional computer vision pipelines, largely because the textual branch supplies semantic priors about pedagogical behaviors that pure visual models lack23. The second pathway involves instructional resource comprehension—extracting pedagogical intent and content structure from visually rich materials such as annotated worksheets, concept maps, and multimedia courseware. Here, the generative capacity of BLIP-type models proves indispensable, as it enables the system to produce structured textual descriptions of visual teaching artifacts that can then feed into diagnostic reasoning modules24. The third, and perhaps most challenging, pathway pertains to holistic teaching competency assessment. By aggregating cross-modal evidence from multiple data streams—observed classroom behaviors, resource design quality, and reflective textual narratives—a VLM-based system can construct a richer evaluative portrait than any single-modality approach would permit25. We note, however, that realizing this potential in rural settings demands careful domain adaptation, since pre-trained VLMs carry distributional biases toward well-resourced, urban-centric educational imagery, a challenge we address explicitly in the system design described in subsequent Sect26.

Adaptive learning path optimization theory

The notion that instruction should adapt to the evolving state of each learner is far from new—its intellectual roots stretch back to Crowder’s branching programmed instruction in the 1960s—but the computational machinery to realize this vision at scale has only matured in recent years. Knowledge tracing, the task of modeling a learner’s latent mastery over time, has undergone successive paradigm shifts: from early Bayesian Knowledge Tracing models built on hidden Markov assumptions, through item response theory variants, to contemporary deep knowledge tracing architectures that harness recurrent and attention-based neural networks to capture complex temporal learning dynamics27. These advances have been instrumental in providing the state estimation backbone that any adaptive path planner requires. Yet accurate diagnosis of what a learner knows is only half the problem. The harder question—and the one more directly pertinent to our system—is how to translate that evolving diagnosis into a sequence of learning activities that optimally advances the learner toward specified competency targets.

Reinforcement learning (RL) offers an elegant formalism for this sequential decision problem. Rather than prescribing a fixed curriculum, an RL-based path planner treats each recommendation as an action taken in response to the learner’s current state, receiving reward signals tied to observed competency gains28. Concurrently, graph neural networks (GNNs) have proven effective at encoding the relational structure among knowledge concepts and training modules, capturing prerequisite dependencies and semantic affinities that flat vector representations inevitably miss29. Several recent works have demonstrated that coupling GNN-derived knowledge graph embeddings with RL-based policy learning yields path recommendations superior to those generated by either technique in isolation30.

Intelligent diagnostic system design and algorithm implementation

Overall system architecture design

Translating the theoretical constructs outlined in Section II into a functioning diagnostic system demands an architecture that can accommodate heterogeneous data inputs, perform cross-modal reasoning, and deliver actionable developmental guidance—all while remaining responsive enough for iterative use in bandwidth-constrained rural environments. After considering several candidate designs, we settled on a four-layer architecture that separates concerns cleanly while maintaining tight data flow integration across layers. The foundation of the system is the Data Acquisition Layer, which serves as the primary interface between the real-world teaching environment and the computational pipeline. This layer aggregates multimodal inputs from diverse sources: classroom observation videos captured via low-cost recording devices, digitized lesson plans and teaching reflections uploaded through a lightweight mobile application, structured self-assessment questionnaires aligned with the competency indicators defined in Table 1, and contextual metadata describing the teacher’s school resources and geographic conditions. A key design decision here was to support asynchronous, offline-capable data collection—a practical necessity given that many rural schools experience intermittent network connectivity34. Raw inputs undergo preliminary validation and format standardization before being forwarded to the subsequent processing stage.

Sitting above this ingestion layer, the Feature Extraction Layer transforms heterogeneous raw data into semantically meaningful representations amenable to diagnostic reasoning. Visual data streams—video keyframes, photographs of classroom artifacts, scanned handwritten materials—pass through the visual encoder of our fine-tuned VLM, while textual inputs are processed by the corresponding language branch. The contrastive alignment mechanism described in Eqs. (4) and (5) ensures that extracted visual and textual features reside in a shared embedding space, enabling direct cross-modal comparison and fusion. For structured numerical inputs such as questionnaire scores and institutional metadata, we employ a separate encoding submodule based on a lightweight multi-layer perceptron, whose outputs are projected into the same representational space via a learned linear transformation35. The fused multimodal feature vectors then constitute the state representation that feeds both the diagnostic engine and the path planner.

The Intelligent Diagnostic Layer constitutes the analytical core of the system. Receiving the fused feature representations, this module performs two complementary functions: dimension-level competency scoring and holistic profile classification. Dimension-level scoring maps each teacher’s multimodal evidence onto the four primary competency dimensions ( through ) using the weighted aggregation model in Eq. (3), producing fine-grained assessments that pinpoint specific developmental needs. Holistic profile classification, meanwhile, assigns each teacher to one of several developmental archetypes—such as “content-strong but digitally underequipped” or “practically skilled but lacking reflective depth”—through a clustering mechanism trained on historical diagnostic data36. Together, these outputs provide both the quantitative precision and the qualitative interpretability necessary for meaningful professional development guidance.

Finally, the Path Recommendation Layer consumes the diagnostic outputs and generates personalized, sequenced learning activity plans. Grounded in the MDP framework formalized in Eq. (6) through (8), this layer employs an Actor-Critic policy network augmented with GNN-encoded knowledge graph structure to select and order training resources, micro-courses, peer collaboration opportunities, and practice tasks that align with each teacher’s diagnosed deficiencies and available resource conditions. Critically, the path recommendation module operates in a closed-loop fashion: as teachers engage with recommended activities and new multimodal evidence accumulates, the diagnostic layer updates its competency estimates, which in turn trigger path recalibration. This iterative feedback cycle—from data acquisition through diagnosis to recommendation and back—ensures that the system adapts continuously rather than offering a single static prescription, a property we regard as essential for sustaining engagement among rural teachers whose professional development trajectories are rarely linear or predictable37.

Multimodal feature fusion diagnostic model

The diagnostic accuracy of our system hinges on its ability to construct a unified representation from data sources that differ not merely in format but in fundamental semantic granularity. A classroom video keyframe captures spatial-temporal behavioral patterns; a teaching design document encodes pedagogical intentionality through linguistic structure; student feedback data reflects learning outcomes in quantified or semi-structured form. Naively concatenating features extracted independently from these modalities—an approach still common in many educational analytics systems—discards precisely the inter-modal correlations that carry the most diagnostic signal38. Our design instead pursues deep fusion through a cross-modal attention mechanism that allows each modality to selectively attend to informative elements in the others before any diagnostic inference occurs.

Adaptive learning path generation algorithm

With diagnostic outputs providing a granular portrait of each teacher’s competency profile, the remaining challenge is to translate those assessments into a coherent, temporally ordered sequence of learning activities that respects both pedagogical logic and the practical realities of rural professional life. The first step toward this goal involves constructing a learning resource association network—essentially a knowledge graph whose nodes represent individual training modules, micro-courses, practice tasks, and peer mentoring opportunities, and whose edges encode prerequisite dependencies, thematic affinity, and difficulty progression relationships. We build this graph by combining expert-curated curriculum structures with data-driven edge weight estimation based on historical learner transition patterns43. Each node in the resource graph is characterized by an attribute vector that includes its target competency dimension, estimated completion time, bandwidth requirement, and offline availability status—attributes whose importance becomes clear when we consider that a rural teacher may have only sporadic evening hours and unreliable internet access to devote to professional learning.

Experimental verification and result analysis

Experimental design and dataset construction

Validating the proposed system requires a dataset that faithfully reflects the multimodal complexity of rural teaching practice—something that, to our knowledge, no publicly available benchmark currently provides. We therefore undertook a dedicated data collection effort spanning 18 months across 47 rural primary and secondary schools located in five provinces with varying levels of economic development and geographic accessibility. Participating teachers ( N=386) were recruited through local education bureaus and consented to contribute classroom recordings, lesson planning documents, written teaching reflections, student feedback questionnaires, and institutional metadata. Each teacher contributed data across two to four observation cycles spaced approximately one semester apart, yielding a longitudinal dimension that proved essential for validating the system’s adaptive path recommendations over time.

The question of generalization deserves particular scrutiny, since rural teachers are far from a homogeneous population. Teaching experience, subject specialization, and geographic context all shape a teacher’s competency profile in ways that could interact unpredictably with model behavior. We partitioned the test set into four subgroups based on teaching experience: novice (0–3 years, n=24), developing (4–8 years, n=31), experienced (9–15 years, n=16), and veteran (16 + years, n=7).

The baseline, by contrast, exhibits a sharper decline for both novice and veteran teachers—groups whose competency profiles deviate most from population averages. Learning path optimization effect verification

Diagnostic accuracy, however strong, means little if the paths generated from those diagnoses fail to produce tangible professional growth. To evaluate this downstream impact, we conducted a quasi-experimental field study over a 12-week period involving 96 rural teachers drawn from 23 schools that had not participated in the dataset construction phase described in Sect. 4.1. Participants were assigned to one of three conditions through stratified random assignment based on teaching experience and school resource level. We characterize this as a quasi-experimental design because, although assignment was randomized within strata, neither participants nor assessors were blinded to condition, and the sample was drawn from volunteer schools rather than a probability sample of the full rural teacher population. The three conditions were: an experimental group receiving fully adaptive path recommendations from our system ( n=34), an active control group following expert-curated but non-personalized training sequences ( n=32), and a self-directed group given access to the same learning resource library without any structured guidance ( n=30). All three groups had identical access to the underlying training materials—the sole difference lay in how resources were sequenced and recommended.

A one-way ANCOVA controlling for baseline scores confirmed that these differences were statistically significant (, p<0.001), with post-hoc Bonferroni comparisons revealing significant pairwise differences between the adaptive group and both control conditions ( p<0.01 in each case). The partial eta-squared effect size was 0.286, indicating a large effect. Adjusted group means (controlling for baseline) were 0.691 for the adaptive group, 0.588 for the expert-curated group, and 0.534 for the self-directed group. Homogeneity of regression slopes was verified (interaction p = 0.41), and Levene’s test confirmed equal variances (p = 0.23)53. Regarding study integrity, 91 of the 96 enrolled teachers completed all three assessment points (attrition rate: 5.2%), with 2 dropouts from the adaptive group (both due to school transfer), 1 from the expert-curated group, and 2 from the self-directed group. Among completers, mean resource engagement rates were 87.3% in the adaptive group, 79.1% in the expert-curated group, and 61.4% in the self-directed group, measured as the proportion of recommended or available modules actually accessed. We detected no evidence of cross-group contamination, as participants were drawn from geographically separated schools. What surprised us, in retrospect, was not merely the magnitude of the improvement gap but its trajectory. The adaptive group’s gains accelerated between and , whereas the expert-curated group’s growth rate plateaued and the self-directed group’s actually decelerated—a pattern consistent with the hypothesis that ongoing diagnostic recalibration keeps recommendations aligned with evolving needs, preventing the stagnation that afflicts static curricula.

Discussion

The experimental evidence assembled in Section IV paints a largely encouraging picture, but it would be premature to accept these results without probing their boundaries, examining what they reveal about underlying mechanisms, and confronting the practical complications that separate a research prototype from a deployed educational tool. We organize this discussion around four interlocking themes.

The first concerns what the VLM-based diagnostic approach genuinely adds—and where it falls short. The ablation results in Table 6 make clear that cross-modal attention is the single most consequential architectural choice, contributing nearly 40% of the total performance gain. This is not a trivial finding. It suggests that the diagnostic value of multimodal data lies less in the sheer volume of information and more in the interplay between modalities—the way a teacher’s written reflection can disambiguate a visually ambiguous classroom interaction, or the way a photograph of a cluttered blackboard can contextualize an otherwise competent-seeming lesson plan. A question worth examining more closely is why text appears more informative than video in this diagnostic context (Table 5: text-only F1 = 69.1% vs. video-only F1 = 65.0%). We suspect this reflects the nature of the expert annotation process itself: annotators weighted pedagogical reasoning and intentionality heavily, and these qualities are more directly encoded in written lesson plans and reflections than in visual behavioral patterns, which require substantial inference to connect to underlying competency. Video, however, contributes unique complementary signals—classroom management behaviors, student engagement cues, and physical resource utilization—that written documents cannot capture, which explains why multimodal fusion still substantially outperforms text alone. Regarding the disproportionate fine-tuning benefit for Rural Contextual Adaptability (Fig. 4 and 11.3-point gain versus 5.7 points for Pedagogical Content Knowledge), we attribute this to the distributional gap between pre-training data and rural educational content: generic VLMs have encountered abundant pedagogical content in their pre-training corpora but very little content depicting rural-specific practices such as multi-grade instruction, community-integrated lessons, or improvisation under resource constraints. Fine-tuning on our rural dataset therefore has the greatest marginal impact on precisely these underrepresented dimensions. At the same time, we must acknowledge that the system’s diagnostic accuracy, while strong in aggregate, remains imperfect in ways that matter. The 82.4% F1-score for veteran teachers, though not statistically distinguishable from other subgroups, hints at a ceiling effect: teachers with decades of experience often display competency patterns that are subtle, idiosyncratic, and poorly represented in the training data. Our model, trained predominantly on developing and mid-career teacher profiles, may lack sufficient exposure to the nuanced signatures of expert practice. This is a limitation we do not wish to minimize.

Second, the learning path optimization results deserve careful mechanistic interpretation. The accelerating improvement trajectory observed in the adaptive group (Fig. 8) contrasts sharply with the plateau in the expert-curated condition and, we believe, reflects a fundamentally different dynamic. Static curricula—even those designed by skilled experts—inevitably drift out of alignment with individual learner states as training progresses, because each teacher’s competency profile evolves along a unique trajectory that no predetermined sequence can anticipate. The closed-loop design of our system, where diagnostic reassessment triggers path recalibration at each step, maintains this alignment continuously. In essence, the system converts professional development from a one-time prescription into an ongoing conversation between the teacher’s evolving needs and the available resource ecology. The narrowing variance within the adaptive group further suggests an equity-enhancing property: the system appears to invest more intensively in teachers who start from lower baselines, gradually compressing the competency distribution rather than amplifying pre-existing gaps. The practical implication of this narrowing variance is significant for policy: it suggests that adaptive systems of this kind could serve as equity-enhancing tools that partially compensate for the uneven starting conditions among rural teachers, though we stress that a 12-week window is too brief to confirm whether this compression persists over longer time horizons or reverses once system support is withdrawn.

Third, we must confront the deployment challenges that any system of this kind faces in rural contexts. The most immediate is computational infrastructure. Our full model requires GPU-accelerated inference for real-time cross-modal attention computation—a resource unavailable at most rural schools. During the field study, we addressed this through a hybrid architecture where data was collected locally on mobile devices and transmitted to a cloud server for processing during off-peak hours. This asynchronous design worked adequately in our study context, but it introduces latency that could undermine user engagement if recommendation turnaround times stretch beyond what teachers perceive as responsive. Edge deployment of distilled model variants represents one promising mitigation path, though the performance trade-offs of model compression in this specific application domain remain to be quantified. A second, subtler challenge is teacher trust. Several participants in informal post-study interviews expressed uncertainty about why particular resources were recommended, highlighting a familiar interpretability gap. The system currently generates recommendations without accompanying explanations—an omission that future iterations should rectify through attention visualization or natural language rationale generation.

Finally, situating our work within the broader landscape of AI-assisted teacher development reveals both convergences and departures. Prior systems have demonstrated the feasibility of automated classroom observation analysis and text-based competency assessment as independent capabilities, but none—to our knowledge—has integrated these within a single closed-loop framework that feeds multimodal diagnosis directly into adaptive path planning. Our approach also differs from most existing learning path recommendation systems in its explicit modeling of resource accessibility constraints through the penalty terms in Eq. (17), a design choice motivated by the specific realities of rural education but potentially transferable to other resource-constrained professional development contexts. Where our work converges with prior findings is in confirming that personalization matters more than content quality alone: even when all groups accessed identical resources, the sequencing and timing of recommendations proved decisive, reaffirming a principle that the adaptive learning literature has long advocated but that teacher professional development research has been slow to operationalize at scale.

Conclusion

This paper has presented an integrated intelligent diagnostic system that fuses vision-language model capabilities with adaptive learning path optimization to address the persistent challenge of rural teacher professional development. The work spanned from theoretical grounding—constructing a four-dimensional competency model calibrated to rural teaching realities through AHP-weighted indicators—through system architecture design and algorithm development, to empirical validation via both offline benchmarking and a 12-week field experiment. What emerged is not merely a technical artifact but, we hope, a step toward rethinking how AI can serve educational equity in contexts that mainstream research too often overlooks.

The core contributions of this research can be distilled into three threads. First, the multimodal diagnostic framework demonstrated that cross-modal attention between visual and textual evidence streams yields substantially richer competency assessments than any single-modality or naive fusion approach. The 84.9% F1-score and 0.918 rank correlation achieved by the full VLM-Fusion model represent competitive results for automated teacher competency diagnosis in rural settings, though we note that direct comparison with prior work is complicated by the absence of a shared public benchmark in this domain. The gating mechanism proved particularly consequential for practical robustness—maintaining diagnostic quality even when individual modality streams were missing or degraded, a scenario that is the norm rather than the exception in under-resourced schools. Second, the Actor-Critic-based adaptive path generation algorithm, with its explicit modeling of time constraints and resource accessibility through the reward function in Eq. (17), produced measurably superior professional development outcomes compared to both expert-curated and self-directed alternatives. The 23.7% mean competency improvement in the adaptive group, against 14.2% and 8.9% in the control conditions, speaks to a fundamental insight: personalized sequencing matters as much as content quality. Third, the narrowing competency variance within the adaptive group provides a preliminary indication of a possible equity-enhancing dynamic—the system appears to invest proportionally more in teachers with greater initial deficits—though longer-term studies are needed to determine whether this compression persists and whether it translates into durable pedagogical improvement rather than short-term score gains.

The theoretical implications extend in two directions. For the educational technology community, this work demonstrates that contemporary VLMs, when appropriately fine-tuned and embedded within closed-loop systems, can transcend their origins in generic computer vision and natural language processing to address domain-specific diagnostic challenges that demand contextual sensitivity. For teacher education research more broadly, the results offer encouraging (though preliminary) empirical support for the long-standing argument that professional development should be treated as an adaptive, individualized process rather than a uniform curricular delivery.

We are candid about the limitations. The dataset, while carefully constructed, encompasses 386 teachers from five provinces—a sample that, however diverse within its scope, cannot claim to represent the full heterogeneity of rural education across an entire nation. The 12-week field study, though sufficient to detect meaningful competency gains, is too short to assess whether those gains persist or how the system performs over multiple academic years of continuous use. The model’s relative weakness on veteran teacher profiles, noted in Sect. 4.2, points to a data imbalance that targeted collection efforts could address but that we did not resolve in this iteration. Furthermore, the computational demands of real-time multimodal inference remain a barrier to fully autonomous deployment at the school level, currently necessitating cloud-based processing with attendant latency and connectivity dependencies.

These limitations naturally chart the course for future work. Expanding the dataset to encompass additional provinces and minority-language teaching contexts would strengthen generalizability claims. Longitudinal studies tracking teachers over two to three years would clarify whether adaptive path recommendations produce durable competency transformation or merely accelerate short-term gains. On the technical front, model distillation and edge deployment strategies deserve systematic investigation to reduce the infrastructure burden on rural schools. Perhaps most ambitiously, incorporating explainable AI mechanisms—generating human-readable justifications for both diagnostic assessments and path recommendations—could address the trust concerns surfaced in our field study and move the system from a black-box tool toward a genuine pedagogical partner. We close with a deliberately modest framing. What we present here is a promising, well-supported early-stage system demonstration, not a deployment-ready solution; the sample scope (386 teachers across five provinces), the short 12-week follow-up window, the underrepresentation of veteran teachers in the training data, and the current reliance on cloud-based inference all mark the distance that remains between this prototype and an infrastructure that could be recommended for broad adoption. If this work contributes to a future in which a teacher’s geographic circumstance no longer predetermines the quality of professional growth support available to them, it will have served its purpose.
