Diabetes mellitus, ABO Blood groups, and biochemical correlates in pancreatic cancer– A retrospective study from Jeddah
Authors: Syed Sameer Aga, Muhammad Anwar Khan, Mohammed S. Thabet, Nawaf Muteb Alharbi, Waleed Mohammed Aljehani, Abdulazziz Khalid Alhazmi, Mohammed Fayih Alotaibi, Saniya Nissar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Pancreatic cancer (PC) is the seventh-leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide. Studies have established a 2-fold risk of PC among individuals with diabetes mellitus (DM). In this study, we aimed to determine the prevalence and biochemical correlates of DM and in PC patients.Methods:The current retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted between 2016 and 2022. The study sample included all patients aged >18 who were admitted after a primary diagnosis of PC at our center. A comprehensive review of the charts was conducted to gather demographic data, including diabetes and ABO Rh blood group (BG).Results:A total of 186 PC patients were included, 76 (40.9%) of whom were females and 110 (59.1%) were males. DM was found in 120 (64.5%) patients, of whom 125 (67.2%) were diagnosed pre-PC and constituted 72 (60%) males and 48 (40%) females. The most prevalent type of PC was adenocarcinoma (68.6%), with stage IV being 97.8% of the cases. The most common BG among PC patients was type O in 68 (36.6%) patients, followed by type A in 45 (24.2%). Also, 124 (66.7%) were Rh-positive, while 14 (7.5%) were Rh-negative. Serum CA19-9 was the most commonly detected biomarker, present in 27.4% of patients.Conclusion:The percentage of DM among PC patients was found to be 64.5%, of whom 3.8% were diagnosed after the diagnosis of PC. Blood group O was the most common; however BG type B was associated with a higher risk of DM in (P = 0.028) in PC patients.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a common chronic metabolic illness that is becoming recognized as a comorbidity and moderator of cancer risk and outcomes.[1] Epidemiological data suggest that approximately 8–18% of patients with cancer have co-existing DM.[2] DM has been reported to be associated with an increased incidence of several malignancies, including pancreatic, liver, colorectal, breast, endometrial, and some gastrointestinal cancers.[23] Among all cancers, the link between diabetes and pancreatic cancer (PC) is particularly strong and bidirectional. Long-term type 2 diabetes increases the incidence of pancreatic cancer by 1.5-2 times. PC can also cause type 3c diabetes due to exocrine breakdown and tumor-mediated metabolic disturbances.[4567]

PC, a severe malignancy, has a poor survival rate. Multiple investigations have shown that diabetes is an independent risk factor for the development of PC.[56789] PC ranks 12th in global incidence and almost 6th in cancer-related mortality, reflecting its poor prognosis.[1011] In the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, the burden of PC is already above the global average and rising, with many Middle Eastern countries, especially higher-income Gulf states, currently reporting PC rates equivalent or higher than the worldwide average.[1213] The burden of PC in Saudi Arabia is relatively low, ranking 22nd and predominantly affecting the elderly population (>50 years).[1415]

The association between PC and DM is well reported in the literature, during the early stages, almost half of PC patients develop type 2 diabetic mellitus (T2DM) or reduced glucose tolerance. T2DM is a well-established risk factor for PC, and new-onset diabetes (NODM) might be an early symptom of PC.[5111617] PC has also been connected with the age of diabetes patients, their smoking habits, and their family history of digestive tract cancer.[69] However, there appear to be issues in specialized prediction models based on risk factors alone and hence the nature of association between diabetes and PC remains controversial as some studies report positive effects on overall survival (OS),[1819] while others fail to do so.[2021] Furthermore, recent meta-analysis and observational studies showed that the duration of DM has a strong correlation with PC, with about two-fold higher risk of developing PC than non-diabetics, particularly when diabetes duration is under 4–5 years, but elevated risk persists even beyond 10 years.[568212223] Data from Saudi Arabia on the link is limited and only a few studies exist, one of which reports that people of higher economic status to have a higher chance of developing PC.[15]

There is a limitation of the local data and literature on the incidence of DM among PC patients from Saudi Arabia, which is pivotal for understanding the relationship between DM and PC for an improved healthcare and a better prognosis. Additionally, there are not many population-specific data is available on the relationship between PC and DM. Therefore, the aim of this study was to find out the prevalence of DM among PC patients and the association between DM and clinic-pathological characteristics of PC in King Abdulaziz Medical City (KAMC), Jeddah.

Methods

Study design

The selected study design was an analytical cross-sectional study to get the exact burden of the disease in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The study was conducted at Princess Noorah Oncology Center, KAMC, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia between the period of January 2016 to December 2022.

Consent and ethical approval

The study was carried out in line with the Helsinki protocol and ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of King Abdullah International Medical Research Centre (KAIMRC), KSAU-HS, Jeddah was duly acquired prior to conducting this study (IRB-0708-23; SP23J/044/03; Dated: 19/03/2023). No participant names or identification were gathered, and the data was kept on the PI’s work PC in 64-bit encrypted software that was difficult for unauthorized individuals to access.

Study sample

The study included all patients who were diagnosed with primary PC. Patients were identified from the medical records databases by using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9) codes for pancreatic cancer (C25). Two inclusion criteria applied a) age not less than 18; b) confirmed histological or cytological diagnosis as per European Society for Medical Oncology guidelines. The exclusion criteria were any patient diagnosed with other types of cancer, primarily (such as liver cancer, gastric, lung cancer, or colorectal cancer) or secondary due to metastasis from the pancreas.

As the study was a periodic study, we included all the patients who fit the inclusion criteria. However, for reference purposes the required sample size was calculated at a 95% confidence level with a margin of error of 5% for a population of 20000, which was about 377 odd PC patients.

Data collection tool

The data collection tool was formulated from the available literature to include demographics, risk factors, clinical manifestations of the PC and DM. The demographics included the following: gender, education level, age, weight, family history, blood group type, rhesus factor (Rh), date of diagnosis of PC type of PC, the anatomical position of the tumor, diagnostic methods, biomarkers used for the diagnosis, the stage of the tumor, history of cancers, family history of cancers, histopathological grade, laterality, treatment outcome, recurrence rate, type of treatment intervention, and other clinic-pathological features. For the DM, various information’s including the date of diagnosis of DM, the type of DM, physical activity, body mass index, HbA1c etc., were collected.

Data and statistical analysis

Data were entered into MS Excel, and before the analysis by SPSS software, the dataset was prepared and checked for any missing data. Chi-square test was performed on the data. Descriptive statistics (e.g., mean and standard deviation) were used to describe continuous variables, while categorical variables were presented in frequencies and percentages. An independent sample t-test was used to determine the mean score differences. The confidence was set at 95%, and statistical significance was determined at P ≤ 0.05.

Results

The retrospective data were collected between January 2016 and December 2022. After the application of the inclusion criteria and data cleaning, 94 patients were excluded. The final sample size was 186 PC patients. Among 186 participants, 76 (40.9%) were females, 110 (59.1%) were males, 141 (75.8%) were married, 136 (87.2%) were deceased, 88 (47.3%) were unemployed, 5 (2.7%, n = 178) had a family history of PC, and 16 (8.7%) were smokers. Analysis of the association of BG types and DM among PC patients revealed that the most common BG was type O – found in 51 (42.5%) of the PC patients with DM, followed by type A in 23 (19.2%). Also, 83 (69.2%) PC patients with DM had Rh-positive blood, while only 4 (3.3%) had negative, respectively [Table 6]. Contrarily, having BG type B was associated with higher odds (OR = 2.74) of DM in PC patients (P = 0.028). In addition, Rh negativity was found to be significantly associated with higher odds of DM (OR 4.58, 95% CI 1.35–15.51, P = 0.008) in diabetic PC patients [see Table 6].

Discussion

In our current study, we found that 120 (64.5%) of the PC patients were diagnosed with DM, either preceding or following the diagnosis of PC, indicating a very high burden of glycemic disturbance in our cohort. These proportions, although higher than the global proportions of 30-40%, do concur with the many published studies around the world.[52425] Furthermore, the prevalence of diabetes among PC patients in our cohort did not differ significantly between men and women, indicating that gender is unlikely to be a major predictor of diabetes in this population, which also matches the existing literature.[56811] The high frequency of diabetes in our study highlights the clinical interdependence between DM and PC, thereby highlighting the importance of examining PC in older persons with new-onset or quickly worsening diabetes, as well as optimizing metabolic control after cancer diagnosis.[6212223]

Aggarwal et al.[24] reported that patients with PC exhibited a higher prevalence of diabetes (68%) compared to those with lung, breast, prostate, or colorectal malignancies (15-21%) and non-cancer controls (24%), despite similar age and gender distributions across groups. In PC patients, 40% had diabetes by 36 months of diagnosis, compared to only 3-6% in other cancer groups and controls, which also matches our post-diagnostic rate of 3.8%. Amri et al.[5] in their retrospective cohort study reported that approximately 38% of pancreatic cancer patients had diabetes, with a significant proportion of new-onset diabetes diagnosed within a year or at the time of cancer diagnosis. They also concluded that diabetic patients with pancreatic cancer were typically older and more frequently obese. Additionally, Conway et al.,[25] in their recent study among African American and White adults, reported that diabetes was associated with a 54% higher risk of PC overall (OR = 1.5), and demonstrated that African American race remained independently related with around 36% greater pancreatic cancer risk, even after correcting for diabetes and other variables.

Furthermore, one of the demographics of our cohort was that most PC patients were married (75%), which was in concordance with the existing literature. The high proportion of married patients in a PC cohort is consistent with large population-based studies that reveal most persons diagnosed with PC are married at the time of diagnosis. In Baine et al.’s[26] SEER investigation, married individuals accounted for around two-thirds of PC cases, and marital status was a significant sociodemographic variable in their survival models. Similar patterns have been observed in other registry and hospital-based series, where 60-75% of PC patients were reported to be married, reflecting both the typical age at diagnosis and the status confirmed to be an independent prognostic factor in both univariate and multivariate analyses.[2728]

ABO BG has been reported to be a major risk linked to several types of cancers, although its association with overall cancer risk remains controversial.[29303132] ABO BG refers to the phenotypes of two antigens, A and B, on a polypeptide termed H antigen, which is produced by a single multi-exon ABO gene on chromosome 9q34.2. Antigens A and B are oligosaccharides, with A being N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) and B being galactose (Gal), and a lack of expression of either antigen makes the O phenotype.[33] In our earlier study on gynecological cancer, we found a significant association between BG type B and endometrial and serous histology in the fallopian tube and BG type AB and ovarian malignancies, respectively.[34] Current understanding implies a relationship between ABO BG antigens and cancer, possibly through the activation of inflammatory pathway molecules that influence cancer formation and progression.[3233] ABO BG antigens are major histocompatibility complex antigens, which could influence immune responses to cancer cells. ABO gene locus and genetic variants have been linked to circulating levels of inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, ICAM-1, E-selectin, and P-selectin, which are crucial for immune cell recruitment.[353637]

In our current study, the most prevalent BG among PC patients with DM was BG type O, which was identified in 51 (42.5%) of them. Among diabetic PC patients, BG type O was significantly associated with a lower risk of DM (P = 0.023), but BG type B was associated with a higher risk (OR = 2.74) of DM in PC patients (P = 0.028), which was consistent with many studies.[31383940] Pelzer et al.[38] in their German study, they reported that the most common BG among the PC patients was type 0 (56.6%), but demonstrated that patients with blood group A had a significant association with PC (P < 0.001) and Rh had no impact on ABO-distribution. Nakao et al.[39] in their case-control study, they concluded that non-O ABO blood group alleles, particularly A and B, confer a dose-dependent increased risk of pancreatic cancer in Japanese patients (OR 1.67 for AO, P = 0.012), confirming O›s protective role across ethnicities. Kim et al.[40] discovered that the increased PC risk associated with non-O ABO blood groups is significantly modulated by the secretor (FUT2) genotype, with strong secretors increasing the risk for non-O types. Additionally, BG type O remained protective regardless of secretor status, corroborating our result that O predominance is associated with decreased DM chances among diabetic PC patients. The recent study by Williams et al.[41] concluded that ABO blood group, including type O, shows no significant association with overall or disease-free survival for PC.

In our current research, adenocarcinoma is the most common histopathologic feature in PC patients. It accounts for 90% of the cancer in patients with known pathology reports and 68.6% generally in all cases. Similar to the previously published study, where adenocarcinoma was the most common type of PC, with more than 86% of all cases.[42] Most of the anatomical distribution of PC was in the head of the pancreas with 64.1%, the body 27% and the tail 21.1%, respectively. This was in concordance with the previous study, where 60-70% of PC patients were reported to have a tumor in the head and 20-25% in the body and tail region.[4344]

Furthermore, we found that 97.8% of patients with PC had stage 4 at the time of diagnosis, and 71.5% of patients had metastasis, with the liver being the most common site of metastasis in 57.5% of the patients. Zhang et al.[45] highlighted the PC’s rising global incidence in their study, and reported the late-stage diagnosis in 80–90% of cases, and liver as the primary metastatic site (50–60%), attributing poor outcomes to absent screening and nonspecific symptoms. Rawla et al.[46] reported that PC is the seventh-leading cause of cancer death globally having the late-stage diagnosis (>80% advanced) and dismal 9% 5-year survival, mirroring the current cohort’s patterns. Mao et al.[47] identified diabetes as an independent unfavorable prognostic factor in PC, the effect of diabetes on overall survival was linked to tumor stage and diabetes duration, which is consistent with the current cohort’s high DM prevalence (64.5%), which may contribute to its advanced presentation and prognosis.

Finally, among the range of biomarkers utilized for the early detection of PC,[4849] we found two biomarkers - CA19-9 and CEA to be primary detectable biomarkers in our studied patients with detectable percentage of 27.4% and 13.4%, respectively. CA19-9 is the most validated serum biomarker and has the best positive predictive value as a standalone marker.[49] It has been reported to outperform CEA as a reliable biomarker for the diagnosis of PC, with a pooled sensitivity of 78-81% vs 44% for CEA, with both having comparable specificity. Combining CA19-9 with CEA improves its overall accuracy.[4950] This supports our cohort’s findings reflecting established diagnostic hierarchies in advanced PC.

Study limitations

This study has some potential limitations. The data from this single-center retrospective study may not be representative of the broader community because it was conducted at a single oncology center in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. Due to its retrospective design, the data had some irregularities in many parameters, especially the missing data on key variables like blood groups, biomarkers (e.g., CK7 and CDX2), and pathology reports, which introduced potential selection bias and reduced statistical power for subgroup analyses.

Conclusion

Overall, the prevalence of DM among PC patients in KAMC, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia was found to be 64.5%, of whom 3.8% were diagnosed after diagnosis of malignancy. The most common blood group among PC patients with diabetes was BG type O, which was found to be significantly related to a lower risk of DM (P = 0.023), as opposed to BG type B, which was associated with a higher risk of DM in PC patients (P = 0.028). In addition, Rh-negative status was found to be significantly associated with a higher risk of DM (OR 4.58, P = 0.008). Finally, future multicenter prospective studies with complete data capture would strengthen these findings.
