Insular spontaneous activity changes after dialectical behavior therapy skills training for depressed patients with non-suicidal self-injury: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Maochun Lei, Sichu Wu, Yaming Hang, Minlu Liang, Pei Zhang, Kun Yang, Jing Long, Chun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) frequently co-occurs with major depressive disorder (MDD). This study aims to investigate the efficacy of dialectical behavior therapy skills training (DBT-ST) in treating major depressive disorder with non-suicidal self-injury (nsMDDs) under routine medication, as well as its modulatory effects on neural mechanisms.MethodsThis assessor-masked, multicenter, randomized controlled trial enrolled 80 patients with nsMDDs from outpatient or inpatient departments of four hospitals. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either DBT-ST or social support group therapy (SSGT) once weekly for 13 weeks. NSSI severity, depressive severity, and spontaneous brain activity were assessed in both training groups before and after the intervention. The data analysis was conducted using SPSS 23 and MATLAB 2013a.ResultsFindings demonstrated that both intervention groups were effective in alleviating clinical symptoms. Subgroup analyses by age revealed that DBT-ST demonstrated more significant improvements in NSSI behavior (B = 1.15, p < 0.001, d = -1.24) and depressive symptoms (B = 22.22, p < 0.001, d = -1.61) among adolescents (13 ~ 17 years old). Resting-state data revealed that following intervention, the DBT-ST group exhibited significantly reduced amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) in the right insula (F = 11.85, p = 0.003, d = 0.93) and regional homogeneity (ReHo) in the left insula (F = 18.48, p < 0.001, d = 1.24). Furthermore, following intervention, improvement in depressive symptoms within the DBT-ST group showed a significantly correlated with the reduction in ALFF in the right insula (r = 0.694, p = 0.038).ConclusionsDBT-ST may demonstrate superior applicability for improving clinical symptoms in adolescents with nsMDDs. Concurrently, DBT-ST may modulate abnormal spontaneous brain activity within neural circuits associated with nsMDDs. However, as this study did not track medication changes during the intervention period, the confounding effect of medication on neural alterations cannot be ruled out.Trial registrationClinicalTrials No.: NCT04094623, retrospectively registered 13 September 2019.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08254-z.

Background

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is intentional, self-inflicted damage to the body without suicidal intent [1]. This includes cutting, scratching, or burning body surfaces, as well as hitting objects that cause direct injury to the skin or bones [2]. In the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), NSSI is considered a separate category of behavior. A survey in Germany found that 25 ~ 35% of randomly selected adolescents in schools had at least one NSSI, and the prevalence of NSSI among child and adolescent with mental disorders was about 50% [2]. The results of a survey on NSSI behaviors in Chinese mental disorders patients showed that the prevalence of NSSI in outpatient clinics and wards was 6.8% and 6.5%, respectively, with the highest prevalence of 15.9% in the age of 13 ~ 17 years old, followed by the age of 18 ~ 22 years old with 13.6%, while the prevalence of other age groups was lower [3]. Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a debilitating disease that is characterized by depressed mood, diminished interests, impaired cognitive function and vegetative symptoms, such as disturbed sleep or appetite [4]. The proportion of the global population affected by MDD is estimated at 4.4%, equivalent to approximately 322 million people [5]. Research indicates that NSSI behaviors represent a dysfunctional emotion regulation strategy employed by depressed adolescents to manage intense negative emotions and alleviate depressive distress [6]. Furthermore, MDD is a significant risk factor for NSSI among adolescents, with the severity of depressive symptoms highly correlated with NSSI [7]. Therefore, individuals with MDD may experience relief from depression symptoms through NSSI behaviors, which may be one reason for the high degree of co-morbidity between MDD and NSSI behaviors [8–10]. New evidence indicates that NSSI affects 12% to 20% of adolescents worldwide, with prevalence rates reaching as high as 40% among those with depression [11]. Despite the critical need, MDD accompanied by NSSI (nsMDDs) remains understudied. The underlying neurobiological mechanisms are still unclear, and treating this population continues to pose significant challenges.

Previous studies have confirmed that patients with MDD exhibit significant emotional dysregulation (ED) [12]. Studies on outpatient depression patients further indicate that ED serves as an independent and robust predictor of depressive severity [13]. Additionally, a systematic review of community adolescents identified ED as a core predictor of NSSI [14]. Therefore, ED may be considered as a target for preventive interventions for nsMDDs. Empirical research indicates that dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) effectively improves ED, and also has a specific module on ED in DBT group skills training (DBT-ST). Short-term DBT-ST administered to a cross-diagnostic outpatient population significantly alleviated ED and depressive symptoms [15]. Among patients with MDD, DBT interventions integrating emotion regulation and mindfulness skills demonstrated marked improvements in depressive symptoms [16]. For adolescents, DBT significantly reduced NSSI behaviors among those with severe suicidal ideation [17]. A systematic review further provided evidence-based support for its precise efficacy in reducing adolescent NSSI [18]. Although results of the effects of DBT interventions on nsMDDs are scarce at this time, existing evidence indicates DBT holds cross-diagnostic and cross-population clinical value in improving ED, alleviating depression, and reducing NSSI behaviors. Therefore, DBT-ST intervention may be an effective tool for the treatment of nsMDDs.

Despite the relative paucity of research specifically addressing nsMDDs, accumulating neuroimaging evidence consistently points to salience network (SN) dysfunction as a shared neural substrate of both MDD and NSSI [19]. Specifically, aberrant activity and connectivity of the amygdala and insula—core nodes of the SN—have been reported in both MDD [19–21] and NSSI [22]. The SN, comprising the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), insula, and amygdala, plays a critical role in attention, emotional regulation, and the integration and filtering of internal sensory and emotional information [23–24]. Converging evidence has closely linked SN dysfunction to the clinical manifestations of MDD and NSSI, particularly in the domains of emotional regulation and cognitive processing [22, 25]. Compared with healthy controls, individuals with NSSI exhibit significant SN overactivation during emotional and cognitive tasks [26], while patients with MDD show higher baseline SN activity during rest [27]. Notably, effective treatment of MDD has been associated with decreased activation within this network, particularly in the insula [27–28].

Previous neuroimaging studies have shown that DBT can modulate brain function in clinical populations. In patients with borderline personality disorder (BPD), DBT has been shown to reduce hyperactivity in SN-related regions, particularly the insula [29–30]. Specifically, following DBT treatment, patients with BPD exhibited reduced hemodynamic responses in the left insula to negative stimuli [29]. Another study further reported reduced activation in the insula following DBT during a cognitive reappraisal task in this population [30]. Previous research has confirmed that adolescence is a critical period for brain structural remodeling, during which neural plasticity is significantly enhanced [31–33]. Compared with the relatively stable neural architecture of adults, adolescents are in a critical phase of SN reorganization [34], during which connectivity between the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and anterior insula continues to strengthen, while emotion regulation circuits remain in development [35–36]. These developmental characteristics may render adolescents more responsive to external interventions. A prominent question about the effects of DBT is how it affects neurological functioning in patients with nsMDDs, and whether these effects vary with age. While there is evidence that DBT may help to reduce depressive symptoms and NSSI behaviors, the neurological effects of the process on these participants, as well as the potential moderating role of age, remain unknown.

This study aimed to investigate the effects of DBT-ST on clinical symptoms and spontaneous brain activity in individuals with nsMDDs, compared with an active control condition (social support group therapy, SSGT), and to explore whether age moderates these outcomes. This may provide a deeper understanding of the psychotherapeutic mechanisms underlying DBT-ST for nsMDDs. Based on the aforementioned findings, we proposed the following hypotheses: (1) For patients with nsMDDs, both DBT-ST and SSGT would significantly reduce depressive symptoms and NSSI behaviors in the overall sample. DBT-ST would yield greater clinical improvements in adolescents compared to adults, whereas SSGT would show comparable effects across age groups. (2) Compared with SSGT, DBT-ST would be associated with more pronounced neurophysiological changes, particularly in adolescents. Specifically, we predicted that DBT-ST would reduce hyperactivity in regions of the SN, and that these neural changes would be more prominent in adolescents than in adults.

Methods

Participants

We recruited 80 patients with nsMDDs from four hospitals between September 2019 and January 2020: Nanjing Brain Hospital (The Affiliated Brain Hospital of Nanjing Medical University), The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, The Third Affiliated Hospital of Mianyang, and the Tianjin Anding Hospital. Participants were recruited from both outpatient and inpatient. All patients with nsMDDs were diagnosed by experienced senior psychiatrists using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Axis I Disorders (SCID-I). Researchers then assessed whether the remaining criteria for enrollment are met. Eligible participants (and guardians of adolescent subjects) received full disclosure from researchers regarding the study’s purpose, procedures, potential benefits, and risks. Participants (or their guardians) who voluntarily consent to enrollment sign a written informed consent form. Demographic, symptom, and imaging data were collected within one week of enrollment. Imaging data and symptom data collection were repeated within one week of intervention completion, consistent with the pre-intervention assessment timeline. The severity of symptoms was assessed using the Hamilton depression scale (HAMD) [37], Ottawa self-injury inventory (OSI) [38–39] and Columbia-suicide severity rating scale (C-SSRS) [40]. Participants were allowed to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence (Fig. 1). The trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04094623). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Brain Hospital (No. 2019-KY043-01).

The inclusion criteria for the patients were as follows: (1) Meet the diagnostic criteria for MDD in DSM-5; (2) According to the proposed diagnostic criteria for NSSI disorder outlined in Section III (condition requiring further study) of the DSM-5, assessment requires at least five days of deliberate self-harming behavior within the past year (e.g., cutting, burning, stabbing, hitting, excessive rubbing), without suicidal intent; (3) Aged 13 ~ 59 years; (4) The subjects participated voluntarily and signed the informed consent form, and the subjects under the age of 18 needed the consent and signature of the subjects’ guardians; (5) Ability to understand written language and administer questionnaires.

The exclusion criteria for the patients were as follows: (1) Apparent suicidal plans and behaviors within one month; (2) Obvious tendency to attack others; (3) Meets the diagnostic criteria for a manic episode according to DSM-5; (4) Psychotic symptoms (according to DSM-5, specifically refers to the presence of hallucinations, delusions, or thought disorder, as distinct from depressive or anxious moods commonly associated with depression itself); (5) History of substance addiction; (6) Other mental or physical illness requiring hospitalization; (7) MRI scanning contradictions, pregnant women, breastfeeding women, or women planning pregnancy; (8) Other situations where group intervention is not possible.

The termination criteria were as follows: Severe adverse reactions during treatment and the failure to complete expected treatment procedures, such as absence from group psychotherapy for more than 4 times. For patients who withdraw, the therapist needs to assess the risk of suicide and other. If the risk exceeds the confidentiality agreement, the family or guardian should be notified in time, and corresponding measures should be recommended.

Procedure

This study used a multicenter, randomized, assessor-masked, clinically controlled intervention design (see Methods S1 for details). A simple randomization procedure was used to allocate participants to treatment groups. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent statistician using a random number table in SPSS 23. To ensure allocation concealment, the statistician was not involved in participant recruitment or intervention delivery. Both interventions—DBT-ST and SSGT—consisted of 13 weekly sessions, each lasting 120 min. Participants were required not to receive any other individual or group psychotherapy during the study period. DBT-ST followed a standardized treatment protocol, with its origins traceable to the manual developed by Marsha Linehan [41], and incorporated procedural modifications as revised by Soler et al. [42] (see Methods S2 for details). The intervention was structured around four core skill modules: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness (see Methods S3 for details). Each session adhered to a structured format, comprising a brief mindfulness exercise, review of the previous week’s homework, instruction in new skills, and assignment of practice tasks for the upcoming week. In SSGT, therapists acted as facilitators of group interactions rather than instructors of structured skills. This intervention leveraged peer support to establish a therapeutic environment centered on relational experiences (see Methods S4 for details).

To ensure consistency, the same therapist conducted both the DBT-ST and SSGT interventions at each center, thereby eliminating potential therapist effects. These therapists received training in DBT from Dr. Jan Glasenapp of the Institute of German-Chinese Psychotherapy. For quality assurance, therapists were supervised weekly by Dr. Marcus Rodriguez from Duke University, USA, for DBT-ST interventions, and by Dr. Wenjuan Tan from Nanjing University for SSGT interventions. Uniform intervention manuals were used across centers, including: therapist manual, patient manual, and homework manual to improve consistency of the multicenter intervention. Meanwhile, patients in both groups were given psychiatric clinical routine medication by psychiatrists according to the patients’ diagnosis and in accordance with the treatment guidelines.

Rs-fMRI acquisition and preprocessing

Rs-fMRI data was acquired using a 3T scanner (Siemens Medical Systems, Germany). Prior to scanning, participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed, remain motionless, stay awake, and minimize active thinking as much as possible. And rs-fMRI data was acquired using a gradient-echo single-shot echo planar imaging (GRE-SS-EPI) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms; echo time (TE) = 30 ms; field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 mm2; matrix = 64 × 64; flip angle (FA) = 90◦; 36 interleaved axial slices; 240 volumes; slice number = 36; slice thickness = 4 mm; slice gap = 0 mm; acquisition time = 8 min. All available rs-fMRIs were preprocessed using DPARSF. The first 10 volumes of images were discarded in order to allow subjects to adapt to the scanning environment and to make the magnetization signal relatively stable. Preprocessing consisted of the following steps: (1) slice timing: each layer of acquired magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data was calibrated to a single point in time by time correction; (2) spatial alignment; (3) normalization: the images were normalized to standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space and resampled to a resolution of 3 × 3 × 3 mm3; (4) filtering (0.01 to 0.08 Hz): to remove high-frequency physiological noise and low-frequency drift; (5) regression of interfering covariates: including head movement parameters, global mean signal, cerebrospinal fluid signal and white matter signal.

ALFF analysis

Following the previous computational procedure, the preprocessed time series were converted to the frequency domain to estimate the power spectrum for each voxel [43]. The average square root of the power spectra computed in the range 0.01 to 0.08 Hz for each voxel was used as the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF), and for normalization purposes, the ALFF for each voxel was divided by the global average of the ALFF within the grey matter mask.

ReHo analysis

To construct the regional homogeneity (ReHo) brain map, the Kendall coefficient of consistency (KCC) was calculated for the time-series consistency of each voxel with its 26 neighboring voxels [44]. The ReHo brain map for each subject was then calibrated by dividing the KCC of each voxel by the whole-brain average KCC. The calibrated ReHo maps were further smoothed using a 6 mm half-height full-width (FWHM) Gaussian kernel.

FC analysis

To investigate brain network alterations associated with intervention, we performed a two-stage functional connectivity (FC) analysis on all participants across both groups. First, based on group (DBT vs. SSGT) × time (post-intervention vs. pre-intervention) repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), brain regions exhibiting significant interaction effects in ALFF/ReHo analyses (e.g., bilateral insula) were defined as seed points. Second, whole-brain FC maps were computed using the REST toolbox for each subject’s seed points before and after intervention. These connectivity maps were subsequently reanalyzed using group (DBT vs. SSGT) × time (post-intervention vs. pre-intervention) repeated measures ANOVA to identify brain networks exhibiting group differences in connectivity strength changes.

Statistical analysis

This study employed a complete case analysis. The original sample comprised 80 patients with nsMDDs, randomly assigned into two groups (40 participants per group). Following the intervention, 5 participants dropped out from DBT-ST and 6 from SSGT, resulting in 11 participants who did not provide any post-intervention data. Ultimately, 69 participants who completed the intervention were included in the analysis (35 in DBT-ST and 34 in SSGT). The dropout rates for the two groups were 12.5% and 15.0%, respectively, showing no statistically significant difference (χ² = 0.11, p = 0.74) and data missing is respectively random in each group. Under random missing conditions, complete case analysis yields unbiased estimates [45]. Another study also noted that in randomized trials with low overall dropout and balanced attrition between groups, complete case analysis represents a straightforward and reasonable approach, as it is unlikely to introduce substantial bias and preserves simplicity of interpretation [46]. Given these methodological considerations, the use of complete case analysis as a preliminary basis for efficacy comparison is justified.

Among the 80 participants who completed baseline fMRI acquisition, 60 (75.0%) were excluded from pre- and post-intervention comparison analyses due to failure to obtain valid baseline and/or post-intervention fMRI data. Primary reasons for exclusion of imaging data included: withdrawal during the study (11 cases, 13.8%), refusal to undergo a second scan (42 cases, 52.5%), excessive motion (3 cases at baseline and 1 case at post-intervention, totaling 4 cases, 5.0%), and substandard image quality (3 cases at baseline, 3.8%). Ultimately, 20 participants with high-quality baseline and post-intervention fMRI data were included in the pre- and post-treatment imaging comparison analysis.

SPSS 23 was used to compare the demographics and clinical characteristics. After applying the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test for normal distribution, Chi-square test was used to analysis the demographics. Generalized estimating equation (GEE) modelling was used to compare the different changes in clinical symptom outcomes relative to baseline in the intervention groups. GEE modelling was applied to repeated data to analyze the overall treatment effect over time. Where the group × time interaction was significant, simple effects analysis was conducted with Bonferroni correction to control for Type I error in multiple comparisons. A two-sided p-value less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

ALFF maps, ReHo data and FC maps were compared between the DBT-ST and SSGT groups before and after intervention using repeated measures ANOVA in SPM12. The repeated measures ANOVA employed age, gender, and sub-center as covariates, with Gaussian Random Field (GRF) correction performed using the DPABI toolbox (ALFF and ReHo: voxel p < 0.01, cluster p < 0.05; FC: voxel p < 0.05, cluster p < 0.05). The REST toolbox was used to extract signal values before and after treatment for all of the above brain regions that had significant time × group interaction effects. Subsequently, simple effect analyses were conducted in SPSS 23 to evaluate specific changes within different brain regions across the groups. To address statistical assumptions, this study employed a balanced sample size design (n = 10 per group) to ensure the robustness of F-tests against deviations from normality [47]. Concurrently, SPM’s built-in non-spherical correction was activated in the model, directly estimating the covariance structure from the data to control for potential heteroscedasticity and non-sphericity issues. Statistical analyses were performed separately for the three imaging parameters: ALFF, ReHo, and FC. To control for the risk of false positives due to multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied, with the significance threshold set at p < 0.017 (0.05/3).

To further investigate the association between brain activity changes and clinical symptom improvement, a brain-behavior correlation analysis was conducted: first, ALFF, ReHo, and FC values at pre- and post-intervention were extracted for each subject using the REST toolbox from brain regions exhibiting significant group × time interaction effects. Second, the change values for each brain imaging metric were calculated (∆brain = pre-training minus post-training), as well as the change values for each clinical scale score (∆clinical = pre-training minus post-training, where positive values indicate symptom improvement). Finally, Spearman correlation analysis was used to examine the association between ∆brain and ∆clinical, and Fisher’s z test was used to compare their correlation coefficients. All analyses were conducted independently within the DBT-ST and SSGT groups. A two-sided p-value less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess the potential moderating effect of age on treatment outcomes, and considering both sample size feasibility and prior evidence suggesting that treatment response in MDD may vary by age [48–49], participants were divided into an adolescent group (13 ~ 17 years old) and an adult group (18 years and older). For clinical scale data, GEE models consistent with the primary analysis were used within each age subgroup to examine the effects of group (DBT vs. SSGT), time (post-intervention vs. pre-intervention), and their interaction on clinical symptom improvement. Where the group × time interaction was significant, simple effects analysis was conducted with Bonferroni correction to control for Type I error in multiple comparisons. For imaging data, the limited sample size in each age subgroup after quality control precluded reliable voxel-based statistical inference, as direct subgroup comparisons would have resulted in insufficient statistical power and potentially spurious findings. Therefore, rather than dichotomizing age, we included it as a continuous predictor in a moderation model to preserve information and maximize statistical power [50]. Specifically, we extracted signal values from brain regions exhibiting a significant group × time interaction in the ALFF, ReHo and FC analyses, and then constructed general linear models in SPSS to test the age × group interaction. Where the interaction was significant, simple effects analysis was subsequently conducted (with Bonferroni correction) to elucidate its specific pattern. A two-sided p-value less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

In this study, 80 subjects were enrolled across four subcenters, comprising 40 in the DBT-ST group and 40 in the SSGT group. During the study, 11 subjects withdrew due to failure to complete the full intervention process. The final valid sample included in statistical analysis comprised 69 subjects (35 in the DBT-ST group and 34 in the SSGT group). There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of demographic data (Table 1), other comorbidities (see Table S1 for details), or routine medication use (see Table S2 for details).

Brain-behavior correlation analyses

We subsequently examined the correlation between changes in spontaneous brain activity (ALFF, ReHo) in patients with nsMDDs and improvements in clinical symptoms. Brain-behavior correlation analysis revealed distinct association patterns between the two groups. Pearson correlation analysis between ALFF changes in the right insula and HAMD score changes showed that the DBT-ST group exhibited a positive correlation (r = 0.69, p = 0.038, Fig. 2), whereas the SSGT group showed no statistically significant correlation (r = -0.09, p = 0.797). However, the difference in correlation coefficients between the two groups did not reach statistical significance (z = 1.77, p = 0.077) (see Table S3 for details).

Effect of age on intervention outcomes

The results of the age-subgroup analysis of clinical scale data showed that, in the adolescent group, significant time effects were observed for NSSI thoughts (B = -1.57, p = 0.011, d = 0.79) and suicidal behavior (B = -2.93, p < 0.001, d = 1.80). However, neither the main effect of group nor the group × time interaction reached statistical significance for either outcome. For NSSI behavior and HAMD scores, significant time effects (B = -2.07, p = 0.002, d = 0.81; B = -39.35, p < 0.001, d = 0.93) and interaction effects (B = 0.92, p = 0.020, d = 0.97; B = 16.71, p = 0.003, d = 1.12) were observed, while the group main effect showed no statistically significant difference. To elucidate these interactions, simple effects analysis was further conducted. Intergroup comparisons revealed: pre-intervention, no statistically significant differences existed between groups in NSSI behaviors or HAMD scores (B = 0.58, p = 0.068, d = 0.66; B = 4.13, p = 0.401, d = 0.31). Post-intervention, there was no statistically significant difference in NSSI behaviors (B = -0.25, p = 0.413, d = -0.29), while HAMD scores showed significant differences between groups (B = 11.96, p = 0.023, d = -0.78). Intragroup comparisons indicated significant pre-to-post differences in NSSI behaviors (B = 1.15, p < 0.001, d = -1.24) and HAMD scores (B = 22.22, p < 0.001, d = -1.61) for the DBT-ST group, whereas the SSGT group showed no significant changes in either variable (B = 0.29, p = 0.183, d = -0.39; B = 5.94, p = 0.131, d = -0.46). The adult group exhibited significant time effects for NSSI thoughts, suicidal ideation and suicidal behavior (B = -1.26, p = 0.038, d = 0.94; B = -2.21, p = 0.008, d = 0.27; B = -2.73, p = 0.001, d = 1.29), but neither group nor interaction effects were significant (see Table S4 for detail).

To examine whether age moderated the intervention effects on regional brain function, signal values were extracted from insula regions that exhibited significant group × time interactions in the ALFF and ReHo analyses. These values were then entered into general linear models to test the age × group interaction. The results showed that the age × group interaction did not reach statistical significance in either the right insula (F = 0.06, p = 0.811, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\eta\:}_{p}^{2}$$\end{document}= 0.00) or the left insula (F = 1.75, p = 0.204, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\eta\:}_{p}^{2}$$\end{document} = 0.10) These findings suggest that, within the current sample, the intervention effects on insula function did not vary significantly as a function of age.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the few studies to date utilizing rs-fMRI technology to investigate the therapeutic effects of DBT-ST on nsMDDs and the corresponding changes in spontaneous brain activity. Both DBT-ST and SSGT effectively reduced NSSI thoughts, NSSI behaviors, suicidal ideation, suicidal behaviors and depressive symptoms. However, DBT-ST demonstrates superior intervention effects in improving adolescent NSSI behaviors and depressive symptoms. Notably, distinct neuroimaging patterns emerged between the two groups: the DBT-ST group showed decreased ALFF in the right insula and decreased ReHo in the left insula post-treatment, whereas the SSGT group showed increases in both measures. Furthermore, improvement in depressive symptoms within the DBT-ST group showed a significant positive correlation with reduced right insula ALFF. These findings suggest that DBT-ST intervention may be associated with the regulation of activity in brain regions related to the SN, aligning with the preliminary hypothesis of this study.

Our study revealed that the insula, a critical node of the SN, exhibited reduced activity following DBT-ST intervention. Previous research has consistently linked depression to hyperactivation of the SN, with elevated ALFF values observed in the insula of individuals with MDD [27]. Research indicated that compared to healthy controls, nsMDDs exhibit increased fALFF values in the right insula [51]. The SN acts as a hub of brain networks, facilitating dynamic switching between networks, evaluating and processing internal and external stimuli, and playing a critical role in attentional control by integrating and filtering sensory and emotional information [23–24, 52–54]. Previous research has also demonstrated that elevated activity levels in the SN are linked to heightened attention toward negative stimuli, thereby facilitating patients’ focus on threatening cues [55]. This negative attentional bias represents a critical clinical characteristic of MDD and significantly contributes to NSSI [27, 56–57]. Furthermore, effective treatment for depression has been associated with decreased activation in the insula, a key node of the SN [27–28, 58]. This finding is consistent with our results, which indicate that reduced ALFF in the right insula is significantly associated with improvements in depressive symptoms. A meta-analysis of 22 studies on psychotherapy for patients with depression and anxiety found that one of the most robust changes following psychotherapy was a significant reduction in activation in emotion-related brain regions such as the insula and ACC [28]. Further support comes from an attention bias modification (ABM) study in individuals with depression, which reported significant reductions in ALFF in the right anterior insula (AI) following intervention, accompanied by enhanced attention to positive stimuli [27]. Therefore, it is suggested that abnormal activation in the SN may be associated with the pervasive negative attentional bias and deficits in cognitive control observed in patients with nsMDDs [59–60]. The DBT-ST intervention may alleviate depressive symptoms and NSSI behaviors by reducing negative attentional bias through the suppression of SN activity. However, since this study did not employ an ABM paradigm nor directly measure attentional bias, the aforementioned mechanisms remain speculative and require further validation in future research.

This study identified a noteworthy pattern: although the two groups showed comparable clinical improvements in the overall sample, they exhibited divergent neuroimaging changes. This behavioral convergence - neuroimaging divergence phenomenon suggests that neuroimaging may be more sensitive than clinical scales in distinguishing the specific mechanisms of different interventions. A meta-analysis comparing the predictive power of clinical characteristics versus brain imaging features for treatment outcomes in depression revealed that brain imaging studies demonstrated significantly superior sensitivity compared to clinical studies [61]. Another study successfully distinguished the distinct treatment response patterns of sertraline versus placebo in depression using multimodal brain network deep learning, identifying their respective specific neural circuit characteristics [62]. Concurrently, neuroimaging alterations may precede behavioral improvements, serving as their neural basis. A study employing diffusion-weighted imaging to track microstructural changes following Targeted Memory Reactivation revealed that the striatum of healthy volunteers exhibited plasticity alterations within 24 h, while behavioral benefits only became significantly apparent after 20 days [63]. This finding demonstrates the predictive value of imaging changes for subsequent behavioral improvements. The decreased insular activity in the DBT-ST group correlated with symptom improvement, suggesting it may underlie treatment efficacy. Conversely, the increased insular activity in the SSGT group may reflect compensatory activation through alternative pathways. Both groups achieved similar outcomes through different mechanisms, reflecting the brain’s neural redundancy and functional compensation properties [64]. Despite these suggestive patterns, the brain–behavior correlation patterns observed in the two groups did not support treatment-specific effects. Although a significant within-group correlation was observed between ALFF changes and HAMD score changes in the right insula in DBT-ST group, the between-group difference did not reach statistical significance. One possible explanation for these patterns is that DBT-ST and SSGT, despite their differing therapeutic approaches, may engage overlapping emotion regulation circuits—such as the insula—through distinct pathways, consistent with the concept of neural redundancy. Given the limited statistical power due to the small sample size, these findings should be considered preliminary and exploratory, and warrant replication in larger cohorts.

Current research on the moderating effect of age on the efficacy of interventions for NSSI/MDD remains limited. Existing studies suggest that these age-related treatment differences may stem from neurobiological variations. As a core hub of the SN, the insula is involved in interoception and emotional awareness [65] and undergoes significant structural and functional remodeling during adolescence [31, 66]. Previous studies have shown that insula dysfunction during this developmental period is associated with the pathophysiology of depression [67] and impulsivity [31]. Systematic reviews further reveal that, compared to adults, adolescents with depression exhibit more extensive and complex cortical and subcortical connectivity abnormalities, particularly in emotion-regulation regions including the insula [68–69]. DBT has been shown to effectively modulate insula function [29–30] and randomized controlled trials have confirmed its clinical efficacy in reducing depressive symptoms [16] and NSSI behaviors [70] in adolescents. Collectively, these findings suggest that DBT-ST may improve depressive symptoms and NSSI behaviors in adolescents through its modulation of emotion-regulation brain regions, including the insula.

However, in this study, the age effect observed in the clinical scale analysis was not replicated in the imaging analysis. Considering the current state of research in this field, we believe this discrepancy may stem from the following reasons. First, clinical scale data exhibit high reliability and validity with low measurement noise, and the sample sizes for the adolescent subgroups (DBT = 14, SSGT = 16) were sufficient to detect between-group differences of moderate effect size. In contrast, imaging metrics (such as ALFF and ReHo) exhibit greater measurement variability and a lower signal-to-noise ratio [71]; detecting equivalent effect sizes requires larger sample sizes, and the current sample may be insufficient to detect age-related modulatory effects at the brain level. Second, the definition of “adolescents” in the clinical analyses is based on developmental stage (age < 18 years), whereas the imaging analysis employs a continuous variable model to examine linear trends across the entire age range of the sample (13 ~ 59 years). If the age effect of the intervention occurs only within a specific developmental window (e.g., adolescence) rather than as a linear change across all ages, the continuous variable model may fail to capture this nonlinear moderation. A large body of research indicates that adolescence is a critical window for brain structural remodeling. Research has found that the thickness of the anterior insular cortex decreases significantly with age between 10 and 22 years, and this change is closely associated with a reduction in impulsivity [31]. Another study further confirmed that FC between the insula and the frontal lobe and limbic system undergoes significant restructuring from childhood to adulthood [32]. Adolescence has been conceptualized as a “sensitive window” for environmental experiences and interventions, during which neural plasticity is significantly enhanced [33]. Consequently, the neural effects of interventions may only be significant within this window and may be difficult to detect in linear models spanning the entire lifespan.

Despite the potential significance of this study, some limitations should be recognized. First, the relatively small sample size in this study, particularly for the fMRI component, limited statistical power and generalizability. Additionally, the stringent exclusion criteria may have introduced participation bias, limiting sample representativeness. Future research should increase sample size and conduct stage-specific subgroup analyses targeting key developmental windows (e.g., adolescence). Second, detailed records of medication use (e.g., treatment duration, adherence, and dose adjustments) were not collected, precluding analysis of the interaction between pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy. Furthermore, this study did not collect data on the number of depressive episodes experienced by patients, which limits the ability to assess the impact of disease duration on treatment outcomes. Although baseline comorbidities were balanced, their presence could still influence the generalizability of results to specific subgroups (e.g., nsMDDs with anxiety). Future studies should collect more comprehensive clinical information, including medication details and psychiatric history, to support refined subgroup analyses and better control for potential confounders. Third, the use of complete case analysis instead of intention-to-treat analysis may have introduced selection bias. Additionally, the absence of a formal assessment of blinding success constitutes a methodological limitation, potentially leading to implementation bias. Future studies should adopt intention-to-treat principles and incorporate rigorous blinding effectiveness evaluation to mitigate such limitations. Fourth, findings from previous rs-fMRI studies examining the pathological state of nsMDDs remain inconclusive, and the clinical significance of the “behavior–neural dissociation” phenomenon warrants further investigation. Given the inherent limitations of ALFF and ReHo metrics as indirect indicators of neural activity, future research should employ multimodal neuroimaging techniques to further elucidate the relationship between brain functional changes and clinical outcomes in psychotherapy. As an exploratory study, our findings offer preliminary evidence for distinguishing the neural mechanisms underlying psychological interventions while further validation is still required.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that patients with nsMDDs (especially adolescents) exhibited a significant reduction in symptoms following DBT-ST, accompanied by alterations in spontaneous activity within brain regions of the SN. These findings suggest that DBT-ST may alleviate depressive symptoms and NSSI behaviors in nsMDDs patients alongside regulation of spontaneous brain activity patterns. Furthermore, the brain imaging markers identified in this study may serve as potential functional imaging markers for future research into the neural mechanisms underlying this population.
