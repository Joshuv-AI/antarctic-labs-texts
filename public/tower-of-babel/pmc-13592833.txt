Efficacy of Physical Modalities for Hemiplegic Shoulder Pain After Stroke: A Systematic Review
Authors: Stefania Spina, Salvatore Facciorusso, Milena Carmela D’Ascanio, Mirko Filippetti, Marco Battaglia, Alessio Baricich, Alessandro Picelli, Andrea Santamato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Purpose:Hemiplegic shoulder pain (HSP) represents a common and disabling complication following stroke, which has multiple causes and important functional consequences. Physical modalities are frequently used to manage HSP, but evidence on their effectiveness remains inconsistent. Our aim is to systematically review the evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on the efficacy and safety of physical modalities, compared with sham or standard care, for improving pain and function in adults with poststroke HSP.Methods:This systematic review was conducted following the Cochrane Guidelines and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement. We included RCTs assessing physical modalities versus sham or standard therapy in adult stroke survivors with HSP. The primary outcome was pain reduction; secondary outcomes included functional mobility, quality of life, and adverse events. A comprehensive search was conducted in MEDLINE, EMBASE, CENTRAL, PEDro, CINAHL, Web of Science, ClinicalTrials.gov, and World Health Organisation International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO ICTRP) up to July 30, 2025. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool 2.Results:Twelve RCTs involving 567 participants were included. Interventions comprised electrotherapy (n = 6), shock wave therapy (n = 3), and laser therapy (n = 3). Most studies showed significant pain reduction compared with control. Electrotherapy provided sustained analgesia; extracorporeal shock wave therapy improved structural abnormalities and pain; laser therapy enhanced both pain and functional outcomes. Adverse events were mostly mild. However, safety conclusions are limited by incomplete and inconsistent adverse event reporting. Heterogeneity in interventions and outcomes precluded meta-analysis.Discussion and Conclusions:Based on low to very low certainty evidence, physical modalities may offer benefit for HSP, but uncertainty remains regarding both long-term effectiveness and safety. Integration into multimodal rehabilitation protocols may be considered.

INTRODUCTION

Hemiplegic shoulder pain (HSP) represents a prevalent and significantly disabling sequela following stroke, with an incidence ranging from 30% to 65% within the first 6 months.1,2 This wide variability is attributed to differences in the operational definitions and heterogeneity in study populations.3 The pathophysiology of HSP is multifactorial, involving musculoskeletal abnormalities, altered nociceptive processing, and spasticity-related dysfunctions,4,5 which complicates diagnosis and treatment.6

HSP significantly impacts upper extremity motor function and proprioception,7 reduces functional use of the upper limb, impairs participation in rehabilitation, and worsens quality of life.8 Early, multidisciplinary approaches mitigate HSP’s detrimental effects and improve functional recovery.9

Multiple rehabilitation strategies have been proposed, including preventative measures,10 manual therapy and mobilization,11,12 pharmacological interventions,13 nerve blocks,14,15 botulinum toxin,16–18 dry needling,19 robotic-assisted therapy,20 and neuromodulation.21,22 Among these, therapeutic physical modalities are commonly used because they deliver physical agents within rehabilitation settings and have the potential to reduce pain, improve motor function, and enhance rehabilitation outcomes.23 However, evidence regarding their efficacy in treating HSP remains inconclusive.

Although several reviews on HSP treatment exist, they have either addressed all HSP treatment together24,25 or focused on invasive approaches14 or examined a single physical modality.26–28 As a result, the evidence base for physical modalities remains fragmented.

This systematic review synthesized the evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on the efficacy and safety of physical modalities, compared with sham or standard care, for improving pain, function, and quality of life in adults with HSP.

METHODS

This systematic review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and adheres to the recommendations outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.29 The review protocol was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD42024605455.

Selection Criteria

Eligible studies were RCTs including crossover designs conducted in rehabilitation settings and published in English. We excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, non-randomized and observational studies, and abstracts without full text.

Eligibility followed the PICOTS framework. P (Population): adults (≥18 years) with HSP following ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke. I (Intervention): physical modalities using physical agents, such as electrotherapy (ES), vibration, ultrasound, shock wave, or laser therapy. Modalities with an ablative effect (eg radiofrequency), taping, and nonmodalities physical therapies (eg exercise-only) were excluded. C (Comparison): sham/placebo intervention or a standard/usual care control group (eg conventional physical therapy without the experimental modality); O (Outcomes): pain reduction as the primary endpoint assessed via validated scales such as visual analog scale (VAS) or numeric rating scale (NRS)30; secondary outcomes included shoulder mobility (eg Shoulder Pain and Disability Index—[SPADI], goniometry31,32), quality of life (eg SF-36, EQ-5D33,34), and adverse effects; T (Timeframe): short- (4 weeks after randomization), medium- (>4 weeks to 6 months), and long-term (>6 months) follow-up. S (Setting): inpatient and outpatient rehabilitation.

Search Strategy

We searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, CENTRAL, Web of Science, CINAHL, PEDro, and trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov, WHOICTRP) to August 4, 2024, updated July 30, 2025 (Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, available at: https://links.lww.com/JNPT/A580). Search strategy combined terms for “stroke,” “shoulder pain,” and “physical modalities.” We also screened reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews to uncover any further studies that met the inclusion criteria.

Study Selection

Three independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of all identified records using the Rayyan platform.35 Full-text articles were retrieved for studies that satisfied the inclusion criteria or when their eligibility remained uncertain. Two reviewers independently assessed the full-text articles for final inclusion. Conflicts between reviewers were addressed through discussion or with a third reviewer.

Data Extraction

Two independent reviewers carried out data extraction from the selected studies using the Rayyan platform. The extracted information encompassed study characteristics, such as year of publication, author names, article title, journal of publication, country of origin, and source of funding. Additionally, details regarding participant characteristics were collected, including the specific groups under study, average participant age, sex distribution, stroke type, and time elapsed since stroke onset. Intervention details were recorded for both the experimental intervention (description of the physical modality used, duration, frequency) and the control intervention (description, duration, frequency).

Outcomes were documented, including types of outcomes assessed, measurement tools, and time points of assessment (baseline, postintervention, follow-up). Adverse events were defined as any untoward medical occurrence temporally associated with the intervention, irrespective of causality, and a serious adverse event as one leading to death, being life-threatening, requiring or prolonging hospitalization, or causing persistent disability.

In cases where data were missing or incomplete, attempts were made to contact the original study authors to obtain the necessary additional information; no additional contact was undertaken specifically for adverse-event data. Because adverse events were monitored and reported heterogeneously across studies, we summarized harms descriptively and did not perform a quantitative meta-analysis. Data on the type and frequency of any reported adverse events were extracted as described in the primary studies.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Each included study underwent a risk of bias (RoB) evaluation by 2 independent reviewers using the Cochrane RoB tool (RoB 2).36 The domains evaluate bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was involved to reach a final decision.

Data Synthesis and Analysis

No substantive deviations from the registered protocol occurred; meta-analysis was planned but was not feasible because of clinical heterogeneity; therefore, a narrative synthesis was performed. The heterogeneity arose from differences in the types of physical modalities used, variations in intervention protocols (eg duration, frequency, and intensity), diverse patient populations (eg time since stroke onset, severity of shoulder pain), and variability in outcome measures and assessment tools. The overall certainty of the body of evidence for the primary outcome of pain reduction and key secondary outcomes was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. The final certainty of evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low. Judgments were made by 2 independent reviewers, with disagreements resolved by a third. Adverse events related to the interventions were a prespecified secondary outcome. In line with Cochrane guidance for synthesizing harms data, studies that did not mention adverse events were coded as “not reported,” and no assumption of zero events was made. Clinical importance was assessed using minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds:32,37 ≥1.4 cm for the VAS,37 ≥2.17 points for the NRS,38 and ≥8 points for SPADI based on validation studies in primary care and rehabilitation settings.39

RESULTS

An initial search across multiple databases and registries yielded 448 records. After removing 112 duplicates, 336 records remained for screening, of which 288 were excluded based on title and abstract. Subsequently, 48 reports were assessed for eligibility, resulting in the exclusion of 36 studies that did not satisfy the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 12 studies met all requirements for the final analysis (Figure 1).

Characteristics of Studies

A total of 12 RCTs, involving 567 participants, were included in this review. All included RCTs used a parallel-group design; no crossover trials were identified among included studies. These studies were published between 2005 and 2025. Population

Among the participants, 277 were male and 274 were female (data missing for 6 patients). All patients experienced moderate to severe pain defined as a VAS score ≥31 mm, and an NRS score ≥4, according to the WHO severity classification system.52 Age ranged from 50.8 years in Khalifa et al45 study to 67.13 years in Suriya-Amarit et al42 The time since stroke onset varied significantly, from 73.61 days50 to 135 weeks,42 encompassing both subacute and chronic stroke survivors. Sample sizes also differed across studies, ranging from 25 participants in Wilson et al’s37 study to 81 in Zhou et al.’s study.43

Interventions

Among the 12 studies included, 7 investigated electrotherapy,40,42,45–47,49,50 three evaluated shock wave therapy,43,48,51 and 3 focused on laser therapy.41,44,49 One of these studies included 3 groups and assessed both electrotherapy and laser therapy.49 Notably, 7 of the 12 studies incorporated physiotherapy in combination with the instrumental therapy under investigation. Accordingly, in most included trials, the modality was delivered as an adjunct to conventional physiotherapy. There was a wide variability in treatment designs across studies, with the number of sessions ranging from 4 to 42 and durations spanning from 3 to 8 weeks; only 1 study utilized a single treatment session.45

Comparisons

In 6 studies, the control group received conventional physiotherapy,40,41,44,46,50 while in 3 studies, the control group received sham modality combined with physiotherapy.43,45,48 In Kim et al48 the control group underwent sham modality as a standalone intervention. In Badaru and Aminu44 the control group received physiotherapy, including active and passive range of motion (ROM) exercises, plus soft tissue massage (STM). Finally, in Chae et al,39 the control group received a tutor placement as part of the rehabilitation process.

Outcomes

Pain outcomes were evaluated using a variety of tools across the studies. The VAS was the most commonly employed measure, appearing in 6 studies.41,43,47,49–51 The Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) was utilized in 2 studies40,42 to provide a detailed assessment of both pain intensity and its interference with daily activities. Similarly, the SPADI, which integrates pain and functional disability, was applied in 1 study.48 The NRS was used in 2 studies45,46 to measure pain severity and pain-free ROM, while the Multidimensional Pain Questionnaire offered a comprehensive analysis of pain dimensions in another study.44 Beyond pain, the studies assessed several key functional and disability-related outcomes using various tools. Additionally, structural integrity was assessed in some studies using imaging modalities like ultrasound. We extracted adverse-event data as reported in the original trials and did not create new composite categories not prespecified in the primary studies.

Risk of bias

The RoB analysis showed differences in the methodological quality among the studies included in the review. Figures 2 and 3 report the overall judgment. The main concerns were related to allocation concealment (D1) and blinding of participants and personnel (D2). Critically, a high RoB in the measurement of the outcome (D4) was identified across all 12 included studies, as outcome assessors were likely aware of group allocation.

Electrotherapy

Seven studies investigated various electrotherapy modalities, including percutaneous intramuscular electrotherapy, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS), neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES), functional electrical stimulation (FES), and interferential current (IFC), for the management of HSP. The studies compare these electrotherapy interventions to conventional physiotherapy, placebo, or other nonsurgical rehabilitation treatments.

Wilson et al37 (n = 25) found that PNS, administered for 6 hours daily over 3 weeks, resulted in significantly greater pain reduction than control, with effects sustained at 6- and 12-week post-treatment. At the 12-week follow-up, the mean pain interference score (BPI-SF9) was 1.1 (SD 0.7) in the PNS group versus 3.5 (SD 0.8) in the usual care group. Similarly, the physical component summary score of the SF-36v2 improved in both groups, but the between-group difference was not significant. For secondary outcomes, both the PNS and usual care groups showed within-group nonsignificant improvements in daily activity interference and quality of life.

Chae et al39 (n = 61) demonstrated that percutaneous intramuscular electrotherapy (6 hours/day for 6 weeks) had a significantly higher success rate in pain reduction compared with cuff-type sling, with pain relief maintained for up to 12 months. The mean reduction in pain (BPI-12) at 12 months was −5.00 points in the ES group versus −2.31 points in the control group (P < 0.001). However, secondary outcomes such as subluxation, ROM, and spasticity did not show significant improvements.

Zhou et al43 (n = 81) directly compared the efficacy of NMES, TENS, and a control group, with all groups receiving conventional physical therapy. At the 4-week primary endpoint, NMES demonstrated the greatest reduction in pain on the NRS, with a mean change of −2.03 points. This was significantly greater than the reduction seen with TENS (−1.44 points, P = 0.043) and the control group (−0.61 points, P < 0.001). The analgesic effect of NMES was maintained at the 8-week follow-up, whereas the effect of TENS diminished. Similar to NMES, TENS did not significantly improve shoulder mobility, upper limb function, spasticity, daily activity ability, or quality of life relative to conventional physiotherapy.

Badaru and Aminu44 (n = 50) compared TENS with STM for managing HSP in stroke patients. While both interventions significantly reduced pain from baseline (P < 0.05), a direct comparison at the end of the 8-week treatment period showed that TENS was more effective. The TENS group reported a significantly lower mean VAS score of 1.48 (SD 0.51) compared with 2.12 (SD 1.17) for the STM group, representing a mean difference of −0.64 (P < 0.05).

Koyuncu et al47 (n = 50) investigated the efficacy of FES targeting the deltoid and supraspinatus muscles compared with conventional therapy for shoulder subluxation and pain in hemiplegic patients. While both the FES and control groups experienced some pain reduction over the 4-week treatment period, the between-group comparison of the change in VAS scores showed no statistically significant difference for pain at rest, during passive motion, or during active motion (P > 0.05). The FES group exhibited significant reductions in shoulder subluxation (P < 0.001).

Suriya-Amarit et al42 (n = 30) assessed the immediate effects of IFC against placebo on pain and passive ROM in HSP patients. The IFC group experienced a significantly greater immediate reduction in pain during the most painful movement, with a mean change on the NRS of −2.73 points (95% confidence interval [CI]: −3.22 to −2.25) compared to −0.80 points (95% CI: −1.28 to −0.32) in the placebo group. IFC also demonstrated significantly greater improvements in pain-free passive ROM in all directions compared with the sham group.

Finally, a recent 3-arm RCT by Başaran et al46 (n = 75),compared both low-level laser therapy (LLLT) and NMES against a control group receiving only conventional physical therapy. The median post-treatment VAS score was 2.0 in the NMES group compared with 4.0 in the control group (P < 0.005). Notably, this study was one of the few to also demonstrate a significant functional benefit for NMES, with the SPADI score improving significantly more than in the control group (P < 0.005). Overall, 5 out of 7 trials (71%) reported within-group improvements for the experimental group that met the MCID for pain. The added value of electrotherapy for HSP remains potentially relevant but not consistently established across all protocols.

Shock Wave Therapy

Three studies evaluating the efficacy of radial extracorporeal shock wave therapy (rESWT) and combined radial and focused ESWT (fESWT) for HSP. The studies compare these interventions to sham treatment or physical therapy alone.

Kim et al48 (n = 34) compared rESWT with sham rESWT for HSP. They reported that 8 sessions of rESWT resulted in a mean VAS score reduction of −1.61 points at 4 weeks, significantly greater than the −0.33-point reduction in the sham group (P = 0.016).

Khalifa et al45 (n = 30) found a significantly greater reduction in pain and disability in the ESWT group compared with the control group. (post-treatment SPADI pain score 53.47 vs. 84.00; P < 0.001) and also demonstrated a significant improvement in structural abnormalities on ultrasound, a unique finding among the included studies.

Haghighat et al40 (n = 43) investigated the combination of rESWT and fESWT versus sham modality for poststroke HSP (5.75 vs. 8.00; P < 0.001). The intervention group showed significant improvements in pain scores at 4 and 8 weeks and increased passive ROM in abduction and forward flexion compared with the sham group. However, no significant differences were observed between groups in functional outcomes, such as Fugl-Meyer upper extremity assessment or SPADI. For shock wave therapy, all 3 included trials reported within-group improvements for the experimental arm that met MCID thresholds for pain and disability. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the “very low” GRADE certainty.

Laser Therapy

The 3 studies included compare laser therapy combined with therapeutic exercises to therapeutic exercises alone.

Abdelhakiem et al41 (n = 44) evaluated high-intensity laser therapy (HILT) combined with therapeutic exercises versus therapeutic exercises alone. They found a large and significant improvement in pain on the McGill Pain Questionnaire, with a mean change of −33.45 points (95% CI: −35.95 to −30.95) in the HILT group versus −2.86 points in the control group (P < 0.001). This study also reported significantly greater improvements in shoulder function (University of California-Los Angeles score) and handgrip strength in the HILT group.

Korkmaz et al38 (n = 44) also compared HILT combined with therapeutic exercises to therapeutic exercises alone for HSP with partial-thickness rotator cuff tear. They reported a mean VAS change of −3.5 points in the HILT group compared with just −0.73 points in the control group (P < 0.001). The HILT group also showed significantly greater improvements on all secondary measures, including the SPADI, functional independence measure, and Nottingham health profile.

In the previously mentioned RCT by Başaran et al46 (n = 75), the LLLT group demonstrated a statistically significant and clinically meaningful reduction in pain compared with the control group receiving physical therapy alone. The median post-treatment VAS score was 2.0 for the LLLT group versus 4.0 for the control group (P < 0.005). This study also reported a significant improvement in functional outcomes, with the LLLT group showing greater improvement on the SPADI and Barthel Index compared with the control group (all P < 0.005). For laser therapy, the 3 trials consistently reported changes exceeding the MCID for pain reduction. Despite this consistent clinical magnitude across the 3 trials, the small sample sizes and “very low” certainty of evidence limit the strength of recommendations for these modalities in routine HSP management.

Adverse Events

Adverse events were explicitly reported in 5 of the 12 included RCTs (approximately 42%). Wilson et al37 reported retained electrode fragments (21.4%), dislodgement of the electrode 7.8% and subsequent reimplantation, pruritus at the electrode or bandage site (46.2%), and pain after implantation (15.4%) resolved without intervention. Chae et al39 noted granuloma or pain at the electrode exit site in 3.9% of cases and electrode tip breakage during removal. Kim et al48 observed mild skin reactions, including petechiae at the treatment site in 3 patients, which resolved spontaneously, and a bulla in 1 patient. Two studies45,46 reported no adverse events. Detailed frequencies are presented in Table 1. In the remaining 7 studies (58%), adverse events were not mentioned. These studies were therefore classified as “not reported/unclear” with respect to harms, rather than assumed to have zero events. Given the small number of events and the heterogeneity of adverse-event definitions and ascertainment, adverse events were summarized descriptively and not pooled statistically.

GRADE Assessment

According to the GRADE assessment, the certainty of the evidence for pain and functional outcomes with electrotherapy, shock wave therapy, and laser therapy was rated as very low, mainly because of high RoB, inconsistency across trials, and imprecision related to small samples and wide CIs. For adverse events, the certainty was rated as low, reflecting the high RoB and the substantial uncertainty due to incomplete and heterogeneous harms reporting. DISCUSSION

The purpose of this systematic review was to evaluate the evidence on the efficacy and safety of physical modalities for managing HSP poststroke. We synthesized data from 12 RCTs, encompassing 567 participants, to determine if these interventions, when compared with sham or standard care, improve pain, function, and quality of life.

The studies included in this review investigated 3 distinct therapeutic physical modalities: electrotherapy, shock wave therapy, and laser therapy. Electrotherapy was administered using various techniques, including percutaneous intramuscular stimulation, FES, TENS, and IFC therapy. Shock wave therapy encompassed both fESWT and rESWT, and laser therapy was delivered as low-level laser therapy. The considerable differences in both methodology and clinical characteristics among the included studies prevented the completion of a planned meta-analysis. Our synthesis of evidence suggests that physical modalities are a promising adjunct for managing HSP, though the overall certainty of this evidence is low to very low. Intramuscular ES achieved significant and sustained pain relief compared with controls, with benefits lasting up to 1 year.42 FES outperformed conventional physiotherapy in reducing shoulder subluxation, although pain outcomes were inconsistent.50 IFC and TENS provided greater immediate pain relief than placebo or STM, with TENS proving more effective for pain management.45,47 The analgesic effect of TENS was also confirmed in an independent RCT, which demonstrated a significant reduction in HSP compared with control after 4 weeks of treatment.46 However, when directly compared with NMES, TENS was less effective, as NMES produced a significantly greater reduction in pain intensity and maintained this effect during follow-up. Both NMES and TENS, despite their analgesic efficacy, did not result in superior outcomes compared with control in terms of shoulder ROM, upper limb motor function, spasticity, or quality of life.46 Shock wave therapy may reduce pain in HSP, particularly when compared with sham procedures; however, its advantage over conventional physiotherapy is smaller and less consistent, so its added value over active rehabilitation remains uncertain.43,48,51 Finally, laser therapy consistently delivered superior results, improving pain, shoulder function, and disability compared with therapeutic exercises alone.41,44 Our findings complement previous syntheses in stroke survivors. Dyer et al25 reported beneficial but often imprecise effects for electrotherapy, while de Sire et al.24 prioritized invasive techniques over physical agents. This review advances knowledge by integrating 4 recent RCTs; unlike prior inconclusive reports, our analysis provides clearer evidence for the clinically meaningful impact of shockwave and laser therapy in the subacute phase. These findings are consistent with the multifactorial pathophysiology of HSP, which involves musculoskeletal alterations, peripheral and central nervous system dysfunction, and inflammatory processes. Physical modalities address these underlying mechanisms, increasing blood flow, which helps to alleviate pain related to hypoxia and inflammation,53,54 reducing peripheral and central sensitization, thereby diminishing hyperalgesia and lowering pain perception55–57 and reducing spasticity and involuntary muscle contractions.58–61 By simultaneously addressing these critical pathophysiological components, physical modalities contribute to an improved quality of life in patients affected by HSP.

The association between the presence or severity of subluxation and the intensity of pain is controversial, and often weak,62 and interventions could have a significant effect on pain without concurrently improving subluxation.42 Consistent with prior evidence that FES improves subluxation with variable analgesic effects,26 pain relief may occur even without measurable changes in alignment, supporting a multifactorial pain model.63

Among the 5 trials that reported adverse events, events were infrequent and mostly mild.40,51 Because adverse events were often not prespecified, not consistently monitored, and frequently not reported, the apparent absence of serious harms should not be interpreted as evidence that these modalities are definitively safe. While the reported clinical outcomes and mechanistic rationale are promising, interpretation is limited by the small number of studies, heterogeneity of interventions, and methodological weaknesses. RoB assessments revealed methodological inconsistencies and highlighted the need for greater methodological rigor and improved reporting standards in future trials.

In addition to methodological concerns, the limited assessment of long-term outcomes further weakens the robustness of current evidence. Most included studies focused predominantly on short-term effects, without adequately exploring whether the therapeutic benefits were maintained over time. Only 5 out of 12 studies incorporated follow-up assessments with durations ranging from 1 week to 1 year. Overall, the evidence indicates that physical modalities can maintain analgesic benefits beyond the immediate treatment phase, and follow-up often revealed differences between intervention and control groups that became more evident as time progressed.40,42,43,46,50,51

Limitations

This review has limitations. We included English-language RCTs only, which may have excluded relevant evidence. Substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity (modalities, dosing, populations, and outcome measures) precluded meta-analysis and, together with small sample sizes and high RoB in several domains, limits confidence in effect estimates. Harms reporting was incomplete, and adverse-event incidence may be underestimated.

CONCLUSIONS

Based on evidence of low to very low certainty, physical modalities appear to offer a benefit in managing HSP. However, variability in treatment protocols, follow-up durations, and reported outcomes limits confidence in these findings and highlights the need for more robust and standardized research.
