Optical properties, surface roughness, and bacterial adhesion of feldspathic CAD/CAM ceramic material after immersion in staining media with different pH
Authors: Mohammed M. Al Moaleem, Arwa Daghrery, Nassreen Albar, Abdullah Ahmed Meshni, Thrya S. Gadah, Khalid Ahmed Bajawi, Ibrahim Madini, Ismail Ali Alagem, Mohmed Isaqali Karobari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to evaluate the effects of acidic and alkaline staining solutions on the optical-mechanical properties of feldspathic (Vitablocs Mark II) Computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) ceramic materials after 4 weeks of immersion in media. Specifically, this investigation focused on assessing mean colour change (ΔE*), the translucency parameter (TP00), the opalescence parameter (OPBW), surface roughness, and bacterial adhesion by colony-forming unit (CFU) count. A total of 96 Vitablocs Mark II CAD/CAM ceramic specimens were prepared and assigned to eight groups (A–H) according to the type of immersion solution: smokeless snuff, Black Shammah, Yerba mate, Saudi Coffee, MIRINDA Citrus, CodeRed, Nescafe, and normal saline (control group). The specimens were immersed for 4 weeks at 37 °C, with solutions replaced according to manufacturer instructions. Optical parameters, including ΔE*, TP00, and OPBW, were measured using a spectrophotometer (VITA Easyshade V). Surface roughness was analysed through white light interferometric microscopy, and the bacterial adhesion of Streptococcus mutans was quantified by calculating colony-forming units (CFUs) per square millimetre. Statistical analysis included Shapiro–Wilk tests for normality, one-way ANOVA, Tukey HSD post-hoc comparisons, and paired t-tests, with significance set at p < 0.05. The recorded pH values of the staining solutions ranged from acidic (MIRINDA Citrus: 3.25) to alkaline (Black Shammah: 9.45). Black Shammah exhibited the highest ΔE* value (6.75 ± 5.21), followed by smokeless snuff (6.53 ± 3.59). The lowest value (2.41 ± 1.04) was observed for Yerba mate, and significant differences were observed across staining solutions (p < 0.001). TP00 significantly decreased post-immersion, and the highest mean reduction was observed in CodeRed (5.87 ± 0.92; p < 0.001). By contrast, OPBW increased significantly, with the most significant mean increase observed in Black Shammah (6.93 ± 5.38; p = 0.001) and the smallest in Saudi coffee (1.19 ± 1.77; p = 0.040). Surface roughness varied among groups, ranging from 0.458 ± 0.04 (CodeRed) to 0.739 ± 0.03 µm (smokeless snuff). Bacterial adhesion was the highest in CodeRed (480.08 ± 40.77 CFUs) and lowest in normal saline (273.00 ± 42.29 CFUs; ANOVA, p < 0.001). Exposure to staining media significantly decreased translucency and increased opalescence, surface roughness, and bacterial adhesion in feldspathic CAD/CAM ceramic. Acidic solutions promoted greater Streptococcus mutans adhesion, while alkaline tobacco products caused the highest color change.

Introduction

Feldspathic CAD/CAM ceramic restorations are subjected to a range of optical, mechanical, chemical, and bacterial challenges in the oral cavity1,2. Acidic or alkaline beverages exacerbate these issues by altering the ceramics’ optical properties1–3. Pigments from such drinks infiltrate a material’s porous structure, causing discolouration, while chemical agents erode the surface, increasing roughness4. This textured surface fosters bacterial adhesion, particularly by pathogens, such as Streptococcus mutans and Lactobacillus, leading to biofilm accumulation5. Over time, plaque formation, secondary caries, and interfacial degradation from bacterial byproducts and mechanical strain collectively accelerate material fatigue, reducing a restoration’s longevity6.

The oral environment subjects ceramics to dynamic pH shifts, ranging from acidic (pH ~ 2.0) during the consumption of citrus beverages to alkaline under specific conditions7. These fluctuations, combined with enzymatic activity and thermal variations, promote the chemical dissolution of a ceramic’s glassy matrix, weakening its structural integrity8. Ceramic stability depends on various factors, such as composition, manufacturing methods, environmental pH, exposure duration, and temperature9,10. Erosive agents leach alkaline ions from materials, reducing flexural strength and increasing susceptibility to cracks11. Degraded surfaces exhibit increased roughness, which promotes plaque retention, accelerates opposing tooth wear, diminishes strength, and compromises aesthetics, ultimately shortening clinical service life12.

Two primary formulas are used to calculate mean colour difference (ΔE): CIELAB (ΔELab) and CIEDE2000 (ΔE*). Although both are applicable in dental material studies, recent evidence suggests that CIEDE2000 offers greater accuracy13. For the CIEDE2000 system, studies have established perceptibility and acceptability thresholds of 0.81 and 1.77, respectively14–16.

Achieving aesthetic, colour-stable ceramic dental restorations require managing optical properties, such as translucency and opalescence17,18. Translucency, quantified by the translucency parameter (TP00), reflects a material’s ability to transmit light17. TP00 is calculated by measuring the colour difference of a ceramic sample against black and white backgrounds. High TP00 values correspond to increased light transmission, which is vital for achieving natural-looking dental prosthetics18. OPBW is another essential optical characteristic resulting from the scattering of short wavelengths, producing a distinctive visual effect characterised by a bluish hue in reflected light and an amber-brown tint in transmitted light19. The OPBW quantifies this phenomenon by evaluating discrepancies in colour coordinates between reflected and transmitted light spectra20. This parameter is typically assessed using standardised black-and-white backgrounds to ensure consistency21.

Social habits (e.g., smokeless tobacco, Yerba mate and coffee consumption) and surface treatments considerably influence the optical-mechanical properties of aesthetic ceramics22–24. Substantial colour changes have been observed in CAD/CAM ceramics exposed to tobacco products, with ΔE* values exceeding the clinically acceptable threshold. These changes potentially compromise aesthetic outcomes for patients25.

The surface smoothness and quality of prosthetic materials are essential for aesthetic outcomes and long-term clinical performance24. Rough surfaces contribute to discolouration, compromised shade matching, and enhanced adhesion of Streptococcus mutans, thereby elevating the risk of secondary caries or periodontal inflammation26. These adverse effects become particularly pronounced when surface roughness exceeds 0.2 µm25. Glazing significantly reduces surface roughness, enhancing the clinical performance and longevity of restorations27,28. Numerous studies have consistently demonstrated that glazed ceramic surfaces exhibit greater colour stability, minimal changes in surface topography, and reduced bacterial colonisation compared with unglazed or inadequately polished surfaces22,29.

The effects of various acidic beverages on the optical and mechanical properties of ceramic restorations have been explored in several studies22,24,29,30. However, few studies have comprehensively assessed optical properties (e.g., ΔE*, TP00, and OPBW), mechanical surface alterations, and bacterial adhesion (measured as colony-forming units [CFUs]) in a single study involving immersion in staining solutions at different pH levels.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the ΔE*, TP00, OPBW, surface roughness, and Streptococcus mutans adhesion (measured as colony-forming units, CFUs) of a glazed feldspathic CAD/CAM ceramic material (Vitablocs Mark II) following 4 weeks of immersion in various staining media: smokeless snuff, Black Shammah, yerba mate, Saudi Coffee, MIRINDA Citrus, CodeRed, Nescafe, and normal saline as a control. The null hypotheses were that immersion in media with varying pH levels would not result in statistically significant differences in: (1) the materials’ optical properties (ΔE*, TP00, and OPBW); (2) the surface roughness; and (3) the number of Streptococcus mutans CFUs.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample size calculation

We assessed the effects of 4 weeks of immersion in different staining solutions with varying pH levels on the optical properties (ΔE*, TP00, OPBW), surface topography, and bacterial adhesion (CFU) of feldspathic CAD/CAM ceramics. The number of rectangular specimens was calculated using G*Power (version 3.1.9.4, Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf). The effect size (d) was 0.5, α was 0.05, and 1−β (power) was 0.85 based on preliminary data from a similar ceramic immersion study31. The required sample size was 96 samples, divided equally across 8 staining media groups, to detect a large effect size (0.40) with a 1−β (power) of 0.80.

Specimen’s manufacturing and distribution

The 96 rectangular specimens were prepared from feldspathic Vitablocs Mark II CAD/CAM ceramic restorative materials (VITA Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany). All specimens were milled using the same milling machine (Amann Girrbach, Germany), with each group having a new bur to minimise variability in initial surface roughness. Glazing was performed uniformly to all samples according to the manufacturer’s instructions using VITA Akzent Glaze (VITA Zahnfabrik, Germany). A single layer of glaze was applied with a brush and fired in a ceramic furnace (Programat P310, Ivoclar Vivadent) according to the manufacturer’s recommended firing cycle.

Each rectangular specimen measured 10 mm × 10 ± 0.25 mm in diameter and 2.0 ± 0.25 mm in thickness. The specimens were then randomly divided into 8 groups (n = 12 per group). Each group was immersed in one of the following solutions: smokeless snuff, Black Shammah, Yerba mate, Saudi Coffee, MIRINDA Citrus, CodeRed, Nescafe, and normal saline (control group). Before baseline color measurements, the glazed specimens were cleaned in an ultrasonic bath of isopropyl alcohol for 5 min.

pH measurement

To determine the pH of each staining material before the time of preparation, samples were placed in experimental glass test tubes compatible with the pH-measuring device (EC Multi-parameter Benchtop Lab pH Meter; LAB1ST, USA). A pH probe was inserted into each sample, and the pH value was recorded from the digital display32–34. For each material, two measurements were obtained at the beginning of the immersion period, and the average was recorded as the final pH value. pH was not measured during the immersion period, as solutions were freshly prepared at each change interval. The groups were designated A–H.

Optical properties measurements

Mean colour changes (ΔE*)

One trained operator measured the colour parameters under standardised conditions with a spectrophotometer device (Vita Easyshade Compact version V; Vita Zahnfabrik H. Rauter GmbH & Co. KG, Bad Säckingen, Germany) with a 4 mm-diameter tip. Colour differences were calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula, which incorporates a rotational factor (RT) accounting for the interaction between chroma and hue differences, particularly in the blue region13. The coefficient K, also known as the parametric factor, represents the influence of specific observational conditions on the perception and evaluation of colour differences. The S coefficient accounts for the efficiency of visual homogeneity. RT is the rotational factor addressing deficiencies in the blue-violet region; L′, C′, and H′ are the transformed forms of LCH; and KL, SL, KC, SC, and KH are the weighting functions for lightness, chroma, and hue, respectively. In the present study, the parametric factors of the ΔE* difference formula were set to 1.

The optical handpiece of the spectrophotometer was positioned at a 90° angle to the surface of each rectangular specimen, and all measurements were conducted in the absence of incandescent lighting. A probe tip was inserted into the device’s calibration port aperture after each measurement to calibrate the device according to the manufacturer’s recommendations20,23,24. The ΔE* was measured under a white background.

Translucency parameter

TP00 was determined by calculating the colour difference in the CIE L*a*b* colour space for each glazed feldspathic CAD/CAM sample, measured against black and white backgrounds. Opalescence parameter (OPBW)

OPBW is the difference in chroma between the reflected and transmitted colours. The colour parameters (ΔE*, TP00, OPBW) were recorded before staining in different media, which were considered the base readings. They were repeated after the 4-week immersion period, and ΔE*, TP00, and OPBW were calculated separately using the aforementioned equations. All measurements were repeated three times, and the average was recorded in the Excel sheet for statistical analysis.

Immersion and staining of glazed feldspathic samples

All glazed feldspathic rectangular specimens were immersed in and stained with smokeless snuff and Black Shammah, according to previously published literature22,25. Each stain material was mixed with distilled water in a standardized ratio (10 g powder to 5 mL water) to achieve a thick consistency. The samples were immersed in the prepared mixture for 10 min, which corresponds to the typical duration of oral exposure during the actual use of smokeless snuff and Black Shammah. To simulate intraoral pressure generated during use, a constant weight was applied to each specimen to ensure complete and uniform immersion. The boluses of Black Shammah were replaced two times daily to replicate the actual daily usage23,34–36. The smokeless snuff was supplied with a covering bed and was formulated to dissolve in saliva upon placement in the buccal sulcus. The rectangular specimens were immersed between the snuff and its cover. A constant load was applied to the rectangular specimens immersed in Black Shammah, and the snuff was kept constant. Both staining materials were replaced every 12 h in accordance with simulated actual usage30.

Yerba mate tea, Saudi Coffee, and Nescafe staining materials were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, that is, 15 g of a staining material (powdered or packaged) was used for 250 mL of boiled distilled to achieve the desired consistency. All staining powders and solutions were prepared fresh every 12 h to maintain homogeneity23,24. For MIRINDA Citrus, CodeRed, and normal saline groups, the solutions were used in their ready-to-use commercial forms. An adequate volume (50 mL per specimen) was poured into separate containers to completely immerse the specimens. These solutions were replaced every 12 h23.

All specimens in their respective solutions were incubated at 37 °C for 4 weeks. During the staining period, all samples were cleaned twice daily (at each solution change) using a manual toothbrush (Oral-B Soft Manual Toothbrush, Charcoal; Procter & Gamble) and toothpaste (Colgate Triple Action Toothpaste; Colgate-Palmolive) for 20 s with a persistent load of approximately 100 g applied manually in a circular motion by a single operator [M. M. Al]37. After the immersion period, specimens were removed, rinsed in distilled water 10 times, wiped with tissue paper, and air-dried before assessment.

Surface roughness and topography measurements

White-light interferometric microscopy (Contour GT-K1, Bruker Nano GmbH, Berlin, Germany) was used to analyze the surface topography of the feldspathic CAD/CAM ceramics after the 4-week staining period. For quantitative analysis, all specimens were scanned at three specific points, and the average surface roughness (Ra) was calculated in µm. As baseline surface roughness was not recorded prior to immersion, the post-immersion roughness values are presented descriptively to illustrate the final surface state achieved after exposure to the different staining media. Surface roughness measurements were performed in accordance with the ISO 11,562 standards30,38.

Surface morphology analysis through scanning electron microscopy

One glazed feldspathic CAD/CAM specimen from each group (n = 8) was immersed in stained materials for 4 weeks and examined by SEM. The eight specimens were gold sputter-coated with Quorum Q-150R (East Sussex, BN8 6BN, United Kingdom). Then, the specimens were mounted on the specimen stage of a JEOL JSM-6610 LV scanning electron microscope (Akishima, Tokyo, Japan). Feldspathic specimens were scanned, and images were captured at 20 kV and 5000 × magnification.

Bacterial adhesion of Streptococcus mutans analysis

For the bacterial adhesion assay, Streptococcus mutans (ATCC 25,175) was used. The bacteria were cultured in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth (Oxoid, UK) at 37°C in a 10% CO₂ environment for 24 h. The culture was then centrifuged, and the pellet was washed and resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to obtain a standardised suspension of 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL, corresponding to a 0.5 McFarland standard. The ceramic specimens were placed in sterile 24-well plates, and 1 mL of the standardised bacterial suspension was added to each well. No artificial or human saliva was used in this adhesion phase to isolate the direct effect of the stained surface on bacterial attachment. The plates were incubated at 37 °C in 10% CO₂ for 24 h to allow for biofilm formation.

After incubation, specimens were gently washed with PBS to remove non-adherent bacteria. The adherent bacteria were detached by vortexing in fresh PBS, and serial dilutions were plated on BHI agar. Colonies were counted after 48 h of incubation, and the results were expressed as CFUs per square millimeter (CFUs/mm2)39–41.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 26 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for data analysis. Most of the data were normally distributed, as determined by the Shapiro–Wilk test (p value ≥ 0.05). Descriptive statistics, including mean and standard deviation, were calculated for all variables. One-way ANOVA was used to evaluate differences among staining solutions in ΔE* values and Streptococcus mutans CFUs. A Tukey HSD post hoc test was applied for pairwise comparisons to identify differences between each pair of staining solutions for these parameters. A paired t-test was used to assess changes in TP00 and OPBW after immersion. As baseline surface roughness was not measured, the roughness data were analyzed descriptively, and no inferential statistical tests were applied to these values. A p value of < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance for all inferential tests.

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of immersion in various alkaline and acidic solutions on the optical properties (ΔE*, TP00, OPBW), mechanical properties (surface alterations), and bacterial adhesion (measured in CFU) of Vitablocs Mark II CAD feldspathic ceramic. However, previous studies have reported high clinical success rates for metal–ceramic restorations in the oral environment42. In this study, the immersion time was 4 weeks, corresponding to 24 months of intraoral or clinical service10, to assess the effects of various alkaline and acidic solutions.

The first null hypothesis, that no significant differences in optical properties would occur among staining solutions, was rejected. All alkaline and acidic solutions produced colour changes on Vitablocs Mark II ceramic exceeding the CIEDE2000 perceptibility (0.81) and acceptability (1.77) thresholds13,14,16. Black Shammah (6.75) and smokeless snuff (6.53) exhibited the highest ΔE*values, consistent with previous reports on tobacco products’ staining potential22,23,25. These changes likely result from interactions between the ceramic matrix and chemicals in smokeless tobacco, including nicotine, heavy metals, and preservatives that may infiltrate the microstructure and induce pigmentation43,44.

Interestingly, Yerba mate (2.41) showed lower ΔE* values than the control group (2.88), suggesting possible protective effects or reduced staining affinity. This finding contrasts with previous reports on coffee-based beverages18 and may relate to the specific phenolic compound profile of Yerba mate, which could interact differently with the glazed ceramic surface. Further investigation into the chemical composition of staining solutions and their specific interactions with ceramic materials is warranted.

The colour changes observed for acidic beverages (CodeRed: 3.93, MIRINDA Citrus: 3.36) remained above acceptability thresholds but were lower than tobacco products. This aligns with research by Prajapati et al.44, who found that carbonated acidic beverages caused notable colour changes in Vita VMK Master, IPS e.max, and zirconia, though material-specific interactions play critical roles in outcomes.

The masking and aesthetic qualities of dental materials may be correlated with their translucency and opalescence, which play crucial roles in determining the optical performance of ceramic restorations17,18,45. In the present study, all tested solutions decreased TP00 and increased OPBW following immersion, consistent with previous studies examining pH effects on Vitablocs Mark II23,29,30. CodeRed showed the most significant TP00 reduction (5.87), while Black Shammah produced the greatest OPBW increase (6.93). The glazing protocol likely contributed to the observed TP00 values, as glazed layers can minimize extrinsic stain adhesion and maintain optical properties16,19. These findings align with Alzahrani et al.31, who reported that glazed Mark II exhibited lower TP00 and OPBW variations than polished surfaces after immersion. Also, Daghrery et al.23 found that CAD-CAM materials experienced translucency shifts and staining from colourants, such as mixed fruit juice, which notably reduced OPBW.

However, contrasting results have been reported, and some studies demonstrated elevated TP00 and OPBW values for Vita Mark II after acid exposure and 2 weeks of immersion in gastric acid46 or khat extract29,30. The thick specimens (2.0 ± 0.25 mm) and the glazing protocol used here likely contributed to their lower TP00 values compared with those of thin, unglazed samples in other studies. Applying a glazed layer to ceramic surfaces can minimise the adhesion of extrinsic stains and improve shade longevity22.

Maintaining a microscopically smooth surface is critical for achieving colour stability in dental materials, as surface roughness can promote stain retention24. The second null hypothesis, that no significant differences in surface roughness would occur, was also rejected. All staining agents produced roughness values exceeding the clinically acceptable threshold of 0.2 µm47,48, While baseline roughness was not measured, the final surface roughness for all groups, including the control immersed in normal saline (0.494 µm), suggests that even the glazed surface, when subjected to a 4-week immersion protocol with regular toothbrushing, can exceed this critical threshold. The high values observed for smokeless snuff (0.739 µm) are likely due to the deposition of inorganic particulate matter from the tobacco product itself onto the ceramic surface43, rather than just erosive degradation.

The increased roughness observed in acidic groups (MIRINDA Citrus: 0.568 µm, CodeRed: 0.458 µm) supports the known effects of acidic environments on ceramic materials, where chemical etching can exacerbate surface degradation and create microporosities19,49. SEM analysis confirmed these findings, with acidic media showing homogeneous surface degradation and irregular topography. Moaleem et al. documented a lower value of 1.26 µm for glazed Vitablocs Mark II following khat exposure30. In similar, Duymus et al. reported surface roughness of (4.46 ± 2.9 µm) for Noritake porcelain exposed to lemonade34. The variation in roughness values compared to previous studies may reflect differences in polishing protocols, sample preparation, or measurement techniques (profilometry vs. interferometry).

It is important to note that while the post-immersion surface roughness values for all staining agents exceeded the clinically acceptable threshold, these findings are presented descriptively, as baseline roughness was not recorded in this study. However, the post-immersion values provide a clinically relevant picture of the final surface texture that would be present on a restoration after a period of use. This descriptive comparison with the 0.2 µm threshold remains valid for assessing the clinical acceptability of the final surface. The variability in post-immersion roughness observed across groups, such as the higher values for smokeless snuff, may reflects the combined effect of the initial surface finish and the subsequent interaction with the staining media. This methodological consideration should be kept in mind when comparing these absolute values with studies that measured and statistically analyzed changes from a known baseline30,35.

Acidic solutions promoted significantly higher bacterial adhesion (CodeRed: 480.08 CFU/mm2, MIRINDA Citrus: 449.83 CFU/mm2) compared to alkaline solutions and the control, leading to the rejection of the third part of the null hypothesis. This disparity may be attributed to the high sugar content of acidic beverages, which promotes biofilm development by supplying substrates for cariogenic bacteria43. Furthermore, the acidic nature of these beverages can cause chemical etching of ceramic surfaces, increasing surface roughness50. Such structural changes create favourable conditions for microbial colonization. The higher CFU values in this study compared to Tarrosh41, who reported low CFU after 2 weeks of coffee immersion likely reflect the longer immersion period (4 weeks) and different ceramic materials tested.

The observed trend suggested association between acidic pH and increased bacterial adhesion which aligns with Kreve and Dos Reis48, who systematically reviewed ceramic surface properties and bacterial adhesion, concluding that rougher surfaces enhance microbial retention. Notably, Black Shammah (alkaline, pH 9.45) showed low bacterial adhesion comparable to the control, despite causing high colour change and moderate surface roughness. This suggests that factors beyond surface roughness—such as antimicrobial components in tobacco products or alkaline pH effects on bacterial viability—may influence adhesion outcomes49.

Strength of the study

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively evaluate the optical-mechanical properties and bacterial adhesion of feldspathic CAD/CAM ceramic materials after immersion in staining solutions with varying pH levels. Although some findings align with previous research, this study provides novel insights into how pH variations in commonly consumed substances affect ceramic restorations. These results offer clinically relevant guidelines for material selection, particularly for patients with habits, such as tobacco use or frequent consumption of acidic beverages, facilitating the optimisation of aesthetic outcomes and long-term restoration performance.

Clinical implications

These findings provide clinically relevant guidance for material selection and patient education. For patients with smokeless tobacco habits, feldspathic ceramics may exhibit unacceptable colour changes, necessitating consideration of alternative materials or more frequent replacement. Patients consuming acidic beverages should be counselled regarding increased bacterial adhesion risk and the importance of meticulous oral hygiene. The glazed surface, while providing some protection, does not completely prevent staining or bacterial colonisation, supporting recommendations for regular professional maintenance.

Limitations of the study

The current laboratory study has inherent limitations. Despite rigorous methodologies, it fails to fully replicate the dynamic oral environment, including salivary flow, temperature variations, and mechanical stresses from chewing, all of which can influence material degradation and bacterial adhesion. The 4-week immersion protocol provides only short-term insights and does not capture long-term clinical outcomes. Focusing exclusively on Streptococcus mutans adhesion overlooks the complexities of oral microbiomes and polymicrobial interactions. Baseline surface roughness after milling and glazing was not recorded, and variability in milling burs and glazing application may have influenced the final roughness values, which should be considered when interpreting results in the context of other studies. Testing only glazed feldspathic ceramics excludes other restorative materials, such as zirconia. Fixed-immersion protocols and sample sizes may limit generalizability to actual dietary habits and staining exposures. Future research should incorporate in vivo simulations, longer timelines, investigations of various materials and surface treatments, and patient-centred assessments.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, it can be concluded that the optical integrity and surface characteristics of glazed feldspathic CAD/CAM ceramics are susceptible to modification by commonly consumed staining media. Alkaline smokeless tobacco products were identified as potent colorants, producing the highest ΔE* values. Conversely, acidic beverages like CodeRed and MIRINDA Citrus were associated with the highest levels of Streptococcus mutans adhesion, highlighting a potential increased caries risk in patients with high consumption of such drinks. The final surface roughness of all specimens exceeded the clinically recommended threshold, underscoring the challenges in maintaining a smooth restoration surface over time.
