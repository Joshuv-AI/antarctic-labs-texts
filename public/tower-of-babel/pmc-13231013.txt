Production of dehydrated cabbage on an industrial scale: A comparative evaluation of quality, nutritional, and chemical components
Authors: Chengcheng Zhang, Lihao Jiang, Jiali Li, Daqun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drying is widely used in industrial cabbage processing. Here, different drying methods were applied for the production of dehydrated cabbage on industrial scale. The microwave-assisted hot air drying (MW + HAD) treatment produced superior quality, as reflected in improved color, hardness, and rehydration raThese authors have contributed equally to this work.tio, and also showed higher retention of chlorophyll A, free amino acids, total phenol content, and antioxidant capacity compared to individual hot air drying (HAD) or microwave drying (MD). Our findings revealed 98 volatile compounds across all samples, with aldehydes the most predominant in dehydrated cabbage. Notably, MW + HAD increased both diversity (93 vs. 71 compounds) and content (1336.01 vs. 719.08 μg/100 g) of volatile flavor compounds compared with conventional HAD. Non-targeted metabolomics identified 42,028 metabolite ions, with greater release and generation of cinnamic acids and derivatives, triterpenoids, and alkaloids in thermal drying treatments (HAD, MD, and MW + HAD) compared with freeze drying. Overall, MW + HAD provides clear benefits for maintaining cabbage quality and its nutritional and volatile constituents, supporting its use as an effective industrial drying method.

Introduction

Cabbage (Brassica oleraceae var. capitata L.) is extensively grown and eaten worldwide. It contains substantial amounts of protein (15.4%), dietary fiber (8%), vitamin B (0.6 mg/100 g), vitamin C (36.6 mg/100 g), and minerals such as calcium, phosphorus, iron, and potassium (Luka et al., 2023). In addition, this vegetable is also highly valued for its rich content of phytochemicals, including polyphenolics (caffeic acid, p-cumaric acid, gallic acid, epicatechin, and kaempherol) (Cvetković et al., 2019), glucosinolates (glucoraphanin, glucobrassicin, and glucoiberin) (Zayed et al., 2023), and carotenoids (Nawaz et al., 2018). Cabbage is a perishable vegetable. To slow spoilage, improve shelf stability, drying has become a widely adopted approach in industrial cabbage processing. Furthermore, drying effectively reduces the volume and weight of cabbage, thereby facilitating more efficient storage, packaging, and transportation (Luka et al., 2024). A comprehensive understanding of the drying process is crucial for preserving the nutritional quality of cabbage, such as vitamin C and phytochemicals, and for enabling the application of dried cabbage in a variety of food products. Actually, the food industry commonly uses dehydrated cabbage as an ingredient for supplementation and product fortification. Dried cabbage, for example, is a primary ingredient in the vegetable packets of instant noodles and similar convenience foods (Li et al., 2022), and cabbage powder is commonly utilized for cookies, bread, and other baked goods (Luka et al., 2024). Various drying processes can markedly influence the quality characteristics of dehydrated cabbage, particularly color, texture, flavor, and nutrient retention. Therefore, exploring appropriate drying techniques for cabbage is crucial to preserving its quality.

Current mechanical drying options for agricultural products include hot air drying (HAD), freeze-drying (FD), infrared drying (IRD), and microwave drying (MD) (Asrate & Ali, 2025). Although conventional HAD is widely implemented and straightforward to run, it often results in a loss in quality, requires longer drying times, and consumes more energy because the product is exposed to elevated temperatures and oxygen for extended periods (An et al., 2025). Within this framework, alternative approaches such as IRD and MD have become increasingly prominent because they reduce drying time and improve end-product quality versus conventional HAD (Soares De Mendonça et al., 2023). In particular, MD has attracted growing interest because it enables fast drying, uses energy efficiently, and provides inherent sterilization. Nevertheless, using MD alone often leads to uneven moisture distribution because of non-uniform electromagnetic field exposure and rapid surface evaporation, resulting in non-uniform drying and edge hardening (Pei et al., 2025). To address the limitations of single MD, microwave-assisted hot air drying (MW + HAD) technologies have been engineered for various fruits, vegetables, and crops (An et al., 2022; An et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). Microwave-assisted hot air drying of Gastrodia elata reduces heating duration and energy usage by 53.3% and 59.0%, respectively, compared with single HAD (Pei et al., 2025). Moreover, Yang et al. (2025) confirmed that MW + HAD can significantly improve the texture characteristics and antioxidant capacity of taro rounds. Although MW + HAD has been studied for various agricultural products, most research has been conducted at the laboratory scale.

MW + HAD integrates rapid drying performance with more uniform energy distribution, leading to improved product quality and positioning it as a promising approach for industrial-scale drying of agricultural products. Cabbage drying is commercially important and has attracted growing academic interest in laboratory-scale research (Luka et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2020). However, there are no reports dedicated explicitly to cabbage drying under MW + HAD conditions on an industrial scale. Moreover, the impact of MW + HAD on the overall nutritional value and chemical profile of cabbage has not yet been reported. Therefore, the present study systematically compares HAD, MD, and MW + HAD with respect to product quality, nutritional composition, antioxidant properties, and volatile and non-volatile profiles of industrially produced dehydrated cabbage. The findings of our study support the development of appropriate industrial drying strategies for dehydrated cabbage.

Materials and methods

Materials

White cabbages (Brassica oleraceae var. capitata L.) (Ningbo Xiangqin Nong Food Co., LTD.) were grown on local farms in Ningbo, Zhejiang Province, China. The cabbages were harvested from the same batch in May 2025, transported to a cold-storage facility, and held at 5 °C until processing. Information regarding the additional reagents used is available in the Supplementary Materials.

Hot water blanching pretreatment

The cabbages were washed, cut into slices (approximately 3 × 4 cm), and moved along conveyor systems under wet conditions. Defective samples were manually removed prior to blanching. Next, the cabbage slices were transferred into a sodium bicarbonate solution maintained in a water bath (pH 8.0–8.5, and temperature 90–95 °C) to reduce the initial enzymatic activity. After remaining in the water bath for 2 min, the cabbages were immediately transferred to cooling water and centrifuged (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Systematic diagram of the industrial-scale production of dehydrated cabbage.Fig. 1

Systematic diagram of the industrial-scale production of dehydrated cabbage.

Drying process

Hot air drying (HAD)

Hot air drying was conducted in an industrial dryer (Zhejiang Caiweiju Agricultural Development Co., Ltd., Lishui, China). Approximately 50 kg of the pretreated cabbage was oven-dried until the moisture content dropped to a final value of 7.38% (65–75 °C; Table S1).

Microwave drying (MD)

MD was conducted in a conveyor-belt microwave dryer (Zhejiang Caiweiju Agricultural Development Co., Ltd., Lishui, China). Pretreated cabbage samples were spread in a single layer (approximately 1.5 cm thick) on the belt of the MD system. The belt conveyed the samples through the heating chamber for drying, and the material exited after 6 min. This process was then repeated until the final moisture content reached 8.14% (Table S1).

Microwave-assisted hot air drying (MW-HAD)

For microwave-assisted hot air drying (MW + HAD), following a procedure analogous to the MD process described above, pretreated cabbage samples were microwave-dried to approximately 50% moisture content (w.b.). The samples were then hot air dried in an oven until the moisture content decreased to the safe storage moisture content (8.18%; Table S1).

Freeze-drying (FD)

Freeze-drying was utilized as the control drying method for cabbage. Before drying, the samples were frozen at −80 °C for 12 h and then quickly transferred to a freeze-drier (SCIENTZ-18 N; Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., LTD, Ningbo, China) at a vacuum pressure of 0.47 mbar for 48 h.

Sampling

The dehydrated cabbage samples were randomly taken in triplicate from different drying treatments. After sampling, one portion of the samples was used for moisture, color, texture, and rehydration capacity analyses immediately, while the other portion was ground and sieved through a 40-mesh sieve, then stored at −80 °C until subsequent analysis.

Analysis

Moisture content

The moisture content was determined using the oven-drying technique (National Standard of the People's Republic of China GB5009.3–2016).

Color and texture analysis

Cabbage color was measured with a CR-400 Lab Mini colorimeter (Konica Minolta Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). L⁎, a⁎, and b⁎ values were recorded. These were used to calculate the total color difference (ΔE) with the following equation:ΔE=L∗−L∗02+a∗−a∗02+b∗−b2where L⁎, a⁎, and b⁎ represent the color values of dried cabbage, whereas L0⁎, a0⁎, and b0⁎ indicate those of fresh cabbage.

Texture was evaluated using a TA.XTplus Texture Analyzer (Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Godalming, UK) (2-mm diameter SMS P/2 punch probe) (Zhang et al., 2018).

Rehydration capacity

The rehydration capacity was determined by immersing a weighed sample of dehydrated cabbage in water at 40 °C for 30 min. Following rehydration, surface moisture was gently removed, and samples reweighed. The rehydration ratio was computed as:

Rehydration capacity = Mr /Md.

where Md is the mass of the dehydrated cabbage, and Mr is the mass after rehydration.

Quantification of chlorophyll and free amino acids

Chlorophyll content was determined using a UV–Vis method in accordance with NY/T 3082–2017. Briefly, 0.20 g of cabbage powder was mixed thoroughly with 4.0 mL of absolute ethanol (95%) and protected from light for 10 min to solubilize pigments. The mixture was then centrifuged at 3000 g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant recovered. Optical density was measured at 665 and 649 nm, and concentrations of total chlorophyll as well as chlorophyll a and b were obtained based on these readings. (Xu et al., 2020).

Free amino acids (FAAs) concentrations in cabbage were analyzed using an Agilent 1260 HPLC system (Agilent, USA). Following ultrasonic extraction with 0.02 mol/L HCl, FAAs in the extract were derivatized with sulfosalicylic acid, trimethylamine, and phenyl isothiocyanate. Subsequently, the mixture was filtered and subjected to chromatographic separation on a C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, Shiseido). The mobile phase A consists of 0.1 mol/L sodium acetate solution and acetonitrile in a 93:7 (v/v) ratio, while mobile phase B is 80% (v/v) acetonitrile in water. The elution conditions were followed as previously described (Zhang et al., 2021).

Determination of total flavonoids and total phenolic levels

Dried cabbage powder (1 g) from each drying treatment was retrieved ultrasonically with 10 mL of 70% (v/v) ethanol (40 °C for 30 min). This was then used to quantify total flavonoid content (TFC) and total phenolic content (TPC) using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method and the Folin-Ciocalteu method, respectively, as described previously (Zhang et al., 2022).

Antioxidant activity

Antioxidant capacity was assessed using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) radical scavenging assays, and the ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay. Antioxidant values are reported as Trolox equivalents on a dry-weight basis (μmol TE/g DW) (Zhang et al., 2022).

Analysis of volatile compounds

Volatile compounds in cabbages were profiled using headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) for those produced under the various drying treatments (Li et al., 2024). Dried cabbage powder (1.0 g) was placed in a 20-mL headspace vial and equilibrated at 60 °C for 20 min. An aged SPME fiber was placed in the vial for 50 min to adsorb volatile compounds from the headspace. The SPME fiber was inserted into the GC inlet and thermally desorbed at 250 °C for 5 min before analysis. Volatile compounds were then separated (HP-5MS capillary column; 30 m × 0.25 mm inner diameter, 0.25 μm film thickness; Agilent) (Li et al., 2024). Mass spectrometric detection was conducted across an m/z range of 35–550 u. The resulting mass spectra were compared against the NIST 14.0 standard library for compound identification. Only volatile compounds with a matching degree greater than 80 were considered reliably identified and retained.

Analysis of non-volatile compounds

Cabbage samples were weighed into 2-mL tubes, and methanol (600 μL) was added. After vortexing for 30 s, steel beads were added, and the sample was ground (55 Hz, 60 s). The extract was sonicated at room temperature for 15 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was filtered (0.22 μm) before LC–MS analysis. Non-volatile metabolite profiles were collected (UHPLC–Q Exactive HF-X system; Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, USA) (Zhao et al., 2025). Metabolites were detected on a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with an ESI source. Compounds were tentatively assigned by searching the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB). MetaboAnalyst 5.0 was used to assess significantly changed metabolites for pathway enrichment.

Statistical analysis

Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) (n = 3). One-way ANOVA was used to compare statistical differences, followed by Duncan's multiple range test (SPSS Statistics, version 25.0). P < 0.05 was taken as significant. Multivariate analyses were performed using SIMCA 14.0 and the OmicStudio platform (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool).

Results and discussion

Surface color and chlorophyll content

Surface color is an important factor in assessing the sensory characteristics and overall quality of dehydrated agricultural products. The appearance and color parameters of dehydrated cabbages subjected to different drying methods are presented in Fig. 2. The surface color of the FD sample was bright green, with no significant shrinkage observed. Compared with FD, the HAD and MD samples showed greater yellowing and browning, and their surfaces gradually shrank and deformed. Especially in MD samples, the color distribution was non-uniform, and surface burning occurred locally. MD often results in adverse effects, including surface scorching, due to uneven electromagnetic field distribution and rapid surface moisture loss (Koné et al., 2013). The extent of browning and the collapse of the sample structure were lower in the MW + HAD sample than in either its individual HAD or MD. This effect may be attributed to shorter microwave exposure and more uniform heat distribution under MW + HAD conditions (Dehghannya & Habibi-Ghods, 2025).Fig. 2Surface color, chlorophyll content, and rehydration ratio of dehydrated cabbage under different drying treatments on an industrial scale. The dehydrated cabbage samples were treated with various drying processes, and their L*, a*, b*, and ΔE values are shown in Fig. 2B. In general, higher L* values together with lower ΔE indicate better color performance in dried plant-based foods (Soares De Mendonça et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2019). Except for FD samples, dried cabbage samples appeared darker than fresh cabbage, consistent with their lower L* values. In particular, the MD sample had the lowest L value (67.81) and was notably darker than the other samples. This color shift may result from browning reactions during thermal drying, including the Maillard reaction, oxidation of phenolic compounds, and caramelization (Tepe & Tepe, 2025). Furthermore, all samples exhibited negative a* values, with the FD sample being the greenest, indicating the least chlorophyll degradation. HAD and MD also produced comparatively high b* values, suggesting greater yellowness in samples from these treatments. As a result, ΔE ranked as MD > HAD > FD > MW + HAD across the four drying methods. The MD sample differed most from fresh cabbage, showing the highest ΔE. Conversely, the ∆E value for MW + HAD was the lowest, indicating that MW + HAD exhibits superior performance in color deterioration compared to its individual HAD or MD method. Similar trends have been observed in other agricultural products, where the MW + HAD sample had a more favorable surface color than individual HAD or MD, such as Gastrodia elata (Pei et al., 2025), potato (Dehghannya & Habibi-Ghods, 2025), and corn (An et al., 2024).

Chlorophyll content in dehydrated cabbages processed under different drying conditions was analyzed and compared (Fig. 2C). Notably, chlorophyll content decreased after undergoing different drying conditions. The fresh sample exhibited the highest chlorophyll B and total chlorophyll content (103.45 and 161.37 mg/100 g, respectively), whereas the FD sample exhibited the highest chlorophyll A content (68.48 mg/100 g). Compared with FD, the chlorophyll content of the cabbage dried by HAD, MD, and MW + HAD was lower. Prolonged thermal drying treatment resulted in the oxidative decomposition of chlorophyll (Xu et al., 2020). The MD sample demonstrated the poorest chlorophyll A retention, resulting in the most pronounced color deterioration and the highest b* and lowest a* values. By contrast, MW + HAD retained significantly more chlorophyll a than HAD or MD alone (Fig. 2C; p < 0.05), possibly because the shorter drying duration limited chlorophyll degradation (Bhat et al., 2022). As a result, MW + HAD excelled in preserving cabbage color quality.

Texture and rehydration ratio

Texture is a key feature for determining the quality of dried vegetable products and is commonly characterized by hardness, chewiness, and rehydration ratio (Soares De Mendonça et al., 2023). The drying method significantly affected cabbage texture characteristics (Table 1). HAD produced the highest cabbage hardness (17.43 ± 1.50 N), which was significantly greater than that of the other drying methods (p < 0.05). During HAD, moisture migrates outward, leading to the leaching of cytoplasmic material at the surface. High thermal exposure reduces tissue elasticity and increases the production of a rigid surface layer after drying (Liao et al., 2025). Relative to HAD, hardness was significantly lower for MD- and MW + HAD-dried cabbage, measuring 3.61 ± 2.43 N and 2.21 ± 0.54 N, respectively. Similarly, the chewiness of MD and MW + HAD was considerably less versus HAD (p < 0.05). These findings are congruent with those published by Xu et al. Rehydration capacity is another crucial characteristic for assessing the quality of dried samples. The rehydration of dehydrated cabbage is affected by the drying method (Fig. 2D and Fig. S1). Samples dried by FD exhibited the highest rehydration ratio versus thermally dried samples (p < 0.05), possibly due to the porous microstructure of the FD sample, which increases its water absorption capacity (Yan et al., 2025). Conversely, HAD generated the lowest rehydration capacity. The hard surface shell formed by HAD not only reduced the number of pores but also impaired the openness of the structure, thereby decreasing the rehydration ratio. The MW + HAD treatment showed a numerically higher rehydration capacity than HAD or MD alone; however, no significant differences were detected among MW + HAD, HAD and MD (p > 0.05).

Free amino acid content

Cabbage is a good source of FAAs. FAAs exert a vital impact on the nutritional value and flavor of dehydrated cabbage. In this study, 12 FAAs were identified in cabbage, comprising two umami-related amino acids (Glu and Asp), five sweet-tasting amino acids (Ser, Thr, Ala, Pro, and Lys), and five bitter-tasting amino acids (His, Arg, Met, Val, and Tyr), consistent with prior findings (Park et al., 2014). The total FAAs in fresh cabbage were 3314.4 mg/kg DW, with Glu, Asp, Ser, and Thr as the predominant FAAs. All dehydrated cabbage samples showed a higher proportion of umami amino acids and a lower proportion of bitter amino acids compared with fresh samples (Fig. S2). This phenomenon indicates the prominent, characteristic umami taste, or palatable flavor of the dehydrated cabbage samples. However, the total FAA content decreased in all dehydrated cabbage samples compared to the fresh samples (Fig. 3). This alteration can be partially ascribed to the hot-water blanching pretreatment prior to drying, which led to the leaching of amino acids. In industrial processing, blanching pretreatment is a critical step before drying because it inactivates quality-related enzymes in plants, including polyphenol oxidase and peroxidase. FAA levels were the most elevated in FD cabbage, followed by MW + HAD, HAD, and MD (Fig. 3). The total FAA content for MW + HAD cabbage (920.5 mg/kg DW) was slightly higher than that of individual MD (862.2 mg/kg DW) or HAD (879.1 mg/kg DW). A similar substantial loss of FAA was reported by Yan et al. (2025) in MD- and HAD-treated Porphyra yezoensis, which may have been Maillard reaction-related. Moreover, FAAs are precursors for many volatile compounds; under oxygen exposure, heat, or enzymatic activity, they can promote Maillard reactions and Strecker degradation, thereby generating characteristic flavor compounds (Bi et al., 2024). Here, extended heating promoted the decomposition of FAAs in HAD and MD. MW + HAD rapidly eliminated most of the moisture from the material using microwaves, thereby shortening overall processing time and reducing the cabbage's exposure to high temperatures. This process may mitigate the Maillard reaction and thermal damage, leading to superior FAAs retention compared to individual MD or HAD.

TPC, TFC, and antioxidant capacity

Cabbage contains phenolics and flavonoids that contribute to its antioxidant bioactivity (Llorent-Martínez et al., 2025; Zayed et al., 2023). However, drying typically resulted in substantial decreases in TFC, TPC, and antioxidant activity of vegetables (Xu et al., 2020). Different drying methods substantially influenced the TFC, TPC, and antioxidant capacity in dehydrated cabbages (Fig. 4). The TFC of fresh cabbage was 14.43 mg RT/g DW and decreased significantly after all drying treatments (p < 0.05). Furthermore, FD samples had considerably greater TFC than those produced using heat drying procedures. However, there were no significant differences among the three thermal methods for TFC (HAD, MD, and MW + HAD; p > 0.05). As flavonoids are thermally unstable, thermal drying can induce structural degradation and lead to flavonoid loss (Hu et al., 2023). In contrast to TFC, TPC exhibited a different trend. The TPC order from highest to lowest was: UD (undried cabbage) > MW + HAD > HAD > FD > MD. Compared with the FD sample, cabbages dried with MW + HAD and HAD exhibited a notably higher TPC. The shorter drying time of MW + HAD may reduce thermal load and limit polyphenol degradation. Moreover, certain microwave radiation energy disrupts cellular structures, which may promote fiber-bound polyphenol release from the cell matrix (Kubra & Jagan Mohan Rao, 2012). DPPH, FRAP, and ABTS assays were performed to investigate the impact of various drying techniques on cabbage antioxidant capacity. As shown in Fig. 4, cabbage exhibited vigorous scavenging activity against both DPPH and ABTS radicals. Phytochemical studies have shown that phenolic acids and flavonoids in plants are crucial constituents responsible for their antioxidant activity and for the treatment of ROS-driven oxidative stress (Kurt-Celep et al., 2024; Yagi et al., 2025). Consistently, the antioxidant activity of dehydrated cabbage is directly associated with TPC and TFC, all drying treatments showed a similar decreasing trend in antioxidant levels. The antioxidant ability of dehydrated cabbage, based on the drying method, was ordered as UD > FD > MW + HAD > HAD > MD. These findings suggest that MW + HAD is an attractive strategy for retaining antioxidant capacity in cabbage.

Volatile compounds

Aroma quality is a key indicator of dehydrated cabbage. In this study, SPME-GC–MS was applied to characterize volatile flavor compounds in dehydrated cabbage produced using different drying treatments. A total of 98 volatile flavor substances were detected, including 6 isothiocyanates, 10 nitriles, 26 aldehydes, 7 esters, 10 alcohols, 8 acids, 9 hydrocarbons, 12 ketones, 6 sulfur-containing compounds, and 4 heterocyclic compounds (Table S2). These results indicated that aldehydes were the most predominant volatile compounds in cabbage. Drying method significantly influenced both the composition and abundance of volatile flavor compounds in cabbage (Fig. 5). Specifically, MD and MW + HAD treatment increased the total volatile content in cabbage by 51.14% and 34.06%, respectively, compared to the fresh (Fig. 5A). By contrast, HAD reduced the total volatile compound content by 27.84% relative to fresh cabbage, possibly because of volatilization and decomposition of volatile components in cabbage caused by prolonged high-temperature exposure. Regarding the diversity of volatile compounds, the number of flavor compounds obtained from FD, HAD, MD, and MW + HAD cabbage was 83, 71, 90, and 93, respectively (Fig. 5B). In fresh cabbage, the relative abundances were 26.44% for alcohols, 23.80% for isothiocyanates, 15.13% for fatty acids, and 10.16% for aldehydes. In HAD cabbage, nitriles (36.78%), aldehydes (24.03%), sulfur-containing compounds (16.98%), and alcohols (10.91%) accounted for the largest proportions of volatile compounds. By contrast, MW + HAD cabbage was dominated by aldehydes (40.92%), followed by nitriles (19.25%), alcohols (13.59%), and sulfur-containing compounds (13.44%). The primary volatile compounds in MD cabbage exhibited similarities to those in MW + HAD, with aldehydes, nitriles, and alcohols being the most prevalent. Fresh and FD cabbage contained higher levels of alcohols, esters, and ketones, indicating that thermal drying treatment resulted in a loss of these volatiles (Fig. 6). By contrast, the content of aldehydes in MD and MW + HAD was higher than that in fresh and HAD cabbage. Notably, 3-methylbutanal (chocolate, peach), 2-methylbutyraldehyde (roasted), benzeneacetaldehyde (green, hyacinth), decanal (citrus), and dodecanal (floral) were found at the highest concentrations in MW + HAD cabbage. The partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS–DA) result further confirmed that aldehydes and alcohols were characteristic aroma substances in MD and MW + HAD treatments. The differential compounds with VIP values exceeding 1.0, included butanal, 2-methylbutyraldehyde, 3-methylbutanal, 1-octyn-3-ol, 2-methylpropanal, and 2-methylpropanal (Fig. S3). Aldehyde concentration in Morchella sextelata also increases after HAD-MVD treatment (Liao et al., 2025). These aldehydes may result from Strecker degradation of amino acids in the Maillard reaction under high-temperature conditions and during microwave irradiation (Hofmann et al., 2000). In addition, sulfur-containing compounds, including dimethyl trisulfide and dimethyl disulfide, are important volatile flavor components in MW + HAD cabbage. Cabbage, belonging to the Brassicaceae family, is rich in glucosinolates, which can undergo degradation to form isothiocyanates and nitrile volatiles (Hanschen et al., 2012). Isothiocyanates were the main volatile compounds in Brassica vegetables, with the highest levels in fresh cabbage, which usually exhibits a pungent flavor (Liu et al., 2021). Isothiocyanates such as allyl isothiocyanate, 3-butanylisothiocyanate, benzyl isothiocyanate, and phenethyl isothiocyanate were obviously decreased in HAD, MD, and MW + HAD after thermal drying treatment (Fig. 6D). However, nitrile volatiles emerged as the primary volatile compounds in HAD cabbage. Elevated nitrile levels in HAD may represent thermal degradation products of cabbage glucosinolates (Zhang et al., 2021). Notably, nitriles usually showed only low or even adverse health effects (Kupke et al., 2016; Renz et al., 2024). In summary, the drying method can significantly alter the overall flavor of dehydrated cabbage by changing the composition, content, and relative proportion of volatile compounds. The FD method can better preserve the flavor compounds of cabbage, especially the unstable alcohols, esters, and ketones. HAD may promote the formation of adverse nitrile volatiles. MW + HAD can increase the formation of target volatile compounds, including aldehydes, alcohols, and sulfur-containing compounds, and improve the flavor of dehydrated cabbage by increasing floral and fruity aromatic notes.

Non-volatile metabolite profiles

To assess how drying treatments reshaped cabbage metabolite profiles, we performed untargeted profiling using UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometry. Across all samples, 42,028 metabolite ion features were initially captured. After filtering, 1420 metabolites remained (800 in negative-ion mode and 620 in positive-ion mode). They were then classified into 10 categories (Fig. 7A), dominated by lipids and lipid-like molecules (30.9%) and organoheterocyclic compounds (23.7%), followed by organic acids and derivatives (14.2%), benzenoids (10.1%), phenylpropanoids and polyketides (8.0%), organic oxygen compounds (6.3%), alkaloids and derivatives (2.7%), organic nitrogen compounds (1.1%), and other compounds. To further highlight the key variables altered by the drying process, semi-quantitative differences in phytochemical content between fresh versus dried samples were analyzed. The chord diagrams in Fig. 8B illustrate the relationships between different drying methods and metabolites. Cabbage is rich in key metabolites such as steroids and steroid derivatives, glycerophospholipids, benzene and substituted derivatives, indoles and derivatives, glucosinolates, triterpenoids, amino acids, peptides, and analogues. These compounds contribute to the nutritional and medicinal value of cabbage, and exhibited significant changes between groups (Fig. 8C). Specifically, amino acids, fatty acids, glycerophospholipids, flavonoids, and alkaloids, and their overall intensity, were significantly reduced after drying. With increasing drying time, enhanced Maillard reactions and increased Strecker degradation promote amino acid depletion, as observed in the targeted free amino acid analysis. The decrease in lipid and glycerophospholipid levels is predominantly because of lipid oxidation during the drying process, which increases levels of aldehyde volatiles and improves aroma quality (Wang et al., 2022). Moreover, flavonoids and alkaloids are typically heat sensitive and readily degrade at elevated temperatures, leading to reduced levels after drying (Song et al., 2025). Conversely, drying enhanced the overall intensity of several other metabolite categories. For example, drying notably increased the total intensity of cinnamic acids and derivatives, triterpenoids, glucosinolates and indoles and derivatives, and steroids and steroid derivatives, compared to fresh. Interestingly, samples dried using thermal drying treatment (HAD, MD, and MW + HAD) showed higher content of bioactive compounds, including cinnamic acids and derivatives, triterpenoids, and alkaloids, compared with FD. Broccoli samples dried by microwave and hot air drying contain higher levels of phenolic compounds, triterpenoids, and alkaloids than those dried by FD (Zhao et al., 2025). This could be attributed to disruption of plant cell structure or to thermally induced breakdown of compounds, which promotes phenolic acid, alkaloid, and terpenoid release (Cao et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2025).

Conclusion

Here, different drying procedures (HAD, MD, and MW + HAD) were applied on an industrial scale for the production of dehydrated cabbage, and the quality, nutritional, and chemical components of dehydrated cabbage were comparatively evaluated. Applying MW + HAD to cabbage dehydration not only shortens drying time but also yields superior product quality versus cabbage dried by MD or HAD alone. Notably, MW + HAD also exhibited higher retention of chlorophyll A, FAAs, TPC, and antioxidant capacity compared to conventional HAD. Furthermore, MW + HAD increased the diversity and overall levels of volatile flavor compounds relative to HAD alone and promoted the formation of key volatiles, including aldehydes, alcohols, and sulfur-containing compounds. In terms of non-volatile metabolites, thermal drying treatments (HAD, MD, and MW + HAD) had a more favorable release and generation of bioactive compounds, including cinnamic acids and derivatives, triterpenoids, and alkaloids, compared with freeze drying. Nevertheless, the present study had certain limitations. Although the drying time was shortened, the energy consumption or cost of MW + HAD was not evaluated. Moreover, future research should include larger-scale validation to enable a more comprehensive assessment of the advantages of MW + HAD. This will enhance the generalizability of the findings and increase their practical application value.
