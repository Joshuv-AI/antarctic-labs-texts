SUMMARY: In compliance with the Paperwork Reduction Act of 1995 (PRA), 
this notice announces that the Information Collection Request (ICR)

    Summary of the Collection of Information: The National Highway 
Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) will collect information from the 
public as part of a multi-year research effort to learn about drivers' 
acceptance and effectiveness of ISA technology.
    The overall objective of this research is to advance the state of 
knowledge regarding the interplay between driver acceptance and 
effectiveness of different ISA systems and is not immediately intended 
to inform regulations or policy. All study procedures are approved by 
the University of Iowa Institutional Review Board. The research will be 
conducted in three parts, referred to as Study 1, Study 2, and Study 3.
    For Study 1, participants will complete an intake and eligibility 
procedure, a training presentation, a familiarization drive, wellness 
questionnaires to assess susceptibility to simulator sickness, five 
study drives accompanied by a questionnaire after each drive to assess 
their acceptance and attitudes of ISA, and a questionnaire at the end 
of the study to measure their driving and speed behaviors.
    For Study 2 and Study 3, participants will complete an intake and 
eligibility procedure, a training presentation, a pre-drive 
questionnaire to assess their acceptance and attitudes of ISA, a 
familiarization drive, wellness questionnaires to assess susceptibility 
to simulator sickness, two study drives, and a post-drive questionnaire 
to assess their acceptance and attitudes of ISA and their driving and 
speed behaviors. The NADS-1 simulator will collect vehicle data (e.g., 
brake inputs, steering wheel angle) and data about the surrounding 
environment (e.g., distance to surrounding vehicles and lane markings).
    Description of the Need for the Information and Proposed Use of the 
Information: NHTSA's mission is to save lives, prevent injuries, and 
reduce the economic costs of road traffic crashes through education, 
research, safety standards, and enforcement activity. As vehicle 
technologies advance, they have the potential to dramatically reduce 
the loss of life each day in roadway crashes. Alternatively, the system 
may not reach this potential if drivers do not accept and do not use 
the systems. This new information collection supports the department's 
strategic goal of safety. Speeding contributes to a significant number 
of fatal motor vehicle crashes each year. ISA technology has the 
potential to mitigate and prevent speeding-related crashes by alerting 
the driver of their speed. To evaluate the effectiveness of ISA 
systems, research is needed to understand to what extent ISA system 
characteristics influence drivers' speeding behavior, attitudes, and 
perceptions of the technology.
    The following components will be used to obtain the necessary 
information to achieve this purpose.
    (1) Eligibility Questionnaire (NHTSA Form 2179): This questionnaire 
is necessary for determining respondent suitability for the study based 
on driving experience and history, ability to adhere to study 
requirements, general health, sleeping behavior, and ability to safely 
drive the NADS-1 without concerns.
    (2) Informed Consent Document 1 (NHTSA Form 2180): This form is 
necessary for obtaining documented informed consent from the 
participant to participate in Study 1. The form describes all study 
procedures, data storage and use, and potential risks from the study.
    (3) Informed Consent Document 2 (NHTSA Form 2181): This form is 
necessary for obtaining documented informed consent from the 
participant to participate in Study 2 or 3. The form describes all 
study procedures, data storage and use, and potential risks from the 
study.
    (4) Intake & Eligibility Confirmation (NHTSA Form 2267): This 
process includes completion of the research participant substitute W-9 
for payment, review of the driver's license to confirm it is valid by 
checking the expiration date and confirming age (for eligibility) and 
sex (for balancing sample) as listed on the license. These procedures 
are necessary to pay the participant and ensure they meet eligibility 
requirements before continuing with the experiment.
    (5) Wellness Questionnaire (NHTSA Form 2182): This form is 
necessary to measure signs and symptoms of simulator sickness. If 
sickness scores increase during the drive, participants will be asked 
to take a break or end the experiment.
    (6) Questionnaire 1 (NHTSA Form 2183): This form is necessary for 
Study 1 to assess participants' acceptance and attitudes of ISA. 
Collecting these data after each of the five study drives in Study 1 
will let us measure how exposure to ISA impacts acceptance and 
attitudes of ISA. It will be administered during the driving behavior 
assessment.
    (7) Driving Behavior Assessment for Study 1 and Questionnaire 2 
(NHTSA Form 2184): This procedure for Study 1 is necessary to measure 
the impact of ISA system characteristics on speeding behavior and 
acceptance of ISA. The participant will drive for 80 minutes in the 
driving simulator, ample time to briefly experience different ISA 
systems and form an opinion of different system characteristics. 
Questionnaire 2 is necessary for Study 1 to measure participants' self-
reported driving and speeding behaviors. These data will be used as 
covariates in the statistical analyses. It will be administered after 
the driving behavior assessment.
    (8) Questionnaire 3 (NHTSA Form 2185): This form is necessary for 
Study 2 and 3 to assess participants' acceptance and attitudes of ISA. 
It will be administered before the driving behavior assessment.
    (9) Driving Behavior Assessment for Study 2 and 3 and Questionnaire 
4 (NHTSA Form 2186): This procedure is necessary for Study 2 and 3 to

    60-Day Notice: A Federal Register notice with a 60-day comment 
period soliciting public comments on the following information 
collection was published on June 25, 2026 (91 FR 38469). Five comments 
were received in response to that notice.
    One of the comments was outside the scope of the Paperwork 
Reduction Act and this collection of information. Therefore, the 
comment will not be expanded on.
    The second comment was submitted by the National Transportation 
Safety Board (NTSB). NTSB expressed concern that a simulator-based 
study ``will not address the real-world environment that may affect ISA 
user acceptance,'' noting that although assessing ISA user acceptance 
and effectiveness is important and much research has already been 
conducted in this area, real-world environmental and social factors are 
not captured in a simulator setting.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment and agrees that a simulator-based 
study, by design, cannot fully capture the real-world environmental and 
social factors that influence sustained driver acceptance. This 
limitation will be documented in the resulting technical report.
    NTSB further noted that NHTSA's own 2024 Traffic Safety Facts 
report on speeding found that ``young male drivers were the most likely 
to be speeding at the time of fatal crashes,'' and expressed concern 
that the study's Eastern Iowa volunteer pool may over-select younger 
drivers who could be the least accepting of technologies that limit 
their speed, reducing the applicability of the study's findings.
    NHTSA appreciates NTSB's continued engagement in ISA research and 
its long-standing safety recommendations in this area. NHTSA agrees 
that a volunteer sample recruited from a single geographic area cannot, 
on its own, represent the full range of ages, driving experience, and 
speeding propensity found in the general driving population. NHTSA will 
document the demographic composition of the enrolled sample in the 
resulting technical report so that readers can appropriately weigh the 
generalizability of the findings, and efforts will be made to recruit a 
broad range of ages and driving experience within the constraints of 
the study's geographic recruitment area.
    Finally, NTSB encouraged NHTSA to assess the limitations of the 
proposed study to ensure that NHTSA continues moving expeditiously 
toward requiring as standard equipment in all new vehicles ISA systems 
that, at a minimum, warn the driver when the vehicle exceeds the speed 
limit.
    NHTSA appreciates NTSB's recommendations over the years. As stated 
previously, NHTSA will assess the limitations of the study and reduce 
them where possible. NHTSA notes, however, that this research is 
intended to advance the state of knowledge regarding driver acceptance 
and effectiveness of ISA system characteristics and is not immediately 
intended to inform rulemaking.
    The third comment was submitted by the Alliance for Automotive 
Innovation (Auto Innovators).
    Auto Innovators encouraged NHTSA ``to distinguish clearly among 
different ISA system designs and feedback modalities, including 
warning-based, haptic, and speed-control approaches,'' noting that 
driver acceptance may vary significantly depending on how a system 
communicates with or assists the driver.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment and concurs. The driving behavior 
assessment in Study 1 exposes participants to multiple ISA systems 
within a single session, and Studies 2 and 3 provide prolonged exposure 
to two ISA systems per participant. Participants in Study 1 also rank 
their preference across the visual, auditory, haptic, and speed-
limiting ISA variants they experience. The specific systems and 
feedback modalities evaluated will be described in the resulting 
technical report. These systems reflect a mix of designs and feedback 
modalities--warning-based, haptic, and speed-control approaches--
consistent with Auto Innovators' recommendation. NHTSA's research team 
also intends to draw on accuracy metrics from the earlier ISA project 
conducted with Exponent (``Intelligent Speed Assistance (ISA) 
Assessment of Enabling Technologies and Implementation Approaches'') 
when designing the driving scenarios for this study.
    Auto Innovators recommended that NHTSA evaluate ISA acceptance 
using ``measures that go beyond general favorability or perceived 
usefulness,'' including driver trust in the accuracy and reliability of 
speed limit information, nuisance or annoyance associated with alerts, 
understanding of system limitations, willingness to use the system over 
time, and the likelihood that drivers could override, disable, or 
otherwise adapt their behavior in response to the system.
    NHTSA appreciates this recommendation and agrees that driver 
acceptance is a multi-dimensional construct. The study's driver 
acceptance and attitude questionnaires will assess several of these 
dimensions: a nine-item semantic differential scale captures both 
perceived usefulness and the nuisance or annoyance associated with each 
ISA system, and separate items ask whether participants would use the 
system regularly and whether they would frequently override it. NHTSA 
acknowledges that the current instruments do not include an item that 
isolates driver trust in the accuracy and reliability of speed-limit 
information specifically, or driver understanding of situations in 
which an ISA system may fail to perform as expected; these dimensions 
are currently captured only indirectly, through more general items on 
system dependability and familiarity. NHTSA will consider adding 
targeted items addressing these two constructs as the study instruments 
are finalized.
    Auto Innovators also recommended that NHTSA evaluate ``drivers' 
acceptance of the system's default settings, including whether those 
settings influence drivers' willingness to use ISA over time or their 
likelihood of overriding or disabling it.''
    NHTSA appreciates this comment. The study's questionnaires will 
include an item asking whether participants would find it acceptable 
for the ISA system to be active by default at the start of every trip, 
directly addressing this recommendation. The experimental design 
supports the comparison of within-subject experiences with different 
ISA systems. This foundational work will provide insights for potential

[[Page 65617]]

future longitudinal studies where willingness to use ISA can be 
assessed over time.
    In addition, Auto Innovators recommended that the research 
``include realistic scenarios that reflect the complexity of speed 
limit identification in real-world driving, including temporary speed 
limits, work zones, school zones, variable speed limits, obscured or 
missing signs, ramps, adjacent roads with different posted limits, and 
situations where map-based and sign-based information may conflict.''
    NHTSA appreciates this comment. All three studies ask participants 
to rate expected ISA effectiveness separately by road type, including 
school zones and construction zones. The questionnaire items align with 
what they will experience in the driving simulator scenarios. The 
acceptance and effectiveness of ISA systems will be assessed using 
output from the questionnaire and driving simulator. NHTSA will 
consider incorporating additional scenario elements identified by Auto 
Innovators, such as temporary or variable speed limits and obscured 
signage, into the simulator scenario design as the studies are 
finalized.
    Auto Innovators cautioned that ``simulator studies cannot fully 
replicate the motivations, contextual cues, perceived risk, and 
repeated-use dynamics that influence driver behavior outside the 
research setting,'' and recommended that any simulator-based findings 
``be treated as complementary to, rather than a substitute for, real-
world or naturalistic data on sustained driver acceptance, adaptation, 
and ISA use.'' Auto Innovators further recommended that NHTSA study 
additional factors affecting real-world ISA use, including false 
positive and false negative alerts, alert timing and frequency, and the 
potential for habituation or alert fatigue over time.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment and agrees. The ISA systems used in 
the simulator-based study drives are not modeled as 100% accurate; 
participants will encounter instances of imperfect system performance 
during the study drives, allowing NHTSA to collect acceptance and 
effectiveness data under conditions that reflect the error-prone 
performance of ISA systems currently deployed on public roads rather 
than an idealized system. NHTSA agrees that simulator-based findings 
should be treated as complementary to, rather than a substitute for, 
real-world and naturalistic data, and will characterize this limitation 
in the technical report.
    Auto Innovators encouraged NHTSA ``to draw from Europe's experience 
with ISA while recognizing important differences between the European 
and U.S. regulatory and operating environments,'' noting that ISA has 
been mandatory in the EU since 2022 for new vehicle types and since 
July 2024 for all new vehicles, but that publicly available post-
mandate data on real-world user acceptance and disablement rates 
remains limited.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment and will consider available European 
research and post-mandate data, where relevant, in the design and 
interpretation of this study.
    Finally, Auto Innovators encouraged NHTSA ``to continue exploring 
speeding solutions that go beyond vehicle-based technology, consistent 
with the Safe System approach.''
    NHTSA appreciates this comment and notes that ISA is one of several 
speeding countermeasures NHTSA is pursuing as part of a broader Safe 
System approach to reducing speeding-related crashes.
    The fourth comment was submitted anonymously. The commenter argued 
that measuring driver acceptance in a simulator creates a ``false 
acceptance'' bias because participants do not experience the real-world 
social or physical consequences of maintaining a lower speed, such as 
pressure from surrounding traffic to exceed the posted limit. The 
commenter recommended that NHTSA inject operational stressors, such as 
aggressive tailgating and tight merge maneuvers, into the simulator 
scenarios to measure driver behavior under more realistic conditions.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment and acknowledges that a simulator 
environment cannot fully replicate the social and physical pressures of 
real-world driving. This limitation will be documented in the resulting 
technical report. NHTSA will consider the commenter's recommendation 
regarding surrounding traffic behavior as the simulator scenarios are 
finalized.
    The anonymous commenter also argued that real-world ISA systems, 
which rely on camera-based sign recognition and GPS map data, are 
subject to error, citing June 2026 real-world testing published by 
Thatcham Research finding that event-based accuracy at speed-limit 
transitions can be substantially lower than overall distance-based 
accuracy. Similar to the Auto Innovators, the commenter expressed 
concern that a simulator study using error-free, pristine ISA 
performance would measure driver acceptance of a theoretical, flawless 
technology rather than the error-prone systems currently deployed on 
public roads, and recommended that NHTSA adopt event-based rather than 
distance-based accuracy metrics.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment and agrees that ISA performance 
should not be modeled as error-free. The ISA systems used in the 
simulator-based study drives will not be modeled as 100% accurate; 
participants will encounter instances of imperfect system performance 
during the study drives, providing data on driver responses under 
conditions that reflect the error-prone performance of ISA systems 
currently deployed on public roads. NHTSA will consider the commenter's 
recommendation regarding event-based accuracy metrics in the design of 
the study's performance measures. NHTSA also notes that accuracy 
metrics from the earlier ISA project conducted with Exponent, 
referenced in an earlier response, may help inform this effort.
    This anonymous commenter also raised concerns regarding the 
susceptibility of camera-based sign-recognition systems to physical 
adversarial attacks, such as altered or perturbed signage, and 
recommended that NHTSA test ISA responses to degraded or adversarially 
altered signs.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment and agrees this is an important 
consideration for the underlying technology. However, this driver 
acceptance and effectiveness study is focused on driver response to ISA 
system behavior rather than the robustness of any particular 
manufacturer's computer-vision algorithm. Therefore, this study will 
not include the evaluation of camera-based sign-recognition systems 
exposed to adversarial attacks.
    Finally, the anonymous commenter argued that recruiting 
participants from the DSRI volunteer registry in Eastern Iowa risks 
oversampling younger, more technology-familiar drivers, and noted that 
the study as described does not evaluate the unique risks faced by 
older drivers who are less familiar with driver-assistance technologies 
or by drivers who use adaptive driving controls, such as hand-operated 
accelerator and brake controls. The commenter recommended that NHTSA 
broaden recruitment beyond the university-affiliated volunteer pool to 
include dedicated cohorts of older and less technology-familiar 
drivers, and evaluate compatibility with adaptive driving controls.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment and acknowledges that a volunteer 
sample recruited from a single geographic area will not fully represent 
the national driving population. NHTSA will document the demographic 
composition of the final sample, including age, in the resulting 
technical report. Stratification

[[Page 65618]]

will be used to balance the sample by age. The database that will be 
used to recruit participants for this study includes individuals across 
the lifespan, most of whom are not enrolled as students.
    NHTSA agrees that the needs of drivers who use adaptive driving 
controls and other special populations are important considerations for 
ISA research and will consider these populations in the design of 
future studies; potential participants are screened for health factors 
and simulator safety limitations, most of which are contraindications 
for participating in the motion-based driving simulator used in these 
studies.
    The fifth comment was submitted by the Insurance Institute for 
Highway Safety (IIHS). IIHS expressed support for NHTSA's planned study 
and recommended that the research include analysis of driver eye-glance 
behavior, de-emphasize measures of ISA effectiveness on speeding, and 
focus on addressing knowledge gaps about ISA acceptance.
    IIHS also recommended that NHTSA include analysis of driver eye-
glance behavior, referencing their recent report detailing a simulator 
study (Reagan et al., 2026). Reagan and colleagues (2026) found that 
ISA warnings were associated with more frequent but shorter glances to 
the instrument cluster compared with similar instances of speeding 
without ISA, a pattern that held for both visual-only and visual-plus-
audible warnings. IIHS noted that drivers' visual reaction to 
cautionary displays like ISA is understudied.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment and has reviewed the accompanying 
report. The simulator that will be used for this data collection can 
record driver eye-glance behavior. NHTSA will consider incorporating 
eye-glance metrics into the driving behavior assessments as the study 
protocol is finalized.
    IIHS noted that though its own study found decreases in speeding 
behavior associated with ISA, it placed little value on these findings 
because simulators poorly replicate the motivational factors that 
compel drivers to speed in the real world, which severely limits the 
external validity of speeding outcomes. IIHS encouraged NHTSA to adopt 
a similar perspective and to rely on field data to the extent the 
relationship between advisory ISA and speeding behavior is of equal or 
greater importance than the relationship between advisory ISA and 
acceptance.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment and agrees that simulator-based 
speeding outcomes should be interpreted with caution given the limits 
on external validity that IIHS describes. The IIHS report indicates 
that speeding metrics were reduced in each ISA condition compared with 
not driving with ISA. This suggests that although performed in a 
simulator, the within-subject comparisons provide support for the 
effectiveness of ISA systems. Consistent with NHTSA's responses to 
other comments in this notice, NHTSA will characterize simulator-based 
findings, including any observed changes in speeding behavior, as 
complementary to rather than a substitute for real-world and 
naturalistic data, and will prioritize the study's driver acceptance 
measures accordingly.
    IIHS reported that in a representative survey of U.S. drivers, only 
about a third agreed that an ISA system with audible and visual 
warnings at 1-2 mph over the speed limit would be acceptable, while 79 
percent would accept a system intervening at 10 mph over the limit. 
IIHS recommended that NHTSA validate this self-reported finding using 
the simulator study, and noted that many other parameters likely 
influencing acceptance, such as warning timing, have received little 
research attention.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment. The study's questionnaires will 
include items asking whether participants would find ISA intervention 
acceptable at 1-2 mph, 5 mph, and 10 mph over the posted speed limit, 
which will allow NHTSA to compare simulator-based acceptance ratings at 
each threshold against IIHS's survey findings. In Study 1, drivers will 
experience different speed limit thresholds, allowing us to better 
understand drivers' acceptance after experiencing these systems. NHTSA 
agrees that other parameters affecting acceptance, such as warning 
timing, warrant further study and will consider them as the study 
protocol is finalized
    IIHS also reported that in its own study, visual-only ISA warnings 
ranked higher in acceptance than warnings paired with audible cues, and 
suggested this effect may be magnified by the false alert rate, since 
conditions with false alerts would better reflect the accuracy levels 
of current ISA implementations. IIHS recommended that NHTSA examine the 
influence of speed limit detection accuracy on acceptance, noting that 
real-world ISA performance is imperfect and that no study has 
systematically manipulated the false alert rate of speed warnings while 
measuring acceptance.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment and agrees that the interaction 
between alert accuracy and display modality is an important research 
gap. As noted in NHTSA's response to other comments in this notice, the 
ISA systems used in the simulator-based study drives are not modeled as 
100% accurate; participants will encounter instances of imperfect 
system performance across the ISA systems and modalities evaluated, 
which will allow NHTSA to examine how alert accuracy interacts with 
acceptance of different display types.
    IIHS commented that in its study, early exposure to audible cues 
led participants to glance at the instrument cluster and reduce speed 
before the onset of the audible alert, suggesting behavioral adaptation 
to avoid the alert, and cited other literature describing similar 
longer-term adaptations, such as drivers maintaining speed just below 
alert thresholds to avoid repeated warnings. IIHS recommended deeper 
exploration of these learning effects.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment. NHTSA agrees that behavioral 
adaptation to repeated ISA alerts is an important consideration. 
Studies 2 and 3 involve prolonged, within-subject exposure to two ISA 
systems, which may surface some early adaptation effects; however, a 
single-visit study cannot capture the longer-term learning effects IIHS 
describes. NHTSA will consider this a priority area for potential 
future longitudinal ISA research.
    Furthermore, IIHS recommended that NHTSA examine relative 
differences in acceptance among visual-only, visual-auditory, and 
visual-haptic ISA displays, noting that a reliable acceptance advantage 
for haptic over auditory warnings has been observed in other crash-
avoidance contexts, such as lane departure warning, but has not been 
studied for ISA. IIHS noted that this research gap may partly reflect 
European law's prohibition on haptic ISA warnings unless delivered 
through the accelerator pedal, a restriction that would not apply to 
U.S. vehicles.
    NHTSA appreciates this comment. The ISA systems evaluated in Study 
1 include a haptic variant alongside visual and auditory systems, 
allowing NHTSA to compare acceptance across modalities, including the 
visual-haptic comparison IIHS highlights. NHTSA will ensure this 
comparison is addressed in the resulting technical report.

    Estimated Total Annual Burden Cost: $0.
    Respondents will not incur any reporting or recordkeeping costs 
from the information collection. The only cost respondents will incur 
is a one-time cost for local travel to and from DSRI. The costs are 
minimal and are expected to be offset by the monetary compensation that 
will be provided to all participants who enroll.

this information collection, including (a) whether the proposed 
collection of information is necessary for the proper performance of 
the functions of the agency, including whether the information will 
have practical utility; (b) the accuracy of the agency's estimate of 
the burden of the proposed collection of information, including the 
validity of the methodology and assumptions used; (c) ways to enhance 
the quality, utility and clarity of the information to be collected; 
and (d) ways to minimize the burden of the collection of information on 
respondents, including the use of appropriate automated, electronic, 
mechanical, or other technological collection techniques or other forms 
of information technology, e.g., permitting electronic submission of 
responses.
