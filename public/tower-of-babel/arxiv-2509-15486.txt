Discrete Flow-Based Generative Models for Measurement Optimization in Quantum Computing
Isaac L. Huidobro-Meezs, Jun Dai, Rodrigo A. Vargas-Hernández

Abstract
Achieving chemical accuracy in quantum simulations is often constrained by the measurement bottleneck: estimating operators requires a large number of shots, which remains costly even on fault-tolerant devices and is further exacerbated on today’s noisy hardware by finite circuit fidelity and error-mitigation overhead. Addressing this challenge involves a multiobjective optimization problem that balances total shot count, the number of distinct measurement circuits, and hardware-specific compilation constraints. Existing methods typically rely on heuristic graph-coloring strategies to group commuting or qubit-wise commuting Hamiltonian terms, or on greedy allocation schemes for distributing measurements. Such approaches explore only a limited portion of the combinatorial solution space, potentially missing superior solutions. We introduce an algorithm that adapts Generative Flow Networks (GFlowNets) for coloring graph representations of Hamiltonians, enabling the generation of diverse, high-quality groupings. Our approach samples colored graphs in proportion to a user-defined reward, naturally capturing multiobjective trade-offs and discovering multiple competitive solutions. On benchmark molecular Hamiltonians, our method outperforms sorted-insertion baselines by reducing measurement costs. We further analyze the role of composite rewards, incorporating both the number of circuits and measurement costs, leading to additional improvements. This generative policy framework not only reduces measurement costs but also offers flexibility for potential hardware-aware adaptations through its reward function.

Introduction

In the context of quantum computing for the electronic structure problem, reducing the cost of measurements remains a critical challenge for scaling up quantum algorithms. A prominent example is the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE)
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
, where estimating the expectation values of molecular Hamiltonians often requires a prohibitively large number of measurements to achieve chemical accuracy, constituting the measurement problem in VQE. Consequently, various strategies have been developed to reduce measurement requirements
[
7
]
.
On the algorithmic side, clever grouping of commuting operators has been shown to reduce measurement overhead significantly. Examples include splitting the Hamiltonian into simultaneously measurable fragments using (anti)commutation structure, unitary factorization, fermionic-space transformations, and symmetry considerations
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,

,

,

,

,

,

]
. These approaches typically rely on greedy sequential optimization strategies
[
17
,
11
]
to identify optimal groupings with precomputed covariance dictionaries and tackle fully commuting (FC) or qubit-wise commuting (QWC) groupings using coloring algorithms
[
13
]
.

Taken together, these measurement-reduction strategies underscore the importance of combinatorial optimization in quantum computing. Generative models and reinforcement learning (RL) are increasingly being developed to tackle problems in quantum science and related fields, including those with a strong sequential and combinatorial nature
[
18
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,
29
,
30
]
. These AI-driven methods have proposed solutions that were neither previously proposed nor anticipated by humans. For example, in Refs.
[
25
,
31
]
, the authors developed an autonomous algorithm for quantum optics experiments that identified a configuration capable of producing significantly greater entanglement than expected. The insight gained from this unexpected result led the researchers to formulate a new principle of entanglement generation
[
19
,
32
]
. Similarly, machine learning (ML) methods are emerging as powerful tools for solving challenging combinatorial optimization problems
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
. A recent study on the elementary shortest path problem employed an unsupervised graph neural network to identify near-optimal routes, outperforming classical heuristic methods on graphs, and was capable of generalizing to much larger unseen graphs (with more than 100 nodes)
[
36
]
. These examples illustrate how AI/ML models can explore vast solution spaces, often revealing high-quality solutions or hidden patterns that conventional greedy optimization methods might overlook.

For the measurement problem, ML-based approaches have been developed in recent years. For example, Liang et al.
[
37
]
developed an RL agent that dynamically allocates measurement shots during a VQE optimization, decreasing the total measurements while still converging to the ground-state energy.
Each of these developments, from analytic grouping to RL-based policies, contributes to reducing quantum measurement overhead within the current noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era. Additionally, early evidence of efficiency gains from generative models in quantum computation is already emerging. Zou et al.
[
38
]
demonstrated that embedding a flow-based generative model into VQE can further accelerate its convergence
[
39
]
. Additionally, Nakaji et al. optimized a classical generative model to produce quantum circuits with desired properties.

Despite these advances, most current methods focus on optimizing a single objective or tasks (e.g., minimizing variance and identifying one locally optimal shot distribution). Greedy optimization and RL-based algorithms often converge to a single local optimal solution, neglecting alternative candidates that may be as effective or more optimal. This highlights a limitation of both approaches, particularly in RL, where it is well known that agents can be trapped in a single mode of the solution space
[
40
,
41
]
. Such a lack of diversity can be problematic in scientific applications, where multiple distinct measurement schemes or experimental designs could yield satisfactory results. Restricting exploration to a single solution risks overlooking other viable and potentially more practical alternatives for quantum technologies.

Generative models offer a complementary perspective; rather than producing a single optimal solution, they aim to learn a distribution over many possible solutions. Traditionally, this class of models relies on the existence of a dataset for the task problem and can be adapted to different tasks through conditional sampling schemes, making them powerful but inherently data-dependent
[
42
]
.
The recently proposed Generative Flow Networks (GFlowNets) overcome this limitation by providing a framework for sampling complex objects in proportion to a reward function
[
43
,
41
,
20
]
, thereby bypassing the need for extensive training datasets. Rather than optimizing for a single objective, GFlowNets learn to generate a diverse set of high-reward solutions, effectively sampling from an unnormalized target distribution of interest, allowing for the inclusion of different figures of merit in the reward. Each sampled solution is constructed step-by-step, similar to an RL policy, but the learning objective ensures that the probability of generating any given solution is proportional to its reward.
This fundamentally different probabilistic approach enables a GFlowNet to output a rich ensemble of distinct, high-quality solutions independently by learning a direct generative policy
[
43
]
. Sampling methods such as Markov chain Monte Carlo can be considered to address this problem, but they have the disadvantage of a lengthy stochastic search of the solution space and the challenge of mode mixing
[
44
]
.

The potential of GFlowNets is especially promising for the measurement problem, where multiple disparate configurations (of required circuits, experimental parameters, commutativity conditions, etc.) might yield near-optimal outcomes. By covering many modes of the solution landscape, a GFlowNet-based approach could avoid the "dominant mode" trap of conventional RL. There is growing evidence that this diversity-driven approach pays off. In domains ranging from graph combinatorial problems
[
20
]
, drug discovery
[
45
,
46
]
to materials design
[
47
]
, GFlowNets have been shown to generate more diverse solution candidates, yet still high-performing compared to those generated by RL or genetic algorithms.

For quantum physics applications, GFlowNets have been applied to open quantum system simulations to select a subset of Feynman paths to approximate a complex influence functional
[
48
]
. The model not only learned to recover accurate dynamics with far fewer paths, but also notably discovered multiple distinct sets of paths ("solutions") that achieved similar accuracy. The authors observed that the model identified about five qualitatively different high-reward modes in the problem’s solution space. This showcases the ability of the GFlowNets to find alternative solutions. Such multimodal solution discovery is precisely what we seek in optimizing quantum measurement strategies, where there may be many ways to group fragments with comparable performance.

In this work, encouraged by these developments, we recast the measurement problem as a generative search problem over valid Hamiltonian groupings and introduce GFlowNets to learn policies that sample high-quality groupings with probability proportional to a task-specific reward (e.g., an estimator inversely related to measurement requirements). Our construction leverages the graph-theoretic equivalence between minimum-clique cover and coloring of the complement graph
[
13
]
, allowing a policy to color a graph step by step while respecting validity constraints, for example, disallowing the same color between node neighbors.
In what follows, we briefly describe the Hamiltonian grouping techniques used for molecular Hamiltonians in quantum computing in Section
2.1
. In Sections
2.2
and
2.3
, we give an introduction to GFlowNets along with a description of the algorithm for the measurement problem, while in Section
2.4
we discuss the architecture of our model. The results are presented in Section
3
, starting with a measurement-only reward followed by a comparison with composite rewards, mixing measurement count with the number of groups produced.

2
Methods

2.1
Grouping techniques for molecular Hamiltonians

VQE techniques for the electronic structure problems are one of the most common applications of quantum algorithms in NISQ devices
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
. These approaches rely on measuring the expectation value of the electronic Hamiltonian, starting from the second-quantized operator projected onto a set of
N
spin orbitals,

H=\sum_{pq}^{N}h_{pq}a_{p}^{\dagger}a_{q}+\sum_{pqrs}^{N}g_{pqrs}a_{p}^{\dagger}a_{q}^{\dagger}a_{r}a_{s},

(1)

where
a_{p}^{\dagger}\;(a_{p})
are the creation (annihilation) operators for spin orbital
p
, and
h_{pq}
,
g_{pqrs}
are found from one- and two-body integrals. For its measurement, a fermion-to-qubit mapping is required, e.g., Jordan-Wigner (JW)
[
49
]
and Bravyi-Kitaev (BK)
[
50
]
, leading to a qubit Hamiltonian in terms of Pauli products,

\hat{H}_{q}=\sum_{k}^{N_{P}}\omega_{k}\hat{P}_{k},\ \ \ \hat{P}_{n}=\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N_{q}}\sigma_{n},

(2)

with every Pauli product
\hat{P}_{n}
being a tensor product of Pauli operators and identities for the corresponding qubit
\sigma_{n}\in\{\hat{x}_{n},\hat{y}_{n},\hat{z}_{n},\hat{1}_{n}\}
.
N_{P}
is the number of Pauli words in
\hat{H}_{q}
, and
N_{q}
is the number of qubits.

To find molecular energies, VQE performs an iterative optimization of a parameterized wavefunction,
|\psi_{\theta}\rangle
, as
E_{\theta}=\min_{\theta}\langle\psi_{\theta}|\hat{H}_{q}|\psi_{\theta}\rangle
.
Since
\hat{H}_{q}
contains terms that do not commute with each other, a direct measurement of the operator is not possible. For this reason, the Hamiltonian needs to be partitioned into
N_{f}
compatible fragments
[
13
]
and measure each of them separately,

\hat{H}_{q}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\hat{H}_{\alpha};\ \ \ \ E_{\theta}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\langle\psi_{\theta}|\hat{H}_{\alpha}|\psi_{\theta}\rangle.

(3)

Moreover, we require diagonalizing each fragment to make them compatible with the computational basis, transforming them into their Ising form through unitary operations
[
7
]
, incurring additional costs depending on the grouping scheme due to circuit implementation in current NISQ devices.

Several grouping schemes exist; this work focuses on FC and QWC groupings for the VQE problem, although intermediate schemes have shown improvements in measurement allocation
[
51
,
52
]
. For FC, the requirement is that Pauli products, within a group, commute with each other
[\hat{P}_{n},\hat{P}_{m}]=0
. QWC is a more strict condition since, for a pair of Pauli products, every single-qubit operator must commute
[\hat{\sigma}_{i},\hat{\tau}_{i}]_{\text{QWC}}=0,\ \forall\ \sigma_{i}\in\hat{P}_{n},\ \tau_{i}\in\hat{P}_{m}
. Both of these partitionings can be generated by constructing the corresponding commutativity graph and identifying the minimum-clique cover, with each clique in the graph representing a distinct group. This approach is equivalent to coloring the complementary graph, as is well-known in graph theory and as is often employed in quantum computing
[
13
,
12
]
. Although efficient algorithms are known for the coloring problem
[
53
]
, they do not provide any optimality guarantees for the solution. Additionally, we need to consider the cost of implementing each partitioning for current NISQ devices, i.e., FC-fragments require Clifford transformations to get to the Ising form. In contrast, QWC-fragments require single-qubit Clifford transformations, bypassing CNOT gates and their associated measurement overhead due to circuit fidelity
[
7
]
.

Substantial efforts have been put into developing algorithms to reduce the number of measurements through grouping techniques, going from greedy algorithms
[
54
,
11
]
to techniques which introduce "ghost" Pauli terms
[
55
]
or modify the Hamiltonian through fermionic algebra manipulations
[
17
]
, with the drawback of requiring iterative optimizations and potentially finding sub-optimal solutions. For a more comprehensive list of developments in this area, we refer the reader to Ref.
[
7
]
.

2.2
GFlowNets

GFlowNets
[
41
,
43
]
are a class of generative models over a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
G=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A})
of states with initial state
s_{0}
and terminal set
\mathcal{S}^{f}
. Here,
\mathcal{S}
denotes the set of states and
\mathcal{A}
the set of discrete actions. The set of actions corresponds to transitions between two states
s\rightarrow s^{\prime}
with a complete trajectory
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
defined by the sequence
\tau=(s_{0}\rightarrow s_{1}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow s_{n}=s_{f})
with
s_{f}\in\mathcal{S}^{f}
. GFlowNets learn the probability of sampling a compositional object
s_{f}\in\mathcal{S}^{f}
, where the probability of reaching it is proportional to a positive reward function
R(s_{f})
.

We define a non-negative function
F:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, called flow, together with edge flows
F\left(s\rightarrow s^{\prime}\right)\geq 0
for
s\rightarrow s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
. The flow satisfies the conservation law at every non-terminal state
s\notin\mathcal{S}^{f}
,

F(s)=\sum_{s^{\prime},s\to s^{\prime}}F(s\to s^{\prime})=\sum_{s^{\prime\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\to s}F(s^{\prime\prime}\to s),

(4)

with boundary conditions at terminals
F(s_{f})=R(s_{f})
, and
F(s_{0})=Z=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}^{f}}R(s)
. These equalities define a Markovian flow
[
43
]
. The forward transition probability and the backward probability are
P_{F}(s^{\prime}|s)={F(s\to s^{\prime})}/{F(s)}
and
P_{B}(s|s^{\prime})={F(s\to s^{\prime})}/{F(s^{\prime})}
, respectively, which satisfy the detailed balance condition on the edges
F(s)P_{F}(s^{\prime}|s)=F(s^{\prime})P_{B}(s|s^{\prime})
.
Sampling a trajectory under
P_{F}
factorizes along the DAG,

\displaystyle P(\tau)=\prod_{t=1}^{n}P_{F}(s_{t}|s_{t-1})

(5)

\displaystyle P(\tau|s_{n}=s_{f})=\prod_{t=1}^{n}P_{B}(s_{t-1}|s_{t}).

(6)

with the terminal marginal
P_{T}(s_{f})=R(s_{f})/Z
. In GFlowNets, the training objective ensures that the flow satisfies these conservation constraints. In practice, this is achieved by parameterizing
P_{F}
, and optionally
P_{B}
, so that the conservation law holds at all internal states and the boundary matches the reward at terminals
[
43
]
.
Previous work
[
56
]
achieved this by minimizing the violation of local consistency conditions, such as the flow matching (Eq.
4
). Although effective, these local objectives can suffer from a slow credit assignment problem
[
57
]
. The reward function
R(s_{f})
at a terminal state only provides a direct learning signal to the final transition, and this information must propagate backward through the DAG one step at a time during training. This can be inefficient for long trajectories or when the reward landscape is complex.

In this work, instead, we use the trajectory balance (TB) objective
[
57
]
. TB enforces the flow consistency condition over an entire trajectory in a single step. We have for all complete trajectories the condition,

Z\prod_{t=1}^{n}P_{F}(s_{t}|s_{t-1})=R(s_{f})\prod_{t=1}^{n}P_{B}(s_{t-1}|s_{t}).

(7)

We parameterize the total flow
Z
and the forward
P_{F}(s^{\prime}|s)
and backward probabilities
P_{B}(s|s^{\prime})
with a graph neural network (GNN) with parameters
\theta
. The learning objective is to minimize the squared difference loss,

L(\tau;\theta)=\left(\log Z_{\theta}+\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log P_{F,\theta}(s_{t}|s_{t-1})\right.\\
\left.-\log R(s_{f})-\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log P_{B,\theta}(s_{t-1}|s_{t})\right)^{2}.

(8)

Eq.
8
directly assigns credit for the outcome
R(s_{f})
to all transitions in the trajectory simultaneously, substantially improving the efficiency of learning in complex, high-dimensional state spaces.

GFlowNets thus offer a principled method for sampling from a distribution proportional to a reward function
R(s)
, or equivalently from an energy function
E(s):=-\log R(s)

[
43
]
, allowing for customizable rewards tailored to specific problems. Contrary to most generative models, the training protocol co-occurs with the sampling stage. Furthermore, GFlowNets-based approaches are particularly effective in problems exhibiting a compositional structure, where sequential generation is natural, the reward function is non-negative and easy to evaluate, and the target distribution is highly multimodal
[
46
]
.

2.3
Color assignment with GFlowNets

For the measurement problem, the algorithm consists of the sequential coloring of the graph representing the qubit Hamiltonian. The goal is to color the complement of the commutativity graph for the system, where each node represents a Pauli word in the Hamiltonian, and the node color indicates which group it belongs to.

We start with the uncolored graph for the system. This state is given to a GNN, as described in Section
2.4
, which will estimate the forward flow of assigning a color to the next node,
P_{F}
, alongside the associated backward policy
P_{B}
. The color assignment on each node with the available colors represents the set of actions for GFlowNet, and is masked according to validity constraints, i.e., a graph is not valid if two connected nodes have the same color. The step of the trajectory defines the node to be colored. To reduce the size of the sampling space, we set an upper bound on the number of colors according to a greedy coloring algorithm, which tries to color the graph using as few colors as possible, following a random sequential strategy, as implemented in NetworkX
[
58
]
. This bound is also implemented in the mask function.

Figure 1:
Diagram of GFlowNet sampling process. The initial state is an uncolored graph, for which different trajectories are generated. Each circle represents a state in the DAG. When the graph is fully colored, the reward function is computed. The forward and backward probabilities are parametrized by a GNN. As can be seen in the red trajectory, states leading to invalid graphs (two neighbors with the same color) and any subsequent trajectory are masked from the set of actions. The reward function for any invalid state is set to zero.

Our proposed algorithm continues until a fully colored graph is produced, representing a terminal state
s_{f}
, for which a reward function
R(s_{f})
is computed. The loss function is generated for the trajectory, and the model is updated after
n_update
full-trajectories. The algorithm stops when the preset number of total samples is generated. See Fig.
1
for a visualization of the proposed algorithm.

To sample useful and valid groupings with GFlowNets, the reward function must be carefully designed. We define the total reward
R(x)
as a weighted sum of two components: i) the number of measurements required to reach a target accuracy
R_{M}
, and ii) a regularization term penalizing excessive groupings (colors)
R_{G}
,

R(x)=\lambda_{0}R_{M}(x)+\lambda_{1}R_{G}(x),

(9)

where
\lambda_{0}
and
\lambda_{1}
are hyperparameters controlling the relative importance of each term. The measurement term is given by

\displaystyle R_{M}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}M(x)},

(10)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}M(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{H}_{\alpha})}\right)^{2},

(11)

where
M(x)
is the total number of measurements needed to achieve a target accuracy
\varepsilon

[
14
,
11
]
, and
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{H}_{\alpha})
is the variance of the fragment
\alpha
.
In this work, we compute variances from the exact full configuration interaction wavefunction, setting
\varepsilon=1
mHa, below chemical accuracy (< 1.6 mHa). Classically efficient wavefunctions can also be used to estimate the variances, introducing less than 9% error for systems similar to those studied here
[
17
]
.
The second term,

R_{G}(x)=N_{P}-N_{G}(x),

(12)

regularizes the number of groups (
N_{G}
), accounting for the number of quantum circuits required, and is given by
N_{G}=\text{max}\_\text{color}(x)
where
\text{max}\_\text{color}(x)
is the maximum number of distinct colors found in the graph. This encourages GFlowNets to explore alternative, potentially more efficient circuit configurations instead of focusing solely on minimizing measurements. Additional optimization objectives can be included in the reward, for example, the fidelity overheads
[
59
]
arising from the circuit implementation.

2.4
Flow function architecture

In the proposed GFlowNets for measurement optimization, the forward and backward policies are computed by the global add pooling operation after two graph convolution layers.
Our GNN architecture is based on the graph isomorphism network
[
60
]
, modified to incorporate edge features (GINE)
[
61
]
.
The node update rule is given by

x_{i}^{\prime}=h_{\theta}\left(x_{i}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\text{ReLU}(x_{j}+e_{j,i})\right),

(13)

where
x_{i}
are the input node features,
x_{i}^{\prime}
are the updated node features, corresponding here to the forward and backward policies for object generation,
\mathcal{N}_{i}
is the set of neighbors of node
i
,
e_{j,i}
are the edge features, and
h_{\theta}
is a multilayer perceptron (MLP).

The GNN input represents a state in which each node corresponds to a term of the Hamiltonian (Pauli words), and its feature encodes the associated color or group. All edges are initialized with a feature value of
-1
to encode anticorrelation, since neighbors can’t share the same color in the complementary commutativity graph.
The architecture begins with an embedding layer, with a dimension
emb_d
, followed by two GINE convolutional layers, each containing a two-layer MLP of dimension
hidden_d
and ReLU activations.
The logits for the sequential coloring probabilities are produced by a two-layer MLP with the same
hidden_d
size. These logits are then used to sample from a categorical distribution with a fixed seed for all simulations, outputting the action for each step in the state construction. We denote this model as
GINE
throughout the manuscript.

Figure 2:
Diagram for the
\texttt{GINE}_{w}
model architecture. The values
n_{emb}
and
n_{hid}
are equal to
emb_d
and
hidden_d
. After passing the node colors through an embedding layer, the representation is augmented by the coefficients of Eq.
2
. For the
GINE
model, we just discard the coefficients vector, added after the embedding.

A variant architecture, denoted
\texttt{GINE}_{w}
, follows the same structure but augments the node embedding with the weights of the Pauli words from Eq.
2
as an extra feature dimension. To visualize this architecture, see Fig.
2
. We found that for most systems, this architecture outperforms the initial
GINE
implementation.

Our analysis provides valuable insight into the QWC grouping problem and suggests promising directions for future implementations of GNNs. For instance, instead of applying coloring on the complement of the commutativity graph, one could directly use the commutativity graph, which is typically sparser, and solve the minimum-clique cover problem. 4
Conclusion and discussion

In this study, we presented a discrete-flow-based generative model using GFlowNets for the measurement problem in quantum computing. We demonstrated that GFlowNets discover grouping configurations that reduce estimated measurement budgets relative to standard sorted-insertion heuristics
[
54
]
. Beyond these empirical gains, our central conceptual contribution is a principled mechanism for learning from families of solutions and returning multiple high-quality groupings instead of committing to a single heuristic output. GFlowNets operates as a sampling-based scheme, enabling the generation of multiple circuits within a single training trajectory. Unlike deterministic pipelines that yield a single partition, GFlowNets produce a diverse set of candidates that naturally support anytime improvement and ensemble selection. Domain-specific constraints can also be seamlessly incorporated into the reward function, e.g., accounting for varying noise conditions, circuit fidelity overhead
[
59
]
, or hardware-aware restrictions. In doing so, GFlowNets adaptively sample solutions that are better aligned with the realities of near-future quantum devices.

While GlowNets bypasses the pre-training stage in generative models, it still requires samples to learn the regions with high reward. Two main limitations for scaling this class of algorithms are the length of the trajectory
\tau
and the associated cost to evaluate the reward function. While our results for
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
are impressive, even though we used fewer samples, managing to further reduce the number of iterations will allow for scaling. Furthermore, GNNs were a natural fit to the measurement problem due to the graph construction for the commutativity graph; however, as mentioned earlier in the paper, larger graphs require more message-passing stages, which impacts the scalability of this algorithm. For a more complex reward function that involves multiple rewards, strategies like Ref.
[
74
]
could be considered.
Additional extensions could include more flexible grouping strategies by loosening the QWC conditions. More recently, the GALIC framework
[
52
]
was introduced to interpolate between FC and QWC measurement schemes, like
k
-commutativity
[
51
]
. This method achieves roughly 20% lower variance in energy estimates compared to standard grouping approaches, making this an attractive extension to our protocol.

Given the structure of the grouping strategy through coloring, the early stages of GFlowNets training are spent learning how to sample valid graphs before converging toward high-reward regions; see Fig.
3
. Introducing a pre-training phase, where the model is “warmed up” using circuits generated by heuristic approaches such as RLF, could accelerate this process, an idea analogous to strategies previously proposed for molecular graph generation
[
75
,
76
]
. In addition, recent advances in GFlowNets
[
77
,
78
,
79
]
provide promising directions to strengthen our framework. These future developments offer mechanisms for fine-tuning, diversity promotion, and variance reduction, which could enable solutions competitive with covariance-based measurement allocation techniques
[
11
]
. Leveraging precomputed covariance dictionaries from classically efficient methods may further enhance learning efficiency and reduce the computational overhead associated with reward evaluation.

Together, our results indicate that generative policies are a promising tool for the measurement bottleneck in NISQ-era quantum chemistry. GFlowNets, in particular, offer a compelling paradigm for complex design problems in quantum computing and beyond. They unify the strengths of generative modeling, the ability to sequentially construct solutions under domain-specific constraints, and the flexibility to sample diverse candidates according to a user-defined reward. In the context of quantum measurement optimization, this combination not only reduces experimental costs but also enables exploration of a richer landscape of viable solutions.
We hope that this work encourages further efforts to integrate flow-based generative models into optimization tasks across quantum computing. At the same time, it provides a fertile testing ground for advancing GFlowNet methodology itself, motivating the design of more efficient training protocols, richer reward structures, and scalable implementations tailored to real hardware.
