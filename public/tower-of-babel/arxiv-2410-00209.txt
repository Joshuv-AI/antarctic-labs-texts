Closed Repeats
Dmitry Kosolobov

Abstract
Much research in stringology focuses on structures that can, in a way, “grasp” repeats (substrings that occur multiple times) as, for example, the so-called runs, a.k.a. maximal repetitions, compactly describe all tandem repeats. In this paper we introduce closed repeats: given a string s s , its non-empty substring s [ i . . j ] s[i\,..\,j] is a right (left) closed repeat if its closest occurrence s [ i ′ . . j ′ ] s[i^{\prime}\,..\,j^{\prime}] with i ′ > i i^{\prime}>i cannot be “extended” to the right (respectively, left) matching s ⁡ [ j + 1 ] = s ⁡ [ j ′ + 1 ] s[j{+}1]=s[j^{\prime}{+}1] (respectively, s ⁡ [ i − 1 ] = s ⁡ [ i ′ − 1 ] s[i{-}1]=s[i^{\prime}{-}1] ); the repeat is closed if it is both left and right closed. We note that the closed repeats correspond to the maximal closed substrings recently proposed by Badkobeh et al. and they include all runs as a special case. We prove that the number of right/left closed repeats is O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) , where n n is the length of s s , and we show that this bound is tight. The (right/left) closed repeats can be computed in the optimal time O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) ; as we prove, the computation time cannot be lower than Ω ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ σ ) \Omega(n\log\sigma) over a general ordered alphabet of size σ \sigma even when the number of the closed repeats is O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) . As an application, we describe data structures using the closed repeats for a number of substring queries: finding the period of the substring provided it is “periodic”, finding the longest repeat in the substring, computing the rightmost LZ77 parsing of the substring. Keywords: closed repeats, maximal repeats, repetitions, runs, closed words, LZ77, Lempel–Ziv

Closed Repeats and Other Repetitive Structures

For a string
s=c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{n}

|s|
its length

. Denote

Main definition

Given a string

is called a

(respectively,

)

if it has an occurrence

with
i^{\prime}>i
such that
s[i\,..\,j]
does not occur at positions
i{+}1,i{+}2,\ldots,i^{\prime}{-}1
and either
s[j{+}1]\neq s[j^{\prime}{+}1]
or
j^{\prime}=n
(respectively,
s[i{-}1]\neq s[i^{\prime}{-}1]
or

); the repeat

is

s[i^{\prime}\,..\,j^{\prime}]
the

s[i\,..\,j]
.

To put it differently,

is a closed repeat if its next occurrence cannot be “extended” to the left and to the right matching
s[i{-}1]
(if
i>1
) and
s[j{+}1]
(if
j<n
), respectively. It is important to emphasize that we treat as different those closed repeats that are equal as strings but occur at different positions in

Example
.

The string
s=banana
has the only closed repeat
s[2\,..\,4]
, three right closed repeats
s[2\,..\,4]
,
s[3\,..\,4]
,
s[4\,..\,4]
, and three left closed repeats
s[2\,..\,2]
,
s[2\,..\,3]
,
s[2\,..\,4]
.

We consider the (right/left) closed repeats as position-dependent structures that can “describe” all repeats in the following sense: given an arbitrary repeat
t
, each its occurrence
s[i\,..\,j]=t
, except the rightmost one, is contained in at least one right closed repeat of the form
s[i\,..\,j{+}r]
, for some
r\geq 0
. To see this, one can take the next occurrence
s[i^{\prime}\,..\,j^{\prime}]
of
s[i\,..\,j]
and take as
r
the length of the longest common prefix of
s[j{+}1\,..\,n]
and
s[j^{\prime}{+}1\,..\,n]
. Note that the right closed repeat containing
s[i\,..\,j]
is not unique: for instance, the repeat
s[1\,..\,1]
in
s=abcababc
is contained in two right closed repeats
s[1\,..\,2]=ab
and
s[1\,..\,3]=abc
. Analogously, one can show that
s[i\,..\,j]
is contained in a (not necessarily unique) left closed repeat
s[i{-}\ell\,..\,j]
and a (not necessarily unique) closed repeat
s[i{-}\ell\,..\,j{+}r]

\ell\geq 0
and
r\geq 0
.

Let us relate the closed repeats to some other well-known combinatorial objects describing repeats.

Maximal repeats.

A string
t
is called a
maximal repeat
of a string
s
if
t
occurs in
s
at least twice and, for each letter
a
, the strings
at
and
ta
have strictly less occurrences in
s
. Note that the maximal repeats, unlike the closed repeats, are not tied to specific positions in
s
. Clearly, every closed repeat is a maximal repeat (when treated as a separate string, not a substring). Further, every maximal repeat
t
has at least one occurrence in
s
that is a right (respectively, left) closed repeat; but
t
does not necessarily occur as a closed repeat, as the following example shows: the string
bacacab
has a maximal repeat

but all occurrences of
a
in
s
are not closed repeats.

Runs.

A
period
of a string

i\in[1\,..\,|t|{-}p]

is a substring

whose minimal period

is such that
2p\leq j-i+1
and neither
s[i{-}1\,..\,j]
(if

) nor

) have

p

n

(see
[

21
]

Maximal closed substrings.

A

t
. The border is

if it is not equal to
t

[
4

the following definition was proposed: a substring

i>1
) nor

Combinatorics of Closed Repeats

Perhaps, the most important fact about the (right/left) closed repeats is that there are at most
O(n\log n)
of them, which makes them a relatively compact structure that stores the information about all repeats, in a sense. This was essentially proved in
[
3
]
for the closed repeats using an algorithm computing all maximal closed substrings (the bound followed since the algorithm worked in
O(n\log n)
time). We provide a direct combinatorial proof adapted to the right/left closed repeats.

Theorem 1
.

Any string of length
n
contains at most
O(n\log n)
left closed repeats and right closed repeats.

Proof.

Consider a string
s
of length
n
. Denote by
T
the suffix tree of the string
s\$
, where
\$
is a special letter that does not occur in
s
. We call an edge
xy
of
T
, which connects two nodes
x
and
y
, light if
\size(x)\geq 2\cdot\size(y)
, where
\size(x)
denotes the number of leaves in the subtree rooted at
x
. Evidently, for any leaf
z
, the root–
z
path (i.e., the path from the root to
z
) has at most
\log n
light edges. Hence, there are at most
n\log n
pairs
(z,xy)
such that
z
is a leaf and
xy
is a light edge on the root–
z
path.

Consider a right closed repeat
s[i\,..\,j]
and its next occurrence
s[i^{\prime}\,..\,j^{\prime}]
. It is straightforward that the tree
T
has a node
x
such that the string
s[i\,..\,j]
is written on the root–
x
path and
x
has children
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
such that
s[j{+}1]
is the first letter written on the edge
xy_{1}
and
s[j^{\prime}{+}1]
is the first letter on
xy_{2}
(assuming
s[j^{\prime}{+}1]=\$
if
j^{\prime}=n
). Observe that at least one of the edges
xy_{1}
and
xy_{2}
must be light. Denote by
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
the leaves of
T
corresponding to the suffixes
s[i\,..\,n]
and
s[i^{\prime}\,..\,n]
, respectively. We associate the repeat
s[i\,..\,j]
with the pair
(z_{1},xy_{1})
if the edge
xy_{1}
is light, and with
(z_{2},xy_{2})
otherwise (in this case
xy_{2}
is light).

Given a pair
(z,xy)
such that
z
is a leaf and
xy
is a light edge on the root–
z
path, we claim that the pair could be associated with at most two right closed repeats. Denote by
t
the string written on the root–
x
path. Let
s[k\,..\,n]
be the suffix corresponding to
z
. The pair
(z,xy)
could be associated either with the right closed repeat
s[k\,..\,k{+}|t|)
(if any) or with a closed repeat
s[i\,..\,j]=t
whose next occurrence is
s[k\,..\,k{+}|t|)
. There is at most one such
s[i\,..\,j]
since
s[i\,..\,j]
must be the closest to
s[k\,..\,k{+}|t|)
occurrence of
t
that precedes
s[k\,..\,k{+}|t|)
. Thus, since there are at most
n\log n
such pairs
(z,xy)
, the number of right closed repeats is at most
2n\log n
.

For left closed repeats, one can observe that whenever
s[i\,..\,j]
is a left closed repeat and
s[i^{\prime}\,..\,j^{\prime}]
is its next occurrence, the substring of the reversed string
{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-4.30554pt\cr$\displaystyle s$\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-4.30554pt\cr$\textstyle s$\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-3.01389pt\cr$\scriptstyle s$\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{}\mkern 4.0mu$}\cr\kern-2.15277pt\cr$\scriptscriptstyle s$\cr}}}}=s[n]\cdots s[2]s[1]

In particular, Theorem
1
implies that the number of closed repeats is
O(n\log n)
, which was already proved in
[
3

(in terms of maximal closed substrings). This bound is tight due to the following result.

Theorem 2
.

The uniformly random binary string of length
n
has
\Omega(n\log n)
closed repeats in expectation.

Proof.

Consider a uniformly random binary string
s
of length
n
. For a position
i\in[2\,..\,n/2]
and an integer
b\in[1\,..\,\frac{1}{3}\log n]
, denote by
I(i,b)
an indicator random variable that is equal to
1
iff
s[i\,..\,i{+}b)
is a closed repeat in
s
. Then, the total number of closed repeats is at least
I=\sum_{i\in[2\,..\,n/2]}\sum_{b\in[1\,..\,\frac{1}{3}\log n]}I(i,b)
. We are to show that
\E[I(i,b)]>\frac{1}{8}
when
n
is large enough, which implies that
\E[I]\geq\Omega(n\log n)
and, thus, the random string has
\Omega(n\log n)
closed repeats in expectation.

Fix
i\in[2\,..\,n/2]
and
b\in[1\,..\,\frac{1}{3}\log n]
. It is well known that
1-1/k\leq e^{-1/k}
, for
k\geq 1
. Since the number of binary strings with length
b
is
2^{b}\leq\sqrt[3]{n}
, the probability that the string
s[i\,..\,i{+}b)
has no occurrences at positions
i{+}b,i{+}2b,i{+}3b,\ldots
(recall that
i\leq n/2
) is at most
(1-1/2^{b})^{\Theta(n/b)}\leq(1-1/\sqrt[3]{n})^{\Theta(n/b)}\leq(e^{-1/\sqrt[3]{n}})^{\Theta(n/b)}=e^{-\Theta(n^{2/3}/b)}
, which is upperbounded by
1/e^{\Theta(\sqrt{n})}
since
b<\log n
. Hence, with probability at least
1-1/e^{\Theta(\sqrt{n})}
, the string
s[i\,..\,i{+}b)
has an occurrence at one of the positions
i{+}1,i{+}2,\ldots
(the probability is taken for all random strings
s
with the fixed length
n
and the fixed
i
and
b
).

For integer
j>i
, denote by
E_{j}
the following event:
s[i\,..\,i{+}b)
occurs at position
j
but does not occur at positions
i{+}1,i{+}2,\ldots,j{-}1
. Evidently, the events
E_{j}
are mutually exclusive and
\sum_{j>i}\Pr(E_{j})
equals the probability that
s[i\,..\,i{+}b)
occurs at one of the positions
i{+}1,i{+}2,\ldots
, which, as was shown above, is at least
1-1/e^{\Theta(\sqrt{n})}
. Denote by
C
the event that
s[i\,..\,i{+}b)
is a closed repeat. Conditioned on the event
E_{j}
, the event
C
holds iff
s[i{-}1]\neq s[j{-}1]
(recall that
i>1
) and either
s[i{+}b]\neq s[j{+}b]
or
j{+}b>n
. Since, provided
E_{j}
happens, the letter
s[i{-}1]
is chosen independently of other letters, we have
s[i{-}1]\neq s[j{-}1]
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
; analogously, provided
E_{j}
happens and
j{+}b\leq n
,
s[j{+}b]
is independent of other letters and, thus,
s[i{+}b]\neq s[j{+}b]
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore,
\Pr(C|E_{j})=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{4}
, for
i<j\leq n{-}b
, and
\Pr(C|E_{j})=\frac{1}{2}
, for
j=n-b+1
. Applying the formula of total probability, we lowerbound the probability that
s[i\,..\,i{+}b)
is a closed repeat as follows:

\Pr(C)=\sum_{j>i}\Pr(C|E_{j})\cdot\Pr(E_{j})\geq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j>i}\Pr(E_{j})\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\frac{1}{e^{\Theta(\sqrt{n})}}\right).

We obtain that
I(i,b)=1
with probability at least
\frac{1}{4}(1-1/e^{\Theta(\sqrt{n})})
, which is greater than
\frac{1}{8}
when
n
is sufficiently large. Thus, we obtain
\E[I(i,b)]>\frac{1}{8}
as required.
∎

The definition of (right/left) closed repeats immediately implies the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

For a string
s
, let
s[i\,..\,i{+}t_{1}),\ldots,s[i\,..\,i{+}t_{k})
be all (right/left) closed repeats at position

and let
s[q_{1}\,..\,q_{1}{+}t_{1}),\ldots,s[q_{k}\,..\,q_{k}{+}t_{k})
be their corresponding next occurrences. Suppose that
t_{1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{k}
. Then,
q_{1}<\cdots<q_{k}
and
t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}
.

The following lemma somewhat details the character of growth for the sequence
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}
in Lemma

.

Lemma 2
.

For a string
s
, let
s[i\,..\,i{+}t_{1}),\ldots,s[i\,..\,i{+}t_{k})
be all (right) closed repeats at position
i
and let
s[q_{1}\,..\,q_{1}{+}t_{1}),\ldots,s[q_{k}\,..\,q_{k}{+}t_{k})
be their corresponding next occurrences. Suppose that
t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}
. Then, for any

, we have
q_{x+2}>q_{x}+t_{x}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
1
, we have
q_{1}<\cdots<q_{k}
. Assume that
q_{x+1}<q_{x}+t_{x}
(otherwise there is nothing to prove). Since the string
s[q_{x}\,..\,q_{x}{+}t_{x})
occurs at position
q_{x+1}
,
p=q_{x+1}-q_{x}
is a period of
s[q_{x}\,..\,q_{x}{+}t_{x})
. Further, since
t_{x+1}>t_{x}
, the definition of right closed repeats implies that the letters
s[q_{x}{+}t_{x}]
and
s[q_{x+1}{+}t_{x}]=s[q_{x}{+}p{+}t_{x}]
are distinct. Thus, any substring with period
p
cannot contain both
s[q_{x}{+}t_{x}]
and
s[q_{x}{+}p{+}t_{x}]
. In particular,
s[q_{x}\,..\,q_{x}{+}t_{x})
cannot occur at positions from
(q_{x}{+}p\,..\,q_{x}{+}t_{x}]=(q_{x+1}\,..\,q_{x}{+}t_{x}]
. Hence,
q_{x+2}>q_{x}+t_{x}
.
∎

4
Computation

In
[
3
]
an algorithm was proposed that computes all maximal closed substrings in a string of length
n
in
O(n\log n)
time using the suffix tree. On closer inspection, it is straightforward that this same algorithm also computes all closed repeats, due to the correspondence outlined in Section
2
, and all right/left closed repeats (in fact, it computes precisely the right closed repeats but filters out those of them that are not left closed). For the sake of brevity, we do not describe this simple modification here and refer the reader to
[
3
]
.

While the time
O(n\log n)
is optimal in the worst case when there are
\Theta(n\log n)
closed repeats in the string, it is natural to ask whether a better output-dependent algorithm with
O(n+c)
time is possible, where
c
is the number of (right/left) closed repeats. We investigate this problem under the assumption of
general ordered alphabet
where the alphabet is totally ordered and the only operation on two letters is the comparison that evaluates their relative order in
O(1)
time.

As it will be seen in Section
5
, the right closed repeats can be used to compute the so-called LZ77 parsing, which requires
\Omega(n\log\sigma)
time on the general ordered alphabet of size
\sigma
, as was proved in
[
12
,
24
]
. This observation suggests that any algorithm computing the closed repeats must contain a kind of indexing data structure, like the suffix tree, that takes
\Omega(n\log\sigma)
time to construct on the general alphabet. Unfortunately, the “hard” strings for the LZ77 parsing that were used in
[
12
,
24
]
contain
\Omega(n\log n)
closed repeats in the worst case. Hence, we prove the lower bound
\Omega(n\log\sigma)
using a different reduction.

Theorem 3
.

Any algorithm computing all closed repeats in strings of length
n
over a general ordered alphabet of size
\Theta(\sigma)
, for
\sigma\geq n^{\Omega(1)}
, spends
\Omega(n\log n)
time on at least one string containing
O(n)
closed repeats.

Proof.

We devise a reduction from the
Alphabet Set Testing
problem defined by Ellert
[
12
]
: given two disjoint alphabets
\Sigma=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{\sigma}\}
,
\Sigma^{\prime}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{\sigma}\}
, and a string
x=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{\bar{n}}\in(\Sigma\cup\Sigma^{\prime})^{\bar{n}}
, one must decide whether
x\in\Sigma^{\bar{n}}
. It was proved in
[
12
]
that this problem requires
\Omega(\bar{n}\log\sigma)
time in the worst case on the general alphabet. Let
\sigma\geq n^{1/(d-1)}
, for constant
d>1
. Consider
s=b_{1}\cdots b_{\sigma}x_{1}Z_{1}x_{2}Z_{2}\cdots x_{\bar{n}}Z_{\bar{n}}a_{1}\cdots a_{\sigma}
, where
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{\bar{n}}
are distinct strings, each of length
d-1
, over a new alphabet
\{\$_{1},\ldots,\$_{\sigma}\}
. We will put
\bar{n}\leq n
so that
\sigma^{d-1}\geq n\geq\bar{n}
to guarantee that there are at least
\bar{n}
distinct strings
Z_{i}
of length
d-1
. Since
x\in\Sigma^{\bar{n}}
iff each substring
x_{i}
in
s
is a closed repeat, the algorithm computing all closed repeats for
s
solves the Alphabet Set Testing. Since all
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{\bar{n}}
are distinct,
s
contains less than
2d|s|=O(|s|)
closed repeats. Further, we have
|s|=d\bar{n}+2\sigma
so that
|s|=n
whenever
\bar{n}=n/d-2\sigma/d
. We assume that
\sigma\leq n/4
and set
\bar{n}=\lfloor n/d-2\sigma/d\rfloor
, padding the string
s
arbitrarily to make its length
n
. Since
\sigma\leq n/4
, we have
n\geq\bar{n}\geq\lfloor n/(2d)\rfloor=\Omega(n)
. Thus, we obtain for the algorithm the lower bound
\Omega(\bar{n}\log\sigma)=\Omega(n\log\sigma)=\Omega(n\log n)
attained on a string over an alphabet of size
\Theta(\sigma)
with
O(n)
closed repeats.
∎

Theorem
3
contrasts with the known results about runs, which are a particular case of closed repeats: all runs can be computed in
O(n)
time on the general ordered alphabet, as was shown by Ellert and Fischer
[
13
]
. It remains open whether the (right/left) closed repeats can be computed in
O(n+c)
time over a linearly-sortable alphabet, where
c
is the number of closed repeats, or in
O(n\log\sigma+c)
time over the general ordered alphabet of size
\sigma
.

Given a string
s

[

]
is one of the key structures in data compression. Its rightmost variant is in focus of research

Given a substring
s[i\,..\,i{+}\ell)

6
Open Problems

The presented results naturally inspire a number of open problems.

1.

Can we find all (right/left) closed repeats in
O(n\log\sigma+c)
time over a general ordered alphabet of size
\sigma
, where
c
is the number of found closed repeats?

2.

Can we reduce the time to
O(n+c)
on linearly-sortable alphabets?

3.

Can the query time in the longest substring repeat problem be
O(\log\log n)
? It is natural to expect the improvement due to the specificity of range queries in the current solution.

4.

The
O(n\log n)
space required by closed repeats might be a bottleneck in many applications. As a partial cure, the following conjecture, if true, might help to devise more light-weight solutions for some problems: given
d\geq\Omega(\log^{2}n)
, the number of closed repeats with length at least
d
is
O(n)
.

5.

In a similar vein, we conjecture that, given a set of
k
positions
P\subseteq[1\,..\,n]
, there are at most
O(n+k\log n)
right closed repeats starting at positions from
P
.

6.

The algorithm from
[
3
]
that can be adapted to compute the (right/left) closed repeats is practical and relatively easily implementable. However, it requires the construction of the suffix tree for its work, which is not a problem
per se
but it suggests that, maybe, there can be a more direct algorithm that constructs the closed repeats without first building an indexing data structure like the suffix tree. We note, however, that, due to Theorem
3
, a certain kind of indexing is still unavoidable.
