The Frequency Reduced-Basis method: Reduced order models for time-dependent problems using the Laplace transform
Ricardo Reyes

Abstract
In this paper, we propose the Frequency Reduced-Basis method: a reduced-basis method to solve time-dependent partial differential equations based on the Laplace transform. Unlike traditional approaches, we begin by applying the Laplace transform to the evolution problem, yielding a time-independent boundary value problem that depends on the complex Laplace parameter. First, in an offline stage, we appropriately sample the Laplace parameter and solve the collection of problems using the finite element method. Next, we apply a Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) to this collection of solutions in order to obtain a reduced basis that is of dimension much smaller than that of the original solution space. This reduced basis, in turn, is then used to solve the evolution problem using any suitable time-stepping method. A key insight to justify the formulation of the method relies on Hardy spaces of analytic functions. By applying the Paley-Wiener theorem we can then define an isometry between the solution of the time-dependent problem and its Laplace transform. As a consequence of this result, one may conclude that computing a POD with samples taken in the Laplace domain produces an exponentially accurate reduced basis for the time-dependent problem. Numerical experiments characterizing the performance of the method, in terms of accuracy and speed-up, are included for a variety of relevant time-evolution equations.

Introduction

Solving multiple-query or forecasting problems often requires the solution of multiple parametric partial differential equations quickly and reliably. Unfortunately, numerical approximations are often either computationally expensive or insufficiently accurate. At this point, the development of surrogate models that are fast and reliable becomes necessary. Reduced basis methods are suitable surrogate models, where the original high-dimensional problem is replaced by a low-rank approximation. These methods consist of two stages: an offline part, where we construct a reduced basis from a collection of ‘high-fidelity’ solutions at selected sample points, and an online part, where we solve the low-dimensional problem defined as the projection of the original equation onto the subspace spanned by this reduced basis.

Reduced bases for time-dependent problems are usually constructed using a standard
POD

In problems where the solution can evolve without a clear pattern or at least without one that we can easily predict, we may need to sample data at a high frequency and over a long time window to be able to construct an appropriate reduced basis. If we also consider any other additional parametrization (for example physical or geometrical parameters), we may face issues sampling the data for large problems as they can become prohibitively expensive and time consuming.

For general parametrised problems, a substantial body of work applies a Greedy sampling technique

Although this is ideal for most parametric problems, the evolution behaviour of a time-dependent problem does not allow us to follow a random sampling in most cases. Therefore, when we deal with parametrised time-dependent problems, it is common to find techniques that have a mix of both methods: a Greedy approach to select the sample set in the parameter space, and either an impulse response

Thus, for real-world applications with computational and time constraints it is not always possible to construct an affordable and accurate reduced basis by following a traditional reduced-basis method.

We intend to explain why this method works using some elements of harmonic analysis. We start by defining the approximation spaces for each problem and the equivalence between their norms: a
L^{2}
Hilbert space for the time-domain problem and a
H^{2}
Hardy space for the frequency-domain problem. Then we map the frequency-domain problem, set in the complex half-plane, onto the unit circle. And lastly, we justify why we only use the real part of the solution as data input using the properties of analytic functions.

We validate the method using three numerical examples: a wave equation and a heat equation to evaluate convergence, efficiency and performance, and a advection problem to test stability.

1.1
Method Summary

In this work, we introduce the
Frequency Reduced-Basis (FRB)
method: a model reduction technique for time-dependent partial differential equations. The method uses the Laplace transform and the
POD
to build a model reduction framework that operates in the frequency domain during the offline stage and in the time domain during the online stage.

Offline stage (frequency domain)

We apply the Laplace transform to the time-dependent PDE, which yields a parametric elliptic boundary value problem in the complex Laplace domain. We then evaluate the solution at a selected set of complex frequencies and collect the corresponding snapshots. Finally, we construct a reduced basis using a
Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
of these frequency-domain solutions.

Online stage (time domain)

We project the original time-dependent PDE onto the reduced subspace, following the standard reduced-basis procedure. Finally, we solve the resulting low-dimensional system using a suitable time integration scheme.

This approach yields several advantages:

•

Time discretization is decoupled from basis construction, reducing sensitivity to high temporal resolution.

•

Efficient parallelization in the offline stage due to the independence of frequency-domain solutions.

•

Robustness for wave-like solutions, as the frequency-domain representation can capture oscillatory modes and long-time behaviour with fewer samples.

(3.8)

with initial conditions

In the same way as for the high fidelity solution in

We set

(Reduced fully-discrete problem)

4.1
The Laplace Transform and the Hardy space

First, let us introduce an appropriate space for
\widehat{f}
. We set

\displaystyle\mathbbs{C}_{\alpha}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{s\in\mathbb{C}:\,\Re(s)>\alpha\}.

(4.1)

where
\alpha
ensures that the singularities of
\widehat{f}
are to the left of the semiplane
\mathbbs{C}_{\alpha}
.

Definition 1

(Hardy space in
\mathbbs{C}_{\alpha}
)
.

We denote
H^{p}(\mathbbs{C}_{\alpha},\mathbb{V})
the space of holomorphic functions
\widehat{f}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
in the positive half complex plane. For
1\leq p\leq\infty
and any Banach space
\mathbb{V}
we define the norm

\lVert\widehat{f}\rVert_{H^{p}(\mathbbs{C}_{\alpha},\mathbb{V})}=\sup_{\sigma>\alpha}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lVert\widehat{f}(\sigma+\imath\tau)\rVert_{\mathbb{V}}^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}<\infty

(4.2)

Proposition 1
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\alpha>0
. For
f\in H^{p}(\mathbbs{C}_{\alpha},\mathbb{V})
, the function

T(\sigma,f)=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\lVert\widehat{f}(\sigma+\imath\tau)\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{V}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(4.3)

is continuous monotone decreasing function of
\sigma
for
\sigma\geq\alpha
.
In particular,
T(\alpha,f)=\lVert f\rVert^{p}_{H^{p}(\mathbbs{C}_{\alpha},\mathbb{V})}
and
\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow\infty}T(\sigma,f)=0
.

Proposition 2
.

The function
\widehat{f}(\sigma+\imath\tau)
has boundary values for
\widehat{f}_{\alpha}(\imath\tau)=\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow\alpha^{+}}\widehat{f}(\sigma+\imath\tau)
almost everywhere. The boundary function
\widehat{f}_{\alpha}
lies in
L^{p}(\imath\mathbbs{R},\mathbb{V})
and satisfies
\lVert\widehat{f}_{\alpha}\rVert_{L^{p}(\imath\mathbbs{R},\mathbb{V})}=\lVert\widehat{f}\rVert_{H^{p}(\mathbbs{C}_{\alpha},\mathbb{V})}
. By identifying
\widehat{f}
with
\widehat{f}_{\alpha}(\imath\tau)
,
H^{p}(\mathbbs{C}_{\alpha},\mathbb{V})
can be regarded as a closed subspace of
L^{p}(\imath\mathbbs{R},\mathbb{V})
.

4.2
Isometry between time and frequency domains

Theorem 1

(Paley-Wiener)
.

\widehat{f}\in L^{2}(\imath\mathbbs{R})
if and only if there exists a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbbs{R})
such that
\widehat{f}(s)=\mathcal{L}(f)
.
We define
L^{2}(\mathbbs{R})=L^{2}(-\infty,0)\oplus L^{2}(\mathbbs{R}_{+})
by decomposing the function into its values
t<0
and
t>0
. Then, by this decomposition we see

(L^{2}(\mathbbs{R})=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-})\oplus L^{2}(\mathbbs{R}_{+}))\xrightarrow{\makebox[14.22636pt]{$\mathcal{L}$}}(L^{2}(i\mathbbs{R})=H^{2}(\mathbbs{C}_{-})\oplus H^{2}(\mathbbs{C}_{+})).

(4.4)

We can define the Laplace transform as the map
\mathcal{L}:L^{2}(\mathbbs{R}_{+};\mathbb{V})\mapsto H^{2}(\mathbbs{C}_{+};\mathbb{V})
.

Corollary 1
.

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{V},\alpha)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\{f\}\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\mathbbs{C}_{\alpha},\mathbb{V})}

(4.5)

Proof.

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbbs{R}_{+},\mathbb{V},\alpha)
with
\alpha>0
we set

. Recalling the properties of the Laplace transform
we have that

\mathcal{L}\{f_{\alpha}\}(s)=\mathcal{L}\{f\}(s+\alpha),\quad\Re\{s\}>0.

(4.6)

We observe that
f_{\alpha}\in L^{2}(\mathbbs{R}_{+},\mathbb{V})
. Hence, it follows from
theorem

1
that

\lVert f_{\alpha}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{V})}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\{f_{\alpha}\}\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\mathbbs{C}_{+},\mathbb{V})}.

(4.7)

By shifting the vertical integration line in the definition of

H^{2}(\mathbbs{C}_{\alpha},V)
and recalling (
4.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\mathcal{L}\{f_{\alpha}\}\rVert_{H^{2}(\mathbbs{C}_{+},\mathbb{V})}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\sigma>0}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{-\infty}\lVert\mathcal{L}\{f_{\alpha}\}(\sigma+\imath\tau)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{V}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(4.8)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\sigma>0}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{-\infty}\lVert\mathcal{L}\{f\}(\sigma+\alpha+\imath\tau)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{V}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\sigma>\alpha}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{-\infty}\lVert\mathcal{L}\{f\}(\sigma+\imath\tau)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{V}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathcal{L}\{f\}\rVert_{H^{2}(\mathbbs{C}_{\alpha},\mathbb{V})}.

From
equation

2.2
, we also know that

\lVert f_{\alpha}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{V})}=\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{V},\alpha)}.

(4.9)

By combining
equation

4.9
and
equation

4.8
, together with
equation

4.7
, one obtains the final result.
∎

4.3
Hardy spaces in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
and the unit circle
\mathbb{T}

Since the behaviour of
\widehat{f}_{\alpha}
can be highly oscillatory as

and therefore

would be difficult to compute, we apply a transformation to map

to a new complex variable that has a better behaviour. Let
\mathbb{D}=\{\omega\in\mathbbs{C}:\lVert\omega\rVert<1\}
denote the unit disk in the complex plane and
\mathbb{T}=\{\omega\in\mathbbs{C}:\lVert\omega\rVert=1=\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}:0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi\}

in the half-plane are mapped to the exterior of the unit disk

we extend the sampling to higher frequencies (approaching

in the Bromwich integral) and therefore we should carefully choose this parameter in function of the mesh size and other parameters that we discuss later on.

Let us now define the appropriate space for the function

in the unit disk and some theorems regarding the norm and the interoperability between the Hardy space in the disk and the half plane.

Definition 2

(Hardy space in
\mathbb{D}
)
.

We denote

the space of holomorphic functions
\widehat{g}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{V}

(4.10)

Theorem 2

(Fatou’s theorem)
.

The functions
\widehat{g}\in H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
have a radial limit

\widehat{g}_{\circ}(\mathrm{e}^{i\theta})=\lim_{r\rightarrow 1}\widehat{g}(r\mathrm{e}^{i\theta})

that exist almost everywhere in
\mathbb{T}

. We also identify
\widehat{g}
with
\widehat{g}_{\circ}
, setting
H^{p}(\mathbb{D},\mathbb{V})
as a closed subspace of
L^{p}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{V})
and a Banach space. A proof of this theorem is available in
[
33
]

Theorem 3
.

A function
\widehat{g}
defined on
\mathbb{T}
is in

if and only if the function
\widehat{f}_{\alpha}:\imath\mathbbs{R}\mapsto\mathbbs{C}
defined by

\widehat{f}_{\alpha}(s)=\frac{\beta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}(\beta-\alpha+s)^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{p}}}}\widehat{g}(\mathcal{M}(s))

(4.11)

is in
L^{p}(\imath\mathbbs{R},\mathbb{V})
. Setting
\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\omega)=s=\frac{2\beta}{1-\omega}+\alpha-\beta
we also verify

\widehat{g}(\omega)=\frac{(4\beta\pi)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}}{(1-\omega)^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{p}}}}\widehat{f}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\omega)),

(4.12)

and moreover
\lVert\widehat{f}_{\alpha}\rVert_{L^{p}(\imath\mathbbs{R},\mathbb{V})}=\lVert\widehat{g}\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{V})}
.

Proof.

We follow a similar proof as the one in
[
29
]
, for a similar Möbius transformation. First we check that
\lvert\omega\rvert<1
if and only if

, and that

. Then we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widehat{f}_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\imath\mathbbs{R},\mathbb{V})}^{p}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\lVert\widehat{f}(s)\right\rVert^{p}_{\mathbb{V}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=-\imath\int_{\imath\mathbbs{R}}\frac{\beta}{\pi\lvert\beta-\alpha+s\rvert^{2}}\left\lVert\widehat{g}(\mathcal{M}(s))\right\rVert^{p}_{\mathbb{V}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.13)

Using
\mathcal{M}^{-1}(s)
, we replace
\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\mathrm{d}\omega}=-\frac{2\beta}{(1-\omega)^{2}}
, and
\beta-\alpha+s=\frac{2\beta}{1-\omega}
. Noting that
\frac{\lvert 1-\omega\rvert^{2}}{(1-\omega)^{2}}=-\frac{1}{\omega}
for

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widehat{f}_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\imath\mathbbs{R},\mathbb{V})}^{p}=-\imath\int_{\lvert\omega\rvert=1}\frac{\lvert 1-\omega\rvert^{2}\beta}{4\beta^{2}\pi}\left\lVert\widehat{g}(\omega)\right\rVert^{p}_{\mathbb{V}}\frac{2\beta}{(1-\omega)^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\imath\int_{\lvert\omega\rvert=1}\frac{1}{\omega}\left\lVert\widehat{g}(\omega)\right\rVert^{p}_{\mathbb{V}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega.

(4.14)

Lastly, writing

and

, we get

\left\lVert\widehat{f}_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\imath\mathbbs{R},\mathbb{V})}^{p}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\imath\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\imath\mathrm{e}^{\imath\theta}}{\mathrm{e}^{\imath\theta}}\left\lVert\widehat{g}(\mathrm{e}^{\imath\theta})\right\rVert^{p}_{\mathbb{V}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left\lVert\widehat{g}(\mathrm{e}^{\imath\theta})\right\rVert^{p}_{\mathbb{V}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta.

(4.15)

∎

Corollary 2
.

Let

Proof.

A direct consequence of

Definition 3

(iii)

denotes the inverse Fourier-Laplace transform

of

using the scope of differential geometry.
Let us write the reduced solution in the frequency domain solution space
\widehat{u}_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\mathbbs{C},\textrm{R}}
in terms of its real and imaginary parts as
\widehat{u}_{h}^{\textrm{R}}=\Re\{\widehat{u}_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}+\imath\Im\{\widehat{u}_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}
. Since in this section we work with both real and complex-valued spaces
\mathbb{V}_{h}
, we indicate them as
\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\mathbbs{R}}
and
\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\mathbbs{C}}
. Using the results from
theorems

4
and
3
, let us also consider the following matrix representation of the complex-valued reduced solution

\widehat{u}_{h}\approx\Phi{C}\widehat{u}_{h}^{\textrm{R}}=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi^{\Re}&-\Phi^{\Im}\\
\Phi^{\Im}&\Phi^{\Re}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{\widehat{u}_{h}\}\\
\Im\{\widehat{u}_{h}\}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi^{\Re}&-\Phi^{\Im}\\
\Phi^{\Im}&\Phi^{\Re}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{\widehat{u}_{h}\}\\
\mathcal{H}\Re\{\widehat{u}_{h}\}\end{bmatrix},

(4.29)

with
\Phi^{\Re}
and
\Phi^{\Im}
the real and imaginary parts of the complex-valued basis
\Phi{C}
. Note that by using
equation

4.20
we construct the complex-valued basis
\Phi{C}
, as we do it by solving an
SVD
of a complex-value data set. As we discussed in this section, and as a direct consequence of
theorems

1
and
2
the reduced basis we are constructing is a basis for both the frequency-domain and the time-domain problems. Thus, we also write the real-value reduced solution for the time domain as

u_{h}\approx\Phi{C}u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi^{\Re}&-\Phi^{\Im}\\
\Phi^{\Im}&\Phi^{\Re}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}\\
\Im\{u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi^{\Re}&-\Phi^{\Im}\\
\Phi^{\Im}&\Phi^{\Re}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}\\
\mathcal{H}\Re\{u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}\end{bmatrix}.

(4.30)

Since we know that
u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}
in the time-dependent problem is real-valued, we get

\Phi^{\Im}\Re\{u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}+\Phi^{\Re}\Im\{u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}=\Phi^{\Im}\Re\{u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}+\Phi^{\Re}\mathcal{H}\Re\{u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}=0.

(4.31)

This matrix representation implies that the complex
n_{s}
-dimensional space
\mathbb{V}{C}_{h}
, can be defined using a real space
\mathbb{V}{R}_{h}
. To explain this let us start by writing some basic definitions and theorems used in holomorphic differential geometry, for which we follow
[
41
, chapter 4]
and
[
42
]
.

Definition 4

(
\mathcal{H}
as the linear complex structure of
\mathbb{V}_{h}{R}
)
.

We define a linear complex structure
\mathcal{J}
on a real vector space
\mathbb{V}{R}
as the endomorphism
\mathcal{J}:\mathbb{V}_{h}{R}\to\mathbb{V}_{h}{R}
such that
\mathcal{J}\cdot\mathcal{J}=-I
, with
I
the identity.
Noting that by definition the Hilbert transform is an anti-involution
\mathcal{H}^{2}=-I
, we define the endomorphism

\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{H}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\mathcal{H}\\
\mathcal{H}&0\end{bmatrix}

(4.32)

as a linear complex structure on
\mathbb{V}_{h}{R}
.

Definition 5

(Real structure of
\mathbb{V}_{h}{C}
)
.

A real structure on a complex vector space
\mathbb{V}{C}
is an antilinear map
\varsigma:\mathbb{V}_{h}{C}\to\mathbb{V}_{h}{C}
such that
\varsigma\cdot\varsigma=I
. Let us write any
u\in\mathbb{V}_{h}{C}
as the sum of
\widehat{u}=\widehat{u}_{\Re}+\widehat{u}_{\Im}=\frac{1}{2}(\widehat{u}+\varsigma\widehat{u})+\frac{1}{2}(\widehat{u}-\varsigma\widehat{u})
. This way, we define the subspaces

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\Re}=\{\widehat{u}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}{C}|\varsigma\widehat{u}=\widehat{u}^{*}\},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\Im}=\{\widehat{u}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}{C}|\varsigma\widehat{u}=-\widehat{u}^{*}\}

(4.33)

with
\widehat{u}^{*}
the complex conjugate of
\widehat{u}
. Note that since
\widehat{u}_{\Re}
and
\widehat{u}_{\Im}
are orthogonal, then
\dim{R}\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\Re}=\dim{R}\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\Im}=\dim{C}\mathbb{V}_{h}{C}
.

Definition 6

(Complexification and realification)
.

For a real vector space
\mathbb{V}_{h}{R}
we may define its complexification by extension of scalars and for a complex vector space
\mathbb{V}_{h}{C}
we may define its realification by the restriction of scalars. We get the relation
\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\mathbbs{C}}=\mathbbs{C}\otimes_{\mathbbs{R}}\mathbb{V}_{h}{R}
.

Proposition 4

(Basis representation of
\mathbb{V}_{h}{C}
and
\mathbb{V}_{h}{R}
)
.

Let
\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n_{s}}\}
be a basis of
\mathbb{V}_{h}{C}
. Then,

\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n_{s}},\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{H}}(\varphi_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{H}}(\varphi_{n_{s}})\}

forms a real basis for
\mathbb{V}_{h}{R}
. Note that
\dim{R}\mathbb{V}_{h}{R}=2\dim{C}\mathbb{V}_{h}{C}
.

Proposition 5

(Totally real subspace)
.

Let
\mathbb{V}_{h}
be a finite-dimensional
\mathbbs{R}
-vector space with linear complex structure
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{H}}
. A subspace
\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\mathbbs{R}}\subseteq\mathbb{V}_{h}
is totally real if
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\mathbbs{R}})\cap\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\mathbbs{R}}=\{0\}
. The next two conditions are equivalent

•

\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\mathbbs{R}}
is a totally real subspace.

•

\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\mathbbs{R}})
and
\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\mathbbs{R}}
are orthogonal in the inner product sense.

Corollary 4
.

Let
\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\textrm{R}}
be a reduced space for the time dependent problem and let
\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n_{s}}\}
be a real-valued reduced basis of
\mathbb{V}_{h}{R}
, then
\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n_{s}},\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{H}}(\phi_{i}),\ldots,\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{H}}(\phi_{n_{r}})\}
is a basis of
\mathbb{V}_{h}{C}
.

Proof.

Let
v^{\textrm{R}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{V}_{h}{R})
. Then, to prove the orthogonality between
\phi_{j}
and
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{H}}(\phi_{j})
we can show that any
v^{\textrm{R}}_{j}
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{H}}(v^{\textrm{R}}_{j})
. Noting that
\mathcal{H}
is anti-self adjoint we write

\left\langle\mathcal{H}(v_{j}),v_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathcal{H}^{2}(v_{j}),\mathcal{H}(v_{j})\right\rangle=\left\langle v_{j},-\mathcal{H}(v_{j})\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathcal{H}(v_{j}),v_{j}\right\rangle=0.

(4.34)

Using
proposition

5
we see that
\mathbb{V}_{h}{R}
is a totally real subspace and can be described with
\Phi^{\Re}
. We check that for
equation

4.31
we satisfy
\Phi^{\Im}\Re\{u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}+\Phi^{\Re}\Im\{u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}=0
is by assuming that
\Phi^{\Re}
and
\Phi^{\Im}
are orthogonal, and that
\Re\{u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}\in\spn\{\phi_{1}^{\Re},\ldots,\phi_{n_{r}}^{\Re}\}
and
\Im\{u_{h}^{\textrm{R}}\}\in\spn\{\phi_{1}^{\Im},\ldots,\phi_{n_{r}}^{\Im}\}
.
∎

So far we have described real and complex vector spaces. Here it is clear that
\mathbb{V}_{h}{C}
represents the frequency-domain solution space, but there is not an unique way to construct a real space or obtain a real-valued reduced solution. Moreover, we see that the spaces
\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\Re}
and
\mathbb{V}_{h}{R}
are not equivalent.

This non-equivalence follows directly from the structure of the snapshot spaces. Given a set of complex-valued snapshots
\hat{u}(s_{j})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we define the complex reduced space
V_{h}^{\mathbb{C}}:=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\hat{u}(s_{j})\}
, and its realification corresponds to a real subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. In contrast, the space
V_{h}^{\Re}:=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\Re\{\hat{u}(s_{j})\}\}
lies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and does not include the imaginary components. Although the imaginary parts
\Im\{\hat{u}(s_{j})\}
are functionally related to the real parts via the Hilbert transform due to analyticity (as noted in
corollary

3
), this relation is non-linear and does not preserve span equivalence in the finite-dimensional
POD
setting.

Consequently, the reduced space
V_{h}^{\Re}
cannot reproduce the full solution space captured by
V_{h}^{\mathbb{C}}
, even though it may yield an effective approximation in practice. To recover a real-valued basis that faithfully represents the original complex space, we introduce the symplectic lifting approach in the next section, which constructs a real space in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
that is isomorphic to the complex snapshot span.

Definition 7

(Symplectic lift)
.

Given a complex vector
\hat{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we define its
symplectic lift
as the real vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
given by

\mathcal{L}_{S}(\hat{u}):=\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{\hat{u}\}\\
\Im\{\hat{u}\}\end{bmatrix}.

(4.35)

We extend this definition component-wise to a set of snapshots
\{\hat{u}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{s}}
to obtain the real-valued snapshot matrix

S_{S}:=\left[\mathcal{L}_{S}(\hat{u}_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{S}(\hat{u}_{n_{s}})\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times n_{s}}.

(4.36)

Using
definitions

7
and
4
in
\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{S}}}
(
equation

3.4
), and replacing
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Re\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}_{j}\})=\Im\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}_{j}\}
as per its definition. We obtain the rearranged snapshot set

\bm{\mathsf{S}}^{S}=\{\Re\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}_{1}\},\ldots,\Re\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}_{n_{s}}\},\Im\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}_{1}\},\ldots,\Im\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}_{n_{s}}\}\}

(4.37)

Now, if we compute the
SVD
and obtain a basis for this rearranged set, we are effectively constructing a symplectic basis. This technique is the same as the one presented in
[
20
]
for Hamiltonian problems as the cotangent lift method.

Theorem 5

(Cotangent lift method)
.

Let
\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}
be the frequency discrete solutions and
\bm{\mathsf{S}}^{S}
the data set as described in
equation

4.37
. The solution of the minimization problem in
equation

4.20
is given by
\bm{\mathsf{S}}^{S}=\Phi^{S}\Sigma(\Psi^{S})^{*}
, which results in the symplectic basis

\Phi^{S}=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi&0\\
0&\Phi\end{bmatrix}

(4.38)

which is the optimal projection of the sample set
\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{S}}}
onto the column space of the symplectic vector space
\mathbbs{R}^{2n_{s}}
.

Proof.

Writing
equation

4.20
in terms of the real and imaginary parts, we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}\}\\
\Im\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}\}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\Phi&0\\
0&\Phi\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Phi^{\top}&0\\
0&\Phi^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}\}\\
\Im\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}\}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\left(I_{n_{s}}-\Phi\Phi^{\top}\right)\Re\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}\}\\
\left(I_{n_{s}}-\Phi\Phi^{\top}\right)\Im\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}\}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

(4.39)

\displaystyle\quad=\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}I_{n_{s}}-\Phi\Phi^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}\}&\Im\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}\}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}\}&\Im\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}\}\end{bmatrix}-\Phi\Phi^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}\}&\Im\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}\}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

(4.40)

giving the minimization

\Phi^{S}=\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Phi^{S}\in\mathbbs{R}^{n_{h}\times n_{r}}:\\
(\Phi^{S})^{\top}\Phi^{S}=\bm{I}_{n_{r}\times n_{r}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{s}}w_{j}\left\lVert\left[\Re\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}(s_{j})\},\Im\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}(s_{j})\}\right]-\sum_{k=1}^{n_{r}}\left(\phi_{k}^{S},\left[\Re\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}(s_{j})\},\Im\{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}(s_{j})\}\right]\right)\phi_{k}^{S}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{V}_{h})}

(4.41)

∎

Remark 1
.

The equivalence between the complex-valued basis, the symplectic basis, and the real-only
POD
basis can be understood geometrically. The symplectic lift maps the complex vector space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
into the real vector space
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, endowing it with both a canonical symplectic form and a compatible complex structure. When the lifted snapshot matrix is used to compute a real basis, the resulting space naturally preserves both phase and amplitude information, just as the complex basis does.
This triplet of structures —orthogonal inner product, symplectic form, and complex structure— defines a Kähler manifold in the reduced space. In this setting, the symplectic basis can be viewed as a real coordinate representation of the complex basis.

Since our goal is to obtain accurate reduced-order models at low computational cost, it is natural to consider real-valued formulations. Among these, the symplectic construction of the reduced basis
\Phi^{S}
offers the best alternative: it avoids complex-valued arithmetic, it is straightforward to implement using standard real-valued linear algebra routines, and its performance comparable to the complex-valued basis.

Computational trade-offs.

Both
FRB
and standard time-domain reduced-basis methods use Galerkin projection in the online stage, so their online complexity is essentially identical. The main difference lies in the offline stage.

For standard reduced-basis, constructing the basis requires solving the full-order time-dependent problem over
n_{t}
time steps, resulting in a total cost of approximately
\mathcal{O}(n_{t}\cdot n_{h}^{3})
when using direct solvers, where
n_{h}
denotes the number of spatial degrees of freedom. In contrast, the
FRB
method constructs the basis by solving
n_{s}
independent elliptic problems in the frequency domain, each of cost
\mathcal{O}(n_{h}^{3})
. The total offline complexity for
FRB
is thus
\mathcal{O}(n_{s}\cdot n_{h}^{3})
.

This represents a significant reduction in offline cost when
n_{s}\ll n_{t}
, particularly since the
FRB
solves are fully parallelisable. Moreover, because the reduced basis is constructed independently of the time discretization, the
FRB
method is highly adaptable to different time integration schemes.

Remark 2
.

The numerical robustness of the
FRB
method in convection-dominated regimes is notable, as no additional stabilization is applied to the reduced-order model. This behaviour can be partly attributed to the structural properties of the Laplace-transformed formulation. By converting the original time-dependent problem into a parametric elliptic problem in the frequency domain, the method avoids direct time integration and its associated stability constraints.

In contrast to time-domain reduced-basis methods, which rely on real-valued snapshots that may contain localized transients or numerical noise, the
FRB
method constructs the basis from smooth, globally defined frequency-domain solutions. These solutions correspond to elliptic problems parametrized by complex frequencies, and their spatial structure often reflects integrated, steady-state-like behaviour. As a result, the reduced basis inherits a degree of regularity that improves its capacity to represent advective features without oscillation or overshoot. Moreover, the use of frequency-domain solutions inherently captures oscillatory modes and long-time behaviour, which are typically challenging to resolve using real-valued time snapshots.

While a full theoretical justification for robustness in the hyperbolic or high-Péclet regime is beyond the scope of this work, the numerical results suggest that the
FRB
formulation remains stable without additional stabilization mechanisms, even in cases where traditional
POD
-Galerkin methods may require them.

6
Summary

We propose the
Frequency Reduced-Basis
method, a model reduction technique for time-dependent problems based on sampling and compression in the Laplace domain. The method follows a traditional
POD
but with an important distinction: the sampling is done over the frequency domain. In the paper we provided a path for the derivation of the method following some theorems of harmonic analysis. We also show an interpretation of the constructed basis and the equivalences using some concepts of differential geometry.
The most important features of this method are the following:

•

As the method is based on the norm equivalence between time and frequency domains, the obtained reduced basis is a basis for both problems.

•

The solutions obtained with this reduced basis maintain good accuracy while keeping the computational cost below the high fidelity solution, even when including the sampling and construction of the basis.

•

Since we are not modifying the
POD
and the Laplace transform is a linear map, any extension of the reduced basis method proposed for stationary problems can be implemented. For example parametric reduced basis, local basis, etc.

•

Problems with linear transformations in the source term have an equivalent reduced basis. For example, we can think of problems where the source term is time shifted, in which case the Laplace transform is invariant.

•

Initial conditions behave as right hand side terms in the frequency domain problem, so any linear transformation on them results in equivalent reduced bases.

•

The
FRB
method is compatible with any time-stepping scheme, as the reduced basis is constructed independently of the time discretization.

Limitations and Outlook.

While the
FRB
method provides clear advantages in terms of sampling efficiency and phase accuracy, it is inherently limited to problems for which the Laplace transform yields a well-posed elliptic surrogate. Non-linear systems, or problems with strongly time-dependent coefficients or memory effects, fall outside the scope of the current framework. Extending the method to such settings remains an important direction for future work.
