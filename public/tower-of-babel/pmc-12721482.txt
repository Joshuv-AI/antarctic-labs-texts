High‐ and low‐dose topiramate for the treatment of persons with alcohol use disorder who smoke cigarettes: A randomized control trial
Authors: Jason D Robinson, Robert M Anthenelli, Paul M Cinciripini, Maher Karam‐Hage, Yong Cui, George Kypriotakis, Nassima Ait‐Daoud Tiouririne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTobacco use disorder (TUD) and alcohol use disorder (AUD) frequently co‐occur and together are associated with worse prognoses. There are few options for treating both disorders concurrently. This study examined whether the antiepileptic drug topiramate would be effective for treating adults with both AUD and TUD.MethodsParticipants (n = 236) from three sites were randomly assigned to receive treatment adherence counseling and either placebo, low‐dose topiramate (up to 125 mg/day), or high‐dose topiramate (up to 250 mg/day) for up to 18 weeks, with a 5‐week titration period. The primary outcomes were the mean percentage of heavy drinking days (PHDD) and the smoking continuous abstinence rate with biochemical verification during the last 4 weeks of treatment. Secondary outcomes included quality of life and craving for alcohol and cigarettes. We also conducted post hoc exploratory repeated measures analyses to maximize the use of available data.ResultsNone of the prespecified primary or secondary outcomes differed across medication groups. However, the exploratory analyses indicated that participants treated with the 250 mg dose had lower mean PHDD and drinks per day than those taking the lower dose or placebo. Additionally, participants in both the 125 and 250 mg groups smoked fewer cigarettes per day and reported greater cigarette abstinence than those in the placebo group.ConclusionsWhile the primary analyses did not find evidence that topiramate decreases drinking and smoking behavior, likely influenced by a high attrition rate and poor medication adherence, exploratory repeated measures analyses suggest that topiramate 250 mg reduces drinking behavior and that both the 125 mg and 250 mg doses reduce smoking behavior. Future studies that maximize treatment adherence are needed to more definitively determine whether topiramate is an effective treatment for AUD and TUD.Keywords: alcohol use disorder, quality of life, tobacco use disorder, topiramate, withdrawal

INTRODUCTION

Tobacco use disorder (TUD) frequently co‐occurs among those with alcohol use disorder (AUD) and is associated with heavier drinking and psychiatric comorbidities (Moss et al., 2015). One reason for this association is that there are interactions between alcohol and nicotine that might increase use of both (Littleton & Little, 2002), including evidence that the pharmacokinetics (Blomqvist et al., 2002; Seaton & Vesell, 1993) and pharmacodynamics (Morel et al., 2019) of both compounds might be mutually reinforcing. The concurrent use of tobacco and alcohol has a wide range of adverse health outcomes beyond the additive effects of each substance alone, including an increased risk of cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and respiratory illnesses (Hurley et al., 2012). Thus, it is clinically advantageous if both disorders can be treated together.

However, despite the evidence for substantial neurochemical and biological overlap between AUD and TUD, there are few effective options for treating both disorders concurrently using a single medication. Varenicline, a nicotinic acetylcholine receptor partial agonist that is highly effective for treating TUD (Livingstone‐Banks et al., 2023), has been evaluated in many clinical trials designed to treat AUD, but a meta‐analysis found mixed results in terms of reducing drinking behaviors compared to placebo (Phimarn et al., 2023). One study evaluated the impact of varenicline on comorbid AUD and TUD and found that it was effective in reducing heavy drinking days only for men (O'Malley et al., 2018), while another study found that combining naltrexone with varenicline did not significantly decrease drinking or smoking behaviors compared to varenicline and placebo (Ray et al., 2021). The opioid antagonist naltrexone, a moderately effective treatment for AUD (Rösner et al., 2010), has not been found to reduce smoking behavior among those with comorbid AUD and TUD (Guo et al., 2021). Identifying a single effective pharmacotherapy for those with both AUD and TUD may reduce relapse, adverse events, and costs and increase treatment adherence.

One such co‐treatment medication candidate is the antiepileptic topiramate, a sulfamate‐substituted fructopyranose derivative with both GABAergic and glutamatergic properties. It antagonizes both α‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) and kainate glutamatergic receptors (Gibbs et al., 2000; Skradski & White, 2000). The antagonism of these receptors has been found to reduce dopamine (DA) release in the nucleus accumbens from nicotine (Kenny et al., 2009; Kosowski et al., 2004; Schilström et al., 1997; Sziráki et al., 2002) and alcohol (Xiao et al., 2009). Reduction of mesolimbic DA levels has been associated with decreased self‐administration of nicotine (Corrigall & Coen, 1991) and alcohol (Rassnick et al., 1992) in animals and decreased cue‐elicited craving in individuals with AUD (Modell et al., 1993).

In terms of clinical outcomes, topiramate has been shown to reduce alcohol consumption among individuals with AUD (Johnson et al., 2007; Kranzler et al., 2014; Kranzler et al., 2021). In addition to its effects on alcohol consumption (Johnson, Ait‐Daoud, et al., 2003), secondary analyses indicated that individuals treated with topiramate exhibited higher rates of smoking abstinence compared to those given a placebo (Johnson et al., 2005). These promising effects on smoking cessation have been further supported by other studies (Baltieri et al., 2009; Khazaal et al., 2006; Oncken et al., 2014). However, the effectiveness of topiramate for individuals undergoing concurrent treatment for both AUD and TUD remains unclear. A recent study reported no significant differences between topiramate and placebo in facilitating smoking cessation or preventing alcohol relapse among men who smoked while their AUD was in remission (Anthenelli et al., 2017). Secondary analyses of these data (Isgro et al., 2017) indicated that topiramate reduced smoking in a specific AUD subtype (Type B; i.e., those with earlier onset and greater dysfunction; Babor et al., 1992). Combined with evidence showing topiramate's effects on treating alcohol and smoking individually, the mixed findings for the concurrent treatment in smaller samples and/or post hoc subgroup analyses highlight the need for further investigation into topiramate's role in treating co‐occurring AUD and TUD in a prospective larger scale clinical trial.

The primary objectives of this study were to assess the efficacy and tolerability of both a lower (125 mg per day) and higher (250 mg) dose of topiramate compared with placebo in reducing heavy drinking and cigarette smoking behaviors in persons motivated to try to quit using both substances. We hypothesized that participants in both the low‐ and high‐dose topiramate groups would exhibit (1) greater reductions in the percentage of heavy drinking days (PHDD) and (2) higher continuous abstinence rates from smoking during weeks 14–18 (i.e., the last 4 weeks) of active treatment compared with placebo. We also hypothesized that high‐dose topiramate would be more efficacious than low‐dose topiramate at reducing PHDD and increasing the continuous abstinence rate from smoking during this time period. Secondary objectives included assessing the impact of topiramate dose on quality of life and on alcohol and nicotine craving across weeks 14–18. We hypothesized that high‐dose topiramate would be more efficacious than low‐dose topiramate at improving quality of life and reducing craving in weeks 14–18. We also conducted post hoc exploratory analyses on these and other measures of substance use, dependence, and withdrawal.

METHODS

Participants

Participants were 236 adults who met criteria for AUD and TUD recruited from three metropolitan areas in the United States, including San Diego, CA (University of California at San Diego); Houston, TX (The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center); and Charlottesville, VA (University of Virginia) from 2011 to 2016. Inclusion criteria included having a Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders‐5 (DSM‐5) diagnosis of mild to severe alcohol use disorder, drinking at least eight standard drink units (SDUs) per week (women) or at least 15 SDUs per week (men) in the 30 days prior to randomization for men, and having smoked an average of five or more cigarettes/day in the 30 days prior to randomization. See the Supporting Information document for the complete list of eligibility criteria. Participants were recruited by each site using multiple methods including flyers, newspaper advertisements, radio advertisements, and internet advertisements. Participants received compensation for completing all study clinic visits, biospecimen sample collections, and questionnaires. This protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at all three sites. A subset of these data for participants recruited at the MD Anderson site were previously reported in a study that examined potential treatment mechanisms of topiramate using electroencephalography (Robinson et al., 2022).

Procedures

Study design

Participants in this double‐blinded randomized clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov registration #NCT01182766, which contains the study protocol and statistical analysis plan) were assigned to one of three treatment groups—placebo, low‐dose topiramate (up to 125 mg/day), or high‐dose topiramate (up to 250 mg/day). Randomization, conducted electronically by the data manager at the University of Virginia, was achieved using a block randomization procedure in which treatment groups were stratified to achieve a balance on age of onset for AUD (<26 or ≥26 years old), gender, drinks per drinking day (<8 or ≥8 drinks per drinking day), and the number of cigarettes smoked (<20 or ≥20 cigarettes per day). Following randomization, participants were asked to complete an 18‐week, double‐blind treatment period during which they received study medication and weekly treatment adherence counseling including a self‐help manual for smoking cessation. Topiramate and matching placebo (i.e., 0 mg topiramate) were titrated for the first 5 weeks of the 18‐week treatment period. The slow upward titration with topiramate over 5 weeks was done to minimize potential adverse events that have been associated with no gradual titration of the drug (Blodgett et al., 2014) and was based on previous work by Johnson and colleagues using this titration period (Johnson et al., 2007; Johnson et al., 2008). The target quit date (TQD) was set at the beginning of the sixth week of treatment. Participants were permitted to cut back but encouraged not to attempt smoking or alcohol cessation prior to the TQD to minimize any discouragement a relapsing participant might experience if attempting to quit before topiramate was titrated to its full therapeutic dose. Follow‐up data were also collected at 1 month and 3 months after the end of the treatment phase.

Study pre‐screening procedures

Potential participants were screened by telephone for study interest and initial eligibility. Information collected included demographic data, health status, psychiatric history, alcohol and smoking history, and questions to assess motivation to engage in treatment. Those who met initial eligibility were scheduled for a screening visit within the next 30 days.

Study screening procedures

At the screening visit, participants were provided with information about the study and completed additional steps to verify study eligibility, including smoking and drinking history, expired carbon monoxide (CO) testing, a physical exam, a pregnancy test (if applicable), blood collection for hematology/chemistry, urine collection for urinalysis (pregnancy status and drug screen), saliva collection for cotinine level, the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) to screen for a DSM‐5 diagnosis of mild to severe AUD (Bohn et al., 1995), and the Mini‐International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI; Sheehan et al., 1998) to screen for psychiatric disorders. Eligible participants completed the questionnaires described below. Participants were required to produce a breath alcohol content (BAC) of 0.000 before signing the study informed consent document. Those eligible were scheduled for a randomization visit (visit 1). Those ineligible for the study were provided with a list of local treatment centers.

Intervention procedures

Participants were seen in person at the clinical site for 17 visits (screening, intervention phase and the 1‐month follow‐up visit) and completed the 3‐month follow‐up visit over the phone. See Table S1 in the Supporting Information document for the timeline of study procedures and assessments. Participants must have produced a BAC of 0.020 or less to proceed with each in clinic visit.

Study medication

Topiramate was self‐administered by participants in a dosing regimen of one capsule in the evening beginning on Day 1 and then moving to one capsule in the morning and one capsule in the evening beginning on Day 8 and continuing through Day 126 (see Table S2 for the dose escalation schedules and missed dose instructions). At the end of every session, study staff used a checklist to record the type and amount of treatment received. Study medication was compounded and packaged by Greenpark Compounding Pharmacy (Houston, TX). At the end of the study, all unused investigational products were inventoried and destroyed by each study site.

Brief behavioral compliance enhancement treatment (BBCET)

The BBCET is a brief (15–30 min per session) standardized treatment program used in conjunction with a pharmacological intervention for the treatment of alcohol dependence that increases and enhances adherence with the investigational products and other aspects of the treatment regimen (Johnson, Diclemente, et al., 2003). A compliance form was used to measure the type and amount of treatment received. Sessions were audiotaped and 10% of tapes were reviewed by study staff at the University of Virginia (participants could decline audiotaping). Research staff involved in counseling the subjects were trained to deliver BBCET.

One‐ and 3‐month follow‐up visits

Participants attended a follow‐up assessment session at 1‐month post‐treatment and a follow‐up phone call at 3 months post‐treatment, where additional assessments were collected, including adverse events (see Table S1).

Measures

Primary outcomes

Primary outcomes included percentage of heavy drinking days (PHDD) with heavy drinking defined as ≥5 drinks per drinking day for men and ≥4 drinks per drinking day for women (US National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, 2025) and smoking continuous abstinence rate with (CO) verification (<10 ppm) during the last 4 weeks of treatment. Smoking abstinence was scored using the Python package abstcal (Cui et al., 2022). Daily alcohol and cigarette use was recorded at each session using the timeline follow‐back (TLFB) method (Brown et al., 1998). The original rationale for using weeks 14–18 (i.e., the last 4 weeks) of active treatment as the primary outcome measurement period stems from past FDA guidance for evaluating the efficacy of substance abuse disorder treatment, most recently reiterated in 2023 (US Food and Drug Administration, 2023).

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included the Quality of Life Enjoyment and Satisfaction Questionnaire (Q‐LES‐Q; Endicott et al., 1993) and the Alcohol and Cigarette Craving Visual Analog Scales (VAS; Johnson & Cowen, 1993) calculated as means over the last 4 weeks of treatment. The Q‐LES‐Q consisted of the Subjective Feelings, Physical Health, Household Duties, Leisure Time Activities, Social Relationships, General Activities, Work, and School/Coursework subscales, and the scores for Q‐LES‐Q subscales were calculated by normalizing the raw scores to the maximal possible scores for each subscale, such that the derived scores had a range of 0–100% (Endicott et al., 1993).

Exploratory outcomes

Exploratory outcomes included continuous measures of alcohol use (drinks per day [DPD], cigarette use, cigarettes per day [CPD]), alcohol withdrawal severity using the Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol‐revised (CIWA‐AR; Sullivan et al., 1989), smoking withdrawal severity using the Wisconsin Smoking Withdrawal Scale (WSWS; Welsch et al., 1999), AUD symptom severity using the Drinker Inventory of Consequences (DrInC; Miller et al., 1995), cigarette dependence using the Fagerström Test for Cigarette Dependence (FTCD; Fagerström, 2012), sleep quality using the Medical Outcomes Study Sleep Scale (MOS‐Sleep; Allen et al., 2009), affect using the Positive and Negative Affect Scale (PANAS; Watson et al., 1988), and depressive symptoms using The Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES‐D; Ross & Mirowsky, 1984).

Adverse events

Adverse events, using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0 (US Department of Health and Human Services, 2017), were collected at all study visits and at the 1‐ and 3‐month follow‐ups.

Statistical analyses

For outcome variables with count values (e.g., CPD and DPD), we compared between fractional regression, Poisson, Poisson zero inflation, and negative binomial inflation models to determine which model was best fitting. We also compared the fit of these models with that of fractional regression for bounded variables (e.g., PHDD; values ranging from 0 to 1). The normally distributed continuous outcome measures (e.g., VAS) were examined using general linear models. For the post hoc exploratory repeated measures models we conducted to maximize the use of available data, we included all visits after the target quit date along with the covariates listed below. Besides PHDD calculated for the 7 days preceding each visit, we also calculated the mean of the previous 7 days for the substance use variables (i.e., DPD and CPD) and 7‐day point prevalence for cigarette abstinence for the exploratory repeated measures models, with cigarette abstinence examined using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM). For all models, we conducted analyses on the unadjusted (i.e., treatment group only) and adjusted models (i.e., treatment group and sex at birth, race/ethnicity, employment status, marriage/cohabitation status, income, adverse event count, adverse event maximum severity, and the baseline observations of the modeled substance use dependent variable as covariates). For post hoc pairwise comparisons, we applied the Holm–Bonferroni correction to control the Type I error rate (Seaman et al., 1991).

To account for the high attrition rate (see Figure 1), we conducted multiple imputation analyses on our primary and secondary outcome models to maximize the statistical power. Both unadjusted (treatment group predictor only) and adjusted (treatment group plus above covariates) models were conducted using fractional regression (all primary and secondary variables were convertible to a 0 to 1 scale) with 20 imputations run for each model.

Over half of participants dropped out before end‐of‐treatment (EOT) visit 15 (0 mg: 53.57%, 125 mg: 56.0%, 250 mg: 54.55%), but the dropout rates did not significantly differ by treatment group (χ2[2] = 0.95, p = 0.95). When we examined whether there were differences in demographics or baseline smoking and drinking characteristics between those who dropped out or completed the study, only age differed significantly (t[234] = −2.23, p = 0.0266), with those who dropped out being younger than study completers (M = 49.74, SD = 9.43). In terms of medication adherence, only 30.1% of participants took the study medication as prescribed, at either a full or reduced dose due to adverse events, on at least 80% of the treatment days, but this did not differ by treatment group (0 mg: 27.4%, 125 mg: 30.7%, 250 mg: 32.5%; χ2[2] = 0.51, p = 0.77).

Primary outcomes

We analyzed the impact of treatment group and its covariates (listed in the Statistical Analyses section) on the PHDD during weeks 14–18 using a Poisson model. PHDD (range 0–1) during weeks 14–18 for the 0 mg (M = 0.159, SD = 0.27), 125 mg (M = 0.213, SD = 0.29), and 250 mg (M = 0.075, SD = 0.16) groups did not differ significantly for the unadjusted (χ2[2] = 2.46, p = 0.29) or adjusted models (χ2[2] = 1.77, p = 0.41). Likewise, the unadjusted and adjusted multiple imputation models did not yield significant differences (see Table S3).

For the smoking continuous abstinence rates during weeks 14–18, we analyzed unadjusted and adjusted logistical regression models. Smoking continuous abstinence rates during weeks 14–18 for the 0 mg (5.6%), 125 mg (14.7%), and 250 mg (17.1%) groups did not differ significantly for the unadjusted (χ2[2] = 2.72, p = 0.26) or adjusted models (χ2[2] = 2.32, p = 0.31). The multiple imputations models were also not significant (see Table S4).

Secondary outcomes

To address the secondary outcomes, we examined the impact of treatment group on quality of life (Q‐LES‐Q) and on alcohol (VAS‐ALC) and nicotine (VAS‐NIC) craving during weeks 14–18 using general linear models. There were no statistically significant effects of treatment group on any of the Q‐LES‐Q, VAS‐ALC, or VAS‐NIC scales (see Table S5). Similarly, there were no significant effects of treatment group for the multiple imputation analyses of the Q‐LES‐Q (Table S6) or VAS (Table S7) scales.

Exploratory analyses

As part of the post hoc exploratory analyses, we conducted repeated measures analyses on the 7‐day point‐prevalence substance use variables, including PHDD, DPD, CPD, and smoking abstinence, including all time points after the target quit date (see Table 2). In terms of alcohol consumption, participants in the 250 mg group had lower mean PHDD and DPD than those assigned to the placebo or 125 mg dose arm. The 125 mg group did not differ from the placebo group on either measure. For the smoking measures, participants in both the 125 and 250 mg dose groups had lower mean CPD and greater cigarette 7‐day point‐prevalence abstinence than 0 mg. Both active doses also resulted in greater abstinence over the sessions compared to the 0 mg dose.

We also examined the Q‐LES‐Q, VAS‐ALC, and VAS‐NIC, along with the other alcohol‐ and smoking‐related dependence and withdrawal measures, using repeated measures analyses. None of the measures varied by treatment group after applying the Holm–Bonferroni correction (see Table S8).

Adverse events

One participant in the 0 mg group experienced a serious adverse event (intracranial hemorrhage), but none were experienced by those in the 125 or 250 mg groups. There were 22 unique severe adverse events (i.e., a severity score of 3 or higher), including seven deemed to be at least possibly related to the study drug, including concentration impairment (2 events in the 250 mg group), depression (125 mg group), dysgeusia (125 mg group), intracranial hemorrhage (0 mg group), nausea (0 mg group), and pruritus (125 mg group). There were no differences in severe adverse events by treatment group. There was no significant impact of treatment group on total unique adverse events (χ2[2] = 1.11, p = 0.58) or AE severity (χ2[6] = 4.59, p = 0.60). However, paresthesia, dysgeusia, and irritability were significantly more likely to be reported by those in the 250 mg group than those in the 0 mg group (see Table S9).

DISCUSSION

The findings for the primary objectives for this RCT did not support our hypotheses that both low‐ and high‐dose topiramate would be more efficacious than placebo at reducing the percentage of heavy drinking days (PHDD) and increasing the continuous abstinence rate for smoking during the last 4 weeks of treatment (weeks 14–18). However, our exploratory analyses, which accounted for the unexpectedly high dropout rate and utilized all available data, indicate that treatment with up to 250 mg of topiramate dose reduced both alcohol and smoking behaviors compared with placebo, and that treatment with up to 125 mg reduced smoking behavior as compared to placebo. Topiramate treatment had no significant impact on quality of life or craving‐related measures. Finally, three types of adverse events, paresthesia, dysgeusia, and irritability, were more likely to be experienced by those in the 250 mg topiramate group, although total adverse events did not differ by treatment group.

The absence of significant effects on the primary alcohol use outcome (i.e., PHDD during the final 4 weeks of treatment) stands in contrast to prior research reporting reductions in alcohol use with topiramate (Johnson et al., 2007; Kranzler et al., 2014; Kranzler et al., 2021). Similarly, our nonsignificant primary smoking abstinence findings (i.e., continuous abstinence rates during weeks 14–18) are inconsistent with previous reports that topiramate reduced smoking behavior (Baltieri et al., 2009; Johnson et al., 2005; Khazaal et al., 2006; Oncken et al., 2014), particularly among men (Anthenelli et al., 2008). However, the fact that the majority of study participants terminated treatment before these endpoints reduces our confidence in the primary outcome findings. Our EOT completion rates (44.0–46.43%) were lower than other alcohol and smoking studies involving topiramate, which have ranged from 65.9% (Anthenelli et al., 2017) to 91.8% (Kranzler et al., 2021), but our treatment period was 6 weeks longer than the 12 weeks provided in those studies. Due to the notable extent of missing data, we conducted additional post hoc exploratory multiple imputation analyses on our primary outcomes, which yielded findings consistent with our a priori analyses. This result is perhaps unsurprising, as the multiple imputation models were based solely on the observed data from the final 4 weeks of treatment. However, when we incorporated all available data across the treatment period into exploratory repeated measures analyses, we found evidence suggesting that topiramate reduces both drinking and smoking behaviors among individuals with comorbid AUD and TUD.

In terms of the secondary objectives, topiramate dose had no significant impact on the outcomes related to quality of life or craving, and the exploratory analyses involving multiple imputations were consistent with these findings. Our null findings for alcohol craving contrast with previous clinical trials indicating that topiramate reduces self‐reported alcohol craving (Batki et al., 2014; Johnson, Ait‐Daoud, et al., 2003; Wetherill et al., 2021). However, these findings are consistent with other findings that neither tonic‐ nor cue‐induced alcohol craving was reduced by topiramate (Miranda et al., 2008) and that topiramate failed to reduce alcohol craving non‐drinking times (Miranda et al., 2016). The lack of consistency across these studies might be due to the lack of consistency in craving assessment, whether in terms of measures (and what aspects of craving are assessed), time points relative to drinking, or assessment modalities (e.g., tonic‐ vs. cue‐induced craving) (Sayette et al., 2000). In contrast, our null findings for smoking craving are largely consistent with earlier work reporting no significant effects of topiramate on craving to smoke (Anthenelli et al., 2017). However, unlike that study, we did not replicate topiramate's reported efficacy in reducing smoking withdrawal symptoms. Taken together, these results suggest that topiramate has little consistent impact on either AUD or TUD withdrawal symptoms.

In addition to the high study dropout, medication adherence was low. Less than a third of all participants took the study medication as prescribed, at either a full or reduced dose due to adverse events, on at least 80% of the treatment days. These rates could have been influenced by adverse events, but only seven severe AEs were deemed possibly related to the study drug. However, in terms of AEs, paresthesia, dysgeusia, and irritability were more likely to be reported by those in the 250 mg group than those in the placebo group. These findings are consistent with the literature, as paresthesia and dysgeusia are among the most commonly reported AEs among those exposed to topiramate (Johnson et al., 2007; Likhitsathian et al., 2013). While concentration impairment is another frequently reported AE (Johnson et al., 2007), we did not find significant differences by drug dose, although two participants in the 250 mg group experienced severe AEs possibly related to the study drug.

Conclusions regarding this study's findings are hampered by low study adherence. The high treatment dropout rate directly impacted the a priori analyses of the primary and secondary outcomes because the majority of participants dropped out before weeks 14–18. High study dropout rates have been common in topiramate studies, including 1‐year discontinuation rates of approximately 45% among those treated for seizure disorder (Bootsma et al., 2004; Kellett et al., 1999). This is consistent with dropout rates found among patients of outpatient substance use treatment center, which have been found to range from 23% to 50% (Brorson et al., 2013). Additionally, this study's medication adherence rates were relatively low and may have influenced both study dropout and treatment outcomes. One reason for low adherence may be the lower drinking threshold used in this study, as individuals with less severe alcohol‐related issues may have perceived less of a need to adhere to the medication regimen. However, it should be noted that study dropout and medication adherence rates were not significantly affected by topiramate dose, suggesting that other factors—such as participant selection, recruitment methods, or the inclusion of individuals with more severe AUD—may have contributed. Additionally, there is evidence that polysubstance abusers have reduced treatment engagement (Coulson et al., 2009), which may be due to an increased risk of psychosocial stressors and medical disorders (Bhalla et al., 2017). Regardless, we found significant effects of topiramate on drinking and smoking outcomes with our exploratory repeated measures analyses, despite these limitations.

In conclusion, while our primary analyses did not find evidence that topiramate decreases drinking and smoking behavior, our exploratory repeated measures analyses suggest that topiramate 250 mg reduces drinking behavior and that both the 125 and 250 mg doses reduce smoking behavior in individuals with AUD and TUD. We did not find evidence that topiramate treatment impacts quality of life or withdrawal‐related symptoms. However, this study experienced high rates of treatment dropout and low rates of medication adherence, which likely influenced the outcomes. Our findings suggest that there is likely little benefit to extending topiramate treatment to 18 weeks to incorporate a 5‐week titration period. Future studies should seek to enhance treatment adherence, perhaps by only enrolling patients from residential treatment facilities.
