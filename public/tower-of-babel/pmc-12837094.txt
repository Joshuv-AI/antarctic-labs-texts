The relationship between sustainable dietary behaviors, sustainable food literacy, healthy life awareness, and social media addiction in adults: a cross-sectional study in Türkiye
Authors: Fatma Elif Eroğlu, Büşra Açıkalın Göktürk, Zeynep Begüm Kalyoncu Atasoy, Özge Mengi Çelik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSustainable food literacy, healthy life awareness, and adherence to sustainable dietary models such as the Mediterranean diet are critical for promoting both public health and environmental well-being. While sociodemographic influences have been examined in previous research, the potential role of social media addiction as a behavioral factor in shaping sustainable dietary practices remains underexplored.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate how sustainable food literacy, healthy life awareness, and social media addiction relate to Mediterranean diet adherence and nutritional sustainability in adults, while also accounting for key sociodemographic characteristics.MethodsThis cross-sectional online study included 2,263 adults (60.5% female; mean age, 29.4 ± 11.7 years; mean BMI, 24.54 ± 6.8 kg/m²). Data were collected using the Sustainable Food Literacy Scale (SFLS), Social Media Addiction Scale (SMAS), Adherence to the Mediterranean Diet and Nutritional Sustainability Questionnaire Turkish Version (MedQ-Sus-Tr), and Healthy Life Awareness Scale (HLAS). Pearson correlation and multiple linear regression analyses were performed.ResultsBased on the MedQ-Sus-Tr, 91.0% of participants were classified as having low adherence to the Mediterranean diet, and 70.1% were classified as having low nutritional sustainability. Mediterranean diet adherence showed the strongest correlation with nutritional sustainability (r = 0.728, p < 0.001). Predictors of Mediterranean diet adherence included marital status, income, SFLS, and HLAS (R² = 0.365), while nutritional sustainability was predicted by marital status, income, SFLS, and HLAS (R² = 0.301). SFLS was associated with sex, education level, marital status, income, social media addiction, HLAS, Mediterranean diet adherence, and nutritional sustainability (R² = 0.704). HLAS was predicted by sex, age, SFLS, social media addiction, Mediterranean diet adherence, and nutritional sustainability (R² = 0.696).ConclusionsOur findings highlight that higher sustainable food literacy and healthy life awareness significantly predict greater adherence to the Mediterranean diet and improved nutritional sustainability, regardless of sociodemographic characteristics. Social media addiction was positively associated with both literacy and awareness, though it did not directly predict dietary adherence or sustainability. These results suggest that while social media use may enhance exposure to nutrition-related information, its impact on actual dietary behaviors is less direct. Targeted interventions should therefore leverage the informational potential of digital platforms, while also fostering translation of awareness into healthier and more sustainable eating practices.

Introduction

In recent years, climate change, environmental degradation, and the increasing prevalence of noncommunicable diseases (e.g., cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, cancer, obesity), which account for approximately 74% of all global deaths [1] have made sustainable eating practices a public health necessity. Global food systems are responsible for approximately one-third of total greenhouse gas emissions, underscoring the urgent need for dietary shifts towards sustainable diets [2]. The concept of sustainable nutrition encompasses a multifaceted approach to dietary management, with the overarching objective being the promotion of human health and well-being, while concurrently aiming to minimize its environmental impact and foster intergenerational equity [2, 3]. This concept is garnering increasing attention on a global scale. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 1 (No Poverty), SDG 2 (Zero Hunger), SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-Being), and SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), highlight the need to transform food systems into environmentally sensitive and socially equitable structures [4]. A recent systematic review has also emphasized the intersection between food literacy and sustainability, highlighting the importance of integrated educational approaches aligned with the SDGs [5].

A sustainable eating pattern includes consuming high amounts of plant-based foods, limiting animal food consumption (especially red meat), opting for minimally processed foods, and prioritizing seasonal and locally produced options [6, 7]. Evidence indicates that adopting such dietary patterns supports sustainable development through improvements in both human health and environmental outcomes [8]. These practices have been shown to correlate with positive health outcomes and a reduced risk of chronic disease; for example, adherence to the EAT-Lancet planetary health diet is associated with lower risk of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, and type 2 diabetes [9, 10].Recent international evidence further supports that transitions towards plant-based dietary patterns can substantially reduce greenhouse gas emissions, land use, and freshwater demand, while improving overall diet quality and nutrient adequacy [4, 11, 12].

Adopting sustainable eating habits is largely dependent on sustainable food literacy, which is influenced by individuals’ knowledge, motivation, and skills [13, 14]. By shaping everyday food choices, food literacy plays a central role in promoting health and encouraging sustainable eating behaviors. It encompasses consideration of the health, nutritional value, freshness, degree of processing, safety, and quality of foods consumed in daily life. Sustainable food literacy is not limited to nutrition knowledge and culinary skills; it also encompasses ecological awareness, ethical food choices, and long-term thinking by planning dietary preferences while considering the impact on future generations’ food security and the environment [15, 16]. In addition, sustainable food literacy has been found to be positively associated with climate change awareness in adult populations [17]. A study demonstrated that individuals with a high level of food literacy contribute more significantly to both the health of the individual and the planet [13].

Several factors influence food literacy, and studies conducted in Europe and Türkiye have shown that sustainable food literacy is particularly lower among younger individuals and those from socioeconomically disadvantaged groups [17, 18]. In Türkiye, recent surveys indicate that sustainable nutrition awareness remains relatively low, particularly among younger adults and those with lower education levels, despite the recommendations outlined in the 2022 Türkiye Dietary Guidelines [19]. Recent research in Türkiye also indicates that healthy eating attitude and sex are significant predictors of sustainable food literacy among young adults [20]. Such limited awareness and the uneven distribution of sustainable food literacy across demographic groups suggest that broader lifestyle factors and general health consciousness may also influence individuals’ ability to adopt sustainable dietary behaviors.

In addition to sustainable food literacy, healthy lifestyle awareness, which is defined as the extent to which individuals recognize the connection between healthy behaviors and general well-being, plays a role in sustainable lifestyle choices [21]. Studies have shown that individuals with a high level of health awareness tend to have healthier eating habits that align with health and environmental values [20, 22]. In Türkiye, positive attitudes towards healthy eating have been associated with higher food literacy and sustainable consumption trends [23]. Research conducted in Europe has highlighted that food-related decision-making processes are influenced by a multitude of factors. Among these factors, environmental identity, environmental sensitivity, and social impact have been shown to play important roles in shaping sustainable dietary habits [24, 25]. Among these social influences, social media has become a prominent channel for exposure to nutrition and health information, with the potential to increase awareness even if its impact on actual dietary behaviors remains less consistent. By delivering constantly updated content and fostering rapid feedback, social media platforms have the potential to shape individuals’ eating habits, meal planning, and physical activity routines. At the same time, misinformation about health and nutrition can reduce healthy living awareness [26, 27]. In addition, highlights that social media platforms can serve as effective tools for promoting sustainable consumption and digital food literacy when guided by credible nutrition experts [28].A study shows that exposure to nutrition-related content on social media can positively affect nutritional behaviors, with the percentage of individuals reporting behavioral changes in intervention groups increasing from 44.9% to 60.7% [29]. Another study found that individuals exposed to nutrition-related content on social media reported various positive changes in their eating behaviors, including increased water consumption, greater attention to food label information, and a reduction in the consumption of ready-to-eat foods [30].

Taken together, these findings suggest that social media may play a critical role in shaping food literacy and health awareness, yet its relationship with sustainable dietary behaviors has received limited scholarly attention. Hence, this study addresses this gap by investigating, within a large adult sample in Türkiye, the associations between sustainable dietary behaviors, sustainable food literacy, healthy life awareness, and social media addiction. While previous Mediterranean studies have mainly focused on dietary patterns and sustainability from nutritional or environmental perspectives, this research uniquely integrates digital behavior and psychosocial determinants into the sustainability framework. By doing so, it contributes new insights from a Mediterranean population, offering cross-cultural relevance beyond the regional context. The findings are expected to inform targeted educational and public health interventions that integrate sustainability, food literacy, and digital engagement to promote healthier and more environmentally responsible eating patterns.

Methods

Study design

In this cross-sectional study, 2263 adults between the ages of 18 and 65 participated from January 2025 to May 2025. A web-based survey was gathered using Google Forms and social media platforms like email and WhatsApp. With decision number 2025/222, University of Health Sciences, Gülhane Scientific Research Ethics Committee granted ethical approval. Informed consent was obtained electronically, with participants required to select the “I agree to participate voluntarily” option before accessing the survey. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Of these, 20 participants were excluded due to incomplete responses (n = 12) or not meeting the inclusion criteria (n = 8). Thus, the final sample consisted of 2263 participants (Fig. 1). On average, participants completed the survey in about fifteen minutes. The survey collected information on participants’ general characteristics (age, sex, education, marital status, employment status), self-reported anthropometric data (body weight (kg) and height (cm)), and daily digital media usage time (minutes). In addition, the following validated scales were applied: the Healthy Life Awareness Scale to assess healthy life awareness, the Sustainable Food Literacy Scale to determine sustainable food literacy levels, the Social Media Addiction Scale (SMAS) to evaluate social media addiction, and the Adherence to the Mediterranean Diet and Nutritional Sustainability Questionnaire (MedQ-Sus-Tr) to assess adherence to the Mediterranean diet and nutritional sustainability.

Sustainable food literacy scale (SFLS)

The Sustainable Food Literacy Scale was originally developed by Cheng-Chung Teng et al. (2022) [31] and adapted into Turkish by Kubilay et al. (2024) for use in Türkiye[32]. The Turkish version of the scale consists of 26 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale. The total score ranges from 26 to 182, with higher scores indicating greater sustainable food literacy. There are no reverse-scored items in the Turkish version. Higher scores indicate greater sustainable food literacy. The scale’s Cronbach’s α is 0.941.

Healthy life awareness scale (HLAS)

The Healthy Life Awareness Scale, developed by Özer et al. (2020), measures individuals’ awareness of healthy lifestyle behavior [33]. It consists of 15 items and is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (“strongly disagree” = 1 to “strongly agree” = 5). Higher scores reflect greater awareness of healthy life behaviors. The scale’s Cronbach’s α is 0.81.

Social media addiction scale (SMAS)

Based on the Internet Addiction Test (Young, 1998) [34] the Internet Addiction Scale (Pawlikowski et al., 2013) [35], the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (Andreassen et al., 2016) [36], and the Social Media Disorder Scale (van den Eijnden et al., 2016) [37], the Social Media Addiction Scale was developed by Hazar and Avcı (2024) [38]. The Social Media Addiction Scale (SMAS) was used to assess the level of social media addiction. It reflects problematic or excessive use but does not differentiate whether the use has positive or negative outcomes. The scale consists of 23 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater addiction levels. The scale’s Cronbach’s α is 0.886.

Adherence to the mediterranean diet and nutritional sustainability questionnaire (MedQ-Sus-Tr)

The MedQ-Sus, originally developed by Ruggeri et al. (2022) [39], was adapted into Turkish by Ülker and Pourdeh (2024) to assess adherence to the Mediterranean diet and nutritional sustainability [40]. The scale is scored based on the frequency of food consumption, and higher scores indicate greater adherence to the Mediterranean diet and higher nutritional sustainability. The Turkish version consists of 8 items and provides two related scores: the Adherence of Mediterranean Diet Score (0–16 points) and the Nutritional Sustainability Score (0–8 points). The scale’s Cronbach’s α is 0.954.

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric information (height and body weight) was obtained through self-reported data provided by participants. Body mass index (BMI) value was calculated by dividing body weight by the square of height. Body mass index values were classified as underweight (< 18.50 kg/m²), normal (18.50–24.99 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.99 kg/m²) and obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m²) [41].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage, were utilized to summarize the data. The distribution of the data was assessed through histograms, the coefficient of variation, skewness, kurtosis, and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the relationships among the scale scores (Sustainable Food Literacy Scale, Healthy Life Awareness Scale, Social Media Addiction Scale, and the Mediterranean Diet and Nutritional Sustainability Questionnaire). Linear regression analyses were conducted to predict the scores of Adherence to the Mediterranean Diet, Nutritional Sustainability, Sustainable Food Literacy Scale, and Healthy Life Awareness Scale. Independent variables included in the multiple linear regression models were selected using the backward elimination method. The backward elimination method was employed to obtain a parsimonious model by retaining the most significant predictors and eliminating non-contributing variables. To assess potential multicollinearity among independent variables, Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) were calculated, and all values were below 5, indicating no multicollinearity issue All results were interpreted within a 95% confidence interval, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 22.0.

Results

Of the 2263 participants, 60.5% of the participants were female, and the mean age was 29.4 ± 11.7 years. Most participants were university graduates (57.4%) and single (66.6%). More than half reported income equal to expenses (53.8%). Regarding BMI classification, 56.6% were normal weight, 28.2% overweight, and 10.0% obese with a mean BMI of 24.54 ± 6.8 kg/m². The mean scores of the scales were as follows: Sustainable Food Literacy Scale 127.2 ± 24.5, Social Media Addiction Scale 71.8 ± 14.3, Healthy Life Awareness Scale 54.5 ± 9.0, and the Adherence to the Mediterranean Diet and Nutritional Sustainability Questionnaire (MedQ-Sus-Tr), with subscale scores of 6.4 ± 2.3 for Mediterranean diet adherence and 2.7 ± 1.4 for nutritional sustainability. The average daily social media usage time was 3.3 ± 2.2 h. The majority of participants reported using smartphones (84.5%) and television (40.8%) as their primary digital devices. Nearly half reported using digital media while eating, with 17.2% indicating they always did so and 31.8% often doing so. Based on classifications, the majority of participants showed low compliance with both the Mediterranean diet (91.0%) and nutritional sustainability (70.1%) (Table 1).

Table 2 presents the correlations among study variables. The strongest correlation was observed between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and nutritional sustainability (r = 0.728, p < 0.001). Sustainable food literacy also showed a strong positive association with healthy life awareness (r = 0.692, p < 0.001). In addition, sustainable food literacy was positively correlated with nutritional sustainability (r = 0.222, p < 0.001). A weak but statistically significant association was observed between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and healthy life awareness (r = 0.064, p = 0.002), while nutritional sustainability showed a similarly weak association with healthy life awareness (r = 0.063, p = 0.003).Social media addiction correlated positively with sustainable food literacy and healthy life awareness, but it showed no significant association with Mediterranean diet adherence (p = 0.197) or nutritional sustainability (p = 0.058) (Fig. 2).

Regression analyses further demonstrated the predictive power of sociodemographic and nutrition-related variables. In the model for adherence to the Mediterranean diet, being married (B = − 0.696, p < 0.001), higher income (B = 0.162, p = 0.025), greater sustainable food literacy (B = 0.006, p = 0.003), and higher healthy life awareness (B = 0.016, p = 0.002) were significant predictors, together explaining 36.5% of the variance (R² = 0.365, p < 0.001). Similarly, nutritional sustainability was significantly predicted by being married (B = − 0.155, p = 0.019), higher income (B = 0.165, p < 0.001), sustainable food literacy (B = 0.179, p < 0.001), and healthy life awareness (B = 0.151, p < 0.001), with the model accounting for 30.1% of the variance (R² = 0.301, p < 0.001) (Table 3). These results supported H1a and H1b, indicating that both sustainable food literacy and healthy life awareness significantly predicted sustainable dietary behaviors.

These findings indicate that married individuals, those with higher income, and those with greater food literacy and health awareness tend to demonstrate stronger adherence to the Mediterranean diet and higher nutritional sustainability. Figure 3 presents the standardized beta coefficients for predictors of Mediterranean diet adherence and nutritional sustainability, highlighting the stronger effect of sustainable food literacy. Figure 4 illustrates, via a heatmap, that sustainable food literacy and healthy life awareness were most strongly predicted by social media addiction, sociodemographic characteristics, and reciprocal associations with each other.

When predicting sustainable food literacy, both sociodemographic factors (sex, education, marital status, income) and nutrition- and lifestyle-associated factors (social media addiction, healthy life awareness, adherence to the Mediterranean diet, and nutritional sustainability) were significant, with the model explaining 70.4% of the variance (R² = 0.704, p < 0.001) (Table 4). In the model for healthy life awareness, demographic variables (sex and age) together with sustainable food literacy, social media addiction, adherence to the Mediterranean diet, and nutritional sustainability emerged as significant predictors, explaining 69.6% of the variance (R² = 0.696, p < 0.001) (Table 4and Fig. 4). Social media addiction was positively associated with sustainable food literacy and healthy life awareness but did not directly predict sustainable dietary behaviors, partially supporting H1c.

Discussion

The findings showed a significant association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sustainable nutritional behaviors. Higher sustainable food literacy and healthy lifestyle awareness were associated with sustainable nutritional behaviors, while sex, marital status, and income were significant predictors. Social media addiction was positively related to healthy lifestyle awareness. Sustainable nutrition is a form of nutrition that supports all aspects of individual health and is considered environmentally friendly, reliable, economical, and accessible [42]. Our findings align with the broader framework of sustainable nutrition, which emphasizes dietary practices that benefit both individual health and environmental well-being. Consistent with our first and second hypotheses (H1a and H1b), both sustainable food literacy and healthy living awareness were significant predictors of sustainable dietary behaviors (β = 0.063 and β = 0.064, respectively; p < 0.01), confirming that literacy and awareness play essential roles in shaping sustainability-oriented eating patterns. In contrast, our third hypothesis (H1c) was only partially supported, as social media addiction did not directly predict sustainable dietary behaviors (p > 0.05), although it was positively correlated with sustainable food literacy and healthy living awareness (r = 0.067, p = 0.001).

Global authorities such as the FAO and WHO highlight that sustainable diets should not only enhance health but also reduce environmental burdens including greenhouse gas emissions and biodiversity loss [43]. Despite growing global awareness, our findings revealed that adherence to sustainable nutrition remains low among adults in Türkiye, reflecting similar trends in other Mediterranean countries such as Greece and Spain, where positive attitudes toward sustainability do not consistently translate into dietary practices [44–48]. These results highlight the need for stronger and culturally tailored public health strategies to reinforce sustainable eating patterns in Mediterranean populations. In contrast, Northern European countries show higher sustainability awareness, supported by national dietary guidelines and environmental policies [49, 50].Comparable evidence from Asian contexts indicates that traditional diets such as the Korean and Japanese models also align with sustainability and public health goals [51, 52]. This cross-regional comparison emphasizes how cultural and structural factors shape the translation of sustainability awareness into dietary behavior.

This highlights a gap between positive attitudes and actual dietary practices, suggesting that structural barriers such as cost, convenience, and cultural preferences may exert a stronger influence than individual motivation. Addressing these barriers is particularly important, as global evidence consistently shows that greater adherence to plant-based diets improves health outcomes while reducing greenhouse gas emissions, land use, and water demand [53].

Consistlently, higher adherence to Mediterranean diet is expected to align with sustainable nutrition behaviors [54]. However, prior studies have reported low adherence levels, often declining with age [55, 56]. Consistent with these findings, our study also observed limited adherence among Turkish adults, despite the well-established health and environmental benefits of the Mediterranean diet (Table 1). This finding is consistent with trends observed in other Mediterranean countries, where traditional dietary patterns are increasingly replaced by Western-style habits characterized by greater consumption of processed foods, red meat, and sugary beverages [57, 58].

Socioeconomic barriers, including limited nutrition knowledge and the higher cost of key Mediterranean diet components such as olive oil, fresh produce, and fish, may further hinder adherence [59]. Moreover, lower food literacy reduces individuals’ ability to apply Mediterranean diet principles despite cultural familiarity [48]. In this study, Mediterranean Diet Adherence was positively correlated with Nutrition Sustainability (Table 2), suggesting that adherence may serve as a proxy for broader sustainable eating behaviors. Similar findings from recent Turkish studies confirm that higher plant-based diet indices are linked with greater sustainability, underscoring food literacy as a key determinant of diet quality [40,48].

Moreover, this study’s unique contribution lies in demonstrating that sustainability-oriented dietary behaviors are influenced not only by literacy and awareness but also by socioeconomic status and culturally embedded gender roles, offering new insights for targeted nutrition and sustainability interventions in Türkiye. In our study, marital status (B = − 0.696, β = − 0.140, t = − 6.580, p < 0.001) and income level (B = 0.162, β = 0.047, t = 2.239, p = 0.025) were identified as statistically significant predictors of both Mediterranean diet adherence and sustainable eating behaviors (Table 3).

Consistent with earlier studies, we also found a positive correlation between food literacy scores and Mediterranean diet adherence [60]. Higher levels of food literacy—characterized by adequate nutrition knowledge and practical skills—facilitate adherence to the Mediterranean diet by enabling individuals to make informed food choices and prepare meals in line with health and sustainability principles.Together, these findings suggest that strengthening food literacy may serve as a practical intervention point to improve adherence to sustainable dietary patterns, thereby supporting both individual and environmental health.

Consistent with prior evidence, this study confirmed significant positive correlations between sustainable food literacy and both Mediterranean Diet Compliance (r = 0.163, p < 0.001) and Nutrition Sustainability (r = 0.222, p < 0.001) (Table 2). These results reinforce that food literacy is not only an educational outcome but also a determinant of sustainable lifestyle behaviors [61, 62]. In addition, sex was identified as a predictor of sustainable food literacy, with women scoring higher than men (Table 4), consistent with previous research showing that women tend to be more environmentally conscious and maintain more ecological lifestyles [63].

This may be explained by gender-based household roles, as women often devote more time to food preparation and household management, contributing to stronger engagement with sustainability-related practices [61]. Thus, this gendered dimension of sustainability behaviors indicates that future interventions may need to be tailored differently for men and women to maximize impact.

In the digital age, individuals can easily access nutrition-related information through social media. However, their ability to understand, evaluate, and apply this information should be measured using valid and reliable tools [64]. Previous studies have reported conflicting findings: while frequent social media users may exhibit higher food literacy and healthier eating behaviors [65], other research has shown that greater time spent on social media is associated with lower adherence to the Mediterranean diet, possibly due to nutrition misinformation and increased screen exposure [66, 67]. Furthermore, excessive digital media use has been linked to poorer sustainable eating behaviors, often accompanied by greater consumption of unhealthy snacks during screen time [68].

A particularly noteworthy finding relates to the paradoxical role of social media. Unlike much of the international literature, this study did not find a statistically significant relationship between Social Media Addiction Scale scores and either Mediterranean Diet Adherence (r = 0.027, p = 0.197) or Nutrition Sustainability (r = 0.040, p = 0.058). Although social media addiction was positively associated with healthy life awareness, this awareness does not always translate into healthier or more sustainable eating behaviors. This suggests that educational and societal mechanisms supporting the transformation of awareness into behavioral change could play an important role.

Consistent with our findings, a recent Turkish study also reported moderate food literacy but limited translation of knowledge into sustainable behaviors [69]. These results highlight the need to address structural and cultural barriers to fostering health-promoting and environmentally conscious dietary patterns.

Our results showed that nearly half of the participants reported using digital media while eating, suggesting that digital engagement may shape eating contexts by reinforcing distracted eating and reducing mindful dietary practices, as noted in previous studies on screen use during meals. Nevertheless, recent evidence indicates that well-designed digital interventions can effectively promote sustainable eating behaviors, highlighting technology’s potential as a tool for behavior change [70].

Beyond digital engagement, healthy living awareness emerges as another key psychosocial factor influencing sustainable behaviors. This concept encompasses individuals’ capacity to understand the consequences of their health-related choices and to make decisions benefiting both personal and environmental well-being [71]. Prior research demonstrates that individuals with higher health awareness tend to adopt more sustainable and healthy eating patterns and that healthy lifestyle behaviors enhance food literacy [72–74]. Similar findings from Korea and studies among youth indicate that greater nutrition knowledge and awareness of food labels are linked to healthier dietary choices [75].

A study conducted among adults indicated that individuals with high awareness of healthy living tend to prefer local foods, reflecting a link between the perception of locally produced foods as healthy and the concept of sustainable nutrition [76]. Consistent with previous findings, this study observed a statistically significant positive correlation between Sustainable Food Literacy and Healthy Living Awareness. Moreover, the Healthy Lifestyle Awareness Scale and the Mediterranean Diet Compliance Scale showed a weak but significant positive correlation, while the Nutrition Sustainability score demonstrated a strong one. These results highlight the interconnectedness of awareness, literacy, and sustainable nutrition, suggesting that improvements in one domain may reinforce others. Furthermore, online social media platforms may serve as effective tools for enhancing healthy living awareness and promoting public health [77].

Furthermore, previous research has shown that social media engagement can exert both positive and negative influences on health-related behaviors. One study found that social media use enhances healthy eating habits, self-efficacy, and interest in physical activity, although no relationship was observed with food safety [28]. Another study reported that frequent users of platforms such as Facebook and Instagram had higher awareness of disease symptoms and prevention compared to non-users [78]. In our study, a statistically significant positive correlation was observed between the Social Media Addiction Scale and the Healthy Living Awareness Scale (r = 0.067, p = 0.001) (Table 2). This finding underscores the dual role of social media, where risks of misinformation coexist with opportunities for health promotion, suggesting that carefully designed digital campaigns could foster healthier and more sustainable behaviors.

This study examined sustainable food literacy, Mediterranean diet adherence, digital media use, and healthy lifestyle awareness together, offering an integrated perspective. The large and diverse sample from Türkiye strengthens the reliability and regional relevance of the findings .However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design restricts causal inference, and therefore the observed associations cannot establish directionality. Second, data were collected through self-reported online questionnaires, which may have introduced social desirability, recall, or response bias. Furthermore, online sampling might have limited the representation of individuals with lower digital literacy or limited internet access. Moreover, the online recruitment strategy may have introduced potential selection bias, as individuals with higher socioeconomic status, greater digital engagement, or stronger interest in nutrition-related topics may have been more likely to participate. Consequently, representativeness across age and income groups may be limited, and findings should be interpreted with caution regarding generalizability.

One limitation of this study is that the Social Media Addiction Scale (SMAS) quantifies the degree of social media addiction but does not differentiate between maladaptive (harmful) and potentially adaptive (beneficial) engagement. Consequently, while the scale effectively captures problematic usage patterns, it does not provide information regarding the functional or constructive aspects of social media use.

Despite these limitations, the integration of both behavioral and environmental dimensions of nutrition provides a comprehensive perspective on sustainable dietary patterns. Future research should build on these findings through longitudinal and intervention-based designs to confirm causal relationships, investigate the mechanisms linking digital engagement with sustainable eating behaviors, and develop culturally tailored educational strategies that enhance food literacy and adherence to the Mediterranean diet.

Strengthening food literacy, embedding sustainability into nutrition education, and leveraging digital platforms responsibly could support healthier populations while advancing environmental sustainability in Mediterranean contexts. This study contributes theoretically by integrating digital behavior into the sustainability–nutrition framework, expanding understanding of how online engagement can influence sustainable dietary practices and food literacy. Practically, the findings highlight the need for educational and digital interventions guided by nutrition professionals to enhance sustainability awareness, improve healthy eating behaviors, and promote environmentally responsible consumption. These behavioral dimensions can also be evaluated and addressed by dietitians in clinical practice to support sustainable and health-promoting dietary changes.

In Türkiye, public health–oriented interventions could include integrating the themes of sustainable nutrition and digital awareness into the undergraduate Nutrition and Dietetics curriculum, initiating community-based sustainable food literacy programs, and developing social media campaigns that promote the principles of the Mediterranean diet.For adults, it is important to extend these initiatives beyond school-based programs and integrate them into workplace and community lifelong learning activities. In addition, nationwide digital health literacy campaigns conducted through social media platforms could support mindful screen use and healthier eating habits. In clinical practice, dietitians should assess digital media use habits during nutrition counseling to identify screen-related eating behaviors at an early stage and provide individualized nutrition education and intervention plans.

At the policy level, aligning national nutrition and sustainability strategies with the Türkiye Dietary Guidelines, which includes a dedicated section on sustainable and environmentally friendly dietary patterns, can strengthen public awareness and foster environmentally responsible food choices. Integrating sustainability education into national dietary guidelines, expanding community-based food literacy initiatives, and supporting digital health promotion campaigns within the Turkey Healthy Nutrition and Active Life Program may enhance population-level impact. Moreover, promoting plant-based and locally sourced food options, as emphasized in the national guidelines, could further support national goals for sustainable and healthy nutrition in Türkiye.
