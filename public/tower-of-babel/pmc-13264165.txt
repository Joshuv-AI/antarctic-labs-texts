Two sequential saphenofemoral junctions of a single great saphenous vein separately perforating the cribriform fascia lata
Authors: Knuth Rass, Lilly Kneisel
Published: 2026

A 33-year-old man presented with symptomatic varicose veins affecting both legs for surgical treatment. The patient's past medical history was unremarkable, with no prior vascular surgery.

Preoperative duplex ultrasound examination demonstrated bilateral great saphenous vein (GSV) incompetence (C2aEpAs,d,pPr). On the left side, duplex ultrasound examination revealed GSV incompetence with duplication of the saphenofemoral junction (SFJ), which emptied approximately 1.5 cm separately into the common femoral vein (CFV). Both SFJs exhibited a reflux lasting of >5 seconds (A/Cover). No anatomical abnormalities were found contralaterally. Intraoperatively, we found that the duplication of the SFJ was accompanied by a separate passage through the cribriform fascia (B). The patient underwent bilateral flush ligation and stripping of the GSV with adjunctive phlebectomies. On the left side, both SFJ ostia were identified and each GSV was flush ligated to the CFV (C). The GSV stumps were oversewn and closure of both openings of the cribriform fascia was performed following the principles of a modern crossectomy (D).1 The postoperative course was uneventful, VCSS dropped from 4 to 0.

Anatomical variations of the SFJ have been reported in the literature, such as duplication of the GSV (1.6%-2.0%), duplication of the SFJ with a bifid junction (9.6%), or duplication of the SFJ with two separate junctions (1.7%).2,3 However, the present case demonstrates a single GSV with two consecutive ostial terminations into the CFV separately perforating the cribriform fascia, a configuration that appears to be exceedingly rare and, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously reported.

A misinterpretation of the SFJ anatomy as in this case can essentially lead to two possible complications. First, failure to identify and ligate the distal ostium may cause significant intraoperative bleeding if stripping is performed through the proximal entry. Second, conversely and more likely, overlooking the proximal ostium and addressing only the distal entry may result in incomplete surgical treatment with an increased risk of recurrence from the proximal stump. Therefore, thorough preoperative duplex ultrasound mapping of the SFJ is essential to ensure sufficient and safe surgical management.
