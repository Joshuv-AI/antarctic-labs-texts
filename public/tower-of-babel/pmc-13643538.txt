Contemporary Management of Valvular Cardiogenic Shock Using Transcatheter Valve Interventions
Authors: Karl Aramouni, Chloe Kharsa, Mangesh Kritya, Karl Abou Zeid, Arvin Bhimaraj, Ashrith Guha, Janardhana R Gorthi, Nadeen N Faza, Stephen H Little, Marvin D Atkins, Joe Aoun, Michael J Reardon, Neal S Kleiman, Sachin S Goel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractValvular cardiogenic shock is an under-recognized, yet potentially reversible cause of hemodynamic collapse associated with high mortality. Rapid recognition, comprehensive hemodynamic assessment, and multidisciplinary Heart Team management are critical for successful treatment. Surgical intervention is often prohibitive because of hemodynamic instability, advanced comorbidities, prior cardiac surgeries, or multiorgan dysfunction. We report a case series of patients with diverse valvular pathologies presenting with cardiogenic shock who were treated with transcatheter valve interventions, including transcatheter aortic valve replacement, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, and balloon mitral valvuloplasty. Mechanical circulatory support was individualized according to shock phenotype and ventricular function. Although all procedures achieved technical success with immediate hemodynamic improvement, clinical outcomes varied according to the severity of underlying ventricular dysfunction, right ventricular failure, renal injury, and postcardiotomy physiology.Key Words: balloon valvuloplasty, mechanical circulatory support, transcatheter aortic valve replacement, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, valvular cardiogenic shock

Cardiogenic shock (CS) remains a life-threatening condition associated with substantial morbidity and mortality despite advances in critical care, mechanical circulatory support (MCS), and contemporary cardiovascular therapies.1,2 Although acute myocardial infarction remains the predominant cause of CS, valvular heart disease is increasingly recognized as an important and potentially reversible contributor to hemodynamic collapse.3 Diagnosis and management are often complex and require rapid echocardiographic assessment, invasive hemodynamic evaluation, multidisciplinary Heart Team collaboration, and individualized MCS strategies.4

The emergence of transcatheter valve therapies has expanded treatment options for patients considered prohibitive surgical candidates because of advanced shock, severe ventricular dysfunction, prior cardiac surgery, or multiorgan failure.4 In patients with severe aortic stenosis (AS) complicated by CS, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has demonstrated high procedural success rates despite elevated early mortality.5 Similarly, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) for severe mitral regurgitation (MR) in CS has been associated with reductions in mortality and heart failure hospitalization.4

We present 8 patients with diverse valvular pathologies complicated by CS who underwent transcatheter valve interventions supported by individualized MCS strategies. Left-Sided Obstructive and Prosthetic Aortic Valve Shock

Patients with severe left-sided obstructive lesions frequently presented with impaired forward cardiac output and low-flow physiology requiring urgent transcatheter intervention and temporary MCS support.

Case 1

A 76-year-old woman with multivessel coronary artery disease, severe AS, and ischemic cardiomyopathy with severely reduced left ventricular (LV) systolic function (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] 25%-30%) presented with back pain and elevated cardiac biomarkers, suggestive of acute myocardial injury. Subsequent evaluation demonstrated severe symptomatic AS (aortic valve area 0.58-0.61 cm2, mean gradient 40.6 mm Hg), and her rapid hemodynamic deterioration with progressive hypotension and low-output physiology was attributed primarily to acute valvular decompensation secondary to severe symptomatic AS. Her hospitalization was further complicated by mixed cardiogenic and septic shock requiring vasopressor support and intensive care unit admission.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated extensive nonviable myocardium involving the left anterior descending territory, whereas transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) confirmed severe AS (Video 1, Video 2, Video 3). Her hospitalization was further complicated by acute hypoxic respiratory failure and acute kidney injury on chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage 3a.

Given persistent low-output physiology despite medical therapy, advanced ischemic cardiomyopathy, severe valvular obstruction, and prohibitive operative risk, the multidisciplinary Heart Team elected to proceed with urgent transfemoral TAVR. A 23-mm Edwards SAPIEN 3 valve (Edwards Lifesciences) was successfully implanted. Postdeployment imaging demonstrated normalization of transvalvular gradients.

The procedure was complicated by complete heart block requiring temporary transvenous pacing. Despite successful valve implantation, persistent CS with hypotension and elevated LV end-diastolic pressure prompted insertion of an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) to provide temporary MCS. Although the patient initially improved, recurrent CS after early IABP removal necessitated reinsertion of the IABP with initiation of inotropic therapy. Her course was further complicated by atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response requiring antiarrhythmic therapy.

After gradual hemodynamic recovery, vasopressors and MCS were successfully weaned after 12 days. Cardiac resynchronization therapy–defibrillator implantation was subsequently performed because of persistent severe ventricular dysfunction and conduction disease. Despite recurrent pulmonary edema during recovery, the patient ultimately improved clinically and was discharged home in stable condition.

Case 2

An 83-year-old woman with prior TAVR in 2018 using a 23-mm Edwards SAPIEN 3 valve, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and CKD stage 3b presented with acute hypoxic respiratory failure secondary to acute decompensated heart failure.

One month before presentation, outpatient TTE demonstrated severe degeneration of the transcatheter bioprosthetic valve manifesting predominantly as severe prosthetic AS with trace aortic regurgitation (AR) and moderate-to-severe MR. Despite aggressive medical therapy, she developed worsening respiratory distress requiring endotracheal intubation, followed by progressive hypotension necessitating norepinephrine and dobutamine support.

Repeat TTE confirmed persistent severe prosthetic AS with progression to mild-to-moderate AR and newly reduced LV systolic function (LVEF 30%-34%). The patient's hemodynamic deterioration was attributed primarily to severe prosthetic AS, whereas the associated AR was considered a secondary contributor. Her course was further complicated by acute kidney injury with acute tubular necrosis.

Given refractory CS, predominantly driven by severe prosthetic AS, and prohibitive risk for redo surgical valve replacement, the multidisciplinary Heart Team elected to proceed with urgent valve-in-valve TAVR as the most appropriate strategy to rapidly relieve the valvular obstruction. Because of persistent hemodynamic instability and the anticipated risk of periprocedural circulatory collapse, a 23-mm Edwards SAPIEN 3 Ultra valve was successfully implanted with concomitant IABP support for periprocedural MCS (Video 4, Video 5, Video 6). The IABP was selected to provide temporary hemodynamic stabilization and maintain systemic perfusion during the periprocedural period while awaiting recovery after relief of the valvular obstruction. Postprocedural imaging demonstrated normalization of prosthetic valve hemodynamics.

The patient was successfully weaned from IABP support 6 days later; however, renal dysfunction progressed, ultimately requiring intermittent hemodialysis. Follow-up TTE demonstrated marked improvement in ventricular systolic function (LVEF >70%). She was discharged to a skilled nursing facility with outpatient dialysis and close cardiology follow-up.

Case 3

A 73-year-old woman with a suspected bicuspid aortic valve, severe low-flow, low-gradient AS, CKD stage 3, and severe LV dysfunction initially presented to an affiliated hospital with acute decompensated heart failure complicated by CS. Because TAVR was not immediately available at the referring hospital, she was stabilized with vasopressor support, milrinone infusion, femoral IABP support, and continuous renal replacement therapy as bridging measures to maintain end-organ perfusion and hemodynamic stability while awaiting transfer to the structural heart center for urgent multidisciplinary team evaluation and definitive transcatheter intervention.

TTE demonstrated severe calcific AS with severely reduced ventricular systolic function (LVEF 20%-24%). Preprocedural computed tomography demonstrated a Sievers type 1 bicuspid aortic valve with left-right cusp fusion and a heavily calcified raphe. After transfer and expedited multidisciplinary evaluation, the patient was considered an appropriate candidate for definitive transcatheter therapy. Given the complex bicuspid anatomy with a heavily calcified raphe and the patient's profound hemodynamic instability, a self-expanding valve was selected to facilitate controlled deployment. Urgent transfemoral TAVR was performed using a 29-mm Evolut FX valve (Medtronic) with complete resolution of transvalvular gradients.

The procedure was complicated by new-onset left bundle branch block requiring temporary pacing support. Postprocedurally, the patient was successfully weaned from IABP and inotropic therapy. Her hospitalization was complicated by acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy later transitioned to intermittent hemodialysis.

After stabilization of hemodynamics and optimization of medical therapy, she was discharged home with outpatient dialysis. At 1-month follow-up, she reported marked symptomatic improvement.

Case 4

A 59-year-old man with multiple prior AV interventions, including surgical AV replacement, redo valve surgery, prior prosthetic valve endocarditis, and valve-in-valve TAVR in 2018, presented with dyspnea and hypoxic respiratory failure. His medical history was additionally notable for coronary artery disease status post coronary artery bypass grafting and heart failure (LVEF 40%).

Recent transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated significant paravalvular leak of the transcatheter valve, and the patient was awaiting surgical patch repair. However, repeat imaging during the current hospitalization demonstrated that the dominant mechanism of valve failure was severe transvalvular prosthetic AR with severely reduced LV systolic function (LVEF 20%-24%), whereas the previously identified paravalvular leak was no longer considered the primary driver of the patient's hemodynamic deterioration. During hospitalization, the patient developed worsening hypotension and progressive CS.

Given severe transvalvular prosthetic valve dysfunction and prohibitive surgical risk after multiple prior sternotomies, the multidisciplinary Heart Team proceeded with emergent valve-in-valve TAVR using a 34-mm Evolut FX valve with femoral IABP and vasopressor support.

Postprocedural imaging demonstrated trace residual transvalvular AR and no significant paravalvular leak, with stable prosthetic valve function. The patient was successfully weaned from MCS 3 days later with stabilization of hemodynamics and respiratory status. At 3-month follow-up, he reported improvement in heart failure symptoms, although severe LV dysfunction persisted (LVEF 25%).

Case 5

A 69-year-old man with ischemic cardiomyopathy, prior surgical bioprosthetic AV replacement, valve-in-valve TAVR with a 34-mm Evolut XL valve (Medtronic), coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, and severe LV dysfunction presented with acute decompensated heart failure and progressive hypoxemia.

TTE demonstrated degeneration of the prosthetic valve with combined severe AS and severe AR (LVEF 25%-29%).

Given worsening heart failure and concern for CS physiology, urgent valve-in-valve TAVR was performed using a 26-mm Edwards SAPIEN 3 valve. Postprocedural imaging demonstrated marked improvement in prosthetic valve hemodynamics without significant residual regurgitation.

The patient remained hemodynamically stable after intervention and was discharged on optimized guideline-directed medical therapy. At 1-month follow-up, he reported symptomatic improvement without recurrent heart failure hospitalization.

Multivalvular and Right-Sided Shock Physiology

Patients with multivalvular disease and advanced right ventricular dysfunction represented a more complex shock phenotype characterized by persistent pulmonary hypertension, progressive end-organ dysfunction, and incomplete recovery despite technically successful intervention.

Case 6

A 73-year-old man with chronic diastolic heart failure, moderate-to-severe right ventricular dysfunction, and pulmonary hypertension presented with near syncope and acute decompensated heart failure. Preprocedural TTE demonstrated severe rheumatic mitral stenosis (mitral valve area 1.3 cm2, mean transmitral gradient 9 mm Hg at a heart rate of 60 beats/min and a Wilkins score of 11) along with severe AS. Because the patient was deemed prohibitive risk for multivalve surgery, the multidisciplinary Heart Team considered severe AS to be the predominant lesion responsible for the patient's acute clinical presentation and elected to perform transfemoral TAVR as the initial strategy, with planned reassessment of the hemodynamic significance of the mitral stenosis after restoration of aortic valve function. A 29-mm Evolut FX valve was implanted.

Despite successful TAVR, his course was complicated by worsening right ventricular dysfunction, progressive CS, and acute-on-chronic renal failure requiring continuous renal replacement therapy, vasopressor and inotropic support. Serial echocardiography demonstrated persistent severe mitral stenosis despite a normally functioning transcatheter aortic valve, with transmitral mean gradients increasing from 14 mm Hg (mitral valve area 0.6 cm2) to 18 mm Hg (mitral valve area 0.3 cm2).

Because severe mitral stenosis persisted despite successful TAVR and continued to contribute to low-output physiology and pulmonary hypertension, high-risk balloon mitral valvuloplasty was subsequently performed, reducing the transmitral gradient from 12-14 mm Hg to 7-8 mm Hg. Follow-up TTE confirmed sustained improvement with a mean transmitral gradient of 8 mm Hg and a mitral valve area of 1.1 cm2. Despite transient hemodynamic improvement, the patient developed progressive multiorgan failure with worsening hepatic congestion and metabolic encephalopathy.

Because of concern for a significant left-to-right shunt across an iatrogenic atrial septal defect, percutaneous closure was performed; however, refractory shock persisted, and the patient ultimately died after pulseless electrical activity arrest.

Postcardiotomy Shock and Secondary MR

In the following cases, severe secondary MR contributed to impaired forward cardiac output and inability to wean MCS in the setting of postcardiotomy shock and profound biventricular dysfunction.

Case 7

A 69-year-old man with bicuspid AS and multivessel CAD underwent surgical aortic valve replacement with concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting. Preoperative TTE demonstrated preserved LV systolic function with no structural mitral valve abnormality or significant MR; therefore, concomitant mitral valve intervention was not indicated at the time of the index surgery. His postoperative course was complicated by severe postcardiotomy CS requiring central venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO).

After separation from cardiopulmonary bypass, intraoperative TEE demonstrated severe secondary MR caused by mitral annular dilatation and bileaflet tethering in the setting of severe LV dysfunction (20%-24%) and right ventricular dysfunction, contributing to impaired forward cardiac output and inability to wean MCS.

Given persistent hemodynamic instability and prohibitive surgical risk for repeat intervention, salvage mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) was performed using a MitraClip XTW device (Abbott) at the A2-P2 scallops, resulting in reduction of MR to trace.

Despite procedural success, the patient remained dependent on VA-ECMO with persistent biventricular dysfunction and escalating vasopressor requirements. After multidisciplinary discussions with the family, care was transitioned to comfort-focused measures, and the patient died 8 days after intervention.

Case 8

A 51-year-old woman underwent minimally invasive surgical aortic valve replacement with a mechanical prosthesis for bicuspid AS. Preoperative TTE demonstrated preserved LV systolic function with only mild MR; therefore, mitral valve intervention was not indicated during the initial surgical procedure. Her postoperative course was complicated by acute prosthetic valve dysfunction with CS requiring emergent Bentall procedure, IABP placement, and central VA-ECMO support. She subsequently developed severe right ventricular failure requiring right ventricular assist device support.

Persistent pulmonary edema and inability to wean MCS prompted repeat TEE, which demonstrated new severe secondary MR caused by LV dysfunction and severe posterior leaflet tethering with absent leaflet coaptation.

Given refractory shock physiology and prohibitive surgical risk, salvage M-TEER was performed using a single MitraClip XTW device at the A2-P2 position, resulting in reduction of MR to mild.

Although the intervention improved forward cardiac output and allowed subsequent IABP removal, the patient later developed catastrophic neurologic injury with right middle cerebral artery infarction and subarachnoid hemorrhage while receiving anticoagulation for MCS and prosthetic valve thrombosis prevention. Despite maximal supportive care, neurologic injury progressed, and the patient ultimately died.

Discussion

The present series reflects real-world complexity and highlights practical contemporary approaches to transcatheter management in critically ill patients under-represented in clinical trials. Our experience underscores important principles relevant to the management of valvular cardiogenic shock in the structural heart era.

First, outcomes appeared to depend less on procedural success alone and more on shock phenotype, ventricular reserve, and extent of end-organ dysfunction at the time of intervention. Although all patients achieved immediate technical success with significant hemodynamic improvement, recovery remained highly variable, particularly among patients with advanced right ventricular dysfunction, postcardiotomy shock, or multiorgan failure.

Patients with predominant left-sided obstructive physiology generally experienced the greatest hemodynamic stabilization after restoration of forward flow with TAVR or valve-in-valve TAVR. In these patients, temporary MCS, particularly IABP support, frequently provided an effective bridge to intervention by improving coronary perfusion and reducing afterload during severe low-output states. In contrast, patients with postcardiotomy shock or advanced biventricular dysfunction often demonstrated persistent circulatory failure despite technically successful intervention, emphasizing that correction of valvular pathology alone may be insufficient once advanced myocardial injury and systemic hypoperfusion are established.1

Our series also highlights the expanding role of transcatheter rescue therapies in critically ill patients considered prohibitive surgical candidates because of prior sternotomies, severe ventricular dysfunction, or multiorgan failure. In these scenarios, transcatheter therapies provided a feasible strategy for rapid hemodynamic stabilization with lower procedural burden than conventional surgery.6

Valve platform selection also represents an important procedural consideration in patients with valvular CS. Although both balloon-expandable and self-expanding transcatheter valves achieve excellent procedural outcomes, device selection should be individualized according to patient anatomy, hemodynamic status, and procedural objectives. In patients with complex bicuspid anatomy, heavily calcified valves, or profound hemodynamic instability, self-expanding valves may offer procedural advantages through their controlled, recapturable deployment and reduced reliance on prolonged rapid ventricular pacing compared with balloon-expandable platforms. Conversely, balloon-expandable valves remain an excellent option in appropriately selected patients and may be preferred in other anatomic or procedural scenarios. Given the limited comparative evidence in patients with CS, valve platform selection should remain guided by multidisciplinary Heart Team assessment and operator experience.

The postcardiotomy cases further illustrated the complex interaction between secondary MR, ventricular dysfunction, and persistent shock physiology. Although M-TEER resulted in immediate reduction in MR severity and transient hemodynamic improvement, both patients ultimately experienced poor outcomes because of refractory biventricular failure and severe systemic complications.

Renal dysfunction also emerged as an important determinant of outcome throughout the series. Acute kidney injury frequently persisted despite successful valve intervention and initial hemodynamic improvement, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and treatment before progression to advanced end-organ dysfunction.

Finally, these cases highlight the importance of individualized multidisciplinary management involving structural interventional cardiology, advanced heart failure, cardiac surgery, imaging, and critical care teams.7 Given the heterogeneity of presentations and lack of standardized treatment pathways, management remains highly patient-specific and time-sensitive.

Conclusions

Valvular cardiogenic shock is a high-risk condition in which severe structural valve dysfunction leads to profound hemodynamic compromise and multiorgan failure. Transcatheter valve interventions may provide meaningful hemodynamic stabilization in carefully selected patients with prohibitive surgical risk.

Successful management requires early recognition, rapid hemodynamic and echocardiographic assessment, individualized MCS, and close multidisciplinary collaboration. However, outcomes remain strongly influenced by ventricular reserve and the severity of end-organ dysfunction at the time of intervention.

Phenotype-Based Management of Valvular Cardiogenic Shock

Rapid recognition, multimodality imaging, invasive hemodynamic assessment, and multidisciplinary Heart Team evaluation are critical for timely diagnosis and management of valvular cardiogenic shock. Mechanical circulatory support (MCS) strategies should be individualized according to shock phenotype and ventricular failure pattern, ranging from intra-aortic balloon pump support in left-sided obstructive shock to venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and right ventricular assist device support in postcardiotomy biventricular failure. Contemporary transcatheter interventions, including transcatheter aortic valve replacement, valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), balloon mitral valvuloplasty, and transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, may provide meaningful hemodynamic stabilization in carefully selected patients with prohibitive surgical risk. However, outcomes remain strongly influenced by ventricular reserve and the severity of end-organ dysfunction at the time of intervention.
