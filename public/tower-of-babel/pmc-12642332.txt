The mediating role of emotion regulation and cognitive flexibility in the relationship between cooperative learning and social skills of students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wenwen Ma, Saeid Motevalli, Xing Kuang, Rui Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study examines the innovative roles of emotion regulation (ER) and cognitive flexibility (CF) as mediators in the association between cooperative learning (CL) and social skills among university students. Although prior research has investigated CL or social skills in isolation, limited studies have analyzed the concurrent mediation of this connection by ER and CF.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was executed with 502 university students in Shandong, China, chosen by stratified random sampling. Participants administered the Social Skills Inventory, Cooperative Learning Application Scale, Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, and Cognitive Flexibility Inventory. Data were examined utilizing Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) to investigate direct and indirect effects.ResultsCooperative learning markedly improved students’ social competencies. Both emotional regulation and cognitive flexibility partially mediated this association, with cognitive load cooperative learning enhancing emotional sensitivity and emotion regulation, while cognitive flexibility affected social sensitivity and expressiveness.ConclusionsThe integration of emotional regulation and cognitive flexibility in educational curricula promotes the efficacy of cooperative learning, fostering emotional and social abilities. These findings offer practical insights for educators and policymakers to create organized interventions that promote whole student development.

Introduction

Social skills are crucial for the personal and professional growth of university students, markedly affecting their academic achievement, life happiness, and future employability. Research indicates that “soft skills,” often known as “people skills,” which include communication, empathy, and problem-solving capabilities, are crucial for success in academic and professional environments [1, 2]. Effective communication and cooperation abilities empower students to engage successfully in group assignments, navigate interpersonal relationships, and adjust to varied surroundings. Enhancing social skills cultivates robust relationships, collaboration, and flexibility in professional environments, rendering them essential in the contemporary interconnected landscape [3, 4].

Despite their significance, there is escalating apprehension that contemporary challenges—such as heightened dependence on digital communication and diminished in-person interactions—may impede the natural growth of these abilities. Excessive screen time and improper internet usage might diminish interpersonal ability, particularly among younger students [5]. The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the significance of social skills, especially in healthcare professions where empathy and efficient communication are essential [6]. These challenges highlight the necessity of discovering effective educational interventions to alleviate the effects of social isolation and digital dependency.

In response to the changing employment market, higher educational institutions are progressively integrating 21st-century skills into their courses [7]. By prioritizing both technical proficiency and interpersonal skills, institutions can more effectively equip graduates for the contemporary workforce. Research indicates that numerous first-year students overrate their social abilities, highlighting the necessity for early interventions and self-assessment instruments [8]. The gap in accurate self-perception underscores the essential requirement for structured programs that promote social skills development, guaranteeing that pupils are both technically proficient and socially skilled. Moreover, developing sustainable leadership skills fosters personal development and equips students for future professional obligations [9].

Cooperative learning is a successful pedagogical method that enhances students’ social skills and academic performance by fostering mutual support, accountability, empathy, and meaningful communication in varied group environments [10]. Cooperative learning enhances students’ social competency and cultivates a friendly classroom culture. This method equips learners to navigate intricate interpersonal dynamics and adapt proficiently in professional environments. Research repeatedly demonstrates that cooperative learning strategies—such as the Community of Inquiry model and Aronson’s Jigsaw technique—enhance both interpersonal skills and academic performance [11, 12]. This cooperative setting fosters analytical reasoning, emotional intelligence, and appreciation for variety. However, problems in group dynamics may emerge, underscoring the necessity for proficient facilitation [13]. Gender disparities may also affect cooperative experiences; female students frequently acknowledge its significance for cultivating teaching and collaborative abilities [12].

The motivation and preceding experiences of students influence their attitudes towards cooperative learning. Favorable experiences in collaborative environments augment engagement and facilitate the development of social skills [14]. In summary, cooperative learning enhances communication, teamwork, and analytical skills—essential components for success in both academic and professional environments. Highlighting collaborative learning cultivates socially adept graduates prepared to engage with their communities and professional environments.

Moreover, cooperative learning yields emotional advantages. Strategies that foster goal interdependence and peer support augment enjoyment and engagement, especially in language acquisition [15]. However, difficulties in emotion regulation (ER) can influence the link between social anxiety and problematic internet use [16]. Consequently, incorporating emotional control training with cooperative learning can enhance students’ emotional well-being and task performance. Enhancing emotional regulation capabilities additionally augments self-management, diminishes procrastination, and elevates academic perseverance [17, 18]. Integrating emotion control into university curricula fosters emotional and intellectual development, thereby equipping students for professional and personal problems.

Cooperative learning favorably influences several facets of student development, including the reduction of bullying and the enhancement of empathy [19], the improvement of social skills [20], and the augmentation of emotional intelligence in physical education [17]. Cognitive flexibility serves as a vital mediating element in these processes. As a fundamental element of executive processes, it profoundly impacts academic success, especially in older pupils [21]. Cognitive flexibility is associated with resilience and distress tolerance [22], both of which are crucial for effective social adaptation and voluntary self-regulation [23]. CF enhances learning and social adaption by assisting students in acclimating to varied perspectives and changing circumstances.

Emotion regulation (ER) denotes the mechanisms by which humans govern their emotional experiences, manifestations, and reactions. Gross [24] asserts that emotional regulation includes a range of adaptive and maladaptive methods that affect mental health and interpersonal efficacy. The capacity to regulate emotions is essential for stress management, goal attainment, and sustaining positive relationships. Research highlights the significance of emotional regulation in students’ well-being, resilience, and psychological capital [25–27]. Efficient emotion regulation improves emotional stability, learning pleasure, and general well-being [28]. Tang and He discovered a substantial correlation between ER and learning satisfaction and engagement among Chinese university students during the COVID-19 epidemic [27]. Similarly, Zheng and Zhou reported that well-regulated emotions enhance language learning satisfaction in cooperative learning environments [15]. Nonetheless, inadequate emotion rehulation can intensify social anxiety and maladaptive behaviors, including detrimental online habits [16]. Hence, promoting emotional regulation within collaborative learning environments is essential for equitable emotional and social development.

Although previous research has investigated cooperative learning and emotional regulation separately, limited studies have analyzed the mediating role of emotional regulation in their link with social and academic results. This gap prompts the present research, which examines emotional regulation as a mediation mechanism connecting cooperative learning to enhanced social skills. Comprehending this pathway facilitates the creation of therapies that concurrently enhance learning outcomes and students’ emotional well-being.

Cognitive flexibility (CF) enhances emotional regulation (ER) by allowing individuals to alter viewpoints, modify techniques, and adapt to intricate learning environments. It enables pupils to respond adeptly to feedback, tackle assignments innovatively, and uphold open-mindedness [29]. Research repeatedly demonstrates CF’s beneficial effects on social skills, emotional intelligence, and academic achievement [17, 19, 20]. Collaborative learning environments that foster collective accountability and peer cooperation boost CF, resulting in improved communication, empathy, and problem-solving skills [21–23]. Early-life stress influences behavioral flexibility, highlighting the interconnectedness of cognitive processes in both social and non-social environments [30]. Nonetheless, investigations into CF’s particular mediating function in cooperative learning are scarce. Numerous research have concentrated on direct impacts while neglecting to investigate the underlying mechanisms, resulting in a significant empirical void.

This study seeks to address this gap by investigating the mediating function of cognitive flexibility in the association between cooperative learning and the development of social skills among university students. It aims to elucidate how CF amplifies the advantages of cooperative learning, offering insights for the formulation of evidence-based educational methodologies. This research enhances the literature by including emotional and cognitive factors to elucidate how cooperative learning fosters students’ comprehensive development. It examines the dual mediating effects of emotional regulation and cognitive flexibility on the relationship between cooperative learning and social skills. This study addresses the pressing necessity for treatments that enhance social competence in higher education by examining these interconnected aspects. The importance is heightened by current issues such as digital reliance, social alienation, and changing workplace requirements. This study advances higher education and behavioral research by investigating how cooperative learning improves social skills via the mediating roles of emotion control and cognitive flexibility. It emphasizes the necessity of developing interpersonal and adaptive skills among university students through empirical research and practical recommendations—skills essential for academic achievement, employment, and enduring resilience.

Literature review

Social skills are crucial for the academic achievement, personal development, and employability of university students. Research demonstrates that skills such as communication, collaboration, and problem-solving substantially impact academic success and career preparedness, highlighting the necessity of organized interventions to enhance these capabilities [1]. Gonzalez Moreno et al. discovered that children exhibiting elevated emotional intelligence and interpersonal competence demonstrated greater engagement in group activities and sustained good social relationships, so underscoring the significance of social skills for comprehensive development [2]. Likewise, students possessing advanced social competencies had superior academic results and exhibited enhanced collaboration and conflict-resolution abilities, indicating that social skill training has to be incorporated into higher education curricula [3]. Mohammad and Ozdamli investigated experiential learning programs, indicating that organized collaborative activities improved communication, empathy, and adaptive social behaviors, hence endorsing the integration of cooperative pedagogical tactics [4].

Contemporary problems, like excessive digital reliance, social isolation, and diminished in-person encounters, impede the development of social skills. Romero-Lopez et al. indicated that excessive online activity constrains interpersonal interaction and diminishes opportunities for social development [5]. This issue was particularly pronounced during the COVID-19 epidemic, notably in healthcare education, where empathy and good communication are essential [6]. These findings underscore the necessity of integrating social skill training into both physical and virtual educational settings.

Cooperative learning (CL) is widely acknowledged as an excellent instructional method for enhancing social skills, academic performance, and psychological well-being. Research indicates that frameworks like the Community of Inquiry and Aronson’s Jigsaw improve communication, empathy, collaboration, and problem-solving among peers [11, 12]. Lenkauskaite observed issues in group coordination and gender-based involvement, emphasizing the necessity for proficient facilitation to optimize the benefits of CL [13]. Moreover, Bächtold et al. observed that students’ previous experiences and motivation significantly influence involvement and results in collaborative activities, impacting the development of social skills [14].

Emotion regulation (ER) is essential for maximizing the advantages of cooperative learning. Gross characterized ER as the mechanisms through which humans govern their emotional experiences, expressions, and perceptions [24]. Studies indicate that effective emotional regulation improves resilience, psychological capital, learning pleasure, and engagement [25–27]. In collaborative settings, positive goal interdependence and peer support have demonstrated the capacity to enhance emotional regulation, hence facilitating the acquisition of social skills [15]. Poor emotional regulation can exacerbate social anxiety and result in maladaptive behaviors, including excessive internet usage [16–18].

Cognitive flexibility (CF) functions as an essential mediating variable. Research demonstrates that CL facilitates students’ adaptation to evolving academic and social environments, enhancing empathy, communication, and performance [17, 19, 20]. Moe discovered that reflective activities, such as episodic recall, enhanced well-being and cognitive adaptability in university students [31], whereas [32] illustrated the connections between CF, learner autonomy, and reflective thinking, emphasizing CF’s significance in self-regulated learning. Moreover, Orakci and Khalili indicated that CF positively affected the critical thinking dispositions of prospective EFL teachers, mediated by self-efficacy, demonstrating CF’s extensive applicability in enhancing cognitive and social competencies [33]. Cognitive flexibility can be cultivated through mindfulness training, enhancing adaptability in collaborative learning contexts [34], whereas early-life stress affects behavioral flexibility, highlighting the interaction between cognitive processes and social engagement [30].

Although data substantiates the distinct advantages of CL, ER, and CF, there is a paucity of research investigating the mechanisms that connect CL to the development of social skills through ER and CF. This work fills the empirical vacuum by investigating how ER and CF regulate the relationship between cooperative learning and students’ social abilities, offering innovative theoretical and practical insights for developing evidence-based interventions in higher education.

Theoretical background

This research is based on four fundamental theoretical frameworks: Vygotsky’s Sociocultural Theory, Bandura’s Social Cognitive Learning Theory, and Gross’s Emotion Regulation Theory. Each theory enhances comprehension of the interaction between cooperative learning, emotional control, and the cultivation of social skills in university students.

Social constructivism theory

Social Constructivism, as proposed by Vygotsky [35, 36] asserts that knowledge is collaboratively built through social interaction and cultural environment, rather than by individual discovery alone. The Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) is fundamental to this perspective, representing the disparity between a learner’s autonomous capabilities and their potential achievements with assistance. Consequently, learning transpires through partnership with educators or peers who offer scaffolding to enhance cognitive development [36, 37].

Constructivist theories, such as Piaget’s cognitive constructivism, assert that learning must be an active process of meaning-making rather than a passive reception. Educators serve as facilitators who direct inquiry and reflection, whereas learners engage actively in the construction of knowledge. Cooperative learning closely adheres to these concepts, facilitating the interchange of viewpoints, co-construction of understanding, and enhancement of cognitive and social abilities through dialogue and collaborative discovery [13, 38]. In collaborative settings, engagement and discourse facilitate the reorganization and internalization of knowledge among learners. This process fosters emotional management and cognitive flexibility, as pupils reconcile differing opinions, handle frustration, and adjust to various perspectives. Thus, social constructivism underpins the comprehension of how collaboration enhances both academic success and social proficiency [38, 39].

Group dynamics theory

John Dewey’s notion of democratic education highlighted active participation and collaboration to cultivate critical thinking and social responsibility among students [40]. Herbert Thelen advanced Group Dynamics Theory (GDT), emphasizing that organized group interactions facilitate individual and collective learning [41]. Cooperative learning based on GDT improves academic and social skills via collaboration, communication, and mutual accountability. Kurt Lewin advanced this domain by analyzing human behavior in group contexts, emphasizing processes like leadership and decision-making [42]. This principle corresponds with Johnson and Johnson’s categorizations of dependency types [43].

Collaboration and communication—fundamental to both GDT and Vygotsky’s sociocultural theory [36]—facilitate students in exchanging ideas, negotiating meaning, and enhancing social and cognitive skills. Group Dynamics Theory offers a pragmatic framework for developing cooperative learning activities that improve cooperation, empathy, and communication, while fostering emotional regulation and cognitive flexibility. Comprehending group dynamics enables educators to enhance collaborative learning, so fostering academic achievement, social skills, and psychological health in students.

Emotion regulation theory

Emotions are intricate psychophysiological reactions that affect cognition, behavior, and social interaction [44]. Gross’s Emotion Regulation Theory (ERT) delineates five principal mechanisms by which individuals regulate their emotions: context selection, situation alteration, attentional deployment, cognitive change, and response modulation [24, 45, 46]. These mechanisms dictate the timing and manner of emotional experiences and expressions, influencing mental health and interpersonal interactions.

In collaborative learning settings, students experience diverse emotional signals, including excitement, anxiety, and dissatisfaction. The capacity to manage these emotions is essential for sustaining engagement and facilitating effective collaboration. Antecedent-focused methods assist students in predicting and alleviating emotional challenges, whereas response-focused strategies facilitate the management of expressions and reactions during group interactions. Emotional regulation improves communication, empathy, and group cohesiveness, hence promoting a positive and inclusive learning environment.

Furthermore, proficient emotion management enhances cognitive flexibility, enabling students to adjust their thoughts and behaviors in reaction to peer feedback and evolving group dynamics. The interplay between emotional regulation and adaptation enhances social competence and assists educators in creating collaborative learning environments that foster emotional resilience and social growth.

Cognitive flexibility theory

Cognitive flexibility denotes the mental capacity to modify thoughts and behaviors in reaction to novel, unforeseen, or evolving situations [47]. It is an essential element of executive processes, allowing individuals to transition between views, tasks, or tactics when circumstances change [48, 49]. In psychological research, it is regarded as a domain-general ability that enhances flexible problem-solving and learning across various contexts [50, 51].

Cognitive Flexibility Theory (CFT), formulated by Spiro and others, asserts that conventional, linear learning models are insufficient for intricate settings. Instead, learners must cultivate interconnected and dynamic knowledge frameworks that enable them to comprehend information from various perspectives [52–54]. Effective learning necessitates strategic knowledge of cognitive processes and the capacity to choose suitable problem-solving methods based on circumstances [55, 56]. Cognitive flexibility in cooperative learning situations allows students to adapt to varied group interactions and evolving social or academic requirements. This adaptability cultivates empathy, creativity, and emotional management, enabling kids to respond constructively to diverse ideas. Fostering cognitive flexibility via active, discussion-oriented learning—such as case-based or problem-based activities—enhances comprehensive development by reinforcing problem-solving abilities, emotional resilience, and social competence.

Integrative summary

The four theoretical perspectives—SCT, GDT, ERT, and CFT—collectively provide a robust framework for comprehending how cooperative learning enhances social, emotional, and cognitive skill development in university students. Specifically, SCT emphasizes that knowledge is collaboratively created through social interaction and shared cultural experiences, thereby establishing the basis for cooperative learning. In addition, GDT enhances this perspective by highlighting how organized group interactions foster collaboration, communication, and interdependence—essential components for cultivating both emotional and cognitive competencies. Furthermore, ERT elucidates how students navigate emotional responses in collaborative settings, thereby promoting empathy, communication, and peer cohesion vital for enduring cooperation. Likewise, CFT elucidates how learners modify their cognitive techniques in response to evolving group dynamics, varied perspectives, and problem-solving requirements. Taken together, these perspectives demonstrate that social interaction promotes engagement (SCT), group processes foster cooperation (GDT), emotional regulation maintains productive relationships (ERT), and cognitive flexibility supports adaptive learning (CFT). Therefore, they collectively establish a strong theoretical foundation for comprehending how cooperative learning environments enhance students’ holistic development in higher education.

Cooperative learning (CL) enhances emotion regulation (ER) by creating structured, interactive settings that necessitate student collaboration, role negotiation, and conflict resolution. For example, previous studies investigated emotional presence in collaborative learning, demonstrating that students who actively participated in peer interactions were more adept at regulating their emotional states, hence enhancing engagement and motivation [57, 58]. In addition, Naykki et al., conducted a process-oriented case study in higher education, emphasizing that socio-emotional conflicts in group work provided students with opportunity to reflect on their emotions, regulate frustration, and cultivate interpersonal coping methods [59]. Together, these studies highlight that structured CL activities foster dynamic social situations in which students can develop adaptive ER skills in real-time contexts.

Furthermore, collaborative learning contexts facilitate both antecedent-focused and response-focused emotion regulation. For instance, Nguyen examined gamified flipped classrooms incorporating social-emotional learning, revealing that students engaged in collaborative activities exhibited enhanced emotional regulation, increased communication skills, and elevated academic performance [60]. Research showed that students participating in collaborative learning activities demonstrated enhanced well-being and academic engagement attributable to increased emotional regulation skills [27]. Methods

Research design

This study employed a quantitative, cross-sectional survey methodology to examine the influence of cooperating learning on students’ social skills, with emotion regulation and cognitive flexibility as mediating variables. This design was selected due to its capacity for efficient data gathering from a substantial sample at a single point, rendering it appropriate for analyzing existing correlations among various variables.

Population and sample

The research was carried out at the Shandong University of Engineering and Vocational Technology in Jinan City, Shandong Province, China. The study examined the mediating effects of emotion control and cognitive flexibility on the link between cooperative learning and social skills in students. A stratified random sampling strategy was utilized to guarantee a methodologically rigorous approach, facilitating proportional representation of essential subgroups within the population. A stratified random sample procedure was utilized to guarantee a thorough representation of the student population, considering the varied features of students from different disciplines, so improving the generalizability and representativeness of the results. The overall student population was categorized into strata according to their academic disciplines among 16 faculties. encompassing engineering, vocational studies, and liberal arts. The researchers sought to obtain a sample size that appropriately represented the distribution of students in these programs. A total sample of 400 students was designated, including 200 from engineering, 150 from vocational studies, and 50 from liberal arts. This proportionate distribution guaranteed that each field was sufficiently represented in the investigation. The stratified random sampling method was employed by randomly choosing students from each stratum, so ensuring that the characteristics of each subgroup were accurately represented in the sample. This selection technique was developed to represent the diversity of the whole university community, guaranteeing that the findings may be extrapolated to students from all fields, academic levels, and program kinds. Moreover, particular inclusion criteria were established to pick participants, encompassing full-time undergraduate enrollment, active engagement in cooperative learning activities, and the availability to complete the study questionnaire. Students with incomplete enrollment or those who did not engage in cooperative learning modules were eliminated to ensure the sample accurately represented the population pertinent to the study objectives. Further attempts were undertaken to incorporate people from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds and varying academic achievement levels to enhance the sample’s representativeness. Moreover, intentional measures were implemented to incorporate kids from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds and varying levels of academic achievement, hence enhancing the study’s representativeness and external validity.

Participants were notified that their replies would be utilized solely for research purposes, with guarantees of confidentiality and anonymity. Data collection was executed methodically by engaging participants during designated class sessions and through university email solicitations. Researchers supplied explicit instructions and consent forms before participation, ensuring that students comprehended the study’s goal, procedures, and voluntary nature. The questionnaire was sent in both paper and online modes to enhance accessibility and increase the response rate. Moreover, follow-up reminders and assistance were offered to elucidate any inquiries and promote completion. Data collection occurred across four months, from February to April 2024, employing a systematic questionnaire to evaluate many aspects of cooperative learning, emotion control, cognitive flexibility, and social skills. To augment the study’s rigor, the sample was stratified by gender to provide extensive gender-based analysis, hence enhancing the comprehensiveness and objectivity of the findings. Measures

A structured questionnaire was modified and employed to gather data from participants, organized into four principal parts, each aligned with the primary variables of the study.

The cooperative learning application scale

The Cooperative Learning Application Scale was developed by [63] to evaluate the concurrent validity of four of the five sub-scales of our questionnaire, the following aspects of the CLAS were utilized: social skills (four items), group processing (four items), positive interdependence (four items), and promotive interaction (four items). Participants are requested to recall instances when they perceive themselves as more effective in an academic setting: “I feel successful at school when.”. Responses were provided on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from (5) strongly agree to (1) strongly disagree. The various subscales demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency (≥ 0.70). Due to the absence of a Chinese version of the CLAS, the instrument was translated employing a rigorous translation and back-translation methodology to guarantee conceptual and language comparability. Initially, two multilingual specialists independently translated the questionnaire from English into Chinese. The translated versions were subsequently compared, and discrepancies were addressed through debate to provide a preliminary Chinese version. A distinct multilingual expert, unaware of the original version, subsequently back-translated the questionnaire into English. The back-translated version was juxtaposed with the original scale to ascertain semantic comparability and coherence. Minor adjustments were implemented to accommodate cultural subtleties and improve clarity for Chinese university students. This procedure guaranteed that the translated CLAS preserved the structural integrity, meaning, and psychometric characteristics of the original instrument while being culturally suitable for the target audience.

The social skill inventory (SSI)

The research utilized the 90-item Social Skills Inventory (SSI) [64] to evaluate six dimensions of social competencies: Emotional Expressivity (EE), Emotional Sensitivity (ES), Emotional Control (EC), Social Expressivity (SE), Social Sensitivity (SS), and Social Control (SC), comprising 15 items for each subscale. Responses were gathered on a 5-point Likert scale, with subscale scores varying from 15 to 75, and the overall SSI score calculated by aggregating all subscales. Prior studies have shown adequate psychometric properties of the SSI for collegiate populations, featuring a Cronbach’s α of 0.81, test-retest reliability of 0.75, split-half reliability of 0.84, and correlations between total and subscale scores varying from 0.305 to 0.802 [65]. Confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated satisfactory model fit (χ²/df = 2.2, GFI = 0.888, CFI = 0.903, IFI = 0.916, RMSEA = 0.076). The results affirm that the SSI is a dependable and valid tool for assessing social skills in Chinese university students.

Emotion regulation questionnaire (ERQ)

The research utilized the 10-item Chinese Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ-C) [66] to assess two primary strategies: cognitive reappraisal (6 questions, e.g., “I modify my thoughts to manage emotions”) and expressive suppression (4 items, e.g., “I hide my emotions”). Responses were gathered on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 7 = strongly agree), with subscale scores calculated independently. The ERQ-C was meticulously translated and modified to capture cultural nuances in Chinese collectivist settings while preserving structural equivalence to the original [67].

Cognitive flexibility inventory (CFI)

The research utilized the 20-item Chinese Cognitive Flexibility Inventory (CFI) [68] to evaluate two principal dimensions: Alternatives (the development of many choices) and Control (the modification of cognition in response to environmental demands). Responses were documented using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never; 5 = always), comprising six items, with items 2, 4, and 7 being reverse scored. Subscale scores were computed independently (Alternatives: 10–50; Control: 10–50), resulting in a total score range of 20–100, with higher values signifying enhanced cognitive flexibility. The Chinese CFI exhibits strong reliability, with Cronbach’s α coefficients and composite reliability surpassing 0.80, and test-retest reliability exceeding 0.65, validating its appropriateness for evaluating cognitive flexibility in Chinese university students [55, 68, 69]. The CFI was meticulously modified for cultural relevance while preserving authenticity to the original instrument, so providing precise assessment of cognitive flexibility within the Chinese educational framework.

A pilot study involving 48 university students from Shandong University of Engineering and Vocational Technology was undertaken to evaluate the reliability of the scales employed in this research. Cronbach’s Alpha, a prominent technique for assessing internal consistency in educational research, was utilized. The Social Skills Inventory exhibited high reliability (α = 0.857), the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire displayed strong consistency (α = 0.834), and the Cooperative Learning Questionnaire demonstrated satisfactory reliability (α = 0.820), signifying that the items accurately assess their intended constructs. The Cognitive Flexibility measure, with a coefficient of α = 0.648, somewhat underperformed relative to the required threshold, indicating that additional refining may improve its consistency. KS Taber [70] asserts that Cronbach’s Alpha scores ranging from 0.60 to 0.70 are deemed minimally acceptable. The findings suggest that most scales are reliable and appropriate for the study, except for the Cognitive Flexibility index, which necessitates further assessment prior to its use in the main study.

Discussion and implications

This study emphasizes the mediating functions of emotion regulation (ER) and cognitive flexibility (CF) in the association between cooperative learning (CL) and social skills competences. Our findings demonstrate that students participating in collaborative learning (CL) showed enhancements in Emotional Sensitivity (H4-1), Emotional Control (H4-2), Emotional Expressivity (H4-3), and Social Expressivity (H4-6), indicating that collaborative settings can cultivate adaptive emotional strategies and improve learning engagement. Although previous studies [25–27] affirm the overall relationship between emotional regulation (ER) and academic performance, our research offers empirical evidence for the distinct mediation role of ER within the framework of collaborative learning (CL). The absence of substantial mediation for Social Sensitivity (H4-4) and Social Control (H4-5) indicates that emotional regulation may not uniformly affect all dimensions of social skills, implying the existence of contextual moderators or task-specific limitations that require further examination.

Cognitive flexibility was identified as a crucial mediator for Social Sensitivity (H5-4) and Social Expressivity (H5-6). These findings correspond with previous research indicating that collaborative learning (CL) can improve critical thinking (CF) by necessitating that students integrate diverse viewpoints, negotiate group decisions, and adjust to evolving social environments [17, 19, 20]. Importantly, CF did not significantly mediate Emotional Sensitivity (H5-1), Emotional Control (H5-2), Emotional Expressivity (H5-3), or Social Control (H5-5), indicating that although CF influences certain facets of social competence, its effect is selective and may be contingent upon the characteristics of the cooperative tasks or the social requirements present. This discovery contests the presumption that CF consistently improves all social and emotional outcomes and suggests the necessity of considering task structure and peer interaction dynamics in the design of CL interventions.

While our findings endorse the theoretical amalgamation of Emotional Regulation (ER) and Cognitive Flexibility (CF) in improving social skills, many limits necessitate examination. Initially, while CL promotes both antecedent-focused and response-focused emotion regulation [15, 60], the heterogeneity in student engagement and prior experience may influence the effectiveness of both mechanisms. The advantages of CF, although apparent in activities necessitating negotiation and perspective-taking, may be limited in more organized or individual-centric cooperative tasks [21, 30]. The nuances indicate that both emotional regulation (ER) and cognitive flexibility (CF) are essential yet insufficient prerequisites for optimal social skill development; their efficacy likely hinges on the congruence of task complexity, group makeup, and pedagogical scaffolding.

This study enhances current research by conclusively showing that the mediating processes of Emotional Regulation (ER) and Cognitive Flexibility (CF) in Coping Loneliness (CL) are specific to particular domains rather than being universal. Although previous research has established the advantages of collaborative learning (CL) for social skills [57–59], our results offer a more refined comprehension of the mechanisms and circumstances under which these internal regulating processes affect student outcomes. The observed selective mediation effects indicate that educators should create collaborative learning activities that explicitly address both cognitive and emotional demands, ensuring that tasks concurrently foster adaptive emotional regulation and conflict resolution to optimize social skill development.

Practical implications

This study’s findings possess numerous practical implications for educators and higher education institutions. Integrating cooperative learning (CL) into curricula can effectively boost students’ social competencies by promoting emotion regulation (ER) and cognitive flexibility (CF). Educators ought to provide collaborative activities that necessitate peer bargaining, perspective-taking, and problem-solving, as these components specifically enhance adaptive emotional regulation and cognitive flexibility, resulting in increased social skills.

Secondly, teacher training programs ought to prioritize the establishment of structured collaborative learning environments that expressly foster emotional and cognitive regulation. Methods include mindfulness practices, structured reflection, and facilitation of group dynamics can enhance students’ adaptive responses to social and academic obstacles [34, 60].

Third, policymakers ought to advocate for the integration of CL-based pedagogies into evidence-based educational reforms to foster comprehensive student development. Allocating resources to enhance collaborative learning infrastructure, digital platforms, and instructor training will guarantee uniform implementation and optimize the advantages of ER and CF development among varied student demographics [57, 58].

Ultimately, curriculum designers must customize collaborative projects to align with students’ cognitive and emotional preparedness, guaranteeing that activities are both demanding and nurturing, accompanied by systems for feedback and reflection. By specifically focusing on ER and CF in collaborative learning, educational institutions can develop graduates possessing vital 21st-century skills such as adaptability, emotional intelligence, and proficient social interaction, thus enhancing academic performance and long-term employability.

Theoretical contributions

This research significantly enhances the theoretical comprehension of emotional and social competences by synthesizing fundamental ideas from Social Constructivism Theory, Group Dynamics Theory, ER Theory, and CF Theory. This study explores the mediating roles of ER and CF within Cooperative Learning, broadening the theoretical framework beyond individual-centric models and emphasizing the interaction between external and internal factors that affect student behavior. From a Social Constructivism viewpoint, the findings highlight the significance of collaborative learning environments, where students interact with peers and educators, promoting the co-construction of knowledge and emotional competencies. The Group Dynamics Theory elucidates how group interactions and connections influence students’ social abilities and emotional control.

The incorporation of Emotion control Theory highlights the essential function of emotional competencies in educational environments, illustrating how proficient control may improve student engagement and overall success. Cognitive Flexibility Theory enhances these findings by emphasizing the significance of flexibility in thought and behavior, crucial for managing social interactions and emotional obstacles within a learning environment. This research fits with and enriches current theoretical frameworks, providing a more thorough understanding of how teacher motivation and cooperative learning settings impact student self-regulation, emotional development, and social abilities. These contributions establish a robust basis for forthcoming research focused on comprehending and enhancing student outcomes in educational environments.

Practical implications

This study holds significant practical implications for educators and policymakers in the formulation and execution of teaching strategies that foster emotional and social capabilities via Cooperative Learning. Educators are urged to incorporate Cooperative Learning into their instructional methodologies, as this approach has demonstrated enhancements in students’ emotional regulation, motivation, and social competencies [11, 76]. By cultivating a collaborative learning environment, educators can facilitate chances for students to collaborate, exchange knowledge, and enhance vital social skills while interacting with academic material.

Educational institutions should prioritize the enhancement of teacher training programs centered on successful Cooperative Learning methodologies to support this approach. Workshops and training sessions can enable educators to foster emotional regulation in pupils, promoting a proactive learning approach. Equipping educators with these tools enhances student support and motivation, resulting in enhanced academic outcomes [15].

Furthermore, policymakers ought to take into account the results of this study when formulating educational policies and curriculum. Highlighting Cooperative Learning can improve educational quality by fostering individualized learning plans, collaborative projects, and peer interactions, which are essential for mitigating procrastination tendencies. Investing resources in teacher training programs that prioritize Cooperative Learning methodologies would guarantee effective and uniform implementation in schools.

This study, although conducted in a unique university context, demonstrates that the concepts of Cooperative Learning, along with the mediating functions of emotion control and cognitive flexibility, are relevant across various educational environments. Prior studies indicate that Cooperative Learning tactics can be modified for various cultural, institutional, and age-specific contexts, while meticulous attention to local resources, pedagogical approaches, and student attributes is essential [77, 78]. Consequently, educators and policymakers in different contexts can utilize these findings as a framework, modifying implementation tactics to address specific contextual requirements.

By emphasizing both academic material and the social-emotional growth of children, the educational system can cultivate a more comprehensive learning environment that encourages well-rounded development. This study offers significant insights for educators and policymakers seeking to enhance student achievements in both the original context and diverse global educational settings, contingent upon the consideration of cultural and institutional modifications.

Limitations and recommendations of the study

One of the limitations of the current quantitative study is the relatively small sample size, which may impact the generalizability of the findings across various educational environments. Future study ought to employ bigger and more heterogeneous samples, including students from diverse universities, fields, and geographical areas, to substantiate the findings in wider contexts. The study utilized a cross-sectional research methodology, limiting the capacity to infer causal links across time. The findings reveal substantial connections; nevertheless, a longitudinal design would provide more profound insights into the evolution of instructors’ academic motivation and students’ self-regulation, as well as their enduring impacts on procrastinating behavior. This study utilized self-report tools for data collection instead of interviews, potentially introducing response biases. Future research may employ mixed approaches to enhance the data and offer a more thorough comprehension of the phenomena being examined. This research examined Emotion Regulation and study habits as mediators between teacher academic motivation and student procrastination behavior; future studies could investigate additional mediators, such as self-efficacy, academic resilience, or goal orientation, to enhance understanding of the impact of teacher motivation on student outcomes.

Future direction

Subsequent research may rectify the limitations of this study by employing longitudinal designs to investigate the enduring impacts of ER and CF on the association between Cooperative Learning and emotional and social competencies. It would be beneficial to investigate more mediators, such as self-efficacy, academic resilience, or goal orientation, to enhance comprehension of their impact on student results in Cooperative Learning settings. Furthermore, subsequent research might examine the efficacy of treatments designed to enhance teacher-student relationships, emotional management, and cognitive flexibility. Executing and evaluating targeted initiatives aimed at improving these abilities may yield significant insights into effective methods for cultivating emotional and social competencies in pupils. By concentrating on these domains, researchers may enhance the comprehension of how Cooperative Learning can be refined to promote student growth and well-being.

Conclusions

This study clarified the mediation roles of Emotional Regulation (ER) and Cognitive Flexibility (CF) in the association between Cooperative Learning and students’ emotional and social competences. The analysis of twelve hypotheses revealed that multiple pathways exhibit partial mediation, particularly highlighting substantial impacts on Emotional Sensitivity and Emotional Control through Emotional Regulation. The results demonstrate that Cooperative Learning directly improves emotional and social competencies while enhancing emotional regulation. Moreover, the study uncovered additional partial mediation effects of CF, particularly for its association with Social Sensitivity and Social Expressivity. Notably, specific routes, such as those connecting Cooperative Learning to Emotional Sensitivity and Emotional Control through CF, demonstrated no significant mediation, indicating a more complex interplay of elements involved. These findings underscore the necessity of integrating tactics that improve emotional regulation and cognitive flexibility in Cooperative Learning, thus facilitating students’ acquisition of essential social and emotional competencies and promoting overall academic and personal achievement.
