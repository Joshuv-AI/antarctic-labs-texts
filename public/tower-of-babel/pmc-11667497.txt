Impact of skilled nursing facility quality on Medicare beneficiaries with dementia: Evidence from vacancies
Authors: Cyrus M Kosar, Vincent Mor, Amal N Trivedi, Momotazur Rahman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONPeople living with dementia are less likely to be admitted to high‐rated nursing homes than people without dementia, despite their increased care needs. We investigated the effect of admission to nursing homes with higher staffing ratings on adverse outcomes for individuals with and without dementia post‐hospitalization.METHODSAmong Traditional Medicare beneficiaries discharged to nursing homes between 2011 and 2017, we examined the relationship between facility staffing star‐ratings and short‐term readmission and mortality using an instrumental variables approach to account for selection bias. The instrumental variables were the number of nearby vacant beds in high‐rated facilities.RESULTSAdmission to a higher‐rated nursing home lowered post‐discharge mortality risk at 90 days and reduced 30‐ and 90‐day readmission. Point estimates were larger for people with dementia.DISCUSSIONFindings underscore the need for enhancing direct care staffing in nursing homes and addressing access disparities, particularly for individuals with dementia who benefit significantly from high‐quality care.Highlights We assessed how admission to nursing homes with higher staffing ratings impacted outcomes for individuals with and without dementia by exploiting variation in local bed vacancies as a source of quasi‐random assignment.For both persons with and without dementia, adjusted short‐term mortality and readmission rates were lower among those discharged to nursing homes with higher staffing ratings.Effects were larger for persons with dementia, indicating welfare loss from inequitable access to higher‐rated nursing homes.Increasing staffing in nursing homes and reducing disparities for persons with dementia is essential for enhancing both equity and value. Keywords: medicaid, medicare, mortality, nursing home, quality of care, readmission, skilled nursing facility, staffing

BACKGROUND

Over 3 million unique individuals per year in the United States receive care in nursing homes, including a disproportionate share of individuals living with dementia. Because of the high health risk of nursing home residents and because the largest payers of nursing home services are the federal and state governments, quality of care has been a policy priority for several decades. 1 , 2 The delivery of care in nursing homes has also received persistent media attention and has undergone heightened scrutiny since the beginning of the COVID‐19 pandemic, which to date has claimed the lives of nearly 175,000 residents. 3 Despite the long focus on nursing home quality and the substantial amount of resources devoted to regulation and delivery improvement initiatives, nursing homes continue to face challenges in providing high‐quality care, there is considerable heterogeneity in quality across nursing homes and substantial barriers to accessing high‐quality facilities. 4 , 5

In a landmark study, Mor and colleagues described nursing home care as a “two‐tiered” system. 4 While nearly all nursing homes are certified by both Medicare and Medicaid, the lower tier of facilities predominantly serve Medicaid‐financed, long‐stay residents. Nursing homes in the bottom tier demonstrate lower performance on publicly‐reported quality measures in comparison to the upper tier of facilities, which more frequently serve individuals who require Medicare‐financed post‐acute care. Implicitly, low‐quality nursing homes have censuses that are more likely to have individuals living with dementia who are more likely to need long‐term care services. However, the disparity in nursing home quality for people living with dementia is not entirely driven by the demand for long‐term versus post‐acute skilled nursing facility (SNF) care; people with dementia have been shown to be discharged to lower‐quality nursing homes, under the Medicare SNF benefit, compared to their counterparts without dementia residing in the same ZIP code. 6

Broadly, individuals discharged to SNFs with lower star ratings (aggregate publicly‐reported measures of quality) are more likely to experience adverse outcomes such as short‐term readmission and mortality. 7 Given their unequal access to high‐quality facilities, people living with dementia may disproportionately bear the consequences of lower SNF quality. On the other hand, if the impact of high‐ versus low‐quality care on outcomes is heterogeneous across patient populations, then the level to which disparities in access to quality translate to disparities in outcomes is uncertain. For instance, high SNF quality may not be as consequential for patients with already high health status. By contrast, patients with poorer health status such as those with dementia may be more sensitive to the effects of poor quality, but this may not be the case if better‐rated SNFs have less experience treating sicker patients.

How the receipt of high versus low quality SNF care impacts different patient populations is an under‐studied subject. However, research demonstrating heterogeneity in overall post‐acute care delivery and its relation to patient outcomes provides motivating evidence for this question. For example, the Institute of Medicine documented large geographic variations in Medicare spending on post‐acute care that had no discernable relationship to outcomes. 8 , 9 In our study, we examine the differential impact of SNF quality on people with dementia given the barriers to accessing high‐quality facilities and the high health risk of this population. In addition to this analysis of treatment heterogeneity, we contribute to the literature by using a novel instrumental variables approach that exploits quasi‐random assignment to high‐, average‐, and low‐quality nursing homes based on local occupancy rates. We focus on nursing home staffing quality, which is timely given the recent finalization of a controversial Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services rule establishing more stringent federal standards for nursing staff.

METHODS

This study received approval from the institutional review board of Brown University and received a waiver for informed consent due to the analysis of deidentified administrative data.

Data sources

Our analyses rely on several sources of patient‐ and nursing home‐level administrative data. First, the Medicare Beneficiary Summary File (MBSF) contains vital status dates, demographic data, and payer source information for all current and previous Medicare enrollees. Second, the Medicare Provider and Analysis and Review (MedPAR) database was the source of hospital and SNF claims, respectively. Third, the Minimum Data Set (MDS) is a nursing home resident assessment tool that captures admission and discharge dates and also includes numerous types of clinical data. Fourth, nursing home quality measures were obtained from Medicare's Care Compare website, which includes five‐star (higher is better) summary ratings. Fifth, we used annual nursing home surveys recorded in the Certification and Survey Provider Enhanced Reports (CASPER) database as the source of nursing homes’ geographic coordinates. Sixth, we obtained and geocoded the addresses of hospitals using the Provider of Services Files.

Study sample

We identified Traditional Medicare beneficiaries discharged from a general acute hospital to a nursing home under the SNF benefit between 2011 and 2017. Medicare Advantage (MA) enrollees were excluded because their SNF claims are not included in MedPAR. To reduce heterogeneity and ensure focus on post‐acute and not long‐term care, we excluded patients below age 65 and those who had been in a nursing home in the year prior to hospitalization. In addition, a small fraction of patients who did not stay in the hospital for at least three overnights, which is generally required to receive Part A SNF coverage, were also excluded.

Outcomes

The outcomes of this study were readmission and mortality within 30 and 90 days of nursing home entry. Outcomes during the first 30 and 90 days of post‐acute care are arguably modifiable by the delivery and quality of care and are frequently targeted by policy initiatives. The date of death listed in the MBSF was used to define mortality outcomes, while hospital claims were used to indicate readmission. Because death and readmission outcomes represent competing risks, we also assessed them as combined outcomes of either readmission or death.

Main explanatory variable

The staffing‐star rating of patients’ destination nursing homes at the time (month and year) of discharge was our quality of care measure. Destination nursing homes were identified based on the SNF claim provider number which was then matched to the Care Compare star rating data. Although nursing home quality is multidimensional, 10 , 11 , 12 we argue that the provision of high‐quality care cannot be done without an adequate number of well‐trained staff. Conveniently, the staffing star rating reflects both staffing hours per resident day and the training level of nursing staff because it accounts for care provided by registered nurses separately. Another advantage of using the staffing star rating is that it is more tangible relative to several other quality measures, as nursing homes can modify their staffing levels to improve it more readily (we acknowledge that in practice, this may be difficult in an era of workforce shortages).

Dementia diagnosis

We captured dementia status based on whether a patient's hospital claim included an International Classification of Diseases code meeting the Chronic Condition Warehouse's (CCW) definition of Alzheimer's disease or other related dementias. 13 Although it is possible to use a dementia diagnosis from the MBSF Chronic Condition Segment, which includes dementia indicators based on a patient's history of fee‐for‐service claims, there are two advantages to using the hospital claim‐based diagnosis. First, the MBSF diagnosis is based on a strict application of the CCW algorithm which includes several diagnoses that are related to but are distinct from dementia, such as delirium. 14 Accordingly, we excluded delirium from our application of the CCW algorithm to the hospital claim. Second, the MDS‐based dementia diagnosis may suffer from differential ascertainment bias, in which higher or lower‐quality facilities document the diagnosis more frequently. 15

Covariates

We used a large set of covariates to capture baseline demographics and pre‐nursing home entry health status based on comorbidities documented during hospitalization. We did not use clinical data from the MDS data due to the potential for differential ascertainment attributable to known variation in the quality and completeness of nursing homes conducting the MDS assessment. 15 The covariates used were: age, female sex, race, total hospital length of stay, intensive care unit length of stay, emergency room use, the Elixhauser Index, 16 and an indicator for lower extremity joint replacement. Calendar year, hospital and ZIP code fixed effects were also included in all analyses (hospital and ZIP code fixed effects are also central to our empirical strategy, discussed below and in further detail in our Supplemental Methods).

Statistical analysis

We estimated the relationship between staffing star rating and outcomes using an instrumental variables approach due to the high potential for residual confounding from unmeasured health status (e.g., patients with a high propensity for adverse outcomes may have higher demands for high‐quality care or more difficulty accessing it). This method exploits variation in the admitting facility star rating that is due to an external factor, the instrumental variable, that is unlikely to be a confounder in the quality‐outcomes relationship (i.e., unrelated to baseline health status, at least conditionally). For this study, we used two instrumental variables: the number of vacant beds in nursing homes with three‐star ratings (i.e., average‐quality beds) and the number of vacant beds in nursing homes with four‐ to five‐star ratings (i.e., high‐quality beds) available to patients on the day prior to hospital discharge. We considered beds to be available if they were within 6 miles of the patient's discharging hospital or residence. The Supplemental Methods (A1) includes a more detailed description of how instruments were conceptualized and constructed.

The notion underlying our approach is that if beds in higher‐rated nursing homes become available, a patient is more likely to go to one of these facilities, leading to higher star ratings for SNF patients on average. If beds in these facilities are not available, then the average star rating will decrease. As a necessary step, we examine whether this is true in the data. We also investigate whether patient characteristics differ on days when high‐star beds are more available versus less available (our approach for doing this is detailed in the Supplemental Methods [A2]). If this were the case, then our approach would still likely suffer from residual confounding. Due to the correlation between neighborhood socioeconomic status and healthcare quality, implicitly more high‐quality beds will be available to healthier or higher‐income individuals. However, we do not expect a correlation between observed health status or income (e.g., Medicaid enrollment) and high‐quality bed availability after conditioning our analysis on neighborhood (ZIP code) and hospital via fixed effects.

Our instrumental variables estimation was conducted via two‐stage least squares (2SLS) regression. In the first stage, we regress star rating on the instrumental variables as well as all covariates. From this, we obtained a predicted, instrument‐dependent star rating of the nursing home entered for each patient. In the second stage, we regress a given outcome on the predicted star rating and all covariates. Since all outcomes are binary, the 2SLS estimates should interpreted in the same manner as estimates derived from a simple linear probability model. In this case, this is the percentage point change in the outcome due to a one‐star difference in admitting SNF quality. We stratified regressions by dementia status and compared estimates derived from the stratified models to determine whether nursing home quality differentially impacted patients with dementia. We also compared all 2SLS estimates to those derived from a simple linear probability model. Data were analyzed with Stata MP 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Null hypotheses were tested assuming a two‐sided type I error probability of 0.05. Standard errors were clustered by ZIP code and discharging hospital.

Sensitivity analyses

We repeat analyses using versions of the instrumental variables constructed from different bed availability radiuses (specifically, 4, 5, and 7 miles). In addition, we provide estimates from an exactly identified specification in which our exposure is a binary indicator for admission to a 4‐ or 5‐star SNF instrumented with the availability of four‐ and five‐star beds alone. We also show estimates after excluding those with instrument values that may be impacted by a differential amount of measurement error or biased. Specifically, we exclude records with instrument values that were in the 1st and 99th percentiles within their ZIP code, patients from rural counties (who may require a larger radius for instrument construction), and individuals who were not admitted from the emergency room to ensure admissions were unplanned and not related to bed availability. For the purpose of parsimony, we display results of sensitivity analyses for the overall sample without dementia stratification.

RESULTS

The sample consisted of 5.6 million discharges to nursing homes, including 1.3 million (23.1%) with dementia (Table 1). The sample was on average 81.8 (standard deviation [SD] 8.3) years old, 65% female, and 88% White individuals. Patients with dementia were as expected older (85.3 years vs. 80.7 for those without dementia), more likely to be dually enrolled in Medicaid (21% vs. 17%), had a greater number of chronic conditions (11.3 vs. 10.3), and had a higher Elixhauser index (9.0 vs. 7.7) at discharge. In addition, patients with dementia were more likely to have been admitted via the emergency department (89% vs. 68%) and were less likely to have undergone surgery (24% vs. 47%) during the hospital stay. The nursing homes patients with dementia were admitted to were larger (131.1 vs. 123.6 total beds), were more likely to be for‐profit (72% vs. 67%), were less likely to be hospital‐based (9% vs. 5%), and had lower staffing star‐ratings. Specifically, 52% of patients with dementia were admitted to high‐rated (four‐ or five‐star) nursing homes compared to 59% of patients without dementia. Correspondingly, 23% of patients with dementia were discharged to low‐rated (one‐ or two‐star) nursing homes compared to 18% of patients without dementia.

Based on the simple linear model, admission to a higher‐rated facility was associated with reduced rates of readmission and mortality for both patients with and without dementia. In the instrumental variables analysis, higher nursing home quality was also associated with reduced mortality at 90 days and with reduced readmission at both 30 and 90 days. Specifically, the percentage point difference in the probability of 90‐day mortality associated with a star rating change of one was −0.51 (p < 0.001) among patients without dementia and −0.78 (p < 0.01) for patients with dementia. Although the instrumental variable estimates for 30‐day mortality were not statistically significant, as with other outcomes their magnitude was larger for people with dementia.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the effect of being treated at nursing homes with higher quality ratings for staffing on mortality and readmission and whether the effects differed for patients with dementia. To answer this, we used a novel instrumental variables approach to circumvent residual confounding common to most observational designs. We find that admission to a better‐staffed nursing home was associated with reduced hospital readmission and mortality overall. However, the impact of entering a better‐staffed nursing home on these outcomes was stronger for patients with dementia. For instance, if a patient's destination nursing home's staffing star rating was greater by one, the probability of 30‐day readmission decreases by 0.34 percentage points if the patient does not have dementia and by 0.81 percentage points if the patient has dementia. At the same time, patients with dementia were less likely to be admitted to high‐quality nursing facilities, suggesting disparities in access to higher‐quality facilities have substantial adverse effects.

There is mixed evidence as to whether exposure to higher nursing home quality confers reduced risk of adverse outcomes because few studies have overcome residual confounding. 17 , 18 , 19 A recent rigorous study using a different methodology found that admission to a nursing home with a higher overall star rating was better for patients by lowering the probability of mortality and readmission. 7 The results of our study are largely consistent, however, in addition to our distinct study design, our analysis differs in two important ways. First, we focus on the staffing quality measure, which at least theoretically is more easily modifiable (through hiring additional direct staff) and thus may offer a more accessible path to quality improvement. Second, we specifically focus on post‐acute care for individuals with dementia, who are typically the focus of analyses pertaining to long‐term nursing home care.

Policy implications

Improved post‐acute outcomes and lower Medicare spending (through readmission reductions) for individuals at better‐staffed nursing homes plainly implies that initiatives aimed at increasing nursing homes’ direct care staffing levels are critical. There are multiple policy levers that may be used, and at surface increasing direct care staffing is a cost‐effective way to improve quality of care compared to improving or investing in capital or recruiting and retaining physicians. However, due to the tightening of labor markets post‐COVID‐19 and increased health risk to health care workers, the health care sector and especially nursing homes have face persistent direct care staffing shortages. 20 , 21 Unsurprisingly, most nursing homes do not meet the minimum staffing standards established recently by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS). 22 , 23 The new CMS rule on minimum staffing includes a $75 million budget for staff training, but in light of the labor shortage and persistent inflation, more permanent funding through reimbursement rate increases may be a more effective route for financing the recruitment and retention of competitively paid staff.

Our findings speak to not only the need for more staffing broadly, but also to how staffing is allocated across patient populations. The fact that patients with dementia are less likely to receive nursing home care in facilities with better staffing, but may especially benefit from it implies inefficiency in how quality is currently allocated. As such, reducing disparities in nursing home quality for persons with dementia will not only enhance equity but also may enhance social welfare. However, there are no simple policy levers to achieve this. Prioritizing better staffing at lower‐rated facilities is a seemingly obvious remedy, but historical evidence for substantial quality improvement at poor‐performing nursing homes is lacking. 4 Relatedly, preferences for nursing homes nearby to individuals’ residences typically outweigh preferences for facility quality, and persons with dementia are typically situated near lower‐quality facilities. 4 , 6 , 24 , 25 A more feasible approach for improving the quality of care received by persons with dementia is increasing access to higher‐rated facilities. Public reporting is a tool historically used to improve access by placing market pressure on facilities to improve. Although facilities with lower ratings lose market share theoretically, consumers with cognitive impairment or reduced access to technology may be less like to use public report cards. 26 More active efforts by hospital discharge planners may be warranted to ensure persons with dementia are placed in higher‐quality nursing homes that are not simply nearby their residences.

Incentivizing nursing homes to treat patients with dementia is another potential avenue for reform. Historically, nursing homes have been reimbursed based on the level of skilled therapies provided with rudimentary case mix adjustment. It is likely that patients with dementia both generate low revenues and also have high ancillary costs associated with their care. For instance, a patient with dementia may be unable to tolerate large amounts of therapy and also require uncompensated behavioral symptom management. Under a different payment mechanism and better risk adjustment, higher‐quality nursing homes may find it more worthwhile to serve patients with dementia.

Medicare made changes to the SNF reimbursement system that were implemented during the 2019 fiscal year. Under the new Patient Driven Payment Model (PDPM), reimbursements are less therapy‐based and better risk adjusted for comorbidity burden. 27 Although this may make providing care to patients with dementia more attractive, there are reasons to suspect disparities in quality will continue to persist. One reason is that high‐quality nursing homes with greater demand may avoid providing post‐acute care to those at higher risk for become long‐stay residents, such as persons with dementia. Individuals who reside in a nursing home for 100 or more days are commonly thought of as long‐stay since the Medicare SNF benefit expires at this point. Since Medicaid is the primary financier of long‐term care and pays a fraction of Medicare or private pay rates, at the 100‐day mark nursing homes can face a sharp decline in revenue. Scholars have attributed low nursing home quality for persons with dementia and dual Medicare‐Medicaid enrollees to low Medicaid reimbursement rates. 4 , 6 , 25 , 26 Although Medicare does not pay for long‐term care, policies to improve staffing for post‐acute care that are blind to the interaction of post‐acute and long‐term care may not be effective.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, the results cannot be generalized to patients excluded from the sample, including the under‐65 population, MA enrollees, and patients who previously used nursing home services. Second, the results can only be generalized to patients whose treatment status changed because of the instrument: patients entering better or poorer quality facilities due to bed availability at the time of discharge. More broadly, the instrumental variables approach produces a local average treatment effect, but not the general average treatment effect. Third, while we provide evidence that patient characteristics did not significantly differ according to values of the instrumental variable and provide consistent results across a series of robustness checks, residual confounding may still exist. Fourth, our study time period precludes the use of staffing measures based on Payroll‐Based Journal Data, which may have greater accuracy. Fifth, dementia is a wide‐ranging illness and our data do not include a reliable measure of dementia severity. Sixth, our analyses do not pinpoint the mechanism for why there is a stronger impact of nursing home quality on patients with dementia. We presume that these populations may be more sensitive to the impact of quality due to their greater frailty. However, better staffed facilities may have other attributes that are responsible for improved outcomes, such as dementia special care units. 28 We cannot distinguish between the effects of staffing and special care units, or any other correlated factor at the facility‐level because we only instrument for selection at the patient‐level. 29 As such, our result is a plausibly causal estimate of the impact of being admitted to a nursing home with a high versus low staffing rating, and not the direct impact of staffing. Identifying the mechanisms underlying the association between nursing home quality and improved outcomes is an important area for future research.

CONCLUSION

Using the time‐varying availability of beds in higher‐rated nursing homes as a source of quasi‐random assignment, we found that admission to a better‐staffed nursing home was associated with reduced hospital readmission and mortality overall and especially for persons with dementia. Although persons with dementia strongly benefit from higher nursing home quality, they are less likely to be admitted to high‐quality facilities, indicating welfare loss from inequitable access.
