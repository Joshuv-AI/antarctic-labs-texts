Controlling and optimizing the volatile organic compound and organic contaminant removal by improved water management in plastic recycling plants
Authors: Noa Collier, Tobias De Somer, Martijn Roosen, Steven De Meester, Dave Manhaeghe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Water-based washing is a critical step in the mechanical recycling of flexible post-consumer polyolefin packaging, essential for achieving high-quality recyclates. Despite its importance, the process remains poorly understood and entails a substantial water consumption. This study aims to optimize the trade-off between water usage and recyclate quality, focusing on organic contaminants (i.e. chemical oxygen demand (COD), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP)) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Lab-scale experiments were conducted to: (1) characterize various plastic input streams and identify correlations among key parameters, (2) assess the influence of input stream variability and solid-to-liquid (SL) ratios on washing efficiency, (3) investigate water reuse effects on both recyclate quality and contaminant removal kinetics, and (4) test alternative wastewater treatment methods. A kinetic model was adopted, calibrated, and implemented in Aspen Plus® using experimental data. Findings revealed no correlation between COD and VOC loads on plastic films. Washing experiments demonstrated a dynamic equilibrium between the contaminant load on plastics and the final concentration in the washing water. Given the inherent heterogeneity of plastic waste, real-time monitoring of water quality and treatment performance is essential to consistently ensure recyclate quality. Additionally, VOC-removal was found to be more sensitive to water reuse and quality than the removal of organic contaminants. Based on these insights, a modified washing line incorporating a third polishing wash with a separate water cycle is proposed. This configuration improved removal efficiencies for VOCs (25%), COD (12%), TN (19%), and TP (9%) compared to conventional washing systems.

Introduction

In 2023, the global flexible packaging consumption has reached 33.5 Mt, and is expected to grow up to 41.2 Mt in 2028 (Platt, 2023). The mechanical recycling of this flexible packaging has proven to be challenging, among others due to malodorous compounds, printed inks, and multilayer films (estimated to be 20–40% of all flexible films) (Mumladze et al., 2018; Tartakowski, 2010). Especially for household film recycling, this results in often dark-coloured and somehow smelly pellets after extrusion, which have limited market potential (Horodytska et al., 2021; Lase et al., 2022). Indeed, current mechanical washing processes often fail to sufficiently remove unwanted odours from post-consumer polyolefins (Strangl et al., 2020). From a physicochemical perspective, water may not be the most efficient washing medium for removing contaminants with a broad polarity range that are adsorbed on non-polar plastics (Demets et al., 2020; Roosen et al., 2021). Therefore, water is usually supplemented with caustic soda (NaOH) and/or detergents. NaOH is effective for removing labels or dissolving glues, but struggles to remove certain odorous components, being more polar than water (Lange, 2021; Roosen et al., 2022, Roosen et al., 2021). Detergents such as cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) can enhance volatile organic compound (VOC) removal efficiency from plastic film waste by 20 to 30% compared to NaOH (Roosen et al., 2021). Additionally, the detergent stabilizes the dissolved compounds, acting as a scavenger, which reduces the redeposition on the plastic surface (Horodytska et al., 2021). Next to medium selection, it has also been found that increasing temperature (28°C to 85°C) and decreasing the particle size (1.1 cm to 0.045 cm) yield improved VOC-removals, up to 72%–79% (De Somer et al., 2022). Interestingly, the accompanying thermodynamics revealed that the VOCs prefer to be physically adsorbed on the plastics rather than to be dissolved in the water-based medium. As a consequence, the VOC-removal in the recycling plant can quickly drop when the water is not sufficiently cleaned. This highlights the need for a well-thought water management.

When extending the search to organic contaminants, the wastewater from different types of plastic waste has already been assessed (Table 1). Parameters that are often measured include pH, turbidity, chemical oxygen demand (COD), total suspended solids (TSS), volatile suspended solids (VSS) and oil and grease. Overall, there was a high variation in the water quality between these studies, which might be due to different collection methods (leading to cross-contamination), standard heterogeneity of the contamination on the plastic waste input material or differences in the washing procedure. The main differences found were the applied temperature (hot wash vs. cold wash) and the addition of chemicals such as NaOH and detergent. From the study of Soto et al. (2020), it could be concluded that washing with cold (20°C) or hot (60°C) water increases the concentration of COD (34%), TN (31%) and TP (43%). Washing with hot water (60°C) and NaOH resulted in even higher concentrations (61%, 64% and 0% for COD, TN and TP, respectively). The latter was explained by an increased removal of glues and dyes. The influence of the detergent was not clearly stated in the studies, although Jabłońska (2018) mentioned that the used detergent in the washing process influences the wastewater quality. The generated wastewater in the studies shown in Table 1 were treated by different techniques commonly applied in industry, ranging from biological treatment with activated sludge to physicochemical treatments with coagulation-flocculation, adsorption, filtration and ion exchange. The overall removal efficiency ranges between 80%–94% for COD, 71%–82% for TN and 93% for TP (only 1 value was published for TP). New research is ongoing to improve the performance of the washing steps, for example by means of ultrasonication (Cecon et al., 2025). The influence of reusing the treated water was assessed by Soto et al. (2020). In their study, three different recycling rates were used, expressed as %fresh/%recycled: 90/10, 70/30 and 50/50. The recycled water was treated by coagulation-flocculation. The results indicated that the water quality deteriorated when increasing the amount of recycled water. Interestingly, it was also found that the water quality kept deteriorating when multiple washings were performed. However, no stabilization in water quality parameters was obtained. It is therefore important to assess the water quality over multiple cycles, to avoid pollutant accumulation in the washing line.

Despite significant progress in plastic recycling technologies, several critical challenges remain that limit both the predictability and efficiency of industrial washing processes. First, while these empirical studies provide valuable insights, there is a clear need for the development and experimental calibration of kinetic models that can quantitatively describe the complex interplay between plastic dirt load and water contamination. Such models would allow for predictive simulation of full-scale washing operations, moving beyond heuristic or trial-and-error approaches and enabling data-driven optimization of process parameters. Second, conventional washing strategies, typically two-step processes, often fail to achieve sufficient removal of both volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and persistent organic contaminants (Lase et al., 2022). This underscores the need for novel process designs, such as a three-step washing system that includes a dedicated polishing wash with a separate water cycle. By experimentally validating such approaches, it becomes possible to substantially improve contaminant removal efficiency while minimizing water consumption and cross-contamination. Finally, industrial plastic streams are highly heterogeneous, and washing operations are subject to variable operational conditions (Abbas-Abadi et al., 2023). Addressing this requires systematic scenario analyses that explore a wide range of input materials, contamination levels, and process parameters. Such analyses would demonstrate the generalizability of the modelling framework and process design, providing a robust, transferable foundation for process optimization across diverse industrial settings. Together, these research directions aim to bridge the gap between laboratory-scale understanding and full-scale industrial implementation, enabling more predictable, efficient, and sustainable plastic recycling operations.

The aim of this study was to optimize the water management in a plastic recycling facility treating post-consumer polyolefin film waste. This was done by combining laboratory scale experiments on post-consumer polyolefin films with simulations of full scale plant layouts in Aspen Plus®. The laboratory experiments focused on (1) the characterization of different plastic input streams for organic dirt (i.e. COD, TN and TP) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs), (2) the effect of different plastic input streams and solid-liquid ratios on the washing, (3) the effect of water reuse on the final recyclate quality and removal kinetics, and (4) different wastewater treatment techniques. Using this data, a reversed-first-order model previously used in scientific work to model the VOC-removal from plastics was adopted and calibrated. The model was then implemented in Aspen Plus® and different scenarios and plastic waste input materials were simulated to optimize the water management while keeping in mind the balance between costs and quality of the washed flakes. The latter is important as the price of virgin plastic sets a limit on the price of recycled plastic, underscoring the importance of making recycling processes cost-effective without compromising quality (Hopewell et al., 2009).

Material and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Sodium hydroxide pellets (NaOH, >99.0% a.r. purity), nitric acid (HNO3, 65.0% a.r. purity) and iron (III) chloride anhydrous (FeCl3, >98.0% purity) were provided by Chem-Lab. Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, ≥98.0% purity) and carbon disulfide (CS2, >99.0% purity) were supplied by Sigma Aldrich (Merck). Calcium chloride technical (CaCl2, 94–98% purity) was supplied by VWR chemicals. Acenaphtene-d10 (99 atom% purity, Sigma Aldrich) was used as internal standard for Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis.

Description of the flexible packaging waste sample

Two post-consumer flexible packaging PE film waste samples were obtained from industry. Firstly, a waste bale of 138 kg was provided by a sorting site of SUEZ SA (Belgium), and was manually sorted into 17 different categories based on the intended content of the packaging (Fig. S1). More details about the sampling plan and sampling techniques used can be found in Roosen et al. (2023). Homogeneous samples were obtained by shredding each fraction separately using a 4 mm sieve (Universal Cutting Mill PULVERISETTE 19 from FRITSCH). Secondly, 80 L of low-density polyethylene (LDPE) waste was collected at a medium-sized material sorting facility in the Netherlands at the end of the sorting process prior to baling. For random sample generation, the coning and quartering method was used, where the sample was first spread out, divided into four quadrants, after which approximately 20% of the sample was taken from each quadrant. This was repeated until the sample size was reduced to 10 L. Next, the sample was shredded using a SHINI low speed granulator with a 4 mm sieve. Before analysis, samples were stored in airtight plastic bags at 4°C in a dark environment.

Analysis of COD, TN, TP, TSS/VSS, NTU, EC and pH

These parameters were determined in duplicate by analysing the wastewater after a washing experiment in cold water (Cabanes et al., 2020; Roosen et al., 2021). No NaOH and/or detergent was added as the focus of this work is not on the removal of labels, glues and inks (Jabłońska, 2018; Soto et al., 2020). For this, 3 g of shredded film waste sample was washed 5 consecutive times with fresh aliquots of 100 mL tap water in a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask. The stirring speed was 200 rpm (IKA magnetic stirrer Mini MR standard). After 10 minutes of mixing, the plastics were separated from the washing medium using a sieve (Fig. S2), after which they could be either reused for a next wash, or rinsed with 100 mL of tap water (to remove any remaining chemicals) followed by a drying step (desiccator filled with CaCl2 for 72 h) to allow for e.g. VOC-analysis.

COD, TN and TP in the water samples were spectrophotometrically analysed using Hach test kits. TSS and VSS were determined gravimetrically using glass fiber filters with pore size 0.7 μm (VWR 698) (APHA, 2017). Turbidity was quantified and expressed in nephelometric turbidity units (NTU) using the portable Hanna instruments HI 98703 turbidimeter. The Hach HQ4300 multimeter measured the electrical conductivity (EC) and pH of the water. All contaminant measurements were performed in duplicate. The total contaminant concentration (expressed in g contaminant per kg plastic) was finally calculated by taking the sum of the obtained concentrations (g.L−1) of each of the five consecutive washes, multiplying it with the volume of the water (i.e. 0.1 L) and dividing it by the mass of the plastics (i.e. 0.003 kg).

VOCs

Volatile organic compounds present on the various plastic fractions were initially sampled using both headspace solid-phase micro-extraction (HS-SPME) and solvent desorption (SD), following the methodology described by Roosen et al. (2023). In both approaches, the plastic samples were pre-heated to 65°C for 10 minutes to facilitate VOC-release. Subsequently, VOCs were collected over a 15-minute period either onto an SPME fiber or onto a NIOSH-type activated charcoal tube (Dräger), respectively. For the SD method, VOCs were desorbed using carbon disulfide (CS₂), with the addition of a 50 ppm internal standard (acenaphthene-d10). The extracted VOCs were then separated and identified using the GC–MS protocol developed by Demets et al. (2020). VOC-profiles of all fractions from the PE-bale sourced in Belgium have been previously reported by Roosen et al. (2023).

Parameters affecting water management

The influence of three key parameters was evaluated: (1) the impact of the initial dirt load on the plastic on the maximum concentrations of COD, TN, and TP in the resulting wastewater, (2) the effect of varying solid-to-liquid (SL) ratios, and (3) the influence of water reuse on contaminant removal kinetics.

Maximum concentration of COD, TN and TP in the wastewater

To determine the maximum concentrations in the wastewater, 15 sequential washes were performed with the same water, each involving 3 g of newly introduced waste material (see Section 2.3.1). After each wash, the water volume was determined, and fresh water was added to make up for water losses due to water uptake by the plastic films. The added amounts of water were corrected for in the calculations. These experiments were performed on both input samples (Section 2.2). COD, TN and TP were analysed as described in Section 2.3.1. Experiments were conducted in duplicate.

Effect of the solid-liquid ratio

The main PE-bale obtained from Belgium was washed according to Section 2.3.1 using tap water at SL-ratios of 0.01-0.02-0.03-0.05-0.07 wt% with a fixed volume of 100 mL. Higher ratios could not be conducted on a laboratory scale due to the substantial plastic volume, resulting in plastics that were no longer immersed in water, rendering it physically unfeasible. It needs to be stated that the SL-ratio at which this will occur depends on the particle size. The wastewater was finally analysed for COD, TN and TP after 15 minutes of contact time (Section 2.3.1). Experiments were conducted in duplicate.

Removal kinetics in clean and reused water

Given the lack of studies exploring the kinetics of compounds such as COD, TN, and TP (Section 1), the kinetics of these contaminants in both clean (= tap water) and reused water (= wastewater obtained after washing the PE bale from Belgium with tap water, comprising 576 mg COD.L−1, 10.9 mg TN.L−1 and 2.06 mg TP.L−1) were assessed. This was done by washing the main PE bale sample as described in Section 2.3.1. Only this time, water samples were taken after 1-3-5-7-10-15 minutes. Next, these samples were analysed on COD, TN and TP (Section 2.3.1). Experiments were conducted in duplicate.

Wastewater treatment

Wastewater treatment experiments were performed on the wastewater obtained from washing the main PE bale obtained from Belgium, as described in Section 2.3.1. Firstly, coagulation experiments were performed using the often applied coagulant FeCl3 (Amuda and Amoo, 2007; Aziz et al., 2007; Gotvajn et al., 2009). Based on preliminary tests (results not shown), doses of 5 mL, 7 mL, 9 mL, 11 mL and 13 mL of a 2.5 g.L−1 FeCl3-solution were added to 100 mL wastewater. Coagulation consisted of rapid mixing for 2 minutes at 300 rpm (VELP Scientifica FC 6S stirrer), after which flocculation occurred by slow mixing at 60 rpm for 15 minutes, followed by 60 minutes of settling. The filtered (paper filter, 5–13 μm, VWR 413) supernatant was then analysed (Section 2.3.1). Secondly, granulated activated carbon (GAC) adsorption (ORGANOSORB 10-AA; 0.60–2.36 mm, Desotec) was performed. The GAC was first conditioned (Liu et al., 2012). Next, a 100 mL column was filled with 40 g GAC and wettened by sending 100 mL of distilled water over the column. Then, 100 mL of the wastewater was sent over the column. Lastly, microfiltration was applied to further purify the treated wastewater after coagulation. Here, 50 mL of the supernatant was sent over a cellulose acetate filter (0.45 μm, Porafil, Macherey-Nagel) under vacuum (Vacuubrand MZ 2C), and the filtrate was analysed (Section 2.3.1).

Model development of a washing line in Aspen Plus®

Kinetics and model calibration

The model framework is based on the model developed by Roosen et al. (2021) and De Somer et al. (2022), who compared eight kinetic models to determine the mechanisms behind the VOC-removal of plastic waste. They concluded that the reversed first order (RFO) model was the most suitable to describe the observed behavior in lab-scale experiments. These kinetics involve desorption of the contaminants from the plastics into the water and re-adsorption from the water back onto the plastics. Consequently, in this work, Eq. (1) was used to calculate the removal kinetics of VOCs, COD, TN and TP:(1)dqdt=−kdes∗q+kads∗q0−q∗mV+Cini,H2O

With q the concentration of contaminant on the plastic (mg contaminant.kg plastic−1), t the time (s), kdes the desorption rate constant (s−1), kads the adsorption rate constant (L.kg−1.s−1), q0 the concentration of contaminant on the plastic at t = 0 (mg contaminant.kg plastic−1), m the mass of the plastic (kg), V the volume of the liquid phase (L) and Cini,H2O the concentration of contaminant in the liquid phase at t = 0 (mg contaminant.L−1). Model calibration was done in MS Excel. The only parameters used for model calibration were kdes and kads. The experimental data from Section 2.4 was used for model calibration. As such, the model considers the removal in clean and dirty water, at different SL-ratios and the maximum concentrations in the wastewater. As this data was obtained for the main PE bale from Belgium and for the post-sorted LDPE bale from The Netherlands, q0-values for VOCs, COD, TN and TP obtained during characterization (Section 2.3.1) were adopted for the corresponding plastic waste. Next, the solver function in MS Excel was used to minimize the sum of squared errors (SSE) between the modelled and experimental values, by varying kdes and kads. The Solver function GRG Nonlinear (Generalized reduced gradient) was used. The overall goodness-of-fit was determined using Theils Inequality Coefficient (TIC), where a TIC-value below 0.3 indicates a good model fit (Manhaeghe et al., 2020). For VOCs, kdes and kads were calibrated based on the removal efficiencies obtained in earlier (in-house) research (Table S8) (Roosen et al., 2022). As different chemical classes result in different removal efficiencies, the VOCs were categorized into five classes: carboxylic acids, aromatic compounds, oxygenated compounds, terpenes and hydrocarbons. In addition, two washing media were incorporated: (1) cold water and (2) a 2 wt% NaOH solution with 9.2 mmol CTAB.L−1. The inclusion of the latter washing medium was done because this results in higher VOC-removal efficiencies in comparison to cold water, and because it is often used in industry. It needs to be stated that this could not be done for the removal efficiencies of COD, TN and TP as this washing medium would result in the removal of labels, glues and inks from the plastic waste, which was out of the scope of this work.

Implementation in Aspen Plus®

The calibrated model was implemented in Aspen Plus® version 14, which is a process simulation software used primarily in chemical engineering and process industries. It allows to implement and simulate models to optimize (chemical) processes. Firstly, pseudo-components for VOCs, COD, TN and TP were defined in the properties tab, and the NRTL method was used to calculate the properties. In the simulation tab, continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR) blocks were used to model the washing reactors. The washing time was set at 10 minutes, temperature at 25°C and pressure at 1 bar. Reaction kinetics were modelled using customer terms, and were defined for VOCs, COD, TN and TP. VOC-kinetics were defined in tap water with and without caustic soda and detergent given the improved VOC removal in the latter. The wastewater treatment was modelled using a user2-block. Experimental removal percentages were used for this (Section 2.5). Finally, the mass flow of the plastics was defined as a constant (1000 kg.hr−1). The water flow was calculated according to the preferred solid-liquid ratio. The reader is referred to the Supplementary information (Section 4.2) for more details on the Aspen Plus® implementation.

Scenario analysis in Aspen Plus®

Different scenarios were simulated in Aspen Plus® in order to gain more insight into the importance of certain process parameters in the recycling process. Three distinct process flow configurations were evaluated. The first two designs represent conventional two-step washing lines operated in (1) co-current mode (Fig. S10), where the plastics flow along with the water and (2) counter-current mode (Fig. S11), where plastics initially contact the dirtiest water in the first washing stage, followed by cleaner water in the second stage. The third configuration introduces a novel three-step washing process, building upon the conventional counter-current setup by incorporating an additional polishing wash step with a separate wastewater treatment (Fig. 4) to enhance cleaning efficiency. Importantly, two completely separated water cycles are proposed in the latter design, one for the conventional line and one for the polishing step. This was done to further enhance VOC-removal, as previous research has shown that contaminated water has a detrimental effect on the overall VOC-removal (De Somer et al., 2022). Wastewater treatment was done by coagulation in all designs. Only the wastewater from the polishing wash in the three-step process was treated by means of coagulation followed by a GAC adsorption to assure high VOC-removal rates from the wastewater. Secondly, for these three process flow designs, the effect of different washing media (i.e. ‘tap water’ and ‘water + 2wt% NaOH + 9.2 mmol CTAB·L−1) on the overall VOC-removal was examined. Thirdly, the influence of the water recirculation rate (i.e. water reuse before treatment, 0% → 50%) and different SL-ratios (0.01 → 0.07) was investigated. Finally, four different input materials varying in organic dirt load and VOC-contamination were simulated to assess the influence of the dirt load on the water management.

Results and discussion

Characterization of flexible packaging waste samples

Table 3 shows the total dirt load for both samples (Section 2.2), both in absolute and relative values. The latter comprises values for the PE bale from Belgium and its subfractions which are corrected for the weight fraction of the whole bale, therefore allowing for a comparison of the contribution of each fraction to the overall dirt load of the entire bale. It can be seen that the input sample from the Netherlands contains higher concentrations of COD, TN and TP in comparison to the PE bale from Belgium. These high differences between input materials can be related to the inherent heterogeneity of plastic household waste (Altieri et al., 2021; Bordonalli and Mendes, 2009; Calero et al., 2018; Soto et al., 2020). Regarding the PE bale from Belgium and its subfractions, a first important observation was that there is a positive correlation between COD, TN, TP, TSS, VSS and turbidity. However, no significant correlation was found between the COD- and VOC-load on the plastics (Pearson correlation test, p = 0.24). The latter is somewhat surprising, as it was expected that a higher organic load would result in a higher amount of (microbial) degradation products, causing the production of (malodorous) VOCs (Cabanes et al., 2020). A plausible explanation lies in the low odour threshold values of these compounds. Indeed, Thoden van Velzen et al. (2024) reported that most malodorous substances associated with the degradation of milk residues during the recycling of HDPE milk bottles were present at concentrations below the detection limits of standard analytical methods. This observation is consistent with our findings, where VOC-concentrations were significantly lower than COD levels (i.e. by approximately three orders of magnitude) indicating that VOCs constitute only a minor fraction of the total organic load. When comparing the non-food and food fraction, it was found that the TSS and VSS concentration in the non-food fraction (40.6 g TSS.kg−1 and 33.0 g VSS.kg−1) was almost twice as high as for the food-fraction (20.6 g TSS.kg−1 and 18.4 g VSS.kg−1), which is also reflected in a higher turbidity (228 NTU vs. 132 NTU). The COD, TN and TP load is also higher for the non-food fraction than for the food fraction, albeit less pronounced, with values of 52.7 g COD.kg−1, 1.44 g TN.kg−1, 0.19 g TP.kg−1 and 46.7 g COD.kg−1, 1.14 g TN.kg−1 and 0.12 g TP.kg−1, respectively. Upon closer examination, this difference can be addressed to the collection bag fraction, which exhibits considerably higher concentrations of COD (85.9 g.kg−1), TN (2.33 g.kg−1) and TP (0.53 g.kg−1) in comparison to the other fractions. Interestingly, the VOC-concentration of this fraction was the lowest one of all fractions (1.95 mg.kg−1). This shows that cross-contamination during collection and sorting plays a significant role for organic dirt (i.e. COD, TN, TP, TSS and VSS), especially to the collection bag, which is only limited in the case of VOCs. According to Thoden van Velzen et al. (2024), the most likely location for said cross-contamination is the bale pressing of mixed packages at the end of the sorting line, which causes product residues to be squeezed out of the packaging and come into contact with other packaging. Possible hypotheses of why this affects organic dirt more than VOCs include the significantly lower concentration of VOCs (up to 1000 times lower) and the fundamental differences in chemical nature between organic contaminants and VOCs (e.g. molecular weight, volatility, shape) (Zhong et al., 2025). Indeed, while VOCs have the tendency to diffuse into the polymer matrix (Hahladakis et al., 2018), organic dirt (i.e. food/product residues) will mainly stick onto the polymer surface rather than being deeply adsorbed in the polymer matrix (Ragaert et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2014). Consequently, this might facilitate cross-contamination of organic contaminants. Washing experiments

Fig. 1 illustrates the reduction in COD, TN, and TP after each successive wash with fresh water (SL-ratio = 0.03). A similar trend was observed for TSS and VSS (Figs. S3–S4). Notably, 75%–87% of COD, TN, and TP were removed within the first two washing steps, increasing to 90%–94% after three washes. Beyond this point, changes in the removal efficiency stagnated. These findings are further supported by a decrease in turbidity, from 617 NTU in W1 to 150 NTU in W2, and 87 NTU in W3. In contrast, W4 and W5 showed only modest reductions in turbidity (48 and 32 NTU, respectively). These results – in combination with industry needs to not induce excessive costs - suggest that two to three washing steps are sufficient for effective organic contaminant removal.Fig. 1COD, TN, TP concentrations in water after washing (n = 2). Fig. 2 shows that the first washing step results in the highest VOC-removal (56% for carboxylic acids, 86% for aromatics, 51% for oxygenated compounds, 77% for terpenes and 60% for hydrocarbons). Although the second and third washing step still achieve some additional removal of carboxylic acids (14%), aromatics (5%), and terpenes (3%), the overall VOC-removal is rather limited. The observed increase in hydrocarbons in the consecutive washes might be explained by plastic degradation due to the friction during the washing steps or by degradation/migration of (non-)intentionally added compounds (Fuller et al., 2020; Roosen et al., 2021; Thoden van Velzen et al., 2024). When interpreting these results, an important element that should be highlighted, is that this test was performed in clean tap water. Indeed, it has already been stated in scientific literature that the VOC-removal is a very fast process, but that the main issue lies in washing with dirty water that already contains VOCs (De Somer et al., 2022; Roosen et al., 2021). Therefore, achieving higher VOC-removal efficiencies in water-based media does not necessarily require an additional wash, but rather the application of a wash with clean water in terms of VOCs.Fig. 2Relative VOC intensities on plastic after five washing steps, measured by SD-GC–MS (n = 2), Wi = after step i. Hence, based on this hypothesis and on the results for COD, TN and TP, a novel three-step washing process is proposed. In this process, the first and second wash would, similar as already applied in industry, consecutively serve as a pre-wash to remove the largest dirt particles (macro-physical contamination) from the plastics and as main wash to remove attached dirt and odour from the surface (Ragaert et al., 2017; Soto et al., 2018). The additional third wash would then serve as a polishing wash designed to remove VOCs and some remaining organic dirt. In order to obtain clean water, this third polishing wash would have a separate water cycle with its own water treatment unit. Although this will lead to additional capital expenses, the operational expenses will be rather low given the fact that most of the organic dirt (i.e. COD, TN and TP) will already be removed, resulting in relatively clean water. These additional expenses might then be compensated by higher recyclate quality, and therefore higher economic value.

Parameters affecting water management

Maximum concentration of COD, TN and TP in the wastewater

After applying the same washing medium 15 times on fresh waste, the results for the PE bale from Belgium indicate final concentrations of 4084 mg COD.L−1, 112 mg TN.L−1 and 20.9 mg TP.L−1 (Fig. 3 and Table S2). It needs to be stated that while the COD and TN concentrations are indeed stabilizing, the TP concentration is still increasing after 15 washes. This can be explained by the lower TP load on the plastic waste, resulting in water that is still not saturated for phosphorus based contaminants. The final concentrations in the test with the waste sample from The Netherlands were 16,240 mg COD.L−1, 329 mg TN.L−1 and 63.4 mg TP.L−1 (Fig. 3 and Table S3). These concentrations are higher than the ones obtained for the sample from Belgium. Given the higher organic dirt load of this sample (Section 3.1), this clearly shows that there is a dynamic equilibrium between the dirt load on the plastic (‘q’ in Eq. (1)) and the maximum concentration in the wastewater. An important consequence is that when treating multiple inputs with different dirt load, re-adsorption of organic contaminants might take place when a rather clean sample is washed with untreated water from a previous run with a dirty sample. Influence of the solid-liquid ratio

The influence of the SL-ratio is depicted in Fig. S5. It can be seen that the SL-ratio is inversely correlated with the removal efficiency of COD, TN and TP. Indeed, removal efficiencies drop from 88%–95% to 49–61% when increasing the SL-ratio from 0.01 to 0.07 (Table S4). For COD, this effect is already clear when increasing the SL-ratio from 0.01 (95.2%) to 0.02 (72.2%). The optimal SL-ratio that results in a good trade-off between treatment cost and recyclate quality will be determined in Section 3.5.2.2.

Removal kinetics in clean and reused water

The results (Fig. S6) revealed that most of the COD, TN and TP was removed after 1 to 2 minutes, after which the removal slowed down to reach an equilibrium around 7 to 10 minutes. Interestingly, the removal rate in reused water was lower than in tap water, but still the same amount of COD, TN and TP were removed after 10 minutes. This indicates that washing with dirty water initially lowers the removal rate for COD, TN and TP, and that the overall removal efficiency only drops when the contaminants' concentrations are reaching their maximum levels. Indeed, the final concentrations in the reused water after 10 minutes were 1346 ± 212 mg COD.L−1, 37 ± 9 mg TN.L−1 and 4.4 ± 0.7 mg TP.L−1, which are lower than the maximum concentrations obtained in Section 3.3.1. These observations can be explained by the lower driving force of the desorption process when the water quality is deteriorating.

Wastewater treatment

Removal efficiencies of different techniques

The jar tests with FeCl3 (Section 2.5) resulted in an optimal coagulant dose of 9 mL of a 2.5 g.L−1 FeCl3, corresponding to a solution containing 0.225 g FeCl3.L−1 or 0.114 g Fe3+.g COD−1 in the influent (Table S5), which is similar to other research (Amuda and Amoo, 2007; Aziz et al., 2007; Gotvajn et al., 2009; Jabłońska, 2018). This dose resulted in a COD, TN and TP removal of 93.6%, 64.3% and 100%, respectively. These removal rates are similar to values reported in literature (see Section 1), although the removal rate of TN is somewhat lower (64.3% vs. 71%–82%). These differences can be addressed to the wastewater type, which is why coagulant doses need to be determined ad-hoc.

In order to further treat the wastewater, e.g. for the third polishing wash as proposed in Section 3.2, GAC adsorption and microfiltration were also tested on the water after coagulation. This resulted in an overall COD-removal of 98.8% and 91.1%, TN-removal of 87.1% and 69.3%, and TP-removal of 100% and 100% for GAC adsorption and microfiltration, respectively. As microfiltration only resulted in a limited TN removal, it was chosen to proceed with a combined treatment approach involving coagulation and GAC adsorption. Given the low start concentrations for the GAC adsorption test, a second GAC adsorption experiment was conducted, this time on the wastewater from the third washing step, which contained 124 mg COD.L−1, 2.19 mg TN.L−1 and 0.47 mg TP.L−1. GAC adsorption resulted in removal efficiencies for COD, TN and TP of 80%, 82% and 100%, respectively. These lower removal efficiencies for COD might be due to the different wastewater composition. Indeed, it is expected that higher molecular weight compounds with a lower polarity are removed in the polishing wash. According to a Best Available Technique (BAT) document from the Joint Research Centre (JRC), GAC adsorption is the most commonly used technique to remove VOCs (Brinkmann et al., 2016). The main advantage is that it can remove both polar and non-polar compounds up to 80%–95%.

Influence washing medium

Industry often makes use of 2 wt% NaOH and a detergent such as CTAB as washing agents. When analysing these washing agents (i.e. pure solution without any washing step), it was found that the CTAB solution (9.2 mmol.L−1) resulted in an increased turbidity (271 NTU) and high COD (6805 mg.L−1) and TN (105 mg.L−1) levels (Table S6). Next to the water quality, some of the surfactants such as CTAB also remain adsorbed on the polymer, which is confirmed by chemical analysis by means of pyro-GC–MS. These analyses are presented in SI Section 3.5.2. Future research could focus on the effect of such residual surfactant on the final recyclate quality, and potentially ways to remove the residual surfactant. The addition of NaOH resulted in a marked increase in pH, reaching a maximum value of 12.7. This was accompanied by a substantial rise in electrical conductivity (104.1 mS·cm−1), attributable to the dissociation of NaOH into its constituent ions.

Next, the influence of these pure washing media on the applied wastewater treatment techniques in this work was assessed. For NaOH, an additional neutralization step was necessary, therefore increasing the use of chemicals and overall wastewater treatment cost. Furthermore, higher salt concentrations might increase the risk of corrosion throughout the process. For the CTAB solution, the coagulation step with FeCl3 resulted in a 42.0% and 53.0% removal for COD and TN, respectively. For GAC adsorption, the removal rates increased up to 90.1% and 89.1%, respectively. These results are similar to Kaleta and Elektorowicz (2013). Hence, it can be concluded that using CTAB as a washing medium might indeed improve the removal rate of VOCs and inks, but that this washing medium will highly affect the wastewater treatment. Indeed, higher doses of coagulants and/or flocculants will be needed in the case of coagulation/flocculation, and an increased GAC consumption in the case of GAC adsorption. Again, this shows the importance of finding a good balance between treatment cost and final recyclate quality. Further research should focus on the effect of detergents added to the washing medium, which might not be removed, or only partially removed, during wastewater treatment. Additionally, also the packaging itself might still contain detergents that influence the washing, for example in case of detergents and cosmetics. These factors may necessitate adjustments to wastewater treatment parameters depending on the composition of the plastic waste. Alternatively, exploring different treatment techniques, such as foam fractionation, could offer more effective solutions for handling diverse plastic input streams.

Optimizing water management: Aspen Plus® simulations

Model calibration

Calibration of the rate constants kdes and kads of the RFO model (Eq. (1)) was successful, with TIC values lower than 0.30 for all experiments except one (Table S7 and Figs. S7–S9). Indeed, the experiment to determine the maximum concentration of COD for the sample from The Netherlands yielded a TIC-value of 0.70. It could be seen that the model was able to correctly predict the first three washes, with COD-concentrations up to 6360 mg.L−1. However, the model obtains an equilibrium around 9000 mg COD.L−1, whereas the measured COD-concentration increased up to 16,760 mg.L−1. In an attempt to explain this discrepancy, the soluble COD (sCOD, i.e. the COD-concentration obtained after filtration through a 0.45 μm filter) was also assessed for the sample from The Netherlands after 1, 10 and 15 washes. This yielded values of 873 mg sCOD.L−1 (58.7% of total COD), 2180 mg sCOD.L−1 (17% of total COD) and 3844 mg sCOD.L−1 (24% of total COD), respectively. This high difference between COD and sCOD concentrations could be related to remains of glue or paper, flocculation of the COD present in the washing medium or saturation of the sCOD. Nonetheless, it appears that larger particulate matter (non-soluble COD) can still be removed from the plastics through further washing. Regarding the model, it seems that this large difference between COD and sCOD influences the dynamic equilibrium. Hence, the simulation was performed again, but this time using the sCOD. To determine q0, the characterization of the PE bale from the Netherlands was repeated. This yielded an sCOD load of 39.5 g sCOD.kg plastic−1, which is lower than the observed COD load of 118.7 g COD.kg−1. Using this q0 and the obtained sCOD-values after 1, 10 and 15 washes, the model yielded a TIC-value of 0.13. Although this value is smaller than 0.30 and therefore the model has a good prediction of the experimental values, it should be stated that only 3 values were used to calculate this statistic. However, this does confirm the importance of making a distinction between total COD and sCOD. To avoid this issue in this work, only the experiments with the PE bale obtained from Belgium, which yielded good calibration results for all experiments, will be used. Regarding the general applicability of this model, it is important to note that it is not deterministic. Future research should further explore the distinction between COD and sCOD, and assess their respective impacts on the removal rate and the final COD equilibrium. From a practical perspective, the model seems adequate at COD-concentrations up to 6360 mg.L−1, although this number might vary according to the ratio of sCOD/COD of the sample under investigation.

Scenario analysis

The different scenarios in this section are based on the input concentration of the PE bale obtained from Belgium or its subfractions.

Process flow design and washing medium selection

The three different process flow designs described in Section 2.6.3 were simulated. As was expected, the counter-current system yielded better removal efficiencies than the co-current system (Table 5). Adding a third polishing wash further improved the COD-, TN-, TP- and VOC-removal to 93%, 89%, 95% and 70%, respectively. This can be explained by the lower concentrations in the second water cycle (effluent concentrations wash 2 vs. wash 3 were 596 mg COD.L−1 vs. 210 mg COD.L−1, 31.2 mg TN.L−1 vs. 8.2 mg TN.L−1, 1.83 mg TP.L−1 vs. 0.63 mg TP.L−1 and 49.6 μg VOC.L−1 vs. 27.7 μg VOC.L−1), which results in a higher driving force and therefore higher overall removal from the plastic waste. Changing the washing medium from water to 2 wt% NaOH +9.2 mmol CTAB.L−1 further improves the VOC-removal in all systems, although this effect is more pronounced for the two-step systems. Overall, the counter-current system with 2 wt% NaOH and CTAB results in a VOC-removal of 72%, whereas the three-step process with water results in a VOC-removal of 70%. It needs to be stated that NaOH is often added to improve the removal of grease and labels as well, which was out of the scope of this work. It is important to note that the reported removal percentages refer to values obtained prior to extrusion. When extrusion degassing is applied, an additional VOC removal of approximately 60% can be expected (Wu et al., 2024). However, this figure is subject to variation depending on the type of plastic and the specific VOCs present. Incorporating this additional removal step, the total VOC reduction—including washing—would range between 89% and 92% for the three-step washing process using water and for a counter-current wash with NaOH and CTAB. In contrast, the conventional counter-current system using only water achieves an overall VOC removal of approximately 80%. Further enhancement of VOC-removal can be achieved through the implementation of additional treatments, such as air or steam stripping, as suggested by Cabanes and Fullana (2021). These methods can contribute an additional 77%–84% VOC-reduction, resulting in a total removal efficiency of 96%–98%. Consequently, this supplementary treatment step may render the third polishing wash redundant with respect to VOC-removal. Nevertheless, the polishing step remains valuable for the elimination of organic contaminants, which is crucial for producing high-quality recyclates. Future research should focus on a comprehensive evaluation of the washing process, (vacuum) extrusion, and potential hot air treatment steps to assess their impact on the final recyclate quality as well as their costs. Indeed, an economic analysis is warranted to compare the costs and benefits of implementing a third washing step (comprising a third reactor and a second water treatment unit with coagulation and GAC adsorption) versus an air stripper, which entails significant energy costs. This analysis should weigh the financial investment against the potential improvement in recyclate quality and corresponding market value. In the following sections, the focus will remain on the washing process. The three-step washing protocol will be employed, consisting of two initial water washes followed by a polishing wash using a solution of 2 wt% NaOH and 9.2 mmol·L−1 CTAB.

Effect of solid-liquid ratio and water recirculation rate

It can be seen from Fig. 5 that the removal efficiency of COD, TN, TP and VOCs decreases with increasing SL-ratio. This trend can be attributed to the higher amount of contaminants introduced into a relatively smaller volume of liquid, which accelerates reaching equilibrium and consequently reduces the driving force for desorption. Interestingly, the VOC-removal is affected more than the COD-, TN- and TP-removal, showing that VOCs are more sensitive towards water quality to yield high removal efficiencies. This effect is most pronounced for VOCs belonging to the chemical classes of the terpenes and hydrocarbons. It is important to note that the hydrocarbons are primarily responsible for the characteristic ‘plastic smell’. Consequently, terpenes are the most challenging class of VOCs to be removed. The enhanced removal of organic dirt can be attributed by their higher water solubility in comparison to that of VOCs. Indeed, VOCs are often non-polar and consequently have lower water solubilities, lowering its removal in water. Next to their overall removal, the successive removal of COD, TN, TP and VOCs through the three washing steps was explored (Fig. S12). This shows that as the SL-ratio increases, the importance of the second and third washing step increases in terms of COD-, TN-, TP- and VOC-removal. For example, for VOCs classified as terpenes and hydrocarbons, a notable trend was observed: as the SL-ratio increases up to 0.03, the removal efficiency in the second washing step begins to decline. Conversely, in the third polishing wash, as more contaminants are still present on the incoming waste, the removal efficiency improves with increasing SL-ratio from 0.01 to 0.03. The behavior of these compounds can be attributed to their lower water solubility, which facilitates faster equilibrium during washing. As a result, when operating at higher SL-ratios, the inclusion of a third washing step becomes increasingly critical to achieve acceptable levels of contaminant removal. Based on this sensitivity analysis, a SL-ratio of 0.03 was chosen to continue the scenario analyses. As stated by Roosen et al. (2021), the recycling industry typically applies SL-ratios between 0.01 and 0.02. The chosen value in this work is somewhat higher, but this can be addressed by the lower particle size than typically applied in industry.

Fig. 6 shows the effect of the water recirculation rate on the removal efficiency of COD, TN, TP and VOC. It can be seen that increasing the recirculation rate of the first cycle (i.e. two-step counter-current wash with water) has a higher effect on the overall removal of COD, TN and TP than increasing the recirculation rate of the second cycle. Regarding VOCs, this effect was less pronounced, indicating that the polishing wash is more important to remove VOCs than it is to remove COD, TN and TP. Indeed, after the first two washes with water, the polishing wash was responsible for the additional removal of 25.3% VOCs, which is a lot higher than for COD (12.0%), TN (18.9%) and TP (9.3%). Overall, it was chosen to apply a recirculation rate of 25% for the first water cycle and of 35% for the second water cycle. These recirculation rates resulted in removal efficiencies of 87% COD, 84% TN, 91% TP and 69% VOCs. Higher recirculation rates resulted in a further drop of removal efficiencies, especially for VOCs. For the recycling industry, an ad-hoc strategic trade-off will be necessary between the final plastic recyclate quality and overall treatment cost to find the optimal recirculation rate and SL-ratio.

Different input materials: effect dirt load on water consumption

Four different input materials were analysed: the entire PE-bale (moderate dirt load, moderate VOC-load), collection bags (high dirt load, low VOC-load), fresh products (moderate dirt load, high VOC-load) and secondary packing from industry (low dirt load, moderate VOC-load). The three-step washing process was used, with a SL-ratio of 0.03 and recirculation rates (i.e. amount of water that is reused and not treated) of 25% and 35% for the first and second water cycle, respectively. Overall, the simulated washing line treats 1 ton of input material per hour. Note that in typical recycling plants, several washing lines can run in parallel to increase the scale of the whole plant, but this was excluded from the simulation as results would be similar for other lines. The water losses due to remaining water on the plastic films are 0.12 kg water.kg plastic−1 (assuming 11.5% remaining water after mechanical drying (Larrain et al., 2021)), and the first and second water cycle treat 25.0 m3.hr−1 and 21.7 m3.hr−1, respectively.

The results showed that the quality of the output material followed the same trends as the input materials, but that the overall removal rates for COD, TN and TP were similar. Only for VOCs, the removal efficiencies showed small differences, with a removal efficiency of 66.7% for fresh products (i.e. high VOC-contamination) and of 72.4% for secondary packaging from industry (low VOC-contamination). These results can be explained by the dynamic equilibrium between the plastics and washing medium. Specifically, highly contaminated plastics released greater concentrations of VOCs into the washing medium, while less contaminated plastics resulted in lower concentrations (see Table 6). As can be seen for VOCs, this dynamic equilibrium reaches a certain point, at which the removal rates start dropping. Furthermore, as fresh products contain elevated levels of terpenes (Table S1), this dynamic equilibrium was obtained even faster (Section 3.5.2.2), thereby resulting in reduced removal efficiencies. For COD, TN and TP, this point was not yet reached, with maximum concentrations in the water phase of 3072 mg.L−1, 118 mg.L−1 and 19 mg.L−1, respectively. These simulated wastewater concentrations are within the range reported by Altieri et al. (2021) and Soto et al. (2020), who treated LDPE and PE films, respectively. When interpreting the simulation results, some important elements should be pointed out. Firstly, the wastewater treatment efficiency was kept the same for all different inputs. This assumption needs some caution, as the optimal coagulant dose is prone to changing water composition. Given the high heterogeneity of (post-consumer) plastic waste, this means that a good online measuring technique should be used to assure good wastewater treatment at all times. Examples are the application of a turbidity or UV254nm sensor (Ratnaweera and Fettig, 2015). When selecting the appropriate technique, it is important to stress that, depending on the wastewater composition, the contaminant removal might not be correlated with e.g. turbidity. Hence, this should be tested on beforehand for representative water samples. Moreover, given the variability in plastic waste streams, the composition of the resulting wastewater can fluctuate, potentially compromising the effectiveness of a fixed monitoring approach. Secondly, the dynamic equilibrium between the concentrations in the wastewater and the dirt load on the plastic input has two important consequences. On the one hand, as long as the wastewater is not saturated and the wastewater treatment efficiency is kept stable, this means that the washing system can cope with different input qualities. On the other hand, when a relatively clean input sample is treated after a dirty input sample, the wastewater will be highly contaminated, which will result in lower removal efficiencies for the first cycles, until the system has reached a new dynamic equilibrium. Overall, it can be concluded that maintaining a good wastewater treatment efficiency at all times is key in obtaining high removal rates for both dirt load (i.e. COD, TN and TP) and VOCs.

Generalization and applicability of the approach

While this study focused on post-consumer polyolefin film waste streams from specific sources, the modelling framework and process optimization strategy developed here are broadly applicable to other types of plastic waste and recycling plant configurations. The kinetic model, once calibrated with representative experimental data, can be adapted to different input materials, contaminant profiles, and operational scales. The scenario analyses performed in this work demonstrate that the core principles, such as the dynamic equilibrium between contaminant load and washing water quality, and the benefits of a dedicated polishing wash, are relevant across a range of feedstock qualities and process conditions. However, it is important to note that certain model parameters (e.g., desorption and adsorption rate constants) and process settings (e.g., solid-liquid ratios, recirculation rates) may require adjustment based on the specific characteristics of the waste stream and plant operation. Future research should further validate this approach with additional plastic types and under varying industrial conditions to fully establish its generalizability. Nevertheless, the integrated experimental-modelling methodology presented here provides a robust foundation for optimizing water management and contaminant removal in diverse plastic recycling contexts.

Conclusions

This study investigated the removal of organic contaminants (i.e. COD, TN and TP) and VOCs from post-consumer flexible packaging waste, proposing an optimized washing process based on extensive experimental data and a calibrated Aspen Plus® model. Firstly, when characterizing different plastic input materials, it was found that there was no correlation between the COD- and VOC-load on the plastic input, which might be related to the 1000-fold lower concentration for VOCs or to the difference in nature between VOCs and organic dirt, which could influence cross-contamination. Furthermore, it could be concluded that there is a dynamic equilibrium between the dirt load on the plastics and the concentration in the washing water. Important consequences are that good online monitoring of the wastewater treatment plant is necessary to assure good water quality at all times. Removal rates in this work for coagulation and GAC adsorption ranged from 80%–94%, 64%–82% and >99% for COD, TN and TP, respectively. Moreover, as it was found that the VOC-removal is more sensitive to water reuse/quality than the removal of organic dirt, washing with dirty water will affect the VOC-removal more than it will affect the organic dirt removal. Hence, as an addition to the conventional two-step counter-current washing process, a third polishing wash with a separated water cycle was proposed and simulated in Aspen Plus®. This new process design resulted in a final washing step with cleaner water that clearly improved the overall removal rates for VOC (25%), COD (12%), TN (19%) and TP (9%) in comparison with a two-step counter current process. Overall removal rates were 69% VOCs, 87% COD, 84% TN, and 91% TP. Future research should focus on the economical balance between the final plastic recyclate quality and overall treatment cost.
