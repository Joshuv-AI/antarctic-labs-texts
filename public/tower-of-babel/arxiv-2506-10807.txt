Prompts to Summaries: Zero - Shot Language - Guided Video Summarization
Mario Barbara Alaa Maalouf

Abstract
The explosive growth of video data intensified the need for flexible user - controllable summarization tools that can operate without domain - specific training data. Existing methods either rely on datasets, limiting generalization, or cannot incorporate user intent expressed in natural language. We introduce Prompts-to-Summaries; the first zero-shot, text-queryable video-summarizer that converts off-the-shelf video–language models (VidLMs) captions into user-guided skims via large-language-models (LLMs) judging, without the use of training data at all, beating all unsupervised and matching supervised methods. Our pipeline (i) segments raw video footage into coherent scenes, (ii) generates rich scene-level descriptions through a memory-efficient, batch-style VidLM prompting scheme that scales to hours-long videos on a single GPU, (iii) leverages an LLM as a judge to assign scene - level importance scores under a carefully crafted prompt, and finally, (iv) propagates those scores to short segments level via two new metrics—consistency (temporal coherency) and uniqueness (novelty)—yielding fine-grained frame importance. On SumMe and TVSum, our data-free approach surpasses all prior data-hungry unsupervised methods. It also performs competitively on the Query - Focused Video Summarization (QFVS) benchmark, despite using no training data and the competing methods requiring supervised frame-level importance. To spur further research, we release VidSum - Reason (can be found here ), a new query-driven dataset featuring long-tailed concepts and multi-step reasoning; our framework attains robust F1 scores and serves as the first challenging baseline. Overall, our results demonstrate that pretrained multimodal models, when orchestrated with principled prompting and score propagation, already provide a powerful foundation for universal, text - queryable video summarization. 1.3
Problem statement

Motivated by the previous discussion, we now define the problem as follows.
Given a sequence of video frames

F=\{f_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},

the goal of the
traditional
video summarization task is to develop an algorithm, denoted as
ALG
, which receives
F
as input and produces a video skim composed of selected video segments

S=\{s_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N},

ordered chronologically to create a shorter version of the original video. Each segment
s_{n}
is a contiguous set of consecutive frames, that is,

s_{n}=\{f_{t_{n}},f_{t_{n}+1},\ldots,f_{t_{n}+\Delta_{n}}\},

for some starting frame
t_{n}
and segment length
\Delta_{n}
. This form of summarization aims to maintain temporal continuity, motion, and audio, thereby offering a richer and more natural viewing experience.

In our formulation, we introduce additional complexity and broaden the applicability of the task: the algorithm
ALG

can also accept an optional user-provided text query
T
to guide the summarization process
. In this case, the selected segments
S=\{s_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
are chosen according to the guidance provided by
T
, for example, by focusing on specific parts of the video or ignoring less desired content. Additionally,
ALG

must operate without relying on any training data
.

1.4
Main idea

Elsewhere, video language models (VideoLMs) have emerged as a promising tool, enabling language-guided question answering on videos. These models possess some of the desired traits needed to achieve an open-set, text queryable method for video summarization, as they are already trained on a huge amount of internet-scale data. This extensive training allows them to be highly adaptable, making them not confined to a specific data distribution.

Simply put, while there are no VideoLMs specialized for video summarization, VideoLMs can answer textual queries about videos by generating text descriptions (e.g., "What do you see in this scene?"). These text outputs can then be processed by large language models (LLMs), which, using carefully crafted prompts (e.g., "Given this scene description <Here> and the full video summary <Here>, rate the importance of this scene from 1 to 10"), can rank or evaluate the scenes.
This flexibility enables this approach to perform summarization tasks across various domains without being limited to one specific application. In addition one can add guidance by text to the prompt, e.g. "Given this scene description <Here>, the full video summary <Here>, and the user query: <Prioritize/highlight/Focus-on scenes with negative emotions>, rate the importance of this scene from 1 to 10".

1.5
Challenges

Notably, its not as easy as described as LLM and VideoLMs where not designed for this purpose. For instance, it is still unclear how to: (i) formulate effective queries for ranking scenes or generating informative text descriptors; (ii) segment videos into semantically meaningful and coherent scenes; (iii) ensure that each scene is both unique and complete;
(iv) preserving the temporal context in long videos, especially since video-language models are unable to process the entire video at once due to input length limitations.
(v) propagate scene-level importance scores to individual frames—especially since certain frames may better capture the essence of a scene than others; and, most critically, (vi) develop an integrated framework that combines all these stages while leveraging the capabilities of LLMs and VideoLMs in a principled and effective manner. Thus, these challenges and gaps in current knowledge require a careful and critical examination of how to effectively apply VideoLMs and LLMs toward a zero-shot text-queryable video summarization.

3
Method

We now define our framework, which is designed to effectively capture and represent the essential content of videos without relying on annotated training data.

Introduction to the framework.
Our proposed pipeline, composed of the following key components, each of which must be carefully evaluated to determine the optimal design (see Figure
1
).

1.

Scene detection:
The video is segmented into temporally coherent and semantically meaningful scenes.

2.

Scene description:
For each detected scene, a textual descriptor is generated to summarize its salient content as part of the video in natural language.

3.

Scene-level importance estimation:
Using the generated textual descriptors, an importance score is assigned to each scene, incorporating both semantic relevance and temporal context within the video.

4.

Frame-level weight estimation within the scene
Within each scene, the representativeness of individual frames is evaluated to determine their relative importance (to the scene).

5.

Fusion of scene and frame importance:
For each frame in the full video, the scene-level (scene importance within the video) and frame-level (representativeness within the scene) scores are combined/fused to produce a final frame (importance) score.

3.1
Scene detection

To segment the input video into coherent scenes, we apply a two stages process: initial scene detection with dynamic thresholding and scene boundary refinement.

3.1.1
Initial scene detection with dynamic thresholding

Goal:
Segment the video into non-overlapping, chronologically ordered scenes, where each scene captures a coherent event or action.

Approach:
For each consecutive frame pair
(F_{t},F_{t+1})
, we
use PySceneDetect’s
contentDetector

[
34
]
to compute the mean absolute pixel–intensity difference
D_{t}
as:

D_{t}=\frac{1}{H\times W}\sum_{i=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{W}\left|F_{t}(i,j)-F_{t+1}(i,j)\right|

(1)

where
H
and
W
are the height and width of the frame, respectively, and
F_{t}(h,w)
denotes the value at pixel location
(h,w)
in frame
t
. A cut is declared when
D_{t}\geq\tau
, otherwise
F_{t+1}
is merged with the current shot. Then, scenes shorter than 2 s are merged to avoid over-fragmentation. Finally, scanning the entire video thus produces
N(\tau)
scenes (see Figure
3
).

Rather than relying on a fixed threshold
\tau
to detect scene boundaries, we dynamically determine the optimal threshold
\tau^{*}
by evaluating a predefined range of candidate values.
For each candidate threshold
\tau_{i}\in\{\tau_{\min},\tau_{min}+\Delta\tau,\dots,\tau_{\max}\}
, we compute the number of detected scenes
N(\tau_{i})
and select the threshold that maximizes the rate of change in the number of scenes with respect to
\tau
:

\tau^{*}=\arg\max_{\tau_{i}}-\left(\frac{N(\tau_{i}+\Delta\tau)-N(\tau_{i})}{\Delta\tau}\right).

(2)

When applying this method (Equation
2
), we observe an initial increase at low threshold values, reaching a maximum, and then a sharp decrease with the number of detected scenes. We select the optimal threshold which yields the maximum negative slope after this peak, as shown in Figure
3
, capturing the point with the most significant reduction of scene count. This slope captures the moment when noise-driven fragments disappear yet true boundaries remain (Figure
3
), yielding a segmentation that balances between over- and under-segmentation while ensuring temporal coherence in the detected scenes (Figure
4
(a)).

3.1.2
Scene boundary refinement

Goal:
To improve coherence and semantic consistency, we refine the initial scene segmentation by merging excessively short scenes (not containing sufficient information)—often caused by noise or minor visual changes—with their most similar neighbors, reducing unnecessary fragmentation.

Approach:

A scene is defined as
short
if it contains fewer than
M=150
frames (approximately 5 seconds, we observe that defining short segments as those under 5 seconds is not sufficient to fully prevent over-fragmentation).

The previous stage marked scene boundaries by detecting sharp intensity changes. To avoid spurious cuts, we now refine them: any short segment whose visual embedding closely matches its neighbors is merged back, ensuring temporally coherent and visually consistent boundaries. Given a short scene
S_{i}
, we compute its mean embedding:

\mathbf{e}_{i}=\frac{1}{|S_{i}|}\sum_{\mathbf{f}\in S_{i}}\mathbf{f},

(3)

where
\mathbf{f}
denotes the frame-level embedding (e.g., based on DINO
[
35
]
or CLIP
[
36
]
). We then calculate cosine similarity between
\mathbf{e}_{i}
and the mean embeddings of its adjacent scenes,
\mathbf{e}_{i-1}
and
\mathbf{e}_{i+1}
:

\text{sim}_{\text{prev}}=\frac{\mathbf{e}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{i-1}}{\|\mathbf{e}_{i}\|\,\|\mathbf{e}_{i-1}\|},\quad\text{sim}_{\text{next}}=\frac{\mathbf{e}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{i+1}}{\|\mathbf{e}_{i}\|\,\|\mathbf{e}_{i+1}\|}.

(4)

The scene
S_{i}
is merged with the neighboring scene yielding the higher similarity;
\max(\text{sim}_{\text{prev}},\text{sim}_{\text{next}})
.
This refinement reduces over-segmentation and yields more temporally coherent scenes that better capture complete events or actions, as shown in Figure
4
b.

3.2
Scene description generation

Goal:
Generating textual description summarizing the scene’s content and the overall video.

Approach:

After establishing scene boundaries (fig.
4
b), we generate textual descriptions for each detected scene and the full video using a pre-trained video-language model. The prompt template used for the VideoLM is defined as:

\mathcal{P}_{VideoLM{}}
:
“Describe this video in detail”

which produces detailed meaningful descriptions for the corresponding frame sets.

To address memory constraints
of VideoLMs when processing long frame sequences, we adopt a batch-level processing strategy: the sampled frames are divided into manageable batches, a description is generated for each batch, and finally, the batch-level descriptions are aggregated/concatenated to form a complete description for the scene or the entire video.
To ensure that the description aggregation was performed correctly across the batches, we refined the generated description for each batch by enforcing consistent format. Specifically, each batch description was changed to begin with "The video continues" and ends with "The scene concludes", rather than "The video begins/ends", unless the batch occurred at the start or the end of the video (or the scene). This ensured coherence and continues description after aggregating or stitching different batch descriptions.

Capturing larger context.
To enable providing the VideoLM a larger window (context) within each batch text description, we sample frames at a rate of 1 frame per second, selecting the middle frame of each second to avoid capturing transition frames or black frames that may appear at the edges of cuts.
This strategy ensures the selection of representative content from each second of the video and enables the VideoLM to parse batches representing larger windows and thus larger context from the video, but are of the same size/budget. This strategy proved effective in our experiments, consistently capturing broader context, minimizing misinformation, and achieving near-zero omission of important details across videos of varying durations.

This procedure is consistently applied to generate both scene-level descriptions and a holistic description for the full video.

3.3
Scene level scoring

Goal:
For every scene, we wish to generate a score which represent the importance of this scene as part of the whole video, based on the text descriptions as shown in Figure
4
(c).

Approach:

We utilize the capabilities of LLMs to estimate scene-level importance scores based on the textual descriptions of both the individual scenes and the overall video, which were generated earlier. Specifically, we employ LLMs as judges to assess the contribution of each scene via a single carefully designed prompt template (shown in Section
A
of the appendix); For each scene, we construct an LLM query by plugging the scene description, and the overall video description, into the prompt template, as detailed in Algorithm
2
in the Appendix.

The carefully designed prompt:
The prompt in Listing (Appednix
A
) was meticulously engineered to elicit consistent, fine
-
grained importance scores from LLMs. First, we cast the LLM in the explicit role of an “objective evaluator”, which anchors the model’s behavior and reduces response variance.
Next, we supply both the global (video
-
level) and local (scene
-
level) descriptions, ensuring the model reasons in context rather than in isolation.
A five
-
band rubric—complete with numeric intervals, textual labels, and concrete definitions—calibrates the 1
-
to
-
100 scale, so identical semantic cues map to comparable scores across videos.
Crucially, the prompt enforces a sparse
-
high prior: it instructs the model to reserve scores above 80 for scenes that directly advance the core narrative, while encouraging low scores for ancillary content. This bias guards against score inflation and aligns the LLM’s output with human summarization objectives, where only a handful of pivotal moments should survive into a concise synopsis.
Finally, explicit reminders to “prioritize” essential scenes and “omit” secondary ones further steer the model toward selective, summary
-
oriented judgments—yielding reproducible importance estimates that integrate seamlessly with our downstream ranking pipeline.

3.4
User guided scoring

Goal:
Guiding the summary process based on textual user-provided query.

Approach.

To enable user-guided video summarization, we adapt the same prompt used in the standart scene scoring stage (see Appendix
A
) with one key modification: we add instructions that explicitly consider the user’s query when evaluating scene importance. When a query is given, the prompt guides the language model to assess how well each scene aligns with the user’s content preference.
Scenes that match key events or strongly relate to the query are given higher importance scores. In contrast, scenes that are irrelevant or conflict with the user’s intent are down-weighted.

This lightweight modification enables zero-shot, query-aware summarization via prompt engineering alone, without changing the model or core scoring logic. A more detailed explanation, along with user-guided summarize examples, can be found in Appendix (section
A
).

User-guided extension to the scene scoring prompt.
We now give the extension of the base scene scoring prompt (a full version of the prompt can be found in Section appendix
A
):

The user has provided the following content preference to guide

the summarization:

User Query: <user query>

When assigning a score, consider how well the scene aligns with

this preference.
Scenes that closely match or contradict the user’s intent should be scored accordingly,

reflecting their relevance or

irrelevance to the desired summary focus.

If the scene is not clearly related to this preference, assign a score based on the default scale and

criteria below.

3.5
Frame level scoring

To obtain frame
-
level importance scores, we follow a three
-
stage procedure. First, each frame initially inherits the importance score of its parent scene, as shown in fig.
4
c. Then, a temporal smoothing function is applied around scene boundaries to avoid abrupt changes and create a gradual transition between adjacent scenes. After that, within every scene, we compute a representativeness weight for each frame (how well it reflects that scene). Finally, this weight is then fused with the smoothed scene score, yielding the final per
-
frame importance value for the entire video.

3.5.1
Scene to frames : scene score normalization and temporal smoothing function

Goal and approach:

After establishing scene boundaries (Figure
4
b) and computing the score of each scene (Figure
4
c), a normalization step is applied to the scores of the scenes to emphasize relative differences in importance as perceived by the LLMs.
Since the scores are not derived from a binary scale (important or not important), but rather fall along a spectrum of relevance (1-100), normalization helps highlight subtle distinctions and amplifying the contrast between different scenes, ensuring that highly important scenes are more prominently reflected while less relevant scenes are de-prioritized. See Section
4.1.4
for the exact used normalization procedure. Now, each frame within the scene is initially assigned the same normalized score as the scene to which it belongs (Figure
4
d).

In order to gradually blend a scene score to the score of its neighboring, we employ a temporal smoothing function, for each two consecutive scenes, starting from the midpoint of the first scene and ending in the midpoint of the second, a cosine function is used to interpolate between the score of the two scenes based on their relative positions (Figure
4
d). The transition helps eliminate unnatural jumps in scores values, resulting in more coherent and gradual shift across scene boundaries.
This smoothing is crucial for generating a natural and visually consistent video summary, where the importance of each frame reflects not only the content of its own scene but also the surrounding temporal context.

3.5.2
Frame weighting with in a scene

Goal:
To go beyond our scene-level importance scores—which already summarize contextual, semantic, and temporal cues—we now assign frame-level weights. Each weight quantifies how well a given frame describes its entire scene, letting us pinpoint the most representative and informative moments within that scene; i.e., assessing every frame a representativeness score within the scene.

Approach:

First, we note that Algorithm
4
in the appendix provides the full details for this step. We first segment each scene into non-overlapping segments each of size
W
. For each segment, we define and compute two key properties to assess its contribution to the overall summary:
consistency
and
uniqueness
. These two scores aim to balance stability and novelty in the selected content. To compute these properties, we begin by employing KMeans
[
37
,
38
]
for clustering the frame embeddings of each scene, to reveal the underlying structure and visual patterns. The optimal number of cluster for each scene, donated as
K^{*}
, is selected dynamically by evaluating a predefined range of candidate values; For each candidate value
K_{i}\in\{K_{min},K_{min}+\Delta{k},\dots,K_{max}\}
, we compute Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS)
[
39
]
, donated, we then apply the Elbow method
[
40
]
to the WCSS curve to select
K^{*}
, the value at which the rate of decrease in WCSS significantly slows down—identified as the point with the greatest change in the slope of the WCSS curve.

Consistency
Reflects the internal coherence of the segment. It quantifies how uniformly the frames within a segment belong to the same visual pattern, as indicated by their cluster labels.
A segment with high consistency suggests a stable and semantically meaningful portion of the video. Formally, given a segment
S
of
W
consecutive frames with cluster labels
\{l_{t+1},l_{t+2},\dots,l_{t+W}\}
, the consistency score is defined as :

\text{Consistency}(S)=\frac{\max\limits_{c}\left|\{l_{t+i}\mid l_{t+i}=c\}\right|_{i=1}^{W}}{W}

(5)

where
c
ranges over all cluster labels present in the segment
S
of size
W
.

Uniqueness
(or dissimilarity) measures how distinct the segment is with respect to internal variation in visual features. It captures how much the frame embeddings in the segment deviate from the segment’s mean, which helps highlight rare or visually rich content. Given the frame embeddings
\{f_{t+1},f_{t+2},\dots,f_{t+W}\}
in segment
S
, we compute the mean embedding
\bar{\mathbf{f}}
and define uniqueness as:

\text{Uniqueness}(S)=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{i=1}^{W}\left\|\mathbf{f}_{t+i}-\bar{\mathbf{f}}\right\|_{2},\bar{\mathbf{f}}=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{i=1}^{W}\mathbf{f}_{t+i},\quad

(6)

Finally, we combine these two factors — consistency and uniqueness — into a unified score to assign a weight to each segment.

Weight(S)=\sigma\cdot Consistency(S)+(1-\sigma)\cdot Uniqueness(S)

(7)

The weighting of these factors is governed by the parameter
\sigma
, which allows us to adjust the relative importance of consistency versus uniqueness. All frames within a given segment inherit the segments computed weight. Furthermore, the frame consistency weight
\sigma
and segment duration
W
are dynamically determined according to the video duration, donated as
T
,as detailed in Equation
8
.

F_{S}(\text{Video})=\begin{cases}\{\sigma{=}0.1,\;W{=}1\,\text{s}\}&\text{if }T>5S\\

3.5.3
Frame-level importance scoring

Goal:
Fusing scene importance and frame weights to create per-frame importance.

Approach:

The final step calculates frame-level importance scores, which are the output of our algorithm; providing the importance each frame within the video.
Given the smoothed per-frame score (from Section
3.5.1
) and per-frame wights (as in Section
3.5.2

This ensues that every scene within the video contributes fairly to the final summary, based on its own internal dynamics and relative frame importance.

The result is a set of frame-level importance scores (Figure

4.1
Standard video summarization

4.1.1
Datasets

We utilize two widely used datasets:
SumMe

[
25
]
and
TVSum

[
26
]
. The SumMe dataset consist of 25 videos, ranging from 1 to 6 minutes in duration, comprising of a various range of content including holidays, events, and sports; captured from both first-person and third-person perspectives. Each video has been annotated by 15-18 users, who provided key-fragments. Consequently, each video is accompanied by multiple user-generated summaries of varying lengths, typically ranging from 5% to 15% of the original video’s duration. The TVSum dataset consist of 50 videos, ranging from 1 to 61 minutes in duration. These videos belong to
10 different categories comprising of various activities like changing a vehicle tire, making a sandwich, and visiting an award wining sandwich shop, with 5 videos in each category, and were collected from YouTube.
For annotation, each video was reviewed and rated by 20 users, who assigned frame-level importance
scores on a scale from 1 (not important) to 5 (very important).
[
15
]

4.1.2
Evaluation protocol

We utilize the classic keyshot evaluation method, introduced by
[
6
]
and widely adopted by state-of-the-art video summarization methods. This metric assesses the similarity between the generated summary (A), limited to less than 15% of the the original video duration, and the user provided summary (B). It compute the temporal overlap between the summaries using (P)recision and (R)ecall:

\displaystyle\text{(P)recision}

\displaystyle=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A|}\quad\text{and,}\quad\text{(R)ecall}

\displaystyle=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|B|},

(9)

where
|A|
denotes the duration of the set
A
, measured in number of frames. Then we compute the F1-score (F), which represents the harmonic mean of (P)recision and (R)ecall, as:

\displaystyle\text{F}

\displaystyle=2\cdot\frac{P\cdot R}{P+R}

(10)

In SumMe, annotations are provided as key-fragments, this formula is directly applied. However, in the TVSum, annotations are provided at the frame level, with translation from the frame level to the key fragment using methodologies presented in
[
6
]
. After obtaining key-fragment annotations for a given test video, we calculate the F1-score for the generated summary against each user summary for that video. The F1 scores for the TVSum dataset are averages as proposed inr
[
26
]
, while for the SumMe dataset, the F1 score for a given test video is the maximum F1 score calculated for that video, as proposed in its paper
[
41
]
. To obtain the final F1 score for each data set, we calculate the mean F1 score in the test videos, and take the mean results in 5 different splits of generated video tests, following the methodology in
[
6
]
. This score serves as a performance metric.

4.1.3
Design choices

For each dataset, we choose one of three normalization functions—Min-Max normalization, Exponential normalization, or their combination—for the normalization step described in Section
3.5.1
, based on the dataset’s characteristics.

The used normalization for SumMe.
Min-Max normalization linearly scales values between 0 and 1, preserving relative distances but remaining sensitive to outliers. This characteristic makes it particularly well-suited to the SumMe dataset, where misalignment between user-selected keyframes often occurs due to subjective differences in user preferences. As noted in
[
6
]
, the evaluation protocol for SumMe selects the closest user summary by taking the maximum F1-score across all references. Consequently, we adopt Min-Max normalization to better align the computed importance scores with the variability inherent in human annotations.

The used normalization for TVSum.
In contrast, Exponential normalization amplifies differences among higher values, making it particularly effective for highlighting prominent segments. This characteristic makes it especially well-suited to the TVSum dataset, where there is no misalignment between user-selected keyframes, and the user scores tend to be more uniformly assigned, as the evaluation protocol for TVSum is computed based on the average F1-score across all users.
Therefore, we adopt Exponential normalization to better align the computed importance scores with the relative consistency (invariability) present in human annotations.

The combined approach aims to leverage the benefits of both techniques, balancing local contrast with global consistency in frame-level importance scores. To validate the correctness of our choices, we conducted additional experiments comparing different normalization methods across multiple variations on both datasets. Further details are provided in the Section
4.4
.

From this point forward, we employ Min-Max normalization for the sumMe dataset and Exponential normalization for the TVSum dataset.

4.3.2
Evaluation protocol

We utilize the same method commonly employed for evaluating generic video summaries — the keyshot evaluation method — originally introduced by
[
6
]
and widely adopted in state-of-the-art video summarization research. This metric evaluates the similarity between the generated summary (A), constrained by a duration budget, and the user-provided summary (B). It computes the temporal overlap between the two using precision (P) and recall (R), as defined in Equations
9
and
10
.

To generate a summary from frame-level importance scores, we uniformly divide each video into non-overlapping fragments, where each fragment spans
d\%
of the video’s total duration. For each fragment, we compute a fragment-level score by averaging the importance scores of all frames within that fragment. To select the final summary, we apply the Knapsack algorithm, subject to a summary budget of
M\%
of the total duration of the video, as in TVSum and SumMe
[
6
,
26
]
. In our experiments, we set the fragment size to 3% and the summary budget to 36% (12 fragments). Additional design details are provided in the section
C
of the Appendix.

Global vs. Local Visual Inputs for Description Generation.
To understand the impact of visual context on the quality and informativeness of scene-level descriptions, we conducted experiments comparing two approaches: global (masking a scene from a video) input versus local-only (single scene) input. The idea is to compare whether it’s easier to assess a scene’s importance by contrasting it with the entire video, or by comparing the full video without that scene to the original full video; i.e., the gain by adding it vs the loss of removing it.

In the global approach, we masked a specific scene by replacing its frames with black frames that includes the text "MASKED SCENE", while keeping the rest of the video intact.
This masked video was then passed to the VideoLM with a modified VideoLM’s prompt template that asked the model, not only describe the video in details as demonstrated in
3.2

Similar to VideoLM’s prompt template, the LLMs prompt template was also modified enforcing the LLM to assign an importance score based on the missing information from the global masked textual description on a certain scene.
This allowed the model to reason about the scene’s absence in the context of the entire video, implicitly assessing its importance based on the degradation of the global narrative.
In contrast, the local approach involves using only the frames of the scene in question as input to generate a description. This method relies purely on the scene’s standalone visual content without incorporating broader temporal context.
Contrary to our expectations, the local-only input consistently outperformed the global masked method across both the TVSum and SumMe datasets, as shown in Figure

and Figure

Interestingly, in SumMe, while the global approach (where the scene in question is masked) achieved competitive results-54.76% f1 score, this can be attributed to the nature of certain SumMe videos, which include more abstract scenes (e.g., burning matchsticks video). These scenes are often difficult to assess using local information alone.
Nevertheless, the use of local descriptions proved to be more accurate, focused, and relevant to the content of the scene. In contrast, the global approach occasionally introduced missing information, misleading content, or overgeneralized descriptions due to the absence of visual evidence from the masked segment, leading to noisier importance estimates.
These findings suggest that, in the context of description-driven video summarization, local visual input provides a stronger grounding for generating semantically rich and precise descriptions, particularly when the objective is to evaluate the informativeness of individual scenes in isolation.

4.5
Implementation details

We choose to employ frame value density for scene detection, utilizing a dynamic threshold that is defined in section II-A1. This approach allows us to detect scene boundaries effectively by applying the
contentDetector
function from PySceneDetect
[
34
]
, which identifies these boundaries based on the dynamically selected threshold, as shown in II-A1. For feature extraction, we utilize the CLIP/ViT-Large-Patch14 model
[
36
]
, which generates frame embeddings for each frame. This model allows us to capture both visual and contextual information from each frame accurately.

For the task of description generation, we utilize the LLAVA/Qwen-2.0-7B Video Language Model
[
44
,
45
]
, which extracts textual information both at the video level and at the scene level. To ensure accurate and contextually rich descriptions, frames are sampled at a rate of 1 frame per second (FPS), with the middle frame of each second selected as the representative input for description generation. This sampling strategy ensures that each frame used captures the essence of its corresponding second, providing a strong basis for textual representation.

As previously noted, processing long videos in their entirety can lead to memory issues. To address this, we employ a batching strategy. Specifically, the sampled frames are divided into manageable batches, with each batch containing 80 frames. Since we sample at 1 FPS, each batch effectively represents 80 seconds of video. This batching approach allows the VideoLM to generate detailed descriptions while maintaining maximum temporal coherence and staying within memory constraints.

We utilize ChatGPT with the GPT-4o
[
46
]
model as an LLM judge for scoring scenes based on textual descriptions, setting the temperature parameter to 0.5. The temperature is a key parameter in large language models (LLMs) that controls the randomness or creativity of the generated text. A lower temperature (closer to 0) results in more deterministic and focused outputs, while a higher temperature (closer to 1) leads to more diverse and creative responses. Setting it to 0.5 strikes a balance between coherence and variability, allowing for both accurate and contextually rich descriptions in our summarization process.

Optimizations for Longer Videos.
To handle the large number of frames in long videos—particularly in the QFVS dataset, where videos can span up to 5 hours and contain over 220k frames with 45 queries per video—we introduced an optimization strategy. Normally, each video–query pair would be processed individually with the relevant description, which would be computationally expensive and time-consuming, especially when using VideoLM. To reduce overhead, we process all queries at once for each scene. Specifically, instead of evaluating each scene multiple times for different queries, we pass all queries to the LLM in a single prompt and ask it to assign relevance scores to the scene for each query simultaneously. This significantly cuts down on processing time, resources and cost-by up to
50x
- though handling long videos remains resource-intensive due to the sheer volume of frames.

All the experiments detailed in this paper were conducted on a PC equipped with one NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB GPU and AMD EPYC 7742 64-Core CPUs .

Conclusion

This work introduces
Prompts to Summaries
, the first
zero-shot, text-queryable
video–summarization framework that orchestrates large video–language models (VideoLMs) and large language models (LLMs)
without any task-specific training data
. Extensive experiments, a new benchmark, and detailed ablations yield the following key findings:

•

Training-free state of the art.

Our plug-and-play pipeline surpasses all prior
unsupervised
methods on SumMe and TVSum and rivals the best
supervised
systems—despite requiring no frame-level annotations, data, domain tuning, or hyper-parameter search.

•

VidLM captions are “summary-ready.”

Off-the-shelf VideoLMs already produce rich, coherent scene descriptions that can double as draft summaries.
By simply ranking these captions with an LLM judge and propagating scores, we convert them into competitive, user-controllable video skims with no additional training.

•

SOTA LLMs are great text-based “scene judges”

With careful prompt design, off-the-shelf LLMs ranked the generated captions in a zero shot manner, leading to SOTA results.

•

Effective query guidance.

A single prompt extension lets users steer the summary with natural-language queries; on QFVS we match the fully-supervised CLIP-It while outperforming four classical baselines by up to 20 pp F
1
.

•

New fine-grained benchmark.

We release
VidSum
-
Reason
, a new fine-grained text guided video summarization benchmark whose queries demand temporal reasoning and external knowledge, opening a new testbed for more sophisticated video summarization; our method attains a mean 43.4 F
1
, beating a random baseline by nearly 10 pp.

•

Local trumps global masking.

For the video summarization task, measuring the importance of adding a scene to the summary performs better that computing the loss of excluding it from it, this may be explained as descriptions generated from the scene itself outperform those from globally masked scenes withing video videos, confirming the value of focused visual context.

•

CLIP embeddings remain strongest.

CLIP/ViT-L14 leads DINO variants by up to 2 pp F
1
across datasets in the context of frame embedding for summarization.

•

Match normalization to annotation style.

Min–Max works best for SumMe, exponential for TVSum; a hybrid scheme is robust for unseen domains.

•

scalable.

Our batch split technique for generating video summaries enables end-to-end processing for a 10-minutes video in realtime and proved to be scalable and robust for a 5-hours video on a single A100 GPU, paving the way for practical deployment.

6
Limitations and feature work

Our work suffers from the following two limitations:

•

Prompt sensitivity.

Small wording changes in the LLM judge prompt can modulate scene scores by several points; a formal stability study is still missing.

•

Compute overhead for very long videos.

Although processing is real-time for ~10-minute clips, multi-hour footage still requires batched VideoLM inference, and thus the run time is proportional to the number of baches we wish to generate a description for.

Future work induces:

•

Audio–visual fusion.

Integrate ASR transcripts and learned audio embeddings so that sound events and speech can influence scene scoring and user queries can reference audio content.

•

Faster summarization.
Compress the entire stack to make it faster and enabling it on mobile device, enabling privacy-preserving, real-time highlights on wearable and drones.
