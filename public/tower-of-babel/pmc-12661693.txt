Association of body roundness index with cardiovascular disease in early Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic syndrome stage 0–3: mediation by the triglyceride-glucose index in a national cohort study
Authors: Jiamiao Cai, Dongze Zheng, Sifan Xu, Shiyan Jiang, Yuxuan Jiang, Rou Zhang, Chenlin Li, Li Zhang, Bin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome, encompassing metabolic dysfunction, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease (CVD), represents a growing global health challenge. Body Roundness Index (BRI) is an index representing viscera obesity, and its prospective association with CVD across CKM stages and mediation by insulin resistance remain unexamined.ObjectiveThe aim of the study was to explore the association between BRI and CVD among the population of CKM syndrome stage 0–3, and discover the potential mediation of insulin resistance.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 7,214 participants from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). BRI was calculated using waist circumference and height. CKM stages (0–3) were classified according to the American Heart Association criteria. CVD was defined as new-onset myocardial infarction, angina, coronary heart disease, heart failure, or other physician-diagnosed cardiac events during follow-up and was reported by participants. Cox models adjusted for demographics and metabolic factors assessed BRI-CVD associations. Restricted cubic splines tested nonlinearity, and mediation analysis quantified the contribution of TyG index.ResultsAmong participants (mean age 59.8 ± 9.4 years, 52.9% female), each 1-unit BRI increase elevated CVD risk by 12.2% (HR = 1.122, 95% CI:1.080–1.166). The highest BRI quartile (vs. lowest) showed 58% higher CVD risk (HR = 1.585, 95% CI:1.351–1.859). A linear dose-response relationship was observed (P nonlinear > 0.05). Stronger relationship between BRI and CVD was observed in CKM stage 1–2. TyG index mediated 22.7% of the BRI-CVD association (P = 0.008).ConclusionsBRI positively linear associated with CVD, especially in early CKM syndrome population. Insulin resistance mediated the BRI-CVD association. The findings highlight BRI’s potential for early risk stratification and the multifactorial pathophysiology linking adiposity to CVD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-02007-w.Keywords: Body roundness index, Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic syndrome, Cardiovascular diseases, Insulin resistance, Visceral obesity

Introduction

Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome represents a cluster of interrelated conditions encompassing metabolic dysfunction, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular disease (CVD), including heart failure, coronary artery disease, and stroke [1]. Impaired CKM health is strongly associated with premature mortality and heightened risks of cardiorenal complications [2]. Despite advances in prevention, CVD remains the leading contributor to global disease burden, with mortality rates rising disproportionately among middle-aged and elderly populations [3]. In the United States alone, over 5% of adults exhibit concurrent cardiac, renal, and metabolic disorders, a prevalence projected to escalate with aging demographics [4]. Obesity, particularly visceral adiposity, is a pivotal driver of CKM progression. Accumulating evidence links obesity to endothelial dysfunction, glomerular hyperfiltration, and arterial stiffness, thereby amplifying risks of CKD and CVD [5, 6]. A meta-analysis of 1.2 million participants identified elevated body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), and waist-to-height ratio as independent predictors of CKD onset [7]. Notably, abdominal obesity demonstrates stronger associations with CVD and metabolic dysregulation than BMI, underscoring the limitations of conventional anthropometric indices [8].

Globally, obesity prevalence reached 18.5% in women and 14% in men by 2022, necessitating refined metrics for adiposity assessment [9]. While BMI remains widely utilized, emerging indices such as the Body Roundness Index (BRI)—a geometric model integrating WC and height—show superior ability to assess visceral fat distribution and predict obesity-related comorbidities [10, 11]. Prospective studies associate BRI with incident CVD, metabolic syndrome, and insulin resistance, though U-shaped relationships with mortality have also been reported [12–15]. Moreover, the Triglyceride-Glucose (TyG) index, a surrogate marker of insulin resistance derived from fasting triglycerides and glucose, is increasingly implicated in CVD pathogenesis [16, 17]. Previous study has found that the relationship of both general and central obesity with CVD could be mediated through the TyG index [18]. While prior studies have established BRI’s association with cardiometabolic risk factors, its stage-specific predictive value for CVD across the continuum of CKM syndrome, and the potential mediating role of insulin resistance (IR) therein, remain unexplored. This study therefore aimed to evaluate the prospective association between BRI and CVD risk across CKM stages 0–3, and assess the proportion of this relationship mediated by the TyG index.

Method

Study population

This current study was a secondary study from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), which is a national population-based cohort study (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/) targeted at Chinese adults aged 45 years and above conducting surveys periodically from 2011 to 2020. The study cohort was enrolled from both rural and urban settings via a multistage stratified sampling method weighted by population size, spanning 150 counties or districts across 28 provinces in China [19]. The CHARLS study complied with the ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). Trained investigators administered standardized questionnaires through in-person interviews to collect sociodemographic data, medical history, health status, and depressive symptoms [19]. All participants provided informed consent before participating in the study.

In this study, participants interviewed from 2011 to 2012 were considered as the baseline, and followed up from 2013 to 2020. The flowchart (Fig. 1) illustrates the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the study. Initially, we included 11,847 participants into our study. Totally 1668 participants who had CVD at baseline, including those participants missing information on CVD. 240 participants younger than 45 years were excluded. Subsequently, we further removed 1657 participants lacking weight or height data, 1068 participants missing other laboratory data. Finally, 7214 participants were finally enrolled into the study.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was incident cardiovascular disease (CVD), defined as new-onset myocardial infarction, angina, coronary heart disease, heart failure, or other physician-diagnosed cardiac events during follow-up (Waves 2–5). CVD events were evaluated through these questions: “Have you been told by a doctor that you had a heart attack, angina, coronary heart disease, heart failure, or other heart problems?” [1, 20] The CHARLS study team carried out strict quality control procedures for data collection and verification to guarantee the dependability of the data [21].

Data collection

Demographic and clinical variables such as age, sex, educational attainment, marital status, geographic residence, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, and self-reported medical history (hypertension and diabetes) were incorporated as covariates in the analytical models. Educational attainment was stratified into two levels: primary or below, middle or above. Marital status was grouped as “Married” or " Unmarried,” while geographic residence was classified as “urban” or “rural.” Smoking behavior was categorized into three groups: “Smokers (containing current smokers and former smokers)”, and “non-smokers”. Hypertension was diagnosed based on either systolic blood pressure readings of ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or a prior clinical diagnosis of hypertension reported by participants, or current use of antihypertensive drugs [22]. Similarly, diabetes mellitus was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: fasting blood glucose levels ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or an HbA1c of ≥ 6.5% at baseline, or self-reported history of diabetes confirmed by healthcare providers, or active use of prescribed hypoglycemic agents [23, 24]. Liver diseases and lung diseases were mainly determined by self-reported.

Fasting venous blood specimens were obtained by healthcare professionals affiliated with the Chinese Centre for Disease Control and Prevention following standardized operational protocols, with subsequent analyses conducted at a centralized laboratory facility. Serum creatinine (µmol/L) is measured using the rate blank and compensated Jaffe creatinine method. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) is measured by immunoturbidimetry on a Hitachi 7180 chemical analyzer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Lipid indicators were measured by Enzymatic colormetric test. BUN (Blood urea nitrogen) is measured by Enzymatic UV method with urease and Uric acid was measured by UA Plus methods.

Definition of CKM stage0-3

The classification of CKM Syndrome stages 0 to 3 follows the AHA Presidential Advisory Statement on CKM Syndrome [1]. Here’s the stratifications: Stage 0 is defined as the absence of CKM Syndrome risk factors; Stage 1 is marked by excess or dysfunctional adiposity, which contained the population of BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2 and waist circumference ≥ 80 cm in women or ≥ 90 cm in men; Stage 2 classified as Metabolic Risk Factors and Kidney Disease; and Stage 3 defined as Subclinical CVD in CKM Syndrome. In this study, the very high risk of CKD mainly the CKD population in stage G4 or G5. A high 10 - year CVD risk predicted by the Framingham risk score was considered equivalent to subclinical CVD risk [25]. The eGFR level (mL/min/1.73 m2) was calculated according to the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) [26] and classified into CKD stages according to Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) [1]. More details were presented in Table S4.

Statistical analysis

Participants were categorized into BRI quartiles (Q1–Q4). Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, with between-group differences assessed via ANOVA. Non-normally distributed variables are reported as median (IQR) and analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (%) with χ² tests. Cox proportional hazards models (univariable and multivariable-adjusted) estimated hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs for BRI-CVD associations across CKM stages 0–3. Multivariable models were adjusted for all covariates presented in Table 1, including age, sex, education, smoking, alcohol use, marital status, hypertension, diabetes, triglycerides, total cholesterol, HDL-C, eGFR, uric acid, and CRP. Stage-stratified Cox analyses evaluated heterogeneity. In order to investigate the potential nonlinear relationship between BRI and CVD incidence, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with 3 knots were carried out to test nonlinearity in BRI-CVD relationships overall and by CKM stage. Subgroup analyses assessed interactions across age (< 65 vs. ≥65 years), sex, smoking, alcohol use, and clinical profiles. Sensitivity analyses employed inverse probability treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores.

We conducted a mediation analysis to assess the potential mechanism under the relationship of BRI and CVD in the CKM syndrome stage 0–3 via insulin resistance, measured by TyG index. The BRI was used as a predictor variable (X), and the TyG was regarded as a mediator (M) and the CVD onset was set as outcome variable (Y). The analysis process consists of four steps: (1) confirming the link between X and Y (Model Y = βTot X) (βTot = total effect); (2) confirming the link between X and M (Model M = β1 X) (β1 = indirect effect1); (3) determining how much of Y is explained by controlling for X (Model Y = β2 M + βDir X) (β2 = indirect effect, βDir = direct effect); and (4) calculating the proportion of indirect effect: mediation effect (%) = (β1 × β2/βTot) × 100% [27].

All analyses used R v4.2.2, with two-tailed P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Result

Baseline characteristics

A total of 7214 participants in CHARLS from 2011 to 2020 were finally enrolled, of which 3813 (52.9%) were female and 3401(47.1%) were male. The mean (SD) age was 59.82 ± 9.35 years. More baseline information is presented in Table 1 according to the quartile of BRI. Significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed across most variables. It revealed that smoking and alcohol consumption prevalence decreased progressively with higher BRI. The proportion of hypertension and diabetes was higher in the higher BRI groups as well. Compared to male, female tended to have higher BRI lever according to the baseline. It also demonstrates the positive correlation between BRI and TG (triglyceride), TC (cholesterol), Glu(glucose), SBP (systolic blood pressure) and DBP (diastolic blood pressure. Additionally, higher BRI groups exhibited lower levels of HDLC (high-density lipoprotein). The proportion of participants with advanced CKM stages (stage 2–3) increased with higher BRI. Inflammatory markers such as CRP also escalated along with BRI. No significant differences were observed in rural/urban distribution or liver disease prevalence.

The relationship between BRI and the incidence of CVD in the population with CKM syndrome of stage 0–3.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed a progressive increase in 9-year cumulative cardiovascular disease (CVD) incidence across ascending BRI quartiles (Q1-Q4) among individuals with Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome stages 0–3 (log-rank P < 0.0001). Participants in the highest quartile (Q4) exhibited a terminal CVD incidence rate twice that of the lowest quartile, with survival curves diverging markedly over time (Fig. 2). Restricted cubic spline analysis confirmed a near-linear positive association between BRI and CVD risk (P overall < 0.001; P nonlinear = 0.12) (Fig S1).

Among 1,674 incident CVD cases (23.2% cumulative incidence), Cox regression models demonstrated robust associations (Table 2). Each 1-unit BRI increment conferred a 19.5% elevated CVD risk in unadjusted models (HR = 1.195, 95%CI:1.157–1.234), which attenuated to 12.2% after full adjustment for demographic, metabolic, and inflammatory covariates (HR = 1.122, 95%CI:1.080–1.166).

Quartile-stratified analyses revealed differential risk patterns (Table 2). While Q2 showed borderline significance in partially adjusted models (Model II: HR = 1.199, 95%CI:1.029–1.397), this association dissipated in fully adjusted models (HR = 1.157, 95%CI:0.992–1.349, P > 0.05). In contrast, Q3 and Q4 maintained significant risk escalation across all models, with Q3 participants exhibiting an proximately 44% increase in CVD risk (HR = 1.4362, 95% CI: 1.2329–1.6730, P < 0.001), Q4 participants presenting 58% higher CVD risk versus Q1 in fully adjusted analyses (HR = 1.585, 95%CI:1.351–1.859, P < 0.001). Sensitivity analyses using inverse probability treatment weighting (IPTW) corroborated these findings (Table S1).

Moreover, to evaluate the comparative utility of BRI, we conducted ROC analysis against established anthropometric and clinical lipid metrics (Table S3). The AUC for predicting incident CVD was 0.58 (95% CI: 0.57–0.60) for BRI. This was comparable to the predictive performance of BMI (AUC = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.57–0.60) and waist circumference WC (AUC = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.55–0.59). Notably, all adiposity indices demonstrated superior discriminatory ability compared to individual lipid parameters, including LDL-C (AUC = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.52–0.55), HDL-C (AUC = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.52–0.55), triglycerides TG (AUC = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.53–0.56), and total cholesterol TC (AUC = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.52–0.55).

Association between BRI and CVD incidence among specific CKM syndrome stages 1–3 population

Restricted cubic spline Cox regression analyses further clarified the association between Body Roundness Index (BRI) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk across Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome stages 0–3. A significant linear dose-response relationship (Fig S2) was observed in stages 1–3 (P overall < 0.05; P nonlinear > 0.05), whereas no association was detected in stage 0 (P overall > 0.05). Adjusted models revealed a progressive attenuation of BRI-associated CVD risk with advancing CKM severity (Table 3): each 1-unit increase in BRI elevated CVD risk by 22.5% in stage 1 (HR = 1.225, 95% CI: 1.105–1.358), declining to 8.7% in stage 3 (HR = 1.087, 95% CI: 1.020–1.160). Quartile-stratified analyses revealed threshold-dependent CVD risk patterns. Despite advancing CKM severity, the third (Q3) and fourth (Q4) BRI quartiles consistently demonstrated elevated risks: Q3 conferred 52.5% (HR = 1.525, 95%CI:1.094–2.125, P = 0.013) and 54.1% (HR = 1.541, 95%CI:1.236–1.921, P < 0.001) higher CVD risk in stages 2 and 3, respectively. Q4 exhibited pronounced escalation across stages, ranging from 82.1% (stage 1: HR = 1.821, 95%CI:1.206–2.751, P = 0.004) to 47.7% (stage 3: HR = 1.477, 95%CI:1.148–1.899, P = 0.002). Notably, while Q3 showed no association in stage 1 (P > 0.05), Q4 remained strongly predictive even in early disease, underscoring that only extreme BRI thresholds independently drive CVD risk.

Subgroup analyses (Table S2) confirmed BRI’s consistency as a CVD predictor across demographic and clinical strata (P < 0.05 for all subgroups). Significant effect modifications emerged for age and preexisting lung disease (P for interaction < 0.05), with borderline interaction observed for education level (P = 0.054). No interactions were detected for smoking, alcohol use, hypertension, or liver disease (P for interaction > 0.05), underscoring BRI’s robustness despite heterogeneous patient profiles.

Mediation effect of insulin resistance between the BRI and CVD

The mediation analysis (Fig. 3) evaluated the role of the TyG index, which is the indicator reflecting the insulin resistance, in explaining the association between BRI and CVD was carried out in the study. The TyG index accounted for 25.60% (P = 0.015) in the unadjusted model and explained 22.73% (P = 0.008) after adjusted for covariates of the total effect, indicating that approximately one-quarter of the association between BRI and CVD was mediated through the TyG index. After adjustment for covariates, the proportion mediated by TyG slightly decreased but remained statistically meaningful.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of our primary findings by using the China-PAR model instead of the Framingham Risk Score (Table S5). The results demonstrated consistent and significant associations between higher Body Roundness Index (BRI) values and increased risk of CVD across most stages of Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome. The results confirmed that the association between BRI and CVD risk remains robust regardless of the risk assessment algorithm used to define CKM stages, strengthening the validity of our primary conclusions about BRI’s predictive value in early CKM syndrome populations.

Discussion

This prospective cohort study of 7,214 middle-aged and older adults provides novel insights into the association between Body Roundness Index (BRI) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk across Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome stages 0–3. We identified BRI as an independent predictor of CVD in early CKM syndrome stages (1–2), with insulin resistance mediating 22.7% of this relationship. Previous studies had confirmed the predictive value of BRI toward CVD outcomes [28, 29]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a graded, stage-specific association between BRI and CVD risk across the early stages of CKM syndrome, and to quantify the substantial mediating role of insulin resistance in this relationship.

Abdominal obesity, characterized by visceral adipocyte hypertrophy and ectopic lipid deposition, drives systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation. Previous studies have demonstrated a stronger association of visceral fat with CVD and CKD compared to subcutaneous fat [26, 27]. Unlike conventional metrics such as waist circumference (WC), BRI integrates height-adjusted abdominal adiposity, demonstrating superior clinical utility in predicting cardiometabolic risk [30].

As shown in the results, there was the near-linear dose-response relationship between BRI and CVD incidence in the population of CKM syndrome in stage 0–3. Each 1-unit BRI increase conferred a 12.2% elevated CVD risk after full adjustment, with the highest quartile (Q4) exhibiting 58% greater risk versus Q1. Our findings align with prior cross-sectional studies highlighting BRI’s predictive capacity for hypertension, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance [12–14, 31], yet extend these observations through longitudinal evidence of dose-dependent CVD risk escalation. A large Chinese cross- sectional study demonstrated that the BRI was better than other abdominal obesity indicators for predicting CVD risk factors [28]. Maessen et al., showed that the incidence of CVD in the greater quartiles was significantly greater compared with the lowest BRI quintile, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) further demonstrated that the Body Roundness Index (BRI) exhibited high accuracy in detecting established cardiometabolic risk factors, including hypertension and hypercholesterolemia [29]. A study conducted in South Africa identified robust correlations between the BRI and established cardiometabolic risk markers, specifically elevated LDL cholesterol levels and blood pressure in a young adult population [30].

Notably, the study analyzed the association between BRI and CVD across different stages of the CKM syndrome population. In metabolically intact individuals (stage 0), BRI showed no significant association with CVD incidence (P >0.05), potentially reflecting compensatory mechanisms that mitigate early metabolic perturbations—a phenomenon consistent with the limited predictive value of BMI in metabolically healthy populations [1, 10]. The Q4 participants exhibited consistent associations with CVD risk across all CKM syndrome stages, emphasizing the critical need for interventions in populations with elevated BRI. Notably, stages 1–2 demonstrated strong linear relationships, suggesting visceral adiposity accelerates CVD progression during subclinical metabolic dysfunction. However, the association between BRI and CVD weakened (HR = 1.087 per 1-unit BRI increase) in advanced CKM syndrome (Stage 3). The attenuated association between BRI and CVD in advanced CKM syndrome may be partly explained by the ‘obesity paradox’, but alternative explanations include survivor bias, competing risks from non-CVD mortality, and reverse causality whereby weight loss occurs due to subclinical illness. In advanced stages, the overwhelming risk conferred by existing end-organ damage and comorbidities may eclipse the more modest contribution of adiposity [32]. The obesity paradox remained to be controversial and the mechanisms were unclear. One of the potential mechanisms may related to the energy reservation to provide substrates for metabolic demands and immune function. Some overweighted or obese people with better healthy condition and better preserve muscle mass always show better prognosis with lower BMI and less muscle mass person [33, 34]. In a retrospective study by Tsujimoto et al. enrolling 3310 patients with HFpEF demonstrated that abdominal obesity was associated with reduction in mortality in unadjusted analysis [35]. Another study carried out in 2018 including 6493 participants with stable coronary heart diseases found that compared with normal BMI, those overweight and obesity class I had reduced all-cause mortality risk [36]. McAuley et al. confirmed the paradox that CHD patients with higher WC did not relate with higher mortality [37]. A meta-analysis conducted 39 general cohorts of 5 459 014 participants between 1970 and 2017 found that increased risk of death was not associated with lower waist circumference or waist-to-height ratio, as was seen with body mass index [7]. Our findings suggests that while BRI is a valuable tool for early risk stratification and prevention in stages 0–2, its utility for predicting new CVD events may be limited in stage 3, where management should focus on mitigating existing end-organ damage and competing risks. Furthermore, our analyses adjusted for several demographic and lifestyle factors, including education level, marital status, and alcohol consumption, which may influence CVD risk through socioeconomic, behavioral, or psychosocial pathways. For instance, lower educational attainment, a key marker of socioeconomic status, has been consistently linked to an increased risk of CVD, potentially mediated by lower health literacy, adoption of adverse health behaviors, and delayed access to healthcare [38]. The relationship between alcohol consumption and CVD is complex and dose-dependent. While some studies have suggested a potential protective effect of light-to-moderate drinking, the most recent and comprehensive evidence emphasizes that the overall health risks associated with alcohol consumption argue for caution, and any potential benefit is likely outweighed by the risks, particularly with heavier drinking patterns [39]. Although these factors were not the primary focus of this study, their inclusion as covariates strengthens the robustness of our findings regarding the independent association between BRI and CVD.

Mediation analysis revealed insulin resistance (TyG index) accounts for 22.7% of the BRI-CVD relationship, highlighting insulin resistance as a key pathway linking visceral adiposity to CVD. The decreased insulin sensitivity can lead to endothelial dysfunction, and contributed to the formation of atherosclerotic plaque, playing a critical role in the pathology of CVD [40, 41]. A cohort study recruited 94,136 participants from the Kailuan study found that the relationship of both general and central obesity with CVD could be mediated through the TyG index, with the mediating proportion of more than 25% [18]. The potential mechanisms of the mediation of TyG for abdominal obesity and CVD among CKM syndrome stage0-3 population were as followed. Excess visceral adipose tissue will release basal lipolysis and releases free fatty acids (FFA), some inflammatory factors such as interleukins and cytokines, which will lead to cardiac dysfunction by accelerating atherosclerotic processes and contribute to inflammation and endothelial and coagulation dysfunction [42, 43]. FFA is associated with promoting serine phosphorylation of insulin receptors and result in the insulin resistance as well [44]. Furthermore, chronic inflammatory and oxidative stress associated with obesity will contributes to the destruction of insulin sensitivity and the elevation of atherosclerotic risk [44, 45] Besides, the adipocyte hypertrophy and abnormal accumulation will exaggerate the metabolic disturbance and decrease insulin sensitivity [44]. Clinically, combining BRI with TyG may enhance risk prediction and guide targeted interventions.

There are some advantages of our study: firstly, this is a prospective cohort study focusing on the relationship between BRI and CVD in the population of CKM syndrome stage0-3. Secondly, the relationship was verified after adjustment of different covariates step by step, which improve the reliability and solidity of the results. Thirdly, our comprehensive stratified analyses identified stage-specific associations between Body Roundness Index (BRI) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) progression across heterogeneous population cohorts, which make sense for precision-tailored intervention protocols and early risk stratification. Fourthly, we carried out subgroup analysis and the result demonstrated robust generalizability across various patient subgroups, enhance the Suitability stability of the clinical significance.

Nevertheless, the limitations of the study should not be ignored. Firstly, we applied the Framingham 10-year cardiovascular risk score rather than the latest PREVENT equations to define CVD risks [43]. Secondly, the diagnosis of CVD was reported by participants and was lack of the auxiliary examination for precise diagnosis, which is susceptible to recall bias and misclassification. This likely led to an underestimation of the true effect sizes, as non-differential misclassification of the outcome typically biases hazard ratios toward the null. Therefore, the true strength of the association between BRI and CVD risk may be stronger than what we observed. Thirdly, the CHARLS cohort focus on middle-aged and elderly Chinese adults, limiting the generalizability of the research. Fourthly, the influence of dynamic changes of BRI and TyG index were lack of discover in the research. Furthermore, the mediation analysis assumes no unmeasured confounding between BRI, the TyG index, and CVD. While we adjusted for a wide range of covariates, this assumption is strong and its violation remains a potential limitation. Therefore, more cohort study focusing on different population should be carried out. Fourthly, our index was a static indicator, longitudinal studies tracking BRI trajectories are required to assess dynamic risk. Finally, the bias by cofounding factors was inevitable despite adjustment of different clinical factors.

Conclusions

This prospective cohort study identifies Body Roundness Index (BRI) as a stage-specific predictor of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in early Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndromes (stages 1–2), with attenuated utility in advanced organ damage (stage 3). Insulin resistance mediated 22.7% of this association, highlighting multifactorial pathways linking adiposity to CVD. Clinically, BRI enables early risk stratification across CKM syndrome stages, while future research must delineate mechanistic drivers and validate its utility in diverse populations.
