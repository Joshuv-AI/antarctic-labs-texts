Adaptive Nonlinear Vector Autoregression: Robust Forecasting for Noisy Chaotic Time Series
Azimov Sherkhon, Susana López-Moreno, Eric Dolores-Cuenca, Sieun Lee, Sangil Kim

Abstract
Nonlinear vector autoregression (NVAR) and reservoir computing (RC) have shown promise in forecasting chaotic dynamical systems, such as the Lorenz-63 model and El Niño–Southern Oscillation. However, their reliance on fixed nonlinearities—polynomial expansions in NVAR or random feature maps in RC—limits their adaptability to high noise or real-world data. These methods also scale poorly in high-dimensional settings due to costly matrix inversion during readout computation. We propose an adaptive NVAR model that combines delay-embedded linear inputs with features generated by a shallow, learnable multi-layer perceptron (MLP). The MLP and linear readout are jointly trained using gradient-based optimization, enabling the model to learn data-driven nonlinearities while preserving a simple readout structure. Unlike standard NVAR, our approach avoids the need for an exhaustive and sensitive grid search over ridge and delay parameters. Instead, tuning is restricted to neural network hyperparameters, improving scalability. Initial experiments on chaotic systems tested under noise-free and synthetically noisy conditions showed that the adaptive model outperformed the standard NVAR in predictive accuracy and showed robust forecasting under noisy conditions with a lower observation frequency.

Reservoir computing (RC) is a machine learning technique developed in the early 2000s that uses reservoirs to learn spatiotemporal features in time series data

Additionally, a universal approximation theorem was established in

Next-generation reservoir computing (NG-RC) was introduced in
[
7

NG-RC formalizes the nonlinear vector autoregression (NVAR) framework. It was also shown in
[
3
]

A comparative study in
[
36
]
showed how NVAR outperforms long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, gated recurrent unit, and several echo state network architectures in the prediction of chaotic time series. In
[
7
]
, it was demonstrated that NVAR performs well on three challenging RC benchmark problems. However, to the best of our knowledge no studies have conducted experiments on noisy data. Standard NVAR uses fixed polynomial basis functions to incorporate nonlinearity, thereby limiting its adaptability in handling chaotic, high-dimensional or noisy data. Moreover, the linear readout matrix in both RC and NVAR depends on the nonlinear feature vector and is therefore susceptible to accumulating errors originating from this vector. This linear readout matrix calculation, which we introduce in Section
2.1
, also involves computing a matrix inverse, which can become a computational bottleneck when the reservoir—or feature vector, in the case of NVAR— is large.
A kernel ridge regression approach for the NVAR with polynomial basis functions was introduced in
[
9
]
. That study also explored replacing the polynomial kernel by a Volterra kernel. However, kernel methods suffer from scalability issues when applied to large-scale data.

We address the limitations of standard NVAR by replacing the handcrafted nonlinear feature vector with a learnable, shallow multilayer perceptron (MLP) that transforms delay-embedded inputs in a data-adaptive manner. The MLP is equipped with a dropout layer
[
37
]
, which helps the model generalize better under noisy conditions, thereby enhancing prediction accuracy. By using an MLP, we trade the interpretability offered by the NVAR technique for improved performance on noisy data in chaotic systems, an essential trade-off for forecasting real-life scenarios. A key innovation of our method is the joint optimization of both the nonlinear feature vector and the linear readout matrix through a gradient-based method, within a skip-connection architecture
[
42
]
,
[
13
]
. This end-to-end training enables the linear readout matrix and nonlinear features to be learned simultaneously, yielding an optimal joint configuration and decoupling the readout from any fixed feature representation. As a result, the learned features are explicitly tuned to the data, and the model’s adaptability to chaotic and noise-perturbed systems is enhanced, making the approach well suited for forecasting geophysical processes such as ocean salinity, sea surface temperature and climate indices.

The work in
[
29
]
is closely related to our approach in the sense that it also considers skip connection layers and neural networks to replace the nonlinear feature vector of a standard NVAR. However, the architecture described in that work uses fixed weights, and the readout matrix is still computed through least squares regression, which limits their method to noise-free observations.

Background

2.1
Reservoir Computing

In this section, we briefly introduce traditional RC, extended to include leaky integrator neurons.
Let
X_{i}=[x_{1,i},x_{2,i},\dots,x_{d,i}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a time series training data for time
i\in\{1,\dots,T\}
. This linear vector is introduced into the reservoir through the input layer with a fixed, randomly sampled matrix
W_{\text{in}}
. The dynamic reservoir system can then be represented by the following iterations

\displaystyle r_{i+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)r_{i}+\alpha f(Ar_{i}+W_{\text{in}}X_{i}+b),

(1)

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=W_{\text{out}}r_{i+1},

where
r_{i}
is an
m
-dimensional vector whose dimension is generally taken to be of a much higher dimension
m>d
. The parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
is the leaking (or decay) rate of the nodes,
f
is an activation function (commonly,
f(x)=tanh(x)
),
A
is a fixed, randomly sampled connectivity matrix, and
b
is a bias vector. Finally,
W_{\text{out}}
is trained on the data and obtained by least-squares regression with Tikhonov regularization. The loss function is as

L(W_{\text{out}})=\|W_{\text{out}}H_{\text{total}}(t)-X(t+dt)\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma\|W_{\text{out}}\|_{2}^{2},

(2)

where
X(t+dt)
is the desired output and its closed-form solution
[
40
]
is as follows

W_{\text{out}}=XH_{\text{total}}^{\intercal}(H_{\text{total}}H_{\text{total}}^{\intercal}+\gamma I)^{-1},

(3)

where
H_{\text{total},i+1}=r_{i+1}
. After computing the linear readout weights, forecasts are generated iteratively, with each output fed back as an input for the next prediction step.

2.2
Nonlinear Vector Autoregression

Let
X_{i}=[x_{1,i},x_{2,i},\dots,x_{d,i}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a time series training data for
i\in\{1,\dots,T\}
. The standard NVAR (in particular, NG-RC in
[
7
]
) approach constructs the feature vector as follows:

H_{\text{total}}=b\oplus H_{\text{lin}}\oplus H_{\text{nonlin}},

(4)

where
b
is a bias constant and
\oplus
indicates concatenation of vectors.

The linear feature vector
H_{\text{lin}}
at step
i
is constructed as follows
[
20
]

\displaystyle H_{\text{lin},i}

\displaystyle=X_{i}\oplus X_{i-s}\oplus X_{i-2s}\oplus\cdots\oplus X_{i-(k-1)s}

(5)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}X_{i}\\
X_{i-s}\\
X_{i-2s}\\
\vdots\\
X_{i-(k-1)s}\end{bmatrix},

where
k
is the delay parameter, meaning the vector includes
k-1
previous time steps, each spaced by
s
, the number of skipped steps between consecutive observations. Thus,
H_{\text{lin},i}
contains
d\times k
components in total.

Remark 2.1
.

This construction implies that while traditional RCs require long warm-up periods—up to
10^{3}
or more
[
7
]
—to ensure that the reservoir state does not depend on initial conditions, the warm-up period of standard NVAR includes only
k\times s
steps, which are required to construct the linear feature vector.

Remark 2.2
.

For simplicity, we assume
s=1
for both standard and adaptive NVAR, as formulated in Section
5.1
.

In contrast, the nonlinear feature vector
H_{\text{nonlin}}
is derived directly from the linear feature vector as a nonlinear function of
H_{\text{lin}}
. In
[
7
]
, this nonlinear function is defined as a quadratic polynomial, and
H_{\text{nonlin}}
is composed of the
\frac{(dk)(dk+1)}{2}
unique monomials of the outer product
H_{\text{lin}}\otimes H_{\text{lin}}
. The loss function is then defined as

L(W_{\text{out}})=\|W_{\text{out}}H_{\text{total}}(t)-Y(t+dt)\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma\|W_{\text{out}}\|_{2}^{2}.

In other words, RC and NVAR share the same loss function. As before,
W_{\text{out}}
is obtained by least-squares regression with Tikhonov regularization, which has the closed-form solution

W_{\text{out}}=YH_{\text{total}}^{\intercal}(H_{\text{total}}H_{\text{total}}^{\intercal}+\gamma I)^{-1}.

In
[
7
]
, the desired output
Y
is assumed to be the difference
X_{i+1}-X_{i}
, and the linear readout then takes an Euler-like form

\hat{X}_{i+1}=\hat{X}_{i}+W_{\text{out}}H_{\text{total}}.

(6)

We also adopt this difference-based training in the adaptive NVAR model.

One of the challenges in standard NVAR is choosing appropriate values for
\gamma
and
k
, as the model is highly sensitive to these parameters. Supplementary Figures 1 to 13 show our efforts to optimize these parameters to ensure a fair comparison between standard and our adaptive NVAR in the experiments described in Section
3

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dt}(t)

\displaystyle=\sigma\bigl(y(t)-x(t)\bigr),

(7)

\displaystyle\frac{dy}{dt}(t)

\displaystyle=x(t)\,\bigl(\rho-z(t)\bigr)-y(t),

\displaystyle\frac{dz}{dt}(t)

\displaystyle=x(t)\,y(t)-\beta\,z(t).

.

We will implement the Lorenz-63 system with the parameter values

When trajectories separate exponentially, the system exhibits sensitivity to initial conditions—a defining property of chaos. The strength of this chaotic behavior is quantified by a properly averaged exponent of this divergence, known as the Lyapunov exponent

4
Discussion

We presented an adaptive NVAR model that learns both a linear readout matrix and a learnable nonlinear feature vector using a skip-connection MLP. Standard NVAR models determine predictions via linear regression after concatenating a delay-embedded input vector with a fixed nonlinear feature vector, typically constructed using polynomial basis functions. Because of its fixed nature and lack of flexibility, this approach performs poorly in noisy environments, even though it works well in deterministic regimes. By contrast, our model enables data-driven feature learning by jointly training a neural network and a linear readout matrix within a skip-connection architecture. This design yields a data-adaptive forecasting framework capable of capturing not only higher-order dynamics, but also noise-induced nonlinearities— features that fixed-basis methods such as standard NVAR fail to represent.

Remarkably, the adaptive NVAR overcomes the scalability limitations of standard NVAR. Standard NVAR requires computing a potentially large matrix inverse to solve the analytical ridge regression problem, which becomes memory-intensive and often unfeasible for high-dimensional systems on CPUs. In contrast, our model avoids explicit matrix inversion by learning the linear readout matrix and nonlinear transformation via gradient-based optimization. This makes the method suitable for real-world applications, allowing GPU training, support for mini-batch learning, and favorable scaling the system dimensionality. Furthermore, while standard NVAR is highly sensitive to the delay parameter
k
and ridge regularization parameter
\gamma
—necessitating an expensive grid search— the adaptive NVAR demonstrates more stable performance across a wider range of parameter settings. An additional benefit of approximating the NVAR architecture with neural networks is the ability to incorporate regularization techniques, such as dropout
[
37
]
. Dropout is widely used in machine learning to prevent overfitting and contributes to the robustness of our model under noisy conditions.

Therefore, the adaptive NVAR provides a robust and scalable framework for simulating chaotic and nonlinear dynamics in the presence of noise. In future research, we intend to explore its application to higher-dimensional datasets, real-world systems and online learning scenarios involving streaming data.

5
Methods

5.1
Adaptive Nonlinear Vector Autoregression

In this section, we introduce the adaptive NVAR model. Figure
6
illustrates the differences between NG-RC (or NVAR) and adaptive NVAR. The construction of the linear feature vector and computation of the output vector are the same in both models. The difference lies in the computation of the nonlinear feature vector: whereas NVAR constructs a fixed nonlinear vector derived from the linear feature vector, our adaptive NVAR model trains a shallow MLP in order to compute it. We also incorporate
W_{\text{out}}
into the training architecture introducing a skip connection so that it is trained simultaneously with the nonlinear feature vector. This eliminates the need for matrix inversion in (
3
) by using gradient-based optimization instead.

Figure 6
:
Comparison of a standard NVAR (top) and the data-adaptive NVAR (bottom). The differences lie first in the construction of the nonlinear feature vector, created as a (quadratic) polynomial in the case of the standard NVAR, whereas in the adaptive model,
H_{\mathcal{NN}}
is the output on a trained MLP. Moreover, the linear readout matrix
W_{\text{out}}
is computed as the closed-form solution of a least-squares regression with Tikhonov regularization (ridge regression) in the standard NVAR. In contrast,
W_{\text{out}}
is a trainable weight matrix in the skip-connection architecture of the adaptive model and is optimized via gradient descent.

Let
X_{i}=[x_{1,i},x_{2,i},\dots,x_{d,i}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a vector in a time series with time index
i\in\{1,2,\dots,T\}
, where
T
is the total number of times steps. Similar to standard NVAR, we construct a delay-embedded input vector as follows

H_{\text{lin},i}=X_{i}\oplus X_{i-s}\oplus X_{i-2s}\oplus\cdots\oplus X_{i-(k-1)s}\in\mathbb{R}^{dk},

where
k
is the number of time delays,
\oplus
denotes vector concatenation and
s
is the number of time steps skipped between each delay. For simplicity we assume
s=1
.

Remark 5.1
.

Initiating the model construction in accordance with the NVAR method ensures that the warm-up period is identical to that of standard NVAR.

The ability of neural networks, particularly MLPs, to approximate complex nonlinear functions is well established. As demonstrated in
[
16
]
, MLPs are universal function approximators that, with sufficient capacity, can represent a wide class of nonlinear mappings. Owing to their ability to capture nonlinear temporal dependencies, neural networks have also been extensively used in time series forecasting
[
41
]
,
[
35
]
. For readers seeking a mathematical viewpoint on neural network architectures and deep learning, we recommend
[
14
]
, which provides a thorough introduction for applied mathematicians. Building upon these foundations, we define an MLP denoted as
\mathcal{NN}(\cdot;\theta)
, which transforms the delay embedding vector into the following nonlinear feature vector

H_{\mathcal{NN},i}=\mathcal{NN}(H_{\text{lin},i};\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

where
\theta
denotes the trainable parameters of the network and
m
is the output dimensionality of the nonlinear feature space produced by the neural network. To match the NVAR approach, we define
m=\frac{(dk)(dk+1)}{2}
. The MLP includes a dropout layer that helps prevent overfitting on noisy data and supports robust forecasting under noisy conditions. We then compute
H_{\mathcal{NN},i}
as

H_{\mathcal{NN},i}=W\bigl(\text{dropout}\bigl(\tanh(W_{\text{in}}H_{\text{lin},i}+b_{1})\bigr)\bigr)+b_{2},

(8)

where
W
and
W_{\text{in}}
are trainable weight matrices. This neural transformation replaces the fixed nonlinear feature map traditionally used in NVAR. We construct the total feature vector by concatenating the linear and nonlinear components,

H_{\text{total},i}=H_{\text{lin},i}\oplus H_{\mathcal{NN},i}\in\mathbb{R}^{dk+m}.

(9)

This differs from the standard NVAR in that we do not include a bias constant.

For prediction, we apply a linear readout

\hat{Y}_{i}=\hat{X}_{i+1}-\hat{X}_{i}=W_{\text{out}}H_{\text{total},i},

(10)

since, as in (
6
), we are predicting the difference between states
\hat{X}_{i+1}-\hat{X}_{i}
, rather than the absolute state itself. Here,
W_{\text{out}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(dk+m)}
is a trainable weight matrix. To further clarify this skip-connection architecture, as used in
8
,
9
and

1
.

Algorithm 1

Adaptive NVAR Model

The model is trained end-to-end, jointly optimizing both the nonlinear neural feature parameters
\theta
and the linear readout weights
W_{\text{out}}
, by minimizing the prediction loss

\displaystyle L(\theta,W_{\text{out}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum\limits_{i=i_{0}+1}^{n-1}\|\hat{Y}-Y\|_{2}^{2}

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum\limits_{i=i_{0}+1}^{n-1}\|W_{\text{out}}H_{\text{total},i}-Y\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum\limits_{i=i_{0}+1}^{n-1}\|W_{\text{out}}[H_{\text{lin},i}\oplus\mathcal{NN}(H_{\text{lin},i};\theta)]-Y\|_{2}^{2},

where
i_{0}+1
is the first valid training index. Supplementary Example 1 shows a simple working case to understand the indices and the formulation of the model.

Unlike traditional NVAR, which solves the linear readout analytically, our adaptive approach treats all components as differentiable and trainable, allowing full backpropagation through both the delay embedding and nonlinear transformation.
We adopt a two-phase training strategy:

(i)
Adam pretraining: we first optimized the model parameters using the Adam optimizer
[
21
]
, which is well-suited for fast convergence during the initial training phase. This step helps the model discover a good initial weight configuration.

(ii)
L-BFGS fine-tuning: after pretraining, we refined the model using the L-BFGS optimizer
[
25
]
—a quasi-Newton method that leverages second-order curvature information. L-BFGS is particularly effective for fine-tuning models with a relatively small number of parameters, such as adaptive NVAR.

Remark 5.2
.

We note that when performing experiments on noise-free data, we use the following formulation for the MLP instead of (
8
):

H_{\mathcal{NN},i}=W\bigl(\tanh(W_{\text{in}}H_{\text{lin}}+b_{1})\bigr)+b_{2},

i.e., we eliminate the dropout layer when the data is not noisy.

Remark 5.3
.

Unlike RC, which is mathematically equivalent to NVAR under certain conditions
[
3
]
, our adaptive NVAR method is not mathematically equivalent to an NVAR model when the MLP uses the identity function as its activation. In fact, with the identity function in place of
\tanh
and without the
dropout
layer, the proposed method becomes linear.

To understand the relationship between the adaptive NVAR architecture and standard NVAR, let us denote the span of the shallow MLP in the adaptive NVAR by:

\sum_{n}=\operatorname{span}\{\tanh(W\cdot x+b_{1})\ |\ W\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ b_{1}\in\mathbb{R}\}.

From the work of
[
23
]
, if
\mu
is a non-negative finite measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with compact support and absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, then
\displaystyle\sum_{n}
is dense in
L^{p}(\mu)
, for
1\leq p<\infty
. In particular, a shallow MLP can approximate the vector of polynomial features used in any NVAR architecture. Therefore, the adaptive NVAR can approximate any NVAR architecture, not just using quadratic polynomials. As a result, any dynamical system that can be approximated by standard NVAR can also be approximated by the adaptive NVAR.
