Gene regulatory network analysis identifies auranofin as an anti-atherosclerotic drug
Authors: Letizia Amadori, Claudia Calcagno, Ravneet Kaur, Hauke Horstmann, Tianshe He, Michael Gildea, Ken Sakurai, Peik Sean Chong, Roza Shamailova, Sonum Naidu, Christopher A Hill, Swathy Sajja, Jordan Bresciani, Maxwell La Forest, Adam A Ghotbi, Raphael Soler, Christopher Faries, Edward A Fisher, Zahi A Fayad, Nathanael R Fillmore, Jaime Ramos-Cejudo, Benjamin Readhead, Joel T Dudley, Johan L M Bjorkegren, Chiara Giannarelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGene-regulatory networks (GRNs) offer a mechanistic framework for dissecting the complex molecular and genetic architecture of coronary artery disease and for identifying new therapeutic opportunities. Here, we present a GRN-driven drug-repurposing strategy that integrates transcriptional signatures induced by silencing key drivers of the human arterial wall foam cell regulator GRN 42 with drug-induced gene expression profiles from the NIH LINCS program. In vitro screening of top candidate compounds validates the computational predictions, identifying candidate foam cell modulators and showing that auranofin, an FDA-approved gold salt used to treat rheumatoid arthritis, effectively reduces foam cell formation. In vivo, auranofin attenuates atherosclerosis and inflammation in both male mice and rabbits. Clinically, auranofin was associated with reduced cardiovascular risk in a retrospective cohort of patients with rheumatoid arthritis. Together, these findings show that a GRN-based drug-repurposing framework, coupled with preclinical and clinical validation, can uncover new therapeutic applications for existing drugs, including auranofin, in coronary artery disease.Subject terms: Phenotypic screening, Atherosclerosis, Gene regulatory networks, Cardiology

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is a major public health concern and a leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide1,2. Despite the effectiveness of lipid-lowering treatments such as statins, residual morbidity and mortality among CAD patients remain high3–5, highlighting the need to identify new druggable molecular targets. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified robust genetic associations with CAD and myocardial infarction (MI), guiding the design of new highly effective lipid lowering drugs6,7 such as PCSK9 inhibitors8,9. However, identified GWAS loci together explain 30-40% of the estimated CAD heritability6,10, indicating that new drug discovery strategies are needed. The field of systems genetics has revolutionized our understanding of the molecular basis of CAD11, which arises from the interaction of environmental factors with genetic susceptibility11. This biological complexity is better understood through gene regulatory networks (GRNs)11–13, which model the regulation of gene expression in networks operating within and across tissues14, providing a comprehensive understanding of the genetic and environmental interactions underlying CAD not explained by GWAS loci11–13,15–19. Drawing upon our combined expertise in modeling GRNs in CAD20,21 and drug repurposing22, we employed a systems genetics and computational drug repurposing approach to identify existing compounds predicted to restore the function of GRNs involved in atherosclerosis, the underlying pathology of CAD. Specifically, we focused on GRN 42, an atherosclerotic arterial-wall specific GRN identified as causal for CAD23. GRN 42 was inferred from a human genetic and gene expression dataset obtained from 612 tissue samples of seven CAD-relevant tissues from 114 patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) enrolled in the Stockholm Atherosclerosis Gene Expression (STAGE) study23. The activity of GRN 42, derived from its 109 genes, strongly correlates with CAD severity as measured by pre-operative angiograms23. Moreover, a meta-analysis of multiple CAD GWAS24 indicated that the expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) of GRN 42 are highly enriched in CAD risk, according to the CARDIoGRAM C4D dataset. GRN 42 was also prioritized due to its strong evolutionary conservation, as it reemerged in gene expression data from the Hybrid Mouse Diversity Panel (HMDP)23,25 where it was also highly associated with the extent of atherosclerosis, recapitulating the findings in the STAGE study. Silencing four of the seven key driver genes within GRN 42 in THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages incubated with acetylated low-density lipoprotein (AcLDL) showed a profound influence on foam cell formation23. Specifically, silencing of PQBP1 (encoding Polyglutamine Binding Protein 1), DRAP1 (encoding DR1 Associated Protein 1), or POLR2I (encoding RNA Polymerase II Subunit I) reduced foam cell formation, while silencing AIP (encoding Aryl Hydrocarbon Receptor Interacting Protein), increased foam cell formation23. To identify small molecules targeting GRN 42, we focused on genes within a sub-network correlated with foam cell formation in THP-1 derived macrophages. By applying these gene expression data to the L1000 transcriptional signatures from the NIH LINCS Program, we identified several candidate compounds predicted to either increase or reduce foam cell formation. In vitro functional screening confirmed the predicted positive or negative effects, with auranofin, a gold-containing FDA approved oral compound to treat rheumatoid arthritis (RA)26–28, emerging as the most promising candidate in reducing foam cell formation. Experimental studies confirmed that auranofin reduces foam cell formation in vitro, and atherosclerosis progression and inflammation of the arterial wall in vivo. These preclinical observations were further validated in humans, as propensity score–matched analysis of a retrospective cohort of patients with rheumatoid arthritis from the nationwide VA Corporate Data Warehouse (VA-CDW) showed that auranofin use was associated with reduced cardiovascular risk. These results underscore the pivotal role of GRNs in the etiology of complex traits and demonstrate how the agnostic identification of GRNs can facilitate drug repurposing. Our findings highlight the anti-atherosclerotic effects of auranofin through its regulation of foam cell formation, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic option for CAD and similar atherosclerotic conditions.

Results

Identification of candidate compounds influencing foam cell formation by targeting GRN 42

Given the pivotal role of GRN 42 in CAD and foam cell formation, we hypothesized that small molecules capable of restoring its function would reduce foam cell formation and have anti-atherosclerotic properties. To identify these compounds, we first derived a sub-network of transcriptional signatures obtained from the silencing of four key drivers of GRN 42: AIP, PQBP1, DRAP1, and POLR2I. The sub-network signatures included the key drivers and genes differentially expressed at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.1, classified as either positively or negatively correlated with cholesteryl ester (CE) accumulation in THP-1-derived macrophages. Next, we identified concordant and opposing transcriptional signatures of different chemical perturbagens (i.e., small molecule, whose transcriptional signatures are profiled to identify gene‑expression changes) in the L1000 NIH Library of Integrated Network-based Cellular Signatures (LINCS) Program, which contains transcriptional signatures from 20,000 small molecules at different stage of development assayed across a range of cell lines, using various concentrations and treatment durations29,30 (Fig. 1A). To identify small molecules targeting GRN 42, we focused our analysis on LINCS Level 4 data for chemical perturbagens profiled at least once in THP-1 cells, which resulted in 371 unique small molecules (Supplementary Fig. 1; Source Data). We calculated a Connectivity Score (CS)29 for each GRN 42 key driver perturbation; CS values ranged from +0.252 to −0.221 (Supplementary Fig. 2; Source Data). To integrate instance-level scores across different cellular and dosage contexts for each perturbagen, we first selected the ten signatures with the highest absolute CS scores and then averaged these top ten CS values to obtain a single CS for each small molecule (Supplementary Fig. 1).

For further experimental validation of these computational predictions, we selected the top small molecules predicted to decrease, increase, or have no effect on foam cell formation, prioritizing those that were either FDA-approved or Phase 2a-ready (Fig. 1B). Auranofin (mean CS = 0.186), an approved drug to treat rheumatoid arthritis (RA)26–28, and BX-795 (CS = 0.131), a Phase 2-ready inhibitor of both 3-phosphoinositide-dependent kinase-1 (PDK1) and TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1)31–35, were selected for validation among those predicted to decrease foam cell formation. Methyl-fasudil (CS = 0.019), a Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK) inhibitor that was approved in 1995 in Japan for treating cerebral vasospasm associated with subarachnoid hemorrhage36–39, was selected among small molecules predicted to have no effect on foam cell formation. Nicardipine (CS = −0.043), FDA-approved to treat hypertension and angina, and flutamide (CS = −0.083), to treat prostate cancer40–43, were predicted to increase foam cell formation (Fig. 1B). Auranofin ranked 5th overall across all 371 screened compounds and was the top-ranked FDA-approved excluding FDA-approved oncology drugs vorinostat (CS = 0.252; approved for cutaneous T-cell lymphoma) and SN-38 (CS = 0.214; the active metabolite of irinotecan used in colorectal cancer). In contrast, broad anti-inflammatory agents (dexamethasone, CS = −0.070, rank 276) and lipid-lowering drugs (atorvastatin, CS = −0.014, rank 181; simvastatin, CS = +0.086, rank 30) scored substantially lower, suggesting that the GRN 42-based approach can effectively identify additional therapeutics with anti-atherogenic potential (Supplementary Fig. 2; Source Data).

Auranofin reduces foam cell formation in vitro

To experimentally validate the computational predictions of our GRN-based drug repurposing, we tested the effect of compounds predicted to reduce, have no effect on, or increase foam cell formation by quantifying CE accumulation in THP-1 derived macrophages pre-treated with the compound for 24 h and then treated further with the compound plus AcLDL for 48 h. As computationally predicted, auranofin (0.5 µM and 1 µM) reduced CE accumulation in foam cells compared to vehicle (DMSO, < 0.1% v/v) (Fig. 1C). BX-795 (0.1–10 µM) and methyl-fasudil (0.1–10 µM) had no effect on CE accumulation. Treatment with nicardipine (10 µM) and flutamide (10 µM) increased CE accumulation (Fig. 1C). Based on these results, auranofin was selected for further studies.

Auranofin influences GRN 42 gene expression in foam cells

To confirm that the mechanism of action of auranofin involves the regulation of GRN 42, we first examined the expression of GRN 42 genes in foam cells treated with auranofin (0.5 µM) compared to vehicle. Several of the GRN 42 genes were downregulated, while others were upregulated, indicating that auranofin influences the function of GRN 42 in foam cells (Fig. 2A). Next, to identify areas of significant overlap between the gene expression profiles induced by auranofin in foam cells and previous independent microarray data obtained by silencing key drivers of GRN 42 in foam cells from Talukdar et al.23, we employed the Rank-Rank Hypergeometric Overlap (RRHO) algorithm44,45. This approach, which compares gene-expression profiles using the hypergeometric distribution (Fig. 2B), revealed that those GRN 42 genes significantly downregulated by auranofin were upregulated when the key drivers AIP, PQBP1 and DRAP1, but not POLR2I, were silenced (Fig. 2C–F). This is evidenced by the concentration of low/significant p values on the top left quadrant (for AIP, PQBP1 and DRAP1) and on the top right quadrant (for POLR2I). These discordant gene expression patterns indicate that auranofin affects foam cell formation through GRN 42 by modulating the activity of its key drivers AIP, PQBP1 and DRAP1 (Fig. 2C–F). This was confirmed functionally: the increased foam cell formation caused by AIP silencing, was rescued by auranofin (Fig. 2G), indicating convergence on AIP-regulated pathways within GRN 42. In contrast, the reduction of foam cell formation caused by PQBP1 silencing, was maintained with auranofin co-treatment (Fig. 2H), consistent with shared downstream effectors of AIP. Importantly, auranofin did not significantly alter AIP or PQBP1 mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 3), supporting engagement of GRN 42 through downstream network effects rather than direct transcriptional regulation of key drivers.

Auranofin induces anti-atherosclerotic transcriptional reprogramming in foam cells

To study the transcriptional changes associated with the reduction in foam cell formation induced by auranofin, we compared RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) gene expression profiles of foam cells treated with auranofin and those treated with vehicle. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis identified transcripts encoding proteins known to be involved in reverse cholesterol transport and lipid processing (Fig. 3A). Specifically, auranofin influenced the expression of genes regulating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPAR) signaling and lipid metabolism46, including PPARA, which is involved in negative regulation of both lipid and cholesterol storage (GO:0010888; GO:0010887) and foam cell differentiation (GO:0010745)46. Auranofin increased the expression of LDLR (encoding low density lipoprotein receptor) and the cholesterol transport gene CES1 (encoding carboxylesterase 1)47,48 while it downregulated the expression of STAB1 (encoding the scavenger receptor stabilin-1) and the pattern recognition receptor CLEC7A49,50. Auranofin treatment increased the expression of NR1H3, which encodes Liver X receptor alpha (LXR-alpha), and NPC1, which encodes the Niemann-Pick type C1 protein. Both proteins are known to regulate cholesterol transport from lysosomes to the endoplasmic reticulum46,51. Consistent with its effect on foam cell formation, auranofin downregulated CD209, a marker expressed in foam cells52, as well as TREM2, which encodes a lipid sensing receptor expressed by foamy-macrophages that increases lipid uptake and influences atherosclerosis53,54. Auranofin also induced transcriptional changes that regulate the balance between glycolysis and gluconeogenesis55,56. Genes associated with increased glycolysis (SLC2A5, HK3, TPI1, PDK4)57–60 were downregulated (Fig. 3A), indicating a metabolic reprogramming of foam cells towards a reduced inflammatory state. The anti-inflammatory effect of auranofin was confirmed by the upregulation of NFKBIA, which encodes the NFKB Inhibitor Alpha involved in the inhibition of NF-kappa-B/REL complexes61, as well as IL10RB and IL27RA, which encode the receptor for the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and IL-2762,63 (Fig. 3A). The downregulation of IL6R, IL12RB1 and IL12RB264–66 (Fig. 3A) also demonstrated that auranofin induces an anti-inflammatory transcriptional programs in foam cells. Notably, auranofin also decreased interferon responses associated with atherosclerosis by inhibiting genes involved in the TNF-alpha response (i.e., IFIT1)67 and inflammation (e.g., IFITM1 and MX1)68,69 (Fig. 3A). Additionally, auranofin treatment led to transcriptional changes consistent with upregulation of glutathione and anti-oxidative stress pathways, which have atheroprotective functions70–80 (Fig. 3A). Genes involved in the glutathione pathway, such as NFE2L2 (encoding NFE2 Like BZIP Transcription Factor 2), HMOX1 (encoding Heme Oxygenase 1)72,73,80, and NQO1 (encoding NAD(P)H dehydrogenase [quinone] 1)81, were upregulated by auranofin (Fig. 3A). Auranofin also reduced the expression of genes associated with oxidative stress, such as FOXM1 (encoding Forkhead box protein M1) and SELENOP (encoding selenoprotein)82,83 (Fig. 3A). This anti-oxidant effect of auranofin aligns with known function of auranofin in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis84,85. Auranofin also inhibited the expression of integrins with established pro-atherogenic roles (ITGB5, ITGAL, ITGA7, ITGA4, ITGA9)86–90 (Fig. 3A).

Auranofin had profound effects on the expression of genes involved in the ubiquitination pathway (Fig. 3A). It increased the expression of UBE2R2 (encoding ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2 R2) and UBE2D3 (encoding a member of the E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme family), while decreasing BIRC6, which encodes the Baculoviral IAP repeat-containing 6a protein (BIRC6). Higher BIRC6 expression is linked to plaque vulnerability and increased risk of stroke in atherosclerotic patients91. UBE2R2, UBE2D3 and BIRC6 all interact with UBA1 (Ubiquitin-like modifier-activating enzyme 1), a known target of auranofin92. UBA1 activates ubiquitin, marking proteins for degradation. By interacting with UBA1, auranofin promotes ubiquitination and degradation of misfolded endoplasmic reticulum (ER) proteins during ER-associated degradation and facilitates outer mitochondrial membrane-associated degradation93. This activity can mitigate cellular stress and inflammation, critical factors in atherosclerosis94.

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of biological processes (BP) confirmed the role of auranofin in regulating lipid metabolism, as evidenced by the upregulation of genes involved in lipid and fatty acid metabolic processes in auranofin-treated foam cells (Fig. 3B). Conversely, GO terms including DNA replication, and immune cell activation, cell adhesion and migration were downregulated (Fig. 3C).

Overall, auranofin positively regulates cholesterol metabolism, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory pathways in foam cells, highlighting its potential as an anti-atherosclerotic therapeutic agent.

Auranofin reduces atherosclerotic progression in mice

To further validate the computationally predicted anti-atherosclerotic effect of auranofin and our findings in vitro, we tested the effect of the drug in Apolipoprotein E-deficient mice (Apoe−/−). Six week old mice were randomized to receiving the following diets for 16 weeks: (1) Western diet (WD) (controls), (2) WD containing standard-care of treatment atorvastatin (AT; 10 mg/kg/day), (3) WD containing auranofin (AF; 3 mg/kg/day) and (4) WD containing auranofin plus atorvastatin (AF + AT, 3 + 10 mg/kg/day) (Fig. 4A). These doses were within established therapeutic ranges in mice26,27,95–97. Auranofin, either alone or in combination with atorvastatin, did not alter plasma total cholesterol levels or body weight (Fig. 4B, C). Fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC)-based lipoprotein profiling revealed no differences in cholesterol or triglyceride levels across VLDL, LDL, or HDL fractions between groups (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B), consistent with the reported resistance of Apoe−/− mice fed a WD to statin-induced lipid reduction97–99. OxLDL did not differ between groups (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Despite this, auranofin reduced plaque area in the whole mouse aorta, either when used as single treatment (−54.6%) or in combination with standard-of-care atorvastatin (−67.8%) compared to the WD group (Fig. 4D, E). In particular, the combination of auranofin with atorvastatin induced an antiatherogenic effect that added to the benefit of atorvastatin monotherapy (−54.1% vs atorvastatin) (Fig. 4D, E). Having validated the computationally predicted antiatherogenic effect of auranofin through its effect on foam cell formation, and the additive benefit observed when auranofin was combined with a statin compared to statin alone, we next investigated whether plaque size reduction was associated with reduced plaque macrophage infiltration. Auranofin significantly reduced plaque macrophage content compared with WD controls. Auranofin combined with atorvastatin exerted a greater effect on plaque macrophage content than either monotherapy, reducing it by 57% relative to WD, compared with 49% reduction for auranofin alone and 31% reduction for atorvastatin alone (Fig. 4F, G). Consistent with this enhanced effect, the combination lowered plaque macrophage content by 34% compared with atorvastatin monotherapy (Fig. 4F, G). These findings indicate that auranofin, particularly in combination with atorvastatin, exerts greater anti-atherogenic effects than atorvastatin alone. These data indicate that auranofin enhances the efficacy of standard lipid-lowering therapy by further reducing plaque inflammation and limiting plaque progression.

Since the administration of WD in mice is well-established to induce hepatic steatosis100—a common cardiometabolic comorbidity that increases cardiovascular outcomes—and the evidence that auranofin reduced lipid accumulation in aortic plaques, we hypothesized that auranofin may also reduce lipid accumulation in the liver of Apoe−/− mice fed a WD. Auranofin, whether administered alone or in combination with atorvastatin, reduced liver lipid content compared to untreated mice (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). This effect is consistent with similar reductions in liver lipid content observed in a diet induced obesity mouse model101.

These results indicate that auranofin acts directly in the arterial wall through GRN 42 by reducing foam cell formation in atherosclerosis, while also offering additional benefits in the context of cardiometabolic co-morbidities.

Transcriptional signatures and pathways modulated by auranofin in atherosclerotic aorta

Using RNA-seq we identified biologically relevant gene programs induced by auranofin in the aorta of atherosclerotic mice (Fig. 5A–E, Source Data). GO enrichment analysis of biological process (BP) pathways of DEGs upregulated in whole mouse aorta from mice that underwent single treatment with either auranofin (AF) or atorvastatin (AT) compared to control mice (WD), or combination treatment (AF + AT vs WD) identified shared and unique GO terms (Fig. 5A, B). Auranofin alone upregulated pathways related to lipid metabolic processes (GO:0019216), fatty acid metabolic processes (GO:0019217), and regulation of lipid storage (GO:0010883) (Fig. 5C). The combination treatment of auranofin and atorvastatin upregulated fatty acid metabolic processes (GO:0033559, GO:0051122) (Fig. 5D), which was confirmed in the pathways shared between single treatment with auranofin and combined treatment (GO:0006631) (Fig. 5E). This effect occurred together with the regulation of both triglyceride metabolism (GO:0010889, GO:0006641) and fatty acid oxidation (GO:0019395), whose upregulation is considered to be crucial for the atheroprotective cellular metabolic reprogramming of macrophages towards their anti-inflammatory state102–104 (Fig. 5E).

Overall, these results in vivo corroborated our in vitro findings that auranofin plays a significant role in cellular cholesterol and lipid metabolism. This was confirmed further by cross-species comparison of RNA-seq signatures from human THP-1-derived foam cells and whole aortic tissue from auranofin-treated Apoe−/− mice, which identified five shared upregulated GO biological process pathways: regulation of lipid metabolic process, regulation of fatty acid metabolic process, positive regulation of lipid metabolic process, lipid storage, and immune response-regulating signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. 6). The convergence of four lipid-related GO terms supports a conserved mechanism centered on lipid and fatty acid metabolic reprogramming across species. The immune response pathway was shared but reached substantially greater significance in the mouse model (−log₁₀(q) = 16 vs 2), consistent with auranofin modulating a multicellular immune microenvironment in intact aortic tissue rather than isolated macrophages in vitro.

Auranofin halts atherosclerosis progression in rabbits

Having demonstrated the anti-atherogenic effects of auranofin in a progression model, we aimed to evaluate its efficacy on advanced plaques in a large animal model of atherosclerosis to better mimic the clinical scenario of treating patients with pre-existing atherosclerosis. To achieve this, we used an established rabbit model of atherosclerosis, which develops advanced plaques with pathological characteristics resembling the complexity of human lesions105–110. In this model, the atherosclerotic abdominal aorta can be imaged using 18F fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography–magnetic resonance imaging (18F-FDG PET–MRI), a technique commonly used in clinical vascular research with clinical-grade scanners and protocols111. This approach is suitable for drug testing, as 18FDG uptake in the arterial wall reflects the metabolic activity and content of plaque macrophages in both rabbits and humans108,109,112,113. Thus, 18F-FDG PET–MRI enables the quantification of plaque inflammation in rabbits using imaging modalities directly translatable to humans111.

Briefly, after 8 weeks of WD with 0.3% cholesterol and two double balloon injuries, New Zealand White rabbits (≈2.5 kg) were fed a WD with 0.15% cholesterol for additional 8 weeks to complete atherosclerosis induction22. At 16 weeks, the rabbits underwent a baseline 18F-FDG PET-MRI scan and were then randomized into four groups: (1) WD only (controls); (2) WD with atorvastatin (3 mg/kg/day); (3) WD with auranofin (1 mg/kg/day), and (4) WD with auranofin plus atorvastatin (1/3 mg/kg/day). After 12 weeks of treatment, a follow-up 18F-FDG PET-MRI scan was performed at 28 weeks to evaluate the effects on plaque inflammation and size (Fig. 6A). As expected for this model22,112,113, there were no differences in cholesterol plasma levels and body weight across groups (Fig. 6B, C), as hypercholesterolaemia is driven primarily by the high dietary cholesterol absorption in WD-fed animals combined with low hepatic lipase activity in this species114, which limits lipoprotein remodeling independently of statin treatment.

Auranofin use is associated with reduced cardiovascular risk in patients with rheumatoid arthritis

To provide clinical evidence for our GRN-based repurposing strategy, we conducted a propensity score-matched (PSM) retrospective cohort study among patients with rheumatoid arthritis using the nationwide Veterans Affairs Corporate Data Warehouse (VA-CDW), one of the largest integrated health record systems in the United States115. Patients with a history of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), fewer than 12 weeks of auranofin exposure, or prior treatment with TNF inhibitors were excluded (Supplementary Table 1). After PSM, 92 auranofin-treated patients were compared with 815 controls (Table 1). Baseline characteristics were broadly similar; residual imbalances in hypertension, hyperlipidemia, disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARD) use, and time since rheumatoid arthritis diagnosis (standardised mean difference >0.25; Table 1) were adjusted for as covariates in all models.

Discussion

Our study shows that a gene regulatory network (GRN)-guided drug repurposing strategy can identify therapeutically actionable compounds for complex cardiovascular disease. By integrating the transcriptional signature of GRN 42—an arterial wall network previously implicated as causal for CAD and linked to foam cell formation—with LINCS L1000 connectivity mapping, we identified auranofin as a candidate anti-atherosclerotic therapy. Although originally developed as a disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drug, auranofin has attracted renewed interest for repurposing across inflammatory, metabolic, and neoplastic disorders, making it an attractive candidate for rapid translational evaluation.

Auranofin is an orally available, lipophilic gold compound approved in 198526,27,116–119 for the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis (RA)117,120. Although it was later largely replaced by newer therapies, including methotrexate, and biologics targeting tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and interleukin (IL)-1121–123, more recent clinical studies have renewed interest in its therapeutic potential. In an NIH-sponsored Phase I trial (NCT02089048)124, adverse events were mild, reported in 47% of subjects and self-resolved in all, supporting the view that auranofin remains safe for human use116,118. This has prompted investigations of auranofin in a range of indications, including cancer, parasitic infections, and neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s117. Our study is the first to identify and experimentally validate the anti-atherosclerotic properties of auranofin, supporting its potential as a treatment for CAD124.

Drug repurposing offers major practical advantages over de novo drug discovery, but many repurposed candidates fail because mechanistic rationale and translational validation are insufficiently developed before clinical testing125. A major strength of our work is the alignment between network-level prediction, mechanistic validation and translational readouts. Rather than relying on inferred transcriptional reversal alone125, we tested whether auranofin modulates the cellular and tissue phenotypes linked to GRN 42. The data support a model in which auranofin acts primarily through macrophage-centered mechanisms, limiting foam cell formation and attenuating plaque inflammation independently of systemic lipid lowering. This is particularly relevant in CAD, where residual inflammatory risk persists despite effective cholesterol reduction and remains insufficiently addressed by current therapies.

The mechanistic findings further support the biological relevance of GRN 42 as a therapeutic entry point. Auranofin rescued the phenotype induced by AIP silencing while preserving that induced by PQBP1 silencing, consistent with engagement of downstream network architecture rather than direct transcriptional perturbation of individual key drivers. More broadly, auranofin induced transcriptional programs marked by reduced inflammatory and oxidative stress signaling, together with changes in metabolic pathways associated with a less atherogenic macrophage state. These findings are consistent with prior evidence that auranofin modulates redox-sensitive and macrophage-associated processes, including phagocytosis and lysosomal activity117,122, and position those activities within a network framework directly relevant to foam cell formation and atherosclerosis.

The in vivo data reinforce this interpretation. Across two complementary models, auranofin reduced plaque macrophage burden and limited plaque progression, with additive benefit in combination with atorvastatin. The absence of effects on circulating cholesterol is mechanistically informative, indicating that the anti-atherosclerotic activity of auranofin is not mediated by lipid lowering but instead by direct modulation of plaque biology. In rabbits, the concordance between reduced 18F-FDG uptake and reduced plaque macrophage content by histology is especially important, given the translational value of 18F-FDG PET as a surrogate measure of plaque inflammation112,113,126–130 in both preclinical and clinical studies108,109,112,113. Together, these observations support auranofin as a modulator of vascular inflammation that acts independently of lipid lowering. Considering that 18F-FDG PET imaging modality is a surrogate imaging endpoint of inflammation with features associated to increased risk for CV events106,131–133, our results indicate clinically translatable benefit of auranofin for patients with CAD106,134. The retrospective VA cohort extends these findings to humans. Auranofin exposure was associated with lower incident MACE in RA patients, and this association was preserved in individuals with established cardiovascular risk factors. Although observational, the consistency of the association across analytical frameworks and risk strata provides real-world support for the translational relevance of the preclinical findings. More broadly, our findings support the concept that gene regulatory networks inferred from tissue-specific data from patients with coronary artery disease can serve as a powerful framework for identifying repurposed compounds with clinically meaningful cardiovascular benefit. These data also point to a plausible population for near-term clinical translation. Patients with rheumatoid arthritis, who carry increased cardiovascular risk135, may represent an attractive setting for early-phase evaluation. In particular, those with established coronary artery disease, or more broadly with persistent inflammatory cardiovascular risk despite standard lipid-lowering therapy, may be especially well suited for testing auranofin. As these patients remain at substantial residual risk despite current care, they may be particularly likely to benefit from therapies that target plaque biology and vascular inflammation independently of systemic cholesterol lowering.

The reduction in hepatic lipid accumulation observed in both mouse and rabbit models further suggests that auranofin may confer broader cardiometabolic benefit in the setting of cardiovascular disease, consistent with the close links among hepatic metabolic dysfunction, inflammation and atherosclerosis. Whether these effects extend to metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease will require dedicated study. From a translational perspective, the established safety profile of auranofin124 should inform trial design. Its contraindication in severe uncontrolled hypertension, a frequent comorbidity in coronary artery disease, will require careful patient selection in early-phase clinical studies. However, because hypertension is well controlled in many patients with coronary artery disease, this limitation is unlikely to represent a major barrier to clinical evaluation. Dose translation will require prospective study. As it is typical in exploratory preclinical pharmacology, the doses used here were selected on the basis of prior efficacy studies in animal models95–97,101. Using FDA-recommended body surface area scaling, the mouse dose of 3 mg/kg/day corresponds to a human equivalent dose (HED) of approximately 17 mg/day, and the rabbit dose of 1 mg/kg/day corresponds to an HED of approximately 23 mg/day for a 70 kg adult, both above the approved RA dose of 6–9 mg/day in 1985. However, auranofin has a newly established clinical safety record, and doses up to 21 mg/day have been evaluated in FDA-authorized clinical trials (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT01419691, NCT02089048) and in a more recent phase 1 study124. Notably, prior work suggests that biological activity may be achievable at lower exposures: in a diet-induced obesity model, 1 mg/kg/day in mice, corresponding to a HED of approximately 5.7 mg/day, reduced hepatic lipid accumulation101. Together, these observations suggest that the minimum effective cardiovascular dose may ultimately fall within, or close to, the currently approved clinical range.

Defining the minimum effective dose and therapeutic window for chronic use in atherosclerotic disease will require dedicated dose-finding and early-phase clinical studies. Although the preclinical studies were conducted exclusively in male animals, the retrospective VA cohort included both male and female RA patients, with sex incorporated into the propensity score matching framework. This adjustment supports the broader clinical relevance of our findings. Given the predominantly male composition of the VA population, however, future studies should directly assess the effects of auranofin in clinical studies with more balanced sex representation.

Taken together, these findings identify auranofin as a candidate therapy for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and establish a broadly applicable framework for GRN-based drug repurposing. By integrating causal network biology to connectivity mapping, mechanistic validation, translational imaging, and real-world clinical evidence, this approach moves beyond candidate nomination towards functional and clinical prioritization. Our results provide proof of principle that disease-causal regulatory networks can be used to identify repurposed therapies for complex cardiovascular disease.

Methods

Human studies research

Ethics statement

The study was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board of the VA Boston Healthcare System (Protocol 3240-X) and determined to qualify for Exempt Category 4(iii) secondary research under the 2018 Common Rule. The IRB granted a waiver of informed consent and HIPAA authorization because the project involves only retrospective use of existing EHR and laboratory data, poses no more than minimal risk to privacy, and incorporates appropriate safeguards for confidentiality and handling of identifiers in accordance with VA and HIPAA requirements. The IRB further determined that obtaining individual consent was not practicable for a retrospective study of this scope and that the waiver was necessary to permit the conduct of the research.

Study cohort definition and outcomes

A propensity score–matched retrospective cohort study of patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) was conducted using patient-level data from the US Department of Veterans Affairs Corporate Data Warehouse (VA-CDW) between January 1, 2006, and June 1, 2024. The VA-CDW is a centralized, nationwide EHR database that contains demographics, International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 and ICD-10) diagnoses, procedures, prescription fills, and laboratory data of VA patients. ICD code–based definitions of covariates and outcomes are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Eligible participants were adult VA patients ( ≥ 18 years) with a documented diagnosis of RA, defined by ICD-10 codes M05.x–M06.x as previously described136. Patients with a history of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) or exposure to auranofin prior to RA diagnosis were excluded. To minimize confounding from other DMARDs, patients treated with anti-TNF agents (etanercept, infliximab, adalimumab) before baseline (auranofin initiation) were also excluded. The propensity-score–matched cohort comprised 907 patients (92 auranofin-exposed, 815 matched non-exposed controls); the high-CVD-risk subgroup comprised 629 patients (59 auranofin-exposed, 570 controls). Mean age at baseline was 67.0 (SD 11.2) years in the auranofin group and 69.1 (SD 12.7) years in controls (68.4 [9.2] and 69.6 [12.2] years, respectively, in the high-CVD-risk subgroup). Sex was ascertained from the sex recorded in the VA electronic health record (administratively recorded rather than self-reported at the time of study); gender identity was not analysed. The cohort was predominantly male, consistent with the VA population: 822/907 (90.6%) male overall (auranofin 87/92 [94.6%], control 735/815 [90.2%]) and 568/629 (90.3%) male in the high-CVD-risk subgroup. Sex was included as a covariate in propensity-score estimation and balanced after matching (standardized mean difference 0.166 overall, 0.193 in the subgroup), and was adjusted for in all outcome models. A formal sex-stratified analysis was not performed because the cohort was ~91% male, limiting statistical power for sex-disaggregated effect estimates. The primary outcome was time to first MACE, defined as cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke (Supplementary Table 1). Outcomes occurring within 3 months of baseline were excluded to reduce reverse causality. Auranofin exposure was defined as receipt of a prescription lasting ≥12 weeks (ATC code M01CB01 or RxNorm ID). Covariates included age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, smoking status, alcohol use disorder (AUDIT-C), hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, active malignancy, and autoimmune or inflammatory comorbidities, consistent with prior studies136,137. Exposure to comparator conventional synthetic DMARDs (methotrexate, sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine, leflunomide) was also included as covariates for model adjustments.

Statistical analysis for the human retrospective study

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using a nearest-neighbor approach to compare outcomes between auranofin-exposed and non-exposed patients. Propensity scores were estimated using all covariates as well as time-to-treatment from RA diagnosis (linear and quadratic terms) to identify control patients with comparable disease history. Recursive partitioning (RPART) was applied to further align patients by time since RA diagnosis, with a caliper threshold of 0.125 and a resulting 1:10 matching ratio of treated to control patients. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for time to MACE. Competing risks of death were addressed using censoring and Fine-Gray sub-distribution hazard models. A time-varying framework was implemented, in which exposure, covariates, and outcomes were discretized into quarterly bins beginning at RA diagnosis and ending at outcome, death, or censoring at the end of follow-up. A secondary analysis evaluated whether auranofin exposure was associated with reduced MACE risk in a cohort enriched for cardiovascular risk factors (RA patients with prior diabetes, hypertension, and/or hyperlipidemia). Kaplan–Meier curves, adjusted for residual confounders, were generated to visualize MACE-free survival across treatment groups, and likelihood ratio tests were used for statistical comparison. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated visually and met.

Animal studies

Sex as a biological variable for animal studies

Male animals have been shown to minimize the variability due to hormonal changes in female mice in drug testing and development, which can influence metabolism, vascular inflammation, and other factors determining atherosclerosis development138–141. For this reason, only male mice and rabbits were used in this study; additional studies are required to explore the sex-specific effect of auranofin in atherosclerosis.

Mice and housing conditions

All mouse procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-2016-0032) of the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. Mouse experiments were conducted using 6-week-old Apoe−/− male mice (B6.129P2-Apoetm1Unc/J; Jackson Laboratories) that were housed in a barrier-specific pathogen-free conditions (12-h light–dark cycle conditions; temperature 20–24 °C, 30–70% humidity) in the animal facilities of the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, New York, NY, US. Mice were fed a Western diet (WD, % by kcal: protein, 17.3%, carbohydrate, 43%, fat, 40%; catalog no. D12079B, Research Diet, Inc.), alone or in combination with either 10 mg/kg/day atorvastatin, from Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. PHR1422 (catalog no. D16102901, Research Diet, Inc.), 3 mg/kg/day auranofin, from Cayman Chemical Company, catalog no. 15316 (catalog no. D16102902, Research Diet, Inc.) or auranofin plus atorvastatin (catalog no. D16102903, Research Diet, Inc.) for 16 weeks. Mouse weight was monitored weekly. Our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications142,143.

Rabbits, housing conditions and surgical procedure

All rabbit procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-2016-0032) of the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. Male New Zealand white male rabbits (2.5 kg; Strain Code 052, 3-months old) were purchased from Charles River. All rabbits were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions (12 h light–dark cycle conditions; temperature 20–24 °C, 30–70% humidity) in the animal facilities of the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, New York, NY, US. To induce atherosclerotic progression, we used a combination of a high cholesterol diet (4.7% hydrogenated coconut oil and 0.3% cholesterol-enriched western diet (WD), catalog no. C30255, Research Diet, Inc.) and repeated balloon injury of the aorta by a femoral angioplasty under fluoroscopic guidance, at 2 and 6 weeks after WD initiation. Briefly, one 4-F Fogarty embolectomy catheter (catalog no. 120804 F, Edwards Lifesciences) was inserted through the femoral artery up to the thoracic descending aorta. The catheter and attached manometer can be filled with Iopamidol (ISOVUE-370, Bracco Diagnostics) to adequately track the balloon under fluoroscopic guidance. The balloon was inflated gently to a pressure of 2.0 atm with a manometer. To denude the aorta of endothelial cells, the catheter was deflated and pulled back over the entire length of the aorta down to the iliac bifurcation. This step was repeated three times. After 8 weeks of initiating the 0.3% cholesterol-enriched WD, the rabbits were switched to a 0.15% cholesterol enriched diet (4.7% hydrogenated coconut oil and 0.15% cholesterol-enriched WD, catalog no. C30298Y, Research Diet, Inc.) for eight additional weeks. Animals were then randomized to the following treatments: WD (0.15% cholesterol) alone; WD plus 3 mg/kg/day atorvastatin (catalog no. C17120401, Research Diet, Inc.), WD plus 1 mg/kg/day auranofin (catalog no. C18020201, Research Diet, Inc.) or WD plus auranofin and atorvastatin (catalog no. C18020202, Research Diet, Inc.) for 12 weeks. The drugs used for diet preparation were the same stock used for mouse studies. Rabbit weight was recorded regularly to check for adverse drug effects. The sample size was calculated in reference to what was reported in previous studies109,112,113.

Cell lines

The monocytic human cell line THP-1 (Catalog no. TIB-202) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and it was authenticated at ATCC before purchase by standard short tandem repeat (STR) DNA-typing methodology; it tested negative for mycoplasma. THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (catalog no. 10-040-CV, Corning) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; catalog no. 10082147, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (catalog no. 30-002-CI, Corning) and maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO2.

Identification of small molecules affecting GRN 42

GRN 42 is a regulatory gene network previously identified by our team23. To identify small molecules affecting GRN 42 and to assess the therapeutic/indication pair, we used selected genes positively or negatively correlated with CE accumulation in THP-1 among a sub-network of GRN 42 (key drivers plus genes differentially expressed at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.1 after RNA silencing of the key drivers). Then, we prioritized small molecules for their ability to affect transcription of genes in the GRN 42 sub-network in a direction consistent with reduced or increased cholesterol ester accumulation. These transcriptional signatures were derived from a collection of transcriptome-wide expression profiles of over 20,000 small molecule perturbagens, assayed over a range of cell-line and dosage contexts, totaling over 200,000 individual experimental instances (NIH LINCS Program, http://www.lincsproject.org). We subset our analysis to include all LINCS Level 4 data for the set of perturbagens that were profiled at least once in a THP-1 cell line. This corresponded to data from 136,584 perturbation instances, which were aggregated into 35,397 unique signatures (perturbagens assayed under a given set of dosage, duration, and cell type conditions), representing 371 unique perturbagens. For each perturbation instance, we calculated a Connectivity Score (CS)29, a measure of the transcriptional relationship between one instance and the GRN 42 signature. The significance of each CS was estimated by generating an empirical CS distribution from the GRN 42 signature to 1000 permuted perturbation instances. For each compound, instance level scores were combined across cellular and dosage contexts by averaging the CS scores from the 10 conditions with the highest absolute CS scores. P values were combined using Stouffer’s method. The Benjamini-Hochberg method144 was used to control the FDR. Perturbagens were then ordered according to descending mean CS, with priority given to perturbagens whereby the mean CS included contribution from a THP-1 cell derived experiment.

A positive CS indicates transcriptional concordance with GRN 42 key driver silencing, predicting decreased foam cell formation; a negative CS indicates discordance, predicting the opposite. Key drivers are defined as genes with disproportionately high network influence within a GRN, whose perturbation produces the greatest downstream impact on network activity. GRN 42 key drivers were identified by Bayesian network analysis in Talukdar et al. (2016)23 and selected for the repurposing pipeline based on their significant effect on cholesteryl ester accumulation upon siRNA silencing in THP-1-derived foam cells. Connectivity score inputs were restricted to the GRN 42 sub-network rather than using genome-wide differentially expressed genes derived from key driver silencing. This approach prioritizes genes implicated in foam cell formation and coronary artery disease, enhances signal-to-noise, and limits the multiple testing burden.

Foam cell formation assay, drug treatment and gene silencing

To validate the six top-scoring FDA- approved drugs predicted either to negatively or positively modulate foam cell formation through CE accumulation, we used THP-1-derived macrophages. Macrophage (MP) differentiation was induced with phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate (50 ng/mL, catalog no. P8139, Sigma-Aldrich) for 72 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2, as previously described145. MPs were pre-treated for 24 h with the following: (1) Dimethyl sulfoxide as vehicle (DMSO; <0.1% v/v, catalog no. D8418, Sigma-Aldrich; (2) auranofin (AF; 0.1, 0.5 and 1 µM, catalog no. A6733, Sigma-Aldrich); (3) BX-795 (0.1, 1, 10 µM, catalog no. 4318, Tocris Bioscience); (4) methyl-fasudil (FASU; 0.1, 1, 10 µM, catalog no. CDS021620, Sigma-Aldrich); (5) nicardipine (NIC; 0.1, 1 and 10 µM, catalog no. S4181, Selleckchem); (6) flutamide (FLUT; 0.1, 1, 10 µM, Catalog no. S1908, Selleckchem). Next, CE-loaded foam cells (FCs) were generated by adding acetylated Low Density Lipoprotein (AcLDL, 50 µg/mL; produced in house or from Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. L35354) for 48 h and drug treatment was maintained until the end of the experiment.

To test the effect of the key drivers PQBP1 and AIP on foam cell formation induced by Auranofin decreasing CE accumulation, we silenced PQBP1 and AIP by transfecting THP-1-derived macrophages with a pool of 3 small interfering RNA (siRNA) oligonucleotides for each gene (PQBP1’s siRNA IDs: s19619, s19621, s527195; AIP’s siRNA IDs: s225075, s194978, s17250; Ambion, Thermo Fisher Scientific), using Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A pool of 2 control siRNA oligonucleotides (cat. no. 4390843 and 4390846, Ambion, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as control. The CE accumulation after PQBP1 silencing was quantified in 4 independent experiments (PQBP1) and in 3 independent experiments (AIP).

Lipid extraction, cholesteryl esters quantification, and protein quantification

Lipid extraction from THP-1 cells was performed using the Chloroform:Isopropanol:Nonidet P-40 mixture with 7:11:0.1 ratio (catalog no. C2432, Sigma-Aldrich; catalog no. A416-4, Fisher Scientific; NP-40, catalog no. 85124, Thermo Fisher Scientific, respectively).

The effect of each drug treatment on CE accumulation was measured in cell lysates with the Total Cholesterol and Cholesteryl Ester Colorimetric/Fluorometric Assay kit (catalog no. K603-100, BioVision and catalog no. ab65359, Abcam) following manufacturer’s instructions. CEs were calculated as ng of cholesterol/µg of protein. Protein extraction from cell lysates was performed with the CytoBuster Protein Extraction Reagent (catalog no. 71009-3, Millipore Sigma), and quantification was done with the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (catalog no. 23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific). For all treatments, 3 independent experiments with 3 technical replicates per vehicle/drug were performed for each condition (n = 9); values are reported as mean ± SD. Differences respect to untreated FCs (FCs+DMSO) in CE levels were assessed on raw data by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s adjustment for multiple comparisons. We considered p value < 0.05 to be statistically significant. We used Prism v10.2.3 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA) for all analyses.

RNA extraction, gene silencing quantification, and RNA sequencing analysis of THP-1

Total RNA was isolated from THP-1 cells with the RNeasy Mini Kit (catalog no. 74104; Qiagen), and concentrations determined with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer Instrument, using the Agilent RNA 6000 Nano Kit (catalog no. 5067-1511; Agilent Technologies, Inc). Total RNA extracted (250 ng) was used for poly(A) library preparation and sequencing (NovaSeq 6000 SP 100 Cycle Flow Cell v.1.5; Illumina, Inc).

For gene silencing, 0.5 µg of total RNA was used to synthetize cDNA (cat. no. 4387406, High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA Kit, Thermo Scientific). cDNA amplification was assessed through real-time PCR with 1xTaqMan universal PCR master mix (cat. no. 4440040, Applied Biosystems), using the TaqMan assay technology (PQBP1: Hs00172888_m1; AIP: Hs00610222_m1; GAPDH: Hs03929097_g1, Applied Biosystems). The efficiency of target gene silencing by TaqMan analyses was affirmed for up to 72 h. Data were analyzed using the comparative Ct method.

For RNA-seq data analysis, R software (v4.1.2)146 was used. Quality control was performed using FastQC v.0.11.7 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/), a final report was generated for each FASTQ file to assess general sequencing quality. Reads were mapped to the human genome (GRCh38, Gencode version 24) and assigned to gene features using STAR (v2.7.7a)147. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the R package DESeq2 (v1.34.0)148. Genes with a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p value < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. For hierarchical clustering analysis, normalized counts were Z-scored across samples and plotted using Pheatmap package, v1.0.12 in R. GO BP over-representation analysis was performed with enrichGO() from the R package ClusterProfiler (v4.2.2)149. GO terms with a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p value < 0.05 were considered significant.

The Rank-Rank Hypergeometric Overlap (RRHO) approach44,45 was used to evaluate degree of overlap of gene signatures between the RNA-seq analysis and the microarray datasets from THP-1 cells. Gene lists were ranked and signed according to their degree of differential expression (sign(logFC) * -log10(adj-p value)) for each dataset. The analysis was performed using the default settings. p-values were displayed on quadrant plots. Significant changes were defined as those with a nominal p value cutoff of <0.05.

Plasma total cholesterol analysis from mouse blood

For plasma total cholesterol analysis, venous blood was collected from the submandibular vein at baseline (0 weeks), at 8 weeks, and at 16 weeks by cardiac puncture. Whole blood was centrifuged at 1699 × g at 4 °C and plasma was stored at −80 °C. Plasma total cholesterol levels were determined using the Cholesterol E Kit (catalog no. 999-02601; FUJIFILM Wako Diagnostics). Statistical differences were checked with two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s correction for multiple comparisons. Normal distribution of data was confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (Prism, v10.2.3, GraphPad, La Jolla, CA).

En face analysis of the aorta and immunohistochemistry of the aortic root from mice

After 16 weeks of treatment, mice were euthanized by inhalation with isoflurane (5%), and perfusion of the heart and aorta was performed with 20 mL of PBS by using a 23-gauge needle through the apex of the left ventricle. The entire tissue from heart to iliac bifurcation was dissected with small spring scissors. The heart was separated from the aorta, and it was sectioned with a razor blade perpendicular to the axis of the aortic root in the upper part of the heart. For en face analysis, the entire length of the aorta was dissected, perivascular fat carefully removed, and put in 10% formalin for 24 h at 4 °C. The entire aorta was then cut open longitudinally and pinned on a 7% agarose (catalog no. 17852, Thermo Fisher Scientific) plate dish, and any residual fat from aorta dissection was removed. Oil Red O stock solution (1 g Oil Red O, catalog no. O0625, Sigma-Aldrich + 200 mL isopropyl alcohol, catalog no. A416-500, Fisher Scientific) was prepared and a 3:2 (stock solution: distilled water) mix was filtered at room temperature and added to the aortas at 37 °C for 30 min, after washing in 60% isopropyl alcohol for 5 min. Then, aortas were washed in 60% isopropyl alcohol solution once and in distilled water twice. Images were taken using the Olympus MVX10 Macroview Stereo Fluorescence Microscope (EVIDENT).

For immunohistological (IHC) analysis, the heart was separated from the aorta, and then it was sectioned with a razor blade perpendicular to the axis of the aortic root in the upper part of the heart. The bottom part of the heart and the aortic root were harvested and stored in 10% formalin (catalog no. 9990244, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 24 h, then 20% sucrose (catalog no. S5-3, Fisher Scientific) for the next 24 h and they were embedded in optimum cutting temperature (O.C.T., catalog no. 23-730-571, Fisher Scientific) before being frozen at −80 °C. For staining, 5 µm thick serial sections were cut using the Leica CM3050 S Research Cryostat (Leica Biosystems). Sections were stained for macrophages (Rat anti-mouse CD68, MCA1957, dilution 1:25, Bio-Rad). Briefly, sections were fixed in cold acetone (catalog no. HC-300, Fisher Scientific) at room temperature (RT) for 10 min and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 0.03% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, catalog no. H325-100, Fisher Scientific), at room temperature for 10 min, before blocking with 5% BSA (catalog no. 001-000-162, Jackson ImmunoResearch) and primary antibody incubation, overnight at 4 °C. Incubation with secondary antibody (anti-rat IgG-HRP, catalog no. STAR72) was done for 1 h at room temperature. Peroxidase activity was checked using 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) tablets (D4293, Sigma-Aldrich). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (catalog no. 3530-32; Ricca Chemical), de-stained with 1% acid alcohol, blued with 0.03% ammonia water, and dehydrated with serial immersions in ethanol (catalog no. BP2818-100, Fisher Scientific) for 3 min each (95–95–100–100%). Finally, sections were transparentized using Histoclear (catalog no. 50-899-90150, Fisher Scientific) and mounted using Permount mounting medium (catalog no. SP15-100, Fisher Scientific). Images were taken with the BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope (Keyence Corporation). Both whole aorta plaque lesion area and aortic root’s macrophage content (3–4 sections/animal) were quantified in a blinded manner as percentage of total area using the BZ-X800 Analyzer software (v.1.1.2.4) and the BZ-H4C Hybrid Cell Count Application (Keyence Corporation). Following Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov normal distribution tests, statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test.

RNA-sequencing analysis of mouse whole aorta

The aortas used for RNA-sequencing analysis were stored at 16 weeks in RNA later (catalog no. AM7021, Invitrogen) for 24 h at 4 °C and frozen at -80 °C after liquid removal. For RNA extraction, tissue homogenization was done with the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec) in QIAzol Lysis Reagent (catalog no. 79306, Qiagen), followed by chloroform/isopropanol precipitation, and clean-up of the samples using the RNAeasy Mini Kit (catalog no. 74106, Qiagen), according to manufacturer’s instructions. Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer instrument and the Agilent RNA 6000 Nano Kit (catalog no. 5067–1511; Agilent Technologies, Inc) were used to determine sample quality and quantity. RNA (250 ng) was used as input for poly(A) library construction, and RNA-sequencing of treated groups vs WD was performed using the BGISEQ-500 instrument and the DNBseq platform (BGI Americas Corporation).

The mouse RNA-seq reads were processed following the same steps used for the human data with the mouse genome (GRCm39, Ensembl-109). Genes with a p value < 0.05 and a log2(fold-change) > 1 or <−1 were considered differentially expressed. GO Pathway over-representation analysis was performed as described above using a subset of GO biological process terms. GO terms GO:0006955, GO:0010876, and GO:0006629 and all their respective child terms were used. GO pathways were selected using GOBPOFFSPRING from the R package GO.db (v3.14.0). Semantic similarity between GO terms was calculated using calculateSimMatrix() with method = “Rel” from the rrvgo R package (v1.6.0)150. Parent terms were determined using reduceSimMatrix() from rrvgo.

ORO staining of mouse liver sections

At 16 weeks, liver from mice was kept at 4 °C in 10% formalin for 24 h, 20% glucose for additional 24 h, and then frozen at −80 °C after embedding in O.C.T. media. Oil Red O (ORO) liver staining was performed using three to four 10 µm-sections per animal with Oil Red O stock solution (300 mg powder, catalog no. O0625-25G, Sigma-Aldrich in 100 mL isopropanol, catalog no. P355-1, Fisher Scientific) diluted 3:2 with distilled water at room temperature for 15 min. Counterstaining was done using Gill’s Hematoxylin (catalog no. GHS132, Sigma-Aldrich), and slide mounting was done using an aqueous mounting media (Fluoromount-G, catalog no. 00-4958-02, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For image analysis, three representative fields per section (20X) were acquired with the Keyence BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope, and quantitative analysis of percentage of positive area over total area was performed with the HALO image Analysis Software (Indica Labs, v3.6.4134.396), using the Area Quantification module (v2.4.3). Results are shown as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test across all groups (Prism, v10.2.3, GraphPad, La Jolla, CA).

Lipoproteins analysis from mouse plasma

Plasma lipoproteins were isolated using fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC) with two Superose 6 10/300 GL columns (Cytiva, catalog no. 17517201) on a Shimadzu LC instrument (version 1.25), comprised of five components: degassing unit (catalog no. DGU-20A5R), auto sampler (catalog no. SIL-20A HT), communications bus module (catalog no. CBM-20A), diode array detector (catalog no. SPD-M20A), and fraction collector (catalog no. FRC-10A). A 100 µl of pooled plasma was injected for each run at a flow rate of 0.400 mL collected into 80 fractions. Total cholesterol and triglyceride concentrations in plasma and in FPLC-fractionated plasma were measured using colorimetric assays (Wako Diagnostics, catalog no. 999-02601, catalog no. 992-02892, catalog no. 998-02992).

OxLDL measurement in plasma from mice

OxLDL (Oxidized Low-Density Lipoprotein) particles were measured in mouse plasma by ELISA, using (cat. no. #E-EL-M0066, Elabscience, Wuhan, P. R. China) according to manufacturer’s instruction.

Plasma cholesterol from rabbits

For plasma total cholesterol analysis, venous blood was collected at baseline (0 weeks) and at 16 weeks. At 28 weeks, cardiac puncture was performed with an 18-gauge needle in the left ventricle, blood was centrifuged at 1699 × g at 4 °C and plasma was stored at −80 °C. Total cholesterol levels were measured using the Cholesterol E Kit (catalog no. 999-02601; FUJIFILM Wako Diagnostics). Normal distribution of the data was confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s correction for multiple comparisons (Prism, v10.2.3, GraphPad, La Jolla, CA).

ORO staining and immuno-histological analysis of atherosclerotic rabbit aorta

At 28 weeks, rabbits were euthanized with Euthasol (100 mg/kg, catalog no. 078059296, Patterson Veterinary Supply, Inc.), and aorta perfusion was performed using 1 L of 0.9% Sodium Chloride solution (catalog no. 2B1324X, Baxter). Rabbit abdominal aorta was dissected and sectioned in eight pieces and put into 10% formalin for 24 h at 4 °C, 20% sucrose for the next 24 h and finally frozen at −80 °C in OCT medium. 5 µm-thick sections were cut using the Leica CM3050 S Research Cryostat (Leica Biosystems).

Three to four sections/animal were acetone-fixed (catalog no. HC-300; Fisher Scientific), incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (catalog no. H325-100; Fisher Scientific) at RT for 20 min and blocked with 3% BSA (catalog no. 001-000-162; Jackson ImmunoResearch) plus 3% goat serum (catalog no. X0907; Agilent Dako). Macrophages detection was performed using an anti-RAM11 mouse monoclonal antibody (dilution 1:50; catalog no. M0633; Agilent Dako) for 2 h at 37 °C, and an anti-mouse secondary antibody (catalog no. HK335-9M; BioGenex) for 15 min at 37 °C. Streptavidin peroxidase (catalog no. HK330-9K; BioGenex) was incubated for 10 min at 37 °C, and peroxidase activity was detected using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (catalog no. D3939; Sigma-Aldrich). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (catalog no. 3530-32; Ricca Chemical).

For ORO staining, aorta sections (3–4/animal) were stained with Oil Red O stock solution (1 g powder in 200 mL isopropanol) diluted 3:2 with distilled water at 37 °C for 30 min and washed in 60% isopropanol solution once and in distilled water twice. Images were taken with the BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope (Keyence Corporation), and quantification of positively stained areas was performed with the BZ-X800 Analyzer software (v.1.1.2.4) and the BZ-H4C Hybrid Cell Count Application (Keyence Corporation). Both the macrophages content and the lesion size were quantified as a percentage of total area in a blinded manner. Statistical analysis was performed by one-way ANOVA with Fisher’s LSD test across all groups (macrophage content) or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test across all groups (lesion size) using Prism, v10.2.3, GraphPad, La Jolla, CA.

ORO staining of rabbit liver sections

At 28 weeks, liver from rabbits was kept at 4 °C in 10% formalin for 24 h, 20% glucose for additional 24 h, and then frozen at −80 °C after embedding in O.C.T. media. Oil Red O (ORO) liver staining was performed as described above for the mouse study. Images were acquired with the Keyence BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope, three representative fields per section (20X). Percentage of positive area over total area was performed using the HALO Image Analysis Software (Indica Labs, v3.6.4134.396), with the Area Quantification module (v2.4.3). Results are shown as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test across all groups (Prism, v10.2.3, GraphPad, La Jolla, CA).

18FDG PET-MRI acquisition on atherosclerotic rabbits

For Positron Emission Tomography (PET) acquisition, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) was used as radiotracer to measure atherosclerotic plaque inflammation. Rabbits were fasted for 4 h prior to radiotracer injection to avoid interference with 18F-FDG uptake. 5 mCi of 18F-FDG was injected through a marginal ear vein catheter 3 h prior to imaging, to maximize the contrast between the plaque and background activity. The exact amount of 18F-FDG that has been injected, as well as the weight of the animal, was noted, as this is essential for subsequent image analysis. Rabbits were scanned at two different time points: at 16 weeks (baseline) and after 12 weeks of WD −/+ drug-medicated diet (28 weeks). Rabbits were imaged supine under general anesthesia (Ketamine 20 mg/kg plus Xylazine 5 mg/kg, Covetrus) and under isofluorane (1.5%) inhalation during the scan. The bladder was emptied with a catheter before imaging to avoid FDG accumulation in the bladder and impairment in the analysis of FDG uptake in the aorta.

For PET-MRI acquisition, the rabbits were scanned on a clinical 3 Tesla Biograph mMR (Siemens Medical Solutions USA, Inc.), with a flexible matrix coil on the abdomen of the animal, positioned in a headfirst-supine direction. Vital parameters were monitored.

After scout scans, the PET scan was initiated and acquired simultaneously with bright-blood time-of-flight (TOF) non-contrast enhanced angiography from the left renal artery to the iliac bifurcation, for slice planning of subsequent scans. Imaging parameters were the following: number of slices, 200; repetition time (TR), 23 ms; echo time (TE), 2.8 ms; excitation flip angle, 20 degrees; slice thickness, 1 mm; spatial resolution, 0.35 × 0.35 mm2 (interpolated). For vessel wall delineation, a black blood 3D T2 weighted Sampling Perfection with Application optimized Contrasts using different flip angle Evolution (SPACE) sequence was used. Imaging parameters were the following: TR, 1600 ms; TE, 113 ms; flip angle, 90 degrees; echo train length (ETL), 83; acquired spatial resolution, 0.63 × 0.63 × 0.63 mm3; and no interpolation was used.

Images were analyzed blindly, using OsiriX v5.6 software (Pixmeo). For PET, images were fused with TOF bright-blood MRI angiography and, from the left renal artery to the iliac bifurcation, circular regions of interest (ROI) were drawn around the aorta. Standard windowing of the PET signal was used, based on evaluation of the skeletal muscle. For each slice, the aortic ROI maximum standardized uptake values (SUV) were averaged across the entire aorta, as previously done22,112,130,151,152.

For MRI, slice-by-slice inner and outer vessel walls were drawn on three-dimensional T2-weighted SPACE images. The difference between outer and inner wall area was considered to be vessel wall area (mm2), which was averaged from below the left renal artery down to the iliac bifurcation109.

Differences were evaluated using one-way ANOVA with Fisher’s LSD test vs WD (for PET analysis) or one-way ANOVA with Fisher’s LSD test across all groups (for MRI analysis). For all tests, p values < 0.05 represent statistical significance. Statistical analyses were performed with Prism, v10.2.3 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA).

Quantification and statistical analysis

The statistical tests used for experiments can be found in the figure legends. All data are presented as mean ± SD, unless otherwise specified. All statistical analyses were conducted in Prism v10.2.3 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA) or using R software version 4.1.2. We used one-way and two-way ANOVAs to compare means where appropriate. Our primary threshold for statistical significance was p value < 0.05. Analysis of histology, immunohistochemistry, and PET-MRI was performed by operators blinded to study group. Sample size estimation was conducted based on previous references, as explained in the methods section for each assay used in this manuscript.
