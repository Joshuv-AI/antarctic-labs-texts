Citrate accumulation during regional citrate anticoagulation for continuous renal replacement therapy: a narrative review
Authors: Yanyu Chen, Jia Zhang, Zhongbin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) has been increasingly adopted as a preferred anticoagulation strategy in continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). However, Citrate accumulation during RCA-CRRT represents a major clinical challenge. This review provides an overview of the mechanisms underlying citrate accumulation, its diagnostic criteria, and the primary factors contributing to citrate accumulation, including excessive citrate infusion dose, insufficient citrate loss across the filter, impairment of citrate metabolism. In addition, the clinical consequences of citrate accumulation and current management strategies are summarized, aiming to provide insights for optimizing the safety and efficacy of RCA-CRRT.Keywords: Regional citrate anticoagulation, continuous renal replacement therapy, citrate accumulation, review

Introduction

The 2012 KDIGO guidelines recommended citrate anticoagulation as the preferred anticoagulant for Continuous Renal Replacement Therapy (CRRT) [1]. However, clinical practice has revealed that Regional citrate anticoagulation continuous renal replacement therapy(RCA-CRRT) can lead to complications [2], including citrate accumulation, alkalosis, and electrolyte disturbances. Alkalosis can be corrected by increasing the dose of replacement fluid (or dialysate) or reducing the sodium bicarbonate administration rate [3], while electrolyte imbalances may be addressed by adjusting electrolyte supplementation (e.g., calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, potassium) [4]. In contrast, the accumulation of citrate remains a persistent clinical challenge, which limits the broader application of citrate anticoagulation in CRRT [5]. Currently, the summaries of citrate anticoagulation mainly consist of systematic reviews and meta-analyses on the safety of RCA-CRRT in patients with liver failure [6–8]. No narrative reviews specifically focusing on the complications of citrate accumulation associated with citrate anticoagulation have been identified. Therefore, this study will review the accumulation of citrate in CRRT from the aspects of mechanisms, diagnostic criteria, influencing factors, and management strategies, laying a theoretical foundation for exploring the reduction of citrate accumulation incidence.

Mechanisms of citrate accumulation

Sodium citrate is a small-molecular anticoagulant substance with the molecular formula Na3C6H5O7. It is weakly alkaline and can chelate calcium ions (coagulation factor IV) involved in the body’s intrinsic and extrinsic coagulation pathways [9]. The anticoagulation mechanism of sodium citrate is as follows: Sodium citrate is pumped into the arterial limb of the circuit, where it chelates with calcium ions in the blood to form a small-molecular chelate compound, calcium-citrate, with a molecular weight of 298 Da [9]. This reduces the calcium ion concentration in the CRRT circuit, thereby interrupting the coagulation cascade and achieving anticoagulation in the CRRT circuit. Part of the chelate compound, calcium-citrate, is lost into the waste fluid through the filter [10], while the remaining part enters the body through the venous limb of the circuit. Citrate is metabolized and generates bicarbonate equivalents through the tricarboxylic acid cycle in the liver, muscles, and kidneys, while chelated calcium is released. Calcium itself is not metabolized [3]. Calcium-containing solutions are pumped into the venous limb of the circuit to supplement the calcium ions lost through the filter during CRRT, to maintain stable calcium ion levels in the patient’s body [11]. Citrate accumulation occurs when the patient’s metabolic capacity is reduced, leading to persistent retention of circulating citrate–calcium complexes that cannot be adequately metabolized and cleared. By contrast, net citrate overload arises when the rate of citrate administration exceeds the rate of extracorporeal clearance, while the patient’s endogenous citrate metabolism remains functionally normal [3,12].

Diagnosis of citrate accumulation

Direct citrate measurement

Measurement of the citrate concentration in the patient’s blood represents the most direct approach for the diagnosis of citrate accumulation. However, most medical institutions are unable to perform this technique, and it is rarely used in clinical practice. In a multicenter study conducted by Harmony H.M. To et al. in Hong Kong in 2023, serum citrate concentrations were compared between the citrate accumulation (CA) group and the non-CA group. The median serum citrate level was significantly higher in the CA group than in the non-CA group [0.93 mmol/L (IQR 0.81–1.16) vs. 0.37 mmol/L (IQR 0.26–0.57), p < 0.001]. At a cutoff of 0.85 mmol/L, serum citrate exhibited a sensitivity of 0.77 and a specificity of 0.96 for diagnosing CA (AUROC 0.90, p < 0.001). Additionally, the 2-h and 6-h serum citrate levels showed good predictive performance for subsequent CA development, with AUROC values of 0.86 (cutoff 0.34 mmol/L) and 0.83 (cutoff 0.63 mmol/L), respectively (p < 0.001 for both) [13].

Surrogate biochemical criteria

Although the direct measurement of blood citrate concentration is not available in most healthcare institutions, clinicians may still assess citrate concentration indirectly using surrogate biochemical parameters. The total-to-ionized calcium ratio (tCa/iCa) correlates with the serum citrate concentration [14]. A ratio > 2.25 signifies an elevated risk of citrate accumulation, and a ratio ≥ 2.5 is diagnostic of overt citrate accumulation [15]. This method has a predictive sensitivity of 89% and a specificity of 100%. In this study, 32 out of 1070 patients (2.99%) developed citrate accumulation accompanied by hyperlactatemia. In the present study, 32 out of 1,070 patients (2.99%) developed citrate accumulation accompanied by hyperlactatemia [15]. Hetzel et al. reported on the correlation between citrate and the total calcium tCa/iCa ratio [16], finding a strong correlation between the two (R = 0.789, p < 0.001), with a cutoff value of 2.5. Khawaja et al. found albumin correction may increase case detection in hypoalbuminemic patients and may improve mortality risk stratification [17]. The corrected tCa/iCa ratio is calculated as follows: CorrectedRatio=(tCa + 0.02×(40 − albumin))/iCa [18]. However, correction may mechanically increase the calculated ratio and potentially generate false-positive classifications. Therefore, further studies are needed to explore the recommended threshold, validation population, and potential limitations of the albumin-corrected total-to-ionized calcium ratio for the diagnosis of citrate accumulation.

Clinical warning signs

While the tCa/iCa can be used to monitor the citrate concentration in the patient’s blood, this method is limited by its inability to facilitate rapid and dynamic assessment of the tCa/iCa ratio. Beyond changes in the ratio itself, clinicians should also take into account concurrent variations in clinical warning signs. These variations include: (1) progressively worsening hypocalcemia; (2) a gradual increase in calcium supplementation; and (3) metabolic acidosis [15]. However, clinical healthcare providers can use the presence of hypocalcemia and increased calcium supplementation as reminders to monitor the tCa/iCa ratio, enabling early diagnosis of citrate accumulation.

Risk-prediction and early-warning markers

Citrate accumulation may also be evaluated using predictive markers, including the lactate kinetics, lactate level, and pH value. Khadzhynov et al. reported the relationship between hyperlactatemia and citrate accumulation in 2017, discovering that higher lactate levels at the initiation of treatment were associated with a higher incidence of citrate accumulation [19]. Furthermore, the lactate kinetics every 12 h could predict citrate accumulation, with higher predictive performance (AUC = 0.839; 95% CI, 0.751–0.927). Recently, Bidar et al. [20] found that lactate 10.6 mmol/L and pH 7.14 can predict citrate accumulation, with Receiver Operating Characteristic curves of 0.75 and 0.74, respectively. Therefore, for hyperlactatemia patients undergoing RCA-CRRT treatment, close monitoring of lactate levels and pH is essential, as these markers may identify high-risk patients who require intensified biochemical and calcium monitoring, and lactate kinetics may provide more valuable information than a single baseline value.

The primary factors influencing citrate accumulation

Excessive citrate infusion dose

Excessive CRRT blood flow rate

The blood flow rate for RCA-CRRT is typically 100–150 mL/min [21], which is relatively low. To maintain the post-filter ionized calcium concentration at 0.2–0.4 mmol/L, the sodium citrate concentration in the CRRT circuit blood should be 3.0–5.0 mmol/L. Accordingly, a higher blood flow rate requires a proportionally higher citrate infusion rate [22], and consequently, greater sodium citrate administration increases the risk of citrate accumulation. High blood flow rate may affect the degree of chelation between citrate and calcium ions [23]. The higher the blood flow rate, the shorter the contact time between citrate and calcium ions, resulting in poorer chelation [22]. Therefore, in RCA-CRRT, to achieve the target post-filter calcium level, one cannot simply consider increasing the citrate dosage while ignoring the impact of blood flow rate.

Limitations of the citrate-calcium adjustment protocol

The core principle of the citrate–calcium adjustment protocol is to dynamically titrate sodium citrate and calcium infusion based on the systemic ionized calcium concentration and the post‑filter ionized calcium level [22]. However, a notable limitation of this protocol is that it does not adequately account for situations in which systemic hypocalcemia coexists with an elevated post‑filter ionized calcium level, nor does it consider the regulatory effects of parathyroid hormone(PTH) on hypocalcemia [24]. In such cases, guideline‑based management would recommend simultaneous increases in both citrate and calcium infusion. Yet, the increased citrate dose is only sufficient to maintain the current calcium level, while calcium is being slowly raised via exogenous supplementation and endogenous hormonal regulation. This may result in prolonged instability of systemic calcium, delayed achievement of post‑filter targets, and continued escalation of citrate dosing. Whether rapid calcium administration could expedite calcium equilibrium, reduce titration time and PTH interference, and ultimately lower citrate requirements warrants prospective interventional investigation.

Excessive requirements for the target value of calcium ions in the filter

During RCA-CRRT, the conventional target range for post-filter ionized calcium is 0.2–0.4 mmol/L [25]. Nevertheless, in 2021, Wang et al. [26] conducted a retrospective study that stratified patients based on post-filter ionized calcium levels (≤0.45 mmol/L, 0.45–0.55 mmol/L, and ≥0.55 mmol/L). They found no significant difference in RCA-CRRT circuits among the groups (p > 0.05). Similarly, in 2023, Assefi et al. [27] performed a prospective cohort study comparing a low post-filter ionized calcium group (0.25–0.35 mmol/L) with a high group (0.35–0.50 mmol/L). They reported no significant difference in circuit lifespan between the two groups. Therefore, in patients who are expected to receive CRRT for a short duration or who have underlying coagulopathy, clinicians may opt for a broader post-filter ionized calcium target range. An excessively stringent requirement for post-filter ionized calcium levels would demand a higher citrate infusion rate, thereby potentially elevating the risk of citrate accumulation.

Insufficient citrate loss across the filter

During RCA-CRRT, 30%–70% of the citrate administered is cleared by the filter, with the remainder entering the patient’s systemic compartment [28]. At identical treatment doses, the filter clearance of citrate varies across different CRRT modalities. Müller et al. demonstrated that, under equivalent dosing conditions, the Continuous Veno-Venous Hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF) modality was associated with a significantly lower incidence of citrate accumulation compared with Continuous Veno-Venous Hemodialysis (CVVHD) (OR = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.01–0.61; p = 0.027) [29]. This observation was subsequently confirmed by Szamosfalvi et al. [14]. Accordingly, for patients at elevated risk of citrate accumulation, CVVHDF may represent a preferred option over CVVHD.

Impairment of citrate metabolism

Liver failure

Citrate metabolism occurs primarily via the tricarboxylic acid cycle in the liver [30]. Impairment of liver function reduces citrate metabolic capacity and increases the risk of citrate accumulation [31,32]. However, Slowinski et al. stratified patients with liver failure into three groups based on total bilirubin levels (≤2 mg/dl, >2 to ≤7 mg/dl, LF >7 mg/dl)—normal liver function, mild liver failure, and severe liver failure—and found no statistically significant difference in the incidence of citrate accumulation among the three groups [33]. Furthermore, a systematic review has indicated that RCA is safe and feasible in patients with liver failure [6,7]. In contrast, Lin et al. reported that patients with liver failure complicated by hyperlactatemia were at increased risk of citrate accumulation [34], and Bai et al. found that liver failure with concurrent bleeding disorders also carried a high risk [35]. Therefore, in patients with liver failure who also present with hyperlactatemia or bleeding diathesis, the risk of citrate accumulation during RCA-CRRT is relatively elevated. Clinicians should implement individualized anticoagulation strategies in such populations to mitigate the risk of citrate accumulation.

Shock

Shock, a common condition in critically ill patients, directly causes tissue hypoperfusion—a pathological state characterized by reduced blood supply that fails to meet metabolic demands, leading to cellular hypoxia, metabolic derangements, and organ dysfunction [36,37]. Objective indicators of hypoperfusion include lactate levels, vasopressor scores, and dynamic changes in blood pressure [38]. During RCA-CRRT, citrate metabolism proceeds via the aerobic tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle; therefore, tissue hypoperfusion compromises oxygen delivery, impairs citrate metabolism, and increases the risk of citrate accumulation. Higher lactate levels were associated with a higher risk of citrate accumulation, and the risk increased as lactate rose [39,40]. Similarly, Khadzhynov et al. observed that the risk of citrate accumulation increased with rising lactate and decreased when lactate declined [19]. Thus, clinicians may assess the risk of citrate accumulation in patients receiving RCA-CRRT by monitoring changes in tissue perfusion status.

Management strategies of citrate accumulation

Citrate overload

For patients with citrate overload, the initial management strategy includes increasing the replacement fluid or dialysate flow rate to augment effluent flow, thereby enhancing citrate loss across the filter, while concurrently reducing the blood flow rate to limit citrate entry into the patient’s systemic circulation [41]. If citrate overload does not improve substantially and the dialysis equipment permits, conversion from Continuous Veno-Venous Hemofiltration(CVVH) or CVVHD to CVVHDF modality may be considered to further increase citrate clearance via the filter [22].

Citrate accumulation

For patients with citrate accumulation, the underlying cause should first be identified, including tissue hypoperfusion, liver failure, or reduced filter efficiency [42]. Concurrent measures should be taken to reduce citrate delivery into the patient’s systemic circulation, including increasing effluent flow and decreasing blood flow rate [41]. If the dialysis equipment permits, conversion from CVVH or CVVHD to CVVHDF may also be considered [22]. For cases attributable to tissue hypoperfusion, aggressive correction of perfusion status should be implemented to address the cause of citrate accumulation. If citrate accumulation does not improve significantly despite these interventions, citrate anticoagulation may be temporarily suspended, and an alternative anticoagulation strategy—such as anticoagulation-free, low-molecular-weight heparin, or nafamostat mesilate—should be selected based on the patient’s coagulation status [22].

Conclusion

Citrate accumulation is a clinically relevant complication, particularly in high-risk populations such as patients with liver failure or shock. Diagnosis relies primarily on indirect markers such as tCa/iCa ratio ≥ 2.5, a gradual increase in calcium supplementation, and progressively worsening hypocalcmia. The primary factors contributing to citrate accumulation, including excessive citrate infusion dose, insufficient citrate loss across the filter, impairment of citrate metabolism. Once the diagnosis is established, clinicians should identify the underlying causes of citrate accumulation and initiate appropriate interventions, implementing an individualized treatment plan. Future research should focus on strategies to optimize citrate dosing, enhance extracorporeal clearance, and develop reliable real-time monitoring tools for early detection.
