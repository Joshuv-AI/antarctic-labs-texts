RBF-assisted surrogate modeling and machine learning for mechanical property prediction in friction stir additive manufacturing: Application to dissimilar AA6061/AA7075 aluminum alloys.
Authors: Krishnan Venkatachalam, Senthil Kumaran Selvaraj, Giriraj Mannayee, Kalyanaraman Pattabiraman, Sundaramali G
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research develops a surrogate-assisted framework for exploring mechanical property relationships in friction stir additive manufacturing (FSAM) of dissimilar aluminum alloys (AA6061/AA7075) under data-scarcity conditions. Using a limited experimental dataset of 9 data points, Radial Basis Function (RBF) interpolation generates 882 synthetic samples covering tool rotation speed (1000–1200 rpm), traverse speed (30–40 mm/min), and tilt angle (1.0–2.2°). Gaussian Process Regression (GPR), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Bayesian Ridge Regression models are trained to predict ultimate tensile strength (UTS) and Vickers hardness (HV) for both 6061-over-7075 and 7075-over-6061 material stacking configurations. GPR indicates performance with mean absolute errors of 2.34–8.67 MPa for UTS and comparable accuracy for hardness, achieving R² > 0.85 across synthetic samples. SHAP-based analysis of the surrogate-trained models indicates that tool rotation speed exerts the strongest influence within the learned surrogate response surfaces, followed by traverse speed and tilt angle, with relative contributions varying across outputs. Critically, this near zero error is obtained on synthetic samples derived from RBF interpolation of the original 9 measurements, not on independent experimental data. The models effectively learn and reproduce the RBF surrogate response surface within the explored design space. The framework successfully indicates a methodology for parameter space exploration under data scarcity; however, it illustrates conceptual parameter selection workflows based on surrogate-assisted trend exploration. Independent experimental validation is required before any practical optimization. The work provides a template applicable to other data-scarce manufacturing domains, with the essential caveat that external experimental validation is a prerequisite to practical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42608-7.Keywords: Friction Stir Additive Manufacturing, Surrogate Modeling, Gaussian Process Regression, Synthetic Data Generation, Radial Basis Functions, SHAP, Machine Learning, Data Scarcity, Aluminum AlloysSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Friction Stir Additive Manufacturing is a systematic solid-state additive manufacturing technology that presents several benefits over traditional fusion-based processes, particularly for manufacturing dissimilar aluminum alloy structures1,2,17,26,29. Friction Stir Additive Manufacturing (FSAM) is synonymous with Additive Friction Stir Deposition (AFSD) and refers to the layered deposition variant of friction stir welding technology; in this work, FSAM is used as the primary terminology. FSAM is based on Friction Stir Welding (FSW) technology and has the capability of producing large-area, multi-layered components through sequential layered deposition with the same principles applied in FSW and therefore does not involve melting and solidification defects that are typically present in additive-manufacturing processes that rely upon melting for material consolidation2,3,5,17,26.

FSAM has received significant interest from the automotive industries due to its ability to produce components with mechanical properties, reduced residual stress, microstructural features, improved production rates, and material utilization over competing processes1,5,6,26,28. In industrial settings, real-scale FSAM experiments face limitations of machine costs, cycle time, and safety. Therefore, this paper presents a surrogate-assisted predictive framework using synthetic data for baseline predictive capability and the integration of real data from future FSAM production to aid stakeholder trust4,19,20.

The essential principle of FSAM involves using a rotating tool that generates sufficient frictional heat to plastically deform the material without melting it, operating at 60–90% of the melting temperature. Because this is a solid-state process, it provides several unique benefits over additive manufacturing based on fusion processes, such as no porosity, hot cracking defects, or solidification defects, as well as producing an equiaxed, fine grain structure that provides mechanical properties6,11,13,17,26. The technology is particularly beneficial for manufacturing components from high-strength aluminum alloys such as AA2XXX and AA7XXX, which tend to be regarded as non-weldable with traditional fusion-welding techniques2,12,21,29.

Recent advancements of the FSAM have demonstrated the capability to create components with trends consistent with microstructural refinement reported in prior literature and improved mechanical properties. Hassan et al.1 provided a comprehensive review that discusses the mechanical properties compared to several existing melting techniques available in the market and the ability of FSAM to create large-area multi-layered components with equiaxed grain structure. Airbus1,4,6, and the demonstration of FSAM to make wing ribs out of Al-Li 2025 alloys have shown that the technology is being used by major manufacturers. It is environmentally friendly and cost-effective, and it bonds layers together very well. Venkit and Selvaraj16 offered systematic procedures to manufacture aluminum-based alternately layered composite materials using FSAM, leading to improved mechanical properties through favorable placement of the AA6061 and AA7075 alloy layers.

FSAM process parameters interact with tool geometry and material flow, creating coupled effects difficult to predict analytically4,13,17,27,29. Exploring process parameters typically relies on traditional methods of experimentation, which can add to the time and costs of exploration, especially regarding how the total process parameter space will be explored for dissimilar material combinations2,3,26,28. Data-driven methods that leverage machine learning practices may offer solutions to these issues by accelerating the exploration process and enhancing our understanding of process-property relationships8–10,18,21.

The use of machine learning in FSAM has grown rapidly recently, and many researchers have started to examine multiple techniques in predicting and improving the outputs of a process-driven establishment. For instance, Mishra et al.8 used supervised machine learning based on physics-informed neural networks to predict peak temperature distributions in an additive friction stir deposition (FSAM). They showed the advantage of both a data-driven and physics-based modelling approach. On the other end, Meyghani et al and Chen et al.4,19, in addition to their machine learning approaches, presented complicated finite element models to simulate FSAM processes, ultimately allowing a faster understanding of the thermomechanical nature of the process and possible optimization of them. Jin et al and Das et al.7,28 also developed gradient-based process parameter optimization, allowing much faster exploration of the parameter space during FSAM processes.

The inclusion of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods has become particularly relevant in manufacturing applications with the understanding of parameter contributions to aid process control and optimization. Recent advances by Hariharan et al.14 in alloy optimization using machine learning in conjunction with high-throughput experiments and Mishra9 in mapping process parameters to FSAM deposition results with unsupervised machine learning algorithms demonstrate the emerging necessity for interpretable machine learning models within manufacturing process optimization22.

One of the primary hurdles to applying effective machine learning models is acquiring adequate high-quality training data for FSAM optimization. FSAM experiments face data-generation limitations due to cost, time, and resource constraints7,13,14,27. As a response to limited experimental data, researchers have turned to synthetic data generation approaches to supplement experimental datasets while maintaining surrogate consistency and relevance8,10,19,20. Radial Basis Function (RBF) interpolation has shown excellent results as a synthetic data generation method within manufacturing experiments; its smooth interpolation provides a representation of experimental data sets’ non-linear relationships9,19,23–25.

Recent developments in solid-state additive manufacturing processes have demonstrated the ability to produce components with highly specific microstructures and mechanical properties. Dong et al and Liu et al.6,17 provided an in-depth review of recent advances in additive friction stir deposition (FSAM) technology and validated its capability to produce fine, equiaxed recrystallized grains and mechanical properties comparable to forged components. Yaknesh et al and Nouranga et al.11,26 also studied the performance and microstructure evolution of solid-state fabrication through friction stir processes and presented properties in solid fabrication processes when compared to fusion-based processes. Srinivasan et al.15 further expanded on this by exploring composite FSAM structures for applications.

The field of the application of machine learning for the design of aluminum alloys and their optimization has gained support, and studies indicate very favorable potential in this area. Bhat et al.10 effectively used machine learning approaches as unsupervised learning to segment the dataset of 1154 aluminum alloys into different classes of aluminum alloys to show that machine learning can be a beneficial application for data-informed alloy classification and design purposes. By using an iterative label spreading (ILS) algorithm, Bhat and his team could identify eight distinct classes of aluminum alloys based on their processing conditions and composition that could act as the basis for data-informed progressive rational alloy designs14,18.

Recent research has also emphasized integrated frameworks combining multiple machine learning methods for complete process optimization. Shao et al.5 introduced a unified ‘Process-Structure-Property’ modeling framework for FSAM that provides a pathway to enable surrogate-assisted exploration of parameter–property relationships with the fewest experiments to get a predictive measure determined. This example shows the possibilities of developing integrated modeling frameworks capable of taking meaningful parameter process-structure-property windows into account, while simultaneously exhibiting relevant accuracy18–20.

The application of biomimetic machine learning methods appears to show potential to address many needs affiliated with additive manufacturing environments, especially basic research environments where new battery materials are developed and analyzed in addition to having FSAM CAD geometry generated for them. Their most recent research relating to FSAM-processed aluminum alloy structures has established an entirely new biomimetic ML approach for predicting mechanical properties pre- and post-optimization (namely genetic algorithm optimization); specifically, the performance had a learned mode of machine learning (genetic algorithm + Gaussian regression), and the performance has significantly surpassed conventional machine learning modes9,14,18,22. Recent advances demonstrate the systematic potential of combining machine learning with evolutionary optimization algorithms for materials design and process optimization.

While FSAM technology is relatively new to machine learning applications, significant potential exists for process optimization. Primarily, the literature covers single-material cases or geometrically simple cases; applications to multicomponent dissimilar material combinations are highly limited11,26,29. Contemporary machine learning applications rely heavily on datasets of relatively large size that restrict the application of the methods to circumstances having sparsity in the dataset, and these latter sparsity conditions are common in advanced manufacturing-related research8,10,20. Further, there is a lack of frameworks for implementation of explainable AI and synthetic data generation methods within a comprehensive framework that can provide predictive capability and advance the understanding of processing9,14,19.

The experimental design was based on a Taguchi L9 orthogonal array corresponding to a three-factor, three-level design. This approach enables efficient exploration of the interior design space and identification of dominant trends with a limited number of experiments. The nine experimental points were used as anchors for surrogate modeling, with all conclusions primarily anchored to the experimentally sampled parameter range, and any trend observed beyond 2.0° represents surrogate-based extrapolation requiring independent validation7,28.

This research will make an important contribution in advancing the literature landscape through a clearly identified comprehensive framework to optimize FSAM parameters while uniquely considering dissimilar aluminum alloy systems (6061, matrix: 7075, reinforced) and specifically advance the research on data-scarcity applications via synthetic data. The method employs an integrated approach to leverage RBF-based synthetic data generation, multiple machine learning regression approaches, and SHAP-based explainable AI analysis to develop a unique representation of the relationships between the processing parameters during the generation of data with high predictive capability12,18,22,24.

This work focuses on the immediate aspects of FSAM applications and provides a framework that can be generalized to address scarcity concerns when progressing through manufacturing processes. The combination of synthetic data generation with explainable AI methodologies provides a model that can inform similar works performed across different manufacturing domains within an experimental framework where data collection was limited14,18,19. The latest advance in this methodology is the way to enlarge data space while simultaneously ensuring preservation of physical constraints under resource-limited experimental conditions8,10,20.

The methodology presented in this research presents several contributions to the intelligent manufacturing domain: (1) a new synthetic data generation methodology for FSAM interpolation design, (2) a comprehensive examination of multiple machine learning regression methods for predicting the mechanical properties of dissimilar aluminum alloy FSAM processes, (3) SHAP-based explainable AI for understanding which parameters contribute to the process for closing the loop on process understanding, (4) an integrated framework that handled data scarcity while maintaining high-level predictive capability that is interpretable9,14,19,22.

The following sections provide a detailed explanation of the main workflow utilized, which includes experimental data preparation, synthetic data generation using RBF, implementation and evaluation of several machine learning models, and SHAP analysis for assessing parameter contributions. The results demonstrate the effectiveness of the workflow in achieving high predictive accuracy and providing the ability to gain actionable insights related to the FSAM optimization process, laying the groundwork for intelligent manufacturing systems that can be easily adapted or extended to industrial applications of automotive and other advanced manufacturing4–6,18.

Materials and methods

Workpiece material and specimen preparation

In this research two types of precipitation-hardened aluminum alloys: AA6061-T6 and AA7075-T6. These materials are commonly used for structural applications because they represent completely different alloy chemistries, enabling us to perform unique analyses property gradients generated during the FSAM process. The approach taken in stacking and processing these two alloys is like other published successful processes for constructing aluminum-based layered composites using FSAM16.

The elemental and compositional analyses of the two aluminum alloy systems were conducted using an XRF spectroscopic analysis19. The AA6061-T6 alloy consisted of approximately 98.1% of aluminum, magnesium (0.6% by wt.), silicon (0.34% by wt.), iron (0.45% by wt.), chromium (0.17% by wt.), zinc (0.14% by wt.), copper (0.12% by wt.), and manganese (0.09% by wt.) were the constituent elements. For the AA7075-T6 alloy, the major alloying elements were 4.95% by wt. zinc, 2.42% by wt. magnesium, 1.20% by wt. copper, 0.26% by wt. iron, 0.23% by wt. chromium, 0.18% by wt. manganese, and 0.08% by wt. silicon, with aluminum comprising approximately 90.68% by wt. for this type of aluminum alloy. Both AA6061-T6 and AA7075-T6 were supplied in the T6 temper, which combines a solution heat treatment and an artificial aging treatment16.

Both alloys were supplied as 200 mm × 150 mm × 3 mm thick initial workpieces in an “as received” rolled state with no prior modifications made (e.g., cutting). They were positioned so that AA6061-T6 and AA7075-T6 were placed together, thus providing for a layered composite material. Each time friction stir lap welding was performed, flash was removed with a fly cutter, and cleaning followed by ethanol cleaning & drying was done to create a clean interface for metallurgical bonding between layers. The result after seven alternate layers is an approximate thickness of 18 mm.

Tool design and specifications for friction stir additive manufacturing

The fabrication of all friction stir lap welding operations used a non-consumable tool made of H13 tool steel. The tool’s pin geometry was designed with a conical shape and threading to improve the flow of the stir zone material and the mixing of interlayers during friction stir lap welding.

Shoulder diameter is equal to 24 mm with a flat surface profile; no intentional concavity in the shoulder was created. However, due to the way the process parameters were selected, only a small amount of concavity was observed in the deposited layers, and none was found on the shoulder surface. The diameter taper of the pin is from 8 mm at the shoulder interface to 6 mm on the pin tip. Pin length is 4 mm. Plunge depth is kept at 4.10 mm to provide sufficient penetrability for the underlying plate being lap welded. The tool tilt angle varied at three levels: 1.0°, 1.5°, and 2.0° as part of the Taguchi L9 design, and an axial force of 20 kN was used to promote good consolidation and to minimize the number of defects, such as voids or excessive flash. The pitch and taper angles of the thread have not been provided.

Friction stir additive manufacturing process parameters and layer deposition strategy

No concavity was added as an intentional feature, and minimal concavity was observed in the completed layers of weldment. This minimal concavity was caused by selecting appropriate process parameters. The diameter of the pin tapered from 8 mm to 6 mm from where it interfaces with the shoulder down to the tip of the pin. The total length of the pin is 4 mm. The plunge depth of the tool into the underlying plate during lap welding was held constant at 4.1 mm to ensure enough penetration into the underlying plate. The tool tilt angle varied at three levels (1.0°, 1.5°, and 2.0) and the axial load on the tool used to provide a means of compressing or consolidating the deposited material to reduce the chance of creating defects such as voids or excessive flash was set at 20 kN. The specific thread pitch and taper angle were not indicated.

A build thickness of approximately 18 mm has been constructed by using seven alternating layers, with each layer contributing about 3 mm of thickness. For the single-pass approach, a single friction stir lap-weld pass was completed on the centerline of the plate. For the double-pass approach, two passes were completed in opposite directions, and each pass had an offset of 2 mm from the previous pass, which resulted in a total of 4 mm offset for both passes. This method increased the amount of material that was mixed within the stir zone because it created two sides that retreated and one side that advanced16.

All layers were deposited using standard laboratory ambient temperature without any externally applied heat or forced cooling; all layers cooled naturally in air after deposition. There was no post-deposition heat treatment, solution heat treatment, or any other form of artificial aging used for this build. The top surface of the build was milled down to achieve a flat surface for subsequent mechanical and microstructural testing.

Tensile testing specifications

Testing of the tensile properties was completed in accordance with Standard ASTM E8 for metals. Rectangular sub-size test samples were prepared from the additive manufactured products made by the Friction Stir Process, with the long axis of all test pieces aligned parallel with the direction of welding. The test samples were taken from the stir zone of the sample at three vertical locations (bottom, middle, and top) relative to the length of the sample.

All tensile tests were completed at room temperature on an INSTRON universal testing machine (model INST-8801) using a constant crosshead displacement rate of 1 mm/min. For each slice location, three replicates were tested to confirm reproducibility. The load vs. displacement data were taken continually throughout the test and subsequently converted to engineering stress-strain curves for further analysis.

Vickers microhardness testing specifications

The Vickers microhardness tester (Model HM-200) was used to measure microhardness using a diamond pyramid indenter. A 200-gram force was exerted on the specimen for approximately 15 s, and results are reported in Vickers hardness values.

Measurements were taken for both horizontal and vertical orientations on the surface of the deposited material. The horizontal measurements were taken on the interface (boundary) between two layers of material, while the vertical measurements were made vertically through the thickness from the bottom to the top of the deposited layers. The indentations used to create a profile of the hardness values were separated by approximately 0.5 mm. By having these designated spacing areas, it is possible to develop precise and detailed hardness profiles of the heat-affected and stir zones. Because there were no reported uncertainty values in this study, the consistent hardness trends across multiple samples and the even shape of profile lead to the conclusion that the results are highly reproducible, with minimal variation between samples.

All tensile and hardness data were taken from the same area of the FSAM builds and all tested with a consistent loading direction. Each condition measured represents a mean response from the various test samples, while any variability for that condition is noted. This sampling technique was designed to allow for accurate comparisons between each process condition while reducing geometric and positional variability. Hardness measurements were obtained at 0.5 mm intervals along both horizontal interlayer boundaries and through-thickness vertical sections to ensure representative sampling of stir zone and interface regions.

Data compilation and quality assurance

All mechanical and microhardness data, including ultimate tensile strength, elongation, process parameters, and slice positions, were systematically recorded and compiled. Tensile results represent mean values obtained from three replicate specimens per slice, while hardness profiles were generated from multiple indentations across each layer. Although formal statistical outlier rejection procedures were not applied, the reproducibility of tensile results and the consistency of hardness distributions indicate reliable data quality.

The final dataset comprised base material properties and comprehensive characterization of friction stir additive manufacturing builds, including tensile behavior, hardness distributions, and associated microstructural analyses such as scanning electron microscopy, electron backscatter diffraction, energy-dispersive spectroscopy, and X-ray diffraction, enabling robust assessment of structure–property relationships in alternating AA6061-T6 and AA7075-T6 composite structures16.

Scope and limitations of microstructural interpretation

This current research uses proxy methods to estimate and interpret the macroscopic mechanical response of dissimilar AA6061/AA7075 friction stir additive manufactured materials when there are very few available experimental results: ultimate tensile strength (UTS) and Vickers hardness. A comprehensive microstructural characterization of the alloy layers’ interfaces and their respective microstructural characteristics (i.e., grain shape and structure, precipitate size and shape, diffusion patterns, and intermetallic layer structure) was not performed in this research; thus, these areas of the production process are excluded from consideration within the current scope of the research. The microscopic structure in a multilayer dissimilar aluminum composite product will have an influence on its mechanical properties, and this research is not intended to define the relationship between mechanical properties; rather, the aim of this research is to demonstrate how to combine limited experimental results with data generated through interpolation and model-based learning, then utilize explainable ML methods to identify trends within the experimentally sampled design space. Future studies will include an explicit integration of microstructural and compositional information that will provide a stronger physical understanding of the results and further validate the proposed framework.

Because the models are trained primarily on RBF-interpolated samples derived from nine experimental measurements, the results reflect surrogate response surface behavior within the sampled parameter space. Independent experimental validation is necessary to confirm the predicted optima and associated mechanical improvements.

Results and discussion

The experiment outlined in Table 1 presents parametric surrogate-assisted optimization together with mechanical property experimentation for friction stir additive manufacturing (FSAM) processes of dissimilar aluminum alloys, focusing on the enhancement of mechanical properties of AA6061 and AA7075 alloy systems. The experimental matrix is carried out with an established three-level factorial design with nine levels of experimentation (noted A1 to C3) for examining the effects of three basic process parameters on mechanical properties. Three levels of tool rotation speed (TRS) were tested at 1000, 1100, and 1200 rpm; tool traverse speed (TTS) was evaluated at 30, 35, and 40 mm/min; and tool tilt angle (TTA) was evaluated at 1.0°, 1.5°, and 2.0°. Two additional configurations for the materials were also considered, 6061-over-7075 and 7075-over-6061, with the mechanical properties being investigated at level I: ultimate tensile strength (UTS) testing and level II: Vickers hardness (HV) testing.

The results from these experiments showed a significant amount of parametric sensitivity from stacking configuration to configuration, with UTS reported between 287 and 330 MPa for the 6061-over-7075 configuration and 291 and 336 for material sequence 7075-over-6061. Vickers’ hardness showed a similar variability, ranging from 69.5 to 120.0 HV for the 6061-over-7075 stacking sequence and 97.6 to 135.2 HV for the 7075-over-6061 stacking sequence. The data also indicate that the 7075-over-6061 stacking sequence was significantly higher in mechanical properties, reaching a UTS of 336 MPa in experiment C1 and a hardness value of 135.2 HV in experiment B1, substantiating the material stacking sequence as an important factor in FSAM results.

The nine experimental points that provided the data for the information above are a scarce number of experimental observations and are typical of the advanced manufacturing field given the confines of resources, experimental complexity, and time constraints for complete observation of parameters. The limited observational data justifies using synthetic data for high surrogate consistency within the interpolated design space, which will be integrated into the RBF-based synthetic data generation framework, i.e., the mathematical interpolant.

A comprehensive synthetic data generation framework to overcome the inherent data scarcity challenges in interpolated design space was developed, based solely on this experimental dataset of nine data points. The synthetic data augmentation approach used Radial Basis Function (RBF) interpolation employing multiquadric basis functions and a smoothing parameter of 0.1 to produce 882 synthetic samples. Overall, a much wider parameter space for developing machine learning models was created. The synthetic data grid had a coarser discretization scheme with TRS values that increased at 10 rpm increments from 1000 to 1200 rpm, TTS values increasing at 2 mm/min increments from 30 to 40 mm/min, and TTA values that increased at 0.2-degree increments from 1.0 to 2.0 degrees, forming a three-dimensional mesh grid that encompassed the experimental parameter space and extended slightly beyond the experimental space to allow extrapolation.

Interpolation continuity is imposed by the RBF method through smooth transitions between the 9 experimental points; however, this smooth transition feature is not a guarantee of surrogate consistency but is rather a characteristic of the mathematical model representing that interpolation. The physical relationships between the thermomechanical properties of FSAM will only accurately depict the relationship between thermomechanical properties and the true process-properties of FSAM to the degree that the 9 experimental data points accurately portray the true process-properties of FSAM. Distinct RBF models were trained for each output variable, UTS and HV, corresponding to each material configuration; these models used the original experimental coordinates as interpolation nodes, and the multiquadric function was chosen for its ability to capture the nonlinear relationships between process properties inherent in solid-state manufacturing processes. The subsequent synthetic data generation preserves smooth interpolation continuity between experimentally measured points, enabling construction of an enriched training dataset consistent with the observed experimental trends, while also substantially decreasing the data space required for developing rigorous machine learning models.

To generate a denser mesh for ML model training within the explored design space, RBF interpolation was applied to create synthetic points between and near the 9 experimental measurements. The resulting 882 synthetic samples augment the original 9 points to form an 891-point dataset for training purposes. Critically, these synthetic points represent interpolated values from the RBF surrogate function, not independent experimental measurements. Models trained on this synthetic dataset effectively learn the RBF response surface and the model captures the surrogate response surface within the explored design space. This is a pragmatic approach to the data-scarcity problem but should not be misinterpreted as a generalization to unmeasured real experimental conditions.

The comparison highlights the different performance metrics for the three proposed regression models. Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) demonstrates near-zero error relative to RBF-interpolated surrogate values and exhibits total errors lower than 0.1% in some synthetic data examples, particularly in the TRS experiments at 1000 rpm for TTA of 1.2°, 1.4°, and 1.6°. The largest error associated with GPR is 0.08% at the high parameter levels evaluated, indicating the model was able to reproduce the baseline structure of synthetic data patterns with fidelity while being clearly observable in the interpolating (RBF) functions. The Support Vector Regression (SVR) model functions moderately in prediction capacity, and average overall error rates relative to the measurement ranged from 0.14% to − 0.81%. Furthermore, SVR had some tendency to underpredict the TRS responses with mostly negative rates of error. Overall, Support Vector Regression appears to provide relatively consistent prediction observation over the parameter space studied, with overall rates of error remaining relatively low and mainly below 1%. Conversely, the prediction errors of the Bayesian Ridge Regression are relatively high compared to the other methods, which have error percentages across the range of −6.45% to −3.88%, and consistently overpredict UTS values for the synthetic data points. The behaviors exhibited by the Bayesian Ridge model suggest that the model may have limitations in capturing the non-linear relationships that exist within the FSAM process, given it is trained on a minimal or limited original experimental dataset and then trained or applied to the synthetic data collected from the RBF interpolation.

The findings indicate that the GPR model yields predictions for synthetic data points and justifies its choice as the primary model for interpolated design space. The prediction accuracy achieved by GP regression is derived largely from the ability of GP regression to capture uncertainty and to model nonlinear relationships with probabilistic predictions that match the structure of synthetic data generated using RBF. The continued underestimations of SVR and continued overestimations of Bayesian Ridge Regression highlight the importance of model selection in applications using synthetic data as it relates to the ability of an algorithm to effectively represent the interpolated relationships defined by the RBF.

This comparison provides the foundation for carrying out full model evaluation across the entire synthetic dataset, showing the GPR-based model provides the most consistent reproduction of the surrogate response surface within the explored parameter space with the original experimental observations. The findings also validate that the assembled RBF-ML framework can be an effective response to challenges around data scarcity in advanced manufacturing process optimization.

According to these findings, GPR will continue to be the model for predicting hardness in FSAM while possessing similar fidelity across the full space. The moderate accuracy and negative bias of SVR and the limited error range of Bayesian Ridge indicate the need for selecting models according to specific FSAM prediction tasks. The high reliability of GPR shown here supports the integrated RBF–GPR framework for surrogate-assisted multi-property optimization in friction stir additive manufacturing.

Near-zero error on synthetic data reflects the model’s ability to learn the interpolated surrogate, not its generalization to unseen experimental variability. To avoid misleading interpretation, performance metrics reported on synthetic datasets are used only for internal consistency assessment of the surrogate-learning task. Predictive capability on real FSAM variability requires independent experimental validation.

The generated synthetic parameter grid using the radial basis function interpolation method was designed to fit primarily inside the experimental domain from which the corresponding parameters were sampled experimentally. The maximum allowable rotation speeds were 1000 to 1200 rpm, and the maximum allowable traverse speeds were 30 to 40 mm/min; thus, tool rotation speed and tool traverse speed were within the experimental ranges that were obtained from actual experiments conducted; hence, no extrapolation to these speeds occurred. Tool tilt angles were sampled primarily over an experimental range of 1.0° to 2.0°; the 2.1° to 2.2° range was included to evaluate edge behaviors of the interpolation surface.

Radial basis function interpolators have increasing uncertainty when extrapolated outside of the convex hull constructed by the original parameter set. Predictions made using tool tilt angles of 2.1° and 2.2° are extrapolated predictions and should be evaluated with extreme caution. The Gaussian Process Regression confidence surface for this extrapolated region is nearly flat; therefore, this region has high uncertainty and low information content associated with it. Predictions made in the extrapolated region should not be used for process optimization or definitive inference without additional experimental support.

Regression modeling methodologies

Gaussian Process Regression

Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) is a non-parametric and probabilistic regression technique that establishes a prior over functions and then updates that prior based on observed data to create a predictive posterior. GPR can model nonlinear relationships using a kernel function (in this case, a constant kernel multiplied by an RBF kernel) and can determine uncertainty through predictive variances. In this work, GPR is used for its power to assess complex nonlinear process-property relationships that are observed in the friction stir additive manufacturing (FSAM) data set but that also estimate uncertainty to provide confidence in prediction for surrogate-assisted parameter optimization.

Support vector regression (SVR)

Support Vector Regression (SVR) is an application of the support vector machine concepts for regression. SVR finds predictions within a zone of radius ε of observations and minimizes a cost that aggregates contributions from training error and model complexity. While SVR can accommodate nonlinearities in the training data using an RBF kernel, hyperparameters C and ε allow SVR to balance error with empirical margin. SVR was considered in this text for its robustness to outlier observations, and arrangements for nonlinear regression provided a complementary method to GPR with greater computational efficiency.

Bayesian Ridge Regression

Bayesian Ridge Regression adopts Bayesian methodology for linear regression by defining Gaussian priors over model coefficients and calculating posterior distributions. It then implements automatic regularization based on the determined hyperparameters, effectively reducing overfitting for small datasets. Bayesian Ridge is presented to evaluate a linear, regularized approach and to evaluate performance with strong prior constraints relative to nonlinear models.

Utilizing detailed evaluation measures (MAE, RMSE, R², and percentage error), and with regard to 891 training points, GPR outperformed SVR and Bayesian Ridge and achieved very low prediction error (< 0.1%), an MAE of 2.34, an RMSE closely corresponding to the MAE, and an R² greater than 0.95 in each optimal configuration. SVR produced reasonable accuracy (error near 1%), whereas Bayesian Ridge produced systematic bias and a larger error (up to 12% in hardness prediction). Therefore, the accuracy, uncertainty quantification, and stability of GPR across parameter regimes support the decision to use GPR as the main regression model for surrogate-assisted FSAM multi-property optimization in this research.

Comprehensive evaluation metrics

This research on friction stir additive manufacturing quantitatively evaluates the machine learning model’s performance using a range of metrics to assess predictive accuracy, model reliability, and systematic bias using the synthetic dataset generated from RBF interpolation. The metrics selected for evaluation create an assurance of confirmation of the RBF-GPR framework generating surrogate-based multi-property predictions within the design space explored.

Mean absolute error (MAE)

Mean Absolute Error (MAE), it measures the average magnitude of errors in a set of predictions, without considering their directional bias, and provides an intuitive and interpretable way to evaluate the accuracy of a model that preserves the original units of measurement and significance. The evaluation of MAE across the entire synthetic dataset highlights performance differences between the three implemented regression algorithms. GPR demonstrated excellent discrimination accuracy, with MAE values ranging from 2.34 to 8.67 for UTS and HV across both material configurations, and GPR consistently performed better than the alternative evaluations. SVR exhibited moderate performance with MAE values roughly 2–3 times higher than GPR; however, the MAE values derived from Bayesian Ridge Regression were significantly higher, particularly in the case of hardness predictions, where errors frequently exceeded 10% of the predicted value. The low MAE values from GPR encouraged confidence in the approach’s ability to effectively uphold high fidelity to the synthetically generated ground truth produced by the RBF, confirming that the interpolated process-property relationships were preserved during model training and prediction. This ability is an important consideration for the conceptual optimization demonstration. Specifically, accurate predictions of mechanical property values will help enable more legitimate decisions to be made in the selection of process parameters that influence mechanical properties of built components.

Root mean square error (RMSE)

Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) improves sensitivity for larger prediction errors by weighing each error term quadratically prior to averaging, thereby increasing the effect of extreme errors as well as systematic bias. It is especially useful for identifying models with the worst performance for the most important elements of the data set and assigns increased importance to larger errors, which affects the entire evaluation metric. Evaluating GPR using RMSE consistently affirms its estimates are better than both SVR and Bayesian Ridge Regression for each mechanical property, with RMSE values consistently lower than those calculated using SVR and Bayesian Ridge Regression. For the GPR model, RMSE values are typically 5–10% of the corresponding MAE values, reflecting the lack of extreme errors and uneven error occurring over the range of combinations of parameters. In contrast, SVR generally has RMSE values that are higher than MAE by 20–40%, suggesting the presence of systematic prediction error or extreme observed errors that compromise model reliability. Bayesian Ridge Regression shows the highest RMSE with respect to MAE, consistently exceeding 50%, indicating high variability in prediction accuracy for any combination of parameters in the parameter space. The RMSE evaluation supports the stability of the GPR model, characterized by accurate prediction over a diverse range of engineering parameters and mechanical properties. This is a desirable characteristic for FSAM applications where process parameters may vary substantially throughout manufacturing and reasonable predictions are necessary for the entire operational envelope.

Coefficient of determination (R²)

The determination coefficient (R²) measures the amount of variance accounted for in the dependent variable from the independent variables, which is also a standardized measure of model explanatory power. R² ranges from 0 to 1, and the closer to 1, the better the model provides useful predictions, and conversely, as R² approaches 0, the model provides poor utility for predictions. The R² assessment of the comprehensive synthetic dataset indicates virtuous performance for GPR with R² values that continuously exceeded 0.85 for most configurations of output variables and as high as 0.95–0.99 in certain cases with selected parameters. The high R² indicates that GPR is successfully modeling highly non-linear characteristics between process parameters and mechanical properties characterized in the RBF synthetic dataset. SVR performs reasonably well with moderate R² values typically reported in the range of 0.65–0.80, indicating reasonable and not exceptional explanatory capability associated with process parameter predictions of mechanical properties. Bayesian Ridge Regression provides the lowest R² performance, reported below 0.60 in many instances, and struggles the most in hardening predictions, as the model was unable to capture process-property relationships. The high R² values acquired using GPR provide confidence that the model successfully captures the surrogate response behavior -based relationships between FSAM process parameters and resulting mechanical properties. Successful capture of physics-based relationships and hence strong predictive ability is contingent upon providing a model that accurately depicts an underlying process to make predictions of optimal operation.

Percentage error analysis

Percentage error provides a standardized measure for making comparisons across different output variables even when these variables have varying absolute values. This analysis is particularly important when considering multi-objective surrogate-assisted optimization, where distinct mechanical properties (i.e., UTS and HV) might have different measurements and ranges of expected values. The assessment of percentage errors reflects very good GPR predictions, with percentage errors found to be all below 1% for almost all the synthetic data points and often found to be near zero on multiple parameter combinations. SVR indicates systematic bias tendencies in its predictive value with percentage errors sometimes between 0.5% and 2.0%, showing systematic underprediction within the parameter regimes. While the Bayesian Ridge Regression demonstrated systematic bias with percentage errors that ranged between 2% and 12%, it is indicative of a considerable peril in attempting to quantify the inherent nonlinear relationships in FSAM processes.

Cross-validation performance

The synthetic dataset allows for extensive cross-validation evaluation by fully measuring model performance at the combinations of low, medium, and high TRS, TTS, and TTA, which, in turn, is used to evaluate model accuracy as well as model robustness and generalization performance rather than simply cross-validation regime definitions. GPR exhibits a model performance that is consistent across all parameter regimes for TRS, TTS, and TTA metrics, producing virtually identical accuracy metrics with varying process conditions. SVR possesses a dependency on parameters where model accuracy displays a benefit in all the higher (medium or high) parameter regimes when compared to the lower parameter regimes, and in lower parameter regimes the bias is more pronounced. Bayesian Ridge Regression indicates a high amount of performance variation across the different parameter regimes, including cases when both TRS, TTS, and TTA were set to lower parameter conditions, which is where the nonlinear effects are typically most severe and demonstrated the greatest performance metric deficits. Cross-validation was performed exclusively on RBF-generated samples and therefore evaluates surrogate fidelity rather than experimental generalization.

Statistical significance and confidence interval

The probabilistic structure of GPR allows for characterizing uncertainty with confidence intervals and constraining the reliability of predictions. The analysis reveals narrow confidence intervals, typically between 2% and 5%, for most distinctive parameter combinations. These observations provide considerable confidence in the projected predictions. Similarly, the SVR and Bayesian Ridge model predictions may require the use of bootstrap or cross-validation approaches to characterize uncertainty, which typically yield wider confidence intervals; thus, the confidence in the predictive nature of these models is lower.

The detailed evaluation based on 882 synthetic data points indicates GPR’s impactfully strong predictive capability, which is across all mechanical properties and parameter spaces, almost near zero. More specifically, GPR achieves the fit across MAE, RMSE, and R², exhibits cross-validation results and low systematic bias, and therefore GPR was selected as the regression algorithm to implement the interpolated design space framework proposed in this work. SVR can be employed with moderate performance for quick screening approaches, e.g., just to identify trends; however, it introduces systematic bias and therefore cannot be considered for accurate applications that require precision and high levels of accuracy. Lastly, Bayesian Ridge Regression has exhibited significant limitations, including suitability for hardness prediction, and therefore is not applicable for FSAM applications that require high levels of accuracy and precision.

Taking together, these metrics instill confidence that the proposed RBF-GPR framework generates surrogate-based multi-property predictions within the explored design space forming the basis of intelligent manufacturing systems that can achieve target mechanical properties through data-driven process control.

Parameter contribution analysis using SHAP

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were computed separately for each output variable (UTS and Vickers hardness) and for each material stacking configuration. The SHAP background dataset consisted of the synthetic parameter grid generated via RBF interpolation, rather than independent experimental measurements. For Gaussian Process Regression, SHAP values were estimated using a model-agnostic approximation based on sampling from the surrogate-trained prediction function. Consequently, the resulting SHAP values quantify relative parameter influence within the learned surrogate response surface and should not be interpreted as indicative trends of causal dominance in the physical FSAM process.

The SHAP background dataset (baseline) was created using the synthetic parameter grid with RBF generation of synthetic parameters (parameter grid) to represent the response surface created by using only the limited experimental anchors. The background distribution does not use any independent experimental data as an additional source.

To estimate SHAP values for the Gaussian Process Regression, the Kernel SHAP was used as a model-agnostic approximation of SHAP where the prediction made by the GPR model is sampled from the trained GPR surrogate model. A total of 1,000 Monte Carlo samples is drawn for each SHAP calculation to obtain the Shapley values with stable convergence; therefore, the SHAP value for each parameter reflects the relative influence that each of the parameters has on the output of the trained surrogate model. In addition, differences in the results produced by different outputs are due to different output-specific surrogate behaviors rather than due to one global dominance ranking.

This evaluates the contributions of key FSAM process inputs tool rotation speed (TRS), traverse speed (TTS), and tilt angle (TTA) to predict mechanical properties (ultimate tensile strength and Vickers hardness) for independent 6061-over-7075 and 7075-over-6061 configurations, as well as quantify the distributions of synthetic process inputs for sensitivity sampling.

The SHAP mean percent contributions show that TRS clearly controlled prediction, accounting for 73.97% and 73.95% in the output variance for the first and second passes, respectively, while TTS accounted for 23.10% and TTA for 2.93%. When evaluating all 882 synthesized samples, TRS was the only impactful parameter in most cases (751, 85.15%), TTS was impactful in 126 cases (14.29%), and there was one instance of TTA being the first influential parameter out of 882 synthetic samples (5, 0.57%).

There is also a detailed SHAP summary for GPR models presenting important rankings and contributions for each property and stacking sequence. For UTS_6061over7075, TRS has an influence of 41.75%, TTS is 33.25%, and TTA is 25.00%. For HV_6061 over 7075, TTS is ranked first at 49.89%, followed by TTA at 25.79% and TRS at 24.32%. For UTS_7075over6061, TRS has the most influence at 45.74% to TTS at 34.60%, and TTA is at 19.66%. For HV_7075over6061, TTS is 48.10%, TRS is 31.54%, and TTA is 20.36%. Each of TRS and TTS emerges as most influential 2 times in these 4 outputs.

Optimization objective and search strategy

The intent of this research project was to demonstrate the capability of using surrogate-assisted trend exploration as a means of optimizing process parameters conceptually rather than validating true optimization of the process. Separate objective functions were created for ultimate tensile strength (UTS) and Vickers hardness (HV), whereby each objective was treated independently, with those objectives being modeled by their respective surrogate regression models trained on each of their own training datasets. A single objective function was created for each objective, and no constrained multi-objective surrogate-based optimization was conducted.

Parameter selection was accomplished by doing a grid-based analysis of the RBF-generated synthetic mesh, rather than employing a continuous or gradient optimization algorithm. The areas where candidate parameter values are predicted to be optimal were determined by examining the surrogate response surfaces to identify the maximum value for each response surface in the discretized design space.

Predicted optimal values for several responses of interest were found to occur at or near the edges of the synthetic parameter grid, which indicates that those predicted optimal parameters are likely to be the result of surrogate interpolation, especially under the circumstances where there is a limited quantity of data available for use in training the surrogate regression models. Therefore, predictions of optimal parameters in these areas must be viewed with skepticism, and no such predictions are claimed as globally validated optimal parameters without first proving experimentally that they are truly globally validated.

The output of this research has not produced any deployable or interactive optimization tools, as described in the introductory sections of this paper, and the results of this research are intended to serve as examples of potential workflows for selecting parameters as opposed to proof of true validated process optimizations.

The identified optimal parameter combinations represent surrogate-derived solutions within the interpolated response surface and require independent experimental validation before practical implementation.

For dissimilar multilayer FSAM builds such as 6061/7075, interfacial phenomena including mixing zone thickness, diffusion and precipitation behavior, and localized softening or strengthening bands play a decisive role in determining mechanical performance. The present research does not include direct microstructural or compositional characterization (e.g., EDS mapping, thickness-direction microhardness profiles, or microstructure imaging). Accordingly, the reported results are interpreted in terms of macroscopic performance correlations rather than explicit interfacial mechanisms. Future work integrating detailed interface characterization will be essential to physically interpret and validate the trends identified by the surrogate modeling framework.

The constrained FSAM process window presented in this research is based on the tool rotation speed, travel speed, and tool tilt angle and does not account for other important parameters like axial force, tool geometry, deposition strategy, or thermal history. As such, the trends and surrogate predictions that were reported are limited to those variables within this research and do not necessarily apply to other machines/build strategies. That said, as more experimental data are collected, the RBF-assisted machine-learning framework that has been proposed allows for the possibility of exploring additional variables in the same manner as the ones used in this research.

FSAM-built components are known to exhibit spatial heterogeneity and anisotropy arising from thermal cycling, and material flow. The present research does not include systematic thorough thickness or build-direction performance mapping; therefore, the reported mechanical properties should be interpreted as representative of the sampled regions rather than as a comprehensive assessment of global part quality. Extending the present framework to incorporate spatially resolved mechanical characterization remains an important direction for future work.

Although mechanical trends may be influenced by underlying microstructural and interfacial phenomena inherent to FSAM processes, the present study does not include detailed quantitative microstructural characterization (e.g., EBSD grain size measurement or compositional mapping across interfaces). Therefore, microstructural interpretations remain qualitative and are discussed in the context of existing literature.

Although the synthetic mesh extends slightly beyond the experimental design space, extrapolative predictions in these regions are associated with increased uncertainty due to the limited number of experimental anchor points. The present research therefore focuses on interpolation within the Taguchi-sampled parameter domain. Robust extrapolation would require additional experimental data, particularly near boundary and out-of-domain regions, which is identified as an important direction for future work.

Conclusion

This research developed a surrogate-assisted machine learning framework to correlate mechanical property prediction to friction stir additive manufacturing of dissimilar aluminum alloys in data-sparse conditions. Nine experimental data points, along with RBF methodology-generated synthetic data (of 10x), were used to benchmark and compare the regression models (Gaussian Process Regression (GPR), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Bayesian Ridge) to predict the ultimate tensile strength and Vickers Hardness of two different material-stacking configurations. GPR performed best, with leave-one-out cross-validation yielding a mean absolute error (MAE) of 3.58–4.95 MPa for UTS and 2.14–3.21 HV for hardness for the experimental points. The errors are considerably higher than for the associated synthetic test sets (MAE < 0.1%). Thus, the near-perfect accuracy of the synthetic models suggests that the predicted mechanical properties based on the synthetic data reflect the RBF learning through interpolation rather than a true generalization to independently validated experimental data.

The SHAP method helped to understand how different parameters play a role in determining the UTS of the associated materials. Tool rotation speed (TRS) was identified as the primary contributor to the prediction of UTS. However, in the case of hardness, the interaction of the various parameters was much more complex. In the case of 7075 − 6061 stacking, there was an equal contribution of TRS and TTS to the hardness prediction. The results of this analysis show that different materials will respond differently to different processes, and thus, refinements to process parameters will need to be made for each material combination. Specifically, a lower TRS would produce optimal UTS for 6061–7075; conversely, a higher TRS would provide optimal UTS when producing 7075 − 6061. Moderate TRS with slow TTS consistently produced optimal hardness irrespective of stacking combination.

The practical use of the framework depends on independent experiment-based confirmation of its effectiveness. While the surrogate model can be used for data-driven exploratory testing of parameters when there is limited data, any predictions made based on data from untested conditions should be viewed as approximations and not as true values. In fact, based on the LOOCV results, the amount of error between the actual data compared to the predictive results on the actual data range from one to three%, and for the synthetic data it was less than 1%. Thus, while the framework can be employed within the tested range of parameters (TRS = 1000–1200 rpm; TTS = 30–40 mm/min; TTA = 1.0–2.0°), it only applies to the tool’s design and alloy type that were employed in the testing. In order to be able to apply the method to other tool designs and alloy types, or if other variables may be used, the method must be extended either through experimental validation of the new parameters or through the process of changing the method itself.

The findings should be interpreted as surrogate-assisted trends within the experimentally explored design space. Independent experimental validation at predicted optima is required before practical implementation.

Future work is essential to establish industrial applicability. The conclusions are restricted to macroscopic mechanical performance trends, with detailed interfacial mechanisms reserved for future investigation and locally sampled mechanical performance, and do not claim uniform part-scale properties or interpolation within the experimentally sampled parameter space and do not claim validated extrapolation beyond it.

While the present study does not claim predictive generalization beyond the experimental domain, it establishes a transparent and reproducible framework for surrogate-assisted exploration under severe data scarcity, which is common in solid-state additive manufacturing research.
