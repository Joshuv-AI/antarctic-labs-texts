Immune Activation Mediates the Association of Advanced Hepatic Fibrosis With Adverse Outcomes in Patients With Coronary Artery Disease
Authors: Vardhmaan Jain, Anurag Mehta, Terence B Lee, Chang Liu, Nicholas W S Chew, Yi‐An Ko, Matthew E Gold, Daniel A Gold, Nishant Vatsa, Shivang R Desai, Jonathan H Kim, Alireza Rahbar, Yazan Haroun, Kiran Ejaz, Salim S Hayek, Mohammad S Siddiqui, Fadi N Salloum, Laurence S Sperling, Arun J Sanyal, Arshed A Quyyumi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiterature suggests a bidirectional association between advanced hepatic fibrosis (AHF) and coronary artery disease (CAD). We evaluated the association of AHF with immune activation, systemic inflammation, and adverse outcomes in patients with CAD.Methods and ResultsA fibrosis‐4 index cutoff value ≥2.67 was used to define AHF. Circulating levels of soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor and hsCRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein) were measured as markers for immune activation and systemic inflammation, respectively. The relationship of AHF with soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor, hsCRP, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes was evaluated. Among 3406 participants with CAD, 479 had AHF. Participants with AHF were older; were less likely to be Black individuals; and had a lower body mass index, worse renal function, and a prior history of heart failure. In multivariable linear regression models adjusted for clinical and demographic confounders, participants with AHF had 15.6% higher soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor and 24.0% higher hsCRP levels. They were more likely to experience the following adverse outcomes: all‐cause death (adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 1.57 ([95% CI, 1.29–1.92]; P<0.001) and cardiovascular death: (subdistribution HR, 1.50 [95% CI, 1.14–1.95]; P=0.003). Mediation analysis showed that 47.0% (95% CI, 13.6%–81.2%]; P=0.006) of the indirect effect of AHF on cardiovascular death was mediated by circulating soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor levels.ConclusionsAHF is independently associated with immune activation, systemic inflammation, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with CAD. The association of AHF with adverse outcomes is partly mediated by immune activation, and targeting this pathway may help reduce the residual risk in patients with CAD.Keywords: advanced hepatic fibrosis, coronary artery disease, hsCRP, immune activation, suPARSubject Categories: Biomarkers

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a leading cause of end‐stage liver disease, and advanced hepatic fibrosis (AHF) is the hallmark of disease progression. 1 Emerging evidence suggests that there is a bidirectional relationship between the liver and the cardiovascular system. 2 This relationship is partly related to shared metabolic risk factors including obesity, insulin resistance, atherogenic dyslipidemia, and systemic inflammation. Prior studies suggest that patients across the spectrum of liver disease, regardless of cause, are at an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD). 3 Conversely, recent studies have demonstrated that among patients with established coronary artery disease (CAD), the presence of NAFLD and, importantly, severe disease (ie, AHF) is associated with a higher risk of adverse outcomes. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 This association is clinically important because patients with CAD remain at a high risk of adverse outcomes despite adequate treatment of the disease and its underlying risk factors. Understanding the determinants of this relationship can help identify nontraditional therapeutic targets for residual risk reduction.

In this conceptual framework of residual risk, our group and others have demonstrated the independent predictive value of immune activation, estimated by circulating soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR) level, and systemic inflammation, estimated by circulating hsCRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein) level, in patients with CAD. 8 , 9 , 10 However, the interrelationship of AHF, immune activation, and systemic inflammation for outcome prognostication in CAD has not been evaluated to date. In the current study, we addressed this important knowledge gap by studying the independent association of AHF, immune activation, and systemic inflammation with adverse outcomes in patients with CAD with the hypothesis that immune activation and systemic inflammation partly mediate the relationship between AHF and adverse outcomes.

Methods

Data Sharing Statement

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

Consecutive participants enrolled in the Emory Cardiovascular Biobank, a prospective registry of patients undergoing cardiac catheterization for evaluation of CAD at 3 Emory Healthcare–affiliated hospitals were enrolled in this study. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Emory University Institutional Review Board as previously described. 11 For the purpose of this study, participants with missing data regarding liver function tests (serum aspartate aminotransferase or alanine aminotransferase), those who had a diagnosis of hepatocellular cancer (primary or metastatic), decompensated hepatic cirrhosis, or acute myocardial infarction were excluded. A total of 3406 participants (479 with AHF) were analyzed.

Participant Characteristics and Inflammatory Markers

Information regarding demographic characteristics, smoking history, comorbid medical conditions, and medication use was aggregated on the basis of self‐report as well as review of medical records as previously described. 11 Presence of diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and established CVD subtypes (CAD, heart failure [HF], stroke, and peripheral artery disease) was determined by physician diagnosis or ongoing treatment. 11 Anthropometric measures including weight and height were measured at enrollment, and body mass index was calculated by dividing weight by height squared (kg/m2). Laboratory measures including hemoglobin level (g/dL), platelet count (per μL), serum alanine aminotransferase (IU/L), serum aspartate aminotransferase (IU/L) and serum creatinine (mg/dL) were measured at enrollment, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation. 12 Plasma levels of suPAR were measured with the suPARnostic ELISA assay (ViroGates, Copenhagen, Denmark), 13 and serum hsCRP level was measured using a sandwich immunoassay (FirstMark, Division of GenWay Biotech, San Diego, CA). 14

AHF and Fibrosis‐4 Score

The fibrosis‐4 index (FIB‐4) is a well‐validated, noninvasive score that can identify patients with significant liver fibrosis and avoid the need for invasive procedures such as liver biopsy. 15 , 16 A FIB‐4 score ≥2.67 has been shown to have a good discriminant function to identify patients with AHF, regardless of the cause of liver disease. 16 , 17 The FIB‐4 score is derived from 3 laboratory parameters, including alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and platelet count, and participant age as follows 15 :

FIB‐4=[Age (year)× aspartate aminotransferase (U/L])/[platelet count (109/L)× alanine aminotransferase (U/L)1/2].

For the purpose of our study, we used the baseline participant age and laboratory parameters that were drawn at enrollment into the Emory Cardiovascular Biobank. A FIB‐4 score ≥2.67 was used to identify participants with AHF.

Follow‐Up and Outcomes

The outcomes of interest were time to all‐cause death and cardiovascular death. Follow‐up data were obtained using annual phone contact, electronic medical record review, the Social Security Death Index, and state records. 11 The cause of death was determined from medical record review or by direct contact with the participants' family member(s) or both. Cardiovascular death events were adjudicated by 2 independent cardiologists blinded to study data. Cardiovascular death was defined as death attributable to an ischemic cardiovascular cause such as fatal myocardial infarction, stroke, or sudden death secondary to a presumed cardiovascular cause in this high‐risk population.

Statistical Analysis

Participant characteristics, including demographics, comorbid medical conditions, and lab values were stratified by the presence or absence of AHF. Data were reported as frequency and percentage for categorical variables and as mean±SD or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables depending on distribution. Differences between participants with and without AHF were evaluated using the chi‐square test for categorical variables and using the independent‐sample Student's t test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate, for continuous variables.

Given the nonnormal distribution of biomarkers (suPAR and hsCRP), we assessed these variables as continuous log(2)‐transformed variables. The association between AHF and biomarkers was evaluated using linear regression models of the log‐transformed variables. Covariates included age, sex, race, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, history of smoking, body mass index, eGFR, CAD history, and HF history.

The association of AHF with adverse outcomes (time to all‐cause death and cardiovascular death) was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for age, sex, race, diabetes, smoking history, hypertension, body mass index, eGFR, CAD history, and HF history (model 1). The covariates for the regression models were selected a priori on the basis of clinical significance of the exposure with outcome of interest. Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard models were used to analyze the subhazard ratios (sHRs) for time to cardiovascular death where noncardiovascular death was treated as a competing risk event. 18 To evaluate the association of biomarkers of immune activation (suPAR) and inflammation (hsCRP) with AHF and adverse outcomes, we added these biomarkers to regression models in addition to the aforementioned demographic and clinical covariates (models 2 and 3). The proportional hazards assumption was verified by Schoenfeld residuals. The discriminant function of the 2 models was compared using Harrel's c‐statistic.

To investigate whether the association between AHF (exposure) and adverse cardiovascular events (outcome) is partially mediated through immune activation and inflammation (mediators), we used the R package regmedint, which is based on a regression‐based causal mediation analysis approach that incorporates survival data, to test the significance of the direct and indirect (mediated by immune activation and systemic inflammation) associations between AHF and clinical end points. 19 , 20 All analyses were performed using STATA version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX), SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), and R version 3.6.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A 2‐tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Association Between Immune Activation, Inflammation, and AHF

Patients with AHF had higher median (interquartile range) suPAR levels (3735 [2967–5016] versus 2999 [2290–3967] pg/mL; P<0.001) as well as higher hsCRP levels (3.7 [1.3–11] versus 3.3 [1.3–9] mg/L; P=0.021), indicative of higher levels of immune activation and inflammation, respectively (Table 1, Figure 1A and 1B). In multivariable linear regression models, suPAR levels were 15.6% (95% CI, 10.8%–20.7%; P<0.001) higher and hsCRP levels were 24% (95% CI, 7.0%–43.7%; P<0.001) higher in patients with AHF compared with patients without AHF.

Association Between AHF and Adverse Outcomes

Overall, patients with AHF had a higher unadjusted all‐cause mortality rate (38.6% versus 23.5%; P<0.001) during a mean follow‐up period of 6.7±4.2 years (Figure 2A). Mediation Analysis

Given the association of suPAR and hsCRP with cardiovascular death, we performed a mediation analysis to evaluate if the association of AHF with cardiovascular death was partly mediated by them. Mediation analysis demonstrated that 47% (95% CI, 14%–81%; P=0.006) of the indirect effect of AHF on cardiovascular death was mediated by suPAR levels, and an insignificant 10% (95% CI, −7% to 28%; P=0.25) was mediated by hsCRP levels (Figure 3).

Discussion

We report 3 important findings in this study. First, AHF as determined by the FIB‐4 score was independently associated with higher circulating levels of suPAR and hsCRP, markers of immune activation and systemic inflammation, respectively, in patients with CAD. Second, AHF, suPAR, and hsCRP levels were independently associated with increased risk of adverse outcomes in this population. Finally and most importantly, the association of AHF with adverse outcomes in CAD was partly mediated by systemic immune activation.

Association of AHF With Immune Activation and Systemic Inflammation

It is well established that progression of hepatic steatosis to AHF is a multifactorial process that involves immune activation and inflammation. 1 SuPAR is an emerging marker of immune activation that is not only associated with incident cardiorenal disease progression but may also contribute to the atherosclerotic process by its effects on immune dysregulation and altered monocyte function that makes them proatherogenic, even before the onset of clinically significant CAD. 21 Furthermore, there is now epidemiological, genetic, and experimental data to support the concept that higher suPAR levels are associated with accelerated atherosclerosis, leading to adverse cardiovascular events, partly mediated through missense mutations (rs4760, p.Leu317Pro) of the gene encoding suPAR (plasminogen activator, urokinase receptor/PLAUR gene). 21 HsCRP is a well‐established marker that captures systemic inflammation in healthy individuals as well as in patients with CAD and is associated with increased risk of adverse CVD outcomes. 22

In the current study, we observed that AHF was independently associated with nearly 15% higher suPAR and 25% higher hsCRP levels in patients with CAD, even after adjustment for clinical covariates. These findings support the observations by Sjöwall et al 23 that suPAR levels directly correlate with the severity of hepatic fibrosis in patients with NAFLD and untreated hepatitis C virus infection. Moreover, a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrated that 3 inflammatory cytokines, hsCRP, interleukin‐1β, and tumor necrosis factor‐α, were significantly associated with nonalcoholic steatohepatitis and hepatic fibrosis in patients with NAFLD. 24 Furthermore, Ajmal and colleagues 25 have shown that hsCRP and tumor necrosis factor‐α level are associated with NAFLD severity in patients with CVD. Our findings are thus congruent with previous studies and build upon existing literature by highlighting a direct relationship between AHF, immune activation, and inflammation. This relationship has important implications for prognosis in patients with CAD as discussed below.

AHF and Adverse Outcomes in CAD

Our study demonstrates that AHF is independently associated with key adverse outcomes in patients with CAD. Specifically, patients with AHF were at nearly 1.5‐fold higher risk of all‐cause death, cardiovascular death, and the composite of cardiovascular death/nonfatal myocardial infarction as compared with those without AHF. This association was independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Our findings are consistent with those of Chen et al, 7 who recently reported that incremental FIB‐4 scores were associated with higher cardiovascular death in patients with established CAD. Multiple groups have since demonstrated that AHF is associated with a higher risk of CAD phenotypes, including stable atherosclerotic disease and acute coronary syndromes, and after percutaneous coronary intervention. 4 , 6 , 7 , 26 , 27

Immune Activation and Systemic Inflammation Mediate the Association Between AHF With Adverse Outcomes in CAD

Systemic immune activation and inflammation are important pathophysiologic pathways that contribute to progression of CAD and its adverse outcomes. The relationship of suPAR and hsCRP with adverse outcomes in CAD has been extensively evaluated. We have previously shown that elevation of circulating levels of these biomarkers are independent predictors of adverse CVD outcomes in patients with established CAD. 8 , 28 In the present study, we defined this relationship in the context of AHF, which by itself is independently associated with adverse outcomes in CAD as discussed above.

Herein, we demonstrate that AHF, suPAR, and hsCRP are independently associated with adverse outcomes in CAD. However, the addition of suPAR and hsCRP to survival models attenuates the association between AHF and adverse outcomes. Mediation analyses demonstrated that nearly half of the AHF's impact on outcomes was mediated by suPAR. The corresponding proportion for hsCRP was lower and statistically insignificant at 10%. This was likely because the mediation effect of suPAR on cardiovascular death was stronger and persisted after adjustment for confounders (including diabetes, obesity, and hypertension, among other cardiovascular comorbidities), whereas that of hsCRP was attenuated after adjusting for these shared risk factors. Moreover, suPAR levels reflect inflammation and immune dysregulation, whereas hsCRP levels are reflecting primarily inflammation The observations that activation of these important pathophysiologic pathways in AHF contribute to increased risk in CAD need further study, as it may have important mechanistic and therapeutic implications. In a related but smaller, retrospective, case–control study, Chen et al 29 observed that inflammation captured using the neutrophil count and neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio partly mediated the relationship of hepatic fibrosis with angiographic CAD severity measured using the Gensini score.

The role of immune activation, specifically as measured by suPAR levels in liver disease is an emerging area of investigation. Observational studies have reported higher suPAR levels in patients with untreated hepatitis B and C infection, and a graded incremental association of suPAR levels with the severity of fibrosis. 23 , 30 Chounta et al 31 also reported higher levels of suPAR in patients with primary and metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma and, in a prospective cohort of 267 healthy participants, found that those with higher supAR levels progressed more rapidly to hepatocellular carcinoma. However, the mechanism underlying the association between suPAR levels and liver disease remains incompletely understood. SuPAR (a membrane‐bound protein) is generated from its precursor urokinase plasminogen activator receptor by enzymatic cleavage of its glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchor and is most commonly expressed on immunologically active cells such as macrophages, neutrophils, and activated T lymphocytes, as well as on endothelial cells and podocytes. 32 While it has previously been shown in animal models of proteinuric kidney disease that elevated suPAR originates from immature bone marrow–derived myeloid cells, the source in liver disease remains unclear. 33 Limited data from in vitro and in vivo studies support the release of suPAR from monocytes, macrophages, and CD14+ peripheral peritoneal macrophages involved in hepatic inflammation, but not from hepatocytes themselves. 30 , 34

The association of heightened inflammation and hsCRP levels in liver disease is more established. 35 HsCRP is produced mainly by hepatocytes, and to some extent by smooth muscles, macrophages, and adipocytes, and has shown to correlate well with the severity of hepatic fibrosis. 22 Greater inflammation in turn plays an important role in promoting atherosclerosis by interacting with endothelial cells, cytokines, and low‐density lipoprotein particles, as well as promoting plaque instability and rupture and vascular thrombosis. 22

Clinical Implications

Patients with CAD remain at an increased risk for death and recurrent cardiovascular events despite adequate treatment of the disease and underlying risk factors. AHF is emerging as an important prognostic determinant in these patients. Our novel finding that the association of AHF with adverse outcomes in CAD is mediated by circulating levels of suPAR suggests that therapies targeting immune activation and systemic inflammation may help curb the excess cardiovascular risk in these patients. Anti‐inflammatory therapies including colchicine 36 and canakinumab 37 have already been proven to be beneficial in patients with CAD, while therapeutic strategies to target suPAR‐related atherosclerotic risk have shown promising results in preclinical models. 21 Our findings highlight that patients with concomitant CAD and AHF may be an ideal group to benefit from therapies targeting immune activation and systemic inflammation.

Strengths and Limitations

The notable strengths of our study include the large sample size of well‐phenotyped participants undergoing cardiac catheterization for angiographic evaluation of CAD. Study participants underwent long‐term follow‐up for adjudicated adverse events and biomarkers were measured at the time of enrollment. Limitations include, first, use of the FIB‐4 score and not liver biopsy, for defining AHF. However, contemporary studies have shown that a FIB‐4 score ≥2.67 accurately identifies patients with AHF, regardless of the cause of liver failure and can circumvent the need for a liver biopsy. 16 , 17 Second, the underlying cause of liver disease was also not identified. Third, the FIB‐4 score can be falsely elevated in conditions that cause thrombocytopenia. Fourth, the development of clinically significant liver events such as decompensated cirrhosis was not captured during follow‐up. Five, the mechanistic basis for higher immune activation and inflammation in patients with AHF was not evaluated. Finally, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded due to the observational design of this study.

Conclusions

AHF is independently associated with immune activation (measured using suPAR), systemic inflammation (measured using hsCRP), and adverse outcomes in patients with CAD. The association of AHF with adverse outcomes is partly mediated by immune activation. Therapeutic strategies targeting suPAR and hsCRP may help reduce the risk of adverse outcomes in the high‐risk group of patients with CAD and AHF.
