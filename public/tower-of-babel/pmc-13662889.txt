Improving Post‐Fall Assessment Documentation in Older Inpatients Using a Structured Falls Proforma: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Ella V Brockwell‐Mole, Dujinthan Jayabalan, Su Kitchen, Minoti Bhagat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveFalls among hospital inpatients are a major cause of morbidity, mortality, and healthcare burden. The aim of this study was to evaluate the association between the use of a structured Falls Proforma and the completeness of post‐fall assessment documentation in an Australian hospital.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on patients who experienced inpatient falls between January 2022 and December 2024. Fall events were stratified by 6‐month audit intervals, and cases were randomly selected within each stratum using computer‐generated sampling. All falls within this time frame were eligible for inclusion; only a patient's first fall among those aged > 65 years was counted, and data with missing details were excluded from analysis. Falls were categorized into “proforma” or “non‐proforma” groups. Documentation across 12 assessment domains was compared, and demographic and clinical characteristics associated with proforma use were evaluated.ResultsA stratified random sample of 202 inpatient falls was reviewed, with 167 eligible for analysis. Of these, 125 (75%) were assessed using the proforma. Documentation was significantly higher in the proforma group across examination domains: cervical spine (96.8% vs. 47.6%), hip (98.4% vs. 40.5%), cranial nerve (92.8% vs. 40.5%), upper limb neurology (93.6% vs. 45.2%), lower limb neurology (95.2% vs. 42.9%), external head (97.6% vs. 35.7%), cognition (95.2% vs. 19.0%), coagulopathy (98.4% vs. 16.7%), musculoskeletal (95.2% vs. 35.7%), mobility (92.0% vs. 7.1%), and contributing factors (87.2% vs. 59.5%) (all p < 0.001). Scenario documentation was high in both groups (85.6% vs. 95.2%, p = 0.100). In multivariable analysis, preexisting cognitive impairment was associated with lower odds of proforma use, however this association did not reach statistical significance after adjustment (OR = 0.450, 95% CI 0.190–1.030, p = 0.062).ConclusionsUse of a structured Falls Proforma was associated with significantly greater completeness of post‐fall documentation. However, lower uptake among patients with cognitive impairment may represent a disparity in documentation within a high‐risk population. Embedding structured tools into routine practice may standardize documentation and support adherence to recommended post fall assessment frameworks.Keywords: accidental falls, anticoagulants, cognitive dysfunction, cohort study, documentation, geriatrics

Introduction

Falls among hospital inpatients remain a major source of morbidity, mortality, and healthcare burden worldwide. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines falls as “an event which results in a person coming to rest inadvertently on the ground, floor or lower level” [1]. They estimate that falls are the second leading cause of unintentional injury deaths globally, accounting for more than 38 million disability‐adjusted life years lost [1]. Hospital inpatients are particularly vulnerable to falling due to frailty, comorbidities, polypharmacy, and environmental unfamiliarity [2]. In the acute care setting, fall rates are reported to be up to 1.5 per 1000 patient days, with substantially higher rates in geriatric and rehabilitation units where rates are reported as high as 8.1 per 1000 patient days [1]. The presence of cognitive impairment significantly elevates falls risk and complicates post‐fall recovery [3].

The consequences of falls are widespread and often profound. Beyond immediate injuries, falls often precipitate functional decline, increase the risk of subsequent falls and complicate discharge planning. Injuries occur in up to one‐third of cases, with fractures documented in 1%–3% of these [4]. Falls with associated fractures are linked to increased length of stay, higher costs, and significantly elevated mortality risk [5]. Outside the physical sequelae, psychological impacts such as fear of falling, loss of confidence and functional decline often lead to prolonged hospitalization [6].

Despite a breadth of research focusing on fall risk factors and prevention strategies, far less awareness has been devoted to systematic post‐fall management. Post‐fall care refers to the structured assessment, monitoring, and intervention undertaken after a patient experiences a fall [2]. Effective post‐fall management aims to address immediate injuries, identify contributing factors, and inform ongoing care planning [1]. Current guidelines stipulate that medical assessment of an inpatient fall should occur within a 4‐h time frame [2]. Tools such as structured falls proformas provide a standardized framework to guide this process, supporting consistent documentation of key assessment elements [2]. Its use may facilitate adherence to evidence‐based guidelines, enhance medicolegal defensibility, and enable robust audit and quality improvement processes [7].

Emerging evidence suggests that structured post‐fall protocols and proformas may improve consistency of post‐fall management and documentation [8]. Structured proformas are already embedded in several clinical areas, such as admissions, stroke, trauma, and surgical checklists, where they have been shown to improve documentation quality and standardize care [7, 9]. Despite this, variation in practice remains, and further research is required to establish standardized, evidence‐based interventions. Post‐fall care remains less standardized and poorly studied in Australian inpatient settings. This study therefore aimed to evaluate the association between falls proforma use and completeness of post‐fall documentation among older adults in an Australian center.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at an Australian hospital predominantly providing geriatric and rehabilitation services. The primary outcome of this study was to evaluate the association between use of a structured clinical assessment tool and completeness of post‐fall documentation. The secondary outcome was to assess if any demographic features predict the use of the proforma. The falls proforma is a recommended tool for use in the post‐fall assessment; however, it is not mandated across the hospital.

The study included a random selection of older adult patients who experienced a documented inpatient fall between January 2022 and December 2024. Events were identified through those that were logged on the Clinical Incident Management System (CIMs). In the study center, all inpatient falls must be logged on the CIMS system to allow auditing and quality improvement.

To ensure temporal representation across the study period, fall events were stratified into 6‐monthly audit intervals. Within each interval, eligible cases were assigned unique identifiers and randomly selected using computer‐generated random numbers. Approximately 35 falls were selected per interval, resulting in an initial sample of 202 fall events for review. As this was a retrospective exploratory cohort study, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. The sample size was determined by the number of eligible falls identified during the study period and the predefined stratified random sampling strategy.

Inclusion criteria required that the patient be admitted as an inpatient at the time of the fall and be over 65 years of age. Only the first inpatient fall throughout the admission was eligible. Exclusion criteria included duplicate events for the same patient, defined as any subsequent fall occurring after a case had already been included in the sample.

The exposure variable was defined as use of the structured falls proforma during post‐fall assessment. Cases documented exclusively using standard inpatient progress notes were classified as the non‐proforma group. Cases documented using the proforma alone or in combination with progress notes were classified within the proforma group, as the analytical focus was whether the structured tool had been utilized at all. A pre‐piloted data extraction form was developed by the authors. Data were retrospectively extracted from the hospital's paper medical records by a single investigator, ensuring consistency in the interpretation of documentation. Data was collected using a standardized template, de‐identified and stored in Microsoft Excel. Quality assurance procedures included double‐checking 10% of cases to ensure consistency.

Each fall event was evaluated for documentation across 12 predefined clinical assessment domains based on those that are documented in the falls proforma. These comprised cervical spine assessment, hip assessment, cranial nerve review, upper and lower limb neurological examinations, external head assessment, cognitive assessment, coagulopathy status, musculoskeletal assessment, mobility, scenario description, and contributing factors. These assessments were decided on at the time of implementation of the proforma based on national practice guidelines [2]. Data for each domain was recorded in binary format (yes/no). Where documentation was partially complete, the domain was coded as “yes” if it was evident that the assessment had been addressed to any extent. If no documentation was present for a given domain, it was recorded as “no.” Data was deidentified and allocated a unique study identifier number. Additional information on key demographic features was also recorded. This included sex, age at fall, time of fall, history of osteoporosis, history of coagulopathy or prior anticoagulation, background cognitive impairment, and the presence of polypharmacy. Polypharmacy was defined as more than seven medications. Time of fall was categorized into clinically meaningful periods. The day was categorized into three time periods that mostly represent medical shifts within this hospital. These time frames were day (08:00–16:00), evening (16:00–23:00), and night (23:00–08:00).

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. As all data were recorded binomially, categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using Pearson's chi‐squared tests. Continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges and compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test.

All analyses were conducted by the biostatisticians at the Kids Institute Research Department using R [10]. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Supporting Information) [11]. Univariate analyses were followed by a multivariable logistic regression model incorporating all relevant demographic variables, including age, gender, cognitive status, time of fall, polypharmacy, osteoporosis history, and coagulopathy. Odds ratios (OR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p‐values were reported. A two‐sided p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional research committee. As the study was retrospective and de‐identified, the need for informed consent to participate was waived by the institutional research committee.

Results

A total of 202 inpatient fall events were initially reviewed for inclusion in the study. Seven cases were excluded for not meeting the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, resulting in 195 eligible cases. Of these, 28 (14%) were excluded due to missing critical information, most commonly the location of assessment, resulting in a final analytic cohort of 167 patients (Figure 1).

Flow diagram showing selection of inpatient falls from the initial cohort to the 167 included cases, with a breakdown of cases assessed using the falls proforma versus standard documentation.

Falls were documented using one of three methods: 94 cases (56%) were recorded exclusively on the proforma, 42 cases (25%) in the inpatient progress notes only, and 31 cases (19%) were recorded in both. For the purpose of comparative analysis, the “proforma only” and “both” groups were combined into a single category “proforma group,” as the primary analytic interest was whether the proforma was used at all, rather than the exclusivity of its use. Based on this classification, 125 falls (75%) were included in the proforma group, and the remaining 42 falls (25%) formed the non‐proforma group. Demographic features of the cohort were consistent between the two groups with no significant differences between groups (Table 1).

Completeness of post‐fall assessment documentation differed substantially between the proforma and non‐proforma groups (Table 2 and Figure 2). Across all 12 clinical assessment domains, documentation completeness was higher among cases in which the proforma was used (Table 2 and Figure 2). Cervical spine assessment was recorded in 96.8% of cases in the proforma group, compared to 47.6% in the non‐proforma group (p < 0.001). Similarly, hip examination was completed in 98.4% versus 40.5% (p < 0.001), cranial nerve examination in 92.8% versus 40.5% (p < 0.001), and upper limb neurological examination in 93.6% versus 45.2% (p < 0.001), respectively. Lower limb neurological examination was recorded in 95.2% of proforma cases compared to 42.9% without a proforma (p < 0.001), and documentation of an external head assessment was present in 97.6% of proforma cases versus 35.7% in the non‐proforma group (p < 0.001). Cognitive assessment and evaluation of coagulopathy were also significantly more frequently documented when the proforma was used (95.2% vs. 19.0%, and 98.4% vs. 16.7%, respectively, both p < 0.001). Musculoskeletal examination (95.2% vs. 35.7%, p < 0.001) and mobility assessment (92.0% vs. 7.1%, p < 0.001) followed similar patterns. Notably, while the scenario in which the fall occurred was documented slightly more frequently in the non‐proforma group (95.2% vs. 85.6%), this was not statistically significant (p = 0.100). In contrast, contributing factors were more comprehensively documented in the proforma group (87.2% vs. 59.5%, p < 0.001).

A secondary objective of the study was to explore whether certain demographic or clinical characteristics were associated with proforma use. Variables included in the multivariable model were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and existing literature on inpatient falls. Logistic regression analysis, incorporating both bivariate and multivariate models, demonstrated a trend toward lower proforma utilization among patients with cognitive impairment, although this association did not reach statistical significance after adjustment (OR 0.450, 95% CI 0.190–1.030, p = 0.062). Other variables including age, gender, time of fall (day vs. evening vs. night), polypharmacy, and history of coagulopathy did not show a statistically significant association with whether the proforma was used (Table 3).

Discussion

This study found that use of a structured falls proforma was associated with substantially higher completeness of post‐fall assessment documentation across 11 of the 12 predefined clinical domains in an inpatient older adult population. The greatest gains were observed in the evaluation of background cognitive status, mobility after the fall, and the assessment of coagulopathy or anticoagulant use. These domains are particularly important in older adults because of their strong association with injury severity and clinical deterioration following falls. Structured assessment tools may facilitate more complete documentation by providing prompts for key assessment domains. In aging populations, where multimorbidity, frailty, and atypical presentations are common, structured assessment frameworks may serve as cognitive aids that reduce omission error and support safer documentation of important post‐fall assessment domains.

These findings contribute to the growing literature on structured post‐fall documentation practices in hospital inpatients. While fall prevention strategies are well documented, post‐fall care has historically been less standardized, despite its critical role in reducing harm and preventing recurrence [1]. Use of a falls proforma was associated with greater consistency and completeness of post‐fall assessment documentation, aligning with international recommendations and local guidelines [2, 12]. The Australian and World Fall Guidelines recommend that assessment of head, spine, and hip injuries happen in a timely manner, defined as between 30 min and 4 h, depending on the severity of the injury [2]. Without a standardized tool, documentation of these assessment steps may be inconsistent.

The literature consistently supports the need for a comprehensive post‐fall examination which extends beyond identifying immediate injury [13]. Our findings demonstrated substantially higher documentation completeness across 11 of the 12 predefined assessment domains when the proforma was used. By providing a clear prompt for each domain, the proforma may reduce reliance on memory and help standardize documentation variability between clinicians. Structured documentation may facilitate communication between staff and support quality improvement activities.

Patient‐related factors such as cognitive impairment, frailty and intrinsic risk‐taking behaviors continue to drive a large proportion of inpatient falls [3]. Patients with cognitive impairment appeared less likely to have a proforma completed in unadjusted analyses, however, this association was attenuated after multivariable adjustment. The observed trend toward lower proforma use among patients with cognitive impairment warrants further investigation. Cognitive impairment is a recognized risk factor for both sustaining and missing injuries after a fall. Patients with dementia or delirium are more likely to experience fractures and intracranial injuries [14]. Further research is required to determine whether barriers to proforma use differ among patients with cognitive impairment.

Similarly, documentation of anticoagulation status is an essential part of a post‐fall assessment, as coagulopathy is a well‐established risk factor for intracranial hemorrhage following a fall [15]. In this study, anticoagulation status was documented substantially more frequently when the proforma was used (98.4% vs. 16.7%, p < 0.001). This finding highlights the association between proforma use and more comprehensive recording of clinically relevant assessment domains. Early identification may facilitate timely imaging, monitoring, and consideration of reversal strategies where clinically appropriate [2, 15].

While proformas cannot eliminate falls, they may help standardize documentation following inpatient falls. High quality documentation also supports medico‐legal defensibility and contributes to continuous quality improvement initiatives [9].

Strengths of this study include the stratified random sampling across a 3‐year period, use of a standardized data collection template, and higher inter‐rater agreement in the quality‐assurance sample. Large absolute differences observed across multiple domains strengthen the observed association between proforma use and documentation completeness. Although conducted in a single Australian center, the hospital demographic reflects typical subacute geriatric and rehabilitation populations seen internationally, enhancing relevance to aging‐focused inpatient services.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, the study was conducted retrospectively within a single Australian geriatric and rehabilitation center, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other hospital settings and patient populations. Second, improved documentation completeness should not be interpreted as evidence of improved clinical care, as completion of the proforma reflects documentation behavior rather than actual clinical performance or patient outcomes. Furthermore, as proforma use was optional and clinician‐dependent, unmeasured confounding factors such as clinician experience, staffing levels, workload, and ward culture may have influenced both proforma utilization and documentation quality. Exclusion of cases with missing classification variables may have introduced selection bias, potentially favoring more completely documented events. Although having a single data collector improved consistency, it also does introduce the potential for misclassification bias. This was mitigated by introducing quality assurance measures. Finally, as no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, the study may have been underpowered to detect smaller associations within the multivariable analysis.

Prospective, multi‐center studies are warranted to assess whether improved documentation using the proforma translates into improved clinical outcomes such as reduced fall recurrence, injury rates, or length of stay. Integration of the proforma into electronic medical records, coupled with real‐time prompts and mandatory fields, could enhance uptake and completeness. Qualitative research could explore barriers to proforma use in cognitively impaired patients and inform tool adaptation.

Conclusions

Given the rising burden of inpatient falls, structured documentation tools warrant consideration as part of broader post‐fall management strategies. The use of a structured falls proforma was associated with substantially greater completeness of post‐fall assessment documentation in an Australian inpatient setting. Consistent with national and international guideline recommendations, structured tools may represent a practical approach to standardizing post‐fall documentation. While this study did not assess clinical outcomes such as fracture detection, intracranial injury, or length of stay, improved documentation completeness may support more consistent post‐fall assessment practices. Whether improved documentation completeness translates into improved patient outcomes requires evaluation in prospective studies.
