Salivary microbiome in patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia: altered composition and its association with prostate-specific antigen

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsMicrobial dysbiosis in the gut and urine has been implicated in patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) in previous studies. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests that salivary dysbiosis is associated with various diseases and oral health conditions. Building on these findings, this study aimed to investigate whether BPH patients exhibit a distinct salivary microbiome.Methods and materialsWe recruited a cohort of BPH patients (n = 50) and age- and BMI-matched healthy controls (HC; n = 50). Salivary samples were collected for 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels were measured.ResultsComparing the salivary microbial communities between BPH patients and HC revealed significant differences, with BPH patients exhibiting increased bacterial richness and diversity (FDR < 0.05). Further analysis identified 13 bacterial genera, including Clostridia-UCG-014, Oribacterium, and Filifactor, that were enriched in BPH patients. In contrast, 4 bacterial genera, such as Actinomyces, Lachnoanaerobaculum, and Rothia, were depleted in BPH patients (FDR < 0.05). Several bacterial genera showed potential as biomarkers for identifying BPH. Importantly, no correlation was observed between the bacterial community and PSA levels in BPH patients (P > 0.05).ConclusionsOur findings indicate that the salivary microbiome in BPH patients is dysbiotic, with altered richness, diversity, and specific taxonomic changes compared to healthy controls. Importantly, although prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels are known to increase with prostate volume, our analysis found no significant association between the salivary microbiome and PSA levels in BPH patients. This suggests that while salivary dysbiosis is a feature of BPH, it does not appear to contribute directly to PSA dysregulation. Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a prevalent condition that affects more than 50% of men over the age of 60 [1]. BPH can lead to serious medical complications, including urinary retention, prostatectomy, urinary tract infections, bladder calculi, hematuria, and even renal failure [1]. Despite the significant impact of BPH on men’s quality of life, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Metabolic syndrome and inflammation have been implicated as key contributors to BPH; however, the precise factors leading to metabolic disorders and inflammation are still debated [2–4]. Recently, growing evidence suggests that the human microbiome may play a role in modulating metabolites and inflammation [5, 6], positioning microorganisms in body cavities as a novel focus for studying the pathology of BPH.

To date, most research has focused on the gut and urinary microbiomes in relation to BPH. The gut microbiome has been linked to BPH, as demonstrated by Li et al., who found increased bacterial diversity and distinct differences in several bacterial families and genera in BPH rats compared to controls [7]. Studies on the urinary microbiome have similarly identified significant differences in bacterial composition between individuals with chronic prostatitis and controls. For instance, Shoskes et al. reported higher phylogenetic alpha diversity in the urinary microbiomes of patients with chronic prostatitis, with this diversity linked to measures of disease severity and clinical phenotype [8].

The oral cavity also harbors a substantial microbiota population, and previous studies have established strong associations between oral dysbiosis and clinical parameters of oral diseases, such as gingival inflammation [9], bleeding on probing, and periodontal destruction [10]. Moreover, the effects of oral dysbiosis extend beyond oral health, including Alzheimer’s disease [11], Coronary Artery Disease [12], Arthritis [13], Chronic Kidney Disease [14] Systemic Lupus Erythematosus [15] and Colitis [16] are linked to the oral dysbiosis. Despite the substantial body of research on the oral microbiome and its systemic impacts, no studies have yet explored the potential connection between BPH and the salivary microbiome.

Prostate-specific antigen (PSA), which is synthesized and secreted by the prostate acinar epithelial cells, is recognized as an inflammatory marker [17]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that prostatic inflammation significantly contributes to elevated serum levels of PSA [18, 19]. Recently, research has reported associations between microbial diversity and taxonomy signatures in the gut microbiome and serum PSA levels in men [20].

Given the significant impact of the salivary microbiome on human health, investigating the bacterial communities in saliva and their potential role in BPH could unveil a new factor influencing the pathophysiology and management of this condition. Salivary samples are easily accessible and represent a promising diagnostic tool. Therefore, evaluating the potential predictive role of the salivary microbiome in BPH offers a new and promising avenue for research.

Methods and materials

Study subjects’ enrollment

This study was designed as a cross-sectional, multicenter investigation. The participants were recruited from Wuxi No. 2 People’s Hospital, The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, and Huishan District People’s Hospital. The research protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Wuxi Affiliated Second Hospital, Nanjing Medical University (ref. 2021Y8), and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. The sample size was determined based on the observed abundance of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and the desired level of differentiation between ASVs. To calculate the sample size, we utilized a web application developed by Mattiello et al. [21], specifically designed for estimating sample sizes in human microbiome studies. According to the calculations, with a power of 0.80, the required sample size was estimated to be 32 (Figure S1).

Patients were diagnosed with BPH according to the 2011 Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Prostate Hyperplasia. The Criteria for BPH diagnosis were: (1) males over 50 years of age; (2) complaints of urinary tract symptoms; (3) evidence of prostate enlargement on digital rectal examination; and (4) B-mode ultrasound showing a prostate volume of ≥ 31 mL [22]. After initial screening based on these criteria, patients were confirmed to be free of prostate cancer through prostatic biopsies. Exclusion criteria for both BPH and healthy controls (HC) included: a history of prostate cancer before the study or a diagnosis of prostate cancer during the study, sexually transmitted infections, prostate and lower urinary tract surgery, radiation therapy, heart, liver, or kidney failure, drug or alcohol addiction, neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction, and the use of antibiotics or probiotics in the past 30 days. Additionally, men in the HC group were free of any urinary tract symptoms.

In this study, the oral hygiene practices of both BPH patients and healthy controls were thoroughly assessed. It was observed that individuals from both groups consistently maintained good oral hygiene, specifically brushing their teeth after every meal.

Sample collection, DNA isolation, and bioinformatic analysis

One milliliter of saliva was collected from each participant into RNA-free sterile containers after the participants refrained from eating, drinking, smoking, or brushing their teeth for at least one hour prior to collection. The saliva samples were immediately processed by centrifuging at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to pellet the microbial cells. After centrifugation, the supernatant was mostly decanted, and the remaining pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of the residual saliva. This suspension was transferred to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube and centrifuged again at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. Following this, the supernatant was carefully removed, and 500 µL of lysis buffer was added. The composition of the lysis buffer was as follows: 0.1 M Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 2 mM EDTA, and 2% SDS. After the lysis buffer was added, the samples were stored in a −80 °C freezer until DNA extraction, with a storage period of less than 60 days. This lysis buffer has been validated by Guhe Biotechnology Co., Ltd., showing no significant difference in bacterial DNA degradation between fresh samples and those stored in the lysis buffer for up to 60 days (https://www.guhejk.com/wordpress/?p=223). To ensure efficient and complete cell lysis, the samples underwent three freeze–thaw cycles, alternating between liquid nitrogen and a 65 °C water bath.

For DNA isolation, the AMPure XP magnetic bead system (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, USA) was employed. Specifically, 40 µL of Agencourt AMPure XP beads were added to 80 µL of the salivary pellet, vortexed for 30 s, and incubated at room temperature for five minutes to bind DNA. The samples were then placed into a magnetic separator for five minutes, after which the supernatant was carefully removed. The beads were washed twice with 180 µL of 80% ethanol for 30 s, with magnetic separation between washes. Finally, the DNA was eluted with 30 µL of elution buffer, and the DNA-containing supernatant was transferred to a clean tube for further analysis.

PCR Amplification and Quality Control: The bacterial 16S rRNA gene V3-V4 region was PCR-amplified using universal primers 319 F and 806R for 30 cycles. The PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

The quality of the PCR products was assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis, inspecting for visible bands of the expected size (~ 400 base pairs). Samples that did not display clear bands of the correct size were excluded from further analysis. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Purification and Sequencing: PCR amplicons were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, USA) to remove impurities. DNA was quantified using the PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) to ensure consistent concentration. Amplicons with inadequate quality or those shorter than 400 base pairs were excluded from further analysis.

The purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar concentrations to ensure equal representation of all samples. Sequencing was conducted using the Illumina MiSeq platform, which generated paired-end reads for downstream bioinformatics processing.

To account for potential contamination, twelve negative control samples (lacking either saliva or salivary DNA) were included in the analysis. Contaminant sequences were identified and removed using Decontam v.1.2.1 with a threshold of P < 0.10 to ensure the integrity of the microbiome data. This approach is widely accepted for distinguishing between true biological signals and potential contaminants in low-biomass microbiome studies.

Bioinformatic analyses were performed using the QIIME2 pipeline, following standard microbiome protocols. The raw sequencing data were demultiplexed and quality-filtered using the DADA2 pipeline, which performs error correction, removes chimeric sequences, and denoises reads, resulting in high-quality amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Samples with fewer than 2,000 reads were excluded to maintain adequate sequencing depth and minimize the risk of data bias due to insufficient sampling. R package version 4.3.2 was used for additional bioinformatic and statistical analysis [23].

Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was performed using the Silva database (v138) at a 97% similarity threshold, ensuring robust identification of bacterial taxa. Chimeric sequences were further identified and removed using VSEARCH (v2.3.4), refining the ASV table for downstream analysis.

Alpha diversity was assessed to evaluate bacterial richness and diversity within each sample using multiple indices, including the Abundance-based Coverage Estimator (ACE) and Chao 1 for richness, and the Shannon and Simpson indices for diversity. These metrics provide insight into both the total number of taxa and the evenness of their distribution within samples. To account for variations in sequencing depth, rarefaction was applied to ensure that all samples were compared at an equal read count, thus avoiding biases associated with differing sequencing depth.

Beta diversity was assessed using Principal-Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis Dissimilarity indices, allowing for the visualization of differences in microbial community composition between BPH patients and healthy controls. Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) was used to evaluate the statistical significance of these differences, testing whether community composition was associated with the primary grouping factor (BPH vs. control), while also accounting for within-group variability.

To identify bacterial taxa that significantly differentiate BPH patients from healthy controls, we employed Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LefSe). This method highlights taxa exhibiting statistically significant differences in relative abundance between the two groups, providing potential microbial biomarkers associated with BPH. For this analysis, a log LDA score threshold of 3.5 was applied to identify robust biomarkers, focusing on the most biologically relevant taxa.

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed in R Studio (version 3.6.3), utilizing microbiome analysis packages such as phyloseq and vegan. To account for potential confounding variables, study participants were categorized based on demographic factors, including age, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, comorbidities, and medication history for the BPH group. Adonis analysis (permutational multivariate analysis of variance) was conducted to determine whether any of these variables significantly impacted salivary microbiome composition beyond the primary grouping of BPH vs. control.

Blood samples collection and processing

Blood samples were collected on the day of saliva sample collection. Prostate-specific antigen (PSA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, was quantified using an automated chemiluminescent analyzer Unicel Dxl 800 (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Statistical analysis

Fisher’s exact test or Student’s t-test was applied using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) to compare clinical variables between the BPH and HC groups. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare alpha diversity indices and bacterial abundances, with a Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. An FDR of < 0.05 was used as the threshold for statistical significance in comparative tests.

Additionally, a random forest algorithm was employed to identify bacterial genera predictive of BPH. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate diagnostic bacterial genera, with an area under the curve (AUC) above 0.7 considered acceptable for diagnostic purposes and an AUC between 0.8 and 0.9 classified as excellent [24]. Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to assess correlations between the relative abundances of bacterial genera and PSA profiles in BPH samples; correlations with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics and salivary microbiome characterization

A total of 100 salivary samples were included in the analysis, consisting of 50 samples from patients with BPH and 50 samples from age- and BMI-matched controls (Table 1). As expected, BPH patients had significantly higher levels of total PSA (TPSA) and free PSA (FPSA), as well as a lower FPSA/TPSA ratio compared to the controls (P < 0.05).

In this study, we employed a rigorous methodology for DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene amplification to ensure high data quality. The AMPure XP magnetic bead system was used for high-purity DNA isolation, with PCR amplification targeting the V3-V4 region using universal primers. Quality control was maintained by excluding samples with inadequate PCR products (less than 400 base pairs) and applying stringent thresholds during sequencing data processing. Specifically, the DADA2 pipeline was used for error correction and chimera removal, and Decontam software (P < 0.10) was applied to eliminate potential contaminants, ensuring reliable microbial profiling.

After excluding samples that did not display visible bands of the expected size (approximately 400 base pairs) in PCR, we characterized the bacterial composition of salivary samples from 97 individuals, including 47 diagnosed with BPH and 50 healthy controls. From the initial dataset, a total of 16,958,259 raw reads were identified, and after removing low-quality or ambiguous reads, 15,204,023 valid reads remained. After rarefaction and the removal of contaminants (with 9 ASVs removed), we identified a total of 19,540 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). The Good’s coverage index indicated 100% coverage for each sample, demonstrating comprehensive detection of ASVs.

BPH patients had a different bacterial community

The results of the Adonis analysis indicate that, aside from the primary grouping variable (BPH vs. HC; P < 0.05), none of these demographic factors—including medication usage—significantly influenced the salivary microbiome (P > 0.05; Figure S2). When comparing the salivary microbiomes of BPH patients to HC, we found significant differences, with the salivary microbiome in BPH patients showing distinct characteristics (R2 = 0.134, FDR < 0.001; Fig. 1A). Additionally, a dendrogram based on the ASV-level Bray–Curtis index revealed that nearly all samples from BPH patients and controls clustered separately (Figure S3). The distinct microbiome in BPH patients was also reflected in the number of shared ASVs. Specifically, 19,540 ASVs were detected, with the BPH group sharing only 16.40% of the total ASVs with HC group (3,205 out of 19,540) (Fig. 1B).

The salivary microbiome of BPH patients was significantly richer, as measured by ACE and Chao 1 indices, than those of the HC group (FDR < 0.001; Fig. 1C). BPH patients also exhibited significantly higher microbial diversity, as estimated by Shannon and Simpson indices (FDR < 0.001; Fig. 1C).

Differences in Bacterial Composition Between BPH and HC Patients.

At the genus level, the top five most abundant bacteria in the BPH group were Streptococcus (24.36%), Prevotella (12.77%), Veillonella (10.54%), Neisseria (10.04%), and Leptotrichia (4.53%). In contrast, the dominant bacterial genera in the HC group included Streptococcus (28.22%), Neisseria (11.41%), Prevotella (11.30%), Rothia (8.34%), and Veillonella (6.26%; Fig. 2B).

Differences in bacterial phyla and genera in BPH patients

When comparing the bacterial phyla between the BPH and HC group, 6 bacterial phyla were significantly increased in the BPH group, including Campilobacterota, Desulfobacterota, Fusobacteriota, Patescibacteria, Spirochaetota and Synergistota (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 3A). Meanwhile, two phyla—Actinobacteriota and Cyanobacteria— were significantly decreased in the BPH group (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 3A).

We compared bacterial genera with relative abundances greater than 1% of the total abundance between the BPH and HC group. In the BPH group, 13 bacterial genera were significantly enriched, including Campylobacter, Candidatus-Saccharimonas, Capnocytophaga, Clostridia-UCG-014, Filifactor, Leptotrichia, Megasphaera, Oribacterium, Selenomonas, Treponema, Veillonella and a genus from the bacterial family of Saccharimonadaceae (FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 3B). In contrast, 4 bacterial genera, such as Actinomyces, Lachnoanaerobaculum, Rothia, and a genus from the bacterial order of Lactobacillales, were significantly depleted in the BPH group (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 3B).

Bacterial taxa as biomarkers for BPH patients

To identify potential biomarkers for BPH in saliva, we used the LEfSe package to analyze significant variations in the bacterial profiles of both groups across different hierarchical levels. ASVs with LDA scores ≥ 3.5 and the Kruskal–Wallis test P < 0.05 were considered (Fig. 4A & 4B). Several bacterial phyla, including Spirochaetota, Fusobacteriota, and Patescibacteria, were identified as biomarkers for BPH. Similarly, several bacterial genera, such as Capnocytophaga, Leptotrichia, Selenomonas, Treponema, and Veillonella, were found to be important biomarkers for identifying BPH.

Next, we employed a random forest algorithm to identify the bacterial genera above 1% of the total abundance that could serve as classifiers for BPH patients. Treponema, Selenomonas, and others were identified as classifiers for BPH (Figure S4). Furthermore, 7 bacterial genera, including Filifactor, Campylobacter, and Veillonella, were considered acceptable diagnostic markers for BPH, with 2 genera—Capnocytophaga and Treponema—deemed excellent diagnostic biomarkers (Figure S5).

Altered Salivary Microbiome Not Associated with Serum PSA Levels in BPH Patients.

To determine whether the altered salivary microbiome in BPH patients was associated with serum PSA levels, we performed Pearson correlation analysis. No significant correlation was found between the altered bacterial taxa in BPH patients and PSA levels. For instance, the increase in Campylobacter in BPH patients was not significantly correlated with serum FPSA (r = 0.09, P = 0.051; Fig. 5A). Similarly, the increase in Oribacterium was not significantly correlated with the FPSA/TPSA ratio (r = −0.06, P = 0.130; Fig. 5B).

Discussion

Our study is the first to profile the salivary microbiome in BPH patients without prostate cancer, confirmed through biopsy. While previous studies have explored the potential of the gut and urine microbiomes as diagnostic tools for BPH [7, 8], we aimed to assess whether the salivary microbiome could also serve as a potential biomarker. However, our analysis found no significant association between the salivary microbiome and PSA levels, which are known to increase with prostate volume [25].

Using PCoA clustering, we observed distinct bacterial communities in the saliva of our BPH patients. This finding aligns with a previous study by Shoskes et al., who demonstrated different microbial communities in the gut and urine of men with chronic prostatitis [8, 26]. It is important to note that the patients in Shoskes’ studies were not specifically diagnosed with BPH through biopsies. However, it is possible that some of them may have had BPH, as a significant proportion of men diagnosed with BPH also experience prostatitis-like symptoms, and many BPH patients have a history of prostatitis [27].

Higher bacterial diversity in disease states has been observed in several studies, particularly in both oral and systemic conditions. For example, in oral disease states, higher bacterial diversity often reflects a shift in the microbial community associated with poorer oral health, as demonstrated by Chakraborty et al. where disease subjects had higher bacterial diversity compared to healthy controls [28]. Similarly, Cho et al. suggested that disturbances in microbial diversity are linked to immune responses and disease states [29]. In the case of BPH, the salivary microbiome might undergo changes due to systemic inflammation or immune modulation, leading to higher microbial diversity. This phenomenon is not always consistent across different disease states but could be a specific response to certain pathological conditions. The higher bacterial diversity we observed in BPH patients might reflect an adaptive microbial shift influenced by local or systemic factors, similar to how microbiome changes have been reported in other diseases.

In Figs. 3A and 3B, we observed a sharp decline in the abundance of Actinobacteriota and its bacterial genus members, Actinomyces and Rothia in the BPH group. The roles of Actinobacteriota and these genera in maintaining oral health are difficult to determine based on current evidence. As evidenced by Wang et al., Actinobacteriota could be serve as a potential biomarker in the saliva of patients with hypertension, indicating its association with systemic conditions rather than oral health alone [30]. Conversely, Vieira et al. found that Actinobacteriota levels increased in diabetic patients with lower salivary glucose [31], while Rao et al. observed a depletion of Actinobacteriota in patients with cholangiocarcinoma [32]. Furthermore, Actinomyces and Rothia were dominant in the oral cavities of patients with hematological cancers [33] and cirrhotic patients with pneumonia [34], yet Actinomyces levels were lower in the subgingival microbiomes of patients with chronic periodontitis [35]. Additionally, Rothia was enriched in patients with oral leukoplakia, as shown in two previous studies [36, 37]. Further research, including animal transplantation studies, could provide insights into the role of Actinomyces and Rothia in health and disease.

We found that several bacterial genera from bacterial class of Clostridia were biomarkers for identifying BPH patients, with members such as Capnocytophaga, Clostridia-UCG-014, Oribacterium, and Filifactor enriched in the BPH group. Capnocytophaga, known for its role in the pathogenesis of periodontal disease [21], was identified as a diagnostic biomarker by random forest classifier, with excellent classifier value. Further large-scale studies are needed to confirm whether Capnocytophaga could be used as a diagnostic tool in clinical settings. In the human gut, the Clostridia class within the Firmicutes phylum contains species that contribute to gut health and metabolic processes, such as Clostridium butyricum, which produces butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid known for its anti-inflammatory properties [38]. Given the potential health impact of Clostridia members, further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate the diagnostic potential of specific genera within this class.

The enrichment of Filifactor in BPH patients supports the hypothesis that this genus may be pathogenic in the oral cavity, as it has been associated with periodontitis [39, 40] and peri-implantitis [41].

Additionally, our study revealed a depletion of P5D1-392, a bacterium from the Lactobacillales order with potential probiotic properties, in the saliva of BPH patients. Lactobacillales members are known for their production of lactic acid and antioxidant properties, which can inhibit the growth of pathogenic microorganisms [42, 43].

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of our study. We conducted an observational analysis focusing on the saliva of BPH patients, limiting our ability to establish the role of differentiated bacteria in the onset and progression of BPH. Future studies involving animal models and microbiome transplantation experiments could provide deeper insights into the role of the salivary microbiome in BPH. Additionally, our study included only BPH patients without oral diseases, preventing us from exploring the impact of the salivary microbiome on oral health in BPH patients.

Conclusions

Our study reveals a distinct salivary microbiome in BPH patients, marked by significant shifts in richness, diversity, and specific bacterial taxa compared to healthy controls. These alterations highlight the salivary microbiome's promise as a non-invasive diagnostic marker for BPH. Notably, we found no significant correlation between this microbial shift and serum PSA levels. This key finding suggests that while salivary dysbiosis is a feature of BPH, it is likely not a direct driver of PSA dysregulation. Unraveling the precise role of the salivary microbiome in BPH pathogenesis will require further mechanistic studies.
