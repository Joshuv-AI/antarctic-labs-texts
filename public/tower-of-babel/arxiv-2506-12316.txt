The empirical discrete copula process
Gery Geenens, Ivan Kojadinovic, Tommaso Martini

Abstract
This paper develops a general inferential framework for discrete copulas on finite supports in any dimension. The copula of a multivariate discrete distribution is defined as Csiszar’s ℐ {\cal I} ‐projection (i.e., the minimum-Kullback–Leibler divergence projection) of its joint probability array onto the polytope of uniform-margins probability arrays of the same size, and its empirical estimator is obtained by applying that same projection to the array of empirical frequencies observed on the sample. Under the assumption of random sampling, strong consistency and n \sqrt{n} -asymptotic normality of the empirical copula array is established, with an explicit ‘sandwich’ form for its covariance. The theory is illustrated by deriving the large-sample distribution of Yule’s concordance coefficient (the natural analogue of Spearman’s ρ S \rho_{\text{S}} for bivariate discrete distributions) and by constructing a test for quasi-independence in multivariate contingency tables. Our results not only complete the foundations of discrete-copula inference but also connect directly to entropically regularised optimal transport and other minimum-divergence problems.

Introduction

The statistical theory and practice of characterising and quantifying dependence between random variables has long been dominated by Pearson’s product-moment correlation, which virtually served as the only measure of dependence during most of the 20th century. It has now become well understood that Pearson’s correlation, inherently anchored in the ideal world of multivariate Gaussian distributions, is often unsuitable for ‘the real world’, where data are seldom Gaussian. As a result, the early 21st century has witnessed the resurgence of the search for modern, robust and flexible sets of tools for dependence modelling. A major step in that direction has surely been the advent of copulas.

The copula approach stems from a representation theorem due to
Sklar 1959
, which suggests that the dependence structure in any random vector
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
is encapsulated in a continuous
d
-variate function
C_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}}
said
copula
, and this in a way insensitive to the individual behaviour of each
X_{k}
(
k=1,\ldots,d
). This property, often referred to as ‘
margin-freeness
’, allows the construction of very flexible multivariate models by separately specifying marginal distributions for the
X_{k}
’s (
k=1,\ldots,d
) on one hand and the dependence/copula on the other, and is often presented as the main benefit offered by the copula approach
(
Joe 2015
, Section 1.6)
. What is often overlooked, however, is that this appealing property arises only when all the variables
X_{k}
are continuous. Indeed, in the presence of some elements of discreteness, ‘
everything that can go wrong, will go wrong
’, as put by
Embrechts 2009
. The major stumbling block is that copulas lose their margin-free nature outside the ‘all-continuous’ setting, hence their main reason-of-being as Sklar’s formalism does not allow neat separation between marginals and dependence anymore
(
Geenens 2024
)
.

To reconcile copulas with the discrete setting,
Geenens 2020
argued that they should not be imprisoned in
Sklar 1959
’s representation, but should be understood from a more fundamental perspective as the representative of an equivalence class of distributions sharing the same dependence structure. Inspired by century-old ideas of Udny Yule
(
Yule 1912
)
, that different interpretation led to an alternative copula construction where all the pleasant properties of copulas for modelling dependence (in particular: ‘margin-freeness’) could smoothly carry over to the discrete setting – see Section
2
.
Geenens 2020
only laid the foundations of this ‘discrete copula methodology’ but did not investigate empirical estimation or inference for the induced discrete copula models. That task was taken up in
Kojadinovic and Martini 2024
, who addressed a variety of scenarios, from nonparametric, semi-parametric and parametric estimation of discrete copula models to goodness-of-fit tests, but in the bivariate case (
d=2
) only and under a possibly restrictive rectangular support condition.

In this paper, we address the inference problem for discrete copula models in any dimension
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, under assumptions as broad as possible. The only significant restriction is that we considered distributions with finite supports – the case of (countably) infinite support presents unique challenges, which will be investigated in a subsequent paper. Our main theoretical contribution is Theorem
4.1
below, which can be regarded as the discrete analogue of the weak convergence of the empirical copula process in the classical ‘all-continuous’ case
(
Fermanian et al. 2004
,
Segers 2012
)
. That result is arguably the theoretical cornerstone of all inferential results for continuous copulas, and the same essential role is expected for Theorem
4.1
in discrete settings.

The paper is organised as follows. Section
2
provides a brief refresher of
Geenens 2020
’s discrete copula in the bivariate case, which is aimed at facilitating the transition towards the novel multidimensional discrete copula construction introduced in Section
3
. Section
4
studies a nonparametric estimator of
d
-dimensional discrete copulas, and exposes its asymptotic properties which include the above-mentioned asymptotic normality statement of central importance (Theorem
4.1
). Section
5
illustrates the usefulness of the previous result by displaying some examples of application. In particular, the asymptotic distribution of Yule’s coefficient of concordance – which may be thought of as the discrete version of Spearman’s
\rho

(
Geenens 2020
, Section 6.6)
– is derived in Section
5.1
, while a test procedure for the hypothesis of quasi-independence between discrete variables is proposed in Section
5.2
. Section
6
concludes.

2
Bivariate discrete copulas

Consider two finite sets
{\cal X}_{1}=\{x^{(1)}_{1},x^{(1)}_{2},\ldots,x^{(1)}_{r_{1}}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\cal X}_{2}=\{x^{(2)}_{1},x_{2}^{(2)},\ldots,x^{(2)}_{r_{2}}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, with
2\leq r_{1},r_{2}<\infty
and
x^{(1)}_{1}<x^{(1)}_{2}<\ldots<x^{(1)}_{r_{1}}
,
x^{(2)}_{1}<x_{2}^{(2)}<\ldots<x^{(2)}_{r_{2}}
. The distribution of any bivariate discrete vector
(X_{1},X_{2})
taking values in
{\cal X}={\cal X}_{1}\times{\cal X}_{2}
is a probability measure
P
which may be unequivocally characterised by its probability mass function (pmf); viz. the function
\mathsf{p}:{\cal X}\to[0,1]
which, to any
(x_{i_{1}}^{(1)},x_{i_{2}}^{(2)})\in{\cal X}
(
(i_{1},i_{2})\in\{1,\ldots,r_{1}\}\times\{1,\ldots,r_{2}\}
) assigns the probability
\mathsf{p}(x_{i_{1}}^{(1)},x_{i_{2}}^{(2)})=P(\{x_{i_{1}}^{(1)},x_{i_{2}}^{(2)}\})={\mathbb{P}}(X_{1}=x_{i_{1}}^{(1)},X_{2}=x_{i_{2}}^{(2)})
. Such pmf can be broken down into three elements:
(i)
what will be called the
probability array

{\mathbf{p}}=\begin{pmatrix}p_{11}&p_{12}&\ldots&p_{1r_{2}}\\
p_{21}&p_{22}&\ldots&p_{2r_{2}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
p_{r_{1}1}&p_{r_{1}2}&\ldots&p_{r_{1}r_{2}}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times r_{2}},

(2.1)

where for
(i_{1},i_{2})\in\{1,\ldots,r_{1}\}\times\{1,\ldots,r_{2}\}
,
p_{i_{1}i_{2}}\doteq\mathsf{p}(x_{i_{1}}^{(1)},x_{i_{2}}^{(2)})
;
(ii)
a
rank function

\Phi_{1}:{\cal X}_{1}\to\{1,\ldots,r_{1}\}
and
(iii)
a rank function
\Phi_{2}:{\cal X}_{2}\to\{1,\ldots,r_{2}\}
; such that
\Phi_{\ell}(x^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell}})=i_{\ell}
, for
\ell=1,2
and
i_{\ell}\in\{1,\ldots,r_{\ell}\}
. We have
\mathsf{p}(x_{i_{1}}^{(1)},x_{i_{2}}^{(2)})=p_{\Phi_{1}(x^{(1)}_{i_{1}})\Phi_{2}(x^{(2)}_{i_{2}})}
, or equivalently, as
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
are bijective,
p_{i_{1}i_{2}}=\mathsf{p}\left(\Phi_{1}^{-1}(i_{1}),\Phi_{2}^{-1}(i_{2})\right)
, for all
(i_{1},i_{2})
.

Unlike the pmf, the probability array
{\mathbf{p}}
does not carry any information about the initial domains
{\cal X}_{1}
and
{\cal X}_{2}
, which are only determined by
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
. In fact,
{\mathbf{p}}
can be understood as what have in common the distributions of all vectors
(X_{1}^{\prime},X_{2}^{\prime})=(\Psi_{1}(X_{1}),\Psi_{2}(X_{2}))
where
\Psi_{1}:{\cal X}_{1}\to{\cal X}^{\prime}_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}:{\cal X}_{2}\to{\cal X}_{2}^{\prime}
are any two increasing functions, and
{\cal X}^{\prime}_{1}
and
{\cal X}^{\prime}_{2}
are arbitrary sets of cardinalities
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, respectively. This follows from the fact that the ranks returned by
\Phi_{\ell}
(
\ell=1,2
) are maximally invariant under the above group of increasing transformations
(
Lehmann and Romano 2005
, Example 6.2.2(ii))
. Thus, if a representation or quantification of the dependence in the vector
(X_{1},X_{2})
is to be invariant under such increasing marginal transformations – which is precisely what is called ‘margin-free’ in the copula literature – then it must be based on
{\mathbf{p}}
and on
{\mathbf{p}}
only. Indeed, the rank functions (and their inverses) only serve a purpose of labelling the possible values of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
but should not play any further role in understanding their joint behaviour and interactions.

We will denote
\bm{p}^{(1)}=(p_{1\bullet},p_{2\bullet},\ldots,p_{r_{1}\bullet})
and
\bm{p}^{(2)}=(p_{\bullet 1},p_{\bullet 2},\ldots,p_{\bullet r_{2}})
the
margins
of
{\mathbf{p}}
:
p_{i_{1}\bullet}=\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{r_{2}}p_{i_{1}i_{2}}={\mathbb{P}}(X_{1}=x_{i_{1}}^{(1)})
and
p_{\bullet i_{2}}=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}p_{i_{1}i_{2}}={\mathbb{P}}(X_{2}=x_{i_{2}}^{(2)})
. Without loss of generality, it will be assumed throughout that
p_{i_{1}\bullet}p_{\bullet i_{2}}>0\ \forall(i_{1},i_{2})
; i.e., no row or column of
{\mathbf{p}}
is identically null (otherwise these could simply be deleted entirely). Finally, we will define the
support
of
{\mathbf{p}}
as
{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}=\Supp({\mathbf{p}})=\{(i_{1},i_{2})\in\{1,\ldots,r_{1}\}\times\{1,\ldots,r_{2}\}:p_{i_{1}i_{2}}>0\}
.

Now, let
\Gamma
be the set of
(r_{1}\times r_{2})
-probability arrays (
2.1
) whose margins are uniform; viz.

\Gamma\doteq\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times r_{2}}:\forall i_{1},i_{2},\ \gamma_{i_{1}i_{2}}\geq 0,\gamma_{i_{1}\bullet}=\frac{1}{r_{1}},\gamma_{\bullet i_{2}}=\frac{1}{r_{2}}\right\}.

Note that
\Gamma
is a convex subset of a vector space of dimension
(r_{1}-1)(r_{2}-1)
, known as a generalised
Birckhoff polytope

(
Brualdi 2006
,
Perrone et al. 2019
)
. For a given
{\mathbf{p}}
, denote
\Gamma({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})=\{{\bm{\gamma}}\in\Gamma:\Supp({\bm{\gamma}})={\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}\}\subset\Gamma
. Under certain conditions on the shape of
{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}
(essentially, that the null entries of (
2.1
) do not form a ‘bulky set’ in a given sense), one can show that
\Gamma({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})\neq\varnothing
(
Ryser 1960
;
Bacharach 1965
;
Sinkhorn and Knopp 1967
– see also
Brualdi 2006
, Theorem 8.1.7
). When it is the case, it appears from
Geenens 2020
that there exists a
unique
probability array
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}\in\Gamma({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})
such that

{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}\doteq\arg\min_{{\bm{\gamma}}\in\Gamma}\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2})\in\Supp({\mathbf{p}})}\gamma_{i_{1}i_{2}}\log\frac{\gamma_{i_{1}i_{2}}}{p_{i_{1}i_{2}}}=\arg\min_{{\bm{\gamma}}\in\Gamma}{\cal I}({\bm{\gamma}}||{\mathbf{p}}),

(2.2)

where
{\cal I}(\cdot\|\cdot)
will be called ‘
{\cal I}
-divergence’ here following
Csiszár 1975
, but also goes by ‘Kullback-Leibler divergence’ after
Kullback and Leibler 1951
or ‘Mutual Entropy’ in the information theory literature
(
Cover and Thomas 2006
)
. Thus,
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}\in\Gamma({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})
is the element of
\Gamma
the closest to
{\mathbf{p}}
in the
{\cal I}
-divergence sense. Furthermore, results of
Csiszár 1975
and
Rüschendorf and Thomsen 1993
on ‘
{\cal I}
-projections’ such as (
2.2
) characterise
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
as the unique probability array of
\Gamma
satisfying

\gamma_{i_{1}i_{2}}=p_{i_{1}i_{2}}\alpha_{i_{1}}\beta_{i_{2}}\quad\forall(i_{1},i_{2})

(2.3)

for two sets of positive constants
\{\alpha_{i_{1}}\}_{i_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}
and
\{\beta_{i_{2}}\}_{i_{2}=1}^{r_{2}}
. The probability measure
G_{\mathbf{p}}
, reconstructed from the probability (copula) array
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
with the inverse rank functions
\Phi^{-1}_{\ell}(i_{\ell})=\frac{i_{\ell}}{r_{\ell}+1}
(
i_{\ell}=1,\ldots,r_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,2
), captures the distribution of a vector
(U_{1},U_{2})
taking values in
\{\frac{1}{r_{1}+1},\ldots,\frac{r_{1}}{r_{1}+1}\}\times\{\frac{1}{r_{2}+1},\ldots,\frac{r_{2}}{r_{2}+1}\}
, such that
{\mathbb{P}}(U_{1}=\frac{i_{1}}{r_{1}+1})=\frac{1}{r_{1}}
and
{\mathbb{P}}(U_{2}=\frac{i_{2}}{r_{2}+1})=\frac{1}{r_{2}}
(discrete uniform marginal distributions) and, from (
2.3
),

{\mathbb{P}}\left(U_{1}=\frac{i_{1}}{r_{1}+1},U_{2}=\frac{i_{2}}{r_{2}+1}\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{1}=x^{(1)}_{i_{1}},X_{2}=x^{(2)}_{i_{2}}\right)\alpha_{i_{1}}\beta_{i_{2}}.

(2.4)

In the case of a rectangular support for
(X_{1},X_{2})
(i.e., all
p_{i_{1}i_{2}}>0
in
{\mathbf{p}}
), then (
2.3
)-(
2.4
) is equivalent to saying that
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim P
and
(U_{1},U_{2})\sim G_{\mathbf{p}}
admit the same sets of
(r_{1}-1)(r_{2}-1)
odds ratios

\frac{\gamma_{11}\gamma_{i_{1}i_{2}}}{\gamma_{i_{1}1}\gamma_{1i_{2}}}=\frac{p_{11}\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}p_{i_{1}i_{2}}\alpha_{i_{1}}\beta_{i_{2}}}{p_{i_{1}1}\alpha_{i_{1}}\beta_{1}p_{1i_{2}}\alpha_{1}\beta_{i_{2}}}=\frac{p_{11}p_{i_{1}i_{2}}}{p_{i_{1}1}p_{1i_{2}}}\quad\forall(i_{1},i_{2})\in\{2,\ldots,r_{1}\}\times\{2,\ldots,r_{2}\},

which unequivocally describe the
dependence structure
in such bivariate discrete distributions (
Bishop et al. 1975
, Chapter 2
;
Agresti 2013
, Chapter 2
;
Geenens 2023
, Section 4.3.1(a)
). Thus,
P
and
G_{\mathbf{p}}
admit the same dependence structure.

More generally, the number and layout of null entries in
{\mathbf{p}}
form an essential element of the dependence structure of the vector
(X_{1},X_{2})
. Indeed, the presence of any
p_{i_{1}i_{2}}=0
in (
2.1
) (
non-rectangular support
) automatically prevents independence between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
.
1
1

1

Algebraically,
0=p_{i_{1}i_{2}}\neq p_{i_{1}\bullet}p_{\bullet i_{2}}
, as it has been assumed
p_{i_{1}\bullet},p_{\bullet i_{2}}>0
for all
i_{1},i_{2}
. Intuitively, the fact that some values of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are not allowed simultaneously can be understood as a strong form of interaction, indeed.
This contribution to the dependence exclusively due to the shape of
{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}
– what
Holland and Wang 1987
called ‘
regional dependence
’ – is maintained in
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
through (
2.2
) as
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}\in\Gamma({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})
. The rest of the dependence structure (not of ‘regional’ nature) remains unequivocally described by a certain number of odds-ratio-like quantities which essentially ‘avoid’ the null entries
p_{i_{1}i_{2}}=0
; see Example
3.1
below for a simple illustration, or
Geenens 2023
for full details. Again, these odds-ratios are maintained through (
2.3
), and thus shared by
{\mathbf{p}}
and
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
. It follows that
P
and
G_{\mathbf{p}}
admit the same dependence structure in the case of non-rectangular support as well.

In fact, (
2.4
) is what
Geenens 2023
established as a necessary and sufficient condition for
(U_{1},U_{2})\sim G_{\mathbf{p}}
and
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim P
to share the same dependence structure. Thus,
(U_{1},U_{2})
is the random vector whose dependence structure is identical to that of
(X_{1},X_{2})
, but with (discrete) uniform marginals on
\{\frac{1}{r_{1}+1},\ldots,\frac{r_{1}}{r_{1}+1}\}
and
\{\frac{1}{r_{2}+1},\ldots,\frac{r_{2}}{r_{2}+1}\}
– and this, irrespective of the initial marginal domains and distributions of
(X_{1},X_{2})
. In other words,
G_{\mathbf{p}}
is a margin-free distribution-morphic representation of that dependence structure, in a way obviously reminiscent of the (unique) continuous copula
C_{X_{1}X_{2}}
of
(X_{1},X_{2})
if it was a continuous bivariate vector. This justifies to call
G_{\mathbf{p}}
the
discrete copula
measure of
(X_{1},X_{2})\sim P

(
Geenens 2020
, Section 6)
. By analogy with the continuous case, we will call ‘discrete copula’ the joint cumulative distribution function of
(U_{1},U_{2})\sim G_{\mathbf{p}}
and ‘copula pmf’ its probability mass function, as in
Geenens 2020
. Accordingly, we will call
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
the
copula array
of
{\mathbf{p}}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The discrete copula construction is described under the assumption
\Gamma({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})\neq\varnothing
, which is the case when the null entries of
{\mathbf{p}}
are not too prominent in a precised sense.
2
2

2

In language of matrix analysis, this is equivalent to the matrix
{\mathbf{p}}
in (
2.1
) being fully indecomposable; see
Brualdi 2006
.
We can wonder what happens if that condition is not met. There are two cases to distinguish:
(a)

\Gamma({\cal S}^{\prime})=\varnothing
for all
{\cal S}^{\prime}\subseteq{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}
; and
(b)

\Gamma({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})=\varnothing
but there exists
{\cal S}^{\prime}\subset{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}
such that
\Gamma({\cal S}^{\prime})\neq\varnothing
. We discuss this two cases in Appendix
B
, through examples. For reasons clarified there, we refer to case
(a)
as ‘
exclusive regional dependence
’ and case
(b)
as ‘
critical regional dependence
’.∎

This discrete copula construction shares with Sklar’s formalism in continuous settings its principal strength: it allows for the development of highly flexible bivariate models in which the marginal distributions and the dependence structure can be specified separately – but it does so for
discrete
random vectors. Importantly, under the above assumption
\Gamma({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})\neq\varnothing
, the transformation (
2.2
) is reversible: if
\gamma_{\mathbf{p}}
is the
{\cal I}
-projection of
{\mathbf{p}}
onto the class
\Gamma
of probability arrays with uniform margins, then
{\mathbf{p}}
is the
{\cal I}
-projection of
\gamma_{\mathbf{p}}
onto the corresponding
Fréchet class
of all probability arrays sharing the same margins as
{\mathbf{p}}
– Proposition
3.1
below formalises this duality in full generality for the
d
-dimensional case. Furthermore,
\gamma_{\mathbf{p}}
can be
{\cal I}
-projected onto other Fréchet classes (all probability arrays sharing other margins) to produce new probability arrays that retain the dependence structure of
{\mathbf{p}}
while altering the marginals. This process captures the essential idea of copula modelling, and we expect that many problems involving continuous random vectors that have benefited from Sklar’s construction (see
Genest et al. 2024
for a recent comprehensive review) will have natural analogues in the discrete setting via these discrete copulas. A fundamental step toward realising this potential is to develop empirical methods for estimating discrete copulas from data, and this forms the focus of the present paper.

The next section will describe – in detail, as this was not covered in the previous literature – the extension of the above bivariate construction to any arbitrary dimension
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

d
-variate discrete copulas

3.1
d
-dimensional probability arrays

For simplicity, below we denote
[s]\doteq\{1,\ldots,s\}
for any
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
. For given
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
r_{1},\dots,r_{d}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
be the set of all
d
-dimensional arrays whose dimension sizes are
r_{1},\dots,r_{d}
.
3
3

3

Note the distinction between
{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}}
, the set of all
\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}\right)
-dimensional real vectors.
Any array
{\mathbf{a}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
can be expressed explicitly ‘entrywise’ in terms of its elements as
(a_{\bm{i}})_{\bm{i}\in{\cal R}}
, where

{\cal R}=[r_{1}]\times\dots\times[r_{d}]=\left\{{\bm{i}}=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots i_{d}):i_{1}\in[r_{1}],\dots,i_{d}\in[r_{d}]\right\}.

(3.1)

The
support
of such array is then defined as
\Supp({\mathbf{a}})=\left\{\bm{i}\in{\cal R}:a_{\bm{i}}\neq 0\right\}
. When summing the elements of
{\mathbf{a}}
over all dimensions but one, we obtain a one-dimensional array (that is, a vector). Specifically, for any
\ell\in[d]
, we set

a^{(\ell)}_{i}=\sum_{i_{1}\in[r_{1}]}\dots\sum_{i_{\ell-1}\in[r_{\ell-1}]}\sum_{i_{\ell+1}\in[r_{\ell+1}]}\dots\sum_{i_{d}\in[r_{d}]}a_{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1},i,i_{\ell+1},\dots,i_{d}},\qquad i\in[r_{\ell}],

(3.2)

corresponding to the
r_{\ell}
components of the marginal sum vector when summing the elements of
{\mathbf{a}}
over all the dimensions but the
\ell
th one. We will write
\bm{a}^{(\ell)}=(a^{(\ell)}_{1},\ldots,a^{(\ell)}_{r_{\ell}})
and call it the
\ell^{\text{th}}
margin of
{\mathbf{a}}
.

For any
\ell\in[d]
, let
{\cal X}_{\ell}=\{x^{(\ell)}_{1},\dots,x^{(\ell)}_{r_{\ell}}\}
with
x^{(\ell)}_{1}<\dots<x^{(\ell)}_{r_{\ell}}
. To any probability measure
P
defined on
{\cal X}={\cal X}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\cal X}_{d}
, we may associate
(i)

d
rank functions
\Phi_{\ell}:{\cal X}_{\ell}\to\{1,\ldots,r_{\ell}\}
(
\ell\in[d]
) such that
\Phi_{\ell}(x^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell}})=i_{\ell}
(
i_{\ell}\in[r_{\ell}])
, and
(ii)
a probability array; i.e., the element
{\mathbf{p}}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
defined by

p_{\bm{i}}=P\left(\left\{(x_{i_{1}}^{(1)},\dots,x_{i_{d}}^{(d)})\right\}\right),\qquad\bm{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots i_{d})\in{\cal R}.

Like in Section
2
, the probability array
{\mathbf{p}}
represents the constituent of the distribution of the random vector
{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})\sim P
which remains invariant under increasing transformations of its individual components. Consequently, any attempt at describing the dependence structure of
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
in a ‘margin-free’ way should be based on
{\mathbf{p}}
and on
{\mathbf{p}}
only.

Probability arrays are necessarily such that
p_{\bm{i}}\geq 0
(
\forall\bm{i}\in{\cal R}
) and
\sum_{\bm{i}\in{\cal R}}p_{\bm{i}}=1
.
We define the
d
margins of a probability array
{\mathbf{p}}
to be the
d
univariate probability arrays (i.e., vectors)
\bm{p}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{p}^{(d)}
, where
{\bm{p}}^{(\ell)}=(p^{(\ell)}_{1},\dots,p^{(\ell)}_{r_{\ell}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{\ell}}
(
\ell\in[d]
) and
p^{(\ell)}_{i}
(
i\in[r_{\ell}]
) is defined as in (
3.2
). Without loss of generality, it will be assumed throughout that any probability array
{\mathbf{p}}
under consideration belongs to the set

{\cal P}=\left\{{\mathbf{p}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}:p_{\bm{i}}\in[0,1],\,\bm{i}\in{\cal R},\,\sum_{\bm{i}\in{\cal R}}p_{\bm{i}}=1\text{ and }p^{(\ell)}_{i}>0\text{ for all }i\in[r_{\ell}]\text{ and }\ell\in[d]\right\},

(3.3)

that is, probability arrays which do not include any identically null slice. If it happens that
p^{(\ell)}_{i}=0
for some
\ell\in[d],i\in[r_{\ell}]
, it suffices to remove the element
x^{(\ell)}_{i}
from
{\cal X}_{\ell}
and decrement
r_{\ell}
by 1 from the outset.

3.2
Fréchet classes and
{\cal I}
-projections

Consider
d
univariate positive probability arrays (that is, vectors whose entries are positive and sum to one)
\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}},\dots,{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{d}}
, respectively, and call

{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})=\{{\mathbf{p}}\in{\cal P}:\bm{p}^{(\ell)}=\bm{q}^{(\ell)},\ \forall\,\ell\in[d]\}

the
Fréchet class
of probability arrays in
{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
with margins
\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)}
. Any such class
{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
is necessarily a non-empty subset of
{\cal P}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
(
3.3
), as it contains at least the ‘independence’ array denoted
[\bm{q}^{(1)}\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}\dots\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}\bm{q}^{(d)}]
and defined as

[\bm{q}^{(1)}\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}\dots\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}\bm{q}^{(d)}]_{\bm{i}}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}q_{i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)},\quad\bm{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots i_{d})\in{\cal R}.

(3.4)

It is easy to see that any such Fréchet class
{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
is convex and closed in total variation.
4
4

4

For
{\mathbf{p}},{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}\in{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
and
w\in[0,1]
,
[w{\mathbf{p}}+(1-w){\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}]^{(\ell)}=w\bm{p}^{(\ell)}+(1-w)\bm{p}^{\prime(\ell)}=w\bm{q}^{(\ell)}+(1-w)\bm{q}^{(\ell)}=\bm{q}^{(\ell)}

\forall\ell\in[d]
, so
w{\mathbf{p}}+(1-w){\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}\in{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
. Also, the total variation distance between two probability measures
P
and
P^{\prime}
on
{\cal X}
is equivalent to the
\ell_{1}
-distance between their probability arrays
{\mathbf{p}}
and
{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}
. Let
\{{\mathbf{p}}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\in{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
be a sequence of probability arrays converging to some probability array
{\mathbf{p}}
. A necessary condition for this is that
\bm{p}_{n}^{(\ell)}
converges to
\bm{p}^{(\ell)}

\forall\ell\in[d]
; and as
\bm{p}_{n}^{(\ell)}=\bm{q}^{(\ell)}

\forall n\geq 1
,
\bm{p}^{(\ell)}=\bm{q}^{(\ell)}
and
{\mathbf{p}}\in{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
.

For any two probability arrays
{\mathbf{p}},{\mathbf{q}}\in{\cal P}
, the
{\cal I}
-divergence of
{\mathbf{q}}
with respect to
{\mathbf{p}}
is defined as

{\cal I}({\mathbf{q}}\|{\mathbf{p}})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{i}\in\Supp({\mathbf{p}})}q_{\bm{i}}\log{\frac{q_{\bm{i}}}{p_{\bm{i}}}}&\text{ if }\Supp{({\mathbf{q}})}\subseteq\Supp{({\mathbf{p}})},\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

(3.5)

with the convention that
0\log 0=0
. We say that
{\mathbf{q}}^{*}\in{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
is an
{\cal I}
-projection of
{\mathbf{p}}
on
{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
if

{\cal I}({\mathbf{q}}^{*}\|{\mathbf{p}})=\inf_{{\mathbf{q}}\in{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})}{\cal I}({\mathbf{q}}\|{\mathbf{p}}).

In the considered finite discrete framework,
{\cal I}({\mathbf{q}}\|{\mathbf{p}})<\infty\iff\Supp{({\mathbf{q}})}\subseteq\Supp{({\mathbf{p}})}
. If
{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
does not contain any array
{\mathbf{q}}
such that
\Supp{({\mathbf{q}})}\subseteq\Supp{({\mathbf{p}})}
, then all
{\mathbf{q}}\in{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
are such that
{\cal I}({\mathbf{q}}\|{\mathbf{p}})=\infty
and
{\mathbf{p}}
does not admit a proper
{\cal I}
-projection. By contrast, if
{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
contains at least one probability array
{\mathbf{q}}
such that
\Supp({\mathbf{q}})\subseteq\Supp({\mathbf{p}})
, then any
{\cal I}
-projection
{\mathbf{q}}^{*}
is necessarily such that
\Supp({\mathbf{q}}^{*})\subseteq\Supp({\mathbf{p}})
. In fact, the strict convexity of
{\cal I}
as a function of
{\mathbf{q}}
and the convexity and closedness of
{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
guarantee the uniqueness of that
{\cal I}
-projection
(
Csiszár 1975
, Theorem 2.1)
, which we will then denote

{\mathbf{q}}^{*}\doteq{\cal I}_{{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})}({\mathbf{p}}).

(3.6)

Furthermore, if there exists
{\mathbf{q}}\in{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
such that
\Supp({\mathbf{q}})=\Supp({\mathbf{p}})
, then it follows directly from
Csiszár 1975
that
\Supp({\mathbf{q}}^{*})=\Supp({\mathbf{p}})
and
{\mathbf{q}}^{*}
is the unique element of
{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
such that, for all
\bm{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\in{\cal R}
:

q^{*}_{\bm{i}}=p_{\bm{i}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\alpha^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell}}

(3.7)

for sets of positive constants
\{\alpha^{(\ell)}_{1},\ldots,\alpha^{(\ell)}_{r_{\ell}}\}
(
\ell=1,\ldots,d
). Reversely, it follows from
Csiszár 1975
again that, if one can find
{\mathbf{q}}^{*}\in{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
admitting the representation (
3.7
), then it is automatically the (unique)
{\cal I}
-projection of
{\mathbf{p}}
on
{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
.

Remark 3.1
.

Obtaining the
{\cal I}
-projection of a probability array onto a Fréchet class in closed form is generally impossible, with explicit solutions available only in simple cases (Example
3.1
below provides one such instance). However, when an
{\cal I}
-projection exists, it can always be numerically approximated using the Iterated Proportional Fitting (IPF) procedure
(
Ireland and Kullback 1968
,
Fienberg 1970
,
Rüschendorf 1995
,
Idel 2016
)
; see also
Rudas 2018
. This algorithm, also known as Sinkhorn algorithm
(
Sinkhorn 1964
,
Sinkhorn and Knopp 1967
)
in Optimal Transport theory
(
Cuturi 2013
,
Carlier 2022
)
, offers fast convergence and accuracy guarantees. Practical implementation (for any dimension
d
) is provided by the R package
mipfp

(
Barthelemy et al. 2018
)
. Consequently, we can consider any required
{\cal I}
-projection to be effectively accessible to us.
5
5

5

More precisely: capable of numerical approximation to any arbitrary precision.
∎

3.3
Copula arrays and discrete copulas

For all
\ell\in[d]
, let
\bm{u}^{(\ell)}=(\frac{1}{r_{\ell}},\ldots,\frac{1}{r_{\ell}})
, the univariate probability arrays corresponding to (discrete) uniform distributions. We define the Fréchet class

\Gamma\doteq{\cal F}({\bm{u}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bm{u}}^{(d)})=\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}\in{\cal P}:\gamma^{(\ell)}_{i}=\frac{1}{r_{\ell}}\text{ for all }i\in[r_{\ell}]\text{ and }\ell\in[d]\right\},

(3.8)

and call its elements
copula arrays
. We note that
\Gamma
is related to the convex polytope of the polystochastic arrays of
d
-dimensions (‘
d
-dimensional Birckhoff polytope’;
Jurkat and Ryser 1968
,
Linial and Luria 2014
,
Taranenko 2016
).

For any non-empty subset
{\cal S}\subseteq{\cal R}
in (
3.1
), let

\displaystyle{\cal P}({\cal S})=\left\{{\mathbf{p}}\in{\cal P}:\Supp({\mathbf{p}})={\cal S}\right\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Gamma({\cal S})=\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}\in\Gamma:\Supp({\bm{\gamma}})={\cal S}\right\}.

(3.9)

Consider a given
{\mathbf{p}}\in{\cal F}({\bm{p}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{p}^{(d)}})\subset{\cal P}
, whose support is
{\cal S}_{{\mathbf{p}}}=\Supp({\mathbf{p}})
. According to Section
3.2
,
\Gamma({\cal S}_{{\mathbf{p}}})\neq\varnothing
is a sufficient condition for
{\mathbf{p}}
to admit a unique
{\cal I}
-projection on
\Gamma
; viz.

{\bm{\gamma}}_{{\mathbf{p}}}\doteq{\cal I}_{\Gamma}({\mathbf{p}}),

(3.10)

where
{\cal I}_{\Gamma}(\cdot)={\cal I}_{{\cal F}({\bm{u}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bm{u}}^{(d)})}(\cdot)
is defined as in (
3.6
). Explicitly, from (
3.7
),
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}\in\Gamma({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})
and is the unique element of
\Gamma
such that

\gamma_{{\mathbf{p}};\bm{i}}=p_{\bm{i}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\beta_{i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}

(3.11)

for some sets of positive constants
\{\beta_{i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}\}
,
\ell\in[d],i_{\ell}\in[r_{\ell}]
. Reversing (
3.11
), we have directly

p_{\bm{i}}=\frac{\gamma_{{\mathbf{p}};\bm{i}}}{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\beta_{i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}}=\gamma_{{\mathbf{p}};\bm{i}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\beta_{i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}},

(3.12)

and as
\{1/\beta_{i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}\}
(
\ell=[d],i_{\ell}\in[r_{\ell}]
) are evidently positive constants as well, we deduce that
{\mathbf{p}}
is the unique
{\cal I}
-projection of
\gamma_{\mathbf{p}}
on
{\cal F}({\bm{p}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{p}^{(d)}})
. The following result – essentially the
d
-variate analog to
Kojadinovic and Martini 2024
– formalises the previous observations.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{p}}\in{\cal F}(\bm{p}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{p}^{(d)})\subset{\cal P}
have support
{\cal S}_{{\mathbf{p}}}\subseteq{\cal R}
. Then, the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\Gamma({\cal S}_{{\mathbf{p}}})\neq\varnothing
;

(ii)

{\mathbf{p}}
admits a unique
{\cal I}
-projection on
\Gamma
, viz.
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}={\cal I}_{\Gamma}({\mathbf{p}})
, and
\Supp({\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}})={\cal S}_{{\mathbf{p}}}
;

(iii)

if there exists
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\Gamma
such that
{\mathbf{p}}={\cal I}_{{\cal F}({\bm{p}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{p}^{(d)}})}({\bm{\gamma}})
, then
{\bm{\gamma}}={\cal I}_{\Gamma}({\mathbf{p}})
; that is,
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}\doteq{\cal I}_{\Gamma}({\mathbf{p}})
is the only element of
\Gamma
whose
{\cal I}
-projection on
{\cal F}({\bm{p}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{p}^{(d)}})
is
{\mathbf{p}}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A
. ∎

Any probability array
{\mathbf{p}}
may thus be understood as formed by a copula array ‘seed’
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}\in\Gamma
on which relevant margins
{\bm{p}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{p}^{(d)}}
have been ‘mounted’ via (
3.12
). Reversely, that seed
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
may be recovered by ‘unmounting’ those margins as in (
3.11
), which is equivalent to ‘mounting’ (via the same process) uniform margins on
{\mathbf{p}}
. From a statistical modelling perspective, the reversibility of the construction

{\mathbf{p}}={\cal I}_{{\cal F}({\bm{p}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{p}^{(d)}})}({\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}})\iff\gamma_{\mathbf{p}}={\cal I}_{\Gamma}({\mathbf{p}})

(3.13)

is crucial, as it enables the development of flexible models by initially concentrating on the dependence/copula and subsequently transitioning back to the original marginal scales (‘
there and back again
’). This is naturally reminiscent of the classical copula methodology articulated around Sklar’s representation in continuous cases. In fact, (
3.13
) may be interpreted – to some extent – as a discrete analogue to the usual Sklar’s construction.

Now, for any margins
\bm{q}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{q}^{(d)}
such that
\exists{\mathbf{q}}\in{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
with
\Supp({\mathbf{q}})={\cal S}_{{\mathbf{p}}}
, the unique
{\cal I}
-projection
{\mathbf{q}}^{*}
of
{\mathbf{p}}
on
{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
satisfies (
3.7
), and we conclude with (
3.12
):

q^{*}_{\bm{i}}=p_{\bm{i}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\alpha^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell}}=\gamma_{{\mathbf{p}};\bm{i}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\frac{\alpha^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell}}}{\beta_{i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}}\qquad\forall\bm{i}\in{\cal R}.

(3.14)

As
\alpha^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell}}/\beta_{i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}>0
for all
\ell\in[d]
and
i_{\ell}\in[r_{\ell}]
, this means that
{\mathbf{q}}^{*}
is the
{\cal I}
-projection of
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
on
{\cal F}(\bm{q}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{q}^{(d)})
as well, and by Proposition
3.1
,
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
is the copula array seed of
{\mathbf{q}}^{*}
. In fact, as (
3.7
) defines a group action on the space of probability arrays
{\cal P}
, it defines an equivalence relation:

{\mathbf{q}}\sim{\mathbf{p}}\iff\exists\{\alpha^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell}}>0\}_{\ell\in[d],i_{\ell}\in[r_{\ell}]}\ \text{s.t. }\ q_{\bm{i}}=p_{\bm{i}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\alpha^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell}}\ \forall\bm{i}\in{\cal R}\iff{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{q}}={\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}.

The copula array ‘seed’ is thus a maximal invariant under the above group operation, which captures ‘everything but the margins’ of the probability arrays under consideration – that is, according to
Geenens 2023
, the underlying dependence structure. For example, if
\Supp({\mathbf{p}})={\cal R}
(
hyper-rectangular support
), then it follows from (
3.14
) that
{\mathbf{p}}
,
{\mathbf{q}}^{*}
and
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}={\bm{\gamma}}_{{\mathbf{q}}^{*}}
share the same sets of conditional and higher-order odds ratios as described in
Rudas 2018
.

Like in Section
2
, we can now define the (discrete) copula measure
G_{\mathbf{p}}
of a vector
{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})\sim P
with probability array
{\mathbf{p}}
as the probability measure of the vector
{\mathbf{U}}=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
supported on
\bigtimes_{\ell\in[d]}\frac{[r_{\ell}]}{r_{\ell}+1}=\bigtimes_{\ell\in[d]}\{\frac{1}{r_{\ell}+1},\ldots,\frac{r_{\ell}}{r_{\ell}+1}\}
whose copula array is
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
; in other words, the discrete measure obtained by combining the probability array
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
with the
d
inverse rank functions
\Phi^{-1}_{\ell}:[r_{\ell}]\to\frac{[r_{\ell}]}{r_{\ell}+1}:\Phi^{-1}_{\ell}(i)=\frac{i}{r_{\ell}+1}
,
\ell\in[d]
. Such copula measure is a margin-free distribution-morphic representation of the dependence structure of the random vector
{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})\sim P
. The above observations justify defining:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})\sim P
be a
d
-dimensional discrete random vector whose probability array
{\mathbf{p}}\in{\cal P}
has support
{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}\subseteq{\cal R}
. If
\Gamma({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})\neq\varnothing
, we call the unique copula array
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\Gamma
satisfying
{\bm{\gamma}}={\cal I}_{\Gamma}({\mathbf{p}})
the copula array of
{\mathbf{p}}
, denoted
\gamma_{\mathbf{p}}
. The probability measure
G_{\mathbf{p}}
on
\bigtimes_{\ell\in[d]}\frac{[r_{\ell}]}{r_{\ell}+1}
whose copula array is
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
is called the (discrete) copula measure of
{\mathbf{X}}
, the associated joint cumulative distribution is the (discrete) copula of
{\mathbf{X}}
and the associated joint probability mass function is the copula pmf of
{\mathbf{X}}
.

Parameterisation of copula arrays with a given support

As for

(Section
2

that the condition

ensures that the discrete copula machinery fits neatly into place for distributions with probability array

.
6
6

6

In the spirit of Remark
2.1
,
d

3.4.1

Take the probability array

(3.16)

It follows from the definition of

\gamma_{{\bm{i}}}^{(q,{\cal S})}=\left\{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\beta^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell}}\right\}\hbox{${\it 1}\hskip-4.5ptI_{\{\bm{i}\in{\cal S}\}}$}

for some positive constants

\gamma_{{\bm{i}}}^{(q,{\cal R})}
factorises as in (
3.4

{\bm{\gamma}}^{(q,{\cal R})}

{\bm{\gamma}}^{(\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}})}\doteq[{\bm{u}}^{(1)}\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}\dots\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\bm{u}}^{(d)}]

\Delta\doteq{\bm{\gamma}}-{\bm{\gamma}}^{(q,{\cal S})}\quad\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}.

As both
{\bm{\gamma}}
and
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(q,{\cal S})}

\bm{\Delta}^{\circ}({\cal S})=\{{\mathbf{a}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}:\Supp({\mathbf{a}})\subseteq{\cal S}\text{ and }a^{(\ell)}_{i}=0\ \forall\,\ell\in[d],\,i\in[r_{\ell}]\}

{\bm{\gamma}}\doteq{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}})

{\bm{\gamma}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\circ}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
is the function

{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d_{\circ}})\ \mapsto\ {\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{\theta})={\bm{\gamma}}^{(q,{\cal S})}+\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\circ}}\theta_{k}\Delta_{k}.

(3.19)

Identifying
{\bm{\gamma}}={\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}})
means that
{\bm{\gamma}}
is sometimes understood as a copula array and sometimes as the function of
{\bm{\theta}}
returning that copula array. The context clarifies what is intended and this should not cause any confusion.

3.4.2
Practical identification of a basis

It will be necessary here to vectorise the multi-dimensional arrays under consideration, so that we may apply standard operations on vectors and matrices. While this can produce somewhat unwieldy expressions, their complexity is merely notational and the underlying ideas remain entirely grounded in elementary principles of linear algebra. Let
\mathrm{vec}(\cdot)
denote the
d
-dimensional extension of the column-major vectorisation operator which transforms an array in
{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
into a vector of
{\mathbb{R}}^{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}}
. Specifically, given an array
{\mathbf{a}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
,
\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{a}})
is a vector of
{\mathbb{R}}^{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}}
whose elements are given by

\mathrm{vec}{({\mathbf{a}})}_{\phi(\bm{i})}=a_{{\bm{i}}},\qquad{\bm{i}}\in{\cal R},

where
\phi:{\cal R}\to\left[\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}\right]
is

\phi(\bm{i})=i_{1}+\sum_{\ell_{1}=2}^{d}\left((i_{\ell_{1}}-1)\prod_{\ell_{2}=1}^{\ell_{1}-1}r_{\ell_{2}}\right),\qquad{\bm{i}}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\in{\cal R}.

(3.20)

The inverse mapping
\mathrm{vec}^{-1}:{\mathbb{R}}^{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
is such that, for any starting vector
\bm{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}}
, the elements of the resulting array
\mathrm{vec}^{-1}(\bm{v})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
are given by

\mathrm{vec}^{-1}(\bm{v})_{\phi^{-1}(j)}=v_{j},\qquad j\in\left[\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}\right],

where
\phi^{-1}(j)={\bm{i}}=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d})
is computed as follows: initialise
k\leftarrow j-1
; for
\ell\in[d]
: set
i_{\ell}=(k\bmod r_{\ell})+1
and update
k\leftarrow\left\lfloor\frac{k}{r_{\ell}}\right\rfloor
.

Throughout, we will denote
\underline{\bm{p}}\doteq\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{p}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}}
and
\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}\doteq\mathrm{vec}({\bm{\gamma}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}}
, and more generally the vectorised versions of
{\cal P}({\cal S})
and
\Gamma({\cal S})
in (
3.9
) as

\displaystyle\underline{{\cal P}}({\cal S})

\displaystyle\doteq\{\underline{\bm{p}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}}:\underline{\bm{p}}=\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{p}}),{\mathbf{p}}\in{\cal P}({\cal S})\},

\displaystyle\underline{\Gamma}({\cal S})

\displaystyle\doteq\{\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}}:\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}=\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\gamma}),\bm{\gamma}\in\Gamma({\cal S})\}.

Likewise, from
\bm{\Delta}^{\circ}({\cal S})
in (
3.18
), let

\underline{\bm{\Delta}}^{\circ}({\cal S})=\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}}:\bm{x}=\mathrm{vec}(\Delta),\Delta\in\bm{\Delta}^{\circ}({\cal S})\}.

(3.21)

The linear and bijective nature of
\mathrm{vec}(\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}}
has two implications. First,
\dim\left(\underline{\bm{\Delta}}^{\circ}({\cal S})\right)=\dim\left(\bm{\Delta}^{\circ}({\cal S})\right)=d_{\circ}
; and second, the vectors
\underline{\Delta}_{1},\dots,\underline{\Delta}_{d_{\circ}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}}
form a basis of
\underline{\bm{\Delta}}^{\circ}({\cal S})
if and only if the arrays
\mathrm{vec}^{-1}(\underline{\Delta}_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{vec}^{-1}(\underline{\Delta}_{d_{\circ}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
form a basis of
\bm{\Delta}^{\circ}({\cal S})
. Hence, to obtain a basis of
\bm{\Delta}^{\circ}({\cal S})
, it suffices to obtain a basis of
\underline{\bm{\Delta}}^{\circ}({\cal S})
. We can proceed as follows.

As
\bm{x}\in\underline{\bm{\Delta}}^{\circ}({\cal S})\iff\bm{x}=\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)
for some
\Delta\in{\bm{\Delta}}^{\circ}({\cal S})
, any vector
\bm{x}\in\underline{\bm{\Delta}}^{\circ}({\cal S})
is necessarily such that
x_{\phi(\bm{i})}=0
for all
{\bm{i}}\in{\cal R}\setminus{\cal S}
and

\sum_{i_{1}\in[r_{1}]}\dots\sum_{i_{\ell-1}\in[r_{\ell-1}]}\sum_{i_{\ell+1}\in[r_{\ell+1}]}\dots\sum_{i_{d}\in[r_{d}]}x_{\phi(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1},i,i_{\ell+1},\dots,i_{d})}=0

for all
\ell\in[d]
and
i\in[r_{\ell}]
. Let
C_{\cal S}
be the
\left\{\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}-|{\cal S}|+\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}\right)\times\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}\right\}
-matrix whose rows encode the above linear constraints, and consider the homogeneous system

C_{\cal S}\,\bm{x}=\bm{0}.

(3.22)

It appears that
\underline{\bm{\Delta}}^{\circ}({\cal S})
is the null space of the matrix
C_{\cal S}
, and
d_{\circ}
is the dimension of that null space. Any algorithm computing a basis of the null space of a matrix can be used for identifying a basis for
\underline{\bm{\Delta}}^{\circ}({\cal S})
. For example, the function
null(...)
from the
R
package
pracma

(
Borchers 2022
)
does this by exploiting the so-called QR-factorization of the input matrix
(
Trefethen and Bau III 1997
, pp.  49-50)
. Indeed
\texttt{null}(C_{\cal S})
returns a matrix whose column vectors form a basis of the null space of
C_{\cal S}
.

Remark 3.2
.

It is seen that Condition
3.2
holds if and only if the null space of
C_{\cal S}
is non-trivial. A convenient practical test is to attempt to solve (
3.22
), at least numerically. If the system admits only the trivial solution (the zero vector), then Condition
3.2
does not hold. Otherwise, the existence of infinitely many solutions confirms that Condition
3.2
is satisfied. ∎

3.4.3
Resulting parameterisation

Assume Conditions
3.1
-
3.2
and suppose that a basis
\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{d_{\circ}}
of
\bm{\Delta}^{\circ}({\cal S})
has been identified as suggested above (or otherwise). Consider the vectorised version of
{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}})
in (
3.19
); viz. the function
\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\circ}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}}
defined as
\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}(\bm{\theta})\doteq\mathrm{vec}({\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}}))
, or explicitly

\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}(\bm{\theta})=\mathrm{vec}\left({\bm{\gamma}}^{(q,{\cal S})}+\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\circ}}\theta_{k}\Delta_{k}\right)=\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{(q,{\cal S})}+\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\circ}}\theta_{k}\underline{\Delta}_{k}=\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{(q,{\cal S})}+A_{\cal S}\bm{\theta},

(3.23)

with
A_{\cal S}
the
\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}\times d_{\circ}\right)
-matrix whose columns vectors are
\underline{\Delta}_{1},\dots,\underline{\Delta}_{d_{\circ}}
.
7
7

7

This is exactly the outcome of the
null(...)
function of the
pracma
R package mentioned at the end of Section
3.4.2
.
Reversely, we may rewrite (
3.19
) as

{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{\theta})=\mathrm{vec}^{-1}(\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}(\bm{\theta}))={\bm{\gamma}}^{(q,{\cal S})}+\mathrm{vec}^{-1}(A_{\cal S}\bm{\theta}).

(3.24)

Now, for any
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\circ}}
, this array
{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}})
has support
{\cal S}
and discrete uniform margins (
[{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{\theta})]_{i}^{(\ell)}=1/r_{\ell}\ \forall\,i\in[r_{\ell}]
and
\ell\in[d]
) by construction. However, it is not necessarily the case that
0<[{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}})]_{{\bm{i}}}<1
for all
{\bm{i}}\in{\cal S}
; in other words, without restriction on
{\bm{\theta}}
,
{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}})
is not necessarily a valid copula array of
\Gamma({\cal S})
.

Let
R_{\cal S}
be the
(|{\cal S}|\times\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell})
-matrix obtained from the
(\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}\times\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell})
-identity matrix by deleting its rows with index
\phi(\bm{i})
,
\bm{i}\in{\cal R}\setminus{\cal S}
, where
\phi
is defined in (
3.20
). See that
R_{\cal S}
is an ‘entry selection matrix’; that is, left-multiplying by
R_{\cal S}
allows us to, roughly speaking, ‘retain only the elements of vectorised arrays that are in the support
{\cal S}
’ while multiplying by its transpose performs the inverse operation, which amounts to ‘adding zeros at the right spots’ in vectors. Call
\Theta\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\circ}}
the admissible parameter set; that is,
{\bm{\theta}}\in\Theta\iff{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}})\in\Gamma({\cal S})\iff 0<[{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}})]_{{\bm{i}}}<1
for all
\bm{i}\in{\cal S}
. The latter set of inequalities can be written under matrix form as
\bm{0}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\cal S}|}}<R_{\cal S}(\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{(q,{\cal S})}+A_{\cal S}\bm{\theta})<\bm{1}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\cal S}|}}
. Hence,

\Theta=\left\{\bm{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\circ}}:-R_{\cal S}\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{(q,{\cal S})}<\widetilde{A}_{\cal S}\bm{\theta}<\bm{1}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\cal S}|}}-R_{\cal S}\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{(q,{\cal S})}\right\},

(3.25)

where
\widetilde{A}_{\cal S}\doteq R_{\cal S}A_{\cal S}
, the
(|{\cal S}|\times d_{\circ})
-matrix obtained from
A_{\cal S}
by deleting its null rows (corresponding to the structurally null entries of all arrays of
\Delta^{\circ}({\cal S})
). Note that
R_{\cal S}\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{(q,{\cal S})}
is the vector listing the non-null entries of
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(q,{\cal S})}
. As a relatively obvious consequence of the construction, we have:

Lemma 3.1
.

Under Conditions
3.1
-
3.2
, the function
{\bm{\theta}}\mapsto\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}({\bm{\theta}})
in (
3.23
) is an affine bijection between
\Theta
and
\underline{\Gamma}({\cal S})
, and the function
{\bm{\theta}}\mapsto{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}})
in (
3.24
) is a bijection between
\Theta
and
\Gamma({\cal S})
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A
. ∎

Given this one-to-one correspondence between
\Theta
and
\Gamma({\cal S})
, the unique
{\cal I}
-projection of
{\mathbf{p}}\in{\cal P}({\cal S})
onto
\Gamma({\cal S})
can be characterised in terms of its vector
{\bm{\theta}}
. Specifically, if
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
is the
{\cal I}
-projection (
3.10
) of
{\mathbf{p}}
on
\Gamma({\cal S})
, then we have
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}={\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\mathbf{p}})
where

{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathbf{p}}\doteq\arg\min_{{\bm{\theta}}\in\Theta}{\cal I}({\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}})\|{\mathbf{p}}).

(3.26)

Under Conditions
3.1
-
3.2
, such
{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathbf{p}}
exists and is unique in
\Theta
, as
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
exists and is unique in
\Gamma({\cal S})
. All this is illustrated below on two simple examples: one bivariate (
d=2
) and one trivariate (
d=3
).

Example 3.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{p}}
be a
(3\times 3)
probability array with
{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}=\{(i_{1},i_{2})\in[3]^{2}=\{1,2,3\}\times\{1,2,3\}\text{ s.t.\ }i_{1}\neq i_{2}\}
, viz.

{\mathbf{p}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{12}&p_{13}\\
p_{21}&0&p_{23}\\
p_{31}&p_{32}&0\end{pmatrix}

(3.27)

for some positive probabilities
p_{12},p_{13},p_{21},p_{23},p_{31},p_{32}
summing to 1. On this support
{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}
, the quasi-independence copula array is

{\bm{\gamma}}^{(q,{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{6}\\
\frac{1}{6}&0&\frac{1}{6}\\
\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{6}&0\end{pmatrix},

which is in fact directly the array defined by (
3.15
). Each row and column of any array of
{\bm{\Delta}}^{\circ}({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})
contains at most two non-zero entries, hence any single positive value propagates through the matrix via the zero row and column sum constraints, thereby uniquely determining all remaining entries. The set
{\bm{\Delta}}^{\circ}({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})
is thus unidimensional (
d_{\circ}=1
), and we can take for its basis element:

\Delta_{1}\doteq\begin{pmatrix}0&1&-1\\
-1&0&1\\
1&-1&0\end{pmatrix}.

According to (
3.19
), any copula array in
\Gamma({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})
can be written as:

{\bm{\gamma}}(\theta_{1})={\bm{\gamma}}^{(q,{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})}+\theta_{1}\Delta_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{1}{6}+\theta_{1}&\frac{1}{6}-\theta_{1}\\
\frac{1}{6}-\theta_{1}&0&\frac{1}{6}+\theta_{1}\\
\frac{1}{6}+\theta_{1}&\frac{1}{6}-\theta_{1}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
\theta_{1}
is restricted to
\Theta=(-1/6,1/6)
. Some basic algebra reveals that

{\cal I}({\bm{\gamma}}(\theta_{1})||{\mathbf{p}})=\sum_{(i,j)\in{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}}\gamma_{ij}\log\frac{\gamma_{ij}}{p_{ij}}=\left(\frac{1}{6}+\theta_{1}\right)\log\frac{\left(\frac{1}{6}+\theta_{1}\right)^{3}}{p_{12}p_{23}p_{31}}+\left(\frac{1}{6}-\theta_{1}\right)\log\frac{\left(\frac{1}{6}-\theta_{1}\right)^{3}}{p_{13}p_{21}p_{32}}

admits a unique minimum for

\theta_{{\mathbf{p}};1}=\frac{1}{6}\,\frac{{\omega_{\mathbf{p}}}^{1/3}-1}{{\omega_{\mathbf{p}}}^{1/3}+1},\qquad\text{ with }\omega_{\mathbf{p}}=\frac{p_{13}p_{21}p_{32}}{p_{12}p_{23}p_{31}}.

The copula array
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
of
{\mathbf{p}}
in (
3.27
) is thus

{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}={\bm{\gamma}}(\theta_{{\mathbf{p}};1})=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{1}{3}\frac{{\omega_{\mathbf{p}}}^{1/3}}{1+{\omega_{\mathbf{p}}}^{1/3}}&\frac{1}{3}\frac{1}{1+{\omega_{\mathbf{p}}}^{1/3}}\\
\frac{1}{3}\frac{1}{1+{\omega_{\mathbf{p}}}^{1/3}}&0&\frac{1}{3}\frac{{\omega_{\mathbf{p}}}^{1/3}}{1+{\omega_{\mathbf{p}}}^{1/3}}\\
\frac{1}{3}\frac{{\omega_{\mathbf{p}}}^{1/3}}{1+{\omega_{\mathbf{p}}}^{1/3}}&\frac{1}{3}\frac{1}{1+{\omega_{\mathbf{p}}}^{1/3}}&0\end{pmatrix}.

This shows that the dependence of any distribution of type (
3.27
) is entirely described by the ‘odds-ratio-like’ quantity
\omega_{\mathbf{p}}
. In particular, quasi-independence corresponding to the value
\theta_{1}=0
, we conclude that a probability array
{\mathbf{p}}
as in (
3.27
) reflects quasi-independence when
\omega_{\mathbf{p}}=1
or equivalently
p_{13}p_{21}p_{32}=p_{12}p_{23}p_{31}
, confirming
Goodman 1968
. ∎

.

Consider a trivariate
(d=3
) discrete vector
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})\sim P
with
r_{1}=4
,
r_{2}=2
and
r_{3}=2
, and probability array
{\mathbf{p}}
of the form:

{\mathbf{p}}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}p_{111}&p_{121}\\
0&0\\
p_{311}&p_{321}\\
p_{411}&p_{421}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}p_{112}&p_{122}\\
p_{212}&p_{222}\\
p_{312}&p_{322}\\
p_{412}&p_{422}\end{bmatrix}\right\}.

(3.28)

Here the array
{\mathbf{p}}
is represented as a collection of
r_{3}=2
matrices of size
(r_{1}\times r_{2})=(4\times 2)
– each matrix corresponds to a fixed level of
X_{3}
, with entries
p_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}}=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=i_{1},X_{2}=i_{2},X_{3}=i_{3})
arranged in standard matrix form. It is assumed that
p_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}}>0
for all
(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})\in{\cal R}=[4]\times[2]\times[2]
, except for
p_{211}=p_{221}=0
. For this specific support

, the quasi-independence copula array is

{\bm{\gamma}}^{(q,{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1/12&1/12\\
0&0\\
1/12&1/12\\
1/12&1/12\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1/24&1/24\\
1/8&1/8\\
1/24&1/24\\
1/24&1/24\end{bmatrix}\right\},

(3.29)

which is the
{\cal I}
-projection of the ‘quasi-uniform’ array
{\mathbf{q}}
(
3.15
) (that is, the probability array (
3.28
) with all non-zero entries equal to
1/14
) onto the relevant copula Fréchet class (

); viz.

\Gamma={\cal F}({\bm{u}}^{(1)},{\bm{u}}^{(2)},{\bm{u}}^{(3)})=\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}\in{\cal P}:\gamma^{(1)}_{i}=\frac{1}{4}\text{ for all }i\in[4],\gamma^{(2)}_{i}=\gamma^{(3)}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\text{ for all }i\in[2]\right\}.

3.4.4
Differentiability results

Consider now the analogue to (
3.26
) seen as a function from
\underline{{\cal P}}({\cal S})
to
\Theta
; viz.

{\bm{\theta}}^{*}(\underline{\bm{p}})\doteq\arg\inf_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}{\cal I}({\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{\theta})\|\mathrm{vec}^{-1}(\underline{\bm{p}})).

(3.31)

In other words,
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}(\underline{\bm{p}})
is the (unique) vector
{\bm{\theta}}
identifying the
{\cal I}
-projection onto
\Gamma({\cal S})
of the probability array whose vectorisation is
\underline{\bm{p}}
; or equivalently,
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}(\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{p}}))={\bm{\theta}}_{\mathbf{p}}
.
In the same vein, let
\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{*}:\underline{{\cal P}}({\cal S})\to\underline{\Gamma}({\cal S})
:

\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{*}(\underline{\bm{p}})=\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}({\bm{\theta}}^{*}(\underline{\bm{p}})),

(3.32)

where
\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}
is given (
3.23
), and which returns the vectorised copula array of the probability array whose vectorisation is
\underline{\bm{p}}
; i.e.
\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{*}(\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{p}}))=\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\mathbf{p}})
and thus
\mathrm{vec}^{-1}(\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{*}(\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{p}})))={\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\mathbf{p}})={\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
, the copula array of
{\mathbf{p}}
. The following result – essentially a consequence of
Geenens et al. 2024
– establishes that
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}
is a continuously differentiable function of
\underline{\bm{p}}
over the whole of
\underline{{\cal P}}({\cal S})
, and provides its Jacobian.

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume Conditions
3.1
-
3.2
. Then, the function
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}
(
3.31
) is continuously differentiable at any
\underline{\bm{p}}\in\underline{{\cal P}}({\cal S})
, and its Jacobian matrix is

J_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}}(\underline{\bm{p}})=\left[\widetilde{A}_{\cal S}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\diag^{-1}\left(R_{\cal S}\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{*}(\underline{\bm{p}})\right)\widetilde{A}_{\cal S}\right]^{-1}\widetilde{A}_{\cal S}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\diag^{-1}\left(R_{\cal S}\underline{\bm{p}}\right)R_{\cal S}

(3.33)

with the matrices
R_{\cal S}
,
A_{\cal S}
and
\widetilde{A}_{\cal S}=R_{\cal S}A_{\cal S}
defined in Section
3.4.3
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A
. ∎

Remark 3.3
.

The construction described in this section is valid for any general support
{\cal S}\subseteq{\cal R}
; that is, with potentially irregular patterns of zeros in
{\mathbf{p}}\in{\cal P}({\cal S})
. In more structured contexts, however, specific natural parameterisations may emerge for
\Gamma({\cal S})
. In particular, for
{\cal S}={\cal R}
(hyper-rectangular support) in (
3.18
),
{{\bm{\Delta}}}^{\circ}({\cal S})={{\bm{\Delta}}}^{\circ}({\cal R})
is the linear subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times\ldots\times r_{d}}
comprising all arrays whose entries sum to zero along each slice. The dimension of this well-studied subspace is
d_{\circ}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}(r_{\ell}-1)
, with a ‘canonical’ basis which may be defined as follows: let
{\bm{j}}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{d})\in\bigtimes_{\ell=1}^{d}[r_{\ell}-1]
, and define the array
\Delta_{\bm{j}}
entrywise as

\Delta_{{\bm{j}};{\bm{i}}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }{\bm{i}}={\bm{j}},\\
-1&\text{ if exactly one entry }i_{\ell}=r_{\ell}\text{ and all other entries of ${\bm{i}}$ match ${\bm{j}}$}\\
1&\text{ if exactly two entries }i_{\ell_{1}}=r_{\ell_{1}},\ i_{\ell_{2}}=r_{\ell_{2}}\text{ and all other entries of ${\bm{i}}$ match ${\bm{j}}$}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
(-1)^{d}&\text{ if }{\bm{i}}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d}),\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

For example, for the
(3\times 3)
-matrices
(d=2
) of full supports, the dimension of
{\bm{\Delta}}^{\circ}({\cal R})
is
d_{\circ}=4
and the canonical basis elements can be listed as

\Delta_{(1,1)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\
0&0&0\\
-1&0&1\end{pmatrix},\Delta_{(1,2)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&-1\\
0&0&0\\
0&-1&1\end{pmatrix},\Delta_{(2,1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
1&0&-1\\
-1&0&1\end{pmatrix},\Delta_{(2,2)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&1&-1\\
0&-1&1\end{pmatrix}.

(3.34)

In addition, for
{\cal S}={\cal R}
,
R_{\cal S}=I
(the
(\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell}\times\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}r_{\ell})
-identity matrix), and
\widetilde{A}_{\cal R}=A_{\cal R}
is a matrix containing a (vectorised version of a) canonical basis of

5.1
Yule’s coefficient as discrete Spearman’s
\rho_{\text{S}}
, and its empirical estimator

In a bivariate vector
(X_{1},X_{2})
, concordance is the concept capturing the tendency of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
to vary in the same direction: if one increases (resp. decreases), then the other one tends to increase (resp. decrease) as well. Discordance is the reverse effect; i.e.,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
tend to vary in opposite directions. As
(X_{1},X_{2})
is concordant if and only if
(X_{1},-X_{2})
is discordant, discordance is merely negative concordance.
Scarsini 1984
formalised these ideas and showed that concordance reflects a partial ordering of the distributions in a given Fréchet class. As a result,
in continuous vectors
, concordance is intrinsically a property of the underlying (continuous) copula
C_{X_{1}X_{2}}
of the vector
(X_{1},X_{2})
. Specifically, the vector
(X_{1},X_{2})
shows more concordance than the vector
(X_{1}^{\prime},X_{2}^{\prime})
if
C_{X_{1}X_{2}}(u,v)\geq C_{X_{1}^{\prime}X_{2}^{\prime}}(u,v)
for all
(u,v)\in[0,1]
; i.e.,
C_{X_{1}X_{2}}
stochastically dominates
C_{X^{\prime}_{1}X^{\prime}_{2}}

(
Scarsini 1984
, Definition 4)
.

Given the previous definition, in a continuous vector
(X_{1},X_{2})
it must be the case that any valid index quantifying concordance is a parameter of the copula
C_{X_{1}X_{2}}
. One popular such concordance coefficient is Spearman’s
\rho_{\text{S}}

(
Nelsen 2006
, Section 5.1.2)
, the (Pearson’s) correlation coefficient associated to
C_{X_{1}X_{2}}
; that is, the correlation between
F_{X_{1}}(X_{1})
and
F_{X_{2}}(X_{2})
(where
F_{X_{k}}
is the marginal cumulative distribution function of
X_{k}
,
k=1,2
). As
F_{X_{k}}(X_{k})\sim{\cal U}_{[0,1]}
always (Probability Integral Transform, PIT),
\rho_{\text{S}}
is margin-free and takes its extreme values
\pm 1
in case of comonotonicity/countermonotonicity between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
; that is, when the copula
C_{X_{1}X_{2}}
is one of the Fréchet bounds
M
or
W

(
Nelsen 2006
, p. 11)
, or yet equivalently when there exists a monotonic one-to-one relationship between
X_{1}
ans
X_{2}
. Its empirical version is often referred to as ‘Spearman’s rank correlation’, as it is the empirical correlation coefficient between the ranks of the observations in their marginal samples.

Now,
Scarsini 1984
admitted that, for discrete vectors, the above ideas are problematic. Again, this difficulty arises because the classical copula
C_{X_{1}X_{2}}
is ill-defined outside continuous distributions, with PIT not available and the concept of ‘rank’ being ambiguous due to the evident possibility of ties among the observations. As a result, naive attempts at computing Spearman’s
\rho_{\text{S}}
on a bivariate discrete vector lead to well-documented inconsistencies
(
Genest and Nešlehová 2007
, Sections 4.2–4.4)
. In particular,
\rho_{\text{S}}
loses its ‘margin-freeness’ and generally fails to reach its bounds
\pm 1
.
Scarsini 1984
concluded that the above definition of concordance must be replaced by a ‘
(necessarily) more complicated one
’ (p. 211) and proposed a variant based on the subcopula (
Schweizer and Sklar 1974
, Definition 3
;
Geenens 2024
), which he judged himself ‘
cumbersome
’ and ‘
not easily testable in practice
’ (p. 213).

Now, equipped with the above notion of discrete copula, the basic definition applies seamlessly. In particular, inspired by
Tchen 1980
, we define:

Definition 5.1
.

Let
(X_{1},X_{2})
and
(X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2})
be two bivariate discrete vectors with distributions
P
and
P^{\prime}
and associated probability arrays
{\mathbf{p}},{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}\times r_{2}}
(
2\leq r_{1},r_{2}<\infty
). Assume that
P
and
P^{\prime}
admit respective copula arrays
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}\doteq\left(\gamma_{i_{1}i_{2}}\right)_{i_{1}\in[r_{1}],i_{2}\in[r_{2}]}
and
{\bm{\gamma}}_{{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}}\doteq\left(\gamma^{\prime}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\right)_{i_{1}\in[r_{1}],i_{2}\in[r_{2}]}
and copula measures
G_{\mathbf{p}}
and
G_{{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}}
. Then, we say that
(X_{1},X_{2})
show more concordance than
(X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2})
if
G_{\mathbf{p}}
stochastically dominates
G_{{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}}
; i.e.,

\forall(j_{1},j_{2})\in[r_{1}]\times[r_{2}],\quad\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{j_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{j_{2}}\gamma_{i_{1}i_{2}}\geq\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{j_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{j_{2}}\gamma^{\prime}_{i_{1}i_{2}}.

Accordingly, any index quantifying the concordance within a discrete vector
(X_{1},X_{2})
must be a parameter of its discrete copula.
Geenens 2020
proposed a discrete analogue to Spearman’s
\rho_{\text{S}}
, called ‘Yule’s coefficient
\Upsilon
’ and defined as the (Pearson’s) correlation coefficient associated to the discrete copula measure under consideration. Recall that
G_{\mathbf{p}}
is supported on
\{\frac{1}{r_{1}+1},\ldots,\frac{r_{1}}{r_{1}+1}\}\times\{\frac{1}{r_{2}+1},\ldots,\frac{r_{2}}{r_{2}+1}\}
. Then, for a bivariate discrete random vector admitting the copula array
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}\doteq\left(\gamma_{i_{1}i_{2}}\right)_{i_{1}\in[r_{1}],i_{2}\in[r_{2}]}
, Yule’s coefficient is defined as

\Upsilon=\Upsilon({\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}})\doteq\frac{12}{\sqrt{(r_{1}+1)(r_{1}-1)(r_{2}+1)(r_{2}-1)}}\sum_{i_{1}\in[r_{1}]}\sum_{i_{2}\in[r_{2}]}i_{1}i_{2}{\gamma}_{i_{1}i_{2}}-3\frac{\sqrt{(r_{1}+1)(r_{2}+1)}}{\sqrt{(r_{1}-1)(r_{2}-1)}}.

(5.1)

Call the vectors
(r_{1})\doteq(1\ 2\ \ldots\ r_{1})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
and
(r_{2})\doteq(1\ 2\ \ldots\ r_{2})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
, and see that

\sum_{i_{1}\in[r_{1}]}\sum_{i_{2}\in[r_{2}]}i_{1}i_{2}\gamma_{i_{1}i_{2}}=(r_{1})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}(r_{2})=[(r_{2})\otimes(r_{1})]^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}_{\mathbf{p}},

where
(r_{2})\otimes(r_{1})
is the Kronecker product between
(r_{2})
and
(r_{1})
. Evidently,
\Upsilon
is a linear functional of
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
(or
\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}_{\mathbf{p}}
), and it is straightforward to verify that it satisfies the (discrete analogues of the) characterising properties (
\mu 1
)-(
\mu 6
) of valid concordance coefficients listed in
Durante and Sempi 2015
. (The property (
\mu 7
) will actually follow from the developments below.) As noted in
Geenens 2020
,
\Upsilon=\pm 1
if and only if
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
is a diagonal matrix; that is, like in the continuous case, a monotonic one-to-one relationship between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
– which is clearly possible only if
r_{1}=r_{2}
. When
r_{1}\neq r_{2}
, some probability weight allocated to a given row of
\gamma_{\mathbf{p}}
must necessarily be shared between (at least) two columns, or vice-versa; hence no sense of perfect concordance (or discordance) can ever be found between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
and
|\Upsilon|
cannot be equal to 1.

Now, consider the empirical framework of Section
4
, in which we have
n
copies
\{{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\}
of
{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{1},X_{2})\sim P
, and suppose that we are interested in estimating Yule’s coefficient
\Upsilon
from these data. From (
4.2
), (
4.3
) and (
5.1
), the natural ‘plug-in’ estimator is

\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n}=\Upsilon(\widehat{{\bm{\gamma}}}_{n})=\frac{12}{\sqrt{(r_{1}+1)(r_{1}-1)(r_{2}+1)(r_{2}-1)}}\sum_{i_{1}\in[r_{1}]}\sum_{i_{2}\in[r_{2}]}i_{1}i_{2}\widehat{\gamma}_{n;i_{1}i_{2}}-3\frac{\sqrt{(r_{1}+1)(r_{2}+1)}}{\sqrt{(r_{1}-1)(r_{2}-1)}}

(5.2)

which will be referred to as the
empirical Yule’s coefficient
. Let
\kappa_{r_{1},r_{2}}\doteq\frac{12}{\sqrt{(r_{1}-1)(r_{1}+1)(r_{2}-1)(r_{2}+1)}}
. Then

\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n}-\Upsilon=\kappa_{r_{1},r_{2}}[(r_{2})\otimes(r_{1})]^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}(\widehat{\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}}_{n}-{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}),

and the asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n}
follows as a straightforward consequence of Theorem
4.1
.

.

and a basis

As a particular case, consider

, p. 592)

where

takes a simple form linear in

{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1+\Upsilon}{4}&\frac{1-\Upsilon}{4}\\
\frac{1-\Upsilon}{4}&\frac{1+\Upsilon}{4}\end{pmatrix}.

(5.6)

Here,

{\bm{\Delta}}^{\circ}({\cal R})=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}c&-c\\

a linear subset of dimension

of the set of real

-matrices, for which an obvious (vectorised) basis element is

),

), we get

As also

shows that, from an i.i.d. sample, the empirical Yule’s colligation coefficient; viz.

5.2
Testing for quasi-independence

Quasi-independence refers to random variables that are
independent within their joint support

(
Goodman 1968
)
. For a
d
-dimensional discrete random vector
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
with distribution
P
and probability array
\mathbf{p}
on support
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{p}}
, quasi-independence translates into:

{\mathbf{p}}_{\bm{i}}=\left\{\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\beta^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell}}\right\}\hbox{${\it 1}\hskip-4.5ptI_{\{\bm{i}\in{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}\}}$},\qquad\forall{\bm{i}}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\in{\cal R},

for
\{\beta^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell}}\}_{\ell\in[d],i_{\ell}\in[r_{\ell}]}
a set of positive constants. When
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{p}}=\mathcal{R}
(i.e., when the support is hyper-rectangular), this definition is equivalent to the joint independence of the variables
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
. For a non-hyper-rectangular support, the essence of quasi-independence is that the random variables behave as if they were independent
except
for the constraint that certain combinations of values cannot occur together (i.e., the structural zeros of
{\mathbf{p}}
) – see further discussion in
Geenens 2023
. If
\Gamma({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})\neq\varnothing
(Condition
3.1
), the copula array of a distribution showing quasi-independence on
{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}
is naturally the quasi-independence copula array
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(q,{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})}
in Section
3.4.1
.

Assume that
{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}
satisfies Condition
3.2
, and that a basis for
\underline{{\bm{\Delta}}}^{\circ}({\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})
in (
3.21
) has been identified and formed into a matrix
A_{{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}}
(Section
3.4.3
). Then it appears clearly from Lemma
3.1
that
{\mathbf{p}}
shows quasi-independence if and only if
{\bm{\theta}}={\mathbf{0}}
when its copula array
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathbf{p}}
is expressed as in (
3.19
). Thus, testing for quasi-independence within
{\mathbf{p}}
amounts to testing for the null hypothesis
H_{0}:{\bm{\theta}}={\mathbf{0}}
based on a sample
\{{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\}
drawn from
P
.

As a particular case of a familiar parametric hypothesis test, the procedure is relatively straightforward. From the empirical probability array
\widehat{{\mathbf{p}}}_{n}
in (
4.2
), we can compute
\widehat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{n}
as in (
4.5
) where
{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{\gamma}}^{(q,{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})}+\mathrm{vec}^{-1}(A_{{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}}{\bm{\theta}})
. (Alternatively, from the empirical copula array
\widehat{{\bm{\gamma}}}_{n}
of the sample, we may directly identify
\widehat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{n}
by solving
A_{{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}}}\widehat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{n}=\underline{\widehat{{\bm{\gamma}}}}_{n}-\underline{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{(q,{\cal S}_{\mathbf{p}})}
.) Assume that
\{{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\}
is an i.i.d. sample. Then, Proposition
4.1
(c)
establishes that, under
H_{0}
,
\sqrt{n}\,\widehat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{n}{\overset{\mathcal{L}}{\longrightarrow}\ }{\cal N}_{d_{\circ}}({\mathbf{0}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\theta}})
as
n\to\infty
, and therefore, noting that
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\theta}}
is of (full) rank
d_{\circ}
, it is the case that

n\,\widehat{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}_{n}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}}\widehat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{n}{\overset{\mathcal{L}}{\longrightarrow}\ }\chi^{2}_{d_{\circ}}.

Estimating
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\theta}}
by
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{\theta}};n}
(
4.10
), we may compute
t=n\,\widehat{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}_{n}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}_{{\bm{\theta}};n}\widehat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{n}
for the observed data, which yields an (approximated)
p
-value
p={\mathbb{P}}(W>t)
,
W\sim\chi^{2}_{d_{\circ}}
. The decision follows.

6
Conclusion

The discrete copula construction proposed in
Geenens 2020
opened new perspectives for applying copula-like methods to discrete distributions. The present paper provides full theoretical justification for that construction through Proposition
3.1
, which may be regarded as a discrete analogue of Sklar’s theorem. Articulated around the concept of
{\cal I}
-projection
(
Csiszár 1975
)
on Fréchet classes of distributions, it captures the very essence of the copula approach by establishing a clear separation between marginals and dependence – unlike the classical Sklar’s formalism when applied to discrete distributions
(
Geenens 2024
)
.

Building on this foundation, the paper develops a complete inferential framework for empirical estimation of the discrete copula in arbitrary dimension and for any finite support, including non-hyper-rectangular cases. This extends the bivariate results of
Kojadinovic and Martini 2024
, which were limited to rectangular supports. The empirical discrete copula estimator is defined as the
{\cal I}
-projection of the empirical probability array (i.e., the array of empirical proportions observed for each combination of the variables) onto the relevant uniform-margins Fréchet class. This projection is straightforward to compute in practice via the Iterative Proportional Fitting procedure (IPF, also known as Sinkhorn’s algorithm). Our main theoretical result, Theorem
4.1
, can be viewed as a discrete analogue of the weak convergence of the empirical (continuous) copula process
(
Fermanian et al. 2004
,
Segers 2012
)
, and is expected to underpin all subsequent large-sample inference in the discrete copula setting. It establishes that the empirical discrete copula array is
\sqrt{n}
-consistent and asymptotically normal under the standard assumption of random sampling – though our results are general enough to accommodate more complex settings, including serial dependence in the observations. An explicit ‘sandwich’ form is provided for the estimator’s asymptotic covariance, making it particularly amenable to estimation.

The practical reach of our theory has been illustrated in two examples. First, we derived the asymptotic distribution of Yule’s coefficient of concordance, the natural discrete analogue of Spearman’s
\rho_{\text{S}}
, which allows quantification and inference for the level of concordance in a bivariate discrete vector. Second, we formulated a
\chi^{2}
‐test for quasi‐independence in a multivariate discrete distribution. Both procedures exploit only the empirical copula array, and hence are margin‐free.

Beyond dependence modelling, our results may also inform a variety of other areas. For example, given the perfect analogy between the definition (
2.2
) of the discrete copula and the definition of the entropy-regularised optimal transport plan between discrete distributions
(
Peyré and Cuturi 2019
, equation (4.7))
, Theorem
4.1
may be relevant to empirical optimal transport problems – and more generally any methodology that relies on minimum‐divergence fitting of discrete distributions. Ongoing and future work includes the case of countably infinite supports, which presents its particular challenges, and inference for discrete parametric copula models in higher dimensions, extending bivariate ideas exposed in
Kojadinovic and Martini 2024
. We believe that the theory and tools presented here will become foundational for robust, flexible dependence modelling for discrete data across many applied fields.
