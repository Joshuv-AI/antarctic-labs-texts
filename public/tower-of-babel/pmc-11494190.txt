Integrating machine learning and bioinformatics approaches for identifying novel diagnostic gene biomarkers in colorectal cancer
Authors: Ayoub Vaziri-Moghadam, Mohammad-Hadi Foroughmand-Araabi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to identify diagnostic gene biomarkers for colorectal cancer (CRC) by analyzing differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in tumor and adjacent normal samples across five colon cancer gene-expression profiles (GSE10950, GSE25070, GSE41328, GSE74602, GSE142279) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Intersecting identified DEGs with the module with the highest correlation to gene expression patterns of tumor samples in the gene co-expression network analysis revealed 283 overlapped genes. Centrality measures were calculated for these genes in the reconstructed STRING protein–protein interaction network. Applying LASSO logistic regression, eleven genes were ultimately recognized as candidate diagnostic genes. Among these genes, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) values for nine genes (CDC25B, CDK4, IQGAP3, MMP1, MMP7, SLC7A5, TEAD4, TRIB3, and UHRF1) surpassed the threshold of 0.92 in both the training and validation sets. We evaluated the diagnostic performance of these genes with four machine learning algorithms: random forest (RF), support vector machines (SVM), artificial neural network (ANN), and gradient boosting machine (GBM). In the testing dataset (GSE21815 and GSE106582), the AUROC scores were greater than 0.95 for all of the machine learning algorithms, indicating the high diagnostic performance of the nine genes. Besides, these nine genes are also significantly correlated to twelve immune cells, namely Mast cells activated, Macrophages M0, M1, and M2, Neutrophils, T cells CD4 memory activated, T cells follicular helper, T cells CD8, T cells CD4 memory resting, B cells memory, Plasma cells, and Mast cells resting (P < 0.05). These results strongly suggest that all of the nine genes have the potential to serve as reliable diagnostic biomarkers for CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75438-6.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a significant contributor to global cancer-related mortality. In 2024 alone, it accounted for an estimated 152,810 new cases and 53,010 deaths, underscoring its importance and making it the second most common cause of cancer-related death in the United States1. The incidence rate of CRC is on the rise, particularly in developing countries. In these nations, the projected surge of 2.5 million new CRC cases by 2035 emphasizes the escalating global burden2,3. Technological advancements in omics analysis, including microarrays4, RNA-seq5, and next-generation sequencing (NGS)6, have revolutionized the landscape of molecular markers for CRC diagnosis7. Recent studies employing gene microarrays and high-throughput sequencing technologies have explored the differential expression of novel genes in CRC8. For example, Wei et al. identified CLCA1 as a potential diagnostic biomarker for CRC9, while Li et al. identified CDK1 and CDC20 as candidate targets for CRC diagnosis10.

Machine learning (ML), which relies on a series of complex algorithms, has recently become commonly used to identify biomarkers and predict a wide range of diseases. For example, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) is used to identify biomarkers for various carcinoma11,12. Additionally, support vector machines (SVM) analysis13 is used to construct radiomics-based models for diagnosing lung adenocarcinoma, gradient boosting machine (GBM) analysis14 for microbiota studies, random forest (RF) analysis15 for biomarker detection, and artificial neural network (ANN) analysis16 for identifying immune-related signatures.

In this study, we aim to leverage data from the gene expression omnibus (GEO) database to carefully curate multiple microarray datasets comprising both CRC tumors and adjacent normal tissues. The primary objective is to identify potential diagnostic gene biomarkers by employing a combination of bioinformatics analyses and machine learning methods.

Materials and methods

Data collection and processing

The GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) is a publicly accessible functional genomics database. In the GEO database, we initially searched for the keyword ‘Colorectal cancer’ and then screened the data using the following criteria: (1) Homo sapiens array expression profile; (2) Diagnosed as CRC in humans; (3) Contained both CRC tumor tissue and adjacent normal tissue samples; (4) The dataset contains more than 20 samples; (5) The dataset contains complete information about the sample. Among the identified datasets, we selected the balanced datasets as the training group because a balanced dataset avoids bias towards the majority class overfitting error, enabling accurate assessment of ML model performance, enhancing model generalization, and ensuring reliable predictions on test datasets17. The remaining datasets were designated as the test group. Subsequently, we merged all datasets of the training group based on their common genes. Then, we randomly split the samples into a training set and a validation set in a 7:3 ratio. After that, we corrected batch effects in both the training and validation sets using the ‘ComBat’ algorithm18 within the ‘sva’ R package19 (version 3.46.0). The training set aided in the identification of potential diagnostic biomarkers, while the validation set served to validate the analysis results. Moreover, the test group was used for evaluating the diagnostic performance of the biomarkers.

Screening for differentially expressed genes

In the training set, we performed differential gene expression analysis between normal and cancer samples using the ‘limma’ package within the 'R' software (version 3.54.0)20. To ensure robust results, the P-value was adjusted to control the false discovery rate (FDR), employing the Benjamini and Hochberg method. Differential expression genes (DEGs) were considered statistically significant under the conditions |logFC|> 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05.

Functional enrichment analysis

To explore the biological significance of the identified DEGs, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses21–23. These analyses were conducted using the ‘clusterProfiler’ package in R (version 4.6.0)24. The GO database, which includes molecular function (MF), biological process (BP), and cellular components (CC), was utilized to provide a comprehensive understanding of the functional roles of the DEGs.

Gene co-expression network analysis

For the construction of a gene co-expression network within the training set, we employed the R package ‘CEMiTool’ (version 1.24.0)25. To ensure robust and consistent results, we utilized the default parameters in 'CEMiTool,' including the automatic selection of the β parameter. To gauge differences in module activity between the tumor and normal groups, ‘CEMiTool’ conducted a gene set enrichment analysis using the ‘fgsea’ R package (version 1.26.0)26. This analytical approach enabled us to pinpoint modules that exhibited significant correlations with the tumor and normal groups.

PPI network construction and centrality analysis

We initiated the construction of the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network by importing the intersection of DEGs and the significant module genes identified by the ‘CEMiTool’ into the ‘STRING’ database (https://string-db.org/). Various centrality measures, based on network topological features, have been established to identify nodes that significantly impact the entire network. Our goal was to find the most informative measure for identifying the most important nodes in the PPI network. To achieve this, we conducted network centrality analysis using the ‘CINNA’ R package (version 1.2.0)27. Before delving into centrality analysis, we extracted the giant component of the PPI network for further analysis. This step was essential as centrality measures are only applicable to connected components. Next, we applied the principal component analysis (PCA) algorithm within the ‘CINNA’ package to calculate and visualize network centrality measures. Upon identifying the most informative centrality measure, we assigned scores to nodes using this measure. In the final step, we selected nodes with scores surpassing the mean value of scores for all nodes. These nodes were considered as the key genes within the PPI network.

Screening for potential biomarkers

LASSO was performed to screen the candidate genes using the ‘glmnet’ R package (version 4.1.6)28 with ‘binomial’ as the family for classification of CRC samples and normal samples. This process involved 'tenfold cross-validation’ to assess the model’s performance. In this process, the training set, containing 154 samples and 113 key genes extracted from the PPI network analysis, was fed into the LASSO model. Genes with non-zero regression coefficients were identified as candidate diagnostic gene biomarkers. Additionally, the diagnostic efficiency of these genes was validated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves generated with the ‘pROC’ R package (version 1.18.0)29. The ROC curve evaluates a binary classifier’s performance by applying various decision thresholds to gene expression scores, determining cutoffs for classification. The ROC plot visualizes the trade-off between true positive rate and false positive rate across different thresholds, aiding in assessing classifier effectiveness. We selected genes with area under the curve (AUC) scores exceeding 0.9 in both the training and validation sets to ensure their robust diagnostic potential.

Validation of biomarkers using ML method

We utilized four machine-learning algorithms—SVM, RF, GBM, and ANN—for the classification of CRC and normal samples. The R package ‘e1071’ (version 1.7.15)30 was applied to the SVM algorithm with a ‘linear’ kernel. The R package ‘randomForest’ (version 4.7.1.1)31 was adopted for the RF algorithm with 500 trees. The ‘h2o’ package (version 3.44.0.3) was used for the GBM algorithm with 100 trees. The ANN model was implemented using the ‘neuralnet’ R package (version 1.44.2)32. The model consisted of three layers: input layer with as many nodes as the number of diagnostic genes, hidden layer, and output layers with two nodes (representing tumor and normal samples). The diagnostic performance of these four models was evaluated by measuring the AUROC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score.

Immune cell infiltration and enrichment analysis

CIBERSORT (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/), a bioinformatics algorithm, was performed for immune cell infiltration analysis using ‘IOBR’ R package (version 0.99.8)33. The putative abundance of immune cells was estimated using a reference set with 22 types of immune cell subtypes with 1,000 permutations. A correlation heatmap was produced using the ‘corrplot’ R package (version 0.92) to detect the association of each immune cell with the other cells. Violin plots visualized the differences in immune cells between the CRC tumor group and the normal group. A Spearman correlation analysis was performed between diagnostic biomarkers and infiltrating immune cells using the R ‘ggplot2’ package. Finally, the R package ‘genekitr’ (version 1.2.5)34 was used to enrich diagnostic genes through over-representation analysis, which utilizes the expression data of the 9 previously identified genes and returns the biological processes in which each gene participates.

The batch effect correction was applied separately on these sets (Fig. 2a-d). We also used two independent gene expression datasets, GSE21815 and GSE106582 (Table 1) as a separate group for testing models and diagnostic biomarkers. Screening for DEGs and enrichment analysis

We performed differential gene expression analysis on the training set. We applied a criterion of |logFC|> 1 and an adjusted P-value < 0.05 for screening differentially expressed genes (DEGs). This process identified a total of 802 DEGs, with 331 being down-regulated and 471 up-regulated. First, we visualized the distribution of these DEGs through two types of plots: a volcano plot (Fig. 2f) and a heatmap (Supplementary Fig. S1). We then conducted a functional analysis of the DEGs using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). In the GO analysis, we found that the DEGs were primarily enriched in specific biological process terms, including 'mitotic cell cycle phase transition’, 'extracellular matrix organization’, 'extracellular structure organization’, 'external encapsulating structure organization’, and ‘nuclear division’. Among the cellular component terms, 'collagen-containing extracellular matrix’ is prominent. In the molecular function, 'extracellular matrix structure constituent’, ‘cytokine activity’, and 'receptor ligand activity’ were the top three terms (Fig. 2g). Further KEGG functional analysis indicated that these genes were predominantly enriched in the ‘cell cycle’ pathway (Fig. 2h).

Gene co-expression network analysis

In the training set, we employed gene co-expression network analysis using the R package CEMiTool to identify the most associated co-expression module in the tumor and normal groups. For adherence to the scale-free topology, CEMiTool considers only β values with R2 > 0.80. This led to a power of β = 12 with scale-free R2 = 0.9 (Supplementary Fig. S2). In order to assess differences in module activity between tumor and normal groups, a gene set enrichment analysis was performed with the default values in CEMiTool (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table S1). The enrichment score (ES), obtained from the gene set enrichment analysis, is normalized by taking into account the size of each gene set, leading to a normalized ES (NES). The proportion of false positives is controlled by calculating the false discovery rate corresponding to each NES and an adjustment of the respective p-value. Thus, the modules contain genes which present high correlation in their expression in different situations, while with the gene-enrichment analysis among the highly correlated clusters of genes, we select the most significant clusters (modules) with the association between their expression and the objective value, which is being cancerous or normal cell. For the selection phase, we use two criteria: p-value and NES. The lower the adjusted p-value and the higher the absolute value of NES, the more association the module has with the tumor or normal group. Ultimately, five co-expression modules were identified. Among the identified modules, Module M1 exhibited the strongest positive associations with the tumor group. PPI network construction and centrality analysis

The 283 genes, obtained from the intersection of the DEGs and module M1 (Fig. 3c), were imported into the STRING database to construct the PPI network. The obtained PPI network includes 282 nodes and 2633 edges. Before conducting centrality analysis, the giant component of the PPI network, including 244 nodes and 2633 edges, was extracted, and 38 measures with appropriate centrality measures based on the giant component structure were calculated. Centrality analysis was then performed on the giant component to identify the most informative centrality measure. Results showed 'Closeness centrality Latora’ and ‘Harmonic Centrality’ making the most significant contribution to the first two PCA coefficients, establishing them as the most informative centrality measures in the giant component (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Table S2). After calculating the scores of nodes using these measures, we observed that the results of both measures were completely the same, that is, both these measures ranked the nodes in the same order and nodes with scores greater than the mean score were the same in both measures. Therefore, these nodes (113 nodes) were selected as key genes for downstream analyses (Fig. 3d).

Screening for potential biomarkers

We applied the LASSO model to find candidate diagnostic genes. We identified 11 candidate genes (CDC25B, CDK4, GZMB, IQGAP3, MCM4, MMP1, MMP7, SLC7A5, TEAD4, TRIB3, and UHRF1) for the lambda value obtained while minimizing the objective function (λ = 0.00239) of the LASSO algorithm (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. S4 and Table S3). Among these genes, those with lower coefficients showed a high correlation with a high number of the rest of these genes. Specifically, genes with coefficients smaller than 0.5 and positive correlations had a correlation coefficient greater than 0.7 with 7 or more genes (Supplementary Fig. S5 and Table S4). This observation confirmed that low coefficients could be due to the high number of correlating genes. We then assessed the accuracy of these 11 genes in distinguishing CRC tumor samples from normal samples. The results showed that the AUROC values of 9 genes were higher than 0.92 both in the training group and validation group: AUCs = 0.9879 for gene CDC25B, 0.9664 for gene CDK4, 0.872 for gene GZMB, 0.9371 for gene IQGAP3, 0.8941 for gene MCM4, 0.9745 for gene MMP1, 0.9863 for gene MMP7, 0.9777 for gene SLC7A5, 0.9921 for gene TEAD4, 0.9889 for gene TRIB3, 0.9516 for gene UHRF1 in the training set; AUCs = 0.9559 for gene CDC25B, 0.9633 for gene CDK4, 0.8237 for gene GZMB, 0.9431 for gene IQGAP3, 0.9651 for gene MCM4, 0.9284 for gene MMP1, 0.9936 for gene MMP7, 0.9853 for gene SLC7A5, 0.9486 for gene TEAD4, 0.9881 for gene TRIB3, 0.9559 for gene UHRF1 in the validation set (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S6). Validation of biomarkers using ML method

Four machine learning models—random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), artificial neural network (ANN), and gradient boosting machine (GBM)—were successfully established to validate the diagnostic performance of the 9 diagnostic genes obtained. The performance of these models was assessed using AUROC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score. The ANN model consisted of three layers: an input layer with 9 nodes (corresponding to the number of input diagnostic genes), a hidden layer with 5 nodes, and an output layer with two nodes (representing tumor and normal). The AUROC scores for each of the four models were higher than 0.95 for the test group (Fig. 5a, b). In the GSE21815 test dataset, the SVM model achieved an AUROC score of 0.982, with accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score values of 0.978, 0.984, 0.888, and 0.988, respectively. Additionally, in the GSE106582 test dataset, the RF model achieved an AUROC score of 0.980, with accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score values of 0.958, 0.948, 0.965, and 0.948, respectively. These results indicate that the prediction performance of the SVM and RF models, considering the slight differences in performance between them, is superior to that of the other models tested in the GSE21815 and GSE106582 datasets, respectively (Table 2). Enrichment analysis and immune cell infiltration

We conducted an over-representation enrichment analysis to identify the biological processes associated with the nine diagnostic genes (Fig. 5c). This analysis revealed several associations. Specifically, CDC25B, CDK4, and IQGAP3 genes were jointly linked to the 'Mitotic cell cycle transition’. Additionally, MMP1 and MMP7 genes were enriched in processes related to 'Extracellular matrix organization’, 'Extracellular structure organization’, and 'External encapsulating structure organization’. Furthermore, UHRF1, IQGAP3, and CDK4 genes were jointly associated with 'Epithelial cell proliferation’ and its regulation. Lastly, SLC7A5 and IQGAP3 genes were related to ‘Gland development’.

We analyzed the phenotypes of 22 immune cell types across 220 samples from the five microarrays to investigate their association with both CRC and normal groups. The correlation matrix revealed the strongest positive correlation between 'Mast cells activated’ and ‘Neutrophils’ and the strongest negative correlation between 'Mast cells activated’ and ‘Macrophages M2’ (Fig. 5d). Macrophages M2, T cells CD4 memory resting, Plasma cells, B cells memory, B cells naive, T cells regulatory (Tregs), Eosinophils, and Mast cells resting were the most down-regulated cells in CRC. In contrast, Mast cells activated, T cells CD8, T cells CD4 memory activated, T cells follicular helper, Dendritic cells activated, Neutrophils, M0 and M1 Macrophages were the most up-regulated ones (P < 0.05; Fig. 5e). The correlation analysis of the 9 diagnostic genes obtained and 22 kinds of immune cells can help us speculate how these genes participate in the course of CRC by regulating the infiltration of immune cells. Discussion

Colonoscopy is a primary tool for initial colorectal cancer (CRC) diagnosis, but some cases may require further examinations. Diagnostic biomarkers can complement colonoscopy in CRC detection, especially in cases requiring more precise diagnoses. For patients already diagnosed with CRC, monitoring the expression of specific biomarkers present in a patient’s sample can provide more granular insights into disease progression and personalization of treatment. A similar example is seen in breast cancer, where three biomarkers estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and HER2, are widely used in treatment planning to ensure alignment with the patient’s biological makeup35. We hope the current study and similar studies contribute to providing similar protocols for CRC.

Currently the colonoscopy is the main line of CRC detection, despite the advantages of colonoscopy in its high precision, its repeatability poses practical challenges, including patient discomfort, cost, and insufficient sample sizes. To address these challenges, the assessingment of gene biomarkers derived from biopsy samples can complement colonoscopy by providing a molecular perspective on an individual’s risk for CRC. These biomarkers can identify genetic alterations associated with CRC, even in the absence of visible abnormalities during colonoscopy.

The bioinformatics analysis of the microarray dataset from the GEO database identified 802 genes that are expressed differently between the CRC tumor and normal samples. Through the functional enrichment analysis, we found three pathways highly correlated with these genes, including ‘cell cycle’, 'cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction’, and 'Cytoskeleton in muscle cells’ signaling pathways. The cell cycle plays a significant role in the entire process of cancer, and dysregulated genes could affect cancer development by influencing the cell cycle36. Studies have shown that colorectal cancer cell lines exhibit various expression patterns of cell cycle regulators, which may indicate differences in cancer cell biology37. The cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction networks are considered crucial in inflammation and tumor immunology, particularly for colorectal cancer38. Cancer cells often exhibit alterations in the cytoskeleton, which can contribute to their invasive and metastatic capabilities. For instance, changes in the cytoskeleton can affect cell proliferation, cell cycle regulation, and evasion of apoptosis, which are critical processes in cancer development and progression39.

In the Gene Ontology analysis, we identified significant enrichment in the following biological processes: 'Mitotic Cell Cycle Phase Transition’, 'Extracellular Matrix Organization and Its Structure’, 'External Encapsulating Structure Organization’, and ‘Nuclear Division’. The mitotic cell cycle, a series of events leading to the duplication of the cell’s DNA and the division of the cell into two daughter cells, is tightly regulated by checkpoints that ensure the DNA is correctly replicated and that any errors are corrected before cell division proceeds. In cancer, cells often exhibit defects in cell cycle checkpoints, such as the inhibition of cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) activity or the failure to enter checkpoint-dependent cell cycle arrest. This allows cancer cells to bypass the G1/S checkpoint, where DNA replication errors are typically detected and corrected, leading to the accumulation of mutations and chromosomal instability40. The extracellular matrix (ECM) is one of the major components of tumors that plays multiple crucial roles, including mechanical support, modulation of the microenvironment, and a source of signaling molecules. The quantity and cross-linking status of ECM components are major factors determining tissue stiffness. During tumorigenesis, the interplay between cancer cells and the tumor microenvironment often results in the stiffness of the ECM, leading to aberrant mechanotransduction and further malignant transformation41. The external encapsulating structure, including the ECM, plays a critical role in the metastasis. Alterations in this process can lead to changes in the ECM composition and organization, which can affect the tumor’s ability to invade surrounding tissues and metastasize. The ECM can provide a scaffold for cancer cells to invade surrounding tissues and can influence the development of new blood vessels (angiogenesis), which is essential for metastasis42.

Due to their flexibility and power, a combination of bioinformatics analysis and machine learning is increasingly being used to screen novel biomarkers. We employed this approach to identify diagnostic gene biomarkers for CRC, and obtained CDC25B, CDK4, IQGAP3, MMP1, MMP7, SLC7A5, TEAD4, TRIB3, and UHRF1 genes. These nine genes showed significant associations with 12 types of immune cells, including activated mast cells, neutrophils, activated CD4 memory T cells, follicular helper T cells, macrophages M0, macrophages M1, macrophages M2, resting mast cells, plasma cells, memory B cells, CD8 T cells, and resting CD4 memory T cells. We found that activated mast cells differed most between normal and CRC tissue samples and correlated closely with all of the nine biomarkers, implying that activated mast cells may be the most active immune cells in CRC. Mast cells are immune cells that accumulate in the tumors and their microenvironment during disease progression. They are equipped with a wide array of receptors that sense environmental modifications and secrete several biologically active factors involved in the modulation of tumor growth. For example, mast cells can secrete pro-angiogenic and growth factors but also pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators43. This analysis shows that nine selected genes reflect characteristics of the tumor, which are not only differentially expressed in tumor cells but also are differentially expressed in immune cells. Although we don’t know the mechanism behind this observed similarity, the results alone show that these genes have a decisive role in all the cells inside the tumor.

Cell Division Cycle 25B (CDC25B) plays a pivotal role in the progression from the G2 phase to mitosis (M phase) of the cell cycle44. Overexpression of CDC25B has been identified as a potential oncogene, contributing to the advancement of cancer cells during the process of tumorigenesis45,46. Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), particularly CDK4, play a crucial role in regulating the G1-S transition of the cell cycle, a pivotal phase for initiating DNA synthesis47. CDK4’s role extends beyond cell cycle management, affecting normal development and contributing to tumorigenesis48. IQGAP3, a gene that encodes a protein involved in various cellular processes, is crucial for the proliferation of isthmus stem cells, playing a role in tissue regeneration and the maintenance of stem cell properties. It interacts with the Ras pathway, enhancing cell proliferation, and is implicated in the pathogenesis of gastric cancer by promoting cell proliferation49. Matrix metalloproteinase 1 (MMP1) is a collagenase that degrades collagens I, II, and III, playing a role in extracellular matrix remodeling. Overexpression of MMP1 in colorectal and esophageal cancers is linked to poor prognosis, indicating its contribution to cancer progression through enhanced tumor invasion and metastasis50,51. Matrix metalloproteinase 7 (MMP7), the smallest member of the matrix metalloproteinase family, is significantly involved in cancer progression, particularly in metastasis. Its oncogenic role in the carcinogenesis and metastasis of tongue cancer highlights its potential as a therapeutic target52. Solute carrier family 7 member 5 (SLC7A5) functions as a sodium-independent transporter, facilitating the exchange of large neutral amino acids like leucine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan for intracellular glutamine, thereby playing a crucial role in amino acid transport and metabolism53. TEAD4 is vital for cell proliferation, survival, tissue regeneration, and the preservation of stem cell properties. Several studies identify TEAD4 as a promising prognostic indicator for gastric, breast, and colorectal cancers54–56. Tribbles pseudokinase 3 (TRIB3) is a member of the pseudokinase gene family and regulates a series of physiological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism, and cellular stress57. Ubiquitin-like PHD and ring finger domain protein 1 (UHRF1) is a critical gene involved in DNA methylation, a process vital for gene expression regulation. It encodes a protein that facilitates the maintenance of DNA methylation patterns post-replication, ensuring genomic stability and playing a significant role in cancer development due to its impact on gene expression58. Therefore, these genes play critical roles in various aspects of cellular biology, from cell cycle progression and tissue regeneration to cancer development and progression. Their dysregulation or overexpression can lead to pathological conditions, particularly cancer, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets for treating these diseases.

Previous studies have identified eleven genes (ASCL2, BEST4, CFD, DPEPCFD1, FOXQ1, KLF4, MMP7, MMP11, PYY, PDK4, and TRIB3) as diagnostic genes in the CRC59. In coherence with those results, in our study the AUROC values exceeded 0.91 for all 11 key genes in the training set. However, in the validation set, the AUROC reached only approximately 0.7. This suggests that while the constructed model demonstrates robust validation performance, further improvement is needed for its test performance. In the present study, we identified nine potential biomarkers, namely CDC25B, CDK4, IQGAP3, MMP1, MMP7, SLC7A5, TEAD4, TRIB3, and UHRF1, through a combination of bioinformatics analyses and machine learning models. Our results indicate that two genes, MMP7 and TRIB3, overlap with those mentioned in the previous study. Consequently, we have identified seven new genes in addition to those previously reported. The AUROC values for these nine genes exceeded 0.93 in the training set and 0.92 in the validation set. Furthermore, we validated the diagnostic performance of the nine genes using different machine learning methods, achieving AUROC values of greater than 0.98 in the test dataset. This high level of accuracy suggests that these nine genes exhibit a substantial ability to distinguish tumor samples from normal ones, surpassing the capabilities observed in previous studies.

A potential future of CRC diagnostics could be towards the use of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) due to its non-invasive and cheap nature. However, research indicates that ctDNA obtained with current technologies, has not proven helpful in early cancer detection, especially for tumors smaller than 1 cm60,61. With more advanced technologies, ctDNA can offer insights into tumor-specific mutations and genetic alterations, while mRNA analysis also can complement this information. Integrating these approaches could enhance the precision and accessibility of diagnosis, leading to wider CRC screening programs by governments. Also in the case of data availability, it could be a potential research direction to adapt our machine learning methods on the combination of circulating DNA for diagnosis.

Conclusion

In this study, we utilized a combination of bioinformatics and machine learning analyses to identify a novel nine-gene signature, including CDC25B, CDK4, IQGAP3, MMP1, MMP7, SLC7A5, TEAD4, TRIB3, and UHRF1. The application of machine learning methods in this research demonstrated the capability of this approach in discovering these genes. This was followed by functional enrichment analysis and immune correlation analysis. We found that activated mast cells differed most between normal and CRC tissue samples and correlated closely with all nine biomarkers, implying that activated mast cells may be the most active immune cells in CRC Additionally, the diagnostic performance of these genes was validated using various machine-learning models. Consequently, these genes are anticipated to serve as novel diagnostic markers for CRC. However, further verification studies are necessary.
