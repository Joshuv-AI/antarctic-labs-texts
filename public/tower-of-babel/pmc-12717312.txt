Global trends in prostate cancer burden among adults aged 80 years and older from 1990 to 2021 based on the global burden of disease 2021
Authors: Xin Zhu, Yazhe Zhou, Xin Yang, Kaizhuo Zhou, Mi Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the global, regional, and national burden of prostate cancer (PCa) among adults aged 80 years and older (≥ 80 years) from 1990 to 2021.MethodsUsing data from the Global Burden of Disease 2021 (GBD 2021) study, we systematically analyzed PCa incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) among populations aged ≥ 80 years across 204 countries and territories. Patients were grouped by age (80–84, 85–89, 90–94, and ≥ 95 years), and burden was assessed by geographic region and socio-demographic index (SDI). Temporal trends were examined using Joinpoint regression to calculate the annual percentage change (APC) and average annual percentage change (AAPC), with 95% confidence intervals. Regional disparities were visualized using map-based analysis, and the contributions of risk factors were assessed. Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) models projected future trends to 2040.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, the incidence rate of PCa in those aged ≥ 80 years showed an overall decline (AAPC = -0.43%, 95% CI -0.52 to -0.35), while the absolute number of incidence, deaths, and DALYs increased steadily across all age groups, potentially associated with population aging. Incidence and mortality rates peaked in the mid-1990s, then fell most rapidly between 2007 and 2013, especially in high- and high-middle SDI regions. In contrast, low- and low-middle SDI regions experienced slower declines or persistent increases. In 2021, the highest incidence and mortality rates occurred in select Caribbean nations, while the lowest rates were found in Central Asia. Smoking remained the leading modifiable risk factor, but its impact decreased in higher SDI regions. Dietary factors, especially milk and calcium intake, were protective. BAPC modeling suggests that the decline in incidence, mortality, and DALYs is likely to continue through 2040, although uncertainty in projections increases over time, especially in low-SDI regions.ConclusionsWhile substantial global progress has been made in reducing PCa incidence, mortality, and DALYs among adults aged ≥ 80 years, significant disparities persist, especially in low-resource settings, mainly due to differences in detection, treatment, and risk reduction strategies. Ongoing efforts to promote equitable access to early detection, effective treatment, and risk factor mitigation are essential to further reduce the burden of PCa in the aging population worldwide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04028-3.Keywords: Prostate cancer, Incidence, Mortality, DALYs, SDI

Background

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting men worldwide, with an estimated 313,780 new cases projected in the United States for 2025, accounting for approximately 30% of all incident cancer cases in men [1]. It is also a significant contributor to mortality, with 35,770 deaths anticipated, representing 11% of male cancer-related fatalities [1]. Among elderly populations, particularly those aged 80 years and older (≥ 80 years)—often referred to as “super-elderly” or “very old” men in the literature—PCa presents a significant public health challenge owing to its high prevalence and associated morbidity and mortality [2]. The rising global life expectancy and the widespread adoption of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening have contributed to an obvious increase in PCa diagnoses among this age group over the past two decades [3]. However, therapeutic decisions in this population are complex, as clinicians must weigh the potential life-prolonging benefits of aggressive cancer treatment against the possibility of unnecessary intervention in cases where the malignancy may not significantly affect life expectancy. Consistent with these challenges, literature has shown that while elderly men are more frequently diagnosed with higher-risk PCa, they are less likely to receive curative treatment compared to their younger counterparts, contributing to the complexities of management and survival outcomes [4].

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study offers a comprehensive framework for assessing the impact of diseases and injuries across diverse populations and time periods. By employing standardized metrics such as incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), the GBD study offers valuable insights into temporal trends and regional disparities in disease burden [5, 6]. Currently, research analyzing the burden of PCa based on GBD data has primarily focused on global patterns and specific analyses for certain countries and regions—including the USA, China, Brazil, Mexico, North Africa and the Middle East, and various parts of Asia—in both the general population and targeted age groups such as adolescents and young adult males aged 15–49 years [7–18]. However, despite the breadth of existing research on PCa burden in the general population, a significant gap remains in our understanding of its trends among adults aged ≥ 80 years.

This study aims to systematically evaluate the global burden of PCa among adults aged ≥ 80 years from 1990 to 2021, utilizing data from the GBD 2021. By analyzing trends in incidence, mortality, and DALYs, we aim to identify both regional and temporal variations to inform public health strategies, guide resource allocation, and support the development of targeted interventions for this vulnerable and rapidly expanding population.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional design to systematically assess the global burden of PCa using data from the GBD 2021 database.

Data resources

This study utilized data from the GBD 2021 database, which provides a comprehensive assessment of health risks associated with 371 diseases and injuries, as well as 88 risk factors, across 204 countries and territories and 811 subnational regions [19]. Data specific to PCa among adults aged ≥ 80 years, including incidence, mortality, and DALYs, from 1990 to 2021, were retrieved via the Global Health Data Exchange platform (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool).

Data management

Data management involved several key steps to ensure data integrity and reliability. The data from GBD 2021 were retrieved and processed uniformly in data handling. Variable definitions were harmonized to facilitate accurate comparisons across different geographical regions and time periods. For age distribution analysis of the disease burden of PCa among adults aged ≥ 80 years, patients were divided into four specific age groups: 80–84 years, 85–89 years, 90–94 years, and ≥ 95 years.

Global and regional burden analysis

We created global maps and conducted regional comparative analyses to explore the worldwide distribution and regional disparities in the burden of PCa among adults aged ≥ 80 years. The data were categorized according to the geographical regions defined in the GBD study. To visualize the disease burden, we utilized R (version 4.4.2) with the ‘ggplot2’ and ‘sf’ packages for map generation. Regional and national variations were assessed by stratifying the data according to the GBD-defined socio-demographic index (SDI), which categorizes countries into five groups based on income, education, and fertility rates [20, 21].

Temporal trend analysis

The annual percentage change (APC) and average annual percentage change (AAPC) were calculated using the Joinpoint regression model to quantify the direction and magnitude of changes over time and identify significant inflection points in the trends. The Estimated Annual Percentage Change (EAPC) was calculated using linear regression models, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to evaluate statistical significance [22, 23].

Estimation of risk factors

Attributable risk factors for disease were evaluated in the GBD database at four primary levels: Level 1 risk factors include behavioral, environmental, occupational, and metabolic factors; Level 2 risk factors include 20 risks or clusters of risks; Level 3 comprises 52 risk factors or clusters of risks, while Level 4 encompasses 69 specific risk factors [24]. Our analysis explicitly examines the PCa-related mortality rates and DALY rates attributed to these risk factors across five SDI regions, as well as on a global scale.

Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model for forecasting

To estimate the future burden of PCa among patients aged ≥ 80 years, a Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model was utilized. This model, implemented through the “INLA” and “BAPC” packages in R, enabled projections of the disease’s incidence and prevalence up to 2040. By incorporating the influences of age, period, and cohort, the model offers a robust framework for analyzing and predicting trends in disease burden [25].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were generated for all key variables, and results were expressed as means with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). For trend analyses, p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study utilized publicly available GBD 2021 data, which does not require ethical approval since no primary data involving humans were collected. All data were anonymized before analysis, ensuring ethical compliance.

Results

Global trends

Incidence

As shown in Table 1, the number of new PCa cases in individuals aged ≥ 80 years globally increased from 96,649 in 1990 to 259,173 in 2021. However, the incidence rate declined from 486.49 to 423.17 per 100,000 during this period, with an EAPC of −0.81% (95% CI −0.96 to −0.65), indicating a significant downward trend globally. Consistent with this, from 1990 to 2021, the incidence rate of PCa patients aged ≥ 80 years exhibited an overall significant decline, with an AAPC of −0.49% (95% CI −0.56, −0.41). Specifically, the incidence rate rose between 1990 and 1995 (APC = 2.18%, 95% CI 2.03, 2.33), then gradually declined from 1995 to 2007 (APC = −0.99%, 95% CI −1.04, −0.94), and decreased more sharply from 2007 to 2013 (APC = −1.97%, 95% CI −2.12, −1.83). A brief upward trend was observed from 2013 to 2015 (APC = 0.49%, 95% CI −0.39, 1.38), followed by a moderate decline from 2015 to 2021 (APC = −0.54%, 95% CI −0.69, −0.40). These patterns indicate a significant overall reduction in PCa incidence over the past three decades, with the most pronounced decline occurring between 2007 and 2013 (Fig. 1A).

Mortality

As shown in Supplementary Table 1, global PCa deaths among those aged ≥ 80 years rose from 76,810 in 1990 to 194,576 in 2021. Meanwhile, the mortality rate declined from 386.63 to 317.70 per 100,000 (EAPC = −0.91%, 95% CI −1.05, −0.78), showing a clear downward trend. Regarding temporal trends, the mortality rate exhibited a similar pattern, with an initial increase from 1990 to 1995 (APC = 1.48%, 95% CI 1.23, 1.74), peaking in 1995 at approximately 420 deaths per 100,000 population (Fig. 1B). Thereafter, the mortality rate declined in three distinct phases: a gradual decrease from 1995 to 2001 (APC = −0.75%, 95% CI −0.99, −0.50), a more pronounced decline from 2001 to 2012 (APC = −1.59%, 95% CI −1.69, −1.49), and a slower decline from 2012 to 2021 (APC = −0.48%, 95% CI −0.61, −0.34) (Fig. 1B). As shown in Fig. 2C, the number of PCa deaths has increased steadily across all age groups, most prominently among men aged 80–84 years, reflecting the expanding elderly population over time. In contrast, Fig. 2D demonstrates a consistent decline in mortality rates for all age groups, with the most substantial reductions observed in individuals aged 80–84 years, followed by those aged 85–89 years.

DALYs

As shown in Supplementary Table 2, global DALYs due to PCa in individuals aged ≥ 80 years increased from 911,282 in 1990 to 2,222,195 in 2021. Despite this rise, the DALY rate declined from 4,586.99 to 3628.37 per 100,000 during the same period (EAPC = −1.03%, 95% CI −1.15 to −0.91). Regarding temporal trends of DALYs, the trend in DALYs closely mirrored those of incidence and mortality. DALYs increased slightly from 1990 to 1995 (APC = 1.31%), peaking in 1995 at approximately 4900. After 1995, DALYs declined through several distinct phases: a modest decrease from 1995 to 2002 (APC = −0.94%), followed by a steeper decline from 2002 to 2008 (APC = −1.56%) and an even greater decline from 2008 to 2013 (APC = −1.89%). A slight uptick was observed from 2013 to 2015 (APC = 0.33%), followed by a moderate decline from 2015 to 2021 (APC = −0.90%) (Fig. 1C). As shown in Fig. 2E, the absolute number of DALYs increased consistently across all age groups. In contrast, Fig. 2F illustrates a sustained decline in the DALY rate over time. The most pronounced reductions were observed in the younger cohorts (80–84 and 85–89 years), while the older age groups (90–94 and 95 + years) experienced a slower rate of decline, with occasional fluctuations or plateaus in recent years.

Trends in the five SDI regions

Globally and in high- and high-middle SDI regions, the incidence rates of PCa aged ≥ 80 years peaked in the mid-1990s and declined steadily thereafter. Middle SDI regions exhibited a delayed peak in incidence rates, followed by a more gradual decline in rates. In contrast, low-middle and low SDI regions experienced a consistent increase in incidence rates throughout the observed period (Fig. 3). Mortality trends closely mirrored the patterns observed in incidence, with substantial declines in high and high-middle SDI regions after the mid-1990s. Middle SDI regions showed moderate declines, whereas low-middle and low SDI regions exhibited either a gradual decrease or an increasing trend in mortality, particularly in low SDI regions where mortality rates consistently rose (Supplementary Fig. 1). Similarly, DALYs trends showed a global peak in the mid-1990s, followed by substantial declines in high SDI regions. In contrast, DALYs burdens in low SDI regions showed either persistence or an upward trend over time (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Trends in the countries

In 2021, there were substantial international disparities in the incidence, mortality, and DALY rates of PCa among men aged ≥ 80 years (Supplementary Table 3, Fig. 4). The highest incidence rates were reported in Saint Kitts and Nevis (2,710.92 per 100,000), Antigua and Barbuda (2,503.45), and Grenada (2,455.68), reflecting a hefty burden in the Caribbean region. These same countries also topped the list for mortality and DALY rates, with Saint Kitts and Nevis showing the highest mortality rate (2,457.88) and DALY rate (28,005.72), followed by Grenada and Antigua and Barbuda. Conversely, the lowest incidence, mortality, and DALYs rates were found in Central Asian countries, including Tajikistan (incidence: 44.23; mortality: 52.32; DALYs: 613.30), Uzbekistan, and Turkmenistan.

Risk factors

In individuals aged ≥ 80 years with PCa, the attributable proportions of deaths and DALYs due to behavioral and dietary risk factors demonstrated distinct patterns globally and across SDI regions between 1990 and 2021. Smoking consistently exhibited positive attributable fractions, indicating a harmful effect on both mortality and DALYs. In contrast, dietary factors such as low calcium intake and low milk consumption consistently showed negative values, denoting a protective impact. Globally, the contribution of smoking to deaths and DALYs declined markedly in high- and high-middle SDI regions, while remaining relatively stable or decreasing gradually in low- and low-middle SDI regions. Dietary protective effects, particularly from higher intakes of milk and calcium, appeared stable over time, with more pronounced impacts observed in lower SDI regions compared to higher SDI regions (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Our study shows a significant decline in the incidence, mortality, and DALY rates of PCa among people aged ≥ 80 years over the last thirty years, especially in high and high-middle SDI regions. These improvements likely result from organized screening efforts, greater public awareness, and advances in treatment. However, a paradox has emerged: although age-standardized rates are decreasing, the actual numbers of incidence, deaths, and DALYs continue to rise, which might be primarily owing to global population aging. This trend is further underscored by demographic projections, which estimate that the worldwide population aged ≥ 80 years will triple from 137 million in 2017 to 425 million in 2050, and is expected to reach 909 million by 2100, representing nearly a seven-fold increase within the century [26].

PCa screening typically begins with measurement of PSA. Its primary goal is to detect high-risk, localized disease amenable to curative treatment, thereby reducing morbidity and mortality from advanced or metastatic cancer [27]. Population aging is transforming the epidemiology of PCa. Older age remains a key risk factor, and the overall risk increases as life expectancy rises. Consistent with targeted intervention strategies, the most significant declines in incidence and mortality were seen among highly screened age groups (80–84 and 85–89 years), where expected longevity suggests potential benefits. In contrast, minor improvements in the oldest-old (90–94 years and ≥ 95 years) highlight the importance of limiting screening and invasive procedures in those with limited life expectancy and significant health issues.

Life expectancy has therefore become central to clinical decision-making. Current international guidelines discourage routine screening in asymptomatic men with a life expectancy < 10–15 years because potential benefits are limited and the risks of overdiagnosis and overtreatment are higher [28, 29]. Nonetheless, emerging data suggest that radical prostatectomy can be both safe and effective in a carefully selected subgroup of men aged ≥ 80 years, if comorbidities, frailty, and patient preferences are thoroughly evaluated [4, 30–32]. Recent retrospective and prospective studies consistently demonstrate that for fit octogenarians with localized, clinically significant PCa, surgical intervention provides not only acceptable perioperative and functional outcomes but also a survival advantage over conservative management. Shahait and colleagues reported favorable perioperative and functional results for octogenarians undergoing robot-assisted radical prostatectomy, with low complication rates and robust recovery of urinary continence and sexual function [31]. However, for older adults, open and honest discussion of health status and life expectancy is essential to contextualize the potential benefits of active treatment. When cancer-specific mortality risk is low, a non-intervention approach may be appropriate, recognizing that active surveillance, de-intensified surveillance, and watchful waiting exist on a continuum of management strategies [33]. Shared decision-making with regular reassessment of goals of care and life expectancy should guide whether and when treatment intensity is adjusted. Ultimately, physiological rather than chronological age should determine the optimal management approach in the very elderly [3, 28, 29].

Marked disparities remain across SDI levels. High-SDI regions have experienced notable declines in PCa incidence and mortality following the adoption of PSA-based screening, alongside the availability of advanced treatments and multidisciplinary care. Strong health systems and widespread access to prevention and treatment have reinforced these improvements. Conversely, low- and low-middle SDI regions often face a stable or rising PCa burden due to limited resources, restricted screening, weaker health infrastructure, and rapidly aging populations that outpace healthcare expansion. Countries with the highest burdens may struggle with inadequate policies, genetic factors, or gaps in diagnosis and reporting; meanwhile, lower reported rates might result from underdiagnosis and underreporting rather than actual differences in prevalence.

Furthermore, this study highlights not only the harmful effects of smoking but also the protective role of dietary factors, such as calcium and milk intake, in PCa patients ≥ 80 years. Encouragingly, smoking prevalence has declined markedly across all SDI levels since 2000, with particularly steep reductions observed in high- and lower-middle-income regions [34]. These trends, along with improvements in diet and preventive health measures, have significantly contributed to the decline in mortality and DALYs in this population.

Using a BAPC model, we project continued global declines in PCa rates through 2040; however, the widening uncertainty around these estimates—especially in low-SDI settings and among the ultra-elderly—warrants caution. Accurate surveillance, investment in health system capacity, and innovation will be critical to meet future needs. Policymakers should prioritize equitable access to diagnostics and treatment, and the integration of multidisciplinary geriatric oncology services is fundamental to delivering optimal care.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, the GBD study is the largest and most comprehensive effort to measure health loss across different locations and time periods, providing valuable data for analyzing regional and temporal variations in PCa burden. This extensive dataset greatly improves the findings of our study. However, this research has several limitations. The accuracy of GBD estimates largely depends on the quality and completeness of the underlying data, especially in countries with poor cancer registries. Additionally, the GBD database does not distinguish between clinically significant and indolent PCa. This lack of differentiation limits our ability to determine whether the observed decline in mortality and DALYs truly reflects improvements in PCa management or is simply an artifact of increased detection of low-risk or clinically insignificant cases. To gain clearer insights, it would be helpful to analyze the incidence rates of clinically significant versus non-clinically significant disease, as ongoing efforts to reduce overdiagnosis may influence these trends. Therefore, future research that incorporates clinical and pathological information is essential for clarifying these patterns and improving the interpretability of GBD metrics related to PCa. Furthermore, addressing key gaps in surveillance and forecasting is crucial, especially in low-SDI regions with limited data, where outcomes and burdens are still poorly understood. Improving data quality, developing more sophisticated predictive models, and expanding global cancer registries will be vital for refining our understanding and better responding to the evolving challenges of PCa in an aging population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the significant global progress in lowering PCa incidence, mortality, and DALY burdens among people aged ≥ 80 years over the last thirty years, thanks to advances in medical care and public health policy. However, notable regional differences remain, emphasizing the urgent need for targeted actions in resource-limited areas. As the global population continues to age, addressing health inequalities and ensuring universal access to early detection and effective treatments will be crucial for reducing the future burden of PCa.
