Health system barriers to cardiovascular disease prevention and control in Iran: a qualitative study
Authors: Asieh Mansouri, Mostafa Amini-Rarani, Saeide Bahrani, Mahnaz Jozan, Maryam Eghbali Babadi, Ramesh Hoseinkhani, Alireza Khosravi, Hamzeh Ahmadi, Fahimeh Bagherikholenjani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeveral years have passed since the start of the provincial program for the prevention and control of cardiovascular diseases in Isfahan, but little progress has been observed in its indicators. Therefore, we decided to identify the barriers to implementing these programs and provide corrective solutions.MethodsThis exploratory descriptive qualitative study employed directed content analysis guided by the World Health Organization’s health system building blocks framework. Data were collected through 16 semi-structured face-to-face interviews with purposefully selected key informants—including managers, decision-makers, implementers, and service providers—using a maximum variation sampling approach. To ensure trustworthiness, we applied Lincoln and Guba’s criteria: credibility was enhanced through member checking and prolonged engagement; dependability and confirmability were supported by audit trails and peer debriefing; and transferability was addressed by providing thick descriptions. Data were analyzed using MAXQDA 2020 software.ResultsAnalysis of interviews with 16 participants revealed six main themes and 23 sub-themes, aligned with the health system building blocks: governance and leadership, human resources, service delivery, financing, health information systems, and access to essential supplies and medications. The findings point to critical policy-level barriers, such as ineffective inter- and extra-sectoral collaboration, insufficient prioritization of cardiovascular diseases in national agendas, and inadequate financial and human resource planning. These insights underscore the need for strategic policy reforms, including improved intersectoral governance, workforce stabilization, rational drug regulation, and enhanced community-based education initiatives to strengthen the implementation of cardiovascular prevention and control programs.ConclusionThis study highlights systemic and policy-level barriers that hinder the success of cardiovascular disease prevention and control efforts in Isfahan. Addressing these challenges requires coordinated policy actions, including stronger governance mechanisms, strategic resource allocation, and culturally appropriate community engagement. The findings provide practical insights for health policymakers to design more responsive and sustainable interventions that improve cardiovascular health outcomes at the provincial and national levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13674-w.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Health system building block framework, Qualitative research, Directed content analysis

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually—approximately 32% of all global deaths [1]. Despite significant advances in prevention and treatment, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) continue to bear a disproportionate burden, with more than 75% of CVD deaths occurring in these regions [2, 3]. The global increase in CVD is driven by complex interactions between behavioral, environmental, and systemic factors, including urbanization, dietary changes, sedentary lifestyles, and fragmented health systems [4].

Iran, as an upper-middle-income country, illustrates many of these challenges. Recent epidemiological evidence highlights a concerning upward trend in mortality from cardiovascular diseases in Iran [5]. Over the past four decades, Iran has experienced rapid socio-economic and demographic transformations—including accelerated urbanization, industrial expansion, and increased life expectancy—that have significantly reshaped public health dynamics [6]. These transitions have been accompanied by changes in nutritional habits, reduced physical activity, and heightened exposure to modifiable risk factors such as obesity, dyslipidemia, and hypertension [7]. Furthermore, systemic barriers including limited access to healthcare services, financial constraints, and suboptimal treatment adherence—often influenced by economic hardship and psychological stress—have compounded the challenge. Collectively, these factors have contributed to the increasing prevalence and mortality of CVD in Iran, underscoring the need for comprehensive, culturally tailored prevention and intervention strategies [8].

In line with the World Health Organization’s Global Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Non communicable Diseases [9], Iran has initiated national strategies aimed at reducing the burden of non-communicable diseases. As part of this broader initiative, the Health Office of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences – under the leadership of the Ministry of Health and Medical Education – has developed and implemented a provincial framework aimed at the prevention and control of non-communicable diseases. One of the main components of this framework is the Provincial Program for the Prevention and Control of Cardiovascular Diseases, which sets an ambitious goal: a 10% reduction in premature mortality (ages 30–70) due to cardiovascular diseases.

To achieve this goal, the Working Group for the Prevention and Control of Risk Factors for Cardiovascular Diseases was established in 2018. This working group consists of various experts from the health, treatment, and food and drug departments of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, along with specialists and general practitioners, and several faculty members from the university. They have defined indicators to measure the progress of this goal. However, annual assessments of these indicators showed a lack of change and, in some cases, the occurrence of undesirable changes. The underlying causes of these setbacks remain poorly understood.

To address the observed depression in cardiovascular disease (CVD) prevention efforts, a research team—comprised of several provincial Working Group members—initiated a qualitative study to systematically identify barriers to program implementation and progress. The research adopts the World Health Organization’s Building Blocks of Health Systems framework as its analytical lens. This framework, originally introduced in 2007, conceptualizes health systems through six interdependent components: service delivery, health workforce, health information systems, access to essential medicines, financing, and leadership/governance [10]. Since then, the framework has become a cornerstone for assessing and strengthening health systems globally, particularly in achieving universal health coverage and the Sustainable Development Goals [11–13]. By employing this structured approach, the study seeks to uncover actionable insights and propose context-relevant corrective actions. The ultimate goal is to inform policy reforms that can increase system responsiveness, improve program outcomes, and accelerate progress toward reducing premature mortality from cardiovascular disease in Iran.

Method

Study design

This qualitative study employed an exploratory descriptive design using directed content analysis. The World Health Organization’s health system building blocks framework guided the analysis to identify barriers to cardiovascular disease (CVD) prevention and control programs in Isfahan Province, Iran.

Participants and sampling

Sixteen participants were selected through purposive sampling with maximum diversity, including policymakers, health administrators, program administrators, and service providers. The diversity criteria included the level of service provision, the specialized field, and the work area of the interviewees. Inclusion criteria included direct experience with cardiovascular disease prevention and control programs. Sampling continued until data saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new codes or themes emerged and no repetition of themes was observed across the interviews.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured, face-to-face interviews, guided by a detailed and validated interview guide (Supplementary File). The interview guide consisted of two distinct sections: the first section included demographic information from participants, while the second section included open-ended questions designed to explore perceived barriers and potential solutions in six core components of the health system—namely, program management and leadership, human resources, service delivery, financing, health information systems, and access to essential medicines, equipment, and supplies. Additional questions addressed challenges and proposed suggestions of participant beyond these predefined categories. Eligible participants were initially contacted by telephone to confirm their willingness to participate and to coordinate interview scheduling. All interviews were conducted in Persian at mutually agreed-upon locations and times. Interviews continued until thematic saturation was reached, with each session lasting approximately 30–40 min. Audio recordings were made with the informed consent of the participants. Throughout the interview process, the researcher adhered to the study objectives, fostered rapport, avoided leading questions, and sought clarification when necessary to ensure the accuracy and depth of responses. Concurrent note-taking was employed to support interview facilitation and subsequent data analysis. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim shortly after each interview to maintain data integrity.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using MAXQDA 2020 software. A directed content analysis approach was applied according health system building block framework. The researchers read the interviews several times to better understand the participants’ statements and added their field notes. Then, they defined the main categories and determined the coding rules for these categories. The data were analyzed separately by two researchers and the main categories were inductively abstracted from the initial codes. Initially, 91 codes were generated through a combination of deductive coding (based on health system building block framework) and inductive coding (emerging from the data). These codes were grouped into 23 sub-themes and synthesized into six overarching themes aligned with the WHO framework. To ensure the credibility of the findings, measures such as prolonged engagement with the data and spending sufficient time to increase the breadth of vision, purposefully selecting participants with maximum diversity and conducting in-depth interviews with them, using the participant review method to confirm the accuracy of the extracted data and codes or to modify them, reviewing the analysis process by external supervisors (Peer reviewing), and identifying the researchers’ ideas and assumptions in advance to prevent them from affecting the data analysis (Bracketing) were taken. The dependability of the findings was ensured by using an external review method to examine the perception of possible similarities with the researcher and search for discrepancies. Transferability was ensured through detailed descriptions, allowing for contextual understanding and review of findings by experts. Confirmability, ensured by reflexivity and verification of findings by an external auditor.

Results

The study included 16 participants, of whom 62.5% were male and 37.5% were female. Most participants were aged between 41 and 50 years (43.7%), followed by those aged 51–60 years (31.3%). Half of the participants had over 21 years of work experience. Regarding education, 31.2% held a Doctor of Medicine (M.D.) degree and another 31.2% held a Subspecialty Degree. The most common specialties were M.D. (31.2%) and Public Health (25%), with smaller proportions in Endocrinology (12.5%), and other fields (each 6.2%) (Table 1).

List of the extracted themes, sub-themes, and categories

Governance and leadership

The theme of “Governance and Leadership” was the basis for all identified barriers and the corrective solutions proposed by the participants. This theme was divided into six subthemes, each containing specific barriers and their respective solutions.

Cultural promotion and lifestyle change

One of the important barriers identified was “the lack of appropriate dissemination of information and cultural awareness” among the population. This deficiency led to poor community participation in preventive health programs. Participants emphasized that public health messages were either inadequate or not culturally resonant and failed to motivate behavior change. The proposed solution focuses on “building strong intersectoral communication”. This requires the active collaboration of the health system with the National Broadcasting Organization, municipalities, and the education system to create a coherent “social wave” in society. The strategy aims to promote lifestyle changes by supporting a healthy food basket, educating people on how to interpret nutritional labels on food products, and promoting regular exercise and physical activity.

Social determinants of health

This subtheme covered important socio-economic barriers that directly influence the prevention and control of cardiovascular disease. The first was the “inability of the population to access an adequate food basket” due to macroeconomic factors such as price increases, inflation, and unemployment. This barrier was largely considered to be outside the health system, but had direct implications for its objectives. The recommended corrective action requires “long-term, multifaceted national policies managed by country authorities” to control and regulate these economic factors, thereby improving food security.The second related barrier was the “growing prevalence of cardiovascular disease associated with depression, stress, and other mental health disorders”. This highlights the two-way relationship between mental health and cardiovascular disease. This solution necessitates “mental illness and stress management” through formal collaboration between the health system (especially the mental health units of the Ministry of Health) and cultural, recreational, and municipal organizations to comprehensively address these psychosocial risk factors.

Intra-sectoral collaboration in the health system

One of the prominent operational barriers was “the lack of effective inter-sectoral collaboration” for the implementation of the plan. This isolation was observed among key units of the Ministry of Health, including the vice-departments of treatment, food, and medicine, as well as the faculties of medicine, nutrition, and pharmacy, and related research centers. This separate functioning led to inefficiency and duplication of efforts. This solution requires “health system leadership to seriously pursue systematic inter-sectoral collaboration”. This should be actively monitored, encouraged and facilitated by the university administration to create an integrated approach to cardiovascular disease prevention.

Inter-sectoral coordination with external organizations

Beyond internal sectors, a significant obstacle was “lack of coordination between the health system and other service providers”, such as insurance companies and the private sector. This lack of synergy led to duplication of efforts, increased resource costs and reduced service coverage.The corrective solution is “establishing formal coordination mechanisms between the health system and these external organizations”. The aim is to increase service coverage, avoid duplication and significantly improve the overall effectiveness of cardiovascular disease prevention initiatives.

Strategic prioritization in National programs

A strategic-level obstacle was identified as “lack of prioritization and underutilization of capacity” by health system officials to combat noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), particularly cardiovascular diseases. This lack of high-level commitment hampered resource allocation and strategic focus. The solution is to “explicitly prioritize the control of noncommunicable diseases, particularly cardiovascular diseases, in national development documents and plans”. This high-level commitment must be pursued vigorously and accompanied by specific plans for effective implementation, creating synergies among all actors, and ensuring accountability.

Inclination towards superstitions in society

A unique socio-cultural barrier was the “general tendency to people claiming to practice traditional medicine”, which often leads individuals to seek these facilities before consulting doctors. This leads to delayed treatment, disease progression, and increased burden of secondary and tertiary care, ultimately imposing higher medical costs on families. To address this issue, the proposed solutions are threefold: “increased Ministry of Health oversight and regulation of traditional medicine practices”; “promoting cultural awareness” of evidence-based medicine; and “increasing public knowledge” through targeted community education campaigns to empower individuals to make informed health choices.

Human resources

Human resources are a critical issue that directly impacts the capacity and effectiveness of implementing cardiovascular disease (CVD) prevention programs. This includes recruitment, motivation, and competency of the healthcare workforce. Three key sub-themes were identified, with specific barriers and solutions as follows:

Job security and performance appraisal

A significant structural and motivational barrier was “job insecurity” for a large proportion of healthcare workers involved in the program. This was inherently linked to a performance monitoring system that was largely based on employee corporate employment status and quantitative measures of activities recorded in the SIB (System Integrated Health) database. This approach was seen as prioritizing quantity (number) of services over quality of care, creating instability and de-motivating comprehensive and patient-centered preventive activities. The proposed solution was to develop a hybrid performance appraisal system that combines quantitative data with qualitative results and provides more sustainable results.

Physician awareness and engagement

“Lack of timely and accurate communication with physicians” about the details and importance of the cardiovascular disease control program was a significant barrier. This lack of awareness among a key group of implementers resulted in non-participation or low participation rates.The proposed solution was to implement a strategy of” appropriate and targeted communication with all those involved in the process”. This communication should be designed not only to inform, but also to encourage active participation. In addition, increasing motivation through the conscious use of “financial incentives (e.g., performance-based payments) and non-financial motivational mechanisms” (e.g., recognition, professional development opportunities) is considered essential.

Ongoing education and training

A key barrier to delivering high-quality care was the “lack of ongoing updating of educational programs” for general practitioners, specialists and frontline healthcare workers. This educational gap was particularly notable regarding the latest evidence and protocols related to “primary prevention of cardiovascular disease”, leaving the workforce unprepared to effectively screen and manage patient risk. To address this competency gap, the corrective solution focuses on “empowering” those involved through ongoing professional development. This requires “increasing their skills in relation to community screening” for risk factors and providing “up-to-date, evidence-based training on the care of patients with or at risk of cardiovascular disease”, thereby ensuring that clinical practices are in line with current guidelines.

Service delivery

Service delivery as a core theme, includes the organization, management, and quality of care provided to the population. This theme represents the tangible point of contact between the system and its stakeholders. Five key subthemes were identified as follows.

Quality of service delivery by the family physician team

A key barrier was the “systematic emphasis on the quantity of services provided at the expense of quality”. This was seen in the performance measures of family physician teams that prioritized the volume of activities recorded in the SIB system over the effectiveness or comprehensiveness of care. The proposed corrective solution is “establishing robust mechanisms to assess the quality of recorded activities”. This includes incentives such as considering “quality factors for essential screening services and effective patient education”, thereby rewarding high-quality preventive care. A related and critical barrier was “ignoring the longitudinal treatment process”. This was manifested in the lack of effective interaction between primary care physicians (focused on primary prevention) and specialists (focused on secondary prevention) and the lack of structured patient education, which led to fragmented care. The solution is to “create a cohesive and purposeful family physician team” with a holistic role. This includes identifying key health indicators (e.g., cholesterol, blood pressure), monitoring treatment progress, and educating patients at all levels to change attitudes and improve a culture of healthy consumption, thereby ensuring continuity of care.

Competitive nature of healthcare services

**The low competitiveness of public healthcare services** compared to the private sector and insurance organizations was identified as a significant barrier. Patients’ expectations were not met due to unfavorable comparisons with the private sector, which was perceived to provide more specialized and timely services, and with insurance organizations, which provided more services for free, leading to dissatisfaction and disengagement from the public system. To address this issue, the solution requires “strategic coordination between the Health Ministry and other organizations”. The aim is to avoid parallel activities, integrate health information systems, and expand service coverage in a collaborative manner to avoid duplication and waste of resources, thereby increasing the overall attractiveness and efficiency of public health services.

Top-down directive delivery of programs

“The top-down directive delivery of programs” was a major obstacle to local ownership and feasibility. Programs were delivered without considering the unique structure, conditions, and capabilities of each county, making implementation difficult and often ineffective. The proposed solution is “to use a participatory approach with the participation of program stakeholders” at the planning stage before the programs are announced. In addition, “delegation of authority to provinces” to localize programs based on the specific conditions and capabilities of each facility is essential to ensure relevance and successful implementation.

Referral system and service level classification

“The lack of full implementation and trust in the family doctor-based referral system”, especially in urban areas, was a key structural obstacle. Urban populations showed greater trust in specialist doctors at the secondary level, which led to a weak referral system, disregard for the principle of service classification and a tendency to “see the doctor” which resulted in multiple consultations, conflicting opinions and inefficient use of resources. The corrective action focuses on “building trust in family doctors and strengthening the referral system” in urban communities. This requires “targeted cultural promotion and public education” on the benefits of a structured and classified healthcare system to properly guide patient flow.

Financing

Financing is a pivotal theme influencing the delivery and sustainability of cardiovascular healthcare services. Two key sub-themes emerged:

Compliance with treatment guidelines

Participants reported widespread non-compliance with established treatment protocols among healthcare providers, which was perceived to undermine clinical efficiency and inflate costs. Insurance organizations were noted to impose financial penalties—such as reimbursement deductions—on providers failing to adhere to Ministry of Health guidelines. These punitive measures, while intended to enforce standardization, were seen to contribute to resource wastage and service delivery challenges. In response, the health system has prioritized regulatory alignment by mandating strict adherence to nationally endorsed treatment guidelines. This approach aims to reduce unnecessary deductibles, optimize resource utilization, and promote financial sustainability. The emphasis on compliance reflects a broader institutional strategy to harmonize clinical practice with insurance frameworks, thereby enhancing accountability and cost-effectiveness.

Insurance policies and preventive engagement

The second sub-theme centered on the structural limitations of insurance policies, particularly their inability to accommodate the financial demands posed by high-risk individuals and patients with cardiovascular conditions. Participants highlighted that low participation in screening and preventive programs exacerbates treatment costs and threatens the long-term viability of insurance funds. To address this, stakeholders proposed a risk-adjusted premium model, wherein insurance contributions would be calibrated based on individual health risk profiles. This model is designed to incentivize preventive behaviors, foster public engagement in cardiovascular health maintenance, and ultimately reduce the economic burden on the healthcare system. By linking financial responsibility to lifestyle and risk factors, the strategy seeks to align individual incentives with systemic sustainability.

Health information systems

Health Information Systems as a critical theme influencing the efficiency and continuity of cardiovascular care. Two sub-themes emerged:

Infrastructure and program resources

Participants reported significant challenges associated with the Sib program, a national health information platform. These included excessive and often complex data entry requirements, outdated software architecture, and time-consuming form completion processes. Additionally, technical limitations such as slow internet connectivity, frequent disconnections, and insufficient telecommunication infrastructure—particularly a shortage of phone lines for patient follow-up—were cited as barriers to effective care coordination.To address these issues, stakeholders emphasized the need for regular software updates, simplification of data entry protocols, and investment in essential infrastructure. These measures are intended to reduce administrative burden, enhance usability, and support timely patient engagement, particularly for individuals with cardiovascular conditions requiring longitudinal follow-up.

Implementation of integrated information systems

A second sub-theme focused on the lack of interoperability and integration across electronic health platforms. The absence of timely access to patients’ treatment and medication histories—largely due to the non-implementation of electronic referral systems—was identified as a major contributor to delayed diagnosis and suboptimal treatment decisions. Corrective strategies proposed by participants included the full implementation of electronic health records (EHRs), the family physician model, and a tiered electronic referral system. These initiatives are envisioned to operate in parallel with inter-sectoral collaboration involving health authorities, civil registration offices, and information technology agencies. The goal is to enable physicians to access comprehensive patient histories, facilitate early identification of at-risk individuals, and improve continuity of care across service levels.

Essential medicines and supplies

Qualitative findings revealed that systemic barriers in the provision and utilization of essential medicines significantly undermine the management of cardiovascular diseases. Three sub-themes emerged:

Fragmented medication history and prescription oversight

Participants reported a lack of continuity in medication management across different levels of care. Physicians often lacked access to comprehensive medication histories, resulting in inadequate dosage adjustments and poor monitoring of disease progression. This fragmentation contributed to suboptimal treatment outcomes and increased the burden of cardiovascular disease within the community. To address these gaps, stakeholders emphasized the critical role of implementing an electronic referral system. Such a system would facilitate timely access to patient records, enhance coordination among providers, and support more precise and responsive medication management strategies.

Insufficient patient education on medication adherence

Participants described the influence of anti-treatment messaging, misinformation about drug side effects, and the absence of qualified guidance, all of which contributed to arbitrary discontinuation of therapy and incomplete treatment courses. Corrective strategies included the deployment of a national drug information system to serve as a reliable reference for community members. This initiative aims to improve public understanding of medication benefits, counteract misinformation, and promote sustained adherence to prescribed cardiovascular therapies.

Conflicts of interest in the pharmaceutical sector

The third sub-theme addressed economic and ethical concerns related to pharmaceutical marketing practices. Participants noted that promotional pressures to prescribe specific brands often led to increased treatment costs, discouraging patient adherence and, in some cases, resulting in treatment abandonment due to financial constraints. To mitigate these issues, respondents called for enhanced regulatory oversight by health authorities, particularly the Food and Drug Organization. Strengthening governance mechanisms around pharmaceutical conflicts of interest is expected to foster transparency, reduce undue influence, and improve patient trust and engagement in their treatment plans.

Discussion

This qualitative study used the World Health Organization’s health system components framework to systematically examine the barriers that hinder the progress of a provincial cardiovascular disease prevention and control program in Isfahan, Iran. Our findings showed that these barriers are not isolated, but rather deeply interconnected, encompassing governance, human resources, financing, service delivery, Health Information Systems, and Essential Medicines and Supplies. The results of our analysis indicated that the main mechanism of program stagnation is a fragmented health system approach, in which weaknesses in one component exacerbate deficiencies in other components, creating a cycle of inefficiency. This systemic fragmentation is not unique to Iran, but is a common challenge in many health systems that manage and control non-communicable diseases [14]. The World Health Organization has consistently emphasized that strengthening health systems requires synergistic action across all components, as progress in one area is often limited by weakness in another [15, 16]. Our study demonstrated this dynamic in the context of cardiovascular disease prevention.

A key finding was the inadequacy of “governance and leadership”, particularly the lack of effective inter-sectoral collaboration. While Iran has established a high-level committee for non-communicable diseases (INCDC) [17], our results highlight a gap between national policy and local implementation. This is consistent with global analyses that show that “whole of government” and “whole of society” approaches are often mentioned in rhetoric but are poorly implemented in practice, especially in resource-limited settings [18, 19]. The community’s willingness to pursue traditional medicine without supervision, as reported by participants, is not simply a cultural issue, but rather a symptom of a deeper failure in health system management and public health communication. Effective governance must proactively create formal and trusted structures that integrate evidence-based traditional knowledge where appropriate, while protecting people from harmful practices [20].

In addition, “human resource” challenges, including job insecurity and lack of ongoing training, directly undermine the capacity to deliver quality services. The contractual employment model for frontline health workers, which focuses on quantitative targets in the SIB system, creates a perverse incentive that prioritizes workload over value. This finding is consistent with recent studies in other middle-income countries, where motivated health workers are recognized as the mainstay of effective NCD care, but are often demoralized due to unstable working conditions and inadequate support [21, 22]. Without addressing these fundamental human resource barriers, training programs on clinical guidelines will have limited impact.

Inefficient referral systems and low public trust in primary care, especially in urban areas, indicate significant shortcomings in the “service delivery” model. Iran’s experience with the family physician program highlights a global challenge: implementing a robust primary care-based system is as much a sociocultural as it is a technical undertaking [23]. The inability of this system to compete with the perceived quality and speed of private care creates a two-tier system that exacerbates inequalities. Recent comparative studies suggest that successful primary care systems for non-communicable diseases are those that are adequately funded, integrated with community resources, and designed with a patient-centered approach to gain public trust [24, 25].

Regarding “financing”, participants cited insurance companies that penalize providers and cause financial waste as a major barrier. This reflects a mismatch between payment structures and health goals. Participants called for a shift from volume-based to value-based payment models as a solution to this problem, which is a central theme of global health financing reforms [26]. Our study suggests that for cardiovascular disease management programs to be successful, insurers need to shift from passive payers to active purchasers of health and create incentives for prevention, adherence to guidelines, and care coordination [27]. This is important beyond Iran, as many countries are struggling to design sustainable financing models for chronic care.

Finally, barriers related to ‘health information systems” (e.g., SIB software) and “access to essential medicines” (e.g., medication quality, irrational prescribing) are not simply technical issues. Rather, they reflect deficiencies in quality regulation and assurance at the system level. The 2021 World Health Organization report on non-communicable diseases emphasizes that improving the quality of care through better information systems and access to affordable and effective medicines is a cornerstone of global efforts [28]. Our findings call for stronger regulatory bodies, such as the Food and Drug Administration, to enforce standards and eliminate conflicts of interest that compromise patient care.

Based on the results of our study, several policy recommendations are made for policymakers and managers that are also applicable to similar middle-income health systems. The first recommendation is to strengthen inter-sectoral governance. Beyond the establishment of committees, joint and practical planning and budgeting with key sectors should be on the agenda. In this regard, the establishment of transparent accountability frameworks with measurable targets for cardiovascular disease prevention, as recommended in the World Health Organization’s “Best Buys” [19], is essential. To reform health financing, it is recommended to pilot and scale up value-based payment models for cardiovascular care that reward outcomes (e.g., controlled blood pressure, adherence to treatment) rather than volume of services. Also, targeted taxes (e.g. taxes on tobacco and sugar-sweetened beverages) should be considered to create a sustainable and limited fund for the prevention of non-communicable diseases [29].

To invest in the primary care workforce, it is recommended to shift from short-term contracts to career paths for community health workers and family physicians, and to integrate continuous and applied training in behavioral counseling and cardiovascular risk management using digital learning platforms into professional development programs [22].

To create integrated information systems, it is necessary to prioritize the interoperability of SIB systems with electronic health records and referral platforms. Data from information systems should be used not only for reporting but also for feedback loops that inform healthcare providers and policymakers in real time, a strategy that has been used successfully in other contexts [30, 31]. Finally, it is suggested that a coherent, evidence-based public health campaign be deployed across multiple media platforms to counter misinformation and build public trust. This campaign should emphasize the role of primary care, the importance of medication adherence, and a healthy lifestyle, while directly addressing misconceptions about traditional medicine.

The most important limitation of this study was that participants were only health system staff, which may have limited the diversity of perspectives. While these knowledgeable individuals provided important insights into systemic and operational barriers, the lack of input from patients, community representatives, and policymakers limits the depth of sociocultural and behavioral understanding. This limited scope may impact the transferability of findings to broader contexts, particularly where community engagement and multi-sectoral collaboration are essential for successful implementation. To address this limitation, future studies should adopt a more inclusive design that includes a broader range of stakeholders to capture diverse experiences and expectations.

Conclusion

This study highlights critical systemic barriers to effective cardiovascular disease prevention in Isfahan, Iran, including governance, human resources, service delivery, financing, health information systems, and access to essential medicines. These barriers are deeply interrelated and create a cycle of inefficiency that undermines program outcomes. Key barriers include fragmented inter- and intra-sectoral collaboration, inadequate prioritization of cardiovascular disease in national health programs, unstable workforce structures, and limited public trust in primary care services. In addition, technical deficiencies in health information systems and regulatory gaps in drug monitoring further compromise continuity and quality of care. Addressing these multifaceted challenges requires coordinated policy reforms that emphasize integrated governance, strategic resource allocation, and culturally sensitive community engagement. Strengthening inter-sectoral partnerships, investing in workforce development, implementing value-based financing models, and increasing digital infrastructure are important steps toward creating a more resilient and responsive health system. Addressing these issues can accelerate progress toward improving cardiovascular outcomes.
