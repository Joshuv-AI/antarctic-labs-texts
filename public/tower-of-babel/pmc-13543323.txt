Trophies for All? Children's and Adolescents’ Reasoning About Inclusive Versus Selective Awards
Authors: Shuai Shao, Gail D. Heyman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHow do children and adolescents reason about the consequences of institutional reward systems? We examined developmental differences in reasoning about inclusive versus selective awards in a youth sports context. Participants in two age groups (43 in the 7‐ to 9‐year‐old age group and 44 in the 15‐ to 18‐year‐old age group; 46% female, 49% male, 5% other or preferred not to say; majority Asian/White; USA, 2024–2025) read scenarios in which trophies were awarded either to all participants or only to top performers in a running race and predicted motivational, emotional, and behavioral outcomes. Across age groups, participants expected selective awards to increase effort and pride, suggesting that by 7 years of age children recognize links between incentive structures and task engagement. By adolescence, systematic reasoning expanded to include the possibility that selective incentives could motivate behavior that conflicts with social norms or personal safety, including cheating and risk‐taking. In addition, adolescents’, but not children's, preferences for reward systems were systematically related to their own perceived running ability, suggesting that evaluations of incentive systems increasingly align with self‐interest across development. Together, these findings reveal both continuity and change in how young people reason about incentive systems: children as young as 7 linked reward systems to effort and pride, whereas adolescents additionally made inferences about self‐interest and norm‐conflicting behavior.

Introduction

Youth sports are a cultural context in which there is widespread disagreement about how effort and achievement should be recognized. These discussions reflect enduring social tensions between egalitarian and merit‐based principles of fairness, as well as concerns about the downstream effects that different reward structures may have on motivation, emotion, and behavior (Bellows 2009; Brown and Tannock 2009; Starmans et al. 2017). One ongoing debate centers around the allocation of trophies: Should every participant receive a trophy for showing up, or should trophies be reserved for top performers? To some, participation trophies are seen as a valuable way to acknowledge effort and encourage inclusion; to others, they dilute the meaning of achievement and risk undermining motivation by failing to distinguish between effort and success. Performance‐based trophies evoke parallel disagreements: supporters see them as an appropriate way to recognize genuine achievement, whereas critics argue that they can heighten pressure and encourage unethical shortcuts or unnecessary risks. The present study investigates how children and adolescents reason about these competing principles when applied to trophy distribution, and how their inferences about the psychological and behavioral consequences of reward systems change across development.

Debates about trophy distribution in youth sports connect to broader societal discussions about whether distribution systems should prioritize merit or egalitarianism (Bellows 2009; Brown and Tannock 2009; Starmans et al. 2017). Much of the academic discussion has emphasized concerns about merit‐based systems, including their potential to legitimize and perpetuate structural inequalities across demographic groups (Darnon et al. 2018; Hadden et al. 2025; Reay 2020). However, some scholars have highlighted potential benefits of emphasizing merit, such as promoting excellence, curbing nepotism, and encouraging constructive competition (Dahlström et al. 2012; Kim and Choi 2017). These perspectives show that reward systems involve moral as well as practical considerations about who should be recognized and why.

The capacity to consider merit emerges early in childhood. Classic developmental research has shown that children move from simple equality‐based reasoning to more differentiated judgments that take merit and need into account (Damon 1977; Schmidt et al. 2016). Further research suggests that even preschool‐aged children show sensitivity to merit in terms of performance in some contexts. For example, Baumard et al. (2012) presented 3‐ and 4‐year‐olds with a scenario in which two girls baked cookies together and one contributed more than the other. When asked to distribute a large and a small cookie, children tended to give the larger cookie to the harder‐working girl. In another study, when children were asked to distribute three equal cookies, the most common initial response was to give one cookie to each girl; when prompted to distribute the remaining cookie, most children gave it to the greater contributor. These findings suggest that although young children often default to equality, they are also sensitive to merit. More recent work has expanded this picture, showing that children's sense of distributive justice becomes increasingly attuned to forms of legitimate inequality based on factors such as need, rules, and performance (Rizzo et al. 2016; Rizzo et al. 2020; Schmidt et al. 2016). Research has also shown that children as young as 4 years of age recognize that performance outcomes can reflect both effort and ability (Muradoglu and Cimpian 2020), suggesting that even young children understand that merit can reflect multiple factors.

Children's fairness reasoning is also shaped by social context (Elenbaas 2019). In competitive contexts, children become more attentive to personal advantage and group distinctions (Rhodes and Brickman 2011; Shaw et al. 2012). For instance, Shaw et al. (2012) found that although 6‐ to 8‐year‐olds typically preferred equal distributors, they favored a distributor who gave them more when the situation was framed competitively. These findings suggest that children's judgments about fairness and reward systems can shift depending on the incentives involved.

Although much of the existing work focuses on resource‐allocation decisions in concrete contexts, less is known about how people reason about abstract distribution systems and their broader psychological effects. In one study that has begun to address this issue, children, adolescents, and adults learned about two compensation systems: one merit‐based, in which people were paid according to work completed, and one egalitarian, in which everyone was paid equally (Shao and Heyman 2026). Across age groups, participants expected individuals in the merit‐based system to work harder, highlighting early expectations about effort but leaving open how reasoning about other psychological and behavioral consequences may expand across development.

The Present Study

The present research investigated children's and adolescents’ beliefs about egalitarian versus merit‐based reward systems in a developmentally important context: trophy distribution in youth sports. This domain was chosen because sports are a familiar and meaningful part of many young people's lives, and because there is an active cultural debate over how effort and achievement should be recognized. To examine beliefs about trophy distribution, participants were presented with two running race scenarios. In one version, all runners received a trophy as long as they showed up, whereas in the other, only the fastest runners received trophies. Participants were then asked to make a series of predictions about behaviors and emotional outcomes. Specifically, we focused on inferences about personal effort to replicate the finding of Shao and Heyman (2026) and to extend it to a new context. In terms of emotion, we focused on pride because it is an achievement‐related emotion (Sznycer and Cohen 2021) that children begin to experience as early as toddlerhood (Stipek 1995).

We also included two domains that require reasoning about how incentives can push behavior into tension with social norms or personal safety: morality and risk‐taking. In the moral domain, we examined whether a more selective award would incentivize the use of dishonest means to gain an advantage, given that adolescents become more likely to justify dishonest behavior in pressured and competitive environments (Shao et al. 2024; Waltzer et al. 2023). Risk‐taking was defined as a willingness to risk personal injury in pursuit of winning, with evidence showing that adolescents are sensitive to the potential benefits of risk‐taking (Duell and Steinberg 2019).

We focused on children aged 7 to 9 and adolescents aged 15 to 18 to capture two distinct developmental periods. By age 7, children typically recognize simple downstream effects of reward systems (Shao and Heyman 2026). In contrast, adolescents have greater real‐world exposure to competitive reward contexts, which may foster more sophisticated reasoning about the social and emotional implications of rewards (LaFontana and Cillessen 2010; Shao et al. 2024; Waltzer et al. 2023). Although these predictions were not formally specified in the preregistration, we expected that adolescents would show systematic effects across all outcome measures. Specifically, we expected that when only the top performers received trophies, adolescents would infer greater effort, pride, cheating, and risk‐taking. We had no strong expectations about whether younger children would extend their reasoning beyond effort and pride to include moral and risk‐related consequences. We included this younger group to explore the developmental origins of any emerging age‐related patterns. Before this preregistered study, we conducted a pilot study with adults recruited in the same geographical area using a similar paradigm (see Appendix 1).

In addition to our primary research questions, we conducted an exploratory analysis examining whether participants who viewed themselves as strong runners would be more likely to prefer the merit‐based system. This is based on prior findings with adults suggesting that individuals who benefit from a system are more likely to perceive it as fair (DeScioli et al. 2014; Piff et al. 2012), and that people often view unequal outcomes as acceptable when they are perceived to reflect merit (Starmans et al. 2017). Examining this association in children and adolescents allows us to ask when, developmentally, evaluations of fairness begin to incorporate self‐relevance, a process that may contribute to the persistent disagreements about fairness and merit observed in adulthood (Starmans et al. 2017).

By focusing on how children's and adolescents’ inferences about the consequences of structural reward systems expand across development, rather than on individual acts of fairness alone, the present research builds on developmental investigations of structural reasoning about the social world (Amemiya et al. 2023) in the youth sports domain. More broadly, it contributes to understanding how children and adolescents think about the psychological implications of the systems that shape achievement and recognition.

Method

Participants

The sample consists of a group of 7‐ to 9‐year‐old children (n = 43; M age = 8.44, SD age = 0.91, age range = 7.03–9.90; 49% female, 51% male; 35% White, 32% Asian, 16% Latinx, 7% mixed or other race/ethnicity, 5% Black, and 5% Middle Eastern) and a group of 15‐ to 18‐year‐old adolescents (n = 44; M age = 16.87, SD age = 0.93, age range = 15.01–18.99; 43% female, 48% male, 9% other or preferred not to say; 36% Asian, 27% mixed or other race/ethnicity, 21% White, 9% Latinx, 5% Middle Eastern, and 2% Black) in the southwestern United States. The sample size was determined based on an a priori power analysis, indicating that at least 32 participants per age group were required to achieve 80% power to detect a medium‐sized effect (Cohen's h = 0.50) in a two‐tailed binomial test against chance (p = 0.50) at the α = 0.05 level. There were two check questions across the study. Following the preregistration, five additional participants who failed at least one assessment were excluded from further analysis: 1 child and 1 adolescent failed the initial check question, 2 children failed the final check question, and 1 child failed both questions.

Procedure

Child participants were tested in local parks by trained experimenters, while adolescent participants completed the study online via a survey link, using identical materials and question wording. All participants received a vignette describing two school running events that differed in how trophies were awarded: one event gave a trophy to every participant who showed up (“Trophies for All”), and the other gave trophies only to the top finishers (“Trophies for Winners”). The full vignette read as follows: A school is holding two running events. In each event, there are ten runners. Each runner will run around the soccer field one time.This is the “Trophies for All” event. In this event, everyone will receive a trophy if they show up.This is the “Trophies for Winners” event. In this event, only the top three runners will receive a trophy.

A school is holding two running events. In each event, there are ten runners. Each runner will run around the soccer field one time.

This is the “Trophies for All” event. In this event, everyone will receive a trophy if they show up.

This is the “Trophies for Winners” event. In this event, only the top three runners will receive a trophy.

We randomized which of the two events was presented first. After each vignette, participants completed an initial check question by answering the following binary forced‐choice question: “In which event will only the top three runners get a trophy?” The correct answer was the “Trophies for Winners” event. Next, participants responded to a series of core inference questions about the two events, presented in a fixed order. We also included several exploratory questions. At the end of the survey, participants completed a final check question with one correct answer and two distractors. Both check questions served to test whether participants paid attention to the details of the story, and participants who failed either question were excluded from formal analysis.

Measures

Of central interest were participants’ inferences about four dimensions: (1) personal effort, (2) emotion, (3) morality, and (4) risk‐taking. We included eight binary forced‐choice questions, with two items per dimension (one positive statement and one negative statement) to ensure that the results were not specific to the framing of the questions. For each question, participants had to choose between the “Trophies for All” event and the “Trophies for Winners” event (see Table 1 for the questions).

In addition to the core inference questions, we included several exploratory questions. We were particularly interested in the relation between participants’ preferences and self‐evaluated running competence in light of evidence that adults tend to prefer self‐benefiting compensation systems (DeScioli et al. 2014; Piff et al. 2012). Specifically, participants were asked, “Which event would you like to join?” and had to choose between the “Trophies for All” event and the “Trophies for Winners” event (the preference question). They were also asked, “Do you think you run slower, about the same, or faster than an average person around your age?” Responses to this self‐evaluated running competence question were recorded on a 5‐point scale, ranging from 1 (much slower) to 5 (much faster). Results

Core Inference Questions

Throughout the Results, the term “systematically inferred” is used to refer to response patterns that differed reliably from chance at the group level. In addition, we computed correlations between the participants’ responses to the two questions within each domain, which showed high consistency (see Appendix 3 for inter‐item correlation analysis).

As preregistered, binomial tests were used to examine whether participants’ choices differed significantly from chance within each age group. Fisher's exact tests were used to compare the two age groups because some cell frequencies were small or zero. We further conducted Bayesian analyses and computed Bayes factors (BF 10) using the proportionBF function from the BayesFactor package (Morey and Rouder 2024) in R, with the package's default prior (a logistic distribution on the log odds with a medium scale, r = 0.5), for each binomial test to examine the robustness of the frequentist results. Following convention, BF 10 values between 1 and 3 are considered anecdotal evidence, between 3 and 10 moderate evidence, between 10 and 30 strong evidence, and above 30 very strong evidence in favor of choices differing from chance. Conversely, BF 10 values between 1/3 and 1 indicate anecdotal evidence, and values between 1/10 and 1/3 indicate moderate evidence in favor of choices being at chance (Lee and Wagenmakers 2014). These Bayesian analyses were post hoc and not preregistered.

Personal Effort. Both age groups inferred that the runners in the “Trophies for Winners” event would run faster (72.1% children, p = 0.005, BF 10 = 15.37; 100.0% adolescents, p < 0.001, BF 10> 100; age differences: p < 0.001) than the runners in the “Trophies for All” event, and that the runners in the “Trophies for All” event would try less hard (88.4% children, p < 0.001, BF 10> 100; 100.0% adolescents, excluding 1 missing value, p < 0.001, BF 10> 100; age differences: p = 0.055) than the runners in the “Trophies for Winners” event.

Emotion. Both age groups inferred that the runners in the “Trophies for Winners” event would feel more special after getting a trophy (81.4% children, p < 0.001, BF 10> 100; 95.5% adolescents, p < 0.001, BF 10> 100; age differences: p = 0.049) than the runners in the “Trophies for All” event, and that the runners in the “Trophies for All” event would feel less proud after getting a trophy (81.4% children, p < 0.001, BF 10> 100; 95.5% adolescents, p < 0.001, BF 10> 100; age differences: p = 0.049) than the runners in the “Trophies for Winners” event.

Morality. Adolescents (95.5%, p < 0.001, BF 10> 100), but not children (65.1%, p = 0.066, BF 10 = 2.00), systematically inferred that the runners in the “Trophies for Winners” event would be more likely to cheat to win (age differences: p < 0.001) than the runners in the “Trophies for All” event. Likewise, adolescents (68.2%, p = 0.023, BF 10 = 4.67), but not children (62.8%, p = 0.126, BF 10 = 1.21), systematically inferred that the runners in the “Trophies for All” event would be more likely to follow the rules (age differences: p = 0.656) than the runners in the “Trophies for Winners” event.

Risk‐Taking. Adolescents (100.0%, p < 0.001, BF 10> 100), but not children (58.1%, p = 0.360, BF 10 = 0.58), systematically inferred that the runners in the “Trophies for Winners” event would be more likely to risk getting hurt to try to win (age differences: p < 0.001) than the runners in the “Trophies for All” event. Similarly, adolescents (68.2%, p = 0.023, BF 10 = 4.67), but not children (51.2%, p = 1.000, BF 10 = 0.36), inferred that the runners in the “Trophies for All” event would run more carefully to avoid falling down (age differences: p = 0.129) than the runners in the “Trophies for Winners” event (see Figure 1 for results of all four dimensions).

Preference and Self‐Evaluated Competence

In terms of participants’ preference, 62.8% of children and 63.6% of adolescents indicated that they would prefer to join the “Trophies for Winners” event, showing no systematic preference for either system (both ps > 0.096). In addition, there was no significant age group difference in self‐evaluated running competence (children: M = 3.63, SD = 1.00; adolescents: M = 3.30, SD = 1.17), t(83.49) = 1.42, p = 0.158, d = 0.30. A binary logistic regression was conducted to examine the relation between preference and self‐evaluated running competence. This analysis was exploratory and not preregistered, and it was reported to examine potential developmental differences in how self‐relevance relates to reward preferences. The full model included self‐evaluated competence, age group, and their interaction as predictors, with preference as the binary outcome variable. The interaction between self‐evaluated competence and age group was statistically significant (p = 0.048). Follow‐up analyses were conducted separately within each age group. Among children, self‐evaluated competence did not significantly predict their preference, b = 0.00, SE = 0.32, p = 0.988. In contrast, among adolescents, higher self‐evaluated competence was associated with a greater likelihood of preferring the “Trophies for Winners” event, b = 0.90, SE = 0.34, p = 0.008 (see Figure 2).

Discussion

The present study examined how children and adolescents reason about the consequences of different reward systems in the context of youth sports. Across age groups, participants expected individuals to try harder and feel more pride when only the top performers received trophies, suggesting that even children view selective rewards as more motivating and emotionally meaningful than inclusive ones. Adolescents also predicted that selective rewards would increase the likelihood of both cheating and risk‐taking. Together, these findings indicate that children reliably link reward structures to effort and emotion, whereas adolescents additionally reason about how incentives can shape norm‐violating and risky behavior.

Our finding that both children and adolescents view merit‐based, selective reward systems as incentivizing greater effort than egalitarian systems extends findings by Shao and Heyman (2026) to a new context. Specifically, Shao and Heyman (2026) found that by age 7, children held these expectations in a monetary compensation system context, and the present findings show that these results also apply to a youth sports context with non‐monetary rewards. These results suggest that the link between incentive systems and effort may constitute an early emerging lay theory that shapes a wide range of inferences about the social world (Molden and Dweck 2006), even before children consider broader normative or safety‐related consequences of incentives. We also found that both age groups associated the merit‐based, selective reward with more pride, and perceived that runners would feel less special upon receiving an inclusive, participation‐based reward. This finding is consistent with empirical work showing that by age 7, children recognize pride as contingent on an individual's ability and effort (Seidner et al. 1988).

It is worth noting that adolescents, but not children, systematically inferred that selective rewards would lead to more cheating. Although children have some understanding of incentives for personal advantage by age 7 (Mills and Keil 2005), they may be less able to recognize the pressures and incentives that arise in high‐stakes, competitive environments. Such reasoning may depend on richer personal experiences, broader social observations, and a better understanding of moral norms that become more available during adolescence (Waltzer et al. 2023). In addition, younger children are typically taught to behave morally (Heyman et al. 2009) and to avoid risks (Ahern et al. 2016). Reasoning about how incentives might promote immoral or risky behavior may require considering outcomes that conflict with everyday norms or instructions, a form of counterfactual thinking that develops gradually during childhood (Rafetseder et al. 2013). Future research could examine counterfactual thinking as a potential cognitive mechanism underlying inferences about these less obvious consequences of selective and inclusive awards.

Another notable finding was that adolescents who viewed themselves as strong runners were more likely to prefer a merit‐based trophy system. This result is consistent with prior work suggesting that adults tend to endorse distribution systems that benefit them personally (DeScioli et al. 2014; Piff et al. 2012). More broadly, the findings suggest that by adolescence, preferences about fairness may become increasingly aligned with one's perceived position within a competitive structure. Because the present study did not include direct measures of the processes underlying these preferences, the mechanisms responsible for these developmental differences remain uncertain. One possibility is that adolescents become more attentive to how reward systems align with their own anticipated outcomes. Another is that adolescents become increasingly sensitive to social comparison and competitive standing. This pattern was not observed in children, perhaps because younger children have more limited experience navigating competitive reward systems.

A related factor that may contribute to these age differences is that children often hold broadly positive beliefs about both their own abilities (Bjorklund 1997) and the abilities of others (Boseovski 2010). These rose‐colored views may lead them to approach the decision in a more aspirational way, selecting the option they simply find appealing rather than evaluating which system aligns with their actual competitive standing. For some children, this may mean gravitating toward the merit‐based system because they imagine themselves succeeding; for others, it may mean favoring the idea that everyone can win. Adolescents, in contrast, tend to have more calibrated self‐assessments and greater experience with competitive environments, which may help explain why their preferences were more systematically tied to how competent they felt. Although prior research suggests that younger children often express relatively positive views of their own abilities (Bjorklund 1997), the two age groups in the present study did not differ significantly in self‐evaluated running competence.

It is important to note that because the design was cross‐sectional, we cannot rule out the possibility that cohort effects or other contextual factors contributed to the observed age‐related patterns. Future research using narrower age ranges and longitudinal approaches will be needed to clarify the developmental mechanisms underlying these differences.

One limitation of the current study is that we only recruited participants from the United States. Although the sample included a relatively diverse range of ethnic backgrounds, future research should systematically examine how perceptions of selective and inclusive rewards vary across cultures (see Kim et al. 1990). It would also be valuable to explore the influence of family environments, such as the effect of parenting styles and parental traits (Soenens et al. 2015).

Another limitation concerns the phrasing of two items in the morality and risk‐taking domains. Specifically, questions asking whether runners would cheat or risk getting hurt “to win” may have implicitly directed participants toward choosing the “Trophies for Winners” event, because only that event was explicitly framed around winning. Future research should use more neutral phrasing, such as “to get a trophy,” to avoid this potential confound. In addition, although measures within each dimension showed high consistency (see Appendix 3), because the question order was fixed, the repeated‐measures design may make the second question within the same dimension more obvious and subject to demand characteristics.

Although we chose to focus on the youth sports context, an important and meaningful setting in young people's lives, the running race scenario also represents a comparatively clear‐cut case in which merit is relatively easy to assess because performance outcomes and rankings are directly observable. In many real‐world contexts, however, evaluations of merit involve more subjective judgments and are less transparently tied to performance outcomes (Mills and Landrum 2012). Future research should examine whether these findings generalize to contexts in which merit is more difficult to evaluate, such as academic settings (e.g., teachers giving out stickers to recognize students’ participation or merit), or contexts in which performance is evaluated over longer periods of time.

The present study focused on how children and adolescents reason about different reward systems rather than on their own behavioral responses to receiving trophies. This distinction is important because reward systems may influence behavior partly through the meanings and expectations that young people attach to them. However, it is important to note that in some contexts beliefs about fairness and morality are closely linked to behavior, whereas in others the correspondence is weaker (Arsenio and Lemerise 2004; Blake et al. 2014). Understanding how children and adolescents interpret the motivational, emotional, and moral implications of different forms of recognition may nevertheless help clarify a potential pathway through which reward systems influence behavior.

Future research should also examine additional perceived consequences of different trophy systems. For example, children may believe that participation‐based rewards encourage involvement and trying new activities, whereas selective rewards may discourage those who are uncertain about their chances of succeeding. Emotionally, selective reward systems may also be seen as more likely to evoke anxiety, which can have downstream consequences for motivation and performance (Rice et al. 2019). Future research should also examine the perceived fairness of both systems, given connections between award systems and broader sociopolitical ideologies (Shao and Heyman 2026). In addition, although the description and illustration implied that all trophies were identical, the key distinction between conditions concerned who received them rather than how they looked. Real‐world settings often include hybrid models, such as providing rank‐differentiated trophies for top performers alongside participation awards for all. Future work could examine how such mixed systems influence children's and adolescents’ perceptions of fairness and motivation.

Our findings provide important practical insights for parents and educators by highlighting how different reward systems communicate implicit social messages to children. For example, a selective reward system may signal that athletes are expected to perform at their highest potential, whereas an inclusive reward system may be viewed as fostering a less risky environment for individuals to enjoy sports.

In conclusion, we demonstrate that, starting in childhood, individuals make systematic inferences about the motivational, emotional, and behavioral consequences of selective versus inclusive reward systems in the context of youth sports, with adolescents showing a broader range of such inferences. Children reliably linked reward structures to effort and pride, indicating early‐emerging expectations that incentives shape motivation and emotional experience. By adolescence, these expectations expanded to include inferences about how competitive rewards can encourage cheating and risk‐taking, reflecting a growing appreciation of tensions between incentives, norms, and personal safety. Adolescents’ preferences were also associated with their own perceived running ability, suggesting that evaluations of reward systems become increasingly aligned with self‐interest during this period. Together, these findings show how reasoning about incentives becomes more differentiated across development, offering insight into the psychological foundations of disagreement about fairness and reward structures later in life.
