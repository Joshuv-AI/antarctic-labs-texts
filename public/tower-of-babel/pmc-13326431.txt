Structured phantom-assisted simulation for early abdominal ultrasound skill training in ultrasound medicine residents: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Tianhong Wei, Mujing Ke, Bo Zhang, Zhiyou He, Xiuyun Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbdominal ultrasound is a core component of residency training in ultrasound medicine, yet opportunities for repetitive and standardized bedside practice are often limited in routine clinical settings. Although simulation-based teaching may help address this gap, evidence remains limited regarding the role of abdominal ultrasound phantom training in standardized residency curricula. This study evaluated whether integrating structured phantom-assisted simulation into routine residency teaching was associated with improved early abdominal ultrasound skill acquisition and clinically oriented performance among novice residents.MethodsWe conducted a quasi-experimental study with a historical comparison cohort at a tertiary teaching hospital. Consecutive postgraduate year 2 residents in ultrasound medicine were included. The intervention cohort (2023–2025) received the routine curriculum supplemented with a 4-week structured phantom-assisted training module, whereas the historical comparison cohort (2020–2022) underwent the conventional bedside apprenticeship-based curriculum alone. The primary outcome was total score on a standardized patient-based abdominal ultrasound objective structured clinical examination (OSCE). The secondary outcome was learner-reported self-assessment measured using a 6-item questionnaire. Standard-view acquisition and embedded-lesion recognition were assessed as intervention-process measures within the intervention cohort.ResultsA total of 58 residents were included (intervention, n = 32; historical comparison, n = 26). Baseline characteristics were comparable between cohorts. The intervention cohort achieved significantly higher OSCE scores than the historical comparison cohort (87.16 ± 3.81 vs. 84.04 ± 4.00), with a mean difference of 3.12 points (95% CI 1.06–5.18; P = 0.004; Cohen’s d = 0.80). Inter-rater reliability for OSCE scores was good (ICC[2,1] = 0.746). Among residents in the intervention cohort, 71.9% completed all required standard views, and lesion-recognition task performance ranged from 78.6% to 86.5% across assessed domains. Compared with the historical comparison cohort, the intervention cohort also reported higher learner-reported scores in understanding of ultrasound anatomy, perceived skill mastery, and clinical readiness (all P < 0.05).ConclusionsIntegration of a structured phantom-assisted simulation module into residency teaching was associated with improved abdominal ultrasound OSCE performance and better learner-reported outcomes in selected domains. Introduction

Abdominal ultrasound is an important imaging modality in contemporary clinical practice and a core competency in residency training in ultrasound medicine [1–4]. However, learning abdominal ultrasound can be challenging because competent performance requires coordination of probe handling, anatomical orientation, standard-view acquisition, and real-time image interpretation. For novice trainees, these combined technical and cognitive demands may make early skill acquisition difficult.

Traditionally, abdominal ultrasound training has relied largely on bedside apprenticeship in authentic clinical settings [5, 6]. Although this approach remains essential, opportunities for repetitive and supervised hands-on practice may be limited by clinical workload, case availability, patient cooperation, and teaching time. For novice residents, these constraints may reduce opportunities for deliberate practice and timely feedback, thereby affecting the consistency of early skill acquisition.

Simulation-based medical education is increasingly used as a supplement to bedside teaching in clinical skills training [7–9]. By providing a controlled and reproducible learning environment, simulation can support repeated practice without relying solely on patient-based encounters [10–12]. In this study, “phantom-assisted simulation” refers specifically to the use of an abdominal ultrasound training phantom within a structured, faculty-supervised simulation module; it represents one modality of simulation-based education rather than the full scope of simulation-based training.

Despite the increasing use of simulation in medical education, evidence for abdominal ultrasound phantom training in residency education remains limited, particularly for diagnostic abdominal ultrasound. Previous studies have focused mainly on point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) and basic procedural tasks. Unlike POCUS, which targets focused clinical questions, diagnostic abdominal ultrasound requires systematic, multi-organ scanning with rigorous image quality standards. Less attention has been paid to these integrated skills required for diagnostic abdominal ultrasound [13–16]. Few studies have examined how abdominal ultrasound phantom training can be incorporated into a standardized residency curriculum.

This study aimed to evaluate the integration of an abdominal ultrasound training phantom into residency teaching and to examine its association with early skill acquisition and clinically oriented performance among novice residents.

Methods

Study design and participants

This quasi-experimental study used a historical comparison design at Xiangya Hospital, a tertiary teaching hospital (Fig. 1). The study population comprised consecutive postgraduate year 2 (PGY2) residents in ultrasound medicine. In China, residency training in ultrasound medicine is a structured 3-year postgraduate training pathway for physicians who will practice diagnostic ultrasound as a specialty. PGY2 residents were selected because they had completed initial orientation and basic clinical exposure, but were still at an early stage of developing independent abdominal ultrasound scanning skills. Therefore, they represented an appropriate target population for a preparatory phantom-assisted simulation module. The intervention cohort (2023–2025) received the routine curriculum supplemented with structured simulation training using an abdominal ultrasound training phantom (ABDFAN; Kyoto Kagaku Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan), whereas the historical comparison cohort (2020–2022) underwent the routine bedside apprenticeship-based curriculum. To reduce time-related confounding inherent to the historical comparison design, both cohorts followed the same curriculum objectives and objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) scoring standards and were supervised within the same faculty framework. Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, and theoretical examination scores were collected for both cohorts.

Study design and outcome assessment. The primary outcome was the SP-based abdominal ultrasound OSCE total score. The secondary outcome was learner-reported self-assessment measured using the same 6-item questionnaire in both cohorts. Standard-view acquisition and embedded-lesion recognition were assessed as intervention-process measures within the intervention cohort only. PGY, postgraduate year; OSCE, objective structured clinical examination; SP, standardized patient

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. As this study was an educational evaluation using routinely collected, de-identified educational data, no formal ethics approval number was assigned. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the committee, and participant identifiers were removed and replaced with study codes before analysis.

The structured phantom-assisted intervention

The intervention was integrated into the existing PGY2 abdominal ultrasound curriculum as a structured phantom-assisted training module. The module was organized as a stepwise training sequence to support repeated practice of core scanning tasks under faculty supervision.

The module was delivered over 4 consecutive weeks, comprising two 90-minute sessions per week for a total of 12 h of supervised simulation. Each session followed a standardized four-step protocol: (1) Faculty demonstrated standardized scanning procedures, including acquisition of standard views and acoustic window optimization, using the phantom. (2) Following the demonstration, residents engaged in hands-on deliberate practice, focusing on mastering standard views. (3) Within the phantom, trainees were also required to identify and localize various embedded simulated lesions, including cystic lesions, solid masses, and stones. (4) Throughout the entire process, faculty provided immediate feedback and real-time corrective guidance on probe manipulation and image optimization. Outcome measures and assessment

Outcome evaluation was informed by the Kirkpatrick framework and included both learning-related performance measures (Level 2) and learner-reported outcomes (Level 1) [17].

The primary outcome was the total score on a standardized patient (SP)-based objective OSCE administered as part of the PGY2 year-end practical examination [20, 21]. The OSCE cases were selected from the same institutional abdominal ultrasound OSCE case bank and were matched across cohorts in task scope, organ systems assessed, time allocation, and scoring criteria. The scoring rubric and examination procedures remained unchanged during the study period. Residents were required to perform a comprehensive abdominal ultrasound examination on a live SP, with performance independently rated by two faculty examiners. Both examiners were ultrasound physicians with more than 10 years of clinical teaching and assessment experience and had completed national-level examiner training for standardized residency assessment in China. A standardized 100-point rating form was used (Supplementary File S2). The final OSCE score was calculated as the mean of the two examiners’ scores. When the discrepancy between the two ratings exceeded 5 points, the case was reviewed and a consensus score was determined through discussion.

The secondary outcome was learner-reported self-assessment, which was evaluated in both cohorts using the same structured 6-item post-course questionnaire. The questionnaire was routinely administered at the end of the abdominal ultrasound rotation under routine course-evaluation conditions. It covered six domains: understanding of ultrasound anatomy, ability to recognize abdominal lesions, mastery of abdominal ultrasound skills, engagement and participation, learning interest, and clinical readiness. The same questionnaire items, wording, administration timing, and scoring scale were used in both cohorts. Each item was scored on an 11-point numerical rating scale ranging from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating more favorable learner-reported outcomes. This scale was used because it was the routine institutional course-evaluation format. Responses from the historical comparison cohort had been collected prospectively as part of routine course evaluation and were retrospectively retrieved for this study.

In addition, intervention-process measures were collected within the intervention cohort only to describe implementation of the phantom-assisted training module. These measures included completion of predefined standard views across the target organs/systems and performance on phantom-based lesion-recognition tasks. For lesion-recognition tasks, each resident was assessed across 18 embedded lesion sites, and performance was recorded for lesion scanning, localization, measurement, and preliminary diagnosis. Completion of predefined standard views was assessed by faculty supervisors using a structured training checklist. Residents were required to save images of the predefined standard views during training, and completion was judged in real time by the supervising faculty. When the adequacy of a view was uncertain, the saved images were reviewed for confirmation. Because these measures were specific to the phantom-assisted training module, they were not collected in the historical comparison cohort and were not used as comparative outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range (IQR)], and categorical variables as frequency (percentage), as appropriate. Baseline comparability between cohorts was assessed using independent-samples t-tests, chi-square tests, as appropriate. Between-group differences in SP-based abdominal ultrasound OSCE total scores were analyzed using the independent-samples t-test; mean differences, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and Cohen’s d were calculated. Inter-rater reliability for the two independent pre-consensus OSCE ratings was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC [1, 2]), based on a two-way random-effects model for absolute agreement. Intervention-process measures, including standard-view acquisition and phantom-based lesion-recognition task performance, were summarized descriptively. Item-level scores on the 6-item learner-reported questionnaire were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test, with effect size r reported. Internal consistency of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s α. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Primary outcome: SP-based abdominal ultrasound OSCE performance

The primary outcome of this study was the total score on the SP-based abdominal ultrasound OSCE. Residents who received phantom-assisted training performed better than those in the historical comparison cohort, with mean scores of 87.16 ± 3.81 and 84.04 ± 4.00, respectively. This yielded a statistically significant mean difference of 3.12 points (t = 3.03, P = 0.004; 95% CI, 1.06–5.18) (Fig. 3). The magnitude of the between-group difference was moderate to large (Cohen’s d = 0.80), suggesting that the addition of phantom-based training was associated with a practically meaningful improvement in abdominal ultrasound performance.

Scoring consistency between the 2 raters was good (ICC [1, 2] = 0.746), indicating that the observed score difference was unlikely to be explained by substantial rater variability. No OSCE assessment required consensus scoring because no paired examiner scores differed by more than 5 points.

Secondary outcome: learner-reported outcomes

All 58 distributed questionnaires were returned and considered valid, yielding a response rate of 100%. The questionnaire showed good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.856). At the item level (Table 2), the intervention cohort scored significantly higher than the historical comparison cohort in understanding of ultrasound anatomy, mastery of abdominal ultrasound skills, and clinical readiness (all P < 0.05). No statistically significant between-group differences were found for ability to recognize abdominal lesions, engagement and participation, or learning interest (all P > 0.05).

Intervention-process measures: standard-view acquisition and lesion-recognition tasks

Within the intervention cohort, 23 of 32 residents (71.9%) successfully acquired all required standard views across the five target organs/systems. Acquisition failures were concentrated in several technically demanding views. The most frequently missed view was the right posterior hepatic intercostal oblique view, which was not obtained by 6 residents (18.8%). This was followed by the distal common bile duct long-axis view in 5 residents (15.6%), the longitudinal view of the pancreatic neck and uncinate process in 4 residents (12.5%), and the right kidney transverse hilar vascular view in 3 residents (9.4%).

In the phantom-based lesion recognition task, the highest performance was observed for complete and clear lesion scanning (86.5%) and lesion localization (82.5%), whereas lower correct-performance rates were found for lesion measurement (79.3%) and preliminary diagnosis (78.6%) (Table 3, Supplementary File S3).

Discussion

This quasi-experimental study found that integrating a structured, phantom-assisted simulation module into a standardized residency curriculum was associated with improved early abdominal ultrasound skill acquisition. Residents in the intervention cohort achieved higher scores on a clinically oriented, SP-based abdominal ultrasound OSCE than those in the historical comparison cohort, with a moderate-to-large effect size. They also reported higher scores in three learner-reported domains: understanding of ultrasound anatomy, mastery of abdominal ultrasound skills, and clinical readiness.

The observed performance gains are educationally plausible when viewed through the lens of Cognitive Load Theory [18]. Learning diagnostic abdominal ultrasound is inherently demanding for novices because it requires the simultaneous integration of probe manipulation, spatial orientation, and real-time image interpretation. Accordingly, the primary bottleneck in early learning may lie less in knowing what to identify than in consistently acquiring the required views [19, 20]. In authentic clinical settings, extraneous load—such as patient discomfort, time pressure, and case variability—may exceed beginners’ limited working memory capacity, thereby hindering the formation of stable visual-anatomical associations [18, 21]. In the present study, the structured phantom-assisted training may have mitigated this problem by offering a stable and reproducible setting for stepwise supervised practice. Residents could focus first on standard-view acquisition, probe movement, and visual-anatomical linkage under reduced clinical distractions. This interpretation is consistent with the higher SP-based abdominal ultrasound OSCE scores and improved learner-reported outcomes in anatomical understanding, skill mastery, and clinical readiness, suggesting that the intervention may have helped residents develop more stable scanning routines and visual-anatomical schemas.

The intervention-process findings helped identify specific technical bottlenecks during the phantom-assisted module. Although most residents completed the required standard-view acquisition tasks, several technically demanding views were acquired less consistently, particularly the right posterior hepatic intercostal oblique view and the distal common bile duct long-axis view. These views require refined acoustic window selection and subtle probe angulation, skills that can be difficult for novice residents to develop under routine clinical constraints. From the perspective of deliberate practice, the value of simulation lies not simply in increasing repetition, but in enabling trainees to refine scanning strategies under standardized, low-risk conditions with immediate corrective guidance [22, 23]. The intervention-process findings therefore support the instructional role of the module as a setting for focused, repetitive practice on specific technical bottlenecks before transition to real patients. This structured approach may help explain the higher SP-based abdominal ultrasound OSCE scores observed in the intervention cohort.

The pattern of learner-reported outcomes requires cautious interpretation. The intervention cohort reported higher scores for anatomical understanding, perceived skill mastery, and clinical readiness, but not for lesion recognition, engagement, or learning interest. This pattern suggests that the main educational contribution of the phantom-assisted module may have been to provide a more structured environment for repeated scanning practice, anatomical orientation, and preparation for clinical examination, rather than to increase general motivation or learning interest. The lack of difference in learner-reported lesion recognition is also plausible, because diagnostic lesion recognition may require broader clinical exposure and greater case variability than can be provided by a short phantom-based module.

One strength of this study is that the primary outcome was evaluated using an SP-based abdominal ultrasound OSCE, rather than being limited to performance on the training phantom or image-recognition tests [24–27]. This format provides a more clinically oriented assessment because it requires trainees to go beyond phantom-specific proficiency and perform abdominal scanning on a human participant under standardized conditions. During the OSCE, residents were required to integrate multiple components—including patient communication, positioning, scanning sequence, probe handling, and real-time image optimization—within a structured assessment task. Compared with prior ultrasound education studies that focused mainly on classroom teaching, image interpretation, or isolated simulation tasks [25, 28, 29], this assessment approach allowed us to evaluate a more integrated form of abdominal ultrasound performance. The significantly higher OSCE scores observed in the intervention cohort may therefore suggest early transfer of learning to a patient-based assessment setting. However, because an OSCE remains a standardized examination environment, these findings should be interpreted as evidence of improved clinically oriented performance rather than direct proof of improved real-world clinical performance.

The findings may be particularly relevant to residency training environments characterized by high clinical service demands and uneven educational exposure. In many training settings, opportunities for learning abdominal ultrasound are influenced by case mix, workload, teaching time, and variation in faculty experience, resulting in unequal clinical exposure and practice opportunities across trainees [16, 30, 31]. In this context, the value of abdominal ultrasound training phantoms lies not only in providing a technical practice platform, but also in offering a relatively standardized supplementary pathway for early skills training. Its role is not to replace real-patient examination, but to provide a more stable and controlled preparatory phase before supervised clinical practice. Therefore, in settings such as residency training in China, where large-scale training must be delivered under conditions of unevenly distributed clinical teaching resources, this approach may also help promote training equity [30, 31].

From an implementation perspective, the training arrangement used in this study also suggests that integrating phantom-assisted training into the routine residency curriculum is feasible [10, 11, 32]. A relatively short structured module, totaling approximately 12 h, was sufficient to produce measurable educational gains, indicating that such an intervention may be incorporated into residency training in a practical manner. At the same time, the effectiveness of phantom-assisted training is still likely to depend heavily on instructional design itself, including whether training objectives are clearly defined, whether practice is organized around standard views, and whether timely, specific instructor feedback is provided. In other words, a more important issue may not simply be whether a phantom is available, but how phantom-based training is embedded within a continuous instructional sequence that includes demonstration, supervised practice, and subsequent clinical learning [33, 34]. By providing a standardized preparatory phase, this type of training may help residents achieve a more consistent baseline level of competence before entering clinical scanning, thereby improving training efficiency.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings suggest that integrating a structured, phantom-assisted simulation module into a standardized residency curriculum was associated with improved early abdominal ultrasound skill acquisition among novice learners. Compared with the historical comparison cohort, residents in the intervention cohort showed higher performance in a clinically oriented SP-based abdominal ultrasound OSCE and reported higher scores in anatomical understanding, perceived skill mastery, and clinical readiness.

These findings suggest that structured phantom-assisted training may serve as a useful bridge between early skills acquisition and supervised clinical practice, while providing a standardized preparatory phase for novice residents. In residency training environments with high clinical service demands, such a structured, phantom-assisted approach offers a practical and effective strategy to optimize abdominal ultrasound education.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the historical comparison design is susceptible to time-related confounding. Although both cohorts followed the same curriculum objectives, OSCE scoring standards, and faculty framework, accumulated faculty teaching experience during the intervention years and the overlap of the historical comparison period with the COVID-19 pandemic may have influenced teaching conditions, clinical exposure, and patient availability. These factors could not be fully separated from the effect of the phantom-assisted intervention. Second, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, and the generalizability of the findings requires validation in other residency programs. Third, although the SP-based abdominal ultrasound OSCE was more clinically oriented than phantom-based or image-recognition assessments, it remains a surrogate measure of real-world clinical performance and may not fully capture the variability and workflow demands of routine practice. Finally, this study primarily assessed short-term outcomes; whether these improvements translate into sustained competence or better workplace performance requires further longitudinal evaluation.
