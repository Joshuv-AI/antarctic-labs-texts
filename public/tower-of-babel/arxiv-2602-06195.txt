DeDPO: Debiased Direct Preference Optimization for Diffusion Models
Khiem Pham, Quang Nguyen, Tung Nguyen, Jingsen Zhu, Michele Santacatterina, Dimitris Metaxas, Ramin Zabih

Abstract
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has emerged as a predominant alignment method for diffusion models, facilitating off-policy training without explicit reward modeling. However, its reliance on large-scale, high-quality human preference labels presents a severe cost and scalability bottleneck. To overcome this, We propose a semi-supervised framework augmenting limited human data with a large corpus of unlabeled pairs annotated via cost-effective synthetic AI feedback. Our paper introduces Debiased DPO (DeDPO), which uniquely integrates a debiased estimation technique from causal inference into the DPO objective. By explicitly identifying and correcting the systematic bias and noise inherent in synthetic annotators, DeDPO ensures robust learning from imperfect feedback sources, including self-training and Vision-Language Models (VLMs). Experiments demonstrate that DeDPO is robust to the variations in synthetic labeling methods, achieving performance that matches and occasionally exceeds the theoretical upper bound of models trained on fully human-labeled data. This establishes DeDPO as a scalable solution for human-AI alignment using inexpensive synthetic supervision.



1
Introduction

Text-to-image diffusion models have demonstrated impressive capabilities in generating photorealistic and semantically consistent images from natural language prompts
15
;
9
;
44
. These models are typically trained with supervised learning on large-scale image–text datasets
46
, which enables broad generalization but offers limited control over fine-grained image attributes valued by users, such as prompt adherence or aesthetic quality. As a result, generated outputs may often diverge from user intent despite textual relevance.

To bridge this gap, preference-based alignment methods have been developed to fine-tune generative models with human feedback. In language models, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
37
;
18
has proven effective by training a reward model on pairwise human annotations and applying policy-gradient methods
36
;
48
for fine-tuning. Similar RL-based strategies have been extended to image generation
3
;
11
, where diffusion models are optimized using rewards derived from human judgments. However, these methods incur substantial computational costs due to the need to train and maintain a separate reward model. To mitigate this, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
42
was introduced to directly fine-tune language models on human preference pairs, eliminating the reward model requirement. Building on this success, Diffusion-DPO
57
extends DPO to text-to-image diffusion models, achieving efficient human alignment. This approach has since been adapted to various generative tasks, including image inpainting
50
, video generation
31
;
61
, and physically grounded 3D generation
29
.

Despite its effectiveness, DPO-based methods face a key challenge: acquiring large-scale human preference labels is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming, limiting scalability. To address this, recent efforts have explored using synthetic preferences as a cheaper alternative
14
;
25
. However, such feedback may diverge from human judgment, introduce noise, and cause instability during training. Other approaches attempt to create synthetic preferences by degrading high-quality images
39
, but the lack of real human supervision can cause misalignment and limit effectiveness. In essence, directly training on AI-generated or synthetic preferences can be seen as learning from misaligned or noisy feedback, which undermines the reliability of alignment.

To address this problem, we propose
DeDPO
, a simple yet effective method that combines DPO with a debiased estimator to improve the training stability and robustness to imperfect feedback. DeDPO retains the desirable properties of DPO, training with completely offline data, while simultaneously achieving robustness to potential errors in synthetic preferences. Our method achieves performance equal to, and sometimes incrementally better than DPO trained on fully labeled data, even when only a small set of high-quality human preferences is available alongside a larger set of potentially noisy synthetic preferences. Experiments demonstrate that DeDPO consistently improves alignment quality and robustness in settings where human feedback is scarce and synthetic feedback is unreliable, offering a scalable path to aligning diffusion models with human values.

2
Related Work

Alignment by synthetic preferences.

Synthetic preferences for DPO can be obtained from two types of models: reward models trained on large datasets of preference pairs (e.g., PickScore
23
, HPSv2
60
), and pretrained vision-language models (e.g., CLIP
41
, Aesthetic
47
) that were not specifically trained on preference data. The original Diffusion-DPO
57
proposes using both types of these synthetic preferences. Recent work
20
uses reward models to label synthetically generated image pairs for DPO training as a scalable approach for learning from online data. However, this approach inherently assumes access to a large dataset of labeled preference pairs,
whereas we consider a more realistic setting with only a small set of labeled pairs and a larger set of unlabeled pairs
. Therefore, the most relevant approach to our work is the second one, which utilizes pretrained vision-language models that were not trained on preference pairs. Another line of work by
68
uses self-play: the diffusion model iteratively competes against its own earlier versions to improve alignment. These approaches (self-play or pretrained-model feedback) motivate our focus on data-efficient alignment without large human datasets. Beyond text-to-image generation, there is a plethora of works using multimodal synthetic data to train large vision-language models
30
;
27
;
69
;
58
;
26
;
8
;
28
. These typically distill knowledge from responses of much larger models such as ChatGPT and Gemini. However, to our knowledge, there is no prior work on using multimodal models to generate preference labels for DPO training.

Robust alignment with noisy labels.

AI feedback (synthetic preference) may be noisy and inconsistent with real human preference, leading to unstable alignment, so it is crucial to develop a robust alignment strategy against noisy preference labels. Existing work largely follows two directions. The first line assumes a parametric noise model on the labels
4
;
7
;
35
. For instance,
35
introduce a conservative variant of DPO by smoothing the binary labels (analogous to label smoothing
54
), and
7
further debias this loss and analyze its sub-optimality gap. A second line uses distributionally robust optimization (DRO)
63
;
59
;
22
;
34
, either by reweighting DPO gradients to approximate a robust objective
63
or by adding gradient regularization terms
34
. In contrast, we do not assume a specific noise model and, crucially, we assume access to only a small set of high-quality human preference labels. This reference set allows us to correct bias in AI feedback rather than optimizing for worst-case noise alone. Moreover, prior robust DPO work focuses on language models, while we study robustness for visual generation. Related approaches in RLHF learn robust reward models
64
;
5
;
13
;
66
;
38
, whereas our method remains reward-model-free and directly tackles noisy labels from pretrained models.

Doubly Robust / Debiased estimators.

Doubly Robust / Debiased estimators originated in the missing-data and causal-inference literature
43
;
45
;
2
and have since been applied to off-policy reinforcement learning
10
;
19
;
56
and causal machine learning
6
. The key idea is to combine two nuisance models that target the same quantity into a meta-estimator that remains consistent if
either
model is correctly specified, and attains semi-parametric efficiency when they are both sufficiently accurate
6
;
21
;
12
. When used as a loss, such debiased objectives yield efficient parameter estimation. Closest to our setting, these ideas have been used for semi-supervised classification
70
by treating pseudo-labels as noisy surrogates of missing labels and designing a debiased loss robust to pseudo-label errors. However, they neither leverage AI feedback from external models nor enforce sample splitting, which is crucial for unbiased and efficient debiased estimation (see our method section). More recently,
62
apply these techniques to robustly align large language models, but their algorithm requires online data generation, departing from the fully offline nature of DPO. In contrast, we integrate debiased estimation directly into preference optimization for diffusion models, improving robustness to synthetic, noisy preferences while preserving the offline training setting.

3
Preliminaries

Text-to-image diffusion model.

Diffusion models
53
;
16
;
52
;
9
;
44
generate images by learning to reverse a gradual noising process applied to image latents. Formally, they model the transformation from a standard Gaussian noise map
\bm{{\bm{\epsilon}}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,{\bm{I}})
to a sample from the target data distribution via a denoising diffusion process. The forward (noising) process perturbs a clean image latent
{\bm{x}}_{0}
across multiple timesteps
t
as:

{\bm{x}}_{t}=\alpha_{t}{\bm{x}}_{0}+\sigma_{t}{\bm{\epsilon}},

(1)

where
\alpha_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}
are time-dependent scaling coefficients satisfying
\alpha_{0}=\sigma_{T}=1
and
\alpha_{T}=\sigma_{0}=0
. The reverse process learns to recover
{\bm{x}}_{0}
by denoising
{\bm{x}}_{t}
using a noise estimator
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}
, trained to predict the noise
{\bm{\epsilon}}
via:

\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),{\bm{\epsilon}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,{\bm{I}})}\left\|{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)-{\bm{\epsilon}}\right\|^{2}_{2}.

(2)

In the text-to-image setting, models are further conditioned on text embeddings
{\mathbf{c}}
, modifying the noise predictor to
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t,{\mathbf{c}})
. This conditioning enables the generation to be guided by textual prompts. To strengthen alignment between the image and the text, many models incorporate classifier-free guidance (CFG)
9
;
17
, which blends conditional and unconditional predictions using a guidance scale
\gamma
. The guided output is computed as:

\hat{{\bm{\epsilon}}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t,{\mathbf{c}})={\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)+\gamma\left({\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t,{\mathbf{c}})-{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\right),

(3)

where
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t,{\mathbf{c}})
denotes the prediction conditioned on the text, and
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)
denotes the unconditional prediction. CFG enhances both the fidelity and semantic relevance of generated images without relying on an external classifier.

DPO for text-to-image diffusion models.
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
42
offers an efficient alternative to reward modeling for aligning generative models with human preferences. Diffusion-DPO
57
extends this approach to text-to-image diffusion models by adapting the DPO objective to the denoising process. Since diffusion models generate images through a sequence of latent states over
T
timesteps, directly computing the full trajectory likelihood is intractable. To address this, Diffusion-DPO proposes a surrogate loss based on denoising score comparisons at individual timesteps. For a given text prompt
{\bm{c}}
and an image pair
({\bm{x}}_{t}^{w},{\bm{x}}_{t}^{l})
annotated by human preference
{\bm{x}}_{t}^{w}\succ{\bm{x}}_{t}^{l}
, with Gaussian noise samples
({\bm{\epsilon}}^{w},{\bm{\epsilon}}^{l})\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,{\bm{I}})
, the preference loss is approximated as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,{\bm{c}},{\bm{x}}_{t}^{w},{\bm{x}}_{t}^{l}}[-\log{\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{c}},{\bm{x}}_{t}^{l},{\bm{x}}_{t}^{w})],

(4)

\displaystyle\text{where }{\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{c}},{\bm{x}}_{t}^{l},{\bm{x}}_{t}^{w})=

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma\Big(-\beta\big(\|{\bm{\epsilon}}^{w}-{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{w},t)\|_{2}^{2}-\|{\bm{\epsilon}}^{w}-{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\text{ref}}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{w},t)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\|{\bm{\epsilon}}^{l}-{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{l},t)\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\bm{\epsilon}}^{l}-{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\text{ref}}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{l},t)\|_{2}^{2}\big)\Big)

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function,
{\bm{x}}_{t}^{w}
and
{\bm{x}}_{t}^{l}
are the noise-perturbed images at timestep
t
(
Eq.

1
),
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}
is the trainable noise predictor and
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\text{ref}}
is a frozen reference model. This formulation encourages the model to improve denoising quality on preferred samples relative to less preferred ones, effectively aligning generation with preference signals without relying on reward modeling. It also regularizes the model to stay close to the reference model
(
42
)
.

4
Method

Problem setting.
Due to the high cost of acquiring human preference labels, we consider a semi-supervised DPO setting with a small labeled set of text-to-image pairs and a larger unlabeled set. For the latter, we use AI feedback to generate synthetic preference labels.

DPO as binary classification.
We first re-interpret DPO in
Eq.

4
as an equivalent binary classification problem. For an
unordered
image pair
({\bm{x}}^{0},{\bm{x}}^{1})
with text prompt
{\bm{c}}
, we denote
{\bm{z}}
as the preference label over the pair, where
{\bm{z}}=1
denotes preference on
{\bm{x}}^{1}
and
{\bm{z}}=0
otherwise. With this label, we can rewrite the loss in
Eq.

4
as a binary cross-entropy loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,{\bm{c}},{\bm{x}}_{t}^{0},{\bm{x}}_{t}^{1}}\left[{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{c}},{\bm{x}}_{t}^{0},{\bm{x}}_{t}^{1}),{\bm{z}})\right],

(5)

\displaystyle\text{where }{\mathcal{L}}(a,b)=-b\log a+(1-b)\log(1-a)

which is a binary classification loss with predictive model
\hat{\bm{z}}={\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{c}},{\bm{x}}_{t}^{0},{\bm{x}}_{t}^{1})
and target label
{\bm{z}}
.

Notes on symbols.

We drop the subscript
t
for brevity. For example,
{\bm{x}}_{t}^{0}
is written as
{\bm{x}}^{0}
. We also drop the subscripts of the expectation operator
\mathbb{E}
.
\mathbb{E}_{n}
denotes the average over
n
samples (equivalent to the operator
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\cdot
).
{\bm{y}}
denotes the input triplet
{\bm{y}}=({\bm{c}},{\bm{x}}^{0},{\bm{x}}^{1})
. Therefore,
Eq.

5
can be written as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{n}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}),{\bm{z}})

(6)

We will slightly overload
{\mathcal{G}}
to mean any mapping from the input
{\bm{y}}
to the label prediction
\hat{\bm{z}}
, reserving
{\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}
for the model’s implicit margin (
Eq.

4
). Additionally, we note that the expectation is also taken over the denoising time
t
.

Towards a semi-supervised approach.
With our semi-supervised setting, let
{\bm{y}}_{l}
and
{\bm{y}}_{u}
be the labeled and unlabeled data, and
n_{l}
and
n_{u}
be the number of labeled and unlabeled samples. For the unlabeled data, we generate
synthetic preference
labels
\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}}_{u})
by some cheap but possibly incorrect annotator
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
. A straightforward and naive solution to solve the semi-supervised problem is to use the empirical average loss:

L_{\text{OR}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{n_{l}}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}_{l}),{\mathbf{z}}_{l})+\mathbb{E}_{n_{u}}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}_{u}),\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}}_{u}))

(7)

which is also known as the Outcome Regression (OR) estimator of the loss in causal inference literature,
i.e
.
we are
imputing
the missing label
{\mathbf{z}}_{u}
with the synthetic preference. However, this estimator is biased because the synthetic preference
\hat{\mathbf{z}}
differs from the ground truth human preference
{\mathbf{z}}
in general. Alternatively, in this paper, we propose using the
debiased loss
as follows:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle L_{\text{DeDPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{n_{l}+n_{u}}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}),\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}}))

(8)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{n_{l}}({\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}_{l}),{\mathbf{z}}_{l})-{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}_{l}),\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}}_{l})))

In the remaining part of this section, we’ll verify that our proposed debiased loss in
Eq.

8
is unbiased and robust, describe how we generate synthetic preference by
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
to train the diffusion model in practice, and theoretically show the convergence rate of the diffusion model
\theta
(seen as an implicit classifier) is robust to the potentially slow rate of the synthetic preference model
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
.

4.1
Our DeDPO loss is unbiased

The first important property of the debiased loss is that it is an unbiased estimator of the population-level DPO loss
Eq.

6
no matter whether the pseudo label
\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}})
is correct or not.

Proposition 1
.

The expectation of our proposed debiased loss in
Eq.

8
is equal to the expectation of the original DPO loss in
Eq.

4
,
i.e
.

L_{\text{DeDPO}}
is unbiased:

\mathbb{E}[L_{\text{DeDPO}}(\theta)]=\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)]

(9)

Proof.

Simply re-order the terms in
Eq.

8
as:

\displaystyle L_{\text{DeDPO}}(\theta)=\ \mathbb{E}_{n_{l}}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}_{l}),{\mathbf{z}}_{l})

(10)

\displaystyle+\left[\mathbb{E}_{n_{l}+n_{u}}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}),\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}}))-\mathbb{E}_{n_{l}}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}_{l}),\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}}_{l}))\right]

Then, the expectation of the first term is the population-level DPO loss
Eq.

6
,
and the expectation of the second term is 0 because both are unbiased estimators of
\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}),\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}}))]
.
∎

This tells us that we can safely use the synthetic preference labels and still our loss is unbiased, which leads to bias-free learning.
Moreover, when the synthetic labels are perfect i.e.
\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}})={\mathbf{z}}
, the debiased loss becomes
\mathbb{E}_{n_{l}+n_{u}}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}),\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}}))
, effectively utilizing all of the data,
and when they are completely wrong, then asymptotically it becomes
\mathbb{E}_{n_{l}}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}_{l}),{\mathbf{z}}_{l})
i.e. only the labeled data is used.

4.2
Robustness against incorrect synthetic labels

We strive to give an intuitive explanation of how the debiased loss works. First, we combine the 3 loss terms into one with a new target label:

Proposition 2
.

Our debiased loss is characterized by using pure pseudo-labels for unlabeled data and applying an amplified correction from pseudo-labels toward ground-truth labels for labeled data.

L_{\text{DeDPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{n_{l}+n_{u}}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}),\hat{\bm{w}})

(11)

where:

\hat{\bm{w}}=\begin{cases}\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}})&\text{if }{\bm{y}}\text{ is unlabeled}\\
\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}})+\frac{n_{l}+n_{u}}{n_{l}}({\mathbf{z}}-\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}}))&\text{if }{\bm{y}}\text{ is labeled as }{\mathbf{z}}\end{cases}

Please refer to the supplementary for the proof. Intuitively, for unlabeled data points, our loss simply uses the pseudo-labels as the supervision signal. For labeled data points, the loss introduces a correction term
{\mathbf{z}}-\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}})
that moves the pseudo-label toward the true label, amplified by the importance weight
\frac{n_{l}+n_{u}}{n_{l}}\geq 1
, which reflects the scarcity of labeled data. When the pseudo-label is already correct, this correction term vanishes and the loss reduces to standard supervised learning. When the pseudo-label is incorrect, the amplified correction not only fixes the error on the labeled point itself but also implicitly influences nearby unlabeled points, pushing them in the correct direction. Under a smoothness assumption on
\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}})
, the labeled point thus steers its local neighborhood toward the correct label.

The importance weight can also be instance-dependent, especially when the labeled and unlabeled data are from different distributions.
However, we found little gain from using this variant, and in practice, we often
uniformly sample
data from a large initially unlabeled pool for high-quality labeling.

4.3
Synthetic preference labels

In this section, we consider 2 main ways to generate synthetic preference labels:

1.

From the implicit reward margin induced by the current policy
\theta
itself,
i.e
.
self-training.

2.

From a pretrained vision-language model (VLM).

As discussed before, we don’t consider feedback from reward models that have been trained on a large dataset of preference pairs (like
20
) because we are in the label-sparse setting.
The second method is straight forward and the synthetic labels are fixed once DPO starts. We discuss the first method in more detail.

Implicit reward margin as pseudo labels.

Self-training style synthetic labels in image classification are generated by the classifier at the previous iteration
\hat{\theta}
. The synthetic preference model therefore is
\hat{\mathcal{G}}({\bm{y}})={\mathcal{G}}_{\hat{\theta}}({\bm{y}})
i.e. the classifier in
Eq.

5
with parameter
\hat{\theta}
. Specifically, following
51
the loss at each given input is:

\delta({\mathcal{G}}_{\hat{\theta}}(\alpha_{1}({\bm{y}}))){\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}(\alpha_{2}({\bm{y}})),\text{round}({\mathcal{G}}_{\hat{\theta}}(\alpha_{3}({\bm{y}}))))

(12)

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
are augmentations of the input
{\bm{y}}
, respectively,
round
converts the soft sigmoid predictions to hard labels, and
\delta
is a threshold function that zeros out the loss when
{\mathcal{G}}_{\hat{\theta}}(\alpha_{1}({\bm{y}}))
falls below a certain confidence threshold (when the pseudo prediction is close to 0.5). In our alignment setting, since augmentations applied to the original image
{\bm{x}}_{0}
are not standard in image generation, we instead use the noisy images at different time steps
t
as the augmentations, preventing confirmation bias from the same input. This requires 2 additional forward passes of the diffusion model for each input. However, we also found that using no augmentations is already effective.

4.4
Fast convergence of DeDPO under slow convergence of the synthetic preference.

We now discuss the second theoretical property of our debiased loss which relates the speed of convergence of the learning process with respect to the number of samples and the quality of the synthetic preference model
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
.
Technically
70
articulates this property as convergence of the gradient of the debiased loss to a saddle point.
We state an alternative convergence result in the excess risk in the spirit of
12
:

Theorem 1

(Informal)
.

Under certain regularity conditions, namely

1.

the synthetic preference model
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
is trained on data independent of that for DPO training of
\theta

2.

both the diffusion model
\theta
and the preference model
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
are sufficiently smooth

3.

\theta
belongs to a VC-subgraph class

then with high probability, the model
\theta
learned by our DeDPO loss satisfies:

\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq O\left(\frac{1}{n_{l}+n_{u}}\right)+O\left(\|\hat{\mathcal{G}}-{\mathcal{G}}^{*}\|_{4}^{4}\right)

(13)

where
\theta^{*}
and
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
are optimal versions of
\theta
and
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
, and
\|\cdot\|_{2}
and
\|\cdot\|_{4}
are
L_{2}
and
L_{4}
norms:
\|f\|_{2}=(\mathbb{E}[f(x)^{2}])^{1/2}
and
\|f\|_{4}=(\mathbb{E}[f(x)^{4}])^{1/4}
.

The proof and assumptions mostly follow
12
. The most exciting aspect of
Theorem

1
is the 4th-power of right side’s second term
\|\hat{\mathcal{G}}-{\mathcal{G}}^{*}\|
, because it indicates that
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
needs to converge only at a much slower rate
\frac{1}{(n_{l}+n_{u})^{1/4}}
for the target parameter
\theta
to achieve the optimal convergence rate
\frac{1}{n_{l}+n_{u}}
. Therefore, our diffusion model can learn efficiently and robustly to the error in
{\mathcal{G}}
. Notably, the first assumption that the synthetic preference model
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
is trained on an independent set of data from
\theta
is violated by the self-training technique. This has practical consequences because if both
\theta
and
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
are trained on the labeled data, intuitively the correction terms in
Proposition

2
will overfit to 0, providing no signal for correction. Therefore, pretrained vision–language models are the preferred choice, and our setting is well suited to them.

5
Experiments

5.1
Experimental setup

Datasets.

For training, we use the FiFA dataset
65
, a high-quality subset of human preference data filtered from the Pick-a-Pic v2
24
and HPSv2
60
. Following
65
, we use the FiFA split containing 5K generated image pairs with human-labeled preferences, which was shown to outperform training on the full Pick-a-Pic v2 dataset. To study the semi-supervised setting with scarce human feedback, we partition FiFA into 25% labeled and 75% unlabeled pairs. The labeled subset is used with ground-truth human preferences, while the remaining 75% unlabeled pairs are assigned synthetic preferences from an AI feedback
1
1

1

https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct
. For evaluation, we follow standard text-to-image alignment benchmarks and use two prompt sets at test time: (i) PartiPrompt
67
, which contains 1,632 diverse textual prompts, and (ii) the HPSv2 benchmark prompts
60
, which consist of 4 categories with 800 prompts each, for a total of 3,200 prompts.

Evaluation protocols.

We compare DeDPO against supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and Diffusion-DPO
57
using two backbone diffusion models: SD1.5 and SDXL. We use standard automatic preference metrics. On the PartiPrompt test set, we report PickScore
24
(PS) and Aesthetic Score
2
2

2

https://github.com/christophschuhmann/improved-aesthetic-predictor
(AS), computed over images generated from all prompts. On the HPSv2 benchmark
60
, we report the average HPSv2 score across its four categories, following the original protocol.

Implementation details.

Training)
We train on the 25% human-labeled and 75% pseudo-labeled FiFA-5K
65
and HPDv2
60
split described above. For SD1.5, we fine-tune for 1,000 optimization steps using AdamW
32
, a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-7}
, a piecewise-constant learning rate schedule with 10 warmup steps, a global batch size of 128, 2 GPUs, and 8 gradient accumulation steps. For SDXL, we train for 100 steps using the AdaFactor
49
optimizer with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-8}
, 5 warmup steps, and the same piecewise-constant schedule.
Inference)
At test time, we sample images using 20 denoising steps with DDPMSolver++
33
as the noise scheduler and a classifier-free guidance (CFG) scale of 7.5 for all methods and backbones.

Quantitative results.

Conclusion & Discussion

In this work, we address the scalability bottleneck of preference optimization by proposing Debiased DPO (DeDPO). Motivated by the prohibitive cost of human supervision, DeDPO integrates a debiased estimation method from causal inference to robustly leverage large volumes of inexpensive, yet noisy, synthetic preference labels. Empirical results across multiple diffusion models and synthetic annotators demonstrate that DeDPO significantly outperforms naive synthetic training and matches or exceeds models trained on fully human-labeled data. Consequently, our approach facilitates scalable human-AI alignment, proving that properly debiased synthetic feedback is a reliable and cost-effective surrogate for extensive human labeling.

Synthetic Preference Bias.

In the preceding paragraph, we have said that our setting of synthetic preference is different from noisy (flipped) labels, and our robustness method is much more suitable for the synthetic preference setting, although it still performs well on the other setting. The reason is that the synthetic preference’s discrepancy with the human preference is not random noise, but rather systematic bias. This means that there are identifiable patterns in the way the synthetic preference makes errors. Obviously, the noise-model approaches are not suitable for this kind of error. To correct for bias, we need information about the true distribution that we want. Thus, we see that using a small set of high quality labeled data is an obvious solution in retrospect. With the debiased loss, DeDPO is thus able to enhance robustness to synthetic preference. To verify that there are identifiable systematic bias in the synthetic preference, we perform a detailed bias analysis comparing label generation between the synthetic annotator (Qwen-VLM) and human preference. By examining the prompts that yield disagreement in labels, we confirmed that the discrepancies are not random. There exist systematic patterns in how the synthetic model judges an image differently than a human. This systematic bias can stem from various sources, including the prompt engineering techniques used during the synthetic label generation process.
Fig.

5
demonstrates one such specific bias identified in our synthetic labels along with our detailed analysis.

Robustness against randomization.

To verify the robustness of DeDPO’s performance gains over DPO and eliminate the dependency on randomness, we performed 15 independent test runs with different random seeds. Across both SD1.5 and SDXL architecture, DeDPO exhibits variance comparable to or smaller than all DPO variants. For SD1.5, the standard deviation (
\sigma
) of PickScore across runs is
\sigma_{\text{DeDPO}}=0.0076
. This is lower than all DPO variants, with the minimum is
\sigma_{\text{DPO}+25\%}=0.0095
.Similarly, for SDXL, DeDPO demonstrates superior stability with a standard deviation of only
\sigma_{\text{DeDPO}}=0.0094
, compared to the corresponding DPO’s minimum baselines
\sigma_{\text{DPO}+\text{pseudo}}=0.0112
. Moreover, DeDPO consistently maintains higher PickScores across all seeds and models. These results validate that DeDPO performance gains are robust against random initializations.

Qwen-VLM prioritizes semantic coherence and aesthetic adherence, strictly following artistic style constraints.
