Stop Chasing the C-index: This Is How We Should Evaluate Our Survival Models
Christian Marius Lillelund, Shi-ang Qi, Russell Greiner, Christian Fischer Pedersen

Abstract
We argue that many survival analysis and time-to-event models are incorrectly evaluated. First, we survey many examples of evaluation approaches in the literature and find that most rely on concordance (C-index). However, the C-index only measures a model’s discriminative ability and does not assess other important aspects, such as the accuracy of the time-to-event predictions or the calibration of the model’s probabilistic estimates. Next, we present a set of key desiderata for choosing the right evaluation metric and discuss their pros and cons. These are tailored to the challenges in survival analysis, such as sensitivity to miscalibration and various censoring assumptions. We hypothesize that the current development of survival metrics conforms to a double-helix ladder, and that model validity and metric validity must stand on the same rung of the assumption ladder. Finally, we discuss the appropriate methods for evaluating a survival model in practice and summarize various viewpoints opposing our analysis.



1
Introduction

Time-to-event outcomes have an extensive history in research, where the focus was initially to predict the time until an event of interest –
such as death, relapse, or recovery in medicine, service cancellation in business, or failure of a mechanical component in engineering
– for a particular instance.
Much of the pioneering research on time-to-event outcomes focused on time until death, which led to the field being commonly known as
survival analysis

[
1
]
. Unlike standard regression tasks, a key challenge in training and evaluating survival models lies in handling
censored data
– instances where the event of interest has not been observed within the follow-up period
[
2
]
. In a medical study, only some of the patients may experience the event; the others may be lost to follow-up, withdraw from the study, or remain alive at the end of the study. For these patients, we do not know the exact event time, but instead know only a
lower bound
of that event time. This is known as
right-censoring
.

Even though the task resembles regression, few learned models explicitly predict the time until the event, for a novel instance. Instead, many models
M(\cdot)
predict only a risk score,
M(x)\in\mathbb{R}
for each instance encoded as
x\in X
(typically
\mathbb{R}^{d}
), with the understanding that
M(z)>M(y)
means
M(\cdot)
is predicting that
z
will die before
y
. This naturally leads to the concordance measure (aka C-index or simply CI), which (in essence) is measuring how often this ranking is correct –-
i.e.
, of the times when
M(z)>M(y)
, how often did
z

actually
die before
y
. Note that there are many other types of survival models (see
[
3
]
), including individual survival distribution (ISD) models, which for each
x
, estimates
\{S(t\mid x)\mid t\geq 0\}
, where
S(t\mid x)
is the probability that the target event will occur for
x
at a time
T
later than
t

[
4
]
. We can view this model as producing a risk of an instance, by setting an instance’s risk to be (the negative of) the mean of that instance’s distribution. This means that we can use a concordance measure to evaluate such models. But these ISDs provide more than just risk scores -– they can also compute the probability of surviving until a specific time (say, 1 year), the median survival time, or the probability of experiencing the event within a specific time window, etc. With this much flexibility, we are not constrained to only evaluate the model with concordance of the predictions. However, to this day, the research community has focused primarily on optimizing the C-index – for models that can produce a risk score, including simple
“risk models”
, as well as models that generate ISDs, etc. (see Figure
1
).

In a recent survey,
Zhou et al. 2023
report that over 80% of survival analysis studies published in leading statistical journals in 2023 use the C-index as their primary evaluation metric. At the same time, the metric has been criticized from a statistical and clinical perspective
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
Cook 2007
shows that the C-index is insensitive to the addition of new covariates in a model, even if the new covariates are statistically and clinically significant. In low-risk populations, the C-index often compares patients who have very similar risk probabilities, which may not give meaningful insights. For example, consider two very healthy patients, A and B. Patient A dies after 30 years and patient B after 31 years. Although they are considered
“concordant”
by the C-index, this concordance offers little practical value to medical professionals because their risk profiles are nearly identical.
Vickers and Cronin 2010
argue that because the C-index depends only on the ranks of the predicted values, models with inaccurate survival predictions can have C-indices that are much higher than those of a competing model with more accurate predictions.
Halligan et al. 2015
argue that the concepts of sensitivity and specificity can be meaningful in isolation, but since the C-index combines them, it loses meaning.

Position.
In this paper, we encourage researchers and practitioners in survival analysis, when dealing with models, to move beyond the narrow focus on discriminative performance and adopt a broader, more comprehensive evaluation strategy. We suggest first identifying the task at hand and then deciding on which evaluation metric to use that matches that task. Although previous work has highlighted why and when the C-index is problematic
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
, this paper is the first to introduce concrete desiderata for survival evaluation and provide a guide for selecting the appropriate metric for different evaluation needs. Since no single metric is appropriate in all scenarios, we recommend considering the research objective, the assumptions about the data and these proposed desiderata, and then deciding on a metric. Furthermore, in settings where assumptions like random censoring do not hold, evaluation strategies should be adapted accordingly, rather than defaulting to the simple C-index. Lastly, we argue that researchers should consider designing new evaluation metrics, to address the specific task, for when that task does not follow the commonly used assumptions.

Survival analysis

2.1
Notation

We consider a right-censored survival dataset with
N
time-to-event triplets,
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},t_{i},\delta_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
x_{i}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
represents the observed
d
-dimensional features for the
i
-th subject,
t_{i}\in\mathbbm{R}_{+}
denotes the observed time, and
\delta_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is an event indicator where
\delta_{i}=0
means the event has not happened by
t_{i}
(right-censored) and
\delta_{i}=1
means the event occurred at
t_{i}
(uncensored).
We consider each subject to have a (potential) event time
e_{i}
and a right-censoring time
c_{i}
, where we observe only the earlier of the two. That is, we define the observed time and event indicator as:

\displaystyle t_{i}

\displaystyle:=\min\{e_{i},c_{i}\},\quad\text{and}\quad\delta_{i}:=\mathbbm{1}[e_{i}\leq c_{i}]

where
\mathbbm{1}[\cdot]
represents the indicator function.

Let
T_{E}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
be the nonnegative random variable (R.V.) representing the event time,
T_{C}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
be the R.V. representing the censoring time, and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the R.V. for features. In this paper, we focus on a class of survival models that learn to compute an individual’s survival distribution (ISD)
[
3
]
,
i.e.
, the survival probability
S(t)=\text{Pr}(T>t)
, which is the probability that an individual is still alive at each future time point
t\geq 0
. Given an ISD, we can (1) compute time-to-event estimates using the median survival time (
i.e.
, the time when
S(t)=0.5
); (2) obtain single-time probabilities of the event at a specific time point (
e.g.
, 1-year cancer onset probability); and (3) calculate an individual’s predicted risk score from the curve – for instance, by setting the risk to be the negative of the median survival time.
Chen 2024
,
Wang et al. 2019
,
Wiegrebe et al. 2024
and
[
12
]
provide comprehensive reviews of state-of-the-art survival analysis models and evaluation metrics.

2.2
Types of censoring

Censoring is a cornerstone concept in survival analysis. Similar to how theory on missing data classifies missingness mechanisms into Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), Missing at Random (MAR), and Missing Not at Random (MNAR)
[
13
]
, survival analysis categorizes censoring mechanisms based on the relationship between the (partially observed) event time
T_{E}
and the censoring time
T_{C}
. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial because they impact the validity of survival estimates. Here, we focus on
right-censoring
, where the censored time is a lower bound of the true time to the event. There are the three different assumptions about this censoring in survival analysis.

Definition 2.1

(
Random Censoring
)
.

We assume that individuals censored at time
t
are representative of all individuals still at risk at that time, in terms of their survival experience
[
14
, Ch. 1]
. Formally,
T_{E}\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp T_{C}
, where
T_{E}
is the event distribution and
T_{C}
is the censoring distribution. In other words, we assume that the death rate (or failure rate) of censored subjects is the same as that of uncensored subjects who remain in the risk set at the time of censoring.

Definition 2.2

(
Independent Censoring
)
.

In the absence of competing risks (
i.e.
, when an alternative event prevents the main event), we assume that individuals within any subgroup of interest who are censored at time
t
are representative of all subjects in that subgroup who remained at risk at time
t
with respect to their survival experience
[
14
, Ch. 1]
. In other words, censoring is independent provided that it is
random
within any subgroup
given the covariates
. This is also known as the conditional independent assumption, since we then assume that the covariates account for the differences in the censoring times. That is, once we condition on the covariates, the censoring time is assumed to be independent of the event time. Formally, we have
T_{E}\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp T_{C}\mid X
, where
X
are the covariates. The idea behind random and independent censoring is that, even though the censored subjects were not randomly selected, their survival experience would be expected to be the same as if they had been randomly selected from the risk set at time
t
. Random censoring is a stronger assumption and more restrictive than independent censoring.

Definition 2.3

(
Dependent Censoring
)
.

In the absence of covariates, individuals within any subgroup of interest who are censored at time
t
are censored for reasons related to the event of interest. Therefore, they are not representative of all subjects in that subgroup who remained at risk at time
t
. Such censoring is referred to as
dependent censoring
because the censoring mechanism depends on the event process itself. This is problematic in small datasets or simplified analyses, when the learned model does not include some confounding covariates.

In practice, it is typically impossible to verify if censoring is independent or dependent because we only observe one outcome (either event or censoring) per instance, but not both
[
15
]
.

3
Why Evaluation Matters

In general, the recent survival literature does not carefully consider the measure used to evaluate survival models nor learning algorithms. Instead, most simply apply one of the common, off-the-shelf metrics, such as Harrell’s C-index and the Brier score, without justification. Although the C-index can be meaningful for specific applications –
e.g.
, who should receive the next liver on a waiting list
[
16
]
, or who should be prioritized for a fall-prevention program due to limited resources
[
17
]
– it is often used as the standard metric outside of contexts that specifically require concordance-based relative rankings. In order to facilitate a structured evaluation approach of survival models, the metrics we use should be selected based on some objective criteria (or desideratum) for what we actually want to measure.

3.1
Metric Requirements

Let us propose five specific desiderata for evaluating survival models. These are not strictly necessary, but desirable properties for evaluating a model on a wide range of downstream tasks. Based on these desiderata, we will discuss and juxtapose the advantages and disadvantages of common survival metrics and help practitioners pick the metric(s) that are relevant for their task.

D1

Be a proper scoring rule.
A proper scoring rule is defined as a scoring function (or loss) that is minimized (or maximized, depending on convention) exactly when the predicted probabilities match the true underlying probability distribution (see Appendix
B
for a formal definition). A metric should be a proper scoring rule because it rewards truthful predictions –
i.e.
, models that better reflect the underlying data generating process should receive better scores.

D2

Be interpretable by practitioners.
A metric should have an intuitive meaning and be easily interpretable by practitioners or researchers. When users can clearly understand what the evaluation metric measures and how it reflects model behavior, they are better equipped to assess the model’s strengths and weaknesses. Also, metrics that are easily interpretable are easier to communicate to broader audiences, including nontechnical stakeholders.

D3

Be model-agnostic.
A metric should be independent of any model intervals (
i.e.
, parameters) or information from the covariate distribution
P(X)
. This increases its comparability and robustness: we cannot fairly compare two models if the metric we are using depends on their specific internal choices or on quirks of
P(X)
. Moreover, if we change the data slightly (say, sample a different population), the metric could shift for better or worse even if the model’s predictive ability has not. We seek a metric that is applicable to any survival model that can produce an individual survival distribution.

D4

Be sensitive to miscalibration.
A metric should be sensitive to inaccurate survival probabilities within the study period. This is useful for detecting whether a model consistently overestimates or underestimates survival across the event horizon. If a model is miscalibrated, then it means that its predicted probabilities do not reflect reality. For example, a model might say that a patient has a 70% chance of surviving next year, but only 50% of such patients actually do. Even if the model ranks patients correctly, faulty probabilities can be harmful.

D5

Be robust to censoring.
A metric should support censoring in a manner consistent with its occurrence in the data. If our analysis suggests that censoring is indeed random (Definition
2.1
), and censored individuals are equally likely to experience the event, the metric should reflect this observation. Conversely, if our analysis suggests that censoring is dependent (Definition
2.3
), the metric should relax the random censoring assumption.

3.2
Benchmarking Survival Models

The many aspects of evaluating survival models pose the question: even if we have built a supposedly sound model, how do we benchmark it and deem it successful? Although numerous survival models have been proposed over the years, there is still a lack of comprehensive and standardized evaluation toolkits. In contrast to classification and regression tasks – where evaluation methods are well-developed and widely supported – survival analysis still suffers from limited tooling, with only a few Python libraries available for model assessment. See Appendix
C
for a selection of libraries.

Discrimination.
These metrics assess how well a model ranks subjects according to their risk of experiencing the event. Note that this is orthogonal to assessing the accuracy of the actual risk scores. Discrimination is particularly relevant when the main goal is to prioritize high-risk individuals over low-risk individuals. As evident in Figure
1
, the most widely used metric for evaluating discriminative performance is Harrell’s C-index
[
18
]
. It measures the proportion of
concordant
pairs among all
comparable
pairs. A pair
(i,j)
is deemed comparable if (1) subject
i
has an observed event time
t_{i}
that is strictly less than
t_{j}
, and (2)
i
experiences the event (
i.e.
, ,
\delta_{i}=1
). It is a positive scoring rule, meaning that a higher score implies better performance (
i.e.
, more pairs are ranked correctly). Harrell’s C-index can be biased under censoring, because the number of selected pairs do not represent
all possible pairs based on their true event times. To this end,
Uno et al. 2011
introduced a modified version that employs so-called inverse probability of censoring weights (IPCW). Uno’s C-index thus tries to account for censoring by not penalizing the model for being uncertain about censored individuals who might have survived longer or shorter. In this way, each pair is reweighted by the probability of being uncensored at the earlier subject’s event time.

None of the C-indices are proper scoring rules when predicting a
t
-year risk of an event, for example, 10-year mortality
[
20
]
. The C-indices are fairly interpretable in our view, as they indicate the probability that, for a randomly chosen pair, the patient with the highest predicted risk will experience the event first. They are partially model-agnostic, as they do not rely on any model internals, but do require that the model can predict individual
risk scores
, not just ISDs. The C-indices are not sensitive to miscalibration, as they assess only the relative ordering of risk predictions –
i.e.
, two models with a vastly different calibration quality but a similar ranking ability can give similar C-indices. Lastly, neither Harrell’s nor Antolini’s C-indices address the bias that comes from censoring, while Uno’s C-index uses IPCW to correct for it
[
19
]
. However, all of these variants are biased under dependent censoring.

Accuracy.
These metrics assess the accuracy (
i.e.
, the absolute/squared errors) of the time-to-event estimates or the predicted survival probabilities. They thus measure both discrimination and calibration aspects of the model. A popular such hybrid is the Brier score (BS)
[
21
]
, which measures the mean squared error between the predicted probability and the survival status of patient
i
at some time
t
. To incorporate censored instances, the Brier score requires estimating the IPCW weighting
[
22
]
to uniformly transfer a censored instance’s weight to instances with known status at that time. To measure the average error over all prediction times,
e.g.
, from the start to the end of a study, we can calculate the Integrated Brier score (IBS)
[
22
]
. Another useful hybrid is the mean absolute error (MAE), which is computed as the average absolute difference between the true event time and the predicted time. This can be done by excluding all censored instances from the evaluation, as in
Schemper 1990
, or using some de-censoring method –
e.g.
, MAE-Margin
[
3
]
or MAE-PO
[
24
]
. MAE-Margin computes the expected survival time beyond the censoring time using a renormalized Kaplan-Meier estimator, and MAE-PO uses pseudo-observations
[
25
]
. Both methods try to adjust for censoring rather than ignoring censored instances, but neither method guarantees strict consistency with the true MAE. It might seem intuitive to assume that MAE and C-index consistently favor models in the same direction –
i.e.
, if Model A has a lower MAE than Model B, then Model A should also have a higher C-index, and vice versa. However, this assumption does not always hold
[
24
]
. The reason lies in the different aspects of model performance these metrics evaluate: MAE assesses accuracy at the individual prediction level, while the C-index assesses concordance at the pairwise comparison level.

IBS is a proper scoring rule under random censoring
[
26
]
, and MAE is also proper when the predicted time is taken as the median from the conditional survival curve
[
24
]
. While IBS lacks an intuitive explanation without further analysis, MAE has a physical interpretation related to time units (
e.g.
, minutes, days, or months), making it the most interpretable metric of the seven in our opinion. Both IBS and MAE are model-agnostic, as they can be computed from ISDs alone. IBS is sensitive to miscalibration, as it penalizes inaccurate survival probabilities over time, while MAE is not, since it only compares point predictions. Whether IBS or MAE are robust to censoring depends on how censored instances are handled when computing the metric: If we use the Kaplan-Meier estimator to compute IPCW weights or survival times for censored instances, the corresponding metric is biased under dependent censoring
[
27
]
.

Calibration.
These metrics assess the agreement between the observed probabilities/frequencies and the expected probabilities/frequencies predicted by the model. 1-Calibration (or single-time calibration)
[
28
]
assesses if a model’s predicted survival probability at a specific time point matches the actual outcome. For example, if a model predicts that a patient has a 80% chance of surviving beyond
t=100
days, then among 100 similar patients, about 80 should survive past that time. D-Calibration
[
3
]
, on the other hand, checks whether the overall shape of a model’s predicted survival probabilities matches what we would expect if the predictions were perfectly accurate. In theory, if we could observe many possible lifespans for each patient, the predicted survival probability at each event time would follow a uniform distribution (evenly spread from 0 to 1). Since we only see one outcome per individual in real life, we instead collect the predicted survival probabilities at each person’s actual event time and check if those values are uniformly distributed. The output of 1-Calibration (or D-Calibration) is a
p
-value, which we can use to assess the level of calibration using, for example, a chi-squared test with some significance level
\alpha
.

D-Calibration is not a proper scoring rule, since it estimates a
p
-value, which is a hypothesis testing measure, not a scoring metric that rewards or penalizes predictions continuously. It is not easily interpretable, as we cannot interpret it directly, but have to use a null hypothesis test to determine if the survival curves are calibrated or not. D-Calibration is, however, model-agnostic, since it only requires the ISDs. Importantly, it is sensitive to miscalibration, making it valuable for assessing whether a model’s survival predictions match the actual distribution of observations. Lastly, D-Calibration is not robust to dependent censoring.

5
Model-Metric Consistency

This section reviews the current state of model development and evaluation in survival analysis and introduces a guiding principle called the
“Ladder Hypothesis of Model–Metric Consistency”
(see also Figure
2
). In short, model validity and metric validity must
“stand on the same rung of the assumption ladder”
. If the rungs differ, neither the model’s reported performance nor the metric’s estimate can be regarded as reliable.

Ladder Hypothesis of Model-Metric Consistency

A model can be considered trustworthy only when it is evaluated with metrics whose own validity rests on the same set of statistical assumptions that underpins the model. Conversely, an evaluation metric is itself trustworthy only when any auxiliary models or estimators on which it relies satisfy those same assumptions.

In this section, we show that this principle is frequently overlooked. For example, models developed under an independent censoring assumption are often validated with metrics designed for random censoring. Such mismatches arise for several reasons: (1) authors may be unaware of a metric’s limitations and underlying assumptions, or (2) they may recognize those limitations but adhere to convention because there is no widely-accepted alternative.

Figure 2:
Ladder Hypothesis of Model-Metric Consistency:

The double-helix ladder shows the current development of survival metrics, where we divide the ladders into three areas/assumptions (ranked from easy to hard): random censoring, independent censoring, and dependent censoring.
The development of models has started to solve the dependent censoring, while the development of metrics has not completely solved independent censoring yet.

5.1
Model-Metric Consistency under Random Censoring

Random censoring (Definition
2.1
) is the first and most restrictive censoring assumption, and it is rarely satisfied in contemporary applications. Despite this limitation, almost all classical conditional survival algorithms (
i.e.
, methods that predict an individual outcome given covariates
x
) were derived under random censoring, including the Cox Proportional Hazards model
[
29
]
, Accelerated Failure Time (AFT) models
[
30
]
, and Random Survival Forests
[
31
]
, etc. Evaluation practices evolved in parallel. Many popular metrics – such as Harrell’s C-index and the time-dependent Brier score – were also introduced under the random-censoring framework. When the first models were developed, these metrics had not yet been proposed; researchers instead relied on calibration-style checks, for example, comparing average predictions with the Kaplan–Meier estimator. Since the 1980s, the advent of the C-index and other discrimination-focused criteria has led researchers to validate models built under random censoring with metrics that make the same assumption (C-index, Brier score, MAE/MSE)
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
. Consequently, these models and their evaluation procedures remain
model–metric consistent
.

5.2
Model-Metric Consistency under Independent Censoring

The previous random censoring assumption is rarely tenable in practice.
More often, the censoring time
T_{C}
is allowed to depend on the covariates
x
but must remain
conditionally
independent of the event time
T_{E}
once
x
is known (Definition
2.2
):
T_{E}\perp T_{C}\mid x
.
This weaker condition – variously called
independent
,
conditional independent
, or
informative
censoring – is now the default assumption in modern survival analysis.

When the independence holds only
given

x
, naively treating data as randomly censored inflates or attenuates risk estimates, a phenomenon we refer to as
censoring bias
.
Intuitively, patients who leave follow-up early may differ systematically (
e.g.
, younger, healthier, wealthier) from those who remain, skewing both model fitting and evaluation.
Ignoring this bias can yield biased estimations and misleading clinical conclusions. To mitigate censoring bias, recent methods
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
explicitly model the censoring mechanism or re-weight observations using the censoring distribution.
These techniques exploit the additional information in
x
to correct the conditional distribution of
T_{E}
.

On the other hand, many popular metrics are valid
only
under random censoring.
For example, the naive Harrell’s C-index and the plain time-dependent Brier score implicitly assume
T_{C}\perp T_{E}
. To ensure unbiased results under independent censoring, researchers have introduced weighted variants (see Section
4
) to adjust for the censoring bias. These are unbiased if the IPCW weights are correctly estimated. Unfortunately, most papers still report
unweighted
C-index or Brier scores (see Figure
1
); the resulting model–metric mismatch reintroduces the very same bias those models aim to eliminate. To the best of our knowledge, estimating the IPCW weights under the independent censoring assumption is not trivial – because it also suffers from event bias. No models (without extra assumptions) guarantee consistent IPCW estimation under independent censoring. Therefore, these models and their evaluation procedures are not
model–metric consistent
under such an assumption.

5.3
Model-Metric Consistency under Dependent Censoring

When the censoring time
T_{C}
depends on the event time
T_{E}
even after conditioning on the covariates
x
, the data are said to be
dependently
censored (Definition
2.3
). This is the most challenging setting in survival analysis because the joint law of
(T_{E},T_{C})
is no longer identifiable from the observed triplets
(\min{T_{E},T_{C}},\delta,x)
without further structural assumptions
[
15
]
. Dependent censoring arises, for example, when censoring and event have a common unobserved confounder. To address this, recent methods model the joint distribution of
(T_{E},T_{C})
through copulas
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
– link functions that encode cross-dependence while maintaining separate marginal fits – or using proximal causal inference
[
45
]
ideas to identify dependency under unmeasured confounding
[
46
]
.

Unfortunately, virtually all commonly-used performance measures (Harrell’s C-index, Uno’s C-index, Brier score, MAE, D-Calibration) assume at least independent censoring. Under dependency assumption, they become biased, breaking model–metric consistency. A very recent proposal replaces the Kaplan-Meier estimator inside these metrics with a
copula graphical estimator
that jointly models
(T_{E},T_{C})

[
47
]
. While empirically promising, this approach (i) requires specifying the correct copula family – a strong and unverifiable assumption – and (ii) is supported only by simulation evidence; formal proofs of consistency or asymptotic normality are still lacking. Because there are no broadly accepted assumption-aligned metrics, most papers tackling dependent censoring report results on synthetic data where the ground-truth is known. Worryingly, some studies (
e.g.
,
[
43
]
and
[
44
]
) nonetheless declare state-of-the-art performances on real datasets using
random-censoring
metrics such as Antonili’s C-index, IBS, or D-Calibration. Such scores are not just noisy – they are
systematically
biased, so any superiority claim is unfounded. The apparent advance is an artifact of a model–metric mismatch.

In summary, survival analysis lacks broadly-accepted metrics that remain unbiased under dependent censoring. Until such tools are developed and validated, performance claims for state-of-the-art models in this setting are, at best, speculative. Our central message is therefore a plea for recalibration: researchers must scrutinize the assumptions behind the metrics they report, and the community should prioritize creating
assumption-aligned
evaluation procedures before proclaiming new modeling breakthroughs. In short, rigorous measurement – not ever-more complex models – should be the next frontier for progress under dependent censoring.

6
When the Metric Does Not Match the Goal

In this section, we present practical examples in which the evaluation approach does not fully capture what the authors are trying to predict, potentially leading to a mismatch between metric and goal.

Steinberg et al. 2023
presented a foundation model called
MOTOR
, designed to predict time-to-event outcomes based on 55M patient records. The authors focused on accurately predicting the time to a specific task – such as when a particular diagnosis code is assigned to a patient – but never evaluated how accurate these predictions were. Instead, two of their four evaluation metrics were C-indices (Harrell’s and Heagerty’s), which only assess the model’s ability to rank patients by risk. However, their overall objective was to predict the time until a specific event occurs, which requires evaluation metrics that assess the accuracy of the model’s time-to-event predictions, not just its discrimination ability.

Bone-Winkel and Reichenbach 2024
applied survival models to credit risk assessment based on a large loan data set, comprising more than 350,000 records. The authors rightly (
i.e.
, in line with their goal) used the C-index to assess the concordance of their risk predictions. They also plotted the survival curves for different risk groups, but did not empirically evaluate how accurate or calibrated these curves were. Without such checks, it is hard to tell if the survival curves are an accurate representation of the event probability over the span of the study period.

Zisser and Aran 2024
presented a Transformer-based deep survival model to predict patient-specific survival probabilities for acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Although their goal was to make accurate survival predictions, they only evaluated their model using Harrell’s C-index. This means that there was a mismatch between what their objective was and the evaluation. That is, they wanted correct time-to-event predictions, but the C-index only told them how good their model was at discriminating patients. Because of this, it is hard to know if the model’s predictions can be trusted for actual time-to-event predictions, even though it has a high C-index.

7
How to Evaluate Your Survival Model

We now outline best practices for selecting the appropriate metrics. First and foremost, the choice of primary evaluation metric should align with the objective of the study. To ensure a well-informed decision, we recommend referring to the desiderata outlined in Section
3
and the different aspects of evaluation outlined in Section
4
to determine which category best matches the goals of the application.

For instance, if the objective is to rank patients by their severity to prioritize access to limited clinical resources (
e.g.
, organ transplants) based on survival probability at a specific time (
e.g.
, 5 years), a variant of the C-index is the most appropriate. On the other hand, if the goal is to estimate individual treatment effects by quantifying how a given intervention delays survival time, a metric based on MAE/MSE should be the primary choice. Similarly, if the focus is on resource allocation for a population-level intervention (
e.g.
, distributing masks or vaccines in preparation for an outbreak), a variant of the distribution metrics (
e.g.
, 1-Cal or D-Cal) would be the most relevant. Furthermore, no single metric is universally optimal or unbiased under all conditions.
With this in mind, when developing new algorithms, we recommend evaluating the models using a combination of metrics from all three key aspects: discrimination, accuracy, and calibration. Additionally, when working in settings where right-censoring or random censoring assumptions do not hold, practitioners should adapt the evaluation metrics accordingly rather than applying standard choices, such as the C-index, without further consideration.

8
Discussion

8.1
Alternative views

While we argue for moving beyond the concordance index as the primary evaluation metric, several valid perspectives support its continued use. Below, we present two alternative views.

Alternative View 1

There is no overreliance on concordance measures in the literature. The C-index is the primary metric for a reason. Figure
1
accurately describes how the evaluation metric matches the need. Many times, the C-index is the relevant metric, because the application is concerned with only making accurate risk scores, so there is no need for other metrics.

Response

We do not argue that the C-index is an invalid or inappropriate metric for survival analysis, nor that reporting it is inherently problematic. Rather, our concern is with the frequent overreliance on a narrow set of standard metrics – most notably the C-index – without sufficient consideration of whether these metrics align with the specific goals of the application. Based on our analysis (see Appendix
A
), concordance metrics are used 1.7× more frequently than metrics that assess prediction accuracy (
i.e.
, absolute or squared error), and 10× more frequently than metrics that assess calibration.

Alternative View 2

Since dependent censoring is generally unobservable
[
15
]
, attempts to correct for it through specialized metrics or weighting schemes (see Section
5
) are questionable. As we usually cannot tell if or how censoring is related to the event or other factors, trying to adjust for it often relies on assumptions that we cannot check.

Response

While it is true that dependent censoring is difficult to detect and correct for, ignoring it altogether may be even worse than attempting to correct for it to some degree
[
27
]
. As we see it, even if common censoring assumptions cannot be (fully) verified, incorporating methods that attempt to address potential dependent censoring – such as inverse probability weighting or sensitivity analyses – are valuable safeguards. We note that handling dependent censoring is an active area of research (see, for example,
[
42
,
44
,
47
]
).

8.2
Conclusion

We believe that most survival analysis and time-to-event models are incorrectly evaluated, but also that proper evaluation is within reach. In this position paper, we have outlined key desiderata specific to metrics for survival analysis and provided a guide for selecting the appropriate metric for different evaluation needs. Going forward, we hope some readers will reconsider their focus on solely achieving a high concordance index and instead adopt a broader, more comprehensive evaluation strategy.

Appendix B
Proper Scoring Rules

This definition is reproduced from
[
26
]
.

A scoring rule
\mathcal{S}
takes a distribution
S
over some set
\mathcal{Y}
and an observed value
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and returns a score
\mathcal{S}(S,y)
, assessing how well the model predicts the observed value. For a positive scoring rule, a higher score indicates a better model fit.

A scoring rule is called
proper
if the true distribution achieves the optimal expected score,
i.e.
, if

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim S}\mathcal{S}(S,y)\geq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim S}\mathcal{S}(\hat{S},y)

for all distributions
\hat{S}
.

In the context of survival regression, we call the scoring rule
\mathcal{S}
proper if for every true distribution
S
, every censoring distribution
C
, and for every covariate
x
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}_{T,C\mid X=x}\mathcal{S}\big(S(t\mid x),(Z,D)\big)\geq\mathbb{E}_{T,C\mid X=x}\mathcal{S}\big(\hat{S}(t\mid x),(Z,D)\big)

for every distribution
\hat{S}(t\mid x)
, where in the above expression
T\mid X=x\sim S(t\mid x)
.

By taking the expectation with respect to
X
in the above expression, we find that if
S
is a proper score, then also

\mathbb{E}_{T,C,X}\mathcal{S}\big(S(t\mid X),(Z,D)\big)\geq\mathbb{E}_{T,C,X}\mathcal{S}\big(\hat{S}(t\mid X),(Z,D)\big)

for every family of distributions
\hat{S}(t\mid x)
with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
