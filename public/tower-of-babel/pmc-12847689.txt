Pressure–temperature route from disordered BCC to a 2 × 2 × 2 B2 superstructure
Authors: Raimundas Sereika, Andrew D. Pope, Caleb M. Knight, Kallol Chakrabarty, Yogesh K. Vohra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The interplay between configurational entropy and the enthalpy of ordered structures governs phase stability in compositionally complex alloys. In the refractory alloy Re0.6(NbTiZrHf)0.4, this balance is particularly delicate: pressure stabilizes a disordered body-centred-cubic (bcc) solid solution over the ambient hexagonal Laves phase via a martensitic route. Using in situ laser heating with synchrotron X-ray diffraction in a diamond-anvil cell, we demonstrate that the metastable bcc phase can be controllably transformed into a large-scale 2 × 2 × 2 B2-type superstructure with primitive-cubic symmetry (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Pm\stackrel{-}{3}m$$\end{document}). This long-period ordered phase is crystallographically distinct from conventional B2 ordering in multicomponent alloys, establishing a pathway to achieve chemical ordering from pressure-stabilized solid solutions. More broadly, these findings demonstrate that combining compression with subsequent thermal activation can unlock recoverable three-dimensional superstructures, offering new opportunities to tailor strength, transport properties, and stability in compositionally complex alloys.

Introduction

High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have emerged as fertile ground for unconventional structure–property relationships, combining large configurational entropy with simple lattices to achieve unusual phase stability, high yield strength, and superconductivity under extremes1–5. The entropic stabilization of multicomponent solid solutions in high-symmetry lattices sets the stage for rich responses to pressure and temperature, including superconductivity that can persist to megabar pressures in prototypical HEA systems4,5. Within this context, rhenium-containing HEAs and related alloys are of particular interest because the heavy 5d element rhenium naturally favors hexagonal packing and contributes to enhanced mechanical hardness owing to its intrinsic properties6,7. In addition, superconductivity in these alloys can be tuned by composition (including the rhenium fraction), and current data are consistent with conventional, phonon-mediated pairing determining Tc8–10.

Recently, we showed that the rhenium-rich alloy Re0.6(NbTiZrHf)0.4 (Re0.6Nb0.1Ti0.1Zr0.1Hf0.1) exhibits exceptional nanoindentation hardness (~ 18.5 GPa) that exceeds all previously reported values for metallic medium- or high-entropy alloys, and even surpasses the hardness of elemental rhenium11–13. Under pressure it undergoes a first-order hexagonal-to-bcc transition accompanied by a ~ 6% volume collapse, and the bcc phase (A2, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Im\stackrel{-}{3}m$$\end{document}) is retained as a metastable phase upon decompression13. Together, these observations indicate that chemical disorder – coupled with pressure-driven electronic changes – can stabilize phases that are otherwise inaccessible. This opens a pressure-based pathway to phase engineering that complements conventional compositional tuning. However, the fact that the bcc phase remains in a metastable state upon pressure release points to large kinetic barriers and suppressed diffusion at room temperature. This raises the question of whether applying elevated temperature under pressure could unlock chemical ordering or drive the system into a different equilibrium structure. Laser heating in the diamond anvil cell (DAC) provides exactly this lever: at fixed pressure it activates diffusion on experimental timescales, allows access to the melt and controlled quench, and enables simultaneous X-ray tracking of ordering pathways. Therefore, in this study, we combine high pressure with in-situ double-sided laser heating and synchrotron X-ray diffraction to map the stability window of the disordered bcc phase and to probe temperature-induced ordering at high-pressure.

The hexagonal space group \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P{6}_{3}/mmc$$\end{document} can host either the simple two-atom hexagonal close-packed lattice (hcp; A3, hP2) or the 12-atom C14 Laves network (MgZn2-type, hP12). The Re-rich alloy Re0.56(NbTiZrHf)0.44 (Re0.56Nb0.11Ti0.11Zr0.11Hf0.11) was originally reported as a “hcp HEA” (indexed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P{6}_{3}/mmc$$\end{document} with a ≈ 5.255 Å and c ≈ 8.593 Å)9. Closer inspection of the refinement, however, reveals three inequivalent Wyckoff sites – 2a (0,0,0), 6 h (x,2x,1/4), and 4f (1/3,2/3,z) – with element-size-selective partitioning (Re/Nb enriched on 2a/6 h; Ti/Zr/Hf on 4f). Such site multiplicity and occupancy preferences are characteristic of the C14 Laves topology, indicating that these alloys adopt a hexagonal Laves-type framework rather than a simple one-site hcp solid solution. Consistent with this, our arc-melted Re0.6(NbTiZrHf)0.4 also crystallizes as a single-phase hexagonal Laves-type structure at ambient conditions.

These medium entropy values, combined with strong enthalpic size/electronic preferences characteristic of Re-containing systems, favor topologically close-packed Laves order at ambient pressure. However, compression induces a transformation to a bcc phase that is retained in a metastable state on decompression, yielding a chemically disordered solid solution. This behavior suggests that Re0.6(NbTiZrHf)0.4 is situated at an entropy–enthalpy threshold. At ambient pressure it behaves like a compositionally complex intermetallic (enthalpy-dominated), whereas under high pressure it crosses into an entropy-stabilized bcc regime. This underscores the pivotal role of its intermediate configurational entropy in stabilizing the bcc phase. Under compression, the enthalpic penalty associated with the lower packing efficiency of the bcc lattice relative to the Laves phase is reduced, thereby stabilizing a disordered bcc solid solution through configurational entropy. Subsequent laser heating primarily activates diffusion, enabling the system to further reduce its free energy through chemical ordering on the bcc framework, where enthalpic gains from preferential atomic arrangements outweigh the accompanying loss of configurational entropy.

To explore the stability limits of this pressure-induced bcc phase, we extended compression beyond the previously studied 70 GPa range. At room temperature, Re0.6(NbTiZrHf)0.4 undergoes a single, well-defined phase transition from the ambient hexagonal Laves phase to a bcc phase (Fig. 1). The transformation is accompanied by a substantial volume collapse of ΔV/V ≈ 5.9%, consistent with its first-order character as reported previously13. At ambient conditions, XRD patterns display the characteristic reflections of the hexagonal phase (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P{6}_{3}/mmc$$\end{document}), whereas during transition they reduce to the simplified peak set expected for the disordered bcc solid solution (A2, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Im\stackrel{-}{3}m$$\end{document}) (Fig. 1a). Fitting the pressure–volume data with a third-order Birch–Murnaghan equation-of-state (EoS) yields bulk moduli of K0 = 279 ± 2 GPa (pressure derivative K′0 = 5.9) for the hexagonal phase and K0 = 286 ± 5 GPa (K′0 = 4.0) for the bcc phase. Both phases are thus extraordinarily incompressible, consistent with the refractory nature of their constituent elements. Once formed, the bcc phase remains stable with no further changes up to the maximum pressure tested (300 GPa), corresponding to V/V0 ≈ 0.65 (using V0 from the ambient-pressure metastable bcc), demonstrating robustness over > 200 GPa (Fig. 1b). Moreover, the bcc polymorph can be quenched back to ambient pressure, highlighting the diffusionless nature of the transition and confirming its metastable character.

To assess the thermal stability of the pressure-induced bcc phase, we employed double-sided laser heating in a DAC coupled with in-situ synchrotron XRD14–16. In this configuration, pulsed heating is synchronized with simultaneous temperature and diffraction measurements (Fig. 2). Splitting the laser beam through opposing diamond anvils ensures more uniform heating and minimizes thermal gradients across the sample. Experiments were initiated at 23 GPa, a pressure that preserves the metastable bcc structure after quench while accommodating thermal expansion during heating. Stepwise heating was then carried out to probe temperature-induced structural evolution. From room temperature up to ~ 1800 K, diffraction patterns remain consistent with a disordered bcc solid solution (A2), showing only the bcc Bragg peaks (110, 200, 211, 220, 310; marked by vertical tick marks in Fig. 3a), which form smooth continuous rings in the 2D image (Fig. 3b). Upon further heating to ~ 2300 K, additional weak reflections appear at low 2θ while the positions of the bcc fundamentals are unchanged. These new peaks cannot be indexed within the A2 cell and correspond to the first superlattice reflections expected for a primitive-cubic B2-type ordering (e.g., 100 and 111, see Fig. 4a inset), indicating the onset of chemical ordering on the existing bcc framework rather than the formation of a new Bravais lattice. At temperatures exceeding ~ 3000 K, melting occurs, followed by recrystallization upon quench. The resulting diffraction pattern (Fig. 3a, c) displays sharp superlattice peaks of much higher intensity at these positions and additional higher-angle reflections, all of which confirm the formation of a long-range ordered superstructure distinct from the previous disordered bcc solution. This transformation is irreversible upon quench and is accompanied by a modest pressure drop of ~ 1–1.5 GPa, consistent with a reconstructive melt–recrystallization pathway.

Initially, at P = 23 GPa and room temperature, the disordered bcc subcell refines to a = 3.125 Å. After melting above ~ 3000 K and quenching, the diffraction pattern indexes unambiguously to a primitive cubic, CsCl/B2-type ordered phase (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Pm\stackrel{-}{3}m$$\end{document}) with a refined lattice parameter a = 6.640 Å. Upon decompression to P = 2.4 GPa, this ordered phase expands to a = 6.745 Å (Fig. 4a), and its superlattice reflections remain strong – confirming the preservation of long-range order and the metastable retention of this phase upon release. The presence of a characteristic low-angle 100 superlattice peak – absent in any disordered A2 bcc and forbidden in face-centered lattices – together with other odd-index reflections (e.g., 111, 210), confirms that the ordered phase has a primitive cubic lattice. These observations rule out any F-centered (fcc-type) superstructures such as L12, L21 Heusler, or C1b (half-Heusler), as well as any hexagonal or trigonal ordered variants. Even-index reflections of the ordered phase coincide with the bcc Bragg peaks according to an approximate 2 × 2 × 2 relation. The ordered cell thus has an approximately doubled repeat relative to the bcc metric at the same pressure (aordered ≈ 2 abcc), consistent with long-range A/B sublattice ordering on a B2-type framework. The crystallographic mechanism is a group–subgroup reduction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Im\stackrel{-}{3}m$$\end{document} → \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Pm\stackrel{-}{3}m$$\end{document} that removes the body-centering translation17–19. In the parent bcc lattice a single site (2a) hosts a random solid solution. Ordering splits this site into two inequivalent sites, A = 1a (0,0,0) and B = 1b (1/2,1/2,1/2), each adopting a different average composition (Fig. 4b). Based on contrasts in metallic radius, electronegativity, and valence-electron concentration, Re preferentially occupies the A sublattice, while Nb, Ti, Zr, and Hf populate the complementary B sublattice; this A/B partitioning maximizes unlike-atom bonding and accords with B2-type ordering trends in refractory alloys. Although we assign Re to the 1a site and Nb/Ti/Zr/Hf to 1b, we note that quantitative site occupancies cannot be extracted from DAC XRD data due to limited peak multiplicity, spotty Debye–Scherrer rings, and the similar scattering factors of several constituents. The proposed partitioning therefore reflects the combination of (i) chemical size and electronic contrasts between Re and the group-IV elements, and (ii) the high intensity of odd-index superlattice peaks, which requires a strong scattering contrast between the two sublattices. These indirect indicators are widely used in HEA/MEA ordering studies and provide a robust qualitative basis for our site assignment.

In reciprocal space, the symmetry lowering is described by condensation of a commensurate zone-boundary concentration wave with propagation vector k = (1/2,1/2,1/2), which breaks I-centering and produces the odd-sum superlattice reflections characteristic of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Pm\stackrel{-}{3}m$$\end{document}. Geometrically, the ordered structure is two interpenetrating simple-cubic sublattices with alternating average chemistry (CsCl/B2 motif). Because the order parameter couples linearly to strain, the translational repeat is not an exact geometric doubling: minimization of the free energy G with respect to strain ε slightly shifts the cell metric, so the supercell edge becomes ≈ 2a(1 + Δε) ≈ 2.1a. Thus, the ~ 2.1× metric reflects a strain-stabilized ordering rather than a perfect geometric doubling. This non-ideal metric quantitatively explains the observed diffraction pattern. At 23 GPa, the ratio squared is (aordered/abcc)2 ≈ 4.52. Consequently, each bcc Bragg peak aligns with the nearest allowed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Pm\stackrel{-}{3}m$$\end{document} reflection whose index-squared sum (h2 + k2 + l2) is ≈ 4.52 times that of the parent: 110bcc → {300}/{221}, 200bcc → {330}/{411}, 211bcc → {333}/{511}, and 220bcc → {600}/{442}. This correspondence fully accounts for the small peak offsets in Figs. 3 and 4 and confirms the strain-stabilized nature of the primitive B2-type superstructure.

Recent work on Fe–Si at Earth’s core pressures shows that pronounced short-range order can stabilize a re-entrant bcc/B2-like regime, where pressure and non-ideal configurational statistics shift phase selection, yielding a bcc structure with B2-like local chemical correlations20. In contrast, the primitive-cubic 2 × 2 × 2 B2-type superstructure observed here in a Re-rich MEA Re0.6(NbTiZrHf)0.4 constitutes long-range chemical ordering in a multicomponent bcc derivative. Unlike the chemical medium-range order reported for Al9.5CrCoNi, where ordering occurs over ~ 1–5 nm without a well-defined lattice periodicity21, our phase exhibits true long-range translational order. This three-dimensional, volumetric ordering also differs from established ordering motifs in HEA/MEA families, including conventional 1 × 1 × 1 B2 ordering in systems such as AlCoCrFeNi22–25, and cubic F-centered derivatives such as the D03 structure (A3B prototype Fe3Al, space group \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Fm\stackrel{-}{3}m$$\end{document}) and the L21 full-Heusler alloy (X2YZ prototype, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Fm\stackrel{-}{3}m$$\end{document})2,25. By comparison, ordered hexagonal ω/ω′ structures reported in some refractory HEAs/MEAs arise under conventional heat treatments at ambient pressure and represent layered or polytypic transformations involving stacking variations or displacive distortions along one axis, typically preserving in-plane symmetry26. The ordered phase in Re0.6(NbTiZrHf)0.4 is primitive-cubic (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Pm\stackrel{-}{3}m$$\end{document}), with a three-dimensional 2 × 2 × 2 translational repeat (an index-8 supercell) and explicit Wyckoff-site splitting (parent 2a → distinct 1a + 1b). This combination of a larger periodicity and lowered symmetry – accessed via the pressure-then-heat route – clearly distinguishes our phase from previously known ordering patterns in HEAs/MEAs. To our knowledge, no prior reports exist of a primitive-cubic 2 × 2 × 2 B2 superlattice in these systems. The pronounced ~ 2.1× lattice expansion of the supercell relative to the bcc parent provides direct evidence of strong order–strain coupling, demonstrating how extreme-condition processing can stabilize ordered structures beyond conventional synthesis routes. The hexagonal → bcc → B2 pathway documented here is likely composition-sensitive. The present alloy contains a high Re fraction, stabilizing the ambient C14 Laves phase and promoting the observed two-step transformation under pressure and temperature. Alloys with lower Re content or with bcc-stabilizing substitutions (e.g., Ta or V) frequently form bcc solutions at ambient conditions, suggesting that the initial hexagonal stage could be suppressed and that ordering may follow modified routes.

In summary, compression experiments on the Re0.6(NbTiZrHf)0.4 alloy establish that the pressure-induced bcc phase (A2, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Im\stackrel{-}{3}m$$\end{document}) is extremely stable: once formed, it persists without further transitions to at least 300 GPa and can be quenched to ambient conditions. Building on this platform, in situ double-sided laser heating at ~ 23 GPa reveals a temperature-driven route from the disordered bcc lattice to a chemically ordered, primitive-cubic 2 × 2 × 2 B2-type superstructure (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Pm\stackrel{-}{3}m$$\end{document}) that survives decompression. This ordering is accompanied by an expansion of the unit cell, arising from nearest-neighbour chemical differentiation that resolves local packing frustration and bond-length/electronic misfit, thereby reducing the mass density relative to the bcc parent at the same pressure. The pathway from the starting hexagonal phase to the final ordered superstructure involves two distinct transformation mechanisms: first, a martensitic hexagonal-to-bcc transformation, a diffusionless process that traps a chemically random bcc scaffold; second, a bcc-to-superstructure transition that proceeds by diffusion-controlled ordering, converting the random solution into a lower-enthalpy state. Overall, our pressure-then-heat strategy demonstrates that a chemically disordered, high-symmetry parent phase can serve as a template for “writing” long-period chemical order under extreme conditions. This approach offers practical means to tune properties such as mechanical strength, electronic transport, and superconductivity through deliberate superlattice design.

Methods

Fabrication of the alloy

Polycrystalline Re0.6(NbTiZrHf)0.4 (nominal atomic composition Re0.6Nb0.1Ti0.1Zr0.1Hf0.1) ingots were prepared by melting high-purity elemental metals (≥ 99.95%) in the prescribed atomic ratios under an Ar atmosphere. The melting was carried out in the arc melter MAM-1 (Edmund Bühler GmbH, Bodelshausen, Germany). The obtained button was flipped and remelted several times to promote chemical homogeneity. After synthesis, laboratory XRD (Cu-Kα) was performed on a PANalytical Empyrean diffractometer to confirm formation of the hexagonal phase. The diffraction pattern indicated a single-phase structure consistent with the hexagonal symmetry of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P{6}_{3}/mmc$$\end{document} space group (No. 194) and the refined lattice parameters, a = b = 5.257(5) Å and c = 8.6148(5) Å.

Synchrotron X-ray diffraction measurements and data processing

High-pressure synchrotron X-ray diffraction (XRD) experiments were performed using a symmetric diamond anvil cell (DAC) equipped with beveled anvils (300 μm culets beveled at 8.5° to 50 μm central flats). A rhenium gasket was precompressed to a 30 μm thickness, and 20 μm hole was drilled to load the powdered sample together with a copper pressure marker. No pressure-transmitting medium was used. Pressures were determined from the Cu pressure marker using the equation of state of Sakai et al.27 Combining the calibration uncertainty of this EOS with fitting errors and possible pressure gradients in the sample chamber, we estimate an absolute pressure uncertainty of about 2–3% over the entire compression range (approximately ± 1–2 GPa at 60 GPa and ± 8–10 GPa at 300 GPa).

Angle-dispersive XRD measurements were conducted at beamline 16-ID-B of the Advanced Photon Source (APS) at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL). The incident monochromatic X-ray beam (29.2 keV, λ = 0.4246 Å) was focused to a 1.5 μm × 1.5 μm spot using 200 mm × 100 mm Kirkpatrick–Baez mirrors. The wavelength was calibrated against a CeO₂ standard. Two-dimensional diffraction images were collected with a Pilatus 2 M CdTe detector and azimuthally integrated into one-dimensional diffraction patterns using DIOPTAS28. Phase indexing and structural refinements were carried out using Jana202029. Pressure–volume data were fitted with a third-order Birch–Murnaghan equation of state using EoSFit30.

In situ high-pressure laser heating experiments

Laser heating (LH) with in situ XRD was carried out in a symmetric DAC equipped with 250 μm culet, standard-design diamonds. A rhenium gasket was precompressed to 40 μm, and a 120 μm hole was drilled to form the sample chamber. A ~ 20 μm-thick pellet of Re0.6(NbTiZrHf)0.4 was packed in KCl and loaded into the chamber (Fig. 1). Here, the KCl provided both insulation from the anvils and a quasi-hydrostatic medium during compression. The pressure was determined from the diffraction of KCl31 and cross-checked against a small Cu chip that was loaded adjacent to the sample. At ambient conditions Re0.6(NbTiZrHf)0.4 was hexagonal; the cell was first compressed at room temperature to ~ 60 GPa to induce the bcc phase and then decompressed to ~ 23 GPa for laser heating. The small X-ray beam size relative to the sample allowed several heating attempts at distinct spots, improving reproducibility.

All experiments were conducted at beamline 16-ID-B (APS, ANL) under the same monochromatic X-ray conditions described above. The X-ray and laser focal spots were co-aligned on the sample. The LH-DAC system supports double-sided heating with simultaneous temperature measurement from both sides14–16. Thermal emission from the upstream and downstream sample surfaces was imaged onto a pair of ~ 20 μm reflective pinholes at the entrance plane of a Czerny–Turner spectrometer, and emission from both sides was recorded concurrently on the imaging camera. Thermal-emission spectra were dark-subtracted and fitted to a Planck function within the grey-body approximation (emissivity as a wavelength-independent fit parameter). The instrument response was calibrated using a Pt ribbon melt at ambient pressure as a fixed-point reference. Reported temperatures represent the average of the two sides. The overall uncertainty of temperature determination is on the order of ± 100 K, combining fitting errors with side-to-side variations, consistent with prior LH-DAC studies32,33. During laser heating, pressures were obtained from the KCl and Cu Bragg peaks, which agreed within ~ 3%. Allowing for additional contributions from thermal pressure and modest temperature gradients, we estimate a total pressure uncertainty of ≤ 5% at 23 GPa (corresponding to roughly ± 1 GPa).
