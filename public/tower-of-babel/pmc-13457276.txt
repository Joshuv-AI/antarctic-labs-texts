A retrospective analysis of tuberculosis notification trends and spatial distribution in South Africa, 2005–2012
Authors: Dan Kibuuka, Charles Mpofu, Penny Neave, Samuel Manda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a major public health challenge in South Africa. Despite substantial policy and programme changes since 2012, including the GeneXpert rollout, antiretroviral therapy expansion, Universal Test and Treat, and tuberculosis preventive therapy, historical surveillance data remain essential for establishing baselines against which subsequent progress can be measured. This retrospective study analysed TB notification data from 2005-2012 to characterise demographic disparities and district- level spatial patterns preceding these major interventions.MethodsA retrospective analysis of 3,474,320 TB cases recorded in the Electronic TB Register (2005–2012) was conducted. The analysis was limited to the period for which complete national notification data were available. Annual notification rates were calculated by age, sex, province, and district. Trends were assessed using chi-square tests and linear regression. Poisson regression was used to assess the variation across provinces. Age-and sex-standardised rates were derived using the 2011 census population. Spatial autocorrelation and clustering were evaluated using Global and Local Moran's I and Getis-Ord Gi* statistics. KwaZulu-Natal (1,309.3 per 100,000) and North West (1,113.2 per 100,000) provinces recorded the highest rates. There was a statistically significant variation in TB notification rates across provinces (likelihood-ratio (omnibus) test (LR χ² = 442,769.6; df = 8; p < 0.001). The identification of high- burden districts and population subgroups provides a critical historical baseline for evaluating the impact of subsequent TB and HIV interventions. Districts identified as hotspots in this era warrant renewed attention to assess whether they have improved or whether entrenched vulnerabilities persist. By establishing a detailed baseline from the pre-GeneXpert, pre-Universal Test and Treat, and pre tuberculosis preventive therapy era, this study enables policymakers and programme managers to evaluate where progress has been made and where geographic and demographic disparities have proven resistant to intervention. The findings support evidence-based targeting of resources to populations and districts with a historically entrenched burden.Public health impactAlthough the data presented here are from 2005–2012, they provide a vital historical baseline for interpreting subsequent TB trends in South Africa. Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the leading infectious causes of morbidity and mortality globally, with an estimated 10.7 million people developing the disease and 1.23 million dying from it in 2024 [1]. South Africa continues to experience one of the highest TB burdens worldwide, with an incidence rate of 468 per 100,000 population in 2022 nearly eight times the global average. The intersecting Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) epidemic uniquely shapes the country’s TB epidemic, deep seated socioeconomic inequalities, and structural determinants including labour migration, mining, and crowded urban settlements [2–5].

Substantial investments in TB control over the past two decades have transformed the policy and programmatic landscape. The rollout of GeneXpert MTB/RIF from 2011 improved diagnostic sensitivity and turnaround time [6], and has since evolved into a mature, nationwide molecular diagnostic platform integrated within routine TB services, generating substantial health gains and improved case detection across South Africa [7]. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) coverage expanded from approximately 4.5 million people in 2017 to over 5.7 million by 2022, reducing HIV-associated TB risk [8] while the 2016 adoption of Universal Test and Treat (UTT) further accelerated ART initiation [9]. In the years following UTT, the programme has continued to evolve toward more rapid and integrated care models, including same-day ART initiation, which has been shown to improve retention in care and viral suppression in high-burden settings, and earlier initiation strategies that reduce TB incidence among people living with HIV [10]. In parallel, TB prevention has expanded through the scale-up of tuberculosis preventive therapy (TPT) and its integration within HIV care programmes, including delivery at or near the time of ART initiation [11]. Despite these advances, TB incidence declined only modestly between 2015 and 2022 (from 690 to 468 per 100,000), and South Africa remains among the WHO high-burden countries for TB, TB/HIV, and multidrug-resistant TB [12].

Understanding long-term epidemiological patterns requires examining periods preceding major policy shifts. Historical surveillance data provide essential baselines against which the impact of subsequent interventions can be measured [13, 14].

The period 2005–2012 represents a distinct epidemiological era in South Africa: it preceded widespread GeneXpert implementation, occurred as ART coverage expanded but remained suboptimal, and captured the peak of HIV-associated TB before treatment scale-up achieved population-level effects [15–17]. Analysing TB notification patterns from this period can therefore illuminate the structural determinants: poverty, mining, health system inequities, and spatial concentration of HIV, that change slowly and continue to shape TB epidemiology even as absolute rates decline [18, 19].

Spatial epidemiology offers a powerful lens for examining geographic clustering, transmission dynamics, and contextual risk factors that conventional analyses may miss [20, 21]. In South Africa, several studies have documented geographic variation in TB burden at provincial or city levels [14, 22–24], but national-scale spatial analyses covering the pre-GeneXpert rollout, pre-TPT and pre-UTT era remain limited. District-level clustering analyses are particularly valuable because they can identify persistent hotspots which are areas where TB burden remains concentrated despite national progress, and guide geographically targeted interventions [25, 26]. Moreover, spatial regression methods can help disentangle the relative contributions of HIV, poverty, and neighbourhood effects to observed geographic patterns [27].

This study makes three contributions to the literature on TB epidemiology in South Africa. First, it provides a comprehensive national-level description of TB notification trends from 2005 to 2012, disaggregated by age, sex, province, and district, offering a detailed picture of demographic disparities during this critical period. Despite the availability of national TB surveillance data for the pre-2012 period, few studies have comprehensively examined the subnational spatial and demographic distribution of the TB burden at scale during this epidemiological phase. Existing analyses have often been restricted to specific settings or subpopulations, such as provincial studies or drug-resistant TB cohorts [28, 29], or have focused on particular outcomes like mortality [30] or national trends [31] thus limiting the ability to characterise geographic heterogeneity and identify persistent structural patterns.

Second, it applies multiple spatial analytic techniques (Global and Local Moran’s I, Getis-Ord Gi hotspot analysis, and spatial lag regression) to identify statistically significant geographic clustering and to quantify spatial dependence. Third, and most importantly, it establishes a historical baseline against which subsequent changes, including the impacts of GeneXpert rollout, ART expansion, TPT, and UTT, can be evaluated. From 2011 onward, the introduction of GeneXpert and the rapid scale-up of ART substantially altered TB detection, survival, and transmission dynamics. Subsequent studies have shown that increasing ART coverage was associated with declines in TB incidence and changes in spatial clustering [32], while GeneXpert rollout influenced diagnostic yield, reporting patterns, and geographic heterogeneity [33]. However, interpretation of these changes depends critically on the availability of a well-characterised pre-intervention baseline, which remains limited, particularly at fine spatial resolution.

While the data are more than a decade old, the patterns they reveal reflect long-standing structural determinants that evolve slowly; identifying districts that were high-burden in 2005–2012 can help contemporary programme managers assess whether historical hotspots have improved or whether entrenched vulnerabilities persist.

Specifically, this study aimed to: (1) describe TB notification trends from 2005 to 2012 by age, sex, and province; (2) identify spatial patterns and statistically significant clusters of TB burden across district municipalities; and (3) assess associations between district-level TB notification rates, HIV prevalence, and poverty using spatial regression models.

Materials and methods

Study design

We carried out a retrospective cross-sectional study using TB data obtained from the South African National Department of Health. The data were for all the nine provinces of South Africa.

Study population and period

The study included all TB cases notified to the ETR between 1 January 2005 and 31 December 2012 (N = 3,474,320). These eight years were selected because they represent the era preceding major programmatic changes: widespread GeneXpert MTB/RIF implementation (beginning 2011–2012), substantial antiretroviral therapy scale-up to population-level coverage, and the 2016 adoption of UTT. No cases were excluded; the analysis includes all notified pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB cases (Drug-Susceptible TB and Drug-Resistant TB) across all age groups and both sexes. Prior to analysis, the dataset was cleaned to identify and remove duplicate records. Data completeness was assessed for key variables, including sex, age, province, district, sub-district, treatment initiation, and treatment outcome, all of which were complete for the study period. Variables from the notified TB data set with substantial missing information like the HIV data were analysed using a different data source.

Data sources

This study used secondary data from four sources. TB notification data were obtained from the Electronic TB Register (ETR) of the South African National Department of Health for the period 1 January 2005 to 31 December 2012. This is the period for which complete national notification data were available. The ETR is a national surveillance system that registers and tracks all individuals initiated on TB treatment in public health facilities. Captured information included demographic details (age, sex), health district, facility, patient category (new, retreatment), sputum results, HIV status, and HIV-related treatment data. Data were captured at the sub-district level and aggregated at the district and national levels.

Population denominators were derived from the 2011 national census and annual mid-year population estimates from Statistics South Africa [34]. District-level HIV prevalence estimates for 2010 were obtained from the National Department of Health’s antenatal care surveillance and modelled district-level estimates [35]. Socioeconomic data were drawn from the South African Multidimensional Poverty Index (SAMPI), constructed from the 2011 census to profile household-level poverty across districts [36].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive epidemiology

Annual TB notification rates per 100,000 population were calculated at national, provincial, and district municipality levels. Chi-square tests assessed associations between categorical variables (sex, age group, year), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Simple linear regression was used to assess temporal trends in annual notification rates over the eight years, age-and sex-standardisation. To enable comparison across districts with different demographic structures, age-and sex-standardised TB notification rates were derived using the direct standardisation method. A Poisson regression model with a log link function was used to assess the variation in TB notifications across provinces. Differences in TB notification rates across provinces were evaluated using the likelihood-ratio (omnibus) chi-square test derived from the Poisson regression.

The 2011 South African census population served as the standard population. Standardised rates were calculated for the overall population and separately for males and females. All descriptive and trend analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4m6 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and Microsoft Excel.

A queen-contiguity spatial weights matrix (first order) was used, defining neighbours as districts that share a boundary or a vertex. Statistical significance was assessed using 999 permutations.

Local clustering

Local Moran’s I (Local Indicators of Spatial Association, LISA) was used to identify local spatial clusters and outliers [38]. This statistic decomposes the global measure into contributions from each district, identifying four categories: high-high clusters (districts with high rates surrounded by high-rate neighbours), low-low clusters, high-low outliers, and low-high outliers. Hot spot analysis was conducted using the Getis-Ord Gi statistic, which identifies spatial concentrations of high (hot spots) or low (cold spots) values by comparing local sums to expected sums under spatial randomness [39]. Statistical significance was assessed at α = 0.05 with false discovery rate correction for multiple testing.

Ethics approval

Ethical approval was granted by the Auckland University of Technology Ethics Committee (AUTEC), reference 17/369, and the South African Medical Research Council Human Research Ethics Committee, reference EC006-5/2018. The study used fully anonymised secondary data; individual patient consent was not required.

Results

Descriptive epidemiology

The mean annual TB notification rate for South Africa from 2005 to 2012 was 885.2 per 100,000 population at 95% CI [772.0, 945.3]. Trend analysis showed no statistically significant increase in annual TB notification rates (F (1,6) = 3.22, p = 0.12).

Males accounted for 53.5% (1,864,417) of TB cases, while females accounted for 46.5% (1,609,903). The difference was statistically significant (χ² (1) = 15,711.95, p < 0.001). The mean annual TB notification rate was higher for males (974.6 per 100,000) at 95% CI [858.7, 1087.9] than females (800.2 per 100,000) at 95% CI [688.1, 906.7].

The highest mean annual notification rate among males occurred in the 35–44 age group (1,062.2 per 100,000), while among females it occurred in the 25–34 age group (735.5 per 100,000) (Fig. 1).

Pulmonary TB accounted for 83.8% (2,844,475) of cases. The annual increase in pulmonary TB notifications was not statistically significant (F (1,6) = 3.45, p = 0.11).

Provincial and district variation

The mean annual TB notification rate for provinces was 900.9 per 100 000 population at 95% CI [681.6, 1120.2]. KwaZulu-Natal had the highest provincial rate (1,309.3 per 100,000), followed by North West (1,113.2 per 100,000). Limpopo had the lowest (489.0 per 100,000) (Fig. 2).

The Poisson regression model demonstrated a statistically significant variation in TB notification rates across provinces, as indicated by the likelihood-ratio (omnibus) test (LR χ² = 442,769.6; df = 8; p < 0.001).

The mean annual TB notification rate for district municipalities was 984.0 per 100,000 at 95% CI [876.9, 1,091.0]. Dr Kenneth Kaunda (North West) had the highest mean annual rate (1,927.5 per 100,000), followed by Siyanda (Northern Cape) (1,651.0 per 100,000). John Taolo Gaetsewe (Northern Cape) had the lowest (318.4 per 100,000) as provided in ESM1.

Spatial autocorrelation

The Global Moran’s I analysis demonstrated strong and statistically significant spatial autocorrelation in the age-sex standardised TB notification rates across South African district municipalities in 2010. All three models; overall, male, and female the spatial autoregressive coefficient (ρ) was positive, large in magnitude, and highly significant (p < 0.001).

The overall Moran’s I value of 0.556 reflects a moderate to strong degree of global spatial clustering, suggesting that districts with high TB notification rates were geographically concentrated near other high-burden districts, while low-burden districts tended to cluster together.

The male and female analyses showed similar patterns, with Moran’s I values of 0.559 and 0.446, respectively. The slightly higher value for males suggests that spatial clustering of TB burden was more pronounced among men, which aligns with the higher overall TB notification rates observed in males.

The significant Z-scores (ranging from 5.23 to 7.00) confirm that the observed clustering is highly unlikely to be due to chance (Table 1).

Local spatial clustering

The Local Moran’s I analysis revealed pronounced spatial heterogeneity in the distribution of the overall age-sex standardised TB notification rates across South African district municipalities in 2010. A total of seven high-high clusters were identified, all located in the eastern part of the country, in KwaZulu-Natal province (Fig. 3). Districts within these clusters exhibited significantly elevated TB notification rates and were surrounded by neighbouring districts with similarly high rates, indicating geographically concentrated pockets of elevated TB burden.

Local Moran’s I Clustering of the Overall (left), male (centre) and female (right) Age Sex Standardised Tuberculosis Notification Rates by District Municipality, 2010

In contrast, ten low-low clusters were identified in the northern regions, particularly in Gauteng, Limpopo, and Mpumalanga. These districts had consistently low TB notification rates and were surrounded by districts with similarly low rates. The presence of low-low clusters suggests geographic zones of comparatively lower TB transmission.

Additionally, the analysis identified two low-high outlier districts, Thabo Mofutsanyane (Free State) and Alfred Nzo (Eastern Cape). These are districts with low TB rates surrounded by high-burden neighbours. Further, there were two high-low outlier districts, Ehlanzeni (Mpumalanga) and Dr Kenneth Kaunda (North West), where high TB rates were observed despite being surrounded by lower-burden districts.

Furthermore, 31 districts did not exhibit statistically significant local spatial autocorrelation, therefore showing no detectable spatial clustering relative to their neighbouring districts.

Overall, the Local Moran’s I results demonstrate that TB burden in South Africa exhibits strong localised clustering, with clear geographic distinctions between high-burden and low-burden zones.

Hot spot analysis

The Getis-Ord Gi* hot spot analysis further confirmed the presence of statistically significant spatial concentrations of the TB burden. For the overall age-sex standardised TB notification rates, nine hot spot districts were identified, all located in the eastern seaboard of South Africa (Fig. 4). These hot spots overlapped substantially with the seven high-high clusters identified by Local Moran’s I with an additional two high hot spot districts being Thabo Mofutsanyane (Free State) and Alfred Nzo (Eastern Cape).

Conversely, eleven cold spot districts were identified, primarily in the northern interior of the country. These districts consistently exhibited significantly lower TB notification rates than expected under spatial randomness.

Spatial regression analysis

The spatial lag regression model demonstrated a strong and statistically significant spatial dependence in TB notification rates across South African districts in 2010. In all three models; overall, male, and female, the spatial autoregressive coefficient (ρ) was positive, large in magnitude, and highly significant (p < 0.001). This high spatial lag coefficient means that TB rates in neighbouring areas tended to be more similar.

The spatial lag coefficients were 0.534 for the overall model, 0.619 for males, and 0.644 for females. These values indicate positive spatial dependence across all models, with stronger dependence observed in the sex-stratified models. In other words, TB notification rates in a given area were more strongly associated with rates in neighbouring areas when models were stratified by sex, with the largest coefficient observed in the female model, suggesting comparatively stronger spatial dependence among females.

Despite the strong spatial dependence, the associations between TB notification rates and the two district-level covariates, HIV prevalence and SAMPI poverty index were positive but not statistically significant in all models (Table 2).

Discussion

This retrospective analysis of 3.47 million TB notifications from 2005 to 2012 provides a comprehensive characterisation of demographic and geographic disparities in TB burden during a distinct epidemiological era in South Africa. The findings reveal persistently high notification rates (mean 885/100,000), marked male excess, concentration among adults aged 25–44 years, and pronounced geographic clustering in eastern districts. Although the data precede major programmatic changes including widespread GeneXpert implementation, expanded ART coverage, TPT, and UTT, the patterns identified reflect structural determinants that evolve slowly and continue to shape TB epidemiology today [3, 41, 42]. By establishing a detailed historical baseline, this study enables future evaluations of whether subsequent interventions have reduced disparities or whether entrenched vulnerabilities persist.

Regarding the demographic patterns in context, the consistently higher TB notification rates among males (974.6 vs. 800.2 per 100,000) align with global evidence showing that men bear a disproportionate burden of TB disease [43, 44]. Several factors likely contributed to this disparity during the study period. Men in South Africa, particularly those of working age, faced elevated occupational exposures including mining, construction, and transport work sectors with documented TB risk from silica dust, poor ventilation, and crowded living conditions [45–47]. Behavioural factors including higher smoking and alcohol use rates among men, have also been associated with increased TB risk [43]. Importantly, men traditionally delay care-seeking and have lower engagement with primary health services, potentially leading to more advanced disease at diagnosis and greater community transmission [48–50].

The highest burden among adults aged 25–44 years reflects the intersection of TB and HIV epidemics during this period [15, 16]. HIV prevalence in South Africa peaked in this same age group. Before widespread ART coverage, HIV infection increased TB risk 5–20 fold through immunosuppression and accelerated progression from latent to active disease [51, 52]. This age group also represents the economically active population, characterised by high mobility, labour migration, and crowded living conditions, all of the factors facilitating TB transmission [53, 54]. The sex-specific age patterns (peak at 35–44 for males, 25–34 for females) may reflect earlier HIV acquisition among women [55] and different occupational and social exposures [56]. However, the present study did not assess the underlying causes of the differences in sex-specific age patterns.

Concerning geographic disparities and spatial clustering, the pronounced geographic variation observed ranging from 489/100,000 in Limpopo to 1,309/100,000 in KwaZulu-Natal, with a sixfold variation at the district level, and the statistically significant variation of notifications across provinces (LR χ² = 442,769.6; df = 8; p < 0.001), is consistent with previous evidence showing uneven TB distribution across South Africa [24, 57]. KwaZulu-Natal’s highest provincial rate reflects its historically highest HIV prevalence, which exceeded 30% among antenatal clinic attendees during much of the study period [15, 35]. North West province’s second-highest rate likely reflects mining-related exposures: gold and platinum mining employ large numbers of migrant workers, with documented TB incidence rates 3–10 times higher than the general population [46, 58].

The spatial clustering analysis provides the first national-level evidence of statistically significant TB hotspots during this era. All seven high-high clusters identified by Local Moran’s I were located in eastern districts of KwaZulu-Natal, a region characterised by high HIV prevalence, substantial poverty, and mining activity [59]. The Getis-Ord Gi* analysis confirmed nine hotspot districts in the same eastern seaboard region. This spatial concentration suggests that TB transmission during 2005–2012 was influenced by localised factors operating at district and neighbourhood levels: population density, health system performance, socioeconomic deprivation, and mobility patterns [60].

The strong spatial dependence observed in the spatial lag regression models (ρ = 0.53–0.64) indicates that TB burden in any given district was significantly influenced by the burden in neighbouring districts. This finding aligns with the understanding that TB transmission is shaped by population movement, social networks, and shared environmental conditions that cross administrative boundaries [25, 27]. The slightly higher spatial dependence among males (ρ = 0.62) and females (ρ = 0.64) compared to overall (ρ = 0.53) may reflect sex-specific mobility patterns or occupational networks that create distinct spatial structures [43]. As previously highlighted, this study did not investigate the causes of these differences.

Within this interpretive context, it is important to recognise limitations of the ecological design used in this analysis. Ecological analyses use group-level rather than individual-level data and are subject to methodological limitations for causal inference. Aggregation leads to information loss and ecological bias, arising due to the inability to capture within-area variability in exposures and confounders [61]. Despite the practical advantages of ecological studies, they are subject to methodological limitations for example ecological and cross-level bias, limited control of confounding (particularly under nonlinear individual risk models), within-group misclassification, inadequate data, temporal ambiguity, collinearity, and population mobility [62]. Linked to these broader limitations, a key concern in interpreting our findings is the ecological fallacy, whereby inferences about individuals are drawn from group-level associations despite differences between aggregate and individual-level variability [63, 64]. Consequently, associations may differ in magnitude or direction across levels, and group-level relationships do not necessarily reflect individual-level effects [64, 65].

Within this context, our findings have a very impactful interpretation of HIV and poverty, in that the non-significant associations between district-level TB notification rates and HIV prevalence or poverty in the spatial lag models require careful interpretation. Several explanations should be considered. First, ecological fallacy is likely: district-level averages mask substantial within-district heterogeneity. HIV may be concentrated in specific communities or sub-districts that drive TB transmission, but this signal is diluted when aggregated to the district level. Similarly, poverty operates at household and community scales that may not align with administrative boundaries [66, 67].

Secondly, the strong spatial lag coefficient suggests that neighbourhood effects, spillover of TB risk across district boundaries, may capture much of the variation that would otherwise be attributed to HIV and poverty. In spatial regression, including a spatial lag term partitions variance between measured covariates and spatial proximity effects; where spatial dependence is strong, covariates may appear non-significant even if causally important [40].

Thirdly, unmeasured confounders likely play substantial roles.Possible confounders such as migration flows between districts, health system performance (treatment success rates, diagnostic delays), housing quality, and environmental factors such as ventilation and crowding are not captured in district-level HIV and poverty measures. This is evidenced in studies examining these factors that had to use special surveillance systems or community health worker data but not routine district-level measures [68, 69]. Mining, in particular, operates as a cross-district determinant: mine workers often reside in one district while working in another, and return to home communities during leave, creating transmission networks that span multiple districts [46].

Fourthly, measurement timing may affect findings. HIV prevalence estimates for 2010 represent a single time point, whereas TB notification rates are averaged over 2005–2012. The relationship between HIV and TB is also time-lagged: HIV infection increases TB risk over years, and ART reduces risk over years. Cross-sectional ecological analysis may not capture these temporal dynamics [70]. Importantly, these null findings should not be interpreted as evidence that HIV and poverty are unimportant for TB control and that HIV was not a major driver of TB during this study period. Individual-level and community-level studies consistently demonstrate their central role [2, 52, 71]. Rather, they suggest that at the district level in South Africa during this period, TB burden was so strongly structured by spatial proximity and unmeasured contextual factors that the independent signals of HIV and poverty were not detectable in ecological regression.

Relevance to contemporary TB control

Although the data presented end in 2012, this historical baseline remains relevant for several reasons. Firstly, structural determinants persist. Poverty, income inequality, housing quality, and health system inequities change slowly; districts that were poor in 2011 are likely still disadvantaged today [41, 72]. The mining sector continues to employ large migrant workforces with elevated TB risk [45, 73, 74]. HIV prevalence remains high in the same eastern regions, even as ART coverage has expanded [75]. Secondly, TB hotspots tend to persist over time. International evidence from Brazil, China, and elsewhere shows that high-burden areas often remain high-burden for decades unless targeted interventions are implemented [25, 76]. In South Africa, comparison with more recent district-level data (where available) suggests that many districts identified as hotspots in this analysis, particularly in eastern KwaZulu-Natal and parts of the Eastern Cape remain prioritised in national TB programmes [77]. Thirdly, historical baselines enable evaluation. Without knowing where the TB burden was concentrated before the GeneXpert rollout, ART expansion, TPT, and UTT, it is impossible to assess whether these interventions have reduced geographic disparities rigorously. This study provides a baseline, enabling future research to ask whether high-burden districts improved more than low-burden districts. Have hotspots shifted or persisted? Have sex disparities narrowed? Fourthly, the demographic patterns identified particularly male excess and concentration in economically active ages, highlight target populations that remain priority groups today. Men continue to under-utilise TB services, and interventions tailored to workplace and community settings for working-age adults remain essential [48, 78].

Our findings have greater implications for policy and programme evaluation by policymakers and programme managers. This study offers three contributions. Firstly, it identifies districts that were high-burden during 2005–2012, providing a checklist for evaluating current geographic targeting. National TB programmes can ask: Are these same districts still receiving intensified support? Have they improved? If not, what additional interventions are needed? Secondly, it quantifies the magnitude of geographic disparities, six-fold variation at the district level, underscoring the need for resource allocation based on burden rather than population alone. Historical patterns suggest that universal approaches may fail to reach populations with the greatest need [79]. Thirdly, it reinforces the importance of sex-disaggregated surveillance and male-targeted interventions. The male excess observed (53.5% of cases) is not explained by population structure and likely reflects both higher incidence and diagnostic disparities. Programmes should track sex-specific notification rates and evaluate outreach to men.

The contemporary relevance of the findings of this study is underscored by the continued high burden of TB in South Africa and the persistence of key epidemiological patterns identified during the study period. Although TB incidence has declined, the country remains among the highest burden settings globally, with substantial ongoing morbidity and mortality. The higher notification rates among males and the concentration of TB in adults aged 25–44 years remain consistent with current evidence, which shows a disproportionate burden among men and working-age populations. Likewise, the spatial clustering observed particularly in KwaZulu-Natal, and Eastern Cape, aligns with recent studies demonstrating persistent geographic heterogeneity and localised TB hotspots. These patterns suggest that structural drivers, including inequalities in access to care and localised transmission, continue to shape the epidemic [30, 32, 80].

However, the applicability of the study findings requires cautious interpretation given important epidemiological and programmatic changes. The lack of a statistically significant association between TB notification rates and HIV prevalence contrasts with current evidence highlighting strong TB–HIV co-infection in South Africa, likely reflecting ecological limitations. In addition, advances in diagnostics, treatment, and ART scale-up, as well as the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) related disruptions to TB services, have altered disease dynamics and case detection. Accordingly, these findings should be contextualised within evolving health system conditions [81–84].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include its national scope, large sample size (> 3.4 million cases), an eight-year study period enabling stable rate estimation, the use of standardised rates for valid geographic comparison, and the application of multiple complementary spatial analytic techniques. The spatial lag regression approach appropriately accounted for spatial dependence, avoiding the biased inference that would result from ordinary least squares. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the data are from 2005 to 2012 and may not reflect current epidemiology; we have framed this as a historical baseline rather than a limitation per se. Second, data quality issues inherent in routine surveillance, including potential missing data, and incomplete HIV status recording, may affect accuracy, though the large sample size mitigates some concerns. Third, district-level HIV and poverty measures are ecological and may not reflect individual-level exposures; we cannot infer causal relationships. Fourth, the spatial analysis is cross-sectional (2010) and does not capture temporal changes in clustering. Fifth, unmeasured confounders, including migration, health system performance, and environmental factors, could not be included in regression models.

As this study utilised historical baseline data, it opens several avenues for future research. Firstly, repeating this analysis with post-2015 data would enable a rigorous evaluation of whether geographic disparities have narrowed following GeneXpert rollout, ART expansion, TPT, and UTT. Secondly, sub-district spatial analyses could identify within-district heterogeneity masked by our district-level approach. Thirdly, linking TB notification data with individual-level HIV status and treatment records would enable more precise estimation of HIV-TB interactions. Fourthly, qualitative research in persistent hotspot districts could illuminate barriers to TB control that quantitative data cannot capture. Fifth, spatial modelling incorporating migration data could better quantify cross-district transmission dynamics.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this retrospective analysis of 3.47 million TB notifications from 2005 to 2012 reveals marked demographic disparities (male excess, peak burden at ages 25–44) and pronounced geographic clustering in the eastern South African districts. The strong spatial dependence observed indicates that TB burden during this period was shaped by neighbourhood effects and shared district-level determinants. Although HIV prevalence and poverty were not statistically significant predictors in spatial regression, likely due to ecological aggregation and strong spatial spillover effects, they remain central to understanding TB epidemiology at finer scales. By establishing a detailed baseline from the era preceding major programmatic changes, this study enables future evaluations of whether subsequent interventions have reduced disparities and where entrenched vulnerabilities persist. The districts and populations identified warrant renewed attention as South Africa works toward the End TB Strategy targets.
