Fulminant leptomeningeal disease diagnosed as comutant H3F3A and FGFR diffuse midline glioma
Authors: Larysa Benistant, Damien Reita, Maleka Schenck, Vincent Castelain, Hélène Cebula, Benoît Lhermitte, Laura Bender
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiffuse midline gliomas present a particularly intricate and challenging clinical scenario. This rare case involves a patient with comutant H3F3A and FGFR diffuse midline glioma with a clinical presentation of fulminant leptomeningitis. A 22‐year‐old male presented with fatal and fulminant diffuse leptomeningitis. Next‐generation sequencing of plasma and cerebrospinal circulating tumour DNA revealed diffuse midline gliomas with H3F3A and FGFR mutations. Methylome analysis of meningeal tissue collected during autopsy confirmed the diagnosis. Liquid biopsy plays a crucial role in the diagnosis of diffuse midline gliomas, mainly those with exclusively leptomeningeal presentations.

Introduction

Central nervous system (CNS) tumours are the second most common tumour type in young adults, with an annual incidence rate of 12.21 per 100.000 population. 1

The fifth edition of the WHO classification of CNS tumours, introduced new subtypes based on novel diagnostic technologies such as DNA methylome profiling. 2 , 3 The majority of CNS tumour diagnoses are based on tissue biopsies. Recent literature has reported the diagnostic role of liquid biopsy, mainly cerebrospinal fluid circulating tumoral DNA (CSF‐ctDNA) analysis. 4

Diffuse midline glioma (DMG) H3K27 altered, a grade IV glioma, are characterized by either K237M mutation in the H3F31 gene encoding histone H3 or the overexpression of EZHIP or EGFR alterations. 2 , 5 Various co‐mutations in TP53, ACVR1 or PDGFRA genes are described. 2 Recently, concomitant alterations in H3K27M and mitogen‐activated protein kinase pathways, such as the BRAF and FGFR1 genes, have been described. 5 The prognostic impact of these double mutations is still poorly understood.

DMG is located mainly in the thalamus and in the brainstem in the adult and paediatric population, respectively. 2 , 6 Leptomeningeal dissemination (LMD) at diagnosis has been variably described in case series. 7 However, no studies have reported an exclusive leptomeningeal presentation of a comutant H3F3/FGFR diffuse midline glioma.

This rare case involves an exclusive LMD of a fulminant comutant K27M H3F3A and K656E FGFR1 diffuse midline glioma in a young adult diagnosed by next‐generation sequencing (NGS) of CSF and plasma‐circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) confirmed post‐mortem by meningeal tissue methylome analysis.

Case Description

A 22‐year‐old Caucasian patient without a medical history presented, in May 2023, vomiting associated with headaches, photophobia and diffuse dorsolumbar pain. In June 2023, an injected brain CT scan revealed no cerebral abnormalities. A few days later, on June 9, his neurological picture deteriorated, with the onset of confusion, psychomotor slowing, slurred speech, abnormal movements (dyskinesia/chorea) of the upper limbs and disturbances of consciousness, prompting an orotracheal intubation.

Brain and spinal cord MRI revealed diffuse leptomeningitis of the medulla and brain stem, basal cisterns and frontotemporal insular region without signs of hydrocephalus (Fig. 1A–C). CSF analysis revealed hyperproteinorachia at 9.3 g/L and a few round‐nucleated neoplastic cells of undetermined origin with predominantly monocytic hypercellularity. Radiological examinations (thoracic‐abdominal CT, testicular ultrasound) revealed no primary tumour lesions. An 18F‐FDG positron emission tomography scan revealed hypermetabolism of the medullary canal. Extensive aetiological work‐up (infectious, immunological, haematological and toxicological) was not informative. Because of initial suspicion of infectious meningitis, antibiotherapy at meningeal dose was initiated. Secondly, in the hypothesis of inflammatory meningitis, high‐dose corticosteroid (hdCS) therapy (1 g/day during 3 days) was associated with it. The clinical course was initially favourable and he was able to be weaned from mechanical ventilation within 3 days. On June 22, there was a recurrence of signs of meningeal irritation, impaired alertness and dysarthria, justifying the resumption of hdCS therapy. Brain MRI revealed persistent diffuse leptomeningitis, signs of rhombencephalitis and the appearance of hydrocephalus (Fig. 1D). An evacuation (30 mL) lumbar puncture, with an opening pressure >90 cmH2O, led to an improvement in neurological symptoms. Hyperproteinorachia at 9.30 g/L and a few neoplastic cells of undetermined origin were found. The pyramidal and cerebellar syndromes nevertheless persisted. Next‐generation sequencing of CSF and plasma ctDNA was performed. On June 30 and July 2, clinical signs of meningitis (headache, nuchal stiffness, vomiting) recurred, and he was treated with hdCS corticosteroid therapy and an evacuating lumbar puncture without clinical improvement. Hyperproteinorachia persisted between 5.01 and 7.16 g/L. The origin of the few neoplastic cells remained undetermined. On July 6, the patient again presented with intense headaches with photophobia and cervicalgia, which improved little after evacuation via lumbar puncture and hdCS therapy. During the next 4 days, he experienced several epileptic seizures, behavioural disorders (psychomotor agitation) and alertness refractory to medical treatment (corticosteroids, antiepileptics). Brain MRI revealed diffuse leptomeningitis associated with signs of cortical distress and tetraventricular hydrocephalus with obliteration of cortical sulci and transpendymal resorption (Fig. 1E) and spinal cord MRI showed a cervico‐dorsal epiduritis (Fig. 1F). Within 24 h, he developed bilateral mydriasis, dysautonomia and diabetes insipidus, which are clinical signs of encephalic death. On July 11, cerebral angioscan revealed diffuse cerebral oedema and the absence of cerebral perfusion (Fig. 1G). Meningeal biopsy was discussed, but in the absence of an easily biopsied target, it was scheduled for July 11, the date of the patient's death. The diagnosis of DMG was revealed post‐mortem by NGS analysis of CSF and plasma ctDNA, revealing the presence of a K27M mutation in exon 2 of the H3F3A gene and a K656E mutation in exon 14 of the FGFR1 gene. Autopsy revealed a diagnosis of a primary CNS tumour with morphological and cellular features compatible with a diffuse leptomeningeal glioneuronal tumour, but methylome analysis of the meningeal tissue confirmed the diagnosis of a diffuse midline glioma.

Pathological findings

Several cytological studies of CSF have shown the presence of atypical cells with large hyperchromatic nuclei and sparse basophilic cytoplasm grouped together in small clusters (Fig. 2A). Nevertheless, multiple attempts to specify the phenotype of these cells have proven unsuccessful, with negativity for epithelial, lymphoid and glial markers (particularly OLIG2).

Macroscopic and cytological features of CSF and autopsy materials. (A) Cytological analysis of CSF: Atypical cells with large hyperchromatic nuclei and sparse basophilic cytoplasm were grouped together in small clusters. (B) Macroscopic analysis of the spinal cord revealed numerous leptomeningeal petechial lesions. (C) Histopathological analysis of the entire spinal cord revealed diffuse leptomeningeal infiltration by neoplastic uniform cells with round nuclei and sparse basophilic cytoplasm. (D) Immunohistochemical examination of the autolysed autopsy material revealed no contributory alterations, and the tumour cells only invaded the protuberance.

During autopsy, the spinal cord showed numerous leptomeningeal petechial lesions (Fig. 2B). The brain stem, particularly the protuberance, was discretely enlarged and firm. The slices of both the medullary and cerebral sections were unremarkable.

Histopathological examination of the entire spinal cord revealed diffuse leptomeningeal infiltration by uniform neoplastic cells with round nuclei and sparse basophilic cytoplasm. The spinal cord was fully preserved (Fig. 2C). No immunohistochemical examination found any factors that contributed to this type of autolysed autopsy material. It was only at the level of the protuberance that the tumour cells invaded the brain parenchyma (Fig. 2D).

Macroscopic and cytological features, as well as the molecular alterations revealed by the CSF study, led to a differential diagnosis between DLGNT and DMG.

Finally, based on the results of the methylome study, the patient was confirmed to have had diffuse midline glioma, H3K27M‐altered subtype that was either H3K27‐mutant or EZHIP‐expressing, with a calibrated score of 0.99.

Discussion

This rare case involved a fulminant comutant K27M H3F3A and K656E FGFR1 diffuse midline glioma with exclusive leptomeningeal dissemination diagnosed by CSF and plasma ctDNA molecular and methylation profiling.

It highlights the poor prognosis in cases of LMD within 2 months between the first neurological symptoms and death. Among the 122 patients included in the HERBY phase 2 study, the 1‐year overall survival rate was between 68% (95% CI, 54%–78%). 8 In this series, leptomeningeal dissemination was associated with a significantly greater risk of death (18.5 vs. 11.4 months, p = 0.004). 9 Diaz et al. showed similar results in a retrospective study including 42 patients. Leptomeningeal dissemination was associated with a sevenfold greater risk of death (HR: 7.37, 95% CI: 2.98–18.23, p < .0001). 10 Tinkle et al. reported a case of a comutant H3K27M/TP53 diffuse infiltrating pontine glioma with a fatal leptomeningeal dissemination which occurred 3 months after diagnosis. 11 Regardless of molecular profile, leptomeningeal dissemination is a poor prognosis factor which negatively impact overall survival.

In the adult population, the thalamic location is the most common. 2 Leptomeningeal dissemination of DMG has been reported with a 3‐year cumulative incidence of 44.7% (95% CI 26.1%–63.4%) and a median time from tumour diagnosis to LMD of 12.9 months for patients with the H3K27 mutation in a retrospective study including 42 patients. 10 In the HERBY phase 2 study, 16 of the 38 evaluable (42%) patients had LMD. 9 No data have been reported a case of comutant H3K27M/FGFR1 DMG with an exclusive leptomeningeal dissemination.

According to the fifth edition of the WHO Classification of CNS tumours, diffuse midline gliomas are divided into four subtypes depending on their molecular characteristics: DMG H3.3K27‐mutant; DMG H3.1 or H3.2K27—mutant; DMG H3‐wildtype with EZHIP overexpression and DMG EGFR‐altered. 2 Comutation of as TP53, ACVR1 and PDGFRA genes are described. 2 Auffret et al. reported a new subtype of H3K27M‐muted DMG with comutation of BRAF or FGFR1 genes. Both are associated with a better prognosis than H3K27M/TP53‐comutant DMG. 5 , 12 Mitogen‐activated protein kinase pathway gene alterations led to differential diagnosis such diffuse leptomeningeal glioneuronal tumours (DLGNT) all the more so in case of exclusive leptomeningeal dissemination. Diffuse leptomeningeal glioneuronal tumours commonly affect paediatric population but can also manifest in adult population. Regarding imaging feature, DLGNT presents as predominant diffuse abnormal nodular leptomeningeal growth with or without evidence of a primary intraparenchymal lesion. Diffuse leptomeningeal glioneuronal tumours is a low‐grade neoplasms with primarily oligodendroglioma‐like features, neuronal differentiating capacity, and tendency towards extensive leptomeningeal dissemination. 13 Methylome analysis are characterized by KIAA1549‐BRAF fusion or 1p deletion. 2

In our case, imaging and histological features were in favour of DLGNT but DNA methylome analysis classed the tumour as a diffuse midline glioma H327M altered. Donev et al. reported an usual presentation of DMG with diffuse hemispheric lesions and no infiltration of midline structures. The DMG diagnosis was based on the presence of H3 p.K28M (K27M) alterations associated with comutations in PTPN11, PPM1D, TP53, ATRX, NF1 or NOTCH1 genes. 14 Literature data reported three cases of disseminated leptomeningeal infiltration. All three presented glioneuronal immunophenotype, including one with 1p deletion, and they were eventually classed as DMG based on methyome analysis. Radiological and immunohistological features are insufficient to class CNS tumours according to fifth WHO classification. Methylome analysis plays a crucial role.

Cerebral biopsy could be associated with a high risk of complications, mainly when the tumours are in the brainstem. Recent literature has reported the diagnostic role of cerebrospinal fluid liquid biopsy. Neurosurgical biopsy remains the gold standard, but it can be risky and not available for all patients with midline tumours. The advantage of liquid biopsy is that it is easy to perform and enables a reliable analysis via molecular profiling. The analysis of plasma ctDNA and CSF‐ctDNA can predict disease recurrence well before the appearance of radiological lesions and plays a prognostic role. 4 , 15 , 16

Conclusion

This rare case involved a fulminant comutant H3F3A/FGFR diffuse midline glioma with an exclusive leptomeningeal presentation and demonstrated the crucial diagnostic role of liquid biopsy and complex molecular platforms. Liquid biopsy allows the diagnosis of cerebrospinal tumours, mainly in cases where there is a high risk of complications induced by tissue biopsy.
