Newer glucose‐lowering drugs reduce the risk of late‐onset seizure and epilepsy: A meta‐analysis
Authors: Udeept Sindhu, Akshay Sharma, Ifrah Zawar, Vineet Punia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNewer glucose‐lowering drugs (GLDs) protect against cerebrovascular, neurodegenerative, and neuroinflammatory pathologies. Therefore, we performed a meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing newer GLDs to placebo that assessed long‐term cardiovascular and renal outcomes to analyze their potential to prevent late‐onset seizures and epilepsy, separately and as a combined outcome. A comprehensive MEDLINE and CENTRAL databases search for DPP‐4 inhibitors, GLP‐1 receptor agonists, and SGLT2 inhibitor RCTs, which reported adverse effects, including seizures and epilepsy on clinicaltrials.gov, yielded 413 studies. Of them, 27 studies with almost 200 000 patients (mean age 64.9 years, 65.6% males) were included. We calculated relative risk (RR) and odds ratio (OR) using the Mantel–Haenszel method and Peto's method. Patients taking newer GLDs had a 24% lower risk of late‐onset seizures and epilepsy, combined, (RR: 0.76, 95% CI: 0.62–0.95) and 22% lower risk of late‐onset seizures only (RR = 0.78; 95% CI = 0.60–1.00), compared to patients on placebo. This seizure and epilepsy prevention benefit was only noted among patients taking GLP‐1 receptor agonists. Stroke incidence was comparable between newer GLDs and placebo group. GLP‐1 receptor agonists like Semaglutide significantly reduce late‐onset seizures and epilepsy, and their anti‐epileptogenic potential in older adults needs further exploration.Plain Language SummaryOur analysis 27 clinical trials and nearly 200 000 patients evaluated the potential of newer glucose‐lowering drugs (GLDs) to prevent late‐onset seizures and epilepsy in older adults. INTRODUCTION

Current anti‐seizure medications (ASMs) lack disease‐modifying or preventative, that is, anti‐epileptogenic, potential. 1 One of the primary challenges in anti‐epileptogenic drug discovery is the diverse etiology underlying epilepsy. Late‐onset epilepsy (LOE), typically starting after the age of 60–65 years of age, is one of the largest contributors to epilepsy burden, is an acquired form of epilepsy, making it a primary target for potentially anti‐epileptogenic drugs. 2 One of its most common etiologies is cerebrovascular disease, with diabetes mellitus (DM) being a common risk factor. 3 The newer glucose‐lowering drugs (GLDs), beyond their established effectiveness in managing diabetes, seem to have pleiotropic effects, including neuroprotection and anti‐inflammatory properties. A recent randomized controlled trial (RCT) shows that a newer GLD significantly slows Parkinson's disease, suggesting protection against neurodegeneration. 4 They may have anti‐neuroinflammatory properties as they show protective benefits against multiple sclerosis. 5 Etiopathogenesis of late‐onset epilepsy is thought to be a confluence of neurodegeneration and vascular damage, which raises the intriguing possibility that these drugs may potentially impact seizure and epilepsy development. 6 , 7 Therefore, we performed a meta‐analysis of RCTs of these drugs to investigate if newer GLDs have any protective benefit against seizure and epilepsy.

METHODS

Search strategy and study selection

We conducted a comprehensive search of MEDLINE and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from inception to April 2024 to identify RCTs that compared newer GLDs Dipeptidyl Peptidase‐4 [DPP‐4] inhibitors, Glucagon‐Like Peptide‐1 receptor agonists [GLP‐1RAs], and sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 [SGLT2] inhibitors; details (Table S1) to placebo. Given that investigating the impact of a drug on seizure risk or epileptogenesis should have several months to years of follow‐up, we searched for studies on cardiovascular and renal outcomes. Additionally, adverse event reporting in these RCTs was more thorough and consistently included seizures and epilepsy. We also manually searched references from screened studies and relevant reviews to identify any other potential studies.

The following MEDLINE search strategy was used: (Randomized Controlled Trial [MeSH Terms]) AND (newer glucose‐lowering drugs [MeSH Terms]) OR (SGLT2 inhibitors [MeSH Terms]) OR (DPP‐4 inhibitors [MeSH Terms]) OR (GLP‐1 agonists [MeSH Terms]) AND (cardiovascular [Title/Abstract]) OR (myocardial infarction [Title/Abstract]) OR (stroke [Title/Abstract]) OR (renal [Title/Abstract]) OR (kidney [Title/Abstract]) OR (albuminuria [Title/Abstract]).

Screening process

Two independent reviewers used the following screening criteria: Inclusion criteria: (1) Randomized placebo‐controlled cardiovascular and renal outcome trials; (2) trials enrolled adults (≥18 years) with or without type 2 DM; and (3) trials reporting epilepsy or seizure adverse events. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Observational studies (e.g., cohort studies, case–control studies); (2) RCTs comparing different newer GLD classes; (3) Post hoc analyses; (4) Unregistered trials; and (5) Trials with unpublished/incomplete results on www.clinicaltrials.gov.

Data collection and extraction

We used a standardized data extraction form to collect relevant information from eligible studies, including author information, publication date, trial ID, baseline participant characteristics, intervention details, follow‐up duration, outcome measures (cardiovascular or renal), and reported adverse events of seizure, and epilepsy. The latter, along with the number of strokes (ischemic and hemorrhagic), were extracted from trial results published on www.clinicaltrials.gov. We extracted information of incidence of strokes are the most common etiology underlying LOE and can potentially confound the effect of newer GLDs on seizure and epilepsy risk. 2 Adverse events reported as status epilepticus and convulsions were included as seizures. Epilepsy included all subtypes, regardless of whether it was unclassified or subclassified as generalized or focal. Seizures directly attributed to alcohol withdrawal were excluded from the analysis. Different dosages of newer GLDs within a single trial were combined. We assessed the quality of each included study using the Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool, Version 2. 8

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to summarize the demographic characteristics of study participants. Frequency counts with percentages were used to present categorical data. Mean with standard deviation (SD) was used to demonstrate continuous variables. We employed the Mantel–Haenszel method to calculate pooled relative risk (RR), odds ratio (OR), and 95% confidence interval (CI) for the risk of the following events: seizure, epilepsy, combined seizure and epilepsy, and strokes. We performed a combined seizure and epilepsy risk analysis because of lack of information about the precise definitions used to classify seizure and epilepsy in the trials. For example, it is unclear from the available data if a first unprovoked (remote) seizure after a stroke was classified as only a seizure or, more appropriately, as epilepsy. In addition, subgroup analyses were conducted based on the class of newer GLDs (DPP‐4 inhibitors, GLP‐1RAs, and SGLT2 inhibitors). The chi‐squared (χ 2) test assessed the study's heterogeneity and interaction between subgroups.

Post hoc, after we found a low‐event rate for seizures and epilepsy, sensitivity analysis was performed using Peto's method to recalculate the OR. Peto's method is not recommended as a default meta‐analysis method. 9 However, it can be used when the events of interest are infrequent (<1%) and the studies have similar numbers in two comparator groups. 10 In the manuscript, we prioritized the Mantel–Haenszel method for our analysis due to its ability to control for confounding variables, its robustness to zero cells compared to Peto's method, and its ability to provide RR for a more straightforward interpretation of the association when analyzing rare events. 9 Potential publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot. Statistical significance was set at a two‐sided p ≤ 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using RevMan. 11

RESULTS

Out of 413 identified studies, 27 met the criteria for inclusion in the meta‐analysis. Figure S1 shows the PRISMA flowchart. The risk of bias was low for all domains except for selective reporting because seizures and epilepsy were not prespecified outcomes (Figure S2). No publication bias was detected (Figures S3–S6).

There were five trials for DPP‐4 inhibitors, eight for GLP‐1Ras, and 14 for SGLT2 inhibitors. The meta‐analysis included 197 910 participants, with 52% in the newer GLD group and 48% in the placebo group. The average age of the patient population was 64.9 (SD 5.9) years, and 65.6% were males. The mean follow‐up duration averaged 29.2 months (SD 12.6). Seizures and epilepsy events were reported as adverse events in all the included studies. Seizure and epilepsy (combined outcome)

A total of 182 (0.19%) patients in the placebo arm compared to 154 (0.015%) patients receiving newer GLDs had incident seizure or epilepsy during the follow‐up. The pooled meta‐analysis (Figure 1) found that people who received newer GLDs had a significantly lower risk of suffering seizures than the placebo group (RR: 0.76, 95% CI: 0.62–0.95, p = 0.01). The ORs calculated using Mantel–Haenszel and Peto's method showed similar association strength and statistical significance (Figures S11 and S12, respectively). As noted in Figure 1, among the newer GLD classes, the patients taking GLP‐1 receptor agonists had a statistically significant reduced seizure and epilepsy risk compared to placebo (RR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.46–0.98; p: 0.034).

Meta‐analysis of the effects of newer GLDs on seizure and epilepsy risk, combined, and subgrouped by the type of newer GLD (Mantel–Haenszel RR).

Seizures

Seizures occurred in 126 (0.132%) placebo patients and 109 (0.105%) newer GLD patients. A total of two studies (Table 1: study #5 and 24) in the drug arm and four studies (Table 1: study # 9, 10, 27, and 26) in the placebo arm reported a single post‐stroke seizure or epilepsy event. The pooled meta‐analysis (Figure 2) found that people who received newer GLDs had a significantly lower risk of seizures than the placebo group (RR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.60–1.00; p: 0.05). OR calculated using Mantel–Haenszel and Peto's method showed similar association strength and statistical significance (Figures S7 and S8, respectively). No statistical heterogeneity was observed in the meta‐analyses (all p > 0.05). As noted in Figure 2, among the newer GLD classes, the patients taking GLP‐1 receptor agonists had a statistically significant reduced seizure risk compared to placebo (RR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.38–0.94; p: 0.03).

(A) Meta‐analysis of the Effects of Newer GLDs on only Seizure Risk, subgrouped by the type of newer GLD (Mantel–Haenszel RR). (B) Meta‐analysis of the Effects of Newer GLDs on only Epilepsy Risk, subgrouped by the type of newer GLD (Mantel–Haenszel RR).

Epilepsy

A total of 56 (0.059%) patients in the placebo arm were reported to have incident epilepsy during the follow‐up compared to 45 (0.044%) patients receiving newer GLDs. The pooled meta‐analysis (Figure 2B) showed that patients taking newer GLDs had a lower risk of incident epilepsy compared to placebo (RR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.51–1.11; p = 0.15), but this difference was not statistically significant. OR calculated using Mantel–Haenszel and Peto's method showed similar association strength and statistical significance (Figures S9 and S10, respectively). No evidence of statistical heterogeneity was observed in the meta‐analyses (all p > 0.05). Subgroup analyses of the newer GLD classes did not reveal significant associations between their use and reduced epilepsy risk.

Stroke

A total of 944 (0.99%) of patients in the placebo arm had strokes compared to 953 (0.93%) patients receiving newer GLDs. The pooled meta‐analysis showed no statistically significant difference in the stroke risk ratio in patients taking newer GLDs compared to placebo (RR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.86–1.03; p = 0.21; Figure S13). The OR calculated using Mantel–Haenszel and Peto's method showed a similar lack of difference between the two groups (Figures S14 and S15, respectively).

DISCUSSION

Our meta‐analysis of 27 RCTs, which included almost 200 000 patients with a mean age of 65 years, reveals a potentially reduced risk of seizures and epilepsy when taking newer GLDs compared to placebo. During an average follow‐up of more 2 years, older adults taking newer GLDs had a 24% lower risk of seizures and epilepsy, combined, which was even higher in individuals taking GLP‐1 receptor agonists like Semaglutide (brand name Ozempic®). Independent analysis of seizures and epilepsy revealed significantly lower seizure, but not epilepsy, risk in patients taking newer GLDs.

These findings align with preclinical studies, which show that all three newer GLD classes reduce seizure and epileptogenic activity in animal models. 12 , 13 , 14 Their putative anti‐epileptogenic mechanisms are multifactorial. DPP‐4 inhibitors, like sitagliptin, may work via an antioxidant effect, maintaining GABA levels and reducing neuroinflammation. 14 Similarly, liraglutide, a GLP‐1 receptor agonist, may protect against epilepsy by reducing chronic inflammation and mitochondrial stress. 15 Erdogan et al. posits a potential dual anti‐epileptogenic mechanism for SGLT2 inhibitors like dapagliflozin, whereby it restricts cerebral glucose availability to mitigate hyperexcitability and reduces sodium transport across neuronal membranes for a stabilizing effect. 16 In addition to basic science findings, there is sufficient clinical data to justify their investigation as anti‐LOE agents in humans.

A complex interplay among cerebrovascular risk factors, stroke, and dementia leads to LOE. 6 , 7 Newer GLDs' have already demonstrated a pleiotropic effect beyond lowering glucose levels. They provide cerebrovascular protective benefits, reduce the risk of all‐cause dementia, significantly slow down neurodegeneration, and improve neuroinflammation. 5 , 17 , 18 , 19 Therefore, it seems likely that newer GLDs can target the intertwined pathophysiology underlying LOE, and their potential protection against late‐onset seizures and epilepsy may not be too surprising. However, the incidence of stroke does not explain the observed reduction in seizures and epilepsy in our meta‐analysis.

Many of the newer GLDs, like GLP‐1 receptor agonists, have been hailed as “game‐changing” drugs, showing benefits in as varied diseases as alcohol use disorders and infertility. 20 , 21 , 22 It is critical to point out that newer GLDs' substantial protective benefit against late‐onset seizures and epilepsy, when analyzed independently, despite the abovementioned pathophysiological reasons, is statistically precarious with the former and falls short of reaching significance against the latter. This lack of strong statistical significance of seizures and epilepsy, individually, could be secondary to a major limitation of our study—seizure, and incident epilepsy were not the primary or secondary outcomes of the RCTs; instead, the information was extracted from the adverse effects reported on www.clinicaltrials.gov. The underreporting of seizures and incident epilepsy in either or both study arms can impact our findings. Conversely, there is possibly no anti‐epileptogenic benefit, or it is so tiny that a much larger sample size is needed to detect it with a higher statistical certainty. It is unclear whether the ILAE's practical definition of epilepsy, requiring either two unprovoked seizures or one unprovoked seizure with a remote brain insult, was used in reporting adverse events in the included RCTs. 23 To overcome this uncertainty, we decided to analyze the combined outcome of seizure and epilepsy. The longer follow‐up needed for diagnosing epilepsy after a first unprovoked seizure without a remote brain injury may explain the significant reduction in seizure risk, but not epilepsy, in our study. Alternatively, these drugs may only have a direct anti‐seizure effect but not an anti‐epileptogenic effect. Future studies that examine sustained seizure control after newer GLDs' discontinuation can help differentiate whether they have an anti‐seizure or anti‐epileptogenic mechanism because, in the latter case, there should remain a sustained seizure control after treatment cessation.

Despite the average patient age being 65 years, some incident seizures and epilepsy cases may not have been late‐onset, as studies included adults over 18 years old. Due to inconsistent reporting of glucose‐lowering metrics in the included RCTs, we could not assess if the difference in seizure and epilepsy risk was associated with newer GLD's neuroprotective effects from glucose reduction. Also, despite a large sample size, the low seizure and epilepsy event rate poses some challenges to the precision of our estimates. However, post hoc, we performed Peto's method as a sensitivity analysis to counter this limitation, but the conclusions remained unchanged. Aside from a potentially direct anti‐epileptogenic effect of newer GLDs, our finding may be partly or entirely due to decreased comorbidities, like diabetes and obesity, that lead to or predispose to LOE.

Nonetheless, our findings, in combination with preclinical studies, should encourage continued investigations into newer GLDs potential anti‐epileptogenic effects using varying methodologies and diverse, large population datasets, especially from specific older adult populations at significantly higher risk of epilepsy development like stroke patients. The included RCTs did not provide information to conduct such a specific subpopulation analysis. Additionally, unmeasured confounding factors with imbalanced randomization could influence the observed association.

CONCLUSION

This meta‐analysis suggests that newer GLDs may have protective benefits against late‐onset seizures and epilepsy. Our findings are strengthened by supporting evidence of anti‐epileptogenic effects emerging from animal studies and the robust human data demonstrating their neuroprotective effects against cerebrovascular pathology, neurodegeneration, and neuroinflammation. Future research must validate and challenge our meta‐analysis findings, especially because LOE is a growing global challenge that is potentially amenable to preventative therapies.
