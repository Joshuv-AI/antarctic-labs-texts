Thrombolysis in non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction: systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials
Authors: José Nunes de Alencar, Márcio Henrique de Jesus Oliveira, Elisio Bulhoes, Carlos Alexandre Farias, Julia Camargo Kabariti, Henrique Champs Carvalho, Harvey Pendell Meyers, Stephen W Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGuidelines strongly recommend reperfusion therapy, including thrombolysis and percutaneous coronary intervention, for ST-elevation myocardial infarction but contraindicate its use in most non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndromes (ACS). This practice largely stems from the landmark fibrinolytic therapy trialists (FTT) meta-analysis, which reported no benefit in patients without ST elevation (STE). However, the FTT included a subgroup from the ISIS-3 trial with substantial methodological issues, potentially obscuring a genuine treatment effect.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comparing thrombolysis vs placebo or no thrombolysis in ACS. Patients were grouped by ECG findings: STE, ST depression (STD) or absence of STE. All-cause mortality was extracted from each trial’s short-term follow-up (typically 21–35 days). We reassessed outcomes with and without inclusion of the ISIS-3 ‘uncertain diagnosis’ subgroup.ResultsNine RCTs (40 226 patients) were analysed. Thrombolysis significantly reduced mortality in patients without STE (excluding isolated STD) (risk ratio (RR): 0.799; 95% CI 0.668 to 0.956; I²=0%). Including the ISIS-3 ‘uncertain diagnosis’ subgroup (representing 42% of the non-STE population) would have eliminated the statistical significance in non-STE patients (RR: 0.928; 95% CI 0.694 to 1.242) and markedly increased heterogeneity (I²=71%).ConclusionIn historical RCTs, thrombolysis was associated with lower short-term mortality in non-STE presentations excluding isolated ST-segment depression, while isolated STD showed no benefit. Legacy conclusions hinge on outdated methods, delayed treatment and heterogeneous ECG definitions (and are sensitive to ISIS-3). This study exposes a material evidence gap in the foundation of current guidelines. Contemporary randomised trials with prespecified ECG criteria, rapid treatment windows and rigorous safety adjudication are needed.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024573681.Keywords: THROMBOLYSIS, Acute Coronary Syndrome, Meta-Analysis

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Thrombolysis was this initial cornerstone of reperfusion in acute coronary syndromes starting in the 1980s. Since then, mechanical coronary intervention has predominated but using the same ECG guidelines that were generated in the thrombolytic era. These guidelines strongly support its use for ST-elevation myocardial infarction, using ST-segment elevation (STE) as a surrogate for acute coronary occlusion (ACO). However, more than half of patients with ACO do not present STE, and the benefit of thrombolysis in non-STE patients has been underestimated, largely based on methodologically fragile evidence.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

By re-examining legacy randomised trials with ECG-defined strata, this study demonstrates that the evidence base discouraging non-STE thrombolysis and, by extension, all coronary reperfusion therapy, is incomplete and era-bound. The findings reveal a persistent evidence gap—rooted in outdated adjunctive therapy, heterogeneous ECG definitions, delayed treatment windows and sensitivity to methodologically problematic data—rather than a closed question. Modern randomised trials using refined ECG phenotyping of ACO, rapid treatment windows and rigorous bleeding/stroke assessment are therefore warranted.

Introduction

Reperfusion therapy is essential for the management of acute coronary occlusion (ACO). In contemporary practice, however, this strategy is largely restricted to patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), a paradigm shaped by the results of the fibrinolytic therapy trialists (FTT) collaborative study published over three decades ago. Thrombolysis, alongside percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), continues to play a significant role in the management of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), particularly in regions with limited access to PCI. In developing countries, thrombolysis is often the most accessible reperfusion strategy. A multinational survey of ACS management in these regions, including Latin America, found that among patients with STEMI, 39% did not receive any reperfusion therapy, while PCI and thrombolysis were used in similar proportions among those treated.1 ST-segment elevation (STE) is an imperfect surrogate for ACO, a substantial proportion of ACOs present without STE. Thus, some non-STE patients harbour ACO and could plausibly benefit from fibrinolysis. However, the current treatment paradigm does not explicitly consider the degree of coronary obstruction when guiding reperfusion strategies. Today, thrombolysis for STEMI is a class I indication in major guidelines worldwide. For cases without STE, this treatment is generally a class III recommendation, with the notable exceptions of right precordial ST depression (STD) suggestive of lateral wall (formerly ‘posterior’) infarction or diffuse STD with concurrent STE in lead aVR.2,4 The European Society of Cardiology guidelines mention patterns such as de Winter,5 Aslanger6 and Wellens7 as markers of possible ACO but remain ambiguous as to whether thrombolysis should be considered in these cases.3

A meta-analysis of all thrombolysis trials involving over 1000 patients demonstrated that the absence of STE, described in those studies as ‘other abnormalities’, was not associated with a statistically significant reduction in mortality following thrombolytic therapy.8 This finding reinforced the use of STE as both a surrogate marker of ACO and a predictor of benefit from thrombolytic therapy, ultimately shaping the modern treatment paradigm, even for mechanical coronary intervention. This paradigm has been revisited in recent years. For instance, a meta-analysis demonstrated that more than half of patients with ACO (who would therefore be expected to benefit from thrombolysis) do not exhibit STE.9 Thus, STE is not a reliable marker for ACO.10

We do not aim to compare thrombolytic therapy with primary PCI or evaluate the competition between these strategies. We reassessed short-term all-cause mortality with thrombolysis in non-STE presentations, explicitly separating isolated ST-segment depression (STD) from other non-STE patterns. Our goal is to determine whether the ‘no benefit in non-STE’ conclusion truly stands—or whether a substantive evidence gap persists.

Methods

Study objective and outcome definition

The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to evaluate the impact of thrombolytic therapy on mortality in patients with ACS. We aimed to revisit the evidence from randomised controlled trials that tested thrombolysis in the context of acute myocardial infarction, regardless of the electrocardiographic presentation. We analysed outcomes in three strata defined a priori: (1) absence of STE (non-STE excluding isolated STD), constructed from trial categories labelled ‘normal ECG’ and ‘other abnormalities’; (2) isolated STD, as reported by each trial; and (3) STE (contextual/reference).

Mortality was defined as all-cause mortality during the follow-up period reported in each study, which included both in-hospital and post-discharge timeframes when available.

In the primary analysis, we conducted a systematic review including studies that compared thrombolysis versus placebo or no thrombolysis. We compared mortality outcomes among patients with STE, STD and those without STE.

We sought major bleeding and stroke/intracranial haemorrhage by ECG subgroup; reporting was inconsistent and did not permit pooling.

Protocol registration

The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO). The registration number is CRD42024573681. No funding was received for this study, and no authors have competing interests in this study. All data from this review can be obtained from the authors on request.

Eligibility criteria

We included any randomised clinical trial that tested thrombolysis in ACS within up to 24 hours of symptom onset. Eligible studies were those comparing thrombolytic therapy (using any thrombolytic agent) versus placebo or conventional therapy, including heparin. Studies that exclusively enrolled patients with STE were included without requiring subgroup data, as the entire population met the STE criteria. However, for trials that included both patients with and without STE, inclusion was restricted to those providing clearly reported mortality data stratified by electrocardiographic subgroups, such as presence of STE, STD or absence of STE, to ensure subgroup data could be extracted for analysis. Additionally, case reports, reviews, meta-analyses and observational studies were excluded. Language and year of publication were not criteria for exclusion. Importantly, ISIS-3 was excluded from the primary ECG-defined meta-analysis because its ‘uncertain diagnosis’ subgroup was not randomised under consistent ECG criteria and was subject to investigator-level selection. EMERAS was not included because mortality was not reported by ECG subgroup.

We adhered to the PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews and meta-analyses.11

Information sources and search strategy

We searched the MEDLINE, Scopus and Web of Science databases. Keywords and search terms included ‘myocardial infarction’, ‘STEMI’, ‘thrombolysis’, specific thrombolytic agents (eg, streptokinase, urokinase, tenecteplase, fibrinolytic agents, tissue plasminogen activator) and ‘mortality’. Detailed search strings are provided in the online supplemental material 1.

Selection and data collection process

Two researchers (J.K. and H.C.) independently performed the initial screening of titles and abstracts. Discrepancies were resolved by a third researcher (C.A.). In the subsequent full-text screening phase, two researchers (M.H. and JdA.) selected articles based on the inclusion criteria. Data screening was facilitated using the HubMeta software.12 Data extraction was performed in Excel, where mortality data were included. Relative risks were calculated based on the data provided in the papers when they were not readily available.

Data analysis

We conducted a meta-analysis using a random-effects model to account for potential heterogeneity across studies. We pooled risk ratios (RRs) using a random effects model for mortality outcomes and calculated 95% CIs to estimate the overall effect size. Heterogeneity across studies was quantified using the I² statistic, with values above 50% indicating moderate to high heterogeneity. Tau² was also calculated as a measure of between-study variance.13 To assess the presence of publication bias, we generated a funnel plot and applied formal statistical tests. Egger’s test and Begg’s test were also calculated.14

Our primary analysis was a pooled effect in the absence of STE (non-STE excluding isolated STD). STE and isolated STD strata were summarised for context.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

The risk of bias in included studies was assessed using the RoB 2 tool for randomised trials.15

Discussion

The importance of the FTT study cannot be overstated. It is the origin of the STEMI/Non-STEMI dichotomy and is the foundational study which restricts reperfusion therapy and especially thrombolytic therapy (given its risk) to those with STE. In our study, thrombolysis reduced short-term mortality in non-STE presentations excluding isolated STD, while no benefit was observed for isolated STD. When STD was pooled with other non-STE patterns in an exploratory analysis, the effect attenuated towards the null and heterogeneity increased, largely due to ISIS-3. This pattern indicates that the topic is not settled; rather, our study reveals an evidence gap in the foundational data informing current practice.

Given the discrepancies observed between our findings and those reported in the FTT meta-analysis,8 it is essential to compare with our results, as it remains the most frequently cited meta-analysis on this topic. We will explore key methodological differences and data handling practices that may explain these variations.

The FTT separated the subgroups as ‘bundle branch block’, ‘ST elevation in various walls’, ‘ST-segment depression (STD)’, ‘other abnormalities’, and ‘normal ECG’. Notably, there was no explicit ‘absence of STE’ category. In our meta-analysis, we defined the ‘absence of STE’ as the combined population from the ‘other abnormalities’ and ‘normal ECG’ groups across the trials where event extraction was possible. This approach was necessary to provide a consistent point of comparison across both datasets. To ensure a direct comparison with the FTT data, we evaluated the ‘absence of STE’ subgroup against both the FTT’s ‘other abnormalities’ and ‘normal ECG’ categories combined, as these groups represent patients without STE as the defining inclusion criterion. In FTT, in the subgroup defined as ‘other abnormalities’, 7951 patients were included, a number similar to ours, with an event rate of 5.2% in the thrombolysis group and 5.8% in the control group by the end of the truncated 35-day follow-up. When recalculating this difference using Fisher’s exact test, the p value was 0.28, indicating no statistical significance. The results in the FTT for the ‘normal ECG’ subgroup, which comprised 995 patients in the thrombolysis arm and 990 in the control arm, showed a mortality rate of 3.0% (30/995) in the thrombolysis group compared with 2.3% (23/990) in the control group.

A critical factor contributing to the differences between our findings and those reported in the FTT meta-analysis is the inclusion of the ISIS-3 trial,25 which accounted for approximately 15% of the total sample analysed in the FTT. The ISIS-3 trial was excluded from our initial search because it did not compare thrombolysis versus placebo or standard care but rather evaluated the efficacy of different thrombolytic agents against each other. However, a subset of approximately 9000 patients within ISIS-3, described as having an ‘uncertain diagnosis’ (defined as patients presenting either more than 6 hours after symptom onset or without definite STE on the initial ECG), was indeed assessed for thrombolytic therapy versus open-label placebo. Notably, these subgroup mortality data were not presented in the original ISIS-3 publication but instead extracted from FTT’s table 4 legend. The inclusion criteria for this subgroup were inconsistent, as the decision to classify a patient as having a ‘clear’ versus ‘uncertain” indication for thrombolysis was left entirely to the discretion of the treating physician, introducing a significant risk of selection bias. The variability in patient classification even within the same institution further undermines the reliability of the subgroup data.

When absence of STE and isolated STD were combined into a single non-STE group, the pooled effect with all trials was neutral (RR 1.01; 95% CI 0.80 to 1.26) with substantial heterogeneity (I²=66%). Leave-one-out removal of ISIS-3 eliminated heterogeneity (I²=0%) and yielded a non-significant point estimate favouring thrombolysis (RR 0.89; 95% CI 0.78 to 1.03), underscoring ISIS-3’s disproportionate influence. The inclusion of the ISIS-3 trial, with its methodological limitations and inconsistent subgroup definitions, would alone be sufficient to alter the primary conclusions of our meta-analysis. Since it was included in the FTT, its disproportionate influence may have significantly impacted the results and shaped historical recommendations on thrombolysis in AMI, potentially contributing to a longstanding underestimation of the benefits of thrombolysis in patients without clear STE and in turn also underestimating the benefits of any reperfusion therapy, including PCI.

Similarly, the EMERAS trial,26 also included in the FTT, was excluded from our meta-analysis due to its design, which enrolled both patients with and without STE but did not provide subgroup-specific mortality data.

Another important factor that must be considered is the timing of symptom onset among the trials that included patients without STE. The benefit of thrombolysis is highly time-sensitive, particularly in the presence of ACO. Trials permitting the inclusion of patients with non-STE patterns were consistently those that also allowed the enrolment of patients with more prolonged symptom duration. For example, GISSI included patients up to 12 hours after chest pain onset, ISIS-2 permitted inclusion up to 24 hours and, most notably, the LATE trial specifically enrolled patients between 6 and 24 hours after symptom onset. The only exception was the USIM trial, which included patients within less than 4 hours of symptom onset; however, it contributed the smallest number of patients to the analysis.

It is not possible to perform a sub-analysis of the ‘absence of STE’ subgroup stratified by time to treatment or to calculate a median delay across the included trials. However, among ISIS-2 and LATE, approximately 26% of patients received thrombolysis more than 12 hours after symptom onset. In these trials, it is plausible that patients without STE were randomised and treated later than those with clearer STE, further delaying the intervention. Given the well-established time dependency of thrombolytic efficacy, this delayed treatment in the non-STE group may have diminished the observed benefit, potentially underestimating the true effect size for thrombolysis in patients with non-STE presentations.

In our meta-analysis, the presence of STD was not associated with a significant survival benefit when treated with thrombolysis. Among the 795 patients treated with thrombolysis and 793 controls, the pooled RR was 1.074 (95% CI 0.874 to 1.320), with mortality rates of 19.3% in the thrombolysis group and 17.9% in the control group, and no heterogeneity detected (I² = 0%). The lack of benefit observed in the STD subgroup is particularly noteworthy and may be influenced by historical limitations in electrocardiographic classification and study design. STD in the anterior leads V1-V4 is now recognised as a marker for lateral wall infarction, previously referred to as posterior wall infarction.27 This pattern can reflect ACO involving the circumflex artery or other branches, conditions which could plausibly benefit from thrombolytic therapy. However, in the historical trials included in both our analysis and the FTT, the recognition of such patterns was not well established.

Consequently, this heterogeneous grouping may have diluted the potential benefit of thrombolysis in patients with true ACO-related STD, as these high-risk patterns were mixed with non-ischaemic or less critical patterns of STD. Conversely, trials contributing to the ‘absence of STE’ stratum likely enrolled patients with equivocal but ischaemic ECGs—a mix that plausibly included ACO. Historical ‘STD’ bins were broader and noisier: true ACOs may have been pooled with diffuse or low-risk STD patterns due to type 2 myocardial infarction. Such misclassification would dilute any thrombolysis effect in the STD stratum.

In the presence of the STE group (13 656 patients treated with thrombolysis and 13 576 controls), thrombolysis also yielded a statistically significant mortality reduction, with a pooled RR of 0.762 (95% CI, 0.672 to 0.865), and event rates of 9.4% in the thrombolysis group compared with 12.3% in the control group. Heterogeneity was moderate in this group (I² = 50%). When comparing the presence and absence of STE groups, no significant difference in treatment effect was identified (p=0.6708). This finding suggests that thrombolysis conferred a consistent mortality benefit across both STE and non-STE subgroups, challenging the traditional interpretation that thrombolysis is only effective in patients with clear STE.

Finally, these trials predate routine P2Y12 inhibition, low-molecular-weight heparin, high-intensity statins and early invasive strategies; absolute risks and net clinical benefit likely differ today. Modern recognition of type 2 MI would further dilute any potential benefit and increase bleeding risk. Major bleeding and stroke were not consistently reported by ECG subgroup and could not be meta-analysed, a key limitation for clinical translation. Moreover, diffuse STD with STE in aVR is now recognised to only be due to ACS in a small minority of cases.28 29

Taken together, our findings establish that a substantive evidence gap exists: selected non-STE phenotypes—excluding isolated, broadly defined STD—can derive mortality benefit from thrombolysis, yet the legacy data are too heterogeneous and era-specific to close the question. Because ECG definitions, adjunctive therapies and triage pathways have evolved—and because artificial intelligence-enabled ECG phenotyping can now enrich for ACO—guidelines should recognise this as a persistent evidence gap. Although there are contemporary randomised trials in non-STE phenotypes, with prespecified ECG criteria which purport to demonstrate absence of benefit from reperfusion, in all of these negative trials, patients with persistent pain were excluded, or the intervention was not rapid, or both.30 Trials with more rapid intervention showed benefit.31 32 New trials are needed which study patients with ECG findings of ACO, persistent symptoms and rapid time windows, with rigorous bleeding/stroke adjudication.

Limitations

One of the primary limitations of this meta-analysis is the observed heterogeneity across some of the included studies, particularly within the STE group. This heterogeneity can be attributed to several factors, including differences in the time frame for inclusion after symptom onset. Some trials enrolled patients presenting within 6 hours, while others extended the inclusion window to 24 hours, which may have influenced outcomes due to the time-sensitive nature of thrombolysis efficacy. Additionally, variation in thrombolytic agents used across the trials was also a factor. The heterogeneity within the STE group also reflects our inclusion of smaller trials compared with the FTT, which restricted its analysis to studies enrolling at least 1000 patients. However, the benefit of thrombolysis in patients with STE, the group in which heterogeneity was most significant, is already well-established and was not the primary focus of our meta-analysis.

Importantly, there were no standardised definitions for either STE or STD. None of the included trials specified how STE was measured, whether it was assessed at the J-point, or delayed 40 ms, 60 ms or 80 ms after the J-point. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether the measurements were taken relative to the PQ junction (QRS onset) or the TP segment (P-wave onset). The absence of a consistent measurement method introduces potential variability and limits the comparability of results across trials. At the time, there were many competing recommendations for measurement, and we know that the method of measurement has profound implications for the ECG diagnosis of STEMI.33 34 Additionally, original ECG adjudication materials are largely unavailable in key legacy trials. For example, in ISIS-2, randomisation and data capture were conducted by telephone; source ECGs and expert annotations are not retained. This is a limitation not only of our analysis but of the entire evidence base that has informed guidelines.

We did not specifically test the effect of time to thrombolysis on the outcomes. However, given the well-established principle that thrombolysis is more effective when administered earlier during myocardial infarction, this limitation is unlikely to alter the conclusions of our study. The trials contributing patients to the ‘absence of STE’ group, namely LATE, GISSI and ISIS-2, were the same trials that included patients with longer symptom duration prior to thrombolysis (up to 24 hours). If earlier treatment had been performed, the expected impact would likely have been an even greater reduction in mortality rather than a diminished benefit, further reinforcing the observed positive effect in this subgroup.

Importantly, the trials predate routine dual antiplatelet therapy, low-molecular-weight heparin, high-intensity statins, radial access and early invasive pathways; absolute risks and trade-offs with thrombolysis may differ today.

Finally, there is temporal bias. Since most of the included trials were conducted more than three decades ago, the external validity of the findings is very likely to be limited by changes in clinical practice, advances in electrocardiographic interpretation and improvements in reperfusion strategies since the original studies were published. However, this bias exists precisely because thrombolysis in non-STE ACS has been considered a settled topic, leading to a lack of more contemporary trials addressing this question directly.

Conclusion

Across legacy randomised trials, thrombolysis reduced short-term all-cause mortality in non-STE presentations excluding isolated ST-segment depression, whereas isolated STD did not benefit. Crucially, the apparent non-STE signal proved sensitive to inclusion of methodologically problematic data (eg, ISIS-3 ‘uncertain diagnosis’) and arose from studies with heterogeneous ECG definitions, delayed treatment windows and pre-DAPT/LMWH care. By demonstrating these vulnerabilities, our study exposes a substantive evidence gap in the foundation of current contraindications to thrombolysis in non-STE. Modern, adequately powered randomised trials—using prespecified ECG phenotypes, rapid treatment windows and rigorous bleeding and stroke adjudication—are now needed to resolve this question.
