Macrophage interferon regulatory factor 7 aggravates the progression of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis
Authors: Xuhao Ni, Qingfa Bu, Yuan Liang, Bozhou Pan, Jian Wang, Qingxiang Xu, Lei Liu, Qi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) represents a growing clinical burden with increasing prevalence and lacking approved pharmacological therapies. Interferon regulatory factor 7 (IRF7) is a central mediator of type I interferon-dependent immune responses, yet the precise mechanisms by which macrophage-expressed IRF7 shapes inflammatory and metabolic events during MASH pathogenesis remain incompletely defined. This study aimed to dissect the functional role of IRF7 in MASH development. We assessed IRF7 expression in human and mouse liver specimens, and utilized myeloid-specific Irf7-deficient mice fed with high-fat high-cholesterol diet (HFHCD) or methionine-choline-deficient diet (MCD) to recapitulate MASH pathology. IRF7 was significantly elevated in MASH-affected livers, and myeloid-specific Irf7 deletion markedly mitigated MASH progression. Mechanistically, loss of IRF7 blunted IFN-α and IFN-β production, suppressing macrophage-derived type I interferon-dependent immune cascades. IRF7 directly bound to the promoters of pro-inflammatory Tnfa and Il1b to boost their transcription, and interacted with SMAD3 to activate Tgfb1 transcription. The E3 ubiquitin ligase HUWE1 mediated IRF7 monoubiquitination to enhance its nuclear translocation, driving transcription of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic genes. This molecular cascade aggravated hepatic steatosis and triggered hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation, accelerating MASH deterioration. Pharmacological IRF7 inhibition effectively counteracted these pathological alterations. Collectively, myeloid IRF7 fuels MASH progression by amplifying macrophage-mediated liver inflammation, suggesting IRF7 as a promising therapeutic target against MASH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s43556-026-00601-6.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has become the most prevalent chronic liver disease globally [1]. Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), which is defined by the presence of hepatic steatosis, lobular inflammation, and hepatocyte ballooning, may or may not be accompanied by perisinusoidal fibrosis [2], is associated with progressive liver injury, leading to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [3]. Most MASLD cases are asymptomatic, heterogeneous and challenging to diagnose, even at advanced stages [4]. Existing strategies, including long-term weight loss and physical activity, are challenging to sustain, and only a few approved pharmacological treatments are available [5, 6]. Therefore, it is crucial to uncover the molecular mechanisms driving MASH progression to aid in the development of effective treatment options.

Macrophages play a critical role in MASLD progression by producing proinflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-6, ect [7]. During MASH, inflammatory signals recruit circulating monocytes to the liver, where they mature into monocyte-derived macrophages, causing a marked expansion of the liver macrophage population [8]. Our previous studies revealed that type I interferon (IFN) signaling in macrophages plays a key regulatory role in MASH progression [9, 10]. Activation of type I and II interferon receptors (IFNAR and IFNGR) upon ligand binding triggers signaling cascades involving Janus family protein tyrosine kinases (JAK1, TYK2), resulting in the site-specific tyrosine phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT2 [11–13]. STATs, in conjunction with IRF-9, assemble into a heterotrimeric transcription factor complex known as ISGF3, that can perform nucleus translocation and combine the IFN-stimulated response element (ISRE) to initiate the activation of target genes [12, 14]. To date, the specific mechanisms through which type I IFN signaling regulates macrophage immune responses during MASH remain largely unelucidated.

Interferon regulatory factors (IRFs) are critical transcriptional regulators of IFN signaling and innate or adaptive immune responses modulated by macrophages [11]. Among IRF1 ~ 9, IRF7 is a key regulator of type I IFN signaling. Phosphorylated IRF7 translocates into the nucleus binding to ISREs in target genes, promoting cytokine production, including IFN-α, IL-6 and TNF-α [15–18]. It has been reported that IRF7 plays a crucial role in heart fibrosis [19]. IRF7 has also been found to interact with SMAD3, a central mediator of TGF-β signaling related to systemic sclerosis [20]. Furthermore, Research has shown that single nucleotide polymorphisms of IRF7 are linked to systemic sclerosis and HCV infection in patients with cirrhosis [21, 22]. Another study demonstrated that the deficiency of IRF7 inhibits insulin resistance and obesity [23]. However, the role and specific mechanisms by which IRF7 in macrophages regulates MASH progression remain unknown.

Herein, our study investigated the role and mechanisms by which macrophage IRF7 regulates MASH progression using two different murine MASH models, which revealed that IRF7 expression is upregulated in hepatic macrophages of HFHCD- or MCD-fed mice. Of note, correlations between gene expression and histological scores can’t distinguish gene-specific effects from broad hepatic inflammatory activation. We prioritized IRF7 for functional interrogation because it is a central upstream regulator of type-I interferon signalling, motivating us further genetic investigation of its non-redundant role in MASH pathogenesis. Macrophage-specific Irf7 deletion protected mice from HFHCD- and MCD-induced MASH. Irf7 knockout suppressed macrophage type I IFN-dependent immune responses. Moreover, IRF7 directly bound the promoters of Tnfa and Il1b to drive pro-inflammatory transcription, and interacted with SMAD3 to transactivate Tgfb1. Then, the pro-inflammatory and profibrotic cytokine milieu promoted hepatic stellate cell activation and hepatic steatosis. The E3 ligase HUWE1 monoubiquitinated IRF7, facilitating its nuclear translocation and downstream transcription. Pharmacological IRF7 inhibition blocked MASH progression, identifying IRF7 as a promising therapeutic target for MASH.

Results

IRF7 expression is upregulated and IRF7 is activated in the livers of patients and mice with MASH

While IRF7 deficiency has been reported to prevent diet-induced obesity and insulin resistance [20], its specific role in modulating macrophage activation during MASH remains inadequately understood. We compared IRF7 expression in liver tissues between 40 MASH patients and 40 normal patients (Table S1). Elevated macrophage quantity and gene expression of IRF7 was detected in liver tissues from patients with MASH (Fig. 1a, b). Additionally, hepatic IRF7 expression showed a positive correlation with serum transaminase levels (ALT and AST) and inflammatory markers (Tnfa and Il6) (Fig. 1c). Notably, dual immunofluorescence staining of liver tissues from MASH patients revealed prominent colocalization of activated IRF7 with the macrophage marker CD68. In contrast, no significant overlap was observed between activated IRF7 and markers of other cell types, such as Albumin (hepatocytes), Desmin (hepatic stellate cells), CD31 (liver sinusoidal endothelial cells), and CK19 (cholangiocytes). Further analysis confirmed that in MASH liver tissues, activated IRF7 was specifically expressed in macrophages and predominantly localized to their nuclei (Fig. 1d). Subsequent animal experiments revealed increased gene expression of Irf7 in macrophages extracted from the livers of MASH model mice (Fig. 1e). Furthermore, to explore the clinical relevance of the above findings, patients with NASH were divided into 2 groups, an IRF7 high expression group (20/40, IRF7High) and an IRF7 low expression group (20/40, IRF7Low), according to the median value of IRF7 detected by qPCR. The degree of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis was significantly lower in the IRF7Low group than the IRF7High group (Fig. S1a). Moreover, Pearson correlation analysis revealed that NAFLD activity score and Fibrosis score were positively correlated with IRF7 mRNA level (Fig. S1b). Macrophage-specific Irf7 deficiency attenuates MASH in mice

To investigate the involvement of macrophage Irf7 in MASH, we analyzed the scRNA-seq data of the livers from MASH mice (GSE129516). Thirteen main cell types expressing known marker genes were identified including B cells (early B cell factor 1, CD79A antigen, CD79B antigen) (Ebf1, Cd79a, Cd79b), cholangiocytes (Clusterin, secreted phosphoprotein 1, transmembrane 4 superfamily member 4) (Clu, Spp1, Tm4sf4), dendritic cells (DC) (cystatin C, N-acylethanolamine acid amidase, palmitoyl-protein thioesterase 1) (Cst3, Naaa, Ppt1), endothelial cells (aquaporin 1, EH-domain containing 3, kinase insert domain protein receptor) (Aqp1, Ehd3, Kdr), fibroblasts (insulin-like growth factor binding protein 5, complement component 3, secretory leukocyte peptidase inhibitor) (Igfbp5, C3, Slpi), hepatocytes (fatty acid binding protein 1, major urinary protein 20, apolipoprotein A-II) (Fabp1, Mup20, Apoa2), hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) (decorin, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12, gelsolin) (Dcn, Cxcl12, Gsn), KC (CD5 antigen-like, C-type lectin domain family 4, member f, WAP four-disulfide core domain 17) (Cd5l, Clec4f, Wfdc17), MDM (lysozyme 2, placenta-specific 8, lectin, galactose binding, soluble 3) (Lyz2, Plac8, Lgals3), natural killer cells (NK) (granzyme A, chemokine (C motif) ligand 1, killer cell lectin-like receptor subfamily K, member 1) (Gzma, Xcl1, Klrk1), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC) (sialic acid binding Ig-like lectin H, cytochrome c oxidase subunit 6A2, interferon regulatory factor 8) (Siglech, Cox6a2, Irf8), plasma cells (immunoglobulin joining chain, immunoglobulin heavy constant alpha, immunoglobulin kappa constant) (Jchain, Igha, Igkc), and T cells (C–C motif chemokine ligand 5, CD3 antigen, gamma polypeptide, natural killer cell group 7 sequence) (Ccl5, Cd3g, Nkg7) (Fig. 2a, b). Further analysis demonstrated that the percentage of infiltrating macrophages significantly increased in MASH tissues compared with the Chow (Fig. 2c). Moreover, Lyz2 mainly expressed in infiltrating macrophages and Kupffer cells (Fig. 2d). The RNA expression level of Irf7 in infiltrating macrophages and Kupffer cells significantly increased in MASH tissues compared with the Chow (Fig. 2e) and transcriptional level of Irf7 were higher in infiltrating macrophages and Kupffer cells than other cell types (Fig. 2f). Therefore, our study developed macrophage-specific Irf7 knockout mice with Lyz2-Cre system (Fig. 2g). We developed macrophage-specific Irf7 knockout mice by crossbreeding Irf7fl/fl mice with Lyz2-Cre mice to examine whether the conditional deletion of Irf7 in macrophages alleviates MASH in these mice. The results demonstrated that Irf7ΔMϕ mice were significantly protected against MASH, as evidenced by mitigated liver injury with improved liver architecture and reduced Oil red O, α-SMA staining and F4/80 inflammatory cell infiltration in liver samples from HFHCD-fed Irf7ΔMϕ mice compared to Irf7fl/fl mice fed the HFHCD diet (Fig. 3a). Moreover, Irf7ΔMϕ mice fed with HFHCD displayed significantly lower body weight, less NAFLD activity scores (NAS), hepatic triglycerides (TG), lower serum ALT, blood glucose, TNF-α, serum GTT and ITT levels compared to Irf7fl/fl mice fed with HFHCD (Fig. 3b-g and S2). Mitigated liver injury and reduced Oil red O, α-SMA staining and F4/80 inflammatory cell infiltration were observed in MCD-fed Irf7ΔMϕ mice relative to MCD-fed Irf7fl/fl mice (Fig. 3h). Furthermore, significant reductions in NAS scores, serum ALT and hepatic TG levels were observed in Irf7ΔMϕ mice compared to Irf7fl/fl mice under MCD diet conditions (Fig. 3i-k). IRF7 regulates the macrophage type I interferon signaling loop

To investigate the role of IRF7 in MASH, LPS-primed bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) from either Irf7fl/fl or Irf7ΔMϕ mice were treated with palmitic acid/oleic acid (PAOA) for 48 h. Following treatment, Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed with transcriptome RNA-seq data on the Irf7fl/fl and Irf7ΔMϕ BMDMs (Fig. 4a). GO analysis revealed that these genes were primarily enriched in interferon-mediated immune responses (Fig. 4b). RNA sequencing and mRNA expression data revealed significantly lower IFN-α (Ifna1) and IFN-β (Ifnb1) expression in activated Irf7ΔMϕ BMDMs compared to Irf7fl/fl BMDMs (Fig. 4c, d). ELISA analysis of the secreted protein levels of IFN-α and IFN-β confirmed these findings (Fig. 4e). Previous studies have demonstrated that IFN-α and IFN-β might bind to the IFN-I receptor (IFNAR) to activate JAK/STAT signaling and promote the binding of STAT1/STAT2 with IRF9, eventually inducing IRF7 expression [12, 24]. Our results demonstrated that stimulating LPS-primed and PAOA-treated Irf7fl/fl or Irf7ΔMϕ BMDMs with IFN-α or IFN-β further promoted macrophage Irf7 expression (Fig. 4f) by activating JAK/STAT signaling and promoting the binding of STAT1/STAT2 with IRF9, eventually inducing IRF7 expression (Fig. 4g). The use of the JAK1 inhibitor itacitinib revealed that IFNAR activation modulated STAT1/STAT2/IRF9 signaling via JAK/STAT signaling, which ultimately promoted IRF7 expression (Fig. 4g, h). Macrophage IRF7 promotes hepatocyte lipid accumulation and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression by regulating the macrophage inflammatory response

To investigate whether IRF7-driven macrophage inflammation influences hepatic lipid homeostasis, we first analyzed RNA sequencing data. The results showed that the TNF signaling pathway was significantly suppressed in Irf7ΔMϕ BMDMs versus Irf7fl/fl BMDMs primed with LPS, following 48 h of PAOA exposure (Fig. 5a). Downregulated genes analysis of TNF signaling pathway revealed that several key proinflammatory genes, including Tnfa and Il6, were significantly less expressed in the LPS-primed Irf7ΔMϕ BMDMs than in Irf7fl/fl BMDMs exposed to PAOA (Fig. 5b). Further bioinformatics analysis identified a putative IRF7 binding site (−1862/−1849) in the murine Tnfa promoter, which was validated through chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis (Fig. 5c, d). We also found that IRF7 activated Tnfa transcription in a concentration-dependent manner, as shown by the elevated Tnfa-luciferase reporter activity in BMDMs primed with LPS and co-transfected with pGL3-Tnfa-luciferase and Ad-Irf7 (Fig. 5e). Additionally, bioinformatics analysis of the Il6 promoter demonstrated another potential IRF7 binding site (−897/−884), which was validated by ChIP (Fig. 5f). Similarly, IRF7 stimulated Il6 transcription in a concentration-dependent manner, as shown by the elevated Il6-luciferase reporter expression in BMDMs primed with LPS and co-transfected with pGL3-Il6-luciferase and Ad-Irf7 (Fig. 5g). We then assessed the gene expression of Tnfa and Il6 in Irf7fl/fl or Irf7ΔMϕ BMDMs primed with LPS and incubated with PAOA, finding that macrophage-specific Irf7 deletion significantly suppressed Tnfa and Il6 expression (Fig. 5h). To evaluate the effects of TNF-α and IL-6 derived from macrophages on steatohepatitis, we cocultured LPS-primed Irf7ΔMϕ or Irf7fl/fl BMDMs with primary Irf7fl/fl hepatocytes (Fig. 5i). The results showed that macrophage-specific Irf7 knockout markedly decreased hepatocyte lipid deposition and inflammatory responses (Fig. 5j and S3a). Macrophage IRF7 promotes HSC activation by interacting with SMAD3 to regulate Tgfb1 transcription

A previous study identified IRF7 as a protein that interacts with SMAD3, a critical mediator TGF-β1 signaling involved in collagen synthesis in systemic sclerosis [20]. Our previous study demonstrated the important regulatory effect of macrophages on HSC activation [9, 10, 25]. To examine the specific role of macrophage IRF7 in regulating HSC activation during MASH, we performed several experiments. Dual immunofluorescence staining revealed that IRF7 activation increased and that activated IRF7 strongly colocalized with SMAD3 in stimulated macrophages, especially in the nucleus (Fig. 6a). Coimmunoprecipitation assays revealed that activated IRF7 bound with SMAD3 in stimulated macrophages (Fig. 6b). To map the domains mediating the interaction between SMAD3 and p-IRF7, a series of deleting mutants were expressed in BMDMs (Fig. 6c, d). Co-IP assays (Fig. 6d) showed that the region (amino acids 251–425) of SMAD3 bound to p-IRF7. To explore whether IRF7 regulates Tgfb1 transcription via SMAD3 signaling, we overexpressed SMAD3 in stimulated macrophages and found that SMAD3 overexpression in Irf7ΔMϕ macrophages promoted Tgfb1 expression (Fig. 6e). Subsequent experiments demonstrated that Smad3 knockdown in Irf7fl/fl macrophages significantly inhibited Tgfb1 expression, whereas overexpressing Irf7 did not increase Tgfb1 transcription (Fig. 6f). The findings suggest that IRF7 modulates Tgfb1 transcription through interaction with SMAD3 signaling. To further elucidate the mechanism by which SMAD3 influences Tgfb1 transcription, bioinformatics analysis of the murine Tgfb1 promoter revealed a potential SMAD3 binding site (−163/−154). This prediction was validated through ChIP assay results (Fig. 6g, h). SMAD3 stimulated Tgfb1 transcriptional activity in a concentration-dependent manner, as shown by the elevated in Tgfb1-luciferase reporter gene expression in BMDMs stimulated with LPS and co-transfected with the pGL3-Tgfb1-luciferase vector and Ad-Smad3 (Fig. 6i). To confirm the impact of macrophage IRF7 on HSC activation, primary mouse HSCs were cultured with or without TGF-β1 (8 ng/ml) in the presence of CM from LPS-primed Irf7fl/fl or Irf7ΔMϕ BMDMs treated with PAOA for 48 h (Fig. 6j). Gene expression analysis of fibrotic markers in cocultured HSCs revealed that macrophage Irf7 knockout significantly inhibited HSCs activation (Fig. 6k). To investigate the mechanism by which IRF7 regulates macrophage activation during MASH, immunoprecipitation (IP) assays coupled with mass spectrometry screening were conducted to identify proteins interacting with IRF7 in macrophages (Fig. 7a). Furthermore, the expression of profibrogenic markers such as Acta2, Col1a1, and Timp1 was downregulated following the administration of Irf7 siRNA. (Fig. 8c). Discussion

In this study, we identified the reduction of IRF7 in macrophage as an innovative therapeutic target for MASH. Experiments in vivo indicated that IRF7 significantly expressed in the liver tissues of MASH patients and that macrophage-specific Irf7 knockout attenuated MASH in mice. Furthermore, subsequent experiments demonstrated that Irf7 knockout suppressed macrophage type I IFN-dependent immune responses. In addition, IRF7 targeted the promoter of inflammatory genes (Tnfa and Il1b) and promoted proinflammatory gene transcription. Moreover, IRF7 bound to SMAD3 and targeted the Tgfb1 promoter, thus promoting Tgfb1 transcription. Ultimately, IRF7 enhanced the secretion of proinflammatory and profibrotic cytokines, leading to hepatic steatosis and activation of HSC. Additionally, the ubiquitin ligase E3 HUWE1 bound to IRF7, increasing IRF7 monoubiquitination and entry into the nucleus to promote downstream transcription. IRF7 disruption blocked MASH progression. These results highlight the potential of targeting IRF7 or its regulatory pathways to develop effective therapies.

Macrophage heterogeneity plays a crucial role in hepatic diseases, including MASH [27]. Liver macrophages are mainly classified into two major categories: resident kupffer cells (KCs) and infiltrating myeloid-derived macrophages (BMDMs), with distinct differences in origin, localization, phenotypic characteristics and functions. KCs are the major resident macrophage population in the steady-state liver, maintaining their numbers through self-renewal. The elevated Irf7 expression in Kupffer cells may promote the activation of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways. However, during MASH pathogenesis, KCs undergo massive apoptosis and depletion due to lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis, resulting in severe functional exhaustion. In contrast, circulating bone marrow-derived monocytes are extensively recruited into the liver via the CCL2/CCR2 chemokine axis, differentiate into BMDMs, and gradually replace KCs as the predominant hepatic macrophage population in progressive MASH, which lays the foundation for their dominant role in disease progression [27]. Therefore, our study mainly focus on macrophages in general. With the transition of MASH from the early stage to the middle stage, infiltrating macrophages are predominantly polarized into the pro-inflammatory macrophage subtype, which secretes cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, exacerbating steatosis and inflammation [28]. Our previous study demonstrated that the intimate crosstalk between macrophages and hepatocytes plays a key role in steatohepatitis, with proinflammatory macrophages potentially promoting hepatocyte adipogenesis [25]. Advanced liver fibrosis is a critical prognostic indicator for liver-related outcomes and overall mortality in MASH patients [29]. Increased expression of TGF-β1 and HSC activation are key drivers of fibrogenesis during MASH progression [30]. Our previous study also demonstrated that macrophages might secrete profibrotic cytokines, such as TGF-β1, which promote HSC activation during MASH [25]. The results indicated that macrophage-specific Irf7 knockout attenuated MASH by targeting inflammatory gene (Tnfa, Il1b) and profibrotic gene (Tgfb1) promoters and inhibiting proinflammatory gene transcription, which eventually decreased proinflammatory and profibrotic cytokine secretion, leading to less hepatic steatosis and HSC activation.

Interferons (IFNs) have been thoroughly investigated, primarily in the immune response to viral infections. IFNs include 2 categories: type I IFNs (IFN-α and IFN-β) and type II IFNs (IFN-γ). Type I is synthesized by many kinds of cells defensing virus, whereas type II is secreted mostly by activated T cells and NK cells [31, 32]. Type I IFNs can be triggered in differentiated plasmacytoid dendritic cells through activation of the Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) subfamily [33, 34]. Our recent studies suggest that the type I IFN response is crucial for the progression of MASH [9, 10]. IRF7, a novel member of the IRF family, is ubiquitously expressed in the type I IFN response, which is fully regulated by IFN-α/β signaling. A previous study demonstrated that IRF7 was implicated in the development of metabolic abnormalities and that IRF7 deficiency prevented diet-induced obesity and insulin resistance, suggesting that IRF7 might be a new treatment target for obesity and type 2 diabetes [23]. However, the role and specific mechanisms by which macrophage IRF7 regulates MASH progression remain unknown. Our study revealed that macrophage-specific IRF7 deficiency suppressed macrophage inflammation by suppressing the type I IFN response, which ultimately suppressed the secretion of IFN-α and IFN-β by macrophages.

Posttranslational modifications of proteins regulate many biological processes, and the disruption of posttranslational modifications is related to disease. Ubiquitination is a posttranslational modification that regulates numerous biological pathways. The process requires three enzymes: enzyme E1 for ubiquitin-activating, enzyme E2 for ubiquitin-conjugating and enzyme E3, ubiquitin-ligase. E3s are crucial for selecting substrates. Evidence suggests that ubiquitin E3 ligases perform critical regulatory roles in MASH progression [35, 36]. To explore whether E3s affect IRF7 function, we employed mass spectrometry and revealed that the ubiquitin ligase E3 HUWE1 bound to IRF7. Earlier research showed that HUWE1 deficiency in hepatocytes protects against MASH by altering lipid homeostasis [37]. Another study demonstrated that HUWE1 promotes the activation of inflammasome and facilitate the host immunity against bacterial infection [38]. Furthermore, HUWE1 was shown to monoubiquitinate H2AX to promote signaling at stalled forks [39]. To clarify how HUWE1 regulates IRF7 function, we performed ubiquitination analyses and found that HUWE1 mediated the nondegradative monoubiquitination of IRF7, promoted its nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity, and triggered the upregulated expression of downstream inflammatory and profibrotic genes at the transcription level.

Despite the important role of IRF7 in MASH progression, limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our functional evidence is largely derived from mouse genetic models, and the causal role of macrophage IRF7 in human MASH remains to be formally validated. Although we observed correlations between hepatic IRF7 expression and human NAFLD-activity and fibrosis scores, human liver tissue analyses were correlative and cannot recapitulate the cell-specific causal effects observed in myeloid-specific Irf7-deficient mice. Second, we focused on macrophage-intrinsic IRF7 function and we cannot fully exclude potential contributions of IRF7 in other hepatic cell types (e.g. hepatocytes, hepatic stellate cells) in MASH. Third, while we identified HUWE1-dependent monoubiquitination as a critical post-translational modification controlling IRF7 nuclear translocation, we have not fully dissected whether other E3 ligases or deubiquitinating enzymes also modulate IRF7 activity in MASH. Finally, our therapeutic inference that IRF7 inhibition improve MASH is based on genetic knockout evidence, pharmacological targeting of IRF7 still requires further pre-clinical testing to evaluate drug delivery, efficacy and safety before clinical translation.

Overall, our study elucidates the role of IRF7 in the progression of MASH. Irf7 deficiency in macrophages attenuated MASH by inhibiting macrophage type I IFN-dependent immune responses. Moreover, IRF7 targeted the promoter of inflammatory genes (Tnfa and Il1b) and promoted proinflammatory gene transcription. Moreover, IRF7 bound to SMAD3 and targeted the Tgfb1 promoter, thus promoting Tgfb1 transcription. Finally, IRF7 facilitated the production of inflammatory and fibrotic cytokines, leading to hepatic steatosis and activation of HSC. In addition, the ubiquitin ligase E3 HUWE1 bound to IRF7, increasing IRF7 monoubiquitination and entry into the nucleus to promote downstream transcription. IRF7 inhibition blocked MASH progression. These findings suggest that the suppression of IRF7 expression might be an effective therapeutic strategy for MASH management.

Materials and methods

Human liver samples

Liver samples were obtained from 40 MASH patients for gene expression analysis and pathological section examination. As controls, 40 samples were obtained from individuals without hepatic steatosis, who were treated for hepatic hemangioma at the Jiangsu Province Hospital (Table S1). The control group included individuals without diabetes, alcohol consumption, viral hepatitis, or other liver conditions. Two pathologists with extensive experience, unaware of the clinical NAFLD activity score (NAS), independently evaluated all liver specimens. NAS score ≥ 5 was used to classify patients as likely having MASH. Table S1 presents the clinical features of MASH patients. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu Province Hospital (2020-SRFA-288). Before liver biopsy or surgery, informed consents for tissue analysis were obtained from all participants. All investigations were carried out following government policies and the Declaration of Helsinki and Istanbul.

Experimental animal models

Male 8-week-old WT, FloxP-Irf7 (Irf7fl/fl), and Lyz2-Cre Irf7 knockout (Irf7fl/fl; Lyz2-Cre) mice with a C57BL/6 genetic background (purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd.) were utilized for the experiments. The mice were maintained in a SPF environment and provided with sterilized food and water. Hepatic fibrosis models were developed by feeding mice an HFHCD or an MCD diet. Briefly, the mice were randomly divided into four groups: one group received 16 weeks of NCD (n = 6–8/group), another group was fed NCD for 8 weeks (n = 6–8/group), a third group was fed HFHCD (Research Diets, Inc.) for 16 weeks (n = 6–8/group), and the final group was fed MCD (Research Diets, Inc.) for 8 weeks (n = 6–8/group). Liver tissues were then collected for further analysis. Following the manufacturer’s guidelines, Irf7 siRNA or scrambled siRNA (Invitrogen, Stockholm, Sweden) was prepared with mannose-conjugated polymers (Polyplus-transfection, France) and delivered to the mice through tail vein (2 mg/kg). The sequences of Irf7 siRNA: sense, 5’-GGGAUCCAGUUGAUCCGCAUAAGGU-3’; antisense, 5’-ACCUUAUGCGGAUCAACUGGAUCCC-3’. Upon completion of the treatment period, liver tissues were harvested. For mice with HFHCD diet, siRNA was injected every 3 days after 8 weeks of HFHCD feeding, while after 4 weeks for the MCD group. For mice with MCD diet, either saline or liraglutide (200 µg/kg, HY-P0014, MCE, Shanghai, China) was injected daily after 4 weeks for the MCD group, until the end of the animal modeling period. After in vivo siRNA administration, single-cell suspensions are separated through liver perfusion, and various cell types are sorted by gradient centrifugation. The expression changes of target genes in different cells are quantitatively detected using qRT-PCR, in order to accurately assess their knockdown efficiency in each cell type. Body weight was longitudinally monitored throughout the experimental period. Mice were weighed at regular intervals, and serial body-weight values were recorded for each animal. Daily food intake was monitored for one week by daily weighing of cage-rack food, and values were normalized to kilocalories per day (kcal/day). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with guidelines of the IACUC of Nanjing Medical University (IACUC-2304026).

GTT and ITT experiments

For intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (GTT), mice were fasted overnight (12 h) with free access to water. Baseline blood glucose was measured from tail-tip venous blood using a glucometer, followed by intraperitoneal injection of glucose (2 g/kg body weight). Blood glucose levels were sequentially determined at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min post-injection. For insulin tolerance test (ITT), mice were fasted for 6 h before intraperitoneal administration of insulin (0.75 units/kg body weight). Blood glucose was detected at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. Serum samples were collected at designated time points for insulin quantification. GTT-AUC and ITT-AUC were calculated using the trapezoidal rule.

Cell isolation, culture and treatment

Primary hepatocytes, liver macrophages, hepatic stellate cells, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, and cholangiocytes were extracted using the method described earlier [25, 40]. Cells were treated with PA (500 μM; Sigma Aldrich) and OA (1000 μM; Sigman Aldrich). BSA used as a carrier control. Bone marrow cells were isolated from the femurs and tibias of male mice and flushed with DMEM. The cells were cultured at a concentration of 3 × 106/mL in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 20 ng/mL M-CSF (Peprotech, USA) to differentiate into BMDMs. After 7 days, all adherent cells had transitioned into fully mature macrophages.

For the coculture experiments, primary hepatocytes and BMDMs primed with LPS were plated in a coculture chamber (Corning, USA). PAOA were added to the coculture system. After 24 h of treatment with TNF-α (10 ng/mL) or IL-6 (12.5 ng/mL), the hepatocytes were analyzed for lipid accumulation and inflammatory response.

BMDMs primed with LPS were stimulated with PAOA for 48 h, followed by RNA sequencing analysis. Primary mouse HSCs were isolated following the previously described protocol [10]. In brief, following portal vein cannulation, the liver was perfused for 15 min with Ca2+/Mg2+-free HBSS at 37 °C, then with HBSS containing 0.05% collagenase and Ca2+/Mg2+ at a flow rate of 10 mL/min for another 15 min. The liver, after perfusion, was chopped and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer, followed by centrifugation at 50 × g for 3 min. After centrifuging the supernatant at 500 × g for 10 min, the pellet was resuspended in Ficoll plus Percoll (1:10, GE Healthcare) and subjected to centrifugation at 1400 × g for 17 min. HSCs were isolated from the interface and were incubated with BMDM-CM with or without TGF-β1 (8 ng/mL) for 24 h, and the activation status of HSCs was analyzed.

RNA sequencing and analysis

LPS-primed Irf7FL/FL or Irf7ΔMϕ BMDMs were stimulated with PAOA for 48 h, and then Irf7FL/FL or Irf7ΔMϕ BMDMs were subjected to RNA sequencing analysis (n = 3/group). Total RNA was extracted from cells by Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) separately. The cDNA libraries were constructed for each pooled RNA sample with the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Pathway analysis was used to find out the significant pathway of the differential genes according to KEGG database.

Coimmunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry

As previously described [41], specific primary antibodies were conjugated to protein A/G beads (Selleck, CN). Then the harvested cell lysates and the beads were mixed together for incubation at 4 °C overnight. After washing, immunoprecipitated proteins were subjected to the subsequent mass spectrometry (MS) analysis, also boiled with loading buffer for western blot.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

A ChIP Assay Kit (Abcam, MA) were used for ChIP assays. Briefly, BMDMs were fixed with formaldehyde for 10 min to stabilize protein-chromatin interactions, and the process was stopped by adding 125 mM glycine for 5 min. Cells were then rinsed with PBS and lysed in ChIP lysis buffer for 10 min. After centrifugation, nuclear pellets were isolated, resuspended in nuclear lysis buffer, and sonicated for 15 min. Purified chromatin was electrophoresed in a 1.5% agarose gel to assess the size of DNA fragment. For immunoprecipitation, the chromatin samples were incubated overnight with antibodies against IRF7 or SMAD3 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Normal IgG was used as a control. The antibody/chromatin samples mixed with protein A Sepharose beads were subjected to extensive washing, and eluted. DNA was purified by cross-link reversal and analyzed via PCR using primers targeting promoter regions of interest. Primer sequences are provided for SMAD3- and IRF7-associated regions. The primers for the SMAD3-responsive region of the Tgfb1 promoter: forward: 5’-GACTGTCGGGGGCACTTAAT-3’, reverse: 5’-AGGAGACCGACGTCTGAGAG-3’. The primers for the IRF7-responsive region of the Tnfa promoter: forward: 5’-CACACGAGGTCCAGCTCTTT-3’, reverse: 5’-GTAGATGGGAGATGCCGTCG-3’. The primers for the IRF7-responsive region of the Il6 promoter: forward: 5’-TTCTTCACAAGAATCAACCGGC-3’, reverse: 5’-TCTCAGGATCACAATCAGCTTCT-3’.

Luciferase assays

Tgfb1 promoter-, Tnfa promoter-, and Il1b promoter-luciferase reporter plasmids (Tgfb1, Tnfa, and Il1b luciferase) were constructed in the pGL3 luciferase vector (Promega, WI). BMDMs were transfected with these constructs and subsequently infected with adenoviral vectors encoding IRF7, SMAD3, or a control vector [42, 43]. Cells were lysed with passive lysis buffer after 48 h. A luciferase assay system (Promega, USA) was used to measure the transcriptional activity.

Plasmid construction and lentivirus production

Expression plasmids were constructed using the pcDNA3.1-Flag vector. Full-length SMAD3 was amplified from BMDM cDNA by PCR using gene-specific primers and verified by Sanger sequencing. Site-directed mutants were generated by overlap-extension PCR or using the Fast Mutagenesis Kit V2 (Vazyme Biotech, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Chimeric SMAD3 constructs were generated by overlap-extension PCR. All coding sequences were cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector with the indicated epitope tags and confirmed by sequencing. For stable overexpression, the coding sequences were subcloned into the lentiviral vector pLVX containing either a puromycin resistance cassette or GFP selection marker and verified by sequencing. Lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting the pLVX expression plasmids together with the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G into HEK293T cells using polyethyleneimine (PEI; Yeasen Biotechnology, China). Viral supernatants were collected 48 h after transfection, filtered through a 0.45-μm membrane, and used to infect target cells with polybrene (8 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich). Stably transduced cells were selected with puromycin.

Co-IP of truncation mutants

BMDMs were transiently transfected with the indicated full-length or truncation constructs. 24 h after transfection, cells were harvested and lysed in IP lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Cell lysates were incubated with the indicated antibodies followed by Protein A/G agarose or magnetic beads. After extensive washing, the immunocomplexes were eluted and subjected to immunoblot analysis.

Histological, immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analysis

Liver sections were prepared from liver samples after fixation in formalin and paraffin embedding. H&E staining was employed for histopathological evaluation, while Oil Red O staining quantified lipid deposition. For immunohistochemistry, sections were incubated with anti-α-SMA antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), followed by HRP-linked secondary antibodies and stained with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrachloride. Then nuclear counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin. Immunofluorescence staining was to analyze the expression of p-IRF7, Albumin, Desmin, CD68, CD31, and CK19 in liver tissues, and IRF7, HUWE1, p-IRF7, and SMAD3 in cells, using: anti-rabbit p-IRF7 mAbs (Affinity, CN), anti-mouse Albumin mAbs (Proteintech, USA), anti-rabbit Desmin mAbs (Abcam, UK), anti-mouse CD68 mAbs (Abcam, UK), anti-rabbit CD31 mAbs (Abcam, UK), anti-rabbit CK19 mAbs (Abcam, UK), anti-mouse IRF7 mAbs (Santacruz, USA), anti-rabbit HUWE1 pAbs (Invitrogen, USA), anti-rabbit p-IRF7 pAbs (Affinity, China) and anti-mouse SMAD3 mAbs (Invitrogen, USA), goat anti-rabbit IgG (Texas Red-conjugated), goat anti-rabbit IgG (Texas Green-conjugated), goat anti-mouse IgG (Texas Green-conjugated) (Sigma, USA), anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Alexa Fluor® 647 Conjugate), anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Alexa Fluor® 488 Conjugate), anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Alexa Fluor® 647 Conjugate) or anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Alexa Fluor® 488 Conjugate) (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) secondary antibodies. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and fluorescence was visualized via a confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Western blot

Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE, transferred onto membranes, and incubated with primary antibodies: anti-p-IRF7 rabbit pAbs (Affinity, CN), anti-IRF7 mouse mAbs (Santacruz, USA), anti-HUWE1 rabbit mAbs (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA), anti-SMAD3, and anti-β-actin rabbit mAbs (Abcam, UK). Secondary antibodies conjugated with HRP were used for detection.

Ubiquitination analysis

Cells were pretreated with MG132 (Sigma‒Aldrich) before being stimulated by mixture including: LPS (10 ng/ml) + PAOA for 4 h. Immunoprecipitates were isolated from the conjugated beads and prepared for analysis.

Real-time qPCR

Total RNA from liver tissues, BMDMs, or HSCs was extracted using RNA Purification Kit (SHANGHAI YISHAN Biotech Co., Ltd., CN). Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed with a kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., CN). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted by a LightCycler 480 system and SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., CN), with β-actin serving as control. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Data are showed as means ± SDs. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed with Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA in SPSS 22.0 software, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
