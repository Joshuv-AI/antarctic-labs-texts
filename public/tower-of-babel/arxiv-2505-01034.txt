Ramsey with purple edges
Thomas Lesgourgues, Anita Liebenau, Nye Taylor

Abstract
Motivated by a question of Angell, we investigate a variant of Ramsey numbers where some edges are coloured simultaneously red and blue, which we call purple. Specifically, we are interested in the largest number g = g ⁡ ( n , s , t ) g=g(n;s,t) , for some s s and t t and n < R ⁡ ( s , t ) n<R(s,t) , such that there exists a red/blue/purple colouring of K n K_{n} with g g purple edges, with no red/purple copy of K s K_{s} nor blue/purple copy of K t K_{t} . We determine g g asymptotically for a large family of parameters, exhibiting strong dependencies with Ramsey–Turán numbers.



1
Introduction

Ramsey theory is one of the most active areas in combinatorics, with connections to extremal graph theory, number theory, discrete geometry, and theoretical computer science. The starting point of the area is Ramsey’s Theorem
[
41
]
which implies that for given natural numbers
s,t
there exists an integer
n
such that every red/blue colouring of the edges of the complete graph on
n
vertices, denoted by
K_{n}
, contains a red copy of
K_{s}
or a blue copy of
K_{t}
. One of the most prominent open problems in the area is determining the smallest such
n
, called the Ramsey number
R(s,t)
.

Erdős and Szekeres
[
16
]
popularised Ramsey theory and showed that
R(t,t)\leq 4^{t}/\sqrt{t}
. A lower bound on
R(t,t)
of the form
2^{t/2+o(1)}
, first established by Erdős
[
14
]
as a prime example of the power of the probabilistic method, remains the best known to date – having been improved only by a constant factor of two by Spencer
[
46
]
.
The upper bound of the form
4^{t+o(1)}
has been improved by various authors
[
42
,
51
,
13
,
43
]
culminating in the breakthrough result by Campos, Griffiths, Morris, and Sahasrabudhe
[
12
]
, who gave the first exponential improvement on
R(t,t)
. Their result has recently been improved by Gupta, Ndiaye, Norin, and Wei
[
27
]
who showed that for all sufficiently large
t
, we have
R(t,t)\leq 3.8^{t}
.

More is known in the off-diagonal case, when
s
is a small constant and
t
is large with respect to
s
. Ajtai, Komlós and Szemerédi
[
1
]
proved that
R(s,t)=O_{s}(t^{s-1}/(\log t)^{s-2})
for all
s,t
. For
s=3
, Shearer improved this upper bound to
R(3,t)\leq(1+o(1))t^{2}/\log t
, which is now known to be tight up to a factor
(1/4+o(1))
as established by Bohman and Keevash
[
8
]
, and independently by Fiz Pontiveros, Griffiths, and Morris
[
24
]
, improving on earlier work by Kim
[
29
]
. For
s=4
, Mattheus and Verstraete
[
35
]
showed recently that the upper bound by Ajtai, Komlós and Szemerédi is tight up to a factor of
(\log t)^{2}
.

In this article, we consider a natural variant inspired by a problem posed by Angell in
Parabola

[
4
]
, a mathematical magazine for high school students.

Problem 1.1

(Angell
[
4
]
)
.

Consider
K_{8}
, the complete graph on the vertex set
\left\{1,\ldots,8\right\}
, such that the edges
12
,
34
,
56
and
78
are joined by a double edge, one coloured red and one coloured blue.
Show that every colouring of the remaining (simple) edges using colours red and blue produces a red copy of
K_{3}
or a blue copy of
K_{4}
.

We invite the interested reader to find a solution before proceeding. Since
R(3,4)=9
, the addition of some double-coloured edges is necessary in this problem. We initially conjectured that a more general statement holds, namely that for any
s,t\geq 3
, every red/blue colouring of the edges of the complete graph on
R(s,t)-1
vertices, with the addition of a perfect matching of double-coloured edges, contains a red copy of
K_{s}
or a blue copy of
K_{t}
.
This turns out to be false.
McKay and Radziszowski
[
38
]
employed computational methods to prove that
R(4,5)
is equal to
25
, a fact that was recently also formally verified by Gauthier and Brown
[
26
]
. However, there exists a red/blue colouring of the edges of
K_{24}
with a perfect matching of double-coloured edges, that contains neither a red copy of
K_{4}
nor a blue copy of
K_{5}
. We outline the computational methods used to obtain this result and other small cases in
Section

6
.

Beyond the consideration of small cases and of matchings, we find the presence of edges that are both red and blue to be of broader interest. We prefer to view double-coloured edges as edges of a third colour, which we shall henceforth call
purple
. A
red/blue/purple colouring
of
K_{n}
is a partition of the edges of
K_{n}
into three graphs
R\cup B\cup P
.
Problem
1.1
, for example, may be rephrased as showing that any red/blue/purple colouring
R\cup B\cup P
of
K_{8}
, where
P
forms a perfect matching,
contains a red/purple triangle or a blue/purple copy of
K_{4}
, meaning
K_{3}\subseteq R\cup P
or
K_{4}\subseteq B\cup P
.

One possibility to approach this variant is by considering
f(n,m)
, which is the size of the largest red/purple or blue/purple clique guaranteed to exist in every red/blue/purple colouring of
K_{n}
with exactly
m
purple edges.
The Erdős–Szekeres upper bound on
R(t,t)
implies that
f(n,m)
is at least
(\log n)/2
for every
m
, and a random colouring establishes that
f(n,m)=O(\log n)
when
m\leq cn^{2}/2
for every constant
0<c<1
.
Nagy
[
40
]
improved the lower bound by adapting an elegant argument of Erdős and Szemerédi
[
17
]
on a different variant on Ramsey numbers.

In this article, we explore the problem from the following
perspective.
We say that a graph
G
is
H
-free
if
G
does not contain a copy of
H
as a subgraph.
Given integers
s,t
, we say that a red/blue/purple colouring
R\cup B\cup P
is
(s,t)
-free
if
P\cup R
is
K_{s}
-free and
P\cup B
is
K_{t}
-free.

Definition 1.2
.

Given integers
s,t\geq 2
and
n<R(s,t)
, let
g=g(n;s,t)
denote the largest integer such that there exists an
(s,t)
-free red/blue/purple colouring
R\cup B\cup P
of
K_{n}
that satisfies
|P|=g
.

Clearly, for
n\geq R(s,t)
no such colouring exists, whereas for
n<R(s,t)
at least one colouring, possibly with zero purple edges, does exist. So,
g
is well-defined. For simplicity, we extend
Definition

1.2
to non-integers
s
and
t
, as the largest integer such that there exists a red/blue/purple colouring
R\cup B\cup P
of
K_{n}
that satisfies
\omega(R\cup P)<s
,
\omega(B\cup P)<t
and
|P|=g
, where
\omega(G)
denotes the clique number of a graph
G
as usual. That is for non-integers
s,t
we let
g(n;s,t):=g(n;\lceil s\rceil,\lceil t\rceil)
.

It turns out that
g(n;s,t)
is closely related to the so-called Ramsey-Turán numbers. For integers
n
,
s
and
t
, the Ramsey-Turán number
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t)
is the maximum number of edges in an
n
-vertex
K_{s}
-free graph
G
with independence number
\alpha(G)<t
. First considered by Andrásfai
[
3
]
in the triangle case, Ramsey-Turán numbers were systematically introduced by Sós, who proved many deep results throughout her career (e.g.
[
19
,
18
,
20
,
22
,
23
]
). It is not hard to see that Ramsey-Turán numbers yield natural upper bounds on
g(n;s,t)
. Indeed, let
R\cup B\cup P
be a red/blue/purple colouring of
K_{n}
such that
R\cup P
is
K_{s}
-free,
B\cup P
is
K_{t}
-free, and
|P|
is maximal. Then
R\cup P
has independence number
\alpha(R\cup P)=\omega(B)\leq\omega(B\cup P)<t
. It follows that

g(n;s,t)=|P|\leq|P\cup R|\leq\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t).

(1.1)

We refer to the excellent survey by Simonovits and Sós
[
45
]
on Ramsey-Turán numbers, and summarise here only the results that are most important for us. We first note that

\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,cn)
is known precisely for every
c\geq 1/(s-1)
due to Turán’s theorem
[
52
]
, see
Subsection

2.2
. Many classical theorems in the area yield bounds on
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,cn)
when
c
is a sufficiently small constant.
Erdős and Sós
[
15
]
studied this specific problem for odd cliques
K_{s}
, and showed that
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,cn)=((s-3)/(s-1)+O(c))n^{2}/2
.
Brandt
[
11
]
gave asymptotically tight bounds on
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,cn)
when
c\in(0,1/3)
, and Lüders and Reiher
[
34
]
extended this result to all odd integers
s
and small enough
c
.
The even clique cases proved to be much more difficult.
The first result in that direction was obtained by Szemerédi
[
49
]
who proved
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{4}(n,cn)\leq(1/8+O(c))n^{2}
for small enough
c
, using a version of his seminal Regularity Lemma. An ingenious construction by Bollobás and Erdős
[
9
]
shows that this bound is best possible up to the dependency in
c
.
Erdős, Hajnal, Sós, and Szemerédi
[
21
]
used the Bollobás-Erdős construction and showed that
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,cn)=((3s-10)/(3s-4)+O(c))n^{2}/2
for all even integers
s
.
Recently, Fox, Loh, and Zhao
[
25
]

refined the dependency on
c
when
s=4
, and Lüders and Reiher
[
34
]
proved that
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,cn)=((3s-10)/(3s-4)+c-c^{2}+o(1))n^{2}/2
for all even integers
s
.

Our first result shows that the trivial upper bound
1.1
is asymptotically correct in that range, that is when
t=cn
for sufficiently small
c
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any integer
s\geq 3
, and for any
c>0
small enough, we have

g(n;s,cn)=(1-o(1))\;\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,cn).

Our next theorem centres on triangles, where we explore the behaviour of
g(n;3,t)
for a broader range of
t
. Let us first recall what is known about
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,cn)
when
s=3
. It follows from Mantel’s theorem that
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,cn)=\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor
for any
c\geq 1/2
, while Brandt’s work
[
11
]
covers the range
c<1/3
asymptotically. In the intermediate range, Andrásfai proved that certain blow-ups of
C_{5}
are extremal constructions for
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,cn)
when
c\in[2/5,1/2]
, and conjectured that similar constructions should provide extremal examples for the remaining range of
c
. Łuczak, Polcyn, and Reiher
[
31
,
33
]
confirmed this conjecture for
c\in[4/11,2/5]
and recently announced a proof of the conjecture. We refer the reader to
Subsection

2.2
, for a detailed presentation of these results and the Andrásfai Conjecture. Using these, we give an almost complete picture of
g(n;3;t)
, showing first that
1.1
is asymptotically correct when
t=cn
for every
c
for which the Andrásfai Conjecture holds. We also bound
g(n;3,t)
in terms of Ramsey-Turán numbers when
t
is sublinear.

Theorem 1.4
.

(i)

For all
c\in\left(0,1\right]\setminus\left(\frac{1}{3},\frac{4}{11}\right)
we have

g(n;3,cn)=(1-o(1))\;\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,cn).

(1.2)

Moreover, assuming the Andrásfai Conjecture, we obtain that the same result holds also for
c\in\left(\frac{1}{3},\frac{4}{11}\right)
.

(ii)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that for sufficiently large
n
and
t=(1+\varepsilon)\sqrt{2n\log n}
, we have

g(n;3,t)\geq\delta\cdot\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,t).

Moreover, if
t\gg\sqrt{n\log n}
, we have

g(n;3,t)\geq\Big({\frac{1}{2}-o(1)}\Big)\;\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,t).

We remark that, even without assuming the Andrásfai Conjecture,
Theorem

1.4
(i)
for
c=1/3
implies that
g(n;3,c^{\prime}n)
is at least
0.91\,\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,c^{\prime}n)
for
c^{\prime}
in the gap range
c^{\prime}\in\left(1/3,4/11\right)
.

Next we note that the lower bound on
t
in
Theorem

1.4
(ii)
is essentially best possible. Indeed, the current, best known bounds on
R(3,t)
are

\left(\frac{1}{4}-o(1)\right)\,\frac{t^{2}}{\log t}\leq R(3,t)\leq(1+o(1))\,\frac{t^{2}}{\log t},

(1.3)

due to Bohman and Keevash
[
8
]
, Fiz Pontiveros, Griffiths, and Morris
[
24
]
, and Shearer
[
44
]
. The upper bound implies that
g(n;3,t)
is undefined if
t\leq\gamma\sqrt{n\log n}
, for any
\gamma<1/\sqrt{2}
. Moreover, the lower bound in (
1.3
) is conjectured to be asymptotically tight in both
[
8
,
24
]
. If true, this would imply that
g(n;3,t)
is undefined if
t\leq\gamma\sqrt{n\log n}
, for any
\gamma<\sqrt{2}
, that is,
Theorem

1.4
(ii)
would cover practically the full range of
t
.

Finally, we observe that we cannot expect the constant
\delta
in
Theorem

1.4
(ii)
to be close to 1 when
t
is within a constant factor of
\sqrt{n\log n}
. Indeed, we have the following.

Observation 1.5
.

Let
\gamma
and
\delta
be real numbers satisfying
0<\delta<1/(2\gamma^{2})<1
. Then there exists an integer
n_{0}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
and
t=\gamma\sqrt{n\log n}
we have that

g(n;3,t)<(1-\delta)\cdot\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,t),

assuming
g(n;3,t)
is well-defined.

We would like to thank Anastos, Das, Morris and Rathke
[
2
]
for pointing this fact out to us, which is a simple consequence of Shearer’s proof of the lower bound in (
1.3
). We provide the details in
Section

4
.

To discuss smaller ranges of
t
, define the
inverse Ramsey number
, denoted by
R^{-1}(s,n)
, to be the minimum independence number of a
K_{s}
-free graph on
n
vertices. Observe that
g(n;s,t)
is defined if and only if
R^{-1}(s,n)<t
. Using a probabilistic argument, we give a general lower bound on
g(n;s,t)
for
t
almost as small as
R^{-1}(s,n)
.

Theorem 1.6
.

There exists an absolute constant
p>0
such that for any fixed
s\geq 3
, for
n
large enough and any
t\in\left[R^{-1}(s,n)\log n,n\right]
, we have

p\cdot\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t/\log n)\leq g(n;s,t).

To compare this general lower bound with the upper bound in (
1.1
), it is useful to understand the behaviour of the Ramsey-Turán numbers
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t)
and
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t^{\prime})
when
t
and
t^{\prime}
are not too far apart.
For example, Theorem 1.10 in
[
25
]
implies that
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{4}(n,t)\geq n^{2}/8+o(n^{2})
if
t=n/(\log n)^{K}
, for any constant
K>1
. Thus,
Theorem

1.6
implies that for
t=n/(\log n)^{K-1}
we have that
g(n;s,t)=\Theta(n^{2})
. Sudakov proved that if
t=e^{-\omega((\log n)^{1/2})}n
then
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{4}(n,t)=o(n^{2})
, from which we infer via (
1.1
) that
g(n;4,t)=o(n^{2})
for such
t
. More generally, Balogh, Hu, and Simonovits
[
5
]
showed that
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t)=o(n^{2})
when
t=R^{-1}(s,n)n^{\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon\leq 1/s^{2}
. They also gave a more precise range of
t
where a transition from
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t)=\Theta(n^{2})
to
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t)=o(n^{2})
occurs, assuming the
following long-standing conjecture on Ramsey numbers.

Conjecture 1.7
.

For every integer
s\geq 3
, there exist
\vartheta=\vartheta(s)>0
such that for any
t
large enough

R(s-1,t)t^{\vartheta}\leq R(s,t).

Assuming this conjecture, it is proved in
[
5
]
that
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,R^{-1}(\ell,n))
is quadratic in
n
if
\ell\leq\frac{s+1}{2}
and sub-quadratic if
\ell>\frac{s+1}{2}
. Using this result and our probabilistic bound from
Theorem

1.6
, we derive the following general behaviour of
g(n;s,t)
.

Theorem 1.8
.

For any
s\geq 3
, assuming
Conjecture

1.7
,

(i)

if
t\geq R^{-1}\left(\frac{s+1}{2},n\right)\log n
, then
g(n;s,t)=\Theta(n^{2})
.

(ii)

if
t\leq R^{-1}\left(\frac{s+3}{2},n\right)
, then
g(n;s,t)=o(n^{2})
.

Preliminaries

Notation.

We use standard graph theoretic notation. In particular, given a graph
G
, we write
V(G)
for its vertex set and
E(G)
for its edge set, with
v(G)=|V(G)|
and
e(G)=|E(G)|
. We further write
\alpha(G)
,
\omega(G)
, and
\delta(G)
for the independence number, for the clique number, and for the minimum degree of
G
, respectively. Given graphs
F
and
G
, we say that
G
is
F
-free if
G
does not contain a subgraph that is isomorphic to
F
.
A
blow-up
of an
n
-vertex graph
G
is a graph obtained by replacing each vertex
i
of
G
by an independent set
V_{i}
, and each edge
ij
of
G
by a complete bipartite graph between the parts
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
. A blow-up is called
equitable
if
\big||V_{i}|-|V_{j}|\big|\leq 1
for all
i,j
.

We often abuse notation identifying a graph with its edge set. For example, we write
\alpha(R)
for the independence number of the spanning subgraph of
K_{n}
induced on the edges of
R
,
for a
3
-colouring
R\cup B\cup P
of
K_{n}
, and similarly for the clique number
\omega(R)
. Furthermore, for a subset
S\subseteq V(K_{n})
, we write
R[S]
for the subgraph induced on the vertices of
S
and the edges in
R
.

Unless stated otherwise, logarithms are in base
2
. We use the standard Landau symbols
O
,
\Omega
,
\Theta
, and
o
to denote the asymptotic behaviour of functions with respect to
n
, the number of vertices, tending to
\infty
, as well as
f(n)\gg g(n)
to denote
g(n)=o(f(n))
. We say that an event
A(n)
holds with high probability if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[A(n)]=1
.

2.1
Blow-up colouring

We start with a general construction of a red/blue/purple colouring
R\cup B\cup P
of
K_{n}
that we will use throughout the article.

Definition 2.1

(Blow-up colouring)
.

Let
n\geq k\geq 1
be integers and let
G
be a graph on
k
vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
. The
n
-blow-up colouring
of
G
is a colouring
R\cup B\cup P
of
K_{n}
defined as follows.
Let
H
be an equitable blow-up of
G
created by replacing every vertex
v_{i}\in V(G)
by a set of vertices
V_{i}
of size either
\beta^{-}=\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\rfloor
or
\beta^{+}=\lceil\frac{n}{k}\rceil
, such that
\sum_{i}|V_{i}|=n
, and replacing every edge
v_{i}v_{j}
of
G
by a complete bipartite graph between the sets
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
. For each
i\in[k]
, denote the vertices of
V_{i}
by
\{u_{1}^{i},\ldots,u_{\beta^{-}}^{i}\}
or
\{u_{1}^{i},\ldots,u_{\beta^{+}}^{i}\}
. For every edge
v_{i}v_{j}
of
G
, and for every
\ell\in[\beta^{+}]
, colour the edge
u_{\ell}^{i}u_{\ell}^{j}
of
H
, if it exists, red. Colour all other edges of
H
purple. Finally, colour all edges in the complement of
H
blue. Let
R
be the set of all red edges,
B
the set of all blue edges, and
P
the set of all purple edges.

An illustration of a
10
-blow-up colouring of the cycle graph
C_{4}
can be found in
Figure

2.1
. We remark that
n
-blow-up colourings are not uniquely defined, as their definition is up to the choice of vertices being replaced by sets of size
\beta^{+}
or
\beta^{-}
. However, in every
n
-blow-up colouring of a graph
G
, the red edges form
\beta^{-}
vertex-disjoint copies of
G
, and possibly a vertex-disjoint copy of an induced subgraph of
G
.

R
P

This is an example of red and purple edges in a 10-blow-up colouring of
C_{4}
.

The following proposition captures the core properties of blow-up colourings.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
G
be a graph on
k
vertices and
n\geq k
be an integer. Each
n
-blow-up colouring
R\cup B\cup P
of
G
satisfies

(i)

\omega(R\cup P)=\omega(G)
,

(ii)

\alpha(R)=\omega(B\cup P)\leq\left\lceil n/k\right\rceil\,\alpha(G)
,

(iii)

\big||P|-e(G)\,n^{2}/k^{2}\big|\leq e(G)3n/k,
and

(iv)

|R|\leq e(G)\,(n/k+1)
.

Proof.

Let
R\cup B\cup P
be an
n
-blow-up colouring of
G
. Trivially, any edge in
R\cup P
corresponds to an edge in
G
, while for any two vertices
u,v\in V_{i}
for some
i
, we have
uv\not\in R\cup P
. Therefore
\omega(R\cup P)=\omega(G)
, verifying
(i)
.
The red graph
R
consists of
\left\lceil\frac{n}{k}\right\rceil
vertex-disjoint copies of induced subgraphs of
G
that cover all
n
vertices. Therefore,
\alpha(R)\leq\alpha(G)\,\left\lceil\frac{n}{k}\right\rceil

.
To verify

and
(iv)

In this subsection, we summarise results on Ramsey-Turán numbers that we utilise in this paper. We start with Turán’s Theorem.
For non-negative integers

the equitable blow-up of

Theorem 2.3

For

Since the independence number of

\displaystyle=e(T_{n,s-1})=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1-\frac{1}{s-1}\Big)n^{2}+O(n)

for all

It is clear that the red/purple subgraph

Corollary 2.4

For any

Proof.

Let

The interesting range for Ramsey-Turán numbers is therefore when

Theorem 3.1

For every constant

Recall that

\displaystyle V(\Gamma(n;k,t))=

We first prove

Proof of
Observation

.

Let

be large enough and assume that
g(n;3,t)\geq(1-\delta)\cdot\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,t)

be a
(3,\lceil t\rceil)
-free colouring of

such that

. Given that
\alpha(R\cup P)\leq\alpha(R)<t
, we have
|R\cup P|\leq\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,t)
and it follows that
|R|\leq\delta\cdot\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,t)
. By

, we obtain

is a triangle-free graph with average degree at most

showed that

then contains an independent set of size at least

. This leads to the desired contradiction since for

and

n\;\frac{\log(\delta t)-1}{\delta t}=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\delta\gamma\sqrt{\log n}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\,\log n+\frac{1}{2}\,\log\log n+\log(\delta\gamma)-1\Big)\geq\frac{1}{2\delta\gamma}\sqrt{n\log n}\geq t.\qed

The triangle-free process is a key tool in our analysis of

when

is sublinear. This random graph process is defined iteratively, starting with an empty graph

on
k

the random graph obtained after adding

the final random graph resulting from this process.

The triangle-free process was introduced by Bollobás and Erdős (see e.g.

conjectured that it should establish

in 2009. Four years later, Fiz Pontiveros, Griffiths, and Morris
[

, and independently Bohman and Keevash

\displaystyle\alpha(R)\leq\alpha(G)\left\lceil\frac{n}{k}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sqrt{2k\log k}\Big(\frac{n}{k}+1\Big)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sqrt{2n\log n}\Big(\zeta+\frac{1}{\zeta(1-\varepsilon)^{1/4}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\sqrt{2n\log n}\Big(\zeta+\frac{1}{\zeta}\Big)

\displaystyle=\gamma\sqrt{n\log n},

where in the third inequality we use lower and upper bounds of
4.2
, and the last equality is simply the definition of
\zeta
.
In order to bound the number of purple edges, observe that for
n
large enough,
k\geq n/\zeta^{2}
and
\zeta=O(1)
imply that
\log k\geq\log n/2
, say.
Proposition

2.2
and
4.3
then imply that

\displaystyle|P|

\displaystyle\geq e(G)\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}}-\frac{3n}{k}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{8}k^{3/2}\sqrt{\log k}\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}}-\frac{3n}{k}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{4}\zeta^{3}}n^{3/2}\sqrt{\log n}\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}}-\frac{3n}{k}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{3}\zeta^{3}\gamma}\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}}-\frac{3n}{k}\Big)\frac{tn}{2}.

Now,
n/k\geq\zeta^{2}\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}>3
, by
4.2
and since
\varepsilon<1/4
and
\zeta>2
. It follows that
|P|\geq\delta tn/2
for some
\delta=\delta(\gamma,\zeta)>0.

For the second part, when
t\gg\sqrt{n\log n}
, we reason similarly, with some adjustments since we need more precise estimates on
\delta
. Observe that we can assume that
t=o(n)
as otherwise the result follows from
Theorem

1.4
(i)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, let
n
be large enough and let
\gamma=t/\sqrt{n\log n}
. Recall that here we assume
\gamma\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Define
\zeta
by

\gamma=\sqrt{2}\;\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{1-\varepsilon}\Big(\zeta+\frac{1}{\zeta}\Big)\Big(1-\frac{2\log\zeta}{\log n}\Big)^{1/2}.

Note that
\zeta=\Theta(\gamma)
,
and thus our assumptions on

imply that
1\ll\zeta\ll\sqrt{n/\log n}.

Again, let
k
be the smallest integer such that
n\leq\zeta^{2}k
.
Then
k\leq(n/\zeta^{2})^{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}
for
n
large enough, and thus,

\sqrt{\log k}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sqrt{\log n}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{2\log\zeta}{\log n}\Big)^{1/2}.

The computations of
\alpha(R)
and
|P|
are now similar to the first case. From
Propositions

2.2
and
4.3
we deduce that

\displaystyle\alpha(R)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sqrt{2k\log k}\left\lceil\frac{n}{k}\right\rceil\leq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{1-\varepsilon}\sqrt{2n\log n}\Big(\zeta+\frac{1}{\zeta}\Big)\Big(1-\frac{2\log\zeta}{\log n}\Big)^{1/2}=\gamma\sqrt{n\log n},

and

\displaystyle|P|

\displaystyle\geq e(G)\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}}-\frac{3n}{k}\Big)\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{2}}\,k^{3/2}\sqrt{\log k}\,\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}}(1-o(1))

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\gamma\sqrt{2}}\,\Big(\frac{n\log k}{k\log n}\Big)^{1/2}\,\frac{\gamma n^{3/2}\sqrt{\log n}}{2}(1-o(1)),

(4.4)

where we used that

in this case. Now
n/k\geq\zeta^{2}(1-1/k)=\zeta^{2}(1-o(1))
and

\sqrt{\log k}\geq\sqrt{\log n}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{2\log\zeta}{\log n}\Big)^{1/2},

by definition of
k
. Thus,
4.4
implies that

\displaystyle|P|

\displaystyle\geq(1-o(1))\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\zeta}{\gamma\sqrt{2}}\,\Big(1-\frac{2\log\zeta}{\log n}\Big)^{1/2}\,\frac{tn}{2}

(4.5)

\displaystyle=(1-o(1))\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}\,\frac{tn}{4},

(4.6)

by definition of
\zeta
and since
\zeta\to\infty
. The theorem follows since

is arbitrary.
∎

To cover the remaining range of

, that is

for

, we need a much more refined analysis of the triangle-free process. Recall that we denote by

m^{*}(\varepsilon):=\Big(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}-\varepsilon\Big)n^{3/2}\sqrt{\log n}.

(4.7)

Fiz Pontiveros, Griffiths, and Morris
[

]
defined a sequence of

events, denoted here by

for simplicity, such that if

holds then there are still free edges that do not close a triangle with

, implying that the triangle-free process has not stopped yet. They proved the following proposition.

Theorem 4.3

(Fiz Pontiveros, Griffiths, and Morris
[
24
]
Theorems 6.9 and 7.2)
.

For every sufficiently small constant
\varepsilon>0
, and every
n
large enough, let

. Then,

(i)

\mathcal{E}(m)
holds with probability at least
1-n^{-\log n}
.

(ii)

With probability at least

either

fails or

\alpha(G_{m})<\left(\sqrt{2}+10\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)\sqrt{n\log n}
.

We remark that
[

, Proposition 7.2]
is stated in terms of

, that is the final random graph produced by the triangle-free process. However, the stronger statement of
Theorem

4.3
(ii)
is easily extracted from the proof in
[

, Section 7.7]
.
We use these results to prove the following theorem which, together with
2.2
, concludes the proof of

.

Theorem 4.4
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that for
n
large enough and
t=(\sqrt{2}+\varepsilon)\sqrt{n\log n}
we have

g(n;3,t)\geq\delta nt/2.

Proof.

Observe that, by monotonicity, it is sufficient to prove the statement for
\varepsilon
small enough. Therefore let
\varepsilon>0
be small enough and
n
be large enough such that
Theorem

4.3
holds. Let
\varepsilon_{1}:=\varepsilon^{2}/100
, and fix a real number
\varepsilon_{2}\in(0,\varepsilon_{1})
. Let
m_{1}=m^{*}(\varepsilon_{1})
and
m_{2}=m^{*}(\varepsilon_{2})
as given by
4.7
. Our goal is to follow the triangle-free process up to

, colouring all
m_{1}
first edges in red, and then the remaining

We first condition the triangle-free process on

, we know that

4.3

We therefore select an outcome of the triangle-free process such that

with

5
General clique sizes

5.1
Linear independence number

This section is dedicated to the proof of
Theorem

1.3
, showing that

is asymptotically equal to the Ramsey-Turán number when

is fixed,
t=cn
and

is small enough with respect to
1/s
. We will prove this statement in two steps, covering the case when
s
is odd first, and then when
s
is even.
Theorem

1.3
follows easily using the bounds on
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,cn)
from
Theorem

2.7
.

Theorem 5.1
.

For any integer
\ell\geq 1
, and any constant
0<c<\frac{1}{3\ell}
, we have

g(n;2\ell+1,cn)\geq\left(\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}+c+o(1)\right)\frac{n^{2}}{2}.

Proof.

Fix
\eta>0
. Let
d,k
be non-negative integers such that
(c-\eta)\ell<\frac{d}{k}<c\ell
, and such that there exists a triangle-free
d
-regular graph
G
on
k
vertices with independence number
d
, as guaranteed by
Theorem

2.5
. For
n
large enough, assume without loss of generality that
\ell
divides
n
and let
R\cup B\cup P
be an
(n/\ell)
-blow-up colouring of
G
. It follows from
Proposition

2.2
that
R\cup P
is a triangle-free graph and, since
n
is large enough,
R
has independence number at most

\left\lceil\frac{n}{\ell k}\right\rceil\alpha(G)\leq\Big(\frac{d}{\ell k}+\frac{d}{\ell n}\Big)n<cn.

We additionally obtain from
Proposition

2.2
that for
n
large enough,

\displaystyle\lvert P\rvert\geq e(G)\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{\ell^{2}k^{2}}-\frac{3n}{\ell k}\Big)=\frac{n^{2}}{2\ell}\Big(\frac{d}{\ell k}-\frac{3d}{2n\ell}\Big)>\frac{n^{2}}{2\ell}(c-\eta),

and

|R|\leq e(G)\frac{n/\ell+k}{k}=O(n).

We define a colouring
R^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}\cup P^{\prime}
of
F\cong K_{n}
as follows. We partition the vertices of
F
into
\ell
disjoint sets
V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell}
of size
n/\ell
. For each
i\in[\ell]
, we embed a copy of the colouring
R\cup B\cup P
on the vertices
V_{i}
. For each
i\neq j\in[\ell]
we colour the edges between
V_{i},V_{j}
in red and purple (with no blue edges) such that the resulting
R^{\prime}
will be isomorphic to the so-called strong product
R\boxtimes K_{\ell}
. Specifically, for
i\neq j
,
v_{i}\in V_{i}
and
v_{j}\in V_{j}
, colour the edge
v_{i}v_{j}

•

red if
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
are copies of the same vertex of the colouring
R\cup B\cup P
;

•

red if
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
are copies of distinct vertices

in
R\cup B\cup P
such that the edge
uw
is in
R
; and

•

purple otherwise.

First note that
R^{\prime}\cup P^{\prime}
is
K_{2\ell+1}
-free, since
(R^{\prime}\cup P^{\prime})[V_{i}]
is a triangle-free graph for every
i\in\ell
.
Next, it is not hard to see that
\alpha(R^{\prime})\leq\alpha(R)
. Indeed, every
U\subseteq V(F)
that is independent in

can contain at most one copy of
u
, for each
u\in V(R\cup B\cup P)
. Thus,
U=\{u_{1}^{j_{1}},\ldots,u_{h}^{j_{h}}\}
, where
u_{1},\ldots,u_{h}
are distinct vertices of
V(R\cup B\cup P)
, and
u_{i}^{j_{i}}
is the copy of
u_{i}
in
V_{j_{i}}
for every
1\leq i\leq h
. But this set

is independent in
R^{\prime}
if and only if the set of vertices
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{h}\}
is an independent set in
R
. Therefore,
\alpha(R^{\prime})\leq\alpha(R)<cn
.
To bound the number of purple edges from below, note that for any two distinct
i,j\in[\ell]
, the red edges between
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
can be decomposed into a perfect matching, with the addition of the edges
u_{i}w_{j}
and
u_{j}w_{i}
for every edge
uw
of
R
with copies
u_{i}w_{i}
in
V_{i}
and
u_{j}w_{j}
in
V_{j}
. Therefore, there are exactly
2|R|+n/\ell
red edges between
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
, for all distinct
i,j\in[\ell]
. It follows that

\displaystyle g(n;2\ell+1,cn)\geq|P^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=\binom{\ell}{2}\left[\left(\frac{n}{\ell}\right)^{2}-2|R|-\frac{n}{\ell}\right]+\ell|P|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n^{2}}{2}\Big(\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}+c-\eta\Big)-O(n).

Since
\eta>0
can be chosen arbitrarily small, we obtain the desired result.
∎

We now turn our attention to the case of even cliques. Modifying the Bollobás–Erdős construction

]
for the study of

Theorem 5.2

(Fox, Loh, and Zhao
[
25
]
)

For every

, for every even integer

, large enough such that
a\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon k}+\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon=\frac{4(\log\log k)^{3/2}}{\log^{1/2}k}

(i)

-free,

(ii)

\alpha(G)<ak

(iii)

The quantitative relation between

5.2

Theorem 5.3

For any integer
\ell\geq 2
, and any constant

We take a blow-up colouring of a

-free graph as guaranteed by

5.2

(\ell-2)

-free graph as guaranteed by

2.5

Let
\eta>\delta>0
be arbitrarily small constants. Let

be large enough, and without loss of generality, divisible by

. Let

k=2\lfloor\log\log n\rfloor,\quad\varepsilon=\frac{4(\log\log k)^{3/2}}{\log^{1/2}k},\quad a=\frac{(3\ell-2)}{4}(c-\eta).

For
n
large enough, observe that
a\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon k}+\varepsilon
. Therefore, there exists a
K_{4}
-free graph
G_{4}
be on
k
vertices such that

e(G_{4})\geq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{4}+a-a^{2}-2\varepsilon\Big)k^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha(G_{4})\leq ak,

by
Theorem

5.2
. Let
R_{4}\cup B_{4}\cup P_{4}
be a
(4n)/(3\ell-2)
-blow-up colouring of
G_{4}
. It follows from
Proposition

2.2
that
R_{4}\cup P_{4}
is a
K_{4}
-free graph such that

\displaystyle\alpha(R_{4})\leq\left\lceil\frac{4n}{k(3\ell-2)}\right\rceil\alpha(G_{4})\leq\frac{4na}{(3\ell-2)}+ak=(c-\eta)n+ak<(c-\delta)n,

(5.1)

for
n
large enough. We also obtain from
Propositions

2.2
and
5.2
that the number of purple edges is given by

\displaystyle|P_{4}|

\displaystyle\geq e(G_{4})\Big(\frac{(4n)^{2}}{k^{2}(3\ell-2)^{2}}-\frac{12n}{k(3\ell-2)}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{4}+a-a^{2}-o(1)\Big)\frac{(4n)^{2}}{(3\ell-2)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{2}\Big(\frac{4}{(3\ell-2)^{2}}+\frac{4(c-\eta)}{(3\ell-2)}-(c-\eta)^{2}-o(1)\Big).

(5.2)

Observe that if
\ell=2
, we have

\eta
is arbitrarily small. Assume now that
\ell>2
, and let

,

\frac{(3\ell-2)}{3}(c-\eta)<\frac{d}{h}<\frac{(3\ell-2)}{3}(c-\delta)<\frac{1}{3}

on

vertices with independence number

. Let
R_{3}\cup B_{3}\cup P_{3}
be a

-blow-up colouring of

. It follows from

2.2
that for

is a triangle-free graph with

\alpha(R_{3})\leq\left\lceil\frac{3n}{h(3\ell-2)}\right\rceil\alpha(G_{3})\leq\Big(\frac{3d}{h(3\ell-2)}+\frac{d}{n}\Big)n<(c-\delta)n,

(5.4)

We now define a colouring

of

as follows. Informally, we take a copy of

. The edges between any two of these copies are coloured red randomly, with probability

. We show that the independence number of

.

More formally, partition the vertices of

into one set

\ell-2

into

, and for each

, embed a copy of

in

. Let

, and for

Claim 5.4
.

For every
\mu>0
, the following properties are satisfied with probability

(P1)

There are

, there exists a red edge between

and

.

and

. The probability that there is no red edge between

. Therefore,

\displaystyle|P|

\displaystyle\geq|P_{4}|+(\ell-2)|P_{3}|+\binom{\ell-2}{2}\frac{9n^{2}}{(3\ell-2)^{2}}+(\ell-2)\frac{12n^{2}}{(3\ell-2)^{2}}-o(n^{2})

, we could use the better estimate

5.2
A probabilistic bound

Proof of

For some absolute constant

obtained by randomly selecting the edges of
G
with probability
p
, independently of each other. Let
R\cup B\cup P
be the colouring of
K_{n}
defined by

\begin{cases}P&=E(G_{p}),\\
R&=E(G)\setminus E(G_{p}),\\
B&=E(K_{n})\setminus E(G).\\
\end{cases}

By standard concentration results, we know that with high probability

|P|\geq\mathbb{E}\big[|P|\big]/2=p/2\cdot\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t/\log n).

(5.5)

Observe that
R\cup P
is isomorphic to
G
and therefore a
K_{s}
-free graph. By the union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\alpha(R)\geq t]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\subseteq V(G)\\
|T|=t\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left[T\text{ is independent in }R\right]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\subseteq V(G)\\
|T|=t\end{subarray}}p^{e(G[T])}.

For any such fixed set
T
, let
F=G[T]
. Then
F
is a graph on
t
vertices with
\alpha(F)\leq\alpha(G)<t/\log n
. Thus by
Theorem

2.3
applied to the complement of
F
,

e(F)\geq\alpha(F)\frac{(t/\alpha(F))\cdot(t/\alpha(F)-1)}{2}\geq\frac{t^{2}}{4\alpha(F)}\geq\frac{t\log n}{4}.

Therefore for
n
large enough, by the union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\alpha(R)\geq t]

\displaystyle\leq\binom{n}{t}p^{(t\log n)/4}\leq\exp\left[\frac{t\log n}{4}\Big(4+\log p\Big)\right].

Thus for
p<e^{-4}
, with high probability
R
contains no independent set of size
t
. Since
5.5
also holds with probability
1-o(1)
,
there exists an
(s,t)
-free colouring
R\cup B\cup P
of
K_{n}
such that
|P|\geq p/2\cdot\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t/\log n)
,
and the claim follows.
∎

In this section, we present some computational results obtained at the start of this project, which might be of interest for future work.

Angell’s Problem

More generally, let us denote by

7
Concluding remarks

In this article, we initiate the systematic study of double-coloured, or purple edges, in Ramsey theory. To the best of our knowledge, this is novel.
Naturally, there are many open problems in this context.

Ramsey-Turán numbers.

The focus of this paper is the extremal parameter
g(n;s,t)
, and we have seen how this is tightly connected to the Ramsey-Turán number
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t)
.

The numbers
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,cn)
are known asymptotically for all
c\in(0,1]\setminus(1/3,4/11)
, and we expect the gap range
(1/3,4/11)
to be covered as well soon, following the exciting announcement of resolving
Conjecture

2.6
. The outcome of the triangle-free process, see
Theorem

4.1
, together with a blow-up construction similar to the one in the proof of
Theorem

4.2
imply that

(1/4-o(1))tn\leq\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,t)\leq tn/2

whenever
t\geq(\sqrt{2}+o(1))\sqrt{n\log n}
.
For
s\geq 4
, the asymptotic values of
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,cn)
are only known for
c
small enough or for
c\geq 1/(s-1).
In the light of the connection to purple edges in Ramsey theory, we would be interested in any progress of estimating

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,cn)/n^{2}
for all
0<c<1/(s-1)
.
A lower bound of
1/4+2c/3
on this limit is given in
[
25
]
for
s=4
and when
c\leq 1/3
.

While most of the classical results in Ramsey-Turán theory focus on
t
being linear in
n
, not as much is known in the sublinear case. Many results have been obtained on threshold phenomena, that is, functions
f(n)
for which
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t)/n^{2}
differs for
t
above and below
f
, see for example, Kim, Kim, and Liu
[
28
]
for
s=8
and Balogh, Chen, McCourt, and Murley
[
6
]
for
s
up to
13
. For these small cases, there exists a pattern showing
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t)=o(n^{2})
for
t\ll R^{-1}((s+1)/2,n)
, which we know to be true for general
s
assuming
Conjecture

1.7
following
Theorem

5.5
. We would be interested in the behaviour of
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t)
below these thresholds. We note that Sudakov
[
48
]
showed that
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{4}(n,n^{1-1/r})<n^{2-1/r(r+1)}
for any
r\geq 2
, and also asked the general question of determining
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{4}(n,f(n))
for any function
f(n)
.

Question 7.1
.

What is the order of magnitude of
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t)
for
s\geq 4
when
t
is such that
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t)=o(n^{2})
?

At the far-end of the spectrum.

When
t
is very close to
R^{-1}(s,n)
, say up to an additive constant, we expect this question to be rather difficult. At that end of the spectrum, estimating
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t)
becomes a question of estimating the maximum number of red edges in a nearly optimal
(s,t)
-free colouring of
K_{n}
. Given the complexity of estimating Ramsey numbers, this question, although a tantalising open problem, might be out of reach of current methodologies. However, understanding the difference in behaviours between
\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{s}(n,t)
and
g(n;s,t)
for such values of
t
might not be. We have seen in
Theorem

1.4
that
g(n;3,t)=\Omega\big(\mathrm{{\bf RT}}_{3}(n,t)\big)
for
t\geq(1+\varepsilon)\sqrt{2n\log n}
. We do not expect such a result to hold when
t
is very close to
R^{-1}(s,n)
, therefore, determining the order of magnitude of
g(n;3,t)
when
t=R^{-1}(s,n)+1
would be already interesting. In this regime, the results presented in
Section

6
show that for all
t\leq 9
, a perfect matching of purple edges is sufficient to recover the Ramsey property in
K_{n}
. We wonder whether this holds for general
t
.

Question 7.2
.

Is it true that for all integers
t\geq 3
and
n=R(3,t)-1
, every red/blue/purple colouring
R\cup B\cup P
of
K_{n}
, where
P
is a perfect matching, contains a red/purple triangle or a blue/purple copy of
K_{t}
?

As mentioned in the introduction and in
Section

6
, we cannot expect the answer to
Question

7.2
to be yes when 3 is replaced by
s\geq 4
. We also mentioned at the end of
Section

6
that purple matchings do not necessarily have the maximal number of purple edges among all
(s,t)
-free red/blue/purple colouring of
K_{n}
, even when
s=3
. In that case, we would like to understand what happens when
P
contains some other, fixed structure. Natural candidates are Hamilton cycles.

Question 7.3
.

Is it true that for all integers
s,t\geq 3
and
n=R(s,t)-1
, every red/blue/purple colouring
R\cup B\cup P
of
K_{n}
, where
P
is a Hamilton cycle, contains a red/purple copy of
K_{s}
or a blue/purple copy of
K_{t}
?

The symmetric case when
s=t
.

Any of these considerations on
g(n;s,t)
can be studied in the symmetric setting, when
s=t
. Write
g(n;s)
for
g(n;s,s)
.
Observe that
n<R(t,t)
implies that
t>(\log n)/2
. The results of Nagy
[
40
]
imply that
g(n;c\log n)\geq(1-\varepsilon_{1})\binom{n}{2}
for every
c>2
, and that
g(n;c\log n)\leq(1-\varepsilon_{2})\binom{n}{2}
for every
c>3/5
, where
\varepsilon_{1}\approx 2/c
and
\varepsilon_{2}\approx 1/(5c)
. It would be interesting to improve these bounds, and extend them to determine
g(n;s)
when
s=c\log n
, for any
c>1/2
.
