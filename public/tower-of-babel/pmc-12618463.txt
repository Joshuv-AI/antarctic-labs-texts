Inheritance of resistance and fitness cost parameters to cyantraniliprole in the resistant and susceptible strains of Plutella xylostella L.
Authors: Shanmugapriya V, Johnson Thangaraj Edward Y S, Kannan M, Mohan Kumar S, Ramanathan A
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe diamondback moth (Plutella xylostella L.) is a major pest of cruciferous crops, causing significant yield losses worldwide. Its remarkable ability to develop resistance to over 100 insecticides has made it one of the most resilient agricultural pests. Cyantraniliprole, a diamide insecticide targeting ryanodine receptors, was selected for this study due to its novel mode of action and its efficacy against resistant populations. This laboratory study investigated the inheritance pattern and fitness costs associated with cyantraniliprole resistance in P. xylostella. F₂₅S larvae (larvae from the 25th generation of lab reared susceptible DBM) were subjected to increasing median lethal (LC₇₀) concentrations of cyantraniliprole (0.285 to 9.453 mg ml⁻¹) for 15 generations up to F₄₀ generations under continuous selection pressure. Bioassays determined median and post median lethal concentrations (LC₅₀ and LC₇₀), resistance ratios, realized heritability (h² = 0.386), and response quotient (Q = 0.515), indicating substantial genetic variability and rapid resistance evolution. Genetic crosses showed resistance to be autosomal, incompletely recessive, and influenced by maternal effects. Resistant populations exhibited significant fitness costs, including reduced egg hatchability (80% in resistant and 95% in susceptible), lower larval and pupal weight, higher malformed adult emergence and low net reproductive rate (15 and 45 individuals/female in resistant and susceptible respectively). These findings provide critical insights into resistance dynamics and underscore the need for resistance management strategies, such as rotating insecticides with different modes of action, to sustain the effectiveness of cyantraniliprole in managing this economically important pest.Keywords: Cyantraniliprole resistance, Ryanodine receptors, Heritability, Maternal effects, Autosomal trait, Life table parametersSubject terms: Agricultural genetics, Genetic association study, Heritable quantitative trait, Inbreeding, Mutation

Introduction

The diamondback moth (Plutella xylostella L.,) is a globally significant pest of brassicaceae crops, causing yield losses of up to 92% in cabbage and 75% in broccoli under severe infestations1. Its biological features, such as rapid reproduction, short generation time, overlapping generations, and strong migratory capability, enable it to adapt swiftly to diverse agroecological environments2. Over the past few decades, the cost of managing P. xylostella has risen sharply, with global estimates now ranging between USD 4 to 5 billion annually3. Adding to the challenge is P. xylostella’s remarkable ability to develop resistance, with over 1,000 documented cases spanning nearly every major insecticide class, establishing it as one of the most resistant pest species globally4. For instance, field populations have shown high resistance to abamectin and cartap in Brazil5, indoxacarb in Central China6, beta-cypermethrin and abamectin in multiple Chinese provinces2, and pyrethroids like cypermethrin in Australia7. Resistance to Bacillus thuringiensis has been reported in Central America8, and overall, the pest has developed resistance to at least 97–101 active ingredients across insecticide groups including organochlorines, carbamates, and diamides worldwide3,4. These patterns underscore the pest’s rapid evolution of resistance under intensive selection pressure in diverse agro-ecosystems.

Cyantraniliprole, a novel anthranilic diamide insecticide, acts by binding to the ryanodine receptors (RyRs), resulting in uncontrolled calcium release from internal stores, leading to paralysis and eventual death9. Its cyano-substituted structure enhances its efficacy across multiple insect orders, including Lepidoptera, Coleoptera, and Hemiptera10. Cyantraniliprole is particularly valued for its systemic and translaminar activity, allowing effective control of both chewing and sucking pests while maintaining an environmentally sustainable profile11,12. Low mammalian toxicity, persistence, and selective action make it a preferred choice in integrated pest management (IPM) strategies targeting resistant pest populations13,14. Reports highlight its broad-spectrum usefulness, including high efficacy against the coffee berry borer (Hypothenemus hampei) in coffee plantations15, fall armyworm (Spodoptera frugiperda) in Brazil16, Asian citrus psyllid (Diaphorina citri) by reducing feeding, egg production, and populations17, whiteflies (Bemisia tabaci) in field, and sucking pests like thrips and aphids on chili, okra, and cabbage, where it achieved significant population reductions and yield protection18. It has also shown promise against dipteran leafminers, stored-product pests, and tomato pests, demonstrating versatility in various cropping systems19.

However, resistance to cyantraniliprole had been reported to emerge in field populations of P. xylostella20,21. Field-evolved resistance has been documented in B. tabaci in China, with reduced efficacy and up to 80-fold resistance in lab-selected strains22,23, S. frugiperda with up to 23.97-fold resistance24, cotton aphid (Aphis gossypii) at 17.3-fold25, and Diaphorina citri in Florida26. This resistance is primarily linked to mutations in the PxRyR gene, such as I4790M and G4946E, which alter the receptor’s structure and reduce insecticide binding affinity27. Moreover, enhanced detoxification through metabolic enzymes, including cytochrome P450 monooxygenases and glutathione S-transferases, further contributes to cyantraniliprole resistance28. Reports of cross-resistance with other diamides, such as chlorantraniliprole, have intensified concerns over the long-term viability of this insecticide class in field management29,30.

Genetic studies have shown that cyantraniliprole resistance in P. xylostella is autosomal and incompletely recessive, with maternal effects influencing resistance expression in some populations31. Heritability estimates (h²) and response quotients (Q) indicate that resistance has a strong genetic basis and evolves rapidly under insecticide selection pressure32. These parameters highlight the urgency of resistance monitoring and the adoption of proactive management strategies33.

Additionally, resistance is often associated with fitness costs. Resistant populations exhibit delayed development, reduced larval weight, lower pupal survival, decreased adult emergence, and compromised reproductive potential34,35. Key demographic indicators such as the net reproductive rate (R₀), intrinsic rate of increase (r), and finite rate of increase (λ) are significantly suppressed in resistant strains36. These trade-offs may reduce the persistence of resistance alleles in the absence of selection, offering a window for strategic interventions37.

Given the pest’s resistance potential and cyantraniliprole’s importance in current pest control programs, this study was undertaken to assess the inheritance, selection dynamics, and fitness costs of cyantraniliprole resistance under laboratory conditions. The insights gained will be instrumental in guiding effective insecticide resistance management (IRM) programs and sustaining the efficacy of critical insecticides like cyantraniliprole38,39.

Materials and methods

Insect rearing

P. xylostella (L.) populations were reared at the Department of Agricultural Entomology, Tamil Nadu Agricultural University, Coimbatore, India, using modified protocols28. Susceptible populations, unexposed to insecticides, were maintained on mustard (Brassica juncea) and cauliflower (Brassica oleracea var. botrytis) at 28 ± 2 °C with a 14:10 L: D photoperiod. Mustard seedlings were grown in plastic cups (6 × 3 cm) with a 1:1:1 mix of red soil, sand, and farmyard manure, treated with carbendazim (200 mg/100 g seeds), and watered every two days. Seedlings (4–5 cm) were placed in oviposition cages (30 × 30 × 30 cm) with 50 adult pairs, fed 10% sugar solution with multivitamin drops. First-instar larvae fed on mustard leaves, while second-instar larvae were transferred to cauliflower leaves using petiole-wrapped cotton, with fresh leaves provided daily. Pupae were collected with a camel hair brush, stored at 4 °C to synchronize emergence, and placed in emergence cages after 5–6 days. Resistant populations were developed by exposing neonate larvae from the susceptible strain to cyantraniliprole (LC70, 0.285–9.453 mg ml⁻¹) across generations, maintaining 70–80% larval mortality, with similar rearing conditions.

Bioassay

Bioassays followed the leaf-dip method29. Cauliflower leaves were washed with 0.1% Triton X-100 to remove any surface contaminants and enhance wettability and cut into 6–8 cm discs. Discs were immersed in cyantraniliprole 10.26 OD (Syngenta, Benevia®, 10.26% active ingredient) for 5–10 s, air-dried for 1 h, and placed on moist filter paper in bioassay containers. Early third-instar larvae (8–10 days old) were released (5 replicates/treatment, 10 larvae/replicate) with untreated controls. Mortality was recorded every 24 h for 3 days. Experiments with control mortality > 20% were discarded. Corrected mortality was calculated using Abbott’s formula40.

Selection experiments

To develop resistant strains (R), approximately 2,500 third-instar larvae from the F25 generation of a susceptible baseline population were continuously exposed to LC₇₀ cyantraniliprole-treated cauliflower leaves for 15 successive generations (F25R to F40R). In parallel, a susceptible strain (S) with the same number of larvae was maintained without any insecticide exposure over the same period (F25S to F40S), originating from the same F25 population. During the selection process, both strains were monitored to maintain 70–80% mortality, ensuring sufficient survivors to propagate the next generation. To study the inheritance of resistance, parental strains, F₁ hybrids, and reciprocal backcrosses (R × S, F₁ × R, F₁ × S) were established and evaluated.

Results

Development of cyantraniliprole resistance in Plutella Xylostella

Continuous selection from F25 strains of Plutella xylostella larvae over 15 generations (F40R) with increasing LC₇₀ doses of cyantraniliprole resulted in the development of significant resistance. The LC₅₀ values increased from 0.00708 mg ml⁻¹ in F25R to 0.32787 mg ml⁻¹ in F40R, while LC₉₅ values rose from 137.71 mg ml⁻¹ to 12448.6 mg ml⁻¹ (Table 1). Resistance ratios escalated from 95.13-fold in F26R to 3602.97-fold in F40R relative to the susceptible strain (Table 1). In contrast, unselected susceptible strains (F25S to F40S) showed declining LC₅₀ values from 0.00708 mg ml⁻¹ in F25S to 0.000091 mg ml⁻¹ in F40S, indicating increased susceptibility over generations (Table 1). Probit regression slopes ranged from 0.38352 to 1.09357 for resistant strains and 0.03765 to 0.38352 for susceptible strains, with chi-square values (0.155–2.864) confirming good model fit (Table 1).

Heritability and resistance risk

Realized heritability (h²) of cyantraniliprole resistance was 0.386, and the response quotient (Q) was 0.515, indicating moderate to high additive genetic variation. Resistance risk assessment predicted that a 10-fold increase in resistance could occur every 4.5 generations under consistent field-level selection pressure, assuming a 74.4% mortality threshold (Tables 2 and 3).

Fitness cost in resistant vs. susceptible populations

Resistant populations showed significantly reduced fitness in several biological parameters compared to susceptible strains. Comparisons between resistant (R) and susceptible (S) populations revealed differences in developmental and reproductive parameters (F-values ranging from 12.89 to 885.47, all P < 0.001; Table 5). Resistant populations had longer egg periods (3.5 ± 0.1 days ) compared to 2.5 ± 0.05 days in S, lower egg hatchability (80 ± 3.0% as against 95 ± 1.5% in S) and reduced initial larval weight (0.25 ± 0.005 mg vs. 0.45 ± 0.01 mg in S). The larval and pupal periods (5.5 ± 0.15 days and 3.8 ± 0.1 days respectively) were shortened in R compared to S (6.8 ± 0.2 and 4.8 ± 0.15 days respectively), with lower pupal mass (0.3 ± 0.01 mg in R and 0.5 ± 0.02 mg in S) (Table 5). Survival from neonate to pupa was lower in R (60 ± 2.0) than in S (80 ± 2.5), with reduced pupation rates (80 ± 2.0 in R and 92 ± 1.0% in S) and adult emergence (50 ± 1.0 in R and 70 ± 1.5% in S) (Table 5). Higher percentage of un-emerged pupae (15 ± 0.5) and malformed adults (10 ± 0.4 vs.) were observed in R compared to 10 ± 0.3 and 2 ± 0.1% respectively in the S (Fig. 1.).

Fitness parameters of P. xylostella. This composite image illustrates four stages of insect development affected by mortality and abnormalities: a deceased larva with a shrivelled, brownish body; a dead pupa showing yellowish discoloration within a partial cocoon; two un-emerged pupae encased in textured silk cocoons, indicating failed metamorphosis; and a malformed adult insect with distorted wings and irregular leg development, reflecting developmental defects. These visual indicators suggest that cyantraniliprole resistance in P. xylostella imposes significant fitness costs, including reduced survival, prolonged developmental periods, and compromised reproductive potential, which can hinder population growth in the absence of the insecticide.

The mean adult longevity of resistant males and females was shorter (6.0 ± 0.2 and 8.0 ± 0.3 days) compared to susceptible population (8.0 ± 0.3 and 10.5 ± 0.4 days). Similarly, reproductive traits such as fecundity (15 ± 0.5 in R and 20 ± 0.3 eggs in S) and oviposition period (5.0 ± 0.1 in R and 9.0 ± 0.2 days in S) were adversely affected in resistant population. Despite higher fecundity in resistant populations, fertility was lower (70% in R and 95% in S) (Table 5).

Key life table parameters revealed the impact of resistance that the net reproductive rate (R₀) was lower in resistant strains (15 ± 0.8 compared to 45 ± 1.0 individuals/female), the intrinsic rate of increase (r) was also reduced from 0.22 in S to 0.15 eggs/day in R. The finite rate of increase (λ) was lower (1.16) in R and higher (1.24) in S. Mean generation time was also shorter in resistant population (16.40) compared to 17.15 days in S (Table 5).

Genetic basis of resistance

Inheritance studies revealed that cyantraniliprole resistance in P. xylostella is autosomal, incompletely recessive, and influenced by maternal effects. Backcross LC₅₀ values (0.00015–0.24737 mg ml⁻¹) and Degree of dominance (D) values ranged from − 0.998 to 1.00 across reciprocal and backcross populations, with maternally inherited dominance observed (Fig. 2). LC₅₀ values for F₁ hybrids were intermediate: 0.19148 mg ml⁻¹ for RS (R♀ × S♂) and 0.00028 mg ml⁻¹ for SR (S♀ × R♂), with slopes of 0.37319 and 0.57071, respectively (chi-square = 0.255–2.509; Table 5). F40R and F40S progenies showed intermediate LC₅₀ values compared to resistant and susceptible parents, confirming incomplete recessive inheritance (Tables 6 and 7).

Crosses and life history trait variation

Crosses between resistant and susceptible strains showed significant differences in biological traits. F1 crosses and backcross progenies derived from resistant females (RS) consistently exhibited lower hatchability (84 ± 2.85% in RS(f) × S(m) and 97 ± 1.05% in SR(f) × S(m)), reduced larval weight (0.27 ± 0.003 and 0.41 ± 0.014 mg respectively), (83.61 ± 0.45 and 90.20 ± 0.09% respectively), and decreased adult emergence (51 ± 0.76 in RS(f) × S(m) and 68 ± 1.04 in SR(f) × S(m)) compared to those from susceptible females (SR) (Table 8) confirming the maternal influence. Malformation rates were higher in RS-derived crosses (9 ± 0.34), with shorter female longevity (8.19 ± 0.06 days) and reduced net reproductive rates (18.7 ± 0.67 individuals/female) (Table 8).

Discussion

The rapid evolution of cyantraniliprole resistance in Plutella xylostella under continuous selection pressure underscores the pest’s genetic adaptability. Our study observed a significant increase in LC₇₀ values from 0.2850 mg/ml in the F25 generation to 9.453 mg/ml in the F40 generation, with resistance ratios escalating from 95.13-fold to 3602.97-fold over 15 generations (Table 2). Laboratory studies demonstrate a > 30-fold increase in LC₇₀ values over 30 generations, with resistance ratios exceeding 3600-fold, reflecting intense selection dynamics9. Field populations resistance to abamectin and cartap in Brazilian P. xylostella populations5 and indoxacarb in Central China6 mirrors the rapid adaptation seen here, where beta-cypermethrin and abamectin resistance in Chinese provinces2 and pyrethroids like cypermethrin in Australia7 have reached high levels under similar pressures. This rapid escalation, also seen with chlorantraniliprole, highlights the risks of over-reliance on diamides without rotating pesticides with different modes of action. The reversibility of resistance in unselected susceptible strains (F25S to F40S), where LC₅₀ decreased from 0.00708 to 0.000091 mg/ml, suggests that fitness costs may destabilize resistance alleles in the absence of insecticide pressure20. However, widespread diamide use in fields sustains high selection pressure, complicating management38. Critically, the assumption that resistance will consistently decline without selection overlooks potential compensatory mutations, which could stabilize resistant phenotypes3, necessitating field-based longitudinal studies as seen in global resistance to 97–101 active ingredients including organochlorines, carbamates, and diamides3,4.

The moderate to high realized heritability (h² = 0.386) and response quotient (Q = 0.515) for cyantraniliprole resistance indicate substantial additive genetic variation, enabling rapid resistance evolution32. High heritability has driven resistance to profenofos and acetamiprid and Bacillus thuringiensis in Central America8, similar to the genetic basis observed here. A predicted 10-fold resistance increase every 4.5 generations aligns with field observations in regions with intensive insecticide use, where diamide resistance emerges within 2–3 years33. High heritability suggests resistance alleles are readily selected in monoculture systems with limited rotation43. Integrated resistance management (IRM) strategies, such as biological controls, are critical to slow resistance escalation25. However, laboratory heritability estimates may overestimate field risks due to environmental factors like genetic drift41. Critically, assuming high heritability guarantees rapid resistance ignores fitness parameters, which could temper spread in diverse field conditions, warranting further ecological studies4.

Cyantraniliprole-resistant P. xylostella populations exhibit significant fitness costs, including reduced larval and pupal weights, lower hatchability, and diminished reproductive potential37. These align with delayed development and reduced fecundity in chlorantraniliprole-resistant strains36 where net reproductive rate (R₀) and intrinsic rate of increase (r) are suppressed, as seen in our data (R₀ = 15 vs. 45 individuals/female; r = 0.15 vs. 0.22). Similar occurrence was also observed in other pests, such as reduced population growth in cyantraniliprole-resistant Bemisia tabaci with up to 80-fold resistance17,24 and Aphis gossypii at 17.3-fold resistance22. These could facilitate IRM by favoring susceptible alleles without insecticide pressure34. However, assuming fitness costs always limit resistance persistence is risky, as compensatory mechanisms may mitigate these costs in field populations35 as evidenced by persistent resistance in Spodoptera frugiperda (23.97-fold)22 and Diaphorina citri25,26. This means that while fitness costs provide a window for interventions, it must be validated in field trials to ensure effective management. This highlights the need for longitudinal studies to assess fitness cost stability in natural settings.

Cyantraniliprole resistance in P. xylostella is autosomal, incompletely recessive, with maternal effects, driven by target-site mutations (e.g., I4790K) in the ryanodine receptor (PxRyR) and metabolic mechanisms26. CRISPR/Cas9 studies confirm I4790K confers > 1000-fold resistance12. Metabolic resistance, involving cytochrome P450, is prevalent in field populations10. Maternal effects in crosses, with F26 progenies from resistant mothers showing lower hatchability, suggest cytoplasmic influences similar to maternal modulation in D. citri25. The polygenic nature of resistance complicates management29. Critically, the emphasis PxRyR mutations may neglect epigenetic factors, which could modulate resistance expression, warranting further research on mechanisms like those in lab-selected B. tabaci strains (80-fold resistance)19.

Fitness parameters could aid IRM by favoring susceptible alleles42. However, field adaptations may mitigate these costs, requiring ongoing monitoring especially given cyantraniliprole’s broad efficacy against pests like the coffee berry borer15, fall armyworm16,22, and whiteflies19. This implies that understanding maternal effects could improve predictive models for resistance spread, emphasizing the integration of genetic data into IRM frameworks.

Overall, these findings highlight the vulnerability of cyantraniliprole to resistance in P. xylostella, similar to patterns in other species like S. frugiperda25 and B. tabaci26. It indicates that without diversified strategies, such as combining cyantraniliprole with alternatives effective against sucking pests viz., thrips and aphids on chili17,18 or dipteran leafminers44, resistance could compromise its utility in IPM. Future studies should explore field-scale implementations to bridge laboratory insights with practical outcomes. Molecular investigations, including CRISPR/Cas9 validation of PxRyR mutations (I4790M) and epigenetic influences on maternal effects26, could elucidate underlying mechanisms, especially in comparison to resistance in other pests like Bemisia tabaci (up to 80-fold resistance)19 and Spodoptera frugiperda16. Additionally, exploring integrated resistance management (IRM) strategies, such as combining cyantraniliprole with biological controls or novel insecticides effective against regional P. xylostella populations, similar to studies in Brazil5, would help develop sustainable protocols33,38. Finally, assessing cross-resistance with emerging diamides and climate impacts on fitness costs could predict long-term efficacy, ensuring proactive adaptations to global pest challenges.
