First case report of atrial myxoma in interauricular occlusive device in Colombia
Authors: Isabella Montealegre, Camilo Moreno, Camilo Montes, María F. Mazo, Alex Pava, Andrés Ramos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Primary cardiac tumors are extremely rare; myxomas are the most common benign type in adults. Although percutaneous closure of ostium secundum atrial septal defects (osASD) using occluder devices is a standard and safe procedure, the development of a cardiac mass on these devices is a rare event that requires careful differential diagnosis among thrombi, endocarditis, and neoplasms.Case presentation:A 58-year-old woman with a history of an osASD corrected percutaneously with an occluder device in 2021 presented 3 years later for routine follow-up. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed a mobile, heterogeneous, lobulated mass attached to the left atrial disk of the device. Due to the high risk of systemic embolization, surgical intervention was scheduled. A median sternotomy and atriotomy confirmed a gelatinous mass adhering to the device. The mass and the occluder were completely resected, followed by reconstruction of the interatrial septum using a bovine pericardial patch. Histopathological analysis confirmed a cardiac myxoma. The postoperative course was favorable, with complete functional recovery.Clinical discussion:This represents the first reported case in Colombia of an atrial myxoma arising from an ASD occluder device. Although rare, this complication highlights the importance of long-term echocardiographic surveillance after percutaneous cardiac interventions. Surgical resection remains the gold standard treatment to prevent embolic events and obtain a definitive histological diagnosis.Conclusion:This case describes the first Colombian report of a cardiac myxoma associated with a percutaneous interatrial closure device, emphasizing the need for a comprehensive differential diagnosis of late intracardiac masses.

Introduction

Primary cardiac tumors are rare entities with an estimated annual incidence of 0.014% to 0.03%, of which 90% are benign masses[1]. In the adult population, myxomas are the most frequent histological type (50%)[2]. The use of percutaneous devices for the closure of ostium secundum atrial septal defect (osASD) has a well-documented efficacy and safety profile[4]. However, the appearance of intracardiac masses following implantation poses a significant diagnostic challenge, as they must be differentiated from thrombi, infectious vegetations, or, rarely, primary tumors[3].

The present article reports the first case in Colombia, and one of the few documented in the international literature, of an atrial myxoma developing on an atrial septal occluder device. We describe the clinical and surgical management of a female patient with an atrial myxoma attached to a percutaneous atrial septal closure device, who was successfully treated with surgical resection of the device and subsequent atrioseptoplasty. This case report has been written in accordance with the SCARE criteria[5].

Case report

A 58-year-old Colombian woman, a homemaker, presented with a medical history of hypothyroidism, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular risk prevention, for which she was receiving levothyroxine, rosuvastatin, and acetylsalicylic acid. She had no known drug allergies or relevant family history, had a history of heavy smoking, and had an osASD that was confirmed via transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and repaired in 2021 by percutaneous closure with an occluder device.

During the years following the procedure, she maintained annual cardiology follow-up, documenting adequate clinical and echocardiographic progress.

In 2025, she experienced the onset of progressive exertional dyspnea (NYHA class II) with an unremarkable physical examination. Despite seeking medical evaluation on two separate occasions, the initial electrocardiograms were normal, though her symptoms persisted. Subsequently, during a scheduled cardiology check-up, a transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) was ordered, which identified a mobile mass measuring 19 × 7.7 mm attached to the device at the level of the interatrial septum, with a high embolic risk (see Fig. 1A). Baseline laboratory tests were within normal limits: WBC 7350/mm3; neutrophils 2550/mm3; hematocrit 40.2%; platelets 232 000/mm3. The next month, at a follow-up cardiology appointment, a TEE was performed, which showed that the mass had grown by 6 mm, reaching 25 × 7.5 mm despite anticoagulation. Given its refractory nature, the treatment with LMWH was switched to warfarin. Based on the findings in a joint session with the cardiovascular surgery service, it was decided to proceed with surgical intervention due to suspicion that the progressive growth of the mass was caused by a primary neoplasm.

The surgical procedure involved a median sternotomy and pericardiotomy, during which a gelatinous mass was identified as adhering to the percutaneous closure device. This mass was resected along with the device. Additionally, the entire interatrial wall was excised to prevent the growth of a new tumor, followed by repair of the interatrial septum with a bovine pericardial patch (see Fig. 1B).

Postoperatively, the patient had no neurological deficits or focal signs; however, vitals exhibited desaturation, prompting urgent transfer to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU). A chest X-ray revealed an extensive left tension pneumothorax that was corrected with thoracostomy without complications. The patient stayed in the ICU for 7 days with a favorable clinical course, followed by 10 days in the general wards with comprehensive management, including cardiopulmonary rehabilitation, cardiology, and cardiovascular surgery.

In outpatient follow-up, adequate septal repair was observed, with no recurrence of intracavitary masses and complete recovery of respiratory function. At the most recent evaluation, the patient is in NYHA functional class I, asymptomatic, and fully reintegrated into her daily activities.

The patient expressed satisfaction with the surgical outcome and reported a significant improvement in her functional capacity after recovery. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this case report, including the use of clinical images and medical information.

Discussion

Cardiac myxomas (CM) are the most prevalent type of primary cardiac tumor. They frequently affect women aged 30–60[6]. Histologically, these neoplasms derive from proliferating multipotent mesenchymal cells that differentiate along endocardial lines. Pathologically, they present as polypoid, round, or oval masses with a characteristic smooth or gently lobulated appearance[7,8]. On immunohistochemistry, neoplastic cells typically demonstrate positivity for calretinin, CD31, and CD34[9]. Clinical manifestations are highly variable and depend primarily on the tumor’s size and location; symptoms often relate to intracardiac obstruction, systemic embolization, or nonspecific constitutional findings[10–12].

osASD is a common congenital heart disease diagnosed in adults. Catheter-based device occlusion of osASD has been established as a safe and effective intervention[13]. Saliba et al. described an excellent long-term closure success rate of 98.9% in a large retrospective series[13].

Differential diagnoses

However, late complications have been documented, including infective endocarditis (IE), device thrombosis, and, less commonly, CMs[12,13]. Clinically, an elevation of inflammatory markers in conjunction with a new-onset cardiac murmur warrants high suspicion of IE[14]. Conversely, patients with thrombosis or CMs are often asymptomatic or present with nonspecific symptoms, such as dyspnea. In this context, imaging (TTE or TEE) is essential for differentiating between these intracardiac masses[15]. Thrombi are typically avascular, often multiple, sessile, and may decrease in size following anticoagulation, whereas CMs are solitary, pedunculated, lobulated, and vascularized masses arising from the fossa ovalis, complicating the differential diagnosis[12,13].

To date, four cases have been reported in the English literature[3,16–18]. The average age is 68 years; three of the four patients were men and were largely asymptomatic, with only one case reporting dyspnea and hemoptysis[3,16–18]. This profile contrasts with that of our patient, a 58-year-old asymptomatic woman. The mean follow-up period until imaging diagnosis was 2.5 years, similar to our case (Table 1). While no clear etiological relationship between transcatheter closure devices and CM development has been established, this case highlights the critical importance of considering this entity within the spectrum of late post-surgical complications[3]. Post-market surveillance

Beyond differential diagnosis, this case raises a critical surveillance concern. Rather than framing CM solely as a diagnostic challenge, clinicians should recognize it as a long-term surveillance problem: patients who have undergone percutaneous osASD closure may develop CM years after implantation while remaining entirely asymptomatic. According to current ACC/AHA/HRS/ISACHD/SCAI guidelines[19], in stable, asymptomatic patients with no or mild sequelae, surveillance every 2–5 years thereafter is recommended, whereas annual imaging is warranted if high-risk features are present, such as atrial arrhythmias or ventricular dysfunction. The appearance of a new intracardiac mass unresponsive to anticoagulation at any point during this surveillance period should prompt expedited surgical evaluation. Like other rare cardiac anomalies requiring individualized reconstruction, the management of a cardiac mass in an occlusive device demands complex surgical planning. It requires not only complete oncologic resection but also tailored reconstruction of the interatrial septum to restore anatomical integrity[20].

Proposed mechanism

From a pathophysiological perspective, multipotent mesenchymal cells appear to play a pivotal role. We hypothesize that the histogenesis of a cardiac myxoma (CM) arising on an occlusive device may be directly linked to initial thrombus formation. Thrombi frequently develop in the low-flow environments surrounding these devices, sharing macroscopic and embolic characteristics with CMs. In this context, we postulate that chronic cellular stress and localized inflammation within the thrombus microenvironment may induce dysplastic changes. This inflammatory milieu likely recruits endothelial cells to encapsulate the thrombus, subsequently driving abnormal proliferation, secretion of myxoid stroma, and ultimately metaplastic transformation[21].

This hypothesis is further reinforced by established mechanistic theories in CM pathogenesis. Notably, interleukin-6 (IL-6)-mediated inflammation is known to trigger cascades leading to cellular metaplasia. Other recognized pathways include PRKAR1A gene mutations, proliferation from embryonic remnants, and the activation of endocardial mesenchymal cells induced by mechanical or tumor-like stress[22]. Crucially, the theory of thrombus-to-tumor transformation aligns closely with our proposed model. We suggest that a pre-existing thrombus serves as the pathophysiological scaffold; under continuous cellular stress and chronic inflammation, the multipotent properties of local mesenchymal cells are hijacked, triggering a metaplastic shift that transforms a simple blood clot into a true cardiac myxoma.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this report lies in its comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, multimodal imaging follow-up, and definitive histopathological confirmation. However, its limitations include the inability to establish a causal relationship between device implantation and cardiac myxoma development, given the observational nature of a single-case report.

Conclusion

We report the first case in Colombia of CM developing on an osASD occluder device. While percutaneous closure remains a safe gold standard, the emergence of late intracardiac masses poses a significant diagnostic challenge. CM should be a primary differential diagnosis when a mass adherent to a device is refractory to anticoagulation, regardless of the time elapsed since implantation. This case underscores the necessity of long-term echocardiographic surveillance and confirms that the surgical resection of both the mass and the device is a definitive and safe strategy to prevent catastrophic embolic events.
