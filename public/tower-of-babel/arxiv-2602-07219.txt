The Median is Easier than it Looks: Approximation with a Constant-Depth, Linear-Width ReLU Network
Abhigyan Dutta, Itay Safran, Paul Valiant

Abstract
We study the approximation of the median of d d inputs using ReLU neural networks. We present depth-width tradeoffs under several settings, culminating in a constant-depth, linear-width construction that achieves exponentially small approximation error with respect to the uniform distribution over the unit hypercube. By further establishing a general reduction from the maximum to the median, our results break a barrier suggested by prior work on the maximum function, which indicated that linear width should require depth growing at least as log ⁡ log ⁡ d \log\log d to achieve comparable accuracy. Our construction relies on a multi-stage procedure that iteratively eliminates non-central elements while preserving a candidate set around the median. We overcome obstacles that do not arise for the maximum to yield approximation results that are strictly stronger than those previously known for the maximum itself.

Additional related work

L_{2}
approximation of the maximum.

The work most closely related to ours is
25
, which establishes lower and upper bounds for approximating the maximum function with respect to the uniform distribution on the unit hypercube. Their constructions rely on the idempotent property of the maximum where the maximum of maxima of subsets equals the global maximum. Since this property fails to hold for the median, we introduce a more intricate multi-stage construction that yields an efficient approximation for the median and consequently for the maximum. Notably, our approach improves upon the upper bounds in
25
within the regimes we consider. While our results are restricted to an accuracy threshold that is exponentially small in the input dimension, we emphasize that this setting encompasses the error regimes typically encountered in practical applications and captures the most relevant approximation scenarios.

Exact computation of CPWL functions.

The exact computation of CPWL functions has a rich literature. Based on the fact that any CPWL function can be expressed as a nested sum of minima and maxima of affine transformations
[
21
,
32
]

\lceil\log d\rceil+1
suffices to compute any CPWL function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
5
]
or by decreasing the width based on the geometric properties of the target function
[
13
,
14
,
8

. Notably, these upper bounds depend on the number of convex regions in the function’s polyhedral decomposition. While this quantity is exactly
d
for the maximum, for the median it is a combinatorial quantity that grows exponentially with

A related line of work on sorting networks
[
16
]
provides constructions that imply the exact computation of the median with ReLU networks. Using depth that scales as
\mathcal{O}(\log d)
with a large constant
c

\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}d)

. However, because these constructions require either significantly more depth than the maximum or rely on prohibitively large constants, they remain far less efficient than current architectures for the maximum.

In light of the above, to achieve efficient median computation with constant depth and linear width, it is natural to relax the approximation requirement. We show that even demanding exponentially small accuracy results in a dramatic improvement over the exact computation bounds discussed above, bypassing the logarithmic depth barriers inherent to the exact regime.

Neural networks.

Throughout, we use the notation
\left[z\right]_{+}=\max\{0,z\}
for the ReLU activation function. In our work we consider fully connected, feed-forward neural networks; i.e., each neuron in the network computes some non-linear activation function
\sigma

h
Neural network consists of

The

Approximation error.

We focus solely on a regression setting, where a neural network
\mathcal{N}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{D}

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}\right].

Neural network approximation for the median function

In this section, we present our upper bounds for the approximation of the median function. As previously discussed, we establish tradeoffs between width and depth, showing that increasing depth allows a reduction in the required width.

We start with the simplest positive approximation result, which allows the approximation of the median using depth

\mathcal{O}(d^{2})

We emphasize that our constructions are effective for a significantly broader family of continuous distributions. Given that our analysis primarily requires input permutation invariance and sufficiently distinct values, extending these upper bounds to hold for any i.i.d. and bounded continuous distribution is not too difficult: the neural networks do not need to know the input distribution, only bounds on its range and density. However, to maintain clarity within an already technically demanding analysis, we focus on the uniform distribution to provide a more accessible exposition of the core mechanisms.
This result not only provides us with a constructive method for approximating the median using depth 3, it also gives a flavor of the arguments we use in our main result which build upon it, as this architecture is pivotal in building up more sophisticated constructions to extract the median.

The above construction, whose proof is available in Appendix

The main intuition behind the construction is that we can use the first hidden layer to compute pairwise indicators, comparing all pairs of inputs (hence the quadratic width requirement). Thereafter, the second hidden layer aggregates these results, identifies the entry whose indicators sum to the desired rank, and then outputs it. We remark that more generally, this implies a depth-3 width-
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})

3.2

\mathcal{O}(d^{5/3})

The following result demonstrates that even a small increase in depth from 3 to 5 can result in a meaningful reduction in the required width for approximating the median. Specifically, the reduction in width is from a quadratic to
\mathcal{O}(d^{5/3+\gamma})
, where
\gamma>0
is a confidence parameter that can be made arbitrarily small, at the cost of an (exponentially negligible) additional approximation error. More formally, our result is the following.

Theorem 3.2
.

For any dimension
d
, any target accuracy
\epsilon>0
, there exists a ReLU neural network
\mathcal{N}
of depth
5
and width
\mathcal{O}(d^{5/3+\gamma})
, and with magnitude of weights bounded by
\frac{12d^{6}}{\epsilon}
, such that

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{U}\left([0,1]^{d}\right)}\left[\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})-\med(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}\right]\leq\epsilon+\exp\left(-\Omega\left(d^{2\gamma}\right)\right).

The proof of the theorem, which appears in Appendix
C

Our architecture utilizes the first two hidden layers to partition the input into batches and compute their central elements using the construction devised in the previous subsection. Because a sufficiently large sample of central elements is exponentially likely to capture the global median, and because the resulting candidate set is significantly smaller than
d
, the next step employs two additional hidden layers to compare all candidates against all inputs. This identifies the true median while maintaining a width strictly smaller than quadratic. The parameter
\gamma
controls the confidence of this construction’s success, which leads to the inevitable exponentially small error floor.

We remark that our construction carries a width requirement with an exponent of at least
5/3
, representing a fundamental limitation of the current approach that cannot be further improved. In contrast, the analogous result for the maximum in
25
requires an exponent of only
4/3
. This suggests that while the median exhibits depth-width tradeoffs similar to those of the maximum, its required width appears to scale more steeply. This naturally raises the question of whether the median can be approximated by a depth-
\mathcal{O}(\log\log d)
, linear-width network, as is possible for the maximum function. In the next subsection, we show, perhaps surprisingly, that not only is linear width achievable, but it can even be attained using
constant

3.3
Depth
\mathcal{O}(1)
and width
\mathcal{O}(d)
median computation

The following is our main result in this paper.

Theorem 3.3
.

For any dimension
d
and any target accuracy
\epsilon>0
, there exists a ReLU neural network
\mathcal{N}
of depth
46
and width
\mathcal{O}(d)
, with magnitude of weights bounded by
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{\epsilon}\right)
, such that

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{U}\left([0,1]^{d}\right)}\left[\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})-\med(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}\right]\leq\epsilon+\exp\left(-d^{\Omega(1)}\right).

The proof of the above theorem, which appears in Appendix
D
, is technically involved and requires several intermediate constructions. Given the inherent complexity of the proof, we have prioritized clarity of exposition over obtaining a tighter depth analysis, and therefore we did not attempt to optimize the depth, which can likely be further improved to a constant smaller than 46.

At a high level, the proof relies on an iterative procedure that partitions the current elements into batches and propagates only their central elements, effectively zeroing out inputs unlikely to be the median. This process continues until no more than
d^{\alpha}
(for a sufficiently small
\alpha>0
) of the original inputs remain non-zero. While sorting these remaining elements using the construction from Theorem
3.1
would ideally yield the median, a significant implementation hurdle remains: ReLU networks cannot selectively sort only non-zero values. To circumvent this, we employ a novel hashing scheme that maps the non-zero elements into a reduced-dimension vector where they are guaranteed to be distinct with high probability. By subsequently sorting this hashed vector, we can extract the true median.

Lower bounds for computing the median

Having established upper bounds in the previous section, we now turn to the study of lower bounds. While this section, as with the rest of the paper, is primarily concerned with approximation results for the median function, we begin by considering the simpler case of exact computation. Specifically, we present a reduction from a recent exact computation result in
28

Theorem 4.1

(

)
.

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})=\max(\mathbf{x})

The previous section suggested that computing the median is inherently more difficult than computing the maximum, which is reflected in the additional insights required to compute the median in our upper bounds. Theorem

Because the median represents the most challenging rank to compute, the remainder of our analysis focuses exclusively on the median function. 5
Summary and future work

In this work, we have established several lower and upper bounds for the approximation of the median function using neural networks. Our analysis highlights that the median, which is inherently more complex than the maximum, requires novel techniques for efficient approximation, culminating in a constant-depth, linear-width construction for the uniform distribution over the unit hypercube. Together with our exact computation lower bound in Theorem
4.2
, these results establish the first separation between the exact and approximate computation regimes. Specifically, we demonstrate that relaxing the accuracy requirement to even an exponentially small error threshold allows for a dramatic reduction in the depth required to achieve a high-accuracy approximation using a linearly-sized network.

Furthermore, while our constant-depth, linear-width construction cannot achieve arbitrarily high accuracy via weight-scaling alone as the primary upper bound in
25
does, it offers a superior architecture for error thresholds exceeding the exponentially small regime. This highlights that for the levels of approximation most commonly encountered in practical settings, the architectural requirements for the median (and, by extension, for the maximum via Theorem
4.3
) may be significantly more modest than previously established.

There are several natural directions for future research. First, while our lower bounds establish that the median is at least as difficult to approximate as the maximum, our upper bounds strongly suggest that it is strictly more difficult. The latter required significantly more sophisticated insights and techniques to implement. It would be of great interest to explore whether this intuition can be formally verified by deriving lower bounds that do not rely on a reduction from the maximum, potentially yielding strictly stronger complexity results that are specific for the median function.

Second, given that scaling our weights beyond an exponential magnitude yields no additional benefit, it remains to be seen whether alternative constant-depth, linear-width constructions exist that can achieve arbitrarily high accuracy solely through weight-scaling, analogous to the primary result in
25
. Conversely, establishing a provable barrier to such accuracy in the linear-width regime would further delineate the fundamental trade-offs between parameter magnitude and network architecture.

Finally, while Corollary
4.8
establishes that a linear-width approximation of the median to exponentially small accuracy is impossible at depth 3, Theorem
3.3
demonstrates that such an approximation is achievable at depth 46. Since we did not attempt to optimize the constant 46, it remains an open and intriguing question to determine the precise minimal depth required to achieve this level of accuracy within the linear-width regime.

i\neq j
then
\mathcal{P}_{i}\cap\mathcal{P}_{j}=\phi
as there exists a coordinate

whose range of values in
\mathcal{P}_{i}
and in
\mathcal{P}_{j}
are completely disjoint. Also since
\mathcal{P}_{i}\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
we have
\cup_{i}\mathcal{P}_{i}\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
.

Putting all of the above together we get,

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{p}\sim\mathcal{U}\left([0,1]^{d}\right)}\left[\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{p})-\med(\mathbf{p})\right)^{2}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cup_{i}\mathcal{P}_{i}\subseteq[0,1]^{d}}}{{\geq}}\int_{\cup_{i}\mathcal{P}_{i}}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{p})-\med(\mathbf{p})\right)^{2}d\mathbf{p}\\
=\sum_{i}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{i}}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{p})-\med(\mathbf{p})\right)^{2}d\mathbf{p},\end{split}

(5)

where the last equality is due to the fact that
\mathcal{P}_{i}
’s are disjoint.

For ease of exposition, w.l.o.g. we focus on the set
\mathcal{P}_{1}
. We evaluate the integral inside the sum and using the change of variables
\mathbf{p}=P\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1}
,
d\mathbf{p}=\left|\det\left(P\right)\right|d\mathbf{x}
, we have

\begin{split}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{1}}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{p})-\med(\mathbf{p})\right)^{2}d\mathbf{p}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left(\mathcal{N}(P\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1})-\med(P\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1})\right)^{2}|\det(P)|d\mathbf{x}\end{split}

(6)

Letting
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denote the standard unit vector with coordinate
e_{i}=1
, we get from
P\mathbf{x}=\frac{1}{2d}\mathbf{v}x_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{d}\right)x_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}\in\ker(W)
that we can write
\mathcal{N}(P\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1})=c(x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})
for some function
c:\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
. Since
P
is triangular, we have
\left|\det(P)\right|=\frac{1}{2^{d}}\frac{1}{d}\left(1-\frac{2}{d}\right)^{d-1}v_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2^{d}}\frac{1}{10d}v_{1}
for
d\geq 2
. Moreover, since
\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{\infty}=v_{1}
and
\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}=1
, we have that
v_{1}\geq d^{-0.5}
and we can further lower bound the above to obtain
\left|\det(P)\right|\geq\frac{1}{2^{d}}\frac{1}{10d^{1.5}}
. Plugging in
\left|\det(P)\right|
and Equation (
3
) in Equation (
6
), we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left(\mathcal{N}(P\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1})-\med(P\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1})\right)^{2}|\det(P)|d\mathbf{x}\\
\geq\frac{1}{2^{d}}\frac{1}{10d^{1.5}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left(\mathcal{N}(P\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1})-\med(P\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1})\right)^{2}d\mathbf{x}\\
\geq\frac{1}{2^{d}}\frac{1}{10d^{1.5}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left(f(x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})-\left(\frac{1}{2d}v_{1}x_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)\right)\right)^{2}d\mathbf{x}\\
=\frac{1}{2^{d}}\frac{1}{10d^{1.5}}\int_{x_{d}}\ldots\int_{x_{2}}\int_{x_{1}}\left(f(x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})-\left(\frac{1}{2d}v_{1}x_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)\right)\right)^{2}dx_{1}dx_{2}\ldots dx_{d}.\end{split}

(7)

It is easy to verify that the optimal constant approximation for the linear function
\frac{1}{2d}v_{1}x_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)
is
\frac{1}{4d}v_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)
, in which case the optimal
L_{2}
approximation error is,

\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{4d}v_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)-\left(\frac{1}{2d}v_{1}x_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)\right)\right)^{2}dx=\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{4d^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)^{2}dx=\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{48d^{2}}.

Plugging the above back in Equation (
7
) and using the fact that
v_{1}\geq d^{-0.5}
again, we obtain,

\begin{split}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{1}}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{p})-\med(\mathbf{p})\right)^{2}d\mathbf{p}\geq\frac{1}{2^{d}}\frac{1}{10d^{1.5}}\int_{x_{d}}\ldots\int_{x_{2}}\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{48d^{2}}dx_{2}\ldots dx_{d}\geq\frac{1}{2^{d}}\frac{1}{480d^{4.5}}.\end{split}

(8)

It is easy to see that for any choice of the set
\mathcal{P}_{i}
defined by
(P,\mathbf{b}_{i}),
the lower bound on the approximation error is the same as given by Equation (
8
). Thus, plugging this error in Equation (
5
), we get

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{U}\left([0,1]^{d}\right)}\left[\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})-\med(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}\right]\geq\sum_{i}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{i}}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{p})-\med(\mathbf{p})\right)^{2}d\mathbf{p}\\
\geq\frac{c}{10\sqrt{d}}2^{d-1}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{d}}\frac{1}{480d^{4.5}}=\frac{c}{9600d^{5.5}},\\
\end{split}

where the second inequality follows from the lower bound on the total number of choices of
i
(Equation (
4

)), concluding the proof.

The below lemma states standard Chernoff bounds for the process of choosing a subset without replacement, often referred to as the
hypergeometric distribution
. These bounds are used in the probabilistic analysis of the subsampling process in Algorithm
5

Lemma F.3
.

Given a universe
U
of
d^{\prime}
elements, with
T
a subset of size
k
. Let
S
be a random subset of
U
of size
n
(chosen without replacement). Then we have the following bounds on the probability that the random variable

is far from its expectation

:

1.

\textrm{For any }\varepsilon\geq 0,\quad\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left|r-\frac{kn}{d^{\prime}}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right]\leq 2\exp\left({-2\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n}}\right)\quad\quad\quad\quad\textrm{(Additive Chernoff)}

2.

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[r\notin\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{kn}{d^{\prime}},2\frac{kn}{d^{\prime}}\right)\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8}\frac{kn}{d^{\prime}}\right)\quad\quad\textrm{(Multiplicative Chernoff for }\delta=\frac{1}{2},1)

3.

Generalizing the part 2 upper bound, for any
\delta\geq 0
,
\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\left[r\geq\frac{kn}{d^{\prime}}\left(1+\delta\right)\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}kn/d^{\prime}}{2+\delta}\right)

Proof.

The probability that a given entry (of the random permutation) is in

equals
\frac{n}{d^{\prime}}
. If all the entries were independent, then we are bounding the probability that the sum of
k
samples from a
\frac{n}{d^{\prime}}
biased coin is more than
\varepsilon
from
k\frac{n}{d^{\prime}}
. Additive and multiplicative Chernoff bounds yield the respective stated bounds
if
entries are independent.

Finally, we point out that a Chernoff bound for the

Chernoff bound for the “negatively associated” case, which includes sampling without replacement. (See
[
31
]
).

While the previous lemma gives concentration bounds for subsampling without replacement, we next use this to give concentration bounds a sort of “inverse” of this process.

Consider the following game. Alice’s favorite positive integer is

balls arbitrarily arranged at locations

Index
\mathbf{x}

.

F.2
Hash function construction

In this subsection, we explain how to adapt standard hash function techniques to construct a hash function family that will enable a collision-free hash of any
(d^{\prime},\varepsilon)
sparse input vector. Given a hash function
h:[d^{\prime}]\rightarrow[p]
, we apply it to a sparse vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
to map it to a smaller dimensional vector
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
by applying
h
to each input coordinate, and summing the results that map to the same coordinate: the
j^{\textrm{th}}
coordinate of the output will equal
y_{j}=\sum_{i:h(i)=j}x_{i}
. Thus if the locations of non-zero entries of
\mathbf{x}
get hashed to distinct locations by
h
, then these entries will be preserved in our smaller-dimensional vector
\mathbf{y}
.

The size of the hash family
\mathcal{H}
and its output dimension
p
are a function of
d^{\prime}
,
\varepsilon
and a tunable parameter
\gamma
that decides the regime of
d^{\prime}
for which the aforementioned success holds. We start with a few preliminaries before proceeding with the actual construction and its properties.

Definition F.5
.

We call a set
\mathcal{H}
of hash functions mapping from
U\to M
a
\delta
-nearly universal hash function family
if for all
x\neq y\in U
,

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{h\sim\mathcal{U}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)}\left[h(x)=h(y)\right]\leq\delta.

Definition F.6

(Vector representation of indices)
.

Given positive integers
d^{\prime},n,p^{\prime}
such that
n\geq\log_{p}d^{\prime}
, we can represent any number
i\in[d^{\prime}]
as an
n
-digit number in base
p
, considered as a vector with
n
entries. We denote the base-
p
representation of
i
as
\mathrm{base}_{[p]}(i)
, and, using standard indexing notation, the
j^{\textrm{th}}
digit of this base-
p
representation is
\mathrm{base}_{[p]}(i)_{j}
.

Next we construct the hash function family which will help us create
small
hash function families with the desired properties as stated at the start of the section.

Definition F.7

(Vectorized Hashing)
.

Given any
d^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{>0}
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1)
, we let
p
be the smallest prime number greater than
{d^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
. We
define a hashing scheme mapping indices
[d^{\prime}]\to\left[p\right]
using the base
p
vector representation
\mathrm{base}_{p}(i)
of indices
i\in[d^{\prime}]
using
n=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right\rceil
digits (Definition
F.6
). We define the hash function
h_{a}
for each
a\in[p]
as,

h_{a}(i)=a+\sum_{j=1}^{n}a^{j}\cdot\mathrm{base}_{p}(i)_{j}\mod p.

Define
\mathcal{H}_{d^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime}}
to consist of all such
h_{a}
, namely,
\mathcal{H}_{d^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime}}\coloneqq\left\{h_{a}:a\in[p]\right\}
.

In the following lemma we prove that the family of hash functions constructed in Definition
F.7
is a
\delta
-nearly universal hash function family (Definition
F.5
) for some
\delta
depending on the input and the output dimensions of the hash functions.

Lemma F.8
.

For any
d^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
, the hash function family
\mathcal{H}_{d^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime}}:[d^{\prime}]\to\left[p\right]
defined in Definition
F.7
is a
\delta
-
nearly universal hash function family
(Definition
F.5
) with
\delta=\frac{\left\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right\rceil}{d^{\prime\epsilon^{\prime}}}
where
p
is defined to be the the smallest prime number larger than
{d^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Picking
h_{a}\in\mathcal{H}_{d^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime}}
uniformly randomly, we can write the probability of collision between any
i,i^{\prime}\in[d]
with
i\neq i^{\prime}
as,

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{a\sim\mathcal{U}([p])}\left[h_{a}(i)=h_{a}(i^{\prime})\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{a\sim\mathcal{U}([p])}\left[a+\sum_{j=1}^{n}a^{j}\mathrm{base}_{p}(i)_{j}\mod p=a+\sum_{j=1}^{n}a^{j}\mathrm{base}_{p}(i^{\prime})_{j}\mod p\right]\\
=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{a\sim\mathcal{U}([p])}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}a^{j}\mathrm{base}_{p}(i)_{j}-\mathrm{base}_{p}(i^{\prime})_{j})\mod p=0\right].\end{split}

(12)

By our underlying construction (Definition
F.7
) and by our choice of
p
as the smallest prime number greater than
{d^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
and
n=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right\rceil
, we have
p^{n}\geq d^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
, implying that
n
-digit base-
p
representation is unique for each
i\in[d^{\prime}]
; in other words
i\neq i^{\prime}\implies\mathrm{base}_{p,n}(i)\neq\mathrm{base}_{p,n}(i^{\prime})
. Thus, the expression
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a^{j}\left(\mathrm{base}_{p}(i)_{j}-\mathrm{base}_{p}(i^{\prime})_{j}\right)\mod p
is a non-zero polynomial mod
p
(in the variable
a
) of degree at most
n
, and hence has at most
n=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right\rceil
roots. Namely, at most
n
out of the
p
hash functions in
\mathcal{H}_{d^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime}}
make
i
collide with
i^{\prime}
, giving us a bound on Equation
12
of

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{a\sim\mathcal{U}([p])}\left[h_{a}(i)=h_{a}(i^{\prime})\right]\leq\frac{n}{p}\leq\frac{\left\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right\rceil}{{d^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}},\end{split}

where the final inequality is by noting from the definition of
p
that
p\geq{d^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
, concluding the proof.

∎

In our construction we will want to hash a
(d^{\prime},\varepsilon)
sparse vector
\mathbf{x}
to a lower dimension in a way such that no collisions occur between any of its non-zero entries. Since we cannot induce randomness in a neural network we “derandomize” this by hashing

. In the following lemma we show that the family of Definition

sparse vectors without collisions.

Lemma F.9
.

For any
d^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{>0}

d^{\prime}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lceil\frac{1}{2\varepsilon+\gamma}\right\rceil\right)^{1/\gamma}

{d^{\prime}}^{2\varepsilon+\gamma}

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}

At a high level, the proof relies on the fact

\mathcal{H}

Formally, given any
(d^{\prime},\varepsilon)
sparse vector
\mathbf{x}

h
from
\mathcal{H}
then the probability that any two non-zero entries get hashed to the same location) is bounded as

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{h\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H})}\left[\text{Failure}\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{h\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H})}\left[\exists x_{i}\neq 0\neq x_{j};h(i)=h(j)\right]\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\\
i\neq j\\
x_{i}\neq 0\neq x_{j}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{h\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H})}\left[h(i)=h(j)\right]\\
\leq\binom{{d^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon}}{2}\delta<\frac{{d^{\prime}}^{2\varepsilon}}{2}\frac{\left\lceil\frac{1}{2\varepsilon+\gamma}\right\rceil}{{d^{\prime}}^{2\varepsilon+\gamma}}=\left\lceil\frac{1}{2\varepsilon+\gamma}\right\rceil\frac{1}{2{d^{\prime}}^{\gamma}}\leq 1,\end{split}

where the second inequality is from the fact that
\mathcal{H}
is
\delta
-almost universal, taking a union bound over all
\leq\binom{{d^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon}}{2}
pairs of
\{i,j\}
with non-zero entries, which is strictly less than
\frac{{d^{\prime}}^{2\varepsilon}}{2}
, as used in the third inequality;
and the last inequality is from the assumption
d^{\prime}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lceil\frac{1}{2\varepsilon+\gamma}\right\rceil\right)^{1/\gamma}
. Since we have bounded the probability over
h\in\mathcal{H}
of
any
collision as strictly less than 1, we conclude that there exists
h_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}
which has
no collisions
on the non-zero entries of the given
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, as desired.

∎

Appendix G
Neural network constructions and properties

Recall Definition
A.1
. In this section we describe all the components we assemble to produce the neural networks of our main results. We explicitly show how to implement various natural primitives with shallow neural networks, along with a few more technical primitives needed for the specifics of our algorithms. The particular “circuitry” required by these neural networks is sometimes slightly intricate, but overall none of these constructions should be surprising, and this section can be skipped on a first read.

We state each neural network in a definition, and describe its properties immediately afterwards in a lemma.

Sometimes our neural networks may make use of data from previous layers, which could be thought of as using a “skip connection” where the result of a previous layer is sent along a wire that “skips” some intermediate layer(s). However, “skip connections” may lead to subtleties in the definition of width and therefore we do
not
use them in this paper. Instead, we can emulate a skip connection by the
identity transform
, where an input
x
is preserved across a ReLU layer via adding 2 neurons to the width, using the identity
x=[x]_{+}-[-x]_{+}
.

G.1
Maximum neural network

Definition G.1
.

We define our
maximum
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{MAX}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\mathcal{N}^{MAX}\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)=\left[x_{2}\right]_{+}-\left[-x_{2}\right]_{+}+\left[x_{1}-x_{2}\right]_{+}.

G.2
Comparison neural network

We next define a neural network that tests whether
x_{1}>x_{2}
; this network is parameterized by a tolerance
\delta
, where the network will return the correct 0 or 1 answer
unless

x_{1},x_{2}
are within
\delta
of each other.

Definition G.2
.

We define our
comparison
neural network
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
as,

\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)=\left[\frac{1}{\delta}\left(x_{1}-x_{2}\right)\right]_{+}-\left[\frac{1}{\delta}\left(x_{1}-x_{2}-\delta\right)\right]_{+}

Fact G.3
.

Given
\delta>0
, for all inputs
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{If }x_{1}>x_{2}\text{ and }\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|\geq\delta,\\
0,&\text{If }x_{1}\leq x_{2},\\
\frac{1}{\delta}\left(x_{1}-x_{2}\right)\text{ otherwise }.\end{cases}

Additionally, this neural network has 1 hidden layer, employs 2 hidden neurons, and the magnitude of weights is upper bounded by
\frac{1}{\delta}
. Lastly, the output is always in the interval
[0,1]
.

G.3
Non-zero counter neural network

One important primitive in our algorithm is counting the number of non-zero entries in a nonnegative vector, which we implement by repeated application of the comparison neural network
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}
of Definition
G.2
. Parameterized by a tolerance
\delta>0
, we define the
non-zero counter
neural network
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}
to output the number of non-zero entries in a vector

\forall i,x_{i}\in\{0\}\cup[\delta,\infty)

Definition G.4
.

We define our
non-zero counter
neural network
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{R}
as,

\begin{split}\textrm{Given }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}:\quad\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{\prime}}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}\left(x_{i},0\right).\end{split}

Lemma G.5
.

The
non-zero counter
neural network
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}
(Definition
G.4
) when given an input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
such that
\forall i,x_{i}\in\{0\}\cup[\delta,\infty)

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{\prime}}\mathbbm{1}\{x_{i}>0\}.\end{split}

The
non-zero counter
neural network requires
1
hidden layer, a width of
2d^{\prime}
, and the magnitudes of the weights are bounded by
\frac{1}{\delta}
.

Proof.

The output is evident from the use of
comparison
neural network (Definition
G.2
,
\mathcal{N}^{C}_{\delta}
) and the assumption
\forall i,x_{i}\in\{0\}\cup[\delta,\infty)
. It is clear from Definition
G.4
and Definition
G.2
that
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}
only requires
1
hidden layer and each
i\in[d^{\prime}]
uses
2
neurons. Also from the use of
comparison
neural network (Definition
G.2
,
\mathcal{N}^{C}_{\delta}

\frac{1}{\delta}
.

∎

G.4
Masking neural network

We will often want to create a binary “mask”, recording which entries in a vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
lie in a given interval
[\ell,u]
. We make use of
2d^{\prime}
copies of our
comparison
neural network to construct this
masking
neural network, which will be correct as long as each input
x_{i}
is
either
inside the interval
[\ell,u]
or
\delta
-far from the interval
[\ell,u]
.

Definition G.6
.

We define our
masking
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{M}_{\delta}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
to have
i
’th output defined as,

\textrm{Given }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}},u>\ell,i\in[d^{\prime}]:\quad\mathcal{N}^{M}_{\delta}\left(\mathbf{x},u,\ell\right)[i]\coloneqq\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}(x_{i},\ell-\delta)-\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}(x_{i},u)

Lemma G.7
.

The
masking
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{M}_{\delta}
(Definition
G.6
) when given an input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
such that
\forall i\in[d^{\prime}]
we have
x_{i}\notin(\ell-\delta,\ell)\cup(u,u+\delta)
, will output a vector
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
where,

\begin{split}&x^{\prime}_{i}=\mathbbm{1}\{\ell\leq x_{i}\leq u\}.\\
\end{split}

The
masking
neural network requires
1
hidden layer, a width of
4d^{\prime}
, and the magnitudes of the weights are bounded by
\frac{1}{\delta}
.

Proof.

This network consists of
2d^{\prime}
copies of the
comparison
neural network
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}
of Definition
G.2
, each copy of which has 1 hidden layer, width 2, and magnitude of weights bounded by
\frac{1}{\delta}
by Fact
G.3
, which yields our depth, width, and weight bounds. Correctness for each
i
follows from the correctness of
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}
, described in Fact
G.3
.
∎

G.5
Filtering neural network

We next define a neural network that is intuitively similar to the
masking
network, except for
x_{i}\in[\ell,u]
we will return
x_{i}
itself instead of 1 (and 0 if
x_{i}
is
\delta
-far from the interval). We call this a
filtering
neural network—it filters out those
x_{i}
not in the interval
[\ell,u]
and leaves
x_{i}\in[\ell,u]
unchanged.

While the
filtering
neural network is analogous to the
masking
neural network, we will implement it different, explicitly defining a piecewise-linear function of
x_{i}
with 4 breakpoints. The input requirements for this network will be analogous to those for the
masking
network except with the additional requirement that all inputs
x_{i}\in[0,1]
.

Definition G.8
.

We define our
filtering
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{F}_{\delta}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
to have
i
’th output defined as,

\begin{split}&\textrm{Given }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}},u>\ell,i\in[d^{\prime}]:\\
\mathcal{N}^{F}_{\delta}\left(\mathbf{x},u,\ell\right)[i]&\coloneqq\left[\frac{1}{\delta}\left(x_{i}-\ell\right)+x_{i}\right]_{+}-\left[\frac{1}{\delta}\left(x_{i}-\ell\right)\right]_{+}-\left[\frac{1}{\delta}\left(x_{i}-u\right)\right]_{+}+\left[\frac{1}{\delta}\left(x_{i}-u\right)-x_{i}\right]_{+}.\end{split}

Lemma G.9
.

The
filtering
neural networks
\mathcal{N}^{F}_{\delta}
(Definition
G.8
) for
\delta\in(0,1]
, when given an input
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d^{\prime}}
and numbers
0\leq\ell<u\leq 1
such that
\forall i\in[d^{\prime}]
we have
x_{i}\notin(\ell-\delta,\ell)\cup(u,u+\delta)
, will output a vector
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
where,

\begin{split}&x^{\prime}_{i}=x_{i}\cdot\mathbbm{1}{\{\ell\leq x_{i}\leq u\}}.\\
\end{split}

The
filtering
neural network requires
1
hidden layer, a width of
4d^{\prime}
, and the magnitudes of the weights are bounded by
\frac{1}{\delta}+1
.

Proof.

To show correctness, we point out that
\mathcal{N}^{F}_{\delta}\left(\mathbf{x},u,\ell\right)[i]
is defined to be a piecewise-linear function of
x_{i}
with 4 breakpoints at
\frac{\ell}{1+\delta},\ell,u,\frac{u}{1-\delta}
. To the left of the first breakpoint, the function equals 0; between the first two breakpoints the function has slope
\frac{1}{\delta}+1
so attains value
\ell
at the second breakpoint,
x_{i}=\ell
; when
x_{i}\in[\ell,u]
the function has slope
1
and thus will equal
x_{i}
; to the right of the third breakpoint
u
, the function will have slope
\frac{1}{\delta}-1
, and will thus be 0 for
x_{i}
to the right of the last breakpoint. Our neural network will thus be correct for
x_{i}\in[\ell,u]
, and also for
x_{i}
beyond the two outermost breakpoints.

To show correctness for
x\leq\ell-\delta
, we have from above that the function is correct to the left of the first breakpoint, namely, for
x\leq\frac{\ell}{1+\delta}
, and we conclude from the fact that
\ell-\delta\leq\frac{\ell}{1+\delta}
, which we verify by multiplying through by
1+\delta
, moving all terms to the left, and dividing by
\delta
to get the equivalent easily checked inequality that
\ell-1-\delta\leq 0
, since
\ell<1
and
\delta>0
.

Finally, to show correctness for
x\geq u+\delta
though under the guarantee that
x\leq 1
, we have from above that the function is correct to the right of the last breakpoint, namely, for
x\geq\frac{u}{1-\delta}
. Thus the only cases where the function could be wrong are those for which
u+\delta<\frac{u}{1-\delta}
; solving for
u
shows that this can only be true when
u>1-\delta
; but since we only need to show correctness for
x\geq u+\delta
, this means we only need to show correctness when
x>1
, which is trivially true since these values of
x
are out of range of our assumptions, thus showing the lemma.

This network uses
1
hidden layer, a width of
4d^{\prime}
, and the magnitudes of the weights are bounded by
\frac{1}{\delta}+1
.
∎

G.6
Indicator function product neural network

We introduce a simple neural network here that, given an input
x\in[0,1]
and
s\in\mathbb{Z}
will return
x\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{s=0\}
.

Definition G.10
.

We define our
indicator function product
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as,

\begin{split}&\textrm{Given }x,s\in\mathbb{R},\quad\mathcal{N}^{IFP}(x,s)=[x+s]_{+}-[x+s-1]_{+}-[s]_{+}+[s-1]_{+}.\end{split}

Lemma G.11
.

The
indicator function product
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}
(Definition
G.10
), when given input
x\in[0,1]
and integer
s\in\mathbb{Z}
outputs

\mathcal{N}^{IFP}(x,s)=x\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{s=0\}.

The
indicator function product
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}
has 1 hidden layer, width 4, and magnitude of weights 1.

Further, for any
x,s\in\mathbb{R}
, the output of the neural network has magnitude at most
|x|
.

Proof.

For convenience, we define the function
\mathrm{trim}_{[0,1]}(y)
to “trim” the real number
y
to the range
[0,1]
, defined equivalently as
\mathrm{trim}_{[0,1]}(y)\coloneqq\max(0,\min(1,y))
.
It is straightforward to check that trimming can be implemented as the difference of two ReLU units: for a real number
y
, we have
[y]_{+}-[y-1]_{+}=\mathrm{trim}_{[0,1]}(y)
. We use this relation twice in the equation defining
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}(x,s)
to see that
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}(x,s)
equals

\mathrm{trim}_{[0,1]}(x+s)-\mathrm{trim}_{[0,1]}(s)

(13)

Recall that we assume
x\in[0,1]
. When
s=0
, Equation
13
equals
\mathrm{trim}_{[0,1]}(x)=x
; when
s<0
, since
s\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
s\leq-1
, and thus both terms of Equation
13
get trimmed to 0; and analogously, when
s>0
, both terms of Equation
13
get trimmed to 1, and thus yield a difference of 0. Thus in all cases, Equation
13
equals
x\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{s=0\}
, yielding correctness.

This neural network clearly has 1 hidden layer, 4 neurons, and weights of magnitude 1.

Finally, to bound the magnitude for arbitrary
x,s\in\mathbb{R}
, we point out that Equation
13
equals 0 when
x=0
, and, when considered as a function of
x
, has Lipschitz constant 1 since the
\mathrm{trim}()
function has Lipschitz constant 1. Thus
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}(x,s)
has magnitude at most
|x|
, as claimed.
∎

G.7
Rank selection neural network

We now define the
rank selection
neural network, which implicitly sorts its input using an all-pairs quadratic-width approach, and returns elements of the desired ranks. This neural network is the central component of the depth 3, quadratic width median finding result in Theorem
3.1
. While for the subsequent sub-quadratic median finding neural networks we cannot use this network directly on the entire input, this network is a crucial component once we have used other techniques to reduce the size of the input.

The idea behind this neural network is to compare all pairs of elements and use the number of comparisons “won” by each entry to determine if that entry has the designated rank, and should hence be returned.

Definition G.12
.

We define our
rank selection
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{RS}_{\delta}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
as,

\begin{split}\textrm{Given }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}},\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\quad\textrm{ and letting, }\quad y_{k}=1+\sum_{j\in[d^{\prime}]}\mathcal{N}^{C}_{\delta}(x_{k},x_{j}),\quad k\in[d^{\prime}],\\
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{RS}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}\right)[i]=\sum_{k\in[d^{\prime}]}\mathcal{N}^{IFP}(x_{k},r_{i}-y_{k}),\end{split}

where
\mathcal{N}^{RS}_{\delta}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}\right)[i]
is the
i^{\prime}
th coordinate of the output.

Lemma G.13
.

The
rank selection
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{RS}_{\delta}
(Definition
G.12
), when given an input
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d^{\prime}}
where for all
j\neq k
we have either
|x_{j}-x_{k}|\geq\delta
or
x_{j}=x_{k}=0
, and given
a vector of ranks
\mathbf{r}\in\{1,\ldots,d^{\prime}\}^{p}
, outputs a vector
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with

x^{\prime}_{i}=\mathcal{R}_{r_{i}}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\forall i\in[p].

Further, given arbitrary input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
we always have the bound

|x^{\prime}_{i}|\leq d^{\prime}\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|_{\infty}.

Moreover, the
rank selection
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{RS}_{\delta}
requires
2
hidden layers, a width of at most
\max(2{d^{\prime}}^{2}+2d^{\prime}+2p,4pd^{\prime})
and the magnitudes of weights are bounded by
\frac{1}{\delta}
.

Proof.

We first show the correctness property.

The neural network first computes
y_{k}
, comparing
x_{k}
with every
x_{j}
and returning 1 plus the number of strictly smaller elements, yielding that
y_{k}
will be the rank of
x_{k}
in
\mathbf{x}
. We implement this with the
comparison
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{C}_{\delta}
, which will return correct answers by Fact
G.3
because all pairs
x_{j},x_{k}
are either identical or
\delta
-separated. (We point out that our input
\mathbf{x}
may have repeated zeros, and for all such elements we will compute
y_{k}=1
because there are no strictly smaller elements.)

The
i^{\textrm{th}}
element of the return vector is computed in the next line via a
d^{\prime}
-way sum with respect to
k
over
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}(x_{k},r_{i}-y_{k})
, which we analyze with Lemma
G.11
, since
x_{k}\in[0,1]
and
y_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
. By Lemma
G.11
the neural network thus returns, as its
i^{\textrm{th}}
entry,
\sum_{k\in[d^{\prime}]}x_{k}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{y_{k}=r_{i}\}
. Namely, given as input a desired rank
r_{i}
, we return the sum of all entries
x_{k}
whose rank (previously stored as
y_{k}
) equals
r_{i}
. Since all entries except 0 are unique, the neural network will correctly return the element of rank
r_{i}
.

Next, we bound the return value of the neural network for arbitrary
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
: by Lemma
G.11
, the expression
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}(x_{k},r_{i}-y_{k})
has magnitude at most
|x_{k}|
. Summing this over
k\in[d^{\prime}]
immediately gives our desired universal bound
|x^{\prime}_{i}|\leq d^{\prime}\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|_{\infty}
.

For the depth of the network, we use two hidden layers, one corresponding to
\mathcal{N}^{C}_{\delta}
and the other for
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}
. For the width in the first hidden layer, we compute
\mathcal{N}^{C}_{\delta}\left(x_{k},x_{j}\right)
for all pairs
j,k\in[d^{\prime}]
and since
\mathcal{N}^{C}_{\delta}\left(x_{i},x_{j}\right)
requires
2
neurons (Fact
G.3
) this contributes width
2{d^{\prime}}^{2}
in the first layer. In the second layer we use inputs
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}
, and thus need to add 2 extra neurons in the first layer to compute the identity function for each value that we want to reuse later, contributing the remaining
2d^{\prime}+2p
to the width of the first layer. In the second hidden layer we use we use
p\cdot d^{\prime}
copies of
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}
, thus requiring
4pd^{\prime}
neurons (by Lemma
G.11
); taking the max of the neurons in the two layers gives us the stated width bound. The upper bound on the magnitude of the weights follows from the
comparison
neural network (Definition
G.2
,
\mathcal{N}^{C}_{\delta}
) that requires weights of magnitude
\frac{1}{\delta}
(Fact
G.3
), since
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}
only has weights of magnitude 1 by Lemma
G.11
.
∎

G.8
Non-zero element shortlisting neural network

We next define another key primitive that we exclusively use in our linear width construction. The

Lemma G.15

The

The proof is similar to the proof of Lemma

Since

\mathcal{N}^{NZES}_{\delta,p}\left(\mathbf{x},u,\ell\right)[i]=\sum_{j\in[d^{\prime}]}f_{j}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{i=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{j}y_{j^{\prime}}\}

Namely, defining for the purposes of analysis a vector

j
entries of
\mathbf{x}
are non-zero entries in the range
[\ell,u]
; the indicator function will evaluate whether this matches
i
and return
f_{j}
in this case. Since (as shown above)
f_{j}=x_{j}\mathbbm{1}\{x_{j}\in[\ell,u]\}
, we conclude that
\mathcal{N}^{NZES}_{\delta,p}\left(\mathbf{x},u,\ell\right)[i]
will return the
i^{\textrm{th}}
non-zero entry of
\mathbf{x}
lying in the range
[\ell,u]
, if such an entry exists, concluding the correctness proof.

The bounds on the width, depth, and weights result from corresponding bounds on the components
\mathcal{N}^{MAX}(\ell,\delta/2)
,
\mathcal{N}_{\delta/2}^{M}(\mathbf{x},u,\ell^{\prime})
,
\mathcal{N}^{F}_{\delta}\left(\mathbf{x},u,\ell\right)
, and
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}(f_{j},i-\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{j}y_{j^{\prime}})
from Lemmas
G.7
,
G.9
,
G.11
respectively.

∎

G.9
Rank computing neural network

Recall that the idea of Algorithm
5
is to repeatedly “filter”
\mathbf{x}
by zeroing out elements not lying in an interval
[\ell,u]
, in such a way that
\med(\mathbf{x})
is preserved, while returning a much sparser vector
\mathbf{y}
. A crucial component of this is determining the rank
r
of
\med(\mathbf{x})
in (the non-zero portion of) the new vector
\mathbf{y}
, which we do in the
rank computing
neural network. We compute this rank
r
taking an element
e\in\mathbf{y}^{\neq 0}
and noticing that, since
\mathbf{y}^{\neq 0}
is a contiguous portion of
\mathbf{x}
, the difference in ranks of
e,\med(\mathbf{x})
in
\mathbf{x}
equals the difference of their ranks in
\mathbf{y}^{\neq 0}
, and since we know the rank of
\med(\mathbf{x})
in
\mathbf{x}
equals
d^{\prime}/2
, we can recover
r
by computing the rank of
e
in both vectors, and solving for
r
.

Definition G.16
.

Given
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}
, and an element
e\in\mathbb{R}
we define our
rank-computing
neural network
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{RC}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as,

\begin{split}&\textrm{Given }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}},e\in\mathbb{R},\\
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{RC}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},e\right)&=d^{\prime}/2-d^{\prime\prime}+\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}(\mathbf{y})+\sum_{j\in[d^{\prime\prime}]}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}\left(e,y_{j}\right)-\sum_{j\in[d^{\prime}]}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}\left(e,x_{j}\right).\end{split}

Lemma G.17
.

The
rank-computing
neural network
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{RC}
(Definition
G.16
) when given an entirely non-zero input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}^{d^{\prime}}_{\delta}
and another input
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S}^{d^{\prime\prime}}_{\delta}
, whose non-zero entries form a contiguous block in a sorted version of
\mathbf{x}
containing
\med(\mathbf{x})
along with a third input
e
that is any non-zero entry of
\mathbf{y}
, the network outputs a rank
r
such that

\mathcal{R}_{r}\left(\mathbf{y}^{\neq 0}\right)=\med(\mathbf{x}).

Moreover the
rank-computing
requires
1
hidden layer, a width of
\mathcal{O}(\max(d^{\prime},d^{\prime\prime}))
and the magnitudes of weights are bounded by
\frac{1}{\delta}
.

Proof.

Recalling that input
e
is any non-zero entry of
\mathbf{y}
, let
r_{e,\mathbf{x}}
denote its rank among the entries of
\mathbf{x}
and let
r_{e,\mathbf{y}}
denote the rank of
e
among the entries of
\mathbf{y}^{\neq 0}
. Further, let
r
be the rank of
\med(\mathbf{x})
among the non-zero entries of
\mathbf{y}
, where the rank of
\med(\mathbf{x})
in
\mathbf{x}
equals
d^{\prime}/2
by definition of the median. Since both
e
and
\med(\mathbf{x})
lie in the contiguous block
\mathbf{y}^{\neq 0}
, when
\mathbf{x}
is sorted, we have that the difference of ranks of
e
and
\med(\mathbf{x})
in
\mathbf{x}
equals the difference of ranks of these elements in
\mathbf{y}^{\neq 0}
, yielding

\begin{split}r_{e,\mathbf{x}}-d^{\prime}/2=r_{e,\mathbf{y}}-r.\end{split}

(14)

We use this expression to show that our neural network
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{RC}
correctly computes
r
. Since
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}^{d^{\prime}}_{\delta}
and non-zero, all the elements of
\mathbf{x}
are
\delta
-separated, and thus we compute the rank of
e
in
\mathbf{x}
as
r_{e,\mathbf{x}}=1+\sum_{j\in[d^{\prime}]}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}\left(e,x_{j}\right)
, by applying Fact
G.3
to correctly count 1 plus the number of
x_{j}
that are smaller than
e
. Analogously, the rank of
e
in
\mathbf{y}
(including the zero entries for the moment) equals
1+\sum_{j\in[d^{\prime\prime}]}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}\left(e,y_{j}\right)
; and the number of zero entries is found by taking
d^{\prime\prime}
minus the result from the
non-zero counting
neural network (Definition
G.4
)
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}(\mathbf{y})
. Subtracting yields that the rank of
e
in
\mathbf{y}^{\neq 0}
equals
r_{e,\mathbf{y}}=1+\sum_{j\in[d^{\prime\prime}]}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}\left(e,y_{j}\right)-\left(d^{\prime\prime}-\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}(\mathbf{y})\right)
. Solving for
r
in Equation
14
, we find that
r
is exactly the expression computed by our overall neural network.

Since our neural network applies
\mathcal{N}^{NZC}_{\delta}
once, to an input of size
d^{\prime\prime}
, and, in parallel applies
\mathcal{N}^{C}_{\delta}

d^{\prime}+d^{\prime\prime}
times, we have (from Fact
G.3
and Lemma
G.5
) that this network has 1 hidden layer, a width of
\mathcal{O}(\max(d^{\prime},d^{\prime\prime}))
and the magnitudes of weights bounded by
\frac{1}{\delta}
.
∎

G.10
Rank scaling neural network

In our construction, one of the intermediate steps is to compute an analog rank
r^{\prime}
, of a particular rank
r
among a small random subset of entries of the non-zero entries. To do this we need to multiply the rank with the size of this subset and divide it by the number of non-zero entries which is non-trivial as the number of non-zero entries in our construction is a random quantity and we cannot pre-compute it. The
rank scaling
neural network helps us do this by simply computing all possible values
r^{\prime}
can take and zeroing out all but the one that is the true value. Given
b\in[d^{\prime}]
, a rank
r
of a coordinate among the non-zero entries of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}^{d^{\prime}}
and assuming
|\mathbf{x}^{\neq 0}|\geq 1
, this outputs
r^{\prime}
defined as,

r^{\prime}=\frac{rb}{|\mathbf{x}^{\neq 0}|}.

Definition G.18
.

Given
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
and
r\in\mathbb{R}
we define our
rank scaling
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{RSC}_{\delta,b}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as,

\begin{split}\textrm{Given }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}},r\in\mathbb{R},\textrm{ let}\quad\quad\quad\quad z=\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}(\mathbf{x}),\\
r^{\prime}=\sum_{k=1}^{d^{\prime}}\frac{d^{\prime}b}{k}\cdot\mathcal{N}^{IFP}\left(\frac{r}{d^{\prime}},z-k\right)\quad\text{(Definition~\ref{defn:indicator_net})}.\end{split}

Lemma G.19
.

The
rank scaling
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{RSC}_{\delta,b}
(Definition
G.18
) when given
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}^{d^{\prime}}_{\delta}
with
|\mathbf{x}^{\neq 0}|\geq 1
, and an input
r\in[0,d^{\prime}]
, outputs
r^{\prime}
where

\begin{split}r^{\prime}=\frac{rb}{|\mathbf{x}^{\neq 0}|}.\end{split}

Moreover
rank scaling
neural network requires
2
hidden layers, a width of
4d^{\prime}
and magnitude of weights bounded by
\max\left(d^{\prime}b,\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}^{d^{\prime}}_{\delta}
we have by Lemma
G.5
that
z=\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}(\mathbf{x})
correctly computes the number of non-zero entries in
\mathbf{x}
, namely
|\mathbf{x}^{\neq 0}|
. In the next line, we apply
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}
, where since
\frac{r}{d^{\prime}}\in[0,1]
and both
z,k
are integers, we have by Lemma
G.11
that the output value satisfies
r^{\prime}=\sum_{k=1}^{d^{\prime}}\frac{d^{\prime}b}{k}\frac{r}{d^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{k=z\}
. Thus the sum has a non-zero contribution only for the term where
k=|\mathbf{x}^{\neq 0}|
, and thus the neural network outputs
r^{\prime}=\frac{rb}{|\mathbf{x}^{\neq 0}|}
, as desired.

The number of hidden layers required by
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}
is
1
, from Lemma
G.5
, and 1 more for
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}
from Lemma
G.11
. The width of the first layer is
2d^{\prime}
from Lemma
G.5
, and the second layer has width
4d^{\prime}
from Lemma
G.11
. The magnitude of weights required by
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}
is
1/\delta
, and
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}
has weights bounded by 1 by Lemma
G.11
, but we scale the output by
\frac{d^{\prime}b}{k}\leq d^{\prime}b
, to give an overall bound of
\max(d^{\prime}b,1/\delta)
.
∎

G.11
Ceiling neural network

In each step of our construction, we compute ranks scaled by certain quantities to obtain an analogous rank
r^{\prime}
of the
median
in a smaller subset. Since ranks can only be integers we convert this to an integer through the
ceiling
(
\left\lceil.\right\rceil
) or
floor
(
\left\lfloor.\right\rfloor
) operations using the
ceiling
neural network (note that
\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor=-\left\lceil-x\right\rceil
). We are able to do this by exploiting the finite structure of the set of values
r^{\prime}
can take. The
ceiling
neural network when input
\frac{a}{b}
where
b\in[d^{\prime}]
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
-d^{\prime}\leq\frac{a}{b}\leq d^{\prime}
computes
\left\lceil\frac{a}{b}\right\rceil
.

Definition G.20
.

We define our
ceiling
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{CEI}_{d^{\prime}}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

as,

\begin{split}\textrm{Given }x\in\mathbb{R},\\
\mathcal{N}^{CEI}_{d^{\prime}}(x)=-d^{\prime}+\sum_{i\in\{-d^{\prime},\ldots,d^{\prime}\}}d^{\prime}\left(\left[x-i\right]_{+}-\left[x-i-\frac{1}{d^{\prime}}\right]_{+}\right).\end{split}

Lemma G.21
.

The
ceiling
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{CEI}_{d^{\prime}}
(Definition
G.20
), when given input
x\in[-d^{\prime},d^{\prime}]
that is also rational number
x=\frac{a}{b}
with denominator
b\in\{1,\ldots,d^{\prime}\}
will output the ceiling of
x
,

\mathcal{N}^{CEI}_{d^{\prime}}\left(x\right)=\left\lceil x\right\rceil.

Moreover, the
ceiling
neural network requires
1
hidden layer, a width of at most
4(d^{\prime}+1)
and magnitude of weights
d^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We analyze the expression inside the sum for rational
x
. In the case
x\leq i
, then we have
d^{\prime}\left(\left[x-i\right]_{+}-\left[x-i-\frac{1}{d^{\prime}}\right]_{+}\right)=0
. On the other hand, if
x>i
, for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x
a rational number with denominator at most
d^{\prime}
, then
x>i+\frac{1}{d^{\prime}}
, yielding
d^{\prime}\left(\left[x-i\right]_{+}-\left[x-i-\frac{1}{d^{\prime}}\right]_{+}\right)=1
. Thus in general, this expression in the sum equals the indicator function
\mathbbm{1}\{x>i\}
. Thus our neural network computes
\mathcal{N}^{CEI}_{d^{\prime}}(x)=-d^{\prime}+\sum_{i\in\{-d^{\prime},\ldots,d^{\prime}\}}\mathbbm{1}\{x>i\}
, which for
x\in[-d^{\prime},d^{\prime}]
must return exactly
\lceil x\rceil
, as desired.

The number of hidden layers, the width, and the magnitudes of weights are clear from the definition.
∎

G.12
Hashing neural network

In our construction, in one of the steps we reduce the dimensionality of our problem by hashing the locations in a sparse vector to a smaller-dimensional vector, according to hash functions
h
belonging to some family
\mathcal{H}
. Each such hash function is an explicit linear transformation with
0,1
coefficients: for a fixed hash function
h:[d^{\prime}]\to[p^{\prime}]
, and given an input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, we output
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}}
where
y_{i}=\sum_{j:\,h(j)=i}x_{j}
.
This linear transform uses 0 ReLU layers in a neural network; however, we choose to include an artificial ReLU layer in our construction to separate this linear transform from any subsequent processing that occurs. This helps us to treat this neural network as an independent primitive in our construction, simplifying the explanation of our construction.

Definition G.22
.

Given a hash function
h:[d^{\prime}]\to[p^{\prime}]
, we define our
hashing
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{H}_{h}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}}
as,

\begin{split}\textrm{Given }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}},\\
\mathcal{N}^{H}_{h}(\mathbf{x})[i]=\left[\sum_{j:\,h(j)=i}x_{j}\right]_{+}.\end{split}

Lemma G.23
.

The hashing neural network
\mathcal{N}^{H}_{h}
(Definition
G.22
) when given an non-negative input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
outputs
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}}
where each coordinate
y_{i}
is the sum of entries of
\mathbf{x}
hashed to position
i
.
Moreover, hashing neural network require one hidden layer, a width of
p^{\prime}
and weights in
\{0,1\}
.

Proof.

The first statement is true by definition and noting that the entries of
\mathbf{x}
are positive, while the second statement is straightforward from the definition.
∎

G.13
Block extraction neural network

Recall that Algorithm
7
takes as input a sparse vector with
s
non-zero entries, and aims to return its non-zero entries by first trying a few hash functions to hash the locations to a smaller domain, and then identifying a hash function that leads to no collisions, so that we can extract the non-zero entries with small width. In this section we describe the neural network that looks through the results of applying
q
different hash functions, each mapping to a set of size
p
, and identifies the results of the first hash function that has led to zero collisions. Explicitly, we describe a neural network that takes as input
q
blocks of size
p
, and returns the first of these blocks that has exactly
s
non-zero entries; we call this the
block extraction
neural network. This network essentially consists of three layers of indicator functions, assembled via appropriate linear transforms to compute the desired output.

Definition G.24
.

We define our
block extraction
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{BE}_{\delta,p}:\mathbb{R}^{pq}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
by

\begin{split}\textrm{Given }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{pq},s\in\mathbb{R},\\
c_{i}=\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}(x_{p(i-1)+1},\ldots,x_{p\cdot i}),\,\forall i\in[q],\\
m_{i}=\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}(c_{i},s-1)-\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}(c_{i},s),\,\forall i\in[q],\\
\mathcal{N}^{BE}_{\delta,p}(\mathbf{x},s)[j]=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\mathcal{N}^{IFP}\left(x_{j+(i-1)p},m_{i}-1-\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}m_{i^{\prime}}\right),\forall j\in[p]\end{split}

where
\mathcal{N}^{BE}_{\delta,p}(\mathbf{x},s)[j]
is the
j
’th coordinate of the output.

Lemma G.25
.

The
block extraction
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{BE}_{\delta,p}:\mathbb{R}^{pq}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
, when given an input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{pq}
all of whose entries are in the set
\{0\}\cup[\delta,1]
, and given an input
s\in\mathbb{Z}
, will consider
\mathbf{x}
as being divided into
q
blocks of size
p
, and will return a copy of the first block that contains exactly
s
non-zero entries (returning zeros if no such block exists).

The
block extraction
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{BE}_{\delta,p}
requires 3 hidden layers, has width
\mathcal{O}(pq)
, and the magnitudes of the weights are bounded by
\frac{1}{\delta}
.

Proof.

We first apply the
non-zero counter
neural network
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{NZC}
(Definition
G.4
) to each of the
q
blocks of the input, correctly storing in
c_{i}
the number of non-zero entries in block
i
, by Lemma
G.5
. Next, for each
c_{i}
we compute in
m_{i}
the indicator value of whether
c_{i}=s
, which we compute by two applications of the comparison neural network
\mathcal{N}_{d}^{C}
(defined in Definition
G.2
and shown correct in Fact
G.3
).

The final output step is the most intricate. Recall that the
indicator function product
neural network
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}
, on input a real number
y\in[0,1]
and an integer
r
, returns
y\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{r=0\}
(see Definition
G.10
and Lemma
G.11
). Thus the
j
’th output of our neural network equals
\sum_{i=1}^{q}x_{j+(i-1)p}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left\{m_{i}=1+\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}m_{i^{\prime}}\right\}
. We analyze the indicator function: since
m_{i}
each
m_{i}
is either 0 or 1, the indicator function condition “
m_{i}=1+\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}m_{i^{\prime}}
” will be true only when
m_{i}
is 1, and when
i
is the
first
such index (so that
\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}m_{i^{\prime}}
will be 0). Thus the
j
’th output of our neural network looks at the
j
’th element of each of the
q
different blocks

, and outputs it only for the
first
block with exactly
s
non-zero entries, as desired.

The bounds on the width, depth, and weights result from corresponding bounds on the components
\mathcal{N}^{NZC}_{\delta}
,
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}^{C}
, and
\mathcal{N}^{IFP}
from Fact
G.3
and Lemmas
G.5
and
G.11
respectively.
∎
