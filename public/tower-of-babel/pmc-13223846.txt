Pharmacist independent prescribing in Middle Eastern countries: Insights from a systematic review of awareness, readiness, and barriers to implementation☆
Authors: Farah Kais Alhomoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndependent Prescribing Practice (IPP) by pharmacists has been internationally recognized as an effective strategy to improve healthcare access, optimize medication use, and reduce physician workload. This review aimed to examine pharmacists' awareness, readiness, attitudes systematically, and perceived barriers and facilitators toward implementing IPP in selected Middle Eastern countries.MethodsA systematic review followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for conducting and reporting systematic reviews and the Cochrane Handbook guidelines. PubMed, Web of Science, regional journals, and the grey literature published between 2015 and 2025 was systematically searched using predefined keywords related to pharmacist prescribing. Eligible studies were quantitative, peer-reviewed (qualitative studies were excluded), and focused on pharmacists' readiness or perceptions of IPP in the Middle East. Data on study characteristics, awareness, readiness, barriers, and facilitators were extracted and synthesized descriptively.ResultsNine cross-sectional studies from Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and the United Arab Emirates (UAE), involving pharmacists, were included in this analysis. Pharmacists demonstrated high readiness and positive attitudes toward IPP, acknowledging its potential to enhance patient access and improve healthcare efficiency. However, implementation remains limited by the absence of legal frameworks, insufficient diagnostic training, and weak interprofessional collaboration. Identified facilitators included national health reforms, professional development initiatives, and institutional support for expanding clinical roles.ConclusionPharmacists in Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and the UAE demonstrate readiness to adopt independent prescribing; however, evidence is limited to these countries and cannot be generalized to the wider Middle East. Structural, regulatory, and educational barriers persist, highlighting the need for context-specific policy and training frameworks.Keywords: Middle East, Pharmacists, Independent prescribing, Extended pharmacy service

Background

Globally, healthcare systems are experiencing increasing pressure due to the rising burden of chronic diseases, population aging, and a projected shortage of more than ten million healthcare professionals by 2030.1, 2, 3. In response, many countries, including the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, and Australia, have expanded pharmacists' clinical roles by introducing independent prescribing authority as part of broader workforce transformation and healthcare reform strategies.4, 5 International evidence over the past two decades demonstrates that pharmacist independent prescribing can improve access to care, optimize medication use, enhance continuity of care, and reduce physician workload, while maintaining patient safety and quality comparable to physician prescribing.1, 2

Independent prescribing practice (IPP) is defined as the autonomous authority of pharmacists to assess patients, make clinical decisions, and prescribe prescription-only medicines within their scope of competence, without requiring prior authorization from a physician. This definition aligns with international literature, distinguishing independent prescribing from dependent or collaborative models.2 Throughout this review, the term independent prescribing practice (IPP) is used consistently to refer to autonomous pharmacist prescribing; related terms such as “pharmacist prescribing” or “independent prescribing authority” are used only when reflecting terminology reported in the original studies. IPP is further distinguished from other pharmacist prescribing models. Supplementary prescribing refers to a collaborative arrangement conducted under a physician-approved clinical management plan. In contrast, minor ailment prescribing involves the supply or recommendation of medicines for self-limiting conditions within predefined protocols.2 Therapeutic interchange refers to modifying an existing prescription within an established formulary or institutional guideline, rather than initiating independent prescribing decisions. These models are conceptually distinct from IPP, which involves autonomous clinical decision-making, and were therefore not considered equivalent in this review.2

In contrast, independent prescribing practice (IPP) in the Middle East remains at an early stage of development. Although pharmacy practice across the region has gradually shifted from a product-oriented model toward patient-centred pharmaceutical care, formal independent prescribing authority for pharmacists remains limited.6 Several structural and professional barriers have constrained implementation, including the absence of clear legal and regulatory frameworks, insufficient clinical and diagnostic training, and limited interprofessional collaboration between pharmacists and physicians.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 As a result, the role of pharmacists as independent prescribers has not yet been systematically embedded within healthcare systems in most Middle Eastern countries.

Despite growing international recognition of IPP, evidence from the Middle East remains fragmented and largely exploratory. Available studies have predominantly focused on pharmacists' awareness, attitudes, and perceived readiness for independent prescribing practice (IPP), rather than on implementation outcomes, clinical effectiveness, or patient safety. In this context, awareness refers to the level of knowledge and familiarity with IPP, including its scope, regulatory frameworks, and professional roles.5, 8, 9, 13 In contrast, readiness refers to the extent to which individuals or healthcare systems are prepared to implement IPP, encompassing willingness, perceived competence, and the availability of supporting structures such as training and legislation. These constructs are commonly used in pharmacy practice research to assess preparedness for expanded clinical roles.5, 8, 9, 13 To date, limited systematic evidence has synthesized this perception-based literature in the Middle Eastern context. Nevertheless, consolidating such evidence is essential to inform policymakers, educators, and regulatory bodies, particularly in settings where IPP remains at an early stage of conceptualization and policy development.

Accordingly, this systematic review aims to synthesize available quantitative evidence on pharmacists' awareness, readiness, attitudes, and perceived barriers and facilitators related to implementing independent prescribing practice in Middle Eastern countries. By systematically collating and analysing existing perception-based studies, this review seeks to support early framework development, guide educational and regulatory planning, and inform future research directions, rather than to assert outcome-proven readiness for the implementation of IPP.

Methods

Study design and reporting framework

This study was a systematic review of peer-reviewed quantitative evidence examining pharmacists' awareness, readiness, attitudes, and perceived barriers and facilitators to independent prescribing practice (IPP) in Middle Eastern countries. The review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (Fig. 1) and followed methodological guidance from the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.14, 15 Although the review question is descriptive, a systematic review approach was selected because it involves the application of predefined eligibility criteria, reproducible search strategies, and formal methodological quality appraisal. This approach was chosen over a scoping review to allow for structured methodological appraisal and critical interpretation of study quality, thereby supporting more robust, policy-relevant conclusions. The review protocol was not registered in PROSPERO or any other registry.

Studies were excluded if they were reviews, qualitative-only studies, editorials, commentaries, letters, conference abstracts, or scholarly opinions. Studies focusing exclusively on collaborative or supplementary prescribing models, involving pharmacy students or trainees rather than licensed pharmacists, or lacking accessible full text were also excluded. Qualitative-only studies were intentionally excluded to maintain methodological consistency and to enable structured comparison of quantitative, perception-based outcomes (awareness, readiness, barriers, and facilitators) across countries and practice settings. While qualitative evidence provides valuable contextual insights, this review was designed to support early workforce assessment and policy-oriented planning using measurable outcomes; therefore, its findings should be interpreted as a synthesis of quantitative evidence rather than a comprehensive representation of all qualitative perspectives on prescribing readiness in the Middle East.

Information sources

Electronic database searches were conducted in PubMed and Web of Science. In addition, grey literature sources and selected regional journals were searched to enhance coverage. The final search across all sources was completed on 31 December 2025.

Search terms and search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was developed to identify studies addressing pharmacist independent prescribing or non-medical prescribing. The strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms, including Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), with free-text keywords representing two core concepts: pharmacists/pharmacy and independent or non-medical prescribing. This approach was designed to maximize sensitivity while ensuring relevance across databases. For PubMed, the search strategy incorporated combinations of MeSH terms and keywords such as (“Pharmacists”[MeSH] OR pharmacist* OR pharmacy) AND (“Independent Prescribing” OR “Non-Medical Prescribing”). For Web of Science, equivalent keyword combinations were applied to titles, abstracts, and keywords using appropriate Boolean operators and truncation to ensure comprehensive retrieval of relevant literature. Appendix 1 displays the various keywords used to search for relevant articles in this review.

Search limits were applied to include English-language publications, human studies, and articles published between 2015 and 2025. A ten-year search window was deliberately selected to capture contemporary and policy-relevant evidence reflecting rapid healthcare transformation in the Middle East. This period encompasses major regional health reforms, including initiatives such as Saudi Vision 2030, during which the expansion of pharmacists' clinical roles and discussions surrounding independent prescribing have increasingly gained attention.6, 7, 12

The search strategy and eligibility criteria were guided by a structured framework aligned with PICOS elements. Specifically, the Population included licensed pharmacists; the Phenomenon of interest was independent prescribing practice (IPP); Outcomes included awareness, readiness, attitudes, and perceived barriers and facilitators; and the study design was restricted to quantitative studies, primarily cross-sectional. A comparator was not applicable due to the descriptive and perception-based nature of the review.

Grey literature search

Grey literature was systematically searched using Google in accordance with methodological guidance by Godin et al.17 Searches were conducted in December 2025 using combinations of keywords including pharmacist, pharmacist prescribing, independent prescribing, non-medical prescribing, and Middle East. For each query, the first ten pages of search results were screened.

Grey literature sources targeted included websites of the ministries of health in Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and the United Arab Emirates; pharmacy regulatory bodies, such as the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties; professional organizations, such as national pharmacy associations; and national healthcare transformation initiatives, such as Saudi Vision 2030. (Appendix 2: Grey Literature Sources Consulted). Grey literature records were eligible for screening if they addressed pharmacist prescribing or IPP in Middle Eastern countries and provided empirical or policy-relevant information.

All grey literature records were logged (title, source, year, and URL) and screened using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria applied to database records. Grey literature was included to enhance search sensitivity and ensure comprehensive identification of potentially relevant studies during the screening process. However, in accordance with the predefined eligibility criteria, only peer-reviewed quantitative studies were eligible for inclusion in the final synthesis, and no grey literature records met these criteria.

Consequently, grey literature documents such as policy reports, guidelines, and opinion papers were excluded at the full-text stage. A total of 14 records were identified through non-database sources (websites = 8; citation searching = 6), as reflected in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Hand searching

In addition to electronic searches, manual hand searching was conducted for selected regional journals considered relevant to pharmacy practice and health systems research. These included the Saudi Pharmaceutical Journal, the Saudi Journal of Health Systems Research, and the Journal of Pharmaceutical Health Services Research. All issues published between 2015 and 2025 were screened by reviewing article titles and abstracts. Hand searching was conducted independently by two reviewers using a predefined screening checklist, with discrepancies resolved through discussion.

Study selection

All records identified through the database, grey literature, and hand searches were imported into a single screening file, and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers against the predefined eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then retrieved and independently assessed for eligibility. Disagreements at any stage were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented. The study selection process and reasons for exclusion are summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).14

Data extraction and synthesis

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized extraction form. Extracted data included author and year of publication, country, study design, study duration, sample size, participants' age, sample characteristics, and study setting. Extracted data were cross-checked for accuracy. Given the descriptive and perception-based nature of the included studies, a structured narrative synthesis, informed by methodological appraisal, was undertaken without meta-analysis, integrating study quality into the interpretation of findings. Quality appraisal

The methodological quality and potential sources of bias of included cross-sectional studies were assessed using the AXIS tool.18 Although AXIS is not a domain-based risk-of-bias tool, its items were mapped to key bias domains, including selection bias (sampling methods and representativeness), measurement bias (instrument validity and reliability), and non-response bias. This approach enabled a structured interpretation of potential sources of bias influencing the findings, consistent with recommendations for evidence synthesis of observational studies.18 Each AXIS item was rated as “Yes,” “No,” or “Partially”, and results were reported at the item level rather than as a composite numerical score, with the full AXIS appraisal presented in Supplementary Table S1. Quality appraisal was conducted independently by two reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion; where consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer adjudicated. The quality appraisal was used to inform the interpretation of findings rather than as a basis for study exclusion.

Methodological considerations

As this review synthesized evidence derived primarily from cross-sectional, self-reported studies, the findings reflect pharmacists' perceptions and self-assessed readiness rather than objective measures of prescribing competence, clinical effectiveness, patient safety, or health system impact. Accordingly, the results are intended to inform early policy discussion, framework development, and research prioritization rather than to support conclusions regarding real-world implementation or safety of IPP.

Results

Given the descriptive nature of the included studies, the results are presented as a structured synthesis of reported findings, focusing on pharmacists' awareness, readiness, and perceived barriers and facilitators related to independent prescribing practice (IPP) in Middle Eastern countries.

Search outcome

The literature search identified a total of 116 records, including 102 from electronic databases and 14 from other sources. After removal of 39 records prior to screening, 63 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 18 records were excluded. In parallel, additional records were identified through other sources and processed separately, as shown in the PRISMA flow diagram. A total of 55 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility (43 from databases and 12 from other sources). Following full-text review, 46 articles were excluded according to predefined criteria. Ultimately, nine studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final review. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).14

Methodological quality of included studies

All nine included studies met most of the criteria outlined in the AXIS quality assessment framework (Table S1 (Supplementary Material): Full AXIS quality appraisal of included studies). Overall, the studies demonstrated clear research objectives, appropriate study designs, ethical approval and consent procedures, and transparent reporting of data collection and analysis methods. Common methodological limitations included limited reporting on non-respondents and potential response bias associated with self-administered questionnaires. Despite these limitations, all studies provided relevant descriptive data suitable for inclusion in this review. Overall, the included studies were considered to have moderate methodological quality, with a generally low risk of bias in core domains such as study design, reporting, and outcome measurement, but with some concerns related to sampling methods, non-response bias, and reliance on self-reported data.

Characteristics of included studies

The key characteristics of the nine included studies are summarized in Table 1. Studies were published between 2015 and 2025 and were conducted in Saudi Arabia (n = 6), Jordan (n = 2), and the United Arab Emirates (UAE) (n = 1).5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13

Most Saudi-based studies were conducted nationwide or across multiple regions, whereas studies from Jordan and the UAE were conducted in Amman and Al-Ain, respectively.

All included studies employed descriptive cross-sectional questionnaire-based designs, with study durations ranging from 2 to 14 months. Sample sizes ranged from 137 to 521 participants (median = 205; IQR = 200–371), reflecting a clustering of sample sizes toward the lower range, totalling 2257 pharmacists. Participants' ages ranged from 20 to over 65, representing pharmacists at varying stages of professional experience. Study settings included community pharmacies, hospital pharmacies, and, in some studies, academic, regulatory, institutional, and industrial sectors, reflecting diverse practice environments across the region.

Awareness and understanding of independent prescribing practice

Reported awareness and conceptual understanding of IPP varied across countries and study periods (Table 2). Earlier studies from Saudi Arabia reported lower awareness levels,11, 12 while more recent studies demonstrated higher awareness and improved conceptual understanding of independent prescribing among pharmacists.5, 6 Studies conducted in Jordan reported moderate awareness, with some confusion about the distinction between independent and collaborative prescribing models.9, 13 In the UAE, pharmacists reported relatively high awareness and clearer conceptual understanding of IPP.10

Readiness to adopt independent prescribing practice

Across all included studies, pharmacists reported high readiness to adopt independent prescribing roles, contingent on the availability of appropriate legal frameworks, training, and institutional support (Table 2). High levels of readiness were reported among pharmacists practicing in community, hospital, and institutional settings across Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and the UAE.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 Although readiness was consistently reported, none of the included studies assessed objective measures of prescribing competence, clinical effectiveness, or patient outcomes.

Regulatory frameworks and policy context

Regulatory recognition of IPP varied across the reviewed settings. Studies from Saudi Arabia reported the absence of formal independent prescribing authority for pharmacists, although informal involvement in prescribing-related activities such as medication reconciliation and therapeutic interchange was described.11, 12 More recent Saudi studies documented an increase in policy discussions surrounding IPP within national healthcare reform initiatives.5, 6 In Jordan and the UAE, studies similarly reported the absence of explicit legislation authorizing IPP at the time of data collection.9, 10, 13

Perceived barriers to implementation of IPP

Across all studies, the most commonly reported barriers included insufficient diagnostic and clinical training, the absence of clear legal and regulatory frameworks, limited interprofessional collaboration, and restricted access to patient medical records (Table 2). Additional barriers reported in some Saudi studies included organizational hierarchies and limited decision-making authority within hospital settings.11, 12 These barriers were consistently identified across community and hospital pharmacy settings in all three countries.

Facilitators and enablers of IPP

Reported facilitators for IPP implementation included national healthcare reforms, professional recognition of expanded pharmacy roles, availability of targeted education and continuing professional development (CPD) programs, and institutional support (Table 2). Studies from Saudi Arabia highlighted the potential influence of healthcare transformation initiatives under Vision 2030.5, 6 Studies from Jordan and the UAE emphasized the importance of structured training pathways, interprofessional collaboration, and integration of prescribing competencies into pharmacy education.9, 10, 13

Summary of findings across countries

Overall, the reviewed studies consistently reported positive attitudes toward independent prescribing among pharmacists across Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and the UAE. While the level of awareness and policy development varied by country, high readiness and supportive professional attitudes were observed across all settings. However, implementation remained constrained by regulatory, educational, and structural barriers, as summarized in Table 2.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes quantitative evidence from Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and the United Arab Emirates regarding pharmacists' awareness, readiness, and perceived barriers and facilitators related to independent prescribing practice (IPP). Overall, the findings indicate a pharmacy workforce across the region that is professionally receptive, accompanied by persistent regulatory, educational, and interprofessional challenges. Notably, the evidence reflects perception-based readiness rather than demonstrated implementation outcomes, situating IPP in the Middle East at an early developmental stage comparable to the initial phases observed internationally.

Awareness and conceptual understanding of IPP

The findings indicate a temporal improvement in pharmacists' awareness and conceptual understanding of IPP in the Middle East. Earlier evidence from Saudi Arabia suggests that limited familiarity with independent prescribing concepts was likely influenced by the absence of formal independent prescribing authority at the time.11, 12 In contrast, more recent studies demonstrate a clearer understanding and differentiation between prescribing models, particularly in Saudi Arabia and the UAE.5, 6, 10 This progression likely reflects evolving healthcare priorities, national reform agendas, and increased exposure to international practice models. Similar trends have been observed in countries such as the United Kingdom and Canada, where awareness and acceptance of IPP expanded alongside policy development and workforce transformation initiatives.1, 2, 19

Readiness to adopt independent prescribing

The findings suggest a consistently high level of perceived readiness among pharmacists to adopt independent prescribing roles, dependent on the presence of appropriate regulatory frameworks, clinical training, and institutional support. This reflects a broader international pattern, in which early professional enthusiasm in countries such as the UK, Canada, and Australia preceded the establishment of formal credentialing, regulatory structures, and outcome-based evaluation.1, 2, 19 These experiences highlight that, while readiness is an important foundation, it must be supported by robust governance and training systems to ensure safe and effective implementation. Importantly, this readiness reflects perceived willingness and self-assessed preparedness rather than objectively measured prescribing competence and should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical capability or readiness for immediate implementation.

Barriers to implementation

Despite reported readiness, the findings highlight persistent barriers to IPP implementation in the Middle East, particularly the absence of clear legal and regulatory frameworks. Additional constraints relate to limitations in clinical training, access to patient information, and interprofessional collaboration.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 These challenges reflect structural and organizational factors, including professional hierarchies observed in some settings,11, 12 and are consistent with barriers reported during the early adoption of IPP in other regions.19, 20 Collectively, these findings underscore the need for coordinated regulatory, educational, and system-level reforms to support safe implementation.

Facilitators and enabling conditions

The reviewed studies identified several facilitators that may support the future integration of IPP in the region, including national healthcare reforms, professional recognition of expanded pharmacy roles, targeted education, and institutional support. In Saudi Arabia, healthcare transformation initiatives aligned with Vision 2030 have accelerated discussions around expanding pharmacists' clinical responsibilities.5, 6, 19 Similar facilitators have been central to the successful implementation of IPP models in the UK, Canada, and Australia, where regulatory endorsement, postgraduate training, and interprofessional collaboration contributed to professional confidence and public trust.4, 21, 22, 23 These international experiences offer valuable guidance for Middle Eastern countries seeking to develop context-specific IPP frameworks. However, these comparisons should be interpreted with caution, as healthcare systems in the UK, Canada, and Australia differ substantially from those in the Middle East in terms of regulatory frameworks, reimbursement models, and governance structures.

Methodological considerations and research gaps

The evidence synthesized in this review is derived exclusively from cross-sectional, self-reported studies, which limits the ability to assess prescribing competence, clinical effectiveness, patient safety, or health system impact. The reliance on convenience sampling in several studies further constrains generalizability. Additionally, the predominance of studies from Saudi Arabia (six out of nine included studies) may have influenced the overall findings, potentially overrepresenting the Saudi healthcare context, policy environment, and level of readiness, and limiting the extent to which the results reflect the diversity of healthcare systems and professional practices across the wider Middle East. Additionally, eligible studies were identified only from Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and the UAE, leaving substantial gaps across other Middle Eastern countries. Future research should prioritize longitudinal, interventional, and outcome-driven designs, as well as multi-stakeholder perspectives involving physicians, patients, and policymakers, to support evidence-based policy development and implementation planning.

Policy and practice implications

Given that the findings are based on perception-driven evidence rather than objective implementation outcomes, these implications should be interpreted as exploratory considerations rather than definitive policy recommendations. Although the findings reflect perception-based evidence rather than outcome-proven readiness, they highlight a strategic opportunity to integrate IPP within ongoing healthcare reforms in the Middle East. Potential priority areas may include establishing clear legal and regulatory frameworks, embedding prescribing competencies within undergraduate and postgraduate pharmacy education, expanding continuing professional development programs focused on diagnostic reasoning, and promoting collaborative models of practice. The development of national competency standards, credentialing pathways, and reimbursement mechanisms will be important to support safe and sustainable implementation. These implications are therefore intended to inform early framework development and regulatory planning rather than to advocate for immediate widespread implementation.

Limitations and suggestions for future research

Several limitations of this systematic review should be acknowledged. First, the available evidence is limited in both geographic coverage and representativeness, as eligible studies were identified only from Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and the United Arab Emirates. The absence of quantitative studies from other Middle Eastern countries restricts the generalizability of the findings and highlights substantial gaps in regional evidence on independent prescribing practice (IPP). A further limitation relates to the selection of databases. The search was limited to PubMed and Web of Science, which, although widely used and comprehensive for biomedical and pharmacy practice research, may not capture all relevant studies. Additional databases, such as Scopus and Embase, were not included and could have identified further eligible studies. Furthermore, although grey literature was searched, Google Scholar was not used as a primary database due to limitations in search reproducibility and filtering. Therefore, some relevant studies may not have been captured. Second, all included studies employed cross-sectional, questionnaire-based designs and relied on self-reported data. Consequently, the findings reflect pharmacists' perceptions, attitudes, and self-assessed readiness rather than objective measures of prescribing competence, clinical effectiveness, patient safety, or health system impact. The predominance of convenience sampling and limited reporting on non-respondents further increases the risk of selection and response bias. Third, heterogeneity across studies limited direct comparability of findings. Variability was observed across study settings, sample characteristics, outcome measures, and, particularly, in how independent prescribing was defined and operationalized across countries and studies. Differences in regulatory context and scope of practice may therefore have influenced reported levels of awareness, readiness, barriers, and facilitators. Fourth, although grey literature sources were systematically searched, only peer-reviewed quantitative studies met the predefined eligibility criteria and were included in the final synthesis. This restriction introduces the potential for publication bias, as relevant evidence from non–peer-reviewed sources, as well as unpublished studies or those reporting neutral or unfavorable findings, may not have been captured. However, a formal assessment of publication bias (e.g., funnel plot analysis) was not performed because the meta-analysis was not conducted due to the descriptive nature and heterogeneity of the included studies. In addition, the exclusion of qualitative studies may have limited the inclusion of nuanced contextual, professional, and system-level perspectives relevant to IPP development in the region. This may have led to underrepresentation of complex implementation challenges, including organizational culture, interprofessional dynamics, and potential resistance from other healthcare providers, particularly physicians. Additionally, the review protocol was not prospectively registered (e.g., in PROSPERO), which may limit methodological transparency and reproducibility. Future research should expand geographic coverage across Middle Eastern countries; adopt longitudinal, interventional, and outcome-focused study designs; apply standardized definitions of independent prescribing; and integrate mixed-methods approaches incorporating perspectives from pharmacists, physicians, patients, and policymakers. Such efforts are essential to support robust, evidence-informed policy development and safe implementation of independent IPP.

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates that pharmacists in Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and the United Arab Emirates generally report high levels of awareness and readiness to assume independent prescribing roles. However, the available evidence is largely perception-based and derived from cross-sectional surveys, with no implementation trials and no direct evidence on prescribing competence, patient safety, or clinical outcomes. Despite this professional willingness, the integration of independent prescribing practice (IPP) in the Middle East remains limited by the absence of clear legal and regulatory frameworks, insufficient diagnostic and clinical training, and variable interprofessional collaboration. These constraints indicate that independent prescribing in the region is still in its early stages of development. To support safe and sustainable implementation, Middle Eastern healthcare systems should prioritize the development of clear legislation, competency-based education and training pathways, formal credentialing processes, and collaborative models of practice. Future research should move beyond descriptive surveys to evaluate implementation feasibility, patient safety, and health system impact, thereby supporting evidence-informed policy decisions.

She holds a PhD, MSc, and BSc in Pharmacy and has extensive academic and research experience in pharmacy practice, health policy, medication safety, and professional role development. Her research interests include pharmacist prescribing, healthcare workforce development, patient safety, and health system reforms in the Middle East. She has published widely in peer-reviewed international journals and is actively involved in teaching, curriculum development, and policy-relevant research.
