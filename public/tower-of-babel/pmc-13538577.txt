Mineralogical and geochemical provenance reference for turquoise from the Teghout Cu–Mo deposit in Armenia
Authors: Bahareh Shirdam, Mingxing Yang, Khachatur Meliksetian, Edmond Grigoryan, Yue Yang, Yuwei Zheng, Xin Wan, Yan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Turquoise from the Teghout Cu–Mo porphyry deposit (Lori Province, northern Armenia) forms veinlets and nodules in the supergene zone with quartz, Fe–Mn oxides, jarosite, barite and clay minerals, locally chalcanthite and gypsum. We provide the first integrated reference dataset for a substantial Caucasus turquoise source, providing the mineralogical and geochemical reference data needed to support future archaeological provenance research, collection authentication and heritage-science studies of exchange in the region. We emphasise that the Teghout occurrence was only discovered in 1974 during modern exploration and that no archaeological evidence currently links it to ancient turquoise supply; the reference framework presented here is intended to enable, not to substitute for, such future archaeological investigation. Gemmological testing, SEM imaging, EPMA, FTIR, Raman and LA-ICP-MS define a Cu-Al dominant turquoise near the ideal end-member. Raman spectra include variants with sulphate bands near 983 and 1007 cm−1. Trace elements show MREE-peaked, chondrite-normalised REE patterns with a broad Sm–Gd maximum, weak Eu anomalies, enrichment in Sr-Mo-Zn-Sc and very low V-Cr. Comparison with datasets from Meiduk (Iran) and Tongling (China), two porphyry-hosted turquoise localities, tests discrimination within a shared genetic framework; log-transformed Sr/V–Pb/Ba and Sr/U–Pb/U ratios distinguish the deposits statistically, although the Sr/U–Pb/U field is treated as supportive because Teghout and Meiduk show limited envelope overlap.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59932-7.

Introduction

Turquoise, CuAl6(PO4)4(OH)8·4H2O, is a hydrated copper–aluminium phosphate that typically forms in near-surface supergene environments above oxidized copper deposits and Cu-rich lithologies. Its saturated blue to green colors and opaque, waxy to vitreous appearance have made it a culturally important gem across Eurasia and the Americas for several millennia, widely used in beads, inlays and ceremonial objects1–3. Beyond its aesthetic role, turquoise is a sensitive recorder of its formation environment: variations in major and minor cations, trace-element patterns and mineral inclusions reflect the chemistry of percolating fluids, redox conditions and host-rock composition in the supergene zone, as emphasized in recent mineralogical and geochemical case studies4,5.

Establishing reliable criteria for geographic origin remains challenging. Many deposits display wide internal compositional ranges, and several major sources share broadly similar color and macro-textures. Recent work combining mineralogy, vibrational spectroscopy and laser-ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS) trace-element data, evaluated with multivariate statistics, has demonstrated that suites of trace and rare-earth elements can characterise specific deposits. Examples include the multi-deposit Chinese study of Xian et al.6, which uses “sedimentary-environment sensitive elements” to separate turquoise fields, the machine-learning based origin study of Tong et al.7, and the detailed mineralogy and trace-element systematics of Tianhu East turquoise reported by Liu et al.5 Parallel approaches using Sr–Pb isotopes and chemometrics have been applied to artefacts and deposits in China and the American Southwest2,8.

Despite these advances, current reference datasets are strongly biased toward well-known deposits in Iran, China, the Sinai and the American Southwest, whereas turquoise occurrences in the Caucasus and adjacent regions are only briefly mentioned in gemmological and geological reports2,9. This imbalance limits our ability to interpret turquoise artefacts from Armenia and neighbouring areas, which lay astride long-distance exchange networks linking the Near East, the Iranian Plateau and the Eurasian steppe10.

Beyond its gemmological interest, turquoise is a strategically useful tracer material in archaeometry because it is highly visible in the material record, widely exchanged, and frequently encountered in museum collections and the antiquities market. Because the South Caucasus and the Iranian Plateau lie along major trans-Eurasian exchange corridors that have channelled the movement of gem materials over millennia, robust provenance tools for turquoise are directly relevant to heritage-science questions of object biography, authenticity assessment, and responsible sourcing. Strengthening geochemical reference frameworks for underrepresented regions therefore has value that extends well beyond local mineralogical description.

A critical methodological point is that provenance frameworks are most defensible when constructed within coherent genetic families. Turquoise forming as a secondary product above porphyry Cu–Mo systems in magmatic arcs can inherit trace-element characteristics reflecting the ore-forming environment (for example, relative enrichments in Sr–Mo–Zn and comparatively low V–Cr), whereas turquoise hosted by sedimentary–metamorphic terranes may incorporate different detrital and metamorphic inputs that shift transition-metal and trace-element systematics. Accordingly, to maximize discriminatory power while minimizing genetic bias, the provenance discussion in this paper emphasizes porphyry-related turquoise comparisons between Teghout (Armenia) and compositionally analogous occurrences from the Tongling deposit (Anhui, China) and Meiduk porphyry deposit (Kerman, Iran). This focus is particularly appropriate given that Teghout belongs to the Alpine-Himalayan (Tethyan) metallogenic domain outlined below.

The Alpine-Himalayan (Tethyan) orogenic belt was shaped as a result of convergence of the African–Arabian and Eurasian plates, during Mesozoic and Cenozoic times and consist of diverse terranes and microplates of various origin11–13. North-Eastern part of Armenian Highlands and Lesser Caucasus mountains as part of Tethyan belt formed by amalgamation of different terranes such as South Armenian Block (SAB) of Gondwanaland origin13 the Somkheto-Karabakh Mesozoic Island arc system and Lesser Caucasus ophiolites14,15. Metallogeny of Somkheto-Karabakh Mesozoic island arc system is linked to Early Jurassic- Cretaceous subduction-related magmatism hosting several major copper and polymetallic, volcanogenic massive sulphide deposits such as Alaverdi, Shamlugh, Akhtala and also a few Cu-Mo porphyry Late Jurassic Teghout deposit dated to 145.85 ± 0.59 Ma, by Re-Os dating of molybdenite15, and Kashen Cu porphyry deposit. The Teghout Cu–Mo deposit in Lori Province, northern Armenia, is a large porphyry-style copper–molybdenum deposit within the Lesser Caucasus segment of the Tethyan metallogenic belt.

Further south-south-west, within SAB most of deposits are linked to Eocene to Neogene Island arc and collision related magmas and are of Cu-Mo porphyry and epithermal type. Large Cenosoic Cu-Mo porphyry deposit belt is represented by large Kadjaran, Agarak, Dastakert deposits and extend further to NW Iran (Sungun). Open-pit operations at Teghout and the associated ore reserves (quantified in the Geological setting below) are documented in company and geological reports16. Supergene alteration of the orebody and its wall rocks have produced turquoise as a secondary copper phosphate, and material marketed as “Armenian turquoise” from Teghout has recently entered the gem trade (Fig. 1). This study presents the first integrated gemmological, mineralogical and geochemical characterisation of turquoise from the Teghout Cu–Mo deposit. We combine field observations with spectroscopy, microanalysis and imaging in order to (1) describe in detail the color range, internal textures, microstructures and mineral associations of Teghout turquoise, including its major-element chemistry and crystal-chemical formula; (2) clarify its formation within the supergene carapace of a porphyry Cu–Mo system by linking spectroscopic features, sulfate-bearing assemblages and trace-element–rare-earth-element (REE) signatures to specific alteration processes; and (3) establish a deposit-scale chemical and spectroscopic reference framework that can distinguish Teghout turquoise from major deposits in Iran, China and elsewhere, and that can be applied in future studies of source attribution for archaeological and gem materials from the South Caucasus and surrounding regions. To strengthen the provenance relevance, we explicitly evaluate which REE–trace-element parameters and element-ratio fields most effectively separate Teghout from other porphyry-related turquoise sources along this trans-Eurasian corridor, with particular emphasis on the Tongling (Anhui, China) and Meiduk (Kerman, Iran). This targeted within-type comparison is intended to provide a practical reference for archaeometric provenance studies and for geochemical screening of gem materials in modern circulation.

Geological setting

The Teghout Cu porphyry deposit lies in the Alaverdi mining district of Lori Province, within the Lesser Caucasus segment of the Jurassic–Early Cretaceous Somkheto–Karabakh island-arc system. Regional studies describe this belt as a long-lived subduction-related arc that hosts numerous VMS and polymetallic deposits, including Shamlugh, Alaverdi, Akhtala, Kashen and Mehmana formed during Mesozoic convergence along the southern margin of Eurasia15,17,18. In the Alaverdi district, Cu–Au mineralisation is hosted by Middle Jurassic volcanic and volcaniclastic rocks and by Teghout and Cu–Mo porphyry deposits is linked Late Jurassic calc-alkaline Koghb-Shnokh intrusions, as documented for nearby Shamugh deposit such as15,19,20.

Teghout represents the youngest known Cu porphyry deposit of the Alaverdi district and is spatially associated with the polyphase, calc-alkaline Koghb–Shnokh intrusive complex. The intrusion ranges from quartz diorite to granodiorite porphyry and cuts Jurassic volcanic and sedimentary sequences15,17. Porphyry-style stockwork and disseminated sulfide mineralisation—dominated by chalcopyrite, bornite, chalcocite, covellite, pyrite and minor molybdenite—is hosted in potassic and phyllic alteration zones and locally overprinted by late argillic assemblages of quartz, sericite and clay minerals16. Ore-reserve estimates on the order of 460 Mt at 0.34 wt % Cu and 0.01 wt % Mo classify Teghout as a medium- to large-scale porphyry Cu–Mo system within the Lesser Caucasus. Turquoise at Teghout was first discovered by geologist V. Seyranyan in 1974 and occurs within the supergene alteration zone developed at the top of, and immediately above, the porphyry orebody. Field observations and thin-section studies from this work indicate that turquoise forms veinlets, fracture fillings and irregular nodules within strongly weathered quartz-diorite porphyry and brecciated wall rock, commonly along highly fractured bench faces exposed by mining. It is closely associated with goethite–hematite assemblages, jarosite, gypsum, manganese oxides, quartz, barite and clay minerals, reflecting oxidation of the primary sulfide assemblage. During a dedicated field visit in August 2025, we visited the Teghout mine, logged accessible bench exposures and collected 35 representative turquoise-bearing samples from active and reclaimed workings for the mineralogical and geochemical analyses presented in this study. Field access and collection of turquoise-bearing specimens at the Teghout Cu–Mo deposit were conducted with permission from Teghout CJSC. The authors confirm that all necessary permissions for site access, specimen collection, and research use of the collected materials were obtained prior to fieldwork. It should be emphasised that the Teghout turquoise occurrence is presently known only from modern exploration and exploitation: no Bronze Age, Iron Age, Classical or Medieval workings have been reported within the deposit, and no securely provenanced ancient turquoise artefact has yet been compositionally tied to this source. The dataset described below is therefore a reference characterisation of a modern occurrence; any application to archaeological provenance is conditional on future targeted field survey and on independent analyses of dated artefacts2.

Archival resource calculations for the turquoise occurrence at Teghout, compiled in the Soviet State Cadastre of Non-metallic Mineral Deposits (“passport” of the Teghout occurrence), report 79,947 kg of balance reserves of turquoise and a further 20,300 kg classified as off-balance material within the oxidised zone (Teghout turquoise passport, 1991). These estimates refer explicitly to shallow supergene bodies exposed at that time and note that they would largely be recovered during development of the oxidation zone. Subsequent open-pit operations by have focused on Cu–Mo ore, and recent technical summaries do not document additional turquoise-bearing veins at depth; our field observations suggest that turquoise is now restricted to small, discontinuous pockets along upper bench faces, although minor undiscovered bodies may persist in unmined portions of the supergene profile.

Materials and methods

To investigate the microstructural and processing features of the samples, a Leica M205A high-resolution automated microscopic imaging system was employed, which allowed detailed observations and documentation of surface. All 31 specimens were photomicrographed and specimens were screened by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy to confirm turquoise and separate any non-turquoise material. Raman spectroscopy was likewise applied to all 31 to identify turquoise and detect associated/secondary phases. Higher-resolution work using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), electron-probe microanalysis (EPMA), and LA-ICP-MS focused on a representative subset: ARM-001-ARM-025. EPMA was performed on this same set; a total of 35 quantitative wavelength-dispersive spectrometry (WDS) spots were acquired across these specimens, with 1–3 spots per specimen targeted to distinct domains to capture intra-specimen variability. LA-ICP-MS was conducted on the same group of specimens.

Standard gemological testing

Specific gravity (SG) was determined hydrostatically for 31 representative specimens using an electronic balance under ambient laboratory conditions. Long-wave (365 nm) and short-wave (254 nm) fluorescence responses were recorded with a mercury-vapor ultraviolet (UV) source. Microscopic features were examined and photographed using a Leica M205A stereomicroscope. All measurements were conducted at the Gemmological Institute, China University of Geosciences (Wuhan).

Micro-Raman analysis

The material composition of the samples was determined using multiple spectroscopic techniques. Infrared spectroscopy was performed with a Bruker Optics Hyperion 3000 instrument in reflection mode, with a resolution of 4 cm⁻1 over a measurement range of 400–4000 cm⁻1. Raman spectroscopy was conducted using a HORIBA LabRAM HR Evolution spectrometer with a 532 nm laser, ND filter set to 2.5–100% attenuation and a 600 nm (500nm) grating, covering a range of 100–4000 cm⁻1. The acquisition time varied from 5 to 15 s with an accumulation time of 3–5 s.

SEM imaging and EDS screening

Samples were carbon-coated to enhance conductivity. Surface morphology and microtextures were examined on a Thermo Fisher Helios G4 SEM using 20 kV and 5 kV accelerating voltages, 1.4 nA and 0.34 nA beam currents, and variable magnification to optimize contrast for features of interest. Elemental analyses were acquired with an Oxford AZtec Ultim Max 100 SDD EDS operated in AZtec 5.0.

EPMA

Quantitative major-oxide compositions were obtained on a JEOL JXA-8230 electron microprobe equipped with five WDS crystals. Operating conditions were 15 kV accelerating voltage, 5 nA probe current, and a 10 µm spot size. Peak/background counting times were 10 s / 5 s. Raw intensities were corrected using JEOL’s ZAF procedure. WDS configurations and calibration standards were: pyrope garnet (Fe, Al), apatite (Ca, P), sphalerite (Zn), olivine (Si), barite (S), copper (Cu), sanidine (K), chromium (Cr), vanadium (V), rutile (Ti), barium fluoride (Ba), uranium (U). The calculation of their crystal-chemical formula was performed on the basis of six octahedrally coordinated (B-site) cations, Al + Fe3+ = 6, with all iron treated as Fe3+, following the general turquoise formula AB6(PO4)4(OH)8·4H2O of Foord and Taggart21. EPMA comprised 35 spots distributed across representative slabs spanning the principal textures and hues; spots per specimen were allocated to distinct domains (turquoise matrix, Fe-rich disks, veinlets) to capture intra-specimen variability. To minimise contributions from microscale chalcanthite or gypsum intergrowths, all WDS spots were placed on visually homogeneous turquoise domains under reflected-light and back-scattered electron (BSE) imaging at high magnification, following standard quality-control practice for chemically heterogeneous, low-totals phosphate minerals3,21. Analyses showing SO3 > 1 wt.% or anomalous CaO/CuO ratios suggestive of sulfate-mineral contamination were flagged and excluded from formula recalculation. The mean SO3 content of the retained spots (0.40 wt.%) is consistent with low-level structurally accommodated sulfate, as previously documented for porphyry-derived turquoise3,5, rather than mixed-phase contamination.

LA-ICPMS

REE behaviour and trace-element systematics were further constrained by LA-ICP-MS using an Agilent 7700e quadrupole ICP-MS coupled to a GeoLasPro 193 nm ArF excimer laser. Ablation was carried out at 80 mJ laser energy, a fluence of 5.5 J cm⁻2, a 44 μm spot, and 250 pulses at 5 Hz, with each run comprising 30 s background, 50 s ablation, and 30 s washout. Helium (350 mL min⁻1) carried the aerosol and was blended with argon (900 mL min⁻1) before reaching the plasma. Quantification relied on the external glasses NIST SRM 610, BCR-2G, BHVO-2G and BIR-1G (USGS), with 27Al as internal standard fixed to a stoichiometric Al2O3 value of 37.6 wt%7. Concentrations are given in ppm, with element-specific detection limits (about 0.005 ppm for REE to 50 ppm for matrix elements) reported in Table 2. Precision was within 5% RSD on reference materials, and NIST SRM 612 analysed every tenth spot tracked reproducibility. Data were reduced offline in ICPMSDataCal v.10.923.

Every ablation signal was screened in time-resolved mode22,23, and integration intervals were set to skip surface contamination at pit onset and downhole breakthrough at pit termination; spots showing Ca–S or Cu–S spikes indicative of micro-inclusions of gypsum or chalcanthite were discarded. As supergene turquoise forms at low temperature, melt inclusions are not anticipated, in line with their general absence in supergene phosphates21. BSE imaging and high-magnification Raman screening detected no fluid inclusions in turquoise, gypsum, chalcanthite or Fe-oxides, so possibility of aqueous-inclusion contributions to the 44 μm signal can be ruled out. The reported major-, trace- and REE data thus represents the turquoise matrix rather than admixed sulfate or aqueous phases. Chondrite-normalised REE patterns were then derived from these results.

Results

Gemmological characteristics

Thirty-one turquoise specimens from the Teghout Cu–Mo deposit were examined, of which a subset is illustrated in Fig. 3. The material occurs mainly as vein and cavity fillings cutting strongly altered, Fe-oxide–rich host rocks, with subordinate nodular to irregular pocket fillings. Vein-type turquoise (e.g., ARM-001) forms centimetre-scale, relatively planar bands with sharp contacts against the brown limonitic matrix, whereas nodular/pocket material (e.g., ARM-021) appears as more equant to lobate masses that partly envelop or replace the host rock. Colors range from saturated sky blue to slightly greenish blue, generally with good overall saturation and moderate to high uniformity, locally interrupted by narrow selvages of host rock or paler marginal zones. Microphotographs of polished surfaces (Fig. 4a–d) document the intra-sample color and texture variations: (a) an almost flawless, medium-blue turquoise with high saturation and very uniform appearance; (b) an uneven pattern with scattered tiny whitish patches, marking small domains of locally bleached or altered material; (c) distinct color zoning expressed as fine growth layers, reflecting successive stages of turquoise deposition; and (d) a more greenish turquoise with a softer visual texture and slightly higher apparent porosity than in (a). Hydrostatic weighing of 31 representative pieces yielded specific-gravity values between 2.32 and 2.74 g/cm⁻3, with most samples clustering about 2.57 g/cm⁻3. The highest SG values are associated with compact, homogeneous vein material, whereas lower values correspond to more porous, greenish or brecciated aggregates comparable to the texture seen in Fig. 4d. Under long-wave (365 nm) UV radiation, Teghout turquoise shows none to faint chalky bluish-white fluorescence; short-wave (254 nm) excitation produces none to very weak luminescence, and no phosphorescence was observed.

Chemical composition

Electron-probe microanalyses of the 35 WDS spots reported in the Materials and Methods (of which 16 were sited on visually homogeneous turquoise matrix and retained after screening; see Methods) on turquoise from the Teghout Cu–Mo deposit show a chemistry dominated by the turquoise end-member oxides CuO, Al2O3, and P2O5 (Table 1). All oxide concentrations are reported in weight percent (wt.%). The three principal components display only limited dispersion, with CuO contents clustered around 7–8 wt.%, Al2O3 near 35 wt.%, and P2O5 close to 34 wt.%, indicating a Cu-dominant member of the turquoise group and underscoring the overall compositional homogeneity of the Teghout suite. Iron, reported as total FeO (the oxide listed in Table 1) and recalculated as Fe3+ for the structural formula, is the most variable major constituent, averaging about 3 wt.%, whereas CaO is present at only a few tenths of a percent. Other cations occur in trace to minor amounts: K2O, Na2O, MgO, and ZnO are each well below 1 wt.% and together account for only a small fraction of the bulk chemistry; TiO2 is similarly low. Halogens are scarce, with F commonly at or below detection limits and Cl restricted to trace levels, whereas sulfate (as SO3) is present only in minor amounts. Spectroscopic behavior

FTIR spectra obtained from representative Teghout specimens (e.g., ARM-002, ARM-006, ARM-018, ARM-021) are dominated by the bands expected for a well-ordered hydrated Cu–Al phosphate and closely match the RRUFF turquoise reference spectrum R050225 (Fig. 5a)24. All samples display a broad OH–H2O stretching envelope between ~ 3600 and 3000 cm⁻1 with a pronounced maximum at 3466 cm⁻1, accompanied by an H2O bending band near 1630 cm⁻1. In the mid-IR region, a strong absorption at 1113 cm⁻1, together with subordinate bands in the 1050–950 cm⁻1 interval, corresponds to PO43⁻ stretching modes, whereas the intense band at 532 cm⁻1 and additional features between ~ 600 and 450 cm⁻1 are assigned to PO4 bending and lattice vibrations, in agreement with published spectra of turquoise-group minerals25–27. Within analytical uncertainty, band positions and relative intensities are uniform among the Teghout samples and coincide with those reported for natural turquoise from other deposits worldwide28,29. Direct comparison of the measured band positions with published transmission and ATR turquoise spectra agrees within ± 2–3 cm⁻1, with no peak-shape inversion or systematic shift indicative of Christiansen-effect distortion; reflection mode was selected as a non-destructive approach suitable for the gem-quality slabs analysed here. Raman spectroscopy reveals systematic but subtle variations superimposed on this otherwise typical turquoise pattern (Fig. 5b). All spectra show the diagnostic ν3(PO4) band of turquoise near 1039 cm⁻1, a prominent band at ~ 813 cm⁻1, and lower-wavenumber features at ~ 417 and 232 cm⁻1 assigned to PO4 bending and lattice modes, in close agreement with the R050225 reference and with previous studies of turquoise25–27. On this basis, three empirical Raman types can be distinguished in the Teghout material. Type I spectra are essentially indistinguishable from the reference turquoise spectrum. Type II spectra preserve the same turquoise fingerprint but display an additional band near 983 cm⁻1, while Type III spectra likewise match the turquoise pattern but show an extra band near 1007 cm⁻1. These 983 and 1007 cm⁻1 features vary in intensity between spots and between grains, are absent in the reference spectrum, and lie within the region where internal stretching modes of tetrahedral oxyanions in associated accessory phases commonly occur26,27. Their detailed assignment and paragenetic significance are therefore considered in the Discussion. Overall, the combined FTIR–Raman dataset shows that the Teghout specimens are structurally and vibrationally consistent with turquoise sensu Foord and Taggart21, while the occurrence of the three Raman behaviours documents local-scale spectral heterogeneity that is characteristic of this Armenian occurrence. A more detailed inspection of peak parameters confirms that the framework Raman signature of the Teghout material is highly reproducible. The diagnostic ν3(PO4) band of turquoise occurs between 1037 and 1041 cm⁻1 (mean 1039.2 ± 0.8 cm⁻1) with full-widths at half maximum (FWHM) of 8–11 cm⁻1, indistinguishable within analytical uncertainty from the RRUFF reference R05022524 and from values published for end-member Cu–Al turquoise25,26,28. The subordinate bands at 813, 417 and 232 cm⁻1 are likewise stable in position (± 2 cm⁻1) and show no systematic broadening between Type I, II and III spectra. The structural framework of Teghout turquoise is therefore uniform across the deposit, and the inter-type variability is restricted to the additional sulfate-related bands near 983 cm⁻1 (chalcanthite, ν1(SO4), FWHM ~ 6–9 cm⁻1) and 1007 cm⁻1 (gypsum, ν1(SO4), FWHM 5–8 cm⁻1), in agreement with reference Raman spectra reported for these sulfate phases25,27.

Trace-element chemistry

Laser ablation ICP–MS analyses of Teghout turquoise (Table 2) reveal a coherent trace–element pattern in which alkaline-earth and trivalent cations dominate over transition metals. Lithium is generally low, with most analyses in the tens of ppm and only a few spots exceeding 100 ppm. Scandium is systematically enriched (21.47–154.19 ppm; mean 58.98 ppm), consistent with partial substitution for Al in the octahedral site, as documented for turquoise-group minerals elsewhere3. Vanadium and chromium are conspicuously scarce (V 10.89–20.80 ppm; Cr below detection to 46.09 ppm), and gallium occurs at modest levels (1.63–33.76 ppm; mean 13.22 ppm). In contrast, strontium and barium define the most prominent positive anomalies in spider diagrams: Sr spans 44.18–814.61 ppm (mean 223.62 ppm) and Ba ranges from several tens to more than one thousand ppm (mean 482.37 ppm). Lead (5.28–146.78 ppm; mean 50.87 ppm) and uranium (0.39–7.11 ppm; mean 2.40 ppm) are present at low to moderate concentrations, but are consistently detectable. When compared with recently published datasets for major turquoise deposits, the Teghout suite occupies a distinctive position. Sediment-hosted Chinese turquoise from Hami, Qinghai and related localities commonly exhibits V and Cr concentrations in the hundreds to thousands of ppm and substantially higher Ga, together with variable Li and Sr–Ba, reflecting strong interaction with Fe-rich sedimentary protoliths and basin fluids5,6,31. Relative to these deposits, Teghout turquoise is strongly depleted—by roughly one to two orders of magnitude—in V and Cr and shows clearly lower Ga, while retaining intermediate Li and Sc and moderately elevated Sr–Ba. Iranian turquoise from Neyshabur and Kuh-Zar tends to combine high Li and Sr with comparatively lower Ba and very low V–Cr32,33 (the Neyshabur and Kuh-Zar (Baghu) occurrences are hosted in altered volcanic rocks rather than in porphyry copper systems, and are therefore treated here as out-of-type compositional contrasts and excluded from the porphyry-hosted within-type comparison developed in the Discussion), whereas Sinai turquoise is characterized by extreme Sr–Ba and U enrichments that far exceed the Teghout values. In the broader context of recent provenance studies that apply multivariate treatment of LA-ICP-MS data7, the Teghout population therefore defines a trace-element field marked by moderate Li and Sc, very subdued V–Cr, and intermediate Sr–Ba, distinct from the principal clusters defined so far for Chinese, Iranian and Sinai deposits. This numerically constrained signature provides a solid geochemical basis for distinguishing Teghout turquoise in future comparative and provenance work.

Microstructure and morphology

SEM imaging shows that Teghout turquoise is built from closely packed aggregates of euhedral to subhedral microcrystals rather than an amorphous mass (Fig. 6a, b). In the coarser domains (Fig. 6a), turquoise forms dense mosaics of lath- to blade-shaped crystals that commonly reach 5–8 µm in length and a few hundred nanometres in thickness, arranged in broadly parallel trains with local imbrication and step-like terminations. The intercrystalline spaces are narrow and elongate, giving these areas a compact, low-porosity fabric. In contrast, finer-grained zones (Fig. 6b) consist of shorter prisms and plates, mostly 2–3 µm long, oriented in a more radial and irregular fashion. Here the packing is looser, and the pores between crystals are more equant and sub-micrometre to micrometre-scale in projection, indicating a higher micro-porosity than in the coarser lath aggregates34.Fig. 6Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of the microcrystalline habit of Teghout turquoise: (a) a coarser domain of densely packed, lath- to blade-shaped crystals 5–8 µm long arranged in broadly parallel trains, giving a compact, low-porosity fabric (scale bar 8 µm); (b) a finer-grained domain of shorter, 2–3 µm prisms and plates in a more radial arrangement with higher inter-crystalline micro-porosity (scale bar 2 µm).

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of the microcrystalline habit of Teghout turquoise: (a) a coarser domain of densely packed, lath- to blade-shaped crystals 5–8 µm long arranged in broadly parallel trains, giving a compact, low-porosity fabric (scale bar 8 µm); (b) a finer-grained domain of shorter, 2–3 µm prisms and plates in a more radial arrangement with higher inter-crystalline micro-porosity (scale bar 2 µm).

This combination of columnar, platy and lath-like turquoise crystals with variable packing density is consistent with SEM observations of natural, untreated turquoise from other deposits, where triclinic turquoise typically develops as cryptocrystalline aggregates of thin columns, plates and scales with porosity controlled by the spacing of microcrystals5,34. The Teghout material therefore conforms to the characteristic low-temperature growth habit of supergene turquoise described in recent morphological and paragenetic studies, while the contrast between tightly intergrown 5–8 µm laths and more open clusters of 2–3 µm prisms mirrors the density–porosity spectrum documented for turquoise in both gemmological and mineralogical investigations.

Mineral associations

Teghout turquoise is hosted in a supergene assemblage characteristic of the oxidized carapace of porphyry Cu–Mo systems. Turquoise is in sharp to gradational contact with milky to translucent quartz forming veinlets, angular grains and drusy overgrowths that project into the turquoise masses (Fig. 7a). Brown to reddish-brown Fe oxyhydroxides (goethite–hematite–limonite) occur as porous rims, fracture fillings and replacement fronts around turquoise and relict sulfides (Fig. 7b, e), whereas dark Mn oxides form irregular patches at the turquoise–host interface (Fig. 7c). Relict pyrite appears as disseminated euhedral to subhedral grains enclosed by turquoise or embedded in a clay-rich matrix (Fig. 7d). Yellow–orange jarosite occurs as earthy to microcrystalline coatings and veinlets closely intergrown with Fe oxides (Fig. 7e). Additional accessory phases identified by SEM–EDS and Raman spectroscopy include barite, gypsum, anatase and fine-grained kaolinite–illite–group clays, which form pale selvages and matrix between turquoise domains (Fig. 7f). A distinctive feature of the Teghout assemblage is the systematic occurrence of chalcanthite in direct association with turquoise (Fig. 8). Macroscopically, chalcanthite forms pale to medium-blue, vitreous patches and crusts along turquoise margins and on fracture surfaces (Fig. 8a). Back-scattered electron images show that these zones consist of loosely aggregated, elongate to fibrous crystals that cross-cut or overgrow the underlying turquoise (Fig. 8b, c), and microanalytical data confirm a CuSO4·5H2O composition. Chalcanthite is a highly soluble secondary copper sulfate typically reported from oxidized copper deposits and mine workings in arid to semi-arid environments, commonly in assemblages dominated by other Cu sulfates and Fe oxyhydroxides rather than by Cu–Al phosphates4,5. By contrast, recent detailed mineralogical studies of turquoise from Tianhu East5, from historical museum collections spanning Sinai, Santa Fe and Neyshabur3, and from Iranian deposits such as Kuh-Zar (Baghu)32 describe quartz, Fe oxyhydroxides, jarosite–alunite, gypsum, barite, apatite and atacamite as common associates but do not list chalcanthite in direct contact with turquoise. Rare-earth element geochemistry and distribution

Laser ablation ICP–MS analyses show that Teghout turquoise has low to moderate total REE contents and very regular chondrite-normalized patterns (Fig. 9a). Normalization to CI chondrite values of Sun and McDonough35 gives smooth, subparallel curves for all spots, with gentle enrichment of light over heavy REE and limited inter-sample dispersion. The patterns rise from La to a broad maximum in the middle REE (Sm–Gd) and then decline slightly toward Lu, indicating modest light-REE–heavy-REE (LREE–HREE) fractionation rather than strongly fractionated signatures. Most analyses display only a weak Eu anomaly, with Eu lying close to the interpolation between neighboring Sm and Gd, and Ce/Ce* values near unity, pointing to limited Ce redox partitioning during turquoise formation. In terms of overall shape and degree of fractionation, the Teghout REE patterns fall within the range reported for other supergene turquoise occurrences but are less strongly fractionated than the steep LREE-enriched, Eu-depleted patterns documented for several Chinese sediment-hosted deposits and newly characterized vein-type occurrences in northern China.Fig. 9LA-ICP-MS geochemistry of Teghout turquoise: (a) chondrite-normalised REE patterns (normalisation values of Sun and McDonough), showing smooth, sub-parallel, MREE-peaked profiles with weak Eu anomalies; (b) upper-continental-crust-normalised multi-element (spider) diagram (normalisation values of Rudnick and Gao), showing pronounced positive Sr and Mo anomalies and moderate Ba–Zn–Sc enrichment against strongly suppressed Rb, K, Na and transition metals (Co, Cr, Ni).

Upper-continental-crust–normalized multi-element plots constructed using the composition of Rudnick and Gao36 highlight the behavior of REE-bearing and redox-sensitive trace elements in Teghout turquoise (Fig. 9b). The patterns show pronounced positive anomalies in Sr and Mo and moderate enrichments in Ba, Zn and Sc relative to upper crust, whereas large-ion lithophile elements such as Rb, K and Na, together with transition metals Co, Cr and Ni, are strongly suppressed. This combination of MREE-peaked chondrite-normalized profiles and an upper-crust–normalized spider diagram dominated by Sr–Ba–Mo–Zn–Sc peaks are broadly consistent with REE–trace-element “fingerprints” proposed as provenance indicators for turquoise in recent LA-ICP-MS studies from China and elsewhere. Xian et al.6 and Tong et al.7 showed that variations in LREE/HREE ratios, Eu anomalies and Sr–Ba–Mo–Zn–Sc systematics can discriminate between major turquoise districts; within this framework, the Teghout data define a coherent REE–trace-element field characterized by MREE-peaked profiles, weak Eu anomalies and strong Sr–Mo–Sc–Zn.

Discussion

REE and trace-element constraints on source

Interpretation of turquoise as a provenance indicator must explicitly separate “deposit-type” effects from “locality” effects, because trace-element distributions are controlled by both the chemistry of the leached source rocks and the supergene transport–precipitation pathway. Consequently, the discussion below prioritizes comparisons among broadly similar metallogenic contexts, specifically porphyry-related, magmatic-arc settings (Teghout, Armenia; Tongling, China; and Meiduk, Iran), and treats sedimentary–metamorphic hosted turquoise datasets primarily as compositional contrasts that illustrate the magnitude of genetic-type effects. For this reason, the non-porphyry Neyshabur occurrence is retained in Table 2 only as a broader regional reference, but it is omitted from the plotted comparisons, which were deliberately restricted to porphyry-hosted deposits. Within this framework, the first-order provenance test is whether Teghout defines distinctive REE patterns and element-ratio fields relative to the Iranian and Anhui porphyry references. Comparison of Teghout turquoise with material from the Meiduk Cu deposit (Iran) and the Tongling Cu district (China) shows that the Armenian samples define a distinctive rare-earth and trace-element signature (Fig. 10). All three deposits display MREE-peaked, chondrite-normalized REE patterns, but the Teghout curves lie at higher absolute REE levels and are notably smoother and less scattered. Teghout patterns rise from La to a broad middle-REE (MREE) maximum at Sm–Gd and then decrease gently toward Lu, with only weak Eu anomalies, whereas Meiduk and Tongling exhibit lower total REE and more variable LREE/HREE slopes and Eu behavior. It is important to acknowledge that, although Teghout, Meiduk and Tongling all formed in subduction-related magmatic-arc settings, the specific tectonic episode that generated each ore-forming intrusion differs: Teghout (146 Ma) belongs to the Late Jurassic Somkheto–Karabakh island-arc system on the northern margin of the Mesozoic Tethyan domain15,17,19; Meiduk reflects Cenozoic post-collisional magmatism following Neo-Tethys closure in the Urumieh–Dokhtar magmatic arc of Iran37,38; and the Tongling district records Late Mesozoic, distal Pacific-plate-related magmatism in the lower Yangtze fold belt of eastern China39,40. These different magmatic sources and crustal-level settings produce systematic differences in the geochemistry and isotopic composition of the parent porphyries, and supergene turquoise inherits its trace-element budget by leaching of those primary assemblages together with their immediate wall rocks. Far from being a confounding source of error, the tectonic and lithological contrasts between the three deposits are therefore precisely what makes secondary turquoise from each locality chemically distinguishable. The within-type comparison undertaken here is consequently robust: the three deposits are genetically analogous (all porphyry-hosted, all formed by supergene oxidation of Cu-bearing sulfides), so the differences in their turquoise REE–trace-element fingerprints carry tectonic and lithological signal rather than methodological noise. Upper-continental-crust–normalized box plots (Fig. 10b) further highlight the contrast: Teghout turquoise is strongly enriched in Sr, Mo and Zn relative to Meiduk and Tongling, while it remains comparatively depleted in V and Rb. These features mirror the single-deposit spider diagram for Teghout (Fig. 9b), in which pronounced Sr–Mo–Sc–Zn peaks are accompanied by subdued V–Cr and large-ion lithophile elements. Taken together, the REE profiles and multi-element patterns indicate formation from Sr- and Mo-rich, V-poor supergene fluids developed above a Cu–Mo porphyry, in contrast to the Fe-rich sedimentary protoliths that dominate many Chinese turquoise districts. Recent LA-ICP-MS studies have shown that such REE–trace-element “fingerprints” are robust at the deposit scale and can be exploited for turquoise provenance and classification, particularly when combined with multivariate statistics2,5,6.

Element-ratio discrimination diagrams

Element-ratio plots provide a compact way to express the combined information contained in the trace-element datasets from the three copper deposits. In the log10(Pb/Ba) vs log10(Sr/V) diagram (Fig. 11a), Teghout samples form a tight cluster at systematically higher Sr/V than both Meiduk and Tongling for any given Pb/Ba. The visual separation among the three deposits in the Sr/V–Pb/Ba and Sr/U–Pb/U diagrams was confirmed statistically. A non-parametric multivariate analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) on the combined ratios verified that the deposits form distinct, non-random groups (R = 0.935, p < 0.001). Furthermore, convex-hull analysis confirms complete geometric separation in the Sr/V–Pb/Ba space and only minor envelope overlap in the Sr/U–Pb/U space. Full statistical details, including individual Kruskal–Wallis tests for each ratio, are reported in the Supplementary materials (Supplementary Materials-Element-ratio discrimination diagrams). This is consistent with recent provenance work, in which Sr/Ba, Ni/Co, V/Mo and related ratios have been used successfully to distinguish turquoise districts in China and the Middle East6,32. Within that framework, Teghout turquoise is characterized by distinctly elevated Sr/V and Sr/U compared with Meiduk and Tongling, reinforcing the interpretation that it records a Sr-rich, V-poor supergene environment above a porphyry Cu–Mo source. Although Mo is one of the diagnostic enriched elements at Teghout, we have not included a Mo-based ratio in Fig. 11 because in the published comparison datasets Mo concentrations are reported only sporadically and frequently fall close to detection limits, particularly at Tongling, which renders cross-deposit Mo ratios unreliable for the present comparison. Mo is therefore retained as a single-deposit signature rather than as a discrimination axis.

Sulfate signatures from Raman spectroscopy

Raman spectroscopy provides an independent, mineralogical discriminator (Fig. 11c). All three deposits display the dominant turquoise ν3(PO4) band near 1039 cm⁻1, but Teghout spectra frequently show additional bands at approximately 983 and 1007 cm⁻1. Comparison with reference spectra and recent turquoise studies indicates that the 983 cm⁻1 band corresponds to the ν1(SO4) stretching mode of chalcanthite (CuSO4·5H2O), whereas the 1007 cm⁻1 band matches the ν1(SO4) vibration of gypsum (CaSO4·2H2O). In Teghout material these bands are superimposed on the turquoise pattern with variable intensity, implying intimate intergrowths or sub-micrometre mixtures of sulfate phases with the Cu–Al phosphate. By contrast, Meiduk turquoise in our dataset shows only the gypsum-related band at 1007 cm⁻1 in addition to the 1039 cm⁻1 turquoise peak, and Tongling turquoise exhibits a “pure” turquoise spectrum with no discernible sulfate bands. Because barite and jarosite are also part of the Teghout supergene assemblage and their ν1(SO4) modes fall in a comparable spectral window, the chalcanthite and gypsum assignments were tested explicitly against these alternatives. Barite (BaSO4) shows its characteristic ν1(SO4) at 988 cm⁻141, distinguishable from the 983 cm⁻1 band recorded here at the ± 1–2 cm⁻1 resolution of the LabRAM HR Evolution. Jarosite, KFe3(SO4)2(OH)6, displays its strongest ν1(SO4) at 1005–1010 cm⁻1 together with a diagnostic Fe–O lattice mode near 430 cm⁻1 and an OH-bending band near 1100 cm⁻142,43; neither of these diagnostic low-wavenumber/high-wavenumber companion bands is present in the Teghout spots that show the 1007 cm⁻1 feature, whereas the same spots do show the gypsum ν2(SO4) band near 416 cm⁻1 and the H2O libration near 1135 cm⁻1. In addition, SEM–EDS spot analyses on the relevant micro-domains return Cu–S and Ca–S compositions consistent with chalcanthite and gypsum, respectively, and not the Ba–S or K–Fe–S signatures expected for barite or jarosite. We therefore retain the chalcanthite (983 cm⁻1) and gypsum (1007 cm⁻1) assignments and treat them as the most parsimonious interpretation of the combined Raman, SEM–EDS and BSE evidence.

Recent work on turquoise origin indicators has emphasized that deposit-specific accessory phases—such as atacamite at Tianhu East—can provide powerful geographic markers when they occur systematically alongside turquoise4,5. In the same spirit, the combined textural, mineralogical and Raman evidence from Teghout indicates that chalcanthite and gypsum are characteristic sulfate associates of this Armenian porphyry-hosted turquoise. When viewed together with the Sr-rich, V-poor REE–trace-element signature and the Sr/V–Sr/U discrimination fields discussed above, the presence of chalcanthite- and gypsum-related Raman bands provides an additional, independent axis along which Teghout turquoise can be distinguished from Meiduk, Tongling and other copper-deposit sources in future provenance and classification studies.

Significance of mineral source indication

Provenancing of raw materials is a central concern in heritage science because it links artefacts to landscapes, technologies and social networks. For colored stones such as turquoise, establishing a mineralogical and geochemical fingerprint at the deposit scale is now recognised as a prerequisite for any robust archaeological attribution of provenance. Recent studies on Chinese turquoise deposits demonstrate that carefully selected trace-element and isotopic proxies can discriminate between individual deposits and tie artefacts to specific mining districts5,6,44. Within this methodological context, the present work offers the first integrated gemmological, mineralogical and geochemical characterisation of turquoise from the Teghout Cu–Mo deposit in northern Armenia.

We stress, however, that the relationship between a modern deposit-scale reference dataset and the archaeological provenance of ancient turquoise artefacts is not symmetrical. A chemical or isotopic match between a candidate source and an artefact is a necessary but not a sufficient condition for provenance attribution, because as-yet-undiscovered ancient sources may share broadly similar geological characteristics2,8. This is particularly true for porphyry-derived supergene turquoise, where convergent leaching of compositionally comparable parent rocks can generate overlapping trace-element fingerprints. Robust archaeological attribution therefore requires the integration of compositional data with independent archaeological evidence—chronologically diagnostic context, manufacturing traces, and ideally surface or sub-surface indications of ancient mining activity at the candidate source itself. We deliberately make no claim that any specific ancient artefact from the Caucasus was sourced at Teghout; rather, our dataset establishes the analytical baseline against which such hypotheses can now be tested. We hope it will encourage targeted archaeological survey of the Teghout area and of neighbouring oxidised porphyry copper systems in Lori Province and the Lesser Caucasus, since the existence and antiquity of any ancient turquoise workings can only be established through direct field investigation.

Geologically, Teghout is a porphyry-type Cu–Mo deposit in the Alaverdi metallogenic district of the Lesser Caucasus, related to calc-alkaline intrusions of the Koghb–Shnokh plutonic complex and exploited in recent decades as a large open-pit mine10. Turquoise forms there as a supergene Cu–Al phosphate within the weathered carapace of the porphyry system, comparable in paragenesis to turquoise associated with porphyry elsewhere (Meiduk in Iran and Tongling in China). By documenting the crystal chemistry, mineral assemblages, microstructure and trace-element characteristics of Teghout turquoise, our study establishes the type of deposit-scale reference dataset that has underpinned successful provenance work in other regions6,7.

Beyond its geology, Teghout is strategically important because it appears to be the only quantitatively documented turquoise ore body in the Caucasus at the time of writing; we do not exclude the existence of additional, undocumented or minor turquoise occurrences in the region that have yet to receive systematic mineralogical and geochemical characterisation. Archaeometallurgical syntheses emphasise the exceptional concentration of metalliferous resources in Lori Province and explicitly note the presence of turquoise at Teghout since the 1970s10. These authors also stress that long-standing assumptions which automatically assign Caucasian turquoise artefacts to Iranian sources (Nishapur/Neyshabur) require revision once local sources such as Teghout are taken into account.

The archaeological record of turquoise use in the South Caucasus, summarized in Table 3 and mapped in Fig. 12, underscores this point. Turquoise and other exotic stones (lapis lazuli, carnelian) occur in Chalcolithic and Early Bronze Age graves at Kültepe I and related sites in Nakhchivan46,54, where they appear as beads in personal ornaments that signal participation in long-distance exchange networks. Turquoise beads and inlays are also documented in high-status contexts such as the Maikop “royal” kurgan and the Middle Bronze Age Trialeti tumuli, as well as in Roman-period graves at Samtavro and in the Gonio hoard, and later in the medieval Aygestan hoard from Dvin49,51. In Fig. 12 we plot the locations of Teghout and these key sites together, highlighting the close spatial relationship between the only known turquoise deposit and major centres where turquoise artefacts have been excavated and published. At present none of these artefacts can be securely attributed to Teghout, and we deliberately refrain from making such claims in the absence of compositional data from objects. The development in East Asia is instructive: first, detailed geological and mineralogical descriptions of turquoise occurrences; second, establishment of deposit-specific geochemical fingerprints using LA-ICP-MS and isotopic systems; and third, application of these fingerprints to museum and excavated artefacts to reconstruct mining catchments and exchange routes6,44,57. Our study provides the equivalent first step for the Caucasus by defining the Teghout source in sufficient mineralogical and geochemical detail, and by situating it explicitly within the regional archaeological landscape, to enable subsequent artefact-based provenance work.

Conclusion

This study presents the first integrated gemmological, mineralogical and geochemical characterisation of turquoise from the Teghout Cu–Mo deposit in northern Armenia. Turquoise occurs there as supergene vein and nodular material in the weathered carapace of a porphyry system, forming fine-grained aggregates of prismatic microcrystals. Spectroscopic data confirm a structurally typical turquoise, but also reveal three recurring Raman types, two of which carry additional bands attributable to chalcanthite and gypsum, documenting a sulfate-rich supergene environment and an unusually close association between turquoise and these phases.

Trace-element and REE data define a coherent chemical fingerprint characterised by chondrite-normalised REE patterns that rise from La to a broad MREE maximum at Sm–Gd and decline gently toward Lu (MREE-peaked rather than steeply LREE-enriched), weak Eu anomalies, and a distinctive enrichment in Sr, Mo, Zn and Sc coupled with low V and Rb. In element-ratio space (e.g. Sr/V, Sr/U versus Pb/Ba, Pb/U) Teghout turquoise occupies a compact field that is clearly separated from comparison material from Meiduk (Iran) and Tongling (China), while the systematic presence of chalcanthite- and gypsum-related Raman bands provides an independent spectroscopic discriminator. Together, these features distinguish Teghout turquoise from other well-studied deposits and link it to its porphyry Cu–Mo host environment.

From a heritage-science perspective, the Teghout dataset has particular significance because Teghout is currently the only quantitatively characterised documented turquoise ore body in the Caucasus and lies within a region dense in Neolithic, Chalcolithic, Bronze and Iron Age archaeological sites and an area, where mining and metallurgy appeared early (Meliksetian and Pernicka58) Although no archaeological artefacts can yet be securely attributed to this source, the deposit-scale reference framework established here is a necessary precondition for future provenance work on turquoise objects from regional sites such as Kültepe, Maikop, Trialeti, Samtavro, Gonio, Sisian, Dvin and others. More broadly, the study illustrates how detailed mineralogical and geochemical investigation of an actively exploited ore deposit can generate durable reference data for non-renewable gem resources and open concrete avenues for interdisciplinary research, including targeted analysis of museum collections, comparative studies of additional turquoise occurrences in the region, and spatial modelling of potential supply networks in the South Caucasus from the Neolithic to the Medieval period. By adding a well-characterized South Caucasus reference point to existing Meiduk-Iran and Tongling-China porphyry-related datasets, the Teghout results help close a key geographic gap in the porphyry-hosted turquoise reference record of the South Caucasus and the wider trans-Eurasian region. The within-type comparison developed here is therefore directly transferable to archaeometric provenance studies of turquoise artefacts and to first-pass geochemical screening of gem materials in contemporary circulation.
