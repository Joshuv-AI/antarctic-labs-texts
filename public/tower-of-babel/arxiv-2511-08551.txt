Deterministic Negative-Weight Shortest Paths in Nearly Linear Time via Path Covers
Bernhard Haeupler, Yonggang Jiang, Thatchaphol Saranurak

Abstract
We present the first deterministic nearly-linear time algorithm for single-source shortest paths with negative edge weights on directed graphs: given a directed graph G G with n n vertices, m m edges whose weights are integers in { − W , … , W } \{-W,\dots,W\} , our algorithm either computes all distances from a source s s or reports a negative cycle in O ~ ​ ( m ) ⋅ log ⁡ ( n ​ W ) \tilde{O}(m)\cdot\log(nW) time. All known near-linear time algorithms for this problem have been inherently randomized [ BNW25 , BCF23 , FHL+25 , LM24 ] , as they crucially rely on low-diameter decompositions . To overcome this barrier, we introduce a new structural primitive for directed graphs called the path cover . This plays a role analogous to neighborhood covers in undirected graphs [ ABCP98 ] , which have long been central to derandomizing algorithms that use low-diameter decomposition in the undirected setting. We believe that path covers will serve as a fundamental tool for the design of future deterministic algorithms on directed graphs.

Introduction

In the negative weight single-source shortest paths (SSSP) problem, given an
m
-edge
n
-vertex directed weighted graph
G=(V,E,w)
with
possibly negative
edge weight
w(e)\in\{-W,\dots,W-1,W\}
for each
e\in E
and a source vertex
s\in V
, the goal is to either compute the distance from
s
to every other vertex or find a negative-weight cycle.

When weights are non-negative, the textbook Dijkstra algorithm takes near-linear
O(m+n\log n)
time. Another textbook Bellman-Ford algorithm can handle negative weights, but requires much slower
O(mn)
time. This contrast motivates the search for near-linear time algorithms since 1980s
[
Gar85
,
GT89
,
Gol95
,
CMSV17
,
vdBLN+20
,
AMV20
,
VDBLL+21
]
.

In 2022, two algorithmic breakthroughs happened. Chen et al.
[
CKL+22
]
gave a randomized algorithm with
m^{1+o(1)}\log W
time that solves an even more general minimum cost flow problem. Their approach is based on convex optimization and dynamic data structures and was later improved to be deterministic
[
VDBCK+23
,
CKL+24
,
VDBCK+24
]
. However, the dynamic data structures required in this approach are complicated and the

factor in time seems hard to avoid.

Independently, in the same year, Bernstein, Nanongkai, and Wulff-Nilsen
[
BNW25
]
showed an algorithm with

time, which is faster, combinatorial, and bypasses complicated dynamic data structures. The follow-up work
[
BCF23
,

,
LM24
,
FHL+25
]
subsequently sped up the time to
O((m+n\log\log n)\log^{2}(n)\log(nW))
. Unfortunately, algorithms in this line of work are inherently randomized.

Until now, there is no deterministic negative weight SSSP algorithm with near-linear
O(m\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(nW))
time.

Our result.

We give the first deterministic near-linear algorithm, improving upon both lines of work above.

Theorem 1.1

(Deterministic SSSP)
.

There is a
deterministic
algorithm solving the negative weight single-source shortest path problem in
O(m\log^{8}n\log(nW))
time.

To prove
Theorem

1.1
, we introduce a novel graph structure called
path cover
, which we believe will find further applications beyond
Theorem

1.1
itself. We do not optimize the polylogarithmic factor in order to present the clearest version of this new concept. A more relaxed version of path covers can speed up our algorithm to
O(m\log^{5}n\log(nW))
time. Below, we first define the path covering problem to motivate path covers.

The Path Covering Problem.

We say a path
p

d
-path
if its length is at most

covers

Problem 1.1

(Covering
d
-Paths with Clustered Subgraphs)

Fix a graph

-clustered subgraphs

.

The frameworks of
[

]

[

1.1

A Barrier for Subgraphs: No Deterministic Algorithm on Directed Graphs.

Unfortunately, unlike undirected graphs, we show that

For every

We start this section by sketching the
existence

of

We start with some standard notation: For every vertex
v\in V

\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(v)
be the out-degree of
v
, i.e., the number of edges leaving
v
. For a vertex set
S\subseteq V
let
\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(S)=\sum_{v\in S}\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(v)
be the number of edges starting in
S
. Lastly, for any vertex

, define

\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(u,r)=\{v\in V\mid\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq r\}

to be the ball centered at
u
with radius
r
.

Construction.

Our algorithm is recursive.

If the diameter of
G
is one strongly connected component with diameter
O(d\log^{3}n)
, then one can trivially return the whole graph and terminate the recursion.

Otherwise, the algorithm finds a good partition by picking an arbitrary node
u
and growing a ball from it by computing
\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(u,i\cdot d)
for
i=1,2,3,\ldots
until we find a “thin layer”
i^{*}
such that

\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}\!\left(\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(u,i^{*}\cdot d)\right)\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{\log^{2}n}\right)\cdot\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}\!\left(\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(u,(i^{*}-1)\cdot d)\right)\,.

(1)

Since the ball cannot grow multiplicatively too often it is clear that
i^{*}=O(\log^{3}n)
.
The algorithm works separately and recursively on both

B^{\mathrm{out}}:=\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(u,i^{*}\cdot d)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}:=V\setminus\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}\!\left(u,(i^{*}-1)\cdot d\right).

Note that
B^{\mathrm{out}}
and
\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}
only
overlap
at the “ring” around
u
from radius
(i^{*}-1)\cdot d
to
i^{*}\cdot d
.

We focus here on the interesting case that the ball grown from
u
is not more than a constant fraction of the graph, i.e.,
\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}\!\left(B^{\mathrm{out}}\right)\leq 0.9m
, our proof shows that otherwise there is a way to carve out a large ball and still make progress. Note that by running Dijkstra’s algorithm locally from
u
the time needed to grow the ball
B^{\mathrm{out}}
is
\widetilde{O}\left(\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(B^{\mathrm{out}})\right)
and therefore essentially proportional to its size and independent of the size of
\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}
. This makes the recursion efficient. Let
H^{\mathrm{out}}
and
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}}
be
d
-path covers of the induced subgraphs
G[B^{\mathrm{out}}]
and
G[\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}]
, respectively, which are constructed
recursively
.

For technical reasons, we strengthen the property of
H^{\mathrm{out}}
(and likewise for
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}}
): not only do we require each
d
-path in

to have a lift
p^{\prime}
in
H^{\mathrm{out}}
, we require a

mapping between the start vertices of

and

. In other words, each vertex

such that if a

starts at

We build the

-path cover of

\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}}
and
H^{\mathrm{out}}
as follows: for every
(u,v)\in E

Path Covering.

We show that
G^{\prime}
indeed covers all
d
-paths in
G
.

We consider the case where a path
p

v_{p}\in\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}
and leaves
\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}
at some point (i.e., enters

). Other situations (completely inside
\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}
or starting in

Let

be the first vertex on

\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}
.
Let

be the subpath of

(excluding

Notice that

v^{\prime}_{p}\in V\setminus\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}=V\setminus\!\left(V\setminus\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}\!\left(u,(i^{*}-1)\cdot d\right)\right)=\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}\!\left(u,(i^{*}-1)\cdot d\right).

This means that every path starting at
v^{\prime}_{p}
with length at most
d
(in particular,
p_{2}
) is contained in

Therefore

lies completely inside

and

lies completely inside
G[B^{\mathrm{out}}]
, so
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}}
covers

with a lift

and

.

We then concatenate
p^{\prime}_{1}

using the edge from the last vertex of

(which is the representative of

in

Size of the path cover.

We first show that the size of

satisfies

|E(G^{\prime})|\leq\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V(G^{\prime})}\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}\!\bigl(\pi(v^{\prime})\bigr).

\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}}
and
H^{\mathrm{out}}
, where

and

are copies of

and

, respectively, so that

and
v^{\prime}
is the representative of

in

.

and

|E(G^{\prime})|\leq(1+1/\log n)\cdot m

Preliminaries

We use the following notations,
[a]=\{1,2,\dots,a\}
,
\widetilde{O}\left(f\right)=f\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(n)
, where we always use
n,m
to denote the number of nodes and edges of the input graph. For a function
\pi:V\to V^{\prime}
, we write
\pi^{-1}(v^{\prime})=\{v\in V\mid\pi(v)=v^{\prime}\}
. All algorithms in this paper are deterministic.

Graph.

All graphs of this paper, unless specified, are directed graphs with edge weights, denoted by
G=(V,E,w)
. We assume edge weights are
integers
within the range
[-W,W]
.
We use standard definitions of graph terminologies for directed graphs which can be found in textbooks, including paths, distances, strongly connected components (SCCs), negative cycles, shortest-path trees, etc. For a cycle or path
H
, we use
w(H)
to denote the sum of weights of edges on the cycle or path, and
|H|
to denote the number of edges on the cycle or path (note that a path can have repeated edges; in this case, both the weight and the number of such an edge are counted multiple times). We use
w_{\geq 0}
to denote the weight truncated at
0
, i.e.,
w_{\geq 0}(e)=\max\{0,w(e)\}
for every
e\in E
. Similarly, we write
G_{\geq 0}=(V,E,w_{\geq 0})
.

In many cases of this paper, a path is not a
simple path
, so a path can have repeated vertices (but usually a shortest path is a simple path). We use
\mathrm{Start}(p)
and
\mathrm{End}(p)
to denote the starting vertex and ending vertex of a directed path
p
. We use
p_{1}\oplus p_{2}
to denote the concatenation of two paths: if
p_{1}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{z})
and
p_{2}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{z^{\prime}})
, then
p_{1}\oplus p_{2}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{z},u_{1},\dots,u_{z^{\prime}})
. Notice that

is a path only if
(v_{z},u_{1})
is an edge.

We use
V(G)
and
E(G)
to denote the vertex set and edge set of a graph
G

w_{G}
to stress that
w_{G}
is the weight function for
G
. We write
\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(u)
to denote the number of edges
(u,v)
for
v\in V
, and
\deg^{\mathrm{in}}_{G}(v)
to denote the number of edges
(u,v)
for
u\in V
. We write
\deg_{G}(v)=\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(v)+\deg^{\mathrm{in}}_{G}(v)
. We write
\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(A)=\sum_{v\in A}\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(v)
,
\deg^{\mathrm{in}}_{G}(A)=\sum_{v\in A}\deg^{\mathrm{in}}_{G}(v)
, and
\deg_{G}(A)=\sum_{v\in A}\deg_{G}(v)
.

Graph distance.

We use
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,t)
to denote the distance from
s
to
t
. When

is clear from the context, we simply write

. We write

\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G}(s,d)=\{v\in V(G)\mid\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,v)\leq d\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{in}}_{G}(s,d)=\{v\in V(G)\mid\mathrm{dist}_{G}(v,s)\leq d\}.

We allow distance to be
+\infty
(in the case of not connected) or
-\infty
(in the case of reaching a negative cycle) so that every distance is well-defined.

Given a directed graph
G=(V,E,w)
and a vertex set

of

\max_{s,t\in A}\mathrm{dist}_{G[A]}(s,t)
where

is the induced subgraph of

on

. The diameter of

is also the diameter of
G[A]
.

The single-source shortest path problem (SSSP).

Given a directed graph
G=(V,E)
with integer (possibly negative) edge weights and a source
s\in V
, either output
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,v)
for every
v\in V
, or output a negative cycle of
G
. It is folklore that we can get a shortest-path tree from a distance function, and vice versa.

Potential adjustment.

Given a

\phi:V\to{\mathbb{Z}}
and a graph

, we define an adjusted weight
w_{\phi}(u,v)=w(u,v)+\phi(u)-\phi(v)
. We define the adjusted graph
G_{\phi}=(V,E,w_{\phi})
.

The following lemma shows the power of potential adjustment. The lemma is folklore and was already used in
[
Joh77
]
.

3.1
[
Joh77
]
.

Let
G
be a graph and

be a potential function. If

has no negative cycle, a subgraph is a shortest-path tree of

iff it is a shortest-path tree of

. More precisely, let

be the distance in

4
Path Cover via a Clustered Graph

In this section, we present a method for covering every bounded-length path in a directed graph using a clustered graph.
We begin with the necessary definitions. All graphs in this section have non-negative edge weights.

4.1
Definitions and the Main Theorem

We first formally define graph projections introduced in the introduction.

4.1
Graph Projections.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a graph.
A
projection onto
G
is a graph
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},w^{\prime})
together with a projection map
\pi=\pi_{G^{\prime}}
where
\pi
is a
weight-preserving graph homomorphism
: for every
(x,y)\in E(G^{\prime})
, we have
(\pi(x),\pi(y))\in E(G)
and
w^{\prime}(x,y)=w(\pi(x),\pi(y))
.

When we refer to a projection
G^{\prime}
, we implicitly assume its map
\pi_{G^{\prime}}
.

4.2
Path Covering.

Given a path
p=(v_{1},\dots,v_{z})
in
G
, we say that a projection
(G^{\prime},\pi)
onto
G

covers

p
if there exists a path
p^{\prime}=(v^{\prime}_{1},\dots,v^{\prime}_{z})
in
G^{\prime}
such that
\pi(v^{\prime}_{i})=v_{i}
for every
i\in[z]
. We call
p^{\prime}
the
lift
of
p
.
The projection is
d
-path-covering
if every simple path in
G
of length at most
d
is covered.

Our goal is to cover every simple path of length at most
d
by a projection whose SCCs have small diameter.
For this purpose, we define clustered graphs as follows.

4.3
Clustered Graphs.

We say that a directed graph
G^{\prime}
is a
d
-clustered graph
if every strongly connected component (SCC) of
G^{\prime}
has diameter at most
d
.

4.4
Path Cover.

A
d
-path cover
of a directed graph
G=(V,E,w)
with
diameter slack

\lambda
is a
d
-path-covering projection
G^{\prime}
where
G^{\prime}
is a
\lambda\cdot d
-clustered graph.

We will prove the following theorem in this section.

4.5
Path Cover via Clustered DAGs.

There exists a deterministic algorithm that, given a directed graph
G=(V,E,w)
with non-negative edge weights, a diameter parameter
d
, and a slack parameter
\lambda\geq 10000\log^{4}n
, constructs a
d
-path cover
G^{\prime}
of
G
with diameter slack
\lambda
and size at most

\left(1+\tfrac{100\log^{2}n}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)\cdot m,

in
O(\sqrt{\lambda}\cdot m\log n)
time.

Moreover, we have

\sum_{v\in V(G^{\prime})}\deg_{G}(\pi(v))\leq\left(1+\tfrac{100\log^{2}n}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)\cdot m.

To get
Theorem

1.3
, we only need to set
\lambda=10000\log^{6}n
.

4.6
.

The running time can be improved to
O(m\log^{2}n)
, independent of
\lambda
, if we relax the structure of the path cover so that we only guarantee
weak diameter w.r.t.
G
. More precisely, for each strongly connected component
U
in
G^{\prime}
,
\max_{u,v\in U}\mathrm{dist}_{G}(\pi(u),\pi(v))\leq d\lambda
. This weaker guarantee is sufficient for our negative-weight shortest path algorithms, and we can improve the running time from
O(m\log^{8}n\log(nW))
to
O(m\log^{5}n\log(nW))
in this way.
However, we choose to construct the path cover with a strong diameter guarantee to ease the understanding of this new concept.

4.2
The Algorithm

In this section, we describe the algorithm for

be the input directed graph and

The following definition is useful when describing algorithms.

3

3

The representative in our case is basically the first appearance of the copy of

in

(we will have the guarantee that each SCC is a subgraph of

4.7
Layered Projection.

Let

be projections onto

with representatives for

A

G^{\prime}=\mathsf{Layer}((G_{1},\dots,G_{z})\rightarrow G)

with the same projection map on vertices
4
4

4

When

in
G^{\prime}
.
. For every

The algorithm is recursive. For convenience, we use

We define the following parameters:

\epsilon=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}},\qquad\epsilon^{\prime}=\frac{9\log n}{\lambda}.

Base case.

When
|A|=1

with the trivial projection map and representative.

Recursive step.

Pick an arbitrary vertex
u
in
A
. We execute the following two procedures in an interleaved fashion: we perform one memory access of each procedure in turn, alternating between them, until one of them terminates. As soon as one procedure stops, the entire process halts.

1.

(Forward growing)
Run Dijkstra’s algorithm to find the smallest
i^{\mathrm{out}}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that

\deg_{G}(\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G[A]}(u,i^{\mathrm{out}}\cdot d))\leq\left(1+\epsilon^{\prime}\right)\cdot\deg_{G}(\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G[A]}(u,(i^{\mathrm{out}}-1)\cdot d)).

Such an
i^{\mathrm{out}}
must exist because for sufficiently large

\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G[A]}(u,i\cdot d)=G[A]
.

To be precise, Dijkstra’s algorithm fixes the distances from
u
to other nodes in increasing order. We run it only up to the point where the distance exceeds
i\cdot d
, in order to compute
\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G[A]}(u,i\cdot d)
. This can take sublinear time because the algorithm stops once the desired index
i^{\mathrm{out}}
is found.

For technical reasons, we assume that when Dijkstra’s algorithm fixes the distance of a node

\deg_{G}(v)

v
in
G
(but only performs
\deg^{\mathrm{out}}_{G[A]}(v)

2.

(Backward growing)
Identical to the forward growing procedure, but with all occurrences of ‘
\mathrm{out}

\mathrm{in}
’.

For simplicity, we use the following notation. Notice that the algorithm only knows
B^{\mathrm{out}},\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}
but does not know

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{out}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G[A]}(u,i^{\mathrm{out}}\cdot d),

\displaystyle\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}

\displaystyle=A-\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{out}}_{G[A]}(u,(i^{\mathrm{out}}-1)\cdot d),

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{in}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{in}}_{G[A]}(u,i^{\mathrm{in}}\cdot d),

\displaystyle\bar{B}^{\mathrm{in}}

\displaystyle=A-\mathrm{Ball}^{\mathrm{in}}_{G[A]}(u,(i^{\mathrm{in}}-1)\cdot d).

Without loss of generality, assume forward growing stops earlier than backward growing (the other case is handled symmetrically). Consider two cases.

1.

Case 1:
\deg_{G}(B^{\mathrm{out}})<\left(1-\epsilon\right)\deg_{G}(A)
.
In this case, let

H^{\mathrm{out}}\leftarrow\textsc{PathCover}\!\left(B^{\mathrm{out}}\right),\qquad\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}}\leftarrow\textsc{PathCover}\!\left(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}\right).

Let
G^{\prime}
be the layered projection induced from
(\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}},H^{\mathrm{out}})
(

4.7

G^{\prime}
as the output of
\textsc{PathCover}(A)
.

2.

Case 2:
\deg_{G}(B^{\mathrm{out}})\geq(1-\epsilon)\deg_{G}(A)
.
Continue the backward growing until it stops. Now the algorithm also knows
B^{\mathrm{in}},\bar{B}^{\mathrm{in}}
.
Let
T^{\mathrm{out}}
be the shortest-path tree in

with root

be the reversed shortest-path tree in

Let

Let

Similarly, define

Define

Notice that

Make the following recursive calls:

We prove the correctness of the algorithm described in

Base case.

Induction step.

Proof.

For every

4.4
Size and Time Complexity

The algorithm is recursive. We first prove the following lemma.

4.9
.

Suppose
G^{\prime}\leftarrow\textsc{PathCover}(A)
. The size of
G^{\prime}
is at most
\sum_{v\in V(G^{\prime})}\deg_{G[A]}(\pi(v))
.

Proof.

By induction on
|A|
. When
|A|=1
, there are no edges in
G^{\prime}
, so the lemma is trivial. Suppose
|A|>1
. The algorithm returns different
G^{\prime}
based on two cases.

Case 1.

The size of
G^{\prime}
contains two parts: (1) the sizes of
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}},H^{\mathrm{out}}
, which, by induction, are at most

\sum_{v\in V(\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}})}\deg_{G[\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}]}(\pi(v))+\sum_{v\in V(H^{\mathrm{out}})}\deg_{G[B^{\mathrm{out}}]}(\pi(v)).

(2) The edges added by
Definition

4.7
. For every
v\in V(\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}})
, the number of edges added from
v
equals
\deg_{G[A]}(\pi(v))-\deg_{G[\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}]}(\pi(v))
, because only vertices in
A-\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}
can have representatives in
H^{\mathrm{out}}
; all other vertices are present in
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}}
. Summing over
\sum_{v\in V(\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}})}(\deg_{G[A]}(\pi(v))-\deg_{G[\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}]}(\pi(v)))
and
\sum_{v\in V(\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}})}\deg_{G[\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}]}(\pi(v))+\sum_{v\in V(H^{\mathrm{out}})}\deg_{G[B^{\mathrm{out}}]}(\pi(v))
gives the bound
\sum_{v\in V(G^{\prime})}\deg_{G[A]}(\pi(v))
.

Case 2.

(i) The
\tilde{H}^{\mathrm{out}}
-term.

All edges with both endpoints in
\tilde{H}^{\mathrm{out}}
lie inside the induced subgraph

G[\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}\cap B^{\mathrm{in}}]
. Thus

|E(\tilde{H}^{\mathrm{out}})|\;\leq\;\sum_{v\in V(\tilde{H}^{\mathrm{out}})}\deg_{G[\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}\cap B^{\mathrm{in}}]}(\pi(v)).

Any additional edge incident to
v\in V(\tilde{H}^{\mathrm{out}})
that goes to

H^{\mathrm{mid}}\cup\bar{H}^{\mathrm{in}}
must use a representative outside
\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}\cap B^{\mathrm{in}}
, so the
number of such outgoing edges is at most

\sum_{v\in V(\tilde{H}^{\mathrm{out}})}\Bigl(\deg_{G[A]}(\pi(v))-\deg_{G[\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}\cap B^{\mathrm{in}}]}(\pi(v))\Bigr).

Consequently, the total number of edges charged to
\tilde{H}^{\mathrm{out}}
is at most

\sum_{v\in V(\tilde{H}^{\mathrm{out}})}\deg_{G[A]}(\pi(v)).

(ii) The
H^{\mathrm{mid}}
-term.

Edges internal to
H^{\mathrm{mid}}
are contained in the induced subgraph on

V(H^{\mathrm{mid}})
, so

|E(H^{\mathrm{mid}})|\;\leq\;\sum_{v\in V(H^{\mathrm{mid}})}\deg_{G[V(H^{\mathrm{mid}})]}(\pi(v)).

Edges from
H^{\mathrm{mid}}
to
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{in}}
are added only from the
H^{\mathrm{mid}}
-side, and for
each
v\in V(H^{\mathrm{mid}})
there are at most

\deg_{G[A]}(\pi(v))-\deg_{G[V(H^{\mathrm{mid}})]}(\pi(v))

such edges. Hence the total number of edges charged to
H^{\mathrm{mid}}
is at most

\sum_{v\in V(H^{\mathrm{mid}})}\deg_{G[A]}(\pi(v)).

(iii) The
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{in}}
-term.

By the induction hypothesis applied within
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{in}}
, the edges with both
endpoints in
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{in}}
are at most

|E(\bar{H}^{\mathrm{in}})|\;\leq\;\sum_{v\in V(\bar{H}^{\mathrm{in}})}\deg_{G[A]}(\pi(v)).

Inter-piece edges incident to
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{in}}
are
not
charged here (they were
already charged to their tails in
\tilde{H}^{\mathrm{out}}
or
H^{\mathrm{mid}}
), so there is no
double counting.

Summing the contributions from (i)–(iii) yields

|E(G^{\prime})|\;\leq\;\sum_{v\in V(\tilde{H}^{\mathrm{out}})}\deg_{G[A]}(\pi(v))+\sum_{v\in V(H^{\mathrm{mid}})}\deg_{G[A]}(\pi(v))+\sum_{v\in V(\bar{H}^{\mathrm{in}})}\deg_{G[A]}(\pi(v))

=\sum_{v\in V(G^{\prime})}\deg_{G[A]}(\pi(v)),

which completes the proof.

∎

Thus, it remains to bound

, which we prove next.

4.10
.

Suppose
G^{\prime}\leftarrow\textsc{PathCover}(A)
. Then

\sum_{v\in V(G^{\prime})}\deg_{G}(\pi(v))\leq\left(1+9(\log(\deg_{G}(A)))\cdot\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{\epsilon}\right)\deg_{G}(A).

The running time of
\textsc{PathCover}(A)
is at most
\log(\deg_{G}(A))\cdot O(\deg_{G}(A)/\epsilon)

Proof.

By induction on
|A|
. For
|A|=1
,
PathCover
returns
G[A]
, and the claim is trivial.

The algorithm first runs two procedures (forward and backward growing) in lockstep, one edge access at a time, and stops as soon as one stops. The time between two edge accesses is at most
O(\log n)
using a standard heap. Assume forward growing stops earlier.

The forward procedure runs Dijkstra from
u
until it finds
i^{\mathrm{out}}
, costing
O(\log n\cdot\deg_{G}(B^{\mathrm{out}}))
.

We analyze the two cases.

Case 1:
\deg_{G}(B^{\mathrm{out}})<(1-\epsilon)\deg_{G}(A)
.

Here
V(G^{\prime})=V(H^{\mathrm{out}})\cup V(\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}})
with
H^{\mathrm{out}}=\textsc{PathCover}(B^{\mathrm{out}})
and
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{out}}=\textsc{PathCover}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}})
. By induction,

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(G^{\prime})}\deg_{G}(\pi(v))

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{9\epsilon^{\prime}\log\bigl|\deg_{G}(B^{\mathrm{out}})\bigr|}{\epsilon}\right)\deg_{G}(B^{\mathrm{out}})+\left(1+\frac{9\epsilon^{\prime}\log\bigl|\deg_{G}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}})\bigr|}{\epsilon}\right)\deg_{G}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{9\epsilon^{\prime}(\log(1-\epsilon)+\log\bigl|\deg_{G}(A)\bigr|)}{\epsilon}\right)(1+\epsilon^{\prime})\deg_{G}(A-\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}})

\displaystyle\quad+\left(1+\frac{9\epsilon^{\prime}\log\bigl|\deg_{G}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}})\bigr|}{\epsilon}\right)\deg_{G}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{9\epsilon^{\prime}\log\bigl|\deg_{G}(A)\bigr|}{\epsilon}\right)\deg_{G}(A).

The second inequality uses
\deg_{G}(B^{\mathrm{out}})<(1-\epsilon)\deg_{G}(A)
,
\lambda\geq 1000\log^{2}n
, and the definitions of
B^{\mathrm{out}},\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}
. The third inequality uses

\left(1+\frac{9\epsilon^{\prime}(\log(1-\epsilon)+\log\bigl|\deg_{G}(A)\bigr|)}{\epsilon}\right)\leq 2,\qquad\frac{\log(1-\epsilon)}{\epsilon}\leq-0.4,

after plugging in
\epsilon^{\prime},\epsilon
.

The running time consists of two recursive calls and building the layered projection. The latter is bounded by
\sum_{v\in V(G^{\prime})}\deg_{G}(\pi(v))
, since every edge we add corresponds to some
v^{\prime}\in V(G^{\prime})
and an edge

in

. The total complexity is

O(\log n\cdot\deg_{G}(B^{\mathrm{out}}))+\tfrac{\log(\deg_{G}(A))+\log(1-\epsilon)}{\epsilon}\cdot O(\log n\cdot(\deg_{G}(B^{\mathrm{out}})+\deg_{G}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}})))+O(\deg_{G}(A))

where the first term is for finding
i^{\mathrm{out}}
(or
i^{\mathrm{in}}
), the second for recursion by induction, and the last for constructing
G^{\prime}
, which is
\leq 2\deg_{G}(A)
since
\lambda>10000\log^{4}n
.

Case 2:
\deg_{G}(B^{\mathrm{out}})\geq(1-\epsilon)\deg_{G}(A)
.

Now
V(G^{\prime})=V(\tilde{H}^{\mathrm{out}})\cup V(H^{\mathrm{mid}})\cup V(\bar{H}^{\mathrm{in}})
with
\tilde{H}^{\mathrm{out}}=\textsc{PathCover}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}\cap B^{\mathrm{in}})
,
H^{\mathrm{mid}}=G[B^{\mathrm{out}}\cap B^{\mathrm{in}}]
, and
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{in}}=\textsc{PathCover}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{in}})
. We need:

4.11
.

\deg_{G}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}),\deg_{G}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{in}})\leq 2\epsilon\deg_{G}(A)

Proof.

Since forward growing stops earlier than backward growing, the backward procedure accesses at least as many edges before stopping. As each accessed edge contributes to

\deg_{G}(B^{\mathrm{out}}),\deg_{G}(B^{\mathrm{in}})\geq(1-\epsilon)\deg_{G}(A)

,

∎

By induction,

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(G^{\prime})}\deg_{G}(\pi(v))

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{9\epsilon^{\prime}\log\bigl|\deg_{G}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}\cap B^{\mathrm{in}})\bigr|}{\epsilon}\right)\deg_{G}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{out}}\cap B^{\mathrm{in}})

\displaystyle\quad+\left(1+\frac{9\epsilon^{\prime}\log\bigl|\deg_{G}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{in}})\bigr|}{\epsilon}\right)\deg_{G}(\bar{B}^{\mathrm{in}})

\displaystyle\leq\deg_{G}(A)+9\epsilon\deg_{G}(A)\leq\left(1+\frac{9\epsilon^{\prime}\log\bigl|\deg_{G}(A)\bigr|}{\epsilon}\right)\deg_{G}(A),

using

(\log(1-\epsilon)+\log(\deg_{G}(A)))\cdot O(\log n\cdot\deg_{G}(A)/\epsilon)

\log(\deg_{G}(A))\cdot O(\log n\cdot\deg_{G}(A)/\epsilon)

∎

Let

in

>k/2

still uses

by construction of

5.9
.

For every edge

The source
s^{\prime\prime}
has no incoming edges, so assume

. By construction of

, edges go only from

.

5.3
Time Complexity

Let
T(m,k)
denote the running time of
\textsc{kSSSP}(H,s,k)
. We show
T(m,n)=O(m\log^{8}n)
by writing a recurrence for
T(m,k)
.

•

Building the path cover for
H_{\geq 0}
takes
O(m\log^{4}n)
time by
Theorem

4.5
(since
\sqrt{\lambda}=100\log^{3}n
).

•

The recursive call requires an SCC decomposition in
O(m)
time and costs

T\!\left(\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\log n}\right)m,\ \tfrac{k}{2}\right).

•

For computing distances, we build
G^{\prime\prime}
. Each vertex
u^{\prime}_{i}
has out-degree
O(\deg_{H}(\pi(u^{\prime})))
, and there are
x=O(\log^{6}n)
copies, hence

|E(G^{\prime\prime})|\;\leq\;O(\log^{6}n)\cdot\sum_{u^{\prime}\in V(G^{\prime})}\deg_{H}(\pi(u^{\prime}))\;=\;O(m\log^{6}n)

by
Theorem

4.5
. Therefore
Lemma

5.4
on
G^{\prime\prime}
costs
O(m\log^{7}n)
time.

We obtain the recurrence

T(m,k)\;\leq\;T\!\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{\log n}\right)m,\frac{k}{2}\right)\;+\;O\!\left(m\log^{7}n\right),

with base case
T(m,O(\log^{6}n))=O(m\log^{7}n)
by
Lemma

5.5
. Solving gives
T(m,n)=O(m\log^{8}n)
.

Appendix A
Basic Subroutines for SSSP

Proof of
Lemma

5.4
.

Let the SCCs of
G
be
(V_{1},V_{2},\dots,V_{z})
in a topological order of the condensation DAG. Define a potential function
\phi
by

\forall\,i\in[z],\ \forall\,v\in V_{i}:\quad\phi(v)=-W\cdot i.

If
(u,v)\in E
has
u,v
in the same SCC, then
w_{\phi}(u,v)=w(u,v)\geq 0
. Otherwise, if
u\in V_{i}
and
v\in V_{j}
with
i<j
, then

w_{\phi}(u,v)=w(u,v)+\phi(u)-\phi(v)=w(u,v)+W(j-i)\geq w(u,v)+W\geq 0.

Hence all edges of
G_{\phi}
have nonnegative weight, so Dijkstra’s algorithm computes SSSP on
G_{\phi}
in
O(m\log n)
time. By
Lemma

3.1
, this yields a shortest-path tree for
G
as well.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

5.5
.

Build a
(k+1)
-layer graph
G^{\prime}
as follows. For each
i\in[k+1]
, let
G_{i}
be a copy of
G_{\geq 0}
(the subgraph of nonnegative-weight edges), and let
u_{i}
denote the copy of
u\in V
in layer
i
. For every negative edge
(u,v)\in E
with
w(u,v)<0
and every
i\in[k]
, add the interlayer edge
(u_{i},v_{i+1})
with weight
w(u,v)
. Run Dijkstra’s algorithm from
s_{1}
in
G^{\prime}
, and return

\min_{i\in[k+1]}\ \mathrm{dist}_{G^{\prime}}(s_{1},u_{i})\qquad\text{as }\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,u).

Running time.

We have
|V(G^{\prime})|=(k+1)|V|
and

|E(G^{\prime})|=(k+1)|E_{\geq 0}|+k|E_{<0}|\leq O(km).

Thus Dijkstra on
G^{\prime}
takes
O(km\log|V(G^{\prime})|)=O(km\log n)
time since
|V(G^{\prime})|\leq(k+1)n
.

Correctness.

First, fix
i\in[k+1]
and let
P^{\prime}
be any
s_{1}\!\to\!u_{i}
path in
G^{\prime}
. Projecting layers and replacing each interlayer edge by its original negative edge yields an
s\!\to\!u
walk in
G
of weight at most
w(P^{\prime})
. Hence
\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\prime}}(s_{1},u_{i})\geq\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,u)
.

Conversely, let
p
be a shortest
s\!\to\!u
path in
G
. By assumption,
p
uses at most
k
negative edges. Deleting these negative edges decomposes
p
as

p\;=\;p_{1}\oplus p_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus p_{z},

with
z\leq k+1
and each
p_{j}
consisting only of nonnegative edges. For each
j\in[z]
, let
p^{\prime}_{j}
be the corresponding path in layer
G_{j}
(same vertices, same weight). For each negative edge between
p_{j}
and
p_{j+1}
, use the interlayer edge from layer
j
to
j+1
. Concatenating
p^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,p^{\prime}_{z}
with these interlayer edges yields an
s_{1}\!\to\!u_{z}
path in
G^{\prime}
of weight exactly
w(p)=\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,u)
. Therefore

\min_{i}\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\prime}}(s_{1},u_{i})\leq\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,u).

Combining both directions gives
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,u)=\min_{i}\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\prime}}(s_{1},u_{i})
.
∎

Appendix B
Randomized or Undirected Solutions for
Problem

1.1

Low Diameter Decomposition: Randomized Solution.

Let
G
be a directed graph
G
with non-negative weight and
d^{\prime}\geq 0
. A
low-diameter decomposition
(LDD) is an edge set
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
such that
G\setminus E^{\prime}
is a
\tilde{O}(d^{\prime})
-clustered graph and, for each
e\in E
,
\Pr[e\in E^{\prime}]\leq\frac{w(e)}{d^{\prime}}\log n
. Bernstein et al.
[
BNW25
]
showed how to sample
E^{\prime}
in near-linear time.

Observe that, by sampling
z=O(\log n)
copies of LDD
E^{\prime}_{i}
of diameter parameter
d^{\prime}=2d\log n
and set
G^{\prime}_{i}=G\setminus E^{\prime}_{i}
, each length-
d
path
p
is covered by some
G^{\prime}_{i}
with probability at least
1-1/n^{10}
. This is because, for each
i
,

\Pr[p\cap E^{\prime}_{i}\neq\emptyset]\leq\sum_{e\in p}\Pr[e\in E^{\prime}_{i}]\leq\sum_{e\in p}\frac{w(e)}{d^{\prime}}\log n\leq\frac{w(p)}{d^{\prime}}\log n\leq 1/2.

So, there is
i
where
p\subseteq G\setminus E^{\prime}_{i}
with probability
1-1/2^{z}\geq 1-1/n^{10}.
Thus, every path in the unknown target set is covered with probability at least
1-1/n^{8}
by union bound.

Neighborhood Cover: Undirected Solution.

A
d
-clustering

consists of vertex-disjoint subsets called

, each of which has diameter at most

. For any vertex

, let
B_{v}(v,d)=\{w\mid\mathrm{dist}_{G}(v,w)\leq d\}
.

A

-neighborhood cover

of

is a collection of

many

-clustering

such that, for every vertex

,

for some cluster

in some clustering

. For any

, Awerbuch et al.
[

]
showed how to construct

Observe that

gives the solution to

. Indeed, for each

, let

. Consider any length-

Appendix C
A Barrier for Covering Paths via Subgraphs

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.2
.
See
1.2

Fix
m,\lambda\geq 1
. We will build a directed graph
G
with
O(m)
edges and a distance parameter
d
.

Step 1: choose parameters.

Let

L:=\bigl\lfloor\sqrt{m/\lambda}\bigr\rfloor\quad\text{and}\quad d:=2+3L.

So
L=\Theta(\sqrt{m/\lambda})
and
d=\Theta(\sqrt{m/\lambda})
.

We will also use one more parameter

R:=2d\lambda.

Note that
R>d\lambda
by construction.

Step 2: ladder gadget.

Create vertices
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{L+1}
. For each layer
j=1,\dots,L
do:

•

create
R
new vertices
w_{j,1},\dots,w_{j,R}
,

•

add the directed cycle

w_{j,1}\to w_{j,2}\to\cdots\to w_{j,R}\to w_{j,1},

•

add edges
a_{j}\to w_{j,r}
for every
r\in[R]
,

•

add edges
w_{j,r}\to a_{j+1}
for every
r\in[R]
.

Thus, to “go through” layer
j
you can do

a_{j}\to w_{j,r}\to w_{j,r+1}\to a_{j+1},

which is 3 edges.

The number of edges in one layer is

R\text{ (cycle)}+R\text{ (entries)}+R\text{ (exits)}=3R=3\cdot 2d\lambda=6d\lambda.

Since there are
L
layers, the total number of edges in the ladder is

6d\lambda\cdot L=6(2+3L)\lambda L=O(\lambda L^{2})=O(\lambda\cdot(m/\lambda))=O(m).

Key property of the ladder.

Let
H
be any subgraph of
G
whose SCCs all have diameter at most
d\lambda
. Consider a fixed layer
j
.
If
H
contained all
R
edges of the directed cycle of layer
j
, then that SCC would have directed diameter
R-1\geq d\lambda
, contradicting that
H
is
d\lambda
-clustered.
Hence: For every
d\lambda
-clustered subgraph
H
and every layer
j\in[L]
,
H
omits at least one edge of that layer’s cycle.

Step 3: star in front of the ladder.

Add a special vertex
r
(the center of the star) and add the edge
r\to a_{1}
.
Then add

M:=\bigl\lfloor m/20\bigr\rfloor

new vertices
s_{1},\dots,s_{M}
and add star edges

s_{t}\to r\quad\text{for }t=1,\dots,M.

This adds
M+1=O(m)
edges. Since the ladder already cost
O(m)
edges, the whole graph
G
has
O(m)
edges.

Step 4: the
d
-paths we care about.

Consider a path that:

1.

starts with some star edge
s_{t}\to r
,

2.

then takes
r\to a_{1}
,

3.

then for each layer
j=1,\dots,L
takes a 3-edge traversal

a_{j}\to w_{j,r_{j}}\to w_{j,r_{j}+1}\to a_{j+1}.

This path has length

1\;(\text{star})\;+\;1\;(\text{to }a_{1})\;+\;3L\;=\;2+3L\;=\;d.

So these are exactly
d
-paths in
G
.

Step 5: diagonalization for one star edge.

Let
\{G^{\prime}_{1},\dots,G^{\prime}_{z}\}
be any collection of
d\lambda
-clustered subgraphs of
G
that covers all
d
-paths.

Fix one star edge
e_{t}:=s_{t}\to r
.
Define

I_{t}:=\{\,i\in[z]:e_{t}\in E(G^{\prime}_{i})\,\}.

We claim that

|I_{t}|\;\geq\;L.

(2)

Suppose toward a contradiction that
|I_{t}|=q<L
.
For each
i\in I_{t}
, for every layer
j
it omits at least one cycle edge in that layer.
Since
q<L
, we can pick for every
i\in I_{t}
a
distinct
layer
\ell(i)\in[L]
and let
f_{i}
be one cycle edge in layer
\ell(i)
that
G^{\prime}_{i}
omits.

Now we build a
d
-path
P
as in Step 4, but with the following extra choice:
in layer
\ell(i)
we force the traversal to
use
exactly the edge
f_{i}
, and in all other layers we traverse arbitrarily.
This is a valid
d
-path because every layer allows us to pick any two consecutive cycle vertices.

By construction:

•

If
i\notin I_{t}
, then
G^{\prime}_{i}
does not contain the first edge
e_{t}
, so
P\nsubseteq G^{\prime}_{i}
.

•

If
i\in I_{t}
, then
G^{\prime}_{i}
does not contain
f_{i}
(by choice), and
P
uses
f_{i}
, so again
P\nsubseteq G^{\prime}_{i}
.

Thus
P
is a
d
-path that is not contained in any
G^{\prime}_{i}
, contradicting the assumption that the family covers all
d
-paths.
Therefore (
2
) holds.

Step 6: counting.

For each of the
M=\Theta(m)
star edges
e_{t}
we have found at least
L
subgraphs that contain it.
So the total number of
edge–subgraph incidences
is at least

\sum_{t=1}^{M}|I_{t}|\;\geq\;M\cdot L.

But

\sum_{i=1}^{z}|E(G^{\prime}_{i})|

counts all edge–subgraph incidences (each time an edge appears in some
G^{\prime}_{i}
it is counted once).
Hence

\sum_{i=1}^{z}|E(G^{\prime}_{i})|\;\geq\;M\cdot L.

Finally,
M=\Theta(m)
and
L=\Theta\bigl(\sqrt{m/\lambda}\bigr)
, so

\sum_{i=1}^{z}|E(G^{\prime}_{i})|\;\geq\;\Omega(m)\cdot\Omega\bigl(\sqrt{m/\lambda}\bigr)\;=\;\Omega\bigl(m\sqrt{m/\lambda}\bigr),

which is exactly the desired lower bound.

This completes the proof.
