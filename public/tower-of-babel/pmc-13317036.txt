Nature connection and adolescents’ pro-environmental behavior: an analysis based on chain mediation
Authors: Xueping Qie, Yali Tian, Yu Ma, Weifu Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the associations linking nature connection and pro-environmental behavior (PEB) in adolescents by constructing a chain mediation model, with a focus on the mediating effects of empathy with nature and moral identity. A cross-sectional questionnaire survey was administered to 379 adolescents aged 12—16 from three cities in Yunnan Province, China. The findings revealed that: (1) significant positive correlations were observed among adolescents' nature connection, empathy with nature, moral identity, and pro-environmental behaviors; (2) moral identity and empathy with nature are consistent with a chain mediating role between adolescents' nature connections and pro-environmental behaviors. This study suggests cognitive-emotional pathways that are statistically consistent with a link from nature connection to pro-environmental behavior among adolescents, providing a potential theoretical basis and practical guidance for fostering such behavior.

Introduction

As humanity’s material wealth grows, environmental challenges such as pollution, resource depletion, and soil erosion have become increasingly prominent. These environmental issues have significantly impacted human physiology, psychology, and behavior, posing a direct threat to human survival and development. The root cause of these problems lies in the relationship between humans and nature. Therefore, addressing these issues requires not only interventions in science, technology, economics, and law, but also the establishment of a correct and healthy perspective on nature to promote responsible environmental behavior. To this end, numerous scholars have introduced the concept of “pro-environmental behavior” and conducted extensive research. Pro-environmental behavior encompasses actions that reduce environmental harm and improve environmental quality [32], with the aim of protecting the environment or preventing its degradation [11, 31].

In both domestic and international research, pro-environmental behavior is often used interchangeably with concepts such as “environmental behavior” and “responsible environmental behavior.” These behaviors are crucial for reducing natural resource consumption, lowering pollutant emissions, and mitigating environmental degradation; thus, they serve as effective strategies for addressing environmental challenges and ensuring a sustainable environment. However, improving the ecological environment is not an overnight process; it requires the efforts of generations. Adolescents are in a stage of rapid physical and mental development and play a key role in this process. Cultivating and shaping their pro-environmental behaviors is not only of great significance for current environmental protection but also serves as a sustained driving force for future environmental conservation efforts.

The relationship between nature connection and pro-environmental behavior

Nature connection is defined as a deep emotional bond and sense of belonging between humans and the natural environment, indicating the extent to which individuals feel integrated with nature in terms of experience and emotion [24]. In their review of the effects of nature experiences on cognitive function and mental health, [8] proposed a classification system for nature experiences, distinguishing between incidental nature contact and deep nature connection. Incidental nature contact refers to an individual’s passive or brief exposure to natural environments (such as seeing greenery through a window), whereas deep nature connection manifests as an emotional bond and sense of identification between the individual and nature. Unlike incidental contact with nature, the nature connection examined in this study represents a deeper emotional and cognitive bond, manifested as a sense of integration with and identification with the natural environment [24]. This distinction is significant for understanding the relationship between nature and pro-environmental behavior: a deep nature connection may be a stronger predictor of behavior than incidental contact with nature.

Nature connection is a key predictor of pro-environmental behavior [3, 15, 23, 26, 36]. Empirical research indicates that nature connection is positively correlated with pro-environmental behavior among adolescents; that is, the higher the level of nature connection, the more frequently adolescents exhibit pro-environmental behavior [2, 15, 19, 27].

From the perspective of evolutionary psychology, the roots of nature connection can be traced back to human evolutionary history. According to the pro-life hypothesis [23], humans have developed an innate tendency to focus on and seek closeness with life over the course of long-term evolution; this tendency helps individuals identify safe and dangerous environments, acquire resources, and avoid threats. This evolutionary legacy constitutes the biological foundation of nature connection and helps explain why nature connection can predict pro-environmental behavior across cultures and age groups. Specifically, when individuals feel a connection to nature, they effectively activate this deep-seated evolutionary tendency, which in turn generates motivations and behaviors to protect nature.

Nature connection can be understood at both the emotional and cognitive levels. At the emotional level, emotional experiences with nature stimulate individuals’ sense of environmental responsibility [33]; at the cognitive level, frequent exposure to nature enhances individuals’ sensitivity to environmental changes [10]. Meta-analyses indicate that the strength of this relationship is moderated by cultural context: in collectivist cultures (such as China and Japan), the predictive power of nature connection on pro-environmental behavior is significantly stronger than in individualist cultures (such as the United States) [34].

Consequently, Hypothesis 1 posits that nature connection positively predicts pro-environmental behavior.

The relationship between nature connection, moral identity, and pro-environmental behaviors

Moral identity is defined as the self-concept that individuals construct and internalize around moral traits [1]. According to the theoretical framework proposed by [1], moral identity comprises two dimensions: internalization and symbolization. Internalization refers to the alignment of moral values with one’s self-concept, while symbolization refers to the expression of these values through behavior.

According to moral expansiveness theory, an individual’s moral concern can extend from close others to broader entities, including the natural environment and animals (Crimston et al., 2016). When individuals form a strong moral self-concept, this moral concern tends to expand outward, thereby incorporating environmental protection into the realm of moral obligations. Consequently, general moral identity can influence pro-environmental behavior through the mechanism of moral expansiveness.

According to Self-Consistency Theory, individuals with strong moral identity strive to maintain consistency between their behavior and their self-concept to reduce cognitive dissonance. When adolescents incorporate environmental protection into their self-identity, engaging in behaviors that violate ecological principles (such as resource waste) triggers psychological discomfort, which in turn promotes behavioral change [25]. Experimental studies indicate that adolescents who participate in moral self-reflection activities (such as writing environmental pledges) exhibit significantly higher frequencies of pro-environmental behavior than control groups [5].

Furthermore, nature connection may encourage adolescents to view environmental protection as an unshakable moral responsibility by reinforcing the moral perception of “harmony between humans and nature.” When adolescents integrate environmental protection into their self-identity, the positive influence of nature connection on pro-environmental behavior is mediated through the cognitive pathway of moral identity. At the same time, [16] noted that moral identity undergoes significant development during adolescence. Adolescence is a critical period during which individuals begin to develop abstract moral reasoning and internalize moral values into their self-concept; the development of moral identity during this stage has a lasting impact on subsequent moral behavior. Therefore, understanding how adolescents’ moral identity influences pro-environmental behavior holds unique theoretical value. Compared to adults, adolescents’ moral identity may be more malleable and more susceptible to educational interventions and environmental factors, providing an important window of opportunity for fostering pro-environmental behavior among adolescents.

Consequently, Hypothesis 2 is posited, suggesting that moral identity serves as a mediating variable between natural connectedness and adolescents' pro-environmental behaviors.

The relationship between nature connection, empathy with nature, and pro-environmental behavior

Natural empathy refers to an individual’s ability to emotionally perceive and understand the essence of the natural environment, particularly when that environment is under stress [33, 34]. Similar to human empathy, this form of empathy causes individuals to experience emotions such as sadness or anger when faced with environmental degradation (e.g., deforestation), thereby motivating conservation efforts. Neuroscience research provides physiological evidence for this phenomenon: compared to individuals with low levels of empathy with nature, those with high levels of empathy with nature exhibit significant activation in the anterior insula—associated with emotional empathy—and the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex—associated with cognitive regulation—when viewing images of ecological destruction [35].

Nature connection may enhance an individual’s empathy sensitivity by fostering a sense of psychological belonging to the natural environment. [20] were among the first to propose the concept of “emotional affinity for nature,” emphasizing that emotional connections serve as a crucial motivational foundation for environmental behavior. Through a questionnaire study involving 281 participants, they found that emotional affinity for nature, feelings of anger toward the destruction of nature, and interest in nature all significantly predicted conservation behavior, whereas rational cognition alone was insufficient to explain such behavior. This is highly relevant to the construct of “empathy with nature” in the present study, as both emphasize emotional experiences of nature rather than rational cognition. Kals et al.’s research provided early theoretical support for empathy with nature as a key predictor of pro-environmental behavior. [33] longitudinal study showed that adolescents with higher levels of nature connection exhibited a faster rate of increase in their empathy with nature scores one year later, and this increase partially mediated the rise in their pro-environmental behaviors.

According to the Affect-Behavior Linkage Hypothesis, nature-based empathy influences pro-environmental behavior through two mechanisms: first, by directly stimulating conservation motives (e.g., “I must stop behaviors that harm nature”), and second, indirectly through feelings of guilt or responsibility [21]. Experimental studies have shown that adolescents who watched documentaries about endangered species exhibited significantly higher levels of empathy with nature and participated more frequently in school waste sorting activities during the following week [12].

Empathy with nature is a crucial component of an individual’s connection to the natural environment and a key motivational factor in promoting pro-environmental behavior [37]. Research indicates that nature connection positively predicts empathy with nature [34], while empathy with nature, in turn, positively predicts pro-environmental behavior [4, 22, 28]. Nature-based empathy not only fosters intrinsic motivation to protect the environment but also translates into concrete environmental protection actions [4, 18, 29].

Consequently, this study posits Hypothesis 3, suggesting that empathy with nature may mediate the relationship between adolescents' connection to nature and their engagement in pro-environmental behaviors.

The relationship between nature connection, moral identity, empathy with nature, and pro-environmental behavior

According to the Cognitive-Affective Synergy Model (CASM), the connection to nature influences pro-environmental behavior through two pathways: the cognitive pathway (which strengthens moral identity and fosters the belief that “environmental protection is my responsibility”) and the affective pathway (which evokes empathy for nature and fosters the feeling that “nature’s suffering is my suffering”), with the cognitive pathway taking precedence over the affective pathway [14]. This sequence is particularly evident among adolescents, as their moral development is undergoing a transitional phase from “heteronomy” to “autonomy” [30].

Moral identity is a relatively stable cognitive structure representing an individual’s moral definition of “who I am.” Once formed, this self-definition continues to influence an individual’s emotional responses and behavioral tendencies. In contrast, empathy with nature is a situational emotional response that is more easily modulated by existing cognitive frameworks. Therefore, in the process by which a connection to nature influences pro-environmental behavior, moral identity serves as the preceding cognitive mediator, while empathy with nature acts as the subsequent emotional mediator, forming a sequential pathway of “cognition → emotion → behavior.”

When an individual develops the moral identity that “environmental protection is my responsibility,” this sense of responsibility enhances their emotional sensitivity to environmental destruction. In other words, moral identity provides the cognitive foundation for nature-based empathy—only when you perceive environmental protection as relevant to yourself will you generate a strong emotional response to environmental degradation. This aligns with [17] theory of empathy development: the development of empathy depends on self-awareness and the recognition of others (or other entities) as independent entities.

[9] research indicates that early exposure to nature during adolescence indirectly enhances natural empathy and the frequency of pro-environmental behaviors in adulthood by promoting the development of moral identity. Furthermore, [13] found that moral identity influences prosocial behavior tendencies through partial mediation by empathy. Although previous studies have examined the determinants of pro-environmental behavior from various perspectives, few have explored the sequential mediating roles of moral identity and empathy with nature in the relationship between nature connection and pro-environmental behavior..

Methods

Participants and procedures

Initially, to ensure the acquisition of an adequate sample, a priori power analyses were conducted using G*Power 3.1 before the commencement of the study. A multiple linear regression analysis was selected, with a predicted sample size of no less than 119 participants. This estimation was based on setting the effect size at a medium level (effect size f2 = 0.25), the statistical power (1-β) at 0.9, and the probability of a Type I error (α) at 0.05. The study focused on adolescents aged 12—16, corresponding to the secondary school stage (junior high school). A total of 420 questionnaires were distributed. The questionnaires adhered to voluntary participation, data confidentiality, and anonymous completion. Upon collection, invalid questionnaires were excluded, resulting in 379 valid responses, yielding an effective response rate of 90.24%.

Of the participants, 156 (41.2%) were male and 223 (58.8%) were female. The sample comprised 112 (29.6%) junior high school students, 110 (29.0%) high school students, and 157 (41.4%) senior high school students. Additionally, 99 (26.1%) were only children, while 280 (73.9%) had siblings. Furthermore, 117 (30.9%) resided in urban areas, whereas 262 (69.1%) were from non-urban areas.

Measures

Nature connection Scale

This study employed the Nature Connection Scale developed initially by [24] and subsequently revised by Chinese scholars. The scale comprises 14 items, including statements such as "I often feel at one with nature" and "My sense of well-being is not related to how good or bad nature is." Responses are recorded on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher aggregate scores indicating a stronger personal connection to nature. Items 4, 12, and 14 were reverse-scored. The internal consistency reliability of the Chinese version of the Nature Connection Scale was 0.783, the retest reliability was 0.901 (p < 0.01), and the compatibility validity was 0.491 (p < 0.01). The scale is reliable and valid for Chinese students. The Cronbach's alpha for the questionnaire in this study was 0.83, reflecting good internal consistency.

Moral Identity Scale

This study utilized the Chinese version of the Moral Identity Questionnaire developed by [1]. The questionnaire comprises two dimensions: the implicit dimension, which reflects the importance of moral traits in one’s self-concept—that is, the internal identification with moral traits; and the explicit dimension, which reflects the extent to which an individual wishes to demonstrate possession of moral traits in human–computer interactions. The questionnaire consists of 10 items scored on a 5-point scale, where “1” indicates “strongly disagree” and “5” indicates “strongly agree”; items 4 and 7 are reverse-scored. The questionnaire demonstrates good reliability and validity. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the questionnaire is 0.83, with a reliability of 0.77 for the explicit dimension and 0.71 for the implicit dimension. Reliability and validity analyses were conducted using M-plus, and the model fit was good: for the two-factor model, CFI = 0.908, TLI = 0.901, RMSEA = 0.070, and SRMR = 0.067.This indicates that our translated questionnaire has good structural validity. The composite validity coefficients for the two dimensions were 0.68 and 0.71, respectively. Additionally, the correlation coefficient between the two dimensions was r = 0.68, which met the requirements., the Cronbach's alpha for the scale was determined to be 0.81.

Empathy with Nature Scale

This study employed the Nature-Based Empathy Scale developed by [33] and revised by Chinese scholars. Tam’s questionnaire consists of 10 items scored on a 5-point scale, where “1” indicates “strongly disagree” and “7” indicates “strongly agree.” For example, “I imagine how I would feel if I were the suffering animal or plant,” and “I empathize with the feelings of suffering animals and plants.” The Chinese version of the scale contains ten items and also uses a 5-point rating scale. The questionnaire’s Cronbach’s alpha coefficient is 0.90, and the correlations between individual items and the total score are significant. In this study, the internal consistency coefficient of this scale was 0.832.

Pro-Environmental Behaviour Scale

This study employed a self-assessment scale of pro-environmental behaviors, revised by Chinese scholars. The scale comprises 12 items that prompt participants to reflect on their engagement in the specified behaviors over the past year, utilizing a 5-point Likert scale, where '1' denotes "never" and '5' signifies "often." Higher scores on the scale indicate greater levels of pro-environmental behavior exhibited by the individual. The internal consistency coefficient for the scale was 0.783, and for the two dimensions it was 0.681 and 0.768, respectively. The internal consistency coefficient for the scale in this study was found to be 0.820.

Data analysis

The study employed SPSS version 26.0 and Mplus for data analysis. Following data collection, common method bias was assessed using Harman's single-factor test and ULMC. Subsequently, after importing the data into SPSS, descriptive statistics and Pearson's correlation coefficient were applied to examine the relationships among natural associations, moral identity, empathy with nature, and pro-environmental behavior. The mediating effects of the variables were analyzed using the SPSS macro Process version 3.5. Finally, the robustness of the model was tested using Mplus. This methodology facilitated a comprehensive analysis of the study.

Common method bias test

Since all data collected in the study were self-reported by participants, there was a potential risk of common method bias. To address this concern, Harman's single-factor test was employed for factor analysis. The analysis identified ten factors with eigenvalues exceeding 1. Notably, the variance explained by the first principal component was 26.11%, below the threshold value of 40%. This result suggests that common method bias was not a significant issue among the variables measured in this study.

The common method variance (CMV) was re-examined using the ULMC method. First, a baseline four-factor model was established. Then, the common method variable (CMV) was added to the model, with all path coefficients from the CMV to the items set to be equal, thereby constructing a two-factor model. The model fit results are shown in Table X: the fit indices for the baseline model were CFI = 0.802, TLI = 0.792, RMSEA = 0.054, and SRMR = 0.059. The model fit indices of the two-factor model are identical to those of the baseline model (ΔCFI < 0.1, ΔRMSEA < 0.1), indicating that adding the common method variable did not improve model fit. This result suggests that there is no detectable common method bias in this study. It is unlikely that the correlations among all variables are driven by measurement method factors.

Robustness tests

To test the robustness of the chained mediation model, three competing models were compared: (1) the original chained model (connection to nature → moral identity → empathy for nature → pro-environmental behavior); (2) the reverse chained model (connection to nature → empathy for nature → moral identity → pro-environmental behavior); (3) Parallel mediation model (moral identity and empathy with nature acting in parallel). The results showed that the explanatory power (R2) of all three models for pro-environmental behavior was 0.47. Regarding chain mediation effects, the effect size for the original chain model was 0.042 (95% CI [0.022, 0.069]), while that for the reverse chain model was 0.029 (95% CI [0.013, 0.050]), with the former being significantly larger than the latter. The parallel mediation model could not test the sequential relationship between M1 and M2.

Based on the above robustness test results, the core conclusions of this study demonstrate good robustness.

Discussion

This study developed a sequential mediation model to examine the relationship between a connection to nature and pro-environmental behavior among adolescents, as well as the sequential mediating roles of moral identity and ecological empathy. It should be noted that, because this study employed a cross-sectional design, all findings should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than causal relationships. The main findings are discussed below.

The relationship between nature connection and pro-environmental behavior

Correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between nature connection and pro-environmental behavior (r = 0.594, p < 0.001). In the mediation model, after controlling for moral identity and empathy with nature, the direct effect of nature connection on pro-environmental behavior was β = 0.18 (p < 0.001). Although this effect size is significant, it is smaller than the total effect (β = 0.391), indicating that the mediating variables account for part of the association.

This finding is consistent with previous research. For example, [23] found that nature connection explains 15%–20% of the variance in pro-environmental behavior, and a meta-analysis by [36] also confirmed the robustness of this relationship. However, the effect size of the direct effect in this study (β = 0.18) was slightly lower than the level reported by [34] in collectivist cultures (β ≈ 0.35). This difference may stem from sample characteristics: the participants in this study were adolescents aged 12–16, whereas Tam et al.’s study primarily consisted of college students. Adolescents may still be in a critical period of nature-affinity development, and the direct impact of this affinity on behavior may not yet be fully evident.

From a theoretical perspective, nature-affinity may influence pro-environmental behavior through two mechanisms. At the emotional level, emotional experiences with nature (such as the sense of joy derived from being in nature) may stimulate a sense of environmental responsibility [33]; at the cognitive level, frequent exposure to nature may increase sensitivity to environmental changes [10]. However, because this study employed a cross-sectional design, it was unable to determine the direction of causality. Future research could adopt a longitudinal design to examine the bidirectional relationship between nature connection and pro-environmental behavior.

The mediating role of moral identity

Mediation analysis revealed that the indirect effect via moral identity was 0.085 (95% CI [0.042, 0.128]), accounting for 21.79% of the total indirect effect. The path coefficient from nature connection to moral identity was β = 0.30 (p < 0.001), and the path coefficient from moral identity to pro-environmental behavior was β = 0.28 (p < 0.001). Both paths were significant, supporting a mediation model.

This finding is consistent with self-consistency theory [25]: individuals with strong moral identity may be more likely to strive to maintain consistency between their behavior and their self-concept. When adolescents integrate environmental protection into their self-identity, engaging in behaviors that violate ecological principles may trigger psychological discomfort, which in turn could be associated with behavioral change.

It should be noted that this study measured general moral identity rather than environment-specific moral identity. According to the theory of moral extension [38], an individual's moral concern can extend from close others to broader entities, including the natural environment. Therefore, general moral identity may be associated with pro-environmental behavior through the mechanism of moral extension. This explanation offers a potential theoretical basis for understanding the relationship between general moral identity and domain-specific behavior.

An experimental study by [5] found that adolescents who engaged in moral self-reflection activities (such as writing environmental pledges) exhibited significantly higher frequencies of pro-environmental behavior than the control group. The findings of this study complement this experimental evidence from a correlational perspective, indicating that individual differences in moral identity are associated with nature connection and pro-environmental behavior. However, the effect size reported by Bolderdijk et al. (Cohen's d ≈ 0.60) is larger than the indirect effect in this study (0.085), which may be attributed to the stronger causal efficacy of experimental manipulation compared to naturally occurring differences in moral identity.

The cross-sectional design of this study does not allow for determining the temporal sequence of the mediating pathways. Although we hypothesize that nature connection is associated with moral identity, which in turn is associated with pro-environmental behavior, a reverse interpretation is also possible: adolescents with higher moral identity may be more inclined to form connections with nature. Future research should adopt a longitudinal design (such as a cross-lagged panel model) to test the directionality of this relationship.

The mediating role of empathy for nature

Mediation analysis revealed that the indirect effect mediated by empathy for nature was 0.088 (95% CI [0.047, 0.140]), accounting for 22.56% of the total indirect effect. The path coefficient from nature connection to empathy with nature was β = 0.25 (p < 0.001), and the path coefficient from empathy with nature to pro-environmental behavior was β = 0.35 (p < 0.001). Both paths were significant, supporting a mediation model.

This finding is consistent with the emotion-behavior linkage hypothesis [21]: nature-based empathy may be associated with pro-environmental behavior through two pathways—directly stimulating protective motivation (e.g., "I must stop behaviors that harm nature") and indirectly through feelings of guilt or responsibility.

Tam's [33] longitudinal study found that adolescents with higher levels of nature-based connection exhibited a faster rate of increase in nature-based empathy scores one year later (β = 0.28, p < 0.01). The path coefficient in this study (β = 0.25) is similar to Tam's longitudinal estimate. [12] found in an experimental study that adolescents who watched documentaries about endangered species exhibited significantly higher levels of empathy with nature (d = 0.52) and reported more frequent participation in waste sorting activities during the following week. The findings of this study complement this experimental evidence from the perspective of individual differences.

Neuroscience research provides physiological evidence for this mechanism: when viewing images of ecological destruction, individuals with high natural empathy exhibit significant activation in the anterior cingulate cortex (associated with emotional empathy) and the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (associated with cognitive regulation) [35]. This suggests that natural empathy is not merely an emotional experience but also involves cognitive regulatory processes. However, the self-report measurement method used in this study may be subject to social desirability bias; future research could supplement these findings with physiological measures (e.g., skin conductance response, fMRI)..

Chain mediation of moral identity and empathy for nature

The chain mediation analysis revealed that the indirect effect via the pathway "connection to nature → moral identity → empathy for nature → pro-environmental behavior" was 0.042 (95% CI [0.022, 0.069]), accounting for 10.77% of the total indirect effect. The confidence intervals for all three indirect paths (via moral identity, via empathy with nature, and via the sequential path) do not include zero, indicating that the mediation effects are statistically significant.

Among the three indirect paths, the effects via moral identity (0.085) and via empathy with nature (0.088) were comparable, while the sequential path was smaller (0.042). This distribution of effect sizes suggests that nature connection is associated with pro-environmental behavior through single mediating pathways, while the sequential pathway, though significant, makes a relatively smaller contribution. This implies that in practical interventions, targeting both moral identity and empathy with nature simultaneously may yield a greater cumulative effect than targeting a single variable alone.

This finding is consistent with the cognitive-affective synergy model [14]. Moral identity is a relatively stable cognitive structure representing an individual's moral definition of "who I am." Once formed, this self-definition may continuously influence an individual's emotional responses and behavioral tendencies. In contrast, empathy with nature is a situational emotional response that may be more easily moderated by existing cognitive frameworks. Therefore, in the process by which nature connection is associated with pro-environmental behavior, moral identity may serve as a preceding cognitive correlate, while empathy with nature may act as a subsequent emotional correlate, forming a sequential pathway of "cognition → emotion → behavior" [17].

Chawla's [9] longitudinal study indicates that early adolescent exposure to nature is indirectly associated with natural empathy and the frequency of pro-environmental behavior in adulthood through its association with the development of moral identity. The chained indirect effect in that study (β ≈ 0.08) is larger than the estimate in this study (β = 0.042), which may reflect long-term developmental effects captured by the longitudinal design. [13] found that moral identity partially mediates the influence of empathy on prosocial behavior tendencies (indirect effect proportion ≈ 15%), which is similar to the findings of this study (chain path accounting for 10.77%), indicating that the cognitive-to-affective sequential path exhibits cross-situational consistency across different domains.

However, the cross-sectional design of this study could not examine the long-term developmental processes identified by [9]. Future research could adopt a longitudinal tracking design (e.g., multi-wave measurements from junior high school to high school) to further verify the stability of the sequential mediation model over time.

Limitation

Common-method bias: Since all variables in this study were measured using self-reports from adolescents, common-method bias may be present. Although the ULMC test indicated that common-method bias was not severe (ΔCFI = 0, ΔRMSEA = 0), the influence of self-report bias cannot be completely ruled out. Specifically, social desirability bias may lead to overestimation of self-reported pro-environmental behaviors (Paulhus, 2002). Future research could utilize multi-source data (such as peer reports, teacher evaluations, and behavioral observations) to enhance the objectivity of the measurements.

Third-party variables: The findings of this study may be explained by unmeasured third-party variables. For example: Exposure to environmental education: Students who receive more environmental education may simultaneously exhibit higher levels of nature connection, moral identity, and pro-environmental behavior; Parental socialization: Parents’ pro-environmental behavior may influence their children’s pro-environmental attitudes and behaviors through modeling; School climate: Whether a school promotes eco-friendly behavior may influence all variables; General prosocial tendencies: Eco-friendly behavior may share variance with general prosocial tendencies. Future research should incorporate these variables into models to test the specificity of the findings in this study.

The findings of this study may be influenced by unmeasured sociocultural variables. [6] found that in-group norms moderate people’s perceptions of prosocial behavior toward out-groups. Similarly, adolescents’ pro-environmental behavior may be driven not only by internal traits but also by sociocultural factors such as peer norms and school climate. Furthermore, [7] noted that belonging motivation and identity strength jointly moderate individuals’ responses to social inclusion. This suggests that the influence of nature connection on pro-environmental behavior may be moderated by a sense of belonging to the school or the degree of peer acceptance. Future research should incorporate variables such as social norms, school atmosphere, and a sense of belonging into models to test the contextual dependence of the findings in this study.

Sample limitations: The sample for this study consisted of 379 adolescents (aged 12–16) from three cities in Yunnan Province, China, limiting the generalizability of the results. Specifically:

Yunnan Province is one of China’s most biodiverse regions, and local adolescents may have greater opportunities for contact with nature; the urban sample may not be representative of adolescents in rural or remote areas; the age range (12–16 years) cannot be generalized to younger or older adolescents. Future research should validate these findings across broader regions and different age groups.

Cross-sectional design: As noted earlier, the cross-sectional design of this study cannot establish causality. Mediational analysis in cross-sectional data can only demonstrate that the relationships among variables are statistically consistent with the mediational model; it cannot prove temporal sequence or the direction of causality. For example, although we hypothesized that moral identity influences empathy with nature, a reverse interpretation is also possible: strong emotional resonance with nature may prompt individuals to form a stronger moral identity. Future research could adopt a longitudinal design (such as a cross-lagged model) or an experimental design to examine the dynamic relationships among variables.

Conclusion

Based on cross-sectional data, this study found that a positive correlation exists between nature connection and pro-environmental behavior among adolescents (r = 0.59). The findings are consistent with a chain mediation model in which moral identity and empathy with nature serve as sequential mediators. Among the three pathways, the indirect effects consistent with mediation via moral identity (0.085, accounting for 21.79%) and via empathy with nature (0.088, accounting for 22.56%) were comparable, while the sequential path was smaller (0.042, accounting for 10.77%). The potential theoretical contribution lies in integrating the cognitive-affective synergy model and distinguishing between general and domain-specific moral identity. In practical terms, based on these correlational findings, it may be recommended to cultivate nature connection through nature-based experiential activities, strengthen moral identity through moral reflection, and enhance empathy with nature through emotional education. However, as this study employs only a cross-sectional design, it cannot establish causality; future longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to validate these findings.
