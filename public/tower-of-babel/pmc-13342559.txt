Benefits of classroom remote microphone technology for inattentive children
Authors: Lucy Shiels, Dani Tomlin, Jonathan M. Payne, Gary Rance
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effectiveness of remote microphone technology (RMT) for children with inattention and associated listening difficulties. A two-phase trial was conducted. Phase one was a 4-week randomized controlled crossover trial (n = 35), assessing listening and attention in noise with and without RMT. Phase two was a 30-week extension trial following participants continuing with RMT (n = 20) and controls (n = 17). Auditory processing, attention, memory and reading fluency were assessed before and after the extension. Questionnaires were administered throughout to monitor progress. In phase one, RMT use significantly improved speech intelligibility, listening comprehension, and auditory attention (P < 0.05). Participants reported improved classroom listening and ADHD symptoms (P < 0.05). In phase two, the experimental group showed greater improvement in reading fluency and self-reported quality of life (P < 0.01) compared to controls, with no significant differences in auditory processing or cognitive skills. These findings support RMT as an effective intervention for inattentive children with listening difficulties in educational settings. Introduction

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is the most common neurodevelopmental diagnosis, affecting an estimated 7.6% of children globally1. It is characterized by hyperactivity, impulsivity and/or inattention that impacts everyday functioning across multiple settings2. In primary school-aged students, ADHD has been associated with academic underachievement3, increased support requirements3 and difficulties following teacher instruction4. The ability to comprehend verbal information is critical for learning, as 45–60% of a standard school day involves active listening5.

Despite the critical role of listening in education, classroom acoustic design is often overlooked. Recommended signal-to-noise ratios (the difference in volume of target speech and background noise) of at least 15 dB6 are rarely achieved, with actual learning environments ranging from −7 to +5 dB7. Increased classroom noise has been associated with poorer scholarly outcomes, as well as reduced attention and memory capacity8–10. These effects, although pronounced in all students, are amplified in children with listening difficulties (LiD). LiD describes any individual who has challenges discriminating a target speaker amidst background noise11. Dillon and Cameron11 summarized possible causes of LiD as impaired hearing, speech and language, cognition and/or auditory processing, though etiology often remains unknown.

Children with ADHD exhibit challenges across multiple domains that can contribute to LiD. Reduced executive functioning, including attention and working memory, can limit the ability to focus on, retain and retrieve spoken information12. Compared to neurotypical peers, children with ADHD have poorer performance on speech recognition in noise and listening comprehension tasks, which may reflect differences in auditory processing13,14. Additionally, the high cooccurrence of ADHD with speech and language disorders, estimated to range from 2 to 22%, suggests impaired communication may exacerbate LiD in some cases15. While LiD frequently occurs in children with ADHD, listening-related outcomes are rarely considered when evaluating the effectiveness of classroom-based interventions for this population.

Recommendations for supporting ADHD consistently indicate a multifaceted approach, with multimodal regimens showing greater benefits than pharmacological or non-pharmacological alone16. A review of non-pharmacological supports for ADHD highlighted the strongest evidence base for behavioral interventions17. However, even these approaches often have modest effects on classroom attention and learning outcomes, and few interventions directly target the listening challenges that frequently underlie functional difficulties in this group18. This gap underscores the need to explore alternative strategies, such as remote microphone technology (RMT), which may address listening more directly.

RMT is designed to enhance speech understanding in challenging acoustic environments. These devices comprise a microphone worn by the teacher, which wirelessly transmits their voice directly to ear-level receivers worn by the student, effectively replicating a scenario where the teacher’s voice is just 20 cm from the student’s ear, regardless of actual distance. Non-randomized trials on other LiD populations have demonstrated assistive benefits for listening, attention and classroom behavior19–21. While some studies have included participants with ADHD (28–100%)22–28, none have assessed changes in ADHD symptom severity during RMT use, and the broader, long-term effects of RMT on unaided listening abilities in children with LiD remains unclear21,23,28.

This study presents a two-phase trial of RMT for inattentive children. Phase one, a 4-week randomized-controlled trial, investigated potential assistive benefits of RMT during classroom use. We expected that RMT use in noise would improve performance in speech recognition, listening comprehension, and attention tasks. Additionally, we anticipated that teachers and students would report enhanced student attention and listening when using the devices. Phase two, a 30-week extension trial, examined changes in auditory, cognitive and academic development. We hypothesized that, compared to controls who did not use RMT, longer-term device use would improve academic progress and enhance classroom behavior.

Discussion

The success of non-pharmacological educational interventions for ADHD often hinges on the time investment for educators, and the extent to which these adjustments impact the learning environment for other students17. Educators of children with behavioral difficulties report high rates of burnout due to the increased load from modified teaching practice29. RMT offers unique advantages as an intervention in that it requires only the teacher and student to wear devices, avoiding disruption to other students’ learning and eliminating the need for modifications to communication or teaching approach. The present study identified assistive and long-term advantages for inattentive students using this technology in class.

Results of the phase one RCT demonstrated RMT effectiveness in addressing multiple core ADHD symptoms. When using the technology in listening conditions replicating a noisy classroom (0 dB signal-to-noise ratio), participants demonstrated increased auditory attention and sustained attention, while visual attention skills remained unaffected. These findings align with previous research on a broader cohort of children with LiD, where device assistance improved auditory attention and sustained attention scores from below average to match those of typically developing peers20. The present results additionally revealed enhanced auditory response control with the device activated, indicating improved impulsivity management. Use of RMT may therefore provide advantages to other primary traits of ADHD, not attention alone. Supporting these behavioral outcomes, teachers observed a reduction in inattentiveness and hyperactivity during RMT use periods in the phase one trial. Parents also noticed less hyperactivity, despite the trial being school-based. This effect may reflect improved classroom focus, reducing cognitive load during school hours, which in turn supports better self-regulation at home30. Further research is needed to systematically explore these possible secondary benefits.

Speech reception and listening comprehension also improved when participants were utilizing the devices in noise. Moreover, both students and educators reported decreased classroom listening difficulties during the initial four-week trial. While previous findings support enhanced access to verbal instructions using RMT19,20,26,27,31–33, fewer have explored the translation of this to understanding24,34. This is critical for students with ADHD, who exhibit difficulties distinguishing key information, inferencing, understanding causal relationships and focusing on spoken language35–37. RMT may thereby reduce the academic accommodations frequently implemented by teachers for these children, including task, instructional, and environmental adjustments38.

Although the devices benefited listening and attention when activated, long-term classroom use did not lead to improvements in skills associated with LiD, including auditory processing, memory and attention. It is noteworthy that the current sample showed mean baseline abilities within the normal range for most skills, with attention being the exception (see Table 5). This initial performance level may have limited the potential for observable improvements over time. Nonetheless, increasing access to auditory information does not appear to improve listening or cognitive skills above the typical developmental trajectory, aligning with findings from recent longer-term trials on auditory processing disorder populations21,23. One previous study reported improvements in auditory processing (frequency discrimination and side-order abilities) and cortical auditory evoked potentials after a 1-year trial in children with suspected auditory processing disorder (n = 10, 80% ADHD)28. While the same changes were not observed in a control group (n = 10, 100% ADHD), the absence of between-group comparisons limits the conclusion that these changes were device-related rather than developmental. Furthermore, the measures used, particularly evoked potentials, are more proximal to the direct effects of enhanced stimuli access compared to the broader cognitive and language-based outcomes While no device-related improvements were observed in listening skills, the experimental group demonstrated accelerated reading fluency over time. Reading fluency is a strong predictor of general academic progress in children39,40, and is a common challenge in ADHD41. Normative data for primary school-aged children suggest increases of approximately 7–21 words read fluently across three school terms, with larger gains expected for younger cohorts42. Control group performance aligned with the lower end of this range, demonstrating a mean increase of 10.05 words over 30 school weeks, despite a mean age of 9.41 years (equivalent to grade 4). In comparison, the experimental group surpassed this range, exhibiting a mean change of 26.59 words by trial conclusion. RMT may therefore accelerate literacy skill development in inattentive students, helping to reduce the achievement gap with their typically developing peers. This is the first study to see this change in inattentive students, but a previous study on Dyslexic children has demonstrated similar results25. Hornickel et al. 25 showed that RMT use reduced inconsistencies in neural responses to sound, and this was associated with increases in reading and phonological awareness. Combined, these findings indicate RMT as an appropriate classroom support for children with LiD whose academic progress is at risk. Future studies should consider the effects of device use on other scholarly areas also known to be affected by inattention, including spelling and numeracy43.

Children using RMT during the extension reported greater improvements in self-perceived quality of life. By enhancing access to instructions and potentially facilitating academic achievement, RMT may increase student confidence and self-worth. Although the phase one trial demonstrated teacher and student-reported improvements in listening and attention, no further measurable changes were observed in these questionnaire outcomes during the phase two extension. This pattern aligns with the behavioral findings, suggesting that the primary effect on listening and attention skills is immediate rather than cumulative, which has positive long-term consequences for educational attainment and well-being.

Despite its strengths, the current study had some limitations. The small sample of 38 students across two trial phases constrained analyses, particularly in controlling for medication use in ADHD symptom management. Although the phase one sample of 35 did not meet the 50 suggested by a power analysis using a similar study design20, significant differences in the primary outcome of auditory attention were observed, indicating sufficient statistical power. The phase one listening comprehension task used identical materials at each visit, likely resulting in the observed order effect. However, the significant device effect persisted despite randomization of RMT conditions across sessions, suggesting this order effect did not substantially confound the findings. Future research should employ comprehension measures with multiple task lists to prevent recall effects. While baseline characteristics were similar between the experimental and control groups in phase two (see Supplementary Table S1), the non-randomized nature of this extension trial and potential for self-selection bias should be carefully considered when interpreting the findings. A further limitation across both trial phases was the conversion of invalid IVA-QS scores to 0, which may have influenced the distribution of attention findings. However, this approach preserved the clinical meaning of severe presentations while maintaining a maximal sample size. Finally, the lack of data logging in the trialed devices necessitated reliance on teacher estimates of use to monitor compliance. Estimated device use during the phase one trial was the only factor that influenced continuation in the phase two extension, with more hours indicating a higher likelihood of continuing with RMT. Though not documented, lower usage cases might have been due to earpiece comfort, sound quality concerns, or equipment perception by peers, users and educators. Additional research is required to better understand the greatest barriers to device uptake to help guide clinical decisions and improve the design of next-generation devices.

For children exhibiting inattention and associated LiD, RMT use enhances focus and access to verbal instructions during class, yielding long-term positive consequences for academic progress and student quality of life. This trial provides robust evidence for health practitioners working with children who have ADHD to consider RMT as an appropriate non-pharmacological support in educational settings. When LiD is a concern for children with inattention and/or hyperactivity, referral to audiologists for RMT fitting should be considered.

Methods

This research was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of The University of Melbourne (Ethics ID: 23322) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Australian National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research. Researchers obtained informed consent from all participant caregivers and educators prior to study enrollment.

Trial design

This clinical trial consisted of two phases. Phase one was a four-week randomized controlled trial with a two-period crossover design. Participants were randomized using the sealed envelope method44 into one of two sequences: (A) no RMT use for two weeks followed by classroom RMT use for two weeks, or (B) classroom RMT use for two weeks followed by no RMT use for two weeks. Phase two was a 30-school week open-label extension where the phase one participants opted to either continue using RMT in class (experimental group) or cease RMT use (control group). For phase two, additional inattentive children were recruited into the control group who met eligibility criteria but were not interested in trying RMT (see Fig. 1 CONSORT Chart).Fig. 1Consolidated standards of reporting trials chart.RMT remote microphone technology, NVIQ non-verbal intelligence.

Participants

Children were screened for eligibility during their clinical auditory processing assessment at the University of Melbourne Hearing Care Clinic. To ensure a comprehensive assessment of the different possible underlying mechanisms of LiD11, the screening included measures of hearing and middle ear status, auditory processing, attention, memory and non-verbal intelligence. A description of each eligibility screening outcome, task requirements and test conditions is detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

Of 135 children screened for eligibility, 35 were randomized (see Fig. 1 CONSORT Chart). Eligibility criteria were as follows: (1) aged 6–12 years at enrollment, (2) normal hearing and middle ear status bilaterally, (3) parent and/or teacher-reported LiD in noisy environments, (4) evidence of attention deficit, defined as either an ADHD diagnosis (65.8%) or a full scale attention score of <70 on the Integrated Visual and Auditory Quick Screen Continuous Performance Task (IVA-QS)45 completed during screening (34.2%), (5) spoken English as primary communication mode and (6) non-verbal intelligence quotient of >70 as measured by the Test of Non-Verbal Intelligence, fourth edition (TONI-4)46 completed during screening.

Of the 35 phase one participants, 34 continued with the phase two extension, with one withdrawing due to relocation. A further three participants joined phase two only as additional controls (total phase two n = 37). Whilst these new participants had no prior RMT experience, skill development with RMT has only been observed in previous trials following at least 12 weeks of device use25,28,47. Given that phase one participants received only two weeks of device exposure, it was reasonable to assume that this brief experience would not result in sustained skill development or compromise baseline comparability between the new and existing participants in phase two.

Demographic data were collected from caregivers using a history form administered at study enrollment. To measure socioeconomic status, an Index of Relative Socio-Economic Advantage and Disadvantage (IRSAD)48 score was calculated for each participant using their residential postcode. IRSAD scores are standardized (mean = 1000, SD = 100), and higher scores indicate greater socioeconomic advantage. See Table 5 for participant demographics and baseline assessment data for each group.

Intervention

At the first appointment, phase one participants were fit bilaterally with Phonak Roger Focus II-312 receivers wirelessly connected to a Phonak Roger Touchscreen Microphone transmitter. Roger Focus II-312 receivers are designed for normal-hearing wearers. Unlike hearing aids, which amplify environmental sound via ear-level microphones and involve complex signal processing, these receivers exclusively obtain wireless signals from the Roger Touchscreen Transmitter at safe, comfortable and adjustable (−8 to +8 dB) volumes. The receivers were fit to each participant using appropriately-sized slim tubes and open domes to minimize ear canal occlusion49. Receiver volume was checked in quiet and simulated classroom noise, with the audiologist speaking into the transmitter at a conversational level. Receiver volume was adjusted in 2 dB increments until the participant indicated optimal comfort on a 5-point comfort scale. Volume was standardized to 0 dB for the simulated classroom assessments and set to the individualized comfort level for classroom-based trial periods.

Children and caregivers received instructions on the designated period for RMT use (based on their randomized sequence), and device maintenance including troubleshooting, power management, cleaning and insertion. This information, as well as guidance on classroom use, was emailed to the primary educator prior to trial commencement. Devices were taken home at the end of each school day, with caregivers responsible for overnight charging. Caregivers were trained to conduct daily listening checks each morning to minimize troubleshooting requirements for educators. Throughout device use periods, educators reported device usage via a digital Likert scale, estimating average daily hours at the end of phase one and tri-weekly during phase two.

Phase one RCT procedure and outcomes

Of the 35 participants, 18 were randomized to use RMT in their classroom for the first 2-week period and 17 in the second 2-week period. Questionnaires were administered via email to children, parents and educators at baseline and at the end of each trial period. The LIFE-R student and teacher versions50 were used to monitor classroom listening abilities, and the Conners parent and teacher forms51 tracked ADHD behaviors. See Supplementary Table S3 for questionnaire descriptions.

Behavioral testing to assess the assistive effect of RMT was completed before and after the initial four-week trial in a simulated noisy classroom at the University of Melbourne, Department of Audiology and Speech Pathology. The acoustic characteristics of this room were representative of typical classroom environments with an unoccupied noise level of 42dBA and an unoccupied reverberation time of 0.78 s. Outcomes included attention (IVA-QS)45, speech recognition (AZ-Bio Sentences)52 and listening comprehension (Test of Auditory Processing Skills Processing Oral Directions sub-test)53. See Supplementary Table S2 for task descriptions. The IVA-QS comprises eight subscales: full-scale attention, auditory attention, visual attention, auditory sustained attention, visual sustained attention, full-scale response control, auditory response control, and visual response control. The attention scales measure vigilance, focus, and processing speed. The sustained attention scales assess the ability to respond accurately and reliably whilst maintaining attention and adapting to changing task demands. The response control scales measure prudence, consistency, and stamina, providing an indication of impulsivity during task performance.

For each assessment, four-talker babble was played from four corner speakers, and the target stimulus from a front speaker situated 5 meters from the participant. The target signal and noise levels were calibrated at the participant's head position to each be 50 dBA, creating a 0 dB signal-to-noise ratio, which is representative of typical classroom noise levels7. The RMT transmitter was positioned 20 cm under the front speaker to replicate the recommended distance from the educator’s mouth54. At each visit, transmitter operation (on or off) was randomized44. RMT-aided testing was completed by 18 participants at visit one and 17 participants at visit two.

Phase two extension procedures and outcomes

At the conclusion of phase one, 20 of the 34 continuing participants opted to keep using RMT for an additional 30 school weeks (experimental group), whilst 14 opted to discontinue device use (control group). The additional three participants who declined phase one participation were recruited into the control group, resulting in a total phase two control n of 17.

At phase two baseline, weeks 15 and 30, caregivers completed review histories to track changes in diagnoses, medication, and therapy. Phase one questionnaires were readministered to assess ADHD symptoms and classroom listening. Additionally, the Behavior Assessment System of Children, third edition55 anxiety subscale (parent/teacher), Pediatric Quality of Life Generic Scales56 (parent/child), and Evaluation of Children’s Listening and Processing Skills57 (parent) were used to evaluate anxiety, quality of life, and LiD, respectively (Supplementary Table S3). These were also administered at baseline, week 15 and week 30.

Behavioral assessments were conducted at phase two baseline and end-of-trial in a soundproof booth at the University of Melbourne, Department of Audiology and Speech Pathology. Normal hearing and middle ear status were confirmed at the beginning of each appointment using Pure Tone Audiometry and Tympanometry, respectively. Auditory processing outcomes, assessed according to the recommended domains by the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association58, included binaural integration (Dichotic Digits Difference Test, Dichotic Free Recall)59, speech recognition in noise (Listening in Spatialized Noise—Sentences Test, High Cue)60 and temporal sequencing (Frequency Pattern Test)61. Cognitive outcomes included attention (IVA-QS)45 and auditory memory (Test of Auditory Processing Skills, Processing Numbers Forwards and Reverse)53. Reading fluency (Wheldall Assessment of Reading Passages; WARP)42 was also assessed as a strong predictor of general academic progress39. See Supplementary Table S2 for testing conditions and task requirements per measure.

Power analysis

A priori power analysis using attention outcome data from a previous study with similar methodology to phase one20 indicated that a sample size of 50 would provide 90% power (α = 0.005, two-tailed) for this primary outcome. Accounting for 10% anticipated dropout, 55 participants were targeted for recruitment. However, the samples achieved were 35 (phase one) and 37 (phase two) participants due to recruitment timeline constraints.

Statistical methods

Data were analyzed by the first author using Minitab 22.1.0 software. Phase one behavioral outcomes were analyzed using mixed effects models with device condition (RMT on/off) and condition order (first/second appointment) as fixed variables and participant as a random variable. Questionnaire data were analyzed using mixed effects models with device condition (RMT/no RMT) and timepoint (week 2/4) as fixed variables and participant as a random variable.

Phase two assessment and questionnaire results were analyzed using mixed effects models with time (baseline/week 15/week 30), group (control/experimental), and the interaction between time and group as fixed variables, and participant as a random variable.

All mixed effects analyses were followed up with post-hoc Fisher Pairwise comparisons. Invalid IVA-QS subscale scores, resulting from excessive idiopathic errors, were converted to the lowest possible numerical score of 0 for statistical analyses (see Supplementary Table S4). This approach aligns with the interpretation that such errors indicate extreme attentional and/or response control problems45.

Model assumptions were assessed through Ryan-Joiner tests for normality of residuals, Levene’s tests for homogeneity of variance, and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. Assumption checks for all outcomes are presented in Supplementary Table S5.
