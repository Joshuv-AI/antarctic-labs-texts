Effects of attentional focus and cognitive loads on cognitive-motor dual-task performance
Authors: Danxuan Zhang, Song Yang, Yaxin Wang, Bin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPerforming a cognitive task while maintaining postural control (i.e., cognitive–motor dual-tasking) requires strategic allocation of attentional resources to optimize performance and stability. However, it remains unclear how such attentional strategies modulate dual-task postural control under varying cognitive loads. This study investigated the effects of attentional focus on cognitive-motor dual-task performance across different load levels.MethodsThis study employed a within-subjects factorial design. Fourteen healthy young adults performed a cognitive-motor dual-task involving maintaining a tandem stance while completing a change-detection task requiring detection of orientation changes in visual bars under different load conditions. Participants were instructed to focus on their posture, the cognitive task, or both. Postural performance was measured using sway velocity and 95% prediction ellipse area; cognitive performance was assessed by accuracy and response time.ResultsThe results revealed a significant interaction between cognitive load and attentional focus for accuracy, but not for postural sway or response time. In the high load conditions, lower accuracy was found in the equal-focus condition compared to the cognitive-focus condition. No such differences were observed in the low load conditions.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that the conscious allocation of attention to a visual spatial cognitive working memory task modulates dual‑task performance when the task is difficult (high load) but not when it is easy (low load). This finding highlights the potential role of attentional prioritization strategies in cognitive-motor dual-task performance trade-offs.

Introduction

Postural control is rarely performed without concurrent activities in everyday situations [7, 24]. For instance, people may read news while keeping their balance on a bus. This kind of postural control task that is accompanied by cognitive tasks is referred to as cognitive-motor dual-task. Nowadays, postural control is considered an active control process that involves attentional resources to maintain the body's stability [4, 31]. Critically, human information processing capacity is fundamentally limited. If attentional demands exceed available resources during dual-task performance, the tasks share attentional resources, giving rise to dual-task effects such as interference, trade-offs, or facilitation [1]. This sharing underscores the importance of strategically allocating limited resources [18]. The present study examines how strategies for allocating limited resources modulate cognitive-motor dual-task performance, defined as postural and cognitive performance under concurrent task conditions.

A number of studies on dual-task effects have tended to focus on spatial processing. The cognitive loads of spatial working memory can be manipulated by varying the quantity of spatial information to be maintained. Some early research compared dual-task performance between spatial (e.g., location and orientation) and non-spatial (e.g., letter and shape) processing tasks with various cognitive loads and suggested that in the low-cognitive load (e.g., remembering 1 stimulus), cognitive-motor interference between spatial and non-spatial processing tasks is similar. In contrast, in the high cognitive load (e.g., remembering 2 or 3 stimuli), there was more cognitive-motor interference in spatial tasks than in non-spatial tasks, with decreasing postural stability [4, 31] and increasing response time (RT) [28]. The results provided preliminary evidence that the cognitive-motor interference between postural control and spatial processing may be affected by cognitive load.

More recently, researchers have directly manipulated cognitive load within individual studies to examine its effects on cognitive-motor dual-task performance. For instance, Riley et al. [25] found an increase in anteroposterior (AP) postural sway under high cognitive load during dual-tasking compared to single-task control; no such effect was observed under low load. This finding is consistent with Zhang et al. [33] who demonstrated that increased cognitive load interferes with postural stability. Thus, an increased cognitive load would interfere with postural stability.

The interference between postural stability and spatial processing under increased cognitive load is commonly attributed to competition for a shared, resource-limited pool [4, 20, 30]. The adaptive resource sharing model proposes that individual tasks are integrated into a single task representation through cost–benefit analysis. This conceptual framework requires a strategy for allocating limited resources between two tasks [17, 18]. To empirically investigate attention allocation mechanisms in cognitive-motor dual-tasks, researchers experimentally manipulate attentional focus (i.e. prioritizing postural or cognitive tasks). For example, Jehu et al. [8] demonstrated that prioritizing postural control reduces postural stability compared to prioritizing a choice reaction time task, whereas posture-focused conditions show no significant stability difference from simple reaction time task conditions. These findings illustrate that dual-task performance is not fixed but can be modulated by how attention is strategically directed. However, very little is known about competition for a limited pool of resources under increased cognitive load. Based on the evidence reviewed above, we can infer that the increased cognitive loads require more resources for the storage and maintenance of spatial items, which may compete for resources with postural control and thereby result in dual-task effects.

The present study sought to manipulate spatial cognitive load and attentional focus to examine how resources are allocated during cognitive-motor dual-task under different cognitive loads. According to the aforementioned evidence, we hypothesize that under high cognitive load, prioritizing a cognitive task increases postural stability compared to prioritizing a postural control task or equal prioritization.

Methods

Participants

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Zhejiang Normal University and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study employed a within-subjects factorial design, which was a 2 (cognitive load: low vs. high) × 3 (attentional focus: equal-focus, cognitive-focus, postural-focus). To determine the required sample size, we conducted a simulated power analysis using the SuperPower Shiny app [12]. We estimated effect size based on previous empirical evidence [3], with alpha error probability set at 0.05 and correlation between repeated measures assumed to be r = 0.50. We assumed that under high cognitive load, postural sway would be reduced in the cognitive-focus condition (M = 1.96, SD = 0.49) compared to the postural-focus condition (M = 1.94, SD = 0.45) and equal-focus condition (M = 1.79, SD = 0.47). Under low cognitive load, we expected no significant differences in postural sway among the cognitive-focus (M = 1.82, SD = 0.47), postural-focus (M = 1.82, SD = 0.47), and equal-focus (M = 1.75, SD = 0.48) conditions. To satisfy counterbalancing requirements, we tested the power of N = 14 participants. Using 2000 simulations, results showed that a power of 84% to detect an interaction effect. 15 healthy young adults (8 females) with normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no known metabolic, cardiovascular, neuromuscular, or musculoskeletal dysfunctions or injuries were recruited in this study. Due to excessively low correct responses (< 70%) in the cognitive tasks, the data from one male participant was excluded from the analysis. Experimental procedure

All measurements were completed in a quiet room free of distractions. One day before the testing visit, all participants were thoroughly informed about the data collection procedure and signed a written consent form. To familiarize themselves with the spatial working memory tasks, all participants completed practice trials (40 trials per task) while sitting. After the practice, participants were asked whether they were confident in completing the task. If not, they were given an additional 40 trials until they subjectively reported being familiar with the task. In addition to this subjective measure, task accuracy was used to objectively assess task familiarity. When participants reported confidence in task completion, their accuracy was above 80%, which strongly indicates that they had attained adequate familiarity. At the end of all practice, all participants were asked to refrain from vigorous physical activity for at least 24 h and from caffeine and alcohol for at least 12 h prior to the testing visit.

During the test day, due to the cool temperatures during the winter data collection period, all participants began the testing session with a 5-min stretching warm-up of lower back, hips, knees, and ankles. Subsequently, participants were randomly assigned to perform single tasks and equal-focus dual-tasks (5 blocks of 10 trials per task, detailed below) [16]. The single tasks comprised two single-cognitive tasks (3 blocks of 10 trials per task) and a single-postural task (3 blocks) [16], with details explained below. The order of the two loads was randomized and counter balanced. That is, the half of the participants first completed the cognitive task of low cognitive load followed by the high cognitive load, and vice versa. Considering that the cognitive-focus and postural-focus tasks may offer potential instructions for prioritizing resources in the equal-focus task, all participants were asked to undertake the equal-focus dual-tasks first to capture the natural dual-task tendencies. Then, participants were randomly assigned to perform cognitive-focus and postural-focus dual-tasks (5 blocks of 10 trials per task). After each block, participants reported the estimated percentage of attention they paid to the primary task. If the reported value was below 50%, the experimenter reiterated the instructions and the block was repeated to ensure adequate adherence to the attentional-focus instructions. For each participant, the order of the two loads was the same as that of the single-cognitive tasks. 60-s rest intervals were given between blocks to mitigate the influence of fatigue.

Single-cognitive task

The single-cognitive task is the change detection task, which is frequently used to assess spatial working memory [5]. Participants sat on a chair and performed the tasks using the E-prime 3.0 software (Psychology Software Tools, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). The tasks were presented on a screen at eye level, 1 m away. All stimuli were presented within a visual angle of 9 degrees to reduce interference with postural stability from head rotations [6, 26].

Each trial began with a fixation cross (500 ms), followed by a directional arrow cue (200 ms) indicating the side to which participants should attend. Subsequently, bilateral bar arrays were presented for 500 ms, and participants were instructed to memorize only the bars appearing on the cued side while ignoring bars on the opposite side. After a 2500 ms delay, participants were required to detect whether any change in bar orientation had occurred by pressing the left (changed) or right mouse button (not changed). When an orientation change occurred, the difference between the memory and the probe stimulus was 45°. The trial sequence, as shown in Fig. 1, was consistent with a previous study [33]. Single‑cognitive task comprised two cognitive loads (low and high). For each load, participants performed three blocks of 10 trials each (67 s/block). Single-postural task

Participants stood barefoot on a force plate (9281CA, Kistler, Winterthur, Switzerland) in a tandem stance. Tandem stance was selected because it reduces the base of support in the mediolateral direction and therefore poses a greater challenge for postural control compared with normal stance, particularly in healthy young adults. This stance has been shown to be sensitive to cognitive–motor interference under increased cognitive load [33]. The toes of their non-dominant foot touch the heel of their dominant foot while maintaining their center of pressure between both feet. They stood with their arms at their sides and held a computer mouse in their preferred hand while looking at a screen. Participants were instructed to stand as still as possible and respond to a black bar stimulus by pressing the mouse’s left button. Although the button-press response may recruit minimal cognitive resources, this simple response task was included primarily to control for potential postural effects associated with pressing the mouse button.

Dual-task

The dual-tasks consisted of a combination of the postural task and the cognitive task. Each dual-task block consisted of 10 cognitive trials, matching the trial structure of the single-cognitive task. The attentional focus was frequently manipulated through verbal instructions so as to direct individuals' attention towards task-relevant information. In the equal-focus condition, participants were asked to “complete cognitive tasks as quickly and accurately as possible, meanwhile stand as still as possible.” Verbal instructions were used to manipulate attentional focus in the cognitive-focus and postural-focus conditions [19, 27]. In the postural-focus condition, participants were instructed as follows: “The postural task is the primary task. You should stand as still as possible, and then complete cognitive tasks quickly and accurately.” In the cognitive-focus condition, participants were instructed as follows: “The cognitive task is the primary task. You should complete cognitive tasks as quickly and accurately as possible, and then do your best to keep your posture stable.”

Data analysis

Cognitive task

For the cognitive task, accuracy (ACC) was calculated as the number of correct trials divided by the total number of trials. To reduce the influence of outliers, trials were excluded from the final analysis if the RT was shorter than 150 ms (n = 0) or longer than 2000 ms (n = 4) and was outside of 3 standard deviations around the mean of this participant (n = 2; all two trials were above three standard deviations from the mean in dual-task with high load). Mean RT was calculated based on the remaining valid trials following outlier exclusion. ACC and RT were primary outcome measures in the present study.

Postural task

For the postural task, center of pressure (COP) data were collected by the Kistler force plate (1000 Hz sampling frequency) and analyzed using analysis software (MARS, Kistler, Winterthur, Switzerland). The data of the force plate were collected for 60 s immediately following the first cognitive trial in each block. Michaud et al. suggested that five 60-s recordings seems to be a good compromise [16]. The first cognitive trial was discarded to mitigate initial variability, with the last cognitive trial retained due to its stronger representativeness. Sway velocities in the AP and mediolateral (ML) directions were calculated to indicate postural stability, as it is a reliable measurement that provides information on the efficiency of postural control [9, 21]. The decrease in sway velocity is often interpreted as an improvement in balance [22]. No COP data were excluded in the final analysis. Sway velocities and 95% prediction ellipse area were primary outcome measures in the present study.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 27.0 statistics software (IBM Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of distribution for all datasets. All datasets were normally distributed. 2 (cognitive load: low, high) × 3 (attentional focus: equal-focus, cognitive-focus, postural-focus) repeated-measures ANOVAs were used to compare ACC, RT, sway velocity, and 95% prediction ellipse area. When Mauchly's test indicated a violation of sphericity, Greenhouse–Geisser’s correction was used. The level of significance for all tests was set at α = 0.05. The Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc test was used for pairwise comparisons.

Results

Validity of manipulation

To verify that the cognitive load manipulation was effective, we compared single-cognitive task performance between low and high load conditions using paired-samples t-tests. As expected, ACC was significantly higher in the low-load condition (M = 93.10, SD = 5.62) than in the high-load condition (M = 83.81, SD = 9.23), t(13) = 3.869, p = 0.002, Cohen’s d = 1.03. RT was marginally significantly shorter in the low-load condition (M = 688.58, SD = 199.30) than in the high-load condition (M = 741.61, SD = 168.85), t(13) = 2.097, p = 0.056, Cohen’s d = 0.56. These results confirm that the manipulation of cognitive load was successful.

For the single-task postural condition, descriptive data are as follows: ML sway velocity = 10.26 ± 2.68 mm/s, AP sway velocity = 13.18 ± 3.16 mm/s, 95% prediction ellipse area = 377.56 ± 242.97 mm2.

Discussion

Although studies indicate that spatial processing with an increased cognitive load competes with postural control for resources, little is known about how attentional allocation strategies influence dual-task performance under different cognitive loads. We found that in the high load conditions, the ML sway velocity was marginally significantly lower and the ACC was significantly higher in the cognitive-focus condition compared to the equal-focus condition, but there was no significant difference between the equal-focus and postural-focus condition. However, no significant differences were observed in the low load conditions.

In the low load conditions, there was no difference in dual-task performance among all three attentional focuses. It suggests that the attentional focus may have had limited influence on dual-task performance when cognitive demands were relatively low. One possible explanation is that the dual-task demands may not exceed attentional capacity under low-load conditions. Under such conditions, participants may have been able to maintain postural stability while simultaneously performing the cognitive task without competition for attentional resources. Consequently, shifts in attentional priorities toward posture or cognition might not be sufficient to produce observable differences in performance.

In the high load conditions, there was no significant difference in the ML sway velocity and the ACC between the equal-focus condition and the postural-focus condition. According to the adaptive resource sharing model [17, 18], the observed pattern of postural sway depends on how much precision is required in the postural and suprapostural components, as well as the attentional load of each component. When the balance component is demanding yet suprapostural precision requirements are low to modest, autonomous-control is invoked to aid stabilization. The autonomous-control refers to a sway-minimization pattern wherein resources are preferentially allocated to stabilize posture. This interpretation is consistent with previous dual-task research. For example, Patel et al. [23] reported that participants prioritized a more demanding walking task over a visuomotor reaction time task. A similar mechanism may have contributed to performance regulation in the present study.

The tandem stance used in the present study reduces the base of support in the ML direction, which poses a greater challenge for postural control compared to normal stance. Consequently, participants likely adopted a "posture-first" prioritization strategy, which is a typical response when the balancing component is highly demanding [18]. Under high cognitive load, increased processing demands may have intensified competition for limited attentional resources, potentially encouraging participants to allocate resources preferentially toward maintaining stability. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies examining postural control under increased mechanical demands. For example, increased mechanical demands have been shown not only to impair postural stability [15] but also to require greater attentional resources for maintaining standing posture [29]. Together, these findings support the notion that challenging postural demands can influence how attentional resources are allocated during balance regulation. In the present study, participants in the equal-focus condition may therefore have spontaneously shifted toward a posture-prioritization strategy, resulting in performance patterns resembling those observed in the postural-focus condition. Future studies employing postural tasks with different levels of balance constraint are needed to determine whether the posture-prioritization pattern observed in the present study generalizes across varying postural demands.

In addition, in the high load conditions, higher ACC were found in the cognitive-focus instruction compared to the equal-focus instruction. This finding is intuitive because directing attention toward the prioritized task would be expected to facilitate performance. More interestingly, cognitive prioritization also tended to lead to slower ML sway velocity compared with the equal-focus condition. This finding is consistent with previous experimental studies. For example, Burcal et al. [3] asked participants to perform single-limb balance task while performing cognitive tasks under postural-focus, cognitive-focus, or equal-focus instructions. They found that cognitive-focus instructions could enhance stability and cognitive performance regardless of the cognitive tasks (backwards counting and random number generation). Those results demonstrate that cognitive prioritization is facilitative for dual-task performance. Importantly, the present study extends previous findings by showing that this facilitative effect emerged specifically under high cognitive load but not under low cognitive load.

The constrained action hypothesis offers a possible explanation for the observed facilitation effect [32]. According to this framework, attentional focus can be classified as internal or external depending on whether attention is directed toward body movements or toward movement effects and task outcomes. External focus is defined as attention directed toward movement outcomes, whereas internal focus involves conscious monitoring of body movements. External focus has generally been associated with greater movement automaticity and enhanced motor performance, whereas internal focus may interfere with automatic control processes [32]. In the present study, prioritizing the cognitive task may have promoted a more externally oriented attentional state by shifting attention away from conscious monitoring of posture. Because participants allocated greater attention to the cognitive task, postural control may have relied more on automatic control processes. Substantial evidence indicates that young adults can reduce postural sway by adopting automatic postural control when the cognitive tasks are prioritized [2, 13]. This interpretation is consistent with the constrained action hypothesis. Thus, it suggests that the cognitive focus might enhance the external focus for postural control. Moreover, because the present study did not directly assess mechanisms underlying postural control, the interpretation of increased automaticity remains speculative. Future studies incorporating direct measures of postural automaticity or attentional processes may help clarify the mechanisms underlying these effects. Recent event-related force plate analyses [10, 11] may provide a methodological approach for investigating the temporal dynamics of cognitive–motor interactions and the mechanisms underlying postural control.

This study had some potential limitations and recommendations. First, although our power analysis was based on previous effect sizes, small samples can inflate between‑subject variability and lead to overestimation of interaction effects. Therefore, the present findings should be considered preliminary, and larger sample sizes are needed to confirm the robustness of the observed effects. Second, although the equal-focus condition was consistently administered before the cognitive-focus and postural-focus conditions to minimize potential instruction leakage, this fixed ordering may have introduced practice effects. Therefore, some observed effects may partly reflect accumulated task experience. Future studies should explore alternative approaches to better balance the trade-off between instructional carryover and practice effects. Third, Tandem stance was selected because it reduces the base of support in the ML direction and has been shown to be sensitive to cognitive–motor interference in healthy young adults. However, the high postural demands may have reduced its sensitivity to attentional manipulations. This possibility may help explain why instructional effects were observed for ML sway velocity but not for AP sway velocity or ellipse area. Future studies should compare postural tasks with different levels of challenge to determine whether attentional allocation effects vary as a function of postural task demands. Fourth, the present study did not directly assess mechanisms underlying automatic postural control. Future studies incorporating direct measures of postural automaticity or attentional processes are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying these effects.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that the conscious allocation of attention to a visual spatial cognitive working memory task modulates dual‑task performance when the task is difficult (high load) but not when it is easy (low load). This finding highlights the potential role of attentional prioritization strategies in cognitive-motor dual-task performance trade-offs.
