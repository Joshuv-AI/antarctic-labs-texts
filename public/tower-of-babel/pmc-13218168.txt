Knowledge about the health impact of indoor air pollution and associated factors among mothers in northeastern Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Tarikuwa Natnael
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study was designed to determine the level of knowledge about the health impact of indoor air pollution (IAP) and associated factors among mothers in northeastern Ethiopia.DesignA community-based cross-sectional study was carried out from May to June 2024. A systematic sampling technique was used to select the study participants. Using a pretested and structured questionnaire, data were gathered through interviews. The collected data were entered into EpiData V.4.6 and SPSS V.25 was used for the analysis. The adjusted OR with a 95% CI and the significance of associations at p<0.05 were determined.SettingThe study was conducted in northeastern Ethiopia.ParticipantsA total of 401 mothers participated in this study.Outcome measuresThe primary outcome is the prevalence of good knowledge about the health impact of IAP.ResultsWith a response rate of 95%, 401 mothers in all completed the survey. Of the participants, 205 (51.1%) were aged ≤31 years. In this study, 46.4% (95% CI 41.6 to 51.4) of the 401 participants had good knowledge about the health impact of IAP. Higher educational level (adjusted OR (AOR): 4.4, 95% CI 2.38 to 8.14), higher income (AOR: 1.88, 95% CI 1.15 to 3.06) and receiving training (AOR: 2.72, 95% CI 1.14 to 6.46) were factors associated with having good knowledge.ConclusionThis study concluded that less than half of the mothers had good knowledge about the health impact of IAP. The findings of this study highlight the critical need for policymakers to address the inadequate understanding among mothers of the health impact of IAP. Thus, the health education programme on IAP should be strengthened through effective community engagement.

Introduction

Indoor air pollution (IAP) refers to the presence of physical, chemical and/or biological matter within building environments to the extent to which negative health impacts could occur.1 IAP occurs in houses due to a range of factors, including house equipment, paints, tobacco smoke, domestic cleaning chemicals, furniture, central heating and cooling systems and building materials.2 IAP is also caused by outdoor air pollutants, which can enter buildings through a number of locations, such as open windows, gaps in walls and doors and ventilation systems.3 However, the use of biomass fuels for cooking, heating and lighting is likely to be the largest source of IAP on a global scale.4 Biomass fuels, when used in simple cooking stoves, release harmful pollutants that are extremely dangerous to human health.5 6 Exposure levels of residents living in homes with inadequate ventilation are extremely high, particularly for mothers who spend a lot of time indoors.79 Sub-Saharan Africa has high average indoor air Particulate Matter 2.5 concentrations, which typically range from 245 µg/m³ to 818 µg/m³ or higher in homes that use solid biomass fuels.1013

Several studies have documented the extent of IAP and its correlation with health outcomes. Exposure to indoor pollutants is a risk factor for asthma, acute respiratory infection (ARI), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, lung cancer, tuberculosis, cerebrovascular disease, ischaemic heart disease, hypertension, cataract, low birth weight, stillbirth, preterm birth, as well as respiratory and cardiovascular mortality in children aged under 5 years.1417 Low and middle-income countries, especially those in Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, continue to have high rates of these health burdens.1820 According to recent estimates, IAP is responsible for approximately 1.8 million deaths and 60.9 million disability-adjusted life years, constituting a significant health disparity in low-income and middle-income regions.14

Population exposure to IAP is affected by kitchen and stove design, frequency of cooking and cohabitation with tobacco smokers.21 Even though there were significant percentage and absolute reductions due to the use of improved stoves, post-intervention kitchen levels remained extremely high.1 22 Additionally, some techniques for managing IAP exposure include the use of low-emission materials, cautious fuel selection for indoor combustion and ventilation.22 Despite the abundance of preventive measures of IAP, the implementation of these measures is primarily motivated by a thorough understanding of the health impacts of IAP. However, there are knowledge gaps that need to be addressed for the effective implementation of preventive measures.23

Public awareness has been essential in ensuring the success of effective IAP prevention measures and preventing such negative effects.24 However, a study on the knowledge regarding IAP within the community in different areas of the world showed that knowledge addressing IAP was poor.25 26 Despite this, effective IAP prevention practices help to ensure not only effective control of indoor air quality but also human safety and prevention of other unacceptable side effects, including outdoor air pollution and climate change.

While outdoor air pollution tends to be worse in poor urban communities,2729 IAP tends to be worse in peri-urban communities where biomass fuels are more frequently used for cooking, heating and lighting due to limited access to other forms of energy.30 Additionally, evidence has shown that a typical peri-urban population in sub-Saharan Africa uses biomass fuels extensively, with women and those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds being disproportionately affected.31 Through the National Clean Cooking Roadmap (2025–2035), Ethiopia is putting comprehensive policies into place to lessen the health impact of IAP. By 2035, 76% of homes will have access to clean cooking stoves, which aims to lower premature deaths caused by IAP by 90%.32

A recent study in the peri-urban areas of northeastern Ethiopia found that the usage of biomass fuel was the primary cause of the higher prevalence of ARI among children aged under 5 years.33 However, not much information is available in earlier research regarding knowledge about the health impact of IAP in peri-urban areas of northeastern Ethiopia. Additionally, the earlier research has focused on the general population and the knowledge of IAP among.25 34 This hinders the creation of suitable countermeasures for IAP in peri-urban areas of northeastern Ethiopia. To fill these knowledge gaps, this study was carried out to evaluate the knowledge about the health impact of IAP among mothers in peri-urban areas of northeastern Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study design and period

A community-based cross-sectional study was carried out from May to June 2024 to evaluate the knowledge about the health impact of IAP and associated factors among mothers in peri-urban areas of northeastern Ethiopia.

Study setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted in peri-urban areas of Dessie City, northeastern Ethiopia. According to definitions, peri-urban areas are partially urbanised areas of consolidated urban and rural regions.35 The city is roughly 401 km from Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia. It is a popular travel and business destination. According to the 2007 national census conducted by the Central Statistical Agency of Ethiopia, the city had a total population of 151 174, of which 72 932 were male and 78 242 were female.36 The Dessie city administration has 16 kebeles, 10 urban and 6 peri-urban. Of the total, 34 748 (16.4%) lived in peri-urban kebeles.35 The 6 peri-urban kebeles of Dessie City—Tita, Kurkur, Boru-Selase, Kelem-Dereba, Gerado-Bilen and Gerado Endodber—were the sites of this investigation.

Source and study populations

All mothers in the peri-urban areas of the Dessie City were the source population, whereas the randomly selected mothers who lived in selected peri-urban areas were the study population.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula, with the following assumptions: 5% margin of error (d), the proportion (p) of good knowledge about the health impact of IAP among mothers is 50% (no previous study in a similar context) and Zα/2=the value of the standard normal curve score corresponding to the given CI. Accordingly, based on a single population proportion formula: n=(zα/2)2×p(1−p)d237 ; where n=initial sample size, Zα/2=1.96 corresponding to 95% confidence level, p=proportion=50%, d=margin of error=5%=0.05 and n=384. After adding a 10% contingency for the non-response rate, the sample size became 422.

There are six peri-urban kebeles in Dessie City and the study was conducted on the six peri-urban kebeles. All 422 estimated participants were proportionally allocated to the six peri-urban kebeles based on their respective numbers of households. The first household was selected by lottery methods. Then, the study participants were selected using a systematic random sampling method until the required number of sample size was achieved. To reduce the non-response rate, a follow-up visit was performed the same day if an eligible study participant was not available from a particular household. The next day, if they were unavailable once more, a second visit was made. After the third visit, if the individual was still unavailable, they were considered non-respondents.

Operational definitions

Knowledge about the health impact of IAP

The knowledge of the mother was evaluated using 9 questions with ‘yes=1’ for correct or ‘no=0’ for incorrect responses to give values ranging from 0 to 9. Respondents who answered above and equal to the mean of knowledge about the health impact of IAP questions were classified as having good knowledge, whereas respondents who answered below the mean of knowledge about the health impact of IAP questions were classified as having poor knowledge.38

Data collection tools and procedures

Using a pretested and structured questionnaire, data were gathered through interviews. Based on the goals of the study, a review of pertinent literature was conducted before developing the questionnaire. Authors and language specialists first created the questionnaire in English, and then they translated it into Amharic, the native tongue of the research region. Three domains comprised the questionnaire. The purpose of the first domain was to gather sociodemographic information about the respondents, including their age, marital status, level of education, occupational status, household size and income. Training and source of information about IAP was the second domain, and the third domain was intended to gather information on knowledge about the health impact of IAP. The full study questionnaire is provided in the online supplementary materials 1.

Data quality assurance

A standardised questionnaire that was administered by the interviewer was used to gather data. Following a thorough analysis of relevant research and related literature, the questionnaire was modified. A lot of emphasis was placed on the proper design of data collection tools to guarantee the quality of the data that was gathered. English was used to develop the knowledge questions, which were then translated into Amharic and back again. Three data collectors and two supervisors with prior experience and task knowledge were used. Before any actual data collection began, the supervisors and data collectors attended 2 days of training on topics such as informed consent, study objectives, and the roles and responsibilities that both parties must maintain throughout the data collection process.

Both the supervisors and the data collectors were under the supervision of the principal investigator. Furthermore, a pre-test was carried out on 10% of the questionnaires in Kombolcha town’s peri-urban areas 1 week prior to the real data collection period. The response, language clarity, suitability and consistency of the instrument were verified based on the pre-test results. Some terms and misinterpretations were changed, the numbers of questions were lowered without changing the intended outcome to be measured, and unclear questions were clarified in light of the findings of pre-test analysis. On-the-spot discussions with the principal investigator, supervisors and data collectors resolved issues that arose during the data collection process.

Data management and statistical analysis

The completeness and consistency of each questionnaire were reviewed daily. Subsequently, the precise and comprehensive data were labelled, coded, confirmed and categorised before being imported into EpiData software (Version 4.6, EpiData Association, Odense, Denmark) and exported to SPSS software (Version 25.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for additional examination. Frequencies, percentages, means, ±SDs and tables were used in the computation and presentation of descriptive statistics. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were used to evaluate the multicollinearity among independent variables, and the results showed that all variables had VIF values <5. When Cronbach’s alpha (α) was used to test the reliability of the questionnaire, the coefficient value was 0.967. Because of this, it was decided that the internal consistency of the questionnaire in replicating the data from earlier measurements made with the same tool was acceptable. To minimise the potential bias, this study used a rigorous methodology involving random sampling, pretesting of the data collection tool and strict confounding control. Missing values were also addressed using mean imputation.

The normality, outliers and multicollinearity of the variable were checked before running bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses. Logistic regression analysis was used to calculate the bivariate (crude OR (COR)) and multivariable (adjusted OR (AOR)) values with a 95% CI. Variables with p<0.25 in the bivariate analysis were deemed suitable candidates for multivariable analysis. Variables with a significance level of p<0.05 in the multivariable logistic regression analysis were considered statistically significant and independently linked to the knowledge about the health impact of IAP. Using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, which had a p value of >0.05, the model’s fitness was assessed.

Patient and public involvement

Mothers contributed valuable data to this investigation; however, they were not involved in the study design, protocol development, data collection tools or dissemination of findings. Results will be disseminated via open-access publications and the Dessie city administration’s health department.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

With a response rate of 95%, 401 mothers in all completed the survey. Of the participants, 205 (51.1%) were aged ≤31 years, making up more than half of the group. Of the mothers, 219 (54.6%) were married and 70 (17.5%) of the participants were housewives. Two-thirds of the respondents, or 272 (67.8%), had ≤US$22.33 of monthly income, and 181 (45.1%) had household size of ≤5 persons (table 1).

Knowledge about the health impact of IAP

Only about one-third of the participants were aware that IAP is a risk factor for respiratory illnesses, low birth weight and stillbirth, with 130 (32.4%), 148 (36.9%) and 142 (35.4%) participants reporting awareness, respectively. Of all the respondents, 146 (36.4%) knew that IAP is a risk factor for preterm birth, 162 (40.4%) knew that IAP is a risk factor for lung cancer, and only 150 (37.4%) knew that IAP is a risk factor for cataract. Overall, 186 mothers (46.4%) were found to have good knowledge about the health impact of IAP (95% CI 41.9 to 51.4), while 215 (53.6 %; 95% CI 48.6 to 58.1) of the mothers showed poor knowledge about the health impact of IAP (table 3).

Discussion

IAP in developing nations has been recognised by the WHO as a serious global health concern that requires more research and policy work.8 39 40 Furthermore, the increased health risks associated with IAP are made worse by the widespread use of biomass fuels in developing countries.4143 The majority of the high levels of air pollutants released are primarily experienced by women who are traditionally in charge of cooking.44 Despite this, one of the main motivations for implementing effective IAP preventive measures is having a solid understanding of the health impacts of IAP. Thus, the goal of the current study was to evaluate the knowledge of mothers about the health impact of IAP and associated factors in northeastern Ethiopia. The current study found that only 46.4% of the mothers in northeastern Ethiopia had good knowledge about the health impact of IAP, and higher educational level, higher income and receiving training were the factors which showed a significant association with good knowledge.

Mothers and children are more susceptible to the health effects of IAP because they spend more time at home in proximity to kitchens and stoves.45 However, mothers can protect themselves and other family members from the detrimental effects of IAP on their health by being well-informed about it. However, the findings of this study showed that only 46.4% (95% CI 41.6 to 51.4) of mothers had good knowledge about the health impact of IAP. This calls for healthcare workers to implement stringent knowledge enhancement procedures to save the mothers and the family members from the impacts of IAP. Compared with study findings from France (42.9%),46 the observed good knowledge in this study was more common. It did, however, fall short of studies on knowledge of IAP carried out in Ethiopia (66.7%),47 Nigeria (62.8%),26 Ghana (70.5%),48 South Africa (71%),49 Bangladesh (77.36% and 84.66%)50 51 and China (80.5% and 64.59%).45 52 This lower knowledge score in our study could be due to the fact that 18.5% of the studied mothers are unable to read and write. Thus, healthcare workers should facilitate and give regular health information to mothers on IAP considering their educational level. Additionally, local non-governmental organisations should collaborate with the city administration’s health department and other stakeholders on dissemination of information using various media.

Of the participants in the current study, only one-third (130, or 32.4%), more than one-third (148, or 36.7%) and 142 (35.4%) are aware that IAP is a risk factor for stillbirth, low birth weight and respiratory diseases, respectively. Of all respondents, only 150 (37.2%) were aware that IAP is a risk factor for cataracts, 146 (36.4%) were aware that IAP is a risk factor for preterm delivery and 162 (40.4%) were aware that IAP is a risk factor for lung cancer. Most participants in the current study are aware that IAP increases the risk of lung cancer and heart disease among other health effects. The explanation for this could be that a recurring team training programme at Wollo University Health Science College in the area may raise awareness of IAP by placing a strong emphasis on these issues.

Evidence suggests that the type of biomass fuel exposure varies by region of the world, with South Asian pregnant women frequently reporting using wood, crop residue and animal dung as fuel for cooking and heating. African pregnant women report higher use of charcoal and Latin American pregnant women use petroleum gas more frequently.53 The sociocultural characteristics of Ethiopia might also reveal the usage of biomass fuels even while there is access to clean fuels. For instance, the Ethiopian community traditionally believed that biomass fuels would help postpartum women and babies by preventing odours and insects and by fortifying the structure of their homes.54 Therefore, it is imperative that the health bureau, education office and non-governmental organisations work together to create awareness about the negative health effects of IAP.

This study finding indicated that a higher educational level can increase the good knowledge of IAP among mothers by more than four-fold (AOR 4.4). This means that mothers who had a higher educational level were 4.4 times more likely to have good knowledge about the health impact of IAP than the counters. It is similar to the earlier results found in studies done in China45 55 and France.46 Therefore, it is crucial to provide training aimed at mothers with lower levels of education to raise their level of knowledge. So, strengthening air pollution education systems may be one of the most effective methods to involve all citizens in air quality improvement.

The current study also showed a relationship between household income and knowledge of IAP. In this study, mothers who had more income than the counters were nearly twice as likely to know a lot about IAP. A study on the relationship between income and knowledge in Brazil provided compelling evidence for this observation.56 This could be the case due to the fact that mothers with greater incomes have access to a wider range of media sources providing information on the effects of IAP on health. This finding was similar to the two former researches in China.26 55 However, different results were obtained from studies in Nigeria55 and France.46 The difference can be due to different study settings, sample size and difference in the economic levels of respondents. Thus, the intervention programme should focus on respondents with a lower economic level.

In this study, 39 (9.7%) mothers reported having received training, which may have been the main factor contributing to their high level of knowledge regarding the impact of IAP on health. Training in infection control, particularly with regard to the health impacts of IAP, has the potential to improve adherence to IAP prevention measures and lower the risk of IAP.57 58 Moreover, in the current study, mothers who received training about IAP showed 2.7 times better knowledge than the counters. Therefore, health extension workers and other relevant organisations should place a strong emphasis on providing training to mothers to increase their knowledge and prevent the health impacts of IAP. Thus, concerned organisations need to pay close attention to IAP to prevent the health impacts of IAP and other environmental health risks such as outdoor air pollution and climate change.

This is the first study of its kind to look into the understanding of the health impact of IAP and associated factors among mothers in northeastern Ethiopia, where IAP is a major risk factor for higher rates of morbidity and mortality among children aged under 5 years that cause ARI. The data can also be used to create educational programmes that are helpful in improving the general health of mothers and children aged under 5 years. Furthermore, this research will also have a big impact on reaching Sustainable Development Goal 3, which is about ‘good health and well-being for all’.

Several limitations inherent to this study design should be acknowledged. Primarily, as a cross-sectional study, it provides only a single-point snapshot, limiting the ability to track learning progression, knowledge retention over time or establish a clear temporal relationship between exposure and the outcomes variable. Furthermore, the findings are based on self-reported data for sociodemographic, training and source of information about IAP, which may introduce recall and social desirability biases. Geographically, the study was conducted exclusively in northeastern Ethiopia, meaning the results may not represent the situation at the national level. Despite these limitations, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of the health impacts of IAP, identifying it as a critical risk factor for elevated morbidity and mortality among children aged under 5 years, largely driven by ARI within the study area.

Conclusion

This community-based study in northeastern Ethiopia found that less than half of mothers surveyed possessed good knowledge regarding the health impacts of IAP. Receiving training, higher income and higher educational level were significant variables linked to good knowledge. Thus, an integrated set of strategies such as the collaboration of the health bureau, education office and non-governmental organisation is required to enhance knowledge. Moreover, the strengthening of policies and enforcement of laws pertaining to IAP can improve the knowledge about IAP.
