1.
Introduction

The process of
r
-neighbor bootstrap percolation on an undirected graph
G(V,E)
, with an integer
r\geqslant 1
, was introduced by Chalupa, Leith, and Reich
[
13
]
. In this process, each vertex is either infected or healthy, and once a vertex becomes infected, it remains infected forever. Initially, a set of vertices
A_{0}
is infected, and let
A_{i}
denote the set of infected vertices up to step
i
. The bootstrap percolation process evolves in discrete steps as follows: for
i>0
,

A_{i}=A_{i-1}\cup\{v\in V:|\mathcal{N}(v)\cap A_{i-1}|\geqslant r\},

where
\mathcal{N}(v)
is the neighborhood of the vertex
v
in
G
. In simple terms, a vertex that is not initially infected becomes infected at step
i
if it has at least
r
infected neighbors at step
i-1
. Here, we define
r
as the infection threshold for all
v\in V
and a contagious set as a set of initially infected vertices that leads to the complete infection of the entire graph. Percolation occurs if all vertices have been infected by the end of the process. In random bootstrap percolation, each vertex is independently and randomly infected at the beginning with a probability
p
. The central question in random bootstrap percolation is to determine the critical probability,

p_{c}(G,r):=\sup\{p\in(0,1):\mathbb{P}_{p}(A\;\text{percolates on}\>G)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\},

where
A
represents the set of initially infected vertices.

For bootstrap percolation in the random setting, Aizenman and Lebowitz
[
1
]
demonstrated that when the process occurs within a finite box
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}^{d}
, the critical probability scales as
{\Theta}((\log(n))^{1-d})
with
d\geqslant r=2
. In their work
[
2
]
, Balogh and Bollobás established sharp transition for bootstrap percolation on a
d
-dimensional box, building upon the results from
[
17
]
. Holroyd
[
16
]
achieved a significant breakthrough by identifying the precise threshold for
d=r=2
. Subsequent advancements were made by Gravner and Holroyd
[
19
]
as well as by Hartarsky and Morris
[
20
]
, who determined the scaling of the second-order term,

p_{c}([n]^{2},r=2)=\frac{\pi}{18\log n}-\frac{\Theta(1)}{(\log n)^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

For the case where
d=r=3
, Cerf and Cirillo
[
12
]
established that the critical probability scales as
{\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{\log\log(n)}\right)
. For
d\geqslant r\geqslant 3
, Cerf and Manzo
[
11
]
demonstrated that the critical probability behaves as
{\Theta}\left((\log^{*(r-1)}(n))^{r-d-1}\right)
, where
\log^{*(r-1)}
represents the logarithm iterated
r-1
times. The sharp threshold for the
r
-neighbor process on
[n]^{d}
was ultimately determined by Balogh, Bollobás, Duminil-Copin, and Morris
[
4
]
, extending the results by Balogh, Bollobás, and Morris
[
6
]
. For every
d\geqslant r\geqslant 2
, they identified an explicit constant
\lambda(d,r)>0
such that the critical probability can be expressed as follows:

p_{c}([n]^{d},r)=\left[\frac{\lambda(d,r)+o(1)}{\log^{*(r-1)}n}\right]^{d-r+1}.

While a considerable amount of attention has been devoted to the study of random bootstrap percolation in the
d
-dimensional box, it has also been extensively explored on the hypercube. Let
Q_{n}
be a hypercube graph whose vertices are the binary
n
-dimensional vectors and edges connect the pairs of vertices at Hamming distance
1
. Balogh and Bollobás
[
3
]
established the following result: there exist constants
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}>0
such that

\frac{c_{1}}{n^{2}}2^{-2\sqrt{n}}\leqslant p_{c}(Q_{n},2)\leqslant\frac{c_{2}}{n^{2}}2^{-2\sqrt{n}}.

In a subsequent study, Balogh, Bollobás, and Morris further improved upon the bound presented in
[
8
]
and proved the following result: there exists an explicit constant
\lambda>0
such that

\frac{16\lambda}{n^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\right)2^{-2\sqrt{n}}\leqslant p_{c}(Q_{n},2)\leqslant\frac{16\lambda}{n^{2}}\left(1+\frac{5(\log n)^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)2^{-2\sqrt{n}}.

For majority bootstrap percolation on the hypercube
Q_{n}
where the infection threshold
r
is equal to
\frac{1}{2}n
, Balogh, Bollobás and Morris in
[
7
]
proved that:

\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}-2\frac{\log\log n}{\sqrt{n\log n}}\leqslant p_{c}\Big(Q_{n},\frac{n}{2}\Big)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}+\frac{\log\log n}{2\sqrt{n\log n}}+o\left(\frac{\log\log n}{2\sqrt{n\log n}}\right).

Recently, Collares, Erde, Geisler, and Kang
[
14
]
showed that the random majority bootstrap percolation process on graphs satisfying certain structural properties exhibits a universal behavior, which is, in some sense, governed by the degree sequence. Subsequently, the same authors
[
15
]
considered a slightly different class of graphs and established analogous results.

Overview of the results.

Holroyd, Liggett, and Romik
[
21
]
studied bootstrap percolation on a generalized grid graph in which each vertex has more than four neighbors. In Section 9 of
[
14
]
, the authors posed the question of determining the critical probability for majority bootstrap percolation on the halved cube, a graph on
\{0,1\}^{n}
where two vertices are adjacent if their Hamming distance is exactly two. Motivated by these works, we consider an
n
-dimensional hypercube with extended neighborhoods and establish estimates for the critical probability of majority bootstrap percolation.

Let
Q_{k,n}
be a graph with vertices
V(Q_{k,n})=\{0,1\}^{n}
and edges
E(Q_{k,n})=\{xy:d_{H}(x,y)=1,2,\cdots,k\}
, where
k\geqslant 2
. We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Consider a positive integer
k\geqslant 2
, and let
N=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom{n}{i}
. For every
\epsilon>0
, there exists an
n_{0}(\epsilon,k)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geqslant n_{0}(\epsilon,k)
we have

\frac{1}{2}-\left(\frac{\sqrt{kk!}}{2}+\epsilon\right)\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{n^{k/2}}\leqslant p_{c}\left(Q_{k,n},\frac{N}{2}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}-\left(\frac{\sqrt{kk!}}{2}-\epsilon\right)\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{n^{k/2}}.

Note that our graph does not lie on the class of graphs analyzed in

[
14
]
or
[
15
]
while the critical probability exhibits the same universal second-order behavior in
[
14
]
and in
[
15
]
when expressed in terms of its degree
N
.

Our Contributions.
Our first contribution is to apply a theorem of Petrov, a theorem of Gordon, a new lemma
10
for estimating the binomial distribution, and Effron-Stein’s inequality to obtain very precise approximations for binomial tail probabilities. This level of precision plays a crucial role in establishing both the upper and lower bounds for the critical probability.

In the work by Balogh, Bollobás, and Morris
[
7
]
, the second-moment method was used to show that there exists a
\delta>0
such that

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(x\in A_{2})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}+\delta.

The proof was based on the relations
\mathcal{N}(y)\cap\mathcal{N}(z)=\{x,w\}
and
\mathcal{N}(x)\cap\mathcal{N}(y)=\emptyset
, where

and

which made the application of the second-moment analysis manageable. In
[

and

by using the graph properties essentially similar (in some sense) to the hypercube to derive equation

y\neq z\end{subarray}}|\mathcal{N}(y)\cap\mathcal{N}(z)|=\Theta(n^{k-1}),

and

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\\

Another contribution of our paper is the application of Theorems

Our third contribution is a non-trivial enumeration of the possible choices for

Our Proof Strategy.
At a high level we follow the proof strategy of
[
7
]
. The main intuition is as follows. When the initial infection probability is slightly above the critical probability, after a few steps each vertex remains healthy with exponentially small probability. When the initial infection probability is slightly below the critical probability, after a few steps the infection process stops with high probability.

The heuristics behind the constant in the second-order term are as follows. Let the initial infection probability
p=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{C\sqrt{\log n}}{n^{k/2}}
. Let
\tau_{n}=\mathbb{P}(x\in A_{1}|x\notin A_{0})=\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{N}(x)\cap A_{0}|\geqslant r)
. For
r=\frac{N}{2}
, we can apply Lemmas
8
and
9
to get
\tau_{n}=(1+o(1))\frac{\sqrt{k!}}{2C\sqrt{2\pi\log n}}n^{\frac{-2C^{2}}{k!}}
. For a typical healthy vertex
x
initially there are about
Np=\frac{N}{2}-N\frac{C\sqrt{\log n}}{n^{k/2}}
infected neighboring vertices. The deficit to its threshold
r
is about
N\frac{C\sqrt{\log n}}{n^{k/2}}
. Among all the neighboring vertices of
x
, there are roughly
N\tau_{n}
newly infected vertices in the first step. The transition occurs when
\tau_{n}\approx\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{n^{k/2}}
.

For the upper bound of Theorem
1
, we first show equation
1
. Then we show that after a few steps, the probability that
x
remains healthy is exponentially small, of order
\exp(-cn)
. Next we strengthen this estimate so that we can use the union bound over all vertices.

For the lower bound of Theorem
1
, we introduce a less restrictive process in which for the first few steps the number of infected neighbors for a vertex
x
to be infected is slightly less than
r
. Then we show this less restrictive process will stop in a few steps with high probability. This implies that the original process will fail to percolate with high probability.

Fact 4

If

Proof.

Let

We prove by induction on

Initial step:
The event
\{x\in A_{0}\}

\ell=0
.

Inductive step:

Suppose that, for every vertex

A vertex

Therefore,
{x\in A_{\ell}}\text{ is determined by the initial states in }B_{G}(x,\ell)

In the graph

a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{s}
, be natural numbers such that
\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{j}=\binom{\ell}{h}m^{h}
. Then the edges of
K_{\ell\times m}^{h}
can be partitioned into almost regular hypergraphs
(V,\mathscr{E}_{i})
(i.e., the degree of each vertex differs by at most one) so that
|\mathscr{E}_{j}|=a_{j}
for
j=1,...,s
.

3.
Upper Bound

For the ease of notations, from now on by
c=c(k)
we denote a constant which may depend on
k
. We will use
c=c(k)
as generic notations, where the specific form of this function may be different in different expressions.

Let us start by proving the upper bound. To do so, we require a slightly more general concept. Let
A_{i,r}
represent the set of infected vertices up to and including step
i
when the infection threshold is
r
. Hence,
A_{i}=A_{i,\frac{N}{2}}
. In order to establish the upper bound, we will rely on a few lemmas.

Lemma 15
.

Let
\epsilon>0,x\in V(Q_{k,n})
, let
p=\frac{1}{2}-\left(\frac{\sqrt{kk!}}{2}-\epsilon\right)\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{n^{k/2}}
, and let the infection threshold be
r=\frac{N}{2}+O(1)
, where
k\geqslant 2
is an integer. Then for sufficiently large
n(\epsilon)
there exists an absolute constant
\delta>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(x\in A_{2,r})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}+\delta.

In fact we will prove that under the assumptions in Lemma
15
, we have
\mathbb{P}(x\in A_{2,r})\geqslant 1-o(1).

Proof.

The main strategy is to prove that
\mathbb{P}(S_{x}\leqslant\frac{N}{2})=o(1)
, where
S_{x}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(x)}\mathbb{I}_{\{u\in A_{1,r}\}}
. In order to prove this statement, it is very natural to consider using the Chebyshev’s inequality. Thus, we need to estimate the

\mathbb{E}(S_{x})
and
\text{Var}(S_{x})
.

First let us consider
\mathbb{P}(x\in A_{1,r})
and so we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(x\in A_{1,r})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(x\in A_{0})+\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{N}(x)\cap A_{0}|\geqslant r|x\notin A_{0})\mathbb{P}(x\notin A_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(x\in A_{0})+\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{N}(x)\cap A_{0}|\geqslant r)\mathbb{P}(x\notin A_{0}).

Therefore
\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}_{x\in A_{1,r}}]=p+(1-p)\tau_{n}
, where
\tau_{n}=\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{N}(x)\cap A_{0}|\geqslant r)
.

Note that
|\mathcal{N}(x)\cap A_{0}|
is a Binomial
(N,p)
. Let the random variable
X_{N}=|\mathcal{N}(x)\cap A_{0}|
. Therefore, we have

\mu=\mu_{N}:=\mathbb{E}X_{N}=Np
and
\sigma^{2}=\sigma_{N}^{2}:=p(1-p)N
. We would like to use Lemmas
8
and
9
to approximate
\tau_{n}
very accurately. Before that we need to check the uniform boundedness condition.

Note that
X_{N}-pN=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(Z_{i}-p)
where
Z_{i}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)
. Define
Y_{i}=\frac{Z_{i}-p}{p(1-p)}
. It is easy to see
0.49\leqslant p\leqslant\frac{3}{4}
for sufficiently large
n
and therefore
p(1-p)\geqslant\frac{3}{16}
. Since
Z_{i}\in\{0,1\}
,
|Z_{i}-p|\leqslant 1
. Therefore, we have

|Y_{i}|\leqslant\frac{1}{p(1-p)}\leqslant\frac{4}{\sqrt{3}}.

Thus for every
h>0
, we have

e^{h|Y_{i}|}\leqslant e^{\frac{4h}{\sqrt{3}}},

and consequently,

\sup_{i}\mathbb{E}e^{h|Y_{i}|}\leqslant e^{\frac{4h}{\sqrt{3}}}<\infty.

Recall that
\Delta_{n}=\left(\frac{\sqrt{kk!}}{2}-\epsilon\right)\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{n^{k/2}}
and note that

\tau_{n}=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{X_{N}-\mu_{N}}{\sigma_{N}}\geqslant\frac{r-\,u_{N}}{\sigma_{N}}\right)
.

We have
r=\frac{N}{2}+O(1)
, and
pN=\frac{N}{2}-N\Delta_{n}
. Therefore,

r-\mu=N\Delta_{n}+O(1)
, and
\sigma=(\frac{1}{2}+o(1))\sqrt{N}.

Therefore by Lemmas
8
and
9
,

\tau_{n}=(1+o(1))\frac{1}{x\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}).

where
x:=\frac{r-\mu}{\sigma}=\left(\frac{\sqrt{kk!}-2\epsilon}{\sqrt{k!}}+o(1)\right)\log n.

It is easy to see
\tau_{n}=(1+o(1))\frac{\sqrt{k!}}{2C\sqrt{2\pi\log n}}n^{\frac{-2C^{2}}{k!}}
and recall that
C=\frac{\sqrt{kk!}}{2}-\epsilon
.

Note that
\tau_{n}=n^{-\frac{2C^{2}}{k!}+o(1)}\gg\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{n^{k/2}}
. Thus
\mathbb{P}(x\in A_{1,r})=\frac{1}{2}+(\frac{1}{2}-o(1))n^{-\frac{2C^{2}}{k!}+o(1)}
.

Now we need to evaluate
\text{Var}(S_{x})
. This part of the proof is a bit more involved.

For every vertex
x
, let
\xi_{x}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)
denote its initial infection state, independently. Define
I_{u}=\mathbb{I}_{\{u\in A_{1,r}\}}
and let
\alpha=\frac{2C^{2}}{k!}
.

Thus

I_{u}=\mathbb{I}\left\{\xi_{u}=1\ \text{or}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}(u)}\xi_{x}\geqslant r\right\}.

Fix distinct
u,w\in V(Q_{k,n})
, and set
d=d_{H}(u,w)
. We will have the following claim.

Claim 16
.

\text{Cov}(I_{u},I_{w})\leqslant n^{-\lceil d/2\rceil-2\alpha+o(1)}+\mathbb{I}_{{d\leqslant k}}n^{-k/2-\alpha+o(1)}.

Proof.

Note that the events
I_{u}
and
I_{v}
may have dependency due to the common neighbors i.e.,
\mathcal{N}(u)\cap\mathcal{N}(w)
and the facts that
u\in\mathcal{N}(w)
and
w\in\mathcal{N}(u)
when
d\leqslant k
.

Let us estimate the Size of the common neighborhood. Let
M:=\mathcal{N}(u)\cap\mathcal{N}(w)
. We first show
M=O\left(n^{k-\lceil d/2\rceil}\right)
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
u=[0]^{n}
, and write
W:=\{w:||w||_{\ell_{1}}\}=d
. For a common neighbor
x
, write
a=|x\cap W|
and
b=|x\setminus W|
.

Then we have

d_{H}(x,u)=a+b\leqslant k

and

d_{H}(x,w)=d-a+b\leqslant k.

Adding the two inequalities gives

d+2b\leqslant 2k.

Therefore,

b\leqslant k-\left\lceil\frac{d}{2}\right\rceil.

There are only
O(1)
choices for
x\cap W
. Consequently,

M=O_{k}\left(n^{k-\lceil d/2\rceil}\right).

Note that
M=0
if
d>2k
.

Case 1:

d>k
.

In this case,
u\notin\mathcal{N}(w)
and
w\notin\mathcal{N}(u)
. Thus the only initial variables that influence both
I_{u}
and
I_{w}
are those indexed by the common neighborhood
\mathcal{C}
.

Let

Z:=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}}\xi_{x}\sim\text{Binomial}(M,p).

Conditional on all variables
(\xi_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{C}}
, the remaining variables determining
I_{u}
and
I_{w}
are disjoint. Therefore
I_{u}
and
I_{w}
are conditionally independent.

Moreover, the conditional probability depends on the common variables only through their sum
Z
. Define

h(s):=\mathbb{P}(I_{u}=1\mid Z=s).

Since
u
is initially infected with probability
p
,

h(s)=p+(1-p)\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Binomial}(N-M,p)\geqslant r-s\right).

The same function
h
applies to
w
. Conditional independence gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[I_{u}I_{w}\mid Z]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[I_{u}\mid Z]\mathbb{E}[I_{w}\mid Z]

\displaystyle=h(Z)^{2}.

Thus by the tower property of expectation, we have

\text{Cov}(I_{u},I_{w})=\text{Var}(h(Z)).

Therefore it remains to estimate
\text{Var}(h(Z))
.

Write

Z=\xi_{1}+\cdots+\xi_{M},

where the
\xi_{i}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)
.

Let
\xi_{i}^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
\xi_{i}
, and let

Z^{(i)}=Z-\xi_{i}+\xi_{i}^{\prime}.

The Efron–Stein inequality in Lemma
11
gives

\text{Var}(h(Z))\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}\bigl(h(Z)-h(Z^{(i)})\bigr)^{2}

Let
T_{i}:=Z-\xi_{i}\sim\text{Binomial}(M-1,p)
.

If
\xi_{i}=\xi_{i}^{\prime}
, then
Z=Z^{(i)}
. If they differ, then the difference in the arguments of
h
is
1
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\bigl(h(Z)-h(Z^{(i)})\bigr)^{2}=2p(1-p)\mathbb{E}\bigl(h(T_{i}+1)-h(T_{i})\bigr)^{2}.

Hence

\text{Var}(h(Z))\leqslant Mp(1-p)\mathbb{E}\bigl(h(T+1)-h(T)\bigr)^{2}

where
T\sim\text{Binomial}(M-1,p)
.

Note that

h(s+1)-h(s)=(1-p)\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Binomial}(N-M,p)=r-s-1\right).
Now we need to bound this quantity.

Define the event

\mathcal{T}=\{T:|T-(M-1)p|\leqslant\sqrt{M}\log n\}.

By Chernoff’s bound in Lemma
2
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}^{c})\leqslant e^{-\Omega(\log^{2}n)}.

For
T\in\mathcal{T}
, let
q=r-T-1
.Then we have

\displaystyle q-(N-M)p

\displaystyle=r-T-1-(N-M)p

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}-Np+O(1)-\bigl(T-(M-1)p\bigr)

\displaystyle=N\Delta_{n}+O(\sqrt{M}\log n).

Since
\sqrt{M}\log n=o(N\Delta_{n})
, Lemma
10
can be applied.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Binomial}(N-M,p)=q\right)\leqslant N^{-1/2}n^{-\alpha+o(1)}

uniformly on
\mathcal{T}
.

Therefore,

|h(T+1)-h(T)|\leqslant N^{-1/2}n^{-\alpha+o(1)}

on
\mathcal{T}
. On
\mathcal{T}^{c}
, we use the trivial bound
|h(T+1)-h(T)|\leqslant 1
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl(h(T+1)-h(T)\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant N^{-1}n^{-2\alpha+o(1)}+e^{-\Omega(\log^{2}n)}

\displaystyle=N^{-1}n^{-2\alpha+o(1)}.

Consequently,

\text{Var}(h(Z))\leqslant\frac{M}{N}n^{-2\alpha+o(1)}.

Since
N=\Theta(n^{k})
,

\frac{M}{N}=O_{k}\left(n^{-\lceil d/2\rceil}\right).

Consequently, when
d>k
,

\text{Cov}(I_{u},I_{w})\leqslant n^{-\lceil d/2\rceil-2\alpha+o(1)}.

This is the first term in (1).

Case 2:

d\leqslant k

Now
u
and
w
are adjacent, so there is additional dependence:

w\in\mathcal{N}(u),u\in\mathcal{N}(w).

We remove this direct dependence by replacing the two cross-variables with independent copies.

Let
\widehat{\xi}_{u},\widehat{\xi}_{w}
be independent Bernoulli(
p
) variables, independent of all original variables. Define

\displaystyle J_{u}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if
$\xi_{u}=1$ or
$\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}(u)\setminus w}\xi_{x}+\widehat{\xi}_{w}\geqslant r$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and similarly,

\displaystyle J_{w}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if
$\xi_{w}=1$
or
$\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}(w)\setminus{u}}\xi_{x}+\widehat{\xi}_{u}\geqslant r$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Note that
J_{u}\overset{d}{=}I_{u},\qquad J_{w}\overset{d}{=}I_{w}.

After this replacement, the only variables shared by
J_{u}
and
J_{w}
are the common-neighbor variables
(\xi_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{C}}
.

Indeed, put
U=\mathcal{N}(u)\setminus(\mathcal{C}\cup{w})
and
W^{\prime}=\mathcal{N}(w)\setminus(\mathcal{C}\cup{u})
. Then
U\cap W^{\prime}=\emptyset
.

Conditional on the variables in
\mathcal{C}
,
J_{u}
depends on
\xi_{u},\widehat{\xi}_{w}
, and
(\xi_{x})_{x\in U}
and
J_{w}
depends on
\xi_{w},\widehat{\xi}_{u}
, and
(\xi_{x})_{x\in W^{\prime}}
.

These two collections are disjoint and independent. Therefore, exactly as above,

\text{Cov}(J_{u},J_{w})\leqslant n^{-\lceil d/2\rceil-2\alpha+o(1)}.

It remains to compare
I_{u},I_{w}
with
J_{u},J_{w}
.

The variables
I_{u}
and
J_{u}
can differ only when changing the neighbor bit
\xi_{w}
to
\widehat{\xi}_{w}
changes whether
u
reaches threshold.

Let

R_{u}:=\sum_{x\in N(u)\setminus{w}}\xi_{x}\sim\text{Binomial}(N-1,p).

Then
I_{u}\neq J_{u}
can occur only if all three of the following happen:

\xi_{u}=0,\qquad R_{u}=r-1,\qquad\xi_{w}\neq\widehat{\xi}_{w}.

In fact, these conditions are also sufficient. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(I_{u}\neq J_{u})

\displaystyle=(1-p)\mathbb{P}\left(\operatorname{Bin}(N-1,p)=r-1\right)\mathbb{P}(\xi_{w}\neq\widehat{\xi}_{w})

\displaystyle=2p(1-p)^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(\operatorname{Binomial}(N-1,p)=r-1\right).

Now
r-1-(N-1)p=N\Delta_{n}+O(1).
By Lemma
10
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\operatorname{Binomial}(N-1,p)=r-1\right)\leqslant N^{-1/2}n^{-\alpha+o(1)}.

Since
N^{-1/2}=n^{-k/2+o(1)}
,

\mathbb{P}(I_{u}\neq J_{u})\leqslant n^{-k/2-\alpha+o(1)}.

The same bound holds for
w
:

\mathbb{P}(I_{w}\neq J_{w})\leqslant n^{-k/2-\alpha+o(1)}.

Since
J_{u}=I_{u}
and
J_{w}=I_{w}
,

\operatorname{Cov}(I_{u},I_{w})-\operatorname{Cov}(J_{u},J_{w})=\mathbb{E}[I_{u}I_{w}]-\mathbb{E}[J_{u}J_{w}].

If
a,b,c,d\in\{0,1\}
, we have

|ab-cd|\leqslant|a-c|+|b-d|.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}[I_{u}I_{w}]-\mathbb{E}[J_{u}J_{w}]\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}|I_{u}-J_{u}|+\mathbb{E}|I_{w}-J_{w}|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(I_{u}\neq J_{u})+\mathbb{P}(I_{w}\neq J_{w})

\displaystyle\leqslant n^{-k/2-\alpha+o(1)}.

Therefore, for
d\leqslant k

\operatorname{Cov}(I_{u},I_{w})\leqslant n^{-\lceil d/2\rceil-2\alpha+o(1)}+n^{-k/2-\alpha+o(1)}.

For
d>k
, the second term is absent. Hence

\operatorname{Cov}(I_{u},I_{w})\leqslant n^{-\lceil d/2\rceil-2\alpha+o(1)}+\mathbb{I}_{\{d\leqslant k\}}n^{-k/2-\alpha+o(1)}.\qed

Recall
S_{x}:=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(x)}I_{u}
and let
d_{H}(u,w)=d
for
1\leqslant d\leqslant 2k
. Consider
\text{Var}(S_{x})
and we have

\displaystyle\text{Var}(S_{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,w\in\mathcal{N}(x)}\text{Cov}(I_{u},I_{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{2k}N\min\left\{\binom{n}{d},N\right\}\text{Cov}(I_{u},I_{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{2k}O(n^{k+\min\{d,k\}})\text{Cov}(I_{u},I_{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{k}O(n^{k+d})[n^{-\frac{d}{2}-2\alpha+o(1)}+n^{-k/2-\alpha+o(1)}]+\sum_{d=k+1}^{2k}O(n^{2k})n^{-\frac{d}{2}-2\alpha+o(1)}

\displaystyle=O(n^{3k/2-2\alpha+o(1)})+O(n^{3k/2-\alpha+o(1)})

\displaystyle=o((N\tau_{n})^{2}),

where
(N\tau_{n})^{2}=\Theta(n^{2k-2\alpha+o(1)})
, and
\alpha<\frac{k}{2}
.

Note that we have

\mathbb{E}(S_{x})=N\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{C\sqrt{\log n}}{n^{k/2}}\right)+N\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{C\sqrt{\log n}}{n^{k/2}}\right)\tau_{n}

and thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{x}\leqslant\frac{N}{2})

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}(|S_{x}-\mathbb{E}S_{x}|\geqslant\frac{1}{3}N\tau_{n})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\text{Var}(S_{x})}{(\frac{1}{3}N\tau_{n})^{2}}

\displaystyle=o(1).

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(x\in A_{2})=1-o(1)
.
∎

Now we are ready to state the next lemma.

Lemma 17
.

Let
\delta>0
be a real number and
k\geqslant 2
be an integer. Assume that with the initial infection probability
p=\frac{1}{2}-\left(\frac{\sqrt{kk!}}{2}-\epsilon\right)\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{n^{k/2}}
we can establish
\mathbb{P}\left(x\in A_{2,\frac{N}{2}+\binom{3k}{2k}}\right)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}+\delta
. Then there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(x\in A_{5,\frac{N}{2}})\geqslant 1-e^{-cn}.

Proof.

Let
x\in V(Q_{k,n})
. First, assume that
k
divides
n
. Then there exists a partition
S(x,k)=\cup_{i=1}^{m}B_{i}
such that
\text{supp}(x)\cap\text{supp}(y)=\emptyset
for all
x,y\in B_{i}
and all
i\in[m]
. Moreover,
|B_{i}|=\frac{n}{k}
for all
i\in[m]
and
m=\binom{n-1}{k-1}
.

Indeed, we can consider
S(x,k)
as the edges of a
k
-uniform hypergraph on
n
vertices. According to Theorem
13
, such a partition of
S(x,k)
exists given that
k|n
.

Now assume that
k
does not divide
n
. By Theorem
14
, given positive integers
b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{m}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}=\binom{n}{k}
, the family
\binom{[n]}{k}
can be partitioned into

B_{1}\cup B_{2}\cdots\cup B_{m},

with
|B_{i}|=b_{i}
where each
B_{i}
, regarded as a
k
-uniform hypergraph, is almost regular. We can choose the
b_{i}
’s so that
b_{i}\in\{q,q-1\}
.

Note that every
B_{i}
is a matching. Indeed,

\frac{k|B_{i}|}{n}\leqslant\frac{kq}{n}<1,

since
k
does not divide
n
. Because
B_{i}
is almost regular, all its vertex degrees differ by at most
1
. Since the average degree is less than
1
, every vertex has degree
0
or
1
. Therefore each
B_{i}
consists of pairwise disjoint
k
-sets. Thus we have for each
i

\left||B_{i}|-\frac{n}{k}\right|\leqslant 1.

From Lemma
2
we have

\mathbb{P}(|B_{i}\cap A_{0}|\leqslant\frac{n}{2k}-n^{\frac{1.1}{2}})\leqslant\exp(-cn^{0.1}).

Let
J(i,x)
denote this event does not happen, i.e.,
|B_{i}\cap A_{0}|\geqslant\frac{n}{2k}-n^{\frac{1.1}{2}}
. From now on, we assume that
J(i,x)
holds. Note that this event will help us to estimate the size of the set
S
(defined below) which is crucial for our proof.

There exists an
i\in[m]
such that there is a set
S\subset B_{i}\backslash A_{0}
of size
|S|=\frac{n}{2k}+1
. If such a set
S
cannot be found, this implies that for every
i\in[m]
, there does not exist an
S
such that
S\subset B_{i}\setminus A_{0}
and
|S|=\frac{n}{2k}+1
. That means for every
i\in[m]
every (
\frac{n}{2k}
+1)-element subset of
B_{i}
contains at least one element of
A_{0}
. Therefore for every
i\in[m]
,
|B_{i}\cap A_{0}|\geqslant\frac{n}{2k}+1
, then
x\in A_{1}
, and we are done. Indeed, since each support has size
k
,
kb_{i}\leqslant n
. Thus

\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{k}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}\leqslant m\frac{n}{2k}

Since the “+1” term will not affect the calculations, we can safely assume that there exists a set
S\subset B_{i}\backslash A_{0}
with
|S|=\frac{n}{2k}
. Then we will show that
S\cap A_{4}=\emptyset
is highly unlikely.

Let
T(k)=\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S(x,2k)
. Denote
T_{i}(k)=\{y\in T(k):|\mathcal{N}(y)\cap S|=i\}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. It is easy to see that for
k\geqslant 4

|T_{1}(k)|=\binom{n-k}{k}\frac{n}{2k}-2\binom{\frac{n}{2k}}{2}=\frac{n^{k+1}}{2kk!}-\frac{(3k-1)n^{k}}{4k!}+\frac{(k-1)(27k^{2}-19k+2)}{48k!}n^{k-1}+O(n^{k-2})

and

|T_{2}(k)|=\binom{\frac{n}{2k}}{2}=\frac{n^{2}}{8k^{2}}-\frac{n}{4k}.

For
k=2,3,
we need more precise calculations for
T_{1}(k)
. We have

|T_{1}(2)|=\frac{n^{3}}{8}-\frac{11}{16}n^{2}+n,

and

|T_{1}(3)|=\frac{n^{4}-12n^{3}+46n^{2}-54n}{36},

Let
|T_{1}(k)\cap A_{3}|=a(k)
and
|T_{2}(k)\cap A_{3}|=b(k)
. Note that if
S\cap A_{4}=\emptyset
, then for
k\geqslant 3

a(k)+2b(k)<\frac{n}{2k}\left(\frac{1}{2}N\right)=\frac{n^{k+1}}{4kk!}+\frac{(3-k)n^{k}}{8k(k-1)!}+\frac{3k^{2}-19k+50}{96k(k-2)!}n^{k-1}+O(n^{k-2})

and for
k=2
,

a(2)+2b(2)<\frac{n^{3}}{16}+\frac{n^{2}}{16}

We will separate the cases where
k\geqslant 4
,
k=3
, and
k=2
for the clarity even though they are almost the same.

Case 1
:
k\geqslant 4
.

\mathbb{E}|T_{1}\cap A_{0}|=\frac{n^{k+1}}{4kk!}-\frac{(3k-1)n^{k}}{8k!}+O(n^{k-1}\sqrt{\log n}).

By Lemma
2
, that there exits
c>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(||T_{1}\cap A_{0}|-\frac{n^{k+1}}{4kk!}|\geqslant kn^{k})\leqslant\exp(-cn^{k-1}).

Let
G(S)
denote this event doesn’t happen, i.e.,
||T_{1}\cap A_{0}|-\frac{n^{k+1}}{4kk!}|\leqslant kn^{k}
and we have
\mathbb{P}(G(S))\leqslant 1-\exp(-cn^{k-1})
.

From now on, we assume that the event
G(S)
holds. If
|T_{1}\cap A_{3}\backslash A_{0}|\geqslant 2kn^{k}
, then
a\geqslant\frac{n^{k+1}}{4kk!}+kn^{k}
and thus
S\cap A_{4}\neq\emptyset
.

Claim 18
.

If
G(S)
holds, then

\mathbb{P}(|T_{1}\cap A_{3}\backslash A_{0}|=O(n^{k}))\leqslant\exp(-c\delta^{2}\epsilon^{\prime}n).

Proof.

Consider a bipartite graph

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{2b_{n}^{2}s^{2}}{Ms+2(m_{2}+(n-8)m_{3})}\right)\exp\left(\left(s-\frac{2m_{2}}{M_{2}}+o(1)\right)\log n\right)

\mathbb{P}(\exists x\in A_{3}\setminus A_{2})=o(1).\qed

Proof.

25

We will first show that for Boot(

\mathbb{P}(x\in A_{2})=\frac{1}{2}+o(1).

For a fixed vertex

\mathbb{P}(E^{c}|x\notin A_{0})=\mathbb{P}(E^{c})=o(1)
.

Now let us analyze the size of

. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(y\in(A_{1}\setminus A_{0})|x\notin A_{0}\right)
