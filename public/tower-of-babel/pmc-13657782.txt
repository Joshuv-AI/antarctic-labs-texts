The use of intraoperative parathyroid hormone assay in predicting the success and reduce morbidity of targeted minimal invasive parathyroidectomy for primary hyperparathyroidism
Authors: Františka Hanzalová, Jiří Hložek, Jan Rotnágl, Richard Holý, Jiří Soukup, Adéla Rozehnalová, Michal Pancák, Jaromír Astl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is caused by overproduction of parathyroid hormone (PTH) by a hyperfunctioning parathyroid gland (most commonly a parathyroid adenoma, PTA) or glands. Historically, the most commonly described surgical approach has been bilateral neck exploration (BNE). This study focuses on the outcomes of targeted minimally invasive parathyroidectomy [unilateral neck exploration (UNE), focused exploration (FE)—including endoscope and/or exoscope-assisted parathyroidectomy]. Intraoperative measurement of parathyroid hormone levels (ioPTH) is generally considered confirmation of complete removal of all hyperfunctioning parathyroid tissue. Intraoperative surgical success is evaluated using the modified Miami criteria, with a reported sensitivity of 97%. The aim of this study is to determine the safety FE (targeted/focused procedure), sensitivity, specificity and accuracy of ioPTH measurement, based on samples collected at 5, and 15 minutes after removal of pathological tissue. The study further aims to identify the most appropriate evaluation criteria supported the Miami criteria with the high sensitivity and predictive value supported for surgical success of focused/targeted) parathyroidectomy (morbidity including). Study design is a retrospective analysis of minimally invasive parathyroidectomy (MIPT) procedures indicated for primary PHPT performed between 2020 and 2024.MethodsThe analyzed cohort included 92 evaluated patients. The analyzed data included preoperative PTH levels, ioPTH levels measured at 5, 10 and 15 minutes and PTH levels on the third postoperative day. The analyze used statistic: sensitivity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy were evaluated for the various ioPTH criteria. Confidence intervals were calculated using a confusion matrix. We used the Chi-squared test, F-test to compare the time-intervals and box graphs used for presentation. We used the MedCalc software.ResultsFor data analysis, six criteria for evaluating the intraoperative decline in PTH levels were defined. The criterion with the high sensitivity (99%) was assessment of PTH levels 10 minutes after resection of the PTA, combined with evaluation of the decrease in PTH levels between 5 and 15 minutes after resection.ConclusionsThe focused (targeted) lateral (back door) approach parathyroidectomy is the safe and effective therapy of primary PHPT based on the results of very low incidence of RLN palsy (0.0%) in presented cohort. We would like present an additional sensitive criterium for assessment of the procedure function effect supporting Miami criteria’s using ioPTH measurement: (I) 0–10: a ≥50% decrease in PTH or a decrease to within the normal range; (II) 5–15: a ≥50% decrease in PTH or a decrease to within the normal range. This both parameters predict surgical success with a sensitivity of 98.8% and a low false-negative (FN) rate of 1.1%. The PPV was 0.88. Both used intervals eliminated the influence of the spike effect on the prediction of treatment success.Keywords: Parathyroidectomy, parathyroid hormone (PTH), intraoperative monitoring, intraoperative PTH assay

Introduction

Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is the third most common endocrine disorder and the most common cause of hypercalcemia (1,2). It is caused by overproduction of parathyroid hormone (PTH) by a parathyroid adenoma (PTA) (approximately 90% of cases involve a solitary adenoma, while multiple adenomas occur in about 3% of cases) or by parathyroid hyperplasia (10%). Very rarely, overproduction of PTH is caused by parathyroid carcinoma (approximately 1%) (1,3-5).

The incidence of PHPT is approximately 28 new cases per 100,000 population per year. The disease affects women more frequently, with a peak incidence between 50 and 60 years of age (4).

Surgical removal of hyperfunctioning parathyroid glands is the definitive first-line treatment (4). The “gold standard” open approach to parathyroidectomy for PHPT has been bilateral neck exploration (BNE) with excision of all pathologically enlarged parathyroid glands, achieving a success rate of approximately 95–99.5% (6-9). More recently, targeted minimally invasive parathyroidectomy (TE) has become increasingly preferred. This shift has been facilitated by advances in preoperative localization imaging techniques and their widespread availability (7,10). Niederle et al. [2026] summarized four study compared extended BNE with unilateral and/or targeted approaches worldwide and yielded identical postoperative results BNE vs. TE (postoperative success rate 99.5%, permanent hypocalcemia 0.3%, permanent vocal cord paralysis 0.8% and 0.3% mortality) (10).

Many authors consider the use of intraoperative parathyroid hormone (ioPTH) measurement an essential component of targeted parathyroidectomy to confirm complete removal of all hyperfunctioning parathyroid tissue. IoPTH measurement is based on the short half-life of PTH, which is approximately 5 minutes. Because of this short half-life, a decline in PTH levels in peripheral venous blood can be observed intraoperatively after excision of the hyperfunctioning parathyroid gland. This enables assessment of surgical success before completion of the procedure with a reported sensitivity of up to 97% (9).

The method of ioPTH measurement was first described by Irvin et al. in 1991. This work defined the so-called Miami criterion for intraoperative assessment of parathyroidectomy success in PHPT. The Miami criteria specify blood sampling and determination of PTH levels preoperatively, immediately before excision of the pathological parathyroid gland and at 5 and 10 minutes after excision of the pathological parathyroid gland.

Surgical success following parathyroidectomy for hyperparathyroidism is assessed according to the Miami criterion. The criterion is defined as a ≥50% reduction in PTH levels measured 10 minutes after excision of the pathological parathyroid gland compared with the highest baseline value, either the preoperative level or the level measured 5 minutes after resection. If an adequate decline in PTH levels is not observed, an additional sample is obtained at 20 minutes. Alternatively, further neck exploration is undertaken (6-8,11). Analyzers for ioPTH measurement have been commercially available since 1996, with results typically available within 8–15 minutes (8,12).

In recent years, some authors have suggested that in cases where a parathyroid gland is concordantly localized by two different preoperative imaging modalities, the benefit of ioPTH measurement is marginal (9). The Stabiano et al. [2025] start the multicentric a fully powered trial which will standardize and identify the role for IOPTH monitoring in optimizing surgical outcomes for secondary and tertiary hyperparathyroidism (13). Other authors consider the interpretation of ioPTH results to be subjective, as there is no consensus on the optimal algorithm for analysis of test results (10). Accordingly, various criteria have been described in the literature with the aim of defining a highly predictive parameter for surgically effective treatment of PHPT (14).

Among these, the Miami criterion has been reported to have the highest sensitivity. In addition, the Mayo, Vienna, Rome, and Halle criteria have also been described (8,11,14,15). These criteria differ in sensitivity and specificity and may even increase false-positive or false-negative (FN) rates. This may lead either to premature termination of the procedure or to unnecessary neck exploration, thereby increasing operative risk and prolonging operative time (16), as also confirmed by other large studies with substantial numbers of observations (8,14,16).

In the literature were not present the analyze of sensitivity, specificity and predictive value of time intervals. The recommended in Miami protocol based on 0–10 interval, Vienna criteria’s on 0–15 interval. The interactions between the PTH levels in each sampling time [0, 5, 10, 15] and modified criteria’s for success rate of parathyroidectomy was not present in literature (9,13,15-17).

Aim

The aim of this study was to perform a retrospective analysis of PTH levels and to define criteria for predicting the effectiveness of TE focused (targeted) minimal invasive parathyroidectomy, including determination of the sensitivity, specificity and accuracy of predicting surgical success based on ioPTH measurement. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2026-0312/rc).

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Military University Hospital Prague (No. 108/16-92/2021). All participating institutions were informed of and agreed to the study. Informed consent was obtained from all patients in the study before treatment and the collection of all samples for PTH analysis. All patients underwent focused (targeted) minimal invasive parathyroidectomy between January 2020 and December 2024 at the Department of Otorhinolaryngology and Maxillofacial Surgery, 3rd Faculty of Medicine, Charles University, and the Military University Hospital Prague and Institute for Postgraduate Education Prague, Department of Otorhinolaryngology, for PHPT.

Only anonymized data were included in the analysis. All procedures were performed by experienced surgeons with experience of more than 1,000 thyroid and/or parathyroid surgeries. Used surgery procedures were in hole cohort TE (focused targeted minimal invasive unilateral parathyroidectomy) and back door approach was the targeted (focused) minimal invasive lateral (back door) approach parathyroidectomy only.

Inclusion criteria: PHPT only, the PTH level must be ≥7 pmol/L (the base line PTH level sample were checked 24 h before the start of procedure), serum calcium ≥2.55 mmol/L, positive MIBI scintigraphy, positive ultrasonography and/or positive magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) findings.

Exclusion criteria: secondary or tertiary hyperparathyroidism, MEN I syndrome patients, dialysis treatment, bisphosphonate therapy, and/or hemolysis of the postoperative blood sample obtained after parathyroid resection.

Patients were indicated for surgery based on positive findings on ultrasonography, MIBI scintigraphy and/or MRI, CT or choline PET imaging. All patients underwent focused (targeted) minimal invasive parathyroidectomy including back-door parathyroidectomy.

Six criteria based on predefined time-dependent declines in PTH levels were established. For each criterion, sensitivity, specificity and predictive accuracy for surgical treatment effectiveness in PHPT were assessed.

In all patients operated on for PHPT, PTH levels were measured preoperatively at least 15 minutes before the start of surgery. Intraoperative measurements were obtained at 5, 10 and 15 minutes after excision of the parathyroid gland, according to the surgeon’s instructions. Postoperative PTH levels were measured on the first and third postoperative days. All blood samples were obtained from peripheral venous blood. Intraoperative samples were collected from peripheral veins of the lower extremities in order to maintain sterility of the surgical field. Sampling on the third postoperative day was performed prior to patient discharge. The last sampling was performed 3–6 months after the primary surgery procedure (checking of effect for PTH level in physiology range).

PTH levels were determined using the Elecsys PTH assay, an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA), performed on Cobas E analyzers. The excised pathological parathyroid gland was sent for routine histopathological examination.

A retrospective analysis of measured PTH levels was performed. We assessed whether the intraoperative decline in PTH levels could predict definitive surgical success. For this purpose, six criteria were defined during retrospective data analysis. These criteria were based on the Miami criteria and extended by modifications of the Miami criteria described in the literature.

To assess the intraoperative decline in PTH levels, a preoperative sample, hereafter referred to as “0”, and samples obtained at 5, 10 and 15 minutes after excision of the parathyroid gland were used. Definitive surgical success was determined using the PTH level measured on the third postoperative day and 3–6 months later. Parathyroidectomy was considered definitively successful if the PTH level on the third postoperative day was below the upper limit of normal (<6.9 pmol/L). This outcome was subsequently correlated with the postoperative decrease in serum calcium levels and with the final histopathological findings.

Morbidity of surgical treatment was focused on permanent recurrent laryngoscopy-documented recurrent laryngoscopy-associated nerve (RLN) palsy and permanent hypocalcemia, with serum calcium levels falling to 1.95 mmol/L and detected for 6 months after the procedure.

Statistical analysis

Sensitivity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy were evaluated for the various ioPTH criteria. Confidence intervals were calculated using a confusion matrix. Metric parameters were described by median and mean, and minimum and maximum values. We used the Chi-squared test, F-test to compare the time-intervals and box graphs used for presentation. We used the MedCalc software.

Criterion 5–15 is able to identify patients who achieve a 50% decline in PTH levels within 10 minutes, which corresponds to an adequate decrease according to the PTH half-life and the Miami criterion. In these patients, a transient intraoperative spike in PTH levels probably occurred, resulting in a delayed onset of the decline. As these patients would be classified as negative when applying Criterion 0–10, the combined Criterion 0–10 or 5–15 was defined. Using this combined criterion, the resulting sensitivity was 85.2% with a FN rate of 13%.

In the Criterion 0–10 or 5–15 group, a relatively high proportion of FN patients (13%) still remained. During data exploration, we observed that in some patients, PTH levels decreased to within the normal range but did not meet the ≥50% decline threshold for either criterion (0–10 or 5–15).

We defined Criterion 0–10 or 5–15 or normal range. Applying the criterion of a decrease in PTH levels to within the normal range increased sensitivity to 98.8% and reduced the FN rate to 1.1%.

Discussion

The Miami criterion is reported as the criterion with the highest sensitivity for intraoperative assessment of parathyroidectomy success. It is defined as a decrease in PTH levels of more than 50% at 10 minutes after excision of the pathological parathyroid gland compared with the highest measured baseline value (PTH level measured preoperatively and intraoperatively immediately before excision), with a reported sensitivity of 97% (8,15).

In agreement with the available literature, Criterion 0–10 demonstrated lower sensitivity than the Miami criterion. In the Miami criterion, the highest PTH level from both the preoperative sample and the intraoperative sample obtained immediately before excision of the pathological parathyroid gland is used as the baseline value for assessment of intraoperative success. In our cohort, the sensitivity of Criterion 0–10 was 74.1%, and this criterion was associated with a high FN rate of 22.8%.

For Criterion 0–15, sensitivity increased as expected based on the principle of hormone half-life measurement, reaching 88.9%, while the FN rate decreased to 9.8%.

The sensitivity of Criterion 0–10 was 23% lower than that reported for the Miami criteria, while the sensitivity of Criterion 0–15 was 8% lower. The difference between the sensitivity observed in our cohort and that reported for the Miami criteria is likely attributable to the absence of an ioPTH measurement obtained immediately before excision of the pathological parathyroid gland (16). According to the Miami criteria, the highest value from the preoperative sample and the intraoperative sample obtained immediately before excision is used as the baseline, as a so-called PTH spike may occur during surgery. This phenomenon refers to an increase in PTH levels between the start of surgery and excision of the parathyroid gland and has been reported in up to 14% of patients (13).

According to some authors, PTH spikes are caused by manipulation of the parathyroid gland (6), whereas others consider them unpredictable and not reliably identifiable in advance (13). When only the preoperative PTH level is used as the baseline value, these patients may contribute to a high proportion of FN results in our cohort.

If patients with a PTH spike are present in our cohort, they are likely to be classified as intraoperatively negative according to Criterion 0–10, because the baseline PTH level is underestimated.

Given the high proportion of FN patients (22.8%) when using the basic Criterion 0–10, we further analyzed patients with negative intraoperative results. Assuming that a PTH spike occurs in approximately 14% of patients (18) and is not detected when applying Criterion 0–10, we addressed this limitation in the present study by comparing PTH levels between 5 and 15 minutes after excision and assessing a 50% decline over this interval. This approach allowed us to evaluate and define Criterion 5–15.

Patel et al. [2023] (19) analyzed a 15-year cohort of 849 patients operated on for PHPT. Physiological calcium level was achieved in 96.4% of patients and the use of an intraoperative PTH assay showed a sensitivity of 96.8%, a specificity of 83.2% and a PPV of 97.6%. Using a targeted surgical approach, which was also applied in the present study, but without intraoperative PTH assay, a curative outcome was achieved in 94.1% of patients. The results of our study are comparable to this large cohort in terms of effectiveness and PPV of the evaluated intraoperative PTH analysis intervals.

The results of this study, demonstrating PPV when comparing preoperative PTH levels with levels measured 10 minutes after removal of hyperfunctioning tissue, are also supported by the publication by Wächter and Bartsch [2025] (20). In that study, similar sensitivity was demonstrated when comparing PTH levels obtained hours before surgery and immediately before incision with levels measured 15 minutes after removal of hyperfunctioning tissue. These findings therefore support the conclusions drawn for the criteria described in the present study.

Medas et al. [2021] (21) confirmed these findings in a meta-analysis of 28 studies including 13,323 operated patients. They reported surgical failure in 3.2% of procedures using intraoperative PTH assay, compared with a 5.8% failure rate in the control group without intraoperative PTH measurement. Similar results were reported by Lorenz et al. [2020] (22) and by Hargitai et al. (23), whose findings are fully comparable with and consistent with the results of the present study.

The meta-analysis published by Quinn et al. [2021] (9) concluded that the implementation of ioPTH measurement increases the incidence of BNE. This finding cannot be confirmed by the present study; however, our analysis is based on a cohort of 92 operations, whereas the meta-analysis included nearly 3,000 procedures from 12 studies. Similar conclusions were also reported by Vaghaiwalla et al. [2023] (24) and Wolf et al. [2025] (25).

The results of published meta-analyses, as well as the findings of the present study, are further supported by the fact that intraoperative PTH measurement is included in recommendations and clinical guidelines for the surgical treatment of PHPT (26-28). These guidelines build on criteria described in the literature, including the Miami, Vienna and Halle criteria (17,29).

The results of data analysis from our cohort show that a decline in PTH levels after excision of the pathological parathyroid gland is a highly reliable predictor of surgical success when appropriate criteria are applied. In addition to the required magnitude of PTH decline within a defined time interval, the sensitivity of a given criterion is substantially influenced by its ability to capture a potential PTH spike (30). It is also affected by inclusion of patients in whom PTH levels decrease to within the normal range already during parathyroidectomy. The criteria proposed in this study reduce the influence of the so-called PTH spike effect as described by Riss et al. [2009] (18). Measurement of PTH levels before and after removal of hyperfunctioning tissue does not replace preoperative localization of parathyroid glands using near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence imaging techniques (31,32).

In the studied cohort, morbidity of the recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) was monitored. The incidence of temporary RLN palsy was 0.86%, and the incidence of permanent palsy or paralysis of the RLN was 0%. These results indicate a substantial reduction in morbidity (1,3,33-36). Assessment of RLN function was performed routinely by laryngoscopy examination (3). Among the 115 nerves exposed during surgery, transient palsy was observed in a single case (0.86%), with a duration of 4 weeks. No cases of permanent palsy were identified in the cohort (0%). These outcomes are comparable to, or better than, those reported in the literature (3,32-34).

The surgical approach in the study was based on focused exploration (FE). The many authors used the bilateral parathyroid exploration (BE) like the standard surgical options for patients with PHPT. Nigham et al. analyzed 12,743 patients from 19 studies compared the unilateral and bilateral approaches and have had found the similar recurrence rate, similar persistence PHPT and similar rates of reoperation (37). The other meta-analyses advocate the unilateral that focused approach with ioPTH assessment provides like procedure achieved the same long-term results compare to BNE (38). For that we used the unilateral focused approach (UNE, TE) with identification of gland pathology once and unilateral FE in cases of thyroidectomy with exploration of parathyroid gland for PHPT. The results in this study supported this approach like the procedure with similar results to published meta-analyses. The relative risk of recurrence, persistence, overall failure, reoperation, and morbidity of procedure in generally associated with either surgical approach is similar. On the second hand the focused parathyroid exploration based on the surgeon’s knowledge of embryology of pharyngeal pouch migration. The BNE is necessary for MEN I based PHPT only.

In eleven operated patients, no decrease in PTH levels was observed within 15 minutes after removal of the pathological parathyroid gland. In four of these patients, histopathological examination confirmed a PTA in three cases and/or parathyroid gland hyperplasia. These cases can be interpreted as instances of detection of a PTH spike phenomenon following manipulation of the gland (18). In two patients (redo-surgery incidence 1.8%), reoperation was performed as a second-stage procedure because no PTA had been removed. In these cases, the pathological parathyroid gland was subsequently identified in an ectopic location—in front the scaleni muscle area in two patients and intrathyroidal location in one patient. We did not indicate an extension BNE of the surgery, given the suspicion of an intrathyroidal location of the PTA.

The literature does not describe the sensitivity and specificity of ioPTH for each time interval precisely. Barczynski et al. [2009] analyzed at least all recommended criteria (Miami, Vienna, Hale, Jena), so all described the need to achieve a reduction in PTH levels of 50% and/or within the physiological range without comparing time intervals (29). Similar results presented the literature for BNE, UNE and any for OMIP, MIVAPT—VAP techniques (10,28,29,39,40)

Lori et al. [2025] reported a PPV of 97.3%, NPV of 15%, and accuracy of 80% for ioPTH using a 10-minute interval to the normal range of PTH using the Clopper-Pearson method at 95% confidence intervals (39). These results were compared with the data of Barczynski et al. [2009] and yielded similar results. Individual time intervals and their comparisons were not performed (29). To this end, we performed an analysis at each time interval to compare the PPV and NPV of strong predictions of surgical outcomes of targeted minimally invasive parathyroidectomy in the treatment of PHPT, our results being in line with the recommendations in Italy and the UK (41,42). The strongest results were obtained with the Miami Modified Criteria for 10 minutes (sensitivity 91.36%, specificity 72.73%, and accuracy 89.13%) and in the interval 5–15 minutes after removal of the enlarged hyperfunctional parathyroid gland, the sensitivity was 98.8%, specificity 63.64%, and accuracy was 94.6%. The results of the presented study eliminated the effect of PTH spikes (43).

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design inherently carries a risk of selection bias and incomplete data. In addition, procedures were performed by different surgeons with varying levels of experience, which may have influenced intraoperative decision-making and outcomes. These factors may have affected both diagnostic accuracy and clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

The focused (targeted) lateral (back door) approach parathyroidectomy is the safe and effective therapy of PHPT based on the results of very low incidence of RLN palsy (0.0%) in presented cohort. Using of focused (targeted) lateral beck door approach parathyroidectomy can reduce the surgical morbidity—recurrent nerves palsy, permanent hypocalcemia associated to hypoparathyroidism and mortality follow the procedure compared to UNE or BNE.

We would like added an adherent criterion to Miami criteria’s of ioPTH measurement for an easer predicting of successful procedure in PHPT treatment using focused (targeted) parathyroidectomy (UNE):

0–10: a ≥50% decrease in PTH or a decrease to within the normal range

0–10: a ≥50% decrease in PTH or a decrease to within the normal range

5–15: a ≥50% decrease in PTH or a decrease to within the normal range

5–15: a ≥50% decrease in PTH or a decrease to within the normal range

This both parameters predict surgical success with a sensitivity of 98.8% and a low FN rate of 1.1%. The PPV was 0.88 (P=0.05). The interval 5–15 can be use for the sensitivity compare the traditional Miami protocol. The practical benefit of using the protocol with the addition of 5–15 interval can supported by higher sensitivity and accuracy compared to the Miami protocol, as demonstrated by multiple studies ago. A reduction in the incidence of BNU and surgical failures to below 3.2% can be anticipated.

These evaluated criteria (time intervals) mitigate the impact of the PTH spike effect on outcome assessment and complement the modified Miami criteria and/or Vienna criteria’s. This evaluated criterion can be eliminating the spike effect and can be used for limitation of extent of parathyroid surgery in future. These facts have not been described in the literature.

On the other hand, in Italy and the United Kingdom, PTH administration is recommended according to the modified Miami criteria may lead to a reduction in morbidity, hypocalcemia (present 98.2%) and vocal cord paralysis (present 0%). Both used intervals eliminated the influence of the spike effect on the prediction of treatment success.
