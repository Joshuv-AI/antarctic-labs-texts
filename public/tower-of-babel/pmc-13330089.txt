The impact of chronic diseases on labour market exit: a difference-in-differences approach
Authors: Nga Chau My Ha, Carla Sabariego, Diana Pacheco Barzallo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prevalence of chronic diseases, also referred to as non-communicable diseases (NCDs), is rising worldwide, leading to an increasing number of people facing disabilities that impact their labour market participation. This study examines the effect of NCDs on labour market exit across European countries.MethodsData on individuals aged 55 to their respective national retirement age were collected from the Survey of Health, Ageing, and Retirement in Europe (2004–2020) and analysed using a staggered difference-in-differences approach.ResultsCompared to their healthy counterparts, individuals with an NCD were 16% points (pp) more likely to exit the labour market early, losing an average of 3.6 working months. We also document a severity gradient: the probability of premature exit rises from 7 pp for mild conditions to 15 pp for severe diagnoses. The effect was consistent across most European countries, except for Estonia. In some cases, NCDs influenced the decision of labour market exit but had little impact on total working time. Among the countries analysed, Slovenia showed the highest early exit risk and greatest working time loss, followed by Belgium and Italy. Overall, the annual economic burden associated with lost working years due to NCDs is estimated at €136 billion.ConclusionsIn conclusion, people with NCDs are more likely to stop working earlier due to disability. Rehabilitation measures targeting work outcomes should be better incorporated to support the participation of individuals in the labour market. Effective intervention can have a substantial return on investment, given the significant economic losses that impact individuals, their families, and the social and healthcare system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27541-8.Keywords: Labour market exit, Chronic diseases, Staggered difference-in-differences, Healthy ageing, Welfare systems

Background

Disability driven by the rising prevalence of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) [1] represents one of the most critical public health challenges facing ageing populations. The impact of this burden extends beyond individual health outcomes, as it strains healthcare systems through increased utilisation and places a heavy load on national economies by compromising workforce participation – especially through the major life decision to exit the labour market earlier, via disability pension or retirement. When this premature exit is combined with increasing life expectancies, it threatens the sustainability of numerous European pay-as-you-go pension systems. Despite many reforms intended to delay retirement [2], the gap between actual and statutory retirement ages persists [3, 4], implying that health limitations can override legislative incentives. Therefore, it is essential to quantify the effects of NCDs on labour market exit to understand the true extent of the problem and support sustainable policy formulation.

The decision to exit the labour market due to health reasons can have profound financial consequences for individuals, particularly since they may have already faced economic instability throughout their working lives. NCDs are known to reduce work productivity by increasing absenteeism and presenteeism rates [5] and decreasing working hours [6]. This productivity reduction lowers individual earnings, which in turn diminishes savings and retirement contributions – a critical issue in European systems, where pensions are largely earnings-based [4]. Consequently, individuals often face a difficult trade-off: under health-limited circumstances, some may have no choice but to retire early, a pathway more common among those with later disease onset or considerable savings [7, 8]. In contrast, others may be forced to postpone retirement to secure their financial future, often at the further expense of their health [9]. Ultimately, these decisions are also influenced by macro-level factors, such as country-specific labour market policies and social security frameworks [10, 11].

Given such diverse influencing factors, the effects of NCDs on labour market exit behaviour are complex and often inconsistent across studies [12, 13], which is, to some extent, also owing to the condition-specific characteristics of the studies [14–16]. Limited data also hinders research on the link between health issues and labour market exit decisions. Although longitudinal data, essential for causal analysis, were commonly used in addressing this topic, most were country-specific [14, 16], making it difficult to generalise the results across nations due to system-level differences, such as pension and welfare policies. Studies using European cross-national data were available; however, the focus was often on poor- or ill-health [13, 17], which is subjective and simultaneously involves multiple aspects of health.

Given the rising global burden of diseases coming from NCDs [18] and their potential to be barriers to continuing to work, especially among the older working population, knowledge about the impact of NCDs on labour market exit and its associated productivity loss is needed more than ever in guiding legislation and company regulations in addressing the ageing workforce. Such evidence is essential to support and inform the development and implementation of policies facilitating the working lives of persons with NCDs, which are often considered part of the group of “persons with disabilities” [19]. Through these strategies, better-targeted rehabilitation policies can be implemented to optimise the functioning of the working population with chronic conditions, which translates into employee well-being and productivity.

This paper’s main aim is to contribute to this literature by exploring the impact of chronic diseases on labour market exit among European workers aged 55 and over, using micro data from 12 countries, with the following purposes:

i.To estimate the changes in the risk of premature labour market exit due to being diagnosed with any chronic disease, distinguishing between transitions to early retirement versus disability pension, and to determine how this risk scales with the severity of the disease.

To estimate the changes in the risk of premature labour market exit due to being diagnosed with any chronic disease, distinguishing between transitions to early retirement versus disability pension, and to determine how this risk scales with the severity of the disease.

ii.To estimate the working years lost due to being diagnosed with any chronic disease and the associated societal productivity loss.

To estimate the working years lost due to being diagnosed with any chronic disease and the associated societal productivity loss.

Methods

Data

We analysed the data from the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), a longitudinal database providing individual-level information across 27 European countries and Israel. The data were collected biannually using a cross-national questionnaire developed by the SHARE research team, which follows an ex-ante harmonisation approach and is available online for all survey waves [20, 21]. For detailed information on data collection procedures and data comparability, please refer to the official SHARE documentation available at https://share-eric.eu/data/data-documentation.

All observations from the first eight waves (from 2004 to 2020) were included in the simplified dataset, easySHARE [22], which consists of a subset of variables from the main data files. This simplified version was used because it included all the individual characteristics required for the current analysis. We used the first seven waves (except for Wave 3 due to the different focus of the questionnaire) to collect information about the number of health conditions and the time of diagnosis. The two latest waves were not included to avoid the confounding effect of COVID-19. Due to data sufficiency, only twelve countries were included for country-specific analysis.

Our primary analysis restricts the sample to individuals aged 55 and over, who were active in the labour market and did not have NCD at baseline (i.e. the time point when they first participated in the survey). Although labour market exit (particularly via disability insurance) is possible at younger ages, we focus mainly on this age group because it represents the critical “early retirement window” in many European pension systems, where the interaction between health shocks and exit eligibility is most salient. Including younger workers (aged 50–54), who face stricter institutional barriers to exit, could dilute the estimates of this specific phenomenon. However, to address the broader definition of labour market exit, we perform a heterogeneity analysis on the 50–54 age group in section "Heterogeneity by age".

As a result, age-eligible respondents were selected if they responded as either “Employed or self-employed” or “Unemployed” to the survey question “Your current employment situation?” during their first wave. Individuals with unknown retirement status and those who had been diagnosed but had had fewer than three observations were excluded. Details about the sample construction process are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Regarding our age window, we acknowledge that in many European countries, statutory early retirement ages (ERA) typically begin around the age of 60 [23]. However, restricting the sample to the ERA-eligible population would introduce bias by ignoring premature labour market exit among younger workers. For these individuals, the primary exit pathway is disability benefits or disability pension. Therefore, our wide age range aims to capture this extensive margin response. We also validate this approach by checking the treatment effect among the cohorts below the statutory ERA as one of the heterogeneity analyses.

Key variables

We classified individuals with chronic diseases/NCDs or without NCDs based on their self-reported diagnosis (see Supplementary Table S2 for the list of NCDs) in the original seven waves. Specifically, once an individual reported having at least one diagnosis, that person was defined as treated (). The wave at the first diagnosis defined their treatment time group (e.g., individuals first diagnosed in Wave 3 are assigned ). The never-diagnosed were the control group C (i.e. for the never-treated).

The two main outcomes are the probability of having early labour market exit (i.e. ) and the related working years lost to early exit (i.e. ), due to having at least one chronic disease. Regarding the first outcome, to capture the full productivity loss associated with NCDs, we identified ‘early exit’ (i.e. = 1) as a transition to either “Retired” or “Permanently sick or disabled” status before reaching the normal retirement age (NRA). We employed this combined measure as both outcomes represent a permanent exit from the labour market, where the specific pathway – whether via disability benefits or early pension schemes – often depends on national institutional incentives rather than health status alone.

Other variables

The control variables (), which were fixed at the individuals’ first participating waves, included sex, age, country, number of children, partnership status, education level, baseline health status, baseline employment status, baseline income decile, and baseline ability to make ends meet. Since the data were collected at the household level, each household was assigned a unique identifier, which we later used as a clustering variable to adjust for within-household correlations.

Disease classification

Analysing each disease individually presents two econometric challenges: (1) comorbidity (i.e. high rates of multiple simultaneous diagnoses make it difficult to isolate single-disease coefficients), and (2) sample power (i.e. rare conditions provide insufficient observations for independent event studies).

To solve these issues, we adopted a severity-based classification strategy using disability weights from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study [1]. We grouped diagnoses into three groups as follows:

Empirical strategy

Identification strategy

To estimate the effect of chronic disease diagnosis on labour market exit, we employ a staggered Difference-in-Differences design using the approach proposed by Callaway and Sant’Anna [24]. The validity of this strategy relies on the assumption that, conditional on individual fixed effects and time trends, the timing of the formal diagnosis is quasi-exogenous to labour market withdrawal decisions. We argue that the timing of the diagnosis acts as a distinct “information and institutional shock” for three key reasons.

First, access to disability pensions or early retirement schemes in the included countries typically requires a formal medical confirmation. Subjective feelings of ill health are insufficient to trigger these institutional support mechanisms, and thus, the diagnosis represents the moment when the cost of exiting the labour market changes, serving as a sharp “treatment” event.

Second, while disease status in SHARE is self-reported, the specific chronic conditions examined in this study (e.g., cancer, stroke) typically require distinct clinical confirmation to be diagnosed. Unlike conditions defined primarily by subjective symptoms (e.g., general back pain, fatigue), these pathologies are difficult to self-diagnose. This requirement for professional diagnosis minimises the risk of “strategic diagnosis seeking”, where individuals might otherwise pursue an endogenous diagnosis to justify a pre-planned retirement.

Finally, we empirically validate the parallel trend assumption by examining the dynamic treatment effects in the period preceding the diagnosis, shown in the first event-study result. The absence of significant pre-trends confirms that individuals were not systematically exiting the labour market in anticipation of the health shock, supporting the causal interpretation of our estimates.

Estimation

We applied the staggered difference-in-differences (DiD) analysis described by Callaway and Sant’Anna in 2021. Intuitively, this method is intended to recover the counterfactual outcomes of those with NCDs in the absence of NCDs, using the observed outcomes of the never-treated control group. Specifically, at each time point , the average outcome of a specific treated group is estimated and compared to that of the period directly before being diagnosed (i.e. time point). The control group is also synthesised to be as similar to group regarding the characteristics as possible using inverse probability weighting (i.e. weighting observations by the reverse of their propensity score to make the distribution of comparable between the two treatment groups). The evolution in the average outcome from time to time for this control group is then estimated. The difference between the two evolutions for the two groups provides us with the group-time average treatment effect of interest.

All group-time ATTs are then weighted averaged to estimate the overall average treatment effect (). Group-time ATTs with the same length of exposure , after being aggregated and weighted by the relative size of each group (among those having the same length of exposure), provide the event-study estimates (i.e. the average treatment effect among those that have been diagnosed with at least one chronic disease for exactly periods) [25]. The event-study estimates at pre-diagnosis periods serve as a testing tool for scrutinising the common parallel trend assumption [26]. It is also worth mentioning that the period before diagnosis always served as the baseline to avoid the asymmetric construction of pre- and post-diagnosis coefficients [27]. A sensitivity analysis based on a relative magnitude framework was conducted to assess the robustness of the country-specific estimates to potential violations of the parallel trends assumption [28].

Results

Descriptive statistics

The final sample included 17,965 eligible observations of 8,144 individuals from twelve countries, including Austria, Belgium, the Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, France, Germany, Italy, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden, and Switzerland (Table 1)1. Overall, 52% were male, 25% were diagnosed with at least one NCD, and 14% reported early labour market exit. People with NCDs and without NCDs were quite similar regarding gender ratio, average number of children, average years of education, and income distribution. The age distribution of people with NCDs seemed to slightly shift as time progressed, while it was more stable for the non-treated group; however, both groups shared the same age range across all time points (see Supplementary Figure S1). Early exit was more common among those with NCDs, at 7.3%, compared with 5.7% for the non-NCDs group.

Descriptive statistics at baseline

The sample size from each country ranges from 447 in Slovenia to 891 in Germany. Men were more predominant in the samples of Italy and Spain. The distributions of education level, health status, employment status, and income decile were comparable between the two groups in most countries. Premature labour market withdrawal was more prevalent among those with NCDs, except for Estonia and France.

The changes in the risk of early retirement

Global event-study estimates

The event-study estimates (see Fig. 1) detailed the average effect of having an NCD diagnosis on the probability of labour market exit over time for the entire sample. Because the data were collected biannually, any two adjacent periods are two years apart. Specifically, the results showed that the risk of labour market exit increased by 7.3% points (pp) (95%CI: 5.3, 9.3) at the year of diagnosis. This number increased to 15.7 pp (95%CI: 12.7, 18.8) in the second following year and continues to peak at 20.8 pp (95%CI: 15.3, 26.3) in the fourth year and remains at 20.7 pp (95%CI: 10.1, 31.5) by the sixth year. The overall average treatment effect estimate showed that having an NCD diagnosis increases the risk of exiting early across the first six years of being diagnosed by, on average, 16.1 pp (95%CI: 13.7, 18.6).

Changes in the average probability of labour market exit due to having NCD diagnosis over time

Country-specific event-study estimates

Figure 2 presents the event-study estimates for the first outcome across twelve countries. Only periods with more than ten observations were depicted, and the overall average treatment effect was estimated based on the first two periods of diagnosis (i.e. the diagnosis time and the following period) due to the substantial variation in the number of observations from the second post-diagnosis period.

Changes in the average probability of labour market exit due to having NCD diagnosis across countries

Overall, the trends suggested that having at least one NCD triggered the decision to exit the labour market early in all observed countries. However, the effect size varied across countries. Particularly, people in Slovenia reported the highest increased risk of withdrawal. Compared to their healthy counterparts, Slovenian people with NCDs had, on average, a 26.0 pp higher risk of leaving the labour market early across the two years after the diagnosis (95%CI: 16.6, 34.7). In Estonia, the increased risk was the lowest, at 1.3 pp (95%CI: -0.6, 3.3).

Among the countries for which a third post-diagnosis period was observed, the early exiting patterns differed over time. In some countries, the longer people dealt with NCDs, the more likely they were to choose to leave the labour market (e.g., Demark, Germany), while in others, the risk of early exit increased during the first two to four years of diagnosis; then, by the sixth year, it began to drop, suggesting that the effect in these countries tended to weaken as the individuals got closer to retirement age (e.g., Belgium, Italy). However, sensitivity analyses (see Supplementary Figure S2) revealed that estimates for countries like Estonia, France, and Sweden were sensitive to modest violations of the parallel trend assumption, warranting cautious interpretation due to data limitations.

Heterogeneity by pathway of exit

To understand the mechanisms driving labour market withdrawal, we disaggregated the analysis by exit pathway. We first isolated the standard early retirement pathway by excluding individuals who transitioned to “permanently sick or disabled” status (Fig. 3, left). We found that the effect of diagnosis remained robust and statistically significant, with a magnitude similar to the main specification (15 pp vs. 16 pp), suggesting that the response was not driven solely by severe disability.

Event-study estimates of the impact of NCD diagnosis on labour market exit by pathway

Second, we explicitly modelled the transition to disability insurance. As a result, a chronic disease diagnosis led to an increase of approximately 2.0 pp in the risk of becoming “permanently sick or disabled” (Fig. 3, right), indicating that for a specific subset of patients, likely those with the most severe shocks, disability was one of the exit pathways.

Heterogeneity by disease classification

To investigate whether the magnitude of the chronic diseases drives the decision to leave the labour market, we stratified the analysis by disease severity. As shown in Fig. 4, we found a monotonic pattern in the immediate impact at the time of receiving the diagnosis. Specifically, the probability of early retirement increased by 15 pp for the severe group, 11 pp for the moderate group, and 7 pp for the mild group.

Event-study estimates of the impact of chronic disease diagnosis on labour market exit by disease severity

We tested the sensitivity of these results to potential violations of the parallel trend assumption (Supplementary Figure S3). It was shown that our estimates for all severity levels remained statistically significant even under conservative assumptions that allowed for post-diagnosis trend deviations up to twice the magnitude of the observed pre-trends.

The working years lost due to having chronic diseases

The average working time lost to early labour market exit due to having an NCD over the first two years after diagnosis varied across countries, as shown in Table 2. Specifically, the significant time lost was, on average, 3.6 months (95%CI: 3.0–4.3 months), ranging from 2.2 months in Sweden (95%CI: 0.4–4.0 months) to 12.6 months in Slovenia (95%CI: 7.3–18.0 months). Although NCDs triggered early exit in all European countries, their effect on reduced working time was insignificant in all included nations. Particularly, the estimated time lost to premature exit due to having an NCD in Germany, the Czech Republic, Estonia, and France were not statistically significant.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted several robustness checks to validate our main findings.

Heterogeneity by age

As mentioned, we extended our analysis to individuals aged 50–54, who face the strictest institutional barriers, as standard early retirement is typically unavailable and leaving via disability pension is the primary exit pathway. We observed an impact of 2.0 pp increase in the risk of labour market exit (Supplementary Figure S4). Despite facing higher hurdles, this result supports the inclusion of disability pathways in our definition of labour market exit.

We moved to isolate individuals ages 55–60. This group is generally below the statutory retirement age but within the “early retirement window” where exit is possible. The result revealed a significant positive treatment effect of around 5 pp post-diagnosis (Supplementary Figure S5). This result is also crucial for identification as it confirms that the zero pre-trend in our main specification reflects a genuine absence of behavioural response, rather than a “mechanical” inability to retire.

Sensitivity to control group selection

Our primary analysis used the “never treated” as the control to establish a baseline of consistently healthy individuals. This raises a potential concern that these people might represent a distinct population from the general population. To address this, we re-estimated the model using the “not-yet-treated” units as the controls. The result (Supplementary Figure S6) remained statistically significant and similar in magnitude. This consistency mitigates concerns that unobservable differences between healthy and unhealthy individuals drive the results.

Discussion

This paper found that Europeans aged 55 and above with NCDs were significantly more likely to exit from the labour market early than their healthy counterparts. On average, they faced a 16 pp higher risk of early labour market exit, losing approximately 3.6 months of working life. The increased risk was marked for people in Slovenia (26 pp), followed by Germany (21 pp), Belgium (16 pp), Italy (15 pp), and Austria (14 pp). In Switzerland (11 pp), Spain (10 pp), Denmark (10 pp), and the Czech Republic (9 pp), the effect was mild. The resulting annual societal income loss was the highest in Germany, followed by Italy, Spain, and Belgium. The severity gradient supports the hypothesis that the exit from the labour force is a response to the functional limitations imposed by the chronic disease. The trends fluctuated in post-diagnosis periods, potentially due to evolving health trajectories (e.g., recovery vs. deterioration).

Despite significantly increasing early labour market exit risk, the impact of NCD diagnosis on working time was mild, likely due to the late onsets in our sample (see Supplementary Figure S7). However, focusing on the population with later onset can give us more clues about how people with NCDs face the trade-offs in having early retirement under many system-level differences. Specifically, early retirement often comes with reduced retirement benefits and limited access to healthcare; however, it can alleviate the working burden, allowing more time for self-care, especially in voluntary cases [29, 30]. Thus, financial security plays a crucial role in managing early retirement and is more common in countries with strong welfare states or high-income earners [31]. Otherwise, poor health may limit work participation and force early retirement, leading to financial instability. Such situations can be worse among individuals with NCDs, as their incomes have been affected by productivity loss for a long time [6], potentially putting their family in a financially vulnerable position [32] that can be aggravated by old age [33].

The strong effects on the risk of leaving the labour force observed in Belgium, Germany, and Austria can be explained by high earnings [4] and generous healthcare financing in these countries. For instance, Belgium spent 10.9% of their GDP, Germany allocated 12.7%, and Austria 11.4%, compared to the average of 9.2% of the OECD countries in 2022 [34]. Similarly, Denmark, with robust pensions and flexible retirement programs [4], potentially ensures financial security, especially for those unable to continue working due to health issues. In contrast, Sweden, which has a similar welfare system, has a more earnings-dependent pension system [4], leading to higher costs of early retirement and potentially causing the observed difference in the effect size between the two countries.

In Central and Eastern European regions, the physical and mental health status may contribute more to the high labour market exit rates. Specifically, Slovenia has one of the highest age-adjusted disability rates among the selected countries, driven by high mortality rates from alcohol-abuse-related diseases and suicide [35, 36], both strongly linked to depression – a highly work-affecting condition, especially when combined with other health issues [37]. The Slovenian labour market structure is also limited in terms of work flexibility compared to others [38], which likely creates barriers to returning to work, when a full-time job is often the only option. Occupational factors – particularly in physically demanding jobs – may drive individuals out of the labour market following a diagnosis, as returning to such work is challenging [39], and reintegration efforts may offer limited benefit, especially for those nearing retirement age. The lack of financial guarantees after retirement, likely due to relatively low salaries [40] and highly earnings-related pensions [4], may explain the small effect observed in Estonia.

Stiff penalties for early retirements, like in Switzerland, also deter early exits. Swiss workers face a 6.8% pension reduction per year of early claiming, higher than neighbouring countries, such as Austria (max. 5.1%) and Germany (3.6%) [4]. Moreover, high living costs and flexible working conditions can further discourage early retirement in this country. In France, relatively low working hours and a generous sickness insurance system, especially since 2008, show some capacity to manage long-term illness while maintaining employment [41].

Overall, despite variations in national legislation and cultural contexts, our results show that NCDs significantly contribute to labour market exit across Europe. While the average working time lost may appear small at the individual level, when scaled to the population level, the resulting productivity loss is substantial. This is largely driven by the increasing prevalence of NCDs and an aging workforce [18], which pose significant challenges to the sustainability of labour markets and social security systems. Without effective interventions, the system risks experiencing reduced workforce participation and increased financial strain due to rising healthcare and pension costs. Thus, our findings underscore the urgent need for comprehensive policies that promote healthy working lives and support older workers in remaining in the labour market.

Effective policies should prioritise vocational rehabilitation to help individuals with NCDs manage symptoms and maintain work capacity. This includes counselling, workplace accommodations (e.g., flexible schedules, supportive equipment), and medical support from occupational health professionals. Active labour market programs and employer tax incentives can further promote (re)integration into the workforce. Public health campaigns encouraging healthy lifestyles and awareness of preventable conditions are also essential for long-term impact.

Strengths and limitations

This study uses formal medical diagnoses – required to access long-term work-related benefits – as a quasi-exogenous trigger for labour market responses. Combined with a difference-in-differences design that adjusts for socioeconomic factors and self-reported health status, this approach enhances group comparability (Supplementary Figure S8 and Figure S9) and strengthens causal inference. Excluding mental health conditions further preserves the exogeneity of the diagnosis and helps avoid potential reverse causality as well as possible underdiagnosis. The large sample size ensures statistical power, while regional and demographic diversity support generalisability.

Nevertheless, this study has certain limitations. The biannual data collection may miss short-term labour force exits, particularly among older individuals, while survival bias – due to selective attrition of those with severe health conditions – may further suppress observed effects, leading to a potential underestimation of the impact of interest. Although confidence intervals widen in later periods due to study design constraints (e.g., the exclusion of post-retirement data and attrition), our main estimates rely on the first two post-diagnosis periods – where data coverage is strongest – thereby minimising the influence of uncertainty in later years. Additionally, all NCDs included in the analysis were treated equally in terms of their severity. This approach poses an important limitation, but also captures the real-world complexity of overlapping conditions, providing a full picture of how chronic health issues impact work productivity. Future research, with richer data, can try to investigate the effect of specific conditions as well as the mechanism of decision-making processes around labour market exit, as it may differ, for example, across income groups (e.g., lower-income individuals may work longer due to limited safety nets, while higher-income individuals can afford early retirement), for better-targeted policy recommendations. Lastly, potential time-varying confounders, such as health behaviours and resilience, were not fully captured, despite adjusting for income and education level, which potentially leads to a comparison against an especially healthy reference group, thereby biasing the estimates toward the null.

Conclusions

This study found that having at least one NCD triggers the decision to exit the labour market early in all European countries, with the effect size being heterogeneous over time and across countries. This finding, along with the significant income losses observed, affirms the need for more effective prevention-based interventions and a more comprehensive approach to creating inclusive policies that facilitate individuals facing NCDs’ participation in the labour market.
