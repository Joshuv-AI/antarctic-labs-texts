Surgical scene understanding and the structural validation gap in an industry-led AI ecosystem
Authors: Pol MacAonghusa, Ronan A. Cahill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This Matters Arising builds upon the review by Carstens et al. highlighting methodological limitations within the academic surgical scene understanding (SSU) literature. Increasingly clinically deployed SSU occurs within commercial platforms that are incompletely represented in traditional evidence synthesis. This “structural validation gap” between academic research and proprietary systems challenges the field to prioritize the development of independent benchmarking, validation and post-deployment evaluation frameworks for deployed surgical AI systems.

In their systematic review and reporting quality meta-analysis, Carstens et al. provide a rigorous assessment of artificial intelligence (AI) approaches to surgical scene understanding (SSU)1. This evidences that much of the academic literature in this field remains preclinical and task-specific as well as limited by inconsistent reporting, weak external validation and minimal real-world evaluation. Their conclusion—that methodological standards must improve to enable safe clinical translation—is well supported. We propose that these findings should also be understood within the context of a broader structural shift in surgical AI innovation and evaluation.

Although published SSU research is by nature predominantly academic and therefore focused on model development and preclinical validation, an increasing proportion of clinically deployed or near-deployed innovation appears to be occurring within industry-led, platform-based systems. These systems integrate multiple SSU capabilities (including intraoperative guidance, workflow recognition, performance analytics and context-aware decision support) operating in real time within surgical workflows and commercial software ecosystems. Because such platforms are often only partially represented in peer-reviewed literature and because systematic reviews are inherently constrained to the publishable fraction of SSU development, there is a natural structural tension between academic evaluations and contemporary deployment realities regarding the systems most likely to influence clinical practice. The growing prominence of industry-led SSU embedded within integrated clinical systems rather than isolated research models2,3 amplifies broader concerns regarding transparency, reproducibility, benchmarking and validation in surgical AI systems1,4, including those highlighted by Carstens et al. regarding interpretability of “black-box” decision-making. As systems become proprietary and workflow-integrated, independent academic scrutiny becomes simultaneously more difficult and more essential. Without such engagement, evaluation risks becoming vendor-led, limiting external validation and leaving clinicians without independently generated evidence to guide adoption and governance. Reporting deficiencies identified in the academic literature may therefore reflect not only methodological limitations but also a shifting innovation landscape in which deployment may increasingly occur outside traditional academic pathways. Importantly here early real-world academic evaluations of commercially developed intraoperative AI systems have identified gaps between anticipated and realised performance5,6, including requirements for local retraining, delays in producing clinically actionable outputs, limited predictive utility, and challenges achieving methodological transparency7,8. These findings indicate that deployment alone does not equate to maturity and that clinically relevant limitations often emerge only through independent investigation. The central challenge for SSU research now is no longer solely individual algorithmic innovation and refinement but the evolution of academic roles within an increasingly industry-driven ecosystem. Academic groups are uniquely positioned to develop independent evaluation frameworks (building on reporting guidelines such as DECIDE-AI9), benchmarking strategies and validation methodologies suited to platform-level, multi-task SSU systems, including those developed outside traditional academic pipelines. This role is essential to rigorously interrogate the limitations of commercial technologies and guide responsible evolution. To do this effectively, reporting standards and evidence synthesis approaches may need to evolve beyond single-task paradigms to incorporate system-level performance, post-deployment evaluation and insights from implementation and regulatory science. Mechanisms such as mandatory independent benchmarking using standardized datasets, prospective post-market surveillance integrated within multicenter surgical registries, independently governed benchmarking networks using locally acquired operative video, structured post-deployment audit pathways and lifecycle regulatory frameworks requiring sufficient “open-box” access for independent auditing of commercially deployed “black-box” systems (including those contained within existing FDA and EU approaches as appropriate in these jurisdictions) may become critical components of surgical AI governance with likely special, additional developments needed in lower resource settings where the structural validation gap may be expected to be greatest. Innovative means of incentives, including funding, may need consideration, including potential public-private partnership models or conditional regulatory approvals linked to transparency and data-sharing. In this light, the review by Carstens et al. provides an important foundation for a more comprehensive accounting of SSU progress. Ensuring that increasingly capable surgical AI systems remain subject to rigorous independent validation across development, deployment and post-deployment use, alongside methodological transparency and clinician-led oversight, should now be considered a central scientific and regulatory task necessary to maintain patient safety, scientific integrity and clinician trust within an increasingly industry-driven surgical AI ecosystem.
