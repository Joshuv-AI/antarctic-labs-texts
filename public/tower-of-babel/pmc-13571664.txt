Gastrointestinal parasite assemblages in wild mammals from two forest localities in Gabon
Authors: Patrice Makouloutou-Nzassi, Fatim Tine, Arfang Diamanka, Judi Armel Bourobou Bourobou, Neil Michel Longo-Pendy, Paul Yannick Bitome Essono, Félicien Bangueboussa, Rodrigue Mintsa-Nguema, Larson Boundenga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrointestinal parasite assemblages are influenced by host compatibility, exposure, behavior, and environmental conditions; however, baseline data remain limited in Central Africa. We characterized gastrointestinal parasite morphotypes in 279 fecal samples collected from five wild mammal taxa at Lopé National Park and Makatamangoye, Gabon. Parasites were identified using flotation and sedimentation techniques and classified to the lowest morphologically defensible taxonomic level. The analysis included overall infection rates, infracommunity richness, sample-based rarefaction, and presence–absence community composition within localities and between host populations. After excluding unidentified structures, an ectoparasite, and a plant-parasitic nematode, 140/279 samples were tested positive (50.2%; 95% CI 44.2–56.2), resulting in the retention of 25 parasite taxa or morphotypes. Strongylid-type eggs (65 detections), Trichuris spp. (46), Bertiella spp. (24), Ascaris spp. (21) and Ternidens spp. (20) were the most frequently observed. Component-community richness ranged from four taxa in Pan troglodytes to 19 in Potamochoerus porcus. Differences among host populations within localities were apparent for both overall infection and infracommunity richness. Jaccard-based PERMANOVA revealed compositional differences among infected host samples at Lopé (pseudo-F = 2.40, p < 0.001) and Makatamangoye (pseudo-F = 2.24, p = 0.001), although caution is warranted due to heterogeneous dispersion at Lopé. Sørensen dissimilarity between localities within host taxa ranged from 0.33 to 0.73, often driven by nestedness-resultant differences. These findings provide a morphology-based inventory of gastrointestinal parasite assemblages, however, unequal sampling and limited taxonomic resolution prevent causal attribution to habitat disturbance or confirmation of zoonotic transmission. Further molecular characterization and replicated longitudinal sampling are needed.Keywords: Wildlife parasitology, Component community, Supracommunity, Beta diversity, Turnover, Nestedness, Gabon

Introduction

Parasite communities exhibit a hierarchical organization. Parasites co-occurring within an individual host constitute an infracommunity. The parasite fauna associated with a host population forms a component community, and component communities within a locality collectively comprise a supracommunity (Bush et al., 1997). Distinguishing among these scales is essential, as variation among individual hosts, host species, and localities reflects distinct combinations of compatibility, exposure, and sampling processes.

Gastrointestinal parasite assemblages are influenced by host phylogeny, physiology, immune compatibility, body size, diet, substrate use, social organization, ranging behavior, and population density. Parasite traits are also significant: directly transmitted parasites depend on contact with infective stages in feces, soil, or vegetation, whereas heteroxenous parasites require suitable intermediate hosts. Host specificity exhibits considerable variation. Some gastrointestinal parasites are restricted to particular hosts or host lineages, while morphologically similar eggs or cysts may represent multiple cryptic species with distinct host ranges. Therefore, community comparisons based on coprological morphology should be interpreted as comparisons of detectable taxonomic units or morphotypes, rather than confirmed parasite species (Poulin, 2019).

Environmental modification can alter parasite assemblages by affecting host abundance, movement, contact networks, microclimate, and intermediate-host availability. These effects are unlike to be uniform; modification may favor environmentally persistent generalists while reducing parasites dependent on sensitive hosts or vectors. Comparisons between a protected and a human-modified locality can reveal spatial contrasts. However, using only one locality per condition does not allow for the separation of disturbance effects from other site-specific differences. Such comparisons should therefore be considered descriptive rather than constituting a replicated test of habitat effects.

Gabon supports diverse mammal communities, however, inventories of gastrointestinal parasites remain geographically and taxonomically limited. Previous studies have documented parasites in wild primates, galagos, great apes, and sympatric mammals across several Gabonese ecosystems (Makouloutou et al., 2014; Boundenga et al., 2018; Dibakou et al., 2021; Sirima et al., 2021). Additional host-resolved inventories are needed to determine which morphotypes occur in each mammal population and to inform targeted molecular investigation.

This study aimed to characterize the gastrointestinal parasite fauna detected in five wild mammal taxa from Lopé National Park and Makatamangoye. Specifically, the objectives were to (i) present the parasite taxa or morphotypes recorded in each host population at the lowest defensible morphological resolution; (ii) compare overall infection rate and parasite richness among host populations within each locality; and (iii) compare component-community composition among hosts within localities and between host populations across localities using beta-diversity measures. The study did not attempt to rank host identity against disturbance as competing causal drivers.

Materials and methods

Study localities

Sampling was conducted in two Gabonese forest localities. Lopé National Park (approximately 0.4412°S, 11.5248°E) is a protected forest–savanna mosaic located in central Gabon. Makatamangoye (approximately 0.1083°S, 13.6992°E) is a secondary forest landscape situated near a village in eastern Gabon, where agriculture, logging and gold-mining activities are present. The localities differ in protection status, vegetation history, host composition, and sampling structure. As each locality represents a single site, locality differences were not interpreted as replicated effects of habitat disturbance.

Host and faecal sampling

Fresh fecal samples were collected non-invasively from black colobus (Colobus satanas), western lowland gorilla (Gorilla gorilla), central chimpanzee (Pan troglodytes), duikers (Cephalophus spp.) and red river hog (Potamochoerus porcus). The red river hog designation was adopted because P. porcus is the Potamochoerus species occurring in Gabon (Leslie, 2015; Mammal Diversity Database, 2026). Duiker feces were retained as Cephalophus spp. because several sympatric duiker species could not be distinguished reliably from field signs.

Experienced trackers assigned host origin using direct field observations when available, along with fecal characteristics, associated footprints, feeding signs, and nesting locations. To reduce duplicate sampling and contamination, we collected only fresh, spatially distinct deposits, and took material from the center of each fecal mass using clean gloves and tools. We placed approximately 20-30 g in a 50-mL tube and preserved it in ethanol or physiological saline, depending on field conditions. We recorded sample identifier, locality, and coordinates. We transported samples to the laboratory in Franceville and stored them at −20°C until examination.

Coprological examination and taxonomic resolution

Samples underwent sodium-chloride flotation and simple sedimentation in accordance with standard coprological procedures and WHO diagnostic guidance WHO, 2019. Eggs, larvae, cysts, and oocysts were identified microscopically based on size, shape, and visible structural characteristics, referencing published atlases and descriptions. In the absence of molecular confirmation, records were assigned to genus only when morphological evidence was sufficient; otherwise, identification was limited to family-level or genus-type morphotypes. These classifications do not indicate parasite species identity, host specificity, or zoonotic potential.

Twenty-five gastrointestinal taxa or morphotypes were retained for analysis. Unidentified structures were excluded, as they did not contribute to taxonomically interpretable comparisons. Sarcoptes scabiei was excluded due to its status as an ectoparasite, and Meloidogyne spp. was excluded because its presence in feces most likely reflects the passage of plant material rather than gastrointestinal infection. Infection status was recalculated based on the retained variables: a sample was positive if at least one accepted taxon or morphotype was detected.

Community scales and statistical analysis

An infracommunity was defined as the assemblage detected in a single fecal sample. A component community referred to the pooled parasite assemblage detected in one host taxon at a single locality. Comparisons among host component communities within a locality were interpreted at the supracommunity scale.

Overall infection was defined as the percentage of samples containing at least one accepted parasite taxon, while parasite-specific prevalence represented the percentage positive for an individual taxon or morphotype. Exact Clopper-Pearson 95% confidence intervals were calculated.

Infracommunity richness was defined as the number of accepted parasite taxa detected per sample. Observed component richness referred to the number of taxa detected across all samples from a host population. Overall infection among hosts within each locality was compared using chi-square tests. Infracommunity richness was compared using Kruskal-Wallis tests, followed, where appropriate, by pairwise Mann-Whitney tests with Holm adjustment. Sample-based rarefaction employed the hypergeometric expectation for incidence data to illustrate how observed taxon richness accumulated with sampling effort. No abundance-based Shannon index was calculated.

Community composition analyses were conducted using presence–absence data. Among infected samples within each locality, Jaccard dissimilarities were analyzed using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA; 4999 permutations). Parasite-negative samples were excluded because they contained no assemblage for comparison. Homogeneity of multivariate dispersion was evaluated with a permutation test of distances to group centroids. At the component-community level, pairwise Sørensen dissimilarity (βsor) was partitioned into turnover (Simpson dissimilarity, βsim) and nestedness-resultant dissimilarity (βsne) following Baselga (2010). Values ranged from 0 (identical detected assemblages) to 1 (no shared taxa). Analyses were conducted using a fixed random seed and 4999 permutations.

Results

Sampling and overall infection

A total of 279 fecal samples were analyzed, including 97 from Lopé National Park and 182 from Makatamangoye (Table 1). Colobus satanas was sampled exclusively at Lopé. The remaining four host taxa were present in both datasets; however, sampling was highly unbalanced for P. porcus (four samples at Lopé and 69 at Makatamangoye) and G. gorilla (six and 26 samples, respectively).

Parasite fauna

A total of 25 gastrointestinal parasite taxa or morphotypes were identified (Table 2; Fig. 1). Strongylid-type eggs were the most frequently detected (65 occurrences), followed by Trichuris spp. (46), Bertiella spp. (24), Ascaris spp. (21), Ternidens spp. (20), and Echinostoma-type eggs (14). Eleven morphotypes were present at both localities, while three were exclusive to Lopé and 11 to Makatamangoye. Most locality-specific records represented single detections; thus, their apparent restriction should not be interpreted as evidence of established geographic specificity.

Host-population differences within localities

At Lopé, overall infection prevalence varied significantly among host populations (χ2 = 19.27, df = 4, p < 0.001), as did infracommunity richness (Kruskal-Wallis H = 21.49, df = 4, p < 0.001). Mean infracommunity richness ranged from 0.22 taxa per sample in C. satanas to 1.75 in P. porcus; however, the estimate for P. porcus was based on only four samples. At Makatamangoye, overall infection prevalence also differed significantly among host species (χ2 = 40.21, df = 3, p < 0.001), and infracommunity richness showed a strong difference (H = 51.27, df = 3, p < 0.001). P. porcus exhibited the highest mean richness (1.90), whereas G. gorilla (0.38) and P. troglodytes (0.37) had lower values.

Among infected samples, community composition differed significantly among host populations at both Lopé (PERMANOVA pseudo-F = 2.40, p < 0.001) and Makatamangoye (pseudo-F = 2.24, p = 0.001). At Lopé, dispersion also varied among host populations (F = 13.38, p = 0.035), indicating that the PERMANOVA results may reflect differences in both centroid and dispersion. In contrast, dispersion did not differ significantly at Makatamangoye (F = 5.45, p = 0.111). Pairwise Sørensen dissimilarities among host component communities ranged from 0.33 to 0.80 at Lopé and from 0.38 to 0.65 at Makatamangoye (Table 3).

Component-community differences between localities

Dissimilarity between localities varied among host taxa (Table 4; Fig. 3). Cephalophus spp. shared six taxa between localities (βsor = 0.40), with contributions from both turnover (βsim = 0.14) and nestedness-resultant dissimilarity (βsne = 0.26). Gorilla gorilla shared two taxa (βsor = 0.56), with a greater turnover component (βsim = 0.33). Both taxa recorded in Lopé for P. troglodytes were also present at Makatamangoye (βsor = 0.33; entirely nestedness-resultant). Similarly, the three taxa detected in the four Lopé P. porcus samples represented a subset of the 19 detected at Makatamangoye (βsor = 0.73; entirely nestedness-resultant). This contrast is substantially influenced by unequal sampling effort. Across the complete observed fauna, the two localities shared 11 taxa, with three unique to Lopé and 11 unique to Makatamangoye (βsor = 0.39).

Discussion

This study presents a host- and locality-specific inventory of gastrointestinal parasite morphotypes in five wild mammal taxa from Gabon. The analysis intentionally emphasizes observed fauna, richness, and composition, rather than relying on statistical models of broad parasite groups. This approach aligns with the resolution provided by flotation and sedimentation data and prevents the assignment of ecological specificity to parasites that may include multiple morphologically indistinguishable species.

Strongylid-type eggs and Trichuris spp. represented the most frequently detected morphotypes, occurring across multiple host populations. Comparable morphotypes have been documented in wild primates and artiodactyls in Gabon and other regions of Africa (Gillespie et al., 2005; Obanda et al., 2019; Dibakou et al., 2021). However, shared morphology does not confirm cross-species transmission, as strongylid and Trichuris eggs may correspond to host-specific or cryptic lineages. Molecular sequencing is required before shared records can be interpreted as evidence of shared parasite species.

Host populations exhibited differences in overall infection prevalence, infracommunity richness, and assemblage composition within both localities. P. porcus and Cephalophus spp. harbored the most diverse observed assemblages, whereas P. troglodytes exhibited the lowest number of detected taxa. These patterns may reflect differences in exposure, diet, and substrate use, although these factors were not directly measured. Additionally, the host taxa represent distinct evolutionary lineages and were sampled unevenly. Consequently, the study demonstrates variation among the sampled host populations but does not estimate a general primate-versus-artiodactyl or phylogenetic effect.

The beta-diversity analysis elucidates differences in observed assemblages. Within localities, many host pairs exhibited moderate to high Sørensen dissimilarity, resulting from varying contributions of turnover and nestedness. Across localities, contrasts for P. troglodytes and P. porcus were entirely attributable to nestedness, as their Lopé assemblages were subsets of those at Makatamangoye. This pattern should not be interpreted as ecological loss or gain, since nestedness can result from differences in sample sizes and detection probabilities. The four Lopé P. porcus samples are particularly insufficient for a balanced comparison.

Although pooled infection prevalence and observed richness were higher at Makatamangoye, the study design does not attribute this difference to habitat disturbance. The localities differ in geography, host sampling, and potentially numerous unmeasured environmental variables, with each habitat condition represented by a single locality. Replicated sites, standardized sampling effort, and repeated seasonal sampling would be necessary to determine whether anthropogenic modification consistently alters parasite assemblages.

Several recorded genera include species capable of infecting humans; however, the current data do not establish zoonotic infection. Specifically, morphological identification cannot determine whether Schistosoma, Trichuris, Ascaris, or Entamoeba records correspond to human-infective species. The findings highlight samples and host populations that could be prioritized for molecular characterization, but should not be interpreted as evidence of wildlife-to-human transmission.

The principal limitations include opportunistic and uneven sampling, small sample sizes for several host–locality combinations, absence of individual-host confirmation, reliance on morphology-based parasite identification, and lack of infection-intensity data. Rarefaction curves did not consistently approach an asymptote, indicating that component richness is likely underestimated. Freezing and preservation procedures may also affect detection of certain parasite stages. These constraints are now incorporated into the interpretation, rather than being treated as minor qualifications.

Conclusion

The five sampled wild mammal taxa harbored distinct, incompletely characterized assemblages comprising 25 gastrointestinal parasite taxa or morphotypes. Differences among host populations were observed within both localities, while between-locality differences within hosts reflected both taxon replacement and nestedness. These findings offer baseline community data but do not establish causal relationships regarding habitat disturbance, parasite host specificity, or zoonotic transmission. Replicated longitudinal sampling, combined with molecular identification, is necessary to clarify parasite species and transmission pathways.
