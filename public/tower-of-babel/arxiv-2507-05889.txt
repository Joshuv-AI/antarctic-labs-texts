Electromagnetic Boundary Conditions for Space–time Interfaces
J. Gratus, S. A. R. Horsley, M. W. McCall

Abstract
We give a general family of electromagnetic boundary conditions applicable to arbitrary space–time interfaces between electromagnetic media, which include the known space–only and time–only boundary conditions as special cases. These boundary conditions describe a broad class of electromagnetic interfaces, including surfaces in arbitrary motion, ultra-thin (metasurface) media, and cases where the media on one or both sides of the boundary can be both spatially and temporally dispersive. Our approach utilizes 4-dimensional spacetime and addresses the question of how, and in what ways, an electromagnetic field may be connected across a 3-dimensional hypersurface. We show that our proposed boundary conditions are the most general conditions consistent with causality and linearity.



I
Introduction

Electromagnetic (EM) wave propagation in bulk materials is nearly always described in terms of a set of constitutive relations. These determine the displacement field,
\bm{D}
and magnetic flux density,
\bm{B}
, in terms of the electric
\bm{E}
and magnetic
\bm{H}
fields, fixing both the polarization state and wave speed within the medium
Mackay and Lakhtakia 2019
. However, at the boundary of any such material, we must apply boundary conditions to connect the internal field with the outside world. When, for example, such a bulk three–dimensional material is reduced to an ultra–thin two dimensional sheet, it becomes awkward to use the constitutive relations at all. In this case it is simpler to characterize the material in terms of a boundary condition (BC), connecting the field vectors on either side of the sheet. Although there has been significant work deriving these BCs for thin, static sheets of material (often practically realised as metasurfaces
Achouri et al. 2015
), recent work on time–varying metamaterials and metasurfaces
Galiffi et al. 2022
has opened up the possibility of more general, space–time interfaces
Taravati and Eleftheriades 2021
. This leads to the natural question of what BC we should use to characterize a
space–time
metasurface. In four–dimensional language: in what ways can we connect the EM field across an arbitrary 3–dimensional hypersurface? Here we answer this question, providing the general set of BCs, special cases of which can connect the field across any space–time interface.

\displaystyle\left.E^{{\textup{I}}}\right|_{x(t)}=

\displaystyle\left.E^{{\textup{I\!I}}}\right|_{x(t)}

\displaystyle\left.\left[H^{{\textup{I}}}+\frac{\alpha_{\rm E}}{2}\left(\frac{\partial E^{{\textup{I}}}}{\partial t}+\dot{x}(t)\frac{\partial E^{{\textup{I}}}}{\partial x}\right)\right]\right|_{x(t)}=

In section
II
we review the electromagnetic boundary conditions at purely spatial and temporal boundaries and extend these to arbitrarily moving boundaries and to include additional boundary conditions when the media either side of the boundary are spatially or temporally dispersive. In preparation for discussing general boundary conditions, we express these special cases in tensor notation. In section
III
we present our main result, i.e. linear boundary conditions at a general spacetime boundary that embrace the preceding purely spatial and temporal boundaries as special cases. We demonstrate formally that our general boundary conditions are the most general possible that are consistent with linearity and causality. Finally, in section
IV
we conclude.

II
Boundary conditions in four dimensions

II.1
‘Standard’ boundary conditions through integrating Maxwell’s equations

From Maxwell’s equations one can always prescribe natural BCs. Through applying a “pillbox” of integration to Maxwell’s equations in the vicinity of the boundary—as found in standard textbooks
Jackson 1962
(see Fig.
3
b)—a static spatial boundary
\Sigma
containing zero surface charge and current implies the usual continuity conditions

\displaystyle{\bm{E}}^{\textup{I}}_{\parallel}\big|_{\Sigma}={\bm{E}}^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\parallel}\big|_{\Sigma},\quad{\bm{B}}^{\textup{I}}_{\perp}\big|_{\Sigma}={\bm{B}}^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\perp}\big|_{\Sigma},\quad{\bm{H}}^{\textup{I}}_{\parallel}\big|_{\Sigma}={\bm{H}}^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\parallel}\big|_{\Sigma},\quad{\bm{D}}^{\textup{I}}_{\perp}\big|_{\Sigma}={\bm{D}}^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\perp}\big|_{\Sigma}\,.

(3)

where ‘
I
’ and ‘
I​I
’ indicate the space–time regions either side of the boundary and ‘
\parallel
’ and ‘
\perp
’ indicate the components of the vectors in the plane and out of the plane of the interface, respectively.

(4)

An example not often found in textbooks is a temporal boundary, as shown in Fig.

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}t\int{\rm d}^{3}\bm{x}\,\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{E}=-\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}^{3}\bm{x}\left(\bm{B}^{{\textup{I\!I}}}-\bm{B}^{{\textup{I}}}\right)=0

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}t\int{\rm d}^{3}\bm{x}\,\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{H}=\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}^{3}\bm{x}\left(\bm{D}^{{\textup{I\!I}}}-\bm{D}^{{\textup{I}}}\right)=0

(5)

As the shape of the integration domain on the boundary

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}^{\textup{I}}={\bm{B}}^{\textup{I\!I}},\quad{\bm{D}}^{\textup{I}}={\bm{D}}^{\textup{I\!I}}

(6)

These are the same boundary conditions as discussed as in e.g Ref.
Galiffi et al. 2022
. The same approach—integrating over a thin “pillbox” enclosing the boundary,
\Sigma

\bm{D}

The standard BCs, given in Eq. (

The nature of surface currents and how they relate to the fields is another topic, outside the scope of this article. They can be prescribed, in which case the BCs will change from being linear homogenous to linear inhomogeneous. By contrast some surface currents are linearly related to the electromagnetic fields on either side of the boundary. In general, these could be absorbed into the effective linear BCs, for example, the moving polarization density given in Eq. (
2

II.2
Additional boundary conditions and dispersive materials

The above equations, (
4
) and (
6
) (and their generalization to space–time boundaries) need to be supplemented with additional conditions if the media are temporally or spatially dispersive. For a spatial boundary between spatially dispersive (sometimes called “non–local”) media, these extra conditions are known as additional boundary conditions (ABCs), and have been studied since the pioneering work of Pekar
Pekar 1957
. It is less well appreciated that analogous conditions are also required for a temporal boundary between temporally dispersive materials, although this was pointed out in
Gratus et al. 2021
.

Taking the aforementioned case of a temporal interface between temporally dispersive media (as in Fig.
3
a), we assume that both sides of the boundary have a polarization field,
\bm{P}^{\textup{I}}(t,x)
and
\bm{P}^{\textup{I\!I}}(t,x)
, which in each region satisfies the equation of damped simple harmonic motion, e.g.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\bm{P}^{\textup{I}}+\lambda_{\textup{I}}\partial_{t}\bm{P}^{\textup{I}}+\alpha_{\textup{I}}^{2}\bm{P}^{\textup{I}}=\chi_{\textup{I}}\bm{E}^{{\textup{I}}}

(7)

and likewise in the second region, for
\bm{P}^{\textup{I\!I}}
, with corresponding
\lambda_{\textup{I\!I}}
,
\alpha_{\textup{I\!I}}
and
\chi_{\textup{I\!I}}
. If—as is the typical case in e.g optics—there were just region
I
for all time, one could take the Fourier transform, giving the familiar frequency domain relation

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{P}}^{\textup{I}}(\omega,\bm{x})=\frac{\chi_{\textup{I}}}{-\omega^{2}+i\lambda_{\textup{I}}\omega+\alpha_{\textup{I}}^{2}}\tilde{\bm{E}}^{\textup{I}}

(8)

However, this Fourier transform is no longer possible when there is an abrupt change in the material properties. In order for Eq. (
7
) to be well defined across the boundary
\Sigma
, the polarization and its first derivative must be continuous,

\displaystyle\bm{P}^{\textup{I}}=\bm{P}^{\textup{I\!I}}\quad\textup{and}\quad\partial_{t}\bm{P}^{\textup{I}}=\partial_{t}\bm{P}^{\textup{I\!I}}\,.

(9)

For a temporal boundary we must use these conditions in addition to the continuity of the electromagnetic field (
6
) arising from integrating Maxwell’s equations over the boundary. As mentioned above, these additional boundary conditions are the temporal counterpart of those required at a
spatial
boundary between
spatially
dispersive media. In the case of spatially and temporally dispersive materials, the driven simple harmonic oscillator equation of motion for the polarization field (
7
) is usually supplemented with an additional term containing second order spatial derivatives of the polarization,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\bm{P}^{\textup{I}}+\lambda_{\textup{I}}\partial\bm{P}^{\textup{I}}+\alpha_{\textup{I}}^{2}\bm{P}^{\textup{I}}-\beta_{\textup{I}}^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\bm{P}^{\textup{I}}=\chi_{\textup{I}}\bm{E}

(10)

and likewise for
\bm{P}^{\textup{I\!I}}
where
\beta_{\textup{I\!I}}
is interpreted as the polariton velocity. This modification results in polarization waves within the material. In order that Eq. (
10
) is well defined we must have the analogous continuity of the polarization and its first spatial derivative,

\displaystyle\bm{P}^{\textup{I}}=\bm{P}^{\textup{I\!I}}\quad\textup{and}\quad\partial_{x}\bm{P}^{\textup{I}}=\partial_{x}\bm{P}^{\textup{I\!I}}

(11)

Comparing the non dispersive and dispersive cases for either temporal or spatial boundaries we can see that we must either use Eqns. (
4
) or (
6
) in isolation, or—when dispersion is important—supplement these with the additional conditions on the polarization given in Eqns. (
10
) or (
11
).

There are also examples of spatially dispersive media where no additional boundary conditions are required. As an example of such a spatiotemporally dispersive boundary condition, take a static thin sheet at
z=0
, within which there is a confined polarization oriented along the
x
–axis,
P_{x}(t,x,y,z)=\delta(z)\mathcal{P}_{x}(t,x,y)
. Within the sheet we assume this in–plane component of the polarization satisfies the following second order differential equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\mathcal{P}_{x}+2\lambda_{D}\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{x}+(\omega_{0}^{2}\beta^{2}+\lambda_{D}^{2})\mathcal{P}_{x}-\beta^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\mathcal{P}_{x}=\chi_{0}E_{x}

(12)

Performing a Fourier transform of both sides of Eq. (
12
), we can express the inhomogeneous solution to (
12
) in Fourier space as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{x}(\omega,k_{x},k_{y})=\frac{\chi_{0}\tilde{E}_{x}(\omega,k_{x},k_{y})}{-\omega^{2}-2i\lambda_{D}\omega+(\omega_{0}^{2}\beta^{2}+\lambda_{D}^{2})+\beta^{2}k_{x}^{2}}

(13)

which can be understood as a generalization of the usual Lorentz model. After an inverse Fourier transform of Eq. (
13
), we have a convolution in space and time

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{x}({t},{x},{y})=\int d{t}^{\prime}\int d{x}^{\prime}\int d{y}^{\prime}\chi({t}-{t}^{\prime},{x}-{x}^{\prime},{y}-{y}^{\prime})\ E_{x}({t}^{\prime},{x}^{\prime},{y}^{\prime})

(14)

where the kernel of the integral (the in–plane susceptibility) can be found via inverse Fourier transform,

\displaystyle\chi(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\delta(y)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}\omega}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}k}{2\pi}\frac{{\rm e}^{i[kx-\omega t]}}{-\omega^{2}-2i\lambda_{D}\omega+(\omega_{0}^{2}\beta^{2}+\lambda_{D}^{2})+\beta^{2}k^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\beta}\delta(y){\rm e}^{-\lambda_{D}t}\Theta(\beta t-|x|)J_{0}\left(\omega_{0}\sqrt{\beta^{2}t^{2}-x^{2}}\right)

(15)

where we have evaluated the integral using Cauchy’s residue theorem and the integral representation of the Hankel function
S. and Ryzhik 2015
,
J_{0}
is the zeroth order Bessel function of the first kind, and
\Theta
is the Heaviside step function. The current within the sheet is given by
j_{x}=\delta(z)\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{x}
, and using the pillbox integration argument described in Sec.
II.1
, the boundary conditions relating the fields in region
I

(z<0)
and region
I​I

(z>0)
are given by the standard boundary conditions

\displaystyle E^{\textup{I\!I}}_{x}=E^{\textup{I}}_{x},\quad E^{\textup{I\!I}}_{y}=E^{\textup{I}}_{y},\quad B^{\textup{I\!I}}_{z}=B^{\textup{I}}_{z},\quad H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{x}=H^{\textup{I}}_{x},\quad H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{y}=H^{\textup{I}}_{y}-\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{x}\quad\textup{and}\quad D^{\textup{I\!I}}_{z}=D^{\textup{I}}_{z}

(16)

(note that in this case there is no need for additional boundary conditions on the polarization field, due to the confinement of the polarization to propagate within the sheet). Due to the space–time convolution in Eq. (
14
) the boundary conditions in (
16
) are non–local, but are local to the boundary.

It is thus clear that in the general case, the number of boundary conditions is not fixed, and need not be local either. There has to be a sufficient number of boundary conditions for the number of fields, and their derivatives on either side of the boundary. Since we do not want to limit the number of boundary conditions, here we specify simply that there are some number
m
of them. This number increases as the spatial and temporal dispersion becomes more complicated, involving e.g. equations of motion with higher order spatial or temporal derivatives.

II.3
Examples of boundary conditions in four–dimensions

We see that the nature of the above boundary conditions depends not only on the importance of dispersion, but also on the on the nature of the boundary and it is not immediately obvious how either conditions (
4
) and (
6
), or (
10
) and (
11
) should be adapted to the general case. For instance, these conditions become more complicated if the boundary is moving, as discussed briefly in the introduction. Through exploiting the spacetime notation of special and general relativity, the above equations can be naturally combined into a single equation, valid for any space–time interface.

As discussed in many texts on classical electromagnetism (see e.g.
Jackson 1962
or
Landau and Lifshitz 2004
), the electromagnetic field vectors
{\bm{E}}
and
{\bm{B}}
are components of a single rank 2 anti-symmetric tensor
F_{\mu\nu}=-F_{\nu\mu}
, where

\displaystyle E_{i}=c\,F_{0i}\quad\textup{and}\quad B_{i}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon_{0ijk}\,\omega\,g^{j\kappa}\,g^{k\lambda}\,F_{\kappa\lambda}

(17)

where

\displaystyle\omega=\sqrt{|\det(g_{\mu\nu})|}

(18)

is the measure for the metric tensor
g_{\mu\nu}
. The signature of metric is taken as
(-,+,+,+)
. Note that for Minkowski spacetime with standard Cartesian coordinates,
\omega=1
. As a result, in Minkowski spacetime,
\omega
also corresponds to the Jacobian associated with the coordinate transformation from Cartesian coordinates to the new coordinates.
Latin indices range over the spatial coordinates and Greek over the space–time coordinates, and
\epsilon_{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}
is the unit anti–symmetric tensor, equal to
+1
for an even permutation of
\{0,1,2,3\}
,
-1
for an odd permutation, and
0
otherwise. Likewise the excitation fields
{\bm{H}}
and
{\bm{D}}
are encoded in a single anti-symmetric tensor
H_{\mu\nu}
where

\displaystyle H_{i}=H_{0i}\quad\textup{and}\quad D_{i}=\tfrac{1}{2c}\,\epsilon_{0ijk}\,\omega\,g^{j\kappa}\,g^{k\lambda}\,H_{\kappa\lambda}

(19)

Using these two four–dimensional tensors,
F_{\mu\nu}
and
H_{\mu\nu}
, Maxwell’s equations become two simple relations,

\displaystyle\partial_{[\mu}F_{\nu\rho]}=0\quad\textup{and}\quad\partial_{[\mu}H_{\nu\rho]}=\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\,\omega\,J^{\sigma}

(20)

where the square brackets indicate an anti-symmetrization with respect to the enclosed space–time indices. By using this spacetime tensor notation, a single equation can encode boundary conditions that have an arbitrary shape and orientation in space–time, in terms of an arbitrary coordinate system with metric tensor
g_{\mu\nu}
.

We now can re–write the above special cases of space–time boundary conditions—(
4
) and (
6
)—in a general form. To do this we introduce a set of functions to describe the boundary,
\Sigma^{\mu}(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3})

,

, and

. The functions

are defined such that, for the surface normal

. The continuity of the in–plane components of the field tensors across any space–time boundary is thus expressed as,

\displaystyle F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}\frac{\partial\Sigma^{\mu}}{\partial u^{a}}\frac{\partial\Sigma^{\nu}}{\partial u^{b}}=F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}\frac{\partial\Sigma^{\mu}}{\partial u^{a}}\frac{\partial\Sigma^{\nu}}{\partial u^{b}}\quad\textup{and}\quad H^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}\frac{\partial\Sigma^{\mu}}{\partial u^{a}}\frac{\partial\Sigma^{\nu}}{\partial u^{b}}=H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}\frac{\partial\Sigma^{\mu}}{\partial u^{a}}\frac{\partial\Sigma^{\nu}}{\partial u^{b}}.

(21)

where again the superscripts ‘
I
’ and ‘
I​I
’ indicate the regions either side of the boundary. Assuming Cartesian coordinates in Minkowski space–time, a spatial boundary oriented along the

–axis corresponds to
\Sigma^{\mu}=(u_{1},0,u_{2},u_{3})
. In this case Eq. (

) reduces to the continuity of

,

, and

(i.e.
\bm{E}_{\parallel}
and
\bm{B}_{\perp}
) as well as

,

, and
H_{23}
(i.e.
\bm{H}_{\parallel}
and
\bm{D}_{\perp}
). These are precisely the boundary conditions prescribed in Eq. (

\Sigma^{\mu}=(0,u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
, the application of which reduces Eq. (

) to the continuity of

and

and

The boundary conditions (

II.5
General differential BCs

We can—at least in the cases where non–locality is weak enough to be captured in terms of local derivatives of the field—also include dispersion in a four–dimensional boundary condition.
In this case any additional fields at the boundary only depend only on the values of
F_{\mu\nu}
and
H_{\mu\nu}
, and their derivatives at that point. In this case we can generalize the examples (
9
) and (
3
) to cases where the media on one or both sides of a space–time boundary are dispersive. We use the adapted coordinate system
(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3},u^{4})
described above. We write
{\underline{\underline{u}}}=(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3},u^{4})
as the three surface coordinates
{\underline{u}}=(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3})
, plus
u_{4}
which is the coordinate normal to the surface. Hence the point
({\underline{u}},0)
corresponds to a point on the boundary,
\Sigma
.

The generalized boundary conditions are then given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{{\underline{\underline{s}}}}\left({\overline{\kappa}}^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I}}\mu\nu}_{{A}{\underline{\underline{s}}}}({\underline{u}})\,\frac{\partial^{\underline{\underline{s}}}}{\partial{\underline{\underline{u}}}^{\underline{\underline{s}}}}F^{{\textup{I}}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},0)+{\overline{\kappa}}^{{\textup{H}}{\textup{I}}\mu\nu}_{{A}{\underline{\underline{s}}}}({\underline{u}})\,\frac{\partial^{\underline{\underline{s}}}}{\partial{\underline{\underline{u}}}^{\underline{\underline{s}}}}H^{{\textup{I}}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},0)\right)\\
&\qquad=\sum_{{\underline{\underline{s}}}}\left({\overline{\kappa}}^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I\!I}}\mu\nu}_{{A}{\underline{\underline{s}}}}({\underline{u}})\,\frac{\partial^{\underline{\underline{s}}}}{\partial{\underline{\underline{u}}}^{\underline{\underline{s}}}}F^{{\textup{I\!I}}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},0)+{\overline{\kappa}}^{{\textup{H}}{\textup{I\!I}}\mu\nu}_{{A}{\underline{\underline{s}}}}({\underline{u}})\,\frac{\partial^{\underline{\underline{s}}}}{\partial{\underline{\underline{u}}}^{\underline{\underline{s}}}}H^{{\textup{I\!I}}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},0)\right)\end{split}

(26)

and
I​I
.

contains the orders of the derivatives of each of the four coordinates to be evaluated on the boundary.

Note that the sums

range over a finite number of combinations of space–time derivatives. Written out explicitly, this notation implies a product of derivatives of order

with respect to each of the coordinates,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\underline{\underline{s}}}}{\partial{\underline{\underline{u}}}^{\underline{\underline{s}}}}F^{{\textup{I}}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},0)=\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{1}}\Big)^{s_{1}}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{2}}\Big)^{s_{2}}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{3}}\Big)^{s_{3}}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{4}}\Big)^{s_{4}}F^{{\textup{I}}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},0)

III
Generalized Linear Boundary Conditions

The discussion so far has highlighted a large range of possible (linear) boundary conditions. For standard spatial boundaries, these can be encompassed in the usual field continuity conditions (
3
) (with appropriate modifications to allow for surface charge and current), plus the additional conditions due to spatial and temporal dispersion described in Sec.
II.2
. However, it is clear from the preceding section
II.3
that there is a much wider class of boundary conditions at a general space–time interface. In this section we present boundary conditions that encompass all the above examples. We will then proceed in Sec.
III.2
to show that these are the most general boundary conditions that satisfy a set of physically reasonable conditions.

We choose a coordinate system
(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3},z)
which is adapted to the boundary. In the general case the boundary conditions can be written in terms of four integral kernels,
{\left\{\kappa^{{\textup{F}}^{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime}),\kappa^{{\textup{H}}{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime}),\kappa^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I\!I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime}),\kappa^{{\textup{H}}{\textup{I\!I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime})\right\}}

{\underline{u}}^{\prime}
to allow for the

–functions required for local boundary conditions. The kernels are also assumed antisymmetric
\kappa^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime})=-\kappa^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\nu\mu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime})
.
In terms of these integral kernels the general boundary conditions are then given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{r=0}^{k}\int_{J^{\Sigma}({\underline{u}})}\Big(\kappa^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime})\,\frac{\partial^{r}}{\partial z^{r}}F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}}^{\prime},0)+\kappa^{{\textup{H}}{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime})\,\frac{\partial^{r}}{\partial z^{r}}H^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}}^{\prime},0)\Big)\,\omega_{({\underline{u}},z)}\,d^{3}{\underline{u}}^{\prime}\\
&\qquad=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{r=0}^{k}\int_{J^{\Sigma}({\underline{u}})}\Big(\kappa^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I\!I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime})\,\frac{\partial^{r}}{\partial z^{r}}F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}}^{\prime},0)+\kappa^{{\textup{H}}{\textup{I\!I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime})\,\frac{\partial^{r}}{\partial z^{r}}H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}}^{\prime},0)\Big)\,\omega_{({\underline{u}},z)}\,d^{3}{\underline{u}}^{\prime}.\end{split}

(28)

&\hskip 100.00015pt\psi_{0}\Big(\frac{u^{3}{-}v^{3}}{\epsilon}\Big)z^{r}\psi_{0}(z)\Big)\,d^{3}{\underline{u}}^{\prime}\\
&=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2r!\epsilon^{3}}\sum_{s=0}^{k}\int_{J^{\Sigma}({\underline{u}})}\kappa^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I}}}_{As}{}^{\sigma\rho}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime})\,\frac{\partial^{s}}{\partial z^{s}}\Big(\psi_{0}\Big(\frac{u^{1}{-}v^{1}}{\epsilon}\Big)\psi_{0}\Big(\frac{u^{2}{-}v^{2}}{\epsilon}\Big)\psi_{0}\Big(\frac{u^{3}{-}v^{3}}{\epsilon}\Big)z^{r}\psi_{0}(z)\Big)\,d^{3}{\underline{u}}^{\prime}\\
&=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2\epsilon^{3}}\int_{J^{\Sigma}({\underline{u}})}\kappa^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\sigma\rho}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime})\,\psi_{0}\Big(\frac{u^{1}{-}v^{1}}{\epsilon}\Big)\psi_{0}\Big(\frac{u^{2}{-}v^{2}}{\epsilon}\Big)\psi_{0}\Big(\frac{u^{3}{-}v^{3}}{\epsilon}\Big)\,d^{3}{\underline{u}}^{\prime}\\
&=\kappa^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\sigma\rho}({\underline{u}},{\underline{v}})\end{split}

(40)

As stated this assumes the
\kappa
’s are regular functions. For distributional
\kappa
’s the technique will depend on the nature and support of the distibutions.

III.2
Why are these the most general BCs?

In section
III
we gave a set of BCs. We claimed were the most general BCs which satisfy a set of conditions. In this section we give these conditions and show that (
28
) are indeed the most general BCs that satisfy these conditions. The necessary conditions for our boundary conditions to be general are;

•

Linearity.

•

Causality.

•

Dependence only on the fields on the boundary and their derivatives.

•

The boundary conditions are given by a finite set of distributions,
{\left\{R_{1}({\underline{u}}),\ldots R_{m}({\underline{u}})\right\}}
which have a maximum order.

The statement that the BCs are linear can be expressed as follows. Given two sets of fields
{\left\{F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}\right\}}
and
{\left\{\hat{F}^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},\hat{H}^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},\hat{F}^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu},\hat{H}^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}\right\}}
, which both satisfy the BC.
Then we can add them together to create a new set of field which also satisfy the BCs, that is,
{\left\{F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}+\hat{F}^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}+\hat{H}^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}+\hat{F}^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}+\hat{H}^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}\right\}}
which also satisfy the BC. We can also scale the fields by an arbitrary constant
\lambda
, and they satisfy the BCs, so that
{\left\{\lambda F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},\lambda H^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},\lambda F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu},\lambda H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}\right\}}
satisfy the BC. We also assume the BC are satisfies if all fields are zero. This last statement is a trivial implication of linearity if we assume that there are at least one solution to the BC.

As stated above, we demand that the BCs themselves are causal. That means that information about the field on one side of the boundary does not travel faster than the speed of light to affect the fields on the other side of the boundary.

We also demand that the BCs be local to the
boundary. Otherwise they are not really BCs at all. For example one would not expect the value of fields a significant distance from the boundary to be affected directly by the boundary. Thus the BCs should only depend on the value of electromagnetic fields on the boundary and a finite number of derivatives. There is a relationship between the idea of locality and the idea of dispersion. The nature of this dependency may be divided into three categories. Consider a cause
\alpha
and response
\beta
, both of which are fields on
{\cal M}
.
Let
\alpha|_{\bm{p}}
represent the value of the field
\alpha
at the point
{\bm{p}}\in{\cal M}
. Then we have:

•

Pointwise dependence
: In this case
\alpha|_{\bm{p}}
depends only on the
value of
\beta|_{\bm{p}}
. For example a non dispersive electromagnetic
constitutive relation
H_{\mu\nu}=\tfrac{1}{2}\kappa_{\mu\nu}{}^{\rho\sigma}\,F_{\rho\sigma}
, where
\kappa_{\mu\nu}{}^{\rho\sigma}
is a tensor, is pointwise local.

•

Differential dependence
: In this case
\beta|_{\bm{p}}
depends not
only on the value of
\alpha|_{\bm{p}}
but also on a finite number of
derivatives. Many authors model spatial dispersion in this manner
Khrabustovskyi et al. 2017

For example one may consider
H_{\mu\nu}

to depend on a number of derivatives of
F_{\mu\nu}
. Thus to evaluate

H_{\mu\nu}|_{\bm{p}}
, one needs to know the value of
F_{\mu\nu}
, not only at
{\bm{p}}
but
also in an arbitrary small neighbourhood of
{\bm{p}}
.

•

Extended dependence
: In this case
\beta|_{\bm{p}}
depends on values of
\alpha
over an extended spacetime region. An example of this is the Lorentz model of temporal dispersion. In this case
{\bm{D}}
at time
t
requires integrating
{\bm{E}}
in time.

For this reason we consider BCs which are pointwise
dependent or differential dependent with respect to the transverse
direction, but may be any of the above, including extended dependence
within the boundary.

There is some disagreement in the literature as to whether weak spatial dispersion corresponds to pointwise dependent or
differentiably dependent. If one has a medium which is magnetoelectric, including the case of circularly polarised, then the question of whether this is pointwise local or differentiably local depends on how one expresses the constitutive parameters. If one uses
F_{\mu\nu}
and
H_{\mu\nu}
then once can relate them using a tensor which included spatial dispersion, thus the constitutive relation is pointwise local. Alternatively if one only uses
{\bm{E}}
and
{\bm{H}}
then one must use Maxwell’s equations to construct the corresponding
{\bm{B}}
and
{\bm{D}}
, which are differentiated once. In this case one has a differentiably dependent relationship. In this article we use
F_{\mu\nu}
and
H_{\mu\nu}
and would describe the relationship as pointwise.

We say there is a maximal order if there is some positive integer
k
such that for all
{\left\{z^{k+1}F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},z^{k+1}H^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},z^{k+1}F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu},z^{k+1}H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}\right\}}
then the BCs are satisfied.

We are now in a position to show that these are the most general BC

Theorem 1
.

Given a set of BCs which are linear, causal, local to
\Sigma
with differential dependence and a maximal order, we assume there exist a set of distributions
{\left\{D_{1}({\underline{u}}),\ldots D_{m}({\underline{u}})\right\}}
for each point
{\underline{u}}\in\Sigma
which have as arguments
[F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}]
such that the BCs are given by

\displaystyle D_{A}({\underline{u}})[F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}]=0,

(41)

for all
A=1,\ldots m
and
{\underline{u}}\in\Sigma
.
Then these BCs are given by equation (
28
).

Proof.

In the following we assume we are given the
D_{A}({\underline{u}})
only.
We can extract the order of the BCs, as the minimum value of
k
such that

\displaystyle D_{A}({\underline{u}})[z^{k+1}F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},z^{k+1}H^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},z^{k+1}F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu},z^{k+1}H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}]=0

for all
A
, and all
{\left\{F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}\right\}}
.
From linearity we have

\displaystyle D_{A}({\underline{u}})[F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}]=D^{F{\textup{I}}}_{A}({\underline{u}})[F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}]+D^{H{\textup{I}}}_{A}({\underline{u}})[H^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}]-D^{F{\textup{I\!I}}}_{A}({\underline{u}})[F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}]-D^{H{\textup{I\!I}}}_{A}({\underline{u}})[H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}]

where
D^{F{\textup{I}}}_{A}({\underline{u}})[F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}]=D_{A}({\underline{u}})[F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},0,0,0]
,

D^{F{\textup{I\!I}}}_{A}({\underline{u}})[F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}]=-D_{A}({\underline{u}})[0,0,F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu},0]
, etc. We can Taylor expand

\displaystyle F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}=\sum_{r=0}^{k}\frac{z^{r}}{r!}\frac{\partial^{r}F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}}{\partial z^{r}}\Big|_{z=0}+z^{k+1}{\cal E}_{\mu\nu}

where
z^{k+1}{\cal E}_{\mu\nu}
is the remainder.
Now
D^{F{\textup{I}}}_{A}({\underline{u}})[z^{k+1}{\cal E}_{\mu\nu}]=0
so we have

\displaystyle D^{F{\textup{I}}}_{A}({\underline{u}})[F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}]=\sum_{r=0}^{k}\frac{1}{r!}D^{F{\textup{I}}}_{A}({\underline{u}})\Big[z^{r}\frac{\partial^{r}F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}}{\partial z^{r}}\Big|_{z=0}\Big]=\sum_{r=0}^{k}D^{F{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}({\underline{u}})\Big[\frac{\partial^{r}F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}}{\partial z^{r}}\Big|_{z=0}\Big]

where the distribution
D^{F{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}({\underline{u}})
, is given by

\displaystyle D^{F{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}({\underline{u}})[\alpha_{\mu\nu}]=\frac{1}{r!}D^{F{\textup{I}}}_{A}({\underline{u}})\Big[z^{r}\alpha_{\mu\nu}\Big]

where
\alpha_{\mu\nu}
is independent of
z
.
Since each
D^{F{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}({\underline{u}})
is a distribution only on the 3–dimensional hypersurface
\Sigma

corresponding to
z=0
, we can always write this in terms of a kernel
\kappa^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I}}\mu\nu}_{Ar}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime})
so that

\displaystyle D^{F{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}({\underline{u}})[\alpha_{\mu\nu}]=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}\kappa^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime})\,\alpha_{\mu\nu}\,d^{3}{\underline{u}}^{\prime}

However from causality we have to restrict the domain of the integral to
J^{\Sigma}({\underline{u}})
, giving

\displaystyle D^{F{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}({\underline{u}})[\alpha_{\mu\nu}]=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{J^{\Sigma}({\underline{u}})}\kappa^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I}}}_{Ar}{}^{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime})\,\alpha_{\mu\nu}\,d^{3}{\underline{u}}^{\prime}

Thus we see that
D_{A}({\underline{u}})[F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu},F^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu},H^{\textup{I\!I}}_{\mu\nu}]
, is given by the right hand side of (
36
).

∎

IV
Summary and conclusions

In the above we have both discussed the general tensorial form of the electromagnetic boundary conditions at an arbitrary space–time interface, and given a general framework for describing these interfaces where dispersive effects are important, showing that our boundary condition (
28
) encompasses all possible linear BC which conform to the conditions prescribed in section
III.2
. This gives a framework for future researchers to propose ever increasingly interesting BCs, and can prescribe recipes for their manufacture.

We discussed the meaning of causality in the context of boundary conditions. In particular the requirement that information, such as polaritons, can only propagate in the medium, when it is temporal.

This raises some interesting questions. The above formalism can be applied to, for instance, the problem of a ‘space–time corner’, where the boundary has a discontinuous normal as in Figure
1
(a). This also applies to the ‘space–time’ prism discussed recently
Li et al. 2023b
. At this point of discontinuity the conventional BCs may contradict one another, something known to be problematic in spatial BCs containing sharp corners
Dobrzynski and Maradudin 1972
;
Pendry et al. 2017
. Extensions of the standard wedge diffraction problems
Sommerfeld 2004
;
Malyuzhinets 1955
(see
Osipov and Norris 1999
;
Nethercote et al. 2020
) to space–time boundaries do not yet seem to have been explored.

Although we have specified the most general linear boundary conditions, we have not addressed the question as the the existence of solutions. Maxwell’s equations are linear, and so are the boundary conditions presented here. Assuming that the constitutive relations for the two bulk media are also linear, then we know there is always the zero solution. However, we note that the existence of a physical, non-zero solution starting with initial conditions is not guaranteed. A simple example is a time boundary where the medium switches from being dispersive (
8
) to non-dispersive. One can consider two options. The first, arising from the differential equations gives, for example,
{\bm{D}}^{\textup{I\!I}}={\bm{D}}^{I}
,
{\bm{B}}^{\textup{I\!I}}={\bm{B}}^{I}
and
{\bm{P}}^{\textup{I}}=0
and
\partial_{t}{\bm{P}}^{\textup{I}}=0
on the boundary. However, causality requires that the switch in the material properties can only affect the fields
after
it has occurred, which is inconsistent with demanding
{\bm{P}}^{\textup{I}}=0
and
\partial_{t}{\bm{P}}^{\textup{I}}=0
. By contrast, one can set the BCs to be
{\bm{D}}^{\textup{I\!I}}={\bm{D}}^{I}
and
{\bm{B}}^{\textup{I\!I}}={\bm{B}}^{I}
. In this case the boundary conditions are consistent but all the information about
{\bm{P}}^{\textup{I}}
is lost.

Our formalism includes currents, such as in (
2
), which are linear in the local fields. One can easily incorporate prescribed currents, by simply adding them to the right hand side of (
28
). This can be used to define non trivial problems where information is given on the hypersurface boundary of 4–dimensional volume. This generalises the Dirichlet and von Neumann boundary conditions to higher order and extended dependence within the boundary.

In principle our method can be extended to consider nonlinear BC’s to any finite order wherein the kernel is integrated over each variable an appropriate number of times. For example, for a cubic response one can replace the term in (
28
) given by

\displaystyle\int_{J^{\Sigma}({\underline{u}})}d^{3}{\underline{u}}^{\prime}\kappa^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I}}}_{A0}{}^{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime})\,F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}}^{\prime},0)

(42)

with

\displaystyle\int_{J^{\Sigma}({\underline{u}})}d^{3}{\underline{u}}^{\prime}_{1}\int_{J^{\Sigma}({\underline{u}})}d^{3}{\underline{u}}^{\prime}_{2}\int_{J^{\Sigma}({\underline{u}})}d^{3}{\underline{u}}^{\prime}_{3}\kappa^{{\textup{F}}{\textup{I}}}_{A000}{}^{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}},{\underline{u}}^{\prime}_{1},{\underline{u}}^{\prime}_{2},{\underline{u}}^{\prime}_{3})\,F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}}^{\prime}_{1},0)F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}}^{\prime}_{2},0)F^{\textup{I}}_{\mu\nu}({\underline{u}}^{\prime}_{3},0)

(43)

and similarly for higher derivatives. These topics will form the focus of future research.
