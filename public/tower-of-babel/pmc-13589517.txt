The Role of Powerlessness in Self-Management Behaviours Among Patients with Malignant Fungating Wounds: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Xiaoling Gong, Hon Lon Tam, Xiaomei Zhu, Li Zhu, Di Wei, Huayun Liu, Yige Yang, Zhiyong Yu, Xian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis cross-sectional study aimed to examine self-management behaviours in patients with malignant fungating wounds and to explore the structural pathways linking self-efficacy, powerlessness, and social support to self-management behaviours.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in an outpatient oncology setting. Data were collected between January and October 2024. Mediation analysis was conducted using structural equation modelling, with indirect effects evaluated through bootstrapping with 5000 resamples.Results303 participants were included in the analysis. They achieved a mean self-management behaviour score of 97.72 ± 12.67, indicating a moderate level. Self-management behaviour was negatively correlated with powerlessness (r = −0.72) and positively correlated with self-efficacy (r = 0.60) and social support (r = 0.48). Structural equation modelling revealed that powerlessness had a significant direct negative effect on self-management behaviour (β = −1.33). Importantly, both self-efficacy and social support showed significant indirect effects on self-management behaviour through powerlessness (β = 0.69 and 0.69, respectively). The model explained 83.3% of the variance in self-management behaviours.ConclusionFeelings of powerlessness emerged as a potential psychological mediator in the association between psychosocial determinants and self-management behaviours in this population. These findings underscore the critical need for targeted interventions that explicitly address powerlessness. Healthcare providers can strengthen the theoretical foundation for enhancing self-management practices and offering new pathways to improved health outcomes in patients with malignant fungating wounds.

Background

Cancer represents one of the most significant public health challenges globally. According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2022, approximately 20 million new cancer cases diagnosed worldwide in 2022,1 representing a 3.6% increase compared to 2020.2 Within this population, malignant fungating wounds (MFWs) represent a particularly distressing complication.3

MFWs are complex chronic wounds that develop when malignant tumour cells aggressively proliferate and infiltrate the epidermis, blood vessels, lymphatics, and underlying tissues.4 These wounds may develop in various locations in patients with advanced cancer. Approximately 5–10% of patients with advanced cancer will develop MFWs, typically during the final 6–12 months of life.5,6 Breast cancer accounts for the majority of MFW cases (62–66%), followed by head and neck tumours (24%), tumours of the groin and genital area (3%), and other malignancies (8%).7

The symptom burden associated with MFWs is substantial and profoundly distressing. Common symptoms include malodour (70.5%), bleeding (72.7%), excessive exudate (88.6%), and pain (81.8%).8 These symptoms severely compromise patients’ physical and psychological well-being,9 significantly adversely affecting their quality of life and reducing adherence to treatment.10

Self-Management and Its Clinical Significance

Self-management refers to daily self-care activities performed by patients with chronic wounds to reduce the negative impact on their health. It has been shown to improve clinical outcomes, enhance quality of life, and reduce hospital admissions.11 Patients with chronic wounds have reported that regular self-care activities substantially improve their perceived quality of life.12 When patients actively engage in self-management, they develop greater capacity to cope with their wound conditions, gain greater control over wound-related symptoms, and experience improved well-being and quality of life.12

Understanding Behavioural Change

Research supports the influence of psychosocial factors in self-management behaviours. Among patients with coronary artery disease,13 for example, social support and self-efficacy are associated with engagement in physical activity. Similarly, research on adolescents’ autonomous participation in physical learning behaviours demonstrates that social support functions as both an external opportunity and a psychological activator, reinforcing motivation and transforming environmental signals into internal motivational energy, thereby enhancing engagement in physical learning behaviours.14 These findings highlight that behaviour emerges from the dynamic interplay of psychosocial factors.

Although these studies provide valuable evidence for the general population, patients with MFWs encounter unique challenges. Unlike other chronic wounds, MFWs are incurable, and the prolonged nature of self-management often generates persistent physical discomfort coupled with psychological distress.8 This context suggests that feelings of powerlessness, a psychological state that unable to achieve desired outcomes, may play a particularly critical role in self-management for this population. Understanding how psychological factors, particular feelings of powerlessness, influence self-management in patients with MFWs could illuminate new intervention strategies.

Guided by the COM-B model and previous self-management research, self-management behaviours can be understood as being shaped by patients’ capability, opportunity and motivation.15 In this study, self-efficacy was considered to reflect patients’ confidence in managing wound-related demands, whereas social support represented an important external resource for self-management. Powerlessness was included in the hypothesized mediation model because it reflects patients’ perceived loss of control over wound symptoms, disease progression and daily self-care demands, which is particularly relevant to malignant fungating wounds characterized by malodour, exudate, bleeding, pain and unpredictable outcomes.8 Although anxiety,16 depression and resilience are important psychological constructs in patients with chronic illness, powerlessness is conceptually distinct from these broader emotional responses. Specifically, powerlessness refers to the perception that one’s own actions do not significantly influence outcomes, together with a perceived lack of control over current situations or events.17 This construct is therefore more closely aligned with perceived controllability of illness and self-care behaviours. Similarly, previous studies in chronic disease populations have used structural equation modelling to examine complex direct and indirect associations among patient-related factors, disease-related experiences and health outcomes.18,19

Study Aim

This study aimed to explore two research questions: (1) the levels of self-efficacy, powerlessness, and social support among patients with MFWs, and (2) the mediating role of powerlessness in the relationship between psychosocial factors and self-management behaviours.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Participants

This study adopted a cross-sectional design and employed convenience sampling. Patients with malignant fungating wounds (MFWs) were recruited from the outpatient department of a tertiary cancer hospital between January and October 2024. Participants were included if they (1) had a pathologically confirmed diagnosis of malignant tumour cell infiltration in their wound and (2) were aged 18 years or older. Patients were excluded if they had (a) cognitive impairment or a history of psychiatric disorders, (b) coagulation dysfunction, or (c) other major health conditions such as heart failure, renal failure, or respiratory failure.

Sample Size

Based on the recommended ratio of 10 participants per item and the 26-item self-efficacy management behaviour scale, a target sample size of 260 was calculated. Allowing for a 15% potential attrition rate, at least total 299 participants were required for analysis.

Data Collection

Data were collected through an on-site questionnaire survey in the wound and ostomy outpatient clinic. Potentially eligible patients were screened according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. A research assistant provided verbal and written information about the study, including its purpose, procedures, voluntary nature and confidentiality, and potential participants were given the opportunity to ask questions. Those who agreed to participate provided written informed consent and then completed the questionnaire independently in a quiet and relatively private area during the same clinic visit. Standardized instructions were provided by the trained research assistant before completion of the questionnaire, and clarification was given when needed without suggesting or influencing responses. Wound-related clinical information was recorded by the researcher based on wound assessment and information obtained from participants. Completed questionnaires were checked for completeness and response quality, and those with substantial missing responses or obvious logical inconsistencies were excluded from the analysis.

Measurement Instruments

Demographic and Clinical Variables

Demographic variables included sex, age, residential areas, educational level, employment status, and monthly household income. Studies showed that these variables would affect self-management behaviours.20,21 Clinical variables encompassed wound location, wound size (in cm2), pain intensity, wound exudate volume, and odour level.

Self-Management Behaviour

The Chinese self-management behaviour scale for patients with chronic wounds is a 5-point Likert scale comprising 26 items across five dimensions: wound care, nutritional management, daily living activities, management of comorbidities, and emotional regulation.22 Each item is rated from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”), with total scores ranging from 26 to 130. Higher scores indicate stronger self-management behaviours. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.89) and excellent construct validity (0.99).22

Self-Efficacy

The general self-efficacy scale was originally developed by Schwarzer et al and subsequently translated into Chinese.23 It is a unidimensional instrument consisting of 10 items, each rated on a 4-point Likert (1 = “not at all true”; 4 = “exactly true”). Total scores range from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater confidence in performing daily self-care. Scores are categorised into three levels: 10–19 (low), 20–30 (moderate), or 31–40 (high). The scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.87) and a satisfactory construct validity (0.83).23

Powerlessness

The Chinese version of the adult powerlessness assessment assesses feelings of powerlessness across two dimensions: self-perception of behavioural control and decision-making (nine items), and emotional responses to perceived control (three items).24 The 12-item scale uses a 5-point Likert format (1 = “never”; 5 = “always”), with total scores ranging from 12 to 60. Scores are interpreted as: 0–12 indicates no perceived powerlessness, 13–24 mild powerlessness, 25–36 moderate powerlessness, 37–48 severe powerlessness, and 49–60 extreme powerlessness. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.96) and strong construct validity (0.88).

Social Support

The Social Support Rating Scale comprises 10 items measuring three dimensions: subjective support, objective support, and utilisation of social support.25 Total scores range from 12 to 66, and are categorised as low (<22), moderate (23–44), or high (≥45). The scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.77.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 27.0. Continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationships between self-management behaviours, self-efficacy, powerlessness, and social support. Structural equation modelling (SEM) using AMOS version 24.0 explored the structural associations among social support, self-efficacy, and powerlessness to self-management behaviours. The maximum likelihood estimation method was used. Before conducting SEM, model assumptions and potential biases were assessed. The distributional normality of the main observed variables was examined using SPSS 27.0, and the results suggested that the assumption of normality was not fully supported. Given this deviation, bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5000 resamples was used to estimate the indirect effects and their confidence intervals. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor values, which ranged from 1.60 to 3.10, indicating no serious multicollinearity among the variables included in the model. Common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test, and the first unrotated factor accounted for 36.0% of the total variance, suggesting that common method bias was unlikely to substantially affect the findings. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including the chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (CMIN/Df <3), goodness-of-fit index (GFI >0.90), normed fit index (NFI >0.90), comparative fit index (CFI >0.90), parsimonious fit index (PFI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA <0.05). Bootstrap-based mediation analysis was performed using 5000 resamples, and a 95% bias-corrected confidence interval (CI) to evaluate the mediating role of powerlessness in the hypothesised structural model. An indirect effect was considered statistically significant if the 95% CI did not include zero. Statistical significance was set at p <0.05.

Ethical Considerations

This study follows the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the University of South China (approval number: 2023NHHL018). Participation was voluntary and anonymous. All participants were assured of confidentiality, with identifying information removed and data access was restricted to the research team.

Results

Characteristics of the Participants

Of 320 distributed questionnaires, 303 valid responses were returned, yielding a response rate of 94.7%. Questionnaires containing missing item responses were excluded before statistical analysis. Consequently, the final analytic dataset comprised 303 participants with complete data for all study variables, and no missing-value imputation was required. The majority of participants were female (n = 198, 65.3%) with a mean age of 53.09±10.89 years. Approximately half of the participants were aged 45–59 years (n = 150, 49.5%). Most participants resided in urban areas (n = 186, 61.4%) and had completed junior high school or lower education levels (n = 171, 56.4%). The bootstrap-based mediation analysis (Table 5) showed that self-efficacy was significantly indirectly associated with self-management behaviour through powerlessness (β = 0.69, 95% CI [0.376, 2.076]). While the direct association between self-efficacy and self-management behaviour was not statistically significant, the total association remained significant (β = 0.40, 95% CI [0.263, 0.532]). Similarly, social support was significantly indirectly associated with self-management behaviour through powerlessness (β = 0.69, 95% CI [0.415, 2.608]). Although the direct association between social support and self-management behaviour did not reach statistical significance, the total association remained significant (β = 0.35, 95% CI [0.230, 0.562]). Discussion

This study employed structural equation modelling to systematically examine the interrelationships between self-efficacy, powerlessness, social support, and self-management behaviours in patients with MFWs. The hypothesised model accounted for 83.3% of the variance in self-management behaviours, suggesting that it explained a substantial proportion of variance in this sample and providing preliminary support for the relevance of powerlessness in this vulnerable population.

An important finding was that powerlessness appeared to play a potential mediating role in the associations between psychosocial resources and self-management behaviours. In the structural model, self-efficacy and social support were not directly associated with self-management behaviours; instead, they were indirectly associated with self-management behaviours through powerlessness. Both self-efficacy and social support were significantly negatively associated with powerlessness, and powerlessness was negatively associated with self-management behaviours. This statistical mediation pattern may provide preliminary evidence to inform future intervention development.

Distinguishing MFWs from Other Chronic Wounds

Patients with MFWs in this study exhibited a substantially greater symptom burden compared to those in studies examining other chronic wounds, such as diabetic foot ulcers and venous leg ulcers.26,27 Their wounds were characterised by more pronounced odour, exudate, and larger wound size. Moreover, MFWs are closely associated with the progression of the malignancy and deterioration,28,29 creating a fundamentally different clinical context than chronic wounds that may heal or remit.8 Consequently, nursing care for patients with MFWs focuses on palliation, symptom control, and quality of life enhancement rather than disease cure.30

In addition, the study population’s predominantly female composition reflects the epidemiology of MFWs, particularly the high prevalence of breast cancer as a primary cause.10 Furthermore, this study revealed that patients with MFWs reported a mean powerlessness score of 36.80±7.52, substantially higher than previously documented scores among patients with diabetic foot ulcers and venous leg ulcers.31 This finding indicates that the MFW population experiences not only profound physical burden but also significantly greater psychological distress and elevated feelings of powerlessness.

Reducing Powerlessness in Self-Efficacy

Previous research has suggested that self-efficacy is positively associated with self-management behaviours.32 Patients with higher self-efficacy typically tend to have greater confidence when facing challenges, which may help them to correct maladaptive behaviours and maintain healthy lifestyles. However, this direct association was not observed in the present MFW population. Self-efficacy in patients with MFWs was not directly associated with self-management behaviours. Instead, self-efficacy was indirectly associated with self-management behaviours through powerlessness. Even when patients possessed high self-efficacy, feelings of powerlessness or lack of control over their situation were associated with poorer self-management behaviours.

This phenomenon appears closely related to the incurable nature of MFWs and the complex and distressing symptoms.33 Persistent pain and symptoms, including bleeding, exudation, and malodour,34 diminish patient’s perception of disease controllability. They prevent patients from translating their abilities and confidence into effective self-management behaviours. According to the theory of learned helplessness,35 when individuals perceive outcomes as uncontrollable, their inherent capabilities become overwhelmed, leading to passive behaviour even when they possess necessary skills and knowledge. Therefore, in the MFW context, self-efficacy functions as a latent resource rather than a direct driver of behaviour change. Its relevance to self-management may partly depend on patients’ feelings of powerlessness.

The Nuanced Role of Social Support

Prior research emphasised that strong social support provides patients with essential resources, such as medical knowledge, medication-related information, and financial security, while enhancing coping capacity through emotional reassurance and informational guidance.36,37 This support may facilitate the adoption of self-management behaviours.

Nevertheless, the present study revealed that social support was not directly associated with self-management behaviours among patients with MFWs. Instead, social support appeared to be indirectly associated with self-management behaviours through powerlessness. This finding suggests that social support may be relevant to self-management partly through its association with lower levels of powerlessness. Lower levels of powerlessness may partly explain the association between social support and self-management behaviours. One possible interpretation is that patients who perceive adequate social support may also report lower levels of powerlessness, which may be related to greater perceived opportunity and motivation. These perceived motivational and opportunity-related factors may be associated with greater engagement in self-management behaviours. Taken together, these findings support the potential mediating role of powerlessness within the hypothesised model.

Limitations

This study had some limitations that warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, requiring cautious interpretation of mediation effects. The RMSEA value was relatively high, suggesting some degree of model misfit. This may be related to the complexity and heterogeneity of MFWs and the possibility that other clinically relevant variables were not included in the hypothesised model. Second, self-reported data may have introduced social desirability bias. Third, participants were from a single medical centre, potentially limiting generalizability. However, the demographic and clinical heterogeneity of participants partially mitigated this limitation. Future studies should employ multicentre, large-sample, longitudinal designs to examine proposed mediation mechanisms, thereby refining the theoretical model and facilitating its translation into clinical practice.

Implications

The findings of this study have several important implications for nursing practice. While symptom management remains important for patients with MFWs, nurses should prioritise early identification and intervention for feelings of powerlessness.38 Evidence-based wound care protocols combined with individualised health education may help alleviate distressing symptoms, such as malodour, pain, and exudate,8,39 thereby reducing patients’ feelings of powerlessness and enhancing their engagement in self-management behaviours.

Beyond symptom management, psychological approaches warrant attention. Cognitive behavioural interventions and mindfulness-based therapies can effectively mitigate negative emotional responses regarding their health status40 and wound condition,41 while simultaneously strengthening self-efficacy necessary for maintaining effective self-management. Strengthening social support from healthcare providers, family members, and peers is equally essential, as this support reduces the adverse motivational states associated with powerlessness and indirectly enhance self-management behaviours.42 On the other hand, digital health technologies provide continuous support for wound care that reduces powerlessness while enhancing self-management behaviours over time.43

Recommendations for Intervention Development

The findings suggest that simply enhancing self-efficacy or providing external social support may be insufficient to substantially improve self-management behaviours in patients with MFWs. Instead, future intervention development may need to pay greater attention to patients’ negative psychological experiences, particularly the feelings of powerlessness. Strategies targeting powerlessness may help strengthen patients’ intrinsic motivation and support the initiation and maintenance of self-management behaviours. Future intervention studies should integrate powerlessness reduction strategies to optimize outcomes for this vulnerable population.

Conclusions

This study employed structural equation modelling to systematically examine the interrelationships between self-efficacy, social support, powerlessness, and self-management behaviours in patients with MFWs. Powerlessness showed a potential mediating role in the statistical associations between self-efficacy, social support and self-management behaviours, and was negatively associated with self-management behaviours. These findings reframe our understanding of self-management in the MFW population. Rather than viewing self-efficacy and social support as direct drivers of behaviour change, they may be better understood as foundational psychosocial resources that are indirectly associated with self-management behaviours through lower levels of powerlessness. This insight may provide preliminary guidance for future practice and intervention development. By developing and implementing targeted intervention that explicitly address feelings of powerlessness, healthcare providers can unlock the protective and motivational potential of self-efficacy and social support. This approach offers a theoretically grounded direction for enhancing self-management behaviours and improving health outcomes for MFW population facing an incurable, distressing, and life-limiting condition.
