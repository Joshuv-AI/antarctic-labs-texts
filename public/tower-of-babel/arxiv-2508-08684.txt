Out of the Box, into the Clinic? Evaluating State-of-the-Art ASR for Clinical Applications for Older Adults
Bram van Dijk, Tiberon Kuiper, Sirin Aoulad si Ahmed, Armel Levebvre, Jake Johnson, Jan Duin, Simon Mooijaart, Marco Spruit

Abstract
Voice-controlled interfaces can support older adults in clinical contexts – with chatbots being a prime example – but reliable Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) for underrepresented groups remains a bottleneck. This study evaluates state-of-the-art ASR models on language use of older Dutch adults, who interacted with the Welzijn.AI chatbot designed for geriatric contexts. We benchmark generic multilingual ASR models, and models fine-tuned for Dutch spoken by older adults, while also considering processing speed. Our results show that generic multilingual models outperform fine-tuned models, which suggests recent ASR models can generalise well out of the box to realistic datasets. Furthermore, our results suggest that truncating existing architectures is helpful in balancing the accuracy-speed trade-off, though we also identify some cases with high WER due to hallucinations.



1
Introduction

Although there is a surge of interest in AI-driven applications like chatbots in the health domain
(
Guo et al. 2024
;
Huo et al. 2025
)
, tailoring them to groups underrepresented in AI research remains a challenge. Older populations are one example: because they often have different needs and preferences when interacting with AI
(
van Dijk et al. 2025
;
Klaassen et al. 2025
)
, their involvement in system development is key for building clinically relevant systems in geriatrics, the field in healthcare concerned with the health of older adults. This population is increasing in size globally
(
Organisation 2023
)
, in a time where personnel shortages in healthcare become pressing
(
Union 2023
)
, yet addressing these challenges with AI warrants systems that align well with older populations.

Voice control is a key element in chatbots in geriatrics, as older individuals may struggle with small fonts, icons, and typing text in standard interfaces
(
Khamaj 2025
)
. Yet, implementing voice control is not obvious, as the performance of Automatic Speech Transcription (ASR) systems depends on the representation of older people in training data, and on their articulation, speech volume and experience with technology
(
Klaassen et al. 2025
)
. Still, evaluation of state-of-the-art ASR systems on realistic data of older individuals is lagging behind.

In this short paper we evaluate recent ASR models on older individuals’ language use in interaction with
Welzijn.AI
. This is a new digital platform for older users, that currently features a prototype chatbot to converse about clinically relevant topics like quality of life and frailty. Audio data of 10 Dutch older individuals interacting with
Welzijn.AI
was transcribed using generic multilingual ASR models (Whisper and Voxtral), and models fine-tuned specifically for Dutch or older Dutch populations (Whisper and Wav2Vec). Our findings show that i) generic multilingual models outperform fine-tuned models, and also suggest that ii) truncating larger architectures may help striking a good balance between accuracy and speed. These findings also hold for a similar subset of the Common Voice (CV) dataset we use as benchmark.

2
Background

Though work on evaluating ASR models for older persons exists, extrapolating findings to realistic contexts is hard. Performance of ASR models sometimes remains implicit in downstream use, for example through evaluation of overlap of linguistic features extracted from model vs. human-generated transcripts
(
Naffah et al. 2025
)
, or by using only part of ASR models (like the audio encoder) in predicting cognitive impairment in older persons
(
Agbavor and Liang 2024
)
. Other work tailors ASR systems to older individuals by drawing on additional databases of individual users
Xu et al. 2025
, which is not always feasible in a clinical context due to privacy concerns. However,
Xu et al. 2025
also show that fine-tuning generic multilingual models with speech from older persons increases performance. The work by
Shekoufandeh et al. 2025
explores this further by fine-tuning Whisper as common standard ASR model,
(
Radford et al. 2023
)
on the Dutch JASMIN-CGN dataset. This dataset includes language use of older users in a human-machine interaction setting designed to evoke speech phenomena associated with ASR errors, like additional emphasis, hesitation and frustration
Cucchiarini and Van hamme 2012
, which are also relevant to
Welzijn.AI
.

3
Materials and methods

Ten older Dutch volunteers (
\geq
65 years) were recruited from the geriatrics outpatient clinic of the Leiden University Medical Center. The geriatric department’s ethics review board approved this study. Participants interacted individually with the chatbot, with one experimenter standing by for help.

An impression of the chatbot is given in
Figure 1
. Here we discuss only the aspects relevant for supporting conversations, and refer for details on architectures that inspired this system to
van Dijk et al. 2025
. The chatbot was driven by the Llama3.3 70B Instruct language model
Dubey et al. 2024
, prompted to have a conversation structured around the EQ-5D and Clinical Frailty Scale
(
Brooks 1996
;
Rockwood et al. 2005
)
. These are common geriatric instruments to assess health-related quality of life and level of frailty. The instruments retrieve information about mobility, mental wellbeing, pain, physical independence, and so on. They can be used in questionnaire form, or in natural conversations with clinicians, or chatbots (topics emerge in the example conversation in
Figure 1
).

The chatbot ran on a laptop, and interactions took 5-10 minutes and were recorded with a phone, anticipating using the app on handheld devices and relying on built-in microphones. The ASR model used in the chatbot was Whisper large V3, which in early testing we found to work best.

For obtaining gold standard (human) transcriptions, segments were transcribed with the default Whisper large V3 model, and subsequently manually corrected by the first author. Since the Word Error Rate (WER) as standard metric in the ASR field is sensitive to fillers, capitalisation, and punctuation, and since the ‘right’ gold transcription depends on the use case, we created three types of human and model transcriptions as visible in
Table 1
:
orthographic
(including punctuation, fillers, and capitalisation)
orthographic_clean
(only capitalisation, as also used in
Figure 1
), and
normalised
(no capitals, fillers, or punctuation). Orthographic transcription is useful in that it provides additional structure, though speech content is often arguably sufficiently preserved in normalised transcriptions, with orthographic_clean transcription striking a balance between the strictest and most flexible evaluation scenarios.

Table 2
shows the ASR models included in our experiments. We focus on the Whisper model family as the current standard in the field, and include the Voxtral 4.68B model
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
as fitting contender regarding size. We include the Whisper model fine-tuned for Dutch older individuals by
Shekoufandeh et al. 2025
, and also included a work-in-progress Wav2Vec model fine-tuned for Dutch in general
(
Baevski et al. 2020
)
by the same authors, as older but lightweight and fast contender. We note that these fine-tuned models do not output capitals, punctuation, or fillers by default, hence evaluate them with normalised transcripts. We opt for these models as they are all small (compared to Voxtral 24B for example) and open weights, hence suitable for downstream applications. We take processing time into account since a good balance between accuracy and speed is key in many applications, so include models of different sizes.

4
Results

WER and processing time per model are given in
Table 3
. Regarding WER, on both the
Welzijn.AI
and CV data, Whisper V3 large as generic multilingual model outperforms all others, even for normalised transcripts as most flexible evaluation scenario. In terms of processing time, on both datasets, the Wav2Vec model fine-tuned on Dutch data is the fastest, but also the least accurate.

For chatbot applications like
Welzijn.AI
, understanding the trade-off between WER and processing time is crucial, and we visualise performance on normalised transcripts in
Figure 2
. Here we see that the Whisper V3 large turbo model, which is essentially Whisper V3 large but with a truncated decoder 1/8 its size, strikes the best balance. The ‘nearest’ improvement in WER concerns Whisper V3 large, which is almost three times slower, while the ‘nearest’ improvement in processing time comes from Whisper small, at the expense of more than twice its WER.
Figure 2
, also shows CV is an easier dataset regarding WER, though not all models process these data faster. For CV the same observations hold regarding the ‘nearest’ improvements in WER and processing times compared to Whisper V3 large turbo: they lead to considerable drops in speed and accuracy.

To get an idea of problematic inputs for ASR models, we give examples of inputs with the highest WER in
Table 4
, which as expected come only from interaction data. Some seem entirely hallucinated (1, 4 and 5), some seem to align slightly better with phonemes (2, 6), or original meaning (3). High WER occurs for all models, which aligns with earlier documented unpredictability in state-of-the-art ASR models
(
Koenecke et al. 2024
)
, that should inform their development and deployment in high-stakes contexts.

5
Discussion and conclusion

We evaluated state-of-the-art ASR models on transcribing language use of older individuals interacting with the
Welzijn.AI
chatbot, which was designed for geriatrics. We included various generic multilingual models as well as models fine-tuned on language use of older Dutch populations and general Dutch. We found that on both chatbot interaction and CV data, generic multilingual models perform better than fine-tuned models, with Whisper V3 large emerging as best model. Interestingly, its truncated variant Whisper V3 large turbo struck the best balance between accuracy and processing speed. This is useful from a systems development perspective, since these models may perform well out-of-the-box, without the need for training architectures with smaller numbers of parameters for faster processing from scratch, or the need for additional data for fine-tuning to a specific group or task. Still, inspection of transcription errors across the board revealed some challenging inputs with high WER due to what seem hallucinations, where there is little connection between the reference qua semantics or phonemes and transcript.

Other evaluations of state-of-the-art ASR models on the entire Dutch subset in CV data reported WERs of .06 by Voxtral 4.68B
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
, and .04 by Whisper V3 large.

6
Limitations

Though dedicated GPUs in HPC clusters have higher bandwidths and are faster, hence the default choice in experiments like ours, unified architectures (such as provided by Apple’s Silicon M series) are receiving more attention nowadays due to their benefits in terms of latency, energy consumption and system footprint, and are recognized as efficient and competitive alternatives to dedicated GPUs
(
Hübner et al. 2025
;
Kenyon and Capano 2022
)
. So faster processing times could probably be attained by using dedicated GPUs or by using a more recent M-chip. Still, we anticipate a scenario where one device hosts multiple smaller AI models to do different tasks, for which our setup can provide a good lower bound.

Also, though we tried to make comparison fair for different ASR models by evaluating with different kinds of transcripts, developing further transcript normalisations to take ‘acceptable errors’ into account, e.g. writing numbers in digits or letters (‘8’, ‘eight’), were beyond the scope of the current work. This means that there can be some noise in our performance estimates.
