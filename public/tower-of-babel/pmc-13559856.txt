Eriosema glomeratum Possessed Interesting Bioactive Constituents and Semisynthetic Derivatives With Relevant Biosynthetic Correlation and Important Chemophenetic Significance
Authors: Gaétan Tchangou Tabakam, Maurice Ducret Awouafack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlants are sources of compounds with benefits in human health and animal well‐being. Plant‐based foods may contain huge numbers of different phytochemicals. This is the case of a Cameroonian medicinal plant Eriosema glomeratum from which phytochemical studies reported so far, the isolation of 22 compounds (1–22) with 4 new ones. Several semisynthesis were done on erioschalcones A and B, two new dihydrochalcones isolated from this plant, to afford 18 (23–38) derivatives from which thirteen were new. Among the compounds isolated and their derivatives, several had significant inhibitory activities against microbial strains Bacillus megaterium, Escherichia coli, Chlorella fusca and Microbotryum violaceum; and strong to moderate cytotoxicities on human cancer cell lines, lung (A549), breast (MCF‐7), and cervical (HeLa). Compounds were mostly alkyl caffeate, flavonols, isoflavonoids, and dihydrochalcones. The use of medicinal plants for their benefits in the mankind is daily increasing and their chemotaxonomic categorization and the natural origin of their metabolites are essential for more valorization. In this work, we have summarized all the investigations carried out on E. glomeratum and discussed the relevant correlation on the biosynthetic relationship between the different classes of compounds, the structure–activity relationship (SAR), as well as the chemophenetic importance of isoflavonoids and dihydrochalcones.Keywords: biosynthesis and chemophenetic, constituents, Eriosema glomeratum, SAR, semisynthesis

Introduction

Plants are the considerable sources of an inestimable diversity of natural products with manifold chemical structures and functionalities [1]. Flavonoids are low‐molecular‐weight polyphenolic phytochemicals and potent molecules having various cellular targets and could be used for the treatment of several chronic diseases. In the last few decades, antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, antiallergic, antimicrobial, antiviral, antispasmodic, and anticancer properties of flavonoids have been well demonstrated [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. The Fabaceae is commonly known as the most important family of flowering plants. It is widely distributed and regarded as the third‐largest land plant family in terms of number of species, behind the Orchidaceae and Asteraceae, with about 751 genera and some 19 000 known species [7, 8]. Among these different genera, we have the Eriosema genus which contains approximately 160 species, mostly distributed in tropical regions of Africa, and are used in traditional medicine to treat various diseases [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Based on their traditional uses, phytochemical, pharmacological and toxicological investigations of different Eriosema species have been performed. Nevertheless, till now only few reviews dealing with Eriosema species have been published [14]. In 2015, Awouafack et al. [11] demonstrated that Eriosema species represent a rich source of flavonoids with interesting pharmacological activities compiling a total of 52 flavonoids (isoflavones, dihydroflavonols, isoflavanones, flavonols, flavanones, and dihydrochalcones) isolated. However, the pharmacological properties were not limited to the presence of flavonoids. In the literature, compounds from other secondary metabolite classes as well as plant extracts (more easily available and affordable than isolated compounds) demonstrated pharmacological properties [14]. The work of Tabakam et al. in 2022 [13] thus demonstrated the strong cytotoxicity of the aerial part of Eriosema glomeratum. This plant species is used in many countries around Africa for the treatment of several diseases. To the best of our knowledge, the chemotaxonomic significance of chemical constituents from most Eriosema plants have not been reported, but the particular phytochemical study carried out on E. glomeratum species from 2008 to date showed the presence of flavonoids, alcohols, triterpenoids, and steroids [13, 15, 16]; ceramide, alkyl caffeate, and cerebroside [16]; and fatty acid [15]. The present review documented all the compounds reported from E. glomeratum, and their semisynthetic derivatives. It has highlighted the important biosynthetic relationship between the different classes of compounds isolated from E. glomeratum, and has discussed their chemophenetic significance. Although previous reviews have summarized the phytochemistry and pharmacological properties of the genus Eriosema, none has specifically focused on E. glomeratum, one of the most chemically promising species of the genus. Since the publication of earlier reviews, several phytochemical investigations have led to the discovery of new natural metabolites and numerous semisynthetic derivatives, particularly erioschalcones A and B and their structurally modified analogues. These compounds have demonstrated remarkable antimicrobial and cytotoxic activities, making E. glomeratum an attractive source of lead molecules for drug discovery. Despite these advances, the available information remains scattered among independent studies, making it difficult to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the phytochemistry, biosynthetic relationships, biological significance, and chemophenetic value of this species. Moreover, neither previous review has critically discussed the structure–activity relationships (SARs) of the isolated compounds and their derivatives, nor evaluated their potential significance for the chemotaxonomy of the genus Eriosema. The present review therefore aims to fill this knowledge gap by providing a comprehensive and critical overview of all phytochemical investigations carried out on E. glomeratum. In addition to compiling the reported secondary metabolites and their semisynthetic derivatives, this review discusses their biological activities, biosynthetic relationships, SARs, and chemophenetic significance. Particular attention is also given to current limitations of the available studies and future research opportunities, including metabolomics, molecular docking, pharmacokinetic evaluation, biosynthetic gene mining, synthetic biology, and the discovery of new bioactive natural products.

Semisynthetic Derivatives

The major constituents erioschalcone A (6) and erioschalcone B (7), isolated from E. glomeratum, belong to the dihydrochalcone class, a group that is rarely encountered in natural plant sources [17]. These two compounds were subjected to semisynthetic modifications, leading to the production of seventeen dihydrochalcone derivatives (23–37) and one chalcone (38). A first transformation involved linear and angular cyclization reactions between the isoprenyl group and the hydroxyl functions of compounds 6 and 7. This reaction, performed with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) [18], resulted in the loss of the isoprenyl double bond and yielded four derivatives (23–26) [17] (TFA cyclization) (Scheme 1a). Compounds 6 and 7 were then methylated using trimethylsulfonium hydroxide (TMSH) [19] and triethylamine (TEA, 10% in toluene), producing four additional derivatives (27–30) [17] (methylation) (Scheme 1b). A subsequent acetylation step, carried out at room temperature with pyridine and acetic anhydride, afforded four acetylated derivatives (31–34) [13] (acetylation) (Scheme 1c). Finally, further linear and angular cyclization reactions this time preserving the double bond of the isoprenyl group were performed using 2,3‐dichloro‐5,6‐dicyano‐1,4‐benzoquinone (DDQ) in benzene under reflux (1 h at 90°C). This procedure generated four more derivatives (35–38) [13] (DDQ cyclization) (Scheme 1d). Traditional Uses and Ethnopharmacology of E. glomeratum

Traditional Uses

Since centuries, Eriosema species have widely been traditionally used as household remedies against various human ailments [14]. It is particularly the case of E. glomeratum which the leaves and the leafy stem are used for the treatment of diabetes and for promoting the closure of fontanel, respectively in Benin while the roots are applied to hernia treatment in Burkina Faso [16, 23, 24, 25]. In the Bakola Pygmy tribe in Cameroon, the whole plant of E. glomeratum is used to treat leprosy, venereal diseases, pulmonary troubles and nasopharyngeal infections, while their fresh leaves are utilized as remedy for diarrhea and dysentery in Angola [16, 26, 27, 28].

Antimicrobial Activity of Isolated Compounds and Derivatives From E. glomeratum

The antimicrobial activity of erioschalcones A (6) and B (7) and other constituents from E. glomeratum was determined using agar diffusion and the radius of the zone of inhibition (RZI) (in mm) obtained against four microbial strains, Bacillus megaterium (7 and 10 mm), Escherichia coli (10 and 8 mm), Chlorella fusca (7 and 9 mm) and Microbotryum violaceum (7 and 13 mm) [15], respectively. Isoluteolin (2) and quercetin (9) had a RZI value of 10 mm against the green alga, C. fusca. The inhibitions were comparable to those of the control antibiotics, the antibacterial agents, penicillin and tetracycline, the fungicide, nystatin, and the antialgal and antifungal substance, actidione (cycloheximide). Erioschalcones A (6) and B (7) both inhibited the growth of C. fusca [15] (Table 6). Further in vitro antimicrobial activities of erioschalcone A (6) (RZI = 7.0 and 9.0 mm), erioschalcone B (7) (RZI = 7.3 and 7.3 mm) and semisynthetic derivatives were evaluated towards Klebsiella pneumoniae subsp. ozaenae (DSM 681), and E. coli (DSM 682), respectively. The derivatives 1‐(5‐hydroxy‐2,2‐dimethyl‐3,4‐dihydro‐2H‐chromen‐8‐yl)‐3‐(4‐methoxyphenyl) propan‐1‐one (23) and 1‐(5‐hydroxy‐2,2‐dimethyl‐3,4‐dihydro‐2H‐chromen‐6‐yl)‐3‐(4‐methoxyphenyl)propan‐1‐one (24) displayed a RZI value of 7.0 mm in both cases against K. pneumoniae subsp. ozaenae (DSM 681) and E. coli (DSM 682) [17]. Moreover, the in vitro antimicrobial activities of the derivatives 1‐(5‐hydroxy‐2,2‐dimethyl‐3,4‐dihydro‐2H‐chromen‐6‐yl)‐3‐phenylpropan‐1‐one (26) (RZI = 7.3 and 7.0 mm), 1‐[2,4‐dimethoxy‐3‐(3‐methylbut‐2 enyl)phenyl]‐3‐(4‐methoxyphenyl)propan‐1‐one (27) (RZI = 7.3 and 7.3 mm), and 1‐[2,4‐dimethoxy‐3‐(3‐methylbut‐2‐ enyl)phenyl]‐3‐phenylpropan‐1‐one (30) (RZI = 7.3 and 8.0 mm) were reported against the same bacteria K. pneumoniae subsp. ozaenae (DSM 681) and E. coli (DSM 682) with RZI ranging 7 and 8 mm. The inhibitions were comparable to those of the control antibiotics, the antibacterial agents, tetracycline, streptomycin, ampicillin and gentamicin [17] (Table 7).

Cytotoxic Activity of Extract, Fractions, Isolated Compounds and Derivatives From the Roots, and the Aerial Part of E. glomeratum

The in vitro cytotoxic activity of the crude extract, fractions, isolated compounds and derivatives from the roots and aerial part of E. glomeratum on three human [lung (A549), breast (MCF‐7), and cervical (HeLa)] cancer cell lines were reported and suggesting some preliminary potential anticancer agents (Table 8). The MTT [3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazolyl‐2)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide] cell viability assay was used [13]. The IC50 values obtained were classified using reported cut‐off points [31] (Table 8). We reported the strong cytotoxicity of the CH2Cl2/MeOH extract of the aerial part of E. glomeratum against MCF‐7, HeLa and A549 (IC50 11.2 ± 1.3, 8.4 ± 1.0, and 13.1 ± 1.3 µg/mL), respectively; the strong cytotoxicity of the n‐hexane fraction from the aerial part on MCF‐7 (IC50 11.2 ± 3.0 µg/mL) and the moderate activity on HeLa and A549 (IC50 22.7 ± 2.6 and 41.3 ± 4.4 µg/mL); the moderate cytotoxicity of the EtOAc fraction from the aerial part on MCF‐7 (IC50 48.0 µg/mL) and the low cytotoxic activity on HeLa and A549 (IC50 51.5 ± 7.9 and 82.9 ± 5.1 µg/mL); the low cytotoxic activity of the CH2Cl2 fraction from the aerial part on MCF‐7, HeLa and A549 (IC50 97.4 ± 27.4, 65.8 ± 14.2, and 99.0 ± 8.0 µg/mL) [13]. Isolated compounds erioschalcone A (6) from the aerial part of E. glomeratum showed a strong cytotoxicity on MCF‐7 (IC50 7.6 ± 1.4 µM) and moderate activity on HeLa and A549 (IC50 23.7 ± 2.2 and 29.0 ± 5.0 µM), respectively; erioschalcone B (7) showed a moderate activity on MCF‐7, HeLa, and A549 (IC50 10.4 ± 1.9, 23.9 ± 3.3, and 23.0 ± 2.3 µM), respectively; 2',6'‐dihydroxy‐4'‐methoxydihydrochalcone (8) showed a moderate activity on MCF‐7 and HeLa (IC50 19.9 ± 3.3 and 24.7 ± 3.9 µM), respectively; and a low activity on A549 (IC50 61.7 ± 14.5 µM) [13]. We noted the strong cytotoxicity of the derivatives 4‐(3‐(4‐methoxyphenyl)propanoyl)‐2‐(3‐methylbut‐2‐en‐1‐yl)‐1,3‐phenylene diacetate (31) on HeLa (3.1 ± 1.3 µM) and the moderate effect on MCF‐7 and A549 (IC50 17.4 ± 3.0 and 35.6 ± 6.9 µM), respectively; the moderate cytotoxic activity of 3‐hydroxy‐4‐(3‐(4‐methoxyphenyl)propanoyl)‐2‐(3‐methylbut‐2‐en‐1‐yl)phenyl acetate (32) on MCF‐7 and HeLa (IC50 23.7 ± 3.1 and 21.3 ± 2.4 µM), respectively; and a low activity on A549 (IC50 64.9 ± 0.8 µM); the low effect of (E)‐1‐(5‐hydroxy‐2,2‐dimethyl‐2H‐chromen‐8‐yl)‐3‐(4‐methoxyphenyl)prop‐2‐en‐1‐one (37) on MCF‐7 and HeLa (IC50 96.7 ± 5.2 and 71.5 ± 3.0 µM), respectively; the low activity of crotaramin (38) on A549 (IC50 89.5 ± 1.7 µM) [13]. The isolated compounds quercetin (9) from the roots of E. glomeratum showed a moderate activity on HeLa, A549 and MCF‐7 (IC50 19.1, 28.2, and 42.6 µM), respectively; genistein (1) showed moderate cytotoxicity on HeLa and A549 (IC50 27.4 and 42.1 µM), respectively; and a low cytotoxicity against MCF‐7 (IC50 88.9 µM); genistin (4) showed low cytotoxicity on HeLa (IC50 68.7 µM) [10]. Although the reported antimicrobial and cytotoxic activities clearly demonstrate the pharmacological potential of E. glomeratum, several limitations should be acknowledged. Most studies relied exclusively on in vitro assays using a limited number of microbial strains and cancer cell lines. Very few investigations explored the molecular mechanisms responsible for the observed activities, and virtually no pharmacokinetic, toxicological, or in vivo studies have been conducted. Furthermore, differences in extraction procedures and experimental protocols make limitation of direct comparison among studied samples. Consequently, standardized biological evaluation and mechanistic investigations are required before these compounds can be considered as potential therapeutic leads.

Structure–Activity Relationship (SAR)

The cytotoxic evaluation of the isolated dihydrochalcones from E. glomeratum and their semisynthetic derivatives provides valuable insights into the structural features governing their biological activities. Although only a limited number of compounds have been evaluated, several preliminary SARs can be established.

Effect of Prenylation

Comparison of erioschalcone A (6), erioschalcone B (7), and 2′,6′‐dihydroxy‐4′‐methoxydihydrochalcone (8) suggests that prenylation plays a crucial role in cytotoxic activity. The presence of the prenyl substituent at C‐3′ appears to enhance biological activity, probably by increasing lipophilicity and facilitating the interaction of the molecules with cellular membranes and intracellular targets. Prenylated flavonoids are widely recognized to possess higher biological activity than their non‐prenylated analogues because the hydrophobic prenyl chain improves membrane permeability and target affinity [32] (Figure 2).

Effect of Methoxylation

The comparison between erioschalcone A (6) and erioschalcone B (7) indicates that the methoxy group located on ring B contributes positively to cytotoxic activity. Methoxylation generally increases molecular hydrophobicity, improves passive diffusion across biological membranes, and may strengthen hydrophobic interactions within enzyme binding pockets [33]. The superior activity of erioschalcone A against MCF‐7 cells may therefore result from the combined effects of prenylation and methoxyl substitution (Figure 2).

Effect of Acetylation

Acetylation markedly modifies both polarity and lipophilicity. The remarkable activity of derivative 31 against HeLa cells suggests that acetyl protection of phenolic hydroxyl groups improves cellular uptake and may increase intracellular bioavailability. Nevertheless, this structural modification did not uniformly improve activity against all cancer cell lines, indicating that acetylation may produce selective rather than universal enhancement of cytotoxicity (Figure 2).

Effect of Cyclization

Cyclization of the prenyl side chain generally resulted in decreased cytotoxic activity compared with the parent compounds (6, 38 and 37; 7, 36, and 35). This observation suggests that conformational restriction reduces the flexibility of the molecule and may alter its interaction with molecular targets. Moreover, cyclization partially masks the structural contribution of the prenyl group, reducing the beneficial effect associated with its hydrophobic character [34] (Figure 2).

Steric and Electronic Effects

Besides individual functional groups, steric and electronic factors probably contribute significantly to biological activity. The combined presence of prenyl, methoxy, and hydroxyl substituents influences electron distribution, molecular conformation, and steric accessibility of pharmacophoric regions. These parameters collectively determine the affinity of the compounds for their biological targets and may explain the differences observed among structurally related derivatives (35–38) (Figure 2).

Overall, the available data indicate that the highest cytotoxic activities are associated with molecules possessing an optimal balance between hydrophobicity and hydrogen‐bonding capacity. Prenylation and methoxylation appear to favor activity, whereas excessive cyclization reduces biological potency. Acetylation may enhance activity in a cell‐type‐dependent manner, suggesting that further medicinal chemistry optimization of erioschalcones represents a promising strategy for the development of novel anticancer agents. Nevertheless, these conclusions remain preliminary because they are based on a relatively small number of compounds. Additional semisynthetic analogues, computational modelling, molecular docking, and mechanistic studies will be necessary to establish robust quantitative structure–activity relationships (QSARs) and identify the molecular targets responsible for the observed cytotoxic effects.

The partial SAR on erioschalcones A (6) and B (7) and their mono and diacetylated derivatives 3‐hydroxy‐4‐(3‐(4‐methoxyphenyl) propanoyl)‐2‐(3‐methylbut‐2‐en‐1‐yl) phenyl acetate (32), and 3‐hydroxy‐2‐(3‐methylbut‐2‐en‐1‐yl)‐4‐(3‐phenylpropanoyl) phenyl acetate (34); 4‐(3‐(4‐methoxyphenyl) propanoyl)‐2‐(3‐methylbut‐2‐en‐1‐yl)‐1,3‐phenylene diacetate (31), and 2‐(3‐methylbut‐2‐en‐1‐yl)‐4‐(3‐phenylpropanoyl)‐1,3‐phenylene diacetate (33) was suggested. The acetyl group(s) at positions C‐2' and/or C‐4' in association with the methoxyl group at position C‐4 could be responsible for the observed selective activity on human HeLa cancer cell line. The activity of acetylated derivatives 31 (IC50 = 3.1 µM) and 32 (IC50 = 21.3 µM) against the cervical cancer HeLa was most potent than that of their precursor 6 (IC50 = 23.7 µM), as the IC50 value of the latter is higher compare to that of its derivatives. However, the cytotoxic effect of the same derivatives (31 and 32) against MCF‐7, and A549, as well as that of the other acetylated compounds against all the tested cell lines, has decreased compared to that of the respective precursors (Figure 3). These results corroborated with those of Ardaillou et al. [35] concerning the cytotoxicity of prenylated dihydrochalcones [(E)‐1‐(3‐(3,7‐dimethylocta‐2,6‐dien‐1‐yl)‐2,4,6‐trihydroxyphenyl)‐3‐(3‐hydroxy‐4‐methoxyphenyl) propan‐1‐one (IC50 = 28.0 µM)] against human lung (A549) cancer cell line, as well as those of a prenylated dihydrochalcone [montachalcone A (IC50 = 7.0 µM)] [36]. From the results of the above observation, a basic model of the action of dihydrochalcones can be established. This model can then be used in the context of facilitating the development of new acetylated derivatives of the sub‐class of dihydrochalcones, which would serve as more potent cytotoxic agents against human cervical cancer.

The activities of cyclization derivatives 1‐(5‐hydroxy‐2,2‐dimethyl‐2H‐chromen‐6‐yl)‐3‐(4‐methoxyphenyl) propan‐1‐one (37), and (E)‐1‐(5‐hydroxy‐2,2‐dimethyl‐2H‐chromen‐8‐yl)‐3‐(4‐methoxy‐phenyl) prop‐2‐en‐1‐one (38) obtained from erioschalcone A (6) was higher as compared to that of 1‐(5‐hydroxy‐2,2‐dimethyl‐2H‐chromen‐6‐yl)‐3‐phenylpropan‐1‐one (36), and 1‐(5‐hydroxy‐2,2‐dimethyl‐2H‐chromen‐8‐yl)‐3‐phenylpropan‐1‐one (35) from erioschalcone B (7). The cyclization derivative at the position 4' (linear cyclization) (E)‐1‐(5‐hydroxy‐2,2‐dimethyl‐2H‐chromen‐8‐yl)‐3‐(4‐methoxy‐phenyl) prop‐2‐en‐1‐one (38) obtained from erioschalcone A (6) possessed low activity on HeLa cell line with IC50 = 89.5 µM; and was lower than that of erioschalcone A (6) with IC50 = 23.7.

The above results led us to deduce that the cyclization reaction rather decreases the activity and more specifically when they are done upwards or in position 4' (linear cyclization) with the absence of the methoxy group in position 4 as observed in 37 and 38 compareto their precursors erioschalcones A (6) and B (7) that displayed activities on all cell lines. These results allow us to suggest that the cyclization of erioschalcones (angular and linear) makes it possible to reduce the cytotoxic activity when they have a methoxy group in position 4; and cancels this activity when they do not have a methoxy group in position 4.

After suggested that the prenyl group locate at position 3' is partially responsible for the cytotoxic activity of dihydrochalcones, it was evident to observe that all derivatives obtained from cyclization suddenly lose their activities (Figure 4). This result is in accordance with some previous findings of reported prenylated dihydrochalcones [35, 36].

Biosynthetic Relationship Between Classes of Compounds Isolated From E. glomeratum

It is clear that the majority of compounds isolated from E. glomeratum belong to important classes which are biosynthetically related. This observation allows us to better understand the presence of these classes (alkyl caffeate, chalcone, flavanone, isoflavone, dihydrochalcone, and flavonol [13, 15, 16]) of compounds in this plant and there is a little chance that some are artifacts. Indeed, the phenylalanine ammonia‐lyase (PAL) is the enzyme that catalyzes the first committed step of general phenylpropanoid pathways in plants, catalyzing the conversion of L‐phenylalanine (39) to trans‐cinnamic acid (40) via a non‐oxidative deamination reaction [37]. Trans‐cinnamic acid (40) served as the start point of secondary metabolism is then hydroxylated by the cinnamate 4‐hydroxylase (C4H) to yield p‐coumaric acid (41) [38] that is further hydroxylated by 4‐coumarate 3‐hydroxylase (4CMH or C3H) to yield caffeic acid (42) and converted by p‐coumarate: CoA ligase (4CL) to its coenzyme A ester (p‐coumaroyl‐CoA) (43). P‐dihydrocoumaroyl‐CoA (44) is formed from 43 by a NADPH (p‐coumaroyl‐CoA oxidoreductase). The chemical structure similarity of 43 and 44 led to the assumption that CHS (chalcone synthase) could use either molecule as a substrate, with condensation of three molecules of malonyl‐CoA to form naringenin chalcone (45) or phloretin (46), respectively [39]. Chalcone isomerase (CHI) can acts with 45 to form naringenin (47) that can be further metabolized to genistein (1) by action of the enzyme isoflavone synthase (IFS) [40]. The action of flavanone 3‐hydroxylase (F3H) on dihydrochalcone or dihydromyricetin (46) will be able to cyclize it and transform it into dihydromyricetin (48) which can subsequently be transformed into flavonol or kaempferol (49) by the action of flavonol synthase (FLS) [41]. The hydroxylation of flavanol (49) by the cinnamate 3'‐hydroxylase (C3'H) can take place to yield flavonol (50) or quercetin (Scheme 2).

Chemophenetic Significance of E. glomeratum

The phytochemical investigation carried out on E. glomeratum species in this study allowed us to note the isolation and characterization of twenty‐two previously reported compounds among which one alkyl caffeate (20), one ceramide (21), nine flavonoids (1‐9), three steroids (14‐16), four fatty alcohols (10‐13), two terpenoids (17‐18), and one cerebroside (22) [13, 15, 16]. From E. glomeratum, we were able to raise the first characterization of secondary metabolites 20 (eriocaffeate) and 21 (erioceramide) [16]; and erioschalcone A (6) and erioschalcone B (7) [15]. We noted the first report from E. glomeratum of secondary metabolites 5 (lupinisoflavone A), 22 (1‐O‐(β‐D‐glucopyranosyloxy)‐(2S,3S,4R,6E)‐2‐[(2'R)‐2'‐hydroxyheptadecanoylamino]‐8‐nona‐decene‐3,4‐diol) [16]; 3 (genistein‐5‐O‐β‐D‐glucopyranoside), 8 (2',6'‐dihydroxy‐4'‐methoxydihydrochalcone), 13 (erythritol), 10 (1‐tricosanol), 11 (nonacosanol), and 17 (lupeol) [13]. Two flavonoids 5 (lupinisoflavone A) and 8 (2',6'‐dihydroxy‐4'‐methoxydihydrochalcone); three alcohols 13 (erythritol), 10 (1‐tricosanol) and 11 (nonacosanol); and the cerebroside 22 (1‐O‐(β‐D‐glucopyranosyloxy)‐(2S,3S,4R,6E)‐2‐[(2'R)‐2'‐hydroxyheptadecanoylamino]‐8‐nona‐decene‐3,4‐diol) were reported for the first time from the genus Eriosema [13, 16]. Genistein (1) was previously isolated from the roots and twigs of E. tuberosum [43, 44], from the roots of E. chinense [10], from the aerial and underground part of E. Laurentii [42, 43, 44]. Genistein (1), genistin (4) and quercetin (9) from the roots of E. montanum [36]; and from the roots of E. glomeratum [16]; stigmasterol (15) and β‐Sitosterol 3‐O‐β‐D‐glucopyranoside (16) from the leaves of E. montanum [36]. All belonging to the Fabaceae family, this result provides new information on the chemical characteristics of Fabaceae. The fact that some of flavonoids as well as genistein (1), genistin (4) and quercetin (9) are isolated in the same part of the roots of Eriosema genus [16, 36] allow us to suggest that many classes of flavonoids (isoflavonoids and flavonol) excepted dihydrochalcones are more concentrated in the roots. In general, previous studies on plant species of Eriosema genus revealed the presence of dihydrochalcones, flavonoids, isoflavonoids, chromones, terpenoids, triterpenoids, fatty acids, and alcohols [10, 13, 15, 16, 36, 45, 46]. From our results obtained on the isolation of erioschalcones A (6) and B (7), genistin, genistein 5‐O‐β‐D‐glucopyranoside (3), genistein (4) and lupinisoflavone A (5) from E. glomeratum compared to those of other Eriosema species, isoflavonoids, and dihydrochalcones had chemotaxonomic importance at the genus level. Erioschalcones A (6) and B (7) were isolated and characterized for the first time in the whole plant of E. glomeratum by Awouafack et al. [15] and the work of Tabakam et al. 2022 [13] in which he worked on the roots and the aerial part of E. glomeratum separately and isolated three previously reported dihydrochalcones among which erioschalcones A (6) and B (7) allowed us to suggest that the dihydrochalcones would be more present in the aerial part of this plant species and especially erioschalcones A (6) and B (7) might serve as a chemotaxonomic marker for the aerial part of E. glomeratum.

Conclusion and Future Perspective

Eriosema glomeratum has emerged as a chemically rich medicinal species containing structurally diverse secondary metabolites, including isoflavonoids, dihydrochalcones, flavonols, alkyl caffeates, terpenoids, sphingolipids, fatty alcohols, and steroids. Among these constituents, prenylated dihydrochalcones, particularly erioschalcones A and B, constitute unique chemophenetic markers and valuable scaffolds for semisynthetic modification. Their remarkable antimicrobial and cytotoxic activities demonstrate the pharmaceutical potential of this species and highlight its importance as a promising source of lead compounds for drug discovery. Despite these encouraging findings, the current body of knowledge remains limited. Most biological investigations have been restricted to in vitro antimicrobial and cytotoxic assays, whereas other pharmacological properties suggested by the traditional uses of the plant, such as antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, antidiabetic, antiparasitic, antiviral, neuroprotective, and enzyme inhibitory activities, remain largely unexplored. Likewise, very little information is available regarding the molecular mechanisms of action, pharmacokinetic behavior, bioavailability, metabolism, toxicity, and safety of the isolated metabolites.

Future investigations should therefore integrate modern multidisciplinary approaches. Comprehensive metabolomic analyses using liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)‐ and NMR‐based metabolite profiling could improve the identification of minor constituents and facilitate metabolite annotation. Biosynthetic studies combining genomics, transcriptomics, and biosynthetic gene mining would contribute to elucidating the enzymatic pathways responsible for the production of prenylated flavonoids and other characteristic metabolites of the genus. In parallel, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, network pharmacology, and systems biology approaches could help predict molecular targets and clarify the mechanisms underlying the observed biological activities.

From a medicinal chemistry perspective, the encouraging results obtained with acetylated and cyclized derivatives suggest that additional semisynthetic optimization should be pursued to improve potency, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic properties. Rational SAR studies, supported by computational modelling, may facilitate the design of new analogues with enhanced biological efficacy.

Finally, in vivo pharmacological evaluation, toxicological assessment, ADMET studies, and ultimately clinical investigations will be necessary before these promising metabolites can be considered as potential therapeutic agents. Additional phytochemical investigations of E. glomeratum populations collected from different ecological regions may also reveal new chemotypes and contribute to a better understanding of environmental influences on secondary metabolite production. Altogether, the available evidence indicates that E. glomeratum represents an underexplored medicinal resource deserving further phytochemical, pharmacological, and translational research.
