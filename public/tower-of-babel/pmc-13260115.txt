Costs and Budget Impact of a Health System Strengthening Intervention for Hypertension and Cardiovascular Disease Care in Ghana
Authors: Kavita Singh, Eugene Kallson, Elom Hillary Otchi, Rupal Shah-Rohlfs, Juliette Selom Edzeame, Fionn Harnischfeger, Alfred Edwin Yawson, Manuela De Allegri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a leading contributor to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare spending in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Despite this burden, economic evidence on system-level interventions to strengthen hypertension and CVD care in sub-Saharan Africa remains limited. This study estimates the costs and budget impact of the Ghana Heart Initiative (GHI), a multi-component health systems strengthening intervention.Methods We conducted a retrospective cost analysis from the health system perspective using an activity-based costing approach. We estimated the financial and economic costs incurred during the design (2018–2019) and initial implementation phase (2020–2022) of the GHI across 42 public health facilities in the Greater Accra Region. Costs were disaggregated by activity cluster and input category. We then projected the budget impact of hypothetical national scale-up under three implementation scenarios that varied in scope and cost-sharing assumptions. All costs were inflation-adjusted and reported in 2024 US dollars (US $).Results The total economic cost of designing and implementing the GHI over 4 years was US$1.96 million, of which 91.5% was attributable to implementation activities. Human resources were the primary cost driver. Average annual economic costs were US$11,960 per health facility, US$997 per targeted health provider trained, and US$39.9 per outpatient attendee. Projected annual costs for nationwide scale-up ranged from US$12.1 million to US$30.1 million, depending on implementation scenario, with a streamlined service-delivery–only model representing a lower-bound estimate.Conclusions This study provides granular, activity-level cost estimates for a complex health system strengthening intervention targeting hypertension and CVD care. The findings offer policy-relevant inputs for budget planning and highlight how implementation choices influence the fiscal implications of scaling up system-level CVD interventions in LMICs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40258-026-01043-5.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are major drivers of morbidity, service utilization, and health spending worldwide [1]. In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), the growing burden of CVD is straining already constrained health systems [2, 3]. Ghana is no exception; recent increases in health expenditure reflect rising demand for chronic care, with hypertension, stroke, and heart failure accounting for a substantial share of outpatient and inpatient care [4]. As the health system evolves from episodic care toward long-term CVD care, decision makers require reliable estimates of the economic costs of delivering system-level interventions that improve screening, diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up care.

However, the economic evidence base for health system strengthening to improve CVD care in sub-Saharan Africa remains limited. Most studies focus on community campaigns or pharmacologic treatment, or they report average program totals without tracing costs to the activities that generate them [5–8]. This obscures which components are most resource-intensive and hinders planning for implementation and scale-up. Moreover, cost-effectiveness analyses, while essential for assessing value for money, are often abstract from the full cost structure and budgetary requirements faced by ministries of health and purchasing agencies. For policy and planning, health authorities need activity-level costs, with explicit assumptions about inputs, wages, equipment, and overheads, so they can assess affordability, fiscal sustainability, and the organizational implications of scale. While cost-effectiveness analyses are critical to assess value for money, they often provide limited insight into how resources are distributed across system functions or the short-term budgetary implications faced by ministries of health.

The Ghana Heart Initiative (GHI)—a multi-component health systems strengthening intervention for early detection and management of hypertension and CVDs—was implemented across 42 public health facilities in the Greater Accra Region, serving as an initial implementation platform rather than a phased national rollout [9]. Prior to the GHI, Ghana did not have standardized, nationally endorsed clinical guidelines for cardiovascular disease management. Clinical practice for hypertension and CVD relied on heterogeneous protocols, often adapted from international guidance, with limited consistency across facilities. The GHI sought to address this gap by supporting the development of nationally endorsed CVD management guidelines through a multi-stakeholder consultative process led by the Ministry of Health (MoH). The GHI included development and rollout of standardized treatment protocols for hypertension and CVD management; training of healthcare professionals across levels of care; provision of equipment to enable timely screening and diagnosis; establishment of a 24/7 CVD call center to support clinical decision making and referrals; and data management support for program monitoring and evaluation. These elements align with national noncommunicable disease (NCD) strategies and the World Health Organization (WHO) HEARTS technical package [10, 11], yet their economic implications in routine health system operations has not been documented in Ghana.

This study addresses these gaps by estimating the economic costs of developing and implementing the GHI from the healthcare system perspective. Using an activity-based costing (ABC) approach, we disaggregated costs by project phase (design and implementation), program activity, and cost category to identify cost drivers and to evaluate where resources are absorbed within the system. Because policy choices often hinge on fiscal feasibility as much as cost effectiveness, we also examined the budget impact of national scale-up under realistic implementation scenarios. Together, these estimates provide policy-relevant information to the MoH, regional health directorates, and developmental partners considering adoption, adaptation, or expansion of the GHI. By focusing on detailed cost estimation and budget impact rather than comparative effectiveness, this study aims to inform affordability, fiscal planning, and implementation choices for system-level CVD interventions in resource-constrained settings.

Methods

Setting

With an estimated GDP per capita of US$2232 in 2024, Ghana is a lower-middle-income country in sub-Saharan Africa. In 2021, CVD mortality was estimated at 750 deaths per 100,000 males and 563 per 100,000 females. The prevalence of hypertension has risen dramatically over the past two decades, from < 5% in 2000 to approximately 34% in 2021 [12, 13].

Although the burden of hypertension and CVD continues to rise, access to timely and equitable healthcare for acute and chronic CVD conditions remains inadequate. Ghana's healthcare system continues to face challenges including poor infrastructure, severe shortages of human resources and medical supplies, and limited financial capacity [14]. In 2018, total health expenditure per capita was US$76.8, with donor contributions accounting for nearly 9%. Most healthcare services are publicly provided through the Ghana Health Service (GHS) under the MoH. Notably, Ghana was among the first African countries to establish legislation and earmarked financing for universal health coverage through its National Health Insurance Scheme, launched in 2003, which covered approximately 60% of the population by 2023 [15–17]. The Ghana Heart Initiative (GHI) was implemented in 42 health facilities in the Greater Accra Region that were identified jointly with the GHS. These health facilities were not self-selected sites, but rather were purposively selected on feasibility and service-capacity grounds to include hospitals, polyclinics, and health centers involved in outpatient hypertension and CVD care.

Intervention

The first phase of the GHI, implemented from 2018 to 2022, covered 42 public health facilities in five districts of the Greater Accra Region: Accra Metropolitan Area, Tema Municipality, Ga East, Ga West, and Dangme West and East. The GHI consisted of five core components: (i) development and dissemination of national hypertension and CVD management guidelines; (ii) training and mentorship of healthcare workers; (iii) provision of diagnostic equipment; (iv) data management support for facility-level monitoring; and (v) establishment of a 24/7 CVD support and call center. This initial implementation phase of the GHI can be considered a regional pilot designed to inform later national expansion. The GHI targeted healthcare workers involved in outpatient and primary care service delivery for hypertension and cardiovascular disease management, including physicians, nurses, physician assistants, and allied health staff. Training, supervision, and digital support tools were delivered using standardized materials and protocols across participating facilities, reflecting the program’s focus on strengthening system-level capacity rather than tailoring inputs to facility-specific disease burden.

The GHI was a collaborative effort among the GIZ (Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit), GHS, and MoH, incorporating public–private partnerships. The implementing partners had the flexibility to tailor the program to local contexts, introducing innovations such as e-learning platforms, a digital “Akomacare” app for accessing treatment guidelines, and the CVD call center.

Study Design and Economic Perspective

We conducted a retrospective cost analysis from the health system perspective, tracking costs incurred by the GHS, MoH, and GIZ during GHI design and implementation. We estimated both financial and economic costs to reflect the full value of resources used, regardless of appearance in financial records. We did not include patient-related out-of-pocket medical spending or clinical service delivery-related treatment costs. This study does not estimate incremental cost effectiveness or compare the GHI to alternative interventions; rather, it focuses on estimating economic costs associated with the design and implementation of such a health systems strengthening intervention and associated budgetary requirements.

We applied a standard activity-based costing (ABC) approach [18] to link costs to specific activities and inputs. Our analysis covered all costs since the inception of the GHI from 2018 to 2022: program design/start-up phase (September 2018 to December 2019) and implementation phase (January 2020 to June 2022), aligned with initial funding.

Data Sources and Collection Methods

We collected data through semi-structured interviews with key informants, and complemented it with document review. The interviews were either in person or virtual depending on the preference of the key informants, who were purposively selected from the GIZ, the GHS, and the MoH, including program managers, and technical staff. In total, six key informants across GHS, MoH, and implementing partners were interviewed. Interviews focused on identifying all activities undertaken during design and implementation phases, the resources required for each activity, and staff time allocation. Respondents were encouraged to identify additional activities or inputs not captured in financial records (refer to the electronic supplementary material [ESM] for more details on the semi-structured interview guide used for data collection).

More specifically, we used a standardized, semi-structured questionnaire and a three-step process: (1) mapping all design/start-up and implementation phase activities; (2) identifying resources consumed per activity cluster; and (3) estimating unit costs for each resource. Table 1 provides an overview of the nine activity clusters and cost categories constructed based on the information gathered from the key informants. We obtained information regarding resource utilization and unit costs from implementing partner financial statements and key informant interviews. Financial statements were used to identify and validate total program expenditures and broad cost categories (e.g., personnel, training, equipment, and operational costs), rather than as the sole data source for activity-level costing. To achieve the level of granularity required for the ABC approach, financial data were triangulated with detailed expenditure reports, procurement records, budgets, invoices, and training logs. These documents provided information on quantities, unit costs, and timing of expenditures, which enabled costs to be mapped to specific activities and activity clusters. Definition of Activity Clusters and Cost Aggregation

Activity clusters were defined to reflect the major functional components of the GHI and were informed by the program’s implementation framework, annual workplans, and monitoring and evaluation structure. Initial clusters were identified through a review of project documents and subsequently refined and validated through discussions with key informants involved in program design and implementation. The final set of nine activity clusters included design-phase coordination and management; implementation-phase coordination and management; national guideline development; training; equipment support; Akomacare app development; CVD support and call center; data management support; and monitoring and evaluation. These were selected to represent programmatically coherent units that are relevant for planning, implementation, and scale-up decisions.

Individual cost items were assigned to activity clusters based on their primary purpose and contribution to program delivery. Where resources supported multiple activities (e.g., staff time or shared operational costs), costs were allocated proportionally using reported time use or programmatic relevance. Cost categories within each cluster (e.g., human resources, consumables, transportation, and services) were retained to ensure transparency while avoiding unnecessary fragmentation. This approach balanced analytical granularity with interpretability for policymakers and implementers. We first aggregated costs at the micro level, then rolled them up into broader categories for analysis [19].

We estimated both financial and economic costs from the health system perspective, following established guidance for intervention and program costing in health economic analyses [18, 19]. Financial costs were defined as actual monetary expenditures incurred by implementing agencies, including payments for personnel, training, equipment, consumables, travel, and contracted services. These costs were identified through a review of project financial statements, expenditure reports, and budgets provided by implementing partners, and were cross-validated through key informant interviews with program and finance staff.

Economic costs were defined to include financial costs as well as the opportunity costs of resources used in program design and implementation for which no direct financial payment was made. These included the time of government staff and technical advisors not remunerated directly by the project, the use of existing infrastructure (e.g., office space and training venues), and donated goods or in-kind services. Opportunity costs were identified through semi-structured interviews with key informants, who reported time allocation and resource use attributable to the GHI, and were valued using prevailing market wage rates or replacement cost estimates.

Both financial and economic costs were estimated using an ABC approach, whereby resources were first assigned to specific activities and then aggregated into broader activity clusters. Where costs could not be directly attributed to a single activity, they were allocated proportionally based on staff time use or programmatic relevance. All costs were adjusted to a common price year prior to analysis.

In parallel, we conducted a health facility assessment survey to collect data on key service delivery indicators, including the number of outpatient attendees screened for hypertension and proportions of patients with blood pressure control (defined as BP < 140/90 mmHg) at a single timepoint (not sustained change in BP control), as extracted from clinic registers or health facility records [20]. The survey was conducted as part of the GHI mixed-methods evaluation study.

Data Analysis

We performed a descriptive cost analysis to estimate total financial and economic costs, disaggregated by year, activity cluster, and cost category (human resources, room rental, transport, consumables). We maintained distinctions between financial versus economic costs and between design versus implementation phases. We then aggregated costs by activity and by implementing partners.

We calculated cost per health facility, cost per targeted health worker, and cost per outpatient attendee. Costs per provider and per patient are presented as average values across participating facilities. Variation in facility size, service volume, and catchment population was observed across participating sites. However, as the costing analysis focused on program-level activities that were implemented uniformly across facilities, costs are presented as averages (adjusting for number of health workers trained per site and equipment provided) but are not intended to capture site-specific expenditure differences. We also estimated the cost per health gain as a simple descriptive cost-consequences analysis, defined as the average cost per blood pressure control, that is, the proportion of patients with BP < 140/90 mmHg at a single visit based on aggregate facility level records.

Next, to inform scalability of the GHI, we conducted a budget-impact analysis for nationwide expansion, using the ABC approach [18] to track all relevant inputs. National scale-up scenarios represent hypothetical projections assuming replication of core activities across all public health facilities in Ghana. Design-phase activities were treated as either sunk or partially scalable depending on their function, while recurrent implementation activities were assumed to scale proportionally with facility coverage. In the scale-up analysis, cost components were classified as fixed, semi-fixed, or variable based on their expected practical application with increasing coverage. Activities such as national guideline development, training material development, and initial digital platform development were treated as largely fixed costs incurred during the initial implementation phase. For national expansion scenarios, these components were assumed to require only ongoing maintenance cost, rather than full re-development. In contrast, activities related to service delivery such as training delivery, supervision, and facility-level support were assumed to scale proportionally with the number of facilities covered. Specifically, we constructed three budget scenarios to provide a range of implementation costs:Comprehensive scenario: all major implementation activity clusters were included, except full redevelopment of the national guidelines and the Akomacare app, which were already developed during the first phase. However, future maintenance and periodic update costs for these components were included.Service-delivery scenario: only recurrent service-delivery activities (e.g., training, equipment provision, CVD support center) were included, assuming that some coordination and monitoring functions would be shared across other national health programs.Expansion scenario: all components of the comprehensive scenario were included, together with additional design and stakeholder-engagement activities expected to be required for national expansion.

Lastly, we conducted a deterministic one-way sensitivity analysis [21] that varied only foreign staff costs. In the base case, we included the full costs of foreign staff involved in both design and implementation activities, including salary, benefits, per diem, and overheads. In the sensitivity scenario, these foreign staff costs were excluded to approximate a more locally led delivery model for national scale-up. We held all other quantities, unit costs, and outcomes constant. We report the resulting changes in total program cost by phase, as well as average annual cost per facility and per outpatient attendee. We converted original study budget amounts in Euros using the 2022 average exchange rate of 1 EUR = 1.0515 USD (equivalently, 1 USD = 0.951 EUR). Lastly, all cost estimates were adjusted to 2024 US dollar rates to enhance comparability and policy relevance. Cost data were inflated using the Consumer Price Index (CPI). The inflation adjustment factor applied was 1.0716, derived from the ratio of the 2024 CPI (313.7) to the 2022 CPI (292.7) (for details refer to the ESM) [22]. Inflation-adjusted values were applied consistently across all activity clusters, cost categories, and scale-up scenarios. Percentage cost shares and relative differences between scenarios were unaffected by inflation adjustment.

All participants gave written informed consent before data collection.

Over the 4-year project period (September 2018 to June 2022), cost distribution varied significantly by year. The highest expenditure occurred during the second and third years of the project, corresponding to major activities such as (1) preparatory workshops and the development of clinical guidelines for hypertension and CVD in 2019, and (2) training of healthcare workers and procurement as well as distribution of equipment in 2020. It is important to note that these estimates may underestimate true economic costs, as travel and opportunity costs for non-GIZ staff involved in design and implementation phases were not comprehensively captured.

Over the 4-year study period, the GHI corresponded to an average annual economic cost of US$47,846 per participating health facility, with a financial cost of US$46,589 for the combined design and implementation phases and US$42,635 for the implementation phase alone. On an annual basis, this translates to approximately US$11,960 (economic) and US$11,647 or US$10,659 (financial costs for combined design and implementation phase and implementation phase alone), respectively. These are average program costs derived by allocating total costs across the 42 participating facilities and should not be interpreted as site-specific expenditures. Many GHI inputs, including guideline development, training materials, digital tools, and central coordination, were implemented at program level and benefited multiple facilities simultaneously. Accordingly, the estimates are intended to support comparability and budget planning rather than to represent uniform costs across facilities.

The average annual economic cost per targeted healthcare provider was US$997, with corresponding financial costs ranging from US$971 to US$888. The average annual cost per outpatient attendee ranged from US$39.9 to US$35.5, assuming an average monthly outpatient volume of 3600 attendees per facility based on a health facility assessment survey. The average cost per blood pressure control (< 140/90 mmHg) was US$94.8 (economic), with corresponding financial costs ranging from US$92.4 to US$84.7, based on a BP control rate of 41.8% observed in intervention facilities (Table 3). This measure is presented as a descriptive cost-consequences analysis and represents blood pressure control rates observed across participating health facilities at one timepoint. Table 4 presents the projected national-level expansion costs in 2024 US dollars. Assuming full coverage of all 1883 public health facilities in Ghana, the estimated annual budget impact was US$22.3 million under the comprehensive scenario, US$12.1 million under the service-delivery scenario, and US$30.1 million under the expansion scenario. Compared with the comprehensive scenario, the service-delivery scenario reduced total annual costs by 45.7%, while the expansion scenario was substantially more expensive with almost 148% higher costs than the service-delivery scenario, because it re-introduced additional coordination, training, and monitoring-related activities.

Under the comprehensive scenario, the projected average annual cost per health facility was US$12,041, based on a total annual program cost of US$493,687. Under a more conservative 60% coverage assumption, the projected national costs were US$13.6 million, US$7.3 million, and US$18.9 million, under scenarios 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Compared with scenario 1, scenario 2 reduced total annual costs by 45.7%, whereas scenario 3 increased total annual costs by 35.0%, reflecting the inclusion of additional coordination, training, and scale-up related activities. Across coverage assumptions, projected scale-up costs represented approximately 1.1–2.6% of Ghana’s total health budget. The 42 participating facilities represented only a subset of public facilities nationally, and the scale-up analysis was based on replication of average facility-level program inputs rather than an assumption that these facilities served an identical proportion of the national patient population.

Discussion

This study makes an important contribution to the literature, being the first to describe in detail start-up and implementation costs of GHI—a health system strengthening intervention for hypertension and CVD care in Ghana. Our study data provides comprehensive costing and budget impact analysis of the GHI in an LMIC setting, demonstrating that the overall economic cost of designing and implementing the program in 42 public healthcare facilities over four years was US$1.96 million in 2024 US dollars. Implementation accounted for over 90% of the total cost, with human resources constituting the largest share across both design and implementation phases. A key strength of this study is its robust methodology grounded in activity-based costing adopting a health system perspective, enabling precise tracking of financial and economic costs across multiple implementing partners (GIZ, GHS, and MoH) and disaggregated by phase, activity cluster, and input category.

Comparisons with previous studies should be interpreted cautiously because many published estimates focus on implementation or clinical service-delivery costs only, whereas the GHI also included substantial health system strengthening activities such as training, coordination, guideline dissemination, digital tools, and equipment support. Comparisons are therefore more appropriate when limited to the implementation-oriented components of the GHI rather than the full design-plus-implementation package. This difference in scope helps explain why GHI costs are not directly comparable with estimates from other African settings. For instance, a study from Kenya evaluating a multi-component, community-based hypertension intervention among adults over 35 years of age in a large slum in Nairobi reported a total intervention cost of US$3205 per patient achieving blood pressure control, with substantial expenditures on training, coordination, and equipment support [23]. Importantly, the GHI distinguishes itself through the integration of novel digital tools, including the Akomacare app and a 24/7 CVD call center, reflecting a technology-driven approach that sets it apart from many contemporary health systems based quality improvement initiatives.

Published cost estimates from other sub-Saharan African settings are not directly comparable with those of the GHI because they often measure different units of analysis and include a narrower set of inputs. In our study, the reported costs reflect program-level health system strengthening investments, including coordination, training, guideline dissemination, digital tools, equipment support, and monitoring structures across 42 facilities. By contrast, prior studies from Nigeria and Ethiopia primarily report per-patient or per-person service-delivery costs, with medications and diagnostics as major cost drivers [10, 24]. These studies therefore capture the cost of delivering or supporting clinical care more narrowly, whereas the GHI estimates reflect the resources required to establish and operationalize a broader system-level platform for hypertension and CVD care. Comparisons should therefore be interpreted in terms of differences in costing scope, input inclusion, and delivery model, rather than as direct differences in efficiency or costliness.

Our one-way sensitivity analysis showed that excluding foreign staff costs across both design and implementation activities would reduce total program costs by 15.2%, with the largest reductions occurring in design and coordination. This finding highlights the importance of staffing assumptions in program costing, but it should not be interpreted to mean that foreign expertise was unnecessary in the first phase of the GHI. Rather, it suggests that affordability during scale-up may improve as technical and managerial functions are progressively transferred to local institutions. Earlier studies rarely disaggregate personnel costs by staff origin or vary staffing assumptions in sensitivity analyses [10], underscoring the value of reporting these assumptions explicitly. A phased transition strategy based on local knowledge hubs, mentoring, and training-of-trainers models may help contain future costs while maintaining implementation quality.

Of additional interest is the fact that the bottom-up budget impact analysis in this study provides estimates at different levels of aggregation and estimated a range of total costs of national-level scale-up for multiple scenarios, with the simplified service delivery model (scenario 2: focusing on recurrent costs) offering over 50% cost savings—critical insights for policymakers balancing coverage goals against budget constraints. While the average annual program cost per outpatient attendee was approximately US$35–40, this figure should be interpreted with caution. Ghana’s GDP per capita exceeds US$2200, and total health expenditure per capita is approximately US$75–80, of which a substantial share is publicly financed. The reported per-patient cost reflects the allocation of system-level investments such as training, supervision, coordination, digital tools, and guideline dissemination across the volume of outpatient attendees served by participating facilities. When considered at the national level, projected scale-up costs represented approximately 1–3% of Ghana’s total health budget, suggesting that affordability is better assessed relative to public health expenditure. Nonetheless, sustained financing would require prioritization within constrained health budgets and demonstrated health gains to justify long-term investment. Therefore, further expansion of services for hypertension and CVD requires innovative plans for revenue raising and concerted efforts of GHS and MoH for sustainable funding and utilizing task sharing opportunities in the health sector.

Methodological Considerations

The study has several limitations. First, the 42 facilities included in this analysis were selected based on programmatic considerations and regional feasibility rather than representativeness. While they span primary, secondary, and tertiary levels of care, caution is warranted in extrapolating cost structures to regions with substantially different service capacity, levels of care or disease burden. Second, the retrospective nature of data collection made it impossible for us to trace resource consumption across activities as accurately as we would have wished to. Nonetheless, we engaged closely with the implementation team to reconstruct to the extent possible the roll-out of the intervention, complementing information from documents and financial data with information emerging from key informant interviews. Third, this analysis excludes both patient out-of-pocket expenditures and the costs of clinical treatment (e.g., medications, consultations, hospitalizations). Consequently, the reported costs reflect program-level system-strengthening investments rather than the full economic cost of hypertension and CVD care. These limitations may affect the real-world cost estimates and underscore the need for broader societal cost assessments in future studies. Fourth, given the paucity of similar studies focused specifically on the costs of “supply-side” interventions, we recognize an inability to appraise our findings more comprehensively in relation to the experience of other settings. Lastly, we need to acknowledge that the computation of the cost per patient is based on the estimated average number of eligible patients attending the outpatient clinic across primary, secondary, and tertiary care facilities in the Greater Accra Region. Hence, any imperfection in this population-based estimate is also reflected in our own cost estimate.

Implications of Study Results for Future Research and Policy

The findings have important implications for health policy and future research. Many components of the GHI including guideline development, Akomacare app creation, and early stakeholder engagement required initial upfront investments, and are partly sunk after the first phase. This means that future expansion need not fully replicate all start-up investments. A pragmatic scale-up strategy could therefore prioritize the most operationally important recurrent components, such as training delivery, supervision, equipment support, and maintenance of the Akomacare app, while limiting full redevelopment of components already established. Such a phased or targeted implementation approach may reduce marginal expansion costs while preserving core service-delivery functions. While population heterogeneity may affect downstream effectiveness and resource needs over time, the cost estimates presented in this study reflect the system-level investments required to establish a functional platform for hypertension and CVD care. Future research could explore how facility-level characteristics and disease burden influence cost effectiveness and implementation efficiency of the GHI at scale.

Conclusions

Our study represents the first comprehensive effort to trace the economic costs of setting up and delivering an integrated health systems strengthening intervention for screening, diagnosis, and management of hypertension and CVD in Ghana across all levels of public health facilities, examining all activity clusters and cost categories in detail. Taken together, these findings suggest that while national expansion of system-level CVD strengthening is financially viable, decisions to scale up should be contingent on demonstrable health gains, institutional capacity, and alignment with broader health sector priorities. The study's insights into cost drivers and scalable components can guide the efficient allocation of resources for nationwide expansion and replication of such a health system intervention in other LMIC settings.
