Exploring the Effect of Gender Norms on Provider–Client Interactions Relevant to HIV Care Engagement: A Qualitative Study in Uganda
Authors: Katelyn M. Sileo, Trace S. Kershaw, Rhoda K. Wanyenze, John F. Dovidio, Sten H. Vermund, Asha Anecho, Christopher S. Mukama, Barbara S. Taylor, Shari L. Dworkin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gender norms shape health behaviors and access to HIV care in sub-Saharan Africa, but few studies have explored their influence on provider–client interactions and care provision. This qualitative study was conducted in central Uganda in 2020, involving 7 focus group discussions and 16 key informant interviews with 57 participants, including clients with HIV (n = 12), health and lay workers (n = 17), community members (n = 12), and key informants with health system expertise (n = 16). Using thematic analysis guided by an adapted patient-centeredness model, findings were organized around biopsychosocial perspectives, decision-making processes, and features of care. Gender norms were found central to clients’ treatment compliance, and providers were aware of clients’ gender-related barriers. Shared decision-making between clients and providers was limited for both men and women. Women providers reported challenges to their authority by men, who were often unwilling to take direction. As clients, the men were viewed by providers as direct but rude and less open communicators, while women were perceived as agreeable “good patients,” but women who had not disclosed their status to others were viewed as dishonest. Men’s expectations for respect underpinned tensions with women providers, who showed low empathy toward men’s challenges. Despite recognizing the importance of gender-sensitive, patient-centered communication, structural barriers such as time constraints and the scarcity of male providers hindered its delivery. The findings suggest gender-transformative programming with communities (broadly with community leaders, people living with HIV, partners, etc.) could help men and women living with HIV overcome barriers to care engagement. For providers, gender-sensitivity training could enhance their skills and empathy to be patient-centered toward both men and women.

Introduction

The global response to end the HIV epidemic is centered on achieving UNAIDS’ 95–95-95 targets to ensure that 95% of people living with HIV are aware of their status through HIV testing, 95% of those aware of their status receive life-saving antiretroviral treatment (ART), and 95% of those on treatment are virally suppressed (UNAIDS, 2025). ART is central to extending the number of quality life years of those living with HIV and to preventing the spread of HIV (Wouters et al., 2009). In sub-Saharan Africa, where 70% of all new HIV infections occur (World Health Organization, 2025), significant progress has been made; five countries in the region have achieved their 95 targets as of 2022 (UNAIDS, 2023). However, most nations have failed to reduce disparities in HIV outcomes that exist by demographic groups, including stark disparities by sex (UNAIDS, 2022). Women are at an elevated risk of HIV infection compared to men in the region, with adolescent and young women eight times more likely to have HIV than their male counterparts (Kharsany & Karim, 2016). However, men living with HIV have lower levels of engagement across the HIV care continuum compared to women’s high engagement, which has resulted in men’s elevated risk of AIDS-related mortality when on ART compared to women (Colvin, 2019; UNAIDS, 2017).

Gender norms that reinforce gender inequity affect men and women’s HIV outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa. Gender norms are social norms defining acceptable roles, relationships, and actions for men and women in a group or society (Cislaghi & Heise, 2020). Women’s subordinate status within relationships and in society can manifest in different inequities, like dependence on relationships with men for economic survival and fewer opportunities for financial independence. Such inequities increase women’s risk of HIV infection through lower power in relationships to control sex and safer sex, experiences of gender-based violence, and barriers to accessing HIV services (Amin, 2015; Jewkes et al., 2010; Krishnan et al., 2008). Masculine norms (gender norms specific to men) also drive men’s HIV risk behavior (e.g., receiving higher status for having multiple partners, viewing condoms as inconsistent with norms that focus on men’s pleasure and performance) (Fleming et al., 2016). Masculine norms also affect men’s lower engagement in HIV services, as expectations for men to be mentally and physically strong and independent can prevent men from seeking HIV testing and care until an advanced disease stage (Sileo et al., 2018a, 2018b, 2019). Norms that men should be respected and be the head of the household have also been identified as drivers of men’s avoidance of HIV care for fear that HIV stigma (prejudice and discrimination due to one’s HIV status) will result in the loss of their social status (Sileo et al., 2018a, 2018b, 2019).

The aforementioned studies examining gender norms and HIV care engagement have explored individual- or interpersonal-level (in intimate relationships) manifestations of gender norms on HIV outcomes, i.e., how gender influences men’s and women’s individual health-seeking behavior, and their intimate relationships, to affect HIV outcomes. Far less research has explored how health care interactions between health care providers and clients may be shaped by gender norms in ways that create, maintain, or challenge barriers to HIV care. Like other social norms, gender norms can, without full awareness of their influence, shape people’s perception of and actions toward others. Providers are in a unique position of authority, with expertise and resources that patients need, making the nature and quality of provider–client interactions especially important for medically related outcomes.

Because providers are central to clients' care and outcomes, it is critical to understand the ways in which providers carry out patient-centered care (PCC) that is (or is not) gender-sensitive, and how these relate to HIV-related outcomes. The Institute of Medicine (now called the National Academy of Medicine) defined PCC as “care that is respectful of and responsive to individual patient preferences, needs, and values and ensuring that patient values guide all clinical decisions” (Institute of Medicine, 2001). Studies in sub-Saharan Africa demonstrate the importance of PCC to client satisfaction with care and retention, as well as to HIV treatment outcomes specifically, including ART adherence and viral load suppression (Fritz et al., 2025; Muhula et al., 2022; Wachira et al., 2022).

To theoretically frame this work, we draw on two complementary frameworks of PCC. The first is an empirically informed conceptual model for patient-centeredness by Mead and Bower (2000), who identified five dimensions of PCC: (1) biopsychosocial perspective, (2) patient-as-person, (3) sharing power and responsibility, (4) therapeutic alliance, and (5) doctor-as-person. These dimensions were conceived as being affected by multiple layers of influence—including individual characteristics of patients and providers, features of the care setting, and “shapers” (e.g., broader societal and cultural norms).

Recognizing the need for context-specific applications of PCC in HIV service delivery, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, we also draw from an adaptation by Gourlay et al. (2014), who applied Mead and Bower’s (2000) concepts to provider–client interactions in HIV care in Tanzania. Gourlay et al. reorganized the five original dimensions into empirically grounded themes reflective of the HIV context: decision-making processes, trust in providers, and features of care. These domains are influenced by both individual-level (e.g., provider and client attitudes and knowledge) and structural factors (e.g., time constraints, clinic resources, and social norms). Though understudied, there is some evidence to support gender norms as a critical “shaper” of PCC, as well as the need to improve gender-sensitive PCC in HIV care. Applied to gender norms, the biopsychosocial perspective (Mead & Bower, 2000)—captured in Gourlay et al.’s (2014) features of care—might encompass providers’ awareness of men and women’s gender-related barriers to HIV care, as well as their motivation and skills to elicit and respond to them as part of HIV care. In Uganda, low recognition among healthcare workers of the widespread nature of intimate partner violence has been reported as hindering the effective implementation of gender-based violence screening in clinical settings (Anguzu et al., 2022; Thomas et al., 2023). In Malawi and Mozambique, HIV care providers reported limited awareness of the challenges men experience when accessing HIV treatment (Dovel et al., 2023). Our team’s prior work in Uganda uncovered a multitude of gaps in gender-sensitive HIV care provision, shaped by both low awareness and low perceived relative importance among care providers to be gender-sensitive, as well as structural factors that limit the provision of PCC (i.e., consultation-level factors), like time and space limitations in health facilities (Sileo et al., 2025a, 2025b).

Gender is also central to PCC within decision-making processes, a theme highlighted by Gourlay et al. (2014) and aligned with Mead and Bower’s (2000) principle of sharing power and responsibility. Both frameworks emphasize collaborative decision-making between providers and clients as central to PCC. In sub-Saharan Africa, where paternalistic models of care (i.e., providers’ decisions outweigh clients’ preferences) are common, studies report how providers’ occupational status and can clash with gender expectations for power and respect (Knight et al., 2018). In Kenya, female providers felt men did not always recognize their clinical expertise, and male providers had little patience for women who challenged their authority (Knight et al., 2018). These domains also encompass the use of patient-centered communication to shape the process of patient–provider decision-making (Gourlay et al., 2014; Meade & Bower, 2000), which is also shaped by gender norms. For example, providers’ harsh communication can worsen men’s resistance to being in the position of learner relative to health workers (Siu et al., 2013; Skovdal et al., 2011) and norms of men’s stoicism can prevent men from asking for help (Lynch et al., 2010; Nyamhanga et al., 2013). Gender norms about sexuality can also prohibit open communication about HIV prevention and care in some contexts, such as between clients and providers of opposite gender, or between providers and adolescent girls and young women (Hartmann et al., 2024; Knight et al., 2018).

The theme of features of care, as conceptualized by Gourlay et al. (2014), incorporates therapeutic alliance (Mead & Bower, 2000) as a key element—emphasizing the development of trusting, empathetic relationships. Harsh treatment of clients defaulting on HIV care has been documented in sub-Saharan Africa (Gourlay et al., 2014; Layer et al., 2014; Sikombe et al., 2025). Research also supports varying levels of empathy and trust, being shaped by gender—core to both therapeutic alliance (Mead & Bower, 2000) and trust in providers (Gourlay et al., 2014). In one study in Kenya, providers were more empathetic toward men’s barriers to care than to women’s (work vs. home responsibilities) (Knight et al., 2018). In contrast, studies in Malawi, Mozambique, and Uganda have reported negative attitudes toward men as clients and low empathy toward their barriers to care engagement, particularly among women providers (Dovel et al., 2023; Sileo et al., 2025). Other context-specific biases and stereotypes toward clients that can lead to stigmatizing behavior from providers are also gender-related such as judgment toward having multiple partners, infidelity, substance use, and sex work (Amin, 2015; Hartmann et al., 2024; Knight et al., 2018; Wanyenze et al., 2017).

This work underscores the need to examine how gender norms influence PCC and provider–client relationships in HIV service delivery in sub-Saharan Africa. However, to our knowledge, only one other study to-date has explicitly explored how gender norms shape provider–client relationships in HIV care in sub-Saharan Africa, which was in Kenya (Knight et al., 2018). Our study extends this focus to Uganda and is also the first to do so through the theoretical application of PCC frameworks. Uganda, in East Africa, is an appropriate setting for this study. While it has made significant progress toward its 95-95-95 targets through investment in HIV programming (Uganda Ministry of Health, 2022), like those discussed previously, traditional gender norms are pervasive and intertwined with the HIV epidemic (Bajunirwe et al., 2019; Ninsiima et al., 2018; Okumu et al., 2023) and the above-described disparities in HIV outcomes are also observed by gender in Uganda. HIV prevalence is 6.5% in women, compared to 3.6% in men (UNAIDS, 2024). While the country has narrowed the gap in men’s HIV care engagement in recent years, estimates suggest a need to continue to strengthen support efforts for adherence and retention for men (Uganda AIDS Commission, 2022). Guided by an integrated PCC framework informed by both Mead and Bower (2000) and Gourlay et al. (2014), we explore: How do gender norms manifest in provider–client interactions in HIV care in central Uganda? And how do these dynamics shape the delivery of high-quality ART services in ways central to clients’ HIV care engagement?

Method

Participants and Procedure

This qualitative study was conducted between July and August 2020 in a health center serving rural and peri-urban settings in Luwero District in central Uganda, including a total of 7 focus group discussions and 16 individual interviews involving a total of 57 participants. The study was in partnership with Mildmay Uganda, a non-governmental community-based organization that worked as an implementing partner to the Ugandan Ministry of Health in the delivery of free HIV services throughout several districts in Uganda. We invited local health leaders and other stakeholders in HIV care to serve as study steering committee members (e.g., District leadership, Mildmay Uganda representatives, local health workers); at the start of the study, we conducted several meetings with steering committee members to gather feedback on and inform our study protocol (most were virtual due to COVID-19 concerns at the time).

Focus Group Discussions

Focus group discussions were selected to answer our research question because this method has strengths in eliciting both consensus and areas of divergence among individuals who are homogeneous (e.g., living with HIV or HIV care providers) (Krueger & Casey, 2009; Morgan, 1997). Focus groups have limitations such as heavily relying on facilitation and thus, the need for skilled moderators. However, their strengths include the ability to allow researchers to benefit from the group dynamics that emerge in natural conversations and noting how participants build on each other’s points in real time (Leung & Savithiri, 2009).

We followed best practices in focus group methods by forming homogenous groups (Morgan, 1997) with the 7 focus group discussions involving 41 individuals in the following three populations: ART clients (people living with HIV, receiving ART) (2 groups, 6 participants per group); HIV service providers (three focus groups by cadre: ART clinic providers [5 participants], maternity clinic providers [5 participants], lay health workers, such as peer educators [7 participants]); and individuals from the community (2 groups, 6 participants per group). For the ART client and community member focus groups, of the two conducted for each group, one was with men and one with women. For the three HIV service provider groups, all participants were women, which was an outcome of the natural composition of staff at the health facility being largely women (which is typical in public health facilities in Uganda). There were only three men that worked at the facility where focus groups were conducted (e.g., a clinician, a counselor, a peer educator). Given that focus groups should be as homogenous as possible (Krueger & Casey, 2009), we did not place the men in a single group or mix them with the groups of women, but rather engaged them in individual interviews.

Organizing groups/individuals in this way (e.g., by cadre, HIV status, gender) allowed us to elicit information specific to each group and increase participants’ comfort in open discussion by avoiding potential power imbalances that could be introduced by mixing groups. The size per focus group aligns with best practices that recommend 5–10 per group, with 6–8 being ideal (Krueger & Casey, 2009). We aimed to recruit until saturation was reached (no new information arising out of group discussions) (Dworkin, 2024). For providers, we aimed to recruit the entire staff at the participating facility; therefore, the total sample size was also dictated by number of staff providing HIV care at the facility.

Recruitment and Eligibility

Study recruitment took place in a single health center serving both rural and peri-urban areas of Luwero District, as well as in the surrounding community for the community member focus groups. The health center was recommended by the study steering committee, chosen to match the characteristics of several health centers selected for a future intervention, which this qualitative data was used to inform (Sileo et al., 2022). The research team worked with facility leadership to invite eligible providers for participation in, and to schedule, focus groups/interviews. The research assistant informed the referred providers individually about participation, screened them for eligibility, and obtained written informed consent. Inclusion criteria for HIV providers included being: (1) HIV medical and clinical officers, nurses, midwives, counselors, or other lay workers involved in HIV care (e.g., linkage facilitators, peer educators, male champions); (2) 18 years of age or older; and (3) fluent in Luganda.

We recruited ART clients for focus group discussions with support from clinic staff. Health workers in the ART clinic referred interested participants to our research assistant. Our research assistant assessed eligibility and obtained written informed consent for participation. The consent process emphasized voluntary participation and that declining to participate would not affect one’s receipt of healthcare services. Inclusion criteria for HIV patients included being: (1) living with HIV; (2) enrolled in HIV care; (3) 18 years of age or older or emancipated minor (those under 18 but are married, with children, or otherwise caring for themselves and considered legal adults in Uganda); and (4) fluent in Luganda.

To elicit the perspectives of individuals not necessarily involved in HIV care (who may be different from men and women who have sought out HIV testing/treatment), men and women were also recruited from the communities surrounding the selected health center in Luwero District, with the following inclusion criteria: (1) resident of communities surrounding the selected health centers; (2) 18 years of age or emancipated minor; and (3) fluent in Luganda. The study team partnered with Village Health Team, or local community health workers who serve as the liaison between the community and the health facility, to purposively select participants that might meet the eligibility criterion through the out-patient clinic at the facility. Research staff approached individuals in the out-patient clinic and invited them to learn more about the study. For interested participants the research assistant informed them about the study, assessed eligibility, and obtained written informed consent.

Measures

Moderation Procedures and Interview Guides

Focus groups discussions were conducted in Luganda following a semi-structured guide by two trained interviewers experienced in qualitative research. The moderators were trained to probe beyond normative perceptions, and to manage group dynamics that commonly arise in focus groups (e.g., debate at poles, eliciting perspectives from all participants, managing domineering participants, probing based on non-verbal cues) (Krueger & Casey, 2009; Morgan, 1997). The focus group guide included questions to elicit an understanding of local gender norms and roles, perspectives of differences in and drivers of men and women’s engagement in HIV prevention, treatment, and related services, and feedback on potential intervention approaches to reduce the negative effect of gender norms and inequity on HIV outcomes. ART clients and providers were asked about their experience receiving/delivering HIV services to men and women, and community members about their experience seeking health and HIV services and the effect of provider gender on care provision. The discussions lasted approximately 90 min, and each took place at the health facility. A research assistant took detailed notes, and the discussions were audio recorded. All focus group participants received 22,000 Ugandan Shillings for participation (~ 6 USD), which was aligned with human subjects review practices we learned from our research partners at Mildmay Uganda, and was approved by the IRBs.

Key Informant Interview Procedures

In addition to focus groups, we conducted 16 key informant interviews. While there are many valid approaches and varying factors to consider in determining key informant interview sample sizes (Dworkin, 2024), we used guidance from Hennink et al. (2017) and sought an N of 16 based on guidance to reach code saturation or the point when no additional issues are identified and the codebook begins to stabilize. Key informant interviews were selected to identify individuals with more specific expertise on a topic, making the group heterogenous and thus, less appropriate for focus group participation. Individual interviews included the three men who were employed at this clinic, as previously discussed. Otherwise, we sought individuals with expertise in the local health system and HIV services and programming, such as facility and ART clinic supervisors called “in-charges” locally, “HIV focal persons” or district-level leads for HIV programming, District Health Officers and others from the District Health Team (district-level health leadership), and Mildmay Uganda expert trainers (health professionals who provide capacity building to the HIV workforce). Given the exclusion of men from the focus groups, we purposely sought a balance of men and women in interviews. We identified participants through referrals from the study steering committee members, Mildmay Uganda partners, and facility in-charges. The study team provided an overview of the goals of the key informant interviews, and our partners referred potential participants to the study team based on the information provided. In addition to being considered an HIV stakeholder locally, individuals needed to be 18 years of age or older and fluent in Luganda or English to be eligible for participation.

Key informant interviews were conducted one-on-one in a private, agreed upon location, in Luganda or English. A trained qualitative interviewer led the interviews, following a semi-structured guide. Like the focus group guide, the interview guide aimed to understand local gender norms and roles, perceptions on gender disparities in HIV outcomes at the local clinics (including the role of provider–client relationships) and on the health system or district-level priorities and programs related to gender and HIV. Interviews were approximately 40 min. Participants received 25,000 Ugandan Shillings (~ 7 USD) for participation (consistent with Uganda IRB practices).

Data Analysis

Thematic analysis was used to analyze our data (Boyatzis, 1998) using a hybrid deductive-inductive approach. We pre-identified core elements of patient-centeredness to guide the coding scheme deductively (e.g., communication, trust, empathy, shared decision-making) (Gourlay et al., 2014; Mead & Bower, 2000), while revising and inductively expanding on the coding guide through iterative review of the transcripts. We developed a codebook with definitions and illustrative examples from the data. During codebook development, the team met weekly to discuss codes and potential themes, and to revise the coding guide as needed. Decision trails were kept to ensure accuracy and consistency of the codes. After the codebook was set, three research assistants, trained by the PI, coded the data, including one Ugandan bachelor’s-level research assistant, a US bachelor’s student, and a US master’s student. All transcripts were double coded and disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus with the PI. While others, at times, use inter-rater reliability in this process, it is a somewhat contested and inherently quantitative convention that assumes there is an objective set of codes (Armstrong et al., 1997). Thus, we did not use it in our process, consistent with several qualitative experts in the field (Barbour, 2001; Stenfors et al., 2020).

We entered excerpts and code into a Microsoft Excel® spreadsheet to aid in the review of data and development of a results summary with representative quotations. For the analysis for this paper’s specific research questions, we used the conceptual models for patient-centeredness by Gourlay et al. (2014) and Mead and Bower (2000) to guide the organization and interpretation of key themes. We were guided by both models, but found Gourlay et al., (2014)’s version adapted for HIV care in sub-Saharan Africa fit our data best.

The findings of this analysis, along with the findings of the larger study, were disseminated back to the health facilities and communities involved in the study in the form of community presentations at the facilities, as well as to the Ugandan Ministry of Health, who gave feedback on the final themes. Their feedback concurred with the findings and provided more insight on contextual/structural challenges in the provision of PCC in this setting.

Biopsychosocial Perspective

Perceptions of Gender Norms Impacted Providers’ Views of Clients’ Compliance with HIV Care

Women’s socialization to be “good” patients. Provider interviews revealed perceptions that men and women’s gendered socialization manifests in their respective treatment compliance behavior. Health, lay workers, and leadership largely recognized women as having better engagement in HIV care than men. This disparity was often explained by differences in men and women’s ability to be “good” clients, as one provider explained:When you talk about adherence to women, they respond compared to men. They give time. When you are counselling, they give you time to understand, unlike the men. They [men] are ever rushing for other activities. For the women, they always cope, they listen, they request for advice compared to men (Man, Counselor, KII).

They [men] are ever rushing for other activities. For the women, they always cope, they listen, they request for advice compared to men (Man, Counselor, KII).

Women were said to be more willing to do as they have been told, to wait patiently in queue, to have greater trust in the health system than men, and to be able to accept their HIV status more easily than men—attributes described as important to their clinic attendance and ART adherence. Other key components of women’s compliance from the perspective of health and lay workers included being perceived as good at listening and taking advice, and having a greater interest in their health, compared to men. Women’s greater interest in their health was attributed by many providers, and by women themselves, to their role in the family as the primary caregiver to their children.Women are more involved since…they breastfeed these children, they are more attached to the children unlike men who do not do so. Women think of children first and feel it is their responsibility to stay healthy so that they take care of their children. Whatever the service providers tell them, you realize it is positive to women to support her to live longer. Men usually take everything for granted…you find them in the male ward because they wait until they are very ill and admit to be taken to the facility which is not with women (Woman, Maternity health worker focus group discussion [HW FGD]).

Men’s gender-related barriers to care engagement. Similar to women’s social responsibilities leading them to better care engagement, a male health worker (KII) recognized men’s absence from care was linked to men’s “social responsibility to be more of bread earners for their homes” leading them to prioritize work responsibilities over health. They also recognized the cultural influences on men’s health-seeking behavior “[In some tribes] for a man to come and seek for healthcare services, he is weaker…people will say ‘What’s wrong with that man, he is like a woman’” (Man, HW, KII). Relatedly, it was recognized across participants that, in addition to a fear of being sick, HIV stigma prevents many men from seeking HIV testing and care until they have physical symptoms, and that this problem persists despite structural interventions to make clinics more “male-friendly” as one provider stated:The reason could be still stigmatization, and you know men turn up at health facility is low, they have a low health seeking behavior that they rarely come to the facility despite all efforts put in place. They have evening clinics, then flexible hours mainly targeting men and adolescents. We have increased outreaches like for HIV testing, but you still find that we test more women than men (Man, HW, KII).

However, most providers did not explain men’s absence from care from this gender norms or HIV stigma lens. Rather, they described men as taking their health for granted and perceived them as not motivated for care engagement by their role in the family (unlike women)—a view most prominently expressed by women providers. Providers described men as prioritizing work over their health, that they were “always complaining,” and have difficulty waiting in queues, were all discussed as making men difficult. Providers’ perception of this attitude among men and how it can compromise care is showcased further in the next quotation.Whenever the man comes here at the clinic, he will be impatient and expecting you to provide services very fast so that they can go, they claim they have left the business at risk and are needed there very fast. Even they start arguing and showing impatience. They want everything to be done speedy, yet you may find when they may need more services like viral load testing, intensive counseling due to missed appointment and so forth. This is not the case with women, they come to the facility after planning to spend most of their time at the clinic on that specific day to receive all services. In most cases, when a man shows impatience, the service provider may even do errors in prescriptions and recording due to the pressure (Woman, Lay Health Worker [LHW] FGD).

This is not the case with women, they come to the facility after planning to spend most of their time at the clinic on that specific day to receive all services. In most cases, when a man shows impatience, the service provider may even do errors in prescriptions and recording due to the pressure (Woman, Lay Health Worker [LHW] FGD).

In contrast with this view among providers, in the ART client FGD with men, men described feeling ART’s benefits on their health and the desire to stay healthy for their family as the main motivators for continued care engagement, expressing care toward their health and for their role in the family.

Decision-Making Processes

Gender Norms’ Effect on Provider and Client Communication

Provider perceptions of how gender norms shapes client communication styles. Participants described differences in men and women’s communication styles, related to men and women’s gender socialization. Women were described as conforming to social norms of femininity and therefore their communication was described as being more “passive” and “agreeable” than men’s, who were said to speak more directly than women, in that they stated their needs directly, but were less open in their emotional expression or sharing of their personal lives during counseling. Providers’ experience with these communication styles and their effects on the counseling of clients varied. Most providers described women as more honest and open during counseling (except for women struggling with HIV status disclosure, which they described as their main gender-related barriers to care, underpinned by gender inequity in intimate relationships). As one counselor (man, KII) stated: “Women open up. Whatever challenge they go through, they can at least explain, to you unlike men. They [men] cannot even open up to tell you what challenge they go through.”

Other providers described men’s direct communication style as making them easier to talk to because they state what they need more directly. However, for many women providers, men’s direct communication style and tendency to be demanding was perceived as rude. This was a particularly salient theme in the lay health worker focus group. As one peer shared, “Out of 100%, like 5% of the women can show cruelness, but for men, the majority are impatient and cruel when they come to receive services” (Woman, LHW FGD). Across all focus groups, men’s rude communication was attributed to their contextual gender-related barriers to care, with work conflict and the desire to earn income cited among men’s primary barriers to HIV care engagement for men and the reason they present “impatiently” to care.

The effect of provider gender on client’s communication/comfort. As for communication from providers, ART clients and community members mostly described positive experiences with the health and lay health workers locally and did not differentiate between the quality of treatment from men vs. women providers. As such, clients described a willingness to see providers of either gender. When probed further about discussing sensitive issues with providers (e.g., related to sex) or the ability to be open with providers, there were men and women who admitted that they would be more comfortable talking with a fellow man or woman. As one man stated (Men’s FGD, ART clients): “Mostly a man to a man [discussion is preferred] because you do not have any shame to say anything, but with a woman [provider], you may fear a bit for her to see your nakedness or to know about your sexual weaknesses. It is not real fear but you feel more comfortable [talking to a man].” Here, the man admits not wanting to show vulnerability, specifically about sexual health, to women providers, preferring talking to men.

However, counter to clients expressing no gender preference regarding providers, one male provider strongly stated that they perceived men to prefer to see other men, “Men usually despise ladies. You may find that you need a male attendant to attend to these men” (Man, Health Worker, KII). The ability for men to be served by other men, however, is hindered by the greater ratio of women to men providers in the clinics resulting in fewer men as providers to cater to men’s needs. “Most staff here are females offering those services of counseling. [The] ART department is full of female providers compared to the males” (Man, Health Worker, KII).

Providers’ provision of patient-centered communication. In focus groups/interviews with providers, we also asked about the provision of patient-centered communication to clients generally, given its relevance to gender-sensitive care. Health workers described the importance of using client-centered communication (e.g., building rapport, taking a non-judgmental approach, eliciting the clients’ needs) in ensuring that clients will be open with them. However, providers did describe witnessing poor treatment of clients by others and recognized the negative ramifications on patient outcomes. Providers were also open about issues with the structural environment of the clinic impeding their provision of patient-centered care generally. Namely, limited space and time (due to client workload) to provide adequate counseling to both men and women, as one provider explained in the quotation below. While these issues negatively impact both men and women, providers emphasized men’s strong desire for privacy, given the perception that men were more affected by HIV stigma.In some facilities, it could be space, you know privacy also matters, so if there is not enough space where the counselor and client are seated comfortably, then I would personally not open up. Secondly the time factor, the pre-counseling is sometimes passed on in group [sessions, rather than individually] because of the big number [of clients], so this makes one not to open up (Man, Health Worker, KII).

The Effect of Gender Norms on Power Dynamics

Occupational and gender status interact to shape power dynamics and trust between clients and providers. Participants’ narratives suggested there was deference toward the occupational status of health workers. Among providers and stakeholders, respect and status aligned with occupational rank and qualifications. There was consensus that, on a societal-level, men have more power than women, but, in the health system, the hierarchy of medicine determines a hierarchy of power over all else. One health worker explained (Woman, ART Provider FGD): “There is what is called skill and hierarchy in medicine, and this rhymes with your level of education. It does not matter if somebody is a lady. If she is above you, by virtue of her hierarchy, she has to lead. Here [at this clinic], the leader is female.”

Clients’ narratives similarly revealed reverence toward health workers’ status. However, clients also suggested that this status contributes to an inherent power imbalance in client and provider relationships, favoring providers. This imbalance was felt by both men and women clients, as both groups alluded to having no autonomous, or even collaborative (with providers), decision-making in their health care, as one woman said in the client focus groups (with agreement from others, and echoed in the men’s group), “We have no right in making any decision about our treatment and care.”

Client participants collectively expressed respect toward all providers based on their status as health worker alone, irrespective of gender and even of rank, as one man living with HIV said, “So long as she [the provider] is medical personnel, either doctor or nurse, that is the trust I have for the service provider, irrespective of the sex” (Men’s FGD, ART Clients). However, this view differs from the above stated preference among some clients to see a provider of their own gender and clashed with some health workers perceptions of how men interacted with women staff specifically. One health worker (man, KII) explained, for men, “…it is just direct. He [a client] will look at a female [health worker] and will call that one ‘a girl’ and will not really give them the required information.” This example showcases how providers perceived clients who are men have an unwillingness to take advice from a woman provider which can, ultimately, negatively affect their openness with the health worker. Further, the use of the term “girl” to discredit a health worker highlights the importance of age and gender, in combination, in shaping power dynamics within client–provider relationships; even some providers perceived women were more comfortable talking to someone older than themselves. Results presented next in features of care corroborate and expand on these perspectives, with women providers’ describing challenges working with men, underpinned by these conflicting power dynamics and men’s gendered expectations for respect.

Feature of Care

Gender Norms and Clients’ Perceptions of Respectful Care

Related to communication and power dynamics, a key aspect of care that was mentioned was respect. As described above, women providers commonly felt disrespected by the men they served. Yet for men, feeling respected by health workers was paramount to their satisfaction with care. While both men and women clients and community members said the health workers generally treated them with respect, they also described cases to the contrary. Participants described men as especially sensitive to harsh communication and identified it as one factor that can lead men to avoid healthcare. In the community men’s focus groups, for example, one man stated that: “if the health worker doesn’t give him proper attention and at times the health worker shouts at him…it is imprinted in him that there is no help at the health facility; when you go there you are abused” (Men’s Community FGD). An ART in-charge (woman, KII) shared the negative impact of providers’ harsh treatment of men on men’s openness: “Men are harder to open up than women, and especially if you are harsh, they can’t open up.”

Further, the men’s ART client focus group revealed the importance of feeling respected from health workers to men’s satisfaction with care, trust of the provider, and perceived quality of care. While women also said they generally felt they were treated with respect and without judgment from providers, exchanges between women and the moderator in the ART client FGD suggest that being reprimanded by providers (if defaulting on treatment) was accepted, and perhaps normalized, among some women who were quick to take responsibility for their mistakes. One woman said, “even when they reprimand us, they are right” and another said “We don’t judge them over some things. It is because of us, the patients” (Women’s FGD, ART clients).

Provider Gender Biases Shape Empathy for Gender-Related Barriers to Care

While providers expressed awareness of men and women’s differential engagement in care (as described under “Biopsychosocial perspective”) and gender-related barriers to care engagement (e.g., HIV stigma’s impact on men and women, differential disclosure issues, conflicts between men and women’s responsibilities and HIV care, gender-based violence), their empathy for men and women’s specific challenges varied and were influenced by their own gender biases.

The negative perceptions that many providers held toward men as clients (as previously discussed) resulted in limited empathy toward men’s challenges among providers. As one lay health worker stated, “[Men] behave like babies, where you need to console and remind them often about everything. They are not easy people” (Women, LHW FGD). Health and lay workers discussed men’s reasons for missed clinic appointments sometimes with a tone of doubt, qualifying men’s barriers to care with statements like “men claim that…” or “men complain that…” Others described men’s explanations for missed visits or non-adherence as “excuses.” However, once again, there was recognition that men’s excuses are often a cover for a significant underlying barrier for men, HIV stigma, or the fear of being found positive by others in the community. One health worker (Woman, ART Provider FGD) explained how men’s impatience with HIV care is tied to HIV stigma, “some men do not want to spend any time waiting, they want to be offered a service as soon as they reach at the facility, it is mainly because of stigma and they fear that their colleagues may find them waiting in the ART clinic.” While there was recognition that HIV stigma played a role in men’s impatience with clinic appointments and waiting in queue, understanding this barrier to men’s care engagement did not seem to mitigate the frustration providers’ felt toward men.

Despite low empathy displayed for men’s challenges among providers, community members, male clients, and some providers, seemed to view men’s work responsibilities as a more legitimate reason for missing clinic visits or treatment adherence than for women’s responsibilities. This was gleaned from comments that women have an easy time engaging in care because they are “always at home [and] not so occupied with lots of work” as one health worker stated (Woman, ART Provider FGD), and that they have less serious responsibilities than men, as the next quotation suggests.Women have a lot of free time and do not find it a problem to remember such things [appointments/adherence]. They do household chores and other light work, so they cannot forget. They always do some light work around, not moving in different places, unlike men who always go for safari [travel for work] (Men’s FGD, ART Clients).

In contrast, many women in the ART client focus group discussed these responsibilities as creating real challenges to their clinic attendance. One woman living with HIV gave an example from her own life: “The waiting time is too long, and when you have children whom you have to pick during school time, you will either find them lost or feeling angry…it plays a big role in hindering the women” (Women’s FGD, ART Clients).

As for women, most providers expressed empathy toward their primary barrier to care, related to disclosure issues, and a desire to support women clients experiencing these challenges, except for some lay health workers who expressed frustration with women who “prefer to lie” to providers, because they had not disclosed (Woman, LHW FGD). In focus groups and interviews, young women were named by providers and other stakeholders as being difficult to engage and especially affected by stigma. While bias against young women with HIV or at risk of HIV was not apparent in discussions with providers, discussions and interviews with others highlighted biases and stigma that the community carries toward young women living with or at risk for acquiring HIV, with assumptions made and negative perceptions about their number of partners, engagement in transactional sex or sex work, their alcohol use and “partying,” and not taking their health seriously.Yes, women were created differently that they made their life business, some even have a slogan that “I would rather die of HIV than poverty”, especially those girls at the university. But for men, they take this as an important matter and do everything possible to live longer, stay with one partner (Men’s FGD, ART Clients).

Discussion

This study provides critical insights into how gender norms shape provider–client interactions and influence the provision of quality ART services in the context of HIV care in central Uganda, which can be used to improve PCC for both women and men. Despite the availability of literature focused on the role of gender norms on HIV outcomes, few studies exist that explore how they affect provider–client relationships in ways pertinent to clients’ HIV care engagement. This study is the first to examine this topic in Uganda and also uniquely examined this question through a PCC lens. Guided by Gourlay et al.’s (2014) and Mead and Bower’s (2000) conceptual models, our findings highlight the impact of gender norms on provider perceptions of HIV care compliance behaviors, communication and power dynamics with providers, and perceptions of respectful care.

Within the domain biopsychosocial perspective, women were widely perceived by providers and peers as more engaged and compliant with HIV care, driven largely by norms of feminine amiability and due to their caregiving roles and greater acceptance of HIV status, whereas men’s delayed care-seeking and perceived impatience were often attributed to work responsibilities and stigma-related fears. These gender-related patterns were further complicated by entrenched power imbalances within client–provider relationships, shaped by intersecting occupational hierarchies and societal gender expectations (decision-making processes). Importantly, while providers recognized gender-specific barriers to care, feature of care related to empathy and support varied, with notable biases among women providers toward men in particular. These findings underscore the necessity of addressing gender norms and power relations to enhance patient-centered HIV care in this setting.

Our study underscores the importance of gender norms in shaping men and women’s health-seeking and care engagement (Leburu-Masigo & Kgadima, 2021; Sileo et al., 2018a, 2018b, 2019). It extends this literature by delineating how gender norms affect care engagement through their impact on provider–client relationships, including their effect on communication styles within client–provider interactions. Perceived gender norms were found to influence relevant outcomes: openness, trust, and care engagement. Women were commonly described as more passive, agreeable, and willing to listen and to open up during counseling, aligning with societal expectations of obedience. Consequently, providers often perceived women as more honest and compliant during counseling. Although prior research with this aim is scant, studies in Tanzania and Pakistan reported women’s communicative behaviors tend to be more affiliative and relationship-oriented in healthcare settings (Kazi et al., 2021; Kitole et al., 2025).

In contrast, men’s communication was characterized by directness and impatience, traits rooted in masculine socialization and exacerbated by work-related time constraints and stigma-related anxieties (Courtenay, 2000; Sileo et al., 2019). While some providers found men’s straightforwardness easier to navigate, as they were more likely to state their needs quickly, many—especially women providers—perceived it as rude or challenging, complicating efforts to build rapport and provide comprehensive counseling. Despite calls for more research on engaging men, few other studies have examined how health workers’ perceptions of men’s engagement can affect their own behavior toward men. However, one other study in Malawi and Mozambique highlighted that men’s health-seeking behaviors and communication styles can create barriers to effective client–provider relationships (Dovel et al., 2023). Additionally, although clients generally expressed openness to providers of any gender, gender concordance increased comfort for some clients when discussing sensitive topics. Given the greater number of women than men providers in this setting’s structural environment, this reinforces calls in the literature for increasing male provider representation in HIV care to improve men’s engagement (Colvin, 2019).

The findings also highlight how power dynamics within client–provider interactions are influenced by gendered expectations around respect as a feature of care, particularly masculine norms that shape men’s experiences and behaviors in HIV care. Men’s emphasis on receiving respect is intertwined with broader societal notions of masculinity and status, which, when combined with the healthcare hierarchy, can lead to challenging encounters characterized by impatience or directness and not wanting to take direction from young women. While providers’ frustrations with men are grounded in real experiences, these interactions seem to have fostered biases that limit providers’ empathy and obscure the significant role that stigma-driven fear plays in men’s engagement with care. Aligned with other studies (Sileo et al., 2018a, 2018b), men living with HIV in our study said their main motivation for care was to be healthy for their family and to continue to fulfill positive masculine roles. Yet, an important finding of this study is that providers did not recognize this motivation in men. In Dovel et al.’s (2023) study of healthcare workers in Malawi and Mozambique, health workers also viewed men as difficult clients and had little empathy for men’s gender norm related barriers to care, given their greater power in society. These findings suggest the need for more attention paid to the role health workers can play in exacerbating men’s barriers to care, rather than alleviating them.

For women, the power imbalances in decision-making processes manifests differently. Women were more likely to accept harsh treatment from providers—while this aligns with feminine socialization to be agreeable and passive (Camara et al., 2020; Leburu-Masigo & Kgadima, 2021) it warrants more research in relation to provider–client relationships. Additionally, these data highlight how gender inequities and societal expectations often constrain women’s autonomy in decision-making, particularly around disclosure and adherence (Kabir et al., 2022; Katz et al., 2013). Providers in our study were generally aware of these barriers for women and empathetic toward them, but a minority of lay health workers expressing frustration toward women with disclosure issues, calling them dishonest. These themes align with one study in Kenya, where providers interpreted women’s indirect communication style as evasive (Knight et al., 2018). Taken together, the findings highlight the need to increase provider’s awareness and empathy toward gender barriers to care for both men and women in order to improve PCC.

Addressing the intersecting power and gender dynamics underscored by this study requires a nuanced approach that fosters provider empathy and support and offers tailored strategies to enhance engagement for all clients by recognizing both men’s and women’s lived realities in the context of HIV care. Gender-sensitivity trainings for health care providers represent a promising intervention to address biases and improve the quality of client–provider interactions. A systematic review of gender-sensitivity educational interventions in global settings and across health outcomes found promise in their ability to increase gender-related knowledge, attitudes or practice after such trainings (Lindsay et al., 2019). However, the authors of this systematic review concluded that more rigorous research is needed in this area before there is sufficient evidence to determine effectiveness.

Specific to the Ugandan context, our group (Sileo et al., 2022, 2025a, 2025b) demonstrated in a pilot study that a novel gender-sensitivity training was acceptable and feasible in this rural/peri-urban setting, with preliminary evidence to support providers’ increased understanding of gender norms and reduced stigmatizing attitudes toward both men and women living with HIV in Uganda. The training helped providers recognize their own bias against clients, increase empathy toward men and women’s gender-related barriers to care, and enhance the provision of patient-centered HIV care. Such trainings may help providers recognize the structural and social barriers that men and women face, moving beyond stereotypes to appreciate clients’ complex motivations and constraints. However, our piloted intervention is the first intervention, that we aware of, to try to improve PCC and subsequent client outcomes through a gender-sensitivity and norms lens—an approach that warrants further exploration given the relevance of gender norms to provider–client relationships.

This study had several limitations. First, the generalizability of the results is limited due to the qualitative design and the specific regional and clinical contexts in which data were collected. While qualitative methods are valuable for in-depth understanding, the findings may not be representative of all settings or provider populations. The use of two different qualitative data collection methods—focus groups and individual interviews—may introduce variability in the type and depth of information gathered. However, our approach strengthened our understanding of patient–provider interactions by triangulating two differed qualitative methods and gathered perspectives from different types of stakeholders.

In addition, a gender imbalance was present in our sample, with a greater representation of women providers, which aligns with the demographic make-up of providers in Uganda generally. To address this, we purposively sampled more men providers in the individual interviews. However, collecting data from men and women providers through different methods may have introduced methodological bias, complicating direct comparisons across genders. The frustration expressed toward men as clients largely came from women; thus, more research should elicit male provider’s perspectives on working with men to get the full picture. Lastly, the non-random, purposive sampling strategy used to recruit participants aligns with qualitative research best practices aimed at capturing diverse perspectives, but it inherently carries the risk of selection bias. These limitations suggest that while the study provides valuable insights into gender and power dynamics in HIV care, caution is warranted in extrapolating these findings to broader populations or contexts.

In conclusion, this study underscores the influence of gender norms and power relations on provider–client interactions and the delivery of HIV care in central Uganda. By illuminating how these dynamics influence treatment compliance, communication and power dynamics, and features of care related to trust, empathy and respect within clinical encounters, the findings call for intentional efforts by health systems, governments, and non-governmental health organizations to address provider biases and structural inequalities through gender-sensitive approaches. Within the health system, enhancing provider training to cultivate nuanced understanding of men’s and women’s distinct challenges and motivations may hold promise for improving patient-centered care, fostering trust, and ultimately strengthening ART engagement and health outcomes, but more research is needed in this area. Recognizing and integrating how gender norms become part of health care practices and are a central component in HIV service delivery is essential to advancing equitable care and supporting the diverse needs of all clients.
