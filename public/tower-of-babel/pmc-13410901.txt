Effects of dexmedetomidine nasal spray combined with thoracic paravertebral block on sleep and pain in herpes zoster neuralgia: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xiaoxiao Han, Yuanyuan Wang, Xinqiao Zhou, Lingqing Zeng, Xiaokai Zhou, Yinbing Pan, Xiaodi Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHerpes zoster neuralgia(HZN) is a common challenging problem, and there is a growing need to develop safe and effective treatments. Sleep disorders is commonly associated with HZN because pain intensity of HZN conditions is often worse at night. Evidence indicates that dexmedetomidine nasal spray is effective in alleviating pain and improving sleep quality in perioperative patients; however, no randomized controlled trials have evaluated its use in HZN.MethodsIn this randomized, controlled, single-blind trial, a total of 103 patients were screened, and 70 patients were equally randomized to the dexmedetomidine(DEX) group and the control group. The DEX group received dexmedetomidine nasal spray at 1.0 ~ 1.5 µg·kg− 1 for three consecutive nights and the control group did not receive any nasal spray drugs. Both groups received a similar regimen of oral medication and a once-daily ultrasound-guided thoracic paravertebral block. Sleep parameters were monitored using smart wearable devices.ResultsThe primary outcome was total sleep time during treatment. The group-by-time interaction for total sleep time was not statistically significant (F (2,140) = 2.36, P = 0.099). However, a significant main effect of group was observed (F (1,70) = 9.03, P = 0.004), indicating that participants in the DEX group had a significantly longer average total sleep time compared with the control group.The improvement in deep sleep time in the DEX group increased with treatment duration (least squares mean difference after 1 days = − 8.86 min [95% CI, − 23.53 to 5.82]; P = 0.236); after 2 days = 15.34 min [95% CI 0.67 to 30.02] ; P = 0.041; after 3 days = 20.26 min [95% CI 5.58 to 34.93]; P = 0.007). The number of nighttime awakenings was significantly lower in the DEX group at all time points (P < 0.001). Both the pain visual analogue scale score (F (1,70) = 10.90, P = 0.002) and the insomnia severity index score (mean difference = 1.86 [95% CI 0.44 to 3.28]; P = 0.011) were significantly lower in the DEX group compared with the control group. There were no statistical differences in the incidence of adverse events, including dizziness, nausea, drowsiness, hypotension, or the need for rescue medication.ConclusionsDexmedetomidine nasal spray effectively and safely improved sleep quality and alleviated pain in patients with truncal herpes zoster neuralgia. These findings need to be validated in multicenter studies in diverse populations to ensure generalizability.

Introduction

Herpes zoster, also known as shingles, arises from the reactivation of varicella zoster virus and manifests typically as a painful, unilateral, vesicular rash [1]. Based on pain duration, acute pain occurring within 1 month and subacute pain within 3 months are collectively defined as herpes zoster neuralgia (HZN) [2]. This pain is frequently severe and persistent. Oral medications remain the primary treatment; however, many patients still experience inadequate pain control because of adverse effects or limited efficacy. Therefore, identifying novel, nontraditional therapeutic targets has become an important research priority [3]. A multicenter prospective study [4] reported that 63.9% of patients with HZN experienced pain-induced sleep disturbances, which in turn further reduce pain tolerance, creating a vicious cycle of pain and sleep disruption [5]. Thus, improving sleep has emerged as an important therapeutic target in HZN [6]. However, commonly used benzodiazepines hypnotics carry risks of respiratory depression, dependence, and withdrawal symptoms [7]. In addition, Wu et al. [8] reported in a large cohort study that both benzodiazepine and non-benzodiazepine hypnotics were independently associated with an increased risk of frailty in older adults. Therefore, there is an urgent need for sedative–hypnotic regimens that are both effective and safe, particularly for short-term administration.

Dexmedetomidine, as a highly selective α2 adrenergic receptor agonist, has unique pharmacological advantages. It promotes endogenous sleep pathways and mimics natural non–rapid eye movement sleep, allowing patients to remain easily arousable and cooperative. In addition, it exerts both spinal and supraspinal analgesic effects. This dual sedative–analgesic action offers a unique advantage for disrupting the vicious cycle of pain and sleep disturbance in HZN [9]. Unlike traditional hypnotics, dexmedetomidine carries minimal risk of respiratory depression and lacks addictive potential. Intranasal administration bypasses first-pass hepatic metabolism and markedly improves bioavailability [10]. Its intranasal spray formulation is easy to administer without venipuncture or injection, substantially improving treatment adherence, particularly among elderly patients in general ward settings. Therefore, dexmedetomidine represents a promising option for HZN-related sleep disturbances; however, high-quality randomized controlled trials evaluating its efficacy remain limited.

Most current studies rely on patient-reported sleep measures. However, such measures cannot capture sleep architecture and are vulnerable to recall bias [11]. Polysomnography remains the clinical gold standard for assessing sleep stages; however, its intrusive setup may disrupt natural sleep and reduce assessment accuracy [12]. Consequently, it is not recommended as a routine test for sleep disorders in older adults [13]. Wearable smart devices offer a feasible means of objective, longitudinal sleep monitoring owing to their ease of use, low cost, long battery life, and user-friendly interface. Studies have demonstrated good concordance between wearable devices and polysomnography for sleep parameter detection, supporting their growing use in clinical research [14].

Therefore, this single-center, single-blinded randomized controlled trial was designed to investigate the effect of the addition of dexmedetomidine nasal spray on analgesia and sleep quality in hospitalized patients with truncal herpes zoster neuralgia receiving conventional treatment.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethics

This single-center, single-blinded randomized controlled trial was conducted in the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University (Jiangsu, China) from September 2024 to October 2025.

The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee in August 2024 (Approval No. 2024-SR-537.A1) and registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (www.chictr.org.cn; ChiCTR2500109200). The trial was reported in compliance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guidelines.

Participant enrollment

After written informed consents were obtained, patients aged 18–80 years who were clinically diagnosed with truncal HZN—defined as involvement of the thoracic dermatomes (T1~T12)—and had a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score ≥ 4 were enrolled. Exclusion criteria included (1) history of chronic rhinitis; (2) sick sinus syndrome, severe sinus bradycardia (heart rate < 50 beats·min− 1), second-degree or higher atrioventricular block, permanent pacemaker implantation, severe heart failure, or left ventricular ejection fraction < 30%; (3) history of cerebral hemorrhage or stroke; or significant dysfunction of major organs (heart, brain, or kidneys), such as Child–Pugh class B or C liver disease or chronic kidney disease stage 3~5; (4) depression or sleep disorders requiring the use of sedatives, antidepressants, or other psychotropic drugs. (5) known allergy to any component of the study drug, its formulation, or its container; (6) inability to comply with study procedures; (7) trigeminal neuralgia, complex regional pain syndrome, or central neuropathic pain.

Randomization and masking

The patients were randomly assigned to the DEX or control group according to the computer-generated random sequence, which was created with a 1:1 ratio by SPSS 25.0. The random sequence was sealed in an opaque envelope with a serial number. After enrollment, a researcher, who was not involved in data collection and outcome assessment, opened the envelope to reveal the treatment arm and performed dexmedetomidine nasal spray administration. Due to the nature of the intervention, participants and intervention administrators were not blinded. However, outcome assessors and statistical analysts were blinded to group allocation throughout the study.

Study interventions

During the study, all patients received standardized analgesic therapy. The oral medication regimen consisted of pregabalin (75 mg twice daily), and mecobalamin (one tablet three times daily). Beginning on the second hospital day, all patients received a once-daily ultrasound-guided thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB). The operation steps of TPVB are as follows : The skin was disinfected with povidone iodine centered on the paravertebral space corresponding to the damaged skin area. The 2 ~ 5 MHz low frequency convex array probe is selected. The ultrasonic probe and the spinous process connection were placed vertically at the level of the transverse process of the target segment. In the ultrasonic scan, the lower part of the transverse process was the thoracic paravertebral space. The in-plane approach of paravertebral transverse section was adopted. Marked by the transverse process, the needle was inserted from the outside of the probe to the inside until the needle tip reached the lower end of the lateral end of the transverse process, between the intercostal intima and the pleura ( the transition between the intercostal space and the thoracic paravertebral space ), and after bloodless and gasless, a small amount of normal saline was injected, and the phenomenon of subpleural pressure appeared, indicating that the needle tip position was correct. Slow injection of 0.3% lidocaine 20 ml (Fig. 1).

The control group received only the standard therapy and no intranasal medication. In addition to standard therapy, patients in the DEX group received intranasal dexmedetomidine at 21:00 from hospital day 2 to day 4 (Shanghai Hengrui Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The formulation contained 500 µg·mL− 1 and was administered for three consecutive nights. The dose was adjusted to 1.0~1.5 µg·kg− 1 according to body weight. The dosing range was determined based on the drug label and previous research findings. Previous studies have demonstrated that intranasal dexmedetomidine at 1.0~1.5 µg·kg− 1 can safely and effectively prolong sleep duration and reduce nighttime awakenings [15]. Drug administration was conducted by trained research personnel. Patients were seated with their heads slightly tilted forward, and the nasal cavity was cleaned. The spray nozzle was inserted into the nostril at an angle aligned with the nasal passage, and the pump was pressed once (delivering 25 µg of dexmedetomidine per spray). After one spray was administered to each nostril, patients slightly tilted their heads back and were instructed to inhale gently. After approximately 30 s, while keeping the bottle upright, a second spray was administered to each nostril. Vital signs, including blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation, were monitored for 40 min after administration, then hourly for the subsequent 2 h to detect delayed adverse events.

During the study, permitted rescue medications were acetaminophen (500 mg orally per day). All patients were housed in a standardized ward environment. After 21:00, bright lights were turned off, unnecessary nursing activities were minimized, and a consistent sleep environment was maintained. The dosages of all oral medications remained constant throughout the study.

Data collection and measurements

Sleep was continuously monitored with a smart wearable device (HUAWEI Band 9, China). Monitoring was conducted over four nights: the first night after admission (T0) and the first (T1), second (T2), and third (T3) nights of treatment. Before sleep, patients wore the device on the non-dominant wrist to minimize interference with routine activities. The device automatically recorded and calculated the following sleep parameters: Total Sleep Time (TST): Total duration from sleep onset to final awakening (min), excluding periods of wakefulness.Deep Sleep Time (DST): Duration of slow-wave sleep (stage N3) (min), which plays a key role in physiological restoration and pain modulation.Light Sleep Time (LST): Duration of light sleep (stages N1 + N2) (min).Rapid Eye Movement Sleep Time (REMT): Duration of REM sleep (min), which is associated with cognitive and emotional regulation. Number of Awakenings (NA): Frequency of transitions from sleep to wakefulness during the night (times), reflecting sleep continuity.

The primary outcome was TST, measured by a smart wearable device throughout the treatment period. Secondary Outcomes included daily DST, LST, REMT, and NA in both groups during the treatment period.Pain intensity at rest was evaluated using the VAS from T0 to T3. Assessments were performed two to three times within each time window. The average of these three measurements was used in the analysis. The VAS consists of a 10-cm horizontal line anchored at “0 = no pain” and “10 = worst imaginable pain.” Patients marked their perceived pain intensity on the line, and the distance from the “0” anchor to the mark was recorded as the VAS score, with higher scores indicating greater pain severity. The Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) was used to assess patients’ perceived insomnia severity at T0 and post-treatment (T3). The ISI is a seven-item self-report questionnaire evaluating sleep onset and maintenance difficulties, sleep satisfaction, and daytime functional impairment. Total scores range from 0 to 28 and are categorized as follows: 0~7 (no clinically significant insomnia), 8~14 (mild), 15~21 (moderate), and 22~28 (severe insomnia). The ISI demonstrates high reliability and validity and is widely used in clinical settings for insomnia assessment owing to its sensitivity to treatment-related changes.

Adverse events included hypotension, dizziness, hypoxemia, bradycardia. Hypotension was defined as the presence of at least one of the following within 40 min of medication administration: a decrease in systolic blood pressure (SBP) of more than 30% from baseline, a decrease in mean arterial pressure (MAP) of more than 20% from baseline, absolute SBP less than 90 mmHg, or MAP less than 65 mmHg. Bradycardia was defined as a heart rate of less than 50 beats/min for at least 1 min. In both groups, baseline vital signs were recorded each morning to monitor potential fluctuations.Each day during the study period, patients were evaluated using a structured questionnaire regarding dizziness, nausea, drowsiness, and other adverse events.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation

We carried out a small-size feasibility study and found that additional dexmedetomidine increased the TST by approximately 40 min. Sample size calculation was performed using PASS 15.0 (PASS, USA), with a two-sided CI of 95% (α = 0.05) and a power of 90% (β = 0.1), and the ratio between the DEX and control groups was set as 1:1. Considering a dropout rate of 15%, we estimated that each group would require 35 patients, resulting in a total sample size of 70 patients for both groups.

Data analysis

The categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages, and quantitative data are expressed as means ± SDs or medians and interquartile ranges as appropriate.Continuous repeated-measures variables (TST, DST, LST, REMT, NA, and VAS scores) were analyzed using a linear mixed-effect model (LMM) with group, time, and group-by-time interaction as fixed effects and patient indicator as a random effect. T0 was included as covariates to adjust for potential baseline differences. The model estimated adjusted least-squares means, between-group mean differences, and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical inference followed these steps: (1) the significance of the group-by-time interaction was first tested; (2) if significant (P < 0.05), post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed at each time point using Bonferroni correction (adjusted P values capped at 1.0); and (3) if not significant, the main group effect was reported to assess the overall treatment effect.

For ISI, either Student’s t test or the Mann–Whitney U test was performed as appropriate, and the median difference with 95% CI was calculated. Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test when expected frequencies were < 5. A two-tailed P value less than 0.05 was considered to be of statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 25.0.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between September 2024 and October 2025, 103 patients were screened. Of these, 18 patients did not meet the inclusion criteria: eight had diabetes, three had central nervous system disorders, two had pacemaker implantation, three had a history of psychotropic drug use, and two were unable to complete the questionnaire. Among the remaining 85 eligible patients, 15 declined to participate. Ultimately, 70 participants were included in the final analysis on an intention-to-treat basis (Fig. 2). The baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups. The TST, DST and other parameters were similar between the two groups (Table 1).

Primary outcome

The group-by-time interaction was not statistically significant (F (2,140) = 2.36, P = 0.099). This finding indicates that changes in TST over time did not differ significantly between the two groups. Therefore, the treatment effect was assessed based on the main effect of the group.The main effect of the group was statistically significant (F (1,70) = 9.03, P = 0.004), with baseline T0 included as a covariate in the model. After adjustment for baseline differences, the estimated mean TST was significantly higher in the DEX group than in the control group. Furthermore, the main effect of time was significant (F (1,70) = 18.28, P < 0.001), indicating that TST increased significantly after treatment compared with baseline in both groups. These results suggest that while conventional therapy alone improved sleep among patients with herpes zoster–related neuralgia (HZN), the addition of dexmedetomidine conferred an additional benefit (Fig. 3A, Table 2).

Secondary outcomes

The group-by-time interaction for DST was statistically significant (F (2,140) = 4.81, P = 0.010). Post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction revealed no significant difference in DST between the two groups at T1 (least squares (LS) mean difference = − 8.86 [95% CI, − 23.53 to 5.82]; P = 0.236). At T2, the DST in the DEX group was significantly higher than in the control group (LS mean difference = 15.34[95% CI, 0.67 to 30.02]; P = 0.041). The between-group difference further increased at T3 (LS mean difference = 20.26[95% CI, 5.58 to 34.93]; P = 0.007). These findings suggest that dexmedetomidine-induced improvements in deep sleep were time dependent, with progressively greater effects observed as treatment continued (Fig. 3B, Table 2).The group-by-time interaction for LST was not statistically significant (F (2,140) = 1.19, P = 0.307). Likewise, the main effect of group was not statistically significant (F (1,70) = 3.90, P = 0.052). These findings indicate that LST did not differ significantly between the two groups. A significant main effect of time on LST was observed (F (1,70) = 3.57, P = 0.031). These results suggest that changes in light sleep time were comparable between groups, and that improvements in sleep architecture were mainly characterized by prolonged deep sleep and fewer awakenings (Fig. 3C, Table 2).The group-by-time interaction for REMT was not statistically significant (F (2,140) = 0.32, P = 0.725). The main effects of time (F (1,70) = 2.24, P = 0.111) and group (F (1,70) = 1.55, P = 0.218) were also not statistically significant. These findings indicate that dexmedetomidine improves sleep without disrupting the normal REM sleep architecture and may avoid the adverse cognitive and emotional effects commonly associated with traditional sedative-hypnotic agents (Fig. 3D, Table 2). A significant group-by-time interaction was observed for NA (F (2,140) = 4.11, P = 0.018). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction showed that NA in the DEX group was significantly lower than that in the control group at all time points (T1: LS mean difference = − 0.55 [95% CI: −0.86 to − 0.24]; P < 0.001; T2: LS mean difference = − 0.75 [95% CI: −1.06 to − 0.44]; P < 0.001; T3: LS mean difference = − 1.06 [95% CI: −1.38 to − 0.75]; P < 0.001). These findings indicate that dexmedetomidine effectively enhances sleep continuity by reducing nocturnal awakenings (Fig. 3E, Table 2).

The group-by-time interaction for VAS score was not statistically significant (F (2,140) = 0.75, P = 0.473). Both the main effects of time (F (1,70) = 93.60, P < 0.001) and group (F (1,70) = 10.90, P = 0.002) were significant. VAS scores decreased significantly over time in both groups, with a greater reduction in the DEX group. These results suggest that dexmedetomidine provides synergistic analgesic effects alongside its sleep-promoting properties (Fig. 4A). No significant difference was observed in ISI scores at T0 between groups (mean difference = 0.71 [95% CI: − 0.14 to 1.57]; P = 0.098). Post-intervention (T3), the ISI score was significantly lower in the DEX group compared to the control group (mean difference = 1.86 [95% CI: 0.44–3.28]; P = 0.011). These findings suggest that dexmedetomidine effectively alleviates subjective insomnia severity compared to the control group (Fig. 4B).

Adverse events

There was no significant difference in the incidence of adverse events between the two groups during the study (P > 0.05, Table 3). In the control group, 3 patients (8.6%) experienced dizziness, 3(8.6%) nausea, 2 (5.7%) drowsiness, and 2 (5.7%) hypotension. In the DEX group, 5 patients (14.3%) reported dizziness, 2 (5.7%) nausea, 4 (11.4%) drowsiness, and 1 (2.9%) hypotension. All adverse events were mild and resolved after symptomatic treatment (e.g., phenylephrine for hypotension). No serious adverse events were reported. No significant difference was found between groups regarding rescue analgesia use (P = 0.259, Table 3).

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial is the first to show that adding dexmedetomidine nasal spray to conventional treatment in hospitalized patients with HZN significantly increases TST, reduces NA, and prolongs DST. In addition, VAS-assessed pain was significantly alleviated and ISI-measured insomnia severity significantly improved, with no increased adverse events. These findings indicate that dexmedetomidine nasal spray may serve as a safe and effective adjuvant therapy for patients with HZN.

This study showed that the TST in the DEX group was significantly longer than that in the control group, and the NA was reduced at all time points, consistent with the findings of Jinghan Wu et al. [16]. These results support our hypothesis that dexmedetomidine promotes and sustains sleep in patients with HZN while maintaining a favorable safety profile. Notably, the dexmedetomidine-induced increase in DST showed a delayed and progressive pattern. Although no significant difference was observed between groups at T1, DST in the DEX group was significantly higher than in the control group at later time points T2 and T3. The study also demonstrated that dexmedetomidine did not significantly impact REMT despite improving overall sleep quality. These findings are consistent with prior reports in perioperative populations [17].

This distinctive pattern of sleep regulation, which reflects remodeling rather than simple inhibition, may be explained by the unique pharmacological properties of dexmedetomidine. As a highly selective α2-adrenergic receptor agonist, dexmedetomidine reduces norepinephrine release by activating α2-receptors in the locus coeruleus, thereby engaging endogenous sleep-promoting pathways [18] and inducing a calming state resembling natural non–rapid eye movement sleep. The characteristics of this biomimetic sleep may enable dexmedetomidine to more effectively restore pain-disrupted deep-sleep architecture during continuous administration, rather than suppress arousal in a strong but non-physiological manner, as occurs with traditional sedatives [19]. This mechanism may explain why the improvement in DST becomes more pronounced over time. In contrast, traditional sedatives such as benzodiazepines markedly suppress REM sleep, disrupt normal sleep architecture, and frequently lead to rebound phenomena after discontinuation [20]. Stable REM sleep is essential for preserving cognitive function and emotional regulation. Our findings indicate that dexmedetomidine preserves normal REM sleep architecture while concurrently improving overall sleep duration and quality [21].

This study also demonstrated a significant reduction in VAS pain scores in the DEX group, consistent with findings from a previous randomized controlled trial evaluating intranasal dexmedetomidine for pain management in older adults [22]. Existing literature indicates that the effective dose range of intranasal dexmedetomidine in adults is 0.5~1.5 µg/kg. In elderly patients receiving palliative care, dexmedetomidine administered at 1.25 µg/kg via a nasal mucosal atomization device provided analgesia that was non-inferior to subcutaneous opioids. It demonstrated a favorable safety profile, with no observed adverse events such as respiratory depression or severe bradycardia. The observed analgesic effect may be attributed to two potential mechanisms. First, dexmedetomidine exhibits intrinsic analgesic effects by inhibiting nociceptive transmission through activation of α2-adrenergic receptors in the spinal cord [23]. Second, by significantly improving sleep quality and restoring the endogenous pain modulation function of the central nervous system, dexmedetomidine indirectly enhances patients’ ability to tolerate and manage pain [24]. In this study, although the use of rescue medication in the DEX group did not differ significantly from the use in the control group, a clear downward trend was observed, which may be related to the limited sample size. A retrospective study by Seppänen et al. reported that intranasal dexmedetomidine significantly reduced cumulative opioid consumption during total knee arthroplasty [25]. Future studies with larger sample sizes are required to further validate the analgesic efficacy of dexmedetomidine in patients with HZN.

Oral pharmacotherapy remains a key component of current HZN management. Its primary advantages include ease of administration and the ability to act on multiple targets within the sensory nervous system [26]. However, the efficacy of current pharmacotherapies is often suboptimal, and their clinical benefits are limited by dose-dependent adverse effects, including drowsiness and dizziness [27]. Currently, exploring non-traditional therapeutic targets—such as sleep modulation—has become an important focus in pain management research [28]. The primary outcome was TST, which was measured using a smart wearable device throughout the treatment period. The rationale for selecting TST as the primary outcome was as follows: (1) TST is a clinically meaningful indicator in patients with sleep disturbances, directly reflecting changes in sleep duration [29]. (2) Objective TST data obtained through wearable monitoring reduce the subjective bias inherent in self-reported scales and provide more reliable quantitative evidence for evaluating treatment efficacy. (3) As a composite sleep metric, improvements in TST reliably indicate enhanced overall sleep quality, including both sleep initiation and maintenance [30]. (4) Furthermore, as a continuous variable, TST provides greater statistical efficiency in linear mixed-effects models, enabling more sensitive detection of treatment effects within the predefined sample size. In addition, this study employed the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), a validated self-report instrument, to assess both nocturnal insomnia symptoms (difficulty initiating sleep, difficulty maintaining sleep, and early morning awakening) and daytime consequences (sleep satisfaction, interference with daytime functioning, perceived impairment, and distress related to sleep disturbances). The ISI demonstrates high sensitivity to clinically significant changes following insomnia treatment and serves as a quantitative measure of therapeutic efficacy [31]. By integrating objective sleep duration measurements with self-reported assessments, this study provides a comprehensive evaluation of sleep improvement in patients.

A key innovation of this study is the use of smart wearable devices to objectively and continuously monitor sleep patterns. Compared with traditional subjective questionnaires or polysomnography, this approach minimizes recall bias, reduces first-night–effect interference, and allows the collection of more natural and continuous sleep data in the patient’s familiar environment [32]. The smart wearable device used in this study performs sleep staging using accelerometer data and heart rate variability, integrated through a proprietary algorithm. Multiple studies have demonstrated its effectiveness in sleep monitoring. In a clinical study, participants underwent polysomnography (PSG) in a sleep laboratory while simultaneously wearing a smartwatch (Huawei WATCH GT2). The performance of the smartwatch was evaluated using per-cycle consistency analysis and Bland–Altman analysis. The findings indicated that the device achieved high sensitivity (95.9%) and accuracy (85.3%) in distinguishing sleep and wakefulness, and showed good agreement with PSG in total sleep time and sleep architecture. These results suggest that smart wearable devices may serve as an alternative for continuous sleep monitoring. With the development of more sophisticated algorithms, these devices are expected to provide significant benefits for sleep health [33]. These devices are cost-effective, easy to operate, noninvasive (without the electrode attachments required for polysomnography), and well tolerated, making them particularly suitable for older or less mobile patients with HZN. Our results demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of using such devices to monitor sleep outcomes in clinical pain research. Smart wearable devices can serve as routine monitoring tools for studies of pain-related sleep disorders. They address key limitations of traditional PSG, including operational complexity and poor patient tolerance, and provide a feasible approach for future large-scale multicenter studies.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center trial with a relatively small sample size. As a result, the generalizability of the findings requires validation in larger, multicenter studies. Second, the intervention and observation period was relatively short. A 3-day intervention and observation period was selected based on the typical hospital stay of 5~7 days and the clinical role of dexmedetomidine nasal spray as a short-term sedative and hypnotic agent. However, because HZN may persist for weeks or months, the short observation period limits the assessment of sustained sleep improvement, long-term analgesic effects, and delayed adverse events. Future studies with longer follow-up are needed to more comprehensively evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of this intervention. Third, because the dexmedetomidine nasal spray has only recently become commercially available, a matching placebo spray with an identical delivery device could not be obtained, preventing the implementation of a double-blind, placebo-controlled design. To address this limitation, several methodological strategies were implemented to reduce potential bias. The intervention was administered by dedicated research personnel who were not involved in data collection or outcome assessment. This ensured a clear separation between intervention delivery and outcome evaluation. Outcome assessors and statisticians were blinded to group allocation throughout the study. Furthermore, sleep parameters were objectively monitored using smart wearable devices. This approach is not influenced by participant or investigator expectations, thereby substantially reducing assessment bias. Collectively, these design features strengthen the validity of the findings despite the absence of a placebo control.

In summary, this randomized controlled trial demonstrates that adding dexmedetomidine nasal spray to conventional pain management significantly increases total sleep time and deep sleep duration, reduces nocturnal awakenings, and synergistically alleviates pain and insomnia severity in HZN patients, without increasing the risk of adverse events. Future studies should evaluate its long-term efficacy and explore potential applications across diverse patient populations.
