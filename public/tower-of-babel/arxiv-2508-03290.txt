Berry Curvature of Low-Energy Excitons in Rhombohedral Graphene
Henry Davenport, Frank Schindler, Johannes Knolle

Abstract
We investigate low energy excitons in rhombohedral pentalayer graphene encapsulated by hexagonal boron nitride (hBN/R5G/hBN), focusing on the regime at the experimental twist angle θ = 0.77 ∘ \theta=0.77^{\circ} and with an applied electric field. We introduce a new low-energy two-band model of rhombohedral graphene that captures the band structure more accurately than previous models while keeping the number of parameters low. Using this model, we show that the centres of the exciton Wannier functions are displaced from the moiré unit cell origin by a quantised amount – they are instead localised at C 3 C_{3} -symmetric points on the boundary. We also find that the exciton shift is electrically tunable: by varying the electric field strength, the exciton Wannier centre can be exchanged between inequivalent corners of the moiré unit cell. Our results suggest the possibility of detecting excitonic corner or edge modes, as well as novel excitonic crystal defect responses in hBN/R5G/hBN. Lastly, we find that the excitons in hBN/R5G/hBN inherit excitonic Berry curvature from the underlying electronic bands, enriching their semiclassical transport properties. Our results position rhombohedral graphene as a compelling tunable platform for probing exciton topology in moiré materials.



I
Introduction

The study of Berry curvature effects has played a central role in advancing our understanding of non-interacting electrons in condensed matter systems
Xiao et al. 2010
. More recently, there has been growing interest in extending this framework to interacting systems. Efforts in this direction include the topological classification of ground states in the presence of interactions
Soldini et al. 2023
;
Shiozaki et al. 2023
;
Manjunath et al. 2024
;
Herzog-Arbeitman et al. 2024a
, as well as the investigation of Berry curvature effects and the topological classification of interaction-induced excitations, such as excitons
Davenport et al. 2024
;
Kwan et al. 2021
;
Froese et al. 2025
;
Yao and Niu 2008
;
Jankowski et al. 2025
;
Xie et al. 2024
.

Rhombohedral graphene has emerged as a compelling platform for studying non-trivial ground-state topology induced by interactions. Experiments have found that rhombohedral graphene exhibits both the integer and fractional quantum Hall effects
Lu et al. 2023
and a comprehensive theoretical understanding of these phenomena remains an area of active research
Herzog-Arbeitman et al. 2024b
;
Dong et al. 2024a
;
Soejima et al. 2024
;
Bernevig and Kwan 2025
;
Yu et al. 2024
;
Dong et al. 2024b
. In this work, we take an alternative approach and focus on the
excitations
rather than the ground state, specifically the excitons at charge neutrality. We study rhombohedral pentalayer graphene (R5G) encapsulated by hexagonal boron nitride (hBN), shown in Fig.
1
a. When an electric field is applied we can obtain two well-gapped electronic bands around the Fermi level at charge neutrality
Herzog-Arbeitman et al. 2024b
. Since the bands immediately above and below the Fermi energy are layer polarised, the twist angle of the hBN either side of the R5G strongly influences the topology and dispersion of these electronic bands. The electronic bands are therefore highly tunable making hBN/R5G/hBN an ideal testbed for studying the topology of low energy excitons emerging from topological electron bands.

In this paper we show that, at the experimentally relevant twist angle
\theta=0.77^{\circ}

Lu et al. 2023
, the exciton Wannier states are shifted to the edge of the moiré unit cell and their Wannier centre depends on the electronic potential applied across the rhombohedral graphene layers. They are therefore analogous to obstructed atomic insulators in non-interacting electron systems
Bradlyn et al. 2017
. We note however that this shift is inherited from the electronic bands and so is not like the shift excitons described in Ref.
Davenport et al. 2024
where the excitons have maximally localised exciton Wannier centres
Haber et al. 2023
;
Davenport et al. 2025
shifted with respect to the electronic Wannier centres. Despite this, we expect that rhombohedral graphene still exhibits novel ‘excitonic’ defect and edge responses. We also find that the Berry curvature of the excitons can be tuned using the applied electric field, allowing for an experimental exploration of the role of Berry curvature on excitonic properties. Given these attributes, rhombohedral graphene presents a unique system to explore the effects of exciton Berry curvature on exciton transport properties, both experimentally and theoretically.

This paper is structured as follows. We begin by outlining the non-interacting physics underlying the band dispersion and Berry curvature distribution. In Sec.
II
we introduce a new simple low-energy 2 band model of rhombohedral graphene that better captures the dispersion shape around the Dirac point compared to the well-studied
k^{5}
model
Dong et al. 2024b
. In addition, we show how the moiré potential modifies the dispersion and band topology. In Sec.
III
we detail our methodology for calculating the exciton dispersion and exciton Berry curvature. Our findings indicate that the applied electric field can be used to tune the lowest-energy excitons to regimes where the exciton Berry curvature is enhanced. We predict that this could lead to a measurable thermal Hall effect
Zhang et al. 2024
. Additionally, we identify regimes where the exciton Wannier centres are obstructed from the centre of the moiré unit cell, with the Wyckoff position they are centred at tunable using the applied electric field. In Sec.
IV

;
Susarla et al. 2022
.

II
Band structure

We first derive an economical model that captures the band structure of R5G as well as that of hBN/R5G/hBN with a moiré twist.

Excitons

Excitons will thermally occupy the exciton band leading to an enhanced population at the band minimum. Therefore, whilst the overall exciton band is topologically trivial, the locally enhanced Berry curvature in the vicinity of the band minimum (at
u_{D}=20\>\mathrm{meV}
) will influence exciton transport behaviour. The exciton Berry curvature, integrated over circular regions of increasing radius centred at

u_{D}=-20\>\mathrm{meV}
will not have such oscillatory exciton Berry curvature effects. Hence, rhombohedral graphene provides a platform in which it is possible to electrically tune between regimes with and without
excitonic
Berry curvature effects.

IV
Discussion

The response and transport properties of electrons in non-interacting bands are well known to be influenced by the electronic Berry curvature
Xiao et al. 2010
. Fully elucidating the transport effects of exciton Berry curvature represents a new frontier in the study of quantum transport phenomena. Our work implies that rhombohedral graphene is a possibly important material for exploring the interplay of excitons and topology. For
u_{D}=-20\>\mathrm{meV}
, the Berry curvature of the single electron dispersion of rhombohedral graphene is concentrated at the edge of the mBZ, exactly where the low energy excitons are formed, meaning that the lowest energy excitons in rhombohedral graphene inherit the Berry curvature of the electronic bands. The transport properties of excitons are likely to be influenced by this exciton Berry curvature. One possible measurable consequence of the enhanced Berry curvature at a displacement field of
u_{D}=20\>\mathrm{meV}
is a thermal Hall effect
Zhang et al. 2024
. Applying an in-plane temperature gradient to the sample would generate a transverse exciton current,
i.e.
most clearly visible as a chiral exciton current at the edge of the system. Typically such exciton currents are hard to measure because excitons are neutral, however, as we have seen, the conduction band and valence band electronic wave functions are layer polarised. Hence, the exciton flow could be detected by measuring the electron or hole current on either the top or bottom layer of the sample similar to a drag current
Nguyen et al.
;
Qi et al. 2025
.

We have also predicted that the maximally localised exciton Wannier states are shifted away from the centre of the unit cell, directly analogous to the concept of an obstructed atomic insulator in non-interacting electron systems
Bradlyn et al. 2017
. Obstructed atomic insulators are known to possess crystal defect responses that depend on the Wannier state shift, as well as corner or edge polarization
Schindler et al. 2022b
. Hence we expect that the exciton Wannier state shift in rhombohedral graphene could analogously lead to new excitonic crystal defect responses and exciton corner or edge modes
Davenport et al. 2024
. The current spatial resolution of exciton probes would not allow it to be possible to detect these effects in bare graphene with a unit cell of order
2.46\>\text{\r{A}}
, but the enhanced moiré unit cell size increases the shift to within the resolution range of techniques like EELs, which can achieve spatial resolutions on the order of
\sim\mathrm{nm}

Nerl et al. 2017
;
Susarla et al. 2022
. Moiré systems such as hBN/R5G/hBN therefore offer a promising platform for experimentally probing exciton crystalline topology. However, understanding the full range of experimental signatures, beyond edge states
Davenport et al. 2024
, remains a compelling and open direction for future research.

Appendix A
Pentalayer rhombohedral graphene

We derive the low energy model for pentalayer rhombohedral graphene (R5G) introduced in the main text. The model is derived from the full 10 band model used by Ref.

Full pentalayer model

We describe the full model of R5G, see Ref.

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(a,0\right)

(10)

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{2}

\displaystyle=a\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right).

\displaystyle\bm{G}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{a}\left(1,-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)

\displaystyle\bm{G}_{2}

The high symmetry points of graphene are,

\displaystyle\bm{K}^{\prime}_{Gr}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\bm{G}_{1}+\frac{2}{3}\bm{G}_{2}.

(15)

h_{R5G}(\bm{k})=\begin{bmatrix}h_{0}^{(0)}&h^{(1)}&h^{(2)}&0&0\\

\displaystyle h^{(0)}_{l}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}u_{l}&-t_{0}f_{\bm{k}}\\
-t_{0}\bar{f}_{\bm{k}}&u_{l}\end{pmatrix},

(17)

\displaystyle h^{(1)}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}t_{4}&t_{3}\bar{f}_{\bm{k}}\\
t_{1}\bar{f}_{\bm{k}}&t_{4}f_{\bm{k}}\end{pmatrix},

(18)

\displaystyle h^{(2)}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{t_{2}}{2}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(19)

We define,

f_{\bm{k}}=\sum_{i=0}^{2}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}.\bm{\delta}_{i}},

(20)

with
\bm{\delta}_{n}
defined as the vectors between nearest neighbour carbon atoms in each layer
Dong et al. 2024a

t_{0}=3.1\>\mathrm{eV}

t_{1}=380\>\mathrm{meV}
,
t_{2}=-21\>\mathrm{meV}

;

A.2
Low energy model

We now derive the low energy model of pentalayer rhombohedral graphene. For finite
u_{D}
there are two bands immediately above and below the Fermi energy at charge neutrality. These two bands are layer polarised around the
\bm{K}_{Gr}
point. The band which is layer polarised to layer
1
has most of the wave function weight on the
A
sublattice whereas the layer
5
polarised band is on the
B
sublattice. The low energy physics, around
\bm{K}_{Gr}
, can therefore be well described by a model solely in the subspace
1A
,
5B
(where we label the positions in the unit cell by the layer
1,5
and the sublattice
A,B
). We rewrite the equation for the energy dispersion
\epsilon(\bm{k})
as,

\begin{pmatrix}h(\bm{k})_{11}&h(\bm{k})_{12}\\
h(\bm{k})_{21}&h(\bm{k})_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{c}_{1}(\bm{k})\\
\bm{c}_{2}(\bm{k})\end{pmatrix}=\epsilon(\bm{k})\begin{pmatrix}\bm{c}_{1}(\bm{k})\\
\bm{c}_{2}(\bm{k})\end{pmatrix},

(22)

where the matrix
h(\bm{k})_{11}
represents the block of the full matrix
h(\bm{k})
in the subspace
1A,5B
,
h(\bm{k})_{22}
is the high energy subspace (
i.e.

2B,\dots 5A
), and
h(\bm{k})_{12}
and
h(\bm{k})_{21}
represents the coupling between the subspaces. The components of the Bloch function in each subspace are given by the functions
\bm{c}_{1}(\bm{k})
and
\bm{c}_{2}(\bm{k})
. We wish to find a
2\times 2
effective Hamiltonian in the low energy subspace
Zhang et al. 2010
. To do this we expand Eq. (
22
) to give two relations,

\displaystyle\bm{c}_{1}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=(\epsilon-h(\bm{k})_{11})^{-1}h(\bm{k})_{12}\bm{c}_{2}(\bm{k})

(23)

\displaystyle\bm{c}_{2}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=(\epsilon-h(\bm{k})_{22})^{-1}h(\bm{k})_{21}\bm{c}_{1}(\bm{k}).

(24)

Combining these we obtain an equation for the wave function components in the low energy subspace
\bm{c}_{1}(\bm{k})
,

[h_{11}(\bm{k})+h_{12}(\bm{k})(h_{22}(\bm{k})-\epsilon)^{-1}h_{21}(\bm{k})-\epsilon]\bm{c}_{1}(\bm{k})=0.

(25)

We expand to first order in energy
\epsilon
and obtain the effective Hamiltonian,
Zhang et al. 2010

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{k})=

\displaystyle\big\{1-h(\bm{k})_{12}\big[h(\bm{k})_{22}\big]^{-2}h(\bm{k})_{21}\big\}^{-1}

(26)

\displaystyle\big\{h(\bm{k})_{11}+h(\bm{k})_{12}\big[h(\bm{k})_{22}\big]^{-1}h(\bm{k})_{21}\big\}.

We consider small momenta around the
\bm{K}_{Gr}
valley of the R5G, and so we Taylor expand
h_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{k})
in powers of
\bm{k}
about
\bm{K}_{Gr}
. For pentalayer rhombohedral graphene, the dominant off-diagonal terms in
h_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{k})
go as
\sim|\bm{k}|^{5}
(as described in Ref.
Dong et al. 2024b
). When we expand the diagonal elements to zeroth order in
\bm{k}
we also obtain a contribution from the applied displacement field
u_{D}
. We introduce a further correction beyond the model described in Ref.
Dong et al. 2024b
; the full model has a characteristic quadratic curvature which dominates at small momenta
\bm{k}
from the Dirac point
\bm{K}_{Gr}
. We find that this effect can be captured in the low energy model by expanding the diagonal components to second order in momentum
\bm{k}
. This gives an effective hamiltonian in the low energy subspace which is,

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{eff}}(k_{x},k_{y})=

(27)

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}2u_{D}+\gamma_{-}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})&\frac{v_{F}^{5}}{t_{0}^{4}}\left(k_{x}-ik_{y}\right)^{5}\\
\frac{v_{F}^{5}}{t_{0}^{4}}\left(k_{x}+ik_{y}\right)^{5}&-2u_{D}+\gamma_{+}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})\\
\end{array}\right).

The
\gamma_{0/1}
take a simple form if we assume small
u_{D}
(specifically
u_{D}<v_{3}/a\ll v_{F}/a
) and expand to first order in
u_{D}
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\pm}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2v_{F}v_{4}}{t_{1}}\pm\frac{u_{D}v_{F}^{2}}{t_{1}^{2}}.

(28)

This simplified model predicts a sign change in the curvature of the higher-energy band at the
\bm{K}_{Gr}
point at a displacement field of
u_{D}=\frac{2v_{4}t_{1}}{v_{F}}\sim 35\>\mathrm{meV}
, while the band dispersion curvature at the
\bm{K}_{Gr}
point of the lower-energy band remains unchanged for all
u_{D}\geq 0
. This behaviour in good agreement with results from the full model. Note that this curvature sign change is not captured in the simple model described in Ref.
Dong et al. 2024b
so this model more accurately describes the low energy physics of R5G. A comparison between the dispersions of the full and simplified models for a representative parameter set is shown in the main text. As expected, the two models agree well at momenta close to
\bm{K}_{Gr}
, with increasing deviation at larger momenta. Notably, the full model lacks inversion symmetry about the
\bm{K}_{Gr}
point—i.e., it is not symmetric under
(k_{x},k_{y})\rightarrow(-k_{x},-k_{y})
—unlike the low-energy approximation. This asymmetry arises from the inclusion of intra-layer hopping
t_{3}
. When
t_{3}
is neglected, the system possesses a unitary symmetry
U=\bigoplus_{l=0}^{N_{l}-1}\,(-1)^{l}\sigma_{z}
, obeying
Uh(\bm{k})U^{\dagger}=h(-\bm{k})
. This makes the spectrum symmetric about about the
\bm{K}_{Gr}
. Since the low-energy
2\times 2
model does not include
t_{3}
, it is symmetric about the
\bm{K}_{Gr}
point. In addition to capturing the low energy dispersion, the low energy model derived above also captures the Berry curvature distribution of the full model (as the model without the quadratic correction does too
Dong et al. 2024b
).

Appendix B
Moiré Hamiltonian

We study R5G between hBN layers
i.e.
hBN/R5G/hBN stacks. We use the same model as Ref.
Dong et al. 2024a
. The lattice mismatch between the hBN and the R5G leads to a moiré pattern. There are two sources of the lattice mismatch: firstly there is a lattice constant mismatch between hBN and graphene,
\epsilon=a_{\mathrm{hBN}}/{a_{Gr}}=0.018
. Secondly we can rotate the hBN by some angle
\theta
to change the moiré periodicity. We define a rotation matrix
R_{\theta}
to describe rotation of the hBN graphene by angle
\theta
with respect to the R5G. Similarly the matrix
M=(1+\epsilon)I
can be used to calculate the effects of the lattice constant mismatch. The moiré reciprocal lattice vectors are,

\bm{g}_{j}=[I-M^{-1}R_{\theta}]\bm{G}_{j},

(29)

for
j\in\{1,2\}

Dong et al. 2024a
;
Moon and Koshino 2014
. In addition we define,

\bm{g}_{3}=\bm{g}_{1}+\bm{g}_{2}.

(30)

The high symmetry points in the new moiré Brillouin zone are,

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\bm{K}_{\mathrm{Gr}}

(31)

\displaystyle\bm{\kappa}^{+}

\displaystyle=\bm{\gamma}-\frac{1}{3}\bm{g}_{1}+\frac{1}{3}\bm{g}_{2}

(32)

\displaystyle\bm{\kappa}^{-}

\displaystyle=\bm{\gamma}-\frac{2}{3}\bm{g}_{1}-\frac{1}{3}\bm{g}_{2}

(33)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}

\displaystyle=\bm{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{g}_{1}.

(34)

We model the effect of the hBN layers as an effective potential acting on the outermost layers (
l=1,5
) of the R5G.
This moiré potential can be written as,

H_{V}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{r}\\
l\in\{\mathrm{top},\mathrm{btm}\}\end{subarray}}V_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l}(\bm{r})c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},l}c_{\bm{r},l},

(35)

\bm{r}
goes over all unit cells and
c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},l}
creates an electron at position
\bm{r}
and layer
l
.
Since our effective Hamiltonian retains only the
A
sublattice in the bottom layer and the
B
sublattice in the top layer, we apply the moiré potential exclusively to these sites. The moiré potential has the moiré periodicity so we can write the potential as,

V^{l}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{r})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in\{1,-1\}\\
j\in\{1,2,3\}\end{subarray}}V_{l,\eta\bm{g}_{j}}\exp\left[\mathrm{i}\eta\bm{g}_{j}\cdot\bm{r}\right]

(36)

Here, the sum runs over the first shell of moiré reciprocal lattice vectors around the first moiré BZ. This is known as the “first harmonic” approximation. We use values for the prefactors
V_{l,\eta\bm{g}_{j}}
introduced in Ref.
Moon and Koshino 2014
such that the potential can be expressed as,

V^{l}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{r})=V_{0}+V_{1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in\{1,-1\}\\
j\in\{1,2,3\}\end{subarray}}\exp\left[\mathrm{i}\eta\left(\bm{g}_{j}\cdot\bm{r}+\psi_{\xi}+\phi_{l}\right)\right].

(37)

There is a phase shift
\psi_{\xi}
which depends on the the stacking configuration, for example, the
A
sublattice of graphene may lie above either a boron atom (
\xi=0
) or a nitrogen atom (
\xi=1
), as described in Ref.
Moon and Koshino 2014
and this leads to differing phases for the effective potential. We use the values in Refs.
Moon and Koshino 2014
and restrict our study to the
\xi=0
stacking for both the top and bottom layers, for which
\psi_{\xi}=223.5^{\circ}
. The additional phase factor
\phi_{l}
is 0 for the bottom layer but for
N_{l}
layers of graphene, the top layer gains a phase
\phi_{l}=\frac{2\pi N_{l}}{3}
. This phase accounts for the relative shift in the moiré pattern that arises because, in a given unit cell, the
B
sublattice site in the top layer is not directly aligned above the
A
sublattice sites in the bottom layer
Herzog-Arbeitman et al. 2024b
. Note that the moiré coupling does not couple the top and bottom layers. We use
V_{0}=28.9\>\mathrm{meV}
and
V_{1}=21.0\>\mathrm{meV}
following Refs.
Moon and Koshino 2014
;
Dong et al. 2024a
.

We rewrite the Hamiltonian in momentum space using,

c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},l}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},l}.

(38)

where the sum over
\bm{k}
goes over all momenta in the R5G BZ and the system consists of
L^{2}
unit cells. This gives,

H_{V}=\sum_{l,\bm{r},\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in\{1,-1\}\\
j\in\{1,2,3\}\end{subarray}}e^{i(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{g}_{j})\cdot\bm{r}}V_{l,\eta\bm{g}_{j}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},l}c_{\bm{k}^{\prime},l}.

(39)

Performing the summation over
\bm{r}
gives,

H_{V}=\sum_{l,\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in\{1,-1\}\\
j\in\{1,2,3\}\end{subarray}}\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}-\eta\bm{g}_{j}}V_{l,\eta\bm{g}_{j}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},l}c_{\bm{k}^{\prime},l}.

(40)

We now express all momenta in terms of the momentum in the mBZ (
\bm{k}
) and a moiré reciprocal lattice vector (
\bm{Q}
)
i.e.

c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},\bm{Q},l}\equiv c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}+\bm{Q},l}
we obtain,

H_{V}=\sum_{l}\sum_{\bm{k}}\sum_{\bm{Q},\bm{Q}^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in\{1,-1\}\\
j\in\{1,2,3\}\end{subarray}}(\delta_{\bm{Q},\bm{Q}^{\prime}-\eta\bm{g}_{j}}V_{l,\eta\bm{g}_{j}})c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},\bm{Q},l}c_{\bm{k},\bm{Q}^{\prime},l}.

(41)

The sum over
\bm{Q},\bm{Q}^{\prime}
is over
all
moiré reciprocal lattice vectors,
i.e.
not restricted to the first ring about the mBZ. We obtain the full moire Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle H_{M}=

\displaystyle H_{0}+H_{V}=

(42)

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathrm{mBZ}}\sum_{\bm{Q},\bm{Q}^{\prime}}\sum_{l,l^{\prime}}h^{(m)}_{(\bm{Q},l),(\bm{Q}^{\prime},l^{\prime})}(\bm{k})c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},\bm{Q},l}c_{\bm{k},\bm{Q}^{\prime},l^{\prime}},

where,

\displaystyle h^{(m)}_{(\bm{Q},l),(\bm{Q}^{\prime},l^{\prime})}(\bm{k})

(43)

\displaystyle=\left\{[h_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{k}+\bm{Q})]_{l,l^{\prime}}+V_{0}\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}\right\}\delta_{\bm{Q},\bm{Q}^{\prime}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in\{1,-1\}\\
j\in\{1,2,3\}\end{subarray}}(\delta_{\bm{Q},\bm{Q}^{\prime}-\eta\bm{g}_{j}}V_{1}).

Here the first term
h_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{k})
represents the
low energy
R5G without the moiré coupling. The moiré system retains

symmetry and so there are three high symmetry points

\bm{\kappa_{+}}
and
\bm{\kappa_{-}}

Appendix D
Exciton Hamiltonian

We calculate the exciton spectra and wave functions using the following method. We project the fully many-body Hamiltonian into the variational basis,

\ket{\bm{p},\bm{k}}=c^{\dagger}_{\bm{p}+\bm{k},c}c_{\bm{k},v}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}.

(44)

The total momentum of the above state is
\bm{p}
, the relative momentum is
\bm{k}
.

We project the full Hamiltonian onto the variational basis and calculate its matrix elements,

\bra{\bm{p},\bm{k}}\hat{H}\ket{\bm{p},\bm{k}^{\prime}}.

(45)

We have a contribution from the kinetic Hamiltonian [Eq. (
42
)],

\bra{\bm{p},\bm{k}}\hat{H}_{0}\ket{\bm{p},\bm{k}^{\prime}}=\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(E_{\mathrm{G.S.}}-\epsilon_{\bm{k},v}+\epsilon_{\bm{p}+\bm{k},c}),

(46)

where
\epsilon_{\bm{p},c/v}
are the single particle energies of the kinetic hamiltonian for the conduction/valence bands at momentum
\bm{p}
. The ground state energy is
E_{\mathrm{G.S.}}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\epsilon_{\bm{k},v}
.

We also project the interaction term into this basis. We first derive the general form of the matrix elements before showing the results for the moiré Hamiltonian. We begin with the general form of a translationally-symmetric interaction Hamiltonian,

\hat{H}_{int}=\sum_{\bm{R},\bm{R}^{\prime},i,j}U_{\bm{R}-\bm{R}^{\prime},i,j}\hat{n}_{\bm{R},i}\hat{n}_{\bm{R}^{\prime},j}

(47)

where
\hat{n}_{\bm{R},i}
is the number operator at unit cell
R
and
i
labels the sublattices. Now we write this in the momentum basis. First note that

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{\bm{R}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{R},i}

(48)

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{\bm{R},i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{\bm{R}}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},i}.

(49)

We rewrite the number operators as,

\displaystyle n_{\bm{R},i}

\displaystyle=c^{\dagger}_{\bm{R},i}c_{\bm{R},i}

(50)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{p},\bm{q}}e^{-i(\bm{p}-\bm{q})\cdot\bm{R}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{p},i}c_{\bm{q},i}.

(51)

Equation
47

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{R},\bm{R}^{\prime},i,j}\sum_{\bm{p},\bm{q},\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{q}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{V^{2}}U_{\bm{R}-\bm{R}^{\prime},ij}e^{-i(\bm{p}-\bm{q})\cdot\bm{R}-i(\bm{p}^{\prime}-\bm{q}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{R}^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{p}i}c_{\bm{q}i}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{p}^{\prime}j}c_{\bm{q}^{\prime}j}

(52)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{R},\bm{R}^{\prime},i,j}\sum_{\bm{p},\bm{q},\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{q}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{V^{2}}U_{\bm{R},ij}e^{-i(\bm{p}-\bm{q})R-i(\bm{p}^{\prime}-\bm{q}^{\prime}+\bm{p}^{\prime}-\bm{q}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{R}^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{p}i}c_{\bm{q}i}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{p}^{\prime}j}c_{\bm{q}^{\prime}j},

(53)

where we set
\bm{R}-\bm{R}^{\prime}\rightarrow\bm{R}
. This can be further simplified by performing the sum over
\bm{R}^{\prime}
and
\bm{q}^{\prime}
to give

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{R},i,j}\sum_{\bm{p}\bm{q}\bm{p}^{\prime}}U_{\bm{R},ij}e^{-i(\bm{p}-\bm{q})\cdot\bm{R}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{p},i}c_{\bm{q},i}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{p}^{\prime},j}c_{\bm{p}-\bm{q}+\bm{p}^{\prime},j}.

(54)

Let
U_{\bm{p},ij}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{R}}U_{\bm{R},ij}e^{-i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{R}}
,

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=\sum_{\bm{p}\bm{q}\bm{q}^{\prime}ij}U_{\bm{p},ij}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{p}+\bm{q},i}c_{\bm{q},i}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{q}^{\prime},j}c_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}^{\prime},j}.

(55)

To calculate the matrix elements

\bra{\bm{p},\bm{k}}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\ket{\bm{p},\bm{k}^{\prime}}

(56)

we express the interaction Hamiltonian in the band basis. The Bloch function for band
\alpha
, is
u^{\bm{k},\alpha}_{i}
meaning that,

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}u^{\bm{k},\alpha}_{i}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},i}

(57)

\displaystyle c_{\bm{k},\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\bar{u}^{\bm{k},\alpha}_{i}c_{\bm{k},i}

(58)

where
i
sums over the sub-lattices, and
\alpha\in\{c,v\}
. We use the notation
\bar{u}^{\bm{k},\alpha}_{i}=(u^{\bm{k},\alpha}_{i})^{*}
.
Plugging this relation into equation
55
we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{p}\bm{q}\bm{q}^{\prime}ij}U_{\bm{p},ij}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{p}+\bm{q},i}c_{\bm{q},i}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{q}^{\prime},j}c_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}^{\prime},j}

Appendix F
Exciton Berry curvature

In Ref.
Davenport et al. 2025
we derived two exciton Wilson loops which relate to the projection of the electron and hole position operator into the exciton band. For 1D systems we obtain the two exciton Wilson loops for the conduction (
c
) and valence bands (
v
) respectively,

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{exc},c}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\big[\sum_{k}\bar{\phi}^{p+\Delta}_{k-p}\phi^{p}_{k-p}\braket{u_{k+\Delta,c}|u_{k,c}}]

(95)

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{exc},v}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\big[\sum_{k}\bar{\phi}^{p+\Delta}_{k}\phi^{p}_{k+\Delta}\braket{u_{k+\Delta,v}|u_{k,v}}].

(96)

with
\Delta=2\pi/L
for system size
L
. We note that these can both be written as a product of projectors,

W_{\mathrm{exc},c/v}=\bra{\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{exc},c/v}_{p_{1}}}\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\prod^{2}_{i=R}\ket{\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{exc},c/v}_{p_{i}}}\bra{\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{exc},c/v}_{p_{i}}}\ket{\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{exc},c/v}_{p_{1}}}

(97)

using,

\displaystyle\ket{\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{exc},c}_{p}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\phi^{p}_{k}\ket{k}\otimes\ket{u_{p+k,c}}\otimes\ket{\bar{u}_{k,v}}

(98)

\displaystyle\ket{\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{exc},v}_{p}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\phi^{p}_{k}\ket{p+k}\otimes\ket{u_{p+k,c}}\otimes\ket{\bar{u}_{k,v}}.

(99)

See Ref.
Davenport et al. 2025
for details of the derivations.

Following the method introduced by Ref.
Fukui et al. 2005
we can use these Wilson loop expressions to construct expressions for the flux through loops in the BZ. We consider the exciton wave functions which we have calculated on a grid with spacing
\bm{d}_{\mu}
in direction
\mu
. We can define the link variables,

U_{\mu,c/v}(\bm{k})=\frac{\braket{\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{exc},c/v}(\bm{k})|\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{exc},c/v}(\bm{k}+\bm{d}_{\mu})}}{|\braket{\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{exc},c/v}(\bm{k})|\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{exc},c/v}(\bm{k}+\bm{d}_{\mu})}|}.

(100)

We then calculate the lattice field strength,

F_{c/v}(\bm{k})=-\mathrm{i}\ln\left[U_{1,c/v}(\bm{k}+\bm{d}_{1})U_{2,c/v}(\bm{k}+\bm{d}_{2})U_{1,c/v}(\bm{k})^{-1}U_{2,c/v}(\bm{k})^{-1}\right].

(101)

For small
|\bm{d}_{\mu}|
,
F_{c/v}(\bm{k})
is proportional to the Berry curvature
Fukui et al. 2005
and the Chern number of the band can be calculated as,

C=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\bm{k}}F_{c/v}(\bm{k}).

(102)

This gives us a method to calculate the exciton Berry curvature without having to first find a smooth gauge. We have two expressions for the Berry curvature but for the system studied in this paper they both give similar Berry curvature distributions. In addition, they both integrate to the same value and so give the same Chern number. In the main text we therefore only plot one of the possible Berry curvature plots - that calculated using
\ket{\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{exc},c}_{p}}
.
