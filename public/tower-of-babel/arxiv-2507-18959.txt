Higher-order Stirling cycle and subset triangles: Total positivity, continued fractions and real-rootedness
Bishal Deb and Alan D. Sokal, [5mm] Yau Mathematical Sciences Center, Tsinghua University, Beijing 100084, China, Department of Mathematics, University College London, London WC1E 6BT, UK, Department of Physics, New York University, New York, NY 10003, USA[4mm] bishal@gonitsora.com , sokal@nyu.edu

Abstract
Given a lower-triangular matrix of real numbers, one can ask the following four total-positivity questions: total positivity of the triangle itself; total positivity of its row-reversal; Toeplitz-total positivity of its row sequences (equivalent to negative-real-rootedness of the row-generating polynomials); and coefficientwise Hankel-total positivity of the sequence of row-generating polynomials. In this paper, we introduce two infinite families of lower-triangular matrices generalising the Stirling cycle and subset triangles, parametrised by an integer r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 ; we call these the r r th-order Stirling cycle and subset numbers. We then ask the foregoing four questions for each of these triangles, leading us to several conjectures. We then prove some of these conjectures for the case r = 2 r=2 .

Introduction and statement of main results

A (finite or infinite) matrix of real numbers is called

totally positive
(TP) if all its minors are nonnegative.
A matrix of polynomials with real coefficients is called

coefficientwise totally positive

if all its minors are polynomials with nonnegative coefficients.
Let
A=(a_{n,k})_{n,k\geq 0}
be a lower-triangular matrix
of nonnegative real numbers.
One can then ask the following four questions:

(a)

Is
A
totally positive?

(b)

Is the lower-triangular matrix

A^{\rm rev}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}(a_{n,n-k})_{n,k\geq 0}
,
obtained by reversing the rows of
A
, totally positive?
(Here
a_{n,n-k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}0
when
n<k
.)

(c)

Let

denote
the row-generating polynomial of the
n
-th row of
A
.
Are the polynomials
A_{n}(x)
negative-real-rooted?

.
Since we are here assuming that all the matrix entries

So negative-real-rootedness and real-rootedness are equivalent here.

This is equivalent

In this situation we say that the

1.2
Relation with the second-order Eulerian numbers

The second-order (
r=2
) Stirling cycle and subset numbers
are closely related to the second-order Eulerian numbers,
as we now explain.

Let
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{N}
be a word on a totally ordered alphabet
{\mathbb{A}}
.
A pair
(w_{i},w_{i+1})
with
i\in[N-1]
is called an

ascent
(resp.
descent
,
plateau
)
in case
w_{i}<w_{i+1}
(resp.
w_{i}>w_{i+1}
,
w_{i}=w_{i+1}
).
5
5

5

Note that we do not allow
i=N
as a position for an ascent, descent
or plateau. Some authors do allow this, by fixing a “boundary condition”
for
w_{N+1}
(usually defining it to be either
<
every element of
{\mathbb{A}}

or
>
every element of
{\mathbb{A}}
). It is important, when comparing formulae,
to check carefully which convention the author is using.

The word
w
is said to be a
Stirling word
if,
for every letter
a\in{\mathbb{A}}
,
all letters between two successive occurrences of
a
are larger than
a
.
A permutation of a multiset
M

is said to be a
Stirling permutation

if it is a Stirling word.
The second-order Eulerian number
\left\langle\!\!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\!\!\right\rangle

is then defined to be the number of Stirling permutations
of the multiset
\{1,1,2,2,\ldots,n,n\}
with
k
ascents
(or
k
descents: they are equidistributed,
since reversing the word exchanges ascents with descents).
6
6

6

Here we follow the definition of
Graham–Knuth–Patashnik
[
29
, p. 270]
,
which entails that, when
n\geq 1
,

\left\langle\!\!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\!\!\right\rangle
is nonzero for
0\leq k\leq n-1
.
Many other authors
[
28
,
36
,
32
,
19
]

use the convention that the final index

is always the beginning of a descent
(e.g. by imposing the boundary condition

),
so that, when
n\geq 1
,

\left\langle\!\!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\!\!\right\rangle

.

They satisfy the recurrence

Now define the

1.3
Statement of main conjectures and results

Having defined the Stirling cycle and subset numbers of all orders
r
,
we are now ready to state our conjectures,
which constitute our first principal contribution in this paper.
We will then state which cases are known and which cases
we will prove in the present paper.

Our conjecture for the Stirling cycle numbers of all orders is the following:

Conjecture 1.3

(Conjecture for Stirling cycle numbers of all orders)
.

The following are true:

(a)

The triangle
C^{(r)}
is totally positive for all
r\geq 1
.

(b)

The triangle
\widecheck{C}^{(r)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\left(C^{(r)}\right)^{\rm rev}

formed by reversing the rows of
C^{(r)}

is totally positive for
r=1,2

and is not totally positive when
r\geq 3
.

(c)

The row-generating polynomials

are negative-real-rooted
for
r=1,2

and they have non-real complex zeros for
r\geq 3

and
n\geq 3
.
Also, the row sequences of the matrix
C^{(r)}
are log-concave
for
1\leq r\leq 5
.

(d)

The sequence
(c_{r,n}(x))_{n\geq 0}

is coefficientwise Hankel-totally positive in the variable
x

for all
r\geq 1
.

As mentioned earlier,
the
r=1
cases of Conjecture
1.3
are all known to be true.
In this paper we will prove (c) and (d) for
r=2
.
8
8

8

The log-concavity problems in (c) for
r=3,4,5
were proposed
by the first author at the 30th British Combinatorial Conference:
see
[
12
]
for the full list of problems.

Our conjecture for the Stirling subset numbers of all orders is the following:

Conjecture 1.4

(Conjecture for Stirling subset numbers of all orders)
.

The following are true:

(a)

The triangle
S^{(r)}
is totally positive for all
r\geq 1
.

(b)

The triangle
\widecheck{S}^{(r)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\left(S^{(r)}\right)^{\rm rev}

is totally positive for

and is not totally positive when

.

(c)

The row-generating polynomials

are negative-real-rooted
for
r=1,2
and they have non-real complex zeros for
r\geq 3

and
n\geq 3
.
Also, the row sequences of the matrix
S^{(r)}

are log-concave for
1\leq r\leq 5
.

(d)

The sequence
(s_{r,n}(x))_{n\geq 0}

is coefficientwise Hankel-totally positive
in the variable
x
for
r=1,2

and is not coefficientwise Hankel-totally positive for
r\geq 3
.

As mentioned earlier,
the
r=1

are all known to be true.
For

, the sequence of polynomials

was studied by Elvey Price and Sokal

who called them the

[

It then follows from

.
Therefore, part (d) of Conjecture

is known to be true for

.
In this paper, we will prove part (c) for the case
r=2
.

Our main result on Hankel-total positivity is therefore the following:

Theorem 1.5

(Hankel-total positivity of the second-order Stirling cycle polynomials)
.

The sequence of second-order Stirling cycle polynomials
(c_{2,n}(x))_{n\geq 0}

is coefficientwise Hankel-totally positive in the variable
x
.

This theorem is, in fact, an immediate consequence of the
coefficientwise Hankel-total positivity
of the second-order Eulerian polynomials

[
38
, Corollaries 12.35 and 12.36]

together with the identity (
1.23
).
So our main work will be to prove Proposition
1.2
(a):
we will provide an algebraic proof (Section
2
)
and a combinatorial proof (Section
3
).
For our combinatorial proof,
we need a new combinatorial interpretation for the numbers

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}^{(2)}
,
using Stirling permutations, due to David Callan

[
11
]
;
we will do this in Section
3.2
.

More specifically,
[
38
, Corollary 12.35]

showed that the second-order Eulerian polynomial

is equal to a specialization of the multivariate Eulerian polynomial

{\mathcal{P}}_{n}^{(2)}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
introduced in
[

namely,

(We will recall the precise definitions in Section

It then follows immediately from (

)
that the second-order Stirling cycle polynomial

is also equal to a specialisation of the multivariate Eulerian polynomial:

c_{2,n}(x)\;=\;{\mathcal{P}}_{n}^{(2)}(x,1+x,1+x)\;.

(1.26)

And from this, Theorem

as a consequence of the coefficientwise Hankel-total positivity of the
multivariate Eulerian polynomials
[
38
, Corollary 12.3]
.

An analogous argument using
Proposition

(b) and (
1.24

instead, provides an alternate proof
of the coefficientwise Hankel-total positivity
of the second-order Stirling subset polynomials

using 2-branched S-fractions instead of classical T-fractions.
This was already observed in

[
23
, Note Added, p. 12]
.
The important remark here is that (
1.24
)
gives us a way to write the second-order Stirling subset polynomials

s_{2,n}(x)
as a specialization of the multivariate Eulerian polynomials,
namely,

s_{2,n}(x)\;=\;{\mathcal{P}}_{n}^{(2)}(x,x,1+x)\;.

(1.27)

See the Remark at the end of Section

.

Our second main contribution in this paper
is to introduce, in Section
4
,
three further combinatorial interpretations
for the numbers
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}^{(2)}
;
thus, in this paper we provide a total of five combinatorial interpretations
for the second-order Stirling cycle numbers.
Even though these latter interpretations are not necessary
for our proof of Theorem
1.5
,
and we are unable at present to see how to use them to
prove any of our other main conjectures,
we think that they are still interesting in their own right
and provide interesting consequences;
they also suggest new conjectures, as we will explain.
We begin, in Section
4.1
,
by introducing our third combinatorial interpretation
for the second-order Stirling cycle numbers,
in terms of increasing ternary trees,
using a well-known bijection to Stirling permutations.
Then, in Section
4.2
,
we introduce our fourth interpretation in terms of
certain families of weighted increasing trees with ordered children,
and we provide two different proofs:
one bijective and one using generating functions.
Both proofs suggest new lines of investigation,
and also show that the hypothesis in
[
37
, Theorem 1.1(c)]

due to Pétréolle and Sokal, though sufficient, is far from necessary.
In Section
4.3

we introduce our fifth and final interpretation
in terms of certain leaf-labeled trees.
We then show the relation to the multivariate Ward polynomials
introduced by Elvey Price and Sokal

[
23
, pp. 9–11]
,
and we show that these multivariate polynomials are a common generalization
of both the second-order Stirling cycle polynomials
c_{2,n}(x)

and the second-order Stirling subset polynomials
s_{2,n}(x)
.
This observation leads us very naturally to another triangle of numbers
that also seems to satisfy our four positivity properties;
we state this in Conjecture
4.17
.

Our main results on real-rootedness are the following:

Theorem 1.6

(Location of zeros for the of the second-order Stirling cycle and subset polynomials)
.

For each
n\geq 1
,
the polynomials
c_{2,n}(x)
and
s_{2,n}(x)
have
0
as a root.
The zeros of the polynomial sequence
\big(c_{2,n}(x)/x\big)_{n\geq 1}
,
and also of the sequence
\big(s_{2,n}(x)/x\big)_{n\geq 1}
,
are simple, interlacing, and lie in the interval
(-1,0)
.

Thus, in Theorem
1.6
we prove not only
that the zeros are non-positive, but that in fact they lie
in a specific interval.

As mentioned previously,
it is known
[
33
, pp. 395, 399]

that the Toeplitz-total positivity of the rows of a lower-triangular matrix
is equivalent to the negative-real-rootedness
of its row-generating polynomials.
It therefore follows from Theorem
1.6

that the lower-triangular Toeplitz matrices

\big(\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{i-j}^{(2)}\big)_{i,j\geq 0}

and

\big(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{i-j}^{(2)}\big)_{i,j\geq 0}

are totally positive for every
n\geq 0
.
In particular, the positivity of the contiguous
2\times 2
minors
of these Toeplitz matrices implies
that the row sequences of the second-order Stirling cycle and subset matrices

C^{(2)}
and
S^{(2)}
are log-concave.

One can show that Theorem
1.6

follows very easily from a very general theorem
due to Liu and Wang
[
34
]
.
However, for the convenience of the reader
we will provide a direct proof of this theorem
in Section
6
.

Even though the zeros of the polynomials
c_{r,n}(x)
and
s_{r,n}(x)

do not lie on the real line for
r\geq 3
,
it might be worthwhile to try to
understand how they are distributed in the complex plane.
In Section
7
,
we will provide some plots for the distribution of zeros of the polynomials
for orders
r\geq 3
.
These plots will suggest that these zeros, when correctly normalised,
seem to accumulate around some limiting curves.
However, at this moment we are unable to provide
any precise conjectures or proofs describing these limiting curves.

Statement of conjectures

A major longstanding open problem in the theory of total positivity is
Brenti’s conjecture on the total positivity of the triangle of Eulerian numbers

[
9
]
.
Brenti’s conjecture is in fact the first in a family of conjectures
concerning the higher-order Eulerian numbers
that arose in discussions between us and Alex Dyachenko

[

]
:

Conjecture 1.7

(Conjecture for
r
-th order Eulerian numbers
[

]

.

For any

, the following are true:

(a)

The
r
-th order Eulerian triangle

(b)

The shifted-reversed
r
-th order Eulerian triangle

\widecheck{E}^{(r)}=\big(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n-k-1}^{\!(r)}\big)_{n,k\geq 0}

We remark that (a) and (b) are equivalent for

by virtue of the symmetry
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}=\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n-k-1}
for

;
but this is not the case for
r\geq 2
,
where the two conjectures need to be considered separately.
Conjecture
1.7
is unproven even for
r=1
,
where it is Brenti’s conjecture from 1996
[
9
]
.
Dyachenko
[
18
]

has verified Conjecture
1.7
(a,b)
for the leading

submatrix for

and for the leading

submatrix for

by computing the bidiagonal factorization

[

]

Conjecture
1.7
asks our questions (a) and (b)
for the higher-order Eulerian triangles.
Questions (c) and (d) for these triangles
have already been answered in the affirmative:
the negative-real-rootedness of the row-generating polynomials
is due to Brenti
[
7

and their coefficientwise Hankel-total positivity

[

However, as mentioned previously, as far as we know there is no relation

In particular, if

Proof of Proposition

.

(a) The recurrence (

(b) Making the substitution

4
Second-order Stirling cycle numbers: Three further combinatorial interpretations

In this section we will provide three more combinatorial interpretations
for the second-order Stirling cycle numbers.
In Section
4.1

we provide an interpretation using increasing ternary trees.
In Section
4.2

we introduce an interpretation involving increasing trees
with ordered children.
In Section
4.3

we introduce an interpretation involving a class of leaf-labeled trees
with cyclically-ordered children.

4.1
Interpretation III: Increasing ternary trees

We begin by recalling
[
44
, p. 573]

the recursive definition of an
\bm{m}
-ary tree

(
1\leq m<\infty
):
it is either empty or else consists of a root together with
an ordered list of
m
subtrees
(T_{0},\ldots,T_{m-1})
,
each of which is an
m
-ary tree (which may be empty).
We draw an edge from each vertex to the root of each of its nonempty subtrees;
an edge from a vertex to the root of its
i
th subtree (
0\leq i\leq m-1
)
will be called an
\bm{i}
-edge
.
When
m=2
, we refer to a
binary tree

and to its
left
and
right
edges.
When
m=3
, we refer to a
ternary tree

and to its
left
,
middle
and
right
edges.

A rooted tree on a totally ordered vertex set
is called
increasing

if each vertex is higher-numbered than its parent;
equivalently, the labels increase along each path downward from the root.
It is well known
[
44
, p. 45, Proposition 1.5.3(a)]

that the number of increasing binary trees on the vertex set
[n]
is
n!
,
and more generally that the number of increasing
m
-ary trees
on the vertex set
[n]
is the multifactorial
F_{n}^{(m-1)}

[
5
, p. 30, Example 1]
,
where

F_{n}^{(r)}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\;\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1+jr)
.
In particular, the number of increasing ternary trees on the vertex set
[n]

is
(2n-1)!!
.

We write
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
for the set of increasing ternary trees
on the vertex set
[n]
.
(In particular,
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
has a single element, namely the empty tree.)
For
n\geq 1
, we write
{\mathcal{T}}_{n,k}
for the set of increasing ternary trees
on the vertex set
[n]
with
k
left edges.

In what follows we shall also use a variant:
We write
\mathcal{T}^{\,{}^{\prime}}_{n}
for the set of increasing ternary trees
on the vertex set
[n]\cup\{0\}

for which the only edge (if any) emanating from the root
0
is a left edge.
(Thus,
\mathcal{T}^{\,{}^{\prime}}_{0}
has a single element, namely the tree with only
the root vertex 0; and in all trees
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\,{}^{\prime}}_{n}
with
n\geq 1
,
the root vertex 0 has a left child 1 and no other children.)
We write
\mathcal{T}^{\,{}^{\prime}}_{n,k}
for the set of increasing ternary trees

T\in\mathcal{T}^{\,{}^{\prime}}_{n}
that have
k
left edges.

For
n\geq 1
, there is an obvious bijection between
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\,{}^{\prime}}_{n}
,
which also maps
{\mathcal{T}}_{n,k}
to
\mathcal{T}^{\,{}^{\prime}}_{n,k+1}
: namely,
given
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
we define

by introducing a new root vertex 0 and making
T
its left subtree;
and given
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{\,{}^{\prime}}_{n}
, we define
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
by deleting the
root vertex 0 and making the vertex 1
the new root.
For
n=0
, we augment this bijection by sending the empty tree
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{0,0}

to the tree
T^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{0,0}
that has only the root vertex
0
.

Since Stirling permutations
of size
n

and the set of increasing ternary trees

both have cardinality
(2n-1)!!
,
there clearly must exist a bijection between these two sets.
Indeed, such a bijection is well known
[
36
,
32
]
.
9
9

9

This bijection was first mentioned by Park in
[

]
,

]
.

We recall this construction, and the properties that we will need,

Lemma 4.1
.

There is a bijection
\sigma\mapsto T(\sigma)

where
\sigma

n

[n]
.

the first (resp. second) occurrence of the letter
a
in
\sigma

is the beginning of an ascent (i.e.

)
if and only if the vertex
a
in
T(\sigma)

has a middle (resp. right) child.

Proof.
We begin by recalling the bijection
\sigma\mapsto T(\sigma)
,
following
[
32
]
.
Let
\sigma=a_{1}\cdots a_{2n}
be any word on the alphabet

\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
such that

(a)

\sigma

and

\sigma

.

We then recursively define a ternary tree

as follows:

•

•

\sigma

Then

is the ternary tree rooted at

,
T(\sigma_{1})
and

\sigma_{1}

.

Thus, the ascents in

Now define an

Corollary 4.2

There is a bijection

First, we let

Let

\square

Since

For

, we have
\Phi_{n}(T)\in{\mathcal{I}}_{n}
.

Construction of the map
\Psi_{n}:{\mathcal{I}}_{n}\to\mathcal{T}^{\,{}^{\prime}}_{n}
.

Given a tree

,

We start with a tree rooted at

.
We then iterate through

and

Otherwise, we create a left edge emanating from

{\rm left}_{\Psi_{n}(S)}(i)\;=\;{\rm Tree}\big({\rm ch}_{S}(i)\big)\;.

(4.3)

When

.
For

In the construction of

.
Next, notice that a vertex

and

in

,

4.2.2
Generating-function proof of
Proposition
4.5

We will now use generating functions to provide an alternate proof of
Proposition
4.5
;
this approach also situates it as a special case of a more general result,
to be enunciated in Proposition
4.12
.
We begin by introducing a framework for enumerating
increasing ordered trees with very general vertex weights,
which was first introduced by
Bergeron, Flajolet and Salvy
[
5
]

and was further applied in
[
37
]
.

Let
{\bm{\phi}}=(\phi_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be indeterminates,
and consider increasing ordered trees on
n
vertices
(here we will use the labels
0,\ldots,n-1
)
in which each vertex with
i
children gets a weight
\phi_{i}
;
the weight of the tree, denoted
{\rm wt}(T)
,
is the product of the weights of its vertices.
Let
F(t;{\bm{\phi}})
be the weighted exponential generating function of
such trees, not including the empty tree:

F(t;{\bm{\phi}})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\;\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{I}}_{n-1}}{\rm wt}(S)\right)\dfrac{t^{n}}{n!}\;.

(4.6)

Also let
\Phi(u)
be the ordinary generating function of
{\bm{\phi}}
:

\Phi(u)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\phi_{i}u^{i}\;.

(4.7)

We call
\Phi(u)
the
degree function
.
It is not difficult to show,
by classifying trees according to the degree of the root,
that
F(t;{\bm{\phi}})
satisfies the differential equation

[
5
, eq. (8)]

\dfrac{d}{dt}F(t;{\bm{\phi}})\;=\;\Phi(F(t;{\bm{\phi}}))\;.

(4.8)

Notice that even though we defined the generating function

F(t;{\bm{\phi}})
using increasing
ordered
trees,
this set-up is very general and we can use different
degree functions to enumerate different families of weighted trees.
For example, if we substitute
\phi_{i}=\widetilde{\phi}_{i}/i!
,
we may think of
F(t;{\bm{\phi}})
as the exponential generating function
enumerating increasing
unordered
trees
where each vertex with
i
children gets weight
\widetilde{\phi}_{i}
.

It turns out that the connection between
increasing ternary trees and increasing ordered trees
that is asserted in Proposition
4.5

is a special case of a more general connection between
increasing trees with degree functions
\Phi
and
\Psi

that are related in a particular way, as follows:

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
\widehat{\Phi}(u)
be a formal power series with zero constant term,
and let
{\mathcal{X}}(s)
be a formal power series satisfying

\widehat{\Phi}^{\prime}(u)\;=\;{\mathcal{X}}(\widehat{\Phi}(u))\;.

(4.9)

Now define

\Phi(u)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}1\,+\,x\>\widehat{\Phi}(u){}\\
\Psi(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}(1\,+\,xu)\>{\mathcal{X}}(u){}

(4.10)

Let
{\mathcal{A}}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}F(t;{\bm{\phi}})
with degree function
\Phi(u)
,
and let
{\mathcal{B}}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}F(t;{\bm{\psi}})
with degree function
\Psi(u)
.
Then the following identities hold:

{\mathcal{B}}(t)&=\widehat{\Phi}({\mathcal{A}}(t)){}\\
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(t)=1+x{\mathcal{B}}(t){}

(4.11)

Proof.
The definition
{\mathcal{B}}(t)=F(t;{\bm{\psi}})
implies the differential equation

{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}(t)\;=\;\Psi({\mathcal{B}}(t))\;=\;(1\,+\,x{\mathcal{B}}(t))\>{\mathcal{X}}({\mathcal{B}}(t))

(4.12)

by (
4.8
) and (
).
On the other hand, the definition
{\mathcal{A}}(t)=F(t;{\bm{\phi}})

implies the differential equation

\dfrac{d}{dt}\widehat{\Phi}({\mathcal{A}}(t))&=\widehat{\Phi}^{\prime}({\mathcal{A}}(t))\>{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(t)\\
={\mathcal{X}}(\widehat{\Phi}({\mathcal{A}}(t)))\>\Phi({\mathcal{A}}(t))\\
={\mathcal{X}}(\widehat{\Phi}({\mathcal{A}}(t)))\>\big[1+x\widehat{\Phi}({\mathcal{A}}(t))\big]

(4.13)

by the chain rule, (
4.9
),
(
4.8
) and (
).
So
{\mathcal{B}}(t)
and
\widehat{\Phi}({\mathcal{A}}(t))

satisfy the same differential equation with the same initial condition
(namely, they vanish at
t=0
), hence they are equal.
This proves (
).

Then

{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(t)\;=\;\Phi({\mathcal{A}}(t))\;=\;1+x\widehat{\Phi}({\mathcal{A}}(t))\;=\;1+x{\mathcal{B}}(t)

(4.14)

by (
4.8
), (
)
and (
).
This proves (
).

\square

Proof of Proposition
4.5
using generating functions.

We will now prove Proposition
4.5

by using Proposition
4.12
.
First, consider the exponential generating function

{\mathcal{A}}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}F\big(t;(\delta_{i0}+(1-\delta_{i0})xy^{i-1}))_{i\geq 0}\big)
:
thus,
{\mathcal{A}}(t)
is the exponential generating function
for increasing ordered trees on
n
labeled vertices
where each leaf is given weight
\phi_{0}=1

and each vertex with
i\geq 1
children is given weight
\phi_{i}=xy^{i-1}
.
Therefore, the degree function is

\Phi(u)\;=\;1\,+\,\dfrac{xu}{1-yu}\;

(4.15)

and

{\mathcal{A}}(t)
satisfies the differential equation

{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\;=\;1+\dfrac{x{\mathcal{A}}}{1-y{\mathcal{A}}}\;.

(4.16)

In the weight
\phi_{i}=(x/y)y^{i}
for internal vertices,
we can transfer a weight
y
to each of the
i
children;
since every vertex other than the root is a child,
we end up with a weight
y
for leaves and
x
for internal vertices,
with an overall factor
1/y
because the root is not a child.
Therefore, from (
4.6
) we have

{\mathcal{A}}(t)\;=\;\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{I}}_{n-1}}x^{{\rm intvertex}(S)}y^{{\rm leaf}(S)-1}\right)\dfrac{t^{n}}{n!}

(4.17)

where
{\rm intvertex}(S)
and
{\rm leaf}(S)

denote the number of internal vertices and leaves of
of the tree
S\in{\mathcal{I}}_{n-1}
, respectively.

We use the notation from Proposition
4.12
to obtain

\widehat{\Phi}(u)&=\dfrac{u}{1-yu}\\
{\mathcal{X}}(s)=(1+ys)^{2}\\
\Psi(u)=(1+xu)(1+yu)^{2}

(4.18)

We then define
{\mathcal{B}}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}F(t;{\bm{\psi}})

with degree function
\Psi(u)=(1+xu)(1+yu)^{2}
.
We can interpret
{\mathcal{B}}(t)
as
the exponential generating function for increasing ternary trees
on the vertex set
[n]
,
where each left edge gets a weight
x

and each middle or right edge gets a weight
y
,
and the weight of a tree is the product of the weights of its edges.
Passing to trees
on the vertex set
[n]\cup\{0\}

in which the only edge (if any) emanating from the root is a left edge,
we get an exponential generating function

1+x\,{\mathcal{B}}(t)\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}^{\,{}^{\prime}}_{n}}x^{{\rm l}(T)}y^{{\rm m}(T)+{\rm r}(T)}\right)\dfrac{t^{n}}{n!}

(4.19)

where
{\rm l}(T),{\rm m}(T),{\rm r}(T)

denote the number of left, middle and right edges of a tree
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
,
respectively.
Finally, we use Proposition
4.12

to obtain

{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(t)\;=\;1+x\,{\mathcal{B}}(t)

(4.20)

Combining equations (
4.17
),
(
4.19
)
and (
4.20
)

4.3
Interpretation V: Cyclically-ordered phylogenetic trees

Here we introduce our fifth and final combinatorial interpretation of the
second-order Stirling cycle numbers.
This interpretation will lead us to a common generalization
of the second-order Stirling numbers of both kinds
and also lead us to another conjecture
similar to Conjectures
1.3
and
1.4
.

A
phylogenetic tree
is a leaf-labeled rooted tree
(i.e. the leaves are labeled and the non-leaf vertices are unlabeled)
in which all non-leaf vertices have at least two children.
We refer to them as an
unordered phylogenetic tree
,
an
ordered phylogenetic tree
,
or a
cyclically-ordered phylogenetic tree

according to whether the children at each vertex are
unordered, linearly ordered or cyclically ordered.
Cyclically-ordered phylogenetic trees have also been called

series-reduced mobiles

[
35
, A032188]

[
1
]
.
Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{n,k}
denote the set of all cyclically-ordered phylogenetic trees
with
n+1
leaves and
k
internal (i.e. non-leaf) vertices.

We will now establish the following proposition:

Proposition 4.15
.

The cardinality of the set
{\mathcal{M}}_{n,k}
of
cyclically-ordered phylogenetic trees
with

n+1
leaves and
k
internal vertices is given by

\left|{\mathcal{M}}_{n,k}\right|\;=\;\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}^{(2)}.

(4.31)

We will provide two different proofs of Proposition
4.31
.
Our first proof will show that the numbers
\left|{\mathcal{M}}_{n,k}\right|

satisfy the recurrence relation (
1.15
).
Our second proof will use generating functions
for the multivariate Ward polynomials
introduced by Elvey Price and Sokal in
[
23
]
.

Proof of Proposition
4.31
using recurrence relations.

We will show that the numbers
\left|{\mathcal{M}}_{n,k}\right|
satisfy

|{\mathcal{M}}_{n,k}|\;=\;(n+k-1)\,|{\mathcal{M}}_{n-1,k-1}|\>+\>(n+k-1)\,|{\mathcal{M}}_{n-1,k}|\;.

(4.32)

To do this, we will consider two cases whether or not the leaf labeled
n+1

has exactly one sibling.

If
n+1
has at least two siblings,
we can remove it, and the resulting tree belongs to
{\mathcal{M}}_{n-1,k}
.
Conversely, to insert
n+1
into a tree belonging to
{\mathcal{M}}_{n-1,k}
,
it suffices to choose the vertex that would be its immediately preceding
(in the cyclic order) sibling in the resulting tree.
There are
n+k-1
such choices.
This contributes the term
(n+k-1)\,|{\mathcal{M}}_{n-1,k}|
.

If
n+1
has exactly one sibling,
then the tree obtained by removing it is no longer a phylogenetic tree,
as the parent of
n+1
would now have only one child.
Instead, we proceed as follows:

•

If the parent of
n+1
is not the root,
we contract the edge between the parent and grandparent of
n+1

and then remove
n+1
from the resulting tree;
this gives us a tree in
{\mathcal{M}}_{n-1,k-1}
.
Conversely, to insert
n+1
back into this tree,
we need to select an edge,
and elongate it by adding an extra internal vertex
and then insert
n+1
as the other child of this new internal vertex.
As there are
n+k-2
edges,
we get a contribution of
(n+k-2)|{\mathcal{M}}_{n-1,k-1}|
.

•

If the parent of
n+1
is the root,
we remove the root and the vertex
n+1

to obtain a tree rooted at the sibling of
n+1
.
Conversely, we can re-obtain the original tree
by creating a new root whose children are the old root and vertex
n+1
.
This contributes
|{\mathcal{M}}_{n-1,k-1}|
.

Putting these two contributions together gives
(n+k-1)\,|{\mathcal{M}}_{n-1,k-1}|
.

\square

To begin, let us first provide an interpretation for the numbers

To do this, let us first consider the multiset

a_{i}\;=\;\begin{cases}2\qquad\text{if $i\equiv 0\pmod{r-1}$}\\
1\qquad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(5.2)

Thus, for
r=2
we have

M_{n}^{(2)}=\{1,1,2,2,3,3,\ldots,n,n\}

and for
r=3
we have

M_{n}^{(3)}=\{1,2,2,3,4,4,\ldots,2n-1,2n,2n\}
.
Let
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}^{(r)}
denote the set of Stirling permutations of the
multiset
M_{n}^{(r)}
.
It immediately follows from
[
32
, eq. (1)]
that

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n}^{(r)}\;=\;\left|{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}^{(r)}\right|\,.

(5.3)

The set
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}^{(r)}
is our first ingredient.
Notice that each
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}^{(r)}

is a string of length
rn
consisting of
(r-1)n
distinct letters.

Our next ingredient is a new combinatorial statistic on
Stirling permutations in
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}^{(r)}
.
Given
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}^{(r)}
,
we say that
i\in[n]
is a
consecutive ascent
in
\sigma

if the following two conditions hold:

•

if the following
r-1
letters:

(r-1)i+1,(r-1)i+2,\ldots,(r-1)i+(r-2)

and the first occurence of
(r-1)(i+1)
,
occur consecutively in
\sigma
.

•

the letter
(r-1)i+1
is preceeded by a lower-numbered letter in
\sigma
.

An alternate way of saying this is that the string
a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{r-1}

where
a_{j}=(r-1)i+j
occurs as a substring in
\sigma

and
a_{1}
is preceeded by a lower-numbered letter.
Thus, if
i\in[n]
is a consecutive ascent in
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}^{(r)}
,
then the
r-1
letters,

(r-1)i+1,(r-1)i+2,\ldots,(r-1)i+(r-2)

and the first occurence of
(r-1)(i+1)
,
are the ends of ascents (i.e.
a
in
\sigma_{i}<\sigma_{i+1}=a
).
Let
{\rm CAsc}(\sigma)\subseteq[n]
denote the set of consecutive ascents
in
\sigma
.
We then define
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}^{(r)}
to be the set of
Stirling permutations in
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}^{(r)}
with
k
consecutive ascents:

{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}^{(r)}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\;\{\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}^{(r)}\>\colon{\rm CAsc}(\sigma)=k\}\;.

(5.4)

Notice that when
r=2
,
consecutive ascents are the same as ascents in Stirling permutations,
so
|{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}^{(2)}|=\left\langle\!\!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\!\!\right\rangle
.

A
consecutive-ascent-marked Stirling permutation
of order
\bm{r}
and size
\bm{n}
with
\bm{k}
dots

is defined to be a pair
(\sigma,D)
where

\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}^{(r)}
and
D\subseteq{\rm CAsc}(\sigma)\subseteq[n]

and
|D|=k
.
We let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n,k}^{(r)}
denote the set of
consecutive-ascent-marked Stirling permutation
of order
r
and size
n
with
k
dots.
Thus, when
r=2
we have the interpretation in
Section
3.2
.
For
r=3
, we have
{\mathcal{V}}_{2,0}^{(3)}
is equivalent to
{\mathfrak{S}}_{2}^{(3)}

(as we always have
D=\varnothing
)
which has cardinality
40
and hence we refrain from listing out.
For
k=1
, we have

{\mathcal{V}}_{2,1}^{(3)}\;=\;\{122\bm{\cdot}3\bm{\cdot}44,12\bm{\cdot}3\bm{\cdot}442,1\bm{\cdot}3\bm{\cdot}4422\}.

(5.5)

Here we have inserted dots to the
r-1=2
ascents corresponding
to each of the
i\in D
.

Let
v_{n,k}^{(r)}=|{\mathcal{V}}_{n,k}^{(r)}|

and
b_{n,k}^{(r)}=|{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}^{(r)}|
;
then

v_{n,k}^{(r)}\;=\;\sum_{i=k}^{n}b_{n,i}^{(r)}\>\binom{i}{k}\;.

(5.6)

Notice that for
r=2
, the identity
a_{n,k}^{(2)}=\left\langle\!\!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\!\!\right\rangle
together with
Proposition
1.2
(a), which gives us (
3.4
),
implies
v_{n,k}^{(2)}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n-k}^{(2)}
.

We will now establish the following lemma,
which generalises Lemma
3.1
:

Lemma 5.1
.

The numbers
v_{n,k}^{(r)}
satisfy the recurrence

\displaystyle v_{n,k}^{(r)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;(r-1)!\binom{r(n-1)\,-\,(r-1)(k-1)}{r-1}\,v_{n-1,k}^{(r)}

(5.7)

\displaystyle+\;\;[r(n-1)\,-\,(r-1)(k-1)]\,v_{n-1,k-1}^{(r)}

with initial conditions
v_{0,k}^{r}=\delta_{k0}
and
v_{n,-1}^{r}=0
.

Proof.
Let
(\sigma,D)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n,k}^{(r)}
be a
consecutive-ascent-marked Stirling permutation
of order
r
and size
n
with
k
dots.
Throughout this proof,
we set
a_{j}=(r-1)(n-1)+j
where
1\leq j\leq r-1

and
a_{r}=(r-1)n
.

First, notice that on deleting the
r-2
letters

a_{1},\ldots,a_{r-2}

along with the two letters
a_{r-1},a_{r}
in
\sigma

together with
n\in D
, if it exists,
gives an element of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n-1,k}^{(r)}
if
n\not\in D
,
and an element of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n-1,k}^{(r)}
if
n\in D
.

Conversely, to get an element
(\sigma,D)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n,k}^{(r)}

with
n\not\in D
from a one-size-smaller
ascent-marked Stirling permutation,
we insert the
r
letters

a_{1},\ldots,a_{r-2},\,\,a_{r-1},a_{r}
.
For convenience, let
l=r(n-1)-(r-1)k
.
Notice that there are
l+1
choices to insert the letter
a_{1}

(these consist of any of the
r(n-1)+1
spaces
except for the
(r-1)k
corresponding to dots),

l+2
to insert the letter
a_{2}
,
and so on until we finally get

l+(r-1)
choices for inserting
a_{r-1}\,a_{r}
.
Thus, there are a total of
(r-1)!\binom{l+r-1}{r-1}
choices.

In the other case, if
(\sigma,D)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n,k}^{(r)}
with
n\in D
,
then the string

a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{r-1}

is a substring of
\sigma
.
To get such a pair
(\sigma,D)
,
the string
a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{r-1}a_{r}

can be inserted into an element of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n-1,k-1}^{(r)}

in any of the
r(n-1)+1
spaces except the first space
and those corresponding to a dot;
thus, the number of choices is
r(n-1)-(r-1)(k-1)
.

This proves the recurrence (
5.7
).

\square

Finally, we use Lemma
5.1

to obtain
our combinatorial interpretation for the
r
th-order Stirling cycle numbers and
for the
r
th-order quasi-Eulerian cycle numbers;
we state this in the following proposition:

Proposition 5.2
.

The entries of the matrix
Q_{\rm C}^{(r)}=\widecheck{C}^{(r)}B^{-1}

of
r
th-order quasi-Eulerian cycle numbers
have the combinatorial interpretation

\left\langle\!\!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\!\!\right\rangle^{\!(r)}_{\!\rm C}\;=\;|{\mathfrak{S}}_{n,k}^{(r)}|\;.

(5.8)

Also, the
r
th-order Stirling cycle number
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}^{(r)}

counts the number of
consecutive-ascent-marked Stirling permutations of order
r
and size
n

with
n-k
dots,
i.e.,
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}^{(r)}=|{\mathcal{V}}_{n,n-k}^{(r)}|
.

Proof.
We now need to show that

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}^{(r)}=v_{n,n-k}^{(r)}
and

\left\langle\!\!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\!\!\right\rangle^{(r)}_{\!\rm C}=b_{n,k}^{(r)}
.

From (
5.7
) it follows that the numbers

u_{n,k}^{(r)}=v_{n,n-k}^{(r)}
satisfies
the recurrence (
1.13
)
which is satisfied by the numbers
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}^{(r)}
.
Since these numbers also satisfy the same initial conditions,
we infer that
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}^{(r)}=u_{n,k}^{(r)}
.
Finally, from the definition of

and (

), it follows that
\left\langle\!\!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\!\!\right\rangle^{(r)}_{\!\rm C}=b_{n,k}^{(r)}
.

\square

.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

We prove it for the polynomial sequence
\widehat{c}_{2,n}(x)
.
The proof for the polynomial sequence
\widehat{s}_{2,n}(x)

is mostly the same and is left to the reader.

We first rewrite equation (
6.3
) as

\widehat{c}_{n}(x)\;=\;x\>\big((n+2)\widehat{c}_{n-1}(x)+(x+1)\widehat{c}_{n-1}^{\prime}(x)\big)\,+\,(n+1)\>\widehat{c}_{n-1}(x)

(6.8)

Now, we use induction on
n
.
For the case
n=0
,
the polynomial
p_{0}(x)=1

has no roots and hence the theorem is vacuously true.
The case
n=1
can be checked to be true as well.
Let us assume that the zeros of
\widehat{c}_{n-1}(x)

are distinct and are contained in the interval
(-1,0)
.

Now let
p_{n}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}{\rm GCD}\left(\widehat{c}_{n-1}(x),(x+1)\widehat{c}_{n-1}^{\prime}(x)\right)
.
By Rolle’s theorem,
the zeros of
\widehat{c}_{n-1}^{\prime}(x)

interlace those of
\widehat{c}_{n-1}(x)

and thus, lie in the interval

and are simple.
Thus we get
p_{n}(x)=1
.
Thus, there are no common zeros between
\widehat{c}_{n-1}(x)
,

\widehat{c}_{n-1}^{\prime}(x)

and
\widehat{c}_{n}(x)
.

Now let
-1<\lambda_{n-1}^{(n-1)}<\ldots<\lambda_{1}^{(n-1)}<0

be the zeros of
\widehat{c}_{n-1}(x)
.
We then have
\widehat{c}_{n-1}^{\prime}(\lambda_{j}^{(n-1)})>0

if
j
is odd,
and

\widehat{c}_{n-1}^{\prime}(\lambda_{j}^{(n-1)})<0

if
j
is even.
Hence,

{\rm sgn}\Big(\widehat{c}_{n}(\lambda_{j}^{(n-1)})\Big)\;=\;(-1)^{j}\;.

(6.9)

Furthermore, using Lemma
6.7
,
we also have
\widehat{c}_{n}(-1)=(-1)^{n}

and
\widehat{c}_{n}(0)=(n+1)!
.
Thus, the proof of this theorem follows by using
the intermediate value theorem.

\square

7.1
Existence of nonreal roots for
\bm{r\geq 3}

Conjectures
1.3
(c) and
1.4
(c) assert that,
for
r\geq 3
, the
r
th-order Stirling cycle and subset polynomials
are not negative-real-rooted.
In this subsection we will prove this conjecture
for the cycle case for all
r\geq 3
,
and for the subset case for all
r\geq 4
.
The subset case with
r=3
remains open.

We already know that
c_{r,n}(0)=s_{r,n}(0)=0
for
n\geq 1
.
One sufficient condition to show that a polynomial
p(x)
has
non-real complex zeros is to show that the polynomial
\dfrac{d^{m}}{dx^{m}}p(x)

for some
m\leq{\rm deg}(p)
has non-real complex zeros.
In this section, we will show that the polynomials

\dfrac{d^{n-3}}{dx^{n-3}}(c_{r,n}(x)/x)

and
\dfrac{d^{n-3}}{dx^{n-3}}(s_{r,n}(x)/x)
,
which are quadratic polynomials,
have a negative discriminant when
r,n\geq 3
in the cycle case and

r\geq 4
,
n\geq 3
in the subset case.
This will prove that in these cases
the polynomials
c_{r,n}(x)
and
s_{r,n}(x)
must have some complex
non-real zeros.
We will then provide plots for the distribution of these zeros
which suggest that these zeros accumulate around some
mysterious limiting curves.

We first notice that

\dfrac{d^{n-3}}{dx^{n-3}}(c_{r,n}(x)/x)\;=\;\dfrac{(n-1)!}{2}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n}^{(r)}x^{2}\,+\,(n-2)!\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n-1}^{(r)}x\,+\,(n-3)!\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n-2}

(7.1)

We have already seen in (
1.10
) that

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n}^{(r)}=(rn)!/(r^{n}n!)
.
We now obtain the following exact expressions
either by using the
combinatorial descriptions or by the recurrences (
1.13
):

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n-1}^{(r)}&=\dfrac{(r(n-1)+1)!}{(r+1)r^{n-2}(n-2)!}\\
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n-2}^{(r)}=\dfrac{(r(n-2)+2)!\;\bigl(r(r+2)(n-1)\,+\,2\bigr)}{2\,(r+1)^{2}\,(r+2)\,r^{n-3}\,(n-3)!}

(7.2)

With this information, we prove the following proposition:

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
D_{r}(n)
be the discriminant of the
quadratic polynomial

\dfrac{d^{n-3}}{dx^{n-3}}(c_{r,n}(x)/x)
.
For all
r,n\geq 3
,
we have
D_{r}(n)<0
.

Proof.
From (
7.1
), we write out the exact expression for
D_{r}(n)
,
which is

D_{n}(r)&=\left((n-2)!\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n-1}^{(r)}\right)^{2}\,-\,2(n-1)!(n-3)!\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n}^{(r)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n-2}^{(r)}\\
=\dfrac{(r(n-1)+1)!^{2}}{(r+1)^{2}r^{2n-4}}\\
\,-\,\dfrac{(rn)!(r(n-2)+2)!((r+2)r(n-1)+2)}{n(r+2)(r+1)^{2}r^{2n-3}}\\
=\dfrac{(r(n-1)+1)!(r(n-2)+2)!}{(r+1)^{2}r^{2n-4}}\cdot\left(\dfrac{(r(n-1)+1)!}{(r(n-2)+2)!}\right.\\
\,-\,\left.\dfrac{((r+2)r(n-1)+2)}{n(r+2)r}\cdot\dfrac{(rn)!}{(r(n-1)+1)!}\right)\\
=\dfrac{(r(n-1)+1)!(r(n-2)+2)!}{(r+1)^{2}r^{2n-4}}\cdot\\
\Biggl((rn-(r-1))(rn-(r-1)-1)\cdots(rn-2(r-1)+1)\\
\,-\,\dfrac{((r+2)r(n-1)+2)}{n(r+2)r}\cdot(rn)(rn-1)\cdots(rn-(r-1)+1)\Biggr)

(7.3)

Notice that when
r,n\geq 3
,
we have

(rn-(r-1))(rn-(r-1)-1)-\dfrac{((r+2)r(n-1)+2)}{n(r+2)r}\cdot(rn)(rn-1)\\
=\;\dfrac{-n(r^{3}-2r)+(r^{3}-4r+2)}{r+2}<0\;.

(7.4)

It is easy to compare the remaining terms
and it follows that
D_{r}(n)<0
for
r,n\geq 3
.

\square

Appendix C
Report on systematic verification of conjectures

We will now report the results of our systematic experiments,
i.e., we will write out up to which order we have verified each of our conjectures.

Our total positivity tests were performed by using the following methods:

•

For a finite matrix
A
with real entries,
an efficient method to test total positivity is
by computing the bidiagonal factorization
using Neville elimination
[
27
]
.
We used this technique to test the total positivity
of lower-triangular matrices.

•

For checking negative-real-rootedness of polynomials,
we used
Mathematica
’s built-in
NSolve
command
to list out approximate values of the zeros.
This method is very efficient.

•

For checking coefficientwise Hankel-total posivity of a sequence of polynomials,
we unfortunately have to compute and check all minors of the Hankel matrix.
No other efficient method is known.
Compared to the other two types of tests,
this method is very slow and we can check this only for matrices of small sizes.
