The association between social factors and migraine or severe headache in men: a secondary analysis of national population-based studies with machine learning models
Authors: Zhe Yu, Jiaxin Zhang, Mianwang He, Ruozhuo Liu, Shengyuan Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMigraine is recognized as a global public health issue. It is more prevalent among women; therefore, studies focusing solely on migraine in men are scarce. We aimed to investigate the relationship between social factors and migraine or severe headache in men, based on a Chinese national population study and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).MethodIn 2009, we conducted a population-based survey on headaches, for which 5,041 unrelated individuals were randomly selected and visited in all regions of China. A secondary analysis was performed on the data pertaining to men. Data retrieved from the NHANES between 1999 and 2004 were analyzed alongside the same social factors. Logistic regression was used to analyze the factors affecting men. We compared the predictive performance of six machine learning models: XGBoost, Random Forest, Decision Tree, Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine, and K-Nearest Neighbors.ResultsOf 2,496 male Chinese participants, 236 (9.5%) were diagnosed with migraine or severe headache. According to the NHANES data, 714 (16.6%) of 4,303 male participants reported migraine or severe headache. Logistic regression of the two studies consistently revealed a positive association between obesity and migraine or severe headache (Chinese participants: odds ratio [OR] = 2.017, p = 0.020; NHANES sample: OR = 1.256, p = 0.038) as well as a negative correlation between increased income and the same condition. Occupation was related to migraine or severe headache in the Chinese population, and races were associated with headaches in the NHANES data. The five-fold cross-validation results demonstrated that the logistic regression model was the most stable and generalizable classifier. The SHAP analysis clarifies that significant differences existed in the contribution of individual factors to migraine or severe headache between the two studies.ConclusionObesity and high income were found to be associated with migraine or severe headache in men. Given the discrepancies observed between the two studies, further exploration of these factors across different countries may be warranted.

Background

Migraine is a globally prevalent disease, and over a million people suffer from migraine [1–3]. However, significant differences in the prevalence of migraine in men and women have been observed. Population-based estimates show that migraine is two-fold as prevalent in women as that in men, and the mechanism of migraine remains unknown [4]. Given the prevalence of migraine in women, a large number of studies have focused on migraine in women, with few studies focusing solely on migraine in men [5]. Moreover, the clinical manifestations of migraine, including duration, frequency, pain characteristics, and accompanying symptoms may differ between men and women [6–8]. As personalized medicine is becoming an increasingly popular approach to migraine treatment, it is important to investigate underlying risk factors in men [9].

To date, the relationship between marital status, family income, and migraine remains controversial and poorly understood in China. Investigation of the social factors associated with headaches could help us to develop migraine prevention strategies. It is commonly believed that migraine is closely related to estrogen and progesterone levels [10]. Some women with migraine experience relief during pregnancy, while others develop new migraine afterwards [10, 11]. Psychological stress and various socio-economic factors, including the employment category and annual household income, may differ between men and women [12]. It is necessary to explore the influence of these factors on men and women respectively.

A large, nationwide, cross-sectional survey of the Chinese population was conducted with the support of Lifting the Burden: The Global Campaign Against Headache [13]. We reported that the 1-year prevalence of migraine in China is 9.3% [13], and in this study, we conducted a secondary analysis of the social factors for migraine or severe headache in men. In addition, these social factors were also analyzed based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database. We aimed to explore the correlations and discrepancies in the social factors relating to migraine among men in different countries.

Methods

Ethical approval

The protocol of this study was approved by the Chinese ministry of health and the ethics committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital [13].

Data

This is a secondary analysis of a previous epidemiological survey conducted on the Chinese mainland [13]. In 2009, we conducted a nationwide study of primary headaches in China. The methodology has been described previously, and we have summarized it as follows: The Chinese mainland was divided into 24 units according to the population size. Of these, 35 cities or districts were randomly selected for sampling, with 20 to 30 towns or streets chosen at random from each one based on its population. From each town or street, a village or community was chosen at random, and subsequently, a household was randomly selected from each of these chosen villages or communities. Verbal consent was obtained from every household. Upon arriving at this initial household, surveyors randomly chose a direction, then included this household’s family and the next seven consecutive households’ families along that direction in the survey sample. Each sampled family had all its members listed by age, and one adult within the 18–65 age range was chosen to be the respondent via a random sampling form. We applied a Chinese version of the structured questionnaire developed by Lifting the Burden for population-based study [14]. The questionnaire comprised three sections: personal and sociodemographic data; diagnostic questions; and headache-related burden. Among these, the sociodemographic section included questions about age, sex, ethnicity, marital status, educational level, occupation, and total household income. The diagnostic questions began with the screening question, “Have you had a headache in the last year that was not related to flu, a hangover, a cold or a head injury?”, as previously described [13] and recommended by earlier studies [15]. The interviewers were trained neurologists who had completed the training program on epidemiological methods. Diagnosis was made based on the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 2nd edition (ICHD-2) [16]. Headache pain intensity was assessed using a Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) ranging from 0 to 10, and severe headache was defined as an NRS score of ≥ 7 [17]. A migraine or severe headache was defined as either a headache for which the respondents had received a migraine diagnosis, or a headache with a pain intensity rating of ≥ 7.

Data from the US NHANES database between 1999 and 2004 were selected. The NHANES project aimed to assess the health and nutritional conditions of non-institutionalized American residents through a stratified multistage probability survey [18]. The demographic data and detailed health-related information were collected via home visits, screening procedures, and laboratory tests, all of which are carried out by a mobile examination center. NHANES was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the National Center for Health Statistics, and every participant signed a written informed consent form prior to participation. Participants aged 18–65 years who underwent migraine screening and provided complete data on body mass index (BMI), race, marital status, educational level, occupation, and annual household income were included. Migraine or severe headache status was identified based on the participants’ responses to a question in the miscellaneous pain questionnaire, “Have you had a severe headache or migraine in the past 3 months?”

Categories of BMI and annual household income

Following the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines, body weight was classified as normal (BMI: 18.5 to < 25 kg/m²), underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²), overweight (BMI: 25 to < 30 kg/m²), and obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) [19]. Chinese annual household income was categorized as < CNY9,600, CNY9,600–59,999 and >CNY60,000 [13]. The household income of NHANES was categorized based on the poverty income ratio (PIR) as follows: PIR ≤ 1.3, 1.3–3.5, and >3.5 [20].

Statistics

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 27.0, IBM, USA). The demographics, including age, BMI, and income were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess whether the data for continuous variables were normally distributed. Normally distributed results were further subjected to an independent samples t-test. For results that were not normally distributed, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. For categorical data, chi-square test was used. Logistic regression was used to determine the association between the risk factors and migraine and severe headache. We set age as covariance. The other variables were independent and adjusted by age in the logistic regression. Statistical significance was set at p-value < 0.05.

Beyond the conventional statistical analyses, using R version 4.2.3 and python version 3.11.4, we compared the predictive performance of six machine learning models: XGBoost, Random Forest, Decision Tree, Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN). We employed a five-fold cross-validation scheme to evaluate the model performance. The dataset was randomly split into five equal-sized subsets (folds). In each iteration, four folds were used for training, and the remaining fold was held out for testing. This process was repeated five times such that each fold served as the test set exactly once. The performance metrics (area under the curve [AUC], accuracy, etc.) are reported as the mean ± SD of the results from the five test folds. This approach avoids the randomness of a single train-test split and provides a more robust estimate of the model’s generalizability. No fixed hold-out test set was used; all data were involved in the performance evaluation via the cross-validation process. The model evaluation metrics, including the AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and Kappa value, were calculated and reported as mean ± SD across the folds. Furthermore, we used the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis—an interpretability method rooted in game theory, often used to explain ML models—to quantify the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions. This provides a perspective different from p-values or odds ratios (ORs), helping to identify which factors were most important in driving the model’s decisions and visually illustrating their effect direction (positive or negative).

Results

Chinese population study

Of the 5,041 respondents, 2,561 were men. A total of 65 participants were excluded due to lack of data. Ultimately, 2496 male participants were included, and 236 ones (9.5%) were diagnosed with migraine or severe headaches (Fig. 1). Among all the included respondents, marital status was categorized as unmarried (12.3%), married (85.5%), divorced (1.2%), and widowed (1.0%) based on the questionnaire. The majority were Han Chinese, accounting for 94.5%. The educational levels were recorded as illiterate (3.0%), primary school (22.5%), secondary school (42.5%), high school (21.4%), and college level or above (10.6%). Occupations were classified as unemployed (6.1%), farmer (60.0%), worker (15.9%), student (2.6%), office worker (12.8%), soldier (0.5%), or other (2.1%). Of all the participants, 3.8% were underweight, 71.0% of normal weight, 21.7% overweight, and 3.5% obese.

NHANES data

Overall, 4,303 male participants were selected from NHANES between 1999 and 2004. Approximately 16.6% of participants reported migraine or severe headaches. Figure 1 shows the flow chart for exclusion criteria. Among all the respondents, marital status was categorized as follows: married (58.8%), widowed (1%), divorced (7.4%), separated (2.7%), never married (22.4%), and living with a partner (7.7%). Approximately 1.1% of participants were underweight, 30.1% were normal weight, 39.5% were overweight, and 29.3% were obese. For the limited cases in the subgroups, the marital status was collapsed into never married; married/living with partner and other. Statistically significant differences were observed between the migraine or severe headache group and the control group in terms of age, educational level, PIR, and BMI (Table 1). Specifically, compared to the control group, the migraine or severe headache group exhibited a lower proportion of respondents who were over 50 years old, had an education level beyond high school, or had a PIR over 1.3. In contrast, this group showed a higher proportion of obese respondents.

Logistic regression was applied with adjusted to age (Table 2). Compared to normal weight, those who were underweight (OR 2.165, p = 0.017) or obese (OR 1.256, p = 0.038) were positively related to severe headache or migraine. Higher income was negatively related to migraine or severe headache. There were significant associations between the racial group and the headache condition. An education level higher than high school was negatively related to reporting migraine or severe headache than that of less than high school.

Comparison between the Chinese population data and NHANES data

Compared with the NHANES data, the rate of migraine or severe headache was lower among Chinese men aged 18–29 years, 30–39 years, and 40–49 years, but comparable among those aged 50–59 years and 60–65 years (Fig. 2A). Based on the NHANES data, the obesity rate was significantly higher among male participants from the USA than among Chinese males, and these participants from the USA had a relatively higher BMI (Fig. 2B and C).

Machine learning models

The five-fold cross-validation results demonstrated that the LR model was both the most stable and generalizable classifier based on two databases (Fig. 3).

For Chinese data, LR achieved a mean AUC of 0.599 (SD = 0.053) on the validation sets, which was significantly higher and more consistent than the other models (e.g., XGBoost: AUC = 0.547; Random Forest: AUC = 0.536). While complex models like Random Forest and Decision Tree showed perfect performance on the training data (AUC = 1.000), their validation performance markedly decreased (e.g., Decision Tree AUC dropped to 0.509), indicating substantial overfitting. In contrast, LR’s performance metrics remained consistent across training and validation sets (e.g., validation sensitivity = 0.708, and specificity = 0.436), confirming its superior stability.

For NHANES data, LR achieved a mean AUC of 0.614 (SD = 0.000), which was higher than other models. The better stability of LR was confirmed by the fact that its performance measures (validation sensitivity = 0.367 and specificity = 0.752) remained the same throughout the training and validation sets.

Consequently, LR was selected for subsequent SHAP analysis to elucidate the contribution of individual risk factors to migraine and severe headache. These findings underscore that traditional LR provided a more reliable predictive foundation within our cross-validation framework compared to more complex algorithms. The SHAP analysis confirmed that the contribution of social factors to migraine or severe headache varied in two studies.

Discussion

Based on this study, common factors, such as obesity and income were identified in the Chinese and NHANES data for men. Occupation was associated with migraine or severe headache in the Chinese population. Race was related to headaches in the NHANES data. We also found that the contribution of individual risk factors to migraine and severe headache varied between the two studies.

According to the NHANES, approximately 16.6% of male participants reported migraine or severe headache; however, this rate was only 9.5% in Chinese men. This is consistent with previous studies indicating that the prevalence of migraine is significantly lower among Asian populations than among European and American ones [21]. Based on the NHANES data, different racial groups were found to be associated with migraine or severe headache. This finding is supported by previous data indicating that Africans, Asians, and Caucasians living in the USA showed different migraine prevalence [22]. The NHANES data indicated that the prevalence of migraine or severe headache in men decreased significantly in the age group of 50–59 years. Some researchers have suggested that hormonal influences may account for the difference in migraine prevalence across the lifespan [23]. Compared with other age groups, the age group of 50–59 years still had a relatively high prevalence of migraine in the Chinese population, as was also revealed in the Korean population [23]. However, the differences in the prevalence in the two datasets should be interpreted carefully, given the fact that the diagnosis of migraine was derived in a different way in the NHANES sample (self-reported) compared with the Chinese sample (diagnosed by neurologist).

We also observed significant differences in the BMI between the Chinese and NHANES data. The prevalence of obesity and being overweight was higher in the NHANES dataset. This remarkable difference in the BMI between the Chinese and American populations may be associated with the differences in genes and dietary habits between the Eastern and Western cultures [24]. Based on the Chinese population study, a positive relationship between obesity and migraine or severe headache was found and was confirmed by study results of the NHANES data. In this study, the same obesity criteria as the WHO BMI criteria were applied to both datasets. The current results suggest a potential role of obesity in migraine or severe headache, which was partly in line with our previous study [25]. Morbid obesity was found to be associated with a two-fold increase in the odds of experiencing migraine in these Chinese men and women [25]. There is increasing evidence that underweight may also contribute to migraine [26, 27]. This finding is further confirmed by the NHANES data study. However, in the Chinese data study, we failed to find a positive association between underweight and migraine, which may be due to the small sample size.

Based on the Chinese data, we found that a higher income, which was not analyzed in our pervious study [13], was negatively correlated with migraine or severe headache. This finding was consistent with the results from the NHANES data. Due to the nature of the cross-sectional study, it is not possible to establish a causal relationship between a low economic status and migraine or severe headache. Two possible explanations were suggested. Migraine or severe headache may result in disability, affecting work and occupational achievement, which may lead to a low income [28, 29]. Conversely, a low income can create more pressure and stress in life, and it is known that stressful events occur more frequently in lower income groups. Simultaneously, a low income can limit the access to medical care and healthy diet, and result in less money spending on treatment [23, 28, 29]. This would result in more frequent headaches. Based on the Chinese study, migraine or severe headache was related to the occupation for men. Such similar results have not been reported before. It was indicated the effect of migraine on work–family conflict was different between genders, and men with migraine demonstrated a stronger association with work–family conflict [30]. Therefore, the assessment of the relations between occupations and migraine according to sexes may be necessary.

The role of marital status in migraine remains a controversial topic. In the current study, there were no significant associations between the marital status and migraine or severe headache, which was consistent with our previous study [13]. However, for the limited cases in the subgroup, we collapsed the groups of widowed and divorced individuals in the Chinese population, and the subgroups of marital status in the NHANES data were also collapsed. It has been shown that being widowed is an independent predictor of depression. Unhappiness and increased pressure may be associated with headaches [31]. Further studies with larger population samples are needed to confirm the association between marital status and migraine.

We employed machine learning as a complement and extension of the logistic analysis. While LR provides ORs indicating the direction and strength of associations, it does not inherently indicate how well the model might perform or how stable it would be when presented with new data. By applying six different ML models with 5-fold cross-validation, we aimed to systematically compare the predictive performance of different algorithms (via AUC, accuracy, etc.). The finding that LR demonstrated the best stability and generalizability is a valuable insight, suggesting that LR is the most reliable choice for building a predictive model based on these social factors. To enhance interpretability and feature importance ranking, the SHAP analysis was used. The SHAP analysis revealed differences in the contribution of risk factors between the two studies, which may imply the necessity to explore these factors across different countries.

Conclusion

This study, which was based on a large sample population, focused on migraine or severe headache in men. We identified common factors, including obesity and income, related to migraine in different countries. Additionally, different associations between factors and migraine or severe headache were observed in the two countries. Given the different cultures and races, it is necessary to study the risk factors related to migraine in different countries.

Limitation

First, this was a cross-sectional study; therefore, we cannot conclude the causal link between those factors and the migraine or severe headache. For example, the inherent constraints of designs preclude causality, leaving ambiguity in whether the income exacerbates migraines via chronic stress and healthcare barriers or vice versa through disability-induced economic fallout—this led to a bidirectional conundrum, which was not resolved in this study. Second, the NHANES data may have been influenced by the selection bias and lost data. Some other potential confounders such as the psychological comorbidities and lifestyle factors were not included in the current study. The limited cases in the subgroup would influence the precision and stability of the results with LR and machine learning models. However, the six traditional machine learning algorithms we selected are well-established and have been successfully applied to small- and medium-sized datasets. Third, the diagnostic criteria of migraine or severe headache were different between the two countries in this study, potentially inflating the migraine prevalence estimates in NHANES and artifactual differences. In addition, the studies were conducted at least 15 years ago; thus. the findings may not fully seize contemporary shifts, such as the changes in social and occupational structures caused by economic development, the rising obesity trends in Asia, and evolving marital norms amid global urbanization. Future longitudinal follow-ups of the studies are warranted.
