Walled-off necrosis with portal vein fistula and arterial bleeding successfully treated without collection drainage: A case report
Authors: Luciana Coelho Marques, Arthur Petrillo Bellintani, Roberto Rasslan, Carla Micaele de Freitas, Natalia Kimie de Faria Fujiwara, Vitor Matheus Silva, Felipe Kfouri, Sergio Henrique Bastos Damous, Carlos Augusto Menegozzo, Edivaldo Massazo Utiyama, Manoel de Souza Rocha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic portal vein fistula (PPVF) is a rare condition, often a complication of chronic pancreatitis, involving communication between a pancreatic collection and the portal vein. Early diagnosis is crucial to prevent severe complications, including portal vein thrombosis and hemorrhage. This case report presents a 62-year-old man with HIV and congestive heart failure who developed a PPVF following walled-off necrosis due to acute pancreatitis. Diagnosis of this complication resulted from abdominal pain secondary to splenic artery branch bleeding into the collection, which was treated by embolization. Despite systemic complications, the patient recovered without the need for walled off necrosis drainage. This case underscores the challenge and the importance of individualized treatment for PPVFs and its complications.Keywords: Fistula, Pancreatitis, Walled off necrosis, Portal vein, Splenic artery, Pancreatic duct

Introduction

Walled-off necrosis may occur in up to 10% of cases of necrotizing acute pancreatitis. The majority of these collections are typically localized on the body and the tail of the pancreas. Approximately half of the patients develop symptoms such as infection, abdominal pain, gastric or biliary obstruction, fistula formation to adjacent structures, and hemorrhage resulting from blood vessel erosion. Current consensus guidelines advocate for drainage only in symptomatic cases. However, there remains considerable reluctance to adopt a strictly conservative approach for asymptomatic patients with large collections, primarily due to the risk of severe complications, particularly hemorrhage [1,2].

Pancreatic portal vein fistula (PPVF), defined as an abnormal communication between the pancreatic ductal system and the portal vein, is a rare complication of pancreatitis. The main theory regarding its pathogenesis involves leakage of pancreatic enzymes and inflammation secondary to an acute pancreatitis episode, progressing toward erosion into the portal vein wall. Typical patient demographics include males in their 50s with a history of chronic pancreatitis and alcohol use, usually presenting with abdominal pain. The diagnosis of PPVF is typically established through imaging modalities, such as computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging [3,4].

Current literature regarding treatment options for PPVF remains inconclusive, and therapeutic strategies often vary depending on institutional expertise and individual surgeon experience. In this case report, we present a patient with multiple comorbidities and high surgical risk who developed a PPVF and was successfully managed through non drainage treatment, resulting in favorable clinical outcomes.

Case report

We present a 62-year-old male with a history of acute biliary pancreatitis. Eight years earlier, he underwent a subtotal cholecystectomy, but continued to experience recurrent bouts of pancreatitis due to persistent stones in the infundibulum. Then, he developed a walled-off necrosis with no symptoms for which clinical observation was proposed (Fig. 1A). Sixteen months after the collection follow-up, he presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset and non-localized abdominal pain associated with nausea and vomiting. A tomography scan was performed, revealing an increase in the size of the previously known collection with signs of active bleeding originating from a branch of the splenic artery (Fig. 1B). The bleeding was visualized during the arterial phase and became more evident in the delayed phase, corresponding to an increase in attenuation from 40 HU on the precontrast images to 278 HU on the postcontrast images. Additionally, the PPVF was identified, showing the communication between the walled-off necrosis and the portal vein with extensive portal vein thrombosis (Fig. 1C).

The patient was referred to arteriography. The splenic artery was patent, with a proximal pancreatic branch exhibiting irregular wall thickening/irregularity in the topography of the pancreatic pseudocyst (Fig. 2A). A selective embolization of the splenic artery branches was initially attempted. However, after multiple unsuccessful attempts at superselective catheterization, non-selective embolization was performed (Fig. 2B). Control arteriography demonstrated proximal occlusion of the splenic artery, with distal revascularization via short gastric collaterals and no contrast filling of the proximal pancreatic branch.

(A) The splenic artery was patent, with a proximal pancreatic branch exhibiting irregular wall thickening/irregularity in the topography of the pancreatic pseudocyst (arrow). (B) Nonselective splenic arterial embolization (arrow). (C) Location of embolization (*) and the splenic artery filling distally through the short vessels (arrow).

However, after persistent bleeding supplied by collateral vessels (Fig. 2C), definitive management was achieved through nonselective embolization of the splenic artery.

The patient’s abdominal complaints were effectively managed with simple analgesics. Nevertheless, he subsequently developed a new episode of abdominal pain. A follow-up tomograph scan revealed persistence of PPVF and portal vein thrombosis, but no evidence of ongoing bleeding (Fig. 1D). Conservative approach was maintained, and anticoagulation therapy for the portal vein thrombosis was initiated. A bloodstream infection was caused by Staphylococcus lugdunensis. Although this bacteria is not commonly associated with pancreatic fluid collection infection, no other evident source for the infection's etiology was identified. This infection led to organ dysfunction worsened by baseline comorbidities. At this point, endoscopic drainage of the collection was discussed. However, due to the disfavorable anatomy and extensive collateral circulation, the decision was made to pursue exclusive antibiotic treatment. After 45 days of hospitalization, the patient fully recovered without sequelae and was discharged.

During outpatient follow-up, the patient continued to show clinical improvement. Control tomography 3 months after discharge demonstrated a reduction in the walled-off necrosis and significant improvement of the PPVF, with the communication between the portal vein and the necrotic area becoming less apparent (Fig. 3). The patient remains asymptomatic in 1 year of follow up.

Discussion

A fistula between the pancreatic duct and the portal venous system is a rare and potentially fatal condition, and the largest and most comprehensive review to date identified only 74 cases reported in the literature. In this series, hemorrhagic complications were observed in up to 17% of cases, and the overall mortality rate of 16% occurred within this same subset of patients who developed hemorrhagic complications [2].

The highest mortality risks in PPVF are associated with 2 critical complications: massive hemorrhage from the portal vein into the pancreatic collection or systemic lipotoxicity, likely due to the translocation of pancreatic enzymes into the systemic circulation, often presenting with panniculitis and polyarthritis [2,5].

There was no significant correlation between treatment modality and outcome, reinforcing the notion that invasive intervention—when warranted—should be carefully indicated and individualized [2,6].

Patients’ symptoms and anatomical assessment of the collection, fistula and pancreatic duct must be factored into the decision-making process. Ng et al. [7] provided an algorithm for evaluating patients with pancreatic portal vein fistula, beginning with an assessment of portal vein patency by Doppler ultrasonography. A patent portal vein allows for consideration of portal vein stenting, which can simultaneously prevent thrombosis and block communication between the vein and the pancreatic collection. If the portal vein is not patent, pancreatic duct stenting is considered dependent on the communication of the collection and the main pancreatic duct if assessed. In case of failure, operative treatment can be proposed [7,8].

The decision to whether to drain or not the collections resembles cases without PPVT, with a few nuances. Non drainage management presents as one of the primary strategies, especially in clinically stable patients without signs of infection, active bleeding, or significant obstruction of the biliary or gastrointestinal tracts. Thus, as in the case described, this approach is particularly suitable for asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic pancreatic collections with stable dimensions and no evidence of imminent complications, allowing for spontaneous resolution through gradual reabsorption. Regardless of the chosen strategy, therapeutic decisions should be individualized and guided by a specialized and experienced team, taking into account the anatomic and pathophysiologic complexity of pancreatic portal vein fistula and the substantial risks associated with its natural progression [[9], [10], [11]].

Our case reinforces the notion that a more conservative approach can be feasible, especially in a patient with multiple comorbidities and previous surgical procedures. Our patient presented with bleeding; however, the source was not the fistula but rather the splenic artery, which was amenable to treatment by embolization.

Conclusion

While the literature remains limited, available evidence suggests that non drainage management can be safe and effective in selected patients, particularly those who are clinically stable and have significant comorbidities that increase invasive procedure risks. This case reinforces the importance of individualized and continuous evaluation, incorporating detailed anatomical imaging, hemodynamic evaluation, and the presence of complications when determining the most appropriate therapeutic strategy.

As demonstrated, non drainage treatment may not only offer a viable alternative to invasive procedures but can also lead to favorable outcomes when closely monitored. Future studies and cumulative case series will be essential to refine risk stratification and guide optimal management of this complex condition. Until then, vigilance, early recognition, and a tailored approach remain the cornerstones of effective care in patients with pancreatic portal vein fistula.
