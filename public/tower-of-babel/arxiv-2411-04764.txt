Abstract
. Let n n be a positive integer. The Diophantine equation n ⁡ ( x 1 + x 2 + ⋯ + x n ) = x 1 ​ x 2 ​ … ​ x n n(x_{1}+x_{2}+\dots+x_{n})=x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{n} , 1 ≤ x 1 ≤ x 2 ≤ ⋯ ≤ x n 1\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\dots\leq x_{n} is called Erdős’s last equation. 1.
Introduction

On 19 August 1996, a month before his death, Paul Erdős formulated the following problem in a letter written to Richard Guy (see
[
2
]
):

"Do you know anything about the equation

(1)

n(x_{1}+x_{2}+\dots+x_{n})=x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{n}

1\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\dots\leq x_{n}
, where the
x_{i}
are positive integers?
Denote the number of solution in
n
by
f(n)
;
f(n)>n^{\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
seems to be true? …
"

The equation (
1
) is called Erdős last equation. It is easy to see that for
x_{1}=\dots=x_{n-2}=1
, equation (
1
) is equivalent to
n(2n-2)=(x_{n-1}-n)(x_{n}-n)
, therefore
f(n)\geq\lceil\frac{1}{2}d(2n^{2}-2n)\rceil>0
, where
d(.)
is the number of divisors function.

A solution of equation (
1
) is

x_{1}=\dots=x_{n-2}=1,\ x_{n-1}=n+1,\ x_{n}=2n^{2}-n.

Shiu
[
5
]
proved that in fact, for any solution of (
1
) we have
x_{n}\leq 2n^{2}-n
.
Denote by
g(n)
the least possible value of
x_{n}
for equation (
1
).
The solution

x_{1}=\dots=x_{n-2}=1,\ x_{n-1}=2n,\ x_{n}=3n-2

implies that
g(n)\leq 3n-2
.
For
n=m^{2}
, Shiu
[
5
]
gave the solution

x_{1}=\dots x_{m^{2}-4}=1,\ x_{m^{2}-3}=x_{m^{2}-2}=x_{m^{2}-1}=m,\ x_{m^{2}}=m+4,

which yields

(2)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{g(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\leq 1.

The aim of this paper is twofold. In one hand, we study the function
g(n)
(the possible minimum value of
x_{n}

in (
1
)) and derive two results concerning it (see Theorems
1
and
3
).
On the other hand, we deduce an effective finiteness result on equation (
1
) (see Theorem
2
),
and by combining this with the LLL algorithm, we solve completely equation (
1
) for
3\leq x_{n}\leq 10

(see Theorem
4
).

Our first theorem improves the upper bound given in (
2
).

Theorem 1
.

Consider equation (
1
). Then, we have
\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{g(n)}{\sqrt[3]{n}}\leq 1
.

Observe that
n=1=x_{n}
is always a solution to (
1
). We will call such a solution
trivial
.
In what follows, we concentrate to the case where in equation (
1
) the term
x_{n}
is fixed.

If
x_{n}=1
, then since
1\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\ldots\leq x_{n}
, we have
x_{1}=\ldots=x_{n}=1
and
equation (
1
) reduces to
n^{2}=1
, yielding
n=1
. This shows that for
x_{n}=1
the only solution to
(
1
) is the trivial solution
n=1=x_{n}
.

If
x_{n}=2
, then
x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{n}=2^{m}
. Hence
n=2^{u}
and
2^{u}<x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}\leq 2\cdot 2^{u}
, but
x_{1}+x_{2}+\dots+x_{n}=2^{v}
implies that
2^{v}=2^{u+1}
, that is
x_{i}=2
for every
1\leq i\leq n
. Hence
2n^{2}=2^{n}
, which is impossible for
n\geq 2
.
It follows that for
x_{n}=2
the only solution to the equation (
1
) is the trivial one.

In what follows, we assume that
x_{n}\geq 3
is fixed and for
1,2,\ldots,x_{n}
let
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{x_{n}}
denote the number of ones, twos,
\ldots,x_{n}
s among the numbers
1\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\ldots\leq x_{n}
. Then, we clearly have that

(3)

a_{1}+\ldots+a_{x_{n}}=n\ \textrm{with}\ a_{1}\geq 0,\ldots,a_{x_{n}-1}\geq 0,a_{x_{n}}\geq 1

and

(4)

x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}=a_{1}+2a_{2}+\ldots+x_{n}a_{x_{n}}.

Thus, the combination of (
3
) and (
4
) shows that equation (
1
) can be rewritten as

(5)

n(n+a_{2}+2a_{3}+\ldots+(x_{n}-1)a_{x_{n}})=2^{a_{2}}3^{a_{3}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{x_{n}}},

where the nonnegative integer unknowns
(n,a_{2},a_{3},\ldots,a_{x_{n}})
satisfy

(6)

n-(a_{2}+a_{3}+\ldots+a_{x_{n}})=a_{1}\geq 0\ \textrm{with}\ n\geq 1\ \textrm{and}\ x_{n}\geq 3.

Let
k:=\pi(x_{n})
, (where
\pi(x)
counts the number of prime numbers less than or equal to some real number
x
)
and put
2=p_{1}<p_{2}<\ldots<p_{k}=p_{\pi(x_{n})}
. The product
2^{a_{2}}3^{a_{3}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{x_{n}}}
on the right hand side of
(
5
) can be rewritten as
2^{a_{2}}3^{a_{3}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{x_{n}}}=p_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{b_{k}}
,
where

(7)

b_{j}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor x_{n}/p_{j}\rfloor}{a_{p_{j}i}}\cdot{\nu_{p_{j}}(a_{p_{j}i})},\quad(1\leq j\leq k),

where, for a prime
p
and a nonzero integer
m
,
\nu_{p}(m)
denotes the largest nonnegative integer
u
such that
p^{u}\mid m
.
By putting
b:=b_{1}+\ldots+b_{k}
, relation (
7
) implies that

(8)

b\geq a_{2}+\ldots+a_{x_{n}}.

Since
2^{a_{2}}3^{a_{3}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{x_{n}}}=p_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{b_{k}}
, equation (
5
) reduces to

(9)

n(n+a_{2}+2a_{3}+\ldots+(x_{n}-1)a_{x_{n}})=p_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{b_{k}}.

First, we derive an explicit upper bound for
b=b_{1}+\cdots+b_{k}
depending only on
x_{n}
in equation (
9
).

Theorem 2
.

Consider equation (
9
) in integer unknowns
(n,a_{2},a_{3},\ldots,a_{x_{n}})
satisfying
(
6
) and (
7
). Then for
b=b_{1}+\cdots+b_{k}
one has

where

is any upper bound for the largest integer solution of the inequality

(10)

b<(2.8\cdot 30^{k+3}\cdot k^{4.5})\cdot(\log{p_{2}})\cdots(\log{p_{k}})(1+\log{b})+\frac{2\log{((x_{n}-1)b)}}{\log{p_{1}}}.

Remark 1
.

The combination of Theorem
2
and (
8
) shows that

(11)

\max\{a_{2},\ldots,a_{x_{n}}\}\leq a_{2}+\ldots+a_{x_{n}}\leq b\leq B_{0}.

Thus, (
3
), (
9
) and (

) give

Hence, (

) and (

) show that for given

, Theorem

provides an effective upper bound depending only on
x_{n}
for all possible solutions
(n,a_{2},a_{3},\ldots,a_{n})
of equations (

) and (
5
). Thus, for given
x_{n}\geq 3
all solutions

of equation (

) are bounded, as well.

The next theorem provides a quantitative lower bound for

.

Theorem 3
.

Consider equation (
1
). Then, we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{g(n)}{\frac{\log\log n\cdot\log\log\log n}{\log\log\log\log n}}\geq 1.

We note that Theorem

It is clear that from the solutions of (

Lemma 1
.

If

Following Lenstra, Lenstra and Lovász
[
3
]
, we recall the definition of an
LLL-reduced basis
of a lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For a basis
\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}\}
of the lattice
\mathcal{L}
the Gram-Schmidt procedure provides an orthogonal basis
\{b_{1}^{*},b_{2}^{*},\ldots,b_{n}^{*}\}
of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given inductively by

b_{i}^{*}=b_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}{\mu_{ij}b_{j}^{*}}\quad(1\leq i\leq n),\quad\mu_{i,j}=\frac{\langle b_{i},b_{j}^{*}\rangle}{\langle b_{j}^{*},b_{j}^{*}\rangle}\quad(1\leq j<i\leq n).

We call a basis
\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}\}
for a lattice
\mathcal{L}

if

\|\mu_{i,j}\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\quad(1\leq j<i\leq n)

and

\|b_{i}^{*}+\mu_{i,i-1}b_{i-1}^{*}\|^{2}\geq\frac{3}{4}\|b_{i-1}^{*}\|^{2}\quad(1<i\leq n),

where
\|.\|
denotes the ordinary Euclidean length.

Proof of Theorem
3

Proof.

Let us suppose that in the equation (
1
) we have

x_{n}\leq\frac{\log\log n\cdot\log\log\log n}{\log\log\log\log n}.

By Prime number theory,
p_{l}=(1+o(1))l\log l
as
l\to\infty
. Hence
\log\log p_{l}=(1+o(1))\log\log l
and

\log p_{1}\log p_{2}\dots\log p_{k}=e^{(1+o(1))\sum_{l=2}^{k}\log\log l}=e^{(1+o(1))k\log\log k}

as
k\to\infty
, so in Theorem
2
the positive integer
B_{0}
can be chosen as
e^{(1+o(1))k\log\log k}
. It follows from (
12
) that

\frac{(2+o(1))\log n}{\log\log\log n}\leq\frac{2\log n}{\log x_{n}}\leq B_{0}\leq e^{(1+o(1))k\log\log k}.

A routine calculation gives that
k\geq(1+o(1))\frac{\log\log n}{\log\log\log\log n}
. By Prime number theory again, we get

x_{n}\geq p_{k}\geq(1+o(1))\frac{\log\log n\cdot\log\log\log n}{\log\log\log\log n},

which completes the proof of Theorem
3
.
