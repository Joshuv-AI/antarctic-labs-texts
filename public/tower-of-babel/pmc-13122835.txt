Data describing planned routes and driven routes from a last-mile delivery vehicle routing problem
Authors: Anna Konovalenko, Lars Magnus Hvattum, Kim Aleksander Hammer Iversen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis dataset provides real-world last-mile delivery routes from a logistics company, offering valuable resources for research in route optimization and logistics. The dataset addresses a substantial gap between theoretical route planning and routing performed by drivers who often do not follow the proposed routes. The dataset comprises 19,497 pairs of routes, including both planned routes, generated by human dispatchers employing heuristic optimization tools, and the routes that the drivers eventually followed in practice. The routes were completed by 400 unique drivers, covering 10,864 unique locations. In the dataset, the routes are presented as sequences of stops, where each stop is a separate row in the presented datafile. The dataset includes detailed information on routes, stops and drivers’ identifiers, timing, and distance measurements. It allows for a deeper analysis of driver behavior patterns, factors of route deviations, and their impact on delivery performance.Keywords: Logistics, Tacit knowledge, Driver behavior, Transportation optimization

Background

Route optimization is the complex problem in last-mile delivery [1]. Logistics companies are using specialized decision tools based on optimization algorithms to construct routes for drivers while minimizing costs and distances. However, in practice, drivers often deviate from the planned route based on their knowledge gained through personal experience [3,4]. While this knowledge is difficult to capture and formalize, integrating it into route planning decision tools can improve route optimization and better align theoretical planning with real-world implementation.

This dataset [2] was created to provide examples of how the actual visiting stop sequences differ from the given planned sequences in last-mile delivery operations. The dataset presents delivery operations from a logistics company operating in Norway and Denmark. By capturing relevant contextual route information and preferences, the dataset enables the analysis of route deviations and their underlying factors.

Data Description

The dataset is stored as an Excel file, where a row represents a separate stop in one of the routes. Each stop has two sequence indices: a planned index assigned by decision tools, and an actual index reflecting where the stop occurred in the driver's route. Stops within each route are arranged in ascending order based on their planned delivery sequence. In the file, routes are arranged chronologically by their first arrival time (e.g., Route 2′s first arrival is later than Route 1′s first arrival). Additionally, the dataset includes driver identifier and other supplementary details.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

This section describes the methodology used to generate some of the columns and to encode sensitive timing and location information in the dataset. We present the mathematical formulation for transforming raw delivery data into anonymized, relative measurements while preserving the essential relationships between stops, times, and distances.

1. Time encoding

Let S = {s0, s1, …, sn-1} where each sᵢ represents a stop in a single route, and n is the total number of stops in this route:

•ta(s): actual arrival timestamp at stop s∈S (Arrived Time Column).

ta(s): actual arrival timestamp at stop s∈S (Arrived Time Column).

•te(s): earliest allowed arrival timestamp at stop s∈S (Earliest Time column).

te(s): earliest allowed arrival timestamp at stop s∈S (Earliest Time column).

•tl(s): latest allowed arrival timestamp at stop s∈S (Latest Time column).

tl(s): latest allowed arrival timestamp at stop s∈S (Latest Time column).

To protect sensitive timing information, all times are converted to minutes from a reference point, which is

tref=min({ta(s),te(s),tl(s):s∈S}).

For each stop s∈S, the time values are calculated as: Arrived Time(s) = ta(s)−tref, Earliest Time(s) = te(s)−tref, Latest Time(s) = tl(s)−tref.

The distance calculations provide information about the execution of the route. For Stop ID 4 in the route of Table 1 the DistanceP is 0 because it follows Stop ID 3 in the planned sequence, and both stops share the same address. However, DistanceA for Stop ID 4 is 13.95 because the driver actually visited this stop first (IndexA is 1), arriving directly from the depot. Similarly, for Stop ID 3, DistanceA is 0 because the driver visited it immediately after Stop ID 4 at the same address, while DistanceP is 2.49 because according to the plan it should have followed Stop ID 2, which is at a different location.
