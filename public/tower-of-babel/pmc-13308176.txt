Neutrophils blend innate and adaptive immunity in early breast milk
Authors: Gema Gomez-Casado, Natalia Saldaña-García, Antonio Cantarero-Cuenca, Andrés Gonzalez-Jimenez, Guillermo Paz-López, Patricia Garcia-Hurtado, Francisco J. Tinahones, Ernesto González-Mesa, Almudena Ortega-Gomez

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast milk not only is the most optimal source of nutrition for newborns but also provides crucial immune support. While there is extensive evidence highlighting the health benefits of breastfeeding, the scientific understanding behind many of these advantages remains limited. The immune cell fraction of breast milk has been poorly studied, and characterization of this essential component may be crucial to shed light on the subject. Here, we investigate the population of neutrophils in breast milk to unveil their phenotype and functions.MethodsNeutrophils in human breast milk were characterized by flow cytometry, and their phenotype was contrasted with that of their circulating counterparts using a paired approach. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), intracellular granule immunocytochemistry, and Giemsa staining were employed to reveal their intracellular structure. In vitro neutrophil incubations were performed to assess the phenotypic modifications of circulating neutrophils in a breast milk environment. Functional assays were carried out to assess the activity of neutrophils and their potential impact on adaptive immunity. Finally, a proteomic approach was used on isolated neutrophils and their supernatant after cultivation to define the protein-based mechanisms involved in their biology. Paired t-tests and one- and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used for statistical analyses.ResultsIn the breast milk from the first 5 days (known as colostrum), neutrophils represent most of the cellular compartment. Colostrum neutrophils present a cell surface signature markedly different from circulating neutrophils with a clear CXCR4+CD62L−CXCR2mid phenotype and important differences in other markers. These differences are partially phenocopied upon incubation of blood neutrophils with colostrum whey, implying that cell transition occurs owing to the niche environment. Additionally, colostrum neutrophils exhibit reduced cytoplasmic granule content and altered canonical functions. Proteomic analyses reveal that the most important processes in colostrum neutrophils are related to antigen binding and humoral response pathways.ConclusionsThis study provides evidence of a unique population of neutrophils in early breast milk connected to adaptive immunity, which may hold the key to understanding the mechanisms behind the immunological benefits of breastfeeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-026-00920-0.

Background

Breast milk is a unique means to connects the mother and child after birth, serving not only as nourishment but also as the newborn’s immunological supporting vehicle. Colostrum (breast milk until 5 days post-partum), in particular, is considered to be an immunologically enriched fluid that effectively protects newborns, compensating for their immature and slow-responding immune systems.

Children who receive longer periods of breastfeeding have lower infectious morbidity and mortality rates, and higher intelligence, compared with those who are breastfed for shorter periods or not breastfed [1, 2]. This disparity continues into later stages of life. Increasing evidence indicates that breastfeeding also provides protection against future overweight/obesity and diabetes [3, 4]. In addition, nursing mothers experience reduced breast and ovarian cancer rates as well as risk of diabetes [5]. Accordingly, the World Health Organization (WHO) advises that infants be exclusively breastfed for the first 6 months of life [6].

Breast milk composition has been extensively studied and reviewed in terms of nutrients, microbiome, and immunological factors [7–9]. However, the mechanisms by which breastfeeding exert most of its beneficial effects are largely unknown. One aspect of breast milk that is insufficiently examined is its cellular component. Breast milk contains significant numbers of living cells, including leukocytes, which adapt as the milk matures to meet the infant’s needs. Thus, colostrum contains higher levels of immune cells and immunological factors, such as immunoglobulins, than mature milk, reflecting the greater need for protection in the immediate post-partum period [10]. Interestingly, neutrophils have been identified in both colostrum and later stages of lactation, indicating a role for these cells in breast milk [10].

In circulation, neutrophils are the most represented leukocytes. They have traditionally been regarded as reactive cells against acute and, more recently, chronic inflammation [11]. However, recent evidence indicates that neutrophils are also relevant players in maintaining homeostasis [12, 13]. Heterogeneity of neutrophils has been under discussion over the last decades, and their phenotypic and functional diversity seems nowadays acknowledged [14–16]. Among their intrinsic molecular features, neutrophils possess a high number of cytoplasmic granules, loaded with ready-to-use proteins with defensive and pro-inflammatory properties [17]. Notably, changes in the granule composition of neutrophils have been shown to arise from an inherent cellular mechanism governed by circadian rhythms, which lessens the strength of their inflammatory reaction [18].

While the immune protection provided by maternal immunoglobulins transferred from mother to child through breast milk is well established [19, 20], the immune cell populations in this fluid have been poorly studied. Here, we aim to decipher the nature of human neutrophils in colostrum by performing a comparison with their blood counterpart at the cellular and molecular level. We reveal that neutrophils are a notable population in colostrum, with a CXCR4+CD62L−CXCR2mid phenotype and reduced typical cytoplasmic granule content and altered canonical functions. We demonstrate that in vitro incubation of circulating neutrophils with colostrum whey triggers a shift in their surface molecule profile toward colostrum neutrophils, suggesting that the breast milk environment determines their phenotype. Furthermore, we show that the protein content and subsequent cell functions of colostrum neutrophils are strongly related to antigen binding and humoral response, as opposed to blood neutrophils, which are naturally centered on Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NET) formation and serin protease enzymatic processes [21]. In addition, humoral response-related proteins are released to the medium by colostrum neutrophils. Thus, we present a neutrophil niche in early breast milk with unique features, which might be key to understanding the immunological support passed from mother to child.

Methods

Study samples

A total of 16 female healthy volunteers aged between 22 and 40 years giving birth at term at the Regional University Hospital of Málaga participated in the study. Inclusion criteria were: full-term deliveries and mother age between 18 and 40 years. Exclusion criteria were multiple delivery, delivery complications that compromise the well-being of the newborn, congenital abnormalities of the baby, pathologies of the mother (hypertension, HELLP syndrome, thyroid alterations, or nephropathies) and drugs or alcohol abuse history. Additionally, samples from three mothers after preterm delivery (32–34 weeks of gestation) were obtained. The samples were collected and analyzed after informed consent was obtained from the participants, and the study was approved by the ethics committee (Comité de Ética de la Investigación Provincial de Málaga), which acts on the Helsinki Declaration. Peripheral blood and colostrum samples were obtained withing the first 5 days post-partum between 8:00 and 10:00 am, at fasting. To compare different stages of milk maturation, samples of colostrum, transitional milk, and mature milk were collected from 3 mothers at 3, 15, and 30 days postpartum.

Sample processing

A minimum of 0.5 ml of colostrum was manually obtained into sterile syringes, while 2 ml of peripheral blood was drawn into EDTA tubes. Both samples were kept on ice until processed. C-reactive protein (CRP) determinations were performed at the hospital laboratory facilities. The cellular fraction of colostrum samples was obtained after centrifugation at 300g during 10 min. Maternal cell-free milk fraction was further centrifuged at 10,000g for 10 min to separate the microbiota component and extract the milk whey, which was immediately stored at –80 ºC. Plasma samples were obtained after centrifugation of peripheral blood at 2,000g for 5 min at 4 ºC. The maternal cell pellet was then washed with HBSS buffer (Gibco) supplemented with bovine serum albumin (BSA) 0.05% and EDTA 0.03 mM (Sigma), to remove excess of lipids from milk. Blood samples were subjected to erythrocyte lysis and washed to obtain the leukocyte pellet. Next, colostrum and blood cell pellets were used for neutrophil isolation. Blood CRP levels of the participants were measured at the Regional University Hospital of Malaga as part of routine laboratory analyses.

Flow cytometry

Samples of colostrum and blood (25 μl) were washed with supplemented HBSS buffer to remove the milk fat fraction, while blood was incubated with lysis buffer (NH4Cl 155 mM, KHCO3 10 mM, EDTA 0.1 mM) to eliminate erythrocytes. Cell pellets were stained with a cocktail of fluorescence-conjugated antibodies containing CD45 (Biolegend, cat. no. 368514), CD11b (Biolegend, cat. no. 101216), CXCR4 (Biolegend, cat. no. 306506), CD36 (Biolegend, cat. no. 336203), CD64 (Biolegend, cat. no. 305027), CD14 (Biolegend, cat. no. 325622), CD66b (Invitrogen, cat. no. 17-0666-42), CD62L (Invitrogen, cat. no. 47-0629-42), HLA-DR (Invitrogen, cat. no. 61-9952-42), and CXCR2 (BD Biosciences, cat. no. 744197). All antibodies were used at the dilution recommended by the manufacturer. To determine absolute cell numbers, counting beads (Invitrogen) were added to the sample prior acquisition. Stained samples were analyzed in a CytoFlex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter).

Neutrophil isolation

Neutrophils were isolated from pellets of blood and milk samples using a magnetic column separation method. Briefly, cell pellets were incubated with a recombinant biotin anti-CD66b antibody and anti-biotin beads (Miltenyi Biotec), following manufacturer’s instructions. CD66b+ cells were obtained after eluting negative populations. The purity of isolated neutrophils was measured by flow cytometry, reaching 97.6 ± 1.5% in blood and 97.7 ± 0.7% in milk samples (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). After cell counting, the population of CD66+ cells was employed for further analyses.

Cell staining and immunocytochemistry

Neutrophils isolated from colostrum and blood were resuspended in Hanks’ buffer (10% HBSS, 0.6% BSA, 0.1 mM EDTA) and left to sediment on a glass coverslip for 20 min at room temperature. Next, the preparations were fixed with a 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution (Sigma) for 15 min. Giemsa stainings were performed as follows (Sigma): Samples were hydrated with MiliQ water and stained by two serial incubations with undiluted and 1:1 diluted Giemsa solution for 10 and 5 min, respectively. After washing, the samples were mounted with Neo-Mount mounting medium (Sigma). Myeloperoxidase (MPO) immunocytochemistry assays were performed on neutrophils isolated from blood and colostrum of five subjects after permeabilization with Triton X-100 (0.2%) for 10 min. Briefly, samples were blocked with 1% BSA during 30 min. Then, samples were incubated with a goat primary antibody against MPO (R&D Systems) overnight at 4 ºC and subsequently with an anti-goat Alexa Fluor 647 secondary antibody (Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature. The nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (1 μg/ml) (Sigma). The acquisition of the images was carried out with a Leica SP5 HyD Confocal Microscope with 0.5 μm between stacks. Nuclei segmentation was assessed by quantifying the lobuli of cells stained with DAPI. A total of 3–5 subjects and 10–15 cells/subject were quantified.

Transmission electron microscopy

The pellet of neutrophils from blood and colostrum was resuspended in 2% formaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer (pH 7.4) and fixed overnight at 4 °C. After washing with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB), cells were embedded in gelatin, concentrated by centrifugation at 600g, and further fixed. Gelatin blocks were trimmed into 1–2-mm3 pieces.

Postfixation was carried out with 1% osmium tetroxide in PB buffer, followed by graded acetone dehydration (30–90%). During dehydration, samples were stained with 1% uranyl acetate in 50% acetone at 4 °C. The process continued with absolute acetone and embedding in low-viscosity Spurr resin. Samples were polymerized at 60–70 °C for 24 h.

Resin blocks were trimmed. Semithin sections (500–800 nm) stained with toluidine blue identified the area of interest, and ultrathin sections (100 nm) were prepared. Sections on copper grids were stained with 0.08 M lead citrate and imaged using an FEI Tecnai G2 20 Twin Transmission Electron Microscope (Thermo Scientific) at 120 kV.

In vitro neutrophil assays

Phagocytosis

Blood and colostrum samples were collected from five healthy mothers within the first 5 days post-partum. Neutrophils from both samples were isolated using magnetic separation as described above and incubated with pHrodo Staphylococcus aureus Bioparticles (Thermo Fisher Scientific), previously opsonized with autologous blood serum, for 1 h at 37 ºC. Following incubation with pHrodo particles, cells were centrifuged at 300g for 5 min and supernatant was discarded. Samples were subsequently analyzed on a CytoFlex Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and quantified as the percentage of positive cells.

Migration

Blood and colostrum samples were obtained from five healthy mothers within first 5 days after delivery, and neutrophils were isolated by magnetic column separation as described above. Migration assay was performed using 3 μm HTS Transwell 96-well plates (Corning). Neutrophils were resuspended in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI) supplemented with 1% BSA, Ca2+, Mg2+ (1mM, Sigma), and 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (10 mM, Thermo Fisher Scientific). A total of 1 × 105 neutrophils were added at the upper chamber, while 200 μl of either medium (CTL) or medium containing N-formyl-Met-Leu-Phe (fMLP) (10nM, Sigma) was added to the lower chamber. Then, cells were incubated 30 min at 37 ºC. After incubation, the upper chamber was removed and the content of the lower chamber were collected, following gentle scraping of the bottom surface, and analyzed on a CytoFlex Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter).

Blood neutrophil incubation with plasma and colostrum whey

Blood neutrophils were isolated from eight healthy female donors through magnetic column separation, as described above. Neutrophils from each donor (100,000 cells) were incubated for 1 h at 37 ºC either with colostrum whey or plasma from healthy mother donors. To discard possible artifacts derived from the interaction with heterologous plasma, incubations with autologous plasma were also performed. Heterologous colostrum whey and plasma used for the incubation resulted from the mix of five different healthy donors. After 1 h, the cells were stained with a cocktail of fluorescence-conjugated antibodies containing CD45, CD11b, CD184 (CXCR4), CD36, CD64, CD14 (Biolegend), CD66b, CD62L, HLA-DR (Invitrogen), and CD182 (CXCR2, BD Biosciences). Cell viability after plasma/whey incubation was measured by DAPI staining (1μg/ml) (Sigma). Stained samples were analyzed in a CytoFlex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter).

PMA stimulation of blood and colostrum neutrophils

Blood and colostrum samples were collected from eight healthy mothers within the first 5 days post-partum. Neutrophils were isolated through magnetic column separation, as described above. Blood and colostrum neutrophils (30,000 cells) from every donor were incubated either with medium as a control, RPMI medium (Gibco) with 1% BSA, or with PMA (Sigma) at 100 nM for 1 h at 37 ºC. Next, cell supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 300g for 5 min to discard the cell fraction. Supernatants were stored at –80 ºC until processing for proteomic analyses.

Proteomic analysis

Cell protein extraction and purification

Cell protein from pellets of a minimum of 500,000 isolated blood and colostrum neutrophils was extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Sigma), while cell culture supernatants were centrifuged at 16,000g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. The protein concentration was determined with the Pierce bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and normalized to the same concentration (1 µg/µl).

Gel digestion and peptide extraction

Gel-assisted proteolysis involved trapping protein solutions in a polyacrylamide gel matrix, followed by polymerization and cutting the gel into small cubes [22]. The samples were dehydrated, reduced with DTT, and carbamidomethylated with iodoacetamide. Proteins were digested with trypsin solution and thereafter incubated overnight at 37 °C. Peptides were subsequently extracted, dried, redissolved, sonicated, and centrifuged. Finally, peptide samples were quantified using a NanoDrop™, standardized to 0.5 µg/µl, and prepared for injection.

Analysis by ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled to high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRMS)

The samples were analyzed using an Easy nLC 1200 UHPLC system coupled to a Q-Exactive HF-X quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were separated on a C18 analytical column (PepMap RSLC C18, 2 µm, 100 A, 75 µm × 50 cm, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a gradient of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B) over 165 min at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. External calibration was performed with LTQ Velos electrospray ionization (ESI) positive ion solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific), while internal calibration used the polysiloxane ion signal at 445.120024 m/z. MS1 spectra were acquired at a resolution of 120,000 in the 300–1750 m/z range, followed by data-dependent MS/MS fragmentation of the 20 most intense precursor ions (charge +2 to +5). Fragment ions were generated in a higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) cell and detected in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 30,000. Dynamic exclusion of selected ions was set for 30 s, and automatic gain control was applied to avoid saturation, with limits of 3 × 106 ions for MS and 105 ions for MS2.

Data analysis for protein identification

MS and MS2 spectra were analyzed against the Homo sapiens SwissProt database (version 2024.01.24) using Proteome Discoverer™ 2.5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) platform with the SEQUEST® HT engine. Mass tolerances of 10 ppm and 0.02 Da were applied for precursor and fragment ions, respectively. Trypsin was set as the enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages. Methionine oxidation and N-terminal acetylation were set as variable modifications, while cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as fixed. Peptide spectrum matches (PSM) and protein assignments were validated with the Percolator® algorithm [23], using a strict 1% false discovery rate (FDR). Results included only proteins with at least two unique peptide sequences.

Label-free quantification (LFQ) for differential expression analysis

LFQ was conducted using the Minora feature in Proteome Discoverer™ 2.5, with maximum retention time alignment of 10 min and a signal-to-noise ratio threshold of 5. Abundances were based on precursor ion intensities and normalized to total protein content. Relative protein abundances are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of biological replicates.

Lymphocyte in vitro stimulation

T lymphocytes were isolated from pellets of blood samples by negative selection using the Pan T cell isolation kit through magnetic column separation (Miltenyi Biotec; cat. no. 130-096-535), following manufacturer’s instructions. The purity of isolated lymphocytes was measured by flow cytometry, reaching 98.9 ± 2.4% (Supplementary Fig. 1C). For lymphocyte stimulation, a pool of supernatants derived of unstimulated colostrum or blood neutrophils from five mothers was obtained as explained above, and stored at –80 ºC until used. Lymphocytes were incubated in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific) as the control condition, or in a 1:1 mixture of medium and neutrophil supernatant for the stimulation condition. After 18 h of incubation, cells were washed and the expression levels of HLA-DR (Biolegend, cat. no. 327005), CD25 (Biolegend, cat. no. 302605), and CD69 (Biolegend, cat. no. 310911) were assessed by flow cytometry.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantification of the flow cytometer and immunocytochemistry (granule content) results was performed using FCS Express and ImageJ, respectively. Quantification of MPO granules in immunocytochemistry stainings was performed using the ImageJ plugging “DeconvolutionLab2.” After contrast and threshold standardization, the number of granules per cell were calculated using the “3D ROI Manager” ImageJ plugging [24].

Proteomic data were cleaned by removing samples with missing values and those with underrepresented values. Once the data were cleaned, they were analyzed to verify normality and homoskedasticity using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. This verification showed that less than 5% of the variables exhibited a nonnormal distribution. Subsequently, differences in expression values between blood and milk were analyzed using paired Student’s t-test and ANOVA (specified in the figure legend). These analyses were performed using base R functions. The resulting data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Proteomic graphical representations were generated using specific R packages: the volcano plot was created using the EnhancedVolcano package, the heatmap using ComplexHeatmap, and principal component analyses (PCA) using factoextra and FactoMineR, and relationships between sets were visualized with circlize. The Uniprot package was used to query the cellular localization of the proteins. Significant results were then subjected to functional enrichment analysis to identify common pathways and mechanisms, using the clusterProfiler and enrichplot packages. KEGG pathways were selected on the basis of an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and a fold change threshold, with values above 1 indicating higher expression in milk and values below −1 indicating higher expression in blood. The pathway images were generated in R using the Pathview package form Bioconductor, to which the identified genes were provided as input for visualization. Flow cytometry statistical analyses were carried out with Prism software. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Further statistical details of the data (statistical test, n value, and statistical significance) can be found in the figure legends.

Results

The cell fraction of colostrum contains a prominent neutrophil population.

Colostrum is a boost of nutritional and immunological factors that reinforces the newborn’s defense in the first days of life [25]. Although early studies indicated the presence of leukocyte populations including neutrophils, the cellular compartment of colostrum is poorly explored [10]. Paired flow cytometry analyses evidenced a notable myeloid (CD45+ CD11b+) population in colostrum, with a prominent neutrophil part (CD45+ CD11b+CD66b+, Fig. 1B), comparable to that known in blood samples (Fig. 1C). Interestingly, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) representations of blood and colostrum (Fig. 1D) showed a relatively similar population distribution in both samples. However, colostrum samples presented a negligible population of lymphocytes and a nonleukocyte population, predictively corresponding to epithelial cells, which can be observed in the pie chart representation (Fig. 1E). Although the proportion and total quantification of neutrophils showed significant differences between both samples (Fig. 1F, G), a substantial population of neutrophils was found in colostrum, amounting to 0.54 × 106 ± 0.62 × 106 cells/ml, being 3.46 × 106 ± 1.40 × 106 cells/ml in blood. To assess whether these values are comparable in breast milk at more advanced stages of maturation, we analyzed samples of transitional and mature milk. After the high abundance observed in colostrum, when the newborn’s immunological support is most needed, neutrophil population declined but remained present (Supplementary Fig. 2A). In addition, neutrophil counts in colostrum from term and preterm mothers were compared, with no significant differences observed (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Next, to assess whether the presence of neutrophils in colostrum reflects circulating levels, we performed a correlation analysis between the absolute neutrophil counts in colostrum and blood from each mother. Intriguingly, neutrophil levels in colostrum did not correlate with those in the circulation of the same subject (Fig. 1H). Interestingly, systemic inflammation, inferred by plasma C-reactive protein levels, were comparable in cesarean section versus natural deliveries (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Accordingly, no differences were found in circulating neutrophil numbers depending on the delivery mode (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Therefore, colostrum contains a notable community of neutrophils whose presence in breast milk does not appear to result from passive transfer from circulation.

Neutrophils in colostrum exhibit a distinct profile of membrane surface markers, morphology, and function

To describe the profile of colostrum neutrophils, flow cytometry analysis of ten different surface markers was performed and contrasted with blood neutrophils of the same person. Common markers of neutrophil activation, including CD11b and CD66b, exhibited higher expression levels in neutrophils from colostrum compared with those found in circulation (Fig. 2A, B). In contrast, the expression of CXCR2, a key chemokine receptor of neutrophils, was decreased in breast milk, which might point to a lower chemotactic capacity (Fig. 2C). Notably, most neutrophils in colostrum show high CXCR4 and low CD62L levels as opposed blood cells (Fig. 2D, E). This could suggest a similarity to the CXCR4+CD62L− “aged” neutrophil phenotype. However, no differences were found in surface CD10, a neutrophil maturity marker, or in nuclear hypersegmentation between the two neutrophil types (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Intriguingly, other surface markers less distinctive of neutrophils presented high expression in the colostrum population (CD36, CD14, and CD64) (Fig. 2F–H). Under certain circumstances, neutrophils have been suggested to acquire the antigen-presenting function, in a major histocompatibility complex class II (MHCII) class-dependent manner [26–28]. Surprisingly, colostrum neutrophils displayed substantial levels of HLA-DR as compared with their circulating counterparts (Fig. 2I). Representation of CXCR4−CD62L+ and CXCR4+CD62L− proportion of neutrophils confirmed the majority of the latter in colostrum, as depicted in the dot and t-SNE plots (Fig. 2J, K), and quantified in Fig. 2L. The expression of each studied marker presented on a t-SNE heatmap representation can be found in Supplementary Fig. 5. Most of neutrophil functions are associated to the proteins encapsulated in their cytoplasmic granules. Thus, neutrophil granularity helps identify their function. To investigate this aspect, neutrophils from blood and colostrum of the same patient were isolated by magnetic separation, using an anti-CD66b antibody. Giemsa staining showed that circulating neutrophils display a more evident cytoplasmic granule content than colostrum neutrophils (Fig. 2M). Next, we aimed to assess the cell granule content by immunocytochemistry staining of intracellular myeloperoxidase (MPO), as previously described [18]. The quantification of intracellular MPO denoted that neutrophils in colostrum manifest reduced count of cytoplasmic granules (Fig. 2N, O). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of isolated cells evidenced that, while both circulating and colostrum neutrophils presented typical neutrophil segmented nuclei and pseudopodia on the cell membrane surface, blood neutrophils exhibited the full variety of small cytoplasmic granules while those in colostrum cells were larger and sparser (Fig. 2P). These findings were further validated by significantly lower side scatter (SSC) values in colostrum neutrophils compared with blood neutrophils (Fig. 2Q). This could indicate that neutrophils in colostrum might have responded to a stimulus. To assess whether neutrophils in colostrum exhibit alterations in their canonical functions, their phagocytic and migratory capacities were studied in vitro. Colostral neutrophils showed reduced phagocytic activity after incubation with pHrodo™ Red S. aureus compared with peripheral blood neutrophils (Fig. 2R). Additionally, transmigration toward N-formyl-methionyl-leucyl-phenylalanine (fMLP), a potent neutrophil chemoattractant, revealed a significantly diminished migratory capacity (Fig. 2S). Colostrum microenvironment prompts changes in blood neutrophils to resemble breast milk phenotype

Contrary to the traditional belief that neutrophils are simply responders to acute inflammation, we now know that these cells are present in most tissues during homeostasis and even contribute to maintenance functions [13, 29]. Furthermore, they acquire distinct phenotypic and functional properties depending on the tissue [30]. To explore whether the microenvironment of breast milk is responsible for the neutrophil phenotype, we exposed circulating neutrophils from female healthy donors to breast milk whey. Levels of surface protein expression were assessed by flow cytometry after incubation with either a mix of colostrum whey from donor mothers, a mix for plasma from the same group (named heterologous plasma), or a mix of plasma from the same group of blood neutrophil donors (named autologous plasma). Remarkably, the exposure of naïve neutrophils to colostrum microenvironment resulted in a severe change in surface protein expression, partially resembling the phenotype observed in colostrum neutrophils. While CD66b, CXCR2, and HLA-DR levels remained unchanged in the three conditions (Fig. 3B, C, I), CD11b, CXCR4, CD62L, CD36, and CD64 levels of neutrophils incubated with colostrum whey mimicked those observed in colostrum neutrophils as compared with plasma incubation (Fig. 3A, D–F, H). Intriguingly, CD14 expression did not follow this tendency (Fig. 3G), implying that other forces intervene in the resulting colostrum phenotype. Interestingly, no differences were observed between the incubation with heterologous or autologous plasma. Furthermore, in vitro plasma and whey incubations did not impact on neutrophil viability (Supplementary Fig. 6). These results suggest that the breast milk microenvironment induces modifications in circulating neutrophils to resemble, at least partially, the phenotype of colostrum neutrophils. It remains to be elucidated what other mechanisms interfere in this transformation. The proteome of colostrum neutrophils outlines antigen presentation and humoral response-related functions

Neutrophils typically carry a significant load of essential proteins for fighting infections and respond to inflammatory insults, most of them distributed into their cytoplasmic granules [17]. While neutrophils can undergo transcriptional changes, these happen over the course of hours, and reflect poorly dynamic functional changes in the cell. In addition, previous studies have shown little correlation between the transcriptional and protein content in neutrophils [18]. Hence, we chose to implement a proteomic strategy to analyze colostrum neutrophils and compare them with their counterparts in circulation. Blood and colostrum neutrophils isolated from same patients were used for the proteomic assays. A total of 1099 proteins were identified, of which 164 were classified as differentially expressed proteins (DEP), as they met the following threshold criteria: adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2FoldChange|≥ 1. DEPs are presented in a volcano plot in Fig. 4A, showing striking contrasts between both samples, with fold changes above 5 in blood (right side) and 10 in colostrum (left side). PCA revealed two well-defined and separated neutrophil populations corresponding to each sample (Fig. 4B). The heatmap, which displays DEPs with fold changes (FC) exceeding 8, revealed a strikingly different protein profile between neutrophils from blood (left) and colostrum (right) (Fig. 4C). These differences were remarkably consistent between samples. A table with proteins differentially expressed with an FC lower 8 can been found at Supplementary Table 1. Interestingly, proteomic analyses confirmed HLA-DR and CD36 overexpression in colostrum versus blood neutrophils already observed by flow cytometry (Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed on proteomic data. While the assessment of molecular functions, cellular components, and biological processes of blood neutrophils yielded expected outcomes related to enzymatic activities, granule proteins, and response to bacteria, respectively (Fig. 4D), colostrum data revealed surprising results. With a significant difference, antigen binding was the most represented molecular function of colostrum neutrophils, while the most prominent biological processes were humoral immune response, production of molecular mediators of the humoral immune response, and immunoglobulin production. Interestingly, blood microparticles were highly present as cellular components of colostrum neutrophils, followed by external side of plasma membrane and immunoglobulin complexes (Fig. 4E). Consistent with this, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEEG) pathway identification confirmed that the most prevalent pathway in blood neutrophils was “neutrophil extracellular trap formation” (Supplementary Fig. 7A), while in colostrum neutrophils it was the “complement cascade” (Supplementary Fig. 7B). Colostrum neutrophils seem to have a conspicuously active complement cascade involving both classic and alternative pathways. These results imply that neutrophils in early breast milk constitute a completely distinct niche with functions that primarily support host defense through processes involving antigen presentation, humoral response, and complement system-related mechanisms. Colostrum neutrophils release humoral response proteins to the medium and modulate lymphocyte activation

Neutrophils are primarily secretory cells, and analysis of the nature of their secretion provides insight into their function within a specific context. To compare the secretion of blood and colostrum neutrophils, the isolated cells were incubated in vitro for 1 h either unstimulated (to mimic a steady-state situation) or in the presence of phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA). The supernatants were analyzed by proteomics, to identify differences between the secretion of both cells. After 1 h of in vitro incubation without stimulation, the medium of colostrum differed importantly from that of blood neutrophils in 6 proteins, all of them related to the humoral response (Fig. 5A–F). Specifically, parts of immunoglobulins (especially IgA and IgM) were detected to be released by colostrum neutrophils, together with polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (PIGR), the key transporter of mucosal immunoglobulins across the epithelium. These findings align with the results of proteomic analyses of blood and colostrum neutrophils, indicating that neutrophils in breast milk might capture immunoglobulins and release them into the surrounding medium, which has important implications for the transfer of immunological support from the mother to the child. Interestingly, stimulation with PMA confirmed that the primary differences between circulating and colostrum neutrophils in the response to a stimulus were associated with immunoglobulins, as inferred by the group comparison analysis (Table 2). In addition, consistent with cell proteomic results, differences were found in milk fat globule epidermal growth factor 8 (MFGE8) and cathelicidin antimicrobial peptide (CAMP) protein abundance (Table 2). GO analyses illustrated that the differences found in the secretion of both cells upon stimulation were primarily represented in “complement receptor signaling,” “adaptive based built domain/receptors built from immunoglobulins,” and “defense antibacterial/antimicrobial bacterium” pathways (Fig. 5G). To evaluate the effect that the environment derived from colostrum neutrophils would have on effectors of adaptive immunity, isolated circulating lymphocytes from mothers were incubated with supernatant of either blood or colostrum neutrophils. Upon stimulation with colostrum neutrophil supernatant for 18 h, an increase in HLA-DR was detected, accompanied by a decrease in CD25 and no appreciable changes in CD69 (Fig. 5H–J). This pattern might be indicative of a prolonged activation as previously described [31]: CD69 is rapidly regulated following stimulation, and its levels usually decrease afterward; CD25 reaches a transient peak that may disappear at later stages; whereas HLA-DR shows sustained induction. Notably, the incubation with blood neutrophil supernatant did not affect any of the three markers. Discussion

Breast milk constitutes the foundation of a newborn’s nutrition, and over the past few decades, its crucial role in supporting the child’s development has become increasingly clear. While breastfeeding decreases the incidence of future far-reaching diseases for both mother and child in healthy dyads, the importance of breast milk consumption in compromised newborns, either premature or affected by complex conditions, escalates severely [6]. Breast milk contains an important cellular fraction with leukocytes, which is particularly prominent in colostrum [10] and whose purpose has not been investigated so far.

In this study, we reveal that breast milk hosts a unique population of neutrophils with surprising features that evoke novel roles, linking innate and adaptive immunity. We describe that colostrum contains high neutrophil numbers, which do not correlate with circulating neutrophil counts. This observation suggests that breast milk neutrophils do not reach the mammary gland because of variations in circulating cell abundance. Interestingly, nonmyeloid leukocytes (responsible for humoral response) are poorly represented in the pool of immune cells of the breast milk, as inferred by our results and previous reports [10, 32]. These data contrast with the importance of breast milk as a vehicle to transfer maternal immunoglobulins to support the infant’s immature immune system, particularly in the first weeks of life [20].

Neutrophil dynamics are defined by waves of bone marrow cell release, blood circulation and marginal infiltration, recruitment to the site of inflammations if needed, and eventually homing to the bone marrow for their removal [33]. While the lifespan of neutrophils has been under debate for long time, it is nowadays acknowledged that neutrophils change their phenotype since their release from the bone marrow, altering their CXCR4 and CD62L levels [16]. We found that colostral neutrophils exhibit low CD62L and high CXCR4 levels, while simultaneously displaying reduced phagocytic and migratory capacities. However, no signs of advanced maturation were observed, as indicated by normal nuclear segmentation and CD10 expression. It is possible that cohabitation with milk-associated bacteria drives neutrophils to fine-tune their responses, relying on more specific mechanisms to carry out their protective functions.

Besides the drastic variation of CXCR4 and CD62L levels, other surface markers change in colostrum neutrophils. While high levels of CD11b and CD66b in colostrum neutrophils may indicate amplified activation and cell degranulation, lower CXCR2 values suggest a diminished migratory function, as previously suggested for breast milk leukocytes [34] and confirmed by our in vitro migration analyses. Importantly, phagocytic capacity was also reduced. These characteristics may be shaped by the colostrum microenvironment, which could prime neutrophils toward an alternative mode of activation. Notably, CD36 is found in higher amounts on the surface of milk neutrophils compared with those in blood. These findings are further confirmed by proteomic analysis of cell pellets, showing that CD36 abundance is 4.23 times higher in colostrum than in blood neutrophils. Remarkably, incubation of blood neutrophils with colostrum whey resulted in higher CD36 surface expression. Hence, the interaction with the colostrum seems to trigger the change of this scavenger receptor expression. Given that the phagocytic capacity of colostrum neutrophils appears reduced, it can be hypothesized that the high lipid content of breast milk drives this heightened regulation.

Higher levels of uncommon neutrophil markers in milk may reflect specific activation states or functional shifts. For example, tumor-associated neutrophil subsets exhibit increased CD14 expression and enhanced antigen uptake following immunization under certain conditions [35, 36]. Neutrophils also show elevated HLA surface expression when engaging with T cells during antigen presentation [26, 28, 37], and upregulate CD64 (a high-affinity Fcγ receptor expressed in antigen-presenting cells) in response to severe infection or exposure to large amounts of endotoxins [38, 39]. Previous studies have implied that acquisition of dendritic cell-like properties by neutrophils at the sites of inflammation may represent a key process for linking the innate and adaptive arms of immune responses [27, 28]. In addition, it has previously been reported that neutrophils in barrier tissues such as intestine and lung express high levels of MHCII, as we observed in our data for colostrum neutrophils [30]. Interestingly, while colostrum neutrophils exhibit a CD14+ CD64+ HLA-DR+ phenotype versus CD14− CD64− HLA-DR− in blood, the incubation with colostrum whey mimicked only partially the colostrum phenotype in blood neutrophils from donors, with elevated CD64 levels but decreased or unchanged CD14 and HLA-DR, respectively. Taken together, colostrum neutrophils clearly present a different surface marker profile, part of which can be replicated by blood neutrophils under early breast milk conditions. While these findings do not fully clarify the differences in surface marker expression between the two niches, they provide some insight into the factors driving the observed features.

Beyond surface marker expression, the complete proteomic profile of blood and colostrum neutrophils highlighted the uniqueness of the latter. The proteins with higher abundance in colostrum neutrophils revealed that this population has a strong involvement in antigen binding, humoral response, and interactions with immunoglobulin complexes. Conversely, blood neutrophils exhibited characteristic components aligned with their standard functions, closely associated with their granule content.

Maternal immunity supports the newborn’s immature immune system, first via the placenta and then through breast milk. Secretory IgA (SIgA), IgM (SIgM), and IgE in breast milk provide antibody-based immunity, crucial since newborns cannot yet produce IgA, the most abundant breast milk immunoglobulin. Consistently, antibody responses develop after 1–3 months, making colostrum particularly important [20]. PIGR, produced by epithelial cells, transports mucosal IgA and IgM to the apical surface, where it is cleaved into the secretory component, remaining attached to the polymeric immunoglobulin complex [40]. In colostrum neutrophils, the most abundant proteins were PIGR, the immunoglobulin J chain (KCHAIN), and various IgA-derived fragments. Interestingly, the natural IgA receptor (FCAR/CD89) is downregulated, suggesting alternative IgA capture mechanisms.

Our findings show that, in addition to containing IgA, colostrum neutrophils actively release it into the medium under baseline conditions. Thus, in light of our results and previous evidence, IgA-loaded neutrophils from colostrum may be partially contributing to the pool of IgA in the oral mucosa during breastfeeding. Furthermore, the reason for the prominent presence of neutrophils in colostrum might be multifaceted. On the one hand, neutrophils seem to be essential to limit bacterial mammary infection dams in a mouse model [41]. In that respect, the substantial high content of MFGE8 (also termed lactadherin) in colostrum neutrophils and their ability to release it could indicate a prominent phagocytic function in the mammary gland, as this molecule is a well-known bridging molecule in the efferocytosis of apoptotic cells [42]. On the other hand, it has been reported that breast milk leukocytes (probably mostly neutrophils) can persist in the intestine of newborn baboons [43]. Of note, several reports have indicated that MFGE8 has a protective role in the integrity of the intestinal barrier of neonates [44, 45]. As a result, it cannot be excluded that neutrophils might carry out distinct functions in different compartments. Indeed, rather than performing a cellular function, neutrophils may act as carriers of functional molecules that will exert their action once transferred.

T cell activation proceeds sequentially, marked by early (CD69), intermediate (CD25), and late (HLA-DR) activation markers [31]. Interestingly, the upregulation of CD25 on activated decidual T lymphocytes can be reduced in pregnant mothers, a phenomenon hypothesized to contribute to maternal tolerance of the fetal allograft [46]. These changes occur at the protein level, but not at the mRNA level, suggesting post-transcriptional regulation [47]. Our results show that, unlike blood neutrophils, the environment created by colostrum neutrophils primes lymphocytes toward a certain activation state, characterized by low levels of CD25. This peculiarity may be attributed to the need for a degree of tolerance to the breast milk environment, which contains components such as the microbiota. However, further investigations are required to elucidate the reach of breast milk neutrophil functions.

In summary, we describe a population of mature, antigen-presenting HLA-DR+ breast milk neutrophils that, in contrast to their blood counterparts, display a CXCR4⁺CD62L⁻CXCR2mid phenotype, together with high expression of CD11b, CD66b, CD36, CD14, and CD64. Phenotypically, these cells exhibit a reduced number of cytoplasmic granules and an increased abundance of proteins related to adaptive immunity, which they release into the medium. Functionally, they show diminished migratory and phagocytic capacities and are able to modulate T lymphocyte activation.

Regarding the origin of breast milk neutrophils, while stem cells and myeloid progenitors have been found in colostrum and mature breast milk [48–50], several clues in our data point toward a blood derivation. Colostrum neutrophils present high CXCR4 and low CD62L levels, but their CD10 levels are equivalent to those in blood. Furthermore, the change of surface marker expression in blood neutrophils when incubated with milk whey suggests that the switch of phenotypes is determined by the new compartment. Lastly, proteomics data show that blood microparticles are an important cellular component of colostrum neutrophils, which advocates for a circulating origin. The large amounts of PIGR, a transepithelial immunoglobulin transporter, found and released by colostral neutrophils may also indicate that translocation through the mammary epithelium has occurred. However, further analyses are required to confirm this hypothesis.

Conclusions

Here, we describe a unique and significant population of neutrophils in early breast milk, likely derived from blood neutrophils. These cells present exceptional phenotypic and proteomic characteristics, featuring roles of antigen binding, immunoglobulin transit, and humoral response, evoking a transitional role between innate and adaptive immunity. These findings open new avenues of knowledge for understanding the immunological protective power of breast milk.
