Robust and durable zwitterionic hydrogel coating with catechol-Cu2+ coordination for antithrombotic and antibacterial blood-contacting devices
Authors: Guanghang Bai, Yuxin Xie, Ying Sun, Liqi Li, Ye Zhu, Xiaojie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThrombosis and bacterial infection remain two major life-threatening complications associated with blood-contacting medical devices. Although zwitterionic hydrogel coatings mitigate these risks by forming strongly hydrated interfaces that resist protein, platelet, and bacterial adhesion, their clinical translation is often limited by excessive swelling, weak interfacial adhesion, and insufficient long-term biofunctionality. Herein, we report a mechanically robust zwitterionic hydrogel coating reinforced by a hybrid network integrating covalent crosslinks, catechol-Cu2+ coordination, and electrostatic interactions, enabling durable antithrombotic and antibacterial performance. The coating was fabricated via a simple dip-coating process followed by photo-crosslinking, forming a uniform and stable hydrogel layer on thermoplastic polyurethane substrates. This hybrid architecture provides strong interfacial adhesion, enhanced wear resistance, and high tensile strength and extensibility. The hydrated zwitterionic interface significantly reduces friction and effectively suppresses the adsorption of proteins with different charges, as well as the adhesion of mammalian cells, red blood cells, platelets, and bacteria. Meanwhile, incorporated Cu2+ catalyzes sustained nitric oxide generation to actively inhibit platelet activation and thrombus formation, while also exerting broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against S. aureus and E. coli. The coating demonstrates effective catheter patency maintenance and pronounced thromboresistance in an extracorporeal rabbit circulation model. Long-term immersion studies further confirm sustained antifouling, antibacterial, and anticoagulant performance, attributed to high Cu2+ retention within the hybrid network. Overall, this work presents a mechanically reinforced and durable zwitterionic hydrogel coating strategy that integrates passive antifouling with active nitric oxide-mediated regulation, offering a promising surface-engineering platform for blood-contacting medical devices.Keywords: Blood-contacting devices, Zwitterionic hydrogel coating, Mechanical robustness, Durable antithrombotic performance, Antibacterial

Introduction

Blood-contacting medical devices, including central venous catheters, artificial blood vessels, hemodialysis catheters, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenators, are widely used in interventional therapy, blood purification, and extracorporeal life support [1]. Most of these devices are fabricated from hydrophobic polymers such as polyurethane, silicone rubber, and polyvinyl chloride [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. However, hydrophobic surfaces are highly susceptible to thrombosis and bacterial infection during clinical use [[6], [7], [8]]. Upon blood contact, nonspecific protein adsorption rapidly occurs, inducing platelet adhesion and activation, which subsequently triggers the coagulation cascade and thrombus formation, potentially leading to intraluminal obstruction or distal embolism [9,10]. Meanwhile, bacterial adhesion during implantation can promote biofilm formation and device-associated infections [11]. Although anticoagulants and antibiotics are routinely administered to mitigate these complications [12], their effects are often transient, and prolonged use can cause severe side effects, including bleeding risk, thrombocytopenia, and antibiotic resistance [13,14]. Therefore, developing effective surface-based strategies to simultaneously prevent thrombosis and infection remains an urgent clinical need.

Surface modification has been extensively explored to improve the hemocompatibility of blood-contacting devices. Among various approaches, enhancing surface hydrophilicity is considered one of the most effective strategies [15,16]. Hydrophilic coatings reduce nonspecific protein adsorption by forming a hydration layer, thereby suppressing platelet adhesion and bacterial colonization. Two main architectures have been developed: brush-like polymer coatings and hydrogel coatings [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. Brush-like coatings often suffer from limited durability due to low grafting density and nanometer-scale thickness, whereas hydrogel coatings provide superior antifouling performance owing to their continuous coverage and micrometer-scale thickness [[21], [22], [23]]. Conventional hydrophilic polymers, including poly(ethylene glycol), poly(acrylic acid), polyacrylamide, and hyaluronic acid, have been widely used to construct hydrogel coatings [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Nevertheless, these systems are frequently limited by oxidative degradation, weak mechanical strength, or enzymatic instability. Zwitterionic polymers have recently emerged as promising candidates for hydrogel coatings on blood-contacting devices due to their strong hydration capability and favorable immune compatibility [28,29]. However, their pronounced hydrophilicity often leads to excessive swelling and mechanical mismatch with hydrophobic substrates, which can compromise coating integrity and accelerate interfacial delamination under physiological stresses [30,31]. Therefore, simultaneously improving the mechanical robustness and interfacial adhesion of zwitterionic hydrogel coatings is critical for achieving long-term stability.

To address these challenges, various strategies have been explored, including the incorporation of microgels, dynamic crosslinking networks based on reversible coordination, and the use of silane coupling agents to enhance interfacial bonding [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37]]. Although these approaches can improve mechanical strength and adhesion, hydration-mediated passive antifouling alone is often insufficient for long-term blood-contacting applications. During prolonged use, unavoidable adsorption of trace contaminants or exposure to complex biological environments may still promote platelet activation and bacterial colonization, even on highly antifouling zwitterionic surfaces [38,39]. Consequently, integrating durable antithrombotic and antibacterial functionality into zwitterionic hydrogel coatings has become an important design objective.

Substantial efforts have been devoted to integrating the passive antifouling capability of zwitterionic hydrogel coatings with active biofunctional strategies to achieve durable protection. Such active functionalities are commonly introduced through antibacterial agents [[40], [41], [42]], anticoagulant molecules [[43], [44], [45]], or catalytic systems enabling sustained nitric oxide (NO) generation [46,47]. Inspired by vascular endothelium, which continuously releases NO to inhibit platelet adhesion and activation, NO-based strategies have attracted particular attention in the design of hemocompatible interfaces [48]. Recent studies have explored the integration of zwitterionic hydrogel coatings with NO-generating functionalities to enhance hemocompatibility and antibacterial performance. For example, recently reported NO-functionalized zwitterionic coatings have demonstrated effective platelet inhibition and anti-infective properties for blood-contacting devices [49,50]. Compared with NO donor-based approaches, which often suffer from limited stability and poorly controlled release kinetics [51], catalytic NO generation using metal ions such as Cu2+ offers a more sustainable and physiologically relevant solution. However, many reported NO-functionalized zwitterionic coatings rely on complex fabrication processes or stringent reaction conditions, restricting their applicability to devices with complex geometries and large-scale manufacturing [27,52]. Therefore, developing a simple and scalable strategy to construct zwitterionic hydrogel coatings that simultaneously combine robust mechanical performance, stable interfacial adhesion, and durable antithrombotic and antibacterial functionality remains a critical challenge.

Herein, we develop a zwitterionic hydrogel coating with robust mechanical performance and durable antithrombotic and antibacterial properties for blood-contacting devices via a simple dip-coating followed by photo-crosslinking process (Fig. 1). The coating is rationally engineered based on a hybrid network architecture integrating covalent crosslinks, dynamic catechol-Cu2+ coordination, and electrostatic interactions, enabling simultaneous mechanical reinforcement, strong interfacial adhesion, and long-term biofunctionality. Specifically, a catechol-functionalized zwitterionic copolymer (PSDP) was synthesized to provide hydration capability, metal coordination sites, and electrostatic domains. Upon dip-coating and UV curing, a hierarchically crosslinked hydrogel network forms in situ on TPU substrates, in which covalent bonds establish a stable framework, while catechol-Cu2+ coordination and electrostatic interactions function as reversible sacrificial bonds to dissipate mechanical energy and enhance toughness. The zwitterionic matrix establishes a stable hydration layer that resists nonspecific protein adsorption, platelet adhesion, and bacterial colonization, while incorporated Cu2+ catalyzes sustained nitric oxide generation to actively inhibit platelet activation and contributes to antibacterial activity. The antithrombotic performance is further validated in an ex vivo extracorporeal circulation model, and long-term antifouling, antibacterial, and anticoagulant properties are systematically evaluated after prolonged immersion. Collectively, this work presents a simple and scalable strategy to construct mechanically robust and multifunctional zwitterionic hydrogel coatings with durable hemocompatibility and anti-infective performance for blood-contacting medical devices.

Materials and methods

Materials

Dopamine hydrochloride (DA·HCl), methacrylic anhydride (MA), 3-sulfopropyl methacrylate potassium salt (SPM), 2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine (MPC), N, N′-methylenebisacrylamide (MBA), methacrylatoethyl trimethyl ammonium chloride (DMC), azodiisobutyronitrile (AIBN), glutatione (GSH), and s-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. 2-Hydroxy-1-[4-(2-hydroxyethoxy)phenyl]-2-methyl-1-propanone (I2959) and cupric chloride dihydrate (CuCl2·2H2O) were obtained from J&K Scientific Ltd., Beijing, China. Tetrahydrofuran (THF), N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF), ethanol (EtOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl), sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3), ethyl acetate (EtOAc), and sodium tetraborate decahydrate (Na2B4O7·10H2O) were supplied by Siqnopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Escherichia coli (E. coli, CMCC44102) and staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus, CMCC26003) were obtained from the Preservation Biotechnology Center, Shanghai, China. Mouse fibroblast cells (L929) were purchased from Sixin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) kit, thrombin time (TT) test kit, and enhanced BCA protein assay kit were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology Inc., Shanghai, China. Fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled bovine serum albumin (FITC-BSA), bovine serum albumin (BSA), fetal bovine serum (FBS), Dulbecco's modified eagle's medium (DMEM), trypsin-EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) solution (0.25 wt%), penicillin-streptomycin solution, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were purchased from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. N-(3,4-dihydroxyphenethyl) methacrylamide (DMA) was synthesized according to a previously reported procedure (Fig. S1A) [53].

Synthesis of PSDP copolymer

The copolymer poly(3-sulfopropyl methacrylate potassium salt-co-N-(3,4-dihydroxyphenethyl) methacrylamide-co-2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine) [P(SPM-co-DMA-co-MPC), abbreviated as PSDP was synthesized via free radical polymerization (Fig. S1B). SPM (0.98 g, 4 mmol), DMA (0.44 g, 2 mmol), and MPC (4.13 g, 14 mmol) were dissolved in a water/DMF mixture (v/v = 1:1). AIBN (1 wt% relative to total monomer content) was added as the initiator. The flask was sealed and stirred at 65 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere for 24 h. The resulting solution was transferred into a dialysis bag (MWCO = 3500 Da) and dialyzed against deionized water for 3 d to remove unreacted monomers and oligomers. The final product was obtained as a white powder after freeze-drying (4.91 g, yield 88.23%).

Preparation of coatings and hydrogels

The hydrogel coatings were fabricated via a dip-coating-assisted photo-crosslinking process. Briefly, a pre-gel solution containing PSDP, MPC, DMC, MBA, CuCl2, and the photoinitiator I2959 was prepared in a water/ethanol mixed solvent (v/v = 2:1). TPU sheets and transparent TPU catheters (inner diameter = 3.0 mm) were sequentially cleaned with ethanol and deionized water, dried under nitrogen, and treated with O2 plasma for 8 min to activate the surface. For sheet substrates, samples were immersed in the pre-gel solution and slowly withdrawn to remove excess precursor, forming a uniform liquid film. For catheter coatings, the pre-gel solution was injected into the lumen using a syringe until completely filled. The catheters were gently rotated to ensure uniform wetting of the inner surface, followed by air purging to remove excess solution and retain a thin pre-gel layer along the lumen wall. Subsequently, UV irradiation (365 nm) was applied for 5 min to initiate in situ photo-crosslinking, yielding PSDP/MPC/DMC/MBA@Cu (PDM@Cu) hydrogel coatings. After curing, all samples were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water to remove unreacted components.

To investigate the individual contributions of covalent crosslinking and catechol-Cu2+ coordination to the mechanical and biofunctional performance of the hydrogel coatings, two control formulations were designed. The coating prepared from a pre-gel solution containing PSDP, MPC, DMC, Cu2+, and the photoinitiator I2959, which incorporates catechol-Cu2+ coordination and electrostatic interactions but lacks covalent crosslinking, was denoted as PSDP/MPC/DMC/Cu (PD@Cu). In contrast, the coating fabricated from a pre-gel solution consisting of PSDP, MPC, DMC, MBA, and I2959, featuring covalent crosslinking and electrostatic interactions while excluding Cu2+ coordination, was designated as PSDP/MPC/DMC/MBA (PDM). The detailed compositions of all formulations are summarized in Table S1, where water and ethanol served as the mixed solvent (v/v = 2:1).

Characterization of coatings

The coatings were immersed in a rhodamine B solution (2 mg/mL) for 5 min and subsequently photographed. The chemical compositions of the TPU substrate and coatings were analyzed using attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Kratos). The surface morphology and thickness of the coatings were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi) coupled with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Hitachi). The hydrophilicity of the coatings was evaluated by measuring the water contact angle (WCA, DataPhysics). The adhesion strength between the coatings and TPU substrate was determined using a lap shear test. Coated and uncoated TPU samples were bonded with a commercial cyanoacrylate adhesive, and the test was performed at a crosshead speed of 10 mm/min (Fig. S2).

Lubrication properties

The friction coefficients (COF) of the TPU substrate and coated samples were measured in PBS using a friction wear tester (MFT-5000, Rudolph). Measurements were performed at a fixed frequency of 1 Hz under loads ranging from 1 to 5 N. In addition, tests were carried out at frequencies from 1 to 3 Hz under a constant load of 1 N. Friction coefficient curves were recorded after 500 reciprocal cycles at 1 N and 1 Hz. To ensure reproducibility, each test was performed in triplicate using distinct rectangular specimens (10 mm × 20 mm). To visualize the coating morphology, the coatings were divided into two groups. One group was dried under ambient conditions, while another was first hydrated during friction testing and subsequently lyophilized prior to imaging. The surface morphology and thickness of both groups were examined using scanning electron microscopy.

Protein adsorption assays

For the protein adsorption assay, bare substrates and PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu coatings (10 mm × 10 mm) were incubated in 0.1 mg/mL FITC-BSA solution at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the samples were rinsed three times with PBS, and fluorescence images were captured. In a separate experiment, bare substrates and coatings were incubated with bovine serum albumin (BSA, 5 mg/mL), fibrinogen (Fg, 5 mg/mL), or lysozyme (5 mg/mL) solutions at 37 °C for 2 h. Following incubation, the samples were immersed in 1 mL of sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, 2 wt%) and sonicated for 30 min to desorb adsorbed proteins. The amount of protein attachment was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Cell adhesion assays

The bare substrates and PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu coatings were placed in a 12-well plate, after which L929 cells (1 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded onto the sample surfaces and incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 24 h. Subsequently, the samples were stained with 10 μL of FDA solution (5 mg/mL) for 10 min to visualize cells adhering to the surfaces. The number of adherent cells on each sample was quantified from fluorescence images using Image J software.

Red blood cells (RBCs) were separated from fresh rabbit blood by centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 15 min and diluted 1:4 (v/v) with PBS. Then, 1000 μL of RBC suspension was added dropwise onto each sample and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After rinsing with PBS, the adhered RBCs were fixed with 2.5 wt% glutaraldehyde for 2 h. Following fixation, the samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%). The morphology of adhered RBCs was subsequently examined by SEM.

Cu2+ chelation efficiency and release test

Before measurement, an aqueous solution of 0.1 wt% 4-(2-pyridylazo)-resorcinol (PAR) was prepared and mixed with Cu2+ solutions of varying concentrations. The pH of the mixtures was adjusted to 4-6 using an acetic acid-sodium acetate buffer. The absorbance at 510 nm was recorded to generate a standard calibration curve. To assess Cu2+ chelation efficiency, coating samples (10 mm × 10 mm, n = 3) were immersed in 0.1 mol/L nitric acid to extract Cu2+, which was subsequently quantified by the PAR assay. To evaluate Cu2+ retention, coating samples were immersed in deionized water, and the supernatants were collected at 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 15, and 30 days and analyzed using the same protocol.

Nitric oxide catalytic release test

The catalytic nitric oxide (NO) release of the PD@Cu and PDM@Cu coatings was evaluated using a NO commercial detection kit based on the Griess reaction. A standard calibration curve was prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions. Coating samples (10 mm × 10 mm) were immersed in 1 mL of PBS for 30 min, followed by sequential addition of 10 μM GSH and 10 μM GSNO. After incubation for 1 h, the catalytic NO release rate was determined. To assess the durability of catalytic activity, coatings were immersed in PBS at 37 °C for 7, 15, and 30 days. After each immersion period, the NO catalytic release was evaluated using the same protocol.

Platelet adhesion and activation assays

Fresh rabbit blood was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 15 min to obtain platelet-rich plasma (PRP). Each sample was incubated with 1 mL of PRP at 37 °C for 2 h. After incubation, the samples were stained with rhodamine 123 (5 μg/mL) for 10 min and observed by fluorescence microscopy. Platelet adhesion was quantified using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay kit.

For morphological observation, 1 mL of PRP with or without an NO donor solution (10 μM GSH and 10 μM GSNO in saline) was added dropwise to each sample and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min. The adhered platelets were subsequently fixed with 2.5 wt% glutaraldehyde for 2 h, followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%). Finally, the samples were dried at room temperature and examined by SEM.

Platelet-poor plasma (PPP) was obtained by centrifuging whole blood at 3000 rpm for 10 min. Samples were preincubated in PBS at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by the addition of 400 μL of PPP and further incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) and thrombin time (TT) were measured, and the mean clotting time was recorded as the final result.

Whole blood recalcification assays

Circular samples (φ = 10 mm) of TPU, PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu were incubated with 1 mL of citrated rabbit whole blood at 37 °C. For coatings containing copper ions, tests were performed in the presence or absence of an NO donor solution. Clot formation was initiated by adding 50 μL of 0.2 M CaCl2. After 5, 10, and 15 min of exposure, the samples were collected, and their macroscopic appearances were recorded using a digital camera. Subsequently, the thrombi adhering to each surface were rinsed with PBS and weighed.

Antibacterial and antibiofilm assays

Gram-negative E. coli and gram-positive S. aureus were used to evaluate the antibacterial properties of the hydrogel coatings. Bacteria were cultured in LB medium at 37 °C for 12 h and diluted to a concentration of 5 × 105 to 1 × 106 CFU/mL. The samples were placed in centrifuge tubes containing 10 mL of bacterial suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the samples were rinsed with physiological saline and stained with a mixture of SYTO 9 and PI for 10 min, followed by observation under a fluorescence microscope. To quantify adherent bacteria, the samples were ultrasonicated in 5 mL of physiological saline for 5 min to detach bacteria. After serial dilution, 50 μL of the bacterial suspension was spread evenly on agar plates, which were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and colonies were imaged using an optical microscope. For morphological observation, the samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, 100%), and examined by SEM. 1 × 1 cm bare TPU and hydrogel-coated samples were co-incubated with E. coli and S. aureus suspensions for 7 days. After incubation, samples were gently rinsed with PBS to remove non-adherent bacteria, followed by staining with crystal violet. The stained biofilms were then dissolved in ethanol, and the absorbance was quantified at 570 nm to determine biofilm biomass.

Cytotoxicity and hemocompatibility tests

L929 cells (2 × 104 cells/mL) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight, followed by exposure to the extraction medium for 24 or 72 h. Cell viability was quantified using a CCK-8 assay kit. For live/dead staining, L929 cells seeded in 6-well plates were similarly treated with the extraction medium, stained with FDA (5 mg/mL, 50 μL) and PI (1 mg/mL, 25 μL) for 10 min, and imaged by fluorescence microscopy. For the hemolysis assay, RBCs were diluted 1:4 (v/v) with PBS and incubated with the samples at 37 °C for 4 h. Ultrapure water and PBS served as positive and negative controls, respectively. Ex vivo blood circulation assays

The coatings were applied to the inner surfaces of TPU catheters (length: 4 cm; inner diameter: 3 mm). The antithrombogenic performance was evaluated using an ex vivo extracorporeal circulation (ECC) model in New Zealand White rabbits (2.5-3.5 kg, n = 4). For each group, one rabbit was used, and 4 catheter samples were incorporated into a silicone rubber-based circulation loop. Catheter occlusion was monitored at 5 min intervals throughout circulation. After 1 h of circulation, the catheters were collected and imaged on both the outer and cross-sectional surfaces. Thrombi formed on the catheter surfaces were fixed with 2.5 wt% glutaraldehyde, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, 100%), and examined by SEM. Thrombus weight and occlusion rate were quantified to assess the antithrombotic efficacy of the coatings.

Stability and durability of the coatings

To evaluate coating stability under dynamic conditions, the samples were fixed onto the inner surface of a beaker containing 300 mL of PBS and continuously stirred for 7 d. Their surface morphology and thickness were subsequently examined by SEM. For long-term stability assessment, the coatings were immersed in PBS at 37 °C for 30 d, after which the surface morphology and thickness were characterized by SEM.

TPU substrates and PDM@Cu coatings were immersed in 50 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C for 7, 15, and 30 days to evaluate coating durability. After each incubation period, the samples were characterized for protein adsorption, antibacterial activity, as well as platelet adhesion and activation.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of hydrogel coatings

The uniformity and surface coverage of the hydrogel coatings were first evaluated by rhodamine B staining. As shown in Fig. 2A, samples with PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu hydrogel coatings exhibited homogeneous red coloration, indicating continuous coating coverage. Side-view and cross-sectional images further confirmed conformal deposition along both the external surfaces and internal lumens. The surface morphology and coating continuity were further examined by SEM and EDS. Both surface and cross-sectional SEM images (Fig. S4 and Fig. 2B) revealed smooth and continuous coatings with comparable dry thicknesses of approximately 20-22 μm. Elemental mapping of N and P demonstrated uniform distribution throughout the coating surfaces and cross-sections, confirming consistent composition and successful deposition of the zwitterionic hydrogel network on both catheter lumens and flat substrates (Fig. S5). The chemical compositions of the PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu hydrogel coatings were further characterized by ATR-FTIR spectroscopy. Prior to UV irradiation, a characteristic absorption band at 1630 cm−1 associated with the C=C stretching vibrations of MPC, DMC, and MBA was observed (Fig. S6A). After UV irradiation, this band markedly decreased, indicating successful curing of the hydrogel coatings. Compared with bare TPU, the coated samples exhibited characteristic absorption bands corresponding to sulfonate groups (1168 cm−1), P-O (1052 cm−1), and P=O (1232 cm−1) vibrations of MPC, as well as a C-N+ stretching vibration at 970 cm−1, confirming the presence of quaternary ammonium functionalities derived from MPC and DMC (Fig. S6B). XPS analysis further verified the surface chemical composition of the hydrogel coatings. As shown in Fig. 2C, bare TPU exhibited only C, N, and O signals, whereas the coated surfaces displayed additional P and S signals, confirming the successful introduction of phosphorylcholine and sulfonate groups. Cu signals were exclusively detected in PD@Cu and PDM@Cu, indicating effective Cu incorporation. High-resolution XPS spectra (Fig. S7) revealed a characteristic N 1s component at ∼399.3 eV corresponding to quaternary ammonium nitrogen, along with P 2p (∼130.5 eV) and S 2p doublet peaks (∼164.4 and 165.9 eV) attributable to MPC and SPM, respectively. The presence of Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 peaks in PD@Cu and PDM@Cu further confirms the immobilization of Cu species through catechol-Cu2+ coordination.

The hydrogel coatings significantly enhanced surface hydrophilicity, which is a key requirement for blood-contacting interfaces. As shown in Fig. 2D, the water contact angle decreased from 101.5° for bare TPU to 30.5°, 22.7°, and 25.9° for PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu, respectively, reflecting enhanced interfacial hydration from zwitterionic groups and increased surface polarity induced by catechol-Cu2+ coordination. Such highly hydrated surfaces are favorable for minimizing nonspecific protein adsorption and blood component adhesion. Robust interfacial adhesion is critical for the long-term stability of coatings under physiological shear. Lap-shear tests showed comparable adhesion strengths of approximately 340 kPa for all hydrogel coatings (Fig. 2E), indicating that catechol-mediated bonding provided stable attachment to the TPU substrate regardless of the internal crosslinking mode.

Under hydrated conditions, all coatings formed porous hydrogel networks with similar swollen thicknesses of 133.99 ± 6.06 μm for PDM, 127.43 ± 1.65 μm for PD@Cu, and 126.64 ± 1.30 μm for PDM@Cu. Notably, PDM@Cu exhibited a more compact porous structure with an average pore size of approximately 4.2 μm (Fig. 2F), which is beneficial for maintaining coating integrity and resisting structural disruption during blood flow and repeated deformation. The denser network of PDM@Cu results from covalent crosslinking and catechol-Cu2+ coordination, while the comparable swollen thickness is attributed to the hydration-driven swelling of zwitterionic hydrogels with similar solid contents. Mechanical compliance coupled with structural robustness is critical for blood-contacting devices subjected to cyclic deformation. As shown in Fig. 2G, PDM hydrogels, dominated by covalent crosslinks and electrostatic interactions, exhibited moderate tensile strength (4.40 kPa) and limited extensibility (334%). PD@Cu hydrogels, governed by dynamic catechol-Cu2+ coordination, displayed markedly enhanced extensibility (710%) with comparable tensile strength (4.15 kPa), reflecting the energy-dissipative nature of reversible coordination bonds. Notably, PDM@Cu achieved both high tensile strength (15.20 kPa) and extensibility (991%), demonstrating synergistic reinforcement from the integration of covalent crosslinks, coordination bonds, and electrostatic interactions. Young's modulus showed only a modest increase from PDM (3.94 kPa) to PD@Cu (5.13 kPa) and PDM@Cu (5.98 kPa), whereas fracture energy increased substantially from 5.45 to 12.52 and 40.07 kJ m−3, respectively (Table S2), indicating that coordination interactions primarily enhance energy dissipation while covalent networks provide load-bearing capacity. Compression tests at 50% strain revealed compressive strengths of 8.11, 10.24, and 26.64 kPa and moduli of 36.28, 58.49, and 161.49 kPa for PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu, respectively (Fig. S9). These properties are relevant to catheter insertion and vascular compression conditions. Collectively, PDM@Cu exhibited the highest tensile and compressive performance, confirming synergistic mechanical reinforcement.

Tribological performance and wear resistance of hydrogel coatings

Lubricious interfaces are critical for implantable medical devices, as they reduce friction during insertion and minimize tissue damage. Accordingly, the tribological performance of the hydrogel coatings was systematically evaluated under varying normal loads, sliding frequencies, and prolonged sliding cycles. Under low-load conditions (1 N, 1 Hz), bare TPU exhibited a relatively high COF of 0.28 (Fig. 3A). In contrast, the friction of PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu hydrogel coatings significantly reduced to 0.13, 0.09, and 0.10, respectively, which is attributed to hydration boundary layers formed by zwitterionic moieties. With increasing normal load, the COF values of all hydrogel coatings gradually decreased and stabilized at 0.08 for PDM, 0.05 for PD@Cu, and 0.07 for PDM@Cu, indicating a boundary lubrication regime in which load-bearing is facilitated by compression of the hydrated hydrogel matrix. As the sliding frequency increased, the COF of bare TPU remained nearly unchanged, whereas the hydrogel coatings exhibited a moderate increase in COF to 0.19, 0.14, and 0.15 for PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu, respectively (Fig. 3B). This is attributed to restricted water replenishment at higher shear rates, which temporarily weakens the hydration layer and reduces lubrication efficiency.

Wear resistance under prolonged shear was further assessed using a 500-cycle sliding test (Fig. 3C). Bare TPU maintained a high COF of 0.25, while the PDM hydrogel coating showed a rapid increase in COF, reaching approximately 0.17 after about130 cycles, indicating progressive loss of lubricity. In contrast, PD@Cu and PDM@Cu coatings maintained low and stable COF values of 0.13 and 0.14, respectively demonstrating superior durability under repeated friction. SEM observations of coating morphology after friction tests were shown in Fig. 3D and E. After 500 sliding cycles, the surface of the PDM hydrogel coating exhibited evident wear features, accompanied by a noticeable reduction in coating thickness. Cross-sectional SEM images further revealed significant structural disruption and thinning of the PDM hydrogel layer. In contrast, the PD@Cu and PDM@Cu hydrogel coatings showed substantially reduced wear. The PD@Cu coating displayed only shallow abrasion traces on the surface, with limited thickness loss. PDM@Cu coating retained a smooth and continuous surface morphology after friction, and its cross-sectional structure remained intact, exhibiting the least thickness reduction among the tested samples. These results suggest that the synergistic combination of covalent crosslinks, dynamic catechol-Cu2+ coordination, and electrostatic interactions provides both mechanical robustness and efficient energy dissipation. During repeated sliding, reversible coordination bonds accommodate interfacial stresses, while covalent crosslinks maintain network integrity, enabling sustained lubricity and wear resistance. Such adaptive tribological behavior is particularly advantageous for blood-contacting and implantable medical devices subjected to repetitive mechanical loading.

Antifouling performance of hydrogel coatings

Protein adsorption is a critical early event after implantation, often triggering downstream platelet activation and thrombus formation. To evaluate protein-repellent performance, FITC-BSA was used as a model protein. Intense fluorescence signals were observed on bare TPU, indicating substantial protein adsorption, whereas all hydrogel-coated surfaces exhibited markedly reduced fluorescence (Fig. 4A), demonstrating effective protein resistance. To quantitatively assess protein adsorption, the adsorption of negatively charged BSA and fibrinogen and positively charged lysozyme were determined using absorbance-based assays. As shown in Fig. 4B–D, all hydrogel coatings significantly reduced protein adsorption compared with bare TPU, regardless of protein charge. BSA adsorption decreased from an absorbance value of 0.497 for TPU to 0.193, 0.179, and 0.133 for PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu, respectively. Similar trends are observed for fibrinogen and lysozyme. The charge-independent protein resistance indicates the formation of zwitterionic hydration layer that effectively suppresses protein deposition. Notably, the PDM@Cu coating exhibited the lowest protein adsorption among all samples, suggesting enhanced stabilization of the hydration layer. Cell adhesion was further investigated using L929 fibroblasts and RBCs to simulate potential biofouling on catheter surfaces. After 1 d of incubation, the TPU surface was densely covered by L929 cells with well-spread morphologies, whereas significantly fewer cells adhered to the hydrogel-coated surfaces. After 3 d, extensive cell proliferation was observed on TPU, while all hydrogel-coated surfaces maintained low cell densities with predominantly rounded cell morphologies (Fig. 4E), indicating sustained antifouling performance. Consistently, SEM analysis revealed extensive RBC attachment on bare TPU, whereas surfaces with PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu coatings exhibited minimal RBC adhesion with preserved cell morphology (Fig. 4F). These results demonstrate that the hydrogel coatings, particularly the multi-interaction PDM@Cu, exhibit robust antifouling performance against both protein adsorption and cellular adhesion. This behavior provides a favorable interfacial environment for blood-contacting applications and establishes a critical foundation for subsequent inhibition of platelet activation and thrombus formation.

In vitro anti adhesion and activation properties against platelets

Platelet adhesion and activation are central events in early thrombosis, during which activated platelets release procoagulant factors that promote fibrin formation and vascular occlusion. Effective hemocompatibility therefore relies on both active inhibition of platelet activation and passive suppression of platelet adhesion. Accordingly, NO generation and platelet interactions with the hydrogel coatings were systematically investigated. NO release was first quantified using a Griess-based assay (Fig. 5A). Bare TPU exhibited negligible NO generation (0.33 ± 0.04) × 10−10 mol cm−2·min−1, which may originate from weak redox interactions between GSH and GSNO. In contrast, the PDM hydrogel coating generated a higher NO flux (1.01 ± 0.08) × 10−10 mol cm−2·min−1, attributable to the reducing capability of catechol groups. Incorporation of Cu2+ further enhanced NO release, with PD@Cu and PDM@Cu achieving NO fluxes of (5.20 ± 0.29) and (5.44 ± 0.85) × 10−10 mol cm−2·min−1, respectively. The comparable release rates indicate similar Cu2+ chelation efficiencies, enabling sustained catalytic NO generation within physiological levels sufficient to inhibit platelet activation (Fig. S11). Platelet adhesion was evaluated using PRP. Fluorescence imaging with rhodamine 123 revealed dense platelet coverage on bare TPU, whereas all hydrogel-coated surfaces exhibited markedly reduced fluorescence signals (Fig. 5B). PD@Cu and PDM@Cu further suppressed platelet adhesion in the presence of an NO donor, highlighting the contribution of NO-mediated inhibition. These observations were corroborated by LDH assays (Fig. 5C), in which absorbance values for PD@Cu and PDM@Cu decreased from 0.179 to 0.162 and from 0.159 to 0.137, respectively, upon NO donor exposure. SEM analysis further revealed extensive platelet spreading with pseudopodia formation on TPU, whereas minimal platelet adhesion and predominantly rounded morphologies were observed on PD@Cu and PDM@Cu, particularly under NO-releasing conditions (Fig. 5D). The influence of the hydrogel coatings on coagulation pathways was further assessed by measuring APTT and TT. Bare TPU exhibited an APTT of 71 s and a TT of 27 s, whereas all hydrogel coatings significantly prolonged both parameters, with PDM@Cu showing the highest values APTT of 92 s and TT of 36 s (Fig. 5E and F). This anticoagulant behavior can be attributed to reduced platelet and protein interactions, together with the physical barrier provided by the hydrated hydrogel layer. Finally, recalcification assays were performed to simulate in situ thrombus formation. After 5 min, visible thrombi were observed on TPU but not on hydrogel-coated surfaces. After 10 min, coagulation occurred on all samples; however, PD@Cu and PDM@Cu coatings exhibited markedly reduced thrombus formation, particularly in the presence of an NO donor (Fig. 5G). Quantitative thrombus mass measurements confirmed this trend, with NO-enhanced PD@Cu and PDM@Cu reducing thrombus mass to 19 and 15 mg cm−2, respectively, compared with 109 mg cm−2 for TPU (Fig. S12). These results demonstrate that the antithrombotic performance of the hydrogel coatings arises from the synergistic integration of passive antifouling and active NO regulation. The zwitterionic hydration layer suppresses nonspecific protein adsorption and initial platelet adhesion, while catechol-Cu2+ coordination enables sustained NO generation to inhibit platelet activation and coagulation. The hybrid network structure of PDM@Cu stabilizes the hydrated interface and preserves NO activity under physiological conditions, offering a robust strategy for long-term blood-contacting medical devices.

Antibacterial performance of coatings

Bacterial infection is a major complication associated with interventional medical devices, leading to increased morbidity and mortality. To evaluate the antibacterial performance of the hydrogel coatings, S. aureus and E. coli were selected as representative Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, respectively. The antibacterial activity of bare TPU and PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu hydrogel coatings was first assessed using a live/dead bacterial viability assay, in which viable bacteria emit green fluorescence and nonviable bacteria appear red. Extensive green fluorescence was observed on bare TPU, indicating high bacterial viability. In contrast, all hydrogel-coated surfaces exhibited markedly reduced bacterial adhesion, with PD@Cu and PDM@Cu showing predominant red fluorescence, indicative of effective bactericidal activity (Fig. 6A). Plate counting assays further confirmed these observations (Fig. 6B–D). For E. coli, antibacterial efficiencies reached 92.35 ± 2.33% for PDM, 97.48 ± 1.01% for PD@Cu, and 98.53 ± 0.78% for PDM@Cu (Fig. 6C). Similarly, for S. aureus, antibacterial efficiencies of 92.25 ± 2.78%, 97.75 ± 0.71%, and 99.10 ± 0.71% were obtained for PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu, respectively (Fig. 6D). These results indicate that bacterial adhesion is effectively suppressed by the zwitterionic hydrogel coatings, consistent with their antifouling behavior toward proteins. To further elucidate the antibacterial mechanism, SEM imaging was performed after 24 h coincubation with bacterial suspensions (106 CFU mL−1). Bare TPU surfaces were densely covered with intact bacteria, while fewer bacteria were observed on PDM-coated surfaces with largely preserved morphology. In contrast, PD@Cu and PDM@Cu surfaces exhibited extensive bacterial membrane disruption and deformation (Fig. 6E).

(E) SEM images of bacteria morphologies adhered on bare TPU, PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu coated surface after 24 h incubation. (F) Schematic illustration of the dual antibacterial mechanism.

Beyond planktonic antibacterial activity, long-term resistance to biofilm formation was further evaluated using a crystal violet staining assay. As shown in Fig. S13, after co-incubation of bare and hydrogel-coated substrates with E. coli and S. aureus for 7 days, the bare TPU group exhibited intense purple staining, indicating the formation of dense and stable biofilms. In contrast, PDM, PD@Cu, and PDM@Cu-coated surfaces showed markedly reduced staining intensity, demonstrating significant inhibition of biofilm formation. This anti-biofilm behavior can be primarily attributed to the anti-adhesive nature of the zwitterionic hydrogel coating, which suppresses initial bacterial attachment. Notably, PD@Cu and PDM@Cu exhibited significantly lower absorbance values than PDM, indicating that Cu2+ incorporation further enhances resistance to biofilm formation.

These findings demonstrate a dual antibacterial strategy: the zwitterionic hydration layer passively resists bacterial adhesion, while Cu2+-mediated bactericidal activity actively disrupts bacterial membranes, resulting in efficient bacterial inactivation (Fig. 6F). The integration of antifouling and bactericidal functions provides a synergistic and durable antibacterial effect.

Cytotoxicity and hemocompatibility of coatings

The cytocompatibility of the hydrogel coatings was evaluated using L929 fibroblasts to assess their suitability for blood-contacting applications (Fig. 7A and B). After 1 d of incubation, all groups exhibited typical spindle-shaped morphology with no discernible differences from the negative control, along with comparable CCK-8 absorbance values 0.29, indicating the absence of acute cytotoxicity. After 3 d, cell densities increased markedly across all groups, with well-spread cells extending pseudopodia and maintaining normal fibroblastic morphology. Correspondingly, the CCK-8 absorbance increased to 0.74, confirming that cell proliferation was not inhibited by the hydrogel coatings. Hemocompatibility was further assessed by hemolysis testing. All hydrogel-coated samples exhibited hemolysis ratios well below the ISO 10993-4 threshold (5%), with values of 0.76 ± 0.12% (PDM), 0.84 ± 0.05% (PD@Cu), and 0.75 ± 0.09% (PDM@Cu), compared with 1.55 ± 0.29% for bare TPU (Fig. 7C). The positive control induced visible hemolysis, whereas both TPU and hydrogel-coated samples maintained transparent supernatants, indicating negligible erythrocyte lysis (Fig. S14). These results demonstrate that the hydrogel coatings exhibit excellent cytocompatibility and hemocompatibility. The favorable biological response can be attributed to the chemically stable and highly hydrated zwitterionic network, which minimizes leachable species and reduces nonspecific cell damage.

Ex vivo anti-thrombogenicity properties

To evaluate the antithrombotic efficacy of the hydrogel coatings under physiological flow conditions, an ex vivo arteriovenous shunt model was established in rabbits with a circulation time of 1 h (Fig. 8A). After circulation, the catheters were retrieved for macroscopic observation, cross-sectional imaging, and quantitative analysis of thrombus formation and luminal occlusion. Bare TPU catheters exhibited extensive thrombus deposition with near-complete luminal blockage (Fig. 8B and C). The PDM-coated catheters showed reduced thrombus formation, while PD@Cu and PDM@Cu coatings remained almost thrombus-free, maintaining vascular patency.

(A) Schematic illustration of the ex vivo arteriovenous shunt model in rabbits with a circulation time of 1 h. Representative (B) side views and (C) cross-sectional images of bare TPU and hydrogel-coated catheters after blood circulation. (D) Quantification of thrombus weight and (E) luminal occlusion rate of the catheters after blood circulation (n = 3). (F) SEM images of the inner surfaces of catheters after circulation.

Quantitative analysis revealed that thrombus mass decreased significantly from 0.031 ± 0.002 g cm−1 for bare TPU to 0.009 ± 0.001 g cm−1 for PDM, and further to 0.003 ± 0.001 g cm−1 and 0.002 ± 0.001 g cm−1 for PD@Cu and PDM@Cu, respectively (Fig. 8D). Correspondingly, the luminal occlusion ratio declined from 99.37% for TPU to 5.29% for PDM@Cu (Fig. 8E). SEM observations showed dense fibrin networks with entrapped platelets and red blood cells on bare TPU surfaces. In contrast, PDM coatings exhibited only partial fibrin deposition, whereas PD@Cu and PDM@Cu hydrogel coatings displayed minimal thrombus formation and cellular adhesion (Fig. 8F), consistent with the in vitro platelet adhesion results. These results demonstrate that the PDM@Cu coating exhibits robust thromboresistance under dynamic flow conditions, supporting its potential for improving the hemocompatibility of intravascular devices.

Durability of coatings

The durability of surface coatings is critical for blood-contacting devices exposed to continuous mechanical shear and biofluid environments. To evaluate the long-term structural and functional stability of the zwitterionic hydrogel coatings, their physical integrity and biofunctional performance were systematically assessed under simulated physiological conditions. Coating stability was first examined under continuous water flow for 7 d and static immersion for up to 30 d. Thickness measurements showed gradual thinning of PD@Cu coatings. It may associate with the progressive weakening of catechol-Cu2+ coordination during long-term immersion. PDM and PDM@Cu coatings, reinforced by covalent crosslinking, exhibited minimal thickness loss, indicating superior structural stability (Fig. S15). SEM images of freeze-dried hydrogel coatings further confirmed the preservation of the porous microstructure after prolonged immersion, supporting the mechanical robustness of the hydrogel network (Fig. S16 and S17). Both PD@Cu and PDM@Cu coatings exhibited a biphasic Cu2+ release profile, characterized by an initial burst release followed by a prolonged sustained-release phase (Fig. 9A). After 30 days, the cumulative Cu2+ release reached 17.8 μM for PD@Cu and 12.1 μM for PDM@Cu. These concentrations are well below the reported cytotoxic threshold 50 μM for endothelial cells and fibroblasts, indicating an adequate safety margin for long-term blood-contacting applications [54]. Correspondingly, despite partial ion loss, both PD@Cu and PDM@Cu maintained NO release within the physiological range of native endothelium (0.5-4.0 × 10−10 mol cm−2·min−1). Notably, PDM@Cu exhibited higher Cu2+ retention (49.1%) and more stable NO release ((3.91 ± 0.34) × 10−10 mol cm−2·min−1), reflecting the stabilizing effect of covalent crosslinks on metal coordination (Fig. 9B and C). This difference arises from the distinct network architectures. In PD@Cu, catechol-Cu2+ coordination acts as the crosslinking junction, resulting in a highly dynamic network that is prone to coordination dissociation and Cu2+ migration during prolonged immersion. By contrast, in PDM@Cu, catechol-Cu2+ complexes are confined within a robust covalently crosslinked framework, which restricts complex mobility and suppresses Cu2+diffusion, thereby markedly improving long-term Cu2+ retention and functional stability.

Importantly, the zwitterionic hydrogel coatings retained their antifouling performance after 30 d of immersion. Protein adsorption on PDM@Cu increased only slightly from 0.109 ± 0.004% to 0.121 ± 0.004%, whereas bare TPU exhibited pronounced fouling (1.37 ± 0.056%) (Fig. 9D and E). Antibacterial assays demonstrated that PDM@Cu maintained over 85% growth inhibition against both E. coli and S. aureus, indicating effective suppression of bacterial colonization and biofilm formation through the combined effects of hydration-mediated antifouling and Cu2+-mediated bactericidal activity (Fig. 9F–H). Long-term antithrombotic performance was further confirmed by SEM analysis following platelet exposure. After 30 d of immersion, PDM@Cu surfaces exhibited minimal platelet adhesion and no evident activation, with sustained NO generation effectively preventing platelet spreading and pseudopodia formation (Fig. 9I). These results underscore the importance of rational network architecture in achieving durable structural integrity and sustained biofunction. The zwitterionic hydrogel matrix provides a stable, hydrated antifouling interface, while covalent crosslinking enhances mechanical stability and supports long-term Cu2+ retention for continuous NO generation. This integrated design enables persistent resistance to protein, bacterial, and platelet adhesion, offering a robust and durable surface platform for implantable and interventional blood-contacting devices.

Conclusion

In this work, a zwitterionic hydrogel coating PDM@Cu integrating covalent crosslinking, catechol-Cu2+ coordination, and electrostatic interactions was successfully fabricated on TPU substrates via a simple dip-coating and UV-curing process. The resulting hybrid network imparted the hydrogel with enhanced tensile strength and flexibility, while providing strong interfacial adhesion and excellent wear resistance. The hydrated zwitterionic interface significantly reduced friction and effectively suppressed nonspecific protein adsorption as well as cell, platelet, and bacterial adhesion. Meanwhile, incorporated Cu2+ enabled sustained catalytic NO generation to inhibit platelet activation and thrombus formation, while its gradual release conferred broad-spectrum antibacterial activity. Ex vivo blood circulation experiments further demonstrated effective maintenance of catheter patency and pronounced thromboresistance. Importantly, the PDM@Cu hydrogel coating exhibited outstanding durability under prolonged shear and immersion, retaining its antifouling, antibacterial, and anticoagulant functions after 30 d. Overall, this study presents a robust and versatile zwitterionic hydrogel coating strategy that integrates mechanical integrity with long-term antithrombotic and antibacterial performance, offering a promising surface-engineering platform for blood-contacting medical devices.
