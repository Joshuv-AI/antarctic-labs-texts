L-cystine alleviates necrotizing enterocolitis by regulating ferroptosis and Th17 cell differentiation via the IL-6/STAT3 pathway
Authors: Qin Zhu, Yuyin Wang, Yifan Zhou, Xin Wu, Hongyan Zai, Yu Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) severely affects preterm infants with limited treatments. Although intestinal homeostasis dysfunction is considered a trigger for NEC, the key targets and mechanisms remain unclear. Using lipopolysaccharide-stimulated colonic epithelial cells and hypothermic hypoxia-induced NEC mice (both sexes), we demonstrate that the gut metabolite L-cystine alleviates intestinal inflammation by balancing Th17/Treg responses and inhibiting ferroptosis. Mechanistically, L-cystine directly targets KIF11 to suppress RC3H1 expression, blocking IL-6 transcripts through transcriptional modifications, thereby inhibiting IL-6 secretion and ferroptosis. Conditioned medium from L-cystine-treated cells inactivates IL-6/STAT3 signaling, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine release and restoring Th17/Treg balance. Notably, microbiota colonization from NEC preterm infants exacerbates intestinal damage, an effect mitigated by L-cystine and IL-6/STAT3 inhibition. Thus, L-cystine attenuates NEC by suppressing ferroptosis in epithelial cells, restoring immune homeostasis, and preserving intestinal barrier integrity. Targeting intestinal metabolites represents a promising prophylactic and therapeutic strategy for NEC, addressing unmet clinical needs in neonatal intestinal injury management.

Introduction

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a necrotizing gastrointestinal disorder primarily affecting preterm and very low birth weight infants1. The incidence of NEC in preterm neonates ranges from 2% to 13%2. Early NEC symptoms are insidious and nonspecific, but the disease can rapidly progress to fulminant NEC, characterized by intestinal inflammation, necrosis, and complications such as peritonitis, perforation, sepsis, and death3. The average mortality rate of NEC is 20–30%, rising to 50% in neonates requiring surgical resection of affected bowel segments4. Despite its severity, the pathophysiology of NEC remains incompletely understood, and reliable strategies for prevention or treatment are lacking.

Imbalanced gut microbiota colonization is a critical contributor to NEC pathogenesis5. Dysbiosis in preterm infants activates the innate immune receptor Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), leading to impaired immunity, epithelial cell death, and intestinal barrier disruption, ultimately elevating NEC risk6. Gut microbial metabolites, acting as critical mediators between the microbiota and the host, play a pivotal role in regulating disease pathogenesis7. For instance, restoration of bile acid metabolism alleviates NEC8, while acetate contributes to enhancing intestinal barrier integrity9. These findings highlight the gut metabolome as a potential therapeutic target for NEC.

Dysregulated T helper 17 (Th17)/regulatory T (Treg) cell balance, driven by gut microbiota disturbances, exacerbates intestinal inflammation in NEC10. Under physiological conditions, Th17 cells protect against microbial pathogens through pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-17A, IL-21, and IL-22), while Treg cells maintain immune tolerance via TGF-β1 and IL-10. However, during inflammation, excessive Th17 differentiation overwhelms Treg-mediated suppression, resulting in mucosal injury and impaired barrier repair11. Modulating the Th17/Treg axis has been proposed as a therapeutic strategy for NEC12. Intriguingly, IL-6, a recognized early biomarker of NEC13, is pivotal for Th17 differentiation. IL-6 signaling activates the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), promoting Th17 expansion and disrupting Th17/Treg equilibrium, thereby aggravating intestinal inflammation14. Nevertheless, whether gut metabolites influence NEC progression by regulating the IL-6/STAT3 pathway remains unclear.

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent programmed cell death mechanism, has been implicated in NEC pathogenesis15. Preterm intestinal tissues exhibit iron susceptibility, with intracellular iron overload driving lipid peroxide accumulation and ferroptosis16. Ferroptotic cells release damage-associated molecular patterns, lipid peroxidation products, and chemokines, further amplifying intestinal inflammation17. Protecting intestinal epithelial cells from ferroptosis mitigates barrier injury and inflammation in NEC mouse models18,19. Notably, IL-6/STAT3 signaling enhances Fe²⁺-mediated lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis20, while also promoting NCOA4-dependent ferritinophagy, leading to iron overload21. Emerging evidence suggests that gut microbial metabolites modulate ferroptosis to maintain intestinal homeostasis22,23. These findings collectively link gut metabolites, IL-6/STAT3 signaling, and ferroptosis, prompting our investigation into their interplay in NEC.

In this study, we analyzed fecal samples from NEC human infants to identify altered gut metabolites and uncovered the protective role of L-cystine. We demonstrated that L-cystine reduces intestinal IL-6 levels, restores Th17/Treg balance, and suppresses colonic epithelial ferroptosis, ultimately attenuating NEC risk. Mechanistically, L-cystine inhibits the IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway by targeting KIF11/RC3H1. Our findings deepen the understanding of gut metabolites in NEC pathogenesis and provide a theoretical foundation for L-cystine as a preventive or therapeutic agent for NEC.

Results

Intestinal metabolic dysregulation in NEC infants

We analyzed fecal samples from six NEC infants and three healthy controls to identify disease-associated metabolites. Metabolomic profiling revealed distinct clustering between NEC and control infants, with 1,425 metabolites upregulated and 1,431 downregulated in NEC (Fig. 1A). A lollipop plot displayed 15 upregulated and downregulated differential metabolites, predominantly various amino acids (Fig. 1B). KEGG enrichment analysis linked NEC to pathways in Cell Growth and Death and Immune System (Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, L-glutamic acid (C00025), L-cystine (C00491), and adrenic acid (C16527) were associated with ferroptosis, while all-trans-retinoic acid (C00777) was implicated in Th17 differentiation. L-Glutamic acid24 and all-trans-retinoic acid25 have established roles in NEC, adrenic acid may exacerbate inflammation and oxidative stress26,27. Based on representative TICs under both positive and negative ion modes shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, we observed chromatographic peaks corresponding to the target metabolite L-cystine. NEC patients exhibit significantly elevated endogenous L-cystine levels (Fig. 1C), as confirmed by LC-MS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3), which may reflect exacerbated oxidative stress levels in the NEC disease state. L-cystine suppresses LPS-induced IL-6 secretion and ferroptosis in colonic epithelial cells

Cystine uptake is a rate-limiting step in ferroptosis. Reduced cystine uptake disrupts GSH synthesis, impairing antioxidant capacity and promoting ferroptosis28. The standard chemical structure of L-cystine is depicted in Fig. 2A. To evaluate L-cystine’s role in ferroptosis, NCM-460 cells were treated with LPS (1 μg/mL), L-cystine (0.8 mM), or both for 24 h. LPS alone reduced cell viability, whereas L-cystine alone enhanced viability (Fig. 2B). Given the involvement of IL-6/STAT3 signaling in ferroptosis, LPS increased intracellular and secreted IL-6 levels, which were suppressed by L-cystine (Fig. 2C, D). LPS elevated Fe²⁺ accumulation and ROS production—hallmarks of ferroptosis—while L-cystine mitigated these effects (Fig. 2E, F). Immunofluorescence confirmed that LPS increased the lipid peroxidation marker 4-HNE, which L-cystine attenuated (Fig. 2G). Consistently, LPS elevated MDA and reduced GSH/SOD activity, whereas L-cystine reversed these trends (Fig. 2H, I). Mechanistically, LPS upregulated phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) and nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) while downregulating ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1). L-cystine suppressed p-STAT3 and NCOA4 but restored FTH1 expression (Fig. 2J). RC3H1-IL-6 interaction mediates L-cystine’s suppression of IL-6 secretion and ferroptosis

CM from LPS-treated NCM-460 cells reduced Treg frequency and increased Th17 populations, lowering the Treg/Th17 ratio. These effects were reversed by L-cystine and IL-6 Ab (Fig. 7B, C). L-cystine Attenuates Intestinal Injury in Mice by Reprogramming the IL-6/STAT3-Modulated Metabolic Profile

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) restructures the gut microbiota and modifies microbial-derived metabolites, thereby influencing host pathophysiology29. FMT from NEC infants induced NEC-like pathology in both male and female mice. L-cystine and S3I-201 mitigated weight loss in microbiota-colonized NEC mice (Fig. 8A). H&E-stained sections revealed villus disruption, significant crypt loss, compromised integrity of intestinal epithelial cells, and exacerbated inflammatory cell infiltration in NEC mice and microbiota-colonized NEC mice compared to Normal mice, with significantly elevated histopathological scores. L-cystine and S3I-201 treatment mitigated inflammatory responses and intestinal damage in microbiota-colonized NEC mice (Fig. 8B, C). L-cystine and S3I-201 decreased intestinal permeability (Fig. 8D), increased Treg populations, and reduced Th17 frequencies (Fig. 8E, F). Pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-2, IL-17) were downregulated, while anti-inflammatory markers (Foxp3, IL-10, TGF-β1) were upregulated (Fig. 8G, H). L-cystine also reduced Fe²⁺, ROS, and MDA while restoring GSH/SOD levels, effects potentiated by S3I-201 (Fig. 9A–C). TEM revealed that L-cystine and S3I-201 rescued mitochondrial shrinkage and cristae loss in colon tissues (Fig. 9D). Both treatments suppressed p-STAT3 and NCOA4 while restoring FTH1 and reducing 4-HNE (Fig. 9E–G). Discussion

NEC is one of the most severe gastrointestinal diseases in neonates, posing significant threats to survival and long-term quality of life. Emerging evidence suggests that precise regulation of gut microbiota composition may reduce NEC risk30. As the regions with the richest intestinal microbiota, metabolic disturbances in the ileum and colon may constitute a critical hub driving systemic inflammation31. Therefore, the ileum and colon likely share the core pathological process of “intestinal barrier disruption-dysbiosis-systemic inflammation” in NEC32,33. Microbial metabolites serve as key mediators of host-microbiome crosstalk, and their role in NEC prevention and treatment warrants further exploration34. This study demonstrates that L-cystine alleviates intestinal inflammation, preserves barrier integrity, and mitigates NEC progression. Specifically, L-cystine reduces Th17 differentiation, suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and restores Th17/Treg balance. Additionally, L-cystine inhibits ferroptosis in colonic epithelial cells, attenuating oxidative stress and inflammation. Mechanistically, we show that L-cystine directly targets KIF11 to suppress the KIF11/RC3H1 axis, thereby reducing IL-6 expression and inactivating IL-6/STAT3 signaling. These findings highlight L-cystine as a promising therapeutic candidate for NEC.

Cystine, a precursor for GSH synthesis, is recognized for its antioxidative and anti-inflammatory potential35. Cystine depletion exacerbates oxidative stress by reducing GSH levels, thereby triggering ferroptosis36,37. Clinical trials indicate that cystine, combined with theanine, suppresses post-esophagectomy inflammation by enhancing GSH38. Preclinical studies further support cystine’s ability to counteract oxidative stress-induced barrier dysfunction and intestinal inflammation39. Elevated blood L-cystine levels in patients with acute-on-chronic liver failure may reflect a compensatory mechanism to maintain homeostasis during systemic inflammation40. Consistent with these observations, our study reveals that L-cystine inhibits iron overload, restores GSH synthesis, and suppresses ferroptosis in colonic epithelial cells—a critical strategy for mitigating inflammatory bowel pathologies, including NEC41,42.

This study identifies a function for L-cystine in balancing intestinal Th17/Treg dynamics. Th17 cells are pivotal in NEC pathogenesis, as IL-17 disrupts epithelial tight junctions, promotes apoptosis, and inhibits proliferation, whereas IL-17 inhibition-driven Treg activation counteracts these effects43,44. Our co-culture experiments demonstrate that L-cystine reduces Th17 populations and increases Treg frequencies, likely mediated by diminished IL-6 secretion. IL-6, a pleiotropic cytokine produced by epithelial cells during LPS challenge or pro-inflammatory signaling45, drives Treg-to-Th17 transdifferentiation. Blocking IL-6 signaling inhibits this shift and ameliorates NEC46. Here, anti-IL-6 antibodies enhanced Treg populations and suppressed LPS-induced Th17 differentiation, while rIL-6 promoted Th17 polarization. Notably, L-cystine attenuated IL-6 elevation in both cell culture and intestinal tissues, aligning with reports that cystine suppresses sepsis-associated inflammation by reducing IL-6 in blood and spleen47. In vivo, FMT from NEC infants exacerbated intestinal injury in mice, which L-cystine alleviated by inhibiting IL-6/STAT3-driven ferroptosis and pro-inflammatory cytokine release.

Mechanistic investigations revealed that IL-6 activity is regulated by the KIF11/RC3H1 axis. RC3H1 (Roquin), an RNA-binding protein with established roles in inflammation and immunity48, stabilizes IL-6 mRNA to activate IL-6/STAT3 signaling. Our database screening and DARTS assays identified KIF11 as a direct L-cystine target that interacts with RC3H1. KIF11 positively regulates RC3H1 and IL-6/STAT3, counteracting L-cystine’s anti-inflammatory effects. Functionally, KIF11 or RC3H1 overexpression exacerbated LPS-induced ferroptosis and reversed L-cystine’s protective actions. While RC3H1 enhances GPX4 ubiquitination to promote ferroptosis in cancer cells49, and KIF11 correlates with immune cell infiltration in rheumatoid arthritis50, their roles in NEC-associated inflammation and ferroptosis were previously undefined. Our work establishes that L-cystine suppresses ferroptosis and hyperinflammation in NEC via the KIF11/RC3H1/IL-6/STAT3 axis.

This study has several limitations that inform future research priorities. Firstly, the therapeutic potential of L-cystine remains clinically unvalidated. Future work should explore delivery strategies (e.g., chemical modification or nanocarriers) to enhance its bioavailability and efficacy. Secondly, conditional knockout models are essential to dissect the mechanistic roles of the KIF11/RC3H1/IL-6 axis in L-cystine-mediated T-cell differentiation and ferroptosis. The dual functionality of RC3H1 as both an RNA-binding protein and E3 ubiquitin ligase warrants further investigation into its coordinated regulation of IL-6 at transcriptional and post-translational levels. Thirdly, while non-targeted metabolomics identified key metabolites like L-cystine, absolute quantification using targeted LC-MS/MS with certified reference standards is imperative for definitive validation in subsequent mechanistic or clinical studies. We emphasize that all critical metabolites will undergo standard comparisons and MS/MS mirror plot analyses, with multiple testing correction prioritized to ensure statistical rigor. Furthermore, the pathophysiological relevance of ileum-colon crosstalk in NEC and L-cystine’s role therein requires systematic evaluation. Given that hydrogen sulfide (H₂S)—a metabolite derived from L-cystine via cystathionine β-synthase (CBS) and cystathionine γ-lyase (CSE)—alleviates NEC by dilating microvessels and improving mucosal perfusion51,52, future studies must integrate precise H₂S measurement with intestine-specific CBS/CSE manipulation to elucidate the causal relationship between L-cystine metabolism and H₂S generation under NEC pathology. Finally, investigating synergistic interactions between L-cystine and established antioxidants (e.g., vitamin D) could unlock combinatorial therapeutic strategies against NEC.

In conclusion, our findings identify L-cystine supplementation as a viable strategy to alleviate intestinal inflammation and barrier dysfunction. By downregulating KIF11/RC3H1 to reduce IL-6 levels, L-cystine restores Th17/Treg balance and inhibits ferroptosis via IL-6/STAT3 signaling. These insights advance our understanding of gut metabolites in NEC pathogenesis and highlight potential therapeutic avenues.

Methods

Human samples

Twelve neonates diagnosed with stage II or higher necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) based on modified Bell’s staging criteria53 (NEC group) and twelve age-matched infants hospitalized for non-gastrointestinal conditions (e.g., neonatal pneumonia or hyperbilirubinemia; control group) were recruited from the Xiangya Hospital of Central South University between March 2024 and March 2025. The Control group and the NEC group each consisted of 5 male and 7 female infants, with mean ages of 200.75 ± 10.75 days and 202.67 ± 17.23 days, and mean weights of 1240.42 ± 522.58 g and 1200.83 ± 298.59 g, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (AF/SQ2025030101), and written informed consent was obtained from their legal guardians. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Fresh fecal samples were collected using sterile disposable tubes and aliquoted (0.25 g per sample) into pre-sterilized EP tubes for storage at −80°C. Given the limited availability of neonatal fecal specimens, following metabolite extraction, three control and six NEC samples were selected for analysis with uniform sample input to ensure result reliability. The remaining fecal specimens were preserved for fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in subsequent animal experiments. The sex of infants used for metabolomics analysis, FMT, and peripheral blood extraction was not recorded.

Untargeted metabolomics

Fecal metabolites were extracted using methanol/acetonitrile solution under vacuum concentration. The homogenized samples were vortexed in 50% methanol-water and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and subjected to liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Thermo Vanquish UHPLC system equipped with an HSS T3 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.7 μm) under the following conditions: injection volume, 2 μL; column temperature, 40 °C; flow rate, 0.3 mL/min; mobile phases A (0.1% formic acid in water), B (0.1% formic acid in methanol), C (0.05% acetic acid in water), and D (0.05% acetic acid in methanol). Mass spectrometry was conducted on a Thermo QE HF-X system with electrospray ionization (ESI) in both positive and negative ion modes. Key parameters included: sheath gas flow, 50 arb; auxiliary gas flow, 13 arb; spray voltage, 2.5 kV (±); capillary temperature, 325 °C; auxiliary gas temperature, 300 °C; MS/MS collision energy, 30 eV; and scan range, 70–1050 m/z.

Data acquisition for the test samples was performed under the aforementioned LC-MS conditions, yielding Total Ion Chromatography (TIC) profiles. Raw mass spectrometry data were processed using Compound Discovery software to perform peak detection, peak shape alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction. Metabolite structural identification was achieved via accurate mass matching (<10 ppm) and MS/MS spectral matching against the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), MassBank, LipidMaps, mzCloud, and KEGG databases within the Compound Discovery platform, assigning one or more potential metabolite identifications to each feature. The identified metabolites and their peak area data from both positive ion mode (POS) and negative ion mode (NEG) were exported as separate tables. Differential analysis was performed separately on the POS and NEG datasets using R/Python in a Linux environment, with results merged into a master table containing all differential metabolites (a total of 3872). When the same metabolite was identified in both ionization modes, the data row corresponding to the mode with the larger total peak area was retained. All identifications were classified as Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) Level 2 (putatively annotated compounds). Following peak area normalization, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using R packages. Metabolites with a Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) > 1 from the OPLS-DA model were selected and subsequently subjected to independent sample t-tests. Metabolites with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant and defined as differential metabolites. The direction of differential expression was determined by Fold Change (FC), where FC > 1 indicated upregulation and FC < 1 indicated downregulation. Using R, a lollipop plot was generated based on VIP and FC values, displaying 15 upregulated and downregulated differential metabolites. The differential metabolites were subsequently subjected to functional annotation via KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (adjusted p-value < 0.05).

Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS)

Sample analysis was performed using a Shimadzu Prominence UPLC system (Nexera UHPLC LC-30A, Kyoto, Japan) coupled to an AB SCIEX Triple TOF 5600+ system (AB Sciex, Singapore) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. The injection volume was 3 µL. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm particle size; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phases consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile (mobile phase B). The elution gradient was programmed as follows: initial isocratic conditions at 1% B (99% A) for 1.5 min, followed by a linear increase to 99% B over 13 min, which was then maintained for 3.5 min. Subsequently, the mobile phase composition was rapidly returned to 1% B within 0.1 min and held for 5 min for column re-equilibration. The mobile phase flow rate was set at 0.3 mL/min.

The mass spectrometer was operated in both positive and negative ion information-dependent acquisition (IDA) modes. Instrument parameters were set as follows: source temperature, 550°C; ion source gas 1 and 2 pressures, 55 psi; curtain gas pressure, 35 psi; ion spray voltage floating, +5.5 kV (positive mode) and −4.5 kV (negative mode). Full scan mass spectra were acquired with an accumulation time of 150 ms, while each IDA experiment used a 45 ms accumulation time. The mass range was set from m/z 60 to m/z 1,250. Collision energy was set to ±30 eV. Peaks corresponding to compounds with intensities exceeding 100 counts per second (cps), determined by summing the signal from 10 consecutive IDA scans, were selected for further analysis. Data acquisition was performed using Analyst TF 1.6 software (SCIEX). Data analysis, including deconvolution and application of streamline criteria for L-cystine qualification and quantification, was conducted using AB SCIEX OS software.

Animal experiments

All animal protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (AF/SQ2025030101). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Pregnant C57BL/6 J mice (SCXK[Xiang]2021-0002) were purchased from Hunan Slack Jingda Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. Forty-two neonatal pups (both sexes) were fed a standardized formula (Similac formula:Esbilac pet milk = 2:1) via gavage (0.03 mL/g body weight, 5 times daily) from birth. Six mice were randomly allocated to each experimental group, with three designated for histological analysis and three for validation of regulatory mechanisms, respectively. Mice were maintained under a 12-hour light/dark cycle at 23 ± 1 °C with food and water provided ad libitum.

For FMT, pups received an antibiotic cocktail (100 mg/kg piperacillin-tazobactam, 10 mg/kg vancomycin [1404-90-6, Macklin, Shanghai, China], 25 mg/kg ampicillin [69-53-4, Macklin], 50 mg/kg metronidazole [443-48-1, Macklin], in saline solution) via gavage (0.3–0.5 mL/g body weight) for 3 days to establish a germ-free environment54. Subsequently, 100 μL of fecal suspension from NEC infants (in PBS) was administered daily via oral gavage until day 10 55. NEC induction was performed in 10-day-old pups by exposing them to hypoxia (95% N₂) and hypothermia (4°C) twice daily for 10 min over 4 days56–58. During the modeling process, NEC mice exhibited significant loss of appetite, decreased food intake, and dull fur, consistent with disease manifestations. For therapeutic intervention, pups received daily oral L-cystine (200 mg/mL in saline solution; C₆H₁₂N₂O₄S₂, 56-89-3, MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA)59 and intraperitoneal injections of the STAT3 inhibitor S3I-201 (0.5 mg/mL in DMSO and 20% cyclodextrin; 501919-59-1, MCE) twice weekly60. The Normal group mice received an equivalent dose of the vehicle. On day 14, pups were euthanized via overdose sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg in saline solution) injection, and intestinal tissues, luminal contents (PBS-rinsed), and serum samples were collected for analysis.

Cell culture and treatments

Human normal colon epithelial NCM-460 cells (AW-CCH306, Abiowell, Changsha, China) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C under 5% CO₂. Cells were treated with 1 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS; dissolved in sterile water) for 24 h61, with or without 0.8 mM L-cystine intervention62. For transfection experiments, NCM-460 cells were transfected with si-RC3H1 (HG-SH172071), oe-RC3H1 (HG-HO172071), si-KIF11 (HG-SH004523), or oe-KIF11 plasmids (HG-HO004523, HonorGene, Changsha, China) using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (11668019, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and incubated for 48 h.

Human native CD4 + T-Cell isolation and co-culture

Peripheral blood was collected from twelve infants in the control group, and human peripheral blood lymphocytes were isolated via density gradient centrifugation. CD4 + T cells were purified using CD4 (L3T4) MicroBeads (10 μL per 10⁷ cells; 130-117-043, Miltenyi Biotec, Cologne, Germany) and validated by flow cytometry (FITC-conjugated CD4 antibody; 11-0049-80, eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA), achieving >90% purity.

For co-culture experiments, CD4 + T cells were incubated for 48 h with conditioned medium (CM) collected from NCM-460 cells subjected to different treatments63. Co-cultures were treated with anti-IL-6 antibody (IL-6 Ab; 900 nM, SIM0030, BioXCell, West Lebanon, NH, USA)64, recombinant IL-6 (rIL-6; CSB-YP011664HU, CUSABIO, Wuhan, China)65, or S3I-201 (50 µM)66 for 48 h.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

Colon tissues and distal ileal tissues (1 cm) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into 4 μm slices. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol solutions, and stained with hematoxylin (AWI0001a, Abiowell) and eosin (AWI0029a, Abiowell). Histopathological changes were evaluated using a light microscope (BA210T, Motic, Xiamen, China). Two blinded examiners conducted pathological injury assessments on colonic and ileal tissue sections using a 0–4 scale67,68.

Immunofluorescence

For tissue sections, antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer via microwave heating after deparaffinization. Autofluorescence was quenched by sequential incubation in sodium borohydride, 75% ethanol, and Sudan Black B. For cells, coverslips were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with Triton X-100. After blocking, samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-4-HNE primary antibody (ab48506, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), followed by 1 h incubation with Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (AWS0003a, Abiowell) at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured using a Motic BA410 fluorescence microscope (Motic) and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC)-Dextran Permeability Assay

On day 14, pups were fasted for 4 h and administered 44 mg FITC-dextran per 100 g body weight via gavage. Serum was collected 1 h post-administration. Fluorescence intensity was measured at 528 nm using a fluorescence microplate reader.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated in graded acetone, and infiltrated with Epon-812 resin. Ultrathin sections (60–90 nm) were cut using a Leica ultramicrotome (Switzerland), mounted on copper grids, and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Images were acquired using a JEM-1400Flash Electron Microscope (JEOL, Japan).

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) and Biochemical Tests

IL-6 levels in cell supernatants were quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (KE00139, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Intracellular Fe²⁺ (JL-T1255, JONLNBIO, Shanghai, China), glutathione (GSH; A006-2-1), malondialdehyde (MDA; A003-1-3), and superoxide dismutase (SOD; A001-3-2) in cell lysates or tissue homogenates were measured using respective kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Optical density values were determined using a microplate reader (MB-530, HEALES, Shenzhen, China).

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)

Cell viability of NCM-460 cells was assessed using a CCK-8 kit (Dojindo Laboratories, Tokyo, Japan). Briefly, 300 μL of cell suspension (5 × 10⁴ cells/well) was seeded into 24-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, 30 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 4 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from intestinal tissues or NCM-460 cells using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Thermo Scientific, Portsmouth, NH, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the HiFiScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (CW2569M, CWBIO, Taizhou, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. qRT-PCR was performed on a QuantStudio1 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Scientific) with UltraSYBR Mixture (CW2601M, CWBIO). Gene expression was normalized to β-actin and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP)-qPCR

The interaction between RC3H1 and IL-6 mRNA was analyzed using a commercial kit (RIP-12RXN, Sigma-Aldrich). Magnetic beads were pre-incubated with 5 μg of anti-RC3H1 antibody (ab70195, Abcam) or species-matched IgG control (Proteintech) and mixed with cell lysates overnight at 4 °C. After washing with RIP buffer, RNA-protein complexes were digested with Proteinase K. RNA was isolated using TRIzol, and IL-6 m6A levels were quantified via qRT-PCR.

Western blot analysis

Total proteins were extracted from tissues or cells using RIPA buffer (R0010, Solarbio, Beijing, China) containing protease inhibitors. Protein samples were separated via 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h, incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 3) overnight at 4 °C, and then probed with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (SA00001, Proteintech). Signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) on a ChemiScope6100 system (CLiNX, China) and quantified with ImageJ software (NIH, USA). All the uncropped western bot images are provided in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cell lysates were incubated with anti-RC3H1 (84323-1-RR, Proteintech), anti-IL-6 (21865-1-AP, Proteintech) or IgG (B900610, Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C. Protein A/G agarose beads were added for 4 h at 4 °C, followed by extensive washing with IP lysis buffer. Immunoprecipitated complexes were eluted and analyzed by Western blot.

Drug Affinity–Responsive Target Stabilization (DARTS) assay

NCM-460 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer and treated with L-cystine (0.4, 0.8, or 1.2 mM) for 1 h. Lysates were digested with 20 μg/mL Pronase E (HY-114158A, MCE) at room temperature for 30 min. KIF11 protein levels were evaluated by Western blot.

ROS measurement by flow cytometry

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) were detected using a commercial kit (S0033S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). For tissues, intestinal fragments were digested with 0.2% collagenase IV, filtered through a 100 μm strainer, and treated with red blood cell lysis buffer. Cell suspensions were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 20 min. ROS levels were analyzed via flow cytometry (A00-1-1102, Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA) and FlowJo software (TreeStar, Ashland, OR, USA).

Cell sorting by flow cytometry

Treg and Th17 cells were sorted from purified CD4 + T cells. Cells were stained with surface markers CD4-FITC and CD25-APC (17-0257-42). PMA/ionomycin (00-4975-93) was added for 4 h. After fixation and permeabilization, cells were intracellularly stained with IL-17A-PE (12-7178-42) and Foxp3-PE (12-4776-42, eBioscience). Subpopulations were sorted using a flow cytometer. Gating strategies for flow cytometry plots are shown in Supplementary Fig. 5.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between two groups were assessed using Student’s t-test, while multi-group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test (GraphPad Prism 9.0). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments included at least three biological replicates, with each biological replicate comprising three technical replicates.
