Diophantine approximation and the Mass Transference Principle: incorporating the unbounded setup
Bing Li, Lingmin Liao, Sanju Velani, Baowei Wang, Evgeniy Zorin

Abstract
We develop the Mass Transference Principle for rectangles of Wang & Wu (Math. Ann. 2021) to incorporate the ‘unbounded’ setup; that is, when along some direction the lower order (at infinity) of the side lengths of the rectangles under consideration is infinity. As applications, we obtain the Hausdorff dimension of naturally occurring lim sup \limsup sets within the classical framework of simultaneous Diophantine approximation and the dynamical framework of shrinking target problems. For instance, concerning the former, for τ > 0 \tau>0 , let S ⁡ ( τ ) S(\tau) denote the set of ( x 1 , x 2 ) ∈ ℝ 2 (x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2} simultaneously satisfying the inequalities ‖ q ​ x 1 ‖ < q − τ \|qx_{1}\|\,<\,q^{-\tau} and ‖ q ​ x 2 ‖ < e − q \|qx_{2}\|\,<\,e^{-q} for infinitely many q ∈ ℕ q\in{\mathbb{N}} . Then, the ‘unbounded’ Mass Transference Principle enables us to show that dim H S ⁡ ( τ ) = min ⁡ { 1 , 3 / ( 1 + τ ) } \dim_{\rm H}S(\tau)\,=\,\min\big\{1,3/(1+\tau)\big\}\, .

Introduction

To set the scene, we start by recalling the so called ‘weighted’ version of the classical Jarník–Besicovitch Theorem in the theory of simultaneous Diophantine approximation. For
1\leq i\leq d
, let
\psi_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
be a real positive function. For convenience, let

\Psi:=(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{d})

and in turn, let
W_{d}(\Psi)
denote the set of points
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in{\mathbb{I}}^{d}:=[0,1]^{d}
for which the inequalities

\|qx_{i}\|\leq\psi_{i}(q)\quad(1\leq i\leq d)\ \

(1.1)

hold for infinitely many
q\in\mathbb{N}
. Throughout,
\|\ .\ \|
denotes the distance to the nearest integer.
Given
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
\tau_{i}>0
for all
1\leq i\leq d
, in the case
\psi_{i}:q\to q^{-\tau_{i}}
we write
W_{d}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
for
W_{d}(\Psi)
.
On making use of the Borel–Cantelli lemma from probability theory, it is relatively straightforward to show that

m_{d}(W_{d}(\Psi))=0\quad{\rm if}\quad\textstyle{\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}}\psi_{1}(q)\times\dots\times\psi_{d}(q)\,<\,\infty\,,

where
m_{d}
is the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. In this situation, it is natural to consider the size of the set under consideration in terms of Hausdorff dimension
\dim_{\rm H}
.
The following statement is due to Rynne
[
14
]
.
It builds on the fundamental works of Jarník, Besicovitch, Eggleston and Dodson – see
[
14
]
for the references. Throughout, for any
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{+})^{d}
we let

\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t}):=\frac{d+1+\sum_{k:t_{k}<t_{i}}(t_{i}-t_{k})}{1+t_{i}}\qquad\quad(1\leq i\leq d)\,.

(1.2)

Theorem R ​​​
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and assume that
0<\tau_{i}<\infty
for all
1\leq i\leq d

and that
\tau_{1}+\cdots+\tau_{d}>1
. Then

\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\zeta_{i}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\,.

Regarding the general function
\Psi=(\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{d}):{\mathbb{R}}\to({\mathbb{R}}^{+})^{d}
, as pointed out explicitly by Rynne
[
14
, Corollary 1]
, his theorem easily allows us to deduce that if the limits

\lambda_{i}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{-\log\psi_{i}(n)}{\log n}\qquad(1\leq i\leq d)

(1.3)

exist and that
0<\lambda_{i}<\infty
for all
1\leq i\leq d

and
\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{d}>1
, then

\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)=\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\zeta_{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\qquad{\rm where}\qquad\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\,.

(1.4)

In other words, if the order at infinity of the functions
\psi_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq d
) exist (and indeed are finite) then we are in good shape.
However, this is a pretty restrictive condition on
\Psi=(\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{d})
and the problem of determining the dimension of
W_{d}(\Psi)
for general monotonic
\Psi
remained open until recently. In view of earlier work of Dodson (see Remark
1
below) the general consensus was that the dimensional formula given by (
1.4
) is true with the order replaced by the lower order at infinity of
\psi_{i}
(i.e. replacing the limit in (
1.3
) by the limit infimum). In fact, Rynne stated in his paper that this can be proved by combining his arguments with those stemming from the work of Dodson. However, this turns out not to be the case.

The breakthrough for determining the dimension of
W_{d}(\Psi)
for general monotonic
\Psi
was made in the pioneering work of Wang
\&
Wu
[
17
]
in which they developed the so-called ‘rectangles to rectangles’ Mass Transference Principle – see §
2
. In short, as an application of their extremely versatile mass transference principle, they showed that
\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)
is dependent on the set
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
of accumulation points
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{d})
of the sequence

\displaystyle\Big\{\Big(\frac{-\log\psi_{1}(n)}{\log n},\cdots,\frac{-\log\psi_{d}(n)}{\log n}\Big)\Big\}_{n\geq 1}\,.

(1.5)

Theorem WW ​​​
.

For
1\leq i\leq d
, let
\psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a real, positive, non-increasing function and suppose that
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
is bounded. Then

\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)=\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)}{\min}\left\{\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t}){,d}\right\}.

Remark 1
.

It is easily seen that if
\psi_{i}:q\to q^{-\tau_{i}}
(
\tau_{i}>0
) for all
1\leq i\leq d
, then
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)=\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d}){\in({\mathbb{R}}^{+})^{d}}
and so
Theorem WW implies Theorem R. Moreover, in the case
\psi_{1}=\ldots=\psi_{d}:=\psi
, let us write
W_{d}(\psi)
for
W_{d}(\Psi)
and observe that Theorem WW implies that for
\lambda>1/d

\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\psi)=\frac{d+1}{1+\lambda}\,,

where
\lambda
is the lower order of
\psi
.
This is Dodson’s theorem. Specialising further, given a real number
\tau>0
, we write
W_{d}(\tau)
for
W_{d}(\psi:x\mapsto x^{-\tau})
and we recovers the simultaneous version of the classical Jarník–Besicovitch theorem; that is to say that
\tau
replaces
\lambda
in the statement of Dodson’s theorem.

Remark 2
.

Theorem R explicitly requires that the exponents
\tau_{i}\ (1\leq i\leq d)
are bounded or in its slightly more general form that the order
\lambda_{i}
at infinity of the functions
\psi_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq d
) are bounded. The situation in which one or more of the exponents is unbounded cannot be inferred from the bounded statement – see below for an explicit example that illustrates this point.
Turning our attention to Theorem WW, as already mentioned the proof makes use of the ‘rectangles to rectangles’ Mass Transference Principle of Wang
\&
Wu. It turns out that in order to apply the latter it is necessary to impose the condition that the set
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
of accumulation points is bounded. The main goal of this work is to extend their mass transference principle to incorporate the ‘unbounded’ setup (see Theorem
2.1
in §
2.1
) and thereby remove the need for
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
to be bounded within the framework of Theorem WW – see Theorem
1.1
below. In turn, this means that we can establish a version of Theorem R free from the condition that the exponents
\tau_{i}
are bounded – see Corollary
1.1
below.

We now give a concrete example that illustrates the point made in Remark
2
. Given a real number
\tau>0
, let
S(\tau)
denote the set of
(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{I}}^{2}
for which the inequalities

\|qx_{1}\|\,<\,q^{-\tau}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\|qx_{2}\|\,<\,e^{-q}

hold for infinitely many
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Obviously, the lower order of the function
\psi:x\mapsto e^{-x}
is infinity. It is easily seen that for any
\nu>0
,

W(\tau)\times\{0\}\ \subseteq\ S(\tau)\ \subseteq\ W(\tau,\nu)\,

where
W(\tau,\nu)=W_{2}(\Psi)
with
\psi_{1}:x\mapsto x^{-\tau}
and
\psi_{2}:x\mapsto x^{-\nu}
.
It then follows on applying Theorem R and on letting
\nu\to\infty
, that

\dim S(\tau)=1
for
1/2\leq\tau\leq 1
and that for
\tau>1

\frac{2}{1+\tau}\,\leq\,\dim_{\rm H}S(\tau)\,\leq\,\min\Big\{1,\frac{3}{1+\tau}\Big\}\,.

The upshot of this is that Theorem R does not provide a precise formula for
\dim_{\rm H}S(\tau)
when
\tau>1
. To the best of our knowledge it cannot be deduced from existing results. In short, the problem of determining a precise formula highlights a ‘hole’ in classical theory of Diophantine approximation. Just as importantly, it also brings to the forefront the limit of current results and frameworks. As alluded to in Remark
2
above, we extend the Mass Transference Principle of Wang
\&
Wu to incorporate the unbounded setup (such as in the above concrete example when the lower order of an approximating function under consideration is unbounded). In turn, this allows us to establish the following generalisation of Theorem WW from which it is easy to deduce that, for
\tau>0
,

\dim_{\rm H}S(\tau)\,=\,\min\Big\{1,\frac{3}{1+\tau}\Big\}\,.

(1.6)

Theorem 1.1
.

For
1\leq i\leq d
, let
\psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a real, positive, non-increasing function.
Then

\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)=\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)}\min\big\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t}),\ \#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\big\},

where given
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{+}\cup\{+\infty\})^{d}
we set
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t}):=\{1\leq i\leq d:t_{i}<+\infty\}
.

Remark 3
.

Note that with reference to (
1.2
), the definition of
\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t})
is equally valid for
\mathbf{t}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{+}\cup\{+\infty\})^{d}
if we restrict
1\leq i\leq d
to those with
t_{i}<+\infty
; that is to say,
i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})
. In the case
\#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\neq d
, we set

\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t}):=\#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\qquad{\rm for}\quad i\notin\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\,.

In view of this, when
\#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\neq d
, the conclusion of the theorem can be simplified to

\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)=\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)}\;\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t})\,.

In particular, with the above extension of the definition of
\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t})
in mind, note that the dimension formula as stated in Theorem WW is valid in both the bounded case ((i.e., when
\#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})=d
) and the unbounded case (i.e., when
\#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\neq d
). The point being that in that the latter

{\min}\left\{\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t}){,d}\right\}=\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t}).

To see that the theorem implies the dimension result (
1.6
) for
S(\tau)
, let
\psi_{1}:x\mapsto x^{-\tau}
and
\psi_{2}:x\to e^{-x}
and simply note that
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)=\{(\tau,+\infty)\}
and
\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{t})=3/(1+\tau)
.

As a corollary of Theorem
1.1
, we have the following unbounded version of Theorem R for general functions.

Corollary 1.1
.

For
1\leq i\leq d
, let
\psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a real, positive, non-increasing function such that the limit
\lambda_{i}
given by (

)
exists.
Then

\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)=\min\Big\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}\zeta_{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\ \#\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\Big\}\,,

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
and
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\{1\leq i\leq d:0<\lambda_{i}<+\infty\}
.

Note that when
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is bounded (i.e. when

), then

2
The Mass Transference Principle(s)

We start by defining Hausdorff measure and dimension
for completeness and for establishing some notation. Let
E
be a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For
\rho>0
, a countable collection
\left\{B_{i}\right\}
of
Euclidean balls of diameter
|B_{i}|\leq\rho
for each

i
such that
E\subset\bigcup_{i}B_{i}
is called a
\rho
-cover for
E
. Let
s
be a non-negative number and define

{\cal H}^{s}_{\rho}(E)\;=\;\inf\left\{\sum_{i}|B_{i}|^{s}\ :\{B_{i}\}{\rm\ is\ a\ }\text{$\rho$-cover of}\ X\right\}\;,

where the infimum is taken over all possible
\rho
-covers of
E
. The
s-dimensional Hausdorff measure

{\cal H}^{s}(E)
of
E
is defined by

{\cal H}^{s}(E)=\lim_{\rho\rightarrow 0}{\cal H}^{s}_{\rho}(E)=\sup_{\rho>0}{\cal H}^{s}_{\rho}(E)

and the
Hausdorff dimension

\dim_{\rm H}E
of
E
by

\dim_{\rm H}\,E=\inf\left\{s:{\cal H}^{s}(E)=0\right\}=\sup\left\{s:{\cal H}^{s}(E)=\infty\right\}\,.

Further details and alternative definitions of Hausdorff measure and dimension can be found in
[
4
,
11
]
.

We now describe a powerful mechanism, the so called Mass Transference Principle, for obtaining lower bounds for the Hausdorff dimension of a large class of
\limsup
sets.
To set the scene, let
X
be a locally compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
equipped with a non-atomic probability measure
\mu
. Suppose there
exist constants
\delta>0
,
0<a\leq 1\leq b<\infty
and
r_{0}>0
such
that

a\,r^{\delta}\ \leq\ \mu(B)\ \leq\ b\,r^{\delta}

(2.1)

for any ball
B=B(x,r)
with
x\in X
and radius
r\leq r_{0}
. Such a measure is said to be
\delta
-Ahlfors regular
. It is well known that if
X
supports a
\delta
-Ahlfors regular measure
\mu
, then we have

\dim_{\rm H}X=\delta

(2.2)

and moreover
that
\mu
is comparable to the
\delta
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
{\cal H}^{\delta}
– see
[
4
,
11
]
for
the details. The latter implies that (
2.1
) is valid with
\mu
replaced by
\mathcal{H}^{\delta}
. Next, given
s>0
and a ball
B=B(x,r)
we define
the scaled ball

B^{s}:=B\big(x,r^{\frac{s}{\delta}}\big)\,.

So, by definition
B^{\delta}=B
. The original Mass Transference Principle
(MTP) established in
[
2
]
, allows us to transfer

\mathcal{H}^{\delta}
-measure theoretic statements for
\limsup
subsets of

X
arising from balls to general
\mathcal{H}^{s}
-measure theoretic statements.

Theorem BV ​​​​

(MTP: balls to balls)
.

Let
X
be a locally compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
equipped with a
\delta
-Ahlfors regular measure
\mu
. Let
\{B_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of balls in
X
with radius
r(B_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Let
s\geq 0
and suppose that

\mathcal{H}^{\delta}\big(\limsup_{n\to\infty}B_{n}^{s}\big)=\mathcal{H}^{\delta}(X).

Then, for any ball
B

\mathcal{H}^{s}\big(B\cap\limsup_{n\to\infty}B_{n}\big)=\mathcal{H}^{s}(B\cap X)\,

In particular, if
s\leq\delta
then

\dim_{\rm H}\big(\limsup_{n\to\infty}B_{n}\big)\geq s\,.

Note that the ‘in particular’ part follows directly from the definition of dimension and the fact that
\mathcal{H}^{s}(X)>0
when
s\leq\delta=\dim X
. Although Theorem BV has numerous applications, it has its limitations due to the fact that the
\limsup
sets are defined via balls. Indeed, it is not applicable to the classical ‘rectangular’
\limsup
subset
W_{d}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
of
{\mathbb{I}}^{d}
. In short,
\mathbf{w}\in W_{d}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
if and only if there exist infinitely many
(\mathbf{p},q)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}\in R\big((\mathbf{p},q),\boldsymbol{\tau}\big)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Big\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{I}}^{d}:|x_{i}-p_{i}/q|\leq q^{-(\tau_{i}+1)}\ \ (1\leq i\leq d)\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{d}B(p_{i}/q,\psi(q)/q)\cap{\mathbb{I}}^{d}\,;

that is,

W_{d}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\limsup_{q\to\infty}\bigcup_{\mathbf{p}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}R\big((\mathbf{p},q),\boldsymbol{\tau}\big)\ .

Clearly, the sets
R\big((\mathbf{p},q),\boldsymbol{\tau}\big)
are rectangular in shape and so Theorem BV is only applicable when
\tau_{1}=\ldots=\tau_{d}
; i.e. when the rectangles are balls. The upshot of this basic and classical setup is that an analogue of the Mass Transference Principle for
\limsup
sets arising naturally via rectangles is highly desirable. As mentioned in the introduction, the breakthrough was made in the pioneering work of Wang
\&
Wu
[
17
]
who obtained a dimension version of Theorem BV for
\limsup
sets defined via rectangles within the ‘bounded’ setup. We now describe our main result which is a generalisation of their statement to the ‘unbounded’ setup.

2.1
Mass Transference Principle for rectangles

The lower bound for the dimension given by the Mass Transference Principle is in terms of the so-called dimensional number. This we first describe.

Let
p
be a positive integer and let
\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{p}
be given. Now for any

\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{p}
and

\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{+\infty\})^{p}
such that

u_{i}\leq v_{i}\quad\ {\text{for each}}\ \ 1\leq i\leq p\,,

(2.3)

let

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v}):=\{1\leq i\leq p:v_{i}<+\infty\}\ \quad\text{and}\ \quad\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{v}):=\{1\leq i\leq p:v_{i}=+\infty\}

(2.4)

and for each
i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})
, define the quantity

{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(i)}\delta_{k}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{2}(i)}\delta_{k}\left(1-\frac{v_{k}-u_{k}}{v_{i}}\right)+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{3}(i)}\frac{\delta_{k}u_{k}}{v_{i}}

where

\mathcal{K}_{1}(i):=\{1\leq k\leq p:u_{k}>v_{i}\}\,,\ \ \ \mathcal{K}_{2}(i):=\{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v}):v_{k}\leq v_{i}\},\

and

\mathcal{K}_{3}(i):=\{1,\dots,p\}\setminus(\mathcal{K}_{1}(i)\cup\mathcal{K}_{2}(i)).

For each
i\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{v})
, we set

{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\delta_{k}.

Then, the quantity

s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=\min_{1\leq i\leq p}\{{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)\}=\min\left\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\{{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)\},\ \sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\delta_{i}\right\}

(2.5)

is called the
dimensional number
with respect to
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
. Clearly, the right hand side term in the above follows directly from the definition of the quantity
{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)
and also note that in view of (
2.3
), for any
1\leq k\leq p
we have that

s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\leq{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},k)\leq\sum_{1\leq i\leq p}\delta_{i}\,.

(2.6)

Before proceeding with the statement of the Mass Transference Principle, a few remarks are in order.

Remark 5
.

In the ‘bounded’ setup (i.e. when
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{v})=\emptyset
), it is easily seen that the above definition of dimensional number coincides with that of Wang
\&
Wu
[
17
, §3.2]
. Indeed, on putting
u_{i}=a_{i}
and
v_{i}=a_{i}+t_{i}
in the above recovers their formulation.

Remark 6
.

It is evident from the definition of the quantities
{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)
and
s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
that they remain unchanged when
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
are multiplied by a constant
c>0
; that is

{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)={s}(c\mathbf{u},c\mathbf{v},i)\quad{\rm and}\quad s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=s_{0}(c\mathbf{u},c\mathbf{v})\,.

(2.7)

Further properties of the dimensional number, such as continuity, are discussed in §
4
. At this point it suffices to simply note that Proposition
4.2
in §
4
implies that the dimensional number is continuous in
\mathbf{v}
; that is

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}s_{0}(\mathbf{u},(1\pm\epsilon)\mathbf{v})=s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\,

(2.8)

for any
\epsilon>0
.

Now, let us turn to the Mass Transference Principle for rectangles.

Fix an integer
p\geq 1
and for
1\leq i\leq p
, let

X_{i}
be a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i}}
. Obviously, if
B_{i}
is a ball in
X_{i}
then
\prod_{i=1}^{p}B_{i}
is in general a rectangle in the product space
\prod_{i=1}^{p}X_{i}
. The Mass Transference Principle for rectangles provides a lower bound for the dimension of
\limsup
sets arising from sequences of such rectangles in the setting of locally compact sets equipped with Ahlfors regular measures.

Theorem 2.1
.

For each
1\leq i\leq p
, let

X_{i}
be a locally compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i}}
equipped with a
\delta_{i}
-Ahlfors regular measure
\mu_{i}
. Let
\{B_{i,n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of balls
B_{i,n}
in
X_{i}
with radius
r(B_{i,n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for each
1\leq i\leq p
. Furthermore, for each
1\leq i\leq p
assume that there exist sequences of positive real numbers
\{r_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{v_{i,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

r(B_{i,n})=r_{n}^{v_{i,n}}\,

(2.9)

with

\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n}=0

(2.10)

and

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}v_{i,n}=v_{i}

(2.11)

for some
{\mathbf{v}}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{+\infty\})^{p}
.
Finally, suppose there exists
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{p}
verifying (
2.3
) and

\mu_{1}\times\cdots\times\mu_{p}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}}B_{i,n}^{s_{i,n}}\right)=\mu_{1}\times\cdots\times\mu_{p}\left({\prod_{i=1}^{p}}X_{i}\right),

(2.12)

where

s_{i,n}:=\frac{u_{i}\delta_{i}}{v_{i,n}}\,.

(2.13)

Then

\dim_{\rm H}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}}B_{i,n}\right)\geq s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}).

(2.14)

Remark 7
.

Note that since we assume that
{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}
satisfy (
2.3
), we have that (
2.6
) holds. Then, this together with the fact that (
2.2
) holds for each set
X_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq p
), implies that

s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\leq\sum_{1\leq i\leq p}\delta_{i}=\dim\Big({\prod_{i=1}^{p}}X_{i}\Big)\,.

Thus, the lower bound inequality (
2.14
) is consistent with the fact that ‘rectangular’
\limsup
set under consideration is a subset of the product space
{\prod_{i=1}^{p}}X_{i}
and so

\dim_{\rm H}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}}B_{i,n}\right)\leq\dim\Big({\prod_{i=1}^{p}}X_{i}\Big)\,.

Remark 8
.

Note that in view of (
2.9
) and (
2.13
), for each
1\leq i\leq p
, the scaled ball

B_{i,n}^{s_{i,n}}:=B(x_{i,n},r(B_{i,n})^{\frac{s_{i,n}}{\delta_{i}}})=B(x_{i,n},r_{n}^{u_{i}})\,.

(2.15)

Thus the full measure condition (
2.12
) can be rewritten as

\mu_{1}\times\cdots\times\mu_{p}\Big(\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}}B(x_{i,n},r_{n}^{u_{i}})\Big)=\mu_{1}\times\cdots\times\mu_{p}\Big({\prod_{i=1}^{p}}X_{i}\Big).

(2.16)

Also, observe that in view of (
2.10
), if we have the strict inequality
u_{i}<v_{i}
, it follows that for
n
sufficiently large (so that
r_{n}<1
) the radius of any ball
B_{i,n}
from the given sequence of balls is less than the radius of the scaled ball
B_{i,n}^{s_{i,n}}
.
Thus

R_{n}:={\prod_{i=1}^{p}}B_{i,n}\ \subset\ R_{n}^{\mathbf{s}_{n}}:={\prod_{i=1}^{p}}B_{i,n}^{s_{i,n}}

and in short, if the
\limsup
set of the ‘blown up’ rectangles
R_{n}^{\mathbf{s}_{n}}
has full measure then the Mass Transference Principle provides a lower bound for the dimension of the
\limsup
set of the given rectangles
R_{n}
. We will see in Remark
13
that when it comes to proving Theorem
2.1
we can assume without loss of generality that the strict inequality

u_{i}\ <v_{i}
holds.

Remark 9
.

It is worth emphasizing that the assumption on the existence of the sequences
\{r_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{v_{i,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is automatically satisfied for balls satisfying the following relatively mild ‘limit condition’ on the radii. As in the theorem, for each
1\leq i\leq p
, let
\{B_{i,n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of balls
B_{i,n}
in
X_{i}
with radius
r(B_{i,n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

We say that the collection
\{B_{i,n}:1\leq i\leq p,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
of balls satisfies the limit condition
LC
if for each pair
(i,j)
with
1\leq i,j\leq p
, the limit

a_{i,j}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\log r(B_{i,n})\over\log r(B_{j,n})}

exists (possibly equal to
0
or
+\infty
).

To see that (
2.9
) and (
2.11
) are satisfied for such collection of balls, let

r_{n}^{*}:=\max\{r(B_{i,n}):1\leq i\leq p\}.

Then, since
p
is finite there exists an index
1\leq j_{o}\leq p
and an infinite sequence
\{n_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of integers such that
r_{n_{k}}^{*}=r(B_{j_{o},n_{k}})
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Now in view of limit condition
LC
, for each
1\leq i\leq p
the limit

a_{i,j_{o}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log r(B_{i,n})}{\log r(B_{j_{o},n})}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log r(B_{i,n_{k}})}{\log r(B_{j_{o},n_{k}})}\geq 1

exists. The upshot of this is that (
2.9
) and (
2.11
) are satisfied on taking

r_{n}:=r(B_{j_{o},n}),\qquad v_{i,n}:=\frac{\log r(B_{i,n})}{\log r(B_{j_{o},n})}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad v_{i}:=a_{i,j_{o}}\,.

Remark 10
.

In the statement of Theorem
2.1
, if for each
1\leq i\leq p
the sequence of positive real numbers
\{v_{i,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can be taken to be a finite positive constant sequence; that is

0<v_{i,n}=v_{i}<\infty\text{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}\,,

(2.17)

then the theorem reduces to (bounded) Mass Transference Principle of Wang & Wu
[
17
, Theorem 3.4]
- see also
[
10
, Theorem 11]
. Note that in this case, we can simply write
s_{i}
for
s_{i,n}
since by definition it is independent of
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. To be precise, the Mass Transference Principle of Wang & Wu incorporates the more general framework of resonant sets and under (
2.17
) our theorem reduces to theirs when the resonant sets are restricted to points. In §
6
, we briefly discuss the resonant set framework and we formulate the general form of Theorem
2.1
that incorporates resonant sets.

Remark 11
.

With the previous remark in mind, even in bounded case when for each
1\leq i\leq p

v_{i}<+\infty\,,

(2.18)

the statement of our theorem is more general than that of Wang & Wu
[
17
, Theorem 3.4]
. In short we have the ‘extra’ flexibility of the sequence
\{v_{i,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
at out disposal. This, for example, allows to directly deal with the following situation.
For
1\leq i\leq 2
, let
\psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}:x\to\psi_{i}(x):=x^{-\tau_{i}}(\log x)^{-(1+\tau_{i})}
where
\tau_{i}>0
, and consider the corresponding set
W_{2}(\Psi)
. Now with reference to Theorem
2.1
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
let
r_{n}=n^{-1}
,
v_{i,n}=(1+\tau_{i})(1+(\log\log n)/\log n))
and
v_{i}=1+\tau_{i}
. For convenience assume that
\tau_{1}\geq\tau_{2}
and
\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}>1
. Then, Theorem
2.1
implies that

\dim_{\rm H}W_{2}(\Psi)\geq\min\left\{\frac{3+\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}{1+\tau_{1}},\frac{3}{1+\tau_{2}}\right\}\,.

(2.19)

Clearly, the (bounded) Mass Transference Principle of Wang & Wu is not applicable (at least directly) since the exponents
v_{i,n}
are not constant. Indeed, for the same reason, even when
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}
so that
W_{2}(\Psi)
is a
\limsup
of balls, the original Mass Transference Principle (Theorem BV) is not applicable.

Remark 12
.

It is worth pointing out that in the bounded case our more general theorem can in fact be easily deduced from the
theorem of Wang & Wu (that is, the statement of Theorem
2.1
in which (
2.17
) is assumed).
To see this, assume that we are within the framework of Theorem
2.1
with the additional assumption (
2.18
).
Choose and fix
\epsilon>0
and let

\mathbf{\overline{v}}=(\overline{v}_{1},\dots,\overline{v}_{p}):=(1+\epsilon)\mathbf{v}.

In view of (
2.11
), for
n
sufficiently large we have that

v_{i,n}\leq\overline{v}_{i}\quad\text{ for each }\ i=1,\dots,p.

(2.20)

Also, note that in view of (
2.10
), for
n
sufficiently large we have that

r_{n}<1\,.

(2.21)

Assume for the moment that (
2.20
) and (
2.21
) hold for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
rather than just for all but finitely many indices. Then, inequality (
2.20
) implies that

s_{i,n}=\frac{u_{i}\delta_{i}}{v_{i,n}}\geq\frac{u_{i}\delta_{i}}{\overline{v}_{i}}=:\overline{s}_{i}\quad\text{ for each }\ i=1,\dots,p\ \text{ and }\ \forall\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Now denote by
\tilde{B}_{i,n}
the ball with the same centre as
B_{i,n}
and radius
r(\tilde{B}_{i,n})=r_{n}^{\overline{v}_{i}}
.
Then, by definition (see (
2.15
)) we have that

B_{i,n}^{s_{i,n}}=\tilde{B}_{i,n}^{\overline{s}_{i}}\quad\text{ for each }\ i=1,\dots,p\ \text{ and }\ \forall\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

The upshot of this is that we can apply Theorem
2.1
with the same vector
\mathbf{u}
but with
v_{i,n}
replaced by the finite constant sequence
v_{i,n}=\overline{v}_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,p
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
(i.e. we are within the setup of the theorem of Wang & Wu). On applying the theorem we conclude that

\dim_{\rm H}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\tilde{B}_{i,n}\right)\geq s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{\overline{v}}).

In turn, together with the fact that inequalities (
2.20
) and (
2.21
) imply that
\tilde{B}_{i,n}\subset B_{i,n}
for every
i=1,\dots,p
and all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, it follows that for any
\epsilon>0

\dim_{\rm H}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}}B_{i,n}\right)\ \geq\ \dim_{\rm H}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}}\tilde{B}_{i,n}\right)\ \geq\ s_{0}(\mathbf{u},(1+\epsilon)\mathbf{v})\,.

(2.22)

The desired statement follows from (
2.22
) by making use of (
2.8
). Finally, observe that the
\limsup
sets under consideration remain unchanged if we drop a finite set of indices
n
and so, in the above argument, we can indeed assume that (
2.20
) and (
2.21
) hold for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

.

It is convenient to note at this point that when it comes to proving Theorem

,

(2.23)

.

Remark 14
.

With the original Mass Transference Principle in mind (Theorem BV), we note that it is not possible to obtain an analogue of Theorem
2.1
for Hausdorff measures without imposing stronger assumptions. The reason for this is simple. Even though the radii of the balls
B_{i,n}
depend on the sequence
v_{i,n}
, the Hausdorff dimension
s_{0}
of the associated
\limsup
set depends only on its limiting behaviour
v_{i}
. However, as the following situation shows the
s_{0}
-Hausdorff measure can be drastically effected by the particular sequence
v_{i,n}
under consideration. For
\tau>1
, let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}:x\mapsto\psi(x):=x^{-\tau}(\log x)^{-(1+\tau)}
and consider the corresponding sets
W_{1}(\tau)
and
W_{1}(\Psi)
. With reference to Theorem
2.1
, the first of these is a
\limsup
set of balls with
r_{n}=n^{-1}
and
v_{1,n}=1+\tau
while the second is a
\limsup
set of balls with
r_{n}=n^{-1}
and
v_{1,n}=(1+\tau)(1+(\log\log n)/\log n))
. In both cases,
v_{1}=\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{1,n}=1+\tau
and so Theorem
2.1
implies the lower bound (the hard part) in following dimension statement:

\dim_{\rm H}W_{1}(\tau)=\dim_{\rm H}W_{1}(\psi)=s_{0}:=\frac{2}{1+\tau}\,.

Regarding the
s_{0}
-dimensional Hausdorff measure, it follows from Jarník’s Theorem
[
1
, Theorem 1.3.4]
that

\mathcal{H}^{s_{0}}\big(W_{1}(\tau)\big)=\infty\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\mathcal{H}^{s_{0}}\big(W_{1}(\psi)\big)=0\,.

In fact, the first of these statements can easily be deduced from Theorem BV while the other can be easily deduced via a simple covering argument.

Remark 15
.

As mentioned in Remark
4
, recently He
[
8
]
has established a ‘rectangular to rectangular’ Mass Transference Principle from the view point of Hausdorff content. In short,
he ‘essentially’ shows
[
8
, Theorem 2.9]
that if we replace the full measure condition (
2.12
) by the stronger assumption of uniform local ubiquity (see
[
8
, Definition 1.7]
), then information regarding the Hausdorff content of ‘blown up’
\limsup
sets implies ‘full’ Hausdorff measure statements (as well as the large intersection property) for the original sets. We say ‘essentially’ since the full measure part of the statement also requires that the implicit sequences corresponding to
s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}(n))
are non-increasing.
Clearly, such an assumption is necessary as shown by the analysis in Remark
14
concerning the
\mathcal{H}^{s_{0}}
-measure of set
W_{1}(\psi)
– the sequence
s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}(n))=2/v_{1,n}
is increasing.
Without the non-increasing hypothesis,
[
8
, Theorem 2.9]
yields a dimension result only akin to our Theorem
2.1
but under the significantly stronger assumption of uniform local ubiquity. Indeed, it is worth emphasising that under the weaker full measure condition (
2.12
) of Theorem
2.1
, He obtains a dimension statement
[
8
, Theorem 2.10]
but only within the bounded setup of Wang & Wu (see Remark
10
). The upshot is that under (
2.12
), the results in
[
8
]
cannot be applied to obtain statements such as Theorem
1.2
or (
1.6
) within the unbounded setup or indeed (
2.19
) within the bounded setup.

2.2
Proof of Theorem
1.1
modulo Theorem
2.1

The proof of Theorem
1.1
will involve establishing the upper bound (Proposition
2.1
below) and lower bound (Proposition
2.2
below) for
\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)
separately.

2.2.1
The upper bound

The goal is to prove the following statement.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
1\leq i\leq d
, let
\psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a real, positive, non-increasing function.
Then

\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)\leq\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)}\min\big\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t}),\ \#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\big\},

where given
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{+}\cup\{+\infty\})^{d}
we set
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t}):=\{1\leq i\leq d:t_{i}<+\infty\}
.

Proof.

When
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
is bounded, by definition we have that
\#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})=d
and so the desired statement corresponds to the result of Wang & Wu (§
1
, Theorem WW). Thus, without loss of generality, assume that
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
is unbounded.

For any
\epsilon>0
, let
M>0
be sufficiently large so that

\displaystyle\frac{d+1}{1+M}<\epsilon.

(2.25)

Let
\widetilde{\Psi}:=(\widetilde{\psi}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}\
where

\widetilde{\psi}_{i}(q):=\max\{\psi_{i}(q),\ q^{-M}\}\ \ \qquad(1\leq i\leq d)

and define
W_{d}(\widetilde{\Psi})
and
\mathcal{U}(\widetilde{\Psi})
accordingly. Then, by definition

W_{d}(\Psi)\subset W_{d}(\widetilde{\Psi})
and in turn, since
\mathcal{U}(\widetilde{\Psi})
is bounded, it follows via Theorem WW that

\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)\leq\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\widetilde{\Psi})=\sup_{{\widetilde{\mathbb{t}}}\in\mathcal{U}(\widetilde{\Psi})}\min\left\{\min_{1\leq i\leq d}{\zeta}_{i}({\widetilde{\mathbb{t}}}),\,d\right\}.

(2.26)

Now, let
\widetilde{\mathbb{t}}\in\mathcal{U}(\widetilde{\Psi})
be a point such that

\min\left\{\min_{1\leq i\leq d}{\zeta}_{i}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}}),\,d\right\}>\sup_{{\widetilde{\mathbb{t}}}\in\mathcal{U}(\widetilde{\Psi})}\min\left\{\min_{1\leq i\leq d}{\zeta}_{i}({\widetilde{\mathbb{t}}}),\,d\right\}-\epsilon\,.

(2.27)

Note that for any
\widetilde{\mathbb{t}}\in\mathcal{U}(\widetilde{\Psi})
, there exists an unbounded integer sequence
\{q_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{-\log\widetilde{\psi}_{i}(q_{k})}{\log q_{k}}=\widetilde{t}_{i}\qquad\forall\ 1\leq i\leq d

By the definition of
\widetilde{\Psi}
, we clearly have that
\widetilde{t}_{i}\leq M
for each
1\leq i\leq d
. With this in mind, let
\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}}):=\{1\leq i\leq d:\widetilde{t}_{i}<M\}
. In turn,
let
t_{i}=\widetilde{t}_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})
and for
i\not\in\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})
, let

t_{i}=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{-\log{\psi}_{i}(q_{k_{j}})}{\log q_{k_{j}}},

for some subsequence
q_{k_{j}}
of
\{q_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
.
Then, it is easy to check that
{\mathbb{t}}:=({t}_{1},\cdots,{t}_{d})\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
. Moreover, by the definitions of
\widetilde{\psi}_{i}
and
\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})
, one has that

\displaystyle{t}_{i}=\widetilde{t}_{i}<M\ \ {\text{for}}\ i\in\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})\quad\text{and}\quad{t}_{i}\geq\widetilde{t}_{i}=M\ \ {\text{for}}\ i\not\in\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}}).

(2.28)

We now show that

{\zeta}_{i}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})<{\zeta}_{i}({\mathbb{t}})+\epsilon\qquad\forall\ 1\leq i\leq d\,.

(2.29)

To this end, first note that
\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})\subset\mathcal{L}({\mathbb{t}})
and that for
i\not\in\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})
, by (
2.28
) and (
2.25
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\zeta_{i}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})&=\frac{d+1+\sum_{k:\widetilde{t}_{k}<\widetilde{t}_{i}}(\widetilde{t}_{i}-\widetilde{t}_{k})}{1+\widetilde{t}_{i}}=\frac{d+1+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})}(M-t_{k})}{1+M}\\
&\leq\#\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})-\frac{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})}t_{k}}{1+M}+\epsilon.\end{split}

(2.30)

To continue, we consider the following three cases:

•

For
i\in\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})
, it is clear that
\zeta_{i}(\widetilde{{\mathbb{t}}})=\zeta_{i}({\mathbb{t}})
since both of them are only related to the components in
\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})
which are equal for
\widetilde{\mathbb{t}}
and
{\mathbb{t}}
.

•

For
i\in\mathcal{L}({\mathbb{t}})\setminus\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})
, by (
2.28
) and (
2.30
),

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}({\mathbb{t}})

\displaystyle=\frac{d+1+\sum_{k:{t}_{k}<{t}_{i}}({t}_{i}-{t}_{k})}{1+{t}_{i}}\geq\frac{d+1+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})}({t}_{i}-{t}_{k})}{1+{t}_{i}}

\displaystyle\geq\#\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})-\frac{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})}t_{k}}{1+M}\geq\zeta_{i}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})-\epsilon.

•

For
i\not\in\mathcal{L}({\mathbb{t}})
, it is clear that
i\not\in\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})
and so by definition (see Remark
3
) and (
2.30
),

\zeta_{i}({\mathbb{t}})=\#\mathcal{L}({\mathbb{t}})\geq\#\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})\geq\zeta_{i}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})-\epsilon.

Together, these three cases imply (
2.29
) and so it follows that for any
\widetilde{\mathbb{t}}\in\mathcal{U}(\widetilde{\Psi})
there exists a corresponding
{\mathbb{t}}\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
such that

\min\left\{\min_{1\leq i\leq d}{\zeta}_{i}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}}),\,d\right\}\ <\ \min\left\{\min_{1\leq i\leq d}{\zeta}_{i}({\mathbb{t}}),\,d\right\}+\epsilon.

In particular, on combining with (
2.26
) and (
2.27
) we find that

\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)\ \leq\ \min\left\{\min_{1\leq i\leq d}{\zeta}_{i}({\mathbb{t}}),\,d\right\}+2\epsilon\ \leq\ \sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}({\Psi})}\min\left\{\min_{1\leq i\leq d}{\zeta}_{i}({\mathbb{t}}),\,d\right\}+2\epsilon\,.

Now
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary and so the desired statement follows on letting
\epsilon\to 0
and noting that by definition (see Remark
3
), in the unbounded case for any
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}({\Psi})
with
\#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\neq d
we have that

{\min}\left\{\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t}){,d}\right\}\ =\ \min_{1\leq i\leq d}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t})\ =\ {\min}\left\{\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t}){,\#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})}\right\}\,.

We conclude by mentioning that the above argument is equally valid if
\mathcal{L}_{M}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})=\emptyset
for all
M>0
sufficiently large. Indeed, in this case (
2.30
) becomes
\zeta_{i}(\widetilde{\mathbb{t}})\leq\epsilon
for all
1\leq i\leq d
.

∎

2.2.2
The lower bound modulo Theorem
2.1

To prove the complementary lower bound for
\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)
we will make use of the following full measure result. The proof will be given at the end of this section.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathbf{a}=({a}_{1},\cdots,{a}_{d})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{+})^{d}
such that

a_{i}\geq 1\quad(1\leq i\leq d)\qquad{and}\qquad{a}_{1}+{a}_{2}+\cdots+{a}_{d}=d+1.

Let
M=4^{d+1}
and
\widetilde{M}=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}M^{1/a_{i}}
. Then, for any sequence
\{q_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
of integers, the limsup set

W:=\limsup_{\ell\to\infty}E_{\ell}

with

E_{\ell}:=\bigcup_{\frac{1}{M}q_{\ell}\leq q\leq q_{\ell}}\ \bigcup_{0\leq p_{1},\cdots,p_{d}\leq q}\ \prod_{i=1}^{d}B\left(\frac{p_{i}}{q}, \left(\frac{\widetilde{M}}{q_{\ell}}\right)^{a_{i}}\right)

is of full Lebesgue measure.

The following proposition constitutes the desired lower bound.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
1\leq i\leq d
, let
\psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a real, positive, non-increasing function.
Then

\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)\geq\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)}\min\left\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t}),\ \#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\right\},

where given
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{+}\cup\{+\infty\})^{d}
we set
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t}):=\{1\leq i\leq d:t_{i}<+\infty\}
.

Proof.

Fix any point
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
. The proof of the proposition follows on showing

\displaystyle\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)\geq\min\left\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t}),\ \#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\right\}\,.

(2.31)

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\psi_{i}(q)\to 0
as
q\to\infty
for all
1\leq i\leq d
. Indeed, if this was not the case we consider the function
\widetilde{\Psi}:=(\widetilde{\psi}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}\
where

\widetilde{\psi}_{i}(q):=\min\{\psi_{i}(q),\ (\log q)^{-1}\}\ \ \qquad(1\leq i\leq d)

and define
W_{d}(\widetilde{\Psi})
and
\mathcal{U}(\widetilde{\Psi})
accordingly. Then, by definition
\widetilde{\psi}_{i}(q)\to 0
as
q\to\infty
for all
1\leq i\leq d
and
W_{d}(\Psi)\supset W_{d}(\widetilde{\Psi})
. Moreover,
\mathcal{U}(\widetilde{\Psi})=\mathcal{U}({\Psi})
and so the desired statement (
2.31
) for
W_{d}(\Psi)
follows on establishing (
2.31
) with
\Psi
replaced by
\widetilde{\Psi}
. Also note that we can assume that
\#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\neq 0
, since otherwise the right hand side of (
2.31
) is equal to zero and the inequality is trivially true. Furthermore, we will assume that
\#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\neq d
. Indeed, if
\#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})=d
for all
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
the desired statement corresponds to the result of Wang & Wu (§
1
, Theorem WW). With these assumptions in mind, we proceed with establishing (
2.31
).

Step 1.
We first deal with the situation when

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})}t_{i}\,<\,1\,.

(2.32)

For notational simplicity, we assume
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})=\{1,\cdots,m\}
with
1\leq m\leq d-1
. Since
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
and satisfies (
2.32
), it follows form the definition of
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
that there exists an unbounded sequence of integers
\{q_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}

such that

q_{\ell}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m}\psi_{i}(q_{\ell})>1\qquad\forall\ \ell\geq 1.

Thus, by Minkowski’s Convex Body Theorem (
[
15
, Chp 2: Theorem 2B]
), for any
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in{\mathbb{I}}^{m}:=[0,1]^{m}
and each
\ell\geq 1
, there exists a non-zero integer point
(p_{1},\cdots,p_{m},q)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}\times{\mathbb{N}}
with
1\leq q\leq q_{\ell}
such that

|qx_{i}-p_{i}|<\psi_{i}(q_{\ell})\leq\psi_{i}(q)\ \ \qquad(1\leq i\leq m)\,.

Since
\psi_{i}(q_{\ell})\to 0
as
\ell\to\infty
for all
1\leq i\leq d
, we conclude that there exist infinitely many integers
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\|qx_{i}\|<\psi_{i}(q)\ \ \qquad(1\leq i\leq m)\,.

Since
m<d
, the upshot of the above is that

{\mathbb{I}}^{m}\ \times\prod_{i=m+1}^{d}\{0\}\subset W_{d}(\Psi)\,.

Hence,
\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)\geq m=\#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})
and this verifies (
2.31
) in the situation that (
2.32
) holds.

Step 2.
We now deal with the ‘other’ situation. That is, to verify (
2.31
) with
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
satisfying

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})}t_{i}\geq 1.

(2.33)

For this we will make use of Theorem
2.1
.
To start with, note that since
\psi_{i}(q)\to 0
as
q\to\infty
, we have that

t_{i}\geq 0\qquad\forall\ 1\leq i\leq d\,.

Also, by definition, for any
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
there exists an integer subsequence
\{q_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{-\log\psi_{i}(q_{\ell})}{\log q_{\ell}}=t_{i}\qquad\forall\ 1\leq i\leq d\,.

In turn, consider the following
\limsup
set

\displaystyle W\,:=\,\limsup_{\ell\to\infty}\bigcup_{\frac{1}{M}q_{\ell}\leq q\leq q_{\ell}}\ \bigcup_{0\leq p_{1},\cdots,p_{d}\leq q}\ \prod_{i=1}^{d}B\left(\frac{p_{i}}{q},\frac{\psi_{i}(q_{\ell})}{q_{\ell}}\right)\,

(2.34)

where
M:=4^{d+1}
. Then, since for each
1\leq i\leq d
, the function

\psi_{i}
is non-increasing it follows that

W\subset W_{d}(\Psi)\,,

and in view of Lemma
2.1

m_{d}\left(\limsup_{\ell\to\infty}\bigcup_{{1\over M}q_{\ell}\leq q\leq q_{\ell}}\ \bigcup_{0\leq p_{1},\cdots,p_{d}\leq q}\ \prod_{i=1}^{d}B\left(\frac{p_{i}}{q},\ \Big(\frac{\widetilde{M}}{q_{\ell}}\Big)^{a_{i}}\right)\right)=m_{d}({\mathbb{I}}^{d})

(2.35)

for any
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
with

a_{i}\geq 1\quad(1\leq i\leq d)\qquad{\rm and}\qquad a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}=d+1\,.

Now with Theorem
2.1
in mind, for
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let

r_{\ell}={\widetilde{M}\over q_{\ell}}\,

and for
1\leq i\leq d
, let

\delta_{i}=1,\,\qquad u_{i}=a_{i},\qquad v_{i,\ell}={\log\psi_{i}(q_{\ell})-\log q_{\ell}\over\log\widetilde{M}-\log q_{\ell}},

which implies that

v_{i}:=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}v_{i,\ell}=1+t_{i}\,.

Note that if

B_{i,\ell}:=\textstyle{B\Big(\frac{p_{i}}{q},\frac{\psi_{i}(q_{\ell})}{q_{\ell}}\Big)}
is a ball appearing in (
2.34
) then

B_{i,\ell}^{s_{i,\ell}}:=\textstyle{B\Big(\frac{p_{i}}{q},\big(\frac{\widetilde{M}}{q_{\ell}}\big)^{a_{i}}\Big)}\qquad{\rm with}\quad s_{i,n}:=\frac{a_{i}}{v_{i,n}}

is the corresponding ‘enlarged’ ball appearing in the full measure statement (
2.35
).
Thus, in view of Theorem
2.1
we conclude that

\dim_{\rm H}W_{d}(\Psi)\ \geq\ \dim_{\rm H}W\ \geq\ \min\left\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}s(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i),\ \#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})\right\}

(2.36)

with
\mathbf{v}=1+\mathbf{t}
and for any
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
with

1\leq a_{i}\leq 1+t_{i}\quad(1\leq i\leq d)\qquad{\rm and}\qquad a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}=d+1\,.

(2.37)

It is evident that
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})
. Thus, for (
4.14
) to imply (
2.31
), it suffices to show that on choosing
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
satisfying (
2.37
) appropriately, we can guarantee that

\displaystyle\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}s(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)=\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t}).

(2.38)

We divide the proof of this into two cases.

Case 1.
Suppose
\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq d}t_{i}>\frac{1}{d}
. We choose

a_{k}=1+\frac{1}{d}>1\ \qquad\forall\ 1\leq i\leq d\,.

Then,
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}=d+1
. Thus,
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
satisfies (
2.37
) and by definition,
for each
i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})
,

s(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(i)}1+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{2}(i)}\left(1-\frac{t_{k}-{1\over d}}{1+t_{i}}\right)+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{3}(i)}\frac{1+\frac{1}{d}}{1+t_{i}}

(2.39)

where

\mathcal{K}_{1}(i):=\{1\leq k\leq d:1+\frac{1}{d}>1+t_{i}\}=\emptyset,\ \ \mathcal{K}_{2}(i):=\{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t}):t_{k}\leq t_{i}\},\

and

\mathcal{K}_{3}(i):=\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus(\mathcal{K}_{1}(i)\cup\mathcal{K}_{2}(i)).

So,

\displaystyle s(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{k:t_{k}\leq t_{i}}(1+\frac{1}{d}+t_{i}-t_{k})+\sum_{k:t_{k}>t_{i}}(1+\frac{1}{d})}{1+t_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d+1+\sum_{k:t_{k}<t_{i}}(t_{i}-t_{k})}{1+t_{i}}=\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t})\,.

Hence, (
2.38
) holds as desired.

Case 2.
Suppose
\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq d}t_{i}\leq\frac{1}{d}
.
Let

\mathcal{T}:=\left\{t_{j}:j\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\ {\rm and}\ t_{j}\geq\frac{1-\sum_{i:t_{i}<t_{j}}t_{i}}{\#\{1\leq i\leq d:t_{i}\geq t_{j}\}}\right\}.

Note that if
t_{\rm max}:=\max_{j\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})}t_{j}
and
\#_{\rm max}:=\#\{j\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t}):t_{j}=t_{\rm max}\}
, then by (
2.33
) we have that

\sum_{i:t_{i}<t_{\rm max}}t_{i}\ +\ \#_{\rm max}\,\cdot t_{\rm max}\ =\ \sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})}t_{i}\ \geq\ 1

and so
t_{\rm max}\in\mathcal{T}
. Thus,
\mathcal{T}\neq\emptyset
. Now,
let
t_{K}
be the smallest element of
\mathcal{T}
and let

t^{*}:=\frac{1-\sum_{i:t_{i}<t_{K}}t_{i}}{\#\{1\leq i\leq d:t_{i}\geq t_{K}\}}\,.

Clearly,
t^{*}
is the right hand side of the inequality appearing in the definition of
\mathcal{T}
with
j=K
. Hence, trivially

t_{K}\geq t^{*}\,

(2.40)

and moreover
t^{*}>0
. Indeed, to see the latter suppose it was not the case. Then

\sum_{i:t_{i}<t_{K}}t_{i}\geq 1.

Let
t_{i_{0}}:=\max\{t_{i}:t_{i}<t_{K}\}
. It follows that

t_{i_{0}}\ \geq\ 1-\sum_{i\neq i_{0}:t_{i}<t_{K}}t_{i}\ \geq\ {1-\sum_{i\neq i_{0}:t_{i}<t_{K}}t_{i}\over\#\{1\leq i\leq d:t_{i}\geq t_{K}\}+1}\ =\ {1-\sum_{i:t_{i}<t_{i_{0}}}t_{i}\over\#\{1\leq i\leq d:t_{i}\geq t_{i_{0}}\}}\,,

and so
t_{i_{0}}\in\mathcal{T}
. However, this contradicts the fact that
t_{K}
is the smallest element of
\mathcal{T}
.

Now, for
1\leq i\leq d
, let

a_{i}:=\begin{cases}1+t_{i}\ \ &\text{if}\ t_{i}<t_{K}\\[8.61108pt]
1+t^{*}\ \ &\text{if}\ t_{i}\geq t_{K}.\end{cases}

Since
t_{i}\geq 0
and
t^{*}>0
, we have that
a_{i}\geq 1
for all
1\leq i\leq d
.
Moreover, it follows from the definition of
t^{*}
that

\displaystyle a_{1}+a_{2}+\cdots+a_{d}=d+1.

(2.41)

Thus,
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
satisfies (
2.37
) and by definition,
for each
i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})
,

s(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(i)}1+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{2}(i)}\left(1-\frac{1+t_{k}-a_{k}}{1+t_{i}}\right)+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{3}(i)}\frac{a_{k}}{1+t_{i}}

where

\mathcal{K}_{1}(i):=\{1\leq k\leq d:a_{k}>1+t_{i}\},\ \ \mathcal{K}_{2}(i):=\{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t}):t_{k}\leq t_{i}\}.\

and

\mathcal{K}_{3}(i):=\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus(\mathcal{K}_{1}(i)\cup\mathcal{K}_{2}(i)).

To proceed, we further distinguish between two subcases: indices
i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})
with
t_{i}\geq t_{K}
(Case 2.1) and, those with
t_{i}<t_{K}
(Case 2.2).

Case 2.1.
Suppose
i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})
with
t_{i}\geq t_{K}
. Then, by definition, for all
1\leq k\leq d
such that
t_{k}<t_{K}
, we have
a_{k}=1+t_{k}<1+t_{i}
, and for all
1\leq k\leq d
such that
t_{k}\geq t_{K}
, we have
a_{k}=1+t^{*}\leq 1+t_{K}\leq 1+t_{i}
. Hence,
\mathcal{K}_{1}(i)=\emptyset
and since
i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})
, it is obvious that
\mathcal{K}_{2}(i)=\{1\leq k\leq d:t_{k}\leq t_{i}\}
. Therefore, it follows on using (
2.41
) that

\displaystyle s(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k:t_{k}\leq t_{i}}\left(1-\frac{1+t_{k}-a_{k}}{1+t_{i}}\right)+\sum_{k:t_{k}>t_{i}}\frac{a_{k}}{1+t_{i}}

(2.42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d+1+\sum_{k:t_{k}<t_{i}}(t_{i}-t_{k})}{1+t_{i}}=\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t})\,.

Case 2.2.
Suppose
i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})
with
t_{i}<t_{K}
. Then, by the definition of
t_{K}
, we have that

t_{i}<\frac{1-\sum_{k:t_{k}<t_{i}}t_{k}}{\#\{1\leq k\leq d:t_{k}\geq t_{i}\}}.

This is equivalent to

\#\{1\leq k\leq d:t_{k}\geq t_{i}\}\cdot t_{i}\ <\ 1-\sum_{k:t_{k}<t_{i}}t_{k},

(2.43)

which together with the fact that
t_{i}<t_{K}
, implies that

\#\{1\leq k\leq d:t_{k}\geq t_{K}\}\cdot t_{i}\ <\ 1-\sum_{k:t_{k}<t_{K}}t_{k}.

In the other words,

t_{i}\ <\ \frac{1-\sum_{k:t_{k}<t_{K}}t_{k}}{\#\{1\leq k\leq d:t_{k}\geq t_{K}\}}\ =\ t^{*}.

Thus, for all
1\leq k\leq d
such that
t_{k}\geq t_{K}
, the above implies that
a_{k}:=1+t^{*}>1+t_{i}
. Obviously, if
t_{K}>t_{k}>t_{i}
then
a_{k}:=1+t_{k}>1+t_{i}
. Hence,

\mathcal{K}_{1}(i)=\{1\leq k\leq d:t_{k}>t_{i}\}
. On the other hand, since
i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})
it follows that
\mathcal{K}_{2}(i)=\{1\leq k\leq d:t_{k}\leq t_{i}\}

and hence
\mathcal{K}_{3}(i)=\emptyset.
Therefore,

\displaystyle s(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k:t_{k}>t_{i}}1+\sum_{k:t_{k}\leq t_{i}}(1-{1+t_{k}-a_{k}\over 1+t_{i}})

(2.44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k:t_{k}>t_{i}}1+\sum_{k:t_{k}\leq t_{i}}(1-{1+t_{k}-(1+t_{k})\over 1+t_{i}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\,.

Now observe, that in view of (
2.42
) with
i=K
and (
2.40
), it follows that

\displaystyle s(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},K)=\zeta_{K}(\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{d+1+\sum_{k:t_{k}<t_{K}}(t_{K}-t_{k})}{1+t_{K}}

(2.45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d+\frac{\#\{k:t_{k}\geq t_{K}\}}{1+t_{K}}\cdot\left(-t_{K}+t^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d,

which together with (
2.44
) implies that

s(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},K)\leq s(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)\qquad\forall\quad i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\quad{\rm with}\quad t_{i}<t_{K}\,.

(2.46)

On the other hand, for any
i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})
with
t_{i}<t_{K}
, we use (
2.43
) to obtain

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}({\mathbf{t}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d+1+\sum_{k:t_{k}<t_{i}}(t_{i}-t_{k})}{1+t_{i}}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{d+\#\{k:t_{k}<t_{i}\}\cdot t_{i}+\#\{k:t_{k}\geq t_{i}\}\cdot t_{i}}{1+t_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\,,

which together with (
2.45
) implies that

\zeta_{K}(\mathbf{t})<\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t})\qquad\forall\quad i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\quad{\rm with}\quad t_{i}<t_{K}\,.

(2.47)

On combining our findings in the above subcases, namely (
2.42
), (
2.46
) and (
2.47
), we find that

\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}s(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)\ =\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v}):\ t_{i}\geq t_{K}}s(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)\ =\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v}):\ t_{i}\geq t_{K}}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t})=\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{t}).

This establishes (
2.38
) in Case 2 and thereby completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

The above proof of Proposition
2.2
is based on the validity of Lemma
2.1
and of course Theorem
2.1
.
We now establish Lemma
2.1
.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

By the Lebesgue density theorem, it suffices to show that there is a constant
c>0
such that for any ball
B\subset{\mathbb{I}}^{d}=[0,1]^{d}
, we have

m_{d}(B\cap W)\geq c\cdot m_{d}(B).

(2.48)

To this end, we will show that for all large
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

m_{d}(B\cap E_{\ell})\geq c\cdot m_{d}(B),

which clearly implies (
2.48
). Throughout, for convenience, we work with the max norm. Thus, for any ball
B=B(r)
of radius
r
we have
m_{d}(B)=(2r)^{d}
.

Given

\mathbf{a}=({a}_{1},\cdots,{a}_{d})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{+})^{d}
as in the statement of the lemma, let
\bar{\mathbf{a}}=(\bar{a}_{1},\cdots,\bar{a}_{d})
where
\bar{a}_{i}:=a_{i}-1
for each
1\leq i\leq d
. It follows that

\bar{a}_{i}\geq 0\quad(1\leq i\leq d)\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\bar{a}_{1}+\bar{a}_{2}+\cdots+\bar{a}_{d}=1.

Minkowski’s Convex Body Theorem
[
15
, Chp2: Theorem 2B]
, implies that for any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{I}}^{d}
and
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists non-zero integer point
(p_{1},\cdots,p_{d},q)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq q\leq q_{\ell}
such that

|qx_{i}-p_{i}|<q_{\ell}^{-\bar{a}_{i}}\ \ \qquad(1\leq i\leq d)\,.

Thus, for any ball
B=B(r)\subset{\mathbb{I}}^{d}

\displaystyle m_{d}(B)=m_{d}\left(B\cap\bigcup_{1\leq q\leq q_{\ell}}\ \bigcup_{0\leq p_{1},\cdots,p_{d}\leq q}\ \prod_{i=1}^{d}B\Big(\frac{p_{i}}{q},\frac{1}{q\cdot q_{\ell}^{\bar{a}_{i}}}\Big)\right).

(2.49)

We now split the above union into two according to the size of the denominator
q
:

I_{1}:=\bigcup_{1\leq q<\frac{1}{M}\cdot q_{\ell}}\ \ \bigcup_{0\leq p_{1},\cdots,p_{d}\leq q}\ \prod_{i=1}^{d}B\Big(\frac{p_{i}}{q},\frac{1}{q\cdot q_{\ell}^{\bar{a}_{i}}}\Big)

and

I_{2}:=\bigcup_{\frac{1}{M}q_{\ell}\leq q\leq q_{\ell}}\ \ \bigcup_{0\leq p_{1},\cdots,p_{d}\leq q}\ \prod_{i=1}^{d}B\Big(\frac{p_{i}}{q},\frac{1}{q\cdot q_{\ell}^{\bar{a}_{i}}}\Big)\,.

It is easily verified that for any ball
B=B(r)\subset{\mathbb{I}}^{d}
and fixed
q\in{\mathbb{N}}

\#\left\{0\leq p_{1},\cdots,p_{d}\leq q:B\cap\prod_{i=1}^{d}B\Big(\frac{p_{i}}{q},\frac{1}{q\cdot q_{\ell}^{\bar{a}_{i}}}\Big){\neq\emptyset}\right\}\ \leq\ (2rq+2)^{d}\,.

Here we use the fact that rational points with denominator
q
are separated by
1/q
.
Thus,

\displaystyle m_{d}(B\cap I_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q<\frac{1}{M}\cdot q_{\ell}}\ \sum_{0\leq p_{1},\cdots,p_{d}\leq q}m_{d}\left(B\cap\prod_{i=1}^{d}B\Big(\frac{p_{i}}{q},\frac{1}{q\cdot q_{\ell}^{\bar{a}_{i}}}\Big)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q<\frac{1}{M}\cdot q_{\ell}}\ (2rq+2)^{d}\cdot\frac{2^{d}}{q^{d}\cdot q_{\ell}}=\sum_{q<\frac{1}{M}\cdot q_{\ell}}\ (r+{1\over q})^{d}\cdot\frac{4^{d}}{q_{\ell}}.

Since
r+{1\over q}\leq 2\max\{r,{1\over q}\}
and by definition
M=4^{d+1}
, it follows that for all large
\ell

\displaystyle m_{d}(B\cap I_{1})

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\sum_{q<\frac{1}{M}\cdot q_{\ell}}\left(\frac{8^{d}r^{d}}{q_{\ell}}+\frac{8^{d}}{q^{d}\cdot q_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{8^{d}\cdot r^{d}}{M}+\frac{8^{d}\log q_{\ell}}{q_{\ell}}=\frac{1}{4}m_{d}(B)+\frac{8^{d}\log q_{\ell}}{q_{\ell}}<\frac{1}{2}m_{d}(B).

This, together with (
2.49
) implies that

m_{d}(B\cap I_{2})\geq m_{d}(B)-m_{d}(B\cap I_{1})\geq\frac{1}{2}m_{d}(B).

Finally, on noting that

{1\over qq_{\ell}^{\bar{a_{i}}}}\leq{M\over q_{\ell}^{a_{i}}}\leq{\big(\frac{\widetilde{M}}{q_{\ell}}\big)^{a_{i}}},

we have that
B\cap I_{2}\subset B\cap E_{\ell}

and so the desired statement follows.
∎

3
Application to matrix transformation of tori

Let
T
be a
d\times d
non-singular matrix with real coefficients.
Then,
T
determines a self-map of the
d
-dimensional torus

X={\mathbb{T}}^{d}:={\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
; namely, it sends
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
to
T\mathbf{x}
modulo one. In what follows,
T
will denote both the matrix and the transformation and for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, by
T^{n}
we will always mean the
n
-th iteration of the transformation
T
rather than the matrix multiplied
n
times.

As usual, for
1\leq i\leq d
, let
\psi_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
be a real positive function and for convenience, let
\Psi:=(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{d})
and for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
let
\Psi(n):=(\psi_{1}(n),\dots,\psi_{d}(n))
. Fix some point
\mathbf{z}:=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let

R\big(\mathbf{z},\Psi(n)\big):=\Big\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}:\|x_{i}-z_{i}\|\leq\psi_{i}(n)\ (1\leq i\leq d)\Big\}.

Clearly,
R\big(\mathbf{z},\Psi(n)\big)
is a rectangle centred at the fixed point
\mathbf{z}
.
In turn, let

W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z}):=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}:T^{n}(\mathbf{x})\in R\big(\mathbf{z},\Psi(n)\big)\ \ \text{for infinitely many}\ n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}\ .

For obvious reasons the sets
R_{n}:=R\big(\mathbf{z},\Psi(n)\big)
can be thought of as rectangular targets that the orbit under
T
of points in
X
have to hit. The interesting situation is when the diameters of
R_{n}
tend to zero as
n
increases and thus it is natural to refer to
W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})
as the corresponding shrinking target set.
It is easily verified that if

T={\rm diag}\,(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})\qquad(\beta_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}})\,\vskip-12.91663pt

then

W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}:|T_{\beta_{i}}^{n}x_{i}-z_{i}|\leq\psi_{i}(n)\ (1\leq i\leq d)\ \ \text{for infinitely many}\ n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}\,,

(3.1)

where
T_{\beta_{i}}
is the standard
\beta
-transformation with
\beta=\beta_{i}
.
Recall, given a real number
\beta
such that
|\beta|>1
, the associated
\beta
-transformation
T_{\beta}:[0,1)\to[0,1)
is given by

T_{\beta}(x):=\beta x\ (\text{mod}\ 1).

The
\beta
-transformation admits an absolutely continuous invariant measure
\mu_{\beta}
, which is called Parry measure for positive
\beta
and Yrrap measure for negative
\beta
. Let
K(\beta)
be the support of
\mu_{\beta}
which are finite union of intervals. See
[
10
, Section 3.3]
for some more details on
\mu_{\beta}
and
K(\beta)
.

The set
W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})
is non-trivial only when
\mathbf{z}\in K:=\prod_{i=1}^{d}K(\beta_{i})
. A straightforward consequence of
[
10
, Theorem 4]
is that if all the eigenvalues
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{d}
are of absolute value strictly larger than
1
, then for any
\mathbf{z}\in K
,
we have

\displaystyle m_{d}|_{K}\big(W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})\big)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ \ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi_{1}(n)\times\dots\times\psi_{d}(n)<\infty\\[8.61108pt]
1&\text{if}\ \ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi_{1}(n)\times\dots\times\psi_{d}(n)=\infty\,,\end{cases}

where
m_{d}|K
is the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure restricted on
K
. This thus provide a complete description of the size of the shrinking target set
W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})
in terms of the ambient measure.

Turning to the problem of determining its size in terms of Hausdorff dimension, it turns out that
\dim_{\rm H}W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})
is dependent on the set
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
of accumulation points
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{d})
of the sequence

\Big\{\Big(\frac{-\log\psi_{1}(n)}{n},\cdots,\frac{-\log\psi_{d}(n)}{n}\Big)\Big\}_{n\geq 1}\,.

Indeed, the following statement is in line with that claimed in
[
10
, Section 5.3]
. Under the assumption that
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
is bounded, it follows from results in
[
9
,
10
]
– see Remark
16
below. In view of Theorem
2.1
, we are able to remove the ‘bounded’ assumption and obtain a complete result. As in the main body of the paper, for
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{\infty\})^{d}
, let

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t}):=\{1\leq i\leq d:t_{i}<+\infty\}\,.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
T
be a real, non-singular matrix transformation of the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Suppose that
T
is diagonal
and all its eigenvalues
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{d}
are of modulus strictly larger than
1
.
For
1\leq i\leq d
, let
\psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a real positive non-increasing
function and
\mathbf{z}\in K
. Then

\dim_{\rm H}W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})=\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)}\min\big\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})}\{{\xi}_{i}(\mathbf{t})\},\ \#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\big\}\,,

where

\xi_{i}(\mathbf{t}):=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(i)}1+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{2}(i)}\left(1-\frac{t_{k}}{\log|\beta_{i}|+t_{i}}\right)+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{3}(i)}\frac{\log|\beta_{k}|}{\log|\beta_{i}|+t_{i}}

and, in turn

\mathcal{K}_{1}(i):=\{1\leq k\leq d:\log|\beta_{k}|>\log|\beta_{i}|+t_{i}\},\ \mathcal{K}_{2}(i):=\{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t}):\log|\beta_{k}|+t_{k}\leq\log|\beta_{i}|+t_{i}\},\

and

\mathcal{K}_{3}(i):=\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus(\mathcal{K}_{1}(i)\cup\mathcal{K}_{2}(i)).

Remark 16
.

The above theorem was established in

[
10
, Theorem 12]
under the assumption that
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
is bounded and all eigenvalues are strictly large than one. In the case of positive eigenvalues, the non-increasing assumption on the approximation functions
\psi_{i}
is not required. We mention, so as to avoid confusion, that in the statement of
[
10
, Theorem 12]
it is assumed that
1<\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\beta_{d}
but this is not required.
Furthermore, in
[
10
, Section 5.2]
the proof of the lower bound for
\dim_{\rm H}W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})
was generalised to incorporate negative eigenvalues and this is where the non-increasing assumption on the approximation functions comes into play. Subsequently, staying within the bounded setup, the corresponding upper bound for the dimension was established in
[
9
]
. Indeed, under the assumption that
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
is bounded, our theorem corresponds to the statement of Theorem 1.7 together with Remark 9 in
[
9
]
or equivalently Claim 1 in
[
10
, Section 5.2]
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

As is usual when proving dimension statements, we establishing the upper and lower bounds for
\dim_{\rm H}W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})
separately. In both cases, we will freely draw upon the frameworks and results used in
[
9
,
10
]
that deal with the situation when
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
is bounded - see Remark
16
above. With this in mind, the proofs below are best read in conjunction with the relevant cited aspects of these works.

Upper bound
. The goal is to show that

\dim_{\rm H}W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})\leq\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)}\min\big\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})}\{{\xi}_{i}(\mathbf{t})\},\ \#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\big\}\,.

We first observe that when
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
is bounded, by definition we have that
\#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})=d
and so the desired statement corresponds to
[
9
, Proposition 4.1]
. For the proof see
[
9
, Section 4.1]
and note that it does not require the non-increasing assumption on the approximating functions. For the sake of completeness, we mention that in the case the eigenvalues are positive the statement corresponds to
[
10
, Proposition 4]
and is proved in
[
10
, Section 4.2.1]
.
Now to deal with the situation that
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
is unbounded, given that the ‘bounded’ result exists, we use the same arguments as in the proof of Proposition
2.1
in §
2.1
with obvious notational modification.

Lower bound
. We now turn our attention to establishing the complementary lower bound for
\dim_{\rm H}W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})
. As mentioned in Remark
16
, under the assumption that
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
is bounded, the desired lower bound was established in

[
10
, Section 5.2]
. The proof given there built upon the proof of Proposition 5 in
[
10
, Section 4.2.1]
in which the eigenvalues are assumed to be strictly greater than one.

To deal with the unbounded situation we adapt the proof in
[
10
, Section 5.2]
. In short, the key change is that we apply the unbounded Mass Transference Principle (Theorem
2.1
) in place of the bounded Mass Transference Principle (appearing as Theorem 11 in
[
10
]
). To illustrate how to implement this change, for ease of clarity suppose that the eigenvalues are strictly greater than one. Then, the following statement is the upshot of formulae (53) and (54) in
[
10
, §4.2.1]
and is at the heart of establishing Proposition 5 in
[
10
, Section 4.2.1]
. It has nothing to do with
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
being bounded.

Lemma 3.1
.

Under the setting of Theorem
3.1
, if in addition the eigenvalues are positive, we have that

W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})\supset\limsup_{n\to\infty}\bigcup_{j_{1}=1}^{M_{1,n}}\cdots\bigcup_{j_{d}=1}^{M_{d,n}}B\Big(x_{n,j_{1}}^{{(1)}},\beta_{1}^{-n}\psi_{1}(n)\Big)\times\cdots\times B\Big(x_{n,j_{d}}^{(d)},\beta_{d}^{-n}\psi_{d}(n)\Big)\,,

(3.2)

and for each
1\leq i\leq d

{\mathbb{T}}\ =\ \bigcup_{j_{i}=1}^{M_{i,n}}B\big(x_{n,j_{i}}^{(i)},(n+3)\beta_{i}^{-n}\big)\,.

(3.3)

Here, for each
1\leq i\leq d
, the centres
\{x_{n,j_{i}}^{(i)},1\leq j_{i}\leq M_{i,n}\}
are the preimages of
z_{i}
under
T_{\beta_{i}}^{n}
that fall within full cylinders of order
n
for
T_{\beta_{i}}
and
M_{i,n}
is the number of such full cylinders.

We now show that the desired lower bound (in the case the eigenvalues are strictly greater than one) is a consequence of Lemma
3.1
and Theorem
2.1
. So with this in mind, let
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{d})\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
. Then, by definition
there exists an integer subsequence
\{n_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}{-\log\psi_{i}(n_{\ell})\over n_{\ell}}=t_{i}\qquad\forall\ 1\leq i\leq d\,.

In turn, consider the following
\limsup
set

W=\limsup_{\ell\to\infty}\bigcup_{j_{1}=1}^{M_{1,n_{\ell}}}\cdots\bigcup_{j_{d}=1}^{M_{d,n_{\ell}}}B\Big(x_{n_{\ell},j_{1}}^{{(1)}},\beta_{1}^{-n_{\ell}}\psi_{1}(n_{\ell})\Big)\times\cdots\times B\Big(x_{n_{\ell},j_{d}}^{(d)},\beta_{d}^{-n_{\ell}}\psi_{d}(n_{\ell})\Big)\,.

In view of (
3.2
),

W\subset W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})\,.

With Theorem
2.1
in mind,
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, let

r_{\ell}:=e^{-n_{\ell}}

and for any
\epsilon>0
and
1\leq i\leq d
, let

\delta_{i}=1,\,\qquad u_{i}=\log\beta_{i}-\epsilon,\qquad v_{i,\ell}=\log\beta_{i}+{-\log\psi_{i}(n_{\ell})\over n_{\ell}}\,.

Then, for
1\leq i\leq d
and
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have that

r_{\ell}^{v_{i,\ell}}=\beta_{i}^{-n_{\ell}}\psi_{i}(n_{\ell})

and

v_{i}:=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}v_{i,\ell}=\log\beta_{i}+t_{i}\,.

Furthermore, with

s_{i,\ell}:=\frac{u_{i}}{v_{i,\ell}}\,,

it follows that for
\ell
sufficiently large

(\beta_{i}^{-n_{\ell}}\psi_{i}(n_{\ell}))^{s_{i,\ell}}=r_{\ell}^{u_{i}}=\beta_{i}^{-n_{\ell}}e^{n_{\ell}\epsilon}>(n_{\ell}+3)\beta_{i}^{-n_{\ell}}.

(3.4)

The upshot is that
if

B_{i,\ell}:=B\big(x_{n_{\ell},j_{i}}^{{(i)}},\beta_{i}^{-n_{\ell}}\psi_{i}(n_{\ell})\big)

is a ball appearing in the definition of
W
, then by (
3.4
), for
\ell
sufficiently large

B_{i,\ell}^{s_{i,\ell}}\supset\textstyle{B\Big(x_{n_{\ell},j_{i}}^{{(i)}},(n_{\ell}+3)\beta_{i}^{-n_{\ell}}\Big)}\qquad\forall\ 1\leq i\leq d\,.

Thus, in view of (
3.3
), given the
\limsup
set
W
arising from balls
B_{i,\ell}
the corresponding
\limsup
set comprising of ‘enlarged’ balls
B_{i,\ell}^{s_{i,\ell}}
is equal to
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and thus is of full
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.
Therefore, in view of Theorem
2.1
we conclude that

\dim_{\rm H}W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})\geq\ \dim_{\rm H}W\geq\ \min\left\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}s(\mathbf{u}{(\epsilon)}\,\mathbf{v},i),\ \#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})\right\}

(3.5)

with
\mathbf{u}{(\epsilon)}=(\log\beta_{1}-\epsilon,\dots,\log\beta_{d}-\epsilon)
and
\mathbf{v}=(\log\beta_{1},\dots,\log\beta_{d})+\mathbf{t}
.
It is evident that
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})
and that for all
1\leq i\leq d

s(\mathbf{u}{(\epsilon)},\mathbf{v},i)=\xi_{i}(\mathbf{t},\epsilon):=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(i)}1+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{2}(i)}\left(1-\frac{t_{k}}{\log\beta_{i}-\epsilon+t_{i}}\right)+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{3}(i)}\frac{\log\beta_{k}-\epsilon}{\log\beta_{i}-\epsilon+t_{i}}

where

\mathcal{K}_{1}(i):=\{1\leq k\leq d:\log\beta_{k}>\log\beta_{i}+t_{i}\},\ \mathcal{K}_{2}(i):=\{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t}):\log\beta_{k}+t_{k}\leq\log\beta_{i}+t_{i}\},\

and

\mathcal{K}_{3}(i):=\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus(\mathcal{K}_{1}(i)\cup\mathcal{K}_{2}(i)).

However,
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary and so

\dim_{\rm H}W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})\geq\ \min\left\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})}\xi_{i}(\mathbf{t}),\ \#\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{t})\right\}.

This is valid for any
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
and so we obtain the desired lower bound when the eigenvalues are strictly greater than one.

We now turn our attention to establishing the lower bound for
\dim_{\rm H}W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})
when the eigenvalues of
T
are negative. For this we follow the argument in
[
10
, Section 5.2]
and start by exploiting the framework of Markov subsystems to obtain the subset
W^{*}(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})
of
W(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})
under the assumption that the absolute values of the eigenvalues are
>8
. Then for this subset, we have an analogue of Lemma
3.1
, without the assumption that the eigenvalues are positive, in which (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) are respectively replaced by (69) and the displayed formula immediately following (69) in
[
10
, Section 5.2]
. As in the case of positive eigenvalues, this has nothing to do with
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
being bounded.
By the same arguments as in the above proof for positive eigenvalues, we make use of Theorem
2.1
to obtain a lower bound for
\dim_{\rm H}W^{*}(T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})
(and hence
\dim_{\rm H}W^{(}T,\Psi,\mathbf{z})
) for the case that
\mathcal{U}(\Psi)
is unbounded. In order to remove the assumption that the absolute values of all the eigenvalues of
T
are
>8
, we use the same approximating process employed at the end of
[
10
, Section 5.2]
.

4
Properties of the dimensional number

When it comes to proving Theorem
2.1
, it will be more convenient to work with a slightly more general formulation of the dimensional number than that introduced at the start of §
2.1
. As before, let
p
be a positive integer and let
\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{p}
be given. Also, for any

\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{p}
and

\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{+\infty\})^{p}
satisfying (
2.3
), let
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})
and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{v})
be as in
(
2.4
) and let

\mathcal{A}=\{u_{i}:1\leq i\leq p\}\cup\{v_{i}:1\leq i\leq p\}.

For each
A\in\mathcal{A}
, if

•

A<+\infty
, we let

{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},A):=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(A)}\delta_{k}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{2}(A)}\delta_{k}\left(1-\frac{v_{k}-u_{k}}{A}\right)+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{3}(A)}\frac{\delta_{k}u_{k}}{A}

(4.1)

where

\mathcal{K}_{1}(A):=\{1\leq k\leq p:u_{k}>A\},\qquad\mathcal{K}_{2}(A):=\{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v}):v_{k}\leq A\},\

and
\mathcal{K}_{3}(A):=\{1,\dots,p\}\setminus(\mathcal{K}_{1}(A)\cup\mathcal{K}_{2}(A))
.

•

A=+\infty
, we let

{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},A):=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\delta_{k}.

When
A=v_{i}
, we write
s(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},i)
for
s(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},A)
and when
A=u_{i}
we write
\overline{s}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},i)
for
s(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},A)
. Note that in the latter case, since
\mathbf{u}\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{p}
, we are always in the situation
A<+\infty
. Formally, for every
i=1,\dots,p
we define

\displaystyle s(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},i)

\displaystyle:=s(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},v_{i}),

\displaystyle\overline{s}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},i)

\displaystyle:=s(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},u_{i}).

Clearly, the quantity
s(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},i)
is precisely the same as that appearing in §
2.1
and we recall that the dimensional number
s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
with respect to
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
is defined as

s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\min_{1\leq i\leq p}\{{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)\}=\min\Big\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\{{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)\},\ \sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\delta_{i}\Big\}.

The main difference in the above presentation to that in §
2.1
is that it allows us to bring into play the quantity
\overline{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)
. This is important since it will naturally appear in the proof of Theorem
2.1
. Indeed, one initially ends up with showing that

\displaystyle\dim_{\rm H}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}}B_{i,n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\min\Big\{\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\{{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},A)\},\sum_{1\leq i\leq p}\delta_{i}\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\Big\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\{{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)\},\ \min_{1\leq i\leq p}\{\overline{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)\},\ \sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\delta_{i}\Big\}\,.

However, the following statement shows that the middle term on the right hand side involving
\overline{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)
plays no role and so the right hand side can be simply replaced by the dimensional number
s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{p}

and
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{+\infty\})^{p}
satisfying (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq i\leq p}\{\overline{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)\}\geq s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}).

(4.2)

In particular,

\displaystyle s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\min\Big\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\{{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)\},\ \min_{1\leq i\leq p}\{\overline{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},i)\},\ \sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\delta_{i}\Big\}.

(4.3)

The above proposition can be viewed as the unbounded version of
[
17
, Proposition 3.1]
.

Remark 17
.

It is useful to note that the definitions of
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}(A)
,
{\mathcal{K}}_{2}(A)
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{3}(A)
can be modified to either

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}_{1}(A):={\mathcal{K}}_{1}(A)\cup\{1\leq k\leq p:u_{k}=A\},&\hbox{}\\[4.30554pt]
\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}_{2}(A):={\mathcal{K}}_{2}(A)\setminus\{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{v}):v_{k}=A\},&\hbox{}\\[4.30554pt]
\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}_{3}(A):=\{1,\dots,p\}\setminus(\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{1}(A)\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{2}(A)),&\hbox{}\end{array}\right.{\text{\!\!\!or}}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\widehat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{1}(A):={\mathcal{K}}_{1}(A),&\hbox{}\\[4.30554pt]
\widehat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{2}(A):=\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}_{2}(A),&\hbox{}\\[4.30554pt]
\widehat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{3}(A):=\{1,\dots,p\}\setminus(\widehat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{1}(A)\cup\widehat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{2}(A)),&\hbox{}\end{array}\right.\

without effecting the outcome. More precisely, if
\widetilde{s}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},A)
and
\widehat{s}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},A)
denote the corresponding modified analogues of
{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},A)
associated with
\{\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}_{i}(A):1\leq i\leq 3\}
and
\{\widehat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{i}(A):1\leq i\leq 3\}
respectively, then

\widetilde{s}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},A)={s}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},A)\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\widehat{s}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},A)={s}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},A)\,.

(4.4)

Indeed, to see that this is the case, first note that

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}_{3}(A)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{K}}_{3}(A)\cup\{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{v}):v_{k}=A\}\setminus\{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{v}):u_{k}=A\}.

Now when
A=\infty
, it is easily seen that

\{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{v}):v_{k}=A\}=\{1\leq k\leq p:u_{k}=A\}=\emptyset

and so
\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}_{i}(A)={\mathcal{K}}_{i}(A)
for
i=1,2,3
and we are done. For the case
A<\infty
, it follows that

\displaystyle\widetilde{s}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},A)\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(A)}\delta_{k}+\sum_{1\leq k\leq p:u_{k}=A}\delta_{k}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{2}(A)}\delta_{k}\left(1-\frac{v_{k}-u_{k}}{A}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\quad\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{v}):v_{k}=A}\delta_{k}\left(1-\frac{v_{k}-u_{k}}{A}\right)+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{3}(A)}\frac{\delta_{k}u_{k}}{A}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\quad\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{v}):v_{k}=A}\frac{\delta_{k}u_{k}}{A}-\sum_{1\leq k\leq p:u_{k}=A}\frac{\delta_{k}u_{k}}{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(A)}\delta_{k}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{2}(A)}\delta_{k}\left(1-\frac{v_{k}-u_{k}}{A}\right)+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{3}(A)}\frac{\delta_{k}u_{k}}{A}\ =\ s(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},A).

This establishes the first of the equalities in (
4.4
). The proof can be modified in the obvious manner to prove the other equality in (
4.4
) and we leave the details to the reader.

The next result concerns the continuity of the dimensional number
s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v})
. Apart from playing a role in establishing Theorem
2.1
, it has already been exploited within the context of Remark
13
in §

.

Proposition 4.2
.

For any fixed
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{p}
,
the quantities
s(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},i)
and
\overline{s}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v},i)

are continuous with respect to
\mathbb{v}\in\prod_{i=1}^{p}[u_{i},+\infty]
in the following sense: let
\{\mathbb{v}(n)\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of vectors
\mathbb{v}(n)=(v_{1,n},\cdots,v_{p,n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{p}

with

(X,\tilde{\rho})
, that is the ball with the
same center as
R
and the radius 5 times bigger than the radius of
R
.

Recall that any
R=\prod_{i=1}^{p}B(x_{i,n},r_{n}^{u_{i}})\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
is a ball in
(X,\tilde{\rho})
of the radius
r_{n}^{u}
. So
5\tilde{\cdot}R
has radius
5r_{n}^{u}
. Thus, returning to the notation of the initial product space we get that
5\tilde{\cdot}R
is nothing but

\prod_{i=1}^{p}B(x_{i,n},5^{\frac{u_{i}}{u}}r_{n}^{u_{i}}).

The latter set is clearly contained in
5^{\frac{\max_{i=1,\dots,p}u_{i}}{\min_{i=1,\dots,p}u_{i}}}R
(where we now use the notation for dilatation
\kappa R
defined in (
5.3
)), and this completes the proof.
∎

We are ready to establish the
K_{G,B}
lemma for rectangles. Here and throughout,

\mu:=\mu_{1}\times\cdots\times\mu_{p}

is the natural product measure in Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma 5.2

(
K_{G,B}
lemma for rectangles)
.

Let
\{B_{i,n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of balls satisfying the assumptions in Theorem
2.1
, and let
B
be a ball in
X=\prod_{i=1}^{p}X_{i}
. For any
G\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a finite
sub-collection
K_{G,B}
of rectangles in the family

\Gamma:=\Big\{\widetilde{R}=\prod_{i=1}^{p}B(x_{i,n},r^{u_{i}}_{n}):n\geq G,\ \widetilde{R}\cap\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}B\neq\emptyset\Big\}

(5.10)

such that

•

the rectangles in
K_{G,B}
are contained in
{2\over 3}B
;

•

the rectangles in
K_{G,B}
are
5r
-separated; i.e. for distinct
\widetilde{R}
and
\widetilde{R^{\prime}}
in
K_{G,B}

5\widetilde{R}\cap 5\widetilde{R^{\prime}}=\emptyset;

(5.11)

•

the rectangles in
K_{G,B}
almost pack the ball
B
; i.e.

\mu\left(\bigcup_{\widetilde{R}\in K_{G,B}}\widetilde{R}\right)\asymp\mu(B),

(5.12)

where the implied constants are absolute.

Proof.

Since,
r_{n}^{u_{i}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for all
1\leq i\leq p
, we can ensure that every rectangle in
\Gamma
with
n
large enough, is contained in
{2\over 3}B
. Hence, without loss of generality, we can assume that all rectangles in
\Gamma
are contained in
{2\over 3}B
. Furthermore, the conditions of Lemma
5.1
are satisfied for the
5
-times scaled rectangles in
\Gamma
. So, applying Lemma
5.1
, we find a sub-collection
\Gamma_{1}\subset\Gamma
such that all rectangles of
\Gamma_{1}
are
5r
-separated and for which (
5.8
) holds.

Recall the measure condition (
2.12
) in the statement of Theorem
2.1
or equivalently (
2.16
); that is, for some
(u_{1},\dots,u_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{p}
, the
\limsup
set

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{p}B(x_{i,n},r^{u_{i}}_{n})\,,

is of full
\mu
-measure. Applying (
2.12
), we have that the ball
\frac{1}{2}B
is covered in measure by
\bigcup_{R\in\Gamma}R
. Hence,

\mu\left(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}B\right)\leq\mu\left(\bigcup_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma}\widetilde{R}\right)\leq\mu\left(\bigcup_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma}5\widetilde{R}\right)\leq\sum_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{1}}\mu\left(5^{\frac{\max_{i=1,\dots,p}u_{i}}{\min_{i=1,\dots,p}u_{i}}}(5\widetilde{R})\right)\asymp\sum_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{1}}\mu(\widetilde{R}).

Thus,

\sum_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{1}}\mu(\widetilde{R})\gg\mu(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}B)\gg\mu(B).

As all rectangles in
\Gamma_{1}
are disjoint and contained in
{2\over 3}B
, we have

\sum_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{1}}\mu(\widetilde{R})\ll\mu(B).

(5.13)

If the family
\Gamma_{1}
is finite, then we can just take
K_{G,B}=\Gamma_{1}
and the proof is complete. If
\Gamma_{1}
is countable, proceed as follows. First, we introduce a numbering of the rectangles in
\Gamma_{1}
; namely

\Gamma_{1}=\left\{\widetilde{R}_{i}:i\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}.

Then, the upper bound (
5.13
) implies that the series
\sum_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{1}}\mu(\widetilde{R})
is convergent. Hence, for
N
sufficiently large we have that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu(\widetilde{R}_{i})\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu(\widetilde{R}_{i})\,.

To complete the proof we take
K_{G,B}=\{\widetilde{R}_{i}\in\Gamma_{1}\mid i=1,\dots,N\}
and observe that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu(\widetilde{R}_{i})\asymp\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu(\widetilde{R}_{i})\asymp\mu(B).

∎

5.1.3
Notions and sub-collections associated with the
K_{G,B}
lemma.

We now introduce various notions and sub-collections associated with the
K_{G,B}
lemma that will be used in the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
Throughout,
\{B_{i,n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a sequence of balls satisfying the assumptions in Theorem
2.1
and
B
is an arbitrary ball in
X=\prod_{i=1}^{p}X_{i}
.
With this in mind, denote by
\mathcal{I}_{G,B}
the finite collection of indices
n\in\mathbb{N}
associated with the rectangles of the finite sub-collection
K_{G,B}
coming via Lemma
5.2
, so that

K_{G,B}=\Big\{\widetilde{R}:=\prod_{i=1}^{p}B\Big(x_{i,n},r_{n}^{u_{i}}\Big):n\in\mathcal{I}_{G,B}\Big\}.

(5.14)

We remark that the set
\mathcal{I}_{G,B}
is not uniquely defined since potentially we have many choices of the family
K_{G,B}
. However, as we will see, this will be made concrete when it comes to the construction of the desired Cantor set satisfying (
5.2
). Next, in view of the discussion in the previous section, the radii
r_{n}
appearing in (
5.14
) will be assumed to be powers of two and as a consequence we partition the indexing set
\mathcal{I}_{G,B}
according to such powers, thus

\mathcal{I}_{G,B}=\bigsqcup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{I}_{G,B,k},

where

\mathcal{I}_{G,B,k}:=\Big\{n\in\mathcal{I}_{G,B}:r_{n}=2^{-k}\Big\}.

(5.15)

Note that for all but finitely many
k
the set
\mathcal{I}_{G,B,k}
is empty. Now, for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
for which
\mathcal{I}_{G,B,k}\neq\emptyset
and for every
1\leq i\leq p
, define

v_{i}{(G,B,k)}:=\max_{n\in\mathcal{I}_{G,B,k}}v_{i,n}\,,

(5.16)

and in turn, let

S_{G,B,k}:=\left\{R=\prod_{i=1}^{p}B\Big(x_{i,n},2^{-k\,v_{i}{(G,B,k)}}\Big):\ n\in\mathcal{I}_{G,B,k}\right\}

and

S_{G,B}:=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}S_{G,B,k}.

Remark 18
.

We emphasise that
K_{G,B}
is a collection of “big” rectangles coming from Lemma
5.2
and
S_{G,B}
is a collection of “small” rectangles obtained by suitably shrinking the rectangles in
K_{G,B}
. Note that in view of (
5.5
) we have that
u_{i}<v_{i}{(G,B,k)}
and so the descriptors “big” and “small” are justified. There is clearly a one-to-one correspondence between the rectangles in
K_{G,B}
and
S_{G,B}
; namely that

K_{G,B}\ni\widetilde{R}:=\prod_{i=1}^{p}B\Big(x_{i,n},2^{-k\,u_{i}}\Big)\longleftrightarrow R:=\prod_{i=1}^{p}B\Big(x_{i,n},2^{-k\,v_{i}{(G,B,k)}}\Big)\in S_{G,B}.

The upshot is that inside each rectangle
\widetilde{R}\in K_{G,B}
there is precisely one shrunk rectangle
R\in S_{G,B}
.

With (
5.16
) in mind, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
for which
\mathcal{I}_{G,B,k}\neq\emptyset
, let

w(G,B,k):=\max_{1\leq i\leq p}v_{i}{(G,B,k)}\,.

(5.17)

Then, it follows from the
5r
-covering lemma and basic volume arguments that for each rectangle
R\in S_{G,B,k}
, we can find a finite collection
\mathcal{C}(R)
of disjoint balls
B^{\prime}
all with the same radius

r:=2^{-k\,w(G,B,k)}

such that they are
5r
-separated and

R\subset\bigsqcup_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(R)}25B^{\prime}.

By comparing the volume of the rectangle
R
with the sum of volumes of (disjoint) balls in
\mathcal{C}(R)
, we find that

\#\mathcal{C}(R)\asymp\prod_{i=1}^{p}\left(\frac{2^{-k\,v_{i}(G,B,k)\delta_{i}}}{2^{-k\,w(G,B,k)\delta_{i}}}\right)

(5.18)

where the implied constants are absolute (most importantly they are independent of the ball
B
).
Also, note that in view of the additional assumption (
5.4
),
the quantity
w(G,B,k)
increases as
G
increases and so

\lim_{G\to\infty}\ \min_{k:\mathcal{I}_{G,B,k}\neq\emptyset}w(G,B,k)=\infty\,.

(5.19)

The following “separation” statement is a simple consequence of the above definitions and construction.

Proposition 5.1
.

The balls in
\mathcal{F}_{G}(B):=\left\{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(R):R\in S_{G,B}\right\}
are
5r
-separated. In particular, if a given ball
B_{\lambda}:=B(x,\lambda)
intersects at least two balls in
\mathcal{F}_{G}(B)
, then its radius
\lambda
is larger than the radius of any ball in
\mathcal{F}_{G}(B)
intersecting
B_{\lambda}
. In turn, the balls in
\mathcal{F}_{G}(B)
that intersect
B_{\lambda}
are contained in
2B_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

The “in turn” part following directly from the “in particular” part and this follows directly from the first part. Regarding the latter, let
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{G}(B)
.
Then,
B_{1}\in\mathcal{C}(R_{1})
and
B_{2}\in\mathcal{C}(R_{2})
for some
R_{1},R_{2}\in S_{G,B}
.
If
R_{1}=R_{2}
, then
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are
5r
-separated by the definition of
\mathcal{C}(R)
. In the case
R_{1}\neq R_{2}
, let
\widetilde{R}_{1}
and
\widetilde{R}_{2}
respectively be the corresponding “big” rectangles in
K_{G,B}
. It is clear that
\widetilde{R}_{1}\neq\widetilde{R}_{2}
, so they are
5r
-separated by the definition of
K_{G,B}
which in turn implies the
5r
-separation of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
. This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

5.2
Constructing the Cantor set
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}(\epsilon)
and the measure
\nu_{\epsilon}

We fix an
\varepsilon>0
, which should be understood to be an arbitrarily small number given in advance. Recall, given a sequence
\{B_{i,n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of balls in
X_{i}
satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
, the overall goal is to
construct a Cantor type set

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}(\epsilon)\;\subset\;\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}}B_{i,n}\,,

and a measure
\nu_{\epsilon}
supported on
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}(\epsilon)
satisfying the Hölder exponent estimate (
5.1
).
Throughout, for any Borel measure
\theta
on
X={\prod_{i=1}^{p}}X_{i}
and any measurable subset
F\subset X
we use the standard notion
\theta\restriction_{F}
to denote the restriction of
\theta
to
F
; that is

\theta\restriction_{F}(A)=\theta(A\cap F),\ \text{for any measurable subset}\ A\subset X.

Also, recall that
\mu:=\mu_{1}\times\cdots\times\mu_{p}
is the natural product measure in Theorem
2.1
. Finally, from this point onwards, since
\epsilon>0
is fixed, in order to simplify notation we put

\nu=\nu_{\epsilon}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\infty}=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}(\epsilon)\,.

Having established the preliminaries we are now in the position to construct
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
with the desired properties.
The construction is inductive and we start by taking an arbitrary closed ball
B_{0}\subset X
such that
\mu(B_{0})=1
and we set

\mathcal{E}_{0}=\{B_{0}\}\,.

In turn, we let

\nu_{0}:=\mu\!\restriction_{B_{0}}\,.

In other words,
\nu_{0}
is simply the probability measure with support
B_{0}
that has a constant density on
B_{0}
with respect to
\mu
. Now since
r(B_{i,n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we have that

r_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and so there exists an integer
G_{0}
such that for all
n\geq G_{0}
.

\frac{\nu_{0}({B_{0}})}{\mu({B_{0}})}\leq r_{n}^{-\varepsilon}.

Furthermore, in view of (
5.19
) the integer
G_{0}

can be chosen such that for every
G\geq G_{0}

\min_{k:\mathcal{I}_{G,B_{0},k}\neq\emptyset}w(G,B_{0},k)>1.

Let

\mathbb{E}_{0}:=\bigcup_{{B}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}}{B}=B_{0}\,.

To proceed with the induction, assume that we have constructed, for certain
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

•

the finite disjoint collection of balls
\mathcal{E}_{j-1}
and thus the
(j-1)
-th level set

\mathbb{E}_{j-1}:=\bigcup_{{B}\in\mathcal{E}_{j-1}}{B}.

•

the probability measure
\nu_{j-1}
supported on
\mathbb{E}_{j-1}
satisfying

\sum_{B_{j-1}\in\mathcal{E}_{j-1}}\nu_{j-1}(B_{j-1})=1,

•

the sufficiently large integer
G_{j-1}
so that for all
{B}_{j-1}\in\mathcal{E}_{j-1}
,

\min_{k:\mathcal{I}_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1},k}\neq\emptyset}w(G_{j-1},B_{j-1},k)>1,

and

\frac{\nu_{j-1}({B_{j-1}})}{\mu({B_{j-1}})}\leq r_{n}^{-\varepsilon}\ \quad\text{for all \ $n\geq G_{j-1}$}\,.

Then, with the notions and notation of §
5.1.3
in mind, we let

\mathcal{E}_{j}:=\bigcup_{{B_{j-1}}\in\mathcal{E}_{j-1}}\bigcup_{R\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}}\mathcal{C}(R)

and set

\mathbb{E}_{j}:=\bigcup_{{B}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}}{B}.

By construction

\mathbb{E}_{j}\subset\mathbb{E}_{j-1}\,.

Furthermore, the balls in
\mathcal{E}_{j}
are disjoint and so to define the probability measure
\nu_{j}
supported on
\mathbb{E}_{j}
it suffices to define
\nu_{j}
on each ball
B_{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}
.
In order to do this, recall (again by construction) that for each
B_{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}
, there exist a unique ball
B_{j-1}\in\mathcal{E}_{j-1}
and a unique rectangle
R\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}
such that

B_{j}\subset R\subset B_{j-1}.

(5.20)

Then, we define

\nu_{j}\restriction_{B_{j}}:=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{C}(R)}\cdot\frac{\mu(\widetilde{R})}{\sum_{R^{\prime}\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}}\mu(\widetilde{R^{\prime}})}\cdot\nu_{j-1}(B_{j-1})\cdot\frac{\mu\restriction_{B_{j}}}{\mu(B_{j})}.

(5.21)

Hence,

\nu_{j}(B_{j})=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{C}(R)}\cdot\frac{\mu(\widetilde{R})}{\sum_{R^{\prime}\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}}\mu(\widetilde{R^{\prime}})}\cdot\nu_{j-1}(B_{j-1})

(5.22)

and it follows that for any
B_{j-1}\in\mathcal{E}_{j-1}

\nu_{j}(B_{j-1})=\sum_{R\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}}\sum_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(R)}\nu_{j}(B^{\prime})=\nu_{j-1}(B_{j-1}).

(5.23)

In turn, since the balls in
\mathcal{E}_{j}
are disjoint, we have that

\sum_{B_{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{j},B_{j}\subset B_{j-1}}\nu_{j}(B_{j})=\nu_{j}(B_{j-1})\,,

and so together (
•
‣
5.2
) and (
5.23
) it follows that

\sum_{B_{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}}\nu_{j}(B_{j})=\sum_{B_{j-1}\in\mathcal{E}_{j-1}}\sum_{B_{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{j},B_{j}\subset B_{j-1}}\nu_{j}(B_{j})=\sum_{B_{j-1}\in\mathcal{E}_{j-1}}\nu_{j}(B_{j-1})=1.

(5.24)

In other words,
\nu_{j}
is a probability measure supported on
\mathbb{E}_{j}
as desired.

We now turn our attention to choosing the integer
G_{j}
. Recall that the collection of balls
\mathcal{E}_{j}
is finite. Thus, in view of (
5.19
), we can choose
G_{j}
sufficiently large so that for all
{B}_{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}

\min_{k:\mathcal{I}_{G_{j},B_{j},k}\neq\emptyset}w(G_{j},B_{j},k)>j\geq 1.

(5.25)

Furthermore, since
r_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, the integer
G_{j}
can be chosen such that for all
{B}_{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}
and all
n\geq G_{j}

\frac{\nu_{j}({B_{j}})}{\mu({B_{j}})}\leq r_{n}^{-\varepsilon}.

(5.26)

This completes the inductive step and we define

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\ :=\ \bigcap_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{j}\ =\ \bigcap_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{{B}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}}{B}\,.

By construction,
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
is a Cantor subset of
\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}}B_{i,n}
.

Regarding the construction of a measure supported on
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
, we first note that on iterating (
5.23
), we find that for every
j,k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
and any ball
B_{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}

\nu_{j+k}(B_{j})=\nu_{j}(B_{j}).

(5.27)

In particular, it follows that each
\nu_{j}
is a probability measure on
B_{0}
and in turn that the weak-
*
limit
\nu
of the sequence
\{\nu_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
exists (see Remark
19
below). By construction,
\nu
is a probability measure supported on
E_{\infty}
and satisfies

\nu(B_{j})=\nu_{j}(B_{j})\qquad\forall\ B_{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}\quad{\rm and}\quad j\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}\,.

(5.28)

More generally, for any Borel subset
F
of
X

\nu(F):=\nu(F\cap\mathbb{E}_{\infty})\ =\ \inf\;\sum_{B\in\mathfrak{C}(F)}\nu(B)\ ,

where the infimum is taken over all coverings
\mathfrak{C}(F)
of
F\cap\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
by balls
B\in\bigcup_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}}\ \mathcal{E}_{j}
.

Remark 19
.

Although relatively standard and well know, for completeness we show that the sequence of measures
(\nu_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
has a weak-
*
limit
\nu
. Let
f:B_{0}\to[-1,1]
be a continuous function. Since
B_{0}
is closed,
f
is uniformly continuous on
B_{0}
. Hence, for any
\tilde{\varepsilon}>0
, we can find
\delta>0
such that, for every
x\in B_{0}
, we have

\max_{y\in B(x,\delta)}|f(x)-f(y)|<\tilde{\varepsilon}.

As the radii of balls in
\mathcal{E}_{j}
tend to 0 as
j\to\infty
, we infer that there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
m\geq N
and for all
B_{m}\in\mathcal{E}_{m}
, we have
r(B)<\delta
. So, for every
B_{m}\in\mathcal{E}_{m}
and every
x\in B_{m}
,

\left|\int_{B_{m}}fd\nu_{m}-\nu_{m}(B_{m})f(x)\right|<\tilde{\varepsilon}\nu_{m}(B).

Hence, for all
n>m\geq N
, taking any
x\in B_{m}
, by (
5.27
) we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B_{m}}fd\nu_{m}-\int_{B_{m}}fd\nu_{n}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{B_{m}}fd\nu_{m}-\nu_{m}(B_{m})f(x)+\nu_{n}(B_{m})f(x)-\int_{B_{m}}fd\nu_{n}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{B_{m}}fd\nu_{m}-\nu_{m}(B_{m})f(x)\right|+\left|\nu_{n}(B_{m})f(x)-\int_{B_{m}}fd\nu_{n}\right|

\displaystyle<2\tilde{\varepsilon}\nu_{m}(B_{m}).

Summing over all
B_{m}\in\mathcal{E}_{m}
, we obtain that

\left|\int_{B_{0}}fd\nu_{m}-\int_{B_{0}}fd\nu_{n}\right|<2\tilde{\varepsilon}.

Then, by Cauchy’s criterion, there exists a limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{B_{0}}fd\nu_{n}.

Therefore, we have a positive linear functional
\phi
on the set of continuous functions
f:B_{0}\to[-1,1]
defined by

\phi(f):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{B_{0}}fd\nu_{n}.

(5.29)

By Riesz-Markov-Kakutani Theorem (see
[
13
, Theorem 2.14]
), there exists a probability measure
\nu
which represents
\phi
, and hence, by (
5.29
),
\nu
is a weak-
*
limit of
(\nu_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
.

To complete the proof of Theorem
2.1
, it remains to show that
\nu
satisfies the Hölder exponent estimate (
5.1
). To start with, we prove the desired estimate for balls
B_{j}
appearing in the construction of
\mathbb{E}_{j}
.

5.2.1
Measure of balls in the Cantor construction

Let
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
and consider a ball
B_{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}
. Then by construction, there exist a unique ball
B_{j-1}\in\mathcal{E}_{j-1}
and a unique rectangle
R\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}
such that
B_{j}\in\mathcal{C}(R)
.
It follows from (
5.22
)
and (
5.28
) that

\nu(B_{j})=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{C}(R)}\cdot\frac{\mu(\widetilde{R})}{\sum_{R^{\prime}\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}}\mu(\widetilde{R^{\prime}})}\cdot\nu(B_{j-1}).

(5.30)

Throughout this and the subsequent section, we will use the following shorthand to simplify the notation.
Let
B_{j-1}\in\mathcal{E}_{j-1}
be given. Then for each
1\leq i\leq p
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}

for which
\mathcal{I}_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1},k}\neq\emptyset
, we set

\displaystyle v_{i}(j-1,k)

\displaystyle:=v_{i}(G_{j-1},B_{j-1},k),

(5.31)

\displaystyle w(j-1,k)

\displaystyle:=w(G_{j-1},B_{j-1},k).

Thus, the definition of
w(G_{j-1},B_{j-1},k)
(see (
5.17
)) in the above notation reads

w(j-1,k)=\max_{1\leq i\leq p}v_{i}(j-1,k).

(5.32)

Recall that by the definition of
\mathcal{C}(R)
and Remark
18
, for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and some index
n\in\mathcal{I}_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1},k}

B_{j}=B\left(\mathbf{z},2^{-k\,w(j-1,k)}\right),\ \quad R=\prod_{i=1}^{p}B\left(x_{i,n},2^{-k\,v_{i}(j-1,k)}\right),\ \quad\widetilde{R}=\prod_{i=1}^{p}B\left(x_{i,n},2^{-k\,u_{i}}\right)\,.

By the Ahlfors regularity assumption of the measures
\mu_{i}
,
we have that

\mu(\widetilde{R})\asymp\prod_{i=1}^{p}2^{-ku_{i}\delta_{i}}.

(5.33)

Further, it follows from the definition of
S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}
that

\sum_{R^{\prime}\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}}\mu(\widetilde{R^{\prime}})\asymp\mu(B_{j-1}).

(5.34)

Then, by (
5.18
), (
5.30
), (
5.33
) and (
5.34
), it follows that

\displaystyle\nu(B_{j})

\displaystyle\asymp

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{p}\left(\frac{2^{-k\,w(j-1,k)\delta_{i}}}{2^{-k\,v_{i}(j-1,k)\delta_{i}}}\right)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{p}2^{-k\,u_{i}\delta_{i}}\cdot\frac{\nu(B_{j-1})}{\mu(B_{j-1})}

(5.35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-k\,w(j-1,k)\,s(j)}\cdot\frac{\nu(B_{j-1})}{\mu(B_{j-1})}\,,

where
s(j)
is given by

s(j):=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{i}\left(1-\frac{v_{i}(j-1,k)-u_{i}}{w(j-1,k)}\right).

Now note that by the definition of
w(j-1,k)
(see (
5.32
)) and by (
5.19
), we have that

\frac{v_{i}(j-1,k)-u_{i}}{w(j-1,k)}\leq 1\ \ {\text{for all}}\ i\not\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{v})\,,

and

\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{v_{i}(j-1,k)}{w(j-1,k)}=0\ \ {\text{for all}}\ i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{v}).

Thus, it follows that for all
j
sufficiently large

s(j)\geq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{v})}\delta_{i}-\epsilon.

Feeding this into (
5.35
) together with (
5.26
) and (
5.25
), implies that for all
j
sufficiently large

\displaystyle\nu(B_{j})\ll 2^{-k\,w(j-1,k)\cdot s(j)-\epsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r(B_{j})^{s(j)-\epsilon/w(j-1,k)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r(B_{j})^{s(j)-\epsilon}\leq r(B_{j})^{s(\mathbb{v},\epsilon)}\,,

where

s(\mathbb{v},\epsilon):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{v})}\delta_{i}-2\epsilon.

To obtain the desired estimate (
5.1
) for the ball
B_{j}
, we note that by the definition of
s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v})
(see (
2.5
)) we have that

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{v})}\delta_{i}\geq s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v})

and so

\nu(B_{j})\ \ll\ r(B_{j})^{s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v})-2\epsilon}\,.

(5.36)

Remark 20
.

For later use, we note at this point that for a rectangle
R\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}
we can use (
5.30
) to estimate
\nu(R)
. Indeed, since
the balls of
\mathcal{E}_{j}
contained in
R
have the same
\nu
-measure, it follows via (
5.30
) and (
5.34
) that

\nu(\widetilde{R})=\nu(R)=\#\mathcal{C}(R)\cdot\nu(B_{j})\asymp\mu(\widetilde{R})\cdot\frac{\nu(B_{j-1})}{\mu(B_{j-1})}\,.

(5.37)

The fact that

\nu(\widetilde{R})=\nu({R})

immediately follows from the one to one correspondence of
R
and
\widetilde{R}
(see Remark
18
) and the construction of
\nu
; namely that it is defined via balls
B_{j}
contained in rectangles
R\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}
.

5.2.2
Measure of a general ball.

Throughout, let

B:=B(\mathbf{x},r)

be an arbitrary ball in
X
with centre
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
and radius
r>0
. Since
\mathbf{x}
is in the Cantor set, it follows that there exists an integer
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
B(\mathbf{x},r)
intersects only one ball
B_{j-1}
in
\mathcal{E}_{j-1}
and at least two balls in
\mathcal{E}_{j}
. In view of the latter, by Proposition
5.1
we have that
r
is larger than the radius of any ball
B_{j}
in
\mathcal{E}_{j}
intersecting
B(\mathbf{x},r)
; that is

r\,>\,\max\Big\{r(B_{j})\,:\,B_{j}\in\ \mathcal{E}_{j}\ \ {\rm with}\ \ B_{j}\cap B(\mathbf{x},r)\neq\emptyset\Big\}\,.

(5.38)

In this section, we will continue to use the shorthand notation (
5.31
) introduced in the previous section. Recall, that the goal is to show that
\nu(B(\mathbf{x},r))
satisfies the Hölder exponent estimate (
5.1
). First, we deal with the case
r>r({B_{j-1}})
. By (
5.36
),

\nu(B(\mathbf{x},r))\leq\nu(B_{j-1})\ll r(B_{j})^{s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v})-2\epsilon}\leq r^{s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v})-2\epsilon}.\,

Hence, the ball
B(\mathbf{x},r)
satisfies the desired estimate (
5.1
). Without loss of generality, we assume that

r\leq r({B_{j-1}}).

(5.39)

It follows from the construction that the balls in
\mathcal{E}_{j}
that intersect
B(\mathbf{x},r)
are contained in

\{B_{j}\in\mathcal{C}(R):R\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}\}
. Let

\mathcal{R}:=\left\{R\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}:\mathcal{C}(R)\text{ contains a ball}\text{ intersecting }B(\mathbf{x},r)\right\}

and let

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}:=\left\{\widetilde{R}:R\in\mathcal{R}\right\}\,.

It follows that

\nu\Big(B(\mathbf{x},r)\Big)=\nu\Big(B(\mathbf{x},r)\cap\bigcup_{\widetilde{R}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}\widetilde{R}\Big)

(5.40)

Recall (see Remark
18
), each rectangle
\widetilde{R}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}
is of the form

\widetilde{R}=\prod_{i=1}^{p}B(x_{i,n},r_{n}^{u_{i}})\,\quad\text{for some}\ \ n\geq 1\,,

and we let

\tilde{r}:\widetilde{R}\mapsto\tilde{r}({\widetilde{R}}):=r_{n}\,.

For ease of notation, without loss of generality, we assume that
u_{1}=\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\{u_{i}\}
and we divide
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}
into two subfamilies:

\Gamma_{1}:=\Big\{\widetilde{R}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}:\ r\geq(\textstyle{1\over 2}\tilde{r}(\widetilde{R}))^{u_{1}}\Big\},

and

\Gamma_{2}:=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\setminus\Gamma_{1}.

With (
5.40
) in mind, to estimate the measure of
B(\mathbf{x},r)
, we consider separately the contribution from rectangles in
\Gamma_{1}
and from
\Gamma_{2}
.

\bullet
First we consider the contribution from rectangles in
\Gamma_{1}
to the measure of
B(\mathbf{x},r)
. By the definition of
\Gamma_{1}
, the radius
r
is sufficiently large so that all that rectangles in
\Gamma_{1}
are contained in the scaled ball

(2^{u_{1}+1}+1)B=B(\mathbf{x},(2^{u_{1}+1}+1)r)\,.

Thus, by (
5.37
),

\displaystyle\nu\Big(B(\mathbf{x},r)\cap\bigcup_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{1}}\widetilde{R}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{1}}\nu(\widetilde{R})\asymp\sum_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{1}}\mu(\widetilde{R})\cdot\frac{\nu(B_{j-1})}{\mu(B_{j-1})}

\displaystyle\leq\mu\big(B(\mathbf{x},(2^{u_{1}+1}+1)r)\big)\cdot\frac{\nu(B_{j-1})}{\mu(B_{j-1})}.

(5.41)

Then, by using (
5.36
) and then (
5.39
), we infer from (
5.41
) that

\displaystyle\nu\Big(B(\mathbf{x},r)\cap\bigcup_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{1}}\widetilde{R}\Big)

\displaystyle\ll\mu(B(\mathbf{x},r))\cdot\frac{r(B_{j-1})^{s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v})-2\varepsilon}}{\mu(B_{j-1})}

\displaystyle\asymp r^{\delta_{1}+\cdots+\delta_{p}}\cdot\frac{r(B_{j-1})^{s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v})-2\varepsilon}}{r(B_{j-1})^{\delta_{1}+\cdots+\delta_{p}}}

\displaystyle\leq r^{s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v})-2\varepsilon}.

(5.42)

\bullet
To estimate the contribution from rectangles in
\Gamma_{2}
,
we subdivide
\Gamma_{2}
into finer sub-classes. For every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
for which
\mathcal{I}_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1},k}\neq\emptyset
, we let

\Gamma_{2,k}:=\left\{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{2}:R\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1},k}\right\}\,

and we proceed to estimate the quantity

\nu\Big(B(\mathbf{x},r)\cap\bigcup_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{2,k}}\widetilde{R}\Big)\,.

(5.43)

We start with some preliminaries. Let
\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{2,k}
for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Recall that
\widetilde{R}
is of the form
\widetilde{R}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}B(x_{i,n},2^{-k\,u_{i}})
.
Furthermore, by the definition of

\Gamma_{2,k}
we have

r<\big({\textstyle{1\over 2}}\tilde{r}(\widetilde{R})\big)^{u_{1}}<2^{-k\cdot u_{1}}.

(5.44)

On the other hand, it follows from (
5.38
) that

r\geq 2^{-k\cdot w(j-1,k)}.

(5.45)

With the quantities appearing in (
5.31
) in mind, define

\mathcal{A}_{j,k}\ :=\ \big\{u_{i}:1\leq i\leq p\big\}\ \cup\ \big\{v_{i}(j-1,k):1\leq i\leq p\big\}\,.

Note that since
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
\mathcal{I}_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1},k}\neq\emptyset
, both the above sets are non-empty. Also, note that by definition and the fact that we can assume that (
5.5
) is true for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, it follows that
w(j-1,k)
is the maximum of the finite set
\mathcal{A}_{j,k}
. It is clear that there are
2\leq t\leq 2p
elements in
\mathcal{A}_{j,k}
. In what follows, we will denote by
A_{\ell}

(1\leq\ell\leq t)
the elements of
\mathcal{A}_{j,k}
listed in ascending order. In other words,

\mathcal{A}_{j,k}=\left\{A_{1},\dots,A_{t}\right\}

and
A_{\ell}<A_{\ell+1}
for all
1\leq\ell\leq t
.
It follows from (
5.44
) and (
5.45
) that there exists an index
\ell
such that for two consecutive terms
A_{\ell}
and
A_{\ell+1}
of
\mathcal{A}_{j,k}
, we have that

2^{-k\cdot A_{\ell+1}}\,\leq\ r\ <\,2^{-k\cdot A_{\ell}}\,.

(5.46)

With this in mind, let

{\mathcal{K}}_{1}=\{i:u_{i}\geq A_{\ell+1}\},\quad\ {\mathcal{K}}_{2}=\{i:v_{i}(j-1,k)\leq A_{\ell}\},\quad\ {\mathcal{K}}_{3}=\{1,\cdots,p\}\setminus({\mathcal{K}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{K}}_{2}).

We are now in the position to estimate (
5.43
). We proceed in two steps.

(i)

We estimate the cardinality of
\Gamma_{2,k}
. Note that if
i\not\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
, then
u_{i}<A_{\ell+1}
and so
u_{i}\leq A_{\ell}
.
Therefore, we can define an “enlarged” body of the ball
B(\mathbf{x},r)
:

H:=\prod_{i=1}^{p}B(x_{i},(2^{u_{i}+1}+1)\epsilon_{i})\quad{\text{where}}\quad\epsilon_{i}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r&\hbox{if \ $i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1}$;}\\[8.61108pt]
2^{-k\cdot u_{i}}&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Then, all the rectangles
\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{2,k}
are contained in
H
. Since these rectangles
\widetilde{R}
are disjoint, a straightforward volume argument shows that

\#\Gamma_{2,k}\ \ll\ \prod_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}\left(\frac{r^{\delta_{i}}}{2^{-k\cdot u_{i}\delta_{i}}}\right).

(5.47)

(ii)

For every
\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{2,k}
, we bound from above the total number
T
of balls
B_{j}\in\mathcal{C}(R)
intersecting the ball
B(\mathbf{x},r)
.
By (
5.38
) and if necessary also see Proposition
5.1
, it follows that these balls
B_{j}
are contained in
B(\mathbf{x},2r)\cap R
. Also recall that by definition, the radius of any
B_{j}
in
\mathcal{C}(R)
is
2^{-k\cdot w(j-1,\,k)}
. Thus a straightforward volume argument yields that

\displaystyle T\cdot 2^{-k\cdot w(j-1,k)\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{i}}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\mu\Big({R}\cap B(x,2r)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{}\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{K}}_{3}}2^{-k\cdot v_{i}(j-1,k)\delta_{i}}\cdot\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}r^{\delta_{i}}.

In turn, this together with (
5.47
), enables us to bound from above the total number
M
of balls
B_{j}
in the collection
\bigcup_{R\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}}\mathcal{C}(R)

intersecting
B(\mathbf{x},r)
:

\displaystyle M\ \ll\

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}\left(\frac{r^{\delta_{i}}}{2^{-k\cdot u_{i}\delta_{i}}}\right)\cdot T

\displaystyle\ \ll\

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}\left(\frac{r^{\delta_{i}}}{2^{-k\cdot u_{i}\delta_{i}}}\right)\cdot 2^{k\cdot w(j-1,k)\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{i}}\cdot\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{K}}_{3}}2^{-k\cdot v_{i}(j-1,k)\delta_{i}}\cdot\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}r^{\delta_{i}}.

(5.48)

It follows from (
5.22
) and (
5.27
), that for every
B_{j}\in\bigcup_{R\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}}\mathcal{C}(R)
,

\nu(B_{j})\asymp\frac{\mu(\widetilde{R})}{\#\mathcal{C}(R)}\cdot\frac{\nu(B_{j-1})}{\mu(B_{j-1})},

where
R\in S_{G_{j-1},B_{j-1}}
is such that
B_{j}\in\mathcal{C}(R)
. Hence, it follows that

\nu(B(\mathbf{x},r)\cap\Gamma_{2,k})\ \ll\ M\cdot(\#\mathcal{C}(R))^{-1}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{p}2^{-k\cdot u_{i}\delta_{i}}\cdot\frac{\nu(B_{j-1})}{\mu(B_{j-1})}.

By (
5.18
), and (
5.48
) we have

\displaystyle\nu(B(\mathbf{x},r)\cap\Gamma_{2,k})

\displaystyle\ \ll\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}r^{\delta_{i}}\cdot\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}r^{\delta_{i}}\cdot\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}\frac{2^{-k\cdot u_{i}\delta_{i}}}{2^{-k\cdot v_{i}(j-1,k)\delta_{i}}}\cdot\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{3}}2^{-k\cdot u_{i}\delta_{i}}\cdot\frac{\nu(B_{j-1})}{\mu(B_{j-1})}

\displaystyle\leq{r^{s}}\cdot\frac{\nu(B_{j-1})}{\mu(B_{j-1})},

(5.49)

where the last inequality is true if
s
satisfies

s\ \leq\ \sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}\delta_{i}+\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}\delta_{i}+\frac{1}{\log r}\left({\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{3}}u_{i}\delta_{i}}-\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}(v_{i}(j-1,k)-u_{i}))\delta_{i}\right)\log 2^{-k}

(5.50)

for all
r
in the range (
5.46
); i.e.

2^{-k\cdot A_{\ell+1}}\leq r<2^{-k\cdot A_{\ell}}
.

Note that the right hand side of (
5.50
) is monotonic in
r
. Thus,
the optimal choice of
s
is attained at either
r=2^{-k\cdot A_{\ell+1}}
or
r=2^{-k\cdot A_{\ell}}
; i.e. the minimum of the following two numbers:

s_{1}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}\delta_{i}+\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}\delta_{i}+\frac{1}{A_{\ell}}\Big(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{3}}u_{i}\delta_{i}-\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}(v_{i}(j-1,k)-u_{i})\delta_{i}\Big),

and

s_{2}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}\delta_{i}+\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}\delta_{i}+\frac{1}{A_{\ell+1}}\Big(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{3}}u_{i}\delta_{i}-\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}(v_{i}(j-1,k)-u_{i})\delta_{i}\Big).

Now, recall the definition of the dimensional number
{s}_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
given by (
2.5
) and the quantity
{s}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},A)
given by (
4.1
). Also, recall that
A_{\ell}
and
A_{\ell+1}
are two different but neighboring numbers in
\mathcal{A}_{j,k}
. It follows that

\displaystyle{{\mathcal{K}}}_{1}(A_{\ell})=\{i:u_{i}>A_{\ell}\}

\displaystyle=\{i:u_{i}\geq A_{\ell+1}\}{={\mathcal{K}}_{1}},\ \quad\ {{\mathcal{K}}}_{2}(A_{\ell})=\{i:v_{i}(j-1,k)\leq A_{\ell}\}={\mathcal{K}}_{2}.

Hence,
s_{1}
is nothing but the quantity
s(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v}(j-1,k),A_{\ell})
as defined in (
4.1
),
and
s_{2}
is equal to the quantity
s(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v}(j-1,k),A_{\ell+1})
.
The upshot of this is that

\displaystyle s\ \geq\ \min\{s_{1},s_{2}\}\ \geq\ s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v}(j-1,k))\ \geq\ s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v})-\varepsilon,

where we have used first Proposition
4.1
and then (
5.6
). On feeding this into (
5.49
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\nu\Big(B(\mathbf{x},r)\cap\bigcup_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{2,k}}\widetilde{R}\Big)\leq{r^{s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v})-\varepsilon}}\cdot\frac{\nu(B_{j-1})}{\mu(B_{j-1})}.

It follows from (
5.26
) that

\frac{\nu(B_{j-1})}{\mu(B_{j-1})}\leq r_{n}^{-\varepsilon}=2^{-k\epsilon}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\nu\Big(B(\mathbf{x},r)\cap\bigcup_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{2}}\widetilde{R}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\nu\Big(B(\mathbf{x},r)\cap\bigcup_{\widetilde{R}\in\Gamma_{2,k}}\widetilde{R}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}r^{s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v})-\epsilon}\cdot 2^{-k\epsilon}\ \asymp\ r^{s_{0}(\mathbb{u},\mathbb{v})-\epsilon}.

This together with (
5.40
) and (
5.42
) establishes (
5.1
) for an arbitrary ball and thereby completes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

6
Extending Theorem
2.1
to resonant sets

In the setup of Theorem
2.1
, the rectangles arise as products of balls
B_{i,n}
in
X_{i}
. Balls of radius
r
are of course
r
-neighbourhoods of special points; namely their centres.
The general ‘rectangles to rectangles’ Mass Transference Principle of Wang
\&
Wu
[
17
]
is based on the framework of ubiquitous systems. This allows them to naturally consider the situation in which the balls
B_{i,n}
are replaced by neighbourhoods of special sets called resonant sets. It is not particularly surprising that on combining the arguments in Wang-Wu
[
17
]
and the current paper, Theorem
2.1
can be extended to case of resonant sets. In this section we briefly describe the setup and state the resonant set version of Theorem
2.1
. The details are left to the energetic reader.

For each
1\leq i\leq p
, let

X_{i}
be a locally compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i}}
equipped with a
\delta_{i}
-Ahlfors regular measure
\mu_{i}
.
Let
J
be an infinite countable index set and
\beta:J\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a positive function such that for any
M>1
, the set
\{\alpha\in J:\beta_{\alpha}<M\}
is finite. For
1\leq i\leq p
, let
{\cal R}_{i}:=\{R_{i,\alpha}:\alpha\in J\}
be a sequence of subset of
X_{i}
. The sets associated with
{\cal R}_{i}
are referred to as
resonant sets
. Denote by
\Delta(R_{i,\alpha},r)
the
r
-neighborhood of
R_{i,\alpha}
in
X_{i}
. Naturally, in the case that the resonant sets are points
\{x_{i,\alpha}:\alpha\in J\}
as within the framework of Theorem
2.1
, the neighbourhood
\Delta(R_{i,\alpha},r)
corresponds to a ball
B(x_{i,\alpha},r)
. In order to state the analogue of Theorem
2.1
for resonant sets, we need to impose a “scaling” condition on the neighbourhood of resonant sets and in turn appropriately modify the dimension number.

•

As in
[
17
]
, we assume that for each
1\leq i\leq p
, the sequence
{\cal R}_{i}
of subsets of
X_{i}
satisfy the
\kappa
-scaling property. Recall (see for instance
[
17
, Definition 3.1]
), given
{\cal R}_{i}
and
0\leq\kappa<1
, we say that
{\cal R}_{i}
satisfies the
\kappa
-scaling property
if there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2},r_{0}>0
such that for any
\alpha\in J
, any ball
B(x_{i},r)\subset X_{i}
with center
x_{i}\in R_{i,\alpha}
and any
0<\varepsilon<r<r_{0}
, we have

c_{1}\;r_{i}^{\delta_{i}\kappa}\varepsilon^{\delta_{i}(1-\kappa)}\,\leq\,\mu_{i}(B(x_{i},r_{i})\cap\Delta(R_{i,\alpha},\varepsilon))\,\leq\,c_{2}\;r_{i}^{\delta_{i}\kappa}\varepsilon^{\delta_{i}(1-\kappa)}\;.

Observe that in the case the resonant sets are points,
{\cal R}_{i}
satisfies the
\kappa
-scaling property with
\kappa=0
.

•

With the setup leading to (
2.5
) in mind, the following modification of the dimension number
s_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
appearing in Theorem
2.1
reflects the role of the above
\kappa
-scaling property:

s_{0}^{*}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=\min\big\{\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\{{\theta}^{*}_{i}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\},\ \sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})}\delta_{i}\big\},

where

{\theta}^{*}_{i}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(i)}\delta_{k}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{2}(i)}\delta_{k}\left(1-\frac{{(1-\kappa)}(v_{k}-u_{k})}{v_{i}}\right)+{(1-\kappa)}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{3}(i)}\frac{\delta_{k}u_{k}}{v_{i}}.

The following statement constitutes the unbounded version of the ‘rectangles to rectangles’ Mass Transference Principle for resonant sets.

Theorem 6.1
.

Under the setting above, for each
1\leq i\leq p
, let
\psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a real positive function such that
\psi_{i}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
Furthermore, assume that there exist functions
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
v_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
(
1\leq i\leq p
) such that

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\rho(t)=0\,,

\psi_{i}(\beta_{\alpha})=\rho(\beta_{\alpha})^{v_{i}(\beta_{\alpha})}

and

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}v_{i}(t)=v_{i},

for some
{\mathbf{v}}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{+\infty\})^{p}
.
Finally, suppose that there exists
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{p})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{p}
verifying (
2.3
) and

\mu_{1}\times\cdots\times\mu_{p}\left(\limsup_{\alpha\in J:\beta_{\alpha}\to\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\Delta\Big(R_{\alpha,i},\rho(\beta_{\alpha})^{u_{i}}\Big)\right)=\mu_{1}\times\cdots\times\mu_{p}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}X_{i}\right).

(6.1)

Then
