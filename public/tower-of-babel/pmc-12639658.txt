Periodontal ligament stem cells in tissue remodeling: from mechanical forces to inflammatory signals
Authors: Zimo Zhao, Liyuan Chen, Shuyue Zhu, Huajie Yu, Yuetong Chen, Jiayi Song, Yan Liu, Danqing He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe periodontal ligament (PDL), a specialized fibrous connective tissue bridging cementum and alveolar bone, harbors periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) as its key regenerative cellular component. Within the oral cavity, PDLSCs are continually exposed to two predominant stimuli: mechanical forces and inflammatory signals. Under physiological conditions, PDLSCs experience cyclic loading forces during normal mastication. During orthodontic treatment, controlled mechanical force stimulates PDLSCs and mediates tooth movement. However, in pathological scenarios, pathological mechanical stress, whether from occlusal trauma or excessive orthodontic forces, can induce PDL damage, potentially leading to adverse outcomes such as root resorption or pathological alveolar bone loss. Additionally, bacterially-induced inflammation can trigger destructive PDL changes, including alveolar bone and soft tissue degradation. Crucially, PDLSCs serve as central regulators of both the pathogenesis and therapeutic resolution of these processes. This review comprehensively summarizes the modulatory roles of PDLSCs in alveolar bone remodeling under mechanical stimulation and inflammatory conditions. It covers the alterations in the biological properties and functions of PDLSCs, along with their interactions with other bone remodeling-related cells and the microenvironment. Moreover, this review emphasizes the mechanisms by which PDLSCs regulate alveolar bone remodeling.Keywords: Periodontal ligament stem cells, Bone remodeling, Mechanical forces, Inflammatory signals

Introduction

The periodontium, comprising the PDL, alveolar bone, and root cementum, plays a pivotal role in sustaining normal tooth function under continuous masticatory forces and in protecting the teeth, nerves, and blood vessels from damage. However, overloading, inflammation, or trauma can induce periodontal defects, such as alveolar bone resorption and PDL destruction, potentially leading to early tooth loss [1]. The PDL is a specialized connective soft tissue that lies between the cementum and alveolar bone. It maintains and supports the teeth and preserves tissue homeostasis via the collagen fibre network [2]. The PDL is mechanosensitive and constantly responds to masticatory forces. Moreover, it furnishes a microenvironment for cellular reactions under mechanical stimulation or inflammatory conditions, thereby contributing to alveolar bone remodeling [3, 4].

The PDL is highly vascularized and consists of various cell types, such as fibroblasts, stem/progenitor cells, bone-lining cells, osteoclasts, endothelial cells, nerve cells, and others [5]. Periodontal tissues derive from the neural crest, and PDLSCs are derived from some remaining neural crest cells [6]. PDLSCs, which function as the principal mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in the PDL, were initially isolated and characterized by Seo and colleagues in 2004 [5]. PDLSCs exhibit characteristic MSC surface markers and demonstrate fundamental MSC properties, including self-renewal capability, multilineage differentiation potential, and immunomodulatory functions [5].

Notably, PDLSCs exhibit remarkable responsiveness to diverse stimuli, with particular sensitivity to mechanical forces and inflammatory mediators within the periodontal microenvironment. Under diverse environmental conditions, particularly under mechanical and inflammatory stimuli, PDLSCs influence periodontal tissue remodeling through changes in their biological properties and functions and through interactions with other bone remodeling-related cells, including immune cells, osteoclasts, and osteoblasts, as well as the extracellular matrix (ECM) [4, 7–9]. Nevertheless, the detailed mechanisms underlying how PDLSCs respond to the periodontal microenvironment and modulate periodontal soft tissues and alveolar bone remodeling remain to be comprehensively reviewed.

This review provides a comprehensive analysis of four key aspects of PDLSC biology: (1) dynamic changes in biological properties and functions of PDLSCs under mechanical forces and inflammatory microenvironment; (2) intricate cellular interactions between PDLSCs and other cell types, with special emphasis on immune cell crosstalk; (3) PDLSC-mediated ECM remodeling processes; and (4) molecular mechanisms of mechanotransduction and inflammatory signaling pathways. Through these systematic explorations, we aim to establish an integrated understanding of PDLSC behavior in physiological and pathological contexts.

The biological characteristics of PDLSCs and their roles in periodontal tissue development

Comparative analyses reveal that PDLSCs demonstrate enhanced stem cell properties relative to other MSC populations, exhibiting superior proliferative capacity and more pronounced stemness characteristics. Notably, PDLSCs display an expanded repertoire of embryonic stem cell markers and maintain a more primitive, undifferentiated phenotype. For instance, PDLSCs express more embryonic stem cell markers when contrasted with MSCs derived from peripheral blood and umbilical cord [7]. Comparative studies have demonstrated that PDLSCs exhibit significantly higher expression levels of stage-specific embryonic antigen 4 than both dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs), further confirming their relatively primitive undifferentiated state [9]. They also demonstrate significantly enhanced proliferative capacity, maintaining their self-renewal potential through at least 100 population doublings [8], a proliferative capacity more than twofold greater than that observed in human BMMSCs [10]. Liu et al. discovered that the periodontal tissue exhibited strong activity and self-repair ability within the chewing stress zone, suggesting that the proliferation ability of PDLSCs is associated with mechanical load [11].

PDLSCs exhibit remarkable multipotent differentiation capabilities when cultured under appropriate inductive conditions. Beyond their well-documented osteogenic and adipogenic potential, these cells demonstrate the capacity to differentiate into diverse cell lineages, including cardiomyocytes, islet-like cells, neurons, and chondrocytes [12–16]. Importantly, in vivo studies have confirmed PDLSCs’ ability to regenerate cementum-like mineralized tissue and periodontal ligament-like structures [17]. Furthermore, compelling evidence shows that PDLSCs can recapitulate the complex periodontal attachment apparatus through their unique capacity to generate multiple tissue components, including osteoblasts, fibroblasts, and cementoblasts [18, 19]. These distinctive regenerative properties establish PDLSCs as particularly promising candidates for engineering alveolar bone and periodontal tissues.

PDLSCs exhibit pronounced responsiveness to both mechanical forces and inflammatory mediators, demonstrating a unique capacity to transduce biomechanical signals into cellular responses that orchestrate periodontal tissue remodeling [20]. This mechanosensitive and immunomodulatory potential underscores their critical role in periodontal homeostasis and regeneration. This review systematically examines the functional contributions of PDLSCs to periodontal tissue remodeling processes.

PDLSCs mediate periodontal tissue remodeling under mechanical stimulation

PDLSCs exhibit distinct mechanobiological properties that facilitate their ability to perceive and transduce mechanical signals into biochemical responses [21]. In experimental settings, mechanical loading is typically modeled using orthodontic tooth movement (OTM) systems, where nickel-titanium coil springs are surgically placed between the maxillary first molar and incisors to apply controlled forces in vivo. Additionally, in vitro studies often utilize compression, tension, vibration, and other forms of force to explore the mechanisms by which PDLSCs modify their functions or interact with other cells under mechanical force stimuli.

Micro-environment changes of PDL tissues under mechanical stimuli

Following mechanical force application in vivo, periodontal tissues undergo a cascade of cellular and molecular responses that drive alveolar bone remodeling and facilitate tooth movement (Fig. 1). This process involves the rapid upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which initiate a transient sterile inflammatory response [22, 23]. Importantly, unlike chronic inflammation, this mechanically-induced inflammatory cascade, characterized by the acute expression of chemokines such as C-C motif chemokine ligand 2, represents a precisely regulated biological process essential for successful OTM [24].

Immune cells serve as critical mediators in the mechanically-induced aseptic inflammatory cascade within periodontal tissues. Macrophages, functioning both as key innate immune effectors and osteoclast precursors, play a central regulatory role in inflammation-driven bone remodeling during OTM. Experimental evidence demonstrates the critical role of M1-polarized macrophages in this process. Macrophage depletion significantly reduces both tooth movement distance and the number of tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP)+ osteoclasts, while the adoptive transfer of M1 macrophages enhances tooth movement distance and promotes alveolar bone remodeling. Mechanistically, mechanical force application promotes the polarization of CD68+ inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)+ M1 macrophages and upregulates associated pro-inflammatory mediators TNF-α and iNOS [25].

The dynamic equilibrium between M1 and M2 macrophage phenotypes critically regulates periodontal tissue responses. Animal studies reveal that prolonged mechanical loading induces localized root resorption accompanied by M1 macrophage accumulation at compression sites. Notably, force cessation triggers a phenotypic switch to CD68+CD163+ M2 macrophages, which is associated with subsequent reparative processes [26]. This biphasic macrophage response highlights their dual role in different phases of OTM.

T cells, as key mediators of adaptive immunity, critically regulate bone remodeling processes during OTM [27, 28]. Experimental evidence demonstrates that T-cell-deficient mice exhibit significantly attenuated OTM distances compared to wild-type controls, while adoptive transfer of allogeneic T lymphocytes restores normal tooth movement capacity, a recovery strongly correlated with elevated expression of Th1 cytokines (TNF-α, IFN-γ) in periodontal tissues [29]. These pro-inflammatory mediators are characteristically upregulated in the mechanically-loaded periodontal ligament, where activated T cells additionally promote osteoclastogenesis through localized receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) production [29]. The γδT cell subset contributes distinctively to early-phase OTM responses. Genetic ablation studies demonstrate that γδT cells are essential for initiating IL-17 A production, recruiting monocytes and neutrophils to pressure sites, and establishing the initial osteoclast population required for bone resorption [30]. These findings collectively establish T lymphocytes as multifunctional regulators of orthodontic bone remodeling through both cytokine-mediated and cell-contact-dependent mechanisms.

The role of B cells in mechanical force-induced bone remodeling remains less characterized compared to other immune cell populations. Current understanding indicates that B cells possess the capacity to secrete key osteoclastogenic regulators, including RANKL and IL-6, implying their probable participation in OTM. However, force-induced alterations in B cell cytokine profiles or their specific contributions to periodontal remodeling remain to be elucidated. Further investigation is warranted to determine whether mechanical loading modulates B cell activation states and subsequent cytokine secretion patterns during bone remodeling processes.

During OTM, distinct tissue remodeling processes occur on the compressive and tension sides of periodontal tissues. On the compressive side, PDLSCs secrete various bioactive cytokines, including macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and RANKL, which orchestrate bone resorption [31]. M-CSF plays a crucial role in promoting the survival and proliferation of macrophage/osteoclast precursor cells while upregulating the expression of receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B (RANK). This enables RANKL to bind to RANK receptors on osteoclast precursor cell membranes [32]. The RANK-RANKL interaction triggers a signaling cascade that initiates osteoclast differentiation and activation.

This process is counterbalanced by osteoprotegerin (OPG), a decoy receptor that competitively inhibits RANK-RANKL binding [33]. The dynamic equilibrium of the RANKL-RANK-OPG axis facilitates the migration of osteoclast precursors to the bone surface, where they fuse to form multinucleated osteoclasts under compressive mechanical forces [34]. These mature osteoclasts subsequently initiate bone resorption through the secretion of various proteolytic enzymes, including MMP-9, TRAP, and cathepsin K (CTSK), thereby enabling tooth displacement in the direction of orthodontic force [23, 35, 36].

Conversely, on the tension side, mechanical stretching of the periodontal ligament enhances vascular perfusion [37] and stimulates bone formation. This process is mediated by various osteoinductive factors such as bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), cytokines, and growth factors, which promote the differentiation of mesenchymal precursor cells into osteoblasts [38]. Upon differentiation, these osteoblasts actively synthesize bone matrix components including type I collagen (COL-I), osteocalcin (OCN), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP), serving as molecular markers of active bone formation [33].

Force-induced changes of PDLSC biological properties influence periodontal tissue remodeling

Mechanical stimulation constitutes a fundamental physiological stimulus for periodontal tissues. As a potent regulator of PDLSCs, biomechanical forces critically modulate their proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, autophagy, and pyroptosis, core cellular processes that collectively drive periodontal and alveolar bone remodeling (Fig. 2).

Changes of PDLSC function under mechanical stimulation in vivo

Under mechanical stimulation in vivo, PDLSCs exhibit dynamic biological responses, including enhanced proliferation, regulated multilineage differentiation, apoptosis modulation, autophagy activation, and pyroptosis induction. These coordinated cellular adaptations collectively maintain periodontal tissue homeostasis and facilitate OTM.

a. Proliferation.

Proliferation serves as a fundamental mechanism by which PDLSCs maintain periodontal tissue homeostasis and respond to mechanical stimuli. Experimental evidence from rat models demonstrates this phenomenon clearly: within 3 days of orthodontic force application, researchers observed a significant increase in Nestin⁺ and PDGFRα⁺ cell populations on both compression and tension sides, indicating mechanical reactivation of endogenous PDLSCs during tooth movement [22]. Supporting these findings, Jin et al. reported a marked elevation in CD146⁺Ki67⁺ PDLSC populations at days 3 and 7 post-force application, providing direct evidence of mechanical force-induced proliferation enhancement in PDLSCs [39].

b. Multidirectional differentiation.

The multilineage differentiation potential of PDLSCs is critically regulated by the specific characteristics of applied mechanical forces, including their magnitude, duration, and loading pattern. Due to the inherent challenges in tracing cellular origins within complex periodontal tissues in vivo (e.g., determining whether osteoblasts derive from PDLSCs), most investigations employ in vitro models to study PDLSC differentiation. Compelling evidence from in vitro studies demonstrates that mechanical stimulation significantly modulates the osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation capacity of rat PDLSCs [39].

c. Apoptosis.

Apoptosis of PDLSCs serves as a critical regulator in both physiological maintenance and pathological progression of periodontal tissues. Mechanical forces exert biphasic, context-dependent effects on this process, as demonstrated in rat OTM models where apoptosis-related markers exhibit temporal and force-dependent variations [40]. Notably, peak expression of pro-apoptotic markers B-cell lymphoma-2-associated X Protein (Bax) and caspase-3 occurs at day 7 post-force application [41], indicating a time-sensitive apoptotic response. Furthermore, the apoptotic response follows a clear force-magnitude gradient. Experimental data reveal that heavy orthodontic forces preferentially upregulate pro-apoptotic Bax while downregulating anti-apoptotic B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL-2), whereas light forces show the opposite pattern [42]. This suggests that orthodontic force-induced apoptosis is more pronounced with heavy force stimuli.

d. Autophagy.

Autophagy, a lysosome-based degradation pathway in PDLSCs, plays a pivotal role in maintaining their biological functions and periodontal tissue homeostasis during OTM. Both mouse and rat OTM models demonstrate mechanical force-induced autophagy activation, particularly on the compressive side of the PDL [43, 44]. Chen et al. [43] provided compelling evidence of compression-side autophagy activation, showing significantly elevated expression of autophagy markers microtubule-associated protein light chain 3-II(LC3-II) and BCL2-interacting protein-associated gene 1(Beclin-1). These findings were further corroborated by Jiang et al., who observed increased LC3 protein expression in compressed PDL tissues in vivo . In vitro studies revealed that compressive loading induces time-dependent expression of autophagy-related proteins LC3 and sequestosome-1(p62) in PDLSCs [45].

e. Pyroptosis.

Pyroptosis, a highly inflammatory form of programmed cell death mediated by Caspase-1 activation, serves dual roles in tissue homeostasis maintenance and inflammatory cascade initiation. Emerging evidence implicates pyroptosis in the pathogenesis of various inflammatory diseases, including arthritis, myocarditis, and bacterial-induced periodontitis. Recent studies reveal that mechanical loading can also activate PDL progenitor cells in vivo, triggering Caspase-1-dependent pyroptosis. The number of Caspase-1+CD90+ cells, GSDMD+CD90+ cells, and IL-1β+CD90+ cells was all increased in the compression side of the periodontal tissues after force loading. Blocking or enhancing the pyroptosis level, as well as using Caspase-1−/− mice, further illustrated that force-induced Caspase-1-dependent pyroptosis in PDL progenitor cells contributes to OTM and alveolar bone remodeling. This mechanoresponsive cell death pathway amplifies local inflammation and promotes osteoclastic bone resorption, thereby creating a permissive environment for OTM [46].

Changes of PDLSC function under mechanical stimulation in vitro

PDLSCs demonstrate distinct biological responses to different mechanical force modalities. Extensive mechanobiological studies have been conducted to characterize PDLSC responses during OTM. In vitro experimental systems typically apply tensile or compressive forces to cultured PDLSCs, accurately replicating the mechanical microenvironment of tension and compression sides during clinical OTM. Furthermore, researchers have investigated additional mechanical stimulation modalities such as vibration and ultrasound therapy (Table 1), expanding our understanding of PDLSC mechanotransduction pathways.

Tension.

Tension.

Tensile forces play a pivotal role in promoting osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, a critical process for periodontal tissue regeneration. Li et al. demonstrated that cyclic tensile stress upregulates key osteogenic markers RUNX2 and ALP [51]. Further supporting this, Sun et al. revealed that mechanical stimulation triggers nitric oxide (NO) production in hPDLSCs, with the PI3K/Akt/iNOS signaling axis serving as a critical regulator of osteogenic differentiation [47]. Mechanical tension induces significant metabolic reprogramming in PDLSCs, marked by upregulated lactate dehydrogenase activity and downregulated mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 4 expression [48]. This proves that PDLSCs shift from mitochondrial-dependent energy metabolism to glycolysis. Notably, tensile stimulation also demonstrates the capacity to drive PDLSC fate determination beyond osteogenesis, with recent evidence showing their transdifferentiation potential into keratinocyte lineages [67]. These findings collectively underscore the exceptional mechanoresponsive plasticity of PDLSCs, enabling their adaptation to diverse mechanical microenvironments.

Tensile forces also significantly enhance PDLSC proliferation, with optimal effects being strain-dependent and influenced by cellular origin. Liu et al. demonstrated that healthy PDLSCs exhibit maximal proliferation and osteogenic potential under 12% static mechanical strain (SMS). In contrast, periodontitis-derived PDLSCs showed peak responsiveness at a lower 8% SMS, indicating greater mechanosensitivity and reduced mechanical tolerance in pathological conditions [11]. Furthermore, the frequency of cyclic tensile loading critically regulates PDLSC differentiation capacity. As loading frequency increases from 0.1 Hz to 0.7 Hz, progressive downregulation occurs in key stem cell markers, suggesting frequency-dependent modulation of stemness properties [49].

Mitochondria serve as central regulators of PDLSC bioenergetics and mechanotransduction. Tensile stimulation induces a metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis in PDLSCs, as evidenced by a significant increase in lactate production within 24 h and a concurrent reduction in reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. This metabolic reprogramming is mediated through force-induced mitochondrial remodeling, characterized by increased mitochondrial fission, inhibited fusion, and impaired aerobic metabolism [48, 68]. Mitophagy, the selective autophagic clearance of damaged mitochondria, plays a critical role in maintaining cellular homeostasis during mechanical stimulation. Pharmacological inhibition of autophagy reduces ALP activity and calcium nodule formation, while autophagy induction enhances these osteogenic markers [50], demonstrating that mitophagy serves as a quality control mechanism to support force-induced differentiation.

b. Compression.

Compression.

Compression exerts a pivotal role in regulating the fate and behavior of PDLSCs. The proliferative response of PDLSCs to compression follows a biphasic pattern dependent on both the magnitude and duration of applied force. Moderate compressive stimulation enhances PDLSC proliferation [39], while excessive mechanical loading exerts inhibitory effects [69, 70]. Compression also induces significant cytoskeletal reorganization in PDLSCs, leading to morphological alterations and transient downregulation of collagen synthesis. Notably, these changes exhibit remarkable reversibility upon force removal, demonstrating the adaptive plasticity of PDLSCs to mechanical challenges [56].

The lineage commitment of PDLSCs under compressive loading demonstrates both temporal- and force magnitude-dependent regulation. Short-term compression (e.g., 100 kPa for 1 h) promotes osteogenic differentiation [57]. In contrast, prolonged compressive stress suppresses osteogenic potential [55], revealing a critical time window for mechano-induced differentiation. Force magnitude similarly dictates PDLSC fate, with lower compressive loads (typically < 50 kPa) maintaining stemness through upregulation of pluripotency markers (NANOG, Octamer-binding transcription factor 4 (OCT4)) [58], while higher loads trigger differentiation programs. Notably, compression-activated TRPV4 in PDLSCs modulates osteoclastogenesis via the RANKL/OPG axis, creating a mechanobiological coupling between PDLSCs and bone remodeling [39].

Static compressive force triggers autophagic activation in PDLSCs through distinct molecular mechanisms. The autophagy marker LC3 demonstrates force-responsive regulation, with Jiang et al. reporting a significant elevation of the LC3II/LC3I ratio under compressive loading [45]. Moreover, compressive force could also induce pyroptosis in human PDL progenitor cells, which influenced osteoclastogenesis.

Mechanical compression significantly enhances exosome biogenesis in PDLSCs and upregulates Annexin A3 (ANXA3) protein expression within these extracellular vesicles (EVs) [60]. These force-stimulated exosomes exhibit potent pro-osteoclastic activity, promoting osteoclast differentiation through paracrine signaling. Interestingly, while modulating osteoclastogenesis, the exosomes maintain neutral effects on the intrinsic osteogenic differentiation capacity of PDLSCs, suggesting selective regulation of bone remodeling processes [60].

Furthermore, compressive force significantly modulates mitophagic activity in PDLSCs, triggering a cascade of molecular and ultrastructural changes. Mechano-stimulation upregulates both protein and mRNA expression levels of key autophagy markers LC3-II/LC3-I and Beclin-1, while ultrastructural analyses demonstrate increased autophagosome formation and accumulation of autophagic vesicles. This facilitates selective clearance of damaged mitochondria, highlighting compression’s role in regulating mitochondrial quality control through autophagy-dependent pathways [61].

c. Vibration.

Vibration.

Low-amplitude, high-frequency (LMHF) vibration induces significant cytoskeletal reorganization in PDLSCs. Comparative analyses reveal that vibrated PDLSCs develop thicker and more prominent F-actin stress fibers compared to static controls, suggesting the actin cytoskeleton serves as a primary mechanosensor that modulates cellular mechanical properties during early osteogenic differentiation [63]. Mechanical vibration elicits a frequency-dependent response in PDLSCs, characterized by reduced proliferative capacity and enhanced osteogenic differentiation with ALP activity peaking at 50 Hz stimulation [62]. Notably, the osteogenic efficacy of mechanical stimulation demonstrates dual dependence on both frequency and amplitude parameters [63], highlighting the importance of precise vibration characteristics in directing PDLSC differentiation.

d. Other forces.

Other forces.

Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) has emerged as a promising non-invasive therapeutic modality for enhancing bone regeneration. In the context of OTM, LIPUS exerts significant bio-modulatory effects on periodontal tissues. Given that LIPUS can facilitate the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs sheets and expedite new bone formation, it holds promise for promoting periodontal tissue repair [64, 71, 72].

Static magnetic fields (SMFs) represent an emerging biophysical modality for modulating inflammatory tissue remodeling, with established clinical applications in arthritis management. In orthodontic contexts, magnetic appliances generate SMFs that enhance alveolar bone remodeling and accelerate tooth movement. Mechanistic studies reveal that SMFs stimulate osteoclastogenesis through force-dependent secretion of IL-6 from PDL cells [66]. This immunomodulatory effect positions SMFs as a potential adjunct for optimizing orthodontic treatment outcomes.

The interactions between PDLSCs and other cells contribute to periodontal tissue remodeling under mechanical stimulation

Mechanically stimulated PDLSCs orchestrate periodontal tissue remodeling through multimodal regulation of key cellular populations. PDLSCs can affect periodontal tissue remodeling by affecting osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and immune cells under mechanical stimulation in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 3).

PDLSCs and immune cells

Macrophages, as primary immune effectors and osteoclast precursors, play pivotal roles in inflammation-mediated bone remodeling during OTM [73, 74]. These plastic cells undergo phenotype polarization in response to microenvironmental cues, acquiring distinct functional capacities [70]. During early OTM, M1-like macrophage accumulation promotes tooth movement through pro-inflammatory mechanisms. Mechanistically, mechanical loading induces PDLSCs to produce hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), driving M1 polarization [75]. This paracrine regulation establishes a critical link between mechanical stimulation and immunomodulation in periodontal remodeling. During the later stages of OTM, macrophages undergo phenotypic switching to M2 polarization within an anti-inflammatory microenvironment. This alternative activation state likely facilitates bone remodeling through tissue repair mechanisms. Mechanistically, force-stimulated PDLSCs enhance both macrophage chemotaxis and osteoclast differentiation potential [76, 77].

While direct evidence of mechanical force-mediated interactions between PDLSCs and adaptive immune cells (T cells and B cells) in bone remodeling remains to be established, their functional interplay likely contributes to periodontal tissue adaptation. Given the well-documented crosstalk between MSCs and lymphocytes in skeletal homeostasis, future investigations should elucidate force-dependent PDLSC-immune cell communication pathways and potential synergistic/antagonistic effects on osteogenesis.

PDLSCs and osteoclasts

PDLSCs residing in inflammatory periodontal microenvironments release various cytokines and chemokines that critically regulate osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption [37]. Under static magnetic fields, PDLSCs exhibit elevated IL-6 expression on compression sides, promoting osteoclast formation [66]. Compressive forces similarly induce PDLSCs to secrete key regulators including RANKL, which mediate the recruitment and activation of osteoclast precursors [33, 39]. These activated precursors subsequently fuse to bone surfaces and initiate resorptive processes [33]. Furthermore, PDLSC can also generate gas molecules. PDLSC-derived H₂S regulates osteoclast activity through modulation of both the RANKL/OPG axis and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 secretion [78, 79].

Mechanical stimulation triggers autophagy in PDLSCs, which promotes M1 macrophage polarization and enhances osteoclast production, thereby contributing to inflammatory bone remodeling [45, 75]. Static compression additionally upregulates the temporal expression of critical osteoclastogenic mediators such as transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily C member 6 (TRPC6) [80]. Furthermore, mechanical forces stimulate PDLSCs to produce exosomes containing elevated levels of ANXA3 protein. Following internalization, these exosomes ultimately drive osteoclast differentiation [60]. Mature osteoclasts then execute bone resorption through the secretion of proteolytic enzymes including MMP-9, TRAP, and CTSK [35, 36].

PDLSCs and osteoblasts

Mechanical strain facilitates functional crosstalk between PDLSCs and osteoblasts, primarily mediated by EVs. In vitro studies demonstrate that PDLSC-derived EVs significantly enhance both the proliferation of MC3T3-E1 osteoprogenitor cells and their osteogenic differentiation under 2000 µε tensile strain. In vivo, systemic administration of PDLSC-EVs accelerates OTM and stimulates tension-side bone formation in mouse models [81].

In summary, PDLSCs play a pivotal role in mechanical force-induced alveolar bone remodeling and tooth movement through their dynamic interactions with immune cells, osteoclasts, and osteoblasts. These cellular interactions coordinate complex biological processes including inflammation modulation, bone resorption, and new bone formation. However, the precise secretory profile of PDLSCs during these interactions, particularly regarding critical mediators like exosomes and other bioactive molecules, remains to be fully elucidated, warranting further investigation to uncover the complete mechanistic picture of PDLSC-mediated alveolar bone remodeling.

The influence of PDLSC on ECM remodeling under mechanical stimuli

The ECM of the PDL provides the essential structural scaffold that mediates cellular and tissue responses during mechanical remodeling. The PDL ECM is primarily composed of COL-I and COL-III, which exhibit distinct functional properties. COL-I, as the predominant and more mature form, confers mechanical strength and maintains tooth stability. In contrast, COL-III demonstrates greater elasticity and serves as a critical stress-absorbing component that mitigates tensile forces during OTM [82].

During OTM, the collagenous ECM undergoes dynamic remodeling in both compressed and tensioned regions of the PDL [83]. On the compression side, mechanical loading induces PDL fiber compression and disorganization, coupled with suppressed COL-I production in PDLSCs [84]. Notably, this suppression is reversible upon force withdrawal during early relapse, a recovery process mediated by transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling [56].

On the tension side during OTM, the PDL exhibits distinct remodeling patterns characterized by collagen fiber stretching and aligned reorganization, which facilitate osteogenic deposition [85]. Transmission electron microscopy analyses reveal that tensile strains applied to the middle third of the distal root PDL significantly increase both collagen fiber density and cellular density [86]. This mechanical loading preferentially stimulates the production of immature COL-III fibers, particularly in apical regions [87]. As the principal cellular constituents of PDL tissue, PDLSCs serve as the major biosynthetic source for these ECM components, dynamically regulating collagen production in response to mechanical cues.

PDLSCs actively participate in ECM remodeling during OTM through the synthesis and secretion of multiple bioactive molecules, including growth factors (e.g., TGF-β), cytokines, and MMPs that collectively regulate tissue reorganization [88]. These mechanoresponsive cells dynamically adjust their secretory profile during OTM, producing a diverse array of signaling molecules such as prostaglandins and specialized ECM-remodeling enzymes that coordinate the complex tissue adaptations required for tooth movement [89, 90]. This multifaceted paracrine activity enables PDLSCs to precisely regulate the balance between ECM degradation and regeneration throughout the remodeling process.

MMPs play a pivotal role in ECM remodeling by mediating both the synthesis and degradation of matrix proteins during various physiological and pathological processes [91]. For example, MMP-1 specifically targets native triple-helical interstitial collagens, initiating critical tissue remodeling cascades [92]. The activity of MMPs is tightly regulated by tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) [93], and the delicate balance between MMPs and TIMPs constitutes a fundamental regulatory mechanism that governs ECM remodeling during OTM, highlighting their essential roles in periodontal tissue adaptation to mechanical forces [94, 95].

Mechanical tension significantly regulates ECM remodeling by upregulating key ECM-related genes in PDLSCs, as demonstrated by Yang et al., who subjected PDLSCs to 12-hour compressive loading followed by RNA sequencing analysis [96]. Their findings revealed that the majority of differentially expressed long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and messenger RNAs (mRNAs) were functionally associated with ECM organization, collagen fibril assembly, and cellular responses to hypoxia, highlighting the crucial role of mechanical forces in reprogramming PDLSC gene expression to coordinate ECM remodeling during OTM [96].

Mechanical transduction mechanisms are involved in PDLSC-mediated periodontal tissue remodeling under mechanical stimuli

PDLSCs possess remarkable mechanotransduction capabilities, efficiently converting physical forces into biochemical signaling cascades. This process relies on a sophisticated network of mechanosensors, including the cytoskeleton that transmits mechanical forces throughout the cell, mechanosensitive ion channels responding to membrane tension, cell junction molecules mediating intercellular force transmission, integrins linking the extracellular matrix to intracellular signaling, and G-protein coupled receptors detecting mechanical stimuli. Together, this integrated mechanosensory system enables PDLSCs to precisely interpret and dynamically respond to their mechanical microenvironment during orthodontic tooth movement (Fig. 4).

Cytoskeleton

The cytoskeleton forms a continuous structural network connecting the nucleus to the cell membrane and plays a pivotal role in mechanotransduction by transmitting mechanical cues within cells [97]. Mechanical stimulation induces dynamic reorganization of the three major cytoskeletal components (actin filaments, microtubules, and intermediate filaments), which undergo complex, force-dependent deformations. These structural changes not only facilitate mechanical signal propagation but also trigger cytokine release during cytoskeletal remodeling [98], thereby serving as a crucial mechanism for converting physical stimuli into biochemical responses in periodontal ligament stem cells.

LMHF vibration significantly alters the cytoskeletal architecture of PDLSCs, particularly through the reorganization of F-actin stress fibers. Compared to static conditions, vibrated PDLSCs exhibit thicker and more distinctly organized F-actin filaments, which directly modulate cellular proliferation and differentiation patterns. These cytoskeletal modifications suggest that force-induced actin remodeling serves as a fundamental mechanotransduction mechanism underlying the observed biological effects of mechanical vibration on PDLSCs [63].

G-protein coupled receptors

G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs), characterized by their seven transmembrane domains and functionally critical intracellular tails, serve as key mechanosensors during OTM. When PDLSCs experience mechanical loading, GPCRs detect these forces and initiate downstream signaling cascades. These receptors form an integral part of the cellular mechanoreceptor system, working in concert with the cytoskeleton to transduce mechanical stimuli into biochemical responses [98].

TRPC6, a widely expressed mechanosensitive nonselective cation channel, acts as a membrane mechanoreceptor that directly participates in PDLSC mechanotransduction. Importantly, TRPC6 has been shown to regulate the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, suggesting its critical involvement in converting mechanical stimuli into biological responses during OTM [80].

Mechanosensitive ion channels

Mechanosensitive ion channels serve as critical mediators of cellular responses to mechanical forces, with transient receptor potential channels representing a prototypical class of mechanotransducers. These channels participate in diverse sensory functions across various tissues and cell types [99]. Notably, TRPV4, a member of the TRPV subfamily, has been identified as a key mechanosensor in PDLSCs. Its activation under mechanical loading modulates PDLSC stemness properties and orchestrates bone remodeling processes during OTM [39].

The alpha-7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7 nAChR), a mechanosensitive channel, represents a unique isoform. Cyclic compressive stress has the ability to activate the canonical Wingless and Int (Wnt) signaling pathway by increasing the expression of α7 nAChR on PDLSCs. As a consequence, this activation promotes osteoclastogenesis [100].

Recent studies have identified a specialized subpopulation of leptin receptor-expressing PDLSCs (Lepr+ PDLSCs) that serve as key mechanosensors in periodontal tissues. The mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 plays a central role in force transduction, converting mechanical stimuli into biological responses. The activation state of these Lepr+ PDLSCs is dynamically regulated by mechanical loading, highlighting their crucial function in periodontal tissue adaptation to orthodontic forces [101].

Integrins

Integrins represent a major class of glycoprotein transmembrane receptors that serve as critical mechanotransducers in PDLSCs. Composed of 18α and 8β subunits that combine to form 24 unique heterodimers [102, 103], these receptors mediate bidirectional signaling between the ECM and cytoskeleton. These heterodimers play a crucial role in mediating cell adhesion to the ECM, and this process can be pivotal in the way PDLSCs respond to mechanical stimuli.

For instance, intermittent compressive force stimulation for 24 h significantly upregulates TGF-β1 expression in PDLSCs, an effect that is markedly attenuated upon αVβ5 integrin knockdown, demonstrating the critical role of this specific integrin heterodimer in compressive force signaling [104]. Conversely, under tensile loading, PDLSCs exhibit activation of low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 6 (LRP6), which promotes F-actin cytoskeletal dynamics and enhances osteogenic differentiation capacity [105]. These findings highlight how PDLSCs utilize different receptor systems to discriminate between compressive and tensile mechanical stimuli, ultimately leading to distinct cellular responses during OTM.

Non-coding RNA

Non-coding RNAs include microRNAs (miRs), circular RNAs (circRs), and lncRNAs. They serve as critical epigenetic regulators of OTM by modulating gene expression at transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels [106]. These regulatory molecules influence various biological processes involved in periodontal remodeling, such as osteoclastogenesis, osteoblast differentiation, and inflammatory responses, through their ability to fine-tune the expression of target genes.

MiRs demonstrate significant mechanosensitivity and serve as key post-transcriptional regulators of bone remodeling processes. Whole-genome microarray analysis was conducted to compare normal and mechanically stretched PDLSCs, and 53 differentially expressed miRs were identified. Further characterization focused on 10 mechanically sensitive candidate miRs and their predicted target genes, revealing a complex regulatory network that mediates the response of PDLSCs to mechanical stimuli [107].

Mechanical loading induces specific miRNA expression changes that regulate PDLSC differentiation. Cyclic stretching significantly downregulates miR-146a and miR-34a expression in PDLSCs, ultimately suppressing PDLSC osteogenic potential [108]. These findings reveal a mechanosensitive miRNA-mRNA regulatory axis that critically controls force-induced osteogenic differentiation in periodontal tissues.

CircRs represent an important class of endogenous regulatory RNAs that play pivotal roles in modulating cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis, through posttranscriptional regulation. As revealed by Wang et al. [109], circRNAs exhibit tension-responsive expression patterns in PDLSCs. Through this mechanism, circRNAs form intricate regulatory networks that control tension-induced osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, highlighting their significance in mechanical force-mediated periodontal tissue remodeling.

LncRs, a class of non-coding transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides in length, play critical regulatory roles in gene expression and cellular differentiation. In PDLSCs, mechanical compression downregulates the lncRNA hedgehog interacting protein antisense RNA 1 (HHIP-AS1), which serves as an important regulator of PDLSC function. HHIP-AS1 enhances PDLSC osteogenic capacity. And conversely, HHIP-AS1 suppresses PDLSC migration and chemotaxis, suggesting its dual role in coordinating differentiation and motility during mechanical stimulation [110]. These findings establish HHIP-AS1 as a key mechanosensitive lncRNA involved in periodontal tissue remodeling under orthodontic forces.

PDLSCs mediate periodontal tissue remodeling under inflammatory conditions

Periodontitis represents a major pathological factor in alveolar bone resorption and adult tooth loss, primarily through its disruptive effects on the periodontal microenvironment (Fig. 5). The inflammatory characteristic of periodontitis impairs normal tissue repair processes by altering endogenous signaling pathways, ultimately contributing to progressive bone loss [111]. Importantly, a dynamic bidirectional interaction exists between PDLSCs and the inflammatory microenvironment during bone regeneration: PDLSCs possess immunomodulatory capabilities that regulate local immune responses, while inflammatory mediators reciprocally influence PDLSC behavior by modulating their proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation potential through multiple signaling pathways [18, 19]. These complex interactions not only deepen our understanding of periodontal pathogenesis but also highlight the significant therapeutic potential of PDLSCs in periodontal regenerative therapies, suggesting dual strategies to either enhance PDLSC function under inflammatory conditions or modify the inflammatory microenvironment to optimize regeneration outcomes.

Micro-environment changes of PDL tissues under inflammation

Under periodontal inflammatory conditions, the PDL microenvironment undergoes significant alterations characterized by the activation of key pro-inflammatory signaling pathways. Multiple transcription factors are upregulated and drive the expression of inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins and cytokines [112–114]. Experimental studies employing specific inhibitors and genetic knockout models have definitively established the critical role of these factors in periodontal disease pathogenesis, demonstrating their ability to modulate inflammatory bone resorption processes [115].

Emerging research has established the critical involvement of host immune cells, particularly neutrophils, in the pathogenesis of periodontal inflammation [116]. While serving as essential first-line defenders against microbial challenge, neutrophils exhibit dual roles in periodontitis by both initiating and propagating inflammatory responses [117]. These immune cells, present in peripheral blood and gingival crevicular fluid, actively synthesize and secrete multiple pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, and PGE2, that collectively amplify inflammatory cascades and promote tissue destruction in periodontal disease [118].

Macrophages play a pivotal role in orchestrating host defense responses during tissue injury and inflammation. In periodontitis, macrophages predominantly differentiate into the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, leading to increased secretion of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and MMPs that collectively promote osteoclast activation and bone resorption [119, 120]. The inflammatory mediators produced by macrophages significantly contribute to bone resorption through both direct and indirect mechanisms [121]. For instance, IL-1α and IL-1β not only promote the adhesion of neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages to endothelial cells but stimulate PGE2 production and enhance osteoclastic bone resorption [122].

T lymphocytes play a critical role in the pathogenesis of periodontitis through multifaceted immunomodulatory mechanisms. These cells secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1 and IL-17A, which stimulate RANKL expression in periodontal ligament cells and osteoblasts, thereby promoting osteoclastogenesis [123]. Activated T cells further exacerbate bone resorption through excessive production of soluble RANKL and direct modulation of OPG expression to favor osteoclast differentiation [124]. In contrast, regulatory T cells (Tregs) exert protective effects by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines such as TGF-β and IL-10, which promote disease resolution and prevent bone destruction [125].

B lymphocytes significantly contribute to periodontitis pathogenesis through their pro-osteoclastogenic activity. By producing RANKL, B cells directly stimulate osteoclast differentiation and promote alveolar bone loss [126]. Importantly, activated T and B lymphocytes represent the predominant cellular sources of RANKL expression in periodontally diseased tissues [127–130], highlighting their central role in inflammatory bone destruction.

PDLSCs possess multilineage differentiation potential, giving rise to periodontal ligament fibroblasts, cementoblasts, and osteoblasts. Beyond their regenerative capacity, PDLSCs actively participate in immunomodulation by secreting mediators that can either amplify or suppress immune responses. Recent studies reveal that PDLSCs exhibit antibacterial properties by secreting LL-37, which inhibits pathogens like Fusobacterium nucleatum and restores a healthy oral microbiome. This dual role in microbial regulation and tissue regeneration highlights their therapeutic potential for periodontitis [131].

The interactions between PDLSCs and immune cells during periodontal tissue remodeling under inflammatory condition

The inflammatory microenvironment comprises diverse immune cell populations participating in both innate and adaptive immune responses, all of which are subject to modulation by PDLSCs [132].

T cells

PDLSCs exhibit low immunogenicity due to their lack of human leukocyte antigen class II DR and T cell co-stimulatory molecules (CD80/CD86) [133], making them promising candidates for immunomodulatory therapies. Studies demonstrate that PDLSC transplantation alleviates disease symptoms in lupus-prone B6/lpr mice [134]. Their immunosuppressive effects are partly mediated through PGE2-dependent mechanisms. Allogeneic PDLSC transplantation upregulates PGE2 to suppress T cell proliferation, thereby mitigating immune rejection while enhancing tissue regeneration [135].

In addition, PDLSCs indirectly regulate T cells via dendritic cells (DCs). Mature DCs stimulate T cell activation, while PDLSCs inhibit DC maturation to indirectly inhibit T cell activation [136]. STRO-1⁺CD146⁺ PDLSCs can weaken DCs’ ability to present lipid antigens to T cells [136]. High-level methylation of the Dickkopf-1(DKK-1) gene promoter can reduce DKK-1 expression and activate the WNT signaling pathway. This promotes the expression of Fas ligand in PDLSCs, which can induce T cell apoptosis by binding to Fas on T cells [137, 138].

Periodontitis is associated with a significant increase in the T-helper cell 17 (Th17)/Treg ratio, reflecting an imbalance between pro-inflammatory and immunoregulatory responses. Th17 cells drive periodontal inflammation through IL-17-mediated recruitment of inflammatory cells and direct stimulation of osteoclastogenesis, while Treg cells maintain immune homeostasis via anti-inflammatory cytokines like TGF-β and IL-10. PDLSCs play a key immunomodulatory role by secreting exosomal miR-155-5p that targets sirtuin-1 in CD4⁺ T cells to regulate Th17/Treg differentiation [139]. Additionally, PDLSCs modulate IL-6 and Indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 expression to promote Treg expansion and suppress Th17 responses [140, 141]. Interestingly, this immunomodulation is bidirectional. Treg cells enhance PDLSC osteogenic potential through Jagged1-Notch2 signaling, demonstrating a reciprocal relationship that influences both immune regulation and tissue regeneration in periodontitis [142].

B cells

PDLSCs exhibit complex immunomodulatory effects on B cell function through multiple mechanisms. Through direct cell-cell interactions and paracrine signaling, PDLSCs significantly inhibit B cell proliferation, chemotaxis, differentiation, and immunoglobulin (IgM, IgG, IgA) production, demonstrating potent suppression of humoral immunity [7]. Programmed death-ligand 1 expressed by PDLSC binds to programmed cell death protein 1 of B cells to inhibit the activation of B cells [143]. Notably, PDLSCs also display context-dependent B cell modulation. While primarily immunosuppressive, they can enhance B cell survival via IL-6 secretion to inhibit apoptosis [143], highlighting the need for further investigation into these dual regulatory effects.

Macrophages

PDLSCs demonstrate significant immunomodulatory capacity by regulating macrophage polarization, thereby promoting an anti-inflammatory microenvironment conducive to periodontal regeneration. Macrophages, existing along a spectrum from M1 to M2 phenotypes, play a pivotal role in periodontal tissue homeostasis and bone remodeling. Experimental evidence from rat models reveals that allogeneic PDLSC transplantation into periodontal defects induces dynamic changes in macrophage populations: while iNOS-positive M1 macrophages initially increase, their numbers subsequently decline. This M1 reduction coincides with significant improvements in bone parameters. Notably, PDLSCs stimulate the appearance of CD163-positive M0 macrophages from day 7 onward and induce CD206-positive M2 macrophage generation by day 21, demonstrating their ability to shift the macrophage phenotype toward tissue-reparative states that support periodontal regeneration [144].

PDLSCs selectively modulate macrophage phenotypes through paracrine signaling, preferentially promoting anti-inflammatory polarization. PDLSC-derived factors significantly upregulate CD163 expression in both M0 and M2 macrophages. This paracrine activity induces a characteristic anti-inflammatory transcriptional profile [144]. Mechanistic studies reveal that PDLSC-conditioned medium potently enhances IL-10 production at both transcriptional and secretory levels when co-cultured with M0 macrophages while increasing TGF-β gene expression. This distinct cytokine modulation profile confirms PDLSCs’ capacity to drive M0-to-M2 macrophage transition [145], establishing their key role in creating a tissue-reparative microenvironment.

PDLSCs can exhibit context-dependent pro-inflammatory effects, mediated through exosomal signaling under inflammatory conditions. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-pretreated PDLSCs release exosomes that promote M1 macrophage polarization, demonstrating their capacity to enhance inflammatory responses when exposed to pathogenic stimuli [146]. Mechanistically, inflammatory PDLSC-derived exosomes containing miR-143-3p drive their polarization toward the M1 phenotype. This finding reveals novel therapeutic targets for periodontitis intervention [147]. Furthermore, LPS-stimulated PDLSCs secrete EVs that activate the NOD-like receptor protein 3 inflammasome pathway in macrophages, further amplifying M1 polarization and inflammatory responses [148]. Notably, PDLSCs also exhibit significant anti-inflammatory properties. Studies have demonstrated that EVs derived from apoptotic cells can improve mitochondrial function in macrophages and facilitate their polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype through the transfer of mitochondrial DNA [149].

In addition, inflammatory macrophages also impact PDLSCs. The inflammatory factors from macrophages directly damage the osteogenesis of PDLSCs, while induced pluripotent stem cell-derived mesenchymal stem cells protect the osteogenic ability of PDLSCS by inhibiting the polarization of M1 macrophages [150].

Neutrophils

PDLSCs secrete IL-6, which promotes neutrophil proliferation while suppressing their apoptosis [151]. Additionally, PDLSCs can recognize Porphyromonas gingivalis and facilitate neutrophil recruitment and activation via paracrine signaling [152]. These mechanisms may enhance neutrophil function as the first line of defense against pathogens during inflammatory responses.

Changes in PDLSC biological characteristics under inflammatory conditions influence periodontal tissue remodeling

The inflammatory microenvironment modulates key biological properties of PDLSCs, including their proliferative capacity, apoptotic activity, osteogenic potential, and mitochondrial function, ultimately influencing bone remodeling processes.

Proliferation

In vitro experiments simulating periodontal inflammation primarily utilized TNF-α and LPS to establish inflammatory conditions. Exposure to low-concentration TNF-α did not significantly alter PDLSC proliferation, whereas high concentrations markedly suppressed it. Similarly, low-dose LPS enhanced hPDLSC proliferation, while higher concentrations exerted inhibitory effects. Notably, the invasive pathogen Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum) not only suppressed PDLSC proliferation but also triggered both apoptosis and ferroptosis [153]. Recent studies demonstrate that Porphyromonas gingivalis outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) significantly inhibit PDLSC proliferation [154].

Apoptosis

LPS stimulation significantly enhances apoptotic activity in PDLSCs, characterized by elevated expression of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and caspase-3, and concomitant reduction in anti-apoptotic BCL-2 levels [155]. F. nucleatum infection induces time-dependent apoptosis in PDLSCs [157], while Porphyromonas gingivalis OMVs promote dose-dependent apoptotic effects [153]. Furthermore, inflammatory conditions trigger necroptosis-mediated PDLSC apoptosis [156].

Oxidative stress represents a key mediator of PDLSC apoptosis, contributing significantly to periodontal tissue degeneration. Cellular defense mechanisms against oxidative stress include Nrf2 pathway activation and lncRNA JHDM1D-AS1 upregulation, both of which demonstrate anti-apoptotic effects in PDLSCs [157, 158]. Mechanistically, H2O2-induced apoptosis occurs through NF-κB pathway activation [159].

Autophagy demonstrates a close interplay with apoptosis in PDLSCs under inflammatory conditions. The inflammatory microenvironment significantly upregulates autophagy-related markers in PDLSCs, as evidenced by TNF-α-stimulated cells showing increased expression of LC3-II and Beclin-1 [160]. These coordinated changes suggest a potential mechanistic link between autophagic activation and apoptotic regulation in periodontal tissue homeostasis.

Osteogenic differentiation

The inflammatory microenvironment significantly compromises the osteogenic capacity of PDLSCs. PDLSCs derived from periodontitis patients demonstrate both diminished osteogenic differentiation potential and aberrant proliferative activity [161]. Mechanistic studies reveal that LPS stimulation leads to the down-regulation of Brain and Muscle ARNT-Like 1, triggering PANoptosis and subsequently inhibiting osteogenic differentiation in PDLSCs [162].

Emerging evidence suggests that restoration of osteogenic potential involves multiple molecular pathways. Healthy hPDLSC-derived exosomes can restore osteogenic function under inflammatory conditions by suppressing excessive canonical Wnt signaling [163]. LPS-pretreated dental follicle stem cells’ exosomes can alleviate oxidative stress, inflammation, and promote osteogenesis [164]. They provide a theoretical basis for the treatment of inflammatory bone defects with stem cell exosomes. In contrast, Porphyromonas gingivalis OMVs inhibit osteogenesis through Endoplasmic Reticulum stress induction and PERK pathway activation [154]. Interestingly, inflamed DPSC-derived EVs enhance osteogenic differentiation via BMP signaling [165], suggesting a complex interplay between vesicular cargoes and osteogenic regulation in periodontal tissue homeostasis. Certain drugs also affect osteogenic differentiation. Azithromycin, Proanthocyanidins, gallic acid and aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 enhance osteogenic differentiation [166–169]. These findings highlight promising therapeutic candidates for periodontal regeneration, warranting further investigation into their in vivo delivery systems and efficacy.

Mitochondrial changes

Mitochondria serve as central regulators of metabolic and differentiation processes in PDLSCs. Under inflammatory conditions, PDLSCs exhibit significantly impaired cellular respiration and reduced mitochondrial ATP production, resulting in structural and functional mitochondrial damage. The mitophagic flux, a critical quality control mechanism for mitochondrial homeostasis, becomes dysregulated during periodontitis progression [170]. Recent studies demonstrate that high-glucose inflammatory microenvironments induce multiple pathological changes in PDLSCs [171]. Furthermore, the lncRNA NR_045147 has been identified as a negative regulator of mitochondrial function, thereby compromising PDLSC osteogenic differentiation capacity [172]. Importantly, intact mitochondrial function is equally essential for cementoblast differentiation of PDLSCs, highlighting its broad role in periodontal tissue regeneration [173].

Reduced Leucine-rich pentatricopeptide repeat-containing protein in PDLSCs promotes cellular senescence through mitochondrial fragmentation and decreased ATP production. Importantly, Sirtuin 3 counteracts this process, suggesting its therapeutic potential for diabetes-associated periodontitis [174]. Furthermore, capsaicin has been shown to protect PDLSCs by restoring mitochondrial morphology damaged by Porphyromonas gingivalis and reducing intracellular ROS levels [175]. Additionally, researchers have developed an innovative antioxidative polymersome containing ginsenoside Rd that effectively enhances mitophagic flux in PDLSCs under oxidative stress conditions, thereby restoring mitochondrial homeostasis [170]. These findings highlight promising therapeutic approaches targeting mitochondrial dysfunction to improve PDLSC function in periodontal diseases.

Inflammation microenvironment influences osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs via various signaling pathways

The osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs is coordinately regulated by multiple signaling pathways that are profoundly affected by inflammatory microenvironments, leading to impaired osteogenic potential and consequent alveolar bone loss. In addition to the major pathways as follows, Notch signaling, BMP-Smad cascade, and PERK-mediated stress response pathways all significantly contribute to the dysregulation of PDLSC osteogenic differentiation under inflammatory conditions (Fig. 6). These pathways interact in a complex network that responds to inflammatory stimuli by altering key cellular processes, ultimately compromising the bone regenerative capacity of PDLSCs. The identification of these pathway alterations provides crucial insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying periodontitis-associated bone loss and reveals potential therapeutic targets for periodontal tissue regeneration.

Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

The Wnt/β-catenin pathway serves as a classical yet complex regulator of PDLSC osteogenic differentiation, exhibiting context-dependent dual effects. Mechanistically, β-catenin (a cadherin complex component) accumulates in the cytoplasm following Glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK-3β) phosphorylation and inactivation, subsequently translocating to the nucleus where it binds to T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer factor (TCF/LEF) transcription factors to activate osteogenic gene expression. This pathway demonstrates pro-osteogenic activity in certain contexts, as evidenced by LPS enhancement of PDLSC differentiation through transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding Motif (TAZ) nuclear translocation and pathway activation [176]. Conversely, Wnt/β-catenin signaling can also inhibit osteogenesis, demonstrated by the suppressive effects of GSK3β inhibitors (LiCl, Wnt3A) and the Wnt antagonist DKK-1 on PDLSC differentiation [177, 178]. Current models suggest this pathway may exert stage-specific regulation, promoting early differentiation while inhibiting later mineralization stages [179], highlighting its complex temporal regulation of periodontal tissue regeneration.

Wnt/ Ca2+ signaling pathway

In inflammatory conditions, the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs is coordinately regulated through a dynamic balance between Wnt/β-catenin and Wnt/Ca²⁺ signaling pathways [180]. The Wnt/Ca²⁺ pathway, activated by Wnt5a and Wnt11, initiates a cascade through G-protein coupled activation of phospholipase C (PLC), leading to Ca²⁺ release and subsequent activation of protein kinase C (PKC) and calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) [181]. This pathway promotes PDLSC osteogenic differentiation [182], while simultaneously suppressing Wnt/β-catenin signaling through CaMKII and Nemo-like kinase (NLK)-mediated phosphorylation of TCF/LEF transcription factors, thereby inhibiting β-catenin-DNA binding [183]. Conversely, elevated β-catenin activity under inflammatory conditions can reciprocally inhibit the Wnt/Ca²⁺ pathway, creating a regulatory circuit that modulates osteogenic potential [180]. This intricate cross-regulation highlights the complex interplay between these pathways in determining PDLSC differentiation fate during inflammation.

NF-κB signaling pathway

The inflammatory microenvironment significantly suppresses PDLSC osteogenic differentiation through NF-κB pathway activation. Key inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) initiate this process by stimulating the IκB Kinase (IKK) complex, which phosphorylates IκBα, leading to its proteasomal degradation [184]. Notably, F. nucleatum, a keystone periodontal pathogen, exacerbates this pathway through its virulence factor FadA. The surface adhesin FadA binds host phosphatidylethanolamine binding protein 1 (PEBP1), triggering IKK-dependent NF-κB activation [153]. This persistent inflammatory signaling creates a hostile microenvironment that disrupts the transcriptional programs essential for PDLSC-mediated bone regeneration.

MAPK signaling pathway

The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family, comprising extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2), p38, c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), and big mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 subfamilies, plays a critical role in regulating PDLSC osteogenic differentiation under inflammatory conditions. The nuclear adhesin FadA from F. nucleatum binds PEBP1, activating the Raf1-ERK1/2 pathway to suppress osteogenesis [153]. Meanwhile, p38 MAPK exacerbates inflammation by upregulating IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, RANKL, and Cyclooxygenase-2, while facilitating nuclear translocation of inflammatory mediators that contribute to periodontal tissue damage [185]. Additionally, the bacterial chaperonin GroEL promotes p-JNK nuclear accumulation in hPDLSCs, further inhibiting osteogenic differentiation [186]. Notably, ERK5 signaling is essential for BMP9-induced osteogenesis, as its blockade disrupts Ad-BMP9-stimulated differentiation [186]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MAPK pathways act as key negative regulators of PDLSC osteogenic potential in inflammatory microenvironments.

The influence of mechanical force and inflammatory environment with the aging of PDLSCs

PDLSCs can be successfully isolated from donors across different age groups; however, their MSC characteristics exhibit significant age-dependent alterations. Studies demonstrate that aging leads to progressive declines in PDLSC proliferation, migratory capacity, and multi-lineage differentiation potential [187]. These cellular changes contribute to the clinical challenges observed in adult orthodontic treatment, where aged hPDLSCs exhibit delayed osteogenic capacity that compromises tooth movement efficiency. Notably, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a key regulator of stem cell differentiation and survival, shows decreased secretion with advancing age. Experimental evidence reveals that exogenous BDNF administration effectively mitigates hPDLSC senescence, while simultaneously enhancing osteogenic differentiation capacity [188]. These findings suggest that BDNF represents a promising therapeutic target for improving orthodontic outcomes in adult patients by rejuvenating the osteogenic potential of aged PDLSCs.

During periodontal tissue repair, aged PDLSCs demonstrate significantly impaired osteogenic differentiation capacity, failing to generate adequate osteoblasts for proper alveolar bone regeneration. Compounding this deficiency, senescent PDLSCs secrete pro-inflammatory senescence-associated secretory phenotype factors (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α) that activate destructive immune responses, particularly through IL-6/TNF-α-mediated NF-κB pathway activation, which perpetuates inflammatory cascades and further damages periodontal tissues [189]. Fortunately, recent advances identify promising pharmacological interventions: resveratrol attenuates cellular senescence by SIRT1 pathway-mediated ROS reduction [190], while curcumin enhances cellular antioxidant capacity and suppresses inflammation through Nrf2 pathway activation [191]. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting oxidative stress and inflammation to restore the regenerative capacity of aged PDLSCs in periodontal repair.

Summary

This review systematically examines the pivotal role of PDLSCs in orchestrating periodontal tissue remodeling under both mechanical and inflammatory conditions. First, we presented the fundamental biological characteristics of PDLSCs and their crucial contributions to periodontal development. Regarding mechanical stimulation, we detailed how PDLSCs mediate periodontal tissue remodeling through intrinsic mechanoadaptation by changing their own properties/functions or extrinsic paracrine communication with neighboring cells and ECM through inflammatory mediators, gaseous molecules, autophagy-related signals, pyroptosis-related signals, etc. Under pathological inflammatory conditions, periodontitis-induced microenvironmental disruption leads to significant functional impairment of PDLSCs. Pro-inflammatory transcription factors and infiltrating immune cells collaboratively promote alveolar bone resorption, while PDLSCs engage in bidirectional crosstalk with immune cells to modulate local inflammatory responses. The inflammatory milieu further impacts PDLSC fate by dysregulating multiple signaling pathways governing proliferation, apoptosis, and osteogenic differentiation.

These findings not only deepen our understanding of periodontal homeostasis and pathology but also highlight PDLSCs as promising therapeutic targets for periodontal regeneration. While significant progress has been made in understanding PDLSC biology, several critical knowledge gaps remain that warrant further investigation. First, the dynamic changes in the immune microenvironment during mechanical stimulation and periodontal inflammation require deeper exploration, particularly regarding how PDLSCs interact with various immune cell populations to coordinate tissue remodeling. Second, the role of PDLSC-derived EVs (e.g., exosomes) in cell-cell communication and their functional impact on periodontal homeostasis remains poorly characterized. Most intriguingly, the metabolic reprogramming of PDLSCs, particularly the interplay between mitochondrial function and glycolytic metabolism, represents a crucial but understudied regulatory axis. During mechanical loading, shifts in this metabolic balance likely influence cellular energy homeostasis, mechanotransduction, and paracrine signaling. Similarly, under inflammatory conditions, metabolic alterations may determine PDLSC responses to cytokines and their participation in bone resorption/repair processes. Elucidating these integrated mechanisms could uncover novel therapeutic targets for precisely controlling periodontal tissue remodeling in both orthodontic and periodontitis contexts.
