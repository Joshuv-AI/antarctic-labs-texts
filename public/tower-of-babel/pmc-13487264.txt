Turning routine chest CT into a cardiovascular risk test: weston score–based CAC in Hawaiʻi
Authors: Narathorn Kulthamrongsri, Sean Choi, Jennifer Lee, Steven Azuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Coronary artery calcium (CAC) is a robust predictor of cardiovascular risk and is recommended by contemporary prevention guidelines. However, dedicated Agatston scoring is underutilized because of cost and limited availability. Visual assessment using the Weston Score (WS) on routine non-contrast chest CT (NCCT) offers a practical alternative, although data in Asian populations remain limited.Methods: We retrospectively reviewed 1107 consecutive NCCT studies performed in 2024. After exclusions, 842 patients were included. WS was visually assessed and converted to Agatston-equivalent CAC scores using a validated method. MESA reference data were used to estimate age-, sex-, and race-adjusted CAC percentiles. Interobserver agreement among a resident physician, cardiologist, and medical student was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and Bland–Altman analyses.Results: Mean age was 70.8 ± 10.2 years, 51% were female, and 88.5% were Asian. Agatston-equivalent CAC scores were 0 in 17.0%, 1–99 in 20.9%, 100–399 in 34.0%, 400–999 in 17.7%, and > 1000 in 10.5%. Among patients with percentile estimates (n = 830), 52.2% were in the 76th–100th percentile. Interobserver agreement was excellent for WS (ICC 0.88), Agatston-equivalent scores (ICC 0.84), and CAC percentiles (ICC 0.90), with minimal systematic bias.Conclusions: Visual WS assessment on routine NCCT provides reproducible estimation of CAC burden and MESA percentiles, supporting opportunistic CAC reporting to improve cardiovascular risk stratification in multiethnic populations.

Introduction

Coronary artery calcium scoring (CACs) is a robust, noninvasive method for cardiovascular risk assessment [1], [2], [3], [4], [5] and is recommended by American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association consensus statements. [6], [7], [8] The Agatston method is widely used in clinical practice [9], [10], [11], but its routine use may be limited by cost, additional radiation exposure, time, and potential downstream testing that may not benefit all patients.

With the increasing use of non-contrast chest computed tomography (NCCTC) for various clinical indications, visual assessment of CAC using the Weston score (WS) has emerged as a practical alternative and correlates well with the Agatston score. [12], [13], [14] CAC identified on routine NCCTC can be readily interpreted by non-specialist readers to refine cardiovascular risk stratification and guide statin therapy.

However, the applicability of the Weston score (WS) in Hawai‘i's uniquely multiethnic population, particularly among Asian and Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander groups who remain underrepresented in coronary artery calcium research, has not been well established. Accordingly, we sought to evaluate the clinical utility of the Weston score among patients undergoing non-contrast chest CT in Hawai‘i.

Methods

From January 1 to December 31, 2024, a total of 1107 NCCTC studies were reviewed. WS were visually estimated and subsequently converted to Agatston-equivalent values using the method described by Chiles et al. [14] Of these, 265 studies were excluded based on predefined criteria. Exclusion criteria included: 1. duplicate examinations from the same patient, with only the earliest eligible study retained; 2. missing or unreported race/ethnicity, which precluded accurate percentile assignment; 3. age outside the validated reference range (patients younger than 30 years or older than 84 years at the time of imaging), consistent with the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) cohort; 4. incomplete clinical or demographic data required for risk stratification; and 5. technically limited or nondiagnostic CT scans that precluded reliable coronary calcium assessment (e.g., severe motion artifact, poor image quality, or incomplete cardiac visualization).

MESA study data were used to estimate coronary artery calcium percentiles based on age, sex, and self-reported race. Individuals categorized as Asian, including East and Southeast Asian populations, were mapped to the Chinese race category within the MESA dataset for percentile estimation. Here, we report preliminary findings based on analyses of the first six months of included studies.

Results

A total of 842 patients were included. Women comprised 51% of the cohort, with a mean age of 70.8 ± 10.2 years. The population was predominantly Asian (88.5%), followed by White (10.2%), Black (0.5%), and other races (0.4%).

Based on CAC equivalents, 143 patients (17.0%) had a score of 0, 176 (20.9%) had scores 1–99, 286 (34.0%) had scores 100–399, 149 (17.7%) had scores 400–999, and 88 (10.5%) had scores >1000. Patients with zero scores were younger, with a mean age of 62.4 ± 12.8 years. For CAC percentiles (n = 830), 144 patients (17.4%) were in the 0–25th percentile, 54 (6.5%) in the 26–50th percentile, 199 (24.0%) in the 51–75th percentile, and 433 (52.2%) in the 76–100th percentile, indicating that over half of the cohort fell within the highest percentile category. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of a predominantly Asian population with a broad distribution of CAC burden, the Weston score (WS) demonstrated good-to-excellent interobserver reliability, comparable to Agatston-equivalent scoring. ICC values were high for WS (0.88, 95% CI 0.87–0.89) and Agatston-equivalent estimates (0.84, 95% CI 0.82–0.85), while CAC percentiles showed excellent agreement (ICC 0.90, 95% CI 0.89–0.91). Bland–Altman analysis revealed minimal systematic bias and narrow limits of agreement, supporting measurement consistency across readers.

These findings are clinically meaningful because the Agatston score is a well-established independent predictor of cardiovascular events. [5] Moreover, the ACC/AHA 2018 cholesterol guideline formally incorporated CAC as a risk modifier to refine statin decision-making, including the “power of zero,” whereby a CAC score of 0 identifies individuals at very low short-term risk. [15] Landmark data from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) demonstrated that adding CAC to traditional risk equations improved net reclassification by approximately 23–25%, with more than half of clinically intermediate-risk individuals reclassified either upward or downward. [5], [16] Similarly, the PROMISE Trial showed that incorporation of CAC substantially altered risk categorization in symptomatic patients compared with clinical risk scores alone. [17]

Applying this framework to our cohort of 955 unique patients, a substantial proportion would likely be reclassified if CAC were integrated with Pooled Cohort Equation estimates. Based on MESA benchmarks, approximately 20–25% (≈220 patients) would be expected to change risk category. In our dataset, 130 patients had CAC = 0 and would be candidates for downward reclassification if initially deemed intermediate risk, consistent with the “power of zero.” Conversely, 428 patients (52% of those with percentile data) were > 75th percentile, and 523 patients had Agatston-equivalent scores ≥100, suggesting a particularly high burden of subclinical atherosclerosis and a strong likelihood of upward reclassification to a high-risk category warranting aggressive preventive therapy. Given this high prevalence of elevated percentiles, the magnitude of upward reclassification in our cohort may exceed that observed in general-population MESA samples.

Prior studies have demonstrated strong concordance between gated and non-gated Agatston scores [18], [19], [20], and Shemesh et al. showed that even modest CAC (≥4) significantly predicted cardiovascular death. [13] McClelland et al. established age-, sex-, and race/ethnicity-specific CAC percentiles from MESA that are widely used for risk interpretation, although Asian subgroups were underrepresented. [21]

Our findings extend the literature by demonstrating that Agatston-equivalent estimates and CAC percentiles can be reliably derived using the Weston scoring system in a predominantly Asian and Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander population, with good-to-excellent agreement even among non-specialist readers. Beyond reproducibility, the potential for substantial risk reclassification underscores the clinical impact of opportunistic CAC assessment from routine non-contrast chest CT.

The high prevalence of moderate-to-severe CAC may have inflated ICC estimates due to increase between-subject variability, and the retrospective, single-center design may limit generalizability. Nonetheless, the consistent reproducibility across readers, combined with the strong evidence for CAC-driven risk reclassification, supports broader integration of visual CAC scoring into routine clinical practice, particularly in underrepresented multiethnic populations with limited access to dedicated CAC imaging.

Conclusion

Visual CAC assessment using the Weston Score on routine NCCT demonstrated excellent reproducibility and enabled reliable estimation of Agatston-equivalent scores and MESA-based CAC percentiles in a predominantly Asian population. More than half of patients were classified within the highest CAC percentile category, highlighting a substantial burden of occult atherosclerosis. These findings support opportunistic CAC reporting from routine chest CT as a scalable strategy to enhance cardiovascular risk stratification and preventive care in underrepresented multiethnic populations.
