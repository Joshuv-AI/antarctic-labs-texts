Muonium Spectroscopy as a Quantum Sensor for Ultralight Axion Dark Matter
Feng Fang, Kim Siang Khaw, Ce Zhang, Qiaoli Yang, Liangwen Chen, Jie Yang, Lei Yang, Zhiyu Sun

Abstract
High intensity muon beams open new avenues for exploring physics beyond the Standard Model. In this paper, we propose a muonium-based experiment to search for ultralight axion dark matter through resonant quantum transitions between hyperfine states. Combining theoretical calculation with simulation results, we show that a muon beam with a realistic intensity of 10 5 / s 10^{5}\rm/s in the 𝒪 ⁡ ( 10 ​ keV ) \mathcal{O}(10~\text{keV}) energy range could improve constraints on the axion-muon coupling by up to several orders of magnitude compared with existing muon g − 2 g-2 limit, over the axion mass range 10 − 15 10^{-15} eV − 10 − 5 -10^{-5} eV. These results establish muonium spectroscopy currently under construction in Huizhou, as a powerful new probe of physics beyond the Standard Model.



Introduction
—Astrophysical and cosmological observations indicate that approximately 27% the universe’s total energy density is in the form of dark matter. Although the exact nature of dark matter particles remains little known, observations and theoretical considerations suggest that they are not composed of Standard Model (SM) particles. Dark matter particles are generally believed to be weakly interacting and non-relativistic. Numerous theoretical models and experimental searches to address dark matter mystery are in progress.

The leading dark matter candidates are weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) and axion/axion-like particles (ALPs), which are ultra light pseudoscalar particles. The coupling strength of ALPs to SM particles is primarily determined by their decay constant,
f_{a}
. ALPs could have been produced in the early universe and may constitute a substantial fraction of dark matter. Their extremly weak couplings make them challenging to detect
Marsh 2016
;
Sikivie 2021
;
Gelmini 2017
, but successful detection would provide deep insights into the physics beyond Standard Model.

Muonium, a hydrogen-like bound state of a positive muon and an electron, provides an ideal purely leptonic system for precision tests of physics beyond the SM
Blumer et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Tanaka et al. 2021
;
Stadnik 2023
. Unlike hadronic systems, its simple two-body structure avoids complications arising from strong interaction effects, enabling highly precise theoretical predictions and cleaner interpretation of experimental results. The exceptional precision achievable in muonium spectroscopy offers great potential for probing subtle new physics effects
Aiba et al. 2021
. Furthermore, the binary nature of muonium spin states provides a distinct advantage: the ability to perform rapid spin switching creates a quasi-digital measurement scheme that can dramatically suppress systematic backgrounds through clear state discrimination. Importantly, inducing quantum transitions in this scenario does not require assuming coherence of the dark matter field, distinguishing this approach from many other dark matter search strategies. Given the uncertainties in dark matter distribution, this feature, similar to the approach in
Yang and Dong 2023
, may prove important advantageous.

High-intensity muon beams required for muonium production are typically generated at high-energy proton accelerator facilities through pion decay. Several high-intensity, low-energy muon sources are currently operational worldwide, including ISIS, J-PARC, PSI, TRIUMF, and Fermilab
Miyake et al. 2010
;
Thomason 2019
;
Maso et al. 2023
;
Marshall 1992
;
Stratakis et al. 2017
. In addition, several next-generation muon facilities are under development
Cai et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Bao et al. 2023
;
Kim 2020
;
Xu et al. 2025
, which will further expand the opportunities for precision muonium experiments.

In this work, we present a comprehensive study of muonium as a precision probe of ALPs and propose a experimental design optimized to measure ALP-induced muonium transitions. While axion–electron and axion–photon couplings are already tightly constrained, the axion–muon coupling remains largely unexplored due to the muon’s short lifetime and experimental challenges. The most stringent laboratory limits currently come from measurements of the muon
g-2
anomaly, with recent updates yielding even stronger constraints
Athron et al. 2025
;
Wu and Yan 2025
. Our results show that precision muonium spectroscopy can achieve unprecedented sensitivity to ALP–muon couplings, probing regions of parameter space inaccessible to conventional approaches and establishing this method as a powerful new paradigm for exploring physics beyond the SM.

Experimental apparatus
—The experimental approach involves three critical stages: muon beam preparation, muonium formation, and detection of ALP-mediated transitions, as shown in Fig.
1
. The surface muons are moderated by cryogenic target before entering the experimental terminal for scientific research. Firstly, the muon beam passes through a carbon foil. The secondary electrons are guided by an electric field and detected by the detector
D_{1}
. Meanwhile, the detector
D_{3}
is triggered and the formed muonium atoms inherit the kinetic energy of muons. The muoniums then enter the multipass cavity, in which
other quantum states at 1S is reserved but the quantum state of
|F=1,\,m_{F}=1\rangle
is excited to a Rydberg state. Immediately the Rydberg atoms enter the region
E_{1}
of deflection electrodes and are ionized by electric field, while the muoniums at other states keep forward. All muons will be deflected by the electric field and collected by the detector
D_{2}
. In the scan magnet region, a magnetic shield is present and a magnetic field on the level of
10^{-9}\ T
can be reached. Thus the energy splitting between the states of
|1,\,0\rangle
and
|1,\,1\rangle
due to Zeeman effect can reach
10^{-13}\ \text{eV}
. When the energy splitting matches the energy of an axion, the atoms may be excited to the quantum state of
|1,\,1\rangle
. Finally, the excited atoms are ionized by the second set of multipass cavity and deflection electrodes and detected by a specific region of the detector
D_{3}
.

The positive muon beams are readily available at major muon facilities worldwide. At present, the most intense muon beam is on the level of
10^{8}\mu^{+}/s

In the multipass cavity, the atoms are resonant excited to a Rydberg state by three photons. The

Conclusions
—We have proposed and simulated a novel muonium-based experiment to search for ultralight axion-like particles through quantum transitions. Using the Geant4 simulation framework, we validated the feasibility of the design, demonstrated effective background suppression, and identified optimal beam and field parameters. For a realistic muon flux of
10^{5}
/s and benchmark assumptions
m_{a}=10^{-12}\ \rm eV
,
f_{a}=10^{3}\ \rm GeV
, the setup achieves a projected sensitivity of
\mathcal{O}(10^{-1})
events per hour, with maximal performance at
E_{\mu}\sim 10
keV and
L=1
m. The combination of dual electric-field deflection, laser ionization, and selective MCP detection enables a essentially background-free measurement. With the advent of next-generation high-intensity muon sources (such as HiMB, CiADS), this approach can improve existing bounds on ALP–muon couplings by several orders of magnitude. This work thus opens a new window on the ALP–muon couplings, a sector that has been largely inaccessible to colliders and precision muon experiments due to the pseudoscalar nature of the interaction. In the future, muonium spectroscopy promises to be a powerful probe of physics beyond the Standard Model.
