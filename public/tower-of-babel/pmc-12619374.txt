Physical and mental health factors associated with aggression in the South Korean general population: a nationwide survey
Authors: Bokyung Jo, Yeonjung Lim, Eunseo Kim, Juhee Cho, Danbee Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent reports have brought to our attention a growing number of incidents involving aggression among individuals. These alarming statistics highlight the urgent need to investigate and address the factors contributing to aggression. Previous research has predominantly focused on specific groups, such as children, adolescents, or college students, limiting understanding of the primary factors within each age group. The primary objective of this study is to bridge this gap by gaining a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing aggression across various adult age groups, ranging from young adults to older individuals in physical, psychological, and social domains.MethodsThis study comprehensively investigated demographic, physical, psychological, and social factors associated with perpetration of aggression in the general South Korean population. A national cross-sectional survey was conducted from December 2021 to January 2022, and representative participants aged 18–85 years from all 17 South Korean provinces were recruited. Higher aggression was defined as the highest 10 percentiles of the aggression score measured using the Aggression Questionnaire. The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) depression, anxiety, physical function, and pain interference subscales assessed psychological and physical traits of the participants. We performed multivariable logistic regression analyses to estimate adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between higher aggression levels and various demographic, physical, and psychological factors. All models were adjusted for age, sex, marital status, physical function, pain interference, comorbidities, physical disability, anxiety, depression, and psychiatric medication use.ResultsIn total, 2,699 participants completed the survey, with a mean aggression score of 52.20. Demographically, younger age (adjusted prevalence ratio [aPR] 12.61; 95% CI 1.18–4.02) was associated with increased aggression. Severe limitations in physical function (aPR 2.06; 95% CI 1.35–2.78), pain interference (aPR 2.24; 95% CI 1.60–2.88), and a higher level of anxiety (aPR 2.24; 95% CI 1.57–2.89) were also significant factors. Especially when compared to those with the lowest levels of depression, participants with moderate and severe depression had a higher risk of aggression (aPR 2.01; 95% CI 1.38–2.63).ConclusionYoung age, impaired physical function, pain interference, depression, and anxiety were identified as risk factors for aggression.

Background

Aggression is defined as the feeling of anger associated with hostile or violent behavior that may result in harm or injury to another person [1, 2]. It encompasses a range of actions including verbal hostility, physical violence, and property damage, and is considered a major public health concern due to its contribution to injury, disability, and premature death. Globally, interpersonal violence and aggressive behavior have emerged as pressing social and mental health issues, particularly among individuals aged 15 to 44 years [3]. Aggression is not limited to criminal contexts, and it may also manifest in everyday interpersonal settings, such as family, romantic, or workplace relationships [4]. Importantly, nonfatal aggressive behaviors such as sexual abuse or dating violence have resulted in a wide variety of physical, psychological, and sexual problems in many people [5]. Given that aggression is a common and costly mental health problem [6] it is essential to develop effective interventions to manage aggression in everyday life in the general population.

There is a lack of information regarding the prevalence and predisposing factors of this condition among the general population. Previous studies have been conducted on small and unrepresentative samples, including college students [7], wrestlers [8], intimate partners [9], or other limited populations [10]. Few studies have explored a wide range of associated factors across the full adult age spectrum using nationally representative data. Furthermore, according to the General Aggression Model (GAM), aggression is associated with various social, cognitive, developmental, and biological factors [11]. Nonetheless, previous studies have typically examined only one or two domain at a time, such as age [12, 13], sex [10], chronic pain [14], low levels of physical activity [14, 15], depression, anxiety [16–20], and marital status [21]. As a result, the current evidence base remains limited in providing an integrative understanding of the multifactorial contributors to aggression.

Given that aggression can occur in everyday interpersonal contexts and is shaped by diverse psychosocial and health-related factors, identifying its key correlates in the general population is essential. Therefore, this study aimed to comprehensively understand the factors influencing perpetration of aggression across various adult age groups, ranging from young adults to older individuals in the general population using nationally representative survey data.

Methods

Study design and participants

This national cross-sectional survey was conducted between December 2021 and January 2022, during the COVID-19 pandemic, a period characterized by heightened psychological stress, social distancing measures, and limited in-person interactions in Korea. A proportionately stratified sampling method was used to ensure representative sampling of 2.5% of the Korean population. The study included participants aged 18–85 years and was conducted across all 17 provinces in South Korea. Participants were recruited through a professional survey agency using stratified sampling based on national demographic distributions of age, sex, and region. Recruitment was conducted primarily through an existing population panel managed by the agency. Among those contacted for this study, approximately 80% agreed to participate. Because the panel itself was constructed using probability-based sampling to represent the Korean population, the characteristics of respondents and non-respondents are expected to be comparable. A total of 2,699 individuals completed the survey, exceeding the initial target of 2,500. Each participant received a 5,000 KRW (approximately 5 USD) voucher as compensation. As this was a nationally representative survey, no additional exclusion criteria were applied beyond the initial sampling frame. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Samsung Medical Center (IRB number: 2021-03-005).

Measurement

The Korean version of the Aggression Questionnaire (AQ) was used to assess perpetration of aggression [22]. The AQ comprises subscales of physical aggression, verbal aggression, anger, and hostility. These subscales evaluate the behavioral aspects of physical and verbal aggression, cognitive dimensions through measures of hostility, and emotional dimensions through the measurement of anger. The questionnaire comprises 27 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (extremely uncharacteristic of me) to 5 (extremely characteristic of me), reflecting how well each statement described the respondent [22]. The Cronbach’s alpha values for Korean version of AQ was 0.86 [22].

Based on the GAM, which posits that aggressive behavior results from interactions between situational inputs and individual traits [11], we selected health-related variables that are known to influence emotional regulation and behavioral responses. Impaired physical function and chronic pain have been linked to heightened irritability and frustration, which may reduce an individual’s threshold for aggressive reactions. Similarly, psychological factors such as anxiety and depression are associated with impaired impulse control and heightened threat perception, both of which may contribute to reactive aggression. These domains were therefore selected as key predictors to capture the multifactorial underpinnings of aggression in the general population. Sociodemographic characteristics, such as age (≤ 29: Young adults, 30–49: Middle, 50–64: Senior and ≥ 65: Older adults), sex, residential area (Seoul, Gyeonggi-do, and Incheon; Non-metropolitan area: Chungcheong-do, Gangwon-do, Jeolla-do, Gyeongsang-do, and Jeju-do), marital status, employment status, educational level, and annual total household income over the past year, including wages, real estate income, financial instrument income, government subsidies and pensions, allowances from relatives or children, and jobs with heavy manual labor, were gathered through self-reported questionnaires.

Physical factors included physical function, pain interference, comorbidities, and physical disability. Comorbidities were assessed by using medication-related questionnaires. Comorbidities were defined as the presence of physician-diagnosed physical health conditions including hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, cancer, consistent with prior studies. Physical function was assessed using the Korean version of the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Physical Function short form v2.0 (10a), which measures perceived limitations in performing physical tasks. Example items include: “Does your health now limit you in vigorous activities?”, “Does your health now limit you in hiking a couple of miles?”, and “Does your health now limit you in participating in active sports?”. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (unable to do) to 5 (without any difficulty), with higher scores indicating better physical function [23]. To measnure pain, we used the PROMIS Pain Interference Item Bank v1.1, which includes 56 items [23]. The PROMIS(pain interference assessment determines how pain affects physical, mental, and social activities, while also evaluating pain levels. Example items include: “How much did pain interfere with your relationships with other people?” and “How much did pain interfere with your ability to work (including work at home)?” Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater pain interference. The range of Cronbach’s alpha values for Korean version of physical function and pain were 0.87–0.91 and 0.95–0.97, respectively, in various population [24–26]. For physical function, scores ≤ 40 were considered moderate to severe impairment. For pain interference, scores ≥ 60 were also defined as moderate to severe.

Psychological factors assessed included anxiety, depression, and use of psychiatric medication. The use of psychiatric medication within the last 30 days as prescribed by a doctor was repoted using the questionnaire. Anxiety was assessed using the PROMIS-Anxiety questionnaire, which features 29 items that evaluate cognitive, emotional, and physical anxiety symptoms. The response options ranged from 1 (never) to 5 (always), with higher scores indicating higher anxiety levels. Depression was evaluated utilized the PROMIS-Depression 28-item questionnaire, which encompasses diverse depressive aspects on a Likert scale. The scores reflect various depressive experiences, including sadness, loss of interest, hopelessness, low energy, and sleep disturbances [27]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of depression, and anxiety were 0.97, and 0.96, respetvely [28]. For anxiety and depression, scores of 55–59 were classified as mild, and ≥ 60 as moderate to severe. These thresholds are consistent with both U.S.-based scoring guidelines and prior validation studies of the Korean version of PROMIS.

Statistical analysis

We performed a post hoc power calculation using the observed proportion of participants classified in the high aggression group (10%) and the distribution of key exposure variables. With a total sample size of 2,699 and a two-sided α of 0.05, the study had over 80% power to detect an odds ratio of 1.6 or greater for the association between aggression and primary exposures, such as moderate-to-severe depression, using multivariable logistic regression.

To calculate the aggression score, raw AQ scores were linearly transformed to yield domain scores within the range of 27–135, where a higher score indicated a stronger tendency towards aggression [22]. For the analysis, participants who responded in the highest 10 percentiles were grouped into the “higher aggression” category while participants responding below the highest 10 percentiles were grouped into the “normal” category.

The PROMIS scores, which measure physical function, pain interference, depression, and anxiety, were converted into t-scores via the PROMIS Assessment Center. The PROMIS T-scores are based on the US population, with an average score of 50 and a standard deviation of 10 points. Higher pain interference scores indicate greater pain interference. Despite potential differences in local item parameters, previous research has demonstrated that when country-specific parameters are rescaled to the U.S. metric, the resulting PROMIS scores exhibit negligible mean differences while maintaining international interpretability [29].

We conducted multivariable logistic regression analyses to identify the demographic, physical, psychological, and social relational factors associated with perpetration of aggression (prevalence ratio (PR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated). We adjusted for age, sex, physical function, pain interference, comorbidities, physical disability, marital status, psychological characteristics, anxiety, depression, and the use of psychiatric medication. To evaluate the fit of the final multivariable logistic regression model, we calculated the residual deviance and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). These metrics assess how well the model fits the observed data while accounting for model complexity.

Differences in score and distribution between the two groups were identified using the standardized mean difference (SMD). Statistical analyses were performed using the R 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 2,699 participants with a mean age of 48.3 years (standard deviation [SD] = 16.6) were included in the study. Among them, 1,357 (50.3%) were male (Table 1). The mean aggression score for the entire group was 52.2 (SD = 16.7). The mean aggression score in the top 10 percentile group was 89.6 (SD = 9.1) (Fig. 1). Approximately 38% of respondents indicated that “I flare up quickly but get over it quickly” was extremely characteristic of themselves. Conversely, 40% selected “extremely uncharacteristic of me” for the reverse-coded item “I am an even-tempered person,” indicating higher levels of self-reported anger. A similar pattern was observed in the physical aggression domain. For the reverse-coded item “I can think of no good reason for ever hitting a person,” over half of the respondents selected “extremely uncharacteristic of me,” suggesting that strong acceptance of physical aggression in certain contexts (Fig. 2).

The final multivariable logistic regression model demonstrated adequate fit, with a residual deviance of 897.6 on 2,678 degrees of freedom and an AIC of 939.6. These values suggest that the model was both well-calibrated and parsimonious after adjustment for all covariates. In the multivariable model, younger people (≤ 29) were 2.56 times (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.19–3.93) more likely to have higher levels of aggression than older adults(≥ 65).

Additionally, the group with moderate & severe physical functional impairment had 2.06 times (95% CI 1.33–2.96) higher levels of aggression compared to the group with normal physical function. Furthermore, individuals experiencing moderate and severe pain interference were 2.29 times (95% CI 1.62–3.12) more likely to display heightened aggression than those with normal pain interference. In terms of psychological aspect, those with moderate and severe anxiety and depression were 2.39 (95% CI 1.66–3.12) and 2.53 (95% CI 1.68–3.38) times more likely to display higher levels of aggression when compared to individuals with normal anxiety and depression levels (Table 2).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study presents comprehensive findings regarding the factors associated with perpetration of aggression in the general population. A previous study of the general population focused primarily on demographic, behavioral, social, and cultural characteristics, rather than physical or psychological health factors [30]. The results of this study highlight the complex interplay between sociodemographic, physical, and psychological factors influencing aggression levels. We found several significant predictors of higher levels of aggression, including youth, severe physical function impairment, severe pain, and psychological conditions such as anxiety, depression.

In China, a study of 843 university students reported a mean aggression score of 69.6 (SD = 18.3) [19]. Similarly, a large-scale study in Iran (n = 10,957) found a mean score of 77.7 (SD = 22.5) [30], and a study of young adults in Pakistan (n = 500) reported a mean of 78.0 (SD = 18.6) [18]. These values are substantially higher than the mean aggression score in our study, which was 52.2. The differences may reflect variations in study populations, measurement settings, or cultural expression of aggression. Importantly, previous studies focusing on the general population have not provided specific aggression scores, thus presenting challenges in achieving a comprehensive interpretation of the outcomes. The lack of precise aggression metrics in these studies emphasizes the importance of our investigation. Our study has sought to fill this gap in the knowledge by implementing methodologies that differ from those employed in prior research, providing a more nuanced understanding of aggression levels in the general population [31, 32].

Regarding sociodemographic factors, younger age was associated with more severe aggression. Age has consistently been a significant demographic factor associated with aggression [12, 13]. The association may be explained by the fact that regulation of emotions is lower in younger individuals compared to older individuals [33]. Successful regulation of emotions, particularly intense negative emotions such as anger, can be challenging [33]. Underregulation of anger typically occurs when an individual lacks adaptive regulatory strategies to contain anger [34]. Considering aggressive behavior is ranked the fourth leading cause of death globally among young adults [3] there is a critical clinical need for early and targeted interventions that focus on emotion regulation skills, particularly in younger populations.

Physical factors, such as chronic pain and low levels of physical activity, correlate with high aggression levels [14, 15]. The International Association for the Study of Pain describes pain as “an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with a present or potential tissue damage, or described as such” [35]. Chronic pain has been correlated with symptoms of impulsivity, anger, and aggression [36] which could in turn be related to different factors such as an increase in muscle reactivity in the painful area, increased adipose tissue, endogenous opioid system dysfunction, and genetic polymorphisms affecting the opioid, serotonergic, and adrenergic systems [37]. Impaired physical function often leads to increased levels of stress and frustration due to limitations in daily activities and independence. Individuals may experience feelings of helplessness, loss of control, and diminished self-esteem when they struggle with manageable tasks. Psychological distress can manifest as aggression, either as a direct expression of frustration or as a defensive mechanism for coping with perceived vulnerability [38]. The association between chronic pain and physical function with high aggression is not solely based on physical sensation, but also on the emotional and psychological distress it causes. Distress can manifest as aggression owing to feelings of frustration, helplessness, or diminished self-worth. Beyond the emotional dimension, emerging neurobiological evidence suggests that chronic pain and functional limitation may alter brain circuits related to threat perception and emotional regulation. Increased activation of the amygdala and reduced top-down control from the prefrontal cortex have been observed in individuals experiencing chronic stress or pain, both of which are associated with heightened irritability and reactive aggression [39]. In addition, physical impairment and pain may disrupt social functioning by reducing participation in daily activities or work, contributing to isolation and diminished access to social support. This combination of psychological, biological, and social stressors creates a context in which aggression may become a maladaptive coping mechanism. Considering the association between chronic pain, impaired physical function, and aggression, interventions that address both the physical and emotional aspects of these conditions are needed. By adopting this holistic approach, health care providers can better support individuals in navigating the complexities of chronic pain and aggression, leading to improved outcomes and quality of life.

Psychological factors, such as depression and anxiety, are commonly associated with aggression across all age groups. This is consistent with a previous study in which adults with depression directly assessed the prevalence and correlates of aggressive outbursts [40]. The previous study found positive correlations between symptoms of depression, including weight/appetite change, fatigue, recurrent thoughts of death, and the frequency of aggressive outbursts [40]. All three depressive symptoms indicate serotonin dysregulation, which has been linked to aggressive outbursts [41]. Our findings have implications for public health, forensics, and clinical interventions. The link between depression and aggression demonstrates one pathway leading to violence and highlights the potential effectiveness of depression prevention and treatment. Legal settings may include assessing depression to identify risk and motivation for violence. Based on our findings, depression interventions should address the presence of aggressive attacks to reduce functional impairment and increased suicidal behavior.

This study has some limitations. First, the cause-and-effect relationship may not be discernible owing to the inherent nature of this cross-sectional study. The associations among aggression, physical and psychological factors, and social factors may be bidirectional. For instance, individuals with poor physical health may experience mental distress, potentially contributing to aggressive behaviors. Future longitudinal studies or repeated-measures designs are needed to clarify causal directions and better understand dynamic interactions among these factors. Second, since there was no clear threshold to define severe aggression, the participants in this study were classified into the high aggression group based on the top 10% aggression scores and analyzed accordingly. However, because these data were obtained from a representative population, they could be used to generate a cut-off. In addition, PROMIS scores used in this study are standardized based on the U.S. general population. However, prior research has demonstrated that T-score conversions yield comparable results across countries. Given that our analysis focused on relative associations within the Korean population, the use of U.S.-referenced scores is unlikely to have biased the findings. Third, it is essential to consider that this study was conducted in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. A growing number of individuals have experienced feelings of anger and helplessness due to economic hardships or significant disruptions in their daily lives caused by COVID-19 [42]. There is a possibility that study participants who experienced negative consequences of COVID-19, such as decreased sales, closed businesses, and unemployment, might feel angry and lethargic whenever they recall the experience in their daily lives. Therefore, it is plausible that the aggression scores measured in this study were temporarily elevated due to the unique circumstances surrounding the pandemic. Nevertheless, this study may provide an opportunity to comprehensively assess the factors related to aggression during the COVID-19 pandemic. Fourth, response bias is also a possibility, as aggression is a socially undesirable behavior that may be underreported despite anonymity. We attempted to minimize this bias by ensuring that the survey was self-administered and conducted online, which may reduce social desirability effects. Fifth, certain factors that have the potential to influence aggression, such as the frequency of drinking, parental attitudes during the upbringing process, number of siblings, and psychiatric comorbidities, could not be confirmed. Finally, as this study included only South Korean adults, the findings may not be generalizable to other populations with different cultural or healthcare contexts. Future research using multinational data is warranted to confirm the external validity of our results.

Conclusions

This study identified multiple physical, psychological, and social factors associated with aggression in a nationally representative sample of South Korean adults, with variation observed across different adult age groups. In this study, we established reference values for aggression perpetration in the general population stratified by age and sex. Furthermore, by comprehensively examining aggression across adult age groups, our findings offer valuable insights into the demographic patterns and public health implications of aggressive behavior in society. ​​ Young age, severe decline in physical function, pain interference, depression, and anxiety were identified as risk factors for aggression. The findings suggest that public health interventions targeting aggression should adopt a multidimensional approach, particularly for individuals with concurrent physical and psychological symptoms. Screening for aggression risk in primary care and mental health settings could incorporate routine assessments of physical function and pain interference. Additionally, coordinated care models involving pain management, psychosocial support, and behavioral therapy may be effective in high-risk populations. Future research should employ longitudinal and experimental designs to clarify causal pathways and evaluate the effectiveness of interventions targeting physical and psychological correlates of aggression. These efforts could inform tailored prevention strategies for high-risk populations across the life course.
