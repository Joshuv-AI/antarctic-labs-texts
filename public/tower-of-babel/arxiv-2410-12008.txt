Asymptotics and sign patterns of Hecke polynomial coefficients
Erick Ross, Hui Xue

Abstract
. We determine the asymptotic behavior of the coefficients of Hecke polynomials. In particular, this allows us to determine signs of these coefficients when the level or the weight is sufficiently large. In all but finitely many cases, this also verifies a conjecture on the nanvanishing of the coefficients of Hecke polynomials.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 11F25; Secondary 11F72 and 11F11.

1.
Introduction

For integers
m\geq 1
,
N
coprime to
m
, and
k\geq 2
even, let
S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
denote the space of cuspforms of level
N
and weight
k
.
Let
T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k):=\frac{1}{m^{(k-1)/2}}T_{m}(N,k)
denote the normalized
m
-th Hecke operator on
S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
.
For each integer
r\geq 0
, let
c_{r}(m,N,k)
denote the
r
-th coefficient of the characteristic polynomial
T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)(x)
associated to
T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)
as follows:

(1.1)

\displaystyle T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)(x)=\sum_{r=0}^{d}c_{r}(m,N,k)x^{d-r},

where
d=\dim S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
.
Hecke operators are of central importance in the theory of modular forms, and are completely characterized by the Hecke polynomials. We would like to study the coefficients of these Hecke polynomials in order to understand their structure.
In particular, for any fixed
m
and
r
, the main goal of this paper is to determine the asymptotic behavior of
c_{r}(m,N,k)
as
N+k\to\infty
. This will also show that
c_{r}(m,N,k)
is nonvanishing and further determine its sign in all but finitely many cases.

We give an outline of this paper. In Section
2
, we apply the Girard-Newton formula to the coefficients
c_{r}(m,N,k)
, and state the asymptotic behavior of
\Tr T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)
.
In Section
3
, we consider the case when
m
is a perfect square and prove the following result determining the asymptotic behavior of the
c_{r}(m,N,k)
. In the following, all big-
O
notation is with respect to
N
and
k
. Additionally, we use the notation ”
O(N^{\varepsilon})
”, for example, to mean ”
O(N^{\varepsilon})
for all
\varepsilon>0
”.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
and a perfect square
m\geq 1
.
Then for
N
coprime to
m
and
k\geq 2
even,

(1.2)

\displaystyle c_{r}(m,N,k)=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon}).

Here,
\psi(N)
denotes the multiplicative function
\psi(N)=N\prod_{p\mid N}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)
.

In Section
4
, we consider the case when
m
is not a perfect square and establish the following asymptotics of
c_{r}(m,N,k)
. Recall here that
\sigma_{1}(m)
denotes the sum of divisors function
\sigma_{1}(m):=\sum_{d\mid m}d
, and that
(2r)!!
denotes the double factorial
(2r)!!:=2r(2r-2)(2r-4)\cdots 2
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
and a non-square
m\geq 1
.
Then for
N
coprime to
m
and
k\geq 2
even,

(1.3)

\displaystyle c_{2r}(m,N,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{and}

(1.4)

\displaystyle c_{2r+1}(m,N,k)

\displaystyle=c_{1}(m,N,k)\cdot\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon}).

In Section
5
, we extend Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
to the new subspace
S_{k}^{\textnormal{new}}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
.

Finally, in Sections
6
and
7
, we discuss these results. In Section
6
, we discuss how the arguments given in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
for the Hecke polynomials can also be applied to other polynomials. In particular, these arguments reveal a coefficient sign pattern for a wide class of polynomials.
Then in Section
7
, we discuss a conjecture on the nonvanishing of the Hecke polynomial coefficients and survey its current progress.

2.
Preliminary Calculations

For simplicity, we write
c_{r}
for the coefficients
c_{r}(m,N,k)
. Let
d=\dim S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
denote the eigenvalues of
T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)
. Observe that
(-1)^{r}c_{r}
is just the
r
-th elementary symmetric polynomial of these eigenvalues:

(2.1)

\displaystyle c_{0}=1,\quad-c_{1}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq d}\lambda_{i}\,,\quad c_{2}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\,,\quad-c_{3}=\sum_{1\leq i<j<\ell\leq d}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\lambda_{\ell}\,,\quad\ldots

We also write
p_{r}
for the sum of
r
-th powers of these eigenvalues:

(2.2)

\displaystyle p_{r}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{r}.

Then the Girard-Newton identities yield the following relation
between the
c_{r}
and the
p_{r}
.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
13
, p. 38]
)
.

Let
c_{r}
and
p_{r}
be defined as above. Then for
r\geq 1
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle c_{r}=\frac{-1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}c_{r-j}p_{j}.

We also give estimates on the traces of Hecke operators. These estimates will be needed shortly when we express the
p_{r}
in terms of traces of certain Hecke operators.
In a previous paper
[
11
]
, we proved the following result by analyzing the various terms of the Eichler-Selberg trace formula.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
11
, Lemmas 4.1, 4.2]
)
.

Fix an integer
m\geq 1
. Then for
N
coprime to
m
and
k\geq 2
even,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Tr T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)+O(N^{1/2+\varepsilon}),&\text{if $m$ is a perfect square},\\
O(N^{\varepsilon}),&\text{if $m$ is not a perfect square}.\end{cases}

To gauge the growth of the terms in this formula, note that
\psi(N)\geq N
and
\psi(N)=O(N^{1+\varepsilon})

[
7
, Sections 18.1, 22.13]
.

3.
When
m
is a perfect square

In this section, we consider the case when
m
is a perfect square.
We then have the following estimates on the
p_{j}
(
2.2
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 1
and a perfect square
m\geq 1
.
Then for
N
coprime to
m
and
k\geq 2
even,

(3.1)

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)+O(N^{1/2+\varepsilon}),\qquad\text{and}

(3.2)

\displaystyle p_{j}

\displaystyle=O(kN^{1+\varepsilon}),\qquad\text{for all $1\leq j\leq r$}.

Proof.

The first claim follows immediately from Lemma
2.2
.

For the second claim, note from Lemma
2.2
and the fact that
\psi(N)=O(N^{1+\epsilon})
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle d:=\dim S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))=\Tr T^{\prime}_{1}=O(kN^{1+\varepsilon}).

Then utilizing Deligne’s bound
\left\lvert\lambda_{i}\right\lvert\leq\sigma_{0}(m)=\sum_{d\mid m}1
, we obtain

(3.4)

\displaystyle\left\lvert p_{j}\right\lvert=\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{j}\right\lvert\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{0}(m)^{j}=O(kN^{1+\varepsilon}),

as desired.
∎

For
m
and
r
fixed, we now determine the asymptotic behavior of
c_{r}(m,N,k)
as
N+k\to\infty
. Note
c_{r}(m,N,k)
is not technically defined for
N,k
such that
\dim S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))<r
. However, there are only finitely many such pairs
(N,k)

[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
, so it is well-defined here to ask about
c_{r}(m,N,k)
as
N+k\to\infty
.

Theorem
1.1
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
and a perfect square
m\geq 1
.
Then for
N
coprime to
m
and
k\geq 2
even,

(3.5)

\displaystyle c_{r}(m,N,k)=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon}).

Proof.

We proceed by strong induction on
r
. The base case of
r=0
is immediate since
c_{0}=1
.

For
r\geq 1
, we have by Lemma
2.1
that

(3.6)

\displaystyle c_{r}=\frac{-1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}c_{r-j}p_{j}=\frac{-1}{r}\Bigg[c_{r-1}p_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{r}c_{r-j}p_{j}\Bigg].

Then by the induction hypothesis,

(3.7)

\displaystyle c_{r-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r-1}+O(k^{r-2}N^{r-3/2+\varepsilon}),

(3.8)

\displaystyle c_{r-j}

\displaystyle=O(k^{r-2}N^{r-2+\varepsilon}),\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{for $2\leq j\leq r$},

and by Lemma
3.1
,

(3.9)

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)+O(N^{1/2+\varepsilon}),

(3.10)

\displaystyle p_{j}

\displaystyle=O(kN^{1+\varepsilon}),\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{for $2\leq j\leq r$}.

Applying these estimates to (
3.6
), we obtain

(3.11)

\displaystyle c_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{r}\Bigg[c_{r-1}p_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{r}c_{r-j}p_{j}\Bigg]

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{r}\Bigg[\left(\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r-1}+O(k^{r-2}N^{r-3/2+\varepsilon})\right)

(3.13)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)+O(N^{1/2+\varepsilon})\right)

(3.14)

\displaystyle\qquad\ \ \ \ +\sum_{j=2}^{r}O(k^{r-2}N^{r-3/2+\varepsilon})\cdot O(kN^{1+\varepsilon})\Bigg]

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{r}\Bigg[\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon})\Bigg]

(3.16)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon}).

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
1.1
allows us in particular to determine the sign of
c_{r}(m,N,k)
for all but finitely many pairs
(N,k)
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
and a perfect square
m\geq 1
.
Then
c_{r}(m,N,k)
has sign
(-1)^{r}
for all but finitely pairs
(N,k)
.

Proof.

Since
\psi(N)\geq N
, we can write the asymptotic formula from Theorem
1.1
as

(3.17)

\displaystyle c_{r}(m,N,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!\sqrt{m}^{r}}\left(\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}\left[1+O\left(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon}\right)\left(\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{-r}\right]

(3.18)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!\sqrt{m}^{r}}\left(\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}\left[1+O\left(k^{-1}N^{-1/2+\varepsilon}\right)\right].

Then since the
O\left(k^{-1}N^{-1/2+\varepsilon}\right)
term tends to
0
as
N\to\infty
or
k\to\infty
, it will have magnitude less than
1
for all but finitely many pairs
(N,k)
. This then yields the desired result.
∎

4.
When
m
is not a perfect square

In this section, we consider the remaining case when
m
is not a perfect square.
First, we have the following estimates on the
p_{j}
(
2.2
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
and a non-square
m\geq 1
.
Then

(4.1)

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=-c_{1}=O(N^{\varepsilon}),

(4.2)

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)+O(N^{1/2+\varepsilon}),

(4.3)

\displaystyle p_{3}

\displaystyle=O(N^{\varepsilon}),

(4.4)

\displaystyle p_{j}

\displaystyle=O(kN^{1+\varepsilon}),\qquad\text{for all $1\leq j\leq r$}.

Proof.

The first claim follows immediately from Lemma
2.2
.

For the second claim, observe that
p_{2}=\Tr{T_{m}^{\prime}}^{2}
. Then by the Hecke operator composition formula
[
6
, Theorem 10.2.9]
and Lemma
2.2
,

(4.5)

\displaystyle p_{2}=\Tr{T_{m}^{\prime}}^{2}=\sum_{d\mid m}\Tr T_{m^{2}/d^{2}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\mid m}\frac{d}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)+O(N^{1/2+\varepsilon})

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)+O(N^{1/2+\varepsilon}).

For the third claim, we similarly have by the Hecke operator composition formula and Lemma
2.2
,

(4.7)

\displaystyle p_{3}=\Tr{T_{m}^{\prime}}^{3}=\Tr\sum_{d\mid m}T_{m^{2}/d^{2}}^{\prime}T_{m}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\mid m}\ \sum_{\delta\mid(m^{2}/d^{2},m)}\Tr T^{\prime}_{m^{3}/d^{2}\delta^{2}}

(4.8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\mid m}\ \sum_{\delta\mid(m^{2}/d^{2},m)}O(N^{\varepsilon})

(4.9)

\displaystyle=O(N^{\varepsilon}).

Finally, the fourth claim follows from an identical argument as in Lemma
3.1
.
∎

For
m
and
r
fixed, we now determine the asymptotic behavior of
c_{r}(m,N,k)
as
N+k\to\infty
.

Theorem
1.2
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
and a non-square
m\geq 1
. Then for
N
coprime to
m
and
k\geq 2
even,

(4.10)

\displaystyle c_{2r}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{and}

(4.11)

\displaystyle c_{2r+1}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon}).

Proof.

We proceed by strong induction on
r
.
The base case of
r=0
is immediate since
c_{0}=1
and
c_{1}=c_{1}
.

Then for
r\geq 1
, we have from Lemma
2.1
that

(4.12)

\displaystyle c_{2r}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{2r}\sum_{j=1}^{2r}c_{2r-j}p_{j}=\frac{-1}{2r}\Bigg[c_{2r-1}p_{1}+c_{2r-2}p_{2}+\sum_{j=3}^{2r}c_{2r-j}p_{j}\Bigg].

Then by the induction hypotheses,

(4.13)

\displaystyle c_{2r-1}

\displaystyle=O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1+\varepsilon}),

(4.14)

\displaystyle c_{2r-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{(2r-2)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r-1}+O(k^{r-2}N^{r-3/2+\varepsilon}),

(4.15)

\displaystyle c_{2r-j}

\displaystyle=O(k^{r-2}N^{r-2+\varepsilon}),\qquad\qquad\text{for $3\leq j\leq 2r$},

and by Lemma
4.1
,

(4.16)

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=O(N^{\varepsilon}),

(4.17)

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)+O(N^{1/2+\varepsilon}),

(4.18)

\displaystyle p_{j}

\displaystyle=O(kN^{1+\varepsilon}),\qquad\qquad\text{for $3\leq j\leq 2r$}.

Applying these estimates to (
4.12
), we obtain

(4.19)

\displaystyle c_{2r}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{2r}\Bigg[c_{2r-1}p_{1}+c_{2r-2}p_{2}+\sum_{j=3}^{2r}c_{2r-j}p_{j}\Bigg]

(4.20)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{2r}\Bigg[O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1+\varepsilon})\cdot O(N^{\varepsilon})

(4.21)

\displaystyle\qquad\ +\left(\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{(2r-2)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r-1}+O(k^{r-2}N^{r-3/2+\varepsilon})\right)

(4.22)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)+O(N^{1/2+\varepsilon})\right)

(4.23)

\displaystyle\qquad\ +\sum_{j=3}^{2r}O(k^{r-2}N^{r-2+\varepsilon})\cdot O(kN^{1+\varepsilon})\Bigg]

(4.24)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{2r}\Bigg[O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1+\varepsilon})+\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{(2r-2)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}

(4.25)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon})+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1+\varepsilon})\Bigg]

(4.26)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon}),

verifying the first claim of the inductive step.

For the second claim of the inductive step, we similarly have
from Lemma
2.1
that

(4.27)

\displaystyle c_{2r+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{2r+1}\sum_{j=1}^{2r+1}c_{2r+1-j}p_{j}=\frac{-1}{2r+1}\Bigg[c_{2r}p_{1}+c_{2r-1}p_{2}+c_{2r-2}p_{3}+\sum_{j=4}^{2r+1}c_{2r+1-j}p_{j}\Bigg].

Then by the induction hypotheses and the proof for
c_{2r}
,

(4.28)

\displaystyle c_{2r}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon}),

(4.29)

\displaystyle c_{2r-1}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{(2r-2)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r-1}+O(k^{r-2}N^{r-3/2+\varepsilon}),

(4.30)

\displaystyle c_{2r-2}

\displaystyle=O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1+\varepsilon}),

(4.31)

\displaystyle c_{2r+1-j}

\displaystyle=O(k^{r-2}N^{r-2+\varepsilon}),\qquad\qquad\text{for $4\leq j\leq 2r+1$},

and by Lemma
4.1
,

(4.32)

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=-c_{1},

(4.33)

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)+O(N^{1/2+\varepsilon}),

(4.34)

\displaystyle p_{3}

\displaystyle=O(N^{\varepsilon}),

(4.35)

\displaystyle p_{j}

\displaystyle=O(kN^{1+\varepsilon}),\qquad\qquad\text{for $4\leq j\leq 2r+1$}.

Applying these estimates to (
4.27
), we obtain

(4.36)

\displaystyle c_{2r+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{2r+1}\Bigg[c_{2r}p_{1}+c_{2r-1}p_{2}+c_{2r-2}p_{3}+\sum_{j=4}^{2r+1}c_{2r+1-j}p_{j}\Bigg]

(4.37)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{2r+1}\Bigg[\left(\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon})\right)\cdot(-c_{1})

(4.38)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\left(c_{1}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{(2r-2)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r-1}+O(k^{r-2}N^{r-3/2+\varepsilon})\right)

(4.39)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)+O(N^{1/2+\varepsilon})\right)

(4.40)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1+\varepsilon})\cdot O(N^{\varepsilon})+\sum_{j=4}^{2r+1}O(k^{r-2}N^{r-2+\varepsilon})\cdot O(kN^{1+\varepsilon})\Bigg]

(4.41)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{2r+1}\Bigg[-c_{1}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon})

(4.42)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +c_{1}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{(2r-2)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon})

(4.43)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1+\varepsilon})+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1+\varepsilon})\Bigg]

(4.44)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\cdot\frac{1}{2r+1}\left(\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}-\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{(2r-2)!!}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon})

(4.45)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon}),

verifying the second claim of the inductive step.

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
1.2
allows us in particular to determine the sign of the even-indexed coefficients for all but finitely many pairs
(N,k)
. The following corollary can be shown using an identical argument as in Corollary
3.2
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
and a non-square
m\geq 1
.
Then
c_{2r}(m,N,k)
has sign
(-1)^{r}
for all but finitely pairs
(N,k)
.

The behavior of the odd-indexed coefficients, on the other hand, is determined by the behavior of the trace.

Corollary 4.3
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
, a non-square
m\geq 1
, and an even integer
k\geq 2
. Consider
N
such that
\Tr T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)\neq 0
. Then
c_{2r+1}(m,N,k)
has sign
(-1)^{r+1}\sgn(\Tr T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k))
for all but finitely many
N
.

Proof.

Since
\psi(N)\geq N
, we can write the asymptotic formula from Theorem
1.2
as

(4.46)

\displaystyle c_{2r+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}\left[c_{1}+O\left(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon}\right)\left(\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{-r}\right]

(4.47)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{k-1}{12}\psi(N)\right)^{r}\left[c_{1}+O\left(k^{-1}N^{-1/2+\varepsilon}\right)\right]

Then observe that since
\Tr T_{m}\in\ZZ
and
c_{1}=-\Tr T^{\prime}_{m}=-m^{-(k-1)/2}\Tr T_{m}\neq 0
, we must have
\left\lvert c_{1}\right\lvert=\left\lvert\Tr T^{\prime}_{m}\right\lvert\geq m^{-(k-1)/2}
.
And because the
O\left(k^{-1}N^{-1/2+\varepsilon}\right)
term tends to
0
as
N\to\infty
, it will have magnitude less than
m^{-(k-1)/2}
for all but finitely many
N
. This yields the desired result.
∎

We note that the condition
\Tr T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)\neq 0
here is not overly restrictive. Rouse
[
12
, Theorem 1.6]
showed that there are only finitely many
k
for which we could possibly have
\Tr T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)=0
for some
N
. And even for these finitely many remaining
k
, he showed in
[
12
, Theorem 1.7]
that
\Tr T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)\neq 0
for
100\%
of
N
.
He further conjectured in
[
12
, Conjecture 1.5]
that
\Tr T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)\neq 0
for all
N\geq 1
and
k=12
or
\geq 16
.

We also note that there are various ways one could try to improve this result to where
N
and
k
both vary. The only reason we fixed
k
was to guarantee that
c_{1}=-\Tr T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)
was bounded away from
0
. If we relax the condition of
k
being fixed to just that
k\leq(1-\delta)\log_{m}(N)
for some
\delta>0
, then we have the same result for all but finitely many pairs
(N,k)
. One could also try to bound
\Tr T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)
away from
0
using some sort of vertical Atkin-Serre type result for the trace (e.g. along the lines of
[
8
, Theorem 2.2]
).

5.
Extending to the new subspace

All of our results extend to the Hecke polynomial over the new subspace.
Let
T^{\prime\,{\textnormal{new}}}_{m}(N,k)
denote the restriction of
T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)
to the new subspace
S_{k}^{\textnormal{new}}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
.
Let
c^{\textnormal{new}}_{r}(m,N,k)
denote the
r
-th coefficient of the characteristic polynomial
T^{\prime\,{\textnormal{new}}}_{m}(N,k)(x)
as follows:

(5.1)

\displaystyle T^{\prime\,{\textnormal{new}}}_{m}(N,k)(x)=\sum_{r=0}^{d^{\textnormal{new}}}c^{\textnormal{new}}_{r}(m,N,k)x^{d^{\textnormal{new}}-r}.

Here,
d^{\textnormal{new}}=\dim S_{k}^{\textnormal{new}}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
.
Just like before, we can then
determine the asymptotic behavior of
c_{r}^{\textnormal{new}}(m,N,k)
as
N+k\to\infty
.
Note
c_{r}^{\textnormal{new}}(m,N,k)
is not technically defined for
N,k
such that
\dim S_{k}^{\textnormal{new}}(\Gamma_{0}(N))<r
. However, there are only finitely many such pairs
(N,k)

[
10
, Theorem 1.3]
, so it is perfectly well-defined here to ask about
c_{r}^{\textnormal{new}}(m,N,k)
as
N+k\to\infty
.

Let

(5.2)

\psi^{\textnormal{new}}(N):=\prod_{p^{r}\parallel N}\begin{cases}p\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right),&\text{if }r=1,\\
p^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right),&\text{if }r=2,\\
p^{r}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p^{2}}+\frac{1}{p^{3}}\right),&\text{if }r\geq 3,\end{cases}

and note that
\psi^{\textnormal{new}}(N)\leq N
and
\psi^{\textnormal{new}}(N)=\Omega(N^{1-\varepsilon})

[
7
, Sections 18.1, 22.13]
.
In
[
1
, Lemmas 4.2, 4.3]
, Cason et al. showed that for fixed
m
,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\Tr T^{\prime\,{\textnormal{new}}}_{m}(N,k)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi^{\textnormal{new}}(N)+O(N^{1/2}),&\text{if $m$ is a perfect square},\\
O(N^{\varepsilon}),&\text{if $m$ is not a perfect square}.\end{cases}

The following two theorems then follow by an identical argument as in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. The details are omitted.

Theorem 5.1
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
and a perfect square
m\geq 1
.
Then for
N
coprime to
m
and
k\geq 2
even,

(5.4)

\displaystyle c^{\textnormal{new}}_{r}(m,N,k)=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi^{\textnormal{new}}(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2}).

Theorem 5.2
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
and a non-square
m\geq 1
.
Then

(5.5)

\displaystyle c^{\textnormal{new}}_{2r}(m,N,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi^{\textnormal{new}}(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2})\qquad\text{and}

(5.6)

\displaystyle c^{\textnormal{new}}_{2r+1}(m,N,k)

\displaystyle=c^{\textnormal{new}}_{1}(m,N,k)\cdot\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(m)}{m}\frac{k-1}{12}\psi^{\textnormal{new}}(N)\right)^{r}+O(k^{r-1}N^{r-1/2+\varepsilon}).

Then just like in Corollaries
3.2
,
4.2
, and
4.3
, this tells us the sign of the
c^{\textnormal{new}}_{r}
in all but finitely many cases.

Corollary 5.3
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
and a perfect square
m\geq 1
.
Then
c^{\textnormal{new}}_{r}(m,N,k)
has sign
(-1)^{r}
for all but finitely pairs
(N,k)
.

Corollary 5.4
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
and a non-square
m\geq 1
.
Then
c^{\textnormal{new}}_{2r}(m,N,k)
has sign
(-1)^{r}
for all but finitely pairs
(N,k)
.

Corollary 5.5
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 0
, a non-square
m\geq 1
, and an even integer
k\geq 2
. Consider
N
such that
\Tr T^{\prime\,{\textnormal{new}}}_{m}(N,k)\neq 0
. Then
c^{\textnormal{new}}_{2r+1}(m,N,k)
has sign
(-1)^{r+1}\sgn(\Tr T^{\prime\,{\textnormal{new}}}_{m}(N,k))
for all but finitely many

.

6.
Sign patterns for more general polynomials

In response to our previous paper showing that
c_{2}
tends to be negative
[
11
]
, Kimball Martin suggested to us that
c_{2}
might display a similar bias more generally for polynomials with totally real roots. In fact, the sign tendencies for
all
the coefficients given in Corollaries
4.2
and
4.3
hold more generally for a wide class of polynomials with totally real roots. Essentially the only two conditions we need to impose are that the roots are distributed over an interval
[-A,A]
in a roughly symmetric way about the origin, and that the roots are not all clustered at the origin.

More precisely, for
A>0
and
r
fixed, consider a sequence of polynomials
f_{n}
with totally real roots lying in the interval
[-A,A]
. Let
d_{n}
denote the degree of
f_{n}
, and let the
c_{j}(n)
and
p_{j}(n)
be defined as above in Section
2
.
We assume that
p_{1}(n)=o(d_{n}^{1/3})
and
p_{3}(n)=o(d_{n})
(which will occur if the roots
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d_{n}}
of
f_{n}
are distributed in a roughly symmetric way about the origin).
Also note that
p_{2}(n)=\alpha_{n}^{2}d_{n}
, where
\alpha_{n}
denotes the quadratic mean of the roots of
f_{n}
.
We assume that
\alpha_{n}
is bounded away from
0
(which will occur as long as the roots are not all clustered at the origin).
Finally, observe that
\left\lvert p_{j}(n)\right\lvert\leq A^{r}d_{n}=O(d_{n})
for each
1\leq j\leq r
.
These estimates

(6.1)

\displaystyle p_{1}(n)

\displaystyle=-c_{1}(n)=o(d_{n}^{1/3}),

(6.2)

\displaystyle p_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n}^{2}d_{n},

(6.3)

\displaystyle p_{3}(n)

\displaystyle=o(d_{n}),

(6.4)

\displaystyle p_{j}(n)

\displaystyle=O(d_{n})\qquad\text{for each $1\leq j\leq r$},

are essentially the content of Lemma
4.1
(except that Lemma
4.1
has much stronger error bounds, and where in that case,
\alpha_{N,k}
tends to
\sqrt{\sigma_{1}(m)/m}
as
N+k\to\infty

[
2
, Theorem 1.1]
).

Then using an identical argument as in Theorem
1.2
, one can show that

(6.5)

\displaystyle c_{2r}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\alpha_{n}^{2}d_{n}\right)^{r}+o(d_{n}^{r-1/3})\qquad\text{and}

(6.6)

\displaystyle c_{2r+1}(n)

\displaystyle=c_{1}(n)\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2r)!!}\left(\alpha_{n}^{2}d_{n}\right)^{r}+o(d_{n}^{r}).

In particular, this means that as
d_{n}\to\infty
, the coefficients of
f_{n}
will tend to the sign pattern

(6.7)

\displaystyle+--++--++--++\cdots\qquad\text{if $c_{1}$ is bounded below $0$},

(6.8)

\displaystyle++--++--++--+\cdots\qquad\text{if $c_{1}$ is bounded above $0$}.

For example, browsing the polynomials with totally real roots of degree 10 in LMFDB, almost all of them follow this sign pattern; see
[
9
]
(such polynomials given by LMFDB are shifted so that their roots are roughly symmetric about the origin).

If the roots of a polynomial are perfectly symmetric about the origin, then we will have
c_{1}=0
, and the sign pattern becomes

(6.9)

\displaystyle+0-0+0-0+0-0+\cdots.\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \ \ \ \ ~

For example, the roots of the Chebyshev polynomials are distributed in
[-1,1]
in a perfectly symmetric way, and their coefficients follow precisely this pattern.

We also note what happens when the roots of a polynomial are not distributed symmetrically about the origin. If all the roots have the same sign, then the coefficients
c_{r}
follow the sign pattern

(6.10)

\displaystyle+-+-+-+-\ldots\qquad\text{if all the roots are positive},

(6.11)

\displaystyle+\hskip 0.4pt+\hskip 0.4pt+\hskip 0.4pt+\hskip 0.4pt+\hskip 0.4pt+\hskip 0.4pt+\hskip 0.4pt+\hskip 0.4pt\ldots\qquad\text{if all the roots are negative}.

When
m
is a perfect square, most of the roots of
T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)(x)
are positive, and Corollary
3.2
shows that the coefficients of
T^{\prime}_{m}(N,k)(x)
tend to this first pattern.

In

[
