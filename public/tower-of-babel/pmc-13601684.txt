A Framework for the Estimation of Quality-Adjusted Life-Years Using Joint Models of Longitudinal and Survival Data
Authors: Michael J Crowther, Alessandro Gasparini, Sara Ekberg, Federico Felizzi, Elaine Gallagher, Noman Paracha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Length and quality of life are frequently combined in health technology assessments to derive a single, generic measure of health improvement due to a treatment or medicine. One such measure is given by quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), typically calculated assuming discrete health states and quality-of-life values. The accurate estimation of QALYs is crucial for informed decision making in health care policy and resource allocation; however, traditional methods often rely on assumptions that are sometimes biologically and statistically inappropriate. This study aims to develop a framework for estimating QALYs in populations where survival data are collected that addresses these limitations.Methods:A framework for estimating QALYs in continuous time is introduced, based on joint longitudinal-survival models fitted using maximum likelihood; the proposed framework requires patient-level data, including longitudinal health utility values and overall survival. In contrast to the conventional approach, which involves dichotomising health states and separate models, this method allows the estimation of QALYs from a single model while accounting for all the statistical and biological intricacies of the data, providing a more appropriate estimate for cost-effectiveness modeling. The joint modeling approach is validated using Monte Carlo simulation under realistic data-generating mechanisms.Results:Simulations showed that the joint longitudinal-survival modeling approach could recover the true QALYs without bias. Conversely, a comparison method based on calculating QALYs directly from the health utility trajectories, ignoring the survival process, was biased under most scenarios.Conclusions:A new methodology for estimating QALYs within a unified, flexible and extensible framework has been developed. This approach can improve QALY estimation and their use in cost-effectiveness analyses in practice, enabling more timely and robust health technology assessments. User-friendly Stata software is provided.Keywords: quality of life, quality-adjusted life years, health technology assessment, joint modeling

The primary goal of new therapeutic options for cancer patients is to lengthen and improve patient survival, enabling affected individuals to live longer than they would without treatment. 1 Nonetheless, over the past decades, health-related quality-of-life (HRQL) investigations have become increasingly important not only to quantify improvements in survival but also to understand what these improvements mean for patients. HRQL refers to the subjective assessment of the effect of health status on quality of life, encompassing physical, mental and social well-being. One commonly used measure that combines survival and quality of life is quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), providing a single metric for evaluating health outcomes.

The significance of HRQL in cancer treatment is evidenced by several studies highlighting the number of trials collecting and reporting 1 or more patient-reported quality-of-life measures, or using such data to support regulatory submissions.2 –4 HRQL-related and patient-reported outcomes are increasingly measured throughout cancer trials and at regularly scheduled intervals to ensure appropriate data are captured on patient status. These HRQL scores not only are used to assess various trial endpoints but also play a crucial role in subsequent cost-effectiveness analysis, quantifying how much time patients spend at different levels of severity.

Despite the recent development of international standards, 5 statistical challenges persist in modeling and utilising HRQL data appropriately. HRQL data are often treated as a time-varying covariate, changing value only when a new measurement is taken—an assumption that is often (if not always) biologically implausible. Such data inherently suffer from measurement error, with observations that are correlated within patients. Longitudinal modeling of HRQL via mixed-effects models can yield biased estimates when trajectories are informatively truncated (eg, by death). 6 Moreover, mixed models implicitly impute values beyond the time of dropout, which may be unrealistic when dropout is due to death (and thus no HRQL exists after that time, in principle). Given the complexity of the interaction between survival and HRQL, there is a strong rationale for their joint analysis when evaluating a new product (or medicine) as ignoring one can bias the estimation of the other. 7

Joint models for longitudinal and survival data can effectively integrate postrandomisation data (such as HRQL measures of other longitudinal biomarkers recorded during follow-up of a trial) and survival data within a unified framework. 8 In health technology assessment, length of life and quality of life are commonly combined into a single, generic measure of health improvement via the QALY framework. 9 This process usually involves 3 steps: 1) describing the health states experienced by patients, 2) converting each health state into a value (or coefficient) and 3) combining the time spent in each health state and the corresponding value to obtain the total value (eg, QALYs). This article focuses on the estimation of QALYs and introduces a new approach based on joint longitudinal-survival modeling. Specifically, the proposed methodology involves jointly modeling quality of life and survival in continuous time, integrating their correlation and all sources of uncertainty within a single, unified framework. Compared with the usual approach based on dichotomising health states and using separate models for the various steps, the proposed approach enables the estimation of quality-adjusted life from a single model while accounting for all the statistical and biological intricacies of the data, providing a more appropriate estimate for use in cost-effectiveness modeling.

The use of joint longitudinal-survival models to simultaneously analyse quality-of-life data and survival data is not new. For instance, Billingham et al 10 discussed how joint longitudinal-survival models can account for informative dropout in longitudinal HRQL data analysis or simultaneously model longitudinal HRQL data and survival via shared random effects models. The latter topic is further discussed by Billingham and Abrams. 11 Spertus et al 12 used a joint model in a cardiovascular clinical trial, comparing it with separate longitudinal and time-to-event models. Bruno et al 13 discussed the use of joint models to study treatment effects on secondary, alternative endpoints, such as changes in longitudinal biomarkers (eg, HRQL), and how these can complement traditional Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) endpoints. 14 Touraine et al 15 compared linear mixed-effects and joint models for the analysis of HRQL data in cancer clinical trials, showing that biased estimated longitudinal trajectories arise if the survival outcome is ignored. Severe bias in some model parameters was also reported by Henderson et al 16 when the association between the processes is ignored, further motivating the use of joint models.

Approaches other than joint models have been proposed in the literature to integrate repeated quality-of-life measures and survival, including extensions of the Q-TWiST method, partitioned survival models, multistate models and methods based on time-to-death utilities.17 –24 Despite the popularity of these alternative approaches, there is a substantial interest in incorporating survival outcomes and surrogates (such as tumour burden and quality-of-life measures). Joint longitudinal-survival modeling has been recognised in this context as an important technique to leverage all the available information collected throughout clinical trials, addressing some of the limitations of alternative approaches. By providing a more accurate and comprehensive estimate of QALYs, this article aims to fill existing gaps in the literature and improve the assessment of health technologies.

The rest of the article is organised as follows. The next section introduces the joint modeling framework and QALY estimators. The third section (‘Monte Carlo Simulation Study’) reports simulation results, including a misspecification assessment in the fourth section. The fifth section (‘Discussion’) discusses the methodology, explores its limitations and concludes the article with opportunities for future research.

Estimation

Estimation of the joint model relies on the assumption of full conditional independence, which implies that, given the random effects, 1) repeated measurements in each outcome are independent, 2) longitudinal outcomes are independent of one another and 3) longitudinal outcomes and the time-to-event outcome are independent. Moreover, the methodology relies on the common assumption that the observation processes (driving the times at which each longitudinal outcome was recorded) are noninformative: given the trial context with scheduled observations, this assumption seems appropriate. Finally, estimation of the parameters of the joint model is performed via either a frequentist or Bayesian framework and is a computationally intensive task given 1) the (potentially) high dimensional random effects and 2) that there is no closed form for the likelihood of the joint model. More details on the estimation of joint longitudinal-survival models are available elsewhere and omitted for conciseness8,28; this work relies on the frequentist implementation from the user-written merlin command in Stata.26,29

Association structures

The longitudinal and time-to-event submodels are linked in equation (3) via general functions gq(⋅) and gy(⋅) . The most common form of g(⋅) yields the so-called expected value association structure, for example, for the quality-of-life submodel, gq(Qi(t),biq,αq)=αqmiq(t) . The association parameter αq can be interpreted as the log hazard ratio for a unit change in miq(t)=Xiq(t)βq+Ziq(t)biq , that is, the expected value of the quality-of-life measure Q from equation (1).

Several alternatives are possible: the expected rate of change association structure, time-independent association structures that link the submodels by sharing the random effects and association structures that denote cumulative effects, lagged effects or combinations (such as expected value and rate of change, together), among others. These are discussed and described in more detail elsewhere.28,30 The choice of association structure may be data driven (eg, by assessing fit of the joint model with the observed data) or motivated by the clinical question, the literature or computational reasons.

Contrasts of QALY measures

Contrasts involving QALY measures can be readily defined using the potential outcomes, counterfactual framework. 35

For instance, one may want to compare the difference in restricted QALYs had every study participant been exposed compared with the counterfactual scenario in which every participant was assigned to the control arm; this can be defined as QALY(t|X=1)−QALY(t|X=0) , where QALY(t|X=1) and QALY(t|X=0) follow from Equation (7). This can be interpreted as the marginal, population-level difference in QALYs between the experimental and control (reference) arm, restricted at a certain time t . Notably, this quantity could be computed for different values of t , allowing for the comparison and examination of both short- and long-term differences.

Monte Carlo Simulation Study

The performance and validity of the methodology introduced in the ‘Methodology’ section is studied using Monte Carlo simulation. In the interest of space, we report only a summary of the simulation study protocol (‘Short Simulation Protocol’ subsection), with results in the ‘Results’ subsection. A comprehensive protocol document, reported according to the ADEMP framework, 43 is included in the supplementary material available online.

Short Simulation Protocol

The simulation study consisted of 24 distinct data-generating mechanisms (DGMs), varying study sample size, measurement pattern for longitudinal utilities, shape of the utility trajectories and treatment effect on the longitudinal trajectories, in a factorial fashion. Briefly, each DGM is based on a joint model that mimics the settings of a clinical trial with participants randomised 1:1 to a binary treatment (or placebo) at baseline. The joint model includes a longitudinal submodel for the health utility values and a time-to-event submodel for overall survival. The longitudinal submodel for HRQL assumes linear or nonlinear trajectories (depending on the DGM), a random intercept and slope and a possible treatment effect on the longitudinal trajectories. The survival submodel assumes a treatment effect on overall survival, a Weibull baseline hazard function and links with the longitudinal submodel via the shared random effects association structure. Finally, participants are followed up for a maximum of 10 y. Each DGM is described in more detail in the full protocol, included in the online supplementary material.

The estimands for this simulation study are restricted QALYs at time t=2,5,10 y, by treatment arm and difference thereof. These are denoted by Q1(t) for the treatment arm, Q0(t) for the control arm and Q1−0(t)=Q1(t)−Q0(t) for the QALYs difference at time t , respectively.

These estimands are estimated using 3 methods. First, a method that estimates QALYs as the area under the HRQL trajectory, denoted as the AUC method. This effectively ignores the survival process, assuming that quality of life is minimised at the time of death and beyond. Second, a method that uses the QALYs estimators introduced in the ‘Estimation’ subsection and that is based on a multivariate joint model assuming linear trajectories for both the marker and the HRQL trajectories with time-by-treatment statistical interactions, a random intercept in the marker submodel and a random intercept and slope in the HRQL submodel. This is denoted as JMLin and is fully well-specified under all simulation scenarios assuming linear HRQL trajectories. Third, a joint model with nonlinear HRQL trajectories, modelled using restricted cubic splines with 3 degrees of freedom. This is denoted as JMNonLin and is expected to accommodate both linear and nonlinear trajectories (eg, according to every DGM of this study).

Key performance measures are bias, relative bias and coverage probability, including Monte Carlo standard errors to quantify uncertainty in their estimation. 43 Computational time and convergence rates are reported as well.

Finally, the number of repetitions was determined by running a preliminary batch of 10 repetitions per DGM. Based on those repetitions, we estimated that 250 additional repetitions are sufficient to control Monte Carlo error, as described in the full simulation protocol, for a total of 10+250=260 repetitions.

Results

Median survival times, across all simulated datasets, were between 6 and 7 y; by the end of follow-up, approximately 70% to 80% of simulated study participants died. The median number of measurements per participants was 7 and 26 for scenarios with HRQL measurements every 12 and 3 mo, respectively. Detailed values are included in Supplementary Appendix B.1.

Convergence rates were satisfactory: 100% for the AUC method, greater than 94% for JMLin and greater than 91% for JMNonLin (Supplementary Appendix B.2). The median computational time, across simulation scenarios, was 0.3 min for the AUC method, 2.5 min for JMLin and 3.2 min for JMNonLin (Supplementary Appendix B.3).

The results for bias are depicted in Figures 2, 3 and 4 and tabulated in Supplementary Appendix B.4. JMNonLin performed best overall, despite small bias under certain scenarios at prediction time t=2 (up to 0.023 QALYs, in absolute terms); nonetheless, under the same scenarios, JMNonLin still outperformed both the AUC and JMLin methods. Otherwise, JMNonLin consistently yielded unbiased results across estimands, simulation scenarios and prediction times. JMLin performed as well as JMNonLin when correctly specified (ie, under DGMs with linear HRQL trajectories); when misspecified, JMLin yielded biased results (up to 0.174 QALYs for arm-specific estimates and 0.063 QALYs for the difference between arms), performing worse than the AUC method for t=2 and better for t=5,10 . The AUC method performed worst overall, with positive bias for arm-specific estimates under all scenarios (up to 0.256 QALYs) and negative bias for the difference (up to −0.107 QALYs). Despite biased results, the AUC and JMLin methods performed better at estimating the QALYs difference as compared with estimating arm-specific QALYs; the AUC method, specifically, performed as well as the joint models under all scenarios with no treatment effect on the longitudinal HRQL trajectories. Relative bias patterns were similar to absolute bias (Supplementary Appendix B.5). Sample size and observation patterns did not affect bias significantly.

Coverage probabilities followed the same patterns as bias and relative bias (Supplementary Appendix B.6), with JMNonLin performing best and JMLin second best. The AUC method yielded suboptimal coverage probabilities under most scenarios; nonetheless, performance for the difference estimator was close to that of joint models here as well.

In conclusion, the simulation study highlighted that well-specified joint models yield unbiased estimates when longitudinal HRQL is modelled flexibly enough. Some small sample bias may be present, especially at early prediction times, but the more flexible approach still outperformed simpler approaches, highlighting the importance of effective model-building strategies when using the QALY estimators introduced in this article. For instance, JMNonLin modelled HRQL trajectories using restricted cubic splines with 3 degrees of freedom; alternative knots locations or nonlinear formulation should be assessed in practice, as a sensitivity analysis. Later prediction times and QALYs differences were estimated more robustly under all DGMs of this simulation study.

Assessment of Model Misspecification

The results of the previous section highlight that the proposed estimator has satisfactory finite-sample performance when a joint model is either well-specified or flexible enough to capture nonlinear patterns. However, as with any model-based estimator (including other methods routinely used to estimate QALYs), misspecification of the joint model can lead to biased estimates (as in the case of the JMLin method). We further expand on the potential impact of model misspecification in this section by 1) reviewing the literature on model misspecification in joint models for longitudinal and survival data, with a focus on the implications for QALYs estimation, and 2) providing additional simulation results for misspecified estimators.

In the context of QALYs estimation, bias in any of the regression coefficients would result in biased estimates of the QALY measures as well, as these rely on predictions from the joint model, although the magnitude of this bias may be reduced due to the cumulative nature of the QALY estimand. Song et al 44 suggested remarkable robustness to the assumption of normal random effects for the estimation of most model parameters. These results were noted by Hsieh et al 45 as well, citing that the posterior density of the random effects can be well approximated by a normal density when a reasonably large number of longitudinal measurements per study subject is available. Similar results were obtained by Rizopoulos et al, 46 further noting that estimators for parameters of the longitudinal and survival submodels are rather robust to random effects misspecification. Crowther et al 27 showed that parameter estimates were insensitive to the specification of the baseline hazard, as long as a sufficiently flexible distribution is assumed (eg, flexible parametric with a sufficient number of degrees of freedom). Crowther et al 47 specifically tackled the issue of model misspecification, showing that misspecification of the longitudinal trajectory minimally affected the survival parameters when using the expected value association structure. Conversely, bias was observed in the association parameters when assuming a linear trajectory (in place of a true nonlinear trajectory) under the rate of change association structure. Overall, the authors noted that the current value association structure was quite robust to model misspecification, with the rate of change association structure being more sensitive to oversimplified model specifications.

Next, we derive some additional simulation results to further study model misspecification in our context. Specifically, we extend simulation scenario 11 (400 participants, HRQL measurements every 3 mo, nonlinear HRQL trajectories, significant treatment effect on HRQL trajectories) by defining 2 additional scenarios in which all joint models compared in the simulation study of the ‘Monte Carlo Simulation Study’ section are misspecified: a scenario in which the nonlinear HRQL trajectory is simulated from a Beta distribution, and a scenario in which the submodels are linked via the expected value association structure. The models compared in these settings are the same: the AUC method, JMLin and JMNonLin. Both scenarios are described in more detail in the simulation study protocol, available online.

The results of this additional simulation study are included in Appendix C (supplementary material available online). Under both scenarios, previous results from the literature were confirmed, with biased QALY estimates when relying on a misspecified joint model estimator. Bias was larger in the scenario assuming the expected value association structure as compared with the scenario assuming Beta-distributed HRQL trajectories, and the magnitude of this bias varied, with smaller bias at early prediction times and larger bias at later prediction times. Moreover, the more flexibly specified joint model (JMNonLin) performed generally better than the less flexible joint model (JMLin) did, and the AUC method performed slightly better than the joint model under the expected value association structure for the difference estimand, likely due to not assuming proportional hazards for the assumed time-dependent treatment effect.

In conclusion, these findings reinforce the need for careful model specification and the use of modern fit diagnostics (eg, posterior predictive checks as suggested by Rizopoulos et al 48 ).

Discussion

With this article, a new method for estimating QALYs based on multivariate joint models for longitudinal and survival data is introduced. Compared to previous approaches, this method directly and jointly models survival and HRQL trajectories over time, with great flexibility, allowing for the inclusion of any number of other covariates, either time fixed or time varying, and for a unified quantification of uncertainty and heterogeneity across all processes. Intermittent noninformative missing data and informative dropout due to death are handled naturally within the joint modeling framework.8,49

The availability of user-friendly, easy-to-use statistical software (in Stata) makes it accessible to practitioners and health care policy decision makers alike. Therefore, example data and annotated code to apply the methodology in practice are provided, under a permissive licence, on GitHub. The simulation study reported in the ‘Monte Carlo Simulation Study’ section highlighted that the method implementation can estimate QALYs without bias, significantly outperforming a simpler method that disregards the survival process completely. This framework has substantial potential to improve the estimation of QALYs in practice, thereby enhancing cost-effectiveness modeling efforts, as it allows for a comprehensive accounting of the statistical and biological intricacies of data within a single model.

While the joint modeling approach offers significant advantages, it also has limitations. First, given the inherent flexibility of the framework, analysts must rely on subject matter knowledge and modeling assumptions when defining each submodel and their association: this is a nontrivial task, which may result in biased estimates when some of the assumptions are violated or when fit to the observed data is poor, as highlighted by the results of the simulation study and the assessment of model misspecification in the ‘Assessment of Model Misspecification’ section. Effective model-building strategies, including graphical model diagnostics and validation techniques, 48 are therefore essential to ensure adequate model fit and accurate estimates; this limitation is shared with every other modeling approach for the estimation of QALYs. Second, the estimation algorithm is complex and can be time-consuming with larger datasets. Methodological and software improvements, currently under development, and the increased availability of computational resources are likely to alleviate this issue in the future. Third, QALY prediction relies on nested numerical integration without closed-form solutions; users should assess accuracy by testing an increasing number of quadrature points. Fourth, lifetime QALY calculation requires extrapolation of survival and HRQL trajectories beyond the observed data, whose accuracy must be assessed case by case (eg, using prior literature or longer follow-up data). Finally, the simulation study covers a limited number of scenarios and comparison methods; alternative parameters and methods could be explored, where estimand comparability allows, and further methodological studies are warranted. 50

The methodological framework can be further extended. For instance, extrapolation issues can be mitigated by allowing the inclusion of modeling constraints and alternative distributions to ensure biologically plausible extrapolations (eg, to prevent impossible HRQL values). A second natural extension is allowing the inclusion of discounting and costs within the joint modeling framework (eg, similar to the work of Achana et al 51 ); this would further bridge the gap between QALY estimation and trial-based economic evaluations. The addition of discounting (with rate r ) is particularly straightforward, as it simply requires including the discounting factor e−ru within the integrands of equations (5) and (6). Then, further methodology to allow for the indirect comparison of marginal QALY measures could be developed, for example, with a weighting methodology similar to that of matching-adjusted indirect comparisons. 52 This would enable the indirect comparison of treatments and medicines in terms of QALYs as well, allowing for more effective and comprehensive health technology assessments. If sufficient data on disease progression were available, the methodology could be extended to accommodate dropout (and informative truncation of HRQL trajectories) due to other causes by fitting a competing risks joint model and updating the estimands in terms of cumulative incidence functions.53,54 The methodology can also be reframed within a Bayesian framework, allowing the incorporation of prior evidence, more complex model specifications, and, in principle, external treatment effects at the prediction step. Finally, recent deep learning approaches to joint longitudinal-survival modeling relaxed parametric assumptions to capture complex nonlinear patterns.55 –57 However, for health technology assessment, the parametric framework remains advantageous because it is interpretable, supports established uncertainty quantification, is computationally efficient for typical trial sizes, and aligns with current regulatory guidance.

In conclusion, a unified and extensible framework for the estimation of QALYs has been developed, accompanied by user-friendly Stata software for practical applications. Despite some limitations, this methodology has the potential to significantly affect clinical practice, health policy and future research. The framework relies on the timely and accurate collection of quality-of-life data in clinical trials, beyond the usual intercurrent events of disease progression, treatment discontinuation and so on. Therefore, we advocate for the collection of such data to inform timely and robust health technology assessments, thus enabling more effective decisions when bringing new medicines to patients.
