Impacts of affiliated stigma on depression and anxiety in Chinese parents of children with autism spectrum disorders: roles of parental burnout and spouse support
Authors: Wan Wang, Dongchuan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAlthough previous studies have proved parents of children with autism spectrum disorders (ASD) were at increased risks of depression and anxiety, but their associations with affiliated stigma and the underlying mechanism are still unclear. The present study, based on a sample of Chinese parents of ASD children, aimed to clarify the impacts of affiliated stigma on depression and anxiety, and roles of parental burnout and spouse support in these relationship.MethodsA survey was completed by 652 Chinese parents of children with ASD using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9, Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-Item Questionnaire, Affiliate Stigma Scale, Chinese Short Version of Parental Burnout Assessment, and the Support in Intimate Relationships Rating Scale.ResultsThe results shows that rates of moderately to extremely severe levels of depression and anxiety among Chinese parents of ASD children were 42.33% and 39.58%. Affiliated stigma was positively related to depression and anxiety. Parental burnout played a mediating role in the associations between affiliated stigma and depression and anxiety. Spouse support moderated the relations of affiliated stigma with depression and anxiety via parental burnout.ConclusionThis study highlights significant associations consistent with a model in which parental burnout may serve as a mediator in the relationships between affiliate stigma and symptoms of depression and anxiety. Furthermore, the findings point to the potential buffering role of spouse support within these relationships. These findings offer a foundation for developing and testing targeted interventions aimed at alleviating depressive and anxious symptoms among parents of children with ASD.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorders, Parents, Depression, Affiliated stigma, Parental burnout, Spouse support

Introduction

Providing care for children diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is an intricate and often overwhelming task due to the disorder’s diverse and persistent traits, the challenge of finding suitable care options, and the social behaviors that may not align with societal norms [1, 2]. The psychological impact on parents is profound, with a heightened risk of experiencing anxiety and depressive symptoms [2–6]. Extensive research has consistently shown that parents of children with ASD are more likely to exhibit higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms compared to those with typically developing children [5, 7–9]. For instance, a significant percentage of mothers of children with ASD reported depressive symptoms shortly after receiving the diagnosis, and a considerable proportion continued to experience these feelings well over a year later [10]. Another research showed that the prevalence rates of anxiety and depression were 72.4% and 49.6% among mothers of children with ASD [11]. A meta-analysis of 31 studies involving 9208 parents of ASD children has pointed out that that anxiety and depression were the most common emotional disorders among them, with median rates of 33.0% and 31.0%, respectively [12]. While parents of children with ASD may experience a range of mental health challenges including chronic stress, sleep disturbances, and reduced quality of life, anxiety and depression represent particularly prevalent and co-occurring conditions that critically impact parental functioning and well-being. Yet, the rates of anxiety and depression in Chinese parents of ASD children are still not well-documented. In addition, although it has been well established that raising an ASD child is the objective reason of anxious and depressive emotions, the key factors and underlying mechanism in the development of anxiety and depression are necessary to be further explored.

In this context, affiliate stigma emerges as a significant psychosocial stressor for parents of ASD children. AS refers to the internalization of negative evaluations and attitudes directed at themselves, which are not due to their own characteristics but rather their close relationships with other stigmatized individuals [13, 14]. These negative attitudes can stem from the public’s reaction to the atypical behaviors exhibited by ASD children in social settings, which may include blame, humiliation, neglect, or exclusion [13, 15]. The repercussions of stigma are not limited to the children themselves; they extend to their parents as well [16]. Parents of ASD children are often accused for passing on defective genes to their children and producing bad breeds of offspring [17], or are condemned unfairly for their ineffective parenting and failing to discipline their children [16, 18]. Examining affiliate stigma within a Chinese cultural context is particularly salient. Traditional Chinese values emphasize family reputation, social harmony, and collective face, meaning a child’s condition is often perceived as reflecting directly on the entire family. Furthermore, strong cultural emphasis on lineage and parental responsibility may intensify feelings of shame, self-blame, and failure among parents of children with ASD. These cultural dimensions likely amplify both the experience and the psychological impact of affiliate stigma, making its study in this population especially relevant. Unsurprisingly, experiences of affiliate stigma have been consistently linked to poorer mental health outcomes, including elevated levels of depression and anxiety [19, 20]. Yet, the specific processes through which this stigma translates into emotional distress warrant further investigation.

One potential explanatory mechanism is parental burnout, which is a set of symptoms caused by parental roles and chronic parenting stress, such as feeling overwhelmed or being emotionally distant from children [21]. Parenting a child is stressful and challenging, and parents may experience parental burnout when the burden of perceived stress exceeds their personal coping resources [4]. It has been revealed that 8% − 36% of parents experienced parental burnout [22, 23]. Parents of ASD children might experience higher levels of stress because caring for their children creates extra difficulties and challenges [24]. Numerous studies have underscored that parents of ASD children are more susceptible to higher degrees of parental burnout than parents of typically developing children [4, 22, 23, 25]. In turn, parental burnout itself is strongly associated with symptoms of depression and anxiety [26]. This implies that parental burnout could act as a critical mediator in the interplay between the stigma of affiliation and the development of depressive and anxious conditions. However, few studies have explored these associations in parents of ASD children, especially in Chinese samples. Therefore, it is necessary to verify the mediating role of parental burnout in the associations between affiliated stigma and depression and anxiety based on Chinese parents of ASD children.

The impact of these risk factors, however, could be mitigated by protective resources. Spousal support, as a key source of intimate and practical assistance, is recognized as a vital buffer against caregiving stress [27]. As one of the most important social psychological resources, spousal support can serve as protective mechanisms against stress and facilitate one’s ability to better cope with life challenges in parents of children diagnosed with ASD [27, 28]. Collaboratively caring for a child with special needs and sharing caregiving responsibilities with each other may be essential support for parents, and parenting challenges may not be a negative experience if the parents support each other [29]. A study on morthers of ASD children suggested that those who perceived spouses as a good source of support seemed to be relatively satisfied with caregiving demands [28]. On the contrary, parents who lacked spousal support appeared to be more vulnerable to mental health problems [28]. While the general protective role of social support is acknowledged [30], it remains unclear whether spousal support specifically can moderate the proposed pathway from affiliate stigma through parental burnout to emotional outcomes. This constitutes a significant gap, particularly in the Chinese cultural context where family dynamics play a central role.

The present study seeks to extend the existing literature in three key aspects. First, while the links between stigma, stress, and mental health are established, we aim to empirically test the mediating role of parental burnout, a syndrome of chronic parenting-specific exhaustion, in the relationship between affiliate stigma and depression/anxiety in a Chinese context. Second, we go beyond examining main effects to investigate whether spousal support buffers this entire mediated pathway, a nuanced interaction that has received limited attention. Third, by testing this integrated moderated mediation model, we attempt to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how risk and protective factors operate together through a key mechanism to influence parental mental health in a culturally salient setting.

To address these gaps, the present cross-sectional study investigated the interrelationships among affiliate stigma, depression, anxiety, parental burnout, and spousal support in a sample of Chinese parents of children with ASD. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) examine the associations of affiliated stigma and depression and anxiety; (2) test the potential mediating role of parental burnout in these associations; and (3) explore whether spousal support moderates these mediated pathways. Based on previous research, it was hypothesized:

H1: Affiliated stigma was positively related to depression and anxiety;

H1: Affiliated stigma was positively related to depression and anxiety;

H2: Parental burnout would mediate the positive associations between affiliate stigma and symptoms of depression and anxiety;

Methods

Participants

Participants in this study were parents of ASD children in China, recruited using a convenience sampling method from the outpatient clinic of a hospital in Zhengzhou city. Specifically, parents accompanying their children for assessment or intervention services were informed about the study by clinic staff and invited to participate if they met the inclusion criteria (being a primary caregiver of a child diagnosed with ASD).

In line with the Declaration of Helsinki and given ethics approval by the Ethics Committee for Scientific Research of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Grant No. 2023 − 132), the survey was conducted in 2023 using paper questionnaire. After reading the consent form and guidance of the survey, a total sample of 652 participants (110 males, 534 females) completed this survey. All respondents in this research completed an informed consent form. Participants’ ages ranged from 22 to 56 (mean age = 33.85; SD = 5.48). Measures

Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 was used to assess depressive symptoms over past 2 weeks [30]. It contains 9 items rated on a 4 Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). The total score ranges from 0 to 27 points. Participants with 10 or more total scores could be classified as moderate to extremely severe levels of depression [30]. The PHQ-9 has demonstrated great properties in Chinese samples [31, 32]. In our study, the Cronbach’s alpha of the PHQ-9 was 0.915.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-Item Questionnaire (GAD-7)

Anxiety symptoms were measured by the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-Item Questionnaire, which is a 7-item self-report measure examining the levels of anxiety in the past two weeks [33]. Participants are asked to answer the questions on a 4-point scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). The total score ranges from 0 to 21 points. Participants with 10 or more total scores could be classified as moderate to extremely severe levels of anxiety. The Chinese version of GAD-7 reported satisfactory properties in prior studies [31]. In our research, its Cronbach’s alpha was 0.911.

Affiliate Stigma Scale (ASS)

Affiliate stigma was measured using the 22-item ASS [13], which was specifically developed to assess internalized stigma among parents of children with developmental disabilities, making it highly relevant for our study population. Items are rated on a 4-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), with higher total scores indicating greater affiliate stigma. This scale achieved satisfactory internal consistency in prior research [15]. The Cronbach’s alpha of the ASS in current study was 0.964.

Chinese Short Version of Parental Burnout Assessment (S-PBA)

Parental burnout was measured by the Chinese Short Version of Parental Burnout Assessment [34], which was validated into a Chinese Version of the S-PBA [35]. This scale was chosen because it is a direct and psychometrically sound measure of the specific syndrome of parental burnout (e.g., emotional exhaustion related to parenting), which is central to our hypothesized mediation model. It contains 7 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (never) to 7 (every day). This assessment has been proved good reliability and validity among Chinese samples [36]. The Cronbach’s alpha of the ASS in current study was 0.967.

Support in Intimate Relationships Rating Scale (SIRRS)

Spouse support was assessed by the Support in Intimate Relationships Rating Scale. This 25-item scale was selected for its comprehensive assessment of four key support types (esteem/emotional, physical comfort, informational, and tangible), allowing a nuanced evaluation of the protective resource central to our moderation hypothesis [37]. Participants rate how often they receive each form of support from their spouse on a 5-point scale from 0 (never) to 4 (almost always). Higher scores indicate greater perceived support. Previous studies have demonstrated its strong reliability and validity [38, 39]. The Cronbach’s alpha of the SIRRS in current study was 0.982.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS 21.0. The zero-order Pearson’s correlations were computed to explore relations between variables. The mediating role of parental burnout in the association between affiliate stigma and depression/anxiety was tested using PROCESS macro v3.5, Model 4 (simple mediation) with 5,000 bootstrapped samples. Model 4 was chosen as it is the standard and most parsimonious method to test our hypothesis that the association between affiliate stigma and mental health symptoms operates indirectly through parental burnout. To examine whether spouse support buffers this indirect pathway, we used PROCESS Model 59 (moderated mediation), which is specifically designed to test if a moderator influences the strength of the indirect effect [40].

Discussion

The present study, based on a sample of Chinese parents of children with ASD, aimed to clarify depression and anxiety and their associations with affiliated stigma, parental burnout and spouse support. As expected, affiliate stigma was significantly associated with higher levels of depression and anxiety, both directly and indirectly through its association with parental burnout. Furthermore, spousal support emerged as a significant moderator, indicating that its presence may buffer the strength of the positive associations between affiliate stigma and these mental health symptoms.

In line with previous studies [19, 20], affiliated stigma was positively related to depression and anxiety. As we know, ASD children are often disapproved and discriminated by the public when they display social deficits and behavioral problems [41]. Due to biological and relational closeness to ASD children, parents of children with ASD may also be unreasonably criticized and blamed for causing the deficits and problems through passing on bad genes and failing to provide effective parenting [15]. These stigmatizing experiences typically leave parents of children with ASD feeling humiliated, judged, and isolated, and further increase the risks of parental depression and anxiety [19]. Internalization of public stigma is a significant psychological burden for parents of children with ASD across different cultural settings. Specially, this phenomenon is more salient in Chinese culture which places great importance on social identity, emphasizes group harmony, and focuses on family roots of developmental disabilities [15, 42]. Therefore, Chinese parents of ASD children are more likely to experience stigma and suffer from negative emotions than their Western counterparts.

As expected in the hypothesis, the results of our study proved the mediating role of parental burnout in the associations between affiliated stigma and depression and anxiety. This finding is consistent with a growing body of international literature that conceptualizes burnout as a key pathway through which chronic parenting stress erodes well-being [4, 25, 26]. On the one hand, affiliated stigma could cause burnout for parents of ASD children when their parenting resources are insufficient to cope with this stress. Consistent with prior studies, parents might experience higher levels of stress because of extra difficulties and challenges during caring for their ASD children [24]. As one of the main source of stressors, stigmatizing experience could cause increased parental burnout for them [2, 8, 21, 25, 43]. On the other hand, parental burnout could increase risks of parental depression and anxiety. The long-lasting burnout in caring for ASD children might lead to extreme exhaustion related to childcare, emotional distance from children, and loss of enjoyment and efficacy in the parenting role, and further increase parental anxiety and depression [26].

This study also confirmed that spouse support moderated the associations between affiliated stigma, depression, anxiety and parental burnout. Specifically, spouse support could weaken the relation between affiliated stigma and parental burnout. Previously, it has revealed that spouse support is a critical protective factor and important social psychological resource for mental health of people [27, 44]. Collaboratively caring for a child with special needs and sharing caregiving responsibilities with each other may be essential support for parents [29]. Spouse support can serve as protective mechanisms against parental stress and facilitate parents’ ability to better cope with stigma during caring for a child with ASD [28]. It is positively correlated with quality of life and psychological well-being, and negatively correlated with parental stress and depressive symptoms [44, 45]. Thus, parents of ASD children who were supported by their spouse reported lower levels of depression and anxiety. Previous studies have proved the moderating effects of perceived family support on parental depression [46]. Our study expanded this field of existing research by demonstrating the moderating role of spouse support on the associations between affiliated stigma and depression and anxiety. Our study identified spouse support as a significant buffer, which aligns with the central role of the family unit as a source of resilience in collectivist societies. This finding may differ from some Western studies where broader social networks or formal support services sometimes show stronger protective effects than partner support alone [24, 27, 47, 48]. This underscores the importance of developing family-centered intervention models within the Chinese context, where strengthening the spousal dyad may be a particularly strategy. However, it should be noted that the observed moderating effect, while statistically significant, was modest in magnitude. This suggests that spousal support is a meaningful but not overpowering protective factor within the studied model. Thus, the practical impact of this buffering effect is likely to be incremental given its modest size.

Practical and clinical implications

The findings of this study offer several important implications for clinical practice and support services aimed at enhancing the mental well-being of parents of children with ASD. First, the link between affiliate stigma and distress suggests that interventions should actively help parents cope with stigma, perhaps through psychoeducation to reframe self-blame and build a positive parental identity. Second, the mediating role of parental burnout highlights it as a critical, modifiable intervention target; reducing burnout through respite care, stress management, and peer support may alleviate depression and anxiety even when external stigma persists. Third, the buffering effect of spousal support underscores the value of a family-centered approach, encouraging clinical practices that strengthen couple communication, shared caregiving, and mutual emotional support to build family resilience against stigma. Together, these findings advocate for integrated support that addresses stigma, prevents burnout, and activates spousal cooperation.

Limitations

There are several limitations need to be point out. First of all, the most critical limitation is its cross-sectional design, which precludes drawing any causal conclusions. The observed associations among variables, while informative, do not establish directionality or causal mechanisms. Future longitudinal research is essential to examine the temporal dynamics and potential causal pathways linking affiliate stigma, parental burnout, spousal support, and parental depression and anxiety. Secondly, this study did not account for several key child-level clinical variables that are known to influence parental well-being, such as the time since the child’s ASD diagnosis, the severity of ASD symptoms, and the child’s adaptive functioning level. The absence of these factors limits our ability to ascertain whether the observed associations are independent of core clinical characteristics or to explore their potential confounding or moderating effects. Future research should incorporate these clinical variables to refine the proposed model and better understand their role within these complex relationships. Thirdly, the very high correlations observed between depression, anxiety, and parental burnout warrant careful consideration. This substantial shared variance indicates significant conceptual overlap, which may reflect intertwined symptomology or common methodological variance. While the hypothesized mediation model is supported, these strong intercorrelations necessitate a cautious interpretation of the unique mediating role of burnout, and future research with more differentiated measures would be valuable to disentangle these constructs further. Forthly, it is important to note that recruitment from a single clinical center may affect the generalizability of the findings. While this sample provides valuable insights into the experiences of parents seeking services in a regional urban hospital setting, it may not fully represent all Chinese parents of children with ASD, particularly those in other geographical locations or care contexts. Finally, all constructs were measured via parent self-report at the same time point. This methodological approach introduces the possibility of reporting biases (e.g., social desirability) and, more importantly, common method variance, which could inflate the observed relationships between variables. Future research employing multi-informant assessments (e.g., partner ratings) or objective measures would help mitigate this limitation and provide a more robust evaluation of the proposed associations.

Conclusions

In summary, based on a sample of Chinese parents of ASD children, this cross-sectional study reveals reveals significant associations consistent with a model in which parental burnout may mediate the relationships between affiliate stigma and symptoms of depression and anxiety. The findings also point to the potential buffering role of spouse support within these relationships. These results offer preliminary support for an integrative theoretical framework linking affiliate stigma, parental burnout, spousal support, and parental mental health. However, due to the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn. The proposed pathways and interactive effects require verification through longitudinal or intervention studies. Nonetheless, the current findings highlight promising intervention targets. Future research should investigate whether strategies aimed at mitigating affiliate stigma and parental burnout, while enhancing spousal support, could contribute to alleviating depressive and anxious symptoms in this parent population.
