Lisa Sauermann, Zixuan Xu

Abstract
. How many hyperplanes in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} are needed in order to slice every edge of the n n -dimensional hypercube with vertex set { ± 1 } n \{\pm 1\}^{n} ? Here, we say that a hyperplane H ⊆ ℝ n H\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} slices an edge of the hypercube if it contains exactly one interior point of the edge. The problem of determining the minimum possible size of a collection of hyperplanes in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , such that every edge of the hypercube is sliced by at least one of these hyperplanes, is more than 50 years old and has been studied by many researchers. We prove that, for sufficiently large n n , at least Ω ( n 13 / 19 log − 32 / 19 n ) \Omega(n^{13/19}\log^{-32/19}n) hyperplanes are needed, improving upon the best previous lower bound Ω ( n 2 / 3 log − 4 / 3 n ) \Omega(n^{2/3}\log^{-4/3}n) due to Klein.



1.
Introduction

The
n
-dimensional hypercube has vertex set
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and edges between pairs of vertices differing in exactly one coordinate.
We say that a hyperplane
H\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
slices an edge of the
n
-dimensional hypercube if
H
contains exactly one interior point of the edge. In other words, letting
vv^{\prime}
be an edge with endpoints
v,v^{\prime}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and
H
be a hyperplane defined as
H=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle a,x\rangle=b\}
, the hyperplane
H
is said to slice the edge
vv^{\prime}
if
\langle a,v\rangle-b
and
\langle a,v^{\prime}\rangle-b
are both nonzero and have different signs.

It is natural to ask how many hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are needed in order to slice every edge of the
n
-dimensional hypercube. More formally, what is the minimum possible size of a hyperplane collection
\mathcal{H}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that every edge of the
n
-dimensional hypercube
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is sliced by at least one hyperplane in
\mathcal{H}
? This problem has attracted the attention of many researchers over the past 50 years
[
10
,
7
,
5
,
1
,
12
,
6
]
, and has applications to the study of perceptrons
[
10
]
and of threshold circuits for parity
[
11
,
14
]
.

It is conjectured that the minimum possible size of a hyperplane collection
\mathcal{H}
slicing every edge of the
n
-dimensional hypercube is
\Theta(n)
, and the current best known upper bound is
\lceil(5/6)n\rceil
given by a construction of Paterson (see
[
12
]
). So far, this conjecture is only known to be true under some very specific assumptions on the form of the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
. It is not hard to show that at least
n
hyperplanes are needed if for the hyperplanes one can choose normal vectors all of whose entries are non-negative, see
[
1
]
or
[
6
]
. The conjecture is also known to be true in the case where all hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
have normal vectors in
\{1,-1\}^{n}
following a result of Alon–Bergmann–Coppersmith–Odlyzko
[
2
]
. Recently, it was shown in
[
13
]
that the conjecture also holds when all hyperplanes have normal vectors in
\{-C,\dots,C\}^{n}
for a fixed integer
C>0
.

In the general setting, O’Neil
[
10
]
showed that any collection
\mathcal{H}
of hyperplanes slicing every edge of the hypercube must have size
|\mathcal{H}|\geqslant\Omega(\sqrt{n})
. Yehuda and Yehudayoff
[
14
]
improved this lower bound to
|\mathcal{H}|\geqslant\Omega(n^{0.51})
. A few years ago, Klein
[
9
]
showed
|\mathcal{H}|\geqslant\Omega(n^{2/3}\log^{-4/3}n)
, which has been the best known lower bound.

In this paper, we improve upon this, showing the following new lower bound.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a collection of hyperplanes such that every edge of the
n
-dimensional hypercube
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is sliced by some hyperplane in
\mathcal{H}
. For
n
sufficiently large, we have

|\mathcal{H}|>\frac{n^{13/19}}{10^{11}\log^{32/19}n}.

Theorem

1.1
also implies a new lower bound for the number of wires in depth-two threshold circuits computing the parity function, see
[
14
]
for more details.

2.
Proof Overview

We start by outlining the main proof strategy, highlighting the key components of the proof. Our proof combines ideas from
[
14
]
and
[
9
]
together with several new ideas.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\{H_{1},\dots,H_{k}\}
be the collection of hyperplanes, where
H_{i}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle v_{i},x\rangle=\lambda_{i}\}
has normal vector
v_{i}=(v_{i1},\dots,v_{in})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for each
i\in[k]
. Note that rescaling the coefficients of the hyperplane equation does not change the slicing properties of the hyperplane, so we may further assume without loss of generality that
||v_{i}||_{2}=1
for all
i\in[k]
. Then our goal is to find a point
X\in[-1/2,1/2]^{n}
that is far away from most of the hyperplanes. To be slightly more precise, we want to find
X\in[-1/2,1/2]^{n}
such that

|\langle X,v_{i}\rangle-\lambda_{i}|>5\sqrt{\log n}

holds for all but at most
O(\sqrt{n})
indices
i\in[k]
. We can then randomly “round ”
X\in[-1/2,1/2]^{n}
to a vertex
z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
(to be precise, we take the entries
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\in\{\pm 1\}
of
z
to be independent, with distributions chosen in such a way that
\mathbb{E}[z_{i}]=X_{i}
for each
i
), and consider a uniformly random edge adjacent to
z
. We claim that with positive probability this edge is not sliced by any of the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
.

Indeed, with high probability, “rounding”
X\in[-1/2,1/2]^{n}
to a vertex
z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
changes the value of
\langle X,v_{i}\rangle
by no more than
3\sqrt{\log n}
for each
i\in[k]
. So with high probability
z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is at distance at least
2\sqrt{\log n}
from all but at most
O(\sqrt{n})
hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
(more precisely, we have
|\langle z,v_{i}\rangle-\lambda_{i}|>2\sqrt{\log n}
for all of the
k-O(\sqrt{n})
hyperplanes
H_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
with
|\langle X,v_{i}\rangle-\lambda_{i}|>5\sqrt{\log n}
). A hyperplane
H_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
at distance at least
2\sqrt{\log n}
from
z
cannot slice any edge adjacent to
z
, because any adjacent vertex
z^{\prime}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
must satisfy
|\langle z,v_{i}\rangle-\langle z^{\prime},v_{i}\rangle|\leqslant 2
, so
\langle z,v_{i}\rangle-\lambda_{i}
and
\langle z^{\prime},v_{i}\rangle-\lambda_{i}
cannot have different signs. So the only hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
possibly slicing any edge adjacent to
z
are the ones that are close to
X
. Using the Erdős–Littlewood–Offord theorem, one can show that a given hyperplane slices a uniformly random edge adjacent to the random rounding
z
of a given point
X\in[-1/2,1/2]^{n}
with probability at most
O(1/\sqrt{n})
. Since there are at most
O(\sqrt{n})
hyperplanes close to
X
, picking appropriate constants, a union bound implies that with positive probability a random edge adjacent to
z
is not sliced by any of the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
. The details of this part are given in
Section

4
.

So far, the strategy described above was employed in both
[
14
]
and
[
9
]
. The key part of the problem is to construct a point
X\in[-1/2,1/2]^{n}
that is close to only few of the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
. For simplicity, let us assume that for each
i\in[k]
, the hyperplane normal vector
v_{i}
satisfies
||v_{i}||_{\infty}\leqslant 10/\sqrt{n}
. In
[
14
]
,
X
was chosen by applying Bang’s lemma (originating from his solution
[
3
]
to Tarski’s plank problem) to the
k\times n
matrix with rows
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}
. On the other hand, in
[
9
]
,
X
is taken to be a random linear combination of the vectors
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}
. More specifically,

X=10^{-2}\sqrt{\frac{n}{k\log n}}\sum_{i\in[k]}\alpha_{i}v_{i},\quad\quad\alpha_{i}\sim U[-1,1]\text{ independent for all }i\in[k],

where
U[-1,1]
denotes the uniform distribution over the real interval
[-1,1]
and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}
are independent. By our assumption that
||v_{i}||_{\infty}\leqslant 10/\sqrt{n}
for each
i\in[k]
and the Chernoff bound, with high probability
X
is contained in
[-1/2,1/2]^{n}
. For any
i\in[k]
, the probability that
|\langle X,v_{i}\rangle-\lambda_{i}|\leqslant 5\sqrt{\log n}
is at most
O(\sqrt{k}\log n/\sqrt{n})
. So the expected number of such indices
i
is
O(k^{3/2}\log n/\sqrt{n})
. If
k=\tilde{O}(n^{2/3})
where
\tilde{\Omega}(\cdot)
ignores logarithm factors, then
O(k^{3/2}\log n/\sqrt{n})\leqslant O(\sqrt{n})
. Thus, Klein’s proof in
[
9
]
gives a bound of
k=\tilde{\Omega}(n^{2/3})
for the number of hyperplanes in the collection
\mathcal{H}
.

In our proof, we further develop Klein’s strategy from
[
9
]
. We first define
X_{0}
similarly as in Klein’s
[
9
]
proof:

X_{0}=\rho_{0}\sum_{i\in[k]}\alpha_{i}v_{i},\quad\quad\alpha_{i}\sim U[-1,1]\text{ independent for all }i\in[k]

for a suitably chosen parameter
\rho_{0}
. Then for an outcome of
X_{0}
, we say that
i\in[k]
is
bad
if
|\langle X_{0},v_{i}\rangle-\lambda_{i}|\leqslant 10\sqrt{\log n}
, i.e. if
H_{i}
is “close” to
X_{0}
. We then take
X
to be
X=X_{0}+X_{1}
where

X_{1}=\rho_{1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[k]\\
i\text{ bad}\end{subarray}}\beta_{i}v_{i},\quad\quad\beta_{i}\sim U[-1,1]\text{ independent for all bad }i\in[k]

for a suitably chosen parameter
\rho_{1}
. Intuitively, we sample
\beta_{i}
for the bad indices
i\in[k]
and add
\rho_{1}\beta_{i}v_{i}
to
X_{0}
to attempt to push
X_{0}
away from the hyperplanes it is close to. However, the main difficulty comes from analyzing the expected number of hyperplanes close to
X
, since adding the term
X_{1}
might push
X_{0}
towards hyperplanes that were originally far away. The full details are given in
Section

5
.

To remove the extra assumption that
||v_{i}||_{\infty}\leqslant 10/\sqrt{n}
for all
i\in[k]
, we use the matrix decomposition given in
[
14
]
and the notion of vectors containing many scales introduced in
[
14
]
(see
Section

3
).

3.
Preliminaries

Notation.
We write
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
. All logarithms are to base
e
unless specified otherwise. We use
U([a,b])
to denote the uniform distribution over the real interval
[a,b]
. For a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a subset
S\subseteq[n]
, we use
v|_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
to denote the vector
v
restricted to the subset of coordinates in
S
. For two sets
A
and
B
, we use

to denote the set of vectors indexed by elements in
B
with entries in
A
. When writing
x\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, we always mean
x
is uniformly distributed in
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.

We first recall the useful notion of vectors containing many scales introduced in
[
14
]
. For convenience, we will state the definitions and corresponding lemmas with explicit constants. The proofs of all the lemmas stated in this section are either standard or follow directly from
[
14

Definition 3.1

(Distribution
\mu_{p}
)
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a vector with
||p||_{\infty}\leqslant 1
. We define the distribution
\mu_{p}
over
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
by taking independent random entries
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\{\pm 1\}
with distributions given by

x_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{with probability }(1+p_{i})/2\\
-1&\text{with probability }(1-p_{i})/2\end{cases}\quad\text{for all }i\in[n].

In particular, note that that for any
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
||p||_{\infty}\leqslant 1
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu_{p}}[x_{i}]=p_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
.

Definition 3.2

(
[
14
]
)
.

For
\delta>0
and a positive integer
s
, we say that a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

contains
s
scales of size at least
\delta
if there exists disjoint subsets
I_{1},\dots,I_{s}\subseteq[n]
such that
||v|_{I_{s}}||_{2}\geqslant\delta
and for
i\in[s-1]
we have

||v|_{I_{i}}||_{2}\geqslant 100||v|_{I_{i+1}}||_{2}.

The following lemma shows strong anticoncentration properties for vectors containing many scales.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
14
]
)
.

For
\delta>0
and a positive integer
s\geqslant 100
, let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a vector containing
s
scales of size at least
10\delta
. Then for any
b\in\mathbb{R}
and uniformly random
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, we have

\mathbb{P}[|\langle x,v\rangle-b|\leqslant\delta]\leqslant e^{-s/100}.

We need the Chernoff–Hoeffding inequality.

Lemma 3.4

(Chernoff–Hoeffding inequality
[
8
]
)
.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{r},b_{1},\dots,b_{r}
be real numbers with
a_{i}<b_{i}
for all
i\in[r]
, and let
X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
be independent random variables such that
a_{i}\leqslant X_{i}\leqslant b_{i}
for all
i\in[r]
. Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}
, then for all
t>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}[|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\geqslant t]\leqslant 2\exp\Big(-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{i\in[r]}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}\Big).

We need the Erdős–Littlewood–Offord theorem for biased linear forms.

Theorem 3.5

(Erdős–Littlewood–Offord)
.

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
||p||_{\infty}\leqslant 1/2
. Let
t>0
and let
m\geqslant 1
be the number of indices
i\in[n]
such that
|v_{i}|\geqslant t
. Then, for any
b\in\mathbb{R}
, for a random vector
x\sim\mu_{p}
, we have

\mathbb{P}[|\langle x,v\rangle-b|<t]\leqslant\frac{10}{\sqrt{m}}.

We also need a Littlewood–Offord type lemma for continuous random variables, which follows from a more general statement on the anticoncentration of sums of log-concave independent random variables due to Bobkov and Chistyakov
[
4
]
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
be real numbers with
a_{i}<b_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
, and let
X_{i}\sim U[a_{i},b_{i}]
be independent random variables for all
i\in[n]
. Define
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. Then for any
t>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathbb{P}[|X-b|<t]\leqslant\frac{2t}{\sqrt{\textup{Var}(X)}}.

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

Let
n
be sufficiently large, and let
\mathcal{H}=\{H_{1},\dots,H_{k}\}
be the collection of hyperplanes. By slightly “wiggling” the hyperplanes, we may assume without loss of generality that every variable appears with nonzero coefficient in every hyperplane equation, and that the absolute values of all coefficients in all the hyperplane equations are distinct. Suppose for contradiction that
k\leqslant 10^{-11}n^{13/19}\log^{-32/19}n
. Our goal is to show that there exists an edge of the hypercube
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
that is not sliced by any of the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
.

For each
i\in[k]
, we can write
H_{i}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle a_{i},x\rangle-b_{i}=0\}
for some vector
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and some
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. By our assumption, all entries of the vectors
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
are nonzero. So we can apply
4.1
to the
k\times n
matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
with rows
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
. We obtain partitions
[k]=K_{1}\sqcup K_{2}
and
[n]=N_{1}\sqcup N_{2}
with
|N_{2}|\leqslant n/2
and a row rescaling
A^{\prime}
of
A
satisfying the conditions in
4.1
. Since rescaling the hyperplane equations does not affect the hyperplanes and their slicing properties, we may assume without loss of generality that
A=A^{\prime}
(i.e. for each
i\in[k]
, we may assume that the original hyperplane equation
\langle a_{i},x\rangle-b_{i}=0
describing
H_{i}
is scaled in such a way that
a_{i}
agrees with the
i
-th row of
A^{\prime}
).

Most of the work goes into the following proposition and we postpone its proof to

Proposition 4.2
.

For

sufficiently large and

(1)

For

-th row vector

of

.

(2)

For

.

Proof.

We start by finding
w\in\{\pm 1\}^{N_{2}}
satisfying (ii), this argument is identical to the proof of

w\in\{\pm 1\}^{N_{2}}

Now, we can find the desired

to the submatrix
A[K_{1}\times N_{1}]
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{1}}
defined by taking
\lambda_{i}=b_{i}-\langle a_{i}|_{N_{2}},w\rangle
for all
i\in K_{1}
. First note that for

and

we have

hold for the matrix

, because of conditions (1) and (2) in

(recalling that

). In particular, for assumption (2), note that for each

, we can find a point

such that for all but at most

∎

Let us now fix
X\in[-1/2,1/2]^{N_{1}}
and
w\in\{\pm 1\}^{N_{2}}
as in
Lemma

4.3
. Let us consider a random vector
y\sim\mu_{X}
and, independently from
y
, a uniformly random index
h\in N_{1}
. Finally, define
z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
by taking
z|_{N_{1}}=y
and
z|_{N_{2}}=w
, and let

be obtained from
z
flipping coordinate
h
. We will show that with positive probability, the hypercube edge
zz^{\prime}
is not sliced by any of the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
.

First, recall that in order for
zz^{\prime}
to be sliced by a hyperplane
H_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
, the expressions
\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}
and
\langle a_{i},z^{\prime}\rangle-b_{i}
must both be nonzero and have different signs. As
|(\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i})-(\langle a_{i},z^{\prime}\rangle-b_{i})|=2|a_{ih}|
, this in particular implies that
|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|<2|a_{ih}|
. So it suffices to show that with positive probability we have
|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|\geqslant 2|a_{ih}|
for all
i\in[k]
.

For
i\in K_{2}
, we always have

|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|=|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle+\langle a_{i}|_{N_{2}},w\rangle-b_{i}|\geqslant|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{2}},w\rangle-b_{i}|-|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle|\geqslant 2\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{n}>2\geqslant 2|a_{ih}|,

using (ii) and the fact that
|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle|\leqslant||a_{i}|_{N_{1}}||_{2}\cdot||y||_{2}=1\cdot\sqrt{|N_{1}|}\leqslant\sqrt{n}
by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Let

K_{1}^{*}:=\Big\{i\in K_{1}\,\Big|\,|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},X\rangle+\langle a_{i}|_{N_{2}},w\rangle-b_{i}|\leqslant 4\sqrt{\log n}\Big\},

then by (i) we have
|K_{1}^{*}|\leqslant\sqrt{n}/200
. We first show that any hyperplane
i\in K_{1}\setminus K_{1}^{*}
is very unlikely to satisfy
|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|<2|a_{ih}|
.

Claim 4.4
.

For any
i\in K_{1}\setminus K_{1}^{*}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\Big[|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|<2|a_{ih}|\Big]\leqslant\frac{2}{n^{4}}.

Proof.

Note that
|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},X\rangle+\langle a_{i}|_{N_{2}},w\rangle-b_{i}|>4\sqrt{\log n}
, since
i\not\in K_{1}^{*}
. Whenever
|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|<2|a_{ih}|
, we in particular have
|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle+\langle a_{i}|_{N_{2}},w\rangle-b_{i}|=|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|<2|a_{ih}|\leqslant 2<\sqrt{\log n}
and therefore

|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},X\rangle-\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle|\geqslant|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},X\rangle+\langle a_{i}|_{N_{2}},w\rangle-b_{i}|-|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle+\langle a_{i}|_{N_{2}},w\rangle-b_{i}|\geqslant 3\sqrt{\log n}.

Noting that
\mathbb{E}[\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle]=\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},X\rangle
as
y\sim\mu_{X}
, and using
Lemma

3.4
, we can conclude (also recalling
||a_{i}|_{N_{1}}||_{2}=1
)

\mathbb{P}\Big[|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|<2|a_{ih}|\Big]\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Big[|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle-\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},X\rangle|\geqslant 3\sqrt{\log n}\Big]\leqslant 2e^{-(3\sqrt{\log n})^{2}/2}\leqslant 2e^{-4\log n}=\frac{2}{n^{4}}.\qed

On the other hand, the following claim gives a weaker general bound on the probability for an index
i\in K_{1}
(in particular, an index
i\in K_{1}^{*}
) to satisfy
|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|<2|a_{ih}|
.

Claim 4.5
.

For any
i\in K_{1}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\Big[|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|<2|a_{ih}|\Big]\leqslant\frac{100}{\sqrt{n}}.

Proof.

Fix
i\in K_{1}
. Setting
b=b_{i}-\langle a_{i}|_{N_{2}},w\rangle
, we have
\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}=\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle+\langle a_{i}|_{N_{2}},w\rangle-b_{i}=\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle-b
. So we have
|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|<2|a_{ih}|
if and only if
|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle-b|<2|a_{ih}|
.

For
m=1,2,\dots,|N_{1}|
, we consider the event that
|a_{ih}|
is the
m
-th largest entry in
a_{i}|_{N_{1}}
in absolute value. This event only depends on the outcome of the uniformly random index
h\in N_{1}
and has probability
1/|N_{1}|
. Conditioning on this event, we can apply
Theorem

3.5
to the random vector
y\sim\mu_{X}
, observing that the vector
a_{i}|_{N_{1}}
has at least
m
entries of absolute value at least
|a_{ih}|
. Applying
Theorem

3.5
to two intervals of size
2|a_{ih}|
covering an interval of size
4|a_{ih}|
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\Big[|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle-b|<2|a_{ih}|\,\Big|\,\text{$|a_{ih}|$ is the $m$-th largest absolute value in $a_{i}|_{N_{1}}$}\Big]\leqslant\frac{20}{\sqrt{m}}.

and hence

\mathbb{P}\Big[|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle-b|<2|a_{ih}|\text{ and $|a_{ih}|$ is the $m$-th largest absolute value in $a_{i}|_{N_{1}}$}\Big]\leqslant\frac{1}{|N_{1}|}\cdot\frac{20}{\sqrt{m}}.

Summing this over all
m=1,2,\dots,|N_{1}|
yields

\mathbb{P}\Big[|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|<2|a_{ih}|\Big]=\mathbb{P}\Big[|\langle a_{i}|_{N_{1}},y\rangle-b|<2|a_{ih}|\Big]\leqslant\sum_{m=1}^{|N_{1}|}\frac{1}{|N_{1}|}\cdot\frac{20}{\sqrt{m}}=\frac{20}{|N_{1}|}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{1}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}.

Therefore, recalling that
|N_{1}|=n-|N_{2}|\geqslant n/2
, we can conclude

\mathbb{P}\Big[|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|<2|a_{ih}|\Big]\leqslant\frac{20}{|N_{1}|}\Big(1+\sum_{m=2}^{N_{1}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\Big)\leqslant\frac{20}{|N_{1}|}\Big(1+\int_{1}^{|N_{1}|}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\Big)\leqslant\frac{20}{|N_{1}|}\cdot 2\sqrt{|N_{1}|}=\frac{40}{\sqrt{|N_{1}|}}\leqslant\frac{100}{\sqrt{n}}.\qed

Combining
4.4
and
4.5
, the union bound shows that for
n
sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big[|\langle a_{i},z\rangle-b_{i}|\geqslant 2|a_{ih}|\text{ for all }i\in[k]\Big]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\!\!\!\!\sum_{i\in K_{1}\setminus K_{1}^{*}}\!\frac{2}{n^{4}}-\sum_{i\in K_{1}^{*}}\frac{100}{\sqrt{n}}\geqslant 1-n\cdot\frac{2}{n^{4}}-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{200}\cdot\frac{100}{\sqrt{n}}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{n^{3}}>0.\qed

In the next definition, for an outcome of

It might be helpful to always think of an index

Definition 5.2

For an outcome of

|\langle X_{0},v_{i}\rangle-\lambda_{i}|\leqslant 10\sqrt{\log m}.

Now we can define

Definition 5.3
.

Given an outcome of

be defined as

X_{1}=\rho_{1}\sum_{i\text{ bad}}\beta_{i}v_{i},\quad\beta_{i}\sim U([-1,1])\text{ for all bad $i\in[\ell]$},

by first sampling

5.1

given the outcome of

.

Note that by

and

as well as

. The next lemma shows that with high probability we have

Lemma 5.4
.

Let

be defined as in

5.3
, then

\mathbb{P}[||X||_{\infty}\leqslant 1/2]\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{m^{2}}.

Proof.

For
j\in[m]
, let

,
X_{0}(j)
,

denote the

-th coordinate of

,
X_{0}
, and
X_{1}
, respectively. Then note that by definition

with independent

for all

. In particular,

and

is a sum of

Thus by

3.4

\mathbb{P}[|X_{0}(j)|>1/3]\leqslant 2\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{3^{2}}\cdot\frac{2\cdot 10^{2}\log m}{4}\Big)\leqslant\frac{2}{m^{5}}.

\mathbb{P}[||X_{0}||_{\infty}>1/3]\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}[|X_{0}(j)|>1/3]\leqslant\frac{2}{m^{4}}.

Now for any fixed outcome of

, we have

X_{1}(j)=\rho_{1}\sum_{i\text{ bad}}\beta_{i}v_{ij},

with independent
\beta_{i}\sim U([-1,1])
for all bad

. Therefore,

, and

\sum_{i\text{ bad}}(2\rho_{1}v_{ij})^{2}\leqslant 4\rho_{1}^{2}||v_{*j}||_{2}^{2}\leqslant 4\cdot m^{2/19}\log^{-2/19}m\cdot 10^{-2}m^{-6/19}\log^{-13/19}m=4\cdot 10^{-2}m^{-4/19}\log^{-15/19}m.

Again by

\mathbb{P}[|X_{1}(j)|>1/6\mid X_{0}]\leqslant 2\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{6^{2}}\cdot\frac{2\cdot 10^{2}m^{4/19}\log^{15/19}}{4}\Big)\leqslant 2e^{-m^{4/19}\log^{15/19}}\leqslant\frac{1}{m^{5}}.

Taking a union bound over all

\mathbb{P}[||X_{1}||_{\infty}>1/6\mid X_{0}]\leqslant\frac{1}{m^{4}}.

Thus for

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[||X||_{\infty}\leqslant 1/2]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{P}[||X_{0}||_{\infty}\leqslant 1/3]\cdot\inf_{X_{0}}\mathbb{P}[||X_{1}||_{\infty}\leqslant 1/6\mid X_{0}]

\displaystyle\geqslant\Big(1-\frac{2}{m^{4}}\Big)\Big(1-\frac{1}{m^{4}}\Big)\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{m^{2}}.\qed

Analysis

Proof.

Fix an outcome of
X_{0}
and suppose
j
is not activated and not bad. Let
h\geqslant 0
be the smallest integer such that for
t=2^{h}
, we have
|\langle X_{0},v_{j}\rangle-\lambda_{j}|\leqslant 10t\sqrt{\log m}
. Then, since
j
is not bad, we have
h\geqslant 1
and
t\geqslant 2
. In particular, by the minimality of
h
, we can conclude
|\langle X_{0},v_{j}\rangle-\lambda_{j}|>10\cdot 2^{h-1}\sqrt{\log m}=5t\sqrt{\log m}
.

We claim that
\frac{1}{3}\rho_{1}^{2}\sum_{i\text{ bad}}\leqslant t^{2}/4
. If
h\leqslant\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil
, this follows from the fact that
j
is not
t
-activated. If
h>\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil
(and hence
t>m
), this simply follows because
\frac{1}{3}\rho_{1}^{2}\sum_{i\text{ bad}}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{3}\rho_{1}^{2}\ell\leqslant m^{2}/4<t^{2}/4
.

If
j
is close, we must have
|\langle X_{1},v_{j}\rangle|\geqslant|\langle X_{0},v_{j}\rangle-\lambda_{j}|-|\langle X,v_{j}\rangle-\lambda_{j}|>(5t-5)\sqrt{\log m}\geqslant 2t\sqrt{\log m}
. On the other hand, we have
4\rho_{1}^{2}\sum_{i\text{ bad}}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}\leqslant 12(t^{2}/4)=3t^{2}
. Thus, by
Lemma

3.4
, we have

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close}\mid X_{0}]\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Big[|\langle X_{1},v_{j}\rangle|>2t\sqrt{\log m}\,\Big|\,X_{0}\Big]\leqslant 2e^{-2(2t\sqrt{\log m})^{2}/3t^{2}}\leqslant\frac{2}{m^{2}}.\qed

We also compute the probability that
j
is close and activated, which will be useful.

Lemma 5.13
.

For
j\in[\ell]
we have

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close and activated}]\leqslant\frac{1600\log^{2}m}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{j}}}.

Proof.

For any
h=0,\dots,\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil
, setting
t=2^{h}
, by
5.11
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close and $t$-activated}]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is $t$-activated}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close}\mid j\text{ is $t$-activated}]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)]\cdot\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close}\mid j\text{ is $t$-activated}]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{40t\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{j}}}\cdot\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close}\mid j\text{ is $t$-activated}].

To bound the second factor
\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close}\mid j\text{ is $t$-activated}]
, we fix an outcome of
X_{0}
so that
j
is
t
-activated. Let
\lambda_{j}^{\prime}=\lambda_{j}-\langle X_{0},v_{j}\rangle
, and note that then
\langle X,v_{j}\rangle-\lambda_{j}=\langle X_{0}+X_{1},v_{j}\rangle-\lambda_{j}=\langle X_{1},v_{j}\rangle-\lambda_{j}^{\prime}
. Therefore, by
Lemma

3.6
and the fact that
\textup{Var}[\langle X_{1},v_{j}\rangle\mid X_{0}]=\frac{1}{3}\rho_{1}^{2}\sum_{i\text{ bad}}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}>t^{2}/4
, we have

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close}\mid X_{0}]\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Big[\langle X_{1},v_{j}\rangle-\lambda_{j}^{\prime}|\leqslant 5\sqrt{\log m}\,\Big|\,X_{0}\Big]\leqslant\frac{10\sqrt{\log m}}{\sqrt{t^{2}/4}}=\frac{20\sqrt{\log m}}{t}

for any fixed outcome of
X_{0}
such that
j
is
t
-activated. So we can conclude that

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close}\mid j\text{ is $t$-activated}]\leqslant\frac{20\sqrt{\log m}}{t}

and hence

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close and $t$-activated}]\leqslant\frac{40t\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{j}}}\cdot\frac{20\sqrt{\log m}}{t}=\frac{800\log m}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{j}}}.

Now, taking a union bound over
h=0,\dots,\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil
, for
m
sufficiently large we have

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close and activated}]\leqslant\sum_{h=0}^{\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil}\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close and $2^{h}$-activated}]\leqslant\sum_{h=0}^{\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil}\frac{800\log m}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{j}}}\leqslant\frac{1600\log^{2}m}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{j}}}.\qed

The rest of the section will be devoted to bounding the expected number of close indices
j\in[\ell]
by considering five different cases, as listed in the following claim.

Claim 5.14
.

For an outcome of
X_{0}
, every index
j\in[\ell]
falls into (at least) one of the following types:

(1)

j
is bad;

(2)

j
is not bad and not activated;

(3)

j
is activated and heavy;

(4)

j
is light and near bad;

(5)

j
is activated and not near bad.

Proof.

Fix an outcome of
X_{0}
, and let
j\in[\ell]
. If
j
is bad then it falls into (1), so we may assume
j
is not bad. Then if
j
is not activated, it falls into (2), so we may assume
j
is activated. If
j
is heavy, then it falls into (3), so we can assume
j
is light. If
j
is near bad, then it falls into (4). Thus, finally if
j
is not near bad, it falls into (5).
∎

Note that the expected number of close indices
j
of type (2), i.e. where
j
is not bad and not activated, can be bounded using
Lemma

5.12
. The other four types will be covered in the following four subsections.

5.2.1.
j
bad

The following lemma can be used to bound the expected number of close indices that are bad.

Lemma 5.15
.

For
j\in[\ell]
, we have

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is bad and close}]\leqslant\frac{800\log m}{\rho_{0}\rho_{1}}.

Proof.

For an outcome of
X_{0}
, if
j\in[\ell]
is bad, then we have

\textup{Var}[\langle X_{1},v_{j}\rangle\mid X_{0}]=\frac{1}{3}\rho_{1}^{2}\sum_{i\text{ bad}}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}\rho_{1}^{2}\langle v_{j},v_{j}\rangle^{2}=\frac{1}{3}\rho_{1}^{2}.

Thus, defining
\lambda_{j}^{\prime}:=\lambda_{j}-\langle X_{0},v_{j}\rangle
and applying
Lemma

3.6
, we have

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close}\mid X_{0}]\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Big[|\langle X_{1},v_{j}\rangle-\lambda_{j}^{\prime}|\leqslant 5\sqrt{\log m}\,\Big|\,X_{0}\Big]\leqslant\frac{10\sqrt{\log m}}{\sqrt{\rho_{1}^{2}/3}}\leqslant\frac{20\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{1}}

for any outcome of
X_{0}
such that
j
is bad. This implies that

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close}\mid j\text{ is bad}]\leqslant\frac{20\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{1}}.

Now, by
5.7
and the fact that
S_{j}\geqslant 1
, we have

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is bad and close}]=\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is bad}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close}\mid j\text{ is bad}]\leqslant\frac{40\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}}\cdot\frac{20\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{1}}=\frac{800\log m}{\rho_{0}\rho_{1}}.\qed

5.2.2.
j
activated and heavy

We bound the expected number of close indices that are heavy and activated. Recall that an index
j\in[\ell]
is heavy if
S_{j}\geqslant\delta^{2}
.

Lemma 5.16
.

The expected number of close indices that are heavy and activated is at most
1600\ell\log^{2}m/(\rho_{0}\delta)
.

Proof.

Suppose
j\in[\ell]
is heavy, then by
Lemma

5.13
and the fact that
S_{j}\geqslant\delta^{2}
, we have

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close and activated}]\leqslant\frac{1600\log^{2}m}{\rho_{0}\delta}.

Summing over all heavy indices
j\in[\ell]
proves the lemma.
∎

5.2.3.
j
light and near bad

Recall that
j\in[\ell]
is light if
S_{j}<\delta^{2}
, and that
j
is near bad if there exists a bad index
i\in[\ell]
such that
|\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle|>0.9
. In the following lemma, we bound the expected number of indices that are light and near bad.

Lemma 5.17
.

The expected number of indices that are light and near bad is at most
100\delta\ell\sqrt{\log m}/\rho_{0}
.

Proof.

For convenience, we consider the graph
G
defined as follows: the vertex set is
[\ell]
and two vertices
i,j\in[\ell]
are connected by an edge if
|\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle|>0.9
. For
i\in[\ell]
, let
\deg(i)
denote the degree of vertex
i
, and let
N(i)
denote the neighborhood of vertex
i
.

Note that if
|\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle|>0.9
for some
i,j\in[\ell]
, then we have either
||v_{i}-v_{j}||_{2}^{2}\leqslant 0.2
or
||v_{i}+v_{j}||_{2}^{2}\leqslant 0.2
. Indeed, since
||v_{i}||_{2}=1
and
||v_{j}||_{2}=1
, we have

||v_{i}-v_{j}||_{2}^{2}=||v_{i}||_{2}^{2}+||v_{j}||_{2}^{2}-2\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle=2-2\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle.

Similarly, we have
||v_{i}+v_{j}||_{2}^{2}=2+2\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle
. So, depending on the sign of
\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle
, we have either
||v_{i}-v_{j}||_{2}^{2}\leqslant 0.2
or
||v_{i}+v_{j}||_{2}^{2}\leqslant 0.2
.

Claim 5.18
.

For every light index
j\in[\ell]
that is near bad, there exists a bad neighbor
i\in N(j)
such that
\deg(i)\leqslant 3\delta^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
j\in[\ell]
be a light index that is near bad. Then by definition there exists
i\in N(j)
that is bad. For any
h\in N(i)
, since
|\langle v_{i},v_{h}\rangle|>0.9
, we have either
||v_{i}-v_{h}||_{2}^{2}\leqslant 0.2
or
||v_{i}+v_{h}||_{2}^{2}\leqslant 0.2
. We claim that
|\langle v_{j},v_{h}\rangle|\geqslant 0.6
for all
h\in N(i)
. Indeed, assuming without loss of generality that
||v_{i}-v_{j}||_{2}^{2}\leqslant 0.2
and
||v_{i}-v_{h}||_{2}^{2}\leqslant 0.2
, by the triangle inequality we have

||v_{j}-v_{h}||_{2}\leqslant||v_{j}-v_{i}||_{2}+||v_{i}-v_{h}||_{2}\leqslant\sqrt{0.2}+\sqrt{0.2}=2\sqrt{1/5}.

So we have
||v_{j}-v_{h}||_{2}^{2}\leqslant 0.8
and therefore
|\langle v_{j},v_{h}\rangle|\geqslant(2-0.8)/2=0.6
. Now we obtain

0.6^{2}\cdot|N(i)|\leqslant\sum_{h\in N(i)}\langle v_{j},v_{h}\rangle^{2}\leqslant\sum_{h=1}^{k}\langle v_{j},v_{h}\rangle^{2}=S_{j}\leqslant\delta^{2},

recalling the assumption that
j
is light. Thus we have
\deg(i)=|N(i)|\leqslant 3\delta^{2}
, as claimed.
∎

By
5.18
, the number of indices
j\in[\ell]
that are light and near bad is at most

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[\ell]\text{ bad}\\
\deg(i)\leqslant 3\delta^{2}\end{subarray}}\deg(i).

Therefore, the expected number of indices that are light and near bad is at most

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[\ell]\\
\deg(i)\leqslant 3\delta^{2}\end{subarray}}\deg(i)\cdot\mathbb{P}[i\text{ is bad}]\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[\ell]\\
\deg(i)\leqslant 3\delta^{2}\end{subarray}}\deg(i)\cdot\frac{40\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{i}}},

using
5.7
. Now, note that for every
i\in[\ell]
, we have
S_{i}=\sum_{j\in[\ell]}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}\geqslant\sum_{j\in N(i)}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}\geqslant\deg(i)\cdot 0.9^{2}\geqslant\deg(i)/2
. Therefore we can conclude that the expected number of indices that are light and near bad is at most

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[\ell]\\
\deg(i)\leqslant 3\delta^{2}\end{subarray}}\deg(i)\cdot\frac{40\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{\deg(i)/2}}=\frac{40\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[\ell]\\
\deg(i)\leqslant 3\delta^{2}\end{subarray}}\sqrt{2\deg(i)}\leqslant\frac{40\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}}\cdot\ell\cdot\sqrt{6}\delta\leqslant\frac{100\delta\ell\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}}.\qed

Thus we have the following upper bound for the expected number of close indices that are light and near bad.

Lemma 5.19
.

The expected number of close indices that are light and near bad is at most
2000\delta\ell\log m/(\rho_{0}\rho_{1})
.

Proof.

Consider any light index
j\in[\ell]
. For any outcome of
X_{0}
such that
j
is near bad, there exists at least one bad index
i\in[\ell]
satisfying
|\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle|>0.9
, and consequently

\textup{Var}[\langle X_{1},v_{j}\rangle\mid X_{0}]=\frac{1}{3}\cdot\rho_{1}^{2}\sum_{i\text{ bad}}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}\cdot 0.9^{2}\rho_{1}^{2}.

Thus, by
Lemma

3.6
we have

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close}\mid X_{0}]\leqslant\frac{10\sqrt{\log m}}{0.9\rho_{1}/\sqrt{3}}\leqslant\frac{20\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{1}}

for every outcome of
X_{0}
such that
j
is near bad. Therefore, we can conclude that

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close and near bad}]=\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close}\mid j\text{ is near bad}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is near bad}]\leqslant\frac{20\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{1}}\cdot\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is near bad}]

for any light index
j\in[\ell]
. Thus, the expected number of close indices that are light and near bad is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[\ell]\\
j\text{ light}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close and near bad}]\leqslant\frac{20\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{1}}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[\ell]\\
j\text{ light}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is near bad}].

Note that the sum on the right-hand side is precisely the expected number of indices that are light and near bad. Plugging in
Lemma

5.17
to bound this, we can conclude that the expected number of close indices that are light and near bad is at most

\frac{20\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{1}}\cdot\frac{100\delta\ell\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}}=\frac{2000\delta\ell\log m}{\rho_{0}\rho_{1}}.\qed

5.2.4.
j
activated and not near bad

Finally, we bound the expected number of close indices that are activated and not near bad. Recall from
Definition

5.10
that for
j\in[\ell]
and
t\in\{1,2,4,\dots,2^{\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil}\}
,
\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)
denotes the event that
|\langle X,v_{j}\rangle-\lambda_{j}|\leqslant 10t\sqrt{\log m}
. We first prove the following key lemma bounding the probability that
i
is bad and
\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)
holds for distinct
i,j\in[\ell]
satisfying
|\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle|\leqslant 0.9
.

Lemma 5.20
.

For distinct
i,j\in[\ell]
with
|\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle|\leqslant 0.9
and
t\in\{1,2,4,\dots,2^{\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil}\}
, we have

\mathbb{P}[i\text{ bad}\text{ and }\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)]\leqslant\frac{40000t}{\rho_{0}^{2}\sqrt{S_{j}}}\log m.

Proof.

We first show that for any given outcomes of
\alpha_{h}
for all
h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}
, we have

(2)

\mathbb{P}[i\text{ bad}\text{ and }\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)\mid(\alpha_{h})_{h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}}]\leqslant\frac{2000t}{\rho_{0}^{2}}\log m.

To show (
2
), let us fix arbitrary outcomes of
\alpha_{h}
for all
h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}
. Let
A=\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle
. By assumption we have
|A|\leqslant 0.9
. Note that

\langle X_{0},v_{i}\rangle=\rho_{0}\Big(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}\cdot A+\sum_{h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}}\alpha_{h}\langle v_{h},v_{i}\rangle\Big)

and

\langle X_{0},v_{j}\rangle=\rho_{0}\Big(\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{i}\cdot A+\sum_{h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}}\alpha_{h}\langle v_{h},v_{j}\rangle\Big),

and that the values of
\sum_{h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}}\alpha_{h}\langle v_{h},v_{i}\rangle
and
\sum_{h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}}\alpha_{h}\langle v_{h},v_{j}\rangle
are already determined by the fixed outcomes of
\alpha_{h}
for
h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}
.

In the event
\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)
, we have

|\langle X_{0},v_{j}\rangle-\lambda_{j}|\leqslant 10t\sqrt{\log m}.

So, the random variable
\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{i}\cdot A
must be contained in the interval
I_{j}
of length
20t\sqrt{\log m}/\rho_{0}
centered around
\lambda_{j}/\rho_{0}-\sum_{h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}}\alpha_{h}\langle v_{h},v_{j}\rangle
. On the other hand, if
i
is bad, we have

|\langle X_{0},v_{i}\rangle-\lambda_{i}|\leqslant 10\sqrt{\log m},

so the random variable
\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}\cdot A
must be contained in the interval
I_{i}
of length
20\sqrt{\log m}/\rho_{0}
centered around
\lambda_{i}/\rho_{0}-\sum_{h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}}\alpha_{h}\langle v_{h},v_{i}\rangle
.

Thus, if
\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)
holds and
i
is bad, taking the linear combination
(\alpha_{j}+A\cdot\alpha_{i})-A(\alpha_{i}+A\cdot\alpha_{j})=(1-A^{2})\alpha_{j}
, we see that
(1-A^{2})\alpha_{j}
must be contained in the interval
I_{j}-AI_{i}
of length at most
40t\sqrt{\log m}/\rho_{0}
and so
\alpha_{j}
must be contained in a particular interval of length at most
40t\sqrt{\log m}/((1-A^{2})\rho_{0})\leqslant 400t\sqrt{\log m}/\rho_{0}
(namely, the interval
(1-A^{2})^{-1}(I_{j}-AI_{i})
). Here we used the inequality
1-A^{2}\geqslant 1/10
, which follows from
|A|\leqslant 0.9
.

The probability that
\alpha_{j}\sim U[-1,1]
lies in this interval is at most
200t\sqrt{\log m}/\rho_{0}
. Now conditioning on any such outcome of
\alpha_{j}
, for the event of
i
being bad to occur,
\alpha_{i}\sim U[-1,1]
must fall into an interval of length
20\sqrt{\log m}/\rho_{0}
(which happens with probability at most
10\sqrt{\log m}/\rho_{0}
). Thus we can conclude that

\mathbb{P}[i\text{ bad}\text{ and }\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)\mid(\alpha_{h})_{h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}}]\leqslant\frac{200t}{\rho_{0}}\sqrt{\log m}\cdot\frac{10}{\rho_{0}}\sqrt{\log m}=\frac{2000t}{\rho_{0}^{2}}\log m,

showing (
2
).

In the case where
S_{j}\leqslant 400
, the desired inequality in the statement of the lemma follows directly from (
2
). Indeed, in this case we automatically have

\mathbb{P}[i\text{ bad}\text{ and }\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)]\leqslant\frac{2000t}{\rho_{0}^{2}}\log m\leqslant\frac{40000t}{\rho_{0}^{2}\sqrt{S_{j}}}\log m.

So let us assume
S_{j}>400
from now on. We distinguish between the cases
\rho_{0}\leqslant 10t\sqrt{\log m}
and
\rho_{0}>10t\sqrt{\log m}
. In either case, in the event
\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)
, we must have

|\langle X_{0},v_{j}\rangle-\lambda_{j}|=\Big|\rho_{0}\Big(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}\cdot A+\sum_{h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}}\alpha_{h}\langle v_{h},v_{i}\rangle\Big)-\lambda_{j}\Big|\leqslant 10t\sqrt{\log m}.

So the sum
\rho_{0}\sum_{h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}}\alpha_{h}\langle v_{h},v_{i}\rangle
must lie in the interval of length
20t\sqrt{\log m}+4\rho_{0}
centered at
\lambda_{j}
.

If
\rho_{0}\leqslant 10t\sqrt{\log m}
, then this interval has length
20t\sqrt{\log m}+4\rho_{0}\leqslant 60t\sqrt{\log m}
. By
Lemma

3.6
, the probability that
\rho_{0}\sum_{h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}}\alpha_{h}\langle v_{h},v_{i}\rangle
falls into this interval is at most

\frac{60t\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{j}-1-A^{2}}/\sqrt{3}}\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{3}\cdot 60t\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{j}-2}}\leqslant\frac{200t\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{j}}}.

Conditioning on any such outcomes of
\alpha_{h}
for all
h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}
, as well as on an outcome of
\alpha_{j}
, the event of
i
being bad occurs only when
\alpha_{i}
falls into a particular interval of length
20\sqrt{\log m}/\rho_{0}
, and the probability for this is at most
10\sqrt{\log m}/\rho_{0}
. So we can conclude that

\mathbb{P}[i\text{ bad and }\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)]\leqslant\frac{200t\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{j}}}\cdot\frac{10\sqrt{\log m}}{\rho_{0}}=\frac{2000t\log m}{\rho_{0}^{2}\sqrt{S_{j}}}.

If
\rho_{0}>10t\sqrt{\log m}
, in the event
\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)
the sum
\rho_{0}\sum_{h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}}\alpha_{h}\langle v_{h},v_{i}\rangle
must fall into the interval of length
20t\sqrt{\log m}+4\rho_{0}\leqslant 6\rho_{0}
centered at
\lambda_{j}
. By
Lemma

3.6
, this happens with probability at most

\frac{6\rho_{0}}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{j}-1-A^{2}}/\sqrt{3}}\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{3}\cdot 6}{\sqrt{S_{j}-2}}\leqslant\frac{20}{\sqrt{S_{j}}}.

Conditioning on any such outcomes of
\alpha_{h}
for all
h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}
, by (
2
) the probability that
i
is bad and
\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)
holds is at most
(2000t/\rho_{0}^{2})\log m
. So we can conclude

\mathbb{P}[i\text{ bad}\text{ and }\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)]\leqslant\frac{20}{\sqrt{S_{j}}}\cdot\frac{2000t}{\rho_{0}^{2}}\log m=\frac{40000t}{\rho_{0}^{2}\sqrt{S_{j}}}\log m.\qed

Now, for an index
j\in[\ell]
, we can bound the probability of
j
being activated and not near bad.

Lemma 5.21
.

For
j\in[\ell]
, we have

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is activated and not near bad}]\leqslant 120000\cdot\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}}\cdot\log m\sqrt{S_{j}}.

Proof.

First, for any
h=0,\dots,\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil
, we bound the probability of
j
being
t
-activated for
t=2^{h}
and not near bad. For convenience, define the quantity

T_{j}=\frac{1}{3}\cdot\rho_{1}^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[\ell]\text{ bad}\\
|\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle|\leqslant 0.9\end{subarray}}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}=\frac{1}{3}\cdot\rho_{1}^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[\ell]\\
|\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle|\leqslant 0.9\end{subarray}}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}\cdot 1_{i\text{ bad}},

where
1_{i\text{ bad}}
is the indicator variable for the event of
i
being bad. Now, if
j
is not near bad, then
T_{j}=\frac{1}{3}\cdot\rho_{1}^{2}\sum_{i\in[\ell]\text{ bad}}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}
agrees with the sum in the event
\mathcal{E}_{2}(j,t)
. Therefore, if
j
is
t
-activated and not near bad, we must have
T_{j}>t^{2}/4
. Thus, we have

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is $t$-activated and not near bad}]\leqslant\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)\text{ and }T_{j}>t^{2}/4]=\mathbb{P}[T_{j}\cdot 1_{\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)}>t^{2}/4].

By
Lemma

5.20
, the expectation of
T_{j}\cdot 1_{\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)}
can be computed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{j}\cdot 1_{\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\cdot\rho_{1}^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[\ell]\\
|\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle|\leqslant 0.9\end{subarray}}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[1_{i\text{ bad}}\cdot 1_{\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\cdot\rho_{1}^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[\ell]\\
|\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle|\leqslant 0.9\end{subarray}}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}\cdot\mathbb{P}[i\text{ bad}\text{ and }\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{3}\cdot\rho_{1}^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[\ell]\\
|\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle|\leqslant 0.9\end{subarray}}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}\cdot\frac{40000t}{\rho_{0}^{2}\sqrt{S_{j}}}\log m

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{15000t\rho_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}\sqrt{S_{j}}}\log m\sum_{i\in[\ell]}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{2}=\frac{15000t\rho_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}\sqrt{S_{j}}}\log m\cdot S_{j}=\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}}\cdot 15000t\log m\sqrt{S_{j}}.

Thus by Markov’s inequality, we have

\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is $t$-activated and not near bad}]=\mathbb{P}[T_{j}\cdot 1_{\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)}>t^{2}/4]\leqslant\frac{4}{t^{2}}\cdot\mathbb{E}[T_{j}\cdot 1_{\mathcal{E}_{1}(j,t)}]\leqslant 60000\cdot\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\log m\sqrt{S_{j}}}{t}.

Now, taking a union bound, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is activated and not near bad}]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{h=0}^{\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil}\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is $2^{h}$-activated and not near bad}]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{h=0}^{\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil}60000\cdot\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\log m\sqrt{S_{j}}}{2^{h}}\leqslant 120000\cdot\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}}\log m\sqrt{S_{j}}.\qed

Now we are ready to bound the number of close indices that are activated and not near bad.

Corollary 5.22
.

The expected number of close indices that are activated and not near bad is at most
10^{5}\cdot\rho_{1}\ell\log^{3/2}m/\rho_{0}^{3/2}
.

Proof.

For each
j\in[\ell]
, by
Lemma

5.21
and
Lemma

5.13
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close, activated and not near bad}]

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\min\Big(\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is activated and not near bad}],\,\mathbb{P}[j\text{ is close and activated}]\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\min\Big(120000\cdot\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}}\log m\sqrt{S_{j}},\,\frac{1600\log^{2}m}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{j}}}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\Big(120000\cdot\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{2}}\log m\sqrt{S_{j}}\cdot\frac{1600\log^{2}m}{\rho_{0}\sqrt{S_{j}}}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant 10^{5}\cdot\frac{\rho_{1}}{\rho_{0}^{3/2}}\log^{3/2}m.

Summing over all
j\in[\ell]
, it follows that the expected number of close indices that are activated and not near bad is at most
10^{5}\cdot\rho_{1}\ell\log^{3/2}m/\rho_{0}^{3/2}
.
∎
