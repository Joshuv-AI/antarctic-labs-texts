Publication bias and time to the publication of randomised trials conducted in sub-Saharan Africa: a cross-sectional study

What is already known on this topic

Evidence-based healthcare decisions rely on up-to-date information from all relevant randomised controlled trials (RCTs), yet studies consistently show that 25% to 50% of clinical trials globally remain unpublished or are published many years after completion. This delay contributes significantly to research waste and can jeopardise the reliability of healthcare guidelines and clinical decisions. While the number of RCTs conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) has steadily increased, the extent of publication bias and the time to publication in this region remain unknown.

What this study adds

This registry-based study characterised both publication rates and timelines for RCTs conducted across SSA. The study identified a substantial proportion (34.5%) of unpublished SSA RCTs, with an overall median time to publication of 34.2 months from primary completion. It reveals significant variations in dissemination across registries, recruitment status, and funding sources. Notably, RCTs registered in the Pan African Clinical Trials Registry (PACTR) and those funded by non-industry sources faced significantly longer delays.

How this study might affect research, practice, or policy

These findings provide an empirical baseline to evaluate future regional and global initiatives aimed at improving trial transparency and reducing research waste in Africa. The results point to the need for targeted strategies by funders and policymakers, such as making timely registry updates and journal publication explicit conditions of funding. Strengthening the linkages between registry records and publications, including the routine reporting of registry identifiers in journal abstracts. These could improve the discoverability of findings and ensure treatment recommendations are based on more complete evidence.

Background

High-quality evidence is critical for informing clinical practice, shaping health policy decisions, and ensuring optimal healthcare outcomes. Randomised controlled trials (RCTs), known as the gold standard for evaluating the effects of healthcare interventions, play a central role in generating this evidence [1]. A crucial step in promoting RCT transparency is for trialists to register their trial in a clinical trial registry, ideally, prospectively, that is, before participant enrolment begins [2]. This process of clinical trial registration reduces selective reporting and mitigates publication bias [3].

Publication bias is commonly defined as the preferential dissemination of RCT results based on the nature or direction of findings [4]. This includes, but is not limited to, the tendency to publish positive or statistically significant results more often, quickly, or visibly compared to negative, null or unimportant findings [5, 6]. For our study, we refer to ‘publication bias’ more narrowly to refer to the failure to publish the results of a registered RCT as a peer-reviewed journal article, regardless of whether completed or terminated. We did not assess selective outcome reporting within published articles, often described as reporting bias. Clinical trial registries such as ClinicalTrials.gov and the Pan African Clinical Trials Registry (PACTR) are primary registries contributing to the World Health Organisation’s (WHO) International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) [7]. These registries are consistent with the WHO’s criteria for content, quality, and accessibility, ensuring that key information about the trial is publicly available and that scientific and ethical standards are upheld.

Registering interventional clinical trials is deemed to be a scientific, ethical, and moral responsibility [8]. The WHO provides reasons clarifying the importance of clinical trial registration; see Table 1 below [9].

Since 2005, the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) has required the prospective registration of clinical trials in public registries before the enrolment of the first participant as a condition for publishing trial results [3]. The ICMJE accepts trial registrations in primary registries that meet the WHO criteria, as well as in ClinicalTrials.gov, which provides data to the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP). Furthermore, the ICMJE recommends that all medical journal editors require clinical trial registration as a condition for considering publication [11, 12]. The WHO has recognised PACTR as a primary registry since 2009, which is important for trials conducted in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA).

Clinical trials, such as well-designed RCTs, are necessary for reliable, unbiased estimates of differences in outcomes between groups receiving differing interventions. Commonly, researchers share the methods and results of their RCTs through peer-reviewed publications in scientific journals [13]. The availability of results of RCTs in publication or other formats allows healthcare providers and patients to access the latest findings in a comprehensive format. It ensures that policymakers can use relevant findings. Furthermore, published findings of RCTs provide the foundation for systematic reviews of the effects of interventions. When the results of RCTs are not available, this negatively impacts the findings and conclusions of those reviews. Consequently, missing RCT results can jeopardise the reliability of healthcare choices, guidelines, policy judgements, and planning for future research that should rely on systematic reviews to inform these evidence-based decisions [5, 14–21].

Ideally, evidence-based decisions about healthcare interventions should be informed by access to up-to-date information from all relevant RCTs, making it essential that the reports are published soon after study completion [5, 13, 22]. However, studies have consistently shown that between 25 and 50% of clinical trials remain unpublished or are only published many years after completion [5, 6, 23–25]. This delay contributes significantly to research waste [26]. For example, Chalmers and Glasziou estimated in 2009 that 85% of existing research investments had been wasted, with unpublished or incompletely published research forming a large part of this problem [1]. Full publication is an essential part of efforts to avert research waste [27]. Researchers who fail to publish their RCT results violate their obligation to the trial participants and the public, do a disservice to the participants who took part in these studies, and weaken the return on the investment of resources into research [5, 19, 28].

However, researchers face several barriers to publication, such as the long-standing view that it is easier to publish positive than negative findings [29]. Nevertheless, the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines on protecting participants in research identify the ethical requirement of making results publicly obtainable or publishing them, regardless of the study’s outcome [10]. This is in keeping with the desire of many people who participate in RCTs to contribute to biomedical science.

Alongside the complete failure to publish the results of RCTs, problems also arise because of the time taken to publish the results, which is often more than two years after the trial’s completion [5, 19, 22, 30]. Although it needs to be recognised that some researchers face a backdrop of limited research funds and competition for these funds, Ross et al. and WHO suggested that results should be published within 12–24 months [5, 31]. Time and resources are wasted through slow publishing, and there is also the potential for undermining evidence-based clinical decisions [19]. However, given the several steps required after the last participant in a study undergoes their last follow-up, the speed of publication needs to be balanced with the need for time and access to the necessary skills and resources to publish.

In Africa, the WHO has noted a slow but steady increase in the number of RCTs since the mid-2000s, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). However, the continent continues to conduct fewer trials compared to other global regions [32]. Thus, understanding the publication landscape of RCTs conducted in SSA is important, given the growth in regional activity and the potential of these RCTs to inform healthcare practice and policy. However, the extent of publication bias of SSA RCTs remains unknown. Therefore, our study objectives were to assess (1) the proportion of completed or terminated RCTs registered in a primary clinical trials’ register from SSA that have been published and (2) the time from completion to publication [33]. Based on previous literature, we hypothesised that 30% of RCTs were not published and that the time to journal publication takes longer than 24 months from the primary outcome completion date, regardless of cumulative or subgroup analysis [4, 23, 25, 34, 35].

Methods

Stellenbosch University Health Research Ethics Committee provided ethics clearance for this research (project ID: 24380 and ethics reference: S22/06/108 (PhD)). We wrote this manuscript in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [36].

Design, data source and study sample

This cross-sectional study, consisting of a retrospective analysis of registered SSA RCTs, aims to report the proportion of completed and terminated RCTs from SSAs registered in ClinicalTrials.gov and the Pan African Clinical Trials Registry (PACTR) and their time to publication.

We identified registered RCTs that met the following eligibility criteria: (1) at least one trial site in an SSA country, (2) either medical or non-medical related, (3) registered on or after 1 January 2010, (4) actual start date on or after 1 January 2010, (5) primary completion date before 1 January 2025, and (6) recruitment status recorded in the registry as terminated or completed. ClinicalTrials.gov and PACTR were chosen to capture a comprehensive dataset of trials conducted across SSA. The selection of 2010 as the start date is consistent with the WHO’s recognition of PACTR as a primary registry in 2009, which ensures that trials registered in this period of study meet the necessary international registration standards [37].

Records retrieved from both registries were merged and deduplicated using trial registration numbers, trial titles, and descriptions. For RCTs registered in both registries, we inspected both entries and retained the record that was most complete. For our analysis, we removed the duplicate records to avoid double-counting. When information was missing from one registry but present in the other, we cross-referenced both sources. We incorporated the available data into the retained record to ensure a complete and accurate dataset.

We removed records if they were tagged or described as (1) non-RCTs, (2) no SSA sites, (3) start date after February 2023, (4) start date before 2010, (5) completion date not reported, or (6) completion date after February 2023. To avoid misclassifying recently completed trials that would not yet have had sufficient time to reach publication, we required that each RCT have at least 24 months of follow-up time from its registered primary completion date to the end of our search period (February 2025). This ensured that trials were not classified as unpublished simply because they had recently been completed.

Although these fields were considered for subgroup analysis, they were frequently incomplete, inconsistently entered, or ambiguously coded across both registries. For example, the disease or health condition field often lacked uniform nomenclature, was broad and non-specific, or listed multiple unrelated conditions for a single entry, making classification unreliable. The sample size field was inconsistently populated, with fields left empty. Trial phase was sometimes not reported, or the terminology varied, with some describing seamless or integrated clinical trial design that could not be mapped onto standard phase categories. The intervention field was illegible for some records, and the primary outcomes were either unclearly defined or indistinguishable from the secondary outcome, or differed between the registry entry and the publication. These consistencies occurred across the registries, which would have introduced risks of misclassification; therefore, we restricted our analysis to variables that were consistently and reliably recorded across both registries.

Search strategy

On 18 February 2025, we searched ClinicalTrials.gov and PACTR for eligible trials. We used filters to limit the study type to RCTs, date filters to limit to records registered on or after 1 January 2010, and region filters to limit to records with at least one SSA trial site. The records of each country were separately downloaded as XML files and merged into a single Excel spreadsheet for cleaning before the removal of duplicate records and records that did not meet our eligibility criteria. We did not search the WHO’s ICTRP because their datasets did not provide a ‘primary completion date’, which we required to calculate the survival analysis. The WHO’s ICTRP only provides recordings of the posted results date and the completed results date; however, after eligibility screening, removing duplicates, cleaning the data, and analysing the data, we found that numerous dates were within a few days prior to publication or were posted after the publication, thus making it unfeasible for this study.

Publication status

To determine an RCT’s publication status and the publication date for RCTs that were published, four reviewers (AH, LF, LB, and LR) independently searched biomedical literature sources in January and February 2025. We implemented two phases to search for a journal publication of the results of a registered RCT. The first phase required reviewers to search the registry using the registry number and assess whether it contained a link to a journal publication reporting the RCT’s results. If there were no such links, we employed a second phase in which reviewers used the trial registry number to search PubMed and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials for a corresponding publication. If none were found, the reviewers repeated the search of these two resources using keywords from the RCT’s registered title separated by the Boolean operator ‘OR’. If no records were retrieved, we repeated this step using Google Scholar.

We confirmed the relevance of the journal publication by checking its full text for the registry number. If no registry number was reported, we checked if the publication matched the information from ClinicalTrials.gov or PACTR, specifically the detailed description, location, intervention, and outcome measures of the RCT.

For the analysis reported here, we defined an RCT as unpublished after exhausting all the aforementioned strategies to find a corresponding journal publication, i.e. no journal article reported the results, even if the results were available in the trial registry, conference abstracts, or as a pre-print. If a publication was confirmed, we recorded the date of the earliest available publication date, using the online-first (epub) date when reported, as this reflects the point at which the results became publicly accessible; otherwise, we used the print publication date instead. If the day of the month of publication was not reported, we used the first day of the month. We also recorded whether the trial registry number was reported in the article’s abstract. Submission dates were not routinely or consistently reported across journals, limiting the feasibility of incorporating this information into the current analysis.

For all published RCTs, we calculated the time interval between presumed trial completion and publication. This was calculated as the number of months from the RCT’s primary completion date to the publication date of the article reporting its results. We used the online electronic publication date if it was ahead of print. If this was not available, we used the print publication date.

Quality assurance

To avoid errors in merging data from the two primary registries, we removed the variables that did not meet the scope of our analysis and arranged these separately in their respective XML files. Following this, we then checked the columns to ensure the correct variables were entered on the registries, followed by uniformly cleaning the data.

To minimise bias, the lead investigator (AH) reviewed a 10% random sample (n = 790) of PACTR and ClinicalTrials.gov records. For the quality assurance check, AH randomly selected 496 records marked as published and 294 records marked as unpublished. AH verified the publication status, the accuracy of the recorded publication dates, and whether the trial registry number was reported in the abstract. No instances were found in which an RCT was incorrectly recorded as not published or vice versa.

Of the 496 records, we had 12 records that needed corrections to their publication dates, with the changes ranging from − 132 days to 221 days. A further 15 records were corrected, being mistakenly recorded as not reporting the trial registry number in their abstract.

Statistical analysis

The downloaded registry records were cleaned in Excel and analysed using STATA 16 [38, 39]. All records that met our eligibility criteria were included for analysis (n = 3026). The study represents a census of all registered SSA RCTs (completed or terminated); a sample size calculation was not required. Power calculations are needed when estimating the sample required to draw inferences from a subset of a population. We analysed the full available population of RCTs; thus, no sampling error or minimum sample estimation was needed. Hypothesis testing was conducted using the entire dataset, with p-values reflecting the statistical power inherent in the observed population rather than in a sub-sample. We employed descriptive statistics to offer an overview of the dataset and to estimate the publication rates among terminated and completed RCTs. We summarised the independent variables by presenting them as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Subsequently, we classified the publication rates according to the independent variables and represented these as numerical values and percentages. Through a retrospective longitudinal component, we evaluated time to publication using Kaplan-Meier survival plots for the overall sample and the subgroups. We calculated a median time to publication as the time taken for 50% of each sample or subgroup to be published. The median reflects the Kaplan-Meier estimate, which incorporates right-censoring for RCTs that remained unpublished by the end of follow-up. RCTs without a peer-reviewed publication at the end of follow-up were treated as right censored, reflecting incomplete observation of their eventual publication time. The median time to publication was defined as the time point at which this estimate indicated that 50% of RCTs had been published. The difference in survival distributions between groups was assessed using the log-rank, applying a two-sided significance level of p < 0.05. The subgroups included the source registry, recruitment status, participant age groups, funding source, and number of sites (single vs multiple) and the ten SSA countries with the most registered RCTs.

Results

Our searches of ClinicalTrials.gov and PACTR yielded 5689 and 2207 records, respectively, and 1122 duplicate records were removed (Fig. 1). We kept the ClinicalTrials.gov record if the RCT was also registered in PACTR because, on inspection, ClinicalTrials.gov entries were more likely to be regularly updated and up to date. Thus, we removed the PACTR record from our analysis to avoid adding duplicate records. We excluded 3733 records with reasons (Table S1), leaving 3041 records for analysis. We removed 15 records with publication dates preceding the primary completion date recorded in the registry. Thus, our final analysis included 3026 RCTs (Table S2). All included RCTs had a minimum of 24 months between their registered primary completion date and our final search date, ensuring that RCTs had a reasonable opportunity to publish and were not misclassified as unpublished due to insufficient follow-up time.

Time to publication

The overall median time to publication from the primary completion date was 34.2 months (95% CI: 32.4 to 35.5) (Fig. 2).

Cumulative percentage of RCTs published

The overall proportion of RCTs that were published was lower for PACTR-registered RCTs, and their time to publication was longer compared to RCTs registered in ClinicalTrials.gov. The median time to publication for RCTs registered on ClinicalTrials.gov was 31.5 months (95% CI: 30.1 to 32.9), and for PACTR, the median time to publication was 54.8 months (95% CI: 46.5 to 67.7), p < 0.001 (Supplementary Fig. S1).

The median time to publication for RCTs for which the recruitment status was given as ‘completed’ was 33.5 months (95% CI: 31.8 to 34.8), p < 0.0001. For those with a ‘terminated’ recruitment status, the median time to publication was 50.0 months (95% CI: 38.9 to ∞) (Supplementary Fig. S2). Specifically, terminated trials were less likely to be published, and their time to publication was longer.

Median time to publication for RCTs recruiting adults only, children only or both adults and children was 35.3 months (95% CI: 33.5 to 37.9), 33.6 months (95% CI: 30.3 to 36.3), 31.1 months (95% CI: 28.8 to 34.5), respectively, with a statistically significant difference in the times to publication (p = 0.0301) (Supplementary Fig. S3). The global log-rank test showed a significant difference in time to publication between age groups (X2 = 7.00, p = 0.03). Pairwise comparisons demonstrated that adults differed significantly from children (p = 0.024), whereas adults vs no-restriction (p = 0.059) and children vs no-restriction (p = 0.761) were not statistically different.

The median time to publication for industry-funded RCTs was 30.0 months (95% CI: 28.7 to 31.7), while for non-industry-funded RCTs, it was 36.7 months (95% CI: 34.7 to 39.0), p < 0.0001 (Supplementary Fig. S4).

The median time to publication for RCTs with a single SSA study site was 34.7 months (95% CI: 33.4 to 36.5), compared to 25.9 months (95% CI: 23.1 to 29.5) for RCTs with study sites in more than one SSA, p < 0.0001 (Supplementary Fig. S5).

South Africa was dominant in relation to the SSA countries that provided sites for the largest number of RCTs. It was listed as a site for 1231 RCTs (71.2% of which were published), with a median time to publication of 30.7 months (95% CI: 28.9 to 32.4) for these RCTs. The other countries in the top ten were Nigeria (n = 335, 43.3% published), with a median time to publication of 60.7 months (95% CI: 50.8 to ∞); Kenya (n = 303, 64.4% published), with a median time to publication of 35.5 months (95% CI: 29.7 to 39.4); Uganda (n = 286, 66.1% published) with a median time to publication of 31.4 months (95% CI: 27.4 to 35.8); Tanzania (n = 172, 73.3% published) with a median time to publication of 32.2 months (95% CI: 28.7 to 37.8); Ethiopia (n = 127, 66.2% published) with a median time to publication of 30.1 months (95% CI: 27.5 to 37.6); Malawi (n = 111, 82.0% published) with a median time to publication of 26.7 months (95% CI: 23.0 to 32.8); Zambia (n = 97, 71.1% published) with a median time to publication of 29.9 months (95% CI: 20.5 to 38.7); Ghana (n = 94, 64.9% published) with a median time to publication of 35.4 months (95% CI: 28.1 to 50.0); and Burkina Faso (n = 74, 82.4% published) with a median time to publication of 30.6 months (95% CI: 24.6 to 37.8). For the RCTs with multiple SSA sites (n = 186, 81.2% published), the median time to publication was 25.9 months (95% CI: 22.2 to 29.5) (Supplementary Fig. S6.1–S6.11 and Table S3).

Discussion

This study evaluated RCTs conducted in countries in SSA that are registered in PACTR or ClinicalTrials.gov. We assessed the percentage of registered RCTs that were published in journals and the time taken to publish. We included registered RCTs regardless of the condition or interventions being studied. We found that more than a third (34%) of registered RCTs remained unpublished and that the median time to publication was approximately 34.2 months (95% CI: 32.4 to 35.5). These findings can be compared to those in the recently updated Cochrane methodology review of 204 research reports on the scale of publication bias for trials, which found that nearly half (47%) of the trials were not published in full. The median time to a publication from the trial completion date was approximately 25 months [30].

When we compared our findings to other studies that retrieved trial records from ClinicalTrials.gov, we saw that ClinicalTrials.gov registered RCTs conducted in SSA have a longer time to publication, 31.4 months (95% CI: 30.1 to 32.9). For instance, Ross et al. (2011) reported a median of 23 months for NIH-funded trials; Chen et al. (2016) found a median of 22.3 months for U.S. academic medical centres, and van Heteren (2019) reported 24 months for otology-related RCTs [19, 35]. In our sample, if we focus our analyses on only those SSA RCTs that were registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, they had a shorter median time to publication of 31.4 months (95% CI: 30.1 to 32.9) than those registered on PACTR, 54.8 months (95% CI: 46.5 to 67.7). Similarly, PACTR-registered RCTs had a lower percentage of publications (ClinicalTrials.gov: 69.7%; PACTR: 48.8%). These differences suggest that those registering in PACTR face greater challenges, which may include differences in resource support systems. Therefore, targeted strategies to improve publication rates and reduce the time to publication for those conducting RCTs in SSA are necessary.

One notable difference between our findings and those of previous studies of this nature is in the publication percentage for trials funded by the industry and those without industry funding. In our sample of SSA RCTs, the median time to publication for industry-funded RCTs was 30.0 months (95% CI: 28.7 to 31.7), with 71.8% published, while for non-industry-funded RCTs, the median time to publication was 36.7 months (95% CI: 34.7 to 39.0), with 62.7% published. This contrasts with previous studies that found lower publication rates for industry-sponsored trials. For example, Ross et al. (2009) reported that 40% of industry-sponsored trials were published compared with 56% of non-industry trials, but limited their research to trials registered in ClinicalTrials.gov after 31 December 1999 and completed by June 2007, and only searched for published reports in MEDLINE [25]. Similarly, van Heteren et al. (2019) only searched MEDLINE and limited their research to otology trials registered in ClinicalTrials.gov with a primary completion date on or before 31 December 2015 [35]. They reported that 44% of industry-sponsored trials were published, compared with 69% of non-industry-funded trials. Lastly, Showell et al. (2023) searched for RCTs registered from 2007 in the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry and, like our study, searched for publications in multiple databases [41]. They reported that 65% of industry-funded trials were published compared to 74% of those not funded by industry. The overall analysis in the recently updated Cochrane methodology review found that trials funded by non‐industry sources were more likely to be published than trials funded by industry, with an adjusted odds ratio of 2.13 (95% CI 1.82 to 2.49; 14 studies) and that they were published in a shorter time (adjusted hazard ratio: 1.46, 95% CI 1.15 to 1.86; 7 studies) [30]. The reasons for the difference between our findings and those in other studies are unclear. One explanation may be that our sample is more recent and that growing pressures on industry-funded RCTs over recent years have placed greater focus on ensuring that they are published and published with less delay. Non-industry-funded RCTs, usually conducted by academic institutions, may require additional support to achieve timely publications. Academics may face pressure to attain career advancements and qualify for future funding opportunities [42]. We assume that barriers to publishing include, but are not limited to, administrative or analytical support and competing clinical duties.

There is considerable variation in the median time to publication across SSA countries where RCTs take place. For example, Nigeria had a particularly long median time to publication of 60.7 months (95% CI: 50.8 to ∞) compared to South Africa (which has the most RCT sites of any of the SSA in our sample), where the median time to publication was 30.7 months (95% CI: 28.9 to 32.4). Based on these findings, researchers in SSA might be publishing in non-indexed national, regional, or language-specific journals, which our search was unable to detect. Alternatively, these differences might reflect variations in research infrastructure, resources, and publication practices across SSA. Factors that may contribute to this are the local research policies, availability of skilled personnel, and international collaborations. These country-level findings indicate the need to understand better the specific challenges investigators face in different SSA countries and to find ways to address these challenges.

Challenges with using trial registries to investigate the publication of RCTs

On many occasions, when searching either of the two trial registries used for this study, we found a lack of information about the results of the registered RCTs. For example, data were often missing for the date and URL of the first journal publication of results. Thus, forcing us to search for the publications via databases. This failure to update its register entry when an RCT is published makes it more difficult and time-consuming to find the RCT’s results. An example of poor updating of a registry is reported in a recent descriptive analysis of trials registered in PACTR, which showed that of the 36% that had been completed, 3% of these had results available in the registry [43]. Examples like these are a hindrance for systematic reviewers and other consumers who rely on trial registries to find up-to-date information about trials. This problem is despite the information on the summary results being one of the 24 compulsory items required to meet the WHO’s Trial Registration standards [44]. Thus, the quality of other data recorded on the trial registries may also have limited our findings. For example, trials may have been terminated or completed without this being noted in the registry. Therefore, some of those with an ‘ongoing’ status (and therefore not included in our sample) may have been abandoned, terminated, or completed.

Regarding data quality, we removed 15 records from our sample because they had publication dates that were earlier than the primary completion date in the RCT’s register entry. The negative time-to-publication intervals could not be incorporated into our survival analysis without introducing bias. We also found some records with publication dates within a month of their primary completion date, which is a very short time. These timeframes may not reflect the true sequence of trial completion, manuscript preparation, and peer review. These inconsistencies underestimate the true time to publication when the registry dates do not correspond to study timelines [25, 45–47].

A possible inconsistency may underestimate the true tie to publication. These issues relate to delayed updating of registry entries after initial registration, as reported by PIs that manage multiple ongoing studies and face competing demands, including but not limited to clinical, administrative and funding [48–51]. Thus, delaying or omitting to update the registries is not intentional. Therefore, registry data may not always reflect the actual RCT’s timelines [52, 53]. With these potential limitations, one should consider the interpretation of the findings with caution.

Our findings support a need for further research to explore the causes of these ongoing problems with the quality of data captured in trial registers and to identify ways to improve this and publication practices. In relation to this, we have conducted a scoping review to describe strategies to mitigate the publication bias of RCTs. We found no studies with strategies targeted directly towards trialists; however, the literature described several system-level approaches to reduce publication bias [54]. They may include, but are not limited to, providing sufficient writing time, access to statisticians, including enhanced agreements and mandates set out by funders, institutions, and ethics committees; these may include monitoring of RCTs to improve publication practices. ClinicalTrials.gov does require reporting of results within 12 months from primary completion and notifying investigators prior to any deadlines. A notable development for minimising publication bias has emerged from the International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number (ISRCTN) Registry, where the team is implementing a transparency tracker [55] to prompt trialists to update their records with the RCT’s results and links to their publications, or to provide a reason for not publishing their findings if there is no link to a publication after two years.

Challenges in finding publications of registered RCTs

Although registry numbers do not need to appear in abstracts, and PubMed indexes them in metadata fields, we found that these identifiers were inconsistently included in either abstracts or metadata [56]. Therefore, we searched the full-text and multiple fields (including metadata) when attempting to link registry entries to publications. Nevertheless, nearly 16% of SSA RCT publications did not report the trial registry number in the publication’s abstract. In other studies, van Heteren 2019 reported that 45% reported a trial registry number in the general article rather than the abstract and concluded, like us, that not reporting the trial registry number in the abstract makes it unnecessarily difficult to find publications of the results of RCTs [35].

Furthermore, our attempts to find publications when the trial registry number failed to yield results were hampered by the retrieval of large numbers of irrelevant records when we used keywords to search for specific RCTs. Similarly, an added difficulty for our searches was the lack of a clear statement that a report was an RCT, ‘randomised trial’ or similar. This is a failure by authors and editors to adhere to the CONSORT requirement that the study design be included in the publication’s title [57].

Finally, we did not limit our searches to language, and we would have translated articles published in languages other than English if any had been identified (none were). However, our ability to find RCT publications may have been limited because we relied on major English literature databases that may predominantly index English-language journals [58]. French and Portuguese are common written languages in SSA, and future studies should include searches of regional databases to improve the capture of publication pathways in SSA.

Limitations of our findings

Although we searched ClinicalTrials.gov and PACTR, we may have missed RCTs registered on the ISRCTN registry and elsewhere. We did not use the WHO-ICTRP data because the field we required to calculate time-to-publication analysis, particularly the primary completion dates, was found to be either inconsistently populated or missing for a substantial proportion of the SSA records at the time of our first data extraction. WHO-ICTRP, in many cases, provided the ‘results posted’ date or a ‘last updated’ timestamp. These dates were very close to the event after the publication date. Thus, they were not suitable for producing an estimate of the elapsed time taken to publish. The lack of congruence in the order of variables and their naming across trial registries would have made data cleaning and interpretation more difficult. This limitation is consistent with prior evaluations reporting irregular completeness and accuracy across the WHO-ICTRP aggregated fields [51, 59]. Therefore, our decision to limit the search to the two main registries for the region was made to avoid the challenge of managing and merging multiple additional datasets. Furthermore, we are confident that we have captured a significantly large sample of RCTs registered to be conducted in SSA. The two trial registries we searched are regularly used by trialists in SSA. RCTs with at least one site in Africa are expected to be registered in PACTR or, at a minimum, in ClinicalTrials.gov, in line with international trials registration standards. Currently, there are no universal mandates requiring RCTs in Africa to be registered in PACTR. However, according to the WHO, the African Vaccine Regulatory Forum has proposed legislation to mandate such registration [60]. It is also possible that our searches failed to identify publications for some of the RCTs that were included in our sample, and our focus on journal publications meant that we did not include results that were only presented in conference abstracts or within the trial registry. Future research could consider the impact of the presentation of RCT results in these ways on publication and time to publication. Because we applied a 24-month minimum follow-up window from primary completion, recently completed RCTs were excluded from the analysis. This reduced the risk of inflating the unpublished proportion by including trials that had not yet had a reasonable opportunity to disseminate their results.

Potential barriers to publishing

Principal investigators (PIs) in SSA may face multiple structural barriers that contribute to non- or delayed publication. Commonly reported challenges include, but are not limited to, limited funding and institutional infrastructure, competing clinical responsibilities, and restricted access to biostatistical or analytical support. Additional barriers arise from journal rejection, article processing charges, limited mentorship in scientific writing, and language constraints. Data ownership or authorship disputes, sponsor or intellectual property restrictions and weak institutional incentives, particularly for null or inconclusive findings, also impede prompt dissemination. Moreover, structural inequities such as bias against research from low and middle-income countries may reduce acceptance rates or prioritisation of African-led work in some international journals. Together, these factors highlight that publication delays often reflect system-level constraints on African trialists rather than individual investigator shortcomings.

Implications and future research

To our knowledge, this is the first large, registry-based study to characterise both publication rates and time to publication for RCTs conducted in SSA. This provides an empirical baseline against which future regional and global initiatives can be evaluated. Our findings demonstrate that two-thirds of the registered RCTs reach publication, and the overall time for publication is longer than recommended [31]. In addition, there are substantial variations across registries, recruitment status, funding sources, and countries, highlighting possible uneven dissemination practices. The delays to publishing and inconsistencies in reporting are likely to have negative consequences for evidence-based decision-making. Publication bias in either form, including but not limited to missing or incomplete registry entries, no publications or delays in publishing, may result in potential skewing of treatment recommendations that are assumed to be based on complete evidence. Strengthening linkages between registry records and publications, improving the accuracy of registry fields, and ensuring routine reporting of registry identifiers may help improve discoverability and reduce avoidable research waste [61].

Pertaining to the scientific community, our findings highlight the need to establish reliable linkages between registry records and publications, enhance the consistent reporting of registry identifiers in journal articles, and develop methods to keep registry information updated. These changes would directly support systematic reviewers, guideline developers, and health-technology assessment bodies to base their work on more complete evidence.

For funders, institutions and policymakers, our results point to specific pressure points such as longer delays for non-industry-funded and single-country trials and lower publication rates in some registries and countries, which can inform targeted strategies. These may necessitate timely registry updates and journal publication, explicit conditions of funding, monitoring compliance and providing administrative and analytical support to help investigators complete and publish their work and training on publication bias and its consequences.

Further investigation is needed into several findings. First, the causes of the significant differences in the time to publish RCTs specifically between the subgroups, i.e. funder, recruitment status, and the trial registries, should be investigated [32]. Second, future analysis should investigate the timeline from completion to submission, and from submission to publication, where submission dates are available, to better understand how investigator behaviour and peer review and editorial processes influence the overall interval to publication. Third, the variations across SSA suggest that regulatory practices, culture research, and competencies may influence publishing timelines.

As a next step, we plan to survey and interview PIs who conducted RCTs in SSA to better understand the barriers and enablers influencing publication, with the aim of co-developing context-appropriate strategies to mitigate publication bias.

Conclusions

Overall, we found a substantial proportion (34.5%) of unpublished SSA RCTs. Moreover, the median time to publication from primary completion was 34.2 months. The persistence of publication bias threatens the integrity of evidence-based healthcare practice, particularly given that consumers depend on peer-reviewed journal publications as conventional and trusted sources to stay informed. Our findings underscore the importance of continued research to test and implement preventative strategies to mitigate publication bias.
