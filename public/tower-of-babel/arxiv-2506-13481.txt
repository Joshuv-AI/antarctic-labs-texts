The Holography of Spread Complexity A Story of Observers
Zhehan Li, Jia Tian

Abstract
We propose a holographic description of spread complexity and its rate in 2D CFTs, building on the pioneering work [ 1 ] . By exploiting the S ​ L ​ ( 2 , ℝ ) SL(2,\mathbb{R}) symmetry, we construct the Krylov basis and demonstrate that its non-uniqueness gives rise to ambiguities analogous to those in quantum complexity. Within the AdS/CFT correspondence, we identify the spread complexity as the energy measured by a bulk observer, with its rate corresponding to the measured radial momentum. These results suggest a novel holographic interpretation of complexity ambiguity: rather than arising from infinitely many gravitational observables, it may reflect the existence of infinitely many observers, each measuring complexity from their own perspective.

Introduction

Quantum complexity is a concept from quantum information theory that quantifies the minimal size of a quantum circuit required to prepare a given state from a reference state. In recent years, significant developments
[

As first pointed out in
[

[

[

The multiplicity of holographic complexity is not unexpected, as the definition of quantum complexity itself carries a fundamental ambiguity stemming from the choice of reference state and gate set. This ambiguity is conceptually analogous to that encountered in relativity, where different observers may obtain different measurement outcomes. On the other hand, due to this inherent ambiguity and the computational challenges involved, establishing a precise quantitative comparison between quantum complexity and its holographic duals remains a significant challenge.

A promising alternative approach is provided by Krylov complexity
[

[

[

2
The proposal

In this section, we present our proposal within a simple holographic model. The central idea we aim to convey is that the time evolution of a quantum state
|\psi(t)\rangle
can be projected onto different Krylov bases, yielding distinct values of the spread complexity. This ambiguity in spread complexity mirrors that found in quantum complexity. Moreover, the holographic description of the spread complexity and its rate necessarily requires the introduction of an appropriate observer.

We consider the global AdS
3
spacetime with the metric

ds^{2}=-\cosh^{2}\rho dt^{2}+d\rho^{2}+\sinh^{2}\rho d\phi^{2},\quad\phi\in[0,2\pi),

(2.1)

whose asymptotic boundary is a cylinder. To study the CFT on the cylinder, it is convenient to perform a Wick rotation
t\to-\text{i}t_{E}
and introduce the coordinates

z=e^{t_{E}+\text{i}\phi},\quad\bar{z}=e^{t_{E}-\text{i}\phi},

(2.2)

such that the states in the CFT on the time slice
t_{E}=0
can then be prepared by Euclidean path integral on the unit disk:
|z|\leq 1
. The generators of 2D conformal symmetry are given by

L_{n}=\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{dz}{2\pi\text{i}}z^{n+1}T(z),\quad\bar{L}_{n}=\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{d\bar{z}}{2\pi\text{i}}\bar{z}^{n+1}\bar{T}(\bar{z}),

(2.3)

where
T(z)
and
\bar{T}(\bar{z})
are the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic components of the energy-momentum tensor. Without operator insertions, the prepared state is the vacuum state
|\Omega\rangle
. Excited states are obtained by inserting local operators
\mathcal{O}(z_{i},\bar{z}_{i})
within the unit disk into the path integral. Through this operator-state correspondence, the behavior of operator growth under time evolution can be captured by the spread complexity of the corresponding state. For simplicity, we focus on scalar primary operators. Since the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic sectors decouple, we present only the holomorphic sector below; results for the anti-holomorphic sector follow by imposing the reality condition
z^{*}=\bar{z}
.

2.1
The spread complexity

In the global AdS
3
spacetime, the Hamiltonian
H_{\text{CFT}}
generating the time translation is
L_{0}+\bar{L}_{0}
, so the state corresponding to
\mathcal{O}(z(t))
is

|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-\text{i}L_{0}t}|\psi_{0}\rangle=e^{-\text{i}L_{0}t}\mathcal{O}(z)|\Omega\rangle,\quad|\psi_{0}\rangle\equiv\mathcal{O}(z)|\Omega\rangle.

(2.4)

Following the Lanczos algorithm, the Krylov basis is obtained by Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization of the states
\{|\psi_{0}\rangle,H_{\text{CFT}}|\psi_{0}\rangle,H^{2}_{\text{CFT}}|\psi_{0}\rangle\}
. In the Krylov basis
\{|K_{n}\rangle\}
, the Hamiltonian takes the form of a tridiagonal matrix and the quantum state can be expanded as

|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\psi_{n}(t)|K_{n}\rangle.

(2.5)

The Krylov complexity, or spread complexity, is defined as the expectation value of the number operator

C(t)=\langle\psi(t)|\hat{n}|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n|\psi_{n}(t)|^{2}.

(2.6)

This procedure is difficult to implement, particularly in field theory where the inner product may not be well-defined. Typically, the Krylov complexity is obtained from the Lanczos coefficients (the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian), which can be determined iteratively
[
44
]
. For the particular state (
2.4
) corresponding to a local operator,
[
1
]
obtained the spread complexity by mapping the time evolution to an effective
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
dynamics via the identification of return amplitudes. With this approach, the only input is the CFT two-point function, and all information about the Krylov basis remains implicit. However, as noted, this information is crucial for deriving the holographic dual. We therefore propose a method for constructing the Krylov basis that does not employ the Lanczos algorithm or iterative procedure.

For the primary operator
\mathcal{O}(z)
with the conformal dimension
h
, when it is inserted at the origin of the unit disk, the prepared state is called the asymptotic state

\mathcal{O}(0)|\Omega\rangle=|h\rangle,

(2.7)

which satisfies

L_{0}|h\rangle=h|h\rangle,\quad L_{+1}|h\rangle=0.

(2.8)

In other words, the state
|h\rangle
is the highest weight state of the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
algebra spanned by
\{L_{0},L_{\pm 1}\}
. It generates the representation
\{|h,n\rangle\}
of the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
algebra:

\displaystyle|h,n\rangle\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(2h)}{n!\Gamma(2h+n)}}L_{-1}^{n}|h\rangle,\quad\langle h,n|h,m\rangle=\delta_{n,m},

(2.9)

\displaystyle L_{0}|h,n\rangle=(h+n)|h,n\rangle,

(2.10)

\displaystyle L_{+1}|h,n\rangle=\sqrt{n(2h+n-1)}|h,n-1\rangle,

(2.11)

\displaystyle L_{-1}|h,n\rangle=\sqrt{(n+1)(2h+n)}|h,n+1\rangle.

(2.12)

Since
|h\rangle
is an eigenstate of the Hamiltonian
L_{0}
, the state remains unchanged under time evolution, yielding vanishing spread complexity.

When the operator is inserted at a generic point, the corresponding state becomes a linear combination of states
|h,n\rangle
:

|\psi_{0}\rangle=\mathcal{O}(z)|\Omega\rangle=e^{zL_{-1}}\mathcal{O}(0)|\Omega\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}L_{-1}^{n}|h\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(2h+n)}{n!\Gamma(2h)}}z^{n}|h,n\rangle.

(2.13)

As this state is no longer an eigenstate of
L_{0}
, one can construct the Krylov subspace
\{|\psi_{0}\rangle,L_{0}|\psi_{0}\rangle,L_{0}^{2}|\psi_{0}\rangle,\ldots\}
. However, this approach is cumbersome as the state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
is expressed in an inconvenient basis. To find a better representation, we exploit the fact that
|\psi_{0}\rangle
can be viewed as a highest weight state of the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
algebra spanned by a different set of generators
\{\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{\pm 1}\}
(e.g see
[
45
]
). This can be verified by starting from (
2.8
) and applying a generic unitary
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
transformation:

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{i}Q}L_{0}e^{-\mathrm{i}Q}e^{\mathrm{i}Q}\mathcal{O}(0)|\Omega\rangle=he^{\mathrm{i}Q}\mathcal{O}(0)|\Omega\rangle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{L}_{0}\mathcal{O}(z)|\Omega\rangle=h\mathcal{O}(z)|\Omega\rangle,

(2.14)

\displaystyle Q=\alpha L_{0}+\frac{\beta-\mathrm{i}\gamma}{2}L_{+1}+\frac{\beta+\mathrm{i}\gamma}{2}L_{-1},\quad\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R},

(2.15)

where

\mathcal{O}(z)\propto e^{\mathrm{i}Q}\mathcal{O}(0)e^{-\mathrm{i}Q},\quad\mathcal{L}_{n}=e^{\mathrm{i}Q}L_{n}e^{-\mathrm{i}Q}.

(2.16)

The explict relations between
L_{n}
and
\mathcal{L}_{n}
are:

\displaystyle z=\frac{B}{D},\quad|z|\leq 1,

(2.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}=-ABL_{-1}+(AD+BC)L_{0}-CDL_{+1},

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{+1}=B^{2}L_{-1}-2BDL_{0}+D^{2}L_{+1},

(2.19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{-1}=A^{2}L_{-1}-2ACL_{0}+C^{2}L_{+1},

(2.20)

with

\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\left(\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{2}\right)+\frac{\mathrm{i}\alpha\sinh\left(\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\delta}}&\frac{\mathrm{i}(\beta+\mathrm{i}\gamma)\sinh\left(\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\delta}}\\
\frac{-(\gamma+\mathrm{i}\beta)\sinh\left(\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\delta}}&\cosh\left(\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{2}\right)-\frac{\mathrm{i}\alpha\sinh\left(\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\delta}}\end{pmatrix},

(2.21)

where
\delta=\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}-\alpha^{2}
. Inverting relations (
2.18
)–(
2.20
), we obtain

L_{0}=(AD+BC)\mathcal{L}_{0}+BD\mathcal{L}_{-1}+AC\mathcal{L}_{+1}.

(2.22)

Therefore, the state (
2.4
) becomes

|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-\mathrm{i}[(AD+BC)\mathcal{L}_{0}+BD\mathcal{L}_{-1}+AC\mathcal{L}_{+1}]t}|h\rangle_{z},\quad|h\rangle_{z}\equiv\mathcal{O}(z)|\Omega\rangle=|\psi_{0}\rangle.

(2.23)

The Krylov basis can now be directly identified as

|K_{n}\rangle=|h,n\rangle_{z},\quad|h,n\rangle_{z}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(2h)}{n!\Gamma(2h+n)}}\mathcal{L}_{-1}^{n}|h\rangle_{z},

(2.24)

and the resulting spread complexity is given by

C(t)=\frac{\langle\psi(t)|\mathcal{L}_{0}|\psi(t)\rangle}{\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle}-h.

(2.25)

A few comments are in order. The
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
generators
\mathcal{L}_{n}
with
n=0,\pm 1
can be extended to the full set of Virasoro algebra generators, and the state
|h\rangle_{z}
is promoted to a primary state of the Virasoro algebra. When
z
is real, this Virasoro algebra arises from the so-called
Möbius
quantization of 2D CFT studied in
[
46
]
. When the operator is inserted at the boundary of the unit disk, i.e.,
z=1
, which corresponds to the
t=0
time slice in the 2D cylinder spacetime, the prepared state is ill-defined. A commonly used regularization scheme is to perform an infinitesimal Euclidean time evolution
[
47
,
48
]
:

\mathcal{O}(t=0)|\Omega\rangle\to e^{-\epsilon L_{0}}\mathcal{O}(t=0)|\Omega\rangle.

(2.26)

This is the type of state considered in
[
1
]
. The physical interpretation of this infinitesimal Euclidean time evolution is now clear: it simply moves the operator into the unit disk along the radial direction. The spread complexity given in (
2.25
) is invariant under the
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
unitary transformation. By substituting (
2.18
), we can rewrite it as

C(t)=(AD+BC)\frac{\langle\psi(t)|L_{0}|\psi(t)\rangle}{\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle}-AB\frac{\langle\psi(t)|L_{-1}|\psi(t)\rangle}{\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle}-CD\frac{\langle\psi(t)|L_{+1}|\psi(t)\rangle}{\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle}-h.

(2.27)

This is one of the main results of this section, which shows that the spread complexity depends not only on the state
|\psi(t)\rangle
but also on the information about the Krylov basis (the representation) encoded in the unitary transformation generated by
Q
(
2.15
), or equivalently, the matrix (
2.21
).

2.2
The ambiguity of the spread complexity and the state distance

Note that there are three real parameters
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
in the charge
Q
(
2.15
), but only one complex constraint (
2.17
). Consequently, one free parameter remains in the expression (
2.23
), and correspondingly, the resulting spread complexity (
2.25
) also contains a free parameter. This ambiguity is reminiscent of the quantum complexity arising from the freedom in choosing reference states and gate sets. Unlike the ambiguity of the Krylov complexity in field theory contexts, where the ambiguity stems from regularizing the inner product between states, the ambiguity found here originates from a symmetry consideration. For a fixed operator location at
z
, a residual
U(1)
group transformation exists within the global
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
, known as the little group or stabilizer group. The stabilizer group is parameterized as

e^{\text{i}\tilde{Q}}=e^{\text{i}(\tilde{\alpha}L_{0}+\frac{\tilde{\beta}-\text{i}\tilde{\gamma}}{2}L_{+1}+\frac{\tilde{\beta}+\text{i}\tilde{\gamma}}{2}L_{-1})}

(2.28)

and generates the specific
SL(2,R)
transformation

e^{\text{i}\tilde{Q}}\mathcal{O}(z)e^{-\text{i}\tilde{Q}}\propto\mathcal{O}(\tilde{z}),\quad\tilde{z}=\frac{\tilde{A}z+\tilde{B}}{\tilde{C}z+\tilde{D}}.

(2.29)

The parameters are subjected to the condition

\tilde{z}=\frac{\tilde{A}z+\tilde{B}}{\tilde{C}z+\tilde{D}}=z,

(2.30)

where the relationships between
\tilde{A},\tilde{B},\tilde{C},\tilde{D}
and
\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta},\tilde{\gamma}
are also given in (
2.21
).
The solution to this condition is indeed one-dimensional:

\tilde{\alpha}=\frac{\text{i}(z^{2}-1)\tilde{\gamma}-(z^{2}+1)\tilde{\beta}}{2z},\quad\text{Im}(z)\tilde{\beta}=\text{Re}(z)\tilde{\gamma}.

(2.31)

The action of the stabilizer group is to change one suitable representation to another, or equivalently, to change one set of the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
generators
\{\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{\pm 1}\}
to another set
\{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{0},\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\pm 1}\}
, which mimics the change of quantum gate sets in the context of quantum complexity.

To generalize and better understand the spread complexity defined above and its ambiguities, we introduce the notions of
distance complexity
and
state distance
below. We have shown that any scalar primary operator
\mathcal{O}(z_{1})
defines a highest weight state
|h\rangle_{z_{1}}
and the representation
\{|h,n\rangle_{z_{1}}\}
. The same scalar primary operator at another location
\mathcal{O}(z_{2})
can be written as a linear combination

\displaystyle|\mathcal{O}(z_{2})\rangle=\mathcal{N}e^{\text{i}Q}|\mathcal{O}(z_{1})\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}|h,n\rangle_{z_{1}},

(2.32)

\displaystyle Q=\alpha L_{0}+\frac{\beta-\text{i}\gamma}{2}L_{+1}+\frac{\beta+\text{i}\gamma}{2}L_{-1},\quad\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R},

(2.33)

subject to

\frac{Az_{1}+B}{Cz_{1}+D}=z_{2}.

(2.34)

Mimicking the spread complexity, we define the distance complexity as

C_{z_{1}\to z_{2}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|c_{n}|^{2}n.

(2.35)

Recall that the spread complexity characterizes the operator growth under time evolution. Similarly, the distance complexity can be understood as describing operator growth under a specific
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
flow generated by
Q
. It is worth noting that Krylov complexity in 2D CFTs subjected to deformed

SL(2,\mathbb{R})
Hamiltonians has been extensively studied in
[
49
]
.
It is clear that the distance complexity is invariant under the
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
transformation following

\displaystyle e^{\text{i}Q^{\prime}}|\mathcal{O}(z_{2})\rangle=\mathcal{N}e^{\text{i}Q^{\prime}}e^{\text{i}Q}|\mathcal{O}(z_{1})\rangle,\to|\mathcal{O}(z_{2}^{\prime})\rangle=\mathcal{N}^{\prime}e^{\text{i}\mathcal{Q}}|\mathcal{O}(z_{1}^{\prime})\rangle,

(2.36)

\displaystyle C_{z_{1}\to z_{2}}=C_{z_{1}^{\prime}\to z_{2}^{\prime}}

(2.37)

where

\displaystyle z^{\prime}_{1,2}=\frac{A^{\prime}z_{1,2}+B^{\prime}}{C^{\prime}z_{1,2}+D^{\prime}},\quad\mathcal{Q}=\alpha\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{0}+\frac{\beta-\text{i}\gamma}{2}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{+1}+\frac{\beta+\text{i}\gamma}{2}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{-1},\quad\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{n}\equiv e^{\text{i}Q}L_{n}e^{-\text{i}Q}.

(2.38)

The distance complexity exhibits the same ambiguity inherent in the stabilizer group. Motivated by the definition of quantum complexity, we define the state distance as the minimal value of the distance complexity:

\mathfrak{d}(z_{1},z_{2})=\text{Min}(C_{z_{1}\to z_{2}})=\text{Min}(C_{z_{2}\to z_{1}}),

(2.39)

where the second equality follows directly from (
2.37
).
Without loss of generality, we can transform
z_{1}
to the origin and leave the other point as a general point
z_{2}=\tanh\frac{\theta}{2}e^{\text{i}u}
. Then, we find that the state distance is given by

\mathfrak{d}(|z_{21}|)=h\frac{2|z_{21}|^{2}}{1-|z_{21}|^{2}}=h(\cosh\theta-1)=2h\sinh^{2}(\frac{\theta}{2})

(2.40)

and the minimization is achieved by the representative

\mathcal{O}(z_{2})|\Omega\rangle=e^{-\frac{\theta}{2}(L_{+1}e^{-\text{i}u}-L_{-1}e^{\text{i}u})}|\mathcal{O}(0)\rangle.

(2.41)

To prove this, one can show that applying any non-trivial stabilizer group transformation will increase the distance complexity.
Explicitly, applying the stabilizer group transformation (
2.28
) to the representative (
2.41
), we find

\displaystyle-\frac{\theta}{2}(L_{+1}e^{-\text{i}u}-L_{-1}e^{\text{i}u})\to-\theta\sin v\sinh(\theta)L_{0}+e^{-\text{i}u}\frac{\theta}{2}(\cosh(\theta)\sin v+\text{i}\cos v)L_{+1}

\displaystyle+e^{\text{i}u}\frac{\theta}{2}(\cosh(\theta)\sin v-\text{i}\cos v)L_{-1},\quad v\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{\beta}^{2}+\tilde{\gamma}^{2}}{\sinh^{2}(\theta)}}

(2.42)

and the corresponding distance complexity is given by

C=2h\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\left(\cosh^{2}(\theta)\sin^{2}(v)+\cos^{2}(v)\right),

(2.43)

which takes the minimal value at

,
i.e.
\tilde{\alpha}=\tilde{\beta}=\tilde{\gamma}=0
. The state distance (

represents the hyperbolic distance (geodesic distance in the hyperbolic space). This is not a coincidence because the invariance of the distance complexity under

is the isometric group of the hyperbolic space, whose metric is

ds^{2}_{\text{hyperbolic}}=\frac{4dzd\bar{z}}{(1-|z|^{2})^{2}}=d\theta^{2}+\sinh^{2}\theta du^{2}.

(2.44)

With this geometric interpretation, we can immediately write down the state distance between two generic points:

\mathfrak{d}(z_{1},z_{2})=h\frac{2|z_{12}|^{2}}{(1-|z_{1}|^{2})(1-|z_{2}|^{2})}=h(\cosh\ell_{12}-1),

(2.45)

where

is the hyperbolic distance. Furthermore, the representative (
2.41

SL(2,\mathbb{R})/U(1)
is a submanifold of the group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
. Thus, if we want to connect two points in the coset
SL(2,\mathbb{R})/U(1)
(which is identified with the unit disk), there will be multiple paths, giving rise to multiple distances. It is worth mentioning that the state (
2.41

[
50
]
for the Virasoro group, which is associated with an information geometry equipped with a Fubini-Study metric that corresponds exactly to the hyperbolic disk (

X_{0}=\cosh\theta,\quad X_{1}=\sinh\theta\cos u,\quad X_{2}=\sinh\theta\sin u,\quad\vec{X}\cdot\vec{X}\equiv X_{0}^{2}-X_{1}^{2}-X_{2}^{2}=1,

(2.46)

which is equal to the angle (up to a constant shift) between the two points:

\mathfrak{d}(\vec{X},\vec{Y})=h(\vec{X}\cdot\vec{Y}-1).

(2.47)

The spread complexity defined in (

|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-\text{i}L_{0}t}\mathcal{O}(z)|\Omega\rangle=\mathcal{O}(ze^{-\text{i}t})|\Omega\rangle,\quad\to\quad C(t)=C_{z\to ze^{-\text{i}t}}.

\displaystyle=

First, it provides a novel understanding of the ambiguity in spread complexity: from a holographic perspective, since spread complexity is a measured quantity, its value naturally depends on the observer. This reflects a fundamental principle of relativity. Second, the interpretation endows spread complexity with an operational meaning. Analogous to quantum complexity, we can imagine that the quantum state is being manipulated by an experimentalist, whose reference frame determines the measurement outcome. Last but not least, one of the fundamental features of quantum state complexity is its inherent ambiguity, as previously noted. This poses a challenge for its holographic description. In contrast, for Krylov complexity, we propose a different explanation by connecting the ambiguity in relativity (observer dependence) with the ambiguity in quantum mechanics (reference and gate-set dependence).

(4.14)

Under the Lorentzian time evolution
(\theta_{r,0},\phi_{r,0})\to(\theta_{r,0},\phi_{r,0}+t_{r})
, the spread complexity and the its rate become:

C(t_{r})=h(\cosh\theta_{r,0}^{2}\cosh t_{r}-\sinh^{2}\theta_{r,0}-1),\quad\frac{dC(t_{r})}{dt_{r}}=h\cosh\theta_{r,0}^{2}\sinh t_{r}\,.

(4.15)

Without loss of the generality, we can set
\phi_{r,0}=0
, so the initial state is

|\psi_{0}\rangle=\mathcal{O}(\theta_{r,0})|\Psi_{\text{TFD}}\rangle.

(4.16)

Following previous arguments, we can find that time-evolved state
|\psi(t)\rangle
is dual to the geodesic

\tanh\rho_{r}\cosh t_{r}=\tanh\rho_{0},\quad\rho_{0}=\theta_{r,0},\quad t_{r}\geq 0.

(4.17)

As in the global spacetime, identifying
\rho_{0}=\theta_{r,0}
suggests that the hyperbolic space (
4.12
) can be identified with the
t_{r}=0
time slice. The tangent vector along this geodesic and the associated momentum are:

\displaystyle\hat{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sinh\rho_{0}(\coth^{2}\rho_{0}\cosh^{2}t_{r}-1)\partial_{t_{r}}-\cosh\rho_{0}\sinh t_{r}\partial_{\rho},

(4.18)

\displaystyle p_{t_{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-h\sinh^{2}\rho_{0},\quad p_{\phi_{r}}=0,\quad p_{\rho}=-h\cosh\rho_{0}\sinh t_{r}.

(4.19)

In the right wedge of the Rindler space, the (normalized) Killing vectors are

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{0}^{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\text{i}(\partial_{t_{r}}+\partial_{\phi_{r}}),

(4.20)

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\pm 1}^{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\text{i}e^{t_{r}+\phi_{r}}(\cosh\rho\partial_{t_{r}}+\tanh\rho\partial_{\phi_{r}}\mp\partial_{\rho}),

(4.21)

and the conserved charges associated with these Killing vectors relate to the expectation values of the corresponding generators as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\psi(t)|L^{r}_{0}|\psi(t)\rangle}{\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{i}h\sinh\rho_{0}=h\hat{Q}_{0},

(4.22)

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\psi(t)|L^{r}_{\pm}|\psi(t)\rangle}{\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\mp t_{r}}h\cosh\rho_{0}=he^{\mp t_{r}}\hat{Q}_{\pm}.

(4.23)

Substituting (

) into the expression for (

) and retaining only the right-wedge components, we find that the spread complexity corresponds to the generator

\mathcal{L}^{r}_{0}=\text{i}(\sinh\rho_{0}L_{0}^{r}-\cosh\rho_{0}\frac{L_{+1}^{r}+L_{-1}^{r}}{2})\,.

(4.24)

which leads to

\displaystyle C(t)+h=\hat{e}_{0}^{\alpha}p_{\alpha}=h\left(\cosh^{2}\rho_{0}\cosh t_{r}-\sinh^{2}\rho_{0}\right),\quad\hat{e}_{0}^{\alpha}\hat{e}_{0,\alpha}=-1,

(4.27)

\displaystyle\hat{e}_{0}=\text{i}(\sinh\rho_{0}\hat{L}_{0}^{r}-\cosh\rho_{0}\frac{e^{-t_{r}}\hat{L}^{r}_{+1}+e^{t_{r}}\hat{L}^{r}_{-1}}{2}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\cosh\rho_{0}\coth\rho\cosh\phi_{r}+\sinh\rho_{0})\partial_{t_{r}}+(\sinh\rho_{0}-\cosh\rho_{0}\tanh\rho\cosh\phi_{r})

\displaystyle+\cosh\rho_{0}\sinh\phi_{r}\partial_{\rho}.

Similarly, we find that the complexity rate is given by

\frac{dC(t_{r})}{dt}=\frac{\langle\psi(t)|[L^{r}_{0},\mathcal{L}^{r}_{0}]|\psi(t)\rangle}{\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle}=\cosh\rho_{0}\frac{\langle\psi(t)|\text{i}\frac{L_{-1}^{r}-L_{+1}^{r}}{2}|\psi(t)\rangle}{\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle}.

5
Conclusions and Outlooks

In this work, we proposed a holographic description of the spread complexity and its rate in 2D CFTs. By leveraging the underlying symmetries, we explicitly constructed the Krylov basis, which turns out not to be unique, giving rise to ambiguities in spread complexities. This ambiguity closely mirrors that of quantum complexity. To describe the spread complexity under general
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
flows, we introduced the notions of distance complexity and state distance, providing a novel geometric interpretation of the spread complexity and connecting it to Nielsen’s complexity. Within the AdS/CFT correspondence, we related the expectation values of the
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
charges to the conserved quantities associated with the corresponding Killing vectors. Remarkably, the spread complexity can be expressed as the energy measured by an observer moving along the worldline defined by
\hat{e}_{0}
, as shown in eq. (
2.68
). Holographically, the ambiguity in spread complexity is interpreted as an observer dependence, a feature rooted in relativity. Furthermore, the complexity rate is identified with the measured radial momentum, which remains invariant under redefinitions of the radial coordinate. Below, we outline several open issues and potential directions for future research.

First, throughout this work, we have focused on the semi-classical regime where the conformal dimension
h
of the primary operator is in the range of
\frac{1}{G_{N}}\gg h\gg 1
. In this limit, the dual state corresponds to a massive particle propagating along a geodesic in an unperturbed AdS spacetime. When
h\ll 1
, the primary operator should instead be dual to a free bulk scalar field, and the time-evolved state
|\psi(t)\rangle
is described by the wavefunction
\Psi(\rho,t,\phi)=\langle\hat{\phi}(\rho,t,\phi)|\psi(t)\rangle
. In this regime, we expect the conserved charges
\hat{Q}_{n}
to be replaced by appropriate bulk expectation values such as
\langle\Psi^{*},\hat{L}_{n}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\Psi\rangle
, while the spread complexity may still admit an interpretation as the field energy measured by a given observer.
For
h\sim\frac{1}{G_{N}}
, we can still treat the primary operator as a massive particle, but the backreaction of the particle becomes non-negligible. The resulting geometry develops conical singularities around the worldline of the particle. When the particle is at rest at the center of the global AdS
3
, the bulk solution corresponds to the conical AdS
3
spacetime. For off-center static particles, recent studies suggest that the dual geometry can be described by a three-dimensional C-metric
[
61
,
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
,
67
,
68
,
69
]
. In the case of a moving particle, the dual geometry has been argued to correspond to a locally quenched spacetime
[
47
]
. Importantly, all these geometries are related to global AdS
3
via coordinate transformations. Therefore, the worldline of the observer can be extended to these more general settings. A potentially related work is
[
70
]
.

Second, The correspondence between conformal symmetry generators and Killing vectors extends naturally to higher dimensions. Thus, our proposal is expected to generalize to higher-dimensional AdS spacetimes. Consider the AdS
d+1
metric

ds^{2}=-\cosh^{2}\rho dt^{2}+d\rho^{2}+\sinh^{2}\rho d\vec{x}^{2},

(5.1)

where
\vec{x}^{2}=1
parameterizes the sphere
S^{d-1}
. The boundary CFT lives on
\mathbb{R}\times S^{d-1}
. Performing a Wick rotation
t\to-\text{i}\tau
, the conformal symmetry of the CFT is described by the group
SO(1,d+1)
. We denote the generators of
SO(1,d+1)
as
L_{ab},\,,a,b=-1,0,\dots,d
, acting in the embedding space
\mathbb{R}^{1,d+1}
. These relate to the “physical” generators in the CFT as follows;

\displaystyle L_{\mu\nu}=M_{\mu\nu},\quad L_{-1,0}=D,\quad L_{0,\mu}=\frac{1}{2}(P_{\mu}+K_{\mu}),\quad L_{-1,\mu}=\frac{1}{2}(P_{\mu}-K_{\mu}).

(5.2)

The time-evolved state can be written as

|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-\text{i}Dt}\mathcal{O}(\tau_{0},\vec{x}_{0})|\Omega\rangle=e^{-\text{i}Dt}e^{\rho x^{a}(P_{a}-K_{a})}\mathcal{O}(0)|\Omega\rangle

(5.3)

where
\rho
is determined by
\tau_{0}
. As in the 2D case, the
\mathcal{O}(\tau_{0},\vec{x}_{0})|\Omega\rangle
defines a primary state of the generator defined by

\mathcal{L}_{0}=e^{\rho x^{a}(P_{a}-K_{a})}De^{-\rho x^{a}(P_{a}-K_{a})}.

(5.4)

Noting that
\{D,x^{a}P_{a},x^{a}K_{a}\}
forms a
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
algebra, we find

e^{\rho x^{a}(P_{a}-K_{a})}De^{-\rho x^{a}(P_{a}-K_{a})}=\cosh(2\rho)D-\frac{1}{2}\sinh(2\rho)(P_{a}+K_{a}).

(5.5)

Hence, the structure of the complexity remains analogous to the 2D case.

Third, we can consider the second derivative of the spread complexity. For example, in the global AdS
3
, we find

\ddot{C}=\frac{\sinh\rho_{0}}{2}\frac{\langle\psi(t)|(L_{+1}+L_{-1})|\psi(t)\rangle}{\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle}=h\cos t\sinh^{2}\rho_{0}=-\sinh\rho_{0}\dot{p}_{\rho},

(5.6)

which shows that the acceleration of the spread complexity is also related to that of the particles, suggesting an analog of Ehrenfest’s theorem for spread complexity
[
16
]
.

Fourth, from eq. (
5.6
), we see that the operator
(L_{+1}+L_{-1})/2
plays the role of the force. This implies that a state evolved under a more general
SL(2,\mathbb{R})

Hamiltonian
H=\alpha L_{0}+\beta\frac{L_{+1}+L_{-1}}{2}
corresponds to a non-free particle. It would be interesting to explore how the spread complexity encodes features of such accelerated motion.

Fifth, our proposal shares certain similarities with the “complexity = anything” proposal. In this proposal, gravitational observables, defined as integrals over codimension-one surfaces, are specified in terms of two scalar functions:
F_{1}
, which defines the integrand functional, and
F_{2}
, which determines the surface itself. In our case, the holographic spread complexity also depends on two essential ingredients. One is the trajectory of the moving particle, which is determined by Newton’s equation. The other is the observer, which specifies which component of the particle’s momentum is measured.

Sixth, the swichback effect is one of the hallmark features of holographic complexity, reflecting the delayed growth of complexity under perturbation caused by other operator insertions. It has recently been observed in Krylov complexity within the DSSYK model
[
23
]
. Understanding whether and how this effect manifests in Krylov complexity within holographic CFTs would be an important step toward solidifying its role as a holographic observable. In a simple hyperbolic disk model
[
71
]
, it was found that the switchback effect can arise from a small perpendicular displacement of the state’s flow in Krylov space. From the perspective of our proposal, such a displacement may correspond to a perturbation of the dual particle trajectory, potentially induced by interactions or collisions with other particles.

Finally, in our analysis of Rindler spacetime, we inserted the operator only in the right wedge. However, inserting operators in both left and right wedges could prepare states dual to entire spacetime geometries, including the black hole interior. In the semi-classical regime, such configurations may lead to states dual to geodesics crossing the horizon, connecting insertion points in opposite wedges. In such cases, the spread complexity may encode information about the black hole interior. This opens up the exciting possibility of using spread complexity as a probe of quantum gravity effects inside black holes. See
[
43
]
for recent discussions along these lines.

We believe that addressing these questions will yield valuable new insights into the holography of Krylov complexity and quantum complexity, and we plan to revisit some of these ideas in future work.
