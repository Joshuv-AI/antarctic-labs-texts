Clinical applications of tacrolimus in the treatment of ocular diseases: a narrative literature review
Authors: Ali Hendi Alghamdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo narratively review clinical use of topical and periocular tacrolimus in ophthalmology, with emphasis on allergic keratoconjunctivitis (VKC/AKC), ocular GVHD, post‑keratoplasty prophylaxis, and selected keratitis/uveitis indications.MethodsNarrative search of PubMed/Scopus through October 2025 focusing on prospective, randomized, and comparative studies of topical/periocular tacrolimus; recent formulation advances (nanoemulsions, in‑situ gels, cyclodextrin complexes) were included.ResultsAcross VKC/AKC, tacrolimus (0.03–0.1% ointment; 0.05–0.1% solutions/suspensions) improves signs/symptoms and reduces steroid burden; comparative data suggest faster epithelial healing than topical corticosteroid in shield ulcers. In ocular GVHD, pilot randomized data demonstrate symptom relief with acceptable safety. Observational studies suggest benefit as post‑keratoplasty adjunct in high‑risk grafts. Emerging delivery systems address solubility, residence time, and tolerability and show promising preclinical pharmacokinetics.ConclusionTacrolimus is a practical steroid‑sparing therapy for severe ocular surface inflammation. Priority trials should be formulation‑specific with standardized outcomes and pharmacokinetic endpoints, alongside pediatric safety registries and head‑to‑head comparisons versus cyclosporine 2% and soft steroids.

Introduction

Tacrolimus (FK506) was isolated from a soil sample collected from the bottom of Mount Tsukuba in Ibaraki in 1984 [1]. Tacrolimus is a macrolide produced by Streptomyces tsukubaensis[2]. It was among the first immunosuppressants of macrolide family recognized and was found to be highly effective immunosuppressive in vitro [3]. Tacrolimus, a calcineurin inhibitor has been applied to treat skin diseases as a topical form as an alternative to topical steroids. In ocular diseases, it is also broadly used to treat several ocular inflammatory conditions such as scleritis, uveitis, corneal-graft rejection, dry eye, and allergic keratoconjunctivitis with low side effects [4, 5]. Furthermore, its effectiveness motivated pharmaceutical scientists to develop many novel drug delivery systems such as liposomes, nanomicelles, niosomes and nanoemulsions to overcome key barriers—poor aqueous solubility, stability, and limited permeability across ocular barriers [6–8]. Tacrolimus binds FKBP-12 to inhibit calcineurin, preventing NFAT dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation. The resulting blockade suppresses transcription of cytokines, notably IL-2, thereby limiting T-lymphocyte proliferation and downstream inflammatory cascades (Fig. 2) [9, 10]. Through repression of Th1/Th2 cytokines and mast-cell mediators (e.g., prostaglandin D2, histamine), tacrolimus reduces ocular-surface inflammation [11]. Given the drug’s poor aqueous solubility and limited corneal permeability, formulation strategies (e.g., nanoemulsions, nanocapsules, in-situ gels, cyclodextrin complexes) target solubility, residence time, and comfort to achieve clinically meaningful exposure [6–8, 12]. Tacrolimus is effective in inflammatory ocular disease, but its physicochemical properties complicate ophthalmic formulation. One strategy employs tacrolimus-loaded nanocapsules (NCs) for ocular instillation [12]. Albarry et al. [13] assessed the impact of various concentrations of tacrolimus eye suspension on stromal keratocytes and the epithelium of human corneas to identify its safety in cases of vernal keratoconjunctivitis (VKC) through using different tacrolimus concentrations of 0.05%, 0.01%, 0.05%, 0.1%, and 0.2%. They found that all these concentrations revealed no significant adjustments either in corneal transparency or thickness and the 0.1% concentration revealed the best viability of the corneal tissue. In addition, tacrolimus eye suspension was revealed to be effective and safe in severe cases of VKC and the increase in the drug concentration will depend on clinical severity to accomplish the best therapeutic response.Fig. 2Diagram illustrating the main steps in the mechanism of action of tacrolimus. What’s new since the prior comprehensive review?

Compared with the earlier BioDrugs review by Zhai et al. [14], which surveyed systemic and topical tacrolimus across multiple ocular diseases, the present manuscript is explicitly scoped to topical/periocular ophthalmic use and emphasizes post-2012 evidence. First, controlled data have accumulated in key indications—for example, a randomized clinical trial in ocular graft-versus-host disease comparing topical tacrolimus with methylprednisolone showed symptom relief with acceptable safety [15], and prospective pediatric and adult cohorts in VKC/AKC report rapid improvements in signs/symptoms with steroid-sparing effects [10, 16–23]. Second, formulation science has progressed beyond ointments and simple suspensions to nanocapsules, nanoemulsions, and in-situ gels designed to address solubility and residence-time limitations while improving tolerability [6, 12, 24, 25]. Third, recent translational and clinical reviews focused on allergic eye disease synthesize heterogeneous outcomes and underline the need for standardized measures and longer follow-up [20, 26, 27]. Accordingly, this review focuses on indication-specific dosing, vehicle selection, and comparative positioning versus cyclosporine or corticosteroids, supported by comparative tables and figures.

Scope and methods

This narrative review focuses on topical and periocular use of tacrolimus in ophthalmology. We searched PubMed and Scopus through October 2025 for English‑language publications using terms including ‘tacrolimus’, ‘ophthalmic’, ‘vernal’, ‘keratoconjunctivitis’, ‘ocular GVHD’, ‘keratitis’, ‘uveitis’, ‘corneal transplant’, ‘nanoemulsion’, and ‘in‑situ gel’. Randomized and prospective studies, clinically relevant comparative cohorts, and recent formulation research were prioritized.

Clinical uses of topical/periocular tacrolimus

Tacrolimus is employed across ocular-surface and anterior-segment inflammatory disease with two principal objectives: rapid control of inflammation and corticosteroid-sparing. Indications most frequently reported include vernal/atopic keratoconjunctivitis (VKC/AKC), ocular graft-versus-host disease (oGVHD), high-risk corneal graft, herpes simplex stromal keratitis (adjunctive), selected forms of anterior uveitis, and post-refractive corneal haze [4, 5, 15–23, 28–32]. Uses of tacrolimus in keratoconjunctivitis (VKC/AKC)

Clinical context and pathobiology

Keratoconjunctivitis (KC) is driven by coordinated Th1/Th2 responses with contributions from mast cells, eosinophils, basophils, and fibroblasts; etiologies include viral, bacterial, atopic, allergic, and autoimmune mechanisms, and KC coexists with atopic dermatitis in ≈25–40% of patients [33]. VKC is a sight-threatening phenotype with seasonal exacerbations, prevalent among children and young adults in tropical climates [34, 35]. Severe tarsal disease shows giant papillae; eosinophil granule proteins and cytokine/chemokine signaling promote epithelial compromise, fibroplasia, and shield ulcers [26, 27]. Although short steroid bursts control acute flares, cumulative risks (IOP rise, cataract, infection, delayed epithelialization) motivate steroid-sparing maintenance with calcineurin inhibition [36, 37].

Typical use and dosing/formulation

Real-world protocols commonly employ 0.03–0.1% tacrolimus ointment to eyelid/forniceal skin twice daily, tapering to once daily after stabilization; 0.05–0.1% aqueous suspension/solution (compounded) is initiated four times daily and decreased to twice daily, with improvement [10, 16, 19]. For tarsal VKC or shield-ulcer risk, many centres start at 0.1% ointment then down-titrate. Ointment is favored for lid/forniceal-predominant disease or when drop intolerance/ocular pain limits adherence; solutions/suspensions are often selected for bulbar-predominant disease or when ointment blur is a barrier (see Table 2) [10, 16, 19, 20]. Adjuncts (preservative-free lubricants, antihistamine/mast-cell stabilizers) are standard. Combination strategies include cyclosporine 0.05% drops + periocular tacrolimus 0.03% ointment in adults [9] and azelastine 0.05% + tacrolimus 0.1% drops in pediatrics for faster symptomatic decline [21].

Effectiveness

Across prospective cohorts and comparative series, tacrolimus produces clinically meaningful improvements in composite symptom/sign scores (itching, hyperemia, discharge, photophobia), reduces papillary hypertrophy, and improves corneal staining within 2–8 weeks, while enabling steroid tapering or avoidance during maintenance [13, 16–23]. Very-low-dose 0.005% drops provided durable control in steroid-resistant VKC on long-term follow-up [38]. In a mixed VKC/oGVHD cohort, 0.03% ointment reduced corneal and conjunctival inflammation by 8 weeks [39]. A pediatric double-masked study showed 0.1% tacrolimus ointment and 2% cyclosporine both effective over 8 weeks, with sustained gains during an additional open-label tacrolimus phase [10]. Additional series report clinically important benefit of 0.03% ointment in steroid-refractory VKC when combined with ketotifen and lubricants [17], and 0.1% drops rapidly improved symptoms and reduced dendritic cell density in tarsal VKC within 1–4 weeks without IOP elevation or cataract [18]. Pediatric combination therapy with azelastine + tacrolimus improved OSDI and conjunctival hyperemia/papillae more rapidly than tacrolimus alone [21].

Safety/tolerability

The most frequent adverse effect is transient burning/stinging, typically self-limited to the first few days; occasional tingling/discomfort rarely necessitates cessation [16, 18, 19]. Across cohorts, clinically meaningful IOP rise attributable to tacrolimus is uncommon, especially compared with chronic steroid exposure [16, 19, 23]. Initiating periocular ointment in highly inflamed eyes can improve tolerability prior to transitioning to drops; seasonal maintenance is often continued to minimize relapse risk [16, 19, 20].

Appraisal and research needs

Despite supportive prospective cohorts and one masked comparison versus cyclosporine, the evidence remains heterogeneous in dose, vehicle, concomitant therapy, and outcome definitions, limiting pooled effect estimates. Future trials should adopt standardized VKC/AKC activity indices, prespecified steroid-sparing endpoints, and phenotype-stratified analyses (tarsal vs limbal) with defined taper/maintenance protocols; rigorous head-to-head RCTs versus cyclosporine 2% and soft steroids, and formulation-specific dose–response evaluations, are priorities (see Table 1) [10, 11, 20, 40, 41].

Tacrolimus in episcleritis and scleritis

Clinical context and pathobiology

Episcleritis is typically a benign, self-limited inflammation of the episclera, whereas scleritis is a vision- and globe-threatening disorder with autoimmune associations and destructive potential. Immune activation with T-cell and mast-cell mediators contributes to vascular congestion, edema, and tissue damage [42, 43]. Case series describe periocular calcineurin inhibition as a local anti-inflammatory strategy for steroid-intolerant or recurrent disease [44–47].

Typical use and dosing/formulation

Off-label topical/periocular tacrolimus is used mainly as an adjunct in non-necrotizing anterior scleritis and refractory episcleritis: 0.03–0.1% ointment to the eyelid/periocular skin twice daily, with taper to once daily after control; for more active episcleritis, some clinicians add 0.05–0.1% suspension/solution four times daily for 2–4 weeks before taper (see Table 2) [44–47].

Effectiveness

Small case reports and series note reductions in pain/tenderness and vascular injection, steroid-sparing, and control of postoperative necrotizing scleritis when tacrolimus is added locally to systemic therapy [44, 45, 47]. While episcleritis usually remits spontaneously, topical tacrolimus has been helpful in steroid-resistant nodular disease, shortening flare duration and enabling steroid reduction [46, 47].

Safety/tolerability

Local irritation/burning is the most common adverse effect and is generally transient; no signal for scleral thinning or IOP rise attributable to tacrolimus has been observed in these limited reports, although data remain low certainty [44, 45, 47].

Appraisal and research needs

Evidence quality is low (case reports/series, small cohorts) with heterogeneous dosing and concomitant therapy. Prospective controlled studies using validated episcleritis/scleritis activity scores, pain scales, and steroid-sparing endpoints are needed to define the role of topical/periocular tacrolimus relative to NSAIDs, corticosteroids, and systemic immunomodulators [42–47].

Tacrolimus in uveitis

Clinical context and pathobiology

Uveitis comprises diverse non-infectious inflammatory entities driven by dysregulated T-cell responses and cytokine signaling. Experimental models show that topical FK506 suppresses leukocyte infiltration and cytokine expression in endotoxin-induced uveitis, supporting a mechanistic rationale for local calcineurin inhibition [48–51]. Systemic tacrolimus is effective in intermediate/posterior and panuveitides requiring immunosuppression [52–54], but topical/periocular use is being explored for mild–moderate anterior disease.

Typical use and dosing/formulation

In selected anterior uveitis phenotypes where steroid-sparing is desired, compounded 0.05–0.1% tacrolimus suspension/solution is initiated four times daily for induction (4–8 weeks) then reduced to twice daily; ointment 0.03–0.1% twice daily to the lids/periocular skin may be added when concurrent ocular-surface inflammation is present (see Table 2) [48–51, 55–58].

Effectiveness

Preclinical and translational studies demonstrate reductions in cellular reaction and flare with topical/liposomal tacrolimus, and improved exposure with optimized vehicles [48–51, 58]. Limited clinical series report improvement in anterior chamber inflammation and facilitation of steroid taper in selected patients, while systemic tacrolimus retains a primary role for posterior disease [52–54].

Safety/tolerability

Burning/stinging on instillation is common and usually transient. Available pharmacokinetic (PK) work suggests minimal systemic exposure from topical dosing; no consistent signal for IOP elevation has been linked to tacrolimus itself in small cohorts, though standard pressure monitoring is advised during corticosteroid co-administration [57, 58].

Appraisal and research needs

Clinical evidence for topical tacrolimus alone in uveitis remains limited and heterogeneous. Trials should specify anatomic class, standardize inflammation scores and flare/cell grading, prespecify steroid-sparing protocols, and incorporate PK readouts (tear/aqueous levels) to connect formulation to response [48–60].

Tacrolimus in keratitis

Clinical context and pathobiology

Immune-mediated keratitis—including herpes simplex stromal keratitis and post-infectious subepithelial infiltrates—are characterized by T-cell–mediated inflammation and cytokine-driven tissue injury. Calcineurin inhibition can down-regulate corneal inflammatory cascades and reduce stromal haze and neovascularization in models [60, 61].

Typical use and dosing/formulation

As adjunct therapy to pathogen-directed treatment (e.g., systemic/topical antivirals for HSV) and, when indicated, cautious corticosteroid use, tacrolimus 0.05–0.1% solution/suspension four times daily is commonly employed for 2–4 weeks with taper; 0.03% ointment twice daily may be used for symptomatic superficial punctate keratitis (SPK) entities such as Thygeson’s superficial punctate keratitis (see Table 2) [40, 41, 61–63].

Effectiveness

Case series in adenoviral subepithelial infiltrates resistant to steroids showed clinical improvement with 0.03% topical tacrolimus [40, 41]. In animal HSV stromal models, tacrolimus reduced corneal inflammation and improved outcomes, and small clinical reports suggest symptomatic relief in Thygeson’s disease with decreased SPK. [60–63].

Safety/tolerability

Burning/stinging is frequent during initiation and usually subsides within days; no consistent signals for delayed epithelialization or IOP rise have been reported when appropriate antivirals are co-administered for herpetic disease [40, 41, 60–63].

Appraisal and research needs

Most data are non-randomized with short follow-up and variable concomitant antivirals/steroids, limiting effect-size precision. Controlled studies should stratify by etiology (HSV, adenoviral, Thygeson’s), standardize haze/neovascularization and visual outcomes, and quantify steroid-sparing to define the additive value of tacrolimus [40, 41, 60–63].

Tacrolimus in chronic ocular graft versus host disease

Clinical context and pathobiology

oGVHD features immune-mediated lacrimal and conjunctival inflammation with goblet-cell loss, meibomian dysfunction, and severe ocular-surface disease. Local calcineurin inhibition targets T-cell–driven cytokine release and may reduce dependency on topical steroids [15, 28, 29].

Typical use and dosing/formulation

Tacrolimus is used alone or as adjunct to lubrication and punctal occlusion: 0.02–0.1% ointment twice daily to the periocular skin/lids and/or 0.05–0.1% drops four times daily for induction with taper to twice daily maintenance as tolerated (see Table 2) [15, 28, 29].

Effectiveness

A randomized clinical trial showed that topical tacrolimus provided symptom relief comparable to methylprednisolone with acceptable safety [15]. Observational cohorts report improved symptoms/signs and sustained maintenance on tacrolimus ointment, with reduction in steroid requirement [28, 29].

Safety/tolerability

Transient burning or irritation is the most common adverse effect; glaucoma/cataract events during follow-up are typically attributable to concurrent corticosteroid exposure rather than tacrolimus. Regular IOP monitoring is recommended in severe disease and when steroids are co-prescribed [15, 28, 29].

Appraisal and research needs

Although supportive, evidence remains limited in size and heterogeneous in dosing and endpoints. Larger multicenter randomized controlled trials using harmonized ocular Surface Disease Index, corneal staining, Schirmer/tear break up time test (TBUT), and steroid-sparing metrics, along with longer-term safety follow-up, are priorities [15, 28, 29].

Tacrolimus in dry eye disease (DED)

Clinical context and pathobiology

DED is characterized by tear-film instability, hyperosmolarity, and ocular-surface inflammation that drives symptoms and epithelial damage [64]. Tacrolimus is considered for immune-mediated DED and oGVHD-related DED with persistent inflammation or steroid dependence.

Typical use and dosing/formulation

Compounded 0.02–0.05% drops are initiated four times daily for 2–4 weeks, then reduced to twice daily as signs improve; some regimens maintain once-daily prophylaxis during relapse-prone periods. For blepharo-dermatitis overlap or drop intolerance, 0.03% ointment twice daily to lids/periocular skin is used. Newer vehicles—in-situ gels, nanocapsules/Nanocapsules, solid lipid nanoparticles (SLN)/nanostructured lipid carriers (NLC), and cyclodextrin (CD) complexes seek to improve solubility, permeation, residence time, and comfort (see Table 2) [6, 12, 24, 25].

Effectiveness

Early clinical reports and formulation-anchored studies show improvements in TBUT/Schirmer, reduced staining, and symptom relief in selected phenotypes; signals for reduced surface inflammation are strongest with optimized vehicles. In oGVHD-DED, tacrolimus can facilitate steroid-sparing while maintaining symptom control when paired with intensive lubrication [24, 25, 64].

Safety/tolerability

Transient stinging/burning is common at initiation and usually abates; comfort improves as dosing is tapered after induction. No consistent signal for tacrolimus-attributable IOP elevation has been reported in DED cohorts, but routine pressure checks are prudent when corticosteroids are also used [6, 12, 24, 25].

Appraisal and research needs

Evidence spans preclinical to small clinical cohorts with heterogeneous formulations and endpoints. Future trials should prespecify target phenotypes (e.g., inflammatory DED, oGVHD-DED), use harmonized symptom–sign and quality-of-life outcomes, include steroid-sparing as a co-primary endpoint, and embed PK measures (tear/aqueous levels, residence-time proxies) to link delivery science to clinical effect [24, 25, 64].

Conclusion

Topical and periocular tacrolimus is a practical steroid-sparing therapy across anterior-segment inflammatory disease, with the most consistent benefits in VKC/AKC, ocular GVHD–related surface inflammation, high risk corneal transplant settings, and adjunctive use in immune-mediated keratitis. Across these indications, clinically meaningful improvements in symptoms/signs and reduced corticosteroid exposure are achievable when formulation and dose are matched to disease topography (e.g., ointment for lid/forniceal disease; solutions/suspensions for bulbar disease).

The safety profile is favorable: the most frequent events are transient burning or stinging at instillation and occasional periocular irritation, which are typically self-limited. Reports of clinically significant IOP elevation or lens changes attributable to tacrolimus are uncommon relative to chronic corticosteroid exposure, though routine pressure monitoring remains good practice, especially when tacrolimus is co-prescribed with steroids. In herpetic stromal keratitis, tacrolimus should be used adjunctively with appropriate antiviral therapy. Overall, when dosed appropriately and paired with a compatible vehicle, tacrolimus provides reliable control with predictable tolerability.

Recent updates (2024–2025) reinforce and refine these conclusions. A 2025 comparative study reported that tacrolimus eye drops outperformed dexamethasone for epithelial healing and symptom improvement in VKC-associated shield ulcers, with acceptable tolerability [65]. In corneal transplantation, a 2024 randomized clinical trial in high-risk penetrating keratoplasty found good tolerability for 0.1% tacrolimus suspension but no superiority to control for rejection prevention at 52 weeks, emphasizing the need for larger, adequately powered trials and formulation-specific dosing strategies [66]. Formulation science continues to advance: contemporary reviews highlight nano-enabled delivery systems (nanoemulsions, nanocapsules, in-situ gels, cyclodextrin complexes) that improve solubility, residence time, and corneal penetration and may enhance comfort and exposure in future trials [67, 68]; preclinical work with hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin complexes further supports cyclodextrin-enabled tacrolimus as a promising vehicle [69].

Expert opinion

Across indications, tacrolimus’ value is greatest when dosing and vehicle are matched to disease topography: ointment for lid/forniceal-predominant inflammation and compounded solutions/suspensions (or newer nano-enabled systems) for bulbar disease. Evidence is strongest in VKC/AKC and oGVHD surface disease, more limited (but promising) for keratitis adjunct use and high risk corneal transplant. The principal uncertainties are formulation-specific dose–response, durability of steroid-sparing across seasons, and comparative positioning versus cyclosporine 2% and soft steroids.

Research priorities

Formulation-specific, dose-ranging trials. Head-to-head comparisons of 0.03–0.1% ointment versus 0.05–0.1% solutions/suspensions—and newer nano-enabled/in-situ gel systems—should be conducted within indications (VKC/AKC maintenance, oGVHD surface disease, post-keratoplasty prophylaxis). Trials should prespecify taper algorithms and maintenance schedules.Standardized outcomes and steroid-sparing endpoints. Use harmonized, indication-appropriate measures (e.g., VKC/AKC activity indices, OSDI, corneal staining scores, dendritic cell metrics for tarsal disease; Schirmer/TBUT and OSDI for oGVHD-DED; graft-survival/rejection-free survival after keratoplasty) with quantified steroid reduction as a co-primary outcome.Comparative effectiveness. Well-powered RCTs against cyclosporine (e.g., 2%) and soft steroids are needed to define positioning in steroid-sparing algorithms, including non-inferiority/superiority designs and patient-reported outcomes.Pharmacokinetic and translational endpoints. Address core PK barriers—low aqueous solubility, limited corneal permeability, and rapid precorneal clearance—by embedding tear/aqueous tacrolimus levels, residence-time proxies, and exposure–response modeling into clinical trials. Pharmacokinetic and translational endpoints. Address core PK barriers—low aqueous solubility, limited corneal permeability, and rapid precorneal clearance—by embedding tear/aqueous tacrolimus levels, residence-time proxies, and exposure–response modeling into clinical trials. This is especially important for nanoemulsions, nanocapsules/SLN/NLC, in-situ gels, and cyclodextrin complexes to link delivery science with clinical effect.
