Home-Based Cardiac Rehabilitation in Cardiovascular Diseases: A Narrative Review of Progress, Challenges, and Future Directions
Authors: Shuangliang Ma, Junyan Zhang, Yu Yan, Junjie Wang, Yamei Yang, Jumei Yang, Jishi Hu, Jinhu Xu, Yuxiao Li, Li Rao, Yong He, Zhongxiu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac rehabilitation (CR) is an evidence-based secondary prevention strategy for patients with cardiovascular disease, yet participation in conventional center-based programs remains low. Home-based cardiac rehabilitation (HBCR) has emerged as an alternative delivery model intended to reduce access barriers while preserving the core components of CR.MethodsThis narrative review synthesizes contemporary evidence and implementation literature on HBCR, with emphasis on clinical effectiveness, digital and hybrid delivery models, equity, and health-system implementation.ResultsCurrent evidence indicates that HBCR can achieve outcomes comparable to center-based CR in selected clinically stable, low- to moderate-risk patients, including similar effects on mortality, morbidity, exercise capacity, and cardiovascular risk-factor modification. Digital health tools and hybrid models may improve reach, adherence, monitoring, and personalization. However, implementation remains uneven, reimbursement pathways are inconsistent, and many studies have limited representation of women, racial and ethnic minority groups, older adults, socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, and patients with complex comorbidities.ConclusionHBCR should be viewed as a complementary model within comprehensive CR rather than a universal replacement for center-based services. Future work should prioritize transparent implementation frameworks, equitable digital access, long-term outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and identification of patients best served by home-based, center-based, or hybrid CR models.Keywords: home-based cardiac rehabilitation, center-based cardiac rehabilitation, cardiovascular diseases, secondary prevention, narrative review, digital health, hybrid cardiac rehabilitation

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of death worldwide. Heart disease is a main contributor to the burden of CVDs.1 Despite significant advancements in modern medical treatments for heart disease, such as percutaneous coronary interventions for acute coronary events, the risk for recurrent cardiovascular events remains high.2,3 Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) is a well-established multidisciplinary program for secondary prevention of CVDs. Participation in CR reduces cardiovascular mortality and hospital admissions, improves health-related quality of life (HRQOL) and functional capacity, and supports long-term adherence to secondary prevention. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) of CR have consistently demonstrated these benefits in patients with acute coronary syndrome, following coronary revascularization, and in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.4–6 Current studies also demonstrate the cost-effectiveness of CR in these populations.7 Given these benefits, CR receives a class 1A recommendation from the European Society of Cardiology and the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA).8,9 Comprehensive CR is not limited to supervised exercise; therapeutic patient education, behavior change support, risk-factor management, medication adherence, nutrition counselling, smoking cessation, and psychosocial care are central mechanisms through which CR sustains clinical benefit.

However, real-world delivery of traditional center-based CR varies substantially. Only an estimated one in four eligible patients enrolls in CR, and persistent disparities remain by sex, race and ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and geographic location.10–12 Longstanding barriers include geographical distance, work and family commitments, transportation difficulty, limited availability of rehabilitation centers, socioeconomic disadvantage, inadequate referral practices, low motivation, and competing health priorities. The coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic further intensified these barriers,13 but the rationale for alternative CR delivery models predates the pandemic. Home-based cardiac rehabilitation (HBCR) refers to structured delivery of the core components of CR in the patient’s home or community environment, supported by individualized exercise prescription, education, counselling, monitoring, and clinician feedback. Conceptually, CR delivery can be understood along a continuum: center-based CR provides direct in-person supervision, HBCR provides structured rehabilitation outside the center, and hybrid CR combines early or intermittent center-based contact with home-based delivery. The key research gap is not whether HBCR can reproduce selected short-term outcomes in low- to moderate-risk patients, but how it can be implemented safely, equitably, sustainably, and at scale across diverse health systems and patient populations. Therefore, this narrative review summarizes current HBCR practice, evaluates benefits and limitations, and discusses implementation challenges and future directions for digital and hybrid care models.

Methods

This article is a narrative review with thematic synthesis. We searched PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar for English-language publications on HBCR, cardiac telerehabilitation, digital CR, hybrid CR, center-based CR, patient education, behavior change, implementation, equity, and cardiovascular secondary prevention. Priority was given to scientific statements, clinical guidelines, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomized trials, large observational studies, and implementation reports relevant to HBCR delivery and policy. Literature was selected iteratively according to relevance to four predefined themes: current HBCR evidence, participation and access, digital and hybrid models, and equitable implementation. Because the aim was to provide a conceptual and practice-oriented synthesis rather than estimate a pooled effect size, we did not conduct a formal PRISMA-based systematic review or risk-of-bias assessment. Instead, we narratively compared findings across study designs and highlighted areas of consistency, uncertainty, and implementation need.

Current Practice of HBCR

The HBCR model has been studied since the 1990s,14 particularly in a landmark study by DeBusk et al15 in 1994, which demonstrated the positive benefits of HBCR. Home-based programs have been regarded as a promising solution to bridge the gap in the delivery of CR services. Several countries, including Canada, Australia and the United Kingdom, have incorporated HBCR into their national healthcare systems, with the Heart Manual recognized as the “most extensively studied self-management program” in the United Kingdom.16 In 2019, the American Association of Cardiovascular and Pulmonary Rehabilitation (AACVPR) /ACC/AHA scientific statement on HBCR addressed that HBCR refers specifically to the core components of CR delivered in patients’ own environment, rather than CR staff encouraging patients to exercise alone on days when they are not present at center-based CR.16 The HBCR model does not change the core components of the CR programs17 (Figure 1), which includes multidisciplinary interventions incorporating baseline patient assessment, nutritional counseling, risk factor management (lipids, blood pressure, weight, diabetes mellitus, and smoking), psychosocial interventions, physical activity counseling, and exercise training.18 The key difference is that HBCR programs do not offer direct in-person supervision of patients like center-based CR programs.

HBCR has several potential advantages over traditional center-based CR. One significant benefit is its potential ability to overcome logistical barriers such as distance, work conflicts, and severe weather, which often hinder patient participation in center-based CR.16 Additionally, HBCR offers the possibility to expand the breadth and depth of education, counseling, feedback, and monitoring options for patients, as these services can be accessed anytime and anywhere.19 By contrast, center-based CR services are typically limited to a few hours of weekly in-person contact with CR staff. Furthermore, HBCR allows the possibility to improve participation and access to CR services, particularly for underrepresented groups, including women and elderly people who are eligible for CR.20 However, HBCR is not without its disadvantages. First, the absence of specific guidelines or standards for HBCR poses a risk that the quality and effectiveness of HBCR interventions could vary, making it difficult to compare programs and generalize outcomes.16 Furthermore, the additional barrier to widespread adoption of HBCR is the absence of long-term codified reimbursement. For example, most of patients notably lack of reimbursement by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services and other third-party payers in the United States.21

Evidence indicates that CR delivered in the home setting produces outcomes comparable to those achieved through conventional center-based programs.22,23 An increasing body of Cochrane and other systematic reviews of RCTs has demonstrated that, among patients with cardiac conditions, HBCR yields similar effects to supervised center-based CR on mortality, morbidity, and modifiable cardiovascular risk factors.22,24 In an updated Cochrane review encompassing 23 RCTs and 2,890 participants, no significant differences were observed between HBCR and center-based CR across key clinical outcomes at up to 12 months of follow-up, including all-cause mortality [relative risk (RR): 1.19; 95% CI: 0.65–2.16], exercise capacity [standardized mean difference (SMD): −0.13; 95% CI: −0.28 to 0.02], and cardiovascular risk factor modification.22 Beyond equivalence, some evidence suggests potential advantages of remotely delivered models. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 30 telehealth-based RCTs in patients with coronary heart disease reported reductions in rehospitalization and cardiac events (RR: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.39–0.81), along with improvements in risk factor profiles, compared with usual care.23 Similarly, a large cohort study involving 2,556 patients from a demographically diverse population, including individuals at higher risk, found that participation in HBCR was associated with fewer hospitalizations at 12 months relative to center-based CR.25 Adherence also appears to favor HBCR. In a prospective cohort study across 151 US centers involving 99,097 patients, individuals referred to HBCR were approximately four times more likely to initiate participation than those referred to center-based programs.16,26 Consistent with these findings, an observational study within the Veterans Administration system reported a 36% lower mortality rate among HBCR participants compared with nonparticipants.27 Importantly, HBCR has demonstrated a strong safety profile. A systematic review evaluating safety outcomes in 808 patients with various cardiovascular conditions reported an exceptionally low incidence of serious adverse events—approximately one event per 23,823 patient-hours of exercise—with no exercise-related deaths or hospitalizations.28 These results were corroborated by another review of 545 participants, which found no evidence of mortality or clinically significant complications attributable to cardiac telerehabilitation interventions.29

Collectively, these findings suggest that HBCR represents a viable and effective alternative to center-based programs for clinically stable patients at low to moderate risk who are eligible for CR. However, important evidence gaps remain regarding the broader population-level impact of HBCR on cardiovascular health.16 A recent global audit of cardiac rehabilitation programs reported that only 34% of countries offering CR had incorporated HBCR, despite the overall low utilization of traditional center-based models worldwide. Even in settings where HBCR is available, participation typically accounts for only about 20% of enrolled patients.30–32 In addition to limited implementation, concerns persist regarding the representativeness of existing evidence. Most HBCR studies have enrolled relatively small and homogeneous populations, with inadequate inclusion of women, racial and ethnic minority groups, individuals of lower socioeconomic status, and participants across diverse age ranges.16,33 For example, a Cochrane meta-analysis of HBCR trials reported that women constituted less than 20% of the overall study population, with four of 23 trials excluding women entirely; furthermore, race and ethnicity were unreported in the majority of included trials, and the few that did report these data predominantly involved White participants.24 Similarly, the effectiveness of HBCR among patients with more complex clinical profiles—such as those with multiple comorbidities, cognitive impairment, or frailty—remains poorly characterized, as these groups have been consistently underrepresented in prior research.16,22

Taken together, although patients may derive clinical benefits from participation in HBCR that are comparable to those achieved through center-based programs, HBCR remains substantially underutilized and characterized by pronounced disparities in access and participation. Addressing these gaps through sustained efforts to optimize the design, delivery, and dissemination of HBCR for all eligible patients represents both a critical challenge and a promising opportunity to advance contemporary cardiovascular care and population health. The evidence should therefore be interpreted as supportive but not definitive: HBCR is a clinically plausible and increasingly evidence-based delivery model, but its effectiveness depends on patient selection, program fidelity, monitoring intensity, digital access, reimbursement, and local implementation capacity.

Ongoing Efforts to Promote HBCR

Ongoing efforts to advance HBCR concentrate on three key domains (Box 1). First, improving participation among all eligible patients by optimizing referral pathways, enrollment processes, and adherence strategies, thereby ensuring that a greater proportion of patients are identified, engaged, and consistently involved in HBCR. Second, expanding access through the integration of digital health technologies and the implementation of hybrid CR programs, which combine home-based and center-based approaches to accommodate a broader range of patient needs and preferences. Third, promoting equitable delivery by actively addressing disparities in access and participation, ensuring that HBCR programs are inclusive and accessible to diverse populations.

Improving Participation in HBCR

As with center-based CR, patient participation can be a significant challenge in HBCR. Participation strategies should address not only referral and scheduling but also therapeutic patient education and behavior change support, because patients must understand the purpose of rehabilitation, acquire self-management skills, and sustain physical activity and risk-factor control outside the supervised center environment. Several effective evidence-based strategies that improve full participation in center-based CR, such as systematic referral, enrollment strategies, participation incentives, and structured education, are also likely to be successful in improving participation in HBCR.16 Most strategies to help overcome barriers to CR participation include both provider- and system-level components. For example, “TAKEheart”, a new initiative from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, selected two strategies outlined in the Million Hearts/AACVPR Cardiac Rehabilitation Change Package: automatic, opt-out electronic health record referrals for all CR-eligible patients, and use of care coordinators or “liaisons” to educate patients about the benefits of CR and help them enroll during hospitalization or shortly after discharge.12 A study by Grace et al34 demonstrated the effectiveness of a system-based approach to overcome the lack of referral to CR. They developed an automatic electronic medical record-based CR referral system comprising a “default” or “opt-out” CR referral order for all eligible patients, which increased CR referral from 32% to 70%; the combination of automatic CR referral and care coordination resulted in 85% referral and nearly 74% enrollment.

Society and health system-level approaches to improve HBCR participation have included the development and implementation of CR performance measures aimed at encouraging healthcare providers and systems to effectively provide CR to eligible patients.35 For instance, the ACC/AHA published six new recommendations for CR performance measures in 2018.36 Thereafter, these measures began to be used by quality assurance organizations. A new Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS) Cardiac Rehabilitation measure by the National Committee for Quality Assurance was approved for public reporting. This HEDIS measure assesses the percentage of adults 18 years of age and older who attended 2, 12, 24, and 36 CR sessions within various time frames after a cardiac event, providing a standardized way to evaluate and improve CR participation.17

Support for HBCR and reimbursement at the health system level are critical factors influencing the success or failure of HBCR programs. Until the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services and third-party payers obtain sufficient evidence to justify the adoption of policies that provide reimbursement for HBCR services, alternative approaches may include directly contracting with employers or third-party payers to cover the costs of HBCR.16 The Medicare Advantage (MA) Value-Based Insurance Design Model offers MA organizations the opportunity to eliminate cost-sharing for CR, cover alternative CR delivery models, and provide cash or monetary rebates to beneficiaries for their participation in CR. For example, some MA plans offered by Blue Cross Blue Shield Michigan have eliminated cost-sharing for CR, indicating potential opportunities to pilot reimbursement models for HBCR services within the MA Value-Based Insurance Design Model.17 Although, in theory, universal healthcare coverage could reduce some financial barriers to CR participation, the CR participation gap is a multifaceted issue that persists even in countries with universal healthcare, such as Canada37 and the United Kingdom.38 This suggests that reimbursement is not the sole critical factor impacting HBCR implementation. A study by Gaalema et al demonstrated that financial incentives for CR participation and completion significantly influenced CR participation rates and costs for patients receiving Medicaid assistance.39 Thus, incorporating financial incentives may enhance HBCR participation.

All efforts to expand HBCR participation will need to include ways to help improve outcomes in underserved populations.40 It is important to note that methods to increase referral and participation in HBCR may vary depending on local circumstances, as each program and hospital has unique patient populations and barriers to improving quality and access to care. Nevertheless, the widespread adoption of some pragmatic strategies to improve referral and enrollment, together with programmatic adjustments to deliver care efficiently and effectively to more patients, will have a profound impact on the improvement of HBCR delivery.

Expanding Access to HBCR

Ongoing efforts are aiming to explore the potential role of HBCR not only in underrepresented groups but also in patient groups with chronic conditions that are generally excluded from traditional CR programs, including individuals with chronic cardiovascular conditions such as atrial fibrillation,41 heart failure with preserved ejection fraction,42,43 and those who have undergone aortic repair,44 as well as individuals with chronic non-cardiovascular conditions such as certain types of cancer.45,46

The Use of Digital Technology

Early HBCR programs relied primarily on patient self-reporting or intermittent follow-ups, limiting the ability to accurately track exercise adherence, intensity, and physiological outcomes.33 The absence of systematic monitoring and feedback led to inconsistent results and hindered scalability. In contrast, advances in digital technology are increasingly integrated into HBCR, providing both synchronous (ie, virtual HBCR)47,48 and asynchronous monitoring49–51 (ie, remote HBCR). Digital technology-assisted HBCR has the potential to expand access to CR through tailored individual interventions and enhanced two-way human interaction, including patient-to-patient, patient-to-clinician, and clinician-to-clinician communications. To date, various technological tools have been employed to deliver HBCR, including smartphones,51 interactive web applications,52,53 mobile devices,54 biosensing wearables,55 and implanted devices56 for monitoring physical activity, heart rate, blood pressure, electrocardiogram, and other health measures. Multiple studies have demonstrated the feasibility and potential effectiveness of HBCR incorporating digital technology.22,57 A smartphone-based HBCR program showed higher uptake, adherence, and completion rates, along with comparable or superior improvements in functional status relative to traditional center-based CR in patients with acute coronary syndrome.58 In 2019, Kaiser Permanente implemented an HBCR program in which patients exercised independently for seven weeks while receiving weekly coaching via a smartphone application and telephone consultations with case managers. This approach was associated with a 75% increase in CR completion rates, a 30% reduction in hospital readmissions, and a 27% decrease in cardiovascular mortality compared with a center-based CR control group.59 Moreover, a recent review of 113 studies on virtual HBCR found that 48 studies (42.5%) employed multi-component interventions—combining web-based communication, wearable devices, and mobile applications—which led to significantly greater improvements in patient adherence and functional outcomes than programs using a single intervention component.60 Beyond improving adherence and clinical outcomes, digital HBCR allows secondary prevention programs to be delivered over a longer duration than the standard 24–36 sessions typical of traditional CR.33

The integration of digital technologies into HBCR can broaden the reach of CR by enhancing patient uptake and adherence. Despite this potential, implementation within healthcare systems remains limited. Key barriers include low digital health literacy among patients with cardiovascular disease and healthcare professionals, as well as a lack of cost-effectiveness data in real-world settings.61 To address these challenges and optimize personalized CR across diverse populations, technology must be seamlessly integrated with the core components of HBCR, ensuring that digital tools support program delivery rather than being applied in a purely mechanical fashion, consistent with recommendations from the AHA.57 Furthermore, technology-enabled HBCR holds promise for more comprehensive delivery of CR elements in the future,18 encompassing additional factors such as sleep, mental health, social engagement, and quality of life.

Offering Hybrid CR Programs

Remote HBCR programs further benefit from interaction with center-based programs, providing patients with both supervised care and the flexibility of home-based rehabilitation. Consequently, a flexible delivery model—hybrid CR, which integrates the complementary strengths of short-term center-based CR and HBCR62—represents a promising strategy to expand CR coverage and improve access for a larger number of eligible patients. For specific patient populations—especially those with fragile or complex conditions, such as left ventricular assist device recipients, survivors of exercise-related sudden cardiac death, or adults with complex congenital heart disease—hybrid CR provides enhanced monitoring during the initial exercise phase, ensuring safe participation under direct professional supervision. Importantly, exercise barriers are not solely physical and may also involve psychosocial factors.63 Initial center-based sessions can address these concerns, facilitate participation in group education, and allow individualized exercise prescriptions under face-to-face supervision. Clinical evidence supports the comparable efficacy of hybrid CR relative to traditional models. The QUALIREHAB trial, conducted across 21 centers in France with 142 patients, reported significant improvements in HRQOL for congenital heart disease patients assigned to hybrid CR (SMD: 3.8; 95% CI: 0.2–7.3).64 Additionally, a systematic review and meta-analysis of nine randomized clinical trials comparing hybrid CR with usual care demonstrated greater improvements in peak oxygen uptake among heart failure patients (95% CI: 5.12–14.33).62 The ongoing mTECH REHAB trial is evaluating a hybrid CR model that combines initial in-center sessions with HBCR delivered via the Corrie Health technology platform, aiming to assess changes in functional status, safety, and other clinical outcomes across diverse CR-eligible populations, further illustrating the potential value of hybrid CR programs.54

Despite the demonstrated benefits of hybrid CR, several challenges remain for its widespread implementation. Barriers include limited availability of resources, variability in patient digital literacy, and the need for clinician training to effectively integrate home-based and center-based components. To maximize the impact of hybrid CR, future research should focus on identifying optimal delivery strategies, evaluating long-term patient-centered outcomes, and exploring cost-effectiveness across diverse populations and healthcare settings. Leveraging technology to facilitate remote monitoring, real-time feedback, and personalized interventions will be critical to overcoming these barriers and ensuring equitable access to hybrid CR for all eligible patients.

Enhancing the Equitable Delivery of HBCR

To promote equitable delivery of HBCR, efforts should focus on preventing the widening of existing disparities in CR while integrating additional strategies to address inherent inequities in CR.

The use of digital technology holds potential for improving health equity by addressing factors such as work conflicts, time constraints, geographic barriers, and CR deserts.57 However, rapidly advancing technology may also exacerbate the exclusion of certain sociodemographic subgroups or individuals with disabilities, further broadening the digital divide.65,66 Leveraging appropriate health technology is key to improving access to HBCR in an equitable manner. The Association of American Medical Colleges has published competencies for telemedicine including considerations of equity:67 (1) describing and mitigating implicit and explicit biases during telehealth encounters; (2) defining how telehealth can promote health equity and leveraging technology to bridge gaps in access to care; and (3) accommodating patients’ needs, circumstances, preferences, and potential cultural, social, physical, cognitive, linguistic, and communication barriers in telehealth. When incorporating digital technology in HBCR, clinicians should uphold these standards to prevent, mitigate, or address biases related to culture, socioeconomic status, and physical and mental capability. A recent report outlined a 5-step approach to establishing a hybrid in-center and virtual CR program using technology that promotes equitable access for patients with varying levels of digital literacy.68 In addition, selecting wearables with large screens or ensuring that mobile apps have large fonts may be important considerations for older adults who may have impaired vision or dexterity.57

Other approaches to reducing health inequalities in HBCR involve leveraging existing resources. Direct patient care interventions should be implemented throughout various healthcare settings. Healthcare teams should receive education on barriers to care and facilitators of inequities.69 Increasing diversity within the healthcare workforce, including greater racial, ethnic, geographic, and gender diversity, can promote better access to and quality of care.70 Healthcare professionals who are Black, Hispanic/Latino, and American Indian are more likely to practice in under-resourced communities and improve care in these areas.71 Additionally, patient education is crucial. Health-related information should be delivered in a manner consistent with the patient’s literacy, preferred language, and linguistic habits.72 Optimizing multidisciplinary collaboration and interventions is also essential. A collaborative team may include clinicians, nurses, dietitians, pharmacists, case managers, and community health workers (CHWs), who provide patients with core multicomponent CR interventions of exercise training, physical tracking, medication management, nutritional counseling, cardiovascular disease risk factor modification, and psychosocial management. An example of this approach is demonstrated in the study by Dorje et al on the HBCR in coronary heart disease (SMART-CR/SP) study, which included patient-centered cardiovascular risk factor monitoring and management support, and cardiovascular health education over six months.51 In many under-resourced areas, CHWs act as frontline public health workers, bridging the gap between communities and health systems to motivate patients to participate in their care. Substantial evidence supports the effectiveness of interventions that engage CHWs in a team-based care model to improve blood pressure, blood glucose, and other CVD risk factors in at-risk patients.73,74

The importance of using community resources to deliver HBCR to underserved populations is increasing. Community-engaged interventions ensure that patients have the necessary resources to complete their CR programs and maintain a healthy lifestyle beyond graduation. Community-based organizations can facilitate access to resources such as reliable internet and internet-enabled devices, exercise equipment, healthy lifestyle programs, translation services, and peer support.17 An ongoing trial at the Johns Hopkins mTECH Center, the mTECH REHAB trial, exemplifies a partnership with the Community Advisory Board to promote health equity.54 In addition, issues related to insurance reimbursement, which were discussed earlier in this paper, are also pertinent to ensuring equitable access to HBCR.

Future Directions for HBCR

Traditional, in-person CR has a significant and positive effect for eligible patients with CVDs. Due to disparities in access, referral, and participation, the benefits of CR remain largely unattained by large numbers of eligible people. As novel strategies emerge, HBCR has the potential to offer adaptive, personalized, safe, and accessible CR options tailored to the diverse needs of patients at the right time, in the right place, and by the right clinicians. However, important questions remain about how to make HBCR participation more accessible, affordable, equitable, and sustainable. Future research should move beyond efficacy alone and incorporate implementation science outcomes, including adoption, reach, fidelity, acceptability, cost, maintenance, and equity impact. On the basis of these knowledge gaps, several opportunities for improving HBCR include: (1) including diverse populations in HBCR research, especially patients with congenital heart disease, atrial fibrillation, heart transplantation, valvular heart disease, frailty, multimorbidity, and social vulnerability; (2) assessing long-term effectiveness and safety beyond 12 months; (3) leveraging implementation methodologies and digital techniques, including artificial intelligence where appropriate, to enhance consistency and high-quality delivery; (4) clarifying which populations are most likely to benefit from facility-based CR, virtual or remote HBCR, or hybrid CR; and (5) comparing traditional in-person CR with virtual, remote, and hybrid HBCR in large multicenter studies that evaluate clinical, psychosocial, cost-effectiveness, health-system, and patient-centered outcomes.

Conclusion

HBCR is an important and increasingly mature delivery strategy for comprehensive CR. The central take-home message of this review is that HBCR should be developed as a complementary, evidence-informed model that expands access while preserving the core functions of CR: supervised risk assessment, therapeutic patient education, behavior change support, exercise training, risk-factor management, and psychosocial care. Current evidence supports HBCR as a viable alternative for selected low- to moderate-risk patients who are unable or unlikely to participate in center-based CR, but broad conclusions must be tempered by methodological limitations, uneven implementation, and persistent inequities in access and participation. Priority should now be given to implementation-ready HBCR and hybrid CR models that are safe, reimbursable, digitally inclusive, and adaptable to diverse health systems. Clinicians, administrators, policymakers, and communities share responsibility for creating pathways that enable more eligible patients to achieve durable cardiovascular health.
