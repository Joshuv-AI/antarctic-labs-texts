Explainable AI with fine-tuned large language models for sustainable cultural heritage management
Authors: Kai Zhou, Wei Chen, Wenqi Sheng, Bin Hu, Yu Yang, Lili Yang, Huipin Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The redevelopment of cultural heritage areas, especially in historical urban environments, requires a nuanced understanding of public perceptions to balance preservation with modernization. This study introduces an advanced AI-driven framework for Aspect Sentiment Quadruple Prediction (ASQP) to assess public perceptions of Lijiang Ancient Town, a UNESCO World Heritage site in China. We fine-tuned the large language model Qwen-14B using LoRA-based methods to augment sentiment data, effectively uncovering implicit emotional cues in social media content. The model integrates BERT, multi-layer BiLSTM, self-attention, CNN, and CRF for enhanced entity recognition and sentiment classification. Experimental results show that the enhanced model (Qwen-14B + ASQP) improved F1-score by 0.97% (from 75.42% to 76.39%) and Precision by 4.48% (from 76.14% to 80.62%) compared to the baseline. Analyzing data from platforms such as Weibo, Dazhong Dianping, and Xiaohongshu (2018–2024), the research uncovers factors influencing public perception, offering insights for heritage site management, urban planning, and the sustainable preservation of cultural heritage.

Introduction

The preservation of cultural heritage in historical urban areas is a significant challenge for sustainable urban development. Public perceptions of the built environment are central to how these spaces are developed, conserved, and incorporated into urban planning. Striking a balance between safeguarding historical values and meeting the demands of modern urbanization is crucial for ensuring the long-term sustainability of cultural heritage sites. Developing adaptive strategies that align with both preservation goals and urban growth is essential for fostering a harmonious coexistence.

Traditional sentiment analysis methods have been useful in capturing public perceptions; however, they often fail to address the complexities and context-specific nuances of emotional responses toward cultural heritage sites. Techniques such as sentence-level sentiment classification and Importance-Performance Analysis (IPA) offer some insights but do not adequately capture the emotional subtleties associated with heritage preservation.

Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis (ABSA) has emerged as a more refined method, extracting detailed sentiments related to specific aspects of the built environment. A more advanced approach, Aspect Sentiment Quadruple Prediction (ASQP), incorporates not only sentiment polarities but also aspect terms, categories, and opinions (Fig. 1). Despite these advancements, challenges remain in capturing implicit sentiments—subtle, often unconscious emotional responses that are critical in shaping public perceptions of heritage environments.

This study aims to investigate the following research question: “How can AI-driven frameworks, such as ASQP, improve the understanding and analysis of implicit sentiments in public perceptions of cultural heritage sites like Lijiang Ancient Town?” The hypothesis is that incorporating advanced AI techniques, such as LoRA-based fine-tuning on large language models, will enhance the detection of implicit emotional cues, leading to more accurate sentiment analysis of social media content related to heritage sites.

This study introduces the application of Aspect Sentiment Quadruple Prediction (ASQP) to Lijiang Ancient Town, a UNESCO World Heritage site, leveraging AI technologies to improve sentiment understanding. The ASQP framework integrates BERT, multi-layer BiLSTM networks, multi-head self-attention, CNN, and CRF for enhanced sentiment classification and entity recognition. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) ensures transparency in model outputs, enhancing interpretability. Moreover, LoRA fine-tuning on large models allows for implicit sentiment data augmentation, where the model infers emotional polarity from “implicit aspects” and “implicit opinions” that lack explicit terms (originally represented as “NULL” in Fig. 2), using Qwen-14B to generate these inferred cues via large language model augmentation.

The findings contribute to the growing body of knowledge on AI’s role in urban development and cultural heritage management, offering insights that can inform sustainable urban planning and preservation strategies. Although the model is trained on a Chinese-language private dataset and an English-language public dataset, the framework itself is not language-dependent. In this study, we adopt BERT-base-Chinese and BERT-base-cased for representation learning. In different linguistic contexts, these models can be replaced with their corresponding pre-trained BERT variants or multilingual models. Thus, the methodological contributions—such as LoRA-based augmentation for implicit sentiment detection, multi-layer neural architectures for aspect and sentiment modeling, and SHAP for interpretability—are transferable across languages and regions. Literature review

Early research on cultural heritage preservation largely relied on questionnaire surveys to capture the public’s perceptions of the quality and significance of the built environment. Structured instruments offer consistency, allow the use of Likert-type metrics for statistical analysis, and can be deployed at scale and at a low cost. They have therefore become ubiquitous in studies ranging from augmented-reality engagements with Renaissance villages1 to expectancy-disconfirmation surveys in UNESCO World-Heritage cities2.

Yet questionnaire-based approaches face well-documented constraints. Acquiescence bias, building-specific framing and the inability to probe contextual subtleties may obscure the root causes of experience (e.g., uniform colour versus micro-climate)2. To overcome these deficits, scholars increasingly integrate spatial multi-criteria methods—notably analytic hierarchy process (AHP) coupled with geographic information systems (GIS)—to embed social, economic and physical layers into heritage-protection or attractiveness models3–5. Although such hybrid frameworks enrich spatial decision-making, they still depend on a priori criteria and small expert or visitor panels, limiting sensitivity to emergent sentiments expressed outside formal surveys.

Recent advances in natural language processing (NLP) provide an alternative by mining large volumes of unstructured, user-generated text. Initial heritage applications focused on value auditing of conservation documents or knowledge-base construction6,7. Sentiment analysis of social-media posts and online reviews soon followed, revealing nuanced drivers of tourist satisfaction across regions and landscape typologies8–10. Studies linking sentiment scores with landscape attributes, ecosystem-service perception or crowding patterns further demonstrate NLP’s capacity to capture socio-spatial heterogeneity that surveys overlook9,10.

Most early NLP investigations employed lexicon-based or classical machine-learning classifiers, which infer global polarity but ignore the specific aspect that triggers each emotion. As a result, ambiguous terms such as cold or bright are frequently misclassified when context shifts from thermal comfort to food temperature or calendar colour schemes11.

To achieve finer granularity, researchers adopted Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis (ABSA), which jointly extracts aspect terms, opinion words and sentiment polarity. ABSA has been applied to tourism behaviour12, the attractiveness of shrinking areas13 and spatiotemporal monitoring of high-grade tourist attractions in China14. Advanced neural models—BERT variants with local context focus, BiLSTM–CRF pipelines, or grid-tagging schemes—have improved extraction accuracy. Nevertheless, most heritage studies stop at triplet (target–aspect–sentiment) detection.

The emerging Aspect Sentiment Quadruple Prediction (ASQP) task adds an explicit category dimension, enabling simultaneous identification of what (target), which facet (aspect), how (sentiment) and why (opinion)15,16. Quadruple extraction has demonstrated superior performance over triplet models in product review corpora; however, it remains largely unexplored in the context of the public’s perception of the built environment in cultural heritage.

A parallel line of work addresses the “black-box’’ nature of deep NLP models through Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI). Post-hoc methods such as SHAP, LIME, Anchor and local rule-based explanations have been combined with fine-tuned BERT architectures to visualise feature importance and build stakeholder trust in software-requirements elicitation17 or restaurant review classification18. However, heritage-specific deployments of XAI remain scarce, leaving practitioners without transparent guidance on which spatial or cultural facets drive positive or negative sentiment.

A persistent challenge is the detection of implicit aspects and emotions that lack explicit lexical cues. Instruction-tuned frameworks with auxiliary-sentence construction and knowledge-enhanced graph convolution have recently achieved state-of-the-art performance in benchmark ABSA datasets19,20.

Recent advances in LLM-based ABSA techniques, such as the QAIE approach, have shown great promise in improving sentiment extraction accuracy, especially in few-shot learning scenarios. The QAIE model uses LLMs for quantity augmentation and information enhancement, resulting in improved recall for implicit sentiments34. The integration of LLMs, like GPT-4 and Llama, has led to the development of more robust frameworks for cultural heritage sentiment analysis, allowing models to better recognize and interpret implicit emotional cues in user-generated content. Large Language Models (LLMs) can also generate synthetic, aspect-rich training data, reducing annotation costs and improving recall for subtle affective expressions. The use of LLMs has been demonstrated to significantly enhance the detection of implicit sentiment in heritage-related sentiment analysis tasks, ensuring better generalization and more accurate sentiment predictions35. The CATS system, for instance, leverages translations produced by an LLM to enhance cultural-heritage image-generation accuracy21.

Overall framework

The proposed ASQP model consists of three sequential phases: Phase 1 (LoRA-based data augmentation), Phase 2 (entity recognition), and Phase 3 (aspect category and sentiment classification with SHAP for interpretability). This design simplifies the overall workflow while maintaining robustness in handling noisy cultural heritage data (Fig. 3).

In Phase 1, large language models (LLMs) are fine-tuned using LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) to generate augmented training data. This produces text summaries that explicitly include aspect categories and sentiment labels, enriching the dataset with implicit emotional cues that are often absent in raw social media text.

In Phase 2, entity recognition is performed using a hybrid model that integrates BERT, multi-layer BiLSTM, multi-head self-attention, and Conditional Random Fields (CRF).

In Phase 3, the extracted entities are further processed by a classification model that combines BERT, BiLSTM, self-attention, and CNN. The system assigns each entity to predefined categories (e.g., Cultural Experience, Facility Quality) and sentiment polarities across five levels (negative, slightly negative, neutral, slightly positive, positive). To enhance interpretability, SHAP analysis is applied, revealing the contribution of words and phrases to sentiment predictions. This transparency provides heritage managers with actionable insights into which cultural or spatial factors most strongly shape public perception.

Implicit data augmentation

Social media reviews often convey multiple sentiment aspects but lack explicit aspect terms and sentiment words. Emotional cues are often conveyed indirectly through context or phrasing. For example, “The service could have been faster” implies dissatisfaction with speed, even though explicit negative words like “bad” or “slow” are not used. Capturing these implicit sentiments is crucial for a nuanced understanding of public perceptions, especially in cultural heritage contexts.

This study proposes implicit data augmentation by generating sentiment-enriched data using Large Language Models (LLMs). Implicit data augmentation generates training examples where unspoken sentiments are made explicit. For example, the review “The service could have been faster” can be augmented to “The service was not fast enough, leading to dissatisfaction,” making the implicit sentiment explicit. This helps the model identify sentiments implied through contextual cues, improving its ability to handle indirect emotional expressions.

In this work, LLMs are not directly used as the discriminative ASQP predictor. Instead, they are employed in a generative role to create augmented, sentiment-enriched training data, which is then used to train a separate discriminative ASQP model. Fine-tuned LLMs are used to generate sentiment-enriched data by summarizing input reviews with aspect terms (e.g., “service”) and sentiment words (e.g., “slow” or “unsatisfactory”). This method enables the model to better process subtle public sentiments and improves performance in Aspect Sentiment Quadruple Prediction (ASQP).

Fine-tuning Large Language Models (LLMs) is a well-established method for adapting pre-trained models to specific tasks using task-specific data22. However, pre-trained LLMs often lack the specific capabilities required for Aspect Sentiment Quadruple Prediction (ASQP). Major model structures

The proposed ASQP model integrates advanced computational methods across three main layers: embedding, implicit feature extraction, and sentiment classification. The implicit feature extraction layer, consisting of dual-layer BiLSTM, multi-head self-attention, and CNN, effectively captures subtle textual features critical for accurate sentiment analysis (Fig. 5).

Fig. 5Diagram of the main model structure for the ASQP task.

Diagram of the main model structure for the ASQP task.

In this model, BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) is employed exclusively for generating textual embeddings—numerical representations of text that capture semantic meaning24. BERT is advantageous for embedding text due to its capability to capture complex language structures and context-dependent meanings25. BERT transforms input text into vectors (embeddings) by considering both preceding and following context simultaneously, thus providing more meaningful representations of words within sentences.

The dual-layer BiLSTM component enhances the model’s capacity to handle sequential data by processing textual information from both forward (beginning-to-end) and backward (end-to-beginning) directions simultaneously26. Each BiLSTM layer captures intricate relationships between words, retaining context-rich information important for sentiment analysis27. Utilizing two layers allows deeper comprehension of complex and nuanced sentiments typically expressed by visitors, such as subtle positive or negative experiences in historical environments.

Following BiLSTM, the multi-head self-attention mechanism highlights the most informative parts of the text by weighing different words according to their significance. This approach effectively identifies key phrases or sentences that have a strong influence on overall sentiment. Subsequently, a convolutional neural network (CNN) consolidates multiple attention-derived features into a single, refined feature set, simplifying the data and reducing irrelevant details or noise.

The final sentiment classification layer uses these refined textual features to categorize the extracted sentiments into clearly defined categories, such as positive, neutral, or negative. This integration of embedding, sequential modeling, attention mechanisms, and feature refinement results in a robust and interpretable sentiment analysis framework.

CRF is used to refine entity extraction by modeling the sequential dependencies between aspect and opinion terms, ensuring accurate tagging of extracted entities in the context of sentiment analysis.

Experiment and datasets

The datasets utilized in this study were sourced from prominent Chinese social media platforms—Weibo, Dazhong Dianping, and Xiaohongshu—covering reviews from 2018 to 2024 related to Lijiang Ancient Town. Rigorous preprocessing was conducted to remove duplicates, irrelevant posts, advertisements, and entries with insufficient detail, yielding a refined dataset of 32,074 textual entries. A representative subset, comprising approximately one-third of the total data, was selected for annotation, specifically tailored to the study’s analytical needs. Each annotated entry clearly defined sentiment quadruples, including aspect terms, aspect categories, sentiment polarities (positive, negative, neutral), and associated opinion words.

Accurate annotation is critical to developing reliable sentiment analysis models. Given the unique context of heritage tourism and nuanced sentiments in public reviews of Lijiang Ancient Town, a specialized annotation framework was established. Annotation guidelines drew on authoritative references including the Nara Document on Authenticity (ICOMOS, 1994), the World Heritage Convention, and the ICOMOS International Cultural Tourism Charter (1999), ensuring thematic coherence and analytical rigor. The annotation process was performed by a team comprising five postgraduate students and one doctoral candidate specializing in architecture, employing NVivo software for systematic data coding. Each textual entry received dual annotations to ensure accuracy and consistency. The initial Kappa value (0.56) was largely attributed to two factors: (i) the semantic proximity of sentiment polarity categories such as ‘slightly positive’ vs. ‘positive’ and ‘slightly negative’ vs. ‘negative’; and (ii) overlaps between similar aspect categories, for example “Modernization and Personalized Experience” vs. “Authenticity and Integrity of Heritage,” “Dining and Accommodation Services” vs. “Commercialized Experience,” or “Authenticity and Integrity of Heritage” vs. “Historical Narrative and Educational Depth.” In addition, the prevalence of implicit sentiment expressions in culturally nuanced contexts further complicated annotation. Discrepancies were first discussed between the two annotators; unresolved cases were escalated to the architectural professor and associate professor for final arbitration, ensuring consensus on ambiguous sentiment expressions. To improve consistency, the annotation guidelines underwent multiple rounds of refinement and pilot re-annotation. Specifically, a 15% subset of the dataset—comprising both randomly selected texts and previously contentious cases—was repeatedly re-annotated after each guideline update. This iterative process ensured that the revised framework was tested against both typical and ambiguous samples. As a result, inter-annotator agreement progressively improved, ultimately achieving a high Cohen’s Kappa value of 0.87, confirming the robustness of the updated guidelines.

Throughout the annotation process, categories were continuously refined, and each text entry was systematically annotated with a sentiment quadruple comprising aspect terms (specific features mentioned in reviews), aspect categories (broader thematic classifications), opinion words (words expressing specific sentiments), and sentiment polarities. The sentiment polarities were encoded as Negative (0), Slightly Negative (1), Neutral (2), Slightly Positive (3), and Positive (4).

Experimental setup and evaluation metrics

Experiments were conducted using Python 3.13.5 on a system equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU and an AMD Ryzen 9 9950 × 3D CPU (16 cores, 32 threads). Models were implemented in PyTorch (version 2.7.0 + cu128), with the HuggingFace Transformers library (version 4.54.1) for embedding extraction, running on CUDA 12.8. The pre-trained BERT-base-cased model provided initial token embeddings with an encoding dimension of 768, and the BERT parameters were frozen throughout training to mitigate overfitting. The embeddings served as inputs to a dual-layer BiLSTM, configured with a hidden state dimension of 768 to effectively capture sequential contextual relationships. An 8-head self-attention mechanism was applied to emphasize salient text features, followed by a convolutional layer equipped with five 5 × 5 kernels for further feature refinement. Optimization was performed using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 5e-5, a batch size of 32, and a maximum input sequence length of 256 tokens.

The performance of the Aspect Sentiment Quadruple Prediction (ASQP) model is assessed using several standard metrics commonly employed in classification tasks, namely Accuracy (Acc), Precision (P), Recall (R), F1-Score (F1). These metrics offer a comprehensive understanding of the model’s ability to accurately classify aspects, sentiments, and opinions in the context of heritage environment perception.

Accuracy quantifies the proportion of correct predictions made by the model, calculated as the ratio of correct predictions to the total number of predictions. where TP refers to True Positives (correctly predicted positive instances), TN denotes True Negatives (correctly predicted negative instances), FP represents False Positives (incorrectly predicted as positive), and FN indicates False Negatives (incorrectly predicted as negative).

Precision measures the accuracy of the model’s positive predictions. It is calculated as the ratio of True Positives to the total number of predicted positives, expressed as:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}$$\end{document}

where TP is the number of True Positives, and FP is the number of False Positives.

where TP is the number of True Positives, and FN is the number of False Negatives.

Discussion

In this study, we introduce a framework for analyzing public perceptions of the built environment in cultural heritage areas, focusing on Lijiang Ancient Town. The framework uses the Aspect Sentiment Quadruple Prediction (ASQP) model, which incorporates BERT embeddings, BiLSTM networks, and LoRA-based fine-tuning. By extracting sentiment polarities alongside aspects, opinions, and categories, the model provides a nuanced understanding of public perceptions. Compared to baseline methods, ASQP demonstrated superior performance in terms of F1-score and recall, emphasizing its capacity to capture subtle emotional expressions in cultural heritage contexts.

Fine-tuning large language models with implicit sentiment data augmentation resulted in improved model performance. Augmenting training data with implicit emotional cues enhanced both F1-score and Precision, showcasing the value of context-sensitive sentiment analysis for capturing subtle, indirect expressions of visitor emotions.

Our analysis exclusively relied on publicly available social media content, ensuring no private or personally identifiable information was used. Although social media sentiment signals can be affected by coordinated activity or commercial promotion, we carefully filtered anomalies from our dataset. Future work will validate findings through triangulation with survey data to strengthen the robustness of sentiment-informed heritage management.

While Qwen-14B with LoRA-based fine-tuning provided notable improvements, it also introduced significant computational costs. Although LoRA helped reduce the memory footprint and accelerate fine-tuning, real-time deployment still faces latency challenges. Future efforts will explore model compression techniques and more efficient deployment strategies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our experiments were limited to text data from Lijiang Ancient Town, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings to other heritage sites or cultural contexts. The dataset, sourced from platforms like Weibo, Dazhong Dianping, and Xiaohongshu, may be biased demographically, as certain groups (e.g., older adults or less tech-savvy individuals) could be underrepresented. Additionally, the current framework processes only textual data, excluding multimodal cues such as images, videos, and audio, which could provide complementary emotional signals. Furthermore, implicit sentiment labeling, based on heuristic rules and human annotation, may introduce subjectivity. The imbalance in sentiment categories, particularly the scarcity of slightly positive and slightly negative sentiments, could lead to skewed predictions, affecting the model’s recall for subtle shifts in sentiment.

Error analysis revealed difficulties in handling implicit and sarcastic expressions, leading to misclassification of sentiment polarity. Overlapping aspect categories (e.g., “dining experience” vs. “public facilities”) and class imbalance in minority categories, such as “Accessibility Features,” also posed challenges. The model’s performance declined when applied to unrelated domains, underscoring the need for domain adaptation. These issues highlight the need to refine implicit sentiment detection, improve aspect category disambiguation, address data imbalance, and enhance the model’s transferability before real-world deployment.

The reliance on pre-trained models raises concerns regarding domain-specific vocabulary. Although fine-tuning improved performance in the heritage tourism context, further refinements, such as incorporating specialized cultural terms, could enhance the model’s ability to detect subtle aspects of visitor sentiment. Additionally, improving the interpretability of ASQP remains a challenge. While SHAP analysis provided initial insights into feature importance, further advancements in explainable AI could clarify the model’s decision-making process, enhancing its utility for heritage managers and policymakers.

The findings of this study align with existing theories of sentiment formation in environmental psychology and urban heritage tourism39. Visitor sentiment is shaped by cognitive appraisals of environmental quality, place attachment, and authenticity40. Our ASQP results—negative sentiments regarding commercialization and positive sentiments concerning cultural preservation—reflect these established mechanisms of affect formation. By linking AI-driven sentiment analysis with psychological and tourism theories, the framework not only identifies sentiment patterns but also explains how visitors’ emotional responses emerge from the interaction between environmental cues and cultural expectations.

Looking ahead, the ASQP framework holds potential for proactive heritage management. By forecasting sentiment shifts, heritage site managers could anticipate issues such as dissatisfaction or appreciation in specific aspects, enabling preemptive interventions to manage public sentiment before it significantly impacts the site’s overall perception and sustainability.

In practice, the ASQP framework helps identify aspects of visitor perception that influence satisfaction. For instance, positive sentiments around cultural preservation suggest areas for targeted enhancement, while negative sentiments related to commercialization point to the need for regulatory measures. Conclusion

This study introduces and fine-tunes the Aspect Sentiment Quadruple Prediction (ASQP) model to analyze public perceptions of the built environment in Lijiang Ancient Town. By utilizing advanced deep learning techniques such as BERT, multi-layer BiLSTM, and LoRA-based fine-tuning, the model captures sentiment polarities, aspects, opinions, and categories influencing public perceptions. The results show that ASQP outperforms traditional sentiment analysis methods, offering deeper insights into implicit sentiments and enhancing interpretability through SHAP analysis. This approach provides valuable insights for heritage site management and can inform policies to improve public satisfaction and sustainable development in urban heritage environments.

The ASQP model demonstrates the potential for AI-driven sentiment analysis in enhancing the management of cultural heritage sites, ensuring that visitor experiences are accurately captured and addressed. By integrating advanced machine learning techniques with heritage management practices, this study contributes to the growing field of AI-driven solutions for sustainable urban development and cultural preservation.
