Predictors of Incident Dementia in a Cohort of Elders with Essential Tremor: A Prospective, Longitudinal Study
Authors: Diane S. Berry, Stephanie Cosentino, Elan D. Louis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionElders with essential tremor (ET) are at a heightened risk for incident dementia. Although the consequences of this diagnosis are formidable, we know little about what factors elevate the risk of ET-dementia. We analyzed data from a large, prospective, longitudinal study of cognitive status in an ET cohort to examine whether a range of demographic, lifestyle, and clinical variables contribute to dementia risk.MethodsThe cognitive status (normal cognition [NC], mild cognitive impairment [MCI], or dementia) of a baseline sample of 350 ET cases (M age = 79.5 years) was tracked for up to 7.5 years (M follow-up = 4.7 years). Cox proportional hazards regressions identified baseline characteristics that predicted the development of dementia.ResultsOlder age and greater cognitive impairment at baseline (MCI versus NC) each predicted incident dementia, HR > 1.10, p < 0.0001. Diminished functional abilities (HR = 0.39), greater gait impairment (HR = 1.30), lower confidence in one’s balance skills (HR = 0.98), and more depressive symptoms at baseline (HR = 1.08) further predicted conversion to dementia, all p < 0.006. Neither continuous nor categorical measures of ET onset age predicted incident ET-dementia.ConclusionsThese data represent the most comprehensive assessment to date of risk factors for ET-dementia in terms of number of factors studied and cohort size. At least one of these risk factors – depression – is modifiable and may offer intervention points to reduce risk of dementia. Older age of ET onset, previously viewed as a risk factor, did not predict incident ET-dementia.

Introduction

Essential tremor (ET) is one of the most prevalent movement disorders [1] and is estimated to affect approximately 2.2% of the US population [2]. Although ET is best known for its hallmark action tremor [1–5], cognitive decline is a frequently observed non-motor manifestation of ET [6–11]. While some favor classifying the large proportion of ET cases who develop such non-motor features as a separate entity (“ET-plus”), there is considerable debate over the scientific validity of this decision [12–17], and many view the emergence of non-tremor features as merely a stage in the evolution of the ET disease process [15, 17, 18].

An increased risk of dementia is one cognitive feature associated with a diagnosis of ET [19–22]. For example, a prospective study of more than 3,800 elders in Spain revealed that 7.8% of ET cases developed dementia during a 3 year period, a proportion twice that of controls (3.9%) [22]. Similarly, a study of more than 2,000 New Yorkers found that a diagnosis of ET increased the risk of developing dementia over a period of more than 3 years, with incident dementia observed in ET cases more than twice as often as observed in controls (18.3% versus 8.7%) [20]. Consistent with these data, the average cumulative incidence of dementia reported across studies of ET cohorts is a little over 10%, more than twice that observed in the general population [9, 19].

What differentiates ET cases who develop dementia from their peers with ET who do not? Numerous studies investigate risk factors for dementia in either the general population, or, to a lesser extent, in patients diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease (PD), a related tremor disorder [10, 23–34]. This work has identified demographic (e.g., older age [23–25], sex [33]), medical (e.g., presence of head injury [26], diabetes [23], hypertension [27]), psychological (e.g., higher levels of depressive symptoms [24, 28]), sensory (e.g., poor olfaction [29], hearing loss [34]), and lifestyle (e.g., low physical activity [30, 31], not following a Mediterranean-type diet [31], using tobacco [23, 30, 32] or using alcohol [23]) factors that increase the likelihood of developing dementia.

As noted, a diagnosis of ET itself places an individual at a heightened risk for dementia [20–22]. What factors might further exacerbate that risk within an ET cohort? Given the importance of recognizing and treating these individuals in a timely fashion, it would seem critical to identify the variables or combination of variables that increase the risk of ET-linked dementia. However, few existing databases include the prospective longitudinal observations of an ET cohort required to identify specific factors that increase such risks.

To date, the available data indicate that three factors increase dementia risk among ET cases. First, paralleling work with the general population [23, 24] and with PD patients [35], older people with ET are more likely to develop dementia than are younger people [9, 36]. A second risk factor may be the age at which the tremor associated with ET begins; data from a population-based study in Spain [21, 22] indicate that late-onset (i.e., after age 65) ET cases were at greater risk for dementia than controls, whereas cases with tremor onset before age 65 were not. Finally, we recently reported that greater levels of depressive symptoms increase the risk of incident ET-dementia [36].

Our overarching goal was to risk stratify individuals with ET and identify those with the greatest risk of developing dementia. Our primary research questions were: (1) do data from this large cohort provide additional evidence that age, age of onset of ET tremor, or depression increase the risk of incident dementia in people diagnosed with ET? and (2) can we identify additional demographic, lifestyle, or clinical variables that further exacerbate the already heightened risk of incident dementia in this population? To this end, we analyze data drawn from a prospective, longitudinal study of 350 elders with ET for whom cognitive status – defined as either normal cognition (NC), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or dementia – has been tracked for up to 7.5 years. To date, this sample is the largest cohort of ET elders in whom cognitive status has been followed and includes the longest maximum follow-up available in a study of cognitive status in this population.

Methods

Overview of Study

Our sample was drawn from a pool of 380 ET cases enrolled in a prospective, longitudinal study of cognition in elders with ET (COGNET; Clinical Pathological Study of Cognitive Impairment in Essential Tremor; National Institutes of Health Award #R01 NS086736, 2014 - present). Nationwide enrollment began in July 2014; to date, cases reside in 43 US states. Eligibility requirements include: (1) a diagnosis of ET; (2) a baseline age of at least 55 years; and (3) enrollment as an eventual donor in the Essential Tremor Centralized Brain Repository. There were no specific cognitive performance criteria for inclusion in the sample at either baseline or subsequent evaluations. The Yale University, Columbia University, and University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center Institutional Review Boards approved the study protocol. All cases provided written informed consent.

Evaluations took place at baseline, and again at 18-month intervals up to baseline plus 90 months (7.5 years). A trained research assistant administered evaluations during home visits. Each evaluation involved participating in a videotaped neurological examination and completing demographic and clinical questionnaires as well as a battery of neuropsychological tests.

Longitudinal data were collected in two waves. Wave I data collection began in 2014; these cases participated in up to six evaluations conducted approximately 18 months apart. Further funding allowed us to refresh our sample with additional cases and begin a second Wave of data collection in 2019. Cases enrolled in Wave II participated in up to three evaluations scheduled 18 months apart. Of our pool of 380 cases, 242 provided data in Wave I, and 138 in Wave II.

Neurological Examination and Diagnostic Confirmation

A videotaped neurological examination was performed at each evaluation, as detailed elsewhere [37]. The severity of postural tremor (one examination item) and kinetic tremor (five examination items) was assessed in each arm on a 0 to 3 scale, yielding a Total Tremor Score (TTS). TTS values range from 0 (no tremor) to 36 (high severity) [37]. During the examination, cases were also asked to walk ten steps heel to toe in a straight line. Gait impairment was defined as the number of missteps observed and could range from 0 to 10 [37].

Based on a review of the questionnaire and videotaped examination data, an experienced movement disorders neurologist assigned clinical diagnoses of ET based on reliable [38] and valid [39] diagnostic criteria from the Washington Heights-Inwood Genetic Study of Essential Tremor (WHIGET) [40]. PD was diagnosed based on published diagnostic criteria that required the presence of a minimum of two cardinal signs [41].

Demographic and Clinical Questionnaires

Cases provided demographic, lifestyle, and clinical information including details of their tremor history. Collected items included age, sex, race, years of education, whether they currently smoked cigarettes, and the number of alcoholic drinks per week (liquor, beer, or wine) typically consumed. Cases reported the age at which their tremor first appeared, as well as whether any of their family members experienced tremor and, if known, the underlying tremor diagnosis (e.g., ET, PD).

We calculated a measure of tremor duration equal to the difference between age of onset and baseline age. We then divided TTS by tremor duration to create a measure of rapidity of tremor progression [42–44]. Following previous work [21, 22], we also created a dichotomous index of onset age that classified cases as either younger than or at 65 or above 65 years of age when their tremor first appeared.

We used information about family history of tremor to create four individual dichotomous variables; these were (1) a family history of ET versus no family history of tremor of any type, (2) a family history of PD versus no family history of tremor of any type, (3) a family history of tremor resulting from both ET and PD versus no family history of tremor of any type, and (4) a family history of tremor of unspecified origin versus no family history of tremor of any type.

Cases further provided a list of their currently prescribed medications. From this, we calculated the total number of medications used. We also calculated the number of currently prescribed medications used to treat tremor. More specifically, we ascertained whether cases reported using: (1) propranolol, (2) beta blockers other than propranolol (specifically, nadolol, metoprolol, atenolol, or sotalol), (3) primidone, (4) gabapentin, and/or (5) benzodiazepines (specifically, diazepam, alprazolam, or clonazepam). These medications were selected for study because they were previously identified as those most frequently used to treat ET in the current cohort [45]. Values for this measure ranged from 0 (no medication) to 5 (all five types of medication). We further constructed dichotomous indices of whether participants reported using or not using each of the five types of tremor medication. Given extended exposure to primidone and benzodiazepines have been linked to cognitive decline [46, 47], we further obtained self-reports of length of use of these medications.

Finally, cases completed: (1) an abbreviated 10-item version of the Tremor Disability Questionnaire [48], a measure of tremor-related difficulties with everyday activities that yields scores ranging from 0 (low disability) to 100 (high disability); (2) the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) scale [49], which assesses the ability to perform eight daily living activities (e.g., doing housework, traveling independently), on which scores range from 0 (low functioning) to 8 (high functioning); (3) the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE) [50], a 12-item measure of physical activity on which scores range from 0 (no activity) to 400 (high activity); (4) the Activities-Specific Balance Confidence Scale (ABC-6) [51], a measure of confidence in the ability to perform 6 tasks (e.g., reaching for high objects on tiptoe) without becoming unsteady or losing balance on a 0 (no confidence) to 100 (high confidence) scale; and, (5) the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) [52], a 30-item measure of depressive symptoms on which scores may range from 0 (no depressive symptoms) to 30 (severe depressive symptoms).

Neuropsychological Assessment

During each evaluation, cases completed the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [53], and a comprehensive battery of neuropsychological tests requiring little to no reliance on motor functioning, minimizing any disadvantage to cases with moderate to severe tremor. The neuropsychological protocol covered five broad cognitive domains (online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552903) [54–62]. As the majority of neuropsychological tests place demands on multiple cognitive domains, the conceptualization of domain scores for the current study was in part driven by previously established features of ET, particularly the frequently reported deficits observed in executive function. As category fluency and letter fluency are often comparably low in the presence of executive dysfunction, they were both included in the Executive Function domain despite the former’s relatively heavier reliance on semantic language networks [63]. This decision was driven by the initial aims of the COGNET study which sought to determine the extent to which memory and language deficits characteristic of Alzheimer’s disease were present among individuals who may also have a dysexecutive syndrome secondary to cortico-cerebellar dysfunction [11, 64].

For diagnostic purposes, raw test scores were converted to z-scores adjusted based on clinically available normative data. In most cases, norms were derived using the test manuals and reference sources listed in online supplementary Table 1. An exception was made for three measures included in the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center Uniform Dataset (UDS) at the time of protocol development (i.e., Digit Span, Logical Memory, and Boston Naming). In these 3 cases, normative data were derived from this national sample, normed for age, education, sex, and race/ethnicity [65, 66]. “Impairment” on a given test was defined as a z-score of less than or equal to −1.5. In addition, specific tests in each domain were a priori selected for consideration in the subsequent diagnosis of MCI based on the following: (1) the utility of measures for defining MCI in previous studies and (2) general availability of the measures to researchers interested in replication [67].

Cognitive Diagnosis

Following established protocols [68], a neuropsychologist and a geriatric psychiatrist reviewed the evaluations of each case, including neuropsychological test performance, information gathered during informant interviews, and Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) [69] scores. At each evaluation, cases determined to display cognitive impairment due to stroke, injury, substance abuse, or another non-neurodegenerative etiology were identified. Remaining cases were assigned diagnoses of either NC, MCI, or dementia. NC was a broad category including (1) patients identified to have no impairment (CDR = 0 and no impairment on any previously described test); (2) patients with performance below normal limits on one cognitive test which was considered to have unlikely clinical significance (CDR = 0); (3) patients with performance below normal limits on one or more test, considered to be possibly clinically significant but not meeting operational criteria for MCI (CDR = 0 or 0.5); and (4) patients with questionable or isolated functional impairment (CDR = 0.5, and no impairment on any test). MCI was operationally defined as a CDR of 0.5 and impairment (z-score ≤ −1.5) on two MCI-designated tests. Dementia was defined as a CDR >1 and impairment in multiple domains. These operational definitions of NC, MCI, and dementia have been widely used in studies of cognitive impairment [67, 68, 70–74].

Study Sample

We excluded from these analyses 30 of our 380 cases for one or more of the following reasons: (1) tremor was not severe enough to confirm an ET diagnosis after enrollment (n = 19); (2) case was determined to have PD at baseline (n = 4); (3) case was determined to display cognitive impairment due to stroke, injury, substance abuse, or another non-neurodegenerative etiology at baseline (n = 4), or (4) other critical data were missing (n = 5). This yielded a sample of 350, M age = 79.6 ± 8.4 years. Of these 350 cases, 301, 253, 136, 107, and 81 took part in the 18-, 36-, 54-, 72-, and 90-month evaluations, respectively (online suppl. Table 2). Of this initial sample of 350, to date 80 (22.9%) died, 48 (13.7%) withdrew, and 3 (0.9%) were lost to follow-up.

For our current analyses, we further eliminated 66 cases who met one of the following criteria: they (1) were diagnosed with dementia at baseline (n = 22); (2) did not complete multiple evaluations (n = 39); or (3) were missing critical information at later evaluations (n = 5). This yielded a final sample of 284 cases, M baseline age = 78.4 ± 8.3 years, 62% female. Of these cases, 249 (87.7%) were assigned a baseline cognitive diagnosis of NC, and 35 (12.3%) of MCI.

Cox Regression Equations

Cox proportional hazards regression equations calculated cases’ relative risk of incident dementia based on baseline characteristics. For cases who developed dementia, time to event was equivalent to time elapsed between baseline and the evaluation during which they were assigned a dementia diagnosis. For cases who did not develop dementia, time to event was defined as the time elapsed between baseline and their final evaluation.

Multivariate Models. We calculated a series of multivariate Cox equations that included each individual predictor variable revealed to be significantly associated with dementia at p < 0.05 in the univariate models described above, and two control variables. The control variables were included to ensure that they did not mediate any relations observed between potential predictors and dementia risk. First, we included age, as older age is a ubiquitous predictor of dementia across samples drawn from a variety of populations, including those composed of people with and without a diagnosis of ET [10]. Second, although our current sample of ET cases excludes those with a diagnosis of dementia, there is considerable variability in the baseline level of cognitive impairment of the remaining cases. Therefore, we further adjusted for baseline cognitive diagnosis (i.e., a diagnosis of NC versus MCI) in the multivariate analyses.

Sensitivity Analyses. As age is a strong and well-established predictor of dementia [10, 11], the baseline ages of the 29 cases in our sample who developed dementia were – not unexpectedly – higher (M = 85.0 ± 5.7, range = 74–96) than those of the 255 who did not (M = 77.7 ± 8.2, range = 55 - 97). Therefore, we replicated our primary analyses limiting non-converters to an older subset of 189 of the original 255 cases who did not convert to dementia (168 NC, 21 MCI). These 189 non-converters were matched in age range to those cases who converted to dementia. Specifically, this winnowing eliminated 65 non-converters who were younger than 74 years of age, and one non-converter who was older than 96. The mean age of the resulting subset of non-converters was 81.3 ± 5.2 years. We expected that reducing the differences between the age ranges of these two groups (i.e., converters and non-converters) might allow additional important differences between converters and non-converters to emerge in our analyses.

Results

Baseline Case Characteristics: Completers versus Non-Completers

The distributions of demographic and clinical variables for the 80 cases who completed and 204 cases who did not complete all six evaluations appear in online supplementary Table 3. Participants completing six evaluations were younger than those completing fewer evaluations, M baseline age = 73.5 ± 8.8 versus 80.4 ± 7.2 years, respectively, p < 0.001. Relations between length of participation and age are typically observed in longitudinal assessments of elders [75] and reflect, in part, the increased mortality of older participants. Cases who completed six versus fewer than six evaluations did not differ to a significant degree in cognitive diagnosis (i.e., % NC versus % MCI), sex, race, education, tremor severity (TTS) or tremor duration, or use of alcohol, all p > 0.26, use of cigarettes, p = 0.12, or age of tremor onset, p = 0.08.

Baseline Case Characteristics: Non-Converters versus Converters to Dementia

Of the 284 cases in our final sample, 29 (10.2%) converted to dementia during the course of the study, and 255 (89.8%) did not (Table 1). Distributions of our potential baseline predictor variables appear for all cases, as well as separately for converters and non-converters (Table 1). ET cases who converted to dementia during the study were, as expected, significantly older than non-converters at baseline, M ages = 85.0 ± 5.7 and 77.7 ± 8.2 years, respectively, p = 0.001, and more likely than non-converters to be diagnosed with MCI at baseline, 41.4% and 9.0%, respectively, p = 0.001. Converters also had tremor of longer duration than did non-converters (M = 47.2 ± 23.6, Mdn = 44.0 versus 35.5 ± 20.6 and Mdn = 31.0 years, p = 0.01), lower IADL scores (M = 6.4 ± 1.8, Mdn = 7.0 versus 7.8 ± 0.8 and 8.0, p = 0.001), lower PASE scores (M = 52.9 ± 42.5, Mdn = 50.0 versus 109.2 ± 64.4 and 98.3, p = 0.001), more missteps on a tandem gait task (M = 6.6 ± 3.4, Mdn = 7.0 versus 3.9 ± 3.6 and 3.0, p = 0.001), lower ABC-6 scores (M = 44.4 ± 25.3, Mdn = 41.7 versus 59.5 ± 27.0 and 63.8, p = 0.004), and higher GDS scores (M = 8.1 ± 5.1, Mdn = 7.5 versus 5.5 ± 5.0, and 4.0, p = 0.002).

Univariate Cox Regressions

We initially conducted a series of univariate (unadjusted) Cox proportional hazards models in which each of the baseline demographic, lifestyle, and clinical information (including details of tremor history) variables appearing in Table 2 was entered as the sole predictor of incident dementia. In these equations, cognitive diagnosis emerged as a significant predictor of dementia, as did age (our intended control variables), p < 0.001. As one would expect, this reflects the fact that cases with a baseline diagnosis of MCI were ultimately more likely to develop dementia (12 of 35 or 34.3%, Table 1) than were cases diagnosed as cognitively normal at baseline (17 of 249 or 6.8%, Table 1), and that converters to dementia were older than non-converters (Table 1).

Six additional variables emerged as significant predictors of risk for dementia from the univariate analyses (Table 2). Specifically, greater tremor duration, lower IADL scores (i.e., poorer functional skills), lower PASE scores (i.e., less physical activity), more missteps on a tandem gait task, lower ABC-6 scores (i.e., less balance confidence), and higher GDS (i.e., greater depression) were each associated with an increased risk of developing dementia, all p < 0.03. Thus, each of the eight variables on which converters to dementia and non-converters to dementia differed (Table 1) emerged as significant predictors of conversion to dementia in the univariate analyses.

Multivariate Cox Regressions

We calculated six Cox multivariate models. Each included: (1) one of the six baseline predictors significantly associated with an increased risk of dementia in the series of univariate models just described (i.e., tremor duration, IADL scores, PASE scores, tandem gait impairment, ABC-6 scores, GDS scores); (2) baseline cognitive diagnosis (MCI versus NC) as a control variable; and (3) baseline age as a control variable (Table 3).

Sensitivity Analyses

Finally, we recalculated the univariate and multivariate analyses retaining only those non-converters whose baseline age range matched that of converters. This yielded a more age-restricted sample of 218 (189 non-converters and 29 converters) with ages ranging from 74 to 96. Two differences emerged from the equations based on the more restricted age sample. First, the previously observed unadjusted relation between longer tremor duration and conversion to dementia was marginally significant in the new analysis, p = 0.07. Second, lower balance confidence emerged in the new analysis adjusting for age and cognitive diagnosis as a significant predictor of dementia risk, H = 0.98, 95% CI = 0.97–0.99, p = 0.05. No other variables either lost significance or emerged as new significant predictors in any of these equations. Discussion

Our goal was to examine the demographic, lifestyle, or clinical variables that predict incident dementia in a cohort of elders with ET. To this end, we analyzed data from the largest longitudinal study of cognitive status in an ET cohort presently available. As expected, older age and a baseline diagnosis of MCI (versus NC) both increased the risk of dementia in this population, p < 0.001. Consistent with our earlier report [36], ET cases exhibiting greater depressive symptoms were also at higher risk of incident dementia than were their peers, and this effect was independent of baseline age or initial cognitive diagnosis, p = 0.03; the hazards ratio of 1.07 indicates that after adjusting for age and initial cognitive status, a small increase in GDS scores (i.e., only one point on a 30 point scale) was associated with a 7% increase in the likelihood of incident dementia.

Participants who encountered less difficulty performing basic daily activities at baseline (i.e., had higher IADL scores) were also at a reduced risk for subsequent dementia, again independent of age or initial cognitive diagnosis, p = 0.001, adjusted HR = 0.44; thus, a one point increase in the 8-point IADL score was associated with a more than halving of the risk of developing dementia. Stated another way, a very modest (i.e., one point) reduction in IADL score was associated with a more than doubling in the risk of developing dementia. After matching the baseline age range of cases who did not convert to dementia to the range of those who did, greater confidence in one’s sense of balance also emerged as an independent predictor of risk of dementia, p = 0.05, adjusted HR = 0.98; specifically a one point increase in balance confidence yielded a 2% decrease in the risk of subsequent dementia. Finally, level of impairment on a tandem gait task was marginally associated with risk of dementia, adjusting for age and baseline cognitive status, p = 0.06, adjusted HR = 1.14. One additional misstep on the tandem gait test, therefore, yielded an increase of 14% in the likelihood of developing dementia during the study, and two steps, a 28% increase, and three steps, a 42% increase. Although univariate analyses suggested a link between low physical activity and dementia risk, this relation was no longer significant when we controlled for baseline age and cognitive status.

Our observation that the severity of depressive symptoms was significantly associated with incident dementia replicates an earlier report based on the data available from the COGNET cohort at that time [36]. Our current analyses include a sample double the size first reported (284 versus 141), and yield similar results, univariate HRs and CIs = 1.08 (1.02–1.14) and 1.11 (1.02–1.20), respectively, providing strong evidence of a link between depression and dementia risk in ET. People diagnosed with ET are more prone to depression than controls [76], potentially reflecting an emotional response to the physical, functional, and social challenges posed by their tremor [77]. However, depressive symptoms in ET cases can precede the appearance of tremor [78], and are unrelated to tremor severity [79]. Thus, although tremor doubtless takes an emotional toll on ET patients, associations between depression and ET cannot be completely explained as a psychological response to motor manifestations of the disease [78, 79], perhaps suggesting an underlying mechanism similar to that responsible for the links between depression and dementia in individuals who do not have tremor [24, 28]. Given this, more careful scrutiny of how the temporal nature of depression in ET patients may interact with both tremor progression and heightened dementia risk seems warranted. For example, late-onset depression may serve as a subtle early indicator of dementia in ET, as has been observed in PD [80, 81], whereas early-onset depression may be associated with cerebrovascular changes that heighten dementia risk [82]. In light of the importance of these issues, additional attention to the timing of the emergence of depression and dementia in ET cohorts is warranted.

Cognition plays a critical role in many daily activities. Moreover, in a sample of elders with ET, IADL scores were negatively correlated with cognitive test performance (i.e., poorer functioning associated with poorer cognitive performance), and they were better predicted by cognitive performance than by tremor severity [83]. Therefore, it may be surprising that the relation we observed between functional abilities and the development of dementia was independent of cognitive functioning (i.e., cognitive diagnosis at baseline). However, cognitive test scores accounted for less than 10% of the variance in IADL scores in the earlier analysis, and the activities described in IADL items clearly draw on additional skills. Successful locomotion and balance maintenance, for example, each make it easier to do shopping, travel independently, prepare meals, or complete housekeeping tasks, all items featured in the IADL. Moreover, our analyses do suggest links between both our measures of balance and gait impairment and dementia risk independent of age and cognitive status, and these factors may also give rise to the relation between scores on the IADL and risk for dementia. In ET populations, gait impairment has been previously linked to cognitive impairment and increased risk of mortality [68, 84], and balance problems – as quantified by lower balance confidence scores – to poor cognitive functioning [85]. Additional work should further explore the mechanisms that underlie these links.

An earlier study reported that age of tremor onset predicted incident ET-dementia in a population-based cohort drawn from central Spain [21, 22], but neither a continuous measure of age of tremor onset or a dichotomous measure of onset (<65 versus > 65) predicted incident dementia in the current sample, both p > 0.82. We also calculated two separate univariate Cox regressions in which a continuous measure of onset age predicted incident dementia within each of these age-groups, but neither was significant, both p > 0.27. Although the sample size, baseline age, and sex breakdown of ET cases in the Spanish cohort (n = 273, 77.0 years and 60.4% female [21]) were similar to that reported in the current work (n = 284, 78.4 years, and 62.0% female), the samples were drawn from different populations (i.e., three communities in Spain versus people in the USA who agreed to become brain donors). Moreover, perhaps reflecting the donor status of the current sample, the groups differed markedly in education. Specifically, whereas 75% of the Spanish sample completed less than 6 years of education (i.e., primary school), nearly one-half of the current sample completed at least some college. Although education-related levels of cognitive reserve could conceivably impact overall risk of dementia, it is less clear why potential differences in reserve would yield the different associations between age of ET onset and dementia observed in these two samples. We suspect that examination of these relations in additional cohorts of people with ET will be needed to better understand this difference.

Thus, previous research revealed three predictors of ET-dementia; older age, older age of onset of ET, and higher levels of depressive symptoms. Our data replicate earlier findings for age and depression, although we found no evidence of an association between age of ET onset and dementia. Moreover, our analyses suggest three additional predictors of conversion to dementia: reduced instrumental activities of daily living, increased gait impairment, and lower balance confidence. Associations between the capacity of carry out daily activities and cognitive impairment have been noted in the general population [86], and the link between gait impairment and risk for dementia is paralleled by reports that difficulty completing a tandem gait task is associated with cognitive impairment in nondemented ET cases [87].

Finally, the observation that increased age is a risk factor for dementia among those with ET converges with work on predictors of dementia in both people with PD and in the general population [23–25], possibly suggesting a common neurodegenerative pathway. As previously discussed, depression also increases risk for dementia in both the general population [24, 28], and in individuals with PD [80, 81]. Poor balance and slow gait in older adults are also associated with heightened dementia risk [88–90]. Although individuals with dementia have relatively poor functional abilities [91], we are not aware of research documenting that IADL scores are a risk factor for the later development of dementia among either ET patients or other populations.

Our study has notable strengths. First, this is, to our knowledge, the largest longitudinal ET cohort tracked for the emergence of dementia, and the mean length of follow-up (4.7 years, for those participating in longitudinal analyses) is one of the longest reported with such cohorts. Thus, the current data represent the most comprehensive assessment to date of risk factors for ET-dementia in terms of either number of factors studied or cohort size. Second, the current assignment of cognitive diagnoses is exceptionally robust, based on multiple, detailed assessments and assigned by a neuropsychologist and geriatric psychiatrist. Moreover, ET diagnoses were similarly rigorous, based on a standardized neurological examination, and assigned by a highly experienced movement disorders specialist. This rigor of motor and cognitive phenotyping enhances the accuracy and validity of our findings.

A potential limitation of this work involves generalizability. Our participants agreed to take part in a brain donation program, and such individuals may be particularly likely to seek medical care and participate in research [92, 93]. Moreover, our sample was predominantly Caucasian (97.8%), and highly educated (M = 16.0 years), as is often the case with brain donor samples [94, 95], highlighting the need for more diverse study samples. Another potential concern is that not all cases completed all evaluations. This could be problematic if factors associated with length of participation result in selection bias. However, although cases who completed all evaluations were younger than those who did not, as is often the case with longitudinal studies of older populations [75], these groups were, in fact, quite similar on most other demographic and clinical measures.

In sum, consistent with previous studies in ET and in the general population, older age and more depressive symptoms predicted incident dementia. Our analyses further suggest that poorer functional abilities, increased gait impairment, and lower balance confidence are associated with increased risk for dementia in an ET population, whereas older age of ET onset, previously viewed as a risk factor, did not predict incident ET-dementia.

Current dementia risk prevention frameworks embrace a wide range of preventative strategies based on results from both observational studies and clinical trials [96, 97]. Evidence converges on a set of modifiable risk and resilience factors across the lifespan including those that relate to general health (e.g., diabetes, hypertension), lifestyle (e.g., diet, exercise), cognitively enriching experiences (education, occupation, cognitive engagement), social engagement, sleep, stress, and mood, among others. The variables that emerged as significant predictors of dementia in the current study of older adults with ET include non-modifiable (i.e., age) as well as potentially modifiable factors (e.g., depression, balance confidence). Unlike modifiable risk factors relating to lifestyle (e.g., diet, exercise) and lifetime exposures (e.g., education, occupation), however, the significant predictor variables identified in the current study are more proximal to dementia onset and may also be signs of prodromal disease (e.g., IADL, gait, balance confidence, depression). As such, the extent to which they are truly modifiable is unclear. Recent work in the general population has suggested that treating depression may lower risk for later development of dementia [98]. Future work is needed to determine whether intervening on motoric, functional, and/or psychiatric symptoms among older adults with ET can reduce dementia risk, or delay onset.
