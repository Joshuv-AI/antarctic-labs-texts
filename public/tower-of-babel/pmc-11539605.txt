A magnetic epitope-imprinted microsphere used for selective separation and rapid detection of SHV-type β-lactamases in bacteria: a novel strategy of antimicrobial resistance detection
Authors: Yusun Zhou, Kunqi Wang, Lele Li, Hui Li, Qingwu Tian, Baosheng Ge, Yuanyuan Chi, Xiaotong Xu, Shuhui Liu, Meng Han, Tingting Zhou, Yuanqi Zhu, Qing Wang, Bing Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe production of β-lactamases is the most prevalent resistance mechanism for β-lactam antibiotics in Gram-negative bacteria. Presently, over 4900 β-lactamases have been discovered, and they are categorized into hundreds of families. In each enzyme family, amino acid substitutions result in subtle changes to enzyme hydrolysis profiles; in contrast, certain conserved sequences retained by all of the family members can serve as important markers for enzyme family identification.ResultsThe SHV family was chosen as the study object. First, a unique 10-mer peptide was identified as SHV family's epitope by an approach of protein fingerprint analysis. Then, an SHV-specific magnetic epitope-imprinted gel polymer (MEI-GP) was prepared by an epitope surface imprinting technique, and its sorption behavior and recognition mechanism for template epitope and SHV were both elaborated. Finally, the MEI-GP was successfully applied to selectively extract SHV from bacteria, and the extracted SHV was submitted to MALDI-TOF MS for specific determination. By following this strategy, other β-lactamase families can also be specifically detected. According to the molecular weight displayed in mass spectra, the kind of β-lactamase and its associated hydrolysis profile on β-lactams can be easily identified. Based on this, an initial drug option scheme can be quickly formulated for antimicrobial therapy. From protein extraction to medication guidance reporting, the mean time to detection (MTTD) was less than 2 h, which is much faster than conventional phenotype-based methods (at least 16–20 h) and gene-based techniques (usually about 8 h).ConclusionsThis enzyme-specific detection strategy combined the specificity of epitope imprinting with the sensitivity of mass spectrometry, enabling β-lactamase to be selectively extracted from bacteria and clearly presented in mass spectra. Compared with other drug resistance detection methods, this technique has good specificity, high sensitivity (≤ 15 mg of bacteria), a short MTTD (less than 2 h), and simple operation, and therefore has a broad application prospect in clinical medicine.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-024-02949-9.

Background

The discovery of β-lactam antibiotics in the early twentieth century and their subsequent development represent a remarkable advancement in human medicine, allowing for widespread treatment of infectious diseases that plagued humanity throughout history [1]. β-Lactams inhibit bacterial growth by binding to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) to form stable and irreversible acyl-enzyme intermediates, thereby preventing peptidoglycan cross-links and bacterial cell wall biosynthesis [2]. Yet, like other antimicrobial classes, bacterial resistance to β-lactams developed rapidly following their widespread adoption and has remained a constant adversary in their clinical application [3]. In Gram-positive bacteria, the primarily known mechanisms of β-lactam antimicrobial resistance (AMR) include expression of an alternative PBP with a reduced affinity for all β-lactams or upregulation of PBP expression [4], and in Gram-negative bacteria, reduced expression of transport proteins (porin loss) or production of efflux pumps (extrusion gain) are known as the unique evading strategies (due to their distinctive outer membrane) [4]. Particularly, production of β-lactamases, which hydrolyze β-lactam ring, is the most prevalent resistance mechanism in Gram-negative bacteria and has the greatest clinical significance [4, 5].

The first β-lactam penicillin was discovered in 1929, and the first reported β-lactamase capable of hydrolyzing penicillin was identified as an AmpC cephalosporinase extracted from Escherichia coli in 1940 [4, 5]. Mirroring this initial finding, the development of novel β-lactams (such as stable penicillins, cephalosporins, monobactams, and carbapenems) was closely followed by the emergence of new β-lactamases (including penicillinases, cephalosporinases, and carbapenemases) [6]. Presently, over 4900 naturally occurring β-lactamases have been indexed in the Beta-Lactamase Database (BLDB; http://www.bldb.eu/) [7], in which enzymes are categorized into hundreds of families (such as SHV, CMY, OXA, and NDM families) with various hydrolysis profiles (including broad spectrum, extended spectrum, and carbapenem resistance) for each family. Two commonly used schemes of classification were proposed to characterize the numerous β-lactamases, one of which is the Ambler system, based on protein sequence information [8], and the other is the Bush–Jacoby functional classification system, which categorizes β-lactamases into four groups (group 1, 2, 3, and 4) based on their substrates and inhibitor profiles [9]. According to the Ambler molecular classification scheme, β-lactamases are grouped into four classes: class A, C, and D comprising active-site serine β-lactamases (SBLs), and class B comprising zinc metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs), which employ serine and zinc-activated water, respectively, as the reaction nucleophile to hydrolyze β-lactams [8]. The Ambler designation is simple for beginners to understand the molecular class and sequence information of β-lactamases, while the Bush–Jacoby classification is useful for identifying how a β-lactamase contributes to clinical resistance.

In each of the four Ambler classes of β-lactamases, the enzyme families share structural homology with each other; in one specific enzyme family, the β-lactamases share a homologous amino acid sequence [10, 11]. In any one enzyme family, the followings apply: (i) The first reported β-lactamase is named "-1" (such as SHV-1, TEM-1, and CTX-M-1), and the other members are derivatives of the original β-lactamase, with one or more amino acid substitutions, separately named "-2", "-3", and so forth in order of their discovery. (ii) The amino acid substitutions often occur at a limited number of positions, and the combinations of these point mutations result in various subtle changes to enzyme hydrolysis profiles [12, 13]. (iii) Some conserved amino acid sequences are still retained by all of the family members, and these peptide fragments are important markers for enzyme family identification [12, 13]. Therefore, complementary to gene-based techniques (such as oligonucleotide probe recognition, target gene amplification, and whole-genome sequencing) [14–16], a novel technique could be developed for detecting β-lactamase families by identifying their individually specific amino acid sequences.

Protein recognition techniques based on molecular imprinting technology have been developed rapidly in the last decade because protein-imprinted materials offer many advantages compared with natural antibodies, such as environmental stability, storage convenience, and potential reusability [17]. However, because protein imprinting uses the entire macromolecule as template, protein-imprinted material also suffers from obvious disadvantages, including limited ability to maintain the native conformation of template proteins, high difficulty in obtaining a high abundance of imprinted sites, and reduced selectivity and efficiency for target protein binding to the relatively large imprinted sites [17]. To improve the protein recognition performance of the imprinted material, several efforts have been undertaken. In one study, application of non-ionic monomers (acrylamide and methylene bisacrylamide) provided an imprinted gel with high selectivity to the target protein through a strong interaction (hydrogen bonding) between amide groups (–CONH–) and peptide bonds [18]. In another study, an imprinted hydrogel shell prepared on surface of modified core substrate (such as silica microsphere, magnetic nanoparticle, and carbon nanotube) facilitated protein diffusion in and out of the gel [19]. Furthermore, epitope imprinting has been frequently used for protein-specific recognition in recent years [20, 21]. The epitope-imprinted material can be prepared using the target protein-specific peptide (an epitope) as template, which can capture the entire protein molecule by binding its short surface-exposed peptide. Compared with the large-sized protein molecules, the small peptides are easily obtained by artificial synthesis and much more stable during imprinting process (even in organic solvents), and can be conveniently removed from imprinted cavities to produce more homogeneous binding sites.

In epitope imprinting, the length of template peptides is the key factor affecting the material imprinting efficiency and its recognition of target proteins, because imprinting short peptide sequences leads to the formation of less specific binding sites that suffer from cross-reaction with matrix components and inefficient recognition of target proteins, while the long peptides easily form undesirable secondary or 3D-folded structures through intermolecular interactions during imprinting process, causing mismatches between the imprinted cavities and the original peptides of the target protein molecules [22]. Increasingly more research has revealed that desirable template epitope is composed of 9 to 15 amino acids, and the obtained imprinted material shows favorable selectivity and high affinity for both the template peptides and the entire target proteins [20]. Over the last several years, epitope surface imprinting has been proposed for preparing protein-specific imprinted material that combines the advantages of the two techniques, and it has been widely applied in disease diagnosis [23], drug delivery [24], affinity purification [25], and bio-information sensing [26]. In these studies, the core substrate was wrapped tightly by a thin imprinted film with a thickness of several nanometers, in which the binding sites were homogeneously surface-exposed to provide excellent accessibility to target proteins by epitope-specific recognition, which significantly enhanced the imprinting efficiency, binding affinity, and target selectivity compared with traditional bulk imprinting.

In this study, the most prevalent SHV-type β-lactamases were chosen as target proteins. SHV is a representative family of β-lactamases and includes more than 240 enzymes with different hydrolysis profiles (http://www.bldb.eu/). First, using databases associated with bioinformatic tools, a computer-based rational approach was applied to precisely identify SHV-specific epitope. Then, using the defined epitope as the template and submicron Fe3O4 particle as the substrate, a surface imprinting technique was developed to prepare an SHV-specific magnetic epitope-imprinted gel polymer (MEI-GP), which could selectively adsorb SHV based on epitope-specific recognition. Finally, relying on the prepared MEI-GP, a sample extraction procedure was established to selectively extract SHV from Gram-negative bacteria, which was then submitted to MALDI-TOF MS for specific determination. We believe that this entire workflow for SHV detection in bacteria can be applied to all of the β-lactamase families present in BLDB for their identification.

Its bioinformatic features can be retrieved from UniProtKB (https://www.uniprot.org/; accession no. P0AD64). This enzyme is composed of 286 amino acids containing a signal peptide domain (residues 1–21) and a mature protein chain (residues 22–286). The signal peptide directs SHV-1 into the periplasm and then detaches, leaving mature SHV-1 that mediates drug resistance. Preparation of SHV-specific MEI-GP

Synthesis of hydrophilic submicron Fe3O4 particle

Submicron Fe3O4 particles were synthesized according to a controllable solvothermal method [31]. First, FeCl3⋅6H2O (2.7 g, 10 mmol) was dissolved in 80 mL of ethylene glycol by ultrasonication for 10 min. Then, 3.6 g of NaAc and 2 g of polyethylene glycol (PEG) 2000 were added, followed by supersonic dissolution for 20 min and facile stirring for 30 min. Subsequently, the mixture was sealed in a Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave and maintained at 200 °C for 10 h. Next, under N2 protection, hydrophilic modification of the submicron Fe3O4 was performed as follows. First, the cooled system in the Teflon container was completely transferred into a three-necked round-bottomed flask and vigorously stirred (500 rpm) for 30 min under ultrasonic conditions (300 W). Then, a dispersing solution was prepared by adding 1.17 mL of polyacrylic acid (PAA) 3000 (10 mmol AA) to 40 mL of deionized water, which was dropped (1 drop/5 s) into the reaction over 60 min. Subsequently, stirring under supersonic conditions continued for another 30 min after the dropping was completed, and carboxy-modified Fe3O4 (Fe3O4–COOH) was obtained (Fig. 2). Last, the Fe3O4–COOH was rinsed with deionized water three times and further dispersed in water to form an aqueous colloid solution with a concentration of 25 mg/mL.Fig. 2Schematic illustration of the SHV-specific MEI-GP preparation and its sorption mechanism for SHV. Silicon-hydroxy modification of Fe3O4–COOH particle

The Fe3O4–COOH colloid solution (10 mL) was added to 80 mL of ethanol, mixed with 20 mL of deionized water and 1 mL of NH3⋅H2O (25–28 wt%), and dispersed by ultrasonication (300 W) for 10 min. Under continuous stirring (450 rpm) at 30 °C, tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) (0.672 mL, 3 mmol) was added to the system in six equal portions with an interval time of 1 h. After 6 h, the reaction was terminated and silicon-hydroxy-modified Fe3O4–COOH (Fe3O4@SiO2) was obtained (Fig. 2), which was rinsed with ethanol/water (1:1, v/v) three times and then dispersed in ethanol/water (1:1, v/v) to form a 30 mg/mL stable colloid solution.

Preparation of epitope-imprinted nanofilm on surface of Fe3O4@SiO2

The template VDAGDEQLER (5.65 mg, 0.005 mmol) was dissolved in 80 mL of ethanol/1,2-propanediol (1:1, v/v), and three functional monomers (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES, 0.2 mmol), (3-ureidopropyl)triethoxysilane (UPTES, 0.18 mmol), and (3-bis(2-hydroxyethyl)aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (BHEAPTES, 0.06 mmol) were added in order. Each monomer was incubated with the template for 1 h at 30 °C. After 3 h, the cross-linker TEOS (0.4 mmol), 5 mL of the Fe3O4@SiO2 colloid solution, and 20 mL of deionized water were added to the system, and the sol–gel polymerization proceeded for 24 h under continuous stirring (400 rpm). Subsequently, the SHV-specific MEI-GP was obtained (Fig. 2) and magnetically separated, and then washed with ethanol/water (1:1, v/v) and NH3⋅H2O (25–28 wt%)/ethanol/water (1:2:2, v/v/v) three times, respectively. Last, the MEI-GP was rinsed with ethanol/water (1:1, v/v) three times again and then dispersed in ethanol/water (1:1, v/v) to form a 35 mg/mL stable colloid solution.

A magnetic non-imprinted gel polymer (MNI-GP) was also prepared according to the MEI-GP preparation process but without adding the template epitope. Unlike MEI-GP autonomic polymerization, the MNI-GP preparation required a base catalyst (1.5 mL of NH3⋅H2O (25–28 wt%)).

Extraction and determination of SHV in bacteria

Bacteria pretreatment

Three standard Gram-negative bacteria, ATCC®25922 (E. coli, does not produce SHV), ATCC®700603 (Klebsiella pneumonia, produces SHV-18), and ATCC®BAA-3272 (K. pneumonia, produces four β-lactamases, SHV-28, OXA-1, OXA-232, and NDM-1), and two strains maintained by our laboratory, HFK417 (K. pneumoniae, isolated from a clinical specimen, produces SHV-1) and HFK3619 (K. pneumonia, also isolated from a clinical specimen, produces three β-lactamases, SHV-182, DHA-1, and OXA-1), were separately cultured on Columbia blood-agar plates in an environment of 35 °C and 5% CO2 for 18–24 h. Separately, a mass of 300 mg of these pure-cultured strains was homogeneously dispersed in 1 mL of sterile water by vigorously swirling, producing an optical density (OD600) of 102.2, and then an ultrasonic-assisted cytoclasis procedure was applied to the bacteria, with an output power of 85%⋅130 W working intermittently until the bacterial suspension was clear. The bacterial crackate solution was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and a clarified protein supernatant was obtained, which was used for SHV selective extraction.

SHV extraction

For ATCC®25922, 20 μg/mL of recombinant SHV-1 and SHV-18 were separately added to 0.5 mL of protein supernatant, mixed with 0.5 mL of 1,2-propanediol, and then 2% (w/v) SDS was added. As shown in Fig. 6, the mixture was heated to 100 °C in a metal bath and held for 10 min for protein denaturation. Then, 0.4 mL of the denatured protein solution was incubated with 10 mg of MEI-GP at room temperature (25 ± 0.5 °C) for 30 min. Subsequently, the material was separated by an external magnetic field and washed twice with 0.2 mL of deionized water under ultrasonic conditions for 5 min. Finally, SHV was eluted from MEI-GP using 0.1 mL of 20% NH3⋅H2O (25–28 wt%) in water (v/v) for 5 min and then submitted to MALDI-TOF MS for specific determination. SHV extraction by MNI-GP was used for comparison.

For ATCC®700603, ATCC®BAA-3272, HFK417, and HFK3619, SHV extraction was carried out according to the same process described above (Fig. 6), but without adding recombinant SHV to the protein supernatant.

SHV determination

A volume of 2 μL of SHV eluent was applied onto a single spot of an MSP 96 polished steel MALDI target plate (Bruker Daltonik, Germany). After air drying, the sample was overlaid with 2 μL of matrix solution (50 mM sinapinic acid dissolved in a mixed solvent of 50% acetonitrile, 49.9% water, and 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid). The matrix/sample was allowed to crystallize at room temperature and then submitted to a Microflex MALDI-TOF MS system (Bruker Daltonik) for spectrum acquisition under a positive linear ion mode within an m/z range of 20,000–45,000 Da. The instrument operating and parameter settings could be realized using a flexControl 3.4 software (Bruker Daltonik). Spectrum was easily acquired from only one sediment-rich position with 40 laser shots under a laser frequency of 40%⋅60 Hz, which was then analyzed by a flexAnalysis 3.4 software (Bruker Daltonik).

Results and discussion

Rational screening of SHV-specific epitope

Since its inception, epitope imprinting has been followed by different types of applications. However, to fulfil the imprinted material specificity to target protein or protein family (based on epitope recognition), question regarding which kind of epitope to use as template must be first answered. Currently, two commonly used approaches have been applied to identify protein's epitope. The simplest one is direct selection of the terminal sequence as the epitope [32], and the prepared material can directly adsorb the target protein because the selected epitope is linear and exposed. However, the terminal sequence tends to mutate, and once this happens, the specificity of the imprinted material will be lost. To address this deficiency, another approach emerged in recent years, in which a non-terminal peptide sequence is selected as the epitope [33, 34], which defines that the sequence is exposed and has a linear or simple loop structure rather than an α-helix or β-sheet. This approach highly relies on the prediction and analysis of the protein and peptide structure, and therefore is complicated. Using this approach, although the prepared material can directly adsorb protein, the specificity for the target protein or protein family may be not satisfactory.

As early as 2012, Bossi et al. proposed an approach of protein fingerprint analysis for identification of protein's epitope [27]. This approach relies on a simple bioinformatic analysis, and it was confirmed to be effective by later studies [28]. Generally, three basic steps are involved: (i) in silico cleavage of the target protein by a given protease (e.g., trypsin, thermolysin, or enterokinase); (ii) selection of peptide fragments of adequate length to execute a characteristic analysis (including stability, hydrophilicity, and liposolubility); (iii) alignment of the filtered peptides against protein databases to search for sequence similarity using the BLAST tool. Ultimately, a unique and conserved peptide is identified as the target protein's epitope, and the prepared imprinted material presents good specificity to the target protein and parental family. For this approach, it is clear that the defined epitope is highly correlated with the given protease. If the epitope is exposed outside of the protein, it can be directly adsorbed by the material; if it is hidden inside the protein, a protein pretreatment must be performed to expose the epitope.

Compared with the other two epitope-defined approaches, the approach of protein fingerprint analysis is convenient, easy to understand, and favorable to standardize the procedure of screening specific epitopes for target proteins. In this study, by using this approach, a unique 10-mer peptide was identified as the SHV-specific epitope, which was in vitro synthesized and used as the template for MEI-GP preparation. At first, we attempted to directly adsorb SHV by MEI-GP, but unfortunately, no protein was adsorbed on material. This is attributed to its non-linear native conformation and partially-hidden spatial position of the 10-mer peptide in original SHV molecule (Fig. 1C, details described in the following section). Therefore, before material adsorption, a simple protein denaturation treatment was exerted on SHV molecules to eliminate the sorption obstruction derived from the epitope original structure. After that, the MEI-GP presented expected specific adsorption for SHV.

Preparation and recognition mechanism of MEI-GP

Synthesis and hydrophilic modification of Fe3O4 particle

A protein hydrodynamic diameter in aqueous solution is usually approximately 5–10 nm [35]. Therefore, as a substrate of the MEI-GP, the Fe3O4 particle should have a submicron size to provide a sufficient adhesion surface for protein adsorption. Therefore, a controllable solvothermal method was used for Fe3O4 synthesis (Fig. 2), in which the particle size and the magnetic saturation value were tuned by the Fe3+ concentration and the PEG dosage, respectively [31]. To improve the water solubility of Fe3O4, a PAA modification was applied after Fe3O4 synthesis (Fig. 2), in which the carboxy (–COOH) on PAA chain strongly coordinated to iron cations to form a robust coating, while uncoordinated –COOH extended into the solution, conferring Fe3O4 a high degree of monodispersity in the water [36]. However, the PAA modification is a double-edged sword, as a higher PAA dosage or a larger molecular weight caused Fe3O4 to agglomerate. SiO2 coating on surface of Fe3O4–COOH particle

For SiO2 coating, three important parameters were separately optimized, including TEOS dosage, reaction time, and NH3⋅H2O volume. We found that the higher TEOS dosage and longer reaction time did not noticeably increase the thickness of SiO2 membrane; however, they caused Fe3O4@SiO2 agglomeration. Only the NH3⋅H2O volume was the key factor affecting SiO2 thickness. In this study, using 1 mL of NH3⋅H2O (25–28 wt%) catalyzation and adding 3 mmol TEOS to the reaction in six equal portions with an interval time of 1 h, an SiO2 membrane with a thickness of 3–5 nm was coated on surface of Fe3O4–COOH particle (Fig. 3B), and monodispersed Fe3O4@SiO2 was obtained. After SiO2 coating, a large amount of silicon hydroxy (–Si–OH) covered the entire surface of Fe3O4@SiO2 (Fig. 2), guiding the particle participation in subsequent imprinting polymerization.

Imprinting polymerization occurring on surface of Fe3O4@SiO2

A simple and catalyst-free sol–gel polymerization was used for MEI-GP preparation, which was carried out at 30 °C, using three silane coupling agents, APTES, UPTES, and BHEAPTES, as functional monomers, and TEOS as cross-linker. After 3 h of incubation, the template epitope was captured by functional monomers; specifically, surrounding the template, an area rich in functional monomers was formed (Fig. 2). Derived from the hydrolysis of amino group (–NH2) on APTES and UPTES, this area contained plenty of OHˉ, and was sufficient to catalyze the hydrolysis of silicon ethoxy (–Si–OC2H5), followed by the condensation reaction of –Si–OH. We found that when reducing the dosage of functional monomers, the low-produced OHˉ could not replace –OC2H5, and the –Si–OH was not able to be produced; whereas high-produced OHˉ originating from high-dosage functional monomers inhibited the hydrolysis of ⋅OC2H5, and the cumulated ⋅OC2H5 restrained the production of –Si–OH. Thus, the influence of the functional monomer dosage on the sol–gel polymerization followed a parabolic pattern.

For imprinting optimization, we fixed dosages of functional monomers and cross-linker, and template epitope was adjusted within a set molar ratio range of template/APTES/UPTES/BHEAPTES/TEOS = (0.25–3):(5⋅8):(9⋅4):(3⋅4):(20⋅4), and several epitope-imprinted polymers were prepared. We found that the sorption amounts of these materials tapered off as the template dosage increased (Fig. S1). When a low-dosage template was used for epitope imprinting, the material mainly showed non-specific adsorption, and when a high-dosage template was used, the polymer overcrosslinked and caused many imprinted cavities coupled with template molecules to be trapped within polymer network structure. In comparison, a ratio of 1:(5⋅8):(9⋅4):(3⋅4):(20⋅4) was used as the optimal condition for MEI-GP preparation. Under this condition, the sorption amount of the prepared material was stable, with only small fluctuations occurring when template dosage was adjusted up and down. Herein, the molar ratio of 1:(x⋅8):(y⋅4):(z⋅4):((x + y + z)⋅4) can serve as a reference ratio for other epitope imprinting (x, y, and z, are the number of –COOH, –CONH–, and –NH2 groups in template molecule that separately interact with –NH2, –CONH–, and –OH in functional monomer), which researchers can use to find the optimal conditions for their imprinted polymers.

Without areas rich in OHˉ (due to the absence of template epitope), the MNI-GP preparation required the help of NH3⋅H2O for catalysis. Like the formation of MEI-GP, the influence of the NH3⋅H2O volume on non-imprinting polymerization also followed a parabolic pattern.

Due to its participation in sol–gel polymerization of the –Si–OH on Fe3O4@SiO2 surface, the obtained MEI-GP and MNI-GP both had a mononuclear core–shell structure, with an imprinted and non-imprinted film thickness of 2–4 nm (Fig. 3C and D).

Recognition mechanism of MEI-GP for template epitope and SHV

For template epitope recognition by MEI-GP, two non-covalent interactions were involved, including electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding (Fig. 2), which occurred in imprinted cavities. The imprinted cavity completely matched the template in terms of shape, size, and positioning of chemical groups, which is the foundation of epitope-specific recognition. The template VDAGDEQLER has good water solubility, indicating that it can be easily dissolved in polar solvents (e.g., 1,2-propanediol) but difficultly in low-polar liquids (e.g., ethanol). As a strong polar solvent, water can easily disturb hydrogen bonding between chemical groups. Therefore, in this study, a mixed solvent of ethanol/1,2-propanediol (1:1, v/v) was used as the porogen for MEI-GP preparation, and the ethanol/water (1:1, v/v) was used for template adsorption, so that the non-covalent interactions could be expressed efficiently.

Based on epitope-specific recognition, SHV was selectively adsorbed by MEI-GP. However, before adsorption, two factors were taken into consideration. One is that the in vitro synthesized 10-mer epitope peptide tends to maintain a linear structure, which is different from its native conformation (α helix and Ω loop, Fig. 1C) in original SHV molecule. The other is that the epitope peptide partially hides in the globular structure (Fig. 1C) of the original SHV, which would obstruct material adsorption. Therefore, the spherical SHV needed to be denatured to a soluble linear structure before adsorption, to ensure the epitope was completely exposed. For this, 2% (w/v) SDS was added to the protein solution, which was heated at 100 °C for 10 min, and the denatured SHV was enwrapped by plenty of anions, and therefore became soluble in aqueous solution (Fig. 2). In addition, because ethanol has a strong ability to precipitate proteins, a medium of 1,2-propanediol/water (1:1, v/v) was used for SHV adsorption in this study.

Selectivity

As shown in Fig. 5A, sorption amount of the template epitope VDAGDEQLER bound on MEI-GP was much higher than that of the reference peptide VDAGQEQLGR (TEM-specific epitope), indicating that only two amino acid substitutions substantially reduced peptide binding, which confirmed the specific recognition mechanism (shape and size matching and functional group interactions) of MEI-GP.

As can be seen in Fig. 5B, due to epitope-specific recognition, MEI-GP exhibited better selectivity for SHV than for the reference protein (recombinant TEM-1), indicating its ability to purify target proteins from complex matrices. Because MNI-GP had no imprinted cavities, its sorption for target analytes was very similar to that for reference molecules.

Specific detection of SHV in bacteria

Under optimal conditions (Fig. S2 and S3), SHV was selectively extracted from bacteria by MEI-GP and then submitted to MALDI-TOF MS for specific determination (Fig. 6). As shown in Fig. 7 and Fig. S4 and S5, after SHV extracted by MEI-GP, matrix interferences were effectively eliminated and the spectrum baseline became very smooth (compared with direct detection of bacterial crackate solution), enabling SHV signal to be greatly enhanced and clearly presented in mass spectra. This is the first study to visually present bacterial resistance mechanism in a given mass spectrum, which combined the specificity of epitope imprinting (SHV family-specific) with the sensitivity of mass spectrometry. The limit of detection (LOD) of our technique for SHV was ≤ 2 μg/mL, while the LOD for bacteria extraction was ≤ 15 mg, and the equivalent OD600 was ≤ 6.28 (bacteria dispersed in 1 mL of sterile water). The process of determining the LOD is described in Supplementary Information. By following this strategy, other β-lactamase families can also be specifically detected. According to the molecular weight displayed in mass spectra, the kind of β-lactamase and its associated hydrolysis profile on β-lactams can be easily identified. Based on this, an initial drug option scheme can be quickly formulated for antimicrobial therapy. Using this enzyme detection strategy, from protein extraction to medication guidance reporting, the mean time to detection (MTTD) was less than 2 h.

Notably, realizing specific detection of SHV was not our ultimate goal, while that further application of the proposed strategy to all of the drug resistance-related proteins is the real purpose. Based on this study, following studies could focus on applying a series of enzyme family-specific materials to construct mass spectra libraries on AMR-related proteins, which can have great implications for precisely grasping the enzyme epidemiological characteristics and quickly formulating a comprehensive drug option scheme, thus effectively shortening the empirical medication period in clinical medicine.

Comparison with other drug resistance detection methods

(i) Compared with the most commonly used phenotype-based method, antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) (MTTD: at least 16–20 h) [38, 39], our enzyme detection strategy avoids the use of antibiotics, and it takes no more than 2 h to obtain an initial AMR information on β-lactams, thus shortening the empirical medication period. Nevertheless, as a golden standard of AMR detection, the AST method provides a wide range of AMR information on multiple antibiotics. Therefore, currently, our technique could serve as a supplementary tool for AST, by quickly formulating an initial drug option scheme for antimicrobial therapy, and rapidly explaining confusing drug-resistant profiles. However, in the future, by applying a series of enzyme family-specific materials to construct mass spectra libraries on AMR-related proteins, our proposed technique may unseat AST dominance.

(ii) Hydrolysis-based methods have been developed in recent years that rely on MS detection. Unlike our enzyme detection strategy, they can only indirectly detect β-lactamases by detecting the hydrolysates of β-lactams after an incubation of several hours or longer with bacteria [40]. Thus, these methods are laborious and time-consuming, and non-specific to target enzymes. Conversely, by directly detecting β-lactamases, our technique rapidly explains the bacterial AMR profile on a range of β-lactams at a time, thus rendering the technique more efficient.

(iii) Fingerprint-based methods were also developed in the last decade. Before analysis, a bacterial protein profile is obtained by MALDI-TOF MS with an m/z range of 20,000–45,000 Da, which contains all of the mass peaks of β-lactamases (29,000–39,000 Da). However, searching for target enzyme peak from plenty of peaks is a quite complicated work, because the enzyme signal is strongly interfered by matrix proteins, causing target signal to be depressed or even disappear [41]. In comparison, our enzyme-specific detection strategy effectively eliminates matrix interferences, enabling β-lactamase peak to be clearly presented in mass spectra, and therefore has a broad application prospect.

(iv) As an emerging gene editing technique, the CRISPR-Cas system has received widespread attention in various research fields, and has been applied to AMR detection. Peng et al. designed a recombinase polymerase amplification-based CRISPR-Cas12a assay [42], by mixing Cas12a-crRNA (sgRNA) with the amplified fluorophore-labeled target DNA fragments (AMR-related) to activate double-stranded breaks at a specific site, and the release of fluorescence signals indicated that the test pathogen was drug resistant. Similarly, Allan-Blitz et al used an isothermal amplification-based CRISPR-Cas13a system to identify Neisseria gonorrhoeae via the porA gene and predict ciprofloxacin resistance by gyrA gene [43]. Clearly, the CRISPR-Cas system and our proposed technique are both based on the recognition of a specific fragment; the CRISPR-Cas system recognizes a particular section (20–30 nucleotides) on a target single-stranded DNA sequence, and our technique recognizes a specific epitope (9–15 amino acids) on a target protein sequence. However, compared with the present CRISPR-Cas-based technique that is highly reliant on time-consuming DNA extraction, purification, and amplification, our technique is easy to perform in a short time, and the application of MS detection increases the method sensitivity.

(v) Compared with other popular gene-based techniques, such as oligonucleotide probe recognition, target gene amplification, and whole-genome sequencing (MTTD: usually about 8 h) [14–16, 44], our enzyme detection strategy can visually present bacterial resistance mechanism to researchers, and the use of imprinted material (like the probe in gene testing) endows our technique with good specificity for target enzymes, and therefore is strongly complementary to the current gene testing. Moreover, the strategy of targeting an enzyme family as the detection unit rather than a single protein further advances the work efficiency, rendering our technique more suitable for clinical application.

Conclusions

In this study, a simple and catalyst-free sol–gel polymerization was performed to prepare the SHV-specific MEI-GP. The prepared MEI-GP could selectively adsorb all of the enzymes in SHV family based on epitope-specific recognition, and the sorption amount for SHV was much higher than that for any of reference proteins (without SHV-specific epitope). By modeling the sorption data, we found that a van der Waals interaction took place between the adsorbed and free SHV molecules after SHV was non-covalently captured by MEI-GP, which further increased SHV adsorption. Surface imprinting facilitated rapid diffusion of SHV upon the imprinted cavities, and an initial equilibrium was achieved in nearly 30 min, which contributed to a vast majority of SHV adsorption (followed by the van der Waals force contribution). Using MEI-GP as extraction adsorbent, an enzyme-specific detection strategy was developed, which combined the specificity of epitope imprinting with the sensitivity of mass spectrometry, allowing SHV to be selectively extracted from bacteria and clearly presented in mass spectra. By following this strategy, other β-lactamase families can also be specifically detected. Based on this study, following studies could focus on the construction of mass spectra libraries on AMR-related proteins by applying a series of enzyme family-specific materials. Compared with other AMR detection methods, our technique has good specificity, high sensitivity (≤ 15 mg of bacteria), a short MTTD (less than 2 h), and simple operation, and therefore has a broad application prospect in clinical medicine.
