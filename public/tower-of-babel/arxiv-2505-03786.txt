When Reasoning Beats Scale: A 1.5B Reasoning Model Outranks 13B LLMs as Discriminator
Md Fahim Anjum

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLM) with reasoning capabilities offer a promising path for improving candidate evaluation in planning frameworks, but their relative performance against traditional non - reasoning models remains largely underexplored. In this study, we benchmark a distilled 1.5B parameter reasoning model (DeepSeek - R1) against several state - of - the - art non - reasoning LLMs within a generator-discriminator LLM planning framework for the text-to-SQL task. For this, we introduce a novel method for extracting soft scores from the chain-of-thought (CoT) outputs from reasoning that enables fine-grained ranking of candidates. Our central hypothesis is that reasoning models are more effective discriminators than non-reasoning LLMs. Our results show that distilled DeepSeek-R1-1.5B achieves up to 87% higher F1 and 3.7% better discrimination accuracy than CodeLlama-7B, as well as 3.7% higher execution accuracy than CodeLlama-13B, despite having significantly fewer parameters. Furthermore, we find that there is a limit to the logical capabilities of reasoning models, and only providing more context or allowing more compute budget for reasoning is not enough to improve their discrimination performance. Finally, we demonstrate that, unlike non-reasoning LLMs, reasoning models find generation more challenging than discrimination and may underperform as generators compared to smaller non-reasoning LLMs. Our work highlights the potential of reasoning models as discriminators in agentic frameworks, far outweighing their capabilities as generators, offering insights into their optimal role within LLM planning infrastructures.

Introduction

Planning is a fundamental part of intelligent decision-making. Significant research efforts have focused on developing approaches to enable effective and human-like planning, including multi-phase and multi-agent frameworks
[
21
,
41
,
17
,
25

[
14

15
,
43
,
44
,

[
25
,
16
]
. Specifically, LLMs first generate candidate actions (
generation
), evaluate their consequences using themselves, internal simulations, or another model (
discrimination
), and finally integrate these outcomes for optimal solutions (
planning
). This structure aligns closely with how LLMs approach multi-step tasks such as mathematical reasoning
[
35
]
, multi-hop question answering
[
44
]
, and code generation
[
42
]
. Performance of conventional non-reasoning LLMs in such frameworks has been studied comprehensively
[
5
]
.
However, recent developments on LLMs designed for inference-time reasoning via internal deliberation show promising performance across various tasks. Augmented with chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning
[
34
]
, these models offer a structured approach to complex tasks. Large reasoning models such as OpenAI o-series
[
19
]
, Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking
[
8

, DeepSeek-R1
[
6

, LIMO
[

[

have shown impressive stepwise reasoning and state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance across complex benchmarks, far surpassing non-reasoning LLMs
[
40
]
.
Notably, DeepSeek-R1
[
6
]
has achieved SOTA performance on par with OpenAI’s models despite low training cost. With logical and accurate outputs, it consistently outperforms Qwen and LLaMA series models, even under quantization and distillation
[
49
,
38
]
.
Despite the success of reasoning models over traditional non-reasoning LLMs, only a handful of studies have explored their integration into planning or multi-agent frameworks
[
38
,
26
,
3
]
. To the best of our knowledge, there has been no systematic investigation into how reasoning models compare to conventional non-reasoning LLMs within the generator-discriminator LLM planning architecture.

In this work, we examine the role of a reasoning model within a generator-discriminator LLM planning agentic framework applied to the text-to-SQL generation task. For this, we use a distilled 1.5B model of DeepSeek-R1 and hypothesize that reasoning models may serve as more effective discriminators and conduct a systematic investigation guided by the following research questions:

(1)

How does incorporating a reasoning LLM as the discriminator influence the accuracy and overall performance of the agentic framework?

(2)

What is the impact of inference-time CoT compute budget and contextual augmentation on the discrimination performance?

(2)

How does a reasoning model perform in the generator role, and is it better at generation than discrimination?

To this end, we analyze and comprehensively evaluate DeepSeek-R1’s discrimination as well as generation abilities within our LLM planning framework and compare the performance with several SOTA non-reasoning LLMs.
An additional challenge arises in the extraction of soft scores from CoT reasoning outputs. Traditional non-reasoning LLMs can easily provide logit-based scores for discrimination, but in reasoning models, the arbitrarily long and unstructured nature of the CoT trace complicates soft score extraction. To address this, we propose a novel approach for deriving soft scores from the reasoning outputs, enabling more refined discrimination.

Our work evaluates LLMs’ ability to discriminate correct and incorrect SQL queries by re-labeling oracle-generated outputs, measuring both classification and ranking. Discriminator performance is tested in a naive setting and an enhanced setting that filters by executability via environment-based validation. We further test whether LLMs can select correct queries from dynamically generated candidates. We analyze DeepSeek-R1’s reasoning quality through lexical, structural, and semantic diversity and examine three key factors influencing its discrimination performance: (1) increased test-time compute budget, (2) prompt augmentation with database schema context, and (3) the use of binary decisions vs. logit-based soft scores. Finally, we evaluate LLMs’ performance as generators in the full LLM planning pipeline.
Our contributions in this work are as follows:

(1)
We provide a systematic comparison of reasoning and non-reasoning LLMs within a generator-discriminator LLM planning framework for the text-to-SQL task.

(2)
We introduce a novel use of reasoning models as discriminators by deriving soft scores from their outputs for more fine-grained candidate ranking.

(3)
We show that DeepSeek-R1 excels at discrimination, outperforming larger non-reasoning models. In particular, we find that a 1.5B distilled DeepSeek-R1 model achieves
87\%
higher F1 as well as
3.7\%
better discrimination accuracy than CodeLlama-7B and
3.7\%
higher execution accuracy than CodeLlama-13B. Furthermore, logit-based soft scoring vs. binary true/false discrimination yields minimal differences (
<1.5\%

(4)
We find that reasoning models have limited logical capacity and adding more context or compute budget yields diminishing returns (e.g., <0.4% gain beyond 1024 tokens), while extremely low budgets severely degrade performance (
<2\%
accuracy,
>94\%

(5)
Finally, we show that while DeepSeek-R1 is a strong discriminator, it underperforms as a generator, even compared to smaller non-reasoning LLMs. We find that, similar to humans, generation is more challenging than discrimination for reasoning models
[

, in contrast to non-reasoning LLMs

10

In summary, our work investigates the role of DeepSeek-R1 within an LLM planning framework for text-to-SQL task.
Through this study, we seek to advance our understanding of reasoning-based LLMs and inform future efforts in designing robust, efficient, and scalable autonomous systems.

Related Work

In a generator-discriminator LLM planning framework, discrimination is often seen as easier than generation for human and AI agents
[
9
]
. However, West et al. hypothesized that generative models such as LLMs may lack discrimination abilities to match their generation capabilities
[
36
]
.
This aligns with findings from other studies, which suggest that non-reasoning LLMs may misidentify correct self-generated plans as incorrect without external feedback
[
10
,
31
]
.
Huang et al. observed that self-correction performance gains may depend on high-quality external feedback
[
10
]

Chen et al. focused on non-reasoning LLMs’ discrimination abilities and their impact on several planning methods and found a strong correlation between discrimination accuracy and overall task performance
[
5
]
.

In the context of test-time compute for reasoning models, Zeng et al. observed an inverse scaling phenomenon where accuracy declines as CoT length increases
[
47
]
. They also found that correct solutions are consistently shorter than incorrect ones, suggesting longer CoTs may actually harm performance. Other studies have shown that exceeding a model’s inherent reasoning capacity with longer CoTs leads to error accumulation as well as performance degradation
[
11
,
4
,
39
]

and simply increasing inference-time compute budget does not guarantee performance improvement
[
20
,
33
]
.
Moreover, recent studies have found that optimal reasoning length depends on the model’s capability as well as on the complexity of the task.
[
40
,
27
,
2
]

Unlike these prior studies that focus either on non-reasoning models for planning or reasoning models’ CoT without any agentic framework, our work investigates the role of a reasoning model within the LLM planning framework and examines the impact of inference-time compute budget on performance.

Experimental Setup

3.1
Task and Dataset

We evaluate the text-to-SQL task using the Spider dataset

[

The full Spider development set consists of 1,034 examples, with 24% easy (248), 43.1% medium (446), and 32.9% hard or extra hard (340). The random sampling procedure of the examples is described in
[
5
]
.

TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat-v1.0

]
,

,

3.3
Framework for LLM Planning

As illustrated in Figure
1
, we adopt a generator-discriminator framework
[
5
]
, where the generator LLM proposes candidate solutions or actions and the discriminator LLM evaluates them. The planning module ranks candidates based on the evaluation outcomes and orchestrates the interaction between the two models. In this section, we detail each of these components and their specific implementation for text-to-SQL parsing.

Figure 1:
Framework of LLM planning for Text-to-SQL task utilized for this study. For further information, see
[
5
]
.

3.3.1
Generator

We use the generator LLM to produce candidate SQL queries for user-provided natural language questions via zero-shot prompting. Database tables are included as context, and a fixed prompt template is employed to guide candidate generation. For non-reasoning models, we prompt with the instruction
‘Given database schema and a question in natural language, generate the corresponding SQL query.’’
. For the reasoning model (Distill-R1), the instruction is
‘‘Answer the question by generating a valid sqlite SQL query. Final answer must start with SELECT.’’
which allows the model to use reasoning for generating the query. The prompt template in this case is provided in markdown format, facilitating the extraction of the final SQL query generated by the model. Example prompts for both reasoning and non-reasoning models are given in the Appendix (Table
14
and Table
15
).

We generate text-to-SQL outputs using the HuggingFace implementation
[
37
]
, leveraging its model checkpoint and generation function. The hyperparameters are set as follows:
\mathtt{max\_length}=300
,
\mathtt{temperature}=0.2
, and
\mathtt{num\_return\_sequences}=5
. When a single output is generated without planning or discriminator feedback (
\mathtt{num\_return\_sequences}=1
), we refer to it as
greedy generation
.

3.3.2
Discriminator

Given some candidate solutions, we formulate the discrimination task as a binary question answering
[
12
,
13
]
, and generate a discrimination score for each candidate.

Non-reasoning Models:

For conventional LLMs, we follow a straightforward scoring approach: the input prompt is passed to the model, and the discrimination score is computed as the token-level probability of generating
‘‘Yes’’
as the next token by applying a softmax function over the final logits
[
5
]
. Specifically, we prompt the LLMs with the question
‘‘Is the SQL program correct given the utterance?’’
and extract the softmax probability assigned to
‘‘Yes’’
as the output score. An illustrative example is provided in the Appendix (Table
11
).

Reasoning Model:

Unlike the non-reasoning models, Distill-R1 utilizes reasoning to obtain a conclusion, and thus obtaining a soft score is not straightforward. For this, we introduce a novel approach: first, we prompt the reasoning model to think and output the final answer in a specific key-value format (JSON). In particular, we prompt the model with the instruction
‘‘Determine whether the SQL query is correct or not for the given question. Provide final answer in JSON format with the key ‘correct’.’’
An example prompt is provided in the Appendix (Table
12
). After the token generation process, the JSON format is identified and parsed to obtain the value for key
‘correct’
along with its corresponding logit. Then, we normalize the logit via the softmax function to compute the probability
\rho
for the possible key values (‘true’ or ‘false’) and assign the score as
\rho
for ‘true’ or
1-\rho
for ‘false’. (Figure
2
). If both ‘true’ and ‘false’ values are missing, a default score
-0.5
is returned.

Figure 2:
Flowchart illustrating our approach for obtaining soft evaluation scores from a reasoning model (Distill-R1) as a discriminator.

3.3.3
Planning Method

We adopt re-ranking, a simple yet effective planning strategy where the generator first samples a set of candidate queries. These candidates are then individually scored by the discriminator, and the highest-scoring query is returned as the final output. Despite its simplicity, this planning method is widely adopted in code generation
[
18
]
and mathematical reasoning tasks
[
32
,
14
]
.

In our experiments, we utilize the discriminator to score the candidates in two configurations
[
5
]
:

1.

Naive Discriminator
: In this case, the discriminator evaluates the candidates without any additional information.

2.

Enhanced with Executability Check
: Here, the candidate SQL queries are tested on an environment to see whether they are executable (run without producing errors). The discriminator only ranks the executable candidates.

3.4
Evaluation Methods

3.4.1
Intrinsic Evaluation

To assess LLMs’ discrimination performance, we reuse the generation results from the oracle-simulation experiments in
[
5
]
. Here, evaluation scripts were used to re-label the pre-generated SQL queries in simulated re-ranking experiments, where an oracle compares the top five execution results of the predicted and gold SQL queries using table cell overlap.
We evaluate the discrimination ability using a range of metrics to capture both binary classification and ranking performance.

3.4.2
End-to-End Evaluation

Unlike intrinsic evaluation, which assesses discrimination ability on a fixed set of queries, our end-to-end evaluation measures the effectiveness of LLMs in dynamically generating and ranking candidates within our planning framework. Specifically, we assess the full pipeline performance of our LLM planning framework with LLM-based generators and discriminators under the re-ranking planning strategy and determine their ability to generate and select correct queries in real-time decision-making scenarios. For consistency, we use the same 400 problem instances as in the intrinsic evaluation, enabling one-to-one correspondence across query batches.

3.5
Performance Metrics

3.5.1
Intrinsic Evaluation

To measure the discrimination abilities of LLMs, we employ four intrinsic metrics that evaluate both pairwise judgment and ranking quality
[
5
]
. Pairwise Discrimination Accuracy
(PwAcc)
measures the percentage of times the model assigns a higher score to a correct query over an incorrect one, given a pair of queries
[
1
,
30
]
. Classification Macro F1
(F1)
treats “correct” and “incorrect” as separate classes and computes the macro-averaged F1 score across them. Hit@1
(H@1)
evaluates how often the top-ranked query from a candidate set is correct. Finally, mean Reciprocal Rank
(MRR)
computes the average reciprocal rank of the highest-scoring candidate in each batch.

3.5.2
End-to-End Evaluation

We evaluate the end-to-end performance of the models in the LLM-based planning framework using both execution and match-based metrics. This includes execution accuracy for text-to-SQL generation, exact match accuracy to the reference gold query, and partial match performance measured by accuracy, recall, and F1 score. We utilize the semantic evaluation test suite
[
50
]
for the metric calculation.

3.5.3
Reasoning Quality

To measure the richness and non-redundancy of the model’s reasoning output, we assess its reasoning quality using a set of diversity and repetition-based metrics:
Repetition Ratio
quantifies the proportion of repeated sentences,
\boldsymbol{1-}
TTR
(One minus Type-Token Ratio) calculates lexical simplicity by capturing the proportion of repeated sentences relative to the total, with higher values indicating greater redundancy and simpler language,
Repeated n-grams
captures the frequency of recurring n-grams (e.g., 3-grams) by calculating the repetition ratio of phrases,
Entropy
reflects the distribution of sentence occurrences, with higher values signifying richer output. Finally,
Diversity
measures the overall variety in the generated content by utilizing sentence embeddings and cosine similarity to assess the average dissimilarity between sentence pairs. Higher diversity corresponds to lower average similarity.

4.1.2
Increasing Test-Time Compute Yields Diminishing Returns

Given the strong performance of Distill-R1 as a discriminator in both intrinsic and end-to-end evaluations, we further investigate the impact of test-time compute budget on its discrimination capabilities. Specifically, we explore whether allocating additional computation for reasoning enhances its performance. Experiments are conducted using Distill-R1 as the discriminator under varying token budgets.

Figure 4:
Discrimination performance (
top row
) and reasoning quality (
bottom row
) of the reasoning model (Distill-R1) as a function of test-time compute budget (token limit).

Our results show that increasing test-time compute (via the maximum new token limit) significantly enhances discrimination performance initially, but levels off after reaching a certain threshold. At very low token limits (e.g., 256 tokens), the reasoning process is heavily constrained, which causes drastically low pairwise discrimination accuracy (
<2\%
) and a high failure rate (
94+\%
; Figure
4
; Appendix: Table
8
). As the token limit increases, the pairwise accuracy improves gradually and peaks around 1024 tokens. Beyond this point, additional tokens provide only marginal gains, with accuracy increasing by less than
0.4\%
from 1024 to 2048 tokens. Similar trends are observed in other metrics, such as F1, Hit@1, and MRR, while the failure rate decreases sharply with more tokens before stabilizing.

In terms of reasoning quality, our results suggest diminishing returns with increased test-time compute. As the maximum token limit increases,
1-
TTR, repetition ratio, and repeated n-grams all rise (Figure
4
; Appendix: Table
8
), indicating that the model is producing more lexically redundant and repetitive outputs, rather than generating novel content. This suggests that beyond a certain point, additional compute budget leads to more verbose but not necessarily more insightful reasoning. Furthermore, entropy and diversity metrics exhibit peaks between 512 and 1024 tokens and then plateau or slightly decline (Appendix: Table
8
). These indicate that the model’s reasoning variety and information richness improve only up to a moderate compute budget, after which the quality and variety of reasoning stop improving, with longer outputs becoming more repetitive rather than more insightful. Taken together, these results show that while a minimal token threshold is necessary to enable coherent reasoning, simply increasing token limits beyond this threshold leads to diminishing improvements in both discrimination performance and reasoning quality.

5
Discussion

In this work, we study the role of a reasoning model within the generator-discriminator LLM planning framework
[
5
]
. In particular, we use the re-ranking planning method and benchmark a reasoning model (Distill-R1-1.5B) against several non-reasoning LLMs in the text-to-SQL task. Our investigation reveals several key insights into the roles of reasoning models in LLM planning frameworks, with significant implications for both the design and deployment of such models in an agentic framework.

5.1
Distill-R1 Outperforms Larger Non-Reasoning LLMs as Discriminator

First, our findings show that the reasoning model excels as a discriminator not only in distinguishing correct from incorrect queries but also by achieving the highest overall execution accuracy in the LLM planning framework, outperforming larger non-reasoning models in both cases.
During our intrinsic evaluation, Distill-R1 shows strong capabilities in discriminating between correct and incorrect queries more effectively than its non-reasoning counterparts. In particular, compared to CodeLlama-7B, Distill-R1 demonstrates improvements of
2.9\%
in pairwise accuracy,
58\%
in F1,
4.6\%
in Hit@1, and
2.6\%
in MRR under the naive setting, which become
3.5\%
,
87\%
,
2.8\%
, and
1.9\%
respectively, when executability checks are applied (Table
1
).
In terms of overall execution accuracy during end-to-end evaluation of our LLM planning framework,
Distill-R1 with only 1.5B parameters outperformed non-reasoning models that are more than 8 times larger
(
3.7\%
improvement compared to CodeLlama-13B; Table
2
). These findings suggest that reasoning models are particularly effective in evaluating candidate queries, even when a smaller model is used. Thus, by leveraging their inherent reasoning abilities, such models can discern subtle differences between candidates more effectively than non-reasoning models.
Moreover, the performance difference between logit-based soft scoring and binary true/false discrimination is minimal (
<1.5\%
) for Distill-R1, showing that reasoning models are good discriminators in the LLM planning framework even when only binary outcomes are available. This can provide a significant advantage in commercial settings where access to logit values for reasoning models may be restricted or expensive. Together, these results suggest that reasoning models hold substantial promise for robust discrimination tasks, and further research could drive even greater performance improvements.

5.2
Inherent Limits of Reasoning Capabilities

Second, our findings demonstrate that for reasoning models, there is a limit to the benefits of increased test-time compute for evaluating candidates. A very low token limit severely restricts the reasoning process, leading to extremely low accuracy and a very high failure rate, indicating the necessity of a minimum compute threshold. As the token budget increases, performance improves and peaks around 1024 tokens, beyond which the gains become negligible. These show that allocating more test-time compute budget does not directly translate into more insightful reasoning. We also observe that increasing the token budget leads to more redundancy and repetition in reasoning outputs (Figure
4
; Appendix: Table
8
), indicating the occurrence of circular reasoning and loss of context. Furthermore, we find that incorporating database schema information into the prompt does not offer significant improvements for Distill-R1 (Table
4
). In fact, it can introduce redundancy and even elevate failure rates. Our findings are consistent with earlier studies, which also showed a minimum computing budget, a diminishing return after a certain threshold, and an inverse relationship between CoT length and accuracy
[
47
,
39
]
.
Together, these results indicate that
there is a limit to the logical capabilities of these reasoning models, and providing more context or allowing more compute budget for reasoning is not sufficient for improving their performance
. Future studies should focus not only on scaling compute and maximizing context window but also on targeted fine-tuning of such reasoning models.

5.3
Generation is Harder than Discrimination

Third, while Distill-R1 shows strong performance as a discriminator, it performs poorly as a generator. In fact, for generating high-quality SQL queries, it is outperformed by smaller non-reasoning models (Table
7
and Figure
5
). This suggests that while reasoning models are effective at evaluating candidate queries, they may not be well-suited for generating them. This result is not surprising, as SQL generation from natural language involves complex reasoning that can branch into convoluted CoT. With our earlier results indicating a fundamental limitation in the reasoning capabilities, navigating through a complex CoT is challenging for reasoning models. Hence,
while previous studies have shown that for non-reasoning LLMs, the discriminator role is typically more challenging and significant than the generator role
[
5
,
36
,
10
,
31
]
, our results indicate the opposite for reasoning models
, which is similar to humans
[
9
]
.

5.4
Future Works and Limitations

In this study, we demonstrate that reasoning models can be employed to generate soft scores for evaluating candidates, which is a non-trivial task given that CoT reasoning may produce arbitrarily long token outputs, complicating soft score extraction. By leveraging our approach, it is potentially possible to fine-tune such models (with soft score as the cost function) and adjust their reasoning approaches to improve performance even further. We plan to explore this in future studies.

Our study has several limitations. The experiments are conducted within the specific context of text-to-SQL generation and discrimination, which may not directly generalize to other domains or reasoning tasks. Additionally, while our analysis provides insights into the trade-offs between compute budget and performance, further research is necessary to fully understand the interplay between model architecture, token limits, and task complexity. Lastly, we use a relatively small reasoning model (Distill-R1, 1.5B parameters) and a reduced dataset due to our limited budget for computational resources. However, our goal in this work is to investigate and gain a deeper understanding of reasoning models within LLM planning frameworks. In this aspect, we believe our findings will provide significant insights even for larger reasoning models.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we explore the role of a reasoning model, Distilled DeepSeek-R1-1.5B, within an LLM planning framework for text-to-SQL task. We introduce a novel approach to extract soft scores from its CoT outputs for discrimination and systematically evaluate its capabilities as a discriminator well as a generator.
We show that DeepSeek-R1 outperforms much larger non-reasoning models in query discrimination, highlighting the potential of such models as discriminators within LLM planning frameworks. Our study also reveals that reasoning models face inherent limitations. In particular, increasing test-time compute and additional context yield diminishing returns. Finally, while DeepSeek-R1 excels at evaluating candidate queries, it struggles as a generator, where even smaller non-reasoning models deliver higher-quality SQL outputs.
Though our experiments are constrained in scope and scale, these insights can point to promising directions for developing smaller, more efficient agentic systems that strategically integrate reasoning models into planning frameworks.
