Insight into phase-sensitive optical coherence tomography displacement measurement
Authors: Andrea Mazzolani, Jiayue Li, Balázs Dura-Kovács, Brendan F. Kennedy, Peter R. T. Munro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phase-sensitive optical coherence tomography (OCT) measures motion from phase differences between repeated acquisitions, supporting techniques such as Doppler OCT and optical coherence elastography (OCE). A recent numerical and experimental study showed that, even without noise, displacement estimation is less accurate in dark speckles. Here, we demonstrate analytically why displacement is not proportional to phase difference in speckled OCT images. We derive an expansion for compression OCE containing higher-order terms beyond the conventional linear term and show that neglecting them introduces errors that increase with depth and strain-discontinuity magnitude. We also identify two phenomena that perturb the phase linearity, one producing features resembling phase-wrapping events that may be mitigated by phase-unwrapping techniques. Finally, we introduce a displacement-estimation method that overcomes these limitations and achieves errors two orders of magnitude lower than conventional approaches. These phase-instability findings may reshape motion measurement beyond compression OCE, including dynamic OCT.

Introduction

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is increasingly being used to perform functional and label-free imaging. For example, Doppler OCT is an important extension to OCT as it allows the measurement of blood velocity and flow1,2. Optical coherence elastography (OCE)3–8 is another powerful label-free imaging technique that obtains three-dimensional maps of the mechanical properties of biological tissues. Doppler OCT and OCE images are formed from the acquisition of at least two optical coherence tomography (OCT)9,10 images of the sample, each acquired at different times. Dynamic OCT has emerged recently as a powerful label-free approach for observing cell physiology, most commonly by analyzing temporal fluctuations in the intensity or amplitude of the OCT signal11. Although conventional dynamic OCT metrics are generally not based on phase-difference displacement estimation, recent studies have begun to investigate phase-based dynamic OCT analyses. For example, Schulz-Hildebrandt et al.12 introduced a phase-sensitive dynamic OCT approach to quantify intracellular motion from phase changes between repeated complex OCT measurements, while Bellesini et al.13 included phase variance among the methods used to visualize three-dimensional dynamic OCT contrast for breast tumor margin assessment.

The implementation of these techniques leverages the sub-nanometer sensitivity to sample axial displacement14.

We show in this paper, however, why this high sensitivity is significantly compromised in noise-free OCT images containing speckle. For clarity, in the remainder of this paper we consider the application of quasi-static OCE, since considering both local strain and bulk displacement allows a range of other OCT modalities to be analyzed also. For example, to first approximation, Doppler OCT acquires one image, followed by another image after a time delay, during which the sample has undergone bulk displacement due to flow.

In phase-sensitive OCE5,6, the phase difference between OCT signals is calculated to estimate the axial displacement, d(z), of tissue at axial depth z, by using the following equation3,5,15–17, which we denote as the phase-difference equation (PHDE): 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d(z)\approx \frac{\Delta \phi (z)}{2\eta {k}_{0}},$$\end{document}d(z)≈Δϕ(z)2ηk0, where k0 is the central wavenumber, η is the background refractive index and Δϕ(z) ≔ ϕU(z) − ϕL(z) is the difference in phase of unloaded and loaded A-scans and we use the superscripts U, L to denote the unloaded and loaded signals, respectively. In order to simplify the calculations, for the remainder of this article we assume without loss of generality that η = 1.

Although strain is dimensionless, for clarity throughout the manuscript we use the symbol ϵ as a strain marker, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\epsilon }$$\end{document}ϵ^ denotes the strain value used in the model; the hat is used only to distinguish the strain variable from the dimensionless unit ϵ. For example, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\epsilon }=-3\,m\epsilon$$\end{document}ϵ^=−3mϵ corresponds to a compressive strain of −0.3%, where m denotes the metric prefix milli. Under the assumption of constant local axial strain, the axial displacement within the sample can be written as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d(z):=D+\hat{\epsilon }z$$\end{document}d(z):=D+ϵ^z, where D is called bulk displacement, with the same units as z, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\epsilon }$$\end{document}ϵ^ is the local axial strain at depth z. The bulk displacement occurs as a result of axial strain discontinuities across tissue.

In this local description, D accounts for the difference between the actual local displacement at depth z and the displacement that would be obtained in a hypothetical loaded configuration where the same strain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\epsilon }$$\end{document}ϵ^ is applied uniformly throughout the entire sample. Therefore, D > 0 means that the actual loaded position is shifted towards larger z compared with this hypothetical uniformly strained loaded configuration, whereas D < 0 means that it is shifted towards smaller z (see Supplementary Note 3 for further details).

Conventional models estimate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\epsilon }$$\end{document}ϵ^ from experimental data by performing a linear fit to Δϕ(z), using methods such as weighted least squares (WLS)5,14,18, or by applying finite difference methods to complex-valued optical coherence tomography signals to obtain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {\phi }^{{\prime} }(z)$$\end{document}Δϕ′(z), such as the vector method (VM)6,19,20. Recently, a deep learning-based model was demonstrated to reconstruct the unwrapped Δϕ from its wrapped version21,22, in order to improve fitting-based strain retrieval. In the literature, equation (1) is frequently assumed to be a mathematical identity, meaning that differences between d(z), calculated using equation (1), and the actual sample displacement are usually attributed to noise or artifacts of the unwrapping algorithm3,23.

Makita et al.24 conducted a statistical analysis of Δϕ under the assumption of random scatterer displacement, demonstrating how it varies with noise. In this context, equation (1) was described as an approximation, and a quantitative error analysis was performed. In a deterministic displacement of scatterers approach, which is the assumption we make in this article, Hepburn et al.25 explained that the assumption that equation (1) is a mathematical identity is valid in some cases, such as for specular reflections. The same paper, however, also shows that in low-intensity regions known as dark speckles, where the intensity of the OCT signal is low, estimations of displacement based on the PHDE are less accurate than in regions of high OCT signal intensity, called bright speckles. However, a comprehensive and complete explanation of this inaccuracy has, to the best of our knowledge, not yet been provided. Furthermore, previous works have not proposed a method to recover accurate displacement or strain. An understanding of this is important as it reveals why conventional models of strain retrieval can be inaccurate when applied in turbid tissue, which generally results in speckled OCT images. In contrast, our study introduces a displacement-estimation method specifically designed to address this limitation, distinguishing it from prior approaches.

In this article, we derive an equation that shows why Δϕ(z) is, in general, a non-linear function of z in regions of constant strain, in contradiction with the prevailing understanding. We do this by using a one-dimensional (1D) model of OCT image formation 9 to derive an equation connecting OCT signals under different amounts of constant strain. We then use this equation to derive an analytic approximation for Δϕ which includes terms additional to equation (1). We then use this analytical approximation to show that Δϕ exhibits intrinsic oscillations in both dark and bright speckles.

An important result of this paper is an explanation of two perturbations of Δϕ, which occur in the vicinity of dark speckles, that we will call oscillation peaks and 2π-jumps. Both of these perturbations reduce the accuracy of conventional OCE models based on the PHDE. We will also show that 2π-jumps are often misinterpreted as phase-wrapping artifacts, which misdirect research towards improving phase-unwrapping algorithms, rather than developing models that account for these 2π-jumps. We then analyze, qualitatively and quantitatively, the accuracy of conventional strain-retrieval methods employed in OCE. In particular, we show that the true relationship between d(z) and Δϕ includes non-linear terms neglected by equation (1). Furthermore, we show that these non-linear terms are non-negligible in the case of large sample displacements (commonly occurring in deep regions of the sample) or large strain discontinuities across the sample. In these cases, the accuracy of conventional models in OCE is significantly reduced. Finally, we introduce a displacement-estimation method for OCE that addresses the limitations of existing approaches and improves the accuracy of strain retrieval in OCE. This method achieves an error two orders of magnitude lower than traditional techniques and demonstrates a markedly higher strain SNR, making it a robust solution for overcoming the challenges posed by noise and inaccuracies in conventional OCE methods.

Generality of oscillation peaks and 2π-jumps in speckled OCT signals

The purpose of this section is to show that the phase-instability mechanism described above is not restricted to quasi-static compression OCE, but can arise broadly in phase-sensitive OCT approaches based on speckled complex OCT signals. Let AU(z) and AL(z) denote two ideal complex OCT signals used to compute a phase difference in a given modality, such as compression OCE, Doppler OCT, phase-sensitive dynamic OCT, or related approaches in which motion is inferred from phase changes. In a realistic measurement, however, the second complex OCT signal will generally be affected by small perturbations, for example due to stochastic scatterer motion, noise, or other changes occurring between repeated acquisitions. This demonstrates the broad relevance of the mechanism described in the previous section: whenever a speckled OCT signal contains dark speckles, the local complex trajectory can pass close to the origin of the complex plane, where the phase is extremely sensitive to small perturbations. Therefore, these non-linear phase events are not specific to quasi-static compression OCE, but can arise in phase-sensitive OCT modalities in which phase differences are computed from perturbed speckled complex OCT signals. It is important to clarify that, whilst the noise and vibration perturbation analysis performed in this section concerns the broader phase-instability mechanism associated with perturbed speckled complex OCT signals, the series expansion in Eq. (6), the phase-difference approximation in Eq. Discussion on phase unwrapping in OCE

In this section, we discuss how the PHDE assumption may be impeding research into strain retrieval in OCE by diverting unwarranted attention toward phase unwrapping. This is mainly due to the incorrect assumption that the underlying unwrapped phase is inherently smooth and that only noise makes phase unwrapping unstable. The presence of high oscillation peaks, 2π-jumps, and the non-linear terms of Δϕ, which are all present when the phase is correctly unwrapped, have been considered artifacts of the phase-unwrapping algorithms. This has reinforced the hypothesis that phase unwrapping is one of the main problems facing strain retrieval in OCE. This hypothesis has motivated researchers in the last decade to develop new noise-robust phase-unwrapping algorithms14,21,22 or to manipulate Δϕ to avoid phase unwrapping6,19. These approaches have resulted in phase-unwrapping algorithms that tend to smooth Δϕ in both the axial and lateral directions. This smoothing process has the effect of diminishing the non-linear terms present in equation (10), thus locally linearizing Δϕ so that it is better represented by equation (1). As a result, strains can be retrieved more accurately by applying equation (1) to the smoothed Δϕ, compared to applying equation (1) to the correctly unwrapped phase obtained using equation (8). This is because equation (8) causes Δϕ to be highly non-linear, as has been explained.

This overemphasis on developing algorithms aimed at better smoothing Δϕ has diverted attention from the fundamental limitations of the PHDE assumption, which we analyze in the next section. To advance OCE strain retrieval, research should prioritize developing algorithms that are more general and make less restrictive assumptions than the PHDE.

The special case \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D+\hat{\epsilon }z=0$$\end{document}D+ϵ^z=0 also corresponds to zero mean displacement of scatterers randomly drawn from a uniform distribution within a symmetric interval centered at z, as shown in Supplementary Note 4. This implies that, around z, the unloaded and loaded signals are expected to remain more similar than in cases with larger local mean displacement, which explains why VM and WLS can show higher accuracy in this regime.

Furthermore, in case of large \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| \hat{\epsilon }|$$\end{document}∣ϵ^∣, ∣D∣, z or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| D+\hat{\epsilon }z|$$\end{document}∣D+ϵ^z∣, the low-peaks class does not exhibit a significant reduction in error compared to the high-peaks or 2π-jump classes. This underscores that 2π-jumps and high-oscillation peaks notably degrade the accuracy of retrieved strains for small displacements, such as small uniform axial strains in shallow regions. In contrast, in scenarios with large displacements or for deep axial regions or large strain discontinuities, the error in both WLS and VM methods is primarily influenced by the non-linear terms of Δϕ, which are disregarded by conventional methods.

It can be noted that the errors associated with the VM and WLS methods are similar, despite the significant differences between the two methods. This similarity is not surprising, as these errors are primarily due to the shared PHDE assumption. Therefore, to further improve strain-retrieval methods in OCE, it is necessary to take into account the non-linear terms of Δϕ.

It is important to note that the comparison we made between the two methods is contingent upon the parameters we set for the moving averages and the phase unwrapping. Moreover, this comparison is not exhaustive as it is conducted on ideal, noise-free OCE signals generated using a simplified 1D model of OCT image formation, which neglects some phenomena that could influence strain prediction, such as multiple scattering and signal attenuation. Furthermore, there are additional parameters that warrant consideration when comparing the two OCE models, including image resolution and potential artifacts introduced by the OCE models, such as streak-like artifacts observed in certain WLS images18.

At a high level, the method operates directly on the complex OCT signals reconstructed from the measured spectral data, and compares locally deformed versions of the unloaded and loaded A-scans over an axial fitting window.

Computational time and scope of the proposed method

The proposed method is computationally more intensive than conventional OCE methods, mainly because it requires repeated fitting of the local deformation parameters within a three-stage coarse-to-fine search. For the implementation used in this work, the computational times required to estimate strain on the inclusion-phantom dataset were 02: 56: 51 for the proposed method, 00: 01: 37 for WLS (phase unwrapping included), and 00: 00: 03 for VM, where the times are reported in hh:mm:ss format, corresponding to hours, minutes, and seconds, respectively.

The raw 3D OCT spectral datasets processed for this timing comparison contained 2048 wavelength samples and 2000 × 1000 lateral positions in the x and y directions, respectively. Thus, each dataset contained 2000 × 1000 A-scans, each sampled over 2048 spectral points. For visualization only, the images shown in the figures were cropped around the inclusion, displaying a region of 800 × 624 lateral pixels in the x and y directions, respectively. These results show that the implementation used in this work is considerably slower than the WLS and VM methods, but they also demonstrate that modeling the OCT signals beyond the conventional phase-difference assumption enables more accurate and robust strain estimation.

The main computational bottleneck of the current implementation is the repeated evaluation of the fitting criterion over the trial-parameter grid. Although the current implementation already includes a local linearized correction to improve the accuracy of the tested candidate parameters, this step is not intended to reduce the computational time. The reported runtimes correspond to the current vectorized MATLAB implementations, run on a workstation equipped with two AMD EPYC™ 7453 28-core CPUs. At this stage, we did not investigate a dedicated GPU implementation for any of the methods, since the purpose of this work was to validate the robustness of the proposed formulation rather than to optimize execution time through hardware-specific acceleration. Further acceleration will require both GPU implementation and a semi-analytical and/or approximate treatment of the fitting step, which will be investigated in future work with the aim of considerably reducing the runtime.

Discussion

Many implementations of label-free and functional modalities such as Doppler OCT and OCE make use of phase-sensitive OCT.

In this paper, we used strain and bulk displacement retrieval in quasi-static compression OCE to show that the well-known phase-difference equation, which has been widely considered to be a mathematical identity, perturbs the measurement of motion using phase-sensitive OCT data. In particular, we have shown that for constant strain, phase difference can differ significantly from the linear function of depth predicted by the phase-difference equation, so that the accuracy and sensitivity of phase-based strain-retrieval models is reduced. We demonstrated two intrinsic types of oscillation in Δϕ for both bright and dark speckles. We also described the condition under which, in the vicinity of dark speckles, rapid phase changes of 2π may occur, which may be erroneously interpreted as noise or a phase-wrapping artifact. This erroneous interpretation has likely diverted research efforts to focus on developing smoothing-oriented phase-unwrapping algorithms, whose accuracy and robustness are, however, constrained by the limitations of the PHDE assumption.

These non-linear terms of Δϕ also contribute to the degradation of strain estimation quality, especially for large displacements, deep axial regions, or large strain discontinuities. To address this, we derived a more accurate approximation of Δϕ involving the most significant non-linear terms in equation (10). This enhanced understanding of phase difference can be useful in developing more accurate strain-retrieval models. Building on this enhanced understanding, we developed a method for displacement estimation in OCE that does not rely on equation (1) but instead uses a truncated version of equation (6). Our results demonstrate that this method of strain retrieval is more accurate than existing methods.

We believe that this approach will serve as a foundation for a new generation of more accurate and robust displacement-estimation techniques in OCE-type strain estimation, and that the phase-instability analysis performed in this work enhances understanding of the phase of OCT signals more broadly.

We anticipate that this paper will therefore aid in the further development of a range of modalities based on the phase of OCT signals.

The parameter W was then chosen using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W=\pi B/\sqrt{\log (1/\tau )}$$\end{document}W=πB/log(1/τ), where τ is the prescribed relative Gaussian level at the interval edges. The scatterers \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${z}_{j}^{U}$$\end{document}zjU were placed randomly along the z-axis according to a uniform distribution, with a minimum density corresponding to 11 scatterers per coherence length of the OCT signal. For each scatterer, the scattering cross-section parameter ρj was also drawn from a uniform distribution on (0, 1). This choice is consistent with the analysis of Hillman et al.27, where OCT speckle formation is related to the effective number of scatterers contributing within the probed region, with strong deviations from fully developed speckle occurring when this number is below about five. In our one-dimensional OCT model, this corresponds to considering the number of randomly distributed scatterers within one coherence-gated axial interval. We numerically verified that approximately the same lower bound is sufficient to generate realistic OCT speckle signals. Therefore, by using 11 scatterers per coherence-gated axial interval as the minimum value, we ensure that the simulated signals are generated in a well-developed speckle regime.

OCE signal simulation

We generated NU = 160,801 unloaded OCT A-scans using equation (4a) with random scatterer axial positions, zj, and random scattering cross-section parameters, ρj, in an axial range of [0,750]μm. We then simulated the loaded OCT signals using equation (4b). We considered Nϵ = 20 different strains, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\epsilon }$$\end{document}ϵ^, equally spaced within the interval [−3, 3]mϵ, and ND = 9 bulk displacements, D, equally spaced within the interval [−1.5,1.5]μm, resulting in 180 different AL for each AU. Then, for each pair \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\hat{\epsilon },D)$$\end{document}(ϵ^,D) the complex phase difference \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B:={A}^{U}\overline{{A}^{L}}$$\end{document}B:=AUAL¯ was calculated. To analyze the accuracy of the WLS and VM methods in retrieving strain, also as a function of depth z, we selected Nz = 9 axial intervals of width 130 μm from each B. The sampling rate for the OCT signals was dz = 3.6 μm, and the axial fitting range employed for both the WLS and VM methods was 100 μm. With these parameter choices, 9 values of axial strain were retrieved for each processed OCE signal. The shallowest axial interval was centered on z = 60 μm, with subsequent interval centers separated by 65 μm. The deepest axial interval was centered at z = 586 μm. Consequently, for each initial AU, Nϵ × ND × Nz = 1620 OCE A-scan intervals were simulated, for a total of NU × 1620 = 260.5 × 106 OCE A-scan intervals processed by both methods.

Quantifying the error in strain retrieved by the WLS and VM methods, due to the non-linear terms of Δϕ, would ideally involve testing these methods with OCE signals containing only one of: 2π-jumps, oscillation peaks, or bright speckle. However, these three phenomena are interconnected, and furthermore, WLS and VM retrieve pixel strains by utilizing OCE signals from surrounding pixels within a given axial fitting range (typically 100 μm), where the presence of two or three of these phenomena is highly probable. Because of that, simulating OCE signals containing low oscillations in Δϕ across an entire fitting range is challenging.

Due to the challenges described above, we categorized the simulated sections of OCE signals into three classes: (1) containing at least one 2π-jump, (2) without 2π-jumps but with a maximum oscillation peak higher than 0.65 × 2π, and (3) without 2π-jumps but with a maximum oscillation peak lower than 0.55 × 2π. The remaining generated OCE intervals were not used as they were too close to the edge of two classes and were not useful for this analysis. The numbers 0.55 and 0.65 were chosen heuristically to ensure that enough OCE signals for each class were generated; however, their choice did not have a significant impact on the results and did not change the conclusions that we made (see Supplementary Note 5 for further details).

Experimental OCE system

The experimental OCE data were acquired using a fiber-based spectral-domain OCT system (Telesto 220; Thorlabs Inc., USA) in a common-path compression configuration, with a central wavelength of 1300 nm, a 3-dB bandwidth of 170 nm, and measured axial and lateral resolutions of 4.8 μm and 7.2 μm, respectively18.

OCE inclusion-phantom construction

The inclusion is a stiff cube (Young’s modulus of 295 kPa) with a side length of 500 μm. The center of the cube is positioned approximately 500 μm below the surface of a softer bulk cylinder (Young’s modulus of 20 kPa) with a thickness of 3 mm and a diameter of 6 mm. Titanium dioxide particles with a concentration of 2 mg/mL and 0.5 mg/mL were mixed with the silicone of the inclusion and bulk, respectively, to enable optical contrast. Each elastogram is centered on the inclusion, spanning 1600 μm in the x direction and 1250 μm in the y direction. It can be observed that both the VM and WLS elastograms are generally noisier than the elastogram produced by the proposed method. The white streak at the lower part of the inclusion in the WLS elastogram is an artifact resulting from the WLS phase-unwrapping algorithm18.
