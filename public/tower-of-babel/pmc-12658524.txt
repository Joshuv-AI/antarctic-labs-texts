Psychosocial adjustment in patients with diabetic retinopathy in northeastern Iran: a qualitative study based on the transactional stress theory
Authors: Azam-Sadat Salehnezhad, Fatemeh Sedghi, Mehrsadat Mahdizadeh, Jamshid Jamali, Naser Shoeibi, Mohammad Vahedian-Shahroodi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the psychosocial adaptation processes and coping methods among patients with diabetic retinopathy (DR), emphasising the importance of understanding their lived experiences.DesignA qualitative study using semi-structured interviews.SettingA tertiary eye hospital in northeastern Iran.ParticipantsThe participants comprised patients with DR, their families and healthcare professionals.MethodsQualitative data were analysed based on transactional stress theory using deductive thematic analysis to identify psychosocial adaptation concepts.Results65% of participating patients had DR for more than 5 years. Analysis of 49 patient and 14 healthcare professional interviews revealed 6 key themes encompassing 15 categories and 33 subcategories: primary appraisal (threat perception, motivational assessment, self-blame), secondary appraisal (perceived control over disease outcomes, perceived control over emotions, self-efficacy), coping efforts (problem management, emotional regulation), meaning-based coping (positive reassessment, reinforcement of religious beliefs, constructive approach), moderators (seeking information, all-encompassing assistance) and adaptation (emotional health, individual-social functioning).ConclusionsThe analysis revealed that deficits in disease awareness, financial and systemic barriers and emotional distress significantly impeded adaptive coping. Conversely, self-efficacy, social and organisational support, and meaning-based coping strategies were key facilitators. Our findings suggest that facilitating positive psychosocial adjustment in patients with DR may require interventions that address these specific barriers and leverage these facilitators, such as providing comprehensive disease knowledge and constructing robust support systems. This study highlights the potential value of a holistic care approach that integrates medical treatment, targeted patient education and psychological support to improve the overall quality of life for these patients.

Introduction

The WHO identifies diabetes as the eighth leading cause of death and disability, a complex global health issue and a hidden epidemic.1 The International Diabetes Federation predicts that the worldwide diabetes population will rise from 135 million in 1995 to 300 million by 2025 and 642 million by 2040. In the Middle East and North Africa, this number is expected to reach 95 million by 2030 and 136 million by 2045, marking an 87% increase.2 Studies from 1996 to 2023 estimate that 10.8% of Iranians over 25 have diabetes.3 Diabetic retinopathy (DR), nephropathy and neuropathy are among the serious complications of this disease.4 People with diabetes are 25 times more likely to develop eye disease than non-diabetics, and DR is the leading cause of blindness among working adults, affecting about one in three individuals with diabetes.2 5 6

Patients with DR face significant stress and challenges due to the disease’s chronic nature. These stressors can affect their health and well-being, leading to negative emotions and diminished coping abilities if not effectively managed.7 Under these circumstances, adaptation methods are crucial. Adapting to a chronic illness is a dynamic process shaped by individual and environmental factors.8 The individual must overcome personal and environmental challenges to achieve satisfactory health and overall functioning, ultimately leading to successful adaptation.9

Psychosocial adjustment is crucial in chronic diseases as it directly influences self-care behaviours and is their best predictor.10 11 Lazarus and Folkman’s transactional model of stress management explains how stressors trigger physical, psychological and behavioural responses. An individual’s reaction to stress is influenced by their assessment of coping abilities, stress management approaches and the situation’s relevance to their health. The model views all stress experiences, including chronic illnesses, as interactions between individuals and their environments. The fundamental concept is that primary and secondary appraisals, along with coping styles, determine how people perceive and respond to stressors, helping them select appropriate coping strategies.12 13 Sengul and Toruner conducted a randomised clinical trial that investigated the use of training based on the transactional model to improve the psychosocial adjustment of parents and children dealing with cancer. The results showed a significant positive effect, including reduced stress levels for both parents and children, along with enhanced coping skills and overall quality of life.14 Similarly, Bougea et al applied the transactional stress model to study how middle-aged parents adjust to empty nest syndrome, finding that the model effectively promoted adaptation and transformed families into dynamic units.15 Additionally, Desrichard et al explored perceived stress, vulnerability and adaptation through the transactional stress model, discovering a direct link between feelings of ageing and vulnerability and the experience of perceived stress and related symptoms.16

DR patients not only struggle with treatment challenges but also endure significant psychological stress. This stress arises from inadequate disease awareness, high treatment costs, limited access to information and support services, impaired vision and diminished personal and social functioning, ultimately resulting in a lower quality of life and long-term social isolation.7 17 18

While extensive research exists on DR patients in Iran and globally, limited evidence addresses how these individuals perceive stressful situations and their coping mechanisms. It is important to note that stress and its associated factors are deeply rooted in individual beliefs, with each person experiencing and managing it differently, leading to varying coping strategies. Enhancing the awareness of primary care specialists and medical staff about evaluating stress in this disease and understanding patients’ coping styles from their mental perspective is crucial for developing effective management plans. This study aims to explore the psychosocial adaptation processes and coping methods by delving deeper into the lived experiences of those affected by this condition.

Methods

Study design

Our research was grounded in descriptive-content analysis, structured as a sequential exploratory design. Drawing on the constructs of Lazarus and Folkman’s transactional stress theory, we aimed to identify the key factors influencing stress adaptation in patients with DR.

Setting and participant

The study was conducted at Khatam Al-Anbiya Eye Hospital, a tertiary referral centre in northeastern Iran, from 2023 to 2024. To ensure maximum diversity among participants and gather richer insights, a purposeful sampling method was employed. Two target groups were identified: the primary group, consisting of individuals facing the challenges related to the disease or patient care (patients with DR and their families), and the secondary group, which included nurses, doctors and clinic staff.

Participant recruitment

Participant recruitment was conducted in coordination with the hospital’s Health Information Unit to identify eligible patients with confirmed DR. Following phone screening and explanation of the study, interviews were scheduled concurrently with participants’ prearranged clinical visits to minimise burden. Informed consent was obtained prior to all interviews, which were conducted in a private room to ensure confidentiality. Although interviews averaged 45 min, duration was flexibly extended to multiple sessions, when necessary, always contingent on participant preference and availability. This approach ensured ethical rigour and facilitated efficient data collection within a clinical setting.

To address the potential influence of pre-existing or concurrent significant psychological distress on the assessment of DR-specific psychosocial adaptation, a proactive screening protocol was implemented. The research team included three clinical psychologists (PhD level) who assisted in evaluating prospective participants during the recruitment phase. Individuals exhibiting signs of major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders or other significant psychological comorbidities, as well as those who had recently experienced major psychological crises (eg, recent bereavement, divorce), were excluded from the interview cohort. This approach aimed to mitigate the risk of these acute or severe psychological states confounding the reported experiences related to adapting to DR. In cases where further clarification was needed, supplemental information was obtained from family members or caregivers to inform eligibility decisions.

Recruitment of family members and healthcare professionals

Family members, primarily spouses and children who were most involved in the patient’s daily life and care, were purposively sampled during the patient’s hospital visit to provide rich, contextual insights into psychosocial challenges. Healthcare professionals were recruited from the same tertiary eye hospital based on their direct and extensive clinical engagement with DR patients. The sample included ophthalmologists, clinical and educational supervisors, insurance and accounting specialists, head nurses, clinic administrators and social workers, ensuring a comprehensive professional perspective on adaptation processes, systemic barriers and support structures.

Data collection

To gather comprehensive data reflecting participants’ perspectives and experiences, we designed a semistructured interview guide (online supplemental file 1, table S1) aligned with the study objectives and the constructs of Lazarus and Folkman’s transactional stress theory. The guide included open-ended and probing questions.

Prior to the main data collection, five pilot interviews were conducted to refine the question structure and ensure clarity; these data were excluded from the final analysis. Participant recruitment was coordinated with the hospital’s nursing staff and Health Information Unit. Eligible patients with confirmed DR were identified from medical records and contacted by phone. 58 out of 65 contacted individuals agreed to participate and were scheduled for interviews concurrent with their planned clinical visits to minimise burden. All interviews were conducted in Persian by a native-speaking researcher (A-SS, female, PhD researcher skilled in qualitative methods) in a private room within the clinic to ensure confidentiality and comfort. Each interview lasted 30–45 min, was audio-recorded and continued until thematic saturation was achieved—confirmed by three subsequent interviews yielding no new codes. The audio recordings were transcribed verbatim into Persian by the interviewer. To ensure conceptual accuracy and semantic equivalence for analysis and reporting, the Persian transcripts were professionally translated into English by a translator fluent in both languages and proficient in medical and psychosocial terminology. The translated English transcripts were then cross-checked against the original Persian texts by two bilingual members of the research team (FS and MV-S), both experienced in qualitative research. Any discrepancies in meaning or nuance were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached, thereby ensuring the trustworthiness and integrity of the data for thematic analysis. In total, 63 interviews were conducted, including 49 with patients, 8 with family members and 6 with healthcare professionals. Data analysis

Data collection and analysis proceeded concurrently in an iterative manner. A deductive thematic analysis approach was employed, guided by the predefined constructs of Lazarus and Folkman’s Transactional Stress Theory, which served as the initial coding framework. To ensure analytical rigour, three researchers (MM (PhD, Mixed Methodologist, woman), NS (MD, Ophthalmologist, man) and MV-S (PhD, Project manager, man)) independently read and coded the same initial transcript to establish a shared understanding and consensus on the application of the theoretical codes. The diversity of the research team in terms of gender and professional background (health education, clinical ophthalmology and methodology) was leveraged as a resource to enrich data interpretation and enhance methodological reflexivity throughout the analysis process.

The team maintained reflective memos and a research diary to document analytical decisions and the evolution of the interview guide. Following this calibration, the researchers collaboratively coded three transcripts to develop a consolidated list of central codes. Subsequent transcripts were coded individually. The research team then engaged in regular discussions to compare coding, interpret findings and resolve any discrepancies through consensus. A structured classification matrix, aligned with the theoretical framework, was used to organise the data into predefined categories. Codes were grouped into subcategories and main categories based on conceptual similarities and logical relationships. This process continued until thematic saturation was achieved, defined as the point where no new conceptually relevant themes or categories were identified in subsequent interviews. All data management and analysis were supported by MAXQDA software (version 10).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Participant characteristics

Transcripts from interviews with 49 patients were analysed. Among the participants, 60% were female and 40% male, with an average of 5 children. The majority (62%) were aged between 51 and 70. Of these patients, 65% were living with type 1 diabetes, and 35% had type 2 diabetes. Remarkably, 86% had been managing diabetes for over a decade, and 65% had been diagnosed with DR for more than 5 years. The mean number of children per participant was five. Additionally, 14 interviews were conducted with key informants, including spouses, children, ophthalmologists, educational and clinical supervisors, clinic managers, social workers, head nurses and insurance specialists. Identified themes

During the analysis phase, 1228 initial codes were identified. These were organised into 15 main categories, which were further divided into 33 subcategories based on the 6 core themes of the transactional stress theory (see online supplemental file 3, table S3).

Primary appraisal

The way patients perceive the threats associated with this disease and the impact of this perception on their emotions and behaviours were concepts discussed in this theme.

Patients’ initial perception of the disease emerged as a critical theme, often characterised by a lack of awareness or underestimation of its severity. For instance, many participants reported having no prior knowledge of DR before experiencing serious symptoms. One participant noted, “I didn’t know there was a problem; I realized it when my eye started to bleed” (P1). Others expressed disbelief that diabetes could lead to blindness, with some attributing clinical warnings to exaggeration, as captured in the statement: “I thought doctors were just trying to scare me” (P7). Motivational assessment was frequently driven by the fear of vision loss and its consequences. Participants emphasised their willingness to adhere to self-care protocols to prevent further deterioration. For example, P19 stated, “I’m willing to do whatever I can to prevent my vision from getting worse,” while P45 highlighted the urgency: “If I lose this other eye, I will be blind. I have to take very good care of it.”

Self-blame and guilt were common among those who experienced significant vision loss, as well as their family members. Participants often reflected on past negligence, with P13 questioning, “What mistake had I made that I did not take the nurse’s advice seriously the first time? Family members echoed this sentiment; the spouse of P17 expressed regret: “I wish I had forced my husband to follow up when the doctor warned him.”

Secondary appraisal

In this theme, the internal and environmental challenges that impact the thoughts and actions of these patients were identified and categorised under perceived control over disease outcomes, perceived control over emotions and self-efficacy. Some participants cited insufficient access to financial resources and difficulties accessing healthcare facilities as significant challenges in pursuing treatment.

Financial and systemic barriers significantly impacted patients’ perceived control over disease outcomes. Participants cited high treatment costs, inadequate insurance coverage and logistical challenges such as long waiting times and crowded facilities as major obstacles. P11 explained, “I do not have a good financial status … That is why I have not seen a doctor in ten years.” A social worker corroborated this, noting that lack of insurance support often leads to poor follow-up. Additionally, the child of P22 described the emotional toll of logistical barriers: “My mother gets upset and distressed every time we bring her because getting an appointment with the doctor is very difficult.”

Emotional regulation challenges were pervasive, with many patients reporting anxiety, depression and intrusive thoughts related to their condition. P9 shared, “I feel like I’m no longer a normal person. I'm very sad about my situation,” while P45 admitted, “I cry alone… I'm very scared of going blind.”

Self-efficacy, however, was bolstered by beliefs in self-care capabilities and drawing motivation from personal or external examples. P16 affirmed, “I’m still young enough that I don’t want to be bedridden. Thank God I still have very good vision,” and P18 engaged in self-encouragement: I tell myself, ‘Well done, you followed the nurse’s instructions very well this week. I'll go buy myself a gift.’”

Coping effort

This theme highlights the practical and psychological responses of patients when confronting their illness. The main categories identified were problem management and emotional regulation. Within problem management, subcategories included medication adherence and proactive measures to halt disease progression.

Problem management strategies ranged from non-adherence to proactive engagement. Some participants acknowledged poor medication adherence, with P3 admitting, “I forgot to take my pills… it didn’t matter to me.” In contrast, others demonstrated consistent follow-up, as exemplified by P5: “I regularly come to the hospital to see my eye doctor on time.”

Emotional regulation often involved maladaptive strategies such as denial, avoidance or opposition to medical advice. One participant stated, “I don’t go to the doctor; I don’t give injections either,” while a family member described their father’s resistance: “He doesn’t accept that he is sick at all, and we have constant challenges with him.”

Meaning-based coping

This theme highlights the processes patients undergo to manage the psychological and emotional challenges posed by their disease. Coping strategies often involve seeking meaning and purpose, which can empower patients to feel more in control of their condition. By revising personal values and goals, strengthening social connections and accepting the reality of their illness, patients may enhance their overall quality of life. Within the broader theme of coping efforts, key categories include positive reappraisal, religious beliefs and positive experiences. Subcategories of positive reappraisal focus on future-oriented thinking and reevaluating personal goals.

Participants often employed positive reappraisal, religious beliefs and constructive approaches to reframe their experiences. An educational supervisor emphasised, “Disability is not the end point… they should try and turn their weaknesses into strengths.” Religious beliefs provided comfort for some; one patient expressed, “I believe that God gives things to man and takes things away… one should be grateful.” Others, like P49, adopted a pragmatic perspective: “We must accept the disease as a friend… you accept it.” Efforts to maintain social connections and avoid isolation were also noted, with P8 planning regular walks with family to prevent worsening of their situation.

Moderators

Social support, disease awareness and management literacy emerged as critical moderators influencing stress intensity. These factors were pivotal in mitigating psychological burden and enhancing overall quality of life.

Participants highlighted variable access to information, ranging from limited guidance from healthcare providers to proactive self-education through digital platforms. One participant noted, “Every time I visited the health center, they provided me with minimal information about my condition” (P32), while others like P40 turned to independent research: “I did a lot of research using Google and mobile resources.”

Social and organisational support systems were identified as essential yet inconsistently available. While some patients reported strong familial networks—for example, P11 stated, “Thank God, I have a good family and friends who take care of me”—others highlighted systemic gaps, noting a lack of tailored support for DR patients. One participant emphasised, “We are not supported as specific patients,” underscoring that “many factors trace back to the health care system.”

Adaptation

Successful adaptation was characterised by the effective management of disease-related stress through the evaluation of personal circumstances and utilisation of available coping resources. This process was fundamental to enhancing quality of life and reducing psychological complications. Two core dimensions of adaptation were identified: emotional well-being and individual-social functioning.

Emotional well-being was often significantly impacted, with many patients experiencing social withdrawal and a loss of prior enthusiasm for life. This is vividly captured in a family member’s account: “My mother has become more withdrawn. She used to be much happier and more energetic… we now have to persuade her to leave the house” (Child of P4).

Furthermore, adaptation was closely linked to functioning in social and individual roles. The deterioration of vision frequently necessitated profound life adjustments, including withdrawal from professional duties. As one participant starkly stated, “I completely gave up my job after my vision deteriorated” (P25). This loss of vocational identity and social participation was a significant challenge to overall psychosocial adjustment.

Discussion

DR imposes a substantial psychosocial burden that extends beyond its physiological manifestations, significantly impacting patients’ quality of life and self-management capabilities. While extensive quantitative research has documented the prevalence of psychological comorbidities in this population, a critical gap remains in understanding the underlying, nuanced processes of how individuals cognitively and emotionally appraise their condition and subsequently employ coping strategies. This study addressed this gap by utilising Lazarus and Folkman’s Transactional Model of Stress and Coping as a robust theoretical framework to qualitatively explore the lived experiences of patients with DR in northeastern Iran. Our findings provide a deep, contextualised understanding of the psychosocial adaptation journey, moving beyond symptom identification to elucidate the dynamic interplay between appraisal, coping efforts and moderating factors. This granular perspective is essential for developing targeted, theory-driven interventions that address the root cognitive and emotional challenges faced by this patient population.

The spectrum of disease duration among participants, ranging from newly diagnosed to over a decade, revealed an evolution in psychosocial challenges. While the core processes of appraisal and coping, as outlined by the Transactional Model, remained consistent, their manifestations intensified and transformed over time. Individuals with long-standing DR frequently grappled with profound grief, self-blame and significant disruptions to social and vocational roles. In contrast, those recently diagnosed were often navigating initial shock, threat appraisal and urgent information-seeking. This variation, captured by our maximum variation sampling, underscores that psychosocial adaptation to DR is not a static event but a dynamic process, thereby enhancing the transferability of our model across different stages of the illness.

Our analysis, grounded in the Transactional Model, reveals that a limited understanding of the nature of DR, its potential complications and treatment protocols significantly hinders successful adaptation. Participants described initial periods of ignorance or underestimation of the disease’s severity, which often led to delayed care and subsequent irreversible vision loss. This lack of awareness frequently resulted in profound psychological consequences, including guilt and self-blame among patients and their families. These findings underscore the critical importance of patient sensitisation and timely screening programmes. This challenge is not unique to our setting; a report from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention indicates that eye care is addressed in fewer than 40% of initial consultations for diabetes patients.19 Our results corroborate quantitative studies, such as the work by Thykjær et al, which demonstrates a clear link between delayed referrals and DR progression.20 Similarly, Chong et al emphasise the necessity of educating patients about DR risks and proper screening procedures.5 Our qualitative data provide the ‘why’ behind these quantitative findings, highlighting the cognitive appraisals that lead to delay.

A second major barrier identified was the financial and systemic burden of treatment. Participants highlighted the prohibitive cost of care, insufficient insurance support and logistical challenges such as prolonged waiting times and difficulty securing appointments as significant obstacles to consistent disease management. This finding aligns with international research. For instance, studies by Bulathwatta et al and Alamri et al explicitly link financial constraints, poor access to specialised care and inadequate insurance to poorer adaptation outcomes in chronic conditions like DR and chronic kidney disease.11 21 Our study adds depth to this understanding by illustrating how these systemic failures directly impact patients’ secondary appraisal, diminishing their perceived control over disease outcomes and eroding their capacity to cope effectively.

Furthermore, our data highlight the profound impact of DR on emotional regulation. Factors such as the fear of blindness, anxiety and depression were pervasive barriers to psychological well-being. This resonates strongly with existing literature. Gouveia et al established a link between self-perceived anxiety from vision problems and the psychosocial well-being of patients with retinal degeneration.22 Similarly, Li et al) revealed a bidirectional relationship between stress in diabetic patients, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis hyperactivity and the onset of depression.23 The works of Sun et al, Liu et al, Liu et al and Zheng et al further corroborate the strong connection between stress, depression and diabetes.12 24–26 Our study contextualises these correlations within the transactional model, framing them as outcomes of a threatening primary appraisal and maladaptive coping efforts. While research by Bloom et al and Ju and Wang confirms that many DR patients experience underlying fear or anxiety, they often lack access to adequate support.27 28 Consequently, our findings strengthen the argument for integrating psychological interventions into standard treatment plans to enhance acceptance and adaptation.

Conversely, our study also identified positive facilitators of adaptation. Belief in one’s ability to engage in self-care bolstered by feelings of personal achievement, learning from successful role models and positive reinforcement, was a key component of enhancing self-efficacy. This finding is supported by studies showing that self-efficacy in diabetic patients is linked to better treatment adherence and lower anxiety levels.29 The positive impact of social support on self-efficacy is also well-documented.30 31 For example, Wufuer et al demonstrated that education on self-care behaviours increases patient awareness, satisfaction and emotional regulation.32 Furthermore, Kao et al found that adherence to physician recommendations positively affects medication compliance, a notion echoed by the experiences of our participants.33 Research by Madit et al and Al Baiuomy et al also emphasises that proactive behaviours like visiting healthcare centres and seeking information are crucial for controlling disease progression.34 35 Our qualitative results thus provide a rich, process-oriented view of how self-efficacy is built and maintained in the context of DR.

The analysis of coping efforts revealed that many patients initially view their illness as a threat rather than a challenge, leading to the adoption of ineffective strategies such as denial, avoidance and opposition to medical advice. This maladaptive coping aligns with the work of Nosrati et al and Ghafarzadeh et al, who found that such behaviours reduce quality of life and impede treatment success.36 37 Our study delineates these behaviours within the ‘coping efforts’ theme of the transactional model, offering a structured framework for understanding these responses.

Participants cited healthcare professionals, acquaintances and digital media as their primary information sources. This underscores the vital role of clinicians not only as caregivers but also as trusted guides who can direct patients to accurate information and mitigate misconceptions. This need is highlighted in the study by Alhazmi et al on eye care knowledge and misconceptions.38 Parrey et al also identified the internet as a critical platform for seeking eye health information.39 Therefore, empowering healthcare professionals to provide clear, actionable guidance is essential for supporting the adaptation process.

Finally, successful adaptation was closely tied to maintaining emotional well-being and individual-social functioning. The deterioration of vision often led to social withdrawal, loss of vocation and strained family relationships, placing significant strain on patients and their support systems. This finding is consistent with international evidence. Bulathwatta et al and Padamandala et al reported a negative correlation between chronic illness and a patient’s role within family and community settings.11 40 Sturrock et al also described how individuals with vision loss from DR struggle with social isolation and workplace challenges.41 Therefore, support from family, community and the healthcare system is indispensable in helping patients build self-esteem and foster greater independence.

Study limitations and methodological reflections

Several methodological aspects should be considered when interpreting our findings. While the single-centre design at a tertiary hospital in northeastern Iran enabled in-depth contextual inquiry, it may affect the direct transferability of results to primary care settings or other cultural contexts. Furthermore, although clinical screening was employed to exclude major psychological comorbidities, the absence of standardised psychometric tools means that undiagnosed subclinical depression or anxiety could have influenced participants’ reported appraisal and coping processes. Our sampling strategy, while aiming for maximum variation, was necessarily limited to patients engaged with the healthcare system, potentially overlooking the unique adaptation challenges faced by those disconnected from care. Finally, the cross-sectional interview design captures a snapshot of the adaptation journey; longitudinal studies are needed to delineate how these psychosocial processes evolve in response to disease progression and life events.

Recommendations for good practices and future research

Future research should build on this study to address its limitations and translate findings into practice. Key priorities include:

Longitudinal and comparative designs: Employ longitudinal studies to temporally isolate DR-specific adaptation from the general diabetes burden. Directly compare experiences with other chronic conditions (eg, age-related macular degeneration, glaucoma) to identify condition-specific mechanisms. Multicentre studies are needed to enhance generalisability.

Integrated metrics: Incorporate standardised quantitative tools (eg, PHQ-9, GAD-7) within mixed-methods designs to quantify the mental health burden and enable benchmarking against other populations.

Theory-based interventions: Develop and evaluate interventions targeting the core themes identified: improving disease literacy, building self-efficacy, integrating psychological care, such as cognitive behavioural therapy, into ophthalmologic settings, and mobilising family and systemic support.

Focus on underserved groups: Investigate barriers and adaptation processes in populations underrepresented in clinical samples, including those disconnected from care or with severe psychiatric comorbidities.

Conclusions

The findings of this qualitative study provide a deep, contextualised understanding of the psychosocial adaptation processes in patients with DR, as viewed through the lens of the transactional model of stress and coping. While the results are not statistically generalisable, they offer theoretically transferable insights and a robust conceptual framework that illuminates the complex interplay between cognitive appraisal, coping efforts and moderating factors in this population. This nuanced understanding underscores the critical need for interventions that address both the medical and psychological needs of patients with DR. It highlights the importance of comprehensive support systems, including structured patient education and access to mental health resources, to foster positive coping strategies and enhance self-efficacy. Enhancing healthcare professionals’ awareness of these psychosocial dynamics is crucial for developing more empathetic and effective, patient-centred management plans. Ultimately, a holistic care approach that integrates medical treatment with theory-driven psychological and systemic support is essential for improving the overall quality of life for individuals living with this challenging condition. Future research should focus on developing and testing interventions based on these insights, particularly through mixed-methods or quantitative designs that can build on this qualitative foundation to measure efficacy and broader applicability.
