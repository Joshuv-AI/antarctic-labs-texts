The role of microRNAs on autophagy in cervical cancer: review
Authors: Mulu Shiferw Asfaw, Woldeteklehaymanot Kassahun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer (CC) is one of the top five most common cancers among women worldwide. A key cellular alteration in CC is the dysregulation of autophagy a vital process for maintaining cell homeostasis by degrading and recycling damaged components via lysosomes. Disrupted autophagy is associated with various stages of cancer development. This review explores the role of microRNAs (miRNAs) in regulating autophagy in cervical cancer. miRNAs influence autophagy by targeting specific genes or signaling pathways, acting either as promoters or suppressors of the process. Many microRNAs (miRNAs) involved in autophagy are dysregulated in cervical cancer. For example, miR-203, miR-34a, miR-23b, miR-100, and miR-145, known to activate autophagic proteins in various diseases, are found to be suppressed in cervical cancer. This highlights the significant potential of miRNAs in regulating autophagy, making them valuable as diagnostic and prognostic markers, as well as therapeutic targets.Keywords: Autophagy, Cervical cancer, miRNA

Introduction

According to WHO’s 2022 estimation, cervical cancer is among the top five cancers affecting women, with 660,000 new cases and nearly 350,000 deaths globally [1]. Over 99% of cases are primarily caused by human papillomavirus (HPV) [2].

Human papillomaviruses (HPVs) are double-stranded, non-enveloped DNA viruses with genomes about 8 kb in size. Over 200 types have been identified and are classified as high-risk (hr-HPV) or low-risk (lr-HPV) based on clinical outcomes. High-risk types such as HPV16 and HPV18 are linked to precancerous lesions and cancer. In contrast, low-risk types like HPV6 and HPV11 mainly cause benign conditions such as anogenital warts, which rarely become malignant [3–5].

Autophagy is a vital cellular process that maintains homeostasis by using lysosomes to degrade and recycle damaged biomolecules or organelles. When disrupted, autophagy is associated with various diseases, including cancer, neurodegenerative, metabolic, and infectious disorders [6, 7]. In cancer, autophagy seems to have a more complex role that varies based on the biology, environment, and stage of the tumor [8].

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression by inhibiting post-transcriptional translation. They play a crucial role in key biological processes, including cell division, proliferation, metabolism, apoptosis, and autophagy [9, 10].

Many studies have investigated the role of miRNAs in autophagy [11–13]. The biogenesis of miRNAs is tightly controlled, and their dysregulation can lead to diseases like cardiovascular disorders, neurological conditions, and cancer [14, 15]. Altered function and expression levels of various miRNAs in CC have been widely reported; for instance, Causin R.L and colleagues have identified over 25 miRNAs, with 19 being downregulated and 7 upregulated [16].

Approximately 100 miRNAs are known to regulate different stages of the autophagy pathway, such as induction, nucleation, elongation, retrieval, and fusion. Many of these miRN0As target multiple pathways and act as autophagy receptors [17, 18]. This review focuses on key miRNAs involved in autophagy regulation in cervical cancer.

General principles of the autophagy process and its deregulation in cervical cancer

De Duve first coined the term “autophagy” to describe the process by which cel0ls degrade proteins through the fusion of lysosomes and protein-containing vesicles [19]. In extreme conditions like starvation, cells use this process to break down their own components to maintain essential functions [20, 21].

Depending on the lysosomal fusion mechanism, autophagy is classified as macro-autophagy, chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), or micro-autophagy. Mostly, autophagy and macro-autophagy are used interchangeably [22, 23]. The lysosome receives cytoplasmic cargo in macro-autophagy via the autophagosome, a double membrane-bound vesicle that merges with the lysosome to form an autolysosome. On the other hand, in micro-autophagy, the lysosome absorbs cytosolic components directly by invaginating the lysosomal membrane. Engulfing big structures can be achieved by both non-selective and selective mechanisms in macro- and micro-autophagy. The process of chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) involves the translocation of specific proteins across the lysosomal membrane in a complex with chaperone proteins, like Hsc-70. The lysosomal membrane receptor, lysosomal-associated membrane protein 2 A (LAMP-2 A), recognizes these chaperone proteins, which causes the proteins to unfold and degrade [23, 24]. The autophagic process proceeds sequentially through induction, vesicle nucleation, elongation, autophagosome maturation, and lysosome fusion, followed by degradation, recycling, and autophagic lysosome reformation [11] (Fig. 1).

Stages of the autophagy process

Autophagy is triggered under diverse cellular stress conditions, such as nutrient deprivation (e.g., lack of amino acids or glucose) [25]. Once initiated, the ULK1 complex is recruited to the phagophore assembly site (PAS), where it activates the class III PI3K complex (PIK3C3 complex), leading to the production of phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate (PtdIns3P). This lipid acts as a docking platform for PtdIns3P-binding proteins like WIPI2. In cooperation with the ATG12–ATG5–ATG16L1 complex and the ubiquitin-like conjugation machinery (ATG12 and LC3 systems), WIPI2 facilitates LC3 lipidation (LC3 conjugation to phosphatidylethanolamine, PE), a crucial step for phagophore elongation and autophagosome maturation [26].

Subsequently, mature autophagosomes undergo fusion with lysosomes, a process coordinated by tethering factors such as the HOPS complex and SNARE proteins. This fusion event allows lysosomal hydrolases and lipases to degrade and recycle the captured cytoplasmic material. At the molecular level, the ULK1 complex (composed of ULK1, RB1CC1/FIP200, ATG13, and ATG101) is constitutively present, but becomes activated through autophosphorylation at Thr180 and phosphorylation of its subunits during stress. Multiple mechanisms contribute to its recruitment to PAS, including interactions with GABARAP family proteins, small GTPase RAB1A with its effector C9orf72, and ER-resident VAP proteins. Downstream, the PI3KC3-C1 complex, comprising PIK3C3/VPS34, PIK3R4/VPS15, Beclin 1, and ATG14, generates PtdIns3P, with ULK1-mediated phosphorylation enhancing its lipid kinase activity. Notably, recruitment of both ULK1 and PI3KC3-C1 complexes depends on GABARAP interactions [27].

Downstream, the class III PI3K complex I (PI3KC3-C1)—comprising VPS34/PIK3C3, VPS15/PIK3R4, Beclin 1 (BECN1), and ATG14 is activated by ULK1-dependent phosphorylation. This complex generates phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate (PtdIns3P), which recruits PtdIns3P-binding effectors such as WIPI2 to PAS. WIPI2, in concert with the ubiquitin-like conjugation systems (ATG12–ATG5–ATG16L1 and LC3/MAP1LC3 conjugation), promotes LC3 lipidation by attaching LC3 to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE). This step is crucial for elongation and closure of the phagophore into a mature autophagosome [27, 28].

The final maturation step involves fusion of the autophagosome with lysosomes, mediated by the HOPS tethering complex and SNARE proteins. This fusion delivers the autophagic vesicle contents to lysosomal hydrolases and lipases for degradation and recycling. Through this tightly regulated cascade, cells adapt to metabolic stress and maintain homeostasis [29].

Autophagy plays a dual role in cancer, acting to inhibit early tumor development while also helping cancer cells survive stressful conditions and promote tumor progression at later stages [11]. High-risk HPV oncoproteins E5, E6, and E7 play a central role in modulating autophagy during cervical carcinogenesis by interfering with key regulators of the process. HPV E5 has been shown to suppress autophagy by downregulating autophagy-related genes (e.g., Becn1, ATG5, LC3, ULK1, ATG4a, ATG7), inhibiting LC3 lipidation, and disrupting autophagosome assembly, while also reducing degradation of autophagy substrates such as SQSTM1/p62. E6 and E7 further inhibit autophagy through p53-dependent regulation of UBC9, a SUMOylation enzyme normally degraded by autophagy, leading to impaired autophagosome–lysosome fusion and reduced autophagic flux [30]. These proteins also increase ATAD3A expression, which blocks autophagy and contributes to drug resistance. Notably, silencing of E6/E7 restores autophagy-related gene expression and LC3B lipidation, supporting the role of these oncoproteins in suppressing autophagy. However, some studies report context-dependent effects, with E6/E7 able to induce autophagy in specific cell lines, suggesting that their influence on autophagy may vary depending on the cellular environment and transformation stage [30].

Role of microRNA in autophagy

MicroRNA (miRNA) biogenesis is a multi-step pathway that begins in the nucleus and ends with the production of functional mature miRNAs in the cytoplasm. This process is of great interest because of its broad regulatory roles and potential therapeutic applications. The key steps include: transcription of primary miRNAs (pri-miRNAs) by RNA polymerase II, cleavage of these transcripts by the Microprocessor complex (DROSHA and DGCR8) to release hairpin-shaped precursor miRNAs (pre-miRNAs), export of pre-miRNAs to the cytoplasm via Exportin 5 (XPO5), and further processing by DICER with its cofactor TRBP to form a double-stranded RNA duplex. The duplex is then unwound, with the guide strand incorporated into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), enabling sequence-specific binding to target mRNAs. Through this, miRNAs either repress translation or promote degradation of their targets. Each stage of miRNA maturation is tightly regulated, and disruption of this process is implicated in multiple diseases, including cancer, neurodegeneration, and cardiovascular disorders [31, 32].

The regulation of autophagy by microRNA reported by different scholars, creates a new image for the scientific world. Various investigations have demonstrated that a number of miRNAs tightly regulate the genes of nearly all proteins involved in autophagosome development, maturation, and autophagy-related signaling machinery (such as the AMPK, AKT, and mTOR pathways). It’s even possible for many miRNAs to target the same autophagy gene with different reactions to stress [33]. For instance, miRNA-145 [34], miRNA-375 [35], miRNA-125a [36], miRNA-21-3p [37], and miRNA-3473b [38] have been found to target genes for mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), a main autophagy regulator. Other mTOR complex components, including mLST8, RAPTOR, Tti/Tel2, DEPTOR, RICTOR, PRAS40, and mSIN1, as well as mTOR regulators RHEB and TSC1/2, are regulated by distinct sets of miRNAs [11].

Roles of miRNAs on autophagy in cervical cancer

miRNAs’ regulatory role in autophagy and tumor growth is becoming more understood, as is their potential for tumor therapy and diagnosis. Currently, there is a large body of literatures describing the critical role of miRNAs in modulating autophagy process in cervical cancer which have a diagnostic and therapeutic implication. For instance, Shang Liu and his colleagues revealed that the forced expression of miR-211 in SiHa cells induces autophagy, as manifested by the presence of LC3 puncta, increased LC3-II/I and Beclin1 levels, and decreased p62 levels. In addition, Bcl-2 was identified as a target of miR-211 [39]. Another study on the function of miR-875-5p found that, in Caski cells, transfection of the miR-875-5p mimic increased the levels of LC3B II protein and decreased ATG5 and LC3B I levels, whereas transfection of the inhibitor decreased LC3B II levels but did not affect ATG5 and LC3B I levels, demonstrating that miR-875-5p promotes autophagy in cervical cancer [40].

The role of miR-224-3p in the regulation of FIP200(a protein essential for autophagosome formation via its interaction with ULK) expression in vivo was examined by Wang Fang and his colleagues. It was studied how much FIP200 was present in human cervical cancer cell lines (C33A, SiHa, and HeLa cells) following treatment with miR-224-3p mimics (miR-224 m) and miR-224-3p inhibitors (miR-224i). While miR-224-3p inhibition enhanced FIP200 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels, miR-224-3p mimic dramatically decreased FIP200 expression in all three cell lines. When combined, these findings imply that miR-224-3p can directly influence the amount of autophagic activity in CC cells by controlling the expression of FIP200 [5].

The increased expression of miR-143 may enhance apoptosis triggered by Cisplatin and raise CaSki cells’ susceptibility to low concentrations of this chemotherapeutic drug by influencing the expression of genes linked to apoptosis, such as caspase-9, Bax, and Bcl-2 [41]. Additionally, it has been shown that miRNA-143 and Cisplatin work together to promote cell cycle arrest at the sub-G1 and G2-M stages, activate autophagy, and prevent CaSki cell migration by downregulating vimentin. Another study reported that transfection of miR-204 mimics to cancer cells leads to the reduces the expression of autophagy-related protein LC3I/II [42].

A study by Tan et al. revealed that miR-378 directly targeted autophagy-associated protein 12 (ATG12), hence lowering its expression. Tissues from cervical carcinoma with lymph node metastases had higher levels of miR-378, however the expression of ATG12 was downregulated when compared to lymph node-negative patients. The discovery that miR-378 targets ATG12 suggested that miR-378 may have a role in autophagy [43].

Another study that investigated how miR-19-3p affects autophagy used fluorescence microscopy to observe HeLa and SiHa cells. The researchers divided the cells into three groups: a mimic group, a mimic-normal control group, and an inhibitor-normal control group. Cells treated with the mimic or its normal control predominantly displayed green fluorescence (GFP-LC3) with only minimal red fluorescence (mRFP-LC3), reflecting low autophagy activity. In contrast, cells in the inhibitor group showed intense red fluorescence (mRFP-LC3), indicating a marked increase in autophagy, characterized by the buildup of acidic autophagic lysosomal vacuoles and leading to early cell death. The levels of miR-19-3p were markedly elevated in cervical cancer tissues as well as in SiHa and HeLa cells, while PTEN expression was markedly diminished. PTEN was identified as a direct target of miR-19-3p. Introducing a miR-19-3p mimic decreased apoptosis and autophagy and enhanced proliferation and invasive behavior in SiHa and HeLa cells [44].

Reduced autophagy activity may accelerate the development of cervical cancer and increase treatment resistance. According to Xiujie Cui and colleagues, immunoblotting of CaSki cells revealed that p62 expression was downregulated and LC3-II and Beclin1 accumulation were increased when miR-106a expression was inhibited. The elevated level of LC3-II indicated the presence of active autophagy. Rapamycin- or starvation-induced LC3-II protein expression was decreased by miR-106a mimics. These findings imply that miR-106a may inhibit the production of autophagosomes in cervical cancer [45].

In Caski cells, the transfection of miR-875-5p mimic raised the levels of the autophagic protein LC3B II and lowered those of ATG5 and LC3B I. In contrast, the transfection of an inhibitor reduced the levels of LC3B II but had no effect on ATG5 or LC3B I. On the other hand, miR-875-5p transfection reduced the amount of LC3B II in SiHa cells but had no effect on ATG5 or LC3B I level. According to Yingxiu Liang and colleagues, the transfection of miR-875-5p inhibitors didn’t affect the levels of ATG5, LC3B I, and LC3B II [46].

The varying behavior of miRNAs across cell lines is attributed to the complex, context-dependent role of autophagy, which changes based on the specific biology, environment, and transformation stage of the tumor. Additionally, the influence of HPV oncoproteins on autophagy regulation fluctuates depending on the cellular environment, leading to divergent effects in different cell types. Finally, identical miRNAs can target the same autophagy gene yet elicit different biological reactions due to the distinct stress conditions present within each cell line [8, 43].

It has been demonstrated that microRNA-375, which is suppressed in cervical cancer, inhibits autophagy in hepatocellular carcinoma by focusing on the ATG protein. Another study on thyroid papillary carcinoma found that miR-203 stimulates autophagy by targeting one of the proteins involved in vesicle nucleation, Beclin-1, whereas the expression of this miRNA is reduced in cervical cancer. According to Kunmei Liu et al., mycobacteria treated with miR-106a mimics had fewer autophagosomes and autolysosomes when examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), while cervical cancer patients had higher expression of this miRNA [47] (Table 1).

The studies included in this review follows in-vitro experimentation using cervical cell lines majority of them use Hela, SiHa, Caski, C33A, and the normal human cervical epithelial cell (patient tissue sample) [48–51].

Targeting microRNAs that regulate autophagy and cervical cancer development offers a novel strategy for both diagnosis and treatment. Reduced autophagy activity may accelerate the development of cervical cancer and increase treatment resistance. Certain miRNAs, such as miR-100, miR-145, miR-34a, and miR-203, have been shown to have a considerable therapeutic promise for managing cervical cancer by suppressing the growth of the cancer cells by targeting autophagy. By modifying the autophagy pathway, the use of miRNA can also improve the therapeutic effects of cervical cancer chemotherapies. Concurrently, research has shown that co-administering miRNA-143 with cisplatin can increase cervical cancer cells’ susceptibility [91, 92].

Clinical implications of microRNA’s role on autophagy in cervical cancer

Cervical cancer remains a major global health burden but can be effectively controlled through screening and early detection strategies. Conventional diagnostic methods for precancerous lesions, including Pap smear, colposcopy, and biopsy, are widely applied; however, they are often invasive and limited by low sensitivity and specificity. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that dysregulated miRNA expression is linked to cancer initiation and progression, suggesting that miRNA profiles may serve as valuable biomarkers for malignancy. Furthermore, viral oncogenes E6 and E7 have been shown to modulate specific cellular miRNAs. While most investigations of miRNAs in oncology rely on tissue specimens, these approaches are inherently invasive. Owing to their high stability and ease of detection compared to mRNAs, circulating miRNAs provide promising, minimally invasive candidates for cancer diagnostics [93–95].

Recent findings indicate that high-risk HPV (hrHPV) infection in cervical lesions and cancer cell lines enhances the expression of miRNAs involved in autophagy regulation. Under normal physiological conditions, autophagy is tightly regulated and occurs at relatively low levels [96]. Mapping alterations in autophagy-associated miRNA expression in hrHPV-driven cervical cancer may therefore provide clinically relevant biomarkers or therapeutic targets. Differential expression studies have reported significant upregulation of autophagy-related miRNAs, including miR-200a, miR-27a, miR-21, miR-106a, miR-25, miR-10a, miR-185, and miR-92b, in the serum of cervical cancer patients compared with healthy individuals, whereas miR-100, miR-375, miR-23b, miR-145, miR-424, miR-193b, and miR-497 are frequently downregulated (Table 1).

Autophagy-regulating miRNAs have emerged as promising therapeutic agents in cervical cancer by modulating key autophagic machinery and intersecting oncogenic signaling to influence tumor survival, therapy response, and metastatic potential [11, 97]. These miRNAs act either as tumor suppressors by promoting cytotoxic autophagy or inhibiting pro-survival autophagy pathways or as oncogenic drivers that blunt autophagic cell death and sustain proliferation [98]. Mechanistically, they target central nodes such as ATG family proteins, Beclin-1, p62/SQSTM1, and upstream regulators including mTOR, PI3K/AKT and AMPK, thereby reprogramming cancer cell metabolism and apoptotic thresholds. Therapeutically, enhancing tumor-suppressive autophagy-promoting miRNAs with mimics or inhibiting oncogenic autophagy-suppressing miRNAs with antagomirs (chemically modified, synthetic nucleic acids designed to inhibit the function of a specific endogenous miRNA) can improve cervical cancer response to chemo- and radiotherapy, reduce resistance, and limit invasion [99]. Translational delivery strategies like lipid nanoparticles, viral vectors, and exosome-based systems enhance tumor targeting while minimizing systemic toxicity [100]. Despite challenges such as miRNA stability, off-target effects, and the context-dependent role of autophagy, the autophagy–miRNA axis remains a promising and multifaceted target for next-generation cervical cancer therapies [101].

Conclusion and the way forward

This review highlights the crucial molecular link between miRNA regulation, autophagy, and cervical cancer progression. Understanding these interactions is essential for developing effective therapies and identifying reliable disease biomarkers. Therefore, further research should focus on miRNA expression, regulation, their role in autophagy, and potential therapeutic applications.
