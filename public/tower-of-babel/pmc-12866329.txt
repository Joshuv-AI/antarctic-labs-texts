Modeling neighborhood association decay effects improves forest stock volume estimation using UAV lidar and optical data
Authors: Zilong Qin, Jinling Fang, Na Jiang, Ke Hou, Zongyao Sha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Forest stock volume (FSV) is an important indicator for assessing the carbon sequestration potential of forests and is influenced by neighborhood environmental factors. However, most studies have disregarded the spatial neighborhood association of the modeled variables and its decay effect with increasing spatial distance in estimating the FSV. We propose an FSV estimation method that considers the neighborhood association decay (NAD) effect, that is, NAD-based FSV modelling, and constructed a framework for expressing and quantifying the NAD effect, specifically including the design of NAD models, determination of the optimal neighborhood size, and optimization of the NAD strategy. Finally, we estimated the FSV of the dominant tree species using UAV LiDAR and optical remote sensing data from Mengyin County, China, and evaluated the estimated results using field sample data. The results suggest that the proposed NAD-based model can effectively improve the accuracy of FSV estimation for each tree species (R2 = 0.75 ~ 0.96) compared to the conventional pixel-based model. The analysis of the spatial distribution pattern of FSV in Mengyin County revealed high spatial heterogeneity of FSV (15.47-242.82 m3/ha), and a high potential for forest carbon sequestration was found with field surveys.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13021-025-00373-6.

Introduction

Forests are the largest carbon reservoirs in terrestrial ecosystems [13], store more than 80% of the carbon in global terrestrial vegetation [30]. Forest ecosystems are effective in mitigating climate change and the threat they pose to human survival by absorbing atmospheric carbon dioxide [13]. Forests are major terrestrial carbon sinks, and the capacity of the forest carbon sink is a determining factor in whether some regions can achieve carbon neutrality [39]. Therefore, it is necessary to monitor and analyze forest carbon stock accurately and in real-time. However, accurate spatial and temporal dynamics monitoring of large-scale forest carbon sequestration has proven to be challenging [7], owing to the high complexity of forest structure, topography, and land management practices, among others [32]. The development of remote sensing technology has improved the methods for estimating forest carbon stock. Indicators for quantifying the carbon stock of forests mainly include forest above-ground biomass (FAGB) and forest stock volume (FSV); however, field sampling of FAGB often causes irreversible damage to forests, including tree felling, cutting, drying, and weighing [42]. FSV is an important indicator for assessing the carbon sequestration and potential of forests, and its sampling process will not cause damage to forests; thus, FSV was selected as the estimation target in this study.

A variety of remotely sensed data are widely used to estimate forest carbon sinks, including optical remote sensing data, synthetic aperture radar (SAR), light detection and ranging (LiDAR), and the integration of multi-source remote sensing data [3]. LiDAR is independent of light conditions and can penetrate the forest canopy. SAR is more sensitive to terrain variations, and optical remote sensing is more dependent on the absence of clouds [27]. In addition, it has been shown that optical and SAR data are saturated when forest biomass exceeds 100 Mg/ha [18]. Therefore, LiDAR is a better tool that can be used to accurately describe forest structure and estimate forest carbon sequestration. Several studies have estimated regional forest carbon sequestration at different scales based on LiDAR data [5, 11, 17]. Recently, satellite-borne LiDAR has been developed, such as NASA’s Global Ecosystem Dynamics Investigation (GEDI) [23], which can be used to identify large-scale regions with high forest biomass [9]. However, only coarse spatial resolution data can be obtained using GEDI sensors, which do not allow for the identification of forest structure and accurate estimates of forest carbon sequestration [14]. In Comparison, multidimensional information on forests can be captured by LiDAR data retrieved from unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) platforms. Moreover, the spectral reflectance information of forests is highly correlated with carbon sequestration [37]. A better result can be obtained from the estimation of forest carbon sequestration based on multi-source remote sensing data, such as combined LiDAR, multispectral, hyperspectral, SAR, and other remote sensing data. Studies in several regions have confirmed this conclusion [35, 37].

According to the first law of geography, everything is related to each other, and the strength of this relationship decreases with increasing spatial distance [31]. Previous studies mostly used pixel-based methods to estimate the FSV and assumed that the pixel-to-pixel values were independent, without considering the effect of neighborhood characteristics of variables on estimating the FSV [25, 37]. FSV can be affected by modelling variables at its surrounding/neighborhood scale, i.e., the neighborhood association effect exists between FSV and neighborhood values of modelling variables [10, 41]. For example, the terrain in the neighborhoods of trees can affect soil, local climate, surface and groundwater distribution, and the amount of solar radiation received by the forest, thereby affecting its carbon sequestration by altering tree growth processes. Similarly, other variables within the neighborhood of trees can have an impact on their growth. Furthermore, the strength of the association between the neighborhood values of modeling variables and FSV decays with increasing neighborhood scale, that is, the neighborhood association decay (NAD) effect; however, the expression strategy of NAD is not clear. Therefore, it is necessary to construct and determine the optimal expression strategy for the NAD effect and estimate the forest FSV on this basis.

In this study, the FSV of Mengyin County was estimated using the proposed NAD-based model, that is, the FSV estimation model considering the spatial heterogeneity of the neighborhood association effect. Therefore, the aims of this study were (1) to develop an NAD-based model and to determine the optimal neighborhood size; (2) to find the optimal strategy of NAD and decay index for NAD-based FSV modelling based on the genetic algorithm; (3) to estimate the FSV of the study area using NAD-based FSV modelling under the optimal NAD strategy and decay index and to compare the estimation accuracy with that of pixel-based FSV modelling; and (4) to conduct a field survey of the study area to verify the validity of NAD-based FSV modelling, and to further analyze the spatial pattern of FSV in Mengyin County.

Materials and methods

Study area

Mengyin County in China was selected as the study area, as shown in Fig. 1. Mengyin County is located in eastern China (117°45′E-118°15′E, 35°27′N-36°02′N) with a total area of 1601.6 km². Ecological resources are abundant, including 129 species in 43 families of trees, 59 species in 16 families of shrubs, and 200 species in 20 families of herbaceous plants. Mengyin County has rich forest resources, covering 20% of the area covered by forests. Mengyin County is a demonstration area in Shandong Province for monitoring forest resources and carbon sinks. Therefore, the measurement and monitoring of forestry carbon sinks in this area is of practical value and can provide a case study for county carbon measurements.

Data sources and processing

Data sources

The data used included field survey data, LiDAR data, and multispectral remote sensing data, which obtained from Unmanned Aerial Vehicle. The field survey was conducted from September to November 2023 using random sampling, and 8,815 trees were measured. We designed 654 plots of 20 m × 20 m in the study area (Fig. 1c), containing 331 plots for Populus, 124 for pine, 120 for cypress, and 79 for acacia. The surveyors measured the height, diameter at breast height (DBH) greater than 5 cm at 1.3 m tree height, location with real time kinematic (RTK), and species of each tree in each grid, with a total of 8,815 trees measured, including 1,139 populus, 1,787 pines, 3,949 cypresses, and 1,940 acacias (Fig. 2). The FSV of each tree in the grid was obtained from the tree volume table of the National Forestry and Grassland Administration of China (https://www.forestry.gov.cn/) and the measured information of the trees, and the FSV of the grid was summed. The aggregated FSV grid samples were 654 in total, of which 70% were selected for modeling and the remaining 30% were used for validation using the ten-fold cross-validation method (Table 1).

LiDAR data and multispectral remote sensing data for Mengyin County were obtained using the UAV-mounted device CityMapper-2L, laser model Hyperion2. LiDAR and multispectral remote sensing data were obtained on May 2023. The UAV flew at an absolute altitude of 4,000 m during data collection, with a laser wavelength of 905 nm, a light velocity divergence angle of 0.03° (horizontal) × 0.28° (vertical), and an average point density of the point cloud better than 2 pts/m2. multispectral remote sensing data have four bands, that is, red (R), green (G), blue (B), and near-infrared (NIR) bands, with a spatial resolution of 0.5 m.

Data processing and feature extraction

The processing of LiDAR data was first to identify and remove the noise points (including isolated points and outlier points), and LiDAR data were coarsely classified into ground points and vegetation points. The point cloud was further reclassified into ground points and vegetation points using a modified progressive triangular irregular network densification algorithm, and a digital elevation model (DEM) and digital surface model (DSM) were generated from the ground points using kriging with a spatial resolution of 1 m [37]. Finally, the DEM was subtracted from the DSM to obtain the canopy height model, that is, the height of the vegetation canopy above ground. To eliminate the effect of low shrubs, points with vegetation less than 2 m in height were removed. Then, features that could reflect forest structure and radiometric information were extracted from the LiDAR data, including point cloud density, forest canopy density, forest height, and forest canopy structure. The DEM data generated from the LiDAR data were then used to extract the terrain features of the study area, including the elevation, slope, and aspect. Elevation, slope, and aspect can affect soil, climate, groundwater distribution, and the amount of solar radiation that forests receive and further affect forest growth and the accumulation of biomass and carbon sequestration [37]. The details of the forest and terrain features extracted from the LiDAR data are listed in Table S1.

The spectral information of forests reflects the absorption, reflection, and scattering of the solar spectrum by the forest canopy, which is an important indicator for assessing photosynthetic activity and dry matter accumulation [29]. Spectral information in different bands reflects the growth status of the forest. Therefore, vegetation indices obtained from spectral data can monitor forest growth. The multispectral remote sensing data were first processed for atmospheric correction and radiometric calibration and then clipped to obtain multispectral data for the study area. The vegetation indices of the study area were calculated, including the normalized vegetation index (NDVI), ratio vegetation index (RVI), enhanced vegetation index (EVI), enhanced vegetation index2 (EVI2), differential vegetation index (DVI), adjusted soil brightness vegetation index (SAVI), modified soil-adjusted vegetation index (MSAVI), and greenness vegetation index (GVI). Details of the spectral features are listed in Table S2. To match the scale of the field samples, LiDAR data and multispectral data were computed for 20 m × 20 m plots, which eventually generated variables with a spatial resolution of 20 m. A total of 55 variables were used to estimate the FSV, the details of which are listed in Table 2.

NAD-based FSV modelling

The NAD effect is pervasive among geographical entities. Similarly, the value of FSV is influenced not only by the environment at its specific location, but also by environmental factors within its neighborhood [10, 41]. Therefore, we propose an NAD-based method to estimate the FSV, which considers neighborhood characteristics extracted from the FSV modeling variables. NAD-based FSV modelling mainly includes the following parts: (1) We first constructed five NAD models to describe the spatial patterns of the NAD effect. (2) Next, we employed FSV modeling variables modified with NAD-based effects as inputs to the random forest model for estimating the FSV, and the optimal neighborhood size was determined. (3) A genetic algorithm was constructed to determine the optimal decay indices of each NAD model, and the optimal NAD model was determined. (4) Finally, the FSV of the study area was estimated based on the optimal NAD model and index, and its spatial distribution pattern was analyzed. A flowchart of the methodology is shown in Fig. 3.

Random forest model

Random forest (RF) model is a machine learning algorithm that integrates multiple regression and classification trees [28]. The RF model uses bootstrap sampling methods to randomly generate training samples and to construct multiple decision trees based on the training samples. The RF model has a good generalization performance and high tolerance to outliers and noise [28]. A previous study also suggested that the RF model outperformed other machine-learning models in estimating forest resources [40].

The recursive feature elimination (REF) method was adopted to optimize the variable sets for the RF model. The process of optimizing the variables with the REF method is as follows. The variables were first selected for training with the RF model, and the variables with the lowest importance given by the RF model were removed. The remaining variables were then inputted into the model, and the variables with the lowest importance were removed. The accuracy of the model predictions was assessed using a ten-fold cross-validation method. The above steps were repeated until all the variables were removed, and the optimal subset of variables was obtained based on the order in which the variables were removed, and the model accuracy was recorded in each experiment.

The NAD-based characteristics

To compute the metrics indicating the neighborhood characteristics for a specific location, we employed five NAD models that can capture the spatial context of FSV-related variables. Specifically, we determined the optimal decay index for each NAD model based on GA (genetic algorithm, refer to 2.3.4 for details) and then calculated the spatial weights using the five NAD models and modified the values of the central pixel with the weighted mean of the neighboring pixels of the variables. Finally, the variables modified with NAD-based characteristics were used as inputs to the RF model for estimating the FSV, and the optimal NAD model was determined by comparing the differences in accuracy in estimating the FSV with different NAD models. Additionally, the variation in elevation was representative of the minimum heterogeneity scale. The CV of elevation reflects the global characteristics of the terrain, as it varies with the neighborhood size, and the NV of elevation reflects the local characteristics of the terrain. Genetic algorithm

Determining the optimal NAD model and index is essentially a problem of searching for an optimal solution; therefore, a genetic algorithm (GA) is established to solve this problem. The GA is a method for searching for optimal solutions by simulating the natural evolutionary process. GA converts the problem-solving process into a process similar to the crossover and mutation of chromosome genes in biological evolution based on computer simulations. When solving complex combinatorial optimization problems, GA can obtain optimization results faster than conventional optimization algorithms [34].

The steps for constructing the GA include: (a) determining the initial population, which is randomly generated according to the population size and the range of the variable, and the variable in for this study is the NAD index; (b) determining the computational objective, and the objective is to improve the accuracy of estimating the FSV, so the root mean square error (RMSE) of the FSV estimation is selected as the optimization objective (refer to Sect. The NAD-based characteristics). The smaller the RMSE, the more suitable the individual is to survive; (c) selecting the excellent individuals in the population for recombination and mutation to obtain the offspring population, and we adopted the most popular tournament selection operator as the individual optimization strategy [6]; (d) calculating the target value of the offspring population; (e) merging the offspring population and the parent population, and selecting the individuals that are highly adapted to the next-generation population; (f) judging whether it meets the termination condition, if false, the population will continue to be optimized as the parent population, if true, the optimal individual in the population will be output. The termination condition reaches the maximum number of iterations or evolution stops.

In this study, the population size was set to 10, the recombination probability was set to 0.7, the distribution index was set to 20, the mutation probability was set to 1, the maximum number of evolutionary generations was set to 100, and the definition domain of the target variable was set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left[\mathrm{0,1}\right]$$\end{document} based on the accuracy of the FSV estimation. Determination of the optimal neighborhood size

Neighborhood size is an important factor affecting the accuracy of NAD-based FSV modelling; thus, the optimal neighborhood size must be determined before modeling. When the spatial distance between the neighborhood pixel and the center pixel is greater than a certain threshold, the neighborhood association between them is weak. The optimal neighborhood size was determined by calculating and analyzing the variation in CV and NV of elevation in the study area with neighborhood size (Eqs. (8) and (9)). In the experiment to determine the optimal neighborhood size, a 3 × 3 pixel neighborhood size was used as the initial value, with an increment of 2 pixels and a termination value of 201 × 201 pixels. All NAD models are based on the idea that as the spatial distance between pixels increases, the intensity of the neighborhood association decays. Thus, the inverse distance NAD model was used as a typical NAD model to determine the optimal neighborhood size. Spatial pattern of FSV in Mengyin County

We estimated the FSV of Populus, pine, cypress, and acacia in Mengyin County based on the optimal NAD index and NAD-based FSV modelling of each tree species and integrated the estimation results of each tree species to obtain the spatial distribution of FSV, as shown in Fig. 10. The sets of independent variables used to estimate FSV for each tree species are shown in Fig. S2. The spatial distribution of FSV in Mengyin County varies greatly (Fig. 10a), with FSV values ranging from 15.47 to 242.82 m3/ha (Table 4), and high FSV values are concentrated in the southern region of the study area. Mengyin County had higher FSV values for Populus and Pine and lower FSV values for cypress and acacia (Table 4).

Moreover, we found through field surveys that most of the forests were young (80%, Fig. 10a), resulting in overall low FSV values in the study area. It has been shown that the carbon stock capacity of forests is weak when they are young, strongest when they are young to middle-aged, and basically unchanged or declining when they are old [24]. The results of the field survey on the age structure of forests in Mengyin County suggest that they have a large potential for carbon sequestration.

Discussion

Sensitivity analysis of neighborhood size

To analyze the sensitivity of NAD-based FSV modelling to the neighborhood size, we calculated the optimal NAD model and the optimal NAD index for each tree species under neighborhood sizes of 21 × 21 to 33 × 33 pixels based on the GA (Fig. S3). We then further analyzed the estimation accuracies under different neighborhood sizes of the variables, and the results are shown in Fig. 11. The results show that the NAD-based model can effectively reduce the error of FSV estimation compared with a method that does not consider the neighborhood characteristics of variables (i.e., pixel-based model). When the neighborhood size is larger than the optimal neighborhood size, the FSV estimation accuracy of each tree species remains stable, indicating that the determined optimal neighborhood size is appropriate. The spatial distance between the neighborhood pixel and the center pixel increases with an increase in neighborhood size, and the association effect between them becomes weaker [31]. Therefore, the effect of neighborhood association on the accuracy of the FSV estimation is very weak when the neighborhood size is greater than a certain threshold. However, a large neighborhood size will lead to an excessive increase in computational effort and reduce computational efficiency.

As an important factor in spatial relationship modeling, most previous studies on neighborhood characteristics are based on smaller neighborhood sizes, such as 3 × 3 and 5 × 5 pixel neighborhood sizes of windows, which fail to adequately and accurately describe the neighborhood features of the pixels [8]. Small neighborhoods (e.g., 3 × 3 to 9 × 9 pixels) primarily capture fine-scale, local interactions. At this scale, processes such as direct tree-to-tree competition for solar radiation, water, and nutrients, as well as micro-site heterogeneity caused by single-tree fall gaps or individual boulders, are the dominant influences on forest structure and growth [2]. In contrast, medium-sized neighborhoods (e.g., 11 × 11, 13 × 13 pixels) align with stand-level dynamics. A forest stand, often defined as a group of trees sufficiently uniform in species composition, age, and structure to be distinguishable from adjacent areas, typically operates at this spatial extent. This scale integrates processes such as the overall stand structure, soil resource patches that are uniform within a stand, the impact of small-scale disturbances. Finally, large neighborhoods encompass landscape-scale drivers [21]. The optimal neighborhood size of 27 × 27 pixels indicates that the spatial variation of FSV in Mengyin County is predominantly governed by broad-scale environmental drivers. At this scale, the model effectively integrates the effects of topography, micro-climatic patterns, and hydrologic flow accumulation, which control the redistribution of water and nutrients across the landscape. The results of this study also confirmed that a small neighborhood size is not optimal neighborhood size for FSV modeling of Mengyin County. In mountainous terrain like the study area, topography reallocates key resources such as solar radiation (via aspect and slope), temperature, and soil moisture at scales of hundreds of meters. These factors create local habitat conditions that profoundly influence tree growth. A 27 × 27 pixels window is capable of encompassing complete topographic units (e.g., a small catchment or a hillslope), thereby integrating information on these crucial hydro-thermal conditions that determine forest productivity. Moreover, the optimal NAD models and indices for each tree species based on the GA model are different for various neighborhood sizes (Fig. S3), which also affected the computational efficiency and accuracy of the model. Therefore, it is important to determine the appropriate neighborhood size when using NAD-based FSV modelling.

The effect of NAD on estimating FSV

The NAD effect is commonly found in various geographic processes and phenomena and is an important factor to consider in the modeling of spatial relationships. NAD effects, founded on the first law of geography, reflect the distribution pattern of the intensity of spatial associations among geographic entities, and can be effectively used to model and predict the occurrence of geographic phenomena [31]. All geographic entities can be affected by other factors within their neighborhoods. In particular, the growth process and FSV of forests are affected by the surrounding environment, including terrain, soil, and water [10, 41]. For example, soil moisture within a tree’s neighborhood can also affect its growth and FSV. Therefore, it is necessary to consider the neighborhood characteristics of the modeling variables to estimate the FSV. Additionally, the neighborhood association effect between pixels decays with increasing spatial distance, and different expressions of this NAD effect may yield different FSV estimation results. The results of this study also confirmed that different expressions of NAD can affect the estimation accuracy of the FSV (Table 2; Fig. 9).

The finding that different tree species correspond to distinct optimal decay functions (Fig. 7; Table 3) can be grounded in their divergent ecological strategies and distribution patterns. The optimal exponential decay model of Populus suggests a growth dynamic that is intensely sensitive to the immediate neighborhood but retains a non-negligible influence over longer distances. This aligns with the light-demanding nature of pioneers in Shandong province, where competition for light and resources from direct neighbors is fierce. In contrast, the optimal Gaussian decay model for Cypress implies a more gradual and symmetric decay of neighborhood influence. This pattern may be characteristic of shade-tolerant species that persist in the understory or form dense, stable stands. The bi-square decay model, optimal for Pine and Acacia, is defined by a sharp cutoff distance beyond which the neighborhood influence drops to zero. This distinct boundary might mirror well-defined ecological niches or physical constraints on these species. For instance, it could represent the finite root competition zone of a pine stand or the edge of a discrete soil patch (e.g., a rocky outcrop) that Acacia specifically occupies. This model suggests that the factors limiting growth for these species operate within a specific spatial local-region. The above results summarize key aspects of a species growth and its interaction with the environment, transforming our spatial model from a purely statistical tool into a potential proxy for inferring functional ecological traits.

The modeling variables considering neighborhood characteristics are believed to be less affected by noise, and the values of the variables are more stable [19]. Although multi-source remote sensing data have been processed through a series of data corrections before it is used for modeling, some outliers may still exist in the processed data [20]. Considering the NAD effect of modelling variables can effectively reduce the error caused by outliers and can capture the values of the modelling variables more stably. We will consider using the NAD-based model for modelling and predicting other ecological indicators to further test the applicability of the NAD-based method.

Assessment of the efficiency of the NAD-based model

In this study, we estimated the FSV of dominant tree species in Mengyin County using the proposed NAD-based model and various neighborhood decay models. In the computational process, a larger neighborhood scale (27 × 27 pixels) and genetic algorithm were employed for iterative calculations, with the genetic algorithm requiring up to 100 iterations. The multispectral and LiDAR data used in this study, along with the generated variables, have a spatial resolution of 20 m. Each dataset comprises 3,967,585 computational pixels. The large number of iterations, computational units, and large-scale neighborhoods result in significant computational overhead. To reduce model execution time and keep it within acceptable limits, parallel computing based on graphics processing units (GPU) was employed to enhance computational efficiency.

We ran the proposed model on a computer configured with an Intel (R) Core (TM) i5-7500 3.40 GHz CPU, NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1660 GPU, 16GB of memory, and Windows 10. All models were implemented using the Python programming language. The time overhead of the NAD-based model is shown in Table 5. The results indicate that the runtime for estimating FSV across different tree species using various decay models and indices generally takes less than two hours, representing an acceptable time expenditure. This computational efficiency is feasible for forest monitoring and carbon sequestration estimation across larger areas or multiple time-series, and further improvements in computational efficiency can be achieved through methods such as code vectorization and upgrading computer equipment. Therefore, the model proposed in this study holds potential for application in carbon monitoring of large-scale, long-term forest or other ecosystems. We will also further optimize the model and program to enhance its accuracy and computational efficiency.

Advantages and limitations

In this study, we developed an NAD-based FSV modelling for different tree species, determined the optimal NAD index and the optimal model with the GA, and achieved better results. Previous studies only regarded the pixels as independent elements in estimating the FSV, and failed to fully consider the neighborhood environmental characteristics of the pixels [17, 37]. Some studies on neighborhood modeling (e.g., land use change) focused more on the fixed neighborhood association effect, that is, the strength of spatial association between the neighborhood pixels and the central pixel was considered to be the same, ignoring the NAD effect among pixels [16], which is contrary to the first law of geography [31]. Spatial modeling accuracy can be effectively improved by considering neighborhood characteristics and the NAD effect [33]. Although we constructed a model for estimating the FSV and achieved a high accuracy, there are still some limitations.

In terms of field sample collection, due to the high complexity of the terrain in Mengyin County and the fact that acacia usually grows in places that are not easily accessible to surveyors, there are fewer acacia samples, which affects the accuracy of FSV modeling for acacia. Sample imbalance has a certain impact on the estimation accuracy of FSV. In the future, a series of methods will be adopted to mitigate this impact, such as maintaining balanced samples of different tree species during field sampling. However, field sampling typically requires significant labor and financial resources. Therefore, we will increase field measurements and sample collection, establishing a database of actual forest samples from the study area. This will integrate results from multiple field sampling rounds, providing accurate, quantitatively balanced samples for modeling purposes. Additionally, we will further enhance the estimation accuracy of FSV by optimizing the proposed model, thereby mitigating the impact of sample imbalance on estimation accuracy.

In FSV modeling, we considered the spatial NAD effect between pixels; in fact, the pixels are associated with each other in the spatio-temporal neighborhood as well [33]. Further consideration and an accurate description of the spatio-temporal NAD effects among pixels may improve the accuracy and validity of the models. In addition, we used GA to determine the optimal NAD index, which was still time-consuming, although better results were obtained. With the development of various optimization algorithms (e.g., Firefly Algorithm [15], Cuckoo Search Optimization [38], Artificial Bee Colony Optimization [1], and deep learning models, different models can be considered to find the optimal NAD index and describe the NAD effect. Furthermore, Object-Based Image Analysis (OBIA) method is widely applied to extract stand features and perform forest canopy segmentation and extraction [36]. OBIA aggregates pixels into discrete, homogeneous objects (e.g., forest stands), which is highly effective for reducing within-segment noise and extracting stand-level characteristics [26]. In the future research, we envision strong potential for integrating the NAD concept with OBIA, which could further enhance ecological validity. OBIA can serve as a powerful pre-processing step to delineate ecologically coherent forest stands. The NAD framework could then be applied between these stands by modeling the spatial association among stand centroids or forests within stands, thereby capturing neighborhood effects within stands that are currently overlooked in conventional OBIA. For large areas, heterogeneous stands, a fine-scale NAD analysis within a stand object could help explain internal biomass variations. Thus, the NAD framework can be served as a complementary tool, layered upon OBIA, to model continuous spatial processes across and within discrete ecological units, offering new insights for future research. Overall, the NAD-based model with multi-source remote sensing data provides a new theoretical framework for estimating FSV as well as other ecological indicators.

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed an NAD-based FSV modelling to determine the optimal NAD indexes and optimal FSV estimation model for each tree species based on the GA, estimated the FSV, and evaluated the accuracy of the FSV in Mengyin County using UAV LiDAR data, optical remote sensing data, and field sample data. The results showed that the optimal NAD models corresponding to the FSV estimation of different tree species were different, and the NAD-based method was able to effectively improve the estimation accuracy of the FSV. Meanwhile, we analyzed the spatial distribution pattern of FSV by combining field surveys and found that most of the forests in Mengyin County were young, which has a large potential for carbon sequestration. We proposed a new solution for estimating the FSV, which helps realize the accurate detection of forest resources and mapping the distribution of forest carbon sinks with high accuracy.
