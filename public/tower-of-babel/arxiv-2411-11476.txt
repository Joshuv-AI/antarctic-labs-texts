Statistical Mechanics of Heteropolymers from Lattice Gauge Theory
Veronica Panizza, Alessandro Roggero, Philipp Hauke, Pietro Faccioli

Abstract
Lattice models are valuable tools to gain insight into the statistical physics of heteropolymers. We rigorously map the partition function of these models into a vacuum expectation value of a ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} lattice gauge theory (LGT), with both fermionic and bosonic degrees of freedom. Because the associated path integral expression is not affected by a sign problem, it is amenable to Monte Carlo (MC) sampling in both the sequence and structure space, unlike conventional polymer field theory. At the same time, since the LGT encoding relies on qubits, it provides a framework for future efforts to capitalize on the development of quantum computing hardware. We discuss two illustrative applications of our formalism: first, we use it to characterize the thermodynamically stable sequences and structures of small heteropolymers consisting of two types of residues. Next, we assess its efficiency to sample ensembles of compact structures, finding that the MC decorrelation time scales only linearly with the chain length.



Lattice polymers have been extensively studied to gain qualitative insight into the statistical physics of a wide class of soft matter systems
[
1
]
. In particular, compact structures of lattice heteropolymers have emerged as paradigmatic simplified models for protein native states.
While efficient algorithms have been developed for dilute polymer ensembles
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
,
compact structures of heteropolymers have proven significantly more challenging to sample
[
5
,
6
]
: Indeed, growth-based methods become exponentially inefficient, and the low acceptance rate of pivot moves hinders Monte Carlo (MC) schemes.
Combinatorial algorithms derived from graph theory can be more efficient in generating compact structures, particularly Hamiltonian paths
[
7
,
8
]
, but do not satisfy ergodicity. Recently developed advanced MC schemes overcome this limitation
[
9
,
10
]
. However, despite these advances, generating large uncorrelated compact heteropolymer lattice structures remains challenging.

In recent years, the development of quantum computing hardware has inspired alternative approaches that rely on a binary encoding of lattice polymer configurations
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
The upgrade from classical to quantum encoding involves replacing each binary variable with a two-level quantum system (qubit). In the classical and the quantum encoding, the task of sampling the ergodic surface can be formulated as a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) problem, enabling the characterization of remarkably complex polymer ensembles, such as self-assembled ring melts
[
14
]
.
Unfortunately, the computational advantage of the QUBO encoding rapidly degrades when it is applied to ensembles that do not contain ring polymers. In these cases, many new hard constraints and ancillary variables must be introduced to remove rings up to a given size. The number of these interactions and related ancillary variables diverges in the thermodynamic limit, where rings of any size can occur.
In addition, the QUBO approach cannot be directly applied to sample heteropolymer ensembles, because the viable conformational states are not confined to a ground space.
In principle, this problem may be overcome by resorting to
MC schemes. However, naive local trial moves based on randomly flipping binary variables would not preserve the chain’s topology, leading to exceedingly high rejection rates.

In this work, we overcome all these limitations by introducing a new quantum encoding, in which the partition function is mapped into a vacuum expectation value of a quantum field theory with both fermionic and bosonic degrees of freedom. The dynamics of this theory is shaped by its
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge symmetry, which expresses the chain’s continuity condition and inspires MC moves that preserve the chain’s topology.
By tuning the coupling constants of our LGT, we can vary the ring density or even set it to zero. As we show below, this scheme is particularly apt to sample compact polymer configurations.
Furthermore, this LGT can be naturally extended to study heteropolymers by adding chemistry-specific soft interactions. Finally, this encoding relies on qubits, so it provides a suitable framework to develop future quantum computing algorithms (see Sec.
of the Supplementary Material (SM)
[
15
]
for an introductory survey on quantum computing application to LGTs).

From polymers to LGT:
To establish our mapping, we begin by assigning a qubit state
|\Gamma_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}\rangle
to each edge connecting
neighboring
lattice sites
{\bf i}
and
{\bf j}
(see Fig.
1
) and a qubit state
|\Gamma_{\bf i}\rangle
to each lattice site
{\bf i}
.

The presence (absence) of a covalent bond between residues located at neighboring sites
{\bf i}
and
{\bf j}
is signaled by
\ket{\Gamma_{\ii\jj}}=\ket{1}(\ket{0})
, which we call
active
(
inactive
) bond state. Conversely, a chain terminal occupies site
{\bf i}
if
\ket{\Gamma_\ii}=\ket{1}
.
We also define the bond operator
\hat{B}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}\equiv\outerproduct{1}{1}
(projector on active bonds) and the site operator
\hat{g}_{\bf i}=\outerproduct{1}{1}
(projector on occupied sites).

Figure 1:
Our quantum encoding, illustrated for the so-called HP model, where residues are grouped into hydrophobic and polar.
The colored circles highlight the sites connected to active bonds.

We now consider the tensor product states of all the qubits,

|\Psi\rangle=\left(\prod_{\otimes\langle{\bf i},{\bf j}\rangle}\ket{\Gamma_{\ii\jj}}\right)\otimes\prod_{{\bf i}}\ket{\Gamma_\ii}
, constituting our computational basis.
In a subset of these states, the qubits in the
\ket{1}
state align to form continuous paths in the
d
-dimensional lattice. We now show that these so-called
polymer states

are solutions of

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{d}(-1)^{\hat{B}_{{\bf i}\,{\bf i}+{\bf e}_{k}}}\,(-1)^{\hat{B}_{{\bf i}\,{\bf i}-{\bf e}_{k}}}|\Psi\rangle=(-1)^{\hat{g}_{\bf i}}\,|\Psi\rangle,

(1)

where
\{{\bf e}_{k}\}_{k=1,\ldots,d}
is the set of lattice basis vectors.

To prove Eq. (
1
), we first note that the computational basis states are eigenstates of the operators at the two sides of the equation. The corresponding eigenvalues obey

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{d}(-1)^{\Gamma_{{\bf i}\,{\bf i}+{\bf e}_{k}}+\Gamma_{{\bf i}\,{\bf i}-{\bf e}_{k}}}=(-1)^{\Gamma_{\bf i}}.

(2)

Equation (
2
) expresses a flux conservation condition associated with a binary field
\Gamma_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}
that is “emitted” and “absorbed” at the chain endpoints (see also Fig.
in the SM). To see this, consider first a generic site
{\bf i}
where
\Gamma_{\bf i}=0
. There, Eq. (
2
) is fulfilled only if the number of active bonds connected to
{\bf i}
, given by the sum

\rho_{{\bf i}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\Gamma_{{\bf i}\,{\bf i}+{\bf e}_{k}}+\Gamma_{{\bf i}\,{\bf i}-{\bf e}_{k}}\right)\;,

(3)

is zero in empty sites and even in occupied sites. In particular, for the
3
-dimensional cubic lattice it is equal to 2 along the chain’s backbone and 4 or 6 at the intersection point between chains. Conversely, on the same lattice, the number of active bonds attached to the sites with eigenvalue
\Gamma_{\bf i}=1
(chain endpoints) is equal to 1, or to 3 and 5 if chain intersections occur at the endpoint. A graphical representation of the analogous constraints for a
2
-d square lattice is reported in Fig.
of the SM.

The continuity Eq. (
1
) is in fact the Gauss’ law of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
LGT
introduced by Wegner and Kogout in Ref.
[
16
]
, with
\hat{B}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}
playing the role of the electric operator and
\hat{g}_{\bf i}
that of the topological charge operator.
Inspired by this connection, we also introduce a so-called
chain deformation operator

\hat{C}_{\square}
, which plays the role of the magnetic operator of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
LGT, and is defined on each plaquette on the lattice as

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{\square}=\prod_{k}\hat{X}_{k}\qquad\textrm{with}\quad\hat{X}=\outerproduct{1}{0}+\outerproduct{0}{1}\,,

(4)

where
k
labels the edges defining the given plaquette
\square
.

Realizing the existence of an underlying gauge symmetry is the key to devise a new type of MC algorithm to sample polymer configurations. Indeed, we note that
\hat{C}_{\square}
commutes with the operator on the left-hand side of Eq. (
1
). This implies that if
|\Psi\rangle
is a polymer state, then
\hat{C}_{\square}|\Psi\rangle
is a different (i.e., locally deformed) polymer state. In practice,
\hat{C}_{\square}
flips the state of all qubits assigned to the edges of a plaquette (see Fig.
of the SM).
Another class of operators that preserve Eq. (
1
) is the chain terminal displacement operator
\hat{D}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}
that acts on the qubits at the neighboring sites
{\bf i}
and
{\bf j}
and on the bond qubit connecting them,

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}=\hat{X}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bf i}\,\hat{a}_{\bf j}+\mathrm{h.c.}\,,

(5)

where
\hat{a}_{{\bf i}}=\outerproduct{0}{1}
.
In a LGT, this term represents the motion of charged matter with a corresponding instantaneous adjustment of electric field lines.
Two arbitrary polymer configurations with the same number of open chains can be deformed one into the other by a combination of chain deformations and terminal displacements.

Let us further restrict the set of viable tensor product states to retain only
self-avoiding
polymer states. These can be identified with the ground
space
of the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\textrm{Gauss}}+\hat{H}_{\textrm{SA}}
, with

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\textrm{Gauss}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{0}\sum_{\bf i}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{d}(-1)^{\hat{B}_{{\bf i}\,{\bf i}+{\bf e}_{k}}+\hat{B}_{{\bf i}\,{\bf i}-{\bf e}_{k}}}-(-1)^{\hat{g}_{\bf i}}\right)^{2},

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\textrm{SA}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{1}\,\sum_{{\bf i}}\big(\hat{\rho}_{\bf i}+\hat{g}_{\bf i})(\hat{\rho}_{\bf i}+(\hat{g}_{\bf i}-2)\big)\,,

(7)

where
\hat{\rho}_{\bf i}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\hat{B}_{{\bf i}\,{\bf i}+{\bf e}_{k}}+\hat{B}_{{\bf i}\,{\bf i}-{\bf e}_{k}}\right)
counts the number of active links at the site
{\bf i}
. If the couplings
e_{0}
and
e_{1}
are large and
e_{0}>e_{1}
,
\hat{H}
implements two hard constraints:
\hat{H}_{\textrm{Gauss}}
enforces the Gauss’ law in Eq. (
1
), while
\hat{H}_{\textrm{SA}}
the self-avoidance condition. Indeed, lattice sites where
\ket{\Gamma_\ii}=\ket{0}
yield null eigenvalues of
\hat{H}_{\textrm{SA}}
as long as they are either not attached to any active bond (empty sites) or they are linked to exactly two active bonds (backbone sites).
Lattice sites where
\ket{\Gamma_\ii}=\ket{1}
avoid an energetic penalty if they are attached to exactly 1 active bond, thus forming one of the chain’s endpoints.

Without loss of generality, in the following we specialize to the case of a single open chain, i.e., we require that only two
|\Gamma_{\bf i}\rangle
qubits must be in the
\ket{1}
states (note, at this stage this restriction still allows for an arbitrary number of additional ring polymers). We denote with
\left\{|\Psi_{s}\rangle\right\}_{s=1,2,\ldots}
the numerable set of all self-avoiding polymer states (i.e., the subset of degenerate ground states of
\hat{H}
) and introduce the linear sum
|\Omega\rangle\equiv\sum_{s}|\Psi_{s}\rangle
.
The matrix element
\mathcal{Z}\equiv\langle\Omega|\Omega\rangle
counts the total number of distinct self-avoiding configurations and, thus, is identified with the classical (grand-canonical) partition function of a system consisting of a single chain and arbitrary rings.
Upon inserting the resolutions of the identity in their respective spaces,
\hat{\mathbb{1}}=\sum_{\Gamma_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}}|\Gamma_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}\rangle\langle\Gamma_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}|
and

\hat{\mathbb{1}}^{\prime}=\sum_{\Gamma_{\bf i}}|\Gamma_{{\bf i}}\rangle\langle\Gamma_{{\bf i}}|
, we obtain an explicit representation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}=\sum_{\Gamma}\left(\prod_{\bf i}\delta\left(\mathcal{G}_{\bf i}[\Gamma]\right)\right)\,e^{-\beta H_{\mathrm{SA}}[\Gamma]},\quad\beta=(k_{B}T)^{-1}.

(8)

In this expression, we have collectively denoted with
\Gamma
the fields defined by the eigenstates of the bond and chain terminal operators.
In Eq. (
8
), we have imposed the Gauss’ law via a set of delta-functions for the functions
\mathcal{G}_{\bf i}[\Gamma]\equiv\prod_{k=1}^{d}(-1)^{\Gamma_{{\bf i}\,{\bf i}+{\bf e}_{k}}+\Gamma_{{\bf i}\,{\bf i}-{\bf e}_{k}}}-(-1)^{\Gamma_{\bf i}}
, while the self-avoiding condition follows from choosing a large coupling
e_{1}
in the Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{SA}}
, ensuring
\beta e_{1}\gg 1
for all feasible temperatures. The motivation for using two different notations to express these hard constraints is that we can define ergodic trial moves that preserve Gauss’ law while we resort to a Metropolis criterion to enforce self-avoidance.

In the ensemble defined by Eq. (
8
), the density of ring polymers and the average length of the open chain is not fixed. We now show that this limitation is overcome by coupling the qubits
|\Gamma_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}\rangle
and
\ket{\Gamma_\ii}
to “spinless fermions” with
M
degenerate flavors, via the tight-binding Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\langle{\bf i}\,{\bf j}\rangle}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{\psi}^{(m){\dagger}}_{{\bf i}}\hat{\psi}^{(m)}_{{\bf j}}\,\hat{T}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\delta_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}\big(m_{\mathrm{f}}-\bar{g}^{2}\hat{\rho}_{\bf i}\big)-\lambda^{2}\hat{B}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}\right],

(10)

where
\hat{\psi}^{(m)}_{{\bf i}}
and
\hat{\psi}^{(m){\dagger}}_{{\bf i}}
are anti-commuting fields.
The motivation for introducing these fermions into the theory is that the density of rings in the ensemble can be tuned by varying the parameters
m_{\mathrm{f}},\bar{g}
, and
\lambda
.

Again, we consider the matrix element

\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}=\langle\Omega^{\prime}|\Omega^{\prime}\rangle
, where
now
|\Omega^{\prime}\rangle
denotes the linear sum of all the degenerate ground states of
\hat{H}^{\prime}=\hat{H}+\hat{H}_{\textrm{F}}
.
The explicit expression for
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
involves a Gaussian path integral over
M
Grassmann fields, which we carry out analytically to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}=\sum_{\Gamma}\left(\det T[\Gamma]\right)^{M}\prod_{\bf i}\delta(\mathcal{G}_{\bf i}[\Gamma])\,e^{-\beta H_{\textrm{SA}}[\Gamma]},

(11)

where
T_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}(\Gamma)=\delta_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}\big(m_{\mathrm{f}}-\bar{g}^{2}\rho_{\bf i}\big)-\lambda^{2}\Gamma_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}
.
Appendix
A
of the End Matter, we explicitly show that by setting

m_{\mathrm{f}}=4\bar{g}^{2}\;\mathrm{and}\;\lambda^{2}=\bar{g}^{2},
ring polymers are completely removed, because the determinant
\det T(\Gamma)
vanishes identically for all configurations of the field
\Gamma
that contain at least one isolated ring.

Once ring polymers have been suppressed, the length of the remaining single chain is fixed by the total number of bonds. Any given average length
\ell_{0}
can be set by introducing a
soft
constraint via the additional Hamiltonian term

\hat{H}_{\textrm{L}}=c\sum_{\langle{\bf i}{\bf j}\rangle}(\hat{B}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}-\overline{\Gamma})^{2}
,
corresponding to the new matrix element
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime\prime}=\langle\Omega^{\prime}|e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{\textrm{L}}}|\Omega^{\prime}\rangle
.
The parameter
\overline{\Gamma}\in(0,1)
controls the average propensity of bond qubits to be in the
\ket{1}
state. Using the idempotence of
\hat{B}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}
, one can recast the operator
e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{\textrm{L}}}
into the form of a chemical potential term, yielding
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime\prime}\propto\langle\Omega^{\prime}|e^{\beta\mu\hat{L}}|\Omega^{\prime}\rangle
,
where

\hat{L}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bf i}\hat{\rho}_{\bf i}

counts the total number of active bonds and
\mu=2c(2\bar{\Gamma}-1)
.

The number of degenerate fermion flavors
M
and the parameter
\mu
determine the average chain length
\ell_{0}
and the size of the length fluctuations. In Appendix
of the End Matter, we derive the value of
\mu
that yields a given desired
\ell_{0}
, valid in the limit
M\gg 1
:

\displaystyle\mu\,{\simeq}\,\frac{M}{\beta}\bigg(2\ln 2-\frac{1}{\ell_{0}+1}\bigg).

(12)

In the same limit, fluctuations around
\ell_{0}
are exponentially suppressed.

Heteropolymers:

Let us now generalize our LGT theory to include soft interactions that depend on the chemical properties of the residues.
To account for chemical variability, we introduce additional qubits at each lattice site –one for each type of residue– and a Hamiltonian that encodes non-covalent interactions:

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt\hat{H}_{\mathrm{chem}}=\sum_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}\sum_{a,b=1}^{D}C_{ab}\,\hat{\chi}_{\bf i}\,\hat{\chi}_{\bf j}\,(1-\hat{B}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}})\hat{n}^{a}_{\bf i}\,\hat{n}^{b}_{\bf j}\,\overline{\Delta}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}},

(13)

where
D
is the size of the chemical alphabet,

\hat{n}^{a}_{\bf i}=\outerproduct{1_a}{1_a}
is the lattice site occupation operator acting on the qubit of type
a
located at site
{\bf i}
, and
\hat{\chi}_{\bf i}\equiv\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\rho}_{\bf i}+\hat{g}_{\bf i})
is an operator that identifies the sites belonging to the chain.
With the self-avoiding constraint, we have
\rho_{\bf i}=0
(site
{\bf i}
not occupied by the polymer),
\rho_{\bf i}=1
(site occupied by the endpoint), and
\rho_{\bf i}=2
(site along the backbone), so
\hat{\chi}_{\bf i}=\hat{\mathbb{1}}
at the endpoints and along the backbone.
The factor
\hat{\chi}_{\bf i}\hat{\chi}_{\bf j}
in Eq. (
13
) ensures that the non-covalent interaction Hamiltonian only couples qubits located at lattice sites that are occupied by the chain.
Furthermore, the term
(1-\hat{B}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}})
excludes non-covalent interactions between covalently bound residues.
\overline{\Delta}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}
is a decaying function of the distance
|{\bf i}-{\bf j}|
. In a nearest-neighbor model,
\overline{\Delta}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}=1
if
|{\bf i}-{\bf j}|=1
and 0 otherwise.
C_{ab}
is a
D\times D
matrix that defines the interaction between the residue types. Finally, to set the relative abundance of the different residue types along the chain, we introduce the term

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{rel}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g^{\prime}\sum_{a=1}^{D}\,\sum_{\bf i}\hat{\chi}_{\bf i}\bigg(\hat{n}^{a}_{\bf i}-\nu_{a}\bigg)^{2}\,.

(14)

This term can also be written as
\hat{H}_{\textrm{rel}}=-\sum_{a}\mu_{a}\hat{N}^{a}+g^{\prime}\sum_{a}\nu_{a}^{2}\sum_{\bf i}\hat{\chi}_{\bf i}
, where
\hat{N}^{a}=\sum_{\bf i}\hat{\chi}_{\bf i}\,\hat{n}^{a}_{\bf i}
counts the number of residues of type
a
in the polymer backbone and
\mu_{a}\equiv g^{\prime}(2\nu_{a}-1)
are the associated chemical potentials.
Since for a single chain
\sum_{i}\hat{\chi}_{\bf i}=\hat{L}+1
, the second term contributing to
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{rel}}
can be absorbed into the chemical potential term
\sim\mu
acting on the bond qubits.
After combining all terms, we obtain the final expression for the partition function of a single heteropolymer:

\displaystyle\hskip-17.07182pt\mathcal{Z}_{\textrm{het}}=\sum_{\Gamma,\eta}\prod_{\bf i}\delta[\mathcal{G}_{{\bf i}}(\Gamma)]\delta[\Phi_{\bf i}(\eta)]\,e^{\beta(\mathcal{L}[\eta,\Gamma]-\mathcal{H}[\eta,\Gamma])}.\qquad

(15)

The summation
\sum_{\eta}
spans the configurations of the
D
fields
\eta^{a}_{\bf i}
, constructed from the eigenvalues of
\hat{n}^{a}_{\bf i}
, while

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\Gamma,\eta]

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mu L+\sum_{a}\mu_{a}N_{a}+M\textrm{Tr}\log T

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}[\Gamma,\eta]

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle H_{\textrm{SA}}+H_{\textrm{chem}}.

(17)

H_{\textrm{chem}}
is obtained by replacing
\hat{\Gamma}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}
and
\hat{n}^{a}_{\bf i}
with the binary fields
\Gamma_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}
and
\eta^{a}_{\bf i}
in Eq. (
13
). Setting
\Phi_{\bf i}=(\sum_{a=1}^{D}\eta^{a}_{\bf i}-1)^{2}
, so
\prod_{\bf i}\delta[\Phi_{\bf i}]
ensures that at any lattice site
{\bf i}
there is exactly one active residue-type state (see bottom-right panel in Fig.
1
).

Importantly, the theory defined by Eq. (
15
) does not allow us, in general, to choose a specific primary sequence.
Nevertheless, it can be used to characterize the thermodynamically stable sequences along with their structures.
Specific patterns in the sequence may be imposed by additional interactions. For example, co-polymers can be sampled by adding an “anti-ferromagnetic” interaction,

\hat{H}_{\textrm{co-pol}}=\sum_{a,b}\sum_{\langle{\bf i}{\bf j}\rangle}\hat{n}_{\bf i}^{a}\hat{n}_{\bf i}^{b}\hat{B}_{{\bf i}{\bf j}}
, while block co-polymers are obtained from the analog “ferromagnetic” term.

MC Algorithm:
Equation (
15
) enables us to simultaneously sample the heteropolymer’s conformational and chemical space, using a Metropolis MC scheme. To this end, we first initialize the binary field
\Gamma
to fulfill all conformational constraints, i.e., to represent an arbitrary self-avoiding polymer configuration. Likewise, the initial binary fields
\eta_{a}
should obey the hard constraint set by the term
\prod_{\bf i}\delta[\Phi_{\bf i}]
in Eq. (
15
).
To propose conformational trial moves, we act at a random location with the deformation operator, Eq. (
4
), or with the terminal displacement operator, Eq. (
5
).
New sequences are proposed by random point-wise mutations of the chemical residue at arbitrary positions in the lattice.
These trial moves are accepted or rejected according to a Metropolis criterion defined by the probability density
\propto e^{\beta(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{H})}
.

Computational efficiency:

The example discussed in Sec.
of the SM illustrates why our LGT approach is particularly suited to sample compact configurations. To showcase its computational efficiency, in Fig.

Discussion and outlook:
Starting from the pioneering work of Edwards
[
19
]
and DeGennes
[
20
]
, self-consistent field theory methods have been extensively employed in polymer science
[
21
,
22
]
, with applications ranging from material science
[
23
]
to biophysics
[
24
,
25
]
.
A key limitation of the conventional polymer field theory is that a sign problem prevents the application of stochastic sampling methods. While successful heuristic applications of the complex Langevin equation have been reported
[
26
,
27
]
, a general understanding
of the conditions under which these schemes converge is still lacking.
In contrast, for even values of
M
our LGT does not suffer from a sign problem, and can thus be solved by MC. Furthermore, the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge symmetry informs conformational moves that are particularly apt to sample compact structures. In the tests we performed on 2-dimensional lattices, the computational cost was found to scale linearly with the median chain length.

LGT was originally developed to solve quantum chromo-dynamics (QCD) in the strong coupling regime.
The present work paves the way for exporting to polymer physics field-theory methods originally developed to investigate QCD’s spectrum and phase diagram. For example, the vacuum structure and the mass gap in the light-hadron spectrum were shown to be shaped by the spontaneous breaking of chiral symmetry (for a recent review, see, e.g.,
[
28
]
), which involves a transformation of the quark flavors. Using the LGT formulation, it would be interesting to explore if the stability and the low entropy of protein native states can also be explained by a spontaneously symmetry breaking.
Indeed, previous theoretical
[
29
]
and experimental
[
30
]
studies have shown that the key properties of the thermodynamics of proteins are preserved by interchanging specific types of amino acids or grouping them into a few effective families, thus providing an approximate “flavor” symmetry.
