Adam Exploits ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} -geometry of Loss Landscape via Coordinate-wise Adaptivity
Shuo Xie, Mohamad Amin Mohamadi, Zhiyuan Li

Abstract
Adam outperforms SGD when training language models. Yet this advantage is not well-understood theoretically – previous convergence analysis for Adam and SGD mainly focuses on the number of steps T T and is already minimax-optimal in non-convex cases, which are both O ~ ( T − 1 / 4 ) \widetilde{O}(T^{-1/4}) . In this work, we argue that the exploitation of nice ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} -geometry is the key advantage of Adam over SGD. More specifically, we give a new convergence analysis for Adam under novel assumptions that loss is smooth under ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} -geometry rather than the more common ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -geometry, which yields a much better empirical smoothness constant for GPT-2 and ResNet models. Our experiments confirm that Adam performs much worse when the favorable ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} -geometry is changed while SGD provably remains unaffected. We also extend the convergence analysis to blockwise Adam under novel blockwise smoothness assumptions.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have gained phenomenal capabilities as their scale grows
(
Radford et al., 2019
;
Kaplan et al., 2020
;
Brown et al., 2020
;
Zhang et al., 2022a
;
Touvron et al., 2023
;
OpenAI, 2023
;
Reid et al., 2024
)
. However, pre-training LLMs is incredibly time-consuming.
\mathtt{Adam}

(
Kingma & Ba, 2014
)
is the current to-go optimization algorithm for LLMs due to its fast convergence.
In contrast,
\mathtt{SGD}
, a popular and arguably the simplest optimizer, optimizes language model loss much more slowly than
\mathtt{Adam}
.

However, the optimization benefit of
\mathtt{Adam}
over
\mathtt{SGD}
cannot be explained by existing theory. Current convergence analyses for
\mathtt{Adam}
and
\mathtt{SGD}
focus on the dependence on the number of steps under assumptions on the smoothness of the loss and gradient bounds
(
Défossez et al., 2022
)
, and it has been shown that both
\mathtt{Adam}
and
\mathtt{SGD}
achieve the minimax convergence rate
\widetilde{O}(T^{-1/4})
in the non-convex settings
(
Arjevani et al., 2023
)
. Thus according to the theory, in the worst case,
\mathtt{SGD}
would be more desirable compared to
\mathtt{Adam}
because they have the same convergence rate, and yet
\mathtt{Adam}
is less memory-efficient due to its coordinate-wise adaptivity, which needs to store the empirical moving average of second-order moments of past stochastic gradients. Therefore, we hypothesize that the coordinate-wise adaptivity in
\mathtt{Adam}
is exploiting some unknown properties of LLMs which
\mathtt{SGD}
cannot make use of.

Towards this end, we identified a big difference between
\mathtt{Adam}
and
\mathtt{SGD}
, which is ignored in previous works. That is,
\mathtt{SGD}
is rotation-equivariant, while
\mathtt{Adam}
is only permutation equivariant (
Definition

2.1
). Intuitively, if we rotate the loss landscape, the trajectory of
\mathtt{SGD}
would be the same (up to some rotation), while the trajectory of
\mathtt{Adam}
can be completely different. If
\mathtt{Adam}
optimizes much more slowly after rotation, it suggests
\mathtt{Adam}
is exploiting some non-rotation-invariant properties of the loss, which is not captured by standard smoothness assumptions in the convergence analysis.

Figure

1
summarizes our findings by comparing
\mathtt{Adam}
on the original and rotated loss. The performance of
\mathtt{Adam}
on the rotated loss does become much worse than
\mathtt{Adam}
on the original loss. We also test a memory-efficient and rotation-equivariant variant of
\mathtt{SGD}
,
\mathtt{AdaSGD}

(
Wang & Wiens, 2020
)
, defined in
Algorithm

2
.
Surprisingly, the rotated
\mathtt{Adam}
performs even much worse than the
\mathtt{SGD}
variant. The results suggest it is impossible to explain the superior optimization performance of Adam over SGD just using rotation-invariant assumptions on the loss function, which raises the natural question,

​​​​​​​
What non-rotation-invariant properties of loss functions enable
\mathtt{Adam}
to converge faster than
\mathtt{SGD}
?
​​​​​​

We hypothesize that the
\ell_{2}
-lipschitzness of loss gradient does not provide a tight-enough characterization of loss landscape of deep learning models in practice, such that we can separate
\mathtt{Adam}
and other rotation-equivariant algorithms.
Inspired by the similarity between
\mathtt{Adam}
and
\mathtt{SignGD}
and the fact that
\mathtt{SignGD}
is the normalized steepest descent with respect to
\ell_{\infty}
-norm, we propose to use
\ell_{\infty}
-norm related smoothness as a better tool to analyze
\mathtt{Adam}
. In particular, our main results use the
(1,1)
-norm of the Hessian of the loss normalized by variable dimension
d
, as the smoothness measure, instead of its spectral norm. And we prove a convergence rate of
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
for
\mathtt{Adam}
without noise, or
O((\frac{\log T}{T})^{1/4})
with noise. Our results have the same dependence on
T
as previous results, but a much smaller smoothness constant when measured empirically. We empirically verify
(1,1)
-norm of Hessian positively correlates with final training loss of
\mathtt{Adam}
on both synthetic tasks like quadratic loss and real tasks like training GPT2 on OpenWebText and ResNet on CIFAR10.

We summarize our contributions below:

1.

We show by experiments that the empirical optimization advantage of
\mathtt{Adam}
over
\mathtt{SGD}
can not be explained solely under rotation-invariant assumptions. (
Figure

1
)

2.

We propose a new complexity metric for the optimization problem, which is the
(1,1)
-norm of the Hessian matrix of loss,
\left\|\nabla^{2}L(x)\right\|_{1,1}
. We present a novel convergence result for
\mathtt{Adam}
depending on this metric in the case of
\beta_{1}=0
. (
Theorem

3.5
)

3.

We further generalize the theoretical analysis (
Theorem

3.11
) for
\mathtt{Adam}
to blockwise
\mathtt{Adam}
(
Algorithm

3
) whose convergence rate can be characterized by a novel smoothness measure (
Definition

3.9
).
\mathtt{Adam}
and
\mathtt{AdaSGD}
are two notable examples of blockwise
\mathtt{Adam}
. In
\mathtt{Adam}
, all blocks are of size
1
. In
\mathtt{AdaSGD}
, there is only one block.

4.

We empirically verify that
when
\mathtt{Adam}
converges more slowly on the rotated loss, the
(1,1)
-norm of Hessian also increases, which suggests that our new complexity metric for
\mathtt{Adam}
’s convergence is practically relevant. (
Section

4
).

3
Main Results: Convergence Rates of
\mathtt{Adam}

In this section, we present our main theoretical results, starting with a convergence analysis of
\mathtt{Adam}
for stochastic smooth losses with coordinate-wise gradient noise (
Theorem

3.5
).
We allow non-convex losses and thus the convergence is measured by the
\ell_{1}
norm of the gradient.
For the deterministic loss, our best convergence rate (
Theorem

3.2
) is achieved by
\mathtt{SignGD}
(
\mathtt{Adam}
with
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=0
).
For the stochastic loss with bounded gradient noise variance, our best rate (
Corollary

3.6
) is achieved by
\mathtt{RMSProp}
(
\mathtt{Adam}
with
\beta_{1}=0
and
\beta_{2}\in[0,1]
).

Then we extend our analysis of
\mathtt{Adam}
to more general blockwise
\mathtt{Adam}
(
Theorem

3.11
), which contains both
\mathtt{Adam}
and
\mathtt{AdaSGD}
as special cases. We also come up with novel smoothness measures (
Definition

3.12
) corresponding to the set of blocks used in blockwise
\mathtt{Adam}
.

Similar to previous works
(
Défossez et al., 2022
)
, our analysis could be extended to the most general case of
\mathtt{Adam}
, where both
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
are non-zero. But the rate becomes strictly worse than
\mathtt{RMSProp}
(the case of
\beta_{1}=0
), as there will be some extra polynomials of
\frac{1}{1-\beta_{1}}
.
We decide not to include the result for the most general case, on the one hand for ease of presentation, and on the other hand, because such result can not explain the optimization benefit of momentum (
\beta_{1}>0
) in practice and does not add any insight on the benefit of
\mathtt{Adam}
. We hypothesize that we are missing some important features of loss landscape of transformers in the theoretical assumptions and we leave this for future work.

3.1
Warmup:
\mathtt{SignGD}
(
\mathtt{Adam}
with
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=0

In this section, we present the convergence analysis for
\mathtt{SignGD}
as a warm-up and illustrate how
\mathtt{Adam}
could benefit from a non-rotation-invariant property of the loss landscape, which in particular is the

\mathtt{SignGD}
is the normalized steepest descent with respect to

Xie & Li, 2024

Definition 3.1
.

Given a norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|

{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have that
\left\|\nabla L({\bm{x}})-\nabla L({\bm{y}})\right\|_{*}\leq H\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\|
.

Theorem

3.2
gives the convergence rate for
\mathtt{SignGD}
and the proof is in
Appendix

B

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
L
be
H
-smooth w.r.t.
\ell_{\infty}
norm and
\{{\bm{x}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
be the iterates of
\mathtt{SignGD}
(
\mathtt{Adam}
with
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=0
) on
L
with initialization
{\bm{x}}_{0}
and learning rate

\min_{1\leq t\leq T}\left\|\nabla L({\bm{x}}_{t})\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{L({\bm{x}}_{0})-\min L({\bm{x}})}{T\eta}+\frac{H\eta}{2}.

If we choose
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{2(L({\bm{x}}_{0})-\min L({\bm{x}}))}{TH}}
, then
\min_{1\leq t\leq T}\left\|\nabla L({\bm{x}}_{t})\right\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2H(L({\bm{x}}_{0})-\min L({\bm{x}}))}{T}}

3.2
Main result:
\mathtt{RMSProp}
(
\mathtt{Adam}
with
\beta_{1}=0,\beta_{2}\in[0,1]
)

It is well-known that
\mathtt{SignGD}
might not converge in the stochastic case as the expectation of descent direction for mini-batch loss may not be a descent direction for
L
.
\mathtt{RMSProp}
is proposed to address this issue by using a moving average of the squared gradient per coordinate to reduce the correlation between the denominator and the numerator, thus making the expected update direction less biased
(
Hinton et al., 2012
)
. In this subsection we formalize the above intuition and show indeed a positive
\beta_{2}
in
\mathtt{Adam}
helps convergence in the stochastic case. The main challenges here are from both lower bounding the first-order term and upper bounding the second-order term in the modified descent lemma (the
\mathtt{RMSProp}
counterpart of
9
).

L({\bm{x}}_{t})-L({\bm{x}}_{t-1})\leq-\eta_{t}\nabla L({\bm{x}}_{t})^{\top}\frac{{\bm{g}}_{t}}{\sqrt{{\bm{v}}_{t}+\epsilon}}+\frac{H}{2}\eta_{t}^{2}\left\|\frac{{\bm{g}}_{t}}{\sqrt{{\bm{v}}_{t}+\epsilon}}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}

We can only upper bound
\left\|\frac{{\bm{g}}_{t}}{\sqrt{{\bm{v}}_{t}+\epsilon}}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}
by
\frac{1}{1-\beta_{2}}
without more fine-grained analysis on the relationship between gradients in each step, which will greatly hurt the dependence of convergence rate on
1-\beta_{2}
. However, even though the update
\frac{g_{t,i}}{\sqrt{v_{t,i}+\epsilon}}
at step
t
can be as large as
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta_{2}}}
with some very large
g_{t,i}
, the average moving speed for each coordinate should be close to
1
. Therefore, we introduce
Definition

3.3
, which is slightly stronger than
Definition

3.1
but allows us to decompose the second order term into each coordinate according to
Lemma

3.13
. It also facilitates the coordinate-wise analysis for the first order term. We note this definition also appears in Assumption 2.3 of the concurrent work
Maladkar et al. (2024)
.

Definition 3.3
.

For any
\mathbf{H}=(H_{1},\ldots,H_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we say a function
L
is
\mathbf{H}
-smooth coordinate-wisely w.r.t.
\ell_{\infty}
norm
, if and only if
\left|\nabla_{i}L({\bm{x}})-\nabla_{i}L({\bm{y}})\right|\leq H_{i}\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\|_{\infty}
for any
i\in[d]
,
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(1,1)-norm as a surrogate complexity measure for coordinate-wise smoothness.

H_{i}
in
Definition

3.3
is determined by
\sup_{{\bm{x}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|\nabla^{2}_{i,j}L({\bm{x}})\right|

-norm of Hessian of loss along the training trajectory. We are the first to provide an efficient approximation algorithm with concentration guarantees in

By definition,
\mathbf{H}
-smoothness coordinate-wisely w.r.t.
\ell_{\infty}

-smoothness w.r.t.
\ell_{\infty}

Assumption 3.4

(Coordinate-wise noise)

There exist constants

(Main,

3.3
A unified analysis for blockwise
\mathtt{Adam}

In this subsection, we present convergence analysis for a broader class of adaptive algorithms defined in
Algorithm

3
. It can be viewed as a coarser version of
\mathtt{Adam}
because it does pre-conditioning blockwisely instead of coordinate-wisely. Since
\mathtt{Adam}
and
\mathtt{AdaSGD}
can be viewed as special cases of blockwise
\mathtt{Adam}
(
Algorithm

3
) with
\Phi_{\mathtt{Adam}}:i\mapsto i
and
\Phi_{\mathtt{AdaSGD}}:i\mapsto 1
respectively, any convergence results for
Algorithm

3
would imply convergence of
\mathtt{Adam}
and
\mathtt{AdaSGD}
. Finally we also note that such blockwise
\mathtt{Adam}
has been recently studied empirically by some concurrent work, where the algorithm is named by Adam-mini
(
Zhang et al., 2024b
)
and Adalayer
(
Zhao et al., 2024
)
.

Algorithm 3

Blockwise
\mathtt{Adam}

\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\epsilon\geq 0
, block partition
\Phi:[d]\to[B]
, total steps
T
, learning rate schedule
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
,
\epsilon
, initial
{\bm{m}}_{0}
,
v_{0}
.

initialization
{\bm{x}}_{0}
, stochastic losses
\{L_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}

v_{0,b}\leftarrow v_{0}

for

t=1,2,\cdots,T
:

g_{t,i}\leftarrow\nabla_{i}L_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t-1})

m_{t,i}\leftarrow\beta_{1}m_{t-1,i}+(1-\beta_{1})g_{t,i}

v_{t,b}\leftarrow\beta_{2}v_{t-1,b}+(1-\beta_{2})\left(\sum_{\Phi(i)=b}g_{t,i}^{2}\right)/d_{b}

We first introduce more notations. For a partition function
\Phi:[d]\rightarrow[B]
where

\Phi^{-1}(b)=\{i|\Phi(i)=b\}
and

{\bm{x}}_{(b)}
as

and the submatrix

Definition 3.8

(
\Phi
-norm)
.

We define the
(\infty,2)
-norm w.r.t. partition
\Phi
of the vector
{\bm{x}}
as the
\ell_{\infty}
norm of the vector
\left(\frac{\left\|{\bm{x}}_{(b)}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d_{b}}}\right)_{b=1}^{B}
, which is
\max_{b\in[B]}\frac{\left\|{\bm{x}}_{(b)}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d_{b}}}
. For convenience, we will denote it by
\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{\Phi}
or just call it
\Phi
-norm. We denote its dual norm by
\|{\bm{x}}\|_{\Phi,*}
, which is equal to
\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sqrt{d_{b}}\left\|{\bm{x}}_{(b)}\right\|_{2}
.

We will need
Definition

3.9
to characterize the smoothness of the loss function when analyzing general blockwise
\mathtt{Adam}
. We note
\Phi_{\mathtt{Adam}}
-smoothness also appears in Assumption 2 in
Bernstein et al. (2018)
to analyze
\mathtt{SignGD}
. Previous and concurrent work
(
Ene et al., 2021
;
Liu et al., 2024
;
Jiang et al., 2024
)
employ the same assumption to analyze AdaGrad. We are the first to analyze
\mathtt{Adam}
under such smoothness assumption. Moreover, we are the first to empirically measure it for loss functions in real task with theoretical guarantee.

Definition 3.9

(
\Phi
-smoothness)
.

We say a diagonal matrix
{\bm{A}}
follows partition
\Phi
if and only if the diagonal elements of
{\bm{A}}
are constant within each block defined by
\Phi
, i.e., there are
a_{1},\cdots,a_{B}\in\mathbb{R}
, such that
{\bm{A}}_{i,i}=a_{\Phi(i)}
for any
i\in[d]
.

We say a twice-differentiable
L
is
H
-smooth under partition
\Phi
if there exists a diagonal matrix
{\bm{A}}
following partition
\Phi
such that
H=\Tr({\bm{A}})
and
{\bm{A}}
dominates
\left|\nabla^{2}L({\bm{x}})\right|
for all
x
.
We further define the
\Phi
-smoothness of loss
L
, denoted by
H(L,\Phi)
, as the smallest constant
H
such that
L
is
H
-smooth under parition
\Phi
, that is,

\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}H(L,\Phi)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{A}}\text{ follows }\Phi\\
{\bm{A}}\succeq\left|\nabla^{2}L({\bm{x}})\right|,\forall{\bm{x}}\end{subarray}}\Tr({\bm{A}})

(1)

We note that
\Phi
-smoothness is different from the smoothness under
\Phi
-norm, where the latter is equal to
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\left\|{\bm{u}}\right\|_{\Phi}\leq 1}\left\|\nabla^{2}L({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}}\right\|_{\Phi,*}
. For each
{\bm{x}}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\|{\bm{u}}\right\|_{\Phi}\leq 1}\left\|\nabla^{2}L({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}}\right\|_{\Phi,*}=\sup_{\left\|{\bm{u}}\right\|_{\Phi}\leq 1}\sup_{\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|_{\Phi}\leq 1}{\bm{v}}^{\top}\nabla^{2}L({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}}=\sup_{\left\|{\bm{u}}\right\|_{\Phi}\leq 1}\left|{\bm{u}}^{\top}\nabla^{2}L({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}}\right|.

When
{\bm{A}}\succeq\left|\nabla^{2}L({\bm{x}})\right|
, we have that
\sup_{\left\|{\bm{u}}\right\|_{\Phi}\leq 1}\left|{\bm{u}}^{\top}\nabla^{2}L({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}}\right|\leq\sup_{\left\|{\bm{u}}\right\|_{\Phi}\leq 1}{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}
. When diagonal
{\bm{A}}
follows partition
\Phi
, we have that
\sup_{\left\|{\bm{u}}\right\|_{\Phi}\leq 1}{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}=\Tr({\bm{A}})
.
So
\Phi
-smoothness defined in
Definition

3.9
is always no smaller than the smoothness under
\Phi
-norm.

Another advantage with the definition is that the
H(L,\Phi)
will always non-increase with a more fine-grained partition.
For two partition functions
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
, we say
\Phi_{1}
includes
\Phi_{2}
if and only if
\Phi_{1}(i)=\Phi_{1}(j)

such that

follows partition

and partition

includes
\Phi_{2}
, then
{\bm{A}}
also follows
\Phi_{2}
.
So we have that
\{{\bm{A}}\mid{\bm{A}}\text{ follows }\Phi_{1},{\bm{A}}\succeq\left|\nabla^{2}L({\bm{x}})\right|\text{ for all }{\bm{x}}\}\subseteq\{{\bm{A}}\mid{\bm{A}}\text{ follows }\Phi_{2},{\bm{A}}\succeq\left|\nabla^{2}L({\bm{x}})\right|\text{ for all }{\bm{x}}\}
and

is no smaller than
H(L,\Phi_{2})
. Since
\Phi_{\mathtt{AdaSGD}}
includes any partition and any partition includes
\Phi_{\mathtt{Adam}}
,

is the largest and

is the smallest among all the partitions
\Phi
.

Proof sketch of
Theorem

3.11

We will use
\bar{{\bm{g}}}_{t}=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{g}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{<t}]=\nabla L({\bm{x}}_{t-1})
to denote the full batch gradient. We start by considering the decrease of
L({\bm{x}}_{t})
in a single step
t
. We can upper bound the second order term in the Taylor expansion (
Equation

3
) with
{\bm{H}}\succeq\nabla^{2}L({\bm{x}})
that achieves
\Tr({\bm{H}})=H(L,\Phi)
. Then we can get

\displaystyle L({\bm{x}}_{t})-L({\bm{x}}_{t-1})

\displaystyle\leq-\eta\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{g_{t,i}\bar{g}_{t,i}}{\sqrt{v_{t,\Phi(i)}+\epsilon}}+\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\sum_{b=1}^{B}H_{b}\frac{\left\|{\bm{g}}_{t,(b)}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{v_{t,b}+\epsilon}

(3)

\displaystyle\leq-\eta\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{{\bm{g}}_{t,(b)}^{\top}\bar{{\bm{g}}}_{t,(b)}}{\sqrt{v_{t,b}+\epsilon}}+\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\sum_{b=1}^{B}H_{b}d_{b}\frac{\left\|{\bm{g}}_{t,(b)}\right\|_{2}^{2}/d_{b}}{v_{t,b}+\epsilon}

(4)

The proof contains two main parts: lower bounding the first order term using
\|\bar{{\bm{g}}}_{t}\|_{\Phi,*}
and upper bounding the second order term. Below we address the second-order term first. As mentioned in
Section

, the second order term

can be as large as

to bound the sum by

rather than
\frac{T}{1-\beta_{2}}
, where we set

and
g_{t}\triangleq\left\|{\bm{g}}_{t,(b)}\right\|_{2}/\sqrt{d_{b}}
.

Lemma 3.14
.

Given any

, for any scalar sequences
\{v_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
and

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{g_{t}^{2}}{v_{t}}\leq T+\frac{\beta_{2}}{1-\beta_{2}}\ln{\frac{v_{T}}{v_{0}}}.

(5)

Now we turn to the first term. Ideally, for each block
b\in[B]
, we would like to connect the first order term to
\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{t,(b)}\|_{2}^{2}
by taking expectation, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{t}\frac{{\bm{g}}_{t,(b)}^{\top}\bar{{\bm{g}}}_{t,(b)}}{\sqrt{v_{t,b}+\epsilon}}\approx\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t}{\bm{g}}_{t,(b)}^{\top}\bar{{\bm{g}}}_{t,(b)}}{\sqrt{v_{t,b}+\epsilon}}=\frac{\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{t,(b)}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{t,b}+\epsilon}}

\mathbb{E}[\cdot|{\bm{x}}_{<t}]

\frac{{\bm{g}}_{t,(b)}^{\top}\bar{{\bm{g}}}_{t,(b)}}{\sqrt{v_{t,b}+\epsilon}}

{\bm{g}}_{t}
. To circumvent this difficulty, we lower bound each conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{t}\frac{{\bm{g}}_{t,(b)}^{\top}\bar{{\bm{g}}}_{t,(b)}}{\sqrt{v_{t,b}+\epsilon}}
by
\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t}{\bm{g}}_{t,(b)}^{\top}\bar{{\bm{g}}}_{t,(b)}}{2\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{t}v_{t,b}+\epsilon}}
, minus error terms related to noise magnitude
\sigma_{b}
.
We can further have
\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t}{\bm{g}}_{t,(b)}^{\top}\bar{{\bm{g}}}_{t,(b)}}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{t}v_{t,b}+\epsilon}}\geq\frac{\left\|\bar{{\bm{g}}}_{t,(b)}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\sqrt{\tilde{v}_{t,b}+\epsilon}}

. This leads to

3.15
whose proof is deferred to

.

Efficient generation of random orthogonal matrices.

Due to the huge parameter count, it is computationally infeasible to sample a random orthogonal matrix from Haar measure over the orthogonal group. Thus we decide to sample from an alternative distribution over orthogonal matrices, where the final rotation matrix is the composition of a sequence of simple orthogonal matrices, including random permutation and apply random orthogonal transformation on both sides of matrix-valued parameters.
See details in
Section

D.1
. Concurrent work

Efficient estimation of
(1,1)
-norm of Hessian matrix.

The algorithm is defined in
Algorithm

4
. We first subsample a fixed batch of training data for estimating the
(1,1)
-norm of Hessian matrix. The high-level idea is to compute the matrix vector products between Hessian of training loss on this batch and a sequence of random Cauchy vectors. Then we take the
\ell_{1}
norm of the coordinate-wise median of the resulting sequence of Hessian vector products. Because the Cauchy distribution is 1-stable, the resulting product is also a vector of Cauchy random variables, and the magnitude of each element equals to
\ell_{1}
norm of the corresponding row of the Hessian. Thus with infinitely many samples, the
\ell_{1}
norm of the coordinate-wise median converges almost surely to the
(1,1)
-norm of the Hessian. Below we provide a non-asymptotic high-probability multiplicative bound for the estimation error which depends mildly on the dimension
d
. More explanations and the proof of
Theorem

4.1
are in
Section

D.2
. Concurrent work
Maes et al. (2024)
also proposed another algorithm to measure
(1,1)
-norm.

Theorem 4.1
.

For the estimate in
Algorithm

4
with
n
Cauchy vectors, it holds that

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d}\textup{median}(\left|\mathbf{H}_{j,:}\right|)-\left\|\nabla^{2}L({\bm{x}})\right\|_{1,1}\right|\geq\epsilon\left\|\nabla^{2}L({\bm{x}})\right\|_{1,1}\right)\leq 2d\exp(-\frac{n\Delta^{2}}{2})+2\exp{\left(-\frac{2n\epsilon^{2}\cos^{4}((1+\Delta)\frac{\pi}{4})}{\pi^{2}}\right)}

for every

when

.

In other words, for any
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, we can use
n=\Omega(\ln d+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln\frac{1}{\delta})
hessian-vector product of the loss
L
at parameter
{\bm{x}}
and
nd
extra computation time to get an estimation of (1,1)-norm of
L
with at most
\epsilon

5
Related Works

Comparison between Adam and SGD

Previous work tries to analyze the difference between
\mathtt{Adam}
and
\mathtt{SGD}
from different perspectives.
Zhou et al. (2018)
proves a faster convergence rate of
\mathtt{Adam}
than
\mathtt{SGD}
when the stochastic gradients are sparse.
Zhang et al. (2020)
suggests that
\mathtt{SGD}
suffers more from heavy-tailed noise than
\mathtt{Adam}
.
Pan & Li (2023)
claims that
\mathtt{Adam}
has lower directional sharpness because of the effect of coordinate-wise clipping. Other works also consider the coordinate-wise normalization of
\mathtt{Adam}

(
Balles & Hennig, 2018
;
Kunstner et al., 2022
)
.
Kunstner et al. (2024)
shows that the heavy-tailed class imbalance in language modeling tasks will cause
\mathtt{SGD}
to converge slower when it can only optimize majority class well.
Zhang et al. (2024a)
finds that
\mathtt{Adam}
is better at handling the block heterogeneity of Hessian matrix, which is a specific phenomenon in transformers.
When viewing
\mathtt{Adam}
as an adaptive method, there are works showing that adaptive methods have an advantage of achieving optimal convergence rate without relying on problem-dependent constant
(
Ward et al., 2020
;
Levy et al., 2021
)
.
Ling et al. (2022)
focuses on the rotation equivariance property in the geometry optimization setting.

Convergence rate of Adam

There are many works showing convergence rate for
\mathtt{Adam}

(
Zhou et al., 2018
;
Chen et al., 2018
;
Zou et al., 2019
;
Shi & Li, 2021
;
Guo et al., 2021
;
Défossez et al., 2022
;
Zhang et al., 2022b
)
. Most of them rely on the smoothness of the loss function, which is measured w.r.t.
\ell_{2}
norm.
Zhang et al. (2019)
proposes the
(L_{0},L_{1})
smoothness condition should be more reasonable than globally bounded smoothness.
Li et al. (2024)
further generalizes the
(L_{0},L_{1})
smoothness condition. However, they still focus on the default
\ell_{2}
norm which is rotation-invariant. Concurrent work
Maes et al. (2024)
claims analyzing
\mathtt{Adam}
requires rotation dependent assumptions by conducting various rotating experiments.
To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to assume gradient Lipschitzness under
\ell_{\infty}
norm for the analysis on
\mathtt{Adam}
.

Comparison with
Li & Lin (2024)

Li & Lin (2024)
employs the same
\ell_{1}
norm for gradient and improves the dependence on dimension
d
compared to previous results for
\ell_{2}
norm. But they still assume the common
\ell_{2}
norm smoothness while we adapt their results under
\ell_{\infty}
norm smoothness to potentially further improve dependence on
d
. Another drawback of
Li & Lin (2024)
is setting
{\bm{v}}_{0}
based on noise magnitude
\sigma
. which is impractical in real experiments because
\sigma
is unknown. Overestimation for
\sigma
will result in slow convergence because large
{\bm{v}}_{0}
causes
\mathtt{Adam}
to behave similarly with
\mathtt{SGD}
without adjusting the coordinate-wise learning rate adaptively. In contrast, we allow for general initialization for
{\bm{v}}_{0}
and our convergence rate can work well in both noisy setting and deterministic setting. We also use
1-\beta_{2}=\Theta\left(\frac{\log{T}}{T}\right)
to obtain our convergence rate while
Li & Lin (2024)
requires
1-\beta_{2}=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)
.

6
Conclusion

We give a new convergence analysis (
Theorem

3.5
) for
\mathtt{Adam}
in the stochastic non-convex setting using a novel smoothness assumption. We show the convergence rate for the
\ell_{1}
norm of the gradient is
O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right)
in the deterministic case (
Corollaries

3.7
and
3.7
) and
O\left(\left(\frac{\log T}{T}\right)^{1/4}\right)
in the stochastic case (
Corollary

3.6
). We also extend our analysis to blockwise
\mathtt{Adam}
on loss
L
with respect to an arbitrary partition of the parameters
\Phi
(
Theorem

3.11
) using the corresponding smoothness
H(L,\Phi)
(
Definition

3.12
). Our bound for
\mathtt{Adam}
involves
(1,1)
-norm of Hessian, rather than the spectral norm of Hessian, which is relevant to the convergence speed of
\mathtt{AdaSGD}
. This leads to significantly better smoothness conditions for deep learning models including ResNet-18 and GPT2 empirically. Our experiments also verify that the smoothness measure
H(L,\Phi)
positively correlates with the optimization speed of blockwise
\mathtt{Adam}
with respect to the partition
\Phi
.

Appendix A
Equivariance Property of
\mathtt{Adam}
and
\mathtt{SGD}

See

Proof of
Theorem

2.2
.

For
\mathtt{SGD}
and
\mathtt{AdaSGD}
, we will show they are rotation-equivariant by induction. For any rotating transformation
\mathcal{R}({\bm{x}})=\bm{R}{\bm{x}}
, suppose
\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{s}=\mathcal{R}^{-1}({\bm{x}}_{s})=\bm{R}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{s}
holds for
s\leq t-1
. Then we have that
\tilde{{\bm{g}}}_{t}=\nabla_{\tilde{{\bm{x}}}}\tilde{L}_{t}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t})=\bm{R}^{\top}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}L(\bm{R}^{-1}\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t-1})=\bm{R}^{\top}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}L({\bm{x}}_{t-1})=\bm{R}^{\top}{\bm{g}}_{t}
and
\tilde{{\bm{m}}}_{t}=\bm{R}^{\top}{\bm{m}}_{t}
. From the update rule of
\mathtt{SGD}
, we have that
\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t}=\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}\tilde{{\bm{m}}}_{t}=\bm{R}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}\bm{R}^{\top}{\bm{m}}_{t}=\bm{R}^{\top}({\bm{x}}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}{\bm{m}}_{t})=\bm{R}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{t}
. For the update rule of
\mathtt{AdaSGD}
, we further have that
\left\|\tilde{{\bm{g}}}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|{\bm{g}}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}
because
\bm{R}
is an orthogonal matrix. Then
\tilde{v}_{t}=v_{t}
and the derivation is similar.

For
\mathtt{Adam}
and
\mathtt{SignGD}
, it is easy to show by induction they are equivariant w.r.t. any permutating transformation because the operation on gradient is performed on each coordinate separately. We only need to show they are not equivariant w.r.t. a rotating transformation. We choose
\bm{R}=[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}};\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}]
,
L_{t}({\bm{x}})=L({\bm{x}})=2x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}
. Due to the update rule of
\mathtt{SignGD}
, it can only update
{\bm{x}}
and
\tilde{{\bm{x}}}
in the direction of
[1,1]
and
[1,-1]
. But when rotating the update direction on
\tilde{{\bm{x}}}
back to the space of
{\bm{x}}
. The update direction can only be
[1,0]
or
[0,1]
that are different from the update direction in the original space. Because the first step in
\mathtt{Adam}
takes the same direction in
\mathtt{SignGD}
, we simultaneously show that both
\mathtt{SignGD}
and
\mathtt{Adam}
are not rotation-equivariant.
∎

From

\displaystyle H_{b}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{\Delta}}}\frac{\left\|\nabla_{(b)}L({\bm{x}}+{\bm{\Delta}})-\nabla_{(b)}L({\bm{x}})\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d_{b}}\max_{b^{\prime}\in[B]}\frac{\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}_{(b^{\prime})}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d_{b^{\prime}}}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{\Delta}}}\frac{\left\|\sum_{b^{\prime}=1}^{B}\nabla_{(b),(b^{\prime})}^{2}L({\bm{x}}){\bm{\Delta}}_{(b^{\prime})}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d_{b}}\max_{b^{\prime}\in[B]}\frac{\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}_{(b^{\prime})}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d_{b^{\prime}}}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{{\bm{x}},\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}_{(b^{\prime})}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d_{b^{\prime}}},\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}^{\prime}_{(b)}\right\|_{2}\leq 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{b}}}\left\langle{\bm{\Delta}}^{\prime}_{(b)},\sum_{b^{\prime}=1}^{B}\nabla_{(b),(b^{\prime})}^{2}L({\bm{x}}){\bm{\Delta}}_{(b^{\prime})}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{\Delta}},{\bm{\Delta}}^{\prime}}\sum_{b^{\prime}=1}^{B}\frac{\sqrt{d_{b^{\prime}}}}{\sqrt{d_{b}}\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}^{\prime}_{(b)}\right\|_{2}\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}_{(b^{\prime})}\right\|_{2}}\left\langle{\bm{\Delta}}^{\prime}_{(b)},\nabla_{(b),(b^{\prime})}^{2}L({\bm{x}}){\bm{\Delta}}_{(b^{\prime})}\right\rangle.

Then for any
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{\Delta}}

D.1
\mathtt{Adam}
on a rotated loss

A key difficulty in implementing
\mathtt{rotated\ Adam}
arises from applying an orthogonal rotation on the parameters before calculating the loss. It is computationally infeasible to apply a 125M
\times
125M orthogonal matrix on the 125M-sized parameter vector. To avoid such computation, we design a new orthogonal transformer to rotate the parameters of the network. In what follows, we elaborate on this rotation.

\mathtt{RandPerm}
.

Given a vector
v
of size
d
, we can orthogonally rotate it by repeatedly applying these consecutive operations: 1. Permute the entries of the vector according to a randomly chosen permutation
\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{d}
. 2. Reshape the permuted vector into a 3D tensor of size
[s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}]
, apply a fixed orthogonal rotation of size
s\times s
on each side of the tensor and then reshape it back to a vector of size
d
.

This operation performs an orthogonal transformation
\mathcal{R}
on the input vector
v
. We can chain multiple operations of this kind and construct
\text{$\mathtt{RandPerm}$ }^{k}
, where
k
is a positive number indicating the number of consecutive
\mathtt{RandPerm}
s applied. Building upon this rotation, we train GPT-2 125M with
\mathtt{Adam}
on
L\circ\text{$\mathtt{RandPerm}$ }^{2}
to analyze our hypothesis regarding the
\ell_{\infty}
geometry of the loss landscape and to verify that
\mathtt{Adam}
will indeed suffer from the induced orthogonal equivariance.
Figure

1
confirms our findings, as the performance of
\mathtt{rotated\ Adam}
with
\text{$\mathtt{RandPerm}$ }^{2}
is significantly worse than
\mathtt{Adam}
. This suggests that
\mathtt{Adam}
is highly sensitive to the rotation and adaptivity alone can’t explain its advantage.

D.2
Computation of matrix norms

As mentioned in
Section

3.3
, it is computationally infeasible to get the full Hessian matrix and directly compute norms of it. Instead we leverage Hessian vector product function in Jax to probe the Hessian matrix. We use Lanczos algorithm to estimate spectral norm.

We propose
Algorithm

4
to estimate the sum of absolute values for each row and sum over all the rows to get
(1,1)
-norm of Hessian matrix. We first subsample a fixed batch of training data for estimating the
(1,1)
-norm of Hessian matrix. The high-level idea is to compute the matrix vector products between Hessian of training loss on this batch and a sequence of random Cauchy vectors. Then we take the
\ell_{1}
norm of the coordinate-wise median of the resulting sequence of Hessian vector products. Because the Cauchy distribution is 1-stable, the resulting product is also a vector of Cauchy random variables, and the magnitude of each element equals to
\ell_{1}
norm of the corresponding row of the Hessian. Thus with infinitely many samples, the
\ell_{1}
norm of the coordinate-wise median converges almost surely to the
(1,1)
-norm of the Hessian.

We choose
n=200
for the measurement experiments on GPT-2 and
n=50
for the measurement experiments on ResNet18.
We also prove a non-asymptotic high-probability multiplicative bound for the estimation error which depends mildly on the dimension
d
in
Theorem

4.1
.

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}P\left(\left|\textup{median}(X_{j,1},\cdots,X_{j,n})-\frac{\pi}{4}\right|>\Delta\frac{\pi}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2d\exp{(-\frac{n\Delta^{2}}{2})}

where we apply
Lemma

D.1
in the last step.

For the second part, we first know that
\textup{median}(X_{j,1},\cdots,X_{j,n})-\frac{\pi}{4}
is sub-Gaussian variable with
\sigma^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{16n}
from
Lemma

D.1
. Then
f(\textup{median}(X_{j,1},\cdots,X_{j,n}))-\mathbb{E}f(\textup{median}(X_{j,1},\cdots,X_{j,n}))
is sub-Gaussian variable with
\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{\cos^{4}((1+\Delta)\frac{\pi}{4})}\frac{\pi^{2}}{16n}
because
f^{\prime}(x)\leq\frac{1}{\cos^{2}((1+\Delta)\frac{\pi}{4})}
. And
\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}f(\textup{median}(X_{j,1},\cdots,X_{j,n}))-\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}\mathbb{E}f(\textup{median}(X_{j,1},\cdots,X_{j,n}))
is sub-Gaussian variable with
\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{\cos^{4}((1+\Delta)\frac{\pi}{4})}\frac{\pi^{2}}{16n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}\right)^{2}
.

When
n\geq\frac{\pi^{3}}{2\epsilon^{2}\cos^{4}((1+\Delta)\frac{\pi}{4})}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}f(\textup{median}(X_{j,1},\cdots,X_{j,n}))-1\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left|f(\textup{median}(X_{j,1},\cdots,X_{j,n}))-f(\frac{\pi}{4})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{x}f^{\prime}(x)\mathbb{E}\left|\textup{median}(X_{j,1},\cdots,X_{j,n})-\frac{\pi}{4}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\cos^{2}((1+\Delta)\frac{\pi}{4})}\sqrt{2\pi}\frac{\pi}{4\sqrt{n}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}

and

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}f(\textup{median}(X_{j,1},\cdots,X_{j,n}))-\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}\right|>\epsilon\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}f(\textup{median}(X_{j,1},\cdots,X_{j,n}))-\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}\mathbb{E}f(\textup{median}(X_{j,1},\cdots,X_{j,n}))\right|>\frac{\epsilon}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}P\left(\left|\mathbb{E}f(\textup{median}(X_{j,1},\cdots,X_{j,n}))-1\right|>\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp{\left(-\frac{2n\epsilon^{2}\cos^{4}((1+\Delta)\frac{\pi}{4})}{\pi^{2}}\right)}.

Combining these two parts, we can get that

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}\textup{median}(\tan(X_{j,1}),\cdots,\tan(X_{j,n}))-\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}\right|\geq\epsilon\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2d\exp(-\frac{n\Delta^{2}}{2})+2\exp{\left(-\frac{2n\epsilon^{2}\cos^{4}((1+\Delta)\frac{\pi}{4})}{\pi^{2}}\right)}

for
n\geq\frac{\pi^{3}}{2\epsilon^{2}\cos^{4}((1+\Delta)\frac{\pi}{4})}
.
∎
