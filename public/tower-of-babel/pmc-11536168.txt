AMBRA1 controls the translation of immune-specific genes in T lymphocytes
Authors: Simone Gottlieb, Wanjing Shang, Deji Ye, Satoshi Kubo, Ping Du Jiang, Samantha Shafer, Leilei Xu, Lixin Zheng, Ann Y. Park, Jian Song, Waipan Chan, Zhiqin Zeng, Tingyan He, Benjamin Schwarz, Björn Häupl, Thomas Oellerich, Michael J. Lenardo, Yikun Yao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceUnderstanding the molecular control of protein expression after TCR stimulation, including the role of translation, is crucial for developing immunological treatments for autoimmune diseases and cancers. In this study, we identified an unexpected role for the AMBRA1 gene in the translational control of TCR-induced pathways and T cell death signaling including the FAS receptor. Our findings suggest possible directions for studying the translational control of TCR signaling.

Upon encountering an antigenic stimulus, T cells transition from an inactive state to an active one by reconfiguring their gene expression and metabolic processes. Activated T cells intensify their transcriptional and translational activity, rapidly initiate glycolysis to generate the energy and essential components that facilitate T cell proliferation, and differentiate into effector or memory T cells (1–3). Most abundant proteins are typically expressed through a combination of transcription, translation, and posttranslational modification (4). A few studies suggest that regulation of cellular protein abundance occurs predominantly at the translational level (5–7). While comprehensive reprogramming of activated T cells is being investigated through epigenomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analyses, the dynamics of protein translation control in T cells remain less explored (2, 8–10). Nonetheless, it is known that T cell receptor (TCR) stimulation leads to the phosphorylation and activation of PI3K, which subsequently activates AKT Serine/Threonine Kinase 1 (AKT1) and inhibits tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (TSC2) (11). Mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) then forms and promotes phosphorylation and later dissociation of eukaryotic initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1), a negative translation regulator, from eukaryotic initiation factor 4E (eIF4E). The free eIF4E then binds to the mRNA 5’ cap structure along with eIF4F and eIF4G, which then recruits ribosomes and tRNA to promote protein synthesis (12, 13). While amino acid uptake has been reported to increase rapidly after T cell activation, mRNA levels have not demonstrated the same degree of increase, suggesting that translation, rather than transcription, may drive a significant portion of the expanded protein synthesis in activated T cells (12, 14, 15). However, other factors contributing to increased protein translation in response to T cell activation remain largely unexplored.

The “Activating molecule in BECN1-regulated autophagy protein 1” (AMBRA1) protein is a phylogenetically conserved adapter protein that helps initiate autophagy, a cellular process in which unnecessary or damaged cell components are degraded and recycled (16). Under basal conditions, AMBRA1 is bound in an inactive state with beclin-1 (BECN1) and class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3KIII) to dynein light chain 1 of the dynein motor complex. After induction of autophagy, AMBRA1 is phosphorylated by unc-51-like autophagy activating kinase 1 (ULK1) and subsequently relocates to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), along with BECN1 and VPS34 (PI3KIII), to assist in autophagosome formation (17). The resulting autophagosome fuses with a lysosome to create an autolysosome, allowing engulfed cellular components to be degraded by hydrolytic enzymes (18). Additionally, AMBRA1 functions as a tumor-suppressor gene that helps maintain the G1 to S phase transition by regulating D-type cyclins (19–21). AMBRA1 helps recruit phosphorylated cyclin D to the E3 ligase, composed of Ring-Box 1 (RBX1), Cullin-4 (CUL4), and DNA damage-binding protein 1 (DDB1), which ultimately results in the ubiquitination and degradation of cyclin D (19–21). While AMBRA1 has been well studied within the contexts of autophagy and the cell cycle, its full function in lymphocytes or their regulatory processes is unknown.

Programmed cell death is crucial in maintaining cell homeostasis (22). Defects in cell death can lead to diverse pathological conditions, including neurodegenerative diseases, autoimmune diseases, and cancer (22, 23). In the canonical FAS pathway, the FAS receptor binds to FAS ligand (FASL), and then, the FAS-associated death domain (FADD) adapter molecule and procaspase-8 or -10 are recruited. The formation of these molecules into the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC) leads to the cleavage of procaspase-8 and -10, which in turn cleave downstream substrates to ultimately result in cell death (24, 25). While defects in genes within the FAS pathway, including FAS, FASL, and CASP10 (25, 26), account for approximately 67% of patients with the autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome (ALPS), the remaining patients with ALPS-like symptoms have no identified genetic mutations, indicating that there are potentially still unknown genes involved in the regulation of the FAS signaling pathway (26).

Here, we report that AMBRA1 was detected in a CRISPR screen for regulators of FAS-induced cell death in T lymphocytes. Our investigation revealed that AMBRA1 knockout (KO) T cells resist FAS-mediated cell death by translationally regulating FAS protein expression. We also reveal a role for AMBRA1 as an inducible translational control protein that controls ribosome protein expression to balance the TCR signaling pathways, cell cycle progression, and the death of T cells.

Results

Whole-Genomic CRISPR Screen Reveals that AMBRA1 Promotes FAS-Induced Cell Death.

To identify genes that mediate FAS-induced cell death, we performed a whole-genomic CRISPR library screen using FAS-stimulated death selection in Jurkat T cells (Fig. 1A). To demonstrate proof of concept for our screening method, we generated nontargeting control and FAS KO cell lines. As compared to normal control (NC), we observed more surviving viable cells in the FAS KO group after stimulation with either anti-FAS antibodies or FAS ligand (FASL) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Additionally, the CRISPR library transduction produced more live cells following death selection (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B), suggesting that the deficiency of certain genes increases the number of cells surviving FAS-induced death. Higher doses of FAS stimulation increased the selection pressure and reduced guide RNA counts (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Those observations demonstrated that our screening was working properly. As expected, we observed an enrichment of small guide RNAs (sgRNAs) indicating deletion of the FAS, FADD, and CASP8 genes after anti-FAS or FASL treatment (Fig. 1 B and C). Another highly ranked sgRNA selected was AMBRA1 (Fig. 1C) (19–21). To confirm hits, we transduced top candidate sgRNAs into Jurkat T cells to generate stable gene KO cell lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). After anti-FAS antibody or FAS ligand (FASL) treatments, we calculated the relative cell survival fold-change. FAS and BCL2 antagonist/killer 1 (BAK1), a proapoptotic protein localized to mitochondria, KO produced a strong resistance to FAS-induced cell death compared to NC sgRNA groups (Fig. 1D). Many other candidate genes from the screen were verified to resist cell death and, notably, despite differences, some genes conferring resistance against death were shared between anti-FAS antibodies and FASL treatments. However, AMBRA1 sgRNAs conferred clear protection against cell death induced by either stimulus (Fig. 1D). We confirmed that deletion of AMBRA1 using two different guide RNAs (gRNAs) increased cell survival after FAS engagement in both Jurkat (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A) and human primary T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). We reintroduced FAS into AMBRA1 KO cells. We found that reintroducing FAS can restore cell death to a level even comparable to that of the wild-type cells. Therefore, AMBRA1 predominantly regulates FAS-mediated cell death by controlling FAS synthesis, with little to no effect on other proteins involved in the cell death pathway, or at least to a much lesser extent than on FAS (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C).

Loss of AMBRA1 Leads to the Reduction of FAS Protein Expression.

We also determined that AMBRA1 was the only resistance gene we found whose KO reduced FAS cell surface expression besides KO of the FAS gene itself (Fig. 2A). We therefore hypothesized that AMBRA1 regulates FAS death by facilitating FAS receptor expression. Immunoblotting and flow cytometry revealed that both surface and intracellular FAS expression decreased with AMBRA1 KO in Jurkat cells or either primary T cells (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D and E). By contrast, knockout of autophagy regulator BECN1 did not reduce FAS expression (Fig. 2C), implying that this phenomenon relates to AMBRA1 but not general autophagy. Furthermore, reintroduction of AMBRA1 by overexpression (OE) boosted FAS expression beyond the normal control (NC) (Fig. 2D). Taken together, our data confirm that AMBRA1 controls a rate-limiting step in FAS protein expression, which is likely why AMBRA1 KO cells resist FAS-mediated cell death.

AMBRA1 does not Control the FAS Protein through mRNA Levels or Protein Degradation.

We next investigated FAS mRNA levels and found that qPCR revealed no difference in FAS mRNA levels (measured by three different FAS-specific primers), upon KO or overexpression of AMBRA1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2F). The mRNA half-life, as determined by Actinomycin D treatment, was also unchanged between control and AMBRA1 KO cells (Fig. 2E). We then tested whether AMBRA1 controlled FAS protein degradation. We applied cycloheximide (CHX) at different time points to inhibit protein synthesis and then quantified the degradation of surface and intracellular FAS. We observed no differences in degradation rates between control, AMBRA1 KO, or BECN1 KO cells (Fig. 2F). Moreover, treatment (tx) with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 or autolysosome/lysosome inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) did not rescue FAS surface expression in AMBRA1 KO cells (Fig. 2 G and H). Thus, loss of AMBRA1 does not cause a decrease in FAS protein expression by altering mRNA levels, protein degradation, or autophagy.

AMBRA1 Controls Translation of FAS mRNA.

We next hypothesized that AMBRA1 might control FAS mRNA translation. For these investigations, we applied CHX to cells for 1 h to arrest protein synthesis, washed it away, and then measured the recovery of FAS translation in fresh medium at different time points (Fig. 3A). This approach showed clearly that recovery of FAS protein translation was reduced at all time points in AMBRA1 KO cells compared to NC or BECN1 KO cells. We next used assays designed to quantify newly synthesized proteins. AMBRA1 KO and control Jurkat cells were incubated in methionine-free medium along with homopropargylglycine (HPG), a methionine analogue with an azide (27), to incorporate HPG into newly synthesized proteins (Fig. 3B). Click chemistry enabled the conjugation of HPG with tetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA) alkyne. We then performed immunoprecipitations using FAS antibodies and blotted with anti-TAMRA antibody. We found that AMBRA1 KO cells had a striking reduction in FAS signal compared to control cells (Fig. 3B). Since our HPG incorporation analyses suggested that AMBRA1 may control FAS protein translation, we performed polysome profiling to investigate whether AMBRA1 associates with ribosomal subunit proteins or polysomes (Fig. 3C). We saw no major differences in absorbance curves between control and AMBRA1 KO Jurkat cells and western blotting revealed AMBRA1 to be mainly present in the soluble and small ribosomal subunit fractions (Fig. 3C). Although we did not see AMBRA1 associate with polysomes, we purified polysome fractions and qPCR showed reduced FAS mRNA in AMBRA1 KO cells, whereas there was little difference in the control genes CXCR4 and ACTB (Fig. 3D). These results indicated that there was less ribosome loading on FAS mRNA in AMBRA1 KO cells, suggesting impaired translation efficiency of FAS mRNA into protein in AMBRA1 KO cells. Given these observations, we measured the levels of FAS mRNA and protein at the naive and activation stage of T cells. We found FAS protein was induced much more strongly than its mRNA after TCR stimulation (Fig. 3E). Thus, these results suggested that AMBRA1-boosted translation is important for full FAS expression after T cell activation.

AMBRA1 Translation Is Increased by TCR Induction of the CD28–PI3K–mTORC1–eIF4F Axis.

We next investigated whether the translation of AMBRA1 was altered after T cell activation. Indeed, blotting revealed that AMBRA1 protein expression is strongly increased beginning at 18 h after stimulation using anti-CD3/CD28 beads (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, qPCR revealed that AMBRA1 mRNA was dramatically reduced rather than increased after TCR engagement (Fig. 4B). The difference between protein and mRNA levels indicated that the translation of AMBRA1 mRNA may be increased 30-fold or more by TCR stimulation. We observed a similar phenomenon in B cells after B cell receptor engagement (Fig. 4 C and D). We also examined whether other genes showed a similar apparent increase in translation. We found a small group of genes that displayed the same expression pattern as AMBRA1 after TCR stimulation (Fig. 4E, blue). Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were involved in cytoplasmic translation (Fig. 4 F and G and Dataset S2).

To elucidate the mechanism of translational regulation, we applied various inhibitors to block signaling proteins downstream of TCR-stimulation pathways (Fig. 4H). Several of these inhibitors, except the MEK inhibitor, significantly decreased AMBRA1 translation, suggesting that TCR signaling pathways are required for AMBRA1 translation. CD28 costimulation during T cell activation is known to drive an early burst in translation through mTOR activity (28, 29). Therefore, we stimulated T cells with anti-CD28 antibodies and found that the CD28 pathway alone could partially induce AMBRA1 translation, and this was blocked by the mTOR inhibitors Torin-1 and rapamycin (Fig. 4I). mTOR is known to regulate translation of mRNAs, mainly translation factors, that have a 5′-terminal oligopyrimidine sequence just after the 7-methylguanosine cap, called TOP mRNAs (30, 31). TOP mRNAs are regulated by mTOR-mediated assembly of the eIF4F initiation complex for translation. We therefore investigated whether AMBRA1 could be translationally regulated through a TOP mRNA motif. Analyzing the nucleotide sequence of the AMBRA1 5’ space untranslated region (UTR), which is largely conserved across species, we found pyrimidine-rich sequences resembling the eIF4F complex recognition element (Fig. 4J). Applying eIF4F complex inhibitor 4EGI-1 caused a reduction in AMBRA1 protein expression and phospho-S6 ribosomal protein abundance after T cell stimulation (Fig. 4K), implying that AMBRA1 protein expression is influenced by the eIF4F complex through a TOP mRNA motif.

To understand further how AMBRA1 is translationally regulated, we designed GFP plasmid constructs with or without the 5’UTR of AMBRA1 (Fig. 4L). Flow cytometry and qPCR revealed no mRNA increase but a significant increase in GFP protein with the addition of the AMBRA1 5’UTR and that 4EGI-1 and Torin-1 reduced GFP expression. These data suggest that AMBRA1 is translationally regulated through its TOP mRNA motif and that the CD28–PI3K–mTORC1–eIF4F axis controls AMBRA1 protein expression (Fig. 4M). Thus, AMBRA1 is a stimulation-inducible translational control circuit that regulates the expression of FAS and other genes during T cell activation.

AMBRA1 Affects Ribosomal Proteins to Control Protein Expression of Genes Downstream of the TCR.

To elucidate further the mechanism of AMBRA1-controlled immune protein expression, we used proximity-dependent biotin identification (BioID) mass spectrometry analysis and found many AMBRA1 interacting proteins were cellular or mitochondrial ribosome proteins (Fig. 5A). Pathway analysis confirmed that the most significant enrichments were in proteins involved in ribosomal RNA (rRNA) metabolism and ribosome complex biogenesis (Fig. 5B). We therefore tested whether ribosomal proteins were reduced in AMBRA1 KO cells. We used “multiplexed enhanced protein dynamic mass spectrometry” (mePROD MS) to analyze Jurkat cells with heavy amino acids incorporated at different time points into newly synthesized proteins (32) (Fig. 5C). Mass spectrometry revealed a reduction in newly synthesized ribosomal proteins in AMBRA1 KO cells at all time points after the heavy amino acid pulse (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–C and Dataset S2). Pathway analysis confirmed that the most significantly affected proteins were involved in ribosome functions (Fig. 5E).

Using a complementary approach, we labeled newly synthesized intracellular and surface proteins with either O-propargyl-puromycin (OPP) or puromycin and performed click chemistry or applied puromycin antibody to detect OPP and puromycin levels, respectively (Fig. 5F). Regardless of the method, flow cytometric analysis revealed less labeling in AMBRA1 KO cells compared to control cells after T cell stimulation (Fig. 5 G and H). CHX addition blocked all labeling, confirming its dependency on new protein synthesis (Fig. 5 G and H). Moreover, mass spectrometry analysis revealed an increased accumulation of free amino acids, such as L-glutamine and L-arginine, in AMBRA1 KO cells compared to control cells both before and after TCR stimulation (Fig. 5I). Taken together, we establish AMBRA1 as a T cell stimulation-inducible translational control circuit which enhances the translation of selective TCR downstream genes and that protein synthesis, especially of proteins related to ribosome biogenesis, is impaired when AMBRA1 is absent.

Additional Immune Proteins are Reduced in the Absence of AMBRA1.

To investigate the global impact of AMBRA1 translational control, we next performed total cell proteomics in control and KO Jurkat cells. Several cell cycle genes, such as CCNB1 and CDKN1B, were found to have about a 1.5- to 2-fold increase in AMBRA1 KO cells (Fig. 6A), likely due to protein stabilization rather than translation given previous studies that established AMBRA1 as a mediator of cyclin D degradation (19–21). TCR signaling proteins such as CD69 and CD28, as well as FAS, were found to be decreased about 1.5- to 2-fold compared to controls such as CXCR4 (Fig. 6 A–C). Pathway analysis showed reduced expression of cell adhesion molecules, and proteins associated with hematopoietic cells, natural killer cell cytotoxicity, TCR signaling, and other pathways; by contrast, up-regulated proteins were associated with cell cycle control (Fig. 6B). As was the case for FAS, we found that the mRNA levels of TCR signaling genes, such as CD69, CD28, PD1, and CTLA4, were not significantly decreased, despite them all having reduced protein expression in the AMBRA1 KO cells (Fig. 6 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). The AMBRA1 KO had no effect on the protein or mRNA of other genes such as CXCR4 (Fig. 6D).

Since previous papers showed that the CUL4 complex regulates cyclin D (CCND) protein stability through E3 activity, we examined several down-regulated proteins in CUL4 KO and AMBRA1/CUL4 double KO cells. The results showed that FAS and CD28 were not rescued in CUL4 KO cells despite showing the expected CCND3 protein increase (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C–E). Further studies showed that both the FAS and CD28 proteins were reduced in the absence of AMBRA1 in human primary T cells and Jurkat T cells (Fig. 6 E and F). We reintroduced AMBRA1 into AMBRA1 KO Jurkat cells and found that reintroducing AMBRA1 can restore the expression of FAS, CD28, and cell death to a level to that of the wild-type cells (Fig. 6F and SI Appendix, Fig. S4F). Moreover, the levels of FAS and CD28 protein expression were correlated to the level of AMBRA1 expression (Fig. 6G). Similar to FAS, reinitiation of CD28 translation after a CHX-washout at different time points was markedly impaired in AMBRA1 KO cells but not in control BECN1 KO cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4G). We also observed reduced CD28 mRNA in AMBRA1 KO polysome fractions (SI Appendix, Fig. S4H). Taken together, our data demonstrate that loss of AMBRA1 has a broad effect on translation of T cell signaling proteins.

Discussion

AMBRA1 has been implicated in the regulation of autophagy and as a regulator responsible for maintaining the stability of all three D-type cyclins. It serves as the substrate receptor for approximately 40 CUL4-RING E3 ubiquitin ligase (CRL4) complexes, facilitating the polyubiquitylation and subsequent degradation of cyclin D proteins. However, AMBRA1 is a very large protein (1,298 amino acids) with a complex domain structure and involvement in many cellular processes. We now provide genetic and biochemical evidence that AMBRA1 positively regulates the expression of many immune-related genes independently of ubiquitin-mediated mechanisms. We find that AMBRA1 functions as a ribosome interactor and translational regulator in T cells that promotes T cell activation and FAS-induced cell death. Although we have tried to investigate the possibility that AMBRA1 might function as an RNA-binding protein (RBP) to bind RNA and thereby regulate the translation of certain specific mRNAs, we did not find any evidence of AMBRA1 having the ability to bind mRNA. Many published studies have also identified RBPs in different cell types using various screen methods, but none have found AMBRA1 to be an RBP (33–36). However, we still cannot rule out the possibility that AMBRA1 is an RBP due to these technical limitations. We also show that AMBRA1 itself can be translationally regulated by TCR signaling through the CD28–PI3K–mTORC1–EIF4F pathway acting upon a TOP motif in its 5’-UTR. Future work will be directed at determining the precise molecular mechanism by which AMBRA1 influences specific mRNAs after T cell activation.

It is well established that after TCR activation, naive T cells rapidly increase their energy production and generate many new mRNA transcripts and proteins. This comprehensive transformation of the cellular transcriptome and proteome is essential for the effector and memory responses crucial for T cell immunity. Prior studies of T cell activation have principally focused on transcriptional regulation; by comparison, translational regulation has been rarely investigated even though studies have indicated its prominent role (1, 6, 13, 37–39). The machinery responsible for ribosomal assembly and protein production increases to support the extensive clonal expansion of pathogen-specific T cells and their differentiation into effector cell subsets. However, following pathogen clearance, the population of effector T cells contracts, while memory T cells persist in a quiescent state, poised for reactivation if reinfection occurs. In this process, the regulation at the protein translation level is also largely unknown. Our results suggest that AMBRA1 plays a unique role as a translational regulator that controls T cell homeostasis.

FAS-induced cell death has been well studied, and key regulators in the FAS pathway have been identified, including FAS, FADD, caspase-8, caspase-10, and others. Autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome (ALPS) is caused by defects in the FAS-mediated cell death pathway and mutations in genes encoding FAS, FADD, and CASP10 can cause ALPS or related disorders. The activation of the FAS pathway primarily involves the assembly of downstream proteins. Our results indicate that AMBRA1 can be induced after T cell activation, thereby enhancing the protein synthesis of the FAS receptor to effectively promote the cell death pathway, ultimately controlling the number of activated T cells by cell death. It will be interesting to determine whether translation control mechanisms represented by AMBRA1 can explain currently unresolved cases of ALPS.

By simultaneously comparing changes in RNA and protein levels after T cell activation, we found that most genes exhibited correlated changes in both RNA and protein levels. However, we now have identified a subset of genes where RNA levels decreased or remained unchanged, while, contemporaneously, the protein levels significantly increased following T cell activation. Clustering analysis of these genes revealed that many are involved in the process of protein translation. Future research should clarify the impact of translational control on T cell immunity. Additionally, further research will be needed to elucidate the mechanism by which AMBRA1 regulates protein translation and explain the varying degrees of translation regulation by AMBRA1 for different proteins.

Materials and Methods

Flow Cytometry.

T cells were harvested and washed once with ice-cold fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (PBS with 0.5% BSA and 2 mM EDTA) at 4 °C as described in ref. 40. The cells were then stained in 50 µL of FACS buffer containing the specified fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies and Fixable Viability Dye (eBioscience Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 780, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat: 65-0865-18) for 30 min at 4 °C. After staining, the cells were washed three times with FACS buffer and either acquired using an LSR II or LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences) or fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. For fixed samples, the cells were washed three times with PBS before acquisition. The following antibodies were used for cell staining: PE anti-human CD3 Antibody (BioLegend, clone HIT3a, cat no. 300307), FITC anti-human CD4 (BioLegend, clone SK3, cat no. 980802), APC-anti-human CD8 (BioLegend, clone SK1, cat no. 980904), APC anti-human FAS (BioLegend, clone DX2, cat no. 305611), BV605 anti-human CD28 (BioLegend, clone CD28.2, cat no. 302967), APC anti-human CD69 (BioLegend, clone FN50, cat no. 310909), BV421 anti-human CXCR4 (BioLegend, clone 12G5, cat no. 306517), PerCP anti-human CTLA-4 (BioLegend, clone BNI3, cat no. 369607), and PE anti-human PD-1 (BioLegend, clone A17188A, cat no. 379209).

For intracellular antigen staining, T cells were harvested and resuspended in cell staining buffer with a concentration of 1 × 106 cells mL−1. Cells were fixed and permeabilized by BD Cytofix/Cytoperm fixation/permeabilization kit (cat: 554714) following the manufacturers’ protocols. The primary antibody was diluted with permeabilization buffer for an optimal working concentration and used to resuspend the fixed/permeabilized cells. Cells were incubated for 15 to 20 min at 4 °C. Cells were centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was removed. Cells were washed with permeabilization buffer and centrifuged at 350×g for 5 min. Cells were resuspended in 200 µL cell staining buffer for final flow cytometric analysis. Results were analyzed using FlowJo 10.4 software.

Western Blot Analysis.

Cells were lysed in NP-40 buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8.0; 1 mM EDTA; 1% NP-40; and 150 mM NaCl) supplemented with Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat: 78439, 78427) as described in ref. 40. The lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C after incubating on ice for 30 min. After collecting the supernatant, and protein concentrations were determined by using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat: 23225). Equal amounts of protein were mixed with NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (4X) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat: NP0008) with Reducing Agent (10X) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat: NP0004). The samples were heated at 70 °C for 10 min, and 25 μg of each protein sample was loaded onto NuPAGE 4 to 12% Bis-Tris Protein Gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat: NP0336BOX) for electrophoresis. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to 0.22 μM PVDF membranes (Millipore, cat: ISEQ00010) and blocked with 5% skim milk. Samples were analyzed using immunoblotting techniques. Antibodies were used: rabbit anti-HSP90 Ab (CST, #2118), rabbit anti-HA-Tag (C29F4) Ab (CST, #3724), anti-AMBRA1 (CST, #24907), anti-HA tag (CST, clone 6E2), anti-CD28 (CST, #38774), anti-FAS rabbit polyclonal (cat no. L8919; Sigma-Aldrich), anti-FAS (CST, #8023S), anti-TAMRA (PA5-60858; Thermo Fischer Scientific), anti-human HSP90 (HPA019137; Sigma-Aldrich), anti-human GAPDH (HPA020268; Sigma-Aldrich), anti-RPL8 (clone BT7R; Life Technologies), anti-human RPS26 antibodies (Proteintech, 14909-1-AP), anti-human RPL8 (Abcam, ab169538), anti-human p-S6 antibody (CST, #4858), goat anti-mouse (murine) IgG, goat anti-rabbit IgG, and goat anti-rat IgG (all from Li-COR Biosciences). Protein bands were visualized using the fluorescent imager Azure 500 (Azure Biosystems).

Lentivirus Production and Infection.

On day 0, Lenti-X 293 T cells (Takara, cat: 632180) were plated in either 10 cm dishes or 6-well plates. On day 1, cells that reached 60 to 70% confluence were transfected with Lipofectamine 3000 Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat: L3000075) following the manufacturer’s online protocol. Specifically, for each well of the 6-well plate, a mixture of 1,250 ng psPAX2, 125 ng pMD2.G (Addgene, cat: 12259, 12260), along with 1,250 ng 3rd generation lentivirus backbone plasmids of the transfer plasmid was used. For the 10 cm dishes, 1.1 μg of pMD2.G, 11 μg of psPAX2, and 11 μg backbone plasmids were employed. Cells were incubated for 48 h, after which the supernatants were collected. The supernatants were either briefly centrifuged (500×g for 10 min) or filtered through a 0.45 µm filter. One volume of Lenti-X Concentrator (Takara, cat: 631232) was added to three volumes of the clarified supernatant. The mixture was gently inverted to ensure thorough mixing and then incubated at 4 °C overnight. The following day, the mixture was centrifuged at 1,500×g for 45 min at 4 °C, and any remaining liquid was removed by a quick spin at 1,500×g. Following centrifugation, an off-white pellet became visible. The pellet was gently resuspended in complete DMEM or RPMI 1,640 medium at 1/20 of the original volume. The viruses were then stored at –80 °C in single-use aliquots.

Human Primary T Cell Culture.

Blood from anonymous healthy donors was obtained at the NIH Clinical Center under approved protocols. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare) density gradient centrifugation and washed twice in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Primary T cells were further isolated with the Pan T Cell Isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec) and resuspended at 106 mL−1 in complete RPMI 1640 (cRPMI) medium. Cells were activated with Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated Gene Knockout in Primary T Cells.

For lentivirus-mediated gene knockout, human primary T cells were isolated as described above and activated with Dynabeads Human T-activator CD3/CD28. After 24 h, T cells were transduced by resuspension to 5 × 105 cells mL−1 in cRPMI with 100 U mL−1 rhIL-2, 10 μg mL−1 of polybrene and 100 μL concentrated viral supernatant per 5 × 105 cells, followed by centrifugation (2,000 rpm, 90 min, 32 °C). Then, 72 h after transduction, cells were purified by resuspension in cRPMI with 1 μg mL−1 of puromycin for 72 h.

For crRNAs–tracrRNA–Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) electroporation-mediated gene knockout, each crRNA and tracrRNA (Integrated DNA Technologies, cat. no. 1073191) was reconstituted to 100 μM with Nuclease-Free Duplex Buffer (Integrated DNA Technologies). These were then mixed at equimolar concentrations in a sterile PCR tube. Mixed oligonucleotides were annealed by heating at 95 °C for 5 min in a PCR thermocycler. After cooling of complexes to 24 °C, 9 μL of three crRNA–tracrRNA complexes (3 μL equal to 150 pmol each), 4.5 μL Cas9 protein (30 μg per 180 pmol, MacroLab Facility) and 1.5 μL nuclease-free duplex buffer were gently triturated and incubated at 23 °C for 10 min. Then, 10 × 106 purified human primary T cells were resuspended in 20 μL of P2 buffer (P2 Primary Cell 4D-NucleofectorTM X kit L, Lonza). T cells were added to sterile PCR tubes containing the RNP mixture and then transferred to nucleofection cuvettes (4D-Nucleofector X kit L, Lonza). Electroporation of the T cells was performed using a 4D-Nucleofector (Lonza) with the EH100 program for resting human T cells. T cells were transferred to 6-well plates containing prewarmed cRPMI. Dead cells were removed using the dead cell removal kit (Miltenyi Biotec) after 24 h. Transfection efficiency, exceeding 90%, was confirmed by assessing a fluorescently tagged crRNA control (IDT, cat. no. 1077024) after 24 h.

CRISPR-Cas9 Screening.

The genome-scale sgRNA library lentiCRISPR v2 (#52961) was purchased from Addgene. Cas9-expressing Jurkat T cells, described previously (41, 42), were cultured in RPMI. Cells were infected with the pooled lentiviral library at an MOI of 0.3 to ensure one gene was knocked out in one cell. To ensure comprehensive representation of the library sgRNA sequences in the transduced cell population, the library coverage was set to approximately 500 transduced cells per sgRNA (43). The transduced cells were selected for 5 d with puromycin (1 μg mL−1). For the genome-wide screen, cells were treated with low [10 ng mL−1 anti-FAS with cross-linker (0.5 µg mL−1 protein A)], middle [30 ng mL−1 anti-FAS with cross-linker (0.5 µg mL−1 protein A)], or high [100 ng mL−1 anti-FAS with cross-linker (0.5 µg mL−1 protein A)] doses of anti-FAS treatment, or 100 ng mL−1 FAS ligand (FASL). Surviving cells were maintained until there were enough cells for NGS sgRNA sequencing.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated Gene Knockout in Cell Lines.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout in Jurkat cell line was described in ref. 40. AMBRA1-specific sgRNAs were designed by using Benchling (https://www.benchling.com) and then inserted into the sgRNA expression vector lentiGuide-Puro (Addgene, cat: 52963). Cloning was performed essentially as described in ref. 44. The lentiGuide-Puro vector was first digested with BsmBI and purified by gel electrophoresis. sgRNA oligos obtained from IDT were annealed by mixing 1 µL of 10× T4 Ligation Buffer, 1 µL of 100 µM forward oligo, 1 µL of 100 µM reverse oligo, 0.5 µL of T4 PNK (NEB, cat: M0201), and 6.5 µL of nuclease-free water. This mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by a slow cooling to 25 °C at a rate of 5 °C per minute. The annealed oligos were then ligated into the digested lentiGuide-Puro vector by combining 1 µL of 20x diluted annealed oligos, 50 ng of digested vector, 1 µL of 10× T4 Ligation Buffer, and 1 µL of NEB T4 DNA ligase (NEB M0202), adjusting the total volume to 10 µL with nuclease-free water. This reaction was incubated at room temperature for 18 h. The plasmids were validated by Sanger sequencing (Genewiz), and all sgRNA sequences are available in Dataset S1.

For the infection process, lentiCas9-Blast (Addgene, cat: 52962) and lentiGuide-Puro-sgRNA lentiviral particles were produced using the Lenti-X 293 T Cell Line as described in the Lentivirus production and infection protocol. Concentrated lentiCas9-Blast virus was added to Jurkat cells to generate stable Cas9 expression cell lines. Cells were incubated overnight in growth medium containing the virus and polybrene (10 µg mL−1, Sigma-Aldrich, cat: TR-1003). The next day, the viral medium was replaced with fresh growth medium. After 72 h postinfection, cell selection was initiated using 5 µg mL−1 Blasticidin S HCl (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat: A1113903). Afterward, lentiGuide-Puro-sgRNA lentiviral particles were added, and selection was performed with 2 µg mL−1 puromycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat: A1113803) starting 72 h postinfection to generate AMBRA1 KO cells.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-time PCR (RT-qPCR).

Total RNA was extracted from isolated human T cells and in vitro-cultured Jurkat cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, cat: 15596026) or the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, cat: 74136), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, cat: RR037A) at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by deactivation at 85 °C for 5 s. QPCR was conducted using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat: A25777) in a StepOnePlus detection system (Applied Biosystems). The fold change in mRNA expression between treatment groups was calculated using the ΔΔCt method. β-actin (ACTB), GAPDH, or HSP90 served as human internal controls.

mRNA-seq analysis was described in ref. 40. Briefly, sample reads were removed adapter sequences and low-quality bases. The cleaned reads were then aligned to the GRCh38.p13 reference genome and its associated transcript annotations using STAR. Read counts were obtained with HTSeq, using the parameters -m union, -stranded = reverse, and the mm10 genes.gtf file from UCSC. Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 within R. Genes with fewer than 10 counts across all samples were excluded from further analysis. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plots were created using the rlog function in DESeq2. Normalization was carried out using DESeq2’s default settings, and results were adjusted with the lfcShrink function. Volcano plots were generated using the EnhancedVolcano package, while heatmaps were created with pheatmap using the normalized count data from DESeq2. To create bigwig files, BAM files were first indexed with samtools using default settings, and bigwig files were generated with the bamCoverage function from deepTools. For pathway analysis, genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and a log2 fold change (log2FC) >1 or <−1 were selected. Up-regulated and down-regulated genes were input separately into Enrichr (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) (45).

Polyribosome Profiling/polysome Profiling.

A total of 5 × 106 Jurkat cells from either the NC or KO groups were cultured to 70 to 80% confluence and treated with 100 μg mL−1 cycloheximide (MilliporeSigma, catalog number: C1988) at 37 °C for 3 min to inhibit translation elongation. The cells were then washed twice with PBS, collected, and lysed using a cold polysome lysis buffer composed of 200 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 50 mM KCl, 1% Triton X-100, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 0.5% (w/v) sodium deoxycholate, 400 U mL−1 RNaseOUT recombinant RNase inhibitor (Invitrogen, 10777019), 100 μg mL−1 cycloheximide, an EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (MilliporeSigma, 4693159001), and Phosphatase Inhibitor Mini Tablets (Pierce, A32957). The lysate was incubated on ice with intermittent vortexing, followed by two centrifugation steps at 2,000×g and >13,000×g to collect the supernatant and remove debris and nuclei, respectively. The supernatant was loaded onto 7 to 47% sucrose gradients, which were subjected to gradient centrifugation at 39,000 rpm for 90 min at 4 °C using a Beckman Coulter Optima L-90 K equipped with an SW 41 rotor. After centrifugation, the sucrose gradient was fractionated using a density gradient fractionation system, which included a tube piercer and UA-6 detector, with fractions collected in 2 mL RNase-free microcentrifuge tubes. UV absorbance at 260 nm was continuously monitored during fractionation to assess RNA content. Each fraction collected underwent RNA extraction utilizing TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, catalog number: 15596026). The extraction procedure included chloroform extraction, isopropanol precipitation, and rinsing the RNA pellet with 70% ethanol, following the protocol provided by Invitrogen. The RNA pellets were allowed to air-dry and then resuspended in RNase-free water. The integrity and quantity of the RNA were assessed using the RNA Integrity Number (RIN) methodology with an Agilent Bioanalyzer, ensuring the RNA met the quality standards for subsequent applications. The targeted proteins RPL8, RPS26, and AMBRA1 were examined in each fraction through western blot analysis.

Homopropargylglycine (HPG) Pulse-labeling Assay.

NC and KO Jurkat cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. The cells were harvested and rinsed with PBS, followed by incubation in methionine-free RPMI medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific #A1451701) containing 5% FBS for 40 min to deplete methionine. After that, cells were treated with 40 μM HPG (Thermo Fisher Scientific #C10186) in L-methionine-free DMEM for 1 h to allow for standard labeling. Following the labeling process, cells were washed with PBS to remove any unbound HPG before being returned to complete normal medium. For immunoprecipitation (IP), cells were collected in a lysis buffer consisting of 1% SDS in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). During treatments of varying durations, each lysate was kept on ice until the final time point, at which all samples were processed concurrently for IP. The IP procedure was conducted using Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #10004D) and an antibody targeting FAS (CST, cat #8023). FAS protein that was immunoprecipitated was subsequently labeled with Tetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA) Azide (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #T10182) using a copper-catalyzed reaction between Azide and HPG, facilitated by the Click-iT reaction buffer kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #C10276). The presence of TAMRA Azide on the protein was detected using standard immunoblotting with an anti-TAMRA antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #MA1-041). It should be noted that the molecular weight of the detected protein is expected to be slightly higher than predicted, as it includes the addition of TAMRA Azide (555 Da) multiplied by the number of methionine residues in the labeled protein. Consequently, this labeled protein may not be recognized by a regular antibody due to the presence of integrated TAMRA Azide. Negative controls were included by omitting HPG from the samples.

Proximity-dependent Biotinylation (BioID) Interaction Protein Screen.

BioID and mass spectrometry protocols were performed as described in a prior publication (46). In summary, Jurkat cells were stably transfected using a lentiviral system containing the Flag-BirA-Ambra1 bait construct (Addgene #35700), which facilitated subsequent protein interaction analyses. Cells were harvested from 175 mm culture flasks, and the resulting cell pellets were resuspended and lysed in ice-cold modified RIPA buffer supplemented with MgCl2. The buffer formulation included 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EGTA, 0.1% SDS, protease inhibitors, and 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, prepared at a ratio of 1:10 (pellet weight to buffer volume). Cells were treated with 1 µL of benzonase and incubated at 4 °C for 20 min while gently agitated. After incubation, the lysates were subjected to brief sonication. Then, the lysates were centrifuged at 14,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C to pellet cellular debris. The resulting supernatants, which contained the biotinylated proteins, were collected for further purification. Subsequently, these supernatants were incubated with streptavidin-sepharose beads (GE Healthcare) for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation to facilitate the capture of biotinylated proteins. Following affinity purification, the beads were extensively washed with modified RIPA buffer containing 2 M urea and 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. The captured proteins were then digested directly on the beads using a trypsin/Lys-C enzyme mixture (Promega, V5072). The initial digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C. After digestion, the peptides were acidified with formic acid to a final concentration of 2% and concentrated using a SpeedVac concentrator. The resulting dried peptides were stored at −80 °C until they were ready for mass spectrometry analysis.

Multiplexed Enhanced Protein Dynamics (mePROD) Proteomics.

For the mePROD experiment, we adhered to the protocol outlined in ref. 32. In brief, cells were collected and then washed two times with prewarmed PBS to remove any remaining nonheavy amino acids. The cells were then incubated in SILAC medium (GIBCO #88365) with the same concentration of compounds as the regular medium for 2, 5, 10, and 24 h. After incubation, the cells were lysed in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 2% SDS, 150 mM NaCl, a protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (Roche), and a phosphatase inhibitor tablet (Roche). The lysates were heated at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by sonication using a Sonic Vibra Cell with 1-s ON/1-s OFF pulses for about 30 s at a 30% amplitude to shear genomic DNA. After sonication, samples were further incubated at 95 °C for 10 min. The prepared lysates were then processed for LC-MS/MS analysis.

The Surface Sensing of Translation assay (SUnSET).

The Surface Sensing of Translation assay (SUnSET) was performed on four experimental groups: NC and KO, each subjected to treatment with either cycloheximide (CHX) or a stimulant. Jurkat cells were harvested and incubated with 10 µg mL−1 of either OPP (Protein Synthesis Assay Kit, cat: 601100) or puromycin (Sigma #P8833) for 10 min at 37 °C. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with RPMI complete medium. Cells were resuspended in 150 µL of prewarmed RPMI complete medium and incubated for 50 min at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were then collected by centrifugation at 4 °C and washed twice with cold PBS supplemented with 0.1% BSA (PBS/BSA). For OPP labeling, cells were handled according to the instructions of Protein Synthesis Assay Kit. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed in 2 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 40 min, followed by washing with 1 × Permeabilization Buffer (Thermo, 00-8333-56). After centrifugation at 400 g for 5 min at room temperature (RT), either OPP or AF647-labeled mouse IgG2a anti-puromycin antibody (clone#12D10, Sigma, MABE343-AF647) was added, and cells were incubated for 1 h at RT in the dark. Finally, the cells were washed, resuspended in FACS buffer, and analyzed using flow cytometry. For surface staining, cells were resuspended in 30 µL of cold PBS/BSA containing OPP or 1 µg of AF647-labeled mouse IgG2a anti-puromycin antibody (clone 12D10) and incubated on ice for 30 min. For OPP labeling, the cells were handled according to the instructions of Protein Synthesis Assay Kit. The cells were then washed twice with 150 µL of PBS/BSA, fixed by resuspending in 40 µL of 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution (diluted in PBS/BSA), and stored at 4 °C until flow cytometry analysis.

Cytosol Amino Acid Measurement.

NC or KO Jurkat cells, either stimulated with anti-CD3 or left unstimulated, were collected and washed with PBS. The cells were subsequently lysed, and the cytosolic fraction was obtained by centrifuging the postnuclear supernatant (PNS) at 16,000×g for 3 min at 4 °C to pellet all organelles. The resulting supernatant was then combined with an equal volume of 100% LC-MS grade methanol. For LC-MS analysis, each sample was prepared by adding 5 µL of an isotopically labeled standard mixture. Amino acids were assessed by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry according to published methods as part of a larger targeted metabolite profiling method (47). Data were total sum normalized prior to analysis.

Statistical Analysis.

The graph preparation and statistical analysis of data (Student’s t test or two-way ANOVA) were performed by Prism 10 software (GraphPad) or Excel (Microsoft). The statistical significance cutoff was set at P < 0.05.
