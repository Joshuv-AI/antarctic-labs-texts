Effect of salicylic acid and silicon on physiological parameters and anatomical index of Scrophularia striata L. under drought stress
Authors: Fariba Shohani, Siavash Hosseini Sarghien, Nicolas L. Taylor, Arash Fazeli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drought stress is a major environmental constraint that limits plant growth and alters secondary metabolism, particularly in medicinal plants. This study investigated the combined effects of drought stress (50% and 100% field capacity), salicylic acid (SA; 0 and 100 ppm), and silicon (Si; 0 and 1 g L⁻1) on the physiological, anatomical, and phytochemical responses of Scrophularia striata roots in two ecotypes (Ilam and Abdanan) under greenhouse conditions. A factorial experiment based on a completely randomized design with three replications was employed. Results showed that drought stress significantly reduced chlorophyll content, relative water content (RWC), total protein levels, and anatomical traits including (xylem vessel diameter and phloem vessel diameter), while increasing ion accumulation. However, foliar application of Si enhnaced antioxidant activity, as measured by the 2, 2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay, in addition, SA and Si treatments under drought stress enhanced chlorophyll b, carotenoid content, RWC, and total protein, accompanied by modifications in vascular tissues structure. GC–MS analysis revealed that drought stress alone decreased both the number and diversity of secondary metabolites in both ecotypes. In contrast, the application of SA and/or Si under drought conditions induced treatment- and ecotype-specific alterations in metabolite composition, characterized by changes in the relative abundance of major metabolite classes and the emergence of specific compounds, rather than a uniform increase in total metabolite content. Furthermore, the combined application of SA and Si resulted in additional modifications of the phytochemical profile, indicating qualitative metabolic reprogramming. Overall, these findings demonstrate that the phytochemical responses of Scrophuralia striata to drought stress are primarily governed by interactions between drought stress and elicitor treatments, highlighting the modulatory role of SA and Si in enhancing plant adaptation to drought stress.

Introduction

Drought is one of the most significant limiting factors in agricultural production, adversely affecting plant growth and yield1. Environmental stresses in arid and semi-arid conditions induce the accumulation of Reactive oxygen species (ROS) in plants, thereby causing oxidative damage at cellular level2 . such stress can directly trigger lipid peroxidation, leading to the formation of compounds that impair mitochondrial function and disrupt photorespiratory metabolism3. The RWC of leaves is a vital regulator of plant physiological processes, and its reduction represents one of the earliest indicators of drought stress4. Decreased RWC, loss of cell turgor, and stomata closure are among the primary responses to drought stress in Hordeum vulgare4. To cope with water deficit, Plants undergo a range of anatomical and morphological adaptation at both organ and cellular levels. In the early stages of drought stress, plants typically maintain water balance by enhancing root water uptake while minimizing transpiration water loss through stomata closure5. However, Prolonged drought conditions negatively affect anatomical, morpho-physiological, and metabolic processes, ultimately leading to reduced growth and productivity. Structural traits such as cuticle thickness and increased epidermal cell thickness are critical indicators that help the plant adapt to drought stress6. Additionally, drought stress reduces the size of parenchyma cells and decreased intercellular space. For example, in Arachis hypogaea, drought stress decreases both the number and size of parenchyma cells in palisade and spongy tissues, along with reduced thickness of the upper and lower epidermis6. Scrophularia striata, belongs to the Scrophulariaceae family, which comprises approximately 220 genera and 300 species distributed widely in regions such as Iran, Turkey, and Azerbaijan. This species contains a variety of bioactive compounds, including cinnamic acid, flavonoids (e.g., quercetin), isorhamnetin-3-O-rutinoside, nepetrin, and phenylpropanoid glycosides. It is predominantly found in western regions of Iran, particularly in Ilam province, and exhibits notable antibacterial, antifungal, and anticancer properties7–9. These bioactive compounds constituents contribute to human health, primarily through their strong antioxidant capacity, which enables the scavenging of free radicals associated with aging and chronic diseases10. Salicylic acid (SA) belongs to a group of molecules known as salicylates11. It contains a phenolic ring and a hydroxyl functional group, it has been used for centuries as an analgesic and play a key role in enhancing plant tolerance to drought stress when applied exogenously12. SA application reduces chlorophyll degradation under stress conditions by protecting the photosynthetic apparatus, partly through enhanced accumulation of compatible solutes13. It also improves plant growth and morphology by increasing stomatal density and promoting grana development with more thylakoids, ultimately improving photosynthetic efficiency under drought conditions. Numerous studies have also shown that the application of SA increases the accumulation of compatible solutes and decreases the activity of chlorophyll-synthesizing enzymes13. Dianat et al., (2016) reported that the application of SA increased photosynthetic pigments and RWC in Ocimum basilicum under drought stress. It has also been reported that SA treatment increases the protein content in Nigella sativa under drought stress, as SA regulates the activity of enzymes involved in protein synthesis, such as glutamine syntheses and nitrate reductase14 and applying 1 mM SA enabled morphological leaf traits in squash plants to grow well under drought-stress conditions. Exposure of squash plants to drought stress reduced leaf anatomical characteristics, possibly due to inhibition of cellular expansion and elongation. These changes led to a decrease in the number and size of palisade and spongy parenchyma cells, and this beneficial effect of SA on leaf structure may be attributed to its vital role in cell division and cell expansion, contributing to plant adaptation under drought stress15. Silicon (Si) although not classified as a plant growth regulator by the International Plant Nutrition Institute (IPNI), plays a beneficial role in enhancing plant tolerance to abiotic stress16,17. Si application has been reported to increased photosynthetic activity and chlorophyll content in Camellia sasanqua under drought conditions18. In Poaceae species such as wheat, Si accumulation mitigates drought stress by improving photosynthesis, transpiration, stomatal conductance, and water use efficiency19. Additionally, Si contributes to leaf thickening, thereby reducing water loss through the epidermis20. However, Si deposition may also reduce mesophyll cell area by accumulating in intercellular spaces and constricting cells expansion21. For instance, drought stress has been shown to reduce mesophyll thickness (both palisade and spongy layers) in thyme plants, an effect that may be modulated by Si application22. Despite extensive research on drought stress and the role of SA and Si in improving plant tolerance, most previous studies have examined these factors independently, primarily in crop species. However, there is a lack of information on the combined effect of SA and Si on physiological, phytochemical, and leaf anatomical responses in medicinal plants, particularly those in the Scrophulariaceae family, under drought stress conditions. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the effects of drought stress on the physiological, phytochemical, and anatomical responses in S. striata, as well as to evaluate the potential synergistic roles of SA and Si in mitigating drought-induced damage. It is hypothesized that both SA and Si individually enhance drought tolerance by modulating key physiological, phytochemical, and anatomical responses. Furthermore, their combined application may produce a more pronounce and synergistic effect.

Results

Content of photosynthetic pigments

The analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed that drought stress significantly influenced chlorophyll a and carotenoid content in the aerial parts of both ecotypes at P < 0.01. Whereas its effect on chlorophyll b and total chlorophyll content were not statistically significant. The four-way interaction among ecotype × stress × SA × Si was a statistically significant (P < 0.01) for chlorophyll a, b, and total chlorophyll content, but did not significantly affect carotenoid content (Table 1). According to the mean comparison using the least significant differences (LSD) test, the lowest chlorophyll a content was observed in the control × Si × SA treatment, whereas the highest value was recorded in the control × Si treatment for the Abdanan ecotype. In contrast, Chlorophyll b content was lowest in the Abdanan ecotype under the FC50% × SA × Si treatment, while the highest level was detected in the Ilam ecotype under the FC50% × SA treatment. Carotenoid content was significantly reduced in the Abdanan ecotype under FC50% treatment, whereas the highest carotenoid level was observed in the Ilam ecotype under FC50% × SA × Si treatment. Total chlorophyll content was lowest in the Abdanan ecotype under control × SA and SA × Si treatments, as well as in the Ilam ecotype under FC50% treatment, with no statistically significant differences among these treatments. Relative water content (RWC)

The analysis of variance (ANOVA) indicated that drought stress significantly affected the RWC of both ecotypes at P < 0.01. However, the four-way interaction among ecotype, stress, SA, and Si did not have a significant effect on RWC content. In contrast, the three-way interactions of ecotype × stress × SA and ecotype × stress × Si were significant effects (P < 0.01) for RWC (Table 1). Membrane electrolyte leakage (EL)

The analysis of (ANOVA) demonstrated that drought stress significantly affected EL (P < 0.01). However, no significant differences were observed between ecotypes with respect to EL. The four-way interaction of ecotype × stress × SA × Si was significantly significant (P < 0.01) for electrolyte leakage (Table 1). Results from the mean comparison using the test indicated that lowest EL values were recorded in the Ilam ecotype under control treatment, as well as, in the Abdanan ecotype under FC50% × SA × Si and FC50% × SA treatments, with no statistically significant differences among these groups. In contrast, the highest EL was observed in the Abdanan ecotype under FC50% treatment (Fig. 2).

Total soluble protein (TSP)

The analysis of variance revealed that neither drought stress nor ecotype had a significant impact on the TSP content in the shoot P < 0.01. Additionally, the four-way interaction among ecotype × drought stress × SA × Si was not statistically significant for protein content. In contrast, the triple interaction of ecotype × stress × SA and ecotype × stress × Si were statistically significant at P < 0.01, while the triple interaction effects of stress × SA × Si were significant at a probability level of P < 0.05 (Table 1). Upon further examination of the mean comparison results indicated that the lowest TSP content was observed in the Ilam ecotype under FC50% treatment, whereas the highest protein content was recorded in the Abdanan ecotype under FC50% treatment, FC50% × Si treatment, as well as in the Ilam ecotype under control and control × SA treatment. However, these differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 2).

Radical scavenging percentage (DPPH)

The results of analysis of variance showed that drought stress significantly affected antioxidant activity in the aerial parts, as determined by the DPPH assay at P < 0.01. furthermore, the four-way interaction among ecotype × drought stress × SA × Si was also statistically significant P < 0.05 (Table 1). According to the mean comparison (LSD), the lowest antioxidant activity was observed in the Ilam ecotype under control conditions, while the highest activity was recorded in the Ilam ecotype under the FC50% × Si treatment (Fig. 2).

Anatomical indices

The results of the analysis of variance indicate that both drought stress and ecotype significantly impact on stomatal morphological structure. Specifically, stomatal length and lower epidermis thickness were significantly affected at P < 0.05, whereas stomatal width, guard cell width, stomatal size, stomatal density, upper epidermis thickness, palisade and spongy parenchyma thickness, xylem and phloem diameter were significantly affected at P < 0.01. Additionally, the four-way interaction among ecotype, stress, SA, and Si was significant at P < 0.01 for stomatal length, upper and lower epidermis thickness, and palisade and pit parenchyma thickness, and at P < 0.05 for stomatal width, guard cell width, and phloem diameter. Furthermore, stomatal density and stomatal size were significantly affected by both four-way and three-way interactions (Table 2). Upon further examination of the mean comparison results, it was observed that the Ilam ecotype treated with FC50% × Si had the lowest stomatal length and size content. In the Abdanan ecotype, the FC50% treatment resulted in the lowest stomatal width and stomatal guard cell width. Conversely, the highest stomatal length and stomatal guard cell width were observed in the Ilam ecotype treated with FC50% × SA. Additionally, the maximum stomatal width was found in the Ilam ecotype treated with FC50% × SA and the Abdanan ecotype treated with control × SA × Si, although the difference between them was not significant. The structural and anatomical changes of the leaf under drought stress conditions and the adjustment treatments were also examined. The mean comparison results showed that the lowest upper and lower epidermis thickness and phloem diameter were observed in the Abdanan ecotype treated with control × SA × Si. In the Ilam ecotype, the FC50% × Si treatment resulted in the lowest thickness of the palisade parenchyma. The highest thickness of the lower epidermis, palisade parenchyma, and pit were observed in the Ilam ecotype of the control treatment. The maximum thickness of the upper epidermis and the diameter of the xylem were determined in the Ilam ecotype of the control treatment × Si and the Abdanan ecotype of the control treatment × Si, respectively. Additionally, the highest diameter of the phloem was observed in the Ilam ecotype of the control treatment × SA. In summary, the results of the analysis of variance and mean comparison indicate that both drought stress and ecotype differences have a significant impact on stomatal morphological structure. The four-way interaction effect between ecotype, stress, SA, and Si was also found to be significant. Further research is needed to fully understand the effects of these factors on stomatal structure. Additionally, the structural and anatomical changes of the leaf under drought stress conditions and the adjustment treatments should be further examined to gain a better understanding of their impact Discussion

Effects of drought stress on physiological traits

The results of this study demonstrate that drought stress has a significant impact on the content of photosynthetic pigments, disrupting the physiological processes of the plant. In the Ilam ecotype, the control × Si treatment resulted in a significant increase in the content of chlorophyll a and total chlorophyll, indicating the potential role of Si in maintaining the stability of the photosynthetic structure. Conversely, under drought stress conditions, a decrease in the content of chlorophyll a was observed, likely due to oxidative stress and pigment destruction23. These findings are consistent with previous studies24,25 that reported a decrease in chlorophyll concentration in Thymus vulgaris and Nigella Sativa plants under water deficit. The increase in chlorophyll a content in the Ilam ecotype treated with SA × FC50% suggests that SA may mitigate the harmful effects of drought stress on the photosynthetic apparatus to some extent. This response can be attributed to the role of SA in regulating endogenous hormones, particularly cytokinins, which prevent chlorophyll degradation by enhancing biosynthesis and facilitating differentiation26. In support of this, 27reported that SA increased photosynthetic pigments in Ocimum basilicum under drought stress. On the other hand, the FC50% treatment of the Abdanan ecotype resulted in a decrease in carotenoid content, which may be part of an adaptive response rather than simply damage. This carotenoid degradation may initiate the production of hormones, signals, and volatile compounds, all of which are important components in the response to drought stress28. These findings are consistent with the results of28, who reported a reduction in carotenoid content in Fagopyrum esculentum under drought stress. In the Ilam ecotype treated with SA × FC50% × Si, the increase in carotenoid content is likely a reaction to the activation of the plant’s non-enzymatic antioxidant defense system in response to drought stress29. Carotenoids play a protective role in scavenging ROS30, contributing to the stability of photosynthetic pigments and the protection of the photosynthetic apparatus, reducing oxidative stress30. The different responses of ecotypes to treatments may be due to the concentration of olivines or plant physiology. In this regard, the increase in RWC content in the Abdanan ecotype treated with FC50% × SA indicates that SA, by increasing compatible osmolytes, enables the cell to increase RWC by regulating osmotic pressure31. Consistent with these findings,31 reported that SA application increased RWC in Leymus chinensis under drought stress. Our results also showed a significant increase in electrolyte leakage during drought stress in the Ilam ecotype treated with FC50%. Electrolyte leakage (EL) serves as an indicator of changes in cell membrane structure under drought stress conditions and allows for the assessment of damage to the structure and function of cell membranes under different stresses32. Furthermore, this study confirmed a reduction in protein content in the aerial tissues of the Ilam ecotype treated with FC50% under drought stress. This is in line with research33, that attributed the decrease in protein levels in some herbaceous plants to the degradation of protein into amino acids, such as proline34. Plants under drought stress have a higher accumulation of free proline than plants under control conditions. Merajipoor et al.25 reported a reduction in protein content in Nigella sativa under drought stress conditions. The antioxidant activity of DPPH increased in the Ilam ecotype treated with FC50% × Si. Previous studies have shown that Si application activates antioxidant defense systems and protects plants under drought stress conditions35. These results are consistent with those reported by36, who found that Si application increases DPPH activity in Cicer arietinum under drought stress. In conclusion, the results of this study demonstrate that drought stress induce significant alterations in plant physiological parameters by disrupting the photosynthetic system and enhancing oxidative damage, in contrast, the application of silicon and salicylic acid partially modulate these adverse effects and play an important role in improving plant tolerance to drought stress. However, further investigations are required to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms and clarify ecotype-specific differences in response to these treatments.

Effects of drought stress on anatomical indices

The observation of leaf anatomical traits under water deficit conditions provides important visual indicators of a plant’s sensitivity and tolerance to drought conditions37. In the present study, stomatal width decreased in the Ilam ecotype under FC50% treatment, while stomatal guard cell width decreased in the Abdanan ecotype under the same treatment, both relative to the control. Stomatal size plays a critical role in plant responses to water stress. Plants with larger stomata exhibit higher transpiration rates, which can exacerbate wilting under drought conditions. Reduced turgor pressure alters stomatal dimensions, rendering plants with larger stomata more susceptible to drought stress6. Correspondingly, stomatal density decreased in the Abdanan ecotype under FC50% treatment, reflecting the variability of this trait across species and environmental conditions. This reduction under drought helps minimize transpiration and conserve water. Plants possess extensive mechanisms to optimize gas exchange and water conservation, exemplified by the remarkable plasticity of stomatal responses to drought, enabling growth even under challenging conditions6. Supporting these findings, Yavas et al., (2024) reported that reduced stomatal density in Hordeum vulgare promotes leaf water retention, decreases transpiration, and preserves soil moisture, thereby mitigating the adverse effects of drought on plant growth. Conversely, stomatal length and width, as well as stomatal guard cell width, increased in the Ilam ecotype under FC50% × SA treatment. These results align with previous studies demonstrating the pivotal role of stomata in gas exchange, including CO2 uptake for photosynthesis and water loss as vapor during transpiration38. It has been reported that SA stimulates cell growth, division, and proliferation through signaling pathways39, thereby alleviating drought-induced cellular damage15. In this study, the thickness of the lower epidermis in the Ilam ecotype under the control treatment was greater than that observed under FC50% conditions. Supporting these findings,40reported that drought stress reduces leaf thickness through mechanisms associated with decreased net carbon uptake and impaired photosynthetic performance. They further noted that greater drought tolerance is linked to an enhanced ability to minimize biomass loss through physiological regulation and internal adaptive processes. Additionally, in the Ilam ecotype × Si, the thickness of the upper epidermis increased significantly compared to the other treatments. This response may reflect the reinforcing effect of Si in strengthening leaf structural tissues and enhancing plant resistance to adverse environmental conditions6. The results also showed that the thickness of the palisade parenchyma in the Ilam ecotype treatment with FC50% × Si increased significantly compared to the other treatments. In contrast,41 reported that Si deposition, as an adaptive mechanism, can reduce palisade parenchyma thickness through the regulation of gas exchange. Furthermore, the diameter of xylem vessels in the Ilam ecotype and phloem vessels in the Abdanan ecotype decreased under FC50% treatment compared to the control. This reduction in vascular diameter may be associated with the activation of phenylalanine and tyrosine metabolic pathways, leading to enhanced lignin biosynthesis in the cell walls. Lignin deposition strengthens the vascular tissues and reduces cellular permeability to water. Collectively, these anatomical modifications may contribute to improved stress resistance by reinforcing vascular structure and limiting water loss under drought conditions42. Finally, the results of this section indicate that drought stress affects plant adaptation mechanisms by causing significant alterations in leaf anatomical indices, including stomata, parenchymal and vascular tissues, while the application of Si and SA can modulate some of these changes and help improve leaf structure; nevertheless, further studies are necessary to more accurately understand the relationship between anatomical changes and plant physiological function.

Effects of drought stress on phytochemical compounds

GC–MS analysis of hexane extracts from S. striata roots revealed a significant diversity of secondary metabolites, including carboxylic acids, fatty acids, esters, alkanes, and alcohols. These compounds have shown significant biological activity43, and may play a significant role in plant response to stresses44, and their pharmacological properties43 (Tables 1 and 4). While some metabolites were detected in all treatments, others were specifically restricted to certain treatment conditions. In particular, compounds such as oxalic acid, allyl nonyl ester, and imidazolium derivatives were detected in most treatments. These results suggest that the type of extraction solvent used in GC–MS analysis can affect the efficiency and diversity of chemical compounds detected45. In this study, the Ilam ecotype treated with FC50% resulted in a decrease in the number of active compounds compared to the control treatment. However, in the Ilam ecotype, the FC50% × SA treatment increased the abundance of carboxylic acids compared to the control treatment. Additionally, the FC50% × Si treatment resulted in an increase in the number and diversity of compounds, including the appearance of alkane compounds. The simultaneous application of SA and Si induced the greatest increase in carboxylic acids. Under drought stress conditions, the abundance of carboxylic acids increased compared to the control treatments. This suggests that plants produce diverse secondary metabolites in response to adverse environmental conditions. Furthermore, the combination of drought stress with the application of SA or Si produced distinct and treatment-specific metabolic responses, indicating that these changes are dependent on the presence and type of elicitor. Similarly, under drought stress conditions, Si application was associated with increased metabolite diversity and induction of new compounds that were not observed in control treatments. Previous studies have shown that SA and Si application can alter enzymatic reactions of biosynthetic pathways related to drought stress tolerance, including changes in the activities of phenylpropanoid-dependent phenolic pathways (Shohani, Fazeli, et al., 2023; Shohani, Hosseini Sarghein, et al., 2023). This study supports these findings and suggests that SA and Si can modulate metabolic pathways induced by drought stress, including stress-responsive pathways related to the biosynthesis of phenolic compounds. Studies on the Abdanan ecotype showed that the FC50% × Si treatment led to increased synthesis of secondary metabolites, with alkanes, carboxylic acids, and esters being the most abundant compounds. This is consistent with previous research that has shown sterols, which are key components of cell membranes, play an important role in cellular homeostasis and can increase cell tolerance to environmental stresses46. Additionally,47reported that Si can increase the activity of secondary metabolite synthesis pathways and reduce drought stress in Capsicum annuum. Collectively, the results of this section show that drought stress, along with the application of SA and Si individually and especially synergistically, causes significant and specific changes in the profile of secondary metabolites and, by increasing diversity and inducing new compounds, plays an important role in regulating the biochemical responses of the plant to adverse environmental conditions.

Conclusion

Plants are continuously exposed to environmental stresses due to their sessile nature, with drought stress representing one of the most critical limiting factors for growth and secondary metabolism. The present study demonstrates that drought stress–induced responses in S. striata cannot be attributed to water deficit alone but are strongly governed by interactions between drought stress and the application of SA and Si. The combined physiological, anatomical, and phytochemical results showed that drought stress increased oxidative damage, whereas the application of SA and Si under drought conditions significantly mitigated these adverse effects by enhancing antioxidant activity, photosynthetic pigment content, relative water status, and anatomical integrity. These protective effects were most pronounced when SA and Si were applied simultaneously, indicating a synergistic interaction between the two elicitors. GC–MS analysis further revealed that drought stress reduced secondary metabolite diversity in both Ilam and Abdanan ecotypes. However, under drought conditions, SA and Si did not uniformly increase metabolite accumulation; instead, they modulated the composition and relative abundance of specific metabolite classes in a treatment- and ecotype-dependent manner. Where significant interactions were detected, changes in phytochemical profiles reflected qualitative metabolic reprogramming rather than simple quantitative increases in total compounds. This interaction-driven modulation of secondary metabolism may play an important role in enhancing drought tolerance and preserving the medicinal value of S. striata under water-limited conditions. Although this study provides valuable evidence of the role of physiological, biochemical, and anatomical parameters in improving drought tolerance, there were limitations to this study. This study was conducted under greenhouse conditions, and since in nature, drought stress is accompanied by other variable factors such as heat stress, salinity, and the presence of soil microorganisms, the responses observed are likely to be more complex in field conditions. The separate and synergistic effects of SA and Si on different stages of plant growth from germination to reproductive stage (flowering and seed production) have not been investigated. Also, physiological, anatomical and phytochemical responses may vary throughout the plant life cycle. In addition, the present study focused on two ecotypes, Ilam and Abdanan, so the results obtained cannot be attributed to the entire genetic population of S. striata. Although metabolite changes were demonstrated by GC–MS analysis, most of these changes were based on qualitative identification and the detailed molecular mechanisms such as gene regulation involved in these pathways were not investigated in this study. Therefore, future studies should employ proteomics and transcriptomics approaches to identify and characterize the target genes and proteins involved in the response to FC50% × SA × Si interactions. Also, the individual and synergistic effects of SA and Si under multiple concurrent stresses, such as combined heat and salinity, which are prevalent in arid and semi-arid regions, warrant further investigation. Given that secondary metabolite profiles were significantly influenced by the treatments in this study, future research could focus on metabolic engineering strategies aimed at maximizing the production of specific high-value medicinal compounds in S. striata through targeted application of these stimuli.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

Seeds of the Scrophularia striata flower plant ecotype were sourced from two distinct regions in Ilam. The first region, characterised by a temperate mountainous climate, is located at an altitude of 1427 m, with an average annual rainfall of 590.8 mm, an average temperature of 18.1 °C, and coordinates of 23° 21′ 30" N latitude and 46° 51′ 19" E longitude. The second collection site, Abdanan in southern Ilam, features a hot, arid climate, situated at 880 m above sea level, with an annual rainfall of 619.3 mm, an average temperature of 24.1 °C, and coordinates of 47° 25’ N latitude and 32° 59’ E longitude. Both regions are part of the Zagros Mountains in western Iran, and the seeds were collected in January 2020. The botanical identification of these samples was verified using Flora Iranica48. A voucher specimen of S. striata was deposited in the Herbarium (gene bank) of Ilam University under the accession code IUGB02076, where it is available for future reference and research purposes. Also the collection process and field studies were conducted according to the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) guidelines, ensuring that no endangered or protected species were collected. Thereafter, seed samples were collected with official permission obtained from Ilam University Gene Bank (permission letter) and thus, seeds from two ecotypes have been stored in the GenBank of ilam university under the code IUGB02076. To prepare the plants, seeds were sterilised by immersing them in a 10% (w/v) sodium hypochlorite solution for 30 s, followed by rinsing with distilled water. Seeds were then treated with 400 ppm gibberellic acid for 48 h, following the protocol described by29,49. After treatment, the seeds were incubated in a germinator (BINDER KBWF 240, Germany) at 25 °C with 90% relative humidity for 48 h. The germinated seedlings were transferred into pots measuring 120 mm in height and 180 mm in diameter. The pots were filled with a soil mixture comprising soil, animal manure, and clay in a 2:1:1 ratio, exhibiting physicochemical properties of 1.3 EC (µmol/cm), 28% relative humidity, and a pH of 7.4. These pots were placed in a greenhouse maintained under controlled conditions, including a 16/8-h light/dark cycle (220 µmoles of photons m⁻2 s⁻1), day/night temperatures of 25/30 °C, and relative humidity levels ranging between 50 and 70%50.

Treatments and experimental design

In order to investigate the response of Si and SA-treated plants to drought stress, a factorial completely randomized design (FCRD) experiment was carried out in three replicates in the Research Greenhouse of the Faculty of Agriculture, Ilam University during the years 2020–2021. In this research, drought stress was induced at two levels (100% and 50% field capacity), and SA and Si were also used at two levels of (0 and 100 ppm) and (0 and 1 g/L), respectively49,51. After 127 days of uniform growth under controlled environmental conditions, S. striata seedlings with four fully developed true leaves were selected, and three pots were randomly assigned for each treatment. The treatments included two concentrations of SA and Si, applied as foliar sprays early in the morning every four days. These applications were repeated eight times over four weeks, ensuring complete coverage of the plant’s aerial parts. Forty-eight hours following the last application of SA and Si, drought stress was induced at two levels: control (full field capacity) and 50% of field capacity, maintained for 50 days. Field capacity was determined using the weighing method. To measure, the soil in the pots was saturated with water, allowed to drain, and then sampled. A portion of the saturated soil was covered with aluminium foil and left at room temperature for 24 h. Measurement of total soluble protein

Total Soluble Protein (TSP) was measured using a modified version of the method described by54. A buffer solution containing Tris (0.2 M), ascorbic acid (0.05 mM), and sucrose (50 mM) at pH 8 was prepared, and 2 mL of this buffer was added to 0.1 g of dry plant tissue. The mixture was kept at 4 °C for 24 h to extract the proteins. After incubation, the supernatant was collected by centrifuging the mixture at 4000 g for 20 min. To quantify soluble proteins, 5 mL of the supernatant was combined with 2 mL of copper sulfate reagent and allowed to react for 10 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of diluted Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (1:10 dilution) was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark for 30 min to facilitate color development. Absorbance was then measured at 660 nm using a SPECORD 50 spectrophotometer (Analytik Jena, Germany). Protein concentration was determined by reference to a standard curve prepared with bovine serum albumin (BSA).

Morphological traits of stomata

A modified protocol, adapted from57, was used to perform an anatomical analysis of stomata. Middle leaves were collected from each treatment replicate and the upper surface (adaxial) was covered with a layer of colorless varnish to prepare the epidermal effect. The leaf was then gently pressed onto colorless adhesive tape and the tape, containing the epidermal effect, was transferred to a clean microscope slide. Stomata observations were made using a light microscope (Olympus CX, made in Japan) at × 10 objective magnification. Five fields of view were randomly selected from each sample and imaged using the Truechrome metric camera and tcapture software. The average data from these five fields of view, including length, width, size, width of guard cells, and stomatal density (µm), were calculated and recorded for statistical analysis.

Leaf morphological traits

Leaf samples were prepared following the method described by58 with minor modifications. Fresh leaf tissue was fixed in a series of ethanol solutions and then embedded in paraffin blocks. Using a microtome (Panand-P1215, Iran), seven-micrometer sections were prepared. To enhance the quality of the images, the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Observations of leaf anatomical features were made using a light microscope (Olympus, CX, made in Japan) at a magnification of × 10 objective 10×. Five fields of view were randomly selected from each sample and captured using software (True chrome metric camera and tcapture). The mean data for key anatomical traits, including epidermal and mesophyll cell thickness, and xylem and phloem diameters, were calculated in micrometers (µm) and recorded for statistical analysis.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) of root organs

Extraction of crude extract

The roots of S. striata plants were meticulously cleaned by washing under running water for 15 min, followed by four thorough rinses with distilled water. Then, the roots were sterilised and air-dried in the shade. Using a milling apparatus, the dried root material was then ground into a fine powder. Extraction was performed following the protocol outlined by59 with minor modifications. Initially, 2 g of the powdered sample were immersed in 6 mL of hexane for 2 to 3 h. The solvent was then separated from the extract using a rotary device (Heidolf model, made in Germany), and the extract was subsequently dried in an oven. Finally, the extract was stored in a dark environment at 4 °C for further use GC–MS analysis was performed using a Thermo Finnigan GC–MS instrument including TRACE GC 2000 connected to MS plusTRACE. Sample injection was performed in split mode with a ratio of 70:1 and an injection volume of 1 μl. The injector temperature was set to 280 °C. Helium gas was used as the carrier gas with a constant flow rate of 1 ml/min. Separation of compounds was performed on a HP 5MS capillary column with a length of 30 m and an internal diameter of 0.25 mm. Mass spectrometry was performed in electron ionization (EL) mode with an energy of 70 eV. The oven temperature program increased from 4 °C/min to 150 °C. Then the temperature was increased to 260 °C and this temperature was held constant for 2 min. Identification of chemical compounds was carried out based on comparison of the resulting mass spectra with the NEST and PubBash databases, according to the method presented by60.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data was checked and confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test in SPSS software (version 29). The data were analyzed using SAS software (version 9.4). The experimental design was a four-way factorial design in a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications. The factors included drought stress (control, FC50%), SA (0, 100 ppm), Si (1 gr L-1), and ecotype (Llam and Abdann). The analysis model included the main effects of all four factors and two-way, three-way, and four-way interaction effects. Comparison of means was evaluated using the LSD test at a significance level of P ≤ 0.05. Graphs were created using GraphPad software (version 9).
