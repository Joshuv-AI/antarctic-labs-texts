TWIST: Training-free and Label-free Short Text Clustering through Iterative Vector Updating with LLMs
I-Fan Lin, Faegheh Hasibi, Suzan Verberne

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a training-free and label-free method for short text clustering that can be used on top of any existing embedder. In the context of customer-facing chatbots, companies are dealing with large amounts of user utterances that need to be clustered according to their intent. In these commercial settings, no labeled data is typically available, and the number of clusters is not known. Our method is based on iterative vector updating: it constructs sparse vectors based on representative texts, and then iteratively refines them through LLM guidance. Our method achieves comparable or superior results to state-of-the-art methods that use contrastive learning, but without assuming prior knowledge of clusters or labels. Experiments on diverse datasets and smaller LLMs show that our method is model agnostic and can be applied to any embedder, with relatively small LLMs, and different clustering methods. We also show that our method scales to large datasets, reducing the computational cost of the LLM. These low-resource, adaptable settings and the scalability of our method make it more aligned with real-world scenarios than existing clustering methods. Our source code is available here: https://anonymous.4open.science/r/sparse_vector-F4C9/README.md



1
Introduction

Short text clustering is an NLP task that automatically groups unlabeled data into clusters and is widely used in practical applications for text classification and new category detection.
For example, in conversational understanding, user intents in real-world scenarios are dynamic and continuously evolving
(
Liang et al. 2024c
)
, and relying on expert labeling can be costly.
The short-text clustering task enables companies to efficiently organize tens of thousands of client requests in real time. Other downstream tasks, such as mining topics and opinions from online platforms,
also rely on effective clustering
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
.

The clustering task is often approached as a
Generalized Category Discovery (GCD) scenario

(
Vaze et al. 2022
)
, where limited labeled data and substantial unlabeled data are used to cluster all unlabeled samples, which include both known and unknown classes.
Recently, other studies have explored an
emerging data scenario
, where no labels are provided at all
(
De Raedt et al. 2023
;
Viswanathan et al. 2024b
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2025
)
. This scenario reduces reliance on human annotation and shows greater flexibility.

A common approach for the emerging data scenarios is to train an embedder with contrastive learning to improve text representations
(
Zhang et al. 2021
)
. With the advancement of LLMs, training-free approaches have also been proposed
(
De Raedt et al. 2023
)
.

The emerging data scenario has attracted increasing attention in recent research
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Feng et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2025
)
. Despite the progress of prior work, these label-free approaches still face certain limitations and leave room for further improvement. First, methods involving LLM feedback often assume
known number of clusters
during method development
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Feng et al. 2024
)
, which is difficult to estimate in real-world settings. Second, training-free methods have mainly been explored with large, proprietary models
(
Viswanathan et al. 2024b
;
De Raedt et al. 2023
)
, which can be costly. Third, the
scalability
of these methods remains a concern.
Motivated by these challenges, we propose a training-free approach that requires only a smaller LLM, alleviates the known-cluster assumption, and is scalable.

The core idea of our method is to construct new representations (referred to as sparse vectors in this paper) such that texts belonging to the same cluster become more similar.

Our method consists of two stages: we first construct sparse representations based on representative texts, and then iteratively refine them by combining LLM guidance with pre-trained embeddings.

The contributions of our method are as follows:

•

Inclusive and easy to optimize:
Builds on existing embedders and requires only some hyperparameter tuning.

•

Flexible:
Does not assume a known number of clusters, nor require labeled data, contrastive learning on the task specific datasets, or fine-tuning, enabling easier adaptation to new domains.

•

Low-resource:
Even with small LLMs and embedders, the results are already competitive with methods that rely on labeled data or fine-tuning.

•

Scalable:
Does not require LLM feedback for every text, unlike many previous studies.

2
Related Work

Short text clustering

Generalized Category Discovery (GCD) is often studied for this task
(
Liang et al. 2024b
;
Liang and Liao 2023
;
Zou et al. 2025b
)
. These works use limited labeled text and substantial unlabeled text to learn clustering representations. However, these studies use train–test splits within a single dataset, which implicitly assumes that new categories are similar to the known ones. In practice, emerging data may show topic drift. Recently, with the advancement of LLMs, many studies have explored the emerging data scenario
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Viswanathan et al. 2024b
;
Lin et al. 2025
)
, where no labels are available. We follow this unsupervised scenario.

Boosting Unsupervised Short Text Clustering with LLM-Guided Contrastive Learning

Contrastive learning is commonly used in unsupervised short text clustering
(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Liang et al. 2024a
)
.
Zhang et al. 2023
use LLMs to construct hard triplets to derive training pairs, which are then used to fine-tune a small embedder.
Liang et al. 2024a
use LLMs to refine texts by generating descriptors, then using small language models for contrastive learning to align the embeddings of texts and their descriptors. However, these methods either rely on knowing the number of clusters, proprietary models, or fine-tuning. Our method does not have these requirements.

Training-Free Methods for Unsupervised Short Text Clustering

Training-free approaches use a frozen pre-trained embedder:
De Raedt et al. 2023
select prototypical utterances and then use an LLM to generate a set of descriptive utterance labels for these prototypes. The labels and texts are then encoded to form better representations.
Viswanathan et al. 2024a
use LLMs to improve texts by generating key phrases for each text, which are then separately encoded into embeddings and concatenated for clustering.
Lin et al. 2025
use LLMs for selection rather than text enrichment. They first encode all of the texts, and then use the LLM to select similar pairs for each text. Each text embedding is then obtained by average pooling the embeddings of the text and its similar pairs. These methods have in common that they refine original embeddings to improve clustering. In contrast, we construct a new vector using LLM feedback.
Feng et al. 2024
use LLMs to refine edge points. However, the assumption of a known number of clusters can still be a core problem in real-world applications.

3
Theoretic formulation and assumptions

3.1
Problem definition

In our work, we consider two variations of the short text clustering task: (i) the emerging data scenario and (ii) the GCD scenario. In the emerging data scenario
(
Lin et al. 2025
)
, the only available resource is a collection of unlabeled texts
D^{test}\triangleq\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where each
x_{i}\in D^{test}
corresponds to a short text and
N
is the total number of samples. The dataset is used as a whole for clustering and evaluation, without any train-test split. In contrast, the GCD scenario has access to additional data, typically including some labeled samples
(
Zou et al. 2025b
)
.
In this work, we adapt this setting by considering only an additional unlabeled dataset
D^{u}\triangleq\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{u}}
, in line with the goal of low-resource
. The GCD scenario is included for the purpose of testing whether extra data improves clustering and generalizability of our method.

For both scenarios, the task is to partition
D^{test}
into clusters
\{\hat{S}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{\hat{K}}
, where
\hat{K}
is the number of predicted clusters. Note that
\sum_{l=1}^{\hat{K}}|\hat{S}_{l}|=N
. The goal is to ensure that the clustering results
\{\hat{S}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{\hat{K}}
are as close as possible to the true partition
\{S_{l}\}_{l=1}^{K}
, where
K
is the number of unique clusters in the ground truth. Importantly, unlike many existing studies
(
De Raedt et al. 2023
;
Feng et al. 2024
;
Zou et al. 2025b
)
, which assume that
K
is known in advance, we make no such assumption.

For notation convenience, we define the full dataset as
D\triangleq D^{test}\cup D^{u}
to simplify the explanation of our proposed method. Note that
D=D^{test}
in the emerging data scenario.

3.2
Constructing the Sparse Vector

Basic Assumption

Considering the fact that texts belonging to the same cluster are similar to each other, we represent each text as a sparse bag-of-texts vector that directly encodes its similarity to other texts.
Formally, for any
x_{i},x_{j}\in D
with
i\neq j
:

•

x_{i}
and
x_{j}
are similar if
x_{i},x_{j}\in S_{k}
for some cluster
S_{k}
.

•

x_{i}
and
x_{j}
are not similar if
x_{i}\in S_{k}
and
x_{j}\in S_{l}
with
k\neq l
.

Construction of Bag-of-Texts

Since similarity is determined by clusters, it is not necessary to use all texts in
D
to represent sparse vectors; instead, a representative subset of texts can constitute our bag of texts, denoted as
D^{r}\subseteq D
, where
|D^{r}|\triangleq d
.

The set of representative texts
D^{r}
should contain at least one element from each cluster.
That is, for any cluster
S\in\{S_{l}\}^{K}_{l=1}
, there exists at least one text
x\in D^{r}
such that
x\in S
.

Therefore, to cover all possible clusters, the minimum size of
d
is
K-1
. Since
K
is unknown, the most practical approach is to choose a relatively large
d
.
For each
x\in D
, we define a
d
-diemnsional vector
\mathbf{z}

assigned
to the representative texts, where the
j
-th coordinate takes the value 1 if
x
is similar to the
j
-th representative text, and 0 otherwise.

Proposition 1
.

Given the assumption, for any
x_{i},x_{j}\in D
with
i\neq j
that belong to the same cluster
S_{k}
, the corresponding sparse vectors
\mathbf{z}_{i}
and
\mathbf{z}_{j}
will be identical.

The proposition is straightforward, so we omit the proof.

Adaptation to real-world practice

Although the proposition holds in the idealized setting, in practice similarity is typically determined using LLMs, which may introduce errors
, and scanning the entire dataset for each
x
is computationally expensive. To address these, we construct a sparse vector
\mathbf{z}
in a way that is adaptable to real-world practice: for each
x\in D
, instead of assigning fixed value in
\{0,1\}
based on representative texts, we propose an updating procedure that accounts for uncertainty. In addition, to address limited computational resources, we use an existing embedder to identify a relatively small set
of
m
similar candidates
that are likely to belong to the same category, as empirically shown in previous research
(
Lin et al. 2025
;
Zou et al. 2025a
)
. The details of the method will be discussed in the next section.

4
Computational Method

Our method aims to construct sparse vectors that capture the similarity of texts within the same cluster. It consists of two stages. In the initial stage, we select representative texts, which are then used to construct a sparse vector for each text. In the second stage, we iteratively update each text’s sparse vector by considering its similar texts until convergence, illustrated in Figure
1
.

Unlike many prior studies that assume the number of clusters
K
is known, we consider a more practical setting in which
K
is unknown, and thus approximate the true number of clusters by the number of representative texts,
d
.
This is less challenging than estimating
K
, as it only requires ensuring that
d
is large enough to cover
K
clusters.

Figure 1:
Construction of Sparse vectors.
Initial stage
: We first encode each text in
D
using a pretrained embedder and partition them into
d
clusters. We then select the medoid of each cluster as the representative text and construct one-hot vectors for these representatives. The remaining text vectors are obtained based on LLM selection.
Iterative stage
: We update all of the vectors iteratively until convergence.

4.1
Initial stage: construction of sparse representation

To build sparse vector representations, we first need to create a set of representative samples
D^{r}
. To this end, we first encode each text using an existing embedder
F
to obtain pre-trained embeddings:

\mathbf{x}_{i}=F(x_{i}).

Next, we apply a clustering algorithm to partition these embeddings into
d
clusters.

We use agglomerative clustering
(
Murtagh and Legendre 2014
)
as it produces deterministic results, which facilitates reproducibility.
For each initial cluster , we derive the medoid. We refer to the set of medoids as the representative text, denoted as:

D^{r}\triangleq\{x_{c_{1}},\dots,x_{c_{d}}\}.

and let
\mathbf{X}^{r}
be
D^{r}
corresponding pre-trained embeddings, where
\mathbf{X}^{r}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}:x_{i}\in D^{r}\}
.
We then transform the representative texts into one-hot vectors, which serve as the basis vectors for the sparse representation.

For the remaining texts
x_{j}\in D\setminus D^{r}
, we construct their sparse vectors using this basis. For each
x_{j}
, we compute the pairwise cosine distances between its embedding
\mathbf{x}_{j}
and the medoid embeddings in
\mathbf{X}^{r}
. By sorting these distances, we select the top
m
closest representative texts to
x_{j}
, denoted as
x_{j1},\dots,x_{jm}
. Then, we use an LLM to identify the similar text(s) to
x_{j}
among these representative texts, denoted as
M_{j}\triangleq LLM(x,x_{j1},\dots,x_{jm})\in\mathcal{P}(\{x_{j1},\dots,x_{jm}\})\cup\{\text{None}\}
, where
\mathcal{P}(\cdot)
represents the power set, i.e., all possible subsets of the candidate texts, and ‘None’ indicates that no representative text is selected.
If the LLM output is not
None
, the sparse vector of
x_{j}
is then derived by averaging the sparse vectors of the texts in
M_{j}
, denoted as
\mathbf{z}_{j}
. Instead of assigning a fixed value of 1 to the selected dimension, we average the vectors to capture how strongly each representative text contributes to the feature. We then apply L2 normalization to ensure a consistent scale for the iterative stage. If the LLM output is None, we make this text into a representative text itself and increase the dimensionality of all sparse vectors by one and construct its corresponding one-hot vectors.

Note:
if we set
d=N
, all texts will be representative texts and one-hot encoded.

4.2
Iterative stage: sparse vectors refinements

In this stage, we update the sparse vectors of all texts using the dynamic subset of
m
candidates.

At each iteration step
t
(with the initial stage being
t=0
), for each
x_{j}\in D
, we first concatenate its pre-trained embedding and sparse vector:
\mathbf{u}_{j}^{t}\triangleq\mathbf{x}_{j}\oplus\mathbf{z}_{j}^{t}
.
We then compute the pairwise cosine distances between
\mathbf{u}_{j}^{t}
and the concatenated representations of the all texts
\mathbf{U}^{t}
. By sorting these distances, we select the top
m
closest texts to
x_{j}
, denoted as
x^{t}_{j1},\dots,x^{t}_{jm}
. The concatenation ensures that the pre-trained embedding guides the sparse vectors. Also, the reason we do iterative updating instead of just once is to gain more stable sparse vectors.

Next, we use an LLM to identify the subset of the texts most similar to
x_{j}
, denoted as

M^{t}_{j}\triangleq LLM(x_{j},x^{t}_{j1},\dots,x^{t}_{jm})\in\mathcal{P}(\{x^{t}_{j1},\dots,x^{t}_{jm}\})\cup\{\text{None}\}
.
If the LLM output is
None
, we fix sparse vectors, i.e.,

\mathbf{z}_{j}^{t+1}=\mathbf{z}_{j}^{t}
.
Otherwise, we update the sparse vectors by averaging the sparse vector of the texts in
M^{t}_{j}
.
We then apply L2 normalization, as explained previously.

For
x_{j}
, the update process is ended if the sparse vector at iteration
t+1
is almost identical to those at iteration
t
, as we define by a cosine similarity greater than 0.99. For efficiency, we set
m=30
and cap the maximum number of iterations at 10.

5.3
Hyperparameter setting

The main hyperparameter in our approach is the initial dimension (number of representatives) of the sparse vector
d
, which we set to 2,048 based on validation results on two external datasets: ArxivS2S and Reddit
(
Muennighoff et al. 2023
)
. We use external datasets to prevent overfitting on a specific dataset. See details in Appendix
B
. Qwen3-8B (as LLM) and All-MiniLM-L6-v2 (as embedder) are used in the hyperparameter search.

5.4
Evaluation

The emerging and GCD scenarios are evaluated under two conditions: with HDBSCAN
(
McInnes et al. 2017
)
and K-means
(
Lloyd 1982
)
.

To reflect real-world settings with unknown
K
, we apply HDBSCAN
(
McInnes et al. 2017
)
to partition
D^{test}
into
\hat{K}
clusters. We set
min_samples = 1
to reduce noise. The noise points are assigned to the nearest cluster centroid. We perform an incremental grid search over
min_cluster_size
values of
10,15,\ldots,50
, stopping early if the silhouette score does not improve
(
Rousseeuw 1987
;
Gung et al. 2023
)
.

K-means clustering is the dominant evaluation practice because many studies assume a known cluster number
K
and incorporate it in their method pipelines
Zhang et al. 2023
;
De Raedt et al. 2023
;
Liang et al. 2024b
;
Feng et al. 2024
;
Zou et al. 2025b
. Following their convention, the number of clusters for K-means is set to match the number of ground-truth cluster number
K
.

To reduce cost, we follow
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
and perform a single pipeline run per dataset using a fixed seed to obtain a sparse vector for each text. Most steps are deterministic. The main source of variability comes from splitting candidate examples across multiple LLM calls (e.g., three calls with 10 examples instead of one with 30), done only to save time and GPU memory. LLM outputs are generated via greedy decoding, without sampling. For evaluation, K-means is run over 5 different seeds and averaged. HDBSCAN, which is near-deterministic, is run once. After we derive the clusters, we apply standard clustering metrics for evaluation. These metrics include normalized mutual information (NMI) and clustering accuracy (Acc)
(
Rand 1971
;
Meilă 2007
;
Huang et al. 2014
;
Gung et al. 2023
)
.

A scalable implementation of our method

Our representation relies on LLM guidance for each text, which can be computationally expensive when the dataset is extremely large. To assess scalability, we additionally explore clustering over the full dataset. The goal is to implement our approach on a subset of the texts and then use a model to generalize to the remaining texts (a form of knowledge distillation), without requiring further LLM computation. Specifically, we train a lightweight MLP (
\sim
10M parameters) to map pre-trained embeddings to sparse vectors. See Appendix
G
for experiment details. Note that we do not use any labeled data. Table
8
shows that our approach still improves the original embeddings by a large margin, which proves the scalability of our method, offering substantial savings in time and computational resources.

Conclusions

We propose TWIST (Short Text Clustering through Iterative Sparse Vector Updating with LLMs), an intuitive, domain-adaptive, label-free, and training-free method for short text clustering. Compared with other SOTA methods, TWIST is built under minimal assumptions and requirements. TWIST achieves better or similar results to state-of-the-art approaches without assuming prior knowledge of clusters or labels, making it more aligned with real-world scenarios. Experiments on diverse datasets and smaller LLMs show that our method is robust in low-resource settings, and also scalable to large datasets.
In the future, we plan to expand our approach to clustering in specialized domains and explore ways to incorporate human feedback into our method pipeline.

Limitations

Language.
Like most of the prior work, we only focus on English utterance datasets. This is relevant because most of benchmark datasets are in English. Our method is completely data-driven and not dependent on labeled data and it is therefore applicable to other languages than English.

LLM capability.
Our method relies on the abilities of LLMs. Although we empirically show that small LLMs can achieve good results, this is not guaranteed when the dataset comes from a rare domain.

Embedder capability.
Although we empirically show that our approach generally improves performance compared to directly using the embedder for clustering, if the embeddings provide a poor initial result, the improvement might be limited.

We first encode all examples into pre-trained embeddings using all-MiniLM-L6-v2, then use the trained MLP to derive sparse versions of these embeddings. These sparse vectors are used to cluster all examples.
