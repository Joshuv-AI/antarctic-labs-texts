Fine-tuning Aligned Classifiers for Merging Outputs: Towards a Superior Evaluation Protocol in Model Merging
Fanshuang Kong, Richong Zhang, Zhijie Nie, Ziqiao Wang, Qiang Sun

Abstract
Model merging combines multiple fine-tuned models into a single one via parameter fusion, achieving improvements across many tasks. However, in the classification task, we find a misalignment issue between merging outputs and the fine-tuned classifier, which limits its effectiveness. In this paper, we first demonstrate the following observations: (1) Merging outputs exhibit the comparable cluster effect with fine-tuned outputs, and already contain necessary classification information; (2) The misalignment between merging outputs and the fine-tuned classifier can converge to an orthogonal transformation, and alleviating this misalignment can significantly enhance the performance of merging models. Based on these observations, we then propose a new protocol FT-Classifier, which fine-tunes an aligned classifier with few-shot unlabeled samples, enabling better evaluation of merging methods and improved classification performance.



1
Introduction

Fine-tuning a pre-trained language model on downstream tasks to obtain a task-specific fine-tuned model is a common practice nowadays
Devlin et al. 2018
. Building on this training paradigm, real-world applications increasingly aim to leverage a single model that can be fine-tuned across multiple tasks, thus enabling it to address all tasks simultaneously
Zhang and Yang 2021
. This is known as multi-task learning (MTL), which generally aggregates all task data for joint training. However, MTL not only requires the full amount of task data, which raises data privacy concerns, but also involves a fully fine-tuning process, leading to high training costs and poor scalability
Yang et al. 2024b
. Therefore, researchers are actively exploring model merging as an alternative
Izmailov et al. 2018
;
Jin et al. 2022
;
Ilharco et al. 2022
;
Yang et al. 2024c
;
Yang et al. 2024a
.

Model merging aims to directly combine multiple homologous fine-tuned task-specific models in the parameter space, without requiring the full dataset and the fine-tuning process, to obtain a merged model capable of solving multiple tasks simultaneously
Yadav et al. 2023
. Several studies have investigated model merging in areas such as continual learning
Marczak et al. 2024
, information retrieval
Li et al. 2024
, and classification
Yu et al. 2024
. Interestingly, we find that compared to other tasks, model merging brings fewer benefits in classification tasks.

We argue that this is due to the inevitable changes in the parameters of the merged model compared to the fine-tuned model. However, the current evaluation protocol (Current Eval) still utilizes the fine-tuned classifier for merged model assessment. This misalignment between merging outputs and the fine-tuned classifier leads to inaccurate evaluations of the merging method.
In this work, we conduct the following analysis to validate our argument.

Merging outputs already exhibit a good clustering effect.

We visualize the merging outputs of three representative methods on text classification datasets using T-SNE
Van der Maaten and Hinton 2008
, as shown in Figure
1
. From the Figure, we observe that although the performance of different merging methods varies across specific tasks, all merging outputs of representative methods exhibit comparable clustering performance to that of fine-tuned outputs. This motivates us to ponder that the merging outputs already include the necessary classification information, yet it is not accurately evaluated in the Current Eval.

Misalignment between merging outputs and fine-tuned classifier leads to the undervaluation of merging methods.

We bypass the fine-tuned classifier and instead use a few-shot set of labeled samples as class anchors. Employing a KNN-based method—commonly applied in sentence representation studies—we directly measure classification accuracy. The results of this KNN evaluation protocol (KNN Eval) are reported in Figure
2
. Surprisingly, KNN Eval significantly outperforms the Current Eval on three representative methods, even with limited labeled data, demonstrating that Current Eval tends to underestimate merging methods. Since KNN Eval does not require a classifier at all, while Current Eval requires a fine-tuned classifier, this also proves that this underestimation may be caused by the misalignment between merging outputs and the fine-tuned classifier.

The misalignment can converge into an orthogonal transformation.

In conjunction with Figure
1
, which illustrates a certain bias between merging outputs and fine-tuned outputs in the latent space, we hypothesize some simulation of transformation to align them. Specifically, we employ a series of transformations, varying the strength of the constraints, for empirical verification. The definition of different transformations and corresponding results are detailed in Table
1
and Figure
4
, respectively.
The results demonstrate that after transformation alignment, all merging methods exhibit significant improvement compared to the Current Eval, even approaching the upper bound of the fine-tuned model. Moreover, when these transformations converge to an orthogonal transformation, there is only a slight drop in classification performance. This indicates that through a rigid transformation such as rotation or reflection, the misalignment can be easily alleviated. As a result, the fine-tuned classifier may only need a simple alignment with the merging outputs to achieve better evaluation results.

Fine-tuning an aligned classifier can alleviate the misalignment and facilitate merging methods assessment.

Based on the above observations, we believe that Current Eval mainly assesses the compatibility between the merging outputs and the fine-tuned classifier rather than effectively evaluating the capability of the merging outputs themselves, which in turn limits the final classification performance. Therefore, we propose the FT-Classifier evaluation protocol (FT-Classifier Eval), which leverages few-shot unlabeled data to train an aligned classifier for merging outputs. This protocol does not introduce additional parameters and requires only minimal training steps, making it a convincing alternative to Current Eval.
The results of the FT-Classifier Eval, as presented in Figure
5
, demonstrate that such a lightweight and computationally feasible post-processing operation improves the effectiveness of merging methods assessment.

In short, we make the following contributions:

•

Through empirical analysis, we reveal that the misalignment issue between merging outputs and the fine-tuned classifier renders the Current Eval ineffective for accurately assessing merging methods.

•

We prove that this misalignment can converge to an orthogonal transformation, indicating that the intrinsic structure and geometric properties of the merging outputs remain consistent with those of the fine-tuned model.

•

We introduce a new FT-Classifier Eval that alleviates the misalignment issue using limited data, without adding parameters or changing the model structure. This enables a more accurate assessment of merging methods while significantly enhancing their performance.

Background

2.1
Definition of Model Merging

Let
f_{b}
denote the pre-trained base model with parameter
\mathbf{\Theta}_{b}
. Given
T
specific tasks with training data
X_{t}
and corresponding label
Y_{t}
for each data, one can obtain
T
fine-tuned models. The fine-tuned model for task
t
can be denoted as
f_{t}
with parameter
\mathbf{\Theta}_{t}
.
Without fully fine-tuning, model merging aims to merge these homologous models to generate a single
f_{m}
with parameter
\mathbf{\Theta}_{m}
, which can simultaneously perform well across all
T
tasks.

2.2
Representative Methods

Up to date, numerous model merging approaches have been proposed, which we will discuss in detail in Section
7
. In this section, we primarily focus on introducing the three most representative methods.

Weight averaging

Wortsman et al. 2022
simply averages
T
fine-tuned models for merging, which is formulated as:

\mathbf{\Theta}_{m}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{\Theta}_{t}.

(1)

Task Arithmetic

Ilharco et al. 2022
defines the difference between the fine-tuned model and the pre-trained model in parameter space as a task vector, denoted by
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}
. Experiments demonstrate that performing arithmetic operations on task vectors can effectively guide the merged model. The process is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Theta}_{t}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{b},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Theta}_{m}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Theta}_{b}+\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t},

where
\lambda\in[0,1]
is a scaling term determined by held-out validation sets.

Ties

Yadav et al. 2023
posits that parameter conflicts are widespread across multiple task vectors. To address this, it preserves task parameters with large magnitudes and resolves sign conflicts using an election mechanism. We denote the task vector processed by Ties as
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}^{\rm Ties}
, then the parameters merged by Ties are calculated as follows:

\mathbf{\Theta}_{m}=\mathbf{\Theta}_{b}+\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}^{\rm Ties}.

(3)

2.3
Evaluation of Merging Models

Existing merging methods in classification tasks primarily focus on merging fine-tuned encoders, while not performing the merging operation on fine-tuned classifiers. That is, the merging encoders
f_{m}
should be evaluated on the fine-tuned classier, which can be formulated as:

\hat{Y}_{t}=f_{m}(X_{t};\mathbf{\Theta}_{m})^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{t},

(4)

where
\mathbf{W}_{t}
represents the weight of the fine-tuned classifier of task
t
, which maps the merging outputs to the predicted labels
\hat{Y}_{t}
. In the following descriptions, this currently adopted evaluation protocol is referred to as ‘Current Eval’.

3
Existence of the Misalignment

From Eq.
4
, we can easily find the fact that due to the change in parameters, the merging outputs of
X_{t}
generated by
f_{m}
are inevitably shifted in the latent space compared to those generated by
f_{t}
. However, the evaluation of
f_{m}
still relies on
\mathbf{W}_{t}
, which is tailored to
f_{t}
. Naturally, this raises a question:
Does the outputs of
f_{m}
align well with the fine-tuned classifier
\mathbf{W}_{t}
?
To analyze this issue, this section delves into an in-depth analysis of the output of
f_{m}
.

3.2
KNN Evaluation of Merging outputs

To validate our hypothesis, we use a KNN-based method to directly evaluate the classification performance of different merging outputs.
Specially, for the KNN evaluation (KNN Eval), we first randomly select few-shot labeled data as anchors for each class, then calculate the average Euclidean Distance between the test sample and these anchors for each class, and finally assign the class with the minimum distance as the prediction. The overall process is formulated as:

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{t}=\arg\min_{y_{t}\in Y_{t}}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}d(

represents the randomly selected anchors for label

[\cdot]

4
Analysis of the Misalignment

Through empirical analysis, we observe that there exists a misalignment issue between the merging outputs and the fine-tuned classifier, and this misalignment leads to the undervaluation of
f_{m}
’s capability in the Current Eval.
Therefore, we hypothesize that:
Alleviating the misalignment in latent space could lead to a better evaluation of
f_{m}
.

4.1
Different Transformation Hypothesis

To further explore this misalignment, we hypothesize a transformation between the merged outputs and the fine-tuned outputs, and we propose conjectures based on varying degrees of constraint on this transformation. Specifically, we align the merged outputs with the fine-tuned outputs by optimizing the following objective function:

\arg\min_{G_{t}}||G_{t}(f_{m}(X_{t};\mathbf{\Theta}_{m}))-f_{t}(X_{t};\mathbf{\Theta}_{t})||_{1},

t
, which can be instantiated using the transformations listed in Tabel

G_{t}(\cdot)
are updated, while all other parameters remain frozen. We refer to these merging outputs aligned method as ‘Aligned
f_{m}
’, illustrated in Figure
3

4.2
Further Discussion

Since orthogonal transformations in space are rigid, such as rotations or reflections, these transformations do not alter the intrinsic structure or geometric properties of the manifold. This demonstrates that the merging outputs largely retain the classification characteristics of the fine-tuned outputs. By further analyzing Figure
4
and considering the properties of orthogonal transformations, we can draw the following conclusions:

(1) With only a simple transformation with a limited number of parameters, the misalignment can be captured, achieving surprising classification performance that approaches the upper bound of the fine-tuned model. This further explains why merging outputs deliver strong performance even under direct classification (e.g., KNN Eval), as they preserve the essential information necessary for effective classification.

(2) The outputs of different
f_{m}
contain comparable classification information, and Current Eval does not effectively assess
f_{m}
. Since the performance gap of different Aligned
f_{m}
under the same transformation is much smaller than
f_{m}
.
For example, the performance of Weight Averaging was initially around 10% lower than that of Task Arithmetic, but after alignment, the performance of Aligned Weight Averaging and Aligned Task Arithmetic becomes nearly identical.

These findings demonstrate that the misalignment issue hinders the Current Eval from effectively assessing the capacity of
f_{m}
. Clearly, alleviating the misalignment problem could potentially improve classification performance. Therefore, we hope to explore a new evaluation protocol that can effectively assess different merging methods by alleviating the misalignment.

FT-Classifier Evaluation Protocol

Model merging combines the parameters of multiple fine-tuned models
f_{t}
to produce a merged model
f_{m}
. Consequently, as discussed before, the output of
f_{m}
inevitably shifts in the latent space relative to the original
f_{t}
, making this shift unavoidable.
In this case, using
\mathbf{W}_{t}
, which is aligned well with
f_{t}
, to evaluate
f_{m}
is inappropriate. The evaluation result reflects not the capability of
f_{m}
, but rather its compatibility with the fine-tuned classifier
\mathbf{W}_{t}
.
Moreover, through experiments, we find that simply aligning the merging outputs and the fine-tuned outputs in the latent space can significantly improve the classification performance of
f_{m}
.
Naturally, this motivates us to propose a new and reasonable evaluation protocol, which addresses the misalignment issue to more accurately assess
f_{m}
and achieve improved performance.

Revisiting the objective of model merging, which aims to generate a new model
f_{m}
using
f_{1},...,f_{T}
that performs well across all
T
tasks, we note that the upper bound of
f_{m}
’s performance on a specific task
t
corresponds to the result from fine-tuned model
f_{t}
. Therefore, the objective of model merging is to minimize this performance discrepancy of
f_{m}
and
f_{t}

Ortiz-Jimenez et al. 2023
.
By optimizing this discrepancy, we could directly train an aligned classifier for
f_{m}
. That is, in the new evaluation protocol, we introduce an additional post-processing step while keeping the parameter scale and network structure unchanged, referred to as FT-Classifier Eval, which is defined as:

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\mathbf{W}_{t}^{\prime}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}g(f_{m}(X_{t};\mathbf{\Theta}_{m})\!^{\top}\!\mathbf{W}_{t}^{\prime},f_{t}(X_{t};\mathbf{\Theta}_{t})\!^{\top}\!\mathbf{W}_{t})

(8)

where
g
is a measurement function. In this objective, we instantiate
g
as KL divergence
Kullback and Leibler 1951
, and the new classifier
\mathbf{W}_{t}^{\prime}
is initialized with the value of
\mathbf{W}_{t}
.

In model merging, obtaining few-shot data for each task is a relatively flexible requirement. Many studies use few-shot data to guide the merging methods
Xiao et al. 2023
, and some even rely on large-scale labeled or unlabeled data to determine merging weights
Yang et al. 2024c
;
Jin et al. 2022
or the parameters to be pruned
Ortiz-Jimenez et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2024
. Therefore, we also incorporate few-shot samples for aligned classifier training in FT-Classifier Eval.

Comparable results of Current Eval and FT-Classifier Eval are presented in Figure

, and detailed results, including KNN Eval, utilizing 5-shot samples are shown Table

. From the Figure, we observe that after simple fine-tuning even on 1-shot samples, FT-Classifier Eval performs significantly better than the Current Eval. This further demonstrates that the misalignment issue is highly manageable. Such a lightweight and computationally feasible post-processing operation facilitates the evaluation of
f_{m}
.

6.5
FT-Classifier vs. Surgery

The related work Surgery
Yang et al. 2024b
characterizes the overall difference between the merging outputs and the fine-tuned outputs as “representation bias”, and reduces this bias by training additional parameters on a large amount of unlabeled data.

We argue that merging models naturally alters the output distribution (representation bias) but does not necessarily harm classification performance. The main cause of performance decline is the misalignment between the merging output and the fine-tuned classifier. We also show that this misalignment is simple (converging to an orthogonal transformation) and does not affect the intrinsic properties of merging outputs. Consequently, it can be mitigated by fine-tuning an aligned classifier for each task with very few samples. In addition, the FT-Classifier can also keep the parameter scale and network structure unchanged. In the context of model merging, we believe that such a lightweight and computationally efficient approach is more favorable. Results in Figure
7
demonstrate that the FT-Classifier outperforms Surgery when only a small number of samples are available.

Merging Weight Based Methods

Instead of assigning an averaging weight for each fine-tuned model, some methods utilize dataset statistics or gradients to adjust the merging weights.
Fisher Merging
Matena and Raffel 2022
computes per-weight importance for each model by the Fisher Information matrix, and reweights the weights with this importance factor during merging.
RegMean
Jin et al. 2022
conducts a local linear regression problem that minimizes prediction differences between the merged model and the individual models, and the closed-form solution is the merging weight.
AdaMerging
Yang et al. 2024c
autonomously learns task-wise or layer-wise merging coefficients without using the original training data. Specifically, it employs entropy minimization on unlabeled test samples as a surrogate objective to iteratively refine the merging coefficients.

Task Vector Based Methods

The task vector indicates the offset of a certain task based on the pre-trained model in the model parameter space. We introduce the following latest methods except for the representative referred to before. Motivated by model pruning that eliminating unnecessary parameters could also maintain performance, Dare
Yu et al. 2024
reduces potential conflicts in the delta parameters by randomly dropping. To mitigate forgetting while training, MagMax
Marczak et al. 2024
combines sequential fine-tuning with maximum-magnitude weight selection to enable effective knowledge integration across tasks. TALL-masks
Wang et al. 2024
localizes task-specific information within the multi-task vector by deactivating irrelevant parts for each task using binary masks. Model Tailor
Zhu et al. 2024
derives a sparse mask to identify the “model patch” using a fusion of salience and sensitivity analysis, then introduces a compensation mechanism to “decorate the patch”, enhancing performance on both target and original tasks.

8
Conclusion

Model merging combines multiple fine-tuned models into a single one in the parameter space, enabling multi-task ability without full datasets or extra fine-tuning. In this paper, we first identify the misalignment issue between merging outputs and fine-tuned classifiers in classification tasks and show that merging outputs already contain necessary classification information. We then prove that this misalignment can be converged as an orthogonal transformation, offering insights into misalignment alleviation. We finally introduce an enhanced evaluation protocol, FT-Classifier Eval, by fine-tuning an aligned classifier with few-shot examples, boosting evaluation quality with minimal time cost.

In the ViT series of experiments, the image input is processed through the image encoder, and text inputs (label names) are processed through the text encoder, obtaining the image embedding
\mathbf{E}_{image}
and text embeddings
\mathbf{E}_{text}
respectively. Classification is performed by matching
\mathbf{E}_{image}
with
\mathbf{E}_{text}
.
In the settings of most model merging papers, the text encoder is frozen, meaning that
\mathbf{E}_{text}
remains unchanged. In this case, we consider
\mathbf{E}_{text}
can be understood as the classifier parameter
\mathbf{W}_{t}
, that is, initializing the classifier with label name embeddings. This approach is consistent with the way many text classification methods initialize label embeddings.
