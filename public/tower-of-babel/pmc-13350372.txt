Evolving Beyond Anatomy
Authors: Doyeon Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: completeness of revascularization, coronary artery disease, Murray law-based QFR, pullback pressure gradient index, quantitative flow ratio

Relief of angina and the subsequent improvement in quality of life remain the fundamental objectives of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in patients with chronic coronary syndromes. However, despite significant continuous advancements in stent design, deployment techniques, and adjunctive pharmacotherapy over the past few decades, recurrent or residual angina after PCI continues to present a substantial clinical challenge.1,2 Historically, persistent symptoms after PCI were primarily attributed to anatomical and mechanical factors, such as suboptimal stent implantation or visually unrecognized incomplete revascularization in remote epicardial vessels. As the routine integration of wire-based fractional flow reserve and instantaneous wave-free ratio became standard clinical practice, it became unequivocally evident that visual angiographic assessment alone is intrinsically limited and highly insufficient for accurately predicting physiological ischemia.3 Consequently, optimizing post-PCI physiological outcomes has emerged as a clinical imperative, particularly given that invasive physiologic assessments reveal that 10% to 20% of angiographically successful procedures still exhibit significant residual epicardial ischemia, largely secondary to unrecognized focal pressure gradients, unmasked diffuse disease, or suboptimal stent expansion.4,5

To comprehend the mechanisms driving these persistent symptoms, it is essential to recognize the critical importance of physiologically optimal PCI. Recent evidence, particularly the prespecified analysis of the ISCHEMIA (International Study of Comparative Health Effectiveness With Medical and Invasive Approaches) trial, demonstrates that achieving functionally complete revascularization is paramount for maximizing improvements in angina-related health status.6 The physiological benefit derived from optimal PCI is highly dependent on the baseline phenotype of the treated vessel. Using invasive pullback pressure gradients, Collet et al demonstrated that patients with predominantly focal coronary artery disease achieve significantly greater functional gains and angina relief following PCI than those with diffuse disease patterns.7 This observation raises a crucial clinical question: if operators could guarantee functionally complete revascularization for every patient, would the gap in symptom relief between focal and diffuse disease simply disappear? However, clinical practice reveals a persistent discrepancy. Even when a PCI is a complete physiological success—meaning all epicardial ischemia is objectively eliminated—some patients still continue to suffer from residual angina. The fact that symptoms can persist despite “perfect” epicardial stenting highlights a critical gap in our clinical understanding. It suggests that achieving optimal epicardial hemodynamics is not the ultimate cure-all. In addition, it emphasizes the need to understand how a vessel's baseline disease pattern intrinsically dictates the patient's ultimate symptomatic outcome, setting the stage for the current investigation.

In this subanalysis of the ASET-Japan (Acetyl Salicylic Elimination Trial JAPAN) pilot study published in this issue of JACC: Asia, Miyashita et al8 systematically investigate this relationship, providing valuable insights into the limitations of epicardial revascularization across different physiological disease phenotypes. The ASET-Japan pilot study enrolled patients with chronic coronary syndromes characterized by relatively low anatomical complexity, reflected by a median anatomical SYNTAX score of 7.0. Within this cohort, the investigators used retrospective CoreLab Murray's law-based quantitative flow ratio (uFR) assessments to evaluate 186 patients, defining the concept of “Adequacy of Flow” (AoF) as a rigorous composite endpoint. To achieve AoF, patients were required to demonstrate functionally complete revascularization, wherein all untreated vessels maintained a uFR >0.80, and optimal post-PCI hemodynamics, wherein all treated vessels achieved a postprocedural uFR >0.90. Concurrently, the baseline disease phenotype was categorized using the pre-PCI pullback pressure gradients index, dividing the lesions into focal and diffuse patterns.

The analysis demonstrated a significant interaction between the baseline physiological disease pattern and the attainment of physiologically optimal results. Notably, achieving true functional AoF proved challenging, occurring in only 61.3% of the study population despite the universal use of intravascular imaging guidance. In patients who failed to achieve AoF, the baseline disease phenotype did not significantly predict the presence of residual angina at the 2-year follow-up. This finding suggests that when revascularization is physiologically incomplete, the persistent macrovascular epicardial ischemia remains the primary, overriding determinant of the patient's anginal symptoms, masking any nuanced effects of the underlying disease pattern. However, a critical divergence in clinical trajectories was observed among the 114 patients who successfully achieved AoF. In this subgroup with theoretically optimized epicardial hemodynamics, patients presenting with a baseline focal disease pattern experienced substantial and sustained symptom relief, with 93.3% reporting complete freedom from angina at the 2-year mark. Conversely, patients presenting with a baseline diffuse disease pattern who achieved the exact same optimal hemodynamic result (AoF) exhibited significantly worse health status outcomes, with 22.0% continuing to report persistent residual angina.

The primary narrative of this study centers on the observation that among patients who successfully achieved AoF, the baseline disease pattern profoundly dictated subsequent symptom relief. Conversely, the finding that disease pattern had no impact on symptoms in the non-AoF cohort reinforces a fundamental clinical principle: achieving physiologically complete revascularization (AoF) is an absolute prerequisite for initial symptom relief. Although previous investigations have already established that a diffuse disease pattern reduces the procedural likelihood of actually achieving optimal hemodynamics, the true novelty of the current study lies in its exploration of the physiological aftermath of “successful” interventions.7 The marked divergence in outcomes among the AoF patients forces a critical reappraisal of what it means to effectively treat diffuse CAD.

To understand why optimal post-PCI hemodynamics fail to guarantee clinical resolution in diffuse disease, we must understand the diagnostic modality utilized. Because angiography-derived fractional flow reserve indices, such as uFR, calculate pressure gradients based on the 2-dimensional angiographic silhouette of the epicardial coronary artery, they possess an inherent diagnostic blind spot. Diffuse atherosclerosis frequently lacks a healthy reference segment, leading to the visual underestimation of disease severity. Consequently, it is highly probable that the diffuse pattern observed on angiography acts as an indicator of extensive “hidden” or “masked” diffuse disease that extends throughout the epicardial tree, escaping precise visual and mathematical detection. When an operator treats the most severe focal segment, the uFR algorithm may yield a normalized result above the 0.90 threshold, but this illusion of perfection should not lead us to overlook the persistent ischemic burden imposed by these masked lesions in other segments.

More importantly, coronary diffuse disease can be more accompanied by a broader spectrum of nonepicardial pathophysiological derangements, such as coronary microvascular dysfunction, generalized endothelial dysfunction, and an increased propensity for vasospasm.9, 10, 11 Achieving a mathematically optimal epicardial AoF in a diffusely diseased vessel effectively eliminates the dominant macrovascular stenosis. However, if a patient continues to experience debilitating angina despite this objective epicardial success, the baseline diffuse pattern provides a potential diagnostic clue. It suggests that the residual symptoms are likely no longer driven by the treated macrovascular obstruction, but rather by the inadvertent unmasking of underlying microvascular ischemia or vasospastic hyperreactivity. This does not imply that every diffuse lesion should be treated primarily as ischemia and no obstructive coronary artery disease (INOCA) or angina and no obstructive coronary artery disease (ANOCA) following intervention. Rather, it highlights that when symptoms do persist in these specific patients, clinicians must pivot their diagnostic framework to consider these complex, systemic vascular disorders as the primary culprits.

The findings of this current study must be carefully interpreted with due consideration of several methodological limitations. First, the relatively small sample size, particularly when subdividing the cohort into AoF and non-AoF groups, limits the statistical power of the multivariable adjustments. Second, the absence of baseline Seattle Angina Questionnaire data restricts the ability to definitively differentiate preexisting refractory angina from de novo angina that may have developed due to disease progression over the 2-year follow-up period. Third, the retrospective nature of the CoreLab physiological assessments renders the conclusions primarily hypothesis generating. Finally, the study did not systematically incorporate adjunctive invasive evaluations of microvascular dysfunction or vasomotor disorders. The lack of concurrent measurements of the Index of Microcirculatory Resistance or Coronary Flow Reserve prevents a definitive mechanistic confirmation that masked epicardial disease and INOCA/ANOCA pathophysiology are the primary culprits for the residual angina observed in the diffuse phenotype group.

Notwithstanding these methodological constraints, the clinical implications of these findings are significant and merit application in routine clinical practice. The integration of baseline physiological phenotyping and the rigorous assessment of post-PCI physiologic status provides a synergistic framework for individualized patient management. For patients presenting with focal lesions, achieving epicardial functional complete revascularization through meticulous stenting is highly predictive of optimal symptom relief. However, when baseline evaluation reveals a diffuse disease pattern, operators must acknowledge that achieving functional complete revascularization, while crucial for mitigating major adverse events, may not entirely eradicate chest pain. The baseline diffuse phenotype should serve as a proactive tool for expectation management. If symptoms persist after a physiologically successful intervention in a diffuse vessel, operators should not merely suspect mechanical procedural failure. Instead, this clinical scenario should trigger a comprehensive evaluation for coexisting INOCA or ANOCA. These patients may ultimately require invasive microcirculatory assessments to formally diagnose their specific endotypes, alongside aggressive, tailored pharmacological therapies directed at the microvascular and endothelial domains. By evolving beyond an exclusively anatomical approach and recognizing when to consider hidden mechanisms in symptomatic patients, physicians can advance toward more sophisticated and patient-centered management of post-PCI angina.
