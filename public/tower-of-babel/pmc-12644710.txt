One-pot green synthesis of Fe3O4@ZnO core–shell nanocomposites with multifunctional properties
Authors: Mahsa Eslamdoust, Fayezeh Samari, Saeed Yousefinejad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces an economical, one-step, and eco-friendly method for synthesizing Fe3O4@ZnO magnetic core–shell nanocomposites (Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs) using an aqueous leaf extract of Cordia myxa (C. myxa). The effects of zinc salt concentration and calcination temperature were investigated, and the products were characterized using XRD, FE-SEM, TEM, BET, FT-IR, TGA, and VSM techniques. The optimized nanocomposite was spherical, with an average size of 23.01 nm, exhibited superparamagnetic behavior, had a point of zero charge at pH 7.3, and a specific surface area of 18.2 m² g⁻1. It showed moderate antioxidant activity and antibacterial effects against Gram-positive (Listeria, Micrococcus) and Gram-negative (Salmonella) bacteria. The adsorption performance for Congo red (CR) dye was evaluated and optimized via Central Composite Design (CCD) in Response Surface Methodology (RSM). Under optimal conditions (adsorbent dosage 11.6 mg L⁻1, pH 6.0, contact time 74 min), nearly 100% dye removal was achieved, highlighting the nanocomposite’s strong potential for pollutant removal and environmental remediation.Keywords: Fe3O4@ZnO magnetic nanocomposites, Green synthesis, Antibacterial activity, Antioxidant activity, Congo red, Central composite design, AdsorptionSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Nanotechnology has received significant attention in recent years due to its unique physicochemical properties and broad applications1–3. Metal and metal oxide nanoparticles (NPs), in particular, possess a high surface area and abundant active sites, which provide catalytic, optical, electronic, antibacterial, and magnetic functionalities2. Among them, transition metal oxides such as iron oxide, especially Fe3O4 magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs), and zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) have emerged as highly versatile nanomaterials owing to their chemical stability, cost-effectiveness, and environmental compatibility4,5.

Fe3O4 MNPs are especially attractive because of their strong magnetic response, superparamagnetic behavior, chemical stability, and low toxicity, which facilitate their easy recovery and reuse in water treatment processes6,7. On the other hand, ZnO NPs, as wide band-gap semiconductors (3.1–3.3 eV) with high activated energy (60 meV), exhibit excellent optical, catalytic, and antimicrobial properties, combined with biocompatibility and thermal stability8–10. The integration of ZnO with Fe3O4 into a core–shell nanostructure provides synergistic advantages: ZnO enhances photocatalytic and antibacterial activity1, while Fe3O4 imparts magnetic separability and prevents nanoparticle aggregation7. This unique combination makes Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs promising candidates for environmental remediation and biomedical applications.

Different chemical and physical methods have been used to fabricate metal/metal oxide nanoparticles such as the sol-gel technique11, co-precipitation method12, sonochemical method13, electrochemical route, 14, and hydrothermal process15. However, most of these routes have disadvantages such as high cost, high energy consumption, pollution from chemical precursors, and generation of hazardous byproducts2,16,17. Recently, to overcome these limitation, environmentally friendly and green routes have been proposed as alternative methods2. Green synthesis of nanoparticles involves the formation of nanostructures capped by organic compounds derived from living organisms or plants. Compared to conventional physical and chemical methods, plant-mediated synthesis offers advantages such as wide availability, safety, simplicity, low cost, non-toxicity, eco-friendliness, avoidance of high energy consumption, elimination of hazardous chemicals, and minimization of byproducts1,2,5. Moreover, the great diversity of plants and the presence of numerous phyto-compounds in different parts enable the production of nanoparticles with unique properties and applications2,18. Plant extracts usually contain terpenoids, proteins, polyphenols, flavonoids, etc. which act as natural reducing agents for metal ions as well as capping agents to prevent the aggregation of NPs1,5,16.

The discharge of large amounts of synthetic dyes from textile, leather, food, and cosmetic industries has become a major environmental problem19,20. These dyes are often non-biodegradable, toxic, and even carcinogenic, posing severe risks to ecosystems and human health21,22. Among the available treatment methods, adsorption is considered one of the most effective due to its simplicity, low cost, high efficiency, and environmental compatibility23,24,. In this context, metal oxide NPs are particularly attractive adsorbents because of their small size, large surface area, and high adsorption capacity19,21. Fe3O4 MNPs are widely studied for their strong magnetization, stability, low toxicity, and easy separation with an external magnet5. However, their tendency to aggregate reduces the availability of active sites6. Coating Fe3O4 with ZnO not only prevents agglomeration but also enhances adsorption efficiency19,25. ZnO itself is a biocompatible and thermally stable semiconductor with photocatalytic, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties, and is even recognized as safe by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)26–31. Therefore, combining Fe3O4 and ZnO into a core–shell structure provides multifunctional nanocomposites with synergistic catalytic, antibacterial, and magnetic properties, making them promising candidates for wastewater treatment applications32.

From above, in the present study, Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs were phyto-synthesized through an economical and one-step route using C. myxa leaf aqueous extract as a reducing and capping agent. The effect of zinc precursor mass ratio and calcination temperature on the formation of Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs was investigated. The phyto-synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs were characterized using various techniques such as XRD, FE-SEM, EDS, TEM, FT-IR, TGA, and BET. The antioxidant activity of the fabricated nanocomposite was studied using the DPPH method. The antibacterial activity of the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs was evaluated against Gram-positive (Listeria and Micrococcus) and Gram-negative (Salmonella) bacteria. Furthermore, the removal efficiency of the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs toward Congo red (CR) adsorption was investigated. CR is an anionic azo dye with toxic, mutagenic, and carcinogenic impacts that cause harmful effects on the environment and marine ecology5,33. To achieve optimal adsorption conditions with maximum efficiency, a minimal number of experiments, and reduced consumption of chemicals, Central Composite Design (CCD) was employed in the in Response Surface Methodology (RSM). In contrast to previous reports on Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs, the present work introduces a one-step, plant-mediated green synthesis using C. myxa leaf aqueous extract as both a reducing and capping agent. Earlier studies on Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs have generally relied on multi-step chemical routes or non-plant-based methods, with limited attention to environmentally friendly, cost-effective approaches; one-step green synthesis of Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs is still very limited38–42. Moreover, the application of Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs for the removal of CR dye has been only sparsely explored, and, where investigated, the synthesis route was not based on a one-step phyto-synthesis34–37. Importantly, prior works have often optimized adsorption conditions using single-variable approaches, without comprehensively evaluating the combined effects of key parameters such as adsorbent dosage, pH, contact time, and initial dye concentration. In this study, a simple, eco-friendly, and economical synthesis method for Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs was established. Furthermore, CCD was employed in RSM to systematically optimize CR adsorption performance. This combination of green synthesis, multifunctional evaluation, and robust statistical optimization highlights the novelty of this approach and provides a valuable reference for future environmental remediation research.

Materials and methods

Materials

The chemicals used were of analytical grade and were directly utilized without purification. Anhydrous iron(III) chloride (FeCl3) (≥ 99%), zinc(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2.6H2O) (≥ 98%), hydrochloric acid (HCl) (37%), and sodium hydroxide pellets (NaOH) (≥ 99%) were procured from Merck Chemical Co. (Germany). Congo red and 2,2′–diphenyl-2-picrylhydrasyl (DPPH) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. Millipore deionized ultrapure water was used for solution preparation. Glassware was washed with aqua regia, followed by double-distilled water. pH adjustments were made with 0.1 M HCl or NaOH solution as necessary.

Preparation of C. myxa leaf extract

A voucher specimen of C. myxa has been deposited in the herbarium of the Faculty of Agriculture, University of Hormozgan, Hormozgan, Iran. The taxonomy of plant was confirmed and authenticated by Dr. Mansoore Shamili (https://orcid.org/0000-0001-7967-438X), Faculty of Agriculture and Natural Science, University of Hormozgan, Iran.

The leaves of Cordia myxa (order Boraginales, family Boraginaceae) were collected from Rooydar, Hormozgan province, Iran (57°6′E, 60°3′N). This plant is easily cultivated, grows abundantly in different parts of the province, and is commonly found in homes and gardens. Thus, it does not require a harvesting permit from the Iran Environmental Protection Agency or the Forestry Organization. Considering its therapeutic properties in traditional medicine, its extraction does not pose toxicological risks to humans and animals, and was carried out in coordination with the permission from Dr. Mansoore Shamili, Faculty of Agriculture and Natural Science, University of Hormozgan, Iran.

Fresh and intact C. myxa leaves were washed with tap water, rinsed with double-distilled water, and air-dried. The dried leaves were finely ground using a mixer grinder. A portion of leaf powder (10.0 g) was mixed with 150 mL of deionized water in a round-bottom flask and subjected to reflux at 80 °C for 30 min. The mixture was allowed to cool to room temperature, filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and stored at 4 °C for future use.

Preparation of Fe3O4 MNPs

To synthesize Fe3O4 MNPs by a green method, 100 mL of 81.4 mM FeCl3 solution was combined with 5.0 mL of leaf extract in a 250 mL round-bottom flask. The mixture was refluxed at 80 °C with stirring. After 60 min, 1.0 M NaOH was slowly added over 40 min to achieve a pH of ∼10. The reaction mixture was stirred for another 60 min, then allowed to cool to room temperature. The black precipitate was separated using a magnet, washed with deionized water, and dried in a vacuum oven at 90 °C for 8 h.

Synthesis of Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs

For the green synthesis of Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs, Fe3O4 MNPs were first synthesized as described in Sect. 2.3. Without separating the Fe3O4 MNPs, the reaction temperature was adjusted to 90 °C, and 2.38 g of Zn(NO3)2.6H2O was added to the mixture. Next, 1.0 M NaOH was slowly added over 40 min to adjust the solution pH to ∼10, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 15 min. The resulting nanocomposite was cooled to room temperature, separated using an external magnet, washed several times with deionized water, and finally calcined at 500 °C for 2 h.

The effect of zinc content was investigated by synthesizing nanocomposite, Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs, with different Zn precursor amounts. The molar ratios of FeCl3 to Zn precursor were 4:1, 2:1, and 1:1, resulting in Fe3O4@ZnO (4:1), Fe3O4@ZnO (2:1), and Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs, respectively. The quantity of iron precursor remained constant, leading to nanocomposites with different ZnO content. Additionally, the effect of calcination temperature was studied by subjecting the optimized product to 300, 500, and 700 °C in air for 2 h.

Characterization techniques

UV-Vis absorption spectra were conducted using a 4802 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Unico, China) with a 10 mm quartz cuvette. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed using a PANalytical X’Pert PRO diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) in the 2θ range of 20°–80°. Field emission scanning electron microscopy–energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (FESEM–EDS, Zeiss Sigma-VP, Germany) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Zeiss-EM10C, 100 kV) were used to study morphology, size, and elemental composition. Magnetic were evaluated at room temperature using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM, Meghnatis Daghigh Kavir Company LBKFB). Specific surface area and pore size were determined using a Brunauer, Emmett, Teller surface area analyzer (Microtrac Bel Corp BEISORP Mini (BET)). Prior to nitrogen adsorption, samples were degassed at 150 °C. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was carried out using a TGA-7 analyzer (Perkin-Elmer corporate, USA) under nitrogen at a heating rate of 10 °C min⁻1 from room temperature up to 750 °C. FT-IR spectra were recorded with a Bruker Alpha FT-IR spectrometer (Germany) equipped with a diamond attenuated total reflection (ATR), recording spectra in the 4000 and 500 cm⁻1 range with a spectral resolution of 4 cm− 1. pH values were measured with a Jenway 3520 bench pH meter (England).

Determination of point of zero charges (pHPZC)

The point of zero charges (pHPZC) of the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs was determined using the pH drift method43,44. At pHPZC, the net charge on the surface of the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs is equal to zero. According to this method43, a 0.010 g of the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs was added to 45.0 mL NaCl solution, as an inert electrolyte, in a series of 50 mL falcon tubes. The pH of the NaCl solution in each falcon had been initially adjusted to a pH value (pHinitial) ranging from 2.0 to 10.0, with a 1.0 unit increment, using HCl and NaOH. After 24 h of shaking, the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs were magnetically separated and isolated from the solution. The pHfinal values were measured, and ∆pH (pHfinal-pHinitial) was plotted against the pHinitial. The point where ΔpH = 0 was considered the pHPZC45,46.

Antibacterial activity testing

The antibacterial activity of Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs was investigated against Gram-positive (Listeria monocytogenes, Micrococcus luteus) and Gram-negative (Salmonella typhimurium) bacteria using the agar disk diffusion method. Three bacterial species were sourced from the fungal and bacterial collection of Iran. Fresh bacterial cultures were prepared in nutrient broth at 37 °C and adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard. Subsequently, approximately 106 colony-forming units (CFU) of the three bacterial species were inoculated on Mueller–Hinton broth, with discs impregnated with varying concentrations of the synthesized MNCs (100, 300, and 500 mg L⁻¹) and aqueous leaf extract. These discs were then placed on agar plates. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and the inhibition zone diameters were measured in millimeters. A standard disk containing 10 µg of erythromycin was used for comparison purposes. All experiments were carried out in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The inhibition zones of synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO in different ratio of Fe3O4 and ZnO was statistically compared with the inhibition zone of control antibacterial agent (erythromycin) using Welch’s t-test because of normality and limited number of replicates.

Experimental design using CCD-RSM

Experimental designs aim to achieve maximum performance while minimizing the number of experimental runs and production costs53. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) with a Central Composite Design (CCD) was applied to optimize CR adsorption. In this study, four independent variables were selected: solution pH (A), Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs dosage (B), dye concentration (C), and contact time (D) (Table 1). Experimental data were analyzed using Design-Expert software (Version 7.0, Stat-Ease, Minneapolis, USA). Each factor was varied at five different levels (-α, -1, 0, + 1, and + α) as outlined in Table 1. The experimental design comprised 30 runs, including three replicates at the center point, to optimize the levels of the independent factors A (solution pH: 3.0–11.0), B (Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs dosage: 0.0–16.0 mg L− 1), C (dye concentration: 5.0–45.0 mg L− 1), and D (contact time: 20–80 min) (Table 2). The statistical significance of the model was evaluated by analysis of variance (ANOVA), and model adequacy was checked by the coefficient of determination (R²), adjusted R², and lack-of-fit tests.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of the synthesized nanostructures

XRD analysis

Powder XRD was used to confirm the crystal structure of the synthesized nanocomposites. In Fig. 1(A), the XRD patterns of Fe3O4 MNPs and Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs with varying molar ratios are shown. A study was conducted to investigate the impact of the molar ratio of iron to zinc source and the calcination temperature on the formation of magnetic nanocomposites. The prepared nanocomposites were characterized using XRD.

The XRD analysis of the synthesized nanocomposites with different Fe: Zn molar ratios revealed characteristic peaks of Fe3O4 MNPs at 2θ values of 30.3°, 35.4°, 43.1°, 53.7°, and 62.6°, corresponding to the (220), (311), (400), (422), and (440) planes of cubic spinel Fe₃O₄, respectively (JCPDS Data Card No. 88–0315)56,57. Additionally, characteristic peaks of ZnO were observed at 2θ values of 31.8°, 34.5°, 36.4°, 47.5°, 56.7°, 62.9°, 66.5°, 68.1°, and 69.2°, confirming the formation of a hexagonal wurtzite ZnO nanostructure (JCPDS Data Card No. 99e0111)7,32,58. No additional peaks of impurities were observed, confirming the successful formation of Fe3O4@ZnO core–shell nanostructures59. Moreover, increasing the amount of Zn precursor enhanced the intensity of ZnO diffraction peaks, confirming the higher ZnO content in the composites7.

Subsequently, the effect of calcination temperature on the formation of Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs was investigated. XRD patterns revealed that calcination at different temperatures (300, 500, and 700 °C) under air atmosphere had varying effects on the crystalline structure of the nanocomposites (Fig. 1(B)). Specifically, it was observed that calcination at 300 °C led to a reduced crystallite size with defects in the nanostructure, resulting from the emergence of broad diffraction peaks with lower intensity60. At 500 °C, the diffraction peaks of ZnO became sharper with increased intensity, indicating an improvement in the integrity of the crystal structure61. However, at 700 °C, the intensity of the diffraction peaks decreased, and some diffraction peaks corresponding to ZnFe2O4 were observed62. Based on these results, magnetic nanocomposites with a 1:1 molar ratio calcined at 500 °C were selected for further studies.

FE-SEM and EDS studies of the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs

The morphologies, sizes, and shapes of the Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs with varying zinc contents were investigated using FE-SEM analysis, and the summarized results are presented in Fig. 2(A)-2(C). The analysis revealed a compact morphology with closely assembled particles in the nanoscale range, predominantly spherical with nearly uniform size. Additionally, the particle size distribution diagrams for the magnetic nanocomposites with different Zn contents are depicted in Fig. 2(D–F). Notably, the synthesized magnetic nanocomposites at different molar ratios exhibited similar average sizes (24.40, 26.87, and 23.01 nm for 4:1, 2:1, and 1:1 molar ratios, respectively). Furthermore, the Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs displayed a narrower size distribution compared to other MNCs, indicating higher uniformity.

FE-SEM micrographs of the synthesized and Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs with molar ratio of (A) 4:1, (B) 2:1, and (C) 1:1; respective particle size distribution profile of (D) 4:1, (E) 2:1, and (F) 1:1; and EDX elemental analysis Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs of (G) 4:1, (H) 2:1, and (I) 1:1.

Elemental composition analysis was also conducted using EDS to explore the composition of the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs (Fig. 2(G–I)). The EDS spectra revealed signals corresponding to zinc, iron, oxygen, carbon, chlorine, and nitrogen. The zinc content was found to be 22.7%, 30.5%, and 45.1% (wt%), while the iron content was measured at 41.4%, 39.7%, and 27.7% (wt%) with increasing zinc precursor content (corresponding to Fe3O4@ZnO (4:1), Fe3O4@ZnO (2:1), and Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs, respectively). These findings confirmed the successful formation of Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs and the increased prevalence of ZnO particles with higher Zn precursor ratios. However, the presence of C, Cl, and N in the analysis was attributed to residual phytochemicals from the plant extracts, representing organic materials attached to the Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs63.

FT-IR analysis of the synthesized ZnO@Fe3O4 MNCs

FT-IR spectroscopy was employed to identify the potential biomolecules responsible for the stabilization and coating during the fabrication of Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs. FT-IR spectra of the crude extract, synthesized Fe3O4 MNPs, and Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs are presented in Fig. 3. The spectra display broad absorption peaks at 3500–3100 cm− 1, associated with O–H stretching and bending vibrations7.

FT-IR spectra of C. myxa leaf extract, Fe3O4, and Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs synthesized with the different ratios of zinc precursor via green synthesis.

Additionally, the FT-IR spectrum of the extract revealed peaks at 3500 − 3100, 1586, 1382, and 1053 cm− 1, indicating the presence of saturated hydrocarbons (Csp3–H), carbonyl groups (C = O), C = C stretching in the aromatic ring, and C–H stretching vibrations, respectively. These peaks suggest the existence of flavonoids and other phenolic compounds in the extract, which are likely responsible for reducing metal ions and stabilizing nanoparticles64. The FT-IR analysis of the synthesized Fe3O4 MNPs and Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs demonstrates noticeable differences in the shape and location of signals compared to the leaf extract, indicating interactions between the formed nanomaterials and phytochemicals, as well as their capping65. It can be inferred that polyphenols and other plant compounds play a significant role in the production and stabilization of the nanomaterials. Furthermore, the peaks observed at 620 cm− 1 and ⁓535 cm− 1 were attributed to the vibrations of Fe‒O and Zn‒O. The characteristic absorption bands confirm the coexistence of Fe₃O₄ and ZnO in the core–shell nanocomposite28,58,62.

TEM study of the optimized Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs

TEM images provide valuable insights into the structure of nanoparticles. TEM image of Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs (Fig. 4) clearly confirmed the core–shell structure, where the darker Fe3O4 cores were surrounded by lighter ZnO shells. In such images, elements with higher electron density typically appear darker; hence, the Fe3O4 cores appear darker than the ZnO shell due to the difference in electron scattering during irradiation55. As shown in Fig. 4, MNCs exhibited nearly spherical morphology with some degree of agglomeration due to magnetic interactions.

TEM image of 1:1 molar ratio of Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs calcined at 500 °C.

Vibrating sample magnetometer studies of the optimized Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs

The shape of nanoparticle hysteresis loops is influenced by the preparation method, stoichiometric ratio of precursors, and crystalline phase62. VSM measurements (Fig. 5) illustrate the superparamagnetic behavior of both the as-prepared Fe3O4 MNPs and the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs under optimum conditions (Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs calcined at 500 ˚C), as evidenced by the absence of remanence or coercivity in the magnetic hysteresis loops66,67.

Vibrating-sample magnetometer patterns of Fe3O4 MNPS and Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs.

The hysteresis loop of the synthesized Fe3O4 MNPs displayed weak superparamagnetic behavior with a saturation magnetization (Ms) of 6.1 emu g⁻¹. Following ZnO coating, the MS of Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs decreased to 5.2 emu/g compared to the pure Fe3O4 MNPs (6.1 emu g⁻¹). This decrease can be attributed to the diamagnetic ZnO layer encapsulating the magnetic cores, resulting from the combination of non-magnetic ZnO with Fe3O4. Nonetheless, the MS value remains sufficient for separation of the nanocomposite from the aqueous solution using an external magnet. The magnetic response of the Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs suspension under an external magnetic field is depicted in Fig. 5 (inset).

BET analysis

The surface area of Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs was analyzed using BET adsorption-desorption isotherms with liquid nitrogen as the adsorbate. BET analysis (Fig. 6) revealed that Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs exhibited a type IV isotherm with H₃ hysteresis, indicating a mesoporous structure. 55,67. The Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs exhibited a specific BET surface area of 18.20 m² g⁻¹ and an average pore diameter of 16.1 nm. The BET surface area and BJH pore size distribution consistently verifies the mesoporous nature of the Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs. Importantly, such mesoporous nanocomposites find diverse applications in biomedical and catalytic fields5.

N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms and pore size distribution profile (inset) of the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed to assess the thermal stability of Fe₃O₄@ZnO (1:1) MNCs. As shown in Fig. 7, the TGA curves revealed three distinct weight reduction phases during thermal degradation. Initially, weight loss occurred at temperatures below 120 °C, attributed to the evaporation of physically adsorbed water from the composite surface68. Subsequently, a gradual 1% weight reduction was observed within the 120–405 °C range, signifying the decomposition of residual organic content in the biosynthesized nanocomposite. Finally, at temperatures exceeding 405 °C, a third stage of weight loss occurred due to the removal of residual organic material chelated with the synthesized nanocomposite5. Remarkably, the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs exhibited stability at higher temperatures, specifically up to 620 °C. Across the entire temperature range, the mass loss percentage for the Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs remained approximately 5.4%, underscoring their exceptional thermal stability.

Thermogravimetric analysis of the Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs.

Determination of pHpzc

The pHPZC represents the pH at which the surface of the considered substance carries no net electric charge. In the context of nanostructures, pHPZC plays a vital role in characterizing surface charge states and their impact on adsorption processes69. For the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs, the pHPZC was determined to be 7.3 using the pH drift method43, across a wide pH range of 2.0–10.0, as depicted in Fig. 8. When the pH exceeds the pHPZC, the surface of the MNCs becomes negatively charged, leading to a preferential adsorption of positively charged substances. Conversely, when the pH is below the pHPZC, the surface charge becomes positive, favoring the adsorption of negatively charged pollutants70,71.

Determination of the pHPZC of the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs by pH drift method.

Study of antioxidant activity using DPPH

The DPPH radical scavenging test is commonly used to evaluate the antioxidant capacity of synthesized compounds and their ability to neutralize free radicals48. In this study, the free radical scavenging capabilities of C. myxa leaf extract and Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs were assessed using the DPPH assay and compared to the standard antioxidant, ascorbic acid. The results were expressed in terms of IC50 (half maximal inhibitory concentration). DPPH, a pink-colored free radical with λmax = 525 nm in ethanolic solution, transitions to a yellow color when reduced by an antioxidant due to a proton transfer reaction1,72.

The determined IC50 values for C. myxa leaf extract and Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs were 156.25 µg mL⁻¹ and 201.05 µg mL⁻¹, respectively, while the standard value was 12.20 µg mL⁻¹. The IC50 of the synthesized Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs was higher than some previously studied plant-mediated ZnO NPs73,74. However, it was lower compared to the standard, indicating inhibitory activity and thereby confirming the antioxidant potential of the nanocomposite. This potential can be attributed to the presence of phytochemical compounds or residual organic content in the leaf extract, mainly flavonoids and phenols, which act as a capping agents for the nanocomposite75,76. Additionally, electron density transfer from the oxygen atom of metal oxide nanostructures to the odd electron in the nitrogen atom in DPPH may contribute to the decrease in intensity of the n→π* transition at 525 nm77.

Optimum conditions for CR removal process

After validating the multi-parameter model discussed in the previous section, simplex optimization was employed to determine the optimal conditions. CCD-based optimization indicated that the optimal conditions were adsorbent dosage 11.6 mg L⁻¹, pH 6.0, and contact time 74 min, yielding nearly 100% CR removal. These results highlight the effectiveness of statistical optimization compared to conventional one-variable approaches.

Solution pH significantly influences adsorption processes through electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions, as well as hydrogen bonding. It impacts not only the charge density of the adsorbent surface but also the ionization degree of the pollutant molecule or analyte69. The pHPZC of Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs was found to be 7.3 (see Sect. 3.1.8). This indicates that the adsorbent surface carries a positive charge at pH < pHPZC and a negative charge at pH > 7.383,84. CR is an anionic dye with pKa=4.185,86. Thus, at pH < pKa, CR molecules contain more protons, leading to a more positively charged surface of MNCs and electrostatic repulsion between CR and the adsorbent, resulting in reduced adsorption. On the other hand, when 4.1 < pH < pHPZC (7.3), CR becomes deprotonated and forms an anionic species, while the MNCs surface remains positively charged (pH < pHPZC), favoring adsorption via electrostatic attraction. At pH > pHPZC, electrostatic repulsion occurs again, decreasing CR adsorption87. Therefore, the adsorption process is most favorable in the range between pKa and pHPZC. These findings confirm the pivotal role of electrostatic attraction in this adsorption process.

To assess the applicability of the proposed method under optimal conditions, CCD was employed to remove CR from a well water sample located in Kahoorestan, Khamir City, Hormozgan Province, Iran. Specifically, 25 mL of the well water was spiked with a CR standard solution to achieve a concentration of 15.5 mg L− 1. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 6.0, followed by the addition of the adsorbent. The removal of CR was then monitored photometrically, revealing a 100% removal efficiency after 74 min.

Conclusion

In this work, Fe3O4@ZnO core–shell magnetic nanocomposites were successfully synthesized via a one-pot, plant-mediated green route using C. myxa leaf aqueous extract as an affordable and sustainable reducing and capping agent. Unlike most previous reports relying on multi-step or non-plant-based syntheses, this study demonstrates an environmentally benign and cost-effective approach while systematically evaluating the effects of zinc precursor ratio and calcination temperature. Comprehensive characterization, such as XRD, FE-SEM, EDS, TEM, BET, FT-IR, TGA, and VSM analyses, confirmed the formation of well-defined core–shell structures with spherical morphology (~ 23 nm), mesoporous structure, high thermal stability, and superparamagnetic behavior. Among the synthesized samples, Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) calcined at 500 °C exhibited the most desirable physicochemical properties, confirming the synergistic contribution of Fe3O4 and ZnO within the core–shell structure. The optimized Fe3O4@ZnO (1:1) MNCs exhibited moderate antioxidant activity (with an IC50 of 201.05 µg/mL in DPPH assays), significant antibacterial effects against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, and remarkable adsorption efficiency for Congo red dye, achieving nearly complete removal under optimized conditions determined by CCD in combination with RSM. The integration of a green synthesis strategy with multifunctional performance underscores the novelty and potential of these MNCs in both environmental remediation and biomedical applications. These findings highlight the novelty of developing a one-step, phyto-mediated Fe3O4@ZnO MNCs with combined magnetic separability, eco-friendly synthesis, and high functional performance. To advance this research toward real-world deployment, future work should investigate material stability and regeneration over multiple cycles, extend the adsorption studies to a broader range of organic and inorganic contaminants, and evaluate photocatalytic activity under natural sunlight. Furthermore, in-depth mechanistic studies of the antioxidant and antimicrobial properties, alongside biocompatibility assessments, will be essential for biomedical translation. These efforts will provide a foundation for scaling up the synthesis process while maintaining sustainability, thereby bridging laboratory innovation with practical, high-impact applications. Overall, the study introduces a promising candidate for practical applications in environmental remediation and biomedical fields, providing valuable insights for future research on green nanotechnology.
