Increased epicardial adipose tissue is associated with left ventricular reverse remodeling in dilated cardiomyopathy
Authors: Yuanwei Xu, Jiajun Guo, Yangjie Li, Shiqian Wang, Ke Wan, Weihao Li, Jie Wang, Ziqian Xu, Wei Cheng, Jiayu Sun, Qing Zhang, Yuchi Han, Yucheng Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpicardial adipose tissue (EAT) has been suggested to play paradoxical roles in patients with heart failure. The role of EAT in dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) patients remains unclear. We aimed to assess the associations between the dynamic changes EAT and left ventricular reverse remodeling (LVRR) in DCM patients based on baseline and follow-up CMR.MethodsIn this prospective study, we consecutive enrolled DCM patients with baseline and follow-up cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) examinations. All participating patients underwent 1–2 years of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) at follow-up. The EAT was measured as pericardial and epicardial fat thickness, and paracardial fat volume, while the abdominal adiposity was measured in terms of subcutaneous and visceral fat thickness. The univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations of changes in abdominal and epicardial adiposities with the presence of LVRR.ResultsA total of 232 patients (mean age, 45.7 ± 15.1 years, 157 male) at baseline were enrolled. After a period of GDMT with a median duration of 15.5 months (interquartile range, 12.5–19.1 months) all participants underwent follow-up CMR with the same standardized protocol. Patients who reached LVRR showed a significant increment in EAT parameters compared to those who did not. After adjusting for age, sex, and delta changes of body mass index (BMI), the increment of pericardial fat thickness (odds ratio [OR]: 1.53; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.27 to 1.83; p < 0.001), epicardial fat thickness (OR: 2.10; 95% CI: 1.68 to 2.63; p < 0.001), and paracardial fat volume (OR: 1.01; 95% CI: 1.01 to 1.02; p = 0.001) were significantly associated with LVRR.ConclusionsIn DCM patients, the CMR-derived EAT parameters increased after 1–2 years of GDMT and significantly correlated with improved ventricular structure and function, independent of changes in BMI and abdominal adiposity, which may indicate the potential protective role of EAT in DCM patients.Trial registration: URL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: ChiCTR1800017058.Graphical abstractSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-024-02517-3.Keywords: Dilated cardiomyopathy, Cardiac adipose tissue, Reverse remodeling, Guideline-directed medical therapy

Background

The epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) is a unique anatomical structure located between the myocardium and the epicardium, and has been gaining increasing traction for its potential pathophysiological role in heart failure (HF) [1, 2]. EAT is involved in a wide range of processes, including hemodynamic and metabolic status, local and systemic inflammation, and atherogenesis [3]. EAT has been demonstrated with two-way roles in heart failure patients. In HF with preserved ejection (HFpEF) patients, the accumulation of was EAT associated with myocardial impairment and poor prognosis [4, 5]. In reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) patients, thinner epicardial fats i are associated with an increased risk for cardiovascular death and hospitalization, as well as arrhythmic and clinical events [6, 7].

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is an important non-ischemic cause of HF, which is characterized by left ventricular (LV) or biventricular systolic dysfunction and enlargement [8]. However, limited data exist on the role of EAT in DCM patients. While the study by Granér et al. demonstrated significantly larger epicardial and pericardial fat volumes in DCM patients compared to normal controls on 1.5 T magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) [9], Doesch et al. found significantly lower EAT mass among congestive heart failure patients compared to healthy controls [10]. Yamaguchi et al. identified higher EAT volume as an independent predictor of LVRR among DCM patients by using computed tomography (CT) [11]. The roles of EAT in DCM thereby remain inconclusive.

Direct assessment of EAT is often challenged by its thinness and proximity to the myocardium [4]. Although both epicardial thickness [12, 13] and area/volume measurement methods [14, 15] have been proven to measure epicardial fat, the evidence for which EAT measurement is more suitable for DCM patients is still insufficient. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) provides the benefit of high-resolution imaging and versatility in image planes, and was considered as the potential imaging modality for the quantitative assessment of EAT parameters [16]. In addition, the use of phase-sensitive inversion recovery (PSIR) images has also been considered helpful in distinguishing epicardial fat from pericardial effusion, with fatty tissue appearing as bright signals and fluid having a low signal [17]. Therefore, our study provides multiple measures of EAT by using CMR.

Dynamic observation of the changes in EAT after treatment helps provide imaging evidence for understanding the pathophysiological value of EAT in DCM patients. Till now, LVRR has become a clinically important treatment target for DCM patients. Approximately one-third of patients who received guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) have been shown to achieve LVRR [18]. However, there are limited data regarding the correlation between changes in EAT and cardiac structure and function in DCM patients. Our study was thereby performed, with the hypothesis that EAT plays a protective role in DCM, by showing a positive correlation with the improvement of cardiac function and volumes after a period of GDMT.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

The participated patients were prospectively enrolled between February 2016 and June 2020 as part of the prospective clinical registration DEMAND study [DEep phenotypic Multimodality-based Analysis of Dilated Cardiomyopathy], which is registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR1800017058). As per our previous study, the inclusion criteria included elevated LV end-diastolic dimension (> 55 mm) and impaired LV ejection fraction (LVEF, < 50%) on CMR [19]. DCM was diagnosed according to the criteria of the ESC working group on myocardial diseases [8]. All patients received medical treatment for HF according to standardized guidelines. The exclusion criteria included significant coronary artery disease, history of significant systemic hypertension, biopsy-proven active myocarditis, primary valve disease, congenital heart disease, systemic rheumatic diseases, or sarcoidosis. Additional exclusion criteria included device implantation during mid-term follow-up, incomplete GDMT, and unwillingness to undergo follow-up CMR imaging. CMR imaging was performed at baseline and mid-term follow-up (1–2 years after initiation of GDMT). GDMT was initiated within 2 weeks of diagnosis [20], and all DCM patients participating in the study were newly initiated on GDMT at the beginning of the study. Fifty age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers were selected as normal controls [21]. The GDMT were administered following the guidelines for the management of patients with heart failure (HF) [20], including angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNi), or ACE (angiotensin converting enzyme) inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers, beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, diuretics, digoxin, and warfarin. Medication doses were adjusted for symptomatic hypotension, bradycardia, worsening renal function, or hyperkalemia. The medications were titrated in patients to the maximum tolerated dose. If any of the following situations occur: 1) stopping medication midway; 2) not taking medication as prescribed; 3) not achieving the target medication dosage, the patient will be considered to have incomplete GDMT.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital and has been registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; ID: ChiCTR1800017058). All procedures were carried out following the Declaration of Helsinki (2000), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging

Baseline and follow-up CMR imaging was performed on a 3 T scanner (MAGNETOM, Trio system; Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) with an 8-channel phased-array body coil. ﻿Subjects underwent standardized imaging protocol for baseline and follow-up CMR examination, as described previously [18]. After gadolinium injection, steady-state free precession cine images were acquired in continuous short-axis slices from the basal to apical levels and in 3 long-axis (2-, 3-, 4-chamber) views. The typical scanning parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR), 34 ms; echo time (TE), 1.3 ms; flip angle, 50°; field of view (FOV), 280 × 340 mm2; matrix size, 162 × 192; average temporal resolution, 35–45 ms; and slice thickness, 8 mm with no gap. Left and right ventricular volume and ejection fraction (EF) were analyzed according to the Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance (SCMR) guidelines [22] by 2 experienced radiologists (YWX and JJG, with 6 and 4 years of CMR experience, respectively).

Measurement of subcutaneous and visceral abdominal fat

Subcutaneous and visceral abdominal fat thickness were determined based on sagittal abdominal diameters measured in the mid-sagittal plane. Visceral abdominal fat thickness was defined as the maximum anteroposterior thickness in the upper abdomen, anterior to the left lobe of the liver. Subcutaneous fat thickness was defined as the distance between the skin and linea alba [5, 23]. All measurements were taken at baseline and follow-up in the same location [12].

Measurement of epicardial adipose tissue

EAT parameters were measured with epicardial and pericardial fat thickness, and paracardial fat volume. Epicardial fat thickness was measured between the outer myocardial border and the visceral pericardium. Pericardial fat thickness was measured between the outer myocardial border and the parietal pericardium. Both measurements were made anterior to the RV free wall on 3-chamber long-axis cine images at end-diastolic phase. The measurement was the maximum thickness perpendicular to the outer layer of the myocardium and avoiding atrioventricular and interventricular groove and pericardial effusion. Paracardial fat volume was assessed using consecutive short-axis images at end-diastolic phase, with manual delineation of the outer myocardium and parietal pericardium [12]. All three EAT parameters were measured at both baseline and follow-up according to the same protocol.

Definition of LVRR

The LVRR of DCM patients was defined as an absolute increase of LVEF > 10% to a final value ≥ 35%, accompanied by a relative decrease of LV end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) > 10% [18]. Accordingly, patients were classified into the LVRR( +) and LVRR(-) groups during follow-up, and the comparison of the changes in cardiac and abdominal adiposity parameters in the two groups was subsequently analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while those of non-normal distribution are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Normality was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov method. Categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentages. Inter-group comparisons were made using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Intra-patient comparisons between baseline and follow-up were performed using the paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Delta values are determined as follow-up values minus the values minus those at baseline and are expressed as [(delta change)/baseline (%)]. Radar plots are generated to illustrate the distribution and trend of cardiac and abdominal adiposity parameters across the patient groups and periods. Parameters were compared using a normalized scale (z-score), with the mean z-score of each group plotted from the center (lower) to the periphery part (higher) of the graph.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association of changes in EAT and abdominal adiposity parameters with LVRR. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to assess their correlations with ventricular structure (delta indexed EDV) and function (delta EF). Multivariable logistic regression analyses were adjusted for age, sex, and delta body mass index (BMI) of the corresponding period.

Reproducibility was assessed in terms of coefficient of variation (CoV) and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) using the Bland–Altman analysis. Intra-observer reproducibility was evaluated by the same operator with a one-month interval, while inter-observer reproducibility was independently assessed by a separate operator using the same workflow.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 24.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R statistical package version 3.6.2 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria), with 2-sided p < 0.05 considered statistical significance.

Association between cardiac and abdominal adiposity and LVRR

During follow-up CMR examination, LVRR was observed in 67 (28.9%) patients. No significant difference in treatment duration was shown between the LVRR ( +) and LVRR (-) groups [16.8 (13.2–20.3) months vs. 14.7 (12.5–18.9] months, p = 0.212). At baseline, LVRR ( +) patients demonstrated significantly higher visceral abdominal fat thickness, as well as significantly lower pericardial and epicardial fat thickness compared to LVRR (-) patients. During the follow-up, a significant increase in pericardial and epicardial fat thickness, and paracardial fat volume from baseline were observed in both LVRR ( +) and LVRR (-) patients, while no significant difference in the changes in subcutaneous and visceral abdominal fat thickness was observed. Correlation of changes in adipose parameters with LVRR cardiac function and volume

Among all participants of the study, we found that the increase in epicardial fat thickness positively correlated with delta LVEF (r = 0.652, p < 0.001) and delta RVEF (r = 0.563, p < 0.001), but negatively correlated with delta LVEDVi (r = -0.515, p < 0.001) and delta RVEDVi (r = -0.351, p < 0.001). Similar trends were shown for pericardial fat thickness and paracardial fat volume. Meanwhile, delta subcutaneous abdominal fat thickness showed a weak correlation with delta LVEDVi, RVEDVi, and RVEF, while delta visceral abdominal fat thickness did not show any significant correlation. All results are presented in Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 1.

The independent association between EAT and LVRR

Based on univariate logistic regression analysis, the increase in pericardial fat thickness (OR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.23–1.72, p < 0.001), epicardial fat thickness (OR = 2.12; 95% CI: 1.70–2.67, p < 0.001), and paracardial fat volume (OR = 1.01; 95% CI: 1.01–1.02, p = 0.001) were significantly associated with LVRR. After adjusting for age, sex, and BMI, all three EAT parameters remained as significant independent predictors for LVRR. All odds ratio results with and without covariate adjustments are presented in Fig. 3. To further explore the potential impact of nutritional status on EAT parameters, the results of the associations between EAT parameters and LVRR, adjusted for delta albumin, subcutaneous fat thickness, and visceral fat thickness, are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Discussion

Our study elucidated the dynamic changes in EAT parameters with GDMT among DCM patients. Our main findings were as follows: (1) DCM patients showed significantly lower measures of epicardial fat at baseline compared to normal controls. (2) After 1–2 years of GDMT, we found that cardiac and visceral adipose tissue significantly increased, with a greater increase shown among patients who achieved LVRR. (3) The increase in epicardial adiposity is significantly associated with improved ventricular structure and function, independent of the changes of BMI. (4) The increase in EAT parameters with GDMT is significantly associated with LVRR, even after adjusting for age, sex, and BMI, suggesting the potential protective effect of cardiac adiposity in DCM patients.

The role of EAT in the cardiovascular system is complex, and the balance between its protective and detrimental effects in patients with HF can be enigmatic. While EAT may act as a lipid-storing depot, an endocrine organ, and a buffer for protection of the myocardium [24, 25], it can, conversely, activate adverse immune signaling pathways, and impair ventricular diastolic filling resulting in atrial enlargement [26, 27]. According to previous studies, the protective mechanisms of EAT among DCM patients, may relate to energy metabolism, nutritional status, and microcirculation. The study by Pugliese et al. found that increased EAT thickness was associated with decreased peak VO2 in HFpEF patients, and increased peak VO2 in HFrEF patients [3]. The shared microcirculation between epicardial fats and the myocardium may further contribute to its influence on cardiac function [28]. The pathophysiological mechanism of EAT parameters in DCM patients is not very sufficient at present, and the EAT has been found with paradoxical effects in HFrEF and HFpEF patients [29]. Among patients with HFpEF, the higher EAT volume has been regarded as unhealthy obesity [11]. Gorter et al. found that increased EAT is associated with higher right-sided filling pressures and reduced exercise capacity in HFpEF [30]. On the other hand, in HFrEF, Jin et al. demonstrated that thicker EAT associated with better LV contractile function [31]. The cardiac remodeling processes could be influenced with adipokines, such as resistin, secreted from EAT [32]. The direct pathophysiological mechanisms of EAT reverse remodeling require further investigation.

Current available evidence on the correlation of EAT with ventricular structure and function in DCM is limited. The present study found that DCM patients showed a decreased amount of epicardial fat compared with normal controls. We further found that the increase in EAT parameters independently correlated with improvements in cardiac function after GDMT. These findings in accordance with previous studies showing that HFrEF patients are more likely to exhibit reduced EAT thickness, with lower EAT values indicating higher left ventricular dysfunction, overall functional impairment, and poorer prognosis [3, 12].

The independence of EAT parameters from overall adiposity, as seen in our study, has also been observed in other clinical disorders. For instance, Song et al. reported that the reduction in epicardial fat thickness following dapagliflozin therapy independently associated with improvements in LV systolic function in type 2 diabetes mellitus patients [33]. The recent meta-analysis by Chong et al. found the increase in epicardial thickness and volume as independent predictors of major adverse cardiovascular events [34]. Altogether, such findings provide additional evidence highlighting the independent protective role of EAT in DCM. However, the underlying mechanisms of such phenomena remain poorly understood, and warrants further exploration.

Many previous studies suggested that EAT have an association with the left ventricular function [15, 31, 35]. Additionally, it has been suggested that reduced EAT is positively associated with the reduced cardiac function on HFrEF patients [3]. However, no studies have focused on the dynamic changes of EAT. Our results suggest that the increment of EAT is significantly correlated with the improvement of bi-ventricular ejection fraction, as well as the reduction of bi-ventricular volumes.

Inflammatory cascades play a crucial role in the occurrence and development of DCM [36]. GDMT may directly affect EAT through its anti-inflammatory properties. Previous studies have shown that beta-blockers and ACE inhibitors could reduce inflammation. Since EAT produces pro-inflammatory cytokines, reducing inflammation may decrease EAT volume [37, 38]. In addition, Kasperova et al. identified a reduction in EAT inflammation in patients with severe heart failure following SGLT-2 inhibitor treatment [39]. Ryk et al. reported that SGLT2i treatment can reverse glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GPR)146-related inflammatory signatures in EAT, affecting lipid and metabolic pathways [40]. Likewise, Requena-Ibáñez et al. found that empagliflozin reduced both EAT volume and inflammatory biomarkers [41]. Further research is needed to clarify the anti-inflammatory effects of GDMT on EAT and their clinical implications. Myasoedova et al. through meta-analysis, observed that SGLT2i may reduce EAT over six months [42]. However, in our study, the LVRR group showed an increase in EAT without significant changes in subcutaneous or visceral fat, likely due to the study period (February 2016 to June 2020), when fewer patients received these anti-inflammatory agents. This suggests further investigation is needed into the anti-inflammatory roles of SGLT2i on EAT remodeling and cardiovascular outcomes.

Various imaging methods have been developed for the evaluation of EAT. Recently, West et al. derived a deep-learning model which quantified and segmented EAT volume in an automated manner from coronary computed tomography angiogram images. Their study found that higher EAT volume associated with coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, and higher risk of all-cause modality and stroke [27]. Our study demonstrated the potential of CMR-derived EAT parameters as biomarkers for treatment response in DCM [25]. The present study also found that measuring EAT parameters and abdominal fat thickness based on CMR is feasible and reproducible, which may be attributed to the high spatial resolution of CMR. The high correlation observed between changes in epicardial fat thickness and cardiac function further supports CMR-derived EAT parameters as practical and promising biomarkers for LVRR in DCM.

Study limitations

The study had several limitations. Firstly, patients with implantable devices, heart transplantation, or those who died before follow-up CMR examination were excluded from our analysis, which may introduce selection bias. Secondly, as with all observational studies, the causal relationship between EAT and LVRR could not be determined. Further animal studies may be helpful in excluding potential confounding biases and in exploring the pathophysiological mechanisms of EAT parameters and cardiac function in DCM. Third, baseline and follow-up cine images may not acquired at the exact same slice, which may lead to some measurement discrepancies. Nonetheless, biases were avoided as much as possible by ensuring that all patients underwent the same standardized CMR imaging protocol. Fourth, SGLT2i has been a new therapeutic class are recommended as cornerstone therapies for patients with HFrEF [43]. However, since SGLT2i was not widely available during the baseline inclusion period of our observational study (February 2016 to June 2020), analyses of SGLT2i usage were not included in the present study.

Conclusion

﻿In DCM patients, the increase in EAT parameters were significantly correlated with LVRR after GDMT, independent of the dynamic changes in BMI. The correlations between EAT parameters and the presence of LVRR indicate the potential protective role of EAT in DCM patients.
