Benign mixed epithelial ovarian tumor with mucinous, serous, and transitional components: a rare case report
Authors: Banan Muhsen Ali, Loui Hasan, Ali Daoud, Moatasem Ali Hussein Al-janabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBenign epithelial ovarian tumors are among the most common ovarian neoplasms, with mucinous, serous, and transitional types representing major subgroups. However, the coexistence of multiple epithelial components within a single ovarian lesion is exceedingly rare. We report the case of a multiparous woman presenting with progressive abdominal distension caused by a large ovarian cyst. Imaging revealed a giant, well-circumscribed cystic mass, which was surgically excised. Histopathological evaluation demonstrated a predominantly mucinous cystadenoma with foci of serous and transitional (Brenner-like) epithelial differentiation, without borderline or malignant features. This case represents a rare example of a benign mixed epithelial ovarian tumor and highlights the importance of thorough histopathological examination to avoid misdiagnosis and to better understand mixed Müllerian epithelial differentiation.Keywords: ovary, mixed epithelial tumor, mucinous cystadenoma, serous cystadenoma, transitional epithelium, Brenner tumor, histopathology, case report

Introduction

Ovarian epithelial tumors comprise the majority of ovarian neoplasms, with serous, mucinous, endometrioid, clear cell, and transitional (Brenner) types representing distinct morphological categories according to the 2020 WHO classification [1]. Mixed epithelial tumors are uncommon and are defined by the presence of two or more distinct epithelial components within the same ovarian lesion [2]. Such combinations most often involve mucinous and Brenner (transitional) elements, while the coexistence of mucinous, serous, and transitional epithelia within one benign cystic tumor is exceptionally rare [3–5]. Recognition of these mixed histological patterns is essential for accurate classification and for understanding the morphogenetic relationships among Müllerian epithelial lineages [6].

Case presentation

A 40-year-old multiparous woman (G2P2) with two prior cesarean sections presented with rapidly progressive abdominal distension over four weeks, associated with mild abdominal discomfort, early satiety, and bloating. She denied acute abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, changes in bowel habits, or urinary symptoms such as retention or frequency. There was no history of weight loss, fever, or other constitutional symptoms. Her past medical history was unremarkable, and there was no family history of gynecologic malignancy.

Pregnancy was excluded clinically and biochemically, with serum β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) within the normal range.

On physical examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable. The abdomen was markedly distended, resembling a full-term pregnancy. Palpation revealed a large, non-tender abdominal mass extending from the pelvis to the epigastrium, without clinical evidence of ascites.

Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count and biochemical profile, were within normal limits. Serum CA-125 was mildly elevated at 69.57 U/ml (reference value < 35 U/ml).

Transabdominal ultrasonography demonstrated a large, well-defined cystic adnexal lesion with thin internal septations and predominantly anechoic content (Fig. 1). Due to the massive size of the lesion, the left ovary could not be clearly visualized and was presumed to be the site of origin. The uterus and right ovary were identified separately and appeared normal in size and echotexture. No free pelvic fluid was detected.

Transabdominal ultrasound image demonstrating a large cystic adnexal mass with internal septations (arrows) and a well-defined outer boundary (arrowheads).

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis revealed a large, well-defined, multiloculated cystic mass measuring approximately 25 × 15 cm. The lesion contained fine internal septations and a small non-enhancing mural thickening at its dependent inferior aspect, without evidence of true solid enhancing components. No ascites, peritoneal implants, or lymphadenopathy were identified. Both kidneys demonstrated normal collecting systems, with no evidence of pelvicalyceal dilatation (Fig. 2).

Contrast-enhanced CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis showing a large multiloculated cystic mass occupying most of the abdominopelvic cavity, with thin internal septations and smooth outer margins.

An exploratory laparotomy was planned. A Pfannenstiel incision was selected based on preoperative imaging suggestive of a benign lesion, the feasibility of safe tumor delivery following controlled decompression, prior cesarean incisions, and cosmetic considerations. Intraoperatively, a large multilobulated ovarian cystic mass occupying the abdominal cavity was identified and delivered intact (Fig. 3). Approximately 4 liters of serous fluid were aspirated in a controlled manner to facilitate removal. The uterus, contralateral ovary, and fallopian tube appeared normal and were preserved.

Intraoperative photographs showing a huge multilobulated ovarian cystic mass occupying the abdominal cavity, delivered through a Pfannenstiel incision.

Gross examination revealed a smooth, tense, multiloculated cystic mass (Fig. 4A). Cut sections demonstrated multiple cystic spaces containing clear to mucinous fluid with focal hemorrhagic areas (Fig. 4B). Microscopically, the tumor was predominantly composed of mucinous cystadenomatous epithelium of gastrointestinal type, with focal areas showing serous (tubal-type ciliated) epithelium and discrete nests of transitional (Brenner-like) cells within dense fibrous stroma (Fig. 5). No epithelial atypia or stromal invasion was identified, consistent with a benign mixed epithelial ovarian tumor (mucinous–serous–transitional type).

Gross morphology of the resected ovarian tumor. (A) the external surface is smooth, tense, and lobulated. (B) the cut section reveals multiloculated cystic spaces filled with clear to mucinous fluid and focal solid and hemorrhagic areas.

Histopathological features of the tumor (A) serous epithelial component lined by a single layer of cuboidal to low columnar cells without papillary formations (H&E stain, 40x). (B) Transitional epithelial component exhibiting multilayered urothelial-like cells (H&E stain, 40x). (C, D) mucinous cystadenoma component composed of cystic glands lined by tall columnar mucin-secreting epithelium without epithelial atypia or stromal invasion (H&E stain, 40x and 100x).

Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated strong cytoplasmic CK7 positivity in the serous and transitional components (Fig. 6A and B), while the mucinous component showed diffuse cytoplasmic CK20 positivity (Fig. 6C and D).

Immunohistochemical profile of the mucinous cystadenoma. (A, B) tumor cells show strong cytoplasmic CK7 positivity (40x and 100x). (C, D) tumor cells exhibit diffuse cytoplasmic CK20 positivity (40x and 100x).

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on the fifth postoperative day. Follow-up was conducted over six months with regular outpatient clinical assessments. Pelvic ultrasonography at three and six months postoperatively demonstrated no evidence of residual or recurrent adnexal masses, and the patient remained asymptomatic.

Discussion

Benign mixed epithelial ovarian tumors are rare entities comprising two or more morphologically distinct epithelial components within a single lesion [1, 2]. The combination of mucinous, serous, and transitional epithelia is particularly unusual, with only a handful of cases reported in the literature [3–5, 7, 8].

Reported cases of benign mixed epithelial ovarian tumors generally occur in middle-aged to postmenopausal women, with most patients presenting in the fourth to sixth decades of life [3, 5, 7]. Clinical presentation is usually nonspecific and related to tumor size, commonly including progressive abdominal distension, pelvic discomfort, or pressure-related gastrointestinal and urinary symptoms [4, 8]. Tumor size in previously reported cases typically ranges from 10 to 20 cm in greatest dimension [5, 7].

In comparison, the present case involved a relatively young, reproductive-aged woman who presented with rapidly progressive abdominal distension caused by a giant ovarian cyst measuring approximately 25 cm. Similar to other reported cases, imaging findings suggested a benign cystic lesion, and the mild elevation of CA-125 was attributed to peritoneal irritation rather than malignancy [9]. This comparison highlights the variability in clinical presentation and tumor size in benign mixed epithelial ovarian tumors, while reinforcing their predominantly benign behavior.

From a pathological perspective, understanding the histogenesis of each epithelial component remains essential for accurate classification.

Mucinous tumors account for approximately 10–20% of benign ovarian neoplasms, while serous cystadenomas and Brenner tumors are less frequent [1]. The coexistence of these components may reflect metaplastic transformation within a single epithelial lineage or divergent differentiation from a common Müllerian progenitor cell [2, 7].

Preoperative differentiation between mixed and pure epithelial tumors remains difficult. Imaging findings are nonspecific, and mild CA-125 elevation can occur due to peritoneal irritation rather than malignancy [9]. In the present case, the absence of papillary projections, ascites, or solid invasive elements suggested a benign lesion, later confirmed by histology.

Histologically, mucinous areas showed gastrointestinal-type epithelium, the serous component exhibited ciliated cuboidal cells, and the transitional (Brenner-like) nests displayed oval nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm within fibrous stroma—consistent with benign morphology in all elements. These findings highlight the morphological spectrum of Müllerian differentiation [6, 8].

Surgically, controlled cyst decompression and cystectomy provided optimal outcomes while preserving fertility. Despite the giant size and rapid progression, most large encapsulated cystic ovarian lesions are benign when imaging reveals smooth margins and low vascularity [10]. Accurate histopathological sampling is therefore essential to identify mixed epithelial differentiation and avoid misclassification.

Conclusion

This case represents a rare example of a benign mixed epithelial ovarian tumor with mucinous, serous, and transitional components. It underscores the importance of comprehensive sampling and meticulous histopathological evaluation of large ovarian cysts, as recognition of multiple epithelial lineages may alter classification and improve our understanding of ovarian tumor histogenesis. Despite its alarming size and rapid progression, the lesion was benign and successfully treated with fertility-preserving surgery.
