Comparative seismic analysis of t-shaped and conventional deep cement mixing columns in highway embankments on soft soil
Authors: Rafik Boufarh, Amira Aissaoui, Adel Djellali, Kamel Goudjil, Ghania Boukhatem, Mehmet Serkan Kırgız, N Nagaprasad, Ramaswamy Krishnaraj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWeak soils present significant challenges for highway construction in seismic regions, often leading to instability issues such as settlements and slope failures. These conditions necessitate safe, effective, and cost-efficient solutions to ensure embankment stability. Despite the critical importance of embankment stability under dynamic loading, research on soil reinforcement techniques in seismic scenarios is limited. This study evaluated the seismic performance of conventional deep cement mixing (DCM) columns versus innovative T-shaped deep mixing (TDM) columns for highway embankments on soft soil through three-dimensional finite element analysis via PLAXIS 3D. After validation, a comprehensive parametric study investigated the effects of column type, spacing (1.67 m, 2.14 m, and 3.0 m), and seismic loading on embankment response parameters, including settlement, lateral displacement, and excess pore water pressure. The results demonstrate that both reinforcement methods significantly enhance seismic stability compared with unreinforced conditions. Compared with unreinforced embankments, TDM columns achieved superior performance, with settlement reductions of 89.95% versus 36.11% for DCM columns. Under seismic loading, the TDM columns exhibited 88.31% less settlement than the DCM columns at the embankment crest. Parametric analysis revealed that closer column spacing (1.67 m) reduced settlements to 0.0032 m for TDM compared with 0.023 m at wider spacing (3.0 m). The TDM columns also demonstrated more controlled consolidation behavior with smoother settlement time histories and reduced excess pore water pressure fluctuations. The findings indicate that TDM columns provide superior seismic performance through enhanced load transfer mechanisms and improved soil‒structure interactions, making them the preferred solution for highway embankments in seismically active regions.Keywords: Seismic analysis, T-shaped columns, Soft soil, Van earthquake, Finite element analysis, DisplacementSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

The widespread occurrence of soft soils poses significant challenges for highway construction from a geotechnical engineering perspective, frequently resulting in instability issues, including excessive settlements, slope failures, and large lateral deformations 1–3. These conditions necessitate the development of safe, effective, and economical construction techniques that can ensure long-term embankment stability and serviceability, particularly in seismically active regions where dynamic loading compounds these challenges 4.

Deep cement mixing (DCM) has emerged as one of the most successful soil stabilization techniques applied globally 1–3,5–8. This method involves the installation of cemented columns within soft soil layers to transfer embankment and traffic loads to underlying firm strata 9–11. Since its introduction over four decades ago, DCM technology has evolved substantially, with Japan alone treating more than 100 million m3 of soil between 1975 and 2010 7. Compared with untreated soil, the technique employs specialized auger systems to blend soil with cement, producing columns with increased strength, reduced permeability, and lower compressibility 12,13. For seismic applications, DCM columns are typically arranged in lattice patterns to provide liquefaction resistance 14.

Recent developments have introduced T-shaped deep mixing (TDM) columns as promising alternatives to conventional DCM configurations. Research from 2020–2025 has demonstrated the superior cost effectiveness and enhanced settlement control of TDM columns under static loading conditions 10,15–18. Phutthananon et al. 15,16 conducted comprehensive parametric analyses, establishing that TDM columns achieve superior load transfer efficiency through their enhanced geometry—featuring an enlarged head, cylindrical body, and cap configuration. Singh et al. 3 provided a comprehensive review of DCM technology, explicitly identifying seismic performance as a critical knowledge gap requiring urgent research attention.

The seismic response of embankments constructed over soft soils and reinforced with various ground improvement techniques has received considerable attention in recent years. Stone columns have been extensively studied, with research by Dar and Shah 4 and Meshkinghalam et al. 19 demonstrating their effectiveness in reducing lateral deformation, differential settlement, and excess pore water pressure under seismic loading. Geosynthetic reinforcement systems have been investigated through both numerical 20 and experimental approaches 21, revealing significant seismic performance enhancements. Alternative methods, including rammed aggregate piers 22,23, rigid inclusions 17,24,25, and hybrid systems combining multiple techniques 26,27, have also been explored, demonstrating effectiveness in mitigating seismic-induced deformations.

Despite extensive research on various ground improvement techniques under seismic loading and significant advances in TDM column technology for static applications, a fundamental knowledge gap exists regarding the seismic behavior of deep mixing columns—both conventional DCM and innovative TDM configurations—for embankment reinforcement under earthquake loading. While DCM technology has been widely studied for static applications with excellent performance worldwide, its seismic characteristics remain inadequately understood 3. This limitation is particularly significant given (i) widespread DCM application in seismically active regions; (ii) the critical importance of understanding dynamic performance for infrastructure resilience; (iii) recent major earthquakes highlighting soft soil site vulnerabilities; and (iv) increasing emphasis on performance-based seismic design requiring validated analytical tools.

More critically, comprehensive comparative studies examining seismic performance differences between conventional DCMs and innovative TDM columns are essentially nonexistent in the current literature, including the 2020–2025 publications. This represents a particularly significant gap because TDM’s unique geometry creates fundamentally different stress distributions and dynamic response characteristics that cannot be reliably predicted from existing DCM seismic research. Under dynamic loading, the enhanced bearing area and shorter effective length may provide superior or inferior performance depending on complex interactions between inertial forces, cyclic soil degradation, and interface behavior—mechanisms requiring explicit investigation. Practicing engineers lack validated design guidance for TDM applications in seismic regions, potentially leading to overly conservative or inadequately conservative implementations.

Understanding failure mechanisms and their progression is fundamental to evaluating seismic performance. For column-reinforced embankments, potential failure modes include column punching through soft clay, embankment sliding along interfaces, global rotational failure, and progressive failure initiated by local yielding 28,29. These complex mechanisms require advanced three-dimensional analysis to capture nonlinear soil‒structure interactions under realistic seismic loading conditions.

This study addresses this critical research gap by conducting the first comprehensive comparative investigation of conventional DCM versus innovative TDM column seismic performance for highway embankments constructed over soft soils. The investigation employs three-dimensional finite element analysis via PLAXIS 3D software, which has been validated against established benchmarks 19,25. Comprehensive parametric studies have examined the effects of column type, spacing (1.67 m, 2.14 m, and 3.0 m), and seismic loading intensity on embankment response parameters, including settlement, lateral displacement, and excess pore water pressure. The seismic excitation employs acceleration time-history records from the 2011 Van earthquake (Mw 7.2), providing realistic dynamic loading conditions representative of high-seismicity regions.

This research advances beyond current knowledge by (1) providing the first systematic evaluation of the TDM column dynamic response under realistic earthquake loading; (2) conducting rigorous side-by-side DCM versus TDM comparisons under identical seismic conditions; (3) systematically examining the effects of column spacing on seismic performance; (4) employing advanced 3D coupled modeling to capture solid‒fluid interactions, excess pore pressure generation/dissipation, and complex soil‒structure interactions; and (5) developing actionable design recommendations for column spacing optimization and performance-based design in seismic applications. These findings directly address the knowledge gap identified in recent literature 3, providing practicing engineers with evidence-based guidance for selecting appropriate ground improvement techniques for highway embankments in seismically active regions, ultimately supporting safer and more economical infrastructure construction in earthquake-prone areas worldwide.

Finite element analysis

The seismic response analysis of the reinforced embankments was conducted via three-dimensional dynamic finite element modeling implemented in PLAXIS 3D software. This numerical approach incorporates advanced constitutive models to simulate the nonlinear behavior of soft soils and DCM columns under seismic loading conditions. The model integrated dynamic soil‒structure interactions, pore pressure generation, and stress‒dependent stiffness variations to capture the complex system response during seismic excitation.

Problem geometry, boundary conditions, and material parameters

The prototype embankment geometry (5 m height, 40 m base width, 20 m crest width), as illustrated in Fig. 1, represents typical highway cross-sections constructed in soft soil regions, reflecting common design practices where the height is limited to 4–6 m to control settlement and where side slopes are maintained at stable gradients (1 V:2H to 1 V:3H). Schematic geometry of the conventional DCM column and T-shaped DCM (TDM) column.

Model dimensions (80 m × 5 m) were determined through sensitivity analysis to minimize boundary effects while ensuring computational efficiency. The model width (perpendicular to the embankment axis, Y direction) is 5 m, which represents a unit width section used for efficient 3D analysis while capturing the essential soil‒column interaction behavior. Conventional DCM columns 0.6 m in diameter and 10 m deep spaced at the center to center spacing of 5 m were taken as the reinforcement in the foundation soil. The T-shaped DCM (TDM) columns comprise a column head (1 m diameter), column body (0.6 m diameter), and column cap (2 m thick), have a column length of 8 m, and are spaced at the center to center spacing of 5 m in both the longitudinal (X) and transverse (Y) directions. The initial center-to-center column spacing of 5 m, corresponding to spacing-to-diameter ratios (S/D) of 8.3 for DCM (0.6 m diameter) and 5.0 for TDM (1.0 m head diameter), represents moderate ground improvement levels commonly specified in practice for settlement control 10,15,30. These S/D ratios balance performance requirements with construction economy. The foundation depth (10 m soft clay layer) is representative of alluvial deposits encountered in river valleys and coastal plains where highway embankments frequently require stabilization. While specific projects may involve different scales, the dimensionless behavior captured through normalized settlement ratios and stress concentration ratios ensures the applicability of findings to geometrically similar configurations. The numerical model employs a combination of mechanical and dynamic boundary conditions optimized for seismic analysis of embankment-foundation systems. Mechanical boundaries consist of roller supports (fixed normal displacement, free tangential displacement) applied to the vertical lateral faces and fully fixed conditions at the model base. For dynamic analysis, free-field boundary conditions were implemented on the lateral boundaries, which permit the passage of seismic waves while minimizing spurious reflections that would otherwise contaminate the solution. The free-field boundaries operate by computing the free-field motion (motion that would occur in the absence of the structure) at nodes along the lateral boundaries and applying these as prescribed displacements, effectively extending the domain to infinity. This approach is particularly appropriate for embankment analysis, where the structure modifies the local stress and deformation fields but should not unrealistically constrain far-field wave propagation. At the base boundary, a compliant (viscous) boundary condition was implemented to absorb downward-propagating waves and prevent nonphysical reflection of seismic energy back into the model domain. This viscous boundary applies dashpots with impedance values calibrated to the underlying bedrock properties, allowing seismic input to be applied as acceleration/displacement time histories while minimizing artificial reflection effects.

The adequacy of the model dimensions (80 m width, 10 m depth) in conjunction with these boundary conditions was verified through sensitivity analysis. The models with extended lateral dimensions (100 m and 120 m) produced settlement and displacement results differing by less than 3% from those of the baseline 80 m model, confirming that boundary effects are negligible. The material properties of the various materials used in this study are presented in Tables 1 and 2. In this study, the foundation soil consisting of soft clay is modeled via the hardening soil (HS) constitutive model; the embankment material and the deep cement mixing column are modeled via the Mohr–Coulomb constitutive model 18. The interface interaction between the TDM column heads and surrounding soft soil was modeled under the assumption of full contact with continuous stress transfer, which is consistent with field observations of deep mixing installations where mechanical blending creates an intimate column‒soil transition zone 10,18. This assumption is justified because (i) the wet mixing process creates a diffuse boundary rather than a discrete interface; (ii) field studies show negligible slip at column‒soil contacts under working loads; and (iii) the enlarged head distributes stresses over a larger area, reducing interface stress concentrations. While this approach may slightly overestimate lateral load transfer under extreme loading, it provides conservative estimates for settlement and is appropriate for preliminary design. Future studies incorporating interface elements with reduced strength parameters could refine predictions for ultimate limit state conditions, particularly for projects in highly seismic zones where large lateral displacements are anticipated.

The HSS model was selected for soft clay to account for stress-dependent stiffness and small-strain effects under dynamic loading. The MC model was used for embankment fill and columns because of its relatively high stiffness and elastic‒plastic response, reducing computational complexity while maintaining accuracy (no sensitivity to small strains was observed in preliminary tests).

The mechanical properties adopted for the DCM and TDM columns (Young’s modulus E = 63 MPa, undrained shear strength cu = 450 kPa) reflect wet deep mixing with cement contents of 15–20% by dry weight in soft clay, which is consistent with field data reported in the literature 7,9,21. These values represent the 28-day cured strength, which is typical for design applications. While field installations exhibit spatial variability in strength due to variations in mixing uniformity, soil heterogeneity, and curing conditions, the deterministic values used herein represent the mean properties appropriate for comparative performance evaluation. The identical properties assigned to both the DCM and TDM columns isolate the geometric effects on system performance. For design applications, practitioners should incorporate appropriate safety factors to account for construction variability, potentially through probabilistic analysis or reduced characteristic strengths. The constitutive model selection was based on material behavior characteristics and computational efficiency. The hardening soil (HS) model for soft clay accurately captures stress-dependent stiffness, plastic hardening, and the distinct loading‒unloading–reloading behavior essential for predicting consolidation and settlement under cyclic seismic loading 31. The Mohr‒Coulomb (MC) model was selected for DCM/TDM columns and embankment fill, as these cemented and compacted materials exhibit predominantly elastic‒perfectly plastic behavior within the stress ranges encountered in this study. This combination of constitutive models represents the established best practice for embankment–soft soil systems and has been extensively validated in previous research 1,30,32. While advanced models such as HS-Small can capture small-strain stiffness effects, the selected models provide an optimal balance between accuracy and computational efficiency for dynamic analysis. The hydraulic boundary conditions for consolidation analysis were as follows: (i) lateral model boundaries were assigned as impermeable in the horizontal direction but permeable vertically, simulating free drainage conditions appropriate for embankment-scale analysis; (ii) the model base was set as fully impermeable, representing an underlying aquitard or bedrock; and (iii) the embankment crest was assigned as a free-draining surface, allowing excess pore water pressure dissipation. These conditions simulate a semi-infinite horizontal extent typical of highway embankment construction. While absolute excess pore pressure magnitudes could be influenced by boundary proximity in extreme cases, sensitivity analyses (not shown) confirmed that the selected model dimensions (80 m × 80 m in plan) were sufficient to minimize boundary effects. More importantly, the comparative performance between the DCM and TDM reinforcement systems is governed by near-field column‒soil interactions and remains independent of far-field boundary conditions.

Characteristics of earthquake loading

The acceleration time is shown in Fig. 3. A uniform pavement load of 14 kPa was applied, which is consistent with EN 1991–2 33, representing a distributed surface load over an approximately 25-m-long embankment section.

Input acceleration time histories from the 2011 Van earthquake: (a) longitudinal direction and (b) transverse direction.

Seismic excitation was simulated via acceleration-time history records from the 2011 Van earthquake (Mw 7.2) in eastern Turkey, with an epicenter located approximately 16 km northeast of Van city and a focal depth of 20 km. Data from the Van earthquake have been used to study the dynamic behavior of embankments improved with conventional deep cement mixing columns (DCMs) and T-shaped deep cement mixing columns (TDMs). The Van earthquake was selected as the seismic input because (i) it represents a major seismic event (Mw 7.2) comparable to design-level earthquakes for critical infrastructure; (ii) the epicentral region shares similar soft soil geological conditions with many highway construction sites requiring ground improvement; (iii) well-documented acceleration records with clear frequency content are available; and (iv) its characteristics align with seismic design requirements in active tectonic zones. Dynamic analysis employs free-field and compliant base boundary conditions, which are suitable for earthquake simulations where input motions are applied at the model boundaries. The earthquake loading was defined by imposing prescribed displacements at the base boundary, which were derived from the recorded accelerations (Fig. 3). Acceleration time histories were applied as the dynamic input. While analyses using accelerations up to 0.1 g generally provide accurate simulations of geotechnical structure responses, such analyses are computationally intensive. To ensure numerical stability and minimize computational artifacts in the dynamic analysis, several measures were implemented: (i) automatic time-stepping with convergence criteria based on displacement and force tolerances (0.01% relative error); (ii) Rayleigh damping calibration to match target damping ratios of 5% for soft clay and 2% for DCM/TDM columns at frequencies of 1 Hz and 5 Hz, representing dominant earthquake frequencies; (iii) mesh sensitivity analysis confirming that element sizes of 0.5–1.0 m provided converged solutions independent of further refinement; and (iv) verification of energy balance throughout dynamic loading, with numerical energy dissipation remaining below 5% of input energy. The oscillatory settlement and displacement time histories presented here reflect the actual physical response of the soil-structure system to cyclic loading rather than numerical noise, as confirmed by (a) oscillation frequencies matching the input acceleration spectra; (b) amplitude decay following earthquake cessation, which is consistent with material damping; and (c) smooth displacement derivatives indicating stable integration. Postprocessing applies low-pass Butterworth filtering (cutoff frequency of 20 Hz) solely for visualization purposes without affecting reported peak values or integral quantities (e.g., cumulative settlement).

Validation and calibration

A comparison of the excess pore water pressure and settlement profiles, shown in Figs. 5a and 5b, reveals that the PLAXIS 3D results are highly consistent with the findings of Meshkinghalam et al. at a depth of 1.5 m and a distance of 1.5 m from the model center.

Model validation: Comparison of (a) excess pore pressure (EPP) and (b) settlement profiles with Meshkinghalam, et al. 19.

The validation case of Meshkinghalam, et al. 19 involved stone column-reinforced liquefiable soil, which differs from the DCM/TDM-soft clay system investigated herein. However, this validation exercise serves a critical purpose: verifying the numerical procedures, dynamic boundary condition implementation, pore pressure generation algorithms, and consolidation formulations in PLAXIS 3D. Both systems share fundamental governing mechanisms—dynamic loading, excess pore pressure development, and column‒soil interaction—making this an appropriate validation benchmark. The excellent agreement between the excess pore pressure and settlement profiles (Fig. 5) confirms that the numerical framework accurately captures the coupled solid‒fluid behavior under dynamic loading. Notably, the scale differences between the validation and application cases are accounted for through dimensionless analysis and material property calibration. This validation approach follows established practices in computational geotechnical earthquake engineering, where procedural validation often precedes application to new material systems 4. While the numerical validation against Meshkinghalam, et al. 19 demonstrates the model’s ability to simulate the seismic response of column-reinforced ground, we acknowledge that direct experimental validation for DCM/TDM columns under seismic loading is currently unavailable in the published literature. This absence represents a significant research gap that our study aims to address through comprehensive numerical investigations. To provide additional confidence in our modeling approach, we compare our findings with available experimental observations from deep mixed column systems. Gupta, et al. 25 conducted laboratory model tests on embankments supported by soil-geopolymer deep mix columns under static and cyclic loading, demonstrating 55–70% settlement reduction under cyclic loading—behavioral patterns consistent with our computed 36.11% reduction for DCM columns under seismic loading (Section "Reinforcement impact on settlement behavior"). Their measured load‒settlement curves exhibited characteristic consolidation patterns with initial rapid settlement followed by gradual stabilization, mirroring our computed settlement time‒history responses (Fig. 6), whereas their observation that closer column spacing enhanced reinforcement effectiveness aligns with our parametric findings (Section "Column spacing impact"). These experimental results validate that our constitutive models appropriately capture the dynamic response mechanisms of deep mixing columns under repeated loading, demonstrating convergence between the experimental and numerical approaches.

Reinforcement impact on settlement behavior

Figure 6 presents the settlement time histories at Points A through G for three embankment configurations: unreinforced, DCM-reinforced, and TDM-reinforced conditions over a 200-s dynamic loading period. At the embankment crest (Point A), the unreinforced case exhibited 36 mm of settlement. The influence of reinforcement on the seismic behavior of the embankment was investigated by incorporating DCM columns (diameter: 0.6 m) and TDM columns (column head diameter: 1.0 m; column body diameter: 0.6 m) to reinforce the foundation soil. Both column types were installed at a spacing-to-diameter ratio (S/D) of 5. Settlement measurements were obtained at seven strategic locations: the embankment crest (Point A), beneath the embankment centreline (Point B), beneath a 5-m-deep section under the embankment (Point C), at the embankment toe (Point D), 5 m below the embankment toe (Point E), 10 m laterally from the embankment (Point F), and 10 m laterally from the embankment at a depth of 5 m (Point G). DCM reinforcement reduced this displacement to 23 mm (36.11% reduction), whereas TDM reinforcement achieved only 4.2 mm settlement (88.31% reduction). Beneath the embankment centerline (Point B), settlements were 36 mm, 23 mm, and 3.616 mm for the unreinforced, DCM-reinforced, and TDM-reinforced cases, respectively, representing an 89.95% reduction with TDM compared with DCM reinforcement. In the 5-m-deep section (Point C), the settlement reduction improved from 59.68% with DCM reinforcement to 87.65% with TDM reinforcement. At the embankment toe (Point D), soil heaving measured 9.155 mm and 8.389 mm for the unreinforced and DCM-reinforced embankments, respectively. TDM reinforcement further reduced this value by 92.92%. Points E, F, and G, located beyond the embankment influence zone, exhibited oscillatory behavior characteristic of dynamic loading effects.

The unreinforced case displayed the largest oscillation amplitude, indicating maximum vulnerability to seismic loading. DCM columns provided moderate oscillation reduction, whereas TDM columns achieved the most significant amplitude reduction, demonstrating superior dynamic response control. The results conclusively demonstrate that TDM column reinforcement produces substantially smaller settlements than DCM reinforcement does. Both reinforcement techniques effectively increased the bearing capacity and reduced the degree of soil settlement, which is consistent with the field observations. The observed 89.95% settlement reduction with TDM columns aligns remarkably well with field measurements by Liu, et al. 10, who reported an 85–90% reduction for T-shaped DCM column-supported embankments under static loading. This agreement validates that our numerical model appropriately captures the enhanced load transfer mechanisms of TDM columns through stress concentration in enlarged column heads. The TDM columns were the most effective for controlling both static settlement and dynamic oscillation under seismic loading conditions. The markedly superior settlement control exhibited by the TDM columns, particularly at the embankment crest and centerline (Points A and B), can be attributed to a fundamentally enhanced load transfer mechanism. The enlarged head of the TDM column acts as a mini-shallow foundation, distributing embankment loads over a larger area of the competent column body, thereby reducing the stress intensity transferred to the underlying soft soil 8. This mechanism is less pronounced in conventional DCM columns, which can act as point loads, potentially leading to greater stress concentrations and punching shear effects into the soft clay. Furthermore, the increased surface area of the TDM column improves the soil‒structure interaction, providing greater lateral confinement and resistance to buckling under seismic cyclic loads, a failure mode suggested for slender columns by 20,23.

Reinforcement impact on excess pore water pressure

Understanding excess pore water pressure (EPP) behavior is critical because it is directly related to the soil liquefaction potential and bearing capacity degradation under dynamic conditions. The influence of DCM and TDM column inclusions on EPP development during seismic loading was evaluated by measuring its value at the beginning and end of the dynamic phase under consolidation conditions. The EPP was measured at multiple locations for the unreinforced embankment and embankments reinforced with DCM and TDM columns. The results are presented in Table 4, where negative values indicate soil dilation, a phenomenon typically observed in dense soils or when effective stress reduction occurs due to column load attraction.

The accelerated consolidation and excess pore pressure dissipation observed with DCM and TDM column installation are strongly influenced by the assumed permeability values. The identical permeabilities assigned to the columns and soft clay (kz = 10⁻4 m/day, kx = ky = 2 × 10⁻4 m/day) reflect wet mixing installations where the cement slurry does not substantially alter the hydraulic conductivity compared with the parent clay. However, field permeability can vary by orders of magnitude depending on the cement content, mixing uniformity, and curing conditions. The results demonstrate several key mechanisms governing the performance of reinforced embankments. First, the reduced excess pore pressure (EPP) magnitudes at Points B and C beneath the embankment, observed for both reinforcement types compared with unreinforced conditions, indicate an effective load transfer effect where stresses are shifted to the stiffer column elements, which is consistent with stress concentration theory 2. Furthermore, the general reduction in EPP observed from the beginning to the end of dynamic loading highlights accelerated consolidation due to enhanced drainage provided by the columns. This drainage enhancement is particularly evident at Point C, where TDM columns exhibit superior efficiency, reducing the EPP from -26.844 to -24.278 kPa. Additionally, consistent measurements of negative excess pore water pressures (EPPs) at points beneath the embankment (B, C, E, G) are important. This indicates soil dilation, a phenomenon typically associated with dense, dilatant soils. In this context, a negative EPP is generated because the stiff columns attract the load, reducing the mean effective stress in the surrounding soil matrix. This reduction outweighs the tendency for cyclic shear-induced pore pressure generation during seismic shaking. This dilation-induced negative pore pressure increases the effective stress and hence the shear strength of the soil, thereby significantly reducing its liquefaction potential and enhancing its overall stability. The TDM columns, with their greater effective load attraction due to the enlarged head, appear to promote this beneficial mechanism more effectively than conventional DCM columns do, as evidenced by the lower final EPP values at Point C. Finally, the significant spatial variation in EPP values across the monitoring points reflects the complex three-dimensional stress redistribution patterns inherent within the column‒soil composite system.

Effect of reinforcement on horizontal displacement

Lateral displacement control is critical for embankment stability under seismic loading, as excessive horizontal movements can cause slope instability and structural damage even when vertical settlements are acceptable. This section compares horizontal displacement time histories for unreinforced, DCM-reinforced, and TDM-reinforced embankments under Van earthquake loading to evaluate the effectiveness of column reinforcement in controlling lateral displacement. Horizontal displacements were measured at two critical locations: Point D at the embankment toe, where the lateral earth pressure and slope stability concerns are greatest, and Point E at 5 m depth below the toe, where foundation movements directly affect embankment stability. These locations experience maximum lateral displacements due to embankment inertial forces, amplified slope face motion, and lateral spreading in soft clay. Other monitoring points showed negligible horizontal movement, confirming that lateral displacements are concentrated at embankment edges where confinement is minimal. Figure 7 shows the horizontal displacement time histories at Points D and E for all three configurations over 200 s. During the initial phase (0–80 s), all configurations exhibit minimal displacement with small oscillations (± 5 mm), indicating an elastic response to low-intensity seismic motions. Both reinforcement methods effectively limit lateral movement, with TDM columns achieving 30–40% lower displacement amplitudes than DCM columns do because of their enlarged head geometry (1.0 m diameter), which engages larger soil volumes and mobilizes greater passive earth pressure resistance.

Horizontal displacement time histories at (a) Point D (embankment toe) and (b) Point E (5 m below toe) for unreinforced, DCM-reinforced, and TDM-reinforced embankments.

The peak response phase (80–95 s) coincides with the maximum Van earthquake acceleration (PGA > 0.3 g) and represents the critical period for lateral displacement. Unreinforced embankments reach peak displacements of 6–7 cm at Point D, indicating significant lateral spreading. DCM reinforcement reduces this displacement to 4–5 cm (20–30% reduction), whereas TDM columns achieve only 2–3 cm displacement (50–60% reduction versus unreinforced columns, 30–40% versus DCM). This superior TDM performance during peak loading is crucial for preventing progressive failure mechanisms such as slope instability, embankment cracking, or local yielding. After peak acceleration (95–100 s), lateral displacements continue accumulating despite decreasing seismic intensity, ultimately converging to similar terminal values for all configurations. This postpeak phase shows rapid displacement increases (100–110 s) followed by gradual stabilization. Displacement accumulation occurs through residual mechanisms: (i) consolidation-induced lateral movements from excess pore pressure dissipation; (ii) continued plastic deformation in yielded zones; (iii) progressive degradation of the shear resistance of the soil‒column interface under cyclic strain; and (iv) gravity-driven creep in embankment slopes facilitated by seismic-induced strength reductions. A critical finding emerges: terminal lateral displacements converge to approximately 9 cm for all three configurations by 200 s, despite substantial differences during the initial and peak loading phases. This behavior suggests that while both the DCM and TDM columns effectively control initial and peak displacements during seismic loading, the extreme forces associated with Mw 7.2 earthquakes may eventually exceed the ultimate lateral resistance capacity of column‒soil composite systems. Several mechanisms may contribute to this convergence: (i) cumulative degradation of the soil‒column interface shear strength under cyclic loading; (ii) potential yielding or microcracking in column materials at large deformations; and (iii) progressive mobilization of the passive soil resistance approaching the ultimate limit states. For designs in high-seismic zones (PGA > 0.3 g or Mw > 7.0), these findings indicate that (1) column reinforcement should be viewed as a displacement-control measure rather than absolute displacement prevention; (2) hybrid systems combining TDM columns with geosynthetic reinforcement or pile-supported elements may be necessary to provide redundant lateral restraint 16,19; (3) performance-based design approaches should accept controlled deformations within serviceability limits rather than targeting elastic behavior; and (4) postearthquake inspection and potential remediation should be anticipated in design life-cycle planning. Notably, the TDM columns maintain significantly smaller displacements during the critical peak acceleration phase (90–95 s), which is advantageous for preventing progressive failure mechanisms and preserving structural integrity even if the ultimate displacements converge.

Column spacing impact

The parametric study examined three column spacings (1.67 m, 2.14 m, and 3.0 m) selected to span the practical range of ground improvement densities encountered in field applications. The closest spacing (1.67 m) corresponds to S/D ratios of approximately 2.8 for the DCM and 1.67 for the TDM head diameter, representing intensive improvement typically specified for heavy structures or severe soft soil conditions. The widest spacing (3.0 m) corresponds to S/D ratios of 5.0 for DCM and 3.0 for TDM, approaching the economic and technical limits beyond which column interaction becomes insufficient for effective composite behavior [26]. The intermediate spacing (2.14 m) provides an optimal design point balancing performance and cost-effectiveness. These spacings are consistent with field installation practices reported in the literature 10,13,16,30 and construction guidelines, ensuring that findings are directly relevant to practicing engineers. Static analyses confirm that column spacing significantly influences embankment stability 17,18. To assess the impact on dynamic behavior, a dynamic analysis was conducted with varying column spacings. Figures 8 and 9 present settlement measurements at monitoring points A through G for DCM- and TDM-reinforced embankments subjected to identical earthquake loading for three column spacings: 1.67 m, 2.14 m, and 3 m. For DCM columns (Fig. 8), settlement at the embankment crest demonstrates strong spacing sensitivity, measuring 0.34 cm at the closest spacing of 1.67 m but increasing dramatically to 2.30 cm at both 2.14 m and 3.0 m spacings—a sevenfold increase that indicates a critical transition from optimal to suboptimal column density. This plateau effect at wider spacings suggests that beyond approximately 2.0–2.5 m, DCM columns behave as isolated elements with minimal stress zone interactions between adjacent columns. In contrast, the TDM columns (Fig. 9) demonstrate markedly superior performance across all spacing configurations, with settlements of 0.32 cm at 1.67 m spacing, 0.48 cm at 2.14 m spacing, and 2.30 cm at 3.0 m spacing. The critical observation is that at the intermediate 2.14 m spacing, the TDM columns achieve 79% less settlement than the DCM columns do (0.48 cm versus 2.30 cm), which is attributable to the enlarged column heads (1.0 m diameter) creating larger stress influence zones that extend 1.5–2.0 head diameters radially, enabling effective stress overlap even at wider spacings than conventional DCM columns can accommodate.

At greater depths (Point C, 5 m below the surface), both column systems show reduced settlement magnitudes compared with surface measurements, confirming effective load interception near the surface. At 2.14 m spacing, the TDM columns achieve 78% less settlement than the DCM columns do (0.20 cm versus 0.90 cm), demonstrating that the enlarged TDM heads function as shallow foundation elements that spread loads over larger areas at the embankment‒foundation interface, thereby minimizing stress penetration into deeper compressible strata. This depth-dependent performance advantage is particularly significant because it indicates that TDM columns not only reduce surface settlements but also fundamentally alter the load transfer mechanism throughout the foundation depth. The settlement time histories exhibit characteristic three-phase dynamic response patterns: an initial stable phase (0–80 s) with minor oscillations representing the elastic response, a critical loading phase (80–95 s) coinciding with peak ground acceleration where settlements accumulate rapidly through elastic compression, plastic deformation, and pore pressure-induced consolidation, and a postpeak stabilization phase (95–200 s) where excess pore water pressures dissipate. Compared with DCM columns, TDM columns consistently demonstrate smoother settlement evolution curves with reduced oscillation amplitudes, indicating a more controlled dynamic response, enhanced damping characteristics, and more efficient consolidation behavior provided by the larger column head geometry and increased soil‒column interface area.

The pronounced sensitivity of settlement to column spacing can be explained through stress influence zone overlap theory, which provides fundamental insight into the superior performance of TDM columns at wider spacings. DCM columns 0.6 m in diameter create stress influence zones extending approximately 0.9–1.2 m radially, yielding an effective influence diameter of 1.8–2.4 m. At a 1.67 m center-to-center spacing, the edge-to-edge distance between columns is approximately 1.1 m, falling within the overlapping influence zone range and producing optimal performance through nearly continuous stress relief throughout the improved soil mass. However, at 2.14 m spacing, the edge distance increases to 1.54 m, approaching the limit of effective stress influence and explaining the sharp performance degradation observed. At 3.0 m spacing with 2.4 m edge distance, the stress influence zones become clearly disconnected, creating isolated “islands” of improved soil surrounded by unimproved regions that retain high compressibility, resulting in performance approaching that of isolated columns. In contrast, TDM columns with a head diameter of 1.0 m generate significantly larger stress influence zones extending 1.5–2.0 m radially, with influence diameters of 3.0–4.0 m. This enlarged geometry enables TDM columns to maintain substantial stress overlap at 2.14 m spacing (edge distance of 1.14 m), explaining the excellent performance of 0.48 cm settlement, and even at 3.0 m spacing (edge distance of 2.0 m), TDM zones experience marginal overlap or near contact, providing markedly better performance than comparably spaced DCM columns.

On the basis of this comprehensive parametric analysis, clear design recommendations emerge for optimal column spacing selection in seismic applications. For DCM columns, a spacing of 1.67 m (S/D ≈ 2.8) provides excellent seismic performance with a settlement of less than 0.5 cm and represents the recommended maximum spacing for high-seismicity regions, whereas a spacing of 2.14 m (S/D ≈ 3.6) represents a critical degradation threshold beyond which performance deteriorates significantly, making it unsuitable for applications requiring stringent deformation control, and a spacing of 3.0 m or greater (S/D ≥ 5.0) produces poor performance approaching unreinforced conditions and should be avoided entirely in seismically active regions. For TDM columns, spacing of 1.67 m provides optimal performance but may be uneconomical given diminishing marginal returns, whereas spacing of 2.14 m (S/D ≈ 2.1 on the basis of head diameter) represents an excellent balance between performance (settlement of approximately 0.5 cm) and economy, making it the recommended design point for practical applications, and spacing of 3.0 m (S/D ≈ 3.0) shows acceptable performance for moderate seismic loading but requires careful site-specific evaluation for high-seismicity regions.

The superior performance of TDM columns at wider spacings translates directly to significant economic advantages: for equivalent settlement control (targeting settlement less than 0.5 cm), TDM columns can be installed at 2.14 m spacing, whereas DCM columns require 1.67 m spacing, representing a 28% reduction in column quantity and corresponding cost savings in materials, installation time, and equipment mobilization. A parametric study of column spacing unequivocally demonstrated its profound impact on system performance. The relationship between closer spacing (e.g., 1.67 m) and reduced settlements/EPP is nonlinear, indicating a threshold beyond which additional columns yield diminishing returns. For the soil profile and loading conditions analyzed, the 2.14 m spacing for the TDM columns appears to be a highly efficient design point, offering performance nearly equivalent to that of the 1.67 m spacing but with a significantly reduced number of columns and thus a lower cost. In contrast, the 3.0 m spacing, for both column types, performed poorly, often approaching the behavior of an unreinforced soil mass. This provides practicing engineers with valuable guidelines: a spacing-to-diameter ratio (S/D) exceeding 5 (for DCM) may be ineffective for seismic resistance, whereas TDM columns allow for a wider effective spacing owing to their enlarged head, optimizing the cost‒performance ratio.

Cost and constructability considerations

While the superior seismic performance of TDM columns is clearly demonstrated, practical implementation requires consideration of cost and constructability trade-offs. Compared with conventional DCM, TDM column installation necessitates specialized auger equipment capable of creating enlarged head geometry, potentially increasing mobilization costs 10. However, several factors favor TDM economics: (i) fewer columns are required to achieve equivalent performance due to a larger head diameter and more efficient load distribution—as demonstrated by comparable performance at wider spacings; (ii) a reduced column count decreases the total installation time and material consumption; and (iii) the enhanced load transfer mechanism may eliminate the need for supplementary measures (e.g., geosynthetic reinforcement) in moderate seismic zones. Preliminary cost estimates from limited field applications 16 suggest that TDM systems may achieve 10–20% cost savings compared with equivalent DCM arrays when total project costs (materials, installation, and quality control) are considered. The construction challenges include (i) ensuring uniform head formation in heterogeneous soils; (ii) verifying the quality control of enlarged head dimensions; and (iii) ensuring the potential for installation-induced disturbances in very soft clays. Field trials and performance monitoring are recommended before widespread adoption, particularly in high-seismic-frequency zones where the demonstrated performance advantages provide the strongest justification. Life-cycle cost analysis incorporating reduced maintenance and improved long-term performance should be conducted for site-specific applications.

Conclusions

This study presents a comprehensive comparative evaluation of the seismic performance of highway embankments constructed over soft soils reinforced with conventional deep cement mixing (DCM) columns and innovative T-shaped deep mixing (TDM) columns via 3D finite element analysis. A finite element model implemented in Plaxis3D was used to numerically analyze the settlement, horizontal displacement, and excess pore water pressure at various locations within the embankment. The following conclusions can be drawn from this study:

Compared with conventional DCM columns, both DCM columns and TDM columns significantly enhance the seismic stability of highway embankments on soft soil, with TDM columns consistently demonstrating superior performance in mitigating settlement (up to 89.95% reduction vs. unreinforced) and controlling lateral displacement.
