Introduction

Pulsed-field ablation (PFA) has recently emerged as a novel nonthermal energy source for atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation. The mechanism of electroporation selectively affects myocardial tissue while relatively sparing adjacent structures such as the esophagus and phrenic nerve.1 Clinical studies evaluating PFA for AF ablation have demonstrated promising efficacy and safety outcomes.2

Although many PFA systems are primarily designed for pulmonary vein isolation using balloon or basket-based catheters, focal PFA catheters may provide additional procedural flexibility for linear lesion sets and substrate modification. The FARAPOINT catheter (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA) is a focal multielectrode PFA catheter designed to deliver both focal and linear lesions with consistent lesion depth.2 Preclinical data suggest lesion depths up to approximately 8 mm depending on voltage and the number of applications delivered.3 We present a case illustrating the use of a focal PFA catheter to achieve posterior wall isolation (PWI) during a redo AF ablation procedure.

Case presentation

A 76-year-old man with prior radiofrequency PVI was referred for recurrent symptomatic atrial arrhythmia. Under general anesthesia, a single transseptal puncture was performed and detailed biatrial high-density mapping was created using the Orion catheter (Boston Scientific) and the OPAL HDx system (Boston Scientific). Baseline rhythm and activation mapping demonstrated typical counterclockwise cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI)–dependent flutter, while all pulmonary veins remained isolated from the index procedure. Because of the patient’s history of persistent AF, we elected to perform an empirical adjunctive PWI in addition to the CTI line.

PWI was performed using a focal PFA catheter (FARAPOINT, Boston Scientific). The catheter’s multielectrode configuration enabled sequential point-by-point lesion delivery while covering a relatively large tissue surface area per application. A series of contiguous lesions were delivered along the superior and inferior aspects of the posterior wall, creating linear lesion sets designed to electrically isolate the posterior wall region. Measurement of lesion spacing showed distances between adjacent applications ranging from approximately 6 to 12 mm, with an overall vertical distance of approximately 37 mm between the superior (roof) and inferior (floor) lesion lines (Figure 1). In total, 16 applications were delivered (2 applications for each ablation lesion), whereas the total duration of PWI was 6 minutes. These measurements were compatible with expected lesion overlap given the anticipated lesion dimensions of PFA. After completion of the lesion set, repeat high-density voltage mapping demonstrated electrical silence within the posterior wall, confirming successful isolation.

Sequential overlapping applications created a continuous lesion set spanning approximately 37 mm between the roof and inferior lines. 4 ablation sites were delivered along the roof line and 4 along the inferior line, with 2 applications per site, resulting in interlesion spacing ranging from 6.25 to 11.7 mm, consistent with adequate lesion overlap.

CTI ablation was subsequently performed using the same catheter, with prophylactic intravenous nitrate administered before energy delivery to minimize the risk of coronary vasospasm. Bidirectional CTI block was achieved, with a total ablation time of 6 minutes. No acute complication was recorded during the procedure, and the patient had an uneventful recovery.

Discussion

PWI with pulsed-field energy is typically performed with a pentaspline catheter (FARAWAVE, Boston Scientific). In contrast, the focal-linear catheter used herein incorporates 4 independent electrodes across a 16-mm treatment segment and is designed to create focal or short linear lesions with predictable catheter handling. In the ADVANTAGE AF Phase 2 trial,4 a large multicenter study of persistent AF, PFA using a pentaspline catheter was used for both PVI and PWI whereas a focal PFA catheter was used for CTI ablation. In this study, PFA PVI plus PWI was feasible and safe, and use of the same focal catheter for CTI ablation was efficient, requiring 18 ± 6 applications over 8 ± 13 minutes after intravenous nitroglycerin prophylaxis. Although that trial did not study PWI with the focal catheter, it supports the overall platform’s workflow efficiency and the focal catheter’s ability to deliver rapid, reproducible linear lesion sets.

This case extends that concept to a more individualized left atrial strategy. PWI with conventional point-by-point radiofrequency ablation catheters is slow and technically demanding. A focal PFA catheter may offer 3 practical advantages in this setting. First, its multielectrode design can create short contiguous lesions with each application, reducing the number of catheter repositionings. Second, lesion formation is not dependent on conductive heating, potentially simplifying workflow in regions where catheter stability is imperfect. Third, a focal platform may allow the operator to tailor lesion orientation to patient-specific anatomy rather than relying exclusively on a fixed flower or basket configuration.

This case illustrates the potential role of focal PFA technology for posterior wall substrate modification, with controlled lesion spacing and predictable lesion depth enabling efficient PWI. Sequential overlapping applications created a continuous posterior wall lesion set spanning approximately 37 mm between the roof and inferior lines, with interlesion spacing ranging from 6.25 to 11.7 mm, ensuring adequate lesion overlap. Although this does not prove adequate lesion penetration or chronic durability, it demonstrates that broad posterior wall coverage can be achieved in a controlled, anatomically directed manner with a focal PFA catheter.

Off-label use for PWI should not be interpreted as equivalent to established workflows. Energy delivery near collateral structures, the optimal number of overlapping applications, ideal interlesion spacing, and long-term durability remain undefined. PFA on the left atrial posterior wall has not been associated with coronary spasm in prior clinical experience, but dedicated evidence for this specific catheter and lesion set is lacking. Accordingly, this technique should presently be viewed as a carefully selected bailout or workflow-optimizing strategy rather than a standard PWI approach.

Conclusion

In a redo atrial arrhythmia procedure, PWI with a focal PFA catheter was feasible, rapid, and effective, with postablation electroanatomic remapping confirming an electrically silent posterior wall and no intraprocedural complications. This case suggests that focal multielectrode pulsed-field technology may broaden the toolbox for tailored PWI, but prospective studies are needed before routine adoption.
