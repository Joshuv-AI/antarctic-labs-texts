Semiformal method I. Pólya’s theorem for the complete graph K ℕ K_{\mathbb{N}} with complex edge weights
M. Klazar (UK, Praha) and R. Horský (VŠE, Praha)

Abstract
Using the semiformal method in combinatorics we generalize Pólya’s theorem. This theorem determines the limiting probability that walks in the grid graph ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} visit the given vertex. We generalize it to the countable complete graph K ℕ K_{\mathbb{N}} with edge weights in ℂ \mathbb{C} . In part II we treat edge weights in ℂ ⁡ [ [ x 1 , … , x k ] ] \mathbb{C}[[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]] .

Let
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
(
=\{1,2,\dots\}
) and
\overline{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
\mathbb{Z}=\{\dots,-1,0,1,\dots\}

\mathbb{Z}^{d}

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{d}(\overline{v},\,n)\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}=1&\dots&\text{if $d\leq 2$ and}\\
<1&\dots&\text{if $d\geq 3$}\,.\end{array}\right.

\overline{v}=\overline{0}
, although he proved it for any vertex
\overline{v}
(see
[
16
, Section 1]
for a translated quote from
[
24
]
). A selection of articles and books discussing Pólya’s theorem is

,

We generalize it as follows. Let

{\textstyle\mathbb{N}_{2}=\binom{\mathbb{N}}{2}=\{e\colon\;e\subset\mathbb{N}\wedge|e|=2\}}

be edges of the countable complete graph

K_{\mathbb{N}}=\langle\mathbb{N},\mathbb{N}_{2}\rangle

on the vertex set
\mathbb{N}
. For a finite set
A
we denote by

|A|
(
\in\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1.\dots\}
) its cardinality. Let

h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}

be a complex edge weight.
\mathbb{C}
denotes the field of complex
numbers, which is endowed with the usual Archimedean absolute value
|z|=\sqrt{z\cdot\overline{z}}
. For

n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
let
W(n)
be the set of walks of length

n
in
K_{\mathbb{N}}
starting at the vertex
1
. Thus
W(0)=\{\langle 1\rangle\}
and for
n>0
the set
W(n)
is infinite and
countable. For
v\in\mathbb{N}
let
W(v,n)
be the subset of
W(n)
of
the walks that visit the vertex
v
in a step

i>0
. We extend the weight in the usual multiplicative way to

{\textstyle h\colon\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}W(n)\to\mathbb{C}\,.}

Let
W\subset\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}W(n)
. If
the series

{\textstyle\sum_{w\in W}h(w)}

absolutely converges, we denote its sum by
h(W)
. (Results on
series are reviewed in the next section.) Suppose that for every

n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the series
\sum_{w\in W(n)}h(w)
absolutely converges. Then so does
\sum_{w\in W(v,n)}h(w)
and we consider the generating function

{\textstyle A_{h,v}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}h(W(v,\,n))x^{n}\ \ (\in\mathbb{C}[[x]])\,.}

Our generalizations of Pólya’s theorem are formulas for the quantities

{\textstyle A_{h,v}(1)=\sum_{n\geq 0}h(W(v,\,n))\,\text{ and }\,\lim_{n\to\infty}h(W(v,\,n))\,.}

The formulas appear in fourteen theorems:
Theorems
3.2
–
3.7
in
Section
3
and Theorems
4.2
–
4.9
in
Section
4
. Pólya’s theorem is subsumed in five of them.
Theorems
3.5
and
3.7
extend cases
d\geq 3
and
d\leq 2
, respectively, when
\overline{v}=\overline{0}
.
Theorems
4.6
(
4.7
) and
4.9
extend
cases
d\geq 3
and
d\leq 2
, respectively, when

\overline{v}\neq\overline{0}
.

We have fourteen generalizations of one theorem for three reasons.
First, in Pólya’s theorem
weights are nonnegative real numbers and convergence of infinite series is
automatically absolute. For complex weights we consider both
kinds of convergence,
absolute and conditional one. We also consider, for nonnegative real weights,
series with sum
+\infty
. Second, Pólya’s theorem lives in the probabilistic setup where weights of
edges incident to a fixed vertex sum up to
1
. We consider the case when this condition is kept (convex weights) and the case when it is dropped. Third,
already Pólya’s theorem is in reality two theorems because the cases

\overline{v}=\overline{0}
and
\overline{v}\neq\overline{0}
differ in complexity of proofs. In our generalization we distinguish
cases
v=1
and
v\neq 1
as well. Now
3\cdot 2\cdot 2=12
; two more
generalizations arise by variations of assumptions.

We explain how our
approach includes Pólya’s original problem. If
f\colon A\to B
is a map and
C
is any set (not necessarily a subset of
A
), we call the set

f[C]=\{f(x)\colon\;x\in A\cap C\}\ \ (\subset B)

the image of
C
by
f
. Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\overline{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We take any bijection

f\colon\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{N}

such that
f(\overline{0})=1
, and set
v=f(\overline{v})
. The grid graph on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, which is the graph
\langle\mathbb{Z}^{d},E_{d}\rangle
with the edges

{\textstyle\{\overline{a},\,\overline{b}\}\in E_{d}\iff\sum_{i=1}^{d}|a_{i}-b_{i}|=1\,,}

determines the weight
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\{\frac{1}{2d},0\}
by the relation

h(e)\neq 0
iff
e=f[e^{\prime}]
for some

e^{\prime}\in E_{d}
. Then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have

P_{d}(\overline{v},\,n)=h(W(v,\,n))\,.

2
Series and generating functions in
\mathbb{C}

We review series and generating functions in the domain
\mathbb{C}
. A
series
(in
\mathbb{C}
) is a map

h\colon X\to\mathbb{C}
, defined on an at most countable set
X
. We write it as

\sum_{x\in X}h(x)
. We say that

\sum_{x\in X}h(x)
absolutely converges
if two equivalent conditions hold.

1.

For some
c>0
and every finite set
Y\subset X
we have
\sum_{x\in Y}|h(x)|\leq c
.

2.

If
X
is countable, then for every bijection
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to X
, the limit

s=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(f(i))}

exists and does not depend on
f
.

We call
s
the
sum
of the series and denote it again by
\sum_{x\in X}h(x)
. If the set

X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\}\neq\emptyset
is finite, then every series

\sum_{x\in X}h(x)
absolutely converges and has the sum

h(x_{1})+h(x_{2})+\dots+h(x_{n})\,.

For
X=\emptyset
we define the sum as
0
. If

{\textstyle U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}\ \ (\in\mathbb{C}[[x]])}

is a formal power series, so that
x
is
a formal variable, we denote for any
x\in\mathbb{C}
by
F_{U}(x)
the
sum of the power series

\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}
,
if it absolutely converges.

Let
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
. A
walk

w
in
K_{\mathbb{N}}
is any
(n+1)
-tuple
w
of
vertices
v_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
,

w=\langle v_{0},\,v_{1},\,\dots,\,v_{n}\rangle\,,

such that
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
v_{i-1}\neq v_{i}
for every
i\in[n]

(
=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
,
[0]=\emptyset
).
The
length
n
of
w
is is
n
(
\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
). We
extend
h
to walks. For
n>0
we set

{\textstyle h(w)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}h(\{v_{i-1},\,v_{i}\})\,,}

and for
n=0
we define
h(w)=1
.
Recall that
W(n)
is the set of walks
w
in
K_{\mathbb{N}}
with length
n
such that
v_{0}=1
.

Definition 2.1
(lightness)

A weight
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
is light if for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the series

{\textstyle\sum_{w\in W(n)}h(w)}

absolutely converges.

Let
S=\sum_{x\in X}h(x)
be a series. If
Y\subset X
, we say that
\sum_{x\in Y}h(x)
is a
subseries
of
S
. Any subseries of an
absolutely convergent series absolutely converges, but we cannot compare sizes of their sums

{\textstyle\big|\sum_{x\in Y}h(x)\big|\,\text{ and }\,\big|\sum_{x\in X}h(x)\big|\,.}

Except when
h\colon X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
: if
\sum_{x\in X}h(x)

absolutely converges and
Y\subset X
, then the sums satisfy

{\textstyle 0\leq\sum_{x\in Y}h(x)\leq\sum_{x\in X}h(x)\,.}

If
R=\sum_{x\in X}g(x)
and
S=\sum_{x\in X}h(x)
are
series and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
, the series

{\textstyle\alpha R+\beta S=\sum_{x\in X}(\alpha g(x)+\beta h(x))}

is their
linear combination
. We omit the
easy proof of the next result.

Proposition 2.2

If
R
and
S
absolutely converge and have
respective sums
r
and
s
, then series
\alpha R+\beta S
absolutely
converges and has the sum
\alpha r+\beta s
.

The next approximation lemma is a key tool.

Lemma 2.3

If
S=\sum_{x\in X}h(x)
absolutely converges and has sum
s
, then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a finite set

Y\subset X
, denoted by

Y(S,\,\varepsilon)
, such that for every finite set
Z
with
Y\subset Z\subset X
we have

{\textstyle\big|s-\sum_{x\in Z}h(x)\big|\leq\varepsilon\,.}

Proof.
Let an
\varepsilon>0
be given.
For finite
X
we set
Y=X
. For countable
X
we take any bijection

f\colon\mathbb{N}\to X
, take an
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

{\textstyle|\sum_{n=1}^{N}h(f(n))-s|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\text{ and }\,\sum_{n>N}|h(f(n))|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}

and set
Y=f[\,[N]\,]
. Then for every finite set
Z
with
Y\subset Z\subset X
we have

{\textstyle|s-\sum_{x\in Z}h(x)|\leq|s-\sum_{x\in Y}h(x)|+\sum_{x\in Z\setminus Y}|h(x)|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon\,.}

\Box

Two more operations with series are
grouping and product. A
partition
of a set
X
is a set
P
of nonempty and
disjoint sets such that
\bigcup P=X
. If
X
is at most countable,
then so is
P
and every set
Z\in P
. If
S=\sum_{x\in X}h(x)
is
a series and
P
is a partition of
X
such that for
every set
Z\in P
the subseries
\sum_{x\in Z}h(x)
absolutely
converges and has
sum
s_{Z}
, then the series

{\textstyle S_{P}=\sum_{Z\in P}s_{Z}}

is called the
grouping
of
S
.

Proposition 2.4

If
S=\sum_{x\in X}h(x)
absolutely
converges and has sum
s
, and if
P

is a partition of
X
, then
S_{P}
is correctly defined, absolutely
converges and has the same sum
s
.

Proof.
For every
Z\in P
the series

S_{Z}=\sum_{x\in Z}h(x)
is a subseries of
S
and absolutely
converges. Thus the series
S_{P}
is correctly defined. We show that it absolutely converges. Let

{\textstyle c=\sup(\{\sum_{x\in Z}|h(x)|\colon\;\text{$Z\subset X$ and $Z$ is finite}\})\ \ (<+\infty)\,.}

Let
\{Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{n}\}\subset P
be a finite set.
We use Lemma
2.3
and take for
i\in[n]
finite sets

Z_{i}^{\prime}=Y(S_{Z_{i}},\,2^{-i})\ \ (\subset Z_{i})\,.

We form the disjoint union
Z_{0}=Z_{1}^{\prime}\cup\dots\cup Z_{n}^{\prime}
(
\subset X
). Then

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big|\sum_{x\in Z_{i}}h(x)\big|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big|\sum_{x\in Z_{i}}h(x)-\sum_{x\in Z_{i}^{\prime}}h(x)\big|\,+}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{x\in Z_{0}}|h(x)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}2^{-i}+c\leq 1+c}

and
S_{P}
absolutely converges.

Let
t
be the sum of
S_{P}
.
We show that
|t-s|\leq\varepsilon
for every
\varepsilon>0
; then
t=s
. Let an
\varepsilon>0
be given. We use
Lemma
2.3
and take finite sets

{\textstyle X^{\prime}=Y(S,\,\frac{\varepsilon}{3})\ \ (\subset X)\,\text{ and }\,P^{\prime}=Y(S_{P},\,\frac{\varepsilon}{3})\ \ (\subset P)\,.}

We take a finite set
\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\}\subset P
such that

{\textstyle P^{\prime}\subset\{Z_{1},\,\dots,\,Z_{n}\}\,\text{ and }\,X^{\prime}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\,.}

We again use Lemma
2.3
and take for
i\in[n]
finite sets

{\textstyle Z_{i}^{\prime}=Y(S_{Z_{i}},\,2^{-i}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{3})\ \ (\subset Z_{i})\,.}

For
i\in[n]
let

Z_{i}^{\prime\prime}=Z_{i}^{\prime}\cup(X^{\prime}\cap Z_{i})\ \ (\subset Z_{i})\,.

We form the disjoint union

{\textstyle X_{0}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{\prime\prime}\ \ (\subset X)\,.}

Then
X_{0}
is finite and
X^{\prime}\subset X_{0}
. Also,
Z_{i}^{\prime}\subset Z_{i}^{\prime\prime}
for every
i\in[n]
. We have

\displaystyle|s-t|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\textstyle|s-\sum_{x\in X_{0}}h(x)|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\sum_{x\in Z_{i}^{\prime\prime}}h(x)-\sum_{x\in Z_{i}}h(x)|\,+}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle{\textstyle|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x\in Z_{i}}h(x)-t|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}2^{-i}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\leq\varepsilon\,.}

\Box

Let
A
and
B
be sets. We denote their ordered pair by

\langle A,\,B\rangle
.
If
S_{i}=\sum_{x\in X_{i}}h_{i}(x)
,
i=1,2
, are series, then the
series

{\textstyle S_{1}\cdot S_{2}=\sum_{\langle x,\,y\rangle\in X_{1}\times X_{2}}h_{1}(x)h_{2}(y)}

is
the
product
of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
.

Proposition 2.5

If
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
absolutely converge and have sums
s_{1}
and

s_{2}
, respectively, then
S_{1}\cdot S_{2}
absolutely converges
and has the sum
s_{1}s_{2}
.

Proof.
We show that
S_{1}\cdot S_{2}
absolutely converges. Let

c\geq 0
be such that for any finite sets
Y_{i}\subset X_{i}
,
i=1,2
, we have

\sum_{x\in Y_{i}}|h_{i}(x)|\leq c
.
Let
A\subset X_{1}\times X_{2}
be a finite set. We
take any finite sets
Y_{i}\subset X_{i}
such that
A\subset Y_{1}\times Y_{2}
and
get the desired bound:

{\textstyle\sum_{\langle x,\,y\rangle\in A}|h_{1}(x)h_{2}(y)|\leq\sum_{x\in Y_{1}}|h_{1}(x)|\cdot\sum_{y\in Y_{2}}|h_{2}(y)|\leq c\cdot c=c^{2}\,.}

We show that the sum
s
of
S_{1}\cdot S_{1}
equals
s_{1}s_{2}
.
It suffices to prove that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have
|s-s_{1}s_{2}|\leq\varepsilon
. So let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
Using Lemma
2.3
we take finite sets

{\textstyle X^{\prime}_{1}=Y(S_{1},\,\frac{\varepsilon}{4(1+|s_{2}|)})\ \ (\subset X_{1}),\ \ X^{\prime}_{2}=Y(S_{2},\,\frac{\varepsilon}{4(1+|s_{1}|)})\ \ (\subset X_{2})\ }

and

{\textstyle Z=Y(S_{1}\cdot S_{2},\frac{\varepsilon}{4})\ \ (\subset X_{1}\times X_{2})\,.}

We take finite
sets
X_{1}^{\prime\prime}
and
X_{2}^{\prime\prime}
such that
X_{1}^{\prime}\subset X_{1}^{\prime\prime}\subset X_{1}
,
X_{2}^{\prime}\subset X_{2}^{\prime\prime}\subset X_{2}
and
Z\subset X_{1}^{\prime\prime}\times X_{2}^{\prime\prime}
. Then
|s-s_{1}s_{2}|
is at most

\displaystyle{\textstyle\big|s-\sum_{\langle x,\,y\rangle\in X_{1}^{\prime\prime}\times X_{2}^{\prime\prime}}h_{1}(x)h_{2}(y)\big|+\big|\sum_{x\in X_{1}^{\prime\prime}}h_{1}(x)\sum_{y\in X_{2}^{\prime\prime}}h_{2}(y)-s_{1}s_{2}\big|}

\displaystyle{\textstyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+|(s_{1}+t_{1})(s_{2}+t_{2})-s_{1}s_{2}|\,,}

where

|t_{1}|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4(1+|s_{2}|)}
and
|t_{2}|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4(1+|s_{1}|)}
. Hence
|s-s_{1}s_{2}|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+|s_{1}t_{2}|+|s_{2}t_{1}|+|t_{1}t_{2}|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}=\varepsilon
.

\Box

Let
S_{i}=\sum_{x\in X_{i}}h_{i}(x)
,

i\in[k]
, be
k\in\mathbb{N}
series. We define more generally their product as the series

{\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}S_{i}=\sum_{\overline{x}\in\overline{X}}h_{1}(x_{1})h_{2}(x_{2})\dots h_{k}(x_{k})\,,}

where
\overline{x}=\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\rangle
is an ordered
k
-tuple of
x_{1}
,
\dots
,
x_{k}

and
\overline{X}=X_{1}\times\dots\times X_{k}
. Iterating
Proposition
2.5
we generalize it as follows.

Proposition 2.6

If the series
S_{1}
,
\dots
,
S_{k}
absolutely converge and have sums
s_{1}
,
\dots
,
s_{k}
, respectively, then
\prod_{i=1}^{k}S_{i}
absolutely converges and has sum
s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k}
.

Interestingly, a reversal of Proposition
2.5
holds.

Corollary 2.7

If nonempty series
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are not identically zero and if
their product
S_{1}\cdot S_{2}
absolutely converges and has the sum
s
,
then
S_{1}
and
S_{2}

absolutely converge and their respective sums
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
satisfy

.

Proof.
This follows from Proposition
2.5
if we
show that
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
absolutely converge. Let
S_{i}=\sum_{x\in X_{i}}h_{i}(x)
,
i=1,2
, and let
c>0
be such that for every finite set

we have

{\textstyle\sum_{\langle x,y\rangle\in X}|h_{1}(x)h_{2}(y)|\leq c\,.}

We prove absolute
convergence of
S_{1}
. We take any
y\in X_{2}
with
h_{2}(y)\neq 0
. Then for
every finite set
X\subset X_{1}
we have

{\textstyle\sum_{x\in X}|h_{1}(x)|=\frac{1}{|h_{2}(y)|}\sum_{\langle x,y\rangle\in X\times\{y\}}|h_{1}(x)h_{2}(y)|\leq\frac{c}{|h_{2}(y)|}\,.}

Thus
S_{1}
absolutely converges. For
S_{2}
we argue similarly.

\Box

It is clear that the assumptions on
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
cannot be

Let
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
We obtain a sufficient condition for
the lightness of the weight
h
in terms of its values. We denote
by
V(h,n)
the set of vertices in
K_{\mathbb{N}}

reachable from
1
by
a walk of length at most
n
and with nonzero weight.

Definition 2.8
(slimness)

A weight
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
is slim if for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there is
a constant
c=c(n)>0
such that for every vertex

u\in V(h,n)
and every finite set
X\subset\mathbb{N}\setminus\{u\}
we have

{\textstyle\sum_{v\in X}|h(\{u,\,v\})|\leq c\,.}

Proposition 2.9

Every slim weight
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
is light.

Proof.
Let
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
be slim. We show by induction on
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
that the series

{\textstyle\sum_{w\in W(n)}h(w)}

absolutely converges. For
n=0
it is trivial because
W(0)=\{\langle 1\rangle\}
.
Let
n>0
and let

c>0
be a constant such that for every finite set
X\subset W(n-1)
we have

{\textstyle\sum_{w\in X}|h(w)|\leq c}

and that for every
u\in V(h,n-1)
and every finite set
X\subset\mathbb{N}\setminus\{u\}
we have

{\textstyle\sum_{v\in X}|h(\{u,\,v\})|\leq c\,.}

Let
X\subset W(n)

be finite. We may assume that
walks in
X
have only edges with nonzero weights. We decompose
every walk
w\in X
as
w=w^{\prime}\,\ell(w)
, where
w^{\prime}\in W(n-1)
and
\ell(w)
is the last vertex. Let
Y
be the set of walks
w^{\prime}
. For
w^{\prime}\in Y
let

X(w^{\prime})=\{v\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{\ell(w^{\prime})\}\colon\;w^{\prime}v\in X\}\,.

We get the bound

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{w\in X}|h(w)|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{w^{\prime}\in Y}|h(w^{\prime})|\cdot\sum_{v\in X(w^{\prime})}|h(\{\ell(w^{\prime}),\,v\})|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{w^{\prime}\in Y}|h(w^{\prime})|\cdot c\leq c^{2}\,.}

\Box

Let
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
. We define

V(h)=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}V(h,n)
.
It is the set of vertices in
K_{\mathbb{N}}

reachable from

by walks with nonzero weights. We define the graph

as the connected component of
1
in the subgraph of
K_{\mathbb{N}}
formed
by edges with nonzero weight. It follows that if

is slim and hence light.

Definition 2.10
(convexity)

A light weight

is convex if for every vertex

the sum

{\textstyle\sum_{e\in E(h),\,e\ni u}h(e)=\sum_{v\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{u\}}h(\{u,\,v\})=1\,.}

We show that these sums exist.

Proposition 2.11

Let
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
be light and

. Then the series

{\textstyle\sum_{v\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{u\}}h(\{u,v\})}

absolutely converges.

Proof.
There is a walk
w_{0}=\langle u_{0},\dots,u_{n}\rangle
in
G(h)
such that
u_{0}=1
and
u_{n}=u
. Let
c=h(w_{0})
(
\neq 0
) and
W
be the set of walks

\langle u_{0},\dots,u_{n},u_{n+1}\rangle
in
K_{\mathbb{N}}
.
Thus vertices
u_{0}
,
\dots
,
u_{n}
are fixed and
u_{n+1}
runs in

\mathbb{N}\setminus\{u_{n}\}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{u\}
. The series
\sum_{w\in W}h(w)
is a subseries of
\sum_{w\in W(n+1)}h(w)

and therefore absolutely
converges. By

,

{\textstyle\sum_{v\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{u\}}h(\{u,v\})=\frac{1}{c}\sum_{w\in W}h(w)}

\Box

We survey some results on
generating functions. We work with univariate formal power series

{\textstyle U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n},\,\text{ where $u_{n}\in\mathbb{C}$ and $x$ is a~formal variable}\,.}

They form an integral domain denoted by
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
. For
U(x)
and
V(x)
in
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
,

{\textstyle U(x)+V(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(u_{n}+v_{n})x^{n},\ U(x)\cdot V(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\big(\sum_{j=0}^{n}u_{j}v_{n-j}\big)x^{n}\,.}

Neutral elements are

0=0+0x+0x^{2}+\dots
and
1=1+0x+0x^{2}+\cdots
. Units in

\mathbb{C}[[x]]
are exactly the formal power series

\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}
with
a_{0}\neq 0
.

Definition 2.12
(
U(1)_{1}
and
U(1)_{2}
)

Let
U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}

be in
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
. We introduce the following terminology and objects.

1.

If the limit

U(1)_{1}=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}u_{j}\ \ (\in\mathbb{C})}

exists, we say that
U(1)_{1}
exists.

2.

If the series

{\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}1^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}}

absolutely converges, we denote the sum by
U(1)_{2}
and say that
U(1)_{2}
exists.

If
U(1)_{2}
exists, then
U(1)_{1}
exists and
U(1)_{1}=U(1)_{2}
, but
the opposite implication is, in general, not true. For example, if

{\textstyle U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n-1}\frac{1}{n}x^{n}}

then
U(1)_{1}
exists and
U(1)_{1}=\log 2
, but
U(1)_{2}
does
not exist. But if

{\textstyle U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[[x]]}

and
U(1)_{1}
exists,
then
U(1)_{2}
exists and
U(1)_{1}=U(1)_{2}
. The following result is well known.

Proposition 2.13

If
U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}\in\mathbb{C}[[x]]
and
U(1)_{1}

exists, then the power series

\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}

absolutely converges for every
x\in\mathbb{C}
with
|x|<1
.

In fact, for the absolute convergence it suffices (and is necessary) by Hadamard’s formula that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}|u_{n}|^{1/n}\leq 1
.

Definition 2.14
(
U(1)_{1}=+\infty
)

If
U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}
is in
\mathbb{R}[[x]]
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}u_{j}=+\infty\,,}

we say that
U(1)_{1}=+\infty
.

Proposition 2.15

Let
U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}
and
V(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}v_{n}x^{n}

be in
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
.
If
V(1)_{1}
exists and

U(x)=V(x)\frac{1}{1-x}
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n}=V(1)_{1}\,.

Proof.
Since
\frac{1}{1-x}=1+x+x^{2}+\dots
, we have
u_{n}=v_{0}+v_{1}+\dots+v_{n}
.

\Box

Quantities
U(1)_{1}
are handled by means of the classical
theorem due to N. Abel, see
[
1
]
and
[
28

II.7]
. We state it for the radius of convergence

.

Theorem 2.16
(Abel’s 1)

Let

U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}\in\mathbb{C}[[x]]
. If
U(1)_{1}
exists then the function

{\textstyle F_{U}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}\colon\{x\in\mathbb{C}\colon\;|x|<1\}\to\mathbb{C}\,,}

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to 1\\

The following folklore result is in fact another variant of Abel’s theorem, incomparable with
Theorem
2.16
. We omit the easy proof.

Theorem 2.17
(Abel’s 2)

Let

U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[[x]]
. If
U(x)
absolutely converges
for every

, then the equality

{\textstyle\lim_{x\to 1}F_{U}(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{n}u_{j}\ \ (\in[0,\,+\infty)\cup\{+\infty\})}

Compared to Theorem
2.16
, the new case is when both limits equal

+\infty

Proposition 2.18

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and

and

exists and

Proof.
Let
U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}

V(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}
and

{\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}u_{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(\alpha v_{j}+\beta w_{j})=\alpha\sum_{j=0}^{n}v_{j}+\beta\sum_{j=0}^{n}w_{j}\,.}

By the limit transition

we get that
that
U(1)_{1}
exists and
U(1)_{1}=\alpha V(1)_{1}+\beta W(1)_{1}
.

\Box

Iterating this result we easily generalize it to

By Propositions

,
2.6

Proposition 2.21

Let

and
V(0)\neq 0

,

and

In

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\lim_{x\to 1}F_{U}(x)=\lim_{x\to 1}\frac{1}{F_{V}(x)}=\frac{1}{\lim_{x\to 1}F_{V}(x)}}

Proposition 2.22

Let
U(x),V(x),W(x)\in\mathbb{C}[[x]]

,

and

{\textstyle\big(U(1)_{1}\big)^{2}=1-\frac{V(1)_{1}}{W(1)_{1}}\,.}

Proof.
In
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
we have the identity

Proposition 2.23

Let
U(x),V(x),W(x)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[[x]]

U(x)

U(1)_{1}=1\,.

Proof.
In
\mathbb{C}[[x]]

.
Using the assumptions and Propositions
2.2
,
2.4

\displaystyle{\textstyle 1-\frac{\lim_{x\to 1}F_{V}(x)}{\lim_{x\to 1}F_{W}(x)}=1-\frac{a}{\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{n}w_{j}}=1-\frac{a}{+\infty}=1}\,.

Proposition 2.24

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and

U(x),V(x),W(x)\in\mathbb{C}[[x]]

U(x)=\alpha V(x)+\beta W(x)
and if
V(1)_{2}
and

exists and

Let
U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}
,
V(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}
and

W(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}

V(1)_{2}
and
W(1)_{2}
are

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}v_{n}
and
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}w_{n}

u_{n}=\alpha v_{n}+\beta w_{n}

2.2
.

\Box

Proposition 2.25

Let
U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}
,
V(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}v_{n}x^{n}
and

be in
\mathbb{C}[[x]]

U(x)=V(x)W(x)
and if

V(1)_{2}
and
W(1)_{2}
exist, then
the following holds.

1.

U(1)_{2}
exists and

U(1)_{2}=V(1)_{2}W(1)_{2}
.

2.

\sum_{n\geq 0}|u_{n}|\leq\sum_{n\geq 0}|v_{n}|\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}|w_{n}|

Proof.
1. We consider the series

{\textstyle S=\sum_{\langle j,\,k\rangle\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}}v_{j}w_{k}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}v_{j}\cdot\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}w_{k}\,.}

By Proposition
2.5
it absolutely converges and has sum
V(1)_{2}W(1)_{2}
. On the other hand,
S
has the grouping

\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{j=0}^{n}v_{j}w_{n-j}

\sum_{j=0}^{n}v_{j}w_{n-j}=u_{n}

By Proposition
2.4

has the sum

Proposition 2.27

Let
U(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}
and
V(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}v_{n}x^{n}
be in
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
and

and

\sum_{n\geq 0}|u_{n}|\leq\frac{1}{1-c}

|V(1)_{2}-1|\leq c<1

V(1)_{2}\neq 0

{\textstyle S=1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{N}\\

For

{\textstyle S_{m}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(v_{1}+v_{2}+\dots)=\sum_{\langle j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{m}\rangle\in\mathbb{N}^{m}}v_{j_{1}}v_{j_{2}}\dots v_{j_{m}}\,.}

On the other hand, in

{\textstyle U(x)=1+\sum_{m\geq 1}(-1)^{m}(v_{1}x+v_{2}x^{2}+\dots)^{m}\,.}

Thus
u_{0}=1
and for every

{\textstyle u_{n}=\sum_{m\geq 1}(-1)^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{m,1},\,j_{m,2},\,\dots,\,j_{m,m}\in\mathbb{N}\\

2.4
,

has also sum

2. Indeed,

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}|u_{k}|=}

\displaystyle{\textstyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big|\sum_{m\geq 1}(-1)^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{m,1},\,j_{m,2},\,\dots,\,j_{m,m}\in\mathbb{N}\\

\displaystyle{\textstyle\leq\sum_{m\geq 0}(|v_{1}|+|v_{2}|+\dots)^{m}=\frac{1}{1-c}\,.}

Lemma 2.28

Let
V(x)\in\mathbb{C}[[x]]
and

V(x)^{2}=tx^{2l}(1+t_{1}x+t_{2}x^{2}+\dots)

where
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
t,t_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
(every square in
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
has
this form). Then there exists
v\in\mathbb{C}
such that
v^{2}=t
and

{\textstyle V(x)=vx^{l}\sum_{n\geq 0}\binom{1/2}{n}(t_{1}x+t_{2}x^{2}+\dots)^{n}\,.}

Proof.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
a,\,b
be formal variables. By the
Vandermonde identity,

{\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{a}{j}\binom{b}{n-j}=\binom{a+b}{n}\ \ (\in\mathbb{Q}[a,\,b])\,.}

Let
V_{0}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\binom{1/2}{n}(t_{1}x+t_{2}x^{2}+\dots)^{n}
. For any
v_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
(v_{0})^{2}=t
the instance
a=b=\frac{1}{2}
of the identity gives

\displaystyle\big(v_{0}x^{l}V_{0}(x)\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle tx^{2l}\sum_{n\geq 0}\big(\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{1/2}{j}\binom{1/2}{n-j}\big)(t_{1}x+t_{2}x^{2}+\dots)^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle tx^{2l}\sum_{n\geq 0}\binom{1}{n}(t_{1}x+t_{2}x^{2}+\dots)^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle tx^{2l}(1+t_{1}x+t_{2}x^{2}+\dots)=V(x)^{2}\,.

Hence
V(x)=\pm v_{0}x^{l}V_{0}(x)
.

\Box

Lemma 2.29

Let
U(x),V(x)\in\mathbb{C}[[x]]
and
U(x)=V(x)^{2}=tx^{2l}(1+t_{1}x+t_{2}x^{2}+\dots)

where
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
t,t_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
. If
c=|t_{1}|+|t_{2}|+\dots<1
,
then
U(1)_{2}
and
V(1)_{2}
exist,
and

Proof.

Proposition 3.1

Let
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
be a light weight and generating functions

,
B_{h}(x)
,

C_{h}(x)
and
D_{h}(x)
be as above. The following relations hold between them.

1.

A_{h}(x)=C_{h}(x)D_{h}(x)
.

2.

B_{h}(x)=\frac{1}{1-C_{h}(x)}
, equivalently,
C_{h}(x)=1-\frac{1}{B_{h}(x)}

Proof.
1. We show that

a_{n}^{h}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}c_{j}^{h}\cdot d_{n-j}^{h}
for every

. For
n\leq 1

it holds,
a_{n}^{h}=c_{n}^{h}=0
. For
n\geq 2
the set

is countable and we split every walk in it at the first revisit of
1
. We get a map

{\textstyle F\colon W(1,\,n)\to\bigcup_{j=0}^{n}W_{c}(j)\times W(n-j)}

that is defined as follows. For any walk

u_{0}=u_{j}=1

u_{i}\neq 1

and

It is easy to see that the map

is a bijection.
F
is
weight-preserving (WP): for
every weight
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
and

.
If
X
is a set of pairs of walks in
K_{\mathbb{N}}
, we write

for the sum of the series

{\textstyle\sum_{\langle w,\,w^{\prime}\rangle\in X}h(w)h(w^{\prime})\,,}

provided that it absolutely converges.

Let
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
be a light weight and
n\geq 2
. Using

and above propositions, we get

\displaystyle a_{n}^{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h(W(1,\,n))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{WP}}}{{=}}h(F[W(1,\,n)])

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}h\big(W_{c}(j)\times W(n-j)\big)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Prop.~\ref{prop_proSer}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}h(W_{c}(j))\cdot h(W(n-j))=\sum_{j=0}^{n}c_{j}^{h}\cdot d_{n-j}^{h}}\,.

{\textstyle B_{h}(x)=\frac{1}{1-C_{h}(x)}=1+\sum_{j\geq 1}(C_{h}(x))^{j}\,.}

b_{0}^{h}=1
and

and show that for every

{\textstyle b_{n}^{h}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\,\dots,\,n_{j}\in\mathbb{N}\\

{\textstyle F\colon W_{b}(n)\to\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\,\dots,\,n_{j}\in\mathbb{N}\\

Proposition 4.1

Let
v\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
,
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
be
a
v
-transitive
light weight and generating functions

A_{h,v}(x)
,
B_{h}(x)
,
B_{h,v}(x)
,
C_{h,v}(x)
,
D_{h}(x)
and
E_{h,v}(x)
be as above. The following relations hold between them.

1.

A_{h,v}(x)=C_{h,v}(x)D_{h}(x)
.

2.

B_{h}(x)=B_{h,v}(x)+C_{h,v}(x)^{2}B_{h}(x)
,
equivalently,

C_{h,v}(x)^{2}=1-\frac{B_{h,v}(x)}{B_{h}(x)}
.

3.

C_{h,v}(x)=B_{h,v}(x)E_{h,v}(x)
.

Proof.
1. Every walk
w
weight-counted by
A_{h,v}(x)
splits at the
first visit of
v
as

w=w_{1}w_{2}
. Walks
w_{1}
are
weight-counted by
C_{h,v}(x)
and
w_{2}
are arbitrary walks starting at
v
. By the
v
-transitivity of
h
, walks
w_{2}
are weight-counted by
D_{h}(x)
.

2. It suffices to prove the first equality.
B_{h}(x)
weight-counts
walks
w
starting and ending at

1
. Those avoiding
v
are weight-counted by
B_{h,v}(x)
. If
w

visits
v
, then it uniquely splits at the first and last visits of
v
as

w=w_{1}w_{2}w_{3}
. Walks
w_{1}

start at
1
, end at

v
and between avoid
v
. Walks
w_{2}
start and end at
v
. Walks

w_{3}
start at
v
, end at
1
and between avoid
v
. We reverse
walks
w_{3}
and see that both
w_{1}
and
w_{3}
are weight-counted by

C_{h,v}(x)
. Due to the
v
-transitivity of
h
, the middle walks
w_{2}
are
weight-counted by
B_{h}(x)
.

3. We consider walks
w
weight-counted by
C_{h,v}(x)
. They
start at
1
, end at
v
, and between avoid
v
. These walks
uniquely split at the last visit of
1
as
w=w_{1}w_{2}
. Walks
w_{1}
are weight-counted by

B_{h,v}(x)
, and
w_{2}
by

E_{h,v}(x)
.

\Box

Let
h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
and
v\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}

then

a_{n}^{h,v}=\sum_{w\in W(v,n)}h(w)=\sum_{w\in W(v,n)}0=0

for every

because every walk starting at

and visiting

contains an edge with zero weight. Henceforth, we therefore

For

it equals
\{0\}
. Else it has two non-zero elements differing by sign. If

.

5
Concluding remarks

In
[
17
]
we consider the same problem of determination
of formulas for the quantities

{\textstyle A_{h,v}(1)=\sum_{n\geq 0}h(W(v,\,n))\,\text{ and }\,\lim_{n\to\infty}h(W(v,\,n))}

for formal weights
h
. These are maps

h\colon\mathbb{N}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}[[x_{1},\,x_{2},\,\dots,\,x_{k}]]\,,

where the last integral domain of formal power series in
k
variables is endowed with the usual non-Archimedean norm.

The main novelty of our generalization is this. In an equality like

P_{d}(\overline{v},\,n)=h(W(v,\,n))

in the Introduction, the left-hand side involves, in the
standard rigorous probabilistic treatment, an
implicit probability space supporting the corresponding
Markov chain. Construction of this probability space is far from
trivial; see, for example,
[
6
]
. In our approach, the
right-hand side is completely self-contained.
