Examining valproate’s potential anticancer effect among patients with bipolar disorder: a territory-wide active-comparator new user study spanning two decades
Authors: Cuiling Wei, Vanessa Wai Sei Ng, Yue Wei, Joe Kwun Nam Chan, Yuqi Hu, Wenlong Liu, Lingyue Zhou, Boyan Liu, Qi Sun, Wenxin Tian, Song Song, Wing Chung Chang, Esther Wai Yin Chan, Francisco Tsz Tsun Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsPreclinical studies have suggested potential anticancer effects of valproate, but clinical evidence is limited and inconclusive. This study aims to reexamine the relationship between valproate use and cancer incidence, using lithium as an active comparator in a cohort of patients with bipolar disorder.MethodsA retrospective, active-comparator, new-user cohort study was conducted using a territory-wide public healthcare database in Hong Kong. Patients diagnosed with bipolar disorder between 2003 and 2023 and initiating monotherapy of either lithium or valproate were included. Those with prior cancer diagnoses or concurrent use of other mood stabilizers were excluded. Cancer incidence was the primary outcome, analyzed using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and modified Poisson regression.ResultsThe study included 5,875 valproate users and 1,439 lithium users. After a median follow-up of 2.96 years, 126 cases of cancer were observed (16 in lithium users and 110 in valproate users). There was no significant difference between valproate and lithium use among patients with bipolar disorder, with an adjusted incidence rate ratio (aIRR) of 1.13 (95% CI 0.66–1.91) for valproate compared to lithium. Results were consistent across subgroup and sensitivity analysis.ConclusionsOur findings do not suggest an anticancer effect from valproate but highlight the importance of weighing the other distinct safety and tolerability profiles of valproate and lithium against individual clinical presentations to ensure optimal and individualized care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07622-5.Keywords: Bipolar disorder, Valproate, Lithium, Anticancer

Introduction

There are both in-vivo and in-vitro preclinical studies suggesting potential anticancer effects of valproate in malignant hematological diseases and solid tumors (e.g., glioma, colon) [1, 2]. These effects may be largely attributable to valproate’s activity as a class I and IIa histone deacetylases (HDACs) inhibitor, a role that triggers epigenetic alterations in gene expression conductive to tumor suppression [3]. These alterations include hyperacetylation of histones H3 and H4 and DNA demethylation of tumor-suppressor genes such as p21 [4]. Beyond HDAC inhabitation, valproate exerts multiple synergistic anticancer mechanisms including the inhibition of angiogenesis through decreased endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) expression [5], and cell type-specific modulation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway at the level of rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma (Raf) to regulate extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (Erk1/2) phosphorylation, cell growth and motility [6]. Additionally, a growing body of phase 1/2 clinical trials have demonstrated a synergistic effect showing that valproate in combination with anticancer pharmacotherapies improves clinical outcomes [7–9]. However, despite these promising preclinical and early-phase clinical findings, larger population-based observational studies have yielded inconsistent or null results regarding valproate’s overall anticancer efficacy. There was a nationwide case-control study of any cancer incidence in Denmark comparing long-term valproate users and never-users registered in the national registry, but it did not identify a significant protective effect as hypothesized [10]. A more recent Swedish case-control study, assessed the association between prostate cancer and multiple antiepileptic drugs (including valproate) by comparing users with non-users rather than making direct comparisons between specific drugs [11]. They observed a weak crude inverse association of valproate with prostate cancer, but it became non-significant after adjusting for healthcare utilization. Indeed, the absence of a protective effect in both Danish and Swedish studies may potentially be due to a lack of a good drug comparator without a strong indication bias [12]. The active-comparator designs have gained traction in observational studies as a robust way to reduce the indication bias [13].

In this study, we limited our population to those living with bipolar disorder and chose lithium as the active comparator to valproate due to their comparable clinical relevance in the management of bipolar disorder. On the one hand, patients with bipolar disorder represent a clinically homogeneous group with a chronic psychiatric condition requiring long-term pharmacological intervention, thus providing a fairer comparison between active treatments. On the other hand, lithium is the first-line long-term pharmacological treatment for bipolar disorder and valproate is recommended as an alternative when lithium is ineffective [14]. While the meta-analysis of five observational studies by Anmella et al. showed a non-significant decreased cancer risk among lithium user with bipolar disorder, lithium’s potential anticancer mechanisms are increasingly recognized [15]. These mechanisms include the inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK-3β), induction of autophagy, modulation of oncogenic signaling pathways like Wnt/β-catenin and NF-κB, and suppression of tumor growth, metastasis, and cell cycle progression [16, 17]. By using lithium as an active comparator, we aimed to enhance the methodological rigor of our study and provide real-world evidence on the potential anticancer properties of valproate. We hypothesize that valproate is associated with significantly lower risk of cancer compared to lithium in people with bipolar disorder.

Method

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective, active-comparator, new-user cohort study using the clinical records of a territory-wide database including patients attending public healthcare facilities, which were maintained in the Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System (CDARS) of the Hospital Authority (HA) in Hong Kong. The HA is the sole provider of public inpatient services and a major provider of public outpatient services in Hong Kong, covering more than 80% of all healthcare service users in the city [18]. The vast majority of patients with chronic diseases are regularly followed up in HA facilities since these conditions are highly subsidized. This database has been used for numerous excellent large-scale epidemiologic studies previously [19, 20].

Cohort selection

Individuals who were diagnosed with bipolar disorder between 2003 and 2023 were identified, by using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revisions, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) codes in Table S1. From this population, we identified new users of lithium or valproate therapy after 2003 by excluding those who had initiated these treatments during the wash-out period from 2001 to 2002. Patients with a diagnosis of bipolar disorder within one year after initiating lithium or valproate were also included in the cohort to avoid the omission of people with missing initial diagnosis, which is not uncommon in the mental health care setting [21]. Moreover, participants were excluded if they initiated lithium and valproate concurrently, lacked valid birth dates, were aged below 18 at treatment initiation, had prior use of other mood stabilizers (i.e., lamotrigine and carbamazepine) [22], had a cancer diagnosis before treatment initiation, or had an observational period of less than 365 days.

Follow-up and outcomes

The index date was defined by the date of the first dispensing record of lithium or valproate. Eligible participants were followed up since index date until the occurrence of the outcome of interest, death, end of data availability (December 31, 2023), treatment switch or discontinuation (i.e., a gap of 90 days without a prescription refill), whichever was earlier.

Our primary outcome was a composite binary indicator of cancer occurrence, i.e., neoplasm malignancy. The secondary outcomes were the major cancer sites, including breast and genital organs. To eliminate the possibility of reverse causality, the lag time was set to two years, and cancer diagnosed within that period after drug initiation was censored. Additionally, we used appendicitis as a negative control outcome to assess potential uncontrolled confounding. In the absence of confounding, the analysis of this outcome should produce a non-significant result [23]. Furthermore, we examined a range of other safety and effectiveness outcomes against existing findings from the existing literature [24], including chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury, hypothyroidism and self-harm, to ensure any bias in our cohort selection can be detected. The ICD-9-CM codes for identifying these conditions were listed in Table S1. Those who experienced those health outcomes before lithium or valproate initiation were excluded. Lastly, we also examined treatment failure, a composite outcome of high clinical relevance in maintenance treatment for severe mental illness, consisting of treatment switch, treatment discontinuation, and psychiatric rehospitalization [25]. While our primary focus was on comparing the anticancer effects of lithium and valproate, evaluating their safety and adherence was equally important, as the triple assessment were crucial for clinical decision on long-term maintenance therapy in bipolar disorder.

For the negative control outcome and safety outcomes, we repeated the analysis using the same study design settings as for cancer (e.g., new-user design, same follow-up rules, covariate adjustment and statistical analysis), while excluding individuals with each condition prior to cohort entry to focus on incident events. For treatment failure, we applied the exact same design as the cancer analysis, since it directly measures treatment persistence critical to assessing cancer risk.

Covariates

We included several pretreatment variables as covariates in our study to ensure a comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders, including sex, age, pretreatment chronic conditions and medication history. We calculated the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score at baseline to account for a broad range of chronic conditions, including physical, immune, and dementia-related diseases [26]. The CCI calculation did not include malignancy because we have excluded those who were diagnosed with cancer at baseline. Besides, when examining chronic kidney disease as the safety outcome, the CCI calculation excluded corresponding terms in the formula. Depression, alcohol misuse, and substance abuse [27] were treated as separate binary variables based on the diagnosis records within one year before study drug initiation. Depression was included as a covariate since bipolar disorder frequently presents initially with depressive episodes that are often misdiagnosed as unipolar depression, and the presence of depression could influence both treatment selection and cancer risk as a potential confounder [28]. Besides, the inclusion of alcohol misuse and substance abuse as covariates was based on studies indicating that low-dose lithium or valproate is often prescribed for patients with these conditions, who are also at a higher risk of developing bipolar disorder and cancer [29, 30]. Additionally, we considered the use of antidepressants and antipsychotic (haloperidol, chlorpromazine, olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone) medications at the baseline or within one year prior to drug initiation as covariates.

Statistical analysis

Incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using modified Poisson regression with sandwich estimation for binary outcome [31]. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was used to balance between the two arms in terms of the baseline characteristics. We calculated the standard mean difference (SMD) for continuous variables and the proportion difference for dichotomous variables before and after weighing to examine the balance between the user groups. Subgroup analyses were performed separately by sex and age group, i.e., < 65 or ≥ 65 years, baseline chronic conditions, and antidepressant and antipsychotic history. Covariates were reweighed in every subgroup analysis.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted three sensitivity analyses to test for the robustness of our results. First, we repeated the main analysis by removing depression as a covariate and excluding those who were diagnosed with depression at baseline. Second, our active-comparator observational study closely resembled a head-to-head randomized controlled trial [32]. Therefore, we also analyzed the intention-to-treat (ITT) dataset, in which participants would not be censored for drug discontinuation and switch. Third, we addressed the competing risk of death by using both univariable and multivariable time-varying Cox regression model with age as the time-dependent variable.

All analyses were carried out using SAS version 9.4 and R software version 4.4.1. The 95% CI that did not overlap one was taken as significant in this study.

Results

Figure 1 shows the procedures of cohort selection. After excluding participants who met exclusion criteria such as initiating lithium and valproate at the same time, being aged 18 years or below at lithium or valproate initiation, receiving other mood stabilizers (i.e., lamotrigine and carbamazepine) prior to or on lithium or valproate initiation, being diagnosed with cancer prior to or on lithium or valproate initiation, having an observational period of less than 365 days, lacking valid prescription records or switching drug, we eventually identified 1,439 lithium new users and 5,875 valproate new users from 2003 to 2023.

Cohort characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table 1. The pre-IPTW SMD for age exceeded 0.1, signifying a notable imbalance, although other baseline characteristics showed no significant disparities with SMDs all below 0.1. The mean age was 37.5 years [Standard Deviation (SD) 13.2] for the lithium group and 41.6 years (SD 15.3) for the valproate group. The proportion of females was 59.1% in the lithium group and 57.1% in the valproate group. Antipsychotic use was recorded in 702 (48.8%) lithium users and 2,692 (45.8%) valproate users. Weighted analysis of cancer incidence

The median follow-up time differed between the treatment groups, with lithium users having a shorter median follow-up of 1.88 years [interquartile range (IQR) 0.37 to 5.63] compared to valproate users, whose median follow-up was 3.37 years (IQR 0.59 to 9.34). During an overall median follow-up of 2.96 (IQR 0.53 to 8.75; mean 5.23) years, the primary outcome, cancer incidence, occurred in 126 participants, of which 16 cases were observed in lithium users and 110 in valproate users. The crude cancer incidence rate per 1,000 person-years was 2.72 (95%CI 1.67 to 4.44) in the lithium group and 3.40 (95%CI 2.82 to 4.10) in the valproate group. As shown in Fig. 2, the overall trend after adjustment indicated that there was no significant overall difference in the cumulative incidence of cancer between lithium and valproate new users throughout the follow-up period. In the first 4,900 days of follow-up, the cumulative risk of cancer between the two groups were similar. After 4,900 days, the cumulative incidence rate of cancer grew more slowly among valproate users compared to lithium users. The cumulative cancer incidence plot showed a similar trend in Figure S1.

Inverse-probability-of treatment weighted cumulative cancer incidence for lithium and valproate new users

As detailed in Fig. 3, after applying IPTW, the adjusted IRR was 1.13 (95% CI 0.66 to 1.91) for valproate new users compared to lithium new users, indicating no significant difference in cancer risk between the two groups. Subgroup analysis by sex showed no significant difference in cancer incidence for women (aIRR 1.08, 95% CI 0.53 to 2.17) or men (aIRR 1.22, 95% CI 0.63 to 2.68) when comparing valproate users to lithium users. Age-stratified analysis indicated similar results, with no significant difference in cancer incidence between valproate users and lithium users for those younger than 65 (aIRR 1.04, 95% CI 0.61 to 1.76). Participants with a CCI of 0 had an aIRR of 1.15 (95% CI 0.66 to 2.02) and those with a CCI of ≥ 1 had an aIRR of 0.86 (95% CI 0.19 to 3.87), showing no significant differences in cancer risk regardless of baseline comorbidity condition when comparing valproate users to lithium users. For those with no depression at baseline, the aIRR was 0.90 (95% CI 0.50 to 1.59), indicating a non-significant lower risk of cancer among valproate users compared to lithium users. Further stratified analysis based on the medication history: the aIRR for those with a history of antidepressants was 2.68 (95% CI 0.82 to 8.80), and the aIRR for those without a history of antidepressants was 0.70 (95% CI 0.38 to 1.28). For antipsychotics users, the aIRR was 0.79 (95% CI 0.41 to 1.53), showing no significant difference when comparing valproate users to lithium users.

Valproate use was associated with a lower risk of breast cancer (aIRR 0.66, 95% CI 0.24 to 1.78), although the result was not statistically significant.

There was no significant difference between the groups in crude or adjusted results of the negative control outcome, appendicitis (IRR 1.46, 95% CI 0.34 to 6.33; aIRR 1.25, 95% CI 0.29 to 5.44). There were no significant differences between the valproate and lithium groups for chronic kidney disease (IRR 1.32, 95% CI 0.52 to 3.33; aIRR 1.17, 95% CI 0.46 to 3.00), acute kidney injury (IRR 1.14, 95% CI 0.54 to 2.40; aIRR 0.87, 95% CI 0.41 to 1.86), hypothyroidism (IRR 0.83, 95% CI 0.50 to 1.39; aIRR 0.84, 95% CI 0.50 to 1.41), or self-harm (IRR 0.76, 95% CI 0.55 to 1.05; aIRR 0.78, 95% CI 0.56 to 1.09).

The results in Table 3a broke down the different endpoints contributing to treatment failure between the lithium and valproate groups. The overall rate of treatment failure was higher in the lithium group (84.13%) compared to the valproate group (76.94%). Notably, a greater proportion of lithium users experienced treatment switch (24.59% vs. 13.56%). The valproate group had higher rates of treatment discontinuation (25.00% vs. 23.97%) and psychiatric readmission (38.39% vs. 35.58%). Overall, the valproate group had a significantly lower rate of treatment failure compared to the lithium group, with an adjusted IRR of 0.79 (95% CI 0.72 to 0.88). Similar reductions in risk were observed among participants regardless of depression and anti-depressant history at baseline. Sensitivity analysis

When depression was not included as a covariate and participants with baseline depression were excluded (Figure S2), the overall association between valproate or lithium use and cancer risk remained consistent with the primary findings, with no significant difference observed (aIRR 0.90, 95% CI 0.50 to 1.59). Furthermore, subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age, comorbidity index, and medication history aligned with the main findings, revealing no significant differences in cancer risk between the two treatment groups.

In the ITT analysis (Figure S3), in which participants were followed up regardless of drug discontinuation or switching, the overall cancer incidence between valproate and lithium users remained consistent with the primary analysis, showing no significant difference (aIRR 0.77, 95% CI 0.56 to 1.05). Subgroup results generally aligned with the primary findings, with no significant differences observed in females, those aged 18 or older, and participants with a CCI equal or greater than one, or those using antipsychotics. However, among males, valproate users had a significantly lower cancer risk compared to lithium users (aIRR 0.56, 95% CI 0.35 to 0.89), and among participants without depression or without prior antidepressant use, valproate was associated with a significantly reduced cancer risk (aIRR 0.67, 95% CI 0.47 to 0.94 and 0.53, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.77, respectively).

In the competing risks analysis using the time-varying Cox regression model, accounting for death as a competing event, the overall crude and adjusted hazard ratio (HR) for cancer risk in valproate users compared to lithium users were 1.72 (95% CI 1.16 to 2.53) and 1.49 (95%CI 1.00 to 2.22), respectively. However, the adjusted HR included a confidence interval that bordered on statistical significance, indicating some level of uncertainty.

Discussion

In this study, we compared the incidence rates of cancer between new users of valproate and lithium in patients with bipolar disorder. We found no significant difference in overall cancer rates between the two groups after adjusting for baseline characteristics. For secondary outcomes, including specific cancer types, the results remained consistent with no significant differences between valproate and lithium users. Additionally, we repeated separate analyses for the incidence of other safety outcomes which are strongly correlated with continued drug use, including chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury and hypothyroidism, with patients with each respective condition prior to initiating valproate or lithium excluded. These analyses showed no significant differences between valproate and lithium users, which are largely consistent with existing evidence [24], lending further support to the validity of our cohort selection.

One potential explanation for the null results in difference in cancer risk observed between valproate and lithium users was the overlapping mechanisms of action that may reduce the likelihood of significant differences in their anticancer effects. Similar impacts on tumor suppression, apoptosis, and cellular proliferation, with lithium acting through GSK-3β inhibition and valproate through HDAC inhibition, may result in similar oncological outcomes. Given that Anmella et al. found lithium to be potentially protective against certain cancers compared to non-users [15], our observation of no significant difference in cancer risk between lithium and valproate users raises the possibility that valproate may also exhibit an anticancer effect, though this cannot be confirmed without comparing both drugs to a non-user group. However, recent research has emphasized the potential of lithium and valproate for drug repositioning in oncology, particularly for brain tumors [33]. Furthermore, a comprehensive interpretation of our findings necessitates considering the underlying epidemiological profile of cancer risk in the bipolar disorder population. It is well established that patients with bipolar disorder exhibit a substantially elevated baseline risk for various malignancies relative to the general population. Meta-analyses have quantified this overall increase in cancer risk at approximately 24% (RR 1.24, 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.46) for those with bipolar disorder, with a particularly heightened risk for breast cancer in women (RR 1.33, 95% CI: 1.15 to 1.55) [15]. In this context, we observed the cumulative cancer incidence rates of 1.1% among lithium users and 1.9% among valproate users over a 20-year observational period. Notably, although population-based absolute cancer incidence rates (expressed as cases per person-time) remain limited in the meta-analysis of Anmella et al., extant observational studies offering cumulative incidence data provide valuable qualitative insights. For example, Martinsson et al. reported a cumulative incidence of 6.0% in bipolar patients compared with 6.4% in the general population over 18.5 years [34], whereas Kahan et al. observed 3.3% versus 2.8% over 13 years [35]. These observations suggest that both valproate and lithium may effectively attenuate, or at minimum stabilize, the inherent heightened cancer susceptibility in the population with bipolar disorder. A similar situation exists for breast cancer, where Martinsson et al. found no significant difference in risk between lithium-treated and non-treated bipolar patients [34], while there is limited evidence for differential effects on other site-specific cancers. Consequently, although our study identified no differential impact on cancer risk between valproate and lithium, it raises the possibility that both agents could play a role in moderating the elevated cancer risk relative to an untreated bipolar state, potentially aligning their overall influence on cancer incidence.

Our study findings were consistent with existing evidence on cancer risk among patients with bipolar disorder treated with valproate and lithium. Territory-wide studies conducted in both Taiwan and Denmark reported no significant overall difference in cancer risk between valproate and lithium users [24, 36]. Lin et al. found no significant overall cancer risk between the two drugs but observed an increased risk of genitourinary cancer in valproate users, particularly among those with treatment durations of less than one year. This study had a relatively smaller sample size, i.e. 3,416 valproate users and 925 lithium users, and relied on a claims-based database instead of direct extraction of electronic health records. They also did not account for important baseline factors such as the use of antidepressants and antipsychotics. The Danish study, while also exploring cancer subtypes, extensively examined a broader range of physical diseases, including cardiovascular and metabolic conditions. Nevertheless, it did not adjust for baseline comorbidity status when evaluating cancer risks and, like the study from Taiwan, did not account for the cancer latent period, potentially leading to reverse causality where early undiagnosed cancers might have influenced medication use rather than being a consequence of it. This limitation could have led to a misidentification of more cancer cases, as some undiagnosed cancers present before treatment initiation might have been incorrectly classified as new cases. In contrast, our study addressed these limitations by incorporating a two-year lag period to minimize reverse causality and adjusting for baseline use of antidepressants and antipsychotics, strengthening the validity of our findings.

Beyond cancer risk, our study found no significant difference in the risk of self-harm between valproate and lithium users. This finding contrasted the results of two U.S. studies reporting a lower risk of self-harm with valproate users [37, 38], as well as studies from Sweden [39] and the UK [40] which found a higher risk compared to lithium users. These discrepancies may stem from differences in the operationalizations of self-harm used in these studies, e.g. variations in the diagnosis codes used to identify self-harm or whether suicide attempts were included. The treatment failure analysis, however, indicated a lower rate of treatment failure in the valproate group compared to the lithium group, consistent across most subgroups. Lithium’s higher treatment failure likely contributed to the shorter follow-up time observed for lithium users in the main analysis, as treatment discontinuation, switching, or psychiatric rehospitalization would have ended follow-up earlier.

Given this elevated cancer risk [15], proactive monitoring and prevention strategies are essential in the management of bipolar disorders. Clinicians should consider regular cancer screenings, especially for high-risk groups such as women. Medication selection should also balance effectiveness with long-term safety profiles. While international guidelines recommend mood stabilizers including lithium and valproate, and antipsychotics for long-term management of bipolar disorder [41], actual prescription practices differed [42]. In Hong Kong, antipsychotics were the most frequently prescribed medications, followed by mood stabilizers like valproate and lithium [22]. Our study, which found no significant difference in overall cancer risk or other safety outcomes between valproate and lithium users based on a reasonably large cohort, contributes evidence to guide their use. However, it is established that valproate use carries substantial teratogenic risks that must be carefully considered in clinical decisions. For women, exposure to valproate during pregnancy is linked to a significant risk of physical birth defects and neurodevelopmental disorders in children, which can result in long-term disabilities [43]. Besides, there is emerging evidence linking paternal use of valproate around conception to a slight increase in the risk of neurodevelopmental disorders in their offspring [44]. Lithium users were observed to have a higher rate of treatment failure as defined in our study compared to valproate users, which may be due to more stringent monitoring requirements [45] (e.g., serum lithium levels, kidney function) or a narrow therapeutic index [46]. It is, nevertheless, important to note that lithium demonstrated very good efficacy in reducing suicide risk [47] and clear benefits for specific subtypes of bipolar disorder [48], which represent unique advantages over other treatment options.

One of the key strengths of this study was the use of a large, real-world cohort from a territory-wide database, which allowed for greater generalization of our results to a broad population of patients with bipolar disorder, in addition to our study design’s consideration of cancer latency as mentioned in the previous paragraph. The active comparator design, with lithium as the control group, effectively reduced indication bias, enabling a more accurate assessment of cancer risk between valproate and lithium users [12]. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the small sample size in certain subgroups, particularly those with multimorbidity or older age, combined with the relatively low incidence of certain cancer subtypes, may have led to underpowered models and constrained our ability to detect significant differences in these populations and in rare cancers. Second, we acknowledge the potential for selection bias in our primary analysis due to censoring participants at treatment discontinuation or switching. This approach may have disproportionately right-censored lithium users, who had higher treatment failure rates, potentially masking potential protection from valproate observed in the ITT analysis among subgroups like men and those without depression or prior antidepressant use. Third, the dual health system in Hong Kong, comprising public and private sectors, may lead to delays or missing cancer records in CDARS, as patients might initially seek diagnosis or treatment in the private sector before transitioning to public hospitals. However, as public hospitals diagnose and treat around 90% of new cancer cases [49], with the private sector playing a largely supplementary role, most cancer patients who opted for care in the private sector eventually receive care in both systems [18]. Thus, their cases are ultimately captured in the database, though with a potential delay. The delay is unlikely to influence the overall cancer incidence rate, as the total number of cancer cases remains accounted for over time. Moreover, since our study employed the incidence rate ratio to compare cancer risk between lithium and valproate user, any delay affects both exposure groups at a similar extent, resulting in non-differential misclassification that minimally impacts our relative risk estimates. Fourth, the absence of serum therapeutic drug monitoring levels for lithium and valproate precluded evaluation of dose-response relationships pertinent to any putative anticancer effects. Nevertheless, this limitation is unlikely to substantially affect our primary results, as both treatment groups were drawn from the same real-world clinical database where standard monitoring protocols are routinely applied to maintain therapeutic ranges, thereby reflecting practical clinical outcomes rather than controlled experimental conditions. Fifth, like other observational studies, residual confounding could not be entirely excluded despite our comprehensive adjustment for known covariates. Specifically, we were unable to account for unmeasured factors such as lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, diet), genetic predispositions and family history of cancer, which may still have influenced cancer risk. The absence of these factors is a limitation, as incorporating them in future studies could provide a more accurate understanding of the role of mood stabilizers in cancer prevention.

In conclusion, this study found no significant differences in overall or site-specific cancer incidence rates between new users of valproate and lithium among patients with bipolar disorder, suggesting potential anticancer effects for both drugs in this high-risk population. The clinical decision between them should be guided by their known side effects, such as valproate’s teratogenic effect and lithium’s impact on renal function, with individualized treatment plans tailored to patient-specific factors and needs.
