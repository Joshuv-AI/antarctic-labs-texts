The detection and utilization of volatile metabolomics in Klebsiella pneumoniae by gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry
Authors: Fuxing Li, Shumin Gu, Chuwen Zhao, Yunwei Zheng, Junqi Zhu, Longhua Hu, Yaping Hang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This research aimed to analyze the volatile compounds emitted during the proliferation of Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) in the laboratory setting using gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) and to investigate the potential of volatile metabolomics for detecting carbapenemase-producing strains of K. pneumoniae. The volatile metabolomics of K. pneumoniae were comprehensively analyzed using GC-IMS in tryptic soy broth (TSB) as the culture medium. Afterward, the growth stabilization period (T2) served as the primary time point for analysis, with the introduction of imipenem and carbapenemase inhibitors (avibactam sodium or EDTA) during the exponential growth phase (T0) to further investigate alterations in volatile molecules associated with K. pneumoniae. Standard strains were utilized as references, while clinical strains were employed for validation purposes. At T2, a total of 22 volatile organic compounds (VOCs) associated with K. pneumoniae were identified (3 VOCs found in both monomer and dimer forms). Significant differences in VOCs were observed between carbapenemase-negative and carbapenemase-positive strains, both standard and clinical, following the introduction of imipenem. Furthermore, the addition of avibactam sodium led to distinct changes in the VOC content of strains producing class A carbapenemase, while the addition of EDTA resulted in specific alterations in the volatile metabolic profiles of strains producing class B carbapenemase. GC-IMS demonstrated significant promise for analyzing bacterial volatile metabolomics, and its application in evaluating the volatolomics of K. pneumoniae may facilitate the timely detection of carbapenemase-producing strains.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77746-3.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), a member of the Enterobacteriaceae family, commonly colonizes the human intestinal and respiratory tracts, leading to a diverse array of infections in individuals1. These infections encompass respiratory2, urinary3, neurological4, intra-abdominal5, and bloodstream infections6, among others, making it a significant pathogen responsible for hospital-acquired infections. Notably, the excessive use of antibiotics in the clinical setting has led to a rise in the resistance of K. pneumoniae to conventional antibiotics7, of particular concern is the emergence of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP), which poses a great challenge to public health due to its limited treatment options and elevated rates of morbidity and mortality8,9. Meanwhile, the majority of recent studies have focused on the epidemiological and molecular characteristics of K. pneumoniae infections10–12, with a limited number of studies comprehensively exploring the metabolic processes of K. pneumoniae. Indeed, prior research has demonstrated similarities in the metabolism of bacteria between in vitro growth and in vivo infections13,14. Thus, a comprehensive analysis of the volatile metabolomics of K. pneumoniae, along with the identification of specific metabolic molecules, can aid in the detection of infections caused by this bacterium and contribute to the non-invasive diagnosis of bacterial infections.

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are characterized by high vapor pressures and low boiling points15. VOCs originating from human metabolic processes are excreted through various bodily excretions, including the skin, feces, urine, and exhaled breath. Interestingly, disease-induced alteration in cellular metabolism can lead to changes in VOC profiles, making them potential biomarkers for specific pathophysiological conditions16. At present, numerous volatile biomarkers linked to a range of diseases and disorders have been identified and categorized from multiple tissue sources through the non-invasive detection of VOCs emitted by patients (Volatolomics), which has the potential to emerge as a new direction for future diagnostics17. Moreover, given the increasing application of volatile metabolomics, numerous studies have been undertaken to investigate microbial volatile organic compounds (mVOCs)18–22. These studies have consistently demonstrated that various bacterial species are capable of synthesizing distinct mVOCs, enabling the timely detection of pathogenic bacteria. For instance, a prior study demonstrated that cultures of K. pneumoniae generated 3-hydroxy-2-butanone and 1-hydroxy-2-propanone23, offering valuable insights into the early identification of K. pneumoniae infection.

Presently, various detection techniques have been developed for VOCs, such as the electronic nose24, high field asymmetric ion mobility spectrometry (FAIMS)25, multi-bundle capillary column-ion mobility spectrometry (MCC-IMS)26, gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS)27, etc. However, besides the GC-MS technique27–29, a limited number of assays have been employed to analyze the volatile metabolomics of K. pneumoniae. Gas chromatograph-ion mobility spectroscopy (IMS), a novel detection method for VOCs, has the following distinct advantages: (1) As opposed to MS, IMS operates without the need for high vacuum conditions, resulting in lower costs, smaller equipment size, and a simpler analytical process20; (2) This methodology integrates the superior separation capacity of GC with the high resolution, sensitivity, and accuracy of ion mobility spectrometry IMS30; (3) The GC-IMS software enables the visualization of volatile substance profiles in samples based on their composition, facilitating intuitive categorization, comparison, and sample analysis31. In addition, existing research13,32,33 on the volatile metabolomics of K. pneumoniae primarily focused on strain identification, with limited attention given to the exploration of volatile metabolomics further for identifying CRKP.

Based on the aforementioned background, this research aimed to utilize GC-IMS to comprehensively analyze the volatile metabolomics of K. pneumoniae and the potential application of volatile metabolomics in identifying CRKP through the use of standard strains as references and clinical strains for validation.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experiments were performed in accordance with the guideline “Biomedical research ethics review method involving people” of China and were approved by the medical research ethics committee at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. Informed consents were obtained from the participants.

Sources of the samples

Carbapenemase-negative (ref. No. ATCC BAA-1706) and carbapenemase-positive K. pneumoniae strains [ref. No. ATCC BAA-1705 (Klebsiella pneumoniae-carbapenemases (KPC)-positive), 2146 (New delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM)-positive), 2524 (Oxacillinase-48 (OXA-48)-carbapenemase-positive)] were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Clinical isolates were sourced from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University from January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2022, and are presently being preserved within the research team’s strain repository (29), comprising 20 carbapenem-sensitive strains (CSKP) and 44 carbapenem-resistant strains (CRKP). Afterward, the 44 CRKP strains were categorized into subgroups based on their carbapenemase types: KPC-positive (n = 20), NDM-positive (n = 15), Imipenemase metallo-β-lactamase (IMP)-positive (n = 4), and carbapenemase-negative CRKP (n = 5). Prior to conducting experiments, the aforestated strains were preserved in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB, Solarbio, China) supplemented with 15% glycerol and stored at a temperature of -80 °C. Meanwhile, in vitro antimicrobial susceptibility testing was re-performed on all strains using the VITEK2 system (Bio-Merieux, Inc., France) or Kirby-Bauer (KB) test. The identification of CRKP aligned with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s (CDC) definition for carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE)34, which includes resistance to one or more carbapenem antibiotics (imipenem, meropenem, ertapenem, or doripenem) or the production of carbapenemase. Carbapenemases were identified and characterized utilizing the modified carbapenemase inactivation method (mCIM) and the EDTA-modified carbapenemase inactivation method (eCIM), as described by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) in 202235. Validation was conducted through multiplexed polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detection of carbapenemase-encoding carbapenem-type resistance genes. Moreover, all experimental strains underwent testing for carbapenemase-related genes using macrogenomic next-generation sequencing [mNGS, Qiantang Life Science Technology Co. Ltd. (Suzhou, China)], resulting in the identification of 5 carbapenemase-negative CRKP strains that did not harbor carbapenemase genes. The information of clinical CRKP strains was listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Bacterial culture and sample preparation

Experimental strains were cultured on Columbia blood agar plates and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Following this, the bacterial suspensions were transferred to test tubes containing culture medium (total volume: 6 mL; microbial concentration: 107 colony forming units (CFU)/mL; culture medium: TSB; temperature: 37 oC; agitation: 200 rpm). The growth curves of K. pneumoniae in TSB were depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1 (one clinical strain out of 64 clinical strains was selected randomly, all experimental strains were replicated six times using the same technique). As anticipated, all strains exhibited consistent growth patterns, with K. pneumoniae entering the exponential growth phase at approximately 3 h and reaching the growth stabilization phase at around 5 h.

Subsequently, based on the results of the growth curves, the metabolic kinetics of K. pneumoniae-associated VOCs were assessed using GC-IMS at hourly intervals until 7 h of incubation (T4), using the entry of K. pneumoniae into the exponential growth phase (3 h of incubation) as the initial time of detection (T0). To identify the optimal analytical time nodes for subsequent analyses, a blank sterile medium was utilized as a control. To further explore the impact of imipenem (IPM) on the volatile metabolomics of K. pneumoniae, a solution containing IPM (Solarbio, China) was prepared and introduced into the test medium at the initial time point (T0, final concentration: 0.25 mg/mL; considering the inability of carbapenemase-negative CRKP clinical strains to tolerate high concentrations of IPM, the final concentration of IPM was 16 µg/mL). Afterward, alterations in volatile compounds linked to K. pneumoniae were evaluated over the time span ranging from T0 to T4 (incubation for 3 h to 7 h). Similarly, following the identification of the optimal time point for analysis, the impact of carbapenemase inhibitors, such as avibactam sodium (Solarbio, China, final concentration: 1 mg/L) and EDTA (Solarbio, China, final concentration: 0.5 mmol/L), on the volatile metabolomics of K. pneumoniae was examined (addition at T0 together with IPM). The growth curves of the standard strains of K. pneumoniae under the above three different treatments were shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Throughout the experiments, no additional processing required, a total of 500 mL of bacterial culture fluid for each tested sample was used for GC-IMS analysis. All experiments using standard strains were replicated six times, while experiments using clinical strains were performed in triplicates. Headspace sampling and GC-IMS measurements

In the present study, VOCs from samples were measured using FlavourSpec® (G.A.S., Dortmund, Germany) GC-IMS instrument based on the principles of Gas Chromatograph-Ion Mobility Spectrometry (GC-IMS)36. The detailed detection principle of GC-IMS was as follows: High-purity nitrogen gas (99.99% purity) was used as the carrier gas in the gas chromatography (GC) process. The headspace gas from the sample being analyzed was extracted by a sampling device (G.A.S., Dortmund, Germany), and a specific volume was subsequently injected into the injection port. Following this, the sample gas was transported by the carrier gas to the ionization reaction zone, wherein both carrier gas molecules and sample molecules underwent a sequence of ionization reactions facilitated by the ion source (tritium source; average radiation energy: 5.68 keV), resulting in the generation of multiple product ions. Under the influence of an electric field, ions traversed an intermittently opened ion gate into the drift region, where they collided with counter-flowing neutral drift gas molecules, leading to energy loss and the acquisition of unique migration rates. Consequently, ions of varying types arrived at the Faraday plate at different intervals, facilitating their separation based on arrival times and enabling the detection of the sample20.

Tested samples (500 µL) were introduced into a headspace vial and subsequently analyzed using GC-IMS under the following conditions: a MXT-WAX column (a high-polar column, 15 m × 0.53 mm, 0.1 μm, RESTEK, Bellefonte, PA, America) was employed, with an IMS temperature of 45 oC, column temperature of 80 oC, analysis duration of 10 min, N2 as the carrier/drift gas, positive ion mode ionization, and radiation treatment with β-rays (tritium, 3 H). At the same time, the automatic headspace injection conditions included a sample volume of 500 µL, a headspace gas injection volume of 1000 µL, an incubation time of 3 min, an incubation temperature of 60 oC, an injection needle temperature of 85 oC, and an incubation speed of 500 r/min. The carrier gas flow rate was initiated at 2 mL/min and maintained for 3 min, then linearly increased to 10 mL/min for 7 min before attaining a final flow rate of 100 mL/min. The drifter gas flow rate was set at 150 mL/min.

Data processing and statistical analysis

GC-IMS data on volatile compounds in samples were acquired and analyzed using VOCal Version 0.1.3 software (G.A.S., Dortmund, Germany). At first, C4-C9 ketones (specifically, 2-butanone, 2-pentanone, 2-hexanone, 2-heptanone, 2-octanone, and 2-nonanone) were employed as reference standards. Following data calibration, the GC-IMS Library Search software was utilized for qualitative analysis of substances, based on retention index (RI) and drift time (RIP relative), using the IMS Library37. Following this, the peak volumes (semi-quantitative) of VOC molecules generated by the software were then transferred into an EXCEL (EXCEL 2021) spreadsheet for the ensuing statistical analyses.

Given the limited number of experiments conducted, fold change (FC) values exceeding 1.20 were used to denote an increase in the levels of VOCs during inter-group comparisons, while decreases below 0.83 were classified as a decreased level of VOCs, provided that the differences were statistically significant (Mann-Whitney U test, P < 0.05)38.

Statistical analyses were performed using R and R-Studio (R version 4.4.2, Austria). The fingerprints of VOCs and the spectra of 3-methyl-1-butanol (dimer) were generated using VOCal (G.A.S., Dortmund, Germany) and Reporter. The remaining analyses and visualization were carried out using the online tools Chiplot (https://www.chiplot.online)39, OmicStudio (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool)40, and Microsoft Office PowerPoint 2021 (Microsoft, Seattle, WA) .

Results

Identification of K. pneumoniae-associated volatile substances using GC-IMS

K. pneumoniae was incubated in TSB until reaching the mid-exponential phase (T0, 3 h of incubation), at which point volatile metabolites were analyzed using GC-IMS. Then, bacteria were incubated until 2 h after reaching the growth stabilization phase (T2, 5 h of incubation), with GC-IMS assays conducted at 1-h intervals (T0-T4, incubation for 3 h to 7 h). A sterile medium was concurrently incubated under identical conditions for comparative analysis. Meanwhile, the retention indices (RI) of each volatile substance were calculated based on the retention time and migration time of the characteristic substances, and volatile metabolites were characterized by the GC-IMS library (IMS databases). A total of 55 VOCs were detected throughout the experiment (without the addition of imipenem), among which 31 VOCs were successfully identified (8 VOCs found in both monomer and dimer forms, Table 1). As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 1, all experimental strains achieved growth stabilization after 5 h of incubation (T2) in TSB, facilitating the establishment of a relatively stable fingerprint of VOCs. Next, the subsequent study was centered on the T2 time point. Variations in the levels of VOCs among the four standard strains (ATCC BAA-1706, ATCC BAA-1705, ATCC BAA-2146, and ATCC BAA-2524) at the T2 time point compared to the blank media were presented in Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 3. Thereafter, the data were utilized to construct a fingerprint of K. pneumoniae-specific VOCs (Fig. 2B). Overall, our analysis yielded 22 volatile molecules linked to K. pneumonia (the monomers and dimers of the 3 VOCs displayed consistent variation patterns), categorized based on their molecular structure, as depicted in Fig. 2B; Table 2. Interestingly, compared with blank control, the temporal variation of these K. pneumoniae-associated VOCs suggested that this difference (increase or decrease in the number of VOCs rather than specific content) stabilized at the T2 time point, with no further variation observed over time (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 2).

The effect of imipenem on the volatile metabolic profile of K. pneumoniae (standard strains)

In order to investigate the impact of imipenem (IPM) on the volatile metabolomic of K. pneumoniae, 0.25 mg/mL of IPM was introduced into the experimental medium at the initial time point (T0) while the remaining conditions remained unchanged. Following the introduction of IPM, the GC-IMS spectra at the T2 time point for ATCC BAA-1706 was presented in Supplementary Fig. 5. Meanwhile, the temporal alterations in the levels of various VOCs in the four standard strains following the introduction of imipenem are outlined in Supplementary Table 4.

Utilizing K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-1706 (carbapenemase-negative) as a control, variations in VOC levels among the remaining three standard strains (carbapenemase-positive) compared with the control at the T2 time point were shown in Fig. 3A, Supplementary Figs. 6, 7 and Supplementary Table 5. The generated fingerprint illustrated the composition of VOCs for each of the standard strains following the introduction of IPM (Fig. 3B). As anticipated, the remaining strains exhibited significantly higher levels of butyl propionate and 3-methyl-1-butanol-D and significantly lower levels of 3-methyl-2-butenal-M compared with the control strain. As illustrated in Fig. 3C, PCA analysis utilizing these three distinct VOCs revealed that it was not possible to differentiate between carbapenemase-negative and positive strains at the T2 time point in the absence of IPM. Conversely, the inclusion of IPM resulted in a discernible separation between carbapenemase-negative and positive strains, with this distinction consistently maintained from the T2 time point onwards (Supplementary Fig. 8).

The effects of carbapenemase inhibitors on the volatile metabolic profile of K. pneumoniae (standard strains)

Furthermore, in order to explore the potential use of volatile metabolomic for detecting carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae, each standard strain was categorized into three groups at the T0 time point: one with IPM (final concentration: 0.25 mg/mL) alone, another with IPM (final concentration: 0.25 mg/mL) and avibactam sodium (final concentration: 1 mg/L), and a third with IPM (final concentration: 0.25 mg/mL) and EDTA (final concentration: 0.5 mmol/L). The impact of carbapenem inhibitors on the volatile metabolomic of each standard strain was then analyzed by comparing the results to the control group exclusively administered IPM. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2, the growth curves of the three treatments for each standard strain indicated that the bacterial load of ATCC BAA-1705 was notably decreased with the inclusion of avibactam sodium at the T2 time point, while the total bacterial load of ATCC BAA-2146 was significantly reduced with the addition of EDTA. In contrast, the growth of ATCC BAA-2524 did not exhibit a significant change compared to the control group without the addition of IPM.

Variations between each standard strain pre- and post-introduction of carbapenemase inhibitors were summarized in Supplementary Table 6. It was observed that, at the T2 time point, the inclusion of avibactam sodium resulted in a distinct alteration solely in the class A carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae (ATCC BAA-1705), while the addition of EDTA caused specific alterations only in the class B carbapenemase-producing standard strain (ATCC BAA-2146) (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 9). PCA also demonstrated (Fig. 5) that the addition of carbapenemase inhibitors allowed the identification of K. pneumoniae-producing class A or class B carbapenemases.

The clinical application of volatile metabolomic in the identification of carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae

To enhance the credibility of our results, 64 clinical isolates were selected for subsequent investigations and categorized into five distinct groups: the CSKP group (n = 20), the KPC-positive CRKP group (n = 20), the NDM-positive CRKP group (n = 15), the IMP-positive CRKP group (n = 4), and the carbapenemase-negative CRKP group (n = 5). All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with established protocols for standard strains, with three replicates performed for each sample. Following the addition of IPM (at a final concentration of 16 µg/mL in the carbapenemase-negative CRKP group), significant variations in VOC levels were observed among the three groups (the KPC-positive, NDM-positive and IMP-positive groups) at the T2 time point compared to the CSKP group, with the exception of the carbapenemase-negative CRKP group, as presented in Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 7. Notably, the VOCs that exhibited elevated levels in all three groups of carbapenemase-positive strains as compared to the CSKP group included 1-hexanol, propionic acid-D, 3-methyl-1-butanol-D, 3-hydroxybutan-2-one (acetoin)-D, 2-ethyl-5-methylpyrazine, 2-methylpyrazine, and 5 unidentified compounds. Conversely, the concentrations of ethyl acrylate, nonanal, 2,3-pentanedione, and 2 unidentified compounds decreased significantly (all P < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 10). Subsequently, the PCA confirmed that the addition of IPM was effective in differentiating between carbapenemase-positive and carbapenemase-negative K. pneumoniae strains using these differential VOCs (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Further analysis of the clinical strains of CRKP after the addition of carbapenemase inhibitors exposed that similar to the alterations in the standard strains, the addition of avibactam sodium induced specific changes in the levels of VOCs of the KPC-positive CRKP strains, whereas the addition of EDTA triggered specific changes in the VOCs of the NDM- and IMP-positive CRKP strains (Supplementary Tables 8 and Fig. 6).

Discussion

To date, numerous studies have focused on the volatile metabolomics of K. pneumoniae in TSB27–29,41–44. In line with the findings of previous studies13,32, this study focused on VOCs of K. pneumoniae during the stationary growth phases (T2). A total of 22 volatile substances associated with K. pneumoniae were identified in this study (Table 2). K. pneumoniae exhibited uptake of various VOCs from the medium, including aldehydes (3-methyl-2-butenal, benzaldehyde), cyclohexanone, and an unidentified compound. Conversely, the released VOCs predominantly consisted of organic acids (acetic acid, butanoic acid, propionic acid), alcohols (1-hexanol, 3-methyl-1-butanol, 1-pentanol), ketones (3-hydroxybutan-2-one, acetophenone), butyl propionate, diallyl sulfide, and 1-heptene. In addition, seven other VOCs released by K. pneumoniae were not accurately identified. As described in Table 2, a number of K. pneumoniae-associated volatile molecules have been documented in previous studies and were consistent with our experimental results.

On the other hand, the sources of some volatile molecules have been reported in previous literature. For example, the release of 3-methyl-1-butanol has been linked to the catabolism of leucine by K. pneumoniae via the Ehrlich pathway29,51, whereas the release of 3-hydroxy-2-butanone has been associated with glycolysis52. Moreover, the uptake of benzaldehyde has been linked to the provision of energy for bacterial growth53. In general, K. pneumoniae can synthesize several volatile metabolites during its growth and metabolism. Combined with the findings of a previous systematic review54 on volatile metabolites of pathogenic microorganisms, the rationale for the production of various types of volatile organic compounds in the present study can be summarized as follows: (1) The synthesis of organic acids is intricately related to anaerobic metabolism; (2) The generation of alcohols is associated with fatty acid oxidation; (3) Furthermore, the formation of ketones can be attributed to the decarboxylation of fatty acid derivatives; (4) Eesters are produced as a result of the chemical reaction between acids and alcohols. However, the origins of the remaining substances remain elusive. Admittedly, these hypotheses require further detailed investigation and should be interpreted with caution.

Regrettably, GC-IMS is predominantly utilized for food flavor analysis rather than in medical applications, resulting in a lack of inclusion of characterized medical-related VOCs in the GC-IMS library. This limitation hinders the accurate characterization of certain volatile metabolites. However, given the popularity of GC-IMS, the database is expected to be expanded. Additionally, the present study successfully determined the retention indices of these unidentified volatile compounds by combining retention time with drift time in GC-IMS (Table 1), indicating that technological advancements will aid in identifying those unidentified VOCs. Indeed, this also gives a plausible explanation for the limited diversity of K. pneumoniae-associated VOCs detected in this study.

The current global prevalence of CRKP, featured by high antimicrobial resistance and mortality rates, coupled with restricted therapeutic alternatives, presents a significant healthcare challenge11,55,56. Consequently, timely detection and characterization of CRKP are imperative for the effective management of antibiotic therapy in patients. Herein, the value of K. pneumoniae-associated VOCs in the identification of CRKPs was evaluated after determining the volatolomics of the organism. Consistent with prior researches27,29,46, we opted to include IPM in our study to investigate alterations in the volatile metabolomics of K, pneumoniae. Additionally, in accordance with previous studies35,57,58, we selected a concentration of 0.25 mg/mL of IPM as one of our experimental conditions and tried to explore the volatile products of IPM, considering the recommendation by the CLSI (CLSI 2022) to utilize matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) as a method for detecting carbapenemase-positive Enterobacteriaceae bacteria, including the hydrolysis products of IPM.

The addition of IPM yielded significant changes in the levels of specific VOCs between carbapenemase-negative and carbapenemase-positive strains (both standard and clinical strains). Interestingly, only three differential VOCs were detected in the standard strains (Fig. 3A and B). In contrast, the number of differential VOCs in carbapenemase-producing strains was over 20 in the clinical strains compared to the CSKP (Supplementary Fig. 10). More importantly, the majority of these differential VOCs were closely related to K. pneumoniae (Fig. 2B; Table 2). The following considerations may account for this difference: (1) Standard strains exhibit consistent performance compared to clinical strains owing to their stable nature, storage under controlled conditions, and low susceptibility to mutations. Clinical strains, on the other hand, are more susceptible to exposure to antibiotics and consequent mutation in the hospital setting. These characteristics result in variations in metabolic processes between standard and clinical strains27; (2) Significant genetic diversity was noted in a research study focusing on the genome structure of K. pneumoniae, contributing to metabolic heterogeneity59.

Furthermore, numerous studies have evinced that VOC metabolic can be utilized for the characterization of in vitro-cultured bacterial monocultures27,29,45,54,60–62. As reported in previous studies27,63, the introduction of antibiotics has resulted in a reduction in the levels of metabolites and an elevation in nutrient levels being consumed in susceptible bacteria. The variations detected in the metabolic profiles of VOCs herein could be attributed to this phenomenon. However, discrepant from the observations of prior research27, our study did not detect imipenem-related VOC molecules using GC-IMS, which may be ascribed to variations in detection methodologies and the brief incubation period of IPM, necessitating cautious interpretation and further investigation in future studies. Besides, analyzing changes in the VOC metabolic profile of K. pneumoniae allowed the accurate differentiation between carbapenemase-negative and carbapenemase-positive strains of the bacterium (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 10). Thus, additional investigation into imipenem concentrations for carbapenemase-negative CRKP is warranted. Indeed, the methodology employed in this study appears to be superior for the detection of carbapenemase-positive CRKP strains. This holds significant implications, given that there is an urgent need for the early identification and categorization of carbapenemase-positive CRKP strains due to variations in antimicrobial susceptibility among different carbapenemase-producing strains64. Hence, the accurate and timely identification of carbapenemase-positive CRKP is crucial for the selection of appropriate antimicrobial therapies in the clinical setting.

Subsequently, a supplementary investigation was undertaken, incorporating findings from our earlier research29 with the EDTA-carbapenem inactivation method (eCIM) endorsed by the CLSI (CLSI 2022)35. Avibactam demonstrates potent inhibitory activity towards class A carbapenemase (serine) hydrolase enzymes, yet exhibits no effectiveness in inhibiting class B carbapenemase (metalloenzyme) enzymes. It belongs to the class of diazabicyclooctanone compounds and is a novel β-lactamase inhibitor with a wide range of anti-enzyme spectrum that can reversibly covalently bind to enzymes. Avibactam’s mechanism of action involves the serine nucleophilic attack on the amide bond of avibactam by the β-lactamase, resulting in the formation of a covalent enzyme-inhibitor complex. This complex effectively inhibits the enzyme without undergoing hydrolysis, enabling the formation of the lactam ring and release of avibactam. The nucleophilic attack results in a higher rate of ring opening compared to cyclisation, leading to significant suppression of β-lactamase activity. Moreover, avibactam’s structure can be regenerated through a reverse reaction, providing prolonged enzyme inhibition65,66. Zinc ions are essential for the catalytic activity of metalloenzymes, whereas EDTA functions as a chelating agent for metal ions, specifically binding to divalent zinc ions and subsequently inhibiting the activity of metalloenzymes. This property of EDTA is commonly utilized in the screening of strains that produce metalloenzymes35. This study validated the characteristic alterations in metabolic profiles of VOCs in CRKP strains harboring various carbapenemases. The findings signaled that GC-IMS holds promise for the early identification of class A- and B-carbapenemase-producing CRKPs (Figs. 4, 5 and 6).

Finally, based on the aforementioned results, a simple method was developed, utilizing alterations in the relative concentration of 3-methyl-1-butanol to successfully distinguish between carbapenemase-positive and non-carbapenemase-positive strains (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 12). Additionally, the introduction of a carbapenemase inhibitor induced distinct changes in the content of 3-methyl-1-butanol in these specific strains, as evidenced by our prior research29. The benefits of this GC-IMS-based method are as follows: (1) The identification of K. pneumoniae can be based on the characteristic VOC fingerprint (Fig. 2B); (2) The GC-IMS spectra visually displayed variations in the content of 3-methyl-1-butanol in the different groups (Supplementary Fig. 12); (3) The relative peak volume (semi-quantitative data) of 3-methyl-1-butanol facilitates subsequent statistical analysis and comparison (Fig. 7); (4) Most importantly, following sample preparation, the GC-IMS assay requires only 13 min, thereby fulfilling the requirement for prompt detection.

Limitations

Notwithstanding the aforementioned results, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this exploratory study. To begin, there were instances where certain VOC substances were detected but not accurately identified, necessitating further investigation in future studies. It is worthwhile pointing out that the identification of volatile metabolites of pathogenic bacteria using GC-IMS is still in its early stages, and this challenge is anticipated to be addressed with the widespread adoption of this technology. Furthermore, the limited number of CRKP strains utilized in our study limits the applicability of our findings. Specifically, clinical trial strains were sourced from a collection of clinical strains gathered within the past six years by our research team, largely consisting of KPC-positive K. pneumoniae strains that comprised over 90% of CRKPs29, consistent with established epidemiological trends8. To resolve this shortcoming, additional multicentre prospective studies with a broader range of carbapenemase-producing CRKP are expected to corroborate our findings. Thirdly, in the case of clinical use of this method and patient samples, in order to exclude the interference of other bacteria, isolation and purification of K. pneumoniae strains are still required during the experiment. Fourthly, this study aimed to assess the alterations in volatile metabolomics of K. pneumoniae at the T2 time point under various treatments. However, it did not delve into the variations in IPM concentration, the impacts of alternative carbapenem antibiotics (meropenem, ertapenem, or doripenem), and the temporal changes in volatile metabolomics under different treatments to identify optimal experimental conditions. These aspects warrant further investigation in subsequent experiments and prospective studies involving multiple centers. Fifthly, we did not investigate the impact of genetic heterogeneity of K. pneumoniae strains on their volatile metabolomics, and further clarify alterations in volatile metabolomics specific to strains with different carbapenemases, due to the limited number of strains available for analysis. Further exploration of this relationship is warranted. Sixth, this research focused more on the changes of VOCs (increase or decrease) under different treatment conditions, and did not deeply explore the relationship between the relative content of VOCs and the number of bacteria, which needs to be further improved in subsequent studies. Finally, compared with similar studies13,27,32, the present study failed to identify as many K. pneumoniae-associated volatile molecules in TSB. However, the present study effectively analyzed the volatile metabolomics of K. pneumoniae using GC-IMS and successfully identified carbapenemase-producing CRKPs, thereby complementing and expanding on the results of previous studies.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to use GC-IMS to comprehensively analyze the volatile metabolomic of K. pneumoniae in TSB. By establishing a robust volatile metabolomic of K. pneumoniae, this study further validates the value of GC-IMS in the identification of carbapenemase-producing CRKPs. Nevertheless, the findings of this exploratory study are preliminary, requiring the optimization of experimental conditions and the collection of a wider array of CRKPs to confirm our findings.
