Supervised Learning of Random Neural Architectures Structured by Latent Random Fields on Compact Boundaryless Multiply-Connected Manifolds Journal: ArXiv
Christian Soize

Abstract
This paper introduces a new probabilistic framework for supervised learning in neural systems. It is designed to model complex, uncertain systems whose random outputs are strongly non-Gaussian given deterministic inputs. The architecture itself is a random object stochastically generated by a latent anisotropic Gaussian random field defined on a compact, boundaryless, multiply-connected manifold. The objective is not to propose an alternative to existing fixed-topology architectures or to outperform current models in specific empirical settings. Rather, the goal is to establish a novel conceptual and mathematical framework in which neural architectures are realizations of a geometry-aware, field-driven generative process. In this formulation, both the neural topology and synaptic weights emerge jointly from a latent random field, integrating structural and topological uncertainty as intrinsic features of the model. A reduced-order parameterization governs the spatial intensity of an inhomogeneous Poisson process on the manifold, from which neuron locations are sampled. Input and output neurons are identified via extremal evaluations of the latent field, while connectivity is established through geodesic proximity and local field affinity. Synaptic weights are conditionally sampled from the field realization, inducing stochastic output responses even for deterministic inputs. To ensure scalability, the architecture is sparsified via percentile-based diffusion masking, yielding geometry-aware sparse connectivity without ad hoc structural assumptions. Supervised learning is formulated as inference on the generative hyperparameters of the latent field, using a negative log-likelihood loss estimated through Monte Carlo sampling from single-observation-per-input datasets. The paper initiates a mathematical analysis of the model, establishing foundational properties such as well-posedness, measurability, and a preliminary analysis of the expressive variability of the induced stochastic mappings, which support its internal coherence and lay the groundwork for a broader theory of geometry-driven stochastic learning. A numerical illustration on a torus is provided, not to demonstrate predictive superiority over existing models, but to elucidate how topological, anisotropic, and stochastic field parameters influence the emergent neural architecture and its induced function class.

Introduction

(i) Objective of the paper
.
The objective of this paper is to develop a mathematically rigorous probabilistic framework for supervised learning in neural systems, designed to model complex, uncertain systems whose random outputs are strongly non-Gaussian given deterministic inputs.
The architectures are not fixed but are instead stochastically generated. These architectures are defined on compact, boundaryless, multiply-connected manifolds and are governed by latent Gaussian random fields. The proposed model generates both the neural topology and synaptic weights as emergent properties of a reduced-order representation of such a latent field.

Neurons are stochastically positioned via a Poisson point process whose spatial intensity is modulated by the latent field. Input and output neurons are selected from these points based on extremal evaluations of the field, and connections are established based on geodesic proximity and field affinity. Random weights are then assigned conditionally on the field realization, inducing a stochastic response even for deterministic inputs.

To enable scalability, a sparsification procedure is introduced that significantly reduces network connectivity via percentile-thresholded diffusion kernels. Crucially, supervised learning is not framed as direct weight optimization but as the statistical inference of the hyperparameters controlling the generative process. A negative log-likelihood loss is defined from the conditional density of outputs given inputs, which is estimated via Monte Carlo sampling over network realizations. This formulation allows training with only a single observation per input, reflecting the model’s capacity to internalize structural uncertainty.

(v) Novelty and contribution
.
This paper introduces a novel probabilistic framework for supervised learning in neural systems whose structure is not fixed, but stochastically generated by a latent Gaussian random field defined on a compact, boundaryless, multiply-connected manifold
{\mathscr{S}}
. Unlike conventional neural networks with predefined connectivity, the proposed approach models both the topology and the random weights of the neural network as emergent properties of a hidden random field. Specifically, a Gaussian

random field
U(\cdot;{\bm{\theta}})
,
parameterized by a hyperparameter vector
{\bm{\theta}}
, governs the spatial intensity of a Poisson point process over the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}
, yielding the stochastic locations of neural nodes. From these points, a subset of
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}
input and
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}
output neurons is selected based on the

extreme values of
U(\cdot;{\bm{\theta}})

evaluated at the Poisson-distributed locations. The connectivity graph is then constructed using both spatial proximity and dependence on the realized field. The weights are randomly generated conditionally on the field, allowing variability in the neural responses for fixed inputs. The training task consists of estimating the hyperparameters
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
from supervised input-output data, using the negative log-likelihood loss function constructed from conditional Monte Carlo simulations. The originality of the proposed framework lies in the following:

•

It defines neural architectures as realizations from a stochastic generative process on compact, boundaryless, multiply-connected manifolds, enabling principled modeling of geometric uncertainty and topology.

•

Both neuron placements and synaptic weights emerge from a reduced-order latent Gaussian random field and a Poisson spatial process, without assuming a fixed topology or connectivity.

•

The selection of input and output neurons is also stochastic, based on extremal evaluations of the latent field at Poisson-sampled locations, which further internalizes the structural randomness into the architecture.

•

Graph sparsification is not imposed heuristically but arises from percentile-based diffusion masking, modulated by geodesic distances and field-affinity scores.

•

The resulting network defines a stochastic mapping from deterministic inputs to random outputs, capturing functional uncertainty induced by the latent generative process.

•

Supervised learning is formulated as statistical inference of the hyperparameters
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
that govern the architecture generator, rather than direct weight optimization on a fixed network.

•

A negative log-likelihood loss is minimized using conditional Monte Carlo estimation across architecture realizations, enabling learning
from a dataset for which each input vector is associated with a single realization of the random output vector
.

We propose a Stochastic Graph Neural Network (GNN) whose architecture is sampled based on hyperparameter vector
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\beta}})
. Unlike standard GNNs or feedforward neural networks, the graph structure varies across simulations, enabling flexible and adaptive representation learning.

This formulation is particularly advantageous for implementing Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) with randomized architectures. Specifically, the latent random field defined on a manifold can serve as the stochastic germ for constructing the random fields that appear as coefficients in the partial differential equations (PDEs) governing inhomogeneous physical systems. In this way, the randomized neural network architecture becomes intrinsically aligned with the mathematical structure of the physical model underlying the inverse statistical problem to be solved from a target dataset. For example, in the case of complex systems with periodic random microstructures, a boundaryless manifold proves useful. Conversely, when the physical domain exhibits boundaries, the model incorporates a manifold with boundary, along with appropriately defined boundary conditions for the stochastic PDE that generates the latent random field.
In this approach, the vector-valued stochastic germ used in the stochastic PDE of the physics model is also used for the reduced-order latent random field of the ANN, and consequently, ensures

that the neural network architecture is statistically consistent with, and structurally adapted to, the physical and probabilistic characteristics of the problem.

(vii) Important remarks on positioning and intent
.
The aim of this work is not to propose an alternative to existing fixed-topology neural architectures or to outperform current models in specific empirical settings. Rather, it is to explore a new conceptual and mathematical framework in which the neural architecture itself is a random object, a realization of a geometry-aware, field-driven generative process defined over compact boundaryless manifolds. This formulation integrates structural uncertainty at the topological level and offers a probabilistically grounded perspective where connectivity, weights, and node roles emerge jointly from a latent anisotropic field. We view this not as a refinement of current methods, but as the opening of a distinct research direction, one that invites reconsideration of the architectural priors, inductive biases, and stochastic mechanisms underpinning learning on non-Euclidean and manifold-structured domains.

While this approach shares certain high-level concerns with Bayesian machine learning—such as the modeling of uncertainty and stochastic representation, it departs from the Bayesian deep learning paradigm. In particular, the proposed framework does not involve prior distributions over neural weights, nor does it rely on posterior inference within a fixed architecture. Instead, both the architecture and the weight distribution are emergent properties of a generative process defined by a latent Gaussian random field on a manifold. The resulting randomness is structural and geometric in nature, rather than epistemic. Learning is therefore recast as the inference of the hyperparameters governing the generative field, via a likelihood-based formulation, rather than as posterior inference over parameters. This distinction underlines the originality of the framework: it establishes a stochastic foundation for learning systems in which function, geometry, and topology are jointly governed by an underlying random field, rather than externally imposed or optimized through conventional learning procedures.

This work also initiates the mathematical analysis of a stochastic neural model whose architecture is generated by a latent Gaussian random field on a compact manifold. While a complete theoretical characterization remains open, the results already establish fundamental properties, such as a preliminary analysis of the expressive variability of the induced stochastic mappings, supporting the model internal coherence and expressive potential. These initial developments lay the groundwork for a broader mathematical theory of geometry-driven stochastic learning systems.

Finally, the numerical illustration presented in this work is not intended to demonstrate superior predictive accuracy or to benchmark against existing architectures. Rather, it serves to elucidate how topological, anisotropic, and latent stochastic parameters influence the emergent neural architecture and its induced function class. These diagnostics illustrate the operational consequences of the proposed generative model, highlighting how structural uncertainty and geometric priors shape the expressive behavior of the resulting stochastic neural system.

2
Modeling of a parameterized anisotropic latent Gaussian random field on a compact, boundaryless, multiply-connected 2-dimensional manifold immersed in 3D Euclidean space, and its reduced-order representation-based random generator

The construction of a Gaussian random field on a compact, boundaryless, multiply-connected manifold
{\mathscr{S}}
of dimension 2 immersed in the Euclidean space of dimension 3 is based on the Karhunen-Loève expansion of random fields. However, the explicit construction of such a random field on the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}
requires not only truncating the expansion, but also constructing a manageable parametrization of the manifold with a finite (though large) number of parameters, and defining, over this parameterized representation of the manifold, the mean function and the covariance operator of the random field. We thus propose an efficient construction based on the following steps:

•

Use the finite element method to construct an approximation
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
of the multiply-connected manifold
{\mathscr{S}}
, using 3-node isoparametric triangles
[
64
]
. The parameters consist of all the 3D coordinates of the
n_{o}
finite element nodes of the mesh. This yields a geometrical approximation
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
of the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}
and allows the construction of the finite element basis
\{\varphi_{p}({\textbf{x}}),{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{h}\}_{p=1}^{n_{o}}
for representing any sufficiently smooth function defined on
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
.

•

Define the centered, second-order, Gaussian, real-valued random field
U
on the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}
, represented on
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
using a relatively small number of hyperparameters. For this Gaussian random field
U
indexed by
{\mathscr{S}}
, following the approach in
[
52
,
53
]
, we define a stochastic linear boundary value problem on
{\mathscr{S}}
, whose nonhogeneous source term is a Gaussian white generalized random field
{\mathcal{B}}
indexed by
{\mathscr{S}}
. The coefficients of the linear differential operator depend on the location on
{\mathscr{S}}
and on hyperparameters that control the anisotropy of the random field. The unique solution of this stochastic problem is the centered generalized Gaussian random field, represented by a classical centered, second-order, Gaussian, real-valued random field
U
indexed by
{\mathscr{S}}
(see
[
47
]
).

•

Construct the weak formulation of the stochastic boundary value problem and perform its spatial discretization using the finite element model
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
of the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}

[
65
]
. This yields a finite-dimensional stochastic representation
U({\textbf{x}})=\sum_{p=1}^{n_{o}}U_{p}\,\varphi_{p}({\textbf{x}})
for
{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
, which satisfies a linear random matrix equation of the form
(\tau_{0}\,[g]+[\kappa_{n_{h}}])\,{\textbf{U}}={\textbf{B}}
,

where
{\textbf{U}}=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n_{o}})
and
B
is a centered, second-order, Gaussian,
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{o}}
-valued random variable
.

•

Express the covariance matrix
[C_{\textbf{U}}]
of the
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{o}}
-valued random variable
U
, which is the solution of the above linear random matrix equation.

•

To reduce the stochastic dimension of the representation of the random field
U
, construct a reduced-order stochastic model of
U
with dimension
m\ll n_{o}
, using a classical principal component analysis. This involves solving the eigenvalue problem for the matrix
[C_{\textbf{U}}]
, retaining the
m
largest eigenvalues (represented by the diagonal matrix
[\lambda^{m}]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{m}^{+}
) and their associated eigenvectors (represented by the matrix
[\Phi^{m}]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n_{o},m}
).

•

The final representation
\{U^{m}({\textbf{x}}),{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{h}\}
of the random field
U
on
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
is given by

U^{m}({\textbf{x}})=\langle\,{\bm{\psi}}^{m}({\textbf{x}}),{\textbf{H}}\,\rangle
, where
{\bm{\psi}}^{m}({\textbf{x}})=[\lambda^{m}]^{1/2}[\Phi^{m}]^{T}\,{\bm{\varphi}}({\textbf{x}})
,

{\bm{\varphi}}({\textbf{x}})=(\varphi_{1}({\textbf{x}}),\ldots,\varphi_{n_{o}}({\textbf{x}}))
,
and
H
is the normalized Gaussian
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
-valued random variable with mean
E\{{\textbf{H}}\}={\hbox{\bf 0}}_{m}
and covariance matrix

E\{{\textbf{H}}\otimes{\textbf{H}}\}=[I_{m}]
.

2.1
Construction of a parameterized anisotropic latent Gaussian random field and its generator

(i) Geometry
.
Let
{\textbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
denote a point in the Cartesian coordinate system of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
We consider a smooth, compact, boundaryless, multiply-connected 2-dimensional manifold
{\mathscr{S}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, oriented by its outward unit
normal
{\textbf{n}}({\textbf{x}})

to
{\mathscr{S}}
, and immersed in the Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

(ii) Gaussian white generalized random field
.
Let
d\sigma({\textbf{x}})
be the intrinsic surface measure on
{\mathscr{S}}
.
Let
\{{\mathcal{B}}({\textbf{x}}),{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}\}
be a real-valued, centered, Gaussian white generalized random field defined on the probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{T}},{\mathcal{P}})
and indexed by
{\mathscr{S}}
. Its covariance
C_{\mathcal{B}}
is a generalized function
[
66
,
47
]

such that, for all real-valued continuous function
f
on
{\mathscr{S}}
,

\langle C_{\mathcal{B}},f\otimes f\rangle=\|\,f\,\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathscr{S}})}\,,

(2.1)

which can, formally, be written as

\int_{\mathscr{S}}\int_{\mathscr{S}}C_{\mathcal{B}}({\textbf{x}},{\textbf{x}}^{\prime})\,f({\textbf{x}})\,f({\textbf{x}}^{\prime})\,d\sigma({\textbf{x}})\,d\sigma({\textbf{x}}^{\prime})=\int_{\mathscr{S}}f({\textbf{x}})^{2}\,d\sigma({\textbf{x}})
.

(iii) Stochastic partial differential equation
.
Let
\{U({\textbf{x}}),{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}\}
be the centered real-valued random field defined on the probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{T}},{\mathcal{P}})
and indexed by
{\mathscr{S}}
, such that

E\{U({\textbf{x}})\}=0\quad,\quad\forall{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}\,.

(2.2)

The random field
U
on
{\mathscr{S}}
is constructed as the solution of the stochastic partial differential equation,

(\tau_{0}-\langle\,\nabla,[K_{n_{h}}({\textbf{x}})]\,\nabla\,\rangle)\,U({\textbf{x}})={\mathcal{B}}({\textbf{x}})\quad,\quad\forall{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}\,.

(2.3)

In Eq. (
2.3
),
\tau_{0}>0
is fixed, and
{\textbf{x}}\mapsto[K_{n_{h}}({\textbf{x}})]
is a function from
{\mathscr{S}}
into
{\mathbb{M}}_{3}^{+0}
.
Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{n_{h}}
be the operator defined by

{\mathcal{L}}_{n_{h}}=\tau_{0}-\langle\nabla,[K_{n_{h}}({\textbf{x}})]\,\nabla\rangle\,,

(2.4)

where
\nabla
denotes the surface gradient acting tangentially to
{\mathscr{S}}
. At each point
{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}
, the matrix
[K_{n_{h}}({\textbf{x}})]
, when projected onto the tangent space
T_{\textbf{x}}{\mathscr{S}}
, defines a symmetric, positive-definite endomorphism in
\mathrm{End}(T_{\textbf{x}}{\mathscr{S}})
.

In addition, the construction of the field
[K_{n_{h}}]
is carried out in order that, there exit two constants
k_{\star}
and
k^{\star}
with
0<k_{\star}\leq k^{\star}<+\infty
such that

k_{\star}\,\|{\textbf{u}}\|^{2}\,\leq\langle\,[K_{n_{h}}({\textbf{x}})]\,{\textbf{u}},{\textbf{u}}\,\rangle\leq\,k^{\star}\,\|{\textbf{u}}\|^{2}\quad,\quad\forall{\textbf{u}}\in T_{\textbf{x}}{\mathscr{S}}\quad,\quad\forall{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}\,.

(2.5)

These bounds guarantee the uniform ellipticity of the operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{n_{h}}
.

Remark 1
. We consider a centered random field, as a non-zero mean function would not influence the proposed hyperparameterized probabilistic model of the random neural architecture, and thus would have no effect on the supervised learning process used to estimate the optimal hyperparameter value controlling the neural network.

(iv) Anisotropic
{\mathscr{S}}
-manifold model
.
Let
{\textbf{e}}^{1}({\textbf{x}}),{\textbf{e}}^{2}({\textbf{x}}),{\textbf{e}}^{3}({\textbf{x}})
be a direct local orthonormal basis at the point
{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}
, such that
{\textbf{e}}^{3}({\textbf{x}})={\textbf{n}}({\textbf{x}})
. Consequently,
{\textbf{e}}^{1}({\textbf{x}})
and

belong to the tangent space
T_{\textbf{x}}{\mathscr{S}}
. Let

and

be positive real-valued fields on
{\mathscr{S}}
. We then define the diagonal tensor field
[K_{n_{h}}]
by

[K_{n_{h}}({\textbf{x}})]=h^{(1)}_{n_{h}}({\textbf{x}})\,{\textbf{e}}^{1}({\textbf{x}})\otimes{\textbf{e}}^{1}({\textbf{x}})+h^{(2)}_{n_{h}}({\textbf{x}})\,\,{\textbf{e}}^{2}({\textbf{x}})\otimes{\textbf{e}}^{2}({\textbf{x}})\quad,\quad\forall\,{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}\,,

(2.6)

where, for
k\in\{1,2\}
, the field

is an
n_{h}
-finite representation of a real-valued field on
{\mathscr{S}}
.
It can be seen that

. When restricted to the tangent space

, it defines a symmetric, positive-definite endomorphism of

, that is,
[K_{n_{h}}({\textbf{x}})]\,|\,{T_{\textbf{x}}{\mathscr{S}}}\in\mathrm{End}(T_{\textbf{x}}{\mathscr{S}})

As such, it defines an anisotropic Riemannian metric on
{\mathscr{S}}
, since the restriction of

to

is positive-definite. Consequently, the differential operator

becomes intrinsic to the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}
, that is, invariant under isometries and independent of the immersion into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For
k\in\{1,2\}
and

for given

,

a low-rank representation of

is introduced and written as

0<c_{\star}^{(k)}\,\leq\,h^{(k)}_{n_{h}}({\textbf{x}})=h_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{h}}\beta_{j}^{\,(k)}\,{\mathbb{h}}^{(k,j)}({\textbf{x}})\,\leq\,c^{(k)\star}\quad,\quad\forall\,{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}\,,

(2.7)

where
h_{k}>0
and
0<c_{\star}^{(k)}\,\leq c^{(k)\star}
, which implies that Eq. (
2.5
) holds, and where

\int_{\mathscr{S}}{\mathbb{h}}^{(k,j)}({\textbf{x}})\,{\mathbb{h}}^{(k,j^{\prime})}({\textbf{x}})\,d\sigma({\textbf{x}})=\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\quad,\quad\int_{\mathscr{S}}{\mathbb{h}}^{(k,j)}({\textbf{x}})\,d\sigma({\textbf{x}})=0\,.

(2.8)

For each given
h_{k}>0
, the hyperparameters
\{\beta_{j}^{\,(k)}\}_{j}
belong to an admissible set

{\mathscr{C}}_{{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ad}}}},k}
ensuring that

satisfies Eq. (
2.7
)

for all
{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}
. We then have

{\bm{\beta}}^{\,(k)}_{n_{h}}=(\beta_{1}^{\,(k)},\ldots,\beta_{n_{h}}^{\,(k)})\,\in\,{\mathscr{C}}_{{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac ad}}}},k}\,\subset\,{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{h}}\quad,\quad k\in\{1,2\}\,.

(2.9)

Note that for

, we have
h^{(k)}_{n_{h}}({\textbf{x}})=h_{k}
, a positive constant.

(v) Weak formulation and existence of a unique second-order Gaussian random field
.
Let
{\mathbb{H}}^{1}({\mathscr{S}})\subset L^{2}({\mathscr{S}})
denote the Sobolev space of order

, equipped with the surface measure

of Eq. (

(2.10)

It can be seen (see Eq. (
2.1
)) that

is a centered, second-order, Gaussian, real-valued random variable such that

. In Eq. (

are understood in the mean-square sense.
Under the assumptions on
\tau_{0}
and the field
[K_{n_{h}}]
, it can be shown (using, for instance,

is a centered, second-order, Gaussian, real-valued random field, meaning that

for all

(vi) Construction of an approximation by the finite element method
.
As previously explained, the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}
is meshed with
3
-node finite elements (triangles) with linear interpolation functions in the isoparametric finite element framework
[
64

yielding the geometrical approximation

Let

(2.11)

Substituting

2.11

is replaced by

) and taking for

-valued stochastic equation,

(\tau_{0}\,[g]+[\kappa_{n_{h}}])\,{\textbf{U}}={\textbf{B}}\quad,\quad{\textbf{U}}=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n_{o}})\quad,\quad{\textbf{B}}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{n_{o}})\,,

(2.12)

where

and

are a centered, second-order, Gaussian,

-valued random variables, and where the matrix

With the finite element method,

The sparse positive-definite symmetric real matrix

Under the introduced hypotheses,

Note that

(viii) Eigenvalue problem, reduced-order representation

of the random field

, and its generator
.
Let

(2.16)

For

, let

be the reduced-order representation of the centered random vector

(2.17)

where
{\textbf{H}}=(H_{1},\ldots,H_{m})
is a centered, normalized second-order, Gaussian,

-valued random variable, with independent components,

E\{{\textbf{H}}\}={\hbox{\bf 0}}_{m}\quad,\quad E\{{\textbf{H}}\otimes{\textbf{H}}\}=[I_{m}]\,.

(2.18)

Note that
{\textbf{U}}^{m}
depends on the hyperparameters

,
h_{2}
,
{\bm{\beta}}^{\,(1)}_{n_{h}}
, and
{\bm{\beta}}^{\,(2)}_{n_{h}}
(we recall that

is considered as a fixed parameter of the model rather than as a

\hbox{{\Ppcarac err}}_{{\hbox{{\pppppcarac PCA}}}}(m)=\frac{E\{\|\,{\textbf{U}}-{\textbf{U}}^{m}\,\|^{2}\}}{E\{\|\,{\textbf{U}}\,\|^{2}\}}=1-\frac{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\lambda_{\alpha}}{\tr\{[C_{{\textbf{U}}}]\}}\quad,\quad m<n_{o}-1\,.

(2.19)

Using Eqs. (
2.14

(2.21)

Note that since

, we have
{\bm{\psi}}^{m}\in C^{0}({\mathscr{S}}_{h},{\mathbb{R}}^{m})
.
Let
\{{\bm{\eta}}^{\ell},\ell=1,\ldots,n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}\}

From Eq. (

(2.22)

Finally, considering that

2.2

U_{c}

Such a generator of

(iii.a) If the Cholesky factorization

Let
[u]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n_{o},n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}}
be the rectangular matrix that groups columnwise the

{\textbf{u}}^{\ell}=(\tau_{0}\,[g]+[\kappa])^{-1}\,[L_{g}]^{T}\,{\bm{\gamma}}^{\ell}

(2.23)

(iii.b) If the Cholesky factorization of

(iii.c) Let

[

3
Generation of neurons via an inhomogeneous Poisson process

In this section, the hyperparameter
{\bm{\theta}}=(h_{1},h_{2},\zeta_{s},{\bm{\beta}}^{\,(1)}_{n_{h}},{\bm{\beta}}^{\,(2)}_{n_{h}})
, defined in Section
1
-(iii) (where
\zeta_{s}
is defined in Section
5
), is fixed
.

(i) Principle of the construction
.
For all
x
fixed in
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
, let
\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
be the variance of the real-valued random variable
U^{m}({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
.
Taking into account Eqs. (
2.18
), (
2.21
), and (
2.20
),
\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
is written as

\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})=\|{\bm{\psi}}^{m}({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})\|^{2}\quad,\quad{\bm{\psi}}^{m}({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})=[\lambda^{m}({\bm{\theta}})]^{1/2}[\Phi^{m}({\bm{\theta}})]^{T}\,{\bm{\varphi}}({\textbf{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\,.

(3.1)

To generate the positions
\{{\textbf{x}}^{i}_{\bm{\theta}},i=1,\ldots,N\}
of
N
neurons on the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
, we employ a spatial point process that adapts to the local variability of the latent Gaussian field
\{U^{m}({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}}),{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{h}\}
.
It should be noted that the variance
\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
may vanish at certain points on the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
,
especially when the reduced-order dimension
m
is small. In such regions, the intensity function of the inhomogeneous Poisson process becomes zero, and no neurons will be sampled there. This behavior is consistent with the probabilistic structure of the model, in which neuron allocation is concentrated in areas of higher uncertainty (i.e., higher local variance). However, if large parts of
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
exhibit vanishing variance, this may result in under-sampling or complete omission of those regions from the neural graph. In practice, this effect is mitigated by ensuring that the reduced-order dimension
m
captures enough variability of the latent field over
{\mathscr{S}}
.
We thus define an inhomogeneous Poisson point process
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Lambda}
on
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
with intensity
\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})\,d\sigma({\textbf{x}})
, where
d\sigma({\textbf{x}})
denotes the surface measure on the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
induced by its embedding in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
This means that, for any measurable subset
A\subset{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
, the random number
{\mathcal{N}}_{A}({\bm{\theta}})
of points falling in
A
is a Poisson random variable with mean value

E\{{\mathcal{N}}_{A}({\bm{\theta}})\}=\int_{A}\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})\,d\sigma({\textbf{x}})\,.

(3.2)

Conditional on the event
{\mathcal{N}}_{\!{\mathscr{S}}_{h}}({\bm{\theta}})=N
,

the locations of the
N
points are independently and identically distributed over
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
, with probability density function
p({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
with respect to the measure
d\sigma({\textbf{x}})
, given by

p({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})=\frac{\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})}{\int_{{\mathscr{S}}_{h}}\Lambda({\textbf{y}};{\bm{\theta}})\,d\sigma({\textbf{y}})}\quad,\quad{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{h}\,.

(3.3)

The set of accepted points
\{{\textbf{x}}^{i}_{\bm{\theta}},i=1,\ldots,N\}\subset{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
constitutes the
N
neurons of the graph,

{\mathcal{V}}_{\bm{\theta}}=\{{\textbf{x}}^{1}_{\bm{\theta}},{\textbf{x}}^{2}_{\bm{\theta}},\dots,{\textbf{x}}^{N}_{\bm{\theta}}\}\quad,\quad{\textbf{x}}^{i}_{\bm{\theta}}\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{\Lambda}\,.

(3.4)

In practice, the realization of
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Lambda}
is approximated numerically by the thinning procedure described below.
With this construction, the higher the local variance
\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
, the more likely a neuron is placed at
x
, thus allocating model capacity where the field is more uncertain or varying.
This construction follows standard properties of inhomogeneous Poisson point processes, including the conditional i.i.d. distribution of points given their number
[
69
,
70
]
.

(ii) Sampling from an inhomogeneous Poisson process via rejection method
.
To construct a sample of neurons according to the inhomogeneous Poisson point process
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Lambda}
defined on the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
, we employ a

rejection-type (approximate thinning)
algorithm
[
71
,
72
]
, which is particularly suitable for spatial domains discretized by finite elements. The method enables efficient simulation of a realization of
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Lambda}
by filtering candidate points sampled from a proposal distribution over
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
based on the target intensity function
\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
. This process is fully compatible with the mesh-based representation of
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
described in the geometric framework.
First, we generate a large set of
M
candidate points
\{{\textbf{z}}^{j},j=1,\ldots,M\}\subset{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
using a proposal probability measure
\pi({\textbf{x}})\,d\sigma({\textbf{x}})
. A typical and computationally efficient choice for
\pi({\textbf{x}})
is the piecewise uniform distribution over the triangulated mesh of
{\mathscr{S}}
, with each triangle contributing proportionally to its surface area. Such sampling can be performed using barycentric coordinates and local area weighting over each triangular element, following standard techniques in mesh-based Monte Carlo integration.
Then, for each candidate point
{\textbf{z}}^{j}
, the normalized intensity value
\Lambda({\textbf{z}}^{j};{\bm{\theta}})
is computed using Eq. (
3.1
), which reflects the local variance of the latent Gaussian field
\{U^{m}({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}}),{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{h}\}
at that location. To control the thinning acceptance rates, we compute the maximum observed value over the sample,

\Lambda_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac max}}}({\bm{\theta}})=\max_{j=1,\ldots,M}\Lambda({\textbf{z}}^{j};{\bm{\theta}})\,.

(3.5)

The value
\Lambda_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac max}}}({\bm{\theta}})
is an empirical estimate obtained from the finite set of candidate points, and may

overestimate

the true supremum of
\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
over
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
; such
overestimation
can reduce the efficiency of

the rejection step
by decreasing the expected number of accepted neurons.
Each candidate point
{\textbf{z}}^{j}
is then retained (i.e., selected as a neuron) with probability

p_{j}({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{\Lambda({\textbf{z}}^{j};{\bm{\theta}})}{\Lambda_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac max}}}({\bm{\theta}})}\,,

(3.6)

by drawing an independent Bernoulli random variable
B_{j}({\bm{\theta}})\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p_{j}({\bm{\theta}}))
. The resulting set of accepted neurons is then given by

{\mathcal{V}}_{\bm{\theta}}=\left\{{\textbf{z}}^{j}\in\{{\textbf{z}}^{1},\dots,{\textbf{z}}^{M}\}\,|\,B_{j}({\bm{\theta}})=1\right\}\subset{\mathscr{S}}_{h}\,.

(3.7)

This set approximates a realization of the inhomogeneous Poisson point process
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Lambda}
.
Note that this thinning algorithm provides an approximation of the inhomogeneous Poisson process
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Lambda}
, which becomes accurate in the limit of a large number of candidate points
M
and when the proposal distribution
\pi({\textbf{x}})
is close to uniform with respect to the surface measure
d\sigma({\textbf{x}})
; exact sampling would require
\pi({\textbf{x}})\propto 1
and
M\rightarrow+\infty
.

Let
{\mathcal{N}}_{\!{\mathscr{S}}_{h}}({\bm{\theta}})
denote

the random variable representing the number of accepted points.
This sampling procedure results in a realization of a point process whose spatial distribution approximates the target inhomogeneous Poisson process
{\mathbb{P}}_{\Lambda}
.

In particular, for any region
A\subset{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
, the expected number of accepted points in
A
approximates
\int_{A}\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})\,d\sigma({\textbf{x}})
, and, conditional on the random number
{\mathcal{N}}_{\!{\mathscr{S}}_{h}}({\bm{\theta}})
of accepted points, the accepted locations are approximately i.i.d. over
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
according to the normalized intensity function
p({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
defined in Eq. (
3.3
).

The expected number of accepted candidate points (i.e., neurons) is then approximated by

E\{{\mathcal{N}}_{\!{\mathscr{S}}_{h}}({\bm{\theta}})\}=\frac{M}{|{\mathscr{S}}_{h}|}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac max}}}({\bm{\theta}})}\int_{{\mathscr{S}}_{h}}\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})\,d\sigma({\textbf{x}})\,.

(3.8)

This formula holds under the assumption that the proposal distribution
\pi({\textbf{x}})
is approximately uniform with respect to the surface measure
d\sigma({\textbf{x}})
.
Consequently, regions on the manifold where the variance
\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
is large, typically indicating regions of higher uncertainty or variability of the latent field
U^{m}({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
, receive proportionally more neurons
[
73
]
.
From a numerical standpoint, this method is straightforward to implement and highly parallelizable.

(iii) On the relationship between the latent Gaussian random field and the spatial distribution of neurons
.
It is important to distinguish between the pointwise variance function
{\textbf{x}}\mapsto\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
and any particular realization
{\textbf{x}}\mapsto U^{m}({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
of the associated latent Gaussian random field. While the variance
\Lambda({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}})
quantifies the local uncertainty of the field at each point of the manifold, a realization may take high or low values regardless of the corresponding variance level. In particular, regions where the field vanishes or takes extreme values may still exhibit either high or vanishing variance, depending on the structure of the reduced-order representation.

(iv) Implementation of random sampling in the algorithm
.
In the numerical realization of the inhomogeneous Poisson process on the discretized manifold
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
, the sampling of neuron locations is performed using four independent random vectors:
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac elem}}}
,
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{a}
,
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{b}
, and
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac poisson}}}
, each with entries that are independent, uniformly distributed random variables on
[0,1]
. First, we introduce the random vector

{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac elem}}}
with values in
[0,1]^{M}
, which is used to select triangles on the mesh in proportion to their surface areas. The number
M
of candidate points is generated by
M=p_{M}\times n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac elem}}}
, where
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac elem}}}
is the number of finite elements (triangles) in the mesh, and
p_{M}
is a given tunable parameter representing the number of points per element (for instance, we can choose
p_{M}=2
). Each entry in
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac elem}}}
determines, through inversion of the cumulative area function, which mesh element is chosen for candidate point placement. Within each selected triangle, the random vectors
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{a}
and
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{b}
, with values in
[0,1]^{M}
, are employed as barycentric coordinates to generate a point uniformly inside the triangle. If their sum exceeds one, a reflection is applied to ensure the sample remains within the triangle. This approach results in a set of
M
candidate points
\{{\textbf{z}}^{j}\}
that are approximately uniformly distributed over
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
with respect to the surface measure.
Once the intensities
\Lambda({\textbf{z}}^{j};{\bm{\theta}})
at all candidate points have been computed, a discrete probability distribution proportional to these intensities is constructed. The final selection of
N
neurons from the
M
candidates is then achieved by drawing
N
independent samples according to this distribution. The
[0,1]^{N}
-valued random variable
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac poisson}}}
is mapped via the cumulative distribution function of the normalized intensities, yielding the indices of the selected neuron locations. This sampling procedure ensures that the empirical distribution of neurons closely matches the theoretical spatial distribution

prescribed by the intensity function of the inhomogeneous Poisson process
,
thereby concentrating neurons in regions of higher variance of the latent Gaussian field and accurately reflecting the probabilistic structure of the model.

4
Neural connectivity graph construction from geometry and latent fields

In Section
3
, we introduced the stochastic model for generating
N
neurons with locations on the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
, governed by an inhomogeneous Poisson process whose intensity is modulated by the local variability of the latent Gaussian field
\{U^{m}({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}}),{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}\}
. The hyperparameter vector
{\bm{\theta}}
controls the statistical properties of this field and is inferred from supervised input–output training data. Consequently, the neurons locations
\{{\textbf{x}}^{1}_{\bm{\theta}},\ldots,{\textbf{x}}^{N}_{\bm{\theta}}\}
depend on
{\bm{\theta}}
, which is considered fixed throughout this section.
For a given value of
{\bm{\theta}}
, we consider a

realization
\{u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}),{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{h}\}
of the random field
\{U^{m}({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}}),{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{h}\}
.

Note that all generated realizations are assumed to be statistically independent across the different values of
{\bm{\theta}}
used by the training algorithm (see Section
8
).
Using this value of
{\bm{\theta}}
, the generation of the geometric–probabilistic neural topology begins using the realization
\{u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}),{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{h}\}
. More precisely, for a fixed realization
\omega_{\bm{\theta}}\in\Omega
, we denote
u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}})=U^{m}({\textbf{x}};{\bm{\theta}};\omega_{\bm{\theta}})
.
From the resulting set of
N
neurons, subsets of
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}
input and
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}
output neurons (independent of
{\bm{\theta}}
) are selected based on the lowest and highest values, respectively, of
\{u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{j}),j=1,\ldots,N\}
, evaluated at the Poisson-sampled locations, as previously explained.
The neural connectivity graph is defined by a binary sparsity mask derived by combining spatial proximity with dependence on the
realization
\{u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}),{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{h}\}
of

the latent random field . This mask is applied to the fully connected weight matrix to obtain the sparsely connected weights. Synaptic weights are then randomly assigned, conditional on the latent field realization, introducing variability in neural responses even under fixed input values. Scalability is achieved through the sparsification mechanism incorporated into the stochastic neural topology.

4.1
Input and output neuron selection from the latent field for graph topology

For a fixed value of
{\bm{\theta}}
, let
\{u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}),{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{h}\}
be the given realization of the random field
\{U^{m}({\textbf{x}};\theta),{\textbf{x}}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{h}\}
introduced above, from which the graph construction proceeds. Let
\{{\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{j},j=1,\ldots,N\}
denote the
N
neurons stochastically located on the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
as described in Section
3
, and depending on
{\bm{\theta}}
. For each neuron located at
{\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{j}
, the value
u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{j})
of the realized Gaussian random variable
U^{m}({\textbf{x}}^{j};{\bm{\theta}})
is computed.
The subset of
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}
input neurons is selected as the nodes corresponding to the
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}
smallest values among
\{u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{j}),j=1,\ldots,N\}
. Let
{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}=\{i^{\,{\bm{\theta}}}_{1},\ldots,i^{\,{\bm{\theta}}}_{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac in}}}}\}\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}
be the index set corresponding to the sorted order,

u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{i^{\,{\bm{\theta}}}_{1}})\leq u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{i^{\,{\bm{\theta}}}_{2}})\leq\dots\leq u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{i^{\,{\bm{\theta}}}_{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac in}}}}})\,.

(4.1)

Similarly, the subset of
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}
output neurons is selected as the nodes corresponding to the
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}
largest values of
u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}^{j})
. Let
{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}=\{o^{\,{\bm{\theta}}}_{1},\ldots,o^{\,{\bm{\theta}}}_{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}}}\}\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}
be the index set such that,

u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{o^{\,{\bm{\theta}}}_{1}})\geq u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{o^{\,{\bm{\theta}}}_{2}})\geq\ldots\geq u^{m}_{\bm{\theta}}({\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{o^{\,{\bm{\theta}}}_{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}}}})\,.

(4.2)

Since
N
,
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}
, and
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}
are given, the indices of the
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac int}}}
internal (hidden) neurons, which depend on
{\bm{\theta}}
, are grouped in
{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac int}}}
, and we have

N=n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}+n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac int}}}+n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}\quad,\quad{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}\cup{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac int}}}\cup{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}=\{1,\ldots,N\}\quad,\quad{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}\cap{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac int}}}\cap{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}=\emptyset.

(4.3)

Note that if
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}
,
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}
,
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac int}}}

(and thus
N
) are fixed independently of
{\bm{\theta}}
, the index sets

{\mathcal{J}}_{\theta}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}
,
{\mathcal{J}}_{\theta}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac int}}}
, and
{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}
still depend on
{\bm{\theta}}
.
This selection strategy induces a functional differentiation of neurons based on the intensity landscape of the latent field:

input neurons lie in low-activation regions (the smallest values of the random latent field), whereas output neurons lie in high-activation regions (the largest values of the random latent field).

This choice introduces a geometric and probabilistic bias into the architecture, governed by the underlying generative process.

The input and output amplitudes of the random neural network are then represented by the vectors
{\mathbb{x}}=({\mathbb{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbb{x}}_{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac in}}}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac in}}}}
and
{\mathbb{y}}=({\mathbb{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbb{y}}_{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}}}
, respectively, whose components correspond to the activation values of the neurons selected by the process described above.

4.2
Sparsity binary mask construction for neural graph topology

Having generated the vertex set
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bm{\theta}}=\{{\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{1},\ldots,{\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{N}\}
from the Poisson process (Section
3
) and the preliminary fully connected weight matrix
[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]
(Section
4.2.2
-(i)), we construct a set of weighted edges
{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}
to obtain

a sparse matrix

[w_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}]
. This defines the graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{\bm{\theta}}=({\mathcal{V}}_{\bm{\theta}},{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}})
, whose topology reflects both spatial geometry and field-induced connectivity.

This topology captures local geometric structure and manifold connectivity and critically influences subsequent training.

In what follows, we briefly review standard methodologies for edge construction and sparsification, before presenting the approach adopted in this work.

4.2.1
Short survey of edge construction and sparsification methodologies

The preliminary weight matrix
[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]
corresponds to a fully connected graph on
N
nodes, resulting in
O(N^{2})
edges, which is often computationally prohibitive. To obtain a sparser and more tractable representation, several well-established methods are commonly used:

•

k
-nearest neighbors (
k
-NN) graph. For each node
{\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{i}
, retain only the
k
edges
({\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{i},{\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{j})
with the highest weights
[w_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ij}
, corresponding to the
k
most strongly connected neighbors. This results in a locally adaptive graph with fixed degree
k

75
]
.

•

Radius-based graph. Include the edge
({\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{i},{\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{j})
whenever the approximate geodesic distance satisfies
\hat{d}_{g}({\textbf{x}}^{i}_{\bm{\theta}},{\textbf{x}}^{j}_{\bm{\theta}})

\leq r

can be chosen either globally or based on local statistics. This approach induces a variable node degree while preserving spatial locality
[
76
]
.

•

Spectral sparsification. Construct a sparse graph with weight matrix
[w^{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}_{\bm{\theta}}]
that preserves the spectral (Laplacian) properties of the original fully connected graph. Techniques such as effective resistance sampling are often used to approximate the graph spectrum while reducing the edge count to
O(N\log N)

[
77
]

•

Locally scaled diffusion kernel. Following
[
78
]

\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{\theta}},i}
as the geodesic distance between
{\textbf{x}}^{i}_{\bm{\theta}}
and its
k
-th nearest neighbor, denoted
{\textbf{x}}^{i,k}_{\bm{\theta}}

In this work, we have selected the locally scaled diffusion kernel method.

4.2.2
Self-tuning diffusion kernel with percentile-based sparsification

We construct a neuron–neuron affinity graph by combining intrinsic geodesic distances computed on the mesh with a self-tuning diffusion kernel
[
78
]
, followed by percentile-based thresholding to enforce a desired sparsity level.
While the preliminary affinity weights are defined by Eq.(
4.4
), the sparsification procedure considers only geometric information. Specifically, we use a temporary kernel based solely on intrinsic geodesic distances, omitting neuron feature values, which will be introduced

in Section
5
for

defining the random weights of the neural architecture.
This kernel is used to derive a binary sparsity mask
[M^{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}_{\bm{\theta}}]\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
, which determines the connectivity pattern of the final graph. The original affinity weights from Eq. (
4.4
) are then assigned to the selected edges.

(i) Computation of edge weights for constructing the binary sparsity mask
.
Consider two neurons located at positions
{\textbf{x}}^{i}_{\bm{\theta}}
and
{\textbf{x}}^{j}_{\bm{\theta}}
on the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
. We define the geodesic distance
d_{g}({\textbf{x}}^{i}_{\bm{\theta}},{\textbf{x}}^{j}_{\bm{\theta}})
as the length of the shortest path connecting these two points on
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
.
A finite element approximation
\hat{d}_{g}({\textbf{x}}^{i}_{\bm{\theta}},{\textbf{x}}^{j}_{\bm{\theta}})
of this geodesic distance is given by Eq. (
4.7
) in Section
4.2.2
-(ii).
Using this geodesic metric, we construct the preliminary (fully connected) edge weight matrix
[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]
, whose entries are defined by

[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ij}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{d_{g}({\textbf{x}}^{i}_{\bm{\theta}},{\textbf{x}}^{j}_{\bm{\theta}})^{2}}{\sigma_{{\bm{\theta}},i}\,\sigma_{{\bm{\theta}},j}}\right)\quad,\quad[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ii}=0\,.

(4.4)

Here, the parameter
\sigma_{{\bm{\theta}},i}
(resp.
\sigma_{{\bm{\theta}},j}
) is local geometric bandwidths, defined by the geodesic distance from neuron location
{\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{i}
(resp.
{\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{j}
) to its
k
-th nearest neighbor (estimated using Eq. (
4.9
)). These bandwidths act as local scaling factors that adaptively normalize distances according to neuron density around each point, ensuring robustness to spatially varying sampling rates.
The exponential term encodes adaptive geometric proximity, generalizing ideas from
[
23
,
79
]
, and ensures that neurons close in geodesic distance and located in denser regions are assigned stronger weights. Consequently, a pair of neurons will have a large edge weight only if they are spatially proximal, while distant pairs will have exponentially small weights.

As previously explained, since a fully connected graph
is computationally prohibitive for large neuron counts, a sparsification procedure (discussed in the next section) is applied to obtain a computationally tractable network while preserving the essential geometric and probabilistic structure of the neuron topology.

Remark 2
. In Section
5
, we will consider a more general expression than Eq. (
4.4
) for constructing the random weight matrix
[{\textbf{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}]
and its sparse approximation
[{\textbf{W}}^{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}_{\bm{\theta}}]
for the random neural architecture. These will be constructed using the binary sparsity mask introduced here. This more general formulation of the weights will impose additional constraints by promoting strong connections between neurons with similar latent field values. For now, we use the simpler expression in Eq. (

) to construct the binary mask matrix that yields the sparse connectivity pattern of the random neuron system.

(ii) Intrinsic geodesic distances.

Recall that the manifold
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
denotes the finite-element mesh of
{\mathscr{S}}
, consisting

of
n_{o}
nodes (see Section
2.1
-(vi)). Denote the coordinates of the
p

for
p\in\{1,\ldots,n_{o}\}
,
and define the set of all mesh node coordinates as
\{{\textbf{x}}^{p}_{o},p=1,\ldots,n_{o}\}
. Note that the finite element nodes belong to
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
but also to
{\mathscr{S}}
.
We now consider the triangular connectivity of the mesh (i.e., the list of triangle elements defined over the nodes). Using this connectivity, we construct the adjacency matrix
[A]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n_{o}}
, where each entry represents the Euclidean distance between directly connected mesh nodes,

A_{pq}=\|{\textbf{x}}^{p}_{o}-{\textbf{x}}^{p}_{o}\|\,\,\text{if}\,\,(p,q)\,\,\text{is an edge of the mesh},\,\,\text{and}\,=0\,\,\text{otherwise}\,.

(4.5)

To compute intrinsic geodesic distances between the nodes of the mesh, we first compute the shortest-path geodesic distances between all pairs of mesh nodes using the adjacency matrix
[A]
.

[D_{g}]_{pq}=\min_{\xi\,\in\,\Xi_{pq}}\sum_{(p^{\prime},\,q^{\prime})\,\in\,\xi}A_{p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}\quad\,\quad\forall\,(p,q)\in\{1,\ldots,n_{o}\}\times\{1,\ldots,n_{o}\}\,,

(4.6)

where
\Xi_{pq}
is the set of all paths in the graph induced by the mesh connectivity that connect nodes
p
and
q

[
80
]

Intrinsic geodesic distances accurately reflect manifold-based separation and avoid artificial shortcuts through Euclidean space.
Since the neuron locations
\{{\textbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{i},i=1,\ldots,N\}
are not located at the mesh nodes
\{{\textbf{x}}^{p}_{o},p=1,\ldots,n_{o}\}
, but rather lie in the interior of finite elements, the geodesic distance between two neurons must be interpolated from the mesh-based distances
[D_{g}]_{pq}

Let
\{\varphi_{p}({\textbf{x}}),p=1,\ldots,n_{o}\}
denote the finite element basis functions associated with the mesh nodes (see Section
2.1
-(vi)).
Then, for two arbitrary points
x
and
y
in
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}

\hat{d}_{g}({\textbf{x}},{\textbf{y}})
of their true geodesic distance

\hat{d}_{g}({\textbf{x}},{\textbf{y}})=\sum_{p=1}^{n_{o}}\sum_{q=1}^{n_{o}}\varphi_{p}({\textbf{x}})\,[D_{g}]_{pq}\,\varphi_{q}({\textbf{y}})\,.

(4.7)

This bilinear interpolation ensures that
\hat{d}_{g}({\textbf{x}}^{p}_{o},{\textbf{x}}^{q}_{o})=[D_{g}]_{pq}
at the mesh nodes, is independent of
{\bm{\theta}}
, and extends geodesic distances to all of
{\mathscr{S}}_{h}
.
Applying Eq. (
4.7
) to the neuron coordinates
\{{\textbf{x}}^{i}_{\bm{\theta}},i=1,\ldots,N\}
yields the geodesic distance matrix between neurons,

[D_{g,{\bm{\theta}}}^{(\mathrm{neu})}]_{ij}=\hat{d}_{g}({\textbf{x}}^{i}_{\bm{\theta}},{\textbf{x}}^{j}_{\bm{\theta}})=\sum_{p=1}^{n_{o}}\sum_{q=1}^{n_{o}}\varphi_{p}({\textbf{x}}^{i}_{\bm{\theta}})\,[D_{g}]_{pq}\,\varphi_{q}({\textbf{x}}^{j}_{\bm{\theta}})\quad,\quad i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\,.

(4.8)

(iii) Local bandwidth estimation
\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{\theta}},i}
.
To obtain locally adaptive scales, for each neuron
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we use the geodesic distances

\{[D_{g,{\bm{\theta}}}^{(\mathrm{neu})}]_{ij},j\neq i\}
to all other neurons
j
. These distances are sorted in increasing order:

\{[D_{g,{\bm{\theta}}}^{(\mathrm{neu})}]_{i,(1)}<[D_{g,{\bm{\theta}}}^{(\mathrm{neu})}]_{i,(2)}<\ldots<[D_{g,{\bm{\theta}}}^{(\mathrm{neu})}]_{i,(M)}\}
, and the approximate local bandwidth
\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{\theta}},i}
is defined as the distance to the
(k+1)
th nearest neighbor,

\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{\theta}},i}=[D_{g,{\bm{\theta}}}^{(\mathrm{neu})}]_{i,(k+1)}\quad,\quad k=\min(\sqrt{N},\,N-1)\,.

(4.9)

This choice of
k
trades off

fine-scale local geometry (for small
k
) and robustness to noise or outliers (for larger
k
). This local scale
\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{\theta}},i}
adapts to the sampling density of the neuron distribution: it is smaller in dense regions and larger in sparse regions.

(iv) Self-tuning diffusion kernel
.
Using the locally adaptive scales
\{\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{\theta}},i},i=1,\ldots,N\}
defined in Eq. (
4.9
), we construct a symmetric affinity matrix
[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{N}
(corresponding to the estimation of Eq. (
4.4
)), whose entries encode both proximity and local density adaptation. The self-tuning diffusion kernel is defined as

[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ij}=\exp\left(-\frac{[D_{g,{\bm{\theta}}}^{(\mathrm{neu})}]_{ij}^{2}}{\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{\theta}},i}\,\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{\theta}},j}}\right)\quad,\quad[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ii}=0\,,\quad i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\,.

(4.10)

This kernel follows the approach proposed in
[
78
]
, where each node adapts its own local bandwidth based on its neighborhood density. The resulting graph affinity is stronger between neurons that are both close in geodesic distance and embedded in dense regions (i.e., when
\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{\theta}},i}\,\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{\theta}},j}
is small), and weaker for pairs in sparser regions. This self-tuning property eliminates the need to manually specify a global scale and ensures that both local and global geometric features are faithfully encoded in the resulting weighted graph.

(v) Percentile-based sparsification.

To control the sparsity of the graph, we introduce a global percentile threshold parameter
\tau_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac prc}}}}\in[0,100]
. Let
a_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac prc}}}}^{\bm{\theta}}
denote the
\tau_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac prc}}}}
th percentile of the upper-triangular (off-diagonal) entries of the symmetric matrix
[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]
,

a_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac prc}}}}^{\bm{\theta}}=\texttt{prctile}\left(\{[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ij}\,:\,1\leq i<j\leq N\},\,\tau_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac prc}}}}\right)\,.

(4.11)

Here,
\texttt{prctile}(z,\tau_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac prc}}}})
denotes the value below which a fraction
\tau_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac prc}}}}/100
of the elements in the
set
{\mathcal{Z}}=\{[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ij}\,:\,1\leq i<j\leq N\}
fall.
We define a binary sparsity edge-mask
[M^{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}_{\bm{\theta}}]\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
that encodes the sparsity pattern induced by the thresholded self-tuning kernel
[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]
as follows,

[M^{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ij}=1\,\,\text{if}\,\,[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ij}\geq a_{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac prc}}}}^{\bm{\theta}}\,\,\text{and}\,\,i\neq j\,,\,\,\text{and}\,\,=0\,\,\text{otherwise}\,.

(4.12)

The final sparse, undirected neural affinity matrix is denoted by
[w^{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}_{\bm{\theta}}]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{N}

[w^{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ij}=[M^{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ij}\,[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ij}\,,

(4.13)

where
[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]
is

defined by Eq. (
4.10
).

This construction guarantees that sparsity is determined purely from geometry (via
[w^{g}_{\bm{\theta}}]
).
In addition, this procedure preserves only the top
(100-\tau_{\mathrm{prc}})\%
strongest affinities, thereby enforcing global control over graph density. For instance, the choice such as
\tau_{\mathrm{prc}}=75\%
(i.e., keeping the top
25\%
of edge weights) yields a favorable trade-off between computational efficiency and preservation of the manifold’s local geometry.
The number of undirected edges in the sparse graph is then given by

|\,{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}\,|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}[M^{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ij}\,.

(4.14)

(vi) Connectivity validation of the random graph
.
For each fixed
{\bm{\theta}}

(iv) Existence, uniqueness, and numerical solution
.
Each realization
\ell
of Eq. (
7.4

\hat{\textbf{a}}^{\ell}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}=\hat{\textbf{f}}(\,[\hat{\textbf{w}}^{\ell}_{\bm{\theta}}]\,\hat{\textbf{a}}^{\ell}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}+\hat{\mathbb{b}}^{\ell}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}})\,.

(7.7)

The nonlinear system defined by Eq. (
7.7

This system can be reformulated as the fixed-point problem

\hat{\textbf{a}}={\textbf{F}}(\hat{\textbf{a}})\quad,\quad{\textbf{F}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{\hat{n}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\hat{n}}\,.

(7.8)

Here, the indices
\ell
and
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
have been suppressed

in
\hat{\textbf{a}}^{\ell}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}

and

, and we define

{\textbf{F}}(\hat{\textbf{a}})=\hat{\textbf{f}}\,(\,[\hat{\textbf{w}}^{\ell}_{\bm{\theta}}]\,\hat{\textbf{a}}\,+\,\hat{\mathbb{b}}^{\ell}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\,)\quad,\quad\hat{\textbf{a}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\hat{n}}\,.

(7.9)

It is easy to verify that the matrix
[\hat{\textbf{w}}^{\ell}_{\bm{\theta}}]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{\hat{n}}
is symmetric, has zero diagonal entries (
[\hat{\textbf{w}}^{\ell}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ii}=0
), and strictly positive off-diagonal entries (
0<[\hat{\textbf{w}}^{\ell}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ij}<1
for
i\neq j
).
The analysis of Eqs. (
7.4
) and (
7.5
) shows that the parameter
n_{b}
must satisfy
n_{b}\leq\hat{n}=N-n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}
.

(iv-1) Existence of at least one solution
.
Let
{\mathcal{D}}=[-1,1]^{\hat{n}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{\hat{n}}
. For any
\hat{\textbf{a}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\hat{n}}
, each component of
{\textbf{F}}(\hat{\textbf{a}})
lies in the open interval
]-1,1[\subset[-1,1]
, so
F
defines a continuous map
F:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{D}}
. Since
{\mathcal{D}}
is nonempty, compact, and convex, the Brouwer fixed-point theorem
[
88
]
guarantees the existence of

at least one
\hat{\textbf{a}}^{\ell}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\in{\mathcal{D}}
with

{\textbf{F}}(\hat{\textbf{a}}^{\ell}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}})=\hat{\textbf{a}}^{\ell}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}
.

(iv-2) Fixed-point iteration (under-relaxation) for constructing a solution
.
Although uniqueness does not generally hold, a scalable iterative scheme can be used. Given an initial guess
\hat{\textbf{a}}^{(0)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\hat{n}}
and a relaxation parameter
\alpha\in]0,1]
,
iterate for
i=0,1,2,\dots
,

\hat{\textbf{a}}^{(i+1)}=(1-\alpha)\,\hat{\textbf{a}}^{(i)}+\alpha\,{\textbf{F}}(\hat{\textbf{a}}^{(i)})\,,

(7.10)

and stop then when
\|\hat{\textbf{a}}^{(i+1)}-\hat{\textbf{a}}^{(i)}\bigr\|<\varepsilon

for a prescribed tolerance
\varepsilon>0
. Upon convergence, set

\hat{\textbf{a}}^{\ell}_{\bm{\theta}}\approx\hat{\textbf{a}}^{(i+1)}
, form

\hat{\textbf{a}}^{\ell}_{\bm{\theta}}=(\hat{a}^{\ell}_{{\bm{\theta}},1},\ldots,\hat{a}^{\ell}_{{\bm{\theta}},\hat{n}})
,
and extract

the output
{\mathbb{y}}^{\ell}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\,|\,{\textbf{x}})
via

Eqs. (
7.6
) and (
7.7
).
In practice, for a relatively small number of neurons (typically a few hundred), numerical experiments indicate that the fixed-point iteration is efficient in terms of computational cost. This observation is limited to the specific test cases and computational setup considered, and should not be generalized to larger network sizes or different hardware configurations.

Training the vector valued hyperparameter from Network Supervision

Using the low-rank representation of the bias vector, which involves the hyperparameter

(8.1)

(i) Fixing a realization of the random germs generating the Gaussian latent random field and the inhomogeneous Poisson process
.
As we explained in Section
1
, training the neural network consists of estimating an optimal value of the hyperparameter
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\beta}})
by minimizing the cost function
{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
, defined as the negative log-likelihood function and evaluated on the input-outputs of the training dataset. For the optimization problem, based on the projected gradient descent with Adam updates and trial-based initialization, to be well-posed, we draw a single realization of each of the random vectors
H
(see Eq. (
2.20
)) and
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac elem}}}

{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{a}
,
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{b}

before starting the optimization algorithm. Thus, these realizations are not modified
during optimization and are independent of

(and also of
{\bm{\beta}}
). This does not mean that the architecture of the neural network is deterministic; it remains random and changes for each value of
{\bm{\theta}}
. Indeed, when
{\bm{\theta}}
is modified, the spectrum of
the covariance matrix of
{\textbf{U}}^{m}
changes, due to changes of

,
h_{2}
,
{\bm{\beta}}^{\,(1)}_{n_{h}}
, and
{\bm{\beta}}^{\,(2)}_{n_{h}}

within
{\bm{\theta}}
(see Eq. (
3.1

N
neurons. Moreover, Section
4
shows that the location of the inputs-outputs on the one hand, and the binary sparsity mask
[M_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}]
defined by Eq. (
4.12
) and the edge weight matrix
[w_{\bm{\theta}}^{g}]

defined by Eq. (
4.10
)
,
on the other part, both depend on
{\bm{\theta}}
.
However, as we explained in Section
5

(and in particular of

of

of

(see Eqs. (
5.4

(see Eqs. (

(ii) Definition of the cost function
.
In the random neural architecture with random weights and deterministic bias, that we have proposed, the model output is an
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}}}
-valued random variable
{\mathbb{Y}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\mathbb{x}})
depending on a given
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}}}
-valued input
{\mathbb{x}}
and the hyperparameter
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
. For a fixed hyperparameter value
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\in{\mathscr{C}}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}
and a given input
{\mathbb{x}}
, the random network produces
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}
independent realizations
\{{\mathbb{y}}^{\ell}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\mathbb{x}}),\ell=1,\ldots,n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}\}
of
{\mathbb{Y}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\mathbb{x}})
.
Given a training dataset
\{({\mathbb{x}}^{i},{\mathbb{y}}^{i}),i=1,\ldots,n_{d}\}
, the goal is to estimate the optimal hyperparameter
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
by fitting the probability distribution of the model outputs
{\mathbb{Y}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\,|\,{\mathbb{x}}^{i})
to the observed outputs
{\mathbb{y}}^{i}
.
A statistical efficient and widely used cost function in this setting is the negative log-likelihood (NLL), defined as,

{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{d}}\log p^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}({\mathbb{y}}^{i}\,|\,{\mathbb{x}}^{i};{\bm{\theta}}_{t})\quad,\quad{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\in{\mathscr{C}}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\,,

(8.2)

where
{\mathbb{y}}\mapsto p^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}({\mathbb{y}}\,|\,{\mathbb{x}};{\bm{\theta}}_{t})
denotes the conditional probability density function of
{\mathbb{Y}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\,|\,{\mathbb{x}})
.
Since the network random output
{\mathbb{Y}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\,|\,{\mathbb{x}})
is only available through sampling, its conditional probability density function can be approximated using Gaussian Kernel Density Estimation (GKDE)
[
89
,
90
,
91
]
as

p^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}({\mathbb{y}}\,|\,{\mathbb{x}};{\bm{\theta}}_{t})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{1}{n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}}}\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\,s_{\hbox{{\ppppppcarac SB}}})^{\,n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}}}\,\sigma_{{\mathbb{y}},1}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\mathbb{x}})\times\ldots\times\sigma_{{\mathbb{y}},n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\mathbb{x}})}\,\exp\Bigg( -\frac{1}{2\, s_\pSB^2} \sum_{k=1}^{n_\ppout}
\Bigg(\frac{ \yy_k - \yy_k^\ell(\bftheta_t \vert\xx) } { \sigma_{\yy,k}(\bftheta_t \vert\xx)} \Bigg)^2 \Bigg)\,,

(8.3)

where
s_{\hbox{{\ppppppcarac SB}}}=(4/(n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}\,(2+n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}})))^{1/(4+n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}})}
and where
\sigma_{{\mathbb{y}},k}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\mathbb{x}})
is the empirical standard deviation estimated from
\{{\mathbb{y}}^{\ell}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\mathbb{x}}),\ell=1,\ldots,n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}\}
.
Evaluating the kernel involves computing exponential functions, which can be computationally intensive. Nevertheless, GKDE remains an accurate and widely used method in practice. Several techniques have been developed to reduce its computational cost; see, for example,
[
92
,
93
]
for standard references on kernel density estimation theory and practice, and
[
94
,
95
,
96
,
97
]
for acceleration methods and computational considerations. These acceleration methods preserve the statistical robustness of GKDE while significantly improving its computational efficiency. One such approximation involves approximating Eq. (
8.3

(iii) Optimization problem
.
The estimation of an optimal value
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}=({\bm{\theta}}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}},{\bm{\beta}}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}})
of the hyperparameter
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\beta}})
is obtained by solving the optimization problem,

{\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}=\arg\min_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\in{\mathscr{C}}_{\theta_{t}}}{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\,,

(8.4)

where
{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
is defined in Eq. (
8.2
) and the admissible set
{\mathscr{C}}_{\theta_{t}}
in Eq. (
5.7
).
This nonconvex optimization problem cannot be solved directly using projected gradient descent, as the optimal point generally corresponds to a local minimum that depends on the initial conditions. Consequently, hybrid optimization strategies are necessary, such as multi-start gradient methods, basin-hopping algorithms, or Bayesian optimization. In this work, we adopt a projected gradient descent scheme with Adam updates and trial-based initialization
[
98
,
99
]
.
The main steps of the proposed algorithm are as follows:

Step 1: Definition of a grid-based admissible set for the hyperparameters
h_{1}
,
h_{2}
, and
\zeta_{s}
.
To prepare the trial-based method for global optimization, we introduce the first three components of
{\bm{\theta}}

{\bm{\theta}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}=(h_{1},h_{2},\zeta_{s})
, and define its admissible set
{\mathscr{C}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}
as

{\mathscr{C}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}=\{{\bm{\theta}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}\in[c_{1},d_{1}]\times[c_{2},d_{2}]\times[c_{3},d_{3}]\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\,\,\,|\,\,\,0<c_{i}<d_{i}<+\infty\,,\,i=1,2,3\}\,.

(8.5)

The constants
c_{i}
and

are prescribed. We then define a grid
{\mathscr{C}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac grid}}}\subset{\mathscr{C}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}
consisting of
n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac grid}}}
nodes
{\bm{\theta}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}^{\,j}=(h_{1}^{j},h_{2}^{j},\zeta_{s}^{\,j})
for
j=1,\ldots,n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac grid}}}
.

Step 2: Trial-based method for global optimization, used to initialize local optimization with projected gradient descent and Adam updates
. In this trial-based step, we set
n_{h}=0
, so that
{\bm{\beta}}^{\,(1)}_{n_{h}}
and
{\bm{\beta}}^{\,(2)}_{n_{h}}
are not involved. Therefore, this step estimates an optimal value
{\bm{\theta}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
for the trial hyperparameter
{\bm{\theta}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}=(h_{1},h_{2},\zeta_{s})
.
For each point
{\bm{\theta}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}^{\,j}\in{\mathscr{C}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac grid}}}
, we estimate a corresponding value
{\bm{\beta}}^{\,j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{b}}
, as detailed below. The trial-based global optimization problem then consists of solving the discrete minimization problem,

{\bm{\theta}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}=\arg\min_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac trial}}}\in{\mathscr{C}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac grid}}}}{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}})\quad,\quad{\bm{\theta}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}=(h^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}_{1,{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}}\,,h^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}_{2,{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}}\,,\zeta^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}_{s,{\hbox{{\ppppcarac trial}}}})\,.

(8.6)

The value of
{\bm{\beta}}
corresponding to the optimal value
{\bm{\theta}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
is denoted by
{\bm{\beta}}_{{\hbox{{\pppcarac trial}}}}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
.

Estimation of
{\bm{\beta}}^{\,j}
for
{\bm{\theta}}^{\,j}\in{\mathscr{C}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac grid}}}

Let
\{{\mathbb{x}}^{i},i=1,\ldots,n_{d}\}
denote the set of input vectors from the training dataset. For notational simplicity, we write
{\bm{\theta}}^{\,j}
instead of

For each

\hat{\textbf{A}}^{i,j}=\hat{\textbf{f}}(\,[\hat{\textbf{W}}^{j}]\,\hat{\textbf{A}}^{i,j}+[\hat{\textbf{W}}^{{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}},j}]\,{\mathbb{x}}^{i}+\hat{\textbf{b}}^{j})\quad,\quad{\bm{\theta}}^{\,j}\in{\mathscr{C}}_{\hbox{{\pppcarac grid}}}\quad,\quad j\in\{1,\ldots,n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac grid}}}\}\,,

(8.7)

where
[\hat{\textbf{W}}^{j}]=[\hat{O}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{\,j}}]\,[{\textbf{W}}^{{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{\,j}}]\,[\hat{O}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{\,j}}]^{T}

For each

by temporarily setting

(v) Numerical noise and stability of hyperparameter optimization
.
During the optimization of hyperparameters via gradient descent, small fluctuations in the computed loss function were systematically observed, despite smooth and stable convergence of the hyperparameters. Such fluctuations originate primarily from numerical noise inherent in floating-point arithmetic, amplified significantly by evaluations of nonlinear activation function, particularly for
f(z)=tanh(z)
, and to a lesser extend for
f(z)=z/(1+|z|)
) in their saturation regions. The parallel implementation on multiple CPU cores and the utilization of GPUs exacerbate these variations due to differences in the order of floating-point arithmetic operations across computational threads or GPU kernels. Such non-deterministic ordering introduces hardware-dependent stochastic perturbations that manifest as numerical noise.
Remarkably, even though the instantaneous loss values exhibit small fluctuations, the hyperparameters themselves converge smoothly. This stability arises because the hyperparameter update rule, based on gradient computations obtained via central finite differences, inherently averages out these independent numerical perturbations. The central difference approximation, symmetric by construction, effectively reduces noise in the gradient estimate, ensuring unbiasedness of gradient computations on average, provided hyperparameter updates are sufficiently incremental.
It should be emphasized that this explanation relies upon the assumptions that the numerical noise is unbiased, independent across iterations, and small relative to the scale of hyperparameter updates. Under these conditions, gradient-based hyperparameter optimization acts implicitly as a denoising mechanism, resulting in robust convergence despite observed fluctuations in the loss function.

9.2
Criterion
\hbox{{\ppcarac Cr}}_{o}
for assessing overfitting in random neural networks

Traditional statistical learning theory connects overfitting to a trade-off between model complexity and data availability.
In classical models, overfitting typically occurs when a model

captures spurious fluctuations

or "noise" in the training data, leading to poor generalization.
However, in the present framework, the situation is fundamentally different. The randomness is not observational noise, but rather an inherent structural feature of the model, resulting from the stochastic generation of network configurations. Consequently, overfitting must be interpreted through a new perspective: not as fitting noise, but as selecting hyperparameters that induce overly specialized, improbable network structures.
The optimal negative log-likelihood,
{\hbox{\pcarac NLL}}_{\hbox{\ppppcarac ANN}}^{\,\hbox{{\pppcarac opt}}}
, computed using Eq. (
8.14
) on the training dataset of size
n_{d}
, and the negative log-likelihood,
{\hbox{\pcarac NLL}}_{\hbox{\ppppcarac ANN}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac test}}}
, computed on an independent test dataset of size
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}}
using Eq. (
9.1
), provide fundamental insights into the predictive performance and potential overfitting of the random neural network.
Given the high complexity of the stochastic neural architecture generated by hyperparameter
{\bm{\theta}}
in
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\beta}})
, each configuration can approximate complex functions (see Section
10
), resulting in a potentially significant discrepancy between training and testing performance due to over-specialized random architectures. To quantify this form of overfitting, distinct from classical parameter-driven overfitting, we refine the criterion
\hbox{{\ppcarac Cr}}_{o}
as follows:

\hbox{{\ppcarac Cr}}_{o}=\frac{1}{|{\hbox{\pcarac NLL}}_{\hbox{\ppppcarac ANN}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac test}}}|}\times\Bigg|\frac{n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}}}{n_{d}}\,{\hbox{\pcarac NLL}}_{\hbox{\ppppcarac ANN}}^{\,\hbox{{\pppcarac opt}}}-{\hbox{\pcarac NLL}}_{\hbox{\ppppcarac ANN}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac test}}}\Bigg|\,.

(9.2)

This criterion measures the relative discrepancy between the scaled training and test negative log-likelihood values. Because the random network has no intrinsic regularization and exhibits architectural flexibility,
\hbox{{\ppcarac Cr}}_{o}
serves as a statistical test to evaluate whether the hyperparameter selection
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
leads to over-specialized random architectures.
Under the assumption that the training and test datasets are composed of independent and identically distributed samples (as is the case here), and with a sufficiently large test set (for instance,
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}}=n_{d}/5
), the criterion
\hbox{{\ppcarac Cr}}_{o}
provides robust insight into generalization. A value
\hbox{{\ppcarac Cr}}_{o}\ll 1
indicates no significant overfitting: the network generalizes well beyond the training data. In contrast, a large value of
\hbox{{\ppcarac Cr}}_{o}
reveals overfitting due to the selection of hyperparameters that induce random network realizations overly tailored to the training set.

9.4
Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS)

The Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS)
[
100
,
101
,
102
]
is a strictly proper scoring rule that quantifies the accuracy of a predictive cumulative distribution function (CDF) by comparing it to an observed outcome. Unlike the NLL criterion, which evaluates the pointwise likelihood, the CRPS captures the entire shape of the predictive distribution, making it particularly effective for assessing non-Gaussian outputs. It complements the NLL and confidence interval-based criteria by measuring how well the full predictive distribution aligns with the observed data.
For a given input
{\mathbb{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac in}}}}
and a selected output component

{\mathbb{Y}}_{k}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{\,\hbox{{\pppcarac opt}}}\,|\,{\mathbb{x}})
be the

ANN

F_{k}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}(\cdot\,|\,{\mathbb{x}})
be its cumulative distribution function (CDF). Given an observed value
{\mathbb{y}}_{k}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac obs}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the CRPS is defined by

{\hbox{{\ppcarac CRPS}}}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}_{k}({\mathbb{x}})=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(F_{k}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}(z\,|\,{\mathbb{x}})-\mathbf{1}_{]-\infty,{\mathbb{y}}_{k}^{\hbox{{\pppppcarac obs}}}]}(z)\right)^{2}dz\,,

(9.7)

where
\mathbf{1}_{]-\infty,y]}
denotes the Heaviside function, equal to
1
is
z\leq y
and
0
otherwise.
Since
F_{k}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}
is not available in closed form but can be sampled via
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}
independent realizations
\{{\mathbb{y}}_{k}^{\ell}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}\,|\,{\mathbb{x}})\}_{\ell=1}^{n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}}
, we use the standard Monte Carlo approximation

{\hbox{{\ppcarac CRPS}}}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}_{k}({\mathbb{x}})\approx\frac{1}{n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}}\left|{\mathbb{y}}_{k}^{\ell}-{\mathbb{y}}_{k}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac obs}}}\right|-\frac{1}{2n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sim}}}}|{\mathbb{y}}_{k}^{\ell}-{\mathbb{y}}_{k}^{\ell^{\prime}}|\,,

(9.8)

where

10
Mathematical analysis of the proposed random neural network

The present section introduces the first mathematical elements that characterize the proposed stochastic neural model from a theoretical perspective. The emphasis is not on deriving exhaustive results or closed-form theorems but on establishing foundational properties that substantiate the internal coherence, expressive capacity, and well-posedness of the architecture as a probabilistic function generator. In particular, we identify and prove structural properties related to the existence and uniqueness of the latent field, the measurability of the random architecture generator, and the statistical consistency of the induced stochastic mappings. These results already support a
preliminary characterization, in a nonparametric sense, of the expressive variability and functional diversity of the model.

Several deeper questions, such as the full characterization of the induced function class, identifiability of the generative hyperparameters, and convergence properties of the supervised learning estimator, remain open. These are mathematically subtle and require further development. Nevertheless, the analysis presented here provides a rigorous starting point for formalizing the proposed model as a geometric and stochastic learning framework grounded in applied mathematics.

10.1
Expressive stochastic capacity of the random neural network model

We consider the supervised learning of an unknown random mapping

{\mathbb{x}}\mapsto{\mathbb{F}}({\mathbb{x}}):{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac in}}}}\to L^{2}_{\mathcal{P}}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}}})\,,

defined on the probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{T}},{\mathcal{P}})
, for which only a finite training dataset
\{({\mathbb{x}}^{i},{\mathbb{y}}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{n_{d}}
is available, where each
{\mathbb{y}}^{i}={\mathbb{F}}({\mathbb{x}}^{i};\omega_{i})
is a realization associated with
\omega_{i}\in\Omega
.

The random mapping

{\mathbb{F}}
itself is not known and cannot be queried outside of the given training dataset.
In Sections
2
to
7
, we have constructed a family of random neural network surrogates defined by random mappings

\left\{{\mathbb{x}}\mapsto{\mathbb{F}}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}({\mathbb{x}};{\bm{\theta}}_{t}):{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac in}}}}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L^{2}_{\mathcal{P}}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}}})}\,\,,\,\,{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\beta}})\in{\mathscr{C}}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}}\right\}\,,

structured via latent random fields on a compact, boundaryless, multiply-connected manifold. Our goal is to define a notion of
expressive stochastic capacity
for this model class that is independent of any specific
{\mathbb{F}}
and that characterizes the expressive capacity of the architecture class.

To this end, we define the expressive stochastic capacity of the model class

\{{\mathbb{F}}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}(\cdot\,;{\bm{\theta}}_{t}):{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\in{\mathscr{C}}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\}

as the
density
of this set in the Hilbert space,

{\mathbb{H}}=L^{2}_{P_{\mathbb{X}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac in}}}}\,;\,L^{2}_{\mathcal{P}}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}}}))\,,

where
P_{\mathbb{X}}
is the probability measure of the random input
{\mathbb{X}}
. Vector space
{\mathbb{H}}
is the Hilbert space of measurable functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac in}}}}
with values in
L^{2}_{\mathcal{P}}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}}})
, square integrable with respect to
P_{\mathbb{X}}
. Therefore, the model class is said to possess expressive stochastic capacity if, for every target mapping
{\mathbb{F}}\in{\mathbb{H}}
and every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a parameter vector
{\bm{\theta}}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}\in{\mathscr{C}}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}
such that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac in}}}}}E\left\{\,\|\,{\mathbb{F}}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}({\mathbb{x}};{\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}})-{\mathbb{F}}({\mathbb{x}})\,\|^{2}\right\}\,P_{\mathbb{X}}(d{\mathbb{x}})<\varepsilon^{2}.

This capacity condition characterizes the approximation power of the model class independently of any specific training dataset or optimization method. In practice, the expressive stochastic capacity of the model could be ensured by constructing
{\mathbb{F}}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}({\mathbb{x}};{\bm{\theta}}_{t})
via a chaos expansion of the form

{\mathbb{F}}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}({\mathbb{x}};{\bm{\theta}}_{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{a}}_{k}({\mathbb{x}}\,;{\bm{\theta}}_{t})\,\xi_{k}
,

where
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k}
is a Hilbert basis in
L^{2}_{\mathcal{P}}(\Omega)
, and where the coefficient functions

{\mathbb{a}}_{k}(\cdot\,;{\bm{\theta}}_{t})
with values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac out}}}}

are drawn from a class of expressive stochastic function generators.
Under suitable conditions, the resulting model class forms a dense subset of
{\mathbb{H}}
, thereby exhibiting strong representational capacity of the random neural network architecture.

Basing a proof on such a chaos expansion seems difficult, and since there are three main ingredients in the proposed random neural network model which, by composition, give
{\mathbb{F}}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}(\cdot;{\bm{\theta}}_{t})
, we propose to prove separately the following four properties:

•

The expressive stochastic approximation of centered, second-order, Gaussian, real-valued random fields indexed by
{\mathscr{S}}
, and differentiability of the associated SPDE solutions with respect to the hyperparameter vector.

•

The expressive stochastic encoding of the probability measure of the random weight matrix.

•

The differentiability of the solution of the constrained random neural network equation.

Each of the following three sections addresses these three points.

Remark 4. Constructive choice of the sequence
b^{(n)}_{ij}
supporting expressive stochastic capability
.
In this remark, we clarify a constructive choice of the sequence
\{b^{(n)}_{ij}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
that supports the expressive stochastic capability condition of Proposition
2
. The objective is to provide an explicit rule ensuring that the coefficient sequences
\{f^{(n)}_{ij\,,\,\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, defined
by Eq. (
10.7
),

belong to the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})
and exhibit fast tail decay.

For sufficiently large

, a suitable constructive choice for the sequence
b^{(n)}_{ij}
is

b^{(n)}_{ij}\sim\gamma\,n^{-r}\quad,\quad r>0\quad,\quad 0<\gamma<+\infty\,.

(10.13)

With this explicit definition, the Hermite chaos expansion coefficients from Eq. (
10.7
) become

f^{(n)}_{ij\,,\,\alpha}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\frac{\sqrt{(2\alpha)!}}{\alpha!}\frac{(-1)^{\alpha}}{n^{r\alpha}\,(1/\gamma+2/n^{r})^{\alpha+1/2}}\,,

(10.14)

Each sequence
\{f^{(n)}_{ij\,,\,\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is clearly square-summable, hence belongs to
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})
.
The factorial growth in the numerator

is dominated by the exponential-type decay in the denominator as
\alpha\to\infty
(for fixed
n
), which guarantees convergence of
\sum_{\alpha\geq 0}|f^{(n)}_{ij\,,\,\alpha}|^{2}
.
Moreover, as
n
varies, the family
\big\{\{f^{(n)}_{ij\,,\,\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}}\big\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is nontrivial and provides a flexible range of coefficient profiles (not confined to a single fixed finite-dimensional subspace), which is sufficient for the entrywise
L^{2}
-approximation scheme used in Proposition
2
.
In summary, the choice
b^{(n)}_{ij}=\gamma\,n^{-r}
supplies a deterministic, explicit rule yielding square-summable Hermite coefficients with fast tail decay and parameter continuity, thereby supporting the expressive stochastic encoding employed in Proposition
2
.

Remark 5. Behavior of the hyperparameter
\zeta_{s}^{(n)}
.
We examine a particular asymptotic behavior of
a^{(n)}_{ij}
based on the Gaussian structure of the random vector
{\textbf{U}}^{(n)}=(U^{(n)}_{1},\ldots,U^{(n)}_{N})
, where
U^{(n)}_{j}=U^{m}({\textbf{x}}^{j}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{\,(n)}};{\bm{\theta}}^{\,(n)})
. Let
[C_{\textbf{U}}^{(n)}]
denote its covariance matrix. Since
{\textbf{U}}^{(n)}
is centered, we have

(\sigma_{ij}^{(n)})^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(U_{i}^{(n)}-U_{j}^{(n)})=[C_{\textbf{U}}^{(n)}]_{ii}+[C_{\textbf{U}}^{(n)}]_{jj}-2\,[C_{\textbf{U}}^{(n)}]_{ij}
, and
therefore,

a^{(n)}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2\,\sigma^{2}_{{\textbf{S}}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{\,(n)}}}}[C_{\textbf{U}}^{(n)}]_{ii}+[C_{\textbf{U}}^{(n)}]_{jj}-2\,[C_{\textbf{U}}^{(n)}]_{ij}\,.

If
[C_{\textbf{U}}^{(n)}]_{ii}\to C_{\infty}>
and
[C_{\textbf{U}}^{(n)}]_{ij}\to C_{\infty}
for all
i\neq j
as
n\to+\infty
, then
a_{ij}^{(n)}\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
.
This condition means that the covariance matrix
[C_{\textbf{U}}^{(n)}]
tends toward the rank-one matrix
C_{\infty}\,\mathbf{1}\otimes\mathbf{1}
, where all entries converge to the same positive constant. Equivalently, the differences
U_{i}^{(n)}-U_{j}^{(n)}
converge to zero

in
L^{2}_{\mathcal{P}}
, indicating a perfectly correlated limit.
If such a condition holds, then

(\sigma_{ij}^{(n)})^{2}\to 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad a_{ij}^{(n)}\to 0\quad\text{as }\,\,n\to+\infty\,.

(10.15)

Thus, assuming that
a_{ij}^{(n)}\sim n^{-p}
as
n\to+\infty
with
p>r
, we obtain

(\zeta_{s}^{(n)})^{2}\sim\gamma^{-1}\,n^{r-p}\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
,
which implies that
\zeta_{s}^{(n)}
should be a decreasing function of
n
.
This shows that, in the asymptotic regime where the covariance matrix
[C_{\textbf{U}}^{(n)}]
degenerates to a constant rank-one structure, the condition for expressive stochastic capability (i.e., the decay of
b_{ij}^{(n)}
) leads naturally to a vanishing
\zeta_{s}^{(n)}
. The practically observed convergence behavior of the optimization algorithm is consistent with a decreasing sequence
\zeta_{s}^{(n)}
.

10.4
Differentiability of the solution of the constrained random neural network equation

As explained in Step 3
of Section
8
-(iii), the use of the local optimization based on projected gradient descent requires the differentiability of the solution of the constrained random nonlinear neural network equation.
Here, we investigate the differentiability, with respect to the parameter vector
{\bm{\theta}}
, of the random vector
\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}
, defined implicitly by Eqs. (
7.4
) to (
7.6
), as the solution of the nonlinear random equation,

\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}=\hat{\textbf{f}}(\,[\hat{\textbf{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}]\,\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}+\hat{\mathbb{b}}_{\bm{\theta}})\,,

where
\hat{\textbf{f}}
is the element-wise hyperbolic tangent function,
\hat{\textbf{f}}({\textbf{z}})=\tanh(\bfz)
.
As stated

in Step 3 of
Section
8
-(iii), we treat the matrices
[\hat{O}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}]
,
[O_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}]
, and
[O_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}^{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}]
as fixed (that is, independent of
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
), where
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
is defined in Eq. (
8.13
). Therefore, we have,

[\hat{\textbf{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}]=[\hat{O}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}]\,[{\textbf{W}}^{{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}}_{\bm{\theta}}]\,[\hat{O}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}]^{T}\quad,\quad\hat{\mathbb{b}}_{\bm{\theta}}=[\hat{O}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}]\,[{\textbf{W}}^{{{\hbox{{\ppppcarac sp}}}}}_{\bm{\theta}}]\,[O^{\,{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}]^{T}\,{\mathbb{x}}+\hat{\textbf{b}}_{\bm{\theta}}\quad,\quad\hat{\textbf{b}}_{\bm{\theta}}=[\hat{h}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}]\,{\bm{\beta}}\quad,\quad[\hat{h}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}]=[\hat{O}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}]\,[h]\,.

Let us introduce the implicit function,

{\textbf{g}}(\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\theta}})=\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}-\hat{\textbf{f}}(\,[\hat{\textbf{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}]\,\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}+\hat{\mathbb{b}}_{\bm{\theta}})\,,

which defines the dependence of
\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}
on
{\bm{\theta}}
through the condition
{\textbf{g}}(\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\theta}})={\hbox{\bf 0}}
.
Since
\hat{\textbf{f}}=\tanh
is infinitely differentiable, the differentiability of
\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}
depends entirely on the invertibility of the Jacobian matrix of
g
with respect to
\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}
.
The Jacobian
[{\textbf{J}}_{\textbf{g}}]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{\hat{n}}
is given by

[{\textbf{J}}_{\textbf{g}}]=\frac{\partial{\textbf{g}}}{\partial\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}}=[I_{\hat{n}}]-[{\textbf{D}}_{\,\hat{\textbf{f}}}]\,[\hat{\textbf{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}]\,,

where
[{\textbf{D}}_{\,\hat{\textbf{f}}}]
is the diagonal matrix containing the derivatives of
\hat{\textbf{f}}
, defined by

[{\textbf{D}}_{\,\hat{\textbf{f}}}]_{ii}=1-\tanh^{2}((\,[\hat{\textbf{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}]\,\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}+\hat{\mathbb{b}}_{\bm{\theta}})_{i}),\quad i=1,\dots,\hat{n}\,.

This makes
[{\textbf{D}}_{\,\hat{\textbf{f}}}]
a strictly positive diagonal matrix, with entries satisfying
0<[{\textbf{D}}_{\,\hat{\textbf{f}}}]_{ii}\leq 1
for all
i
.
Hence, the Jacobian
[{\textbf{J}}_{\textbf{g}}]
is invertible if and only if the spectral radius satisfies

\rho(\,[{\textbf{D}}_{\,\hat{\textbf{f}}}]\,[\hat{\textbf{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}]\,)\,<\,1\,.

This condition ensures local uniqueness and differentiability via the implicit function theorem.
Given the structure of
[\hat{\textbf{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}]
(symmetric, with zero diagonal and strictly positive off-diagonal entries), this spectral condition is typically satisfied under mild assumptions on the parameter bounds.
By invoking the implicit function theorem, we conclude that
\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}
is locally differentiable with respect to
{\bm{\theta}}
.
Moreover, the theorem provides the explicit expression for the derivative,

\frac{\partial\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}=-[{\textbf{J}}_{\textbf{g}}]^{-1}\,\frac{\partial{\textbf{g}}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}\quad,\quad\frac{\partial{\textbf{g}}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}=-[{\textbf{D}}_{\,\hat{\textbf{f}}}]\,\left(\frac{\partial[\hat{\textbf{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}]}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}+\frac{\partial\hat{\mathbb{b}}_{\bm{\theta}}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}\right)\,.

Since both
[\hat{\textbf{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}]
and
\hat{\mathbb{b}}_{\bm{\theta}}
are differentiable with respect to
{\bm{\theta}}
,
it follows that
\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}
is smoothly differentiable with respect to
{\bm{\theta}}
.

Remark 6. Probabilistic interpretation and existence of solutions
.
Since
\hat{\textbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}}
is a random vector, the differentiability with respect to
{\bm{\theta}}
described above holds in an almost-sure sense. Consequently, the derivatives obtained via the implicit function theorem are themselves random variables, allowing for the study of statistical quantities.
The differentiability analysis relies on the existence of at least one solution, which is guaranteed by the Brouwer fixed-point theorem, as discussed in Section
7
-(iv-1).

(11.1)

(i) Non-smooth differentiable case
.
The procedure is as follows:
Generate two random matrices with independent standard normal entries. Construct a projection that removes the mean from each column, ensuring each column is mean-free. Apply this projection to the matrices, and orthonormalize the resulting mean-free columns using an economy-size QR decomposition. This produces two independent sets of orthonormal, mean-free vectors satisfying Eq. (
11.1

(ii) Smooth differentiable case

For the smooth differentiable case, we construct these bases using trigonometric products,

\phi_{m_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac tor}}},n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac tor}}}}^{(\ell)}(u,v)=\begin{cases}c\,\cos(m_\tor\, \uc)\,\cos(n_\tor\, \vc),&\ell=1,\cr c\,\sin(m_\tor\, \uc)\,\cos(n_\tor\, \vc),&\ell=2,\cr c\,\cos(m_\tor\, \uc)\,\sin(n_\tor\, \vc),&\ell=3,\cr c\,\sin(m_\tor\, \uc)\,\sin(n_\tor\, \vc),&\ell=4,\cr\end{cases}

Training the vector-valued hyperparameter from network supervision

(i) Defining the training and test datasets
.
The supervised learning is conducted as described in Section
8
. The training dataset
\{({\mathbb{x}}^{i},{\mathbb{y}}^{i}),i=1,\ldots,n_{d}\}
consists of
n_{d}=3000
samples in
{\mathbb{R}}^{20}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{100}
, while the test dataset

\{({\mathbb{x}}^{i}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}},{\mathbb{y}}^{i}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}}),i=1,\ldots,n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}}\}
consists of
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}}=600
samples. For a given input
{\mathbb{x}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{20}
, the probabilistic model defines the random output
{\mathbb{Y}}|{\mathbb{X}}={\mathbb{x}}
as an
{\mathbb{R}}^{100}
-valued random variable that is highly non-Gaussian.
Both datasets are generated from the same complex probabilistic model using non linear mappings and polynomial chaos expansions. These datasets can be obtained upon request to the author. Note that, as previously explained, for both datasets the only available information consists, for each
{\mathbb{x}}^{i}
(or
{\mathbb{x}}^{i}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}})
, of a single realization
{\mathbb{y}}^{i}
(or
{\mathbb{y}}^{i}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}}
) of the random vector
{\mathbb{Y}}|{\mathbb{X}}={\mathbb{x}}^{i}
(or
{\mathbb{Y}}|{\mathbb{X}}={\mathbb{x}}^{i}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}}
). This constitutes an additional difficulty for training the neural network model.

(ii) Additional details on model and algorithm choices
.

The dimension of the vector
{\bm{\beta}}
is chosen as

n_{b}=\hat{n}=N-n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}=200-20=180
.
The fixed-point iteration defined

in Eq. (
7.10
) is used

to solve the random nonlinear neural network equation, with a relaxation parameter
\alpha=0.5
and a tolerance
\varepsilon=0.01
. Convergence was consistently achieved within a few dozen iterations. We also testes smaller values of
\varepsilon
, which increased the number of iterations but did not significantly affect
the final optimized
value
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{\,\hbox{{\ppppcarac opt}}}
of the hyperparameter
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
. In all cases, the number of iterations remainded below
500
.
In cases

, we use the smooth differentiable model of the functions
{\mathbb{h}}^{(k,j)}
on
{\mathscr{S}}
, as defined in Section
11.2
-(ii).

(i) Neuron placement induced by the latent field.

This allocation strategy reflects an intrinsic adaptation of the architecture to the epistemic structure of the latent field, concentrating representational capacity in regions of high uncertainty where functional variability is most pronounced.

(iv) Generalization and overfitting analysis

The overfitting analysis is based on the normalized comparison of negative log-likelihoods (NLL) computed on the training and test datasets through the criterion

The criterion

quantifies the normalized discrepancy between the training and test likelihoods after correcting for dataset size. The value
\hbox{{\ppcarac Cr}}_{o}=0.028\ll 1
indicates a relatively small deviation between train and test losses, suggesting an absence of significant overfitting.
Importantly, the low value of
\hbox{{\ppcarac Cr}}_{o}
is particularly significant given the non-Gaussian nature and high-dimensionality of the output space (here,
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}=100
), where overfitting is typically more likely due to the curse of dimensionality.
This effect is mitigated in our model by the geometric constraint imposed on neuron placement and connectivity, which guides capacity toward regions of functional complexity and suppresses overparameterization.
Classical neural network models tend to overfit when data are sparse relative to the number of parameters or when outputs are not Gaussian. The present random ANN framework, relying on a generative latent geometry and a limited number of neurons, seems to maintain generalization capability even in these conditions.
The overfitting analysis supports the statistical soundness of the random ANN model in high dimensions. Despite the relatively small network size, the model generalizes well, demonstrating its robustness for learning non-Gaussian conditional distributions with high output dimension.

(v) Confidence interval accuracy for non-Gaussian output random systems in high dimensions

Let us give a brief positioning related to the confidence interval accuracy.
In the context of modeling conditional random variables
{\mathbb{Y}}\,|\,{\mathbb{x}}
with strongly non-Gaussian distributions using neural networks with random weights, the accurate estimation of
p_{c}
-confidence intervals
\hbox{{\pcarac CI}}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}_{k}({\mathbb{x}})=[\alpha^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}_{k}({\mathbb{x}})\,,\beta^{\,{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}}_{k}({\mathbb{x}})]
for each component
{\mathbb{Y}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathbb{x}}
remains a challenging task. When employing state-of-the-art methodologies such as Bayesian neural networks with approximate inference
[
13
,
108
]
, Monte Carlo dropout
[
14
]
, or deep ensembles
[
109
]
, empirical coverage errors for nominal
p_{c}=95\%
intervals typically range between
5\%
and
15\%
. Quantile regression networks
[
110
]
often exhibit higher miscoverage rates in the
10\%-20\%
range due to quantile crossing and data sparsity. More expressive conditional density estimators such as normalizing flows
[
111
]
, multimodal neural processes
[
112
]
, or neural conditional probability models based on operator learning
[
113
]
can reduce this error below
10\%
when adequately trained on large datasets. Recently, likelihood-ratio-based confidence intervals have been proposed to construct asymmetric, data-adaptive intervals with theoretical guarantees
[
114
]
.
In contrast, neural architectures with purely random weights (e.g., Extreme Learning Machines or random feature models) tend to yield significantly less reliable confidence intervals, with typical errors exceeding
20\%
unless complemented with Bayesian output layers, bootstrap aggregation, or conformal prediction techniques
[
115
,
116
]
.

In relatively higher-dimensional settings, such as our illustrative example with input dimension
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac in}}}=20
and output dimension
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}=100
, the complexity of modeling the non-Gaussian conditional distribution
{\mathbb{Y}}\,|\,{\mathbb{x}}
increases considerably.

To estimate per-component confidence intervals reliably in such regimes, a significantly larger number of hidden neurons is required. Based on random feature approximation theory
[
7
,
117
]
, quantile resolution requirements, and multimodal density complexity, we estimate that a random-weight single hidden layer neural network would require between
5\,000
and
15\,000
hidden units to accurately resolve
95\%
confidence intervals for all
n_{\hbox{{\pppcarac out}}}
components. This scaling is consistent with theoretical bounds on random feature-based kernel approximations
[
118
]
, recent asymptotic results on random-feature models in high-dimensional regimes
[
119
,
120
]
, and the need to represent high-entropy conditional output distributions with adequate resolution
[
121
]
. The neuron count may be reduced when structural assumptions (that is, conditional independence, low-rank covariance
in
{\mathbb{Y}}|{\mathbb{x}}
) or compressive priors are exploited.

In this work, we have presented a random neural network architecture structured by latent random fields defined on compact, boundaryless, and multiply-connected manifolds, as introduced in the supervised learning framework. Using only
N=200
neurons with
25\%
connectivity, trained on a dataset of
n_{d}=1\,000

200

\hbox{{\pcarac CI}}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}_{k}({\mathbb{x}})
by separately evaluating the errors on the lower bounds
\alpha^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}_{k}({\mathbb{x}})
and the upper bounds
\beta^{\,{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}}_{k}({\mathbb{x}})
.
We obtained an empirical average miscoverage error of
20\%
on the lower bounds and
11\%
on the upper bounds, computed across all output components and test samples. These values represent average absolute deviations from the nominal
95\%
coverage rate and reflect pointwise empirical coverage performance.
This performance, achieved with significantly reduced network complexity, suggests that the geometric structure and stochastic flexibility introduced by the latent random field framework compensate for the lower neuron count. These results indicate that complex, high-dimensional, non-Gaussian conditional distributions can be effectively learned and quantified even with relatively compact random architectures, provided that structural priors are suitably exploited.

(vi) Probabilistic validation using CRPS

As with the confidence interval analysis, we begin by providing a brief positioning of the CRPS criterion in the context of probabilistic model validation.

The Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) is widely recognized as a strictly proper scoring rule for validating probabilistic predictions, particularly in non-Gaussian and high-dimensional contexts
[
102
,
100
]
. It quantifies the discrepancy between a predicted cumulative distribution function (CDF) and the empirical step function associated with an observed outcome by integrating the squared difference across the real line. In contrast to pointwise metrics such as the negative log-likelihood (NLL), CRPS captures the full shape of the predictive distribution, making it sensitive to both bias and dispersion. It is robust to heavy tails, supports diagnostic insight in multimodal settings, and has been successfully applied in various domains, including weather forecasting
[
101
]
, Bayesian deep learning
[
109
]
, and operator learning
[
122
]
.
For univariate predictions on normalized or moderately scaled variables, well-calibrated probabilistic models typically yield CRPS values between
0.1
and
0.5
, with smaller values indicating better probabilistic fit. For instance, in high-dimensional regression problems with non-Gaussian targets, modern approaches such as deep ensembles
[
109
]
, quantile flows
[
110
]
, or diffusion-based conditional models
[
112
]
report CRPS values ranging from
0.2
to
0.5
, depending on task complexity, data availability, and output dimensionality. In such regimes, relative CRPS comparisons tend to be more informative than absolute values, especially when evaluating models of differing architectural complexity or inference paradigms.

The accuracy of the random ANN surrogate was assessed using the CRPS score
{\hbox{{\ppcarac CRPS}}}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}}
, and compared with
{\hbox{{\ppcarac CRPS}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac train}}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}}
, computed using a nonparametric Gaussian Kernel Density Estimation (GKDE) reference model based on the training dataset. For a random ANN with
N=200
neurons,
n_{d}=1000
training samples,
n_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}}=200
test samples, and
n_{h}=30
, we obtained

{\hbox{{\ppcarac CRPS}}}^{\hbox{\pppppcarac ANN}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}}=0.499
and
{\hbox{{\ppcarac CRPS}}}^{\hbox{{\ppppcarac train}}}_{\hbox{{\ppppcarac test}}}=0.504
,
with a relative discrepancy, computed using Eq. (
9.10
), given by
\hbox{{\ppcarac Cr}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac CRPS}}}=0.214
(for the best model, we obtain
\hbox{{\ppcarac Cr}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac CRPS}}}=0.194
).

The CRPS values are remarkably close in absolute terms, differing by only
0.005
. This numerical proximity demonstrates that the predictive distribution generated by the random ANN is statistically nearly equivalent to the nonparametric GKDE benchmark,
which serves as a biased but smooth reference estimator,
despite the model’s structural simplicity and compactness. Although the normalized criterion
\hbox{{\ppcarac Cr}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac CRPS}}}
appears moderate at
21.4\%
(and
19.4.4\%
for the best model), it is primarily a consequence of normalization by small CRPS values, which magnifies even small absolute differences. As such,
\hbox{{\ppcarac Cr}}_{\hbox{{\pppppcarac CRPS}}}
should be interpreted with care: its value reflects relative sensitivity rather than a substantial discrepancy in forecast skill.

That the random ANN achieves this level of accuracy using only
200
neurons and
25\%
connectivity underscores the expressive power of the model, particularly when its architecture is informed by latent geometric priors. This result supports the hypothesis that structured randomness can act as an effective inductive bias, enabling accurate distributional modeling even in high-dimensional, non-Gaussian settings with limited training data.

These findings reinforce the conclusions drawn from the NLL and confidence interval analyses. The proposed random ANN architecture, despite the absence of variational inference layers or deep hierarchical components, provides a robust and statistically valid surrogate model for uncertainty quantification. Its CRPS-based performance confirms its capacity to learn and represent complex conditional distributions with a level of fidelity comparable to nonparametric methods, while remaining computationally efficient and architecturally compact.

(vi) Efficiency and comparative evaluation

The results obtained for the random neural architecture structured by latent random fields on compact boundaryless multiply-connected manifolds are evaluated with respect to established probabilistic performance criteria and the state of the art in high-dimensional non-Gaussian modeling. Using the results of the best model, the following points summarize the key findings:

Global distributional accuracy
. The model yields a CRPS score of

Local distributional discrepancy
. The relative discrepancy criterion yields a value of

Importantly, the global match in CRPS shows that the model’s deviations are not systematically biased but arise from localized fluctuations, which are natural in such settings.

Modeling efficiency
. The model uses only
200

25\%
connectivity (i.e., sparse architecture), yet
successfully maintains predictive fidelity across

test samples after
training on

, thereby

This represents a highly efficient use of computational and statistical resources. The architectural regularization introduced by the latent manifold structure plays a key role in enabling generalization in such a high-dimensional stochastic setting. Note that this efficiency also holds for only

Conclusions

Before summarizing the main contributions, we clarify the epistemological intent of this work. Rather than proposing a refinement of existing neural architectures or attempting to replace standard models through performance gains, this paper introduces a distinct paradigm in which the architecture of the neural system is itself a stochastic object, a realization of a generative process governed by latent anisotropic fields defined over compact, boundaryless, multiply-connected manifolds. In this framework, neurons, their connectivity, and synaptic weights emerge jointly from a spatially structured random field, incorporating topological and geometric uncertainty as intrinsic components of the model.
This shift reframes supervised learning as inference over a generative process that produces random function approximators, rather than optimization within a fixed architectural space. It opens a principled new axis of investigation, conceptually distinct from mainstream approaches rooted in deterministic network designs and parametric weight fitting.
This work introduced a new class of geometrically structured random neural networks defined on compact, boundaryless, multiply-connected manifolds, driven by latent anisotropic Gaussian fields and inhomogeneous Poisson point processes. The proposed architecture is not fixed but stochastically generated, yielding both neuron placements and synaptic weights as emergent properties of a latent spatial process. Supervised learning is recast as the inference of generative hyperparameters, rather than optimization over fixed weights, enabling a principled treatment of architectural uncertainty.
This work also initiates a mathematical analysis of a stochastic neural model whose architecture is generated by a latent Gaussian random field on a compact manifold. While a complete theoretical characterization remains open, the results already establish fundamental properties, such as a form of of expressive stochastic capability, supporting the model internal coherence and expressive potential. These initial developments lay the groundwork for a broader mathematical theory of geometry-driven stochastic learning systems.
The presented numerical experiments do not aim to benchmark predictive accuracy or compare against existing architectures. Instead, we provide diagnostic illustrations that elucidate how topological, anisotropic, and latent stochastic parameters influence the emergent neural architecture and its induced function class. These numerical experiments validated the expressivity and efficiency of the model in a challenging test case involving
100
-dimensional outputs with strong non-Gaussian features.

The model demonstrates strong performance under severe resource constraints (e.g.,
200
neurons,
25\%
connectivity), achieving CRPS scores on par (in the considered setting) with those typically reported for significantly larger architectures, such as Bayesian deep ensembles, deep Gaussian processes, or process models with input-dependent noise.

Moreover, the model maintained high-quality empirical coverage of confidence intervals and exhibited minimal overfitting, highlighting its ability to generalize from limited data in high-dimensional output spaces.
The CRPS-based comparisons and overfitting diagnostics support the conclusion that geometric priors, introduced via latent manifold fields, act as powerful inductive biases. They allow the model to concentrate its representational capacity on informative regions of the input manifold, avoiding the overparameterization
that often affects
conventional neural approaches. Furthermore, the model supports a statistically coherent treatment of output uncertainty without resorting to variational inference, dropout sampling, or deep ensembling.
While promising, several ways improvement remain. First, the training algorithm could be further optimized. Second, scaling the method to higher-dimensional manifolds or more complex output structures (e.g., tensor-valued responses, sequential data) will require algorithmic and computational refinement.
Future directions include embedding this geometry-driven framework within deep hierarchical compositions, or coupling it with learned activation dynamics via neural ODEs or latent diffusion processes. Such hybrid models may combine the generalization ability of structured randomness with the representational depth of modern architectures.
Finally, hybridizing this latent-geometric approach with learned activation dynamics or deep hierarchical structures may yield architectures that combine statistical robustness with greater modeling depth.
It is worth noting that the proposed model differs fundamentally from traditional Bayesian neural networks or GP-based methods, which define uncertainty in parameter or function space under fixed architectures.

In contrast, our approach places a prior on the architecture itself, which in turn induces a distribution over functions through geometric variability, essentially a function level prior shaped by manifold structure.

This may complement recent developments in function-space inference by offering an alternative, geometry-centered route to model uncertainty.
In summary, this work establishes a probabilistic foundation for tractable and geometry-aware uncertainty quantification in neural systems, with interpretable inductive structure and scalable simulation algorithms. The integration of random topology, latent-field-driven weights, and manifold-based geometry offers a novel path toward statistically principled learning in data-scarce, geometrically structured domains.

This work also opens a new research direction in the development of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) with randomized neural architectures that are statistically consistent with the structure of inhomogeneous physical systems. By leveraging latent random fields on manifolds as stochastic germs for generating PDE coefficients, the network architecture becomes naturally adapted to both the geometry and boundary conditions of the problem domain. This formulation establishes a principled framework for aligning the architecture of PINNs with the physical and probabilistic properties of the target system, laying the groundwork for ongoing developments in inverse modeling, uncertainty quantification, and data-driven discovery in complex stochastic media.
