New and Improved Bounds for Markov Paging
Chirag Pabbaraju, Ali Vakilian

Abstract
In the Markov paging model, one assumes that page requests are drawn from a Markov chain over the pages in memory, and the goal is to maintain a fast cache that suffers few page faults in expectation. While computing the optimal online algorithm ( OPT ) (\mathrm{OPT}) for this problem naively takes time exponential in the size of the cache, the best-known polynomial-time approximation algorithm is the dominating distribution algorithm due to Lund, Phillips and Reingold (FOCS 1994), who showed that the algorithm is 4 4 -competitive against OPT \mathrm{OPT} . We substantially improve their analysis and show that the dominating distribution algorithm is in fact 2 2 -competitive against OPT \mathrm{OPT} . We also show a lower bound of 1.5907 1.5907 -competitiveness for this algorithm—to the best of our knowledge, no such lower bound was previously known.



1
Introduction

The online paging problem is a fundamental problem in the area of online
algorithms. There are
n
pages in slow memory, and requests for these pages arrive in a sequential manner. We are allowed to maintain a fast cache of size
k
, which initially comprises of some
k
pages. At time step
t
, if the page that is requested, say
p_{t}
, exists in the cache, this corresponds to a cache hit: we suffer a zero cost, the cache stays as is, and we move on to the next request. Otherwise, we incur a cache miss/page fault, suffer a unit cost, and are forced to evict some page from the cache so as to bring
p_{t}
from the slow memory into the cache. A paging algorithm/policy is then specified by how it chooses the page to evict whenever it suffers a cache miss.

An optimal offline algorithm for this problem is the algorithm that has entire knowledge of the page request sequence, and thereafter makes its eviction choices in a way that minimizes the total number of cache misses it incurs. The classical Farthest-in-Future algorithm
(
Belady 1966
)
, which at any cache miss, evicts the page that is next requested latest in the remainder of the sequence, is known to be an optimal offline policy. In the spirit of competitive analysis as introduced by
Sleator and Tarjan 1985
, one way to benchmark any online algorithm (i.e., one which does not have knowledge of the page request sequence) is to compute the ratio of the number of page faults that the algorithm suffers, to the number of page faults that the optimal offline algorithm suffers, on a
worst-case
sequence of page requests. It is known that any deterministic online algorithm has a worst-case ratio that is at least
k
, while any randomized online algorithm has a worst-case ratio of
\Omega(\log k)
.

Taking a slightly more optimistic view, one can also consider benchmarking an algorithm against the best
online
algorithm, that does not know beforehand the realized sequence of page requests. Additionally, in the context of beyond worst-case analysis, one can impose assumptions on the sequence of page requests to more accurately model the behavioral characteristics of real-world page requests on computers. For example, page requests generally follow the
locality of reference
principle, either in time (the next time a particular page is requested is close to its previous time) or in space (the page that will be requested next is likely to be in a nearby memory location).

The Markov Paging model, introduced by
Karlin et al. 1992
, is one of the ways to model such locality of reference in the page requests. The model assumes that page requests are drawn from a (known) Markov chain over the entire set of
n
pages. Once such a distributional assumption over the page requests is imposed, one can ask how an online paging algorithm fares, in comparison to the optimal online algorithm that suffers the smallest number of page misses
in expectation
over the draw of a page sequence from the distribution. As it turns out, one can compute such an optimal online algorithm, but only in time exponential in the cache size
k
. Therefore, one seeks efficient polynomial-time algorithms that are approximately optimal.

In 1994,
Lund et al. 1994
proposed an extremely elegant randomized algorithm for the Markov Paging problem, known as the dominating distribution algorithm. The dominating distribution algorithm, whenever it suffers a cache miss, evicts a random page drawn from a special distribution, which has the property that a randomly drawn page is likely to be next requested latest among all the existing pages in the cache. This algorithm, which has since become a popular textbook algorithm due to its simplicity (e.g., see Chapter 5 in
Borodin and El-Yaniv 2005
, or the lecture notes by
Roughgarden 2010
), runs in time polynomial in
k
; furthermore,
Lund et al. 1994
show that the algorithm suffers only 4 times more cache misses in expectation than the optimal online algorithm. Since then, this has remained state-of-the-art—we do not know any other polynomial-time algorithms that achieve a better performance guarantee. It has nevertheless been conjectured that the above guarantee for the dominating distribution algorithm is suboptimal (e.g., see Section 6.2 in
Roughgarden 2010
).

Our main result in this work establishes an improved upper bound for the dominating distribution algorithm.

Theorem 1
.

The dominating distribution algorithm suffers at most 2 times more cache misses in expectation compared to the optimal online algorithm in the Markov Paging model.

In fact, as mentioned in
Lund et al. 1994
, our guarantee for the performance of the dominating distribution algorithm holds more generally for
pairwise-predictive
distributions. These are distributions for which one can compute any time, for any pair of pages
p
and
q
in the cache, the probability that
p
is next requested before
q
in the future (conditioned on the most recent request).

While the dominating distribution algorithm is a randomized algorithm,
Lund et al. 1994
also propose and study a simple deterministic algorithm for the Markov Paging model, known as the median algorithm. On any cache miss, the median algorithm evicts the page in cache that has the largest median time at which it is requested next.
Lund et al. 1994
show that the median algorithm suffers at most 5 times more cache misses in expectation than the optimal online algorithm—to the best of our knowledge, this is the state of the art for deterministic algorithms. As an additional convenient consequence of our improved analysis, we are also able to improve the performance guarantee for the median algorithm.

Theorem 2
.

The median algorithm suffers at most 4 times more cache misses in expectation compared to the optimal online algorithm in the Markov Paging model.

Given the improved performance guarantee in
Theorem

1
, one can ask: is this the best bound that we can show for the dominating distribution algorithm? Or is there hope to show that it is
the
optimal online algorithm? A detail here is that the dominating distribution algorithm is defined as any algorithm satisfying a specific property (see (
4
)); in particular, there can exist different dominating distribution algorithms. Our upper bound (
Theorem

1
) holds uniformly for
all
dominating distribution algorithms. However, our next theorem shows a lower bound that at least one of these is significantly suboptimal.

Theorem 3
.

There exists a dominating distribution algorithm that suffers at least
1.5907
times more cache misses in expectation compared to the optimal online algorithm in the Markov Paging model.

Theorem

3
shows that one cannot hope to show optimality uniformly for all dominating distribution algorithms. To the best of our knowledge, no such lower bounds for the dominating distribution algorithm have been previously shown. While we believe that the bound in
Theorem

1
is the correct bound, it is an interesting open direction to close the gap between the upper and lower bound.

Finally, we also consider a setting where the algorithm does not exactly know the Markov chain generating the page requests, but only has access to a dataset of past page requests. In this case, one can use standard estimators from the literature to approximate the transition matrix of the Markov chain from this dataset. But can an approximate estimate of the transition matrix translate to a tight competitive ratio for a paging algorithm, and if so, how large of a dataset does it require to achieve it? 1.1
Other Related Work

There is a rich literature by this point on studying the online paging problem under assumptions on the permissible page requests that are supposed to model real-world scenarios (see e.g., the excellent surveys by
Irani 2005
and
Dorrigiv and López-Ortiz 2005
). In particular, several models are motivated by the aforementioned locality of reference principle. The access graph model, proposed originally by
Borodin et al. 1991
, and developed further in the works of
Irani et al. 1992
,
Fiat and Karlin 1995
and
Fiat and Mendel 1997
, assumes an underlying graph on the pages, which a request sequence is supposed to abide by. Namely, if a page
p_{t}
has been requested at time
t
, then only the neighbors of
p_{t}
in the graph may be requested at time
t+1
. In this sense, the Markov Paging model can be thought of as a probabilistic variant of the access graph model.

Torng 1998
models locality of reference based on the “working sets” concept, introduced by
Denning 1968
, and also shows how a finite lookahead of the page sequence can be used to obtain improved guarantees. A related form of competitive analysis, proposed by
Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou 2000
, assumes that there is a family of valid distributions, and page sequences are drawn from some distribution belonging to this family. The goal of an algorithm is then to be simultaneously competitive with the optimal algorithm for every distribution in the family. A particular family of interest suggested by
Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou 2000
(which is incomparable to the Markov Paging model) is that of
diffuse adversaries
, which has since been developed further in follow-up works by
Young 1998
;
Young 2000
and
Becchetti 2004
. Finally,
Angelopoulos and Schweitzer 2009
study paging under the
bijective analysis
framework, which compares the performance of two algorithms under bijections on page request sequences.

Background and Setup

2.1
Markov Paging

The Markov Paging model assumes that the page requests are drawn from a time-homogeneous Markov chain, whose state space is the set of all pages. More precisely, the Markov Paging model is specified by an initial distribution on the
n
pages, and a transition matrix
M\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
, where
M_{i,j}
specifies the probability that page
j
is requested next, conditioned on the most recently requested page being
i
. The form of the initial distribution will not be too important for our purposes, but for concreteness, we can assume it to be the uniform distribution on the
n
pages. It is typically assumed that the transition matrix
M
is completely known to the paging algorithm/page eviction policy—however, the realized page sequence is not. Upon drawing
T
page requests from the Markov chain, the objective function that a page eviction policy
\mathcal{A}
seeks to minimize is:

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal{A},T)]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{M,\mathcal{A}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathds{1}[\text{$\mathcal{A}$ suffers a cache miss at time $t$}]\right],

(1)

where the expectation is with respect to both the random page request sequence drawn from
M
, and the randomness in the policy
\mathcal{A}
. We denote by
\mathrm{OPT}
the policy that minimizes the objective in (
1
). For
c\geq 1
, an eviction policy
\mathcal{A}
is said to
c
-competitive against
\mathrm{OPT}
if for every
T
,
\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal{A},T)]\leq c\cdot\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathrm{OPT},T)]
.

It is worth emphasizing again that the competitive analysis above is with respect to the optimal
online
strategy, in that
\mathrm{OPT}
does not know beforehand the realized sequence of page requests. Contrast this to the best
offline
strategy, which also additionally knows the realized sequence of page requests. As mentioned previously, the Farthest-in-Future policy
(
Belady 1966
)
, which at any cache miss, evicts the page that is next requested latest in the remainder of the sequence, is known to be an optimal offline policy.

It is indeed possible to compute
\mathrm{OPT}
exactly—however, the best known ways do this are computationally very expensive, requiring an exponential amount of computation in
k
.

Theorem 4

(Theorems 1, 2 in
Karlin et al. 1992
)
.

The optimal online policy that minimizes
\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\cdot\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal{A},T)]
over all policies
\mathcal{A}
can be computed exactly by solving a linear program in
n\binom{n}{k}
variables. Furthermore, for any finite
T
, an optimal online policy that minimizes
\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal{A},T)]
can be computed in time
T\cdot n^{\Omega(k)}
.

In the latter case above when
T
is finite, the optimal online policy can be computed, for example, via dynamic programming (using the “Bellman operator”), where one writes the recurrence for taking a single step of the optimal policy, and unrolls time backwards from
T
. However, the state space in the dynamic program comprises of all
\{
cache state, requested page
\}
pairs—there are
n^{\Omega(k)}
many of these. Similarly, the linear program in the case where
T\to\infty
also comprises of a single variable for each such state. Unfortunately, we do not know of any better algorithms that are both optimal and run in polynomial time in
k
. Therefore, one seeks efficient polynomial time (in

c

\mathrm{OPT}
for

1

Towards this,
Karlin et al. 1992
showed that several intuitive eviction policies (e.g., on a cache miss, evict the page in cache that has the largest
expected

\Omega(k)
factor suboptimal compared to
\mathrm{OPT}
. They then showed a policy
\mathcal{A}
that is provably
O(1)
-competitive against
\mathrm{OPT}
; however, the constant in the

is somewhat large. Thereafter,
Lund et al. 1999

2.2
Dominating Distribution Algorithm

The dominating distribution algorithm, which was proposed by
Lund et al. 1999

Intuitively, we want

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{p\sim\mu,M}[\text{$q$ is next requested no later than $p$ }|\text{ $s$ is the most recently requested page}]\geq\frac{1}{2}.

\mathcal{D}
remain unchanged. It remains to reason about

. Because

1b

\mathcal{O}
.

Item

either decreases
\mathcal{O}
by 1 or causes no change to it.
While the request to

\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{O}
can increase by at most 1 due to this. In total,
\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{O}+\mathcal{U}
either increases or stays the same.

Case 4:
s

\mathcal{A}
’s cache.

This means that

Case 5:

\mathcal{A}

clears the charge that

by 1, whereas

Thus, in any way that the requested page

might not satisfy the condition,
\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{O}+\mathcal{U}
either increases or stays the same, concluding the proof.
∎

Claim 5.3
.

If the page
s
requested at time

satisfies the condition:
s

\mathrm{OPT}
’s cache and
s
does not exist in
\mathcal{A}
’s cache and
s
is not giving a charge and
s
is not bearing any charges, then
\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{O}+\mathcal{U}
is strictly positive just before this request.

Proof.

Consider any time
t
where the requested page
s
satisfies the condition. We can associate to the page
s
two past events occurring at times
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
(where
t_{1}<t_{2}<t
) such that: (1)
\mathcal{A}
evicts
s
at
t_{1}
, but
s
continues to live in
\mathrm{OPT}
’s cache, resulting in

giving a charge to some other page

\mathcal{A}
’s cache (

2b
), and (2)

). Observe that these two past events need to necessarily occur for

to satisfy the condition. Now observe that when

gets requested at

,

is the bearer of a

\mathcal{I},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{U}
remain unchanged. Because

\mathcal{O}
decreases by

. Finally, because

is in
\mathcal{A}
’s cache at this time, no new charges are added to
\mathcal{O}
. Thus,
\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{O}+\mathcal{U}
strictly
increases
by 1 at step (2). Also, note that for each distinct time step where the requested page

Now consider the

where the requested page

The factor of 4 in the analysis of

6
Lower bound for Dominating Distribution Algorithms

In this section, we show that there exist problem instances where a dominating distribution algorithm
9
9

9

Note that we cannot hope to prove a lower bound that applies to
all
dominating distribution algorithms, since the optimal Farthest-in-Future algorithm is technically also a dominating distribution algorithm.
can provably be at least
c
times worse than
\mathrm{OPT}
, for
c\geq 1.5907
. We start with a warm-up analysis that captures the central idea behind the lower bound, and shows a bound of 1.5, before generalizing the analysis to improve the bound. Our lower bound instances throughout are small and simple: they comprise of a Markov chain on
n=3
pages with a cache size
k=2
.

6.1
Warm-up: A 1.5-competitive Lower Bound

Consider a simple setting with 3 total pages, and a cache of size 2. At each time step independently, page 1 is requested with probability
1-\varepsilon
, and page 2 and 3 are requested each with probability
\varepsilon/2
. This corresponds to the Markov chain with the transition matrix

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1-\varepsilon&\varepsilon/2&\varepsilon/2\\
1-\varepsilon&\varepsilon/2&\varepsilon/2\\
1-\varepsilon&\varepsilon/2&\varepsilon/2\end{bmatrix}.

We start with the initial cache being
[1,2]
. The reference algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ref}}
we will compete against always keeps page 1 in its cache. If it suffers a cache miss, it swaps out the other page with the requested page. At any time
t
, the probability that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ref}}
suffers a miss is therefore exactly
\varepsilon/2
, and hence the expected total number of misses through
T
timesteps is
\varepsilon T/2
.

Now, consider
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}
. We want to capitalize on the fact that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}
evicts page 1 with some positive probability, in contrast to
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ref}}
. Namely, observe that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ suffers cache miss at time $t$}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$1$ in cache of $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ at $t$}]\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$1$ not in cache of $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ at $t$}]\cdot(1-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=p_{t}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+(1-p_{t})\cdot(1-\varepsilon),

(12)

where we denote
p_{t}=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$1$ in cache of $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ at $t$}]
. The latter probability is non-zero for
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}
, which already makes its expected total number of misses larger than that of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ref}}
. We will therefore aim to maximize this difference.

Observe that

\displaystyle p_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$1$ requested at $t-1$}]+\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$1$ in cache of $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ at $t-1$}]\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$1$ not requested at $t-1$}]\cdot

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$1$ not evicted by $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ at $t-1~\big|$ $1$ not requested, $1$ in cache of $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ at $t-1$}]

\displaystyle=1-\varepsilon+p_{t-1}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$1$ not evicted by $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ at $t-1~\big|$ $1$ not requested, $1$ in cache of $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ at $t-1$}].

We can analytically calculate the last probability. Say the cache of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}
at
t-1
is, without loss of generality,
[1,2]
. Given that
1
is not requested, we have that either of
2
or
3
are requested, each with probability
1/2
. If
2
is requested, there is no cache miss. If on the other hand,
3
is requested,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}
calculates a dominating distribution
\mu
over
[1,2]
. This can be easily calculated. Let
\alpha(p<q)
again be the probability that
p
is requested next before
q
(conditioned on the current page, which in this case, does not matter since the distributions at every time step are independent and identical). We have that

\displaystyle\alpha(1<1)=\alpha(2<2)=0,

\displaystyle\alpha(2<1)=\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2\cdot\alpha(2<1)\implies\quad\alpha(2<1)=\frac{\varepsilon/2}{1-\varepsilon/2},

\displaystyle\alpha(1<2)=1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/2\cdot\alpha(1<2)\implies\quad\alpha(1<2)=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon/2}.

The condition for the dominating distribution is that, for every fixed page
q
in the cache,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{p\sim\mu}[\alpha(p<q)]\leq 1/2.

Letting
\mu(1)=x
so that
\mu(2)=1-x
, this translates to

\displaystyle(1-x)\cdot\alpha(2<1)\leq 1/2,\quad x\cdot\alpha(1<2)\leq 1/2

\displaystyle\implies\qquad

\displaystyle\frac{3\varepsilon-2}{2\varepsilon}\leq x\leq\frac{2-\varepsilon}{4-4\varepsilon}.

We want
x
to be large as possible, because we want
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}
to evict
1
with the highest feasible probability, so that it will suffer a lot of cache misses. Thus, we set
x=\frac{2-\varepsilon}{4-4\varepsilon}
. This gives us that,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$1$ not evicted by $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ at $t-1~\big|$ $1$ not requested, $1$ in cache of $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ at $t-1$}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot(1-x)=1-\frac{x}{2}=\frac{6-7\varepsilon}{8-8\varepsilon}.

In total, we get that

\displaystyle p_{t}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{1-\varepsilon}_{c}+p_{t-1}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\varepsilon(6-7\varepsilon)}{8-8\varepsilon}}_{d}.

Unrolling the recurrence, and using that
p_{1}=1
, we obtain that for
t\geq 2
,

\displaystyle p_{t}

\displaystyle=c(1+d+\dots+d^{t-2})+d^{t-1}=c\cdot\frac{1-d^{t-1}}{1-d}+d^{t-1}.

Summing up
(
12
)
until
T
, and substituting
p_{t}
from above, we get that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}},T)]

\displaystyle=T(1-\varepsilon)+\left(\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}-1\right)\sum_{t=1}^{T}p_{t}

\displaystyle=T(1-\varepsilon)+\left(\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}-1\right)\left[\frac{c}{1-d}\left(T-\frac{1-d^{T}}{1-d}\right)+\frac{1-d^{T}}{1-d}\right].

Taking the limit as
T\to\infty
, we get that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}},T)]}{\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ref}},T)]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\varepsilon+\left(\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}-1\right)\left(\frac{c}{1-d}\right)}{\varepsilon/2}.

Substituting the values of
c
and
d
, and taking the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
, the ratio above converges to
1.5
.

6.2
A Slightly More General Analysis that Gives a Better Lower Bound

In the instance above, instead of assigning
\varepsilon/2
mass equally to pages 2 and 3, we can also imagine splitting the
\varepsilon
mass unequally between these pages. Namely, let the underlying distribution on the pages be such that, at each time step, page 1 is requested with probability
1-\varepsilon
, page 2 is requested with probability
\varepsilon_{1}
and page 3 is requested with probability
\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1}
, where the regime we want to keep in mind is
1-\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{1}\geq\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1}
. Note that
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon/2
corresponds to the instance above. Let the initial cache be
[1,2]
.

The reference algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ref}}
still always maintains page 1 in its cache. Let

\displaystyle p_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ref}}$'s state is $[1,2]$ at $t$}].

We have that

\displaystyle p_{t}=p_{t-1}\cdot(1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon_{1})+(1-p_{t-1})\cdot\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{1}+p_{t-1}\cdot(1-\varepsilon)=\varepsilon_{1}\left(\frac{1-(1-\varepsilon)^{t-1}}{\varepsilon}\right)+(1-\varepsilon)^{t-1},

where we unrolled the recurrence in the last step, and used that
p_{1}=1
. Then,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ref}}\text{ suffers miss at }t]=p_{t}\cdot(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1})+(1-p_{t})\cdot\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{1}+(\varepsilon-2\varepsilon_{1})\cdot p_{t},

and hence

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ref}},T)]

\displaystyle=T\varepsilon_{1}+(\varepsilon-2\varepsilon_{1})\sum_{t=1}^{T}p_{t}

\displaystyle=T\varepsilon_{1}+(\varepsilon-2\varepsilon_{1})\left[\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{\varepsilon}\left(T-\frac{1-(1-\varepsilon)^{T}}{\varepsilon}\right)+\frac{1-(1-\varepsilon)^{T}}{\varepsilon}\right].

(13)

Now, we analyze the behavior of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}
. Let

\displaystyle p_{t}=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$'s state is $[1,2]$ at $t$}]

\displaystyle q_{t}=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$'s state is $[1,3]$ at $t$}]

\displaystyle r_{t}=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$'s state is $[2,3]$ at $t$}],

where
p_{1}=1,q_{1}=0,r_{1}=0
. Then, we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}\text{ suffers miss at }t]

\displaystyle=p_{t}\cdot(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1})+q_{t}\cdot\varepsilon_{1}+r_{t}\cdot(1-\varepsilon).

(14)

Observe further that

\displaystyle p_{t}

\displaystyle=p_{t-1}\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{1 or 2 requested at }t-1]+q_{t-1}\cdot\varepsilon_{1}\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 3 from }[1,3]]

\displaystyle\qquad+r_{t-1}\cdot(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 3 from }[2,3]]

\displaystyle=p_{t-1}\cdot(1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon_{1})+q_{t-1}\cdot\varepsilon_{1}\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 3 from }[1,3]]

\displaystyle\qquad+r_{t-1}\cdot(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 3 from }[2,3]]

\displaystyle q_{t}

\displaystyle=q_{t-1}\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{1 or 3 requested at }t-1]+p_{t-1}\cdot(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1})\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 2 from }[1,2]]

\displaystyle\qquad+r_{t-1}\cdot(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 2 from }[2,3]]

\displaystyle=q_{t-1}\cdot(1-\varepsilon_{1})+p_{t-1}\cdot(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1})\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 2 from }[1,2]]

\displaystyle\qquad+r_{t-1}\cdot(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 2 from }[2,3]]

\displaystyle r_{t}

\displaystyle=r_{t-1}\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{2 or 3 requested at }t-1]+p_{t-1}\cdot(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1})\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 1 from }[1,2]]

\displaystyle\qquad+q_{t-1}\cdot\varepsilon_{1}\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 1 from }[1,3]]

\displaystyle=r_{t-1}\cdot\varepsilon+p_{t-1}\cdot(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1})\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 1 from }[1,2]]+q_{t-1}\cdot\varepsilon_{1}\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 1 from }[1,3]].

We can calculate each of the eviction probabilities above while respecting the properties that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}
must satisfy. Namely, consider that the cache state is
[1,2]
. Let
\mu
be the dominating distribution that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}
constructs, where
\mu(1)=x
and
\mu(2)=1-x
. We have that

\displaystyle\alpha(2<1)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{1}+(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1})\alpha(2<1)\implies\quad\alpha(2<1)=\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon_{1}}

\displaystyle\alpha(1<2)

\displaystyle=1-\alpha(2<1)=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon_{1}}.

The dominating distribution condition requires that

\displaystyle(1-x)\cdot\alpha(2<1)\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad x\cdot\alpha(1<2)\leq\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\implies\qquad

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha(2<1)-1/2}{\alpha(2<1)}\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha(1<2)}.

We choose to set the upper condition to be tight, which results in

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 1 from }[1,2]]=\frac{1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon_{1}}{2-2\varepsilon},

\displaystyle 1-x

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 2 from }[1,2]]=\frac{1-\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1}}{2-2\varepsilon}.

Now, consider the same calculation, where the cache state is
[1,3]
,
\mu(1)=x
and
\mu(3)=1-x
. We have that

\displaystyle\alpha(3<1)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{1}\cdot\alpha(3<1)\implies\quad\alpha(3<1)=\frac{\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1}}{1-\varepsilon_{1}}

\displaystyle\alpha(1<3)

\displaystyle=1-\alpha(3<1)=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon_{1}}.

The dominating distribution condition requires

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha(3<1)-1/2}{\alpha(3<1)}\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha(1<3)}.

Again, we make the upper bound tight to get

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 1 from }[1,3]]=\frac{1-\varepsilon_{1}}{2-2\varepsilon},

\displaystyle 1-x

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 3 from }[1,3]]=\frac{1+\varepsilon_{1}-2\varepsilon}{2-2\varepsilon}.

Finally, consider the calculation, where the cache state is
[2,3]
,
\mu(2)=x
and
\mu(3)=1-x
. We have that

\displaystyle\alpha(3<2)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1}+(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\alpha(3<2)\implies\quad\alpha(3<2)=\frac{\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\alpha(2<3)

\displaystyle=1-\alpha(3<2)=\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{\varepsilon}.

The dominating distribution condition requires

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha(3<2)-1/2}{\alpha(3<2)}\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha(2<3)},

so we set

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 2 from }[2,3]]=\frac{\varepsilon}{2\varepsilon_{1}},

\displaystyle 1-x

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}$ evicts 3 from }[2,3]]=\frac{2\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon}{2\varepsilon_{1}}.

Substituting all these eviction probabilities, and writing the system of equations for
p_{t},q_{t},r_{t}
above in matrix form, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}p_{t}\\
q_{t}\\
r_{t}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon_{1}&\varepsilon_{1}\cdot\frac{1+\varepsilon_{1}-2\varepsilon}{2-2\varepsilon}&(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{2\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon}{2\varepsilon_{1}}\\
(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1})\cdot\frac{1-\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1}}{2-2\varepsilon}&1-\varepsilon_{1}&(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{2\varepsilon_{1}}\\
(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1})\cdot\frac{1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon_{1}}{2-2\varepsilon}&\varepsilon_{1}\cdot\frac{1-\varepsilon_{1}}{2-2\varepsilon}&\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}p_{t-1}\\
q_{t-1}\\
r_{t-1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=B^{t-1}\begin{bmatrix}p_{1}\\
q_{1}\\
r_{1}\end{bmatrix}=B^{t-1}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix},

where we denoted the matrix by
B
and rolled out the recurrence. Substituting in (

), we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}}\text{ suffers miss at }t]

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1}&\varepsilon_{1}&1-\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}B^{t-1}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix},

and thus,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}},T)]

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{1}&\varepsilon_{1}&1-\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}(I+B+B^{2}+\dots+B^{T-1})\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

(15)

Now that we have exact expressions given by (

) and (

), we can do a computer search over assignments to

—each assignment gives a lower bound for
\frac{\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}},T)]}{\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ref}},T)]}
. For example, our (admittedly limited) search yields

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dom}},T)]}{\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ref}},T)]}\geq 1.5907,

for

.

Remark 4
.

While we do believe that further tuning

will likely not improve the lower bound by too much, it is conceivable that a better lower bound may be obtained by going beyond
n=3,k=2
(albeit with messier calculations). For example, there appears to be scope for improvement within instances having the same blueprint as above for slightly larger values of
n
and
k
, where there are certain high-probability pages that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ref}}
always keeps in the cache.

7
Learning the Markov Chain from Samples

It is worth pointing out that the form of
Lemma

5.1
allows us to obtain performance guarantees even when we only have approximate estimates of the
\alpha(p<q)
values, as also noted in passing by
Lund et al. 1999
. In particular, suppose that we only have
\varepsilon
-approximate (multiplicate or additive) estimates
\hat{\alpha}(p<q)
of
\alpha(p<q)
, that still satisfy
\hat{\alpha}(p<q)+\hat{\alpha}(q<p)=1
, and
\hat{\alpha}(p<p)=0
. Suppose that at each cache miss, we use these
\hat{\alpha}(p<q)
values to compute a dominating distribution
\hat{\mu}
, and draw the page
p
to evict from
\hat{\mu}
.

Even with such an approximate dominating distribution, we can still guarantee either
\frac{2}{1-2\varepsilon}
-competitiveness (in the case that
\hat{\alpha}(p<q)\in[\alpha(p<q)-\varepsilon,\alpha(p<q)+\varepsilon]
), or
\frac{2-2\varepsilon}{1-2\varepsilon}
-competitiveness (in the case that
\hat{\alpha}(p<q)\in[(1-\varepsilon)\alpha(p<q),(1+\varepsilon)\alpha(p<q)]
). This follows as a direct consequence of
2.1
and
Lemma

5.1
.

Perhaps the first scenario to consider here is when we do not assume prior knowledge of the transition matrix
M
, but only have access to samples previously drawn from the Markov chain. For example, we can imagine that the algorithm designer has available a large training dataset of past page requests. This dataset can be used to derive estimates of the entries in the transition matrix
M
, for instance, using one of the several existing estimators
(
Hao et al. 2018
;
Wolfer and Kontorovich 2021
;
Huang and Li 2024
)
. In fact, recall that the computation of the
\alpha(p<q)
values requires only for us to solve a linear system determined by the entries in the transition matrix (
Equation

3
). Hence, if we have accurate estimates for the entries in
M
, we can use the theory of perturbed linear systems to bound the resulting error in the estimates of the
\alpha(p<q)
values. Thereafter, using the argument from the previous paragraph, we can obtain a performance guarantee for the competitiveness of a dominating distribution algorithm that uses these estimates.

We can turn the above informal argument into a formal learning-theoretic result. Formally, suppose that every entry in
M
is at least some
\delta>0
. In this case, the Markov chain is irreducible (meaning that there is a positive, and unique probability of eventually getting from any state to another). Thus, in this case, for every fixed
p,q
, the linear system (
3
) has a (unique) solution, meaning that matrix associated with it is invertible. The relevant quantity here will be the conditioning of the
worst
linear system that we might ever have to solve. Towards this, let
L_{p,q}
be the matrix associated with the linear system in (
3
) for pages
p,q
, and define:

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\neq q}\|L_{p,q}^{-1}\|_{\infty},

(16)

where for a square matrix
A\in\mathds{R}^{n\times n}
,
\|A\|_{\infty}=\sup_{i}\sum_{j}|A_{i,j}|
. Observe from (
3
) that
1\leq\|L_{p,q}\|_{\infty}\leq 2
for every
p,q
.

We choose to adopt the following result of
Hao et al. 2018
for our exposition:

Theorem 6

(Theorem 4 in
Hao et al. 2018
)
.

Suppose we unroll a Markov chain to obtain
m
samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
, where the initial distribution of
X_{1}
is arbitrary, but the transition matrix
M\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
of the Markov chain satisfies
M_{i,j}\geq\delta>0
for every
i,j
. Then, there exists an estimator
\hat{M}=\hat{M}(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})
that satisfies

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{X_{1},\dots,X_{m}}\|M(i,:)-\hat{M}(i,:)\|_{2}^{2}\leq O\left(\frac{1}{m\delta}\right)

for every
i\in[n]
, where
M(i,:)
(respectively
\hat{M}(i,:)
) denotes the
i^{\text{th}}
row of
M
(respectively
\hat{M}
).

Using this theorem, we can obtain the following result:

Theorem 7
.

Suppose that the page requests are generated from an unknown Markov chain where every entry in the transition matrix
M
is at least
\delta>0
. Let
\gamma
be as defined in (
16
). Given a training dataset of
m=O\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}n^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\delta}\right)
past page requests from
M
, there is an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
which is
\frac{2}{1-2\varepsilon}
-competitive against
\mathrm{OPT}
with probability at least
0.99
over the
m
samples.

Proof.

From the initial
m
page requests,
\mathcal{A}
constructs the estimator
\hat{M}
given by
Theorem

6
. Applying Markov’s inequality to the in-expectation guarantee on
\hat{M}
, for every fixed
i\in[n]
, we have that with probability at least
1-0.01\cdot\frac{1}{n}
,

\displaystyle\|M(i,:)-\hat{M}(i,:)\|_{2}^{2}\leq O\left(\frac{n}{m\delta}\right).

A union bound over the universe of
n
pages gives that with probability at least
0.99
, we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[n]}\|M(i,:)-\hat{M}(i,:)\|_{2}^{2}\leq O\left(\frac{n}{m\delta}\right)

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality then implies that with probability at least
0.99
,

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|M(i,j)-\hat{M}(i,j)|\leq O\left(\frac{n}{\sqrt{m\delta}}\right).

Denoting
\Delta=\hat{M}-M
, we have effectively argued that
\|\Delta\|_{\infty}\leq O\left(\frac{n}{\sqrt{m\delta}}\right)
with probability at least
0.99
.

The algorithm
\mathcal{A}
plays the dominating distribution algorithm as its eviction policy on a cache miss; however, it constructs the dominating distribution using the estimator
\hat{M}
. Namely, for every pair
p,q
, let
\hat{L}_{p,q}
be the matrix associated with the linear system in (
3
), where instead of the entries in
M
, we plug in entries from our estimator
\hat{M}
. Consider the solution
\hat{x}
of the perturbed linear system
\hat{L}_{p,q}\cdot\hat{x}=b_{p,q}
, where
b_{p,q}
is the right-hand side of (
3
). We care about bounding the difference between
\hat{x}
and
x
, where
x
is the solution to
L_{p,q}\cdot x=b_{p,q}
. Note that the right-hand side in both the perturbed and unperturbed systems (namely
b_{p,q}
) is still the same.

From a standard analysis of solutions to perturbed linear systems (e.g., see Theorem 1 in
(
Falk 2016
)
), we have that

\displaystyle\|\hat{x}-x\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|L_{p,q}^{-1}\|_{\infty}\|\hat{L}_{p,q}-L_{p,q}\|_{\infty}\|x\|_{\infty}}{1-\|L_{p,q}^{-1}\|_{\infty}\|\hat{L}_{p,q}-L_{p,q}\|_{\infty}}.

Now, observe that
\|\hat{L}_{p,q}-L_{p,q}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\Delta\|_{\infty}
, and also that
\|x\|_{\infty}=1
. Plugging in our bound above on
\Delta
, we get

\displaystyle\|\hat{x}-x\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{O(\gamma n/\sqrt{m\delta})}{1-O(\gamma n/\sqrt{m\delta})}.

Setting
m=C\cdot\frac{\gamma^{2}n^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\delta}
for a constant
C
is sufficient to make the right-hand side above to be at most
\varepsilon
.

(
Falk 2016
)
that we used.
But this means that all the
\hat{\alpha}(p<q)

constructs satisfy

\displaystyle{\alpha}(p<q)-\varepsilon\leq\hat{\alpha}(p<q)\leq{\alpha}(p<q)+\varepsilon.

Thus, the dominating distribution

that
\mathcal{A}
constructs at every cache miss has the property that for every fixed

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{p\sim\hat{\mu}}[\hat{\alpha}(p<q)]\leq\frac{1}{2}\quad\implies\quad\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{p\sim\hat{\mu}}[{\alpha}(p<q)]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon.

Instantiating
2.1
and

5.1
