Identifying key regulators in neuronal transdifferentiation by gene regulatory network analysis
Authors: Li Li, Binglin Zhu, Jian Feng, Eric Klann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe have found that the overexpression of ASCL1, miR9/9 *-124, nPTB shRNA, and p53 shRNA efficiently converted human skin fibroblasts to neurons. To identify key regulators of the transdifferentiation, we analyzed longitudinal RNA-seq data of human skin fibroblasts being converted with various combinations of these reprogramming factors, and constructed gene regulatory network (GRN) models capturing the high-order information important for neuronal conversion. Examination of gene communities and transcription factors (TFs) in the GRNs identified OTX2 and LMX1A as the key regulators of the conversion to neurons, as they had strongest connections to genes functionally associated with neuronal development and differentiation. Indeed, knocking down OTX2 or LMX1A significantly impaired the transdifferentiation of human skin fibroblasts to neurons. We also validated the approach in neuronal conversion of mouse embryonic stem cells. The study demonstrates the effectiveness of using GRN models to identify key regulators of neuronal conversion. The strategy will enhance mechanistic understanding of cellular reprogramming in general.Keywords: neuronal transdifferentiation, gene regulatory network, transcription factors

The direct conversion of human fibroblasts to neurons enables many useful applications. To understand the underlying mechanism, we constructed gene regulatory networks (GRNs) using RNA-sequencing data from cells undergoing the conversion. By analyzing the GRN structure and annotation with graph theory principles, we identified and experimentally verified the critical role of the transcription factors LMX1A and OTX2 in the conversion. This approach would enhance mechanistic understanding and aid the search for critical regulators of cell reprogramming in general, as we validated it in the neuronal conversion of mouse embryonic stem cells.

Introduction

The transdifferentiation of one cell type to another allows the rapid and direct conversion of cell fate without going through a stem cell stage and thus preserving age-associated epigenetic information (1–3). Compared to neurons differentiated from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), induced neurons (iNs) converted from easily accessible somatic cells, such as skin fibroblasts, have distinct advantages in disease modeling and therapeutic discovery by preserving epigenetic fidelity from the donor cells (1, 4). To realize the full potential in the applications of iNs, it is necessary to understand the molecular mechanism of neuronal transdifferentiation by identifying key regulators in the conversion. Such efforts will improve the efficiency and yield of iNs, thus making them accessible to more investigators. Previous studies have mainly focused on identifying the optimal combination of reprogramming factors including transcription factors (TFs) (5), microRNAs (6), RNA splicing regulators (7), and small-molecule compounds (8). It remains unclear how a mitotic cell, such as a human skin fibroblast, can be converted in two weeks to iNs. The lack of a clear mechanistic understanding of neuronal transdifferentiation, since its discovery in 2010 (3), has hampered further improvement of this technology. A systematic approach to identifying key regulators in transdifferentiation holds promise to overcoming the roadblock.

Analysis of gene regulatory network (GRN) in cells undergoing transdifferentiation has great potential in revealing critical molecular and cellular changes in the direct conversion. The regulatory relationship in gene interactions can be represented as a GRN, which is composed of genes as nodes and interactions between genes as edges. Changes in cell identity occur concomitantly with alterations in gene expression pattern because of the coordinated changes at molecular level. Transdifferentiation involves the transition between two stable cell states. Cells in a stable state are resistant to random perturbations of genes, and the transition only occurs when the perturbation is sufficient to trigger widespread changes in gene expression pattern. In network theory, a network that has most nodes with a few edges and a few nodes with many edges is described as having a scale-free topology (9). A highly clustered network where nodes can be reached by small path lengths between each other is a small-world network (10). These properties were applied to study biological networks including GRNs (11–13), as networks exhibiting such properties are robust to random node deletion and vulnerable to targeted attack (i.e. perturbation of hubs) (14). This facilitates predictions of perturbation targets that can trigger widespread changes and disturb the stable system.

Our study has shown that overexpression of ASCL1 (A), MiR9/9 *-124 (M), nPTB shRNA (n), and p53 shRNA (p) converts human skin fibroblasts to neurons. Different combinations of the four reprogramming factors have varying conversion efficiency in an irreversible and reproducible manner (7). Here, to predict the key regulators promoting the most efficient conversion, we analyzed longitudinal RNA-seq data of AG22056 human skin fibroblasts being converted to iNs at days 3, 7, and 14 by the four most effective combinations of these reprogramming factors (Anp, AMn, AMp, or AMnp) and constructed the corresponding GRNs with a polynomial regression-based method (15). Combining topological characteristics of the networks, genes, and external biological annotations, we identified OTX2 and LMX1A as key regulators in the most efficient conversion conditions, and experimentally confirmed their critical roles in the transdifferentiation. We further validated the strategy by analyzing RNA-seq data from the conversion of mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) to iNs (16), which produced predictions consistent with experimental results in that independent study.

Results

Longitudinal RNA-seq analysis of human skin fibroblasts being converted to iNs

We have identified an effective and rapid method to convert human skin fibroblasts directly to iNs by overexpressing ASCL1 (A) and MiR9/9 *-124 (M), and knocking down nPTB (n) and p53 (p) (7). To understand the conversion mechanism, we performed the transdifferentiation using the most efficient four combinations of A, M, n, and p (Anp, AMn, AMp, and AMnp), and performed RNA-seq analysis at days 3, 7, and 14 of the conversion (Fig. 1A). Immunostaining at day 14 confirmed the generation of MAP2 + and TUJ1 + cells at varying efficiency (Fig. 1B–F) as we have shown (7). AMnp generated neurons most effectively (Fig. 1F). As A is obligatory in making neurons (7), we removed one of the three other factors (m, n, and p) and found markedly decrease in conversion (Fig. 1C–E). Principal component analysis (PCA) of longitudinal RNA-seq data showed that principal component 1 (PC1) correlated with the transcriptional transition toward neurons as time progressed (Fig. 1G). PC2 reflected the distinct molecular changes triggered by different combinations of reprogramming factors (Fig. 1G). Anp and AMnp were the closest on PC2, suggesting a moderate role of M (MiR9/9 *-124 overexpression) in this regard. AMn generated the most different features on PC2. As all the other three conditions had p (p53 knockdown), the role of p53 knockdown appeared to be distinct. Its absence in AMn produced a PC2 profile very different from the other three combinations that include p (AMnp, Anp, and AMp), despite the similar conversion efficiencies of AMn and AMp (7). Thus, the four combinations of reprogramming factors perturb the GRN of human fibroblasts in distinctive manners that would allow us to extract useful information to identify key regulators. These results suggest that all four factors in the protocol are important for instructing neuronal identity through participating in different processes that lead to a neuronal fate at varying efficiencies. As the variation may not be linearly decomposable into several genes that are differentially expressed, we introduced GRN analysis integrating nonlinear features and external biological knowledge to identify key regulators in the process.

Interpreting genetic interactions by constructing gene regulatory networks

Genes in a GRN are not equally important. A handful of genes at high level of hierarchy, such as transcription factors (TFs), tend to control the expression of the majority of genes that have little regulatory significance. We employed a systems biology approach to model the gene expression changes during cell fate transition. A pipeline for GRN modeling was applied (Fig. 2), where the genes were represented by network nodes and gene interactions were represented by network edges. To extract network information from variations between genetic modulation conditions, we started with the gene expression matrix from differentially expressed genes (DEGs) accounting for most of the variations between conditions (Fig. 2A). To capture the high-order genetic interactions beyond linear effects, we applied Polynomial Lasso (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) bagging (PoLoBag) (15) to construct GRNs, which uses regularized polynomial regression to estimate the connection strengths and directions between genes in a bootstrapping setup, yielding GRNs with weighted and directed edges for each condition (Fig. 2A, see Materials and methods for details). Following the GRN construction, we evaluated the topology of the networks (Fig. 2B), then subjected the networks to community detection, where the external biological information was applied to annotate the nodes in each community (Fig. 2C). Finally, the nodes in the community of interest were ranked based on network structure, and the top ranked nodes were selected for further validation (Fig. 2D).

Workflow of GRN analysis. A) GRN is constructed by PoLoBag on gene expression matrix. The input gene expression matrix was bootstrapped on both genes and experiments for multiple rounds of computation. Polynomial LASSO regression was applied to each bootstrapped matrix to compute weighted edges from variable nodes (marked in black) to target node (marked in red). All nodes were looped over as target node while other nodes served as variable nodes. The complete GRN was generated by merging nodes and averaging over edge weights. B) The network topology is evaluated by parameters reflecting the density of local clustering (small-worldness) to determine the presence of highly connected hubs. C) Nodes were clustered by network structure and the clusters of nodes were annotated with biological functions from external sources. D) The importance of nodes in target communities is ranked based on network structure. A candidate for experimental verification can be a hub gene with the highest rank.

The topological features of the GRNs were examined by computing small-world coefficient (σ) (Fig. 2B), which reflects the presence of highly connected clusters and a short path length between any two nodes relative to those of a random network (17). To evaluate the effect of network size determined by edge weight cut-off threshold, a step-wise network sparsification was applied. Weak edges and disconnected nodes were discarded by gradually increasing the weight threshold of edge removal, yielding subgraphs of original GRNs with various sizes, while strongest edges and corresponding nodes were always preserved.

We computed the average small-world σ with a moving window of 30 nodes for GRNs from each genetic modulation condition, where subgraphs with undirected positive (Fig. 3A) or negative edges (Fig. 3A’) were considered separately. All transdifferentiation GRNs with positive edges were small-world networks (Fig. 3A). With the sparsification of the GRN, the network σ gradually increased to the maximum then decreased dramatically, which indicates that the network achieved a topology with the highest clustering and shortest average path length when weak edges were removed, then the topology became more uniform when strong edges were removed. Moreover, Anp, AMp, and AMnp GRNs showed stronger clustering than AMn, whose σ was close to that of a random network. On the contrary, none of the GRNs with negative edges was nearly small-world (Fig. 3A’). To statistically determine whether the GRNs were scale-free, we then evaluated the maximum likelihood power law fit for the network degree distribution against other probability distributions (18 , 19 ). The log likelihood ratio distribution showed that regardless of selected sparsification threshold, Anp, AMp, and AMnp GRNs significantly favored exponential than power law distribution (Fig. 3B and B’), and all the GRNs significantly favored log-normal than power law distribution (Fig. 3C and C’). Collectively, these results suggest the constructed transdifferentiation GRNs with positive edges were small-world networks containing highly connected clusters with short path length for node communication. Since the GRNs were not scale-free, the features of highly connected nodes could be influenced by network sizes, which required closer examination. As such, we performed the analysis in the following sections with varied GRN sizes. Negative edges in GRNs were disregarded in further analysis as a lack of small-worldness indicated random gene interactions that are not biologically plausible.

Identifying top regulators in GRNs by network structure

A gene is considered to be important relative to other genes if the gene itself has strong direct interactions with many genes, or it interacts with important genes that have strong direct interactions with many genes. To quantify this feature of the genes in the context of a network, we calculated and ranked the betweenness centrality of genes in the GRNs. Since the GRN structure differs in different transdifferentiation conditions, and changes in network size also influence node features, we examined the distribution of betweenness centrality (normalized by network size) and overall rank of genes within transdifferentiation condition during network sparsification. As shown by violin plots (Fig. 4), regardless of the conditions for generating GRNs, most nodes have zero betweenness centrality because they are located at the terminal of the network. Only a small proportion of nodes has large values, which is consistent with the small-world characteristics of the GRNs. The range of betweenness centrality variation for each transdifferentiation condition showed a similar trend in the σ values (Fig. 3A). It indicates that a network with higher clustering could discriminate the hierarchy of nodes with better resolution, and the rank rather than absolute value of betweenness centrality of the node could be more stable and comparable between GRNs. To prioritize key genes with the best confidence, we first identified the top ranked genes within each computed GRN. Among the 10 top ranked genes within each computed GRN (represented as strip plot), TFs and non-TFs (represented as red and gray dots, respectively) have no observable segregation in their betweenness centrality distribution (Fig. 4). To quantify the overall difference of TFs and non-TFs in the network, we first counted zero and nonzero betweenness centrality genes in all GRNs for the two gene categories (Fig. S1A), and performed Fisher’s exact test to identify association between gene categories and betweenness centrality value. Based on the odds ratio, a trend was observed for TFs being more likely to have nonzero values (locate in the middle of the network) and non-TFs being more likely to have zero values (locate at the terminal if the network) in Anp and AMn GRNs, and vice versa in AMp and AMnp GRNs (Fig. S1B). However, none of the tests reached statistical significance after adjustment for multiple comparison. Further analysis with Wilcoxon rank-sum test revealed similar conclusion that no significant difference was held between the betweenness centrality values of TFs and non-TFs (Fig. S1C).

Although the biological characteristics of TFs determine that they are at the top of the hierarchy of genetic regulation, the function of genes that are regulated by TFs also determine whether they attain biological importance in the context of transdifferentiation to neurons. Thus, we highlighted genes that were most frequently observed in the pool of top ranked genes for functional annotation. This reveals a list of key genes that are specific to each transdifferentiation condition, including TFs associated with brain and neuron development (OTX2, HEY1, LMX1A, ISL1, and PRDM1) in Anp, AMn, and AMnp, genes involved in synaptic transmission (CHRNB4, GRIA4, and OPRD1) in Anp and AMnp, genes closely related to TP53 (MLXIPL, H1-0, and SNHG1) (20) in AMn, and skin enriched genes (DLX3, NR1D1, and VIM) (21) in AMp. The function of these genes is consistent with the conversion outcome of each condition, suggesting key genes specific to Anp and AMnp are better candidates for promoting neuronal fate.

Identifying key regulators of GRN communities with functional implications

Next, in order to evaluate the closely clustered communities in the GRNs and their corresponding biological function, we first detected communities in each GRN by applying a modularity optimization algorithm (22), then annotated the genes in each community by gene ontology (GO). Since any changes in network size would remove nodes that can be assigned to a community, the biological annotation of a community was used to assess the stability of communities regardless of the number of genes from the community. To compare the topological and biological importance of key genes during transdifferentiation, we plotted the rank quantile of key TFs among all genes in the GRN by betweenness centrality, and annotated the communities containing key TFs using GO biological process terms for each transdifferentiation condition as network sparsification threshold changes. We found that TFs ranked first as shown in Fig. 4 also had smaller and more stable rank quantile than TFs ranked second and third (Figs. 5 and S2), and the rank of LMX1A was most consistent among all top TFs (Fig. 5D). Communities from Anp, AMn, and AMnp that contain OTX2, HEY1, and LMX1A respectively were all enriched in neuron development and differentiation (Fig. 5A, B, and D), while communities from AMp containing DLX3 were enriched in muscle development (Fig. 5C). Moreover, GO terms associated with OTX2-containing communities were the most consensus compared to other top TFs, as indicated by the shape of the bars (Fig. 5A’). Among other key TFs, GO terms exclusively associated with neuron development were also found in AMnp GRNs from communities containing PRDM1 and ISL1 (Fig. S2).

To provide a representative overview of all communities within GRN and community memberships of top TFs, we selected and analyzed one GRN from each condition when most genes were included in such GRN without compromising the rank resolution provided by betweenness centrality (Fig. 6). In agreement with Fig. 5, for Anp, AMn, and AMnp GRNs, OTX2, HEY1, and LMX1A were all located in communities associated with neuron differentiation. In contrast, none of the AMp top TFs were from the neuron-related community (Fig. 6). Consistent with Fig. 3A showing that AMn GRNs had lower levels of clustering with longer paths between nodes, AMn GRN exhibited more homogeneous hierarchy with fewer nodes having nonzero betweenness centrality, and even the largest node betweenness centrality value was much smaller than other GRNs (Fig. 6B). Based on these observations, we chose the top TF from Anp and AMnp, namely OTX2 and LMX1A, as experimentally relevant predictions that are key regulators of neurogenesis for experimental verification.

OTX2 and LMX1A knockdown impair transdifferentiation

We first tested our predictions by their overexpression or knockdown on top of their respective reprogramming factors and evaluated the neuronal marker expression under the same conditions described in Fig. 1. Immunostaining at day 14 showed that OTX2 overexpression plus Anp slightly enhanced reprogramming efficiency in the ratio of MAP2 + to DAPI + cells (Fig. 7E), without significant changes in cell morphology (Fig. 7A and B) or reprogramming yield (Fig. 7D). Consistent with these, OTX2 knockdown markedly impaired Anp-mediated transdifferentiation (Fig. 7A and C) by reducing reprogramming yield (Fig. 7D) and efficiency (Fig. 7E). On the other hand, LMX1A overexpression had no observable effect on AMnp-mediated reprogramming (Fig. 7F and G), as reflected in the unchanged reprogramming yield (Fig. 7I) and efficiency (Fig. 7J). LMX1A knockdown drastically attenuated transdifferentiation (Fig. 7F and H) by significantly decreasing reprogramming yield (Fig. 7I) and efficiency (Fig. 7J).

To examine whether effects of OTX2 and LMX1A were specific to their transdifferentiation GRNs, we also overexpressed and knocked down OTX2 and LMX1A during AMnp or Anp-mediated reprogramming, respectively. Overexpression of LMX1A did not change the reprogramming yield and efficiency of Anp (Fig. S3A, B, D, and E). LMX1A knockdown significantly attenuated the reprogramming yield and efficiency of Anp (Fig. S3A, C, D, and E). We found similar effect of OTX2 in that OTX2 overexpression did not affect AMnp-mediated reprogramming yield and efficiency (Fig. S3F, G, I, and J), while OTX2 knockdown significantly reduced the reprogramming yield and efficiency of AMnp (Fig. S3F, H, I, and J). These results suggest that both OTX2 and LMX1A are critical for AMnp and Anp-mediated transdifferentiation.

Discovering GRNs in Ascl1 or Ngn2-mediated conversion of mouse embryonic stem cells to iNs

To examine the applicability of the GRN analysis to neuronal conversion in general, we further implemented our method to analyze the longitudinal RNA-seq data from the conversion of mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) to iNs by Ascl1 or Ngn2 (16). Ascl1 or Ngn2 produces similar neuronal cells but distinct side lineage cells. The cells are sorted by fluorescence of Venus tagged to endogenously expressed neuronal marker Mapt from day 3 (16). Sorted Venus + and Venus − cells are sequenced separately (16) as illustrated in Figs. 8A and S5A. We first constructed GRNs for putative neurons (Ascl1-Venus +, Ngn2-Venus +) and non-neurons (Ascl1-Venus −, Ngn2-Venus −) on gene expression of later days (days 3, 4, and 6) when neurons have been sorted. After processing for small-world σ for the GRNs (Fig. S4A) and betweenness centrality for nodes (Fig. S4B–E), we identified TFs expressed in hindbrain and neural crest from Ascl1 induced GRNs (Shox2, Phox2a, and Zic5) (24–26), TFs expressed in various brain subregions (Otp, Lhx9, Lhx4, Otx2, and Irx3) (27) and neural stem cell marker Pax6 from Ngn2 induced GRNs, and most distinctly, TFs expressed in trophoblast from Ascl1 induced non-neural GRNs (Hand1 and Elf3) (28).

After community detection and GO annotation, examination of representative GRNs on later days revealed that neuronal genes were predominant in both putative neuron groups (Ascl1-Venus +, Ngn2-Venus +) (Fig. 8B and D left), while the gene groups from non-neuron GRNs by Ascl1 and Ngn2 were different. For example, Ascl1-Venus − GRN contains genes for placenta development (Fig. 8C left), and Ngn2-Veuns- GRN has genes involved in mitotic cell cycle (Fig. 8E left). Importantly, the GO terms of the top TFs were replicable across GRNs from the same cell group when only network sparcification threshold was changed (Fig. S7). This was consistent with the experimental observations that along with iNs, Ascl1 also induced trophoblast-like cells, and Ngn2 induced neuron stem cell-like cells that were actively proliferating (16). To investigate the biological regulation between key TFs and induced transgenes, the differential binding sites of Ascl1 or Ngn2 surrounding the TFs were determined, along with H3K4me3 histone marks indicating accessible chromatin in mouse embryo forebrain (23) (Fig. 8B–E right). Indeed, the key TFs were preferentially bound by the transgenes used to induce the mESCs within or outside the open chromatin region, suggesting a possible direct regulation relationship. In addition, we predicted Hand1 and lower-ranked Ascl2 in the placenta development related community in Ascl1-Venus − GRN (Fig. 8C). Consistent with the rank in the GRN, Hand1 induction increases the number of trophoblast-like cells and abolishes neuronal conversion, while Ascl2 has little effect (16).

We also performed the same analysis on gene expression of the entire time series (days 0, 2, 3, 4, and 6) to examine the GRNs when the earlier stages of conversion were included (Fig. S4A’–E’). The top TFs from GRNs with complete time series are associated with broader lineages, apart from TFs related to peripheral (Shox2, Phox2b, Tlx2) (26, 29, 30) or central nervous system (Lhx9, Lef1, Pax6, Nr2f1, and Pou3f3) (27, 31, 32). There are also TFs involved in hepatocyte (Foxa3) (33), dendric cell (Batf3) (34), gonad differentiation (Rhox9) (35), and kidney (Pou3f3) (36) and tooth (Lef1, Pax9) (37, 38) morphogenesis, with several TFs participating in multiple lineages. In contrast to GRNs on later days when neurons emerge, GRNs of the entire time series contained more non-neuron-related communities in putative neuron groups (Fig. S5B and D) and more neuron-related communities in putative non-neuron groups (Fig. S5C and E). To better understand such difference, we assessed the average expression of genes in each GRN community (Fig. S6). Compared to GRNs on later days, GRNs of the entire time series for each condition contained communities with monotonically decreased expression during differentiation. None of such communities were related to neuron development, and a lack of GO consensus was observed for these communities in general (Fig. S6A’–D’). These were in agreement with the observation that a broader range of top TFs from more lineages were present from GRNs the entire time series (Figs. S4B’–E’ and S8). Interestingly, ribosome biogenesis was consistently decreased in Ngn2 induced GRNs (Fig. S6C’ and D’), and was more prominent in Ngn2-Venus + than in Ngn2-Venus − GRN, though no TF was among ribosome biogenesis communities (Fig. S5D and E). Notably, the placenta related communities in Ascl1-Venus − GRNs were the only monotonically upregulated non-neuron communities (Fig. S5B and B’), supporting the de novo induction of trophoblast like off-target lineage by Ascl1 in mESCs. Taken together, GRNs constructed with the complete time series suggested a mixed group of stem cell genes and alternative lineage genes that were repressed during neuronal induction, while GRNs on later days focused more on lineage specific genes upregulated by the inductions when lineage commitment was ongoing.

Discussion

By using a data-driven GRN analysis method to search for key regulators in neuronal conversion, we identified and verified two transcription factors (OTX2 and LMX1A) critically involved in the transdifferentiation of human skin fibroblasts to induced neurons (iNs). We also validated the approach by generating predictions consistent with experimental results in the conversion of mESCs to iNs by Ascl1 or Ngn2 (16). To construct GRNs from RNA-seq data, linear correlation and mutual information-based methods are widely used. Considering the underlying assumption of pairwise dependencies between two genes in these methods and limited sample size in our experimental setting, we think that these methods can be prone to produce false positives for indirect interactions and unrelated but highly correlated variables (39, 40). In contrast, regression-based method for GRN construction is scalable and has the potential to capture high-order conditional dependencies among multiple genes (40). Thus, we adopted an ensemble regression algorithm, which incorporates both linear and nonlinear features in the regression kernel and merges the subnetworks obtained from multiple computations on randomly selected subsets of data to obtain more robust predictions (15). Such an approach is inspired by the results of blind assessment on a panel of regression methods in GRN inference contest (41).

Using this approach, we found that the constructed GRNs are small-world networks but not strictly scale-free (Fig. 3). It has been suggested that scale-free networks with power-law degree distribution confer certain properties including robustness to random node deletion and vulnerable to targeted hub attack (9, 14). This provides a plausible explanation for modeling biological systems and has been found to be the case in some biological networks (11, 42). However, the power-law fit of biological networks has been challenged by statistical tests. Multiple studies have demonstrated that scale-free networks are empirically uncommon (19, 43, 44). Instead, small-worldness is less stringent than scale-freeness, as it only restricts the network topology to where highly connected clusters and short path lengths between nodes should be present (17). Despite the debates on the strictness of definitions, the purpose of modeling biological networks is to provide functional and mechanistic understanding of the biological system, and to further help predicting the outcomes of perturbation.

By analyzing the GRNs, our results showed that critical TFs specific to different experimental conditions can be stably prioritized using betweenness centrality regardless of network completeness. This behavior of regulatory network can be explained if it is minimally frustrated or exhibits some level of frustration (45). Minimal frustration refers to the feature that a simplified subnetwork can still preserve the behavior of the original network after removing some nodes and edges, as it is shown in the biological networks regulating cell-fate choice (45). As a result, keeping only genes with highly variant expression for GRN construction and removing weak interactions can help reducing noises and computational complexity from excessive trivial interactions between genes, while retaining the critical behavior of the larger GRN. In this regard, one of the lessons of the community-based crowdsourcing effort for inferring and evaluating biology models—Dialog for Reverse Engineering Assessment and Methods (DREAM) challenges—is that the formula for a successful model relies more on the fine tuning of the model rather than the basic algorithm it settled on.

There are multiple aspects to consider regarding conversion mediated by genetic modulation: the establishment of target cell GRNs, the suppression of starting cell GRNs, the suppression of off-target lineage GRNs installed by certain reprogramming factors, and the dosage and timing of genetic modulation to trigger the aforementioned changes. We have previously reported a combination screening approach to identifying efficient conversion conditions (7), while a systemic dissection of the underlying genetic regulations was lacking. The GRN analysis in the present study provided an overview of the genetic network communities with distinct biological functions installed by different conditions and produced actionable TF target predictions from neuronal GRNs that played key roles. This emphasizes the establishment of target cell GRNs. In contrast, the GRNs from the inferior condition AMp were predominated by muscle and myocyte related communities and TFs (Figs. 5C and S2C), in line with previous findings that off-target cells with myocyte markers appeared in Ascl1-mediated transdifferentiation of mouse embryonic fibroblasts to iNs (46, 47). The experimental results of the two prioritized TFs (LMX1A, OTX2) support that they are critical to neuronal transdifferentiation and may facilitate the commitment of neuronal fate in conjunction with the other reprogramming factors. To address the question of whether the approach is applicable to neuronal conversion in general, we analyzed the RNA-seq data from the conversion of mESCs to iNs in an independent study (16). Our analysis showed that mESCs induced by Ascl1 installed neuron GRN as well as placenta related GRN, while Ngn2 induced both neuron and proliferating neural stem cell GRNs. Both predictions, including the identification of trophoblast fate induced Hand1, were consistent with the original study (16). Additionally, GRNs including the earlier time points before the emergence of neurons revealed communities of genes that were suppressed, though a consensus biological function for such communities was lacking. One of the reasons for such ambiguity is genes with multiple functions across time series were forced into a single community. Future efforts on improving the GRN construction algorithm regarding dynamic network configuration may address this limitation.

In addition to facilitating a pan-neuronal GRN, the key TFs may engage in the subtype specification of neurons and promote further functional maturity. Indeed, functioning synergistically with Ascl1, Otx2 controls the specification and patterning of multiple brain regions, especially in forebrain where interneurons are generated (48, 49). LMX1A contributes to the determination of dopaminergic neuronal fate. It promotes the generation of dopaminergic neurons in during development (50, 51) and transdifferentiation (52, 53). Lmx1a also specifies the generation of cortical hem in telencephalon (54). Although overexpression study showed a mild effect quantified by pan-neuronal marker, it is likely that they are engaged in the subtype specification stage, which requires further functional characterization. As a parallel example, the most consistent top neuronal TF with high confidence GO annotation in Ngn2 induced GRNs was Lhx9, whose expression was specifically detected in a subgroup of thalamus and midbrain excitatory neurons (27). Recent effort in studying NGN2 iNs has confirmed that overexpression of LHX9 was sufficient to guide iPSCs toward midbrain neurons, and its knockout depleted such subtypes in NGN2 induced iPSCs (55).

Taken together, our results demonstrated the usefulness of a GRN approach in understanding neuronal conversion through a systemic dissection of the underlying genetic regulations and actionable target predictions. To efficiently generate neurons with molecular and functional fidelity, future effort remains to investigate the iNs regarding subtype specificity and physiological function, and fine tuning the reprogramming factors to establish a pure neuronal GRN with minimal off-target effect.

Methods

Cell culture

HEK293FT cells (Invitrogen, R70007) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (GIBCO, 11965092) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (GIBCO, 10437028), 0.1 mM MEM nonessential amino acids (NEAA) (GIBCO, 11140050), 2mM L-glutamine (GIBCO, 25030081), and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (GIBCO, 11360070). Human skin fibroblasts AG22056 (Coriell) were maintained in DMEM containing 10% heat inactivated FBS, 0.1 mM MEM NEAA, 2mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (GIBCO, 15140122). All cells were incubated at 37 ∘C with 5% CO2. Other experimental methods are available in Supplementary material.

RNA sequencing and preprocessing

RNA samples were obtained from cells at 3, 7, and 14 days post transduction with three parallel biological replicates for all conditions. RNA was extracted from homogenized cells with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018) using RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, 74104). TruSeq stranded RNA library prep (Illumina) and NovaSeq 6000 SP reagent kit (Illumina) were used for library preparation. Sequencing was performed on NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) by Genomics and Bioinformatics Core of the State University of New York at Buffalo. Raw reads were trimmed by Cutadapt (version 1.16) (56) to remove low quality 4-bp regions at the start and discard reads with mapping quality <20, then aligned to hg38 reference genome by HISAT2 (version 2.1.0) (57). Counts of fragments aligned to genes annotated on the reference genome (RefSeq Release 109.20201120) were generated by featureCounts (version 2.0.1) (58). Counts were normalized by rlog transformation with DESeq2 package (version 1.32.0) (59) for PCA. The variance of gene expression between treatment conditions was evaluated by two-way ANOVA with treatment condition as the main effect for calculating Bonferroni-corrected P value. Top 800 most variable genes with smallest P values were retained for GRN construction.

Gene regulatory network construction

GRNs were constructed using PoLoBag (15) in Python3. The rlog transformed counts were inversed by exp2 to obtain the gene expression data as the input of PoLoBag. For constructing the treatment condition specific GRNs, the input matrix is composed of N rows (N genes, in this case N=800) as target and regulators, and M columns (M conditions, in this case same treatment condition sampled at 3, 7, 14 days times three replicates M=9) as features. For computing one Lasso regression, m fraction of features was randomly selected (Fig. 2A, left panel, gray columns), one gene was selected as target (response variable, a vector of m×M values; Fig. 2A, left panel, red row), the remaining (N−1) genes served as the pool of regulators (predictor variables) where n21×N of linear regulators and n22×N of nonlinear regulators (Fig. 2A, left panel, gray rows) were randomly selected without reusing regulator. The predictor variables (matrix of m × M rows and (n21+n22)×N columns; Fig. 2A, left, black blocks) were composed of linear variables (matrix of m×M rows and n21×N columns) and nonlinear variables (matrix of m×M rows and n22×N columns, calculated by multiplying the m×N features from 12n22×N nonlinear regulators and the values of the remaining 12n22×N nonlinear regulators with value and sign adjustment). Then, the response variable y and predictor variables X were trained in Lasso model 12mM‖y−Xβ‖22+α‖β‖1, which solved the coefficient β as the signed weight for each regulator toward the target (Fig. 2A, middle panel). This procedure was repeated for b times for the same target. Then the same repeated procedure was repeated for every gene in the input data so all the genes have been the target in regression. The final weight for an edge from one gene to another gene were the mean value of all computed weights for the same pair (Fig. 2A, right panel). The parameters m, n21, n22, b and α regularization for Lasso were optimized by PoLoBag (15). GRNs for the same treatment condition were constructed with ten different random initializations to account for the variability from bootstrapping (random selection and repeating) process. The resulting GRNs each contained 800 nodes (genes) and weighted directed edges (interactions) with weights ranging from −1 to 1.

For the GRNs in mESC conversion, RNA-seq was downloaded as reads per million (RPM) from GSE208199 (16). The variance of gene expression was evaluated by two-way ANOVA with treatment and time (days) as two factors for calculating P value. Top 450 most variable genes by treatment and by time with smallest P values were retained for GRN construction. For samples on days 3, 4, and 6, 730 genes were used. For samples on days 0, 2, 3, 4, and 6, 765 genes were used. GRNs for the same treatment condition were constructed with five different random initializations.

Gene regulatory network analysis

For computing network topological properties, each GRN was separated into two subgraphs with positively signed edges and negatively signed edges exclusively. Small-world coefficient (σ) is defined as follows:σ=CCrLLr (17), where C represents mean clustering coefficient of all nodes, L represents mean shortest path length between all node pairs, Cr and Lr represent the mean clustering coefficient and mean shortest path length of a randomized equivalent of the network, respectively. Specifically, cluster coefficient is the ratio between the number of triangles in which a node participates and the maximum possible number of triangles around the node (10). Shortest path length is the length of the path between a pair of nodes with the minimum number of edges. A network is considered as small-world network if σ>1. For computing small-world coefficient, weighted directed networks were transformed to unweighted undirected networks.

GRNs were sparsified by eliminating edges with absolute edge weight values lower than a threshold value, and removing all disconnected nodes. To apply a soft thresholding, the threshold value range was set at 0.2 to 0.9 with 0.002 step for GRNs with positive edges, 0.1 to 0.6 with 0.003 step for AMn GRNs with negative edges, and 0.04 to 0.4 with 0.002 step for other GRNs with negative edges. For the GRNs in mESC conversion, the threshold value range was set at 0.24 to 0.7 with 0.023 step for GRNs with positive edges. Other processing steps were the same as described above. The lowest threshold was set to generate random equivalent network for calculating small-world coefficient with reasonable computational time, and the highest threshold was set to include the smallest GRNs containing 20 nodes at least. To determine whether the network degree distributions were power law, the data from each GRN was fit to power law via optimization of maximum likelihood, then exponential or log-normal distribution fit was compared with power law fit by log-likelihood ratio to find the better distribution that describes the data. Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to calculate the P value (18, 19).

Betweenness centrality of a node in a weighted and directed network is computed based on node pair dependency CB(v)=1(n−1)(n−2)∑s≠vδs⋅(v), δs⋅(v)=∑w:v∈Ps(w)=(1+δs⋅(w))σsvσsw (60), where v is a node from the network, n is the total number of nodes, betweenness centrality CB of node v is dependent on the ratio of shortest paths σswv between any other nodes s and w where v lies on. Betweenness centrality of a node reflects its participation in the shortest paths between other nodes, and removal of the nodes with highest betweenness will most disrupt the communications between other nodes in the network (61). Directed Louvain algorithm (22) was used for detecting communities in GRNs with resolution parameter 1. Community gene expression was calculated by 200 fits of locally estimated scatter plot smoothing (LOESS) regression with 80% of data randomly selected for each fit, where all the genes from the community from the entire time series were used as expression data for regression.

Gene annotation and ontology

TFs were retrieved from QuickGO http://ebi.ac.uk/QuickGO/ with terms GO:0001228, GO:0003700, GO:0001227, GO:0000981, GO:0001217, GO:0004879, GO:0001216, GO:0034246, GO:0098531 in Homo sapiens and Mus musculus. GO enrichment analysis of biological process was performed with clusterProfiler (version 4.2.2) (62). enrichGO function was applied to genes from GRN communities with minimum community size = 20 nodes, and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 denoted as significant.

ChIP-seq

ChIP-seq of Ascl1 and Ngn2 on mESCs was downloaded as FASTQ from GSE208199 and processed as previously described (16). Briefly, reads were aligned to mm10 reference genome by Bowtie2 (version 2.4.4) (63) and converted to BAM files by SAMtools (version 1.16.1) (64). SAM files were used for peak calling by MACS2 (version 2.2.7.1) (65). Diffbind (66) was used to merge consensus regions between biological replicates and calculate fold change and P values for differentially bound regions between Ascl1 and Ngn2. For visualization, bigWig files were generated with deepTools (version 3.5.5) (67), and multiple files from the same treatment were averaged by bigwigAverage. Mouse embryo forebrain (E10.5) H3K4me3 binding profile was downloaded from ENCODE with accession ENCFF055QJW (23). karyoploteR (68) was used to generate plots at region of interest.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed and visualized with Python3 and R. Network analysis was performed with NetworkX (version 2.5) and scikit-network (version 0.20.0). Data were represented as mean ± SD or mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated. Statistical tests applied for the data were indicated in figure legends. Values of P<0.05 were denoted as significant.
