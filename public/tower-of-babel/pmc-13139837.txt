An Abbreviated Assessment Tool for Detection of Pulmonary Hypertension in Patients With Interstitial Lung Disease
Authors: Chebly Dagher, David M. O'Sullivan, Harrison W. Farber, Raj Parikh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary hypertension (PH) is a major complication of interstitial lung disease (ILD) and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Early recognition of PH in ILD is crucial for guiding diagnostic evaluation, initiating treatment, and considering lung transplantation. The previously developed PH‐ILD Detection Tool by Parikh et al. incorporated eight variables to stratify patients into low‐, intermediate‐, and high‐risk groups, and was subsequently validated in an independent cohort. While effective, the multi‐component score may be difficult to implement in routine practice. The aim of this study was to develop a simplified assessment tool for PH in ILD that relies on easily obtainable clinical data. We retrospectively evaluated 154 ILD patients who underwent right heart catheterization. The abbreviated PH‐ILD score assigned one point each for: (1) physical examination findings suggestive of PH, (2) diffusion capacity for carbon monoxide < 40% predicted, and (3) supplemental oxygen use (range 0–3). Diagnostic performance was assessed using sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, and an ROC curve. The mean age was 70.0 ± 12.1 years, and the overall prevalence of PH was 48.1%. PH prevalence increased with higher scores: 14.3% (score 0), 58.1% (score 1), 79.0% (score 2), and 87.2% (score 3). The abbreviated tool demonstrated good discriminatory ability (AUC 0.74; 95% CI 0.65–0.82; p < 0.001). A threshold of ≥ 2 yielded a sensitivity of 90%, specificity of 58%, PPV of 78%, and NPV of 79%, while a score of 3 produced higher specificity (88%) with lower sensitivity (54%). This simplified 3‐point PH‐ILD assessment tool effectively stratifies PH risk in ILD using readily accessible clinical variables. A threshold of ≥ 2 provides high sensitivity for screening, whereas a score of 3 offers improved specificity. This tool may support early identification of PH in ILD and prompt timely referral for specialized evaluation and advanced therapies.

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a serious complication in patients with interstitial lung disease (ILD) [1]. Its development is associated with significantly worse outcomes, as multiple studies have demonstrated increased morbidity and mortality, particularly in patients with advanced lung disease as PH progresses [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Early recognition of PH in ILD is therefore critical to guide diagnostic evaluation, initiate appropriate therapies, and facilitate timely referral for lung transplantation.

In an attempt to facilitate earlier recognition, Parikh et al. developed a non‐invasive screening tool for identifying PH in ILD patients [8]. This tool incorporated eight variables, including history, physical examination findings, 6‐min walk test (6MWT), pulmonary function tests (PFTs), chest imaging, and cardiac biomarkers [8]. Based on the score, patients were stratified into three categories: low, intermediate, and high risk, helping clinicians decide whether further testing and referral were warranted [8]. The tool was subsequently externally validated in an independent cohort [9]. Validation of these findings confirmed the tool's accuracy and highlighted its usefulness in identifying PH among ILD patients and guiding their overall management [8, 9]. Furthermore, higher scores on the PH‐ILD Detection tool were found to correlate with worse hemodynamics, including higher mean pulmonary artery pressure, higher pulmonary vascular resistance, and lower Fick‐derived cardiac index, underscoring its additional prognostic value [10].

Building on this work, we aimed to create a more streamlined and accessible early detection approach for clinical practice. Using the same patient cohort, we developed an abbreviated tool composed of three readily available variables: physical examination findings, diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO), and supplemental oxygen use. This simplified tool is designed to aid clinicians in the early identification of PH in ILD patients and facilitate timely diagnostic evaluation and referral.

Methods

Study Design and Sample

This was a retrospective cohort study using data from a previously published study conducted at Hartford Hospital between October 2016 and February 2022. Of the initial 162 patients with a confirmed diagnosis of ILD, eight were excluded because they were receiving PH therapy at the time of evaluation. Patients were identified using the 10th Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD‐10). The final cohort included 154 ILD patients, all of whom underwent right heart catheterization (RHC) as part of their clinical workup. The diagnosis of ILD was confirmed by high‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT), and PH was defined according to prevailing guidelines at the time of data collection: mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) ≥ 25 mmHg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) ≤ 15 mmHg, and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) > 3 Wood units. This definition was used as it reflected the prevailing guidelines at the time of data collection and allowed for consistency with the original cohort.

Outcome Definition

The primary outcome was the presence of PH, as defined by RHC, in patients with ILD. Patients were stratified based on their abbreviated score: ≤ 1 being low‐risk, 2 being intermediate‐risk, and 3 being high‐risk. The prevalence of PH was calculated within each score group.

This study was approved by the Hartford HealthCare Institutional Review Board (HHC‐2022‐0014).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics and PH prevalence by score category. Diagnostic performance of the abbreviated score was assessed by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) for thresholds of ≥ 2 and = 3. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was constructed to evaluate the overall discriminatory ability of the abbreviated score, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated with 95% confidence intervals. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

When stratified by the abbreviated 3‐point score, the prevalence of PH increased with higher scores. Among patients with a score of 0 (n = 14), 14.3% had PH; with a score of 1 (n = 31), 58.1% had PH; with a score of 2 (n = 62), 79.0% had PH; and with a score of 3 (n = 47), 87.2% had PH.

A ROC curve was constructed using data derived from score‐stratified PH prevalence across the full range of abbreviated assessment tool scores (0–3). The abbreviated 3‐point tool demonstrated an AUC of 0.74, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.65 to 0.82 and a p‐value < 0.001, indicating statistically significant discriminatory performance in identifying PH‐ILD (Figure 1). Using a threshold of score ≥ 2, the abbreviated tool demonstrated a sensitivity of 90%, specificity of 58%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 78%, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 79%. A threshold of score = 3 yielded a sensitivity of 54%, specificity of 88%, PPV of 87%, and NPV of 56%.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of patients with ILD, we demonstrated that a simplified 3‐point PH‐ILD detection tool can effectively stratify patients by their risk of PH. These findings suggest that a streamlined approach based on three easily obtainable clinical variables, physical examination findings (if any sign is present), DLCO < 40% predicted, and the use of supplemental oxygen, can provide a practical method for early identification of PH in ILD.

Our results build on prior work by Parikh et al., who developed and validated the original PH‐ILD Detection Tool using eight variables [8, 9]. While that model demonstrated strong predictive accuracy, its multi‐component design could pose challenges for routine clinical use. By condensing the number of metrics to three variables that are readily available in most clinical settings, our abbreviated tool maintains the core predictive concept while improving feasibility.

The three variables incorporated into our abbreviated assessment tool were chosen because of their clinical relevance and accessibility, as well as high combined SN and SP within the original study. Physical examination findings such as elevated JVP, pedal edema, ascites, an accentuated pulmonic component of P2, and/or parasternal heave are classic signs of right heart dysfunction associated with PH [11, 12]. In ILD, these are often the features that prompt clinicians to suspect PH. When the DLCO is disproportionately reduced compared with other components of the PFTs, functional status, or radiographic findings, it may suggest the presence of concomitant PH due to ventilation‐perfusion inhomogeneity [3]. A lower DLCO has consistently been associated with worse prognosis in ILD and a greater likelihood of concurrent PH [13, 14, 15, 16, 17].

Supplemental oxygen use is another practical marker that has been independently linked to PH in ILD [13, 18, 19]. Its inclusion highlights the role of hypoxemia as both a consequence of advanced parenchymal disease and a driver of pulmonary vascular remodeling [13, 19, 20].

When evaluated collectively, each of the three components provided meaningful discriminatory value, with physical exam findings showing the highest specificity, supplemental oxygen use offering the highest sensitivity, and DLCO demonstrating a balanced profile between sensitivity and specificity. Stratification by the total 3‐point score revealed a progressive increase in PH prevalence, ranging from 14.3% in patients with a score of 0% to 87.2% in those with a score of 3. Notably, even patients with an abbreviated score of 0 demonstrated a 14.3% prevalence of PH. This likely reflects the high pre‐test probability in our referral cohort, as all patients underwent RHC based on clinician concern prior to scoring. A score of ≥ 2 identified most PH‐ILD cases with high sensitivity, while a score of 3 offered greater specificity, suggesting that the tool can be flexibly applied depending on the clinical context. The abbreviated score is best interpreted as a risk stratification tool, with a threshold of ≥ 2 favoring sensitivity for screening and a score of 3 providing higher specificity. More granular, multi‐level risk models may further enhance clinical utility and warrant evaluation in future studies. Importantly, the overall AUC of 0.74 confirmed that the abbreviated tool achieves statistically significant discriminatory performance in identifying PH among ILD patients despite its simplicity.

Strengths of this study include the use of a well‐characterized cohort in which all patients underwent RHC, the gold standard for diagnosing PH, and the practical nature of the variables selected. However, this study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, it was retrospective in design and conducted at a single tertiary center, which may limit generalizability to broader ILD populations. The moderate sample size further restricts the ability to detect more subtle associations, and the absence of an external validation cohort warrants caution in extrapolating the utility of the abbreviated tool. While the original PH‐ILD Detection Tool has undergone external validation, further studies are needed to validate this abbreviated model in larger, independent cohorts. Additionally, the presence of PH in 14.3% of patients with an abbreviated score of 0 likely reflects the high pre‐test probability in this cohort, as all patients underwent RHC based on clinician concern prior to scoring. This introduces spectrum bias and suggests that the tool's performance may differ in unselected ILD populations. Importantly, this finding highlights that the abbreviated score should not be used to exclude PH, particularly in patients with persistent clinical suspicion. Finally, while the abbreviated tool demonstrated reasonable discriminatory ability (AUC 0.74), its predictive performance is less robust than the original eight‐variable model, underscoring that it should serve as a practical complement to, rather than a replacement for, comprehensive evaluation and confirmatory testing. While equal weighting was chosen to preserve simplicity, prior models assigned greater weight to certain variables such as supplemental oxygen, and future studies may explore weighted approaches to optimize predictive performance. We acknowledge that current guidelines define pulmonary hypertension using lower hemodynamic thresholds, and future studies should evaluate the performance of this tool using updated criteria.

Nonetheless, these findings have important clinical implications. The proposed abbreviated tool offers a pragmatic and accessible approach that can be implemented in both tertiary and community settings to support early recognition of PH among ILD patients. Earlier identification of PH facilitates timely referral to specialized centers, initiation of appropriate therapies, and consideration of advanced interventions such as inhaled treprostinil or lung transplantation.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a simplified 3‐point PH‐ILD assessment tool based on physical examination findings, DLCO < 40% predicted, and supplemental oxygen use. The score demonstrated a progressive increase in PH prevalence with higher values and good discriminatory accuracy (AUC 0.74). A threshold of ≥ 2 achieved high sensitivity, while a score of 3 provided high specificity, allowing flexible use depending on clinical context. This abbreviated tool provides a pragmatic approach for the early recognition of PH among ILD patients and may lead to earlier diagnostic evaluation, referral, and initiation of advanced therapies. Further multicenter validation is needed to confirm its generalizability.
