Adaptation and Translation of the Brain Fog Scale into the Indian Psycho-Socio-Cultural Context
Authors: Himanshi Khanna, Neeraj Panwar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrain fog is a subjective experience characterised by forgetfulness, poor concentration, mental slowness, dissociation and fatigue, with individuals describing experiences such as a ‘mental wall’ over formerly simple tasks. During and after the 2019 pandemic, scientific enquiry into brain fog was revived, explored and studied worldwide. Specifically, a large number of studies focused on the COVID-19 population and established strong associations with long COVID. Further, this in-depth research interest has led to the emergence of brain fog as a transdiagnostic factor.PurposeOver the past half-decade, given the said and established facts, the majority of scientific evidence has been contributed by researchers outside India. This highlights that brain fog is subjectively experienced worldwide, but its objective expression varies. Given the existing literature gap, the present article focuses on developing the psychometric properties of the Brain Fog Scale (BFS) and on adapting and translating it into Hindi.MethodsFor this purpose, a set of data was collected from participants (N = 159) with a mean age of 32.5 years, including representation from both genders. Standardised measures, the original and translated forms of the BFS, along with quality of life (QoL), were administered.ResultsPrincipal component analysis revealed a refined 26-item, two-component structure comprising Psychophysiological Brain Fog (PsyPhyBF) and Cognitive Brain Fog, accounting for 52.8% of the total variance. The adapted scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.91 and 0.92 for the two components, respectively, and 0.95 for the overall scale. Both dimensions of brain fog demonstrated significant negative associations with QoL, with PsyPhyBF emerging as a significant predictor of poorer QoL.ConclusionThe findings support the reliability and construct validity of the adapted Hindi version of the BFS and highlight the importance of culturally sensitive assessment tools for understanding subjective cognitive experiences in diverse populations.

Introduction

Brain fog is widely used in both social contexts and scientific literature to describe a subjective experience of perceived cognitive dysfunction. 1 Colloquially, it refers to forgetfulness, poor concentration, mental slowness, dissociation and fatigue, with individuals describing experiences such as a ‘mental wall’ over formerly simple tasks.2, 3 Empirical literature similarly characterises brain fog as a state of mental cloudiness, lack of focus and clarity, difficulty thinking or multitasking and problems with attention, speech and memory—resulting in an altered state of reduced alertness.4–6

Before the COVID-19 pandemic, brain fog was primarily reported in conditions such as chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia (often referred to as fibro-fog) and functional neurological disorders.5, 7–9 Although subjective cognitive difficulties were acknowledged, standardised assessment tools were limited, and research predominantly relied on self-reported descriptions rather than structured measures. 10

The COVID-19 pandemic, especially long COVID, revived global scientific interest in brain fog.3, 11, 12 Emerging evidence has identified measurable neurobiological correlates, 13 supporting the validity of brain fog as a clinically relevant construct rather than solely a subjective experience.

Lately, social media has been increasingly examined as a valuable source of data for understanding lived experiences, offering a platform where individuals openly share their health-related concerns. In the context of brain fog, analyses of platforms such as Reddit and other social media have provided insights into how individuals describe their subjective experiences and attribute symptoms to factors such as illness, medication, psychiatric conditions and lifestyle influences.14, 15 These narratives further suggest that brain fog can significantly affect self-perception, everyday functioning and broader psychosocial experiences, often characterised by fluctuating cloudiness, mental heaviness and cognitive difficulties that interfere with daily engagement and social interaction. Experiences involving cognitive difficulties and health-related concerns have also been associated with broader psychosocial and developmental outcomes, 16 while stronger social well-being (SWB) has been linked with better functioning and slower decline in physical outcomes. 17 Collectively, these findings indicate that subjective cognitive experiences such as brain fog may extend beyond cognition alone and may influence broader dimensions of quality of life (QoL) and SWB.

A growing body of literature indicates brain fog as a transdiagnostic phenomenon, appearing across several medical and psychological conditions.18–26 In a recent study, Denno et al. 27 conceptualised brain fog across three levels: a sense of mental cloudiness or impaired focus, often synonymous with ‘poor concentration’; as a syndrome: a cluster of cognitive symptoms that may be disorder-specific (e.g., fibro-fog, COVID-19 brain fog) or cross-diagnostic and as a vague descriptor: a broad term for cognitive complaints without precise boundaries; across multiple medical and psychological conditions.

Although the underlying mechanisms of brain fog are not yet fully understood, it is proposed to arise from shared biological mechanisms (such as neuroinflammation and blood–brain barrier dysfunction), common cognitive processes (including slowed processing, attentional deficits, fatigue and metacognitive distortions) and linguistic overlap, where diverse experiences are grouped under the same label ‘brain fog’, potentially creating an apparent sense of shared pathology, 27 as also reflected in subjective reports.

Given the subjective and functionally impairing nature of brain fog, understanding its relationship with broader indicators such as QoL and SWB may provide a more comprehensive understanding of its real-world implications.

While comprehensive analyses of online discourse are limited, protocols such as Dass and Packham 28 highlight the subjective and socially constructed nature of brain fog, the emerging commonalities in how people describe it and the role of language and cultural context in shaping these descriptions. Globally, similar underlying experiences have been reported,25, 26, 29 although the ways they are expressed vary across cultures. This underscores the ongoing need for systematic and culturally sensitive approaches to capture subjective cognitive experiences in a standardised manner.30–32 This gap remains particularly relevant for brain fog, which currently lacks widely accepted standardised instruments for cross-cultural research.

Most existing brain fog measures have been developed in Western contexts33–36 or are clinically focused scales,37, 38 with limited availability of culturally adapted and validated tools for use in the general population in Asian contexts, particularly India.

Rationale

Considering brain fog as a subjective experience, its accurate assessment requires linguistically and culturally appropriate tools that allow individuals to meaningfully express their experiences. The Brain Fog Scale (BFS) 33 is a 30-item measure assessing brain fog across three domains—cognitive, physiological and psychological—with demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.945). Translating and adapting this scale into Hindi may enhance comprehension and response accuracy, thereby enabling a culturally grounded and contextually relevant assessment of brain fog among Indian populations.

In line with the existing literature, there remains a need to systematically capture subjective cognitive experiences across diverse populations through culturally adapted and standardised measurement tools, while also examining their relationship with broader psychosocial indicators such as QoL and SWB.

Methods

Research Design

This study employed a correlational research design to translate and culturally adapt the BFS 33 for use in the Indian context and to evaluate its psychometric properties. A correlational approach was appropriate as the study aimed to assess relationships among variables to establish reliability and validity, without manipulation or causal inference.

Participants

Using the G*Power tool, 39 a priori power analysis was conducted under the family type Exact, with the statistical test set to Correlation. The analysis was based on an effect size of 0.70, an alpha level of 0.05 and a desired statistical power of 0.95. The initial estimated sample size required for the study was 115 participants. Accounting for a 10% anticipated dropout rate, the adjusted target sample size was increased to 130 participants. 40 Ultimately, a total of 180 participants took part, but only 159 were retained in the final analysis.

A total of 159 adults aged 21–59 years residing in Delhi NCR participated in the study. Participants were included if they were fluent in both Hindi and English, able to provide informed consent and able to read and understand Hindi and English.

Measures

A self-developed socio-demographic sheet was used for collecting information on various important components of the individual, including name, age, sex, date of birth, socio-economic status, family type, occupation, languages known and any present or history of physical, neurological or psychiatric problems. To assess brain fog, the BFS, 33 a 30-item self-reporting scale rated on a 5-point Likert scale, was used, divided into three subscales: Cognitive symptoms, physiological symptoms and psychological symptoms. Higher scores reflect greater levels of brain fog (Cronbach’s α = 0.95). The QoL was assessed using the WHO QoL Scale BREF, Hindi Version, 41 a 26-item self-report measure. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale. It has demonstrated acceptable reliability in Indian populations (domain alphas typically 0.66–0.84). The SWB Scale 42 is a 15-item, 7-point Likert-type self-report scale that assesses SWB. Cronbach’s alpha values for the domains range from 0.41 to 0.81, indicating varying levels of internal consistency.

Procedure

This study received Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval RCEC/00533/05/25. The present scale was selected for research, translation and revalidated in the Indian Psychosociocultural context. Permission was obtained from the author who developed the original scale, dated 16 April 2025. Once the author granted permission, the translation process was initiated using the ABA pattern. The BFS was translated into Hindi in accordance with standard guidelines for cross-cultural adaptation.43, 44

Two independent bilingual translators conducted forward translation from English to Hindi, followed by reconciliation into a single version. Two additional bilingual translators, blinded to the original, performed backward translation. An expert committee, comprising the principal investigator (a clinical psychologist), the supervising guide and bilingual linguists, reviewed the translations to ensure semantic, idiomatic, experiential and conceptual equivalence, resolving discrepancies and evaluating cognitive and clinical relevance.

Data were collected via a secure Google Form survey. Regarding human participation, ethical protocols were followed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, including informed consent from participants. Once they understood the primary objective of the research, which was handled by the researcher/investigator through the process of deception and debriefing at the beginning and end of data collection, respectively.

Anonymity and confidentiality were maintained throughout the data collection process. All measures were self-administered and required approximately 10–15 min to complete.

Participants were contacted, and each, in small groups of 5–6 or individually, was provided with instructions and motivated to volunteer for the research. The investigator ensured that the Google Form was being filled out by the participants in their presence. It was ensured that no participant was allowed to fill it out later or share it with anyone in their contact list via social media handles.

A total of 180 individuals were approached, of whom 159 participants completed the study and were included in the final analysis.

Statistical Analysis

To achieve the objectives of the present research, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted, followed by reliability testing and correlation analysis of revised factors along with QoL and SWB. To analyse the data, Jamovi Project (ver. 2.7) 45 was used.

During the psychometric refinement of the adapted BFS, four items from the original Cognitive domain were excluded from the final version. These included Item 3: I have difficulty finding the right words while speaking (मुझे बात करते समय सही शदों का चयन करने में किठनाई होती है।), Item 5: I am easily distracted (मेरा यान आसानी से भटक जाता है।), Item 11: I have difficulty making decisions (मुझे िनणय लेने में कठिनाई होती है।) and Item 16: I make unexpected mistakes while performing a task (मैं कोई काय करते समय अनपेित गलितयाँ कर बैठता/बैठती हूँ।). These items were excluded during the exploratory psychometric refinement process because they did not contribute adequately to the final factor structure. Their exclusion resulted in a more parsimonious and interpretable 26-item, two-factor solution that demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties.

The first dimension, labelled Psychophysiological Brain Fog (PsyPhyBF), consisted of items reflecting fatigue, slowed functioning, reduced mental energy, bodily heaviness and psychophysiological slowing. The second dimension, labelled Cognitive Brain Fog (CogBF), comprised items related to attentional difficulties, forgetfulness, impaired concentration, mental confusion and reduced cognitive clarity. Most retained items demonstrated moderate to strong component loadings, supporting the preliminary interpretability of the adapted two-factor structure.

The overall adapted scale yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95, indicating high internal homogeneity among the retained items. Further examination of item-deleted statistics indicated that removal of any individual item did not substantially improve reliability and internal consistency, suggesting that all retained items contributed meaningfully to the measurement of their respective constructs.

Based on eigenvalues greater than 1 and visual inspection of the scree plot, a two-component solution comprising 26 items was retained. The adapted Hindi version of the BFS demonstrated a modified two-factor structure comprising 26 retained items, differing from the original 30-item, three-factor structure proposed by Atik and Manav. 33 The retained items were reorganised into two dimensions: PsyPhyBF (Factor 1: items 29, 20, 25, 22, 28, 26, 27, 30, 17, 21, 24, 18 and 19) and CogBF (Factor 2: items 10, 9, 7, 12, 4, 8, 6, 13, 14, 23, 1, 2 and 15). The adapted structure reflected a merging of psychological and physiological symptoms into a single psychophysiological dimension alongside a distinct cognitive factor, further suggesting variation in the manifestation and organisation of brain fog symptoms within the present cultural context.

Discussion

The present study examined the psychometric properties of the adapted Hindi version of the BFS and explored the relationship between brain fog, QoL and SWB in a non-clinical adult sample. Overall, the findings suggest that brain fog is a relevant subjective cognitive experience within the general population and is meaningfully associated with broader indicators of psychosocial functioning and well-being. The adapted version of the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency and yielded a refined two-factor structure comprising PsyPhyBF and CogBF. Additionally, higher levels of brain fog, particularly psychophysiological manifestations, were associated with poorer QoL and reduced SWB.

The findings support the growing conceptualisation of brain fog as a multidimensional and heterogeneous phenomenon rather than a singular cognitive complaint.10, 29 Considerable variability in participant scores further suggests that the intensity and expression of brain fog may differ across individuals and may be shaped by psychological, physiological, social and cultural influences.46, 47

Although brain fog is often discussed primarily in relation to attentional and memory difficulties, the present findings indicate that its impact extends beyond cognition alone and may influence broader dimensions of daily functioning and perceived well-being. This is consistent with previous literature suggesting that subjective cognitive difficulties may affect productivity, communication, daily engagement and overall functioning even in the absence of measurable neuropsychological deficits.14, 48, 49

A notable finding of the present study was the emergence of a two-factor structure rather than replication of the original three-factor model. While the original BFS comprised separate Cognitive, Psychological and Physiological domains, the adapted Hindi version yielded two components: PsyPhyBF and CogBF. Specifically, the original Psychological and Physiological domains merged into a single PsyPhyBF factor, whereas the CogBF domain remained largely preserved following psychometric refinement despite the exclusion of four cognitive items. This finding suggests that, within the present sample, psychological and physiological manifestations of brain fog were represented as a unified psychophysiological construct, whereas cognitive symptoms remained relatively distinct. One possible interpretation is that participants experienced or perceived psychological and physiological manifestations of brain fog as closely interconnected rather than as separate dimensions. However, as cultural, linguistic, contextual and related variables were not directly assessed, this interpretation should be considered tentative and warrants further investigation. Furthermore, the exclusion of four items from the original Cognitive domain highlights the importance of evaluating individual item performance during cross-cultural adaptation to ensure that the adapted instrument adequately represents the underlying construct within the target population. These findings also suggest that the latent structure of subjective cognitive experiences may not necessarily replicate identically across populations.44, 51

The psychophysiological dimension of brain fog demonstrated significant negative associations with both SWB and QoL, indicating that greater experiences of fatigue, slowed functioning, reduced mental energy and psychophysiological strain were associated with poorer perceived functioning and lower well-being. These findings are consistent with previous literature indicating that mental fatigue, cognitive strain, reduced concentration and persistent fatigue are associated with poorer psychological and functional outcomes, including reduced QoL.50, 52–54

The present findings further suggest that the more externally experienced and functionally impairing aspects of brain fog may have a stronger influence on individuals’ day-to-day adjustment, interpersonal engagement and overall perception of life quality.15, 55 Since psychophysiological symptoms are often more behaviourally observable, they may interfere more directly with productivity, social participation, emotional functioning and routine activities, thereby contributing to poorer QoL and SWB.29, 56

In contrast, the CogBF dimension showed comparatively weaker associations with QoL and was not significantly associated with SWB. This may suggest that subjective cognitive difficulties, although recognised by participants as a distinct component of brain fog, were less closely related to perceived social functioning than psychophysiological manifestations within the present sample. However, this interpretation should be considered tentative and requires further investigation in independent and clinical samples.

The findings of the present study also highlight the potential utility of assessing brain fog in relation to broader psychosocial constructs such as QoL and SWB. Given the covert and subjective nature of brain fog, understanding its functional implications through associated domains may provide a more comprehensive picture of its real-world impact.15, 47 The adapted Hindi version of the BFS may therefore serve as a culturally relevant tool for identifying subjective cognitive difficulties and understanding their broader psychosocial correlates within non-clinical Indian adults.

Limitations

The findings of the present study should be interpreted in light of certain limitations. First, the factor structure of the adapted BFS was derived using PCA, an exploratory technique appropriate for scale adaptation. Although the proposed two-factor solution demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties within the present sample, future studies should examine the proposed structure using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) in an independent sample to further establish its structural validity and generalisability. In addition, the present study assessed only the internal consistency of the adapted scale. Test–retest reliability was not examined; therefore, the temporal stability of the instrument could not be established. Future studies should evaluate test–retest reliability to further strengthen the evidence supporting the reliability of the adapted scale. Additionally, the correlational design limits causal interpretation of the observed relationships between brain fog, QoL and SWB. The study also relied exclusively on self-report measures, which may be influenced by response biases and subjective interpretation. Furthermore, the study employed convenience sampling and included a non-clinical adult sample; therefore, the findings may not be directly generalisable to clinical populations or to individuals from different demographic and cultural backgrounds. Further validation across diverse and clinical samples is warranted. Future research employing independent validation samples, CFA, longitudinal designs, clinical populations and multimethod assessment approaches would further strengthen the evidence for the structural validity, reliability and broader applicability of the adapted BFS.

Conclusion

The present study supports the cultural adaptation and Hindi translation of the BFS within the Indian psycho-socio-cultural context. The findings indicated that the adapted scale demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties, including excellent internal consistency and a two-component structure identified within the present sample, comprising PsyPhyBF and CogBF. The results further indicated significant negative associations between brain fog and QoL, with PsyPhyBF emerging as a significant predictor of poorer QoL outcomes.

These findings highlight the broader psychosocial and functional relevance of subjective cognitive difficulties and reinforce the importance of culturally sensitive assessment tools for diverse populations. The adapted Hindi version of the BFS demonstrated excellent internal consistency and may serve as a useful instrument for assessing subjective cognitive experiences in non-clinical Indian adults.

Furthermore, the study contributes to the growing literature conceptualising brain fog as a transdiagnostic phenomenon with implications extending beyond post-COVID conditions. Future research should examine the clinical utility of the adapted scale across diverse populations using longitudinal designs and independent validation samples before broader clinical application.
