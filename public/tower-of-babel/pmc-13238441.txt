Facilitating Research Participation: How Researchers Support People With Communication Support Needs to Decide to Participate
Authors: Deborah L Lutz, Karen R Fisher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEvidence is needed about the practicalities of how researchers support people with intellectual disabilities and communication support needs to facilitate their choice to participate in research. What are the elements of effective support from researchers that build on trusted relationships between the person and supporters for that decision?MethodThe article applies an analytical framework about effective support for decision making to qualitative data about researchers' support for choices to participate in three ethnographic case studies.ResultsThe analysis found researchers became supporters in the person's decision making by directly supporting the person and engaging with their supporters in ways that prioritised the person's rights.ConclusionThe analysis extends the framework by finding that when researchers position themselves as supporters, they can leverage trusted relationships and demonstrate to facilitate the person's right to choose whether to participate in research, representing a methodological and ethical advance for inclusive research practice.Keywords: communication support needs, decision making, intellectual disabilities, qualitative field research, research participation, supporters

Introduction

Including and valuing the perspectives of people with intellectual disabilities has become an expectation of ethical practice in disability research (Walmsley et al. 2018). Supporting people to decide whether to participate in research requires the researcher to be cognisant of the person's communication support needs and their understanding the information about the study (Iacono and Murray 2003; Lutz 2023). Building this awareness means the researcher usually relies on supporters who are close to the person and familiar with the person's communication preferences (Hall et al. 2025; Lutz et al. 2016). The supporters' expectations of the person's capacity to be involved in research can influence the person's decision to participate in research. The researcher needs to manage these expectations to facilitate the person's decision making about whether to participate in research (Boettcher et al. 2022; McDonald and Kidney 2012).

In this context, the researcher's role entails providing information about the person's rights to participate and supporting the person to make that decision (Brinkmann and Kvale 2015; Wittich et al. 2023). This principle helps protect their autonomy and dignity, ensuring they are not excluded or coerced into research participation (Nilsson and Broström 2019). While supported decision making in everyday life is well documented, little is known about the specific practice of how researchers manage this role when they first enter a research setting to gain consent to spend time with the person with communication support needs and their supporters. The decision to participate in research is a distinct context, involving an unfamiliar person, the researcher, navigating existing relationships and power dynamics within a support network.

This article draws on qualitative data collected as part of a study how relationships between people with intellectual disabilities, some of whom had communication support needs, and their personal assistants were influenced by personalised support policies (Lutz 2020). It analyses three case studies from this study to examine the support to people to decide whether to participate. It adapts the Bigby and Douglas (2020) features of effective support for decision making practice to reflect on how researchers can engage people with communication support needs and their supporters to facilitate decision making to participate in research.

Supported Decision Making for People With Intellectual Disabilities

Article 12 of the CRPD focuses on ensuring equal recognition before the law for people with disabilities (United Nations 2006). It emphasises autonomy and the right to make decisions for oneself, including support to make informed decisions about participation in research (Arstein‐Kerslake and Flynn 2016; Iacono and Carling‐Jenkins 2012). When support is needed for decision making, the process means support to respect the person's will and preferences so their participation and self‐determination are promoted (Gooding 2013; Shogren and Wehmeyer 2015). Implementing these principles requires that people with intellectual disabilities receive the assistance and accommodations they need to make their own decisions (Watson 2023).

In supported decision making, the supporter acts as a facilitator who assists the person to understand information and communicate their preferences while respecting their autonomy and right to make their own decisions (Bigby and Douglas 2020; Watson et al. 2017). The supporter's role can be taken by legal representatives, guardians, social advocates, house mates, support staff, friends and family members (Boettcher et al. 2022; McDonald and Kidney 2012). The role entails assisting the person to make a decision. Distinct from substitute decision making, it is not to replace the person's decision and not to decide on the person's behalf based on what the supporter believes is in the best interest of the person (Davidson et al. 2015). This support approach aims to facilitate the person's self‐determination to make decisions about their lives, including decisions about their participation in research (Shogren and Wehmeyer 2015).

Within supported decision making literature about people with intellectual disabilities, studies have explored the role of relationships between supporters and people with intellectual disabilities (e.g., Bigby et al. 2022; Browning et al. 2021; Shogren and Wehmeyer 2015). Some of these studies focus specifically on people with intellectual disabilities and communication support needs (e.g., Watson 2016, 2023). Several studies focus on the role of the supporter (e.g., Bigby et al. 2019; Watson 2016; Watson et al. 2017) and highlight the centrality of the supporter's role in enhancing the person's agency as a decision maker (Bigby and Douglas 2020; Browning et al. 2021). Watson et al. (2017), for example, examined the experiences of supported decision making for people intellectual disabilities and communication support needs and the role of supporters. The analysis identified that supporters were most effective when they were relationally close to the person and had knowledge of their life history. This finding implies that involving supporters early in research planning facilitates mutual learning and respects the person's preferences about whether to participate.

Research Participation of People With Communication Support Needs

Including people with intellectual disabilities and communication support needs in research is key to understanding their experiences and to gain critical insights into their daily lives and social circumstances (e.g., Iacono and Carling‐Jenkins 2012; Lutz 2023). Informed consent requires that potential participants understand the nature, purpose, risks and benefits of the research and voluntarily agree to participate (Morris et al. 2024). People with communication support needs may need this process to be adapted with information in accessible formats through easy‐to‐read language, visual aids and supported decision making processes (Lutz and Fisher 2023; Meltzer et al. 2025). Supporters' knowledge about the person's communication preferences can help to make these arrangements. Spending prolonged time observing can help build trusting relationships and understanding of the person's communication behaviour (Lutz 2023).

Researchers often directly engage with supporters to facilitate the participation of people with intellectual disabilities and communication support needs in research. Lutz et al. (2016) defined supporters as trusted people who help the person with communication support needs to answer for themselves in the interview and emphasised collaborative engagement between the researcher and supporter as a facilitator for inclusive research practice. Their research did not analyse the earlier process of how to support the person to decide whether to participate.

Consent can be a one‐off event and a continuous process of assent and re‐affirmation, where the person's willingness to participate is monitored and respected throughout the research (Iacono and Murray 2003; Lutz 2023). Before gaining consent or continuous assent from the person, researchers often liaise with supporters to seek access to the person with intellectual disabilities, meet with them to explain the research and engage them about the potential research process. Given the central role that supporters play in facilitating communication and access, it is essential to understand how interactions between the researcher, the person and their supporters can empower the person to make informed, voluntary decisions about participation. This article adds an empirical perspective on how research engagements can facilitate the person's decision making about whether to participate in research.

Analytical Framework

Frameworks to enable good support for decision making practice emphasise that the supporter needs to be committed to the rights of the person and responsive to their will and preferences (e.g., Browning et al. 2021; Bigby et al. 2022; Shogren and Wehmeyer 2015). Bigby and Douglas (2020) summarised seven features of effective support for decision making with people with intellectual disabilities, grounded in research about their experiences. As a practice framework, it functions as a practical guide and educational program tailored for supporters. This focus makes it particularly relevant for our research, which analyses the researcher role in supporting decision making about research. We extend the framework by analysing the researcher's role as a supporter to decide whether to participate.

We grouped the features from Bigby and Douglas (2020) into four dimensions of support for decision making practice based on their common themes for the purpose of applying it as an analytical framework (see Table 1).

Study and Method

The article uses a case study approach to analyse the interactions between the people with communication support needs, their supporters, and the researcher. The data from the three case studies (Vincent, Laura, and Jeff) were collected during 12 months of ethnographic fieldwork, where the field researcher (first author) conducted interviews and participant observation with 10 people with intellectual disabilities and their personal assistant/s in the primary study (Lutz 2020). The ethnographic fieldwork required ongoing reflexive attention to her dual role as researcher and supporter and to the power dynamics inherent in that position. Ethics approval was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee at the University of New South Wales in Sydney, Australia.

The study used two consent forms for the people with intellectual disabilities; one in an easy‐read format with pictures from Photosymbols Ltd. 2016 and the other in plain language only. The first form served as a communication aid to explain and understand the study and obtain verbal (e.g., by saying yes, making a sound that implied an agreement), non‐verbal (e.g., by nodding, other signs of approval) and/or written consent. All the people with intellectual disabilities preferred this first form.

The participant observation method focused on the participation of the researcher rather than just observation. Participation meant the field researcher could respond flexibly to participants' communication preferences (Lutz 2023). Adapting to existing ways of communication, such as using a communication App or engaging in pictorial aids in an interview schedule, helped build trust, prioritise participants' concerns and better understand their communication (Lutz 2023).

The three people with intellectual disabilities all had parents acting as their legal guardians, whose verbal and written consent was sought prior to accessing the person. All names used for research participants are pseudonyms. In Vincent's case, the researcher gained access to the mother through a work colleague. In Laura's and Jeff's cases, the researcher gained access to the mothers through disability organisations that organised support for the person. Initial contact with the parents happened through a phone conversation or an in‐person meeting where the researcher introduced herself and explained the study. After their consent was sought, the researcher tried to access the person directly by organising an in‐person meeting. In Jeff's case, this was not possible and the researcher met Jeff on the day she began the fieldwork with Jeff. The reason was the team leader of the day centre Jeff attended blocked the researcher's access, despite the previous consent from the parent and day centre manager. Because gatekeeping limited the researcher's direct access to Jeff and complicated the continuous assent process, his case required more sustained researcher effort across all four dimensions of the framework and therefore receives slightly more attention in the analysis. The time spent from identifying the supporter to first meeting the person and beginning the continuous assent process for each person ranged from seven to 10 h within a period of four to 11 weeks' time.

Consent from the people with intellectual disabilities and communication support needs was a process of continuous assent and usually supported by a trusted person, such as a parent or personal assistant (Morris et al. 2024). The researcher met two of the three research participants (Vincent, Laura) a few weeks prior to the fieldwork to introduce herself, talked to them about the research in ways she thought would be best understood by them and obtained their written consent. Continuous assent often required explaining again why the researcher was spending time with them and observing signals of agreement and comfort with what was happening. This reflects an ethical commitment to prioritising the person's right to withdraw at any point, even where formal written consent had already been obtained.

The data were coded with the NVivo Software Package 11 by QRS International and assigned to the preliminary category ‘obtaining consent’, created during the coding process of the primary study. Data in this category were further analysed for this article by thematically analysing the data within the Bigby and Douglas (2020) framework, an approach not used in the primary study.

A case study approach is limited in generalising findings to a larger population. Further research could extend the application of the analytical framework to a larger sample. The authors are experienced disability researchers, with field research experience, but they are not people with intellectual disabilities and communication support needs. Another limitation is the use of an older dataset. The data remain highly relevant to current disability research practice about how researchers support people with intellectual disabilities and communication support needs to decide to participate in research.

Case Studies

Vincent

Vincent was in his mid‐20s and lived with his parents, Margaret and Victor, and his sister, Sara, in their family home. Vincent had autism and typically used one or two words to communicate with others, though he mostly relied on non‐verbal communication, such as gently touching the hand of someone he liked and maintaining focused eye contact. Music was calming him and he would listen to songs when he wanted time and space to himself. Vincent had a team of six personal assistants similar in age to Vincent who worked with him on different days throughout the week.

Laura

Laura was a woman in her early forties with Down Syndrome. She displayed a gentle, observant demeanour and lived with her friend Paul, with whom she worked part‐time in restaurants and shared household tasks. William was their primary personal assistant and had worked with them for 5 years. Laura spoke in short sentences; usually single words very quietly and often relied on trusted people like her mother or William to be heard by others.

Jeff

Jeff was 19 and had recently graduated from high school. He lived with his parents and his brother and attended a day centre during the week. Jeff's voice would rise in pitch when he tried to express himself. He used vowels, which he pronounced firmly and loudly, and used facial expressions and gestures to communicate his moods. He did not use recognisable words or conventional verbal speech. When Jeff made sounds, he often looked at people around him, seeming to seek a reaction from them. The staff at the centre often perceived Jeff's vocalisations as shouts and would respond with phrases like, “Soft voice, Jeff” or “Inside voice, Jeff.”

Findings

The findings are structured according to the four dimensions adapted from the Bigby and Douglas (2020) framework: relational and values‐based foundations, person‐centred knowledge and understanding, communication and accessibility, and supporter sustainability. The findings analyse the role of the researcher as a supporter facilitating decision making to participate in research by engaging with the person and their supporters. The analysis draws on examples from the three case studies to illustrate how the four dimensions are connected and build upon the previous one, with the researcher acting as a supporter and contributing to the person realising their right to make decisions.

Relational and Values‐Based Foundations

The first dimension of the framework addresses how relational and values‐based foundations shape the researcher's approach to engaging with the person and their supporters to facilitate decision making about research participation. The researcher had to build trusting relationships with supporters and obtain their consent as guardians before she could access the person.

The initial step to develop a trusting relationship with the person included conversations with supporters as guardians, gaining their trust and building rapport with them. In Jeff's case, the researcher first spoke to his mother, Christine, on the phone before meeting with Jeff.

I (researcher) explained who I was and why I called. She said, “It's completely fine for Jeff to be involved in the study, but you have to check with Bridget, the day centre team leader.” I said I had already spoken to Bridget who referred me onto her as she was Jeff's guardian and mother. I said I would like to email her the consent forms and said, “I will also seek consent from Jeff.” She said, “That's not possible. Jeff doesn't understand what's going on.” (Field notes)

The situation showed that the mother preferred the researcher to engage and build a trusting relationship with her before engaging with Jeff at a later stage, which would probably rely on continuous assent from him. The researcher understood the value of developing a relationship with the supporter as the foundation advocate for Jeff's right to decide to participate.

Managing different assumptions about the person's capacity to make decisions was part of the researcher building relationships with the person's supporters. This approach included modelling values about the person's right to decision making when supporters were less sure about their capacity to make decisions. In Vincent's case, I spoke to his mother Margaret, who was also Vincent's guardian, before I could meet with Vincent. She prepared Vincent and his assistants for the researcher's visit by providing advance notice, facilitating access to Vincent, and positioning him as a potential research participant.

Charlotte [assistant] asked Vincent if he wanted to talk about the research now or shower first. Vincent indicated he wanted the shower by walking towards the bathroom. Victor, Vincent's father explained that Vincent's parents usually sign on behalf of Vincent. I said, “I prefer to let Vincent try and sign the paper himself as Margaret told me that he would be able to do so if he agrees.” (Field notes)

The three supporters, Margaret, Victor, and Charlotte, held differing expectations about Vincent's capacity to make decisions to participate in the research. Margaret demonstrated a positive and facilitative stance, envisioning her son's active involvement in the study. Victor expressed scepticism, reflecting more restrictive assumptions about Vincent's capacity, and Charlotte appeared neutral and supportive.

The researcher's relational and values‐based foundations shaped her approach towards engaging with supporters to facilitate the person's decision making. When supporters held positive expectations, it was easier for the researcher to access the person. When assumptions potentially limited the person's decision making, the researcher relied on her values about maintaining a rights‐based approach, presuming support for the person's capacity. Respectfully managing the supporters' assumptions formed part of building relationships with supporters, illustrating that the researcher's entry into the field is itself a rights‐based intervention.

Person‐Centred Knowledge and Understanding

The second dimension is about the depth of knowledge and understanding of the person supported and how these aspects influence the researcher's engagements to facilitate the decision making. When the researcher gained a supporter's trust, the supporter was more likely to share their knowledge with the researcher about the person's communication preferences. Shared understanding about communication helped the researcher to build a trusting relationship with the person, which Laura's case demonstrates.

Prior to meeting Laura, initial contact was established through a telephone conversation with her mother, Catherine. During this call, Catherine demonstrated investment in safeguarding Laura's autonomy, asking questions about the methods, particularly how information would be elicited from Laura. This early exchange helped gain the supporter's trust and Catherine consented for the researcher to meet Laura. Catherine introduced the researcher to Laura and organised for William, Laura's personal assistant, to be present, as he would inevitably facilitate the decision to participate in the study.

We sat in the living room. Laura looked at me. While I was explaining the planned field research with my own words, Catherine paraphrased sentences and used specific words to facilitate Laura's understanding. She knew how to prompt Laura to check if she understood what I had said. I said, “If there are days where you don't want me to be around, you should let me know.” William looked at Laura and said, “We can kick her out”. Laura laughed. (Field notes)

Catherine's understanding of Laura's communication preferences facilitated preferred explanations of the research. Her presence, alongside familiar people like William, created a safe context in which the researcher could start to gain Laura's trust. Catherine's knowledge of Laura's communication preferences and interests, as well as the humour exchanged between William and Laura, further signalled a comfortable environment. This combination supported the early development of rapport and trust between Laura and the researcher.

A case where it was harder to benefit from the supporter's knowledge and understanding was with Jeff's supporters. The researcher had to model good support for decision making to the supporter by sharing her understanding of the person's right to decision making. In this situation, trust with the supporter was limited and difficult to gain. The field research took place in Jeff's day centre. Bridget, the day centre team leader, acted as a gatekeeper and did not seem open to supporting Jeff's participation in research.

I introduced myself to Bridget. She started talking to other people while looking at me. I asked her about the days I could be present. No response. I suggested days. She asked, “How long are you staying? We have guys with behaviours here. We might have to put you in the staff room.” I showed her the consent forms. She noticed the participants were receiving vouchers and said that they will need to go to the organisation. I said, “They are for the research participants, which has been approved by the university ethics committee.” She walked away. (Field notes)

The field notes illustrate that when supporters withheld person‐centred knowledge, the researcher's ability to facilitate the person's decision making was structurally constrained, not merely practically complicated. Bridget's disengagement and preference for organisational control over participation revealed a lack of understanding for Jeff's right to participate in research. When supporters like Bridget did not contribute to supporting the person to make their own decision to participate in research, the researcher had to rely on her own person‐centred knowledge and other supporters' understanding to support the person's decision making. The researcher was able to assert herself in situations that respected Jeff's right to decide to participate. She reiterated to Bridget that she had permission from Bridget's manager and consent from Jeff's parents to approach Jeff directly to seek his assent to be included in the research.

These findings highlight the importance of the researcher's active role in building trust with supporters to access their knowledge of the person's communication preferences. When supporters shared person‐centred knowledge with the researcher, they created space for collaborative communication support and placed the person at the centre. When supporters were unwilling to share knowledge or facilitate access, the researcher had to approach other supporters or rely on her own knowledge of the person's right to decision making and demonstrate that to the supporter. This tension between dimensions two and three, where limited supporter knowledge constrained communication accessibility, was most evident in Jeff's case.

Communication and Accessibility

The communication and accessibility dimension is about tailoring decision making processes to the person's communication support needs by making communication about the research process accessible to the person. Application of this dimension to research builds on the previous one by the researcher drawing on shared knowledge and understanding of the person's communication preferences to implement good support for decision making.

Usually, this dimension of supported decision making built on communication support that was already in place. In Laura's case, the researcher tailored the communication about the decision process by making the conversation accessible, familiar, and collaborative through Catherine's communication support.

I said that the planned interviews are chats and that people can answer the questions they want to answer. I looked at Laura and said, “You can also ask me questions when we chat.” I explained to Laura that I won't be using her real name and that she could choose one if she wanted. Catherine gave an example using her cousin's name and Laura came up with a name. (Field notes)

The researcher used simple, conversational language, referring to interviews as chats to reduce pressure and make the process feel more approachable for Laura. Catherine gave an example that was personally meaningful to Laura (her cousin's name), which helped her engage with the abstract concept of choosing a pseudonym. This interaction reflects how the researcher, by listening to and working with Catherine, adapted communication to Laura's strengths and supported her autonomy in a way that respected her pace, preferences, and understanding of the research.

Accessibly communicating about the research to the person was usually facilitated by the supporter's presence during these first interactions. The supporter did not necessarily have an active role but was sometimes simply present when the researcher first met the person. On the first day the researcher met Vincent, she spoke to him and Charlotte about the research and explained in plain English what participating in research means. At this point, the researcher had already spent some hours with him and acquired some understanding of what might influence his moods and communication through observation and information from Charlotte and other supporters.

Charlotte, Vincent and I talked about what it means to take part in the research. Vincent gave me a gentle kiss on the forehead and smiled when I explained the research to him. He said, “Yes” when I asked if he wanted to take part and wrote his name onto the form. (Field notes)

It cannot be certain how much Vincent understood in that moment, but it seemed that he understood the researcher would spend time with him and he seemed comfortable with that. Charlotte took a passive role, but signalled through her presence that it was a safe space for Vincent to engage with the researcher. The presence of a familiar person contributed to accessible communication about the research, which facilitated the person's decision making to participate in research.

Both encounters highlight the need to tailor decision making processes to the person's communication support needs by making research‐related communication accessible and responsive. The researcher's prior interactions with supporters provided insights into the person's communication preferences and the conditions that helped them to engage. This shared knowledge enabled the researcher to adapt the conversation to their strengths, using plain words in a quiet, familiar space that supported them to focus and respond. The person's full understanding could not be confirmed in the single encounter, but the presence of trusted people and adapted communication allowed them to express their initial assent.

Supporter Sustainability

Unlike the preceding dimensions, supporter sustainability shifts the focus from the researcher's direct engagement with the person to the researcher's contribution to the broader support network, acknowledging the demands placed on supporters and the benefit of sustaining effective practices over time. The analysis with this dimension highlights the active role of the researcher within a team of supporters and the researcher's contributions through acts of reciprocity. Becoming part of the team to sustain support influenced how other supporters managed demands on their support and positioned the person as a decision maker.

The researcher's integration into Jeff's network of support highlights collaboration in sustained support and the shifting roles within a team striving to uphold Jeff's right to make decisions. The researcher first spoke to Jeff's mother, Christine, and to Bridget, the day centre team leader, before meeting in person with Jeff's father, Albert. When I first spoke to Albert, I noticed his familiarity with the study, his emotional exhaustion, and the family's need for support to facilitate Jeff's decision making practices.

Albert handed me the signed consent forms and commented, “We tried everything to support Jeff in talking. He now goes to speech therapy. We just can't help him talk. We've gone to all sorts of communication therapists, but nothing seemed to work and help. It's so frustrating.” (Field notes)

The situation shows the researcher hearing the concerns of the supporter. The researcher took an active role in fostering sustainable support by engaging with supporters in ways that acknowledged their challenges. The father's frustrations supporting Jeff explain the parents' limited confidence in Jeff's capacity to make a choice to participate. The researcher demonstrated, as a new person in Jeff's team of supporters, confidence to approach Jeff and seek his assent to involve him in the study. This confidence signalled that it is important to continue to support Jeff with decision making through various encounters.

As a participant observer, the researcher joined interactions contributing to moments of shared support for decision making. This collaborative presence helped strengthen the network around Laura. Towards the end of the first meeting with Laura, when the researcher felt that she had started to build rapport with Laura through William's and Catherine's presence, supporter sustainability was further promoted through the researcher's acts of reciprocity.

I explained the things listed in the consent form (e.g., voucher, counselling service, contact details, taking photos etc.). I looked at Laura to check in. I said, “Do you have any questions for me?”. She didn't say anything at first, then gave me a curious look. I asked, “Do you want to know a little bit more about me, where I live, how old I am, what I'm like?”. She nodded and then asked how old I was. (Field notes)

This moment of mutual exchange illustrates how reciprocity can foster trust, affirm the participant's agency in the researcher‐participant relationship and reposition the person as an active agent rather than a passive subject of consent procedures. By responding to Laura's curiosity with openness, the researcher positioned Laura as an active decision maker with capacity to express assent.

Support for decision making was sustained through collaborative efforts of a team. The researcher was not an independent observer, but rather contributed to the shared work of continuing to develop effective ways to support decision making, including the choice to participate in the research. Although researcher involvement as a supporter was time‐limited, collaborative encounters with family members and daily supporters offered moments of reflection and encouragement that strengthened the team's long‐term capacity to support decision making.

Discussion

The article explored how researchers can engage with people with communication support needs and their supporters to facilitate the person's decision making for participation in research. It analysed how support for decision making can be implemented by reflecting on the role of a researcher in fieldwork by applying the four key dimensions adapted from the Bigby and Douglas (2020) framework. The findings stressed that the researcher becomes a supporter of the person's decision making by directly supporting the person and engaging with their supporters in ways that respect the person's right to decide whether to participate.

In the first dimension, the researcher's relational and values‐based foundations shaped the approach to develop trusting relationships with supporters. By modelling the support approach based on trust, the researcher navigated supporters' assumptions of the person's capacity to make decisions. Central to this process is the researcher's presumption of support for the person's capacity, which is a relational and values‐based foundation. This finding is consistent with research that shows that a person's access to decision making is shaped by how supporters perceive their capacity (Bigby and Douglas 2020; Watson 2016). Ensuring equitable access to supported decision making sometimes requires the researcher to respectfully challenge these assumptions. This is necessary so that the person's rights are not only recognised but actively upheld. This finding reinforces previous studies (e.g., Bigby and Douglas 2020; Lutz et al. 2016; McDonald and Kidney 2012; Watson 2016; Watson et al. 2017) that emphasise that supporting people with intellectual disabilities to make their own decisions requires supporters to hold high expectations for the person's participation and be committed to fostering decision making. It further emphasises the importance of the researcher modelling respect and rights‐based support in relation to the person's decision making, particularly when other supporters might not initially share the same values.

The second dimension, person‐centred knowledge and understanding, showed that engagements with supporters familiar with the person's communication preferences sometimes helped the researcher understand the person's communication behaviour (Hall et al. 2025; Lutz et al. 2016). Gaining the supporter's trust encouraged them to share insights into the person's communication preferences, which helped the researcher build a trusting relationship with the person. In more challenging support relationships, the researcher relied on values and rights‐based training to model good support for decision making in the absence of strong collaboration (Bigby and Douglas 2020). This finding links the second dimension directly to the researcher's values‐based foundations.

In the third dimension, communication and accessibility, the researcher tailored communication to the person through the supporter's shared knowledge of the person's communication preferences, as suggested by Lutz and Fisher (2023). Prior interactions with supporters helped the researcher tailor the setting and language to the person's preferences, enabling assent when full understanding could not be confirmed in single encounters (Morris et al. 2024). The researcher took a facilitative role, which included learning how to tailor communication to the person's strengths and challenges, often by observing and interacting with the team of supporters to understand which accessible formats worked best for the person (Meltzer et al. 2025). The researcher became part of a support team whose collaborative support promoted the person's participation in decision making and their self‐determination (Gooding 2013; Shogren and Wehmeyer 2015; Watson 2023).

The analysis of the fourth dimension, supporter sustainability, revealed the recognition of the researcher as a supporter, temporarily joining a support team through active, reciprocal engagement with the person and supporters. This engagement contributed to sustaining support for the person. Interpreted through the fourth dimension of the framework, it provided an opportunity to verify whether the person was expressing assent. This finding confirms researchers in fieldwork with people with intellectual disabilities and communication support needs are positioned as subjective, active participants, not independent observers (Lutz 2023). The researcher contributes to sustaining support by taking the joint role of a supporter and a participant observer within a broader team of supporters to respect and promote the person's right to decision making. However, institutional gatekeeping and unequal power dynamics within support networks can constrain whose participation is enabled, affecting the transferability of these findings to contexts where organisational resistance limits the researcher's access.

Conclusion

Facilitating a person's participation in research requires researchers to advocate for and model inclusive, respectful approaches to supported decision making. The decision to participate or not positions the person at the centre of attention and their rights to make autonomous decisions with the support their team of supporters. The Bigby and Douglas (2020) framework was valuable for analysing the researcher's role, particularly in relation to building trust with supporters, securing the guardian's consent and the person's consent or assent. Its adaptation required careful attention to where the researcher's role diverges from that of a care supporter, particularly regarding accountability to research integrity alongside the person's right. Consent and continuous assent were not isolated moments but relational processes, grounded in the gradual building of familiarity, mutual recognition and responsiveness to the person's cues and preferences throughout the fieldwork.

The Bigby and Douglas framework centres on supporters in care or service roles and can be adapted for reflexively examining the positionality of the researcher as a supporter in fieldwork. The research extended the framework's original purpose by acknowledging that field researchers are engaged in a dual role of relationship‐building within data collection. Future research and inclusive researcher training could draw on the framework as a practical guide for building trust, tailoring communication and sustaining support for decision making in field research. Ethics committees could apply the framework's dimensions when reviewing research protocols involving people with communication support needs, ensuring that researcher positionality and supporter dynamics are explicitly considered at the governance stage.

The purpose of the framework is to focus primarily on the strategies and practices of supporters. Applying the framework may also need to turn to the agency and communicative efforts of people with communication support needs in the context of decision making. Their contributions may be nuanced, embodied, or context‐specific within the general framework. The novel application of the framework in this article suggests opportunities to further develop ways that account for the complexities of researcher‐participant relationships in research with people with intellectual disabilities and communication support needs.
