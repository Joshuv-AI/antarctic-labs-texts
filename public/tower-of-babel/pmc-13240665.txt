Incidence and Outcomes of Refractory Septic Shock per Consensus Clinical Criteria: A Multicohort Retrospective Study
Authors: Seth R. Bauer, Patrick M. Wieruszewski, Ashish K. Khanna, Marc Leone, Erin F. Barreto, Siddharth Dugar, Gretchen L. Sacha, Lyla Mourany, Paul R. Gunsalus, Alex Milinovich, Anita J. Reddy, Sandra L. Kane-Gill, Yasir Tarabichi, Xiaofeng Wang, Jarrod E. Dalton, Vidula Vachharajani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES:Consensus clinical criteria for refractory septic shock were recently developed, but the incidence and outcomes with these criteria are unknown. There were two aims: 1) to describe the incidence of refractory septic shock and 2) to assess outcomes of patients with septic shock meeting refractory septic shock criteria compared with those who did not.DESIGN:Multicohort, retrospective study. For aim 1, a descriptive epidemiologic study was designed to ascertain the cumulative incidence of refractory septic shock in patients with septic shock. For aim 2, a cohort study was conducted to compare hospital mortality between patients meeting refractory septic shock clinical criteria compared with those who did not.SETTING:Large U.S. health system from 2012 to 2024.PATIENTS:Adults (age ≥ 18 yr) meeting both Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Adult Sepsis Event and Sepsis-3 clinical criteria for septic shock. Patients with missing hospital discharge disposition and incomplete vasopressor dosage information were excluded.INTERVENTIONS:None.MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS:Clinical criteria for refractory septic shock were operationalized as concomitant occurrence of norepinephrine (base) equivalent greater than 0.5 µg/kg/min and lactate greater than 2 mmol/L. Risk-adjusted hospital mortality was assessed with a generalized linear mixed-effects model with a random effect for hospital and fixed effects for age, sex, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (original description, without cardiovascular component). Of 35,914 patients assessed, 15,732 patients with septic shock were included. Refractory septic shock occurred in 3423 patients (21.8%), with a cumulative incidence of 218 cases (95% CI, 211–224) per 1000 patients with septic shock. Hospital mortality with refractory septic shock was 64.4%, and risk-adjusted hospital mortality odds were 4.87-fold higher (95% CI, 4.46–5.31) compared with patients without refractory septic shock.CONCLUSIONS:Clinical criteria for refractory septic shock were fulfilled in about one in five patients with septic shock and associated with substantially higher mortality.

KEY POINTS

Question: What is the cumulative incidence of refractory septic shock and comparative outcomes of patients with clinical criteria for refractory septic shock?

Findings: In a retrospective descriptive epidemiological study, the cumulative incidence of refractory septic shock was 218 cases per 1000 patients with septic shock. In a retrospective cohort study, hospital mortality with refractory septic shock was 64.4%, and risk-adjusted odds of hospital mortality were 4.87-fold higher compared with patients without refractory septic shock.

Meaning: Refractory septic shock is common and associated with considerably higher hospital mortality odds.

Septic shock is a sepsis subset where profound circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormalities substantially increase mortality risk (1). Refractory septic shock, a more severe form of septic shock, was recently defined and clinical criteria established through a Delphi consensus process supported by the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine and Society of Critical Care Medicine (2, 3). The clinical criteria for refractory septic shock include “persistently elevated lactate concentrations and or prolonged capillary refill time in patients with septic shock who are fluid unresponsive, require a norepinephrine (base) equivalent dose > 0.5 micrograms per kilogram per minute, and undergo [critical care ultrasound] assessment when mixed shock is suspected.” However, the incidence of refractory septic shock as conceptualized by these clinical criteria and comparative outcomes between patients meeting vs. not meeting the criteria are unknown. These data are crucial for clinicians and researchers to understand the scope of the problem and its impact as outlined by the newly established criteria. This study was designed with two aims: 1) to describe the cumulative incidence of refractory septic shock and 2) to compare outcomes of patients with septic shock meeting vs. not meeting clinical criteria for refractory septic shock.

METHODS

We used a database of adults with septic shock admitted to Cleveland Clinic locations in Ohio and Florida between 2012 and 2024. The derivation of this database was detailed elsewhere (4). Briefly, sepsis and septic shock per the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Adult Sepsis Event (CDC ASE) and Sepsis-3 clinical criteria were assessed for each patient encounter, with cases restricted to the first sepsis encounter per patient (1,5,6). Septic shock criteria were required to be satisfied within the sepsis criteria organ dysfunction time frame.

For the first aim, we designed a retrospective descriptive epidemiologic study to ascertain refractory septic shock cumulative incidence during the first vasopressor administration course in patients with septic shock. For the second aim, we designed a retrospective cohort study primarily to compare hospital mortality between cohorts of patients meeting vs. not meeting the clinical criteria for refractory septic shock. Secondarily, we sought to describe patient characteristics and compare alive discharge disposition between patients who did and did not meet refractory shock criteria.

We included adults (age ≥ 18 yr) meeting both CDC ASE and Sepsis-3 clinical criteria for septic shock. Although there is no consensus for retrospective septic shock case classification, fulfillment of multiple criteria can increase confidence in syndrome identification (4). All patients meeting both criteria had microbial cultures collected, antibiotic and vasopressor administered, mean arterial pressure less than 65 mm Hg, and serum lactate concentration greater than 2 mmol/L within the sepsis time epoch. We excluded patients with missing hospital discharge disposition (including mortality) and incomplete vasopressor dosage information. Patients were followed throughout their emergency department and hospital course, including care transfers within the health system.

Clinical criteria for refractory septic shock were operationalized as both of the following occurring during a patient’s first vasopressor course for septic shock: 1) norepinephrine (base) equivalent (NEE) greater than 0.5 µg/kg/min based on the Kotani et al (7) equivalence equation and 2) serum lactate concentration greater than 2 mmol/L. The closest lactate value measured within 6 hours before each documented NEE dosage was used. Patients meeting NEE criteria but missing concomitant lactate values were classified as not meeting refractory septic shock criteria.

Statistical Analyses

Patient characteristics were summarized with descriptive statistics. To assess risk-adjusted mortality, we fit a multivariable generalized linear mixed-effects model with a logit link evaluating the association between refractory septic shock and hospital mortality, including a random effect for hospital and fixed effects for age, sex, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (original description) score at shock onset without the cardiovascular component (6). We calculated discharge disposition absolute risk differences and unadjusted odds ratios. Missing data are described. R, Version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used for data handling and statistical analyses. The study was approved by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board (number 22-212, title “Personalized vasopressor therapy in patients with septic shock,” date November 11, 2025), and study procedures were followed in accordance with the ethical standards set forth by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. The analytic code used to generate this article is available at https://github.com/ClevelandClinicQHS/refractory-septic-shock-incidence-and-outcomes.

An a priori sample size calculation was challenging to perform confidently because the incidence of refractory septic shock per the newly established clinical criteria was unknown. In our previous study describing the dataset, in 16,001 patients meeting both CDC ASE and Sepsis-3 septic shock criteria, hospital mortality incidence was 31.1% (4). Assuming a 10% refractory shock incidence (1,600 cases) and 33% hospital mortality incidence in patients with refractory septic shock, 11,483 patients without refractory septic shock were required to detect a mortality difference greater than or equal to 4% (effect size = 0.0865, mortality in patients without refractory septic shock ≤ 29%) with 90% power (β = 0.1) at the 0.05 significance level (α = 0.05).

Sensitivity Analyses

Three a priori sensitivity analyses were undertaken using alternative operationalizations of the refractory septic shock clinical criteria. First, we substituted the Goradia (8) equation for NEE calculation. Second, we used a serum lactate threshold greater than 4 mmol/L (with the primary NEE criteria). Third, a 1 hour minimum of overall vasopressor therapy was added to the primary approach criteria. Although the Delphi panel agreed that the duration of the clinical condition/therapies applied should be included in the refractory septic shock clinical criteria, consensus was not achieved on a given duration (2). The modal response was “up to 1 hour,” and therefore, this duration was explored. Fourth, inclusion criteria were adjusted to singularly require Sepsis-3 clinical criteria for septic shock (with or without CDC ASE criteria fulfillment).

RESULTS

Of 35,914 patients with septic shock per CDC ASE or Sepsis-3 clinical criteria, 15,732 patients were included from 23 locations in the health system (eFig. 1 in the Supplement, https://links.lww.com/CCM/I15). Refractory septic shock developed in 3423 patients (21.8%), resulting in a cumulative incidence of 218 cases (95% CI, 211–224) per 1000 patients with septic shock. There was some variability in cumulative incidence by year (eFig. 2 in the Supplement, https://links.lww.com/CCM/I15). In patients meeting criteria, refractory septic shock developed a median of 5.2 hours (interquartile range, 1.8–13.6 hr) after vasopressor initiation.

Characteristics differed between patients with and without refractory septic shock (Table 1 and eTable 1 in the Supplement, https://links.lww.com/CCM/I15). Most patients with refractory septic shock received combination vasopressor therapy (94.4%) and corticosteroids (70.3%), indicative of nonresponse to initial therapy. Hospital mortality in patients with refractory septic shock was 64.4% vs. 22.0% in patients without refractory septic shock (risk-adjusted odds ratio, 4.87; 95% CI, 4.46–5.31; Table 2). Between-group mortality differences appeared similar by year (eFig. 3 and eTable 2 in the Supplement, https://links.lww.com/CCM/I15). Hospital discharge to home occurred in 9.9% of patients with refractory septic shock vs. 34.1% of patients without refractory septic shock (absolute risk difference, –24.2%; 95% CI, –25.5% to –22.9%).

In the sensitivity analyses, cumulative incidence of refractory septic shock and hospital mortality were similar when NEE was calculated with the Goradia (8) equivalence equation (225 cases/1000 patients with septic shock and 64.0%, respectively) and when requiring vasopressor duration greater than or equal to 1 hour (210 cases/1000 patients with septic shock and 65.2%, respectively). However, when a lactate threshold greater than 4 mmol/L was used the cumulative incidence was lower and hospital mortality was higher (173 cases/1000 patients with septic shock and 70.4%, respectively). In the cohort of patients singularly meeting Sepsis-3 septic shock criteria (n = 20,285), cumulative incidence of refractory septic shock was lower (185 cases/1000 patients with septic shock) and hospital mortality was similar (63.5%). In post hoc subgroup analyses, refractory septic shock incidence and hospital mortality appeared higher in larger hospitals (eTables 3 and 4 in the Supplement, https://links.lww.com/CCM/I15).

DISCUSSION

In our primary analyses, we found that refractory septic shock occurred in 218 cases per 1000 patients with septic shock and was associated with substantially higher mortality odds. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report cumulative incidence of refractory septic shock in a large cohort. In patients with refractory septic shock hospital mortality was 64.4% and 4.3% were discharged to hospice; when combined this composite discharge disposition approximates 30-day mortality (9). Our described mortality aligns with previous reports and can be useful for bedside prognostication and future research (10). Refractory septic shock predominantly developed early during the vasopressor course, which could serve as a period for heightened monitoring. Use of an alternative NEE calculation equation or vasopressor duration requirement greater than or equal to 1 hour did not markedly affect the findings, but a higher lactate threshold resulted in lower refractory septic shock incidence and higher mortality when criteria were met. Future studies should explore the relative contributions of potential refractory septic shock clinical criteria components for outcome prediction.

Several limitations warrant mention. First, the data set did not include assessments of fluid responsiveness, capillary refill time, or critical care ultrasound results. Therefore, these components of the refractory shock clinical criteria were assumed to be fulfilled in cases (lack of fluid responsiveness, prolonged capillary refill time, and no alternative causes of shock). Requiring all elements would likely have resulted in lower incidence and higher mortality. Because these data elements are unlikely to exist in routinely collected electronic health record data, prospective studies are warranted. Second, we primarily required satisfaction of both CDC ASE and Sepsis-3 clinical criteria for septic shock for inclusion. Cumulative incidence was lower, but mortality was similar when only Sepsis-3 clinical criteria were required. Whether the results would differ with other criteria is unknown. Third, we analyzed refractory septic shock as a fixed exposure using hierarchical logistic regression. While this does not strictly account for the time-varying onset of the condition, the median time to onset was only 5.2 hours, rendering the window for potential immortal time bias minimal. This approach allows for a direct and standard assessment of cumulative hospital mortality risk. Fourth, we were unable to account for all potential confounders for mortality (e.g., antimicrobial timeliness and adequacy); therefore, residual confounding for our effect estimate may remain. Fifth, our data set only included the first occurrence of septic shock within a patient’s encounter; therefore, we restricted our analysis to the first vasopressor administration course to ensure concomitant fulfillment of septic shock clinical criteria. The impact of this design decision is unclear but may have resulted in an underestimation of cumulative incidence. Last, the findings are from one health system with 87% of patients treated in Ohio. Whether the findings are generalizable to other settings is uncertain; therefore, analyses should be repeated in other clinical cohorts.

CONCLUSIONS

Among patients with septic shock, clinical criteria for refractory septic shock were fulfilled in about one in five and associated with 4.9-fold higher mortality odds.
