Diagnostic Performance of Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio and CA19-9 Added to EUS-FNA for Solid Pancreatic Lesions: A Single-Center Retrospective Study
Authors: Shasha Liu, Chen Hu, Yujing Fan, Lina Liu, Ying Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the incremental diagnostic value of the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) and carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA19-9) when combined with endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) in patients with solid pancreatic lesions.Patients and MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included 127 consecutive patients who underwent EUS-FNA for solid pancreatic lesions between December 29, 2020, and October 23, 2023. Final diagnoses were established using a reference standard independent of the index EUS-FNA. Malignancy was confirmed by surgical pathology, independent cytologic or histologic evidence, or unequivocal radiologic progression or metastasis. Non-malignancy was established by independent benign pathology or lesion stability or regression without anticancer therapy during at least 24 months of clinical and imaging follow-up.ResultsAmong 127 patients, 80 had malignant lesions and 47 had non-malignant lesions. EUS-FNA achieved an accuracy of 91.3%, sensitivity of 93.8%, specificity of 87.2%, positive predictive value of 92.6%, and negative predictive value of 89.1%. CA19-9 levels were significantly higher in malignant than in non-malignant lesions (median [IQR], 197.21 [9.51–928.36] vs 14.30 [3.97–211.02] U/mL; P=0.001), with an AUC of 0.692. The PLR plus CA19-9 model yielded an apparent AUC of 0.734 and an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.717. The EUS-FNA plus PLR plus CA19-9 model showed the highest discrimination, with an apparent AUC of 0.986 (bootstrap 95% CI, 0.962–1.000) and an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.981; the corresponding Brier scores were 0.033 and 0.041.ConclusionEUS-FNA demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for solid pancreatic lesions. PLR and CA19-9 provided complementary diagnostic value, and the three-variable model showed high diagnostic performance after internal validation.Keywords: endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, carbohydrate antigen 19-9, solid pancreatic lesions, diagnostic accuracy

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality because most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, and only a small proportion are eligible for potentially curative resection.1,2 Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are the principal cross-sectional imaging modalities for lesion characterization, staging, and assessment of resectability. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) provides complementary high-resolution imaging and enables tissue acquisition from solid pancreatic lesions.3–5

EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) has traditionally been widely used for cytologic diagnosis. However, false-negative and nondiagnostic results may occur, particularly in patients with chronic pancreatitis, fibrotic or small lesions, and specimens with limited cellularity.6–8 Current guidelines increasingly recommend end-cutting fine-needle biopsy (FNB) needles because they generally provide greater tissue adequacy and better preservation of histologic architecture. Nevertheless, EUS-FNA has the advantages of a longer history of clinical use, greater availability of needle devices, and more established specimen-processing workflows and cytopathological diagnostic systems.9–11 Accordingly, the present study evaluates an existing EUS-FNA cohort.

Carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA19-9) is commonly used as an adjunctive biomarker in the evaluation and follow-up of patients with suspected pancreatic malignancy. However, it is unsuitable for population-based screening because elevations may occur in benign biliary obstruction and inflammatory conditions, false-negative results may arise in Lewis-antigen-negative individuals, and its positive predictive value is low in asymptomatic populations.12–14 The platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) is an inexpensive systemic inflammatory index derived from routine blood counts. Previous studies have demonstrated that pancreatic cancer patients with a platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) ≥129.1 exhibit significantly reduced 5-year overall survival;15 however, the diagnostic value of the PLR has not been fully evaluated. Recent advances have been made in strategies that combine multiple biomarkers with EUS-FNA. The research confirmed that combining KRAS mutation analysis with EUS-FNA markedly enhances diagnostic sensitivity and accuracy, highlighting the potential merits of multi-parameter integration.16 Nevertheless, there is a paucity of systematic clinical research on the synergistic role of PLR, CA19-9 and EUS-FNA.

This retrospective study evaluated the diagnostic performance of EUS-FNA, PLR, and CA19-9, both individually and in exploratory combinations, for differentiating malignant from non-malignant solid pancreatic lesions. We also examined clinical characteristics associated with malignancy. The proposed approach may offer additional diagnostic value and potentially contribute to improved patient management in clinical practice.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective cohort study included 127 consecutive patients evaluated in the Department of Gastroenterology and Hepatology, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University, between December 29, 2020 and October 23, 2023. Eligible patients had a solid pancreatic lesion identified on imaging, underwent EUS-FNA, had pre-procedural complete blood count and tumor-marker results, and had an evaluable final reference diagnosis. Patients were excluded if they had another active malignancy, previous pancreatic surgery or anticancer treatment, incomplete clinical or laboratory data, or a contraindication to EUS-guided tissue acquisition.

The final reference diagnosis was established by surgical pathology when available. For patients without surgical resection, malignancy required either unequivocal cytologic or histologic confirmation obtained independently of the index EUS-FNA procedure, or unequivocal radiologic progression or metastasis during follow-up. The result of the index EUS-FNA under evaluation was not incorporated into the final reference diagnosis. A non-malignant diagnosis required benign pathology obtained independently of the index EUS-FNA or lesion stability or regression without anticancer therapy during at least 24 months of clinical and imaging follow-up. Symptoms such as weight loss, anemia, or death were not used as independent classification criteria.

The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (approval No. SYDW2020-106). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for EUS-FNA and use of clinical data was obtained from all participants.

EUS-FNA Procedure

EUS-FNA was performed by an experienced endosonographer using a linear-array echoendoscope and a 22-gauge aspiration needle. Sedation was administered and titrated by the anesthesia team according to the institutional protocol, with continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring. After Doppler assessment to avoid intervening vessels, the needle was advanced into the lesion under real-time EUS guidance. Tissue was obtained using repeated to-and-fro movements. A 5-mL or 10-mL syringe was used when suction was applied; syringe volume is reported as the suction technique and not as a pressure measurement. Additional passes were performed when the macroscopic specimen was considered inadequate.

Cytology and Specimen Processing

Aspirated material was expressed onto glass slides for direct-smear cytology, and the residual material was placed in preservative solution or formalin for additional cytologic or cell-block preparation when sufficient. Direct smears were stained using the Papanicolaou method. The stained slides and cell-block sections were reviewed by experienced pathologists according to the institutional diagnostic workflow.

Laboratory Measurements

Peripheral venous blood was collected before EUS-FNA. Complete blood counts were measured using a Sysmex XE-2100 analyzer. PLR was calculated as platelet count divided by lymphocyte count, and NLR as neutrophil count divided by lymphocyte count. RPR was defined as red-cell distribution width divided by platelet count. Serum CA19-9 and CEA were measured in the hospital clinical laboratory using the routine validated assay platform.

Model Development and Internal Validation

The final diagnosis was coded as malignant=1 and non-malignant=0, and EUS-FNA was coded as malignant=1 and non-malignant=0. Three exploratory logistic regression models were fitted. For the EUS-FNA plus PLR plus CA19-9 model, separation was identified because the EUS-FNA result showed near-perfect discrimination of the final reference diagnosis. Therefore, Firth penalized logistic regression was used to obtain finite and less biased coefficient estimates. Continuous predictors were entered as their original values without categorization, transformation, or standardization. Predicted probabilities from the fitted logistic models were used for ROC analysis and calculation of Brier scores. Internal validation was performed using 1000 stratified bootstrap resamples, with the complete model refitted in each resample. Optimism was estimated by comparing bootstrap-sample performance with performance in the original dataset, and optimism-corrected AUCs and Brier scores were subsequently calculated. The apparent and optimism-corrected AUCs and Brier scores for the three exploratory models are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. The regression coefficients, standard errors, odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and fitted equation for the EUS-FNA plus PLR plus CA19-9 model are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0, with model construction and bootstrap validation independently implemented in Python. Firth penalized logistic regression was applied to the three-variable model because of separation. Continuous variables were assessed for normality. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent-samples t test. Variables that did not meet the normality assumption, including CA19-9, CEA, PLR, NLR, and RPR, are presented as median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are presented as n (%) and were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to estimate the area under the curve (AUC), 95% confidence interval (CI), sensitivity, specificity, and Youden-index cutoff. The EUS-FNA AUC was recalculated from the corresponding 2×2 table, and combined models were developed using binary logistic regression. Internal validation was performed using 1000 stratified bootstrap resamples with model refitting in each sample. Apparent and optimism-corrected AUCs and Brier scores were reported, with percentile-based bootstrap 95% CIs. All tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Laboratory Findings

As shown in Table 2 and Figure 2, serum CA19-9 levels were significantly higher in the malignant group than in the non-malignant group (median [IQR], 197.21 [9.51–928.36] U/mL vs 14.30 [3.97–211.02] U/mL; Mann–Whitney U=803.50, P=0.001). Serum CEA levels were also significantly elevated in patients with malignant lesions (3.58 [2.26–5.89] ng/mL vs 2.40 [1.76–3.85] ng/mL; U=919.0, P=0.024). Among the inflammatory indices, PLR was significantly higher in the malignant group than in the non-malignant group (125.56 [73.86–167.32] vs 72.16 [30.49–147.50]; U=1217.0, P=0.005). Significant between-group differences were also observed for NLR (2.60 [1.96–3.80] vs 2.27 [1.85–2.84]; U=1318.0, P=0.022) and RPR (0.22 [0.18–0.29] vs 0.19 [0.16–0.21]; U=1174.0, P=0.049). In contrast, no statistically significant between-group differences were found in WBC, neutrophil, lymphocyte, platelet, or monocyte counts, or in RDW (all P>0.05).

Diagnostic Performance of EUS-FNA

As shown in Table 3, EUS-FNA correctly identified 75 of 80 malignant lesions and 41 of 47 non-malignant lesions. Five malignant lesions were classified as non-malignant, whereas six non-malignant lesions were classified as malignant. The overall diagnostic accuracy of EUS-FNA was 91.3%, with a sensitivity of 93.8%, specificity of 87.2%, positive predictive value of 92.6%, and negative predictive value of 89.1%.

ROC Analysis and Exploratory Combinations

The ROC analysis results for the individual diagnostic variables are presented in Table 4 and Figure 3. EUS-FNA demonstrated good diagnostic performance for differentiating malignant from non-malignant pancreatic lesions, with an AUC of 0.905 (95% CI, 0.841–0.969), a sensitivity of 93.8%, and a specificity of 87.2% (P<0.001). Among the individual biomarkers, CA19-9 showed the highest diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.692 (95% CI, 0.588–0.797). At a cutoff value of 74.79 U/mL, its sensitivity and specificity were 62.3% and 78.4%, respectively (P=0.001). PLR yielded an AUC of 0.643 (95% CI, 0.533–0.753), with a sensitivity of 87.5% and a specificity of 44.2% at a cutoff value of 46.45 (P=0.009). CEA showed an AUC of 0.633 (95% CI, 0.521–0.744), with a sensitivity of 52.5% and a specificity of 73.2% at a cutoff value of 3.435 ng/mL (P=0.024). By contrast, NLR and RPR showed limited discriminatory ability. NLR had an AUC of 0.578 (95% CI, 0.477–0.680), with a sensitivity of 37.2% and a specificity of 82.2% at a cutoff value of 3.148 (P=0.149), whereas RPR had an AUC of 0.602 (95% CI, 0.501–0.703), with a sensitivity of 51.3% and a specificity of 64.4% at a cutoff value of 0.214 (P=0.061).

Internal Validation of Exploratory Combination Models

The CA19-9 plus CEA model had an apparent AUC of 0.704 and an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.690, with apparent and corrected Brier scores of 0.192 and 0.201, respectively. The PLR plus CA19-9 model had an apparent AUC of 0.734 and an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.717, with corresponding Brier scores of 0.204 and 0.213. The EUS-FNA plus PLR plus CA19-9 model showed an apparent AUC of 0.986 and an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.981; the apparent and corrected Brier scores were 0.033 and 0.041, respectively. Internal validation results are summarized in Supplementary Table S1, whereas the fitted coefficients and prediction equation for the EUS-FNA plus PLR plus CA19-9 model are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Discussion

Pancreatic cancer is characterized by multifactorial pathogenesis, insidious onset, limited treatment options, and a poor prognosis, with the overall 5-year survival rate remaining at approximately 10% despite advances in diagnosis and treatment.17 Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are currently regarded as the standard cross-sectional imaging modalities, whereas endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) serves as an important complementary technique by providing high-resolution imaging and enabling tissue acquisition. During EUS, a miniaturized ultrasound transducer is advanced into the stomach or duodenum through an endoscope, thereby minimizing interference from gastrointestinal gas and abdominal adipose tissue and allowing close-range visualization of pancreatic lesions.

Despite these advantages, the sensitivity and specificity of EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) remain variable in clinical practice.18–20 The validated 2×2 diagnostic results in the present study are consistent with the broader literature, confirming the value of EUS-guided tissue acquisition while also demonstrating that false-negative and false-positive results remain clinically relevant.3–8 False-negative findings may result from sampling error, abundant desmoplastic stroma, coexisting chronic pancreatitis, small lesion size, necrosis, suboptimal specimen processing, or interpretive limitations. Recent guidelines from the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy and the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy recommend fine-needle biopsy needles for solid pancreatic lesions because they generally provide more adequate histologic specimens. Nevertheless, EUS-FNA remains widely used and retains substantial practical value, particularly in centers with well-established cytopathology workflows and rapid on-site evaluation.9–11

In the present study, the discriminatory performance of the individual biomarkers was modest, with apparent AUCs of 0.643 for PLR and 0.692 for CA19-9, respectively, which is broadly consistent with previous reports. CA19-9 has shown moderate sensitivity for pancreatic cancer, but its specificity is limited by elevations in benign biliary and inflammatory conditions.12–14 Although PLR has been investigated as a prognostic marker, its diagnostic utility remains to be fully elucidated.15,16 By contrast, combining PLR with CA19-9 yielded an apparent AUC of 0.734, while the combination of EUS-FNA, PLR, and CA19-9 achieved an apparent AUC of 0.986. These findings suggest that integrating cytopathologic and laboratory parameters may improve discrimination beyond that provided by any single indicator.

The clinical characteristics observed in the malignant group further support the potential value of a multidimensional diagnostic approach. Patients with malignant lesions were generally older and more likely to have lesions with a long-axis diameter of at least 2 cm, biliary or pancreatic duct dilatation, and elevated inflammatory indices, including NLR and RPR. Symptoms such as abdominal pain, anorexia, and jaundice were also more frequent in the malignant group.

This combined diagnostic strategy may have several potential clinical advantages. First, if validated in prospective cohorts, this approach may assist risk stratification and inform decisions regarding repeat sampling or closer follow-up.20,21 This may be particularly relevant in diagnostically challenging settings such as chronic pancreatitis, in which the accuracy of EUS-FNA can be reduced.3,5 Second, the integration of patient characteristics and readily available blood biomarkers may improve risk stratification and assist clinicians in determining the need for repeat sampling, closer follow-up, or surgical consultation.22,23 From a practical perspective, combining EUS-FNA with routinely measured laboratory biomarkers is technically feasible and relatively cost-effective, supporting its potential applicability in routine clinical practice.24

This study has several limitations. Although bootstrap internal validation was performed, the models were developed and evaluated in the same single-center cohort. Independent external validation remains necessary. Formal calibration plots and decision-curve analysis were not performed, limiting assessment of model calibration and clinical utility. Additional biomarker approaches may also warrant investigation. For example, CA19-9 concentrations in aspirated material or CA19-9 immunostaining in cell-block or core-biopsy specimens could be evaluated. PLR is a peripheral-blood-derived ratio and cannot be directly measured or immunostained as a single tissue marker. Tissue-based investigations would require separate platelet and lymphocyte markers and predefined quantitative methods.

Conclusion

EUS-FNA demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy in this retrospective cohort. PLR and CA19-9 provided complementary diagnostic information, and the three-variable model showed high apparent and optimism-corrected discrimination.
