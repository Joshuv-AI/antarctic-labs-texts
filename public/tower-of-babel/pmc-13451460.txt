Helicobacter pylori and Porphyromonas gingivalis infections as emerging biomarkers associated with Correa’s cascade
Authors: Jannis Kountouras, Evangelos Kazakos, Vardaka Elisabeth, Maria C Mouratidou, Christos Zavos, Michael Doulberis
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

In their review, He et al. [1] emphasized that gastric intestinal metaplasia (GIM) is a highly heterogeneous condition associated with an increased population-level risk of gastric cancer (GC); importantly, however, most individuals with GIM will not progress to malignancy [1]. The authors further summarized both established and emerging biomarkers related to GIM and highlighted their potential value in predicting malignant transformation along the Correa cascade.

Recent evidence indicates that Helicobacter pylori (Hp), also discussed by the authors [1], in conjunction with additional risk factors such as male sex, advanced age, smoking, alcohol consumption, and poor-quality water sources, is strongly associated with progression through the Correa sequence [2].

In this context, Hp and Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg) are two highly prevalent pathogens that may serve as important emerging biomarkers of severe GIM progression and gastric carcinogenesis along the Correa cascade [3, 4].

Although Hp primarily colonizes the stomach, it can also inhabit the oral cavity, which may function as an important extragastric reservoir. This oral–gastric distribution may contribute to persistent infection, reinfection after eradication therapy, and person-to-person transmission [4]. Likewise, the oral cavity represents a principal reservoir for Pg [4]. Increasing evidence indicates that Pg and its virulence factors, particularly gingipains, may contribute to gastric carcinogenesis within the Correa sequence [4].

Several emerging biomarkers associated with GIM and GC in the setting of Hp and/or Pg infection deserve particular attention. Among GIM subtypes, incomplete GIM is more strongly associated with progression to GC than complete GIM [3, 4]. Hp-induced inflammation, demonstrated in both murine models and humans (Houghton’s theory), promotes migration of bone marrow-derived stem cells (BMDSCs) to the gastric mucosa, where they may undergo metaplastic and dysplastic transformation, ultimately contributing to GC development in accordance with Correa’s cascade [3, 4]. These findings support the hypothesis that GC may originate, at least in part, from BMDSCs.

Increased mast cell density has also been observed in areas of severe inflammation, gastric atrophy, GIM, and GC, correlating with Hp-induced gastritis and neoplastic progression [4]. The Hp-derived VacA cytotoxin exerts chemotactic and activating effects on bone marrow-derived mast cells, stimulating the release of proinflammatory cytokines that may contribute to both local gastric pathology and systemic disease [3]. Moreover, the Hp virulence factors CagA and VacA play central roles in gastric oncogenesis and are closely linked to progression along Correa’s cascade [4].

Mutations in the p53 tumor suppressor gene associated with Hp infection have been linked to familial GC [4]. In parallel, Pg infection has also been associated with altered p53 expression [4]. Beyond p53, increased immunoexpression of Ki-67 and cyclin D1 has been reported in patients with Hp-related chronic gastritis and GIM [5]. Ki-67 is a nuclear protein expressed exclusively in proliferating cells, whereas p53 regulates cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis in response to DNA damage [5]. Cyclin D1 and its associated regulatory proteins are involved in cell growth, differentiation, survival, and apoptosis, and their dysregulation contributes to uncontrolled proliferation in multiple malignancies [5, 6]. Altered expression of these cell-cycle regulators is therefore considered critical in gastric carcinogenesis. In addition to p53 dysregulation, Pg may further promote carcinogenesis through modulation of Ki-67 and cyclin D1 expression [6]. Importantly, Hp eradication has been associated with significant reductions in Ki-67, p53, and cyclin D1 immunoexpression, suggesting that timely eradication therapy may reverse precancerous alterations within the gastric mucosa [5].

Conversely, the absence of these molecular and histopathological alterations in GIM may indicate a more favorable prognosis. For instance, Hwang et al. [7] demonstrated that GIM may disappear five or more years after Hp eradication. Furthermore, Hp eradication appears to reduce GC risk in patients with GIM or dysplasia during follow-up periods ranging from 2 to 26.5 years and may even reverse these premalignant lesions. Incomplete GIM has also been reported to regress within 10 years following Hp eradication [8].

Notably, radiofrequency ablation has shown potential for eradicating incomplete GIM [4]. In addition, endoscopic grading systems for GIM represent valuable surveillance tools that may reduce the need for routine biopsy sampling [4].

Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of further large-scale, multicenter studies to better characterize the aforementioned emerging biomarkers associated with the Correa cascade and to establish more definitive conclusions regarding their prognostic and therapeutic implications.
