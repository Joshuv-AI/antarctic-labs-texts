Isoperimetric Inequality on Manifolds with Quadratically Decaying Curvature
Dangyang He

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the reverse improvement property of Sobolev inequalities on manifolds with quadratically decaying Ricci curvature. Specifically, we establish conditions under which the uniform decay of the heat kernel implies the validity of an isoperimetric inequality. As an application, we demonstrate the existence of isoperimetric sets in generalized Grushin spaces. Our approach is built on a weak-type Sobolev inequality, gradient estimates on remote balls, and a Hardy-type gluing technique. This method provides new insights into the deep connections between geometric and functional analysis.

1.
Introduction

The isoperimetric inequality constitutes a fundamental result in geometry and analysis. In its classical planar formulation, it asserts that, among all simple closed curves of prescribed perimeter, the circle encloses the largest possible area. Equivalently, for any planar region of prescribed area, the circle achieves the least perimeter. This principle extends naturally to higher dimensions: among all bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of fixed volume, the ball has the smallest surface area, and conversely, among all bodies with a given surface area, the ball encloses the greatest volume.

Let
M
be a complete non-compact Riemannian manifold with measure
d\mu=dx=d\textrm{vol}
. Let
n>1
be some parameter. We say the classical isoperimetric inequality holds on
M
if

(
\textrm{ISO}_{n}
)

|\Omega|^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\leq C|\partial\Omega|,

where
\Omega\subset M
is any bounded domain with smooth boundary. In the above notation,
|\Omega|
refers to the volume of the set
\Omega
and
|\partial\Omega|
denotes the standard Riemannian surface measure of its boundary. One says
M
has isoperimetric dimension
n
if (

ISO
n

) holds for all large set, i.e.
|\Omega|\gtrsim 1
. Note that on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the topological dimension coincides with its isoperimetric dimension.

Bridged by co-area formula, it is well-known that (

ISO
n

) is equivalent to the Sobolev inequality (see
[
19
]
):

(
\textrm{S}_{n}^{1}
)

\left(\int_{M}|f|^{\frac{n}{n-1}}dx\right)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\leq C\int_{M}|\nabla f|dx

for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
. Generally, one studies
L^{p}\textit{-}
Sobolev inequality

(
\textrm{S}_{n}^{p}
)

\left(\int_{M}|f|^{p^{*}}dx\right)^{1/p^{*}}\leq C\left(\int_{M}|\nabla f|^{p}dx\right)^{1/p},

where
p^{*}=\frac{np}{n-p}
and
1<p<n
.

A simple but noteworthy feature of this family of inequalities is that they exhibit a self-improvement property. Specifically, if
\nabla
is a local gradient, then from (

S
n

p

), one can derive
(\textrm{S}_{n}^{r})
for any
1\leq p<r<n
; see, for example,
[
51
]
. Moreover, it is possible to extend this property to the cases
p=n
and
p>n
by considering Trudinger–Moser type and Gagliardo–Nirenberg type inequalities, respectively; see
[
9
]
.

However, in this article, our focus shifts to a reverse problem, which we refer to as the
reverse-improvement
problem. Originally posed by Coulhon in
[
8
]
, it asks under what conditions one can derive (

ISO
n

) from
(\textrm{S}_{n}^{2})
. Let
\Delta
denote the Laplace-Beltrami operator. The background of this problem can be traced to earlier results from
[
49
]
,
[
38
]
, and
[
5
]
, which show that the uniform decay of the heat kernel is equivalent to the Sobolev inequality
(\textrm{S}_{n}^{2})
for
n>2
or a Nash-type inequality for
n>1
. Concretely,

\displaystyle\|e^{-t\Delta}\|_{1\to\infty}\leq Ct^{-n/2}\iff\begin{cases}(\textrm{S}_{n}^{2}),&n>2,\\
\|f\|_{2}^{1+2/n}\leq C\|\nabla f\|_{2}\|f\|_{1}^{2/n},&n>1.\end{cases}

Hence, the
reverse-improvement
problem can be restated as follows: under what conditions does uniform decay of the heat kernel imply the isoperimetric inequality?

From the perspective of curvature, the argument in
[
50
]
shows that this
reverse-improvement
property holds on manifolds with nonnegative Ricci curvature. Furthermore, if the Ricci curvature is bounded below by some constant, the property still holds but only in a local sense; see also
[
42
]
. Recall that we say
M
satisfies volume doubling condition if for any
x\in M
and
R\geq r>0
, and for some
\mu>0
,

(
D
)

\frac{V(x,R)}{V(x,r)}\leq C\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{\mu},

where
B(x,r)
denotes the geodesic ball centered at
x
with radius
r
and
V(x,r)=\mu(B(x,r))
denotes the volume of the ball. It is also known that (
D
) implies the so-called reverse doubling property

(
\textrm{RD}_{\nu}
)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{\nu}\leq C\frac{V(x,R)}{V(x,r)},

for some
0<\nu\leq\mu
. Denote by
\nabla
, the Riemannian gradient operator on
M
. We say
M
satisfies
L^{p}\textit{-}
Poincaré inequality
(1\leq p<\infty)
if for every ball
B=B(x,r)
and
f\in C^{\infty}(B)
,

(
\textrm{P}_{p}
)

\displaystyle\int_{B}|f-f_{B}|^{p}dx\leq Cr^{p}\int_{B}|\nabla f|^{p}dx.

Next, through a purely analytical approach, Coulhon and Saloff-Coste (see
[
14
]
) verify that this reverse implication holds when
M
satisfies (
D
) and a so-called pseudo-Poincaré inequality; see also
[
43
]
. In particular, their argument implies

(1.1)

\displaystyle\eqref{Doubling}+\eqref{Pp}+(\textrm{S}_{n}^{2})\implies\eqref{S_n^p}.

Our study begins by examining a specific Riemannian manifold: the connected sum of two copies of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Formally, we consider the
reverse-improvement
problem on

\displaystyle M=\mathbb{R}^{n}\#\mathbb{R}^{n}.

This manifold is obtained by gluing two copies of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,1)
along a smooth compact set; see
[
25
]
for a detailed description. It is known that
M
satisfies (
D
) and
(\textrm{S}_{n}^{2})
(
n\geq 3
), but (

P
p

) does not hold for all
1\leq p\leq n
. Moreover,
M
does not need to have non-negative Ricci curvature everywhere. Consequently, neither the curvature method
[
50
]
nor the Poincaré technique
[
43
]
can be employed to this case directly.

To go further, let us first set some notation. Denote by
e^{-t\Delta}(x,y)
the Schwartz kernel of the heat semigroup. We use (
DUE
) to represent the following on-diagonal upper estimate for the heat kernel:

(DUE)

e^{-t\Delta}(x,x)\leq\frac{C}{V(x,\sqrt{t})},\quad\forall x\in M.

By
[
23
]
, under (
D
), the above on-diagonal bound self-improves to the off-diagonal upper bound

(UE)

e^{-t\Delta}(x,y)\leq\frac{C}{V(x,\sqrt{t})}e^{-\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{ct}}.

We also recall the following heat kernel regularity condition:

(
\textrm{G}_{p}
)

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{t}\nabla e^{-t\Delta}\|_{p\to p}\leq C,\quad\forall t>0.

From
[
3
, Theorem 9.1]
(and the comments that follow), the authors provide a general method for proving a so-called weak-type Sobolev inequality. Our first result applies their method to the heat semigroup, yielding a criterion for the
reverse-improvement
property.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
M
be a complete non-compact Riemannian manifold. Let
1\leq p<2
and
n>2
. Then,

\displaystyle(\textrm{S}_{n}^{2})+(\textrm{G}_{p^{\prime}})\implies\eqref{S_n^p}.

Note that this result generalizes the one from
[
50
]
, because it is well-known that
(\textrm{G}_{\infty})
holds on manifolds with nonnegative Ricci curvature. Returning to our model case
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}\#\mathbb{R}^{n}
,it was showed in
[
13
, Theorem 1.6 and Example 4, Page 45]
that (

G
p

) holds on
M
if and only if
1<p<n
. Consequently, the above result (Proposition
1.1
) implies that (

S
n

p

) holds on
M
for
p\in(n^{\prime},n)
. There is no doubt that this compactly supported geometric perturbation (the gluing of two manifolds) can influence analytic properties. For instance, unlike the Euclidean setting, the Riesz transform on
M
is
L^{p}
-bounded only on a finite interval; see
[
7
]
. Nevertheless, it is questionable whether this perturbation so drastically affects the geometry as to lose all information about (

S
n

p

) for
1\leq p\leq n^{\prime}
, given that the perturbation occurs only within a compact region.

To improve the above result, let us elaborate another strategy to consider the
reverse-improvement
problem. The first step is to recall a result from
[
49
]
: for
n>2
,

\displaystyle(\textrm{S}_{n}^{2})\implies\|\Delta^{-1/2}\|_{p\to p^{*}}\lesssim 1,\quad\forall 1<p<n,\quad p^{*}=\frac{np}{n-p}.

Combine this with the so-called reverse Riesz inequality:

(
\textrm{RR}_{p}
)

\displaystyle\|\Delta^{1/2}f\|_{p}\leq C\|\nabla f\|_{p},\quad\forall f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M).

We obtain another criterion for
reverse-improvement
problem:

(1.2)

\displaystyle(\textrm{S}_{n}^{2})+\eqref{RRp}\implies\eqref{S_n^p}.

Now, it was proved in
[
30
]
that (

RR
p

) holds on
M
for all
1<p<\infty
. So, the above criterion indicates that (

S
n

p

) holds on
M
for all
1<p<n
. Note that this criterion improve the inequality (

S
n

p

) on
M
to
1<p<n
. However, first, proving (

RR
p

) itself could be tricky and second, this criterion cannot yield any information for
p=1
, which is potentially the most significant and interesting case.

To study further in this direction, let us introduce the following notation. Let
o\in M
be fixed. Denote by
r(x)
, the distance from
x
to
o
, i.e.
d(x,o)
. In the rest of this note, we say
M
has quadratically decaying Ricci curvature if for all
x\in M
, and some
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
,

(QD)

\textrm{Ric}_{x}\geq-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\left(1+r(x)\right)^{2}}g_{x},

where
g
is the Riemannian metric. Several copies of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, manifold with conical ends are two particular examples which satisfy (
QD
).

Suppose
M
has at most finitely many ends. We say
M
satisfies
(\textrm{RCE})
if there exists a constant
\theta\in(0,1)
such that for all
R\geq 1
and all
x\in\partial B(o,R)
, there is a continuous path
\gamma:[0,1]\to B(o,R)\setminus B(o,\theta R)
and a geodesic ray
\tau:[0,\infty)\to M\setminus B(o,R)
such that

\displaystyle\bullet\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=\tau(0),\quad\bullet\textrm{length}(\gamma)\leq R/\theta.

This
(\textrm{RCE})
condition is a generalization of the so-called
(\textrm{RCA})
condition introduced in
[
24
]
to manifolds with finitely many ends. For more details about
(\textrm{RCE})
, see
[
24
,
6
]
.

For any
R\geq 1
and any
x\in\partial B(o,R)
, we say
M
satisfies volume comparison condition if

(VC)

V(o,R)\leq CV(x,R/2).

It was verified by Carron
[
6
, Theorem 2.4]
and
[
22
]
that

\displaystyle\eqref{QD}+\eqref{VC}+(\textrm{RCE})\implies\eqref{Doubling}+\eqref{DUE}.

Recall the following version Hardy-type inequality

(
\textrm{H}_{p}
)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\left|\frac{f(x)}{r(x)}\right|^{p}dx\leq C\int_{M}|\nabla f(x)|^{p}dx,\quad\forall f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M).

Our next result appears in the following way.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
M
be a complete non-comapct Riemannian manifold satisfying (
VC
), (
QD
),
(\textrm{RCE})
, and (

RD
ν

) for some
\nu>1
. Then, for
1\leq p<n
,

\displaystyle(\textrm{S}_{n}^{2})+\eqref{Hp}\implies\eqref{S_n^p}.

Now, by a corollary of Theorem
1.2
(see Corollary
4.5
later), we eventually verify that (

ISO
n

) (or equivalently (
\textrm{S}_{n}^{1}
)) holds on
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}\#\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In the above result, the usual Poincaré inequality (

P
p

) (see
[
14
,
43
]
) is replaced by a Hardy-type inequality (

H
p

). The condition (

H
p

) can be treated as a ”gluing” tool, which combines isoperimetric estimates for the so-called remote balls together. We mention that the assumption
n>2
should not be treated too seriously. This assumption could be replaced by the uniform decay of the heat kernel or a Nash-type inequality (see Remark
3.1
below). The only reason for presenting the theorem in this way is to maintain uniformity in the notation.

Recall that we say
M
has isoperimetric dimension
n
if (

ISO
n

) holds on
M
for all large sets. Usually, this diemnsion
n
is expected to coincide with the topological dimension, for instance
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. However, in the general setting, these two dimensions do not need to be the same, and the inequality (

ISO
n

) can be spoiled by very simple perturbations. For instance, one considers the cylinder
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{S}^{m-n}
for some
m>n
, where
\mathbb{S}^{m-n}
is the unit sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{m-n+1}
; see
[

]
. Then, the classical (

ISO

) fails to hold, and the isoperimetric dimension turns to be

. In other words, the topological dimension may not reflect the ”real” geometric properties on a general Riemannian manifold.

To further study isoperimetric problem, instead of establishing (

ISO
n

), one can also consider a generalized isoperimetric inequality associated with a function
\Phi
, i.e. the inequality:

(
\textrm{ISO}_{\Phi}
)

|\Omega|\leq C\Phi(|\Omega|)|\partial\Omega|,

where
\Phi
, defined on
(0,\infty)
, is non-negative and non-decreasing. From
[
14
]
, it is well-known that (

ISO
Φ

) holds on
M
provided
M
satisfies (
D
) and Poincaré inequality
(\textrm{P}_{1})
, where

\Phi(t):=\inf\{r>0;V(r)\geq t\},

and
V(r)=\inf_{x\in M}V(x,r)
, is assumed to be strictly positive.

In our next investigation, we may focus on a class of non-doubling connected sum introduced in
[
25
]
, which generalizes the previous model
\mathbb{R}^{n}\#\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Recall notations from
[
25
]
. We consider connected sums:

(1.3)

\displaystyle M=(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times M_{1})\#\dots\#(\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}\times M_{l})\quad n_{*}\geq 3\quad l\geq 2,

where for all
1\leq i\leq l
,
M_{i}
is a compact manifold with
n_{i}+\textrm{dim}(M_{i})=N
and
n_{*}=\min_{i}n_{i}
. We set
E_{i}:=\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\times M_{i}\setminus K_{i}
, where
K_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\times M_{i}
is a compact set, and denote the central connection part by
K
. That is

M=K\cup(\cup_{i}E_{i}).

Note that
M
satisfies (
QD
),
(\textrm{RCE})
but it does not satisfy either (

) nor (

P

) (

). Although (

) is violated—for instance, if

—the curvature remains “flat” outside a compact set. We show that our techniques used in Theorem
1.2
can be adapted to this further perturbed setting. That is we verify that there exists some function
\Phi
such that the generalized isoperimetric inequality (

ISO
Φ

) holds on

.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
M
be a manifold with ends defined by (
1.3
). Then, (

ISO
Φ

) holds on
M
with

\Phi(t)=\begin{cases}C_{1}t^{1/N},&t\leq 1,\\
C_{2}t^{1/n_{*}},&t\geq 1.\end{cases},

where
n_{*}=\min_{i}n_{i}
and

.

It is clear that each end of

, defined in (

), has isoperimetric dimension

(see Corollary

below). However, Theorem
1.3
shows that

itself has isoperimetric dimension

. Unlike Theorem

, where a Hardy-type inequality (

) is assumed, we develop an alternative “gluing” tool; see Lemma

below.

Let
V
and
P
denote, respectively, the volume and perimeter measures on
M
. For a subset
\Omega\subset M
with smooth boundary, the usual isoperimetric problem can be formulated by the following minimization:

(1.4)

\displaystyle\min\left(\left\{P(\Omega);\Omega\in\mathcal{S}\quad\textrm{such that}\quad V(\Omega)=\epsilon\right\}\right)

where
\epsilon>0
is fixed and
\mathcal{S}
is a given family of sets. A set
\Omega
that attains the minimum in (
1.4
) is called an isoperimetric set. Our next goal is to investigate the existence of such sets in Grushin spaces.

Let
\beta\geq 0
. On
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the classical Grushin operator is given by
-\partial_{x}^{2}-x^{2\beta}\partial_{y}^{2}
; see
[
27
]
. For
n\geq 1
,
m\geq 1
, we consider the following quadratic form:

\displaystyle Q(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+m}}\nabla_{L}f(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{L}f(\xi)d\xi,

defined for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})
, where the gradient operator
\nabla_{L}
is defined by

(1.5)

\displaystyle\nabla_{L}=\left(\nabla_{x},|x|^{\beta}\nabla_{y}\right).

Since
Q
is closable (see
[
39
, Lemma 2.1]
), the Friedrichs extension guarantees the existence of a unique positive self-adjoint operator
L
associated with
Q
. This operator is expressed by

(1.6)

\displaystyle L=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}-|x|^{2\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\partial_{y_{i}}^{2}=\Delta_{x}+|x|^{2\beta}\Delta_{y},\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}.

We refer to
L
as the generalized Grushin operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
with parameter
\beta
.

In
[
37
]
, when

, the authors prove that the isoperimetric inequality
(\textrm{ISO}_{\mathcal{Q}})
holds on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
\mathcal{Q}=n+m(\beta+1)
is the homogeneous dimension of

and its associated Grushin space. Moreover, they obtain an explicit formula for the isoperimetric sets. For higher dimensions,
[
20
]
shows that if
n=1
and

, then
(\textrm{ISO}_{\mathcal{Q}})
still holds; and if

, the inequality holds for all
x
-spherically symmetric sets, with a corresponding characterization of the isoperimetric sets provided. Furthermore, for
n\geq 1
and
m\geq 1
,
[
39
, Proposition 3.1]
establishes that
\left(\textrm{S}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}\right)
holds on the Grushin spaces. Moreover, by
[
40
]
, the Poincaré inequality
(\textrm{P}_{2})
also holds. Therefore, by
[
33
]
and then applying (
1.1
) (the method of Poincaré inequality), it is evident that
\left(S_{\mathcal{Q}}^{p}\right)
holds for
2-\epsilon<p<2
for some
\epsilon>0
. In particular, when the parameter
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
, a result from
[

]
shows that the Riesz transform associated with

is bounded on

for

. Hence, by duality, (

RR

) holds for

, and
\left(S_{\mathcal{Q}}^{p}\right)
can be extended to all

using (

) (the reverse Riesz method).

Consider
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
equipped with the metric

(1.7)

\displaystyle g_{\xi}=dx^{2}+|x|^{-2\beta}dy^{2},\quad\xi=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}.

By viewing the space as a doubly warped product, i.e.

with metric:

, an application of the formula for doubly warped product space shows that such structure attains Ricci lower bound:

for all

. Followed with a local Li–Yau-type gradient estimate or Harnack inequality; see

, one derives gradient estimate for the heat kernel outside the hyperplane

. Note that for

, the Grushin operator

can be expressed as the weighted Laplacian

, where

is the Laplace-Beltrami operator according to the metric (

), and

is a first order drift term. Instead of estimating Ricci curvature, one can verify that the curvature dimension inequality

.

By adapting the methods used in Theorem

, we prove the following result.

Theorem 1.4

Let

be the operator defined in (

), and let

,

.

2.
Preliminaries: weak-type Sobolev inequality

In this section, we introduce one of the key ingredients used in our argument. Let
(E,\mu)
be a measure space with a non-negative
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of functions on
E
and
W
be a norm or semi-norm on
\mathcal{F}
. We mainly need that
\mathcal{F}
has property: if
f\in\mathcal{F}
, then
(f-t)^{+}\wedge s\in\mathcal{F}
for all
t,s\geq 0
. Moreover, to avoid integrability problem, one also assumes that
\mathcal{F}\in\cap_{p}L^{p}
. For instance, on a Riemannian manifold
M
with Riemannian measure
\mu
, one can take
\mathcal{F}
to be the space of Lipschitz functions with compact support. For
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
f\in\mathcal{F}
with
f\geq 0
, we define truncated function

\displaystyle f_{k}:=(f-2^{k})^{+}\wedge 2^{k}.

Let
0<q\leq\infty
. We say the functional
W
satisfies condition
(\textrm{W}_{q})
if

(
\textrm{W}_{q}
)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}W(f_{k})^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq C_{q}W(f)

for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
. In particular, as also mentioned in
[
3
, Section 2]
, the semi-norm:
W(f)=\left(\int_{M}|\nabla f|^{p}d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
, where
\nabla
denote the Riemannian gradient, satisfies (

W
q

) for all
q\in[p,\infty]
. Introduce condition:

(
\textrm{S}_{r_{0},s_{0}}^{*,\theta_{0}}
)

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda>0}\left(\lambda\mu\left(\{|f|\geq\lambda\}\right)^{\frac{1}{r_{0}}}\right)\leq CW(f)^{\theta_{0}}\left(\|f\|_{\infty}\left[\mu(\textrm{supp}(f))\right]^{\frac{1}{s_{0}}}\right)^{1-\theta_{0}}.

The following is one of the main results in
[
3
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

[
3
, Theorem 3.1, Proposition 3.5]

Assume (

3.
Via heat kernel regularity

In this section, we give a formal proof of Proposition

, which is a criterion of ”reverse-improvement” of Sobolev inequality. This result is actually implicitly included in the paper
[
3
, Theorem 9.1]
. In
[

, the authors proved that a certain family of ”weak type” Sobolev inequalities directly implies the strong type estimate. Especially, in
[

, Theorem 9.1]
(also see

), they gave a general method to prove the so-called ”weak type” Sobolev inequality. Here, we run their argument rigorously with respect to heat semigroup.

Remark 3.1
.

We mention that the assumption

in the statement is not essential. As can be seen from the proof below,
(\textrm{S}_{n}^{2})
only contributes to the decay of heat kernel. In the case where

, one may replace the assumption
(\textrm{S}_{n}^{2})
by a Nash type inequality:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{2}^{1+2/n}\leq C\|\nabla f\|_{2}\|f\|_{1}^{2/n},

which is well-known to be equivalent to

, see
[

, Section 2]
and

. In short, the implication

holds as long as

for all

.

Proof of Proposition
1.1
.

Let

and

for some

We follow the ideas from

We recall the following lemma from

Lemma 4.6
.

[

Proposition 4.7

Let

a the manifold with ends defined by (

Proof of Proposition

.

Let
\Omega\subset M
with
|\Omega|\geq 1
. We pick a sequence of smooth cut-off functions

and

with

[

\{x\in M;|f|\geq 1\}.

Next, we decompose

It is clear to see that for each

(by zero extension) and
f_{0}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\tilde{K})

is a small neighbourhood of the compact connection set

with smooth boundary. By the decomposition, one can split the level set by

\displaystyle\textrm{vol}\left(\{|f|\geq 1\}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l}\textrm{vol}\left(\{E_{i};|f_{i}|\geq 1/(l+1)\}\right)+\textrm{vol}\left(\left\{\tilde{K};|f_{0}|\geq 1/(l+1)\right\}\right)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\mathcal{L}_{i}+\mathcal{L}_{0}.

For each

for some

\displaystyle\|e^{-t_{i}\Delta_{i}}f_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq C\|f\|_{1}t_{i}^{-n_{i}/2}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\textrm{vol}\left(\left\{x\in E_{i};\int_{0}^{t_{i}}|\Delta_{i}e^{-s\Delta_{i}}f_{i}|ds\geq 1/2(l+1)\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\|\nabla f_{i}\|_{1}\|\nabla e^{-s\Delta_{i}}\|_{\infty\to\infty}ds

.

Regarding the term

Lemma 4.8
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{1}(\tilde{K})}\lesssim\|\nabla u\|_{1}

5.
Isoperimetric inequality on Grushin spaces

In this section, we study Sobolev inequalities on Grushin spaces. Recall that for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
(
n,m\geq 1
), one defines Grushin type operator

(5.1)

\displaystyle L=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}-|x|^{2\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\partial_{y_{i}}^{2}=\Delta_{x}+|x|^{2\beta}\Delta_{y},\quad\beta\geq 0.

Define its associated gradient operator by

(5.2)

\displaystyle\nabla_{L}=\left(\nabla_{x},|x|^{\beta}\nabla_{y}\right),

and the square of the length of the gradient (
carre du champ
)

\displaystyle|\nabla_{L}f|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\partial_{x_{i}}f|^{2}+|x|^{2\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\partial_{y_{j}}f|^{2}.

One checks easily that the functional
W(f)=\|\nabla_{L}f\|_{p}
satisfies (

W
q

) for all
q\geq p
.

Next, we have integration by parts:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+m}}Lf(\xi)g(\xi)d\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+m}}\nabla_{L}f(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{L}g(\xi)d\xi,

for all
f,g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m})
. Set

\displaystyle D=\left\{\phi\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+m});|\nabla_{L}\phi|^{2}\leq 1\right\},

One defines the canonical distance:

\displaystyle d(\xi,\eta):=\sup_{\phi\in D}\left|\phi(\xi)-\phi(\eta)\right|\in[0,\infty]

for all
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
. Its associated geodesic ball
B(\xi,r)
can be then described by the set

\displaystyle B(\xi,r)=\left\{\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m};d(\eta,\xi)<r\right\},

and we write
V(\xi,r)
to be the volume of
B(\xi,r)
with respect to the usual Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
.

5.1.
Curvature dimension inequality and gradient estimates

In their seminal work on diffusion generators, Bakry and Émery
[
4
]
introduced the curvature–dimension condition, denoted
\textrm{CD}(K,N)
, as an abstract substitute for a lower Ricci bound and an upper dimension bound in a purely analytic framework. Given a symmetric Markov generator
\mathcal{L}
with carré du champ
\Gamma
and iterate
\Gamma_{2}
, the inequality

(
\textrm{CD}(K,N)
)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(f)\geq K\Gamma(f)+\frac{1}{N}\left(L(f)\right)^{2},\quad\forall f\in C^{\infty}

encodes both a “Ricci curvature” lower bound
K
and “effective dimension”
N
. Originally motivated by hypercontractivity and logarithmic Sobolev inequalities,
\textrm{CD}(K,N)
unified a variety of functional–inequality proofs under one versatile hypothesis.

On a smooth Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
with
\mathcal{L}=-\Delta_{g}
the negative sign Laplace-Beltrami operator on
M
, one recovers exactly the classical Ricci curvature bound
\textrm{Ric}_{g}\geq Kg
when
\textrm{dim}(M)=N
. In this setting, the Bochner identity shows that
\textrm{CD}(K,N)
is equivalent to the Ricci curvature lower bound
\textrm{Ric}_{g}\geq Kg
on Riemannian manifolds, tying the analytic condition directly to geometric curvature. One of the most celebrated consequences of
\textrm{CD}(K,N)
is the derivation of Li–Yau gradient estimates and Harnack inequalities (see
[
34
]
) for the heat semigroup: the same maximum‐principle arguments that yield pointwise bounds on
|\nabla\log{p_{t}}|
under
\textrm{Ric}\geq K
work verbatim under
\textrm{CD}(K,N)
. This analytic versatility paved the way for extending curvature–dimension ideas beyond smooth manifolds.

In the past decade, Lott, Villani
[
36
]
and Sturm
[
46
,
47
]
formulated a synthetic curvature–dimension condition
\textrm{CD}(K,N)
for metric measure spaces, and Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré
[
1
]
further generalized it to the
\textrm{RCD}(K,N)
and
\textrm{RCD}^{*}(K,N)
frameworks. In particular,
\textrm{RCD}^{*}(K,N)
coincides with the original
\textrm{Ric}\geq K
and dimension
\leq N
on the Riemannian setting, and for the case of the weighted manifolds, the notion
\textrm{RCD}^{*}(K,N)
coincides with
\textrm{CD}(K,N)
condition. These developments combine optimal‐transport techniques with Bakry–Émery calculus, yielding a robust theory of nonsmooth spaces with lower Ricci bounds and finite dimension.

For the conventional issue, we let
\mathcal{L}
be a
non-positive
self-adjoint diffusion operator. Formally, one defines bilinear form:

(5.3)

\displaystyle\Gamma(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{L}(fg)-f\mathcal{L}(g)-g\mathcal{L}(f)\right),\quad\forall f,g\in C^{\infty}.

Note that
\Gamma
is symmetric and
\Gamma(f)=\Gamma(f,f)
is the so-called
carre du champ
operator. Next, one defines the iterated bilinear form:

(5.4)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{L}\Gamma(f,g)-\Gamma(f,\mathcal{L}(g))-\Gamma(g,\mathcal{L}(f))\right),

and
\Gamma_{2}(f)=\Gamma_{2}(f,f)
is the second
carre du champ
operator.

The first step of our argument is to estimate the ”Ricci curvature” of the Grushin space.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
n,m\geq 1
and
\beta\geq 0
. The Grushin space satisfies the following curvature dimension inequality:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(f)\geq-\frac{c_{1}(n,m,\beta)}{|x|^{2}}\Gamma(f)+c_{2}(n,m,\beta)\left(L(f)\right)^{2},\quad\forall f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}).

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

By (
A.4
) in the Appendix
A
, a direct and lengthy computation yields that

(5.5)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(f)(x,y)=S_{xx}+2|x|^{2\beta}S_{xy}+|x|^{4\beta}S_{yy}+\beta(n+2\beta-2)|x|^{2\beta-2}|\nabla_{y}f|^{2}

\displaystyle+4\beta|x|^{2\beta-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{i}f_{y_{j}}f_{x_{i},y_{j}}+2\beta|x|^{2\beta-2}\Delta_{y}f\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}f_{x_{i}},

and
\Gamma(f)=|\nabla_{L}f|^{2}=|\nabla_{x}f|^{2}+|x|^{2\beta}|\nabla_{y}f|^{2}
and
\left(L(f)\right)^{2}=\left(\Delta_{x}f+|x|^{2\beta}\Delta_{y}f\right)^{2}
, where
S_{xx}=\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}f_{x_{i},x_{k}}^{2}
,
S_{xy}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{x_{i},y_{j}}^{2}
and
S_{yy}=\sum_{j,l=1}^{m}f_{y_{j},y_{l}}^{2}
. We first estimate the cross-terms. By Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have

\displaystyle 4\beta|x|^{2\beta-2}x_{i}f_{y_{j}}f_{x_{i},y_{j}}\geq-|x|^{2\beta}f_{x_{i},y_{j}}^{2}-4\beta^{2}x_{i}^{2}|x|^{2\beta-4}f_{y_{j}}^{2}.

Summing over
i,j
, one deduces that

(5.6)

\displaystyle 4\beta|x|^{2\beta-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{i}f_{y_{j}}f_{x_{i},y_{j}}\geq-|x|^{2\beta}S_{xy}-4\beta^{2}|x|^{2\beta-2}|\nabla_{y}f|^{2}.

Next, by using a similar method, one gets

(5.7)

\displaystyle 2\beta|x|^{2\beta-2}\Delta_{y}f\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}f_{x_{i}}\geq-\epsilon|x|^{4\beta}\left(\Delta_{y}f\right)^{2}-\epsilon^{-1}4\beta^{2}|x|^{-2}|\nabla_{x}f|^{2},\quad\forall\epsilon>0.

Plug (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) into (
5.5
). One obtains

(5.8)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(f)\geq\frac{S_{xx}+2|x|^{2\beta}S_{xy}+|x|^{4\beta}S_{yy}}{2}-\frac{c_{1}(n,m,\beta,\epsilon)}{|x|^{2}}\Gamma(f)-\epsilon\left(L(f)\right)^{2},\quad\epsilon>0.

To this end, one considers
(n+m)\times(n+m)
matrix:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\textrm{Hess}_{xx}f&\textrm{Hess}_{xy}f\\
\textrm{Hess}_{xy}^{T}f&\textrm{Hess}_{yy}f\end{bmatrix},

where
Hess
is the Hessian matrix with respective to the gradient
\nabla_{L}=(\nabla_{x},|x|^{\beta}\nabla_{y})
. Note that the square of the Frobenius norm of
\mathcal{H}
is just
S_{xx}+2|x|^{2\beta}S_{xy}+|x|^{4\beta}S_{yy}
and the trace of
\mathcal{H}
coincides with
L(f)
. It then follows by trace inequality that

To apply our method, the next step is to develop the following Hardy’s inequality.

In the appendix, we check (

) explicitly. One thing needs to be careful is that by our definition, the Grushin operator is defined as

) to
\mathcal{L}=-L
. In what follows, we assume
1\leq i,k\leq n
,

.

Let

. Then

\displaystyle\Gamma(f)=\sum_{i}f_{x_{i}}^{2}+|x|^{2\beta}\sum_{j}f_{y_{j}}^{2}=|\nabla_{L}f|^{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(f)^{2}=L(f)^{2}=|\Delta_{x}f|^{2}+|x|^{4\beta}|\Delta_{y}f|^{2}+2|x|^{2\beta}\Delta_{x}f\Delta_{y}f,

which are clear.

Next, we compute
\Gamma_{2}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{L}\Gamma(f)-2\Gamma(f,\mathcal{L}f)\right)
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\Gamma(f)

\displaystyle=-L\left(\sum_{i}f_{x_{i}}^{2}+|x|^{2\beta}\sum_{j}f_{y_{j}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i}L\left(f_{x_{i}}^{2}\right)-L\left(|x|^{2\beta}\sum_{j}f_{y_{j}}^{2}\right)

By Leibuniz’s rule, one shows that

(A.1)

\displaystyle L(gh)=(Lg)h+g(Lh)-2\nabla_{L}g\cdot\nabla_{L}h=(Lg)h+g(Lh)-2\Gamma(g,h).

Therefore,

\displaystyle L\left(f_{x_{i}}^{2}\right)=2L\left(f_{x_{i}}\right)f_{x_{i}}-2\Gamma(f_{x_{i}}),

and

\displaystyle L\left(|x|^{2\beta}\sum_{j}f_{y_{j}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=L\left(|x|^{2\beta}\right)\sum_{j}f_{y_{j}}^{2}+|x|^{2\beta}\sum_{j}L\left(f_{y_{j}}^{2}\right)-2\sum_{j}\Gamma\left(|x|^{2\beta},f_{y_{j}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-2\beta(n+2\beta-2)|x|^{2\beta-2}\sum_{j}f_{y_{j}}^{2}+2|x|^{2\beta}\sum_{j}L(f_{y_{j}})f_{y_{j}}

\displaystyle-2|x|^{2\beta}\sum_{j}\Gamma(f_{y_{j}})-2\sum_{j}\Gamma\left(|x|^{2\beta},f_{y_{j}}^{2}\right).

Set
S_{xx}=\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}f_{x_{i},x_{k}}^{2}
,
S_{xy}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{x_{i},y_{j}}^{2}
and
S_{yy}=\sum_{j,l=1}^{m}f_{y_{j},y_{l}}^{2}
. One observes that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\Gamma(f_{x_{i}})=\sum_{i,k}f_{x_{i},x_{k}}^{2}+|x|^{2\beta}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}f_{x_{i},y_{j}}^{2}=S_{xx}+|x|^{2\beta}S_{xy},

and

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\Gamma(f_{y_{j}})=S_{xy}+|x|^{2\beta}S_{yy}.

One concludes

(A.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\Gamma(f)

\displaystyle=2S_{xx}+4|x|^{2\beta}S_{xy}+2|x|^{4\beta}S_{yy}-2\sum_{i}L(f_{x_{i}})f_{x_{i}}-2|x|^{2\beta}\sum_{j}L(f_{y_{j}})f_{y_{j}}

\displaystyle+2\beta(n+2\beta-2)|x|^{2\beta-2}|\nabla_{y}f|^{2}+2\sum_{j}\Gamma\left(|x|^{2\beta},f_{y_{j}}^{2}\right).

Next,

\displaystyle\Gamma(f,\mathcal{L}f)=-\Gamma(f,Lf)=-\nabla_{L}f\cdot\nabla_{L}\left(Lf\right)=-\sum_{i}f_{x_{i}}(Lf)_{x_{i}}-|x|^{2\beta}\sum_{j}f_{y_{j}}(Lf)_{y_{j}}.

Note that

\displaystyle(Lf)_{x_{i}}

\displaystyle=L(f_{x_{i}})+2\beta|x|^{2\beta-2}x_{i}\Delta_{y}f.

\displaystyle(Lf)_{y_{j}}

\displaystyle=L(f_{y_{j}}).

We deduce

(A.3)
