A global product of 150-m urban building height based on spaceborne lidar
Authors: Xiao Ma, Guang Zheng, Chi Xu, L. Monika Moskal, Peng Gong, Qinghua Guo, Huabing Huang, Xuecao Li, Xinlian Liang, Yong Pang, Cheng Wang, Huan Xie, Bailang Yu, Bo Zhao, Yuyu Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Urban building height, as a fundamental 3D urban structural feature, has far-reaching applications. However, creating readily available datasets of recent urban building heights with fine spatial resolutions and global coverage remains a challenging task. Here, we provide a 150-m global urban building heights dataset around 2020 by combining the spaceborne lidar (Global Ecosystem Dynamics Investigation, GEDI), multi-sourced data (Landsat-8, Sentinel-2, and Sentinel-1), and topographic data. The validation results revealed that the GEDI-estimated building height samples were effective compared to the reference data (Pearson’s r = 0.81, RMSE = 3.58 m). The mapping product also demonstrated good performance, as indicated by its strong correlation with the reference data (Pearson’s r = 0.71, RMSE = 4.73 m). Compared with the currently existing datasets, it holds the ability to provide a spatial resolution (150 m) with a great level of inherent details about the spatial heterogeneity and flexibility of updating using the GEDI samples as inputs. This product will boost future urban studies across many fields, including environmental, ecological, and social sciences.

Background & Summary

Cities are currently home to ~45 billion people, or ~57% of the world’s total population, and these numbers are projected to grow in the coming decades1. Achieving sustainable, resilient cities as the major habitat of our species is therefore a crucial goal for sustaining the existence of human civilizations in the face of increasingly severe climate change and other impacts2. The rapid development of cloud computing services, multiple remote sensing observations, and artificial intelligence in recent years has boosted the ability to characterize, understand, and monitor urban systems with big data3,4. However, high-precision urban data are primarily available for the most developed megacities, leaving significant gaps for many other cities worldwide, especially in Global South countries. This disparity creates a key inequality that hampers accurate assessment of the impact of urban environmental changes.

In the past, a large amount of research focused on extracting the two-dimensional form of cities and their dynamic evolution, developing numerous datasets (e.g., urban boundary and artificial impervious surface)5–7. The generation of datasets related to urban vertical information is relatively less. Three-dimensional urban structure, such as building height, is fundamental information about urban environments and has far-reaching applications for scientific research and management of urban systems8–10. With the increasing availabilities of remotely sensed data and topographic data, growing studies try to map building heights at the regional or global scale based on direct and indirect methods. The direct method generates the global building height maps from the normalized digital surface model (nDSM), which is mainly derived from stereo remotely sensed images (e.g., GaoFen-7) or digital elevation model (DEM) products (e.g., ALOS DSM and TanDEM-X)11–13. Esch, et al.12 first produced the world settlement footprint 3D map at a 90-m grid size using the TanDEM-X product. He, et al.14 used the ALOS DSM product to generate a 30-m global building height. Due to the limited DEM products, most previous studies chose to produce the building height map by calibrating the regression relationship between the building height samples and various features derived from the multi-source spatial data8,15. For instance, Li, et al.16 built random forest models to map global built-up 3D patterns at the 1-km resolution around 2015 including the building height parameter. Based on mapping global built-up height using Sentinel-1 data, Zhou, et al.17 revealed the large inequality in infrastructure availability in the Global South. A notable advance is the recent production of a global building height distribution map from the Global Human Settlement Layer Built-Height (GHSL-H) dataset at a 100-m grid size for the year 201818, which mainly estimated the building height using the DEM-derived features (i.e., ALOS DSM and SRTM DEM) and updated the last predictions from the Sentinel-2 data.

However, existing global products are mainly subject to relatively coarse spatial resolutions (e.g., 500 m or 1 km) or time lags. Especially, the nDSM-derived global building height products show limitations in the analysis of the current urban environment, which mainly used the DEM products about ten years ago15. Although the new Chinese multi-view satellite of GaoFen-7 showed great potential in estimating the high-resolution building height map, the GaoFen-7 images don’t have full coverage in the world11. Moreover, in the indirect approach, researchers paid more attention to exploring effectively explanatory variables and developing various regression algorithms, such as adding nighttime light (NTL) data and employing the deep learning method19–21. Nevertheless, obtaining global continuous distribution of building height samples is also challenging as they were produced from very different sources, such as digital elevation model (DEM) data, open GIS data, and lidar (light detection and ranging) data, with contrasting levels of accuracy and uncertainty16,22.

Spaceborne lidar is so far the most promising tool for sampling building heights with close-to-global coverage. Particularly, the recently launched Global Ecosystem Dynamics Investigation (GEDI) instrument has shown great power in discretely retrieving 3D structures on the Earth (i.e., from 51.6° N to 51.6° S) based on the full-waveform lidar within each footprint with a diameter of 25 m, while GEDI was originally designed to measure forest vertical structure with high sampling density concerning carbon storage and deforestation23–25. It has been proven to be a useful tool for mapping regional urban building heights at the 150-m spatial resolution26. Compared with the complex noise filtering and photon classification processes of the ICEsat-2 data27–29, the GEDI-based relative height metrics are more straightforward in building height estimation.

Here we provide a global product of urban building height at the 150-m spatial resolution around 2020 through extrapolating GEDI-based building height samples using wall-to-wall time-series features of spectral and backscattering coefficients about buildings and topography information across the world. We developed random forest (RF) models to correlate between the GEDI-derived building height samples and the variables from spatially continuous remotely sensed imageries for 68 sub-regions at a global level, respectively. We then employed these models to obtain a global map of spatially continuous urban building heights. To conduct the validation and comparisons, we collected the reference data from North America, Europe, China, Brazil, and New Zealand and the recently released 100-m and 1-km global building height datasets produced by Pesaresi and Politis18 and Li, et al.16, respectively. The global dataset of our urban building heights is available on Figshare (10.6084/m9.figshare.25729248.v6) and can be viewed by following the provided website (https://nju-eco-lidar.projects.earthengine.app/view/gbh2020), powered by the Google Earth Engine (GEE). Multi-sourced datasets applied in the global urban building height mapping.

The global GEDI footprints covering the world’s human settlement regions were derived from the mouth raster GEDI L2A product ranging from 2019 to 2021 by synthesizing the monthly orbits of the raw GEDI L2A product34 with geolocation, relative height, and quality flags information within the GEE platform. With the cross-track pointing ability of the GEDI system35, we assume that the laser footprint has a lower probability of covering the same area in each month and convert the mouth raster GEDI L2A layer into a vector point layer as GEDI footprints (i.e., diameter = 25 m) to represent the samples of vertical structures within global coverages. We filtered out the low-quality (i.e., quality flag = 0 and sensitivity < 0.9) or potentially deteriorated (i.e., degrade flag > 0) footprints for the GEDI L2A product. We also masked out all GEDI footprints falling outside built-up areas using the World Settlement Footprint data 201936. Figure 2d displays the number of high-quality GEDI footprints in 150-km grids worldwide. Following the similar procedures of our previous method26, we kept GEDI footprints with lesser potential geolocation errors by calculating the proportion of building area in each footprint (0.1~0.9). We finally produced a high-quality GEDI footprints vector dataset with the 85th (RH85) and 95th (RH95) percentiles of relative height metrics in each footprint for all built-up areas in the world. Compared with the existing urban boundary maps produced using remotely sensed data with coarse resolution, the GAIA-derived global urban boundary datasets (GUB2018) hold the promising ability to exhibit a high level of detail about the city’s extent and boundary45. It was applied to determine the mapping areas in this study. GEDI-based building height samples

We created global building height samples by aggregating filtered high-quality GEDI footprints into a 150-m grid size. However, only using single relative height metrics may cause an overestimation of the residential areas consisting of low-rise single-family house buildings surrounded by tall trees26. To overcome this problem, we computed the maximum NDVI value during the vegetation growing season of 2020 from Landsat-8 for each GEDI footprint. We finally estimated building heights using a composite relative height index (RH) at footprint level by (a) setting the GEDI-based RH95 metrics as primary building height; (b) using the RH95 metrics to represent building height if the maximum NDVI value was less than 0.5; (c) replacing the RH95 metrics with the RH85 metrics if the maximum NDVI value was greater than or equal to 0.5 and the proportion of building area in the footprint buffer region (i.e., radius = 12.5 m) was greater than or equal to 0.6 and the value of RH95 metrics was greater than 9 m; (d) keeping the GEDI-based RH95 metrics as the building height if the maximum NDVI value was greater than or equal 0.5 and the value of RH95 metrics value was less than or equal to 9 m; (e) and saving all footprints with the values of RH were greater equal than 2.5 m. By doing so, we computed the mean values of the RH index of all high-quality footprints (count ≥ 3) located in each grid with a 150-m size as the actual mean structure height and used them as the dependent variable to calibrate building height models. The uneven spatial distribution patterns of GEDI-based building height sample numbers could be observed (Fig. 2e) ranging from less than 100 to more than 10,000 in each 150-km grid around the world. More samples are located in the regions of Europe, East Asia, North Africa, and southwestern North America based on the different building footprint densities, levels of urbanization, and orbit track design of GEDI aboard platform (i.e., the International Space Station).

RS-based explanatory features

We used five spectral feature variables (i.e., NDBI, NDVI, mNDWI, RVI64, and DVI64) and two radar bands (i.e., VV and VH) to compute the multitemporal statistical features (Table 1) to reduce the effects of inconsistent temporal observations by acquiring the phenological signals15,25. The ability of effectively capturing the surrounding building information of texture features could obtain complete shadow features as much as possible from a 10-m or 30-m pixel19. Therefore, we also computed four texture features (i.e., mean, variance, contrast, and dissimilarity) using the Grey Level Co-occurrence Matrixes48 based on the multitemporal statistic features. Following Ma, et al.26, the window size of texture features were set as 5 × 5, 11 × 11, and 13 × 13 for Landsat-8, Sentinel-2, and Sentinel-1, respectively. Topographic variables include elevation, aspect, and slope. In total, 323 explanatory variables were involved in extrapolating discrete samples to the spatially continuous map. We projected all explanatory variables into the World Mollweide coordinate system and reduced their spatial resolution from raw resolutions to 150 m.

RF-based Global Urban Building Height Estimation

In this study, we used the subregion modeling method to estimate building heights by combining GEDI-based building height samples, RS-derived features, and topographic features (Fig. 1). Previous studies found that models trained within a limited spatial coverage could perform better than that calibrated within a global scale25,49. The structure style and urban environment may be affected by factors such as the local climate, level of urbanization, and cultural norms. To ensure consistency and reduce variation, the modeling subregion was generated by merging the administrative regions with a similar climate context50. Next, building height samples from the global GEDI were assigned to grids on a 150-km scale (Fig. 2e), and samples for the training model were selected based on the corresponding subregion’s 150-km grids. Due to variations in development levels and GEDI track densities worldwide, we selected GEDI-based samples to ensure that the proportion of sample values greater than or equal to 9 m was at least 10% in subregions with sample numbers over 5,000. The final number of samples per subregion is shown in Fig. 2f. Since GEDI does not cover high latitude areas above 51.6° N, we trained 5 models of the northern region based on the samples within 600 km nearby. Random Forest algorithm is an ensemble method used for prediction and has been widely applied to estimate vertical parameters (e.g., forest canopy height & building height)51. Under the Law of Large Numbers, it can effectively reduce overfitting26,52. Therefore, Random Forest (RF) modeling was used to establish the regression relationship between the GEDI-based sample and independent features. All modeling procedures were conducted using the randomForest package in RStudio53. To maintain consistent interpretation, we retained all features with a count of 323 for each subregion modeling. The RF parameters for mtry and ntree were established at one-third of the number of candidate features and 1,500, correspondingly.

Technical Validation

Validation of GEDI-based samples

Indicators of Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) and root mean square error (RMSE) were used for accuracy assessment. We used the maximum height and the area-weighted mean height derived from the reference data consisting of building footprint vector files to evaluate the effectiveness of GEDI-based building height estimation at the footprint and sample levels, respectively. We assumed that the mean RH value could represent the actual building height calculated by the area-weighted method.

The assessment of the building height estimation based on the GEDI data is shown in Table 3. At the footprint level, the RH index exhibits consistent agreement with reference data, indicated by Pearson’s r of 0.70 and RMSE of 5.54 m. Across three regions (i.e., NA, CHN, and Southern Hemisphere), the r values range between 0.53 and 0.69. The lowest and highest values of RMSE are 5.26 m and 14.79 m, respectively. Overall, the building height estimations at the footprint level were distributed symmetrically on the reference line. Nevertheless, the estimation of certain high-rise buildings ( ≥ 27 m) had increased uncertainty with the GEDI L2A product. When utilizing GEDI-based building height samples, the mean values of the RH index demonstrated a stronger correlation (i.e., Pearson’s r = 0.81) and a lower error (i.e., RMSE = 3.58 m) compared to the RH index. Although the correlation coefficient (r) and root-mean-square error (RMSE) values in China were only 0.42 and 8.32 m, respectively, due to the impact of discrete points, Fig. 5b illustrates that most of the points were concentrated along the 1:1 line. It was noted that the validation in the Southern Hemisphere didn’t contain the reference data of Sao Paulo because of no GEDI-based samples within the extent of that. Comparison to the reference data

We converted all the reference data to the World Mollweide projection and generated the actual mean building height using the area-weighted and downscaling methods, respectively, based on the vector and raster reference data to evaluate the building height dataset.

The validation results showed that the building height dataset had a Pearson’s r of 0.71 and an RMSE value of 4.73 m (Fig. 6). To provide a comprehensive evaluation, we divided the reference data into five regions, namely North America, Europe, China, the southern hemisphere, and the northern region without GEDI coverage. The r values for these regions are similar with a range of 0.70 to 0.78. Compared to Europe, regions outside of it displayed much larger RMSE values ranging from 7.17 to 10.47 m. Our dataset showed satisfactory alignment with reference data in the northern region without GEDI data, with an RMSE value of 3.85 m and a correlation coefficient of 0.71. Assessment results at the city level are presented in Fig. 6g. Our dataset demonstrated a strong correlation (i.e., r ≥ 0.70) with the reference data in 20 cities and exhibited lower errors with an RMSE value below 5 m in 24 cities. Remarkably, even in Helsinki, the northernmost city within the reference data at approximately 60°N latitude, the dataset effectively captured the urban building height distribution, achieving r and RMSE values of 0.73 and 4.52 m, respectively. Lower errors were found in European cities compared to the metropolises (e.g., New York, Shanghai, and San Francisco). Figure 6a–f illustrate a strong correlation between the predicted heights and the reference data from 3 m to 30 m. The values are concentrated near the 1:1 line. Comparison to the existing products

We compared our product to two recent global building height datasets (i.e., GHSL-H and Li, et al.16) using the GUB2018 dataset for cities. To ensure consistent results, we selected the average net building height product of GHSL-H for further comparison18. The GHSL-H product, with a grid size of 100 m, reports on global building heights in 2018. Li, et al.16 constructed a global 3-D map of built-up areas, including building height, with a grid size of 1 km for 2015. This was done by creating a regression relationship between reference data and features derived from multiple sources of spatial data. Both products utilized the Mollweide projection. We resampled GHSL-H from the raw resolution to the 150-m resolution in accordance with our building height dataset. Usage Notes

Our dataset offers the benefits of fine resolution, reduced time lags, and ease of updating, indicating considerable potential to assist urban research. Our analysis provided novel insights into the recent state of building heights in urban areas worldwide and revealed significant variations in 3-D city forms across the globe. For instance, the building height patterns of metropolises usually exhibited a 3-D form of a single peak with a gradual decrease from the center to the surrounding area in Europe, America, and Australia (Fig. 3a-I,a-II, b-I,b-II, g-I,g-II,i-I,i-II). However, some cities showed different 3-D forms, such as Lagos with generally low buildings (Fig. 3d-I,d-II) and Beijing with a U-shape form (Fig. 3e-I,e-II). Furthermore, we also paid close attention to the close-up scenes with four-by-eight grids to validate the consistency of our product and the actual building height distribution. As indicated in Fig. 3g-III,g-IV, there were obvious long shadows of tall buildings in the corresponding areas of red pixels by referring to the very high resolution (VHR) Bing images (https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/bingmaps/rest-services/directly-accessing-the-bing-maps-tiles). The actual region covered with blue pixels represented an opposite situation, which gathered a lot of low buildings with fewer shadows (Fig. 3c-III,c-IV). Therefore, our dataset could correctly perform the spatial distributions of building heights for the global cities.

Based on calculations using the GUB2018 dataset, the average building height and coefficient of variation were determined for large cities with urban areas greater than or equal to 100 km2. Notably, when compared to those in North America and Europe, Asian and South American cities displayed greater heterogeneity in their vertical landscapes (Fig. 9b). Figure 9a illustrates the high concentration of high-rise buildings in Chinese cities. The mean building heights of major Chinese cities were over twice those of cities in the United States, in line with Zhou’s findings17 (Fig. 9a). Additionally, we noted disparities in the development of African cities. In addition, we used the %IncMSE indicator based on the random forest algorithm to statistically determine the top five variables for each partition model. Figure 9c illustrates the features that appear with a frequency of 5 or more. Features related to the built environment exhibit greater explanatory power. For example, features derived from the NDBI index or related to the SWIR1 band appear more frequently than those from other indices. Topographic features also played a relatively significant role, possibly due to their ability to explain complex urban terrain. Although there were fewer Sentinel-1-derived features in the top rank, radar-based features could provide vital information about building height for cities significantly affected by cloud cover. Texture features or multitemporal statistical features that enhance variability of urban vertical characteristic were always useful for explaining building heights. This statistic offers a reference for subsequent related work.

The validation results observed a saturation effect exhibiting a significant underestimation for grids covering tall buildings (>30 m). This could be due to issues related to samples and independent features. The uncertainty and low proportion of GEDI-based samples with high values, resulting from geolocation error of GEDI system, the number of GEDI orbits, limitation of GEDI relative height metrics, urbanization level, and policy restrict, may affect the accurate calibration and estimation ability of the model. For instance, high-rise buildings have a low proportion in Africa, concentrated in a few major cities55. Some European cities enforce stringent building restrictions to preserve their historic landscapes56. The urban centres usually show high vertical landscape heterogeneity. The GEDI system’s swath of 4.2 km and across-track distance of 600 m35 make it more likely to miss measuring the height of tall buildings in cities with low density. Testing the availability of additional spaceborne lidar, such as ICESat-227,28 and the Chinese terrestrial ecosystem carbon inventory satellite (TECIS, a.k.a GouMang)57, is worthwhile as a solution for the limited number of high-rise building samples derived from spaceborne lidar.

Unequally available satellite observations, limited explanatory ability, and masking loss of effective pixels of optical or radar features may result in the underestimation of dense areas with high-rise buildings15,25,26. The uncertainty in the availability of satellite observations of each grid (Fig. 2a~c) could result in differences in the interpretability of multitemporal statistics metrics derived from remote sensing. The previous study has examined the limitations of utilizing optical images to measure overlapping shadow data15, hindering the measurement of shadows in areas of dense buildings. Complex urban designs and building materials may also increase the uncertainty of the backscatter coefficient8. Nonetheless, reducing latitudes correspond to shorter building shadows, resulting in less disparity in the shadows of buildings at various heights within the equatorial area. Additionally, the high-precision mask data may lose some effective pixels in 150-m grids with sparse structures26. Consequently, our findings demonstrate greater uncertainty regarding high-rise buildings in specific European and equatorial cities, such as Singapore and Paris (Figs. 7c3 and 8b4).
