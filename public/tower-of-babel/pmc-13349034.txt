Hand hygiene practices: knowledge and habits of children aged 8–10 years
Authors: Anabela Fonseca Pereira, Anaísa Natário Dias, Karla Alonso Vázquez, Laura Rejo García, Rocío Rodríguez Mateo, Lucía Fernández Gómez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHand hygiene is a simple, low-cost, and effective measure for preventing infection by infectious agents and promoting child health. When introduced early, handwashing can become sustained health-promoting behaviour with long-term benefits.PurposeTo characterize the knowledge and hand hygiene practices among a sample of primary school children aged 8–10 years.MethodsA descriptive, cross-sectional, and quantitative study with a qualitative component, was conducted among 92 primary school children. Data were collected in april 2025 using a questionnaire.ResultsFindings showed that children frequently wash their hands at critical moments (after using the bathroom, before meals and after playtime). Handwashing practices were generally consistent across home and school contexts, predominantly involving soap and water, and were often performed autonomously. Participants demonstrated good knowledge of the role of hand hygiene in disease prevention and in reducing the transmission of contamination to objects, food, and other individuals.ConclusionThis study identified consistently adopted hand hygiene practices, supported by a good level of knowledge within the studied group. Schools represent a key setting for health promotion, while families play a crucial role in consolidating behaviours. COVID-19 may have served as a contextual catalyst for the consolidation of hand hygiene routines into children’s daily lives.Practice implicationsNurses should assume a proactive role as health educators, ensuring the reinforcement of hand hygiene education through evidence-based interventions developed in partnership with families and schools. The integration of technical, pedagogical, and behavioural strategies can contribute positively to public health outcomes.

Background

Over the past century, child and adolescent health has undergone a global epidemiological transition. Evidence indicates that investments in health promotion programmes and interventions targeting early childhood yield high returns for society [1], because the paediatric population is especially vulnerable to the spread of infections, playing a relevant role as a reservoir of pathogenic agents and contributing to transmission to adults [2]. Accordingly, the best health outcomes for children and adolescents are achieved through promoting healthy behaviours, preventing disease, and early detection of health problems within the community [3].

Health promotion has great potential to improve the population´s health by promoting healthy behaviours, making it an effective way for children, teenagers, and families to exercise greater control over their health and contribute to its improvement [1]. WHO also promotes an integrated approach to managing childhood illness that considers all aspects of a child’s health, and a continuum of care throughout the early years to safeguard their developmental outcomes, including reducing risk factors for diseases that can arise later in life [4].

In this context, hand hygiene is recognised as one of the most cost-effective, accessible, and impactful measures for preventing the transmission of viruses and other microorganisms [5]. To strengthen surveillance and global response capacity, the World Health Organization has also published a priority list of bacterial pathogens that pose significant threats to public health [6].

Beyond eliminating microorganisms, preventing transmission, and breaking the chain of infection [7], it is well documented that hand hygiene promotion programmes implemented in early childhood increase adherence to this practice and reduce the incidence of gastrointestinal and respiratory diseases, as well as school absenteeism [8]. Nevertheless, as recently as 2021, an estimated 2.3 billion people worldwide were unable to wash their hands with soap and water at home, and nearly half of schools globally lacked basic hygiene services, affecting approximately 817 million children [5].

Health, however, does not begin in hospitals but within communities and schools. Although studies demonstrate that handwashing can reduce disease incidence by up to 40%, adherence among young children remains insufficient [9]. The literature further identifies several factors contributing to low adherence, including limited access to resources and facilities, forgetfulness, knowledge gaps, the absence of social norms, and habitual behavioural patterns [10].

Ensuring healthy children and adolescents requires implementing appropriate preventive measures from early childhood and sustaining them throughout adolescence and adulthood, to reduce exposure risks and support the development of healthy active adults [5]. Given that the literature points to a deficit in the systematic promotion of daily hygiene practices at home and school, the integration of health education programmes focused on hand hygiene into the school curriculum is imperative, with active involvement from teachers and health professionals [11].

WHO has also emphasised the importance of global awareness strategies, including community-based campaigns and behaviour-change programmes [12]. These strategies should be supported by a multimodal educational approach and continuous monitoring, in which nurses´role as health educators is particularly relevant [3]. In addition, the strategic role of schools in promoting child and adolescent health has been widely recognised worldwide, with more than 40 European countries, Australia, the Asia-Pacific region, and over 30 African countries implementing school-based health promotion initiatives [5].

In Portugal, although health inequalities persist due to social, environmental, and behavioural determinants, nursing-led programs targeting children up to six years of age and vulnerable pre-adolescents stand out for adopting intersectoral and interdisciplinary approaches [1]. These initiatives aim to promote health-related decision-making, self-confidence, and mental well-being [1].

Despite this, available research on hand hygiene practices among children remains limited, particularly in primary school settings, highlighting the need for more in-depth studies focusing on habitual hand hygiene behaviours [13]. Therefore, this study aimed to describe children’s knowledge and habits regarding hand hygiene practices.

Methods

Study design

This is a descriptive, cross-sectional, and quantitative study with a qualitative component, conducted in a primary school in Northern Portugal, in April 2025. The general objective of the study was to characterize knowledge and habits regarding hand hygiene practices of children aged 8–10 years. The specific objectives were to identify children´s hand hygiene habits at school, at home and to assess children´s perceptions of hand hygiene practices.

The inclusion criteria were: children aged 8–10 years; acceptance of participation in the study (after completion of the Informed, Free and Clarified Consent by the participant’s legal representative).

The exclusion criteria were: children under 8 years of age; children not attending primary school; and parents or legal representatives. These exclusion criteria are based on the fact that children in this age group do not yet have the reading, writing, and comprehension skills essential for completing a questionnaire. The option for non-probabilistic sampling of convenience was chosen for better access to the target population.

In accordance with the problem to be investigated, the study aimed to answer the following research question: “what are the knowledge and habits of children aged 8–10 years regarding hand hygiene practices?”

Data collection

Given the data collection instrument and the target population, the questionnaire survey was considered the most appropriate method. For its construction and in order to ensure that it was grounded in scientific evidence, a literature review was conducted based on a search of the following electronic databases: EBSCOhost Online Research Databases (CINAHL Complete; MEDLINE Complete; Cochrane Collection Plus; Nursing & Allied Health Collection) with full text; Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE); Scientific Electronic Library Online (SciELO) with full text; ELSEVIER with full text; PubMed Central (PMC) with full text; and Repositório Científico de Acesso Aberto de Portugal (RCAAP). The search was conducted during October 2024, using the following search strategy: ((”child” OR “children”) AND (“infection” OR “infection control” OR “hand disinfection” OR “hand hygiene” OR “hand washing”) AND (“primary school”) AND (“health knowledge, attitudes, practice” OR “health promotion” OR “education, nursing” OR “school health services” OR “program development” OR “preventive health services”)).

Regarding the questionnaire design and structure, to avoid a negative impact on the response rate, we avoided creating an excessively long questionnaire. Particular care was taken to formulate clear, concise, and accessible questions, free from ambiguity or unnecessary technical terminology. The questions were organized into a logical sequence of topics, with an estimated completion time of approximately 10 min.

The final version of the questionnaire consisted of three sections: sample sociodemographic characterization (gender and age), hand hygiene practices at home, and hand hygiene practices at school. The instrument contained a total of seventeen questions: two semi-structured and fifteen closed. The variables considered relevant were defined based on the object of study, research questions, study objectives, and the characteristics of the target population, in alignment with the conceptual framework (Table 1).

To analyze and interpret responses to the open-ended questions, two researchers (AP and AD) independently employed a content analysis technique to ensure consistency, inter-rater reliability, and depth of analysis. The process followed an inductive thematic approach, involving an initial open coding phase followed by the grouping of codes into broader, cohesive categories. To ensure the trustworthiness of the findings, any discrepancies in categorization between the two researchers were resolved through iterative discussion until a 100% consensus was reached. All data were manually managed and organized using Microsoft Excel spreadsheets. It is important to emphasize that this study aimed not only to quantify habits and routines but also to explore the underlying children’s perceptions. The inclusion of two open-ended questions provided a platform for the children’s voices, adding significant qualitative depth to the numerical data.

Following the thematic categorization, basic descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were calculated for each category to provide a quantitative overview of the most prevalent views. The analysis focused on key themes: justifications for handwashing after bathroom use; discrepancies between practices at school and at home; reliance on reminders in both settings; and the perceived need for assistance during handwashing. All responses were also anonymized and assigned alphanumeric identification codes ranging from C1 to C92. This approach offered a more holistic perspective, enriching the evidence regarding children’s views on hand hygiene practices.

Ethical considerations

To ensure compliance with procedures for the protection of fundamental human rights, an Informed, Free and Clarified Consent was prepared for the participant’s legal representative, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The school director provided this document in paper format to the participants for delivery to their legal representative. In this draft, a brief description of the study was provided (including the general objective, risks, and benefits), and respect for self-determination, privacy, anonymity, confidentiality, protection against discomfort and harm, and the right to fair and equitable treatment were also explicitly stated.

To proceed with the application of the data collection instrument, authorization was first requested from the school Director. Posteriorly, the research was validated and authorized by the ethics committee (authorization n. 06/2025).

After obtaining the Free and Informed Consent signed by the participant’s legal representative, the first version of the questionnaire underwent a pre-test to assess its effectiveness, comprehension, and clarity. This pre-test was conducted with a small sample (45 children) presenting characteristics similar to those of the target population. An additional validation was carried out by three experts (two PhD professors in Nursing and one primary school teacher), which refined question 6 (“hand hygiene practices at home”) and question 7 (“hand hygiene practices at school”) to ensured the effectiveness, validity, and reliability of the data collection instrument.

To apply the final version of the questionnaire, a date was scheduled with the school director. On the agreed day, the researchers were present in the classroom and provided a clear and simple explanation of the study and its objectives. The questionnaires were distributed across five classes and completed by the participants. The researchers withdrew from the classroom to minimize response bias and collected the questionnaires upon completion. A total of 92 valid questionnaires were obtained, from 120 delivered questionnaires, resulting in a response rate of 76.6%.

Statistical analyses

The collected data were analyzed using the statistical program Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM Corp. Released 2013. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 30.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). To analyze the sociodemographic characterization, a descriptive analysis was used. For inferential analysis, the chi-square (χ) test was used (Pereira et al., 2023), assuming a significance level of 0.05 (p < 0.05).

Hand hygiene practices at school

After using the bathroom, 91.3% (n = 84) of the children reported washing their hands, 5.4% (n = 5) reported washing them only sometimes, and 3.3% (n = 3) reported not washing their hands. Before meals, 65.2% (n = 60) reported washing their hands, 27.2% (n = 25) indicated that they washed them sometimes, and 7.6% (n = 7) reported not washing their hands. After meals, 58.7% (n = 54) stated that they washed their hands, while 41.3% (n = 38) reported washing them only sometimes. Following playtime, 92.4% (n = 85) of the children considered that hands should be washed, and 7.6% (n = 7) believed that they should not. Regarding the products used for handwashing, 84.8% (n = 78) reported using soap and water, and 15.2% (n = 14) reported washing their hands with only water. With respect to who taught them the importance of hand hygiene, 89.2% (n = 82) indicated that was their parents, 6.5% (n = 6) stated that was the teachers at school, and 4.3% (n = 4) reported having been taught by nurses at school. Concerning whether they washed their hands independently or required assistance, 97.8% (n = 90) stated that washed their hands by themselves, and 2.2% (n = 2) reported needing help from teachers. Before entering the classroom, 42.4% (n = 39) of the children reported washing their hands sometimes, 33.7% (n = 31) reported always washing their hands, and 23.9% (n = 22) reported not washing their hands. Finally, 81.5% (n = 75) of the children affirmed that they did not need to be reminded to wash their hands, and 18.5% (n = 17) stated that they did (Fig. 1).

The chi-square analyses did not demonstrate statistically significant associations between age and most hand hygiene practices in the school context, however, a statistically significant association was observed between age and handwashing after playtime, with higher adherence among 9 years old (χ²=6.364, p = 0.041). Hand hygiene practices at home

Most participants reported washing their hands after using the bathroom (90.2%; n = 83), while 9.8% (n = 9) indicated doing so only sometimes. Before meals, 77.2% (n = 71) reported always washing their hands, 18.5% (n = 17) reported sometimes washing their hands and 4.3% (n = 4) reported not washing their hands. When arriving home after school, 60.9% (n = 56) reported always washing their hands, 30,4% (n = 28) reported doing so sometimes, and 8.7% (n = 8) reported not washing their hands. Regarding hand hygiene products, the vast majority of children reported using water and soap (95.7%; n = 88), with a small proportion indicating the use of hand sanitizer (2.2%; n = 2) or water only (2.2%; n = 2). Most participants stated that their hand hygiene practices were similar at home and at school (83.7%; n = 77). In terms of autonomy, 63.0% (n = 58) of the children reported not needing reminders to wash their hands, while 33.7% (n = 31) reported sometimes needing reminders and 3.3% (n = 3) reported requiring reminders (Fig. 2).

Based on the collected data and the established research goals, eight distinct categories were identified: food and objects contamination, transmission to others; forgetfulness; contaminated environment; contact with feces or urine; disease emergence; presence of microorganisms; dirty hands), the assigned names arose naturally from the participants’ words, and the concepts were part of this research conceptual framework. The nomenclature for these categories emerged naturally from the participants’ own narratives (inductive coding), ensuring that the concepts remained consistent with the research’s conceptual framework.

The responses show that, among the children (45 participants), hand hygiene practices are considered essential for removing dirt from the hands. This indicates an understanding that hygiene behaviours contribute to the development of basic self-care skills and, consequently, strengthen autonomy, personal responsibility, and the adoption of health-promoting behaviours that will have an impact throughout life.

Discussion

This study aimed to describe children’s knowledge and habits regarding hand hygiene practices. The results showed that children demonstrated well-established preventive behaviours, a uniformity of practices across school and home settings, and substantial knowledge regarding the importance of hand hygiene. These findings are consistent with the international literature, reinforcing a global trend toward increased awareness of the importance of hand hygiene among children, particularly in the post-COVID-19 pandemic period. As reported in other studies, hand hygiene practices are performed at critical moments, such as after using the toilet [14–16] and before meals [14, 15, 17–19].

It is important to acknowledge that the consolidation and repetition of autonomous health-related behaviours in children largely depend on the continuity of supervised routines and structured educational interventions implemented within the school environment [11, 19–24]. A randomized study conducted in Kenya illustrates this process: prior to the intervention, none of the children washed their hands after using the toilet; following the implementation of behavioural strategies and the provision of appropriate materials, a marked increase in this practice was observed [25].

Conversely, children also identified barriers such as the lack of water and soap [14, 20, 26] and the absence of supervision or positive role models in school and family contexts [26]. It should also be noted that, although children’s right to a healthy environment with access to hygiene infrastructure is widely recognised [3], socioeconomic, educational, and structural factors continue to influence the adoption of good hygiene practices. While supervision plays an important role, particularly in the choice between soap and water or hand sanitiser [27], children in this study identified the use of soap and water as the most effective method of hand cleansing, in line with previous research [7, 11, 14, 27, 28].

These findings clearly indicate that the visible availability of soap and the presence of adequate school infrastructure, combined with interventions aimed at promoting healthy habits, are decisive factors for the incorporation of regular hand hygiene routines [3, 15, 17, 18, 27–31]. Access to handwashing facilities, soap and sanitiser dispensers, and appropriate hand-drying options, such as paper towels or hand dryers, is essential, as the availability of facilities and materials enables children to integrate hand hygiene more consistently into their daily routines [5,8,]. Other studies have also shown that children consider hand sanitiser important, particularly outside the home, as well as soap and water handwashing for at least 20 s, reflecting technical knowledge of the procedures [19].

The present study indicates that younger children recognise the importance of handwashing after playtime, with higher adherence observed among 9 years old, which may be related to school routines and levels of adult supervision at this stage of schooling, as found in other studies [16,17,26,32,]. In contrast, handwashing before entering the classroom appears to vary by age. The findings suggest that younger children are more likely to comply with this routine, whereas adherence becomes less consistent at older ages, potentially reflecting reduced direct supervision and increasing autonomy [13, 14, 16, 17, 26, 29, 32]. Girls also demonstrated higher adherence to handwashing after playtime, which may be associated with gender-specific behavioural and social patterns. Several studies report that girls show higher adherence to hand hygiene practices than boys, particularly in school settings [13, 18, 27, 33, 34].

It should be noted that previous studies have reported mixed findings regarding the role of age and gender in children’s hand hygiene practices [13, 18, 27, 33]. However, as reported in this study, hand hygiene practices were not uniform, suggesting that demographic factors alone do not fully explain children’s hand hygiene behaviours. In this sense, the children´s hand hygiene behaviour is multifactorial, shaped by institutional routines, levels of adult supervision, or the organisation of school time, reinforcing the notion that children’s hand hygiene behaviour is multifactorial [10, 34, 35].

Previous studies suggest that daily school activities represent increased exposure to pathogens and that play activities act as vectors for microbial exposure, making them a critical dimension in infection prevention among children, so structured educational interventions that promote consistent improvements in post-play hygiene behaviours are therefore essential [20, 24]. One potential strategy is to place visual cues, such as handwashing posters, stickers, and other materials, in strategic areas within schools [8].

Children in this study identified hand hygiene as essential for removing visible dirt from the hands; although this may reflect a perception associated with physical cleanliness, they also demonstrated an understanding that handwashing eliminates invisible microorganisms and prevents the transmission of pathogens, thereby reducing disease risk. This pattern is consistent with the literature [16, 19, 22, 36, 37]. This evidence indicates that children integrate fundamental concepts of health and self-protection and that early interventions contribute to the development of autonomy, self-care skills, and preventive behaviours with long-term impacts on adult life and public health. Recognising that hand hygiene interrupts chains of transmission is essential for effective public health behaviours [12], therefore, these findings support the relevance of maintaining educational interventions that are non-coercive, culturally adapted, participatory, and facilitative of internalising the salutogenic and preventive value of hand hygiene [20, 22, 28, 37, 38].

Children in this study demonstrated knowledge regarding hand contamination with organic matter (faeces or urine) after toilet use reflects a specific understanding of the relationship between biological residues and the risk of pathogen transmission to objects and food, as well as awareness that hand hygiene prevents cross-contamination and promotes food safety and environmental hygiene. Similar findings have been reported in other studies [13, 15, 18]. The recognition of bathrooms as environments conducive to microbial proliferation also reflects an understanding of high-risk settings, consistent with previous research [13, 15, 18, 22].

This study also revealed a homogeneous pattern of behaviours, with age and gender not playing a significantly differentiating role in children’s habits, except in the school context, particularly after playtime and before entering the classroom. Indeed, the literature indicates that variables such as age, gender, socioeconomic conditions, and education may influence behaviours [33], but this overall homogeneity may reflect the widespread internalisation of hand hygiene routines among children, likely reinforced by consistent messaging and practices across both school and family environments. Moreover, the absence of group differences may also be interpreted in the context of the post COVID-19 pandemic, during which intensified public health messaging and school-based preventive measures appear to have contributed to the normalisation of hand hygiene behaviours in daily routines.

The findings further reinforce the importance of infrastructure as a key influencing factor. Evidence indicates that adequate facilities are determinant, as educational campaigns alone have limited effectiveness in the absence of soap and appropriate handwashing points, particularly among children aged 7 to 11 years, a critical period for habit formation [28, 30, 31]. Therefore, when implementing intervention strategies, it is essential to ensure resources and public policies that guarantee physical access to adequate conditions, as the lack of such conditions may also contribute to school absenteeism and poorer educational performance [3]. Governments also have a role in exploring innovative, sustainable, and cost-effective approaches to support hand hygiene in schools, ensuring the long-term availability of appropriate conditions for all children [5].

The fact that children did not require reminders to wash their hands at critical moments indicates autonomy and integration of this practice into daily routines. Similarly, other studies suggest that children’s self-efficacy and autonomy can be enhanced through interactive strategies or early interventions in kindergarten settings [39, 40].

In this context, it is also important to highlight that the transfer of habits between school and home environments is not automatic, as children attribute a crucial role to parents as positive role models and behaviour reinforcers [13, 32, 34]. In the present study, although hand hygiene habits were similar at home and at school, the influence of parents’ educational role in reinforcing this practice was evident, aligning with studies that emphasise the impact of adults through positive reinforcement, visual and gestural instructions, and systematic supervision [13, 19, 28, 30, 34, 37]. In addition to parents, the presence of other professionals as role models and active reminders significantly increases children’s adherence to handwashing at key moments [14].

Accordingly, the implementation of mandatory daily routines within schools, combined with structured, systematic, supervised educational strategies involving teachers and non-teaching staff, appears decisive in producing significant effects on children’s behaviours and perceptions regarding hygiene habits [7,11,23,29,36,].

Based on the “Theory of Planned Behaviour” and the “Health Belief Model”, widely used theoretical frameworks for explaining health-related behaviours, the findings indicate that cognitive knowledge alone is not a sufficient determinant of children’s behaviour [35]. Instead, behavioural outcomes are primarily shaped by favourable attitudes, social modelling, the development of practical skills, and active social engagement [35, 38].

Therefore, an intersectoral approach involving interdisciplinary teams in promoting correct hand hygiene knowledge and practices among primary school children has been widely recognised across international contexts [19, 22]. In this regard, the involvement of nurses has enabled the implementation of technically sound interventions with tangible impacts on children’s personal and school routines, as nurses are well positioned to lead strategies that integrate pedagogical and behavioural components and generate critical knowledge for promoting child development in safer and healthier environments [1, 3, 6, 7, 21, 28, 37, 41]. In fact, the usefulness of health promotion is highlighted in several studies, and nurses’ role in leading the health promotion strategies adoption is also highlighted, thus making a decisive contribution in increasing the population’s health literacy and promoting the ability of informed health decisions [1].

While many questions remain about the most effective ways to promote and support hand hygiene, there is a clear need for innovative thinking, multisectoral collaboration, and partnerships with governments to establish hand hygiene as a widely adopted practice that protects all [5].

This study contributes to the scientific evidence on primary school children’s knowledge and habits regarding hand hygiene practices. Children in this study demonstrated consolidated health-promoting behaviours, autonomy, and a correct and conscious perception of regular hand hygiene, both at school and at home. It is noteworthy that the findings of this study potentially reflect a lasting contextual influence of the COVID-19 pandemic. In Portugal, the return to face-to-face classes in 2020 and 2021 was marked by stringent national health protocols. These high-visibility public health measures, combined with supervised handwashing routines, likely acted as a catalyst for internalizing hygiene literacy. Although the absence of pre-pandemic baseline data precludes a definitive causal claim, the persistence of these habits in the post-pandemic period suggests that the intensive socialization of hygiene during the crisis may have successfully embedded these practices into children’s daily lives as a “new normal”.

In this sense, it is essential to maintain and reinforce these transversal norms through collaborative interventions involving families and schools. Accordingly, given children’s developmental specificities and needs, further research is required to identify best practices for promoting knowledge and behaviours with a meaningful impact on public health, maximising growth and developmental potential from childhood into adulthood.

Limitations of the Study

A primary limitation of this study, is the relatively small sample size, which constrains the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of primary school children. Future research should incorporate larger, more diverse cohorts and a greater number of schools to enhance representativeness and bolster the robustness of the results. Furthermore, there is a clear need for multi-center validation across different geographical regions to determine whether these hand hygiene patterns remain consistent across varied educational settings. Additionally, incorporating parental perspectives would provide a more holistic understanding of children’s hand hygiene practices and the family-related factors that influence their maintenance. Furthermore, the lack of pre-pandemic baseline data prevents the establishment of a formal causal relationship between COVID-19 interventions and current behaviors. Nevertheless, it is plausible that these intensive public health efforts acted as a catalyst, integrating preventive habits into children’s daily routines. Addressing these aspects in future research may support the development of more effective school and family-based health promotion strategies led by nurses.

Implications to practice

Promoting healthy habits among children and families, particularly regarding hand hygiene practices, is essential for reducing the transmission of infections and consolidating preventive behaviours from an early age. Hand hygiene is not a technical act, but an educational and structured investment that contributes to the development of essential health competencies throughout childhood. Although parents have been identified as important role models, it is also essential that nurses assume their role as health educators within the community by implementing health-promoting initiatives, campaigns, and interventions grounded in scientific evidence, thereby contributing to safer and healthier environments for children.

The continuity of habits at home is fundamental for establishing consistent routines, and recognising the important role of parents, the recommendation of hand hygiene practices can be reinforced during nursing consultations. The identification of critical moments (such as entering the classroom after recess) may also serve as a basis for implementing interventions that use visual strategies to remind children to wash their hands.

Since nursing practice occurs across multiple settings, the results of this study may also serve as a guide for nurses to implement specific, structured, consistent, motivating, and culturally sensitive interventions in school settings and in partnership with parents and teachers. These interventions should aim to foster skill development and autonomy, and to promote children’s capacity for conscious decision-making, in order to achieve their fullest health potential.

Conclusion

The results of the present research revealed that children demonstrate consistent hand hygiene habits, evidencing a good level of knowledge and perception regarding the importance of this practice, particularly at critical moments such as after using the bathroom, before meals, and after playtime.

Schools are recognized as key settings for health promotion, where supervised routines combined with adequate infrastructure are essential for fostering adherence to hand hygiene practices. The heightened hygiene awareness observed in this study may reflect a lasting contextual influence of the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, the family´s educational role constitutes the primary model and determinant in the consolidation of these behaviours. Therefore, the continuity and reinforcement of multicomponent interventions are recommended. These should be grounded in a partnership between families, schools, and health professionals, combining technical, pedagogical, and behavioural components to ensure the sustainable development of preventive behaviours with an impact on present and future public health.
