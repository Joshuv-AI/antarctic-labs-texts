Learning thermodynamic master equations for open quantum systems
Peter Sentz, Stanley Nicholson, Yujin Cho, Sohail Reddy, Brendan Keith, Stefanie Günther

Abstract
The characterization of Hamiltonians and other components of open quantum dynamical systems plays a crucial role in quantum computing and other applications. Scientific machine learning techniques have been applied to this problem in a variety of ways, including by modeling with deep neural networks. However, the majority of mathematical models describing open quantum systems are linear, and the natural nonlinearities in learnable models have not been incorporated using physical principles. We present a data-driven model for open quantum systems that includes learnable, thermodynamically consistent terms. The trained model is interpretable, as it directly estimates the system Hamiltonian and linear components of coupling to the environment. We validate the model on synthetic two and three-level data, as well as experimental two-level data collected from a quantum device at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory.



1
Introduction

Characterizing quantum dynamical processes is central to the advancement of quantum computing devices, with significant impacts on quantum control, compilation, and error mitigation
[
1
]
.
Accurate dynamical models are essential to address two challenges: 1) optimized quantum compilation, enabling optimal quantum gate sequences and resource allocation, and 2) quantum error mitigation and correction to achieve overall improved system performance
[
2
]
.
A precise model of the underlying quantum dynamics requires accurate knowledge of system parameters such as qubit frequencies, coupling strengths, and noise channels.
Extracting such information from measurement data is inherently challenging, yet recent advances in data-driven methods incorporating machine learning (ML) have led to significant progress in this area. A comprehensive overview of ML for quantum systems can be found in
[
3
]
, featuring recent developments for the tasks of learning quantum states, quantum dynamics, and quantum measurements.

Hamiltonian dynamics can be reconstructed using observed time series of a complete set of measurements
[
4
]
, and quantum process tomography allows for the identification of linear non-unitary dynamics governed by the Lindblad master equations
[
5
]
. However, the number of measurements required to fully characterize the dynamics of a quantum system grows exponentially with the number of levels. Therefore, a variety of approaches have been developed that pursue estimates of the unknown parameters of the Hamiltonian and Lindblad operators using limited measurements
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Nevertheless, a growing array of approaches incorporate deep neural networks in the characterization process, introducing nonlinear transformations of the quantum state into the dynamical model
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
.

For instance, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) have been employed to predict measurement outcomes under dissipative dynamics
[
15
]
, and to reconstruct quantum dynamics using trajectory data from superconducting qubits under microwave control sequences
[
16
]
. These methods, which leverage machine learning to approximate quantum states and dynamical processes, often fall under the category of “black-box” approaches, where the learned model lacks explicit physical interpretability, hence requiring post-processing of the learned agent.

On the other hand, “gray-box” approaches combine data-driven learning models with physically understandable dynamical structures, incorporating known physics directly into the machine learning agent to improve accuracy and interpretability.
For example, Youssry et al.
[
17
]
combine the output of a black-box machine learning agent with numerically evolved quantum dynamical processes to address uncertainties in the Hamiltonian model and capture noise characteristics.

More intrusively, machine learning models based on physics-informed neural ordinary differential equations
[
18
]
, also termed universal differential equations (UDEs)
[
19
]
, use trajectory data to train a correction to an underlying approximate physical model described by a differential equation. Such learning approaches often require smaller training data sets to achieve accurate model fidelity and are more resilient to noisy training data
[
20
,
21
]
. The UDE approach was recently applied to the problem of Hamiltonian learning in
[
22
]
, where a nonlinear correction to the Hamiltonian dynamics is learned from trajectory data.

An application of UDEs to learning open quantum systems is proposed in
[
23
]
, where a parameterized augmentation of the Lindblad equation was trained to identify latent dynamics in a single qubit superconducting quantum processing unit using microwave-driven control pulses. There, a structure-preserving ansatz to learn corrections to approximate Hamiltonian and Lindblad decoherence operators is compared to a nonlinear augmentation using a nonlinear feed-forward neural network. While the former guarantees completely-positive trace-preserving (CPTP) maps
[
5
]
, the latter has demonstrated higher model accuracy after training, indicating the need for nonlinear augmentations to the linear Lindblad equation. However, ensuring that a learned nonlinear dynamical model yields physically consistent dynamics when evaluated outside of the training data remains a crucial challenge.

Approaches that incorporate physical laws as inductive biases
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
, have demonstrated improvements in both accuracy and generalization. Thus, incorporating nonlinearity into quantum master equations in a physically consistent manner has the potential for more robust learned models. In particular, a recent family of ML techniques that embed the laws of thermodynamics into the learnable model has been successfully applied to dissipative dynamical systems
[
28
]
. Among these methods, the so-called metriplectic
[
29
]
neural networks are based on a unifying formalism for reversible and irreversible dynamics known as the generic equation for non-equilibrium reversible-irreversible coupling (GENERIC)
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
. Thermodynamics-informed neural networks (TINNs) parametrize the GENERIC structure of dynamics as neural networks, and weakly enforce the necessary symmetry and degeneracy conditions through penalization terms in the loss function
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
. In contrast, GENERIC formalism-informed neural networks (GFINNs) employ architectures that directly enforce the properties of GENERIC for every configuration of the neural network parameters
[
36
]
. Other works strongly enforce the necessary conditions by learning structure-preserving brackets parametrized by learnable tensors
[
37
,
38
]
. The recent port-metriplectic approach
[
39
]
decomposes dissipative systems into local subsystems, reducing the computational complexity of training the model. In this work, we extend the use of ML enforcing thermodynamic consistency to the context of open quantum dynamical systems.

1.1
Contributions and highlights

We present a thermodynamically consistent, data-driven model for learning dynamics of open quantum systems. In particular, the model incorporates nonlinear terms based on physical principles via the GENERIC formalism, in contrast to methods that augment linear master equations with nonlinear transformations in an ad hoc manner. This yields dynamical equations that guarantee valid quantum states for two-level systems. The learned model is interpretable, directly providing an estimate of the Hamiltonian and the operators that govern the coupling to an external environment. We describe this model within the density operator formalism and detail its construction using the density operator’s representation in the Pauli or Gell–Mann basis.

Next, we test the accuracy and generalizability of the model on two (qubit) and three (qutrit) level synthetic quantum systems. Finally, we validate its efficiency on noisy, two-level experimental data taken from Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory’s (LLNL) QuDIT device.

1.2
Outline

We continue by discussing the shortcomings of the linear Lindblad master equation in terms of thermodynamical consistency and introduce an alternative nonlinear master equation (Section
2
). We then reformulate the master equation in terms of generalized Bloch vector dynamics in Section
3
, and show in Section
4
how this model can be augmented or approximated by neural networks, addressing key features such as Hamiltonian characterization, environmental coupling operators, and nonlinear interactions. Our numerical results in Section
5
demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach on synthetic and experimental data. Conclusions and future work are discussed in Section
6
.

2
Nonlinear thermodynamic master equation

The dynamics of open quantum systems are conveniently expressed in terms of the density matrix
\rho=\rho^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, which describes the probability distribution of a statistical ensemble of quantum states
[
40
]
. One of the most widely used models of these dynamics is the Lindblad master equation
[
41
]
, which derives from the Markov process generated by a quantum dynamical semigroup.
Specifically, the Lindblad equation for an
N
-dimensional quantum system describes the evolution of
\rho=\rho(t)
in terms of a Hermitian, time-dependent Hamiltonian
H(t)
and a set of Lindblad operators
\{L_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
,

\dot{\rho}=-\frac{\im}{\hbar}[H,\rho]+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\gamma_{k}\left(L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},\rho\right\}\right).

(1)

Here,
\hbar
denotes the reduced Planck constant, while
[A,B]\equiv AB-BA
and
\{A,B\}\equiv AB+BA
denote the commutator and anti-commutator, respectively.
The term
-\im[H,\rho]/\hbar
in (
1
) describes the unitary evolution of a closed quantum system, equivalent to the Liouville-von Neumann equation, while the summation that follows captures the dissipative effects of the environment on the quantum system.
In the most general setting, there are
d=N^{2}-1
linearly independent terms in the summation, incorporating a separate relaxation rate
\gamma_{k}
for each so-called Lindblad operator
L_{k}

[
40
]
.

Despite being a popular model, the failure of (
1
) to describe thermodynamically-consistent dynamics was first shown in
[
42
]
.
Indeed, all
linear
dissipation models violate the important fluctuation-dissipation theorem
[
43
,
44
]
.

An alternative,
nonlinear
dissipative thermodynamic master equation for open quantum systems was proposed and studied in
[
45
,
46
]
.
When an isothermal heat bath is used to represent the surrounding environment, this master equation takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\rho}=-\frac{\im}{\hbar}[H,\rho]&-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\Big[Q_{k},[Q_{k},\rho]\Big]\\
&-\frac{1}{k_{B}T_{e}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\Big[Q_{k},\mathcal{C}_{\rho}[Q_{k},H]\Big]\,.\end{split}

(2)

In this case, the dynamics of
\rho
are governed by the Hamiltonian
H
and a set of coupling operators
Q_{k}=Q_{k}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
. The temperature of the heat bath is denoted by
T_{e}
, which we assume to be constant (isothermal). Finally, the super-operator
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
, known as the
canonical correlation operator

[
46
]
, is defined as

\mathcal{C}_{\rho}A\equiv\int_{0}^{1}\rho^{s}A\rho^{1-s}\dif s\,.

(3)

The master equation (
2
) generalizes to arbitrary thermodynamic environments; see
[
45
,
47
]
for details.

As shown in
[
42
,
45
]
, the nonlinear master equation in (
2
) is consistent with the fluctuation-dissipation theorem and thus admits thermodynamically admissible dynamics. This is in contrast to the linear Lindblad evolution equation (
1
). In fact,
[
46
]
demonstrates how the nonlinear master equation can be represented in a general framework for nonequilibrium thermodynamics, which we describe next.

2.1
Thermodynamic consistency

The GENERIC formalism (General Equation for the Non-Equilibrium Reversible-Irreversible Coupling)
[
32
,
30
,
31
]
is a framework describing the time evolution of nonequilibrium thermodynamic systems as a sum of reversible and irreversible contributions. Modeling a particular system within the context of GENERIC requires the identification of appropriate energy and entropy functionals, along with (state-dependent) linear operators acting on these functionals.
In
[
46
]
, it is shown that (
2
) is consistent with the GENERIC formalism, which we now describe.

The GENERIC formulation of a dynamical system described by variables
\boldsymbol{x}
takes the form

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathscr{L}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathscr{E}+\mathscr{M}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathscr{S}\,,

(4)

where
\mathscr{E}
and
\mathscr{S}
denote the energy and entropy of the system, respectively,
\mathscr{L}
is a skew-symmetric matrix, and
\mathscr{M}
is a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix. The
\mathscr{L}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathscr{E}
term represents reversible (non-dissipative) dynamics and
\mathscr{M}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathscr{S}
represents irreversible (dissipative) dynamics. The non-interaction between these two dynamics is encoded in the following compatibility conditions:

\mathscr{L}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathscr{S}=\mathscr{M}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathscr{E}=\mathbf{0}\,,

(5)

which ensures conservation of energy under irreversible dynamics and that entropy is left unchanged by reversible processes
[
32
]
.

When considering the quantum master equation (
2
) with an isothermal heat bath, the variables of the problem consist of

\boldsymbol{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\[1.5pt]
H_{e}\end{bmatrix},

(6)

where
H_{e}
is the internal energy of the surrounding bath. The corresponding energy and entropy functionals are

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{E}&=\textrm{Tr}(\rho H)+H_{e},\\
\mathscr{S}&=-k_{B}\textrm{Tr}(\rho\log(\rho))+\frac{1}{T_{e}}H_{e}.\end{split}

(7)

The gradients of these functionals are given by

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathscr{E}=\begin{bmatrix}H\\[1.5pt]
1\end{bmatrix},\quad\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathscr{S}=\begin{bmatrix}-k_{B}\log(\rho)\\[1.5pt]
\frac{1}{T_{e}}\end{bmatrix},

(8)

and the GENERIC equations (
4
) take the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\rho}\\[1.5pt]
\dot{H_{e}}\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}\mathscr{L}_{11}&0\\[1.5pt]
0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}H\\[1.5pt]
1\end{bmatrix}\\[2.0pt]
&+\begin{bmatrix}\mathscr{M}_{11}&\mathscr{M}_{12}\\[2.0pt]
\mathscr{M}_{12}^{T}&\mathscr{M}_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-k_{B}\log(\rho)\\[1.5pt]
\frac{1}{T_{e}}\end{bmatrix},\end{split}

(9)

where
\mathscr{L}_{11}=\mathscr{L}_{11}(\rho)
and
\mathscr{M}_{ij}=\mathscr{M}_{ij}(\rho,Q_{k},H)
depend on the density matrix, the coupling operators, and the Hamiltonian. In (
9
), the reversible term captures the unitary dynamics, i.e.,
\mathscr{L}_{11}H=-\mathrm{i}[H,\rho]
, while the irreversible portion accounts for the additional terms in (
2
). In the isothermal case, the energy of the environment
H_{e}
does not directly contribute to the dynamics of the quantum system. However, for more general thermodynamic environments, the external degrees of freedom will directly affect the quantum system dynamics and must be accounted for explicitly.

2.2
Neural network models via GFINNs

Recent dynamics-learning approaches approximate GENERIC systems (
4
) while enforcing the key properties which ensure thermodynamic consistency
[
39
,
38
,
36
]
. In particular, the GFINNs approach taken in
[
36
]
parametrizes each of the building blocks
\mathscr{L}
,
\mathscr{M}
,
\mathscr{E}
, and
\mathscr{S}
separately by feedforward neural networks. Because of this separation, prior knowledge about any of the components can be inserted directly into the governing dynamical system, reducing the complexity of the learned model.

It is natural to approach learning the unknown terms of (
9
) by replacing
\mathscr{M}
by a neural network while using the known forms of the other components. However, the entropy gradient in (
9
) is numerically problematic when dealing with quantum systems in a pure state. While the quantum entropy
-k_{B}\textrm{Tr}(\rho\log(\rho))
is well-defined for both pure and mixed states, its gradient features a singularity via the term
-k_{B}\log(\rho)
.

The
\log(\rho)
singularity does not appear in (
2
); indeed, the right-hand side is well-defined and smooth for all valid quantum states. This result relies on the so-called “miracle relation”
[
48
]
, which establishes the following relationship between the canonical correlation operator
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
and
\log(\rho)
,

\mathcal{C}_{\rho}[A,\log(\rho)]=[A,\rho]\,,

(10)

for any linear operator
A
. The GENERIC analog of this relation amounts to the fact that the singularity is canceled out when applying
\mathscr{M}_{11}
to
-k_{B}\log(\rho)
in (
9
).

For this reason, we avoid the separate treatment of
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{M}(\boldsymbol{x})
, and instead develop an appropriate architecture for learning the unknown terms in (
2
) directly. We choose to parametrize the density matrix
\rho
through its Bloch representation, representing the density matrix as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In the next section, we review this parametrization and describe how it is used to represent (
2
) as a system of real-valued ordinary differential equations. Section
4
then describes how the unknown terms are approximated by learnable parameters.

3
Bloch-vector Dynamics of the Nonlinear Thermodynamics Master Equation

The density matrix of an
N
-level quantum system can be written in the Bloch-vector representation
[
49
]
as

\rho=\frac{1}{N}I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}v_{j}\sigma_{j}\equiv\frac{1}{N}I+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(11)

where
d=N^{2}-1
, the matrices
\sigma_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
are Hermitian matrices forming a basis for Liouville space, and the real vector
\boldsymbol{v}
with entries
(\boldsymbol{v})_{j}=v_{j}=\textrm{Tr}(\sigma_{j}\rho)
is the
Bloch
vector of
\rho
.
Similarly, any self-adjoint
N\times N
operator
A
can be written as

A=\frac{a_{0}}{N}I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}\sigma_{j}\equiv\frac{a_{0}}{N}I+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}

(12)

where the coefficients
a_{j}
are given by

a_{0}=\textrm{Tr}(A)
and
a_{j}=\textrm{Tr}(\sigma_{j}A)
.
The basis matrices
\sigma_{j}
are the generators of the special unitary group
\textrm{SU}(N)
and satisfy the following property,

\textrm{Tr}(\sigma_{j})=0\,,\quad\textrm{Tr}(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j})=2\delta_{ij}\,.

(13)

While there are a number of ways to explicitly define a basis satisfying (
13
), we here consider the generalized Gell–Mann matrices defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ket{j}\bra{k}+\ket{k}\bra{j},&\\
-\im\ket{j}\bra{k}+\im\ket{k}\bra{j},&\\
\left(\frac{2}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\ket{j}\bra{j}-\ell\ket{\ell}\bra{\ell}\right),&\end{split}

(14)

for
0\leq j<k\leq N-1
and
1\leq\ell\leq N-1
. In fact, when
N=2
, these matrices coincide with the two-level Pauli matrices, and coincide with the Gell–Mann matrices when
N=3
.

Using the Bloch-vector representation, the equation of motion for the density matrix in (
2
) can be written as a differential equation of the Bloch components
as follows.
Given the generalized Gell–Mann matrices
\sigma_{j}
, we define the structure constants

\displaystyle\begin{split}f_{ijk}&=-\frac{\im}{4}\textrm{Tr}\left(\sigma_{i}[\sigma_{j},\sigma_{k}]\right),\\
g_{ijk}&=-\frac{\im}{4}\textrm{Tr}\left(\sigma_{i}\{\sigma_{j},\sigma_{k}\}\right).\end{split}

(15)

The constants
f_{ijk}
define an anti-symmetric tensor, and the
g_{ijk}
define a totally symmetric tensor. Note that for
N=2
, the entries
f_{ijk}
coincide with the Levi–Civita symbol, and
g_{ijk}\equiv 0
.
For
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the matrix-valued functions
L(\boldsymbol{a})
and
G(\boldsymbol{a})
with entries

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left[L(\boldsymbol{a})\right]_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}f_{ijk}\,,\\
\left[G(\boldsymbol{a})\right]_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}g_{ijk}\,.\end{split}

(16)

The symmetry properties of the structure constants are inherited by these operators, i.e.,
L(\boldsymbol{a})
is skew-symmetric and
G(\boldsymbol{a})
is symmetric for every
\boldsymbol{a}
.
The matrix
L(\cdot)
is closely related to the commutator of two operators. In particular, if
A
and
B
are Hermitian operators with Bloch vector representations
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
, respectively, then

\textrm{Tr}\left(\im[A,B]\sigma_{j}\right)=\left(L(\boldsymbol{a})\boldsymbol{b}\right)_{j}=-\left(L(\boldsymbol{b})\boldsymbol{a}\right)_{j}.

(17)

The matrix
G(\cdot)
is related to the anti-commutator of two Hermitian operators as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textrm{Tr}\left(\{A,B\}\sigma_{j}\right)=\ &\frac{2}{N}\left(\textrm{Tr}(A)(\boldsymbol{b})_{j}+\textrm{Tr}(B)(\boldsymbol{a})_{j}\right)\\
&+\left(G(\boldsymbol{a})\boldsymbol{b}\right)_{j}.\end{split}

(18)

In addition, the equality
G(\boldsymbol{b})\boldsymbol{a}=G(\boldsymbol{a})\boldsymbol{b}
holds, and if
A
and
B
are traceless, in which case (
18
) reduces to

\textrm{Tr}\left(\{A,B\}\sigma_{j}\right)=\left(G(\boldsymbol{a})\boldsymbol{b}\right)_{j}=\left(G(\boldsymbol{b})\boldsymbol{a}\right)_{j}.

(19)

Using these matrices, we show an explicit representation of the nonlinear thermodynamic master equation (
2
) for the Bloch vector
\boldsymbol{v}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{h}
and
\boldsymbol{q}_{k}
denote the Bloch vector representation of the Hamiltonian
H
and the coupling operators
Q_{k}
, respectively. The Bloch vector representation of the first two terms on the right-hand side of (
2
) is given by

\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}\left(-\frac{\im}{\hbar}[H,\rho]\sigma_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar}\left(L(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{h}\right)_{j},

(20)

\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}\left(-[Q_{k},[Q_{k},\rho]]\sigma_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(L^{2}(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{j},

(21)

by equation (
17
).
The nonlinear term in (
2
) is handled by decomposing the super-operator in (
3
) as:

\mathcal{C}_{\rho}A=\frac{1}{2}\{A,\rho\}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{C}_{\rho}^{\prime}A

(22)

where

\mathcal{C}_{\rho}^{\prime}A\equiv-\int_{0}^{1}[\rho^{s},[\rho^{1-s},A]]\dif s

(23)

has a double commutator structure.
The Bloch representation of the nonlinear term then has a similar decomposition:

\textrm{Tr}\left(-[Q_{k},\mathcal{C}_{\rho}[Q_{k},H]]\sigma_{j}\right)=\left(C(\boldsymbol{v})+D(\boldsymbol{v})\right)_{j}.

(24)

The first term,
C(\boldsymbol{v})
, is affine in the Bloch-vector
\boldsymbol{v}
:

C(\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{1}{N}L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{1}{2}L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})G(\boldsymbol{v})L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})\boldsymbol{h},

(25)

while the nonlinear contribution takes the form

D(\boldsymbol{v})=-\frac{1}{2}L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})M(\boldsymbol{v})L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})\boldsymbol{h}.

(26)

The matrix
M(\boldsymbol{v})
is nonlinear in the Bloch vector
\boldsymbol{v}
, and its action on a Bloch vector
\boldsymbol{a}
for a hermitian operator
A
corresponds to (
40
). While an explicit expression for
M(\boldsymbol{v})
is not available for arbitrary
N
, we describe the properties of
M(\boldsymbol{v})
in the next section, including its approximation by the neural network.

Altogether, the nonlinear thermodynamic master equation (
2
) is equivalent to the following differential equation for the Bloch vector
\boldsymbol{v}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}=\frac{1}{\hbar}L(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{h}+\sum_{k}L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})\boldsymbol{v}\\
&+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{k}L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})\left(\frac{2}{N}I+G(\boldsymbol{v})-M(\boldsymbol{v})\right)L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})\boldsymbol{h}\end{split}

(27)

with
\beta=(k_{B}T_{e})^{-1}
.

We briefly discuss the correspondence between (
27
) and the GENERIC equations given in (
9
). The gradients of the energy and entropy functionals in (
8
) are:

\nabla\mathscr{E}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\\[2.5pt]
1\end{bmatrix},\quad\nabla\mathscr{S}=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{k_{B}}{2}\boldsymbol{w}\\[2.5pt]
\frac{1}{T_{e}}\end{bmatrix}

(28)

where
\boldsymbol{w}
is the Bloch-representation of the matrix
\log(\rho)
.
The blocks in the skew-symmetric matrix
\mathscr{L}
and the symmetric positive semi-definite matrix
\mathscr{M}
in (
9
) correspond to

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{11}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\hbar}L(\boldsymbol{v})

(29)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{M}_{11}(\boldsymbol{v})&=\\
-\frac{1}{k_{B}}&\sum_{k}L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})\left(\frac{2}{N}I+G(\boldsymbol{v})-M(\boldsymbol{v})\right)L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})\end{split}

(30)

\displaystyle\mathscr{M}_{12}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\mathscr{M}_{21}^{T}(\boldsymbol{v})=-\frac{1}{2}\mathscr{M}_{11}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{h}

(31)

\displaystyle\mathscr{M}_{22}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\boldsymbol{h}^{T}\mathscr{M}_{11}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{h}

(32)

Thus, the reversible dynamics in (
27
) correspond to the term
\dfrac{1}{\hbar}L(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{h}
.
The positive semi-definiteness of
\mathscr{M}
corresponds to the fact that it is the Hessian of the
dual dissipation potential
; see
[
46
]
for details.

As mentioned previously, pure quantum states lead to a singularity in the entropy gradient, i.e., in the vector
\boldsymbol{w}
. The “miracle relation” (equation (
10
)), which removes this singularity, corresponds to the fact that

-\frac{1}{2}\mathscr{M}_{11}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{w}=L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})\boldsymbol{v},

(33)

which is why no singular terms appear in (
27
).

4
Thermodynamically Consistent Learning of the Nonlinear Master Equation

We describe how each unknown component of (
27
) can be approximated in a learning framework. Below, we denote by
\theta
the collection of learnable parameters in the model.

4.1
Hamiltonian

The self-adjoint Hamiltonian
H
is determined by its Bloch representation
\boldsymbol{h}
and its trace
h_{0}=\textrm{Tr}(H)
. Because of the double commutator structure of (
2
), however, the value of
h_{0}
is immaterial when considering the dynamics of
\rho
. This is also apparent from the differential equation for the Bloch vector (
27
), where
h_{0}
plays no role. Thus, if the Hamiltonian is unknown, it can be learned only up to a translation by a multiple of the identity.
To approximate
H
, we learn a Bloch representation
\boldsymbol{h}_{\theta}\approx\boldsymbol{h}
where each component of
\boldsymbol{h}_{\theta}
is a learnable parameter.

4.2
Coupling Operators

Similarly to the Hamiltonian, the trace of each coupling operator
Q_{k}
in (
2
) does not affect the dynamical evolution of
\rho
. Hence, the operators
Q_{k}
are only determined up to translation by a multiple of the identity operator.
Let
\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}
denote the Bloch vector representation of a traceless coupling operator
Q_{k}
.
The sum of double commutators between
\rho
and the coupling operators can then be expressed as

\sum_{k}\left[Q_{k},[Q_{k},\rho]\right]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\Gamma_{ij}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{i},\left[\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{j},\rho\right]\right],

(34)

where
\Gamma
is a semi-definite matrix defined by

\Gamma_{ij}\equiv\sum_{k}x_{i}^{(k)}x_{j}^{(k)}.

(35)

A similar expressions holds for the term in (
2
) with the nonlinearity in
\rho
:

\sum_{k}\left[Q_{k},\mathcal{C}_{\rho}[Q_{k},H]\right]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\Gamma_{ij}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{i},\mathcal{C}_{\rho}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{j},H\right]\right].

(36)

Since the Bloch representation of
\dfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{j}
is the canonical basis vector
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
, then we can express the terms of (
27
) using the entries of
\Gamma
and the operators
L_{i}\equiv L(\boldsymbol{e}_{i})
. Specifically, letting
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
B(\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{k}L^{2}(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})\boldsymbol{w}&=\sum_{i,j}\Gamma_{ij}L_{i}L_{j}\boldsymbol{w},\\
\sum_{k}L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})B(\boldsymbol{v})L(\boldsymbol{q}_{k})\boldsymbol{w}&=\sum_{i,j}\Gamma_{ij}L_{i}B(\boldsymbol{v})L_{j}\boldsymbol{w}.\end{split}

(37)

Equation (
27
) is therefore equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}=\frac{1}{\hbar}L(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{h}+\sum_{i,j}\Gamma_{ij}L_{i}L_{j}\boldsymbol{v}\ +\\
&\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{i,j}\Gamma_{ij}L_{i}\left(\frac{2}{N}I+G(\boldsymbol{v})-M(\boldsymbol{v})\right)L_{j}\boldsymbol{h}.\end{split}

(38)

We are led to the conclusion that learning the coupling operators
Q_{k}
is equivalent to learning an approximation
\Gamma_{\theta}\approx\Gamma
. To enforce semi-definiteness of
\Gamma_{\theta}
, we parametrize
\Gamma_{\theta}
through its Cholesky factorization,
\Gamma_{\theta}=\widehat{X}_{\theta}\widehat{X}_{\theta}^{T}
where the entries of the lower-triangular factor
\widehat{X}_{\theta}
are learnable parameters. If desired, coupling operators leading to equivalent dynamics can be obtained from the entries
x_{j}^{(k)}\equiv(\widehat{X}_{\theta})_{jk}
by the expression

Q_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N^{2}-1}x_{j}^{(k)}\sigma_{j}.

(39)

We also note that in addition to the entries of
\Gamma
, the temperature of the environment
T_{e}
can also be treated as a learnable parameter.

4.3
Nonlinearity in
\rho

The final piece to reconstructing (
27
), or (
38
), is the approximation of a nonlinear matrix
M(\boldsymbol{v})
whose action on a Bloch vector
\boldsymbol{a}
of a Hermitian operator
A
corresponds to

\mathcal{C}_{\rho}^{\prime}A\equiv-\int_{0}^{1}[\rho^{s},[\rho^{1-s},A]]\dif s.

(40)

In Appendix A, we derive three necessary conditions on
M(\boldsymbol{v})

for which (
40
) is equivalent to the operation
\boldsymbol{a}\mapsto-M(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{a}
. Specifically, we show that the matrix
M(\boldsymbol{v})
should:

1.

be symmetric positive semi-definite;

2.

satisfy the null-space condition
\textrm{null}\left[L(\boldsymbol{v})\right]\subseteq\textrm{null}\left[M(\boldsymbol{v})\right]
; and

3.

collapse to the operator
L^{T}(\boldsymbol{v})L(\boldsymbol{v})
whenever
\boldsymbol{v}
represents a pure quantum state,
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}=2(N-1)/N
.

To approximate
M(\boldsymbol{v})
and to enforce these three conditions, we take the following approach. We decompose the matrix
M(\boldsymbol{v})
as follows:

M(\boldsymbol{v})=L^{T}(\boldsymbol{v})R^{T}(\boldsymbol{v})R(\boldsymbol{v})L(\boldsymbol{v}),

(41)

where
R(\boldsymbol{v})
is the upper triangular factor of a Cholesky factorization, and
L(\boldsymbol{v})
is the skew-symmetric matrix defined in (
16
). The Cholesky factorization ensures positive semi-definiteness, while the multiplication of
L(\boldsymbol{v})
on the right ensures that the null-space condition is satisfied
[
50
]
.

To enforce the third property, construct the matrix
R(\boldsymbol{v})
as a convex combination of the identity matrix and a triangular matrix
\widetilde{R}(\boldsymbol{v})
,

R(\boldsymbol{v})=\widetilde{r}^{2}I+(1-\widetilde{r}^{2})\widetilde{R}(\boldsymbol{v})

(42)

where

\widetilde{r}^{2}\equiv\frac{N\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}}{2(N-1)},

(43)

so that pure states are characterized by
\widetilde{r}^{2}=1
. We construct the triangular matrix
\widetilde{R}(\boldsymbol{v})
by reshaping the output of a feedforward neural network

\textrm{NN}_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{\frac{d(d+1)}{2}}

(44)

into the diagonals of an upper triangular matrix.

4.4
Learning

The right-hand side of (
27
) is replaced a parametrized family of functions,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{F}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w})\equiv\frac{1}{\hbar}L(\boldsymbol{w})\boldsymbol{h}_{\theta}+\sum_{i,j}(\Gamma_{\theta})_{ij}L_{i}L_{j}\boldsymbol{w}\\
&+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{i,j}(\Gamma_{\theta})_{ij}L_{i}\left(\frac{2}{N}I+G(\boldsymbol{w})-M_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{w})\right)L_{j}\boldsymbol{h}_{\theta},\end{split}

(45)

with the corresponding parameterized solution

\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\theta}=\mathcal{F}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\theta})
.
Optimal parameters are found by minimizing the squared
\ell_{2}
-norm between the predicted and ground-truth Bloch vectors over a set of discrete time values and initial conditions:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\min_{\theta}\quad\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\\
&\frac{1}{N_{\textrm{ic}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\textrm{ic}}}\frac{1}{N_{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{T}}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{\theta}(t_{i};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j})-\boldsymbol{v}(t_{i};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j})\|_{2}^{2},\\
\end{split}

(46)

subject to the constraints

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}&\boldsymbol{v}_{\theta}(t;\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j})=\mathcal{F}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\theta}(t;\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j})),\\
&\boldsymbol{v}_{\theta}(0;\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j})=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}.\end{split}

(47)

Here,
\boldsymbol{v}(t_{i};\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
is the value of the exact trajectory at time
t_{i}
starting from the initial condition
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}
, and
\boldsymbol{v}_{\theta}(t_{i};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j})
is the prediction of the model. In Section
5.2
we average over several trajectories beginning at the same initial condition (the ground state), but driven by control Hamiltonians with varying pulse strength.

The true Bloch vectors can be estimated experimentally through quantum state tomography
[
51
]
at selected time steps
t_{j}
. For our numerical results, we use the complete Bloch vector to define loss functions as in (
46
). However, when the number of levels
N
is large, the exponential data requirement to reconstruct the entire density matrix may be prohibitive. Instead, one could define a loss function involving the expectation value of observables
\langle O_{i}\rangle=\textrm{Tr}(\rho O_{i})
which defines a function of
\boldsymbol{v}
.

The interpretability of the model may be used to further refine the model, e.g., the learned components with small magnitude can be fixed to zero, and additional training used to refine the approximation of the remaining components.

6
Summary and Conclusion

In this work, we developed a data-driven framework for characterizing the dynamics of open quantum systems that yield thermodynamically consistent predictions. Our approach employs the GENERIC framework for constructing a structure-preserving, thermodynamically consistent parameterization of the nonlinear master equation governing quantum state evolution. By embedding fundamental physical constraints within the model ansatz, the method ensures physically valid predictions of the evolution of quantum states. We demonstrate our approach on synthetic test problems for characterizing a single qubit as well as a qutrit system, and perform training on experimental data taken from a quantum processing unit at LLNL’s QuDIT. We demonstrate that, for sufficiently long training time intervals, our method preserves positive semi-definiteness of the density matrices to high accuracy and trace of the density matrices to machine precision. Furthermore, the approach was able to accurately predict the dynamics of the quantum system beyond the training window.

The results highlight the capability of the method to learn and generalize quantum dynamical models with high fidelity, offering a robust tool for studying dissipative quantum processes and non-equilibrium dynamics. Furthermore, the learned model can serve as a digital twin of the quantum system, providing a computationally efficient and accurate surrogate that can be leveraged for optimizing control pulses and validating noise mitigation techniques.

denote the Bloch representation of the self-adjoint matrix
\rho^{x}
. From the connection between commutators and the matrix
L(\cdot)
(see equation (
17
)), we have

[\rho^{s},[\rho^{1-s},A]]\ \longleftrightarrow\ \widetilde{M}(s)\boldsymbol{a},

(62)

where

\widetilde{M}(s)\equiv L^{T}(\boldsymbol{v}^{s})L(\boldsymbol{v}^{1-s}).

(63)

Since
\rho^{s}
and
\rho^{1-s}
commute, it follows that

[\rho^{s},[\rho^{1-s},A]]=[\rho^{1-s},[\rho^{s},A]],

(64)

from which the symmetry of
\widetilde{M}(s)
follows immediately.

Note that if
A
commutes with
\rho
, then
[\rho^{s},[\rho^{1-s},A]]
for any
s\in(0,1)
. Thus,

[\rho,A]=0\implies\widetilde{M}(s)\boldsymbol{a}=\mathbf{0}.

(65)

From the relationship between commutators and
L(\cdot)
in (
17
), it follows that

\textrm{null}\left[L(\boldsymbol{v})\right]\subseteq\textrm{null}\left[\widetilde{M}(s)\right].

(66)

Furthermore, we show in Appendix B that
\widetilde{M}(s)
is positive semi-definite.

From these properties, it follows that the matrix

M(\boldsymbol{v})\equiv\int_{0}^{1}L^{T}(\boldsymbol{v}^{s})L(\boldsymbol{v}^{1-s})\dif s,

(67)

must be symmetric positive semi-definite, and also satisfy the null-space condition
\textrm{null}\left[L(\boldsymbol{v})\right]\subseteq\textrm{null}\left[M(\boldsymbol{v})\right]
. In addition to these two properties, the super-operator
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}^{\prime}
takes a particularly simple form when the density matrix corresponds to a pure quantum state, i.e.,

\rho=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi},

(68)

is a rank-one projection onto the span of the quantum state
\ket{\psi}
. In this case,
\rho^{x}=\rho
for any positive
x
, so that (
40
) reduces to

\mathcal{C}_{\rho}^{\prime}A=-\left[\rho,[\rho,A]\right].

(69)

In terms of the Bloch representation, it follows that when
\rho
is a pure state, the matrix
M(\boldsymbol{v})
in (
67
) takes the form

M(\boldsymbol{v})=L^{T}(\boldsymbol{v})L(\boldsymbol{v})=-L^{2}(\boldsymbol{v}).

(70)

For
N
-level systems, the set of admissible quantum states is a subset of the hypersphere defined by

r^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\leq\frac{2(N-1)}{N},

(71)

with equality corresponding to pure quantum states.

Appendix B: Positive semi-definiteness of
\widetilde{M}(s)

We prove that the matrix
\widetilde{M}(s)
defined in (
63
) is positive semi-definite. Let
A
and
B
be two operators with Bloch representations
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
respectively. Assuming at least one of the operators has trace zero, it follows from (
12
) and (
13
) that

\textrm{Tr}(AB)=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b},

(72)

where
\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}
is the inner product between
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
.

Fix a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and define the matrix

X\equiv\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}x_{k}\sigma_{k},

(73)

which has trace zero. Then from (
72
) and (
62
), we have:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textrm{Tr}(X[\rho^{s},[\rho^{1-s},X]])&=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{T}L^{T}(s)L(1-s)\boldsymbol{x}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\widetilde{M}(s)\boldsymbol{x}.\end{split}

(74)

If we use the eigenvalue decomposition of
\rho
(with an orthonormal eigenbasis):

\rho=\sum_{j=1}^{N}r_{j}\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\boldsymbol{w}_{j}^{\dagger},

(75)

then

[\rho^{1-s},X]=\sum_{j}r_{j}^{1-s}(\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\boldsymbol{w}_{j}^{\dagger}X-X\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\boldsymbol{w}_{j}^{\dagger}),

(76)

and the double commutator satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}[\rho^{s},[\rho^{1-s},X]]&=\sum_{i}r_{i}(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\dagger}X+X\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\dagger})\\
-\sum_{ij}&r_{i}^{s}r_{j}^{1-s}((\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\dagger}X\boldsymbol{w}_{j})\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\boldsymbol{w}_{j}^{\dagger}+(\boldsymbol{w}_{j}^{\dagger}X\boldsymbol{w}_{i})\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\dagger}).\end{split}

(77)

The trace can be computed using the basis vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
:

\textrm{Tr}(X[\rho^{s},[\rho^{1-s},X]])=\sum_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\dagger}X[\rho^{s},[\rho^{1-s},X]]\boldsymbol{w}_{k}.

(78)

For fixed
k
we have:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\dagger}X[\rho^{s},[\rho^{1-s},X]]\boldsymbol{w}_{k}&=r_{k}\|X\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\\
+\sum_{i}|\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\dagger}X\boldsymbol{w}_{k}|^{2}&(r_{i}-r_{i}^{s}r_{k}^{1-s}-r_{i}^{1-s}r_{k}^{s}).\end{split}

(79)

For
0\leq s\leq 1
and any
a,b\geq 0
, we have the standard inequality

a^{s}b^{1-s}\leq sa+(1-s)b,

(80)

from which it follows that

r_{i}^{s}r_{k}^{1-s}+r_{i}^{1-s}r_{k}^{s}\leq r_{i}+r_{k},

(81)

since
r_{i}\geq 0
.

Applying (
81
) to (
79
), we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\dagger}X[\rho^{s},[\rho^{1-s},X]]\boldsymbol{w}_{k}&\geq r_{k}\|X\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}-r_{k}\sum_{i}|\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\dagger}X\boldsymbol{w}_{k}|^{2}\\
&=r_{k}\left(\|X\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}-\sum_{i}|\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\dagger}X\boldsymbol{w}_{k}|^{2}\right)\\
&=0,\end{split}

(82)

Summing over
k
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\widetilde{M}(s)\boldsymbol{x}=\textrm{Tr}(X[\rho^{s},[\rho^{1-s},X]])\geq 0

(83)

showing that
\widetilde{M}(s)
is positive semi-definite.

Appendix C: Analytic expression for Nonlinearity
C^{\prime}_{\rho}A

We present here an analytic expression to evaluate the nonlinearity
C^{\prime}_{\rho}A
. Consider the spectral decomposition of the density matrix

\rho=\sum_{i}^{N}\lambda_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|=\sum_{i}^{N}\lambda_{i}\Psi_{i},

(84)

with
\Psi_{i}=|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|
and
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=1
. Then the nonlinear term becomes

\displaystyle C^{\prime}_{\rho}A

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\left[\rho^{s},\left[\rho^{1-s},A\right]\right]ds

(85)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda^{s}_{i}\lambda^{1-s}_{j}\left[\Psi_{i},\left[\Psi_{j},A\right]\right]ds

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\Psi_{i},\left[\Psi_{j},A\right]\right]\int_{0}^{1}\lambda^{s}_{i}\lambda^{1-s}_{j}ds,

where the logarithmic mean is defined as

\Lambda_{ij}=\int_{0}^{1}\lambda^{s}_{i}\lambda^{1-s}_{j}ds=\dfrac{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}{\ln{\lambda_{i}}-\ln{\lambda_{j}}},

(86)

with

\lim_{\lambda_{j}\rightarrow\lambda_{i}}\dfrac{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}{\ln{\lambda_{i}}-\ln{\lambda_{j}}}=\lambda_{i}.

(87)

Then, letting
\varphi_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{a}
be the Bloch representations of
\Psi_{i}
and
A
, respectively, the nonlinear term reads

\displaystyle C^{\prime}_{\rho}A

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Lambda_{ij}L^{T}(\varphi_{i})L(\varphi_{j})\boldsymbol{a}

(88)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Lambda_{ij}L(\varphi_{i})L(\varphi_{j})\boldsymbol{a}.

We utilize this expression for generating synthetic data trajectories for the nonlinear thermodynamic master equation in Section
5.2
.

Appendix D: Experimental Setup and Data Acquisition

In this work, we used a single-qubit Tantalum-based device mounted at a base temperature of 10 mK. This device is a 2D planar superconducting transmon with Al-AlOx Josephson junction on sapphire substrate
[
56
]
. The qubit parameters are estimated with standard characterization protocols, yielding
\omega_{01}=3.422\,\mathrm{GHz}
.

We used a standard pulse sequence for Rabi measurement to obtain the trajectories used in Section
5.3
. We fixed the amplitudes of
p(t)
and
q(t)
and varied the pulse lengths to
20\,\mu\mathrm{s}
. The measured data was classified into
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle
states using a Gaussian Mixture Model using
mixture
in
sklearn
Python package. The measured populations on
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle
oscillate between 0 and 1 as we apply longer pulses, representing that the Bloch vector rotates around the Bloch sphere vertically. The amplitude ratio between
p(t)
and
q(t)
determines the azimuthal angle offset from
x
-axis on the Bloch sphere. Finally, we applied the inverse of a confusion matrix to the measured populations to mitigate state preparation and measurement (SPAM) error. The confusion matrix was given by

\begin{pmatrix}0.988875&0.010875&0.00025\\
0.054875&0.936375&0.00875\\
0.016750&0.042625&0.94063\end{pmatrix}.

(89)

To obtain the three Bloch vector components, we applied projection operators before measuring the population. For example, to measure it on Pauli
y
-basis,
\sigma_{1}
, we applied
\mathrm{RX}(\pi/2)
gate. A single qubit density matrix can be written as

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1+v_{3}&v_{1}-v_{2}i\\
v_{1}+v_{2}i&1-v_{3}\end{pmatrix},

(90)

where
v_{j},\penalty\ j=1,2,3
is the Bloch vector component in Pauli bases,
\sigma_{j}
. When we apply
\mathrm{RX}(\pi/2)
gate, the density matrix becomes
\rho^{\prime}=\mathrm{RX}(\pi/2)\rho\,\mathrm{RX}^{\dagger}(\pi/2)
, resulting in the ground state population
P_{y}(|0\rangle)=\frac{1}{2}(1+v_{2})
. Thus,
v_{2}=2P_{y}(|0\rangle)-1
. Similarly,
v_{1}=2P_{x}(|0\rangle)-1
can be obtained by applying
\mathrm{Ry}(\pi/2)
gate. In superconducting qubits, the default is
z
-basis measurement and
v_{3}=2P_{z}(|0\rangle)-1
.
