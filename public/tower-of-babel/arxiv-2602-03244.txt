A third law of thermodynamics is an unnecessary complexity Preprint: This is a preprint posted in Zenodo under doi: 10.5281/zenodo.18454229
José-María Martín-Olalla

Abstract
This paper elaborates on the implications of the relationship between the Second and Third Laws and provides a comprehensive formal and historical justification for the logical redundancy of the Nernst heat theorem. By revisiting the Nernst-Einstein debate, the underlying hypotheses that lead to the traditional view of the Third Law as an independent postulate are examined. It is argued that the historical rejection of Nernst’s proof—motivated by Einstein’s insistence on the practical non-performability of cycles at absolute zero—overlooks the fact that a universal Second Law already precludes such cycles, rendering an independent Third Law an unnecessary complexity. Ultimately, the Nernst theorem is shown to be an essential consistency regulator rather than an independent physical discovery. This version is also published in Zenodo doi: 10.5281/zenodo.18454229 . The following analysis considers only thermodynamic arguments, excluding any microscopic description in relation to the limit

Why is a third law of thermodynamics NOT needed?

Landsberg 1956
contends that the crux of the controversy lies in the status of boundary points (equilibrium states at
T=0
) relative to an open set of points (regular equilibrium states at non-zero temperature). He notes: “In mathematics the status of such points is often the subject of a separate assumption. Nernst assumed that, in the absence of information to the contrary, the points could be regarded as belonging to the set. Einstein suggested that they should not belong to the set. From the axiomatic point of view, however, both views represent an additional postulate.”

While this interpretation holds in certain abstract mathematical contexts, the physical challenge is to validate the consequences of the framework originating from the Second Law and determine if these consequences align with empirical evidence—particularly in the limit as
T\to 0
. This section demonstrates the various ways in which the Second Law implicitly predicts the Nernst heat theorem, and the various ways in which the framework originated by Einstein’s rebuttal fails to address foundational issues.

3.1
The continuity of Planck’s statement of the Second Law at the boundary

A consistent physical theory requires that the consequences of the Second Law do not merely apply to the interior of the state space, but remain continuous and well-behaved as the boundary of the state space is approached. This section is devoted to show that this idea derives Nernst’s theorem from Planck’s statement

Planck’s statement formalizes the early observation by
Carnot 1824
that motive power is derived from the transport of “caloric” from a hot to a cold body. Indeed, the most direct consequence of this statement is that raising a weight via a cyclic process necessitates two distinct heat exchanges: (i) with a boiler, which is cooled (mandated by energy conservation), and (ii) with a cooler, which must be heated (mandated by Planck’s statement). This constitutes the fundamental Carnot engine.

Planck’s statement (
\mathrm{P}
) can be represented by three material implications:

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle(W>0\Rightarrow Q_{c}<0),

(1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle(W\leq 0\Leftarrow Q_{c}=0),

(2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle(W<0\Leftarrow Q_{c}>0),

(3)

where
Q_{c}
is the heat exchanged with the cooler and
W
is the work performed. Implicitly, the first law of thermodynamics (energy conservation) requires:

Q_{b}+Q_{c}=W,

(4)

where
Q_{b}
is the heat exchanged with the boiler. Relation (
1
) formalizes the statement itself: if a weight is raised, a third effect (the heating of a cooler) must occur. Relation (
2
) describes the dissipation of work into heat, while Relation (
3
) characterizes refrigeration: if a cooler is cooled, an equivalent amount of work must be performed (a weight must be lowered).

The statement is also the starting point of a framework that aligns with common experience. It formally introduces the concept of irreversibility and formally identifies heat as a form of energy that, unlike work, is subject to this specific restrictions, circumventing alternative complex characterizations of heat.
(
Beretta and Gyftopoulos 2015b
)

Reversible engines are established as the theoretical limit of efficiency (Carnot’s theorem). Therefore, reversible exchanges are governed by the properties of nature and not by experimentalist’s will or capacity. Hereafter, quantities exchanged in a reversible process are denoted by sans-serif letters, such as
\mathsf{W}
or
\mathsf{Q}
.

Reversible heat exchanges can be linked to the thermophysical properties of the working substance and the reservoirs. First, Carnot’s temperature
T
is defined as:

\frac{T_{c}}{T_{b}}:=-\frac{\mathsf{Q}_{c}}{\mathsf{Q}_{b}},

(5)

where energy conservation (
4
) makes
-\mathsf{Q}_{c}<\mathsf{Q}_{b}
and, therefore,
T_{c}<T_{b}
. Second, Clausius’ entropy
S
is defined by:

dS:=\frac{\mathsf{Q}}{T}.

(6)

Combining these with energy conservation (
4
), work in a Carnot engine can be expressed as:

\mathsf{W}=(T_{b}-T_{c})\times\Delta S,

(7)

where
\Delta S=\mathsf{Q}_{b}/T_{b}=-\mathsf{Q}_{c}/T_{c}
represents the finite entropy flux from the boiler to the cooler.

For reversible engines, relation (
2
) becomes more stringent: the Planck statement mandates that if a reversible engine rejects no heat into the cooler, the engine must produce no net work:

\mathrm{P}\Longrightarrow(\mathsf{W}=0\Leftarrow\mathsf{Q}_{c}=0).

(8)

This relation must hold not only as a point assigment, but also as a limit:

\mathrm{P}\Longrightarrow(\mathsf{W}\to 0^{+}\Leftarrow\mathsf{Q}_{c}\to 0^{-}),

(9)

because reversible exchanges are linked to the thermophysical properties of the working fluid and the reservoirs, see (
5
) and (
7
). In a consistent physical theory, these properties must be continuous and well-behaved as the boundary of the state space is approached; consequently the behaviour of
\mathsf{Q}_{c}
must be governed by the same continuity.

Relation (
9
) is the Nernst’s theorem illustrated in Figure
1

\mathsf{Q}_{c}
linked to
T_{c}
as per (
5

\mathsf{W}
linked to
\Delta S
, as per (
7

T_{c}\to 0^{+}\Rightarrow\mathsf{Q}_{c}\to 0^{-}\Rightarrow\mathsf{W}\to 0^{+}\Rightarrow\Delta S\to 0^{+}.

(10)

In the first implication, the case
\mathsf{Q}_{b}\to\infty
is excluded by the principle of energy conservation.
(
Heidrich 2016
)
This proof of consistency follows directly from Planck’s statement without the need to hypothesize the negation of the theorem to seek a contradiction, as was required in Nernst’s original proof.

Relation (
9
) provides a conceptual link between Planck’s statement and Nernst’s theorem which can be understood as follows: the Second Law requires that a non-zero heat
\mathsf{Q}_{c}
is rejected if work is produced. In a logical context, the non-vanishing character of
\mathsf{Q}_{c}
is determined by the laws of nature, not the experimentalist. In Figure

In Figure

\mathsf{Q}_{c}
arbitrarily close to zero while maintaining finite work by exploiting specific fluid properties. However, this creates a fundamental continuity problem: if a reversible Carnot engine is strictly forbidden at the limit
T=0

T=0
and the working substance cannot carry entropy down to this limit, a consistent physical theory requires that the substance’s capacity to carry entropy must also vanish as that limit is approached. To suggest otherwise is to treat Planck’s statement not as a robust principle of nature, but as a mathematical point-discontinuity that the experimentalist can circumvent asymptotically.

This line of thought may be summarized as: if an engine provides some finite

to a cooler. This requirement is implicit in Planck’s statement and, arguably, does not require a separate formalization
(

3.2
The ontological consistency of the Second Law

Notwithstanding the derivation in Section
3.1
, this section shows an alternative line of reasoning that directly links Planck’s statement to the Nernst heat theorem.

In the classical rebuttal of
Nernst 1912
’s proof, much is made of the “point of logic” connecting Nernst’s contradiction to the hypothetical experiments that would be feasible if the behavior in Figure
1
B were possible.
(
Epstein 1937
;
Boas 1960
)
However, these remarks may ultimately be irrelevant to the core theoretical discussion.

Planck’s statement and the principle of energy conservation constitute the axioms of a framework that yields both the concept and metric of temperature (
5
), and the concept and metric of entropy (
6
), see section
3.1
. This framework is built upon the postulate that Planck’s statement formalizes a criterion of evolution for discriminating whether a process is possible. This criterion is typically expressed via the law of entropy increase and is universally accepted as robust at non-zero temperatures.

At
T=0
, Einstein’s rebuttal effectively maintains that this criterion is no longer operative; he suggests that Planck’s statement is not the arbiter for the
ABCDA
cycle, but rather that practical, extrinsic limitations render the process impossible. While the Third Law, as an independent postulate, restores this criterion at the boundary, one must ask: is this the most parsimonious way to describe the laws of nature?

It is crucial to note that Planck’s statement did not introduce a previously unknown property—it was already well understood that heat cannot be fully converted into work, just as it was known that heat does not flow spontaneously from cold to hot. Instead,
Planck 1897
elevated a known physical observation to a first principle from which all subsequent consequences are derived. As long as these consequences do not contradict empirical evidence, the statement prevails.

A commitment to Planck’s statement as a universal criterion for discriminating whether a process is possible yields the Nernst theorem—for instance, through Nernst’s original proof by contradiction—which aligns perfectly with experimental evidence.

Ultimately, Planck’s statement is sufficient to explain why substances of the kind depicted in panel B are not observed in nature. Is it relevant that an alternative line of thought may provide a different explanation on how experimentalists would handle Carnot’s engines in the neighborhood of absolute zero, given that a definitive experimental test to distinguish between these two viewpoints remains fundamentally unavailable?

3.3
Ideal adiabatic processes

Carathéodory 1909
reformulated thermodynamics by introducing the concepts of adiabatic connectivity and accessibility, relating the former to the First Law and the latter to the Second Law. He defined a
simple system
and noted: “Quasi-static, adiabatic changes of state of a simple system are ‘reversible’.” Regarding non-simple systems,
Carathéodory 1909
added: “Our theory cannot be readily applied to such systems, which is likely also the case for classical thermodynamics.” Indeed, this limitation is inherent to the classical framework, as reversible Carnot cycles necessitate two adiabatic processes that must themselves be reversible.

Temperature is conceptually alien to the reversibility of adiabatic state changes. To perform such a process, an experimentalist requires only a perfectly adiabatic enclosure, a controlled mechanical variable (such as pressure
p
), and a record of the system’s response via the conjugate mechanical parameter (such as volume
V
). Reversibility is demonstrated when the conjugate parameter is restored to its initial value after the controlled variable is slowly varied and subsequently returned to its starting point. Because the system is isolated, no thermometer is required. Consequently, the characterization of quasi-static adiabatic processes as reversible is,
prima facie
, a valid assumption across the entire temperature domain.

Figure
1
B describes a non-simple substance in this context. If Einstein’s viewpoint is adopted, the process
B\to C\to E
is quasi-static and adiabatic, yet inherently non-reversible: at point
E
, if
x
is quasi-statically and adiabatically restored to
x_{1}
, the original equilibrium state
B
would not be recovered.

Accepting this view requires the introduction of a restrictive auxiliary assumption: that a quasi-static, adiabatic process is reversible
only if
T>0
. This is theoretically problematic because adiabatic reversibility can be defined prior to the concept of temperature—that is, before thermal contact between two systems is even established. Under Einstein’s logic, one must introduce this restriction only to later formalize a third, independent law whose sole purpose is to extend the assumption of reversibility down to
T=0
.

Instead, following the logic of
Carathéodory 1909
, we may conclude that simple systems must, by definition, obey the Nernst theorem. Therefore, the persistent observation that finite-density, homogeneous substances abide by the theorem serves as empirical evidence that they can be treated as simple systems down to
T=0
.

3.4
Ideal isotherms at
T=0

Much of the early 20th-century discourse regarding the boundary of accessible states centered on the characterization of an ideal (reversible) isothermal process at
T=0

Nernst 1924

In a sense, both perspectives are simultaneously valid and flawed. The crux of the issue is that Figure

Beyond these historical “mind games,” ideal isotherms are formally required to establish the metrics of both temperature (
5

\lim_{T\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathsf{Q}}{T}\in\mathbb{R}.

(11)

Conceptually, this limit must also account for the entropy variation associated with the reversible exchange of a null heat at absolute zero. Consequently, this ratio can only be zero; any non-zero value would lead to a negation of Planck’s statement. Paradoxically, denying the existence of reversible isotherms at

at the cooler’s temperature, measured by the ratio
\mathsf{Q}_{c}/\Delta S
. As
\Delta S\to 0

(
Liboff 1994

In contrast, applying this to Figure

3.5
Equations of state and the boundary of accessible states

This section reformulates the criticism by
Nernst 1924
regarding the process
B\to C\to E
in Figure

S(T,x)=cT+b(x),

(12)

where

The fact that
T=0
constitutes a boundary—below which no states exist—is extrinsic to the equation of state itself, therefore extrinsic to the substance under study. This boundary arises from a global constraint: the requirement that
T
remains non-negative, as established by Planck’s statement of the Second Law via (

3.2
, Einstein’s perspective presents a striking inconsistency: the postulate is invoked to define the boundary at

In contrast, the equation of state corresponding to Figure

(13)

where

Equation (
13

as an accumulation point in the
T

S

x

at

x

T=0

It is worth noting that accumulation points of this nature can theoretically occur at any temperature. Unlike specific heats or susceptibilities, Planck’s statement prescribes no specific constraints on cross-effects, such as the isothermal change of entropy. The profound significance of the Nernst theorem lies in its status as a universal property observed in all known homogeneous, finite-density substances. The universal validity of this property at
T=0
implies that no auxiliary substance can facilitate the cooling of a system to absolute zero via thermal contact.

4
Conclusion

Planck’s statement arose from observations at non-zero temperature, yet the statement is valid irrespective of temperature, as wisely noted by
Planck 1897
.
‡
‡

‡

“The temperature of the reservoirs does not enter into the question. If such a machine [the one that negates the statement] is possible with a reservoir at 1000 celsius, it is also possible with a reservoir at 0 celsius.”
While that remark was not explicitly intended to address the limit
T=0
, the interpretation of Nernst’s empirical observations follows this logic: they provide the purest evidence that the Second Law holds at any temperature, including the boundary
T=0
. It can hardly be otherwise if one acknowledges that temperature is not an independent background variable, but a consequence of the statement, and the nature of irreversibility.

In his autobiographical notes,
Einstein 1979
famously remarked that “a theory is the more impressive the greater the simplicity of its premises, the more different kinds of things it relates, and the more extended its area of applicability.” It is an irony that Einstein wrote these words as a specific tribute to the universal content of thermodynamics. Yet, in his historical rejection of Nernst’s proof, he advocated for a more restricted, less universal Second Law—one that required a “patch” in the form of a Third Law to maintain consistency at the boundary. By maintaining the universality of Planck’s statement irrespective of temperature, we do not merely add a Third Law; we fulfill Einstein’s own desire for a theory of universal applicability. This extension reduces the complexity of the premises by removing the need for a separate, independent postulate to govern the boundary.

The history of science often conflates the order of discovery with the order of logical necessity. One could easily imagine a counter-factual history in which a theorist, working strictly from the axiomatic frameworks of classical thermodynamics, predicted the vanishing of specific heats and the convergence of entropy as a requirement for the consistency of the Second Law. In such a scenario, Nernst’s empirical observations would have been hailed as the brilliant experimental confirmation of an existing theoretical prediction, rather than the discovery of an independent law that only introduces an unnecessary complexity.

In conclusion, the Third Law is not a separate pillar of thermodynamics, but the necessary condition that preserves the integrity of the Second Law at its limit. Acknowledging this redundancy does not diminish Nernst’s heat theorem; rather, it elevates the Second Law to the truly
supreme position

(
Eddington 1915
)
that Einstein himself admired.
