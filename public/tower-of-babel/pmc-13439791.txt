Effect of restrictive fluid balance in critically ill patients with acute kidney injury: a target trial emulation
Authors: Kyle Christopher White, Ary Serpa Neto, Kevin B Laupland, Michelle Gatton, Mahesh Ramanan, Anis Chaba, Stephen Whebell, Alexis Tabah, Kiran Shekar, James McCullough, Aashish Kumar, Peter Garrett, Antony Attokaran, Stephen Luke, Sebastiaan Blank
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo estimate the effect of restrictive fluid management on renal function and mortality in critically ill patients with acute kidney injury (AKI) at high risk of developing positive fluid balance.MethodsWe conducted a target trial emulation using data from 12 intensive care units in Queensland, Australia (2015–2021). Adults with AKI within 72 h of intensive care unit (ICU) admission and an AKI-Fluid Balance Risk Score ≥ 32 were included. The intervention comprised conservative crystalloid administration, restricted nutritional fluid intake, and diuretic use for 72 h. We estimated per-protocol effects using a sequential doubly robust estimator for longitudinal modified treatment policies. The primary outcome was AKI Rank at day 7; secondary outcomes included 30-day mortality.ResultsAmong 8,685 patients, the intervention was associated with substantial reduction in fluid balance (− 2,304 mL at 72 h; 95% CI, − 2,465 to − 2,144) and modest improvement in AKI Rank (mean difference − 0.8; 95% confidence interval (CI), − 1.3 to − 0.3). Heterogeneity by AKI stage was observed: the intervention was associated with improved renal function in stage 1 AKI (mean difference − 3.1; 95% CI, − 3.8 to − 2.5) but worse outcomes in stage 2 AKI (mean difference 4.5; 95% CI, 3.8 to 5.1) and stage 3 AKI (mean difference 6.3; 95% CI, 4.7 to 7.9) with increased 30-day mortality (relative risk 1.20; 95% CI, 1.09 to 1.33). Effective sample sizes were limited (7–16%), indicating reliance on extrapolation.ConclusionsRestrictive fluid management was associated with improved renal function in early AKI but potentially harmful effects in stages 2 and 3 AKI. These hypothesis-generating findings suggest any future trial should incorporate safety criteria for patients with advanced renal injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-06094-7.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, Fluid balance, Critical illness, Target trial emulation, Fluid therapy

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a prevalent condition among critically ill patients and has been linked to substantial morbidity and mortality rates [1]. Fluid management is a key component of AKI management [2] and a positive fluid balance (FB) has been consistently associated with worse outcomes in critically ill patients with AKI [3]. Consistent with these notions, fluid therapy has been identified as a research priority in critically ill patients with AKI [4].

Despite such potential importance, a recent scoping review of fluid management of critically ill adult patients with AKI found limited evidence in terms of nature, design, and extent [5]. Only the REVERSE-AKI pilot trial demonstrated the feasibility of restrictive fluid therapy in 100 critically ill adults with AKI, with a statistically significant difference in FB at 72 h [6]. However, in this study, diuretic therapy was used sparingly, and there was minimal difference in fluid input between the groups. The methods for achieving a less positive FB and their impact on patient outcomes remain uncertain.

More recent research has elucidated how patients diagnosed with AKI develop a positive FB, demonstrating that continued crystalloid administration, increased nutritional intake, limited urine output, and minimal use of diuretics all contributed to positive FB [3]. Importantly, positive FB may contribute to renal venous congestion, creating a cycle where AKI begets fluid accumulation, which in turn exacerbates renal dysfunction [7, 8]. Furthermore, the AKI FB Risk Score has been developed to identify, at the time of AKI diagnosis, patients who have a high probability of developing a strongly positive FB within 72 h [9]. Given that not all patients with AKI develop a positive FB, this tool is essential and will facilitate the identification of patients who may benefit from a restrictive fluid protocol after AKI diagnosis.

Accordingly, we conducted a target trial emulation (TTE) using a large, multicentre cohort to estimate the effect of restrictive fluid protocols on mortality in critically ill patients with AKI. We aimed to test the primary hypothesis that a neutral FB during the first 72 h after AKI diagnosis would be associated with improved renal function, as determined by AKI Rank, a measure of AKI severity, within 7 days. Our secondary hypothesis was that a neutral FB would not be associated with changes in mortality at 30 days.

Methods

Study design and sites

Fluid management TTE in critically ill patients with AKI utilising routinely collected electronic medical record clinical data. Study sites included 12 intensive care units (ICUs) in Queensland, Australia, comprising five tertiary, three outer metropolitan, and four regional centres. The study sites accounted for most of the state’s ICU capacity. Study dates were January, 2015 to December, 2021.

Participants

All adult patients (aged 18 years or older) with a diagnosis of AKI and an AKI FB Risk Score of 32 or greater on the day of diagnosis, indicating a high risk of positive FB, [9] were considered for inclusion. The AKI FB Risk Score ranges from 0 to 71, with a higher score representing increased risk (Table S1). Patients were excluded if they were admitted to the ICU more than 72 h prior to meeting inclusion criteria, required renal replacement therapy (RRT) on the day of inclusion, received chronic haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis, required extra-corporeal membrane oxygenation at any time during their admission, were pregnant, or were transferred from another ICU.

Intervention

TTE assigned patients to treatment groups based on eligibility criteria within the first 72 h of ICU admission. In the primary analysis, the intervention was a multi-component strategy in which all three of the following elements were applied concurrently: 1) Conservative crystalloid fluid administration, 2) Conservative nutritional fluid intake, and 3) Administration of diuretics. The intervention was administered for 72 h after AKI diagnosis and adjusted every 12-h time block, aligned to clinical shift times (0700–1900 and 1900–0700) (Fig. 1).

Restrictive fluid management intervention protocol

Conservative crystalloid fluid administration was dependent on rate: if the rate was > 80 mL/h, it was reduced to 25% of the previous rate, or 100 mL/h, whichever was lower; if the rate was 25–80 mL/h, it was reduced to 25 mL/h; if the rate was < 25mL/h it was unchanged. For the conservative nutritional fluid intake, all patients had a maximum allowable rate of 20 mL/h. Administration of diuretics was dependent on FB: if the FB was positive in the preceding time block, the patient was not on RRT, and the Vasoactive Inotrope Score (VIS) was less than 20, then diuretics were administered.

Data definitions

AKI was defined using the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria [10] and utilising previously published methods applied to daily serum creatinine and hourly urine output data. A rolling assessment of urine output per kilogram of body weight was conducted from admission. Every serum creatinine measurement was compared to the estimated baseline creatinine, calculated using a previously validated approach [11, 12].

Data sources

Data was collected from Australia-based ICUs using the eCritical MetaVisionTM (iMDsoft, Boston, MA, USA) clinical information systems and the Australia and New Zealand Intensive Care Society (ANZICS) CORE Adult Patient Database (APD) [13, 14]. Primary and secondary diagnoses from the International Classification of Diseases 10 Australia Modification (ICD-10-AM) codes, as well as mortality data, were collected from the Queensland Health Admitted Patient Data Collection (QHAPDC) and Queensland Births, Deaths, and Marriages Registry, respectively. Admission diagnoses were grouped into categories to optimise data accuracy and interpretability (Supplementary Methods, Table S2). The Charlson-defined comorbidities and index were calculated from ICD-10 codes (Supplementary Methods, Table S3) [15, 16]. The conversion of vasopressor dosage to norepinephrine equivalent and calculation of VIS was carried out per protocol (Supplementary Methods, Table S4-S5) [17]. Sepsis and septic shock were defined according to SEPSIS-3 guidelines and operationalised using previously published methods [18–20].

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was AKI Rank at 7 days after AKI diagnosis. AKI Rank is calculated as the cumulative difference between the AKI stage at each time-block and the baseline AKI stage (defined as the maximum AKI stage during the enrolment block, prior to treatment) across 14 time-blocks over 7 days. For patients remaining in ICU, the maximum AKI stage during each time-block is used. For patients discharged alive, AKI stage is set to 0 from discharge onwards; for patients who died, AKI stage is set to 3 from the time of death. The measure ranges from − 42 to + 42, with more negative values indicating greater improvement in renal function.

Secondary outcomes were 30-day ICU mortality, 30-day mortality, days alive and free of ICU at 30 days, and maximal AKI stage at day 7. The mechanistic outcomes of this study were maximal FB at 72 h after AKI diagnosis and maximal urea at day 7. Outcome definitions are provided in Supplementary Methods Table S6.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the association between the multi-facet intervention and outcomes, we conducted a TTE using a sequential double robust estimator (SDR) based on the efficient influence function (EIF), as described by Diaz et al., for longitudinal modified treatment policies (Supplementary Methods Table S7) [21].

In brief, the SDR is a multi-step procedure. First, we estimated time-varying policy density ratios, which compare the probability of each patient’s observed treatment history under the intervention strategy with the probability under usual care. Second, we performed a series of backward (“Q-”) regressions. At each recursive step, the predicted outcome was updated using an EIF-based transformation involving the cumulative product of the per-time density ratio, yielding a sequentially double-robust estimator (consistent if, at each time, either the treatment or the outcome models are correctly specified). Density ratios were globally trimmed to 0.99 for most outcomes and to 0.998 for ICU mortality to ensure equivalent weights in the setting of an increased horizon.

For ICU mortality, we estimated the full 30-day cumulative incidence function, treating ICU discharge as a competing event. For all other outcomes, 4 regressions were fit at each time point to model the transition process (ICU outcome), alongside the terminal regression used in the recursive mechanism. The different components enter the recursion through a probabilistic mixture to account for ICU discharge timing and discharge outcome (alive vs dead) when estimating the counterfactual outcomes. The last time point modelled in ICU for these outcomes is day 7 (time point 14). The terminal time point was chosen because the intervention was only active during the first 3 days, our primary outcome is at day 7 and the rapid decline in ICU population over time.

All regressions were fitted using an ensemble machine-learning algorithm (Super Learner) with an inner fivefold cross-validation, embedded within an outer fivefold cross-fitted sample split (nested cross-validation scheme). Folds were defined at the hospital level so that all patients from a given site were assigned to the same fold. Combined with EIF-based variance estimation aggregated by site, this gives cluster-robust estimates and confidence intervals under within-site correlation. All predictions were bounded at 10⁻4. Detailed information on the Super Learner stacks used can be found in the supplement (Supplementary Table S9-S10).

Models were adjusted for baseline covariates, including comorbidities, admission diagnosis, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) III score, admission circumstances, and cumulative FB at the time of AKI diagnosis. Time-varying covariates included mechanical ventilation status, renal replacement therapy, urea, fluid output, daily FB, VIS, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score. Time-varying covariates and treatments were lagged by two time-blocks (approximately 24 h) to ensure appropriate temporal ordering, such that adjustment was performed using covariate values measured prior to each treatment decision. A complete list of covariates and their distributional assumptions is provided in Supplementary Table S8.

Any patient with missing data in the baseline variables was excluded. Missing data in the time-varying variables occurred only for urea per time period; these were imputed downward, followed by upward filling from other measurements in the same patient at different time periods (Supplementary Figure S1).

The analysis was performed using R (v4.5.2; R Core Team 2025) and packages lmtp (1.5.3), [21, 22] SuperLearner, [23, 24] hal9001 (0.4.6), [25] xgboost (3.1.1.1), [26] mgcv (1.9–1), [27] glmnet(4.1–8), [28] earth (5.3.4), [29] ggplot2 (4.0.0), [30] data.Table (1.17.8), [31] and forestploter (1.1.3).

Statement of ethics

The Metro South Hospital and Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/2022/QMS/82024) approved the study, with an individual waiver of consent granted. The study protocol was developed and finalised prior to data analysis but was not prospectively registered in a public repository.

Results

Participants

Within the seven-year study period, there were 89,116 admissions to the twelve study ICUs. Within this population, 8,685 patients had AKI within 72 h of ICU admission and an AKI-FB Risk Score ≥ 32, making them eligible for the target trial (Fig. 2).

Patient flow diagram

Their baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. The median age was 61 years (interquartile range (IQR) 47–71), and the median body mass index was 28 kg/m2 (IQR 26–33). The median Charlson co-morbidity index was 3 (IQR 1–5), and the most common admission diagnosis group was cardiovascular (2215; 26%), followed by gastrointestinal (1212; 14%) and trauma (1154; 13%). Most patients were admitted from the operating theatre (3813; 44%) or the emergency department (3409; 39%).

The median APACHE II score was 18 (IQR 13–24), and the APACHE III score was 60 (IQR 43–81), corresponding to a predicted risk of death of 22%. The median AKI-FB Risk Score at enrolment was 38 (IQR 33–42). At the time of AKI diagnosis, 7,620 (88%) patients were mechanically ventilated, and 5,486 (63%) required vasopressor support. The majority of patients had stage 1 AKI (6205; 71%), with 1772 (20%) having stage 2 and 708 (8%) having stage 3 AKI. The median maximum SOFA score was 7 (IQR 5–9), and the median peak lactate was 1.9 mmol/L (IQR 1.3–3.3).

Most patients were admitted to tertiary ICUs (6,104; 70%), with the remainder admitted to regional (1,586; 18%) and outer metropolitan (995; 11%) centres. Most patients (8,081; 93%) were admitted with full active treatment goals.

Primary outcome—AKI rank at day 7

As shown in Table 2 and Fig. 3, among the 8,685 patients analysed in the target trial emulation, the estimated AKI Rank at day 7 was − 9.9 (95% confidence intervals (CI), − 11.1 to − 8.7) under usual care and − 10.7 (95% CI, − 11.8 to − 9.6) under the restrictive fluid management intervention. The mean difference in AKI Rank was − 0.8 (95% CI, − 1.3 to − 0.3), indicating that the restrictive fluid intervention was associated with modestly greater improvement in renal function compared to usual care.

Estimated AKI rank trajectories over 7 days by treatment strategy

Subgroup analysis by AKI stage at enrolment revealed significant effect modification (Table 2). Among patients with stage 1 AKI (n = 6,205), the estimated AKI Rank at day 7 was − 7.3 (95% CI, − 8.0 to − 6.6) under usual care and − 10.1 (95% CI, − 11.2 to − 9.6) under the restrictive fluid intervention; mean difference − 3.1 (95% CI, − 3.8 to − 2.5), indicating greater improvement in renal function with the intervention. In contrast, among patients with stage 2 AKI (n = 1,772), the intervention was associated with worse renal outcomes: − 14.9 (95% CI, − 17.1 to − 12.7) under usual care versus − 10.5 (95% CI, − 12.4 to − 8.5) under the intervention; mean difference 4.5 (95% CI, 3.8 to 5.1). This pattern was more pronounced in stage 3 AKI (n = 708): − 19.8 (95% CI, − 22.3 to − 17.3) under usual care versus − 13.5 (95% CI, − 15.7 to − 11.3) under the intervention; mean difference 6.3 (95% CI, 4.7 to 7.9).

Secondary outcomes—mortality

The associations between mortality and the restrictive fluid management intervention is shown in Fig. 4 and Table 2. The estimated 30-day ICU mortality was 11.3% (95% CI, 8.6 to 14.1) under usual care and 11.9% (95% CI, 9.2 to 14.6) under the intervention; risk difference 0.6% (95% CI, − 0.2 to 1.3); relative risk 1.05 (95% CI, 0.98 to 1.12). The estimated 30-day all-cause mortality was 15.5% (95% CI, 10.0 to 21.1) under usual care and 16.5% (95% CI, 11.6 to 21.4) under the intervention; risk difference 1.0% (95% CI, 0.2 to 1.7); relative risk 1.06 (95% CI, 1.00 to 1.13).

Forest plot of primary and secondary outcomes

Subgroup analysis of 30-day all-cause mortality by AKI stage at enrolment revealed effect modification consistent with the primary outcome (Table 2). Among patients with stage 1 AKI (n = 6,205), the estimated 30-day mortality was 14.5% (95% CI, 10.1 to 19.0) under usual care and 15.2% (95% CI, 11.5 to 19.0) under the intervention; risk difference 0.7% (95% CI, − 0.3 to 1.7); relative risk 1.05 (95% CI, 0.97 to 1.13). Among patients with stage 2 AKI (n = 1,772), the estimated mortality was 17.9% (95% CI, 9.2 to 26.6) under usual care and 18.7% (95% CI, 11.0 to 26.4) under the intervention; risk difference 0.8% (95% CI, − 0.1 to 3.0); relative risk 1.04 (95% CI, 0.92 to 1.19). In contrast, among patients with stage 3 AKI (n = 708), the intervention was associated with a statistically significant increase in mortality: 18.4% (95% CI, 9.4 to 27.4) under usual care versus 22.1% (95% CI, 12.3 to 32.0) under the intervention; risk difference 3.7% (95% CI, 1.8 to 5.6); relative risk 1.20 (95% CI, 1.09 to 1.33).

Secondary outcomes — days alive and free of ICU

The restrictive fluid management intervention was associated with increased days alive and free of ICU at 30 days (Table 2, Fig. 4). The estimated days alive and free of ICU was 21.7 (95% CI, 19.2 to 24.3) under usual care and 22.8 (95% CI, 21.1 to 24.5) under the intervention; mean difference 1.1 days (95% CI, 0.2 to 1.9). Subgroup analysis by AKI stage revealed effect modification. Among patients with stage 1 AKI (n = 6,205) and stage 2 AKI (n = 1,772), the intervention was associated with 1.2 (95% CI, 0.3 to 2.1), and 0.9 days (95% CI, 0.0 to 1.9) additional days alive and free of ICU, respectively. Among patients with stage 3 AKI (n = 708), no significant difference was observed: 0.2 days (95% CI, − 0.7 to 1.1).

Secondary outcomes – AKI stage

As shown in Table 2 and Fig. 4, the restrictive fluid management intervention was not associated with a statistically significant change in maximal AKI stage within the first 7 days. Under usual care, 45% (95% CI, 41 to 48) of patients had a maximal stage of 1, 34% (95% CI, 32 to 36) had stage 2, and 21% (95% CI, 19 to 23) had stage 3. Under the intervention, 48% (95% CI, 46 to 49) had a maximal stage of 1, 32% (95% CI, 30 to 33) had stage 2, and 21% (95% CI, 19 to 22) had stage 3. The differences between groups were not statistically significant: stage 1 risk difference 2.7% (95% CI, − 1.0 to 6.4); stage 2 risk difference − 2.4% (95% CI, − 5.3 to 0.5); stage 3 risk difference − 0.3% (95% CI, − 1.6 to 1.0).

Mechanistic outcomes—fluid balance, urine output and urea

As shown in Fig. 5, the restrictive fluid management intervention was associated with a substantial reduction in fluid accumulation. The estimated maximum cumulative FB at 72 h was 3,509 mL (95% CI, 3,259 to 3,758) under usual care and 1,205 mL (95% CI, 1,083 to 1,327) under the intervention. The mean difference in FB at 72 h was − 2,304 mL (95% CI, − 2,465 to − 2,144), demonstrating that the intervention was associated with a substantial reduction in fluid accumulation. This separation was maintained over the 7-day follow-up period, with the maximal FB difference reaching − 2,567 mL (95% CI, − 2,766 to − 2,368).

Estimated trajectories of intervention components and fluid balance over time

The intervention’s individual components were each associated with group separation. Crystalloid administration was lower under the intervention throughout the 72-h treatment period. By the end of the intervention period, the estimated hourly crystalloid intake was approximately 50.6 mL/h (95% CI, 45.3 to 56.1) under usual care and 13.8 mL/h (95% CI, 13.1 to 14.6) under the intervention. Similarly, nutritional fluid intake was lower under the intervention, with estimated hourly intake of 40.1 mL/h (95% CI, 35.2 to 44.9) under usual care and 8.9 mL/h (95% CI, 8.6 to 9.1) under the intervention by the end of the treatment period. Diuretic use was higher under the intervention; by the end of the 72-h period, 47% (95% CI, 40 to 54) of patients under usual care had received diuretics compared with 86% (95% CI, 84 to 88) under the intervention.

The restrictive fluid intervention was also associated with lower peak urea levels. The estimated maximum urea within the first 7 days was 13.1 mmol/L (95% CI, 12.3 to 13.9) under usual care and 12.4 mmol/L (95% CI, 11.9 to 12.8) under the intervention; mean difference − 0.7 mmol/L (95% CI, − 1.2 to − 0.3).

Model diagnostics and positivity assessment

The intervention was active during time points 2 through 7 (corresponding to the first 72 h after AKI diagnosis). During this period, the instantaneous effective sample sizes for the entire cohort ranged from 7 to 16% (approximately 600 to 950 patients), indicating limited overlap between observed treatment patterns and the target intervention strategy at each time point. (Supplementary Material Table S11 and Figure S1). The cumulative effective sample size across follow-up dropped quickly to very low levels, indicating a significant lack of overlap between the observed treatment patterns and the target intervention strategy. These findings indicate strain on the positivity assumption and suggest that the estimates rely heavily on model-based extrapolation rather than direct causal contrast; accordingly, they should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The instantaneous effective sample sizes by AKI stage are presented in Supplementary Tables S12-14. The ESS-ratio remained stable across AKI stages, indicating that positivity violations were not more severe in the subgroup analyses. Detailed Super Learner contributions and other model diagnostics are presented in the Supplementary Material (Fig. S2-S9).

Discussion

Key findings

In this study, an emulated, hypothetical target trial evaluating restrictive fluid management in critically ill patients with AKI who were at high risk of developing a strongly positive FB, there were several key findings. First, we found that restrictive fluid management was associated with a substantial reduction in cumulative FB at 72 h, approximately 2.3 L, demonstrating that meaningful separation between strategies was observed. Second, we found that the restrictive fluid intervention was associated with improved renal function. Third, we found significant heterogeneity in the effect of AKI stage on renal function. Fourth, we found that the intervention was associated with a statistically significant increase in 30-day all-cause mortality. Last, and importantly, we found that the effective sample sizes during the intervention period were limited, indicating substantial reliance on model-based extrapolation rather than direct causal contrast.

Relationship to previous studies

The only randomised evidence evaluating restrictive fluid therapy in critically ill adults with AKI comes from the REVERSE-AKI pilot trial [6]. This feasibility study of 100 patients demonstrated that restrictive fluid therapy was achievable, with a mean difference in cumulative fluid balance at 72 h of − 1,148 mL (95% CI, − 2,200 to − 96 mL) between groups. However, the difference in fluid intake between restrictive and usual care groups was modest, and diuretic use was similar between arms. Our TTE was associated with a substantially larger separation in FB, approximately 2,300 mL, through a multi-component intervention targeting crystalloid administration, nutritional fluids, and diuretic use. This suggests that greater mechanistic separation may be attainable. However, our finding of potential harm in patients with advanced AKI raises important questions about whether such separation is desirable in all patients.

Multiple observational studies have demonstrated associations between positive FB and adverse outcomes in critically ill patients with AKI [32, 33]. Our prior work showed that each additional millilitre of positive FB after AKI diagnosis was independently associated with increased mortality risk [3]. However, these associations may reflect confounding by indication, sicker patients may receive more fluid and have worse outcomes regardless of fluid management strategy. Our finding that restrictive fluid management was associated with improved renal function in stage 1 AKI but worse outcomes in stages 2 and 3 suggests that the relationship between FB and outcomes may differ according to AKI severity. Patients with established severe AKI may require adequate circulating volume to maintain renal perfusion, and aggressive fluid restriction in this population could be harmful.

Recent large fluid trials in broader critical care populations have yielded neutral results. The CLASSIC trial found no benefit from restrictive versus liberal fluid therapy in patients with septic shock, [34] while the CLOVERS trial similarly demonstrated no advantage of restrictive resuscitation in early sepsis [35]. Notably, both trials intervened during initial resuscitation, focused on reducing intake only, and neither trial specifically enrolled patients with AKI. Our findings suggest that the effect of fluid management may be modified by AKI stage, and that heterogeneity of treatment effect by renal status warrants further investigation in future trials.

Implications of study findings

Our findings have several important implications for future research and clinical practice. First, the clinical significance of the primary outcome warrants consideration. AKI Rank integrates AKI severity trajectory, survival, and ICU discharge over 14 time-blocks. The overall difference of − 0.8 approximates, on average, one fewer time-block (12 h) spent at an elevated AKI stage. While modest at the population level, the subgroup effect in stage 1 AKI (− 3.2) represents a more clinically meaningful improvement equivalent to approximately 1.5 days at a lower AKI stage.

Second, our multi-component intervention, targeting crystalloid administration, nutritional fluids, and diuretic use, was associated with a clinically meaningful separation in FB of approximately 2,300 mL, suggesting that the mechanistic target may be attainable.

Third, and most importantly, the heterogeneity in treatment effect by AKI stage has direct implications for both clinical practice and trial design. If confirmed, these findings would support a precision medicine approach to fluid management in AKI, where the timing and severity of renal injury guide therapeutic strategy. Clinicians may need to consider early, proactive fluid restriction in patients with stage 1 AKI, when the kidney may still be amenable to interventions that limit further injury, while maintaining more liberal fluid targets in patients with established stage 2–3 AKI to preserve residual renal perfusion. However, the apparent harm signal in stage 3 AKI must be interpreted cautiously: this was the smallest subgroup with the greatest positivity strain, and the finding may reflect model instability rather than a true biological effect. Days alive and free of ICU provides a complementary perspective. This composite outcome demonstrated benefit with the restrictive intervention overall and in stage 1 AKI, with no signal of harm in stage 3 AKI. The absence of harm on this endpoint in stage 3 AKI, despite the mortality signal, may reflect competing effects on survival and ICU length of stay, or may further suggest that the mortality finding is driven by model instability in this smallest subgroup. Future trials should consider AKI stage not merely as a stratification variable but as a potential effect modifier that fundamentally alters the risk–benefit balance of restrictive fluid strategies.

Fourth, the finding of lower peak urea levels with the restrictive intervention is reassuring from a safety perspective, suggesting that the intervention did not result in clinically significant under-resuscitation at the population level. However, this must be interpreted alongside the mortality signal in stage 3 patients, indicating that population-level averages may mask important subgroup effects.

Fifth, we acknowledge that enteral and intravenous fluids have distinct physiological effects, with enteral fluids promoting mucosal integrity without directly contributing to glycocalyx injury. However, nutritional fluids contribute to total body fluid accumulation regardless of route, and it is total fluid volume that is implicated in renal venous congestion. Future trial designs could consider refining the nutritional component, for example by distinguishing enteral from parenteral sources.

The limited positivity observed in our analysis, with effective sample sizes of only 7–16% during the intervention period, indicates that restrictive fluid management, as we defined it, is not routinely practised in current care. This supports the presence of clinical equipoise and the ethical justification for a randomised trial. However, our findings suggest that such a trial should carefully consider patient selection.

Based on our findings, we recommend that any future randomised controlled trial of restrictive fluid management in critically ill patients with AKI incorporate several design features: use of the AKI-FB Risk Score for patient selection to enrich for patients at high risk of fluid accumulation; pre-specified stratification by AKI stage at enrolment; and careful documentation of protocol adherence to ensure that meaningful separation in FB is attained between arms. Given the signal toward potential harm in patients with advanced AKI observed in our analysis, trial protocols should incorporate safety criteria to identify intravascular hypovolaemia during the intervention period, for example, mandating regular reassessment of fluid restriction. A pilot trial with embedded safety monitoring would help establish whether restrictive fluid management can be safely applied across the spectrum of AKI severity, rather than excluding patients with advanced AKI and limiting generalisability.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, we utilised detailed, routinely collected electronic medical record data from 12 ICUs representing the majority of intensive care capacity in a large Australian state, thereby enhancing the generalisability of the baseline characteristics and usual care patterns observed. Second, we employed sophisticated causal inference methodology, the sequential doubly robust estimator for longitudinal modified treatment policies, which yields valid inference when either the treatment or the outcome models are correctly specified at each time point. Third, we used a validated risk prediction score to identify patients at high risk of fluid accumulation, demonstrating a practical approach for trial enrichment. Fourth, our multi-component intervention targeted the specific mechanisms previously identified as driving positive FB in AKI, ensuring it was mechanistically plausible and clinically implementable. Fifth, we conducted prespecified subgroup analyses by AKI stage, which revealed clinically important heterogeneity in treatment effects that would have been obscured by examining overall effects alone.

We acknowledge several important limitations. Most notably, the effective sample sizes during the intervention period were limited, ranging from 7 to 16% of the cohort at each time point. This indicates that the observed treatment patterns in our population differed substantially from the target intervention, requiring reliance on model-based extrapolation. Consequently, our effect estimates carry considerable uncertainty, and the point estimates, including the subgroup findings, should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of benefit or harm. The subgroup analyses, while pre-specified, involved smaller sample sizes, particularly for stage 3 AKI, and the observed effects may be subject to residual confounding or model misspecification. Furthermore, fluid and diuretic titration in the ICU is frequently guided by bedside assessments, such as clinical examination, point-of-care ultrasound, and real-time haemodynamic responses, that are incompletely captured in electronic health records. Although we adjusted for a comprehensive set of time-varying covariates, residual confounding by indication may persist. Additional limitations include the single-country setting, which may limit generalisability to healthcare systems with different fluid management practices; the specific intervention thresholds chosen, which may not represent optimal targets; and the use of the AKI Rank as the primary outcome, which carries inherent limitations. Because follow-up data were only available during ICU admission, the composite outcome cannot distinguish between earlier ICU discharge and true renal recovery, and death may not be substantially penalised relative to discharge alive within the scoring system. We provide days alive and free of ICU distribution and mortality as secondary outcomes to allow readers to interpret the clinical significance of the primary outcome in context. Finally, although we adjusted for measured confounders, unmeasured confounding cannot be ruled out in this observational study design. Our exclusion of patients who received ECMO at any time during admission, including after AKI diagnosis, may introduce selection bias if the intervention influenced ECMO requirement. This broad exclusion was necessitated by data limitations that precluded distinguishing pre-baseline from post-baseline ECMO initiation. However, ECMO patients were excluded because their fluid management is fundamentally different and not amenable to the studied intervention.

Conclusion

In this emulation of a hypothetical target trial, we we found that a multi-component restrictive fluid management strategy was associated with substantial separation in FB, approximately 2,300 mL at 72 h, compared to usual care in critically ill patients with AKI at high risk of fluid accumulation. We identified significant heterogeneity in treatment effect by AKI stage: the intervention was associated with improved renal function in patients with stage 1 AKI, but with worse outcomes in patients with stage 2 and 3 AKI. These findings are hypothesis-generating, given the methodological limitations inherent to observational causal inference and the limited effective sample sizes observed. If a randomised controlled trial is pursued, our findings suggest it should incorporate protocol-level safety criteria, particularly in patients with advanced renal injury.

Take away message

This target trial emulation found that restrictive fluid management in critically ill patients with AKI was associated with improved renal function in stage 1 AKI but worse outcomes, including increased mortality, in stage 2 and 3 AKI. These hypothesis-generating findings suggest that AKI stage may be a critical effect modifier, and future trials of fluid restriction should incorporate stage-specific safety criteria.
