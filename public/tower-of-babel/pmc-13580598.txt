One-third of global lake nitrous oxide emissions are concentrated in the northern temperate zone (40°N–50°N)
Authors: Yujie Zhou, Jilin Huang, Yang Gao, Qingsong Jiang, Yue Qin, Zhen Wu, Yong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lacustrine nitrous oxide (N2O) emissions remain highly uncertain in the global greenhouse gas budget largely because of sparse observations and the omission of small lakes. Here we compile the Global Lake N2O database (GLON2O), integrating 6550 in situ measurements from 3829 lakes worldwide, and develop a machine learning framework to estimate emissions from lakes ≥0.1 square kilometers. To quantify previously overlooked systems, we construct the Global Small Lakes database (GSLAKES). We estimate that global lakes emit 0.208 teragrams of nitrogen per year of N2O, with approximately one-third originating from the northern temperate zone (40° to 50°N). Despite accounting for only 9.6% of total lake area, small lakes (<0.1 square kilometers) contribute 34.6% of global emissions, revealing a pronounced U-shaped relationship between emission intensity and lake size. We further identify hierarchical predictors of lake N2O emissions: Natural constraints determine biogeochemical potential, anthropogenic nitrogen inputs amplify emissions, and lake morphometry regulates spatial expression. These findings refine global lake N2O inventories and provide an empirical basis for scale-aware mitigation.

INTRODUCTION

Lakes are integral components of inland aquatic systems and increasingly recognized contributors to global greenhouse gas budgets (1–3). Although nitrous oxide (N2O) has received far less attention than carbon dioxide (CO2), its 100-year global warming potential is nearly 300 times greater (4–7), underscoring its disproportionate climate impact. Lakes have emerged as important natural sources of atmospheric N2O (8–10). At the same time, rapid population growth and socioeconomic development have intensified nutrient loading, accelerating eutrophication and fundamentally reshaping lake biogeochemical processes and greenhouse gas dynamics (11, 12).

However, current estimates of global N2O emissions from lakes (e.g., 63.00 to 630.00 Gg N year−1) (9, 13–15) exhibit uncertainties as high as a factor of 10. These estimates have three fundamental and long-ignored methodological limitations that collectively weaken our understanding of the global patterns of nitrogen cycling in lakes. (i) Existing global datasets suffer from both data sparsity and pronounced spatial bias (16, 17), with observations concentrated in North America, Europe, and East Asia, leaving vast regions such as Siberia, Africa, and South America severely underrepresented. Moreover, most measurements are limited to easily accessible snapshot or annual-average samples, which fail to capture emission hot moments (e.g., spring/autumn overturn, post–storm runoff pulses, and seasonal hypoxia). The reliance on static data likely leads to a systematic underestimation of fluxes in global budget estimates. (ii) Despite their numerical dominance, small water bodies (<0.1 km2) have been largely excluded from global inventories due to mapping and observational challenges (9, 18), leaving their contribution to global N2O emissions poorly quantified. (iii) Traditional approaches largely rely on mean-value extrapolation or linear models that assume monotonic relationships between N2O emissions and lake characteristics (13). If biogeochemical regimes differ fundamentally across lake sizes, such as terrestrial input–dominated versus internally cycled systems, these assumptions introduce substantial scaling biases.

N2O production in lakes is a complex process controlled by multiple microbial pathways (19, 20), which are jointly regulated in a nonlinear manner by topography, climate, hydrology, and nutrient inputs (21–28). For instance, low temperatures at high altitudes may suppress microbial metabolism, while high nitrogen inputs from agricultural runoff can substantially enhance N2O production (29–31). Such high-dimensional, nonlinear interactions (32, 33) cannot be captured by traditional regression methods, underscoring the need for machine learning models, which are capable of handling complex environmental drivers. Despite recent studies (3, 6) using spatially explicit process-based models to estimate inland-water and lentic-system N2O emissions, the 0.5° × 0.5° grid-cell representation of lentic waters remains relatively aggregated and coarse grained when it comes to representing lakes with high heterogeneity. Contributions from different lakes within the same grid cell may be smoothed out and obscured during spatial aggregation.

To directly address the abovementioned methodological challenges and reshape the understanding of lake N2O dynamics, we propose a data-driven global quantification framework. First, we have compiled the most comprehensive global lake N2O (GLON2O) database to date, which includes 6550 in situ measurements from 3829 lakes. This database allows us to quantify the extent of bias in the global data (fig. S1) and provides an unprecedented data foundation for model training. Second, we apply an integrated machine learning framework [including Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and other models] with the goal of establishing the robust baseline for annual average emissions flux in global lakes ≥0.1 km2. Third, we have developed a Global Small Lakes Database (GSLAKES) to specifically address the gap of small lake omission. Last, the core objective of this study is to use this integrated framework to test the unverified hypothesis: whether N2O emission mechanisms and fluxes exhibit fundamental scale dependencies. Through this approach, we aim to reevaluate the contribution of standing water bodies to the global greenhouse gas budget and decouple the major environmental and anthropogenic drivers of lake N2O fluxes at global scale.

RESULTS

GLON2O insights: U-shaped scale dependence

The GLON2O database compiled in this study (n = 3829 lakes, 6550 measurements) reveals substantial variability in the global annual N2O fluxes of lakes, ranging from −9.94 to 145.81 mg N m−2 day−1 (median = 0.11, mean = 0.58 mg N m−2 day−1). A nonmonotonic relationship was identified between lake N2O emissions and lake area (Fig. 1). N2O flux does not scale linearly with lake area. Instead, a U-shaped pattern emerges: (i) Extremely small lakes (<0.001 km2) exhibit the highest emissions (median: 0.39 mg N m−2 day−1); (ii) emissions decline sharply with increasing lake area, reaching a global minimum in small to mid-sized lakes (0.1 to 1 km2; median: 0.08 mg N m−2 day−1); and (iii) as lake area continues to increase (1 to 100 km2), emission rates exhibit a modest rebound.

This empirical pattern challenges the conventional upscaling logic, which is based on monotonic relationships (linear or power-law). It suggests that the dominant biogeochemical mechanisms controlling N2O production may differ across lakes of varying scales: (i) In extremely small lakes, emissions may be driven by exogenous nitrogen inputs tightly coupled to land and sedimentary denitrification (3, 34); and (ii) in large lakes, emissions may be more influenced by internal processes such as water-column nitrification (35). Stratified analyses by climate zone, lake depth, and human impact further indicate that the size-dependent area-flux pattern remains evident across major environmental subsets (figs. S2 to S5). Therefore, this nonmonotonic relationship provides crucial biogeochemical justification to treat small lakes (<0.1 km2) as a distinct category with specific mechanisms and quantify their emissions using a different approach (e.g., Monte Carlo).

Performance and robustness evaluation of machine learning models

To avoid spatial autocorrelation biasing, we used strict spatial cross-validation. In this framework, the RF model demonstrated the best overall performance and robustness, achieving a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.626 and an RMSE of 0.4673 mg N m−2 day−1 (fig. S6 and table S1). XGBoost performed comparably (R2 = 0.558), while multilayer perceptron performed slightly worse (R2 = 0.425). All models performed better in predicting mid- and low-range fluxes but struggled to predict extremely high values. This is as expected since the input data represent annual-average conditions, and the models are designed to capture the baseline rather than extreme hot moments. On the basis of its optimal performance and robustness, we selected the RF model for subsequent global predictions. However, we must acknowledge that because of the spatial bias in the global data (fig. S1), predictions in data-sparse areas (e.g., Siberia, the Congo Basin, and the Amazon) inherently carry high uncertainty. Generating an accurate spatial prediction uncertainty map is a key future goal, but this will require more complex physics-based models and denser global validation data. Thus, the global emission maps generated in this study (Fig. 2) should be viewed as the best estimate so far given the current data availability, with much higher confidence in data-rich areas (North America and Europe) compared to data-sparse regions.

Global annual N2O flux baseline and spatial patterns for lakes (≥0.1 km2)

Using the trained RF model, we predicted the annual N2O fluxes for all lakes larger than 0.1 km2 globally (n = 1.4 million), generating the spatially lake-explicit global N2O flux baseline map (Fig. 2A). It is essential to emphasize that this is a baseline estimate, with higher confidence in data-rich areas (North America and Europe). Despite uncertainties, it reveals a clear global pattern: (i) The global average N2O flux is 0.064 ± 0.038 mg N m−2 day−1. Emission hotspots are primarily concentrated in temperate and boreal regions, particularly the Scandinavian Peninsula, the Great Lakes region in North America, and northern Eurasia; (ii) Russia (0.077 Tg N year−1), Canada (0.056 Tg N year−1), and the United States (0.046 Tg N year−1) are the largest emitters (table S2), together accounting for 65.7% of global emissions for lakes ≥0.1 km2, primarily because of their vast lake areas; (iii) spatially, when combining with the small lakes (table S3), the 40° to 50°N latitude band serves as the primary global hotspot, contributing 30.7% of the total global lake N2O emissions. This substantial contribution largely mirrors the region’s vast lake surface area (30.0% of the global total). However, a distinct intensification is observed in medium-to-large lakes (≥0.1 km2): Within this size class, the 40° to 50°N band accounts for 41.2% (0.056 of 0.136) of the category-specific emissions, clearly exceeding its category-specific area share of 32.1% (939.48 of 2926.72; table S4). Consistently, after normalizing by lake area, medium-to-large lakes in this band show a higher emission intensity of 0.163 mg N m−2 day−1, compared with 0.127 mg N m−2 day−1 for medium-to-large lakes globally and 0.110 mg N m−2 day−1 outside the 40° to 50°N band.

Emission contributions from global small lakes (<0.1 km2)

Our GSLAKES database offers a much higher spatial resolution than existing databases (e.g., GLAKES). For example, GSLAKES identifies 22,751 thousand lakes in the 0.001 to 0.01 km2 range, whereas GLAKES only identifies 267 thousand, substantially enhancing the completeness of the global lake inventory (fig. S7). Subsequently, we applied the log-normal Monte Carlo simulation on the basis of the nonmonotonic scale effect in Fig. 1.

The results indicate that the total N2O emissions from small lakes (<0.1 km2) globally are estimated at 0.072 Tg N year−1 (Q1 to Q3: 0.017 to 0.062). Human-impacted small lakes contribute 0.031 Tg N year−1, whereas natural small lakes contribute 0.041 Tg N year−1 (table S5). When combined with the emissions from large lakes (0.136 Tg N year−1), the total global N2O emissions from lakes are 0.208 Tg N year−1. This finding confirms the disproportionate contribution of small lakes. Despite their total area (310.31 × 103 km2) accounting for only 9.6% of the global lake area (3237.03 × 103 km2), small lakes contribute 34.6% (0.072 of 0.208) of total global lake N2O emissions. This study provides global-scale evidence that small lakes represent a long-neglected but crucial hotspot in the global N2O budget.

Multiscale drivers of global lake N2O emissions

To decouple the complex drivers of annual lake N2O fluxes, we used the multiscale complementary analysis framework. First, at the global-mean scale, we used global permutation importance (GPI) to assess the contribution of variables to the global model’s average prediction accuracy. The results show that elevation is the most important predictor (Fig. 3). This aligns with biogeochemical macro-relationships as elevation is the strongest integrator of temperature, pressure, and hydrological gradients, fundamentally setting a region’s microbial metabolic potential and baseline emission potential. In addition, population density and annual runoff, representing anthropogenic nitrogen inputs and hydrological transport, also show high global importance.

Next, at the local-individual scale, we used the Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) method to explain the most important environmental drivers associated with every single lake (fig. S8 and table S6). The results (fig. S8A) revealed a distinct but complementary pattern of local model sensitivity. In most lakes across different regions, lake area is among the most important predictors influencing individual-lake emission predictions. This also aligns with physical limnological principles, as lake area is the fundamental morphological parameter that dictates specific physical-chemical conditions (e.g., stratification, mixing, residence time, and sediment-water interface) (36, 37) and modulates the expression of the potential set by elevation. Thus, the results from Fig. 3 and fig. S8 are not contradictory but instead suggest a multiscale hierarchy in model-derived predictor importance. We interpret this structure as a hypothesis-generating framework: (i) Natural factors such as elevation set the emission potential for a region; (ii) population density and cropland (fig. S8B) represent anthropogenic nitrogen inputs, enhancing the substrate that enables the realization of this potential; (iii) morphological features such as lake area, acting as intermediary mechanisms, regulate the physical-chemical expression of this potential and input in individual lakes. In general, at the global scale (Fig. 3), elevation, as the strong representative of macroclimate (temperature and pressure) and hydrological gradients, exhibits great predictive ability at the global scale for distinguishing high-emission and low-emission regions. At the local scale (fig. S8), lake area, as the most fundamental morphological characteristic of a lake, is the dominant factor for determining that lake’s physical-chemical conditions (e.g., stratification, mixing, and residence time). In other words, elevation explains a region’s emission potential, while lake area regulates how this potential is expressed in individual lakes.

DISCUSSION

Why adopt a dual methodology?

The identification of a nonmonotonic scale effect provides the primary scientific rationale for adopting the dual machine learning–Monte Carlo (ML + MC) framework. As illustrated in Fig. 1, small lakes (<0.1 km2) and large lakes (≥0.1 km2) may be governed by fundamentally distinct biogeochemical regimes: Small lakes are predominantly controlled by terrestrial inputs, whereas large lakes are driven mainly by internal cycling. Consequently, applying a machine learning model trained on large lakes to predict emissions from small lakes would inevitably lead to severe mechanistic misextrapolation. To avoid this seemingly convenient yet scientifically unsound practice, we adopted a more conservative and robust strategy. Specifically, we used Monte Carlo simulations constrained by the empirically observed log-normal distribution of N2O fluxes in small lakes. This approach ensures that the estimated ∼30% contribution from small lakes is firmly anchored in their own observational evidence rather than inferred through erroneous cross-scale extrapolation.

Baseline estimates of global lake N2O emissions

Before interpreting the findings, we first clarify the methodological foundations and limitations. Regarding the static model and averaging biases, as we explicitly stated before, N2O is a highly episodic gas driven by hot moments such as turnover events and intense storm pulses. However, since GLON2O is a historical compilation database, the data it contains are heterogeneous in terms of sampling frequency and spatial-temporal coverage. Therefore, aggregating raw measurements into an annual average flux is a deliberate and necessary methodological choice. The goal of this approach is not (and could not be) to capture the short-term dynamics of individual lakes but rather to establish the spatially explicit annual flux baseline for global lake N2O emissions. In the absence of globally standardized high-frequency monitoring data, this baseline serves as an essential foundation for all more complex dynamic models in the future. The GLON2O database suffers from substantial spatial bias (fig. S1). Consequently, our machine learning models necessarily come with high uncertainty in data-sparse regions (e.g., Siberia, Africa, and South America). The global map we generated (Fig. 2) should be considered the current best estimate based on current data limitations rather than an exact ground truth for all regions.

The emergence of the 40° to 50°N band as the primary global hotspot, contributing 30.7% of global lake N2O emissions (table S4), aligns with recent findings (38) that identify temperate zones as the largest contributors to the global budget. This regional dominance is first supported by the large lake-area base in this latitude band, which accounts for 30.0% of the global lake area (table S4). Beyond this area effect, our results suggest that this regional dominance may arise from a synergy between lake morphometry and anthropogenic forcing: First, this latitude appears to be governed by a distinct “large lake effect.” As shown in the latitudinal profile (fig. S9), the 40° to 50°N band exhibits the largest mean lake area globally. Given the U-shaped scale dependence identified in this study (Fig. 1), where N2O fluxes rebound in large lakes (>10 km2) potentially due to enhanced internal nitrogen cycling, this region is geographically predisposed to high emissions by hosting the specific size class of lakes that occupy the high-emission “right tail” of the U curve. Second, these large lakes function as “integrators” of anthropogenic nitrogen. Geographically coinciding with globally major agricultural and population belts, these large water bodies have vast drainage basins capable of intercepting and accumulating nutrient runoff. The convergence of favorable climatic conditions and moderate elevation (which facilitate microbial metabolism) and excessive substrate supply (anthropogenic loading) may jointly enhance emissions (21, 35), as reflected by the higher area-normalized emission intensity of medium-to-large lakes within the 40° to 50°N band than those outside this latitude band (0.163 versus 0.110 mg N m−2 day−1), also plausibly accounting for the exceptional 41.2% contribution of medium-to-large lakes in this region to the category’s global emissions (table S4).

This study provides a refined quantitative estimate of global N2O emissions from lakes through an integrated, data-driven framework. Our total estimated emissions are 0.208 Tg N year−1, which aligns closely with the statistical model of DelSontro et al. (9) (0.16 to 0.38 Tg N year−1), but is lower than the earlier estimate of Soued et al. (13) (0.63 Tg N year−1) (Table 1). The key difference stems from fundamental methodological advances. First, our model includes 6550 measurements, more than 20 times the number of measurements in some previous global studies [e.g., 309 observations in reference (9)]. This substantially enhances the statistical power of the model and minimizes the geographical bias associated with earlier studies that overly relied on temperate, eutrophic lakes. Second, for lakes ≥0.1 km2, we developed a spatially lake-explicit, size-aware, and multifactor upscaling framework. Rather than representing lentic waters as 0.5° × 0.5° grid-cell averages (3, 6), our framework predicts N2O emissions for individual lakes and then sums these estimates globally. This design is important because lakes within the same grid cell can differ markedly in size, morphology, land use, and nutrient inputs. Aggregating them into a single grid–level response may smooth out or obscure these differences. In addition, when the flux-area relationship is nonlinear, grid-level averaging and lake-by-lake summation are not mathematically equivalent. Our approach therefore provides a more direct way to evaluate how lake-size heterogeneity affects global N2O upscaling. Third, we systematically quantify the contribution of small lakes (<0.1 km2) through the GSLAKES database, estimating their contribution at 0.072 Tg N year−1 (34.6% of the total). Previous studies [e.g., reference (15)], which failed to include this crucial source of emissions, likely underestimated global lake N2O emissions, given their more conservative estimate (0.063 ± 0.041 Tg N year−1).

Biogeochemical interpretation of nonmonotonic scale effects

Beyond the total emissions budget, the most fundamental scientific contribution of this study lies in revealing the profound nonmonotonic (U-shaped) relationship between N2O flux and lake area (Fig. 1). These findings challenge the scaling logic implicit in previous studies, which assumed a simple linear or power-law relationship. This further points to the possibility that the dominant biogeochemical processes controlling N2O production may differ substantially between lakes of different scales.

Hotspot effect in extremely small lakes (<0.01 km2)

These water bodies, with extremely high shoreline-to-area ratios and shallow depths, exhibit the strongest biogeochemical coupling with land (watershed) processes (39). They receive disproportionately high loads of nitrogen and carbon from land and have shallow sediments that easily develop anoxic conditions, thereby promoting denitrification and leading to the highest emissions per unit area (40).

Transition zone in small- to medium-sized lakes (0.1 to 1 km2)

At this scale, lakes begin to deepen, leading to a partial dilution of land-derived nutrient inputs and weakening their dominance over biogeochemical processes, while the physical stratification and internal circulation of larger lakes are not yet fully established. This may represent a biogeochemical transition zone that leads to the lowest emissions fluxes globally.

Rebound in medium to large lakes (1 to 100 km2)

As lakes increase in area and volume, physical stratification (e.g., thermocline) becomes stable, and water residence times increase. This creates favorable conditions for both nitrification in oxygen-rich surface waters and denitrification in anoxic bottom waters. The slight rebound in emissions may reflect a shift from land-derived inputs as the dominant mechanism to internal cycling (e.g., nitrification) as the dominant driver (9).

Decoupling drivers: A multiscale complementary hierarchy

Another purpose of this study was to decouple the drivers of N2O emissions. The global permutation-importance analysis and the local LIME analysis are not contradictory (Fig. 3 and fig. S8), but they provide complementary perspectives on how different variables contribute to model predictions across analytical scales. We interpret these patterns as a conceptual, hypothesis-generating framework:

(i) Macroconstraints: Elevation

Elevation is the most important predictor at the global scale (Fig. 3), which is entirely reasonable. It serves as the strongest integrator of temperature, atmospheric pressure, and hydrological gradients, essentially setting the upper limit for microbial metabolic potential in a region. Low temperatures at high altitudes (41, 42) and changes in atmospheric pressure (affecting O2 solubility) together suppress N2O production (43, 44).

(ii) Direct drivers: Anthropogenic inputs

Under macroconstraints, population density and cropland area are the secondary drivers (Fig. 3 and fig. S8B). These represent direct anthropogenic nitrogen inputs from wastewater and fertilizers (7). This explains why our emission hotspots (Fig. 2) are concentrated in the temperate Northern Hemisphere, regions where high anthropogenic nitrogen inputs and favorable biogeochemical conditions overlap (45–48).

(iii) Local effect: Lake morphology

Lake area (accounting for 99.65% contribution in LIME analysis) is the most important local factor that regulates the final expression of the above potential and input at individual lakes (fig. S8). Lake area (and its associated depth and volume) explains the physical mixing, thermal stratification, water residence time, and sediment-water interface dynamics (36), which ultimately influence the location and intensity of nitrification and denitrification processes. Thus, our analysis jointly reveals a clear hierarchy: Elevation sets the potential, anthropogenic nitrogen provides the substrate, and lake area regulates the process (Fig. 3 and fig. S8).

Our findings have direct implications for global nitrogen management and climate mitigation. With the multiscale driver analysis, we propose an integrated framework for lake nitrogen and N2O emission control built on three principles. (i) Small-lake prioritization: although covering limited area, small lakes (<0.1 km2) contribute nearly one-third of global emissions, serving as both sentinels and hotspots of watershed nitrogen cycling; their exclusion would systematically undermine mitigation efforts. (ii) Scale-aware, context-specific management: The nonmonotonic size dependence of emissions demonstrates that uniform strategies are ineffective. Mitigation in very small lakes should emphasize interception of land-derived nitrogen inputs (e.g., riparian buffers), whereas larger systems require regulation of internal biogeochemical processes (e.g., algal bloom control and hypolimnetic reoxygenation). Medium-to-large lakes in the northern temperate zone (40°–50°N), where anthropogenic pressures and data availability converge, emerge as high-leverage targets for reducing the global aquatic N2O burden. (iii) Source-targeted watershed management: Population density and cropland area are identified as dominant anthropogenic drivers, highlighting the necessity of basin-scale interventions, including precision agriculture and advanced wastewater treatment, to curb upstream nitrogen loading.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

GLON2O database compilation

We constructed the comprehensive Global Lake N2O (GLON2O) database and developed an integrated methodological framework (Fig. 4). The database compiles 6550 N2O flux measurements from 3829 lakes worldwide (including 3670 lakes ≥0.1 km2) and contains detailed geographic coordinates of sampling sites along with associated physical and biogeochemical parameters. It was compiled through extensive literature reviews of Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar, supplemented by citation tracking from review articles. Nonlake systems (e.g., wastewater treatment facilities, aquaculture ponds, and rice paddies) were intentionally excluded to maintain data homogeneity.

Integrated data–model framework for global lake N2O emission estimation.

The lakes are classified into two groups, each with a dedicated estimation method. For data-rich lakes ≥0.1 km2, we applied a machine learning framework to predict spatially explicit annual N2O fluxes based on key environmental drivers. For the previously overlooked small lakes (<0.1 km2), we constructed the GSLAKES database and used a Monte Carlo upscaling approach informed by their distinct statistical size distribution. The GSLAKES database was derived primarily from the well-validated 30-m gridded SARL database (2022 layer) (49), supplemented by small lakes (>0.03 km2) from GLAKES (50). We applied spatial overlay analysis to exclude water bodies overlapping with the HydroLAKES datasets (51) to ensure a distinct inventory of small lakes. This database not only provides information on the abundance and area distribution of small lakes but also matches key environmental and anthropogenic parameters, including temperature, precipitation, lake surface area, and population density.

Identification of predictive variables

We extracted 34 potential predictive variables from global spatial datasets, covering seven major categories (topography, meteorology, hydrology, water quality, land use, soils, and anthropogenic impacts; see table S7). Unless otherwise specified, lake morphometric, hydrological, and catchment-aggregated variables were derived from HydroLAKES (51); population density was obtained from GPWv4 (52); and lake surface water temperature and ice phenology variables were obtained from LakeTEMP (53). These variables were selected on the basis of their theoretical relevance to N2O cycling (e.g., nitrification/denitrification substrate supply, temperature dependence, and hydrological transport) (17, 54–58). To reduce model interpretation complexity and collinearity issues, we excluded strongly correlated variables (Pearson’s r > 0.9), such as Tyear_mean and tmp_dc_lyr.

Machine learning modeling for lakes ≥0.1 km2

We adopted an integrated machine learning framework that includes RF, XGBoost, and Neural Networks. Before model training, we first removed outliers by excluding the top and bottom 1% of N2O flux. This preprocessing step removed all nonpositive flux values from the modeling dataset. Thus, the remaining fluxes were strictly positive and were log10-transformed for model fitting. Given the spatial bias in the GLON2O database (fig. S1), standard random 80/20 sampling would result in spatial autocorrelation, leading to overestimation of model predictive performance. To obtain a more conservative and robust evaluation of model generalization ability, we used a spatially blocked cross-validation framework. The data were divided by geographic region or watershed, ensuring that the training and validation sets are spatially independent. This approach enables our performance metrics (R2 and RMSE) to more accurately reflect the model’s predictive ability in truly unknown areas.

To decouple the complex drivers of N2O emissions, we used two complementary model interpretation methods, i.e., GPI and LIME. The GPI assesses the average impact of a variable on the model’s overall global prediction accuracy (R2) by randomly shuffling the global data for that variable. It measures a variable’s overall predictive capability at the macro scale. The LIME focuses on explaining why a specific lake received a specific predicted value. It measures the individual contribution of variables at the local scale.

Upscaling N2O emissions from small lakes (<0.1 km2)

Quantifying emissions from small lakes (<0.1 km2) is a central objective of this study and represents a key methodological challenge. A straightforward option would be to directly apply the machine learning model trained for large lakes to small lakes in the GSLAKES database. However, we deliberately avoided this seemingly consistent but scientifically unsound blind extrapolation. It is grounded in a core finding of our analysis (see Results) that lake N2O emissions exhibit a fundamentally nonmonotonic, U-shaped relationship with lake area. The smallest lakes show the highest emission rates, whereas mid-sized lakes (0.1 to 1 km2) exhibit the lowest. This pattern indicates that the biogeochemical processes associated with emissions in very small, land-coupled lakes (<0.1 km2) differ substantially from those operating in larger, internally regulated lakes (≥0.1 km2). Consequently, extending a model trained on large lakes to small lakes would constitute an inappropriate mechanistic extrapolation.

We instead used a stratified log-normal Monte Carlo framework based on small-lake observations from the GLON2O database (n = 159; fig. S10). Before upscaling, we first classified small lakes into human-impacted and natural categories using population density around each lake as a globally available proxy for anthropogenic influence. To avoid imposing an arbitrary threshold, we fitted an unsupervised two-component Gaussian mixture model to log10(population density + 1). Lakes assigned to the low population–density component were classified as natural small lakes, whereas those assigned to the high population–density component were classified as human-impacted small lakes.

We then stratified observations by lake type and size class, using two lake types and three size classes: 0.0001 to 0.001, 0.001 to 0.01, and 0.01 to 0.1 km2. For each lake-type–by–size-class stratum, we calculated the mean and SD of log-transformed N2O-N fluxes and used these parameters to define a stratum-specific log-normal distribution. We performed 20,000 Monte Carlo simulations and multiplied simulated fluxes by the corresponding global surface area of each stratum from the GSLAKES database (table S5). Although this approach does not fully resolve driver-based spatial variability among small lakes, it provides a conservative and observation-constrained estimate under current data availability.
