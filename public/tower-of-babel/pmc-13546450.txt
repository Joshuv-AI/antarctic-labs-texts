Evaluation of the Diabetes Action Canada (DAC) Research-To-Action Fellowship Program for Patient Partners: A Multi-Method Case Study
Authors: Julie Makarski, Isabella Herrington, Laure Perrier, Krystle Amog, Linxi Mytkolli, Tracy McQuire, Monika Kastner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective of our study was to assess the feasibility, acceptability, and early impacts of the inaugural Research-to-Action Fellowship Program (FP) on patient partnership and knowledge mobilization (KM) practice within a diabetes research network. The FP is a patient-partner-centric initiative co-designed by patient partners, FP interest holders and implementers to build capacity in KM. Fellows receive mentorship, training, and financial support to co-design and disseminate KM products with community-based partner organizations. A formative, multi-method case study using surveys, focus groups, and interviews was conducted with patient-partner fellows, partner-organization representatives, and program implementers. Quantitative data were analyzed descriptively; qualitative data were examined using directed content analysis. Fifteen of 16 invited participants contributed data. Fellows reported perceived gains in KM-related knowledge, clarity of roles, support, collaboration, and community engagement. Qualitative themes highlighted the value of paid, leadership-based roles for patient partners, dyad structures, strong relational infrastructure, and iterative adaptation. Overall, our study showed that a patient-partner-led KM fellowship was perceived as feasible and acceptable, and as a means of building capacity for more meaningful patient partnership.Keywords: diabetes, patient partner, patient experience, knowledge mobilization, program evaluation, qualitative methods, survey data

Introduction

Meaningful patient partnership is central to improving the relevance and impact of health research. Diabetes Action Canada (DAC) is a pan-Canadian research network that unites patients, caregivers, clinicians, and researchers to address diabetes-related gaps through collaborative, patient-oriented research (POR). 1 POR engages people with lived experience and their care partners as active collaborators, focusing on patient-identified priorities to improve outcomes and experiences. 2 This approach can improve study acceptability, strengthen rigour, and enhance the relevance and uptake of findings. 3 Including patient partners in knowledge mobilization (KM), the processes by which research is translated, co-produced, and used in practice, 4 can improve the clarity and resonance of research messages, build trust, and help tailor dissemination to local contexts.5-8 In practice, patient partners are often engaged in advisory roles in research (including KM) rather than in compensated, leadership-based KM fellowship positions. To address this gap, DAC launched the Research-to-Action Fellowship Program (FP) in 2024. Co-designed with patient partners and implementation researchers, the FP aims to build patient partners’ capacity to co-design and disseminate KM products grounded in the lived experience of diabetes. Fellows receive mentorship, targeted training in KM and patient engagement, and stipends to lead a project with a community-based partner organization (PO). Fellows are paired to work in dyads and matched with POs serving diverse communities affected by diabetes, enabling peer learning and workload sharing while drawing on existing KM resources.

This study reports a formative, multi-method evaluation of the inaugural DAC FP cohort (2024–2025). The objective was to assess the FP’s feasibility and acceptability within one research network (DAC) and its partner organizations, and to describe participants’ early perceptions of the program’s focus on patient partnership and KM practice. The research question was: “How do patient partners, partner-organization representatives, and program implementers perceive the feasibility, acceptability, and early insights of the Research-to-Action Fellowship within their organizations?”

Methods

Design and Framework

We used a formative, multi-method case study design to evaluate the inaugural FP, focusing on its implementability, defined as the perceived feasibility, acceptability, and organizational fit of the program. The evaluation was informed by the RE-AIM framework: Reach and Effectiveness were considered in terms of who participated and participants’ perceived early insights, while Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance guided examination of uptake, delivery, and potential sustainability of the FP.9-11 The study was not powered to test effectiveness but to explore feasibility, acceptability, and perceived early insights in a single cohort. Research ethics approval was obtained from the North York General Hospital Research Ethics Board (Protocol #2024-0401-1099), and the study adhered to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research. 12

Participants and Recruitment

16 individuals involved in the inaugural FP were invited to participate: patient-partner fellows (n=8), PO representatives (n=6), and program implementers from DAC (n=2).A whole-population, purposive sampling approach was used to capture the full range of interest-holder perspectives. 13 Recruitment materials were available in English and French, and participation in the evaluation was voluntary and not required for FP participation. Informed consent and demographic data were collected via an online survey (Qualtrics) (see Appendix A). Interview guides are in Appendix B. Of the 16 invited, 15 individuals representing fellows, PO representatives, and implementers contributed data to at least one component of the evaluation. PO representatives worked in non-profit organizations serving diverse communities, and implementers were experienced staff within DAC.

Data Collection

Data were collected from all 15 participants. Fellows were invited to complete four online surveys (baseline, post-training, midpoint, end-of-program) and to participate in two virtual focus groups. Surveys included 5-point Likert-type items and open-ended questions assessing knowledge and skills in KM and implementation science, clarity of program components, perceived support and communication, collaboration, community engagement, and overall satisfaction. Focus groups explored experiences, perceived facilitators and barriers to participation, and recommendations for improvement. PO representatives completed one-on-one virtual interviews after FP completion, focusing on their experiences partnering with fellows, perceived benefits and challenges, and suggestions for future iterations. Program implementers participated in individual virtual interviews to reflect on implementation processes, relational dynamics, and opportunities to strengthen the FP. Data-collection instruments were pilot-tested for clarity and alignment with study objectives. Focus groups and interviews were conducted via videoconference platforms (Zoom or Microsoft Teams) to support accessibility across time zones and minimize geographic barriers to participation.

Analysis and Rigor

Quantitative survey data were analyzed descriptively in Microsoft Excel using frequencies, percentages, and means. Given the small sample size and missing responses at some time points, analyses were limited to descriptive summaries; no inferential tests were conducted. Trends over time were depicted in bar graphs, and pre-post changes were summarized for fellows with matched baseline and end-of-program responses. Qualitative data from focus groups and interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using a directed content analysis that combined deductive and inductive approaches. 14 A preliminary codebook was developed around three categories about the FP: (i) successes (facilitators, enablers), (ii) opportunities for improvement (challenges, barriers), and (iii) recommendations, aligned with key FP components. Three researchers coded focus group transcripts and refined the codebook through discussion; remaining transcripts were coded independently, with discrepancies resolved by team consensus. Codes were aggregated into broader themes, and illustrative quotations were selected. To enhance trustworthiness, we used team-based coding, detailed documentation of context and analytic decisions, and researchers’ reflexive journaling. Recruitment and all data collection (via interviews and focus groups) involving implementers were led by team members not involved in FP delivery. Analytic decisions were recorded in an audit trail. These strategies helped mitigate potential bias arising from dual roles while acknowledging the evaluation’s embedded nature.

Participant Characteristics

Table 1 shows the demographic characteristics of participants. Fifteen of 16 invited participants contributed to at least one evaluation component (7 fellows, 6 PO representatives, 2 implementers). Most fellows reported living with diabetes (83%), were mostly white (67%), identified as female (50%), a woman (50%), and lived in urban areas (67%). PO representatives came from four community organizations operating at municipal, national, and North American levels. The implementers were two staff members with 3 years’ experience at DAC.

Quantitative Findings

Table 2 and Appendix C (Figures 1-9) highlight survey results. Across domains, fellows’ responses suggested perceived improvements in KM knowledge and FP clarity, support, collaboration, and community engagement over the course of the program. Baseline scores for KM and implementation science terms were in the “slightly knowledgeable” range and increased into the “moderately knowledgeable” range by program completion among fellows with matched responses at baseline. The largest gains were observed in patient engagement in KM activities, in developing a KM plan, and in developing a KM product. Fellows reported increasing clarity regarding program goals, expectations, and training over time, although some mid-program fluctuations were observed. Perceived support and communication from the FP team showed improvement, with fellows reporting greater confidence that they had sufficient information to meet program requirements by the end of the FP. Fellows consistently rated dyadic collaboration highly and reported that working with POs was useful; as the program progressed, they found it increasingly easy to apply KM principles. Ratings of community engagement activities (including co-creating materials with community members and incorporating community feedback) also increased from baseline to program end. Overall satisfaction scores indicated that the FP met or exceeded fellows’ expectations by the final time point.

Qualitative Themes

Ten themes across three categories described participants’ experiences with the FP. 1) Within the Foundation and design category, participants highlighted the value of the dyad model, paid participation, and collaboration with POs in supporting engagement, flexibility, and peer learning. They emphasized the importance of clear structure, timelines, and communication and recommended enhanced project management support and more explicit expectations. Fellows and POs viewed the focus on lived experience and diversity as strengths and encouraged continued efforts to broaden outreach and representation. 2) Within the Program delivery category, participants described strong facilitation, supportive relationships, and helpful tools such as the FP handbook and KM resources. They suggested pacing and simplifying KM training and expanding the topics to include leadership and policy impact. Collaboration emerged as a core feature: dyads fostered mutual support and accountability; fellow-PO partnerships and relationships with the FP lead built trust and shared purpose. Co-design sessions with community members and in-person events were described as meaningful opportunities to deepen relationships and align knowledge products with community needs, while participants noted the need to clarify timelines, scope, and ownership of KM products. Ongoing feedback mechanisms and regular check-ins were seen as integral to monitoring and adaptation. 3) Within the Reflections and future directions category, participants described the FP as meaningful, rewarding, and impactful in their own lives and in their organizations. Fellows reported increased confidence, skills, and a stronger sense of belonging in patient-oriented research, and POs perceived benefits for organizational reach and capacity, particularly for smaller organizations. Many fellows expressed interest in continued involvement as mentors or ambassadors and advocated for formal, compensated alumni roles to support future cohorts. Discussion

This formative evaluation offers early insights into the feasibility and acceptability of a patient-partner-centric KM fellowship in a diabetes research network. Across survey and qualitative data collection, participants perceived that the FP helped build patient partners’ knowledge and skills in KM, strengthened collaboration with community organizations, and fostered psychological safety, trust, and peer support. These findings suggest that structured, compensated fellowships that position patient partners as KM leads rather than advisors may offer a promising pathway to advance equity and shared leadership in patient-oriented research. Compared with existing KM and patient-oriented research fellowships (which typically fund researchers or clinicians and involve patient partners in advisory or collaborative roles),15-17 the FP is distinct because it awards and compensates patient partner fellows directly for their KM leadership. By embedding patient partners in paid, leadership-based roles, the program redistributes power and decision-making authority toward those with lived experience. Our study design aligns with calls for co-production and participatory approaches and offers a concrete example of how an organization can operationalize these principles in practice.

Our evaluation study also underscores the importance of relational infrastructure in patient-partner capacity building. Participants emphasized mentorship, peer dyads, clear communication pathways, and opportunities for co-design with community members as critical to engagement and confidence. Iterative feedback loops and responsiveness to participant input were seen as key to sustaining trust and adapting the program in real time. Similar to findings from other patient engagement initiatives,18,19 participants in our study highlighted the need for ongoing training, organizational support, and connection to other patient partners to enable meaningful participation.

Limitations

This evaluation has several limitations. The sample was small, drawn from a single cohort within one research network and a limited number of POs, and study participants predominantly identified as urban, White, and female. These factors restrict representativeness and limit generalizability to other settings and populations. Quantitative analyses were descriptive and based on small sample sizes, with missing data at some time points. As such, any observed trends should be interpreted with caution as self-reported, preliminary perceptions. Our quantitative findings are descriptive only. The small numbers in the repeated-measures design precluded inferential testing, so any observed changes should be interpreted as preliminary, self-reported trends. The evaluation was embedded within the host organization, and some team members held dual roles in program delivery and evaluation. We used a role-separation strategy, independent moderation, and reflexive documentation to mitigate this risk, but some bias may still have been introduced. While formative evaluation enabled real-time adjustments to the FP, member checking was not feasible, and some data collection and analysis were led by team members with dual roles as program implementers, necessitating robust reflexivity and audit strategies. Finally, as a single-year formative case study, the evaluation could not examine long-term outcomes such as sustained engagement, impacts on organizational capacity, or policy influence. Despite these constraints, the evaluation offered a valuable opportunity for real-time assessment and iterative improvement within a novel program. Evaluations such as ours are vital for understanding not only program outcomes but also the processes and contexts that drive effective patient partnership in healthcare innovation.

Conclusion

The Research-to-Action Fellowship Program demonstrates a feasible and acceptable model for positioning patient partners as compensated leaders in Knowledge Mobilization within a diabetes research network. Participants perceived gains in knowledge, confidence, collaboration, and community engagement, and partner organizations identified benefits for reach and capacity. While findings are preliminary and based on one small cohort, the Diabetes Action Canada Fellowship Program offers early, practice-oriented insight into how patient-partner-centric fellowships can be designed and delivered. Future research should examine larger and more diverse cohorts, longer-term outcomes, and policy and funding mechanisms that support patient partners as KM leaders alongside traditional researcher-focused fellowships.
