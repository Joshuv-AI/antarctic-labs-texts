Beyond RAG: Reinforced Reasoning Augmented Generation for Clinical Notes
Lo Pang-Yun Ting, Chengshuai Zhao, Yu-Hua Zeng, Yuan Jee Lim, Kun-Ta Chuang

Abstract
Clinical note generation aims to automatically produce free-text summaries of a patient’s condition and diagnostic process, with discharge instructions being a representative long-form example. While recent large language model (LLM)-based methods pre-trained on general clinical corpora show promise in clinical text generation, they fall short in producing long-form notes from limited patient information. In this paper, we propose R 2 AG , the first reinforced retriever for long-form discharge instruction generation based on pre-admission data. R 2 AG is trained with reinforcement learning to retrieve reasoning paths from a medical knowledge graph, providing explicit semantic guidance to the LLM. To bridge the information gap, we propose Group-Based Retriever Optimization (GRO) which improves retrieval quality with group-relative rewards, encouraging reasoning leaps for deeper inference by the LLM. Comprehensive experiments on the MIMIC-IV-Note dataset show that R 2 AG outperforms baselines in both clinical efficacy and natural language generation metrics. Further analysis reveals that R 2 AG fills semantic gaps in sparse input scenarios, and retrieved reasoning paths help LLMs avoid clinical misinterpretation by focusing on key evidence and following coherent reasoning.

Introduction

Clinical note generation facilitates efficient communication and decision-making among healthcare professionals and patients, while also reducing the time burden of manually writing reports
Arndt et al. 2017
;
Sinsky et al. 2016
. This has motivated research into automatic clinical note generation based on large language models (LLMs), such as summarizing doctor-patient conversations
Abacha et al. 2023
or radiology reports
Jin et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
, which has achieved notable success. However, many approaches focus on generating short or structured summaries covering specific aspects, such as diagnoses or treatments, rather than rich long-form outputs.

Distribution of keywords among UMLS semantic groups.

Patient discharge instruction
summarizes a wide range of information, including diagnoses, medications, and the patient’s condition throughout hospitalization, and provides essential guidance for post-discharge care. Automatically generating discharge instructions can reduce clinicians’ documentation burden. Moreover, if we can generate a preliminary discharge instruction from pre-admission information, it would provide clinicians with an early snapshot of likely diagnoses, treatments, and follow-up needs before the patient’s hospital stay, serving as a useful reference for decisions made throughout hospitalization. To support this inference process, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
Lewis et al. 2020
techniques, which retrieve relevant knowledge beyond the given input, hold the potential to enable earlier and more informed clinical planning based on anticipated patient trajectories. Despite its significance,
the automatic generation of discharge instructions based on pre-admission information
remains largely underexplored, which motivates the focus of this work.

Generating preliminary discharge instructions based on pre-admission information presents several challenges.
❶ First, it is inherently an open-ended generation task, where the correct content may not be explicitly present in the data. In contrast, most existing medical LLMs, such as MMed-Llama3
Qiu et al. 2024
and PMC-LLaMA
Wu et al. 2024a
, which are pre-trained on general clinical corpora, or RAG models like Almanac
Zakka et al. 2024
, Clinfo.ai
Lozano et al. 2023
, and MedRAG
Xiong et al. 2024a
, are mainly designed for answering questions with direct evidence in a single document or for closed-ended tasks with predefined answer choices. As a result, they may not be well suited to our scenario. ❷ Secondly, there is a significant information gap between pre-admission data and discharge instructions, as the latter typically relies on hospital-stay information. In Figure

, we analyze 500 patients’ discharge summaries from MIMIC-IV-note

. To quantify this gap, we extract keywords from each text and map them to semantic groups in the UMLS (Unified Medical Language System)

Related Works

Medical-Specialized LLMs.

A growing number of medical-specialized LLMs have been pre-trained on clinical corpora. Examples include Meditron
Chen et al. 2023
, ClinicalGPT
Wang et al. 2023
, HuatuoGPT

, BioMistral
Labrak et al. 2024
, PMC-LLaMA
Wu et al. 2024a
, and MMed-Llama3
Qiu et al. 2024
, which have demonstrated improved fluency and factuality across tasks such as ICD coding and short-form clinical QA benchmarks.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation in Medical.
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) techniques play a predominant role in the medical domain by enhancing clinical text generation
Li et al. 2023
;
Zakka et al. 2024
;
Lozano et al. 2023
;
Xiong et al. 2024a
;
Xiong et al. 2024b
;
Wu et al. 2024d
. Recent studies have incorporated knowledge graph (KG) retrieval to guide LLMs toward concise clinical answers. For instance, MindMap
Wen et al. 2023
, Knowledge Seeds
Wu et al. 2024c
, and DR.KNOWS
Gao et al. 2025
retrieve relevant KG triples or paths to prompt the model.
Most focus on questions that have direct answers in a single document or involve selecting limited answer options and short-form outputs such as diagnostic options, probable diseases, or drug recommendations.

Clinical Note Generation.

Other clinical note generation efforts focus on distinct settings, such as summarizing doctor–patient dialogues
Abacha et al. 2023
or generating radiology reports
Jin et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Yin et al. 2019
from X-ray images.

Although the above approaches achieve strong performance within their respective settings, they mainly focus on generating short-form outputs. A few studies have explored the generation of long-form discharge summaries
Li et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024b
;
Williams et al. 2024
;
Ellershaw et al. 2024
, but these efforts typically rely on rich in-hospital data, such as progress notes or complete EHRs, that only become available after a prolonged hospital stay. By contrast, we tackle a more challenging scenario of generating long-form discharge instructions using only pre-admission data and design an RL-based retriever over the medical knowledge graph to augment LLM generation.

3
Methodology

The proposed model
R
einforced
R
easoning
A
ugmentation for Clinical Note
G
eneration,
R
2
AG
, is comprised of two main components:
(1)
Retrieval Network
: An RL-based retriever that selects semantic groups, followed by concept retrieval within the selected group to construct reasoning paths over the medical KG.
(2)
Group-Based Retriever Optimization
: A training strategy that optimizes the retriever using a combination of rewards, ensuring that the retrieved reasoning paths are informative and effective in guiding accurate long-form discharge instruction generation.

3.1
Basic Setup

3.1.1
Problem Definition.

Formally, a medical knowledge graph (KG) is represented as
\mathcal{G}=\{(c,r,c^{\prime})\mid c,c^{\prime}\in C,r\in R\}
, where
C
is the set of medical concepts and
R
is the set of relations.
\mathcal{G}^{k}
denotes semantic groups, with each concept
c\in\mathcal{C}
belongs to a group
k\in\mathcal{G}^{k}
based on its semantic. Given a patient’s pre-admission information
Q
, we use QuickUMLS
(
Soldaini and Goharian 2016
)
, a tool for extracting and linking medical terms to UMLS concepts, to obtain a set of medical keywords
S^{Q}
, which are then mapped to corresponding concepts in
\mathcal{G}
. Our goal is to retrieve reasoning paths from
\mathcal{G}
that guide the LLM to generate accurate discharge instructions. To initialize the paths, we identify the semantic group
k_{\text{init}}
that covers the most keywords in
S^{Q}
, and define an initial path
P^{q}_{0}=\{c_{0}=q\}
for each
q\in k_{\text{init}}
. Therefore, given
Q
,
\mathcal{G}
, and initial reasoning paths
\{P^{q}_{0}\}_{q\in k_{\text{init}}}
, the objective is to retrieve and extend paths that augment LLM generation for accurate discharge instruction.

3.1.2
Retrieval Environment Formulation.

For each concept
c\in\mathcal{G}
and each semantic group
k\in\mathcal{G}^{k}
, we assign a vector embedding
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
to represent the states and actions of the retriever.

State.
To represent the currently retrieved information, we formulate the
group state
and
concept state
, respectively.

Group State
s^{k}_{t}
.

To incorporate semantic group information at step
t
, the group state representation
s^{k}_{t}
is constructed based on both the currently selected group
k_{t}
and a scarce group
k_{\text{scarce}}
, which is defined as the group with the fewest keywords in
S^{Q}
. This design encourages the retriever to reason not only within the current group but also toward underrepresented semantic areas, promoting the discovery of informative paths.

\mathbf{s}^{k}_{t}=[\mathbf{k}_{t}\,\|\,\mathbf{k}_{\text{scarce}}],

(1)

where
\mathbf{k}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
denotes the embedding of the current visited group, and
\mathbf{k}_{\text{scarce}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
denotes the embedding of the scarce group. The symbol
\|
represent embedding concatenation. Specifically,
\textbf{k}_{t}
contains state-dependent information while
\textbf{k}_{\text{scarce}}
are the context shared by all states.

Concept State
s^{c}_{t}
.
To preserve all explored concepts, the concept state representation at time step
t
is defined as:

\mathbf{s}^{c}_{t}=\mathbf{M}\cdot\text{avg}(\{\mathbf{c}=\text{encoder}(c)|c\in C_{t}\}),

(2)

where
C_{t}
denotes the concepts explored up to step
t
. Each concept
c
is encoded using a pretrained SapBERT encoder
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
, which is trained on the UMLS dataset. The matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a learnable projection.

Action.
The set of possible actions
A^{k}_{t}
consists of the neighboring groups of the previously visited group
k_{t-1}
in
\mathcal{G}
. When selecting an action
a^{k}_{t}=(k_{t-1}\rightarrow k_{t})\in A^{k}_{t}
, the model transitions from group
k_{t-1}
to group
k_{t}
. Each action is represented directly by the embeddings of the previously visited and the selected groups, formulated as follows:

\mathbf{a}^{k}_{t}=[\mathbf{k}_{t-1}\|\mathbf{k}_{t}].

(3)

Note that in our setting, we assume that every pair of groups in
\mathcal{G}
is connected by a relation at each step
t
, enabling the model to perform reasoning leaps across semantic groups and encourages exploration beyond local concept boundaries. At each step, the agent is allowed to stay in the current group to retrieve
sufficient
information for the future retrieval. Thus, the number of available actions is
|A^{k}_{t}|=|\mathcal{G}^{k}|
for each group
k
at each step
t
. Details of the reward design will be presented in the subsequent sections.

3.2
Retrieval Network

Our reinforced retriever aims to retrieve reasoning paths from the medical KG by deciding when to select semantically similar concepts and when to apply reasoning leaps across groups, forming the two levels of retrieval, as shown in Figure
2
.

Semantic-Level Retrieval.
Following the RL paradigm, our retrieval process is guided by a policy network
\pi_{\theta}
, which determines whether to apply a reasoning leap to activate the predictive capability of the LLM. Specifically, it decides
which semantic group to visit next
based on the current state (Figure
2
(a)). Thus, to jointly consider the group and concept state representations
(\mathbf{s}^{k}_{t},\mathbf{s}^{c}_{t})
, we employ a two-layer feedforward network to compute the hidden representation
\mathbf{z}_{t}
derived from the concatenated state representations. Based on
\mathbf{z}_{t}
, the policy distribution
\mathbf{b}_{t}
over the action space
A^{k}_{t}
is then estimated, reflecting the probability of selecting each possible action (group) at step
t
given the current states. The hidden representation
\mathbf{z}_{t}
and the policy distribution
\mathbf{d}_{t}
are as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{t}=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|s^{k}_{t},s^{c}_{t})=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{A}^{k}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{z}_{t}),

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{t}=\mathbf{W}_{2}\cdot\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_{1}[\mathbf{s}_{t}^{k}||\mathbf{s}_{t}^{c}]),

where
\mathbf{W}_{1},\mathbf{W}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{4d\times 4d}
are the learnable weights,
\mathbf{A}^{k}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{|A^{k}_{t}|\times 4d}
represent the embeddings of next possible actions
A^{k}_{t}
. Based on
\mathbf{d}_{t}
, the action
a_{t}
, which indicates the next group
k_{t}
to visit, is selected as follows:

a_{t}\sim\text{categorical}(\textbf{d}_{t}).

(5)

Figure 2:
The illustration of two levels of retrieval to formulate a reasoning path.

Concept-Level Retrieval.

Once the next semantic group
k_{t}
is selected by the policy network, the retriever proceeds to
identify concepts within this group to extend the

reasoning paths.
This step grounds the high-level group selection in a concrete concept-to-concept transition within the medical KG. We mainly have two actions for retrieving concepts in the selected group
k_{t}
, as shown in Figure
2
(b).

Connect.

If the selected group
k_{t}
differs from
k_{t-1}
, we first establish a connection between them. Let
C_{\text{cand}}
denote the set of concepts in
k_{t}
that either appeared in previous paths or belong to unexplored keywords in
S^{Q}
. We then select a connection point
c_{\text{leap}}\in C_{\text{cand}}
to link with the previous path
P^{q}_{t-1}=\{c_{0}(q),...r_{t-1},c_{t-1}\}
start from keyword
q\in S^{Q}
. The connection point is chosen based on maximum cosine similarity with the path embedding:
c_{\text{leap}}=\arg\max_{c\in C_{cand}}(\text{sim}(\textbf{c},\textbf{P}^{q}_{t-1}))
, where
\mathbf{c}
is the embedding of concept
c
, and
\mathbf{P}^{q}_{t-1}
is the average embedding of concepts in path
P^{q}_{t-1}
. The path is then updated as
P^{q}_{t}=P^{q}_{t-1}\cup\{\text{``group leap''},c_{\text{leap}}\}
.

Retrieve.
After establishing the connection, we retrieve new concepts by selecting concepts from the neighbors
C_{\text{neighbor}}
of
c_{\text{leap}}
within group
k_{t}
. These new concepts provide semantically novel yet coherent information that extends and supports the prior reasoning path, guiding the LLM to perform reasoning leaps and activate its predictive capability.

Let
\mathbf{S}^{Q}
be the average embedding of all keywords in
Q
, and
\mathbf{P}^{q}_{t}
the average embedding of concepts in path
P^{q}_{t}
. The new concept
c_{t}
for
P^{q}_{t}
is selected as:

c_{t}=\arg\max_{c^{\prime}\in C_{\text{neighbor}}}\big[(\mathbf{c}^{\prime},\mathbf{S}^{Q}),(\mathbf{c}^{\prime},\mathbf{P}^{q}_{t})\big]_{\text{sim}},

(6)

where
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\text{sim}}
denotes the average of the cosine similarities between the two pairs of embeddings.
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
is the embedding of candidate concept
c^{\prime}
. Therefore, the path is updated as
P^{q}_{t}=P^{q}_{t}\cup\{r_{t},c_{t}\}
, where
r_{t}
denotes the relation connecting
c_{\text{leap}}
and
c_{t}
in graph
\mathcal{G}
.

3.3
GRO: Group-Based Retriever Optimization

To ensure that the retrieved reasoning paths effectively enhance LLM generation, the reward is provided when the reasoning paths reaches the maximum length. This delayed feedback (episodic reward) allows the model to evaluate the overall quality of complete paths in supporting long-form discharge instruction generation.

Mixture of Rewards.

In our work, the reward is designed to evaluate and refine each reasoning path based on two criteria:
❶ it contains concepts that appear in the ground-truth discharge instruction (ground-truth concepts), directly contributing to accurate LLM outputs; and
❷ it includes semantically related concepts that can guide the LLM toward generating relevant and coherent content.

To ensure these objectives are reflected in the episodic rewards, we adopt the following design. First, inspired by recent formulations of verifiable rewards
Lambert et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2025
, we introduce a binary reward that assigns a value of 1 if the path contains any ground-truth concept. Second, we incorporate a soft reward based on the embedding similarity between the concepts explored in the path
P
and the ground-truth concepts
\hat{C}
. Thus, the reward for a reasoning path
P
is formulated as:

R_{P}=\sum_{c\in P}\mathbb{I}\{c\in\hat{C}\}+\lambda\cdot\text{sim}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\hat{C}}),

(7)

where
\lambda
is a weighting factor.
\mathbf{P}
denotes the average embedding of concepts in
P
, and
\mathbf{\hat{C}}
denotes the average embedding of the ground-truth concept set
\hat{C}
.
\text{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
represents the cosine similarity.
\mathbb{I}\{\cdot\}
is the indicator function, which returns 1 if
c
belongs to
\hat{C}
, and 0 otherwise.

Group-Based Optimization.

After each episode, the policy network is updated based on the explored paths and received rewards. Inspired by GRPO
Shao et al. 2024
, we adopt its idea of using multiple rollouts per input to estimate relative rewards. Based on this, we propose the
GRO
mechanism (
G
roup-Based
R
etriever
O
ptimization) to further improve the quality of retrieved reasoning paths under sparse episodic rewards, ultimately enhancing LLM generation. This also stabilizes learning by better attributing credit across entire reasoning paths. Specifically, for each patient, we conduct multiple retrieval processes with the number of
G
, and the relative reward for each retrieval path
\{P^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{G}
is formulated as:

\tilde{R}_{P^{(i)}}=\frac{\exp(R_{P^{(i)}})}{\sum_{j=1}^{G}\exp(R_{P^{(j)}})}.

(8)

The optimization aims to maximize the expected cumulative return.
We revise the REINFORCE algorithm
(
Williams 1992
)
by using discounted cumulative returns based on relative rewards from a set of sampled retrieval paths
\{P^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{G}
:

{J(\theta)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\{P^{(i)}\}^{G}_{i=1}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t}^{T}\gamma^{(T-t)}\cdot\tilde{R}_{P^{(i)}}\right],}

(9)

where
T
is maximum path length and
\gamma\in[0,1]
is the discount factor. Then, the policy network
\pi_{\theta}
is updated via the gradient of the objective:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=

(10)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\{P^{(i)}\}^{G}_{i=1}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a^{(i)}_{t}|s^{(i)}_{t})\cdot\gamma^{(T-t)}\tilde{R}_{P^{(i)}}\right].

Finally, given a well-trained retriever with policy
\hat{\pi}_{\theta}
, KG
\mathcal{G}
, a new patient’s pre-admission information
Q^{\prime}
, and initial semantic group
k^{\prime}_{\text{init}}\in\mathcal{G}^{k}
covers the most keywords in
Q^{\prime}
, the reasoning paths
\{P^{q}\}_{q\in k^{\prime}_{\text{init}}}\sim\hat{\pi}_{\theta}
are retrieved from
\mathcal{G}
. The LLM
\mathcal{M}
then generates the ideal discharge instruction
\hat{\mathcal{I}}
using our
R
2
AG
model as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{I}}=\operatorname{R^{2}AG}(Q^{\prime};\mathcal{M},\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\mathcal{G})

(11)

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\Big(\mathcal{I}\>\big|\>Q^{\prime},\{P^{q}\}_{q\in k^{\prime}_{\text{init}}}\sim\hat{\pi}_{\theta}\Big).

Baselines.

To verify our proposed model, we compare with three groups of baselines:

•

Vanilla LLMs
include LLaMA-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen-UMLS-7B, Mistral-7B, for generation and pre-admission information as prompt.

•

Medical-Domain LLMs
include LLMs pre-trained or instruction-tuned on biomedical literature, clinical notes, or medical QA corpora, including ChatDoctor-7B
Li et al. 2023
, Med-Alpaca-7B
Shu et al. 2023
, Meditron-7B
Chen et al. 2023
, Biomistral-7B
Labrak et al. 2024
, PMC-LLaMA-13B
Wu et al. 2024a
, and MMed-Llama-3-8B
Qiu et al. 2024
.

•

Retrieved-Based Methods
consider two one-hop neighbor retrieval baselines. Both identify KG concepts structurally connected to keywords extracted from the pre-admission information: one randomly selects one-hop neighbors, denoted as “Random1hop”, and the other selects those most semantically similar to the full pre-admission input, denoted as “Sim1hop”. The retrieved concepts and original input are fed into base LLMs (LLaMA-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen-UMLS-7B, Mistral-7B) for generation. We also compare with DR.KNOWS
Gao et al. 2025
, which performs path-based retrieval on the KG, using Flan-T5-Large (its original base model), LLaMA-3.1-8B, and Mistral-7B for generation. These methods are denoted as “Retrieval Method + Base Model” (e.g., “Sim1hop + LLaMA-3.1-8B”, “DR.KNOWS + Flan-T5-Large”).

Evaluation Metrics.

We evaluate these models with two types of metrics to compare generated and reference (true) discharge instructions:

•

Clinical Efficacy (CE)
: We evaluate the keywords and medical concepts identified in the generated texts using precision, recall, F1 score, Hamming loss, and Jaccard similarity. These metrics focuse on assessing the correctness of medical relevant word generation.

•

Natural Language Generation (NLG)
: We report ROUGE-1/2/L
Lin 2004
, BLEU-1/2
Papineni et al. 2002
, METEOR
Denkowski and Lavie 2011
, BERTScore (precision, recall, and F1), and Sentence-BERT
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
similarity scores to measure the fluency and semantic consistency between generated and reference discharge instructions.

Conclusion

This paper introduces a novel model,
R
2
AG
, a RL–based framework that retrieves reasoning paths to guide LLMs in generating discharge instructions merely using pre-admission information. By leveraging a medical knowledge graph,
R
2
AG
enables structured semantic retrieval through reasoning leaps, bridging the information gap in early-stage clinical scenarios. To further enhance retrieval quality, we propose
GRO
, a group-based optimization strategy that utilizes relative rewards across multiple paths. Experimental results on the MIMIC-IV-Note dataset demonstrate that our method outperforms baseline approaches in both clinical efficacy and natural language generation. The case study further validates the effectiveness of our RL-based retrieval, showing that
R
2
AG
helps LLMs avoid clinical misinterpretation and generate accurate, coherent discharge instructions. Our work highlights the potential of RL to improve retrieval-guided clinical note generation under limited information settings.

Limitations

While
R
2
AG
