Retrieval of a Free-Floating Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair Clip in a Giant Left Atrium
Authors: Ruitao Hou, Munire Zaibi, Jiayi Weng, Zhongshan Gou, Iokfai Cheang, Haifeng Zhang, Tianbao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDevice embolization during transcatheter edge-to-edge repair is a rare and catastrophic complication. Retrieval can be particularly challenging in a giant left atrium (LA), where jet-driven device motion may render active “chasing” ineffective.Case SummaryA DragonFly clip completely detached in a 75-year-old man with a giant LA (anteroposterior diameter ∼10 cm). The device moved erratically, driven by eccentric regurgitant jets, making active pursuit unsuccessful. We switched to a fluoroscopy-guided passive capture strategy after identifying a consistent turning/impact zone on the lateral atrial wall. The snare was stationed at this specific site to await and intercept the recirculating clip, which allowed for subsequent orientation and atraumatic extraction.DiscussionThis case illustrates the use of a practical “floating clip” phenomenon in a giant LA. When fluoroscopy demonstrates a repeatable device trajectory, passive capture at a reproducible turning/impact zone may facilitate device retrieval and support continued percutaneous management in selected cases.Key words: device embolization, giant left atrium, mitral regurgitation, percutaneous retrieval, snare technique, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair

History of Presentation

A 75-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a 10-day history of worsening exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea. He had a 10-year history of recurrent chest tightness and palpitations that had been managed conservatively. Ten days before admission, his symptoms worsened markedly, accompanied by headache and an inability to lie flat. He denied acute chest pain, cough, or syncope.

On examination, he was afebrile with a blood pressure of 124/93 mm Hg and a respiratory rate of 22 breaths/min. A pulse deficit was present, with an auscultated heart rate of 127 beats/min and a palpable pulse rate of approximately 80 beats/min. Lung auscultation revealed coarse breath sounds with bibasilar crackles. Cardiac examination showed an irregularly irregular rhythm with an accentuated pulmonic component of the second heart sound (P2), consistent with pulmonary hypertension. No prominent murmur was detected on initial bedside examination. There was no lower extremity edema.

The admission electrocardiogram demonstrated atrial fibrillation with a rapid ventricular response (126 beats/min).

Past Medical History

The patient had a notable cardiovascular history, including a 10-year history of grade 3 hypertension, with systolic blood pressure previously recorded as high as 180 mm Hg; he was currently adhering to a regimen of sacubitril/valsartan (50 mg twice daily). He had a 10-year history of permanent atrial fibrillation, managed with rivaroxaban (15 mg once daily) for stroke prophylaxis. He carried a long-standing clinical diagnosis of hyperlipidemia (atorvastatin 20 mg daily) and had a 3-year history of heart failure. His surgical history included a left submandibular cyst excision in 2003. He denied diabetes mellitus, tobacco use, or alcohol consumption.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential for acute decompensated heart failure included ischemia, pulmonary embolism, and severe valvular disease, as well as tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy.

Investigations

Initial laboratory testing revealed mild thrombocytopenia (platelets: 102 × 109/L), creatinine 105.3 μmol/L, total bilirubin 32.7 μmol/L, and D-dimer 1.92 mg/L. The admission electrocardiogram demonstrated atrial fibrillation with a rapid ventricular response (126 beats/min) and nonspecific ST-T changes. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a giant left atrium (LA; anteroposterior diameter approximately 10 cm),1 left ventricular dilation (end-diastolic diameter: 66 mm) with diffuse hypokinesis (left ventricular ejection fraction: 42%) and severe eccentric mitral regurgitation due to asymmetric leaflet tethering and annular dilation (Figures 1A to 1C, Videos 1 and 2). Chest computed tomography showed marked cardiomegaly with bilateral pleural effusions. Coronary angiography revealed no significant stenosis.

Management

Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) with a DragonFly transcatheter mitral valve repair system (Valgen MedTech) was performed under general anesthesia via the right femoral vein. Approximately 15 minutes after the deployment of the first clip at the A2-P2 segment (Figures 1D to 1F, Video 3), transesophageal echocardiography indicated a single-leaflet device attachment (SLDA), which rapidly progressed to complete bilateral leaflet detachment. The fully closed clip separated from the valve, becoming a free-floating embolus in the giant LA.

Retrieval strategy: from active pursuit to passive capture

Procedural access was maintained through the 24-F DragonFly steerable guide sheath. The retrieval system comprised two 20-mm single-loop snares (Snare-20, Shanghai Shape Memory Alloy Co Ltd). A 5-F, 125-cm MPA2 angiographic catheter (Cordis) was advanced through the sheath to facilitate snare manipulation and directional control. The retrieval process evolved in 2 phases. Phase 1 (active pursuit) proved futile: For the initial 15 minutes, the massive LA dimensions (approximately 10 cm in diameter) and high-velocity regurgitant jet caused the clip to circulate rapidly along the atrial wall. Catheter manipulation consistently lagged behind the clip's rapid trajectory, rendering active capture attempts unsuccessful (Video 4). Consequently, the team shifted to phase 2 (passive capture). Continuous fluoroscopy suggested a repeatable circulation pattern: The clip periodically approached and contacted a consistent region of the lateral atrial wall before recirculating. Based on this trajectory, the snare was fully opened and held stationary at the apparent turning/impact point, using the atrial wall as a backstop. Within approximately 5 minutes, the circulating clip entered the open snare loop and was captured at its mid-arm (Figure 2A, Video 5).

Extraction: the “double-lock” technique

Simple retraction failed because the clip, grasped at its arm, adopted a transverse “T-shape” orientation. In this orientation, the long axis of the clip exceeded the sheath diameter, precluding entry, and forcible retraction risked injury to the atrial septum and venous vasculature. To resolve this, a dual-snare strategy, with one snare for stabilization and the other for positional adjustment, was adopted. Initially, the primary snare captured the clip at its mid-arm, retracting it to the guide sheath tip. With the device stabilized, a second 20-mm snare was introduced to grasp the atrial cap (approximately 4-5 mm from the tip). The primary snare was then released and repositioned to encircle the cap alongside the second snare, establishing a robust “double-lock mechanism” (Figure 2B, Video 6). This configuration enforced a coaxial “nose-first” orientation (Figure 2C), enabling smooth retraction into the guide sheath (Figure 3, Video 7).

Outcome

Given the patient's frailty and the risks associated with the giant LA, surgical conversion was deemed prohibitively high risk. No further clip implantation was attempted. The patient was managed conservatively and was discharged on guideline-directed medical therapy.

Discussion

Device-related complications after TEER most frequently present as SLDA, reported in 1.5% to 4.8% in large registries.2,3 Complete detachment with free embolization into the LA is uncommon, and percutaneous retrieval remains nonstandardized. In the present case, embolization was likely multifactorial: Suboptimal leaflet insertion depth with marginal initial grasping may have contributed to mechanical instability in the setting of asymmetric leaflet tethering, annular dilatation, and severe eccentric mitral regurgitation, ultimately allowing progression from SLDA to complete detachment. Against this background, the giant LA created an additional challenge for retrieval and motivated the stepwise bailout strategy described here.

Published experience suggests that retrieval strategy is largely influenced by: 1) whether the device remains tethered to the delivery system; and 2) the dominant extraction barrier (capture vs coaxial sheath entry).4,5 When the clip remains connected via the gripper line, the tether may serve as a fail-safe and can permit controlled traction or snare-assisted rescue.6 For completely detached clips, active pursuit is described but can be prolonged in high-flow chambers.7 Furthermore, after capture, the device often assumes a transverse “T-shape” that prevents sheath entry.5,6 Percutaneous solutions typically involve either upsizing to large-bore sheaths to accommodate the bulk or using dual-snare techniques to enforce a coaxial profile, often requiring additional access.5

A giant LA amplifies device excursion, producing chaotic tumbling that renders active pursuit inefficient. However, the fluid dynamics in this case provided a unique advantage. Unlike the central regurgitation typically seen in functional mitral disease, the patient's asymmetric leaflet tethering generated a high-velocity eccentric jet. Retrospectively, the trajectory of the device may be explained by the Coanda effect, whereby the regurgitant stream adheres to the curved atrial surface (“wall-hugging jet”).8 The free-floating clip, entrained within this flow, was driven along the atrial perimeter to a predictable impact zone on the lateral wall. Recognizing this hydrodynamic pattern prompted the operators to abandon active pursuit in favor of passive capture.

Once the clip is captured, sheath entry becomes the dominant geometric challenge. Stripe et al5 reported that a 2-snare approach can enforce a “nose-first” profile; in that report, large-bore sheath exchange and additional access were used to accommodate device bulk and facilitate maneuverability. The “double-lock” technique applies the same principle of active geometric reconfiguration while prioritizing directional control within the original access: One snare stabilizes an arm to limit rotation, and a second snare captures the proximal tip (device nose) to enforce strict coaxial alignment. In the present case, this configuration enabled atraumatic extraction through the original 24-F guide sheath, and may prove feasible in selected anatomies when geometric constraints allow.

Device-specific sheath characteristics may also be relevant to bailout retrieval. As summarized in Table 1, the DragonFly guiding sheath used in the present case and the MitraClip G4/G5 steerable guide catheter (Abbott) are similar in overall roles but differ in lumen dimensions. For double-snare retrieval, the practical implication is that successful capture alone does not guarantee safe sheath re-entry; device-sheath compatibility and strict coaxial alignment remain critical. Notably, the MitraClip steerable guide catheter has a smaller inner diameter (16 F, ≈22.1 mm2) compared with the DragonFly guiding sheath (21 F, ≈38.5 mm2), which may further increase the geometric difficulty of dual-snare retrieval and coaxial device extraction.

Limitations of this report include the single-case design and dependence on a stable, repeatable fluoroscopic trajectory; without consistent recurrence of a turning/impact zone, passive capture may not be feasible. Similarly, extraction through the original sheath may not be generalizable across device models, orientations, or access conditions, and surgical standby remains prudent. Nonetheless, in the setting of a giant LA, retrieval of embolized devices may be better approached as a trajectory-and-geometry problem rather than as a purely mechanical pursuit. The combination of fluoroscopy-guided passive capture and dual-snare “double-lock” alignment may offer a feasible percutaneous bailout pathway in selected patients.

Conclusions

In selected patients with a giant LA, fluoroscopy-guided passive capture followed by dual-snare (“double-lock”) alignment may constitute a viable percutaneous bailout option when a reproducible trajectory and favorable geometry are present.
