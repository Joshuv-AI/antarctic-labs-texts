PLGA-PEG Nano-Adjuvant-Delivered ClfA Vaccine Elicits IL-17A-Mediated Neutrophil Activation to Confer Complete Protection Against Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Zhuoyue Shi, Zhuo Wan, Guodong Tan, Jiayue Xi, Minxuan Cui, Yufei Hou, Zhengjun Ma, Nan Sun, Yupu Zhu, Muqiong Li, Dong Wang, Xin He, Qian Yang, Chaojun Song, Li Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMethicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a major cause of severe, life-threatening infections worldwide, highlighting an urgent and unmet need for effective vaccines. Current Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) vaccine candidates predominantly employ aluminum-based adjuvants, which potently induce Th2-biased humoral immunity but fail to adequately elicit protective Th1/Th17 cellular responses. Given that Th1 and Th17 responses are critical for clearance of S. aureus infection, developing an adjuvant platform capable of redirecting immunity toward these pathways is urgently needed. To address this limitation, we aimed to develop a novel nano-vaccine and systematically evaluate its immunogenicity and protective efficacy.MethodsWe constructed a nano-vaccine using PLGA15k-PEG5k-COOH nanoparticles (25%NPs) as the adjuvant and recombinant clumping factor A (rClfA) as the antigen. The immunogenicity of the 25%NPs-rClfA vaccine was compared with that of an aluminum-adjuvanted formulation. Specific antibody levels, neutralizing activity, and cytokine production (IFN-γ and IL-17A) were measured. Protective efficacy was assessed by challenging immunized subjects with a lethal dose of S. aureus strain ATCC25923. Mechanistic studies included evaluation of neutrophil phagocytosis and reactive oxygen species (ROS) release. The necessity of the Th17 pathway was confirmed via IL-17A blockade experiments.ResultsCompared to the aluminum-adjuvanted vaccine, the 25%NPs-rClfA nano-vaccine elicited lower specific antibody titers but generated antibodies with superior neutralizing activity. It significantly enhanced the secretion of IFN-γ and IL-17A, with IL-17A sustaining elevated levels over an extended period. Crucially, the nano-vaccine conferred 100% protection against death following lethal S. aureus challenge. Mechanistically, it enhanced neutrophil phagocytosis and ROS production—key processes for bacterial clearance. Blockade of the Th17 pathway abrogated vaccine protection, demonstrating that IL-17A is essential for its efficacy.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that the PLGA-PEG nanoparticle-based rClfA nano-vaccine can effectively redirect immune responses toward protective Th1/Th17 immunity and robustly protect against lethal S. aureus infection, largely through an IL-17A-dependent mechanism. These findings provide important experimental and theoretical support for the translational development of S. aureus vaccines.Keywords: Staphylococcus aureus, vaccine, nano-adjuvant, interleukin-17A, neutrophil, reactive oxygen species

Introduction

Of the myriad challenges confronting modern medicine, the relentless rise of antimicrobial resistance represents a paramount threat, heralding a precarious post-antibiotic era.1 Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) stands as a formidable protagonist in this narrative, responsible for life-threatening infections that evade conventional therapeutics.2–4 Approximately 30% of adults carry S. aureus in the nasal cavity, which can cause skin and soft tissue infections and may invade deeper tissues and organs, leading to bacteremia and even severe septic shock, with a mortality rate of up to 25% in serious infections.5

Current treatment options for S. aureus infections rely primarily on antibiotics. Vancomycin, daptomycin, linezolid, and ceftaroline are among the frontline agents used to treat MRSA infections. In parallel, recent studies have identified novel small-molecule inhibitors targeting key bacterial proteins such as FmtA,6,7 LLM,8 and YsxC,9 offering potential new leads for antibacterial drug development. However, the rapid development of multidrug resistance has made the situation even more dire. An estimated 1.27 million deaths were directly attributable to antibiotic resistance in 2019, with MRSA showing the largest increase in mortality, directly causing 130,000 deaths in 2021.10 Consequently, the development of a prophylactic MRSA vaccine has been pursued as a critical strategy to curb the prevalence of this superbug.11 Over the past decades, numerous vaccine candidates have entered clinical trials, targeting a range of surface antigens and secreted virulence factors.12–14 For example, Nabi’s StaphVax, a capsular polysaccharide vaccine targeting CP5 and CP8, was evaluated in a Phase III trial with 3600 hemodialysis patients.15,16 The results showed no statistically significant difference in infection rates between the vaccinated group and the placebo control.16–18 Similarly, Merck developed V710, a recombinant subunit vaccine based on the IsdB antigen.19 Despite inducing a robust immune response in cardiothoracic surgery patients during its Phase II/III trial, it failed to demonstrate protective efficacy.16,20,21 Likewise, Pfizer’s SA4Ag, a multi-component vaccine containing ClfA, MntC, CP5, and CP8, also proved unsuccessful.22 Although it effectively stimulated antibody production in a Phase IIb study, no significant reduction in infection rates was observed compared to placebo.15,23,24

These failures reveal several recurring shortcomings. First, most of these candidates relied on alum or no adjuvant, inducing Th2-biased humoral immunity but failing to elicit cellular responses essential for S. aureus clearance. Second, even when antibodies are induced, their quantity does not necessarily reflect protective quality; the neutralization capacity of antibodies demands greater scrutiny. Third, the intrinsic complexity of S. aureus, including its large and redundant virulence factor repertoire, high strain diversity, and facultative intracellular survival, renders antibody-alone strategies insufficient. Importantly, the concept of antigenic imprinting may further explain these failures: most humans are primed by early exposure to S. aureus, generating pre-existing non-protective memory responses (eg, high titers of low-quality anti-IsdB antibodies), which can be preferentially recalled by recombinant protein vaccines, actively suppressing de novo protective immunity.25,26

Yet, despite considerable investment and effort, not a single MRSA vaccine has successfully navigated the path to clinical licensure. This repeated failure underscores a fundamental gap in our understanding of the correlates of protection against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), suggesting that the prevailing vaccine paradigms may be inadequate.27–29

A critical analysis of previous vaccine endeavors reveals several potential shortcomings. Historically, the design of MRSA vaccines has been heavily skewed towards eliciting robust antibody titers and potent humoral immunity.30 While desirable, this narrow focus has often overlooked the indispensable roles of cellular and innate immune arms.31 Evidence is mounting that sterilizing immunity against S. aureus may be unattainable, and protection instead relies on the coordinated action of multiple immune components.32–34 Specifically, the neutralization capacity of induced antibodies, rather than their mere quantity, is a crucial parameter that demands greater scrutiny. Furthermore, the activation of T helper 17 cells, a key orchestrator of innate defense, emerges as a particularly promising avenue.35,36 Th17-derived IL-17 is pivotal for recruiting and activating neutrophils, the primary phagocytic cells responsible for clearing staphylococcal infections.37–40 Therefore, a vaccine capable of driving a potent and sustained Th17 response could significantly enhance neutrophil-mediated bacterial killing, a feature largely absents from alum-adjuvant vaccines that preferentially induce high-level neutralizing antibodies.41–43 Finally, the quest for durable protection necessitates the exploration of vaccine platforms that can confer long-lasting immunity, a challenge that remains largely unaddressed.

In this context, we reasoned that a next-generation MRSA vaccine must be rationally designed to co-engage the humoral, cellular, and innate immune systems in a synergistic manner. Drawing on our previous work in biomimetic nano-vaccine adjuvants, we developed a novel platform inspired by the physical properties of the S. aureus capsule. We engineered PLGA nanoparticles with tunable stiffness to mimic the native pathogen and covalently conjugated them with a panel of staphylococcal secreted and surface-anchored proteins.44–46 From this screen, we identified a candidate antigen, ClfA, which, when combined with our PLGA-PEG-based adjuvant, formed a cohesive vaccine system.

Our findings demonstrate that this nano-vaccine induces lower antibody responses than traditional alum-adjuvant vaccines but, more importantly, generates antibodies with superior neutralizing activity.44,47 Crucially, it elicits a profoundly enhanced cellular and innate immune response, characterized by a robust Th17 polarization and substantial IL-17 production. This Th17 bias translates into a remarkable in vivo protective efficacy against a challenge with twice the lethal dose of S. aureus. Mechanistic studies confirm that the vaccine-induced IL-17 surge potently enhances neutrophil recruitment and, critically, high neutralizing antibodies enhance neutrophil phagocytosis through Fc receptor, while increased IL-17A promotes ROS-mediated bacterial killing (Figure 1). This multi-faceted immune engagement, bridging adaptive Th17 immunity with innate neutrophil effector functions, underpins the potent and durable protection afforded by our biomimetic nano-vaccine, offering a promising new strategy in the protracted battle against MRSA.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Commercial sources for all materials were as follows: BL21(DE3)-competent E. coli (TIANGENBIOTECH, Beijing, China); Ni-Sepharose (Cytiva, Shanghai, China); Bradford protein assay kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China); and PLGA15000-PEG5000-COOH polymer (Xi’an Ruixi Biological Technology, Xi’an, China). The following reagents were procured from Merck (Shanghai, China): Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-carbodiimide (EDC), N-Hydroxy succinimide (NHS), 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzo thiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG, and 0.45 µm sterile filters. Additionally, Isopropylbeta-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), imidazole, dichloromethane (DCM), acetone, 2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid hydrate (MES), Tween 20 were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent (Shanghai, China). Other reagents included: Alhydrogel™ adjuvant (Croda, UK); 96-well ELISA plates (Corning, NY, USA); ELISPOT kits (Mabtech, Stockholm, Sweden); flow cytometry antibodies—including PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-mouse Ly6G, APC anti-mouse CD3, PerCP anti-mouse CD4, and PE anti-mouse IL-17A (BioLegend, San Diego, USA); the Th17 inhibitor (CZC24832, MedChemExpress, NJ, USA); and a Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Detection Kit (Biyuntian, Shanghai, China).

Preparation of Nanoparticles

25%NPs were synthesized by solvent emulsion volatilization method. Firstly, an organic phase was prepared by dissolving 100 mg of the material in 2.5 mL of a 3:2 (v/v) blend of dichloromethane and acetone. After complete dissolution, it was dropped vertically into 10 mL of 0.5% PVA solution, and the solution was stirred overnight at 600 rpm to mix well. The mixed liquid was ultrasonic under ice bath conditions for 2 min and then solidified by dropping it vertically into 40 mL of pure water to facilitate solvent evaporation and continuous stirring at 600 rpm overnight. The NPs were isolated by filtration and centrifugation at 18,000×g for 1 h at 4°C. The obtained NPs were washed once with pure water, then the PLGA-PEG 25%NPs were harvested by discarding the supernatant and lyophilized for storage.

Production of Recombinant ClfA Antigen

The recombinant ClfA (rClfA) pET-28a expression vector constructed by Beijing Tsingke Biotech. Recombinant ClfA (rClfA) expression was achieved by introducing the plasmid into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells, and induced with IPTG. Bacteria were collected, resuspended in pure water and broken by ultrasonication to release soluble rClfA. rClfA was purified by Ni2+-NTA affinity chromatography using a gradient elution with a buffer containing imidazole. The purity and concentration of purified rClfA was determined by SDS-PAGE and BCA. Endotoxin was removed using an EtEraserTM Endotoxin Removal Resin (Xiamen Bioendo Technology, Xiamen, China). The residual endotoxin level was determined by the Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) assay, with an acceptance criterion of <0.1 EU/μg protein.

Synthesis and Characterization of Nano-Vaccine

Take 10 mg of lyophilised 25% NPs, dissolve in 10 mL of 25 mM MES buffer solution, add 1 mol/L EDC 400 μL and 1 mol/L NHS 250 μL to activate the carboxyl terminus of the nanoparticles and centrifuge at 600 rpm for 3 hours at room temperature. The activated 25% NPs were resuspended with 1 mg/mL of antigen and mixed overnight at 4°C with rotation. On the following day, the vaccine was centrifuged at 4°C, 14000 rpm for 45 min, the supernatant was discarded, washed once with PBS, and finally resuspended in PBS to obtain the 25%NPs-rClfA vaccine. The 25%NPs-rClfA were characterized using dynamic light scattering for hydrodynamic size, PDI, and zeta potential. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy confirmed successful conjugation, and antigen loading was quantified by the Bradford method.

Animal Immunization

Female BALB/c mice (aged 6–8 weeks) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. They were randomly assigned to four groups (n=10 per group) using a computer-generated random number sequence: PBS, rClfA, Alum-rClfA, and 25%NPs-rClfA. Mice were immunized subcutaneously with a 25 μg dose of rClfA or its respective formulated equivalent. Booster immunizations were given on days 0, 14 and 28. Two independent cohorts of mice were used: blood serum was collected from the first cohort on day 35, and from the second cohort on day 180. All animal experiments were conducted with blinding where feasible. Group allocation and immunization were performed by an investigator aware of the treatment groups due to visible differences in formulation appearance. However, outcome assessments (survival monitoring, bacterial load quantification, histopathology evaluation, and flow cytometry analysis) were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation. For histopathology, slides were coded and evaluated by a certified pathologist blinded to the groups. For flow cytometry, samples were labeled with coded numbers, and gating was performed before unblinding.

Evaluation of in vitro and in vivo Biocompatibility of Nano-Vaccine

L929 cells were seeded in 96-well plate (100 μL/well) and incubated for 24 h. Then, the culture medium was aspirated, and cells were exposed to concentration gradients (7.8 μg/mL to 1 mg/mL) of rClfA, 25%NPs, or the nano-vaccine. Cell viability was subsequently quantified with the CCK-8 assay (n=6 per group). To evaluate systemic toxicity, the body weights of the immunized mice were recorded every 7 days following the primary immunization. On day 35, the mice were anesthetized with 1% isoflurane via inhalation and subsequently euthanized via cervical dislocation. Heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were harvested and evaluated for potential tissue toxicity of the nano-vaccine using H&E staining (n=3 per group).

Measurement of Antibody Titer by ELISA

Serum antibody titers were determined by indirect ELISA on days 35 and 180 post-immunization using sera from the 6 randomly selected mice per group. Briefly, 96-well plates were coated with rClfA antigen and then blocked. Serially diluted mouse serum in 0.1% casein was added to the plates and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. After washing, with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse antibodies for 45 min at 37°C. The color reaction was developed using ABTS and H2O2 as substrates, and absorbance readings at 410 nm were obtained (Biotek SYNERGY LX).

Detection of Cytokine Secretion by ELISPOT

Splenocyte secretion of cytokines was assessed by ELISPOT using splenocytes from the same 6 mice per group. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 1% isoflurane via inhalation and euthanized by cervical dislocation prior to spleen harvest on days 35 and 180 (n=6 per group). Isolated splenocytes were then stimulated and assayed for IL-4, IFN-γ, and IL-17A secretion. First, pre-coated plates were washed and incubated with complete RPMI-1640 medium for 30 min. Subsequently, splenocytes (1.0 × 106 cells/well) from immunized mice were stimulated with rClfA (4 μg/mL) or medium control and cultured for 24 h. After incubation, washed and incubated with detection antibodies for 2 h. Wash the plate 5 times and add TMB substrate until distinct spots appear then rinse the plate with plenty of purified water. Quantify spots using an ELISPOT reader (Cellular Technology Limited).

Lethal Challenge

To assess the immunization efficacy, a lethal challenge was performed on day 35, wherein mice received an intravenous injection of S. aureus (ATCC 25923) at LD100 (2.56 × 109 CFU/mL) or 2×LD100 (5.12 × 109 CFU/mL) (n=10 per group). Although the experimental endpoint was mortality, stringent humane endpoint criteria were applied to minimize suffering. Mice exhibiting signs of severe distress (eg, prostration or dyspnea) were humanely euthanized before succumbing to the infection (Table S1). The same procedure was repeated on day 180 post-immunization to evaluate long-term immunity.

Assessment of Neutrophil Phagocytic Activity

Seven days post-third immunization, mice were deeply anesthetized with 1% isoflurane inhalation, and peripheral blood was collected via retro-orbital puncture (n=3 per group). Neutrophils were isolated using Ficoll density gradient centrifugation (25°C, 1000 × g, 25 min). Purity of isolated neutrophils (Ly6G⁺ cells) was >90% as determined by flow cytometry, and viability (trypan blue exclusion) was >95% immediately prior to use. Isolated neutrophils were resuspended in medium at 1×105 cells/mL and added into 96-well round (U)-bottom plate (200 μL/well). Subsequently, 10 μL of mouse serum from each group and 5 μL of GFP-expressed S. aureus (with an OD600 value of 0.5) were added sequentially, followed by 2 h co-culture in 37°C with 5% CO2. After that, external bacteria were removed by repeated PBS washing. Cells were subsequently labeled with an anti-Ly6G antibody and analyzed by flow cytometry (FCM) (Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX). Concurrently, a subset of cells was fixed, stained with DAPI, and subjected to laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) for visual assessment of bacterial uptake (Olympus FluoViewTM FV300).

Inhibition of Th17 Differentiation

The mice after three immunizations were randomly divided into two groups, with or without CZC24832 (Th17 inhibitor). To inhibit the production of IL-17A, mice were orally administered a Th17 inhibitor suspension (prepared in 10% DMSO) at a dose of 1 mg/kg twice daily (every 12 hours), starting two days prior to S. aureus challenge and continuing until the death of the mouse or the end of the experiment. On day 35 post-immunization, S. aureus ATCC 25923 (4 × 108 CFU/mL in 100 μL) was delivered to mice by tail vein injection. Three days post-infection, mice were humanely euthanized, and spleens were aseptically harvested. A single-cell suspension of spleen was prepared and resuspended in PBS at 1 × 106 cells/mL and stained with fluorescently labeled surface antibodies (APC-CD3, PerCP-CD4), mix thoroughly, and incubated in dark at 4 °C for 30 min. After washing twice, Cytofix/Cytoperm solution was added and incubated at room temperature in dark for 20 min. Following fixation, the intracellular antibody (PE-IL-17A) was added and incubated for 30 min. Finally, centrifuge and wash twice, discard supernatant, resuspend cells in 200 μL PBS and immediately detect and analyze by FCM.

Measurement of Reactive Oxygen Species

The mice after three immunizations were divided into two groups, with or without CZC24832. All immunized mice were injected with 4×108 S. aureus (ATCC25923) via the tail vein. Peripheral blood was collected from deeply anesthetized mice prior to euthanasia, and neutrophils were isolated using Ficoll density gradient centrifugation, the cell suspension was diluted to 1 × 106 cells/mL. Purity (>90%) and viability (>95%) of isolated neutrophils were verified before use. Transfer it to a new tube and resuspend them with 1mL of dihydroethidium (DHE) staining solution as a single-cell suspension. Subsequently, the cells were incubated at 37°C in the dark for 20 min. At the end of incubation, the stained cells are washed 1–3 times with PBS to remove any residual staining solution. The treated cells are ready for direct analysis by FCM.

Bacterial Plate Counting

All immunized mice were injected with 4×108 S. aureus (ATCC25923) through tail vein. After humane euthanasia at 12 h post-infection, the liver, spleen, and peripheral blood were harvested from mice in each group, and the organ homogenate was diluted and spread plate counting method was used (n=6 per group).

Statistical Analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± SD and subjected to statistical analysis with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. For quantitative data involving multiple groups, normality was evaluated via the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was checked through Levene’s test. Comparisons among three or more experimental groups were performed using one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. Survival significance was analyzed via the Log rank test. Fluorescence intensity was measured and quantified with Image J. A p-value of less than 0.05 was taken to indicate a significant difference.

Results

Synthesis and Characteristics of Nano-Vaccine System

The nano vaccine system, composed of PLGA-25%PEG NPs as adjuvant and recombinant protein rClfA of S. aureus as antigen, was established as our previous nano vaccine preparation method44,47 (Figure 2a). The morphology of the nano-vaccine (25% NPs-rClfA) as well as the pure 25% NPs adjuvant were well studied by SEM and DLS. As shown in Figure 2b–d, the nano-vaccine presented spherical shape with average diameter of 170.967 ± 2.603 nm (n = 3) and negative charged surface (zeta potential ~-20.150 ± 0.436 mV, n = 3), with small increase in diameter and slightly more negative in zeta potential compared with 25% NPs. The PDI was 0.12 ± 0.02 (n = 3). The trend was similar with other nano vaccines using the same protocols as we published before.44,47 The rClfA confirmed by SDS-PAGE was conjugated with 25% NPs by covalent bonding (Figures 2e, f and S1). The conjugation efficiency of rClfA to 25% NPs was 1.29 ± 0.17% (Figure S2). The purified rClfA antigen contained less than 0.1 EU/μg protein as determined by the Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) assay, meeting the acceptance criterion for in vivo experiments.

Biosafety Evaluation of 25% NPs and Nano-Vaccine

The biosafety issues were first evaluated both in vitro and in vivo, the overall experimental procedure is shown in Figure 3a. The CCK-8 assay was employed to demonstrate the non-cytotoxicity of the NPs and nano-vaccine, and the results showed that the cell viability of both 25% NPs and 25%NPs-rClfA groups were above 90% (Figure 3b). Moreover, we monitored the mice weight changes after vaccination and steady increase of the mice weight were found in both 25% NPs and 25%NPs-rClfA groups (Figure 3c). The H&E staining of major organs collected at day 35 after first vaccination showed that no lesions in heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney in the NPs and nano-vaccine (Figure 3d), further demonstrating the biosafety of the nanoparticles and nano-vaccine.

The Immune Efficacy and Protection Ability Evaluation of Nano-Vaccine

After confirming the biosafety of the NPs and nano-vaccine, we further investigated the immune efficacy of the nano-vaccine, including both humoral and cellular immune response activation efficacy. The mice were randomly divided into 4 groups, including the blank control PBS group, negative control free antigen rClfA group, positive control Alum-rClfA group and the nano-vaccine 25%NPs-rClfA group. The vaccination was conducted under standard vaccine administration schedule (Figure 4a). Blood samples for antibody titer ELISA assay were collected at day 35 after the first vaccination. The rClfA-specific IgG titer of Alum-rClfA was the highest in all treatment groups while the titer of PBS and free rClfA groups were hardly found (Figure 4b). The positive and negative control groups showed corresponding reasonable results indicating the feasibility of the assay method and reliability of the data. Surprisingly, the titer of 25% NPs-rClfA group was significantly higher than that of PBS and free antigen groups, but lower than the Alum-vaccine, which exhibits discrepancy with our previous studies47 (Figure 4b). The amount of cytokines release was further investigated by ELISPOT using spleen cells of all treatment groups, in order to study the cellular response activation. Similar with the antibody titer results, the IL-4 spots number per spleen cell in Alum-vaccine and nano-vaccine groups were significantly higher than that in PBS and free antigen groups (Figure 4c and h). Due to the IL-4 secretion reflected the humoral response activation level, similar trend found in antibody titer results and the IL-4 specific ELISPOT results was reasonable, which demonstrated that the humoral response could be significantly enhanced after Alum and nano-vaccine administration. In the cellular immune response activation aspects, the IFN-γ cytokine release representing the Th1 cellular immune response activation level, was also investigated using ELISPOT in all treatment groups. Similar with the antibody titer and the IL-4 secretion results, both Alum and nano-vaccine groups could elicit significant IFN-γ secretion. However, opposite with the humoral immune response activation data, the spots amount of nano-vaccine (25% NPs-rClfA) was significantly higher than that of Alum ones. It was pointing out that the 25% NPs serves as the vaccine adjuvant could elicit stronger cellular response activation than the Alum ones (Figure 4d and h). IL-17A secretion levels were also investigated in all treatment groups. The difference of IL-17A secretion between the nano-vaccine group and the Alum one was even higher than that of IFN-γ (Figure 4e and h), indicating that the 25%NPs served as nano-vaccine adjuvant could more effectively elicit the secretion of IL-17A, resulted in elevated recruiting and activating neutrophils. In summary, although the 25%NPsrClfA induced lower antibody titers than the Alum adjuvanted formulation, it elicited substantially higher levels of IFN-γ and IL-17A, demonstrating a superior capacity to induce Th1/Th17 cellular immunity compared to Alum-rClfA. It could be inferred that besides the protection of neutralizing antibody and Th1 cells activation, the S. aureus killing efficiency of neutrophil enhanced by the IL-17A secreted from Th17 will be another important contribution for the protection against S. aureus.48,49

In order to testified the hypothesis, lethal challenge experiments were then carried out at the lethal dose of S. aureus in the immunized mice of all treatment groups. As expected, the mice of PBS and free rClfA groups die out in 2 days and 60% of mice in Alum-rClfA group survived (Figure 4f). Surprisingly, 100% of the mice in 25% NPs-rClfA group survived at the end of lethal challenge (Figure 4f). In order to further testify the protection ability of the nano-vaccine, the challenge dose increased to double lethal dose of S. aureus. In this case, all mice in PBS, free antigen and Alum-vaccine groups died out in just 2 days (Figure 4g). However, 60% of mice in 25% NPs-rClfA group still survived after the challenge experiment (Figure 4g). We compared the lethal challenge results with Alum-rClfA and 25% NPs-rClfA groups. No matter in the lethal or the double lethal dose challenge experiment, the survival percentage of the mice in 25% NPs-rClfA group is significantly higher than the Alum-rClfA group (Figure 4f and g).

We further evaluated the long-term immune activation efficacy of nano-vaccine with prolonging the sampling date at day 180 after first vaccination, the vaccination was conducted under standard vaccine administration schedule (Figure 5a). Similar with the data of day 35 after first vaccination, the antibody titer of both Alum and nano-vaccine is significantly higher than that of PBS and free antigen treatment groups (Figure 5b). Moreover, the antibody titer level of Alum-vaccine was still significantly higher than that of nano-vaccine (Figure 5b), which is consistent with the data at day 35. However, the IL-4 secretion elicited by nano-vaccine was much higher than that of Alum-vaccine (Figure 5c). Comparing the humoral immune activation data (antibody titer and IL-4 secretion) with day 35 and 180, it could be obviously found that at day 180, the level of IL-4 secretion in the Alum-vaccine group showed a significant decrease. In contrast, the IL-4 secretion level of nano-vaccine group remained relatively higher (or the decline was significantly smaller compared to the Alum-vaccine), indicating that it may possess the capacity for long-term activation of the humoral immune response. This phenomenon has also been verified in the comparison of antibody titer levels between the two groups.

Also, we studied the IFN-γ and IL-17A secretion at day 180 after first vaccination. Similar trend was found in day 180 compared with day 35, both IFN-γ and IL-17A secretion of nano-vaccine group were the highest in all treatment groups (Figure 5d, e and g), indicating that the nano-vaccine may have the ability for long-term cellular immune response activation.

However, 60% of the mice in nano-vaccine group survived after 14 days challenge (Figure 5f), suggesting that the nano-vaccine possessed longer time protection ability compared with the Alum-vaccine.

Superior Neutralizing Activity Antibodies Enhance Neutrophil Phagocytosis of S. aureus

In order to figure out the reason for the excellent protection efficacy of nano-vaccine, we further investigated neutrophils, which plays key role in bacterial clearance and can be effectively activated by IL-17A.5,35 As mentioned before, the antibody titer of nano-vaccine group is lower than the Alum-vaccine group, but the cytokines elicited by nano-vaccine, which represented for cellular immune response, are significantly higher than those of Alum-vaccines. Moreover, the level of IL-17A, which secreted by Th17 cells, is the highest in nano-vaccine treatment group. It was a direct guidance for us to study the activation of neutrophils in all treatment groups. The neutrophils (labeled with Ly6G) of each group were collected from peripheral blood and then co-cultured with S. aureus (expressed with GFP) and serum from the immunized mice. Fluorescent technics including flow cytometry and confocal microscopy were employed to observe the phagocytosis of neutrophils and S. aureus (Figure 6a). As expected, the percentage of double positive cells (GFP+Ly6G+) in nano-vaccine group is the highest in all treatment groups, ~2 times higher than the Alum-vaccine group (Figure 6b and c). IL-17A Promotes S. aureus Clearance by Enhancing ROS Levels

To investigate the role of IL-17A in the specific immune response against S. aureus, we used the Th17 inhibitor CZC24832, which can selectively suppress PI3K signaling, downregulate the activity and expression of RORγt, thereby achieving the goal of inhibiting Th17 cell differentiation and IL-17A production.51 The mice were divided into two benches, with or without CZC24832 adding with the same grouping as mentioned above. All immunized mice were injected with S. aureus expressing GFP via the tail vein. After 72 hours, splenocytes were collected for flow cytometry detection to determine the proportion of IL-17A⁺ cells among CD4⁺T cells. The results showed that the proportion of CD4⁺IL-17A⁺ double-positive cells in the spleen of the nano-adjuvant vaccine group was approximately twice that of the Alum-adjuvant vaccine group, and significantly higher than that of the other groups. This indicated that the nano-adjuvant vaccine could induce more Th17 cell differentiation. After administration of CZC24832, the proportion of CD4⁺IL-17A⁺ double-positive cells in the nano-adjuvant vaccine group decreased by 59% significantly compared with the CZC24832 blank control group, while that in the Alum-adjuvant vaccine group only decreased by 19%. These results suggested that the proportion of S. aureus-specific Th17 cells induced by the nano-adjuvant vaccine was significantly higher than that induced by the Alum-adjuvant vaccine (Figure 7b and c).

The results showed that without the use of Th17 inhibitor, the level of ROS produced by neutrophils in the nano-adjuvant vaccine group was significantly higher than that in the Alum-adjuvant vaccine group. After using the CZC24832, the ROS level of neutrophils in the nano-adjuvant vaccine group decreased significantly (~50%), while the ROS level in the Alum-adjuvant vaccine group changed slightly (Figure 7d–f). The above results indicated that following subcutaneous immunization, the nano-adjuvant vaccine triggers a cascade in which APCs present the antigen to CD4⁺ T cells, driving their differentiation into Th17 cells and the secretion of IL-17A, which in turn significantly enhances neutrophil ROS production and ultimately boosts bacterial killing (Figure 7a).

Th17 Inhibitor Leads to Significant Decline in the Protective Efficacy of Nano-Vaccine

To systematically verify the core role of the Th17 pathway in in vivo immune protection against S. aureus, this study used S. aureus ATCC25923 as the challenge strain. A mouse infection model was established via intravenous challenge, with bacterial load and mouse survival rate as the core indicators to evaluate the Th17 pathway’s impact on vaccine protective efficacy. The mice were divided into two groups (with or without CZC24832 treatment), following the same grouping as mentioned above. All immunized mice were injected with ATCC25923 via the tail vein.

For the detection of organ bacterial load, the organ homogenate dilution and spread plate counting method was used to quantitatively analyze the bacterial load in the spleen, liver, and peripheral blood of mice after challenge. The results showed that without the Th17 inhibitor, both vaccines effectively reduced the bacterial load in mice. However, after adding CZC24832 to inhibit the Th17 pathway, significant differences emerged between the two groups: the bacterial load in the spleen, liver, and blood of the nano-adjuvanted vaccine group increased by approximately 10-fold, 27-fold, and 26-fold respectively compared with the group without the Th17 inhibitor. This suggests that its bacterial clearance ability was severely impaired due to Th17 pathway blockage (Figure 8a–c). In contrast, the Alum-adjuvanted vaccine group showed a much smaller increase in bacterial load after inhibitor treatment—only about 7.5-fold, 9.6-fold, and 3-fold increases in the spleen, liver, and blood respectively. This indicates that its bacterial clearance process is far less dependent on the Th17 pathway than the nano-adjuvanted vaccine.

In the lethal challenge experiment, mouse survival rate was observed and recorded continuously for 14 days. The results showed that without Th17 pathway inhibition, the survival rate of the nano-adjuvanted vaccine group reached 100%, and the Alum-adjuvanted vaccine group also exhibited a certain level of protective efficacy (60%). After inhibiting the Th17 pathway, the survival rate of the nano-adjuvanted vaccine group significantly decreased from 100% to 30%, with a substantial attenuation of protective effect. In contrast, the survival rate of the Alum-adjuvanted vaccine group decreased from 60% to 40%, showed no statistically significant change (Figure 8d).

The above results further confirm that the in vivo protective efficacy of the traditional Alum-adjuvanted vaccine mainly relies on antibody-mediated humoral immunity and has no obvious dependence on the Th17 pathway. In contrast, the nano-adjuvanted vaccine has a dual protective mechanism: in addition to humoral immunity, it also requires activating the Th17 pathway to enhance neutrophils’ bactericidal ability. These two mechanisms work synergistically to achieve efficient in vivo protection against S. aureus.

Discussion

The recurrent failure of S. aureus vaccine candidates, despite robust antibody induction, underscores a critical flaw in traditional vaccinology paradigms centered predominantly on humoral immunity.52,53 Our study demonstrates that shifting the immune focus from mere antibody quantity to the quality of the adaptive response and engagement of innate effector mechanisms can yield superior protection. The PLGA-PEG nanoparticle (25%NPs)-based rClfA nano-vaccine developed here, while eliciting lower antigen-specific IgG titers than an alum-adjuvanted counterpart, conferred 100% survival against a lethal S. aureus challenge. This potent protection is not attributed to antibody titers alone but is mechanistically rooted in the vaccine’s unique capacity to drive a potent and sustained Th17-polarized cellular immune response, culminating in enhanced neutrophil bactericidal activity. Beyond these adaptive mechanisms, the concept of antigenic imprinting further explains why previous vaccines failed. Tsai et al demonstrated that prior exposure to S. aureus induces a non-protective humoral imprint, characterized by elevated IL-10 production and hyper-α2,3 sialylation of antibodies, which interferes with the phagocytic-promoting properties of vaccine-elicited antibodies. This imprinting phenomenon provides a plausible explanation for the failure of the IsdB-based V710 vaccine and likely other alum-adjuvanted candidates. Importantly, the PLGA-PEG nanoparticle platform may circumvent this imprinting by promoting activation of protective Th17 responses, rather than recalling pre-existing non-protective memory.54 This work thus provides direct experimental evidence that a next-generation S. aureus vaccine must co-opt both arms of adaptive immunity and functionally link them to innate defense for efficacy.

The most significant finding of this study is the identification of the IL-17A/neutrophil axis as the cornerstone of the nano-vaccine’s protective efficacy. While alum-adjuvanted rClfA induced higher antibody titers, our nano-vaccine elicited significantly stronger and more durable IFN-γ and, most notably, IL-17A secretion. The functional indispensability of this Th17 response was unequivocally proven: pharmacological inhibition of Th17 differentiation abrogated the nano-vaccine’s protective efficacy, drastically increasing bacterial organ burden and slashing survival rates from 100% to 30%. This aligns with and extends previous work establishing CD4+ T cell-derived IL-17A as essential for protection against mucosal and systemic staphylococcal infections.37,38 Our data move beyond correlation by mechanistically linking vaccine-induced IL-17A to the enhanced functional state of neutrophils—the primary cellular executors of S. aureus clearance.5

The enhanced protection is explained by a dual mechanism converging on neutrophils. First, the antibodies induced by the nano-vaccine, albeit lower in titer, exhibited superior neutralizing activity. This likely enhanced opsonization and Fc receptor-mediated phagocytosis (ADCP) by neutrophils, as visualized by increased uptake of GFP-expressed bacteria.39 Second, and crucially, the high levels of IL-17A promoted by the nano-vaccine amplified the neutrophil’s bactericidal capacity. We showed that neutrophils from nano-vaccinated mice produced significantly higher levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), a key microbicidal mechanism.55–57 This ROS burst was profoundly dependent on the Th17 pathway,58 as it was sharply reduced upon inhibition with CZC24832. Therefore, our nano-vaccine orchestrates a synergistic defense: high-quality antibodies promote bacterial uptake, while IL-17A “arms” the neutrophils to efficiently kill the internalized pathogens. This coordinated action bridges the adaptive Th17 response with innate effector function, a strategy largely absent from alum-based formulations that favor a Th2-biased, antibody-centric response with limited cellular activation.31

The innovative use of PLGA-PEG nanoparticles as an adjuvant is central to this redirected immune polarization. Unlike alum, which poorly promotes MHC class II presentation and Th1/Th17 responses, nanoparticle platforms are known to enhance antigen uptake by dendritic cells and promote lymph node trafficking, facilitating potent CD4+ T cell priming.44,46,59 Our findings, consistent with our prior work,47 confirm that the physicochemical properties of 25%NPs favor a Th17-skewed microenvironment. The durability of both IL-17A responses and protective immunity at day 180 (Figure 5) suggests the establishment of long-lived Th17 memory, a critical advantage over the transient protection from antibody-based vaccines. This positions PLGA-PEG nanoparticles not merely as passive antigen carriers but as active immune modulators capable of instructing a protective cellular profile essential against intracellular and difficult-to-clear pathogens like S. aureus. Moreover, PLGA is FDA‑approved for clinical use. Regarding scale‑up, microfluidic manufacturing platforms enable consistent production of PLGA nanoparticles with reproducible size and drug loading.60

This study also refines the understanding of correlates of protection against S. aureus. The field’s historical over-reliance on antibody titers as a primary endpoint is challenged by our data, where higher titers (Alum group) did not translate to superior survival.18,20 Instead, the functional quality of the antibody response (neutralizing capacity) combined with a specific cellular signature (Th17/IL-17A) emerged as a stronger predictor of in vivo efficacy.61 Furthermore, antibody glycosylation patterns, which critically determine Fc-mediated effector functions such as opsonophagocytic activity, may also contribute to the protective efficacy observed with the nano‑vaccine despite its lower antibody titers.62,63 This supports the growing consensus that sterilizing immunity may be unattainable, and protection relies on a multi-layered host response that limits infection and facilitates rapid bacterial clearance.32

Nevertheless, certain limitations must be acknowledged to contextualize these promising findings. First, the proof-of-concept was established using a methicillin-sensitive S. aureus laboratory strain (ATCC 25923). The efficacy of this platform against clinically prevalent and often more virulent MRSA strains, including community-acquired (CA)-MRSA, warrants urgent investigation. Second, the study was conducted exclusively in a murine model. While mice are invaluable for mechanistic studies, interspecies differences in neutrophil biology, IL-17 signaling, and S. aureus pathogenesis necessitate validation in more predictive models, such as rabbits or humanized mice, before clinical translation.5 Third, we did not evaluate tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells in barrier tissues such as the skin, though systemic IL-17A responses were sustained and S. aureus-specific IL-17A-producing TRM cells are abundant in healthy human skin.29 Future work should address these gaps by expanding challenge strain diversity and employing advanced preclinical models.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this work provides a compelling experimental and theoretical framework for developing effective S. aureus vaccines. We demonstrate that a biomimetic nano-vaccine platform can overcome the limitations of traditional adjuvants by eliciting a synergistic immune response characterized by high-quality neutralizing antibodies and a dominant, durable Th17/IL-17A pathway. The resultant potent activation of neutrophil phagocytosis and ROS-mediated killing constitutes a mechanistic blueprint for protection. By shifting the paradigm from humoral-centric to a coordinated cellular-innate strategy, our study offers a promising and rationally designed path forward in the protracted battle against MRSA infections.
