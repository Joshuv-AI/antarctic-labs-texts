Quadruple parametric optical mapping to measure cardiac metabolism-excitation-contraction coupling
Authors: Amber Mills, Sharon Ann George
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metabolism-excitation-contraction coupling (MECC) refers to the interdependent facets of cardiac physiology working synchronously to generate each heartbeat. Simultaneous imaging of MECC parameters: transmembrane potential (Vm), intracellular calcium (Ca2+) and metabolic markers (NADH, FAD), provides a wholistic picture of cardiac physiology. Quadruple parametric optical mapping of Vm, Ca2+, NADH and FAD is performed during pharmacological (nifedipine, flecainide, FCCP) or pathological (ischemia/reperfusion) perturbations. Vm and Ca2+ are imaged using fluorescent dyes (RH237 and Rhod2-AM) along with NADH and FAD autofluorescence. While nifedipine alters only Ca2+-related parameters, flecainide and FCCP modulates all three aspects of MECC. The sequence of modulation of MECC parameters in response to FCCP and ischemia/reperfusion was also determined. Using this technique, we demonstrate known and novel cardiac responses to nifedipine and flecainide. We determine chronological changes in MECC with FCCP and ischemia/reperfusion to understand the interplay between electrical, mechanical, and metabolic functions in the heart and mechanisms underlying arrhythmogenesis.

Introduction

The origin of cardiac imaging traces back to the early 1900s when George Mines utilized cinematography to capture the dynamics of a frog heart1. The field of optocardiography, since then, has undergone advancements encompassing 3D printing, panoramic imaging, optogenetics, and multi-parametric optical mapping of the heart. Optocardiography employs light-based methods to study cardiac physiology, offering insights into the dynamics of electrical excitation, which triggers changes in membrane potential, intracellular signaling molecules such as calcium, and metabolic substrates such as NADH and FAD. Traditionally, transmembrane potential (Vm) is studied using a glass pipette microelectrode, but this technique has limitations in spatial resolution and requires technical precision2,3. In 1976, Salama and Morad performed the first optical recording of Vm in the heart4.

Optical mapping began with measurements of Vm and, over the years, has expanded to include NADH, intracellular ions such as calcium, sodium, potassium, magnesium, and oxidative stress markers5–12. More recently, this technique has been advanced to perform combined measurements of multiple parameters by dual and triple parametric optical mapping6,9,11,13–15. While it is important to study each of these parameters individually in healthy and diseased hearts, complex cardiac dynamics in diseased hearts usually impact multiple factors of cardiac function, and their interactions are missed when only one parameter is measured at a time. For this reason, it has become more popular to simultaneously measure excitation-contraction coupling and, more recently, metabolism-excitation-contraction coupling (MECC) in the heart in response to drug treatment and diseases.

Cardiac physiology is remarkably complex, with electrical, mechanical, and metabolic components intricately changing from beat to beat. Electrical activity involves the influx and efflux of ions through ion channels and pumps, which result in depolarization and repolarization of the cell membrane. Depolarization of the cell causes the opening of calcium ion channels that trigger a calcium-induced calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, cytoskeletal binding of these calcium ions, and mechanical contraction. These physiological processes produce a high energy demand in the heart that requires a constant supply of ATP16. To properly supply the heart with ATP, mitochondria make up almost half of a cardiomyocyte's volume17. If metabolism in these mitochondria is chronically impaired, electrical and contractile functions can be affected, and arrhythmias and heart failure can ensue18. Thus, the relationship between metabolism, Vm, and contraction (MECC) is fundamental to understanding cardiac physiology and is essential to study as a whole.

In this study, we add to the current literature of multiple-parameter optical mapping by imaging Vm, calcium transients, NADH, and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) simultaneously, from the same field of view. This quadruple parametric optical mapping approach is an advancement of our previously published triple mapping technique for Vm, intracellular calcium, and NADH imaging. The addition of FAD offers several advantages. Both NADH and FAD are cofactors in cellular respiration and energy metabolism. FAD is a cofactor for metabolic reactions of the flavin protein that utilizes multiple redox states to shuttle electrons19. The resulting electron transfer is important for maintenance of the redox balance, and calculating the ratio of FAD to NADH gives the redox ratio of the tissue. Thus, the addition of FAD allows us to assess the oxidative burden in the tissue, which was not possible with the previous technique. Furthermore, FAD is more stable than NADH, providing a consistent recording between animals or during different experimental conditions, and removing the need for complex normalization processes that were previously applied20,21. This is the first study to report simultaneous optical measurement of oxidative stress and electrophysiology in the heart. Technically, the addition of the single optical channel was not a trivial task. The spectrum of each of the four optical channels and excitation lights in the quadruple parametric optical mapping approach was finely adjusted and selected to prevent signal overlap, and validation studies to confirm signal integrity were performed. Furthermore, the hardware design to house the quadruple mapping system (Fig. 1) was significantly different from that of previous versions of multiparametric imaging to allow for the implementation of a tandem lens, episcopic, four-channel imaging system. All four parameters measured in this technique of quadruple parametric optical mapping are intertwined to affect cardiac function and MECC. In this study, we demonstrate the utility of quadruple parametric optical mapping in cardiotoxicity testing of known pharmacological agents – nifedipine, flecainide, and FCCP. We validate the ability of the technique to identify known and expected effects of these drugs on cardiac physiology and additionally demonstrate the significance of this approach by identifying novel effects that were previously unknown. Lastly, we also map out the chronological modulation of various aspects of cardiac MECC during FCCP and ischemia/reperfusion studies that lead to or set up conditions for arrhythmogenesis.

Results

Design and validation of the quadruple parametric optical mapping system

Cameras 1, 2, 3, and 4 were set up to record NADH, FAD, Ca2+, and Vm, respectively. The measurement of each of the twelve parameters of cardiac physiology measured in this study is illustrated in Fig. 1D, E. From Vm sensitive dye signals, Vm rise time (RT), action potential duration (APD80), transverse and longitudinal conduction velocity (CVT and CVL, respectively) and anisotropic ratio (AR) were calculated. From intracellular calcium indicator signals, Ca2+ RT, calcium transient duration (CaTD80), calcium decay constant (Ca τ), and voltage-calcium activation delay (Vm-Ca2+) delay were measured. As for NADH and FAD intensity, Fig. 1E shows the normalization of each intensity value to the first recording (Control or Baseline at 200 ms BCL) of that individual heart to limit experimental variability.

As a first step, signals in the four channels were assessed in order to ensure that there was no significant overlap or crossover between the four optical channels. Vials of 1.25 mg/mL RH237 dye, 1 mg/mL Rhod2-AM dye, 500 µg/mL NADH solution, 500 µg/mL FAD solution, DMSO, and MilliQ water were placed in the field of view and brought into focus, one at a time. Both the excitation light sources were turned on, and the intensity values within the pixels capturing the vials were recorded (n = 5). When the NADH solution was imaged, Camera 1 alone recorded a significant increase in intensity compared to Blank (DMSO was used for the blank, Fig. 2, p < 0.05). Similarly, Camera 2 intensity was increased when FAD solution was imaged, Camera 3 intensity increased when Rhod2-AM was imaged, and Camera 4 intensity increased when RH237 was imaged. Thus, each camera recorded significantly elevated intensities when its respective dye or solution was imaged, and signal overlap was minimal (Fig. 2, p < 0.05).Fig. 2Validation of the separation of optical channels.Intensity values obtained in the four cameras while imaging of NADH solution, FAD solution, Rhod2-AM (Ca2+ indicator dye), or RH237 (voltage-sensitive dye) individually during illumination with both excitation light sources are illustrated (n = 5/group).

Measuring MECC during pharmacological manipulation with nifedipine and flecainide

The four optical signals were measured from Langendorff-perfused mouse hearts during control and nifedipine perfusion. The modulation of each of the 12 parameters, at varying BCLs, is reported in Fig. 3C. Nifedipine significantly increased Ca2+ RT. The chronological changes in MECC parameters after FCCP treatment are summarized in Fig. 5C until it resulted in arrhythmia (Fig. 5D). Redox ratio was the first parameter to be significantly modulated, as early as 1 min after FCCP treatment due to NADH depletion and FAD accumulation (not significant). This was followed by a significant decrease in NADH and an increase in Vm RT at 2 min, decreases in CVT and Vm-Ca delay at 3 min, a decrease in CVL and an increase in Ca RT at 4 min, and an increase in FAD at 5 min. Most of these effects were sustained until the heart went into arrhythmia. The earliest incidence of arrhythmia in a heart was 3 min after FCCP perfusion, and by 10 min all 5 hearts were in arrhythmia. Optical recordings were not obtained after a heart was in arrhythmia.

Measuring MECC during pathological manipulation with ischemia/reperfusion

Lastly, a separate set of hearts was subjected to 5 min of no-flow ischemia followed by 5 min of reperfusion with Tyrode’s solution without BDM. During ischemia and reperfusion, optical imaging was performed at 1-min intervals to determine the sequence of change in MECC parameters. Whole heart maps of Vm activation time, Ca2+ RT, NADH, and FAD intensities at baseline, ischemia (5 min), and reperfusion (5 min) are illustrated in Fig. 6A, and time-dependent modulation of each of the 9 measured parameters are reported in Fig. 6B. First, 5 min of no-flow ischemia altered both metabolic and electrical excitation parameters in mouse hearts (Fig. 4C). Vm RT (4.529 to 7.469 ms) and NADH (1.000 to 1.294) increased within 1 min of ischemia and remained elevated throughout the ischemic period (Fig. 6B). Discussion

We present here the first report of a quadruple parametric optical mapping approach to measure Vm, intracellular Ca2+, NADH, and FAD, simultaneously. The open-source system design is provided, and validation of the technique was performed. This approach was then applied to assess MECC with pharmacological and pathological interventions, and up to 12 parameters of cardiac MECC were measured. We report here the expected as well as novel effects of known compounds nifedipine, flecainide and FCCP on cardiac MECC and the interaction and sequential modulation of MECC parameters during ischemia and reperfusion.

Nifedipine treatment primarily induced modulation of calcium handling parameters, and these included prolongation of Ca2+ RT, increased Vm-Ca2+ delay, and Ca2+ τ. The first two effects, an increase in Ca2+ RT and Vm-Ca2+ delay, are directly related to reduced calcium ion entry into the cytoplasm with an L-type calcium channel (LTCC) blocker such as nifedipine. Nifedipine’s effect on Ca2+ τ is potentially due to the low intracellular calcium ion concentration ([Ca2+]) that is a result of LTCC blocking. Inhibition of the LTCC slows the influx of extracellular calcium into the cardiomyocyte, which, in turn, results in a longer time for activation of ryanodine receptors and the calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Thus, LTCC blocking can result in longer time to calcium release after depolarization (increased Vm-Ca2+ delay) and for calcium build up within the cytoplasm (increased Ca2+ RT). Increased Ca2+ RT can lead to arrhythmias22 and if these effects are persistent, cardiac contractions can weaken, and cardiac output can be reduced23. An increase in Vm-Ca2+ delay indicates excitation-contraction uncoupling and disruption of normal cardiac function. Other consequences of LTCC blocking include reduced calcium-induced calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and lower intracellular [Ca2+] as well as reduced calcium load in the sarcoplasmic reticulum and reduced calcium transient amplitude24,25. Low [Ca2+] induces interaction between sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca-APTase 2 A (SERCA2A) and its regulator phospholamban (PLN), resulting in reduced calcium sensitivity of SERCA2A and, thereby, reduced rate of calcium reuptake into the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Thus, low [Ca2+] can result in slower calcium reuptake or increased calcium decay times as indicated in this study26,27. Other than the three calcium-related parameters, nifedipine did not have any off-target effects on cardiac MECC. Nifedipine did not impact voltage parameters, including Vm RT, APD, CVT, CVL, or AR, or metabolic parameters NADH, FAD, and redox ratio. Although it has been previously reported that nifedipine affects APD, this finding is dependent on the concentration used, and the dose of 0.1 µm used in this study was not expected to alter APD28,29.

Contrary to nifedipine, we measured multiple on- and off-target effects of flecainide on cardiac MECC. First, the effects of flecainide, a sodium channel blocker, on Vm RT prolongation and CV slowing were expected as previously reported11,30. In addition to these expected on-target effects, we also measured increased Ca2+ τ and NADH intensity, particularly at faster pacing rates, and increased redox ratio at 150 ms BCL. Inhibition of Nav1.5 by flecainide could result in low intracellular sodium ion concentration ([Na+]). Additionally, flecainide ( > 5 µM) has been demonstrated to reduce ryanodine receptor-mediated Ca2+ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum31, which could result in a lower intracellular calcium ion concentration [Ca2+]. While lower [Na+] could increase sodium calcium exchanger (NCX) function in forward mode, lower [Ca2+] could reduce it. In the latter instance, it could lead to an increase in calcium decay time, as observed in this study. Lastly, increased intracellular [Na+] has been reported to impair mitochondrial function and cause oxidation of NADH by altering the mitochondrial calcium dynamics32. This is particularly true during beta-adrenergic stimulation with isoproterenol. In our study, we demonstrate the reciprocal relationship, where reduced intracellular [Na+] by sodium channel inhibition with flecainide increases NADH accumulation, particularly at faster heart rates. Thus, by off-target inhibitory effects on alternative molecules and by indirect effects of regulation of ion pools in the cytoplasm and mitochondria, flecainide alters multiple parameters of cardiac function related to metabolism, electrical excitation, and contraction. The present study with flecainide, along with previously published results using lidocaine, demonstrates the role of sodium channel inhibition in the prevention of ischemic injury, laying the foundation for investigating sodium channel blockers in the preservation of cardiac metabolism33,34.

Unlike nifedipine and flecainide treatment, FCCP modulated all three parameters of metabolic function measured in this study—NADH, FAD, and redox ratio. FCCP is a mitochondrial uncoupler that can alter reactive oxygen species production, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane35. FCCP does not directly impact the electron transport chain; rather, it disrupts the proton gradient by acting as a shuttle to remove protons36. ATP production is dependent on this proton gradient, and without it, ATP cannot be produced. In the absence of ATP synthesis, mitochondria will increase oxygen consumption to compensate for the proton leak37. Another physiological response to this disruption, in addition to the increased cellular oxygen consumption, is the oxidation of NADH to NAD+ 38. As a compensatory mechanism, FADH2 oxidation can increase its electron donation to the electron transport chain, leading to the reduction of the additional oxygen and buildup of FAD+ 39,40.

Even though FCCP does not have any known direct effects on cardiac electrophysiology and calcium handling functions, we report here that FCCP increased Vm RT and Ca RT while decreasing CVT, CVL, and Vm-Ca delay. The reduction in ATP synthesis, which is critical in the functioning of ion channels and pumps involved in electrical excitation, its propagation, and mechanical contraction, could lead to disruption of these functions as reported in this study. Eventually, within 10 min of FCCP treatment, this led to arrhythmias in 100% of the hearts used in this study. These results clearly indicate that modulation of metabolic function alters electrical excitation and contraction and can set up arrhythmogenic substrates.

Ischemia is a complex pathological condition that is a result of reduced or blocked oxygenated blood perfusion to the heart and is known to affect several MECC parameters11. Even though the effect of ischemia on various MECC parameters is well-established, not all these parameters and their interrelationship are measured simultaneously. Here, we measured Vm, intracellular Ca2+, NADH, and FAD during 5 min of no-flow ischemia and reperfusion. First, ischemia elevated NADH levels in the heart, within 1 min of its induction. It has been well-documented that ischemia increases NADH because ATP production is reduced41–44. Energy is needed for the transfer of NADH and NAD, and with the loss of ATP, NADH levels build45. NADH levels remained elevated and continued to rise throughout the 5 min of ischemia with no change in FAD, which resulted in a decreasing trend in the redox ratio. Next, ischemia altered multiple parameters related to electrical excitation. Ischemia induced an increase in Vm RT, a decrease in Vm-Ca2+ delay, and slowed CVT and CVL, as previously reported11,46. Reduced availability of ATP for efficient ion channel function could contribute to these effects. On the other hand, 5 min of no-flow ischemia and 5 min of reperfusion did not affect calcium handling-related parameters in this study.

This study is a sequel to our previously published triple parametric optical mapping technique, where we measured electrical excitation, intracellular calcium, and NADH11. In the present study, we additionally recorded changes in FAD levels in the heart in response to pharmacological interventions and ischemia-reperfusion. Measurements of NADH, FAD, and redox ratio were made by adapting previously published protocols applied in ex vivo whole heart preparations11,41,47–49. The autofluorescence property of the metabolic markers is specific to their oxidative state. While the reduced NADH and oxidized FAD demonstrate autofluorescence, the oxidized NAD+ and reduced FADH2 do not. The simultaneous measurements of both NADH and FAD allowed the calculation of the redox ratio. While FAD was not significantly altered due to nifedipine, flecainide, or ischemia/reperfusion, the redox ratio was significantly decreased with ischemia (8%) due to NADH accumulation. Previous work testing FAD in an ischemia model found that it was either not altered or slightly decreased47,50. FAD decrease during ischemia is likely due to a lack of oxygen, which limits the electron transport chain, preventing the regeneration of oxidized FAD from FADH249. It is for these same reasons that NADH increases with ischemia49. With the loss of oxygen, there is a switch to anaerobic metabolism, which produces NADH, and mitochondria can no longer oxidize NADH back to NAD10. During ischemia, the redox ratio (FAD/NADH) should be reduced, because even if FAD does not change or slightly decreases, as we see, NADH is significantly increased51. A decrease in this ratio indicates oxidative stress and is a diagnostic marker to assess the severity of ischemia52. Therefore, being able to measure FAD in addition to NADH is vital to provide a more holistic picture of cardiac function.

Taken together, this study demonstrates the feasibility and necessity of the quadruple parametric optical mapping approach in investigating cardiac pathologies and drug testing studies. Quadruple parametric optical mapping of MECC allowed the measurement of up to twelve parameters of cardiac function that could be used to study drug cardiotoxicity, which is currently limited. Further, this methodology can improve our understanding of cardiac pathological remodeling during an insult such as ischemia and can improve therapeutics.

Due to the nature of the Langendorff system, the heart is removed from all systemic influence, such as autonomic and hormonal regulation. There are changes, especially with ischemia and reperfusion, that could impact an in vivo heart differently compared to an ex vivo preparation. Regardless, this study still demonstrated the capability of quadruple parametric optical mapping in measuring cardiac MECC ex vivo. Another limitation of the ischemia and FCCP studies in this report is the requirement not to mechanically arrest the heart for optical imaging, which induces motion artifacts in the recorded signals. The use of electromechanical uncouplers such as BDM will reduce the metabolic demand as the heart stops contracting and can provide a less accurate picture of the metabolic parameters measured in this study. As a result of not electromechanically uncoupling the hearts in the ischemia and FCCP study, repolarization and calcium reuptake-related parameters such as APD, CaTD, and Ca2+ τ were not measured. To overcome this limitation, we recommend repeating the measurements, first without electromechanical uncoupling and then with it. Increasing the sample size could potentially demonstrate modulation of additional parameters of MECC by the interventions tested here. However, for the purposes of validating the quadruple parametric optical mapping system, we feel the sample size is adequate. Lastly, while we report novel aspects of cardiac function modulation by known compounds like flecainide and discuss potential mechanisms by which these off-target effects may be induced, we did not do any mechanistic analyses to confirm these pathways. Such mechanistic studies are beyond the scope of this technical study and could be an area of future research.

Methods

All procedures were conducted with approval from the University of Pittsburgh’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Animals were housed in standard satellite boxes with bedding and enrichment huts. Unless necessary, animals were not single-housed.

System design

Quadruple-parametric optical mapping system parts were custom-built at the University of Pittsburgh Machine Shop, as illustrated in Fig. 1A (labeled schematic) & 1B (CAD model). The system consists of the hardware that houses the filter and dichroic mirrors, the optics, and the camera system. The hardware consists of an elongated cube (Fig. 1B) made from an aluminum tooling plate, which is precision ground on the thickness tolerance, also referred to as Mic 6 plate or jig plate. It has three adjustable frames (green circles in Fig. 1B) that hold the dichroic mirrors in the center. Rotating these frames allows adjustment of the dichroic mirrors to align the fields of view of the four cameras. The elongated cube also opens to allow attachment of the objective lens on one end and four projection lenses placed in reverse orientation to allow a tandem lens configuration. A second set of frames (purple in Fig. 1B) is located at the entrance to each lens to hold filters appropriate for each optical channel. The tissue bath to hold the mouse heart and the paddle to position the heart and hold ECG electrodes in the tissue bath were 3-D printed using Formlabs Form4 printer using clear resin V5 and black resin V5, respectively. All design files are available at https://github.com/TheHEARTLab.

Figure 1C illustrates the excitation and emission spectra of the dyes and autofluorescent markers used in this study and how they were separated using dichroic mirrors and filters. The dichroic mirrors used were at 505, 560, and 660 nm wavelengths, placed sequentially from the objective to the projection lens in the elongated cube. This allows the separation of the recorded light into the NADH, FAD, Ca2+, and Vm channels, in that order, to be recorded by Cameras 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively. Bandpass filters were used to further remove noise in the optical channels. Filters at 455 ± 50 nm, 510 ± 20 nm, 585 ± 40 nm, and 700 ± 50 nm were placed in the NADH, FAD, Ca2+, and Vm optical channels, respectively. While these filter properties can slightly vary, care should be taken to avoid signal overlap, particularly between the spectrally close NADH and FAD channels, as well as the green excitation light for the dyes’ excitation. This is the first study to perform simultaneous imaging of FAD and green-excited reporters such as Rhod2-AM and RH237. In order to accommodate these excitation and emission channels, the green excitation light wavelength was slightly shifted using an excitation filter to 545 ± 30 nm. This results in the dyes being excited at a wavelength that does not induce peak fluorescence emission, but signal overlap studies and signal quality below indicate that this did not affect overall Rhod2-AM and RH237 signals.

Validation of optical channels

First, the system design in Fig. 1 and the separation of signals in the four optical channels were tested using aliquots of RH237 (Biotium, Cat. No. 61018), Rhod2-AM (Biotium, Cat. No. 50024), NADH (Cayman Chemicals, Cat. No. 16078), and FAD (Cayman Chemicals, Cat. No. 23386). Vials with DMSO or MilliQ water were used as blank controls. Images were obtained during excitation with both light sources at 365 and 520 nm, and at each condition, imaging was performed in replicates of 5, with new solutions used each time. Signal intensity in the vial was quantified using BV Ana (SciMedia) software.

Langendorff preparation

Male and female mice (~ 2 months old) on a C57BL/6 J background were used and housed in an AAALAC-certified vivarium facility. Mice were anesthetized, and hearts were quickly excised following cervical dislocation and thoracotomy, and the aorta was cannulated and perfused with a modified Tyrode’s solution (adapted from11 (in mM) 130 NaCl, 24 NaHCO3, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 1 MgCl2, 5.6 Glucose, 4 KCl, 1.8 CaCl2, pH 7.40 and bubbled with carbogen (95% O2 and 5% CO2) at 37 °C). The heart was allowed to equilibrate in a temperature-controlled (37 °C) bath. Electrocardiogram (ECG) leads were placed in the chamber to record volume conducted pseudo-ECGs, and a pressure transducer was attached to the Langendorf system to measure coronary pressure. The pressure was maintained around 80 mmHg.

A platinum bipolar electrode was placed in the center of the anterior surface of the heart, and the cameras were focused onto this surface. Electrical stimuli were applied to determine the threshold of pacing, and once that was determined, hearts were paced at 1.5 times the pacing threshold amplitude and 2 ms stimulus duration during optical recording. The pacing wire was then moved out of the way to allow for the heart to hang unhindered during the perfusion of dyes RH237 (transmembrane potential) and Rhod2-AM (calcium). Dyes were sonicated for 10 min in an ultrasonic bath before being injected into the perfusion system. A 5-min excess dye washout period was introduced after each dye perfusion. To prepare the dye solutions for injection, a 1 mL syringe was used to mix 30 µL of dye (RH237 at 1.25 mg/mL and Rhod2-AM at 1 mg/mL) with 970 µL of warmed Tyrode’s solution (total 1 mL). Then 0.4-0.6 mL was injected into a dye injection port, making sure there were no air bubbles in either the port or the syringe. After the dyes were injected, the pacing wire was re-placed, and the restitution protocol was applied where the basic cycle length (BCL) of the electrical stimulation was sequentially reduced from 200 ms until loss of 1:1 capture or arrhythmia. The heart was illuminated by two LED excitation light sources at 365 nm and 520 ± 5 nm wavelengths. While the former induces autofluorescence of NADH and FAD in the tissue, the latter excites RH237 and Rhod2-AM dyes. Control optical recordings of NADH, FAD, Vm, and Ca2+ were simultaneously acquired at a 1 kHz sampling rate.

Pharmacological treatment

Six mice were used (4 males and 2 females) for this study. In these studies, cardiac motion was arrested by the use of an electromechanical uncoupler, 2,3-butanedionemonoxime (BDM). After initial baseline recordings, hearts were perfused with 0.1 μM nifedipine (Cayman, Catalog #11106). After a 15-min delay, optical recordings were acquired as above. Next, nifedipine was washed out by perfusion with control Tyrode’s solution for 10 min, and the restitution protocol was repeated after washout. Finally, the hearts were perfused with flecainide (0.1 μM, Millipore Sigma Catalog # F6777). Optical recordings were once again acquired after a 15-min acclimation period as described above. Flecainide perfusion was always performed last due to the inability to wash out within the experimental time period.

In a separate set of 5 hearts (3 males, and 2 females) perfused with Tyrode’s solution without BDM, after an initial equilibration period, dye incubation, and baseline measurements as described above, the heart was treated with Carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP, 30 nM). The heart was paced at 150 ms BCL, and optical recordings were obtained at 1-min intervals until the heart went into arrhythmia to determine the mechanism of arrhythmogenesis by FCCP treatment.

Ischemia—no flow ischemia

In a separate set of 5 hearts (2 males, and 3 females), after the initial equilibration period, dye staining/washout, and baseline measurements described above, perfusion to the heart was stopped and the heart was subjected to 5 min of no-flow ischemia. Optical recordings were obtained at 1-min intervals during the ischemic period at 150 ms BCL (150 ms BCL is considered normal heart rate for ex vivo mouse heart preparation). After the last recording (5 min total), perfusion of Tyrode’s solution was reinstated, and optical recordings at 1 min intervals were made during the first 5 min of the reperfusion phase of the protocol.

Data analysis

Optical mapping data of NADH, FAD, Ca2+, and Vm were analyzed using custom Matlab software, Rhythm 4.0, which is available in an open-source format at https://github.com/TheHEARTLab/Rhythm-4.0. Rhythm 4.0 is an upgraded version of Rhythm 3.0 used for dual- and triple-parameter optical mapping data analysis11. Using this program, 12 different parameters were measured from the four optical signals. These parameters include, from (Vm): (1) rise time (Vm RT), (2) action potential duration (APD80), (3) transverse and (4) longitudinal conduction velocity (CVT and CVL), (5) anisotropic ratio (AR); from intracellular calcium: (6) rise time (Ca2+ RT), (7) calcium transient duration (CaTD80), (8) calcium decay time constant (τ), (9) Vm-Ca2+ delay; from NADH: (10) NADH autofluorescence intensity and from FAD: (11) FAD autofluorescence intensity, and (12) redox ratio.

Upstroke rise time (RT) from Vm and Ca2+ signals was measured as the time from 20 to 90% of the upstroke of the action potential and calcium transient, respectively. For Vm, RT is a function of depolarizing currents. For Ca2+, RT indicates the time for the influx of calcium into the cell from the extracellular space and release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. APD80 and CaTD80 were measured as the time interval between activation time (time of maximum first derivative of the upstroke) and 80% of repolarization and calcium transient decay, respectively. A change in either direction, an increase or a decrease, in these two parameters can lead to arrhythmia. Conduction velocity (CV) is the speed at which the activation wavefront travels in either the longitudinal (parallel to fiber orientation) or transverse direction (perpendicular to fiber orientation). It is calculated as the ratio of the interpixel distance between two points and the difference in activation time between those two points in the direction of fastest propagation. The ratio of CVL to CVT gives the anisotropic ratio (AR), which is indicative of the ellipticity of the propagating wavefront. As AR increases, the risk of arrhythmia also increases. The Ca2+ τ was determined by fitting an exponential to the last 50% (50–100%) of the calcium transient decay phase. Vm-Ca2+ delay was defined as the time interval between the activation time of the Vm signal minus the activation time of the Ca2+ signal. Increasing the delay between voltage and calcium activation suggests that there is uncoupling between the electrical and mechanical aspects of heart function. NADH intensity was measured as the average of the absolute autofluorescence intensity value in the optical recording. NADH intensity in each heart was normalized to the first measured intensity value in order to avoid interexperimental variability and to avoid the effects of self-filtering by the myocardial tissue. FAD intensity was measured similarly to NADH except using the FAD camera signal, and the redox ratio (FAD/NADH) was calculated from the non-normalized values of FAD divided by non-normalized values of NADH. The three repolarization parameters, APD80, CaTD80, and Ca τ, are only reliably measured when motion is arrested in the heart using an electromechanical uncoupler. In the nifedipine and flecainide studies, BDM was used to arrest motion, but in the FCCP and ischemia study, BDM was not used, and thus these parameters could not be assessed. All data measured from these studies are reported in the Supplementary Data.

Statistics and reproducibility

All data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise noted. The sample size was 5–6 hearts for the pharmacological treatment studies and 5 hearts for the ischemia study, determined based on power analysis and randomly assigned to experimental groups. An alpha level of 0.05 was used in all tests. Regression analysis was performed on the restitution data using GraphPad Prism Version 10.3.1 software. Nonlinear regression analysis was performed using the least squares regression fitting method. If significance was found in the slope or intercept of the experimental group compared to the control or baseline, a one-way ANOVA was used to assess differences in parameters at individual BCLs. Bonferroni post hoc correction was applied for multiple comparisons.
