From knowledge to action: a blended training to enhance shared decision making skills in primary care physicians – a pilot study
Authors: Jasmien Jaeken, Pieter Van Bostraeten, Lien Mertens, Geertruida Bekkering, Bert Aertgeerts, Jacobus Jeroen Klaas Bossen, Hilde Vandenneucker, Birgitte Schoenmakers, David Spitaels, Gabriël Simon Beerten, Tiemen Peys, Cathoo Billiouw, Nicolas Delvaux

ABSTRACT
BackgroundShared decision making (SDM) is a process to actively involve both patients and clinicians to weigh the benefits and risks of a healthcare decision, based on clinical guidelines and the patients’ preferences, needs and values. Despite the ethical foundation of SDM, its implementation remains limited. Possible physician-reported barriers for this limited uptake include insufficient level of SDM training. Training physicians in SDM could be a part of the puzzle. A recent systematic review showed that there is a shift towards blended training more than live or online learning. We therefore developed and pilot-tested a blended training program for general practitioners (GPs) in SDM in Belgium. Acquired skills were evaluated by three viewpoint – observer, patient and physician.MethodsIn a pre-post study, GPs participated in the blended training program consisting of an e-learning and a face-to-face session with simulation patients (SPs) GPs and SPs completed surveys before (T0) and after (T1) the blended training. Consultations were recorded for analysis by observer reported scales (OPTION12 and 4SDM scale). Secondary outcomes were SDM-Q9-patient, satisfaction with consultation, knowledge and intentions towards SDM.FindingsTen GPs were included. There was a significant increase in both OPTION12 (mean (SD) from 19·37 before to 37·70 after training, p = 0·0010, 95% CI [9·65 – 27·02]) and 4SDM scale (mean (SD) from 9·2 (4·66) before to 17·00 (5·08) after training, p = 0·0001, 95% CI [5·47 – 10·13]) with a moderate-large effect after training (Cohen’s D = 2·39, 95% CI [1·13 – 3·63].InterpretationThe SDM blended training for GPs improved their skills, knowledge and intentions in SDM in simulated consultations on the short term.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03351-0.

Introduction

In medicine, shared decision making (SDM) is the preferred approach for decisions where there is more than one acceptable option. It is a process that actively involves both patients and clinicians to weigh the benefits and risks of a healthcare decision, based on clinical guidelines and the patients’ preferences, needs and values [1]. Approximately 60% of the physicians prefer SDM and think they already adequately involve patients, yet physicians tend to overestimate their own skills and their perceptions often do not match the patients’ ideas and preferences [2]. Although many physicians report that they already engage in shared decision making, observational studies consistently show low levels of SDM behaviour in clinical practice, indicating a gap between perceived and actual skills [2–4]. This mismatch can lead to unmet expectations of treatment outcomes and decreased patient satisfaction [5, 6]. Despite strong theoretical and ethical foundations, the implementation of SDM in primary care remains limited. Furthermore, lack of training and insufficient skills have consistently been identified as key barriers to SDM implementation across multiple studies [7–9]. In addition, previous qualitative work among GPs identified a clear need for more training in SDM and highlighted a lack of confidence in applying SDM in daily practice [10].

To tackle these barriers, numerous educational SDM interventions have been developed aimed at improving SDM knowledge, awareness and use among physicians [11–13]. They have been shown to increase the uptake of SDM by physicians with positive effects on patient and doctor satisfaction and increased patient participation. However, the quality of evidence is low due to a high heterogeneity in setting (primary vs secondary care), in teaching methods and outcome objectives which makes it difficult to identify the most effective and suitable teaching strategy [14–16]. More targeted, comparative studies with a combination of measurements of SDM skills and experiences from patient, physician and observer perspectives are needed [8, 9]. Moreover, a recent systematic review showed that there is a shift towards blended training more than live (use face to face OR in-person) or online learning [11, 17]. Blended SDM training programs have been developed and tested successfully but mainly in secondary care [18] even though previous research has shown that GPs are willing to be trained more in SDM [10, 19] but they lack confidence to engage in SDM [1, 20]. We therefore developed a blended training program for primary care physicians and evaluated its effect on the SDM skills measured from different viewpoints.

The main objective of this study was (1)to evaluate whether a blended training program for GPs improves observable SDM performance in simulated consultations measured by GPs’, patients’ and observers’ perspectives, and (2) to examine whether the training improves GPs' attitudes, confidence and intentions toward SDM.

Methods

We performed a before-after study using a mixed-method approach (Fig. 1). We developed a blended training program and tested this using pre-post recorded consultations between a simulation patient (SP) and a GP. SDM skills in the consultations were analyzed by three observers independently using the OPTION12 and 4SDM scale. The SPs also completed the SDM-Q9-patient questionnaire.Fig. 1Study Procedure. Recruitment

Study participants in the pilot study were a convenience sample of GPs recruited in the context of obligatory continuous medical education (CME) initiatives in Flanders, Belgium. In Belgium, GPs are required to earn CME accreditation points through locally organized trainings. The participants were invited to participate by phone and e-mail by the primary researcher who is a GP herself. All participants provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Based on the feedback of our previous qualitative study [10], an ‘ideal’ SDM training should be case-based because it incorporates principles of the adult learning theory and it allows direct connection of the theory with clinical practice. This allows newly acquired knowledge and skills to be applied to real-life situations that GPs encounter in daily practice. The training adopted techniques from the theory of planned behavior and used teaching methods such as instruction (e-learning and PowerPoint presentation), modelling (videos demonstrating SDM in a primary care setting) and practice (role-play with simulation patients) [22]. We decided to develop a live workshop about osteoarthritis and its evidence-based practice to teach the participants the process of SDM using a self-developed decision aid (Appendix A1). We chose osteoarthritis because it is a very common health problem in primary care, with many different treatment options. This workshop was developed in cooperation with different experts in the field: two orthopedic surgeons from the orthopedics department of UZ Leuven (JB, HV) and four GPs with an educational background (BS, ND, DS and JJ). Simulated consultation

SPs were sent to the participants to present with a health problem common in primary care. An SP is a trained healthy individual who presents a specific personal, physical, social, and psychological history. SPs are extensively used as a teaching and training resource in various medical training programs. Human simulation with SPs can create a realistic patient encounter for learners to practice and enhance skills and techniques in a risk-free environment.

SPs were trained in advance to teach them about the clinical case osteo-arthritis and the principles of SDM. They were also asked to provide feedback from a patient’s view about the training program and the case scenario used. We furthermore discussed what they think is important in involving patients during a clinical encounter. This first consultation was used as baseline measurement (= T0) and was held before the participants gained access to the e-learning and the face-to-face session. The second consultation was held 4–5 weeks after completing the training (= T1). The participants were aware an SP would visit them since in Belgium it is not possible to book a consult with a GP as an unknown patient. SPs were recruited from a database available at the university of Leuven.

Qualitative assessment

A few weeks after the second GP-SP consultation, we performed face-to-face interviews with the participants and held a focus group session with the SPs to elucidate their experiences and pitfalls with SDM in daily practice during and after the blended program. This qualitative assessment will be reported elsewhere.

Data collection

Our primary outcome was an observer-reported outcome using the OPTION12 and 4SDM scale.

Primary outcome: observer reported outcomes

Before and after the face-to-face session, SPs visited the participants to conduct a consultation about knee osteoarthritis. These consultations were video- and audio recorded for analysis using the OPTION12 and the 4SDM scale. The OPTION12 scale is a validated observer scale and consists of 12 items scored on a five-point scale from (0) the behavior was not observed to (4) the behavior is executed to a high standard. A validated Dutch version of the scale has been developed previously. Observed SDM was also assessed with the 4SDM scale, a new instrument based on the 4-stage SDM model [23]. The 4SDM (1) assesses essential elements of SDM, leaving out all non-specific communication skills, (2) assesses SDM, irrespective of who shows the behavior, thereby giving credit to both clinicians’ and patients’ initiatives, and (3) allows for the distinction between the four SDM steps. The 4SDM has eight items (two for each step) which are coded on a 4 point scale (0 “not observed” to 3 “observed and of high quality”). Although the 4SDM scale has not yet been validated within primary care settings, its underlying structure corresponds closely to the design of our training intervention, suggesting strong alignment with our outcome measures. Data collection of the video-recordings pre-training and post-training were carried out respectively in February and May 2025.

Patient reported outcome

SPs completed the Shared Decision-Making Questionnaire (SDM-Q9) immediately after leaving the GP. The SDM-Q9 patient consisted of a 9-item questionnaire on a six-point rating scale (0: completely disagree – 5 completely agree). Responses were given on a visual analog scale and rescaled to a score 0–100 as recommended by the authors. Data analysis

For measuring the level of SDM skills, the total OPTION score is generated by converting the scores to a 0 to 100 scale and then calculating the average, as recommended by the authors of the OPTION [24]. A higher score indicates a higher level of SDM. As for the 4SDM scale, the eight items were coded on a 4-point likert scale leading to a total score of 24 where again, a higher score indicates a higher level of SDM. Each item is reported separately to more clearly identify areas for improvement and, if necessary, adjust the training accordingly. All three observers were trained by using the OPTION package and 4SDM scale, that included the manuals and a pre-recorded consultation showing an ‘ideal’ and ‘less ideal’ SDM consult. They compared their ratings and resolved discrepancies. If consensus was not reached, a fourth researcher (PVB) reviewed the recordings. Researchers were blinded for T0 or T1. Consistency was determined by Cronbach’s alpha and was considered sufficient if the ICC’s and the Fleiss kappa across items were higher than 0.60 and the raters do not differ more than one point in their scores across items and consultations. The intraclass correlation was > 0.80 for both OPTION12 (0.912) and 4SDM (0.94) indicating excellent agreement between raters in their overall scoring. The Fleiss κ was 0.035 for OPTION12 and 0.024 for the 4SDM scale. Since the data met the assumption of normality, results are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

We calculated means and SDs for each scale and paired t-test was used to compare the results before and after training. Cohen’s d was presented as a measure of effect size (d = 0.20 small, d = 0.50 medium, d = 0.80 large effects). Furthermore, we used a Pearson correlation to correlate the patient scores with the observed SDM scores (OPTION12 and 4SDM) and the observer scores among themselves. Data was analyzed using the statistical software R.

Results

A total of 10 GPs participated in our pilot in April 2025 in Flanders, Belgium. Mean age of the participants was 44 years, three were female and nine participants work in a group practice. The mean number practicing as a GP was 17 years. The participants completed two consultations in total (one before and one after the training).

Primary outcome: observer reported outcome

The OPTION12 scale increased from 19·37 (SD 11·78) to 37.70 (SD 16·15) and the 4SDM also significantly increased from 9·20 (SD 4·66) to 17·00 (SD 5·08). Both instruments measure highly similar constructs related to SDM behavior as there was a high correlation between the OPTION12 and 4SDM scales both before and after training (Table 3). Theoretical knowledge of SDM

Nine of the 10 participants (90%) correctly answered that SDM is not simply informing the patient of their options and letting them choose compared to one out of ten participants (10%) before the e-learning. In the pre-test, all participants thought that in the process of SDM, the patient needs to make the decision while in the post-test, nine of the 10 participants answered correctly. In the pre-test, three of the 10 participants answered correctly to the statement “If a patient stated “you can decide for me doc”, it means you do not have to further explore the wishes and preferences of the patient” compared to all of the participants (100%) in the post-test. Discussion

This study primarily aimed to evaluate SDM from multiple perspectives (observer, patient, and physician) and to explore the extent to which these perspectives align following a blended training intervention. We noted a significant and moderate-large effect of our blended SDM training program for GPs on SDM performance in simulated conversations, measured by both the OPTION12 and 4SDM scale indicating the potential for blended training to further support the uptake of SDM in GPs. The participants felt more confident in performing SDM after the training and planned to perform more SDM in future daily practice. There was a high satisfaction with consultation for both the participants as for the SPs.

Although we found a significant improvement for the OPTION12 scale, the overall scores remained low. This is in line with previous studies where untrained GPs showed a mean score of 26 compared to trained GPs with a mean score of 36 on the OPTION scale [3, 4, 25–27]. GPs performed poorly on eliciting patients’ level of involvement and assessing patients’ preferred approach to receiving information, similar to the study of Bakhit et al. [4]. On the contrary, GPs scored high on equipoise, listening the options and discussing the pros and cons, which is in line with Driever and Dillon et al. [28, 29]. Physicians tended to limit SDM to discussing the treatment options rather than paying attention to other key steps in the 4step SDM model. It has been reported that physicians think these items are inappropriate to ask a patient, nevertheless current clinical practice should go beyond solely correctly diagnosing a patient and explaining the pros and cons of a certain treatment option [30, 31]. Besides explaining the options, patient preferences should also be a key element in a consult to avoid misdiagnosed preferences and expectations. A possible explanation for these low scores could be this was one single face-to-face session and some GPs never heard of SDM. Furthermore, this could indicate that GPs should be more specifically trained in the other steps of the SDM model. Literature also suggests multiple training sessions are needed for a better and sustained SDM behavior over time [32, 33]. Another important note is that there is no clear cut-off concerning the OPTION12 scale. There is no consensus what score to obtain to consider the consultation as being a ‘good SDM’ consult. Currently, the higher the OPTION12 score, the better however, despite the significant increase, in our study we can conclude there is room for improvement in the SDM skills. As for the 4SDM scale, we also found a significant improvement with a doubled mean from 9.2/24 at T0 to a score of 17/24 at T1 which is in line with a study in an oncology setting [34]. The 4SDM scale performs comparably to the well-established OPTION12 in capturing variations in SDM performance, supporting its suitability as an outcome measure in this context.

Blended training in SDM is a relatively new teaching method for GPs. To our knowledge, blended trainings in SDM for GPs exist only for disease-specific cases like antibiotics prescription, palliative care or asthma and are limited to reaction to training and self-reported learning [35–37]. One study assessed SDM using a self-developed scale showing a moderate effect of the blended training for GPs in SDM skills in a palliative care setting [8]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first SDM blended training program for GPs where SDM behaviors are evaluated in the context of clinical practice consultations and where feedback and reflection on performance were included according to the learning circle of Kolb [21].

The SP’s perceived level of SDM was measured using the SQM-Q-9 patient. We found no significant difference after training which is in line with other studies [38–41]. The SDM-Q-9 relies entirely on patients’ self-reported perceptions, which can be influenced by memory or social desirability bias [42]. Patients do generally know what they feel about the encounter with their physician and whether they are satisfied. Consequently, patients might not be able to distinguish between the constructs of involvement and satisfaction leading to an overestimation or underestimation of the extent of SDM. It can be questioned whether patients really correctly interpret what they are asked about when using the SDM-Q-9 and whether the SDM-Q-9 patient truly elicits the patients’ level of involvement and not mainly discussing the options [43]. This is also in line with our findings of the OPTION12 where GPs scored low in eliciting patients’ level of involvement and assessing patients’ preferred approach to receiving information. Furthermore, patients’ understanding of SDM can vary across cultures, health literacy levels, and healthcare settings [44, 45]. Therefore assessment of patients’ perceptions of SDM support would help understand whether the training program indeed benefit patients in making treatment decisions. Furthermore, there is a recall bias. The SPs completed the SDM-Q-9 directly after the consultation where the observers could rewatch the recorded consultations for multiple times when completing the observer scales. At last, although we chose for SPs fitting the profile of an osteo-arthritis patient, it was still a simulated consult.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, the development of the training was in co-creation with SPs to include a patients’ view. Second, despite the low number of participants, we found a significant improvement of SDM skills based on the OPTION12 and 4SDM scale. Next, we had three independent and blinded observer raters to objectively score both scales. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) among the three independent observers was high, demonstrating a strong level of inter-rater reliability and suggesting that the scoring procedure was consistent and reproducible. The observers were also trained in using both scales before analyzing the recordings. Furthermore, SPs were trained in SDM consults on forehand and we trained the clinical scenarios with possible SDM-minded questions and answers. The use of SPs is also a great strength of this study as it allowed investigation of GP’s SDM skills under equivalent circumstances increasing the internal validity of our study [46, 47]. In addition, SPs were also asked to participate in co-creating the training. They provided their view and feedback on the training content. The GPs were also asked for their qualitative evaluation of the blended training program to obtain information regarding effective training components and any needs for modification or change (results reported elsewhere). This way, we could combine the feedback of different stakeholders to develop and optimize the training program. Furthermore, in terms of learning we both evaluated the participants’ ‘knows how’ (knowledge test) and ‘shows how’ in terms of Miller’s model of clinical competence [48]. Finally, this study combined observer, patient and doctor reported outcome measures which followed the recommendation of a triadic approach for measuring SDM [49, 50].

Our study has several limitations. First, an easily accessible sample of physicians were recruited who were willing to participate in the study. This means that it was likely that the study sample largely consisted of physicians with a particularly positive attitude towards SDM. These GPs were colleagues from the primary researcher (JJ) from different GP practices. Next, we were unable to blind the GPs and SPs. GPs knew they would be recorded and evaluated by an SP. In Belgium it is not possible to make an appointment with a GP if you are not subscribed to that particular practice. This means that the SPs had to identify themselves to make an appointment with the GPs. This could lead to measurement bias due to the Hawthorne effect and a possible overestimation of our results.

Future research

Muller et al. also suggested that a qualitative assessment would provide valuable input for adapting and improving a training combined with quantitative measures [51]. We organized focus groups and face-to-face interviews with the participants which we are currently analyzing. Future research should also demonstrate if the acquired skills remain over time. One year after the training session, the SPs will visit the GPs again for another consultation which will be audio-and video recorded for further analysis. At last, this pilot study showed a significant improvement of the SDM skills so implementation of this training in CME for GPs would be the next step.

Conclusion

This study showed that a blended SDM training for GPs significantly improved SDM behaviors, knowledge and confidence, with a high satisfaction of consultation for both GPs and SPs. Although significant, the overall OPTION scores remain low after training. It is conceivable that physicians need opportunities to train SDM multiple times and to reflect more on their daily practice for learning basic patient-centered communication skills before being able to implement SDM.
