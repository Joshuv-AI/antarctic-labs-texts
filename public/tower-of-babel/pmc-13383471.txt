Investigating the effect of educational intervention based on the theory of planned behavior on environmentally responsible behaviors among lower secondary school students
Authors: Zhina Ebrahimi, Hamid Reza Khalkhali, Nahid Navidjouy, Mina Maheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmong the key strategies for protecting the environment are education, modifying people’s behavior, fostering pro-environmental values, and promoting active citizenship. With this in mind, the present study sought to investigate the effect of an educational intervention based on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) on environmentally responsible behaviors (ERBs) among lower secondary students.MethodsThis quasi-experimental study was conducted among 100 lower secondary school students in Piranshahr, Iran, selected using a multistage cluster sampling method. Schools were randomly allocated to the intervention or control groups, resulting in 50 students in each group. A demographic form and a researcher-developed questionnaire, grounded in the constructs of the TPB and centered on environmental issues as well as ERBs, served as the data collection instruments. The educational program, also designed according to TPB constructs, focused on three key aspects of ERBs. This program was delivered to intervention group students over three 45-minute sessions, along with a separate 60-minute session for parents and school staff. Data gathering took place at two time points: prior to the educational intervention and again three months later.ResultsIn the intervention group, following the educational intervention, there was a statistically significant increase in the mean scores of TPB constructs—including attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, intention, and behavior—as well as in knowledge about the environment and ERBs, compared with the pre-test and the control group (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe educational intervention grounded in the TPB was found to be effective in enhancing ERBs among lower secondary school students. Given that students are the future builders of society, environmental education holds particular importance for this group. Therefore, the intervention developed in this study is proposed as a straightforward, low-cost strategy that can complement existing school programs. The adoption of such interventions has the potential to significantly reinforce ERBs and, over time, lead to better environmental outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27762-x.Keywords: Environmentally responsible behavior, Health education, Schools, Students, Theory of planned behavior

Introduction

Environmental challenges have become major concerns for many countries in recent decades. Numerous international conferences and summits are held annually, during which nations commit themselves to various treaties and conventions aimed at preventing further degradation of the global environment. Moreover, the developments of the past century, along with population growth and technological advancement, have ushered in a new phase of environmental degradation [1]. Issues such as global warming, air pollution, water scarcity, noise pollution, and the loss of biodiversity largely originate from human behavior. Therefore, creating opportunities to address these problems and to promote behavioral change is essential [2].

Enhancing the scientific knowledge and environmental understanding of younger generations is of great importance. Accordingly, environmental education aims to deliver targeted learning experiences that increase awareness, knowledge, attitudes, skills, values, and motivations, enabling adolescents and youth to become sensitive and responsible toward the environment. Since behavior is influenced by multiple factors, effective environmental education provides a comprehensive approach for education, knowledge transfer, improvement of environmental attitudes and beliefs, enhancement of self-efficacy, and development of individual skills, thereby equipping individuals to address environmental challenges, protect natural resources, and achieve environmental protection goals [3–6].

Environmental knowledge is a key prerequisite for environmentally responsible behaviors (ERBs). Individuals with greater awareness of environmental issues tend to be more sensitive toward environmental conditions and more likely to engage in responsible behaviors [7].

Environmental knowledge includes understanding environmental problems, the factors contributing to them, and the actions that can be taken to improve environmental conditions. This knowledge positively influences environmental attitudes and subsequently promotes responsible behaviors [2]. Environmental attitude refers to a set of favorable or unfavorable feelings toward environmental conditions and related issues [7]. It also mediates the relationship between environmental knowledge and environmental behavior [8].

Environmentally responsible behaviors (ERBs) refer to actions undertaken by individuals or groups to reduce potential negative environmental impacts. Examples include the use of eco-friendly packaging, purchasing environmentally friendly products, conserving water and energy, and protecting natural habitats [9].

Students—particularly those in secondary school—represent an important target group for environmental education and the development of ERBs, as shaping responsible citizens requires early educational efforts [10]. Schools, as the primary setting for nurturing the next generation, play a pivotal role in raising environmental awareness and fostering environmental ethics among students [1, 11].

The effectiveness of health education programs largely depends on the proper application of existing models and theories in the field of health education [12]. One of the most widely used theories in health education—and one that is suitable and applicable for promoting ERBs—is the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [5, 13]. TPB provides a framework for identifying the factors that influence behavioral intention and ultimately behavioral change. According to this theory, an individual’s intention to perform a behavior is influenced by three independent constructs: attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. Attitude reflects a person’s positive or negative evaluation of a behavior. Subjective norms refer to perceived social pressures to perform or not perform a behavior. Finally, perceived behavioral control reflects an individual’s perception of the ease or difficulty of performing a behavior and is assumed to have both direct and indirect effects on behavior [14].

When designing an educational intervention for a specific target group based on the TPB to promote ERBs, each construct of the theory can be strategically addressed. To modify attitude, brainstorming sessions can first be conducted to explore all possible perceived consequences of ERBs within the target group. This can be followed by structured discussions emphasizing the positive outcomes of ERBs, such as reducing water, soil, and air pollution, improving human health, and protecting natural resources. Highlighting these beneficial consequences can strengthen favorable evaluations of performing ERBs.

To modify the construct of subjective norms, important individuals in the target group’s lives can be identified and integrated into the educational intervention and asked to encourage and support the target group in performing and adhering to ERBs. In addition, holding group discussions can help participants recognize what important people in their lives think about ERBs and how important these opinions are to them.

To enhance perceived behavioral control, discussions can focus on identifying facilitators and barriers related to performing ERBs. Teaching ERBs in small, manageable steps can increase self-efficacy. Presenting positive role models who consistently practice ERBs and removing barriers can further strengthen individuals’ confidence in their ability to perform these behaviors. By systematically targeting these TPB constructs, behavioral intention can be strengthened, ultimately increasing the likelihood of sustained ERBs within the target group [5, 12].

Despite the efficiency and effectiveness of the TPB, some researchers believe that the proportion of variance predicted by the theory’s constructs can be significantly improved by incorporating additional psychological constructs and variables. Moreover, experts argue that mitigating the environmental crisis largely depends on increasing public knowledge, which in turn can lead to changes in unaware behaviors that threaten both human well-being and the surrounding environment. Therefore, considering that enhancing knowledge and improving environmental literacy are integral components of environmental education and can contribute to the promotion of ERBs and the improvement of environmental conditions in society, knowledge—although not a core TPB construct—was included in this study as a predisposing and facilitating factor within the educational intervention [4].

Although several studies have examined environmental knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors among students in Iran, most have been cross-sectional in design. Based on a review of the existing literature, there is a relative paucity of theory-driven interventional studies aimed at promoting ERBs among school students in Iran. In particular, few interventions have been explicitly grounded in the TPB and systematically designed to target its core constructs—attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and behavioral intention—in order to achieve behavioral change. Furthermore, multicomponent TPB-based interventions that simultaneously involve students, parents, and school staff appear to be limited. Addressing these gaps is essential for developing structured, theory-based, and contextually appropriate educational programs capable of producing sustainable improvements in ERBs within school settings. Therefore, the present study was conducted to evaluate the effect of a TPB-based educational intervention on ERBs among lower secondary school students. The findings may help enhance students’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding environmental protection.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

From May 2024 to June 2025, this study was conducted using a quasi-experimental design that included both pre-test and post-test assessments with a control group. The target population comprised lower secondary school students residing in Piranshahr, Iran. Due to practical and ethical challenges in the school environment, randomizing students individually was not possible. If students from the same school were assigned to different conditions, this could result in discontent, feelings of unfairness, and between-group contamination. Furthermore, constraints related to time and logistics prevented us from conducting an individually randomized controlled trial. As a result, a quasi-experimental design was employed in which schools—rather than individual students—served as the allocation units (clusters) and were randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group. This design helped reduce the risk of between-group contamination and made the intervention easier to implement within the school setting [15].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Students were eligible for inclusion if they met the following conditions: (a) they were studying in a public lower secondary school at the time of the study; (b) they had not taken part in any similar educational programs in the six months prior to the study; (c) written informed consent was obtained from both the student and their parents; and (d) they possessed sufficient physical and mental capacity to participate. Exclusion criteria comprised: incomplete responses on questionnaires, reluctance to continue participation, unforeseen events (e.g., illness or death), missing more than one educational session, or relocating to another area during the study period.

Sample size calculation

To calculate the sample size, we referred to a previous investigation by Khanzadeh et al. [16]. In that study, following an educational intervention focused on environmental protection, the reported mean ± SD for environmental behavior within the recycling dimension was 12.01 ± 4.27 in the intervention arm versus 8.39 ± 4.27 in the control arm. With a 99% confidence level (α = 0.01) and 90% statistical power (1 – β = 0.90), the required sample size per group was computed using the standard formula for comparing two independent means. Plugging the aforementioned values into this formula yielded a minimum of 42 participants per group. To allow for a 20% anticipated attrition rate, the final sample size was adjusted to 50 participants per group, resulting in a total of 100 participants.

Sampling method

A multistage cluster sampling approach was used. Initially, from among all public lower secondary schools in Piranshahr city, two girls’ schools and two boys’ schools were randomly selected using a simple random sampling technique (lottery method). Next, one of the two girls’ schools was assigned by lottery to the intervention group, while the other served as the control group. The same process was repeated for the boys’ schools. In the third step, within each selected school, one class was randomly chosen from each grade level (seventh, eighth, and ninth), again using the lottery method. For each chosen class, a roster of students satisfying the inclusion criteria was compiled, and between 8 and 9 students were randomly drawn from each roster via the lottery method. Overall, 100 students were selected from four schools.

Data collection tool

We used a researcher-developed questionnaire as the data collection instrument for this study, which comprised two sections. The first section gathered participants’ demographic characteristics, namely age, gender, grade level, birth order, family size, parents’ age, parents’ education level, and economic status. The second section covered two main domains: (a) the constructs of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB)—including attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, behavioral intention, and environmentally responsible behaviors (ERBs) as the behavior construct—and (b) participants’ knowledge regarding environmental issues as well as ERBs. This section further focused on three key aspects of ERBs: proper use of energy, use of products meeting environmental standards, and reuse of usable items [17].

Knowledge was measured using 12 items, with an example being: “Which of the following represents the recycling symbol?” Attitude was assessed through 11 items (e.g., “Using cloth bags instead of plastic bags when shopping is a beneficial/harmful behavior”). For subjective norms, 11 items were used, including “People in my life whose opinions I value want me to use cloth bags instead of plastic bags when shopping.” Perceived behavioral control was evaluated with 15 items, such as “If I want to, I can use cloth bags instead of plastic bags when shopping.” Behavioral intention was also measured using 15 items (e.g., “I intend to use cloth bags instead of plastic bags when shopping”). Finally, behavior (i.e., ERBs) was assessed with 15 items, for instance, “I use cloth bags instead of plastic bags when shopping.”

A multiple-choice format was used for the knowledge items. Each question had four response options: a single correct answer, two distractors, and one “I don’t know” choice. One point was given for each correct answer, while zero points were awarded for either an incorrect response or a selection of “I don’t know.” The overall knowledge score for each participant was derived by adding up all correct answers, resulting in a scale from 0 to 12. Thus, a higher score signified more comprehensive knowledge concerning environmental issues and ERBs.

A set of bipolar semantic differential items was used for measuring attitude. Each item presented two opposite adjectives (e.g., useful vs. useless, responsible vs. irresponsible, desirable vs. undesirable, hygienic vs. unhygienic, beneficial vs. harmful, and reasonable vs. unreasonable). Responses were recorded on a 7-point scale (e.g., useful: 7 6 5 4 3 2 1: useless). Higher scores on this scale represented a more positive orientation toward ERBs. A 7-point Likert scale was employed for subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and behavioral intention, anchored by 1 (strongly disagree) and 7 (strongly agree). A separate 7-point frequency scale was used for the ERBs (behavior) items, with the following labels: never (1), seldom (2), occasionally (3), sometimes (4), often (5), usually (6), and always (7). Possible total scores fell between 11 and 77 for both attitude and subjective norms, while the remaining three constructs—perceived behavioral control, behavioral intention, and behavior—each had possible totals ranging from 15 to 105. For every construct, a higher total score signified a more favorable outcome.

The research team personally visited the selected schools to hand out the questionnaires. Under the researcher’s guidance, participants filled out the forms independently. Anyone encountering trouble comprehending an item was given the opportunity to request additional clarification from the researcher.

Validity and reliability of data collection tool

To generate the initial set of items measuring the TPB constructs, we drew upon an extensive review of the existing literature as well as feedback from specialists in environmental health engineering, health education, and epidemiology. Following item development, the validity and reliability of the measures were evaluated and established.

Two approaches were used to examine validity: qualitative and quantitative face validity, as well as qualitative and quantitative content validity. For the qualitative assessment of face validity, we individually interviewed 10 students drawn from the target population. They were asked to judge the appropriateness and relevance of the items, identify any ambiguities or unclear wording, and report any difficulties in understanding the phrases or terms. For the quantitative aspect of face validity, an impact score was computed for each item. A group of experts—consisting of 4 specialists in environmental health engineering, 4 in health education, and 2 epidemiologists—reviewed all items. If the impact score was above 1.5, the item was deemed suitable for further analyses and was retained; otherwise, it was removed [18, 19].

The same expert panel that participated in the face validity phase also evaluated content validity. In the qualitative part, experts were instructed to go through the questionnaire and give written input on modifications they deemed necessary. They were specifically asked to consider proper grammar, appropriate wording, the importance and placement of items, as well as the approximate time needed to complete the questionnaire. Feedback from all panel members was collected and used to revise the questionnaire. For quantitative content validity, we computed the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) based on necessity and the Content Validity Index (CVI) based on relevance, clarity, and simplicity. Based on Lawshe’s criterion, with a panel of 10 experts, each item needed to achieve a CVR higher than 0.62. Any item judged as either non-essential or not sufficiently essential was eliminated from the questionnaire. For the CVI, items scoring higher than 0.79 were kept, those in the 0.70–0.79 range were considered doubtful and reworded, and any item with a CVI below 0.70 was judged unacceptable and dropped [18, 19].

The questionnaire’s reliability was assessed by calculating Cronbach’s alpha. The pilot version of the instrument was distributed to 30 individuals drawn from the target population and was completed by them. Following data collection, the Cronbach’s alpha values obtained for the constructs were as follows: attitude = 0.827, subjective norms = 0.842, perceived behavioral control = 0.875, behavioral intention = 0.839, and behavior (ERBs) = 0.861. These values were all regarded as acceptable [18].

It is necessary to reiterate again that this questionnaire was specifically developed for the present study and has not been previously published. The original version was created in Persian, as the study population consisted of Persian-speaking students. Although the English version has not undergone psychometric evaluation, an English translation has been uploaded as a supplementary file for transparency and to allow readers to review the content of the measurement tool (Supplementary File 1). This English version is not intended for research use.

Implementation of the educational program and assessment

In the pre-test phase (baseline assessment), the research team conducted in-person visits to the selected schools. After explaining the study objectives and procedures, eligible students in both the intervention and control groups were asked to complete the study questionnaire under the supervision of the researchers. The baseline assessment was performed in a classroom setting to ensure standardized administration conditions. Participants were asked to answer the questions independently, and clarification was provided, when necessary, without influencing responses.

Following baseline data collection, the educational intervention was developed based on the TPB and the identified needs of the target population. The intervention was designed to address environmental knowledge as well as key TPB constructs, including attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. The program focused on three key aspects of ERBs: proper use of energy resources, use of products meeting environmental standards, and reuse of usable items [17].

The intervention was implemented exclusively for the intervention group and was delivered over three structured educational sessions, each lasting approximately 45 min, conducted within a one-month period. To enhance participant engagement and interaction, students were trained in small groups of approximately 10 participants. The educational intervention was delivered at the school premises by trained members of the research team with academic backgrounds in health education and environmental health. Prior to implementation, these individuals were trained using a standardized protocol. All sessions were conducted using standardized educational materials and a predefined instructional protocol to ensure consistency in the delivery of educational content and methods across sessions.

The educational sessions employed multiple educational methods, including lectures, group discussions, question-and-answer segments, brainstorming, and practical demonstrations. Various educational materials and aids were used, including audio-visual clips, photographs, printed materials (booklets, posters, and pamphlets), and digital media. To strengthen the subjective norms construct, an additional educational session was organized for parents and school staff of students in the intervention group. This session lasted approximately 60 min and aimed to encourage social support for ERBs by increasing knowledge and emphasizing the influential role of family members and school personnel in shaping students’ behaviors.

Upon completion of the student training sessions, participants in the intervention group received an educational reinforcement package containing printed materials (a booklet and a pamphlet) summarizing the key messages of the program. Furthermore, during the three-month follow-up period, reminder and reinforcement strategies were implemented through phone contacts every two weeks to enhance retention of the educational content and support sustained behavioral change.

The post-test, serving as the outcome assessment, was conducted three months after the completion of the educational intervention. During this phase, students in both the intervention and control groups completed the same questionnaire used at baseline, under conditions similar to those of the pre-test.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved (code: IR.UMSU.REC.1403.010) by the Research Ethics Committee of Urmia University of Medical Sciences. To pave the way for fieldwork, the Vice-Chancellor for Research and Technology at Urmia University of Medical Sciences issued an official letter, which the research team submitted to school principals. A member of the research team went to each school involved, outlining what the study aimed to achieve and securing signed informed consent forms from parents and their children. Everyone taking part got a clear picture of the study’s nature and steps, with a strong emphasis on the fact that joining was voluntary and completely up to them. A get-together with families and students was organized so that any questions could be answered face-to-face. It was also made clear that leaving the study at any moment was an option, with no need to give a reason. The confidentiality of all information was guaranteed, and questionnaires contained no personal details (for instance, full names). After the conclusion of the study, the control group was provided with all educational materials along with a concise summary of the intervention.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 16. Participants’ demographic characteristics and research variables were described using means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. Normality testing for continuous data was performed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov procedure.

To examine baseline comparability, differences between the two study arms (intervention versus control) were checked. For categorical variables, either the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was chosen, based on the data. For continuous variables, after verifying their distribution, the appropriate comparative test was applied: independent t-test for normal distributions and the Mann–Whitney U when normality was violated. Paired t-tests were used to examine changes in continuous variables within each study arm from pre-intervention to post-intervention.

To control for the influence of baseline scores and demographic characteristics, ANCOVA was performed. This allowed comparison of the two arms’ post-test mean scores while adjusting for covariates. The practical importance of any observed difference was judged using effect sizes, partial eta squared (ηp²). Following Cohen’s rules [20], ηp² values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 were taken as small, medium, and large effects, respectively. Statistical significance was set at a p-value below 0.05.

Intra-group analyses revealed that the intervention group experienced statistically significant increases in mean knowledge scores and all TPB constructs between the pre-test and post-test phases (p < 0.05). In the control group, no significant differences were observed between the two time points for the TPB constructs (p > 0.05). Nonetheless, a significant elevation in mean knowledge scores was noted in the control group at post-test relative to pre-test (p < 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

The findings of the present study indicate that the designed educational intervention was effective in improving knowledge regarding environmental issues and ERBs among students in the intervention group. This finding is consistent with previous research demonstrating that health education programs can significantly enhance environmental knowledge across different populations and age groups [21–23].

Increased knowledge is considered a key prerequisite for behavior change, as it enhances individuals’ understanding of environmental problems and their consequences, thereby facilitating more informed decision-making and promoting pro-environmental behaviors [21–23].

One possible explanation for the substantial improvement in knowledge observed in the present study, as well as in previous research, is the use of structured educational sessions combined with diverse educational methods, including interactive discussions, visual materials, and reinforcement strategies. These approaches may have enhanced students’ engagement and retention of information.

It is worth noting that in the present study, comparison of pre- and post-intervention mean knowledge scores in the control group also showed an increase in knowledge. The observed increase in knowledge scores in the control group may be attributed to the testing effect, as completing the pre-test could have heightened participants’ awareness and sensitivity to environmental topics, potentially prompting them to seek or retain related information over time. However, this increase was significantly smaller than that observed in the intervention group. Specifically, comparison of post-test mean knowledge scores between the intervention and control groups revealed a markedly greater improvement in the intervention group, underscoring the effectiveness of the educational intervention.

The results of this study suggest that the educational intervention—together with the set of strategies, methods, contents, and instructional materials used to enhance the attitude construct as reported in Table 1—was effective in improving the attitudes of students in the intervention group toward ERBs. This finding is consistent with previous research demonstrating that educational interventions can enhance pro-environmental attitudes and related behaviors across different populations and settings [16, 21, 24].

One possible explanation for the observed improvement in attitudes is the use of targeted, interactive educational strategies designed to address the attitudinal component of the TPB. In the present study, students were encouraged to reflect on the negative consequences of environmentally destructive behaviors and the positive outcomes of ERBs through brainstorming and group discussion [12]. These approaches likely facilitated deeper cognitive processing and personal engagement, thereby strengthening favorable evaluations of the target behaviors.

The consistency of these findings with previous studies may be attributed to the use of diverse learner-centered instructional approaches across interventions. Although the instructional methods differed in their specific design and implementation, they can be broadly described as interactive, reflective, and knowledge-enhancing strategies. For instance, the Education for Sustainable Development (ESD)-based program by Iqbal et al. emphasized critical thinking, problem-solving, and learn-by-doing approaches, promoting students’ engagement and responsibility toward environmental issues [24]. Similarly, Khanzadeh et al. employed inquiry-based, process-oriented, and question–answer methods to enhance cognitive involvement and deepen understanding of environmental concepts [16]. In addition, Asgaripor et al. used participatory and creative techniques such as storytelling, videos, drawing, and group discussion, which facilitated active learning and emotional engagement [21]. Collectively, these approaches indicate that combining theory-based content with interactive, reflective, and experiential learning strategies can effectively enhance attitudes toward environmentally responsible behaviors.

Findings from this research highlight that the educational intervention designed and implemented was effective in improving the subjective norms construct among students in the intervention group. In the present study, to enhance the subjective norms construct, an educational session was held for the parents and school staff of the intervention group students. During this session, key environmental concepts and ERBs were taught, and family members and school staff were encouraged to support and motivate students in maintaining and practicing ERBs.

Additionally, students were asked to discuss during recess time whether the important people in their lives approved of their engaging in ERBs.

Consistent with this finding, a study conducted by Fallah-Nejad et al. on the effect of an educational intervention based on the TPB in promoting waste separation behavior among primary school students reported that, following the intervention, the mean score of subjective norms significantly increased in the intervention group compared with the control group. The authors attributed this increase in subjective norms to greater interaction between participants and their teachers, health educators, and classmates as significant others, as well as their influence on waste separation behavior [25].

In contrast to these results, a study by Jafarzadeh et al., which aimed to empower housewives in waste separation, found that three months after the educational intervention, the mean score of subjective norms in the intervention group did not differ significantly from that of the control group. Their findings indicated that educating housewives alone may not lead to a meaningful change in the subjective norms construct, as this construct is not solely dependent on the individual and is strongly influenced by significant others. One possible explanation for the lack of significant change is that the intervention primarily targeted the housewives themselves due to existing constraints. The authors noted that if family members had been included in the intervention, it might have had a greater impact on improving subjective norms [22].

The current findings reveal that the educational intervention designed in this research was effective in improving perceived behavioral control regarding ERBs among students. This finding is consistent with previous research demonstrating that TPB-based educational interventions can enhance individuals’ perceived control over performing pro-environmental behaviors such as waste separation [22, 25].

One possible explanation for the observed improvement in perceived behavioral control construct is the use of targeted strategies aimed at reducing perceived barriers and strengthening facilitators of behavior. In the present study, these strategies included identifying barriers and facilitators related to ERBs, addressing misperceptions, teaching specific behaviors (e.g., waste separation) through step-by-step instruction, delivering persuasive and reinforcing messages via mobile phone, and introducing role models such as peers and environmentally engaged individuals [4, 12].

Furthermore, the relatively strong improvement observed in perceived behavioral control may be attributed to the practical and skill-based nature of the intervention, which focused not only on increasing awareness but also on enhancing students’ perceived ability to perform ERBs in real-life contexts. This suggests that combining theory-based content with skill-building and reinforcement strategies can play a critical role in strengthening perceived behavioral control and facilitating behavior change.

According to the results obtained in this study, the TPB-based educational intervention was effective in enhancing the intention to adopt ERBs among lower secondary school students in the intervention group. Consistent with this finding, in studies conducted by Jafarzadeh et al. [22] and Fallah-Nejad et al. [25], following TPB-based educational interventions, the behavioral intention toward waste separation increased significantly in the intervention group compared to before the intervention and relative to the control group.

Although no specific strategy was included in the design of the educational intervention to directly modify behavioral intention, the significant increase observed in this construct after the intervention is noteworthy. This improvement can be explained through the framework of the TPB, as attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control are identified as key predictors of behavioral intention. Supporting this explanation, studies by De Leeuw et al. [13] and Aziz [26] also found that attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control were significant predictors of the intention to engage in pro-environmental behaviors.

In this regard, it can be concluded that the strategies, methods, content, materials, and educational tools employed in this study—designed and implemented to enhance the constructs of attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control—indirectly yet effectively increased participants’ intention to engage in ERBs. Therefore, it can be argued that strengthening these predictor constructs played a key role in enhancing behavioral intention, and this effect, despite the lack of direct focus on intention, was achieved through the underlying mechanisms of the TPB.

The present study’s results demonstrate that the TPB-based educational intervention was effective in promoting ERBs among lower secondary school students in the intervention group. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that educational interventions, particularly theory-based interventions, can lead to significant improvements in pro-environmental behaviors across different populations [4, 27].

One possible explanation for the observed behavioral change is that the intervention targeted multiple determinants of behavior within the TPB framework, including knowledge, attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. By simultaneously addressing these constructs, the intervention may have strengthened students’ behavioral intentions and facilitated the translation of intention into actual behavior.

Furthermore, the relatively large effect size observed for behavior suggests that the intervention produced a meaningful change beyond statistical significance. This substantial effect may be attributed to the multicomponent and interactive nature of the program, which not only increased knowledge but also enhanced skills and social support. These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive, theory-based interventions in achieving sustainable behavioral change among students.

As previously noted, although the TPB is an effective framework, its explanatory power may be enhanced by incorporating additional psychological constructs. For example, motivation is a key determinant of behavioral change in educational interventions and can be integrated with the constructs of the Theory of Planned Behavior to better explain and predict behavior. While TPB focuses on attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, motivation provides an additional psychological mechanism that may strengthen individuals’ intention and actual engagement in target behaviors. In this context, Ahmadi et al. developed a classification system for teachers’ motivational behaviors that can be used to design more effective educational interventions. Future studies are recommended to incorporate such motivational strategies alongside TPB constructs to enhance the effectiveness and sustainability of behavior change programs [28].

One limitation of the present study was the use of a self-reported questionnaire for data collection, which may have led to participants not providing fully accurate responses. To mitigate this, measures were taken to maximize anonymity and build trust with the participants, aiming to enhance the accuracy of self-reported data. Additionally, exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses (EFA and CFA) were not conducted to fully validate the measurement instruments. Furthermore, the study was limited to public schools in Piranshahr, and therefore the findings may not be generalizable to schools with different characteristics, such as private or rural schools. The implementation of the multicomponent intervention, which included both parents and teachers, may be difficult to replicate in schools with lower levels of family or staff involvement. Finally, access to educational equipment, such as video projectors, may be limited in resource-constrained schools, posing challenges for implementing a similar intervention.

Another potential limitation of the present study is that although schools, rather than individuals, were randomly selected, cluster effects—including intra-cluster correlation—were not assessed. Therefore, future studies are recommended to include a larger number of clusters and to formally account for intra-cluster correlation in the analysis. In addition, although participants’ adherence to the intervention was supported through attendance tracking, regular follow-up phone contacts, and reinforcement activities, it was not formally measured using a standardized adherence scale. This may also be considered a limitation of the study.

Conclusion

The findings of the present study demonstrate that the TPB-based educational intervention effectively enhanced environmental knowledge and strengthened the key predictor constructs—attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control—thereby increasing students’ intention to perform ERBs and ultimately promoting ERBs among lower secondary school students. These results highlight the pivotal role of reinforcing TPB constructs in shaping both behavioral intention and actual behavior through the theory’s underlying mechanisms.

Given the importance of educating students as future citizens and the limited number of theory-based interventional studies in this field, the educational program developed in this study may serve as a practical and low-cost approach to complement existing school-based environmental education programs and sustainability activities. The implementation of such interventions could strengthen ERBs and contribute to long-term environmental improvement. Engagement of health education and health promotion specialists in schools and health centers may further enhance the sustainability and effectiveness of these programs. The findings may guide policymakers, school instructors, health professionals, and researchers in the development and implementation of effective strategies to enhance environmental health and promote ERBs among students, while also increasing knowledge and engagement among students and their families.
