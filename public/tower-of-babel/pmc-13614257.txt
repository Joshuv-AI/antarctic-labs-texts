Assessment of readiness for return to skiing after coronary artery bypass grafting and left ventricular reconstruction using cardiopulmonary exercise testing and field-based expiratory gas analysis: a case report
Authors: Shuhei Koyama, Takashi Murakami, Masaru Nagasaki, Takahiro Ohki, Tsutomu Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReturning to skiing after myocardial infarction and complex cardiac surgery is challenging because cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) performed in laboratory settings may not fully reflect real-world sports-specific demands.Case summaryA 63-year-old male ski instructor experienced an acute myocardial infarction while skiing and subsequently underwent coronary artery bypass grafting combined with surgical ventricular restoration. After structured outpatient cardiac rehabilitation incorporating high-intensity interval training, serial CPET demonstrated an improvement in peak oxygen uptake from 19.4 to 22.1 mL/kg/min without adverse events. To assess real-world cardiopulmonary stress, a field-based breath-by-breath expiratory gas analysis was performed during skiing using a portable metabolic analyser. Three ski techniques (skidding, long radius carving, and short radius carving) were evaluated. Peak oxygen uptake and heart rate during all ski conditions did not exceed the corresponding peak values obtained during CPET or the prescribed high-intensity interval training intensity, indicating that skiing was performed within the predefined physiological safety margin.DiscussionThis case demonstrated a clinically applicable strategy for assessing readiness for return to skiing by integrating CPET-guided rehabilitation with field-based physiological assessment, thereby enabling objective evaluation of sport-specific safety.

Introduction

Returning to sports after myocardial infarction (MI) and complex cardiac surgery is challenging, particularly for high-intensity activities including skiing. European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines recommend individualised risk stratification using cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) and shared decision-making1,2; however, CPET is performed under controlled laboratory conditions and may not reflect actual sports-associated stress.

Exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation after MI and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) improves functional capacity and clinical outcomes.3 Although aerobic exercise is part of cardiac rehabilitation, physiological demands during sports exceed those during rehabilitation. Therefore, safety when returning to sports should be confirmed under conditions closely reflecting sports-specific demands.

For advanced skiers, particularly in regions with 1-km-long slopes, skiing involves repeated bouts of high-intensity activity lasting 30–60 s, interspersed with passive recovery during chairlift ascent; this resembles ESC-recommended high-intensity interval training (HIIT). Compared with moderate continuous training, HIIT drastically improves endurance capacity in selected patients with heart failure (HF) and coronary artery disease (CAD).4,5

Here, we report a patient who wished to resume skiing. An HIIT-based cardiac rehabilitation programme was implemented, followed by field-based expiratory gas analysis during skiing that allowed cardiopulmonary response evaluation and comparison with laboratory-derived CPET parameters. Our institutional ethics committee approved this case report.

Case presentation

A 63-year-old male ski instructor developed an anterior ST-segment elevation MI while skiing. Emergency coronary angiography revealed 100% occlusion in the proximal left anterior descending coronary artery (Segment 6). Stent implantation was anatomically inappropriate because of a crank-like tortuosity; however, antegrade TIMI grade 3 flow was secured by balloon angioplasty [peak creatine kinase 4006 U/L (institutional reference range: 59–248 U/L)]. Subsequently, an anterior-apical left ventricular (LV) aneurysm and LV thrombus developed. For complete revascularization of the residual stenosis and one-stage treatment of complications, successful CABG (left internal thoracic artery to the left anterior descending artery, right internal thoracic artery to the first diagonal branch), LV reconstruction using the septal anterior ventricular exclusion technique, and thrombectomy were performed (Figure 1). Postoperative transthoracic echocardiography revealed an LV ejection fraction of 32%. The postoperative course was uneventful.

After discharge, the patient underwent outpatient cardiac rehabilitation. He had a 34 pack-year smoking history (15 cigarettes/day at age 18–63 years) but had stopped smoking after the MI. He was asymptomatic at rest and during daily activities. Heart failure medications, initiated at the referring hospital, included a sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor, an angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor, and a beta-blocker (carvedilol 5 mg twice daily). Although we recommended switching the ACE inhibitor to sacubitril/valsartan to optimize therapy, the ACE inhibitor was continued owing to patient preference. Antithrombotic therapy included edoxaban 60 mg and aspirin 100 mg daily, considering a history of LV thrombus, postoperative symptoms indicative of embolism (including transient amaurosis fugax), and underlying CAD. Although guidelines recommend clopidogrel with an oral anticoagulant, clopidogrel was replaced with aspirin owing to a high prevalence of CYP2C19 poor metabolizers among East Asians.6 Standard-dose edoxaban was maintained to manage the high embolic risk from the LV thrombus; de-escalation to edoxaban monotherapy was planned as only arterial grafts were used for CABG. Given his approximately 40-year history of skiing and desire to resume, a rehabilitation programme incorporating aerobic exercise, supervised HIIT, and resistance training was implemented. Exercise intensity was prescribed according to CPET-derived parameters, consistent with established recommendations.7

At 10-month follow-up, transthoracic echocardiography revealed an improved LV ejection fraction (46%, Figure 1). The 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrated low voltage in the limb leads, a QS pattern in leads V3 to V5, and T-wave inversions in leads I, aVL, and V3 to V6, consistent with prior anterior MI (Figure 2). Serial CPET was performed 5 and 10 months post-surgery (Table 1, Figure 3). Peak metabolic equivalents of task (METs) increased (from 5.6 to 6.3). No exercise-induced symptoms or arrhythmias were observed.

To assess real-world cardiopulmonary stress, breath-by-breath expiratory gas analysis was performed during skidding and long- and short-radius carving at 10 months postoperatively (Figure 4). Peak VO2 and heart rate (HR) during all skiing conditions did not exceed the corresponding CPET-derived peak values or prescribed HIIT intensity.

Discussion

This case illustrates a practical assessment of eligibility for return to skiing after MI treated with CABG and LV reconstruction. Field-based expiratory gas analysis enabled direct comparison between CPET-derived physiological limits and those during real-world skiing. These findings support a stepwise approach combining supervised HIIT with sports-specific physiological assessment.

Exercise intensity was prescribed using CPET-derived HRs at ventilatory threshold (VT)1 and VT2 as physiological reference points, consistent with recommendations.8,9 The supervised HIIT intensity was slightly above VT2, and field-based assessment confirmed that the physiological demands of skiing did not exceed the corresponding CPET-derived peak values or prescribed HIIT intensity.

Anticoagulant use must be balanced against traumatic bleeding risk. Given the patient’s history of LV thrombus, anticoagulant therapy was inevitable. Approximately 80% of ski-related injuries result from falls, with 20% caused by collisions. Our patient was advised to avoid high-risk conditions (including icy slopes or crowded environments) and prioritize controlled skiing techniques to minimize fall risk. Recent evidence supports short-term anticoagulant withdrawal to mitigate haemorrhagic risk while maintaining thromboembolic protection.10

Cold exposure at ski resorts may increase cardiovascular demand by augmenting resting and exercise-related oxygen consumption, imposing physiological stress in patients with cardiovascular disease.11,12 Increasing altitude should also be considered when prescribing alpine exercise, as it may impair aerobic exercise capacity: peak oxygen uptake decreases with increasing altitude, with a 6% reduction per 1000 m elevation gain.13 Accordingly, skiing at high-altitude resorts may impose greater cardiopulmonary stress than the present field assessment.

Beta-blocker therapy might blunt the chronotropic response during exercise under cold and hypoxic conditions at high altitude; this should be considered when interpreting HR responses during CPET and real-world exercise. HR responses should be interpreted together with perceived exertion and clinical symptoms.

Previous MI is a major risk factor for sudden cardiac death during skiing.14 Recommendations advise high-risk competitive athletes with structural heart disease and premature ventricular contraction to engage in low-intensity competitive sports.15 No arrhythmias were observed during CPET, and our patient had no documented history of ventricular arrhythmias. He was not participating in competitive sports; therefore, returning to skiing was considered acceptable. However, CPET is performed over a limited period. Although ambulatory Holter monitoring was not performed, wearable rhythm-monitoring devices may represent a more comprehensive rhythm monitoring and help detect exercise-related arrhythmias during real-world activity. Furthermore, in individuals with a high risk for exercise-related cardiac events, optimal medical therapy, including sacubitril/valsartan to improve LVEF and reduce ventricular arrhythmias, should ideally precede exercise prescription. In this case, the patient preferred continuing ACE inhibitor therapy, although switching to sacubitril/valsartan was recommended, and this lack of full medical optimization represents a limitation of this case management.

In the present field assessment, physiological responses during skiing at an altitude of approximately 300 m remained within the prescribed exercise intensity. Evaluating physiological responses at multiple altitudes was not practical. Therefore, rather than recommending a specific altitude cut-off, the patient was instructed to monitor exercise intensity using HR and perceived exertion while considering altitude and cold exposure. This case supports ESC recommendations emphasizing individualized risk assessment, objective physiological testing, and shared decision-making on return to sports after complex cardiac surgery.

Lead author biography

Shuhei Koyama is a physical therapist at Ichikawa General Hospital, Tokyo Dental College, Japan. He specializes in cardiac rehabilitation and exercise therapy for patients with cardiovascular disease. His clinical and research interests focus on cardiopulmonary exercise testing, high-intensity interval training, and return-to-sport assessment after cardiac surgery.
