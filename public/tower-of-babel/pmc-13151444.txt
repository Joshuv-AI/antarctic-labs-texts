Bizarre case of syncope in an adolescent
Authors: Jing Wang, Boyu Kong, Dianbo Cao
Published: 2026

Clinical introduction

A male adolescent suffered a sudden syncopal episode lasting 15 s, accompanied by loss of consciousness without convulsions. On physical examination, his vital signs were stable, P2 was loud and a systolic murmur was detected at left sternal border. Laboratory tests showed decreases in haemoglobin (103 g/L; reference 129–172), platelet (126×109/L; reference 150–407) and serum albumin (31.4 g/L; reference 42–56) and increases in D-dimer (6585.76 ng/mL; reference 0–500), B-type natriuretic peptide (156.02 pg/mL; reference 0–96), total bile acid (91.3 µmol/L; reference 0–10) and ammonia (63 µmol/L; reference 9–47). Prothrombin time was extended to 15.8 s (reference 9–13), prothrombin time activity was 54.2% (reference 80–120%) and fibrinogen was 1.78 g/L (reference 1.8–4.0). Serological markers of viral hepatitis and the auto-immune antibodies were negative. Alpha-fetoprotein level was within normal range (2.20 ng/mL, range 0–7.0). The echocardiography revealed the right atrium and right ventricle were dilated to 63 mm and 34 mm, respectively, and the pulmonary artery was enlarged with an inner diameter of 36 mm. Right heart catheterisation identified increased pulmonary artery pressure up to 63 mm Hg. Chest radiography and thoracic contrast-enhanced CT findings are shown in figure 1.

Answer: E

Chest radiography revealed a moderately enlarged, pear-shaped heart. Thoracic contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated dilated pulmonary artery, right heart enlargement and absence of the portal vein. Further abdominal CT showed splenic and superior mesenteric veins joined to a blind end, continuing as a dilated inferior mesenteric vein. This vein descended, bifurcated and its branches ultimately drained into bilateral internal iliac veins (figure 2). Liver MRI indicated nodular regenerative hyperplasia (NRH). Brain MRI showed T1 hyperintensity in the globus pallidus with mild atrophy (online supplemental figure).

Volume-rendered CT venogram of the abdomen. IMV, inferior mesenteric vein; LB-IMV, left branch-inferior mesenteric vein; LIIV, left internal iliac vein; RB-IMV, right branch-inferior mesenteric vein; RIIV, right internal iliac vein; SMV, superior mesenteric vein; SV, splenic vein.

The diagnosis was type I Abernethy malformation with unique vascular communication complicated by pulmonary hypertension (PH), hepatic NRH and encephalopathy.1 The patient is under medical management and awaiting liver transplantation.2

PH in this patient was clear. Laboratory tests showed multiple abnormalities, but imaging excluded cardiac or thromboembolic causes. Incidental liver findings prompted further abdominal investigation, which identified Abernethy malformation as the underlying aetiology. Syncope, an uncommon presentation of this malformation, was likely caused by hyperammonaemia or decreased cerebral perfusion secondary to PH.3

Teaching point: Despite its rarity, this case highlights the importance of a systematic search for extracardiac shunts, including portosystemic anomalies such as Abernethy malformation, in any patient with unexplained PH, even in the absence of portal hypertension.
