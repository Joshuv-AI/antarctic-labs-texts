Schwannoma of the penis: a case report
Authors: Alireza Sayahan, Siavash Dadras, Eisa Khaledi, Seyed Reza Yahyazadeh, Mahdi Ramezani-binabaj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchwannomas are benign nerve sheath tumors arising from Schwann cells, most commonly affecting cranial and peripheral nerves. Penile schwannomas are exceptionally rare, with fewer than 50 cases reported worldwide. Their clinical presentation often mimics other soft tissue lesions, posing diagnostic challenges for urologists. This report describes a rare case of penile schwannoma in a middle-aged Iranian man, highlighting its clinical, histopathological, and surgical features.Case presentationA 52-year-old Iranian Caucasian male presented with a painless, slow-growing, mobile mass on the dorsal glans penis, noted for 2 years with recent enlargement. Physical examination revealed a 10 × 10 mm lobulated lesion without ulceration or discharge. No lymphadenopathy or systemic findings were observed. Surgical excision was performed under local anesthesia, preserving the dorsal penile nerve. Histopathology revealed a well-defined, encapsulated biphasic spindle cell neoplasm with Antoni A and B areas, nuclear palisading, and dense chromatin. Immunohistochemistry showed strong positivity for S100 and SOX10, confirming the diagnosis of schwannoma. Postoperative follow-up over 1 year showed no erectile dysfunction or penile curvature.ConclusionPenile schwannoma, though rare, should be considered in the differential diagnosis of penile masses. Accurate diagnosis relies on histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation. Complete surgical excision with nerve preservation offers favorable outcomes and minimal functional impairment.Keywords: Penis, Schwannoma, Solid tumor, Case report

Introduction

Cranial, peripheral, and autonomic nerve Schwann cells give rise to neoplasms known as Schwannomas. When these tumors are present clinically, they can lead to spinal cord compression, abdominal or soft tissue masses, intracranial lesions, or cranial neuropathy. The tumors exhibit a homogeneous pattern of biphasic fusiform-shaped cells, which may give them a palisaded appearance and are histologically encapsulated and highly vascularized [1]. One schwannoma per 50,000 persons was the overall incidence, and one schwannoma per 80,000 persons was the intracranial indication [2]. A total of 0.6 cases of peripheral schwannomas were reported for every 100,000 individuals [3]. The penis dorsal, ventral, body/shaft, glans, and root are the locations where penile schwannoma is most uncommon, with fewer than 50 cases recorded [4, 5]. The diagnosis of penile schwannoma can therefore be challenging for a urologist when they see a patient in the clinic because it can be confused with other diagnoses, such as lipoma, fibroma, and sclerosis [6]. The typical presentation of patients with penile schwannoma varies. Their symptoms, which include pain, erectile dysfunction, urinary tract irritation, obstruction, penile curvature, and discomfort during sexual activity [6], are often accompanied by gradually expanding, painless nodules that are primarily found on the dorsal side of the penis and the penile body [4]. The diagnosis of nerve sheath tumors can be aided by imaging methods, such as MRI and US, but these methods are not particularly specific. In some cases, they offer clues as to whether the tumor is benign or malignant; however, pathology and immunohistochemistry corroborate the definitive diagnosis [7]. Complete tumor resection is the primary treatment strategy for benign nerve sheath tumors. During surgery, the impacts of the procedure on the patient's sexual function should be considered and minimized, surrounding tissues should be protected, and damage to blood vessels and nerves should be avoided [4, 6]. This report describes a rare case of painless, slow-growing swelling in the penis of a 52-year-old man.

Case report

A 52-year-old Iranian Caucasian male presented with a painless, slow-growing, and mobile solitary spherical mass on the dorsal aspect of the glans penis for the past 2 years. There is no evidence of additional lesions or lymphadenopathy in other parts of the body upon physical examination. The individual had no significant comorbidities and was not on regular medications. Family history was negative for tumors or neurofibromatosis. No remarkable psychosocial issues were reported. He had noticed an increase in size for 9 months, without ulceration or discharge. On examination, a lobulated swelling lesion measuring 10*10 mm was observed on the dorsal glans of the penis near the shaft (Fig. 1).

Dorsal glans penile lesion showing its anatomical location

Blood tests (such as those for viral markers) and other investigations were within normal limits. The patient underwent surgery under local anesthesia. The mass was excised completely to a depth of 5 mm, with margins, while the dorsal penile nerve was preserved. Bleeding was controlled, and mucocutaneous approximation was carried out during the surgery. The biopsy of the lesion revealed a well-defined, encapsulated neoplasm that contained spindle cells with blood vessels. While Antoni A had palisemic nuclei, Antoni B had fewer cellular structures with more foam cells. Schwannoma was confirmed on the basis of these findings. The sections revealed a biphasic spindle cell neoplasm consisting of compact, hypercellular Antoni A areas intermingled with myxoid, hypocellular Antoni B areas. Foci of nuclear palisading around the fibrillary process are observed. On high-power examination, tumor cells have ill-defined cytoplasm and dense chromatin (Fig. 2).

Histopathological sections showing a biphasic spindle cell neoplasm with Antoni A and Antoni B areas consistent with schwannoma

IHC-stained tumor cells were positive for S100 and SOX10 (Fig. 3).

Immunohistochemical staining showing diff use positivity of tumor cells for S100 and SOX10

The wound healed uneventfully without infection. No sensory changes were observed postoperatively. Follow-up visits at 2 weeks, 3 months, and 1 year revealed no recurrence. Over a year after surgery, there was no sign of erectile dysfunction or penile curvature.

Discussion

Very slow-growing benign tumors of the nerve sheath are called schwannomas. They might not have been diagnosed for many years because of their slow growth. The malignant type of these tumors is extremely uncommon and is associated with neurofibromatosis [7–9]. In our patient, the lesion was solitary, and there was no evidence of neurofibromatosis. Sexual intercourse may be affected if a tumor appears distal to the penis. The link between a schwannoma and erectile dysfunction is not well established with sufficient evidence [10, 11], although our patient reported experiencing sexual discomfort. Lipoma, atheroma, sarcoma, Peyronie's disease, and fibrosis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of this superficial penile tumor [12]. In contrast to penile schwannomas, a softer and more superficial nature suggests a diagnosis of lipoma or atheroma. Patients often complain about erectile dysfunction and painful intercourse in Peyronie's disease, but these complaints are uncommon in patients with penile schwannoma. Biopsy of the tumor by imaging studies, such as ultrasonography and magnetic resonance imaging, can be used as diagnostic modalities. The presence of biphasic Antoni A and Antoni B areas is an indication of a benign Schwannoma on histopathology. Concentrated spindle cells that are interlaced in bundles or fascicles with blurred cytoplasmic borders are hallmark antoni A areas. Spindle cells are scattered in the myxoid stroma in the Antoni B areas. In addition, the S-100 protein is highly expressed in schwannoma [13].

Conclusion

Penile schwannomas, although uncommon, should be included in the differential diagnosis of penile tumors. Complete surgical excision is the preferred treatment, with careful attention given to preserving nerve function. Histopathological examination remains the definitive method for diagnosing schwannomas and differentiating them from other penile masses.
