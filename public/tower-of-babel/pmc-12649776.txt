The German LAKE-score reliably predicts urinary and dietary potential renal acid load: a three-armed translational study
Authors: Maximilian Andreas Storz, Johanna Sophie Dürrschnabel, Julian Herter, Alberto Trinchieri, Alvaro Luis Ronco, Roman Huber, Stefanie Kowarschik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence supports kidney injury from a high potential renal acid load (PRAL). Existing predictive equations to estimate the PRAL have important limitations and require either a 24-h urine collection or the estimation of an individual’s nutrient intake. The LAKE (load of acid to kidney evaluation) score offers a more pragmatic solution; however, the score has never been translated into the German language.Materials and methodsWe translated the LAKE score into German, and validated it in a three-armed study in a healthy population comprised of omnivores, lacto-ovo-vegetarians and vegans. Using weighed food diaries, we estimated the participants’ dietary PRAL (PRALR). The urinary PRAL (UPRAL) was calculated based on 24-h urine collection-derived ionogram data. Correlation analyses were run to examine the score’s validity. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated to determine the score’s reliability.ResultsThe study sample comprised 89 individuals. Results suggested a strong correlation between the long version of the LAKE score and the UPRAL (rp = 0.54, p < 0.001), which is considered an objective measure for the dietary exposure to precursors of non-volatile acids. Likewise, we observed a significant correlation between the PRALR and the LAKE score (rp = 0.65, p < 0.001). Associations were comparable when using the short version of the LAKE score. Both scores indicated good reliability (ICC of the long version: 0.86; ICC of the short version: 0.87).ConclusionThe German version of the LAKE score is a rapid, inexpensive and feasible tool for an initial PRAL evaluation in a clinical context, characterized by a good validity and reliability. Results suggest that the German LAKE score could be suitable as an estimate for an individual’s PRAL, and could also guide clinicians to make dietary recommendations and monitor PRAL-relevant changes in individual patients.Keywords: Acid–base balance, kidney health, LAKE score, nutrition, plant-based diet, potential renal acid load, vegetarian, vegan

Introduction

Food intake and diet composition are important determinants of the acid-based equilibrium in humans [1–3]. A low intake of base precursors (in the form of potassium salts of metabolizable organic anions) paired with a high intake of acid precursors (such as sulfur-containing amino acids and phosphorus) may result in acid stress [1,4,5].

The acid-stress continuum defined by Wesson has ‘chronic metabolic acidosis at its most extreme end and high-acid-producing diets at the other, less extreme, yet likewise detrimental, end’ [5,6]. The deleterious health effects of acid stress have recently gained a lot of attention [2,5,6], as growing evidence supports kidney injury and other adverse health conditions from acid challenges to the acid–base status [6–9]. The Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) from the National Kidney Foundation thus emphasized in its dietary recommendations for chronic kidney disease patients that an ‘increased dietary intake of fruits and vegetables’ is suggested to reduce net acid production and to ‘reduce the rate of decline of residual kidney function’ in these individuals [1,10].

Of note, determining a diet’s impact on acid production in humans is technically challenging, and multiple predictive equations exist [11]. In dietetics and in clinical practice, the PRAL (potential renal acid load) is the most common estimation method [12,13]. Introduced by Remer and Manz, the PRALR estimation formula considers absorption rates for various nutrients (protein, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and phosphate) and has been validated against urine pH levels in healthy adults [13,14]. For a reliable PRALR estimation, however, individually representative and quantitative nutrient intake data is required. This often poses a challenge in daily clinical practice and requires the completion of food frequency questionnaires or weighed food diaries over a certain period of time, which are often subject to recall and reporting bias. Urinary laboratory measurements are available, as well, with the UPRAL (urinary PRAL) estimation being a widespread method [11,15]. Notably, the UPRAL determination requires a 24-h urine collection, which comes with its own challenges and caveats [16].

One potential solution that allows for a quicker and more pragmatic estimation of the PRAL is the LAKE (load of acid to kidney evaluation) score [17–19]. Developed by the Italian physician A. Trinchieri, the LAKE score has been shown to reliably evaluate the dietary PRAL in kidney stone formers in Italy [17–19]. The LAKE score is a unitless measure; the lower the score, the higher a person’s intake of base precursors. Regrettably, the LAKE score has never been translated into the German language and is thus rarely used in Germany, Austria and Switzerland. We translated the LAKE score into German, and validated the score in a three-armed parallel study in a healthy study population comprised of omnivores, lacto-ovo-vegetarians and vegans with a varying PRAL.

We hypothesized that a German version of the LAKE score would show an at least moderate correlation with the UPRAL and PRALR in omnivores with a regular meat consumption. For dietetic and scientific purposes, we also investigated said correlations in lacto-ovo-vegetarians and vegans, who often consume diets abundant in base precursors.

Materials and methods

General information and course of the study

This study was conducted between October 2024 and January 2025 at the Department of Internal Medicine II (Centre for Complementary Medicine) at Freiburg University Medical Center in Freiburg, Germany. The study employed a non-randomized parallel group design and recruited I) omnivores (on a mixed diet), II) lacto-ovo-vegetarians (defined by excluding animal flesh foods, including milk, dairy and eggs [20]), and III) vegans (defined by excluding all animal-based foods [20]). The ethical committee at the University Medical Center of Freiburg approved this study in August 2024 (approval number: 24-1337-S1). We prospectively registered the study prior to its onset at the German Clinical Trial Register (DRKS00035243) [21]. All study participants provided written informed consent to participate. Figure 1 depicts a study scheme, summarizing the course of the study. This study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study scheme.

Potentially eligible individuals were initially screened by phone and – if eligible – subsequently invited for an in-person appointment at our clinic. Study specific in- and exclusion criteria were carefully checked on both occasions (see below).

After providing informed consent, participants were asked to complete several questionnaires, which included questions on sociodemographic and physical activity data. Anthropometric data was then measured. All participants were measured without shoes, wearing light clothes only, and with empty pockets using a calibrated digital scale and a portable stadiometer. Afterwards, participants were asked to complete the LAKE score in both its short and long version. Upon completion, all participants received an explanation of the further study course.

In a first step, study material was issued to all participants. Regardless of sex and weight, all participants received two 24-h urine collection containers and a written manual. The urine collection process was then explained again as part of the in-person appointment. Participants received weighed food diaries templates for 2 days. Afterwards, the individual schedule for each participant was coordinated. Participants were given up to 3 days (72 h) to start the individual study phase (d1, see Figure 1). The individual phase included 2 days: Day I, where participants had to complete the weighed food diary, and day 2, where participants had to collect 24-h urine and completed another weighed food diary. Once the individual study phase was started, no further interruptions were permitted. The second (and last) in-person appointment (A2) was scheduled right at the morning after day 2 of the individual phase. Participants had to complete this sequence (d1 -> d2 -> A2) within 72 h or were excluded otherwise.

During the second appointment (A2), urine samples were collected and immediately processed. Weighed food diaries were assessed for completeness and participants were instructed to complete the LAKE score for a second time. Participants received a financial remuneration of 50€ for successfully completing the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All in- and exclusion criteria were purposefully selected to minimize the risk of malabsorption-related confounders and other aspects interfering with nutrient metabolism. Only healthy individuals aged 18–60 years were considered for this study. Individuals aged older than 60 years were not considered due to a higher risk of an impaired kidney function. Participants had to be proficient in German in order to understand the translated version of the LAKE score. Participants had to be willing to complete weighed food diaries on 2 days and to collect 24-h-urine on one day. As per the study protocol, only individuals who indicated strict long-term dietary adherence (defined by a period of one year) to either an omnivorous, a lacto-ovo-vegetarian or a vegan diet without interruptions were considered eligible. Participants were asked not to take any supplements during the course of the study.

As for the exclusion criteria, we did not consider individuals with a recent history of antacids or proton pump inhibitor intake, as both may alter an individual’s micronutrient absorption [22]. Individuals with gastrointestinal tract diseases which may be associated with an altered micronutrient absorption, such as chronic inflammatory bowel disease, malabsorption syndromes, or celiac disease were also excluded. Likewise, we did not consider individuals with a history of gastrointestinal operations (except appendectomy) or a history of bariatric surgery, which may also affect nutrient absorption. Additional exclusion criteria included known liver disease or renal insufficiency, a history of a urinary tract infection within the last four weeks, intake of diuretics or uricosurics, vitamin D supplementation within the last two weeks (which may alter calcium resorption), eating disorders, abuse of drugs, alcohol (>20 g/day) or nicotine (daily cigarette consumption) as well as pregnancy and breastfeeding. Individuals with underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m2) or severe overweight (BMI >30 kg/m2) were not considered either. Food consumption-based exclusion criteria included the consumption of more than four cups of coffee (250 ml) per day, consumption of more than 100 g of fresh berries per day, or consumption of more than 20 g of algae per day (dry matter) [23].

Urine collection

Participants received detailed instructions with regard to the urine collection process. Participants were asked to start the urine collection right in the morning after waking up on d2 (see Figure 1) and after having emptied their bladder for the first time. They were instructed to note the time when the collection began and to collect even small amounts of urine during the 24-h period. Urine was collected in brown-colored 3 l Sarstedt urine containers with an inspection strip and with light protection. All participants were asked to store the containers in a refrigerator or outside on their balcony when applicable (e.g. at temperatures between 1 and 7 °C, without sun exposure). Collection ended in the morning of the day when the second appointment took place (A2, see Figure 1) and included the first morning micturition. The total collected urine amount was registered. Collected urine samples were mixed and aliquoted at the study center and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses. The majority of analyses took place at the same day (excluding 24-h urine pH and sulfur excretion). Following the recommendations by Yilmaz et al., collection took place without any preservatives, as this was found to be unnecessary for the measurement of calcium, magnesium, phosphate and uric acid in promptly assayed 24-h urine samples [24].

Laboratory analyses

Laboratory analyses were performed at the accredited Central Laboratory of the University Medical Center of Freiburg (Institute of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine, Medical Centre – University of Freiburg, Hugstetter Str. 55, 79106 Freiburg im Breisgau, Germany). All parameters (except albumin) were measured using Cobas® 8000 modular analyzer series (Roche Diagnostics). For 24-h urine pH measurements, we used a pH meter (Labor-pH Meter inoLab® pH 7110, Xylem Analytics Germany GmbH, 82362 Weilheim, Germany). Urinary sulfate concentrations were measured using the MAK 132 Sulfate Assay Kit from Sigma-Aldrich. 24-h urine pH and sulfate were measured from frozen aliquots in February 2025 for logistic reasons. Said parameters were found to be stable in previous analyses when adequately stored [25]. LAKE scoring

The LAKE score has been described previously in detail [17,18]. In a first step, we contacted its creator, Alberto Trinchieri MD, to obtain an English version of the score. In a second step, we translated the score into German. Following established procedures, we employed a forward–backward translation methodology involving both individuals from the study team and a professional translator proficient in German, English and Italian [26,27]. The employed procedure has been described in detail elsewhere [28]. While often criticized, this approach is still considered the gold standard in medical research and widely employed [27]. The obtained end product was internally checked and distributed among individuals who were unfamiliar with the study goals to gather subjective assessments upon completing the score.

The LAKE score includes the following food groups: grains, meats, cured meats, eggs, cheeses, legumes, potatoes, vegetables, fruit & juices, milk & dairies and bread. Scores for one portion of each food group were as follows: +2, +10, +6, +4, +10, −2, −10, −10, −10, +1 and +1. The evaluation itself is simple: the higher the total score, the higher the amount of acid precursors in the diet. Negative scores suggested a diet with a surplus of base precursors [17,18]. Two report forms exist: a short form (SF) without portion quantities (which allows for a rapid collection of data), and a long form (LF) listing portion quantities. Both scores were translated and used for this analysis (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2).

Nutrient intake estimations

Dietary intake assessment followed previously established procedures [29,30]. Here, we estimated nutrient intakes based on a 2-day weighed food diary. All participants were asked to quantify and list all consumed food items (including tap water and other beverages) to the nearest 0.1 g or ml, respectively. All study participants received detailed instructions in filling in the food diaries by qualified medical personnel. A completed template protocol was provided to aid each participant. Upon request, we provided participants with calibrated precision scales (ProScale XC-2000, HBI Europe, 41812 Erkelenz, Germany). The study attracted many local students who ate at the university hospital cafeteria. In cases where exact weighing was deemed unfeasible (e.g. in the case described above or when students were eating out in a restaurant or bar), semi-quantitative household recordings with usual measures (e.g. spoons, glasses, cups, etc.) were allowed following an established method by Alexy et al. [31]. Participants were instructed to eat and cook at home during the course of the study, when possible, in order to increase the precision of the nutrient intake estimations. NutriGuide® plus software (Version 4.9, Nutri-Science GmbH, 77756 Hausach, Germany) was used for the analysis.

Since many participants reported the regular consumption of tap water, and since mineral intake via tap water may play an important role for PRAL estimations [32], special attention was paid to this aspect. For this, we consulted the homepages of the local drinking water suppliers and obtained the tap water nutrient content for the respective areas (for an example, see [33]). Using participants’ zip codes and personal information obtained during the interviews, we calculated tap water-depended mineral intake on an individual basis for each participant. Covariates

Potentially relevant covariates were assessed in great detail. Important covariates included sociodemographic data (age, ethnicity, marital status), anthropometric data, physical activity data (as assessed by the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire [34]), and supplementation behavior. For the latter, we relied on previously reported procedures [29,30]. All participants were asked to bring any supplement or medication taken within the last 30 days prior to the first appointment (A1, see Figure 1). Supplement names and content as well as intake frequency and quantity were noted, following an approach described earlier [35].

Statistics

The minimum sample size for the primary outcome of this study was estimated a priori using G*Power software. The pre-registered study protocol included two primary outcomes: I) the correlation between the LAKE score and the PRALR in omnivores adhering to a mixed diet, and II) the correlation between the LAKE score and the UPRAL in omnivores adhering to a mixed-balanced diet. With an estimated correlation of ρ = 0.5 and a power of 0.80, a total of 23 omnivorous participants would be needed to detect a significant correlation (α = .05). A null hypothesis with a ρ of 0 served as a reference here (G*Power software output, see Supplementary Figure S3). Anticipating a drop-out rate of approximately 15%, at least 27 participants were recruited per group. Considering the parallel group design, 81 participants were considered necessary in total.

We used scatterplots, Pearson’s correlation coefficients, and Spearman’s correlation coefficients to examine associations between the estimated PRALR, the UPRAL and the LAKE score. Scatterplots were then used to visualize the results. The aforementioned correlations were used to examine the score’s validity, which represents the ability of a questionnaire to determine the measured outcome parameter of interest [28]. Correlations were used over Bland–Altman plots because the LAKE score is unitless, whereas both PRAL estimates have a unit (mEq/d). However, for additional insights, we also constructed Bland–Altman plots with Z score transformed variables. To determine reliability, we sought to calculate the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) [36]. The ICC assesses the strength and direction of the correlation (connection) between repeated measurements of a characteristic on the same individual [37]. Test–retest reliability is generally considered good if the ICC ranges between 0.4 and 0.75 [28]. A score of >0.75 describes an excellent test–retest reliability [28]. In this particular case, we used the user-written Stata command ‘kappaetc’ to estimate ICCs for repeated measures. Given the study structure, this estimated the intrarater reliability. Determining internal consistency using Crohnbach’s alpha was not deemed useful in the present study, due to the very special structure of the questionnaire.

The statistical analysis was performed in Stata 14 software (StataCorp. 2015. Stata Statistical Software: Release 14. College Station, TX: StataCorp LP). The analysis followed previously established procedures [29,30]. Box plots and histograms were used in conjunction with Stata’s Shapiro–Wilk test to test for normality. Normally distributed variables were described with their mean and standard deviation whereas categorical variables were described as follows: number of observations (percentage). For the between group comparisons, we used parametric and non-parametric tests as applicable. When comparing non-normally distributed continuous variables, we used the Kruskal–Wallis H test to determine if there were statistically significant differences between the three groups in the independent variables of interest. For normally distributed data, we used one-way analysis of variance to determine whether the mean of the dependent variable of interest was the same in the three groups. Statistical significance was determined at α = 0.05.

Discussion

The present study sought to translate the Italian version of the LAKE score into the German language and validated it in a three-armed parallel trial in a healthy study population comprising 3 different dietary patterns. Our results suggest a significant and strong correlation between the long version of the LAKE score and the UPRAL, which is considered a direct and objective measure for the dietary exposure to precursors of non-volatile acids [38]. Likewise, we observed a strong association between the PRALR and the LAKE score. Associations were comparable when using the short version of the LAKE score. Both scores indicated good reliability, as shown by an ICC of 0.86 for the long version and an ICC of 0.87 for the short version of the LAKE score. Significant between group differences were found for the LAKE score as well as for the UPRAL and PRALR, with vegans having the highest intake of alkali precursors.

The impact of foods and different dietary patterns on the acid–base equilibrium has recently gained a lot of attention [2]. Original investigations as well as systematic reviews repeatedly emphasized that a high dietary acid load may have unfavorable effects on human health [1,2,39–42], in particular with regard to (chronic) kidney disease [6–9]. The KDOQI thus emphasized in its dietary recommendations for chronic kidney disease patients that an ‘increased dietary intake of fruits and vegetables’ is suggested to reduce net acid production and to ‘reduce the rate of decline of residual kidney function’ in these individuals [1,10]. Assessing the PRAL is thus of high clinical importance for both disease prevention and treatment. The currently available predictive equations to estimate the PRAL have recently been reviewed by Parmenter et al. [11]. While all equations were shown to be reliable, they also exposed weaknesses and the measurement of urinary markers was thus strongly encouraged. This, however, is often difficult in daily clinical practice and requires a 24-h urine collection [11]. The PRAL may also be estimated from nutrient intakes [12]. Again, this requires reliable dietary intake data derived from food frequency questionnaires or weighed food diaries. As a consequence, both methods do not allow for a rapid estimation of the PRAL.

The LAKE score might be of great help in this context, and allows for a quick and efficient estimation of the acid load from diet to the human kidneys [17,18]. Both its forms (the short and the long version) were tested in our study, with a strong positive correlation between both scores. While not quantitatively assessed, we recognized during the study that the long version was preferred by many participants due to the listed food quantities. The score may be completed in less than 4 min and may be particularly useful to guide medical nutrition therapies when repetitive measurements are performed over a certain period. A reservation must be made, however, that the score is not intended to precisely assess the PRAL. In fact, our findings suggest that the score would be more suitable as an estimate to determine whether an individual’s PRAL is very high, high, low or very low. For more precise estimations, 24-h urinary collection or the usage of weighed food diaries might still be indicated. Nevertheless, the score might be a useful information for clinicians to make dietary recommendations and monitor PRAL-relevant changes in individual patients.

It must be emphasized that the score was originally not designed for the German cuisine [18]. In fact, the score’s creator explicitly emphasized that it could be less ‘appropriate for people living in other nations’ [18]. Moreover, the score dates back to the early 2000s, and many potentially PRAL-relevant food groups (such as soft drinks) were not included. This applies in particular for foods that are particularly relevant in vegetarian and vegan diets, including plant-based meat alternatives, edible mushrooms and plant-based milks [43–45]. This could in fact explain the non-significant correlation in the vegetarian subgroup. Although our study recruited slightly more participants than necessary based on the sample size calculations, we acknowledge the modest sample size. Participants were recruited in blocks of two and over-recruitment happened because the dropout rate was much lower than expected, potentially due to the offered remuneration. The study mainly recruited Caucasians and statements with regard to other ethnicities may thus not be made. Further studies might be warranted in this particular context.

Finally, some authors recommend urine collection under oil to limit the escape of CO2 on air exposure for a more accurate measurement of urinary pH [46–48]. This was not done in the present study. 24-h urinary pH values without collection under oil could thus overestimate the actual pH value, especially in those samples that had a higher initial pH, while the measurement is considered more reliable for samples with a pH value <6.0. This could have accentuated the pH differences between omnivores and vegetarians/vegans, who tend to present with higher urine pH values. While we transparently acknowledge this limitation, urinary pH was not the primary outcome of this study.

Despite these limitations, we overall observed a strong positive association with the UPRAL and believe in the potential usefulness of the LAKE score. Additional strengths of the study include the thorough consideration of potential confounders, the supplement intake assessment, the consideration of local tap water’s mineral content and the fact that three different dietary patterns were considered. The usage of weighed food diaries is considered the gold standard method to accurately capture nutrient intakes and further adds to the study [49]. This allowed us to meticulously capture the intake of table salt and other condiments, which may have contributed to the PRAL of individual study participants. Measured creatine values in 24-h urine samples suggested that urinary undercollection was not prevalent in the study sample.

In future food-based studies, we intend to investigate whether a modified LAKE form (e.g. a form that also includes soft drinks and vegan convenience products) would yield an even higher validity. We also intend to examine associations with vegetarian-diet specific food groups.

Our results also reiterate that noticeable PRAL differences exist between the different plant-based dietary patterns [1,50–52]. Consistent with previous investigations, our data suggest that a vegan diet results in the lowest PRAL score (highest intake of base precursors) [1]. Vegetarian diets range between vegan and omnivorous diets, and often result in a slightly negative total PRAL score. A lower protein intake corresponding with lower sulfate and lower phosphate intakes appear to be the main drivers of these between group difference [1,3,15,52–54].

Conclusions

The German version of the LAKE score may constitute a rapid, inexpensive and feasible tool for the initial PRAL evaluation in a clinical context. The herein presented translated version of the score is characterized by a very good validity and reliability.
