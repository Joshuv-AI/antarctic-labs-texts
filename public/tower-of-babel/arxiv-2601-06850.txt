Explosion and non-explosion in pure birth Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes
Oleksii Galganov, Andrii Ilienko

Abstract
In this short note, we provide an explicit sufficient condition for non-explosion of Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes with pure birth reproduction. It shows that the standard sufficient condition for explosion, namely the convergence of the series of reciprocals of the birth rates, is — at least for rate sequences without excessive oscillations — remarkably close to being necessary. At the same time, it is not necessary in full generality: we construct a counterexample which also yields a general preferential attachment tree without fitness with an infinite path and no vertices of infinite degree, thereby answering an open question previously raised in the literature.



Keywords:
Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes; pure birth processes; random recursive trees; preferential attachment.

1
Introduction

Crump–Mode–Jagers (CMJ) branching processes, that is, branching processes constructed from general reproduction point processes (see, e.g., Chapter 6 in
[
5
]
), form a very broad class. They are defined as follows. The initial ancestor is born at time
0
. Its children are born at times given by the atoms of a reproduction point process
\xi
. Each child then evolves in the same way, using an independent copy of
\xi
shifted to start at the child’s birth time, and the construction continues recursively. In particular, writing
\delta_{a}
for the unit point mass at
a
, if
\xi=N\delta_{T}
for random
\mathds{N}_{0}
-valued
N
and
\mathds{R}_{+}
-valued
T
, we obtain the Sevastyanov splitting process. Special cases include the Bellman–Harris process when
N
and
T
are independent, and the Galton–Watson process when
T
is constant. Many further examples can be found in
[
6
]
. We do not describe the construction above in full formal detail, referring the reader instead to any of the recent papers
[
3
,
4
,
6
,
7
]
if needed.

In this note, we consider a pure birth CMJ branching process, that is, a CMJ process whose reproduction
\xi
is given by a pure birth point process with rates
\lambda_{i}
,
i\geq 1
.
Recall that a pure birth counting process is a continuous-time Markov chain
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathds{N}
, started from
X_{0}=1
1
1

1

The state space is usually taken to be
\mathds{N}_{0}
and the process is started at
0
. We adopt a shift by one for notational convenience.
,
with infinitesimal generator matrix
G=(g_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathds{N}}
given by

g_{i,i+1}=\lambda_{i},\qquad g_{ii}=-\lambda_{i},\qquad g_{ij}=0\text{ for }j\notin\{i,i+1\},\quad i\in\mathds{N};

see, e.g., Section 2.5 in
[
8
]
. The associated pure birth point process is
\xi=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\delta_{T_{i}}
,
where
T_{i}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\,X_{t}>i\}
. It is well known that

(T_{i})_{i\geq 1}\overset{d}{=}\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{i}E_{j}\Biggr)_{i\geq 1},

where
E_{j}
,
j\geq 1
, are independent
\mathsf{Exp}(\lambda_{j})
random variables.

Our interest in pure birth CMJ processes stems from the fact that they play a central role in the study of preferential attachment random recursive trees. We recall the basic definitions. Let
f:\mathds{N}_{0}\to(0,\infty)
be a fixed function. We now construct a consistent sequence
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}
of random trees as follows. The tree
\mathcal{T}_{0}
consists of a single vertex
0
. At step
n\geq 1
, a new vertex
n
appears and an edge is added from an existing vertex
m
to
n
, where
m
is chosen with probability proportional to
f
of its current out-degree:

\mathds{P}\{m\to n\}=\frac{f(\deg_{n-1}^{+}(m))}{\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f(\deg_{n-1}^{+}(j))},\qquad m=0,\ldots,n-1.

(1)

Here the out-degree
\deg_{n-1}^{+}(m)
is the number of children of
m
in
\mathcal{T}_{n-1}
, that is, immediately before
n
is attached. One also considers preferential attachment trees with fitness, where
f
in (
1
) depends not only on the out-degree of
m
but also on its random weight
W_{m}
, with
(W_{m})_{m\in\mathds{N}_{0}}
forming an i.i.d. sequence; see, e.g.,
[
3
,
4
,
7
]
.

The connection between preferential attachment trees and CMJ processes was first observed in
[
10
]
and was used there to study the asymptotics of the height of trees with affine preferential attachment. Subsequently, this approach has become standard for affine and general preferential attachment trees; see, e.g.,
[
2
,
9
,
11
]
. The core of this connection, going back to the classical poissonization technique, is as follows. Let
\tau_{n}
,
n\geq 0
, be the birth time of the
n
th individual in the pure birth CMJ process with rates
\lambda_{i}=f(i-1)
,
i\in\mathds{N}
. Then the sequence of genealogical trees of this process, observed at times
\tau_{n}
, after relabelling the vertices in each tree in chronological order of birth, has the same distribution as
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}_{0}}
. This remains true for preferential attachment trees with fitness as well (see Proposition 3.14 in
[
3
]
), but in this case one has to replace the pure birth CMJ process by a CMJ process that is pure birth conditionally on the random weights.

Recall that explosion in a CMJ process is the event
\mathcal{E}=\{\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_{n}<\infty\}
, that is, the birth of infinitely many individuals in finite time. It follows from Proposition 3.5 in
[
3
]
that for a pure birth CMJ process one has
\mathds{P}(\mathcal{E})\in\{0,1\}
. In view of the above discussion, the question of necessary and sufficient conditions for
\mathds{P}(\mathcal{E})=1
becomes particularly important. Specifically, the presence or absence of explosion in the associated pure birth CMJ process determines the shape of the corresponding preferential attachment tree without fitness — namely, whether it contains infinite stars, that is, vertices of infinite degree, and/or infinite paths. Unfortunately, even if such conditions exist in principle in a reasonably explicit form, deriving them still seems to be out of reach at present. This stands in sharp contrast to, say, Bellman–Harris branching processes, for which such conditions are known in many cases; see, e.g.,
[
1
]
. The general operator approach, presented in its most complete form in Section 2 of
[
6
]
, does not yield any explicit conditions either.

In this situation, one can only rely on sufficient conditions for explosion. A simple condition stated directly in terms of the rates, namely

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{-1}<\infty,

(2)

follows immediately from the standard explosion criterion for the non-branching pure birth process. In part (ii) of Theorem
1
, we give a fairly general sufficient condition for non-explosion. As shown in Remark
1
, it implies that (
2
), at least within the class of rate sequences that do not oscillate too strongly, turns out to be remarkably close to being a necessary condition for explosion. Part (iii), however, shows by means of a rather pathological sequence of rates that it is not exactly necessary in general. This yields an example of a general preferential attachment tree without fitness with no infinite stars and a unique infinite path; see Remark
2
. In turn, this answers the open question raised in Section 3.4 of
[
4
]
concerning whether such a situation can occur.

2
Main result and discussion

The following theorem provides sufficient conditions for explosion/non-explosion of a pure birth CMJ process. For completeness, we include (
2
) as (i).

Theorem 1
.

1.

If
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{-1}<\infty
, then the process is explosive.

2.

If

\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{i}=\infty

(3)

and

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\!\!\sum_{i=\lceil\varepsilon\log n\rceil}^{n}\!\!\lambda_{i}^{-1}>0\quad\text{for some $\varepsilon>0$},

(4)

then the process is non-explosive.

The same holds if
(\lambda_{i})
is dominated by a sequence satisfying (
3
) and (
4
).

3.

There exists a sequence
(\lambda_{i})
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{-1}=\infty
and the process is explosive.

Remark 1
.

Consider the class of sequences
(\lambda_{i})
that may come very close to the convergence boundary of the series
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{-1}
:

\lambda_{i}=\mathcal{O}\bigl(i\cdot\log i\cdot\log\log i\cdot\ldots\cdot\log^{(k)}i\bigr)\quad\text{for some }k\geq 1,

where
\log^{(k)}
denotes the
k
-fold iterated logarithm. Since, as
c>0
and
n\to\infty
,

\sum_{i=\lceil\log n\rceil}^{n}\Bigl(c\hskip 1.0pti\cdot\log i\cdot\log\log i\cdot\ldots\cdot\log^{(k)}i\Bigr)^{-1}\!\!\sim c^{-1}\bigl(\log^{(k+1)}n-\log^{(k+2)}n\bigr)\to\infty,

(5)

the process is non-explosive by (ii). The substantial gap between (
4
) and (
5
) leaves ample room for constructing non-explosive sequences of rates much closer to the convergence boundary.

This example shows that the sufficient condition for explosion in (i) is, in a sense, very close to being necessary, whereas (iii) demonstrates that it is not exactly so.

Remark 2
.

Consider the preferential attachment tree without fitness, which is the discrete-time skeleton of the explosive CMJ process in (iii). By item 3 of Theorem 3.16 in
[
3
]
, this tree has a unique infinite path and no infinite stars, that is, vertices of infinite degree. A related example was constructed in Corollary 3.19 of
[
3
]
in the context of linear preferential attachment trees with heavy-tailed fitness, in particular in the Bianconi–Barabási model. (iii) shows that, surprisingly, the same effect can occur for some general preferential attachment trees with no fitness at all. As already mentioned, this answers the open question raised in Section 3.4 of
[
4
]
.

Thus, all three possible shapes of preferential attachment trees that are known to arise in the presence of fitness — (a) every vertex is an infinite star and every path is infinite, (b) a unique infinite star and no infinite paths, and (c) a unique infinite path and no infinite stars — can also be obtained without fitness: (a) occurs whenever the associated CMJ process is non-explosive; (b) is realized, for instance, in super-linear power preferential attachment (Theorem 1.2 in
[
9
]
) as well as under the assumptions of Corollary 2.16 in
[
7
]
; and (c) corresponds to the explosive CMJ process in (iii).

Remark 3
.

The rates
\lambda_{i}
used in the proof of (iii) are highly irregular: they take astronomically large values along long stretches, while returning to the baseline value
1
infinitely often, at an increasingly sparse set of indices. We were not able to construct an example with more regular rates — for instance, increasing or regularly varying ones. It would be interesting to determine whether such a construction is possible at all.

Proof of Theorem
1

As noted above, (i) follows immediately from the standard explosion criterion for a usual pure birth process, since in this case even the initial individual produces infinitely many offspring in finite time.

For (ii), first fix
\varepsilon>0
. Since

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\to 0,\\
r\to\infty\end{subarray}}\Bigl(t+\frac{2}{\beta}\Bigr)\Bigl(1+\frac{\beta}{r\varepsilon}\Bigr)=0,

by (
4
) we may choose
t,\beta,r>0
such that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\!\!\sum_{i=\lceil\varepsilon\log n\rceil}^{n}\!\!\lambda_{i}^{-1}>\Bigl(t+\frac{2}{\beta}\Bigr)\Bigl(1+\frac{\beta}{r\varepsilon}\Bigr).

(6)

Moreover, by (
3
),
i\geq\lceil\varepsilon\log n\rceil
implies
\lambda_{i}\geq ri\geq r\varepsilon\log n
for large
n
, and hence
\beta\log n\leq\frac{\beta\lambda_{i}}{r\varepsilon}
. Therefore, by (
6
),

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}+\beta\log n}\geq\sum_{i=\lceil\varepsilon\log n\rceil}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}+\beta\log n}\geq\Bigl(1+\frac{\beta}{r\varepsilon}\Bigr)^{-1}\!\!\!\sum_{i=\lceil\varepsilon\log n\rceil}^{n}\!\lambda_{i}^{-1}>t+\frac{2}{\beta}

(7)

for large
n
.

It is well known that, to prove non-explosion of a CMJ process constructed from a reproduction point process
\xi
with no atom at the origin, it suffices to show that
\xi
has a finite intensity measure in a neighborhood of the origin, that is,

\mathds{E}\xi\bigl([0,t]\bigr)<\infty

(8)

for some
t>0
; see Theorem 6.2.2 in
[
5
]
or Theorem 3.1 (b) in
[
6
]
.

Let
E_{i}\sim\mathsf{Exp}(\lambda_{i})
,
i\geq 1
, be independent. Then, in our case,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\xi\bigl([0,t]\bigr)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathds{P}\biggl\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}\leq t\biggr\}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathds{P}\biggl\{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E_{i}\log n}\geq\mathrm{e}^{-\beta t\log n}\biggr\}

(9)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\beta t\log n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{E}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E_{i}\log n}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\beta t\log n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}+\beta\log n}.

The
n
th term on the right-hand side is

\exp\biggl\{\beta t\log n+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\Bigl(1-\frac{\beta\log n}{\lambda_{i}+\beta\log n}\Bigr)\biggr\}\leq\exp\Bigl\{\beta\log n\Bigl(t-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}+\beta\log n}\Bigr)\biggr\},

which, in view of (
7
), is bounded by
n^{-2}
for large
n
.
Hence, the series on the right-hand side of (
9
) converges, and (
8
) follows.

If
(\lambda_{i})
is only dominated by a sequence satisfying (
3
) and (
4
), it suffices to increase all
E_{i}
accordingly, which does not cause explosion. This completes the proof of (ii).

We now turn to the construction of an example for (iii). By Theorem 3.8 in
[
3
]
, to prove explosion of a CMJ process with reproduction point process
\xi
, it suffices to show that

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1-\mathds{P}\bigl\{\xi\bigl([0,t_{i}]\bigr)>M_{i+1}\bigr\}\bigr)^{M_{i}}<\infty

for some
t_{i}>0
with
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t_{i}<\infty
and some
M_{i}\in\mathds{N}
. Since
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-i}<\infty
and
\log(1-x)\leq-x
, it is enough to prove that

\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{M_{i}}{i}\cdot\mathds{P}\bigl\{\xi\bigl([0,t_{i}]\bigr)>M_{i+1}\bigr\}=\infty.

(10)

Consider a pure birth CMJ process with rates
\lambda_{i}
given by

1,\underbrace{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{1}}_{d_{1}\text{ times}},1,\underbrace{\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{2}}_{d_{2}\text{ times}},1,\ldots

(11)

with
d_{k}=4^{k^{2}}
and
\alpha_{k}=2^{2^{k^{3}}}
. Clearly,
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{-1}=\infty
. Set
t_{i}=2^{-i}
and
M_{i}=i+\sum_{k=1}^{i}d_{k}
, so that
M_{i}>4^{i^{2}}
. For independent
E_{j}\sim\mathsf{Exp}(\lambda_{j})
, we have:

\mathds{P}\bigl\{\xi\bigl([0,t_{i}]\bigr)>M_{i+1}\bigr\}=\mathds{P}\Biggl\{\sum_{j=1}^{M_{i+1}+1}E_{j}\leq t_{i}\Biggr\}\\
=\mathds{P}\Biggl\{E_{1}+\!\sum_{j=2}^{1+d_{1}}E_{j}+E_{2+d_{1}}+\!\!\sum_{j=3+d_{1}}^{2+d_{1}+d_{2}}\!E_{j}+\ldots+\!\!\!\sum_{j=i+2+\sum_{k=1}^{i}d_{k}}^{i+1+\sum_{k=1}^{i+1}d_{k}}\!\!E_{j}+E_{i+2+\sum_{k=1}^{i+1}d_{k}}\leq 2^{-i}\Biggr\}.

In view of (
11
), all single summands above are
\mathsf{Exp}(1)
, the terms in the first sum are
\mathsf{Exp}(\alpha_{1})
, and so on, with the terms in the last sum being
\mathsf{Exp}(\alpha_{i+1})
. Denote the sum of all single summands by
S_{0}
, and the
k
th sum by
S_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,i+1
. Then
S_{0}\sim\mathsf{\Gamma}(i+2,1)
,
S_{k}\sim\mathsf{\Gamma}(d_{k},\alpha_{k})
, and all these are independent. Hence,

\mathds{P}\bigl\{\xi\bigl([0,t_{i}]\bigr)>M_{i+1}\bigr\}\geq\mathds{P}\bigl\{S_{0}\leq 2^{-i-1}\bigr\}\prod_{k=1}^{i+1}\mathds{P}\bigl\{S_{k}\leq 2^{-k-i-1}\bigr\}.

(12)

Let
X\sim\mathsf{\Gamma}(d,\alpha)
with
d\in\mathds{N}
. Then, for any
t>0
, writing
\alpha t=y
, we have:

\mathds{P}\{X\leq t\}=\frac{\alpha^{d}}{(d-1)!}\int_{0}^{t}x^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha x}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\frac{\alpha^{d}\mathrm{e}^{-y}}{(d-1)!}\int_{0}^{t}x^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{y^{d}\mathrm{e}^{-y}}{d\hskip 1.0pt!}\geq\mathrm{e}^{-y}\Bigl(\frac{y}{d}\Bigr)^{d}.

(13)

Therefore,

\mathds{P}\bigl\{S_{0}\leq 2^{-i-1}\bigr\}\geq\mathrm{e}^{-2^{-i-1}}\biggl(\frac{2^{-i-1}}{i+2}\biggr)^{i+2},

and a simple calculation shows that the right-hand side dominates

i\hskip 1.0pt3^{-i^{2}}
as
i\to\infty
.
The remainder of the proof is devoted to showing that

\lim_{i\to\infty}\biggl(\frac{4}{3}\biggr)^{\!i^{2}}\!\!\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{i+1}\mathds{P}\bigl\{S_{k}\leq 2^{-k-i-1}\bigr\}=\infty,

(14)

which, combined with the previous bound and the inequalities (
12
) and
M_{i}>4^{i^{2}}
, yields (
10
).

Let
t_{i,k}=2^{-k-i-1}
and
y_{i,k}=\alpha_{k}t_{i,k}=2^{2^{k^{3}}-k-i-1}
.
For
k=1
, we have
d_{1}=\alpha_{1}=4
and
y_{i,1}=2^{-i}\leq 1
, and hence, by (
13
),

\mathds{P}\{S_{1}\leq t_{i,1}\}\geq\mathrm{e}^{-1}2^{-4i-8}.

(15)

The remaining factors will be bounded in two different ways: for large
k
by Markov’s inequality, and for small
k
by (
13
).
Define

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{A}_{i}=\bigl\{k\in\{2,\ldots,i+1\}:2^{k^{3}}\geq 2k^{2}+k+i+2\bigr\},\\
\mathcal{B}_{i}=\bigl\{k\in\{2,\ldots,i+1\}:2^{k^{3}}\leq 2k^{2}+k+i+1\bigr\}.\end{gathered}

(16)

For
k\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
, we have

\mathds{E}S_{k}=\frac{d_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}=2^{2k^{2}-2^{k^{3}}}
, and hence, by Markov’s inequality and the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
,

\mathds{P}\{S_{k}\leq t_{i,k}\}\geq 1-\frac{\mathds{E}S_{k}}{t_{i,k}}=1-2^{-2^{k^{3}}+2k^{2}+k+i+1}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

(17)

For
k\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
, we have
0\geq 2^{k^{3}}-2k^{2}-k-i-1\geq-i
, which implies

2^{-i}\leq\frac{y_{i,k}}{d_{k}}\leq 1
.
Thus, by (
13
),

\mathds{P}\{S_{k}\leq t_{i,k}\}\geq\mathrm{e}^{-d_{k}}\,2^{-id_{k}}.

(18)

Let
\lvert\cdot\rvert
denote the cardinality of a set and
D_{i}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{B}_{i}}d_{k}
. Combining (
15
), (
17
), (
18
), we obtain

\prod_{k=1}^{i+1}\mathds{P}\bigl\{S_{k}\leq 2^{-k-i-1}\bigr\}\geq 2^{-|\mathcal{A}_{i}|-iD_{i}-4i-8}\mathrm{e}^{-D_{i}-1}\geq 2^{-iD_{i}-5i-8}\mathrm{e}^{-D_{i}-1}.

(19)

From (
16
) it is apparent that
\lvert\mathcal{B}_{i}\rvert
is of order
(\log_{2}i)^{1/3}
. More rigorously, elementary but routine calculations show that

\lfloor(\log_{2}i)^{1/3}\rfloor+2\notin\mathcal{B}_{i}
for large
i
; thus,
\lvert\mathcal{B}_{i}\rvert<(\log_{2}i)^{1/3}
for such
i
. Hence, since
d_{k}
are increasing, we have

D_{i}\leq d_{\lfloor(\log_{2}i)^{1/3}\rfloor+1}\hskip 1.0pt\lvert\mathcal{B}_{i}\rvert<4^{\left((\log_{2}i)^{1/3}+1\right)^{2}}(\log_{2}i)^{1/3}.

(20)

A straightforward verification shows that the right-hand side of (
19
), together with the bound (
20
), satisfies (
14
). This completes the proof of (iii).
