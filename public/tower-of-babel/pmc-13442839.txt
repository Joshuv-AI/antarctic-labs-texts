Temporal evolution of spatial water quality heterogeneity in Oman’s largest water supply reservoir
Authors: Mohammad Raoof Rashedi, Mohammad Reza Nikoo, Sadegh Vanda, Reza Kerachian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Maintaining optimal water quality in reservoirs is a global challenge, particularly in arid or semi-arid regions where these water bodies are critical yet vulnerable ecosystems. A prerequisite for effective environmental preservation is the precise delineation of spatiotemporal and vertical variability in water-quality variables, especially across a complete seasonal cycle. This study was therefore designed to systematically investigate and document the detailed spatial and seasonal variation in the water quality of the Wadi Dayqah Dam (Oman). This characterization was based on a robust, year-long dataset spanning 19 separate sampling occasions and incorporating high-resolution depth profile measurements of temperature, dissolved oxygen (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{D}\mathrm{O}$$\end{document}), oxidation-reduction potential (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{O}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}$$\end{document}), pH, turbidity and chlorophyll-a from 20 distinct depth-profiling stations. Self-organizing map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:(\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document}) was used to identify spatial zones, while thermal stratification dynamics were quantified using the Schmidt Stability Index (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{I}$$\end{document}) and the Thermocline Strength Index (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{T}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{I}$$\end{document}). This integrated approach combines high-resolution spatial clustering with complementary stratification metrics to reveal zone-specific water quality patterns typically obscured by single-point monitoring. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document} differentiated distinctly phased lacustrine, transitional, and riverine regions with spatial heterogeneity in water quality, with pronounced longitudinal gradients in temperature, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{D}\mathrm{O}$$\end{document}, turbidity, and chlorophyll-a across zones, an aspect traditionally overlooked by localized single-point, as opposed to distributed, observation. This spatial heterogeneity highlights the importance of zone-specific reservoir management strategies. On-site, observations revealed pronounced yearly patterns of thermal stratification and mixing, with the most intense and persistent stratification occurring in the lacustrine zone, where Schmidt stability reached a maximum of approximately 1144 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{J}{\:\mathrm{m}}^{-2}$$\end{document}. Notably, chemical stratification closely followed the temporal dynamics of thermal stratification, resulting in pronounced hypolimnetic anoxia in the deeper lacustrine waters, typically persisting from May to October. In contrast, suspended particulate matter and organic substances exerted a stronger control on turbidity and chlorophyll-a concentrations, with the latter reaching peak values of approximately 27 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upmu\:}\mathrm{g}\:\mathrm{L}^{-1}$$\end{document}. Introduction

Reservoirs constitute indispensable infrastructure for global water security, supplying domestic, agricultural, and industrial demands, while also generating hydropower1–3. Yet their long-term viability hinges on water quality management, a challenge compounded by these systems’ inherent dynamics. Unlike static pools, reservoirs function as complex, spatially heterogeneous ecosystems where water quality fluctuates across seasons, depths, and longitudinal gradients4–6. Capturing this multidimensional variability remains pivotal for safeguarding their ecological and operational integrity.

Central to this complexity is stratification: the formation of density-separated layers that govern biogeochemical trajectories7–9. Thermal stratification separates the water column into a warm, oxygenated surface layer (epilimnion) and a cold, isolated deep layer (hypolimnion), with a sharp temperature gradient (thermocline or metalimnion) between them. Once stratified, vertical mixing is suppressed, cutting off the hypolimnion from atmospheric oxygen supply. Without replenishment, decomposition of organic matter consumes the remaining oxygen until anoxic conditions develop7,10,11. Critically, this physical barrier stifles reaeration, enabling organic matter decomposition that leads hypolimnetic dissolved oxygen (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{D}\mathrm{O}$$\end{document}) to anoxic (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 2\:{\mathrm{mg}}\:{\mathrm{L}}^{{ - 1}}$$\end{document}) conditions12–14. Such conditions trigger cascading deterioration: sediment-bound phosphorus and nitrogen mobilize under reducing conditions, fueling algal blooms15,16, while redox-sensitive metals (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{F}\mathrm{e}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{n}$$\end{document}) and toxic gases like \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{H}}_{2} {\mathrm{S}}$$\end{document} (hydrogen sulfide) leach into the water column, compromising drinking water safety through taste, odor, and corrosion issues17–19. Though thermal structure dominates these processes, nutrient influxes20–22, light availability23, and inflow/outflow regimes22,24 further modulate biological responses such as chlorophyll-a proliferation.

Quantifying stratification thus demands integrated stability metrics. While indices like the Brunt-Väisälä frequency or Thermocline Strength Index (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{T}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{I}$$\end{document}) capture the intensity of thermal gradients25,26, others, such as the Wedderburn Number (which characterizes resistance to wind-driven mixing)27 or Schmidt Stability Index (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{I}$$\end{document}; representing the total energy required to fully homogenize the water column)28–30, quantify the resilience of the stratified system. Equally vital is chemical stratification, which may decouple from thermal patterns (e.g., Sabalan Reservoir13 yet critically influences water quality14,31. The dimensionless Index of Chemical Stratification (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{I}\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{i}$$\end{document}) introduced by Yu et al. (2010) was employed to assess chemocline strength: higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{I}\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{i}$$\end{document} values, reflecting greater epilimnetic than hypolimnetic solute concentrations, were used to distinguish systems dominated by biogeochemical reactions from those controlled by physical mixing25,32,33.

A critical methodological gap persists, however. Most stratification studies extrapolate from single-point measurements at deep stations13,14,34, ignoring reservoirs’ longitudinal compartmentalization into riverine, transitional, and lacustrine zones, each with distinct hydrodynamics and biogeochemistry35,36. This spatial oversimplification not only biases stability indices but also obscures lateral and longitudinal water quality gradients, leading to flawed management decisions. For instance, undetected anoxic conditions in shallow riverine or transitional zones may result in poorly sited or undersized aeration systems, reducing their effectiveness. Similarly, nutrient control strategies based solely on deep-station data may misallocate resources if pollution hotspots arise in under-sampled littoral or inflow-affected regions. Consequently, spatially blind monitoring undermines both diagnostic accuracy and the efficiency of remediation efforts. To resolve such complexity, multivariate techniques offer promise: Cluster Analysis (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{C}\mathrm{A}$$\end{document}) delineates spatial heterogeneity (e.g., Turkish37, Chinese38, Korean39, Indian40, and Ethiopian reservoirs41,42, Principal Component Analysis (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{P}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{A}$$\end{document}) identifies drivers like natural vs. anthropogenic solute sources43–45, and Discriminant Analysis (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{D}\mathrm{A}$$\end{document}) validates zonal classifications while pinpointing key monitoring variables46,47. Together, they enable source apportionment and targeted intervention design.

This study confronts these gaps at Oman’s Wadi Dayqah Dam, the nation’s largest reservoir, by leveraging a high-resolution spatiotemporal dataset (20 depth-profiling stations across 19 sampling campaigns) and novel analytical integration. The study’s main contributions are threefold: (1) High-resolution spatial zoning via Self-Organizing Map (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document}), capturing holistic water quality patterns across longitudinal gradients; (2) Cross-zonal stratification diagnostics through site-specific and reservoir-wide stability indices (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{I}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{T}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{I}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{I}\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{i}$$\end{document}); (3) Actionable insights for management by explicitly linking spatial heterogeneity to water quality outcomes.

Study area

Wadi Dayqah Dam (23°5′N, 58°49′E), located approximately 83 km southwest of Muscat, Oman (Fig. 1a, b), is the nation’s largest reservoir with a storage capacity of 100 million cubic meters (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document}). It serves as the primary source of potable water for the Muscat and Qurrayat regions and provides flood mitigation for downstream communities48. The reservoir lies in an arid to semi‑arid environment with low, highly variable rainfall and high evaporative demand, conditions that promote strong and persistent thermal stratification48,49. Such stratification, together with long residence times, can favor oxygen depletion in deep waters and redox‑driven changes in nutrient and metal cycling, as shown in stratified reservoirs under similar climates50. Based on the reservoir’s hypsometric relationship (See Fig. S3), the water surface area at full supply level is approximately 400 ha (4.0\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{km}}^{{\mathrm{2}}}$$\end{document}), with a maximum depth of ~ 71 m near the dam wall. The bathymetry exhibits relatively steep-sided morphology in the lacustrine zone, transitioning to shallower, gently sloping margins in the riverine and transitional zones. The flow of water into the reservoir is irregular and occurs in short, intense events. These events are caused mainly by spring rainfall in April and occasional summer storms in the Eastern Hajar Mountains. During these times, flash floods move through the wadi channels, carrying large amounts of sediment, nutrients, and organic matter into the reservoir. This creates strong variations in water quality along the length of the reservoir51,52. Water is withdrawn via an intake tower equipped with six gated outlets at elevations of 124, 128, 137, 146, 155, and 164 m above sea level. During the 2023 study period, active withdrawals occurred primarily from the 128, 146, and 155 m gates to supply: (1) drinking water (~ 1 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document} month⁻¹) to Muscat and Qurrayat; and (2) biannual agricultural and environmental releases (~ 20 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document} total), mainly to supply the environmental rights of farmers located in the downstream region of the dam50. These coupled hydrometeorological and geomorphological controls make Wadi Dayqah an ideal natural laboratory for examining the interactions among climate forcing, reservoir operations, and biogeochemical processes that determine water quality in arid-region reservoirs49,50.

Materials and methods

The analytical framework for this study is depicted in Fig. 2. High-resolution field monitoring was first integrated with rigorous quality control procedures, followed by feature engineering to prepare the dataset for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document} clustering. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document} analysis partitioned the reservoir into three hydrologically coherent zones (lacustrine, transitional, and riverine)36, which then served as the spatial framework for parallel analysis. In the thermal stratification branch, reservoir-wide and local-scale stratification dynamics were quantified using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{I}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{T}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{I}$$\end{document}, respectively. A k-means clustering approach was subsequently applied to derive an objective, site-specific threshold for stratification onset, following the data-driven methodology established by Castrillo et al. (2024)53. Simultaneously, water quality variables were analyzed within each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document}-derived zone to assess spatial heterogeneity, temporal patterns, and cause-and-effect relationships between stratification phases and water quality impairment. Chemical stratification dynamics were then evaluated using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{I}\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{i}$$\end{document}25, integrated with multivariate diagnostics to dissect the spatiotemporal dynamics of Wadi Dayqah. This pathway was designed to facilitate direct interrogation of how thermal and chemical stratification govern critical water quality variables within each reservoir zone. The approach thereby transcends limitations inherent in conventional single-point assessment methodologies.

In situ water quality sampling and data preparation

Twenty sampling stations were selected from candidate locations based on two criteria applied jointly: (1) spatial representativeness across the full longitudinal gradient from the main inflow to the dam wall, ensuring coverage of all three reservoir zones; and (2) depth distribution, prioritizing stations that capture the full water column depth profile, particularly in the lacustrine zone. Logistical constraints (boat access and equipment deployment within a single campaign day) also constrained the final number to 20. These stations, dispersed throughout the Wadi Dayqah reservoir (Fig. 1c), were then utilized to collect in-situ water quality data. Using an AAQ-RINKO multi-variable water quality profiler (JFE Advantech Co., Ltd.), sampling was conducted over a one-year period comprising 19 campaigns starting from January 15th to December 6th. While monthly coverage was maintained (minimum once per month), frequency varied (1–3 samples/month) to capture seasonal transitions and specific hydrological events. All profiles were collected within a consistent daytime sampling window during each field campaign to maintain comparability among stations and minimize the potential influence of diel variability. In addition, operational data (inflow and withdrawal rates) were recorded to contextualize the hydraulic conditions (see Fig. S2 in supplementary materials). Vertical hydrochemical profiles were acquired from surface to reservoir bottom at all 20 sampling stations by profiler. Continuous measurements were recorded at 2–3 cm depth intervals, generating high-resolution records of temperature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:^\circ\:\mathrm{C}$$\end{document}), turbidity, chlorophyll-a, pH, oxidation-reduction potential (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{O}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}$$\end{document}), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{D}\mathrm{O}$$\end{document}. All raw records underwent quality control: readings affected by bottom disturbance (agitated sludge or sediment) were identified and removed, and turbidity data were filtered to retain only values within the acceptable range of 0.3–6 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{N}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{U}$$\end{document}54. The resulting dataset captures variations in the water quality variables along the length, depth, and width, as well as over time, providing a basis to investigate the reservoir’s spatiotemporal water quality dynamics.

Water quality zoning in the reservoir

Water quality zoning was implemented to delineate reservoir regions with coherent chemical and thermal signatures using an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document}, an unsupervised neural network projecting high-dimensional data onto a low-dimensional grid55. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document} is particularly well-suited to this task because it detects complex, nonlinear patterns and relationships in multivariate datasets without requiring preassigned class labels. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document}s have been successfully deployed in prior water quality studies for spatial classification and pollution source apportionment56,57, a capability extended herein through explicit integration of temporal dimensions into the clustering framework. To encapsulate seasonal cycles and hydroclimatic forcing events, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document} input matrix was compiled from spatiotemporal water quality records across all 20 sampling stations. Clustering features for each station were computed as the minimum, maximum, and mean values of six hydrochemical discriminants: temperature, turbidity, pH, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{D}\mathrm{O}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{O}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}$$\end{document}, and chlorophyll-a. This preprocessing step enabled the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document} to delineate zones that concurrently represent spatial heterogeneity and temporal trajectories, capturing reservoir dynamics beyond static spatial snapshots.

Variables exhibiting minimal temporal variance were excluded to prevent clustering bias toward non-discriminatory dimensions, enhancing the interpretability of resultant hydrological zones. Specifically, temperature minima and maxima were omitted due to negligible inter-station variability, which was less than 0.5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:^\circ\:\mathrm{C}$$\end{document}, leaving only mean temperature as a representative thermal metric. The resulting feature set comprises 14 informative variables that provide a robust basis for the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document} analysis; the complete input matrix and the retained variables are reported in Table S1 in Supplementary materials. All 14 hydrochemical variables were subsequently scaled to the range [0, 1] using Min-Max normalization prior to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document} training, ensuring equal contribution of each feature to the distance calculations underlying the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document}‘s competitive learning process. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document} was implemented using the MiniSom library58 in Python, configured as a 5 × 5 rectangular grid with a Gaussian neighborhood function, an initial neighborhood radius (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document}) of 1.5, and a learning rate of 0.5. The network was trained for 5,000 iterations using random weight initialization with a fixed random seed of 42 to ensure full reproducibility. The choice of a 5 × 5 topology yielding 25 neurons was guided by the commonly applied heuristic of N ≈ \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:5\sqrt{n}$$\end{document}, where n is the number of observations59,60; for n = 20 stations this yields N ≈ 22, making the 25-neuron grid the nearest standard rectangular topology that satisfies the criterion.

Following training, each station was assigned to its Best Matching Unit (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{B}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{U}$$\end{document}), the neuron whose weight vector most closely matched the station’s normalized feature vector in Euclidean distance. The trained codebook, comprising the weight vectors of all 25 neurons, was subsequently passed to the k-means algorithm, which partitioned the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document} nodes into k discrete clusters. Each station then inherited the cluster label of its \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{B}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{U}$$\end{document}, completing the two-stage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document}, k-means pipeline60. Cluster validity was quantified using the Silhouette Score61 and the Calinski–Harabász (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{C}\mathrm{H}$$\end{document}) Index62, both evaluated on the station-level normalized feature matrix using the propagated cluster labels.

The final zonation was consolidated into three clusters (k = 3) to reflect the canonical reservoir compartmentalization into lacustrine, transitional, and riverine zones, a framework widely adopted in reservoir limnology and management35,36. The sensitivity of this choice was assessed by comparing solutions across k = 3, 4, 5, and 6, as well as across three normalization strategies: Min-Max scaling, Z-score standardization, and log-score transformation, to confirm the robustness of the selected configuration (see Supplementary Material, Fig. S4). Both validity indices declined consistently as k increased beyond 3 regardless of the normalization method applied, indicating diminishing cluster compactness and separation with finer partitioning. Solutions with k > 3 yielded additional clusters that could not be attributed to any recognisable hydrodynamic or limnological archetype within the reservoir, while k = 2 collapsed the transitional zone into one of the two end-member archetypes, obscuring ecologically meaningful longitudinal gradients along the reservoir axis. Taken together, the quantitative indices and the limnological interpretability of the resulting spatial patterns converge on k = 3 as the optimal zonation for this system.

Thermal stratification analysis

Thermal stratification dynamics in Wadi Dayqah Reservoir were assessed through a two-step analytical workflow: (1) quantification of zone-specific thermocline strength via curve-fitted TSI, and (2) derivation of an objective, site-specific stratification onset threshold via unsupervised clustering. Station selection was guided by both representativeness and data availability. P5 (lacustrine zone) was chosen because it is located near the dam wall, is one of the deepest stations, and consistently exhibits strong thermal stratification with minimal riverine influence. P31 (transitional zone) was selected as it falls within the mean range of both geographic location and maximum depth for its zone, offering a balanced representation of transitional hydrodynamics. For the riverine zone, P34 was initially considered due to its position near the primary inflow, but it was excluded due to missing data on several sampling dates. P6, which belongs to the same \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document}-derived cluster as P34, was therefore selected as the most reliable alternative. Although P6 is located further downstream, its shallow depth and the influence of surrounding mountain inflows result in biogeochemical conditions closely resembling those at P34, making it a suitable representative of riverine dynamics. A summary of each station’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{M}$$\end{document}-assigned zone, is provided in Fig. S5 of Supplementary Materials.

Temperature profiles at the zone-representative stations P5 (lacustrine), P31 (transitional), and P6 (riverine) were fitted using the Cook & Rimmer (2010) function (Eq. 5) to estimate thermocline depth, epilimnetic/hypolimnetic temperatures (see Fig. S9), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{T}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{I}$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:^\circ\:\mathrm{C}\:\mathrm{m} ^{{ - 1}}$$\end{document}). Curve-fitting results, alongside Rimmer’s equation parameters, for all three zones are presented in Figs. S6–S8 of Supplementary Materials, demonstrating robust convergence for > 90% of profiles; linear regression fallbacks were applied where necessary and flagged for quality control. This approach aligns with established practices for deriving robust thermocline metrics from high-frequency vertical profiles70,72.

Zone-specific comparisons reveal marked spatial heterogeneity in stratification behavior. While the lacustrine zone (P5) exhibited the full five-phase cycle with pronounced thermal gradients (Fig. 5a), the transitional (P31) and riverine (P6) zones showed only ephemeral, shallow stratification even during Phase 3. The sole exceptions occurred at P31 on June 9th and July 4th, when conditions met the stratification threshold criteria. Atmospheric forcing and inflow turbulence dominated mixing in these shallower regions, limiting the development of stable density gradients required to satisfy the multi-metric classification. This underscores the importance of multi-station monitoring for capturing longitudinal variability.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{O}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}$$\end{document} measurements in the riverine zone exhibited modest variability, with values fluctuating between − 25.7 mV and + 22.1 mV during the June sampling campaign (Fig. 7b). The absence of negative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{O}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}$$\end{document} values later in the year indicates recovery to sufficiently oxidizing conditions, consistent with the zone’s high flushing rates and limited residence time. Nutrient concentrations are also typically elevated in shallow, near-inflow regions due to catchment loading91, which may further contribute to short-term redox fluctuations.

This zone did not experience eutrophication, with peak chlorophyll-a concentrations reaching only 4.2 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upmu\:}\mathrm{g}\:\mathrm{L} ^{{ - 1}}$$\end{document} in January (Fig. 7c). Turbidity was consistently higher than in the lacustrine zone (Fig. 7e), reflecting greater suspended sediment loads associated with inflow events and shallow-water resuspension. pH remained slightly alkaline throughout the year, fluctuating narrowly between 7.5 and 8.0 across most of the water column (Fig. 7d), well within typical ranges for drinking water sources. Collectively, these observations: persistent oxygenation, elevated turbidity, limited algal biomass, and stable pH, are consistent with the hydrodynamic and material-loading characteristics expected for riverine reservoir zones, mirroring patterns documented in other inflow-dominated systems79,92.

Chemical stratification correlation analysis

Comprehensive correlation analyses of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{I}\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{i}$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{D}\mathrm{O}$$\end{document}, pH, chlorophyll-a, and turbidity across the three monitoring stations confirmed that chemical stratification patterns can be systematically quantified and compared along the reservoir’s longitudinal gradient, consistent with the original \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{I}\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{i}$$\end{document} framework and its integration with thermal stability metrics (See Figs. Exceptionally strong \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{I}\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{D}\mathrm{O}$$\end{document} /\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{I}\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{p}\mathrm{H}$$\end{document} correlations in the lacustrine zone (r = 0.914) were interpreted as evidence of tightly coupled metabolic processes, aligning with established observations that DO and pH change together over time due to photosynthetic oxygen production and respiratory consumption93–95. The lacustrine zone includes 9 stations (n = 99 per depth bin during stratified conditions, 72 during non-stratified), the transitional zone 7 stations (n = 77 stratified, 56 non-stratified), and the riverine zone 4 stations (n = 44 stratified, 32 non-stratified). Study limitations

Certain limitations regarding monitoring scope should be acknowledged. The absence of concurrent nutrient measurements (nitrogen, phosphorus) precluded detailed analysis of nutrient-algal feedbacks, while limited carbonate system data (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{CO}}_{2}$$\end{document}, alkalinity) constrained mechanistic interpretation of pH dynamics. Similarly, although high-frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{O}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}$$\end{document} data were available, the lack of concurrent measurements of redox-sensitive metals (e.g., iron, manganese) precluded direct assessment of their biogeochemical coupling. Furthermore, while this study established a robust link between thermal stratification metrics and hypolimnetic anoxia, direct quantification of hypolimnetic oxygen demand (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{H}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{D}$$\end{document}) and external organic matter loading was not feasible. Notwithstanding these constraints, the methodological framework presented here remains transferable to other arid-zone reservoirs, and future work incorporating these additional variables would further refine predictive capacity for water quality management.
