Abstract
We study how verifiable evidence travels through networks when messengers are biased but care about future influence. A fundamental evolves as a diffusion and a decision maker chooses actions to track it. Experts receive verifiable signals that must be relayed through a directed network of intermediaries. Each intermediary has a directional bias and values reputational capital, summarized by public beliefs about her type. Crucially, intermediaries choose not only whether to forward evidence, but also a publicly observed disclosure clock (reporting cadence) that governs the arrival intensity of disclosure opportunities on their outgoing links. Because clocks are observable, silence is state dependent: when a channel is active, delay is informative and can damage reputation; when the channel is switched off, silence is mechanically uninformative. Disclosure therefore becomes a real option written on reputational capital. Along any fixed expert-decision maker path, Markov perfect Bayesian equilibria admit a ladder representation: disclosure and clock choices are pinned down by finitely many posterior cutoffs, and short clock-off windows eliminate knife-edge randomization. When reputational stakes are sufficiently large and agents are sufficiently patient, dynamic incentives discipline persistent suppression and yield eventual transmission of all verifiable evidence along that path, even with bias reversals that block static unraveling. Embedding the path analysis into the full network, we characterize disclosure-sustaining topologies. For general parameter values (i.e., absent the high-reputation/patient regime), among trees these are exactly the bias-monotone ones. When the high-reputation/patient conditions hold along all relevant expert-decision maker paths, any tree is disclosure-sustaining and topology affects timing and welfare rather than feasibility. Under homogeneous reputational intensities, the ordered line that sorts intermediaries by bias remains dynamically optimal among trees; with heterogeneous intensities, optimal design can instead screen by topology, placing highly reputationally sensitive intermediaries on direct, parallel routes rather than at interior positions. In an endogenous link-formation game, pairwise stable networks can be either under-connected or over-connected relative to the utilitarian benchmark, because agents do not internalize the real-option value their links create or destroy for others’ reputational assets. Introduction

1.1
Motivation and examples

Many of the most consequential decisions in modern economies are mediated by
networks of information intermediaries.
Sell-side analysts, research boutiques, policy think tanks, ESG raters, AI safety labs, credit rating
agencies, and certification platforms all collect information about fundamentals, transform it into
recommendations or ratings, and transmit these messages to downstream decision makers.
The value of these systems depends not only on the quality of the underlying information, but also
on three interconnected features: (i) who is connected to whom, (ii) how reputational incentives
shape the strategic release or withholding of information over time, and (iii) how the timing
and absence of messages are interpreted.

Three classes of examples are particularly salient.

First, consider networks of financial and policy analysts.
Banks, brokerages, and macro research firms produce reports that diffuse through overlapping
networks of investors, journalists, and policymakers.
Analysts have heterogeneous ideological or client-driven biases, and they care about their
reputations for being well-informed and accurate.
Their incentives to publicize or to remain silent about particular pieces of evidence depend on both
their current views and their expected future influence.
Because reports typically propagate along specific relationships, for example, from niche
boutiques to lead underwriters or from think tanks to central banks, the network structure shapes
which signals move, which are suppressed, and which are amplified.
Silences are informative: a prominent analyst or think tank that does not take a public
position on a salient issue may suffer reputational consequences, and intermediaries anticipate this
when deciding when to speak.

Second, reputation-driven rating and credit networks have become central to capital
allocation.
Credit rating agencies, ESG rating firms, and investor syndicates provide assessments of borrower
quality or project viability.
These assessments, and the histories of which raters have been right or wrong, feed into
reputations that are themselves valuable assets.

These examples share three features central to this paper.
First, information flows along a network: messages travel through sequences of intermediaries, and
the set of possible paths is shaped by the network topology.
Second, intermediaries are biased: they have preferred actions or narratives and may wish to
distort the eventual decision in a particular direction, as in classic models of strategic
communication such as

Third, reputations are dynamic assets: an intermediary’s future influence and payoff depend
on how often she is perceived to have been correct or transparent in the past.

The interaction of these three elements is still poorly understood.
Static models of disclosure show that full unraveling of verifiable information can fail in
the presence of biased intermediaries along a path, even when information is fully verifiable and
all players are rational.
Dynamic models of disclosure show that firms may strategically delay revealing news.
Network models show how the pattern of connections affects long-run beliefs and welfare.
Yet we lack a unified framework in which all of the following hold: (i) disclosure incentives are dynamic and
reputational, (ii) information flows through a network of biased intermediaries, and
(iii) the network itself can be viewed as a portfolio of option-like exposures to others’
reputational assets.
This paper’s goal is to develop such a framework.
To position the contribution, it is useful to contrast three strands that have largely been studied in isolation.

We contribute by integrating these elements in a single continuous-time equilibrium framework with networks of biased intermediaries, reputational state dynamics, and endogenous disclosure timing.
This integration yields new implications that do not arise in earlier models: reputational timing incentives can restore eventual unraveling despite static bias reversals, and network topology and network formation shape where reputational bottlenecks arise and how quickly verifiable evidence reaches the decision maker.

1.2
Preview of the model and main results

We study a continuous-time model in which a payoff-relevant fundamental evolves as a diffusion.
A decision maker (or principal) chooses actions that are rewarded for tracking the fundamental.
Information about the fundamental is generated in the form of verifiable signals that originate with
experts and must travel to the decision maker along a directed network of intermediaries. The decision maker is modeled as a Bayesian best responder once verifiable evidence is disclosed; we abstract from receiver-side commitment or contracting, and instead study how reputational incentives and network architecture shape disclosure timing, which allows the network-design results in Section
5
to be read as guidance for a principal who selects reporting relationships ex ante.

We focus on hard information (verifiable evidence); Section
2.3
briefly discusses how the analysis would change if communication were non-verifiable.

Each agent in the network has a publicly known bias in the action, in the spirit of classic cheap-talk and disclosure models,
and also cares about her reputational capital, a forward-looking payoff component reflecting how often her disclosures are ex post corroborated and how transparently she communicates.

The key departure from existing work is that each intermediary controls not just what
information to relay, but also when and how often verifiable signals can arrive along
her outgoing links.

Formally, each agent chooses a disclosure clock: a publicly observed intensity process (a reporting cadence) that gates Poisson arrivals of verification/disclosure opportunities on her outgoing links.

Agents can temporarily switch this clock off, creating short, observable windows of local silence (publicly announced reporting pauses).
When an agent posts such a silence window, the component of public beliefs driven by her potential
disclosures loses its martingale part and evolves with bounded variation (only this component, not the full posterior).
This feature turns disclosure into a real option on reputational capital: by delaying, an agent
preserves the possibility to disclose later but risks reputational losses if others infer that she
has strategically suppressed information.
In other words, intermediaries behave as if they wait to exercise a disclosure option: they remain inactive inside an inaction region and disclose once beliefs cross a threshold, trading off the option value of waiting against the reputational cost of informative silence when the clock is on.

We obtain three main sets of results.
Together, these results link static network unraveling, dynamic disclosure timing, and an option-theoretic view of reputation within one equilibrium framework.

First, we characterize equilibrium disclosure along a fixed path from an expert to a
decision maker.
In a static environment with biased intermediaries,
30

shows that full unraveling of verifiable information along a path occurs only if all intermediaries’
biases are aligned on one side of the decision maker’s preference; one single bias reversal along
the path generically (i.e. except on a null set of states/parameters) blocks full disclosure.
In our dynamic environment with reputational clocks, we show that equilibrium disclosure strategies
along a path can be represented by a finite number of belief thresholds at which intermediaries
switch between passing and withholding information, and at which they accelerate or slow their
clocks.
Once intermediaries can announce short local-silence windows, knife-edge mixed strategies are
eliminated: equilibria are essentially pure threshold strategies, and the dynamic disclosure problem admits a ladder-type representation: a finite set of disclosure cutoffs in belief space, akin to an
S
-
s
policy in real-options models.

A first main theorem gives sufficient conditions, i.e. a high-
\beta
/low-
\rho
regime in which
reputational stakes dominate short-run bias payoffs, under which dynamic reputational incentives
restore full eventual disclosure along any fixed expert-DM path, even when static bias
reversals would preclude unraveling.

If agents care sufficiently about their future reputational capital relative to current bias
payoffs, and discounting is not too severe, any equilibrium that withholds verifiable information
can be profitably deviated from, through adjusting both the disclosure rule and the disclosure clock.
In that regime, every verifiable signal that reaches an expert almost surely reaches the decision
maker in finite time, and the timing of disclosures follows a structured ladder.

Second, we embed that path analysis into the full network and study which network topologies can sustain full dynamic disclosure.
We call a network disclosure-sustaining if, for any expert and decision-maker pair, there exists a Markov Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium with reputational incentives and clocks, in which all verifiable information eventually reaches the decision maker along some path.
When we do not impose the high-reputation and patient conditions of Theorem
2
(i.e., for arbitrary
\beta
and
\rho
), we show that among minimally connected networks (trees), the disclosure-sustaining topologies are precisely those that are bias-monotone: intermediaries can be ordered by bias so that along every simple expert-DM path biases move in one direction (no reversals).
When Theorem
2
applies along all relevant paths, any tree becomes disclosure-sustaining, and topology matters for the speed and welfare consequences of disclosure rather than for feasibility.

Under homogeneous reputational intensities and clock technologies, the ordered line that sorts
intermediaries by bias remains dynamically optimal: it maximizes both the decision maker’s
ex ante payoff and a utilitarian welfare measure, and it delivers a ladder that is weakly steeper,
in the sense of earlier revelation in belief space, than in any other tree with the same nodes.

Dynamic forces, however, also generate genuinely new network effects that have no static analogue.
When intermediaries differ in reputational sensitivity, it can be optimal to screen by
topology, i.e. placing highly reputationally sensitive intermediaries on direct, parallel paths rather
than at interior positions in an ordered chain.
In such reputational stars, the fast ladders operated by high-intensity intermediaries are
never bottlenecked by slower ones.
We then analyze a network formation game in which agents form links subject to costs and obtain
expected discounted payoffs from the induced dynamic disclosure equilibrium.

We show that pairwise stable networks can be either under-connected or over-connected relative to the utilitarian benchmark: unlike standard connection models, link externalities are two-sided because an added link can both activate faster disclosure ladders and pre-empt the option value embedded in existing routes; high-value links that activate fast ladders may fail to form,
while low-value or redundant links that dilute reputational incentives may persist because their
endpoints capture local reputational gains that are smaller than the social losses they cause.

Third, we take an explicit real-options view of reputations and links.
Building on the idea that reputation is a tradeable asset
(
31
)
and on the
real-options perspective on connections in reputation networks
(
29
)
, we
interpret each agent’s reputational capital as an underlying asset and each network link as a
portfolio of options written on others’ reputations.
In a tractable linear-quadratic Gaussian specification, we show how this interpretation yields
path-level ladder characterizations, network-level comparative statics, and a transparent valuation
of link additions and deletions as real options on reputational processes.

This theoretical analysis is directly interpretable in the real-world settings described
above.

For instance, in networks of analysts, our model rationalizes when reputational concerns and observable reporting
cadences can discipline biased intermediaries so that all research eventually reaches key decision makers, and when
network structure instead creates bottlenecks, either because no directed expert-DM path exists, or because the
high-reputation/patient conditions of Theorem
2
fail along every feasible path, so that
some verifiable evidence may remain blocked upstream. In rating and credit networks, it provides a microfoundation for the value of establishing or
severing rating relationships as real options on reputational capital and for the role of overlapping
coverage in accelerating or slowing the flow of credit-relevant information.
In AI and other technological domains, it suggests how design of audit in networks and disclosure-timing
regulation could promote revelation of safety-relevant evidence in systems where both firms
and auditors have strategic disclosure incentives.

Beyond the qualitative ladder structure, the model delivers crisp comparative statics on observable disclosure timing.
Proposition
4
implies three empirical predictions that are natural to take to data in settings such as analyst reports, rating actions, corporate guidance, audit releases, or policy briefings:

(P1)

Uncertainty and clustering.
Higher fundamental uncertainty (higher volatility of
X_{t}
) expands intermediaries’ inaction regions in belief space.
As a result, the model predicts longer silent spells and more clustered disclosure events: information is withheld for longer, and then released in bunches when beliefs move far enough to cross one or several thresholds in quick succession.

(P2)

Reputational stakes and speed.
Intermediaries with larger reputational intensity
\beta_{i}
(or, equivalently, a larger present value of reputational consequences, e.g. a lower effective discount rate) have tighter inaction regions and therefore disclose earlier and more frequently.
Thus, organizations facing greater accountability or more valuable future influence should exhibit shorter disclosure lags and fewer prolonged “under review” periods.

(P3)

Bias and asymmetric delay.
Changes in directional bias shift thresholds asymmetrically.
An upward-biased intermediary becomes systematically quicker to pass good news and slower to pass bad news (and vice versa for downward bias), generating a sign-dependent pattern of delays even when the underlying information is verifiable.

These predictions are particularly transparent because the model treats disclosures as verifiable events and silence as an equilibrium signal conditional on observable reporting regimes (clocks).
They suggest empirical designs based on the distribution of inter-disclosure durations, the incidence of bursty “silence-then-flurry” episodes, and how these objects co-move with volatility proxies and measures of reputational exposure.

1.3
Related literature

This paper connects four active literatures: strategic disclosure, dynamic reputation, networks, and
real options.

First, we build on the classic theory of voluntary disclosure of verifiable information.

20
and
27
show that in simple environments
with a single sender and a single receiver, verifiability leads to unraveling: in equilibrium,
all information is disclosed.
Subsequent work in accounting and finance introduced information costs, partial verifiability, and
proprietary costs that can break unraveling; see
16
and the survey in

32

In a static network environment with verifiable information and biased intermediaries,

30
shows that full disclosure along a path requires a
one-sidedness condition on biases and characterizes the ex ante optimal tree as an ordered line
sorted by bliss points.
We adopt the core static disclosure logic and the network environment from this literature, but we
introduce a dynamic reputational state and a disclosure-timing technology that fundamentally alter
the conditions under which unraveling obtains.

Second, we contribute to the literature on dynamic disclosure and timing.

21
and
28
study environments in
which a single agent chooses when to disclose evolving information to a principal who can take
irreversible actions; the timing of disclosure encodes information and yields intricate signalling
equilibria. Recent work on dynamic evidence disclosure includes
24

Our model shares with this literature the focus on continuous time, evolving fundamentals, and the informational content of delay, but it departs in ways that matter for both equilibrium behavior and welfare.
First, information is transmitted through a directed network of intermediaries with heterogeneous biases, so delay and disclosure become strategic at each step of a path and can generate bottlenecks that have no analogue in single-sender environments.
Second, we introduce a publicly observed disclosure clock that endogenizes the arrival rate of verifiable evidence on links (that is, the arrival of verification/disclosure opportunities for releasing verifiable evidence downstream); this technology selects essentially pure threshold disclosure policies and yields a tractable ladder structure.
Third, because the communication architecture is explicit, we can characterize which topologies sustain eventual disclosure and study how link additions or deletions reshape incentives, comparative statics, and efficiency.

In a related work,

timing decisions under reputational diffusion and show how publicly posted clock-off windows
can select pure-strategy ladders.
We adapt that selection mechanism to a network setting and show how it interacts with biased
intermediaries and topology to restore unraveling dynamically.

Third, our reputational state is grounded in the literature on dynamic reputation and career concerns.

In the same spirit, our intermediaries face a career-concerns motive: disclosure and clock choices shift the public posterior about their informational quality and or transparency, which in turn determines future influence and payoffs through the reputational term

First, disclosure is a timing choice in continuous time, so intermediaries behave as if they hold an option to disclose later, trading off the option value of waiting against reputational losses from informative silence when the clock is on.
Second, reputations are networked: disclosures by one intermediary change what another intermediary’s silence implies, strengthening reputational discipline in some topologies while creating timing bottlenecks in others.

When signals are noisy-but-verifiable, reputations reflect ex post accuracy once sufficiently informative verifiable evidence becomes public.
In our fully revealing benchmark, disclosed signals are mechanically correct; reputations then primarily discipline
selective withholding
and
timeliness
because publicly observed clocks make silence informative when a channel is active.
Section
4.1
formalizes the Markov property we impose for tractability and clarifies when it can fail and how the analysis extends when reputations are finite-dimensional.

All agents discount the future at a common rate
\rho>0
.
Given a strategy profile and an initial state
(X_{0},R^{1}_{0},\dots,R^{n}_{0})
, the ex ante objective of agent
i
is

U_{i}\;=\;\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\,u_{i}\bigl(y_{t},X_{t},R^{i}_{t}\bigr)\,dt\Biggr],

(3)

with the convention that for the DM we use
u_{0}
as defined above and ignore
R^{0}_{t}
.

Functional forms.

We adopt quadratic losses to keep the decision maker’s best response and intermediaries’ material incentives transparent, and to align the static and dynamic sections.
The qualitative forces we study do not hinge on quadraticity: with strictly convex loss functions, the decision maker’s best response is still a monotone functional of her posterior and intermediaries face the same disclosure-versus-reputation trade-off.
Appendix
C
discusses robustness to alternative loss functions and the resulting changes in the state variables that summarize public beliefs.

2.2
Network and paths of communication

Information travels through a fixed directed network.
Formally, let
G=(N,L)
be a directed graph with node set
N
and edge set
L\subseteq N\times N
.
An ordered pair
(i,j)\in L
means that
i
can directly transmit verifiable messages to
j
.
We write
\mathcal{N}^{+}_{i}=\{j:(i,j)\in L\}
for
i
’s set of out-neighbors and
\mathcal{N}^{-}_{i}=\{j:(j,i)\in L\}
for
i
’s set of in-neighbors.

We assume that for every intermediary
i\in N\setminus\{0\}
there is a directed path from
i
to the decision maker
0
, and that for every node in a designated
set of experts
E\subseteq N\setminus\{0\}
there is a directed path from that node to
0
.
A
simple path
from node
i
to node
j
is a finite sequence
(i_{0},\dots,i_{k})
with
i_{0}=i
,
i_{k}=j
,
(i_{\ell},i_{\ell+1})\in L
for all
\ell
, and all nodes distinct.

We are particularly interested in simple paths from experts
e\in E
to the decision maker
0
.
We call such a path an expert-DM path.
For a given pair
(e,0)
there may be many such paths; our assumptions ensure that at least one expert-DM path exists for each
e\in E
.

The network is taken as exogenous in Sections
2
to
4
.
In Section
5
we endogenize link formation and study which networks arise in equilibrium.
The baseline network assumptions above are standard in the literature on strategic communication in networks, see, e.g.,

18
,
2
, and
30
,
and are chosen so that the role of path structure in information transmission is transparent.

Trees and redundant paths.

The model is defined for arbitrary directed graphs and therefore allows multiple expert-DM routes and (undirected) cycles.
In Sections
3
and
5
we will often focus on acyclic communication structures and, for sharp ordering and welfare comparisons, on
trees
(directed arborescences rooted at the decision maker, so that
every node
has a unique directed path to
0
).
We single out experts only because they are the economically relevant originators of raw verifiable signals.
The reason is that with
verifiable
evidence, additional links beyond such a tree do not create new informational content once a signal is disclosed; they primarily create
redundant routes
through which the same hard evidence could arrive.
Redundancy can matter strategically (e.g. for attribution of reputational credit, free-riding across routes, or pre-emption incentives to disclose first), and we discuss these forces and their likely implications for our results in Section
5
.

2.3
Information structure and disclosure technology

We now describe how information about the fundamental
X_{t}
is generated and how it can be transmitted through the network.

Verifiable signals.

At random times, experts receive verifiable signals about the current value of
X_{t}
.
A verifiable signal at time
t
is a realization of a random variable
S_{t}
that is perfectly observed by the recipient and, if disclosed,
is observed by all intended recipients and cannot be misrepresented.
Thus, conditional on disclosure, message content is pinned down, and strategic behavior operates through suppression and timing rather than through distortion.
For most of the analysis, it is convenient to treat
S_{t}
as revealing
X_{t}
exactly.
All results extend to the case in which signals are noisy but still
verifiable
in the sense that the realized
S_{t}
cannot be fabricated or disguised once disclosed.
6
6

6

The model is therefore tailored to environments in which evidence is hard information, such as audited accounting releases, documented rating actions, certified test results, or formal inspection reports.
If instead communication were non-verifiable (cheap talk), then an intermediary could fabricate or distort a report, and the unraveling logic for hard information would not apply.
In that case, truth-telling would have to be supported by reputational incentives and by the possibility of ex post detection of misstatements, as in classic reputation-based communication settings.
See, for example, the cheap-talk and network-communication literatures in
12
;
4
;
18
;
2
.
Extending our analysis to non-verifiable messages in networks with endogenous disclosure timing is beyond the scope of this paper and is left for future work.
In regimes where we establish eventual disclosure of verifiable evidence, our results can be read as a benchmark for the disclosure-timing margin, and as an upper bound on information transmission relative to pure cheap talk.

Raw verifiable signals originate with experts.
For each expert
e\in E
, the times at which new raw verifiable evidence becomes available coincide with the jump times of her disclosure clock process
N^{e}
induced by the publicly observed intensity path
(\lambda^{e}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(defined below).
That is, when
N^{e}
jumps at time
t
, expert
e
privately observes a verifiable signal
S_{t}
about the current fundamental
X_{t}
.
This same jump time is also the first disclosure opportunity for
S_{t}
: at time
t
the expert may transmit
S_{t}
to any subset of her out-neighbors.
If
e
transmits along a link to
j\in\mathcal{N}^{+}_{e}
, then
j
observes the true realization of
S_{t}
; if she withholds from
j
, only
e
observes the content.

Disclosure decisions.

More generally, whenever an agent
i
has observed a verifiable signal, she may decide whether to transmit it to any subset of her out-neighbors.
Messages are
non-falsifiable
: if
i
transmits a signal, all recipients learn its true realization and know that the signal originated upstream and has been passed along without modification.
An agent can, however, suppress a signal by choosing not to transmit it, in which case downstream agents observe only her subsequent behavior and the absence of messages, not the suppressed content.

Messages can be relayed downstream.

If an intermediary
i
receives a verifiable signal at time
\tau
from some in-neighbor, she observes its content and can retain it.
Importantly, receipt of a message updates
i
’s private information immediately, but the message can be relayed further only at
i
’s own disclosure opportunities (the jump times of
N^{i}
).
Transmission is instantaneous
conditional on disclosure
, but disclosure is only feasible at the jump times of
i
’s disclosure clock
N^{i}
(defined below).
Thus, after receiving a signal,
i
may either disclose it at the next available disclosure opportunity or hold it for later opportunities, and she may then pass it to any subset of her out-neighbors, and so on, until the message either reaches the decision maker or is withheld at some intermediate node.

Disclosure clocks and local silence.

A key feature of the model is that agents can control not only whether to disclose a given signal, but also the
arrival intensity
of potential disclosure opportunities.
For each
i\in N
we introduce a nonnegative,
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted process
(\lambda^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, the disclosure clock of agent
i
.

Formally, for each
i
there exists a unit-intensity Poisson process
(\tilde{N}^{i}_{u})_{u\geq 0}
, independent across
i
and independent of
(X_{t})
.
Given a clock path
(\lambda^{i}_{s})_{0\leq s\leq t}
, define the cumulative intensity

A^{i}_{t}\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{i}_{s}\,ds,

and set
N^{i}_{t}=\tilde{N}^{i}_{A^{i}_{t}}
.
Conditional on the path of
\lambda^{i}
, the process
(N^{i}_{t})
is a Poisson process with time-varying intensity
\lambda^{i}_{t}
.

Interpretation: verification and disclosure opportunities.

A jump of
N^{i}
should be interpreted as a
verification/disclosure opportunity
for agent
i
on her outgoing links.
For experts, each jump of
N^{e}
simultaneously generates a new raw verifiable signal
S_{t}
and is the first opportunity to disclose it downstream.
For intermediaries, a jump of
N^{i}
need not represent the arrival of new information: it is the moment at which they can choose whether to certify and relay any verifiable evidence already in their possession (including signals received earlier from upstream).
This is the sense in which the publicly observed clock gates the
arrival of verifiable evidence on links
: downstream recipients can receive verifiable evidence from
i
only at those jump times at which
i
elects to disclose.

When
\lambda^{i}_{t}=0
on an interval
[t,t+\Delta)
, no disclosure opportunities for
i
arrive on that interval.
We refer to such an interval as a
local silence window
for
i
.
We assume that the paths of
\lambda^{i}
are càdlàg and that
\lambda^{i}_{t}
is publicly observed for all
t
.
7
7

7

The key assumption is not that agents literally “choose Poisson intensities,” but that outsiders can observe when a channel is in an
active reporting
regime versus in a publicly announced pause, so that silence has different informational content across regimes.
In applications, this corresponds to scheduled earnings and audit calendars, periodic rating reviews, publicly announced coverage suspensions by analysts, posted evaluation cadences (e.g. model cards or safety-audit schedules), and related institutional reporting conventions.

We view this public observability as a reduced form for reporting calendars and disclosure commitments; it is also the key equilibrium-selection device that replaces knife-edge mixing by pure threshold behavior in continuous time.

Terminology (signals vs. disclosure opportunities).

A jump time of
N^{i}
is a
verification/ disclosure opportunity
for agent
i
on her outgoing links.
To streamline exposition, we sometimes refer to such jump times as
(potential) signal arrivals
for
i
,
meaning times at which verifiable evidence
could
arrive downstream through
i
if she disclosed.
Only experts generate
raw
signals at their own jump times; for an intermediary, a jump of
N^{i}

does not create new information and merely provides an opportunity to disclose/relay verifiable evidence
already in her possession.

Belief implications of on/off regimes.

Because
\lambda^{i}
is publicly observed and
N^{i}
has intensity
\lambda^{i}_{t}
, the informational content of
no disclosure
depends on whether
i
is on or off the clock.
If
\lambda^{i}_{t}=0
on
[t,t+\Delta)
, outsiders know that no disclosure opportunity can arrive through
i
’s channel on that interval; hence silence carries no inference about suppressed evidence, and public beliefs about
X
evolve only through the diffusion (and through any other active channels).
If instead
\lambda^{i}_{t}>0
, outsiders know that disclosure opportunities arrive with hazard
\lambda^{i}_{t}
; observing no disclosure is then consistent either with
no arrival
or with an
arrival that was suppressed
.
This is why “silence while on the clock” becomes informative and can affect both beliefs about the fundamental and assessments of the intermediary’s reputational capital.

Why we model clocks explicitly.

In continuous-time disclosure problems without an observable clock, intermediaries can only make silence less revealing by randomizing over disclosure timing around cutoff beliefs.
This typically generates knife-edge mixing and substantially more complicated belief dynamics.
Allowing agents to post short, publicly observed clock-off windows provides an economically interpretable commitment device (a posted reporting blackout) that selects pure threshold behavior and yields the ladder representation characterized in Section
4
.
We return to this interpretation in the toy example in Section
2.4
and in the applications in Section
6
.

The
public filtration

(\mathcal{F}^{p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is generated by

•

all disclosed messages and their contents;

•

all clock paths
(\lambda^{i}_{s})_{0\leq s\leq t}
for all
i
;

•

the DM’s past actions
(y_{s})_{0\leq s\leq t}
.

For each intermediary
i\in N\setminus\{0\}
, the
private filtration

(\mathcal{F}^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is generated by
\mathcal{F}^{p}_{t}
together with
i
’s private signals and the subset of past arrivals and suppressions that
i
can infer from her private history.
Agents’ strategies are adapted to their information sets, which are subsets of
\mathcal{F}^{i}_{t}
.

2.4
A toy example: one intermediary, informative silence, and ladder behavior

Because the dynamic model combines continuous time, strategic delay, and networked transmission, it is useful to fix ideas with the simplest non-trivial case:
a single expert
e
, a single intermediary
i
, and the decision maker
0

e\to i\to 0
.

Beliefs and the DM’s action.

Let

m_{t}\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[X_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{p}_{t}\right]

denote the decision maker’s posterior mean of the fundamental given the public filtration.
With quadratic loss, the decision maker’s optimal action is
y_{t}=m_{t}
.

A single piece of verifiable evidence.

Suppose that at some time
\tau

S_{\tau}
about

i

8
8

8

For intuition, one can think of

If
i

If
i
does not forward, the decision maker only observes the absence of a message and continues to choose
y_{t}=m_{t}

m_{t}
based on the public law of motion of
(X_{t})
and on what silence implies about
i
’s information and incentives.

Bias creates incentives to delay or suppress.

Suppose
i
has an upward bias,
b_{i}>0
.
If
S_{\tau}
is
good news
relative to the current belief (e.g. it would raise
m_{\tau}

y_{\tau}

i

If
S_{\tau}
is
bad news
(e.g. it would lower
m_{\tau}

y_{\tau}

i

i
has a material incentive to delay or suppress the information.

Why silence is informative when the clock is on.

When
i

\lambda^{i}_{t}

i
’s channel.
Thus, a prolonged absence of disclosure is not innocuous: it is interpreted as evidence that either no useful signal arrived or that an unfavorable signal arrived and was withheld.
This interpretation has two effects that are central for the paper:
(i) it moves the decision maker’s belief
m_{t}
even without messages, and
(ii) it worsens
i

\beta_{i}

What a clock-off window does.

By setting
\lambda^{i}_{t}=0
on a publicly observed interval
[t,t+\Delta)
, the intermediary commits to a local silence window.
During such a window, outsiders know that no disclosure opportunity can arrive through
i
’s channel, so the absence of disclosure carries no adverse inference about withheld evidence.
Operationally, the information component of beliefs coming from
i
’s channel becomes dormant until the clock is turned back on.

Real-world analogue.

A clock-off window corresponds to a publicly announced “no-update” period: e.g. an analyst suspends coverage until a scheduled review, a rating agency announces a review horizon, an auditor posts an inspection calendar, or a lab commits to publishing evaluations only on a fixed cadence.
In such cases, outsiders can distinguish between (i) no disclosure because the channel is temporarily inactive, and (ii) no disclosure despite an active cadence, which invites suspicion of selective withholding.

From delay incentives to threshold rules (the “ladder”).

In equilibrium, the intermediary trades off the material gain from delaying bad news against the reputational cost of being perceived as strategically silent while her clock is on.
This generates cutoff behavior.
In the one-intermediary case, the ladder reduces to a single pair of belief cutoffs: there exist thresholds
\underline{m}_{i}<\overline{m}_{i}
such that
i

m_{t}
.
Between disclosure times,
i
may post short clock-off windows to remove knife-edge indifference and avoid mixed strategies.
In longer expert-to-decision-maker chains, each intermediary contributes its own cutoffs, and the set of nested switching regions across nodes is what we call the equilibrium disclosure ladder.

2.5
Timing and equilibrium concept

We study both a static benchmark and the full dynamic game.

Static benchmark.

The static benchmark abstracts from the diffusion and the clocks, and considers a single draw of the fundamental.
A scalar state
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is drawn from a commonly known prior and is observed only by one or more experts.
Messages are instantaneous and timing is stagewise along the network.
Experts and intermediaries decide, possibly sequentially along a path, whether to disclose their information; if disclosed, the message is verifiable and cannot be falsified.
The decision maker then chooses an action
y\in\mathbb{R}
based on the history of disclosures.

Biases and payoffs are as in (
2
), with
X_{t}
replaced by
\theta
and reputational terms suppressed.
An equilibrium of this static disclosure game is a perfect Bayesian equilibrium (PBE) in which strategies are sequentially rational given beliefs and beliefs are updated by Bayes’ rule wherever possible.
Section
3
recalls the key implications of this benchmark, in particular the conditions under which full unraveling holds along a path

(
27
;
20
;
30
)
.

Dynamic game.

In the dynamic environment, the fundamental evolves according to (
1
), agents observe and possibly suppress verifiable signals as described above, and the DM chooses an action process
(y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
A (pure) strategy for agent
i
specifies, for each time
t
and each history in her information set,

•

the choice of clock intensity
\lambda^{i}_{t}\in[0,\bar{\lambda}_{i}]
;

•

at each disclosure opportunity of
i
(i.e., each jump time of
N^{i}
, a
potential signal arrival
),
and for each verifiable signal in
i
’s possession at that time (including signals previously received from
in-neighbors, and, if
i
is an expert, any new raw signal realized at that jump), whether to disclose it to each
out-neighbor as a function of the history and the signal realization.

A strategy for the DM specifies, for each
t
and each public history, an action
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
.

We focus on Markov strategies in which choices depend on the current public state (for example, the vector of posterior beliefs about
X_{t}
and the reputational state
(R^{i}_{t})_{i\in N\setminus\{0\}}
) and, for disclosure decisions, on realized signals.
A dynamic equilibrium is a perfect Bayesian equilibrium (PBE) of this continuous-time game.
Because the underlying physical state process
(X_{t},R^{1}_{t},\dots,R^{n}_{t})
is Markov, while the fundamental
X_{t}
is not publicly observed and enters behavior only through the induced public posterior, we refer to such an equilibrium as a Markov perfect Bayesian equilibrium (MPBE).
The formal definition of our MPBE, including the construction of beliefs from observed clocks, messages, and actions, is given in Appendix
C.2
.
Sections
4
and
5
characterize the structure of MPBE disclosure and clock strategies along a fixed expert-DM path and in the full network.
We discuss equilibrium multiplicity and the role of observable clocks as a selection device in Section
4.3
.

3
Static Strategic Disclosure in Networks

In this section we freeze time and study a one-shot version of the model.
The fundamental is drawn once, agents receive verifiable information about it, and information travels through the network through a sequence of disclosure decisions.
The section has two purposes.
First, it recalls the classic unraveling logic in the direct expert-decision maker case, as in
20
;
27
.
Second, it summarizes the key static insights from
30
on disclosure through biased intermediaries and on optimal network design.
These results provide the benchmark against which we compare the dynamic analysis in Sections
4
and
5
.
The toy example in Section
2.4
already illustrates how the dynamic mechanism
uses informative silence and reputational incentives; the static benchmark isolates the bias-based
obstruction that the dynamic model can overcome.

Throughout this section, we suppress reputational payoffs and clocks.
The decision maker and intermediaries are as in Section
2.1
, but utilities contain only the (biased) quadratic loss in the one-shot action.

3.1
Benchmark: direct expert-decision maker

We begin with the simplest case of a single expert and a single decision maker.

A scalar state
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is drawn from a commonly known prior distribution
F
with full support on
\Theta
.
The decision maker chooses an action
y\in\mathbb{R}
and has payoff

u_{0}(y,\theta)\;=\;-\bigl(y-\theta\bigr)^{2}.

There is one expert
e
who privately observes
\theta
.
The expert cares about the action but not directly about
\theta
; her payoff is

u_{e}(y,\theta)\;=\;-\alpha_{e}\bigl(y-\theta-b_{e}\bigr)^{2},

where
\alpha_{e}>0
and
b_{e}\in\mathbb{R}
is her bias.
The expert can make at most one verifiable announcement.
If she discloses, the decision maker learns the true state
\theta
.
If she remains silent, the decision maker observes only the prior.
Messages are costless and cannot be falsified: any announcement must either be truthful or absent.

An extensive-form description of the game is as follows.
Nature draws
\theta
and reveals it only to the expert.
The expert chooses
m\in\{\theta,\varnothing\}
, where
m=\varnothing
denotes silence.
The decision maker observes
m
and chooses
y
.
Beliefs and strategies form a perfect Bayesian equilibrium.

The standard benchmark is as follows.

Proposition 1

(Direct unraveling)
.

In any perfect Bayesian equilibrium of the direct expert-decision maker game with verifiable messages, the decision maker’s action is almost surely equal to the realized state:
y(\theta)=\theta
for
F
-almost every
\theta
.
In particular, in equilibrium the expert discloses
\theta
whenever it is payoff relevant, and there is no equilibrium in which positive-measure sets of states remain undisclosed.

The intuition follows the unraveling argument of
27
and
20
.
Suppose there is an interval of states in which the expert remains silent.
Conditional on silence, the decision maker forms beliefs supported on that interval and chooses an action that does not coincide with
\theta
for most states in the interval.
For any such state, the expert can profitably deviate by disclosing: disclosure reveals the true
\theta
and moves the decision maker’s action closer to the expert’s bliss point.
Iterating this argument shows that, except possibly at the boundary of the support, any state for which disclosure is not expected cannot be sustained in equilibrium, and unraveling obtains.

This benchmark is robust to a wide range of preferences and message spaces: under verifiability and a single-crossing (monotonicity) property of payoffs in the decision maker’s action, unraveling obtains even without quadratic losses.
Quadratic utility is thus a convenient normalization for comparability with the dynamic analysis, not a substantive restriction for the static unraveling logic. It provides the reference point against which the failures of unraveling through biased intermediaries will be evaluated.

3.2
Disclosure through intermediaries

We now introduce intermediaries along a path from the expert to the decision maker.
We consider a one-shot version of a single expert-decision maker path in the network defined in Section
2.2
.
Let the nodes on the path be
(e,i_{1},\dots,i_{k},0)
, where
e
is the unique expert and
0
is the decision maker.
The state
\theta
is drawn and observed by
e
only.
At most one verifiable message about
\theta
can be sent along the path.

The timing is sequential along the path.
First,
e
chooses whether to disclose
\theta
to
i_{1}
or to remain silent.
If
e
discloses, then
i_{1}
observes
\theta
and chooses whether to pass it to
i_{2}
or to suppress it.
If
i_{1}
passes, then
i_{2}
observes
\theta
and chooses whether to pass it to
i_{3}
, and so on, until the message either reaches
0
or is withheld at some intermediate node.
If at any point a node on the path chooses not to pass the message, the game ends and the decision maker observes only the fact that no message has arrived.
After the (possibly empty) message history is realized, the decision maker chooses an action
y
.

Preferences are as above: the decision maker has payoff
u_{0}(y,\theta)=-(y-\theta)^{2}
, while each intermediary
i_{j}
has payoff

u_{i_{j}}(y,\theta)\;=\;-\alpha_{i_{j}}\bigl(y-\theta-b_{i_{j}}\bigr)^{2},

with
\alpha_{i_{j}}>0
and bias
b_{i_{j}}\in\mathbb{R}
.
The expert’s payoff is
u_{e}(y,\theta)=-\alpha_{e}\bigl(y-\theta-b_{e}\bigr)^{2}
.

All preference parameters
(\alpha_{\ell},b_{\ell})
are common knowledge.

Messages are verifiable and costless.
No player can alter the content of a message; each can only pass or suppress it.

When the biases
(b_{e},b_{i_{1}},\dots,b_{i_{k}})
all lie on the same side of the DM’s bliss point (zero), all senders have the same
directional
preference over the DM’s action: either everyone prefers higher actions than the DM (all biases weakly positive) or everyone prefers lower actions (all biases weakly negative).
In this one-sided case, the Milgrom-Grossman unraveling logic can be propagated through the chain, and a full-disclosure equilibrium exists along the path
(
30
)
.

If biases are not aligned, unraveling can fail because the chain contains a genuine
directional conflict
.
For some states an upstream sender wants the DM’s action to move up relative to the pooling outcome, while a downstream intermediary with the opposite-signed bias prefers the action to move down; such an intermediary then has an incentive to suppress exactly those messages, creating
dark states
in which verifiable information is blocked.
For example, think of an advisory chain for climate policy in which some upstream advisors systematically favor stronger action (positive bias) while an intermediary later in the chain favors weaker or delayed action (negative bias): the latter may strategically block strong warnings before they reach the final policymaker, even though the underlying evidence is verifiable.

It is convenient to define bias alignment formally.

Definition 1

(Bias-aligned and bias-reversing paths)
.

A path
(e,i_{1},\dots,i_{k},0)
is
bias-aligned
if all biases along the path have the same weak sign:
either
b_{e}\geq 0
and
b_{i_{j}}\geq 0
for all
j=1,\dots,k
, or
b_{e}\leq 0
and
b_{i_{j}}\leq 0
for all
j=1,\dots,k
.
A path is

Proposition 2

(Static path disclosure)

Consider the static one-shot disclosure game along a fixed path

Proposition 3

(Static network optimality: ordered line)

Remark 1

(On uniqueness and the scope of Squintani’s result)
.

The uniqueness claim for the ordered line in
30

different network-design objective than the fixed
(e,0)
benchmark studied here.
In particular, in that design problem the identity of the sender and receiver may vary across
instances (or the planner cares about full disclosure uniformly across multiple expert-DM pairs), and the ordered line is uniquely able (under generic conditions) to avoid bias reversals on
all relevant communication paths.
In contrast, with a fixed expert and a fixed decision maker and with all intermediaries lying on the
unique path, the existence of one bias-aligned ordering implies that all orderings are bias-aligned,
so full disclosure is not unique across trees.

Intuitively, the ordered line is a canonical benchmark because it is bias-monotone.
In our fixed expert-DM benchmark, whenever a bias-aligned ordering exists, all intermediaries lie on
the same side of the DM’s bliss point, so any tree ordering admits a full-disclosure equilibrium and
yields the first-best payoff.
The ordered line becomes a sharp design object in richer environments (as in

30

across multiple potential sender–receiver pairs; in that case, avoiding bias reversals uniformly
across paths pins down the ordered line.
In the dynamic model below, even when eventual disclosure holds across trees, ordering intermediaries
by bias affects the disclosure ladder and hence the

welfare.

Dynamic Reputational Disclosure

We now turn to the full dynamic model along a fixed expert-DM path.

Mechanism on a path: from informative silence to a disclosure ladder.

Section

illustrate the simplest case
(

) and the two forces that drive the results below.
First, when an intermediary’s clock is

This affects the public belief

R^{i}_{t}

Second, by posting a publicly observed

channel while the clock is off.

These two forces turn disclosure into an option problem in belief space.
With a biased material objective, an intermediary prefers to delay the release of signals that would
move the decision maker’s action in the wrong direction, but doing so while the clock is on triggers
adverse inference and reputational losses.
In equilibrium this trade-off generates threshold behavior: for each intermediary there is an
inaction region for the public belief, where delay is optimal, and boundary points where disclosure
is optimal.
On a longer expert-DM path, each intermediary contributes its own inaction region and switching
boundaries, and the resulting nested collection of thresholds across nodes is what we call the
equilibrium disclosure ladder.

The remainder of this section formalizes this mechanism.

Section

restore full eventual disclosure along the path, even when static bias reversals would block
unraveling.

Reputational state (public posterior and what is being assessed).

What does it mean to be “proved correct” when evidence is verifiable?

Verifiability rules out falsification of disclosed messages, but it does not make reputation irrelevant: it shifts the reputational content to
selection and timing

When signals are noisy-but-verifiable, the public can evaluate the
accuracy
of disclosed assessments once sufficiently informative verifiable evidence arrives later
(e.g. a subsequent fully revealing signal, an audit, or an eventual outcome), so posterior updates reward smaller forecast errors and penalize larger ones.
In the fully revealing benchmark (where disclosed signals reveal
X_{t}
exactly), “accuracy” of a disclosed signal is degenerate; instead,
reputation is driven by
transparency and timeliness
:
because clocks are publicly observed,
silence while on the clock
is interpreted as evidence of selective withholding.
Thus, even when a disclosed signal is mechanically correct, reputational capital can rise when an agent discloses in situations where a low-transparency type would suppress,
and it can fall when an agent remains silent in situations where a high-transparency type would disclose.
This is the sense in which past disclosures (and silences) are “confirmed” by subsequent public histories: later verifiable evidence and the evolution of beliefs make selective withholding increasingly difficult to rationalize as mere non-arrival.

We now impose a structural property ensuring that reputational dynamics admit a finite-dimensional Markov representation along the path.

Assumption 1

(Markov reputations)

Along a fixed path

and the jump is strictly increasing in how favorable the new public information is for
i

Remark (why part (ii) can be “local”).

Specifically, in the ladder MPBE characterized below, each intermediary’s clock and disclosure/relay rules are Markov

4.2
Real-options representation of disclosure incentives

We now describe how the dynamic disclosure and clock choices of an intermediary along
P
can be represented as a real-options problem in belief space.

Fix an agent
i
on the path and take as given the Markov strategies of all other agents, which depend on the state
(m_{t},R_{t})
.
As in Section
2.3
, agent
i
controls a disclosure clock
(\lambda^{i}_{t})
and disclosure decisions at potential signal arrivals.
For expositional clarity, we restrict the clock to take two values:

\lambda^{i}_{t}\in\{0,\bar{\lambda}_{i}\},

which we interpret as “clock off” and “clock on”.
When
\lambda^{i}_{t}=\bar{\lambda}_{i}
, disclosure opportunities arrive at
i
according to a Poisson process of intensity
\bar{\lambda}_{i}
; when
\lambda^{i}_{t}=0
, no disclosure opportunities arrive.

Let
S_{t}=(m_{t},R_{t})
denote the state, and fix a discount rate
\rho>0
.
Given an equilibrium strategy profile, the continuation value of agent
i
at state
s
is

V^{i}(s)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{s}\Biggl[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\,u_{i}\bigl(y_{t},X_{t},R^{i}_{t}\bigr)\,dt\Biggr],

where
\mathbb{E}_{s}
denotes expectation conditional on
S_{0}=s
and on the strategies of all agents.
Given the Markov behavior of others,
i
’s problem can be written as an impulse-control problem with two types of controls:

•

Continuous control of the clock:
at each time
t
, agent
i
chooses whether to run the clock
(
\lambda^{i}_{t}=\bar{\lambda}_{i}
) or to set it to zero, as a function of
S_{t}
;

•

Impulse control at disclosure opportunities:
whenever
N^{i}
jumps, agent
i
chooses an action
from a finite set (e.g. disclose to all downstream agents on the path or disclose to none), which may induce
an immediate jump in
(m_{t},R_{t})
and thus in her value.

Standard results on impulse control for diffusions (Appendix
B.2
and, for example,
15
;
6
;
1
) imply that, under mild regularity,
V^{i}
solves a quasi-variational inequality (QVI).
To describe the structure heuristically, let
\mathcal{L}
denote the infinitesimal generator of
(S_{t})
when all clocks are kept at their current values and no new signals are disclosed.

Let
\mathcal{M}^{i}
denote the intervention operator that maps a candidate value function
v
to the value obtained
by optimally disclosing at a
disclosure opportunity
for agent
i
, given the strategies of others.
Then the Bellman QVI for agent
i
can be written as

\max\Bigl\{\rho V^{i}(s)-u_{i}(s)-\sup_{\lambda^{i}\in\{0,\bar{\lambda}_{i}\}}\bigl[\mathcal{L}^{\lambda^{i}}V^{i}(s)\bigr],\;V^{i}(s)-\mathcal{M}^{i}V^{i}(s)\Bigr\}\;=\;0,

(5)

for all relevant states
s
.
Here
\mathcal{L}^{\lambda^{i}}
is the generator of
(S_{t})
when agent
i
holds the clock fixed at
\lambda^{i}
and all agents, including
i
, follow the equilibrium disclosure strategy.

Equation (
5
) has the familiar real-options interpretation.
On the set of states where
V^{i}(s)>\mathcal{M}^{i}V^{i}(s)
, agent
i
optimally refrains from disclosing at an arrival; she waits, and her value evolves according to the continuous-time dynamics (the inaction region).
On the set of states where
V^{i}(s)=\mathcal{M}^{i}V^{i}(s)
, she is indifferent and may optimally exercise the disclosure option (the intervention region).
Similarly, for the clock, it is optimal to run the clock only when the marginal benefit of faster information arrival, in terms of future reputational opportunities, exceeds the marginal cost of increasing the probability that unfavorable evidence arrives at a state that would trigger disclosure.

In the LQG benchmark and under quadratic preferences, the DM’s best response is myopic: at each time
t
, the unique optimal action is

y_{t}=m_{t}.

This choice decouples the DM’s optimization from the intermediaries’ problems: intermediaries take the belief process
(m_{t})
as given and influences it indirectly by controlling when signals arrive and whether they are disclosed.
Their dynamic incentives are entirely option-theoretic: each disclosure or period of silence changes the distribution of future beliefs and reputational payoffs.

Relative to the existing dynamic disclosure literature with a single sender (for example,
21
;
28
), the key novelty here is that the disclosure clock is itself a publicly observed control variable and that it lives on a sequence of biased intermediaries.
Furthermore, in contrast to
10
, where a single firm controls a release clock for its own product line, here a sequence of intermediaries jointly shapes beliefs along a path and each agent’s clock choice interacts with others’ reputational options.

4.3
Equilibrium structure on a path

We now characterize the structure of MPBE along a fixed path
P
.
The main result is that, in a rich but tractable class of environments, equilibrium disclosure and clock strategies take the form of a ladder in belief space.
For each agent on the path there is a finite collection of belief thresholds that govern disclosure and silence, and knife-edge mixing can be eliminated by the use of short local-silence windows.

Why an LQG benchmark and what is robust beyond it.

The environment in Section
2
allows for a general diffusion fundamental, verifiable (possibly noisy) evidence, and an abstract Markov reputational technology.
We impose an LQG benchmark below because it yields a low-dimensional Markov public state and an explicit free-boundary characterization of equilibrium values and disclosure cutoffs.

The qualitative structure, however, is not tied to Gaussianity or quadratic losses.

Threshold (ladder) policies.

The disclosure problem faced by an intermediary is an impulse-control/real-options problem in the public belief state: delaying can be privately valuable because disclosure moves the decision maker’s action, but delay is costly because silence is informative when the clock is on.
In such environments, inaction regions separated by intervention boundaries, i.e. an
(s,S)
-type structure, are generic whenever payoffs satisfy a monotone (single-crossing) structure in beliefs; see, e.g.,
15
;
6
;
1
.
Outside LQG, the intervention set need not be characterized by scalar cutoffs in a single variable, but the equilibrium still takes the form of regions in the relevant belief state space where withholding is optimal and regions where disclosure is optimal.

Fundamental dynamics.

Mean reversion is convenient because it delivers stationary belief dynamics and stationary thresholds.
If instead the fundamental is nonstationary (e.g. a random walk with drift), the same impulse-control logic applies, but the relevant boundaries may drift over time or depend on additional sufficient statistics for beliefs (such as posterior variance).
Our network insights are about the
option value of delay
and the
informativeness of silence conditional on observed clocks
, and these forces do not require stationarity.

Quadratic losses.

Quadratic utility implies
y_{t}=m_{t}
and therefore makes the relevant belief index one-dimensional.
With more general strictly convex loss functions, the DM’s optimal action is still uniquely pinned down and is
monotone in her posterior, but it need not be determined by the posterior mean alone.
When the DM’s best reply can be written as a monotone function of a scalar sufficient statistic of the public posterior,
the ladder characterization carries over after replacing
m_{t}
by that statistic.
When additional features of the posterior are payoff relevant, the public state becomes higher-dimensional and the
impulse-control logic yields a continuation/intervention
region
(threshold surfaces) rather than scalar ladder cutoffs.

Appendix
C
develops several extensions (including noisy but verifiable signals and alternative payoff specifications) and clarifies which parts of the analysis use the LQG algebra versus the general impulse-control structure.

, combining the QVI representation in (
5

B.3
), and a verification argument for impulse-control problems.
Part (iii) is where the clocks play a crucial selection role.
In static or discrete-time dynamic disclosure models without clocks, equilibria with mixed strategies at knife-edge beliefs are common.
Here, an agent who would otherwise mix at a belief threshold can post a short local-silence window around that threshold.
Because beliefs do not accumulate local time at such thresholds, the mixing set has measure zero and can be replaced by a pure threshold without affecting payoffs.
The ladder equilibrium is therefore not simply a convenient representation, but a robust consequence of the interaction between dynamic disclosure and observable clocks.

More generally, the ladder is the networked analogue of an
(s,S)
policy in impulse-control problems, and its existence reflects option-theoretic incentives rather than the specific Gaussian/quadratic algebra of the benchmark.

Equilibrium multiplicity and selection.

The dynamic disclosure game is a stochastic game and, in principle, may admit multiple perfect Bayesian equilibria, including history-dependent (non-Markov) equilibria.
We focus on Markov perfect Bayesian equilibria (MPBE) in which strategies depend on the public state
S_{t}=(m_{t},R_{t})
.
Under Assumption
1
,
S_{t}
is a sufficient statistic for payoff-relevant public history along a fixed path: once clocks are publicly observed and reputations are summarized by
R_{t}
, continuation payoffs and feasible deviations depend on the past only through
(m_{t},R_{t})
and realized signals.

Within this Markov class, Theorem
1
does not merely posit threshold strategies: it provides both existence and a
converse
characterization for continuous MPBE with diffusion belief dynamics.
Moreover, the publicly observed clock is an equilibrium-selection device in the timing dimension.
In continuous-time disclosure models without an observable clock, knife-edge belief states often support mixed disclosure timing.
Here an agent who would otherwise mix at a cutoff can instead post a short, publicly observed clock-off window around that state.
Because beliefs accumulate no local time at fixed thresholds conditional on such windows (Appendix
B.3
), the mixing set has measure zero and can be replaced by a pure threshold without affecting payoffs.
Thus, within the economically natural class of equilibria we study, the clock technology eliminates the usual mixed-strategy indeterminacy and selects pure ladder behavior.

A related concern is whether there exist “pooling-on-silence” outcomes in which some information is never disclosed because agents expect it to be disclosed elsewhere (a self-fulfilling withholding equilibrium).
Along a
single
expert-DM path, such free-riding cannot by itself sustain nondisclosure: every intermediary on the path is pivotal for transmission, so if any node withholds, the information cannot reach the DM through that path.
More importantly, Theorem
2
provides a selection-free discipline on
permanent
withholding.
It is stated for
any
MPBE along the path and shows that when reputational stakes are sufficiently large and agents sufficiently patient, equilibria with absorbing “never-disclose” regions are ruled out: if a signal were to be withheld forever, an intermediary can profitably deviate by turning the clock on and releasing the verifiable evidence, trading a bounded material benefit from delay for a reputational gain whose present value grows with
\beta_{i}/\rho
.
Hence, in the high-reputation/patient regime, the paper’s core completeness prediction, aka eventual disclosure of all verifiable evidence along a path, does not depend on selecting a “good” equilibrium.

What Theorem
1
does
not
by itself guarantee is uniqueness of the
exact
ladder thresholds.
Because intermediaries’ cutoffs interact along the path, the coupled boundary-value system may admit multiple solutions, corresponding to different self-consistent disclosure speeds.
For transparency, Proposition
4
assumes uniqueness when stating comparative statics in single-valued form.
We conjecture that in the LQG benchmark uniqueness is generic, i.e. each intermediary’s impulse-control problem has a unique value function solving the associated QVI given others’ strategies, and the clock makes the best response single-valued, but we do not require a general uniqueness theorem for our main disclosure results.
When multiple ladder equilibria exist, they can be interpreted as alternative equilibrium
timing
regimes (all with the same ladder structure), and publicly posted reporting calendars or disclosure windows (captured here by observable clocks) can be viewed as an institutional design feature that coordinates beliefs and selects among them.

The static benchmark in Section
3.2
shows that bias reversals along a path can block full unraveling.
In the dynamic setting, reputational concerns and clocks mitigate this effect in a strong sense.
The next result provides a sufficient condition for full eventual disclosure along a path, even in the presence of arbitrary bias reversals.

Network-level interpretation.

Scope and the boundary between regimes.

Theorem

It isolates a region of parameter values in which the dynamic reputational stakes dominate the
(short-run) material gains from selective withholding.
Two remarks clarify the trade-off.

First, the relevant object is the

of reputational consequences.
Because reputational payoffs enter flow utility and are discounted at rate

Second, the conditions are economically meaningful rather than technical.
In the polar cases

Mechanism: why reputation forces eventual disclosure.

The key force is that
silence is an equilibrium signal
.
When an intermediary’s clock is on, outsiders know that disclosure opportunities arrive with a
positive hazard.
Observing
no
disclosure therefore triggers an inference that unfavorable evidence may have
arrived and been withheld.
This inference has two consequences that make permanent suppression unattractive when reputational
stakes are high.

(i) Reputational drift from informative silence.

Strategic silence while on the clock worsens public assessments of an intermediary’s transparency
and/or informational quality and therefore depresses her reputational state (and continuation value).
Because the reputational term enters flow utility, this generates a
stream
of losses rather
than a one-shot penalty.

(ii) Eventual verifiability and reputational exposure.

Since disclosures are hard evidence and the fundamental continues to evolve, prolonged selective
withholding cannot be made innocuous by “waiting it out”: over time, the implied informational
content of continued silence becomes inconsistent with the realized evolution of beliefs and
subsequent disclosures along the path.
Thus, an intermediary who attempts to suppress indefinitely faces a reputational capital loss that
accumulates in present value.

The proof in Appendix
A.2
formalizes these ideas via a deviation argument.
Fix a candidate equilibrium history on which some signal would be blocked forever at an intermediary.
Because the material benefit from withholding is bounded on the relevant equilibrium region of the
state space (it is a gain from keeping the DM’s action closer to
X_{t}+b_{i}
for some time), while the
discounted reputational loss from being perceived as selectively silent grows linearly in
\beta_{i}

and is amplified as
\rho

signal after an appropriate clock adjustment.
For
\beta_{i}
large and
\rho
small this deviation yields a strict improvement in continuation value,
contradicting equilibrium.
Iterating the argument along the path rules out permanent blocks at every node.

What the theorem does (and does not) imply.

Theorem
2
establishes
eventual
disclosure, not immediate revelation.
Delays and clustered disclosures remain part of equilibrium behavior (indeed, they are summarized by
the ladder representation in Theorem
1
).
The novelty is that, in the reputationally strong and patient regime, the ladder cannot sustain
absorbing “never-disclose” regions: any attempt to turn selective withholding into a permanent
outcome is disciplined by reputational incentives and informative silence.

Finally, the result is distinct from the single-sender dynamic disclosure models of

21
and
28
: here information must traverse
a sequence of
biased
strategic intermediaries, so static bottlenecks exist, and the theorem
shows that sufficiently strong dynamic reputational forces can overcome those network frictions.
It also complements

by showing that once reputational capital and disclosure
timing instruments are distributed across a path, the same option-theoretic logic yields a robust
restoration of unraveling.

Benchmark restriction: trees versus general graphs.

Then, for
\beta_{i}
sufficiently large relative to
\beta_{j}
and the volatility of
X_{t}
sufficiently small, we have

V_{0}(G^{\mathrm{RS}})\;>\;V_{0}(G^{\mathrm{OL}}),\qquad W(G^{\mathrm{RS}})\;>\;W(G^{\mathrm{OL}}),

even net of the additional link cost in
G^{\mathrm{RS}}
.
In particular, the planner strictly prefers to give the more reputationally sensitive intermediary a direct link to the expert and to the DM, rather than placing her in the middle of an ordered line.

Theorem
3
isolates a genuinely dynamic effect driven by serial bottlenecks.
In
G^{\mathrm{RS}}
(Figure
2
b), the high-reputation intermediary
i
operates her ladder on a direct route to the decision maker.
Hence her higher reputational intensity translates into tighter inaction regions and earlier disclosure, as in the path-level comparative statics in Proposition
4
.
The second intermediary
j
provides an additional, typically slower, route in parallel, but he cannot delay
i
because he does not sit on
i
’s transmission route.

In
G^{\mathrm{OL}}
(Figure
2
a), the low-reputation intermediary
j
lies downstream and therefore controls when information reaches
0
.
When
\beta_{j}
is small,
j
optimally waits in a wide inaction region, and the decision maker experiences long silent spells even in states in which
i
would prefer rapid disclosure.
Thus, placing a highly reputationally sensitive intermediary in the middle of a line need not create fast disclosure at the decision maker, because the downstream agent’s disclosure option remains the binding constraint.

Taken together, Proposition
5
and Theorem
3
show that dynamic disclosure can overturn static optimality rankings in a way that is natural from an economic standpoint.
When reputational intensities are homogeneous and biases admit an aligned ordering, the ordered line remains optimal and the dynamic model strengthens its information-revelation properties.
When reputational intensities are heterogeneous, it can be optimal to
screen by topology
, placing highly reputationally sensitive intermediaries on direct, parallel routes to the decision maker rather than in a single ordered chain.
This mechanism has no counterpart in static models of disclosure on networks and highlights a new channel through which dynamic reputation interacts with network architecture.
The proofs follow the ladder characterization in Theorem
1
and the comparative statics in Proposition
4
, and are provided in Appendix
A.3
.

5.3
Network formation game

We finally endogenize the network by letting agents form links in anticipation of the dynamic disclosure game.
We adapt standard models of bilateral link formation in networks (see, e.g.,
23
) to our setting with dynamic reputational disclosure.

A
network formation game
proceeds as follows.
There is a finite set of potential directed links
L^{\max}\subseteq N\times N
.

For each ordered pair
(i,j)\in L^{\max}
, creating the directed link
(i,j)
requires the
consent of both endpoints:
i
(the sender) must be willing to create the link, and
j
(the receiver)
must be willing to accept it. Maintaining
(i,j)
entails a (present-value) cost
c_{ij}\geq 0

borne by the sender
i
.

A (pure) link-formation strategy for agent
i
therefore specifies consent both as a sender and as a receiver.
Formally, let

L^{\max}_{i,\mathrm{out}}\equiv\{j\in N:(i,j)\in L^{\max}\},\qquad L^{\max}_{i,\mathrm{in}}\equiv\{j\in N:(j,i)\in L^{\max}\}.

Agent
i
’s strategy is a pair
s_{i}=(s_{i}^{\mathrm{out}},s_{i}^{\mathrm{in}})
where

s_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}:L^{\max}_{i,\mathrm{out}}\to\{0,1\}
indicates which outgoing links
(i,j)
she is willing to form
and
s_{i}^{\mathrm{in}}:L^{\max}_{i,\mathrm{in}}\to\{0,1\}
indicates which incoming links
(j,i)
she is willing to accept.
Given a profile
s=(s_{i})_{i\in N}
, the realized link set is

L(s)\;=\;\{(i,j)\in L^{\max}:s_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}(j)=1\ \text{and}\ s_{j}^{\mathrm{in}}(i)=1\},

and the realized network is
G(s)=(N,L(s))
.

Once the network
G
is realized, the dynamic disclosure game described in Sections
2
and
4
is played, and each agent
i
obtains the equilibrium payoff
V_{i}(G)
net of her share of link costs (the sender pays
c_{ij}
, but the receiver may still veto a link if it lowers
V_{j}(G)
).

Thus her overall payoff in the formation game is

\tilde{V}_{i}(G)\;=\;V_{i}(G)\;-\;\sum_{j:(i,j)\in L}c_{ij}.

We focus on two benchmark concepts.

Definition 2

(Pairwise stability)
.

A network
G
is
pairwise stable
if:

(i)

for every link
(i,j)\in L
, neither
i
nor
j
would strictly gain by unilaterally severing it:

\tilde{V}_{i}(G)\;\geq\;\tilde{V}_{i}\bigl(G-(i,j)\bigr),\qquad\tilde{V}_{j}(G)\;\geq\;\tilde{V}_{j}\bigl(G-(i,j)\bigr);

(ii)

for every non-link
(i,j)\in L^{\max}\setminus L
, at least one of the two agents would not strictly gain by adding
(i,j)
:

\tilde{V}_{i}\bigl(G+(i,j)\bigr)\;\leq\;\tilde{V}_{i}(G)\quad\text{or}\quad\tilde{V}_{j}\bigl(G+(i,j)\bigr)\;\leq\;\tilde{V}_{j}(G).

Definition 3

(Efficiency)
.

A network
G
is (utilitarian-)
efficient
if it maximizes the social value
W(G)
defined in (
7
) among all feasible networks.

As in the static network-formation literature, pairwise stable networks need not be efficient.
In our dynamic disclosure environment, the externalities are particularly rich: by altering the set and ordering of paths, a new link can change the timing and completeness of disclosure for multiple experts and decision makers, as well as the reputational trajectories of several intermediaries.
The next proposition provides a simple but robust inefficiency result.

Proposition 6

(Inefficiencies in endogenous networks)
.

Suppose link costs
c_{ij}
are small but strictly positive, and that Theorem
2
applies along any feasible path (so that eventual full disclosure is attainable in all tree networks).
Then:

(i)

there exist parameter configurations under which every pairwise stable network is strictly less efficient than some other feasible network that has strictly more links;

(ii)

there exist parameter configurations under which every pairwise stable network is strictly less efficient than some other feasible network that has strictly fewer links.

In particular, dynamic reputational disclosure can lead to both
under-connected
and
over-connected
stable networks relative to the utilitarian benchmark.

Proposition
6
isolates a basic but useful message: in a dynamic reputational disclosure environment, link externalities are not sign-definite.
A directed link can create
positive
spillovers by accelerating disclosure on other routes, but it can also create
negative
spillovers by changing the option value and reputational incentives embedded in pre-existing routes.

Two channels: reachability and timing incentives.

It is helpful to separate two conceptually distinct effects of adding a link.
First, there is a
reachability
effect: a new link can create an additional expert-to-DM route or bypass a bottleneck, thereby changing which pieces of evidence can reach the decision maker at all.
Second, there is a
timing-incentive
effect: even when reachability is unchanged (e.g. when the evidence is already able to reach the DM), a new link can alter equilibrium clocks and ladder thresholds because it changes what silence implies and who can capture reputational credit.
The second effect is absent in static connection models with reduced-form link payoffs, but it is central here because disclosure is an option exercised in continuous time.

Under-connection: positive timing externalities that are not internalized.

Part (i) corresponds to a classic mechanism in network formation: the endpoints of a link need not capture the full social gain generated by that link.
In our setting, the gain can be large even when the link does not merely “add bandwidth” but instead
disciplines delay
.
A link that gives a reputationally sensitive intermediary access to an expert, or that gives an expert access to an alternative downstream route, can tighten inaction regions and shorten silence spells on other parts of the network.
The benefit is typically realized as (i) earlier disclosure and hence higher
V_{0}(G)
, and (ii) reputational improvements for agents whose silence is now less easily interpreted as strategic withholding.
Because these benefits accrue to third parties, the link may fail to form whenever link costs are borne locally.

Example: a bypass that does not form.

Consider one expert
e
, one DM
0
, and two intermediaries
i
and
j
.
Suppose the existing network routes information through the low-reputation-sensitive intermediary
j
(so the unique path is
e\to j\to 0
) and that
j
’s equilibrium ladder has a wide inaction region.
Adding the two links
e\to i
and
i\to 0
creates a short parallel route on which
i
can operate a fast ladder.
Even if
j
’s behavior is unchanged, the DM’s value can rise because information now reaches
0
sooner through
i
.
Moreover, because the new route changes what prolonged silence downstream signifies, it can also induce
j
to disclose sooner in equilibrium, amplifying the welfare gain.
If the cost of
e\to i
is borne by
e
and the cost of
i\to 0
is borne by
i
, then the endpoints may be unwilling to pay for links whose primary beneficiaries are
0
and other agents, so the socially valuable bypass does not form.

Over-connection: negative timing externalities from redundancy and pre-emption.

Part (ii) reflects a force that is specific to dynamic disclosure.
When the same verifiable evidence can reach the DM through multiple downstream routes, intermediaries compete for reputational credit and the informational content of silence becomes route-dependent.
A new link can reduce the continuation value of existing links by increasing the probability that an incumbent intermediary is
pre-empted
, thereby lowering the option value of waiting embedded in the incumbent’s ladder and worsening her expected reputational trajectory.

If the informational benefit of redundancy is small (because evidence is verifiable and would reach the DM anyway),
the induced reputational race and the associated distortion of clocks and thresholds, i.e., the pre-emption-induced
erosion of the incumbent’s option value of waiting, which compresses her inaction region and shifts her ladder cutoffs
inward, can make the marginal social value of the new link negative even though it is privately attractive.

Example: a redundant parallel route that is privately attractive.

Consider a network in which a high-reputation-sensitive intermediary
i
already provides a fast route from
e
to
0
(so
e\to i\to 0
is present and yields rapid disclosure).
A second intermediary
j
may wish to add a parallel route, for instance by forming
e\to j
and
j\to 0
, because doing so gives
j
opportunities to disclose promptly and build reputation.

However, the added route changes how the public interprets
i
’s silence and can lower
i
’s expected reputational payoff when
j
discloses first
(because
i
’s contemporaneous silence is then perceived as selective withholding),
even if the DM’s information set is little improved.

When reputational stakes are high and link costs are small, the private incentive of
j
to enter can exceed the incremental social value, because
j
does not internalize the reputational and timing losses imposed on incumbents.
In that case, pairwise stability can select a network with redundant links even though a sparser architecture would maximize
W(G)
.

What parameters tilt the balance.

These mechanisms suggest transparent comparative statics.
High link costs
c_{ij}
tend to push toward
under
-connection, because they exacerbate the wedge between local incentives and system-wide timing gains.
By contrast, low link costs combined with strong reputational concerns (large
\beta_{i}
for many agents) tend to push toward
over
-connection, because they increase the private value of “having a shot” at reputational credit on a short route even when the informational benefit of an additional route is small.
Volatility and patience matter through the option-value channel: when the option value of waiting is high (e.g. under high uncertainty or weak discounting), changes in competitive pressure created by additional links can have first-order effects on equilibrium disclosure timing, and therefore on the sign and magnitude of link externalities.

Relation to the connection model and novelty.

In the standard connections model of
23
, the main inefficiency is underinvestment in links driven by positive externalities.
Our environment preserves that force, but it adds a second, genuinely dynamic one: because disclosure is an option exercised over time and reputations are inferred from observable silence, a new link can impose a
negative
externality by pre-empting incumbents and reducing the option value and reputational value of existing routes.
This two-sided externality is why pairwise stable networks can be either under-connected or over-connected relative to the utilitarian benchmark in Proposition
6
.

These inefficiencies highlight a policy dimension that we return to in Section
6
: regulators or platform designers who care about the speed and completeness of information flow in reputational networks may need to shape not only disclosure rules but also the underlying network architecture.
Subsidizing or mandating links that activate high-value ladders, and pruning or taxing links that crowd them out, can move the system closer to the efficient benchmark.
From a methodological standpoint, this extends classic insights on the gap between stability and efficiency in network formation
(
23
)
to a setting in which the payoffs from links arise from dynamic real options on reputational capital rather than from static pairwise benefits.

AI release and audit networks

Consider AI model release and safety auditing.
Here the fundamental diffusion

is a one-dimensional “higher-is-better”

The index can be interpreted as a safety-adjusted capability score that already embeds

The decision maker

To fix ideas, consider the chain

\ell\;\to\;c\;\to\;a\;\to\;0,

This is a minimal “auditor star” at the DM: the regulator/platform can receive

gated
by a slower intermediary

Bias, reputation, and why observability of cadence matters.

Reputational capital
R^{i}_{t}
measures how often a lab, auditor, or consortium is perceived

or timely publication of negative results rather than selective silence).
The disclosure clock
\lambda^{i}_{t}
maps naturally to the cadence of evaluation and reporting

Design implications.

Two design lessons are particularly transparent in this setting.

First, Theorem
2
provides a disciplined sufficient condition
for eventual revelation along any path: when reputational stakes are high and discounting is weak,

In audit terms, this corresponds to environments where being caught as selectively silent has

Second, when reputational stakes are heterogeneous, the topology results suggest that a purely

Finally, Proposition

6.2
Financial and rating networks

A second natural application is to financial and rating networks.
Here the fundamental
X_{t}
is an issuer’s evolving credit quality (or more broadly the discounted
value of future cash flows).
Decision makers are investors, lenders, or risk managers whose actions
y_{t}
represent capital
allocation, pricing, or exposure limits.
Experts are issuers, research boutiques, or lead underwriters with privileged access to verifiable
signals about
X_{t}
.
Intermediaries include credit rating agencies, sell-side analysts, and syndicate members who relay
and interpret evidence.

A concrete schematic: parallel ratings versus serial filtration.

Consider an issuer/expert
e

i
and
j

0
.
A simple comparison that mirrors Theorem

Serial filtration (a chain):

e\to i\to j\to 0,

versus

Parallel disclosure (a reputational star at the DM):

e\to i\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad e\to j\to 0.

This matters even if agencies have similar directional incentives (similar biases

\beta_{i}>\beta_{j}
because one agency is more exposed

Clocks as review cadence and “under review” windows.

The disclosure clock
\lambda^{i}_{t}
corresponds to the pace of rating reviews, watch-list updates,

Publicly observable watch/review periods map naturally to local silence windows: during such

Design implications and interpretation.

In this setting, the model clarifies why redundancy can accelerate rather than slow down

6.3
Policy and advisory networks

A third application is to policy and advisory networks, including think tank ecosystems, expert
councils, and advisory structures that inform fiscal, monetary, or regulatory decisions.
Here the fundamental
X_{t}
is a policy-relevant state (e.g., the output gap, inflationary pressure,
or net benefits of a reform), and the decision maker is a government, central bank committee, or
regulatory commission whose action
y_{t}
is a policy stance.

A concrete schematic: attribution and parallel channels.

Consider a technical unit or statistical office
e
that produces verifiable analysis about
X_{t}
,
an intermediary
i
such as an ideologically slanted think tank or advocacy group, and a policy body
0
.
A basic chain is

e\to i\to 0,

in which
i
has a directional bias
b_{i}\neq 0
and may have incentives to delay or selectively
relay evidence.

Two model-based design points are then immediate.

First, attribution and verifiability are part of the reputational technology:
the force of reputational discipline requires that later realizations (outcomes, audits, revisions)
map back to who disclosed what and when. Institutionally, this corresponds to transparency norms that
make it difficult to “launder” evidence through anonymous summaries: publishing source documents,
timestamping analyses, and clearly identifying authorship strengthens the link between disclosure
timing and reputational capital.

Second, parallel channels mitigate politicized bottlenecks.
If a highly reputable technical unit has high
\beta_{e}
(future access depends strongly on being seen
as accurate and transparent), then giving it a direct channel to the decision maker (a small
reputational star) can dominate routing all information through a politicized intermediary.
This is the policy analogue of screening-by-topology: it prevents slow or biased intermediaries from
sitting in series with fast, high-stakes experts.

Cadence choices.

The clock
\lambda^{i}_{t}
corresponds to the cadence of reports, hearings, briefings, or forecast
updates. Public schedules (e.g., fixed forecast rounds or scheduled testimony) play the same
selection role as in the core model: they clarify when silence is uninformative and when it is
informative, and thereby discipline selective withholding through reputational inference.

Design implications.

Taken together, the model suggests an architecture principle for advisory systems:
maintain diversity of perspectives without forcing high-quality/high-accountability sources
to speak only through a single gatekeeper. Parallel access for highly reputationally sensitive
units (direct testimony, public release channels, independent publication mandates) can increase the
timeliness of verifiable evidence while preserving the disciplining role of reputation.

Unifying takeaway.

Across the three domains, the unifying message is that reputational dynamics, observable disclosure
cadences, and network structure jointly determine which verifiable evidence reaches key decision
makers, when it arrives, and through which channels.
The formal results show why (i) ladder-like timing patterns arise generically, (ii) sufficiently
strong reputational stakes eliminate equilibria with permanent suppression along a path, and
(iii) network topology can either harness or neutralize reputational discipline by placing fast and
slow intermediaries in parallel versus in series.

These applications are deliberately stylized; extending any one of them into a detailed empirical
case study would require institutional measurement of disclosure cadence, reputational stakes, and
network connectivity, which we view as a promising direction for future work.

7
Conclusion

This paper develops a continuous-time model of reputational disclosure in networks of biased intermediaries. A payoff-relevant fundamental evolves as a diffusion, information arrives as verifiable signals that must travel along directed paths, and intermediaries care both about the induced decision and about a reputational state that summarizes how often their past disclosures have been vindicated.
A useful lens is classic career concerns: as in
22
, current actions are disciplined by their effect on a public posterior about type, but here the action is
when
to disclose verifiable evidence (a timing option), and the reputational consequences are shaped by network structure because others’ disclosures affect what one’s silence implies.
A key primitive is a disclosure clock that lets each intermediary control the arrival rate of verifiable signals on her links and, in particular, post publicly observable local-silence windows. These ingredients allow us to combine the static unraveling logic of
20
;
27
with dynamic timing and with explicit network structure.

The analysis proceeds in three steps. Section
3
revisits static disclosure on networks and takes as benchmark the path result in
30
, according to which full unraveling of verifiable information along a path is generically (i.e. except on a null set of states/parameters) possible if and only if intermediaries’ biases are aligned on one side of the decision maker. In that static environment, a single bias reversal along a path blocks full disclosure even when signals are fully verifiable and all players are rational. Section
4
then embeds this environment in continuous time and introduces reputational dynamics and clocks. Along any fixed expert-decision maker path, Theorem
1
shows that any Markov perfect Bayesian equilibrium satisfying the paper’s standing regularity conditions (in particular, continuity in
(m,R)
and a diffusion representation for the public state
(S_{t})
) admits a ladder representation: agents follow pure threshold strategies in belief space, and local silence eliminates knife-edge mixing. When reputational concerns are sufficiently strong relative to static bias payoffs and discounting is not too severe, Theorem
2
establishes that dynamic reputational incentives restore full eventual disclosure along any path, even if static bias reversals would have precluded unraveling. In that regime, every verifiable signal that reaches an expert almost surely reaches the decision maker in finite time, and the timing of disclosure is governed by a tractable real-options structure.

The real-options perspective is central. The combination of a diffusion for beliefs and an impulse-control representation of disclosure leads to equilibria that resemble
S
-
s
policies in belief space. Between disclosure times, the decision maker’s belief drifts with the diffusion, while each intermediary weighs the option value of waiting against the reputational gain from speaking today. The comparative statics in Proposition
4
formalize this tradeoff. Higher reputational intensity compresses inaction regions and induces earlier and more frequent disclosures. Higher volatility enlarges inaction regions and generates longer periods of silence and more clustered announcements. Changes in bias shift thresholds asymmetrically and yield predictable distortions in the relative treatment of good and bad news. These path-level results provide a disciplined way to interpret observed patterns of disclosure, silence, and revision in terms of underlying primitives.

The model’s comparative statics translate directly into observable timing patterns.
A central prediction is
silence-then-flurry
: when uncertainty is high, intermediaries rationally wait longer (higher option value), so inter-disclosure durations lengthen and releases become more clustered.
Conversely, when reputational exposure is high (large
\beta_{i}
or long horizons), silence while “on the clock” is more costly, so disclosure becomes more frequent and lags shrink.
Finally, directional incentives imply sign asymmetries: the same intermediary should be systematically faster to release information that moves downstream actions toward her bias and slower to release information that moves them away.

These implications suggest straightforward empirical strategies.
Treat disclosures (ratings changes, forecast revisions, audit reports, guidance updates) as point-process events and estimate how the hazard of disclosure, the distribution of inter-event times, and burstiness/clustering measures respond to (i) volatility/uncertainty proxies and (ii) proxies for reputational exposure (e.g. regulatory scrutiny, transparency mandates, career concerns, or the value of future influence). These patterns echo empirical evidence on delayed disclosure of negative information (e.g.,
25
),
and connect to recent accounting work modeling joint dynamics of information acquisition, investment, and disclosure (e.g.,
26
).
We view mapping ladder-like disclosure behavior into specific datasets and network environments as a promising direction for future empirical work.

Section
5
lifts the analysis from a single path to the full network.
We define the value of a link and the social value of a directed network, and we characterize which minimally connected topologies can sustain full dynamic disclosure for a given profile of reputational intensities and discounting.
When we do not impose the high-reputation and patient regime in Theorem
2
, the disclosure-sustaining trees are exactly the bias-monotone ones: there exists an ordering of intermediaries by bias such that every simple expert-DM path is monotone in that order.
In contrast, when the conditions of Theorem
2
hold along the relevant expert-DM paths, eventual disclosure obtains regardless of bias reversals, so every tree is disclosure-sustaining and topology primarily governs disclosure timing and welfare.

Under homogeneous reputational intensities and clock technologies, Proposition
5
shows that the ordered line that sorts intermediaries by bias remains optimal in the dynamic sense.
It maximizes both the decision maker’s ex ante payoff and social welfare, and it delivers a ladder that is weakly steeper, in the sense of earlier revelation in belief space, than in any other tree with the same nodes.

However, dynamic forces also generate new network externalities. When intermediaries differ in reputational sensitivity, it can be socially optimal to screen by topology, placing highly reputationally sensitive intermediaries on direct, parallel paths rather than at interior positions in an ordered chain. In such reputational stars, fast ladders operated by high-intensity intermediaries are never bottlenecked by slower ones, as captured by Theorem
3
. Our network-formation analysis then shows that link creation in this environment involves rich externalities. Proposition
6
demonstrates that pairwise-stable networks can be both under-connected and over-connected relative to the utilitarian benchmark. High-value links that activate fast ladders may fail to form because their private benefits are smaller than their social contribution, while low-value or redundant links that dilute reputational incentives may persist because endpoints benefit from local reputation even as aggregate welfare falls. This gap between stability and efficiency highlights normative questions about the regulation of information networks and the design of intermediated communication channels.

A final remark concerns the role of the decision maker. Throughout the disclosure game, the decision maker is passive in the sense that she chooses actions to minimize her loss given public beliefs. This is deliberate: with verifiable evidence, ex post it is optimal to condition on any disclosed signal, so punishing an intermediary by ignoring truthful information is not credible without commitment. Nevertheless, our network-design analysis has a natural implementation interpretation. Proposition
5
and Theorem
3
describe reporting relationships a principal would like to maintain in order to accelerate verifiable disclosure, and Proposition
6
clarifies why decentralized link formation may fail to deliver that architecture. A natural extension is to endogenize receiver-side instruments, for example by allowing the decision maker to choose the network, subsidize or tax links, set reporting cadences or deadlines, or contract on timeliness and ex post corroboration, and to study how these tools interact with equilibrium disclosure ladders. Relatedly, extensions with multiple audiences naturally interact with receiver-side instruments, because the decision maker may be able to shape not only who is connected to whom but also which audiences observe which disclosures.

Section
6
illustrates how the theory maps to real-world settings. In AI release and audit networks,
the model clarifies when reputational incentives and observable evaluation cadences can ensure that safety-relevant evidence
eventually reaches regulators, and when structural features of the audit network prevent this because no directed lab-regulator
path satisfies the sufficient high-reputation/patient conditions of Theorem
2
(or no directed path exists). In financial and rating networks, it provides a microfoundation for the value of rating relationships as real options on reputational capital and for the role of overlapping coverage in accelerating or slowing the flow of credit-relevant information. In policy and advisory networks, it suggests how advisory architecture and disclosure norms influence whether technocratic advice can bypass politicized bottlenecks.

Though stylised, our framework is designed to be extendable.
Allowing for multidimensional fundamentals and actions would replace one-dimensional ladders with higher-dimensional intervention regions and would connect the analysis to portfolio choice over multiple informational dimensions.
Allowing for multiple decision makers or multiple audiences would preserve the logic of verifiable disclosure and informative silence when disclosures are public and commonly observed, but would raise new questions about welfare aggregation and about coordination across receivers.
If instead audiences observe different subsets of messages or intermediaries can target disclosures, the relevant public state would include audience-specific beliefs and reputations, and intermediaries would face a dynamic allocation problem that couples disclosure ladders across audiences.
Allowing for multiple experts would introduce interacting sources of evidence.
Under conditional independence, posteriors aggregate additively and path-level ladders combine in a transparent way, while correlation or heterogeneous access would generate new pre-emption and free-riding forces across sources.
Finally, allowing intermediaries to send non-verifiable messages would add a strategic misreporting margin and would require reputation to discipline both timing and content, turning disclosure into a joint choice of what to say and when to say it.
These extensions would also connect our disclosure framework to dynamic persuasion and information-design models (e.g.,
11
).
We view these extensions as promising directions for future work.

Overall, our results show that reputational dynamics, control over the arrival of verifiable signals, and network topology jointly shape who learns what, when, and through which channels. A network that is static and biased in a way that blocks disclosure in the one-shot model can become dynamically disclosure-sustaining once agents care sufficiently about their reputations and can modulate their clocks, while a network that looks innocuous from a static perspective can destroy the value of reputational incentives by running fast and slow intermediaries in series. By combining tools from dynamic disclosure, social and economic networks, and real options, the paper provides a unified representation of these forces and a set of comparative statics that can guide both empirical work and the design of information networks in practice.

The proof is a standard unraveling argument adapted to quadratic loss, following

Fix a perfect Bayesian equilibrium and let

\theta

Verifiability implies that any on-path announcement of
\theta

Let

Conditional on silence, the decision maker’s posterior is supported on

\theta^{\ast}
and his best response is

Proof.

Lemma 2

Proof.

For OU,
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\mid X_{s}]=\bar{x}+e^{-\kappa(t-s)}(X_{s}-\bar{x})
for some constant
\bar{x}
.
Taking conditional expectations w.r.t.
\mathcal{G}\vee\sigma(X_{s})
and
\mathcal{G}
yields the identity.
The variance bound follows from
\operatorname{Var}(X_{s}\mid\mathcal{G})\leq\operatorname{Var}(X_{s})\leq\bar{v}
.
∎

B.2
Impulse control and verification

We now recall a verification result for Markov impulse-control problems and adapt it to the dynamic disclosure environment.
The formulation parallels the representation in Section
4.2
.

Generic impulse-control problem.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open state space and let
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a time-homogeneous Markov process with values in
S
.
Between intervention times,
S_{t}
evolves as a diffusion with generator
\mathcal{L}
acting on
C_{b}^{2}(S)
as

\mathcal{L}\varphi(s)\;=\;b(s)\cdot\nabla\varphi(s)+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\bigl(a(s)a(s)^{\top}D^{2}\varphi(s)\bigr),

where
b\colon S\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
a\colon S\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are continuous and locally bounded.

Impulse controls are modeled by a sequence of stopping times
(\tau_{k})_{k\geq 1}
and post-intervention states
(\zeta_{k})_{k\geq 1}
, where each
\zeta_{k}
is
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{k}}
-measurable and takes values in a given action set
Z\subset S
.
If the pre-intervention state at time
\tau_{k}
is
S_{\tau_{k}^{-}}
and the controller chooses
\zeta_{k}
, then the post-intervention state is

S_{\tau_{k}}\;=\;\Gamma\bigl(S_{\tau_{k}^{-}},\zeta_{k}\bigr)

for some measurable map
\Gamma\colon S\times Z\to S
.

Given a flow payoff
f\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
, an instantaneous intervention payoff (or cost)
K\colon S\times Z\to\mathbb{R}
, and a discount rate
\rho>0
, the expected discounted payoff under a control policy
(\tau_{k},\zeta_{k})_{k\geq 1}
and initial state
s
is

J\bigl(s;(\tau_{k},\zeta_{k})_{k\geq 1}\bigr)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{s}\Biggl[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}f(S_{t})\,dt+\sum_{k\geq 1}e^{-\rho\tau_{k}}K\bigl(S_{\tau_{k}^{-}},\zeta_{k}\bigr)\Biggr],

(11)

where
\mathbb{E}_{s}
denotes expectation conditional on
S_{0}=s
.

Define the value function

V(s)\;=\;\sup_{(\tau_{k},\zeta_{k})}J\bigl(s;(\tau_{k},\zeta_{k})_{k\geq 1}\bigr).

For a candidate value function
v\in C(S)
, introduce the intervention operator

\mathcal{M}v(s)\;=\;\sup_{\zeta\in Z}\Bigl\{v\bigl(\Gamma(s,\zeta)\bigr)+K(s,\zeta)\Bigr\},

(12)

and the associated quasi-variational inequality (QVI)

\max\Bigl\{\rho v(s)-f(s)-\mathcal{L}v(s),\;v(s)-\mathcal{M}v(s)\Bigr\}\;=\;0\qquad\text{for all }s\in S.

(13)

Lemma 4

(Verification for impulse control)
.

Suppose there exists a continuous function
v\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
and a Borel set
C\subset S
such that:

(i)

v
is twice continuously differentiable on
C
and satisfies

\rho v(s)-f(s)-\mathcal{L}v(s)\;=\;0\quad\text{for all }s\in C;

(ii)

for all
s\in S
,

v(s)\;\geq\;\mathcal{M}v(s),

and for all
s\in S\setminus C
there exists
\zeta^{\ast}(s)\in Z
attaining the supremum in (
12
) such that
v(s)=\mathcal{M}v(s)
;

(iii)

v
has at most linear growth, and the coefficients
b
,
a
, and
f
are such that the family

\Bigl\{e^{-\rho t}v(S_{t})+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\rho u}f(S_{u})\,du:t\geq 0\Bigr\}

is uniformly integrable under any admissible control policy.

Define the candidate optimal policy by:

•

letting the process evolve freely as long as it remains in
C
;

•

whenever
S_{t}
exits
C
, applying an impulse that maps
S_{t}
to
\Gamma\bigl(S_{t},\zeta^{\ast}(S_{t})\bigr)
.

Then
v=V
is the value function of the impulse-control problem and the policy described above is optimal.

Proof.

The argument uses Dynkin’s formula and the dynamic programming principle for impulse control.
Consider any admissible policy
(\tau_{k},\zeta_{k})
and fix a finite horizon
T>0
.
On each interval between interventions,
v
solves

\rho v(S_{t})-f(S_{t})-\mathcal{L}v(S_{t})=0,

so Itô’s formula gives, up to the first intervention time at or before
T
,

e^{-\rho t}v(S_{t})=v(s)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\rho u}f(S_{u})\,du+M_{t},

where
(M_{t})
is a local martingale.
Iterating this relation across intervention times and adding the contributions of
K(S_{\tau_{k}^{-}},\zeta_{k})
implied by (
12
) yields

v(s)\;\geq\;\mathbb{E}_{s}\Biggl[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}f(S_{t})\,dt+\sum_{\tau_{k}\leq T}e^{-\rho\tau_{k}}K(S_{\tau_{k}^{-}},\zeta_{k})+e^{-\rho T}v(S_{T})\Biggr],

where the inequality uses
v\geq\mathcal{M}v
.
Uniform integrability in (iii) and the linear-growth bound imply that
e^{-\rho T}v(S_{T})
converges to zero in
L^{1}
as
T\to\infty
.
Letting
T\to\infty
yields
v(s)\geq J\bigl(s;(\tau_{k},\zeta_{k})\bigr)
for any admissible policy, hence
v\geq V
.

For the candidate policy, the same calculation gives equality at each intervention time because
v(s)=\mathcal{M}v(s)
for all
s\in S\setminus C
and because
\zeta^{\ast}(s)
attains the supremum in (
12
).
The same limitng argument implies that
v(s)=J\bigl(s;(\tau_{k}^{\ast},\zeta_{k}^{\ast})\bigr)
, where
(\tau_{k}^{\ast},\zeta_{k}^{\ast})
denotes the policy induced by
C
and
\zeta^{\ast}(\cdot)
.
Thus
v\leq V
and we conclude
v=V
and optimality of the candidate policy.
∎

Specialization to the dynamic disclosure problem.

On a fixed expert-DM path, the relevant state for an intermediary
i
can be taken as
S_{t}=(m_{t},R^{i}_{t})
.
Between disclosure times,
(m_{t},R^{i}_{t})
evolves as a diffusion on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{R}_{i}
, where
\mathcal{R}_{i}
is the support of
R^{i}_{t}
, with generator
\mathcal{L}^{i}
that combines the drift and diffusion of
(m_{t})
and
R^{i}_{t}
under Assumptions
1
and
2
.
The intervention operator
\mathcal{M}^{i}
corresponds to optimally disclosing at a signal arrival, which induces a jump in
(m_{t},R^{i}_{t})
and an instantaneous change in reputational value.
Lemma
4
applies once we verify smoothness of the candidate value function on the inaction region and the growth and integrability conditions for

This yields the QVI and verification step used in the proof of Theorem

.

B.3
Disclosure clocks and the local-silence lemma

We now formalize the Cox-process representation of disclosure clocks and prove the local-silence result used to eliminate mixed strategies in the path analysis.

Cox processes for potential signal arrivals.

For each agent
i\in N
there exists a unit-intensity Poisson process
(\widetilde{N}^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, independent across
i
and independent of
(X_{t})
.
The publicly observed clock
(\lambda^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted, càdlàg process with values in
[0,\bar{\lambda}_{i}]
.
Define the cumulative intensity

A^{i}_{t}\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{i}_{s}\,ds,

and set

N^{i}_{t}\;=\;\widetilde{N}^{i}_{A^{i}_{t}}.

Then
(N^{i}_{t})
is a Cox (doubly stochastic Poisson) process with
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-predictable intensity
\lambda^{i}_{t}
.

When a jump of
N^{i}
occurs at time
t
, agent
i
has a
verification/ disclosure opportunity
on her
outgoing links; at such a time she may decide whether to disclose any verifiable evidence currently in her possession.
(If
i
is an expert, the opportunity can also coincide with the realization of a new raw verifiable signal about
X_{t}
.)

We call an interval
[t,t+\Delta)
a local silence window for agent
i
if
\lambda^{i}_{s}=0
for all
s\in[t,t+\Delta)
and this choice is publicly announced at time
t
.
On such an interval, no potential signals arrive at
i
and the absence of messages from
i
carries no additional information beyond the announcement.

Local time for finite-variation processes.

We use the standard (global) semimartingale local time, normalized so that
L^{a}_{0}(\cdot)=0
.
When discussing accumulation on a subinterval
[u,t]
, the relevant object is the increment
L^{a}_{t}(\cdot)-L^{a}_{u}(\cdot)
.
We recall a standard fact about local times of continuous semimartingales.

Lemma 5
.

Let
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a real-valued, continuous semimartingale with finite variation on
[u,v]

for some
0\leq u<v<\infty
.
Then for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
the local time
L^{a}_{t}(Z)
of
Z
at level
a
satisfies

L^{a}_{t}(Z)\;=\;L^{a}_{u}(Z)\quad\text{for all }t\in[u,v].

Equivalently,
Z
accumulates no additional local time at level
a
over
[u,v]
:

L^{a}_{t}(Z)-L^{a}_{u}(Z)\;=\;0\quad\text{for all }t\in[u,v].

Proof.

Write the canonical decomposition
Z=Z_{0}+M+V
, where
M
is a continuous local martingale and

V
is a finite-variation process.
If
Z
has finite variation on
[u,v]
, then
M
also has finite variation on
[u,v]
.
A continuous local martingale of finite variation is constant, hence
M_{t}=M_{u}
for all
t\in[u,v]
,
so
\langle M\rangle_{t}=\langle M\rangle_{u}
on
[u,v]
.

By the occupation density characterization of local time for continuous semimartingales,
for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
and every
t\geq u
,

L^{a}_{t}(Z)-L^{a}_{u}(Z)\;=\;\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\;\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{u}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{|Z_{s}-a|<\varepsilon\}}\,d\langle M\rangle_{s}.

Since
\langle M\rangle
is constant on
[u,v]
, the integral is zero for all
t\in[u,v]
, which yields

L^{a}_{t}(Z)=L^{a}_{u}(Z)
on
[u,v]
.
∎

Belief dynamics under local silence.

Fix an agent
i
on a path
P
and consider the contribution of potential signals from
i
to the DM’s posterior mean
m_{t}
along
P
.
It is convenient to write

m_{t}\;=\;m^{(-i)}_{t}+m^{(i)}_{t},

where
m^{(-i)}_{t}
aggregates the effect of all information that does not run through
i
and
m^{(i)}_{t}
captures the part of the belief process that depends on potential signals and disclosures from
i
.

Formally, write the posterior mean as a semimartingale

m_{t}=m_{0}+B_{t}+M_{t},

where
B
is a finite-variation process and
M
is the
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-square-integrable
martingale (innovation) part. Define
M^{(i)}
as the orthogonal projection of
M
onto the
closed linear span of the compensated process
N^{i}-A^{i}
in the Hilbert space of square-integrable
martingales. We then let
m^{(i)}
denote the corresponding component of the filter, i.e. the
semimartingale whose martingale part is
M^{(i)}
(and set
m^{(-i)}:=m-m^{(i)}
).

On any interval on which
\lambda^{i}_{t}=0
, the cumulative intensity
A^{i}_{t}
is constant and
N^{i}_{t}
is constant.
The compensated martingale
N^{i}_{t}-A^{i}_{t}
is therefore constant, and the absence of arrivals from
i
on that interval carries no additional information beyond the announcement of the local-silence window.
The innovation process for the filter associated with
i
is identically zero on such an interval, so
m^{(i)}_{t}
evolves purely through its drift component and is of finite variation on bounded subintervals.

The next lemma is the local-silence result used in the proof of Theorem
1
.

Lemma 6

(Local-silence lemma)
.

Fix an agent
i
and a stopping time
\tau
.
Suppose that at time
\tau
the agent publicly announces a local-silence window of length
\Delta>0
and then sets
\lambda^{i}_{t}=0
for all
t\in[\tau,\tau+\Delta)
.
Conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
, on the interval
[\tau,\tau+\Delta)
:

(i)

the component
m^{(i)}_{t}
of the posterior mean that depends on potential signals from
i
is of finite variation;

(ii)

for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
, the local time of
m^{(i)}
at
a
does not increase on
[\tau,\tau+\Delta)
:

L^{a}_{t}\bigl(m^{(i)}\bigr)\;=\;L^{a}_{\tau}\bigl(m^{(i)}\bigr)\quad\text{for all }t\in[\tau,\tau+\Delta);

(iii)

any local time of the full posterior mean
m_{t}
on
[\tau,\tau+\Delta)
is generated by components that do not depend on
i
’s clock choice.

Proof.

On
[\tau,\tau+\Delta)
, both
A^{i}_{t}
and
N^{i}_{t}
are constant, so the compensated martingale
N^{i}_{t}-A^{i}_{t}
is constant.
As discussed above, the innovation process for the part of the filter driven by potential signals from
i
is therefore identically zero on this interval, and
m^{(i)}_{t}
evolves deterministically through its drift.
Hence
m^{(i)}_{t}
is a continuous process of finite variation on
[\tau,\tau+\Delta)
, which establishes (i).

Since
m^{(i)}_{t}
is continuous and of finite variation on
[\tau,\tau+\Delta)
,
Lemma
5
applied on that interval implies that its local time does not increase there:

L^{a}_{t}(m^{(i)})=L^{a}_{\tau}(m^{(i)})
for all
t\in[\tau,\tau+\Delta)
, which proves (ii).

Finally,
m_{t}=m^{(-i)}_{t}+m^{(i)}_{t}
, and the choice of
\lambda^{i}
only affects
m^{(i)}
.
Thus any local time accumulated by
m_{t}
on
[\tau,\tau+\Delta)
must come from the dynamics of
m^{(-i)}
, which are independent of
i
’s local-silence decision.
This yields (iii).
∎

Lemma
6
underpins the selection step in the proof of Theorem
1
.
By posting a short local-silence window around a candidate mixing threshold and using the fact that
m^{(i)}
accumulates no additional local time on that window, an agent can tilt the probability with which beliefs cross the threshold while keeping the rest of the strategy profile essentially unchanged.
This rules out knife-edge mixing and implies that equilibrium disclosure strategies can be taken to be pure threshold strategies.

B.4
Ladder representations and boundary problems

We finally summarize the boundary-value problems associated with ladder (threshold) policies for diffusions and record the monotone comparative statics used in Proposition
4
.

One-dimensional ladder policies.

Consider a one-dimensional diffusion
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on an open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
with generator

\mathcal{A}\psi(y)\;=\;\alpha(y)\psi^{\prime}(y)+\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma(y)^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(y),

and suppose that an agent can, at discrete times, move
Y_{t}
instantaneously within
I
at some cost, while receiving a flow payoff
g(Y_{t})
between interventions.
Under suitable regularity and monotonicity conditions, optimal policies have a ladder structure: there exists a finite sequence of thresholds

-\infty<\ell_{1}<r_{1}<\ell_{2}<r_{2}<\cdots<\ell_{K}<r_{K}<\infty

such that it is optimal to leave
Y_{t}
unchanged as long as it stays in the union of intervals
\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}(\ell_{k},r_{k})
and to intervene whenever
Y_{t}
hits one of the boundaries.

In the dynamic disclosure application,
Y_{t}
is the belief
m_{t}
, the intervention is disclosure at a signal arrival, and the continuation region for each agent
i
is characterized by a finite family of threshold functions
\alpha^{i}_{\ell}(R^{i})
as in Theorem
1
.
The relevant structure is summarized in the next lemma.

Lemma 7

(Ladder structure and smooth pasting)
.

Let
V\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
be the value function of a one-dimensional impulse-control problem with diffusion generator
\mathcal{A}
, flow payoff
g
, discount rate
\rho>0
, and intervention operator
\mathcal{M}
satisfying the regularity assumptions of Lemma
4
.
Suppose further that:

(i)

g
is strictly concave in the induced action and continuously differentiable in
y
;

(ii)

for each
y
, the post-intervention state
\Gamma(y,\zeta)
and instantaneous payoff
K(y,\zeta)
are continuous in
\zeta
, and the intervention set is compact;

(iii)

the operator
\mathcal{M}
is monotone in the sense that if
y_{1}<y_{2}
and
V(y_{1})\leq V(y_{2})
then
\mathcal{M}V(y_{1})\leq\mathcal{M}V(y_{2})
.

Then there exists a finite collection of open intervals
(\ell_{k},r_{k})\subset I
such that:

(a)

the continuation region is
C=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}(\ell_{k},r_{k})
;

(a)

on each interval
(\ell_{k},r_{k})
,
V
is
C^{2}
and solves the linear ODE

\rho V(y)-g(y)-\mathcal{A}V(y)=0;

(a)

at each boundary point
b\in\{\ell_{1},r_{1},\dots,\ell_{K},r_{K}\}
,
V
satisfies the value-matching condition

V(b)=\mathcal{M}V(b)

and the smooth-pasting condition

V^{\prime}(b^{-})=V^{\prime}(b^{+}),

whenever both one-sided derivatives are defined.

Conversely, any continuous function
V
that is
C^{2}
on
C
, solves the ODE on
C
, and satisfies the value-matching and smooth-pasting conditions at the boundaries yields, via Lemma
4
, an optimal ladder policy.

Proof.

Under the stated conditions, the continuation region is open and can be written as a countable union of disjoint open intervals.
Compactness of the intervention set and concavity of
g
imply that the value function is strictly concave and that the continuation region is bounded in each direction in which
g
is bounded above.
Standard arguments for one-dimensional impulse-control problems (such as those used to derive
(S,s)
policies in real-options models) then imply that only finitely many continuation intervals arise and that on each such interval
V
solves the linear ODE with generator
\mathcal{A}
and discount
\rho
.
Value matching and smooth pasting follow from optimality of intervention at the boundaries and from the ODE inside the continuation region.
The converse statement follows by applying Lemma
4
to the candidate
V
and continuation region
C
.
∎

Monotone comparative statics of thresholds.

For the comparative statics in Proposition
4
, we rely on the monotonicity of the continuation region with respect to parameters that enter the flow payoff or the generator in a single-crossing fashion.

Lemma 8

(Comparative statics of ladder thresholds)
.

Consider a family of one-dimensional impulse-control problems indexed by a parameter
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, with generators
\mathcal{A}_{\eta}
, flow payoffs
g(\cdot;\eta)
, discount rate
\rho>0
, and an intervention operator
\mathcal{M}
that does not depend on
\eta
.
Suppose that:

(i)

for each
\eta
, the problem admits a unique value function
V(\cdot;\eta)
and corresponding continuation region
C(\eta)
of the form in Lemma
7
;

(ii)

the map
\eta\mapsto g(y;\eta)
is increasing for each
y
and satisfies a single-crossing condition in
(y,\eta)
;

(iii)

the generators
\mathcal{A}_{\eta}
are ordered in convex order: if
\eta^{\prime}<\eta^{\prime\prime}

and
\varphi
is convex, then

(\mathcal{A}_{\eta^{\prime}}\varphi)(y)\leq(\mathcal{A}_{\eta^{\prime\prime}}\varphi)(y)\quad\text{for all }y\in I.

Then the continuation regions are monotone in
\eta
in the sense that a higher
\eta
makes continuation (waiting) weakly more attractive.
In particular, if
\eta^{\prime}<\eta^{\prime\prime}
and the continuation region is an interval
(\ell(\eta),r(\eta))
for each
\eta
, then

\ell(\eta^{\prime\prime})\;\leq\;\ell(\eta^{\prime}),\qquad r(\eta^{\prime\prime})\;\geq\;r(\eta^{\prime}).

Proof.

Fix
\eta^{\prime}<\eta^{\prime\prime}
and let
V^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime\prime}
denote the corresponding value functions.
The single-crossing property and the comparison principle imply that, for any admissible control policy, the expected discounted payoff under parameter
\eta^{\prime\prime}
dominates that under parameter
\eta^{\prime}
whenever the state is larger in the natural order.
A coupling argument for the controlled diffusions, together with uniqueness of value functions and continuation regions, then implies that the set of states at which it is optimal to continue rather than intervene expands as
\eta
increases.
When the continuation region is an interval, this is equivalent to the stated monotonicity of the thresholds.
∎

How Lemma
8
is used in the paper.

Lemma
8
is a comparative-statics tool for primitives that raise the value of
waiting
(continuation) through the flow payoff and/or the diffusion dynamics.
In Proposition
4
, it is invoked for the volatility comparative static in part (ii), which is the standard real-options effect that higher fundamental uncertainty expands inaction regions.

By contrast, primitives such as reputational intensity
\beta_{i}
primarily affect the
relative gain from disclosure
because disclosure and silence induce different reputational paths and jumps.
That comparative static is therefore established separately using the value-matching condition
V=\mathcal{M}V
and the fact that the incremental reputational benefit of disclosure is increasing in
\beta_{i}
; see the proof of Proposition
4
(i).

Assumption
2
ensures the ordering and regularity conditions needed
to apply Lemma
8
to the volatility comparative static in
Proposition
4
(ii). The comparative statics in
Proposition
4
(i) and (iii) are proved directly from
value-matching and the single-crossing structure of the disclosure versus silence payoff tradeoff.

Additional Microfoundations and Extensions

This last appendix has three goals.
First, it provides a microfoundation for the reputational state used in Section
2
.
Second, it states formally the Markov perfect Bayesian equilibrium (MPBE) notion used in the dynamic analysis.
Third, it sketches several extensions of the baseline framework and comments on how the main results adapt.
Throughout we keep the notation and standing assumptions of the main text.

C.1
Microfoundations for the reputational state

The reduced-form reputational state
(R^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in Section
2
is intended to capture the continuation value of an intermediary’s “name” or track record, in the spirit of dynamic reputation and career-concerns models.
This subsection sketches a simple microfoundation that rationalizes the flow payoff in (
2
) and Assumption
1
in terms of Bayesian learning about an underlying type and the induced value of future opportunities.

Underlying type and forecasting ability.

For each intermediary
i\in N\setminus\{0\}
there is an unobserved, time-invariant type

\theta_{i}\in\{\theta_{i}^{H},\theta_{i}^{L}\},

interpreted as “high” or “low” forecasting or monitoring ability.
The prior probability that
i
is high type is
\pi^{i}_{0}\in(0,1)
.

Conditional on
\theta_{i}
, whenever
i
receives a verifiable signal about
X_{t}
and chooses to disclose it, the probability that the disclosed message is ex post correct (in the sense of matching the realized fundamental once it becomes publicly verifiable) is

q_{i}(\theta_{i}^{H})\;>\;q_{i}(\theta_{i}^{L}).

Signals and correctness events across agents and over time are independent conditional on
(\theta_{j})_{j\in N\setminus\{0\}}
and on the fundamental
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
The detailed signal structure conditional on
(X_{t},\theta_{i})
can be specified in the usual way; what matters for reputation is the likelihood ratio of correct versus incorrect disclosures under the two types.

Remark (transparency types and the fully revealing benchmark).

The binary-type illustration above is phrased in terms of
ex post accuracy
of disclosed
messages, which is natural when disclosed signals are noisy but verifiable. In the benchmark in the
main text we often take disclosed hard evidence to be fully revealing, so disclosed messages are
mechanically correct. In that case reputational discipline operates primarily through

transparency and timeliness
: because disclosure clocks are publicly observed, silence while a
channel is active is informative about selective withholding.

Formally, the same two-type Bayesian-learning construction applies if we reinterpret
\theta_{i}
as a

transparency
(disclosure-propensity) type rather than an accuracy type. For example, conditional
on
\theta_{i}
, whenever agent
i
is in possession of verifiable evidence and a disclosure opportunity
arrives while her channel is active, she discloses with probability
p_{i}(\theta_{i})
with

p_{i}(\theta_{i}^{H})>p_{i}(\theta_{i}^{L})
(equivalently, a higher disclosure hazard under
\theta_{i}^{H}
). Then
observed disclosure timing and on-clock silence update the public posterior
\pi_{t}^{i}
by Bayes’ rule,
and any increasing transform of
\pi_{t}^{i}
can be used as the reputational state
R_{t}^{i}
exactly as
below.

Posterior beliefs and reputational capital.

Fix any public history
h_{t}
up to time
t
, consisting of disclosed messages (who spoke, to whom, and what was said), realized fundamentals that have become verifiable ex post, clock paths
(\lambda^{j}_{s})_{0\leq s\leq t}
for all
j
, and past DM actions.
Given
h_{t}
, the public posterior belief about
\theta_{i}
is

\pi^{i}_{t}(h_{t})\;\equiv\;\mathbb{P}(\theta_{i}=\theta_{i}^{H}\mid h_{t}),

which evolves as new disclosures by
i
are eventually checked against realized fundamentals and as silence is interpreted through the equilibrium strategy of
i
.

Suppose that, in addition to the ongoing game in Sections
2
-
5
, intermediary
i
anticipates a stream of future projects on which her type matters.
To fix ideas, assume:

(a)

Projects arrive according to a Poisson process of intensity
\kappa_{i}>0
, independent of all other shocks.

(b)

On each project, using
i
rather than a generic outside option generates monetary surplus

\Phi_{i}(\theta_{i})\;=\;\begin{cases}\phi_{i}^{H}&\text{if }\theta_{i}=\theta_{i}^{H},\\[1.99997pt]
\phi_{i}^{L}&\text{if }\theta_{i}=\theta_{i}^{L},\end{cases}

with
\phi_{i}^{H}>\phi_{i}^{L}\geq 0
.

(c)

Competition among principals and intermediaries implies that the expected present value of
i
’s future fee income from these projects at time
t
is linear in the public belief
\pi^{i}_{t}
:

\text{PV}^{i}_{t}\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\Biggl[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\rho(s-t)}\kappa_{i}\,\Phi_{i}(\theta_{i})\,ds\,\Bigm|\,h_{t}\Biggr]\;=\;\frac{\kappa_{i}}{\rho}\Bigl(\phi_{i}^{L}+(\phi_{i}^{H}-\phi_{i}^{L})\,\pi^{i}_{t}\Bigr).

Define the reputational state as the affine transform

R^{i}_{t}\;\equiv\;\phi_{i}^{L}+(\phi_{i}^{H}-\phi_{i}^{L})\,\pi^{i}_{t}.

Then
R^{i}_{t}
can be interpreted directly as the expected per-unit-time surplus from future projects, and

\text{PV}^{i}_{t}\;=\;\frac{\kappa_{i}}{\rho}\,R^{i}_{t}.

Up to a positive multiplicative and monotone transformation, any strictly increasing function of
\pi^{i}_{t}
would serve the same purpose.

Flow utility and reduced form.

Rescaling units so that reputational payoffs are measured directly in units of
R^{i}_{t}
, the flow payoff in (
2
) can be written as

u_{i}(y_{t},X_{t},R^{i}_{t})\;=\;-\alpha_{i}\bigl(y_{t}-X_{t}-b_{i}\bigr)^{2}\;+\;\beta_{i}\,g_{i}(R^{i}_{t}),

where
g_{i}
is continuous and strictly increasing.
A natural benchmark is
g_{i}(R)=R
, so that (under the normalization
R^{i}_{t}=\phi_{i}^{L}+(\phi_{i}^{H}-\phi_{i}^{L})\pi^{i}_{t}
above)
R^{i}_{t}
is exactly the expected
per-unit-time
surplus from future projects and
\text{PV}^{i}_{t}=(\kappa_{i}/\rho)\,R^{i}_{t}
.

Importantly, the project-value calculation pins down this linear benchmark once the reputational state is fixed.
Allowing a nonlinear
g_{i}
should therefore be read as an additional reduced-form preference/curvature layer (e.g. diminishing returns to reputation, risk attitudes, or other non-pecuniary motives), rather than as a literal implication of the project-value derivation itself.
Equivalently, since the reputational state is only identified up to monotone transformations of the underlying posterior
\pi^{i}_{t}
, one could absorb such curvature into the normalization of
R^{i}_{t}
; we keep
R^{i}_{t}
tied to a convenient posterior-based index and allow curvature through
g_{i}
.

Dynamics of the reputational state.

The posterior process
(\pi^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the standard Bayesian filter for a binary type observed
through performance and/or disclosure behavior.
17
17

17

In the accuracy interpretation above,
the data are correctness events; in the transparency interpretation discussed in the remark,
the data are disclosure timing and on-clock silence conditional on the publicly observed clock.

Between times at which
i
’s past disclosures are verified against realized fundamentals,
\pi^{i}_{t}

is constant; at verification times it jumps upward when
i
’s advice proves correct and downward when
it proves wrong.
In the transparency version,
\pi^{i}_{t}
can also drift during active-clock
intervals because the likelihood of observing no disclosure depends on type.

If
i
sometimes discloses noisy but partially revealing signals whose implications for
X_{t}
are assessed only with delay, then
\pi^{i}_{t}
can also move continuously between verification dates.

At a coarser time scale, the pathwise evolution of
R^{i}_{t}
can be approximated by a one-dimensional semimartingale with bounded jumps at verification times and diffusive fluctuations driven by noisy performance signals. In particular:

•

the pair
(m_{t},R^{i}_{t})
is Markov under any strategy profile that is Markov in the underlying state, because
m_{t}
summarizes beliefs about
X_{t}
and
R^{i}_{t}
summarizes beliefs about
\theta_{i}
;

•

between verification times,
R^{i}_{t}
can be approximated by a diffusion with drift and volatility that depend only on
(m_{t},R^{i}_{t})
;

•

at verification times,
R^{i}_{t}
jumps upward or downward depending on whether
i
’s past messages are confirmed or refuted, and the jump is increasing in the surprise relative to prior beliefs.

This is precisely the structure postulated in Assumption
1
: along any fixed path, the joint process
(X_{t},m_{t},R_{t})
is Markov, each
R^{i}_{t}
behaves as a one-dimensional diffusion between disclosure and verification events, and jumps in
R^{i}_{t}
are monotone in ex post correctness.

Continuous-time approximation.

To connect this microfoundation with the diffusion-plus-jumps representation used in Section
4
and Appendix
B
, one can consider a high-frequency limit in which correctness information arrives in many small pieces.
As the intensity of these signals grows and their individual informativeness shrinks, functional central limit theorems for nonlinear filters imply that the posterior process
\pi^{i}_{t}
converges weakly to a diffusion with drift and volatility that are continuous functions of
\pi^{i}_{t}
(and of
m_{t}
when type and state interact).
Mapping
\pi^{i}_{t}
into
R^{i}_{t}
through a smooth increasing function preserves this structure, yielding the diffusive dynamics and monotone jumps used in the main text.

In this sense the reduced-form process
(R^{i}_{t})
and its contribution
g_{i}(R^{i}_{t})
to flow utility can be viewed as a continuous-time approximation to a fully microfounded Bayesian learning and career-concerns model of reputation.

C.2
Formal definition of dynamic equilibrium

We now formalize the dynamic equilibrium concept used in Sections
4
and
5
.
We first describe the continuous-time game with clocks, disclosure decisions, and actions, and then specialize to Markov perfect Bayesian equilibrium in the reduced-form state variables.

Histories and information sets.

Fix a network
G=(N,L)
and primitives as in Sections
2
-
5
.
Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be the filtered probability space supporting the fundamental diffusion
(X_{t})
, the Poisson processes
(\widetilde{N}^{i}_{t})_{i\in N}
that generate potential signals, and any auxiliary randomization devices.

•

The
public filtration

(\mathcal{F}^{p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is generated by disclosed messages (senders, recipients, contents), all clock paths
(\lambda^{j}_{s})_{0\leq s\leq t}
, and the DM’s past actions
(y_{s})_{0\leq s\leq t}
.

•

For each intermediary
i\in N\setminus\{0\}
, the
private filtration

(\mathcal{F}^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is generated by
\mathcal{F}^{p}_{t}
together with
i
’s private signals and knowledge of which potential signal arrivals at herself or her in-neighbours have been suppressed rather than disclosed.

A public history at time
t
is a realization of
\mathcal{F}^{p}_{t}
; a private history for
i
at time
t
is a realization of
\mathcal{F}^{i}_{t}
.
Information sets in the extensive-form sense are equivalence classes of histories that are indistinguishable to the agent given her filtration.

Strategies.

We describe pure strategies; mixed and behavioural strategies are obtained by allowing for measurable randomization.

•

A
clock strategy
for agent
i\in N
is an
(\mathcal{F}^{i}_{t})
-progressively measurable process
(\lambda^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with values in
[0,\bar{\lambda}_{i}]
, where
\bar{\lambda}_{i}<\infty
is the technological upper bound.
In the LQG benchmark used in Section
4
and Assumption
2
, we further restrict
\lambda^{i}_{t}\in\{0,\bar{\lambda}_{i}\}
.

•

A disclosure strategy for agent
i
specifies, at each time
t
at which
i
has a disclosure opportunity
(i.e.,
N^{i}
jumps), which subset of out-neighbors receives each verifiable signal currently in
i
’s possession
(and, if
i
is an expert, any newly realized raw signal at time
t
).
Formally, if
\tau
is such a stopping time, a disclosure strategy is an
\mathcal{F}^{i}_{\tau}
-measurable mapping

D^{i}_{\tau}:\Omega\to 2^{\mathcal{N}^{+}_{i}}

that assigns to each state of the world
\omega
the set
D^{i}_{\tau}(\omega)\subseteq\mathcal{N}^{+}_{i}
of out-neighbours to whom the verifiable signal is transmitted at
\tau
.

•

A
recommendation strategy
for intermediary
i
(when relevant) assigns to each private history a recommendation about the DM’s action.
In the main text we subsume recommendations into disclosure, because all payoff-relevant information is verifiable.

•

A
strategy for the decision maker
is an
(\mathcal{F}^{p}_{t})
-progressively measurable action process
(y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
for each
t
.
In the quadratic environment of Section
2
, the DM’s unique best response is
y_{t}=m_{t}
, the posterior mean of
X_{t}
given
\mathcal{F}^{p}_{t}
.

A
strategy profile

\sigma
collects a clock and disclosure strategy for each intermediary and an action strategy for the DM.

Beliefs.

Beliefs specify, for each agent and each history, conditional distributions over the payoff-relevant uncertainty (the path of
X
, types, and signal processes) consistent with that history.

•

Public beliefs at time
t
are summarized by the posterior over
X_{t}
, typically via the posterior mean
m_{t}=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{p}_{t}]
(and the posterior variance
v_{t}
when needed), and by the reputational state
R_{t}=(R^{i}_{t})_{i\in N\setminus\{0\}}
.

•

Private beliefs for agent
i
refine public beliefs using the additional information in
\mathcal{F}^{i}_{t}
(for instance, suppressed signals).

A belief system
\mu
specifies the law of
(X_{t},R_{t})
and of the signal processes conditional on each public and private history.
Given strategies
\sigma
, we require that
\mu
be
consistent
: on the equilibrium path it is generated by Bayes’ rule and the diffusion-plus-jumps structure of
(X_{t},R_{t})
; off the equilibrium path it is given by a measurable selection that respects the support of strategies.

Perfect Bayesian equilibrium in continuous time.

A
perfect Bayesian equilibrium
(PBE) of the dynamic disclosure game is a pair
(\sigma,\mu)
consisting of a strategy profile and a belief system such that:

(i)

Sequential rationality.
For each agent
i
and for
\mu
-almost every private history
h^{i}_{t}
, the continuation of
i
’s strategy at
h^{i}_{t}
maximizes her expected discounted payoff (
3
) given
\mu
and the continuation strategies of the other agents.

(ii)

Belief consistency.
Given
\sigma
, the belief system
\mu
is obtained from the prior and the laws of
(X_{t})
and the signal processes by Bayes’ rule wherever well defined, and by a measurable extension elsewhere.
Along the equilibrium path, public beliefs coincide with the processes
(m_{t},v_{t},R_{t})
constructed in Section
4
.

This definition parallels the standard discrete-time PBE notion, but is expressed in terms of progressively measurable strategies and filtrations.

Markov perfect Bayesian equilibrium.

We call a PBE
Markov
if there exists a finite-dimensional state process
S_{t}
such that:

•

S_{t}
is adapted to
\mathcal{F}^{p}_{t}
and Markov under
(\sigma,\mu)
;

•

public strategies at time
t
depend on histories only through the current value of
S_{t}
(and, for disclosure decisions, through current signal realizations);

•

the belief system
\mu
is a measurable function of
S_{t}
(and of types), so that
(X_{t},S_{t})
is Markov.

In the main text we take
S_{t}=(m_{t},R_{t})
.
Assumption
1
and the microfoundation above guarantee that
(X_{t},m_{t},R_{t})
is Markov.
A
Markov perfect Bayesian equilibrium
(MPBE) is a PBE in which all agents’ strategies are Markov in
(m_{t},R_{t})
(and realized signals), and beliefs are the associated Markovian posteriors.
All equilibrium statements in Sections
4
and
5
are understood in this sense.

C.3
Further extensions

We briefly outline several extensions of the baseline framework and indicate how the main results adapt or what additional phenomena may arise.

Multi-dimensional fundamentals.

Instead of a scalar fundamental
(X_{t})
, consider a
d
-dimensional diffusion
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

dX_{t}\;=\;\mu(X_{t})\,dt+\Sigma(X_{t})\,dB_{t},

with
B_{t}
a
k
-dimensional Brownian motion.
The DM chooses a vector action
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with quadratic loss around
X_{t}
, and intermediaries have vector biases
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Verifiable signals may reveal some or all components of
X_{t}
.
Under Gaussian priors and linear signals, the posterior
(m_{t},v_{t})
remains finite dimensional and Markov.
The pathwise analysis and ladder representation in Theorem
1
extend if the relevant intervention decision can be expressed in terms of a scalar projection of beliefs (for example, a direction
\ell^{\top}m_{t}
that is most relevant for payoffs).
In genuinely multi-dimensional decision problems, the optimal intervention region becomes a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with more complex geometry; the QVI characterization in Appendix
B
still applies, but the ladder becomes a collection of threshold surfaces rather than scalar thresholds.
Comparative statics in the spirit of Proposition
4
can be recovered under suitable multi-dimensional single-crossing conditions.

Richer signal structures.

The analysis in Sections
4
and
5
focuses on fully revealing verifiable signals that, once disclosed, reveal
X_{t}
exactly.
Two generalizations are natural.

•

Noisy but verifiable signals.

Suppose each disclosed signal is an unbiased but noisy observation of
X_{t}
.
In the LQG benchmark, beliefs then update via linear filtering and
(m_{t},v_{t})
evolves as a diffusion with non-zero volatility even immediately after disclosure.
The ladder and QVI representation are unchanged in form, with modified drift and diffusion coefficients in the generator of
(m_{t},R_{t})
.

•

Partially verifiable or graded signals.

In some applications, an intermediary can commit to a coarse but verifiable summary of her private information (for instance, a rating on a finite scale).
As long as the set of possible disclosures is finite and each disclosed message has a well-defined likelihood ratio under different states, the dynamic problem remains an impulse-control problem with a finite menu of interventions.
The QVI and verification arguments in Appendix
B
apply with an intervention operator
\mathcal{M}
that optimizes over discrete signals.

In both cases, the main qualitative conclusions of Section
4
remain: disclosure rules are threshold-like, waiting has option value, and observable clocks play the same selection role in ruling out mixed strategies.

Multiple decision makers and heterogeneous audiences.

The baseline model has a single DM.
In many settings, intermediaries face several downstream audiences or decision makers (for example, retail and institutional investors, multiple regulators, or distinct user groups).
Let
D\subset N
denote a set of decision makers, each with their own actions
y^{d}_{t}
and preferences over
X_{t}
.

Under verifiable disclosure, a message sent to one audience is often observable, directly or indirectly, by others.
A simple extension assumes that all DMs share the same public filtration but weight intermediaries’ reputations differently in their payoffs.
In that case a common ladder structure still emerges along each expert-DM path, but the payoff from disclosure for an intermediary becomes a weighted combination of reputational effects across audiences.
The QVI representation and Theorem
1
extend with modified flow payoffs.

A more demanding extension allows for partially segmented audiences: some disclosures are targeted and not fuly observed elsewhere.
Public belief then becomes vector valued (one belief process per audience), and clocks and disclosure decisions can differ across audiences.
Intermediaries face a multi-dimensional impulse-control problem that trades off reputational gains in different markets.
The equilibrium structure remains option-theoretic, but the ladders become multi-dimensional objects and can exhibit subsitution across audiences.

Alternative payoff functions and learning objectives.

Quadratic losses and linear reputational payoffs in Section
2
are chosen for tractability and for the connection with the real-options toolkit.
The core machinery extends to broader payoff classes:

•

Let the DM’s material payoff be
u_{0}(y,X_{t})=-\ell_{0}(y,X_{t})
where, for each
x
,
\ell_{0}(\cdot,x)
is strictly convex and twice continuously differentiable.
Then for any public posterior
\pi_{t}
over
X_{t}
the DM has a unique best reply

y_{t}\;=\;y^{\ast}(\pi_{t})\;\in\;\arg\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}\!\bigl[\ell_{0}(y,X_{t})\bigr].

In general
y^{\ast}(\pi_{t})
depends on the
entire
posterior
\pi_{t}
, not only on its mean.
Accordingly, the one-dimensional ladder representation used in Sections
4
-
5

extends to such losses only under an additional
scalar sufficiency
condition:
there exists a measurable map
T
from posteriors into
\mathbb{R}
and a strictly increasing function
\bar{y}

such that, for every posterior
\pi
that arises on the equilibrium path,

y^{\ast}(\pi)\;=\;\bar{y}\!\bigl(T(\pi)\bigr).

(Quadratic loss is the benchmark case with
T(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[X]
.)

If, moreover, intermediaries’ material incentives satisfy a one-dimensional single-crossing property
in the induced action (equivalently, in the scalar index
T(\pi_{t})
), then each intermediary’s dynamic problem
remains an impulse-control problem in the
two-dimensional
state
(T(\pi_{t}),R^{i}_{t})
, and the equilibrium
admits a ladder representation in
T(\pi_{t})
(thresholds in that scalar belief index).

Absent such scalar sufficiency, for example, if the DM’s best reply depends on multiple posterior moments
(e.g.
(m_{t},v_{t})
) or on the full posterior, the payoff-relevant public belief state becomes higher-dimensional
(or infinite-dimensional). The impulse-control/QVI characterization continues to apply in that enlarged state
space (Appendix
B.2
), but the “ladder” is replaced by a higher-dimensional continuation
region with threshold
surfaces
rather than scalar cutoffs.

•

If reputational concerns depend nonlinearly on
R^{i}_{t}
(for example, if
g_{i}
is strictly concave or strictly convex), then the comparative statics in Proposition
4
adapt: stronger reputational intensity
\beta_{i}
still tends to shrink inaction regions under mild regularity, but the exact movement of thresholds depends on the curvature of
g_{i}
.

•

One can also let
R^{i}_{t}
reflect not only forecast accuracy but also other dimensions such as timeliness of disclosure or robustness of methods, by taking the underlying type
\theta_{i}
to be multi-dimensional and aggregating the corresponding posteriors into a one-dimensional reputational index.
The Markov property and diffusion approximation then follow by the same logic as in Section
C.1
.

The extensions above illustrate that the core ingredients of the analysis in Sections
3
-
5
are robust.
A Markov belief and reputational state, verifiable (possibly noisy) signals modulated by observable disclosure clocks, and an impulse-control representation of disclosure incentives together generate ladder-type equilibria and dynamic restoration of unraveling in a broad class of environments.

Multi-dimensional fundamentals, multiple audiences, and alternative payoff specifications can all enlarge the
payoff-relevant public state (e.g. from a scalar belief index to a vector of posterior statistics, or to the full posterior).
In those cases the equilibrium remains option-theoretic and is characterized by continuation/intervention regions,
but one-dimensional ladders are replaced by higher-dimensional threshold surfaces.

A more complete treatment of these extensions is envisaged in future work.
