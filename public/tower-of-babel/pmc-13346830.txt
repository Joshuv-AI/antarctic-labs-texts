Highly flexoelectric response for uranium removal and recovery using the organic-inorganic hybrid material
Authors: Cheng Meng, Di Fu, Chongxiu Li, Zaiqi Cheng, Zhibin Zhang, Xuanting Wei, Bowei Chen, Baowei Hu, Yunhai Liu, Zhenli Sun, Xiangke Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Catalysis was an effective method for uranium recovery and environmental remediation. However, the weak flexoelectric response, despite being universal in dielectric materials, greatly limited its appeal for research and catalytic applications. Here, we proposed a strategy to enhance the flexoelectric response by bridging inorganic chains with metal-organic chains within the structure. The resulting hybrid material, Co[C4H4N2]V2O6, demonstrated excellent uranyl removal performance, surpassing that of state-of-the-art piezocatalysts. Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 showed flexocatalytic uranyl activity across a broad pH range and under high-salinity conditions. Under a dynamic experimental setup, Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 showed strong potential for practical flexocatalytic applications. Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 could efficiently separate uranyl in contaminated potable water, reducing the uranium concentration (~2.0 ppm) to below the drinking water standard (30 ppb). It could also lower the uranium concentration (~5.6 ppm) in mining wastewater to below the discharge limit (300 ppb). Its intrinsic anisotropic mechanical properties and cantilever-like morphology endowed high deformability, which, together with a large dielectric constant, enhanced its flexoelectric polarization. The revealed flexocatalytic mechanism confirmed that uranyl was converted into insoluble (UO2)O2·2H2O by active species generated through dynamic polarization. This work provided a promising avenue for the design of advanced flexocatalysts and offered an effective strategy for uranium recovery and environmental remediation.

Introduction

The piezoelectric effect attracted growing interest for its potential in mechanical energy conversion and process chemistry1. The conversion of mechanical energy into chemical energy through piezoelectric materials could drive catalytic reactions, such as water splitting for hydrogen generation and water purification2. Piezocatalysts were expected to harness not only environmental mechanical vibrations (such as wind and waves), but also redundant vibrations generated in industrial systems to achieve catalytic activity3. When subjected to external mechanical stress, piezoelectric materials induced polarization, which in turn drove charge carrier transfer between catalysts and reactants and subsequently modulated the redox reactions4,5. Low carrier concentration, slow migration rates, and insufficient active sites were recognized as major drawbacks in the field of piezocatalysis, significantly restricting the overall efficiency of the catalytic reactions6. Several approaches, such as increasing the dielectric constant7, controlling the morphology8, and introducing defect engineering9, were employed to enhance the piezocatalytic efficiency of materials. However, these strategies for the large-scale preparation of piezoelectric materials were relatively complicated and difficult to implement, as they often required precise synthetic conditions and costly processes6. Meanwhile, the piezoelectric effect existed only in materials with the non-centrosymmetric structure, limiting the choice of suitable piezocatalysts and their practical applications10.

Flexoelectric polarization was able to occur in all dielectric materials due to the fourth-order tensor nature of the flexoelectric coefficient11–13. This polarization, unlike piezoelectric polarization that was confined to only one or two atomic layers, appeared on the material surface and was distributed throughout the bulk over larger length scales due to significant local strain gradients14. These characteristics significantly broadened the choice of materials available for flexocatalysis. Centrosymmetric MnO2 nanoflowers and SnO2 nanorod clusters were shown to efficiently degrade organic dyes through flexocatalysis, while the flexoelectric effect in centrosymmetric SrTiO3 nanopowder was confirmed to enable hydrogen evolution reactions3,15,16. Existing studies demonstrated that surface charges generated from flexoelectric polarization, such as electrons (e-) and holes (h+), could participate in reactions with H2O and dissolved O2 to produce active oxygen species, including •O2- and •OH17. These active species promoted reactions for water splitting and organic degradation18,19. Meanwhile, reactive species (such as e-, •O2-, and •OH) generated from photocatalytic and piezocatalytic processes had been confirmed to be capable of altering the chemical speciation of soluble UO22+, leading to its transformation into insoluble forms20–22. Therefore, we speculated that flexocatalysis could similarly drive the transformation of UO22+. In particular, the conversion of UO22+ into insoluble species was recognized as an efficient method for uranium recovery and the remediation of radioactive contaminants in the nuclear industry23,24. Nevertheless, approaches for preparing high-performance flexoelectric materials for catalyzing UO22+remained largely unexplored, along with a clear understanding of the catalytic mechanisms.

Flexoelectricity was an electromechanical property that represented the coupling between inhomogeneous strain and electric polarization12,25. The strain gradient could locally break inversion symmetry, inducing polarization with a preferred direction and thereby enabling a piezoelectric-like response in centrosymmetric materials despite the absence of intrinsic piezoelectric components26,27. Generally, the charge density generated by flexoelectric polarization was relatively small, greatly reducing its appeal for research and application in the catalytic field26. Experiments confirmed that the flexoelectric coefficient was positively correlated with the dielectric constant, with higher dielectric constants corresponding to larger flexoelectric coefficients29. Importantly, enhancing the dielectric constant could reduce the exciton binding energy, thereby facilitating the separation of electrons and holes30. Meanwhile, under external mechanical stress, soft dielectric materials generally produced greater inhomogeneous strain, leading to an enhanced flexoelectric response31–33. Therefore, designing materials that combine high dielectric constants with excellent flexibility could pave the way for the rational construction of high-performance flexocatalysts. Inorganic materials generally exhibited high dielectric constants and hardness, whereas organic materials exhibited excellent mechanical flexibility but low dielectric constants. Organic inorganic hybrid materials could integrate the high dielectric constants of inorganic components with the mechanical flexibility of organic moieties34. Therefore, we speculated that bridging inorganic chains with metal-organic chains to fabricate hybrids could achieve an excellent flexoelectric response and high flexocatalytic performance. However, this strategy had not yet been reported and remained to be developed.

Herein, we introduced a strategy to fabricate materials with a high flexoelectric response by bridging inorganic chains with metal-organic chains within the material structure. This approach established the hybrid material Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 as an excellent flexocatalyst for uranyl removal. Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 possessed a bouquet-like morphology assembled from microsheets. The bridging mode endowed these cantilever-like microsheets with facile deformability and a high dielectric constant. These features collectively contributed to the excellent flexocatalytic performance under external mechanical stimuli. Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 exhibited catalytic activity across a broad pH range (1.50-12.00) and in high-salinity environments (1.5 M NaCl, 1.5 M CaCl2, 1.5 M MgCl2, and 1.5 M NaHCO3). Meanwhile, Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 could efficiently catalyze the conversion of uranyl even under low ultrasonic power of 20 W. Importantly, we evaluated the flexocatalytic ability in various water matrices using a dynamic experimental setup. Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 showed outstanding uranyl separation ability in contaminated potable water, contaminated seawater, and uranium mining wastewater. In particular, it could effectively reduce the U levels in contaminated potable water from ~2 ppm to below the acceptable limit (30 ppb) and in mining wastewater from ~5.6 ppm to under the discharge threshold (300 ppb). The revealed flexocatalytic mechanism confirmed that the active radicals (e.g., e-, •O2-, •OH, and H2O2) generated from dynamic polarization could participate in reactions with U(VI), leading to the formation of the insoluble form (UO2)O2·2H2O.

Results

Flexoelectric response capability of the centrosymmetric Co[C4H4N2]V2O6

Guided by our concept, we successfully synthesized the hybrid material Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 by bridging metal-organic chains with vanadium oxide chains via a one-step hydrothermal method (Fig. 1a). The structure of Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 was analyzed by Rietveld refinement of the powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) pattern. Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 crystallized in the centrosymmetric space group Pnna and exhibited high phase purity and crystallinity (Fig. 1b). In the structure, each metal-organic [Co(C4H4N2)]n2n+ chain was cross-linked with three metavanadate [V2O6]n2n- chains via {-V-O-Co-O-V-} linkages, forming a charge-balanced three-dimensional hybrid framework. Morphological analyses revealed that Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 featured a bouquet-like microstructure assembled from microsheets (Fig. 1c, d). These microsheets exhibited preferential growth along the [002] crystallographic direction (Fig. 1d). The (002) interplanar spacing was measured to be 0.396 nm, in good agreement with the refinement result. Meanwhile, the deformability was closely correlated with the elastic constants, with smaller values typically leading to larger deformations35. The theoretical elastic constants of Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 were calculated and found to be non-negative values (Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, all mechanical stability criteria, including [C11 + C22 + C33 + 2(C12 + C13 + C23)] > 0, (C11 + C22 − 2C12) > 0, (C11 + C33 − 2C13) > 0, and (C22 + C33 − 2C23) > 0, were satisfied. These results indicated that Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 exhibited sufficient mechanical stability36, making it suitable for flexocatalytic applications. Analysis of the Young’s and shear moduli revealed that the moduli along the [001] direction (Ez = 40.57 GPa, Gz = 18.59 GPa) were lower than those along the [100] and [010] directions (Ex = 73.20 GPa, Ey = 99.16 GPa, Gx = 23.15 GPa, Gy = 45.01 GPa), indicating preferential deformability along the [001] direction (Fig. 2a–c, Supplementary Table 2). Meanwhile, the Poisson’s ratios along the [001] direction (σzx = 0.30 and σzy = 0.34) approached 0.33 (Fig. 2d), suggesting ductility comparable to that of metallic materials37. Furthermore, 3D surface contours of the Young’s modulus demonstrated a strongly anisotropic mechanical behavior (Fig. 2b). Because C33 was lower than C11 and C22, the Young’s modulus along the [001] direction was reduced (Fig. 2a). The relationship between structure and mechanical behavior was further analyzed to understand the origin of anisotropy. The mechanical property along the [001] direction was dominated by the flexible metal-organic chains [Co(C4H4N2)]n2n+, resulting in a smaller elastic constant (C33 = 71.86 GPa). In contrast, rigid metavanadate [V2O6]n2n- chains along the [100] and [010] directions enhanced strain resistance, as reflected in the higher values of C11 (86.82 GPa) and C22 (157.3 GPa). The flexocatalytic polarization ability was strongly correlated with the dielectric constant, where a larger value corresponded to a higher polarization26. The dielectric constants, including the electronic and ionic dielectric tensors (ɛxx, ɛyy, ɛzz), were calculated (Fig. 3a). The dielectric constants along different crystallographic directions were expressed as ɛ0 = ɛ∞ + ɛi, where ɛ∞ and ɛi represented the electronic and ionic contributions, respectively38,39. The total dielectric constant of Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 (8.07) was higher than that of the isolated pyrazine molecule (2.95) and previously reported vanadium oxides (4.46 for V2O5 and 5.36 for VO2)40,41. The enhanced dielectric constant could improve the polarizability of Co[C4H4N2]V2O6. Moreover, Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 exhibited slightly anisotropic dielectric behavior, with ɛxx, ɛyy, and ɛzz values of 7.67, 8.34, and 8.20, respectively. This anisotropic dielectric response was related to the unique bridging configuration of Co[C4H4N2]V2O6. Furthermore, the projected density of states (PDOS) for Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 was analyzed. The upper valence bands were primarily composed of Co 3 d states, whereas the lower conduction bands consisted of Co 3 d, V 3 d, O 2p, and N 2p states (Fig. 3b–d). Moreover, each Co atom was octahedrally coordinated with O and N atoms in the Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 structure. The flexoelectric polarization capability was evaluated through finite element simulations. Generally, ultrasonic waves were used as the mechanical excitation source in flexocatalysis, generating pressures of 105-106 Pa in the liquid environment42,43. When the cavitation bubbles reached their critical rupture size and collapsed, they could generate peak pressures of 108-109 Pa, exerting forces on the surrounding liquid environment. The spatial pressure distribution could induce inhomogeneous strain in the Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 microsheets, locally disrupting their inversion symmetry and thereby generating internal polarization. This polarization drove e- and h+ to separate and migrate toward opposite directions, resulting in their accumulation on the material surfaces and causing a complete tilting of the energy bands (Fig. 3f, g). To reproduce the mechanical environment in simulations, an external force (100 MPa) was applied to deform the microsheets, which induced a clear strain (Supplementary Fig. 4) and generated substantial strain gradients up to 8 × 105 m-1 and flexoelectric polarization peaking at 0.4 C·m-2 (Fig. 3h, i). The obtained values surpassed those reported for several inorganic materials44,45, demonstrating that Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 exhibited a robust flexoelectric polarization capability.

Flexocatalytic uranyl mechanism

Uranyl (UO22+) ions were the common aqueous form of uranium in wastewater, posing significant environmental and health risks due to their high mobility and toxicity. Under ultrasonic excitation, we found that Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 could effectively transform soluble UO22+ into distinct yellow precipitates (Fig. 4a). The final products were further characterized using PXRD, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and Raman spectroscopy. PXRD pattern confirmed that the yellow products crystallized in the (UO2)O2·2H2O phase, indicating that flexocatalysis effectively altered the uranyl speciation in wastewater (Fig. 4b). XPS spectrum of U 4 f exhibited characteristic peaks at 391.6 eV (U 4f5/2) and 380.7 eV (U 4f7/2)46, confirming the +6 oxidation state of uranium in the final products (Fig. 4c). FTIR spectrum showed a peak at 609 cm-1, corresponding to the asymmetric stretching vibration of the U-O bond, while bands at 728 and 908 cm-1 were assigned to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of the U = O bond, respectively (Fig. 4d)18. The peaks at 1625 and 3420 cm-1 were attributed to the bending and stretching vibrations of the O-H groups, indicating the presence of adsorbed or crystalline water molecules in (UO2)O2·2H2O47. Raman spectrum displayed peaks at 828 and 853 cm-1, corresponding to the symmetric stretching vibration of the O = U = O bond and the O-O stretching vibration of the peroxide group bridging oxygen ligands in (UO2)O2·2H2O, respectively (Fig. 4e)48. The transformation of UO22+ into (UO2)O2·2H2O under flexocatalytic conditions remained unclear and was not previously investigated. For Co[C4H4N2]V2O6, the external mechanical vibrations could induce flexoelectric polarization. Near the material surface, the generated e⁻ and h⁺ participated in redox reactions with species such as dissolved O2 and water molecules, leading to the formation of •O2- and •OH. The •O2- and •OH subsequently promoted the generation of H2O249,50. The accumulation of H2O2 further altered the solution environment, decreasing pH from 6.92 to 5.36 (Supplementary Fig. 6). To confirm the H2O2 generation pathway, control experiments under different quenching conditions were performed. To identify the active species involved, quenching experiments and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were performed. In the quenching experiments, sacrificial agents AgNO3, (NH4)2C2O4, p-BQ, and TBA were used to scavenge e-, h+, •O2-, and •OH, respectively. The results revealed that e-, •O2-, and •OH were the primary reactive species responsible for uranyl removal during the flexocatalytic process (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Table 4). Meanwhile, EPR spectra showed that the signals of •O2- and •OH increased with ultrasonic time, indicating enhanced generation of these reactive species (Fig. 4g). Quantitative analysis indicated that the concentration of H2O2 increased with ultrasonic irradiation time (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Fig. 9). Notably, the H2O2 concentration (1360.33 μmol·g-1) in deionized water was significantly higher than that (594.63 μmol·g-1) in the uranyl solution. This further suggested that H2O2 was effectively generated and played a crucial role in the conversion of uranyl speciation. Based on the above analysis, the overall reaction pathway was proposed, as illustrated in Fig. 4i. The solidification of U(VI) under flexocatalytic conditions resulted from reactions with active radicals, including e-, •O2-, •OH, and H2O2.

Flexocatalytic uranyl performance

In aqueous systems, soluble uranyl existed in various forms, which were governed by the pH conditions52. Therefore, pH-dependent experiments on the flexocatalytic removal of uranyl using Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 were conducted. Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 exhibited good acid-base resistance over a wide pH range of 1.50–12.00 (Supplementary Fig. 12). Meanwhile, Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 displayed flexocatalytic activity throughout the tested pH range (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 7). The catalytic activity peaked at pH = 4.01, where a uranium removal rate of 99.01% was achieved. Notably, Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 also exhibited flexocatalytic capability in removing UO22+ even under conditions (pH > 7), where uranyl predominantly existed as anionic carbonate complexes such as UO2(CO3)34- and UO2(CO3)22- 53. These results confirmed that Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 exhibited flexocatalytic applicability for uranyl removal across a broad pH range. Ultrasonic power was a critical factor in determining the practical applicability of flexocatalysis. Previous studies reported that inorganic materials often required high power (>200 W) to achieve high catalytic activity3,18,54. The catalytic capability of Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 under varying ultrasonic power was systematically investigated. Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 exhibited a notable uranium removal ability at 20 W (Supplementary Fig. 14, Supplementary Table 8). Moreover, the uranyl removal efficiency increased progressively with increasing ultrasonic power (Fig. 5b). This improvement resulted from the higher external force generated by stronger ultrasound, which induced more pronounced structural deformation and larger strain gradients, thereby enhancing the catalytic activity17. Meanwhile, the flexocatalytic activity observed at low ultrasonic power was primarily attributed to the inorganic chains bridging the metal-organic chains of Co[C4H4N2]V2O6. The effect of catalyst size on uranyl removal by Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 was also investigated. Catalysts of all tested sizes exhibited high uranyl removal rates (≥93.78%) (Supplementary Fig. 15, Supplementary Table 9). Meanwhile, uranium removal efficiency increased with decreasing catalyst size (Fig. 5c). The significant size dependence arose from smaller particles, which generated larger strain gradients at the micro-nano scale under deformation, thereby enhancing flexoelectric polarization and catalytic performance55. The influence of catalyst dosage (5, 10, 20, 30, and 50 mg) on uranyl removal from 20 mL of wastewater was further examined. All tested dosages effectively catalyzed uranyl, with the optimal performance observed at a 20 mg dosage, achieving a uranium removal rate of 99.07% (Supplementary Fig. 16, Supplementary Table 10). Higher dosages did not lead to superior performance, likely due to excessive catalyst accumulation, which formed high electric fields at the surface, subsequently affecting radical generation56. Moreover, the nuclear wastewater generally contained varying concentrations of uranium. The flexocatalytic ability of Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 was evaluated for different uranium concentrations (1.96-95.48 ppm). Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 exhibited excellent removal rates, exceeding 97% across all tested uranium concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 17, Supplementary Tables 11–16). The recycling performance of Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 was also investigated. Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 exhibited excellent cycling stability, maintaining over 97% uranium removal rate throughout five consecutive cycles (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Table 17). A series of experiments demonstrated that Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 showed exceptional flexocatalytic performance for uranyl removal, markedly surpassing previously reported piezocatalysts (Supplementary Table 18).

Nuclear wastewater containing uranium frequently exhibited high salinity. Therefore, the flexocatalytic performance of Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 was evaluated in simulated high-salinity solutions to assess its effectiveness under different ionic environments (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Table 19). In the U + 1.5 M NaCl system, the material exhibited excellent performance, reducing uranium concentration from 2.13 ppm to 250.3 ppb (88.25% removal). In the U + 1.5 M NaHCO3 system, the uranium concentration was reduced from 2.08 ppm to 438.5 ppb, corresponding to a 78.92% removal. Similarly, in the U + 1.5 M MgCl2 system, the uranium concentration decreased from 2.12 ppm to 486.0 ppb, achieving an 77.08% removal. In the U + 1.5 M CaCl2 system, Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 still maintained significant catalytic activity, lowering the uranium concentration from 2.03 ppm to 714.2 ppb (64.82% removal). These results confirmed that Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 retained efficient flexocatalytic activity toward uranyl species under high-salinity conditions. Additionally, in seawater environments, pentavalent vanadium species can be converted to VO2+ during coordination, thus potentially competing with UO22+ for active sites52,57,58. Therefore, uranium removal experiments were carried out in the presence of V(V) at various V/U molar ratios (0.94, 2.87, 5.33, 6.84, and 9.36). Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 maintained high uranium removal rates (>90%) (Supplementary Fig. 19, Supplementary Table 20), suggesting that the presence of V(V) had no significant impact on the uranyl removal performance.

Due to the excellent catalytic performance, the uranyl removal capability by Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 in various water matrices (contaminated potable water, contaminated seawater, and uranium mining wastewater) was further investigated using a dynamic experimental system. In this setup, a circulating water pump was used to maintain ambient room temperature (Fig. 5f). The catalyst served as the stationary phase and was packed into a chromatography column (bed volume = 1.71 mL), which was placed inside an ultrasonic cleaner. Uranium-containing water was then pumped through the column using a peristaltic pump. Results indicated that Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 exhibited continuous catalytic capability toward uranyl species, achieving outstanding uranium removal performance across all tested water matrices (Supplementary Data 1–3). When treating 1017 bed volumes of contaminated potable water, Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 achieved a uranium removal rate of 98.57-99.26%. The uranium concentration decreased from 2.07 ppm to 15.4-29.7 ppb, which was below the acceptable limit of 30 ppb for potable water defined by the US Environmental Protection Agency (Fig. 5g)59. During the treatment of 959 bed volumes of contaminated seawater, Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 similarly reduced the uranium concentration (2.16 ppm) to 18.5-29.7 ppb (98.63-99.14% removal) (Fig. 5h). More importantly, during the treatment of 935 bed volumes of uranium mining wastewater, Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 could also lower the uranium concentration from 5.64 ppm to 194.5-288.9 ppb (94.88-96.55% removal), meeting the discharge limit (300 ppb) regulated by China’s Ministry of Ecology and Environment (MEE) (Fig. 5i)60. These results clearly indicated that the flexocatalyst Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 exhibited strong potential for the remediation of U-contaminated potable water, uranium extraction from seawater, and the industrial treatment of uranium mining wastewater. Furthermore, this study was the first to use the dynamic experimental system to evaluate mechanical catalysis performance, providing a reference for its application in other contexts.

To further consolidate extensive parameter-dependent experimental observations into unified, generalizable operational principles, an interpretable data-driven analysis was conducted. This approach followed recent advances that demonstrated data-driven and AI-assisted frameworks could effectively integrate multivariate experimental spaces and identify governing factors beyond empirical trial-and-error optimization61–63. By considering all experimentally accessible variables in this study, Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis identified reaction time as the most influential parameter, followed by initial uranyl concentration, initial pH, and ultrasonic power (Supplementary Fig. 21a). This indicated that the flexocatalytic rate was predominantly governed by cumulative reaction duration and available energy input, whereas catalyst dosage, particle size, and atmosphere played secondary roles. The SHAP dependence of reaction time showed a monotonic increase with saturation (Supplementary Fig. 21b), reflecting the cumulative generation of reactive species and the progressive conversion of uranyl speciation during flexocatalytic treatment. In contrast, higher initial uranyl concentrations led to lower predicted removal rates. At a fixed reaction time, the amount of reactive species generated was insufficient to fully treat the increased uranyl load, which agreed with the experimentally observed concentration-dependent removal trends. These results not only validated the interpretability of the model but also provided guiding principles for the future optimization of flexocatalytic uranyl removal processes.

Discussion

To summarize, we developed a novel strategy to enhance the flexoelectric response of centrosymmetric materials by bridging inorganic chains with metal-organic chains. Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 was shown to be an excellent flexocatalyst for uranyl separation. The bridging approach endowed the cantilever-like microsheets of Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 with facile deformability and a high dielectric constant, both of which contributed to its excellent flexocatalytic polarization capability. Under ultrasonic stimulation, dynamic polarization enabled Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 to achieve outstanding uranyl-separation performance compared with previously reported piezocatalysts. The advantages of Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 for flexocatalytic uranyl separation included (a) straightforward synthesis, (b) high catalytic activity under challenging conditions (broad pH range and high-salinity environment), (c) low ultrasonic power requirement, (d) excellent reusability, and (e) superior uranyl separation ability in various water matrices under a dynamic experimental system. We also elucidated the pathway by which soluble uranyl was transformed into insoluble (UO2)O2·2H2O. The active species (e.g., e−, •O2−, •OH, and H2O2), generated by dynamic polarization, played a key role in driving the solidification of U(VI). This work not only introduced an approach to fabricate high-performance flexocatalysts and established the structure-property relationship governing flexocatalytic performance, but also provided a new paradigm for the remediation of U-contaminated potable water, uranium extraction from seawater, and the industrial treatment of uranium mining wastewater. Moreover, the demonstrated effectiveness and universality of flexocatalysis highlighted its potential for mechanocatalytic applications across diverse fields.

Methods

Materials and flexocatalyst preparation

The detailed information on chemicals and reagents was provided in the Supplementary Note 1. The bridging hybrid material Co[C4H4N2]V2O6 was synthesized through a modified solvothermal method64, which yielded bouquet-like crystals with an efficiency of approximately 85%. The specific synthesis procedure was as follows. 0.18 g of V2O5 was added to 20 mL of deionized water and stirred to form an orange-yellow mixture. 0.09 g of LiOH·H2O was added, and the mixture was stirred for 30 min to yield a light brown transparent solution. 0.42 g of CoSO4·7H2O and 0.12 g of pyrazine were sequentially added and homogenized, followed by pH adjustment to 6.70 using dilute H2SO4. The orange-yellow mixture was transferred into a 40 mL Teflon-lined autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 135 °C for 24 h. After cooling to room temperature, the product was vacuum-filtered, washed several times with deionized water and ethanol, and then dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 60 °C.

Experimental procedures and calculation methods

Catalyst characterizations were provided in the Supplementary Note 2. During the flexocatalytic experiments, ultrasonic waves were used as the mechanical excitation source. The temperature was maintained at room temperature by a circulating water system, and all experiments were conducted in the dark. Detailed flexocatalytic procedures were described in the Supplementary Note 3. Periodic density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP 6.4) to investigate the electronic structure of the catalyst65. The Cauchy stress-strain method was employed to calculate the mechanical properties66, and the Voigt-Reuss-Hill (VRH) approximation was used to determine the bulk modulus (B), Young’s modulus (E), and shear modulus (G)67. Electron-phonon coupling calculations were applied to obtain the dielectric constants68. Finite element method (FEM) simulations were performed in COMSOL Multiphysics 6.3 to analyze the flexoelectric response15. Detailed simulation methods were described in in the Supplementary Note 4. An interpretable data-driven analysis was conducted to identify the key operational factors governing flexocatalytic uranyl removal. A gradient boosting regression model was used to capture nonlinear relationships between experimental parameters and removal performance, with model interpretability achieved via SHAP to quantify parameter contributions at both the global and local levels. Detailed methods were provided in Supplementary Note 5.
