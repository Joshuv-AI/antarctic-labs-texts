Enhanced CRISPR-Cas3-mediated genome editing using circularized crRNAs
Authors: Kouya Mikamo, Kazuto Yoshimi, Naoko Abe, Kohei Takeshita, Hiroshi Abe, Tomoji Mashimo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryType I-E CRISPR-Cas3 represents a genome-editing technology in which large deletions averaging several kilobases are introduced in target regions. However, its genome-editing efficiency varies considerably across targets and cell types, making it difficult to achieve consistent results. Here, we investigated the efficacy and stability of circularized CRISPR RNAs (ccrRNAs) to enhance CRISPR-Cas3-mediated genome editing in human cells. Using in vitro single-strand DNA cleavage assays, we demonstrated that ccrRNA induces Cascade complex formation. Significant genome-editing activity targeting the EMX1 and B2M genes was observed in cellular assays using K562 cells. Long-read sequencing identified large-scale deletion mutations at the target loci and no detectable off-target effects using ccrRNA. Furthermore, ccrRNAs exhibited extended intracellular stability compared with that for linear crRNAs, resulting in an enhanced editing efficiency. These results demonstrate that ccrRNAs enable stable, efficient, and highly specific genome editing and support the broader application of the long-range deletion system.

Introduction

CRISPR (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats) gene editing is an innovative technology enabling precise DNA modifications in living organisms with very high control and efficiency.1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8 The CRISPR-Cas system, originally an adaptive immune mechanism in prokaryotes, includes two main classes: class 1 systems operate through multi-protein complexes, while class 2 systems function via single proteins.9,10,11,12,13 CRISPR-Cas9, a class 2 system, is widely adopted owing to its robust editing abilities across diverse organisms. However, its wide use has limited investigations of alternative CRISPR systems, including CRISPR-Cas3. Recent investigations, including our previous work, have demonstrated that class 1 CRISPR-Cas3 generates unidirectional large-scale genomic deletions extending several kilobases upstream of protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequences in target loci under CRISPR RNA (crRNA) guidance.14,15,16 Unlike CRISPR-Cas9, which induces localized double-strand breaks that rely largely on host-DNA repair pathways, CRISPR-Cas3 degrades target DNA in a unidirectional and processive manner, thereby generating long-range deletions from a programmed target site. This long-range deletion activity provides a distinct genome engineering modality for robust disruption of broader genomic regions, including lncRNA loci, regulatory regions, repetitive sequences, and other extended non-coding elements that may not be efficiently inactivated by small indels alone. In addition, CRISPR-Cas3 recognizes a substantially longer target sequence than CRISPR-Cas9, with a 32-base guide region compared with the 20-base guide sequence of Cas9, which may contribute to enhanced target recognition specificity and reduced off-target activity. Importantly, recent studies have begun to demonstrate the utility of CRISPR-Cas3 in therapeutic contexts, including in vivo and ex vivo gene disruption applications.17,18 The studies indicated that Cas3-mediated deletion can reduce the risk of residual protein function associated with in-frame mutations observed after Cas9-mediated editing and that Cas3-mediated disruption of TRAC and B2M in human T cells can be achieved without detectable off-target editing or impairment of cell viability and CAR-T cell function. These emerging applications support the knockout potential of CRISPR-Cas3 as an innovative genome-editing tool with a distinct and expanding application space. Thus, Cas3-mediated genome editing is not intended to replace Cas9- or Cas12a-mediated editing, which is well suited for compact and localized mutagenesis or nucleotide-level modification, but rather to complement these systems as a programmed long-range deletion platform. In this context, improving the efficiency, stability, and specificity of Cas3 crRNAs represents an important step toward broader use of this long-range deletion system.

The type I-E CRISPR-Cas3 system, a class 1 CRISPR system, mediates the degradation of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) through a two-step process: target DNA recognition by the Cascade (CRISPR-associated complex for antiviral defense) complex and DNA cleavage by the Cas3 nuclease recruited to the Cascade complex. In the type I-E subtype, the Cascade complex is composed of Cas5, Cas6, and Cas8, two Cas11, and six Cas7 subunits. Cas8 is responsible for the initial recognition of the PAM sequence. Upon PAM recognition, the crRNA hybridizes with the complementary target DNA strand, forming a three-stranded R-loop structure. This conformational change facilitates the recruitment of Cas3 to the Cascade complex. Once bound, Cas3 introduces a nick in the non-target DNA strand (NTS) via its nuclease domain. Subsequently, its helicase domain unwinds and translocates the NTS in an ATP-dependent manner upstream of the PAM site. In addition to this primary activity, Cas3 exhibits collateral cleavage of the target strand, resulting in double-strand breaks upstream of the target site. The combination of progressive cleavage and unwinding of the NTS enables target dsDNA degradation as part of the interference mechanism of type I CRISPR systems.19,20,21,22

For activation of CRISPR-Cas3 genome editing in eukaryotic cells, a Cascade protein complex formation reaction termed “processing” is essential. During this reaction, the precursor crRNA (pre-crRNA) is cleaved by Cas6 at a specific site within the repeat sequence to generate mature crRNA (mat-crRNA), enabling subsequent assembly of the Cascade complex with Cas factors.14 However, linear pre-crRNA exhibits limited stability and undergoes rapid intracellular degradation, impeding Cascade formation and limiting editing efficiency. To address this limitation, we employed circularized crRNAs (ccrRNAs) featuring covalently closed loop structures. Circularized RNAs exhibit greater stability than that of linear counterparts and demonstrate resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation.23,24,25,26 These molecules participate in diverse cellular processes, such as gene regulation and microRNA regulation (e.g., by functioning as microRNA sponges able to bind to and inhibit microRNA activity).27,28,29,30 In this study, we artificially synthesized ccrRNAs using T4 RNA ligase with oligonucleotide RNA splints as templates.31 This enzymatic ligation method produces stable, covalently closed circular RNA molecules designed to improve crRNA stability and function in the CRISPR-Cas3 system. We hypothesized that these stable ccrRNAs will enhance CRISPR-Cas3 performance by maintaining RNA integrity during cellular processing, thereby overcoming current system limitations and achieving more reliable and efficient genome editing.

Results

Generation of circularized crRNAs and in vitro validation of cascade assembly

To generate and purify ccrRNAs, we used a splint ligation method for the circularization process (Figure 1A). Target crRNA sequences were incubated with sequence-specific ligation oligonucleotides in the presence of T4 RNA ligase 2 to facilitate intramolecular circularization. Uncircularized crRNAs (un-ccrRNAs), defined here as the linear form of ccrRNA prior to circularization and consisting of a 61-nt linear RNA, were subsequently depleted, and successful circularization was verified by denaturing gel electrophoresis. A fragment analysis revealed the disappearance of the linear crRNA band following circularization treatment, with the appearance of two shifted bands (Figure 1B). This pattern suggested that most linear crRNAs had been circularized. The presence of two bands indicated that both single crRNAs and double-length circular products, resulting from crRNA-splint DNA dimeric reactions, were generated during the process. To ensure accurate activity measurements, the smaller band corresponding to a single ccrRNA was excised and purified for further analyses. Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis revealed that the molecular weight of the circularized RNA is found to be 18 smaller than that of the linear RNA due to the loss of one water molecule (Figures 1C and S1). RNaseR sensitivity assays confirmed the efficient degradation of non-circularized controls, while the intensity of bands corresponding to circularized crRNA was not affected by RNaseR treatment (Figure 1D). To assess the functional capacity of ccrRNAs for Cascade assembly, we performed an in vitro collateral cleavage assay, exploiting the nuclease activity of CRISPR-Cas3 upon functional Cascade formation (Figure 1E). Cas protein components were reconstituted at stoichiometric ratios optimized for Cascade assembly, and collateral cleavage activity was measured using pre-crRNA or ccrRNAs as guide RNA substrates. The experiment demonstrated that ccrRNAs with CRISPR-Cas3 induced collateral cleavage activity comparable to that for pre-crRNAs (Figures 1F and 1G). When Cas factors were mixed at defined molar ratios relative to crRNA (Cas6:crRNA = 1:1), ccrRNA exhibited approximately two-fold higher collateral cleavage activity than pre-crRNA. This result suggests that ccrRNA enables more efficient Cascade assembly (Figures 1G and 1H).

Enhanced genome-editing efficiency of ccrRNAs in cultured human cells

To evaluate the genome-editing potential of ccrRNAs in mammalian cells, we utilized K562 tet-Cas3-cascade cells expressing CRISPR-Cas3 components with doxycycline (Dox). The crRNAs were designed to target the EMX1 gene for genome editing, as previously validated with pre-crRNAs.14 Following pre-induction of CRISPR-Cas3 components using Dox, cells were transfected with crRNAs via electroporation (Figure 2A). Initial experiments in which Dox induction and crRNA delivery were performed simultaneously failed to produce detectable CRISPR-Cas3 activity. We therefore hypothesized that a temporal lag in Cas protein expression limited crRNA functionality. To address this, Dox was added 6 h prior to crRNA electroporation, allowing sufficient Cas expression before transfection, which resulted in robust CRISPR-Cas3 activity. A PCR analysis of the genome 2 days after transfection revealed large deletions characteristic of CRISPR-Cas3 activity in ccrRNA-transfected cells, similar to those observed using pre-crRNAs. In contrast, mat-crRNAs showed no detectable genome-editing activity, confirming that the observed activity could be specifically attributable to circularization (Figure 2B). Next, we assessed genome-editing activity following sequential induction of Cas factors and crRNA transfection to investigate the intracellular stability of different crRNA formats. Modified pre-crRNAs (mod pre-crRNAs) incorporating 2′-O-methyl and phosphorothioate modifications at both termini to enhance stability were included for comparison.18,32,33,34,35,36 When Cas factor expression was induced 6 h prior to crRNA transfection, shorter bands indicating large deletions were detected across all RNA formats. However, pre-crRNAs showed no genome-editing activity when induction occurred simultaneously with or after transfection, indicating its rapid degradation in cells. Both mod pre-crRNAs and ccrRNAs maintained genome-editing activity even when Cas factors were induced 24 h post-transfection (Figures 2D and 2E), demonstrating superior intracellular stability.

We further validated these findings using another target, the B2M gene. Similar to the previous experiments, we examined changes in genome-editing efficiency based on the timing of Dox induction. When Cas-protein expression was induced 6 h prior to transfection, genome-editing activity was detected across all crRNA formats (Figure 2F). Quantitative assessment based on PCR band intensity indicated that activity was highest for ccrRNAs, followed by mod pre-crRNAs and pre-crRNAs (Figure 2G). Notably, when Dox was applied 12 h post-transfection, ccrRNAs maintained robust genome-editing activity, while pre-crRNAs and mod pre-crRNAs showed minimal activity, indicating sustained Cascade formation using ccrRNAs for extended periods. Furthermore, we evaluated phenotypic alterations associated with genome editing. Jurkat cells were electroporated with mRNAs encoding the CRISPR-Cas3 components together with crRNAs targeting B2M or TRAC, respectively. Consistent with previous findings, ccrRNAs induced genomic deletions at the target loci at higher frequencies compared with pre-crRNAs and mod pre-crRNAs (Figures 3C, 3D, and 3G). In addition, flow cytometric analysis revealed that the proportions of B2M- and TCRαβ-expressing cells were most markedly reduced in the ccrRNA-treated groups. This trend was consistent with the observed deletion frequencies at the corresponding loci (Figures 3E, 3F, 3H, and 3I).

Finally, to evaluate ccrRNA functionality in the RNA transfection system, we employed HEK293T-reporter cells containing integrated mCherry-P2A-EGFP sequences and co-transfected ccrRNAs targeting endogenous EGFP with Cap1-capped mRNAs encoding individual Cas factors. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis of GFP-knockout efficiency demonstrated that both mod pre-crRNAs and ccrRNAs exhibited superior genome-editing performance compared with that of pre-crRNAs (Figures S2A and S2B). However, the efficiency difference between mod pre-crRNAs and ccrRNAs was less pronounced in EGFP targeting than in EMX1 or B2M targeting, likely because the mRNA transfection efficiency serves as a bottleneck for genome-editing efficiency. To further validate these findings, the experiment was repeated and analyzed by flow cytometry. The ccrRNA-treated group consistently exhibited the highest proportion of GFP-negative cells (Figures S2C and S2D). Taken together, these results clearly demonstrated that ccrRNAs maintain excellent genome-editing efficiency even in fully RNA-based delivery systems.

Comprehensive mutational analysis of ccrRNAs at target sites

For comprehensive mutation characterization, we employed DAJIN2, an analysis tool for nanopore sequencing data capable of detecting diverse genome-editing outcomes at the target locus. This analysis revealed heterogeneous largely deleted mutation patterns across most treated samples, excluding untreated controls (Figure 4B and Table S1). Consistent with IGV results, only ccrRNA-treated samples showed detectable mutations even with delayed Dox induction at 24 h post-transfection. These findings demonstrate that ccrRNAs enable CRISPR-Cas3 to maintain robust, long-term genome-editing activity with high mutational efficiency.

We then assessed potential off-target effects of CRISPR-Cas3 genome editing. Off-target mutations have not been observed in previous analyses using pre-crRNAs.14 An in silico off-target analysis for the EMX1-targeting crRNA identified two potential off-target sequences (POTs) with five mismatches within the 32-base spacer sequences and four POTs with six mismatches (Table S2). Additionally, we identified one POT adjacent to the PAM sequence extending up to 21 bases including the PAM, which is critical for CRISPR-Cas target recognition. PCR and long-read sequencing analyses were performed for all candidate off-target sites (Figures 5A and 5B). In most cases, no bands other than the wild-type (WT) band were detected in the PCR analysis. Although an extra band was observed at a subset of off-target loci (Figure 5A), subsequent long-read sequencing demonstrated that no deletions were introduced at the corresponding target regions (Figure 5B). Furthermore, DAJIN2-based analysis confirmed the absence of any mutations, including small-scale deletions, within these regions. Circular structure enhances intracellular stability and genome-editing efficacy in human cells

To directly assess whether the circular structure confers enhanced intracellular stability to ccrRNAs, we used quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) to quantify their abundance over time following cellular uptake (Figure 6A). As anticipated, ccrRNAs exhibited more gradual intracellular degradation compared with degradation rates for both pre-crRNAs and mod pre-crRNAs (Figure 6B). To investigate whether the circular structure influences transfection efficiency, we evaluated the initial intracellular levels of ccrRNAs and both types of pre-crRNAs in K562 cells immediately following electroporation. RT-qPCR analyses revealed that both linear crRNA formats achieved comparable molecular uptake levels regardless of chemical modification status, while ccrRNAs were introduced at approximately half the level of linear crRNAs (Figure 6C). This finding suggests that optimal electroporation conditions differ between circular and linear RNA formats. Nevertheless, these results demonstrate that the introduced circular crRNAs exhibit remarkable intracellular stability and persist for extended periods, thereby enhancing genome-editing efficiency.

Discussion

We developed ccrRNAs for the type I-E CRISPR-Cas3 system, achieving a substantially higher genome-editing efficiency than those for conventional linear crRNAs. The enhanced editing efficiency was confirmed through in vitro assays and cellular genome-editing experiments, with consistent improvements observed across multiple targets. Furthermore, ccrRNAs demonstrated higher genome-editing efficiencies than those of chemically modified crRNAs, suggesting that the circular structure represents a more effective modification strategy than chemical modifications for type I-E CRISPR-Cas3. This study establishes circularized crRNA as a guide RNA engineering strategy that improves the efficiency and reliability of the Cas3 long-range deletion system while maintaining high target specificity.

The type I-E CRISPR-Cas3 genome-editing system can induce large deletions spanning several kilobases upstream of target regions, making it highly effective for gene-knockout applications.14,15 However, the genome-editing efficiency remains suboptimal and varies significantly in a target sequence-dependent manner. In this study, we addressed this limitation through guide RNA engineering rather than by modifying the Cas3 protein machinery itself. We hypothesized that this limitation stems from the intracellular instability of crRNAs and developed ccrRNAs incorporating circular structures, known to confer stability in cellular environments (Figure 1B). Although Cas3-mediated long-range deletions have been previously reported,14,15,16 our results demonstrate that the ccrRNAs provide additional functional advantages, including enhanced intracellular stability and improved cellular genome-editing activity. Mechanistically, the engineered ccrRNA forms the Cascade complex more efficiently than pre-crRNA under conditions of limited Cas6 availability. Importantly, the in vitro assays primarily evaluated Cascade assembly and collateral cleavage activity, whereas the cellular genome-editing assays additionally reflect intracellular processes after delivery, including RNA stability, Cascade availability, and the timing of Cas component expression (Figures 1F–1H, 2B, and 2C). This difference may partly contribute to the enhanced cellular-editing activity observed with ccrRNA. Previous studies have shown that crRNA processing is initiated by Cas6-mediated cleavage of repeat sequences. In line with this mechanism, the ccrRNA used in this study contains only a single repeat sequence per molecule, which likely reduces the Cas6 requirement for Cascade assembly relative to pre-crRNA. This property may be particularly advantageous under delivery-limited conditions, such as lipid nanoparticle-based systems, where the intracellular abundance of CRISPR components is constrained. While other genome-editing tools, such as CRISPR-Cas9, CRISPR-Cas12a, and CRISPR-Cas13, commonly employ terminal chemical modifications including 2′-O-methyl and phosphorothioate modifications for crRNA stabilization,32,33,34,35,36 our ccrRNA approach achieved greater enhancement than established chemical modification methods in the type I-E CRISPR-Cas3 system. An off-target analysis revealed that ccrRNAs maintained the editing specificity observed for pre-crRNAs, suggesting that circularization does not compromise target specificity.14 Together, these findings expand the design principles of guide RNAs for Cas3-based genome engineering and provide a practical strategy to improve the efficiency and reliability of programmed long-range deletion.

Previous studies have established that short RNA circularization confers enhanced intracellular stability compared with that for linear counterparts.25,26 Indeed, our ccrRNA design demonstrated improved intracellular stability, with approximately 2-fold higher retention compared with that for pre-crRNAs at 24 h post-transfection (Figures 1D and 5B). Since RNA stability decreases with increasing length,37 we propose that the enhanced editing efficiency with ccrRNAs results from both circular structure-mediated stabilization and additional stability improvements from size reduction (61 nucleotides compared with the previously required 90 nucleotides). This size minimization provides practical advantages, enabling the delivery of greater molecular quantities under volume-limited conditions, such as lipid nanoparticle delivery systems.33,38 Moreover, compared with established chemical modifications, such as 2′-O-methyl and phosphorothioate modifications, for RNA stabilization,39,40 the circular structure approach involves in vitro transcription-based synthesis without requiring chemical modifications, offering advantages with respect to cost and simplicity.41

In conclusion, the ccrRNAs developed in this study achieved superior genome-editing efficiency at the cellular level compared with that of conventional chemical modification methods in the type I-E CRISPR-Cas3 system while maintaining target recognition fidelity. ccrRNAs offer multiple advantages, including circular structure-mediated stabilization, size reduction, cost effectiveness, and procedural simplicity, representing a significant technological advancement toward the practical implementation of type I-E CRISPR-Cas3 and holding substantial promise for future gene therapy and basic research applications.

Limitations of the study

Conversion from pre-crRNAs to ccrRNAs resulted in a reduced electroporation delivery efficiency. While electroporation efficiency is typically independent of molecular size,42,43 specific protocols have shown size-dependent efficiency reductions in DNA delivery,44 suggesting that protocol optimization may improve the results. Alternative delivery methods, such as lipofection, may also mitigate efficiency differences and warrant further investigation. Additionally, while chemically modified sgRNAs for CRISPR-Cas9 and Cas12a have been validated in experimental animals, primarily mice,33,35 in vivo validation remains to be performed for ccrRNA-based CRISPR-Cas3. Such validation is essential for establishing high-efficiency CRISPR-Cas3 genome-editing systems and contributing to diverse research applications.

Cas3-mediated editing also has inherent limitations. Unlike Cas9- or Cas12a-mediated editing, Cas3 induces long-range deletions and is therefore less suitable for applications requiring nucleotide-level precision or highly localized small indels. Because long-range deletions may affect genomic stability depending on the target locus and deletion outcome, careful target selection and characterization of editing products will be important for future applications. However, recent in vivo and ex vivo studies suggest that appropriately targeted Cas3-mediated deletion can achieve complete gene disruption without necessarily compromising cell viability or therapeutic cell function.17,18 In addition, the multi-component Cascade-Cas3 machinery remains more challenging to deliver than compact genome-editing systems. These characteristics should be carefully considered when selecting an appropriate genome-editing platform. At the same time, however, they also define the unique application space of Cas3-mediated editing: programmable long-range deletions may offer particular advantages for perturbing broader genomic elements, including lncRNA loci, regulatory regions, repetitive sequences, and other extended non-coding elements that may not be efficiently disrupted by small indels alone.

Future studies addressing delivery optimization, deletion control, and in vivo validation will further clarify the translational potential of ccrRNA-based CRISPR-Cas3 genome editing as a long-range deletion platform.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

Human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T), reporter-HEK293T, human chronic myelogenous leukemia K562, and human T lymphocyte Jurkat cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. HEK293T and reporter-HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% L-glutamine. K562 and Jurkat cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS.

Spodoptera frugiperda Sf9 insect cells were maintained at 28°C with 130 rpm in PSFM-J1 medium.

The human cell lines and Sf9 cells used in this study were not further authenticated. All mammalian cell lines and Sf9 cells were not tested for mycoplasma contamination.

No human participants or primary human samples were used in this study.

Sex- and gender-based biological variables were not considered in this study because all experiments were conducted using established cell lines and no human participants or animal models were included.

Method details

Oligoribonucleotide synthesis

61-nt linear RNA oligos were prepared in-house using standard phosphoramidite chemistry. Briefly, commercially available phosphoroamidites for RNA synthesis (ChemGenes, ANP-3201, ANP-3202, ANP-3203, ANP-3205) and solid supports made of controlled pore glass (ChemeGenes, N-3201–10) were used for the synthesis. A chemical phosphorylation reagent containing a photo-cleavable hydrophobic tag was used to introduce a 5′ phosphate on the sequence.48 After the synthesis, they were deprotected and purified by reversed-phase HPLC.48 Their purity and integrity were confirmed by denaturing PAGE and LC-MS analysis.

Synthesis and characterization of circularized RNAs

Circular crRNAs were synthesized via intramolecular ligation using T4 RNA Ligase 2 (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). Typically, 5′-Phosphorylated crRNA (100 μM) and a complementary ligation oligonucleotide (100 μM) were mixed at a 2:5 molar ratio in a 4 μL volume, supplemented with 2 μL of T4 Rnl2 Reaction Buffer and 9 μL of nuclease-free water. The mixture was denatured at 90°C for 3 min and gradually cooled to 24°C over 40 min. After annealing, 4 μL of 50% (w/v) PEG 8000 and 1 μL of T4 RNA Ligase 2 were added, and the reaction was incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Reaction products were separated on 15% TBE-Urea gels (Novex, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), stained with SYBR Green II (TaKaRa, Kusatsu, Japan), excised, and purified using the ZR small-RNA PAGE Recovery Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). Circularization was confirmed using TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

To assess exonuclease resistance, 5 μL of purified circularized RNA was incubated with 0.6 μL of RNase R, 2 μL of 10× reaction buffer (both from Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and 12.4 μL of nuclease-free water at 37°C for 15 min. After treatment, 5 μL of the reaction was mixed with 15 μL of denaturing gel-loading buffer (DiagnoCine, Hackensack, NJ, USA) and heated at 70°C for 15 min. Samples were analyzed on 15% TBE-Urea gels (Novex, Thermo Fisher) under constant voltage (180 V) for 90 min. RNA was visualized with SYBR Green II (TaKaRa).

LC-MS analysis of RNA

LC-ESI-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II system coupled to a 6530 LC/Q TOF mass spectrometer. For LC separation, an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (130 Å, 1.7 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm; Waters) was employed. Samples were eluted with a linear gradient of methanol (0–30% over 12 min) in 8.6 mM triethylamine–100 mM hexafluoro 2 propanol buffer (pH 8.3) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, with the column maintained at 60 °C. Mass spectrometric detection was carried out in negative ion mode.

Expression and purification of Cas3, the mixture of cascade components

Recombinant E. coli Cas3 (EcoCas3) was expressed using a baculovirus expression system as previously described.49,50 Briefly, EcoCas3 cDNA containing an octa-histidine tag and a six asparagine–histidine repeat tag was cloned into the pFastBac-1 plasmid, with a TEV protease recognition site inserted between the tags and EcoCas3 to allow tag removal.

For Cascade complex expression, genes encoding each Cascade component were cloned into pFastBac-1 with nuclear localization signal (NLS) sequences at both termini and a His-tag at either the N- or C-terminus, linked by 2A-peptide sequences. Recombinant baculoviruses were generated using the Bac-to-Bac system and used to infect Sf9 insect cells. Infected cells were cultured at 28 °C for 24 h and subsequently at 20 °C for 3 days to induce protein expression.

Cells were harvested, lysed, and the Cas proteins were purified using nickel affinity chromatography (Ni-NTA), followed by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column.

In vitro single-strand DNA cleavage assay

Cascade complexes were assembled by incubating equal concentrations of Cascade proteins (0.8 μg/μL) with either linear or circularized crRNA (250 ng/μL) at 37°C for 30 min in complex formation buffer (5 mM HEPES-K pH 7.5, 60 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 10 μM CoCl2). For Cas3 reactions, the assembled Cascade complex (36 ng/μL), Cas3 protein (40 ng/μL), and target dsDNA (5 nM) were mixed in reaction buffer (5 mM HEPES-K pH 7.5, 60 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 10 μM CoCl2, 1 mM ATP). Then, 20 μL of the reaction mixture was combined with 5 μL of the diluted fluorescence probe solution (10 μM; 56FAM-AAAGGTCGGAA-ZEN-GTCAACGGATTTGGTC-ABkFQ, IDT). Fluorescence intensity was monitored every 30 s at 37°C using the CFX96 Touch Deep Well system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

CRISPR-Cas3-mediated genome editing in human cells

K562 cells were co-transfected with the CRISPR-Cas3 expression plasmid (1 μg) and pCMV-PBase (0.5 μg) using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 h, cells were selected with puromycin (1 μg/mL) for 5 days to establish stable K562 Tet-On Cas3cascade lines. Reporter HEK293T cells expressing mCherry-P2A-EGFP were used to assess gene knockout, as previously described.14 Cells were maintained by passaging every 2–3 days at 0.5 × 105 cells/mL (K562, jurkat) or 1.0 × 105 cells/mL (HEK293T).

Circularized crRNA and mRNA encoding CRISPR-Cas3 components were introduced into cells using the 4D-Nucleofector System (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) with preset programs: FF-120 for K562, CM-130 for jurkat, and DG-150 for HEK293T. For K562 tetCas3cascade cells, doxycycline (2 μg/mL) was added to induce Cas expression. Three days post-electroporation, genomic DNA was extracted using the Maxwell® RSC Whole Blood DNA Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Target regions were amplified from 60 ng of gDNA using either 2× Quick Taq® HS DyeMix (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) or Tks Gflex DNA Polymerase (TaKaRa). PCR products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis and images were obtained using the GelDoc Go System (Bio-Rad).

Genome deletions induced by CRISPR-Cas3 were quantified by ddPCR. Each reaction contained 20 ng of gDNA, 0.9 μL of 10 μM primers, 0.5 μL each of target and internal control TaqMan probes (Thermo Fisher), and nuclease-free water to 10 μL. Reactions were mixed with 10 μL of ddPCR Supermix for Probes (no dUTP; Bio-Rad), and droplets were generated using the Bio-Rad Droplet Generator. PCR was performed using the T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad) with a ramp rate of 2 °C/s. Droplets were read on the QX200 Droplet Digital PCR System (Bio-Rad).

Flow cytometry

The following monoclonal antibodies were used for analysis: Brilliant Violet 421 anti-human HLA-ABC and PE/Cyanine7 anti-human TCR α/β. Data were acquired on a Attune NxT Flow Cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Quantification of intracellular crRNA levels

cDNA synthesis was performed with ReverTra Ace reverse transcriptase (TOYOBO) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Intracellular crRNA levels were quantified by qRT-PCR using either SYBR Green or TaqMan probe-based detection.

For SYBR Green assays, each 10 μL reaction contained 5 μL of SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad), 0.25 μL of 10 μM primers, 3.5 μL of nuclease-free water, and 1 μL of cDNA. For TaqMan assays, reactions contained 5 μL of Luna Universal qPCR Master Mix (NEB), 0.5 μL of TaqMan probe (Thermo Fisher), 3.5 μL of nuclease-free water, and 1 μL of cDNA. Relative RNA levels were quantified using the ΔΔCq method with ACTB used as the internal control for normalization.

Long-read sequencing for detecting deletion mutations

Genomic DNA amplification, library preparation, and sequencing were performed as described previously.16 Briefly, PCR amplification with universal-tailed primers was followed by nested PCR with barcoded primers using KOD Multi & Epi (Toyobo). Barcoded amplicons were pooled, purified, and quantified (20–30 ng/μL). Libraries were prepared using the ONT Ligation Sequencing Kit (SQK-LSK109/114) and NEBNext End Repair/dA-Tailing Module according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was conducted on a MinION Mk1C (Flow Cell R9.4.1 or R10.4.1) for 24 h. Base-called reads were demultiplexed, aligned and filtered for quality by MinKNOW with default settings of the device, and visualized using IGV (ver. 2.16.2).

Genotyping using DAJIN2

Nanopore sequencing data were analyzed using DAJIN2 (v0.6.1) as described previously.16 Briefly, reads were aligned using minimap2 via mappy, converted to MIDSV format for mutation profiling, and clustered to classify alleles. Sorted BAM files for each allele were generated using pysam. Genome coordinates were retrieved from the UCSC Genome Browser to update BAM headers. Consensus sequences were output as FASTA, HTML and Excel files, with mutated bases highlighted. The detailed workflow followed the original publication. Single-nucleotide substitutions as well as single-base insertions or deletions were regarded as sequencing errors and were therefore assigned to the wild-type (WT) allele or merged with alleles sharing the same deletion pattern for downstream quantification. Deletion alleles exhibiting similar breakpoint patterns were grouped and labeled as Large Del#01, #02, and so forth.

Sequencing data by Nanopore is deposited in the SRA at NCBI and the BioProject accession ID is PRJNA1335762.

Analysis of off-target effects

Potential Type I-E CRISPR off-target sites in the human genome (GRCh38/hg38) were identified using GGGenome.14 PAM sequences (AAG, TAG, AAC, GAG, AGG, ATG) were selected based on previous reports [Westra, 2012; Hochstrasser, 2014; Leenay, 2016; Jung, 2017]. Candidate sites located at multiples of 6 positions unrecognized by the Cas3 spacer were exclude and up to 32 mismatches in the on-target sequence were allowed. Perfect matches at the 5′ end from the PAM were additionally identified and ranked. Sequence context was retrieved from Ensembl. Surrounding regions were PCR-amplified with Tks Gflex DNA Polymerase (TaKaRa) and analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis to detect large deletions.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 11. Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. Comparisons between two groups were assessed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Comparisons among three or more groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. The exact value of n, the definition of n, and all statistical details are provided in the corresponding figure legends.
