Groups of finite type: classification and structural properties Thanks: The author is supported by Grigorchuk’s Simons Foundation Grant MP-TSM-00002045 and the department of Mathematics of Texas A&M University.
Santiago Radi

Abstract
. Groups of finite type (also called finitely constrained groups), introduced by Grigorchuk, are known to be the closure of regular branch groups. This article explores many of their properties. Firstly, we prove that being finitely generated, just-infinite and strongly complete are equivalent in a vast family of groups of finite type. As a consequence, we prove that the closure of the Hanoi towers group on 3 pegs is just-infinite although the group itself is not. Secondly, we improve the algorithm given by Bondarenko and Samoilovych in [ 9 ] , to compute all the groups of finite type of a given depth and acting on a given tree. We use this to find the groups of finite type acting on the ternary tree with depth 2 and 3. Thirdly, we give a sufficient condition for a group generated by a finite automaton of Mealy type to have as closure a group of finite type. This allows us to identify groups of finite type as the closure of explicit groups generated by a finite automaton. Lastly, we give an algorithm to prove whether two groups of finite type are isomorphic. With this result, we classify groups of finite type up to isomorphism in the binary tree for depths 2, 3 and 4 and in the ternary tree for depths 2 and 3.

1.
Introduction

A profinite group can be defined as a totally disconnected compact group or as the inverse limit of an inverse system of finite groups. Moreover, it was observed in
[
25
]
that any countably based profinite group can be embedded as a closed subgroup of the group of automorphisms of a spherically homogeneous rooted tree. Three important properties of profinite groups are topologically finitely generation, just-infiniteness and strongly completeness. A profinite group is
topologically finitely generated
if it contains a dense finitely generated subgroup. It is
just-infinite
if every closed non-trivial normal subgroup is of finite index. First results about just-infinite groups were given in
[
29
,
33
]
around 1965. In
[
24
]
, Grigorchuk proved that just-infinite profinite groups split into two categories, being just-infinite branch groups one of the possibilities. In the same article, the author also gave an equivalent condition for a branch group to be just-infinite. In
[
51
]
, Wilson extended his result from
[
50
]
about just-infinite abstract groups to profinite groups, splitting them in those that have finite and infinite structure lattice. In fact, the second class of Wilson’s just-infinite groups coincides with the class of just-infinite branch groups.

A profinite group
G
is
strongly complete
if one of the following equivalent conditions occur:

•

Every subgroup of finite index in
G
is open

•

G
is isomorphic to its profinite completion
\widehat{G}

•

Every group homomorphism from
G
to any other profinite group is continuous.

In other words, this means that the algebraic structure completely determines the topology of
G
.

The problem of determining which profinite groups are strongly complete has been studied by several authors in the last fifty years. In
[
44
]
, Serre proved that topologically finitely generated profinite pro-
p
groups are strongly complete. In
[
40
]
, Pletch generalized the result of Serre, proving that profinite groups with topologically finitely generated pro-
p
-Sylows are strongly complete. Finally, Nikolov and Segal, using the classification of finite simple groups, proved that topologically finitely generated profinite groups are strongly complete in
[
36
]
. Constructions of profinite groups that are not strongly complete also exist (see
[
43
, Example 4.2.13]
).

In 2005, Grigorchuk introduced in
[
21
]
the class of groups of finite type (also known as finitely constrained groups). These groups are profinite groups acting on regular rooted trees whose action locally around every vertex is given by a finite group of allowed actions. This finite group of allowed actions is called the minimal pattern subgroup and it acts on a truncated regular rooted tree with finitely many levels. The number
D
of levels necessary to define the group of finite type is called its depth. The definition of groups of finite type mimics the idea of shifts of finite type, where the set of infinite words in these subshifts are given by those words whose finite snippets avoid a finite set of forbidden words (see
[
31
, Chapter 2]
).

In
[
21
]
, Grigorchuk proved that groups of finite type are regular branch groups (a subfamily of branch groups introduced in
[
24
]
) and in
[
47
]
, Sunic proved the converse, namely, the closure of any regular branch group is a group of finite type. As a consequence, groups of finite type appear to be the closure of many well-studied abstract groups acting on rooted trees such as: the first Grigorchuk group
[
27
]
, Grigorchuk-Gupta-Sidki groups
[
28
]
, the Hanoi towers group
[
22
]
, the iterated monodromy group of the polynomial
z^{2}+i

[
23
]
, iterated wreath products or the whole group of automorphisms of a regular rooted tree. Recently, it has also been shown that certain iterated Galois groups, used in number theory and arithmetic dynamics, are also groups of finite type (see
[
41
]
). In
[
14
]
, the author proved that fractal branch profinite groups are groups of finite type.

The class of groups of finite type has been studied by many authors in the last two decades. For example, in
[
5
,
14
,
37
,
38
,
47
]
, different authors studied possible values of their Hausdorff dimensions, showing that the Hausdorff dimension is always positive (if the group of finite type is not finite), rational in the case of prime power regular trees and calculated the spectrum, namely, all the possible values for the Hausdorff dimension in groups of finite type. In
[
8
,
9
,
48
]
, authors gave necessary and sufficient conditions over the minimal pattern subgroup to know whether a group of finite type is topologically finitely generated. In
[
9
]
, authors computed all groups of finite type of depth
2
,
3
and
4
acting on the binary tree, studying how many of them are topologically finitely generated. Moreover, they gave an explicit description of the minimal pattern subgroups of the topologically finitely generated groups of finite type of depth
4
acting on the binary tree.

If
G
is a group of finite type of depth
D
, its properties are in general studied by understanding the abelianization of the subgroup
\St_{G}(D-1)
, that corresponds to the subgroup of elements in
G
fixing all the vertices at level
D-1
of the tree. In
[
9
, Theorem 3]
, Bondarenko and Samoilovych indirectly proved that just-infinite level-transitive groups of finite type are topologically finitely generated. On the other side, we already observed that Nikolov and Segal proved that topologically finitely generated profinite groups are strongly complete. Let

The condition “

is torsion” has been studied by the author for many groups of finite type and it is suspected that it always holds. Unfortunately, a proof cannot be claimed yet.

Conjecture 2

If

, then
\St_{G}(D-1)/\overline{\St_{G}(D-1)^{\prime}}
is torsion. In particular,

In

, equivalences for

is always torsion and therefore we obtain the following corollary from

Let

and
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
the commutator subgroup of

(i)

(ii)

W_{\mathcal{P}}

(iii)

W_{\mathcal{P}}
is strongly complete.

(iv)

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.

In particular, the whole group of automorphisms of the tree does not have any of these properties.

3
generalizes the result of Bondarenko in
[
8
]
, where he proved that for iterated wreath products, condition (ii) and condition (iv) of

are equivalent.

The second corollary involves the Hanoi towers group on 3 pegs. This group, introduced in
[
22
]
, it is probably the most famous example of a regular branch group that is not just-infinite (see
[
7
, Proposition 3.17]
for a proof of this fact). As the group is regular branch and finitely generated, its closure is a topologically finitely generated group of finite type, and by
1
, we conclude the following:

Corollary 4
.

The closure of the Hanoi towers group on 3 pegs is just-infinite.

This result yields to the first example of a regular branch group that is not just-infinite but its closure is.

We will also be interested in distinguishing when two groups of finite type are isomorphic. Apart from allowing us to do a classification of these groups by isomorphism, the classification has direct applications to abstract groups. Let
G_{1},G_{2}
be two abstract subgroups of
\Aut(T)
, the group of automorphism of a regular rooted tree
T
. For
i=1,2
, let
\overline{G_{i}}
be the closure of
G_{i}
in
\Aut(T)
and
\widehat{G_{i}}
the profinite completion of
G_{i}
. Let us assume that the groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
have the congruence subgroup property, namely,
\widehat{G_{i}}\simeq\overline{G_{i}}
for
i=1,2
. This assumption is known to be true in groups such as the first Grigorchuk Group or GGS groups with no constant defining vector. Assume further that the closure of the groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are groups of finite type, which happens if for example the groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are regular branch. Then, we have two cases: if
\overline{G_{1}}\ncong\overline{G_{2}}
, this means that the profinite completions of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are not isomorphic and therefore, the groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are not isomorphic, giving a tool to distinguish abstract groups. If, contrarily, we have
\overline{G_{1}}\simeq\overline{G_{2}}
and
G_{1}\ncong G_{2}
, this means that the groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are not profinite rigid, leading to another important property of these groups.

To study groups of finite type up to isomorphism, we should start noticing that since they are profinite groups, an isomorphism must be a continuous group isomorphism. To find a condition, denote
G_{\mathcal{P}}
and
G_{\mathcal{Q}}
the groups of finite type, where
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are the respective minimal pattern subgroups. We will see in
Section

7
that we may assume that
G_{\mathcal{P}}
and
G_{\mathcal{Q}}
have the same depth. The first guess one can imagine is that
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is isomorphic to
G_{\mathcal{Q}}
if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{Q}
. However, this will be disproved in
8.3
. Denote by
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}\mathcal{Q}}
the set of elements acting on the truncated tree with
D
levels that conjugate
\mathcal{P}
onto
\mathcal{Q}
. In
Section

7
, we will see that we can endow this set with an equivalence relation and we can endow the set of classes with a graph structure. We will denote this directed graph
\Gamma_{\mathcal{P}\mathcal{Q}}
. Then, the main result is the following:

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
be minimal pattern subgroups of depth
D
and
\Gamma_{\mathcal{P}\mathcal{Q}}
their associated directed graph.

(i)

If
\Gamma_{\mathcal{P}\mathcal{Q}}
has a cycle, then
G_{\mathcal{P}}
and
G_{\mathcal{Q}}
are conjugated in the group of automorphisms of the tree.

(ii)

If
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is fractal, then
G_{\mathcal{P}}
and
G_{\mathcal{Q}}
are conjugated in the group of automorphisms of the tree if and only if
\Gamma_{\mathcal{P}\mathcal{Q}}
has a cycle.

The definition of fractal and equivalences for a group of finite type to be fractal can be found in
Section

4.2
.

As a corollary of
5
and using results of rigidity proved in
[
6
]
and
[
16
]
, we classify groups of finite type of a given depth and acting on a given tree:

Corollary 6
.

We have the following bounds for the amount of isomorphic classes in different trees and with different depths:

(i)

There are 5 isomorphic classes of groups of finite type in the binary tree with depth
2
.

(ii)

There are between 16 and 23 isomorphic classes of groups of finite type in the binary tree with depth
3
.

(iii)

Among the 32 topologically finitely generated groups of finite type in the binary tree of depth
4
, there are exactly 8 different isomorphic classes.

(iv)

There are between 15 and 40 isomorphic classes in the ternary tree with depth
2
.

(v)

Among the 216 topologically finitely generated pro-3-groups of finite type in the ternary tree of depth
3
, there are exactly 12 different isomorphic classes.

As part of
6
, the algorithm presented by Bondarenko and Samoilovich in
[
9
]
to find all minimal pattern subgroups of a certain depth acting on a certain tree will be improved to reach larger depths in bigger trees (see
Section

3
).

By
[
47
]
, the closure of regular branch groups are groups of finite type, but, can we ensure with milder conditions whether an abstract group has as closure a group of finite type? can we explicitly give the depth and minimal pattern subgroup? The following theorem answer this question in the case of groups given by finite automata:

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite automaton and
G(\mathcal{A})
, the group generated by
\mathcal{A}
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a minimal pattern p-subgroup of depth
D
such that its associated group of finite type
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is topologically finitely generated. If the action on the first
D
levels of
G(\mathcal{A})
coincides with the action of
\mathcal{P}
, then
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is the closure of
G(\mathcal{A})
.

Even though
7
gives no information whether the automata group is regular branch or not, it is possible to use the group of finite type that coincides with its closure to answer this question. In
Section

9
, an algorithm and examples are presented.

As the Hausdorff dimension of a group is the same as the Hausdorff dimension of its closure,
7
will be a very good tool to calculate the Hausdorff dimension of automata groups. It will also be a good tool to find automata groups whose closures are given groups of finite type. One corollary is the following:

Corollary 8
.

There exists a
4
-state automaton such that the group generated by this automaton contains the first Grigorchuk group as a subgroup of infinite index and such that its closure equals the closure of the first Grigorchuk group.

Combining
5
with
7
, we also obtain

Corollary 9
.

The closure of the group
\IMG(z^{2}+i)
and the closure of the third Grigorchuk group are isomorphic.

It is known that the third Grigorchuk group has the congruence subgroup property (the proof follows verbatim
[
24
, Proposition 10]
), so its profinite completion is isomorphic to its closure, but it was recently proved in
[
42
]
that
\IMG(z^{2}+i)
does not have the congruence subgroup property, so its profinite completion is not isomorphic to its closure. In virtue of
9

\IMG(z^{2}+i)
are not isomorphic, even though their closures are.

2.2.
Groups acting on rooted trees

A

T

\emptyset

from the root form the

\mathcal{L}_{n}
. The vertices whose distance is at most

will be called

. In this case, we can describe the vertices as finite words on
X={\left\{1,\dots,d\right\}}
.

The group of automorphisms of
T
, denoted
\Aut(T)
, is the group of bijective functions from
T
to
T
that preserve the root and adjacency between vertices. Then,
\Aut(T)
acts on
\mathcal{L}_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
. For any vertex
v
in
T
, the subtree rooted at

which is again a spherically homogeneous infinite rooted tree, is denoted

. Notice also that if
v
and

and

are isomorphic and that if

is
d

is also
d
-regular. In this article, the action of
\Aut(T)
on

will be on the left, so if

and

is a vertex in

, then
(gh)(v)=g(h(v))
.

Given a vertex
v
in
T
, we write

for the

, namely, the subgroup of the elements

such that

, and we call it the

. The subgroup
\mathrm{St}(n)
is a normal subgroup of finite index in

.

We can make

a topological group by declaring

to be a base of neighborhoods of the identity. This topology is called the

and with it,
\Aut(T)
is homeomorphic to a profinite group.

Let
v
be a vertex in
T
,
1\leq n\leq\infty
and

. By preservation of adjacency, we have
g(T_{v})=T_{g(v)}
. The

of

, is the map obtained by restricting the action of

to

. Truncating the action of

to the first

g(vw)=g(v)g|_{v}^{n}(w)

for all

. For

, the permutation

is known as the

of

at

. The collection of all labels
{\left\{g|_{v}^{1}:v\text{ vertex in }T\right\}}
is called the

of

. For every

, we define the map
\pi_{n}:\Aut(T)\to\Aut(T^{n})
given by
\pi_{n}(g):=g|_{\emptyset}^{n}
. The following three properties are satisfied:

(1)

\displaystyle(gh)|_{v}^{n}=g|_{h(v)}^{n}h|_{v}^{n},\qquad(g^{-1})|_{v}^{n}=(g|_{g^{-1}(v)}^{n})^{-1}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad g|_{vw}=(g|_{v})|_{w}.

Sections allow us to have the following isomorphism for any natural number

:

(2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{n}:\Aut(T)&\to\big(\Aut(T_{v_{1}})\times\dotsb\times\Aut(T_{v_{N_{n}}})\big)\rtimes\Aut(T^{n})\\
g&\mapsto(g|_{v_{1}},\dotsc,g|_{v_{N_{n}}})\pi_{n}(g),\end{split}

To describe the elements of

, we will use the isomorphism

Given

\varphi_{v}:\Aut(T)\rightarrow\Aut(T_{v})\text{, given by }g\mapsto g|_{v}.

Let us fix a subgroup

Let

Notice that since

2.3.
Branch groups

Let
T
be a spherically homogeneous rooted tree, a subgroup
G\leq\Aut(T)
, a vertex
v
in
T
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We define the
rigid vertex stabilizer
of
v
(denoted by
\rist_{G}(v)
) to the subgroup of
G
consisting of automorphisms which fix every vertex not in
T_{v}
. We define the
n
-th rigid level stabilizer
and denote it
\RiSt_{G}(n)
to the subgroup generated by the subgroups
\rist_{G}(v)
with
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
. It is well-known that
\RiSt_{G}(n)
is a normal subgroup of

and it is isomorphic to
\prod_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}}\rist(v)
.

We say that a group
G\leq\Aut(T)
is

if
\RiSt_{G}(n)\neq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The following result, due to Abért, is a corollary from
[

, Corollary 1.4]
:

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
G
be a closed weakly branch group and
\mu

\mu({\left\{g\in G:\left|{g}\right|<\infty\right\}}=0.

For branch groups, the definition often used is that the group must be level-transitive and
\RiSt_{G}(n)\leq_{f}G
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. However, for the purpose of this article, we will need a more general definition also given by Grigorchuk in
[
24
, Definition 1]
.

Definition 2.3
.

A group
G
is called branch if there exist a spherically homogeneous infinite rooted tree
T
such that
G\hookrightarrow\Aut(T)
is level-transitive and there exists a family of pairs
{\left\{(H_{n},L_{n})\right\}}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(i)

H_{n}\leq\St(n)
,

(ii)

H_{n}\lhd_{f}G
,

(iii)

H_{n}\simeq\prod_{\mathcal{L}_{n}}L_{n}
, where each copy of
L_{n}
acts non-trivially only on one vertex of
\mathcal{L}_{n}
.

The family of pairs is called a
branch structure
for
G
, and it is not unique. In the case where
G
is a profinite group, we say that
G
is a

if there exists an embedding of
G
into
\Aut(T)
as a closed subgroup and a branch structure defined as before where

is closed for every

.

Recall that a topological infinite group is
just-infinite
if every closed non-trivial normal subgroup has finite index. The main result that it will be used for profinite branch groups in this article is the following:

Theorem 2.4

(
[
24
, Theorem 4]
)
.

Let
G
be a profinite branch group with branch structure
{\left\{(H_{n},L_{n})\right\}}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Then
G
is just-infinite if and only if
L_{n}^{\overline{ab}}
is finite for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

If we now restrict ourselves to work on
d
-regular trees, we can identify
T_{v}
and
T
in a natural way for any
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
, and therefore we can also identify
\Aut(T)
with
\Aut(T_{v})
. We say that a group
G\leq\Aut(T)
is
self-similar
if
g|_{v}\in G
for all
v
vertex in
T
and
g\in G
.

Given a vertex
v
in
T
, define the map
\delta_{v}:\Aut(T)\rightarrow\rist(v)
such that

(4)

\delta_{v}(g)|_{v}=g.

The following properties are easy to check:

(i)

\delta_{v}
is an injective homomorphism of groups.

(ii)

\delta_{v}\circ\delta_{w}=\delta_{vw}
.

If
K
is a subgroup of
\Aut(T)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
geometric product
of
K
on level
n
as

K_{n}:={\left\{g\in\St(n):g|_{v}\in K\text{ for all $v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}$}\right\}}.

Definition 2.5
.

We say that
G\leq\Aut(T)
is regular branch over a subgroup

if
K_{1}\leq_{f}K\leq_{f}G
. The group

is called the branching subgroup.

In the case that

is a self-similar group, the condition
[K:K_{1}]<\infty
need not be checked:

Lemma 2.6
.

Let

be a

-regular tree, a group

self-similar and

. Then,

.

Proof.

Since

is self-similar, we have

and therefore

\displaystyle[K:K_{1}]=[K:K\cap\St_{G}(1)][K\cap\St_{G}(1):K_{1}]\leq[K:\St_{K}(1)][G_{1}:K_{1}]

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\pi_{1}(K)}\right|[G:K]^{d}<\infty.

∎

The following lemma will be used in

5
:

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree and
K\leq\Aut(T)
. Then
(K_{1})^{\prime}=(K^{\prime})_{1}
.

Proof.

The group

is generated by elements of the form

with

. Therefore, there exist
a_{1},\dots,a_{d},b_{1},\dots,b_{d}\in K
such that

and

Conversely, it is not hard to see that

is generated by elements of the form

, where

is a vertex of level one and

is the map defined in

. Then
\delta_{i}([a,b])=[\delta_{i}(a),\delta_{i}(b)]
and each

and

.
∎

:

Let

2.4.
Groups of finite type

Definition 2.9
.

Let
D
be a positive natural number,
T
a
d
-regular rooted tree and
\mathcal{P}
a subgroup of
\Aut(T^{D})
. The group of finite type of depth
D
and set of patterns
\mathcal{P}
is defined as

G_{\mathcal{P}}:={\left\{g\in\Aut(T):g|_{v}^{D}\in\mathcal{P}\text{ for all $v$ vertex in $T$}\right\}}.

A profinite group
G
is said to be of finite type if there exists an embedding to a
d
-regular tree, a number
D\in\mathbb{N}
and a subgroup
\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
such that
G
is isomorphic to
G_{\mathcal{P}}
.

If for example
D=1
, we are forcing the labels
g|_{v}^{1}
of each element
g
in
G_{\mathcal{P}}
to be in a certain subgroup
\mathcal{P}
of
\Sym(d)
. In this case, the group will be isomorphic to the inverse limit of wreath products
\mathcal{P}\wr\mathcal{P}\wr\dots\wr\mathcal{P}
. This latter inverse limit group is known as
iterated wreath product
and it will be denoted
W_{\mathcal{P}}
. Notice that if
\mathcal{P}=1
, then
W_{\mathcal{P}}=1
and if
\mathcal{P}=\Sym(d)
, then
W_{\mathcal{P}}=\Aut(T)
. In the case that
d=q
is a prime power, and
\mathcal{P}={\left\langle{(1,\dots,q)}\right\rangle}
where
(1,\dots,q)
is the cyclic permutation, we will denote the iterated wreath product as
W_{q}
.

The following lemma will be used in
Section

7
:

Lemma 2.10
.

If
p
is prime and
G\leq W_{p}
is infinite and self-similar, then
G
is level-transitive.

Proof.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, choose
w_{n}
any vertex on level
n
. As
G
is infinite and
\st_{G}(w_{n})
has finite index in
G
, then also
\st_{G}(w_{n})
is infinite, so we can find
g\in\st_{G}(w_{n})
non-trivial. Let
m
be the smallest level such that
g|_{u_{m}}^{1}\neq 1
for some vertex
u_{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{m}
below
w_{n}
, and write
u_{m}=u^{\prime}_{m-n}v_{n}
where
u^{\prime}_{m-n}\in\mathcal{L}_{m-n}
. As
m
is the smallest, this means that
g\in\st_{G}(u_{m})
. Since
G
is self-similar, then
g|_{u^{\prime}_{m-n}}\in\st_{G}(v_{n})
and acts non-trivially on the immediate descendants of
v_{n}
. Since
G\leq W_{p}
, the action of the powers of
g|_{u^{\prime}_{m-n}}
below

must be transitive. The result follows by
2.1
.
∎

The following results about groups of finite type are well-known:

Proposition 2.11

(

If

-regular tree

(

Groups of finite type are the closure of regular branch groups. Furthermore if

is a regular branch group branching over a subgroup

containing

Lemma 2.13

(
[
47
, Lemma 10]
)
.

Let

be a
d
-regular tree and

be a self-similar regular branch group, branching over a subgroup

containing

. Then, for all

\St_{G}(n)=(\St_{G}(m))_{n-m},

where the right-hand side corresponds to the geometric product of

.

2.13
is the key result to give a branch structure for groups of finite type:

Lemma 2.14
.

If
G
is a group of finite type of depth
D
embedded in a

{\left\{(\St_{G}(n),\St_{G}(D-1))\right\}}

By

,

, normal and with finite index. Finally,

justifies condition (iii).
∎

As a corollary of

and

If

To finish, we prove that infinite groups of finite type are weakly branch:

Lemma 2.16
.

If
G
is a group of finite type of depth
D
embedded in a
d
-regular tree
T
, then
\St_{G}(n+D-1)\leq\RiSt_{G}(n)
for all

. In particular, if

is infinite, then it is weakly branch.

Proof.

If
g\in\St_{G}(n+D-1)
, for each vertex

we have

. Then
\delta_{v}(g|_{v})\in\rist_{G}(v)

3.
Algorithms to find minimal pattern subgroups

3.1.
Minimal pattern subgroups

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree and
D
a natural number. Then
\mathcal{L}_{D}
has
d^{D}
vertices that can be labeled from left to right as the numbers from
1
to
d^{D}
. This allows us to describe elements in
\Aut(T^{D})
as permutations in
\Sym(d^{D})
that will facilitate the description of explicit examples.

Example 3.1
.

In the
2
-regular tree with depth
D=2
, consider
\mathcal{P}={\left\langle{\alpha}\right\rangle}
where
\alpha=(1,2)(3,4)
. Its portrait is shown in the
Figure

2
, where
\sigma
is the non-trivial permutation of
\Sym(2)
.

\id
\sigma
\sigma

Figure 2
.
Portrait of the element
\alpha
.

Notice that if an element
g\in G_{\mathcal{P}}
had a vertex
v
in
T
such that
g|_{v}^{2}=\alpha
, then the vertices underneath
v
would have to have a pattern that starts with
\sigma
, but
\mathcal{P}
does not contain any pattern like this. Hence,
G_{\mathcal{P}}=1
.

3.1
suggests the existence of a minimal pattern subgroup, that is justified in the following proposition:

Proposition 3.2

(
[
9
, Section “Minimal patterns groups”]
)
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
\mathcal{Q}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
and
G_{\mathcal{Q}}
the group of finite type associated to
\mathcal{Q}
. Define the subgroup
\mathcal{P}=\pi_{D}(G_{\mathcal{Q}})
. Then
G_{\mathcal{Q}}=G_{\mathcal{P}}
and
\mathcal{P}\leq\mathcal{Q}
.

If
G
is a group of finite type of depth
D
, the subgroup
\mathcal{P}
given in
3.2
is called the
minimal pattern subgroup
.

In
[
9
, “Pattern graph”]
, authors provide an algorithm that given a subgroup
H\leq\Aut(T^{D})
, it returns
\mathcal{P}(H)
, the associated minimal pattern subgroup. The algorithm implies constructing a labeled directed graph whose vertices are the patterns in
H
, and the edges are labeled with the vertices at level one of the tree. Two patterns
a
and
b
are joined by an arrow from
a
to
b
with label
x
if the pattern
a
can be extended using the pattern
b
through the vertex
x
. The algorithm deletes those patterns that cannot be extended along some vertex of the tree and when the reduction is no longer possible, the remaining patterns conform
\mathcal{P}(H)
, the minimal pattern subgroup associated to
H
. The problem with this algorithm is that when
H
is too big, the number of vertices and edges in the graph increases considerably and no computer can take a reasonable time to find
\mathcal{P}(H)
. Nevertheless, the algorithm can be improved by using the group structure of
H
and
\mathcal{P}(H)
.

3.2.
Improving the algorithm that finds
\mathcal{P}(H)

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number,
p\in\Aut(T^{D})
a pattern and
x\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
a vertex in level one. The notation
p|_{x}
will refer to the section of
D-1
levels of
p
at the vertex
x\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
. In particular,
p|_{x}\in\pi_{D-1}(\Aut(T^{D-1}))
.

Definition 3.3
.

Given
H
a subgroup of
\Aut(T^{D})
, we say that
K\leq H
is closed under patterns of
H
if for all
k\in K
and
x\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
then
k|_{x}\in\pi_{D-1}(H)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number and
\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
. Then,
\mathcal{P}
is a minimal pattern subgroup if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is closed under patterns of itself.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
If
\mathcal{P}
is a minimal pattern subgroup then there exists
G
a group of finite type of depth
D
such that
\pi_{D}(G)=\mathcal{P}
. Then, if
p\in\mathcal{P}
and
x\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
, there exists
g\in G
such that
g|_{\emptyset}^{D}=p
and since
G
is self-similar, also
g|_{x}^{D-1}=p|_{x}\in\pi_{D-1}(\mathcal{P})
.

(\Leftarrow)
Conversely, consider
G=G_{\mathcal{P}}
and call
\mathcal{Q}=\pi_{D}(G)
the minimal pattern subgroup of
G
. By
3.2
, we know that
\mathcal{Q}\leq\mathcal{P}
and the goal is to prove they are equal. Start with
p\in\mathcal{P}
. Since
p|_{x}\in\pi_{D-1}(\mathcal{P})
because
\mathcal{P}
is closed under patterns of itself, there exists a pattern
q\in\mathcal{P}
such that
p|_{x}=\pi_{D-1}(q)
. Gluing
q
to
p
and doing this for every vertex
x\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
, we extend
p
with depth
D
to an element defined until level
D+1
. But then, each pattern of depth
D
ending the element created so far can also be extended by the property of
\mathcal{P}
, allowing us to construct an element
g\in G
such that
g|_{\emptyset}^{D}=p
. Then
p\in\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{Q}
, implying that
\mathcal{P}
is the minimal pattern subgroup.
∎

In particular by
3.4
, the subgroup
\mathcal{P}(H)
is closed under patterns of
H
. The following lemmas have as goal to prove that
H
has a subgroup that is maximal among the subgroups of
H
closed under patterns of
H
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number,
H
a subgroup of
\Aut(T^{D})
,
K
a subgroup of
H
and
S
a generating set of
K
. Then,
K
is closed under patterns of
H
if and only if for all
s\in S
and
x\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
we have
s|_{x}\in\pi_{D-1}(H)
.

Proof.

Direct is straightforward. For the converse, we apply induction on the length of
g
as a word in
S
. If the length is
1
then
g=s^{\pm 1}
. The case
g=s
is the hypothesis. If
g=s^{-1}
, then
(s^{-1})|_{x}=(s|_{s^{-1}(x)})^{-1}\in\pi_{D-1}(H)
because
H
is a subgroup. Similarly, since
(gh)|_{x}=g|_{h(x)}\,h|_{x}
and each term is in
\pi_{D-1}(H)
, then
(gh)|_{x}\in\pi_{D-1}(H)
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number,
H
a subgroup of
\Aut(T^{D})
and
K_{1},K_{2}\leq H
closed under patterns of
H
. Then
{\left\langle{K_{1},K_{2}}\right\rangle}
is closed under patterns of
H
.

Proof.

If
S_{i}
is a generating set of
K_{i}
for
i=1,2
, then
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
is a generating set of
{\left\langle{K_{1},K_{2}}\right\rangle}
. Since
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are closed under patterns of
H
, then
{\left\langle{K_{1},K_{2}}\right\rangle}
is closed under patterns of
H
by
3.5
.
∎

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number and let
\mathcal{L}
be the lattice of subgroups of
\Aut(T^{D})
ordered by inclusion. If
H\geq K
, we can put a distance between them, defining
d(H,K)
as the smallest
n
such that there is a chain of subgroups
H=H_{0}\gneq H_{1}\gneq\dots\gneq H_{n}=K
where
H_{i+i}
is a maximal subgroup of
H_{i}
for all
i=0,\dots,n-1
. Clearly
d(H,H)=0
and it is not hard to see that if
H\geq K\geq J
, then
d(H,J)\leq d(H,K)+d(K,J)
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number,
H\leq\Aut(T^{D})
and
K_{1}
the closest subgroup of
H
closed under patterns of
H
. If
K_{2}
is another subgroup of
H
closed under patterns of
H
, then
K_{2}\leq K_{1}
.

Proof.

If
K_{2}
is trivial there is nothing to do. Suppose that
K_{2}
is not trivial. By closeness of
K_{1}
to
H
, then necessarily
K_{1}
is also non-trivial. Now, if
K_{2}\nleq K_{1}
, then
K_{1}<{\left\langle{K_{1},K_{2}}\right\rangle}\leq H
and by
3.6
, the join is closed under patterns of
H
. However, this contradicts the fact that
K_{1}
was the closest to
H
.
∎

3.7
justifies the existence of a subgroup
\mathfrak{P}(H)
that is maximal between the subgroups of
H
that are closed under patterns of
H
. Since
\mathcal{P}(H)
is closed under patterns of
H
, necessarily
\mathcal{P}(H)\leq\mathfrak{P}(H)
.

The next step is to find an efficient algorithm that computes
\mathfrak{P}(H)
. Let
S
be a generating set of
H
. We can separate
S
in a disjoint union of sets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
such that
s\in S_{1}
if and only if
s|_{x}\in\pi_{D-1}(H)
for all
x\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
. Let
K={\left\langle{S_{1}}\right\rangle}
. By
3.5
,
K
is closed under patterns of
H
and by
3.7
we have that
K\leq\mathfrak{P}(H)
. We can use any generating set
S
but there is a compromise between choosing
S
big or small. If it is big, there are more chances to have
S_{1}
also big and consequently
K={\left\langle{S_{1}}\right\rangle}
big as well, but on the other side, the bigger
S
is, the harder is for the software to do calculations with
H={\left\langle{S}\right\rangle}
. Furthermore, finding useful extra elements
s
to add in a generating set
S
is not easy to control.

Once
K
is obtained, consider
H/K
. Observe that if
t_{1}\equiv t_{2}\pmod{K}
, then
t_{1}\in\mathfrak{P}(H)
if and only if
t_{2}\in\mathfrak{P}(H)
because
K\leq\mathfrak{P}(H)
. Therefore, a coclass of
H/K
is included in
\mathfrak{P}(H)
independently of the representative of
H/K
we choose. Take
T
a set of representatives of
H/K
and let
t\in T\setminus K
. If
t|_{x}\in\pi_{D-1}(H)
for all
x\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
, this means that
t\in\mathfrak{P}(H)
, so we can replace
K
by
{\left\langle{K,t}\right\rangle}
(that is necessarily bigger than the old
K
) and recalculate
H/K
. Since
H
is a finite group, once we find a
K
such that for some set of representatives
T
of
H/K
, no
t\in\mathfrak{P}(H)
, that means that this latter
K
equals
\mathfrak{P}(H)
.

The last ingredient for the algorithm is the following:

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number and
S
a generating set of
\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
. Then
\mathcal{P}
is a minimal pattern subgroup if and only if for all
s\in S
and
x\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
, we have
s|_{x}\in\pi_{D-1}(\mathcal{P})
.

Proof.

By
3.4
, the subgroup
\mathcal{P}
is a minimal pattern subgroup if and only if it is closed under patterns of itself. Then, the result follows by
3.5
applied to
K=H=\mathcal{P}
.
∎

Finally, the algorithm to find
\mathcal{P}(H)
is as follows:

(1)

Take
S
a minimal generating set of
H
. If
3.8
applies, then
\mathcal{P}(H)=H
.

(2)

If not, we look for
\mathfrak{P}(H)
as it was explained before. Since step 1 did not apply, necessarily
\mathfrak{P}(H)<H
. On the other side, we know that
\mathcal{P}(H)\leq\mathfrak{P}(H)
, so we may replace
H
by
\mathfrak{P}(H)
and start step 1 again.

3.3.
Finding all minimal pattern subgroups

We now focus on constructing an algorithm such that given
d
and
D
gives us all the minimal pattern subgroups in a
d
-regular tree with depth
D
. Since, the number of subgroups that we would have to check is huge as
d
and
D
increase, some theoretic tools are developed to help in the search.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number, and
\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
. If
\mathcal{P}
is a non-trivial minimal pattern subgroup, then
\mathcal{P}\nsubseteq\pi_{D}(\St(1))
.

Proof.

The subgroup
\mathcal{P}
is non-trivial so there is an element
g\in G_{\mathcal{P}}
such that
g|_{v}^{1}\neq 1
at some vertex
v
. Then
g|_{v}^{D}\in\mathcal{P}
and it is not in
\pi_{D}(\St(1))
.
∎

3.9
explains why the subgroup given in
3.1
is not a minimal pattern subgroup.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,

4.
Hausdorff dimension, fractality and torsion

4.1.
Hausdorff dimension

The Hausdorff dimension of a group is typically used in geometric group theory to have another way to measure the size of a group with respect to a group that contains it. The study of the Hausdorff dimension on profinite groups was initiated by Abercrombie in
[
1
]
and by Barnea and Shalev in
[
4
]
, and in the case of groups acting on trees, many interesting results have been proved (see for example
[
3
,
14
,
45
]
).

Let
T
be a spherically homogeneous infinite rooted tree and
H\leq G\leq\Aut(T)
. The
relative Hausdorff dimension
of
H
in
G
is defined as the number

\mathcal{H}_{G}(H):=\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\log(\left|{\pi_{n}(H)}\right|)}{\log(\left|{\pi_{n}(G)}\right|)}.

The
Hausdorff dimension
of
H
is defined as

\mathcal{H}(H):=\mathcal{H}_{\Aut(T)}(H).

Given
\mathcal{P}
a minimal pattern subgroup of depth
D
in a
d
-regular tree
T
, define

\St_{\mathcal{P}}(D-1):=\pi_{D}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1)).

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number and
\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
a minimal pattern subgroup. Then

(5)

\left|{\pi_{n}(G_{\mathcal{P}})}\right|=\left|{\mathcal{P}}\right|\left|{\St_{\mathcal{P}}(D-1)}\right|^{d+\dots+d^{n-D}}

for all
n\geq D
. In particular, its Hausdorff dimension is

(6)

\mathcal{H}(G_{\mathcal{P}})=\frac{1}{d^{D-1}}\frac{\log(\left|{\St_{\mathcal{P}}(D-1)}\right|)}{\log(d!)}.

Proof.

The formula of
\left|{\pi_{n}(G_{\mathcal{P}})}\right|
is deduced in
[
9
, Proposition 1]
. To calculate the Hausdorff dimension, recall that
\Aut(T)
is a group of finite type with depth
1
and
\mathcal{P}=\Sym(d)
. Then
\St_{\Sym(d)}(1-1)=\Sym(d)
and therefore

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(G_{\mathcal{P}})=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log(\left|{\pi_{n}(G_{\mathcal{P}})}\right|}{\log(\left|{\pi_{n}(\Aut(T))}\right|)}=\frac{1}{d^{D-1}}\frac{\log(\left|{\St_{\mathcal{P}}(D-1)}\right|)}{\log(d!)}.

∎

We obtain two corollaries from
4.1
:

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number and
\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
a minimal pattern subgroup. The following statements are equivalent:

•

G_{\mathcal{P}}
is finite,

•

\St_{\mathcal{P}}(D-1)
is trivial,

•

G_{\mathcal{P}}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}
,

•

\mathcal{H}(G_{\mathcal{P}})=0
.

Proof.

A profinite group as
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is finite if and only if there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
then
\left|{\pi_{n_{0}}(G_{\mathcal{P}})}\right|=\left|{\pi_{n}(G_{\mathcal{P}})}\right|
. By

, the sequence
{\left\{\left|{\pi_{n}(G_{\mathcal{P}})}\right|\right\}}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
stabilizes if and only if
\St_{\mathcal{P}}(D-1)
is trivial and it does stabilize with

, so
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is finite if and only if it is isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}
. By
Equation

6
, this implies that
\mathcal{H}(G_{\mathcal{P}})
is zero. Conversely, if
\mathcal{H}(G_{\mathcal{P}})=0
, then
\St_{\mathcal{P}}(D-1)
must be trivial.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,

a natural number and
\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
a minimal pattern subgroup. Then
\mathcal{H}(G_{\mathcal{P}})=1
if and only if

.

Proof.

The group
\St_{\mathcal{P}}(D-1)
is a subgroup of
\pi_{D}(\St(D-1))
and this latter is isomorphic to

. If
\mathcal{H}(G_{\mathcal{P}})=1

for

and we want to show that

and consider

such that

for all

. Since

such that

4.2.
Fractality

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree. Recall the map
\varphi_{v}
defined in
Equation

3
. We say that a group
G\leq\Aut(T)
is
fractal
if
G
is self-similar, level-transitive and
\varphi_{v}(\st_{G}(v))=G
for all
v
vertex in
T
. We say that
G
is
strongly fractal
if
G
is self-similar, level-transitive and
\varphi_{v}(\St_{G}(1))=G
for all
v\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
. Finally we say that a group
G\leq\Aut(T)
is
super strongly fractal
if
G
is self-similar, level-transitive and
\varphi_{v}(\St_{G}(n))=G
for all
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
and
n\geq 1
.

Fractality responds to the level of self-repetition that the group has. Fractal properties of self-similar groups were recently related to the dynamics and ergodic properties of self-similar profinite groups (see
[
13
]
), to the action of random subgroups in profinite groups (see
[
18
]
) and to density problems in number theory (see
[
17
]
).

The three notions of fractality are not equivalent, as it was proved in
[
49
, Section 3 and Proposition 4.3]
, but the following implications are true:

\text{Super strongly fractal}\Rightarrow\text{Strongly fractal}\Rightarrow\text{Fractal}.

The following proposition summarizes necessary and sufficient conditions for a group of finite type to be fractal, strongly fractal or super strongly fractal. For convenience, we set the notation
\varphi^{D-1}_{v}
to refer to
\pi_{D-1}\circ\varphi_{v}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number and
\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
a minimal pattern subgroup.

(1)

If
D=1
, then
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is super strongly fractal if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is transitive.

(2)

If
D\geq 2
, then
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is fractal if and only if
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is transitive on the first level and there exists
v_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
such that
\varphi_{v_{0}}^{D-1}(\st_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(v_{0}))=\pi_{D-1}(\mathcal{P})
.

(3)

If
D\geq 2
, then
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is strongly fractal if and only if
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is transitive on the first level and there exists
v_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
such that
\varphi_{v_{0}}^{D-1}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(1))=\pi_{D-1}(\mathcal{P})
.

(4)

If
D\geq 2
, then
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is super strongly fractal if and only if
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is transitive on the first level and there exists
v_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{D-1}
such that we have the equality
\varphi_{v_{0}}^{D-1}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1))=\pi_{D-1}(\mathcal{P})
.

Proof.

For (1), if
D=1
, the group of finite type is the iterated wreath product
W_{\mathcal{P}}
. In particular, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any vertex
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
, we have
\varphi_{v}(\St_{W_{\mathcal{P}}}(n))=W_{\mathcal{P}}
. So, the group
W_{\mathcal{P}}
is super strongly fractal if and only if it is level-transitive and this is if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is transitive.

For (2), the direct, if
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is fractal and we take any vertex
v_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
, then
\varphi_{v_{0}}(\st_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(v_{0}))=G_{\mathcal{P}}
. So, if we restrict to the first
D-1
levels, we obtain the result.

For the converse, let
g\in G_{\mathcal{P}}
and
p=\pi_{D-1}(g)
. Then, there exists
s_{1}\in\st_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(v_{0})
such that
(s_{1})|_{v_{0}}^{D-1}=p
. Call
g_{1}=(s_{1})|_{v_{0}}
. By self-similarity of
G_{\mathcal{P}}
we have that
g_{1}\in G_{\mathcal{P}}
and therefore
g_{1}^{-1}g\in\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1)
. Define
s=\delta_{v_{0}}(g_{1}^{-1}g)
where
\delta_{v_{0}}
is the funciton defined in
Equation

4
. Then
s\in\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D)
, the element
s_{1}s\in\st_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(v_{0})
and

(s_{1}s)|_{v_{0}}=s_{1}\mid_{v_{0}}\cdot s\mid_{v_{0}}=g.

This proves that
\varphi_{v_{0}}(\st_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(v_{0}))=G_{\mathcal{P}}
. Then, if
v
is another vertex in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
, by transitivity on the first level, there exists
h\in G_{\mathcal{P}}
such that
h(v_{0})=v
. This gives that

\varphi_{v}(\st_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(v))=h\mid_{v_{0}}\varphi_{v_{0}}(\st_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(v_{0}))(h\mid_{v_{0}})^{-1}=G_{\mathcal{P}}.

Finally if
v
is a vertex on level
n
, we proceed by induction on
n
. The case
n=1
is the previous paragraph. Then, let
g\in G_{\mathcal{P}}
,
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
and write

with
v_{1}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
. So, there exists
g_{n-1}\in\st_{G}(w_{n-1})
such that
(g_{n-1})|_{w_{n-1}}=g
. By the hypothesis, there exists
g_{n}\in\st_{G}(v_{1})
such that
(g_{n})|_{v_{1}}=g_{n-1}
and combining everything, we see that

(g_{n})|_{v}=((g_{n})|_{v_{1}})|_{w_{n-1}}=(g_{n-1})|_{w_{n-1}}=g

and
g_{n}(v)=g_{n}(v_{1})(g_{n})|_{v_{1}}(w_{n-1})=v_{1}w_{n-1}=v
, so
g_{n}\in\st_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(v)
as we wanted. The level-transitivity follows from the fact that
G_{\mathcal{P}}
acts transitively on the first level.

For (3), the proof follows the same ideas as the previous point. The direct follows as in (2). For the converse, repeating the process in (2), we conclude that
\varphi_{v_{0}}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(1))=G_{\mathcal{P}}
and by transitivity on the first level that
\varphi_{v}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(1))=G_{\mathcal{P}}
for all
v\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
. Then, since this condition implies fractal and it is transitive on the first level, it is level-transitive by point (2).

For (4), the direct follows as in (2). For the converse, let
g\in G_{\mathcal{P}}
and let
p=\pi_{D-1}(g)
. Then, there exists
s_{1}\in\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1)
such that
(s_{1})|_{v_{0}}^{D-1}=p
. Call
g_{1}=(s_{1})|_{v_{0}}
. By self-similarity of
G_{\mathcal{P}}
we have that
g_{1}\in G_{\mathcal{P}}
and therefore
g_{1}^{-1}g\in\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1)
. Define again
s=\delta_{v_{0}}(g_{1}^{-1}g)
. Then
s\in\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(2D-2)
, the element
s_{1}s\in\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1)
and

(s_{1}s)|_{v_{0}}=s_{1}\mid_{v_{0}}\cdot s\mid_{v_{0}}=g.

This proves that
\varphi_{v_{0}}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1))=G_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Write
v_{0}=w_{0}u_{0}
with
u_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
and
n\leq D-1
. Then, given
g\in G_{\mathcal{P}}
, by the previous paragraph we know there exists
s\in\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1)
such that

g=s|_{v_{0}}=s|_{w_{0}u_{0}}=(s|_{w_{0}})|_{u_{0}}.

By self-similarity, we know that
s|_{w_{0}}\in\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(n)
and so

. Again by self-similarity, we know that
G_{\mathcal{P}}\geq\varphi_{u_{0}}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(n))
, concluding that for any level

, there exists
u_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
such that
G_{\mathcal{P}}=\varphi_{u_{0}}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(n))
.

In the particular case that
n=1
, we have that
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is fractal by (2), so
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is level-transitive. Therefore, given
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
with
n\leq D-1
, there exists
h\in G_{\mathcal{P}}
such that
h(u_{0})=v
with
u_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
and
G_{\mathcal{P}}=\varphi_{u_{0}}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(n))
. Applying the same trick used in (2),

\varphi_{v}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(n))=h\mid_{u_{0}}\varphi_{u_{0}}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(n))(h\mid_{u_{0}})^{-1}=G_{\mathcal{P}}.

Finally, if

with

. Then, by

\varphi_{v}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(n))=\varphi_{w}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1))=G_{\mathcal{P}}.

∎

5.1.
Strongly completeness

Let
T
be a spherically homogeneous infinite rooted tree and
G
a subgroup of
\Aut(T)
. Since
\Aut(T)
is a profinite group with
{\left\{\St(n):n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}
as a base of open neighborhoods of the identity, then the closure of
G
in
\Aut(T)
is isomorphic to

\overline{G}=\varprojlim G/\St_{G}(n)

by
[
43
, Corollary 1.1.8]
. In the case that
\RiSt_{G}(n)\leq_{f}G
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\widetilde{G}:=\varprojlim G/\RiSt_{G}(n)

is also a profinite group and since
\RiSt_{G}(n)\leq\St_{G}(n)
we have a well-defined surjective homomorphism

\psi_{1}:\widetilde{G}\rightarrow\overline{G}.

The kernel of
\psi_{1}
is called the
rigid kernel
.

Another standard construction in group theory is the
profinite completion
of a group, defined as the inverse limit

\widehat{G}:=\varprojlim G/N,

where the limit runs over all normal subgroups
N
of finite index in
G
. In the case that
\RiSt_{G}(n)\leq_{f}G
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the family of rigid stabilizers is a subset of the family of normal subgroups of finite index of
G
, and therefore we have a well-defined surjective homomorphism

\psi_{2}:\widehat{G}\rightarrow\widetilde{G}.

The kernel of
\psi_{2}
is called the
branch kernel
. Composing the last two maps, we obtain an epimorphism

\psi_{3}=\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}:\widehat{G}\rightarrow\overline{G}.

The kernel of
\psi_{3}
is called the
congruence kernel
.

An abstract group
G
is said to have the
congruence subgroup property
if the map
\psi_{3}
is an isomorphism. In the case that
G
is a profinite group, we say that
G
is
strongly complete
.

The following equivalences are well-known results:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
T
be a spherically homogeneous infinite rooted tree and a subgroup
G\leq\Aut(T)
. Then

(1)

The rigid kernel is trivial if and only if for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\RiSt_{G}(n)\geq\St_{G}(m)
.

(2)

The branch kernel is trivial if and only if for any
N\lhd_{f}G
, there exists

has an extension to an element in

with finite order in

Now, set

. By

Therefore,

\exp(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1)^{\overline{ab}})=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\exp(\pi_{n}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1))^{ab}),

where we also have

\exp(\pi_{n}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1))^{ab})\,\,|\,\,\exp(\pi_{n+1}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1))^{ab})

for all

So, in order to prove the result, we will apply induction on

If

, we have

\pi_{n}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1))^{ab}=\St_{\mathcal{P}}(D-1)^{ab}

Assume the result holds until level

. Then, the element

is the extension of some

. Therefore, there exists

such that

and

(9)

\displaystyle 1=\pi_{n+1}(g_{\alpha}^{e})=(gs)^{e}\equiv g^{e}s^{e}\pmod{\pi_{n+1}(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1)^{\prime})}.

Since

is a group of finite type of depth

, by

we have the equality
\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D)=(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1))_{1}
. Then, by

\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D)^{\prime}=(\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1)_{1})^{\prime}=(\St_{G}(D-1)^{\prime})_{1},

and taking closures,

\displaystyle\overline{\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1)^{\prime}}\geq\overline{\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D)^{\prime}}=\left(\overline{\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1)^{\prime}}\right)_{1}.

Write

with

for all

and then by

Replacing in

as we wanted.

Automata groups and groups of finite type

In this section we will prove

An
automaton
is a tuple
\mathcal{A}=(Q,X,\tau)
where
Q
is a set that represents the states,
X
is another set that represents the alphabet and
\tau:Q\times X\rightarrow X\times Q
is the transition function. An automaton is said to be finite if the number of states in
Q
is finite. If each state in the diagram has
d
outgoing arrows and when we gather the labels of these
d
arrows we have a permutation of
\Sym(d)
, then the automata is called
invertible
. If
\mathcal{A}
is an invertible automaton, given a state
q\in Q
, we can construct an automorphism
g_{q}\in\Aut(T)
by identifying
T
with the free monoid
X^{*}
of finite words in
X
and iterating the transition function with the words in
X^{*}
as the inputs. The automata group generated by the automorphisms
{\left\{g_{q}:q\in Q\right\}}
will be denoted
G(\mathcal{A})
. Known examples of automata groups are the first Grigorchuk group
[
27
]
and iterated monodromy groups
[
34
, Chapter 5]
. Note that automata groups are self-similar by construction.

By
Theorem

2.12
, we know that if an automata group is regular branch, then its closure is a group of finite type. However, the following corollary of
Theorem

shows that with a milder condition, the result is still true:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number and
\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
a minimal pattern
p
-subgroup. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite invertible automaton acting on an alphabet of
d
letters. If
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is topologically finitely generated and
\pi_{D}(G(\mathcal{A}))=\mathcal{P}
, then
\overline{G(\mathcal{A})}=G_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
has
r
states and let
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}
be the elements in
G(\mathcal{A})
generated by these states. Since
\mathcal{A}
is an automaton, given

, there exist

and

such that
a_{i}=(a_{i_{1}},\dots,a_{i_{d}})\sigma_{i}
. By induction, this implies that given

and

, there exists

such that

Since

7.
Classification of groups of finite type up to isomorphism

In this section we will give tools to prove whether two groups of finite type acting on the same tree are isomorphic or not. In particular, we will give the proof of
5
. Notice that since groups of finite type are profinite groups, an isomorphism in this context means that the groups are isomorphic as abstract groups and homeomorphic as topological groups.

Let
G_{1}
and

be two groups of finite type acting on the same
d
-regular tree

\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
and
\mathcal{Q}\leq\Aut(T^{E})
such that

and
G_{2}\simeq G_{\mathcal{Q}}
. Although at the beginning they may have different depth, we may assume that the depth is the same by using the following lemma:

Lemma 7.1
.

Let

be a
d
-regular tree and
G
a group of finite type group of depth

acting on
T
. Then

is a group of finite type group of depth

.

Proof.

Consider

the natural projection. If
G
is a group of finite type of depth

, then there exists
\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
such that

. Take
\mathcal{Q}=\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{P})
. We want to prove that
G_{\mathcal{P}}=G_{\mathcal{Q}}
.

If
g\in G_{\mathcal{Q}}
and

is a vertex in

, then

. If

, then for every vertex

in

. Therefore

Lemma 7.5
.

Let

be a
d
-regular tree,

for all

. In other words, we can replace the “for all elements in the class

Proof.

Direct is straightforward. For the converse, let

. Then, there exists

such that

. Since

is a minimal pattern subgroup, there exist

.

.

Let

(2)

For (1)
\Rightarrow
(2), by (a), the pattern

, and by (b), patterns in the same level are in the same class of

, so we can label

the class corresponding to

for any vertex

. By the existence of

for all

and

For (2)

(1), let

and take any

for all

.

.

The converse is not far to be true:

Theorem 7.8
.

Let

be a

-regular tree,

\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}\leq\Aut(T^{D})

Proof.

For the direct, by

(a)

for all

(b)

7.2.
Results of rigidity for groups of finite type

We have characterized what a conjugation between two groups of finite type must satisfy. The problem is that we do not know if all the isomorphisms between groups of finite type must be conjugations. In this subsection, we will adapt known results of rigidity existing in the literature to groups of finite type.

Given
T
a spherically homogeneous tree, we say that a group
G\leq\Aut(T)
is
T
-rigid
if the unique kinds of isomorphisms of
G
in
\Aut(T)
are conjugations by elements in
\Aut(T)
.

Theorem 7.9

(
[
35
, Proposition 2.4.49]
)
.

Let
T
be a spherically homogeneous tree and
G\leq\Aut(T)
such that
\rist_{G}(v)
acts level-transitively on the vertices below
T_{v}
for all
v
vertex in
T
. Then
G
is
T
-rigid.

If
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is a group of finite type, let us denote

the maximal regular branching subgroup of
G_{\mathcal{P}}
. The existence of such a subgroup is justified in
2.8
and more results about it will be obtained in
Section

.

Proposition 7.10
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number, and
\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
a minimal pattern subgroup. If
K_{\mathcal{P}}
acts transitively on the first level of

, then
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is
T
-rigid.

Proof.

By definition of the geometric product,
(K_{\mathcal{P}})_{n}\leq\RiSt_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, if
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
, then
\varphi_{v}(\rist_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(v))\geq K_{\mathcal{P}}
and as
K_{\mathcal{P}}
acts transitively on the first level of
T
, then
\rist_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(v)
acts transitively on the first level of
T_{v}
. Iterating this process, we obtain level-transitivity of
\rist_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(v)
below
T_{v}
.
∎

In the case that

is a prime number and

force

to be

. Indeed, if

, the maximal regular branching subgroup of

Theorem 7.11

(

]
that ensures rigidity for a larger family of groups. Our goal now is to understand the conditions (*) and (N) when we restrict to groups of finite type.

Let us denote

the rightmost vertex on level

-regular tree

Clearly, the number of vertices of

is an infinite group included in

Lemma 7.12

Let

be a
p
-regular tree with

prime,

a natural number, and
\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})

The first step is to prove that any pattern

is different to zero.

As

is infinite, there exists a non-trivial pattern

for some

different to zero. Taking a power of

if necessary, we may assume that

By

is level-transitive, so there exists

7.3.
Other strategies

If the results in
Section

7.2
do not apply, the following tool can be useful when the groups of finite type are just-infinite.

Definition 7.15
.

Denote
\mathfrak{G}
the category of groups. We say that a variant functor
\mathcal{F}:\mathfrak{G}\rightarrow\mathfrak{G}
is a
characteristic functor
if it satisfies the following properties:

(1)

\mathcal{F}(G)
is a characteristic subgroup of
G
for all
G\in\mathfrak{G}
,

(2)

for all
f\in\Hom(G_{1},G_{2})
, the function
\mathcal{F}(f):\mathcal{F}(G_{1})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(G_{2})
is defined as
\mathcal{F}(f)(x)=f(x)
,

(3)

if
f
is surjective, then
\mathcal{F}(f)
is also surjective.

If in (2) we use the inclusion map, we obtain that if
H\leq G
, then
\mathcal{F}(H)\leq\mathcal{F}(G)
. Also, (2) and (3) imply that
\mathcal{F}(f(G_{1}))=f(\mathcal{F}(G_{1}))
. Examples of these functors are
G\mapsto G^{(k)}
and
G\mapsto\gamma_{k}(G)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Hence, if
G_{\mathcal{P}}
and
G_{\mathcal{Q}}
were isomorphic as topological groups, for any characteristic functor
\mathcal{F}
, then
G_{\mathcal{P}}/\overline{\mathcal{F}(G_{\mathcal{P}})}
and
G_{\mathcal{Q}}/\overline{\mathcal{F}(G_{\mathcal{Q}})}
must be isomorphic.

Using the same argument as in
5.14
, we obtain that

G_{\mathcal{P}}/\overline{\mathcal{F}(G_{\mathcal{P}})}\simeq\varprojlim\pi_{n}(G_{\mathcal{P}})/\mathcal{F}(\pi_{n}(G_{\mathcal{P}})).

Since we assume
G_{\mathcal{P}}
is just-infinite, the previous quotient is finite, so there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\varprojlim\pi_{n}(G_{\mathcal{P}})/\mathcal{F}(\pi_{n}(G_{\mathcal{P}}))\simeq\pi_{n_{0}}(G_{\mathcal{P}})/\pi_{n_{0}}(\mathcal{F}(G_{\mathcal{P}}))=\pi_{n_{0}}(G_{\mathcal{P}})/\mathcal{F}(\pi_{n_{0}}(G_{\mathcal{P}})).

What we need to find is an explicit way to give
n_{0}
. The problem that we might have is that such a
n_{0}
is too big to be able to prove that for different groups of finite type, the quotients
\pi_{n_{0}}(G_{\mathcal{P}})/\mathcal{F}(\pi_{n_{0}}(G_{\mathcal{P}}))
and
\pi_{n_{1}}(G_{\mathcal{Q}})/\mathcal{F}(\pi_{n_{1}}(G_{\mathcal{Q}}))
are not isomorphic. To maximize our chances or in other words, to make
n_{0}
and
n_{1}
as small as possible, we will need to use the maximal branching subgroup of each group of finite type.

Lemma 7.16
.

Let
T
be a
d
-regular tree,
D
a natural number,
\mathcal{F}
a characteristic functor and
\mathcal{P}\leq\Aut(T^{D})
a minimal pattern subgroup. Denote by
K_{\mathcal{P}}
the maximal branching subgroup of
G_{\mathcal{P}}
. If
K_{\mathcal{P}}
is normal and there exists
n_{0}\geq D
such that

[\pi_{n_{0}}(K_{\mathcal{P}}):\mathcal{F}(\pi_{n_{0}}(K_{\mathcal{P}}))]=[\pi_{n_{0}+1}(K_{\mathcal{P}}):\mathcal{F}(\pi_{n_{0}+1}(K_{\mathcal{P}}))],

then

\varprojlim\pi_{n}(G_{\mathcal{P}})/\mathcal{F}(\pi_{n}(G_{\mathcal{P}}))\simeq\pi_{n_{0}}(G_{\mathcal{P}})/\mathcal{F}(\pi_{n_{0}}(G_{\mathcal{P}})).

Proof.

Since
K_{\mathcal{P}}
is the maximal branching subgroup of
G
and
G
is regular branch over
\St_{G}(D-1)
, then
\St_{G}(D-1)\leq K_{\mathcal{P}}
. On the other hand, since
(K_{\mathcal{P}})_{1}\leq K_{\mathcal{P}}
, then

Lemma 9.2

\overline{G}
as the disjoint union
\overline{G}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}H
. Intersecting with
G
and taking the closure we get

\overline{G}=\overline{\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(G\cap H)}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(\overline{G\cap H})

since the closure of the union of finitely many sets is the union of the closures.

But now, we have two disjoint descriptions of
\overline{G}
, where
g_{i}(\overline{G\cap H})\subseteq g_{i}H
for all
i=1,\dots,n
, so necessarily
g_{i}(\overline{G\cap H})=g_{i}H
. In particular, if we take
i=1
, then
\overline{G\cap H}=H
.
∎

9.2
is not true if
H
does not have finite index. For an example of uncountable index, consider
\mathcal{G}=\mathbb{R}
,
G=\mathbb{Q}
and
H={\left\langle{\pi}\right\rangle}
. Then
\overline{G}=\mathbb{R}
and
\overline{G\cap H}={\left\{0\right\}}
. For a countable case, consider
\mathcal{G}=\mathbb{Q}
,
G={\left\langle{\mathbb{Z},\frac{1}{2^{k}}:k\geq 1}\right\rangle}
and
H={\left\langle{\frac{1}{3}}\right\rangle}
. Then
\overline{G}=\mathbb{Q}
and since it is countable, any index will be countable. Then
\overline{G\cap H}=\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a topological group and
K,H
two subgroups of
\mathcal{G}
such that
K\leq_{f}H
. Then
[\overline{H}:\overline{K}]\leq[H:K]
.

Proof.

Let
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}
be a set of representatives of
H/K
, so
H=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}K
. Taking closure,

\overline{H}=\overline{\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}K}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\overline{h_{i}K}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\overline{K},

where the last equality was because the translation is a homeomorphism. The intersection may not be pairwise disjoint, so we obtain the inequality.
∎

Theorem 9.4

(
[
20
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Let
G
be a self-similar, level-transitive and regular branch group with maximal branching subgroup
K
. Then
G
has trivial rigid kernel if and only if there exists
n\geq 1
such that
K\geq\St_{G}(n)
.

We are now ready to prove the main theorem of this subsection:

Theorem 9.5
.

Let
G\leq\Aut(T)
be a self-similar group such that
\overline{G}=G_{\mathcal{P}}
is a group of finite type. Let
K
be a subgroup of
G
such that the geometric product
K_{1}\leq G
, the subgroup
K\leq K_{\mathcal{P}}
and
[G:K]=[G_{\mathcal{P}}:K_{\mathcal{P}}]
. Then:

(1)

K=G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}
,

(2)

G
is regular branch over
K
,

(3)

K
is the maximal branching subgroup of
G
.

(4)

If
G
is level-transitive, then
G
has trivial rigid kernel.

Proof.

For (1), by hypothesis,
K\leq K_{\mathcal{P}}
and
K\leq G
, so
K\leq G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}
. Then,

\displaystyle[G:K]=[G:G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}][G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}:K]\geq[\overline{G}:\overline{G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}}][G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}:K]=

\displaystyle[\overline{G}:K_{\mathcal{P}}][G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}:K]=[G:K][G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}:K]

where in the first inequality
9.3
was used, in the next equality we used
9.2
and in the last equality the hypothesis over the indices. Cancelling out
[G:K]
on both sides, we get that
1\geq[G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}:K]
and consequently
G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}=K
.

For (2), we start proving that the geometric product
K_{1}=G\cap(K_{\mathcal{P}})_{1}
. Indeed, since
K\leq K_{\mathcal{P}}
and
K_{1}\leq G
by hypothesis, we have that
K_{1}\leq G\cap(K_{\mathcal{P}})_{1}
. Take now
g=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in G
such that
k_{i}\in K_{\mathcal{P}}
for all
i=1,\dots,d
. Since
G
is self-similar and
g\in G
, then the sections
k_{i}\in G
, so
k_{i}\in G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}=K
for the point 1. Then
g\in K_{1}
, concluding that
K_{1}=G\cap(K_{\mathcal{P}})_{1}
.

Now, by hypothesis
[G:K]=[G_{\mathcal{P}}:K_{\mathcal{P}}]
, so
[G:K]<\infty
by
Theorem

9.1
. Furthermore,

(K_{\mathcal{P}})_{1}\leq K_{\mathcal{P}}
, so
K_{1}=G\cap(K_{\mathcal{P}})_{1}\leq G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}=K
. Then,
G
is regular branch over
K
by
2.6
.

For (3), suppose that
H
is another subgroup regular branching
G
, namely, we have the inclusions
H_{1}\leq_{f}H\leq_{f}G
. Taking closure, we keep the inclusions and the finiteness of the indices by
9.3
, so
\overline{H_{1}}=(\overline{H})_{1}\leq_{f}\overline{H}\leq_{f}\overline{G}
. Consequently,
\overline{G}
is regular branch over
\overline{H}
and by
Theorem

9.1
, we have
\overline{H}\leq K_{\mathcal{P}}
. Finally, intersecting with
G
, we get that
G\cap\overline{H}\leq G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}=K
. In particular
H\leq G\cap\overline{H}\leq K
, proving the result.

For (4), by
Theorem

9.1
we have
K_{\mathcal{P}}\geq\St_{G_{\mathcal{P}}}(D-1)
, so
K=G\cap K_{\mathcal{P}}\geq\St_{G}(D-1)
and by
Theorem

9.4
, then
G
has trivial rigid kernel.
∎

If
G
is a finitely generated group in the conditions of
Theorem

9.5
. We can also give a pseudo-algorithm that helps to find a candidate
K
for
G
. Let
S
be a finite generating set of
G
and assume
\widetilde{K_{\mathcal{P}}}\lhd\mathcal{P}
. Then
\mathcal{P}/\widetilde{K_{\mathcal{P}}}
has a presentation with a generating set
\pi_{D}(S)\widetilde{K_{\mathcal{P}}}
and finitely many relations
r_{1},\dots,r_{m}
since the group is finite. This means that
\widetilde{K_{\mathcal{P}}}={\left\langle{r_{1},\dots,r_{n}}\right\rangle}^{\mathcal{P}}
where
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}
are words in terms of the elements in
S
. Our candidate for
Theorem

9.5
is

K={\left\langle{r_{1},\dots,r_{m}}\right\rangle}^{G}.

First, of course
K\lhd G
and since we added all the relations of
\mathcal{P}/\widetilde{K_{\mathcal{P}}}
, we have the isomorphism of groups
G/K\rightarrow\mathcal{P}/\widetilde{K_{\mathcal{P}}}\simeq G_{\mathcal{P}}/K_{\mathcal{P}}
and consequently we have
K\leq K_{\mathcal{P}}
and the equalities
[G:K]=[\mathcal{P}:\widetilde{K_{\mathcal{P}}}]=[G_{\mathcal{P}}:K_{\mathcal{P}}]
. It remains to check that
K_{1}\leq G
. The following lemma can help in this task:

Lemma 9.6
.

Let
G
be a fractal group such that
(x,1,\dots,1)\in G
.

(1)

If
g\in G
then
(gxg^{-1},1,\dots,1)\in G
.

(2)

If
y\in G
then
([x,y],1,\dots,1)\in G
.

(3)

Let
x,y,z\in G
. Then
(yxz,1,\dots,1)\in G
if and only if
(yz,1,\dots,1)\in G
.

Proof.

For (1), since
G
is fractal, there exists an element
h
fixing the first vertex
1
such that
h|_{1}=g
. Then

h(x,1,\dots,1)h^{-1}=(gxg^{-1},1\dots,1).

For (2), we start using the point 1 to justify that
(yx^{-1}y^{-1},1,\dots,1)\in G
. Then multiplying by
(x,1,\dots,1)
, we get that
(xyx^{-1}y^{-1},1,\dots,1)=([x,y],1,\dots,1)\in G
.

For (3),

(yxz,1,\dots,1)=(yzx[x^{-1},z^{-1}],1,\dots,1)=\\
(yz,1,\dots,1)(x,1,\dots,1)([x^{-1},z^{-1}],1,\dots,1).

Since
(x,1,\dots,1)\in G
, the last two terms are in
G
, giving the result.
∎

The most useful property of
9.6
will be the last one, as it allows us to annihilate terms that we already know that are in
G
.
