A Composite Model – “VASS-DCI” – for Delayed Cerebral Ischemia Following Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: Role of VASOGRADE and Inflammatory Biomarkers
Authors: Prachi Sharma, Radhakrishnan Muthuchellappan, Thomas Francis, Prithu Sharma, Shilpa H Mallesha, Soundarya Nagaraja
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsExisting clinical prediction tools for delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) patients do not integrate systemic inflammatory status, which is increasingly recognized as a contributor to DCI. We evaluated whether adding inflammatory indices, Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) and Systemic Inflammation Response Index (SIRI), to existing tools improves DCI prediction.Patients and methodsThis retrospective observational study included 624 adult patients with aSAH who underwent clipping or coiling over a 2-year period. Patients were stratified into DCI and non-DCI groups. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors of DCI, which were used to construct a bedside score (VASS-DCI) based on adjusted odds ratios. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, DeLong's test, and decision curve analysis (DCA) were used to assess model performance and clinical utility.ResultsA total of 179 patients (28.7%) developed DCI. Increasing age, VASOGRADE (yellow/red), SII ≥ 2,056,309, and SIRI ≥ 5,568 were independent predictors of DCI. A multivariable model developed using these variables significantly outperformed the VASOGRADE score (AUC 0.774 vs 0.637; p < 0.0001). DCA demonstrated a higher net clinical benefit of the full model across a threshold probability range of 10–55% compared to VASOGRADE. A clinical risk score, the VASS-DCI (range 0–6), was developed using these predictors. The score stratified patients into low (0–2), moderate (3–4), and high-risk (5–6) groups, with corresponding DCI incidences of 11.8, 48.8, and 64.3%, respectively.ConclusionThe VASS-DCI score integrates inflammatory, clinical, and radiological parameters into a practical bedside tool for early prediction of DCI in aSAH patients.How to cite this articleSharma P, Muthuchellappan R, Francis T, Sharma P, Mallesha HS, Nagaraja S. Introduction

Delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) following ruptured aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) occurs in approximately 20–30% of patients and significantly contributes to poorer outcomes.1,2 This underscores the need for early identification of patients at risk of developing DCI during the acute phase of aSAH. The pathogenesis of DCI is multifactorial and includes vasospasm, cortical spreading depolarizations, microcirculatory disturbances, thrombosis, and neuroinflammation.3 Inflammatory cell infiltration is a key pathological feature of aneurysmal wall degeneration and rupture.4,5 Following aneurysmal rupture, the inflammatory response gets exaggerated, further injuring the surrounding brain tissue.6 Given these mechanisms, there is growing interest in using simple, bedside-accessible inflammatory biomarkers for early DCI prediction.7

Studies in the past have evaluated systemic inflammatory markers such as leukocyte count, C-reactive protein (CRP), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), and recently, pan-immune inflammation value (PIV), as predictors of DCI and to prognosticate patients after aSAH.8–13 The SII combines neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets, while SIRI incorporates neutrophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes.14 These indices collectively capture the balance between immune activation and inflammatory response, offering a more comprehensive measure than parameters like NLR or PLR. Whether incorporating these inflammatory markers improves the predictive performance of established scoring systems, such as the VASOGRADE, a validated bedside tool for DCI risk stratification in aSAH, has not yet been studied.15 Early, better prediction of DCI through such combined scoring systems could help prioritize high-risk patients in resource-limited settings for more aggressive preventative and therapeutic strategies.

Thus, in this study, we aimed to assess whether incorporating these inflammatory markers could enhance the prediction of DCI beyond VASOGRADE alone and develop a simple, user friendly, bedside scoring system to aid in this prediction.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

This retrospective observational study was conducted in diagnosed cases of aSAH who underwent either surgical clipping or endovascular intervention. Adult patients over 18 years of age admitted to our tertiary care neurosciences center over a 2-year period, from January 2021 to December 2022, were recruited. Our institute manages approximately 400 aSAH cases annually; thus, the chosen time frame was expected to provide a sufficiently large cohort for analysis. All eligible patients undergoing surgical or endovascular intervention for aSAH during this period were screened for inclusion. The available sample size was considered adequate for exploring the association between SII and DCI, permitting multivariable analysis with appropriate adjustment for confounders. The study was approved by the Institute ethics committee (IEC: No. NIMHANS/42nd IEC(BS&NS DIV.)/2023, dated 14 June 2023). Patients with the following conditions were excluded: co-existing acute or chronic infections at admission, history of malignancies, systemic diseases including chronic liver, renal, lung, or heart conditions, those on anticoagulants/antiplatelets, patients undergoing elective surgery more than 14 days after ictus, presence of confounding vascular pathologies (e.g., co-existing arteriovenous malformation, recent stroke, etc.), early postoperative mortality within first 3 days of ictus, and cases with incomplete or missing data for variables of interest. For data collection, patients' case files and electronic hospital records were reviewed. The following data were collected:

Vasospasm was defined as angiographically confirmed narrowing of cerebral vessels significant enough to cause distal flow restriction, identified from check angiography performed postprocedure. At our institute, all patients receive oral nimodipine for 21 days as standard protocol. All patients underwent check angiography at least once during their hospital stay.

Delayed cerebral ischemia was defined as a new focal neurological deficit and/or a decrease of at least 2 points on the GCS, lasting ≥1 hour, not present immediately postprocedure, and/or the appearance of a new infarct on imaging.16 All patients received imaging near discharge. For those who deteriorated later, follow-up scans were reviewed.

Acute hydrocephalus was defined as a bicaudate index ≥0.20 on admission CT scan.

SII and SIRI were calculated using appropriate formulas: SII = (Platelet count × Neutrophil count)/Lymphocyte count; SIRI = (Monocyte count × Neutrophil count)/Lymphocyte count.

The mRS score >2 was considered an unfavorable outcome, calculated at 3 months follow-up.

The VASOGRADE grading system, generated from combining WFNS and mF grades, was used for risk stratification of DCI at presentation.15

Functional outcomes were obtained from patient follow-up records documented during outpatient visits at 3 months post-discharge.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using R software version 4.5.0. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) based on distribution, and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Patients were divided into DCI and non-DCI groups. Group comparisons were performed using the independent t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for skewed data, and the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables.

Univariate logistic regression was conducted to identify variables associated with DCI. Variables with p < 0.1 were considered for inclusion into the multivariable logistic regression, which was constructed using a backwards stepwise approach. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Inflammatory markers (SII and SIRI) were analyzed both as continuous variables and dichotomized using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) derived cut-offs based on Youden's index.

Model performance was assessed using ROC analysis, and area under the curve (AUC) values with 95% CIs were reported. DeLong's test was used to compare AUCs between models. A final predictive model was selected based on a combination of statistical significance, clinical relevance, and model parsimony.

A point-based scoring system was developed from the final model by scaling and rounding regression coefficients, and its discriminatory ability was assessed via ROC analysis. Patients were then categorized into risk groups (low, moderate, and high) based on their total scores. These categories were visualized using a stacked horizontal bar plot constructed with ggplot2, depicting DCI occurrence across score levels.

Subgroup analysis was performed to evaluate the performance of the final model separately in the clipping and coiling cohorts. AUCs for each subgroup model were compared using DeLong's test.

To assess clinical utility, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed using the dcurves package.17 A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, 844 patients were admitted with a diagnosis of aSAH. Two hundred twenty patients' data were excluded since they did not fulfill the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). The final study cohort comprised 624 patients, of whom 179 (28.69%) developed DCI. Of the 624 patients, data on 3-month mRS were not available for 145 patients. Hence, for the 3-month outcome analysis, 479 patients' data were analyzed.

Variables that turned significant on univariate analysis for the DCI occurrence were age, days from ictus to intervention, WFNS grade, mF grade, VASOGRADE score, acute hydrocephalus, vasospasm, SII, and SIRI (Supplementary Table 1).

These variables were included in the multivariate analysis. However, a few parameters that were significant in univariate analysis were not included in the final multivariable model due to clinical redundancy (e.g., WFNS, mF grade represented by VASOGRADE). In this analysis, increasing age, both systemic inflammatory marker indices (SII and SIRI) and VASOGRADE categorized as “red” and “yellow,” turned out as independent predictors for DCI. For this analysis, SII and SIRI were dichotomized based on cut-off values derived using Youden's Index (Supplementary Table 2). These cut-offs were 2,056,309 for SII and 5568 for SIRI. Values above these thresholds approximately doubled the odds of DCI.

Thus, the final predictive model was constructed from four variables: age, VASOGRADE score, and the binary versions of SII and SIRI. This model demonstrated better discriminative performance (AUC = 0.774) compared to the VASOGRADE-only model (AUC = 0.637). The difference in AUCs was statistically significant (Z = 7.46, p < 0.001; 95% CI: 0.10–0.17), as assessed by DeLong's test.

To assess generalizability, the final model was also separately tested in clipping (n = 461) and coiling (n = 163) subgroups. Although the model showed a higher AUC in coiled patients (0.81 vs 0.77 in clipped), DeLong's test revealed no statistically significant difference in performance between the two cohorts (p = 0.336). This lack of significance may be attributed to the smaller sample size in the coiling subgroup, potentially limiting statistical power. Development of the Scoring System: VASS-DCI

The significant variables from the final multivariate model were used to develop a clinically applicable risk score. All continuous predictors: Age, SII, and SIRI, were dichotomized based on optimal cut-off values identified through ROC analysis for DCI prediction: Age ≥ 56 years, SII ≥ 2,056,309, and SIRI ≥ 5,568 (Supplementary Table 3). VASOGRADE was included as a three-level ordinal variable: green, yellow, and red. We propose the name VASS-DCI, an acronym representing these four predictors. Each predictor was assigned a weighted score based on its adjusted OR, using the variable with the lowest OR as the reference (1 point). The total cumulative score ranged from 0 to 6 (Table 3). Patients were subsequently stratified into three risk categories: Low (0–2), moderate (3–4), and high (5–6), and the predictive performance of the score was evaluated. Supplementary Figure 1 shows the distribution of DCI status within risk score groups. The score-based model demonstrated comparable discriminatory performance to the full multivariate model (AUC: 0.773 vs 0.774; p = 0.83) and outperformed the VASOGRADE-only model in predicting DCI (Supplementary Figure 2).

The final multivariable logistic regression model and the simplified score-based model were both compared against VASOGRADE-only classification using DeLong's test. Subgroup analysis for clipping and coiling interventions was also performed. The CI for AUCs is reported at the 95% level. The clinical utility of the model, as assessed using DCA, demonstrated a consistently higher net benefit for the final model compared to the VASOGRADE-only model across a wide range of threshold probabilities, particularly between 0.1 and 0.55 (Fig. 3).

Discussion

We evaluated the predictive ability of two systemic inflammatory indices, SII and SIRI, for DCI in patients with aSAH. Our findings demonstrate that patients with elevated SII and SIRI values at admission had a significantly higher risk of developing DCI, and their increasing levels were associated with more severe clinical presentation and a complicated hospital course. Previous studies have reported the utility of SII in the early prediction of DCI, and a few authors have shown a temporal association between serially rising SII values (up to 5–7 days post-intervention) and subsequent DCI development.11, 18 The acute bleeding event in aSAH is known to trigger a robust inflammatory cascade characterized by leukocytosis and platelet activation. The extent of this immune-inflammatory dysregulation has been positively correlated with early brain injury, which independently contributes to unfavorable outcomes and DCI.19 Neutrophils contribute to blood–brain barrier disruption, platelets sustain the inflammatory response, monocytes interact with platelets and endothelial cells to propagate pro-inflammatory and prothrombotic cascades, while lymphocytes are key modulators of anti-inflammatory responses.20–23 Given this pathophysiological basis, SII and SIRI, both of which integrate the roles of these cellular components, serve as meaningful biomarkers that reflect the underlying inflammatory-immune milieu following aSAH and its contribution to DCI pathogenesis.

Among the variables analyzed, increasing age, the VASOGRADE score (which integrates clinical and radiological severity), and the presence of acute hydrocephalus were significantly associated with DCI occurrence. In contrast, the type of intervention (clipping vs coiling) did not demonstrate a significant association. These findings are consistent with those reported in prior major studies and systematic reviews.24

Unlike some earlier systematic reviews, our study did not find gender to be a significant predictor of DCI.25 The prior models that identified gender as a risk factor often did so in the context of other clinical variables such as age, WFNS grade, or mF score, without accounting for inflammatory biomarkers. Consistent with our findings, more recent studies incorporating SII and other inflammatory markers have similarly found no independent association between gender and DCI in multivariable analyses.11,18

We also developed a bedside scoring system (VASS-DCI) for DCI prediction using a transparent, stepwise statistical methodology. The VASS-DCI score stratified patients into three risk groups, with DCI incidence rising from 12% in the low-risk group to nearly 50% in the moderate-risk group. Thus, nearly 1 in 2 patients with a score ≥3 are at risk of developing DCI. This stratification underscores the importance of prioritizing such patients for enhanced neurological monitoring, frequent TCD imaging, ICU admission (especially in low-middle income countries (LMICs) with high patient turnover), and close hemodynamic surveillance with early vasospasm-directed therapy. In low-resource settings, where invasive monitoring may not be feasible, the VASS-DCI score offers a simple, noninvasive tool to guide optimal allocation of neurocritical care resources. In contrast to previous studies that have employed machine learning-based models, often resulting in tools that require computational support and are not easily applicable at the bedside, our approach focused on preintervention variables with direct clinical relevance.26,27 This score demonstrated better predictive performance compared to VASOGRADE, a well-established clinical tool, with DeLong's test confirming better discrimination. The DCA reinforced the clinical utility of our model by linking its statistical performance to meaningful net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities. A recent systematic review also underscored the absence of validated DCI prediction models in the literature and noted that most studies failed to use standardized definitions of DCI.28 Our study has tried to address this critical gap.

Beyond its clinical utility, a key strength of our model lies in its practicality, as each of the predictive variables can be readily obtained during routine initial assessment using standard blood tests and neuroimaging, making it feasible in most hospital settings. This is particularly relevant given that early-onset DCI, especially within the first 7 days post-ictus, is linked to increased infarct burden and higher mortality.29 Another strength is that, unlike most existing DCI prediction tools that rely solely on clinical and/or radiological parameters our model integrates these with biological markers, offering a more comprehensive risk assessment.15,30,31 The association of elevated inflammatory indices with DCI also highlights a potential mechanistic link between systemic immune dysregulation and neurological deterioration. This raises the prospect of refining DCI prevention strategies as patients presenting with a high inflammatory profile at admission could benefit from more intensive monitoring and proactive interventions. In fact, there is emerging evidence suggesting that strategies aimed at enhancing cisternal blood clearance may mitigate the inflammatory cascade triggered by SAH, thereby reducing the risk of DCI.32 Our findings support the rationale for future research into immune-modulatory therapies tailored to individual inflammatory risk profiles.

Limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may have introduced inherent biases and limited the generalizability of our findings. We did not assess the temporal trends of SII and SIRI or their dynamic association with DCI development and patient outcomes. However, our inclusion reflects real-world referral delays common in LMICs. To mitigate this bias, we included “days from ictus to intervention” as a covariate, and it was analyzed in both uni- and multivariable models. The majority of patients in our cohort underwent surgical intervention, with fewer receiving endovascular treatment. Although subgroup analysis suggested comparable model performance in both groups, the smaller sample size in the coiling group warrants cautious interpretation. We did not include other potentially relevant inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., CRP, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), interleukins, cytokine levels), which may have provided a more comprehensive inflammatory profile. The VASS-DCI score was developed and internally evaluated within a single retrospective cohort without a separate derivation and validation split. External validation in an independent, prospective multicenter cohort is warranted to confirm its generalizability and predictive performance. Finally, although we report outcome data, the VASS-DCI score is specifically intended to predict DCI risk and not functional recovery. Future studies may explore the role of SII and SIRI in long-term outcome prediction.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a simple, bedside clinical score using routine admission variables, i.e., age, VASOGRADE, and systemic inflammatory indices (SII and SIRI), to predict DCI in patients with aSAH. While the model demonstrated a modest AUC, it provided significant clinical utility, as evidenced by a clear net benefit on DCA. This score enables early risk stratification and may guide more targeted neurological monitoring and timely therapy.
