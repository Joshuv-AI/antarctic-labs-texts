Generalizing Eulerian numbers via semipermutations: topological and combinatorial aspects
Giovanni Gaiffi, Giovanni Interdonato

Abstract
. In a paper by Lin an interesting family of semipermutations comes out to index the elements of a cohomology basis of a Hessenberg type variety. The corresponding Betti numbers are a generalization of Eulerian numbers. We show three different subsets of the symmetric group that are in bijection with the set of these semipermutations. These bijections preserve the statistics 𝗅𝖾𝖼 \mathsf{lec} and 𝖽𝖾𝗌 \mathsf{des} : one of these is obtained by an algebraic-topological argument, the others are explicitly described in combinatorial terms.



1.
Introduction

In
[
7
]
the author points out the following family of semipermutations, more precisely the set
\mathcal{S}_{n,k+1}
of permutations of
(k+1)
different numbers chosen from
[n]:=\{1,\cdots,n\}
, equipped with a descent statistic.

Definition 1.1
.

The set
\mathcal{S}_{n,k+1}
is made by all pairs
(\alpha_{0},\rho)
, where
\rho=\rho_{1}\cdots\rho_{k+1}
is a word made by
k+1
different numbers of
[n]
, and
\alpha_{0}=[n]\setminus\{\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{k+1}\}
.

Given a pair
(\alpha_{0},\rho)
as above, we define its
descent set
as

\mathsf{Des}((\alpha_{0},\rho))=\{(w_{i}>w_{i+1})\mid i\in[k]\}\sqcup\{(a>\rho_{1})\mid a\in\alpha_{0}\}

and we will denote by
\mathsf{des}((\alpha_{0},\rho))=|\mathsf{Des}((\alpha_{0},\rho))|
the cardinality of this set.

Example 1.2
.

Let
(\alpha_{0},\tau)=(\{4,6,7\},52318)\in\mathcal{S}_{8,5}
.
We have that

\mathsf{Des}((\{4,6,7\},52318))=\{(5>2),(3>1)\}\sqcup\{(6>5),(7>5)\}

.
Therefore
\mathsf{des}((\{4,6,7\},52318))=2+2=4
.

The number of permutations of
k+1
different numbers in
[n]
with
i
descents appears naturally in
[

]
as the
2i
-th Betti number of a Hessenberg type variety.
The author notices that these Betti numbers are a natural generalization of the Eulerian numbers (when
k=n-2
we are dealing with the permutations of
n-1
different numbers in
[n]
with
i

n
numbers with
i
descents, whose cardinality is the Eulerian number

) and observes that it would be interesting to study further properties of these numbers (see also this related question on MathOverflow
[
1
]
and the OEIS sequence
[
8
]
.

In the present paper we explore this topic, providing a further topological interpretation of these numbers. This gives the inspiration to then find three explicit bijections of the set of permutations of
k+1
different numbers in
[n]
with
i
descents with some sets of permutations considered with the statistics
\mathsf{lec}
or
\mathsf{des}
. One of the involved bijections turns out to be an explicit presentation of a bijection introduced by Foata and Han in
[

]
.

The structure of this paper is the following. After recalling in Section
2
the statistics
\mathsf{des}
and
\mathsf{lec}
, in Section

with

We notice that this variety is isomorphic to a De Concini–Procesi wonderful model (these models were introduced in the seminal paper

.

Motivated by this, we show an explicit combinatorial presentation of the above mentioned bijection. We do this in two steps. First in Section

which preserves the statistics

and

: let

be a non-empty word made of distinct numbers. Let
c
be the position in
w
of the maximum element of

, then

is a

if

(1.1)

w_{1}<w_{2}<\dots<w_{c-1}<w_{\ell}<w_{\ell-1}<\dots<w_{c}.

or if

Then in Section

Finally, in Section

that sends the

\mathsf{lec}

[

]
*Section 6). It is based on the idea of
wave

(1.2)

w_{\ell}<w_{\ell-1}<\dots<w_{c+1}<w_{1}<w_{2}<\dots<w_{c}.

2.
Some preliminary notation

If
w=w_{1}\dots w_{\ell}
is a word, we define its descent set as
\mathsf{Des}(w)=\{(w_{i}>w_{i+1})\mid i\in[\ell-1]\}
and we will denote by
\mathsf{des}(w)=|\mathsf{Des}(w)|
the cardinality of this set. We define its inversion set as
\mathsf{Inv}(w)=\{(i,j)\mid 1\leq i<j\leq\ell,\,w_{i}>w_{j}\}
and we will denote by
\mathsf{inv}(w)=|\mathsf{Inv}(w)|
the cardinality of this set. These definitions naturally extend to permutations, considering the word obtained by writing them in one-line notation.

Example 2.1
.

Let
\sigma=45762318\in\mathcal{S}_{8}
. We will have that
\mathsf{Des}(\sigma)=\{(7>6),(6>2),(3>1)\}
and hence that
\mathsf{des}(\sigma)=3
.

Given a word
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell}
we recall the definition of Hook factorization, first introduced by Gessel in
[
6
]
. There exists and is unique a decomposition of
w=\gamma*\alpha_{1}*\cdots*\alpha_{k}
, where the operator
*
is the concatenation of words, such that the elements in
\gamma
are in increasing order, and each
\alpha_{i}
is a
hook
, that is
\alpha_{i}=a^{(i)}_{1}\cdots a^{(i)}_{s_{i}}
, with
s_{i}\geq 2
,
a^{(i)}_{1}>a^{(i)}_{2}
and
a^{(i)}_{2}<\dots<a^{(i)}_{s_{i}}
, for each
i=1,\dots,k
. We then define the statistic
\displaystyle\mathsf{lec}(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{inv}(\alpha_{i})
as the sum of the number of inversions of each hook.
We will also denote a word with elements in increasing order, such as
\gamma
, as a
trivial hook
.

Remark 2.2
.

We can obtain this factorization recursively, taking as
\alpha_{k}
the suffix of
w
that starts at the position of the rightmost descent of
w
, and then iterating this reasoning on the remaining prefix obtained by removing
\alpha_{k}
. The process ends when no further descents are present, i.e. when we have a word whose elements are in increasing order: this will be
\gamma
.

Example 2.3
.

Let
\tau=18326457\in\mathcal{S}_{8}
. Its Hook factorization is:

(2.1)

\tau=\underline{18}*32*6457,

where
\gamma=18
is the underlined factor. We therefore have that
\mathsf{lec}(\tau)=\mathsf{inv}(32)+\mathsf{inv}(6457)=1+2=3
.

3.
A bijection pointed out by algebraic topology

Let us denote by
\mathcal{X}
the permutohedral variety of dimension
n-1
, i.e. the toric variety associated to the root system of type
A_{n-1}
.
As it is well known (see for instance
[
9
]
), it can also be obtained as an iterated blowup of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
:

\textstyle{\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{n-2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{n-2}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{X}_{n-3}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{n-3}}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{2}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{X}_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{1}\ \ \ \ \ }
\textstyle{\mathcal{X}_{0}=\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}

where each
\mathcal{X}_{k+1}
is the blowup of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
along the strict transform of the
k
-dimensional coordinate spaces (
0\leq k\leq n-3
).

In
[
7
]
the author describes an explicit isomorphism between the permutohedral variety of dimension
n-1
, and the regular semisimple Hessenberg variety
\mathcal{H}ess(\mathbf{S},h_{+})
associated to the Hessenberg function

defined by

for

and
h_{+}(n)=n
.
In this process, he further obtains isomorphisms from
\mathcal{X}_{k}
, the
k
-th step in the iterated blowups, to a “Hessenberg type” subvariety of a partial flag variety which is denoted by
\mathcal{H}ess^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{S},h_{+})
.
Then he exhibits a basis for the integer cohomology

whose elements are in one-to-one correspondence with the permutations of
(k+1)
different numbers chosen from
[n]:=\{1,\cdots,n\}
. This allows for a combinatorial computation of the Betti numbers of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
, more precisely the even Betti numbers of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
are obtained as follows (the odd Betti numbers of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
are all equal to

).

Theorem 3.1
.

[see
[
7
]
]
The
2i
-th Betti number of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
is given by

\displaystyle\beta_{2i}(\mathcal{X}_{k})

\displaystyle=\#(\text{permutations of $k+1$ different numbers in $[n]$ with $i$ descents})

These Betti numbers are a quite natural generalization of the Eulerian numbers. In fact we notice that when
k=n-2
the formula above states that
\beta_{2i}(\mathcal{X}_{n-2})
is the number of permutations of
n-1
different numbers in
[n]
with
i
descents, which is equivalent to saying that it is the number of permutations of
n
numbers with
i
descents, i.e. the Eulerian number
A(n,i+1)
. On the geometrical side, we observe that
\mathcal{X}_{n-2}
is the permutohedral variety and it is well known that its Betti numbers
\beta_{2i}(\mathcal{X}_{n-2})
coincide with the Eulerian numbers.

Let now
k
be an integer such that
0\leq k\leq n-1
and let us now consider in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
the arrangement
\mathcal{G}_{k}
given all the coordinate subspaces of dimension
\leq k
. This arrangement is built according to the definition given by De Concini and Procesi in
[
2
]
; the space that we obtain by blowing up all the subspaces in
\mathcal{G}_{k}
is a wonderful model
Y_{k}
whose cohomology ring is isomorphic to the cohomology of the corresponding projective model i.e.
\mathcal{X}_{k}
. In
[
5
]
a description of a monomial basis for the integer cohomology of De Concini–Procesi wonderful models is provided, and this basis for
Y_{k}
can be described in the following way.

A monomial in this basis is a product of Chern classes of (transforms of) subspaces in
\mathcal{G}_{k}
. Let
\zeta_{G_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\zeta_{G_{2}}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots\zeta_{G_{s}}^{\alpha_{s}}
be such a monomial. Then

(1)

G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{s}
is a chain in
\mathcal{G}_{k}
with respect to inclusion (say
G_{1}\subset\cdots\subset G_{s}
) where in each inclusion the codimension is greater than 1 and the codimension of
G_{s}
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is greater than 1;

(2)

for every
i
,
\alpha_{i}
is a positive integer less than the codimension of
G_{i}
in
G_{i+1}
(for
i=s
we consider the codimension of
G_{s}
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
).

We now use for brevity a notation as in the following example: if
G_{1}
is the subspace given by the equations
x_{1}=x_{2}=x_{4}=x_{6}=0
then we associate to
G_{1}
the subset
{1,2,4,6}
of
[n]
, we denote
G_{1}=H_{1,2,4,6}
and we call
\zeta_{1,2,4,6}
its associated Chern class. According to this notation, there is a bijection between the elements in
\mathcal{G}_{k}
and the subsets of
[n]
whose cardinality is greater than or equal to
n-k
.

Remark 3.2
.

As an example let
k=7

n=11
and consider the monomial

(3.1)

\zeta_{\{1,2,4,5\}}^{2}\zeta_{\{1,2,4,5,6,7,8\}}^{2}\zeta_{\{1,2,4,5,6,7,8,9,11\}}

which is an element of the basis of the cohomology of
\mathcal{X}_{7}
.

For instance, notice that here the exponent of
\zeta_{\{1,2,4,5\}}
is strictly less than
4
, which is the codimension of
H_{1,2,4,5}
in
\mathbb{C}^{11}
.

We now show an algorithm that produces a bijection between this monomial basis of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
and the elements of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
whose first hook from the right in the hook factorization has cardinality at least
n-k
(see also
[
4
]
*Section 5.2 for the case
k=n-1
). This bijection is grade-preserving provided that we consider in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
the grade induced by the statistic
\mathsf{lec}
.
We illustrate the algorithm producing a permutation
\sigma
from the above mentioned monomial

\zeta_{\{1,2,4,5\}}^{2}\zeta_{\{1,2,4,5,6,7,8\}}^{2}\zeta_{\{1,2,4,5,6,7,8,9,11\}}

•

We first look at the elements in
\{1,\ldots,11\}
that do not appear in the description of the monomial. We write them in increasing order obtaining the non-hook part
\gamma
of
\sigma
. In our example we have
\{1,\dotsc,11\}\setminus\{1,2,4,5,6,7,8,9,11\}=\{3,10\}
so
\gamma=3\ 10
.

•

The first (from the right) hook of
\sigma
is obtained by taking the numbers 1,2,4,5, that belong to the smallest set of the chain, and by arranging them in such a way the number of inversions is equal to the exponent 2 of
\zeta_{\{1,2,4,5\}}^{2}
. So we get 4125.

•

The second (from the right) hook of
\sigma
is formed using the numbers of the second set of the chain, i.e.
\{1,2,4,5,6,7,8\}
, that do not appear in the smallest one. In the example those numbers are
\{6,7,8\}
which is
\{1,2,4,5,6,7,8\}\setminus\{1,2,4,5\}
, arranged in such a way to obtain 2 inversions since
2
is the exponent of
\zeta_{\{1,2,4,5,6,7,8\}}
in the monomial: 867.

•

Analogously, the last (from the right) hook is 11 9

In the end we obtain
\sigma=3\ 10\ 11\ 98674125
, which satisfies
\mathsf{lec}(\sigma)=5
.

From this bijection we deduce another way to obtain the Betti numbers of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
:

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
i>0
. The
2i
-th Betti number of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
is given by

\displaystyle\beta_{2i}(\mathcal{X}_{k})=\#(

elements
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, such that
\mathsf{lec}(\sigma)=i
, whose first

\displaystyle\text{hook from the right has cardinality at least $n-k$})

Therefore considering two different cohomology bases of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
allows us to deduce, by double counting, the following result:

Corollary 3.4
.

The cardinality of the set of the permutations of
k+1
different numbers in
[n]
with
i
descents is equal to the cardinality of the set of the elements
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, such that
\mathsf{lec}(\sigma)=i
, whose first hook from the right has cardinality at least
n-k
.

4.
An explicit bijection of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
preserving des-lec statistic: reverse waves

Motivated by Corollary
3.4
we now search for a combinatorial bijection between the set of the permutations of
k+1
different numbers in
[n]
with
i
descents and the set of the elements
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, such that
\mathsf{lec}(\sigma)=i
, whose first hook from the right has cardinality at least
n-k
.
In this section we focus on the case
k=n-2
, i.e. we search for a combinatorial bijection of the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
that sends the
\mathsf{des}
statistic to the
\mathsf{lec}
statistic.

To do so, we will present an explicit bijection of the set

W=\{a_{1}\cdots a_{\ell}\mid\ell\in\mathbb{N},\,a_{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\,\forall i\text{ and }a_{i}\neq a_{j}\,\forall\,i\neq j\}

of all finite words with distinct entries, such that if we restrict to the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, it becomes a bijection of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

Remark 4.1
.

We can evaluate the
\mathsf{lec}
of a word
w
in a recursive way, as suggested by the Remark
2.2
. If
w=\gamma*\alpha_{1}*\cdots*\alpha_{k}
is the Hook factorization, and
k=0
, then
\mathsf{lec}(w)=\mathsf{lec}(\gamma)=0
, while if
k>0
, then we have by definition that

(4.1)

\mathsf{lec}(w)=\mathsf{lec}(\gamma*\alpha_{1}*\cdots*\alpha_{k-1})+\mathsf{lec}(\alpha_{k}).

If a hook
\alpha
has length
\ell
, then we obtain that
1\leq\mathsf{lec}(\alpha)\leq\ell-1
. Moreover, if we have
\ell
distinct numbers
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{\ell}
, for any
0\leq d\leq\ell-1
, we have exactly one way to rearrange them to create a hook, possibly trivial if
d=0
,
\alpha_{d}
with
\mathsf{lec}(\alpha_{d})
equal to
d
, that is,
\alpha_{d}=a_{d+1}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{d}a_{d+2}\cdots a_{\ell}
.

We will define a similar way of evaluating descents by decomposition in smaller words.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell}\in W
be a non-empty word of length
\ell
. Let
c
be the position in
w
of the maximum element of
w
, we say that
w
is a
reverse wave
if
c\neq\ell
and

(4.2)

w_{1}<w_{2}<\dots<w_{c-1}<w_{\ell}<w_{\ell-1}<\dots<w_{c}.

We will also denote a word with elements in increasing order, that is the case with
c=\ell
, as a
trivial reverse wave
.

Remark 4.3
.

If
w
is a reverse wave, then by definition, any prefix of
w
is also a reverse wave, possibly trivial.

Remark 4.4
.

Similarly to what was observed in Remark
4.1
, if a reverse wave
rw
has length
\ell
, then we have that
1\leq\mathsf{des}(rw)\leq\ell-1
. Moreover if we have
\ell
distinct numbers
b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{\ell}
, for any
0\leq d\leq\ell-1
, we have exactly one way to rearrange them to create a reverse wave, possibly trivial if
d=0
,
r_{d}
with
\mathsf{des}(rw_{d})
equal to
d
, that is,
{rw_{d}=b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{\ell-d-1}b_{\ell}b_{\ell-1}\cdots b_{\ell-d}}
.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell}\in W
be a non-empty word of length
\ell
. The
special reverse wave
of
w
is the prefix
\mathsf{srw}(w)=w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{t}\cdots w_{s-1}w_{s}
of

where:

•

1\leq t<\ell
is the index of the first descent of
w
, so it is the minimum such that
w_{t}>w_{t+1}
. If
w
has no descents, if and only if elements of

are in increasing order, then
\mathsf{srw}(w)=w
.

•

t\leq s\leq\ell
is the maximum such that
w_{t}>w_{t+1}>\dots>w_{s-1}>w_{s}>w_{t-1}
, where, if
t=1
, we consider
w_{0}=0
. It exists since

, by minimality of

, and if

, then is it true that

by definition of

.

Remark 4.6
.

If
\mathsf{srw}(w)=w_{1}\cdots w_{t}\cdots w_{s-1}w_{s}
is the special reverse wave of

, then we have

(4.3)

w_{1}<w_{2}<\dots<w_{t-1}<w_{s}<w_{s-1}<\dots<w_{t}

and in particular that
\mathsf{srw}(w)
is a reverse wave, possibly trivial. This is the maximal prefix of
\sigma
that is also a reverse wave. If

has no descents, then
\mathsf{srw}(w)=w
, and in this case

is a trivial reverse wave.

We also have, if

, that

or that

.

We will denote by

Example 4.7
.

Let
\sigma=45762318\in\mathcal{S}_{8}\subset W
, then its special reverse wave is

and

.

We introduce a new statistic that will allow us to correlate the number of the descents of a word with those of its special reverse wave.

Definition 4.8
.

Let
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell}\in W
be a non-empty word and
\mathsf{srw}(w)=w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{t}\cdots w_{s-1}w_{s}
be its special reverse wave. We denote

(4.4)

\mathsf{srdes}(w)=\mathsf{des}(\mathsf{srw}(w))+\chi(w_{s}>w_{s+1})

\chi(P)

otherwise, and if

\chi(w_{s}>w_{s+1})=0
.

Remark 4.9
.

We have that
\mathsf{des}(w)=\mathsf{srdes}(w)+\mathsf{des}(w\setminus\mathsf{srw}(w))
. Indeed if
s=\ell
we obtain that
w=\mathsf{srw}(w)
and
\mathsf{srdes}(w)=\mathsf{des}(\mathsf{srw}(w))=\mathsf{des}(w)
as wanted, while if

, we get that

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathsf{des}(w)=\mathsf{des}(w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{des}(w_{1}\cdots w_{s})+\chi(w_{s}>w_{s+1})+\mathsf{des}(w_{s+1}\cdots w_{l})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{srdes}(w)+\mathsf{des}(w\setminus\mathsf{sw}(w)).

Example 4.10
.

Let
\sigma=45762318\in\mathcal{S}_{8}\subset W
, as in the previous example. We have that
\mathsf{srdes}(w)=\mathsf{des}(4576)+\chi(6>2)=1+1=2
and
\mathsf{des}(w)=3=2+1=\mathsf{srdes}(w)+\mathsf{des}(2318)
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell}\in W
be a non increasing word and
\mathsf{sw}(w)=w_{1}\cdots w_{s}
its special reverse wave. Then we have that

(4.6)

1\leq\mathsf{srdes}(w)\leq s-1.

Moreover if
w
is an increasing word then
\mathsf{srdes}(w)=0
.

Proof.

First of all let us prove that
\mathsf{srdes}(w)\geq 1
if
w
is not increasing. If

Let us now demonstrate the other inequality.

If

Proposition 4.12

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mathsf{srdes}(\mathsf{rins}(a,b,d))

\mathsf{rins}(w\setminus\mathsf{srw}(w),\mathsf{srw}(w),\mathsf{srdes}(w))=w.

Proof.

Let

We will prove that

\mathsf{r}(a,b,d)=\begin{cases}\mathsf{rwave}(b,d)*a=\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{q-d-1}\beta_{q}\cdots\beta_{q-d}a_{1}\cdots a_{p}&\text{if }\beta_{q-d}<a_{1}\text{ or }p=0\\

6.
Second bijection (Foata–Han) preserving des-lec statistic: waves

In this section we present a different bijection
\psi
of the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
that sends the
\mathsf{des}
statistic to the
\mathsf{lec}
statistic.

This bijection
\psi
appears to be equal, unless we compose with easy bijections, to the map
F
defined by Foata and Han in
[
3
]
*Section 6). In particular, it seems to hold that:

(6.1)

\psi=\mathbf{c}\circ F\circ\mathbf{c}\circ\mathbf{r},

where
\mathbf{c}(\sigma)=(n+1-\sigma_{1})\dots(n+1-\sigma_{n})
and
\mathbf{r}(\sigma)=\sigma_{n}\dots\sigma_{1}
. We have verified this equality computationally for

small. We present it anyway because it can be constructed explicitly and directly, using the methods described in the previous sections.

To do so, we will present an explicit bijection of the set

W=\{a_{1}\cdots a_{\ell}\mid\ell\in\mathbb{N},\,a_{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\,\forall i\text{ and }a_{i}\neq a_{j}\,\forall\,i\neq j\}

of all finite words with distinct entries, such that if we restrict to the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, it becomes a bijection of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

We will define a new way of evaluating descents by decomposition in smaller words.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell}\in W
be a non-empty word of length
\ell
. Let
c
be the position in
w
of the maximum element of
w
, we say that
w
is a
wave
if
c\neq\ell
and

(6.2)

w_{\ell}<w_{\ell-1}<\dots<w_{c+1}<w_{1}<w_{2}<\dots<w_{c}.

We will also denote a word with elements in increasing order, that is the case with
c=\ell
, as a
trivial wave
.

Remark 6.2
.

If
w
is a wave of length
\ell
and
c
is the position of its maximum, then
\mathsf{des}(w)=\ell-c
.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell}\in W
be a non-empty word of length
\ell
. The
special wave
of
w
is the subword
\mathsf{sw}(w)=w_{r}w_{r+1}\cdots w_{t}\cdots w_{s-1}w_{s}
of
w
where:

•

1\leq t<\ell
is the index of the first descent of
w
, so it is the minimum such that
w_{t}>w_{t+1}
. If
w
has no descents, if and only if the elements of
w
are in increasing order, then
\mathsf{sw}(w)=w
.

•

1\leq r\leq t
is the minimum such that
w_{r}>w_{t+1}
. It exists since
w_{t}>w_{t+1}
.

•

t<s\leq\ell
is the maximum such that
w_{t+1}>w_{t+2}>\dots>w_{s-1}>w_{s}>w_{r-1}
, where, if
r=1
, we consider
w_{0}=0
. It exists since
w_{t+1}>w_{r-1}
, by minimality of

, and if
r=1
, then is it true that
w_{t+1}>0=w_{r-1}
by definition of
W
.

Remark 6.4
.

If
\mathsf{sw}(w)=w_{r}w_{r+1}\cdots w_{t}\cdots w_{s-1}w_{s}
is the special wave of
w
, then we have

(6.3)

w_{1}<w_{2}<\dots<w_{r-1}<w_{s}<w_{s-1}<\dots<w_{t+1}<w_{r}<\dots<w_{t}

and in particular that

is a wave, since

We also have, if

w_{s+1}>w_{s}
or
w_{s+1}<w_{r-1}
.

If

has no descents, then
\mathsf{sw}(w)=w
, and in this case
\mathsf{sw}(w)
is a trivial wave.

We will denote by

We can define a map that reverses the operation of removing the special reverse wave.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
a=a_{1}\cdots a_{p}
and
b=b_{1}\cdots b_{q}
two words in
W
with distinct entries and such that
b
is a non trivial wave. Let
0\leq x\leq p
be the maximum such that
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{x}<b_{q}
, where
x=0

. Then the map

satisfies

(6.5)

\displaystyle\mathsf{sw}(\mathsf{ins}(a,b))=b,

(6.6)

\displaystyle\mathsf{ins}(a,b)\setminus\mathsf{sw}(\mathsf{ins}(a,b))

\displaystyle=a,

and, for all

that are not increasing,

(6.7)

\mathsf{ins}(w\setminus\mathsf{sw}(w),\mathsf{sw}(w))=w.

Proof.

We observe that, since

is a wave and by the definition of

, we have that
b_{i}>b_{q}>a_{x}>\cdots>a_{1}
, for every
1\leq i<q
. Moreover we have by maximality of

that

, or

. This implies that the first descent of
\mathsf{ins}(a,b)
is the first descent of
b
, and that the extremal points of its special wave are exactly
b_{1}
and
b_{p}
, and so that
\mathsf{sw}(\mathsf{ins}(a,b))=b
. The equation (
6.6
) is clear from the definition of
\mathsf{ins}
and from equation (
6.5
).

To prove the third property, let
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell}\in W
be a word and
\mathsf{sw}(w)=w_{r}w_{r+1}\cdots w_{t}\cdots w_{s-1}w_{s}
its special wave, with

as in the Definition
6.3
. From equation (

) and the fact that if

∎

Example 6.7

Thanks to the definition of the special wave of a word

in a recursive way.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell}\in W
be a word and
\mathsf{sw}(w)=w_{r}w_{r+1}\cdots w_{t}\cdots w_{s-1}w_{s}
its special wave. Then we have that

(6.8)

\mathsf{des}(w)=\mathsf{des}(w\setminus\mathsf{sw}(w))+\mathsf{des}(\mathsf{sw}(w)).

Proof.

First of all, we note that if
r>1
and
s<\ell
we have that

(6.9)

\displaystyle\mathsf{des}(w)=\mathsf{des}(w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell})=

\displaystyle\mathsf{des}(w_{1}\cdots w_{r-1}w_{s+1}\cdots w_{\ell})+\mathsf{des}(w_{r}\cdots w_{s})+

\displaystyle-\chi(w_{r-1}>w_{s+1})+\chi(w_{r-1}>w_{r})+\chi(w_{s}>w_{s+1}),

where
\chi(P)
equals
1
if the property
P
is true, and
0
otherwise.
From the definition of
r
we have that
w_{r-1}<w_{r}
, and so that
\chi(w_{r-1}>w_{r})=0
.

If
w_{s+1}>w_{s}
, then
w_{s+1}>w_{s}>w_{r-1}
and so that
\chi(w_{r-1}>w_{s+1})=\chi(w_{s}>w_{s+1})=0
.
If
w_{s+1}<w_{s}
, then by definition of
s
, we have that
w_{s+1}>w_{r-1}
and so that
\chi(w_{r-1}>w_{s+1})=\chi(w_{s}>w_{s+1})=1
. In both cases equation (
6.9
) simplifies to equation (
6.8
).

If
r=1
and
s<\ell
then

\displaystyle\mathsf{des}(w)=\mathsf{des}(w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{des}(w_{s+1}\cdots w_{\ell})+\mathsf{des}(w_{1}\cdots w_{s})+\chi(w_{s}>w_{s+1})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{des}(w_{s+1}\cdots w_{\ell})+\mathsf{des}(w_{1}\cdots w_{s})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{des}(w\setminus\mathsf{sw}(w))+\mathsf{des}(\mathsf{sw}(w)),

since by definition of
s
in this case
w_{s}<w_{s+1}.

If
r>1
and
s=\ell
then

\displaystyle\mathsf{des}(w)=\mathsf{des}(w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{des}(w_{1}\cdots w_{r-1})+\mathsf{des}(w_{r}\cdots w_{\ell})+\chi(w_{r}>w_{r-1})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{des}(w_{1}\cdots w_{r-1})+\mathsf{des}(w_{r}\cdots w_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{des}(w\setminus\mathsf{sw}(w))+\mathsf{des}(\mathsf{sw}(w)),

since by definition of
r
it holds that
w_{r-1}<w_{r}.

from the definition of
\mathsf{sw}(w)
, we have that
w_{r-1}<w_{r}
or
r=1
.

Finally if
r=1
and
s=\ell
, then
w=\mathsf{sw}(w)
,
w\setminus\mathsf{sw}(w)
is the empty word, and then equation (
6.8
) is easily true.
∎

Remark 6.10
.

Similarly to what was observed in Remarks
4.1
and
4.4
, if a wave
\beta
has length
\ell
, then we have that
1\leq\mathsf{des}(\beta)\leq\ell-1
. Moreover if we have
\ell
distinct numbers
b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{\ell}
, for any
0\leq d\leq\ell-1
, we have exactly one way to rearrange them to create a wave, possibly trivial if
d=0
,
\beta_{d}
with
\mathsf{des}(\beta_{d})
equal to
d
, that is,
{\beta_{d}=b_{d+1}b_{d+2}\cdots b_{\ell}b_{d}b_{d-1}\cdots b_{1}}
.

Now we can define the desired bijection.

Let
\psi:W\to W
be the map such that:

•

If
w=\varepsilon
is the empty word then
\psi(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
.

•

Otherwise

(6.10)

\psi(w)=\psi(w\setminus\mathsf{sw}(w))*\delta(\mathsf{sw}(w))

where the
*
operator is the concatenation of words and
\delta(\mathsf{sw}(w))=c_{d+1}c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{d}c_{d+2}\cdots c_{m}
is a rearrangement of
\mathsf{sw}(w)
, where
c_{1}<c_{2}<\cdots<c_{m}
are the elements of
\mathsf{sw}(w)
and
d=\mathsf{des}(\mathsf{sw}(w))
.

Theorem 6.11
.

The map
\psi:W\to W
just defined is a well defined bijection of
W
, such that

(6.11)

\mathsf{des}(w)=\mathsf{lec}(\psi(w)).

Moreover the restriction to the symmetric group
\psi_{\mathcal{S}_{n}}:\mathcal{S}_{n}\to\mathcal{S}_{n}
is a bijection.

Proof.

We will prove by induction on the length
\ell
of
w\in W
that
\psi(w)
is a rearrangement of
w
, and that it is a well defined map. If
\ell=0
then
w=\varepsilon
is the empty word and by definition
\psi(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
, that is fine. If
w
has length
\ell>0
, then by definition
w\setminus\mathsf{sw}(w)
is a word of length strictly less than
\ell
, and so by the inductive hypothesis
\psi(w\setminus\mathsf{sw}(w))
is a rearrangement of
w\setminus\mathsf{sw}(w)
. Moreover
\delta(\mathsf{sw}(w))
is defined as a rearrangement of
\mathsf{sw}(w)
, so this means that
\psi(w)
is a rearrangement of
w
, and
\psi
is a well defined map.

To prove that
\psi
is a bijection we will define its inverse
\varphi
as the map such that:

•

If
w=\varepsilon
is the empty word then
\varphi(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
.

•

Otherwise, if the rightmost hook
\mathsf{h}(w)
of
w
is not trivial

(6.12)

\varphi(w)=\mathsf{ins}(\varphi(w\setminus\mathsf{h}(w)),\mathsf{wv}(\mathsf{h}(w))),

where
w\setminus\mathsf{h}(w)
is the prefix of
w
obtained removing
\mathsf{h}(w)
and
\mathsf{wv}(\mathsf{h}(w))=c_{d+1}c_{d+2}\cdots c_{m}c_{d}c_{d-1}\cdots c_{1}
is a rearrangement of
\mathsf{h}(w)
, where
c_{1}<c_{2}<\cdots<c_{m}
are the elements of
\mathsf{h}(w)
and
d=\mathsf{lec}(\mathsf{h}(w))=\mathsf{inv}(\mathsf{h}(w))
. If
\mathsf{h}(w)
is a trivial hook, then
\mathsf{h}(w)=w
and in this case
\varphi(w)=w

First of all let us note that if
\alpha
is a hook with
\mathsf{lec}(\alpha)=d
, then, thanks to Remark
6.10
, we have that
\mathsf{wv}(\alpha)
is the unique wave such that its elements are the same of
\alpha
and such that
\mathsf{des}(\mathsf{wv}(\alpha))=\mathsf{lec}(\alpha)=d
. Similarly, if
\beta
is a wave with
\mathsf{des}(\beta)=d
, then, thanks to Remark
4.1
, we have that
\delta(\beta)
is the unique hook such that its elements are the same of
\beta
and such that
\mathsf{lec}(\delta(\beta))=\mathsf{des}(\beta)=d
. Putting these two last observation together, we obtain, if
\alpha
is a hook and
\beta
is a wave, that

(6.13)

\displaystyle\delta(\mathsf{wv}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\alpha

(6.14)

\displaystyle\mathsf{wv}(\delta(\beta))

\displaystyle=\beta.

We can notice that from the definition of
\psi
we have that if
w
is increasing then
\mathsf{sw}(w)=w
and
\psi(w)=w
, and so it is clear that
\psi
and
\varphi
are inverse when restricted to the subset of increasing words, because they are both the identity in this case.

Now let us consider the case where
w\in W
is not increasing.

Since
w\setminus\mathsf{h}(w)
has length strictly less than

and, by definition,
\mathsf{wv}(\mathsf{h}(w))
is a wave, using inductive reasoning, entirely analogous to what was done previously for
\psi
, we obtain that
\varphi(w)
is a rearrangement of
w
and that it is a well defined map.

Let us prove that they are inverse map by induction on the length
\ell
of
w
. If
\ell=0
it is clear. If
\ell>0
then

\displaystyle\psi(\varphi(w))

\displaystyle=\psi(\mathsf{ins}(\varphi(w\setminus\mathsf{h}(w)),\mathsf{wv}(\mathsf{h}(w))))

(6.15)

\displaystyle=\psi(\varphi(w\setminus\mathsf{h}(w)))*\delta(\mathsf{wv}(\mathsf{h}(w)))

\displaystyle=w\setminus\mathsf{h}(w)*\mathsf{h}(w)=w,

where the second equality is true thanks to Proposition
6.6
and the last equality is true thanks to inductive hypothesis, since
w\setminus\mathsf{h}(w)
has length strictly less than
\ell
, and thanks to equation (
6.13
). Moreover

\displaystyle\varphi(\psi(w))

\displaystyle=\mathsf{ins}(\varphi(\psi(w)\setminus\mathsf{h}(\psi(w))),\mathsf{wv}(\mathsf{h}(\psi(w))))

(6.16)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{ins}(\varphi(\psi(w)\setminus\delta(\mathsf{sw}(w))),\mathsf{wv}(\delta(\mathsf{sw}(w))))

\displaystyle=\mathsf{ins}(\varphi(\psi(w\setminus\mathsf{sw}(w))),\mathsf{sw}(w))

\displaystyle=\mathsf{ins}(w\setminus\mathsf{sw}(w)),\mathsf{sw}(w))=w,

where the second equality is true since the rightmost hook of
\psi(w)
is by definition
\delta(\mathsf{sw}(\psi(w)))
, the third equality is true thanks to the equation (

\psi(w)
. The fourth equality holds by inductive hypothesis, since the length of

is strictly less than

We can also show the identity of equation (

of

\displaystyle\mathsf{lec}(\psi(w))

Remark 6.13
.

If

is the Hook factorization of a word

, then we can write

w=\mathsf{conc}(\cdots\mathsf{conc}(\mathsf{conc}(\gamma,\alpha_{1}),\alpha_{2})\cdots,\alpha_{k}),

where
\mathsf{conc}
is the binary operation that concatenates words, i.e.
\mathsf{conc}(w^{\prime},w^{\prime\prime})=w^{\prime}*w^{\prime\prime}
for all

We have that the map
\varphi
is nothing more than the map obtained by replacing each
\mathsf{conc}
with
\mathsf{ins}
, and each hook
\alpha_{i}
with its corresponding wave

, namely:

\varphi(w)=\mathsf{ins}(\cdots\mathsf{ins}(\mathsf{ins}(\gamma,\mathsf{sw}(\alpha_{1})),\mathsf{sw}(\alpha_{2}))\cdots,\mathsf{sw}(\alpha_{k})).

Theorem
6.11

W

\psi
will then be analogously the one that replaces waves

with the corresponding hooks

and insertion operations with concatenation operations.

In particular, the set of permutations

with

and whose rightmost hook has length at least

is in bijection, via

\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
with
\mathsf{des}(\sigma)=i
and whose special wave has length at least
n-k
.
