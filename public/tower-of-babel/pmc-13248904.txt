Development of a pandemic H5N1 influenza vaccine using MDCK suspension cells: A scalable and efficient production platform
Authors: Shaobo Zhang, Meihao Wang, Dongwu Jin, Jiayou Zhang, Jiamin Wang, Zhongren Ma, Jinliang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe persistent threat of H5N1 avian influenza pandemics necessitates the development of rapid and scalable vaccine manufacturing platforms as alternatives to traditional egg-based systems. We established a complete process for manufacturing an inactivated H5N1 vaccine using serum-free MDCK suspension cells, incorporating cell-line screening, systematic optimization of infection parameters, and scale-up in a bioreactor. High-titer virus propagation (1:1024 HA titer; 10.07 log10CCID50/mL) was achieved in a 75 L bioreactor within 48 h. A purification process employing ultrafiltration and two-step chromatography effectively reduced host cell protein and DNA to 2.20 µg/mL and 3.92 ng/mL, respectively. In a preliminary immunogenicity assessment, two immunizations with a 15 µg dose induced 100% seroconversion (geometric mean titer GMT = 183). This work demonstrates a scalable and efficient cell-based production platform for generating high-quality H5N1 vaccines, offering a promising strategy for pandemic preparedness.

Introduction

Influenza is an acute respiratory infectious disease caused by influenza virus. It poses a significant threat to global public health through both seasonal epidemics and sporadic pandemics.1 Among avian influenza A viruses, the H5N1 subtype is of particular concern due to its high pathogenicity and documented cases of zoonotic transmission to humans, carrying a substantial fatality rate.2 While sustained human-to-human transmission remains inefficient, the virus’s continuous evolution and potential for reassortment underscore the critical need for proactive and scalable vaccine strategies.3,4

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted vulnerabilities in global vaccine supply chains and the imperative for manufacturing technologies capable of rapidly responding to emerging threats.5 For influenza, most inactivated vaccines are still produced in embryonated chicken eggs, a system hampered by its dependence on specific pathogen-free (SPF) egg supply, long production cycles, and limited surge capacity.6–8 Additionally, some highly pathogenic avian influenza viruses exhibit suboptimal growth in eggs,9 and egg-adaptive mutations can alter viral antigenicity, potentially impacting vaccine effectiveness. Moreover, residual ovalbumin in egg‑based vaccines poses a risk of allergic reactions in susceptible individuals.10 Consequently, the World Health Organization (WHO) has long advocated for a transition to cell culture-based production, which offers superior scalability, process control, and reduced risk of egg-adaptive mutations.11

Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells have emerged as a leading cellular substrate for influenza virus production, with adherent MDCK cells already employed in licensed vaccines.12,13 The field is advancing toward serum-free suspension cultures, which eliminate serum-associated risks, simplify scale-up, and enable high-cell-density bioreactor processes.14–16 However, developing an optimized, industrial-scale suspension process is complex. It requires the selection of high-performing cell clones, meticulous optimization of infection parameters, and the establishment of rigorous purification protocols to remove process-related impurities such as host cell proteins (HCP) and DNA (HCD), the latter being crucial for addressing substrate safety concerns.17,18

However, comprehensive reports detailing an integrated process from clone selection to a purified, immunogenic vaccine candidate remain scarce. The principal innovation of this study lies in establishing and validating a complete and scalable manufacturing platform for an inactivated H5N1 vaccine using MDCK suspension cells. Our work systematically addresses the entire production chain: (1) screening and selection of a high-yield serum-free adapted cell line; (2) statistical optimization of critical infection parameters; (3) successful scale-up to an industrially relevant 75 L bioreactor; and (4) development of an efficient purification process that effectively clears HCP and HCD, and a preliminary evaluation of the immunogenicity of the final product in a preclinical model. The NIBRG-14 strain (a reverse genetics-derived candidate vaccine virus based on A/Vietnam/1194/2004) was selected as a well-characterized model for this platform proof-of-concept. This integrated approach provides a valuable blueprint for rapid and reliable cell-based vaccine production against pandemic influenza threats.

Materials and methods

Cells and virus

Adherent MDCK cells (American Type Culture Collection) were obtained via the Gansu Province Animal Cell Technology Innovation Center. These cells were used as indicator cells for CCID50 assay and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Lanzhou Bailing Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Lanzhou, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Lanzhou Minhai Biotechnology Engineering Co., Ltd., Lanzhou, China).

Five suspension lines (MDCK‑XF01 to XF05) were generated from adherent MDCK cells as described.19,20 Briefly, adherent cells were adapted to low serum (1% FBS) by gradual reduction, then to a proprietary serum‑free medium (MDCK‑SFM, Lanzhou Bailing Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) while adherent (using TrypLE for detachment), and finally acclimated to suspension growth in spinner flasks (initial density 2–3 × 106 cells/mL, 110–120 rpm, daily medium exchange for 2–3 weeks). Five clones were obtained by limiting dilution. Based on growth kinetics and virus productivity (Sections “Growth characteristics of serum-free adapted MDCK suspension cells”, “Susceptibility of MDCK suspension cells to H5N1 infection”), MDCK‑XF04 was selected. After selection, a master cell bank (P103) and working cell bank (P110) were established. Mycoplasma, sterility, and adventitious virus tests were negative (Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020, ICH Q5A). Genetic stability was confirmed over 20 passages (P110–P130). Production cells were used at P115–P125. Full characterization data are not disclosed due to intellectual property and clinical filing considerations.

The influenza A virus (IAV) H5N1 strain (NIBRG-14, a reverse genetics-derived candidate vaccine virus) was provided by the Wuhan Institute of Biological Products. The hemagglutinin (HA) gene is derived from the wild-type strain A/Vietnam/1194/2004 (H5N1), with the multibasic cleavage site removed for safety. The neuraminidase (NA) and the remaining six internal gene segments are derived from the high-growth A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (H1N1, PR8) strain to ensure robust replication in production systems. A working virus stock was prepared by amplification in embryonated chicken eggs, with an initial hemagglutination (HA) titer of 1:64 and a titer of 5.12 log10CCID50/mL.

Cell growth kinetics analysis

The five MDCK suspension cell lines were resuscitated and subcultured for more than three passages. For growth kinetic studies, cells were seeded at 2.0 × 105 cells/mL in 150 mL spinner flasks (40 mL working volume) and cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2, with 120 rpm agitation. Evaluation of cell susceptibility to virus infection

Cells were seeded at 3.0 × 105 cells/mL in spinner flasks with 2.5 µg/mL TPCK-trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Infection was initiated at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 × 10−5 at 34°C, 5% CO2, 120 rpm. Samples were collected every 12 h post-infection (hpi) for: (1) microscopic observation of cytopathic effect (CPE), (2) viability assessment, and (3) viral quantification via HA assay and 50% cell culture infectious dose (CCID50) determination. Experiments were terminated when cell viability dropped below 60%. All studies were performed in triplicate.

Optimization of virus culture conditions

Single-factor experiments

Four key factors were investigated independently: initial cell density (1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0 × 105 cells/mL), TPCK-trypsin concentration (1.25, 2.5, 3.75, 5.0 µg/mL), MOI (0.001, 0.0001, 0.00001, 0.000001), and harvest time (24, 36, 48, 60, 72 hpi). For each factor tested, other parameters were held constant at baseline values: cell density at 2.0 × 105 cells/mL (except when cell density was the variable), TPCK-trypsin at 2.5 µg/mL (except when trypsin was the variable), MOI at 1 × 10−5 (except when MOI was the variable), and harvest time at 60 hpi (except for harvest time kinetics studies) CPE, cell viability, and viral HA titer were monitored every 12 hpi. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Orthogonal experiments

Following single-factor tests, an L9 (34) orthogonal array was employed to optimize factor interactions. The factor levels for the orthogonal design were chosen based on the promising ranges identified in the single-factor experiments (Figures (1,2)). The factors and their three tested levels were: Cell Density (1.5, 2.0, 2.5 × 105 cells/mL), MOI (5.0 × 10−6, 1.0 × 10−5, 1.5 × 10−5), TPCK-Trypsin (3.50, 3.75, 4.00 µg/mL), and Harvest Time (48, 60, 72 hpi). Log10CCID50/mL was the primary response variable. Range analysis was performed to identify the optimal parameter combination. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Bioreactor scale-up

MDCK-XF04 cells were expanded in shake flasks and transferred to a 7.5 L bioreactor (5 L working volume, pH 7.2, DO 40%, 120 rpm, 37°C) as a seed train. Biomass was scaled up to a 75 L bioreactor. For production, H5N1 was inoculated into a 25 L bioreactor under optimized conditions (pH 7.2, DO 60%, 80 rpm, 34°C). The supernatant was harvested at 48 hpi. Bioreactor runs were performed twice.

Virus purification, inactivation, and quality assessment

The harvested virus supernatant was centrifuged at 4000×g for 30 min and filtered through a 2 + 0.45 μm capsule (Sartorius) to remove cell debris. The clarified virus was then concentrated and buffer‑exchanged into phosphate buffer (PB) using a 300 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) ultrafiltration membrane.

β‑Propiolactone was added to the concentrate at a final concentration of 1:4000 (v/v) and incubated at 4°C for 24 h with agitation (80 rpm), followed by hydrolysis at 37°C for 2 h. Complete inactivation was confirmed by three blind passages in 9–11‑d embryonated chicken eggs (10 eggs per passage); absence of hemagglutination activity after each passage indicated successful inactivation.

The inactivated concentrate was applied to a Capto™ Core 700 column (Cytiva) equilibrated with 150 mmol/L NaCl + 20 mmol/L PB, pH 7.5, at a linear flow rate of 100 cm/h, and the virus was collected in the flow‑through fraction. This flow‑through was diluted 2‑fold with 20 mmol/L PB (pH 7.5) to reduce the NaCl concentration, then loaded onto a Capto Q column (Cytiva). After washing, bound virus was eluted with 0.5 mol/L NaCl. The eluate was further concentrated and buffer‑exchanged into PBS using a 300 kDa MWCO membrane (0.1 m2) and finally filtered through a 0.22 μm filter.

All quality control tests followed the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020, Vol. III). Sterility was assessed by membrane filtration (General Chapter 1101). Endotoxin was measured by the limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) assay (Chapter 1143) with an acceptance criterion of <10 EU/mL. Hemagglutinin content was quantified by single radial immunodiffusion (SRID) using NIBSC standards. Total protein was determined by the Lowry method (Chapter 0731). Host cell protein (HCP) was measured using an MDCK HCP ELISA kit (Cygnus, Catalog F800, USA), and residual host cell DNA (HCD) was quantified by qPCR using an MDCK residual DNA detection kit (Huzhou Shenke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), following the manufacturers’ instructions. DNA fragment size distribution was analyzed by capillary electrophoresis (Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit), and β‑propiolactone residues were quantified by gas chromatography (Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020, General Chapter 0521).

Immunogenicity study in mice

Groups of female BALB/c mice (n = 10) were immunized intramuscularly with inactivated virus (1, 5, or 15 µg HA dose, without adjuvant) or PBS on day 0 and day 21. Sera were analyzed by hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay according to standard WHO protocols using 0.5% (v/v) chicken red blood cells. The homologous inactivated NIBRG-14 virus was used as the antigen. Seropositivity was defined as HI titer ≥1:40. Seroconversion rates and geometric mean titers (GMTs) were calculated. All animal studies were approved by the appropriate Animal Ethics Committee (Approval No. xbmu-sm-2025116) and were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Unpaired two-tailed t-tests or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test were used as appropriate. A p-value <.05 was considered significant. For analysis, viral HA titers were converted to and expressed as log2 values, and infectious virus titers (CCID50) are presented as log10 CCID50/mL. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

The final product is obtained after sterile filtration. Growth characteristics of serum-free adapted MDCK suspension cells

Initial screening excluded XF01 due to poor growth kinetics. The remaining four lines (XF02–XF05) were characterized over 120 h (Figure 3(A)). All exhibited classical sigmoidal growth with an exponential phase between 24 and 72 h. XF05 achieved the highest maximum cell density (1.58 × 106 cells/mL, Figure 3(B)). XF04 and XF05 had the shortest doubling times (~24 h, Figure 3(C))，which were significantly shorter than those of XF02 and XF03 (p < .01) and similar peak specific growth rates at 48 h (~0.037 h−1, Figure 3(D)). Based on an optimal combination of rapid proliferation, sustained high viability, and robust growth to high density, the MDCK-XF04 cell line was selected for all subsequent process development.

Susceptibility of MDCK suspension cells to H5N1 infection

To identify the most productive cell line, all four candidates were infected under standardized conditions. Microscopic evaluation revealed clear differences: XF04 and XF05 showed early CPE (cell rounding/aggregation) by 36 hpi and extensive lysis by 60–72 hpi, while XF02 and XF03 exhibited delayed, milder CPE (Figure 4(A)). These observations were corroborated by viability data; XF04 and XF05 viability dropped sharply after 48 hpi (<60% by 84 hpi), whereas XF02 and XF03 maintained high viability (>80%) throughout (Figure 4(B)). Critically, viral output differed substantially. XF04 and XF05 yielded detectable HA titers by 36 hpi, with XF04 achieving a peak titer of 1:1024 by 48 hpi – the highest and most rapid among all lines (Figure 4(C)). XF05 produced slightly slower kinetics and a lower peak titer, while XF02 and XF03 produced negligible or significantly delayed titers. Therefore, based on superior virus-induced cytopathicity, high viral antigen yield, and rapid production kinetics, MDCK-XF04 was unequivocally selected as the lead production cell line. Initial cell density

Cultures infected at 2.0 × 105 cells/mL (with MOI fixed at 1 × 10−5 and TPCK-trypsin at 2.5 µg/mL) showed efficient infection kinetics and achieved the highest HA titer (1:1024 at 60 hpi, Figure 5(A)). Lower densities (1.0 × 105 cells/mL) led to premature substrate exhaustion and lower final yields, while higher densities (3.0–4.0 × 105 cells/mL) resulted in delayed and less efficient infection, as evidenced by slower CPE progression (Figure 2(A), panels a-1 vs a-4) and prolonged high viability.

TPCK-Trypsin concentration

Both 3.75 and 5.0 µg/mL (with cell density fixed at 2.0 × 105 cells/mL and MOI at 1 × 10−5) supported equally high HA titers (1:1024 by 60 hpi, Figure 5(B)). However, the 3.75 µg/mL condition was selected as optimal to minimize potential trypsin-mediated antigen degradation and reduce impurity load in downstream processing, without compromising viral yield. Corresponding cell morphology is shown in Figure 4(B).

MOI

An MOI of 1 × 10−5 (with cell density fixed at 2.0 × 105 cells/mL and TPCK-trypsin at 2.5 µg/mL) yielded the highest final HA titer (1:1024 at 60 hpi, Figure 5(C)). A higher MOI (1 × 10−3) caused rapid, synchronous infection leading to swift cell death and a lower final yield, whereas a lower MOI (1 × 10−6) significantly delayed the production cycle (Figure 2(C), panel c-4). The 1 × 10−5 condition optimally balanced infection efficiency with sustained cellular substrate availability for multi-cycle amplification.

Harvest time

Under otherwise fixed optimal conditions (cell density = 2.0 × 105 cells/mL, MOI = 1 × 10−5, TPCK-trypsin = 3.75 µg/mL), we monitored both antigen (HA) and infectious virus (CCID50) titers (Figure 5(D)). The HA titer plateaued at 1:1024 between 60 and 72 hpi. In contrast, the infectious virus titer peaked significantly earlier at 48 hpi (8.15 log10CCID50/mL) before declining, indicating that harvesting at 48 hpi is critical for maximizing the yield of infectious virus particles, the key antigenic component for an inactivated vaccine.

Orthogonal experimental optimization

The cell density levels (150, 200, 250 × 104 cells/mL) in the orthogonal design were selected from the effective lower range identified in the single-factor experiment. Additionally, harvesting at 48 h reduces host cell lysis and the accumulation of host cell proteins and residual DNA, which is beneficial for downstream purification. Therefore, the final optimized and validated production parameters were: Initial Cell Density: 2.0 × 105 cells/mL, MOI: 5.0 × 10−6, TPCK‑Trypsin: 3.50 µg/mL, Harvest Time: 48 h post‑infection.

Immunogenicity of the inactivated vaccine

The preliminary immunogenic potency of the final vaccine was evaluated in a murine model. A clear and statistically significant dose–response relationship was observed (Figure 7). After a two-dose regimen, the group receiving the 15 µg HA dose achieved 100% seroconversion with a high geometric mean titer (GMT) of 183. The 5 µg dose also induced 100% seroconversion but with a lower GMT of 98, while the 1 µg dose resulted in only 50% seroconversion (GMT = 30). All control mice remained seronegative. The dose-dependent HI antibody responses indicate that the inactivated virus preparation retained its capacity to elicit functional, virus-specific antibodies. Discussion

This study establishes a fully integrated, scalable platform for the production of an inactivated H5N1 vaccine using serum-free MDCK suspension cells. The high infectious titer (10.07 log10 CCID50/mL) achieved in a 75 L bioreactor robustly demonstrates the industrial relevance and efficiency of this platform for vaccine virus manufacturing, a critical requirement for pandemic response.5,22

Cell line selection and platform advantages

The success of the selected MDCK-XF04 clone can be attributed to its excellent growth kinetics and exceptional permissiveness to H5N1 infection. Compared to other suspension cell lines reported for influenza vaccine production (e.g., PER.C6, EB66, CAP), MDCK cells offer distinct advantages: they are already approved for use in licensed influenza vaccines (e.g., Flucelvax®), which facilitates regulatory acceptance, and they exhibit superior natural susceptibility to influenza viruses without requiring genetic modification.12 Recent advances have demonstrated that suspension-adapted MDCK cells can achieve high cell densities and produce high yields of influenza virus.23 More importantly, the first Chinese MDCK cell-based quadrivalent influenza vaccine has successfully completed phase III clinical trials, confirming the regulatory feasibility and industrial potential of this platform in China.24 The adaptability of MDCK cells to serum-free suspension culture, demonstrated here, aligns with the industry’s goal of eliminating serum-associated risks and enabling high-density bioreactor processes.14–16 The peak infectious titer of 10.07 log10 CCID50/mL achieved in our 75 L bioreactor is comparable to or higher than those reported for H5N1 in other cell-based systems (e.g., 108−109 TCID50/mL, Hu et al., 2008) and for H1N1 in MDCK suspension (~3.6 log10 HAU/100 μL, Wu et al., 2021).25,26 Additionally, this study provides detailed downstream purification data – including step recoveries, impurity clearance, and regulatory-relevant quality attributes – that are less commonly reported for MDCK-based H5N1 vaccines.

Comparison with egg‑based platforms

Traditional egg‑based influenza vaccine production has well‑documented limitations: dependence on SPF egg supply, long production cycles (4–6 months from strain identification to vaccine availability), and limited surge capacity during pandemics.6–8 Moreover, egg‑adaptive mutations can alter viral antigenicity and potentially reduce vaccine effectiveness,9 and residual ovalbumin poses a risk of allergic reactions in susceptible individuals.10 The cell‑based platform described here directly addresses these limitations. The NIBRG-14 strain used in this study is a reverse genetics-derived vaccine strain originally generated for high growth in eggs, yet it also grew efficiently in our suspension MDCK system, achieving a peak titer of 10.07 log10 CCID50/mL. Notably, studies comparing cell-derived and egg-derived influenza vaccines have demonstrated that mammalian cell-derived vaccines induce serum antibodies that are more cross-reactive against divergent strains, while egg-derived vaccines tend to induce antibodies more specific to the homologous antigen.27 This antigenic fidelity of cell-derived vaccines represents an additional advantage for pandemic preparedness, as it may provide broader protection against emerging variants.

Scalability and production metrics

The two 75 L bioreactor runs together yielded approximately 9,000 monovalent doses (0.5 mL/dose, based on 15 μg HA per dose). This output aligns with the dose range used in clinical studies of inactivated H5N1 vaccines.21 The process is directly scalable to larger volumes (e.g., 500–2000 L) commonly used in commercial manufacturing. The entire upstream process from cell thawing to virus harvest takes approximately 7–8 d (including cell expansion and 48 h virus propagation), substantially shorter than egg‑based production timelines. The use of serum‑free, chemically defined medium eliminates batch‑to‑batch variability associated with animal serum and reduces the risk of adventitious agent contamination.28

Immunogenicity and adjuvant considerations

The robust, dose‑dependent HI antibody responses observed in mice confirm that our optimized production and purification process successfully preserved the native conformation of the viral hemagglutinin (HA). The mouse model is the standard preclinical model for influenza vaccine immunogenicity assessment, as recommended by WHO and NMPA guidelines, and is widely used for proof‑of‑concept studies. In this study, we evaluated the intrinsic immunogenicity of the purified antigen without adjuvant. The 15 μg dose achieved 100% seroconversion and a GMT of 183, indicating that the antigen itself is immunogenic. This design is consistent with seasonal influenza vaccines currently licensed in China, which are formulated without adjuvants. Our findings are supported by a recent study on a serum‑free suspension MDCK cell‑based H7N9 vaccine, which also demonstrated robust HI and neutralizing antibody responses in mice without adjuvant.29 Notably, a phase I clinical trial of an MDCK cell‑derived H5N1 whole‑virion vaccine reported that the addition of aluminum adjuvant further increased neutralizing antibody titers compared to the non‑adjuvanted group (11.32 vs. 4.52, p = .02),30 suggesting that while adjuvant is not required for immunogenicity, it may offer dose‑sparing benefits. However, for pandemic influenza vaccines, adjuvants, such as MF59 or AS03, are known to enhance immune responses and enable dose sparing. Adjuvantation therefore represents a promising direction for future optimization to reduce the required antigen dose. We also acknowledge that a direct comparison with an egg‑derived H5N1 antigen was not performed in this study; such a comparator would be valuable to further benchmark the relative immunogenicity of the MDCK‑derived vaccine candidate and will be included in future work.

Limitations and future directions

While this study successfully established a scalable MDCK suspension platform, several limitations should be acknowledged. The immunogenicity assessment was limited to HI titers in a single mouse strain (BALB/c). Neutralizing antibody titers and cellular immune responses were not evaluated, nor was the vaccine tested in other animal models such as ferrets, which are considered the gold standard for influenza transmission studies. Only the NIBRG-14 vaccine strain was tested; whether the platform performs equally well with contemporary circulating H5N1 clades remains to be determined. The immunogenicity study was performed without adjuvant; while this allowed assessment of intrinsic immunogenicity, adjuvanted formulations should be explored for dose sparing. A direct comparison with an egg‑derived H5N1 antigen was not performed and will be included in future work. Finally, the immunogenicity study was conducted with a single batch; repeat studies with multiple independently produced batches would further strengthen reproducibility. Despite these limitations, this study provides a robust proof‑of‑concept for an MDCK suspension cell‑based H5N1 vaccine platform that is scalable, efficient, and capable of rapid pandemic response.

Beyond its application for pandemic vaccine production, this scalable platform can reliably generate large quantities of highly purified virus, serving as a valuable tool for fundamental virological and immunological studies that require standardized virus materials.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have developed and demonstrated a complete, scalable manufacturing process for an inactivated H5N1 influenza vaccine using MDCK suspension cells. Key achievements include the selection of a high-yield cell line, systematic optimization of infection parameters yielding high virus titers in a pilot-scale bioreactor, establishment of an efficient purification process that reduces host cell impurities to levels exceeding international standards, and the generation of a vaccine candidate that shows promising preliminary immunogenicity immune in a preclinical model. This integrated cell-based platform presents a promising, efficient, and scalable alternative to traditional egg-based manufacturing, with strong potential to enhance the speed and flexibility of global responses to influenza pandemics.31,32
