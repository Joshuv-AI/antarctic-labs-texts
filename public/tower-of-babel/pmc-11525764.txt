Effective deep brain stimulation for obsessive-compulsive disorder after failed anterior capsulotomy: illustrative cases
Authors: Anthony K Allam, Nisha Giridharan, Mohammed Hasen, Garrett P Banks, Gabriel Reyes, Huy Dang, Katherine E Kabotyanski, Alyssa G Hertz, Sarah R Heilbronner, Nicole Provenza, Eric A Storch, Wayne K Goodman, Sameer A Sheth
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDObsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a psychiatric condition characterized by recurrent, unwanted thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive behaviors (compulsions) performed to relieve distress related to the obsessions. For patients with severe illness refractory to first-line pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy, neurosurgical treatments such as deep brain stimulation (DBS) and stereotactic lesioning are an option. The choice between DBS and lesioning is often driven by patient preference, but these options are not mutually exclusive. Here, the authors highlight the success of one surgical therapy (DBS) after the failure of another (lesioning).OBSERVATIONSTwo patients with severe, treatment-refractory OCD underwent DBS lead implantation targeting the ventral capsule/ventral striatum after they did not attain any worthwhile benefit from a previous anterior capsulotomy. Both patients showed significant improvement (≥ 35% reduction in Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale [YBOCS] score) of their OCD symptoms at the long-term follow-up after DBS. One patient experienced a 37% reduction in symptom severity as measured by the YBOCS, and the other patient experienced a 47% reduction.LESSONSDBS and lesioning procedures are both effective surgical options for patients with intractable OCD. Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a debilitating psychiatric condition characterized by intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive, ritualized actions (compulsions).1 Patients perform compulsions to alleviate the distress and anxiety stemming from their obsessions. The prevalence of OCD in the US population is approximately 2%–3%.2 First-line therapies include pharmacotherapy and a type of cognitive behavioral therapy called “exposure and response prevention” (ERP).3 However, an estimated 20%–35% of patients do not obtain an adequate response to these standard treatments.3 To be classified as treatment-resistant OCD, adequate trials of medications and expert ERP must have failed. The patients whose condition meets established criteria of severity, chronicity, and refractoriness may be eligible for neurosurgical treatments.3

Modern neurosurgical treatments for OCD include deep brain stimulation (DBS) and stereotactic lesioning procedures such as capsulotomy, cingulotomy, and subcaudate tractotomy.4–6 Of the lesioning procedures in contemporary use, the anterior capsulotomy targeting the ventral portion of the anterior limb of the internal capsule (vALIC) is the most common.3, 4, 7 Radiofrequency ablation, radiosurgery, laser interstitial thermal therapy, and focused ultrasound are all methods utilized to create the lesions.8–12 The DBS target approved for OCD by the US Food and Drug Administration under a humanitarian device exemption is the region of the ventral capsule/ventral striatum (VC/VS).13, 14 The VC/VS is a hub in the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuits implicated in the pathogenesis of OCD.15 Modulation of the white matter fibers coursing through this region is the likely substrate for the effects of DBS.16, 17 The subjacent gray matter structures (VS anterior to the anterior commissure [AC] and bed nucleus of the stria terminalis [BNST] posterior to the AC) are important landmarks for targeting.18 Thus, both the capsulotomy procedure and DBS target roughly the same region (the vALIC or VC/VS) and likely achieve their therapeutic efficacy through overlapping mechanisms.

Capsulotomy and DBS appear to have comparable efficacy based on existing literature.19 Approximately two-thirds of patients have a full response (defined as having at least a 35% reduction in Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale [YBOCS] scores) following either procedure.19–21 Nonetheless, no head-to-head randomized controlled clinical trials have compared the efficacy of these two modalities for OCD, and the choice between them is often determined by provider and patient preferences. DBS offers the potential for reversibility and adjustability, as opposed to the nonmodifiable nature of lesions. Stimulation parameters (e.g., frequency, contact configuration, and amplitude) can be adjusted to strive toward an optimal therapeutic response while minimizing side effects. However, lesioning procedures are beneficial for patients who are unable to attend frequent postoperative programming visits, which is often true for OCD patients who must travel to one of the few institutions that perform these procedures. Additionally, lesioning does not carry long-term hardware-related risks and inconveniences such as device malfunction, infection, and the need for battery replacements.

In this case series, we present two patients with severe, treatment-refractory OCD in whom a complete response following an anterior capsulotomy was not achieved. Given their persistent symptoms impacting their quality of life, we implanted DBS leads into the VC/VS region. Both patients obtained a clinical response. We provide these examples of successful DBS after an unsuccessful lesioning procedure to encourage practitioners to be persistent in treating this challenging disorder. While both procedures target the same region (vALIC or VC/VS), DBS can be successful even after an ablation of this region.

Illustrative Cases

A multidisciplinary group comprising a psychologist (E.A.S.), a psychiatrist (W.K.G.), and a neurosurgeon (S.A.S.) convened to discuss the cases. The corresponding author (S.A.S.) performed the capsulotomy in case 1 and the DBS lead implantations in both cases. Patients underwent standard preoperative imaging with stereotactic computed tomography and 3-T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain. The VC/VS region with subjacent VS and BNST was still well visualized despite the patients’ prior capsulotomy lesions. We planned trajectories to the vALIC both anterior to the AC (toward the subjacent VS) and posterior to the AC (toward the subjacent BNST) and used intraoperative awake testing to adjudicate between them, as previously described.18 The capsulotomy lesion is typically performed several millimeters anterior to the AC, whereas DBS typically targets the region immediately anterior or immediately posterior to the AC.3 Thus, the DBS target is usually 3–8 mm posterior to the typical capsulotomy target.

Patients were seen in the clinic approximately 2 weeks after surgery for the first programming session. Patients attended periodic visits thereafter for programming adjustments and evaluation of OCD symptom severity.

Case 1

A 30-year-old man with severe, treatment-resistant OCD presented with “just-right” obsessions. He experienced an overwhelming necessity to direct extreme attention toward arbitrary objects, feeling compelled to engage with them until a sense of correctness was achieved. He tried numerous medications and ERP with no significant improvement in his symptoms. Sixteen years later, the patient underwent bilateral radiosurgical capsulotomy using the technique described previously3 (Fig. 1). However, his symptoms persisted, with a YBOCS score of 30, indicating severe OCD. Nineteen years after the patient’s initial presentation, following several multidisciplinary discussions, bilateral DBS of the VC/VS was recommended. Case 2

A 31-year-old man with severe OCD and comorbid major depressive disorder was initially seen at an outside institution. The patient had an early childhood onset of OCD; however, his major symptoms started at the age of 19 years after a traumatic event. He became severely depressed and had frequent anxiety attacks. At that time, he started exhibiting compulsive handwashing. He tried numerous medications, including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, mood stabilizers, antipsychotics, psychostimulants, anxiolytics, and various antidepressants. In addition to medical management, he underwent years of ERP for his OCD, over 120 electroconvulsive therapy sessions, and more than 20 treatments of transcranial magnetic stimulation for his concomitant depression. Nine years later, the patient underwent a radiofrequency anterior capsulotomy (Fig. 3). He demonstrated improvement for 3 months before his symptoms relapsed. The lesion in this case was located more dorsally than that in case 1. This more dorsal location is less commonly used in current practice but was attempted in the past.3 Interestingly, the more dorsal location is relatively proximate to the white matter tract that is emerging as important to modulate with DBS and is not far from the area that we identified as most predictive of an improved outcome in a recent study.22, 23 Thus, even though the two lesions are different in location, both are within the region of the capsulotomy, and both have merit as lesion locations. One month postoperatively, the patient reported that his depressive symptoms had significantly improved, with minor improvement in OCD symptom severity. At a follow-up visit 1 year later, the patient reported marked improvement in his OCD symptoms as well as dramatic improvements in depression, affect, and cognition. This pattern is typical; early signs of improvement often appear as reduced anxiety and enhanced mood, whereas substantial relief from OCD symptoms can take several months to emerge. The patient was able to be weaned off his medications and discontinue maintenance electroconvulsive therapy, which was used to control depressive symptoms accompanied by suicidality. The patient’s final YBOCS score at the 2-year follow-up was 18, a 47% reduction from his baseline score of 34.

Discussion

The neurobiological underpinnings of OCD are thought to be related to pathophysiological activity in cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuits involving various regions of the prefrontal cortex.24, 25 Lesioning and neuroimaging studies point to the dysregulation of the orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, striatum, and thalamus as key drivers of the disease.26–28 Traversing the ALIC are bidirectional fibers connecting the prefrontal cortex with the thalamus, subthalamic nucleus, and brainstem. In particular, the vALIC contains fibers traveling from and to the orbitofrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex. Thus, the vALIC has been utilized as a surgical target.

Whereas DBS has similarities to lesion procedures at the surface level (indeed, DBS lead insertion in patients with movement disorders often causes a noticeable and beneficial “microlesion” effect), the mechanism of DBS is far more complex and nuanced in its modulation of circuits.29 Suetens et al. discovered that patients with an ablative capsulotomy had significantly less activity in the medial dorsal thalamus and head of the caudate when compared to patients who had DBS of the same region.30 However, DBS regulates cortical tracts in a more complex manner through both orthodromic and antidromic stimulation.30 Furthermore, DBS has been shown to cause local inhibition, modulate temporal firing patterns, and even lead to neurogenesis.31 These concepts are consistent with the explanation that stimulation in these patients modulated the remaining fibers that had not been previously lesioned and thereby produced the observed meaningful symptomatic improvement.

Observations

We report two patients with severe, treatment-refractory OCD in whom lesioning procedures had failed and who subsequently attained long-term symptom improvement and response status (≥ 35% YBOCS score reduction) following DBS. Often, when a DBS system is implanted after a lesioning procedure, the target is a site different site from that of the initial lesion. This strategy is frequently used in treating movement disorders like Parkinson’s disease and dystonia.32–35 Zhang et al. described the use of habenular DBS after the failure of both a capsulotomy and a cingulotomy for treating patients with OCD.36 The authors reported a significant improvement in OCD symptoms after the implantation of DBS leads.

However, using DBS in the same general location as a prior ablation has rarely been reported. In these cases, DBS leads have been implanted bilaterally in a patient with a prior unilateral lesion, thereby still modulating the intact tissue.37, 38 Only a few case reports have illustrated the success of DBS following bilateral lesioning in movement disorders.39 However, there are no reports of DBS targeting the same general region as a prior ablation for OCD. Our case series illustrates that even in this context, the modulation of the remaining neural tissue can result in clinically significant improvements in OCD symptoms.

This leaves open the question of the mechanism by which DBS was able to achieve efficacy despite a substantial amount of nearby tissue being damaged. In these cases, the active DBS contacts were positioned ventrally and posteriorly relative to the capsulotomy lesions. Intriguingly, because vALIC fibers from the orbitofrontal cortex course ventrally in the fascicles embedded within the VS and AC, they may have been spared by the capsulotomy lesions but impacted by subsequent DBS.40, 41 Modifying connections from the orbitofrontal cortex is likely critical to achieving efficacy in DBS treatment for OCD.42

Lessons

Managing treatment-resistant OCD poses significant challenges, particularly following surgical interventions like an ablative capsulotomy that fail to result in symptomatic improvement. However, our findings indicate that DBS of the VC/VS can markedly alleviate OCD symptoms, even in patients who do not respond to lesioning of the same region, thus underscoring the importance of persistence in pursuing treatment approaches for this complex disorder.

When discussing options following failed lesioning, deciding between a repeat lesion procedure and DBS involves nuanced deliberation by a multidisciplinary board. Repeat lesioning may be favorable in certain circumstances: e.g., the original lesion was effective for a period of time but then regressed in size (as seen on MRI) over a time period coinciding with symptom relapse. In this case, one could reasonably hope that repeating (and possibly expanding) the lesion could be quite effective. On the other hand, if lesioning did not show improvement in OCD symptoms even for a short period of time, then stimulation may be preferable.

While this report is a retrospective, single-center, small cohort study, which limits the widespread applicability of our results, the observed treatment outcomes provide clinically significant and promising insights.
