Adapting multicomponent tobacco use treatment strategies for implementation in the Lebanese primary healthcare system: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Dima S Bteddini, Catherine El Ashkar, Ruba Abla, Magda Montague, Lama El Kadi, Tamar Kabakian-Khasholian, Ji-Hyun Lee, Ryan Theis, Jihan Khaywa, Derek Li, Randa S Hamadeh, Kenneth Ward, Mona H Osman, Donna R Shelley, Maya Romani, Rima Nakkash, Ramzi G Salloum, Jennifer H LeLaurin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionTobacco use treatment (TUT) remains largely absent from Lebanon’s primary healthcare system, even though smoking prevalence ranks among the highest in the region. Although Lebanon has ratified the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control, TUT has not been integrated into routine care. This study describes the adaptation of evidence-based, phone-delivered TUT strategies for integration within Lebanon’s primary healthcare network.MethodsWe conducted an explanatory sequential mixed-methods study. Data sources included surveys (n=107) and semistructured interviews (n=24) with healthcare providers, focus group discussions with patients who smoke (n=61) and workflow assessments of 24 primary healthcare centres. Quantitative data were analysed descriptively, and qualitative data underwent rapid framework analysis. We coded adaptations using the Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications to Evidence-based Interventions (FRAME).ResultsAdaptations were informed by contextual determinants, including gaps in electronic health record infrastructure, patients’ mental health needs, and misconceptions and social norms surrounding waterpipe use. Additional considerations included provider-level constraints, such as limited time to promote tobacco cessation (47% of providers reported having sufficient time), limited cessation training (42% reported sufficient knowledge of pharmacotherapy and 15% reported knowledge of how to prescribe cessation medications) and low confidence in counselling patients about the risks and benefits of these medications (50%). Adaptations included adding paper-based reminders to address variability in electronic health record systems; providing concise, role-specific materials and nicotine replacement therapy guidance for providers; incorporating mental health referral pathways and strengthening counselling content on the harms of waterpipe use; substituting nicotine patches with nicotine gum due to product availability; and developing instructional videos and self-help materials to support implementation.ConclusionsTailoring implementation strategies to contextual realities in Lebanon aimed to improve the relevance, feasibility and potential uptake of TUT. This formative study provides an example of a data-driven, stakeholder-engaged approach to adapting evidence-based TUT for low-resource primary healthcare settings.Trial registration numberNCT05628389.Keywords: Implementation Science, Smoking Reduction, Primary Health Care

Introduction

Tobacco use remains the leading cause of preventable disease, disability and death globally due to its adverse health effects, including cancer, cardiovascular and respiratory diseases.1 2 While many regions have made remarkable progress in curbing the tobacco epidemic, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) continue to lag behind.3 Article 14 of the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC) addresses tobacco use treatment (TUT), and its implementation guidelines recommend integrating behavioural and pharmacological TUT into existing health systems as a key step towards comprehensive cessation support.4

Strong evidence supports the effectiveness of TUT at both clinical and population levels.5–9 However, most evidence on TUT implementation originates from high-income countries,10 raising concerns about feasibility and appropriateness in LMIC contexts, where adaptations to local settings are often required.11 12 The reliance on evidence from high-income countries poses a major barrier to implementing TUT in LMICs.11–13

Lebanon, an Eastern Mediterranean country, has one of the highest smoking prevalence rates worldwide.14 A national household survey conducted in 2019 estimated cigarette use at 48.6% among men and 21.5% among women, while waterpipe use reached 32.7% and 46.2%, respectively.14 Data from the National Cancer Registry (2005–2015) revealed high lung cancer incidence—the highest for females and second highest for males in the Middle East and North Africa.15 Since 2019, Lebanon has faced a complex and multifaceted socioeconomic crisis, characterised by currency collapse, antigovernment protests, a large refugee influx, the COVID-19 pandemic, the Beirut Port explosion, mass emigration of healthcare workers, and, most recently, the 2024 and 2026 war.16–20 This crisis has placed enormous strain on an already resource-limited health system.18 21 22 The number of beneficiaries accessing government primary healthcare centres more than doubled between 2019 and 2023, reaching 3.4 million.23 Despite its resilience, the system continues to struggle to sustain essential services.21

Lebanon ratified the WHO FCTC in 2005,24 yet implementation of Article 14 has not been prioritised, and TUT remains largely absent from the national healthcare system. The in-person tobacco cessation programme at the American University of Beirut Medical Center is among the few available clinical cessation services,25 presenting an opportunity to adapt it for telephone delivery within primary healthcare centres. Telephone delivery was selected based on prior pilot work demonstrating the feasibility and acceptability of the intervention, as well as stakeholder feedback from primary healthcare centres indicating the lack of established cessation referral pathways within primary care settings. Formative findings further confirmed widespread mobile phone access across patient groups supporting the equity and scalability of this delivery modality in the Lebanese context. This paper describes the process of adapting TUT strategies for implementation in Lebanon’s primary healthcare centres, as part of a trial whose protocol has been previously published.26

Methods

This paper reports findings from a mixed-methods formative study conducted to adapt an existing TUT programme for telephone delivery, informed by input from people who smoke, healthcare providers and other key stakeholders.26 The study informed key adaptations to (1) phone-based counselling; (2) nicotine replacement therapy (NRT); (3) provider training aimed at enhancing clinicians’ capacity to systematically screen patients for tobacco use and deliver brief cessation advice; and (4) electronic reminders integrated into the electronic health record (EHR) system to promote consistent application of TUT clinical guidelines. Adaptations were characterised using the Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications to Evidence-based Interventions (FRAME),27 which captures the complex implementation context. Aligning with the FRAME, adaptations were defined broadly as changes made to improve the fit of the intervention to the local context, including modifications to existing intervention components or delivery processes and the addition of new supportive elements to address identified implementation barriers.

The study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies and the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR).

Study setting

Lebanon’s Ministry of Public Health, the main authority for national health policy formulation, funding and implementation, established the national primary healthcare network in 1996.28 29 This network comprises 327 primary healthcare centres across the country, most affiliated with non-governmental organisations or municipalities.29 30 The Ministry of Public Health provides in-kind support to these centres, which deliver a comprehensive package of health services, including consultations, vaccinations, medications for acute and chronic conditions, maternal and child health, non-communicable disease care, sexual and reproductive health, nutrition services, mental healthcare, disability support, dental services and health promotion.19 28 All centres use a unified EHR system developed by the Ministry of Public Health in 2016.31 We recruited 24 primary healthcare centres from this national network in the Beirut metropolitan area, the largest urban region in Lebanon, which comprises approximately one-third of the national population. Centres were purposively selected based on predefined eligibility criteria, including having more than 500 unique patient visits annually, to ensure the feasibility of achieving the target sample size. Recruitment was facilitated by the Ministry of Public Health.

Patient and public involvement

Patients who use tobacco (cigarettes and/or waterpipe) were engaged during the formative phase of the study through focus groups. Their insights guided adaptations to telephone-delivered TUT to address barriers among primary healthcare patients in Lebanon. During these discussions, patients also reviewed the educational materials, and their feedback led to revisions to improve their clarity, cultural relevance and usability. Overall, patient input helped refine strategies to enhance feasibility and cultural appropriateness. In addition, patients were represented on the Stakeholder Advisory Committee convened for this study.

Data collection and measures

We conducted an explanatory sequential mixed-methods study,32 starting with a quantitative survey of providers, followed by semistructured interviews with providers and focus group discussions with primary healthcare patients who smoke. Additional data sources included workflow assessments and stakeholder consultations. Data collection, summarised in table 1, occurred from March 2023 to May 2024. Methodological triangulation was achieved by collecting data from multiple stakeholder groups (providers and patients) and using complementary qualitative methods (interviews and focus groups). Minor adaptations to procedures were made during data collection, including refinements to question phrasing for clarity and emphasis on emerging implementation barriers.

Healthcare provider surveys

Healthcare provider surveys (n=107) were collected from 24 primary healthcare centres in Beirut, Lebanon. The target sample size (n=120 total) was based on feasibility and the total number of physicians and nurses across the 24 primary healthcare centres. Recruitment was facilitated by centre directors. Out of the 175 providers contacted, 107 (61.1%) completed the survey and were included in the final analysis. Healthcare providers working in participating centres were eligible to participate and were recruited using purposive sampling to ensure representation of different roles within each centre, primarily physicians and nurses. Data were collected either in person at the centre or virtually, and surveys were administered by a member of the research team. A standardised questionnaire was used for all participants, and the same instrument and procedures were applied across in-person and virtual data collection. Surveys assessed providers’ utilisation of TUT,33 as well as knowledge, skills, professional roles, beliefs, motivation and goals, using items adapted from Amemori et al,34 rated on a 5-point Likert scale (strongly agree to strongly disagree).

Focus group discussions and interviews

Nine focus group discussions were conducted with 61 patients recruited from two centres chosen for diversity in population, volume and accessibility. Eligible patients were ≥18 years old and smoked cigarettes and/or waterpipe tobacco daily. Patients were selected via convenience sampling, with recruitment facilitated by centre staff. Focus group discussions explored patients’ risk perceptions, quitting history, experience with and attitudes towards cessation programmes, and participation barriers and facilitators. Participants also provided feedback and suggested modifications for educational materials.

A total of 24 interviews were conducted with healthcare providers (11 physicians, 13 nurses), including 22 in-person and two online interviews. All participants had completed the pre-interview survey and were selected based on their central role in the implementation process at their centre (medical director or nurse supervisor). Interviews assessed providers’ tobacco use assessment practices, training needs, implementation barriers and facilitators, and perceptions of the acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility of the TUT strategies.

Following consent, semistructured interviews and focus group discussions were conducted in Arabic and audio-recorded using a digital recorder and Webex for remote interviews, except for two interviews in which participants declined recording and detailed notes were taken instead. All recordings were transcribed, translated into English and back-translated into Arabic. A bilingual team member checked all transcripts against the recordings to ensure data integrity and reviewed all translations to ensure linguistic and cultural accuracy. Transcripts were deidentified prior to analysis to protect participant confidentiality.

Semistructured interview and focus group discussion guides were developed based on implementation science frameworks and study objectives and were iteratively refined during data collection. The team facilitating focus group discussions and interviews included Master of Public Health-level research assistants trained in qualitative methods and implementation science–guided data collection. The research team engaged in reflexive practices through regular debriefings throughout data collection and analysis.

Stakeholder consultations

Purposeful individual consultations were held with a diverse group of stakeholders, including healthcare providers, experts from the Primary Health Care Department at the Ministry of Public Health and directors of primary healthcare centres. Stakeholders provided in-depth, context-specific knowledge of the health system and operational realities of TUT delivery in primary care. Field notes and meeting summaries were systematically documented and reviewed by the research team. This input provided feedback on the feasibility, acceptability and appropriateness of planned TUT strategies and informed iterative adaptations to align with local priorities, workflows and resource constraints.

Workflow assessments

Workflow assessments were conducted in all 24 centres through interviews with centre directors or staff most familiar with the clinical workflow. The assessments aimed to optimise TUT integration and identify factors that could influence adoption. Current clinic policies, patient flow, systems, and staff roles and responsibilities were documented. Notes were taken by team members, cross-validated with healthcare provider interviews and used to create workflow diagrams for each centre.

Data analysis

For quantitative data, we generated descriptive statistics—including mean (SD); ranges and frequencies—using RStudio. All Likert scale items were measured on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 ‘Strongly disagree’ to 5 ‘Strongly agree’. For descriptive purposes, Likert scale responses for selected items were reported as the proportion of respondents endorsing ‘Agree/Strongly agree’ and ‘Disagree/Strongly disagree’. Items were additionally summarised using means (SD) for the stratified analysis by professional role. For the qualitative data, we used a framework analysis approach for rapid analysis of interviews and focus groups.35 We developed a template framework based on the interview guide to extract and summarise data relevant to our study aims. We also included fields to capture content that fell outside of our prespecified domains. Two independent researchers coded each transcript. An audit trail was maintained documenting all stages of data analysis, including coding decisions, codebook revisions and analytic memos to ensure transparency and support confirmability. Team members who conducted the interviews and focus groups contributed to the analysis and interpretation of the data. The process involved connecting the quantitative and qualitative data sources, with quantitative data followed by qualitative interviews complementing and expanding on findings from the quantitative data.36

Figure 1 presents provider survey responses on NRT-related knowledge, skill and self-efficacy, as well as perceived time availability to promote smoking cessation. Overall, 42% reported having sufficient therapeutic knowledge of pharmaceutical products for tobacco cessation, while only 15% reported knowing how to prescribe these products to patients ready to quit. About 50% felt confident counselling patients on the risks and benefits of cessation medications. Overall, 47% reported having sufficient time to promote tobacco abstinence. When stratified by professional role, physicians reported significantly higher scores than nurses and other providers. Physicians reported greater therapeutic knowledge of smoking cessation pharmacotherapy (mean 3.4 vs 2.7, p=0.001), higher confidence in prescribing cessation medications (2.9 vs 2.0, p<0.001), and greater confidence in counselling patients on risks and benefits (3.6 vs 3.0, p=0.007). Provider interviews

Electronic health records

Findings from provider interviews indicated that centres use multiple methods for managing patient data, including the national EHR system, centre-specific EHR systems and paper-based systems. A similar finding was observed in the workflow assessments. Providers reported technical issues with the national system, often using paper forms initially and entering data into the EHR later. In many centres, EHRs are updated post-visit. Although electronic reminders were viewed positively, providers anticipated challenges related to delays in updating the EHR preventing a timely check of the reminder, limited computer access (eg, only in the triage room) and the need to accommodate multiple EHR systems. Some providers noted that documentation within the EHR is typically performed by nurses rather than physicians, which causes reminders to appear at suboptimal times or be perceived as irrelevant by physicians.

Provider knowledge and experience

Qualitative findings revealed gaps in providers’ knowledge regarding NRT medications, limited awareness of their side effects and minimal prescribing experience. These findings align with the survey results, demonstrating convergence between qualitative and quantitative data. Providers recommended incorporating more information on medications into provider trainings to support implementation.

Mental health challenges

Providers noted that many patients use tobacco as a coping mechanism for stress and may have no intention to quit amid the ongoing socioeconomic crisis. One physician noted, “Most of those who smoke are anxious and depressed, and most of them have mental disorders. So, it is not only about smoking”. Additional patient-level stressors included demanding work environments, insufficient time for physical activity or recreation, and limited access to exercise facilities. These contextual factors were perceived to contribute to the high prevalence of waterpipe smoking, particularly among young people. Providers emphasised that mental health support is an essential component to facilitate changes in patients’ smoking behaviour and intention to quit.

Time constraints

Providers identified lack of time and heavy workload as barriers to screening patients for tobacco use and providing cessation advice, consistent with survey findings. Integrating the programme into existing workflows was considered challenging unless activities were brief. This was particularly difficult in centres with personnel shortages or low staff salaries. Providers recommended that the process be clear, well-planned and organised to allow quick and easy implementation. They also suggested that provider training include structured, well-defined roles to facilitate easier adoption and emphasised the need for the treatment approach to be brief and efficient to accommodate busy schedules.

In addition, two key findings emerged from the consultations with stakeholders: first, NRT patches are currently unavailable in Lebanon; second, provider training should be streamlined and focused, clearly outlining providers’ roles in the TUT, especially given their limited time and heavy workload, which is consistent with findings from the provider interviews.

Patient focus groups

We conducted nine focus groups with 61 participants: five focus groups with primarily cigarette users (n=36) and four with primarily waterpipe users (n=25). Participants had a mean age of 41.6 years (SD 14.0), 54.1% were female, and 52.5% reported a previous quit attempt.

Mental health challenges

Findings from focus groups highlighted the impact of the ongoing economic and political crisis on mental health, smoking behaviour, quit intentions and relapse. One participant noted, “If it weren’t for the tragic situation in the country, I could quit smoking without anyone advising me” (cigarette smoker). Stress, anxiety and boredom were frequently described as contributors to relapse. Many described smoking as the primary way to relieve stress or anger arising from work pressure, unemployment, financial hardships or personal situations. Participants acknowledged the need for psychological support as part of the cessation process. These findings align with those from provider interviews.

Waterpipe smoking

Focus group participants noted that waterpipe smoking is widely available and socially acceptable. Socialising, relaxation, pleasure and entertainment were the main motivations. Several misconceptions about waterpipe smoking were identified, including beliefs that lightly inhaling waterpipe smoke reduces harm, that infrequent or social use is harmless, and that waterpipe smoking is easier to quit than cigarettes.

Adaptations

Table 3 summarises the adaptations made during the pre-implementation stage to the TUT strategies to address identified barriers. Study investigators and implementation partners reviewed formative research findings to identify contextual factors that could influence the implementation and effectiveness of the TUT intervention. Adaptation decisions were made iteratively during weekly team meetings and informed by triangulation of patient, provider, stakeholder and workflow data collected during formative research. Proposed modifications were designed to address identified contextual needs while preserving the intervention’s core functions. Patient input specifically informed adaptations to the phone counselling component, whereas adaptations related to NRT, provider training and electronic reminder systems were primarily informed by provider feedback, stakeholder consultations and workflow assessments. The FRAME framework was subsequently used to systematically document and classify the resulting adaptations. A detailed FRAME-based documentation for all adaptations is provided in online supplemental appendix A.

Nicotine replacement therapy

As nicotine replacement patches are unavailable in Lebanon, nicotine gum was selected as a practical alternative. Supportive materials were developed to help patients use NRT effectively, including instructions on proper use, potential side effects and self-monitoring tools for tracking use. A 1-minute instructional video in the local dialect was also recorded to further enhance proper usage, given the absence of such resources locally.

Phone counselling

The planned phone counselling intervention consisted of a structured 6-week behavioural cessation programme incorporating strategies informed by evidence-based approaches, including cognitive behavioural therapy and motivational interviewing. Two key adaptations were made to the phone counselling component. First, given the extent of mental health challenges identified by patients and providers, the team, in coordination with the Ministry of Public Health, identified mental health resources for counsellors to provide to patients and incorporated these into counselling sessions. Second, the counselling content was adapted to emphasise waterpipe-specific health risks, addressing prevalent misconceptions and reinforcing knowledge about associated harms.

Provider training

Brief instructional videos explaining providers’ roles were recorded and incorporated into the training to address time constraints identified in interviews, surveys and stakeholder consultations. Training materials were also adapted to include therapeutic knowledge of pharmaceutical products for tobacco cessation, addressing knowledge gaps identified in the collected data.

Electronic health records

The EHR reminder strategy for documenting smoking status was modified by adding paper-based reminders, in response to findings that centres use both electronic and paper records and that EHR updates are often delayed until after patient visits.

Discussion

Tailoring interventions to local contexts and generating evidence that reflects the priorities of policymakers, implementers and communities are essential for accelerating the implementation of evidence-based practices in diverse LMIC settings.12 13 37 38 This study describes the adaptation of an evidence-based phone-delivered TUT intervention prior to its implementation in a low-resource context. Using a data-driven approach, we generated actionable insights into the barriers and facilitators of integrating TUT into primary healthcare in Lebanon. Findings of our mixed-methods study showed convergence across data sources, informing meaningful adaptations intended to improve the intervention’s contextual fit, feasibility and potential for successful implementation. These findings may provide insights applicable to similar primary healthcare settings in Lebanon and other LMIC contexts with comparable service delivery structures and resource constraints. This study focuses on pre-implementation adaptations, with further refinements anticipated during subsequent implementation phases as real-world challenges and stakeholder feedback emerge.

Our findings revealed substantial gaps in providers’ knowledge and competence in prescribing pharmaceutical cessation products, consistent with other LMICs.39–41 This reflects broader deficits in cessation-related training within medical curricula and continuing medical education, hindering integration into routine care. Targeted, ongoing provider training is therefore critical. Our training model, including structured materials, role-specific guidance and brief instructional videos, may serve as a scalable template to support provider capacity strengthening in Lebanon and similar resource-limited settings. Moreover, physicians reported higher knowledge of and confidence in smoking cessation pharmacotherapy and counselling than nurses and other providers, likely reflecting differences in training and scope of practice. These findings support the need for role-tailored implementation strategies across primary healthcare settings.

The prevalence of smoking among healthcare providers in our sample (34%) is higher than previous global estimates, with a systematic review reporting a prevalence of 21% among healthcare workers.42 Provider tobacco use may hinder the consistent delivery of TUT interventions and influence patient engagement with cessation support. Given the influential role of healthcare workers as health promoters, addressing tobacco use among providers should be a public health priority in Lebanon.

Healthcare providers cited lack of time and heavy workloads as barriers to delivering TUT. While time constraints and competing priorities are recognised globally,43 these challenges are intensified in Lebanon due to the ongoing socioeconomic crisis and strain on the health system.18 21–23 Providers emphasised the importance of well-structured training that clearly defines roles, simplifies implementation and facilitates integration into clinical workflows. Our adaptations addressed these concerns by balancing evidence-based rigour with pragmatic workflow considerations. These findings highlight the need for interventions that are feasible and minimally burdensome in overstretched settings. Potential strategies to reduce provider burden may include task-sharing approaches (eg, shifting counselling to trained non-physician staff), structured telephone-based counselling and simplified protocols that standardise implementation and reduce decision-making demands during clinical encounters.

Based on stakeholder consultations, nicotine patches were replaced with nicotine gum to improve accessibility and feasibility, consistent with TUT implementation experience in Vietnam, where gum was considered more sustainable and accessible.44 While nicotine patches and combination NRT may provide additional cessation benefits,45 46 the selection of pharmacotherapy in low-resource settings must also account for local availability, cost and implementation considerations to support sustainable delivery. In this study, nicotine gum was integrated with the structured 6-week telephone counselling intervention to provide both behavioural and pharmacological cessation support. This adaptation reflects the balance between maintaining evidence-informed cessation support and ensuring alignment with available local resources.

Persistent misconceptions about waterpipe smoking were identified among patients, consistent with regional trends,47 despite strong evidence of associated harms.48 These misperceptions reduce perceived risk and motivation to quit, underscoring the need for culturally tailored education and communication strategies. The lack of waterpipe-specific cessation guidelines is a critical barrier, as few studies have tested interventions targeting waterpipe use, limiting the development of evidence-based programmes.49 Our study partially addressed this gap by integrating waterpipe-related content into counselling; however, scalable impact requires standardised guidelines to ensure consistent delivery in high-burden settings. This also highlights the need for comprehensive TUT guidance covering multiple tobacco products and promoting cessation of all tobacco products rather than substitution between products, since some cigarette smokers may shift to or increase waterpipe use after quitting.50

This study also highlights the intersection of tobacco use and mental health. While mental health factors influencing tobacco use and cessation are well-documented, research typically focuses on subpopulations (eg, individuals with psychiatric diagnoses,51 52 those experiencing homelessness53 or military veterans)54 55 and is largely limited to high-income countries. Our findings suggest that psychological distress, structural adversity and tobacco dependence operate on a national scale, affecting the broader population rather than only marginalised groups. This study underscores the need for population-level mental health and tobacco control strategies that extend beyond traditionally defined at-risk groups.

A key strength of this study is its rigorous yet pragmatic methodology. The mixed-methods design provided a comprehensive understanding of the implementation context. Qualitative methods elicited contextually salient stakeholder perspectives and identified key barriers and facilitators. Active engagement of diverse stakeholders throughout the formative phase contributed to the relevance and applicability of the findings. Rapid analysis enabled timely adaptation. The research team’s proficiency in Arabic and English preserved cultural nuances. The application of an established framework (FRAME) supported transparent and structured documentation of adaptations, with the aim of enhancing reproducibility.27 This systematic, data-driven approach aligns with recommendations from implementation scientists advocating for tools that strengthen the evidence base for effective tobacco control in LMICs.13

Nonetheless, certain limitations warrant consideration. Healthcare provider surveys used purposive sampling and self-reported measures, which may introduce selection and reporting bias. This approach may have led to over-representation of more engaged providers and socially desirable responses regarding TUT practices and beliefs. Focus groups were conducted with patients from only two centres, which may limit the diversity of perspectives across regions and socioeconomic backgrounds. The rapid analysis approach, employed to facilitate timely intervention adaptation, may have limited the depth of qualitative interpretation.56 More in-depth qualitative analyses are underway to further explore these findings. Additionally, this paper focuses primarily on pre-implementation adaptations; further modifications may be needed during operationalisation in response to real-world challenges and evolving feedback.

Conclusion

Overall, this formative study provides practical insights into implementing evidence-based TUT in under-resourced settings. Low-cost, contextually tailored strategies are essential to help these settings meet their FCTC obligations.
