Chaperone-mediated autophagy is required for regulatory T cell function
Authors: Ranee Harrison, Floralba Gjergjova, Sandra Pelka, Adrian Macho-Gonzalez, Bhakti Chavda, Kristen Lindenau, Aiara Gazteluiturri Garcia, Rabia R Khawaja, Jennifer T Aguilan, Simone Sidoli, Susmita Kaushik, Yair Botbol, Ana Maria Cuervo, Fernando Macian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) is a selective form of protein degradation in lysosomes that declines with age. Besides protein quality control, CMA also regulates several cellular processes through timely proteome remodeling. We previously demonstrated the importance of CMA in the activation of helper T cells. In this work, we analyzed the role of CMA in the generation and function of regulatory T cells (Tregs), a specialized type of T cells that suppress immune responses. We found that the basal CMA activity of Tregs further increases upon their activation. Using a Treg-specific CMA-deficient mouse model, we show that CMA is crucial for maintenance of peripheral tolerance by Tregs. Mice with CMA-defective Tregs display signs of chronic inflammation, which results in reduced survival as they age. We demonstrate that CMA-deficient Tregs have reduced suppressive activity in vivo using an experimental model of inflammatory bowel disease and a second model of tumor-induced immune response. Comparative quantitative proteomic analysis enabled us to identify the subproteome degraded by CMA and, consequently, the cellular pathways modulated by this type of autophagy to sustain Treg homeostasis and function. Collectively, our findings uncover a previously unknown role for CMA in regulating Treg function.Subject terms: Chaperone-mediated autophagy, Innate lymphoid cells, T cells

Introduction

Lysosomal degradation of intracellular components by autophagy is used by cells to modulate the abundance and quality of cytoplasmic components1. Chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA)2 is a selective form of autophagy that targets cytosolic proteins containing a KFERQ-like sequence3. Proteins with this motif are recognized by the heat shock cognate protein HSC70 which delivers them to the lysosome for degradation4. Substrate proteins are transported into the lysosomal lumen through CMA translocation complexes formed by multimers of the lysosome-associated membrane protein type 2A (LAMP2A)5. Levels of LAMP2A are limiting for CMA and are a frequent target for the physiological regulation of this pathway.

Degradation of damaged proteins via CMA contributes to quality control in multiple organs and cell types2,6,7. Additionally, CMA remodels functional cellular proteomes by degrading proteins to terminate their function. This results in the ability of CMA to regulate a variety of cellular processes, including cellular metabolism8–10, circadian rhythm11, DNA repair12, cell differentiation8,13, and cell death14, among others. We have previously shown that CMA activity is induced in helper T cells (CD4+ T cells) in response to stimulation through the T cell receptor (TCR)15. Regulation of CD4+ T cell activation by CMA is achieved by the timely targeted degradation of Itch and RCAN1, two negative regulators of T cell signaling15. CMA activity declines with age in most tissues and cell types analyzed due, for the most part, to a reduction in LAMP2A levels16,17. We have shown that reduction of CMA with age in CD4+ T cells leads to reduced T cell function and that restoration of LAMP2A levels in aged T cells enhances their activation-induced responses15. However, the possible contribution of CMA to the functional regulation of other subtypes of T cells remains unexplored.

In this work, we focus on the role of CMA in regulatory T cells (Tregs), a specialized subpopulation of CD4+ T cells that are highly suppressive and responsible for modulating immune responses, maintaining immune homeostasis, and preventing autoimmunity18. In addition to this role in immune self-tolerance, interest in Tregs has also grown recently due to their suppressive effect on anti-tumor immune responses19,20, as well as their contribution to evasion by some pathogens of sterilizing immunity21–23. These findings justify the current interest to explore the therapeutic potential of Tregs in autoimmune diseases24–26, as well as strategies aimed at depleting them in tumors to enhance anti-tumor immune responses27,28. A better understanding of the processes that regulate Treg development, stability, and function is necessary to assist in these therapeutic efforts.

Here, we have investigated the role of CMA in Treg biology and found that CMA activity is upregulated upon Treg activation. This upregulation is required for proper Treg function, as a mouse model with CMA-deficient Tregs displayed increased inflammation and reduced survival. Furthermore, we have unveiled an expansion of the Treg compartment in these mice, comparable to that seen in aging and proven that this expansion can be reversed by pharmacological restoration of CMA in aged mice. We demonstrated that CMA-deficient Tregs were unable to exert their immune suppressive capacity in vivo using an inflammatory bowel disease mouse model. In agreement with loss of Tregs immunosuppressive capacity upon CMA blockage, we showed that mice with CMA-deficient Tregs orchestrate a more effective anti-tumor immune response. Employing comparative quantitative proteomic analysis, we identified that reduced function of CMA-defective Tregs is a consequence of disrupted metabolic regulation and altered signaling pathways necessary for their function. Altogether, this study unveils a previously unknown role for CMA in regulating Treg function and provides evidence in support of the possibility of pharmacologically targeting CMA to restore proper Treg function.

Results

CMA is upregulated in Tregs upon activation

To investigate the potential role for CMA in Tregs, we first analyzed possible changes in CMA activity during Treg activation. CMA activity directly depends on the levels of LAMP2A, the CMA receptor at the lysosomal membrane29 and, in fact, transcriptional upregulation of Lamp2a occurs during conditions that require maximum activation of CMA30. RT-qPCR analysis showed a significant increase in Lamp2a mRNA expression upon 24-h activation of Tregs with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28, but not of Lamp1, another abundant lysosomal membrane protein (Fig. 1a, b). Accordingly, we also detected an increase in LAMP2A protein levels in activated Tregs (Fig. 1c, d).

To directly analyze CMA activity, we isolated Tregs from a recently developed CMA reporter mouse model systemically expressing KFERQDendra31 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Once this fluorescent CMA substrate protein is delivered to lysosomes via CMA, lysosomes can be detected as fluorescent puncta as long as the KFERQDendra protein remains on the lysosomal surface31. However, fluorescence is lost when the protein is unfolded for internalization. Therefore, to determine the amount of KFERQDendra internalized and degraded by lysosomes, intralysosomal degradation must be inhibited, allowing accumulation of translocated KFERQDendra within the lysosomal lumen, which can then be detected using an anti-Dendra antibody (Supplementary Fig. 1a)31. We confirmed that, as in most cell types, inhibition of lysosomal proteolysis by combining NH4Cl (to neutralize lysosomal pH) and leupeptin (to inhibit serine and cysteine lysosomal proteases) did not affect Treg viability (Supplementary Fig. 1b). In Tregs isolated from 4-month-old KFERQDendra mice, activation of Tregs led to a statistically significant increase in the amount of the CMA substrate protein KFERQDendra internalized and degraded within lysosomes (Fig. 1e, f; individual values per condition and rate of Dendra degradation in each condition are shown). Interestingly, when performing similar experiments in Tregs isolated from 22-month-old KFERQDendra mice, we observed a significant decrease in CMA under resting conditions when compared with young mice, and a lack of CMA upregulation upon Treg activation (Fig. 1e, f; note that the decrease in mean fluorescence intensity per puncta upon NH4Cl treatment in old cells is likely due to dilution of fluorescence within each lysosome as a result of NH4Cl-induced swelling. In young cells, this dilution effect is offset by their higher rates of Dendra internalization into lysosomes).

We next set to determine whether similar upregulation of CMA in Tregs was observed in vivo in response to a physiological regulator such as IL-2. To this effect, we injected 4-month-old KFERQDendra mice on 3 consecutive days with the IL-2/IL-2 JES6-1 antibody complex (IL-2/IL-2Ab), previously shown to expand Tregs in vivo and boost their immunomodulatory function32, and isolated Tregs 2 days later (Fig. 1g; Supplementary Fig. 1c shows IL-2 dosage optimization monitored as expansion of the Treg compartment). Analysis of CMA in these cells revealed significantly higher CMA activity in Tregs activated in vivo with IL-2 (Fig. 1 h, i). Furthermore, we found a significant and dose-dependent increase in LAMP2A protein levels in the isolated Tregs from animals that received IL-2/IL-2Ab (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). These results confirm that Tregs upregulate CMA in response to their activation both in vitro and in vivo.

Blockage of CMA in Tregs in mice has a systemic impact

To study the role of CMA in Tregs in vivo and the possible consequences of the decline in CMA with age observed in these cells, we knocked out Lamp2a, the limiting component of CMA and the only isoform of LAMP2 that can act as a CMA receptor33. To this effect, we crossed mice carrying the floxed Lamp2a allele (Lamp2afl/fl)10 with mice expressing the GFP-Cre recombinase protein under the control of the mouse Foxp3 gene promoter (Foxp3Cre) and generated a Treg-specific CMA-deficient mouse line (Foxp3CreLamp2afl/fl, hereafter referred to as TregL2AKO) (Fig. 2a). Control (CTRL) mice were generated by crossing Foxp3Cre mice to C57BL/6 mice to obtain Foxp3CreLamp2a+/+ mice with intact Lamp2a expression. We confirmed that the expression of Lamp2a mRNA was lost in Tregs from TregL2AKO mice but remained unchanged in CD4+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Furthermore, the mRNA expression of the other two Lamp2 spliced variants (Lamp2b and Lamp2c), as well as of Lamp1 were comparable between CTRL and TregL2AKO Tregs (Supplementary Fig. 2b–d). We also confirmed that deletion of LAMP2A in our experimental model did not compromise other autophagic pathways, such as macroautophagy. Analysis of macroautophagy flux in resting or activated Tregs, by measuring changes in LC3 levels upon inhibition of lysosomal proteolysis either by immunoblot (Supplementary Fig. 2e) or by immunofluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 2f–h), revealed no differences between CTRL and TregL2AKO mice. In fact, although macroautophagy was clearly upregulated during Treg activation, this activation was comparable in both mouse groups (Supplementary Fig. 2e–h). Similarly, major lysosomal hydrolases and other lysosomal membrane proteins, including subunits of the lysosomal proton pump responsible for lysosomal acidification, were detected at similar levels in Tregs from CTRL and TregL2AKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 2i). This data supports that, similar to other cell types and tissues6,8,10,13,15,34, deletion of Lamp2a in Tregs did not result in major changes in macroautophagy or the lysosomal compartment.

CTRL and TregL2AKO mice were born at the expected Mendelian rate and with comparable size. However, TregL2AKO mice showed a significantly lower body weight gain compared to CTRL mice as they aged (Fig. 2a, b). Analysis of the body composition of these mice revealed that lower body weight was due to reduction in both lean and fat mass, with a more pronounced decrease in the latter (Fig. 2c). Overall, TregL2AKO mice also displayed a significant decrease in survival (Fig. 2d).

CMA in Tregs is necessary for peripheral tolerance in vivo

The reduced size and survival of the TregL2AKO mice could be indicative of systemic inflammation because Tregs are crucial in maintaining peripheral tolerance and immune homeostasis18. To understand whether CMA in Tregs is important to maintain their immune-suppressive function in vivo, we analyzed the percentage and activation status of splenic CD4+ and CD8+ conventional T cells and found no differences between CTRL and TregL2AKO mice at 3 months of age (Fig. 2e, f and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). However, at 12 months of age, while the percentage of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells remained comparable between CTRL and TregL2AKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 3b), we found that TregL2AKO mice spleens had higher proportion of effector memory CD4+ and CD8+ (CD44hiCD62Llo) conventional T cell populations (Fig. 2e, f). In further confirmation of the activated status of conventional T cells in TregL2AKO mice, we found that they expressed significantly higher levels of cell-mediated immunity-regulating cytokines such as interferon gamma (IFN-γ), interleukin 17 (IL-17) (Fig. 2g, h) and IL-2 (Supplementary Fig. 3c) than CTRL mice.

We next analyzed the consequences of this chronic immune activation in peripheral organs. First, we inspected the intestine of TregL2AKO mice, an organ where Tregs are critical for adjustment of the immune response to self- and non-self antigens35. We found that 12-month-old TregL2AKO mice presented with an altered intestinal mucosa with reduced length and density of villi in the jejunal section as compared to CTRL mice (Fig. 3a–c). These altered intestinal morphological characteristics were associated with a significantly higher intestinal permeability in the TregL2AKO mice, detected using FITC-Dextran36 (Fig. 3d), and transcriptional up-regulation of the proinflammatory gene IL-6 and the macrophage activation marker F4/80 (Supplementary Fig. 3d). A similar analysis of the colonic mucosa of TregL2AKO mice also revealed lower number and density of crypts, along with a lower presence of goblet cells (Fig. 3e–h) and higher IL-6 expression (Supplementary Fig. 3d) compared to CTRL mice. Immunostaining for Foxp3 revealed significantly higher numbers of Tregs in the small intestine of TregL2AKO mice than in CTRL mice (Fig. 3i, j; total Tregs per individual villus and average number of Tregs per villus per mouse are shown to account for heterogeneity among villi). The increase in Tregs content was also confirmed by qPCR for Foxp3 in these tissues (Fig. 3k).

Histological analysis of the adipose tissue, as another organ where Tregs have been shown to play an important role in controlling inflammation37, revealed that TregL2AKO mice had decreased size and weight of epididymal white adipose tissue depots, higher proportion of smaller adipocytes and more visible immune infiltrates compared to CTRL mice (Fig. 3l–n). Altogether, these findings support that CMA in Tregs may be important for maintaining immune tolerance in lymphoid and non-lymphoid organs.

Loss of CMA in Tregs leads to expansion of the Treg compartment

To better understand the status of Tregs upon CMA blockage, we compared Treg properties in lymphoid organs of CTRL and TregL2AKO mice. We did not observe significant differences in the percentage of Tregs in 3-month-old mice (Fig. 4a, c–f and Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). However, by 12 months of age, TregL2AKO mice displayed significant increases in the Treg fraction within the peripheral lymph nodes, mesenteric lymph nodes, and thymus, with a trend toward an increase in the spleen (Fig. 4b–f). This expansion may explain the larger number of Tregs observed in the small intestinal mucosa (Fig. 3i–k).

Despite greater expansion of the Treg compartment, we did not find differences in levels of the proliferative marker Ki67 between CTRL and TregL2AKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 4c), supporting that, as in aging38, the accumulation of Tregs in TregL2AKO mice was not due to increased generation but rather prolonged Treg cell half-life. Analysis of abundance of proteins that are necessary for Treg suppressive function revealed comparable mean fluorescent intensity (MFI) for Foxp3 and CD25 (Supplementary Fig. 4d) between CTRL and TregL2AKO mice and only trends toward decreased fraction of Tregs expressing the suppressive genes GITR, ICOS, CD103, and BLIMP1 in spleens and mesenteric lymph nodes of TregL2AKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 4e). The fraction of Tregs expressing IL-17 in lymphoid organs remained unchanged, whereas we noticed significantly higher percentages of Tregs expressing IFNγ in spleens of TregL2AKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 4f, g). Analysis of CD44 and CD62L, to compare Treg differentiation state, revealed a significant increase in effector memory (CD44hiCD62Llo) and decrease in central memory (CD44hiCD62Lhi) and naïve (CD44loCD62Lhi) Tregs in TregL2AKO mice when compared to CTRL mice (Fig. 4g–i).

Direct analysis of expression of immunosuppressive cytokines, such as Tgfβ and Il10, or the fraction of Tregs expressing IL-10 did not reveal significant differences between Tregs isolated from CTRL and TregL2AKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Additionally, Tregs isolated from TregL2AKO mice showed only slightly reduced suppression of CD4+ and CD8+ T cell proliferation in vitro compared to Tregs from CTRL mice (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). We attribute this absence of change in Treg suppressive function to the well-documented limitations of the in vitro setting39, which often engages activation pathways distinct from those in vivo. Thus, the in vitro model may lack key regulatory factors present in vivo, which could be responsible for the higher activation state of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells observed in our TregL2AKO mice (Fig. 2e, f). Furthermore, because in vitro Treg suppression assays require pre-activation, which we performed using IL-2 and CD3/CD28 beads, this may bypass CMA defects.

Expansion of the Treg compartment and increase in mature Tregs at the expense of naïve ones are common features observed in aging organisms40. Since we observed a marked decrease of CMA in Tregs from old mice (Fig. 1e, f), we next tested if pharmacological upregulation of CMA in vivo could ameliorate some of these age-related changes in Tregs. Our lab has developed first-in-class small molecules (CMA activators - CAs)41 capable of selectively upregulating CMA activity in vivo in multiple cell types and organs, including T cells42,43. These molecules stabilize the interaction between an endogenous inhibitor of CMA—retinoic acid receptor alpha—and its co-repressor, nuclear receptor corepressor 142. Since CMA activity decline starts by 12 months of age in most mouse organs, we initiated daily oral administration of one of the CA molecules (CA77.1) or the vehicle in 18-month-old mice for a total of 5 months (Fig. 5a). The spleens of vehicle-treated 23-month-old mice displayed a significantly higher number of Tregs when compared with young (4-month-old) mice (Fig. 5b). However, the spleens of CA77.1-treated old mice showed a significant reduction in the expansion of their Treg compartment, which made them indistinguishable from young mice (Fig. 5b). Pharmacological activation of CMA also reduced the abnormally elevated fraction of IL-10+ Tregs identified in old mice, bringing them to levels closer to those of young mice (Fig. 5c). This supports the notion that young and CA77.1-treated old mice may have comparable immunosuppressive capacity. Upregulation of CMA in old mice also ameliorated some of the age-related accumulation of Tregs in peripheral organs such as the intestine, where, compared to vehicle-treated mice, those receiving CA77.1 displayed a significant reduction in Foxp3+ cells (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 6a; due to the marked morphological changes and the presence of large clusters of Foxp3+ cells, more frequent in the intestines of vehicle-treated old mice, we acquired z-stacks to quantify the Foxp3+ signal and normalized by area, excluding regions where clusters occupied the full width of the villi). Lastly, to determine whether the CMA activator directly affected Treg function, we compared the immunosuppressive capacity of Tregs isolated from young and old mice, treated in vitro with or without CA39 (a compound from the same family of CMA activators, but more suitable for in vitro studies)42 (Fig. 5e). Despite the previously noted limitations of the in vitro system, we found that Tregs from old mice were less efficient at suppressing CD4+ and CD8+ conventional T cell proliferation, and that the CMA activator improved the suppressive effect of old Tregs on CD4+ T cells but had no effect on CD8+ T cell suppression (Fig. 5f, g and Supplementary Fig. 6b). As extensively reported in other cell types6,42, we also confirmed that the CMA activator did not have any significant effect on macroautophagy activity in Tregs from either young or old mice (Supplementary Fig. 6c).

Overall, our findings support the idea that the decline of CMA with age in Tregs may contribute to their increased abundance and decreased immunosuppressive function in old organisms and that activation of CMA may be effective in preventing these changes.

CMA-deficient Tregs fail to suppress inflammation

We further evaluated whether Tregs require CMA for their suppressive function in vivo by using an inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) recovery mouse model44, as Tregs have proven essential in preventing IBD45. Briefly, we induced colitis by intraperitoneally transferring naïve CD4+ T cells from C57BL/6 J mice into Rag1−/− immunodeficient mice. When mice had lost about 5% of their initial bodyweight (onset of colitis), Tregs either from wild type (WT) or TregL2AKO mice were transferred to these mice intraperitoneally, and body weight recovery was monitored for 24 additional days (Fig. 6a). As expected, mice receiving T cells developed colitis and displayed the expected progressive weight loss. Upon addition of WT Tregs, we saw a partial recovery from colitis, while mice that received CMA-deficient Tregs failed to recover from the wasting disease (Fig. 6b).

Histopathological analysis revealed morphological colonic abnormalities, with loss of crypts, depletion of goblet cells, and severe focal and transmural inflammation in mice that received only T cells and in those receiving L2AKO Tregs, which resulted in a higher colitis score (Fig. 6c, d). Predictably, mice that received WT Tregs partially reversed these colonic alterations, observing less inflammation, better preservation of crypts and lower colitis score (Fig. 6c, d). Even though equal numbers of WT and L2AKO Tregs were injected, Foxp3 staining revealed higher numbers of Tregs in the intestines of mice receiving L2AKO Tregs, in support of diminished Treg function leading to their subsequent accumulation (Fig. 6e, f).

These findings indicate that Tregs require fully functional CMA to perform their immunosuppressive function in pathological conditions.

CMA-deficient Tregs fail to suppress anti-tumor immune responses

As an additional evaluation of the functional attributes of CMA-deficient Tregs, we analyzed their ability to effectively suppress anti-tumor immune responses20,46,47. To do this, we utilized the syngeneic MC38 murine tumor model, where tumor cells were engrafted subcutaneously into the flanks of CTRL and TregL2AKO mice and tumor growth was monitored over time (Fig. 6g). Notably, we observed a severe reduction in tumor growth rate and size in TregL2AKO mice when compared to CTRL mice (Fig. 6h–j). These findings support the idea that CMA-competent Tregs can effectively suppress anti-tumor immune response, hence the increase in tumor volume, whereas CMA blockage in Tregs compromises their suppressive function.

In agreement with this interpretation, analysis of tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) revealed a significant increase in the percentage and number of CD8+ T cells in TILs from TregL2AKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 7a–g). IFN-γ expression was markedly elevated in these CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6k–m), with the latter also displaying higher levels of Granzyme B (GZB) (Fig. 6n–p). Splenic and draining lymph node CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from the TregL2AKO mice were also highly activated and expressed increased levels of IFNγ and as expected, no changes in IL-17 expression (Supplementary Fig. 8a–h) compared to CTRL mice, indicating an effective tumor immune response. Although the percentage of Tregs relative to CD4+ T cells in tumors was comparable between CTRL and TregL2AKO mice, due to overall increase of T cells in the tumor, we found a significant increase in the net number of tumor-infiltrating Tregs in TregL2AKO mice (expressed per mg of tissue) (Fig. 6q–s). Despite the increase in number, the intensity of Treg markers such as Foxp3, CD25 and PD1 was comparable in tumors from CTRL and TregL2AKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 7h-j). As observed in the tumor, the percentages of Tregs in the spleen and draining lymph nodes were the same in CTRL and TregL2AKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 9a). When we further analyzed the functional state of these Tregs, we observed a reduction in the percentage of activated Tregs and in other Treg markers in the TregL2AKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 9b–g), indicative of reduced suppressive capacity.

In summary, findings in this tumor-induced immune response mouse model and in the experimental model of inflammatory bowel disease support the idea that fully functional CMA activity in Tregs is required to sustain their immunosuppressive properties.

CMA remodels the Treg proteome

To identify the subset of Treg proteins that, when no longer degraded through CMA, contribute to the observed phenotypes, we performed comparative quantitative proteomics analysis of Tregs isolated from 6-month-old CTRL and TregL2AKO mice. This time point was chosen to avoid changes related to the overall deterioration of TregL2AKO mice observed at later ages (Fig. 7). We compared the total proteome of Tregs under resting conditions and upon activation and found that blockage of CMA resulted in major changes in the composition of the Treg proteome in both conditions (Fig. 7a, b, Supplementary Fig. 10a, b and Supplementary Data 1). In the resting proteome, 51 proteins displayed higher levels in Tregs from TregL2AKO mice and 49 were significantly reduced in abundance (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Upon activation, levels of an additional 210 proteins increased and 66 decreased in Tregs from TregL2AKO mice, and of those only 123 and 23 proteins, respectively, displayed similar changes in Tregs from CTRL mice (Fig. 7a, b). Pathway enrichment analysis using Enrich-R48 (to compile results from multiple databases) revealed that under resting conditions, proteins with significantly different levels in the Treg proteomes of TregL2AKO mice compared to CTRL were mostly associated with immune function (including cytokine signaling), cellular metabolism (mostly of lipids) and signaling (Fig. 7c). In activated Tregs, changes in the proteome of TregL2AKO Tregs involved proteins related to the immune system (including its interactions with the extracellular matrix), metabolism (in this case, glucose), vesicular trafficking (both secretory and endocytic pathways), and transcription (Fig. 7d). Due to the statistical power limitations associated with using only three samples per condition, we performed an additional pathway analysis based on a scoring approach rather than statistical significance alone. This method considered proteins showing changes above or below defined thresholds in the same direction across all biological replicates. This complementary analysis confirmed enrichment of similar pathways in both conditions while providing greater granularity for specific processes, such as mRNA processing and protein translation, that were enriched in both resting and activated states (Supplementary Fig. 10c).

To directly find out which of the proteins accumulated in L2AKO Tregs due to reduced lysosomal degradation, we performed similar quantitative proteomics but in cells previously incubated or not with inhibitors of lysosomal proteolysis8,10,11. This allowed us to identify CMA substrate proteins as those undergoing lysosomal degradation in a LAMP2A-dependent manner (levels increase in CTRL cells upon inhibition of lysosomal proteolysis but not in L2AKO Tregs) (Supplementary Fig. 11a and Supplementary Data 1). As before, we chose a combination of NH4Cl and leupeptin treatment to effectively inhibit intralysosomal proteolysis without affecting cellular viability (Supplementary Fig. 1b). We found that a fraction of proteins with elevated levels in TregL2AKO were bona fide CMA substrates as they displayed LAMP2A-dependent lysosomal degradation either during resting or activating conditions (Fig. 7e bottom right quadrant and Supplementary Fig. 11b,c cluster 1 (CMA substrates both under resting and activating conditions) and clusters 2 and 3 (CMA substrates only during resting or activation, respectively) and Supplementary Data 2). To further assess their potential susceptibility to CMA, we analyzed the presence of CMA-targeting motifs in their sequences, either canonical or generated through posttranslational modifications such as phosphorylation or acetylation. Figure 7f, g summarizes the presence of these motifs and the functional classification of CMA substrates in Tregs under resting and activating conditions, respectively. This analysis indicated that CMA contributes to the degradation of proteins involved in immune signaling (IL and IFN pathways), chemotaxis and cell motility/adhesion, metabolism (oxidative phosphorylation and lipid, glucose, and amino acid metabolism), RNA metabolism and protein translation, as well as posttranscriptional regulation, including ubiquitination, acetylation, and methylation (Fig. 7f, g).

As shown in other cell types8,11,49, blockage of CMA was also associated with reduced stability of a subset of the Treg proteome, reflected by increased lysosomal degradation rates (Fig. 7e, top left quadrant and Supplementary Fig. 11b, c, clusters 4 and 5, in resting and activating conditions, respectively). In other cell types, enhanced lysosomal degradation upon CMA inhibition has been attributed to rerouting of CMA substrates to alternative autophagic pathways and/or failure to degrade via CMA proteins that mediated their lysosomal degradation11. This group of proteins was involved in functions similar to those reduced in abundance in Tregs from TregL2AKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 11d). Their substantial functional overlap with those identified as bona fide CMA substrates points to an even broader impairment of core Treg functions, including immune signaling, metabolism, and transcriptional and translational regulation.

These findings support that CMA regulates a subset of the Treg proteome and failure to do so results in major direct and indirect changes in the Treg proteome under both resting and activating conditions.

CMA contributes to regulation of Treg metabolism and signaling

To confirm the importance of the CMA-mediated changes in the Treg proteome for Treg function, we focused on two of the identified cellular pathway terms: metabolism and interleukin signaling. Treg differentiation, proliferation, and survival are regulated through metabolic changes50–53. We used the Seahorse metabolic flux assay to measure extracellular acidification and oxygen consumption rates in Tregs from CTRL and TregL2AKO mice upon activation with α-CD3 and α-CD28 (Supplementary Fig. 12a). L2AKO Tregs showed reduced glycolysis rates (Fig. 8a) and lower rates of basal mitochondrial respiration and of non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption (Fig. 8b, c) compared to CTRL cells, indicating that the L2AKO Tregs are less metabolically fit. Reduced glycolysis6 and mitochondrial respiration49 have been reported in other cell types following L2A deletion, but this is the first observation of reduced non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption. In leukocytes, non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption is largely attributed to inflammatory enzymes such as cyclooxygenases, lipoxygenases, and NADPH oxidases54. Leveraging our proteomic data, we analyzed lysosomal degradation rates for the nine enzymes detected in this category. Only four showed lysosomal degradation in control cells, and among these, only two—FTO and HMOX2—exhibited the pattern of CMA substrates, with their lysosomal degradation selectively lost in the TregL2AKO group (Fig. 8d). We focused on the m6A RNA demethylase FTO given its high lysosomal degradation rate, the emerging roles of both FTO and m6A RNA methylation in immune regulation55–57, including metabolic and transcriptional control in Tregs, and the potential broad impact of FTO-mediated changes in m6A RNA methylation58, a common RNA modification that influences mRNA stability and expression.

FTO contains two canonical and two post-translationally generated CMA-targeting motifs (Fig. 8e). Immunostaining of resting and activated Tregs, treated or not with lysosomal proteolysis inhibitors, confirmed significantly higher FTO levels in Tregs from TregL2AKO mice (Fig. 8f), whereas qPCR analysis revealed no differences in Fto RNA abundance between both genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 12b). In control Tregs, activation induced lysosomal degradation of FTO, whereas this process was completely abolished in Tregs from TregL2AKO mice (Fig. 8f, g). Inhibition of macroautophagy with MRT68921 did not affect FTO degradation, confirming CMA as the primary pathway responsible for FTO turnover in these cells (Supplementary Fig. 12c, d).

To assess the functional consequences of altered FTO abundance, we quantified global m⁶A RNA methylation in CTRL and L2AKO Tregs. Consistent with elevated basal FTO levels, L2AKO Tregs exhibited a marked reduction in global m⁶A methylation (Fig. 8h). IL-2 stimulation reduced m⁶A levels in control Tregs to those observed in L2AKO cells, but this IL-2–dependent response was entirely absent in L2AKO Tregs (Fig. 8h). Analysis of 13 additional m⁶A regulators showed minimal differences between genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 12e), with only two EIF3 reader proteins modestly increased in L2AKO cells. Among these factors, only the writer METTL3 underwent lysosomal degradation, which remained unchanged in L2AKO cells, indicating CMA-independent degradation (Supplementary Fig. 12e). Together, these data identify impaired CMA-dependent FTO degradation as the main driver of reduced m⁶A methylation in L2AKO Tregs.

Reduced m⁶A abundance is expected to alter mRNA stability and turnover, leading to transcriptional changes that could compromise Treg immunosuppressive function. Indeed, Tregs lacking the m⁶A methylase METTL3, phenocopying the reduced m⁶A levels observed in our study due to elevated FTO, fail to suppress immune responses59.

The absence of IL-2–induced changes in m⁶A methylation in L2AKO Tregs suggests defective IL-2 receptor signaling, which is crucial for Treg function60–63. Upon IL-2 stimulation, L2AKO Tregs displayed pronounced upregulation of IL-2–responsive transcripts, particularly negative feedback regulators (Socs1, Socs3, Cish), as well as Foxp3, Blimp1, and Ctla4, while other IL-2 targets were unchanged or reduced compared to controls (Fig. 8i). These transcriptional changes were not explained by altered STAT5 activation, as immunoblot revealed that total STAT5 levels were modestly increased in resting L2AKO Tregs (confirming our mass spectrometry findings), but the fraction of phosphorylated STAT5 remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 13d).

We next analyzed the IL2 receptor by both flow cytometry and immunofluorescence and found that, unlike control Tregs, L2AKO Tregs failed to upregulate total and surface levels of the IL-2 receptor γ-chain (CD132) upon CD3/CD28 24 h activation with or without IL-2 stimulation (Fig. 8j and Supplementary Fig. 13a). This uncoupling of transcriptional induction from signaling output is characteristic of perturbed m⁶A dynamics and likely reflects enhanced stability of IL-2–responsive transcripts due to reduced m⁶A marking. Notably, the same negative feedback genes (Socs1, Socs3, Cish) have been reported to be aberrantly upregulated in METTL3-deficient Tregs59; although in our model this phenotype arises from excessive demethylation rather than impaired methylation, in both cases the net outcome will be reduced m⁶A RNA methylation.

Elevated SOCS1, SOCS3, and CISH levels likely contribute to the failure of L2AKO Tregs to sustain IL-2Rα (CD25) transcriptional upregulation (Fig. 8i). However, the reduced levels of IL2Rγ (CD132) in L2AKO Tregs upon IL-2 stimulation (Fig. 8j, also confirmed by mass spectrometry analysis in Fig. 8k) may partly reflect accelerated endocytic turnover. When we analyzed cellular levels of IL2Rγ upon addition or not of lysosomal proteolysis inhibitors, we found that while activation and IL-2 stimulation inhibited lysosomal degradation of IL2Rγ in control Tregs, this protective effect was absent in L2AKO cells (Fig. 8k). Together, these findings indicate that disruption of IL-2 signaling in CMA-defective Tregs occurs at multiple levels, including epitranscriptomic regulation, negative feedback amplification, and receptor trafficking (as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 13). Further studies will be required to distinguish direct FTO-dependent effects on m⁶A-modified transcripts from secondary compensatory responses.

Overall, our data support that the defective proteome maintenance and inability to remodel it in response to activating stimuli observed in CMA-deficient cells leads to deregulation of cellular pathways important for Treg biology and can explain some of the functional defects observed in LAMP2A-deficient Tregs.

Discussion

In this study, we have identified a role for CMA in the maintenance of Tregs immunosuppressive activities and demonstrate the consequences of impaired Treg CMA on self-tolerance, control of the inflammatory response and modulation of the anti-oncogenic immune response (Fig. 8l). The extensive changes in the Treg proteome observed in the CMA-deficient cells, support a large contribution of this autophagic pathway to maintain and remodel their proteome both under basal conditions and in response to activating signals to sustain Tregs’ immunosuppressive capacity.

As seen in other organs and cell types6,8, the functional defect in TregL2AKO mice becomes evident as mice age. The lack of overt phenotype in the younger mice could be a result of compensatory mechanisms or of the need for a chronically sustained state of inflammation—spanning across months—before phenotypic differences become noticeable (i.e., trends to lower weight gain in TregL2AKO mice were observed since early in life but they do not become significant until 8 months of age). While in homeostatic conditions, the defective function of Tregs will only manifest with age, external challenges were able to elicit the underlying functional defect of L2AKO Tregs. Thus, the IBD and tumor studies, where TregL2AKO mice showed pronounced lack of immunosuppressive capabilities, were performed with Tregs isolated from 6-month-old mice (IBD) or in 6-month-old tumor-bearing mice. In fact, most molecular changes (i.e., Fto/m6A methylation, IL-2 signaling, and metabolic enzymes) are already present in the proteome and transcriptome of 6-month-old CMA-deficient Tregs and are further exacerbated upon IL-2 stimulation. These observations highlight the importance of CMA in Tregs in their response to external challenges.

As TregL2AKO mice age, the marked inflammation and presence of highly activated effector T cells in their secondary lymphoid organs supports the inability of CMA-defective Tregs to maintain immune homeostasis. This failure goes beyond lymphoid organs, as distinctive Treg populations have also been identified in non-lymphoid tissues such as adipose tissues, liver, skin, intestinal mucosa, muscle, and lung64, where they perform their suppressive function. Here, we show a severe inflammatory intestinal phenotype in TregL2AKO mice with subsequent compromised gut permeability that we propose contributes in large extent to their failure to gain weight and reduced overall survival. Altered suppressive Tregs function in other tissues such as the adipose may also contribute to the observed phenotype directly or through their metabolic consequences. In fact, Tregs in adipose tissue have been shown to exert a protective role in insulin resistance and other metabolic disorders37,65. The impact of the inflammatory adipose phenotype observed in TregL2AKO mice at the level of full-body metabolism deserves future investigation.

Despite the marked inflammation observed in L2AKO mice, we saw an expansion of the Treg compartment in the lymphoid organs. This aligns with the reported accumulation of Tregs in the spleen and lymph nodes of old mice and in the blood of old humans when compared to their younger counterparts, despite decreased generation of Tregs during aging40,66,67. Treg accumulation with age has been proposed to be a result of their longer half-life38. This expansion in size of the Treg compartment with age does not always associate with a corresponding increase in function67,68, with some studies reporting rather functional defects of old Tregs69. Similarly, despite the expansion, we found that CMA-deficient Tregs have reduced suppressive activity both in the inflammatory bowel disease model and in the tumor model. In fact, we noted an increase in the number of Tregs in the tumors and draining lymph nodes of TregL2AKO mice, even though there was a significantly higher anti-tumor immune response that resulted in smaller tumor volumes. We show that blockage of CMA in Tregs is sufficient to markedly reduce tumor progression. Given the acute nature of our experiments, it is unlikely that systemic effects emerging at later ages in these mice contribute to the observed reduction in tumor growth. Nevertheless, experiments involving the direct transfer of CMA-deficient Tregs into control mice bearing established tumors would further strengthen the conclusion that these effects are Treg-intrinsic.

The phenotype observed upon blockage of CMA in Tregs contrasts sharply with that shown upon macroautophagy blockage in the same cells, which led to loss of Foxp3 expression and Tregs depletion due to increased apoptosis70. This comparison underscores the distinct and nonredundant roles of CMA and macroautophagy in Tregs. Consistent with observations in other cell types, L2A deletion did not cause noticeable changes in the lysosomal system or inhibit macroautophagy. However, unlike other cells such as hepatocytes10 or fibroblasts71, which compensate for CMA loss by upregulating macroautophagy, Tregs failed to mount such a compensatory response. Instead, inhibition of CMA by L2A deletion led to increased lysosomal degradation of a subset of Treg proteins, likely mediated by alternative pathways such as endosomal microautophagy, which does not require L2A and has been shown to be compensatorily upregulated in other settings72,73. The distinct timing and regulatory mechanisms of these pathways may render this degradation either excessive (explaining the lower steady-state levels of some proteins) or temporally misaligned, thereby preventing the precise regulatory control normally provided by CMA.

Age-dependent decline in CMA activity has been reported in many cell types, and organs8,15–17, and, excitingly, genetic, or pharmacological activation of CMA has been shown to improve age-related pathological disorders associated with reduced CMA activity6,8,10,15,42,49,74–76. Here, we provide evidence of the feasibility of restoring the Treg compartment and function in old mice through pharmacological activation of CMA. Treatment with the CMA activator not only reduced the age-related expansion of the Treg compartment, but also the increased heterogeneity (dispersion from animal to animal) that is usually seen during aging.

Our comparative proteomic analysis revealed, as it has been also the case in other cell types and organs, that the effect of CMA cannot be narrowed to failure to degrade a specific protein, but rather to the inability to timely modulate levels of a whole subset of the Treg proteome6,8,10,34,49,75. While the comparative proteomic analysis performed in this study provides a global overview of proteome changes resulting from defective CMA function, follow-up studies using validation by orthogonal approaches are required, as performed here for the metabolic and IL-2 signaling pathways. In the case of Tregs, we found high enrichment of changes in proteins related to metabolic processes and intracellular signaling pathways. CMA has been shown to regulate glucose and lipid metabolism through the selective and timely degradation of key enzymes in these pathways8–10. Different T cell subsets require distinct energetic and metabolic pathways to support their functional needs. Effector T cells are highly glycolytic, while Tregs have increased lipid oxidation and oxidative metabolism50,77,78. The reduced glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates observed in the Tregs of TregL2AKO mice are indicative of an overall disruption in the metabolic fitness of these Tregs. Macroautophagy deficiency, on the other hand, was shown to upregulate glycolysis in Tregs, leading to defective function70. These observations buttress the non-redundant roles different types of autophagy play in cells and highlight the importance of tightly regulated metabolic flux for proper Treg function. Among the metabolic changes identified in CMA-deficient Tregs, one that was not previously noted in any other cell type analyzed to date, is a reduction in the non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption, highly associated to the activity of enzymes important during the inflammatory process, such as cyclo-oxygenase, lipoxygenase, and NADPH oxidases. One of these enzymes identified in this work as a CMA substrate in Tregs is the m6A RNA demethylase FTO79. A link between CMA and epigenetic regulation had previously been established through the identification of histone acetyltransferases and deacetylases as CMA regulators80–82; however, to our knowledge, this represents the first connection between CMA and RNA modification. Failure to degrade FTO results in elevated enzyme levels and global m⁶A RNA hypomethylation. m⁶A methylation is critical for Treg stability and suppressive function, as demonstrated in METTL3-deficient models59. Notably, although hypomethylation arises from reduced methylation in METTL3-deficient Tregs and from increased demethylation in our model, both converge on the same molecular targets—negative-feedback regulators of IL-2 signaling. Loss of m⁶A marks stabilizes these transcripts, enhancing inhibition of IL-2/STAT5 signaling, which is essential for proper Treg function. Thus, the phenotype of CMA-defective Tregs reflects both impaired turnover of CMA substrates and secondary transcriptional changes driven by altered m⁶A dynamics. Although our molecular analyses were performed before overt phenotypic manifestations under basal conditions, some of the observed proteomic and transcriptomic changes may represent compensatory responses. Discriminating primary CMA-dependent effects from secondary adaptations is beyond the scope of this initial characterization of CMA-deficient Tregs. Instead, we used the IL-2 signaling pathway to illustrate the complexity of these coexisting mechanisms. Detailed temporal analyses under basal and immunostimulatory conditions will be required to clearly distinguish primary effects from compensatory adaptations.

In the case of IL-2 signaling, we propose that FTO is a direct CMA substrate and a major contributor to the observed m⁶A hypomethylation. However, because m⁶A effects are transcript-specific and only a limited number of direct FTO targets have been identified55,58,83,84, it remains difficult to distinguish primary m⁶A-driven transcriptional changes from secondary compensatory responses. For instance, sustained expression of negative-feedback transcripts may underlie reduced levels of some IL-2 receptor subunits in CMA-deficient Tregs, whereas others, such as IL-2Rγ, appear primarily affected by defective receptor internalization and endocytosis. Notably, CMA deficiency has been linked to aberrant endocytosis in neurons due to defective turnover of actin cytoskeletal components, trafficking adaptors, and motor proteins6,75. Future studies will be required to identify the CMA substrates responsible for endocytic defects in CMA-deficient Tregs. Despite reduced IL-2R levels, STAT5 phosphorylation was surprisingly largely preserved. It is possible that other factors contribute to sustain STAT5 signaling. For example, our proteomic analysis identified JAK1 as a putative CMA substrate (displaying lysosomal degradation dependent on L2A) (Supplementary Fig. 13c). Its accumulation due to impaired degradation may help sustain STAT5 signaling, similar to the prolonged SMAD2/3 signaling observed in CMA-deficient pre-adipocytes13.

Overall, our study identifies a previously unknown role for CMA in the immunosuppressive function of regulatory T cells and provides support of the potential of pharmacologically targeting CMA against the chronic inflammatory phenotypes that associate with many age-related conditions.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6J, C57BL/6-Tg(Foxp3-GFP)90Pkraj/J (Foxp3GFP), B6129S-Tg(Foxp3-EGFP/icre)1aJbs/J (Foxp3GFPCre)) and B6.129S7-Rag1tm1Mom/J (Rag1-/-) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories. KFERQDendra2 transgenic mice were generated as described31. Treg-specific LAMP2A KO mice were generated by crossing Foxp3GFPCre mice with Lamp2afl/fl10mice. Due to LAMP2A and FOXP3 being X-linked, control mice were either Lamp2afl/fl or Foxp3GFPCre Lamp2a+/+ generated by crossing Foxp3GFPCre mice to C57BL/6 mice to obtain Foxp3GFPCre Lamp2a+/+ mice, which have intact LAMP2A expression and express GFP in Tregs, which allowed for sorting of CMA competent and incompetent Tregs. Lamp2afl/fl or Foxp3GFPCre Lamp2a+/+ were separately analyzed for each test and since no differences were detected among them, they were grouped in the results and collectively called control (CTRL). All experiments were performed using male and female mice. Since we did not find sex-related differences in any of the parameters analyzed, results include both male and female mice as a pool. We performed mouse experiments at 3, 4, 6, or 12 months of age. For the experiments involving the pharmacological restoration of CMA in old mice, the groups of mice used were young (4–6 months) and old (23 months). This last group was randomly divided into mice that daily received vehicle or the CMA activator (30 mg/kg body weight) in the form of sucralose gelatin agar pills42. All mice were maintained on a C57BL/6 background and were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility with a dark/light cycle of 12 h, ambient temperature of 65–75 °F and 30–60% humidity. All mouse procedures were performed in accordance with protocols that were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Albert Einstein College of Medicine.

Reagents

We have provided antibody dilutions and catalog numbers for all antibodies and sources of chemicals and reagents in Supplementary Data 2. When possible, antibodies were validated using knockdown cell lines or knockout mouse tissues. Almost all antibodies used in this work are commercial and have been chosen based on information provided in literature or company’s recommendations.

Tregs cell culture and activation

Tregs from spleen and lymph nodes were either sorted or isolated using CD4+CD25+ regulatory T cell isolation kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were cultured in T cell media: Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 550 nM L-Arg, 240 nM L-Asn, and 14 nM folic acid, 1× penicillin/streptomycin, 1× MEM vitamin solution, 1× MEM non-essential amino acid solution, 100 mM HEPES, 1× GlutaMAX and 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol. Tregs were activated with 1 μg/ml plate-bound anti-CD3 and 1 μg/ml anti-CD28 or mouse CD3/CD28 activator beads at 1:2 beads:cells ratio, and 200 U/ml recombinant mouse IL-2 for 24 h. Stimulation of Tregs with only IL-2 was used for the analysis of IL-2 downstream responsive genes. For the proteomic analysis, cells were activated in the presence of 20 mM ammonium chloride and 100 μM leupeptin (NL) for the last 6 h. Where indicated, activated Tregs were treated with 1 μM MRT68921 with or without the addition of lysosomal inhibitors as above.

Autophagy assays

CMA activity assay: For the analysis of CMA activity Tregs were isolated from spleen and lymph nodes of young (3–5 months) and old (22–24 months) KFERQDendra2 transgenic mice and activated for 24 h. In a separate experiment, mIL-2:αIL-2 complex was injected in young KFERQDendra2 transgenic mice and Tregs were isolated from spleen using CD4+ CD25+ regulatory T cell isolation kit. To measure the degradation of the CMA reporter (KFERQ-Dendra), cells treated or not with lysosomal inhibitors (20 mM ammonium chloride and 100 μM leupeptin) for 5 h were fixed with pre-chilled methanol for 2 min and processed for immunofluorescence with Dendra antibody as detailed below.

LC3 flux assay: Macroautophagy activity was assessed by LC3 flux assay by immunoblot and immunofluorescence. Splenic Tregs isolated as above were incubated for 5 h with or without 20 mM ammonium chloride and 100 μM leupeptin and then processed for immunoblot and immunofluorescence for LC3. For immunoblot: Total cellular lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer (1% NP40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.05 Tris-HCl, pH 7.5) with protease and phosphatase inhibitors added. Samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE and wet-transferred to 0.2 μm nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were then blocked with 10% serum in PBS/Tween 0.1% for 45 min at room temperature and incubated 1 h with anti-LC3B antibody or anti-β-actin antibody in 3 % BSA in PBS/Tween 0.1%. After washing, membranes were incubated with secondary HRP-conjugated antibody and developed on film utilizing chemiluminescent reagent. Unsaturated images were used for densitometry with ImageJ software (NIH, version 2.16.0/1.54p) and arbitrary densitometry units were normalized to total protein loaded or to beta-actin, as indicated in figure legends. For immunofluorescence: Tregs were fixed with pre-chilled methanol for 2 min and processed for immunofluorescence as below.

In vivo Treg expansion

mIL-2:αIL-2 complex was formed incubating rmIL-2 with αIL-2 Mab, in 1X PBS at a 1:5 ug ratio, for 45 min at 37 °C. KFERQDendra2 mice at 3–4 month of age were injected intraperitoneally (IP) with 1, 2, or 3 μg of IL-2 equivalent complex into 200 μl PBS, daily for 3 days. On day 5, splenic Tregs were isolated using CD4+CD25+ regulatory T cell isolation kit and incubated for 5 h with or without 20 mM ammonium chloride and 100 μM leupeptin. Splenocytes were stained for CD4, CD25, Foxp3, LAMP2A, Zombie UV and analyzed by flow cytometry for validation of Treg expansion.

Tregs-mediated suppression assays

Tregs were isolated from mouse spleens using CD4+CD25+ Regulatory T cell isolation kit. For our CTRL versus TregL2AKO suppression assay, Treg suppression was evaluated in resting Treg conditions. For our CA39.1-treated suppression assay, Tregs from young (3–4 months) and old (20–22 months) mice were activated for 24 h at a 1:2 ratio of anti-CD3/CD28 activation bead per Treg in the presence of 50 U/ml IL-2, either with or without 10 μM CA39.1 (additionally, CA39.1 was replenished at 12 h of activation). For co-culture preparation: CD11c+ splenocytes (DC), naïve CD4+ conventional T cells (Tconv), and naïve CD8+ Tconv, were isolated from 6 to 10-week-old C57BL/6 J mice using the CD11c+, CD4+, and CD8+ isolation kits (Miltenyi), respectively. CD4+ or CD8+ Tconv were stained with 5 mM Cell Trace Violet for 5 min at room temperature to monitor proliferation. CD4+ or CD8+ Tconv were plated in the presence of DC (at a constant ratio of 1 DC:5 Tconv) and 1 μg/mL of soluble anti-CD3ε85. Resting or activated Tregs were then collected, counted, and serial diluted to establish ratios (Treg:Tconv) of 1:4, 1:8, 1:16, and 1:32, and added to the co-culture. The final co-culture was then incubated for 72 h at 37 °C, 10% CO2 in a humidified chamber, and Tconv proliferation was assessed by flow cytometry.

RNA isolation, quantitative real-time PCR and m6A RNA methylation

Tregs were sorted from the spleen and lymph nodes of the mice. The mucosa of the small intestine and the colon were also collected. For quantitative PCR, total RNA was isolated using TRizol according to the manufacturer’s protocol and treated with DNase I prior to reverse transcription. For the tissue mucosa, genomic DNA was excluded using a gDNA eliminator spin column. Reverse transcription of RNA to cDNA was performed using the Superscript-III First-Strand Synthesis System. Gene expression was analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR using PowerSYBR and the primers summarized in Supplementary Data 3, in a StepOnePlus Real-Time-PCR system. Expression of each gene was normalized to that of β-actin or of β-2-microglobulin (B2M).

For measurement of m6A mRNA methylation content, RNA was isolated as described above, and 200 ng RNA was used following EpiQuik m6A RNA methylation quantification kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Body composition analysis

Body composition was determined in conscious mice by magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) using an Echo MRS instrument (Echo Medical System) at the animal physiology core of Albert Einstein College of Medicine.

In vivo intestinal permeability test

Intestinal permeability was determined by fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran (FITC-dextran) assay36. Briefly, mice fasted for 4 h received FITC-dextran (molecular mass, 4.4 kDa; FD4) by oral gavage (600 mg/kg body weight in PBS) in a genotype randomized order. After 4 h, blood was drawn, centrifuged (10,000 rcf at 4 °C) for 10 min, and plasma was collected. Fifty microliters of plasma were mixed with 200 µl of PBS (pH 7.4) and added to a 96-well microplate. Fluorescein concentration was measured at λexc = 485 nm and λem = 530 nm in a fluorescence plate reader (TECAN infinite M200 PRO) using serially diluted samples of the FITC-dextran marker as standard curve.

Cell and tissue imaging

Fluorescence microscopy

All immunofluorescence steps for cells were performed in a 96-well plate. Briefly, fixed cells (1 × 105) were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) for 20 min at room temperature followed by blocking with 3% BSA in PBS for 45 min at room temperature. Incubation with primary and secondary antibody conjugated to fluorophore in 3% BSA (0.01% Tween-20 in PBS) were performed at room temperature for 1 h each. Cells were added to positively charged slides and incubated for 1 h at room temperature for adequate adherence, following which they were mounted in DAPI-Fluoromount-G. Images were acquired with Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope using 63× objective (1.25× zoom) and 1.4 numerical aperture. Quantification of images was performed after thresholding with Image J software. The number of puncta was quantified using “spotcounter” (ImageJ). Mean fluorescent intensity was measured with ImageJ.

Tissue imaging

Small intestine (jejunum), colon and epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h, paraffin embedded and sectioned (5 μm thick) for staining with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Sections were imaged using Leica light microscope DMD108. Morphometric analysis of different tissues was performed on images of H&E sections blindly using ImageJ. Cell size of eWAT was analyzed using the “cell magic wand” tool in ImageJ86. Cell area of >175 cells per section was determined. Colon was collected and fixed with 4% PFA for 24 h, washed in PBS and preserved in 30% sucrose in PBS for at least 36 h for cryoprotection. Samples were subsequently frozen in Tissue-Tek optimum cutting temperature compound (Sakura, 4586) with dry ice. Sections (30 μm thick) were collected on pretreated slides. Tissue sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 30 min and blocked with 3% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Tissue sections were then incubated with primary antibody in the blocking solution for 2 h at room temperature. After washing (three times for 5 min each), the sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody in blocking solution and Hoechst 33342 for 2 h at room temperature. After washing (three times for 5 min each), the sections were mounted with ProLong antifade. Images were acquired with Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope using 40× objective and 1.4 numerical aperture. Where indicated, z-stacks spanning the entirety of the section were acquired. After thresholding, mean fluorescent intensity was measured with ImageJ.

Inflammatory bowel disease recovery model

Colitis was induced by IP injection of naïve 5 × 105 CD4+ CD25-CD45RBHi T cells from C57BL/6 mice into RAG1−/− mice44. Mice weight was monitored every 7 days until an initial weight loss of 2%, after which mice weight was monitored every other day. When mice had lost about 5% of initial body weight (indicating onset of colitis), 5 × 105 Tregs were isolated from CTRL and TregL2AKO mice and injected intraperitoneally into the RAG1−/− mice to allow for the recovery from colitis. Afterwards, mice were monitored every 3 days. At day 40, mice were euthanized, and the colon was taken and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h. Tissues were then processed for H&E staining. Histological score of the colon was performed by an expert pathologist blinded to genotype and intervention following a well-established protocol44.

Tumor cell culture and inoculation

MC38 (C57BL6-derived colon adenocarcinoma cells; microsatellite unstable and KRAS WT) were received as kind gift from Dr. Xingxing Zang (Albert Einstein College of Medicine). MC38 cells were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2 in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1× penicillin/streptomycin, 1× GlutaMAX and 100 mM HEPES. Tumor cells were harvested from culture and resuspended in PBS, and 5 × 105 cells were injected subcutaneously into the shaved flanks of CTRL and TregL2AKO mice. Tumor growth was measured with digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the equation V = L × W2/2. Tumors were allowed to grow until the humane endpoints of volume not exceeding 3000 mm3 as permitted by our institutional review board. Upon resection, tumors were minced into small pieces and incubated in a shaking incubator for 45 min at 37 °C, in dissociation cocktail: 0.5 mg/ml Collagenase IV and 200 U/mL DNase I in serum-free DMEM. Dissociated cell suspensions were filtered through 70 μm cell strainers, and subsequently, tumor infiltrating lymphocytes were isolated over a 40–80% Percoll density gradient by centrifugation at 320 rcf for 20 min at room temperature with no brake. The resulting interphase was extracted for flow cytometry analysis.

Flow cytometry

Single cell suspensions were obtained by filtering through 70 μm cell strainers and viability assessed using fixable blue live dead cell stain for 30 min at room temperature. Surface staining of the cell suspension was performed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) with the indicated antibodies for 40 min at 4 °C. For staining with anti-Foxp3 antibody, a Foxp3/Transcription factor staining buffer set was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For staining of intracellular cytokines, cells were stimulated in medium containing 1 μM ionomycin and 50 nM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) in the presence of 5 μg/ml Brefeldin A at 37 °C for 4 h. Subsequently, cells were fixed and permeabilized using Cytofix/Cytoperm fixation/permeabilization kit or Foxp3/Transcription factor staining buffer set according to the manufacturer’s instructions and stained with the indicated antibodies for 45 min at 4 °C. Sources and dilutions of the fluorescent conjugate-labeled antibodies used are compiled in Supplementary Data 2. Flow cytometry data was acquired on a BD LSRII-Yellow (BD) or BD FACSymphony A5 SE (BD) and analyzed with FlowJo v.10. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting was performed on BD FACSAria II (BD).

Ex vivo IL-2 stimulation and phosphorylated Stat5 staining

Ex vivo IL-2 stimulation and phosphorylated Stat5 staining were performed as described87. Briefly, splenocytes from CTRL and TregL2AKO mice were stained for viability with fixable blue live dead cell stain and then ex vivo stimulated with indicated concentrations of recombinant mouse IL-2 for 30 min at 37 °C. Cells were fixed and stained for Foxp3 using the Foxp3/Transcription factor staining buffer set and then fixed with 2% PFA and subsequently permeabilized with 90% cold methanol. Fixed and permeabilized cells were then stained with phospho-STAT5 Tyr694 (PE, Clone: SRBCZX) at room temperature for 40 min.

Quantitative proteomic analysis

Sample preparation

All proteomic experiments were performed using three independent mice per condition. Pooling was not required, as sufficient cell numbers were obtained from individual mice (250,000 cells were used per condition). Tregs were isolated from spleen using the CD4+CD25+ regulatory T Cell Isolation Kit from 6-month-old CTRL and TregL2AKO mice. Purity upon isolation (80-90%) was tested by flow cytometry staining for CD4, CD25, and Foxp3. Isolated cells were kept under resting conditions or activated with mouse CD3/CD28 activator beads for 24 h. Cells were untreated or treated with lysosomal protease inhibitors, ammonium chloride (20 mM), and leupeptin (100 μM) for the last 5 h of activation. Cells were lysed in a buffer containing 6 M urea, 2 M thiourea, 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, 1 mM DTT, Complete Protease Inhibitor (Roche), and 2 mM PMSF. After centrifugation, the soluble fraction was diluted 5 times in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, and 5 mM DTT final was left on the bench for about 1 h for disulfide bond reduction. Samples were then alkylated adding iodoacetamide to a final concentration of 20 mM and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Afterward, 0.5 µg of sequencing grade trypsin (Promega), diluted in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, was added into the sample and digested at room temperature for 18 h. Tryptic peptides quantification is routinely performed by the Proteomic Core prior to MS to ensure enough material for analysis, avoiding column overload, and normalizing of samples.

Sample desalting

Prior to mass spectrometry analysis, samples were desalted using a 96-well plate filter (Orochem) packed with 1 mg of Oasis HLB C-18 resin (Waters). Briefly, the samples were resuspended in 100 µl of 0.1% TFA and loaded onto the HLB resin, which was previously equilibrated using 100 µl of the same buffer. After washing with 100 µl of 0.1% TFA, the samples were eluted with a buffer containing 70 µl of 60% acetonitrile and 0.1% TFA and then dried in a vacuum centrifuge.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Samples were acquired using a nanoElute 2 HLPC system (Bruker Daltonik GmbH) coupled to a timsTOF HT (Bruker Daltonik GmbH). Peptides were separated with a PepSep XTREME (25 cm × 150 µM, 1.5 µm particle size) with a column temperature of 50 °C and through a 20 µm emitter. A 30 min elution window was used with a 2–35% Solvent B (0.1% formic acid Sigma-Aldrich F0507, acetonitrile) linear gradient at a flow rate of 500 nL/min. The column was then at 95% Solvent B for 5 min.

The spectra were acquired in dia-PASEF in the range of 100–1700 m/z and 0.6 1/K0 [V-s/cm-2] to 1.6 1/K0 [V-s/cm-2]. Ramp and accumulation times were set to 100. 32 DIA isolation windows were used in the range of 400-1201 m/z and 0.6 1/K0 [V-s/cm-2] to 1.6 1/K0 [V-s/cm-2]. The collision energy was ramped as a function of increasing mobility started from 20 eV at 0.60 1/K0 to 59 eV at 1.6 1/K0.

Searching acquired mass spectra

The acquired data was searched using DIA-NN (version 1.9.2). A predicted spectral library was generated in DIA-NN with a SwissProt (2024-01-24 release) mouse database. The maximum number of missed cleaves was set to 1 with trypsin. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was enabled as a fixed modification and methionine oxidation and N-terminal protein acetylation were set as variable modifications. Match between runs was enabled and a false discovery rate of 1% was used. The DIA-NN output was further processed with directLFQ (v0.3.0) to get the ion and protein quantification.

Following the search, data were processed as described88. Briefly, proteins were log2 transformed, normalized by the average value of each sample and missing values were imputed using a normal distribution 2 standard deviations lower than the mean. Because of the small sample size (n = 3 per group), data distribution could not be reliably assessed. Statistical regulation was assessed using Mann–Whitney U test unpaired (if p-value < 0.05). Changes in protein levels were thresholded as those above 1.5 or 0.8 fold relative to CTRL and degradation of protein as a change of 1.2 fold in lysosome inhibitor-treated conditions relative to untreated. The 1.2 cut-off has been commonly used in studies on lysosomal protein degradation11,34,43,74,76 as most proteins degraded in lysosomes are long-lived and only a small fraction of the total cellular pool is expected to be turned over during the short experimental window. Because statistical power is limited with only n = 3 samples, we report the number of changing proteins and performed pathway analyses using two complementary approaches: (Method 1) considering only proteins with p < 0.05; and (Method 2) filtering proteins using the thresholds described above and selecting only those that changed in the same direction across all three biological replicates. Pathway analysis was performed using the Enrich suite of gene set enrichment analysis tools48 https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/. Results from Reactome were used as primary analysis and, for reporting, prioritized pathways that are also identified by at least one additional tool (WikiPathways Mouse or STRING).

Seahorse assay

Oxygen consumption rates and extracellular acidification rates were measured using Seahorse Bioanalyzer XF 96 according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Agilent Technologies). Briefly, Tregs were activated as described previously for 24 h. Tregs (100,000 cells per well) were plated onto 96-well plates pre-coated with poly-D-lysine. For extracellular acidification rates, cells were maintained with base medium supplemented with 2mM L-glutamine and 1 mM pyruvate and incubated in a CO2-free chamber at 37 °C for 1 h. Once in the bioanalyzer, wells were sequentially injected with 25 mM glucose, 2 μM oligomycin and 100 mM 2-deoxyglucose. For oxygen consumption rates, cells were maintained with base medium supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM pyruvate and 10 mM glucose and incubated in a CO2-free chamber at 37 °C for 1 h. Once in the bioanalyzer, wells were sequentially injected with 1 μM oligomycin, 2 μM carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP), and 0.5 μM rotenone. Glycolysis, basal respiration, and non-mitochondrial respiration were calculated using Wave software (version 2.6.3.5) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Data was normalized to cell number using CyQuant (ThermoFisher, C7026).

Quantification, statistical analysis, and software

Results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (sem). Prior to statistical testing, normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test, one- or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with multiple comparisons post-hoc tests were used for statistical analysis as indicated in the figure legends. The number of animals used per experiment was calculated through power analysis based on previous results. None of the animals were excluded from the study. All image-based procedures were performed blinded to genotype or condition. Treatments and interventions in animals were performed in mice divided by genotype but randomly allocated to vehicle/control or treatment/intervention. Numerical data were handled using Microsoft Excel (version 16.107.4), graphical plots and statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10.0, and schematics for some figures were generated with BioRender (© BioRender 2022).
