Hypothalamic Inflammaging: A Central Driver of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus and Metabolic Disease in Aging
Authors: Sang Hee Lyoo, Ki Hun Kim, Ki Woo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAging is a major risk factor for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and is accompanied by chronic, low-grade inflammation known as inflammaging. Emerging evidence indicates that the hypothalamus, a central regulator of energy and glucose homeostasis, undergoes age-associated inflammatory remodeling that contributes to metabolic dysfunction. In particular, glial cells, including microglia, astrocytes, tanycytes, and neural stem cells, acquire senescence-associated phenotypes characterized by impaired homeostatic functions and increased secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators. These changes disrupt hypothalamic neuronal circuits involved in glucose sensing, energy balance, and insulin responsiveness, thereby potentially promoting systemic insulin resistance and T2DM progression. In this review, we summarize current evidence on the cellular and molecular mechanisms of hypothalamic glial inflammaging and discuss how age-related glial dysfunction may contribute to metabolic abnormalities. We also highlight emerging therapeutic strategies targeting neuroinflammation and glial senescence for the prevention and treatment of age-associated T2DM.Keywords: Aging; Diabetes mellitus, type 2; Hypothalamus; Inflammation mediators; Insulin resistance; Neuroglia; Obesity

INTRODUCTION

The global extension of human longevity has paradoxically been accompanied by a growing epidemic of age-associated metabolic pathologies. Central to this crisis is type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), which represents a major threat to healthspan in older adults. Aging is now increasingly recognized as an important risk factor for T2DM, operating beyond—and often amplifying—the burden imposed by traditional metabolic stressors such as overnutrition, physical inactivity, and adiposity [1]. A cardinal hallmark of this senescent process is an “inflammaging” state of systemic, sterile, and low-grade chronic inflammation [2,3]. Conventionally, this pro-inflammatory milieu has been thought to orchestrate a deleterious cascade of peripheral cellular failures, including impaired pancreatic β-cell secretory function and diminished peripheral insulin sensitivity. However, this periphery-centered paradigm does not fully account for the central neuroendocrine and neuroimmune networks that govern systemic metabolic homeostasis. Emerging evidence indicates that age-dependent disruption of bidirectional communication between the brain and peripheral metabolic organs—particularly in association with central nervous system (CNS) inflammaging—may increase vulnerability to the onset and progression of T2DM [4].

Although peripheral organs, including the pancreas, liver, adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle, have traditionally dominated conceptual frameworks of metabolic disease, the CNS, particularly the hypothalamus, is increasingly recognized as an indispensable regulator of whole-body energy and glucose homeostasis. The hypothalamus integrates circulating nutrients, hormonal cues, autonomic inputs, and inflammatory signals to coordinate feeding behavior, energy expenditure, hepatic glucose production, pancreatic endocrine function, and peripheral insulin responsiveness [5-7]. Recent studies have revealed that diverse hypothalamic neuronal populations do not operate in isolation but instead form highly coordinated neuroendocrine and autonomic circuits that dynamically regulate systemic metabolism. Moreover, glial cells within the hypothalamus have emerged as active participants, rather than passive support elements, in the maintenance of metabolic homeostasis. These glial populations shape the local brain microenvironment, regulate nutrient and hormone access, modulate innate immune responses, provide metabolic support to neurons, and refine neuron-glia communication within hypothalamic circuits [8].

However, glial cells are themselves vulnerable to aging and undergo profound functional and phenotypic remodeling characterized by heightened inflammatory signaling, impaired metabolic support, and disrupted neuron-glia communication. This age-associated glial activation has been conceptualized as “glial inflammaging,” a process defined by the chronic acquisition of pro-inflammatory, metabolically maladaptive, and circuit-disruptive glial states within the aging brain. In the hypothalamus, glial inflammaging may be particularly consequential because even subtle perturbations in this region can reverberate through the endocrine, autonomic, and behavioral pathways that regulate systemic metabolism. Sustained production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, altered synaptic remodeling, impaired nutrient and hormone sensing, and perturbation of neuroendocrine circuits may collectively compromise metabolic control. Importantly, emerging studies suggest that progressive hypothalamic glial dysfunction during aging contributes to systemic metabolic abnormalities, including insulin resistance, impaired glucose sensing, dysregulated appetite-controlling neuronal networks, and altered energy balance [9-11]. These observations support the concept that T2DM is not solely a disorder of peripheral insulin action, but also a neuroimmune-metabolic disease in which age-dependent hypothalamic inflammation may act as an upstream amplifier of systemic metabolic decline.

In this review, we focus on the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying glial inflammaging in the brain. We further examine how these age-associated glial alterations may contribute to dysregulation of hypothalamic glucose-sensing and glucose-regulatory circuits. We also highlight potential therapeutic strategies targeting central neuroinflammation to mitigate age-associated metabolic decline.

HYPOTHALAMIC INFLAMMATION AS A RISK FACTOR FOR T2DM

The hypothalamic neuro-visceral axis in blood glucose homeostasis

Far beyond its role as an anatomical region in the ventral diencephalon, the hypothalamus serves as a master homeostatic integrator, modulating whole-body metabolism through intricate endocrine and autonomic networks [12]. In the hypothalamus, glucose-sensing neurons, which play indispensable roles in glucose homeostasis, are widely distributed across the arcuate nucleus (ARC), ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH), lateral hypothalamus, and paraventricular nucleus (PVH). These neurons are functionally classified as glucose-excited (GE) and glucose-inhibited (GI) neurons (Fig. 1) [13,14].

Glucose-excited (GE) and glucose-inhibited (GI) neurons within hypothalamic nuclei sense fluctuations in circulating glucose levels and coordinate autonomic and endocrine responses. (A) Elevated glucose activates GE neurons, promoting parasympathetic signaling and insulin secretion while suppressing hepatic glucose production. (B) Conversely, reduced glucose activates GI neurons, enhancing sympathetic outflow and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activity, thereby increasing epinephrine and cortisol secretion from the adrenal gland. In parallel, sympathetic outflow increases glucagon secretion from the pancreas, glycogenolysis in muscle, and gluconeogenesis in the liver. These integrated neural and hormonal pathways regulate glucose metabolism in peripheral tissues and maintain systemic glucose homeostasis. GE neurons are activated by ambient elevations in glucose levels. Circulating glucose is taken up by these neurons via glucose transporter 3 and enters glycolysis to synthesize adenosine triphosphate (ATP). The resulting increase in the intracellular ATP/adenosine diphosphate ratio triggers the closure of ATP-sensitive K+ (KATP) channels, precipitating membrane depolarization [15,16]. This voltage shift induces Ca²+ influx via voltage-dependent Ca²+ channels, ultimately culminating in action potential firing. Although the intracellular mechanisms governing GI neuron activation under low-glucose conditions are not fully elucidated, reductions in intracellular ATP activate adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK). This kinase, in turn, modulates membrane ion conductance, particularly by suppressing potassium currents and engaging nitric oxide-dependent signaling, thereby inducing membrane depolarization and increasing firing activity [17,18].

Once activated by fluctuating glucose levels, GE and GI neurons orchestrate endocrine and autonomic responses that regulate peripheral organs such as the liver, adrenal gland, and pancreas. Specifically, GE neurons in the ARC and VMH project to the PVH, which connects to the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus in the medulla oblongata to enhance parasympathetic tone via the vagus nerve, promoting insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells and suppressing hepatic gluconeogenesis [19-21]. In contrast, GI neurons in the ARC trigger a counter-regulatory response, likewise mediated by the PVH, that activates the rostral ventrolateral medulla and preganglionic sympathetic neurons, leading to cortisol and epinephrine secretion from the adrenal gland. Elevated epinephrine stimulates pancreatic α-cells to promote glucagon secretion, drives glycogenolysis in skeletal muscle, and accelerates hepatic glucose production [18,20]. The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis is also activated to enhance cortisol secretion via corticotropin-releasing hormone-expressing neurons in the PVH; these neurons inhibit peripheral glucose absorption and increase hepatic gluconeogenesis, collectively ensuring that systemic glucose levels are maintained during metabolic challenges (Fig. 1) [22,23].

However, accumulating evidence indicates that inflammation associated with aging and metabolic stress within the hypothalamus disrupts these finely tuned glucose-sensing and autonomic-neuroendocrine circuits, thereby contributing to the development of systemic insulin resistance and T2DM.

Convergence of major risk factors for T2DM: obesity, aging, and meta-inflammation

T2DM is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by impaired glucose homeostasis resulting from insulin resistance and progressive pancreatic β-cell dysfunction [24]. As one of the most prevalent metabolic disorders worldwide, its incidence increases markedly with age, and it is closely linked to severe comorbidities, including hypertension and coronary artery disease [25]. The development of T2DM is driven by multiple genetic, environmental, and lifestyle-related factors, among which obesity and aging are the most prominent contributors [26-28].

Obesity is widely recognized as a critical contributor to T2DM pathogenesis (Fig. 2). Clinical data indicate that 86% of T2DM patients are overweight or obese [26]. Excessive accumulation of adipose tissue promotes the infiltration and activation of macrophages, leading to chronic inflammation, often termed meta-inflammation, and metabolic imbalance. These alterations subsequently impair insulin signaling pathways in peripheral metabolic tissues. In obese individuals, adipose tissue undergoes functional remodeling accompanied by increased production of pro-inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and IL-1β [29,30]. In addition to adipose tissue-derived inflammatory mediators, nutrient excess itself imposes metabolic stress, including saturated fatty acid-associated inflammatory signaling that can engage hypothalamic inflammatory pathways during obesity [31-33]. Crucially, this obesity-associated systemic inflammation reaches the CNS by crossing the blood-brain barrier (BBB), particularly affecting the hypothalamus and thereby disrupting neuronal circuits involved in energy homeostasis and glucose regulation [9,27,34-37].

Convergence of obesity and aging on hypothalamic inflammation and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Obesity and aging represent two major drivers of chronic low-grade inflammation that converge on the hypothalamus. In obesity, nutrient excess and adipose tissue expansion promote macrophage infiltration and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and IL-1β. Excess nutrients, particularly saturated fatty acids (SFAs), can further impose metabolic stress, including endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and engage hypothalamic inflammatory pathways. In parallel, aging is associated with physiological decline, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic sterile low-grade inflammation, referred to as inflammaging, leading to altered hypothalamic function and neuroinflammatory signaling. These processes promote microglial activation, astrocyte reactivity, nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)/c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) signaling, impaired leptin/insulin signaling, and impaired nutrient sensing, ultimately inducing hypothalamic inflammation. Disruption of hypothalamic neuronal circuits involved in energy balance and glucose regulation contributes to metabolic dysfunction in the liver, skeletal muscle, and adipose tissue, resulting in systemic insulin resistance. Together with pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, these alterations promote hyperglycemia and the development of T2DM.

Aging is another major risk factor for T2DM (Fig. 2) [27,28]. Epidemiological analyses from Korea have demonstrated that approximately 29.3% of adults aged ≥65 years are affected by diabetes [27]. Aging is accompanied by progressive physiological decline, impaired mitochondrial function, and chronic low-grade inflammation, commonly referred to as “inflammaging” [38,39]. Age-associated inflammatory responses and cellular dysfunction contribute to insulin resistance and impaired glucose tolerance [40]. Critically, aging has been associated with altered hypothalamic function, neuroinflammatory responses, and dysregulated endocrine outputs [34,36]. Obesity and aging are distinct risk factors for T2DM, driven by different biological stressors. Obesity is primarily linked to nutrient excess, adipose tissue expansion, and metabolic inflammation, whereas aging is characterized by cumulative cellular stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and systemic inflammaging [29-33,38-40]. Despite these differences, both conditions can converge on chronic low-grade inflammatory remodeling of metabolic tissues and central regulatory networks, including the hypothalamus [34-36]. This convergence suggests that hypothalamic inflammation may provide a common central interface through which obesity- and aging-associated stressors disrupt energy balance, insulin responsiveness, and glucose homeostasis [9,27,34,35].

Hypothalamic inflammation: a trigger of metabolic dysfunction

Hypothalamic inflammation refers to a localized inflammatory state characterized by activation of inflammatory signaling pathways within metabolically important hypothalamic nuclei [34,36]. This inflammatory environment can affect hypothalamic neuronal populations involved in systemic metabolism, particularly ARC pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) and agoutirelated peptide/neuropeptide Y neurons. Disruption of these neuronal populations may impair the central regulation of feeding behavior, insulin responsiveness, energy balance, and glucose homeostasis [9,41-44]. Although these neuronal populations mediate the downstream metabolic consequences of hypothalamic inflammation, glial cells are particularly important because microglia and astrocytes respond directly to metabolic and age-related stressors, produce pro-inflammatory mediators, and modulate neuronal function [8,34-36].

Increasing evidence suggests a bidirectional and often selfreinforcing relationship between hypothalamic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction [31,34-36]. Several metabolic stressors have been implicated in the initiation and amplification of hypothalamic inflammatory signaling. Among them, saturated fatty acids function as lipid-derived inflammatory stimuli that can promote hypothalamic glial activation and activate inflammatory pathways [32,33]. This lipid-driven response promotes the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and contributes to microglial activation within the mediobasal hypothalamus. In parallel, overnutrition induces endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in hypothalamic neurons and glial cells, with activation of unfolded protein response path-ways that converge on IκB kinase β/nuclear factor kappa B (IKKβ/NF-κB) signaling [45]. This ER stress–IKKβ/NF-κB axis has been shown to increase hypothalamic suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3) expression, a negative regulator of insulin and leptin signaling, thereby contributing to central metabolic dysregulation. In addition to lipid and ER stress, obesity- and aging-associated activation of hypothalamic transforming growth factor-β signaling has been shown to induce an RNA stress response, which further disrupts hypothalamic metabolic regulation and potentiates diabetic phenotypes [27].

In mouse models fed a high-fat diet (HFD), inflammatory activation of hypothalamic glial cells disrupts neuronal circuits involved in energy homeostasis [37,45]. HFD consumption induces the upregulation of various pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, within the hypothalamus [37]. Activation of inflammatory signaling pathways, including c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and IKKβ/NF-κB signaling, further promotes hypothalamic inflammation and impairs insulin and leptin signaling [45]. Notably, seminal studies have demonstrated that this hypothalamic inflammation occurs during the very early stages of HFD-induced obesity, before substantial peripheral weight gain, suggesting that central neuroinflammation actively drives metabolic dysregulation rather than merely representing a secondary consequence of obesity [46]. Diabetic mouse models also exhibit increased astrocytic pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase-2 (PDK2) expression within the hypothalamus, which promotes metabolic reprogramming of glia and upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines [46]. Human evidence supporting the relevance of hypothalamic inflammation to metabolic dysfunction is emerging. Neuroimaging studies using magnetic resonance imaging-based markers have shown that T2-weighted signal intensity in the mediobasal hypothalamus is elevated in patients with T2DM and obesity, indirectly indicating that insulin resistance is related to hypothalamic gliosis [9,47]. In addition, postmortem analyses of human hypothalamic tissue from individuals with T2DM have revealed alterations in hypothalamic neuropeptide neurons and region-specific glial changes, although consistent evidence of increased microglial activation has not been established [48,49]. Collectively, these studies indicate that hypothalamic inflammation is not merely associated with metabolic dysfunction but may actively contribute to the disruption of energy balance and glucose homeostasis.

GLIAL INFLAMMAGING: THE CELLULAR ENGINE OF HYPOTHALAMIC T2DM PATHOGENESIS

Senescent reprogramming drives glial inflammaging in the aging brain

Cellular senescence is a state of irreversible cell-cycle arrest triggered by diverse stressors, including DNA damage, oxidative stress, and telomere attrition [50,51]. Despite the loss of proliferative capacity, senescent cells remain metabolically active and acquire a distinct phenotype known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). The SASP refers to the characteristic secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and proteases by senescent cells, which actively remodels the surrounding tissue microenvironment and converts localized cellular stress into a sustained inflammatory signal. This process represents a central mechanism underlying inflammaging, a chronic, low-grade, sterile inflammatory state that progressively develops with aging and contributes to multiple age-related pathologies, including cardiovascular disease, neurodegeneration, and metabolic disorders [38,39,52].

Accumulating evidence suggests that inflammaging becomes more pronounced in the aged brain. A key initiating event in this process is the accumulation of senescent cells, particularly glial populations such as astrocytes, microglia, and potentially tanycytes. Age-associated stressors, including DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, are widely believed to promote cellular senescence and SASP acquisition in glial cells, manifesting as cell-type-specific senescent characteristics (Fig. 3) [53,54].

Aging-induced glial senescence drives brain inflammaging. Aging promotes cellular senescence in brain glial cells through elevated oxidative stress, DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and telomere attrition. Senescent microglia exhibit characteristic features of cellular senescence, including morphologically deramified processes, upregulation of cell-cycle inhibitors (cyclindependent kinase inhibitor 2A [CDKN2A, p16INK4a], cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1A [CDKN1A, p21], and tumor protein p53 [p53]), lysosomal dysfunction, and acquisition of a damage-associated microglia (DAM)-like inflammatory phenotype. Likewise, senescent astrocytes exhibit upregulation of cell-cycle inhibitors, decreased metabolic support and neuroprotection, and abnormal crosstalk with microglia. Tanycytes and neural stem cells (NSCs) in the aged brain show decreased stemness and neurogenesis, which may impair nutrient sensing, hormone transport, and inflammatory regulation. Persistent senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) production amplifies glia-mediated chronic neuroinflammation and impairs cellular homeostasis, thereby potentially contributing to hypothalamic dysfunction and metabolic dysregulation. Microglia

In the healthy CNS, microglia serve as highly dynamic tissueresident macrophages responsible for immune surveillance, removal of apoptotic cells, and modulation of synaptic formation. However, in the aged brain, microglia exhibit features of cellular senescence, including increased expression of senescence markers, morphological alterations, transcriptional reprogramming, and development of a SASP.

Morphologically, microglia from aged brains display deramified processes, reduced branching complexity, cytoplasmic fragmentation, and dystrophic morphology. Streit and colleagues first characterized dystrophic microglia in aged human brain tissue by immunohistochemistry using anti-human leukocyte antigen-DR (HLA-DR) monoclonal antibody (clone LN-3) in postmortem cerebral cortex and hippocampal regions from elderly individuals. In this study, microglia from aged human brains exhibited marked morphological abnormalities, including deramification, beading, tortuous processes, spheroid formation, and cytoplasmic fragmentation, which were distinct from the hypertrophic morphology typically observed during acute inflammatory activation [55]. Based on these human postmortem studies, subsequent research has reported that dystrophic microglial morphology with senescence-like phenotypes has also been observed in aged mouse brains. In vivo imaging and histological studies in aged mice demonstrated reduced microglial process complexity, impaired environmental surveillance, lysosomal enlargement, lipofuscin accumulation, and fragmented dystrophic morphology, accompanied by increased oxidative stress and inflamma-tory cytokine production [56-58].

Concurrently, substantial efforts have been directed toward deciphering the molecular signatures of senescent microglia. Microglia isolated from 18- to 24-month-old mice exhibited increased expression of classical senescence markers such as cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A, p16INK4a), cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1A (CDKN1A, p21), and tumor protein p53 (p53) [59-61]. These senescent microglia also demonstrate increased accumulation of lipofuscin granules and elevated histone H2AX phosphorylated at serine 139 (γH2AX) foci, both of which are widely recognized markers of cellular aging and chronic stress responses. Lipofuscin is an autofluorescent intracellular aggregate composed of oxidized lipids and lysosomal degradation residues that progressively accumulates in postmitotic cells during aging [62]. In microglia, increased lipofuscin accumulation is thought to reflect impaired lysosomal clearance, defective autophagy, and chronic oxidative stress exposure. Safaiyan et al. demonstrated that aged mouse brains exhibit progressive myelin fragmentation and accumulation of lipofuscin-like lysosomal inclusions within microglia. Using electron microscopy and confocal imaging, enlarged Lamp1-positive lysosomal compartments and persistent intracellular myelin debris accumulation were observed in aged mice, suggesting that lipofuscin accumulation represents not merely a passive aging marker but also functional deterioration of microglial surveillance and clearance machinery [58]. In parallel, aged microglia frequently exhibit γH2AX foci formation. γH2AX is a phosphorylated form of the histone variant H2AX that rapidly accumulates at sites of DNA double-strand breaks and chromatin damage [63]. Li et al. [60] performed longitudinal transcriptional and epigenetic profiling of microglia isolated from 3-and 24-month-old mice and demonstrated progressive accumulation of aging-associated inflammatory and stress-response programs in aged microglia. Their analyses revealed enhanced activation of DNA damage-associated transcriptional pathways together with epigenetic remodeling linked to inflammatory activation and microglial dysfunction [60]. Furthermore, microglial cells treated with doxorubicin exhibited significantly increased γH2AX foci together with elevated p16INK4a expression, senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity, and inflammatory cytokine production [64]. These results indicate that senescent microglia exhibit the molecular signature of cellular senescence.

A pivotal observation is that senescent microglia also express a damage-associated microglia (DAM)-like transcriptional signature, suggesting that chronic inflammatory challenge to microglia induces cellular senescence. For example, p16INK4a expression is increased in microglia isolated from brain white matter that exhibits transcriptional signatures characteristic of DAM. Another study also reported that p16INK4a-positive microglia showed increased expression of inflammatory and phagocytic genes such as Apoe, Trem2, Lpl, and Cst7 [65]. It is well known that when microglia are exposed to oxidative stress or inflammatory challenges, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or beta amyloid, the cells increase secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6; this is accompanied by elevated NF-κB and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) activation, which are characteristic features of a DAM-like inflammatory phenotype [66,67]. Similar inflammatory profiles were detected in aged mouse brains, where microglia isolated from old animals exhibited exaggerated cytokine responses following peripheral immune stimulation with LPS compared with young controls [68].

Despite these findings, an important conceptual controversy remains regarding whether senescent microglia represent truly irreversible senescent cells or highly activated aging-associated inflammatory states [69]. Because microglia are inherently plastic immune cells capable of rapidly altering transcriptional and functional phenotypes in response to environmental stimuli, definitive discrimination between reactive microglia and canonical senescent microglia, as well as dissociation of these states from DAM, remains challenging [70]. Stojiljkovic et al. [71] directly compared in vitro-induced senescent microglia with aged microglia from old mouse brains, revealing that the latter lack telomere shortening, show only modest p16INK4a upregulation, and exhibit profoundly attenuated IL-6 and IL-1β secretion. This finding suggests that in vitro senescence models may fail to fully recapitulate the complexity of brain aging [71]. Consequently, recent studies increasingly describe these populations as “senescence-like” or “aging-associated dysfunctional microglia” rather than strictly defining them as fully senescent cells.

Astrocytes

Astrocytes are essential glial cells that maintain neuronal homeostasis by regulating synaptic transmission, glutamate clearance, ion buffering, metabolic support, and BBB integrity [72]. During aging, however, astrocytes are exposed to persistent oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage, and inflammatory stimuli, which gradually alter their homeostatic functions and promote a reactive phenotype. Unlike acute reactive astrogliosis, which can be protective after injury, age-associated astrocytes exhibit sustained low-grade inflammatory activity that becomes maladaptive over time [73].

Recent studies suggest that a subset of aged astrocytes undergoes cell-cycle arrest, which is a typical characteristic of cellular senescence. Cell-cycle arrest in senescent astrocytes is associated with the p53/p21 and p16INK4a pathways. Crowe and colleagues reported that, in human astrocytes exposed to oxidative stress, expression of p21 and p16INK4a was significantly increased together with reduced proliferative capacity and enhanced SA-β-gal activity. These cells undergo multiphase cellcycle arrest involving both G1 and G2/M phases rather than simple G1 arrest alone [74]. Furthermore, in postmortem human brain tissue from aging and neurodegenerative conditions, several studies have reported an increased proportion of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)-positive astrocytes expressing senescence-associated markers, including p16INK4a and, in some cases, p21, particularly in cortical regions compared with age-matched control brains. These findings suggest that chronic cellular stress, such as oxidative stress, amyloid pathology, or other disease-related insults, may promote astrocyte senescence and contribute to age-related neurodegenerative processes [75,76].

Aging-associated astrocytes also exhibit impaired metabolic and neuroprotective functions. Astrocytes normally support neuronal survival by supplying metabolic substrates such as lactate, regulating extracellular glutamate levels through transporters such as glutamate transporter 1/excitatory amino acid transporter 2 (GLT-1/EAAT2), maintaining ion homeostasis, and contributing to BBB integrity. During normal brain aging, however, astrocytes can acquire a dysfunctional phenotype characterized by reduced glutamate uptake, altered expression or function of glutamate transporters, mitochondrial impairment, and weakened antioxidant defenses [77]. Previous studies have shown that aging is associated with astrocytic dysfunction characterized by impaired glutamate homeostasis and reduced glutamate uptake, leading to dysregulated glutamatergic signaling in the aged brain. For example, aged hippocampi exhibit reduced glutamate transporter function and decreased glutamate clearance, resulting in enhanced extrasynaptic glutamate signaling and synaptic dysfunction [77,78]. In addition, transcriptomic and metabolic analyses have demonstrated agerelated alterations in astrocytic genes involved in glutamate metabolism and glutamate-glutamine cycling [79,80]. Experimental studies further indicate that physiologically relevant chronic oxidative stress induces astrocytic defects accompanied by increased intracellular and mitochondrial ROS, impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics, reduced antioxidant capacity, and dysregulated glutamate handling [73]. These changes compromise the neuroprotective functions of astrocytes, promote excitotoxic and inflammatory conditions, and may contribute to neuronal dysfunction, synaptic impairment, and age-related cognitive decline.

Another hallmark of astrocyte inflammaging is altered communication with microglia and neurons. During aging, astrocytes acquire a pro-inflammatory phenotype characterized by increased production of cytokines, chemokines, complement components, and SASP factors. At the same time, aged microglia adopt a primed inflammatory state and release mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1α, and complement component 1q (C1q) that promote reactive astrocyte phenotypes. In turn, aged astrocytes further enhance microglial activation through inflammatory signaling, establishing a self-sustaining feedforward inflammatory network [79,81,82]. In the hypothalamus, this reciprocal glial interaction contributes to chronic low-grade neuroinflammation and disrupts neuronal circuits involved in energy balance and glucose sensing. Accumulating evidence suggests that age-associated astrocyte reactivity and hypothalamic gliosis impair insulin signaling and systemic glucose homeostasis, thereby contributing to insulin resistance and increased susceptibility to T2DM [83,84].

Tanycytes and neural stem cells

Tanycytes are specialized radial glia-like cells lining the third ventricle and median eminence, where they function as hypothalamic neural stem/progenitor cells and contribute to nutrient sensing, hormone transport, and regulation of the bloodhypothalamus barrier within the median eminence. Although evidence regarding age-associated tanycyte dysfunction remains comparatively limited, several studies suggest that aged tanycytes exhibit reduced proliferative and neurogenic capacity [85].

In rodent studies, Haan et al. [86] showed that tanycyte proliferation declines with age. Fibroblast growth factor-10 positive (Fgf10+) tanycytes incorporate 5-bromo-2ʹ-deoxyuridine/5-ethynyl-2ʹ-deoxyuridine (BrdU/EdU) during early postnatal life, but this proliferative activity markedly decreases by adulthood, with little BrdU incorporation detected by 12 months of age. In rats, Zoli et al. [87] reported that tanycyte numbers decline by nearly 30% between 3 and 24 months of age, accompanied by reduced dopamine and cyclic AMP-regulated phosphoprotein with a molecular weight of 32,000 (DARPP-32; mature tanycyte marker) immunoreactivity and increased GFAP (reactive tanycyte marker) expression, suggesting ageassociated structural and functional remodeling of tanycytes.

Additionally, recent reviews have emphasized that aging impairs neural stem cell (NSC) function through multiple mechanisms, including altered mitochondrial dynamics, disrupted protein homeostasis, reduced proliferative and self-renewal capacity, and impaired neurogenesis [88]. In this context, hypothalamic NSCs may provide an additional link between hypothalamic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. Li et al. [89] reported that HFD-activated IKKβ/NF-κB signaling in adult mouse hypothalamic NSCs induces apoptosis and impairs neuronal differentiation through Notch signaling, thereby contributing to diet-induced obesity and prediabetes. Consistently, Zhang et al. [90] showed that sex-determining region Y-box 2/B lymphoma Mo-MLV insertion region 1 (Sox2/Bmi1)-positive hypothalamic stem/progenitor cells progressively decline during aging in mice and that experimental ablation of these cells accelerates aging-like physiological deterioration and shortens lifespan. Conversely, transplantation of healthy hypothalamic stem/progenitor cells or treatment with stem cell-derived exosomes ameliorated aging-associated phenotypes. These findings suggest that aging impairs NSC maintenance and neurogenic capacity, thereby reducing hypothalamic plasticity and contributing to age-related metabolic dysfunction [90].

However, most of this evidence comes from rodent models, and direct human studies demonstrating age-associated tanycyte senescence with SASP production and age-associated NSC dysfunction are limited. Therefore, it is more accurate to state that aging-associated tanycyte and NSC dysfunction may contribute to hypothalamic inflammaging by impairing nutrient sensing, barrier function, and neurogenic support.

Importantly, these glial senescent phenotypes are not merely passive markers of aging but also active drivers of local inflammation [91,92]. SASP factors released from senescent glial cells reinforce inflammatory signaling by inducing activation and phenotypic remodeling in neighboring glial cells, thereby amplifying and sustaining brain inflammaging [93,94].

Potential role of hypothalamic glial inflammaging in insulin resistance

In aged mice, hypothalamic glial cells exhibit aging-associated dysfunctional and senescence-like phenotypes. Suda et al. [95] reported that normal aging increases p16INK4a expression in hypothalamic microglia and promotes the accumulation of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)-enriched exhausted microglia and p16INK4a-positive A1-like reactive astrocytes. In parallel, Zhang et al. [96] demonstrated that aging activates hypothalamic microglia and enhances IKKβ/NF-κB-dependent inflammatory signaling, including TNF-α production. Together, these findings suggest that aging promotes glial dysfunction and senescence-associated inflammatory remodeling within the hypothalamus.

Based on these findings, hypothalamic glial inflammaging has emerged as a potential contributor to hypothalamic insulin resistance [96,97]. Among the mechanisms implicated in this process, senescent microglia and astrocytes secrete a broad range of SASP-associated factors, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), and matrix-remodeling enzymes, which may chronically expose neighboring neurons and glial cells to inflammatory stress [93,96,98].

In particular, insulin signaling within hypothalamic nuclei such as the ARC, VMH, and PVH regulates feeding behavior, hepatic glucose production, peripheral insulin sensitivity, and pancreatic hormone secretion [41,99-102]. Under physiological conditions, insulin activates the insulin receptor (IR)/insulin receptor substrate (IRS)/phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) signaling cascade in hypothalamic neurons, particularly POMC neurons, thereby suppressing appetite and promoting glucose-lowering responses [31,44,103, 104]. However, this glia-derived SASP may interfere with insulin signaling pathways through several mechanisms. Mechanistically, TNF-α and IL-1β activate NF-κB- and JNK-dependent signaling pathways, which induce inhibitory serine phosphorylation of IRS proteins and attenuate downstream PI3K/Akt signaling. In parallel, IL-6 promotes expression of SOCS3, a negative regulator of insulin and leptin receptor signaling, thereby further impairing hypothalamic insulin sensitivity [37,99,105]. Furthermore, activation of these inflammatory pathways in the hypothalamus disrupts insulin-mediated suppression of hepatic gluconeogenesis and alters autonomic output regulating peripheral glucose metabolism [16,106,107]. Unlike transient inflammatory responses, persistent SASP signaling establishes a self-sustaining inflammatory microenvironment that can progressively impair neuronal metabolic sensing and intracellular insulin responsiveness [96,97,105].

Although much of the current evidence derives from experimental animal models, these findings support the emerging concept that aspects of the microglia- and astrocyte-derived SASP may contribute to the establishment and persistence of hypothalamic insulin resistance. Tanycytes may further exacerbate insulin insensitivity by impairing nutrient sensing, hormone transport, and neurogenic capacity. However, direct evidence specifically linking hypothalamic glial senescence to insulin resistance remains limited, and further studies, particularly in humans, are required. Nevertheless, accumulating preclinical evidence suggests that targeting glial senescence and SASP signaling pathways may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for preventing or alleviating age-associated metabolic dysfunction and T2DM.

TARGETING HYPOTHALAMIC GLIAL INFLAMMAGING TO TREAT T2DM

Current therapeutic strategies for T2DM and their limitations

Current therapeutic strategies for T2DM have evolved beyond simple glycemic control toward comprehensive management of obesity, cardiovascular complications, and renal dysfunction [108]. However, they remain fundamentally downstream interventions. Metformin primarily suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis and improves peripheral insulin sensitivity, but progressive β-cell dysfunction often limits its long-term efficacy [109,110]. Similarly, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors (e.g., empagliflozin and dapagliflozin) have demonstrated substantial cardiovascular and renoprotective benefits while effectively lowering blood glucose levels through insulinindependent urinary glucose excretion. In addition, these agents can promote modest weight loss and may improve certain metabolic parameters associated with insulin resistance. However, their primary therapeutic effects are not mediated through direct correction of the underlying pathogenic mechanisms driving systemic insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, or progressive β-cell dysfunction [111-113].

Despite substantial therapeutic advances, a critical efficacy ceiling remains: most currently available therapies target downstream metabolic abnormalities rather than the fundamental pathogenic mechanisms underlying disease progression. Increasing evidence suggests that aging-associated hypothalamic glial dysfunction and neuroinflammation serve as central mechanisms linking metabolic stress to impaired glucose homeostasis [96]. Consequently, these findings have generated growing interest in therapeutic strategies targeting senescent glia, SASP-mediated inflammation, and glial metabolic dysfunction within the aging hypothalamus.

Therapeutic opportunities targeting hypothalamic glial inflammaging

Senolytic clearance of senescent glia

Senolytics are pharmacological compounds designed to preferentially induce apoptosis in senescent cells by targeting senescent cell anti-apoptotic pathways (SCAPs), including B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2)/B-cell lymphoma-extra large (BCL-xL) signaling, PI3K/Akt pathways, and tyrosine kinase-associated survival mechanisms [114,115]. Because senescent glial cells remain metabolically active and continuously secrete SASP-associated cytokines, eradicating this cellular burden may halt chronic hypothalamic inflammation and restore neuroimmune homeostasis.

1. Dasatinib and quercetin

The combination of dasatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, and quercetin, a flavonoid, has been studied most extensively. Together, these compounds synergistically inhibit SCAPs. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that dasatinib and quercetin (D+Q) treatment reduces p16INK4a-positive senescent cell burden, suppresses SASP-associated inflammation, improves metabolic function, and improves insulin sensitivity in aging models [116,117]. Although clinical evidence remains limited, a pilot study in patients with diabetic kidney disease demonstrated that treatment with dasatinib and quercetin reduced senescent cell markers and SASP-related factors, supporting the translational feasibility of senolytic therapy [118]. Moreover, experimental studies suggest that D+Q can reduce senescence-associated markers and inflammatory signaling in the aged or diseased CNS, including glia-associated senescent phenotypes [119]. Despite promising preclinical results, translation of dasatinib-based senolytic therapies to CNS disorders remains challenging because adequate drug delivery to the brain and long-term systemic safety have yet to be fully established [120].

2. Fisetin

Fisetin is a naturally occurring flavonoid with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and senolytic properties [121]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that fisetin reduces oxidative stress, attenuates age-associated inflammatory signaling, and decreases the burden of senescent cells in multiple tissues [121]. In addition, fisetin has been shown to suppress microglial activation and neuroinflammatory responses through inhibition of NF-κB and NOD-like receptor pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome signaling pathways, resulting in reduced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and suggesting a potential role in mitigating glial inflammaging [122]. Another study showed that fisetin treatment reduced senescent glia in the brain cortex of old sheep [123]. In aged animal models, fisetin treatment has been associated with improvements in healthspan and metabolic parameters, although its effects on insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis require further investigation [124]. Compared with first-generation senolytic regimens such as D+Q, fisetin exhibits a favorable safety profile and broader tolerability. However, its senolytic activity appears to be cell type-dependent, and limitations in bioavailability may restrict its therapeutic efficacy within the CNS [121].

3. Navitoclax

Navitoclax (ABT-263) is a potent BCL-2/BCL-xL/BCL-w inhibitor that exhibits strong senolytic activity by promoting apoptosis in senescent cells [125]. Preclinical studies have shown that navitoclax can reduce senescent cell burden and attenuate SASP-associated inflammatory signaling, providing important mechanistic proof of concept for targeted senolytic strategies [125,126]. In the CNS, navitoclax has been reported to eliminate senescent astrocytes in ischemia/reperfusion injury models and reduce senescence-associated inflammatory responses, suggesting its potential relevance for glial senescence and neuroinflammation [127]. In addition, ABT-263 treatment improved cognitive function in middle-aged mice and reduced senescence-associated changes in the brain, although evidence for selective clearance of senescent microglia or astrocytes during natural glial inflammaging remains limited [128]. In obesity-associated metabolic models, senescent cell clearance using navitoclax or related senolytic approaches has been associated with improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, although these metabolic benefits may be transient and contextdependent [117]. Despite its efficacy, navitoclax causes significant hematologic toxicity, particularly thrombocytopenia due to BCL-xL inhibition in platelets, which limits its long-term application in aging-associated chronic diseases (Table 1) [129,130].

Senomorphic interventions: quenching the glial SASP

Unlike classical senolytics, senomorphic agents do not directly eliminate senescent glial cells but instead suppress SASP production and inflammatory signaling pathways. By inhibiting NF-κB, mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT), or p38 MAPK signaling, these agents reduce the secretion of proinflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and CCL2, thereby attenuating chronic inflammation [131].

1. mTOR and AMPK modulators (rapamycin and metformin)

Rapamycin reduces inflammatory signaling primarily through inhibition of mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), a key regulator of protein translation and cellular metabolism. In senescent cells, mTOR inhibition suppresses the translation of IL-1α, an upstream regulator of the SASP, thereby attenuating NF-κB-dependent expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-8 [132]. Similarly, rapamycin suppresses SASP production through inhibition of mTOR-dependent inflammatory signaling and has been shown to attenuate senescence-associated phenotypes in aged astrocytes, including reduced expression of p16INK4a, p21, and pro-inflammatory secretory factors [133].

Metformin exerts anti-inflammatory effects through multiple mechanisms, including activation of AMPK and suppression of IKK/NF-κB signaling [134]. These actions reduce the expression of inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), and matrix-remodeling enzymes, while simultaneously improving mitochondrial function and cellular metabolic homeostasis [135]. These effects are also observed in glial cells. Metformin suppresses SASP-associated inflammatory signaling through inhibition of IKK/NF-κB activation and has been reported to attenuate microglial and astrocytic inflammatory activation in experimental models of neurodegeneration and aging [136]. However, because senomorphic agents suppress SASP production without eliminating senescent cells, the underlying senescent cell burden remains in tissues.

2. Ruxolitinib

Persistent activation of the JAK/STAT pathway contributes to SASP production and chronic inflammation in senescent cells. Ruxolitinib, a selective JAK1/2 inhibitor, suppresses the expression of SASP factors including IL-6, IL-1β, MCP-1, and TNF-α, thereby reducing inflammatory signaling and tissue dysfunction. In aged mice, JAK inhibition alleviated age-associated frailty and systemic inflammation without directly eliminating senescent cells [137]. In addition, ruxolitinib has been reported to suppress microglial inflammatory activation and decrease neuroinflammatory cytokine production through inhibition of JAK/STAT- and NF-κB-related signaling pathways [138]. However, because ruxolitinib primarily suppresses SASP production rather than reducing senescent cell burden, sustained treatment may be required to maintain therapeutic efficacy, and long-term administration may increase the risk of immunosuppression [139].

3. Apigenin

Apigenin is a naturally occurring flavonoid that exhibits senomorphic-like effects by suppressing the harmful secretory activity of senescent cells rather than directly eliminating them. Mechanistically, apigenin reduces SASP production mainly through inhibition of NF-κB-dependent inflammatory signaling, leading to decreased expression of SASP-associated cytokines such as IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, and CXC motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8)/IL-8 [140,141]. In senescent cell models, apigenin has been shown to attenuate the paracrine effects of the senescent secretome, thereby reducing SASP-mediated inflammatory signaling in surrounding cells [140]. In addition, apigenin suppresses microglial inflammatory activation and reduces the production of pro-inflammatory mediators through inhibition of NF-κB signaling, suggesting that it may indirectly attenuate glia-associated SASP signaling and neuroinflammation during aging [142]. However, unlike established senomorphic agents such as rapamycin, metformin, or ruxolitinib, the senomorphic efficacy of apigenin remains largely based on preclinical studies and requires further validation in aging-related disease models (Table 1) [131].

Metabolic reprogramming of glial cells

Aging-associated glial dysfunction is closely linked to mitochondrial impairment, oxidative stress accumulation, altered nutrient sensing, and disruption of astrocyte-neuron metabolic coupling. Therefore, reprogramming glial metabolic homeostasis has emerged as another promising therapeutic approach. Oxidized nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)-boosting strategies, including nicotinamide riboside (NR) and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), are being investigated for their potential to restore mitochondrial metabolism and reduce age-associated neuroinflammatory remodeling through activation of sirtuin pathways [143,144].

Incretin-based therapies, particularly glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (e.g., semaglutide) and dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1 receptor agonists (e.g., tirzepatide), have transformed T2DM management by achieving substantial weight loss and glycemic control [145,146]. Importantly, these agents exert effects through both peripheral and central mechanisms, traversing the BBB to modulate appetite [147]. Emerging evidence suggests that GLP-1 receptor agonists can modulate microglial phenotypes and improve astrocyte-neuron metabolic interactions (e.g., lactate shuttling). Combining these therapies with senescence-targeting interventions may produce synergistic benefits in aging-associated metabolic diseases (Table 1) [148].

Translational hurdles and future directions

Despite the substantial therapeutic potential of targeting hypothalamic glial inflammaging, major translational challenges remain. First, reliable biomarkers capable of distinguishing truly senescent glial cells from reactive phenotypes are urgently needed [149]. Second, most mechanistic evidence derives from rodent studies, whereas direct evidence in the human hypothalamus remains limited because of tissue accessibility. In addition, therapeutic delivery into the hypothalamus is hindered by the BBB and the anatomical heterogeneity of hypothalamic nuclei [150]. Nevertheless, recent advances in single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, and CNS drug delivery systems (e.g., focused ultrasound or nanoparticle carriers) are expected to accelerate the development of precise therapeutic approaches.

Collectively, although current therapies effectively improve glycemic control, they remain insufficient to fully reverse disease progression. Future therapeutic strategies must adopt an integrated approach, targeting systemic metabolism in tandem with restoration of neuroendocrine homeostasis and reprogramming of hypothalamic aging.

DISCUSSION

Aging-associated hypothalamic inflammaging has emerged as a critical mechanistic interface linking brain aging to systemic metabolic dysfunction. Accumulating evidence indicates that chronic low-grade inflammatory remodeling within the hypothalamus is principally driven by the senescent reprogramming and dysfunction of glial cells, particularly microglia, astrocytes, and potentially tanycytes. These senescent glial populations lose their homeostatic and neuro-supportive functions while simultaneously acquiring a toxic SASP characterized by persistent secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, ROS, and matrix-remodeling factors. Through inflammatory signaling, the glial SASP amplifies local neuroinflammation and progressively disrupts the highly integrated neuroglial metabolic circuit.

Importantly, hypothalamic inflammaging may affect neuronal populations responsible for the bidirectional regulation of systemic glucose metabolism and energy balance. Chronic exposure to glia-derived inflammatory mediators impairs insulin and leptin signaling pathways through activation of NF-κB, JNK, and SOCS3-associated inflammatory mechanisms, ultimately leading to hypothalamic insulin resistance. In parallel, inflammatory remodeling alters neuronal excitability, synaptic stability, nutrient sensing, and neuroendocrine-autonomic outputs within metabolically important hypothalamic nuclei, including the ARC, VMH, and PVH. Consequently, these alterations contribute to dysregulated feeding behavior, impaired hepatic glucose control, reduced peripheral insulin sensitivity, and the clinical onset of T2DM.

Recent technological advances have substantially resolved the intricate cellular heterogeneity and spatiotemporal inflammatory remodeling in the aged brain, including the hypothalamus. These approaches further support the emerging concept that glial inflammaging is not merely a secondary consequence of disease but may act as a primary driver of disease initiation and progression.

Nevertheless, the translation of these preclinical findings into human disease remains challenging. Although neuroimaging studies, postmortem analyses, and cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers have demonstrated hypothalamic gliosis and neuroinflammatory changes in obesity and T2DM, direct evidence demonstrating hypothalamic glial senescence or SASP-mediated inflammaging in humans remains limited. Furthermore, most mechanistic insights have been obtained from experimental animal models, whereas longitudinal human studies integrating neuroimaging, postmortem tissue analyses, and molecular biomarkers are still lacking. Consequently, the causal contribution of hypothalamic glial inflammaging to the development of human T2DM has yet to be established.

Additional conceptual challenges also remain, including the precise definition of senescent glial states, their distinction from reactive inflammatory phenotypes, and the identification of reliable human biomarkers capable of specifically detecting glial senescence in vivo. Future studies integrating advanced imaging technologies, single-cell and spatial multi-omics, and human postmortem analyses will be essential to determine whether the mechanisms identified in experimental models are conserved in humans and to establish the translational potential of targeting glial inflammaging for the prevention and treatment of age-associated metabolic diseases.

Therapeutically, growing evidence suggests that targeting hypothalamic glial inflammaging may provide novel opportunities for the treatment of aging-associated metabolic disorders. Current antidiabetic agents such as metformin and semaglutide have demonstrated anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects within the hypothalamus beyond their classical metabolic actions. In addition, emerging strategies, including senolytic therapies, SASP suppression, and glial metabolic reprogramming, represent promising approaches for restoring hypothalamic homeostasis. However, most evidence currently derives from experimental animal models, and translation into human clinical applications remains limited.

Future studies should therefore focus on clarifying the molecular mechanisms governing glial senescence, glia-neuron interactions, and hypothalamic inflammatory circuit remodeling during aging. The identification of reliable biomarkers and the development of glia-specific therapeutic interventions (e.g., targeted BBB-penetrant delivery systems) will be essential for advancing translational applications. Collectively, the evidence discussed in this review supports the concept that hypothalamic glial inflammaging represents a potentially central driver of aging-associated insulin resistance and T2DM, highlighting glial-targeted interventions as a promising future therapeutic strategy for reprogramming metabolic disease.
