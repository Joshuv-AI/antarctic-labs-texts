Wave-induced drift in third-order deep–water theory
Raphael Stuhlmeier

Abstract
The goal of this work is to investigate particle motions beneath unidirectional, deep-water waves up to the third-order in nonlinearity. A particular focus is on the approximation known as Stokes drift, and how it relates to the particle kinematics as computed directly from the particle trajectory mapping. The reduced Hamiltonian formulation of Zakharov and Krasitskii serves as a convenient tool to separate the effects of weak nonlinearity, in particular the appearance of bound harmonics and the mutual corrections to the wave frequencies. By numerical integration of the particle trajectory mappings we are able to compute motions and resulting drift for sea-states with one, two and several harmonics. Overall, we find the linear theory yields the largest forward drift close to the surface, followed by second and third order. At greater depth this trend reverses, with linear theory yielding the smallest forward drift. Incorporating difference harmonic terms into the classical Stokes drift formulation is found to yield improved agreement with the drift obtained from nonlinear wave theories, particularly at greater depth.



1
Introduction

Waves on the surface of water are a continuing source of fascination, as well as posing an important scientific and engineering challenge, with impacts on such diverse areas as offshore renewables and marine ecology. While we can observe the myriad forms of waves on the water surface, these shapes are only manifestations of what is going on below the surface: a transport of energy and, to a lesser extent, the movement of water particles themselves. Leonardo da Vinci already observed
(
Mossa 2021
)
that the wave outruns the water, and early mathematical analyses of wave kinematics seemed to confirm this idea. Work in the early 19th century by Green showed that particle paths in linear deep water waves are approximately circular – in seeming agreement with the exact, deep-water solution derived by Gerstner – and the corresponding finite depth particle paths were shown to be approximately elliptical by Airy
(
Craik 2004
)
.

Nonlinear wave theories began to be explored by Russell, Kelland, and most prominently, Stokes, starting in earnest in the 1840s. It was the work of Stokes that first suggested that particle trajectories in periodic waves in deep water might not be closed, and gave rise to the idea of wave-induced motion now known as Stokes drift. In the intervening years the kinematics of nonlinear waves has received significant attention, and the concept of wave-induced drift has taken a prominent role in our understanding of transport in the ocean. Its consequences are still being explored in the movement of floating microplastics
(
van Sebille et al. 2020
)
and water-borne bacteria
(
Ge et al. 2012
)
, and we are coming to develop a fuller picture of particle trajectories in nonlinear wave equations
(
Carter et al. 2020
;
Curtis et al. 2018
;
Ige & Kalisch 2024
)
.

Nevertheless, much remains unknown from both an experimental and theoretical perspective. Experiments are notoriously difficult, owing to the challenge of comparing a closed flume with the situation in the (open) ocean. Recent years have seen significant progress, from flume experiments on monochromatic wave trains
(
Umeyama 2012
)
to more recent investigations of particle kinematics for groups and complex flows
(
Bjørnestad et al. 2021
;
van den Bremer et al. 2019
)
.

The aim of this work is to explore the theoretical particle trajectories – and drift – associated with nonlinear potential flow theories in infinite water depth. While there are recent mathematical results for the full, nonlinear problem showing that no closed particle paths exist in steady, periodic wave trains
(
Constantin 2006
;
Henry 2006
;
Okamoto & Shoji 2012
)
, these results are not quantitative and are difficult to generalise to nonsteady problems. We will take a ‘weakly nonlinear’ approach, and our starting point will be the Stokes drift formulation which arises in linear (1st order) water wave theory. While a rigorous proof that linear, steady, periodic water waves exhibit a forward drift in the direction of wave propagation
(
Constantin & Villari 2008
;
Constantin et al. 2008
)
was recently found, the approximate results due to Stokes form the basis of most practical implementations of drift in forecasting codes.

Stokes’ original results for a single wave train were generalised to a spectrum of waves by
Kenyon 1969
, and the resulting formulation has been extensively explored for different spectral shapes
(
Webb & Fox-Kemper 2011
)
, for its sensitivity to the high-frequency spectral tail
(
Lenain & Pizzo 2020
)
, and new profiles have been developed for practical implementation
(
Breivik et al. 2016
)
. Many more references can be found in the recent review by
van den Bremer & Breivik 2018
.

As the Stokes drift formulation comes about through a simplification of the linear water-wave theory, the differences between it and the linear theory are of interest. This also raises a natural question: what if we progress from linear theory to a second or third–order theory? We expect the higher-order theories to be more accurate, and to include important effects for steeper waves – such as the appearance of additional harmonics – which may have a bearing on comparisons with the Stokes drift. Indeed, these theories may suggest improvements to the Stokes drift derived from linear theory. However, with the exception of recent work of van den Bremer and Taylor
(
van den Bremer & Taylor 2016
)
focusing on wave groups it is difficult to find such comparisons in the literature.

For a successful comparison we need some way to compute the fluid velocity field
\mathbf{u}
, which drives the particle kinematics through the particle trajectory mapping
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbf{u}.
We need a methodology that can deal with several Fourier modes, to simulate irregular seas, and which is accurate to a desired order of nonlinearity. Several options exist, including the use of traditional perturbation theory to obtain a solution to the water wave problem, which has been extended to third-order by
Madsen & Fuhrman 2012
. An alternative – which we employ – is to use the equivalent Hamiltonian expansion, which is due to
Zakharov 1968
and for which the third-order solution has been explored by
Zhang & Chen 1999
and
Gao et al. 2021
.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: in Section
2
we introduce the water wave problem and corresponding Hamiltonian. We give more details about the expansion that allows us to extract linear, second, and third-order terms in Section
2.1
, discuss the neglect of amplitude evolution for our particle kinematics calculations in Section
2.2
, and finally show how the free surface and potential are obtained to each order in Section
2.3
. In Section
3
we consider monochromatic waves, which is the natural setting to introduce Stokes drift, and look at the consequences of higher-order theories on particle trajectories. Section
4
is devoted to bichromatic waves. These are the simplest type of non-steady wave-field, but because only two harmonics are involved the linear superposition is always periodic. Finally, Section
5
considers wave groups and random waves involving multiple lowest-order harmonics. Conclusions are presented in Section
6
. Appendix
A
adds some comments on the non-uniqueness of the monochromatic Stokes wave solution at third-order, while Appendix
B
gives expressions for some coefficients appearing in Section
2.3
.

2
The water wave problem and Hamiltonian formulation

The inviscid, irrotational water wave problem, written in terms of a fluid velocity potential
\phi
and free surface
\zeta,
is

\displaystyle\Delta\phi=0\text{ on }-h<z<\zeta,

(1a)

\displaystyle\zeta_{t}+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{x}\zeta=\phi_{z}\text{ on }z=\zeta,

(1b)

\displaystyle\phi_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\phi)^{2}+g\zeta=0\text{ on }z=\zeta,

(1c)

\displaystyle\phi_{z}=0\text{ on }z=-h.

(1d)

The horizontal (
x,y
) gradient is denoted
\nabla_{x}
for clarity. The advantage of this formulation is that all nonlinearities have been moved to the boundary conditions. We also note that, provided the water is deeper than about half a typical wavelength, the still-water depth
h
can be taken to be infinite. This procedure, using

\phi_{z}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }z\rightarrow\infty

(
1d
’)

in place of (
1d
) simplifies calculations considerably. Despite the restriction to irrotational flow and the neglect of compressibility and viscosity, these equations contain a huge range of interesting physics, including many types of wave phenomena.

During the late 1960s, efforts to find a Hamiltonian formulation of the water wave problem (
1a
)–(
1d
) came to fruition in the independent work of
Zakharov 1968
,
Miles 1977
and
Broer 1974
, who found the Hamiltonian

H=\int\int_{-h}^{\zeta}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}dz+\frac{1}{2}g\zeta^{2}dx,

(2)

which represents the total energy of the fluid. The domain of integration in
x
can in practice depend on the specifics of the problem, and the lower boundary of the
z
-integral can be chosen as
-\infty
if (
’
‣
2
) is used in place of (
1d
).

The canonical variables for the Hamiltonian formulation are defined at the free surface. They are
\zeta(x,t)
and
\psi(x,t)=\phi(x,\zeta(x,t),t),
and the canonical equations are thus

\frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial t}=\frac{\delta H}{\delta\psi},\quad\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{\delta H}{\delta\zeta}.

(3)

These equations are equivalent to the surface boundary conditions (
1b
), (
1c
), and details of the calculation can be understood most easily in
Broer 1974
. Additional background can also be found in the recent review by
Stuhlmeier 2024
.

2.1
Asymptotic analysis and the water wave problem

The water wave problem, whether written as a system of partial differential equations and boundary conditions or using the Hamiltonian reformulation remains mathematically very complex. While the mathematical existence of periodic and solitary wave solutions has been established in ever more general settings (for example, the books by
Constantin 2012
or
Lannes 2013
offer a relatively recent overview of mathematical progress), much of what is known about qualitative and quantitative properties of ocean waves rests on an analysis of simplified equations.

These simplified equations are usually obtained through so-called asymptotic analysis or perturbation theory – a procedure of nondimensionalisation and scaling the equations, followed by the identification of small dimensionless parameters, and an asymptotic expansion of the variables in terms of the small parameters selected. In the case of water waves the most common small parameter is the wave steepness
\epsilon,
written either as the wave amplitude divided by the wavelength or multiplied by the wavenumber. A very thorough discussion of asymptotic analysis as applied to water waves can be found in the textbook by
Johnson 1997
.

Instead of expanding the water wave equations (
1a
)–(
1d
) in this manner (see
Madsen & Fuhrman 2012
for a detailed, relevant account), it is also possible to expand the Hamiltonian directly. This procedure was carried out by
Zakharov 1968
and, with more detail,
Krasitskii 1994
, and it has the advantage of enforcing structure that might otherwise be lost, such as that resulting equations conserve energy.

The first step in this procedure is to write the canonical equations in terms of the Fourier transformed variables
\hat{\zeta}(k)
and
\hat{\phi}(k),
as

\frac{\partial\hat{\zeta}}{\partial t}=\frac{\delta H}{\delta\hat{\psi}^{*}},\quad\frac{\partial\hat{\psi}}{\partial t}=-\frac{\delta H}{\delta\hat{\zeta}^{*}},

with
*
denoting the complex conjugate. Then one defines a new pair of canonical variables
a(k)
and
ia^{*}(k)
related to
\psi
and
\zeta
via Fourier transforms as

\displaystyle\zeta(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\left(\frac{q(k)}{2\omega(k)}\right)^{1/2}\left[a(k)+a^{*}(-k)\right]e^{ikx}dk,

(4)

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\frac{-i}{2\pi}\int\left(\frac{\omega(k)}{2q(k)}\right)^{1/2}\left[a(k)-a^{*}(-k)\right]e^{ikx}dk,

(5)

where

q(k)=|k|\tanh(|k|h),

(6)

\omega=\sqrt{gq(k)}
is the frequency (in rad/s), and
g
is the constant acceleration of gravity (taken to be 9.8 m/s
2
in computations). As we will be interested in deep-water waves (
h\rightarrow\infty)
, we note that the dispersion relation simplifies to

\omega({k})^{2}=g|{k}|.

(7)

We will use this in all the computations that follow.

The subsequent steps in the expansion are somewhat lengthy, and are given by
Krasitskii 1994
. At this stage it is important to emphasise only one key point: at lowest order the problem is linear, so the superposition principle holds. This means that the Fourier amplitudes – which correspond essentially to the magnitudes of the terms
a(k)
introduced in (
4
)–(
5
) – do not change with time. If gravity is the sole restoring force, the surprising fact is that the Fourier amplitudes do not change with time at the next order, either. Only at third-order in nonlinearity, where resonance between quartets of gravity waves becomes possible, do we obtain an evolution equation. This equation gives the slow evolution of the complex amplitude
b_{0}=b(k_{0},t),

i\frac{\partial b_{0}}{\partial t}=\omega_{0}b_{0}+\int\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{0123}b_{1}^{*}b_{2}b_{3}\delta_{0+1-2-3}dk_{123},

(8)

and is known as the Zakharov equation, with a kernel term
\tilde{V}^{(2)}
given in
(
Krasitskii 1994
, (3.5))
. We have introduced subscript notation to denote wavenumbers, so that, for example, the delta-function
\delta_{0+1-2-3}
is a shorthand for
\delta(k_{0}+k_{1}-k_{2}-k_{3}).
In principle
k_{i}
can be either a scalar or a vector wavenumber, although our computations will deal only with the scalar case for simplicity.

If
b
is known then the canonical variable
a
can be recovered via

\displaystyle a_{0}=b_{0}

\displaystyle+\int A^{(1)}_{012}b_{1}b_{2}\delta_{0-1-2}dk_{12}+\int A^{(2)}_{012}b_{1}^{*}b_{2}\delta_{0+1-2}dk_{12}+\int A^{(3)}_{012}b_{1}^{*}b_{2}^{*}\delta_{0+1+2}dk_{12}

\displaystyle+\int B^{(1)}_{0123}b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}\delta_{0-1-2-3}dk_{123}+\int B^{(2)}_{0123}b_{1}^{*}b_{2}b_{3}\delta_{0+1-2-3}dk_{123}

\displaystyle+\int B^{(3)}_{0123}b_{1}^{*}b_{2}^{*}b_{3}\delta_{0+1+2-3}dk_{123}+\int B^{(4)}_{0123}b_{1}^{*}b_{2}^{*}b_{3}^{*}\delta_{0+1+2+3}dk_{123}+\ldots

(9)

where we have written terms up to third order only. This expression is given in
(
Krasitskii 1994
, (2.17))
, as are the relevant interaction kernels
A^{(i)},\,B^{(i)}
. Finally, once
a
is known it is possible to recover
\zeta
and
\psi
up to the desired order by means of (
4
)–(
5
).

2.2
Constant magnitude approximation

The procedure outlined above would seem to suggest that we first need to solve the complicated integro-differential Zakharov equation (
8
). In fact, for certain situations of interest we can circumvent this step. The key insight is that the evolution described by the Zakharov equation occurs on the slow timescale
\epsilon^{-2}T
, where
\epsilon\approx 0.1
is a typical wave steepness and
T
is a typical wave period (e.g.
T\approx 10
s in the ocean). Therefore, for processes taking place on time-scales faster than 100 wave periods, it may be appropriate to consider the complex amplitudes to be constant.

This is indeed the approach we will adopt. There is, however, one important addition: for certain wavenumber combinations the Zakharov equation (
8
) can be solved explicitly. In particular, for all symmetric quartets which satisfy
k_{0}+k_{0}=k_{0}+k_{0}
or
k_{0}+k_{1}=k_{0}+k_{1}
the equation reduces to a system of one or two ordinary differential equations with constant-amplitude solutions, as shown in
(
Mei et al. 2018
, Ch. 14)
. Such solutions represent nonlinear corrections to the frequency of the waves, and were first found by Stokes for a single wave mode. For arbitrarily many modes, or a continuous spectrum, it is possible to write these corrections in a compact form
(
Stuhlmeier & Stiassnie 2019
)
.

Incorporating these corrections the complex amplitudes
b_{i}(t)
can be written in an approximation up to third-order as

b_{i}(t)=|b_{i}(0)|\exp\left(-it\left[\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{iiii}|b_{i}(0)|^{2}+2\sum_{j\neq i}\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{ijij}|b_{j}(0)|^{2}\right]\right),

where the small, quadratic terms in the argument reduce to
\omega a^{2}k^{2}/2
, the well-known Stokes’ correction, when only a single wave-mode is present. This form now contains all the mutual dispersion corrections entailed by the cubically nonlinear water wave theory. It has been demonstrated that taking these corrections into account allows one to predict the short-time propagation of laboratory waves with high accuracy without the need to solve for the evolution of the slow-time variables
(
Stuhlmeier & Stiassnie 2021
;
Galvagno et al. 2021
)
, showing that the constant magnitude approximation is well-justified for short times. Of course, under specific circumstances – for example with a sufficiently narrow spectrum, or close to modulation instability – we may have significant energy transfer
(
Andrade & Stuhlmeier 2023
)
. However, given our focus on particle paths (with a characteristic time on the order of one period) the amplitude evolution is negligible (see also the discussion in
Stuhlmeier & Stiassnie 2019
and Appendix A therein).

If we sum up the consequences of weak-nonlinearity for surface gravity waves in deep water as

1.

dispersion corrections,

2.

changes to wave shape (bound harmonics),

3.

energy exchange between modes,

we will account for
1
–
2
while neglecting the slow effects
3
.

2.3
Recovery of the third-order solution

The Hamiltonian formulation makes clear how the free surface and potential can be recovered from the canonical variables
a(k)
and
a^{*}(k)
up to a given order. In practice, discrete wavenumbers are considered, which means that the integrals in (
9
) can be simplified by the substitution

b(k)=\sum_{i}B_{i}\delta(k-k_{i}).

(10)

Here
B_{i}=B(k_{i},t)
is unknown in principle, and in practice comes from a constant magnitude approximation to (
8
) as discussed in Section
2.2
.

Because the free surface (
4
) is recovered directly from substitution of (
9
), it is clear that we can distinguish linear, quadratic and cubic terms in
\zeta
based on the respective terms in (
9
). The same is not true of
\phi
, as we shall see below.
Taking the inverse Fourier transform yields

\zeta_{1}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{i}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{i}}{2g}}A_{i}\left(e^{i\xi_{i}}+e^{-i\xi_{i}}\right).

(11)

Here we have substituted
B_{i}=A_{i}e^{-i\varphi_{i}t}
with amplitude
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and phase
\varphi_{i}(t),
and employ the compact notation
\xi_{i}=k_{i}x-\Omega_{i}t+\theta_{i}.
Clearly this yields the expected sinusoidal free surface elevation upon defining the amplitude

\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{i}}{2g}}A_{i}=a_{i}.

The nonlinear corrected frequency is given by

\Omega_{i}=\omega_{i}+\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{iiii}|A_{i}|^{2}+2\sum_{j\neq i}\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{ijij}|A_{j}|^{2},

(12)

and for unidirectional waves in infinite depth – which will be our focus – we can simplify the kernels to

\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{iiii}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}k_{i}^{3}\text{ and }\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{ijij}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}k_{i}k_{j}\min(k_{i},k_{j}).

The quadratic components of the free surface elevation can be obtained in analogous manner, with slightly more in the way of algebra required to resolve all delta-functions. We find

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{i,j}A_{i}A_{j}\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{i+j}}{2g}}\left(A^{(1)}_{i+j,i,j}+A^{(3)}_{-i-j,i,j}\right)\left[e^{i(\xi_{i}+\xi_{j})}+e^{-i(\xi_{i}+\xi_{j})}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{i-j}}{2g}}A^{(2)}_{j-i,i,j}\left[e^{i(\xi_{j}-\xi_{i})}+e^{i(\xi_{i}-\xi_{j})}\right]\right\}.

(13)

The shorthand notation
\omega_{i\pm j}
is used to denote
\omega(k_{i}\pm k_{j})=\sqrt{g|k_{i}\pm k_{j}|}.

We can write the cubic components of the free surface elevation as

\displaystyle\zeta_{3}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{i,j,k}A_{i}A_{j}A_{k}\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{i+j+k}}{2g}}\left(B^{(1)}_{i+j+k,i,j,k}+B^{(4)}_{-i-j-k,i,j,k}\right)\left[e^{i(\xi_{i}+\xi_{j}+\xi_{k})}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.+e^{-i(\xi_{i}+\xi_{j}+\xi_{k})}\right]+\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{i-j-k}}{2g}}B^{(2)}_{j+k-i,i,j,k}\left[e^{i(\xi_{j}+\xi_{k}-\xi_{i})}\right.\left.\left.+e^{i(\xi_{i}-\xi_{j}-\xi_{k})}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{i+j-k}}{2g}}B^{(3)}_{k-i-j,i,j,k}\left[e^{i(\xi_{k}-\xi_{i}-\xi_{j})}+e^{i(\xi_{i}+\xi_{j}-\xi_{k})}\right]\right\}.

(14)

The velocity potential at the free surface,
\psi
, is obtained from (
5
) via an identical procedure.

The corrections contained in the second-order and third-order free surface elevation essentially change the wave shape. The resulting harmonics of the form
\exp(i(\xi_{i}\pm\xi_{j}))
behave as waves with wavenumber
k_{i}\pm k_{j}
and frequency
\Omega_{i}\pm\Omega_{j},
but because these sums and differences do not satisfy the dispersion relation they are referred to as “bound waves”, in contrast to the free waves of (

2.3.1
Recovery of the bulk potential

The reformulation of the problem in surface variables is very convenient from many perspectives, but is something of a disadvantage when we wish to recover information about the bulk fluid below the free surface. To obtain this we start with the general solution of the Laplace equation which decays for infinite depth

\phi(x,z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\phi(k)e^{kz}e^{ikx}dk,\quad\phi(k)=\phi^{*}(-k),

(15)

the exact analogue of
(
Krasitskii 1994
, (4.1))
.

We relate the bulk potential
\phi
and the surface trace
\psi
to one another in terms of an expansion. Taking the problem in infinite depth, we need to solve

\displaystyle\Delta\phi=0,

(16)

\displaystyle\phi=\psi\text{ on }z=\zeta,

(17)

\displaystyle\phi_{z}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }z\rightarrow-\infty,

(18)

which we do by assuming the free surface elevation is small (
\zeta\rightarrow\epsilon\zeta
), and then inserting this expansion into the boundary condition (
17
), so that

\phi(\zeta)=\phi(0)+\epsilon\zeta\phi_{z}(0)+\frac{\epsilon^{2}\zeta^{2}}{2}\phi_{z}z(0)+\ldots.

This gives rise to a hierarchy of problems in physical space, whose successive solution yields

\hat{\phi}(k,z,t)=e^{|k|z}\left[\hat{\psi}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\hat{\zeta}_{1}\hat{\psi}_{2}|k_{2}|\delta_{0-1-2}dk_{12}-\int D_{0123}\hat{\psi}_{1}\hat{\zeta}_{2}\hat{\zeta}_{3}\delta_{0-1-2-3}dk_{123}+\ldots\right]

(19)

with

D_{0123}=-\frac{1}{4}|k_{1}|\left(2|k_{1}|-|k_{1}+k_{2}|-|k_{1}+k_{3}|-|k_{0}-k_{3}|-|k_{0}-k_{2}|\right).

(20)

With
\hat{\psi}
and
\hat{\zeta}
known from the formulation in (
4
)–(
5
) we can now recover the bulk potential at each order desired. This procedure is also detailed in
(
Janssen 2004
, below (4.7))
,
(
Krasitskii 1994
, p.15)
and
(
Zakharov 1968
, (1.8))
, although care should be taken in the symmetrisation of (
20
).

The linear contribution consists simply of the first term in (
19
)

\phi_{1}(x,z,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{|k|z}e^{ikx}\hat{\psi}_{1}dk=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{i}A_{i}e^{|k_{i}|z}\sqrt{\frac{g}{2\omega_{i}}}\sin(\xi_{i}).

(21)

The quadratic contribution is

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(x,z,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}A_{i}A_{j}\left[\mathcal{C}^{(2)}_{i+j}\sin(\xi_{i}+\xi_{j})e^{|k_{i}+k_{j}|z}+\mathcal{C}^{(2)}_{i-j}\sin(\xi_{i}-\xi_{j})e^{|k_{i}-k_{j}|z}\right]

where we need to employ the linear and quadratic parts of
\hat{\psi}
and
\hat{\zeta}
(and products thereof) in the formulation. The coefficients
\mathcal{C}^{(2)}
are given in Appendix
B
.
Up to second order, for unidirectional waves in deep water the potential reduces to the simple expression

\phi=\sum_{j}\frac{a_{j}g}{\omega_{j}}e^{|k_{j}|z}\sin(k_{j}x-\omega_{j}t)-\sum_{i>j}\omega_{i}a_{i}a_{j}\sin(\xi_{i}-\xi_{j})e^{|k_{i}-k_{j}|z}.

(22)

This follows also from
Dalzell 1999
.

The cubic contribution to the bulk potential is

\displaystyle\phi_{3}(x,z,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{ijk}A_{i}A_{j}A_{k}\left\{\mathcal{C}^{(3)}_{i+j+k}\sin(\xi_{i}+\xi_{j}+\xi_{k})e^{|k_{i}+k_{j}+k_{k}|z}\right.

\displaystyle+\mathcal{C}^{(3)}_{i-j-k}\sin(\xi_{i}-\xi_{j}-\xi_{k})e^{|k_{i}-k_{j}-k_{k}|z}\left.+\mathcal{C}^{(3)}_{i+j-k}\sin(\xi_{i}+\xi_{j}-\xi_{k})e^{|k_{i}+k_{j}-k_{k}|z}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\mathcal{C}^{(3)}_{i-j+k}\sin(\xi_{i}-\xi_{j}+\xi_{k})e^{|k_{i}-k_{j}+k_{k}|z}\right\}.

(23)

The coefficients
\mathcal{C}^{(3)}
are given in Appendix
B
.
Unfortunately efforts to find a compact simplification the third order have failed except in the case of a single monochromatic wave, where the analytical expression

\phi_{3}=-\frac{a^{3}\omega k}{4}e^{kz}\sin\xi

is recovered (see
Gao et al. 2021
and Appendix
A
).

3
Particle kinematics for monochromatic waves

We have developed above a general formulation of the third-order water wave problem. However, this formulation in terms of a free surface elevation
\zeta
and a velocity potential
\phi
needs to be connected to the kinematics of fluid particles. The most transparent starting point is with monochromatic waves – i.e. initial conditions for which the linear theory is described by a single Fourier mode
k_{i}.
Of course, the lengthy formulation of Section
2.3
is not strictly necessary, since monochromatic solutions to third-order can be readily found in the literature, e.g.
Wehausen & Laitone 1960
. Consequently, we begin by reviewing some familiar ideas.

3.1
Linear theory

Linear deep-water theory poses the water wave problem on the half-plane below
z=0.
Solving the Laplace equation then yields the well-known solution in deep water

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}=a\cos(\xi),\quad\phi_{1}=\frac{a\omega}{k}e^{kz}\sin(\xi),

(24)

where
\xi=kx-\omega t
and

\omega^{2}=g|k|

(25)

is the linear dispersion relation. Here
z\leq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}.

The particle trajectory mapping is the system of ordinary differential equations

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(t)=\nabla\phi(x,z,t).

(26)

It is worth noting that this system has only a tenuous connection to the linear (or weakly nonlinear) theory, but rather represents the link between Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions of the fluid motion. For example, the idea that the free surface is always composed of the same fluid particles is only nearly satisfied (under the assumption of waves of small amplitude), so that the particle trajectory mapping only nearly describes the dynamics of particles on the surface (or any streamline).

Given that we are dealing with linear theory, it seems simple enough to solve the system (
26
). It is an unwelcome surprise that inserting the linear potential yields a nonlinear system of differential equations

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(t)=a\omega e^{kz}\cos(\xi),

(27)

\displaystyle z^{\prime}(t)=a\omega e^{kz}\sin(\xi).

(28)

While this system cannot be solved analytically it can be shown rigorously that its particle trajectories are not closed
(
Constantin & Villari 2008
)
.

In lieu of an analytical treatment, two alternatives remain: approximation or numerical solution. The former approach is found in most textbooks on water waves, and begins with a Taylor expansion of the fluid velocity field
(u,w)
as

u(x,z,t)=\underbrace{u(x_{0},z_{0},t)}_{(i)}+\underbrace{(x-x_{0})\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}|_{(x_{0},z_{0})}+(z-z_{0})\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}|_{(x_{0},z_{0})}}_{(ii)}+\ldots,

(29)

and similarly for
w.
Keeping only the lowest order terms (i) in the Taylor expansion yields the explicit system

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(t)=a\omega e^{kz_{0}}\cos(\xi_{0}),

(30)

\displaystyle z^{\prime}(t)=a\omega e^{kz_{0}}\sin(\xi_{0}),

(31)

with
\xi_{0}=kx_{0}-\omega t,
which yields the circular particle trajectories first found by Green in 1839
(
Craik 2004
)
. Inserting the solution so obtained into the particle trajectory ODEs with terms
(ii)
in (
29
) retained yields another explicit system

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=a\omega e^{kz_{0}}\cos(\xi_{0})-a^{2}k\omega e^{2kz_{0}}\cos(\omega t)+a^{2}k\omega e^{2kz_{0}},

(32)

\displaystyle z^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=a\omega e^{kz_{0}}\sin(\xi_{0})+a^{2}k\omega e^{2kz_{0}}\sin(\omega t).

(33)

Integration of this system yields the familiar result that particles move forward with a Stokes drift velocity

u_{S}=a^{2}k\omega e^{2kz_{0}}.

(34)

This Stokes drift is nominally a second order – and therefore nonlinear – quantity in the small wave amplitude
a
, but is derived from linear theory (
24
). This important reminder can also be found in many textbooks, such as
Dean & Dalrymple 1991
.

3.2
Nonlinear theory

It is well-known that the deep water potential at third order is formally identical to the first order potential given in (
24
) (though see Appendix
A
). Thus, while higher harmonic terms appear in the free surface elevation, the only change to the potential at third order is the nonlinear frequency correction, which means that the characteristic coordinate is now
\xi=kx-\Omega t
for

\Omega=\omega(k)\left(1+\frac{a^{2}k^{2}}{2}\right).

(35)

In particular, this means that the speed at which each particle moves is faster by an amount of order
\epsilon^{2},
which impacts the particle trajectories as shown in Figure

.

For monochromatic waves the procedure used to derive (

{u_{S}}=\frac{a^{2}\omega^{2}ke^{2kz_{0}}}{\Omega}=\frac{2a^{2}\omega ke^{2kz_{0}}}{2+a^{2}k^{2}}.

(36)

Because
\Omega>\omega
it is clear that the drift velocity (

\epsilon

This counterintuitive result – that despite nonlinear waves travelling faster their drift velocity is slower – is due to the fact that the particle trajectories themselves “shrink” with increasing
\epsilon
. To lowest order in the Taylor series expansion, where deep-water trajectories are approximately circular, their radius becomes
\exp(kz_{0})a\omega/\Omega.

For the cases considered in Figure

36

34
)) and a 5 % reduction in the averaged Lagrangian drift obtained from integrating the particle trajectory mapping with the third-order potential inserted. We will encounter this unexpected consequence of nonlinear theory again in our discussion of more complex wave fields below.

4
Bichromatic waves

In the previous section we considered monochromatic waves. The term “monochromatic” refers to the fact that a single mode is assumed in the lowest order problem. As we have seen, higher harmonics are excited at each order of the perturbation expansion, so that the spectrum does not, in fact, remain monochromatic. Far from being a purely mathematical phenomenon, this harmonic generation is observed every time a wavemaker is excited in a flume, and remains an active area of experimental research
(
Khait & Shemer 2019
)
. Consequently, when we speak of bichromatic waves, this means that two modes
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are assumed in the lowest order problem. The second order solution then contains sum and difference terms of any two of these, while the third order contains sum and difference terms of any three, with an attendant increase in complexity. Up to second order the solution is contained in the work of
Dalzell 1999
, while the third order solution for bichromatic waves appears first in the work of
Madsen & Fuhman 2006
. We make use of the equivalent formulation of Section
2.3
.

4.1
Linear theory

We start with the simplest scenario, where we retain only linear terms in the bichromatic velocity potential

\displaystyle\phi(x,z,t)=\frac{a_{1}g}{\omega_{1}}e^{k_{1}z}\sin(\xi_{1})+\frac{a_{2}g}{\omega_{2}}e^{k_{2}z}\sin(\xi_{2}).

(37)

To understand what this means for particle kinematics, we are again confronted with two options: solve the particle trajectory mapping
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(t)=\nabla\phi
numerically or approximate it in the same fashion as the monochromatic Stokes’ drift in Section
3
.

Exactly as for monochromatic waves, if the lowest order terms in a Taylor series expansion of (
37

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(t)=a_{1}\omega_{1}e^{k_{1}z_{0}}\cos(k_{1}x_{0}-\omega_{1}t)+a_{2}\omega_{2}e^{k_{2}z_{0}}\cos(k_{2}x_{0}-\omega_{2}t),

(38)

\displaystyle z^{\prime}(t)=a_{1}\omega_{1}e^{k_{1}z_{0}}\sin(k_{1}x_{0}-\omega_{1}t)+a_{2}\omega_{2}e^{k_{2}z_{0}}\sin(k_{2}x_{0}-\omega_{2}t),

(39)

29

\mathbf{x}
and the velocity field
\nabla\phi
produce terms involving
k_{1}\pm k_{2}
the time dependence of the equations is, of course, explicit.
Upon integration the secular terms lead immediately to the drift velocity

(40)

which is simply the sum of the Stokes’ drifts for modes
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
over a time t. This simple idea, that the Stokes drift of a linear sum of waves is the sum of the Stokes drifts of the waves underlies the successful application of Stokes drift to spectra as pioneered by
Kenyon 1969

4.2.1
Second order

If linear bichromatic waves are very simple, the second order expression obtained from (
22
) is not much more complicated:

\displaystyle\phi(x,z,t)=\frac{a_{1}g}{\omega_{1}}e^{k_{1}z}\sin(\xi_{1})+\frac{a_{2}g}{\omega_{2}}e^{k_{2}z}\sin(\xi_{2})-\omega_{1}a_{1}a_{2}e^{(k_{1}-k_{2})z}\sin(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}),

(41)

where
k_{1}>k_{2},
and the sum harmonic vanishes for unidirectional deep water waves (the same is not true of the free surface elevation, whose prominent sum harmonics can be seen in Figure

The second order solution (
41

3.1

(u,w)

(x_{0},z_{0})

40

k_{1}>k_{2}
).

4.2.2
Difference harmonics and particle drift

Why do we observe this reversal, where linear theory predicts a larger drift near the surface, but second order theory predicts a larger drift at depth? The answer lies in the structure of the difference harmonic terms, and as a prelude to our work on wave groups it is expedient to investigate their role in bichromatic seas.

The first consideration should be of the velocity field which drives the particle trajectory mapping. We consider the waves studied in Figure

4.2.3
Third order

A confluence of the effects of dispersion correction and harmonic generation occurs for the first time at third order. For the frequency correction appearing there we have the notably asymmetric equations

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}

5
Multichromatic waves

When more than two Fourier modes are necessary to describe the linear problem, we call such a configuration multichromatic. The second order contains sums and differences of any two modes, while the third order contains sums and differences of any three. This means that significant algebra can be involved in calculating higher-order solutions.

5.1
Focused linear wave groups

In treating waves with an increasing number of linear harmonic constituents we also have a plethora of parameters that can be tuned. One case of interest, from both an experimental and theoretical standpoint is that of a (focused) wave group. Linear focusing is quite straightforward: if our free surface is a superposition of sinusoids as per (
24

\zeta_{1}=\sum_{i}a_{i}\cos(k_{i}(x-x_{0})-\omega_{i}(t-t_{0}))

(x_{0},t_{0})

a_{i}.
One possible choice is a Gaussian amplitude spectrum of the form

a_{i}=A\exp\left(-\frac{(k_{i}-k_{p})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

(45)

5.3
Random waves

Random waves are the closest mathematical idealisation to the waves we might typically find in the ocean: the phases of one Fourier component are not correlated with the next, and the resulting pattern – while occasionally exhibiting a distinct group – is highly irregular. Random phases at lowest order also means that sum and difference phases at higher orders are random, due to a neglect of slow phase-coherence effects associated with nonlinear coupling
(
Andrade & Stuhlmeier 2023
)
.

Because we are interested in comparing different computations of drift, we also derive a generalised version of the Stokes drift (
42
) with second order difference harmonics suitable for arbitrarily many modes. The procedure is familiar: Taylor expand the velocity field
(u,w)
from (
22
) about an initial position
(x_{0},z_{0})
, and keep the lowest order terms. Solve for the particle trajectories by integrating that expanded velocity field in time. Then substitute the solution thus obtained into the second order terms in the Taylor expansion, and integrate the result to find the corrected particle trajectories. We obtain

u_{S}=\underbrace{\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}\omega_{j}k_{j}e^{2k_{j}z_{0}}}_{\text{(I)}}+\underbrace{\sum_{k_{i}>k_{j}}\frac{\omega_{i}^{2}a_{i}^{2}a_{j}^{2}(k_{i}-k_{j})^{3}}{\omega_{i}-\omega_{j}}e^{2(k_{i}-k_{j})z_{0}}}_{\text{(II)}}.

(46)

Here we recognise term (I) as simply the sum of the individual Stokes drifts, and term (II) as the generalisation of (
42
).

6
Conclusions

The wave induced drift of particles has attracted increasing attention in recent years, particularly in connection with the transport of maritime pollution. Nevertheless, fundamental explorations of this topic are rare. Notable exceptions are the recent articles by
van den Bremer & Taylor 2016
and the follow-up study by
van den Bremer et al. 2019
, which focused on the transport associated with wave groups to second order. Our aim has been to explore particle drift systematically using the first, second, and third order theory, and working our way from monochromatic waves to random seas. This work rests on numerical integration of the particle trajectory mappings and comparison with simplified formulas like that for Stokes drift.

We consistently observe that linear theory gives the largest forward drift close to the free surface. Adding second order effects decreases this drift somewhat when more than one mode is present. The reason is simple: where the linear waves have the largest displacement we find a region of negative velocity in the second order difference harmonics. Third order effects decrease the forward drift further through a combination of changes to wave speed – which shrinks the particle paths – and the addition of further harmonics.

Descending in the water column we find a different overall behaviour of the wave-induced drift. It is well known that the water column acts like a filter, first suppressing the highest frequencies and filtering out ever lower frequencies with increasing depth. Particle motion is typically considered negligible half a wavelength below the surface. It is this filtering effect on linear, free-wave constituents that leads to the dramatic decrease of drift in the linear theory. However, nonlinearity introduces much longer bound-waves which become dominant at depth. Beneath the centre of a wave group these can induce sizable localised return flows. However, their overall contribution is to increase the drift in the direction of wave propagation versus linear theory. Third-order contributions lead to additional bound waves, but also to dispersion corrections; the overall effect of third-order vs. second-order nonlinearity thus depends on parameters of the sea-state such as its bandwidth and amplitude spectrum.

These results for each order require integration of the particle trajectory mapping, which is inconvenient. The more common approach in practice is to use the Stokes drift formula, which approximates the linear theory via a Taylor series expansion. Comparisons show that this approximation is quite poor, particularly close to the surface, where linear theory yields a significantly larger drift. In fact, the Stokes drift formulation inadvertently lies quite close to the smaller drift predictions of the nonlinear (second or third order) theories near the surface, which may explain some of its success.

At depth the Stokes drift hews close to the linear drift, but we have seen that this may differ significantly from the drift in nonlinear wave theories. To this end, we have retained a further term in the Stokes drift formulation which bridges some of this gap, and accounts for second order difference harmonics. For settings with multiple nearby harmonics this adjusted Stokes drift formula captures the larger forward drift associated with nonlinear waves, and represents an improvement on the commonly used formulation.

Our objective has been to provide a systematic and transparent exposition of drift associated with waves in nonlinear potential flow. To this end numerous simplifications have been made. Noteworthy among these is the neglect of viscous effects at the free surface, which has been known since work of
Longuet-Higgins 1953
to play an important role. We have likewise neglected directional effects, which can be expected to significantly complicate any study. Finally, and significantly, we have relied on the simplification of infinite water depth. This has allowed us to present numerous simple and compact formulations which are difficult to find in the existing literature, and contributed – we hope – to a clear presentation. Nevertheless, it is important to note that long bound harmonics may “feel the bottom” even when the free waves do not, and so the inclusion of finite depth is of central importance. This will be treated in a subsequent work.

With all of the caveats above, it is important to mention that the drift incorporated into operational models such as WAVEWATCH III is the simplest infinite-depth Stokes drift formulation
(
Li et al. 2016
)
. This points to a continued need to better understand this formulation – with all the simplifications it involves – and how it may be further refined. For example, it has been pointed out recently
(
Lenain & Pizzo 2020
)
that correct treatment of the high-frequencies is critical for estimating the surface drift. The high-frequency components of the wave spectrum undergo the largest correction due to third-order effects
(
Stuhlmeier & Stiassnie 2019
)
, and further investigation of how to correct the Stokes drift to account for this could be highly relevant for practical applications. It may also be of interest to go beyond the confines of the third-order theory presented here, and to explore particle trajectories for solutions to the full Euler equations e.g. via conformal mapping techniques.

Appendix A
On the non-uniqueness of Stokes expansion

The perturbation expansion associated with Stokes waves is often presented in textbooks as following a set sequence of steps. In fact, numerous choices are made, including whether to add linear terms in
x
or
t
to the potential, and how to treat constants appearing in the expansion. A useful discussion of some of these choices is found in
(
Svendsen 2005

(
Janssen 2009

For a chosen linear wave amplitude there is a non-uniqueness in the Stokes’ expansion, which makes it necessary to specify the potential explicitly. For example, the following pairs of third-order solutions have the same linear amplitude

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{ag}{\omega}e^{kz}\sin(\xi)

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=a\left(1+\frac{a^{2}k^{2}}{8}\right)\cos(\xi)+\frac{a^{2}k}{2}\cos(2\xi)+\frac{3}{8}a^{3}k^{2}\cos(3\xi)

and

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{ag}{\omega}\left(1-\frac{a^{2}k^{2}}{4}\right)e^{kz}\sin(\xi)

(49)

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=a\left(1-\frac{a^{2}k^{2}}{8}\right)\cos(\xi)+\frac{a^{2}k}{2}\cos(2\xi)+\frac{3}{8}a^{3}k^{2}\cos(3\xi)
