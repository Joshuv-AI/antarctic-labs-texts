On sample complexity for covariance estimation via the unadjusted Langevin algorithm
Shogo Nakakita

Abstract
. We establish sample complexity guarantees for estimating the covariance matrix of strongly log-concave smooth distributions using the unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA). We quantitatively compare our complexity estimates on single-chain ULA with embarrassingly parallel ULA and derive that the sample complexity of the single-chain approach is smaller than that of embarrassingly parallel ULA by a logarithmic factor in the dimension and the reciprocal of the prescribed precision, with the difference arising from effective bias reduction through burn-in. The key technical contribution is a concentration bound for the sample covariance matrix around its expectation, derived via a log-Sobolev inequality for the joint distribution of ULA iterates.



1.
Introduction

Sampling from a Gibbs distribution
\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\propto e^{-f(x)}\mathrm{d}x
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a fundamental problem in statistics, physics, and machine learning.
Let
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
be an
\alpha
-convex and
\beta
-smooth potential function, i.e.,
\alpha I_{d}\preceq\nabla^{2}f(x)\preceq\beta I_{d}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is one of the successful strategies for this problem, and the unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA) is a popular MCMC method due to its simplicity and efficiency.

Let us introduce the definition of ULA (with fixed step size). Given (i) an initial value
X_{0}
(a
d
-dimensional random vector, possibly
X_{0}\sim\delta_{x}
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
), (ii) step size
\eta>0
, (iii) the number of burn-in steps
m\geq 0
, and (iv) sample size
n\geq 1
, the algorithm is defined for all
i=1,\ldots,m+n
as

(1)

X_{i}=X_{i-1}-\eta\nabla f(X_{i-1})+\sqrt{2\eta}Z_{i},

where
\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\sim^{\textnormal{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}_{d}(0,I_{d})
independent of
X_{0}
.
While its invariant distribution
\pi_{\eta}
(which exists for sufficiently small
\eta
) differs from the target distribution
\pi
, it is known that
\pi_{\eta}
converges to
\pi
in some metrics or divergences as
\eta\to 0
.

ULA is derived by the Euler–Maruyama discretization of the Langevin dynamics (also known as the overdamped Langevin diffusion) defined as the solution of the following stochastic differential equation:

\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=-\nabla f(Y_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},\ Y_{0}=X_{0},

where
B_{t}
is the standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion.
We can interpret that ULA approximates the continuous-time process
Y_{t}
at discrete time points
t=i\eta
for
i=0,\ldots,m+n
.
The Langevin dynamics has
\pi
as its invariant distribution and has an explicit rate of convergence to
\pi
if
\pi
satisfies functional inequalities such as Poincaré inequalities and log-Sobolev inequalities
(
Bakry et al., 2014
)
.
Owing to this property, ULA is expected to approximate
\pi
well if
\eta\ll 1
and
m\eta\gg 1
.
In fact, the community has unveiled the behaviour of the distribution of
X_{n}
for each
n

(
Dalalyan, 2017
;
Durmus and Moulines, 2017
;
Durmus and Moulines, 2019
;
Vempala and Wibisono, 2019
, e.g.,)
and found favourable non-asymptotic convergence guarantees to the target distribution
\pi
in various metrics such as the 2-Wasserstein distance and Kullback–Leibler divergence in this decade.

1.1.
Problem setting

Our main interest lies in the sample complexity analysis for moment estimation via ULA.
In statistical inference, we are often interested in moments of target distributions, such as means and covariance matrices, and the sample complexities required for their estimation.
We expect that the sample mean
\bar{X}_{n}
and sample covariance matrix
\hat{\Sigma}_{n}
defined as

(2)

\bar{X}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+n}X_{i},\quad\hat{\Sigma}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+n}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\bar{X}_{n}\bar{X}_{n}^{\top}

of ULA should approximate the population mean
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[X]=\int x\pi(\mathrm{d}x)
and population covariance
\textnormal{Cov}(\pi)=\int xx^{\top}\pi(\mathrm{d}x)-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[X]\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[X]^{\top}
when
\eta\ll 1
and
n\eta\gg 1
.
While this idea is asymptotically guaranteed by standard ergodic theorems under fixed
d
, it is not obvious when
d
is large.
Specifically, it is unclear how large
n
should be to achieve a certain accuracy in high-dimensional settings due to the dependence among the ULA outputs and the resulting large variance.

One may consider the embarrassingly parallel ULA to address dependence, which is the main comparison target of this paper.
In this study, we refer to the embarrassingly parallel ULA as the procedure that collects the last iterates of
N
independent paths of ULA
\{X_{m+1}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
defined as follows: for all
k=1,\ldots,N
and
i=1,\ldots,m+1
(with
m
burn-in steps discarded in estimation), with
d
-dimensional random vectors
X_{0}^{k}
,

(3)

X_{i}^{k}=X_{i-1}^{k}-\eta\nabla f(X_{i-1}^{k})+\sqrt{2\eta}Z_{i}^{k},

where
\{Z_{i}\}_{i,k}\sim^{\textnormal{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}_{d}(0,I_{d})
.
While it requires burn-in for every path to remove
bias
, the
variance
of its sample moments is smaller than that of single-chain ULA with
N=n
.
If the sample complexities of both methods coincide up to constant factors, embarrassingly parallel ULA is preferable in practice due to its wall-clock speedup via parallelization.
Hence, one should raise the following question:

Q
.

Does the bias removal of single-chain ULA by burn-in outweigh its large variance from dependence in comparison to embarrassingly parallel ULA in terms of sample complexity? If so, how large is the difference?

To answer this question, we study sample complexity guarantees for covariance estimation of strongly log-concave smooth distributions via single-chain ULA and embarrassingly parallel ULA respectively.
In particular, this study establishes a concentration bound for the sample covariance matrix of single-chain ULA, which is our main theoretical contribution.
Some studies have investigated non-asymptotic guarantees for moment estimation via MCMC
(
Durmus and Moulines, 2019
;
Kook and Zhang, 2024
;
Shen et al., 2025
)
, but this topic remains largely unexplored in the literature.
Recently,
Kook and Zhang, 2024
studied covariance matrix estimation with high-accuracy MCMC (that is, MCMC whose invariant distribution is exactly the target distribution; for example, random-walk Metropolis and Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm) for
\pi
satisfying a Poincaré inequality.
However, their comparison focuses on query complexities rather than sample complexities for covariance estimation.
In contrast, this work examines sample complexity through a concentration analysis of the sample covariance matrix and establishes explicit bounds for both single-chain and parallel ULA approaches.

We address the problem via a bias–variance decomposition and analysing the variance part; specifically, we show concentration bounds on sample moments of ULA around its expectation rather than the moments of
\pi_{\eta}
or
\pi
.
By the triangular inequality with operator norm
\|\cdot\|
, we have

(4)

\left\|\hat{\Sigma}_{n}-\textnormal{Cov}(\pi)\right\|\leq\underbrace{\left\|\textnormal{Cov}(\pi_{\eta})-\textnormal{Cov}(\pi)\right\|}_{\text{bias by discretization}}+\underbrace{\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\Sigma}_{n}\right]-\textnormal{Cov}(\pi_{\eta})\right\|}_{\text{bias by non-stationarity}}+\underbrace{\left\|\hat{\Sigma}_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\Sigma}_{n}\right]\right\|}_{\text{variance by sampling}},

where
\textnormal{Cov}(\pi_{\eta}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}xx^{\top}\pi_{\eta}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\pi_{\eta}(\mathrm{d}x)(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\pi_{\eta}(\mathrm{d}x))^{\top}
.
Since previous studies
(
Durmus and Moulines, 2019
, e.g.,)
show bounds on the 2-Wasserstein distances for the pair of
\pi_{\eta}
and
\pi
and that of the distribution of
X_{i}
and
\pi_{\eta}
, our focus is on evaluating the variance term.

1.2.
Main contributions

Our contributions can be summarized as follows.

•

Concentration for sample covariance matrices under dependence.

Using the log-Sobolev inequality, we derive a concentration bound on the sample covariance matrix around its expectation in operator norm, whose rate of convergence is
(1/\alpha)\sqrt{d/(\alpha\eta n)}
(Theorem
1
).
This rate is reasonable since
1/\alpha
is the largest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix under Gaussian settings, and
\alpha\eta n
can be regarded as the terminal time of the Langevin dynamics which ULA approximates and should serve as the effective sample size in an ordinary sense.

•

Sample complexity for covariance estimation.

Combining the above variance bounds with existing non-asymptotic bias bounds for ULA, we obtain explicit sample complexity guarantees for estimating the covariance matrix of the target distribution
\pi
via a bias–variance decomposition.
Under standard settings, the total sample complexity is shown to be of order
\mathcal{O}(\beta^{2}d^{2}(d+\log(1/\delta))\alpha^{-6}\varepsilon^{-4})
to achieve
\varepsilon
-accuracy in operator norm with probability at least
1-\delta
(Proposition
2
).
Notably, the estimation error does not deteriorate for large
n
beyond the required sample complexity, which is a desirable property in statistics and optimization.

•

Quantitative comparison of sample complexity between ULA and embarrassingly parallel ULA.

We also derive sample complexity guarantees for covariance estimation via embarrassingly parallel ULA by the
N
last iterates of independent path realizations of ULA as a comparison.
The estimate on the total sample complexity of embarrassingly parallel ULA is shown to be larger than that of ULA by a
logarithmic factor in
d
and
\varepsilon
.
While it is the comparison of upper bounds, both the derivations employ tight concentration inequalities via the isoperimetry argument and same known bounds on moments and Wasserstein distances.
In addition, the difference can be attributed to the bias reduction mechanism by burn-in.
The comparison quantifies the difference between single-chain ULA and embarrassingly parallel ULA in sample complexity for covariance estimation.

To derive these results, we establish a log-Sobolev inequality for the joint distribution of
(X_{m+1},\ldots,X_{m+n})
, which is our main technical contribution.
In particular, the joint distribution is shown to satisfy a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
2/(\alpha^{2}\eta)
under a mild burn-in condition (Proposition
4
); this constant is shown to be tight by exemplifying a Gaussian AR(1) process.

1.3.
Literature review

We present literature review on empirical processes of MCMC, invariant distributions of ULA, and concentration under dependence.

Non-asymptotic analysis for empirical processes of MCMC

We first review recent studies about non-asymptotic bounds on empirical processes of MCMC algorithms.

Durmus and Moulines, 2019
consider Lipschitz concentration of ULA under the strong log-concavity and smoothness of target distributions via iterative moment bounds.

Kook and Zhang, 2024
study concentration bounds for sample covariance matrices of high-accuracy MCMC (i.e., MCMC having invariant measures coincide with target measures and resulting polylogarithmic complexity in error tolerance level under warm-start) and query complexities for MCMC and i.i.d. sampling; though our interest is in sample complexities, the interest of their research is close to ours.

Shen et al., 2025
derive high-dimensional normal approximations for the difference between the sample mean of stationary ULA and expectation of its target distribution.

Properties of invariant distributions of ULA

Let us also introduce analysis on the invariant measure
\pi_{\eta}
of ULA with stepsize
\eta>0
.

Vempala and Wibisono, 2019
show that
\pi_{\eta}
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
C\leq 2/\alpha
.

Altschuler and Talwar, 2022
reveal subgaussianity and subexponentiality of
\pi_{\eta}
under the strong log-concavity and log-concavity of target distributions respectively.

Concentration under dependence

Moment bounds and concentration bounds under dependence are also a classical but topical problem
(
Han and Li, 2020
;
Neeman et al., 2024
, e.g.,)
.
While there are several strategies to derive sharp bounds as independent cases, one hopeful approach is to employ the isoperimetry of joint distributions, which is also pointed out in
Kook and Zhang, 2024
.

Adamczak, 2015
studies the Hanson–Wright inequality without assuming independent elements via the isoperimetry approach.

Nakakita et al., 2025
also employ this approach, assuming a log-Sobolev inequality of the joint distribution of dependent random vectors.
Our analysis follows this route: we establish a sharp, dimension-free log-Sobolev constant for the joint law of
(X_{m+1},\ldots,X_{m+n})
generated by ULA, which leads to a concentration bound for the sample covariance matrix in operator norm.

2.
Concentration on the sample covariance matrix of the unadjusted Langevin algorithm

In this section, we present our theorem on the concentration of the sample covariance matrix (
2
) of ULA around its expectation.
Furthermore, we discuss an extension to the projected ULA.

2.1.
Setup

We give definitions and assumptions used in this section and the rest of the paper.

Definition 1
.

A probability distribution
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to satisfy a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
C\geq 0
(denoted by LSI(
C
)) if for any smooth function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{2}(x)|\log g(x)|\mu(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{2}(x)\log g^{2}(x)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{2}(x)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\right)\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{2}(x)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\right)\leq 2C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla g(x)|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}x).

See
Bakry et al., 2014
,
van Handel, 2016
, and
Chewi, 2023
for details on log-Sobolev inequalities and their properties.
For example, the
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}_{d}(0,I_{d})
satisfies an LSI(1).

The following assumption is standard in the analysis of ULA
(
Durmus and Moulines, 2019
, e.g.,)
.

Assumption 1
.

The potential function
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
satisfies
\alpha I_{d}\preceq\nabla^{2}f\preceq\beta I_{d}
with some
\alpha,\beta>0
(
\alpha\leq\beta
).

2.2.
Concentration bound

We now present our theorem on the concentration of the sample covariance matrix of ULA.
The proof is deferred to Appendix
A
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds.
Assume that
\eta\in(0,1/\beta)
and the distribution of
X_{0}
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\kappa_{0}
.
If

m\geq 0\vee\left(\frac{\log(\kappa_{0}/\eta)}{\log(1/(1-\alpha\eta))}-1\right)\vee\left(\frac{\log(4(\mathbb{E}[|X_{0}-x^{\ast}|^{2}]+d/\alpha)/\eta n)}{\log(1/(1-\alpha\eta))}-1\right)

(with convention
\log 0=-\infty
), then for any
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-4\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{\Sigma}_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\Sigma}_{n}\right]\right\|\leq\frac{16}{\alpha}\left(8\sqrt{\frac{9d+4\log\delta^{-1}}{\alpha\eta n}}\vee\left(\frac{9d+4\log\delta^{-1}}{\alpha\eta n}\right)\right).

In particular,
\|\hat{\Sigma}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\Sigma}_{n}]\|=\mathcal{O}((1/\alpha)(\sqrt{(d+\log\delta^{-1})/(\alpha^{2}\eta n)}+(d+\log\delta^{-1})/(\alpha^{2}\eta n)))
.

Note that selecting
x=x^{\ast}=\argmin_{x}f(x)
benefits the reduction of the bias terms in (
4
) in our argument employing
Durmus and Moulines, 2019
.
Solving a optimization problem for strongly convex smooth
f
is computationally easy (e.g., the gradient descent shows linear convergence), and its complexity is negligible in comparison to that of the sampling problem as
Altschuler and Chewi, 2024
point out.
Let us remark that the rate of convergence
(1/\alpha)\sqrt{d/(\alpha\eta n)}
is reasonable: the scaling
\alpha
is natural since
\|\Sigma\|=1/\alpha
for a Gaussian case
f(x)=x^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}x/2
with a positive definite matrix
\Sigma
, and the effective sample size
\alpha n\eta
is standard from the Langevin dynamics as a continuous analogue of ULA, whose effective sample size is
\alpha\eta n

(
Bakry et al., 2014
, e.g., see)
.

2.3.
Technical remarks on Theorem
1

The technical core of Theorem
1
is to show that the joint distribution of
(X_{m+1},\ldots,X_{m+n})
generated by ULA satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
2/(\alpha^{2}\eta)
under a mild burn-in condition.
As the log-Sobolev constant quantifies both the tail behaviour and dependence, we can derive a concentration bound for the sample covariance matrix even under dependence as
Adamczak, 2015
.

We also remark the role of the burn-in condition in Theorem
1
.
First, the condition
m\geq\log(\kappa_{0}/\eta)/\log(1/(1-\alpha\eta))
is to eliminate the effect of the initial distribution of
X_{0}
if its log-Sobolev constant is large and thus its tail behaviour is relatively heavy.
Second, the condition
m\geq\left(\log(4(\mathbb{E}[|X_{0}-x^{\ast}|^{2}]+d/\alpha)/\eta n)/\log(1/(1-\alpha\eta))\right)
is to remove the effect of the non-stationary nature of ULA due to the biased initial distribution.

3.
Sample complexity guarantees for covariance estimation

In this section, using Theorem
1
, we show sample complexity estimates for single-chain ULA and embarrassingly parallel ULA.
For single-chain ULA, we show sample complexity estimates
n(\delta,\varepsilon)
and
m(\delta,\varepsilon)
(burn-in complexity) to achieve that for any
\varepsilon
and
\delta
, for all
m\geq m(\delta,\varepsilon)
and
n\geq n(\delta,\varepsilon)
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\hat{\Sigma}_{n}-\textnormal{Cov}(\pi)\right\|\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta.

For the embarrassingly parallel ULA, we also derive sample complexity estimates
N_{\text{ep}}(\delta,\varepsilon)
and
m_{\text{ep}}(\delta,\varepsilon)
to let the following bound hold for all
m\geq m_{\text{ep}}(\delta,\varepsilon)
and
N\geq N_{\text{ep}}(\delta,\varepsilon)
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\tilde{\Sigma}_{N}-\textnormal{Cov}(\pi)\right\|\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta,

where
\tilde{\Sigma}_{N}
is defined as

\tilde{\Sigma}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{m+1}^{k}(X_{m+1}^{k})^{\top}-\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{m+1}^{k}\right)\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{m+1}^{k}\right)^{\top},

and
X_{m+1}^{k}
is the last iterate of ULA defined in (
3
).
Note that the total sample complexity estimates for single-chain ULA and the embarrassingly parallel ULA are
m(\delta,\varepsilon)+n(\delta,\varepsilon)
and
N_{\text{ep}}(\delta,\varepsilon)m_{\text{ep}}(\delta,\varepsilon)
respectively.

3.1.
Sample complexity for the unadjusted Langevin algorithm

We obtain the following high-probability bound for covariance estimation via ULA.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds. Fix
\varepsilon\leq\min\{1,1/\alpha\}
and assume that
X_{0}=x^{\ast}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)
.
For any
\delta>0
, if

\eta\leq\frac{\alpha^{3}\varepsilon^{2}}{2700\beta^{2}d^{2}},\ n\geq\frac{2^{9}\cdot 3^{2}}{\eta}\left(\frac{9d+4\log(4/\delta)}{\alpha^{3}\varepsilon^{2}}\right),\ m\geq\frac{\log((4d)/(\alpha\eta n))}{\log(1/(1-\alpha\eta))}-1,\

then

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\hat{\Sigma}_{n}-\textnormal{Cov}(\pi)\right\|\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta.

The assumption on the initial value
X_{0}=x^{\ast}
is just for a concise presentation; if one consider other initial values or warm-start, its effect can be eliminated by an additional burn-in phase, whose length is only logarithmic in
|x_{0}-x^{\ast}|
when
X_{0}=x_{0}
is deterministic (cold start), or in
\mathbb{E}[|X_{0}-x^{\ast}|^{2}]
when
X_{0}
is random (warm start).

Using this proposition, we derive the following sample complexity estimate and burn-in complexity estimate: by
\log(1/(1-x))\geq x
for all
x\in[0,1)
,

\displaystyle n(\delta,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}d^{2}(d+\log(1/\delta))}{\alpha^{6}\varepsilon^{4}}\right),\ m(\delta,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}d^{2}}{\alpha^{4}\varepsilon^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}(n(\delta,\varepsilon)).

The total sample complexity, the order of
n(\delta,\varepsilon)+m(\delta,\varepsilon)
, is given as

(5)

n(\delta,\varepsilon)+m(\delta,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}d^{2}(d+\log(1/\delta))}{\alpha^{6}\varepsilon^{4}}\right).

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first estimate for the sample complexity of covariance matrix estimation for strongly log-concave smooth distributions via ULA.
This guarantee enjoys the following properties: (i) the estimation error does not deteriorate for large
n
beyond the sample complexity, which is important in statistics, and (ii) cold start is allowed, which is also important in practice.

3.2.
Comparison with embarrassingly parallel ULA

As a comparison, we derive the following high-probability guarantee for the embarrassingly parallel ULA.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds and fix
\varepsilon\leq\min\{1,1/\alpha\}
.
For all
k=1,\ldots,N
, let
X_{0}^{k}=x^{\ast}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)
.
For any
\delta>0
, if

\eta\leq\frac{\alpha^{3}\varepsilon^{2}}{2700\beta^{2}d^{2}},\ m\geq\frac{2\log(48d/(\alpha\varepsilon))}{\log\left(1/(1-\alpha\eta)\right)}-1,\ N\geq\frac{2^{8}\cdot 3^{2}(9d+4\log(4/\delta))}{\alpha^{2}\varepsilon^{2}},

then

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\tilde{\Sigma}_{N}-\textnormal{Cov}(\pi)\right\|\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta.

We obtain the following sampling complexity estimate: using
\log(1/(1-x))\geq x
for all
x\in[0,1)
, we obtain that

m_{\text{ep}}(\delta,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}d^{2}\log\left(d/(\alpha\varepsilon)\right)}{\alpha^{4}\varepsilon^{2}}\right),\ N_{\text{ep}}(\delta,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d+\log\delta^{-1}}{\alpha^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}\right),

and thus, the total sample complexity is

(6)

N_{\text{ep}}(\delta,\varepsilon)m_{\text{ep}}(\delta,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}d^{2}(d+\log\delta^{-1})}{\alpha^{6}\varepsilon^{4}}\log\left(\frac{d}{\alpha\varepsilon}\right)\right).

Comparing (
5
) and (
6
), the difference in the total sample complexity of single-chain ULA and embarrassingly parallel ULA is a logarithmic factor in
d
and
\varepsilon^{-1}
.
This difference comes from the bias term due to non-stationarity.
For single-chain ULA, the bias by non-stationarity is of order
\mathcal{O}((1/n\eta)(1-\alpha\eta)^{m/2})
; it means that the bias caused by finite burn-in steps
m
is mitigated by large
n\eta
, which is originally taken to be large to reduce the variance term as seen in Theorem
1
.
On the other hand, the corresponding term is of order
\mathcal{O}((1-\alpha\eta)^{m/2})
for embarrassingly parallel ULA, which cannot be reduced by increasing
N
.
It means that embarrassingly parallel ULA requires large
m
for every iteration of the path of ULA.
This difference affects the total sample complexity.

Appendix A
Proofs

A.1.
Preliminaries

In this section, we prepare some technical results for the proofs of Propositions
13
and
14
.
The following proposition gives a log-Sobolev constant of the joint distribution of
(X_{m+1},\ldots,X_{m+n})
, which is a technical key tool in the proofs of the propositions.

Proposition 4

(LSI for joint distributions)
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds.
Moreover, assume the following:

(a)

For some
\kappa_{0}\geq 0
, the distribution of
X_{0}
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\kappa_{0}
.

(b)

\eta\in(0,1/\beta)
.

(c)

m\geq 0\vee(\frac{\log(\kappa_{0}/(2\eta))}{\log(1/(1-\alpha\eta))}-1)
(with convention
\log 0=-\infty
).

Then, the joint probability distribution of
(X_{m+1},\ldots,X_{m+n})
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
2/(\alpha^{2}\eta)
.

Regarding the condition (c), if
\kappa_{0}\leq 2\eta
, it is satisfied for any
m\geq 0
.
In particular, cold start (i.e.,
X_{0}=x_{0}
for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with probability 1) satisfies the condition since
\kappa_{0}=0
, and thus we can take
m=0
.

Let us argue the tightness of the estimate
2/(\alpha^{2}\eta)
.
Exemplify a
1
-dimensional stationary Gaussian AR(1) process

Remark 1
.

Let us discuss the effect of burn-in period
m
.
A typical motivation of introducing burn-in period is to reduce the bias by non-stationarity in (
4
), which results from the initial distribution largely deviating from the invariant distribution
\pi_{\eta}
.
Moreover, Proposition
4
and its proof indicate that burn-in also helps to mitigate the effect of the initial distribution in terms of the log-Sobolev constant.

Proof.

Let us define a scaled version of
X_{0}
and its realization
x_{0}
as
\tilde{X}_{0}=\sqrt{1/\kappa_{0}}X_{0}
and
\tilde{x}_{0}=\sqrt{1/\kappa_{0}}x_{0}
.
Then, the distribution of
\tilde{X}_{0}
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
1
.

It is sufficient to show that
(X_{m+1},X_{1},\ldots,X_{m+n})=g(\tilde{X}_{0},Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{m+n})
for a map
g:\mathbb{R}^{d(m+n+1)}\to\mathbb{R}^{dn}
with
|g(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})-g(\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime},z_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{n}^{\prime})|^{2}\leq 2/(\alpha^{2}\eta)
for arbitrary
\tilde{x}_{0},\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime},z_{i},z_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
due to the Gaussian log-Sobolev inequality and tensorization argument
(
Bakry et al., 2014
)
.

We consider a representation
X_{i}
for
i=m+1,\ldots,m+n
such as

\displaystyle X_{i}(\tilde{X}_{0},Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{i})

\displaystyle=X_{i}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{i})|_{\tilde{x}_{0}=\tilde{X}_{0},z_{j}=Z_{j},j\in[i]}

\displaystyle=g_{i}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m+n})|_{\tilde{x}_{0}=\tilde{X}_{0},z_{j}=Z_{j},j\in[m+n]}

and examine its Lipschitz property.
It is obvious that
\nabla_{z_{j}}g_{i}=O
for any
j>i
,
\nabla_{z_{i}}g_{i}=\sqrt{2\eta}I_{d}
for any
i=m+1,\ldots,m+n
.
For
j<i
,
\|\nabla_{z_{j}}g_{i}\|\leq(1-\alpha\eta)^{i-j}\sqrt{2\eta}
since for any fixed vectors
z_{j},z_{j}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle|g_{i}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j},z_{j+1},\ldots,z_{m+n})-g_{i}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1},z_{j}^{\prime},z_{j+1},\ldots,z_{m+n})|

\displaystyle=|X_{i}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{j},\ldots,z_{i})-X_{i}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{j}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{i})|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(X_{i-1}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{j},\ldots,z_{i-1})-\eta\nabla f(X_{i-1}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{j},\ldots,z_{i-1}))\right)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-\left(X_{i-1}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{j}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{i-1})-\eta\nabla f(X_{i-1}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{j}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{i-1}))\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha\eta)|X_{i-1}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{j},\ldots,z_{i-1})-X_{i-1}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{j}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{i-1})|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha\eta)^{i-j}|X_{j}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1})-X_{j}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\eta}(1-\alpha\eta)^{i-j}|z_{j}-z_{j}^{\prime}|,

where we used the fact that if
\eta\leq 1/\beta
and
f
is
\alpha
-convex, for any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
|x-\eta\nabla f(x)-x^{\prime}+\eta\nabla f(x^{\prime})|\leq(1-\alpha\eta)|x-x^{\prime}|
.
Similarly, we have that for any
\tilde{x}_{0},\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime}
,

|g_{i}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m+n})-g_{i}(\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m+n})|\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{0}}(1-\alpha\eta)^{i}|\tilde{x}_{0}-\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime}|.

We now evaluate the Lipschitz constant of
g
.
For arbitrary
\tilde{x}_{0},\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime},z_{j},z_{j}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle|g(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m+n})-g(\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime},z_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{m+n}^{\prime})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+n}|g_{i}(\tilde{x}_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m+n})-g_{i}(\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime},z_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{m+n}^{\prime})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+n}\left((1-\alpha\eta)^{i}\sqrt{\kappa_{0}}|\tilde{x}_{0}-\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime}|+\sum_{j=1}^{i}(1-\alpha\eta)^{i-j}\sqrt{2\eta}|z_{j}-z_{j}^{\prime}|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+n}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{i}(1-\alpha\eta)^{i-j}\right)\left(\kappa_{0}(1-\alpha\eta)^{i}|\tilde{x}_{0}-\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}+(2\eta)\sum_{j=1}^{i}(1-\alpha\eta)^{i-j}|z_{j}-z_{j}^{\prime}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha\eta}\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+n}\left(\kappa_{0}(1-\alpha\eta)^{i}|\tilde{x}_{0}-\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}+(2\eta)\sum_{j=1}^{i}(1-\alpha\eta)^{i-j}|z_{j}-z_{j}^{\prime}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\alpha\eta}\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+n}(1-\alpha\eta)^{i}|\tilde{x}_{0}-\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{2}{\alpha}\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+n}\sum_{j=1}^{i}(1-\alpha\eta)^{i-j}|z_{j}-z_{j}^{\prime}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\alpha^{2}\eta^{2}}(1-\alpha\eta)^{m+1}|\tilde{x}_{0}-\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{2}{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{m+n}\sum_{i=j\vee(m+1)}^{m+n}(1-\alpha\eta)^{i-j}|z_{j}-z_{j}^{\prime}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\alpha^{2}\eta}\left(|\tilde{x}_{0}-\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{m+n}|z_{j}-z_{j}^{\prime}|^{2}\right),

where we used
\eta\leq 1/\beta\leq 1/\alpha
and the fact
(\kappa_{0}/(\alpha^{2}\eta^{2}))(1-\alpha\eta)^{m+1}\leq 2/(\alpha^{2}\eta)
since if
\kappa_{0}>2\eta
,

(1-\alpha\eta)^{m+1}\leq\frac{2\eta}{\kappa_{0}}\iff m\geq\frac{\log(\kappa_{0}/(2\eta))}{\log(1/(1-\alpha\eta))}-1,

and if
\kappa_{0}\leq 2\eta
,
(\kappa_{0}/(\alpha^{2}\eta^{2}))(1-\alpha\eta)^{m+1}\leq 2/(\alpha^{2}\eta)
for any
m\geq 0
.
This complete the proof.
∎

Lemma 6

(LSI for marginal distributions)

Suppose that Assumption

Let us recall the definition of a Poincaré inequality.
A probability distribution
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to satisfy a Poincaré inequality with constant
C\geq 0
(denoted by PI(
C
)) if for any smooth function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|g(x)|^{2}\mu(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{2}(x)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\right)^{2}\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla g(x)|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}x).

Note that if a distribution satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
C
, it also satisfies a Poincaré inequality with constant
C

(
Bakry et al., 2014
)
.

Lemma 10

(Bounds on the difference of matrices)
.

For any probability distributions
\mu
and
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any vector

(a)

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}[(X-z)(X-z)^{\top}]-\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\nu}[(Y-z)(Y-z)^{\top}]\|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(\sqrt{\int|x-z|^{2}\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}+\sqrt{\int|y-z|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)}\right)W_{2}(\mu,\nu),

(b)

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbb{E}[XX^{\top}]-\mathbb{E}[YY^{\top}]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[X]^{\top}+\mathbb{E}[Y]\mathbb{E}[Y]^{\top}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbb{E}[(X-z)(X-z)^{\top}]-\mathbb{E}[(Y-z)(Y-z)^{\top}]\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[X_{m+1}^{1}(X_{m+1}^{1})^{\top}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\eta}}\left[XX^{\top}\right]\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{N}\left(\tilde{X}_{N}\right)^{\top}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{N}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{N}\right]^{\top}\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{N}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{N}\right]^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\eta}}\left[X\right]\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\eta}}\left[X\right]^{\top}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sqrt{(1-\alpha\eta)^{m+1}2d/\alpha}\left(2\sqrt{2d/\alpha}\right)+\frac{2}{\alpha N}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[X_{m+1}^{1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[X_{m+1}^{1}\right]^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\eta}}\left[X\right]\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\eta}}\left[X\right]^{\top}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{(1-\alpha\eta)^{m+1}2d/\alpha}\left(2\sqrt{2d/\alpha}\right)+\frac{2}{\alpha N}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8d}{\alpha}(1-\alpha\eta)^{(m+1)/2}+\frac{2}{\alpha N}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3},

where we repeatedly used the assumption that
X_{m+1}^{k}
has the same distribution as
X_{m+1}^{1}
for all
k=1,\ldots,N
.
We now consider the variance term.
By the assumption on
N
,
(9d+4\log(4/\delta)^{-1})/N\leq 1
.
Therefore, Proposition
15
yields that with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{\Sigma}_{N}-\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{\Sigma}_{N}\right]\right\|\leq\frac{16}{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{9d+4\log(4/\delta)^{-1}}{N}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

This is the desired conclusion.
∎
