Robust and Adaptive Optimization under a Large Language Model Lens
Dimitris Bertsimas, Georgios Margaritis

Abstract
In this paper, we explore the application of ChatGPT in the domain of Robust and Adaptive Robust Optimization. We demonstrate that with appropriate prompting, ChatGPT can be used to auto-formulate and solve simple Robust and Adaptive Optimization Problems. We first develop specialized informational prompts tailored to the domains of Adaptive and Robust Optimization. Then, we show that using these prompts, ChatGPT is able to (i) formulate an optimization problem, (ii) adapt the problem so that it accounts for user-specified uncertainty, (iii) derive the computationally tractable robust counterpart of the problem and (iv) produce executable code that solves the problem. For simple Optimization Problems, we show that ChatGPT is able to perform these steps with little to no errors. We also highlight some instances of erroneous execution of the steps. Overall, we show that using in-context learning, ChatGPT has the potential to adapt to higlhy specialized and niche domains, in which it would otherwise demonstrate very poor out-of-the-box performance.

1
Introduction

Many real-world decision-making problems
can be modeled and solved as mathematical
optimization problems.
In fact, the field of Operations Research,
has found a lot of success in a variety of important real-world applications,
such as optimizing Air Transportation (
Barnhart et al. (2003)
),
Ride-Sharing (
Agatz et al. (2012)
), Supply Chain Management
(
Donald Warsing (2013)
) and Financial Engineering (
Fabozzi et al. (2010)
),
only to name a few.
However, despite the immense versatility and its variety of uses,
the field of mathematical optimization hasn’t been as popular with the worldwide community
as fields like Machine Learning.
Part of the reason for this is that modeling and solving a real-world problem using Optimization
often requires the user to be a modeling expert, who is able
to recognize specific structures in the problem and exploit them to produce accurate and
tractable optimization formulations.
On the other hand, the widespread availability of Machine Learning packages has made Machine Learning (ML)
widely accessible to everybody, since even non-experts can train and run ML models by writing just
a few lines of code.

Hence, in this paper, we attempt to explore whether Optimization can be made more
accessible to non-experts using Large Language Models, such as ChatGPT.
However, we will focus our attention on a sub-field of Optimization, known as Robust Optimization (RO),
which often requires much more modeling expertise to be applied to a real-world problem.
The reason we are focusing on Robust Optimization is that
although traditional optimization can provide high-quality solutions in a variety of
cases, it has one major drawback; It doesn’t always
account for the uncertainty that is innate to most real-world applications.
In fact, systems optimized in the classical sense can be very sensitive to small changes.
This inherent sensitivity gives rise to Robust Optimization (RO),
the sub-filed of optimization that focuses on finding robust solutions, rather than optimal ones (
Beyer and
Sendhoff (2007)
).
RO helps address (a) the uncertainty in the data of an optimization problem,
(b) the implementation errors of a feasible solution to the problem and (c) multi-stage optimization
under uncertainty (
Bertsimas and
Den Hertog (2022)
).

In traditional RO, we solve the problem only using current data and we
make here-and-now decisions for all the optimization variables of the problem.
However, another important sub-field of RO is Adaptive Robust Optimization (ARO),
which allows us to postpone part of the decision, until we have more information
on the uncertain parameters.
In particular, ARO discerns between here-and-now decision variables and wait-and-see
decision variables, where the former are determined here and now,
while the latter are determined after the values of the uncertain parameters are revealed (
Bertsimas and
Den Hertog (2022)
).
ARO is particularly effective in multi-period (dynamic) decision problems, and generally gives
less conservative and more realistic solution than traditional RO, although at a higher
computational cost.

Both RO and ARO have been applied to a variety of real-world applications,
including Portfolio Optimization (
Fabozzi et al. (2010)
),
Supply Chain Management, Circuit Design and Power Control (
Bertsimas et al. (2011)
).
However, in order to introduce robustness to a classical optimization problem,
there are a variety of challenges.
We first need to identify the uncertain parameters of the
problem and model them using appropriately chosen uncertainty sets.
Then, we need to embed those uncertainties into a unified RO/ARO formulation.
Finally, by applying a range of theoretical results,
we need to convert the original RO/ARO formulation into a precise and tractable optimization
problem, which can be solved by traditional optimizers efficiently.
This process can, in practice, be complex and time-consuming,
and needs to be done by OR/modeling experts that have an very good background in RO.

Clearly, this makes the application of RO much less accessible to modelers without an adequate RO background.
Hence, in this paper, we attempt to Large Language Models (LLMs)
to help make the RO modeling process more accessible to non-experts.
In particular, we utilize ChatGPT and in-context learning in order to automate (i) the translation of a classical optimization formulation
to a unified RO/ARO formulation, (ii)
the translation of the original RO/ARO formulation to a tractable optimization problem and (iii) the generation of executable code that solves the Optimization Problem.

1.1
Related Work

The idea of using Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques
to help in formulating optimization problems has been studied
to some extent in the literature.
In (
Ramamonjison et al. (2023)
), the authors
propose the competition NL4OPT, which aims to gather interest
for the development of machine learning models that turn natural language
to optimization formulations.
The authors outline some of the winning solutions to the competition,
most of which utilize either some older NLP models, such as Bidirection LSTMs and
Conditional Random Fields (
Huang et al. (2015)
), or some of the early transformer-based models which are variations BERT (
Devlin et al. (2019)
).
The authors also tested ChatGPT in the competition dataset, showing very good performance, and in some cases,
even surpassing the winning solutions in certain subtasks of the competition,
despite the fact that those solutions, unlike ChatGPT, were highly taylored to the competition.

Apart from the NL4OPT competition, there have also been other independent approaches which
aim at helping in the formulation of optimization problems starting from natural language.
One such approach is (
Ramamonjison et al. (2022)
), in which
the authors propose a multi-step approach to do so, which includes the use of BERT-based
models and the generative LLM BART (
Lewis et al. (2019)
).
Finally, in the position paper (
Tsouros et al. (2023)
), the authors propose some
ideas and examples of how ChatGPT can be used to model simple optimization problems and auto-generate
code to solve them.

Although the ChatGPT-based approaches described in the literature bear some resemblance to our approach, we take a different
turn and focus solely on RO and ARO problems, which come with their own sets of difficulties.
In particular, since RO and particularly ARO are niche research areas, ChatGPT performs very poorly
out-of-the-box in such problems, due to the limited training data that exists for those topics.
In addition to that, formulating tractable RO and ARO problems requires the modeler
to apply and combine a variety of mathematical theorems, in order to transform the problem into a tractable
formulation that can be solved using standard optimization solvers (e.g. Gurobi, CPLEX).
Again, due to the niche nature of RO and ARO, ChatGPT is not equipped with the mathematical tools
required to perform those transformations.

In order to overcome this difficulties, we design specific prompting schemes which can help ChatGPT auto-formulate and solve RO and ARO problems through in-context learning.
To our knowledge, the process of using ChatGPT or other LLMs to manipulate and solve RO and ARO problems hasn’t been studied in the past, so we argue that our approach is the first approach in that direction.

1.2
Contribution

The contribution of our work is two-fold.
Firstly, we show that with appropriate prompting, generative models like ChatGPT can learn how to reformulate RO and ARO models.
Specifically, for basic uncertainty sets and simple Optimization Problems, we demonstrate that ChatGPT can be trained to (i) account for uncertainty in an optimization problem, (ii) derive the computationally-tractable robust counterpart of the problem and (iii) produce executable code that solves the problem.
We also showcase that when dealing with more complicated problems and uncertainty sets, ChatGPT may make errors in some of those steps.

Secondly, aside from the specific area of RO, we show that Large Language Models can adapt to very niche and specific areas, to which they weren’t explicitly trained on.
We show that this adaptation can happen with very little in-context training and informational prompting, a procedure which resembles the human learning process.

2
Approach

In this chapter, we will first lay out the foundations of the RO and ARO
problems that we are trying to address.
We will describe the necessary mathematical primitives that can be used
to transform an optimization formulation to a formulation that accounts for uncertainty
and finally to a tractable optimization formulation that is amenable to standard solution techniques.
Then, we will describe for both RO and ARO how we can use ChatGPT 3.5 to automate this process
using both in-context-learning and chain-of-thought prompting techniques.

2.1
Robust Optimization

In this section, we will first describe the necessary theoretical foundation
required to formulate RO problems and convert them into tractable optimization
formulations.
Then, we will attempt to teach ChatGPT how to use those tools in order to add uncertainty
to Linear Optimization problem, transform the problem into a tractable formulation
and then produce a code snippet that solves the problem.

2.1.1
Background

For the purpose of this work, we will concern ourselves with Linear Optimization problems (LO)
of the following form:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\text{ }

\displaystyle\bm{c}^{T}\bm{x}

(1)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle\bm{Ax}\leq\bm{b}

where
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\>\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In this setting, we assume that the data
(\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})
are uncertain,
but are known to reside in an uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
.
Hence as described in (
Bertsimas and
Den Hertog (2022)
),
Problem (
1
) is not a single deterministic problem,
but its rather a family of problems, one for each realization of the uncertain
parameters
(\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})
:

\min_{\bm{x}}\{\bm{c}^{T}\bm{x}\>|\>\bm{Ax}\leq\bm{b}\}_{(\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})\in\mathcal{U}}

(2)

Following (
Bertsimas and
Den Hertog (2022)
),
we make the assumption that the user has specified a particular uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
on the data
(\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})

and that the optimal solution
\bm{x}_{*}
has to satisfy the
inequality constraints
\bm{Ax}\leq\bm{b}
for all the values
\bm{A},\bm{b}
such that

(\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})\in\mathcal{U}
.
Under those assumptions, the RO problem (
2
) can be written
as the following deterministic optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x},t}\text{ }

\displaystyle t

(3)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle\bm{c}^{T}\bm{x}-t\leq 0,\quad(\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})\in\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{i}^{T}\bm{x}-b_{i}\leq 0,\quad(\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})\in\mathcal{U}

The resulting problem is an LO problem but with perhaps an infinite number of constraints (one for each
realization of the uncertainty parameters).
Also notice that although in (
2
) we had uncertainty both in the cost function and the constraints,
we now have an LO problem where the uncertainty is only in the constraints, as shown in (
3
).
This means that we only need to consider uncertainty in the constraints and not in the objective.

Additionally, it turns out that instead of considering uncertainty on the entire matrix
\bm{A}
and vector
\bm{b}

at once, we can instead consider row-wise uncertainty on the rows of
\bm{A}
individually, with independent constraints of the following form:

\bm{a}^{T}_{i}\bm{x}-b_{i}\leq 0,\quad\forall(\bm{a}_{i},b_{i})\in\mathcal{U}_{i}

(4)

where
\mathcal{U}_{i}
is a projection of
\mathcal{U}
into the uncertain parameters of the
i
-th constraint.
Finally, we can assume that
b_{i}
is certain and consider uncertainty only in the
\bm{a}_{i}
,
since if
b_{i}
is uncertain, the problem can be transformed into a new uncertain problem where only the variable
coefficients are uncertain.

Hence, although the original robust problem (
2
) had uncertainty in the objective,
in
\bm{A}
and in
\bm{b}
, in fact, we only need to consider problems with row-wise uncertainty on the coefficients
of the variable
\bm{x}
.
This means that from now on, we will only consider problems of the following form:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\text{ }

\displaystyle\bm{c}^{T}\bm{x}

(5)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle\bm{a}^{T}_{i}\bm{x}\leq b_{i},\quad\bm{a}_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}

since all of the uncertain problems of the form shown in (
2
) can be reduced to Problem (
5
).
Finally, following (
Bertsimas and
Den Hertog (2022)
), we rewrite Problem (
5
) as:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\text{ }

\displaystyle\bm{c}^{T}\bm{x}

(6)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle(\bar{\bm{a}}_{i}+\bm{Pz}_{i})^{T}\bm{x}\leq b_{i},\quad\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{i},\>i\in[m]

Let’s now introduce some results from (
Bertsimas and
Den Hertog (2022)
) that will help
us reformulate Problem (
6
) into a tractable optimization problem:

The indicator function of a set
\mathcal{S}
is denoted as:

\delta(\bm{x}|S)=\begin{cases}0,\quad&\text{ if }\bm{x}\in\mathcal{S}\\
\infty,\quad&\text{otherwise}\\
\end{cases}

(7)

Then, the conjugate function of
\delta(\bm{x}|S)
is defined as:

\delta^{*}(\bm{y}|\mathcal{S})=\sup_{\bm{x}}\{\bm{y}^{T}\bm{x}-\delta(\bm{x}|\mathcal{S})\}=\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{S}}\bm{y}^{T}\bm{x}

(8)

Then, it turns out that Problem (
6
) is equivalent to the following problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\text{ }

\displaystyle\bm{c}^{T}\bm{x}

(9)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{a}}^{T}_{i}\bm{x}+\delta^{*}(\bm{P}^{T}\bm{x}|\mathcal{Z}_{i})\leq b_{i},\quad\>i\in[m]

which is called the Robust Counterpart (RC) of the original problem.
Depending on the choice of the uncertainty sets
\mathcal{Z}_{i}
, Problem (
9
) can become
very tractable.
Let’s now focus on a single constraint

(\bar{\bm{a}}+\bm{Pz})^{T}\bm{x}\leq b,\quad\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}

(10)

and let’s see what the RC of the constraint is for some specific uncertainty sets.

The following results are based on (
Bertsimas and
Den Hertog (2022)
):

•

{p}
-norm uncertainty set:
If
\mathcal{Z}=\{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\>:\>\|\bm{z}\|\leq\rho\}
, then
\delta^{*}(\bm{P}^{T}\bm{x}|\mathcal{Z})=\rho\|\bm{Px}\|_{q}
where
1/q+1/p=1
.
Hence, the RC of constraint (
9
) is:

\bar{\bm{a}}^{T}\bm{x}+\rho\|\bm{x}\|_{q}\leq b

(11)

•

Polyhedral uncertainty set
: If
\mathcal{Z}=\{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\>:\>\bm{d}-\bm{Dz}\geq 0\}
then:

\displaystyle\delta^{*}(\bm{P}^{T}\bm{x}|\mathcal{Z})=\min_{\bm{u}}\text{ }

\displaystyle\bm{d}^{T}\bm{u}

(12)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle\bm{D}^{T}\bm{u}=\bm{P}^{T}\bm{x}

\displaystyle\bm{u}\geq 0

and thus the RC of constraint (
9
) is the set of constraints:

\begin{cases}\bar{\bm{a}}^{T}\bm{X}+\bm{d}^{T}\bm{u}\leq b\\
\bm{D}^{T}\bm{u}=\bm{P}^{T}\bm{x}\\
\bm{u}\geq 0\end{cases}

(13)

Notice that we removed the minimization since we have a less than or equal constraint
and we are just looking for a feasible
\bm{u}
that satisfies the constraints.

•

Intersection of uncertainty sets
: If
\mathcal{Z}=\cap_{i\in[p]}\mathcal{Z}_{i}
and some technical conditions are met, then we have:

\delta^{*}(\bm{P}^{T}\bm{x}|\mathcal{Z})=\min_{\bm{v}^{i},i\in[p]}\Bigg\{\sum_{i\in[p]}\delta^{*}(\bm{v}^{i}|\mathcal{Z}_{i})\>\Bigg|\>\sum_{i=1}^{p}\bm{v}^{i}=\bm{P}^{T}\bm{x})\Bigg\}

(14)

Hence, the RC of constraint (9) is the set of constraints:

\begin{cases}\bar{\bm{a}}^{T}\bm{x}+\sum_{i}\delta^{*}(\bm{v}^{i}|\mathcal{Z}_{i})\leq b\\
\sum_{i}\bm{w}^{i}=\bm{P}^{T}\bm{x}\end{cases}

(15)

2.1.2
ChatGPT for RO problems

In order to formulate and solve RO problems using ChatGPT,
we will use in-context learning (i.e. Prompt Engineering) so as
to introduce the concept of Robust Optimization and the necessary
toolkit required for such problems.
As part of our prompting scheme, we will utilize
the chain-of-thought prompting paradigm, in which we ask the
generative model to decompose a multi-step process into smaller,
intermediate steps.
This way, the model can allocate more computation to processes that
require more reasoning steps, which has shown to be very efficient,
especially in symbolic tasks (
Wei et al. (2023)
).

In particular, the steps we are going to follow are the following:

1.

Initial Problem Generation
: We first ask ChatGPT to generate a nominal Linear Optimization problem that doesn’t have
uncertainty in its parameters.
Apart from generic LO problems of the form ”
\text{minimize }\>\bm{c}^{T}\bm{x}\text{ s.t. }\bm{Ax}\leq\bm{b}
”,
ChatGPT can also generate other well-known optimization problems,
such as a Network Flow Problem, a Facility Location Problem,
a Diet Problem etc.
Additionally, the user may also specify a custom, never-seen-before problem that they may want to generate.

2.

Introduction of Uncertain Parameters
: We ask ChatGPT to reformulate the problem of the previous step so that it
also accounts for uncertainty in the problem data.
In this step, we also specify the types of uncertainty sets that we would like to account
for, which can perhaps be different for every constraint.
During this step, we also provide ChatGPT with a quick context-prompt about what is Robust Optimization,
how we should transform the constraints in order to account for uncertainty
and what are the available uncertainty sets.

3.

Formulation of the Robust Counterpart
: We ask ChatGPT to derive the robust counterpart of the problem of Step 2 in order
to transform the problem into a tractable optimization form.
In this step, we provide context-prompts with the value of
\delta^{*}(x|\mathcal{Z})
for different uncertainty sets,
as shown in Section
2.1.1
.
We also provide information about how to handle the combinations of uncertainty sets (e.g. intersection).

4.

Code Generation
: In the last part, we ask ChatGPT to generate a runnable Julia code that solves the problem using example data.
To do this, we also included a prompt that helps ChatGPT treat
L_{1}
and
L_{\infty}
norms (i.e. by linearizing them)
and
L_{2}
norms (by adding second-order cone constraints).

Out of the
4
steps we described above, we use a context prompts only in steps
2,3
and
4
,
since ChatGPT already knows how to formulate basic optimization problems (i.e. Step 1).
Examples of such context prompts are presented below:

•

Prompt for Step 2
: For this step, we use small variations of the following prompt:

{myprop}

Let’s assume that you have a linear optimization problem.
If you want to add robustness with respect to a parameter vector
\bm{a}
which is the coefficient vector of the variables in the constraints, then you first have to bring the inequality constraints that involve your parameter of interest to the form
\bm{a}^{T}\bm{x}<=\bm{b}
.
Then you can add robustness by transforming the constraint to
(\bm{a}+\bm{Pz})^{T}\bm{x}<=\bm{b}

\bm{z}\in Z
, where Z is the desired uncertainty set and P is a predefined parameter matrix.
Some common uncertainty sets
Z
are the
p

Z=\{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|\bm{z}\|_{p}\leq\rho\}

and the polyhedral uncertainty set
Z=\{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{d}-\bm{Dz}\geq 0\}
.

•

Prompts for Step 3
: For this step, we use variations of

The simpler one only includes a small family of uncertainty sets and doesn’t use support functions in deriving the robust counterparts:

{myprop}

{myprop}

2.2
Adaptive Robust Optimization

In this section, we will first give the necessary foundations of Adaptive Robust Optimization (ARO).
Then, we will proceed to see how ChatGPT can be used to convert optimization problems
to ARO problems, and then solve them using the Robust Optimization tools
we presented in the previous section.

2.2.1
Background

The main difference between RO and ARO problems, is that RO assumes
that all optimization variables correspond to here-and-now decisions,
while in ARO, the values of a subset of a decision variables are revealed,
only after the uncertain parameters have been revealed (
Bertsimas and
Den Hertog (2022)
).
Those variables are called wait-and-see variables and are
particularly useful in multi-period and dynamic problem settings.

In that setting, the general form of an ARO problem is shown below (
Bertsimas and
Den Hertog (2022)
):

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x},\bm{y}(\cdot)}\text{ }

\displaystyle\bm{c}^{T}\bm{x}+\max_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}\bm{d}^{T}\bm{y}(\bm{z})

(16)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle\bm{A}(\bm{z})\bm{x}+\bm{B}(\bm{z})\bm{y}(\bm{z})\leq\bm{\beta}(\bm{z}),\quad\forall\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle\bm{x}\geq\bm{0},\>\bm{y}(\bm{z})\geq\bm{0}

where
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
is the first-stage here-and-now decision that
is made before the uncertainty parameter
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
is realized,
\bm{y}(\bm{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
denotes the
second-stage wait-and-see decision that can be adjusted according to the realized
\bm{z}
, and
\bm{A}(\bm{z}),\bm{B}(\bm{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n_{2}}

are coefficient matrices linear in
\bm{z}
, and
\bm{\beta}(\bm{z})
denotes the
possibly uncertain right-hand-side which is also linear in
\bm{z}
.
An important special case is the fixed-recourse case, which appears when
\bm{B}(\bm{z})=\bm{B}
,
which means that the coefficients of the adaptive variable
\bm{y}
don’t depend on
\bm{z}
.

Let’s now focus on a single adaptive constraint of the following form:

(\bar{\bm{a}}+\bm{Pz})^{T}\bm{x}+\bm{d}^{T}\bm{y}(\bm{z})\leq b,\quad\forall\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}

(17)

Here we have assumed a constant RHS and fixed recourse.
Note that in (
16
), we are optimizing over both
\bm{x}
and over functions
\bm{y}(\bm{z})
, which are called decision rules.
It turns out that optimizing over all the functions
\bm{y}(\cdot)
leads to an NP-hard problem (
Bertsimas and
Den Hertog (2022)
).
For this reason, in order to make the problem tractable, we restrict
\bm{y}(\bm{z})
to a particular class
of functions.
Forcing the decision rule to a specific class of functions leads to an approximation and
doesn’t guarantee optimality, but it is necessary to make the problem tractable.

Hence, from now on, we will focus on the important special case where
\bm{y}(\bm{z})
is linear in
\bm{z}
:

\bm{y}(\bm{z})=\bm{u}+\bm{V}^{T}\bm{z}

(18)

If we apply the linear decision rule to our constraint, we get a classic RO problem where we only have to make here-and-now decisions:

(\bar{\bm{a}}+\bm{Pz})^{T}\bm{x}+\bm{d}^{T}(\bm{u}+\bm{V}^{T}\bm{z})\leq b,\quad\forall\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}

(19)

In this case, it can be proven that the constraint can be written in the following tractable way:

\bar{\bm{a}}^{T}\bm{x}+\bm{d}^{T}\bm{u}+\delta^{*}(\bm{P}^{T}\bm{x}+\bm{V}^{T}\bm{D}|\mathcal{Z})\leq b

(20)

where
\delta^{*}(\bm{x}|\mathcal{Z})
corresponds to the conjugate of the support function of the set
\mathcal{Z}
,
as presented in the Robust Optimization section.
Hence, we can use exactly the same tools as we used in the RO case in order to make the problem tractable.

2.2.2
ChatGPT for ARO problems

In order to formulate and solve ARO problems using ChatGPT, we will use very similar steps to the ones
we used in the RO case.
The steps are described below:

1.

Initial Problem Generation
: We first ask ChatGPT to generate a nominal Linear Optimization problem that doesn’t have
uncertain or adaptive variables.
Apart from generic LO problems of the form ”
\text{min }\>\bm{c}^{T}\bm{x}\text{ s.t. }\bm{Ax}\leq\bm{b}
”,
ChatGPT can also generate other well-known optimization problems,
such as a Network Flow Problem, a Facility Location Problem,
a Diet Problem etc.
Additionally, the user may also specify a custom, never-seen-before problem that they may want to generate.

2.

Introduction of Uncertain Parameters
:
We ask ChatGPT to reformulate the problem of the previous step so that it also includes adaptive variables
and uncertain parameters.
In this step, we also specify the types of uncertainty sets and decision rules that should be used.
During this step, we also provide ChatGPT with a quick context-prompt about what is Adaptive Robust Optimization,
and how we can add adaptive variables and account for uncertainty in the constraint.

3.

Formulation of the Robust Counterpart
: We ask ChatGPT to derive the adaptive robust counterpart of the problem of Step 2 in order
to transform the problem into a tractable optimization form.
This step assumes that a linear decision rule is used in the previous steps, since otherwise, it is not necessary that we can make
the problem tractable.
Hence, assuming an LDR, we provide ChatGPT with context-prompts with the value of
\delta^{*}(x|\mathcal{Z})
for different uncertainty sets,
as shown in Section
2.1.1
.
We also provide information about how to handle the combinations of uncertainty sets (e.g. intersection).

We will now present the prompts used for the different steps:

•

Prompt for Step 2
: For this step, we used small variations of the following prompts:

{myprop}

An adaptive robust optimization problem (ARO) is a problem of the following form:

\displaystyle\min_{x,y(z)}

\displaystyle c^{T}x+\max_{z\in Z}d^{T}y(z)

(21)

\displaystyle\text{s.t}

\displaystyle\text{ }A(z)x+B(z)y(z)<=b(z),\forall z\in\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle x}>=0,\bm{y}({z})>=0

Here, Z in an uncertainty set, x is a first-stage here-and-now decision that is made before the uncertainty z is realized, y(z) is the second-stage wait-and-see decision that can be adjusted according to the actual data (when z is realized), and A(z), B(z) are coefficient matrices linear in z.
Finally, b(z) is the (possibly uncertain) right hand side. Note that in this problem we are optimizing over all the functions y(z) and the vector x. One way to make such problems tractable is to force y(z) to belong to a particular class of functions. This process is called ”decision rule” (DR). For instance, forcing y(z) to be a linear function of z (i.e. y(z)=Uz+v) corresponds to a linear decision rule (LDR). An important special case of ARO is when B(z) is constant and doesn’t depend on z, in which case we say that we have constant recourse.

•

Prompt for Step 3
: For taking the Robust Counterpart, we used the following prompt:

{myprop}

Let’s now assume that we have 1 ARO constraint with fixed recourse of the following form:

(a+Pz)^{T}x+d^{T}y(z)<=b
, for all z in Z, where a, P and d are parameters. Note that a is a vector, P is a matrix, z is a vector, d is a vector and b is a scalar.
Let’s also assume that y is a linear decision rule (LDR) with
y=u+Vz
. Then, to make the problem tractable, the constraint can be written equivalently as:

a^{T}x+d^{T}u+d_{*}(P^{T}x+V^{T}d|Z)<=b
. This corresponds to the robust counterpart.
Here,
d_{*}(x|Z)
is the conjugate of the support function of the uncertainty set Z evaluated at x.

The following properties hold for the support function:

- If
Z
is the infinity norm ball (i.e.
Z=\{z:||z||_{\infty}<=r\}
then
d_{*}(x|Z)=r||x||_{1}
.

-If
Z
is the euclidean ball (i.e.
Z=\{z:||z||_{2}<=r\}
) then
d_{*}(x|Z)=r||x||_{2}
.

-If
Z=\{z:\sum_{i}a_{i}*e^{(d_{i})^{T}z}<=r
, then
d_{*}(x|Z)=\sum_{i}{v_{i}log(v_{i}/(ai*u)-v_{i})}
with the constraints that
Dv=P^{T}x,u>=0
and
v>=0
.

-If Z is the intersection of
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
, then
d_{*}(x|Z)
is not explicitly representable, but can be represented using the following constraints:
d_{*}(x|Z)=d_{*}(w_{1}|Z_{1})+d_{*}(w_{2}|Z_{2})
and
x=w1+w2
. Please don’t forget to add both constraints when taking the robust counterpart.

\displaystyle a^{T}x+P^{T}xz+d^{T}u+d^{T}Vz

\displaystyle\leq b

Expression for
d^{T}
:

Let’s express
d^{T}
in terms of its components:
d=[d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n}]
.

\displaystyle a^{T}x+P^{T}xz+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(d_{i}^{T}u+d_{i}^{T}Vz)

\displaystyle\leq b

\displaystyle a^{T}x+P^{T}xz+\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{T}u+\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{T}Vz

\displaystyle\leq b

Conjugate of Support Function
:
Recall that the conjugate of the support function
d^{*}_{Z}(x)
of an uncertainty set
Z
evaluated at
x
is given by:

d^{*}_{Z}(x)=\max_{z\in Z}\{x^{T}z\}

Applying this definition to our ARO constraint, and using the linear decision rule
y(z)=u+Vz
, we have:

\displaystyle d^{*}_{Z}(P^{T}x+V^{T}d|Z)

\displaystyle=\max_{z\in Z}\{(P^{T}x+V^{T}d)^{T}z\}

\displaystyle=\max_{z\in Z}\{x^{T}Pz+d^{T}Vz\}

Substituting in Constraint
:
Substituting the conjugate of the support function back into our constraint, we get:

a^{T}x+d^{T}u+\max_{z\in Z}\{x^{T}Pz+d^{T}Vz\}\leq b

Final Form
:
The constraint in its final form becomes:

a^{T}x+d^{T}u+\max_{z\in Z}\{x^{T}Pz+d^{T}Vz\}\leq b

This is the formulation of the ARO constraint with fixed recourse, linear decision rule, and linear coefficients that are linear in
z
, along with a constant right-hand side.

3
Limitations

Through the various prompting examples we presented, we showed that
ChatGPT, in a variety of scenarios, can take optimization problems, account for uncertainty through RO and ARO,
reformulate the resulting problems using tractable optimization formulations and in some cases, even generate code
to solve the resulting robust counterparts.
However, the presented approach comes with its own sets of limitations, some of which
we will try to present in this section:

•

Lack of consistency
:

One of the main limitations of the approach presented is that ChatGPT doesn’t always produce consistent results.
In particular, different interactions with ChatGPT with exactly the same prompts yields different responses,
despite the fact that those responses are generally similar.
In addition to that, it turns out that in some of those responses, ChatGPT may produce incorrect formulations
or executable Julia codes, despite the fact that those mistakes often don’t deviate too much from the correct results.
In the example below, we give the same prompt as input to
2
different instances of ChatGPT,
which leads us to
2
slightly different answers.
Although both of those answers are correct, different answers to the same prompts may provide different context for
subsequent prompts, thus amplifying the differences of later stages of the pipeline even further.

Applying mathematical theorems
:
Another area where ChatGPT may produce inconsistent results is in identifying which mathematical theorems should be used and then actually applying those theorems.
For instance, in the example below, ChatGPT is asked to use an uncertainty set that is the intersection of a
2

In this case, ChatGPT is able to correctly calculate the support functions for the
2

However, it doesn’t use the correct theorem to combine the uncertainty sets.
It uses the property for the Minkowski sum of uncertainty sets,
instead of the property for the intersection of uncertainty sets.
This leads to an incorrect Robust counterpart of the form
a^{T}x+r_{1}\|P^{T}x\|_{1}+r_{2}\|P^{T}x\|_{2}\leq b
instead of the correct one

a^{T}x+r_{1}\|w_{1}\|_{1}+r_{2}\|w_{2}\|_{2}\leq b
, where
w_{1}+w_{2}=P^{T}x
.

To add robust optimization to the given linear programming problem with uncertainty in the constraint, you should follow these steps:

1. Express the constraint with parameter ’a’ in the form
a^{T}x<=b
.
Given constraint:
3x_{1}+x_{2}<=10

We can rewrite this as:
a^{T}x<=b
, where
a=[3,1]
and
b=10
.

2. Define the two uncertainty sets:

- Infinity-norm ball (Z1):
Z1=\{z:||z||_{\infty}<=r\}

- 2-norm uncertainty set (Z2):
Z2=\{z:||z||_{2}<=r\}
.

3. Construct the robust counterpart for each uncertainty set separately:

a. For Z1 (Infinity-norm ball):

d_{*}(x|Z1)=r*||x||_{1}
(from the properties)
The robust counterpart for Z1 is:
a^{T}x+r*||P^{T}x||_{1}<=b

b. For Z2 (2-norm ball):

d_{*}(x|Z2)=r*||x||_{2}
(from the properties)
The robust counterpart for Z2 is:
a^{T}x+r*||P^{T}x||_{2}<=b

4. Now, you need to combine the two robust counterparts for Z1 and Z2. Since the overall uncertainty set is the intersection of these two, you can add them together:

a^{T}x+r1*||P^{T}x||_{1}+r2*||P^{T}x||_{2}<=b
where r1 and r2 are the parameters for Z1 and Z2, respectively.

5. However, we can’t directly add these two terms because they have different norms. To handle this, you need to use the Minkowski sum property. The Minkowski sum of two sets Z1 and Z2 is a new set Z such that Z =
\{z:z=z_{1}+z_{2},z_{1}\in Z_{1},z_{2}\in Z_{2}\}
.

d_{*}(x|Z)=d_{*}(x|Z1)+d_{*}(x|Z2)
.
So,
d_{*}(x|Z)=r1*||P^{T}x||_{1}+r2*||P^{T}x||_{2}

6. Now, you can construct the robust counterpart for the intersection of Z1 and Z2:

a^{T}x+d_{*}(x|Z)<=b

a^{T}x+r1*||P^{T}x||_{1}+r2*||P^{T}x||_{2}<=b

This is the robust counterpart for the given linear programming problem with the intersection of a 2-norm uncertainty set and an infinity-norm ball uncertainty set.

4
Conclusion

In this work we demonstrated the power of ChatGPT in formulating and solving
Robust and Adaptive Robust Optimization problems.
In particular, through a variety of examples,
we showcased the promise of ChatGPT in taking optimization problems,
introducing user-specified uncertainty, converting the resulting RO problem into a tractable
optimization formulation and finally, producing executable code that solves
the resulting robust counterpart.
What makes those results surprising is that although ChatGPT wasn’t originally trained on RO and ARO problems,
it is able to adapt to those advanced scientific domains through limited prompt engineering and in-context learning.

On the other hand, we also showed that in some cases, despite the specialized prompting,
ChatGPT makes small errors in the execution of the various steps.
In many cases, those errors stem from the inherent weaknesses of Large Language Models in manipulating
mathematical theorems and algebraic expressions.

Nevertheless, despite its weaknesses, our work aims at helping popularize Mathematical Optimization
and specifically Robust and Adaptive Optimization to non-expert users.
We want to showcase that Large Language Models can potentially be used to increase the accessibility
of such expert-dominated fields to non-expert stakeholders.
In particular, LLMs that have been adapted to such specialized Optimization fields can (i) act as a helpful
learning resource for non-experts, and (ii) help users with limited knowledge of the field easily
formulate and solve their problems without requiring much domain expertise.

Such endeavors may serve to increase the visibility of the field of Mathematical Optimization, especially among people
with limited prior exposure to the subject.
After all, the ubiquitous availability of Machine Learning packages that can be used with minimal expertise,
has helped ML soar in popularity over the recent years.
Hence, the integration of LLMs with Optimization workflows and the development of methods that produce
Optimization formulations from natural language has the potential to reduce the barrier of entry
into Mathematical Optimization, thus making the field more popular among both experts and non-experts.
