Screening for diabetic retinopathy with artificial intelligence in a primary care setting: a comparative cost analysis
Authors: Livio Di Matteo, Noelle Whitestone, Vishaal Bhambhwani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCost analysis of autonomous artificial intelligence (AI)-based screening of diabetic retinopathy (DR) for adults with diabetes at a primary care clinic.Methods and analysisThis study provides a comparative cost analysis of actual results using AI-based DR screening with counterfactual results based on all patients going through the physician-based referral system. A cost analysis is conducted using cost data from published sources, provincial billing codes, statistical sources, and patient characteristics from a clinical study to compare autonomous AI-based screening for DR versus physician-based screening. Costs considered include direct costs of operating the AI system, physician fees, and indirect costs to patient time. Along with total cost comparisons, a cost per DR case detected is estimated and a sensitivity analysis based on variations in AI costs is provided.ResultsOver the study period, 202 participants were screened for DR using autonomous AI. The majority (93.6%, n=189) of AI-based DR screening exams were completed successfully. The AI-based scenario results in total direct costs of $C7919.04 and indirect costs of $C5728.80, resulting in total costs of $C13 647.84 per 100 patients. The traditional physician-based approach results in total direct costs of $C8240 and indirect costs of $C19 998.09, resulting in total costs of $C28 238.09 for 100 patients. When costs are converted to costs per unit outcome, the total cost per diagnosed DR case is $C620.36 for the AI-based approach and $C1283.55 for the physician-based approach; the AI-based cost per diagnosed case was 52% lower.ConclusionGiven the lower cost per diagnosed case of the AI-based approach, there are advantages to the implementation of AI-based screening for DR.

Introduction

The global burden of diabetes is increasing, and it is estimated that diabetes contributed to over $C1.3 trillion in global health expenditure in 2021, a 316% increase over the last 15 years.1 In Canada, it is estimated that one in ten people are living with diabetes, with a cost to the healthcare system in Ontario in 2021 of $C1.2 billion.2 Diabetic retinopathy (DR), a complication of diabetes, is the leading cause of vision loss for working age adults.1 3 However, early detection and treatment for DR can prevent vision loss.4 5 The traditional approach to screening for DR in Canada involves family physician referrals to ophthalmologists for a diabetic eye exam. In recent years, the development of non-mydriatic fundus imaging systems and autonomous artificial intelligence (AI) platforms has led to screening model alternatives that may potentially reduce the burden on healthcare systems by providing accurate initial screenings.6–10

Using approaches from the economic evaluation of healthcare,11 there is growing literature on the cost effectiveness of AI-based DR screening relative to traditional specialist-based diagnostic approaches. The evidence to date on cost savings, using a variety of economic evaluation approaches and across a diversity of populations, varies from mixed to encouraging, with results affected by the sensitivity of the AI-based screening system and the cost of screening using traditional approaches across different healthcare systems.12–17

This economic evaluation is a cost analysis using a prospective cohort study6 from real-world on-ground implementation of AI for DR screening, where consenting adult participants previously diagnosed with diabetes were screened for DR using non-mydriatic retinal imaging with autonomous AI interpretation at their routine primary care appointment in Thunder Bay, Ontario, Canada.

Methods

The study is conducted from both a health system and a societal perspective. The method employed is an economic evaluation of two approaches—comparing actual screening results using AI-based DR screening with counterfactual results based on all patients going through the traditional physician-based approach—that generates a comparative cost analysis based on costs per DR case detected. This study generally conforms to the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 reporting guidelines for economic evaluations.

Study design

This is a comparative cost analysis study.

Study population

Patients aged 18 years and older, diagnosed with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, presenting to their primary care provider for a routine appointment at a clinic in Thunder Bay, Ontario, from December 2022 through October 2023 were consecutively offered a screening exam for DR using autonomous AI.

Consenting participants had retinal images taken by a technician at the clinic using the iCare (Vigonza, Italy) DRSPlus fundus imaging system, which captures one disc-centred and one macula-centred image of each retina. Images were uploaded to a Health Canada-approved autonomous AI platform, EyeArt by Eyenuk. (Woodland Hills, California, USA),18 which produced a report recommending referral to an ophthalmologist when more than mild retinopathy was detected. Participants that screened negative for DR were recommended to screen in a year, based on national screening guidelines.3 If initial images were not of sufficient quality, photographs were retaken for analysis. If needed, following multiple imaging attempts, a pupil dilation protocol (tropicamide 1% and phenylephrine 2.5% eyedrops, one drop in each eye) was offered to improve the quality of images. If images of sufficient quality could not be captured, the patient was referred to an ophthalmologist.

Details regarding the original study results including success of screening with the AI system, positive DR screenings and ophthalmologist exam referrals and patient satisfaction/experience questionnaires were previously published.6

This present study uses data from the original published study.6 The present analysis compares the cost of AI-based diagnosis of DR for individuals with diabetes, with referral to and exam by an ophthalmologist only for positive cases, versus the traditional approach of family physician visits followed by referral to an ophthalmologist for all patients. Thus, in the AI-based approach, only patients with a positive result are referred whereas in the traditional approach, all are referred.

Cost components

Direct costs

For scenario 1 (the AI-based screening approach), the cost of the fundus imaging system and laptop to access the AI platform (purchase, maintenance, training) are considered capital costs and treated as ‘sunk costs’—that is a value of zero—with the only cost, therefore, being the cost per exam/operating cost. In economics, sunk costs can be interpreted as ‘bygones are bygones’, implying that operating decisions going forward are made at this margin. This is why sunk costs are assumed to be zero. Moreover, there are ‘sunk’ costs to set up an ophthalmology practice as well (space rent, staff expenses, equipment expenses etc). Thus realistically, both physician-based and AI approaches have sunk costs; we are assuming they are zero for both.

There is not yet a provincial health plan reimbursement rate for AI-based DR exams in Canada; so for an estimate, data from the USA is used. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services19 billing code for AI DR exams in the USA for 2024 (Code CPT 92229) is US$40.94 (with 2025 proposed at US$43.80). At an exchange rate of US$0.72 cents for $C1,20 the US$40.94 fee would be $C56.86. It should be noted that the conversion to Canadian dollars based on the US dollar fee because of the unavailability of a Canadian reimbursement code does introduce an element of uncertainty into the calculations, but we provide additional results with a wider sensitivity range.

The cost of the family physician’s time for the visit and AI-based screening were not considered as the AI screening was part of their regular appointment. The cost of referral visits to ophthalmologists for positive exams plus the proportion for whom the AI screening exam could not be completed results in a total referral rate based on total cases.

For scenario 2 (the traditional approach), the initial visit to the family physician is similar to the AI-based approach; these would be equivalent and a part of the routine regular appointment, and thus need not be considered. The cost of the ophthalmologist’s visit for all referred patients is included, in which the current Ontario Health Insurance Payment (OHIP) fee is $C82.40 for DR screening consults.21

Indirect costs

These would be for both the AI-based and the traditional approach and involve assigning a value based on average income/wages to the time involved for the visit (as a measure of value of time foregone). For AI-based screening by family physician staff, we assume an additional ten minutes per patient for the visit valued at the average industrial hourly wage. For the traditional ophthalmology visit with dilating eye drops, we assume 2 hours for the visit plus 2 hours of recovery time for a total of 4 hours of time. For all patients the value of time represents opportunity foregone to do other things with the time and would be valued at $C28.64 per hour.22 As well, for those who are employed, the assumption is made that they would take a half day off work (4 hours) valued at $C28.64 per hour. The assumption here is that someone who is employed is not only incurring the opportunity cost of the time foregone but also a day of market income. Average hourly earnings including overtime for hourly employees for Ontario for the period January 2022 to December 2023 is $C28.64. The value of caregiver time is also valued at the same hourly rate. Again, it should be noted that the base assumptions for visit time and work hours foregone reflect Northern Ontario as a rural-remote and underserviced area with trips often requiring significant travel as well as long waiting periods given the number of patients per specialist. Again, sensitivity results are offered using lower time off work and visit time estimates.

Additionally, the value of commuting time to the ophthalmology visits needs to be factored in not only for the individual patient but also their caregiver. According to Statistics Canada,23 the average commuting time for a single driver in a car, truck or van in Thunder Bay was 16.6 min while the average public transit commute by bus was 30.7 min. This commuting time can also be valued using the average hourly earnings of $C28.64.

Cost analysis

Total direct and indirect costs for both AI-based approach (based on data characteristics) and the traditional screening approach were calculated. The would-be synthetic estimate assumes 100 patients with the characteristics applied from the collected data set. In the AI-based scenario, all would be screened using AI, and the proportion positive of those whose test was completed would be referred to the ophthalmologist. As well, the proportion whose test was uncertain or could not be completed would also be referred to the ophthalmologist. In the traditional scenario, all 100 would be referred to the ophthalmologist. The scenarios are summarised in the online supplemental appendix. Total direct and indirect costs and costs per detected case of DR (cost per case ratio) were calculated.

Results

Over the study period, a total of 202 participants, mean age 70.8 years (SD±11.7), 38.6% (n=78) female, were screened for DR. Most participants 88.1% (n=179) were Caucasian and resided in Thunder Bay (99%, n=200), of which 94.5% (n=189) arrived using a private vehicle (and we will assume the remaining 5.5% took public transit). 29.2% (n=59) of participants reported being currently employed. Approximately one-third (35.6%, n=72) attended their appointment with a caregiver with the remaining two-thirds (64.4%, n=130) attending their appointments on their own. Most participants (74.8%, n=151) reported having type 2 diabetes, 21.8% (n=44) were unsure and the remaining (3.47%, n=7) reported being diagnosed with type 1 diabetes (table 1).

Base case

The results of the base case are provided in online supplemental appendix A1. The AI-based scenario results in total direct costs of $C7919.04 ($C5686 for AI screening and $C2233 for ophthalmologist fees) and indirect costs of $C5728.80, resulting in total costs of $C13 647.84 per 100 patients. The traditional physician-based approach results in total direct costs of $C8240 (all for ophthalmologist fees) and indirect costs of $C19 998.09, resulting in total costs of $C28 238.09 for 100 patients.

When costs are converted to costs per case outcome, the total cost per diagnosed DR case is $C620.36 for the AI-based approach and $C1283.55 for the traditional physician-based approach. The AI-based cost per diagnosed case was 52% lower.

Modified base case

Screening and diagnosis of patients with DR in certain circumstances is also accompanied by a retinal optical coherence tomography (OCT) to look for macular oedema. The AI system detects macular oedema without any additional costs whereas for the traditional approach, an additional cost for the OCT is incurred. This is not needed for all patients, but conservatively we can assume that up to one-third of patients need to be scanned.24 There is an additional OHIP billing fee of $C35 for the OCT.21 In this case, the total cost per diagnosed DR case is $C634.58 for the AI-based approach and $C1336.05 for the traditional physician-based approach. The AI-based cost per diagnosed case was 53% lower.

Revised AI fee (50% lower)

The AI fees used for our study are an estimate based on a US fee schedule. One can expect that the price eventually negotiated by public health plans in Canada that adopt the technology could be lower, given their buying power. Indeed, when the new AI screening code was first announced in the USA in 2021, it was at a price of US$54.51, but other Medicare administrative contractors priced the service much lower at rates ranging from US$29.45 for Ohio to US$28.42 for Kentucky.25 This suggests that a price up to nearly 50% less than the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services recommended price is possible.

An additional cost scenario is presented here, where all the assumptions are the same as the modified base case except that the AI-based test fee is assumed to be 50% lower at $C28.43. The results as presented in online supplemental appendix A2 show that the direct costs of the AI-based approach are now $C5392.21—a 32% reduction from $C7919.04. As a result, the total cost per diagnosed case of DR falls from $C620.36 to $C505.50 for the AI-based scenario—a 19% reduction. In this final scenario, the total cost per diagnosed case of DR via the AI-based approach is now 62% lower than the traditional physician-based approach.

Discussion

The results of this comparative analysis show that the AI-based approach has a lower cost per case than the traditional physician-based approach when screening for DR. When reviewing the base case, the bulk of the cost difference is in indirect costs to patients and caregivers in terms of income foregone and opportunity cost of their time, given that in the AI approach a much smaller proportion require an additional visit for specialist screening. The direct costs are lower in the AI screening approach as well, but these could be lower or higher based on the actual AI screening fee ultimately negotiated by the provincial government public health plan as well as cost changes over time as the technology is diffused. Thus, it should be acknowledged that the differences in cost per case are more rooted in costs to patients rather than direct healthcare expenditures.

When costs are converted to costs per case outcome, the AI-based cost per diagnosed case was 52% lower. Again, the difference is mainly due to the large difference in indirect time-based costs to patients—which is something that is of importance to patients based on their survey results ranking their experience with the AI approach in the original published study. Patients like not having to wait or come in for an additional specialist visit and not having to use dilating eyedrops with its consequences on their productivity for the next few hours.6 The results are paralleled in the modified base case that includes the retinal OCT screening, with the AI-based cost per diagnosed case being 53% lower than the traditional physician-based approach. In a subsequent scenario in which the AI exam fee is revised to 50% less, the AI-based total cost per diagnosed case of DR falls by 20% relative to the modified base case. In this final result the total cost per diagnosed case of DR via the AI-based approach is now 62% lower than the traditional physician-based approach. In this scenario, the AI-based approach represents substantial savings in terms of both direct as well as indirect costs. As well, additional results were calculated assuming lower hours per visit and lower hours of work and income foregone combined with higher AI fees, and these results also support the AI-based approach having a lower cost per case relative to the physician-based approach.

DR screening programmes have been shown to be cost-effective,26 and using autonomous AI for DR screening has been shown to be lower cost in other countries.13–16 Notably, one of the initial studies to evaluate cost-effectiveness of AI-based DR screening in Australia, a country with universal, publicly funded healthcare similar to Canada, demonstrated net savings for the health system and the potential for improved equity in access to care and health outcomes.16

Our study, the first based in Canada, demonstrates a screening strategy using autonomous AI that has a high sensitivity and good specificity for the detection of referrable DR,27 compared with the traditional ophthalmologist approach, and is shown to result in lower cost per case diagnosis. Given the projected shortages of ophthalmologists28 in Canada and long wait lists,29 decreasing the number of patients requiring specialist time relieves additional health system challenges and has been shown to increase specialist productivity,30 while also increasing adherence to recommended screening guidelines.

Our study has some limitations. First, it is based on data from a single primary care office. The sample size is relatively small and our cohort included predominantly older Caucasian patients. Generalisability to broader Canadian populations, remote communities, Indigenous populations or younger working-age cohorts is therefore limited. While the mean age of the population screened in this study was 70.5 years, screening a younger population with a higher employment rate would likely mean additional savings in terms of both direct and indirect costs. AI-based screening also has the potential to improve access to care for more remote and under-served communities. Second, our cost analysis includes some assumptions which we provide rationale for throughout the manuscript. These include assumptions on sunk costs, time forgone for appointments, and the use of US billing codes for AI fees. We also provide additional sensitivity analyses to ensure accurate presentation of possible different scenarios. Third, the cost-effectiveness of the AI-based approach is dependent both on the sensitivity/specificity/positive and negative predictive values of the AI platform as well as the characteristics of the population being screened. Our study does not evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of the AI programme used; however, as discussed in the manuscript, these have been widely published in the literature before. As the world gains more experience in the use of this technology, we may find the ‘ideal’/most cost-effective way of using AI-based DR screening in different populations. One example is that to avoid patients with false positives on AI screening having to travel for ophthalmologist appointments, we could have an ophthalmologist review just the fundus images of positive AI screens first, which would decrease indirect costs. Despite these limitations, our study is still one of the first comparative cost studies on AI-based DR detection in Canada using real-world data, which can support the increased uptake and implementation of AI-based DR screening programmes in the country.

Conclusion

This analysis compared the costs of AI-based screening of DR for individuals with diabetes, with exams by an ophthalmologist on positive cases versus the traditional approach of family physician visits followed by referral to an ophthalmologist for all patients. Costs per diagnosed case of DR ranged from 52% to 62% lower for the AI-based approach relative to the physician-based approach. Given the cost per case advantage of the AI-based approach, combined with its feasibility and high levels of patient satisfaction, this suggests significant advantages to the implementation of AI-based screening for DR for both physicians and patients.
