Electrocardiographic Dynamism in Arrhythmia Practice
Authors: Tuğba Aktemur Özalp
Published: 2026

Dynamic electrocardiographic (ECG) changes remain among the most clinically relevant and potentially deceptive aspects of arrhythmia practice. Among these changes, inherited channelopathies, particularly Brugada syndrome, continue to occupy a central place because delayed recognition may have fatal consequences. Brugada syndrome is an inherited arrhythmogenic disorder classically associated with ST-segment elevation in the right precordial leads and an increased risk of malignant ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death. Importantly, however, its ECG phenotype is not static; dynamic and atypical presentations are well recognized.1,2 In this context, atypical Brugada presentations deserve special emphasis. Although the classical pattern involves leads V1 to V2, inferior lead ST-segment abnormalities have also been described in the literature. Such reports are important because they remind the clinician that diagnosis should not be reduced to a fixed textbook image. Rather, ECG findings must be interpreted together with symptoms, especially syncope, and with a family history of sudden cardiac death or arrhythmic events.1, 2, 3, 4 The dynamic nature of Brugada-related ECG findings is particularly important in emergency practice. Transient normalization or fluctuation of ST-segment abnormalities may obscure the diagnosis when clinical judgment relies on a single ECG tracing. For that reason, patients presenting with high-risk syncope and a suspicious personal or family history should be monitored closely even when the initial ECG appears incomplete, intermittent, or atypical. In Brugada syndrome, the ECG is not a fixed phenotype; it is a moving target.1,5

A similar principle applies to J-wave patterns. J-wave syndromes, including Brugada syndrome and early repolarization syndrome, represent another important domain in which dynamic ECG findings may carry substantial prognostic significance. In acquired settings, hypothermia remains one of the best-known causes of prominent J waves or Osborn waves. These findings are not merely descriptive. Their presence may be associated with ventricular arrhythmogenesis and should therefore prompt rapid clinical recognition and correction of the underlying cause.6,7 Hypothermia-related ECG abnormalities may include not only J waves, but also bradyarrhythmia, conduction delay, QRS complex widening, and QT prolongation. Importantly, these changes are often reversible after rewarming, thus making them a clear example of the dynamic relationship between systemic physiology and cardiac electrophysiology. This is also relevant in perioperative and postoperative settings, where hypothermia may be iatrogenic rather than environmental. Accordingly, J-wave appearance in such patients should trigger reassessment of temperature status, metabolic profile, conduction parameters, and arrhythmic risk.7,8

Drug-induced dynamic ECG changes are equally important. Propafenone, a class IC antiarrhythmic agent used for atrial fibrillation and supraventricular tachycardia, exerts its effect predominantly through sodium-channel blockade. In toxic or susceptible settings, this effect may lead to PR prolongation, QRS complex widening, QT-related changes, and wide-complex tachycardia. In the emergency department, such a presentation may easily be misinterpreted as ventricular tachycardia or another primary arrhythmic process if the patient’s medication history is not reviewed carefully.9 From a therapeutic standpoint, recognition of sodium-channel blocker toxicity is immediately actionable. Sodium bicarbonate remains a cornerstone of treatment when significant QRS complex widening, dysrhythmia, or hemodynamic instability is present. Case reports of severe propafenone toxicity have documented marked clinical and ECG improvement after bicarbonate therapy, likely through increased extracellular sodium concentration and reduced drug-channel binding in an alkalinized environment. Missing the diagnosis is dangerous; recognizing it may be lifesaving.9,10

Another striking acquired example of dynamic ECG deterioration is propofol-related infusion syndrome. Although uncommon, this syndrome is a catastrophic complication typically observed in critically ill patients receiving prolonged or high-dose propofol infusions. Reviews consistently identify infusion durations longer than 48 hours and doses greater than approximately 4 mg/kg per hour among the major risk factors, together with critical illness and additional metabolic stressors. The underlying pathophysiology is thought to involve mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired fatty acid oxidation, energy depletion, and cellular hypoxia, which may culminate in fatal cardiovascular collapse.11, 12, 13

Taken together, these observations support a central message: ECG findings should not be approached as static images, but rather as evolving biological signals shaped by genotype, temperature, drugs, metabolism, and time. For clinicians in emergency departments, intensive care units, and electrophysiology practice, this issue has direct practical implications. Atypical or transient ECG abnormalities should not be dismissed simply because they do not conform to classical teaching. Conversely, alarming tracings should always be interpreted within the full clinical context, including symptoms, exposure history, medication review, family history, and the reversibility of the underlying condition.1,5,6,9,11 In that sense, the essential lesson is simple: the ECG is dynamic, the patient is dynamic, and our diagnostic reasoning must be equally dynamic.1,5,6
