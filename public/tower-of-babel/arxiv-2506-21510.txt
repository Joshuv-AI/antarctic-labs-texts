Joint Scheduling of DER under Demand Charges: Structure and Approximation * Equal contribution. A preliminary version of this paper was published as a conference paper at the 2025 IEEE Power & Energy Society General Meeting (PESGM), Austin, TX, USA, July 2025 [ 1 ] .
Ruixiao Yang * , Gulai Shen * , Ahmed S. Alahmed , Chuchu Fan

Abstract
We study the joint scheduling of behind-the-meter distributed energy resources (DERs), including flexible loads, renewable generation, and battery energy storage systems, under net energy metering frameworks with demand charges. The problem is formulated as a stochastic dynamic program aimed at maximizing expected operational surplus while accounting for renewable generation uncertainty. We analytically characterize the structure of the optimal control policy and show that it admits a threshold-based form. However, due to the strong temporal coupling of the storage and demand charge constraints, the number of conditional branches in the policy scales combinatorially with the scheduling horizon, as it requires a look-ahead over future states. To overcome the high computational complexity in the general formulation, an efficient approximation algorithm is proposed, which searches for the peak demand under a mildly relaxed problem. We show that the algorithm scales linearly with the scheduling horizon. Extensive simulations using two open-source datasets validate the proposed algorithm and compare its performance against different DER control strategies, including a reinforcement learning-based one. Under varying storage and tariff parameters, the results show that the proposed algorithm outperforms various benchmarks in achieving a relatively small solution gap compared to the theoretical upper bound.

I-B

Summary of Results and Contributions

This paper advances research on optimal scheduling of BTM DERs with four key contributions:

•

We develop a stochastic dynamic programming framework that jointly optimizes flexible demand and BESS scheduling under NEM and demand charge constraints, maximizing prosumer operational surplus.

•

We show that the optimal policy maintains a threshold structure consistent with previous work
[
4
]
while demonstrating how the integration of multiple factors, such as demand flexibility, battery storage, and demand charges under bi-directional power flow leads to prohibitive computational costs of the optimal policy.

•

To overcome the prohibitive computational costs of the optimal solution, we propose an efficient algorithm that optimally searches for the prosumer’s peak demand under a mildly relaxed problem by leveraging key derived properties from the dynamic programming formulation.

•

Through extensive simulations against baseline approaches and a theoretical maximum, we demonstrate how a computationally efficient algorithm outperforms the benchmarks, including a proximal policy optimization-based reinforcement learning algorithm.

The works in
[
4
,
18
]
are most closely related to ours, as both adopt a stochastic dynamic programming framework for the prosumer decision problem. Our approach departs from
[
4
]
in two key aspects: (i) we model demand as flexible and jointly optimize it with battery storage, and (ii) we allow for bi-directional power and monetary flows between the prosumer and the DU under asymmetric import and export prices. Compared to
[
18
]
, our model incorporates demand charges in addition to the volumetric charge, introducing stronger temporal coupling across decision stages. Furthermore, unlike both
[
4
,
18
]
, our work proposes an efficient approximation algorithm that achieves near-optimal performance, as validated by simulations against a theoretical upper bound.

II
Problem Formulation

Fig. 1:
DER setup with flexible demands
d\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, renewable DG
g\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, storage operation
e\in\mathbb{R}
, and net-consumption
z\in\mathbb{R}

We study a HEMS for prosumers that co-optimizes flexible demand, storage operation, and renewable distributed generation under a NEM tariff with distinct buy/sell rates and peak demand charges. The optimization objective maximizes the operational surplus defined as consumption utility minus electricity costs, where demand charges are levied on the maximum consumption drawn from either load, battery charging, or both. As shown in Fig.
1
, the energy storage and flexible loads are jointly scheduled to reduce the demand charge by shaving the load curve, and reduce the volumetric charge by arbitraging NEM rates, while still satisfying prosumer’s consumption.

Prosumer Resources

We consider the operation over a discrete and finite time horizon
t=0,1,\ldots,T
. As shown in Fig.
1

g_{t}

The

II-C

NEM X Tariff with Demand Charges

Under NEM, when the prosumer’s gross loads (from consumption and storage charging) are higher than its generation (from renewable DG), the prosumer pays at the retail rate. Otherwise, the prosumer gets compensated at the export rate.

Let
(p_{t}^{+}\geq p_{t}^{-},p)\geq 0
be the retail, export, and peak demand prices, respectively. We equivalently break down the peak demand charge at the end of time
T
to each time step

pc_{T}=p\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}[z_{t}-c_{t}]^{+}
. Thus, we can write the payment (cost) under NEM X in
[
3
]

P_{t}(z_{t},c_{t})=p_{t}^{+}[z_{t}]^{+}-p_{t}^{-}[z_{t}]^{-}+p[z_{t}-c_{t}]^{+},

(5)

where
P_{t}(\cdot)>0
(
P_{t}(\cdot)<0
) indicates that the prosumer pays (gets compensated) to (by) the DU.

II-E

DER Co-Optimization Problem

To optimally schedule its DER, the prosumer solves a dynamic program that maximizes its reward function by explicitly accounting for the temporal coupling of storage operations and an NEM tariff with bidirectional energy pricing and peak demand charges.

The reward function is defined as the difference between the utility of consumption (
6
) and payment under NEM and demand charges (
5
). Therefore, the reward function is

\displaystyle r_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})=r_{t}((s_{t},g_{t},c_{t}),(e_{t},\mathbf{d}_{t}))=U_{t}(\bm{d}_{t})-P_{t}(z_{t},c_{t}),\quad t=0,\ldots,T-1.

(7)

To encourage the preservation of energy in the battery, we define a linear reward to the remaining energy at the terminal stage
T
,
i.e.

r_{T}(x_{T})=r_{T}(s_{T},g_{T},c_{T})=\gamma s_{T},

(8)

where
\gamma>0
is the value assigned to the remaining charge in the storage at the end of the horizon.

The policy
\pi=(\pi_{0},\ldots,\pi_{T-1})
is a sequence of mapping from state space to action space such that
\pi_{t}(x_{t})=u_{t}
. Given initial state
x_{0}=(s,g,0)
, the storage-consumption co-optimization is then defined as:

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}r_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\right]

(9a)

Subject to

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all}\ t=0,1,\ldots,T-1

\displaystyle x_{0}=(s,g,0)

(9b)

\displaystyle u_{t}=(e_{t},\bm{d}_{t})=\pi_{t}(x_{t})

(9c)

\displaystyle e_{t}\in[-\underline{e},\overline{e}]

(9d)

\displaystyle s_{t+1}=s_{t}+\tau[e_{t}]^{+}-[e_{t}]^{-}/\rho

(9e)

\displaystyle 0\leq s_{t}\leq B

(9f)

\displaystyle g_{t+1}\sim F_{\cdot|g_{t}}

(9g)

\displaystyle c_{t+1}=\max\{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{d}_{t}+e_{t}-g_{t},c_{t}\}

(9h)

\displaystyle\bm{0}\preceq\bm{d}_{t}\preceq\bm{\overline{d}}.

(9i)

Constraints (
9e
) and (
9h
) induce intertemporal coupling, precluding a myopic (stage-wise optimal) policy. Specifically, relaxing either constraint in isolation is insufficient to eliminate the temporal dependencies, and the resulting optimal policy remains non-myopic (see
Thm. 4
in the Appendix). However, when both constraints are simultaneously relaxed, the problem admits a myopic optimal policy characterized by decoupled per-period decisions (see
Lem. 4
).

The value function
V_{t}^{\pi}(\cdot)
is defined as the expected total reward received under the policy
\pi

V_{t}^{\pi}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=0}^{T}r_{t}(x_{t},\pi(x_{t}))|x_{t}=x]
. The optimal value function is defined as
V_{t}^{*}(x)=\max_{\pi}V_{t}^{\pi}(x)

.

u_{t}

(e_{t},\bm{d}_{t})\in\mathcal{F}_{t}=[\max\{-\underline{e},-s_{t}\},\min\{\overline{e},B-s_{t}\}]\times\prod_{i=1}^{K}[0,d_{i}]
is a compact set. From
Lem. 1
to
Lem. 3
, the optimal value function and Q-function is concave in action
u_{t}
, so the optimal action

u_{t}^{*}=\arg\max_{u\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}\{r_{t}(x_{t},u)+Q_{t}^{*}(x_{t},u)\}

(14)

must exist in the interior of the feasible set
\mathcal{F}_{t}
or at its boundary. The optimal action can be obtained by solving concave optimizations with and without boundary constraints, which results in a threshold policy.
∎

Although
Thm. 1
reveals a structured policy,
direct dynamic programming implementation remains impractical. First, optimization necessitates gradients of
r_{t}
and
Q_{t}
, which are computationally prohibitive for complex utility functions. Also, continuous-space Q-function tracking requires costly discretization, as validated in simpler settings
[
4
]
. To this end, we develop an efficient approximation algorithm to overcome these computational barriers.

IV
Efficient Approximation Algorithm

Since computing the exact optimal solution for problem (
9
) is computationally intractable, we propose an efficient approximation algorithm. The large storage peak searching (LSPS) algorithm efficiently searches for the optimal peak demand by solving a relaxed version of the original problem. The resulting solution is then projected onto the feasible region
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, producing near-optimal and computationally tractable approximations. The original formulation prevents myopic solutions through constraints (
9e
) and (
9h
). To address this computational challenge, we first relax constraint (
9e
) by considering a large capacity regime (Sec.
IV-A
), then develop an efficient method to handle constraint (
9h
) (Sec.
IV-B
).

IV-A

Relaxation of the DER Co-Optimization Problem

We consider a large capacity regime as a relaxation of the battery capacity constraint (
9e
). Observing that
e_{t}
and
\mathbf{d}_{t}
consistently appear together in the expression
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{d}_{t}+e_{t}
, we introduce a helper function
h_{t}(v_{t})
:

\displaystyle h_{t}(v_{t}):=\max\{U_{t}(\bm{d}_{t})+\gamma e_{t}:\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{d}_{t}+e_{t}=v_{t},\bm{0}\preceq\bm{d}_{t}\preceq\overline{\bm{d}},-\underline{e}\leq e_{t}\leq\overline{e}\},

(15)

where
v_{t}
represents the net consumption excluding renewables at time step
t
.
The function
h_{t}(v_{t})
is differentiable, monotonically increasing, and concave for all time steps
t
. For any
v_{t}\in[-\underline{e},\overline{e}+\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{\overline{d}}]
, there exists a valid pair
(e_{t},\mathbf{d}_{t})
that achieves
h_{t}(v_{t})
with
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{d}_{t}+e_{t}=v_{t}
. This equivalence allows us to optimize over
v_{t}
after relaxing constraint (
9e
).

Using the helper function (
15
), we formulate the relaxed problem as:

\displaystyle\max_{v_{0},\ldots,v_{T-1}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}h_{t}(v_{t})-p_{t}^{+}[v_{t}-g_{t}]^{+}+p_{t}^{-}[v_{t}-g_{t}]^{-}-p[v_{t}-g_{t}-c_{t}]^{+}

Subject to

\displaystyle-\underline{e}\leq v_{t}\leq\overline{e}+\bm{1}^{\top}\overline{\bm{d}}

(16a)

\displaystyle c_{t+1}=\max\{c_{t},v_{t}-g_{t}\},

(16b)

To eliminate the temporal dependencies introduced by constraint (
9h
), we consider a fixed peak demand bound
c
in the large capacity regime (
16
):

\displaystyle\max_{v_{0},\ldots,v_{T-1}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}h_{t}(v_{t})-p_{t}^{+}[v_{t}-g_{t}]^{+}+p_{t}^{-}[v_{t}-g_{t}]^{-}-pc

(17a)

Subject to

\displaystyle-\underline{e}\leq v_{t}\leq\overline{e}+\bm{1}^{\top}\overline{\bm{d}},

(17b)

\displaystyle v_{t}\leq g_{t}+c.

(17c)

This reformulation replaces the recursive peak demand update (
16b
) with a single-step upper-bound constraint (
17c
), effectively removing temporal dependencies while preserving the optimal policy structure. We show that the optimal policy of (
16
) is preserved under this relaxation.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
\{v_{t}^{*}\}
and
c^{*}
are optimal actions and peak demand for (
16
), then
\{v_{t}^{*}\}
is also optimal for (
17
) with demand bound
c=c^{*}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\{v_{t}^{*}\}
is the optimal solution for problem (
16
). Since
c^{*}
is the optimal peak demand, we have
v_{t}-g_{t}\leq c^{*}=c
for all
t
. This means
\{v_{t}\}
is also feasible for problem (
17
). Since problem (
17
) searches within a subspace of problem (
16
),
\{v_{t}^{*}\}
is also optimal for problem (
17
).
∎

By
Thm. 2
, we can compute the optimal policy for (
16
) by searching for the optimal
c^{*}
and solving (
17
).

IV-B

Solution under Given Peak Demand

With a fixed peak demand bound
c
, the optimization problem decomposes into independent single-step subproblems that can be solved analytically. Due to the concavity of
h_{t}
, we first derive the unconstrained optimum
v^{\dagger}_{t}(g_{t})
, then project it onto the feasible region
\mathcal{F}_{t}
defined by the constraints to obtain the constrained optimum
v^{*}_{t}(g_{t})
.

Lemma 4
.

For
t=0,\ldots,T-1
, the optimal policy for (
17
) is given by

\displaystyle v_{t}^{\ast}(g_{t})=\begin{cases}-\underline{e},\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\ v^{\dagger}(g_{t})\leq-\underline{e},\\
v_{t}^{\dagger}(g_{t}),\quad\quad\quad\quad-\underline{e}\leq v^{\dagger}(g_{t})\leq\min\{c+g_{t},\overline{e}+\bm{1}^{\top}\overline{\bm{d}}\},\\
\min\{c+g_{t},\overline{e}+\bm{1}^{\top}\overline{\bm{d}}\},\min\{c+g_{t},\overline{e}+\bm{1}^{\top}\overline{\bm{d}}\}\leq v^{\dagger}(g_{t}),\end{cases}

(18)

where the unconstrained optimum
v_{t}^{\dagger}(g_{t})
is the solution without constraints (
17b
) and (
17c
), given by

\displaystyle v_{t}^{\dagger}(g_{t})=\begin{cases}(h^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{-}),&(h^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{-})<g_{t},\\
g_{t},&(h^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{+})\leq g_{t}\leq(h^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{-}),\\
(h^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{+}),&g_{t}<(h^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{+}).\end{cases}

(19)

Proof.

With fixed peak demand, the optimization decomposes into independent subproblems at each time step
t=0,1,\cdots,T-1
:

\displaystyle\max_{v_{t}}\quad

\displaystyle h_{t}(v_{t})-p_{t}^{+}[v_{t}-g_{t}]^{+}+p_{t}^{-}[v_{t}-g_{t}]^{-}

(20a)

Subject to

\displaystyle v_{t}\in[-\underline{e},\overline{e}+\bm{1}^{\top}\overline{\bm{d}}],\quad v_{t}-g_{t}\leq c.

(20b)

The unconstrained optimum is

\displaystyle v_{t}^{\dagger}(g_{t})=\arg\max_{v}~~h_{t}(v)-p_{t}^{+}[v-g_{t}]^{+}+p_{t}^{-}[v-g_{t}]^{-}=\begin{cases}(h_{t}^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{-}),&(h_{t}^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{-})<g_{t},\\
g_{t},&(h_{t}^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{+})<g_{t}<(h_{t}^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{-}),\\
(h_{t}^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{+}),&g_{t}<(h_{t}^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{+}).\end{cases}

Projecting onto the feasible region defined by constraint (
20b
) yields the optimal policy in (
18
).
∎

The optimal policy has a clear economic interpretation based on the relationship between renewable generation and consumption needs. The unconstrained solution
v_{t}^{\dagger}(g_{t})
balances the marginal utility of consumption against energy trading costs. When
(h_{t}^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{-})<g_{t}
, the renewable generation
g_{t}
exceeds the energy demanded by consuming at the selling price
\pi^{-}
and charging the battery. In this case, the optimal consumption
v_{t}^{\dagger}(g_{t})=(h_{t}^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{-})
is set such that the marginal utility equals the selling price, meaning excess generation beyond this consumption level should be sold to the grid. When
(h_{t}^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{+})\leq g_{t}\leq(h_{t}^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{-})
, the renewable generation exactly matches the optimal consumption level, so
v_{t}^{\dagger}(g_{t})=g_{t}
and no energy trading occurs. When
g_{t}<(h_{t}^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{+})
, renewable generation is insufficient relative to the consumption level that would justify purchasing energy at price
p_{t}^{+}
. Here, the optimal consumption
v_{t}^{\dagger}(g_{t})=(h_{t}^{\prime})^{-1}(p_{t}^{+})
sets marginal utility equal to the purchase price, requiring additional energy to be bought from the grid. The final policy
v_{t}^{*}(g_{t})
then projects this unconstrained optimum onto the feasible region, ensuring that consumption respects both battery operation limits and peak demand constraints.

IV-C

Algorithmic Search of the Optimal Peak Demand

We leverage the concavity properties of the objective function to efficiently determine the optimal peak demand
c^{*}
without solving the complete optimization problem (
16
). Our approach exploits the structural properties of the relaxed co-optimization problem (
17
).

Lemma 5
.

Let
J(c)
be the optimal objective function of (
17
),

J(c):=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}h_{t}(v_{t}^{*})-p_{t}^{+}[v_{t}^{*}-g_{t}]^{+}+p_{t}^{-}[v_{t}^{*}-g_{t}]^{-}-pc,

(21)

where
v_{t}^{*}
depends on
c
through the optimization. Then
J(c)
is concave in
c
.

Proof.

Let
I(c)=\{t:v_{t}^{*}=c+g_{t}\}
be the set of time indices where the peak demand constraint is active. We can decompose the objective function as

\displaystyle J(c)=-pc+\sum_{t\in I(c)}h_{t}(c+g_{t})-p_{t}^{+}c+\sum_{t\in[T-1]\setminus I(c)}h_{t}(v_{t}^{*})-p_{t}^{+}[v_{t}^{*}-g_{t}]^{+}+p_{t}^{-}[v_{t}^{*}-g_{t}]^{-}.

As
c
increases, the constraint set becomes less restrictive, so
|I(c)|
is non-increasing. For the terms not in
I(c)
, the optimal
v_{t}^{*}
is independent of
c
and determined by the unconstrained optimum.
Taking the first and second derivatives,

\displaystyle J^{\prime}(c)=-p+\sum_{t\in I(c)}h^{\prime}_{t}(c+g_{t})-p_{t}^{+},

\displaystyle J^{\prime\prime}(c)=\sum_{t\in I(c)}h^{\prime\prime}_{t}(c+g_{t})\leq 0,

where the inequality follows from the concavity of
h_{t}
. Therefore,
J(c)
is concave in
c
.
∎

The concavity of
J(c)
ensures that the optimal
c^{*}
is unique and can be found by solving the first-order condition
J^{\prime}(c^{*})=0
. The key insight is that
I(c)
changes only at discrete values of
c
, allowing us to search for the optimum efficiently. Specifically, define the candidate set

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\{\min\{v_{t}^{\dagger}(g_{t})-g_{t},\bm{1}^{\top}\overline{\bm{d}}+\overline{e}-g_{t}\}\}_{t=0}^{T-1},

where
v_{t}^{\dagger}(g_{t})
is the unconstrained optimum from (
19
). The set
I(c)
remains constant for
c\in[c_{1},c_{2}]
, where

\displaystyle c_{1}=\max\{c:c\in\mathcal{C},J^{\prime}(c)<0\},

(22)

\displaystyle c_{2}=\min\{c:c\in\mathcal{C},J^{\prime}(c)>0\}.

The optimal
c^{*}
lies in this interval and satisfies

\displaystyle J^{\prime}(c^{*})=-p+\sum_{t\in I(c_{1})}h^{\prime}_{t}(c^{*}+g_{t})-p_{t}^{+}=0.

(23)

IV-D

Large Storage Peak Searching (LSPS) Algorithm

The LSPS algorithm, in
Alg. 1
, efficiently approximates the problem (
9
) by combining the relaxation techniques from the previous sections. It leverages the key structural properties established in the previous sections: the decomposition enabled by the large capacity relaxation, the analytical solution for fixed peak demand, and the concavity of the objective function in the peak demand parameter. By searching over the discrete candidate set
\mathcal{C}
, we avoid solving the full dynamic optimization problem and rather solve a linear complexity algorithm, as formalized below, while maintaining a small solution gap compared to the optimal.

.

has computational complexity

.

Proof.

Under the assumption that
h_{t}^{\prime}
and
(h_{t}^{\prime})^{-1}

V-C

Control Strategies

We present five control strategies to schedule DER here:

Backup Mode

The backup mode prioritizes using renewables to charge the battery to maintain a high SoC level, which is usually set by the user. The battery discharges only during grid outages, equivalent to the case without a battery system
[
4
]
during normal operations.

Renewable-Adjusted Threshold Policy (RATP)

RATP is a threshold-based battery scheduling policy that adjusts charging and discharging based on the renewable adjusted gross consumption (
\tilde{d}=d-g
). When
\tilde{d}>0
, the battery discharges to reduce grid purchases; when
\tilde{d}<0
, it charges to avoid exporting excess renewables at the low
p^{-}
. When
\tilde{d}=0
the battery stays idle. Although simple, RATP is highly effective under NEM structures, as it arbitrages the difference between the retail and sell rates by minimizing power imports and exports. However, RATP’s performance significantly degrades under NEM with demand charges, as it is agnostic to strategic net consumption decisions that shape demand peaks.

Mathematically, the storage control under RATP is

e_{t}(g_{t})=\begin{cases}\max\{-\underline{e},\rho s_{t},-\tilde{d}_{t}\},&\bar{d}_{t}-g_{t}>0\\
\min\{\overline{e},(B-s_{t})/\tau,-\tilde{d}_{t}\}&\bar{d}_{t}-g_{t}\leq 0,\end{cases}

and
d_{t}=\bar{d}_{t}
in both cases. We use
RATP
and
Threshold
interchangeably.

RL Algorithm

We use a popular model-free RL method of proximal policy optimization (PPO) algorithms to directly learn the optimal policy for controlling the DER system described. PPO uses an actor-network and a critic network with a multilayer perceptron (MLP) structure. For training, each of the actor and critic networks consists of two hidden layers with 256 neurons per layer. The actor-network predicts the optimal action, while the critic network estimates the optimal value function. We train the networks with a learning rate of
1e^{-4}
, batch size of 64, and 16 epochs per update. Other training parameters are kept as default provided by Stable-baselines3
[
29
]
.

Large Storage Peak Searching (LSPS) Algorithm

LSPS algorithm, described in
Alg. 1
, offers an efficient and scalable method to co-optimize DER. Following
Alg. 1
, we first calculate the optimal peak, then, by solving
Eq. 17
with the optimal peak, we get the battery and demand actions at each time step.

Such behavior negatively impacted the utility function and the overall objective, causing gaps in the optimal solutions.

VI
Conclusion

This work provides the first analytical characterization of the joint optimization of flexible demand and BESS under demand charges and bi-directional power flow. We model the problem using a stochastic dynamic program and show that the optimal policy exhibits a threshold structure. To address computational complexity, we develop an efficient demand peak searching approximation method, based on a relaxed version of the problem, leading to a linear-complexity algorithm that identifies the optimal peak demand.

Through simulations with real data from two rich datasets, we demonstrated the performance of the LSPS algorithm in achieving a small solution gap compared to baseline approaches, highlighting its effectiveness compared to relatively more computationally prohibitive methods such as model-predictive control and RL.

The work presented here has several limitations that open avenues for future research.
Firstly, the RL algorithm used in our simulations is relatively simple. Designing a more sophisticated algorithm that reliably converges to the theoretical upper bound remains an important direction for future work. Second, the proposed algorithm assumes full knowledge of the utility’s functional form
